ABSTRACT: Due to the poor electronic conductivity of solid sulfur and sulfides,
the dissolution of Sα−(α = 0, 2/8, 2/6, 2/4) into a liquid electrolyte and the
vehicular diffusion of Sα−to carbon black are necessary for the electrochemical
activity of a sulfur cathode in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries. However, exactly how
much dissolution and diffusion are required for high sulfur utilization and how this
may control the minimum electrolyte/sulfur ratio, (E/S)min, have not been
quantitatively settled. In this work, we show experimentally that a dissolved
polysulfide concentration which is too high (>10−20 MS) may gel the liquid
electrolyte, leading to catastrophic loss of Sα−mobility, a failure mode that is
especially susceptible in a high-donor-number (DN) electrolyte under a lean
condition (low E/S), similar to a traffic jam, resulting in high electrochemical
polarization and low sulfur utilization. In contrast, we show that a low-DN
electrolyte, even with a low polysulfide solubility of 0.1−0.5 MS, will never
encounter a gelation catastrophe even at extremely low E/S, leading to unprecedentedly high energy density. Specifically, high sulfur
utilizations of 96% (1600 mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) are reached in an electrolyte as lean as E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S
coin cells when DME1.6LiFSI-HFE of low solvation capability (DN = 13.9) is adopted, even paired against a high-sulfur-loading
cathode (5 mg cm−2).
KEYWORDS: lithium-sulfur battery, electrolyte/sulfur ratio, gelation, high sulfur utilization, gravimetric energy density
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) is a promising post-lithium-ion battery
chemistry due to the high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh
kg−1, based on the active electrode materials of lithium and
sulfur)1−3 and low price (the price of sulfur is US$0.1 kg−1).4,5
While Li-S chemistry is very attractive in applications where
gravimetric energy density matters a great deal, it confronts
great challenges in coming to the realization of the high actual
energy density on a full-cell level.6−10 One of the most
emerging concerns is the low sulfur utilization under a lean
electrolyte condition (the electrolyte condition in a Li-S
battery can be described by the electrolyte/sulfur ratio, E/S,
which is the total volume of injected liquid electrolyte to the
total weight of sulfur in the cell, in units of μL mg−1; a lean
electrolyte condition means low E/S). The E/S currently used
in Li-S batteries is usually larger than 5 μL mg−1 to boost the
sulfur utilization. This means that the inactive liquid electrolyte
weighs several times more than the active sulfur. The exact
reason why such a high E/S was required has never been clearly
explained in the literature, but what researchers have found
experimentally was that when the E/S ratio was reduced to 2
μL mg−1, high polarization is always observed at the end of the
first discharging plateau in the Li-S battery, accompanied by
the absence of the second discharging plateau and thus low
sulfur utilization (as shown in Figure S1).11
Theoretically, due to the poor electronic conductivity of
solid sulfur and sulfides, the operation of a sulfur cathode relies
on local sulfur mobility (LSM), which means that solid
insulating sulfur species first dissolve into the liquid electrolyte
and subsequently diffuse to the nearest conductive carbon
black to access free electrons and to perform redox reactions,
as shown in Figure 1a.12,13 Therefore, the dissolution and
vehicular diffusion of Sα−(α = 0, 2/8, 2/6, 2/4) are two key
steps for the electrochemical activity of the sulfur cathode. The
dissolution of polysulfides can stimulate the LSM to promote
the electrochemical kinetics of the sulfur cathode, but it can
also cause global sulfur mobility (GSM), which is the
movement of active substances between positive and negative
electrodes, leading to the loss of active substances and the
polysulfide shuttle effect. Therefore, a proper amount of
dissolution and diffusion is vital to achieve a high sulfur
utilization, which fortunately can be optimized by adjusting the
E/S ratio. To make the ensuing discussions easier, we will use
MS ≡mol(Sα−) L−1 as the unit of dissolution: that is, the
number of moles of Sα−dissolved in 1 L of the liquid solution
phase (e.g., if the solubility of Li2S8 is 1 M L−1 then the
dissolution of S1/4−is 8 MS). Roughly speaking, with DOL

For example, with an unrealistically high E/S such as 10 μL

in an actual cell during cycling may be around 5 MS, very high
but not yet problematic. However, when E/S = 3 μL mg−1 of
the same electrolyte is used, the maximum MS value in an
actual cell during cycling will skyrocket to beyond 10−15 MS,
which could be highly problematic for the cell, as we will
demonstrate.
One school of thought why this is necessary is the “complete
dissolution theory”: that is, the complete dissolution of
polysulfides into the liquid electrolyte at some point in the
discharge and charge is a necessary condition for the Li-S
battery to cycle. According to this school of thought, high
polarization in a lean electrolyte Li-S battery can be attributed
to the incomplete dissolution of polysulfide:11,14,15 that is,
some polysulfides are dissolved as Sα−(electrolyte), while
others remain as solid-phase Li2Sn (α = 2/n). For example, the
solubility of Li2S8 was reported to be 6−7 MS in a common
ether-solvent electrolyte, and assuming all sulfur inventory in
the cathode must exist as dissolved Sα−(α = 2/8) in the first
discharge plateau and no solid sulfur inventory exists at that
point, this would require the minimum electrolyte amount to
be (E/S)min = 4−5 μL/mg.15 When the E/S ratio is lower than
4−5 μL mg−1, the “complete dissolution theory” would say
Li2S8 generated in the battery discharge would exceed the
solubility limit and precipitate out as a solid phase, which
somehow will block the conduction pathways and result in
high polarization.14−16 Nazar et al.17,18 have reported that a Li-
S battery could also deliver high sulfur utilization in a sparingly
solvated electrolyte (SSE), which has very small solubility for
Li2Sn. The solubility of Li2Sn in an SSE was estimated to be less
than 3 wt % in a cell, which means that 97 wt % of the
polysulfides cannot dissolve and act as a solid phase during the
battery operation. The normal operation of the Li-S battery in
an SSE indicates that the complete dissolution of polysulfides
at some point is not a necessary condition for a Li-S battery to
cycle, which contradicts the complete dissolution theory.
Therefore, the enlarged polarization in the lean electrolyte Li-S
battery may not necessarily becaused by the incomplete
dissolution of polysulfides and the failure of the battery under a
lean-electrolyte condition might be the outcome of multiple
causes.
Instead, we show experimentally in this work that the high
dissolution of polysulfides (e.g., 30 MS) in ether-solvent
electrolyte is responsible for the high polarization in the lean-
electrolyte Li-S battery. We found that the reported solubility
of Li2S8 (6−7 MS) in the literature mainly comes from the test
results of coin cells through a dilution approach and may not
be accurate.19,20 The total amount of electrolyte added in cells
often cannot be completely absorbed by the positive electrode
due to the existence of a large number of head spaces, and the
fraction of electrolyte that can actually participate in the
dissolution of polysulfide is often much smaller. When we

method, which is more widely applied in solubility tests, we
surprisingly find the value is far more than 6−7 MS and can
exceed 31.2 MS in Li2S8-DME solution. The unexpectedly high
solubility limit means that the solid Li2Sn can indeed mostly
dissolve into the liquid electrolyte, Li2Sn(solid) →2Li+(liquid)

corresponds to 31.2 MS). However, a value greater than 10 MS
is not a good thing. In other words, when E/S sweeps from the
flooded condition (e.g., 5 μL mg−1) to the lean condition (e.g.,
1 μL mg−1), the actual concentration of Sα−in the liquid
electrolyte will skyrocket and can become super highly
concentrated (∼30 MS) or even reach the electrolyte gelation
point (∼60 MS) under an ultralean condition (E/S ≤1 μL
mg−1). When this occurs, the mobility of Sα−in the liquid
electrolyte will experience a catastrophic loss due to the sharp
increased viscosity, as as shown in the schematic in Figure 1d.
A frozen Sα−transport, due to ironically too much dissolved
Sα−, is definitely undesirable for the electrochemical activity of
the sulfur cathode because the Sα−jammed in the electrolyte
cannot reach the carbon black to access free electrons and
perform redox reactions (see Figure 1b). We have shown in
this work that this is the typical failure mode in a lean-
electrolyte Li-S battery with a high-donor-number (DN)
electrolyte. To better interpret the relationship between the
concentration and the mobility of Sα−, we can envision the cars
in a traffic jam, all of which show almost no mobility at high
concentration.21,22
Up to now, few methods have been available to improve the
sulfur utilization under a lean-electrolyte condition. Especially,
a high-sulfur-utilization Li-S battery under an ultralean-
electrolyte condition (e.g., E/S = 1 μL mg−1) has not yet
been reported. Herein, we show that a high dissolution of
polysulfides >5 MS is definitely overkill and unnecessary to
reach high sulfur utilization. Rather, reducing the solubility
limit is an effective way to improve the Li-S battery
performance under a lean-electrolyte condition. According to
Faraday’s law, Q = nzF, we show that only 0.1 MS Sα−
solubility can sustain a 0.5C current density in a Li-S battery
while 0.5 MS solubility can sustain over 2C current density,
even under a lean condition of E/S = 1 μL mg−1, as shown in
Figure S2. This means that a 0.1−0.5 MS solubility limit, far
different from the 5−60 MS we have in an ether-based
electrolyte, is already sufficient to sustain a high electro-
chemical activity of the sulfur cathode. Philosophically this
makes sense, since we know 1 M Li salt is sufficient to support
long-range Li+ transport in all kinds of batteries; thus, in order
to support local sulfur mobility (LSM) while cutting down
global sulfur mobility (GSM),12,13 one should not need much
more than 1 MS for Sα−! This consideration paints a different
conceptual picture from the “complete dissolution theory” and
points to a very different optimal MS-parametric regime (less
than 1 MS, rather than >5 MS) for the liquid electrolyte design
for a Li-S battery. Most importantly, by cutting down the
intrinsic solubility limit, there is never complete dissolution at
any point, and in fact most of the sulfur and sulfides are in the
form of Li2Sn(solid) and only a small fraction is in the form of
Sα−(liquid) in the electrolyte, and so the liquid electrolyte will
never encounter a gelation catastrophe even at an extremely
low E/S. Quantitatively, the optimum [Sα−(liquid)] might be
around 0.5 MS to support LSM, and this value is far from the
electrolyte gelation point (>30 MS). By preserving the
mobility of Sα−under a lean condition (Figure 1d), which
can only move by vehicular diffusion and not by a Grotthuss-
like exchange mechanism like H+/Li+, one can thus achieve an
unprecedentedly low (E/S)min and a high energy density of the
Li-S battery. In this work, through adopting the sparingly
solvated electrolyte DME1.6LiFSI-HFE with a low DN of 13.9,
the dissolution of polysulfides was suppressed to an extremely
low level. As a result, high sulfur utilizations of 96% (1600
mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) in lean electrolytes with
E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S coin cells (tested at room
temperature) have been reached, even paired against a high-
sulfur-loading cathode (5 mg cm−2). Combining with the high-
sulfur-loading cathode we developed in the previous work,23 a
2.3 Ah Li-S pouch cell assembled with industrially satisfactory
parameters (sulfur loading 7 mg cm−2, E/S = 1 μL mg−1, 1×
lithium excess) was demonstrated; it turns out that the
practical energy density can reach 589 Wh kg−1.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Material and Electrolyte Preparation. S8 and Li2S were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich with a purity of 99.9%. 1,2-
Dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), lithium bis-
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), and lithium bis-
(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) were purchased from DodoChem
(Suzhou, China) with a water content of less than 50 ppm. The
electrolytes and Li2Sn solutions were prepared in a glovebox with
water content and oxygen contents of less than 0.1 ppm
(MIKROUNA, Super 1220/750). The electrolytes at different
concentrations were prepared by changing the molar ratio between
lithium salts and solvents (from 1:9 to 1:1.6). 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluor-
oethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) was added to highly
concentrated electrolytes of (DME)3Li salts and (DME)1.6Li salts at a
volume ratio of 2:1. Li2Sn solutions at different S concentrations were
prepared by mixing stoichiometric Li2S and S8 by an evaporation
method. First, a dilute Li2Sn solution (no precipitates at the
beginning) was obtained by mixing stoichiometric Li2S and S8 in
excess solvent (E/S = 10 μL mg−1, 3.12 MS). After a full reaction, the
homogeneous dilute solution was then evaporated gradually until a
solid phase started to appear. The immediate concentration was
recorded as the solubility of Li2Sn.
Solubility Test of Li2S8. In the evaporation method, a dilute Li2S8
solution was first obtained by mixing stoichiometric Li2S and S8 in
DME (the E/S in Li2S8 was controlled at 10 μL mg−1 corresponding
to 3.12 MS L−1). After that, the Li2S8 solution was stirred at 60 °C for
10 h to accelerate the reaction. Finally, the solution was evaporated at
60 °C to reach the target concentration by monitoring the variation of
the solvent weight. In a test of the Li2S4 solubility, the Li2S and S8
could not react adequately, even after stirring at 80 °C for 1 week at a
high E/S of 20 μL mg−1 (1.56 MS).
Construction of a Transparent Capillary Li-S Battery (C-
LSB). The C-LSB was constructed based on a square quartz capillary
tube with a size of 0.4 mm × 0.4 mm × 15 mm. The cathode current
collector (carbon-coated aluminum foil) was cut into long strips (0.3
mm × 5 mm) in advance, and one end was coated with a cathode
slurry (the slurry area was controlled at 0.3 mm × 0.3 mm and with a
sulfur loading of 1.5 mg cm−2). Similarly, the lithium metal anode was
cut into long strips with a size of 0.3 mm × 5 mm in advance. When
the battery was assembled, the lithium anode and sulfur cathode were
inserted from both ends of the quartz capillary and the conductive
wires were led out, respectively. The electrolyte was injected by using
a microinjector, and the electrolyte volume was about 2.4 μL (the E/S
ratio in C-LSB was about 1700 μL mg−1). The ends of the capillary
were sealed with AB glue. A combination of an optical microscope
and some other methods of spectral analysis of C-LSB enabled us to
study the electrochemical process of the sulfur cathode in situ in the
battery operation.

Sulfur Cathode Manufacturing. Sulfur cathodes with a high
sulfur loading (5 mg cm−2 for coin cells and 7 mg cm−2 for pouch
cells) were fabricated by a lyophilization method, as we reported
previously. First, commercial sulfur powder and Ketjen black (KB)
(Lion Co., ECP-600JD) were mixed uniformly in a ratio of 4:1 and
heated at 155 °C for 12 h (80 wt % sulfur). After that, the as-prepared
S/KB composites were mixed with KB and aqueous LA-133 binder
(Chengdu Indigo Power Sources Co., Ltd., China) at mass ratios of
80:15:5 (5 mg cm−2) and 90:5:5 (7 mg cm−2) to reach uniform
slurries. Thereafter, the slurry was coated onto an aluminum foil
current collector at different thicknesses according to the sulfur
loading. The as-prepared electrode was first frozen at −40 °C for 2 h
and then vacuumed to 0.1 Pa for 2 h to induce ice crystal sublimation
and finally compacted to the target thickness and porosity (the
porosity of the sulfur cathode was controlled at 55%; the thickness of
the 5 mg cm−2 cathode was 85 μm and the compact density was 0.6 g
S cm−3 and the thickness of the 7 mg cm−2 cathode was 105 μm and
the compact density was 0.66 g S cm−3). A sulfur cathode with low
sulfur loading (1.5 mg cm−2) was also fabricated to assemble a C-LSB.
The sulfur cathode was fabricated by conventional thermal drying
with the same sulfur ratio as in the 7 mg cm−2 sulfur cathode.

■RESULTS WITH DISCUSSION
Electrolyte Gelation under Lean Conditions. Previ-
ously, the solubility of Li2Sn in the commonly used DOL
+DME electrolyte was reported to be 6−7 MS (based on
Li2S8) through a dilution test approach, where an extra amount
of stoichiometric Li2S and S8 was added to the solvents,
followed by a certain period of stirring (typically at 60 °C for
10 h), and then the saturated solution was diluted gradually
until all species were dissolved.19,20 In this case, the solubility
based on Li2S8 could be calculated with the total added solvent
amount and the Li2S8 molarity according to Li2S + 7
8S8 →
Li2S8. Note that such a dilution approach assumes that Li2S
and S8 react completely in the first place and the only
undissolved species is Li2S8, which, however, is not necessarily
true. As a matter of fact, we examined the precipitates in the
“saturated solution” by preparing concentrated Li2S8 in DME
(15.6 MS, corresponding to E/S = 2 μL mg−1). After stirring at
60 °C for 10 h, there are still a large amount of precipitate in
the bottom of the bottle, as shown in Figure S3a. The Raman
spectrum and XRD analysis (Figure S3b,c) indicate that the
precipitates mainly consist of S8, with a small part being Li2S,
which is consistent with the weight percent of S8 and Li2S used
to reach Li2S8 (∼7:1). That is, the solubility of polysulfides
may be larger than 6−7 MS because the chemical reaction
between Li2S and S8 is actually not complete at high
concentration; we adopted an evaporation method to test
the solubility in this study, which is more widely applied in
solubility tests to obtain a more accurate solubility of Li2Sn. As
shown in Figure 2a, a dilute Li2Sn solution (no precipitates at
the beginning) was first obtained by mixing stoichiometric Li2S
and S8 in excess solvent (e.g., E/S = 10 μL mg−1, 3.12 MS);
after a full reaction, a homogeneous dilute solution was then
evaporated gradually until solid-phase Li2Sn started to appear.
The immediate concentration was recorded as the solubility of
Li2Sn. Surprisingly, we found that the solubility of Li2S8 is far
more than 6−7 MS. Figure S4b and Figure 2b,c show the
optical microscopy images (500× magnification) of DME with
different concentrations of Li2S8. These images were obtained
by absorbing the electrolyte in a capillary tube and then
observing through an optical microscope, as schematically
shown in Figure S4a. This indicated that no solid precipitates
were found in the 10.4 MS (Figure S4b) and 15.6 MS (Figure
2b) electrolytes, even though the current Li2S8 concentrations
of which in DME have exceeded those commonly reported. As
we further evaporated the solvents to 31.2 MS (corresponding
to E/S = 1 μL mg−1), the electrolyte mixture became slurrylike
with precipitates showing up, as revealed in Figure 2c.
However, a Raman spectral analysis identified these precip-
itates as elemental S8 instead of Li2S8 (Figure S5). The
generation of S8 may be attribute to the disproportionation
reaction Li2S8 →S8 + Li2S6/Li2S4/Li2S2, which was reported to
be exacerbated at high Li2S8 concentration.24 Therefore, while
it might be difficult to evaluate the solubility of Li2S8 exactly,
one could conclude that the solubility of Li2S8 must be greaater
than 6−7 MS and may exceed 31.2 MS because no solid Li2S8
precipitated in the 31.2 MS electrolyte.
The above means that, chemically, ethers really prefer to mix
with sulfides. Particular attention should be paid to the
viscosity evolution of the electrolyte as the concentration of
Li2S8 increases, and importantly, it is directly related to the
vehicular diffusion of Sn
2−. From the inset in Figure 2c, the
electrolyte has become quite sticky and could be drawn into a
long line at 31.2 MS, indicating a high viscosity. The red line in
Figure 2e quantitatively shows that the viscosity of the
electrolyte has increased about 3 orders of magnitude from 4.3
to 1.8 × 103 mPa s when the polysulfide concentration
increases from 6.24 to 31.2 MS (the viscosity was tested in a
rheometer with its rotation mode at 25 °C, and the value of the
viscosity was chosen at a shear rate of 500 s−1). Meanwhile,
Figure S6 shows that the electrolyte has changed from a
Newtonian fluid to a non-Newtonian fluid when the
concentration is increased to 31.2 MS. To make it even
worse, as shown in Figure 2d, when the concentration of Li2S8
is increased to 62.4 MS (E/S = 0.5 μL mg−1; the mole ratio of
DME to nominal Li2S8 is about 1.2:1), the electrolyte

Depressing the Solvation Capability of Electrolyte. In
the previous section, we concluded that the gelation of the
electrolyte initiated by the high dissolution of Li2Sn was the
main reason for the enlarged polarization in the lean-
electrolyte Li-S battery. As a verification, in the following
sections, we prepared an electrolyte with low solvation
capability to suppress the dissolution of Li2Sn and to study
the electrochemical performance of a lean-electrolyte Li-S
battery.
Generally speaking, the solubility of polysulfides is
determined by the solvation capability of electrolytes. In this
work, electrolytes with low solvation capabilities were reached
by regulating the concentration of lithium salts, as reported
previously.27−29 The lithium salt used in our experiment is
lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), and the solvent is
DME, both of which are reported to have great compatibility
with the lithium metal anode.28Figure 4a shows the Raman
spectra of electrolytes with different LiFSI concentrations. The
peaks located at 1138 and 1123 cm−1 are respectively
correlated to the asymmetric stretching mode of C−O−C
(νas(C−O)) in DME before and after coordination with
Li+.30,31 Commonly, the content of the free solvents in the
electrolyte is an important index of the solvation capability that
can be reflected by the intensity of the Raman peaks. It can be
found that the ratio between the coordinated DME and free
DME increases rapidly along with the increment of LiFSI
concentration, as indicated by the variation of the peak
intensity at 1123 and 1138 cm−1. In particular, when the
concentration of LiFSI exceeds 3.2 M ((DME)3LiFSI), the
original peak at 1138 cm−1 has disappeared, suggesting that
most of the free DME solvents are complexing with Li+. Nazar
et al. have achieved dendrite-free lithium plating of the
electrolyte at a E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1 by using a diglyme
electrolyte with a saturated LiTFSI salt, G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1), to
adjust the electrolyte network structure.22 As a comparison, we
also studied the Raman spectra of electrolytes adopting lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the salt,
shown in Figure 4b. From Figure 4b we find that LiTFSI is
not good at lowering the electrolyte solvation capability, as
typically there are still a large number of free DME molecules
in the electrolyte even when the concentration of LiTFSI is
increased to 6 M ((DME)1.6LiTFSI, approaching the
saturation concentration of LiTFSI in DME). The high
content of the free DME in the LiTFSI-based electrolyte
makes it incapable of suppressing the dissolution of Li2Sn
effectively, which also results in electrolyte gelation under lean-
electrolyte conditions. The reason for such a contrast in
coordinating DME with the LiFSI salt may be attributed to the
smaller bonding energies between Li+ and FSI−, and thus DME
can easily participate in the solvation shell and coordinate with
Li+, rendering less free DME. On the other hand, the
intensifying coordination environment of Li+ with TFSI−in
the LiTFSI-based electrolyte makes TFSI−more likely to
participate in the solvation shell, resulting in less DME
coordination with Li+ and concomitantly more free DME.32

The donor number (DN) is often a measure of the ability of
a solvent to solvate cations. Herein, to describe the depressed
solvation capability more quantitatively, we develop a donor
number (DN) testing method to study the solvation capability
of electrolytes. The testing method of DN was referred to a
study by Hahn et al.,33 who adopted standard proton (1H)
NMR with chloroform (CHCl3) as the reference acceptor to
establish a correlation between the CHCl3
1H NMR shift and
the solvent donor number. The theoretical basis is that the
molecular interaction between chloroform and the solvent
would decrease the electron shielding at the hydrogen of
chloroform when CHCl3 is added into donor solvents, and the
shift of chloroform in donor solvents will be downfield of the
shift of pure chloroform, since the acceptor hydrogen loses
shielding. The downfield shift (Δδ) increases with increasing
DN and is more pronounced for a dilute solution wherein the
isolated chloroform molecules are surrounded by solvent
molecules. In this study, we use this method to evaluate the
DN of electrolytes with different lithium salts and lithium salt
concentrations to describe the solvation capability of electro-
lyte quantitatively. In detail, 0.1 M CHCl3 was added to the
different electrolytes, and then we measured the chemical shift
of 1H NMR (δ(1H)) of CHCl3. Figure S13a shows that δ(1H)
in pure CHCl3 is 7.29 ppm. Figure 4c shows the δ(1H) values
of CHCl3 in the different electrolytes and the corresponding
Δδ values on comparison with the δ(1H) value in pure CHCl3.
It reveals a lower Δδ with an increasing LiFSI concentration,
indicating a smaller decrease of the electron shielding at the
hydrogen of chloroform, which reflects the lower electron-
donating ability and solvation capability. The NMR testing
results are consistent with the Raman spectrum results shown
in Figure 4a. To describe the solvation capability of electrolyte
quantitatively, we give a simple correction between the Δδ and
DN, DN = λΔδ(CHCl3). To obtain λ, we first tested the Δδ
value of CHCl3 in pure DME solvent, which has a known DN
(DN = 20), to determine the coefficient (see Figure S13b and
Figure 4c) to be λ = 10.1. Then, we calculated the DN of each
electrolyte by using the formula DN = 10.1Δδ(CHCl3). Figure
4d shows the DN of each electrolyte calculated by our method,
from which it is obvious that LiFSI with high concentration
leads to a low DN, and the lowest DN was achieved in
DME1.6LiFSI with a value of 15. Our method is more accurate
and quantitative than Raman spectrometry results. In Raman
spectra (Figure 4a), DME3LiFSI and DME1.6LiFSI have the
same spectra, making it difficult to determine the relatively
greater (or smaller) solvation capability. As a comparison, the
DN of electrolytes with LiTFSI has also been tested in this
work, as shown in Figure 4c,d. Not surprisingly, the LiFSI-
based electrolytes we used have a lower DN than LiTFSI-based
electrolytes at each concentration, which is beneficial to
suppress the dissolution of Li2Sn. These electrolytes with
different solvation capabilities will have different solubilities to
Li2Sn, as we will show in the following. It worth noting that we
have developed a method to evaluate the solvation capability of
electrolytes quantitatively, and the DN calculated in our work
may not equal the DN tested by conventional calorimetry.

electrolyte was injected in the C-LSB by using a microinjector,
and therefore the E/S ratio is about 1700 μL mg−1. Combined
with an optical microscope, C-LSB enables us to observe the
evolution of the electrolyte and sulfur cathode visually at
different discharge stages, as shosn in Figure 5b. Figure S15
shows the optical microscope images of the sulfur cathode in
C-LSB before and after injecting electrolytes. Interestingly,
large numbers of white crystal particles at 101 μm size have
formed on the surface of the sulfur cathode after immersion in
a high-solvation-capability electrolyte ((DME)9LiFSI, DN =
18.3), as shown in Figure S15b. Similar white particles were
also found in (DME)9LiTFSI and 1 M LiTFSI/DOL+DME
(Figure S15c,d). A Raman spectrum study (Figure S15g)

particles formed on the cathode surface are reduced. Even
though the particular reason for the aggregation of S8 is not
clear, it suggests that the formation of S8 is closely related to
the electrolyte solvation capability.
Except for S8, this setup is also very effective in studying the
dissolution of polysulfides. Generally, the discharge process of
a Li-S battery in common high-solvation-capability electrolytes
can be divided into three stages, as shown in Figure 5b. Herein,
we studied the dissolution of polysulfides in the different
discharge stages in C-LSB by observing the morphology and

can observe that significant dissolution of Li2S8/Li2S6 took

electrolyte, which represents the typical polysulfide dissolution

corresponds to the first voltage drop in the discharge curve

In the meantime, the electrochemical results show that
lowering the electrolyte solvation capability is effective in
postponing the electrolyte gelation and is beneficial for sulfur
utilization under the lean-electrolyte condition. A value of 0.1−
0.5 MS should suffice to support LSM and reach high sulfur
utilization in the Li-S battery, as indicated in Figure S2. Here,
we want to emphasize that the solvation capability of high-
concentration electrolytes must be depressed to a certain low
value so that a high sulfur utilization is attainable. As shown
above that LiTFSI is incapable of depressing the solvation
capability of DME, it cannot contribute to a high sulfur
utilization in a lean-electrolyte Li-S battery due to the
complexation between polysulfides and the lithium ion
solvation structure. Herein, we provide a simple method to
identify the solvation capability of electrolytes. We find that the
electrolyte’s solvation capability is related to the reduction
potential of S8 to Li2S8 in the discharge process of the Li-S
battery. Therefore, the reduction peak can be used to evaluate
the solvation capability of electrolytes. Figure 6g shows the
discharge dQ/dV curves of the Li-S battery, from which we can
see that the electrolytes with higher solvation capability lead to
a higher reduction potential. The reduction peaks of
(DME)9LiFSI, (DME)3LiFSI-HFE, and (DME)1.6LiFSI-HFE
are 2.29, 2.19, and 2.16 V, respectively. Also, as shown in
Figure S20b, the reduction peak in (DME)1.6LiTFSI-HFE is
2.22 V, higher than that of (DME)3LiFSI-HFE and lower than
that of (DME)9LiFSI. This reduction potential method is
reasonable because the solvation energy aroused by the
electrolyte to S8 (S8(s) →S8(l), ΔGsolvation) will increase the
total free energy change (ΔGtotal = ΔG + ΔGsolvation) of the
redox reaction, S8 + 2Li+ + 2e−→Li2S8.37,38 According to the
Nernst equation, ΔG = −nFU, a higher ΔGsolvation would result
in a higher ΔGtotal and a higher reduction potential of S8. From
the reduction potential in different electrolytes, we estimate the
upper limit of reduction potential that enables the Li-S battery
to work under a lean-electrolyte condition lies in the range of
2.16−2.19 V. Once the reduction potential exceeds this limit,
the solvation capability of the electrolyte is too high and is
incapable of suppressing polysulfide dissolution effectively. The
reduction potential method provides a convenient way to
elevate the solvation capability of an electrolyte for lean-
electrolyte Li-S batteries and also can be used to guide the

The increased sulfur utilization under a lean-electrolyte
condition certainly helps to improve the practical energy
density (PED) of the Li-S battery. In this work, an Ah-level Li-
S pouch cell with critical parameters was demonstrated to
validate the improvement of PED. Figure 7a gives a schematic
of the structure of the Li-S pouch cell assembled in this work,
which contains two single-side-coated sulfur cathodes, four
double-side-coated sulfur cathodes, and five pieces of lithium
anode (single piece lithium: 200 μm, 40 mAh cm−2). The
cathode was prepared through a commonly used blade-casting
method with commercial cheap raw materials according to our
previous work,23 and the single-side sulfur loading is kept at 7
mg cm−2. The porosity of the cathode is calculated to be 55%
with a compacted density of 0.66 g S cm−3 (Figure 7c). The
pore volume of the S cathode is about 1.75, and 2.1 mL of the
electrolyte was added to the pouch cell. The N/P ratio is about
2 in the pouch cell. Figure 7b gives the weight distribution of
the components used to assemble the Li-S pouch cell (the
weight of each component can be found in Table S1). The
lower part in Figure 7c is the Li-S pouch cell assembled in this
work. Figure 7d gives the voltage−capacity curve of the Li-S
pouch cell tested under a lean-electrolyte condition of E/S = 1
μL mg−1, and the current density used here is 0.05C in the
initial cycle and 0.1C in the following, and the test temperature
is 30 °C. It can be found that the capacity of the Li-S pouch
cell can reach 2.3 Ah. Under such a lean-electrolyte condition,
the PED of Li-S pouch cell can achieve 589 Wh kg−1,
surpassing state-of-the-art lithium-ion batteries and flooded
electrolyte Li-S batteries. Figure 7e gives the cycling perform-
ance of the Li-S pouch cell, which shows a serious capacity
fade. We found that the lithium metal anode was seriously
pulverized during the cycle by disassembling the failed cell,
which will undoubtedly increase the specific surface of the
anode and thus aggravate the consumption of electrolyte. At
the same time, a large number of pores are generated after
cycling, which consume a large amount of electrolyte,
increasing the polarization at the Li anode side and leading
to battery failure. We have to admit that it is a great challenge
to avoid the electrolyte consumption in the lithium−metal
anode at such a high area capacity (10 mAh cm−2) and high
current density without extra protection of the lithium−metal
anode. However, we still have the confidence that the
electrolyte consumption can be alleviated either by electrolyte
optimization or lithium anode protection in the near future, so
as to enable a high practical energy density Li-S battery to
work stably for dozens of cycles (e.g., 50 cycles ending up with
500 Wh kg−1).
■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we found that the real solubility of sulfide in the
electrolyte is far more than the 6−7 MS reported in the
literature by using an evaporation method to test the solubility,
which could influence the viscosity of the electrolyte and
eventually cause a gelation transition (where E/S is reduced to
a certain extent by the liquid into a solid electrolyte), affecting
the utilization efficiency of active substances and PGED of the
Li-sulfur battery. More importantly, we demonstrated that the
sulfur cathode can perform electrochemical reactions inde-
pendently of the dissolution of polysulfide and operate under
an ultralow electrolyte condition (E/S = 1 μL mg−1) by
reducing the solvation capacity of the electrolyte, which can
effectively delay the gelation transition and improve the
electrochemical performance of Li-sulfur batteries under lean-
electrolyte conditions. Specifically, high sulfur utilizations of
96% (1600 mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) are reached in
electrolytes as lean as E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S coin cells,
respetively, even paired against a high-sulfur-loading cathode
(5 mg cm−2). Furthermore, a Li-S pouch cell (with dimensions
of 5.0 cm × 6.0 cm, sulfur loading of 7 mg cm−2, and 1×
lithium excess) was constructed with E/S = 1 μL mg−1 and it
turns out that the actual energy density of the Li-S battery can
reach 589 Wh kg−1, doubling that of state-of-the-art LIBs.
