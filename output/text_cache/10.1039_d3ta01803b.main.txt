Two birds with one stone: engineering siloxane-
based electrolytes for high-performance lithium–
sulfur polyacrylonitrile batteries†

Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has attracted considerable interest for high-energy lithium–sulfur
batteries owing to its high speciﬁc capacity and remarkable cyclic stability. However, common
electrolytes fail to stabilize both the lithium (Li) metal anode and SPAN cathode, thus aﬀecting their
practical applications. Herein, a lightweight siloxane solvent-based electrolyte (below 1 g mL−1)
comprising 1.5 M lithium bis(ﬂuorosuﬂonyl) imide (LiFSI) in dimethyldimethoxysilane (DMMS) has been
designed for linking metallic Li and high-capacity SPAN cathode simultaneously. The weakly solvating
DMMS electrolyte with a moderate salt concentration of 1.5 M not only allows a remarkable Li stripping/
plating CE of 99.6% but also retards the dissolution of polysulﬁdes. As a result, the assembled Li‖SPAN
cells delivered a superior cyclic stability of 513 mA h g−1 at 0.2 A g−1 with 96.9% capacity retention after
300 cycles. Depth-proﬁling X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy results conﬁrm the generation of an
inorganic-rich inner layer in electrode–electrolyte interphases with trace DMMS decomposition in the
outer layer, thus enabling uniform Li deposition. Density functional theory result suggests weak aﬃnity
between the DMMS solvent and polysulﬁdes, which minimizes polysulﬁde shuttling. This study provides
a new avenue to explore novel solvents to simultaneously stabilize metallic Li and SPAN cathodes.
1.
Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have witnessed huge success in
portable electronics, electric vehicles, and grid storage appli-
cations.1 LIBs, consisting of a lithium transition-metal phos-
phate or oxide cathode and a graphite anode, are approaching
their ceiling in energy density (∼300 W h kg−1) and lagging
behind the surging demands.2 To pursue higher energy density,
aggressive chemistries, such as lithium–sulfur (Li–S) and
lithium–oxygen, comprising a lithium metal anode (LMA) with
a high-capacity cathode are under extensive exploration.3–7
Thus, Li–S batteries (LSBs) have emerged as one of the most
promising candidates owing to their ultrahigh theoretical
energy densities of 2600 W h kg−1 and the abundance, low cost,
and environmental friendliness of sulfur.8–10
Although LSBs show great promise, their wide deployment
has been retarded by several intractable challenges. First, the
poor electronic conductivity of sulfur and lithium suldes and

Recently, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has attracted
intense interest as an attractive sulfur cathode owing to its high
electronic conductivity, facile scaling-up preparation, and low
production cost.29,30 It is widely accepted that the active sulfur in
SPAN is atomically dispersed and covalently bonded to the
skeleton of pyrolyzed PAN chains.31 As a result, Li‖SPAN full
cells delivered remarkable cyclic stability with impressive CEs
close to 100% in conventional carbonate electrolytes due to the
solid–solid conversion mechanism with negligible polysulde
dissolution.32,33 However, it is noteworthy that the excessive use
of Li articially prolongs battery lifespan. The poor Li revers-
ibility (<95%) and severe Li dendrite growth in conventional
carbonate electrolytes lead to potential safety hazards, which
should not be ignored (Fig. 1a).34 Although the addition of u-
oroethylene carbonate (FEC) to the conventional carbonate
electrolytes can improve the plating/stripping eﬃciency to some
extent, the reversibility of LMA is still unsatisfactory.35 Ether
electrolytes are well-known to be more compatible with LMAs in
comparison with carbonate electrolytes; however, SPAN cath-
odes suﬀer from polysulde dissolution in routine ether-based
electrolytes, thus degrading their cyclic stability (Fig. 1b).5,25
Therefore, novel electrolytes that can inhibit polysulde shut-
tling and promote uniform and reversible Li deposition/
dissolution are needed for practical Li‖SPAN batteries.
The interfacial chemistry in LSBs is of paramount impor-
tance to battery performance, which is mainly governed by the
solvation structure of electrolytes.3 The competitive coordina-
tion of solvents and anions with Li-ions would trigger distinct
electrode–electrolyte interphases (EEIs). In a conventional
dilute electrolyte (∼1.0 M), the strongly solvating solvents
coordinate with Li-ions associated with the exclusion of most
anions in the primary solvation sheath.36 Such a solvation
structure leads to a solvent-derived EEI, which hardly supports
the long-cycling of LMA and sulfur cathode. In contrast,
increasing the concentration of Li salt in solvents favors an
anion-derived EEI that is inorganic-abundant and mechanically
robust.2 The decreased free solvent and increased viscosity of
electrolytes slow down the shuttling of polysuldes, thus pro-
longing the lifespan of LSBs. Following this rationale, a satu-
rated lithium bis(triuoromethane sulfonyl) imide/diglyme (1 :
0.8 in molar ratio) electrolyte has been reported to improve the
cyclic stability of LSBs by retarding the shuttling of polysuldes
and promoting uniform Li plating/stripping.37 To reduce the
high viscosity and sluggish Li+ transport in high-concentration
electrolytes, non-solvating co-solvents (e.g., hydrouoroethers),
serving as a diluent, have been used to mitigate the aforemen-
tioned deciencies without compromising the high compati-
bility toward LMAs and sulfur cathodes.38 However, most high-
concentration and localized high-concentration electrolytes
inherit a relatively high density (>1.4 g mL−1) and are of high
cost, which substantially hinders their practical applications in
high-energy-density LSBs.39 Therefore, an ideal electrolyte for
practical LSBs should be highly stable against electrodes,
prevent polysulde dissolution, and possess a high ionic
conductivity and low density.
Herein, we propose a lightweight siloxane solvent-based
electrolyte, consisting of 1.5 M lithium diuorosulfonimide
(LiFSI) dissolved in dimethyldimethoxysilane (DMMS), for
ultrastable Li–SPAN batteries. The weakly solvating power of
DMMS solvent results in weak Li+–solvent interactions, forming
a highly ion-paired solvation structure at a moderate concen-
tration of 1.5 M, which promotes a more complete decompo-
sition of FSI−and consequently generates an inorganic-rich SEI.
In addition, the low solvating power of DMMS inherently
prevents the dissolution of polysuldes, thus taming the long-

2.
Results and discussion
2.1
Physicochemical properties and solvation structure of
DMMS-based electrolytes
The
physicochemical
characteristics
of
electrolytes
have
a signicant impact on battery performance, which can be
tailored by lithium salt concentration.16,40 The ionic conduc-
tivity of electrolytes is a critical parameter that determines
battery performance, which can be measured by electro-
chemical impedance spectra. As shown in Fig. 2a, 0.5 M LiFSI
DMMS electrolyte shows an inferior ionic conductivity of 0.21
mS cm−1. When the salt concentration is increased to 1.5 M, the
ionic conductivity can be improved to 1.29 mS cm−1, a value six
times higher than that of the 0.5 M counterpart. Further
increasing the salt concentration to 3.0 M leads to a decrease in
ionic conductivity, which can be attributed to increased
viscosity in high-concentration electrolytes.41 We also measured
the ionic conductivities of 1.5 M LiFSI dimethoxyethane (DME)
and 1.0 M LiPF6 EC/DMC electrolytes, which are 2.80 and 2.60
mS cm−1, respectively; the corresponding Nyquist plots are
shown in Fig. S1a.† The relatively lower ionic conductivity of
DMMS electrolytes can be ascribed to the smaller dielectric
constant of DMMS (3 = 3.7) as compared with that of EC (3 =
89.8) and DME (3 = 7.0), thus resulting in the existence of many
undissociated ion pairs in DMMS electrolytes.42 In addition, the
ionic conductivities of various electrolytes ranging from −40 to
40 °C were also examined (Fig. 2b and S1b–d†). The DME-based
electrolyte maintains higher ionic conductivities than the
DMMS counterpart at all measured temperatures, while the
ionic conductivity of 1 M LiPF6 EC/DMC electrolyte decreases
dramatically with a decrease in the temperature and is even
lower than that of 1.5 M LiFSI DMMS at a temperature below
−30 °C. The density of diﬀerent electrolytes has a signicant
inuence on the energy density of LSBs, which is obtained by
measuring the mass of a determined volume of an electrolyte.
As shown in Fig. 2c, the commercial carbonate electrolyte (i.e.,
1.0 M LiPF6 EC/DMC) exhibits a high density of ∼1.27 g mL−1,
which is the highest among the tested electrolytes. Owing to the
low density of DMMS (0.865 g cm−3), the 1.5 M LiFSI DMMS
electrolyte has a density of only ∼0.99 g mL−1, which is close to
that of 1.5 M LiFSI DME (∼1.02 g mL−1). Notably, high-
concentration and localized high-concentration electrolytes
usually have a much higher density of >1.4 g mL−1, which would
greatly compromise the energy density of LSBs.39
Furthermore, the stability of diﬀerent solvents (DME, DME/
dioxolane (DOL, v/v = 1/1), and DMMS) against Li was investi-
gated. As shown in Fig. S2,† LMAs remain almost unchanged in
the aforementioned solvents, which suggests that DMMS can be
a potential solvent for Li‖SPAN batteries. Polysulde dissolution
tests were also conducted by mixing stoichiometric ratios of Li2S
and

The solvation structure of DMMS-based electrolytes was
studied via Raman measurements (Fig. 2f). Both the Si–O
(∼617 cm−1) peak and the S–N–S bending peak of the FSI−
anions show a slight blue shi(<10 cm−1) with the increase in
salt concentration in DMMS electrolytes. The above features
indicate that the major solvation features in DMMS electrolytes
vary slightly, which is substantially diﬀerent from the conven-
tional HCEs and LCHEs. For comparison, the peak shiof the
FSI−anions (located at ∼730 cm−1) is more evident when DME
is used, which implies the weak interaction of Li+/FSI−in
solvent-separated ion pairs (Fig. S4†).43 The poor polarity of
DMMS solvent is unfavorable for dissociating LiFSI completely,
which generates a large amount of contact ion pairs in the
electrolyte. Therefore, a high degree of Li-anion association will
remarkably promote the onset of the reduction potential of Li–
anion complex, contributing to the anion-derived EEIs. As
a result, the anion-derived EEIs are capable of stabilizing both
LMA and SPAN cathode.2

against Li metal anodes

DMMS-based
electrolytes

The electrochemical window of 1.5 M LiFSI DMMS was
investigated via linear sweep voltammetry, which demonstrates
an oxidation potential of ∼4.5 V that is suitable for Li‖SPAN
batteries (Fig. S7†). It is known that the Li transference number
(tLi+) is a critical parameter that determines the actual Li+
conductivity in the electrolyte. As shown in Fig. S8,† the tLi+ of
1.5 M LiFSI DMMS and 1.5 M LiFSI DME are 0.50 and 0.43,
respectively, which is much higher than that of the conventional
carbonate electrolyte (about 0.2–0.3). The high tLi+ is benecial
for alleviating the Li+ concentration gradient at the interface,
thus directing uniform Li deposition.
Furthermore, the morphology of Li metal deposition on Cu
foil was studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As
displayed in Fig. 3d, with the 1.0 M LiPF6 EC/DMC electrolyte,
the Li deposit exhibits a loose and disconnected structure with
some needle-like dendrites. The inset optical image shows the
inhomogeneous
deposition
of
Li
metal.
This
porous
morphology will lead to severe interfacial reactions, resulting in
incessant electrolyte consumption and Li loss.44 The Li depo-
sition in DME electrolyte displays a relatively dense morphology
with mossy Li, and many pores can still be observed (Fig. 3e). In
sharp contrast, a smooth and uniform Li deposition with
a compact structure was obtained in the 1.5 M LiFSI DMMS
electrolyte (Fig. 3f), originating from its robust SEI.
2.3
Electrochemical performance of Li‖SPAN batteries using
DMMS-based electrolytes
Before the discussion on the electrochemical performance of
Li‖SPAN batteries, the microstructure and chemical structure of
SPAN were characterized. The SEM (Fig. S9a†) and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. S9b†) show that the
SPAN has a granular shape with a size of ∼100 nm. Moreover,
the TEM and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectros-
copy (EDS) elemental mapping further conrm the uniform
distribution of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur elements in the
SPAN composite (Fig. S9c–f†). As shown in Fig. S10a,† SPAN
only exhibits a broad peak at ∼25° without the typical peaks of
sulfur, which indicates that molecular sulfur is well dispersed
in the pyrolyzed PAN skeleton. Raman results further conrm
the formation of C–S bonds, as proven by the existence of two
characteristic peaks at 308 and 384 cm−1 (Fig. S10b†).45 In
addition, a peak centered at 921 cm−1 can be identied as the
S–S bond, and two characteristic peaks at 1377 cm−1 (D-band)
and 1550 cm−1 (G-band) suggest that the amorphous carbon
structure in pyrolyzed PAN. Based on the elemental analysis
(Table S1†), the SPAN contains ∼37.8 wt% S.
The electrochemical performance of SPAN cathodes in
diﬀerent electrolytes was measured at a current density of
0.2 A g−1 aer three activation cycles at 0.1 A g−1 (Fig. 4a). A high
charge capacity of ∼530.0 mA h g−1 was achieved in the 1.5 M
LiFSI DMMS electrolyte, which corresponds to a sulfur capacity
of ∼1400.0 mA h g−1. Notably, the current densities and specic
capacities were calculated based on the mass of SPAN unless
otherwise specied. Aer 300 cycles, the Li‖SPAN battery still
maintains a high capacity of 513.5 mA h g−1, accompanied by
a capacity retention of ∼96.9% for 300 cycles. It should be
mentioned that the Li‖SPAN batteries delivered a high energy
density of ∼1000 W h kg−1 at 0.2 A g−1, outperforming the
conventional oxide cathodes and lithium iron phosphate. Since
the low sulfur content in conventional SPAN discounts the total
energy density of batteries, PAN with a higher molecular weight
can be used as the precursor to increase the sulfur content in
the SPAN composite.46 In addition, embedding microporous
carbon polyhedrons in PAN nanobers can also improve the
sulfur content in the composite.47 The cyclic performance of the
Li‖SPAN cell using 3.0 M LiFSI DMMS was also measured. As
shown in Fig. S11,† a high charge capacity of 437.2 mA h g−1 can
be achieved aer 300 cycles. The slightly lower capacity in 3.0 M
LiFSI DMMS can be assigned to its lower ionic conductivity. In
contrast, only 59.5% of the capacity remains in 1.5 M LiFSI DME
electrolytes aer 100 cycles, originating from the severe poly-
sulde shuttling. The Li‖SPAN cell using DMMS electrolyte
exhibits stable charge/discharge proles (Fig. 4b), which
conrm the excellent stability of the electrolyte with both LMA
and SPAN cathode. For comparison, the polarization of Li‖SPAN
cell in 1.5 M LiFSI DME and 1.0 M LiPF6 EC/DMC electrolytes is
much more extensive, which can be attributed to the poor
stability of EEIs (Fig. 4c and d).
The Nyquist plots of Li‖SPAN batteries using 1.5 M LiFSI
DMMS electrolyte are tted with an equivalent circuit, as dis-
played in Fig. S12.† Before cycling, the charge transfer resis-
tance (Rct) was ∼165.4 U, and it gradually decreased to ∼73.1 U
and ∼42.7 U aer three and ten cycles, respectively, indicating
the stepwise activation of SPAN, which is consistent with the
cyclic voltammetry results (Fig. S13†). Moreover, the rate
performance of Li‖SPAN cells using diﬀerent electrolytes is
compared in Fig. 4e. The assembled Li‖SPAN cell with 1.5 M
LiFSI DMMS delivered specic capacities of ∼528, ∼500, ∼463,
∼437, and ∼397 mA h g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 A g−1,
respectively (Fig. 4f). Notably, the Li‖SPAN cell operated steadily
in 1.0 M LiPF6 EC/DMC when excess Li was used, delivering
a slightly lower capacity of ∼362 mA h g−1 at 2.0 A g−1. In
contrast, the Li‖SPAN cell with 1.5 M LiFSI DME electrolyte

example, the intensities of C–H/C–C (C 1s) and C–O (O 1s)
components in Fig. 5b are much stronger than that in Fig. 5a,
while the intensities of LiF and NSO2F peaks are weaker. The
above results suggest that more organic species are formed in
DME-based electrolytes, originating from the stronger polarity
of DME. As discussed, the weakly solvating power of DMMS
would trigger the formation of contact-ion pairs and aggregates,
thus promoting a more extensive decomposition of FSI−anions.
Furthermore, the chemistries at the cathode-electrolyte
interphases (CEIs) on the SPAN cathode were identied using
depth-proling XPS (Fig. 6). As shown in the C 1s spectra, the
CEIs contain similar components, including C–C/C–H (284.8
eV), C–O/C–N (286.5 eV), and C]O (289.3 eV) in two-electrolyte
systems. Aer Ar+ etching, the intensity of C–C/C–H decreases
sharply and more inorganic species are found when 1.5 M LiFSI
DMMS is used, which indicates a less prominent decomposi-
tion of the solvent during cycling, as evidenced by the weak
intensity of the Si 2p spectra (Fig. S15†). Meanwhile, certain
diﬀerences could be observed in the N 1s, F 1s, and S 2p spectra
(Fig. 6a, b and S13†). For example, the intensities of –NSO2F
(399.9 eV), –NSO2 (398.7 eV), LiF (685.2 eV), sulfate, and sulte
in the CEI derived from FSI−anions are higher.50 The above
results suggest that the use of a weakly solvating solvent, such
as DMMS, results in a more complete decomposition of FSI−on
the SPAN surface, which could stabilize the cathode-electrolyte
interface. To conrm this, the SEM images of SPAN cathodes
aer 10 cycles were investigated (Fig. 6c–e). The SPAN cathode
shows many cracks in the DME-based electrolyte, which may
lead to rapid polysulde dissolution. For comparison, the
integrity of SPAN cathodes in carbonate and DMMS-based
electrolytes is much better, and almost no cracks were found
in the latter, which can explain the excellent electrochemical
performance of Li‖SPAN in 1.5 M LiFSI DMMS electrolyte.
3.
Conclusion
In conclusion, we have reported a lightweight DMMS-based
electrolyte that is compatible with both LMA and SPAN
cathode to prolong the cycle life of Li‖SPAN batteries. The 1.5 M
LiFSI DMMS electrolyte triggers the formation of an inorganic-
rich SEI on LMA, originating from the weakly solvating features
of DMMS solvent. The anion-derived, inorganic-rich SEI enables
