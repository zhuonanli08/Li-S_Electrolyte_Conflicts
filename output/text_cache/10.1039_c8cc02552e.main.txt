A new ether-based electrolyte for lithium sulfur
batteries using a S@pPAN cathode†
Jingjing Zhou,a Yongsheng Guo,b Chengdu Liang,b Lujie Cao,c Hui Pan,
c
Jun Yang
*a and Jiulin Wang
*a
A new electrolyte composed of 4 M LiFSI in dibutyl ether (DBE) is
proposed for Li–S batteries. Dissolution of lithium polysulfides is
definitely inhibited by DBE. More impressively, the electrolyte
ensures a high Coulombic efficiency for Li deposition/stripping
(B99.2%) without dendrite growth. Enhanced cycling stability is
demonstrated when coupled with a sulfurized poly(acrylonitrile)
(S@pPAN) cathode. This electrolyte offers new insight into electro-
lyte design for high performance Li–S batteries.
With the soaring development of electronic devices, electric
vehicles and renewable energy applications, there is an urgent
need for advanced electrochemical energy storage systems.
Lithium sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been regarded as promising
candidates
due
to
their
high theoretical
energy density
(B2600 W h kg1).1 Nevertheless, the application of Li–S
batteries is hindered by specific difficulties. One major issue
stems from the adverse dissolution of lithium polysulfides
(Li2Sn, 4 o n r 8) into ether-based electrolytes.2 The dissolved
polysulfides (PS) spontaneously migrate to the Li anode and
trigger unfavorable side reactions as well as the ‘‘shuttling
effect’’, resulting in capacity fading during cycling and severe
self-discharge.2b In addition, the limited Coulombic efficiency
(CE) of Li deposition/stripping, dendrite formation and the
unstable Li/electrolyte interfaces of Li metal anodes are also
detrimental to Li–S batteries.1b Great efforts have been devoted
to electrolyte research since electrolytes are immediately
responsible for the above issues. In particular, the solvent is
crucial. Unfortunately, PS are known to react with most of the
common electrolyte solvents, such as esters, carbonates, and
phosphates.3 It seems that ether solvents show better compatibility
but the dissolution of PS is still unavoidable. In solvents like
dimethyl sulfoxide or tetrahydrofuran, the concentration of
dissolved PS can exceed 10 M.4 Until now, a 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)/dimethyl ether (DME) (1:1, v/v) mixture has been considered
as an eclectic choice for both sulfur cathodes and Li anodes. Even
so, LiNO3 is indispensable to suppress the redox shuttle of the
dissolved PS on Li anodes. However, since the dissolution of PS
cannot be inhibited radically, the long-term cycling stability is
still problematic, let alone the continuous consumption and the
potential safety hazard of LiNO3.
Although concentrated electrolytes show reduced dissolution
and shuttling of PS due to a limited solvent amount, high viscosity
and the common ion eﬀect,5 the frequently used solvents (e.g.
DME, DEGDME and DOL) cannot eﬀectively prohibit the dis-
solution of PS, even using 5 M LiTFSI dissolved in DME/DOL.6
Meanwhile, concentrated electrolytes are proven to be eﬀective
for suppressing Li dendrite growth on Li anodes.7 Zhang et al.
reported that an electrolyte of 4 M LiFSI/DME enables high CE
(up to 99.1%) without dendrite growth.7a But its application in
Li–S batteries is not yet known.
A sulfurized poly(acrylonitrile) (S@pPAN) composite has
demonstrated stable electrochemical reversibility in carbonate-
based electrolytes.8 But the cycling stability of Li–S batteries
using carbonate-based electrolytes is greatly limited by the poor
performance of Li anodes. Although ether-based electrolytes are
more compatible with Li metal anodes,9 good cycle stability has
not been achieved for S@pPAN cathodes in these electrolytes.
Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop an electrolyte system
which not only prevents the Li anode from continuous side
reactions but also inhibits the dissolution of PS from the
cathode. Inspired by the fact that both the solubility of PS and
the reactivity to metallic lithium decrease with decreasing
molecular polarity of ether solvents,5a,7a dibutyl ether (DBE) with
a relatively low dielectric constant (3.06 at 25 1C), low saturated
vapor pressure (0.63 kPa at 20 1C) and viscosity (0.74 mPa s at
15 1C) is prudently selected as the solvent. LiFSI is adopted due
to its sufficient disassociation and good compatibility with Li
metal.10 Thus, an ether-based electrolyte of 4 M LiFSI/DBE is

Fig. 1a shows the distinct dissolvability diﬀerence of Li2S6 in
DBE and typical ether solvents (DME, DME/DOL and TEGDME)
used in Li–S batteries. The colorless and transparent supernatant
was almost identical to pure DBE, and the yellow sediments
maintained the mixture state of Li2S and sulfur. In contrast, the
color of the three control groups immediately turned to dark red-
brown after stirring. These phenomena indicate the definite
dissolution inhibition of PS in DBE. More importantly, it stays
colorless after 53 days, which further proves the effectiveness of
DBE to resist the dissolution and reaction of PS. The ability
of DBE to inhibit PS dissolution is also superior to that of the
DOL/bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl)ether (BTFE) mixture11 under the
same test conditions. The ionic conductivity (s) and viscosity (Z)
of LiFSI/DBE electrolytes with different salt concentrations are
shown in Table S1 (ESI†), and the viscosity increases obviously
with an increase in salt concentration. The mediocre ionic
conductivity (0.31 mS cm1) of 4 M LiFSI/DBE is due to the
relatively high viscosity (63.3 cP) and low dielectric constant of
DBE. It remains superior to most all-solid-state inorganic or
polymer electrolytes.12
The electrochemical window test in Fig. 1b shows that the
onset of oxidation shifts from 3.9 V to 4.4 V when increasing the
salt from 1 M to 4 M LiFSI in DBE, indicating the improved
anodic stability as revealed for concentrated electrolytes.7b,13
This is enough for Li–S batteries and may be applicable to other
cathodes with higher potentials. Fig. 1c shows the CE and
voltage profiles of the Cu8Li cells with LiFSI/DBE electrolytes
containing different salt concentrations at a current density of
0.5 mA cm2. The CE increases from 67% to 92% and then
drops to less than 20% within 20 cycles in 1 M LiFSI/DBE. The
solvent is more prone to react with deposited Li metal in a
diluted electrolyte and this leads to a lower CE.7a,13 In contrast,
a high CE of 97.5% is achieved in the initial cycles for 4 M
LiFSI/DBE, which rises up to B99.2% after 60 cycles. This is
comparable to or advantageous over the results reported recently
for concentrated electrolytes (Table S2, ESI†). Reduced voltage
hysteresis (Fig. 1c inset) with an increased salt concentration
further verifies the superiority of 4 M LiFSI/DBE. In concentrated
electrolytes, the solvent molecules are strongly coordinated with
Li+. Hence, side reactions between the solvent and Li are strongly
suppressed and a higher CE and good cyclability can be
achieved.6,14 Additionally, metallic Li deposited on Cu was char-
acterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Smooth and
compact granules in sizes around 10 mm (Fig. 1d) are obtained in
4 M LiFSI/DBE, which are similar to those in 4 M LiFSI/DME.7a
The dendrite-free morphology indicates the favorable deposition
mechanism arising from a homogeneous Li+ flux and a beneficial
SEI in concentrated electrolytes.14a It should be noted that
although Li deposits in 1–3 M electrolytes are analogous to that
in 4 M, the morphologies are looser especially in the 1 M
electrolyte (Fig. S1, ESI†). In general, more Li+ ions are available
in a given volume of the higher concentration electrolyte to
maintain a given Li+ flux for Li metal deposition, which could
suppress the formation of ion concentration gradients and
thereby enhance the CE and morphological evenness of the Li
anode.15 Moreover, the beneficial SEI prevents further corrosion
of the Li metal as revealed in other LiFSI-based electrolytes.7a,16
The molecular orbital energies of DBE and LiFSI were
calculated by density functional theory (DFT) to further under-
stand the SEI formation process at the molecular level. Fig. 2a
shows the energy values of the lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital
(HOMO). LiFSI with lower LUMO energy (1.61 eV) is reduced
more easily than DBE (1.15 eV) on the surface of Li metal. The
preferential reduction of FSI by Li metal is dramatically
enhanced when the salt concentration increases to 4 M for a
low molar ratio of DBE/LiFSI (1.47/1). A LiF enriched SEI film is
formed on the deposited Li surface with the 4 M LiFSI/DBE
electrolyte as demonstrated by the XPS results (Fig. 3b and c).

LiF is considered to play a critical role in increasing the CE and
suppressing Li dendrite formation.13,14b,17 The lower activity of
DBE also decreases the reactivity between the Li metal and the
electrolyte.14a Meanwhile, the small surface area of the granule-
like Li deposition could minimize the side reaction between the
Li metal and the electrolyte and improve the electrochemical
reversibility. Cycling of the Li8Li cell with 4 M LiFSI/DBE was
conducted at 0.5 mA cm2 (Fig. S2, ESI†). It lasts more than 750 h
with a stable over voltage (B120 mV), which further proves the
good interfacial compatibility.
The electrochemical performance of the Li–S cell using the
proposed electrolyte and the S@pPAN composite (S content:
46 wt%) cathode is further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3a, the
cycling stability is significantly enhanced by increasing the salt
concentration from 1 to 4 M. The cell with 4 M LiFSI/DBE shows
a high specific capacity of 1403 mA h g1 (based on sulfur
weight) with an excellent capacity retention of 97.0% after
150 cycles at 1.67 A g1. Additionally, the CE is maintained at
almost 100% except for the 1st cycle (81.6%). Fig. 3b shows that
the use of the 4 M electrolyte leads to the highest capacity and
minimal polarization. It is well known that the salt concentration
in electrolytes influences the ionic coordination structure. In the
4 M electrolyte, FSI is apt to form contact ion pairs (CIPs) and
aggregates (AGGs), resulting in the special physicochemical
properties and reaction kinetics of concentrated electrolytes.14a,18
We speculate that the enhanced performance arises from several
possible factors: (i) faster ionic transport in the 4 M solution than
that in a dilute one due to the different Li+ conduction mecha-
nism;18 (ii) a high coefficient of electrochemical reactions due to a
high interfacial Li+ concentration; (iii) a favourable SEI especially
on the Li anode to facilitate Li transport and suppress Li corrosion.
To gain insight into the solvent compatibility with the
S@pPAN cathode, electrolytes of 1 M LiFSI dissolved in commonly
used linear DME, cyclic DOL and the mixture (DOL/DME) were
compared with a DBE-based electrolyte. Fast capacity decay
(Fig. 3c) and a serious shuttling eﬀect (Fig. 3d) were observed
for the DME solvent, which were ascribed to the high dissolvability
of PS in this solvent (Fig. 1a). The cell with DOL solvent shows
normal CE but the capacity is rather low and drops extremely fast.
Pure DOL is reported to decrease the sulfur utilization5a and has
the tendency to self-polymerize10a which might cause the unexpected
increase in viscosity and finally lead to battery failure. Although DOL
helps to alleviative the shuttling eﬀect due to the reduced dissolution
of PS,5a the performance is still poor for the DME/DOL mixture. The
discharge/charge profiles in Fig. S3 (ESI†) clearly demonstrate the
big distinctions of cycle reversibility in these electrolytes. In order to
make it more clear, cells cycled for 50 times in 1 M LiFSI/DME–DOL
(1:1, v/v) and 4 M LiFSI/DBE were disassembled to check the Li
anodes and separators. The compact and smooth Li surface
(Fig. S4b, ESI†) reflects the reduced side reactions and favorable
Li/electrolyte interface in 4 M LiFSI/DBE, compared with the
cracked and loose Li (Fig. S4a, ESI†) cycled in 1 M LiFSI/DME–
DOL (1 : 1, v/v). Moreover, dark yellow precipitate is clearly
observed on the separator that had been immersed in 1 M
LiFSI/DME–DOL (1 : 1, v/v) (Fig. S4a, inset, ESI†). This precipitate
belongs to the dissolved lithium polysulfide migrating from the
S@pPAN cathode. Its color instantly turned to dark blue when
exposed to air because of the rapid oxidation. This observation
confirms the serious dissolution and shuttling effect of PS
(Fig. 3d). In contrast, the separator from 4 M LiFSI/DBE is nearly
identical to the fresh state (Fig. S4b, inset, ESI†), indicating the
sufficient inhibition of PS by DBE just like the result shown in
Fig. 1d. Moreover, the elemental content of sulfur detected on the
Li anode in 1 M LiFSI/DME–DOL (11.57 at%, Fig. S4c, ESI†) is
much higher than that in 4 M LiFSI/DBE (2.65 at%, Fig. S4d,
ESI†). The detected sulfur on the Li anode might be related to the
adsorbed sulfides and the SEI components. LiNO3 has been
widely used as an additive in Li–S batteries to suppress the
shuttling effect and boost the performance. The cell with the
1 M LiFSI/DME–DOL + 2 wt% LiNO3 electrolyte shows good
stability at the initial cycling stage, but declines rapidly after
30 cycles (Fig. S5, ESI†).
In view of its excellent compatibility with Li anodes as
reported,7a concentrated 4 M LiFSI/DME was further examined
for the Li8S@pPAN cell. As shown in Fig. 4a, although the CE of
the cell increases from 94.6% (2nd) to ca. 99% after 50 cycles,
the capacity decays more than 40% within 80 cycles. In addition, the
polarization of the discharge/charge profiles increases significantly
(Fig. S6a, ESI†), which is possibly related to the accumulation of
passivation products (e.g. insoluble Li2S) on the electrodes6 and the
increasing viscosity of the electrolyte caused by the dissolution of PS.
By comparison, the very similar discharge/charge profiles (Fig. 4b)
indicate high electrochemical reversibility in 4 M LiFSI/DBE. So far,
the Li8S@pPAN cell using the commercial electrolyte of 1 M LiPF6/
EC-DMC has been intensively investigated. It is obvious that the cell
with our proposed electrolyte exhibits better cycling stability than
the above one (80.9% retention after 150 cycles). Its discharge/
charge profiles are shown in Fig. S6b (ESI†).

electrolytes. Capacities of 1570, 1500, 1455 and 1356 mA h g1
are obtained respectively at 0.33, 0.84, 1.67, and 3.34 A g1 for

4 M LiFSI/DBE, which are similar to those of the commercial
carbonated-based electrolyte. As for 4 M LiFSI/DME, only a
capacity of less than 700 mA h g1 is retained at 3.34 A g1
compared with 1532 mA h g1 at 0.33 A g1. It is believed that
the parasitic products formed on both electrodes and the
increased electrolyte viscosity, all deriving from the inevitably
dissolved PS, might lead to the poor cell kinetics. EIS plots of
the cells with diﬀerent electrolytes are shown in Fig. S7 (ESI†).
The semicircle at high frequency is mainly related to the charge
transfer resistance (Rct) between the electrodes and the electro-
lyte. The cell cycled in 4 M LiFSI/DME shows a much larger Rct
than those in the other two electrolytes, which accounts for its
inferior rate performance (Fig. 4c). Another disadvantage of PS
dissolution is the severe self-discharge, which occurs in most of
the Li–S cells with elemental sulfur-based cathodes.19 Impress-
ively, the Li8S@pPAN cell using the 4 M LiFSI/DBE electrolyte
possesses an outstanding storage performance, like those using
carbonate-based electrolytes. The cell discharged to 1.7 V was
stored at room temperature for 5 days. The constant open-circuit
voltage during storage (Fig. S8, ESI†) and the almost identical
discharge profiles (Fig. 4d) indicate negligible self-discharge
behavior.
In summary, a concentrated electrolyte of 4 M LiFSI/DBE is
proposed for Li–S batteries. The dissolution of lithium poly-
sulfides is definitely inhibited by the DBE solvent. Moreover,
the compact and dendrite-free Li deposit with a high Coulombic
efficiency for Li deposition/stripping is obtained. As a result,
the Li8S@pPAN cell demonstrates superior cycling and rate
performance. This work offers an approach to develop advanced
high energy Li–S batteries.
