A soluble single atom catalyst promotes lithium
polysulfide conversion in lithium sulfur batteries†
Zhenpu Shi,abc Lan Wang,d Huifang Xu,abc Junqiang Wei,abc Hongyun Yue,*abc
Hongyu Dong,abc Yanhong Yinabc and Shuting Yang
*abc
CoPcCl is used as a catalytic electrolyte additive for lithium sulfur
batteries under the guidance of theoretical calculations. The electrolyte
additive strategy is easier to realize and more eﬀective compared with
the fabrication of catalytic host materials. Adding CoPcCl in the electro-
lyte enhanced the sulfur utilization remarkably.
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have aroused considerable
interest because of the high specific capacity (1672 mA h g1
in theoretical), low cost and nontoxicity of elemental S.1,2
However, the insulative nature of S and its discharge products,
the large volume expansion after lithiation, and the so called
‘‘shuttling effect’’ of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) have hindered
the commercialization of Li–S batteries.3,4 Soluble LiPSs tend to
shuttle to the anode side and react with metal lithium. The
product of Li2S is insoluble and deposited on the anode. The
run off of active materials and the shuttling of LiPSs during the
charging/discharging processes leads to low S utilization, low
Columbic efficiency, corrosion of the Li metal anode and rapid
capacity fading.5,6
To overcome these issues, heteroatom doped porous carbon7,8
or graphene;9,10 metal oxides,11,12 sulfides13,14 or nitrides;15,16
metal organic frameworks17,18 and MXenes19,20 have been used
as host materials for the sulfur cathode. The polar–polar absorption
between host materials and LiPSs alleviates the shuttle effects to
some extent. Unfortunately, the simple absorption ability of the
hosts cannot radically eliminate the shuttling of LiPSs. Most recently,
catalytic host materials, such as transition metal sulfides,21,22 CoP,23
Li2S@Co–N–C24 and single-atom catalysts25,26 have been reported.
Metal-free catalysts, such as framework porphyrin27 and self-healing
biological materials,28 have also been used in lithium sulfur
batteries. On the one hand, these catalytic hosts reveal strong
adsorption capacity to LiPSs and restrain the diffusion of LiPSs
in the electrolyte. On the other hand, the catalysts enhance the
redox kinetics of LiPSs. The absorption and catalysis strategy
enhances the sulfur utilization effectively and is regarded as a
promising way to address shuttling effects. Unlike the oxygen
reduction reaction, a solid - liquid - solid process occurs
during discharging with a gradual chain length decrease of
LiPSs.29 The discharge product Li2S is solid state and might be
covered on the catalyst, which prevents contacts with reactants
and causes failures of the catalyst. Recently, cobaltocene30 has
been used as a redox mediator in electrolytes to promote the
LiPSs conversion. Keeping the catalyst in the electrolyte might
be a good strategy to overcome the catalyst failure issues.
Herein, we propose a new strategy to enhance the sulfur
utilization. A kind of cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc) ramification,
cobalt hexadecachlorophthalocyanine (CoPcCl), is used as an
electrolyte additive and as a catalyst to enhance the redox
kinetics of LiPSs. The catalyst remains in the liquid state during
the whole charging/discharging processes, which avoids the
failures of catalytic activity. Density functional theory (DFT)
calculations and the experimental results have proved that
CoPcCl shows strong catalytic ability to LiPS conversions. The
catalytically inactive nature of CoPc is also explained by DFT
calculations, which can provide research approaches for other
researchers. Moreover, the electrolyte additive strategy is easier
to realize and more eﬀective compared with the fabrication of
catalytic host materials. Adding 0.1 wt% CoPcCl in the electro-
lyte enhanced the sulfur utilization remarkably.

process is the decomposition of Li2S to SLi and Li. The decom-

the catalytic ability of the soluble single atom catalyst. Fig. 1a
shows the initial state of Li2S which anchored onto CoPcCl. The
sulfur atom tends to combine with the metal atom, and the Li
atoms integrate with the N atom. After decomposition, the S
atom remains motionless. The single Li ion combines with the

N and Cl atoms. The final state of the dissociated Li2S shown in
Fig. 1c is a stable conformation. For CoPc, there are no other
adsorption sites except N atoms, as shown in Fig. 1d. Mean-
while, there is no stable conformation that can be formed for
the decomposition of Li2S. In other words, CoPc shows no
catalytic ability for Li2S dissociation. The decomposition energy
barrier of Li2S with CoPcCl is about 0.50 eV (Fig. 1e). And the
value is about 3.12 eV for the pristine Li2S. According to the
DFT calculation results, a good soluble single atom catalyst
should have enough absorption sites. Moreover, the distance
between these sites should be long enough to ensure Li–S bond
breaking.
The catalytic ability of the soluble single atom catalyst is
verified by CV tests of the symmetric cells at a scan rate of
50 mV s1 between 0.8 V and 0.8 V, as shown in Fig. 2a and b.
The current responses are negligible without the addition of
Li2S6 into the electrolytes. When Li2S6 is added into the electro-
lytes, high current response signals are recorded, which indicates
that the electrochemical redox reactions of Li2S6 occurred. More
importantly, the highest current response of STD-CoPcCl indicates
that CoPcCl significantly promotes the reaction dynamics of the
LiPS conversion. The curves of STD and STD-CoPc are coincident.
Based on these results, we can conclude that the CoPcCl additive
has the catalytic ability of LiPS conversion. And CoPc shows no
catalytic eﬀect. The experimental results are consistent with the
theoretical calculation results. Potentiostatic discharge experi-
ments have been carried out to verify the catalytic eﬀects. A Li2S6
containing electrolyte is used as the catholyte.20 A high pre-
cipitation capacity of 105.5 mA h g1 is achieved by adding
CoPcCl (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the capacity is 72.9 mA h g1
without the CoPcCl promoter (Fig. 2d). These results clearly
suggest that the presence of CoPcCl has reduced the energy
barrier of LiPSs conversion and Li2S nucleation, which accelerates
the electrochemical reaction kinetics. The LiPS conversion
promoting eﬀect of CoPcCl is further certified by the CV test
of the Li–S coin cell at different scan rates. At a scan rate of
0.04 mV s1, the second reduced peak locates at B2.03 V for
both STD and STD-CoPcCl electrolytes. When the scan rate
is varied to 1.0 mV s1, the peak shifts to B1.94 V for the
STD-CoPcCl electrolyte (Fig. 2e). For the STD electrolyte, the
value is B1.93 V (Fig. 2f). The intensive polarized voltages (DV)
are 90.2 mV and 99.9 mV with and without the addition of
CoPcCl, respectively. The smaller DV value manifests the faster
LiPS conversion kinetics in Li–S batteries.

The stability of the single atom catalyst containing electro-
lytes is also important. Fig. 3 illustrates the chemical and
electrochemical stability tests of the prepared electrolytes.
A piece of metal Li has been immersed in CoPcCl (Fig. 3a
and b) and CoPc (Fig. 3c and d) containing electrolytes and
stored for 7 days in an Ar-filled glove box. The digital graphic
photos reveal that the Li wafers remain clean and spotless after
contact with the additive containing electrolytes. Obviously, the
prepared electrolytes are stable towards metal Li. The solubility
of CoPcCl in the electrolyte is analyzed by ICP-OES measurements,
and the results are shown in Table S1 (ESI†). The calculated
concentration is about 0.419 g L1. The loss of CoPcCl after long
term cycling is acceptable when compared with the loss during
the sample preparation. Moreover, the ICP-OES results also
confirm that CoPcCl is stable during cycling. The CV curves of
the stainless steel–Li batteries in Fig. 3e show an insignificant
reduction peak at B1.8 V. Therefore, the results still indicate that
the catalyst additives may not introduce new side electrochemical
reactions in a voltage window of 1.8–2.8 V. Fig. 3f demonstrates
the LSV test results of the stainless steel–Li batteries with
different electrolytes. There are no current response signals
observed below 3.5 V for all the electrolytes. The new emerging
peaks in the additive containing electrolytes might be attributed
to the electro-oxidation of the additives.

STD-CoPc. The discharge/charge curves of the battery using the
prepared electrolytes at different current densities are shown in
Fig. 4d–f. The second discharge plateaus decrease from B2.1 V
to B2.0 V with varying current densities from 0.1C to 2C.
Apparently, the curves of STD and STD-CoPc are similar. For
STD, the discharge capacities at 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C and 2C are
1191.8, 899.2, 776.7, 719.9 and 636.6 mA h g1, respectively. And
the values for STD-CoPcCl are 1256.3, 998.7, 906.5, 863.5 and
785.0 mA h g1, respectively. Moreover, the long term cycling
and rate performance of the batteries using the three electro-
lytes are shown in Fig. 4g and h. Interestingly, both of the
cycling stability and rate performance curves of STD and STD-
CoPc are in accordance. And the values of STD-CoPcCl are
higher than those of STD and STD-CoPc. This phenomenon is
in accordance with the conclusion of the theoretical calcula-
tions and catalytic ability tests. However, CoPcCl is a promoter
of LiPS conversion, and not a blocker of LiPSs shuttling. It is
inevitable that the capacity loss of batteries using STD-CoPcCl
still exists. In addition, the content of CoPcCl is low in a battery
and the promotion is limited. Even so, CoPcCl is a good soluble
single atom catalyst and has promoted the LiPS conversion and
enhanced the sulfur utilization in a Li–S battery.
In summary, in this work, a good soluble single atom
catalyst has been designed and synthesized under the guidance
of theoretical calculations. The experimental results have con-
firmed the promoting eﬀect on the LiPS conversion of the
catalyst. Unlike the catalytic host materials, the soluble single
atom catalyst may not be covered by the solid discharge/charge
products and contacts well with LiPS in the electrolyte. Mean-
while, the electrolyte additive strategy is simple and practicable.
The electrochemical measurements have demonstrated that
CoPcCl is a good soluble single atom catalyst and has promoted
the LiPS conversion and enhanced the sulfur utilization in a
Li–S battery. Finally, the soluble single atom catalyst might
have promising application prospects in Li–O2 and Li–CO2
batteries, which undergo a gas - solid phase transformation
during the discharging process. We believe that the catalytic
electrolyte additive strategy might be of interest researchers.
