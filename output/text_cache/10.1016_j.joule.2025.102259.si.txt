1. Supplemental methods 
1.1. Electrochemical measurements 
1.1.1. Electrochemical tests on model systems 
For shuttle current and Coulombic efficiency tests, Li‒S cells were assembled in 
standard 2025-configured coin cells with 600 μm thick Li anodes (16.0 mm in diameter), 
C/S cathodes with a sulfur loading of 1.2 mgS cm−2 (13.0 mm in diameter), PP 
separators (19.0 mm in diameter), and 25 μL TTE solutions. To measure the shuttle 
currents, the cells were galvanostatically charged to 2.90 V and then discharged 
potentiostatically to 2.60, 2.45, 2.43, 2.40, 2.39, 2.38, 2.37, and 2.25 V step by step. 
The shuttle current was determined by the steady-state current at each potentiostatic 
voltage. To evaluate the Coulombic efficiency (CE), the cells were galvanostatically 
cycled at 0.1 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) between 1.7 and 2.6 V on a LAND multichannel 
battery cycler. To guarantee the accuracy and gather the data in a relatively steady state, 
the values of CE in the 2nd cycle were adopted for analysis. 
For LiPS behavior evaluation in Li2S6 symmetric cells, CNT-coated Al foil current 
collectors were prepared as the electrodes for cell assembly. Specifically, CNTs and 
PVDF with a mass ratio of 10:1 were added into NMP. The mixture was sonicated for 
2.0 h and stirred for another 2.0 h to obtain a homogeneous slurry. Then, the slurry was 
coated onto Al foils and dried at 60oC for 12.0 h. The areal mass loading of the CNTs 
was controlled to be 0.20 mg cm−2. The CNT-coated Al foil electrodes were finally 
obtained after being punched into disks with a diameter of 13.0 mm. To construct Li2S6 
symmetric cells, two identical CNT-coated Al foil electrodes were assembled in a

standard 2032 coin cell with a piece of PP separator (19.0 mm in diameter) and 40 μL 
LiTFSI- or LiFSI-based TTE solutions with Li2S6. 
To probe the characteristic kinetic parameters in homogeneous reactions, 
techniques including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) under variable 
temperatures and distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis were applied on Li2S6 
symmetric cells. Concretely, EIS was performed using the potentiostat mode at the 
open-circuit voltage on a Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation. A sinusoidal 
voltage with an amplitude of 10 mV and a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz was 
applied. A high and low temperature alternating test chamber (WGD6005) was used in 
conjunction with the Solartron electrochemical workstation to measure the impedance 
from 0°C to 10°C, 20°C, 25°C, 30°C, and 40°C, respectively. The interval time between

1.1.2. Electrochemical tests on Li‒S full cells 
For Li‒S full cell assembly and tests, sulfur/carbon (S/C) cathode was fabricated 
as follows. 60 mg CNT and 140 mg S8 were mixed in a batch and ball-milled with 
PVDF binder at a weight ratio of 9:1 in NMP to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry 
was then coated on Al foils using doctor blades and dried at 60℃ for 24 h. The areal 
sulfur loading was controlled to be 4.0 mgS cm−2 for the full-cell experiments. The S/C 
cathodes were punched into small disks with a diameter of 13.0 mm.  
The Li‒S coin cells for rate tests were assembled in standard 2032-coin cells with 
50 μm-thick Li anodes (15.0 mm in diameter), S/C cathodes with sulfur loading of 4.0 
or 7.0 mgS cm−2 (13.0 mm in diameter), PP separators (19.0 mm in diameter), and 45 
or 75 μL LiTFSI/LiFSI-based TTE electrolytes for the cells with 4.0 or 7.0 mgS cm−2 
cathodes, respectively. For galvanostatic charge‒discharge tests, the cells were 
activated at 0.05 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) for 2 cycles and then galvanostatically cycled 
at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 C between 1.7 and 2.6 V. Additionally, the charge‒discharge 
profiles were gathered from the 2nd cycle in a relatively steady state. For galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique (GITT) tests, the cells were activated for 2 cycles at 0.05 
C firstly and then charged and discharged at 0.3/0.4/0.5 C for 4/3/2.4 min followed by 
relaxation at the open circuit for 60 min in each step. To avoid missing any tiny 
fluctuation of the voltage and capacity, relative data were recorded every 0.2 s.  
The Li–S coin cells for long-term cycling were assembled in standard 2032-coin

loading of 4.0 mgS cm−2 (13.0 mm in diameter), PP separators (19.0 mm in diameter),

cycled between 1.7−2.6 V on a LAND multichannel battery cycler under the rate of 0.3

1.2. Li–S pouch cell assembly and evaluation 
To fabricate the cathodes for pouch cells, the S/C slurry was double-sided coated 
on Al foils and then dried at 60oC for 24 h. The areal sulfur loading was around 8 mgS 
cm−2 on a single side. Quantitatively, the cathode thickness was controlled to be around 
140 μm on a single side, and the cathode porosity was measured to be around 60% using 
mercury intrusion porosimetry. 4 × 7 cm2 electrodes were cut and directly used as the 
sulfur cathodes in pouch cells. 
The anode of the Li–S pouch cell was double-sided Li metal sheets (75 μm 
thickness on a single side bought from China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) rolled on Cu 
current collectors with a size of 4 × 7 cm2. For the assembly of Li–S pouch cells, the 
cathodes and anodes were stacked layer by layer with PP membrane as the separator 
for rate tests or PE membrane as the separator for cycling tests. The electrodes were 
controlled in parallel connection by using an electrode lug terminal. The electrolyte for 
the Li–S pouch cells was 1.0 mol L−1 LiFSI or LiTFSI, and 5.0 wt % LiNO3 dissolved 
in TTE/DME mixed solvents. The E/S ratio was controlled to be 3.5 or 1.9 gele gS−1 in 
the above Ah-level pouch cells. The Li–S pouch cells were galvanostatically cycled

10 Ah-level pouch cells for cycling tests were cycled under 0.01 C after 0.005 C

2. Computational details 
2.1. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations 
MD simulations were conducted by the Large Scale Atomic/Molecular Massively 
Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) code2. Two models were constructed to simulate 
electrolyte systems with 40 vol% TTE, 60 vol% DME, 2.0 mol[S] L−1 Li2S6, and 1.0 mol 
L−1 lithium salt (LiTFSI or LiFSI), and the detailed compositions of these models are 
listed in Table S2. The force field parameters for solvents were generated by the 
LigParGen web server3 except that restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) atomic 
partial charges were assigned to each atom, which were obtained based on electrostatic 
potential charges using the Multiwfn program4.  
The initial atomic coordinates were generated with Packing Optimization for MD 
Simulations (Packmol) program5. Each system was first equilibrated in the NPT 
ensemble using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat for 2 ns to maintain a temperature of 
298 K and a pressure of 1 atm with time constants of 0.1 and 1 ps, respectively6. After 
that, the electrolytes were heated from 298 to 420 K within 2 ns, and maintained at 420 
K for 4 ns, followed by annealing from 420 to 298 K within 2 ns. Subsequently, all the 
systems were equilibrated at 298 K in the NPT ensemble for another 4 ns. A 12 ns 
production run was finally conducted in the NVT ensemble under Nose–Hoover 
thermostat7,8. Only the final 5 ns trajectory was sampled for the analysis of solvation 
structures. The solvation structures were visualized by VESTA9. 
 
2.2. Density functional theory calculations 

<<PAGE 8>>
SI7 
 
Graphene was regarded as the reaction substrate in this work. Soluble LiPS 
radicals were adsorbed on graphene, while the insoluble Li2S and Li2S2 were treated as 
crystals. All the simulations on the intrinsic kinetic model constructions were performed 
in the frame of density functional theory (DFT)10 methods using the Vienna Ab initio 
Simulation Package (VASP)11. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)12 exchange–
correlation functional within a generalized gradient approximation (GGA)13 was 
implemented. The plane-wave cut-off energy was set at 520 eV, and all the structures 
were optimized to a force tolerance less than 2×10−2 eV Å−1 and the total energy 
convergence lower than 10−5 eV. The k-point of adsorption and crystal configurations 
were set to 8 × 8 × 1 and 7 × 7 × 7, respectively, which was verified in our previous 
work14. The DFT-D3 method was used to include the van der Waals force15. A vacuum 
layer of 20 Å was adopted in the z-axis to minimize the interaction between the adjacent 
layers. The explicit solvation model was considered to describe the impact of solvation. 
The solvation model allowed two solvent molecules (DME or TTE) and one salt 
molecule (LiTFSI or LiFSI, i.e., DME+LiTFSI, DME+LiFSI, TTE+LiTFSI, or 
TTE+LiFSI) to interact with the soluble Li2S4 molecule and LiSx· radicals (x = 1, 2 or 
3) radicals on the substrate, respectively. 
The electrostatic potential calculations of the two anions (TFSI− and FSI−) were 
conducted by Gaussian (G09) program16 with Becke’s three-parameter hybrid method 
using the Lee–Yang–Parr correlation functional (B3LYP) at 6-311++G(d,p) level17. 
Frequency analysis was performed to ensure the ground state of optimized molecules. 
The binding energy (Eb) between a Li2S6–anion complex (i.e., Li2S6–TFSI− or

Li2S6–FSI−) and a solvent (i.e., DME or TTE) to generate a (Li2S6–anion)–solvent 
complex was defined as follows: 
Eb=E(Li2S6‒anion)‒solvent – (ELi2S6‒anion + Esolvent) 
where ELi2S6‒anion , Esolvent, and E(Li2S6‒anion)‒solvent represent the total energy of the 
Li2S6–anion complex, the solvent, and the (Li2S6–anion)–solvent complex, respectively. 
 
2.3. The preferential reaction pathway for DFT calculations 
Based on our previous work, the preferential pathway in this work is the Li2S4 
asymmetrical decomposition by calculating the descriptors in energy profiles18. The 
detailed elementary hypothesized heterogeneous reactions from Li2S4 to Li2S are as 
follows: 
 
Li2S4 + (Li+ + e−) + * →LiS3∙* + Li2S 
(Reaction 1) 
 
LiS3∙* + (Li+ + e−) + *→ LiS2∙* + LiS∙* 
(Reaction 2) 
 
LiS∙* + (Li+ + e−) → Li2S + * 
(Reaction 3) 
 
LiS2∙* + (Li+ + e−) + * → 2LiS∙* 
(Reaction 4) 
Therein, the * represents the reaction substrate (graphene is adopted here). In addition, 
we suppose the Li2S4 molecules are in solvated states with two solvent molecules and 
one salt molecule, LiPS radicals are in solvated states and interact with the substrate, 
while Li2S is in solid crystalline states. Considering the reaction stoichiometric numbers, 
the six-electron-transfer process from Li2S4 to Li2S can be extended to the following 
six steps based on above four elementary reactions: 
 
Li2S4 + (Li+ + e−) + * →LiS3∙* + Li2S 
(Step I)

SI9 
 
 
LiS3∙* + Li2S + (Li+ + e−) + * → LiS2∙* + LiS∙* + Li2S 
(Step II) 
 
LiS2∙* + LiS∙* + Li2S + (Li+ + e−) → LiS2∙* + 2Li2S + * 
(Step III) 
 
LiS2∙* + 2Li2S + (Li+ + e−) + * → 2LiS∙* + 2Li2S 
(Step IV) 
 
2LiS∙* + 2Li2S + (Li+ + e−) → LiS∙* + 3Li2S + * 
(Step V) 
 
LiS∙* + 3Li2S + (Li+ + e−) → 4Li2S + * 
(Step VI) 
Notably, the core reactions of Step III, V, and VI are totally identical, and the elementary 
reaction therein is the Reaction 3. Therefore, all of the Step III, IV, V, and VI can be the 
possible rate-determining steps, but we only investigate the Step III and IV but not the 
Step V and VI. 
 
2.4. Equilibrium potential calculations 
The equilibrium potential (Eeq) of Li2S4 conversion is associated with the ΔG of the 
overall reaction in the following equation: 
 
Li2S4 + 6Li+ + 6e− → 4Li2S  
 
The reference electrode was set to be the Li metal electrode. The ΔG for above 
conversion was defined using: 
 
ΔG = 4G(Li2S) − G(Li2S4) − 6G(e−) − 6G(Li+) 
 
The free energy of Li2S was obtained from its crystal structure. G(Li2S4) was 
acquired from the molecular structure. The reaction (Li+ + e−) → Li is at equilibrium in 
the condition of 0 V vs. Li/Li+ electrode. The free energy of the ion–electron pair (Li+ 
+ e−) is equal to G(Li) at 0 V vs. Li/Li+, which is similar to the method of hydrogen 

<<PAGE 11>>
SI10 
 
electrode19. Therefore, the Gibbs energy can be expressed at any electrode potentials as 
follows:  
 
G(Li+) + G(e−) = G(Li) − eU 
 
Therein, the G(Li) represents the Gibbs free energy of Li solid and U is the 
electrode potential vs. Li/Li+ electrode. Notably, the equilibrium potential Eeq equals to 
U when the value of ΔG equals to zero. 
 
2.5. Calculations of Gibbs free energy changes in elementary reactions 
The Gibbs free energies of radicals on the active sites were defined as follows: 
ΔG(LiS3∙*) = G(LiS3∙*) + G(Li2S) − G(*) − G(Li+)− G(e−) − G(Li2S4) 
 
ΔG(LiS2∙*) = G(Li2S2) + G(*) − G(LiS2∙*) − G(Li+) − G(e−) 
 
ΔG(LiS∙*) = G(LiS∙*) + G(Li2S) − G(*) − G(Li+) − G(e−) − G(Li2S2) 
where G(LiS3∙*), G(LiS2∙*), and G(LiS∙*) are the free energies of adsorbed LiS3∙, LiS2∙ 
and LiS∙ complexes. G(Li2S2) and G(Li2S) are considered in solid states. The structure 
of Li2S2 is taken from a theoretical hypothesized structure20. Following above rules, the 
Gibbs free energy changes for Reaction 1 to 4 can be expressed as follows: 
ΔG1 = ΔG(LiS3∙*) + eU 
ΔG2 = −ΔG(LiS2∙*) + ΔG(LiS∙*) − ΔG(LiS3∙*) + 2G(Li2S2) − 2G(Li+) − 2G(e−) − 
G(Li2S4) + eU 
ΔG3 = −ΔG(LiS∙*) + 2G(Li2S) − 2G(Li+) − 2G(e−) − G(Li2S2) + eU 
ΔG4 = ΔG(LiS2∙*) + 2ΔG(LiS∙*) + G(Li2S2) + 2G(Li+) + 2G(e−) − 2G(Li2S) + eU

To avoid the heavy calculation of vibrations, the Gibbs free energies of all the 
species in the above equations are approximated with total energies due to the small 
contribution of zero-point energy and the entropy change in solid-state conditions21.  
 
2.6. Overpotential calculations. 
To give a quantitative comparison, the calculated overpotential (η) is introduced to 
evaluate the intrinsic reactivity of LiPSs solvated by solvents and salts. The η is 
determined by the potential-determining step, which is the least exothermic step. Taking 
these considerations into account, η can be expressed as 
 
η = Eeq − min (ΔUi) 
 
where the ΔUi (i = 1, 2, 3, or 4) is the potential of an elementary reaction under the 
condition of 0 V vs. Li/Li+, which is defined as: 
 
ΔUi = −ΔGi / e 
 
where ΔGi is the Gibbs free energy change of an elementary reaction. Note that the ΔUi 
is a function of ΔG(LiS∙*) and ΔG(LiS2∙*). Therefore, the η can be directly reflected 
by ΔG(LiS∙*) and ΔG(LiS2∙*), which are greatly affected by the coordinating solvents.

Figure S1 | The schematic illustration of the solvation changes when altering the 
anions in electrolytes. Solvation structure evolutions of LiPSs including inner and 
outer solvation shells in routine electrolyte (DME/LiTFSI electrolyte), low-ESP 
electrolyte (TTE/DME/LiTFSI electrolyte), and anion competitive low-ESP electrolyte 
(TTE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte).  
 
Concretely, the LiPS inner solvation shell refers to the region where solvents and 
anions can directly coordinate. In converse, the LiPS outer solvation shell refers to the 
region where solvents and anions cannot directly coordinate with LiPSs. The 
coordination capability of solvents and anions follows the order of DME > FSI− > 
TFSI− > TTE if we just consider per solvent/anion. However, the specific coordination

circumstances should be judged after considering the number of these anions and 
solvents. For instance, though TFSI− seems stronger than TTE in coordination 
capability, non-negligible amount of TTE molecules can still coordinate with LiPSs 
while some TFSI− cannot enter the LiPS inner solvation shell in high-TTE-content 
electrolytes, since the molecular number of TTE is much more than that of TFSI− (e.g., 
nearly 3 times in 40% TTE electrolytes). 
In the DME/LiTFSI electrolyte, both TFSI− and DME can coordinate with LiPSs 
in the inner solvation shell. After replacing some DME with TTE solvents in electrolyte 
(denoted as the TTE/DME/LiTFSI electrolyte), the TTE molecules contend against 
TFSI− in occupying the coordination sites of LiPSs, and eventually both the TTE 
molecules and the TFSI− can partly participate in the LiPS inner solvation shell. 
Therefore, the LiPS kinetics is determined by both the complex of LiPS coordinated by 
DME and TFSI− and the complex of LiPS coordinated by DME and TTE. The above 
circumstances will be changed if totally replacing LiTFSI by LiFSI in electrolyte 
(denoted as the TTE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte). In detail, due to the stronger coordination 
capability of FSI− than TFSI−, the FSI− competition with TTE in coordinating with 
LiPSs is more advantageous than that of TFSI−. As a result, few amount of TTE can 
participate in the LiPS inner solvation shell in TTE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte, and the 
LiPS kinetics is mainly determined by the complex of LiPS coordinated by DME and 
FSI−, which is endowed with superior charge-transfer kinetics through experimental 
analysis and theoretical calculations.

In principle, the cathodic polarization can be decoupled into ohmic polarization 
(ηohm), activation polarization (ηact), and concentration polarization (ηcon). Concretely, 
ηohm represents the resistance of the IR drop ruled by Ohm’s Law; ηact represents the 
charge-transfer difficulty ruled by Bulter‒Volmer equation; and ηcon represents the 
mass-transfer difficulty ruled by Nernst‒Plank equation (Figure S9a).

In order to accurately distinguish them from the overall polarization, a 
galvanostatic intermittent titration technique combining electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (GITT‒EIS) method is employed (Figure S12). In detail, the bulk 
resistance (Rb) can be read from the EIS Nyquist plot by extrapolating the applied 
frequency to infinity, and the ηohm can be obtained through multiplying Rb with the 
applied current i (Figure S9b). In GITT measurements, the cells are charged and 
discharged at 0.3/0.4/0.5 C for 4/3/2.4 min followed by relaxation at open circuit for 60 
min in each step (Figure S10). During the relaxation step, the voltage will be 
immediately lifted as the applied current is removed, and the voltage difference can be 
attributed to the elimination of ηohm and ηact due to their current-dependent properties 
(Figure S9c). Afterwards, the voltage value rises along with relaxation time and 
eventually approaches to the thermodynamic potential (vs. Li+/Li), and the value of the 
above voltage rise is identified as the ηcon. Accordingly, the values of ηohm, ηact, and ηcon 
at every depth of discharge (DOD) can be assured through the GITT‒EIS method 
(Figure 2C‒E).

Figure S35 | Equilibrium circuit of the Li2S6 symmetric cells based on the DRT 
analysis. 
 
Firstly, a resistor Rb is selected to represent the bulk resistance of the Li2S6 
symmetric cell system, including the ion migration resistance of the bulk electrolyte 
and the ohmic resistance of the current collectors. Note that in this procedure, no 
capacitive responses are produced, therefore no peak exhibits in the DRT profiles. 
Secondly, the prominent peak in the high-frequency stage (denoted as the contact 
region) is due to the contact effect between the nanoparticles and between the 
nanoparticles and the current collectors. The electrochemical process is modeled 
through a parallel circuit of Rcon and Ccon, corresponding to the contact resistance and 
the contact capacitance. The characteristic relaxation time (τ) of this region is around 
10‒4 s in the DRT profiles.

reactions in electrode (denoted as the reaction region). To simulate the accurate
