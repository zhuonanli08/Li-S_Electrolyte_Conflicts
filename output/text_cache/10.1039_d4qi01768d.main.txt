Benzo-crown ether electrolyte additives in
facilitating sulfur evolution and lithium anode
stabilization for high-performance lithium–sulfur
batteries†
Zhihua Wang,‡a Zhenjun Xue,‡b Junru Ke,a Min Dong,a Bei Ma,a Zhe Zhang,a
Hua Ji,*c,d Qingmin Ji,a He Zhu
*a and Si Lan*a
The application of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries faces challenges such as sluggish redox kinetics of sulfur
species and damage to the lithium anode. Herein we introduce crown ether-based electrolytes as addi-
tives to address these issues. Density functional theory (DFT) results veriﬁed the validity of dibenzo-24-
crown-8 (D24C8), which was found to promote sulfur species conversion kinetics. Furthermore, the
D24C8 additive enabled the reversion of lithium plating/stripping, suppressed dendrite growth and miti-
gated detrimental side reactions at the lithium anode caused by LiPSs. D24C8 exhibits outstanding per-
formance in modulating the orbital energy levels of sulfur species and improving lithium anode stability,
so it can be used as a bifunctional additive for regulating Li–S batteries. Li–S batteries with D24C8
demonstrated promising high-rate performance and long-term cycling stability, with over 1200 cycles at
a 2 C rate and a capacity decay of only 0.034% per cycle. This work presents advanced electrolyte design
for next-generation sustainable Li–S batteries and provides insights into optimizing analogous multiphase
electrochemical energy-eﬃcient reaction processes.
1.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered to be one of the
most important candidates for future secondary batteries due
to their high energy density up to 2600 W h kg−1 and abun-
dant sulfur resources.1,2 However, solid sulfur in the cathode
of Li–S batteries is easily converted into soluble long-chain
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in the electrolyte, causing the
“shuttle eﬀect” when migrating to the lithium anode side
through the separator.3–6 The sluggish redox reactions of the
sulfur species cause the accumulation of LiPSs, exacerbating
the “shuttle eﬀect”. This results in an irreversible loss of active
sulfur and rapid capacity decay.7,8 At the lithium anode, the
reactions between lithium and the electrolyte during dis-
charge, as well as the uneven deposition of lithium during
charging,
lead
to
the
uncontrolled
growth
of
lithium
dendrites.9–11 This may cause the separator to rupture, leading
to direct contact between the cathode and anode, and even-
tually, a short circuit.12 Therefore, accelerating the conversion
ability of sulfur species and stabilizing the lithium anode hold
great promise for optimizing Li–S batteries.
To address these challenges, researchers have developed
various strategies, including the introduction of functional
barrier intermediate layers,13–19 the creation of artificial solid
electrolyte
interfaces,20–22
the
modification
of
electrolyte
components23–25 and so on, with varying degrees of success.
However, the first two strategies often involve complex manu-
facturing processes, limiting their practical application. In
contrast, the introduction of additives into the electrolyte com-
ponents is considered a more cost-eﬀective and facile route to
enhance battery performance. These additives can inhibit the
diﬀusion of polysulfides, stabilize the surface of the lithium
anode and promote the complete conversion of sulfur. For
example, hexyl methyl ether (HME)26 and hydrofluoroether
(HFE)27 can change their solvation structure through chemical
interactions with polysulfides, thereby inhibiting the damage
caused by LiPSs to the lithium anode. Additives such as 2,5-di-

Among all existing additives, crown ether (CE)-based mole-
cules stand out as promising regulators of LiPSs due to their
stable
coordination
with
Li+
in
ether-based
electrolyte
systems.32,33 In addition, the CE additive has a positive impact
on stabilizing the lithium anode. In Li–O2 batteries, benzo-18-
crown-6 ether (B18C6) stabilizes the lithium anode by regulat-
ing the migration and electron exchange of Li+.34 Therefore,
the dendritic growth and anode corrosion are eﬀectively
reduced. With lithium metal at high current density, an asym-
metric lithium salt with a pseudo-CE-like structure (lithium
1,1,1-trifluoro-N-[2-[2-(2-methoxyethoxy) ethyl]] methanesulfo-
namide) drives the SEI self-cleaning mechanism and improves
compatibility. Hence a stable high-performance lithium-metal
battery is achieved.35 These findings highlight the significant
influence of CE-based electrolyte additives on regulating the
electrode–electrolyte interface and interface reactions at the
lithium metal. In Li–S batteries, the CE as a functional group
was introduced into the covalent organic framework36 and
polymer interface,37 respectively, to undertake the optimiz-
ation of cell components. Therefore, it is expected that the CE-
based additives can play a crucial role in next-generation Li–S
batteries.
In this study, dibenzo-24-crown-8 (D24C8) was introduced
as an electrolyte additive in Li–S batteries. It was found that
the oxygen atoms in the D24C8 molecule preferred to coordi-
nate with Li+ and bind to Sx
2−, altering the molecular orbital
energy levels of Li2Sx species and accelerating their kinetics. In
addition, D24C8 promotes the formation of a smooth and
dense surface morphology on the lithium anode. This surface
morphology plays a vital role in protecting lithium metal and
maintaining stable cycling performance during battery oper-
ation. Therefore, the D24C8 molecule is expected to enhance
the polysulfide conversion kinetics in Li–S batteries and
improve
the
interface
chemistry
of
the
lithium
anode.
Electrochemical data revealed that the Li–S batteries with
D24C8 exhibited high discharge specific capacity, enhanced
rate capability and excellent long-term cycling stability, achiev-
ing 1200 cycles at 2 C with a decay rate of only 0.034% per
cycle. These findings indicate that D24C8 shows great poten-
tial as a bifunctional additive capable of promoting sulfur
species conversion kinetics and improving lithium anode
stability in order to improve the rate performance and stability
of Li–S batteries.
2.
Results and discussion
The D24C8 molecules are composed of a CE ring and two
benzene rings, which is confirmed by 13C nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) analysis. As shown in Fig. 1a, six NMR peaks
emerge from the aromatic components (1, 2, 3) and from the
CH2–O (4, 5, 6) groups. Attenuated total reflection-Fourier
transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy further verified
the molecular structure of D24C8 (Fig. 1b). The peak signals
for benzene, C–H, and C–O–C were all consistent with D24C8,
verifying the correctness of the D24C8 molecule. Raman spec-
troscopy was used to further characterize the structure of the
electrolyte. As shown in Fig. S1,† Raman spectroscopy exhibi-
ted
clear
correlations
with
lithium
bis(trifluoromethane-
sulfonic)imide (LiTFSI), 1,3-dioxane (DOL) and 1,2-diamethox-
yethane (DME). After the addition of D24C8, the signal associ-
ated with C–O–C did not change significantly. However, the
signal at 1596 cm−1 was associated with the C–C bond
vibration of the benzene cyclic carbon skeleton, indicating the
successful introduction of D24C8.
The electrochemical performance of batteries incorporating
D24C8 was evaluated using Li–S coin cells. A conventional
sulfur loading of 1.2 mg cm−2 was utilized for the evaluation.
Based on the overall capacity from rate performance tests, an
optimal concentration of 100 mM D24C8 was identified
(Fig. S2†). At various current rates (0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2C), the
Li–S battery with D24C8 delivered higher capacities of 1355,
1223, 965, 839, and 772 mA h g−1, respectively, compared to its
counterpart without D24C8 (1135, 785, 660, 577, and 136 mA h
g−1) (Fig. 2a). To gain a further understanding of the increased
capacity, the constant current discharge–charge curve at
diﬀerent rates was analyzed (Fig. 2b). Notably, the high-plateau
capacity (QHigh) during discharge, corresponding to the tran-
sition from S8 to Li2S4, is similar for both batteries at 0.1
C. However, the low-plateau capacity (QLow) during discharge,
representing the transition from Li2S4 to Li2S, increased from

(QLow/QHigh) is commonly employed to evaluate the conversion

which explains the increased capacity at low current density. At
2 C, the QHigh and QLow values of the battery with BD18C6 were
288 and 485 mA h g−1, corresponding to 4.45 and 6.78 times
the capacity of the battery without D24C8, respectively. These
results indicate that D24C8 facilitates both the generation of
Li2S and the conversion of LiPSs at high current densities.
The Li–S battery with D24C8 displayed an improved cycling
stability at 0.2 C, with an initial capacity of 1208 mA h g−1 and
maintained a capacity retention of 77.9% after 100 cycles
(Fig. S3a†). In comparison, the Li–S battery without D24C8
showed rapid capacity decay with a lower capacity retention
(50.4%) after 100 cycles, only about two-thirds of that observed
in the battery with D24C8. From the galvanostatic discharge–
charge curves, stable charge/discharge plateaus were evidenced
for diﬀerent cycles in the batteries with and without D24C8
(Fig. S3b–3c†). The lower capacity attenuation verified by QLow
and QHigh, together with a sustained QLow/QHigh ratio of 2.3 for
diﬀerent cycles (Fig. S3d†), indicate stable reversible reactions
of sulfur species during cycling.
The addition of D24C8 also enhances the cycling stability at
higher current densities of 1 C and 2 C. As shown in Fig. 2d, it
can be seen that the battery without D24C8 shows continuous
capacity
decline.
In
contrast,
the
Li–S
battery
with
D24C8 maintains stable cycling from the initial cycle at 1.0 C,
with a capacity retention rate of 93.4% for 150 cycles. Initially,
the battery with D24C8 delivered a capacity of 778 mA h g−1.
After 1200 cycles, a capacity of 468 mA h g−1 could be retained,
corresponding to a capacity decay of 0.034% per cycle (Fig. 2e).
Importantly, the coulombic eﬃciency remained above 98.8%,
while the batteries without D24C8 were deactivated after 224
cycles, with a decay rate of 0.375% per cycle. This phenom-
enon of battery failure might be attributed to not only the
depletion of the active material but also the detrimental eﬀect
of the slow conversion dynamics. Therefore, factors contribut-
ing to the improved battery performance were further investi-
gated from both the cathode and anode perspectives.
To investigate the kinetics of the D24C8 additives on the
cathode, comprehensive electrochemical tests were performed
to explore the modulation of the additives on conversion of
LiPSs and Li2S deposition/dissociation. Fig. 3a compares the

Li2S. During charging, the inherently low electronic and even

observed to be close to 2.32 V, which is lower than that of the
battery without D24C8 (2.44 V), indicating that D24C8 pro-

a

transition
from
Li2S
to
LiPS.

To investigate the eﬀect of D24C8 on the kinetics of cath-
odes, diﬀusion coeﬃcients of Li+ (DLi+) in the batteries with
and without D24C8 were calculated. According to the CV
curves at diﬀerent scan rates, the currents (I) for the battery
with D24C8 exhibited a rapid increase with the scan rate (v)
(Fig. S4a†). This growing rate was higher than that observed in
the battery without D24C8 (Fig. S4b†). Based on the Randles–
Sevcik equation, the apparent DLi+ of the redox peaks positively
correlated with the k value of the linear fit. The k values of the
oxidation peak C2 and the reduction peak A1 in the battery
with D24C8 were significantly higher than those in the battery
without D24C8 (Fig. 3b and c), indicating that the D24C8 facili-
tated Li+ diﬀusion. The rapid Li+ diﬀusion can promote the
redox reactions of sulfur species, particularly at high current
densities.
Subsequently, the redox kinetics of LiPSs was evaluated by
using batteries with carbon cloth (CC) as symmetric electrodes
and Li2S6 solution as the electrolyte. As illustrated in Fig. 3d,
in the absence of Li2S6, the CV curve showed a horizontal
straight line with negligible current, indicating that the
current collector without Li2S6 was not electrochemically
active, resulting in linear I–V curves. On the other hand, the
battery with Li2S6 exhibited a smaller current compared to that
with Li2S6 + D24C8, with no distinct peaks. In contrast, the CV
curve with Li2S6 + D24C8 exhibited high symmetry with two
distinct redox peaks and an increased peak area at a rate of
3 mV s−1. Compared with the battery with L2S6, the battery
with L2S6 + D24C8 additives facilitated sulfur redox reactions
involving LiPSs.
Furthermore, the redox kinetics of the liquid–solid conver-
sion from LiPSs to Li2S was studied in an Li2S deposition
experiment (Fig. 3e and f). The battery with D24C8 demon-
strated a maximum potentiostatic current much earlier (at
3270 s) than the battery without D24C8 (at 5850 s). Moreover,
the Li2S deposition capacity increased from 345 mA h g−1 to
421 mA h g−1 after adding D24C8. SEM images of the cathode
surface of Li2S nucleation with and without D24C8 additives
are shown in Fig. 3g and h. Compared with the surface of Li2S
from the battery without D24C8, a loose and needle-like mor-
phology of Li2S was observed in the battery with D24C8 as the
additive. This morphology provides a larger surface area to
boost active sites and shortens the electron/ion diﬀusion path,
significantly promoting the utilization of sulfur
species.
Consistent with the constant potential discharge curves, the
needle-like morphology indicates that the presence of D24C8
can eﬀectively enhance the capability of reactive Li2S precipi-
tation. Thus, the high catalytic activity of D24C8 is well main-
tained during the discharge–charge process, achieving desir-
able redox conversion of LiPSs.
For a deeper insight into the conversion mechanism of
sulfur species, first principles calculations were performed.
The obtained electrostatic potential (ESP) map revealed that
the minimum electrostatic potential was mainly distributed at
oxygen (O) atoms (Fig. S5†). Therefore, the CE cavity of D24C8,
along with the conjugated structure of the benzene, enhances
the aﬃnity of D24C8 toward Li+. When D24C8 combines with
Li+ in a 1 : 1 ratio, the binding energy (Eb) is −5.31 eV
(Fig. S6†). However, in this structure, Li+ can only exist on one
side
of
D24C8,
coordinating
with
the
four
O
atoms.

Based on the above computational results, we propose the fol-
lowing conversion mechanism of sulfur species, as illustrated
in Fig. 4d. Initially, the solvent molecule combines with Li+ to
form a stable [D24C8·2Li+] complex. S8 at the cathode captures
electrons to generate adsorbed S8
2−. As the battery operates,
S8
2−dissolves into the electrolyte and coordinates with the
[D24C8·2Li+]
complex
to
yield
the
[D24C8·Li2S8].
Then
[D24C8·Li2S8] gains an additional Li+ and electron to generate
[D24C8·Li2Sx] (x = 1, 2, 4 and 6).
The energy levels of the lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital
(HOMO) of Li2Sx and [D24C8·Li2Sx] were calculated, with Li2S6
representing LiPSs (Fig. 4e). [D24C8·Li2Sx] exhibited lower
LUMO and HOMO energy levels than those of the conventional
Li2Sx. The energy band gaps of [D24C8·Li2S], [D24C8·Li2S2],
and [D24C8·Li2S6] were estimated to be 3.12 eV, 3.46 eV, and
3.23 eV, respectively, which are lower than those of Li2S (4.38
eV), Li2S2 (4.03 eV), and Li2S6 (3.60 eV). Typically, narrower
band gaps indicate higher conductivity and faster charge trans-
fer, confirming the accelerated redox kinetics after the for-
mation of [D24C8·Li2Sx]. These results reveal the origin of the
rapid redox kinetics of sulfur species, and provide a feasible
approach for accelerating conversion reaction kinetics.

3.
Conclusion
In summary, the exceptional electrochemical performance of Li–S
batteries with D24C8 can be attributed to several key factors.
First, the coordination between D24C8 and sulfur species
changes the orbital energy levels of the sulfur species, facilitating
faster electron acceptance, LiPS conversion and Li2S nucleation,
thereby enhancing reaction kinetics. Additionally, the additives
can regulate lithium deposition behaviour, ensuring long-term
-cycling stability and avoidance of dendrite growth. The Li–S bat-
teries with D24C8 show a high initial discharge capacity of
1355 mA h g−1 at a 0.1 C rate and maintain a capacity of 772 mA
h g−1 even at a 2 C rate. The D24C8 electrolyte additive proposed
in this study could inspire new approaches for the commercial
application of Li–S batteries, advancing the practical development
of high-performance energy storage solutions.
