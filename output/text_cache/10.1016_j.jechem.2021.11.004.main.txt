a b s t r a c t
While the sulfur conversion reaction kinetics in Li–S batteries is nowadays improved by the use of appro-
priate electrocatalysts, it remains a challenge for the batteries to perform well under the lean electrolyte
condition where polysulﬁde shuttle, electrode passivation and the loss of electrolyte due to side reac-
tions, are aggravated. These challenges are addressed in this study by the tandem use of a polysulﬁde
conversion catalyst and a redox–targeting mediator in a gel sulfur cathode. Speciﬁcally, the gel cathode
reduces the polysulﬁde mobility and hence the polysulﬁde shuttle and the passivation of the lithium
anode by the crossover polysulﬁdes. The redox mediator restrains the deposition of inactive sulfur spe-
cies in the cathode thereby enabling the Fe–N and Co–N co–doped carbon catalyst to prolong its catalytic
activity. Consequently, the integrated catalytic system is able to increase the discharge capacity of high–
loading (6.8 mg cm2) lean–electrolyte (4.0 mL mg1) Li–S batteries from 630 to 1316 mAh g1, con-
currently with an improvement of the cycle life (600 cycles with 46% capacity retention at 1.0 mA cm2).
Redox mediator assisted catalysis in a gel cathode is therefore an effective strategy to extend the appli-
cation of the sulfur conversion catalyst in lean electrolyte Li–S batteries.

1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered as one of the
more promising successors to lithium–ion batteries because of
their high theoretical energy density (2500 Wh kg1) and low
cost of sulfur (<$150 ton1) [1–3]. However, the actual energy den-
sity of Li–S batteries developed to date (<400 Wh kg1) is notably
lower than the theoretical value [4–7]. The use of a large amount of
electrolyte in the batteries has been identiﬁed as a major con-
tributing factor [8–10]. For example, a Li–S battery with an
electrolyte–to–sulfur (E/S) ratio of 25 mL mg1 has a theoretical
energy density of only 150 Wh kg1 which can be increased to
400 Wh kg1 if the E/S ratio is reduced to 4 mL mg1 [11–13].
However, a low E/S ratio decreases the ion conduction in the sulfur
cathode and impedes the kinetics of the sulfur electrochemical
reaction [14–16]. Consequently, the lean–electrolyte Li–S batteries
as implemented to date have a rather low speciﬁc capacity (<1200
mAh g1) and a low cycling stability (<500 cycles) [17–22].
The speciﬁc capacity and cyclability of Li–S batteries can be
improved by accelerating the sulfur conversion kinetics with suit-
able electrocatalysts [23–26]. The effectiveness of electrocatalysis,
however, diminishes under the lean electrolyte condition. This is
because of the high polysulﬁde concentration in a starved elec-
trolyte, which increases the electrolyte viscosity and the charge
and mass transfer resistance of the sulfur cathode [27–29]. When
the E/S ratio is lowered to the solubility limit of polysulﬁdes (8
molS L1 for ether–based solvents, equivalent to an E/S ratio of
3.9 lL mg1) [11], solid sulfur and Li2S would accumulate in
the sulfur cathode during battery cycling [30,31]. The accumulated
S/Li2S are less utilizable because of the difﬁculty of reactions in the
solid state, resulting in the passivation of the sulfur cathode [32–
34]. The Li2S deposits accumulated to micrometer size, in particu-
lar, is nearly electrochemically inert as dead Li2S and requires a
very high activation potential (3.6 V vs. Li+/Li) to initiate its oxi-
dation [35–37]. In addition, the back–and–forth diffusion of poly-

improved if the polysulﬁde shuttle, electrode passivation and elec-

The integrated approach presented in this study can mitigate
these operating issues of lean–electrolyte Li–S batteries quite
effectively. It involves the tandem use of an effective polysulﬁde
conversion catalyst and a redox–matched mediator in a gel sulfur
cathode, which is coupled to a protected Li electrode (Fig. 1b).
The gel sulfur cathode suppresses the polysulﬁde shuttle and the
polysulﬁde passivation of the lithium anode, and the I3

tor therein is used to oxidize the accumulated dead Li2S to mini-
mize the passivation of the sulfur cathode after repeated cycling.
The electrolyte consumption by the side reactions on the Li anode
is minimized by a Li+ conductive Li3PS4 coating on the protected Li
electrode [47,48]. These measures, when implemented for a previ-
ously developed Fe–N and Co–N co–doped carbon catalyst (Fe–N/
Co–N@C) [49], could increase the discharge capacity of high–load-
ing (6.8 mg cm2) lean–electrolyte (4.0 mL mg1) Li–S batteries
from 630 to 1316 mAh g1, concurrently with an improvement
of the cycle life (600 cycles with 46% capacity retention at
1.0 mA cm2). The experimental results establish the effectiveness
of the integrated approach over the more common methods based
on catalyst optimization only.

2.2. Preparation of Fe–N/Co–N@C catalyst
The Fe–N/Co–N@C catalyst was prepared by pyrolyzing a mix-
ture of K3Fe(CN)6 and K3Co(CN)6 at elevated temperature in argon.
K3Fe(CN)6 and K3Co(CN)6 with the same molar ratio were ﬁrstly
mixed by ball–milling at 300 rpm for 12 h. Then, the collected
powders were heated from room temperature to 1000 C in ﬂow-
ing Ar (40 mL min1) at 5 C min1 and kept at 1000 C for 2 h.
After cooling to room temperature, the obtained solid was washed
with deionized water followed by etching in 50 mL 2 M H2SO4
solution at 80 C for 12 h under magnetic stirring. The ﬁnal solid
products were separated by centrifugation, repeatedly washed
with water thrice; and ﬁnally lyophilized.
2.3. Fabrication of sulfur cathodes
The sulfur working electrode was prepared as follows: 70 wt%
sulfur powder, 10 wt% Fe–N/Co–N@C powder, 5 wt% Super P and
5 wt% CNT were ﬁrst thoroughly mixed by ball–milling at
300 rpm for 12 h. The obtained powders were then added to a vis-
cous slurry containing 10 wt% binder and different amount of LiI
for another 12 h. PEO and PVP with a mass ratio of 4:1 were used
as the binder for the preparation of gel sulfur (denoted as Gel–S)
cathodes, and PVDF binder was used for normal sulfur (denoted
as Nor–S) cathodes. Their compositions are shown in Table 1. The
obtained slurry was cast on an Al foil, and dried at 60 C for
12 h. The C–S electrodes were prepared by blending 70 wt% sulfur
powder, 10% PVDF binder, 10 wt% Super P and 10 wt% CNT. To pre-
pare the sulfur electrode with a catalytic interlayer, the sulfur elec-
trode was coated with a slurry of 90 wt% Fe–N/Co–N@C and 10 wt%
PVDF binder in NMP, and was ﬁnalized after drying at 60 C for
12 h. Fig. S1 shows a typical coated sulfur electrode with a coating
thickness of 0.3 mm, and the coated mass loading is 0.2 mg cm2.
The foils were punched into 12 mm diameter disks and the typical
sulfur loading on each disk was 6.8 mg cm2.

2.4. Electrochemical measurements of lithium–sulfur cells
CR2025–type coin cells were assembled by pairing the sulfur
cathode with a Li3PS4–protected lithium metal anode in an Ar–
ﬁlled glovebox. The cathode and anode were separated by a Cel-
gard 2400 separator. The electrolyte was 1.0 M LiTFSI in a 1:1 (v/
v) DOL/DME mixture with 0.2 M LiNO3, and the electrolyte to sulfur
ratio in the cell was kept at 4 lL mg1. Galvanostatic charging and
discharging were carried at room temperature on a NEWARE BTS–
5 V battery tester at various C rates (1C = 1675 mAh g1). Cyclic
voltammetry, potentiostatic measurements and electrochemical
impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded using an Autolab type III
electrochemical workstation. EIS measurements were carried from
1 MHz to 10 mHz. All tests were conducted at room temperature.
3. Results and discussion
The gel sulfur cathode was prepared by replacing the conven-
tional PVDF) binder for the sulfur cathode with a PEO–PVP binder
(Fig. 2a). Different from the PVDF binder, the PEO–PVP binder has
good gelation properties in liquid electrolyte because of an abun-
dance of oxygen functional groups which bind well with the sol-
vent molecules [50–52]. The inset of Fig. 2b shows an example of
the good gelation ability of the PEO–PVP binder when added with
a small amount of electrolyte at a normalized E/S ratio of
4.0 mL mg1 (18 mg PEO–PVP binder in 0.5 mL electrolyte, assum-
ing a sulfur/binder mass ratio of 7:1 in a fabricated sulfur cathode).
In addition, PEO also forms Li+ conductive complexes with Li salts
and PVP has a good afﬁnity for polysulﬁdes [50,53]. Together they
can contribute to improving the Li+ conduction and polysulﬁde
retention in the gel sulfur cathode. Fig. 2(b) shows the evaporation
of electrolytes in the presence of the PVDF or PEO–PVP binder
respectively. The electrolyte in the PVDF binder evaporated quickly
at 62.5 C while the loss of electrolyte with the PEO–PVP binder
was signiﬁcantly lower (<7 wt%) even at 100 C, indicating good
solvent molecule encapsulation with the PEO–PVP binder.
We ﬁrst measured the impedance of Li–S cells with either a
Gel–S cathode or a Nor–S cathode at the same E/S ratio of
4.0 mL mg1. Fig. 2(c) shows the electrochemical impedance spectra
(EIS) of Li–S cells with either the Gel–S cathode or the Nor–S cath-
ode. The charge transfer resistance of the Gel–S cell (Rct) deduced
from the diameter of the semicircular arc at medium frequency
is notably smaller than that of the Nor–S cell, suggesting a more
facile charge transfer in the Gel–S cell sulfur cathode. In addition,
the Warburg resistance (r) measured from the low frequency
region of the Gel–S cathode is also smaller than that of the Nor–S

The catalytic activities of the Fe–N/Co–N@C catalyst in Gel–S
and Nor–S cathodes were analyzed ﬁrst by cyclic voltammetry
(CV) at the E/S ratio of 4.0 mL mg1. The sharp redox peaks and
low polarization (as shown by the positive shifts in the cathodic
peaks and negative shifts in the anodic peaks) of the sulfur electro-
chemical reaction in the Gel–S cathode suggest more facile poly-
sulﬁde conversions kinetics in the Gel–S cathode (Fig. 3a). In
stark contrast to this is the sulfur cathode without the Fe–N/Co–
N@C catalyst (the C–S electrode, see Experimental section) where
only a broad cathodic peak at 2.22 V (related to the S8 ? Li2S4
reaction) was found. The absence of the second cathodic peak indi-
cates the large polarization in the Li2S4 ? Li2S reaction (and the
need for a high reduction overpotential) without the bimetallic
Fe–N/Co–N@C catalyst. Hence the battery performance under the
lean electrolyte condition was improved signiﬁcantly with the
use of an effective catalyst, based on the Tafel plot analysis in
Fig. S5. The effectiveness of the Fe–N/Co–N@C catalyst in accelerat-
ing the polysulﬁde reaction kinetics especially the short–chain
polysulﬁde conversion reactions has been discussed in our previ-
ous work [49]. The Gel–S and Nor–S cathodes with LiI (denoted
as the Gel–S–LiI and Nor–S–LiI cathodes respectively) were simi-
larly evaluated. Fig. 3(b) shows that the Gel–S–LiI cathode has
the sharpest redox peaks and the lowest polarization, suggesting
further polysulﬁde reaction kinetics improvements in the presence
of LiI. This was also demonstrated by the EIS measurement of a
smaller charge transfer resistance for the Gel–S–LiI cathode
(Fig. 2d). Relative to the Nor–S–LiI cathode, all cathodic peaks of
the Gel–S–LiI cathode are positively shifted and all anodic peaks
are negatively shifted negatively. Hence the catalysis with Fe–N/
Co–N@C under the lean electrolyte condition is more effective in
the Gel–S–LiI cathode than that in the Nor–S–LiI cathode.

Aside from the effectiveness of the Gel–S–LiI cathode in
restraining the polysulﬁde shuttle and cathode passivation, it is
also effective in curtailing potential side reactions between mobile
I3
/I mediator and the Li anode which also consume the electrolyte
solvent. Fig. 5(f) shows that strong I 3d peaks assignable to LiI were
found on the Li anode of the Nor–S–LiI cell, whereas very weak I 3d
signals were detected on the Li anode of the Gel–S–LiI cell. This
indicates that the Gel–S–LiI cathode could effectively impede the
diffusion of I3
/I to the Li anode, and hence the side reactions with
the lithium anode. The side reactions on Li anode could be further
curtailed with a Li3PS4–protected Li anode. The XPS spectrum of
the Li anode of the Gel–S–LiI cell was devoid of the I 3d signal
(Fig. 5f); and the Li3PS4 protection layer still retained its pristine
chemical structure (Fig. S9) [49]. With the suppression of the
anode side reactions, the protected Li anode of the Gel–S–LiI cell
after 100 cycles still displayed a smooth surface without any
growth of dendritic and mossy lithium (Fig. S10), whereas the Li
anode of the Nor–S–LiI cell showed a potholed structure as a result
of severe side reactions. Fig. S11 shows the cycling stability com-
parison of Gel–S–LiI cells with and without the Li3PS4 protected
layer on the Li anode. The measurements showed that the Gel–
S–LiI cell with the Li3PS4 covered Li anode could cycle signiﬁcantly
better, conﬁrming the effectiveness of the Li3PS4 layer in alleviating
the polysulﬁde shuttle and the corrosion of the Li anode. Even
without the addition of LiNO3 additive in the electrolyte, the
Gel–S–LiI cell still sustained good cycling stability without sharp
capacity fading (Fig. S12).
4. Conclusions
In summary, this study presents an integrated approach which
uses a gel electrolyte with dissolved LiI to mitigate, concurrently,
the issues of polysulﬁde shuttle, electrode passivation and elec-
trolyte consumption in lean–electrolyte Li–S batteries. Under the
same E/S ratio of 4.0 mL mg1, EIS measurements indicated that a
Gel–S–LiI cathode could provide higher ion conduction and lower
charge transfer resistance than a Nor–S–LiI cathode. CV analysis
also corroborated a more facile polysulﬁde reaction kinetics. As a
result, sulfur cathodes with a Fe–N and Co–N co–doped carbon cat-
alyst could deliver high discharge capacity (1316 mAh gS
1 at
0.5 mA cm2) and good cycling stability (46% capacity retention
after 600 cycles at 1.0 mA cm2) under a high sulfur loading
(6.8 mg cm2) and lean–electrolyte (4.0 mL mg1) condition. This
integrated approach of combining the redox–mediated electro-
catalysis of polysulﬁde conversion and Gel–S cathode in one Li–S
battery to improve battery cycling performance in lean electrolyte
Li–S batteries is a simple but effective approach worthy of further
exploration.
