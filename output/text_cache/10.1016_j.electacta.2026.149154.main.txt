A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are a promising high-energy-density storage technology, yet their practical 
application is hindered by the dissolution of lithium polysulfides (Li₂Sₓ, 1 ≤x ≤8) and the resulting shuttle 
effect. In this study, we demonstrate a novel electrolyte solvent, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2-methoxyethoxy)ethane 
(C14), designed to suppress Li₂Sₓ dissolution through steric and electronic modulation. Electrochemical mea­
surements revealed that the Li-S cell using the C14-based electrolyte operated reversibly without the infinite 
charging plateau characteristic of the shuttle effect, which was observed in a conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME)-based system. Mechanistic investigations combining FT-IR, Raman spectroscopy, and COSMO-RS calcu­
lations indicated that the electron-withdrawing trifluoromethyl group in C14 causes a delocalization of the 
molecular surface charge and restricts the conformational flexibility required for Li⁺ chelation. Crucially, S K- 
edge X-ray Absorption Near Edge Structure (XANES) analysis provided direct evidence of the solvation mech­
anism: while the DME system exhibited a pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV attributed to Li defects generated by Li₂Sₓ 
ionization, this feature was notably absent in the C14 system. These results confirm that C14 effectively prevents 
the ionization and subsequent leaching of Li₂Sₓ into the electrolyte. This work establishes that suppressing 
solvent-induced ionization via charge delocalization is a vital strategy for realizing stable, high-performance Li-S 
batteries.
1. Introduction
Recent research has focused on developing high-energy-density 
storage devices to meet the growing demands of lightweight applica­
tions. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered a promising tech­
nology due to their exceptionally high theoretical energy density [1]. A 
typical Li-S battery employs sulfur as the cathode and lithium metal as 
the anode. During the charge-discharge process, the global electro­
chemical reactions are represented as follows [2]: 
S8 + 16Li+ + 16e−⇄8Li2S(1)Li⇄Li+ + en−
(2) 
In reaction (1), sulfur undergoes a 16-electron redox process, 
yielding a high theoretical specific capacity of 1672 mAh g⁻¹. However, 
the practical application of sulfur is hindered by its inherent insulating 
nature, characterized by low electronic and ionic conductivities. 
Extensive research has been conducted to enhance the conductivity of 
sulfur cathodes [3–7]. For instance, Ji et al. reported that a carbon-sulfur 
composite cathode, featuring sulfur encapsulated within a mesoporous 
carbon framework, exhibited a high specific capacity of 1200 mAh g⁻¹ 
[3].
Despite these advances, the dissolution of intermediate lithium pol­
ysulfides (Li₂Sₓ, 1 ≤x ≤8) into the electrolyte remains a critical chal­
lenge. The dissolved Li₂Sₓ species migrate between the cathode and 
anode, causing the "shuttle effect," which severely degrades the cycle life 
[6]. To address this, Takahashi et al. demonstrated that confining sulfur 
within microporous carbon (pore diameter < 2 nm) can suppress the 
shuttle effect [5]. In such systems, the micropores are smaller than the 
solvated Li ions, preventing solvent interaction with the Li₂Sₓ species 
and thereby inhibiting their leaching into the electrolyte. However, the 
limited pore volume of microporous carbon inherently restricts the 
sulfur loading, reducing the overall energy density.

Li₂Sₓ dissolution. Additives such as lithium nitrate (LiNO₃) are known to 
mitigate the shuttle effect [8–10]. LiNO₃ decomposes on the cathode 
surface to form various non-stoichiometric decomposition products (e. 
g., LiₓNOy), which act as oxidation catalysts to convert dissolved Li₂Sₓ 
back to elemental sulfur during charging, thereby suppressing the 
shuttle reaction [9]. Torii et al. reported that fluoroethylene carbonate 
(FEC)-based electrolytes impede Li₂Sₓ dissolution through the formation 
of a stable protective layer derived from FEC decomposition [11]. This 
passivation layer remains stable during subsequent cycles, enabling 
reversible Li-S reactions without the need for LiNO₃. Consequently, 
FEC-based Li-S batteries can operate over a wide voltage window 
without significant polysulfide leaching. Beyond these surface passiv­
ation strategies, recent advancements have also explored modifying the 
intrinsic Li⁺ solvation structure to suppress the shuttle effect. For 
instance, high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) [12,13] and localized 
high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) [14,15] have been widely 
investigated, as they inherently restrict polysulfide solubility by mini­
mizing the amount of free solvent molecules.
Despite these successes in suppressing polysulfide dissolution, a 
critical factor determining the practical energy density of Li-S batteries 
is the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio [16]. While a low E/S ratio is 
essential for achieving high energy density, Li-S batteries typically 
consume more electrolyte through irreversible side reactions compared 
to conventional lithium-ion batteries, leading to a shortened cycle life 
under 
lean-electrolyte 
conditions 
[16–18]. 
Furthermore, 
carbonate-based solvents like FEC, as well as the massive amounts of 
heavy lithium salts used in HCEs and LHCEs, possess high densities, 
which increases the total weight of the cell and lowers the gravimetric 
energy density. In contrast, ether-based electrolytes have lower den­
sities, facilitating a reduced E/S ratio and higher energy density. How­
ever, as previously noted, ether-based systems suffer from poor cycle life 
due to severe Li₂Sₓ dissolution. Therefore, it is imperative to develop an 
electrolyte that combines low density with the ability to suppress pol­
ysulfide dissolution to achieve both high energy density and long-term 
stability.
In this study, we focus on a novel fluoroether, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2- 
methoxyethoxy)ethane (C14), designed by introducing an electron- 
withdrawing trifluoromethyl (-CF₃) group into the terminal of a con­
ventional dimethoxyethane (DME) skeleton. It is noteworthy that C14 
possesses a relatively low density of 1.16 g cm⁻³, which is lower than 
those of other typical shuttle-suppressing solvents such as FEC (~1.32 g 
cm⁻³) and sulfolane (~1.26 g cm⁻³).
To fundamentally suppress the shuttle effect without relying on high 
salt concentrations, weakly solvating solvents have garnered significant 
attention. However, conventional designs face a severe practical trade- 
off. While recently reported weakly solvating fluoroethers (WSEs) can 
suppress polysulfide dissolution, they often rely on specific salts such as 
LiFSI [19], which is generally considered less compatible with Li-S 
systems due to potential side reactions with polysulfides. Alterna­
tively, low-molecular-weight mono-ethers (e.g., dipropyl ether or 
methyl tert‑butyl ether) that exhibit weakly solvating properties suffer 
from excessively low boiling points [20]. Such high volatility makes 
them incompatible with standard battery manufacturing processes, 
particularly the vacuum electrolyte injection step. C14 was conceptually 
designed to overcome this structural dilemma. By integrating a single 
-CF₃ group exclusively at the terminal of the highly flexible DME back­
bone, C14 strategically balances three critical requirements within a 
single molecule: (1) sufficient solvating power to dissolve 1.0 M LiTFSI, 
(2) the delocalization of molecular surface charge to suppress poly­
sulfide ionization, and (3) a practically viable boiling point of 104 ◦C, 
which enables stable vacuum filling procedures. We investigate the 
electrochemical properties of Li-S batteries using C14-based electrolytes 
and evaluate their effectiveness in suppressing Li₂Sₓ dissolution while 
maintaining a relatively low-density compared to alternative functional 
electrolytes profile.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of active materials
The cathode active material was a carbon-sulfur (C-S) composite 
prepared by encapsulating sulfur into mesoporous carbon. Sulfur pow­
der (sulfur sublimed, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, 
Japan) and mesoporous carbon (Cnovel®, Toyo Tanso Co., Ltd., Osaka, 
Japan) with an average pore diameter of 5 nm were mixed in a weight 
ratio of 65:35 (Sulfur:Carbon). The mixture was sealed in a vessel and 
heated at 155 ◦C for 6 h to obtain the C-S composite, following the 
method described by Takahashi et al. [5]. The morphology and pore 
distribution of the pristine mesoporous Cnovel®, as well as the thermal 
properties of the synthesized C-S composite, are presented in Figure S1 
(a-c).
2.2. Morphological and elemental characterization
The surface morphology of the prepared C-S composite was observed 
using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Flex SEM 1000, Hitachi High- 
Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). To investigate morphological changes dur­
ing cycling, the cathode and anode were harvested from the cell and 
analyzed via SEM. Elemental distribution within the C-S composite and 
the harvested electrodes was visualized using energy-dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDX). Furthermore, the fine structure of the C-S com­
posite was characterized using field-emission scanning electron micro­
scopy (FE-SEM, SU-9000, Hitachi High-Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). To 
accurately determine the surface composition distribution of the C-S 
composite, EDX analysis was performed at a low accelerating voltage of 
5 kV.
2.3. Electrolyte preparation
Two electrolytes were prepared for this study. The baseline electro­
lyte (denoted as ELDD) consisted of 1.0 M lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd., 
Osaka, Japan) dissolved in a binary solvent mixture of 1,2-dimethoxy­
ethane (DME, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Saitama, Japan) 
and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Saitama, 
Japan) (1:1 v/v). The novel electrolyte (denoted as ELC14) consisted of 
1.0 M LiTFSI dissolved in 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2-methoxyethoxy) ethane 
(C14, DAIKIN INDUSTRIES, Ltd., Osaka, Japan). The physicochemical 
properties of the prepared electrolytes, including viscosity, ionic con­
ductivity, lithium-ion transference number (tLi
+) and density at room 
temperature, are summarized in Table S1 in the Supporting Information.

acetylene black (AB, Denka Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as a conductive

and styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, JSR Corp., Tokyo, Japan) in a

2.6. Analysis of solvation structure
To elucidate the solvation structure of Li⁺ in the electrolytes, Fourier 
transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and Raman spectroscopy were 
employed. FT-IR spectra were recorded using an ALPHA2 spectrometer 
(Bruker Co., Ltd., MA, USA) equipped with an attenuated total reflection 
(ATR) accessory. Raman spectra were obtained using a Nanofinder 30® 
(Tokyo Instruments, Inc., Tokyo, Japan).
To investigate the coordination structure of polysulfides in the sol­
vents, lithium polysulfide solutions were prepared. Li₂S and elemental 
sulfur were mixed in a 1:7 molar ratio in 30 mL of DME or C14 and 
stirred for 24 h. The resulting solutions, denoted as Li₂Sₓ/DME and 
Li₂Sₓ/C14, were analyzed via FT-IR.
2.7. Computational details
Quantum chemical calculations were performed to estimate the 
solvation structure of Li⁺ and the thermodynamic interactions between 
the solvents and polysulfides. The Conductor-like Screening Model for 
Real Solvents (COSMO-RS) method was applied using the COSMO-RS 
module (MOLSIS Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [22,23]. It should be noted that 
direct geometry optimizations of long-chain polysulfide complexes (e.g., 
Li₂S₈ with C14) for conventional binding energy calculations were 
computationally unfeasible, as the initial structures severely diverged 
and failed to form stable coordination complexes in the simulated 
environments.
Therefore, the probability distribution of the surface charge density, 
known as the σ-profile (P(σ)), was utilized as an alternative, robust 
thermodynamic descriptor to evaluate the interaction potential based on 
the intrinsic molecular charge distributions, bypassing the convergence 
issues associated with direct cluster optimization. In the COSMO-RS 
approach, the molecular state in solution is approximated using a 
conductor screening model to determine the stereostructure and surface 
charge density (σ). The molecular surface is divided into segments, and 
the interaction energy is calculated assuming contact between these 
segments. The σ-profile was derived by integrating the surface segment 
areas having a specific charge density σ. The coordination structure of 
the solvent around Li⁺ was estimated based on the obtained σ-profiles 
and interaction energies.
2.8. Mechanism analysis
To clarify the charge-discharge reaction mechanism in the Li-S bat­
teries using the fluorinated ether solvent, the surface compositions of the 
sulfur cathode and lithium anode at various states of charge (SOC) were 
analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI Quantes, 
ULVAC-PHI Inc., Kanagawa, Japan). Depth profiling was performed 
during XPS measurements using an argon gas cluster ion beam (GCIB) to 
minimize etching damage. The binding energies were calibrated refer­
encing the C–H bond peak in the C 1 s spectrum at 284.8 eV.
Furthermore, the relationship between the SOC of the sulfur cathode 
and its electronic structure was investigated using X-ray absorption fine 
structure (XAFS). Measurements were conducted at BL-11 of the Ritsu­
meikan University Synchrotron Radiation Center (Japan) using a 
variable-line-spacing plane grating monochromator. Data were 
collected in both partial fluorescence yield (PFY) and total electron yield 
(TEY) modes. For post-mortem analyses such as SEM, XPS, and XANES, 
the cycled cells were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. The elec­
trodes were thoroughly rinsed with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and then 
dried at room temperature under reduced pressure for 12 h to remove 
any volatile residual solvents and electrolyte salts. The obtained X-ray 
absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra were analyzed using the 
Athena software package [24].
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Solvation structure in C14-based electrolytes
To elucidate the solvation structures of Li⁺ ions in the ELC14 and 
ELDD electrolytes, FT-IR and Raman spectroscopy measurements were 
performed on LiTFSI/C14 and LiTFSI/DME solutions with varying salt 
concentrations.
Fig. 1(a) and 1(b) show the IR spectra of LiTFSI/DME and LiTFSI/ 
C14 solutions with concentrations of 0, 0.5, and 1.0 mol l⁻¹. In the 
spectrum of pure DME (Fig. 1(a)), a peak at roughly 1100 cm⁻¹, corre­
sponding to the C–O-C asymmetric stretching vibration, was observed. 
Upon adding LiTFSI (0.5 and 1.0 mol l⁻¹), the intensity of this peak 
decreased, while new peaks appeared at approximately 1080 and 1140 
cm⁻¹. These new peaks, which are absent in pure DME, are attributed to 
DME molecules coordinated to Li⁺. This suggests that the bonding state 
of the C–O-C group changes upon coordination. Furthermore, in the 
LiTFSI/DME spectra, a peak attributed to C–H bending vibrations 
appeared at roughly 870 cm⁻¹, while the peak intensity at roughly 850 
cm⁻¹ decreased as the LiTFSI concentration increased. It has been re­
ported that the spectral shape in this region depends on the conforma­
tion of the diether structure [25,26]. The variations in peak intensities at 
870, 850, and 820 cm⁻¹ with solution concentration suggest that DME 
undergoes a conformational change upon coordinating with Li⁺.
Fig. 1(b) presents the IR spectra of the LiTFSI/C14 solutions. A peak 
at approximately 1070 cm⁻¹ was observed to increase in intensity with 
increasing electrolyte concentration. This peak is attributed to the S-N-S 
group contained in the LiTFSI electrolyte salt [27]. Furthermore, similar 
to the DME system, the peak at roughly 1100 cm⁻¹ (C–O-C asymmetric 
stretching) decreased in intensity as the solution concentration 
increased, suggesting that the C–O-C structure in C14 coordinates to 
Li⁺. However, unlike DME, the spectral shape in the 850–870 cm⁻¹ region 
remained almost unchanged regardless of the solution concentration. 
This result suggests that the conformation of C14 does not change 
significantly when coordinating to Li⁺ [28]. From these results, it is 
considered that while C14 has a solvation structure similar to DME, its 
coordination geometry with Li⁺ differs from that of DME.
Fig. 1(c) shows the Raman spectra of DME and LiTFSI/DME solu­
tions. The peak intensity at roughly 870 cm⁻¹ increased with increasing 
solution concentration. Although a similar trend was confirmed in the IR 
spectra, the relationship between electrolyte concentration and peak 
intensity was found to be much clearer in the Raman spectra than in the 
IR spectra. These concentration-dependent changes in peak intensity 
likely arise from conformational changes rather than shifts in polarity 
[29].

charging to the anode surface [30]. On the other hand, the Li-SELC14

such a plateau. The high reversibility observed in Li-SELC14 suggests 
that the dissolution of Li₂Sₓ is effectively suppressed.
The mechanism of Li₂Sₓ dissolution has been reported to involve the 
coordination of solvent molecules to Li⁺ within the Li₂Sₓ structure, which 
induces ionization and leaching into the electrolyte [31,32]. Specif­
ically, DME coordinates with Li⁺ by adopting tgt, tgg, and tgg’ confor­
mations, thereby facilitating the dissociation of Li₂Sₓ. However, as 
suggested by the spectroscopic results in Section 3.1, C14 has a lower 
tendency to coordinate with Li⁺. Consequently, it is inferred that C14 is 
sterically or electronically hindered from coordinating with Li₂Sₓ, which 
prevents the dissolution of polysulfides into the electrolyte. Finally, 
considering the practical energy density discussed in the Introduction, 
the density of the electrolyte and the achievable E/S ratio are critical 
parameters. The measured densities of the prepared electrolytes are 
summarized in Table S1. While the density of ELC14 is higher than that 
of the conventional DME-based ELDD, it remains lower than those of 
other typical shuttle-suppressing systems such as carbonate-based 
electrolytes or HCEs. Furthermore, the fundamental suppression of 
polysulfide dissolution demonstrated in this study addresses the primary 
cause of continuous electrolyte consumption. Theoretically, minimizing 
these irreversible side reactions is a prerequisite for reducing the 
required electrolyte volume in practical cells. Therefore, the C14-based 
electrolyte provides a promising conceptual pathway toward achieving 
high-energy-density Li-S batteries by combining a relatively low-density 
profile with the inherent capability to suppress polysulfide leaching 
without relying on excessively high salt concentrations. The shift in the 
discharge plateau potentials between the two electrolytes indicates a 
fundamental difference in the reaction intermediates due to the distinct 
solvation capabilities of the solvents. To further investigate the elec­
trochemical reaction processes, the corresponding dQ/dV profiles are 
provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S3). The dQ/dV profile 
of the ELDD cell exhibits broad and relatively fluctuating reduction 
peaks. This profile suggests that the dissolution of polysulfides leads to 
the coexistence of multiple intermediate states in the vicinity of the 
electrode, resulting in a complex overlap of electrochemical reactions. 
Conversely, the ELC14 cell displays noticeably sharper peaks. This 
characteristic indicates that the effective suppression of polysulfide 
dissolution in the weakly solvating C14 prevents such complexity, 
allowing a more uniform, single electrochemical reaction to proceed at 
the specific reaction potential. To further evaluate the practical aspects 
of the weakly solvating concept, the cycle performance, Coulombic ef­
ficiency (calculated as charge capacity divided by discharge capacity), 
and rate capability of the ELC14 cell are summarized in Figure S3(a). It 
should be noted that the conventional ELDD cell suffers from a severe 
continuous shuttle effect under these testing conditions, making it 
difficult to maintain normal reversible cycling. In contrast, the ELC14 
cell successfully completes the charging process and enables continuous 
cycling, demonstrating that the intrinsic weakly solvating nature of C14 
effectively suppresses polysulfide dissolution.
It is noteworthy that the C14-based electrolyte possesses an ionic 
conductivity (0.111 S m⁻¹) that is more than one order of magnitude 
lower than that of the conventional DME-based electrolyte (1.277 S 
m⁻¹), alongside a higher viscosity (Table S1). Generally, such sluggish 
ion transport properties would severely restrict the rate performance of 
the cell. However, despite these apparent transport bottlenecks, the 
ELC14 cell exhibits a relatively high rate capability, maintaining sig­
nificant reversible capacity even at elevated C-rates (Figure S3). This 
unexpectedly favorable rate performance can be attributed to the 
remarkably high tLi
+ of 0.599 determined via PFG-NMR. In conventional 
DME-based systems, tLi
+ values typically range from 0.2 to 0.4 due to 
strong chelation [33,34]. A high transference number effectively 
mitigates the development of severe salt concentration gradients (con­
centration polarization) near the electrode surface during high-current 
operation. Therefore, the highly efficient cation transport (high tLi
+) in 
the weakly solvating C14 system successfully compensates for its low 
absolute ionic conductivity, enabling uniform redox reactions and sus­
taining electrochemical activity under increased current densities. Over 
50 cycles, a gradual capacity fade is observed, and the Coulombic effi­
ciency stabilizes at approximately 106%. The Coulombic efficiency 
exceeding 100% indicates that the charge capacity is slightly larger than 
the discharge capacity, suggesting the presence of minor parasitic 
reactions-such as a slight residual shuttle effect or oxidative decompo­
sition of the electrolyte-during the charging process. Furthermore, the 
strong thermodynamic confinement of polysulfides in C14 forces the 
redox reaction into a sluggish, undissolved solid-state conversion. The 
observed capacity decay is likely a consequence of these kinetic limi­
tations of the un-dissolved intermediates combined with the continuous 
minor side reactions. Further cell engineering to address these kinetic 
and interfacial challenges represents an important direction for future 
research.
3.3. Coordination structure of solvents with Li₂Sₓ
Fig. 3(a) displays photographs of the Li₂Sₓ/DME and Li₂Sₓ/C14 
mixtures prepared to investigate the solvation interaction between the 
solvents and lithium polysulfides. The Li₂Sₓ/DME mixture was observed 
to be a transparent, reddish-brown solution. This distinct coloration is 
attributed to the formation of soluble polysulfide species solvated by 
DME, indicating high solubility. In contrast, the Li₂Sₓ/C14 mixture 
appeared as a yellow suspension, suggesting that Li₂Sₓ is sparingly sol­
uble in the C14 solvent.
To further elucidate the structural changes of the solvents in these 
mixtures, FT-IR spectra were recorded, as shown in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c). 
As shown in Fig. 3(b), in the IR spectra of Li₂Sₓ/DME, the intensity of the 
peak at approximately 1100 cm⁻¹ decreased with increasing Li₂Sₓ con­
centration, while the shoulder peak at approximately 1080 cm⁻¹ inten­
sified. The peak at ~1100 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the C–O-C asymmetric 
stretching of free DME, whereas the peak at ~1080 cm⁻¹ is attributed to 
the C–O-C stretching of DME coordinated to Li⁺. These spectral changes 
indicate that the ether oxygen atoms of DME actively coordinate to the 
lithium ions in the polysulfides, facilitating their dissolution.
Conversely, the IR spectra of Li₂Sₓ/C14 (Fig. 3(c)) exhibited virtually 
identical profiles regardless of the Li₂Sₓ concentration. The absence of 
significant spectral shifts or intensity changes in the C–O-C stretching 
region implies that C14 has a significantly lower tendency to coordinate 
with Li₂Sₓ compared to DME. This lack of coordination prevents the 
solvation and subsequent dissolution of polysulfides, consistent with the 
visual observation of the suspension.
3.4. Computational analysis of Li-solvent interaction via COSMO-RS
The experimental results from the FT-IR analysis of Li₂Sₓ solutions 
revealed a distinct difference in the solubility of Li₂Sₓ depending on the 
solvent species. We hypothesized that this disparity stems from the 
difference in the interaction strength between the solvent molecules and 
the Li⁺ ions within the Li₂Sₓ structure. To verify this hypothesis, the 
surface charge density distributions of DME and C14 were calculated 
using the COSMO-RS method.
Fig. 4(a) presents the calculated σ-profiles (probability distribution 
of surface charge density), while Fig. 4(b) and 4(c) visualize the surface 
charge density distributions on the molecular surfaces of DME and C14, 
respectively.
As shown in the σ-profiles (Fig. 4(a)), DME exhibits a broader dis­
tribution in the region of high charge density (σ ≥0.01 e Å⁻²) compared 
to C14. In the molecular surface visualizations (Fig. 4(b) and 4(c)), the 
red-colored regions correspond to these areas of high surface charge 
density (σ ≥0.01).

For DME (Fig. 4(b)), it was observed that the charge density on the 
surfaces of the two oxygen atoms is significantly higher than that on 
other atoms. Similarly, C14 (Fig. 4(c)) also exhibits high charge density 
on its oxygen atoms. However, the total surface area corresponding to 
these high charge density regions is notably smaller in C14 compared to 
DME. This reduction is attributed to the electron-withdrawing effect of 
the trifluoromethyl group (-CF₃), which causes the delocalization of 
surface charge across the C14 molecule.
Based on these findings, the reduced solvation capability of C14 to­
ward Li₂Sₓ compared to DME is ascribed to two primary factors: (1) the 
difficulty in adopting the cis-conformation due to the steric hindrance of 
the -CF₃ group (as discussed in Section 3.1), and (2) the delocalization of 
molecular surface charge induced by the electron-withdrawing -CF₃ 
group. These characteristics suggest that employing C14 as a solvent for 
Li-S battery electrolytes is an effective strategy to suppress Li₂Sₓ disso­
lution and mitigate the shuttle effect.
3.5. Ex-situ morphological analysis during cycling
To elucidate the differences in the charge-discharge reaction mech­
anisms between the Li-SELDD and Li-SELC14 systems, ex-situ FE-SEM 
observations were performed on cathodes harvested at various states of 
charge (SOC). The sampling points are indicated by square (■) and 
triangular (▴) symbols in the charge-discharge profiles shown in Fig. 5 
(a) and 5(b). FE-SEM images of the pristine C-S cathode are shown in 
Fig. 5(c) (corresponding to points ■1 and ▴1).
At 15% depth of discharge (DOD) (Points ■2 and ▴2), the surface 
morphologies of the cathodes in both Li-SELDD (Fig. 5(c)) and Li- 
SELC14 (Fig. 5(h)) appeared similar to that of the pristine electrode. 
However, at 50% DOD, a distinct difference emerged. Particulate pre­
cipitates with a diameter of approximately 50 nm were formed on the 
surface of the Li-SELDD cathode (Point ■3, Fig. 5(d)). EDX analysis 
detected high concentrations of sulfur and oxygen from these pre­
cipitates (Figure S4), suggesting they are decomposition products of the 
LiTFSI electrolyte salt. In contrast, the surface of the Li-SELC14 cathode 
at 50% DOD (Point ▴3, Fig. 5(i)) remained comparable to the pristine 
state, indicating superior interfacial stability against electrolyte 
decomposition.
At 100% DOD (Points ■4 and ▴4), the surfaces of both cathodes 
(Fig. 5(e) and 5(j)) were covered with precipitates. EDX analysis of the 
Li-SELDD surface revealed an oxygen- and sulfur-rich composition with 
reduced carbon signal compared to the 50% DOD state. This indicates 
the formation of discharge products, likely Li₂Sₓ and Li₂S. Interestingly, 
for Li-SELC14, while the precipitate-covered regions were rich in sulfur 
and oxygen, the exposed regions showed higher carbon signals 
(Figure S4).
During the subsequent charging process, at 50% SOC (Points ■5 and 
▴5), the precipitates observed after discharge disappeared from the 
surfaces of both electrodes (Fig. 5(f) and 5(k)). This disappearance in­
dicates the oxidation of solid discharge products into soluble species or 
elemental sulfur. Finally, at 100% SOC (Points ■6 and ▴6), the surface 
morphologies of both electrodes (Fig. 5(g) and 5(l)) reverted to a state 
similar to the pristine electrode. However, semi-quantitative EDX 
analysis revealed that the peak intensity ratio of sulfur to carbon (S/C) 
on the Li-SELC14 cathode was significantly higher than that on the Li- 
SELDD cathode (Figure S5). This higher sulfur retention implies that 
the elution of long-chain Li₂Sₓ formed during the latter stages of 
charging was effectively suppressed by the C14 solvent.

Fig. 7(a-d) present the S K-edge XANES spectra obtained via TEY and 
PFY. Guo et al. predicted the XANES spectra based on the composition 
and structure of Li₂Sₓ using Density Functional Theory (DFT) calcula­
tions [36–39]. Based on these theoretical results, the spectral peaks 
observed in this study were assigned as follows.
For Li-SELDD (Fig. 7(a) and 7(b)), a peak assigned to Li₂S₄ (~2482 
eV) appeared at 15% DOD and subsequently decreased, while the Li₂S₂ 
peak (~2478.2 eV) increased. Notably, at 50% and 100% DOD, a pre- 
edge feature appeared at 2470.3 eV. To accurately interpret this 
feature, it is crucial to note that our XANES measurements were per­
formed on the undissolved solid cathode. According to theoretical and 
experimental XANES studies [33,40–42], this pre-edge feature origi­
nates from the creation of unoccupied orbitals on sulfur atoms when Li⁺ 
ions are dissociated or extracted. In the ELDD system, the strong coor­
dinating ability of DME heavily solvates the Li⁺ ions, which is thought to 
effectively extract them from the solid Li₂S₄ on the cathode, creating 
Li-site vacancies (defects). Consequently, the remaining solid poly­
sulfides with these Li-defects are considered to exhibit an electronic state 
analogous to that of solvated polysulfides in the liquid phase, where the 
disruption of local coordination generates unoccupied orbitals on the 
sulfur atoms, manifesting as the pre-edge peak at 2470.3 eV. Addition­
ally, the absorption edge attributed to S₈ at 2471.8 eV was confirmed 
even in the DOD 100% spectrum, indicating incomplete reduction. 
Furthermore, the intensity of the pre-edge at 2470.3 eV was higher at 
100% DOD compared to 50% DOD. This result suggests that the number 
of defects in Li₂S₄ contained in the cathode at 100% DOD increased 
compared to that at 50% DOD. During charging, Li₂S₄ re-formed but 
persisted even at the end of charge, further confirming the shuttle effect.
In contrast, for the Li-SELC14 system (Fig. 7(c) and (d)), during the 
discharge process from 50% to 100% DOD, the intensities of peaks 
assigned to Li₂S₄ and Li₂S₃ decreased, while those assigned to Li₂S₂ and 
Li₂S increased. During the subsequent charging process from 100% DOD 
to 50% SOC, the peak intensities for Li₂S and Li₂S₃ decreased, whereas 
the peak for Li₂S₂ increased. From 50% SOC to the end of charge, the 
peaks assigned to Li₂S₂, Li₂S₃, and Li₂S₄ decreased, and the intensity of 
the peak assigned to S₈ increased. The spectral shape at the end of charge 
was confirmed to be similar to that at 15% DOD.
Notably, the pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV was absent in the Li- 
SELC14 spectra throughout the charge-discharge process. This absence 
strongly suggests that the weakly solvating C14 solvent does not 
strongly interact with the Li⁺ ions in the polysulfides. Thus, it is 
considered that C14 cannot extract Li⁺ to create vacancies, effectively 
maintaining the polysulfides in an undissolved solid state without 
generating such unoccupied sulfur orbitals. This finding is consistent 
with the COSMO-RS and IR results discussed previously, which showed 
that the delocalized surface charge and steric hindrance of C14 prevent 
effective coordination with Li₂Sₓ.
Additionally, in both systems, the ratio of Li₂Sₓ peaks to S₈ peaks was 
lower in the PFY spectra compared to the TEY spectra. This discrepancy 
suggests that unreacted sulfur remains in the bulk (several µm depth) of 
the C-S particles even when the surface has reacted. It implies that the 
electrochemical reaction proceeds more readily at the particle surface 
than in the bulk, which explains why the practical specific capacities of 
Li-SELDD (1200 mAh g⁻¹) and Li-SELC14 (1000 mAh g⁻¹) are lower than 
the theoretical maximum (1672 mAh g⁻¹).
Based on these findings, the proposed reaction mechanism models 
for the initial cycle are summarized in Fig. 8(a) and (b).

pressed shuttle effect. This superior electrochemical performance is

Mechanistic investigations using COSMO-RS calculations revealed 
that the surface charge density of the C14 molecule is significantly more 
delocalized compared to that of DME. Based on this finding, we hy­
pothesized that solvents with low, delocalized surface charge density, 
such as C14, inhibit the ionization of Li₂Sₓ and its subsequent leaching 
into the electrolyte.
To validate this hypothesis, the evolution of the electronic structure 
of the sulfur cathode during the charge-discharge process was probed 
via XAS. In the Li-SELDD system, a pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV, 
assigned to lithium defects in the polysulfide structure, was observed in 
the S K-edge XANES spectra at DODs of 50% and greater. These defects 
are interpreted as the result of Li₂Sₓ ionization induced by the strong 
coordination of the solvent. In stark contrast, this pre-edge feature was 
notably absent in the Li-SELC14 spectra at all depths of discharge, 
indicating that the ionization of Li₂Sₓ is effectively suppressed in the C14 
environment.
In conclusion, the C14 solvent effectively mitigates the ionization 
and dissolution of Li₂Sₓ due to its delocalized surface charge and steric 
properties, thereby enabling the stable and reversible operation of Li-S 
batteries. More broadly, this work highlights that molecular design 
focusing on charge delocalization in fluorinated ethers serves as a highly 
effective and generalizable strategy for developing advanced electro­
lytes in next-generation Li-S batteries.
