A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have emerged as promising alternatives to replace Li-ion technology in light-
weight applications, but they still face some important challenges that hinder their commercialization. To
overcome them, several optimization strategies focusing on each battery component have been investigated. In
this work, we have explored the symbiotic combination of an optimized high-sulfur loading graphene-containing
cathode with a novel sparingly solvating electrolyte (SSE) consisting of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) as solvent and
1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (OCTO) as diluent, at an E/S ratio of 7 μL mg−1. The
impact of graphene incorporation into the cathode formulation and the physicochemical compatibility between
cathode and electrolyte have been evaluated and compared to those obtained for the benchmarking DME/DOL
electrolyte. Using the DOL/OCTO SSE enhanced wettability over our graphene-containing electrodes and
hampered significant polysulfide dissolution. Most importantly, the electrochemistry of this system showed very
promising values at coin cell level, achieving areal discharge capacities of 5.3 mAh cm−2 at C/10, enduring
beyond 100 cycles with 65 % capacity retention. The transferability of the system to prototype cells was suc-
cessfully demonstrated by assembling a monolayer pouch cell which reached initial capacities of 55 mAh and
lasted more than 60 cycles, paving the way for real deployment and commercialization of this energy storage
technology.
1. Introduction
The ever-increasing global energy demand requires alternatives to
the existing electrochemical energy storage (EES) technologies whilst
considering sustainability. Since their commercialization in the early
1990s, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have constituted the most common
system for small portable electronics due to their high energy density
and low self-discharge [1]. Nevertheless, their theoretical limit is
currently being reached and it is insufficient for their implementation in
some demanding areas, such as heavy-duty transportation or stationary
grid storage [2,3]. Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have gained attention
as potential alternatives to LIBs in the last decade, due to the outstanding
specific capacity of sulfur (1675 mAh g−1) and the theoretical high
gravimetric energy density of LSBs (2600 Wh kg−1) [4], which makes
them a suitable choice for lightweight applications such as satellites and
drones [5,6]. Besides, lithium-sulfur technology is promising in
next-generation batteries due to the inherent advantages of using sulfur
as an active material, such as low cost, wide availability, and environ-
mental friendliness [7]. The redox processes that occur in lithium-sulfur
batteries are the following:

The sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) happening in the discharge
process is a multi-step process where solid sulfur reacts with lithium ions
and is transformed into soluble lithium polysulfides, which subsequently
continue reacting with more lithium ions, and finally a liquid-to-solid
reaction occurs to form lithium disulfide (Li2S2) and lithium sulfide
(Li2S).
Nevertheless, commercialization of LSBs is still hindered due to the
intrinsic challenges related to this technology [8–10]. The majority of
them arise on the cathode side, such as the insulating nature of sulfur
and lithium sulfide, the high-volume expansion undergone by the
cathode while discharging [11], and the polysulfide shuttle effect [12,
13]. The latter consists of the diffusion of the soluble intermediate
species (lithium polysulfides, LiPS) through the electrolyte towards the
lithium metal anode (LMA), causing the loss of active material in the
cathode and leading to parasitic reactions with the anode, which could
eventually suffer corrosion with subsequent battery failure. To confront
these challenges, academia has commonly focused on exploring novel
materials for the positive electrode, often overlooking the rest of the cell
components, or selecting non-realistic compositional and operational
parameters for the batteries [14,15], thus rendering the efforts hollow in
terms of their practical application.
Most of the approaches related to tackling the cathode side chal-
lenges comprise the design of a carbonaceous matrix that can host sulfur
particles whilst confining the liquid lithium polysulfides within this
region [16]. Ideally, an optimum carbonaceous sulfur host must exhibit
a high specific surface area, adequate pore size distribution, and tailored
surface chemistry to enhance its compatibility with the lithium poly-
sulfides and keep them anchored to its surface [17], besides high elec-
tronic conductivity for achieving a good electron pathway to enhance
redox kinetics [18]. In practice, a compromise between these properties
is set, as some of them cannot be maximized simultaneously. There is a
plethora of strategies used in literature to find a suitable cathode design
based on different carbon materials [19], most of them involve the use of
different carbon nanostructures [20–22] (hollow or yolk-shell spheres,
carbon nanotubes, carbon nanofibers, graphene derivatives, activated
carbons) either or both as the carbonaceous host [23] and/or as addi-
tives [24] for redox catalysis, as well as the addition or modification of
these carbons with electrocatalysts [25–27] (metal oxides, sulfides, and
nitrides [28,29], single-atom catalysts [30–32] or heteroatom dopants
[33]). These catalysts decrease the energy potential barriers of the
sluggish sulfur evolution and reduction reactions [34], in particular the
liquid-to-solid Li2S4 conversion into Li2S2 and Li2S, which is found to be
the rate-determining step in the discharge process [35,36], and analo-
gously, the solid-to-liquid conversion of Li2S into soluble polysulfides in
the charge process [37], partially mitigating the polysulfide shuttle by
accelerating the conversion of these species.
However, though effective, these advances in the positive electrode
do not lead to a substantial system improvement due to the limited
cyclability exhibited when realistic conditions are applied. Thus, the
development of novel electrolytes that improve cell cyclability due to
their compatibility with the rest of the cell components is essential. In
this regard, one of the most recent trends consists of the use of sparingly
solvating electrolytes (SSE, also known as localized high-concentration
electrolytes) [38]. This family of electrolytes outstands in Li–S tech-
nology not only because they exhibit a distinctive solvation structure
and notable physicochemical properties that facilitate precise regulation
of lithium deposition, but also because they mitigate the LiPS shuttle
effect. SSEs are formulated based on the concept of highly concentrated
electrolytes (HCEs), wherein an increased salt concentration reduces the
presence of free solvent molecules, significantly enhancing stability
against the LMA [39,40]. Nevertheless, the application of HCEs in bat-
teries is impeded by challenges such as high viscosity and poor wetta-
bility over the cathode. Addressing these issues, the incorporation of
hydrofluoroethers (HFEs) as diluents proves beneficial. HFEs are used as
diluents due to their miscibility with ether-based solvents and their
incapacity of dissolving polysulfides due to their low donor numbers,
inhibiting the shuttle effect [41]. Besides, HFEs have an excellent

Considering these strategies for improving cathode and electrolyte,
in our previous research, we studied the combination of our homemade
ResFArGO, a 2D graphene-based activated carbon, and a sparingly-
solvating electrolyte. This system led to the development of prototype
pouch cells with outstanding cycling performances [49]. Still, avoiding
the need for the carbon activation process could reduce the amount of
time and materials used, which is critical for scaling up. In this work, a
direct combination of a reduced graphene oxide (rGO) with a
commercially available carbon (Ketjenblack, KB) was the chosen
approach to avoid activated carbons. KB is a commercially available
carbon black used as sulfur host due to its adequate microstructure and
electrochemical performance compared to other carbon materials
[50–52], and its modifications have provided impressive results, both
via microstructural engineering [53]) or by combining it with other
synthetic carbons [24,54] or compounds [55].
In the KB-rGO composites, KB would provide the microporous
structure to host large amounts of sulfur particles and rGO sheets would
contribute to improving the mechanical properties of the laminates,
high conductivity, and particle interconnection to enhance redox ki-
netics. Previous results on similar systems validate this hypothesis
[56–58], where good electrochemical performances were achieved.
Nevertheless, the non-practical conditions (excessive E/S ratio, low
sulfur loadings) undermined the practical applicability of this approach
[59,60]. Here, these composites are manufactured with a focus on
achieving cathodes with high sulfur loadings that are apt for prototype
cell manufacture.
To evaluate the suitability of this approach, the physicochemical
compatibility and electrochemical performance of these composite
cathodes were tested with both a state-of-the-art (SotA) electrolyte and a
novel SSE in LSBs. The SotA electrolyte comprised the classic ether
mixture between 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL),
using lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the main
salt and LiNO3 as an additive. Regarding the SSE, DOL was used as a
solvent for the LiTFSI salt and 1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2-tetra-
fluoroethyl ether (OCTO) was the selected diluent. OCTO is an HFE that
recently showed good compatibility with LMA in lithium-metal batteries
[61], but its potential applicability in LSBs has been barely tested [62].
The aim of this work is therefore to explore the synergistic effect be-
tween the improved sulfur activation and mechanical integrity of the
KB-rGO cathodes and the weak solubility of LiPS in the novel
DOL/OCTO electrolyte. The combination of both tailored cell compo-
nents would confine LiPS within the cathode, maximizing sulfur utili-
zation, reducing the loss of active material, and extending cell
cyclability. Finally, to demonstrate the validity of this approach, a
prototype 20 cm2 pouch cell is manufactured and successfully tested.
2. Experimental section
2.1. KB-rGO composite manufacture
KB-rGO composites containing 10, 20, and 30 wt% of rGO (herein-
after denoted KB-0.1rGO, KB-0.2rGO, and KB-0.3rGO, respectively)
were synthesized following a facile route. Different ratios of commercial
Ketjenblack® EC-600JD powder (KB, Nouryon), 50 mL of graphene
oxide (GO) water dispersion (4 mg mL−1, Graphenea), and 50 mL of
distilled water were thoroughly mixed using an IKA overhead stirrer and

2.2. S@KB-rGO positive electrode manufacture
For the sulfur-positive electrode manufacture, the water-based slurry
composition followed our recent works [24], with 64 wt% sulfur (S8,
>99 %, Sigma Aldrich) and 26 wt% of the as-prepared KB-rGO com-
posites. As binders, 5 wt% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt (CMC,
Sigma-Aldrich) and 5 wt% styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, Jingrui
Electronic Materials) were used. First, a melt diffusion process between
sulfur and KB-rGO was carried out at 155 ◦C for 12 h under an argon
atmosphere to infiltrate sulfur particles within the carbon pores
(S@KB-rGO). Regarding the slurry manufacture, CMC was first dis-
solved in distilled H2O, and then, S@KB-rGO was added to the solution
and mixed using an IKA overhead stirrer. Afterward, SBR was added to

cell long-cycling tests carried out at a fixed C/10 rate.
2.3. Electrolyte preparation
The electrochemical tests were performed using two different types
of electrolytes, as explained in the introduction. As the SotA electrolyte,
a 0.5 M LiTFSI (99 %, Thermo Scientific) and 0.5 M LiNO3 (99.99 %,
Sigma Aldrich) solution in a 1:1 vol mixture of DME (99+ %, Thermo
Scientific) and DOL (99.8 %, Thermo Scientific) was used as the refer-
ence electrolyte (subsequently simply referred to as DME/DOL). The SSE
comprised a LiTFSI solution in DOL, and OCTO (>98 %, Apollo Scien-
tific) was added as a diluent (1:1 vol) to obtain the final 1.5 M LiTFSI
concentration in the SSE, subsequently referred to as DOL/OCTO.
2.4. Physicochemical characterization
The elemental composition of as-developed carbon composites was
determined using elemental analysis (EA) technique in a CHNS/O Flash
2000 analyzer (Thermo Scientific). The microstructure of the powder
and the electrodes was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) with a FEI Quanta 200 FEG high-resolution microscope (Thermo
Fisher) at 10 kV acceleration voltage and 10 mm working distance. An
Apollo 10 energy-dispersive X-ray spectrophotometer (EDX) was
coupled to the SEM for compositional analysis. Nitrogen adsorption/
desorption isotherms were measured in an ASAP 2460 analyzer
(Micrometrics) at −196 ◦C, degassing the carbon powders beforehand at
250 ◦C for 12 h under vacuum. The specific surface area was calculated
by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method, and the pore size distri-
bution (PSD) was obtained from the desorption branch of the nitrogen
isotherms applying the 2D-NLDFT model with the SAEIUS software. The
polysulfide retention of the carbon materials was evaluated by mixing
10 mg of powder with 2 mL of a 5 mM Li2S6 solution in DME. This so-
lution was prepared by mixing stoichiometric quantities of solid S8 (>99
%, Sigma Aldrich) and Li2S (99.98 %, Sigma Aldrich) in DME and
magnetically stirring for 72 h. A 5 mM Li2S6 solution was prepared
analogously using OCTO as a solvent to evaluate its polysulfide
dissolution ability. Both solutions were filtered using a 0.45 μm PTFE
filter syringe. The compatibility between the two electrolytes and the
electrodes manufactured with different carbons was evaluated through a
wettability test by measuring the contact angle of a 3 μL electrolyte drop
over each electrode. These measurements were carried out at RT with a
LSA100 surface analyzer (Lauda Scientific). The drop deposition was
recorded at 90 fps and the images used to measure the contact angle
correspond to 0.011 s after deposition.
2.5. Electrochemical characterization
To study the influence of rGO content on the electronic conductivity
of the as-developed composites, sulfur-free laminates were also cast
following the procedure described above and keeping the carbon-to-
binder ratio at 2.6:1 wt. The electronic conductivity at room tempera-
ture of the full carbon KB-rGO laminates was calculated by measuring
the composite volume resistivity with a 2460 multipoint probe analyzer
(Hioki).
The ionic conductivity of the DME/DOL and DOL/OCTO electrolytes
was measured from the internal resistance of a 2-pole platinum con-
ductivity cell (CDC641T, Radiometer Analytical) immersed in each
electrolyte at room temperature in the 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz frequency range.
The electrochemical stability of the two electrolytes at low voltages was
measured by cyclic voltammetry (CV) using a BioLogic SAS VMP3
multichannel potentiostat. These tests were carried out in 2032-coin
cells using a Li||Cu configuration and Celgard® 2500 separator soaked
in 60 μL of electrolyte. Cells were cycled from OCV (around 2.75 V) to
−0.2 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a 1 mV s−1 scan rate, and then back to OCV.
Lithium-sulfur 2032-coin cells were assembled inside an argon-filled
glove box, using a 13 mm diameter S@KB-rGO electrode as the cathode,
a 500 μm thickness Li0 disk (China Energy Lithium) as the anode, and a
Celgard® 2500 membrane as a separator between them. The electrolyte-
to-sulfur ratio (E/S) was kept in all cases at 7 μL mg−1. EIS tests were
carried out over these coin cells at OCV using a BioLogic SAS VMP3
multichannel potentiostat in a frequency range between 100 mHz and 1
MHz with 10 mV voltage amplitude. C-rate tests were performed using a
Maccor battery tester at increasing C-rates, with the first two cycles at C/
20 as a preconditioning step, then five cycles at C/5, C/2, C, and 2C,
afterward returning to C/5. At high rates, the charge was capped at C/2
to avoid degradation and to assess the maximum power output obtained
with the different cathodes. Long cycling tests were analogously per-
formed at C/10 with five preconditioning cycles at C/20. The voltage
ranges depended on the electrolyte used, cycling between 2.6 and 1.7 V
when using DME/DOL and between 3.0 and 1.2 V when using DOL/
OCTO for the initial tests, then at 2.7–1.4 V to enhance cycling stability.
To assess the self-discharge behavior of these systems, 2032 coin
cells assembled using the KB-0.3rGO cathode (sulfur loading ~ 2.5 mgS
cm−2) and the DME/DOL or DOL/OCTO electrolytes (E/S = 7 μL mg−1)
were galvanostatically cycled following this protocol: First, cells were
left resting at OCV for 24 h, then two preconditioning discharge cycles
were carried out at C/20, leaving the cells completely charged after-
ward. Cells were subsequently left resting at OCV for different times (3,
5 and 7 days), and then cycled again at C/20.
Cyclic voltammetry tests were performed using two-electrode Swa-
gelok-type cells. These were assembled inside an argon-filled glove box
with a 13 mm diameter S@KB-rGO positive electrode, a 10 mm diameter
Li0 disk punched from a foil as the anode, and a Whatman® glass fiber
(GF/A) separator soaked in DME/DOL electrolyte (E/S ratio = 33 μL
mg−1). Tests were carried out at 0.2 and 0.4 mV s−1 and voltage was
swept from OCV down to 1.7 V and then up to 2.8 V.
Prototype pouch cells were also manufactured inside an argon-filled
glove box. Medium-loading (around 3 mgS cm−2) and high-surface
(20.25 cm2) positive electrodes were used along with a 500 μm thick-
ness Li0 electrode (39 × 55 mm), with a Celgard® 2500 membrane as a
separator, keeping the E/S ratio fixed at 7 μL mg−1. Aluminum and
nickel tabs were welded to the positive and negative electrodes,

3. Results and discussion
As highlighted in the introduction, this work focuses on the perfor-
mance improvement of LSBs through the optimization of both cathode
and electrolyte components. Within this context, the combination of a
KB-rGO composite in the sulfur cathode with a novel sparingly solvating
electrolyte (DOL/OCTO) is addressed to enhance sulfur activation and
extend cycle life in a realistic proof-of-concept pouch cell prototype.
The oxygen content of the samples is responsible for an enhanced
composite polarity, which is beneficial in terms of the stability of water-
based slurries and leads to favored electrode processability. On the other
hand, an excessive oxygen content may diminish the electrical con-
ductivity of the carbon composite. Taking that into account, a balance in
the oxygen concentration in the carbon composite is required for
optimal performance. With the aim of characterizing this concentration,
a chemical analysis of KB-rGO carbon composites was therefore carried
out to elucidate the final oxygen content, which is directly dependent on
the synthesis conditions, such as the atmosphere, temperature, and time
applied for the pyrolysis process. The oxygen content on the surface of
the materials was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
and in the bulk by elemental analysis, which can lead to slight differ-
ences in the measured oxygen content between techniques. As detailed
in Table 1, it increases gradually with rGO content, reaching a maximum
for KB-0.3rGO, although the differences among the composites are not
that abrupt due to the mild thermal reducing conditions, as proven by
both EDX and EA. These oxygen values agree with the ones expected
when compared to the specifications of a commercial rGO powder
(Graphenea), which contains between 14 and 19 wt% of oxygen.
Once the chemical composition of the composites has been evalu-
ated, a microstructural analysis was carried out to further analyze the
influence of the rGO content in the carbon composites. As observed in
the SEM micrographs included in Fig. 1a, as the nominal content of rGO
is increased, the agglomerated sub-micrometric particles characteristic
of Ketjenblack® are progressively covered and interconnected by the
rGO sheets. The homogeneous coverage and intertwining of KB particles
with rGO is expected to enhance the redox kinetics in the positive
electrode by creating a favored electron pathway. Textural properties of
the carbon composites were additionally assessed by nitrogen adsorp-
tion/desorption isotherms at −196 ◦C. As seen in Fig. 1b, all the iso-
therms present a type-IV profile characteristic of mesoporous materials,
similar to those already reported for Ketjenblack® [23]. The BET spe-
cific surface area decreases as the rGO content increases in the samples,
reaching a minimum of 1126 m2 g−1 for KB-0.3rGO, in contrast to the
1383 m2 g−1 calculated for the pure KB. Despite this moderate decrease
in the specific surface area values, they are still high enough for the
composites to act as sulfur hosts in a Li–S cathode. Regarding the pore
size distribution (PSD) calculated from the isotherms, no big differences
were determined, as shown in Fig. S1. Minor differences are found be-
tween the amount of micropores and small mesopores around 4 nm,
which are key for polysulfide trapping [63], with just a slight increase
for KB-0.3rGO. Regarding large mesopores (~ 25 nm), KB-0.3rGO also
presents a lower pore volume than the rest of the carbons, which is also
beneficial in terms of polysulfide retention [64]. Nevertheless, as shown
in Fig. S2, the polysulfide retention tests do not reveal an especially
enhanced affinity of the KB-0.3rGO powder towards lithium poly-
sulfides, as proven by the negligible change in the strong-orange color of
the polysulfide solution. This similar polysulfide retention is probably
due to the increase in the LiPS affinity of the composites due to the
oxygen functional groups balancing out with the negative effect caused
by the decrease in the specific surface area in the rGO composites.
To further determine which the most suitable carbon composite was,
electrical resistivity measurements of the full carbon laminates were
carried out. The electronic conductivity of KB-rGO-based sulfur-free
laminates was measured using a multipoint probe analyzer, as shown in
Fig. S3. The conductivity at RT increased with the rGO content, reaching
a maximum value of 3.2 S cm−1 for the KB-0.3rGO sulfur-free electrodes,
thus demonstrating that the rGO sheets enhance the percolation degree
and successfully provide a suitable electron pathway along the whole
electrode. The KB-only electrode could not be measured this way due to
its poor mechanical integrity, which prevented it from electrical contact
with the analyzer, demonstrating the necessity for developing struc-
turally stable electrodes.
Once the carbon composites have been characterized, the micro-
structure and mechanical properties of the sulfur-based KB-rGO elec-
trodes were analyzed prior to electrochemical characterization. As
perceived in the SEM micrographs of Fig. 2a, there is a clear tendency in
the medium-loading electrodes (2.5 mgS cm−2), where the number of
cracks decreases drastically when the rGO content is increased, to the
point of attaining electrodes with optimum structural integrity for the
highest rGO-content composites. The same trend is observed when the
sulfur loading is increased up to 4 mgS cm−2 (Fig. 2b), where only the
KB-0.3rGO-based electrodes were mechanically acceptable for electro-
chemical testing due to the reduced number of cracks and delamination.
Thus, it has been proven that small compositional differences strongly
affect the carbon composite microstructure, which impacts the quality of
the electrodes processed. The effect of the rGO sheets is evident,
increasing the particle interconnection, and the enhanced polarity due
to the oxygen content helps to develop homogeneous aqueous-based
slurries and mechanically stable high-sulfur loading electrodes.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy conducted for Li–S coin
cells at OCV could offer additional insight into the influence of the
composites on the electrical resistance of the cells. For the sake of
comparison, normalized Nyquist plots registered for the graphene-
containing samples and the reference KB one are included in Fig. 3a.
An equivalent circuit analysis is developed for each spectrum, and the
results are summarized in Table S1. A slight trend is observed with the
addition of rGO, which lightly reduces the charge-transfer resistance of
the electrode. This effect is not extremely significant due to the limited
amount of carbon composite within the cathode composition. The
electrode resistance of graphene-containing electrodes is reduced to
around 10 Ω compared to the reference KB cathode, which is in line with
the previous electrical resistance measurements, further demonstrating
the influence of rGO on increasing the electronic conductivity of the
electrodes.

pected [65]. Differences are negligible among all the explored

As inferred from the electrical resistivity and the EIS results, the
catalytic activity of the composites would be higher with the rGO con-
tent in the positive electrode. To assess this, Li–S cells were tested at
harsh conditions of high C-rates, where the particle interconnection and
the enhanced electronic conductivity are essential for improving redox
kinetics and the differences between compositions would be more
noticeable. To electrochemically evaluate the rGO effect, galvanostatic
charge-discharge tests were conducted in medium-sulfur loading cells
(2.5 mgS cm−2) with a DME/DOL electrolyte. C-rate tests were per-
formed by cycling at successive rates of C/20, C/5, C/2, 1C, and C/5
again, keeping the fastest charge capped at C/2 to prevent anode
instability. As shown in Fig. S5, all the graphene-containing composi-
tions outperform the reference KB-only cell at all rates, being less
remarkable at 1C. To better discern the better-performing composition
at high C-rates, a long cycling test was carried out at 1C, with pre-
conditioning cycles at C/20 and C/5, keeping C/5 as a charging rate. As
shown in Fig. 4a, KB-0.3rGO-based cells outperform the rest in terms
both of discharge capacity and coulombic efficiency, reaching initial
values of 912 mAh g−1 at 1C and 99.7 % CE, whereas KB-only cells offer
just 687 mAh g−1 and 99.2 % CE. As shown in the voltage profiles of
Fig. S6, the addition of small quantities of rGO tuned the morphology of
the second discharge plateau at 1C. However, the KB-0.3rGO cell
overcame this issue while offering higher discharge capacities and lower
voltage polarization than the KB-based cell. The initial discharge ca-
pacities are higher as the rGO content increases, and this might be
explained by the particle interconnection provided by conductive rGO
sheets, which enhance redox kinetics and improve the electrochemical
cell performance even at high C-rates, with the best performance offered
by the composition with the highest rGO content.
Taking into account the improved mechanical integrity and better
electrochemical performance of KB-0.3rGO cathodes, long cycling tests
at C/10 were conducted on high-sulfur loading cathodes (4.5 mgS cm−2)
in DME/DOL with a focus on the possible upscaling of these materials.
As a reference, a much lower loading KB-only cathode (3.1 mgS cm−2)
was used, due to the severe cracking and delamination showed with a
higher-sulfur loading (Fig. 2b). As shown in Fig. 4b, outstanding
discharge capacity values are obtained for KB-0.3rGO cells, reaching
initial values of 1062 mAh g−1 at C/10 (around 4.8 mAh cm−2) with
99.6 % CE. Despite the huge sulfur loading difference, these values are
clearly better than those exhibited by the reference KB cell, thus
demonstrating the improved sulfur activation in the rGO-containing
cathode. However, KB-0.3rGO cells showed limited cyclability of
around 30 cycles due to the combination of the high sulfur loading with
the elevated solubility of lithium polysulfides in the DME/DOL elec-
trolyte. This degradation phenomenon is well known in Li–S cells with
this electrolyte using LiNO3 as an additive [66]. This salt creates a Lix-
NOy-rich SEI layer on top of the lithium metal anode, and when LiNO3 is
exhausted, the lithium metal surface is exposed and subsequently
corroded leading to cell failure. The larger the sulfur content in the
cathode, the more stress the anode suffers and the faster the cell
degrades.

tight E/S ratio and the high-sulfur loading is the Achilles’ heel of this
system. Therefore, it is of adamant importance to develop a suitable
electrolyte compatible with this KB-rGO electrode and the LMA while
inhibiting polysulfide dissolution. In the novel DOL/OCTO SSE, OCTO
was selected as a diluent because of its high fluorination degree and low
LiPS dissolution ability [63], due to its weakened solvating power that
results in a higher ratio of cation-anion aggregates (AGGs), as proven by
Wang et al. [61].
In order to confirm the weakly solvating nature of the novel elec-
trolyte, the polysulfide dissolution ability of DOL/OCTO is addressed
and compared to the one of DME/DOL. The results are shown in Fig. 5a,
where the dark-orange coloration found for DME/DOL indicates a strong
polysulfide dissolution. Aliphatic ethers such as DME with high donor
numbers increase the LiPS solubility due to their Lewis basicity [40],
which makes them not the most suitable candidate to prevent the pol-
ysulfide shuttle. In the case of DOL/OCTO, a yellowish hue is observed
in this test, which is indicative of a decreased Li2S6 presence in the
electrolyte, quite distant from that of the conventional electrolyte. This
might be due to the fact that DOL partially dissolves lithium polysulfides
[67], which could be beneficial to a certain extent for accelerating their
conversion reactions. The absorbance of both solutions was measured
after their dilution with DOL (1:50 v.), and the absorbance spectra
shown in Fig. 5b exhibit negligible signals in the DOL/OCTO LiPS
saturated solution, whereas a strong absorbance band is seen for the
DME/DOL saturated solution. This test also demonstrates that the OCTO
co-solvent impedes the polysulfide dissolution, and the combined effect
obtained between DOL and OCTO in this SSE is promising in terms of
restraining the polysulfide shuttle as well as accelerating the redox ki-
netics of the system.
To assess the suitability of this novel SSE, its ionic conductivity was
first measured (Fig. 5c). As expected, a higher conductivity of 7.1 mS
cm−1 at RT was obtained for the DME/DOL electrolyte compared to 1.4
mS cm−1 for DOL/OCTO. The reduced ionic conductivity of the latter is
intrinsic to the SSE family of compounds, where lithium-ion mobility is
restricted, and this is reflected in a lower ionic conductivity of the
electrolyte. The ionic conductivity of polysulfide-saturated electrolytes
was also measured, taking into account that the LiPS saturation point of
sparingly-solvating electrolytes is much lower that of DME/DOL elec-
trolyte as proven in literature [68]. In our case, it was found out that the
DOL/OCTO electrolyte could not dissolve polysulfide quantities higher
than 0.5 M Li2S6. On the other hand, for the DME/DOL composition, the
polysulfide saturation limit is higher, and a 6 M Li2S6 dissolution was
prepared.
The conductivity of the resulting solutions was calculated from the
impedance spectra, and the results are included in Fig. 5c. It can be
observed that the ionic conductivities decrease for the polysulfide
saturated solutions. As expected, this decrease is more pronounced in
the case of the DME/DOL solutions. At the saturation level, all of them
show a similar conductivity around 1 mS cm−1. The larger ability of
DME/DOL electrolyte to dissolve lithium polysulfides represents a
drawback for the system, whereas the limited polysulfide dissolution in
the DOL/OCTO electrolyte is beneficial for cell stability. This test shows
that DOL/OCTO electrolyte could improve the electrochemical perfor-
mance in lithium sulfur cells compared to the state-of-the-art DME/DOL
electrolyte.
The electrochemical stability of both electrolytes at low voltages is
studied using cyclic voltammetry (CV) in a Li||Cu configuration, scan-
ning from OCV to −0.2 V. Voltammograms registered for both electro-
lytes show a stable profile down to 0.5 V, as shown in Fig. 5d. This
measurement confirms the electrochemical stability of both DOL-
containing electrolytes tested in this work, where the minimum
voltage used for galvanostatic cycling is 1.2 V.
Another important aspect, as commented before, is the electrolyte
compatibility with the rest of the cell components. For that purpose, the
compatibility of the DOL/OCTO electrolyte with the KB-0.3rGO elec-
trode was evaluated and compared with the reference DME/DOL elec-
trolyte by performing wettability tests. The results depicted in Fig. 5e

Encouraged by the promising physicochemical properties of the
novel DOL/OCTO electrolyte and its compatibility with the KB-0.3rGO
electrode, the electrochemical performance of Li–S cells with a high-
sulfur loading (4.5 mgS cm−2) was addressed via galvanostatic cycling
at C/10. As shown in Fig. 6a, the results obtained with the combination
of a KB-0.3rGO cathode and a DOL/OCTO electrolyte were remarkable
in terms both of discharge capacity and cycling stability. Remarkable
initial discharge capacity values at C/10 of over 5.3 mAh cm−2 (around
1200 mAh g−1) are obtained with the DOL/OCTO electrolyte, besides a
stable cycling performance with a 65 % capacity retention after 100
cycles, averaging 96.3 % CE. Regarding the previously discussed results
achieved with DME/DOL, DOL/OCTO clearly outperforms the overall
behavior of the classic ether-electrolyte offering higher discharge ca-
pacities and more stable electrochemical performance due to the
excellent compatibility with the electrode and the reduced shuttle effect
previously demonstrated.
The charge-discharge voltage profiles shown in Fig. 6b and 6c are
initially quite similar for both electrolytes, showing the characteristic
two-plateau mechanism exhibited by liquid electrolytes in Li–S cells.
DOL/OCTO differs from the expected no-plateau quasi-solid profile
manifested by other SSEs, which fully prevent Li2Sn dissolution. Rather,
due to the weakly solvating nature of this electrolyte demonstrated in
the polysulfide dissolution tests, the presence of some polysulfide spe-
cies is evidenced by the potential well occurring between plateaus,
whose intensity increases upon cycling.
Hydrofluoroethers such as OCTO have a low donor ability [42,69,
70], which results in a low but non-negligible solubility of polysulfides.
As reported for other SSE, this type of electrolyte promotes the forma-
tion of medium or short-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2S4) compared to
the long chain polysulfide species such as Li2S8 and Li2S6 favored in the
conventional DME/DOL electrolyte, thus tuning the reaction pathway of
lithium-sulfur discharge process. The different voltage profiles for each
electrolyte can be tentatively ascribed to this fact, although a deeper
analysis is needed to confirm it.
Regarding the overvoltage between charge and discharge plateaus,
in the first cycles it is higher in the case of the SSE electrolyte, but after
33 cycles the overvoltage of DME/DOL increases when LiNO3 is
exhausted and Li0 unprotected, whereas in DOL/OCTO the overvoltage
in the first cycle at C/10 and the 100th cycle is almost the same,
demonstrating an effective reduction of the polysulfide shuttle.
To properly assess the cell degradation depending on the electrolyte
used, additional medium-sulfur loading cells (2.3 mgS cm−2) were tested
at a moderate rate of C/5 for just 20 cycles and a post-cycling analysis of
the fully charged cells was carried out (Fig. 7). The electrochemical
behavior of both cells with medium-sulfur loadings for a limited number
of cycles is almost similar, as shown in Fig. S7. Nevertheless, when
disassembling the cells, huge differences are acknowledged depending
on the electrolyte. Concerning the DME/DOL cell, besides the light-
yellow coloration of the separator due to some polysulfide dissolution,
the anode surface is clearly damaged. In the SEM micrographs, there are
no clear evidences of the positive electrode surface degradation,
whereas the surface of lithium metal clearly shows mossy structures,
pointing out to the uncontrolled and heterogeneous lithium growth
during plating, with possible corrosion coming from the polysulfide
reactivity with the anode, which could eventually lead to a prompt cell
failure. Opposite to this behavior, lithium deposition using DOL/OCTO
electrolyte is smooth and homogeneous across all the Li0 surface, with
the KB-0.3rGO cathode showing the same behavior as for the DME/DOL

As an additional electrochemical characterization of the cells, self-
discharge tests were carried out in order to check the practical appli-
cability of the KB-0.3rGO cells with both electrolytes. The typical
charge-discharge profile of these tests is shown in Fig. S8, where a
resting time is maintained after preconditioning the cells and leaving
them completely charged. The irreversible capacity loss of these systems
was calculated according to the resting time and the electrolyte
composition. The capacity loss is calculated as the relative variation of
the discharge capacity prior and after the resting time.
As shown in Table S2, the initial discharge capacities of the DOL/
OCTO cells are higher compared to those obtained for the DME/DOL
cells. After the resting period, the drop in the capacity increases with the
resting time regardless of the electrolyte, although significant differ-
ences are perceived depending on the compositions. This drop is more
pronounced in DOL/OCTO cells, reaching up to an 18 % capacity loss for
the 7-days resting time, whereas for DME/DOL the maximum capacity
drop is just 5 %. Nevertheless, this drop is almost completely recovered
on the subsequent cycle, and the DOL/OCTO based cells suffer an irre-
versible capacity loss of approximately 3 % with resting time. This value
is slightly higher than for the DME/DOL based cells, but the difference
among systems is not as significant as it might seem based on the first
discharge after resting.
Overall, the electrochemical performance of the high-sulfur loading
cells makes this KB-0.3rGO and DOL/OCTO combination approach
auspicious for prototype pouch cell development. An additional elec-
trochemical test was carried out narrowing the voltage window to
2.7–1.4 V to find the most favorable working conditions for the system
to operate as long as possible while offering adequate discharge
capacities when scaled up into a pouch cell. The cyclability of a cell
operating for more than 175 cycles is shown in Fig. S9. Under these
conditions, initial capacity discharge values of 1108 mAh g−1 and 4.1
mAh cm−2 were obtained at C/10, which is still a good electrochemical
performance. In this case, the capacity decay follows the same trend as
previous cells cycled in a broader voltage window, but still noticeable
areal capacities of 1.7 mAh cm−2 are obtained after 175 cycles, which is
very promising from the realistic application perspective.
Prototype monolayer pouch cells were therefore assembled to assess
the scalability of this system. The KB-0.3rGO cathode total surface was
increased from 1.33 cm2 to 20.25 cm2, and the same electrolyte-to-sulfur
ratios of 7 μL mg−1 were maintained for pouch cell tests. The electro-
chemical performance of these cells with DME/DOL and DOL/OCTO
electrolytes was evaluated, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. Pouch
cells assembled using the conventional DME/DOL electrolyte showed
interesting initial capacities above 65 mAh at C/20 but they failed after
6 cycles. The post-mortem analysis carried out for this cell is shown in
Fig. S10, and it reveals a heterogeneous lithium deposition for this
conventional system, which can be observed both in the optical images
and the SEM micrographs, where the presence of mossy lithium is
confirmed.
In the case of the DOL/OCTO-based pouch cells, the one cycled in the
wider voltage window between 3.0 and 1.2 V shows high initial
discharge capacities (59 mAh at C/10), but it is limited to 17 cycles with
a progressive capacity loss and an erratic coulombic efficiency. Ac-
cording to the coin cell analysis which proved that narrowing the
voltage window of the cells with the DOL/OCTO electrolyte (Fig. S9) led
to a cycle life extension, a similar approach was followed for the pro-
totype pouch cells. The initial discharge capacity of the DOL/OCTO
pouch cell cycled between 2.7 and 1.4 V was 55 mAh at C/10, just a
slight decrease regarding the wider voltage range, and the cycle life was
noticeably improved, reaching over 60 cycles. A smooth capacity loss
also occurred for this cell, reaching a 70 % capacity retention at the 38th
cycle. The average coulombic efficiency was significantly improved in
this voltage window, but still lower than that shown at coin cell level.

4. Conclusion
In this work, a novel system developed by the combination of a
graphene-containing high-sulfur loading cathode (KB-0.3rGO) and a
novel sparingly solvating electrolyte (DOL/OCTO) has demonstrated its
real potential for lithium-sulfur technology. It was found that the
incorporation of rGO sheets increased the electrical conductivity of the
composites, while strongly improving the mechanical properties of the
cathodes and providing oxygen functional groups that enhanced elec-
trode wettability. Besides, using the conventional DME/DOL electrolyte,
the success in this approach for improving the positive electrodes has
been demonstrated, obtaining for KB-0.3rGO high discharge capacities
of 4.8 mAh cm−2 at C/10, as well as good electrochemical performances
at high C-rates. Nevertheless, since the cyclability of these cells was
limited, a novel sparingly solvating electrolyte was developed for pol-
ysulfide
shuttle
inhibition
and
improving
cell
durability.
When
combining these cathodes with the novel DOL/OCTO electrolyte, the
cells showed outstanding discharge capacities of 5.3 mAh cm−2 at C/10
and a 65 % capacity retention after 100 cycles. This improvement is
ascribed to the enhanced wettability and the partial prevention of pol-
ysulfide dissolution. The cycle life could even be extended to 175 cycles
by voltage narrowing with a slight capacity loss. To validate these re-
sults in more realistic conditions, prototype pouch cells were assembled,
achieving a total capacity of 55.2 mAh at C/10 for over 60 cycles. More
studies including these findings and components optimization are un-
derway to overcome the real limitations of this promising energy storage
technology. Additional strategies could be implemented to further
improve the performance of this system, such as carbon surface func-
tionalization, incorporation of catalysts for a more effective anchoring
and conversion of polysulfides; and also including additive salts to the
electrolyte for an enhanced lithium anode protection and cycling
performance.
