The direct reaction of polysulﬁde species with the Li metal anode causes several issues in the perfor- 
mance of lithium-sulfur batteries, from poor capacity utilization and fast capacity fade to poor Coulom- 
bic eﬃciency. Nevertheless, several reports indicate formation of favorable lithium SEI when allowing for 
its contact with polysulﬁdes. With cycling tests, impedance spectroscopy measurements and morphology 
investigation, we have conﬁrmed the beneﬁcial effect of polysulﬁdes on lithium metal stripping and de- 
position even without LiNO 3 , which is usually added. Most importantly, highly concentrated catholytes, 
which are closer to the saturated solutions encountered in lean electrolyte-to-sulfur ratios, were tested. 
In the 1 M Li 2 S 8 catholyte solution, its effect produced Li electrodes without dendritic growth, but rather 
a wrinkled surface. To circumvent the issue of viscosity in such solutions, pre-treatment in polysulﬁde 
solutions was also tested. The performance of such electrodes showed an increase in cycle life and lower 
overpotentials, although dendritic growth could not be fully avoided. In order to better understand the 
effect of pre-treatment on transport/reaction mechanism in Li electrode, a detailed analysis of selected 
impedance spectra is carried out using physics-based transmission line modelling (TLM).

1. Introduction 
As shown in a previous publication [1] the interaction between 
Li metal anode and polysulﬁdes proceeds at a signiﬁcant rate. As 
polysulﬁdes can diffuse away from the cathode, through the sep- 
arator, and reach the Li surface, the Li-polysulﬁde interaction is 
at fault for low eﬃciency, capacity loss, and the presence of the 
self-discharge phenomenon in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries [2] . It 
has already been determined that the presence and composition 
of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on Li, as well as the Li surface 
morphology (dendrites increase the surface available for reaction) 
have a profound inﬂuence on the rate of this conversion, although 
the mechanism remains similar [1] . 
However, one can look at this interaction from a different 
stand point. Not only does the polysulﬁde concentration and ox- 
idation state change due to its reduction with Li, the presence 
of polysulﬁdes likely changes the properties of Li as well - if 
nothing else by short-chain polysulﬁdes precipitating on its sur-

face [3–5] . That means that polysulﬁdes present near the Li sur- 
face may have an effect on the Li SEI composition and proper- 
ties, which in turn changes the Li anode performance during strip- 
ping of Li + ions from its surface and during the reverse process of 
deposition. 
Presence of polysulﬁdes in conjuncture with LiNO 3 additive has 
already been shown to be beneﬁcial to the Li anode performance 
in terms of formation of a more stable SEI layer, which resulted in 
(less dangerous) larger dendrite formations, better Coulombic ef- 
ﬁciencies during stripping and deposition, and extended life time 
[3] . It is proposed that LiNO 3 oxidizes the polysulﬁdes to Li 2 SO 3 
and Li 2 SO 4 while it is reduced to LiNO 2 , resulting in the formation 
of a compact and stable passivation layer, which results in superior 
cycling performance [ 6 , 7 ]. Polysulﬁde based electrolyte was also 
used for electrochemically prepared stable artiﬁcial SEI on Li metal 
anodes [8] . Unfortunately, the concentration of polysulﬁdes em- 
ployed in this and in other similar studies [ 4 , 5 ] was low, and never 
exceeding 0.2 M, a range rarely encountered in commercially viable 
Li-S battery cell setups. Namely, the electrolyte amount in practical 
Li-S cells with glyme based electrolytes is usually severely reduced, 
leading to saturation of the electrolyte with polysulﬁdes and the

Overall, this shows that suﬃcient evaluation of the effect of

be made when using only solutions with low concentrations of 
polysulﬁdes. To enable more thorough understanding of the ef- 
fect of polysulﬁdes on the Li metal stripping and deposition pro- 
cess, different concentrations of polysulﬁdes were added to the 
chosen supporting electrolyte. LiNO 3 additive, which has already 
been proven to have a positive effect on the studied processes 
when added together with polysulﬁdes was omitted. This choice 
was made due to the fact that LiNO 3 is known to have only limited 
ability to stabilize the lithium surface in the case of long term cy- 
cling use [9] . Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) exper- 
iments in conjunction with transmission line modelling were also 
applied and used to explain the differences caused by the presence 
of polysulﬁdes. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials preparation 
All the materials’ preparation and cell assembly was conducted 
inside the glovebox, which was kept with a controlled Ar atmo- 
sphere and low levels of oxygen and water (below 1 ppm). Poly- 
sulﬁde powder (Li 2 S 8 ) was synthesised by mixing stoichiometric 
amounts of Li and sulfur in dry THF at 50 °C until it completely re- 
acted. The powder was isolated under reduced pressure and used 
to prepare different concentrations of catholyte solutions (0.01 M, 
0.1 M and 1.0 M) by weighing the appropriate amount and dis- 
solving it in 1 M LiTFSI in TEGDME:DOL 1:1 (v:v) supporting elec- 
trolyte. For this preparation, volumetric ﬂasks were used. Since 
polysulﬁdes are known to disproportionate, the concentrations and 
composition should be considered nominal. The Li 2 S 8 powder was 
chosen due to high solubility, enabling the preparation of highly 
concentrated catholytes. The supporting electrolyte was prepared 
out of previously dried salt and solvents. LiTFSI (Sigma Aldrich) 
was dried under vacuum at 140 °C overnight, tetraglyme (TEGDME, 
Acros) and dioxolane (DOL, Sigma Aldrich) were both dried in sev- 
eral steps including molecular sieves, K/Na alloy and distillation 
[10] . 
Lithium metal foil (FMC, 110 μm ribbon) was cut into 2 cm 2 
electrodes. No scratching, rolling or smoothing of the Li surface 
was employed, so it should be considered that native SEI was still 
present on the surface of the Li electrodes. The cells were assem- 
bled in pouch cells with Ni contacts by stacking the electrodes 
and one layer of Celgard 2320 separator. 20 μL of electrolyte or 
catholyte solution were added to each cell. 
Pre-treatment of the Li electrodes in highly concentrated 
catholyte solution (1.0 M Li 2 S 8 ) was conducted by assembling a 
symmetrical Li||Li cell and performing a stripping and deposition 
experiment at a current density of 0.1 mA cm −2 for 10 cycles with 
each half cycle lasting 2 h. Before further use, such cells were dis- 
assembled inside the glovebox and the pre-treated Li electrodes 
washed with dried DOL to remove any soluble polysulﬁdes from 
its surface. 
2.2. Electrochemical measurements 
The electrochemical measurements were done on Biologic 
VMP3 potentiostat/galvanostat with EC-lab software. Li||Li sym- 
metrical cell performance evaluation was conducted by strip- 
ping and deposition experiment with two different current rates 
(0.1 mA cm −2 and 0.5 mA cm −2 ). During this experiment, each half 
cycle lasted 2 h. 
On symmetrical cells impedance measurements were also con- 
ducted. The experiment was started within 10 min of cell assem- 
bly, after which impedance was followed over the course of 36 h 
of cell stabilisation. After stabilisation, one stripping and deposi- 
tion cycle at 0.1 mA cm −2 was done (2 h stripping and 2 h de- 
position for the working electrode) before the cell was left to relax 
for 15 min and two consecutive impedance spectra were measured. 
This experiment was then continued for 60 cycles. The impedance 
spectrum was always measured between 1 MHz and 1 mHz with 
an amplitude of 10 mV (rms). 
Coulombic eﬃciency determination was done by assembling 
Li||stainless steel (when poylsulﬁdes were present in the elec- 
trolyte) or Li||copper cells (when no polysulﬁdes were present) 
with the same procedure as described for Li||Li symmetrical cells. 
The measurement was conducted at 0.1 mA cm −2 current rate. The 
discharge was capacity limited (0.2 mAh cm −2 ), while the charge 
was restricted with the potential limit of 1 V vs. Li/Li + . 
2.3. Post-mortem analysis 
After the electrochemical measurements, the cells were disas- 
sembled inside the glovebox and the Li electrodes washed with 
DOL to remove any soluble salts or polysulﬁdes. Scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) was used to determine the morphology of the Li 
deposits on its surface. A special vacuum transfer stage was used 
to ensure no contact of the samples with the atmosphere. Field- 
emission scanning electron microscope (FE SEM) Supra 35VP from 
Zeiss, Germany was used for the analysis. The micrographs were 
collected with the electron gun accelerating voltage of 1 kV, a low 
setting due to instability of the materials under the electron beam. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Experimental results 
The potential response of stripping and deposition in a Li||Li 
symmetrical cell is shown in Fig. 1 . The concentration of poly- 
sulﬁdes in the supporting electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in TEGDME:DOL 
1:1 (v:v)) was varied between 0, 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0 M Li 2 S 8 . Ulti- 
mately, we are searching for a case with a lesser extent of den- 
dritic growth and good ionic conductivity of the SEI layer. These 
conditions would result in a more stable electrochemical response 
with lower overpotential values and smaller scattering of points. 
The latter (scattering) is here presumed as widening of the curves 
attributed to the “double-peak” behaviour due to dendritic growth 
and pitting corrosion of bulk lithium [11] . 
The measurements conducted with lower current densities 
( Fig. 1 - 0.1 mA cm −2 ) conﬁrmed that the addition of low con- 
centrations of polysulﬁdes (0.01 M and 0.1 M) to the electrolyte 
solution reduces the overpotentials needed for the electrochemi- 
cal processes to take place, while at the same time, less “double- 
peaking” behaviour is noticeable. This is clearly seen from the ex- 
ample of half cycle overpotential on Fig. 1 e (cycle number 15 from 
Fig. 1 a–c). Furthermore, as described, this same effect is seen as 
a thinner curve at lower and more stable overpotential values on 
Fig. 1 a–c. We expected the effect to be more pronounced and more 
beneﬁcial if the concentration of polysulﬁdes added is 0.1 M in- 
stead of 0.01 M [3] , however the results we obtained seem similar. 
Nevertheless, one cannot deny that the curves still show an os- 
cillation associated with dendritic growth, suggesting high surface 
area lithium cannot be avoided with the tested concentrations of 
polysulﬁdes. 
For the concentrated polysulﬁde solution (1.0 M Li 2 S 8 , Fig. 1 d), 
the overpotentials are high, likely due to transport limitations

concentrated catholyte ( Fig. 2 d, open circles) follows the men-

and a decrease in the SEI resistance (60 ). As expected due to 
high viscosity of this solution, the impedance contribution due to

proving the hypothesis of transport limitations causing the over-

the resistance of the electrolyte (32 ) and of the SEI (170 ) sig-

deposition on the lithium surface. 
The impedance study was continued by measuring spectra be- 
tween stripping and deposition cycles (0.1 mA cm −2 ). Fig. 3 shows 
the impedance of Li||Li symmetrical cells measured after 20 and af- 
ter 60 cycles. Understanding of the impedance response of Li metal 
cells where high surface area growth is expected is complex, since 
the initial impedance contributions described in correlation with 
Fig. 2 now drastically change due to electrolyte consumption, Li 
surface area increase, porous lithium (still available for the redox 
reaction) and dead lithium formation. A recently published model 
[13] was used to better correlate the data measured to their back- 
ground physicochemical processes. Selected modelling results will 
be discussed in detail later on. For the purpose of this section, 
however, the discussion in is kept simple to enable reader’s suf- 
ﬁcient understanding of the main messages from the analysis of 
present measurements. 
The electrolyte and two lower polysulﬁde concentration 
catholytes (0.01 M and 0.1 M) all show signs of dendritic growth, 
as already expected from the potential curves analysis ( Fig. 1 ). 
Here, that effect is seen as (i) an increase in the resistive inter- 
cept due to electrolyte decomposition and dead lithium formation 
(inserts on Fig. 3 a–c), (ii) a decrease in the size of the high fre- 
quency arc due to surface area increase, (iii) initial high frequency 
contribution nearing 45 ° angle due to (still active) porous lithium 
formation, which serves as a porous electrode, and (iv) the dif- 
fusional part of the spectra extending and becoming more com- 
plex due to several contributions coming from the dead and porous 
lithium. 
For the highest concentration of polysulﬁdes ( Fig. 3 d), on the 
other hand, those same effects are not evident. There is little 
change to the size of the high frequency arc, while the diffusional 
contribution does not become stretched out, yet rather still fol- 
lows the initial Warburg like shape. This could in turn suggest that 
there is little to no dendritic growth happening inside the cell. 
To investigate whether this is the case, the same cells on which 
impedance analysis was conducted were opened after 60 cycles of 
use (Fig. S3). For the lower polysulﬁde concentration, a large extent 
of dead lithium, which could be peeled away with the separator, 
was evident. The electrodes were washed with dry 1,3-dioxolane 
and examined under SEM to determine the extent of dendritic 
growth and the morphology of the deposits. As already suggested 
from other used techniques, Fig. 4 shows dendritic growth on the 
surface of lithium in the case of the three lower concentrations 
of polysulﬁdes. The dendrite morphology is different in the three 
cases, although the thickness of the deposit does not follow any 
evident trend. 
More importantly, the electrode with the highest concentra- 
tion of polysulﬁdes shows no sign of dendrite growth, but rather 
a wrinkling of the Li surface ( Fig. 4 ). In some places, this sur- 
face is covered with desert rose-like deposits. More detailed pic- 
tures of the morphology of this electrode can be seen in Fig. S4. A 
detailed analysis of the deposits was not attempted, although ac- 
cording to already published XPS analyses [3–5] and the similarity 
of the morphology with Li 2 S deposits [ 14 , 15 ], we assume that the 
clusters are precipitated short chain polysulﬁdes. Since XPS study 
conducted on lithium electrodes in contact with polysulﬁdes shows 
the presence of decomposed sulfur species (S 2 −, S 2 2 −) [16] , similar 
decomposition products are expected in this case as well. The stark 
difference in the morphology of the lithium deposits also suggest 
a difference in the SEI composition of the electrodes in contact 
with different concentrations of polysulﬁdes. As stated, a chem- 
ical analysis was not attempted, although one can deduce what 
differences might be expected according to the controlled differ- 
ences in the environment under which the SEI were formed. In 
the electrolyte without polysulﬁdes, a clear difference in the SEI 
composition would be that there are no polysulﬁde decomposition 
products expected. Since all the components in the three catholyte 
solutions tested are the same, we can conclude that the SEI will 
be composed of the same compounds in all three cases. Since the 
concentration of the polysulﬁdes is the only variable in the so- 
lutions, this suggest that the SEI formed in more highly concen- 
trated catholytes will have a higher percentage of decomposed sul- 
fur species and lower degree of oxide layers and LiTFSI salt decom- 
position products. On the other hand, the latter compounds will

To summarise, this experiment clearly shows that polysulﬁde 
addition alone can be extremely effective with dendritic growth 
suppression, although unfortunately the high viscosity of such 
catholyte solutions prevents it from eﬃcient use. Therefore, we 
have tried to circumvent these shortcomings by pre-cycling the Li 
electrodes in the highly concentrated (1.0 M Li 2 S 8 ) catholyte solu- 
tion (10 cycles, 0.1 mA cm −2 , 2 h each half cycle), washing the 
electrodes with DOL and then testing their performance in the 
pure supporting electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in TEGDME:DOL 1:1 (v:v)). 
Similar electrochemical artiﬁcial SEI formation have already been 
proposed for polymer based SEI layer [17] and low concentration 
polysulﬁde based ternary electrolyte [8] . Before conducting these 
experiments, the pre-treated electrode was examined under SEM 
( Figs. 5 a and S5). As expected, no dendrite growth was visible on 
the surface. Li 2 S particle deposits, on the other hand were seen 
(the difference between dendritic growth and sulﬁde deposition is 
evident from Fig. S5, showing Li surface after extended deposition 
caused a few dendrites to form in the 1.0 M Li 2 S 8 cathoylte solu- 
tion as well). The thickness of the polysulﬁde reacted layer on Li is 
on the size order of 1 μm. This is in line with research [18] show- 
ing similar thicknesses of such deposits on the carbon cathode af- 
ter Li-S battery cell discharge. 
As shown on Fig. 5 , if the stripping and deposition experiment 
at 0.1 mA cm −2 is conducted on such cells, the performance seems 
to be far superior that of the cell without any pre-treatment. The 
curve is at a lower overpotential, which also does not increase 
signiﬁcantly during the duration of the experiment. The cell with 
no pre-treatment, on the other hand, starts showing signs of dry- 
out after 500 h with the overpotential considerably increasing ( > 
100%, Fig. 5 b). For the comparison to be more correct, one has to 
take into account that the pre-treated electrode has been cycled 
for 10 full cycles before the test shown in Fig. 5 b. Therefore, the 
initial 10 cycles of the non-treated electrode should also be con- 
sidered formational, which suggests that the black curve should be 
shifted to the left as shown in Fig. S6. The potential curves for the 
pre-treated lithium also do not show any clear sign of “double- 
peaking” due to dendritic growth ( Fig. 5 e). Eﬃciency of pre-treated 
electrodes was also improved in comparison to electrodes without 
pre-treatment (Fig. S7). The pre-treated cell was tested with the 
same impedance test as explained for other cells with Figs. 2 and 
3 . The change in impedance and the SEM micrograph taken after 
60 cycles of use are shown on Fig. 5 c and e. As evident, high sur- 
face area dendritic growth was not stopped by the pre-treatment, 
indicating that the lithium SEI formed with 1 M Li 2 S 8 is not robust 
enough to sustain the effects shown previously if the catholyte is 
removed. Similar results are also shown in impedance spectra with 
a large increase in the electrolyte resistance and a large decrease 
in the high frequency arc, indicative of surface area increase. 
3.2. Explanation of measured impedance trends using physics-based 
transmission line models (TLMs) 
In order to explain the measured impedance spectra under var- 
ious conditions of interest we use two variants of transmission 
line models (TLM) that were introduced and discussed in con- 
siderable detail in our recent paper [13] . The ﬁrst TLM shown in 
Fig. 6 a is appropriate for description of impedance spectra involv- 
ing a “quasi-ﬂat” lithium electrode. This means that the Li surface 
is close to a pristine state, i.e. it has not developed any signiﬁcant 
porosity in terms of live porous dendrites. This surface is covered 
with a compact (pore-free) solid electrolyte interface, known as SEI 
(green area in Fig. 6 a). Due to contact with polysulﬁdes, there is 
a porous ﬁlm consisting mainly of Li 2 S deposits on top of SEI as 
indicated in Fig. 5 a (denoted as blue area in Fig. 6 a). Finally, the 
half-cell modelled by TLM contains half of the separator that di- 
vides both Li electrodes (yellow area).

that this model was upgraded from its basic version published in

other extension is introduction of the so-called geometrical capac-

with the response of adjacent porous phase ﬁlled with electrolyte 
(the coupling elements are denoted with capital index “INT”). Also, 
we could take into account the dielectric response of the porous 
phase (denoted with blue colour) which was partly seen at high- 
est frequencies. The more basic model published previously cannot 
simulate such dielectric features of non-SEI phases. 
The upper resistive rail in Fig. 6 a represents migration of 
lithium ions through all the phases (separator, porous ﬁlm, SEI). 
When reaching the surface of metallic lithium, Li + undergoes elec- 
trochemical reaction – transformation into metallic Li - experienc- 
ing a charge transfer resistance of R 1,CT which is in parallel to the 
corresponding double layer capacitance, C l,DL . The lower resistive 
rail in Fig. 6 a describes the transport of counter ions, mostly an- 
ions that move from the separator across the porous blue-shaded 
area until reaching solid SEI. However, they cannot enter into solid 
SEI structure. The blocking of negative ions at SEI is denoted with 
the capacitive element C e/a,INT . Further, we assumed that SEI ex- 
hibits some conduction of electrons (even if the resistance may 
be high) so that the lower resistive rail also continues inside SEI 
which also experience a special resistance when moving from the 
SEI into metallic lithium phase (here we use a notation of R e,CT , 
by analogy with upper resistive rail). As already mentioned, we 
also fully took into account the dielectric properties of involved 
phases (usually skipped in interpretation of EIS of batteries). These 
properties are described by the middle horizontal capacitive rail 
in Fig. 6 a. Finally, we took into account the chemical capacitances 
of transported species that are represented as capacitive elements 
on the horizontal lines across the phases. Note, that in the spe- 
ciﬁc case of SEI ﬁlm we omitted the chemical capacitors. This is 
because SEI is very thin and the corresponding chemical capaci- 
tances very small. In fact, they are of similar magnitude as the ge- 
ometrical capacitances due to dielectric capacitance. In such cases 
both capacitances (dielectric and chemical merge into simpler el- 
ements, here shown as C d,SEI , C l,DL and C e,DL (for more details see 
the general discussion in ref [19] ). 
After prolonged cycling the surface of metallic lithium is cov- 
ered with rather thick layer of porous metallic dendrites. EIS of 
porous conductive electrodes cannot be approximated with a pla- 
nar model such as shown in Fig. 6 a. Instead, a Bisquert-like basic 
transmission line for porous electrodes [20] upgraded with the an- 
ionic rail and corresponding chemical capacitors [ 13 , 21 ] needs to 
be used as shown in Fig. 6 b. Note that here we omitted the mid- 
dle horizontal line with capacitors due to dielectric relaxation as 
their response was not detected in the response of cycled elec- 
trodes. However, we retained the upgraded SEI response with two 
relaxation times due to reaction and transport contribution inside 
the SEI. More about modelling of dendritic lithium using TLM can 
be found in our previous work [13] .

As for the other parameters in Table 1 , we can see that many 
values are predetermined, have limiting values (0 or ∞ ) or are 
not applicable (N/A). This is due to the fact that before applying 
the corresponding TLM model ( Fig. 6 a) to the present case, we 
found that it could be signiﬁcantly simpliﬁed. A major part of sim- 
pliﬁcation was based on our previous analysis [23] in which we 
found that the electronic resistance of SEI (several hundreds of k ) 
was many orders of magnitude bigger than the ionic resistance for 
lithium ions (several tens of ). For this reason we could neglect 
the left part of bottom rail in Fig. 6 a containing R e,CT, C e,DL and 
R e,SEI by setting the values proposed in Table 1 . Furthermore, sen- 
sitivity analysis showed that of the 5 elements belonging to the 
interface, R l,INT, C l,INT, R l,CT, C d,INT, C a,INT and C e/a,INT only the latter 
is of signiﬁcant importance, i.e. there is no separate arc due to 
this interface in the present speciﬁc case. Finally, due to inabil- 
ity to separate the chemical capacitances of individual species in 
the same phase, we assumed that both values were the same. All 
these simpliﬁcation eliminated 10 out of 22 model parameters so 
the simulations had a very small degree of freedom. Despite that 
one may estimate that the simulated spectra resemble the mea- 
surements quite well. Partly this is also due to the use of the CPE 
elements ( Q values in Table 1 ) instead of physically sound capaci- 
tances. Generally we do not support the use of CPEs as they may 
blur the underlying physical picture. In this particular case, how- 
ever, we are more focused to the values of resistances which are 
not affected by CPEs. Thus CPEs are used primarily to demonstrate 
that the present model can fully replicate all, even the smallest 
features of measured spectra.

lithium electrodes containing signiﬁcant amount of porous lithium 
dendrites ( Fig. 8 ). We were interested in physical reasons for the 
differences between the impedance response of a pristine lithium 
and polysulﬁde-treated lithium after prolonged cycling (60 cycles). 
As can be seen from Fig. 8 , the proposed TLM model describes 
both cases well. Again, the speciﬁc differences can be best seen by 
comparing the element values for both cases ( Table 2 ). We focus 
mostly on the values of various resistances as they determine the 
transport/reaction rates of the cell. It is seen that the biggest differ- 
ences are in the migration resistances inside the dendritic lithium:

words, it seems that the penetration of ions through dendritic 
structure is much easier in the case of polysulﬁde-pre-treated sam- 
ple. There are several possible reasons for that: either dendrites in 
pre-treated lithium are thinner or their porosity is bigger and/or 
tortuosity smaller. More surprisingly, also the migration resistances 
through the separator ( R l,S , R a,S ) are signiﬁcantly bigger in the case 
of pristine lithium. It is rather diﬃcult to understand why the 
properties of the separator as such would depend on the state of 
lithium. A possible explanation could be that in pristine lithium 
the dendrite growth is very non-uniform so that parts of lithium 
surface gets blocked for transport of ions and, consequently, the 
effective surface area of lithium is reduced (if so, the reduction 
would be almost by a factor of two). Such an explanation would 
be in line with recent experiments by Bazant et al [24] . Namely, in 
pristine lithium the dendritic growth is quite non-uniform due to 
extreme build-up of concentrational gradients near the electrode 
surface. If however, these gradients are damped by a certain inter- 
phase (such as porous polysulﬁde ﬁlm in our case) it is possible 
that the dendritic growth gets more uniform across the whole sur- 
face. There are also some differences in the SEI-related resistances 
( R CT , R SEI ), however the absolute values of those resistance are low 
so they have little effect on the total reaction/transport rate. Fi- 
nally, we should also comment on the values related to the porous 
ﬁlm ( R l,P , R a,P ). Please note that although the model is the same in 
both cases, the meaning of the porous layer is physically different. 
In the case of cycled pristine lithium such porous layer consists of 
the so-called dead porous lithium, i.e. non-conductive porous de- 
posits that can reach considerable thickness (see our previous work 
[13] ). In the cases of pre-treated lithium, however, the amount of 
such dead dendrites is marginal and the porous layer mainly con- 
sists of polysulﬁde deposits as demonstrated in Fig. 5 . 
4. Conclusions 
Performance of Li metal during stripping and deposition with 
several different concentrations of polysulﬁde catholyte was eval- 
uated. The Li 2 S 8 concentration was increased to 1.0 M, which is 
closer to the actual polysulﬁde concentration in saturated elec- 
trolyte solutions of practical battery setups. Impedance was used 
to evaluate the dendrite growth. Unlike the lower concentrations, 
the highest concentration (1.0 M) of added Li 2 S 8 suppressed the 
growth of dendrites. In this case a wrinkled surface was observed 
after prolonged cycling. In addition, such surface was also covered 
with large deposits indicative of lithium sulﬁde particulates. Since 
the viscosity of the highly concentrated catholyte solutions is also 
high, an attempt was made to avoid the detrimental effects associ- 
ated with it by only pre-treating the lithium electrodes in this so- 
lution. After pre-treatment the electrolyte containing 1 M of Li 2 S 8 
was replaced with the conventional electrolyte which led to a sig- 
niﬁcant improvement of cycling behaviour with lower overpoten- 
tials and longer cycle life. 
In order to better understand the effect of pre-treatment on 
transport/reaction mechanism in Li electrode, a detailed analysis 
of selected impedance spectra was carried out using physics-based 
transmission line modelling (TLM). It was found that the pres- 
ence of 1 M Li 2 S 8 increases the migration resistances of mobile 
species (by a factor up to 33) in all liquid phases. At low frequen- 
cies (long relaxation times) this increase is equivalent to a decrease 
of lithium chemical diffusion coeﬃcient by a similar factor. By con- 
trast, the presence of 1 M Li 2 S 8 has only a negligible effect on the 
resistance of SEI ﬁlm as well as on charge transfer resistance. Thus 
the main reason for poor cycling is the very slow transport of Li in 
the liquid phases. 
The effect of polysulﬁde-based ﬁlm on cycling was also investi- 
gated using TLM modelling of measured EIS spectra. It was found 
that after 60 cycles the dendrites formed on pre-treated lithium 
are much more permeable (less resistive for mobile species) com- 
pared to dendrites formed on pristine lithium (the resistance is 
lower by a factor of 3–8, depending on the species). Additionally, 
the migration resistance through separator was less affected in the 
case of polysulﬁde-pre-treated lithium. It is hypothesized that this 
is due to more uniform formation of dendrites across the lithium 
surface, i.e. the porous polysulﬁde ﬁlm prevents excessive local 
growth of dendrites. 
Although the research in the present manuscript has been con- 
ducted more with the aim to deepen the fundamental under- 
standing of lithium stripping and deposition in high concentra- 
tions of polysulﬁde based catholytes, this pre-treatment neverthe- 
less seems an interesting course of direction for improvement of Li 
metal batteries. If the limiting process during battery operation is 
Li anode performance, the present results suggest the battery cy- 
clability could be signiﬁcantly improved.
