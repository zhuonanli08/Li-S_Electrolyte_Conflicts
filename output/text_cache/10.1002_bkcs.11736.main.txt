Essential Features of Electrolyte Solvents that Enable
High-Performance Li S Batteries
Jin Kyeong Kang,† Tae Jeong Kim,† Jong Won Park,† and Yongju Jung*
Department of Applied Chemical Engineering, Korea University of Technology and Education
(KOREATECH), Cheonan 31253, Republic of Korea. *E-mail: yjung@koreatech.ac.kr
†These authors contributed equally to this work.
Received January 10, 2019, Accepted April 8, 2019, Published online April 29, 2019
In a lithium-ion battery, the organic solvent in the electrolyte simply acts as a medium that facilitates ionic
charge transfer, whereas for the lithium-sulfur (Li—S) battery, the choice of the electrolyte is critical as it
inﬂuences the chemical nature of the soluble polysulﬁdes and the cycling stability of the lithium metal
anode. Li-S batteries currently use ether-based electrolytes with high polysulﬁde solubility. Until now,
however, the necessary requirements and the roles of the electrolytes have been inconclusive. This work
elucidates the essential features and functions of the electrolyte based on an intensive investigation into
17 variants of solvents. Furthermore, examples of electrolytes for high performance, rechargeable Li—S
batteries are suggested. The three major study procedures are (1) analyzing the chemical stability of reac-
tive polysulﬁde species and lithium metal in the solvents of choice, (2) inspecting the chemical reactivity
of lithium metal in a polysulﬁde solution, and (3) evaluating the inﬂuences of the solvent on the perfor-
mance of Li—S cells. Based on the chemical and electrochemical test results, we suggest that the stability
of the active materials (i.e., lithium metal and lithium polysulﬁdes) should be ensured in an electrolyte for
the proper operation of Li—S cells, that chemical reactions between the polysulﬁde species and lithium
metal should be negligible during the charge–discharge process. In addition, we have conﬁrmed that the
high polysulﬁde solubility is essential for high rate performance.
Keywords: Li—S battery, Electrolyte solvent, Chemical stability of active materials, Polysulﬁdes
Introduction
State of the art lithium-ion batteries face a variety of limita-
tions with regard to energy density, safety, and cost.1,2 To
meet the exponentially growing demand for the emerging
battery-powered systems, many studies of high-energy-density
batteries have been recently conducted worldwide.1,2 Lith-
ium–sulfur (Li S) batteries exhibit the strongest potential for
high-performance energy storage devices owing to the low
cost and high theoretical capacity of the sulfur cathode.2–4
Despite the substantial research advances of Li S batte-
ries, mass production has not yet been attained due to vari-
ous limitations.3,4 For instance, present Li S batteries have
limited cycling stability arising from the diffusion of solu-
ble polysulﬁdes as well as safety concerns caused by the
lithium metal anode. In the past decade, a number of efforts
have been devoted to address these challenges.3,4 The main
approaches include encapsulating sulfur with carbon mate-
rials, immobilizing polysulﬁdes through chemisorption or
physisorption, placing a physical barrier between the cath-
ode and the separator, and protecting the active lithium
anode.5–14 Thus, the electrochemical performance of Li S
batteries has been notably improved in terms of cyclability
and capacity.5–9 Most studies have focused on blocking the
migration of the polysulﬁdes, mainly through physical
strategies such as conﬁning active materials within a cath-
ode structure.10–14 The tendency of many studies being ori-
ented on physical methods may be attributed to the lack of
an in-depth understanding of the physicochemical proper-
ties of polysulﬁdes in an electrolyte, such as their chemical
or physical interactions with electrolytes and the impor-
tance of their solubility on the electrochemical behavior of
a sulfur cathode. Pioneering works revealed, however, that
(1) sulfur is reduced via extremely complex reactions in
which several charge transfer steps are coupled with vari-
ous chemical reactions of polysulﬁdes, and (2) an electro-
lyte solvent is highly engaged in the electrochemical
reactions of sulfur.15–21 This feature is thought to be associ-
ated with the solubility and solvated structure of polysulﬁde
intermediates, which are sensitive to the surrounding sol-
vent system.21 Especially, the reaction between an electro-
lyte solvent and lithium metal intrinsically affects the
structure and composition of the passivation layer formed
on the surface of lithium metal.22 In a lithium-ion battery, a
solvent in an electrolyte simply acts as a medium facilitat-
ing ionic charge transfer, whereas in a Li S battery it sub-
stantially impacts on the chemical nature of soluble
polysulﬁdes and the cycling stability of the lithium metal
anode.23,24 In this context, it is crucial to select appropriate

However, few works have addressed the inﬂuence of an
electrolyte solvent on the electrochemical behaviors of
Li S batteries.24,25 To date, no comprehensive study has
examined the role of an electrolyte solvent. This study,
therefore, contributes a breakthrough in the development of
Li S batteries by providing a fundamental understanding
of the chemical interactions of active materials with
solvents.
In order to optimize the requirements of an electrolyte sol-
vent for Li S batteries, we scrutinized the behavior of
17 types of solvent on the basis of a clear intuitive assumption
that active materials should remain intact without any irrevers-
ible side reactions during the charge–discharge cycles.
Experimental
Encyclopedic studies of the inﬂuence of electrolyte solvents
on the chemical stability of active materials (i.e., poly-
sulﬁdes and lithium metal) and the electrochemical perfor-
mance of Li S batteries were performed with 17 types of
solvent, as listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

of lithium metal with each solvent, a lithium foil strip was
submerged in a vial containing 15 mL of a solvent and dig-
ital images of the lithium strip were taken with time over
3 days. In addition, the chemical reaction of lithium metal
and polysulﬁdes was inspected in the solvents where lith-

had been soaked in the polysulﬁde-dissolved solution
which was stored for 3 days to ensure the stability of poly-
sulﬁde in the solvent, and its morphology change was
observed with a digital camera. For the fabrication of sulfur
cathodes, elemental sulfur, Ketjenblack (EC-300, Akzo
Nobel, Chicago, IL, USA) and polyethylene oxide (PEO,
Alfa Aesar, Lancashire, UK) at a 6:2:2 weight ratio were
mixed in acetonitrile (Junsei, Saitama, Japan). The slurry
was cast onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil current collec-
tor. The electrodes were dried at 40 C for 12 h and then
pressed. On average, the sulfur loading of the cathode was
approximately 2.0 mg/cm2. CR2016 coin cells were
assembled with the sulfur cathode (area = 2 cm2) and a
lithium foil anode inside an argon-ﬁlled glovebox, using
an electrolyte containing a 1.0 M LiN(CF3SO2)2 and
0.1 M LiNO3 in mixed solvents. To investigate the effect
of each solvent on the performance of Li S cells, a sol-
vent of choice was mixed with 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) with a 1:1 volumetric ratio.
After the cell assembly, the cells were aged on a bench
for 12 h, and galvanostatic tests were performed with an
operational voltage window between 2.7 and 1.8 V at
0.05C (1C = 1675 mA/g) for the initial two cycles. Next,
the cells were cycled at 0.25C to examine the inﬂuence of
the electrolyte solvent on the cycling stability. After the
ﬁrst two cycles, the rate properties were also evaluated at
different discharge current densities ranging from 0.05 and
1.5C with a ﬁxed charge current density of 0.1C.
Results and Discussion
A 0.3 mL aliquot of Li2S8 solution was slowly dissolved in
15 mL of m-xylene, a representative nonpolar solvent.
When the long-chain polysulﬁdes were dissolved in m-
xylene, the solution color changed from dark reddish brown
to yellow, indicating that they were converted to short-
chain polysulﬁdes (Figure 1(a)). As expected, the lithium
metal foil was chemically stable and did not react with m-
xylene, which is an inert solvent without any functional
groups
(Figure
1(b)).
The
Li S
cell
with
an
m-
xylene/DME (1:1, v/v) electrolyte showed a signiﬁcantly
low capacity (<10 mAh/g) in the ﬁrst cycle of the cycling
tests performed after the initial two cycles (Figure S1). This
feature may be primarily ascribed to the poor solubility of
lithium polysulﬁdes in m-xylene. Upon discharging, various
polysulﬁde intermediates, the reduced species, are gener-
ated in the cell and most of them are dissolved exclusively
in DME among two solvents. The increasingly high con-
centration of lithium polysulﬁdes accommodated by DME
molecules causes phase separation in the binary electrolyte,
which is directly responsible for the malfunction of the cell.
Such chemical and electrochemical behaviors were also
veriﬁed
in
trimethylamine,
a
common
organic
base
(Figure S2). These results conﬁrm that the high solubility
of lithium polysulﬁdes is vital for the proper operation of
Li S cells.24,26,27

In n-haxane, a completely nonpolar solvent, lithium
polysulﬁdes were insoluble and lithium metal was stable, as
expected (Figure S2). However, the effect of n-hexane on
the performance of the Li S cell was not evaluated
because lithium salts are insoluble in n-hexane.
Cyclic and linear carbonate solvents, the most commonly
used electrolytes for lithium-ion batteries, were considered
unsuitable as electrolyte solvents of Li S batteries.25,28 For
example, a linear carbonate solvent is decomposed and
polysulﬁdes undergo degradation by a nucleophilic attack
of polysulﬁdes on the central carbon atom with partial posi-
tive charge. We directly investigated the chemical stability
of polysulﬁdes in DMC with time. Polysulﬁdes initially
appeared to dissolve favorably and to be chemically stable
in DMC, and they were completely decomposed 1 day after
the addition of polysulﬁdes to the DMC (Figure 2(a)).
Meanwhile, the lithium metal foil remained stable for 3 days
(Figure 2(b)).
The cells with a DMC-based electrolyte showed negligi-
ble capacities (<20 mAh/g) in the cycling tests, which
agrees with the previous results (Figure S1).28 This directly
indicates that the polysulﬁde intermediates within the cath-
ode were converted to electrochemically inactive species.
Meanwhile, the capacities remained less than 20 mAh/g
in the cycling tests of the cells containing propionyl chlo-
ride, which has similar chemical properties to DMC

stability of active materials (i.e., polysulﬁdes and lithium

Lithium polysulﬁdes were highly soluble and maintained
their chemical nature during the test period in DMF, a com-
mon polar aprotic solvent (Figure 3(a)). In contrast, the lith-
ium foil reacted vigorously with DMF, as shown in
Figure 3(b). The Li S cell with a DMF-based electrolyte
experienced drastic capacity loss, reaching 82 mAh after
10 cycles (Figure 3(c)), arising from the severe depletion of
the electrolyte due to continual side reactions between lith-
ium metal and the electrolyte. This result was reproduced
in n-butylamine with comparable chemical reactivity to
lithium (Figure S2).
The electrochemical reactions of sulfur have been inten-
sively studied in DMF.15–19,21 Previous cyclic voltammetric
experiments found that (1) the redox reactions of sulfur
proceed much faster in DMF than in glyme solvents (e.g.,
DME and DEGDME), the most common solvents for the
electrolyte of Li S cells, and (2) the second redox reaction
features high reversibility.21 We expect that if protection
methods for lithium metal anodes with superior stability are
successfully developed, the use of DMF and n-butylamine
will afford high-performance Li S batteries due to their
attractive properties of high polysulﬁde solubility and fast
reaction kinetics.
In DMSO with polar sulfonyl oxygens, the lithium poly-
sulﬁdes were soluble and formed an intensely colored solu-
tion, and lithium metal was chemically stable (Figure 4
(a) and (b)). The chemical reaction between polysulﬁdes and
lithium metal appeared to be negligible (Figure 4(c)). This
implies that lithium metal possessed sufﬁcient pseudo-
stability to prevent irreversible reactions of DMSO and poly-
sulﬁdes with lithium due to the formation of a passivation
layer on the lithium surface. Despite such positive features,
the Li S cell with DMSO-based electrolyte showed rapid
capacity deterioration before 10 cycles (Figure 4(d)). Post-
mortem analysis of the cells revealed that the lithium metal
anode was severely damaged and the electrolyte was signiﬁ-
cantly
depleted
after
20 cycles.
We
consider
that
deposition/dissolution of lithium during cycling may have
caused the passivation interphase to become unstable, and
the lithium metal to perpetually undergo irreversible reactions
with DMSO and reactive polysulﬁde intermediates. A similar
tendency
was
found
in
sulfolane,
ethyl
acetate,
and
2,2-dimethoxy propane, in which lithium metal mildly
reacted with polysulﬁdes (Figure S3).
Previous cyclic voltammetric studies of sulfur revealed
that the reduction kinetics of sulfur and polysulﬁde interme-
diates were much faster in DMSO than in glyme solvents
(e.g., DME and DEGDME).21 In this regard, we expect that
this solvent can also be utilized in the presence of a
protected Li anode and signiﬁcantly contribute to the devel-
opment of advanced Li S batteries.
DEGDME, currently the most common solvent of Li S
batteries, displayed desirable chemical properties in the
three kinds of test, similar to DMSO (Figure 5(a) and (c)).
In contrast to DMSO, the cells with a DEGDME-based
electrolyte showed excellent performance in terms of
cycling stability and rate capability (Figure 5(d) and (e)).
The capacity of the cell with this electrolyte reached as
high as 778 mAh/g at 0.25C with a good retention of 87%
after 50 cycles. Discharge capacities as high as 832, 745,
and 687 mAh/g were retained at higher current densities of
0.5C, 1C, and 1.5C, respectively. In addition, tetraethylene
glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME) with chemical reaction
characteristics similar to DEGDME exhibited good electro-
chemical performance.

In THF, a weak polar solvent, lithium polysulﬁde was
soluble and chemically stable, as shown in Figure 6(a).
Lithium metal was unreactive in both polysulﬁde solution
and pure THF solution (Figure 6(b) and (c)). Discrepancies
in cycling stability and rate property between THF- and
DEGDME-based electrolytes were observed (Figure 6
(d) and (e)). However, the cells with a THF-based electro-
lyte exhibited no noticeable difference in discharge proﬁles
at 0.05C, compared with the cells including a DEGDME-
based electrolyte (Figure 6(e)). Note that 1,4-dioxane expe-
rienced chemical and electrochemical behaviors similar to
those of THF (Figure S3).
Especially, THF-containing cells showed much lower
capacities of 275 and 111 mAh/g at the current densities of
0.5
and
1C,
respectively,
compared
with
those
of
DEGDME-containing cells (Figure 6(e)), mainly due to the
difference in the solubility of lithium polysulﬁdes between
THF and DEGDME. In THF, lithium polysulﬁdes are
slightly soluble due to their high ionic nature, whereas even
though DEGDME is also a moderately polar solvent, it pos-
sesses a pronounced ability to chelate lithium ions.29 Lith-
ium ions are strongly coordinated by a tridentate DEGDME
molecule, making DEGDME more effective in dissolving
lithium polysulﬁdes than THF. In a THF-based electrolyte
during the charge–discharge process, a considerable amount
of lithium polysulﬁdes is expected to be present in the form
of solid precipitates due to the polysulﬁde solubility limit
in the cell. This causes a signiﬁcantly high reaction over-
potential associated with chemical reactions between poly-
sulﬁdes, leading to reduced capacities at higher C rates.
Overall, our experimental results clearly veriﬁed that
(1) active materials should be stable in an electrolyte, and
(2) an electrolyte with high polysulﬁde solubility is essen-
tial for manufacturing high-performance Li S batteries.
Conclusion
To recommend the optimal type of electrolytes for Li S
batteries, we have explored 17 kinds of solvent with three
basic criteria based on an intuitive understanding that the
active materials such as lithium metal and lithium poly-
sulﬁdes should be chemically stable in Li S cells. Overall,
our results indicate that for ordinary operation of recharge-
able Li S batteries, the electrolyte solvents should meet
the following requirements: (1) polysulﬁde species should
be soluble and stable in the solvent, (2) lithium metal
should be chemically stable in the solvent, and (3) lithium
metal should not undergo a chemical reaction with the
polysulﬁdes dissolved in the solvent.
In addition, we have conﬁrmed that high polysulﬁde sol-
ubility is necessary to facilitate the sulfur cathode’s fast
reaction kinetics, which directly affect the rate performance
of Li S batteries. We anticipate that polar solvents with
high polysulﬁde solubility, such as DMSO and DMF, not
only promise advanced Li S batteries, because the electro-
chemical reactions are kinetically favorable in such sol-
vents, but also promote the practical realization of Li S
batteries, as their application will address the safety con-
cerns arising from the use of highly inﬂammable glyme-
based electrolytes.
