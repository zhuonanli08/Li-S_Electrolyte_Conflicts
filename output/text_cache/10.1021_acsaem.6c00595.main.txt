ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur battery has attracted attention as a
next-generation secondary battery because of its high energy density
(2500 Wh kg−1), low cost, and low environmental impact.
However, it has some disadvantages such as poor electronic
conductivity of Li2S, which leads to high overvoltage, and
polysulfide dissolution, which causes capacity decay and charge−
discharge inefficiency. The overvoltage during charge was overcome
by utilizing charge redox mediators (C-RM). Meanwhile, sulfolane
(SL)-based electrolytes have been reported to reduce polysulfides
elution. This paper first reports a selection of suitable C-RMs and
modifications of functional groups to obtain suitable redox
potentials in SL electrolyte. The findings of this study are
particularly useful for systems with high overvoltage, such as
those using Li2S cathode, and systems with low polysulfide solubility, since these systems are significantly affected by RM.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, redox mediators, electrolyte additives, lithium sulfide, sulfolane electrolytes
1. INTRODUCTION
In recent years, climate change, such as rising global average
temperatures, has become a global issue for all humankind, and
efforts are underway to realize a sustainable society. To achieve
this, technologies such as electric vehicles and stationary
energy storage systems for the effective use of renewable
energy have been developed, increasing the demand for
batteries with higher energy densities than those of conven-
tional lithium-ion batteries. Among these, the lithium−sulfur
battery (LSB) has attracted attention as a promising next-
generation secondary battery. This is because LSBs offer high
energy density (2500 Wh kg−1) and do not require rare metals,
utilizing sulfur as an earth-abundant and low-cost cathode
material.1 Sulfur, used as the cathode material in LSBs, is
abundant, environmentally benign, cheap, and possesses a high
theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g−1).1−7 However, the
practical application of LSBs is hindered by several key
challenges. First, both sulfur and Li2S exhibit low electronic
and ionic conductivities, leading to high overvoltage during
charge and discharge. As a result, electrochemical reactions
tend to occur only near the conductive surfaces.8 Second, the
cathode experiences approximately 79% volume expansion
during discharge due to the density difference between sulfur
(2.07 g cm−3) and Li2S (1.66 g cm−3), which can cause a loss
of electrical contact and reduce active material utilization.
Third, reaction intermediates (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) dissolve into
the electrolyte and can shuttle between the electrodes, forming
insoluble products on the lithium metal anode and leading to
overcharging and low Coulombic efficiency.2,3,8
In addition, lithium metal anodes pose safety risks due to
dendrite growth upon repeated cycling, potentially causing
short circuits. Therefore, alternative anode materials such as
graphite and silicon are often used in practical applications.9−11
To enhance the electronic conductivity of the cathode,
conductive carbon additives such as carbon black, Ketjen
black, and carbon nanotubes have been employed.12−15
Sulfolane (SL)-based electrolytes, which exhibit low poly-
sulfide solubility, have been investigated for LSBs and lithium−
metal batteries.16,17 Moreover, the addition of hydrofluor-
oether (HFE) to SL-based electrolytes reduces viscosity and
improves ionic diffusivity.18
For weakly solvating electrolyte (WSE) systems, including
HFE-based electrolytes, Huang et al. have investigated their
effectiveness in LSB.19−21 In addition, although WSEs tend to
slow the kinetics of polysulfides oxidation, the introduction of
a redox mediator (RM) has been shown to improve the rate
performance.22 Thus, RMs are effective at improving the
sluggish oxidation kinetics of polysulfides. However, the

oxidation of Li2S is also crucial for anode-free battery systems.
When Li2S is used as the cathode material, lithium-free anodes,
such as graphite or silicon, can be utilized, avoiding dendritic
lithium deposition. However, due to its low conductivity, Li2S
is electrochemically inactive and shows a large charge
overpotential (∼1 V) at the beginning of the first charge.2,23
Previous studies have reported the use of charge redox
mediators (C-RMs) to mitigate the charge overpotential. A C-
RM is oxidized at the cathode before Li2S oxidation, and the
oxidized C-RM then chemically oxidizes Li2S. This process is
repeated, promoting Li2S oxidation and reducing charge
overpotential.24−28
For
example,
1,5-bis(2-(2-(2-
methoxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)anthraquinone (AQT) has
been reported as a C-RM. However, such C-RMs have been
mainly studied in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) electrolytes, where the high polysulfide solubility
facilitates charge transfer and reduces overpotential.24 In
contrast, SL-based electrolytes have low polysulfide solubility,
resulting in limited reaction zones and higher overpotentials.
Therefore, the development of effective C-RMs for SL-based
electrolyte systems is highly desirable. Although SL is a highly
polar solvent with a large dielectric constant and dipole
moment,29 it lacks a π-conjugated structure. In contrast,
anthraquinone possesses an extended π-conjugated planar
structure with a high electron density. Consequently,
anthraquinone exhibits a poor solubility in SL-based electro-
lytes. To improve the solubility of anthraquinone-based C-
RMs, we introduced a polar functional group into the
anthraquinone framework to enhance dipole−dipole inter-
actions and hydrogen-bonding interactions with the solvent. In
this study, we investigate 1-aminoanthraquinone (amino AQ),
which exhibits higher solubility in SL-based electrolytes than
AQ, and evaluate its potential as an effective C-RM.
Additionally, amino AQ is considered for its cost advantage
in practical applications.
2. EXPERIMENTAL METHOD
2.1. Materials
Sulfur (Sigma-Aldrich), Ketjen black EC600JD (KB, Lion), poly-
(vinylidene difluoride) (PVdF, Sigma-Aldrich), N-methyl-2-pyrroli-
done (NMP, KANTO CHEMICAL), lithium sulfide (Li2S, Sigma-
Aldrich), toluene (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), lithium bis
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, KISHIDA CHEMICAL),
sulfolane (SL, KISHIDA CHEMICAL), 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (denoted as HFE, DAIKIN INDUS-
TRIES), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), anthra-
quinone-2-carboxylic acid (AQC, Tokyo Chemical Industry), 1-
aminoanthraquinone (amino AQ, Tokyo Chemical Industry), acetone
(JUNSEI CHEMICAL), and carbon paper TGPH-90 (CP, TORAY
INDUSTRIES) were used as received.
2.2. Preparation of Electrolyte
Saturated electrolyte solutions of AQC (sat-AQC) and amino AQ
(sat-amino AQ) were prepared by dissolving each compound in 1 M
LiTFSI/DOL-DME (1:1 v/v) and in a LiTFSI:SL:HFE = 1:2:2
(molar ratio). All procedures were carried out in an Ar-filled glovebox
(dew point < −90 °C).
2.3. Preparation of Cathodes
The S/KB (50:50 wt %) composite was prepared by mixing sulfur and
KB, followed by heating at 155 °C for 12 h under a nitrogen
atmosphere. 90 wt % of S/KB composite and 10 wt % of PVdF are
dispersed in NMP to prepare a slurry. Subsequently, the slurry was
coated onto aluminum foil and dried. Li2S cathode is prepared by
dropping a Li2S dispersion onto CP and vacuum drying. The
dispersion of Li2S is prepared by adding Li2S to toluene and stirring.
Unless otherwise noted, all procedures were performed in a dry room
(dew point < −50 °C).
2.4. Electrochemical Measurements
Electrochemical measurements were carried out using 2032 coin-type
cells with Li metal foil as the anode and S/KB and Li2S electrodes
(loading amounts of sulfur and Li2S are 0.3 and 0.25 mg/cm2,
respectively) as the cathode and polyolefin separator (UPORE,
UP3085, Ube Maxell). The electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio (μL/mg)
was 200 and 240 for sulfur and Li2S cathode cells, respectively. Cell
assembly was carried out in an Ar-filled glovebox (dew point < −90
°C). Cyclic voltammetry was performed using a VSP-3/EC-Lab
(Biologic) at a scanning rate of 0.5 mV s−1 with a voltage range of
1.5−3.0 V vs Li.
The galvanostatic charge−discharge measurements are performed
on TOSCAT-3100 (TOYO SYSTEM) with a voltage range of 1.5−
3.0 V vs Li.
2.5. Characterization
UV−vis absorption spectroscopy was performed using a V-630
(Jasco) instrument with the range of 350−600 nm.
The electrolytes were diluted 100-fold with acetone to dissolve all
amino AQ and reduce the absorbance to a measurable level. A
calibration curve was made using the absorbance in blank SL
electrolyte as the baseline.
2.6. DFT Conditions
The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels were calculated
using density functional theory (DFT) with the B3LYP functional and
the 6-311G** basis set. All calculations were performed with the Psi4
package.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 1a shows how C-RM reacts with Li2S and reduces the
overvoltage during charging. C-RM is oxidized at its redox
potential on an electric conductive surface. Oxidized C-RM
reacts with Li2S, and Li2S is oxidized.24,26,27 In the case of the
anthraquinone RM system, RMOx is anthraquinone (AQ) and
RMRed is anthraquinone radical (AQ•−) or anthraquinone
dianion (AQ2−). Thus, the Li2S surface facing an electrolyte
can be reduced. By repeating these steps, the overvoltage
during charging due to Li2S was decreased. On the other hand,
in the case without C-RM, Li2S is reduced around only the
three phase boundaries (Figure 1b).

The redox potentials of AQC and amino AQ were evaluated
by using a KB electrode by cyclic voltammetry. Anthraqui-
none-based redox mediators have two oxidation and reduction
peaks.30,31 The peak current due to the redox reaction of the
amino AQ was more than five times higher than that of AQC
(Figure 3a), indicating an increased number of electroactive
species participating in the redox process. This enhancement is
attributed to the higher solubility of amino AQ in the SL
electrolyte compared to AQC. Furthermore, the oxidation
peak of amino AQ was shifted by approximately 100 mV to
lower potentials relative to that of AQC. This shift can be
ascribed to the presence of the electron-donating amino group.
In addition, the diffusivity of AQC and amino AQ in the SL-
based electrolyte was evaluated by the CV method (Figure S2).
Based on the concentration of AQC (∼1 mM) and amino AQ
(∼7.5 mM), their diffusion coefficients were calculated to be
2.5 × 10−4 and 8.17 × 10−5 cm2 s−1, respectively. Note that the
peak current in the AQC system was relatively small, which
may have limited the accuracy of peak current determination
due to the double-layer charging current, consequently leading
to an overestimation of the diffusion coefficient. Thus,
although the diffusivity of amino AQ might be less than that
of AQC, the higher solubility of amino AQ increases the
apparent standard rate constant (k0) for the Li2S oxidation
reaction, resulting in a reduced overpotential and a sharper
peak profile.
DFT calculations were performed to better understand this
phenomenon. The calculations revealed that the LUMO and
HOMO levels of amino AQ were higher than those of AQC
(Figure 3c). These results indicate that amino AQ is oxidized
and reduced at a lower potential than AQC.25 These results
also suggest that the effect of the amino group modification is
greater than that of the position of the amino group (Figure
3c③,④).
Cyclic voltammetry was performed with S/KB as the
cathode to compare charge overvoltage in the Li−S cell
(Figure 3b). The addition of the amino AQ in the SL
electrolyte shifts the potential of the oxidation peak from 2.6 to
2.5 V compared with the electrolyte without amino AQ. The
onset potentials of the amino AQ in the KB electrode cell
(Figure 3a) and the S/KB cathode cell (Figure 3c) are almost
the same (2.0−2.1 V), suggesting that amino AQ effectively
works as a redox mediator and reduces charge overpotential.
To investigate the effect of redox mediators on Li/S cells
composed of SL-based electrolyte, rate performance was
compared among the cells without C-RM, with AQC, and
with amino AQ. The cell with amino AQ indicates the highest
rate performance (Figure 4a), the discharge capacities were
1239, 1109, 1064, 1015, 943, 837, and 660 mAh g−1 at 0.05,
0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 C. In contrast, the cells without
C-RM and with AQC, the discharge capacities were 204 and
147 mAh g−1 at 4.0 C, respectively. The low discharge capacity
at 4 C caused by a large overpotential (Figure S3). That is, the

Cycle performances using SL electrolytes with low solubility
of polysulfides are compared with those of DOL/DME
electrolytes, which are commonly used in LSB, to improve
cycle performance. In case of using DOL/DME electrolyte
without R-CM, the discharge capacity at the 100th cycle is
29.0% against that of the first cycle (Figure 4d). On the other
hand, when amino AQ was incorporated into DOL/DME
electrolyte, the discharge capacity at the 100th cycle became
worse to 8.1% against that of the first cycle, and overcharging
was confirmed in first cycle (Figure S5). To investigate the
contribution of amino AQ in the overcharging, the redox
capacity of amino AQ itself in the voltage range of the Li−S
battery. In the sulfur-free cell, the overcharge capacity arising
from the shuttle phenomenon of amino AQ was 3.21 × 10−3
mAh (corresponding to 0.4% of the overcharge capacity in the
Li−S cell), as determined from charge−discharge measure-
ments at a current of 0.01 mA (approximately 0.03 C), as
shown in Figure S6. It is extremely smaller than the capacity of
overcharging at the Li−S cell. Considering that overcharging
was not confirmed in DOL/DME electrolyte without the C-
RM, a combination of polysulfides and amino AQ could cause
shuttle phenomenon (Figure S7). On the other hand, when SL
electrolytes were used, a high capacity retention rate was
confirmed with and without amino AQ (Figure 4b). When
amino AQ was added, the discharge capacity was higher than
that of the blank SL electrolyte up to the 40th cycle, but
thereafter the discharge capacity remained at the same level.
This result suggests that the addition of amino AQ facilitates
the formation of S8 even at less electrically conductive areas at
a lower cycle number. It is possible that the later-stage
discharge reaction occurs during the initial cycles by adding
amino AQ, making it more difficult for the later-stage discharge
reaction to occur as the cycles progress. Overcharging was not
observed when amino AQ was incorporated into SL electrolyte
(Figure S8), presumably because polysulfides were insoluble in
SL electrolyte, suppressing overcharging due to the combina-
tion effect of polysulfide and amino AQ that is observed in the
DOL/DME system.
To investigate the feasibility of batteries composed of
lithium-free anode such as silicon and graphite, a charge and
discharge test was carried out using Li2S cathode. When SL
electrolytes without or with AQC were used, the cutoff
potential was reached quickly during charging and little charge
and discharge capacity was observed (Figure 4e). In contrast,
when amino AQ was added, although overvoltage was
observed in the first cycle of charging, reversible capacities in
excess of 500 mAh g−1 were confirmed. This suggests that
amino AQ works as C-RM, whereas AQC does not. This result
shows that the reduction of charge overvoltage by the addition
of amino AQ is more pronounced in systems with high
overvoltage such as the Li2S cathode.
4. CONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed amino AQ, which has a lower redox
potential than AQC, as a charge redox mediator. Replacing the
carboxyl group in C-RM with an amino group improved its
solubility in the SL electrolyte and enhanced its oxidizing
ability toward Li2S as a redox mediator. The addition of amino
AQ to SL electrolyte increased the specific capacity from 204
to 660 mAh g−1 at 4C. This is particularly beneficial in systems
with high overvoltage, such as Li2S cathodes, where charging
would not proceed without the additive; on the other hand,
adding amino AQ makes charging and discharging possible.
When improving the electrolyte and electrodes to improve
cycle characteristics, it is important to develop a redox
mediator with an appropriate redox potential in order to
simultaneously improve rate characteristics. This paper helps
design redox mediators, including their redox potential. In this
paper, the selection of a molecule with a more suitable redox
potential by changing the functional group of the redox
mediator is useful for the design of electrolyte additives.
