ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are identified as one of
the most promising next-generation battery technologies. However,
commercialization of Li−S batteries has not been widespread due to
severe technical challenges, such as lithium polysulfide dissolution and
shuttling inherent to the battery chemistry. In this work, we demonstrate
a strategy of integrating a nanoengineered sulfur cathode with a
functionalized electrolyte to overcome some of the major technical
barriers and realize the high specific capacity and high-performance
potentials of Li−S batteries. The nanoengineered sulfur cathode,
architectured by applying an ultrathin film of nanolayer-polymer-
coated-carbons on a sulfur electrode, is able to achieve a high discharge
specific capacity of ∼1600 mAh/g, approaching sulfur’s theoretical
specific capacity of 1672 mAh/g, due to the increased redox kinetics and
the PS-trapping power. The functionalized electrolyte is designed by utilizing, for the first time, 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-1,4-
dimethoxybutane (FDMB) as a cosolvent in the Li−S electrolyte, which helps maintain the high specific capacity over extended
cycles due to the strong PS-trapping power enabled by FDMB. This strategy rendered not only the highest possible specific
discharge capacity but also an unprecedented cycle stability (90% capacity retention after 500 cycles at 1 C rate) in the resultant Li−
S batteries. The unprecedented level of performance, along with the near-theoretical high specific capacity, was realized without
using complex processes and costly materials. The synergistic strategy used in this work represents a significant advancement of the
Li−S battery technology, with unprecedented high specific capacity, which can render high energy density, robust cycle life, and
enhanced safety, toward commercialization.
KEYWORDS: Li−S batteries, lithium polysulfide trapping, sulfur cathode with nanolayer polymer coated carbons,
fluorinated ether-based electrolyte, first-principle calculation of polysulfide binding energy
1. INTRODUCTION
Li−S batteries have garnered significant attention as promising
energy storage systems due to their exceptionally high
theoretical specific capacity of 1672 mAh/(g, sulfur), high
specific energy of ∼2600 Wh/kg, abundance of active
materials, and low cost.1−3 Li−S batteries are attractive for a
wide range of potential applications, e.g., electric vehicles,
unmanned aerial vehicles, aerospace, maritime, and future
urban air transportations, where high specific energy and
energy density are paramount factors, and for grid energy
storage where battery energy density as well as cost are primary
business-driven factors.4−6 However, the commercialization of
Li−S batteries has not been widespread due to severe technical
challenges,7−9 including the lithium polysulfide (PS) dis-
solution and the associated shuttling effect, a major factor
responsible for fast capacity degradation over cycling, a well-
known phenomenon in Li−S batteries.10,11
In this work, a novel strategy was implemented where a
nanoengineered sulfur cathode coated with an ultrathin film of
nanolayer-polymer-coated-carbons (NPC) was integrated with
a 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-1,4-dimethoxybutane (FDMB) based
electrolyte. To the best of our knowledge, FDMB was used
in Li−S batteries for the first time in this work. The integrated
strategy yielded a synergistic effect leading to the realization of
unprecedented high specific capacity of ∼1600 mAh/g,
approaching sulfur’s theoretical specific capacity of 1672
mAh/g, and robust cycle stability, maintaining 90% of the

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparations of Baseline and NPC Coated Sulfur
Cathodes. The baseline sulfur cathode (Baseline-S) is composed
of 70 wt % sulfur, 15 wt % carbon black, 5 wt % carbon nanotubes,
and 10 wt % polyvinylidene fluoride (HSV 900), with a typical
loading of ∼2 mg/cm2 corresponding to an areal capacity ∼2.3 mAh/
cm2. Future work will study higher sulfur cathode loadings (areal
capacities) for practical applications.
In the preparation of NPC, 0.5 g of commercially available high-
surface-area carbons (MTI, BET surface area of 1694 m2/g) and 0.05
g of poly(4-vinylpyridine) (P4VP, Sigma-Aldrich) were dispersed in
100 mL of ethanol. The suspension was sonicated with an ultrasonic
cleaner (VWR Symphony) at 35 kHz and 60 °C for 2 h to ensure
thorough dispersion and uniform adsorption of P4VP onto the carbon
surface. After sonication, the mixture was left undisturbed for ∼12 h.
After removing the residual solvent, the precipitated NPC was first air-
dried in a fume hood for 12 h and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60
°C for 12 h.
In preparation of the NPC-S cathode, 0.6 mg of the NPC powders
were placed in 10 mL of ethanol and stirred by a magnetic bar for ∼10
min at room temperature, resulting in uniformly suspended NPC in
solution. The NPC solution was applied to the sulfur cathode by drop
casting. In this study, a drop of the NPC solution ∼50 μL was applied
to a sulfur cathode disk of 12 mm diameter. Visual inspection showed
uniform spreading of the NPC solution on the surface of the sulfur
cathode disk without spillage due to low surface tension and low
viscosity of the ethanol solvent, which yielded an estimated NPC
loading of 2.65 μg/cm2. The coated sulfur cathode (NPC-S) was
dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 30 min. The NPC-S disks (12
mm diameter) showed no measurable weight increase when measured
with a lab balance of 0.01 mg precision (Aczet CY 165C) before and
after the NPC film coating. Our preliminary study of the impact of
NPC film thickness suggests an optimal NPC coating is needed to
yield the maximum discharge specific capacity. This will be
investigated further in future work.
2.2. Preparation of Baseline and FDMB-Based Electrolytes.
The baseline electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1 M lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) 1:1 vol % with 1.5 wt %
lithium nitrate LiNO3 (Baseline electrolyte). The FDMB-based
electrolyte consisted of 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME/FDMB 1:1:2
vol % (abbreviated as FDMB-electrolyte or FE-electrolyte). The

2.3. Material Characterizations. Ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis)
spectroscopy was conducted by using a Thermo Scientific Evolution
UV−vis Spectrophotometer and a 560 μL volume quartz cuvette.
Samples containing PS for UV−vis test were prepared the same way
as the PS adsorption test (Section 2.4). UV−vis reference runs were
taken for the corresponding solvents without PS.
Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopies of the solvent
and electrolyte samples were conducted using a Nanalysis 60e
instrument operating at a 1H frequency of 60 MHz. The samples were
suspended in deuterated chloroform CDCl3. Each sample was
measured with 16 NMR scans to ensure measurement accuracy.
In situ Raman spectroscopy for PS detection was performed by
using a Raman spectrometer (ffTA FORAM X3) at a laser wavelength
of 532 nm. A split cell (MTI) with a Raman transparent quartz
window was used for the in situ Raman spectroscopy measurement
(Figure S2a).
2.4. PS Adsorption Test. An exemplary PS-solution with a
nominal composition of Li2S8 was prepared by dissolving 0.00184 g of
lithium sulfide (Li2S) and 0.00898 g of sulfur (S) in 2 mL of 1:1 vol %
DOL/DME or 1:1:2 vol % DOL/DME/FDMB 1:1:2 vol %. The
mixture was stirred with a magnetic bar at 35 °C overnight inside the
glovebox. For the PS adsorption test, 0.1 g of the test material
(solvent or electrolyte) was added to a 5 mL PS-solution and stirred
for 30 min at room temperature in the glovebox.
2.5. Electrochemical Characterization. The Li−S batteries
were prepared by using coin-type cells (e.g., CR2032). Commercial
polyolefin membranes (Celgard 2320, PP/PE/PP trilayer) were
employed as separators. Lithium metal disks were used as anodes. The
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S) was maintained at approximately 35
μL/mg. Future work will study lower E/S ratios for practical
applications.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was measured using a potentiostat/
galvanostat (Corrtest Instruments, model CS310) at a scan rate of
0.01 mV/s, between 1.8 and 2.5 V (vs Li/L+). Electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured using the CS310 unit
with a frequency range of 1 mHz to 100 kHz and an AC voltage of 10
mV.
Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests of the Li−S battery cells were
performed by using a battery test station (Neware BTS4000). The
Li−S cells were initially cycled at a C/20 current rate for two charge/
discharge cycles (formation cycles) with a voltage window of 1.8−2.5
V. For long-term cycle life tests, the Li−S battery cells were cycled at
higher current rates to expedite and assess cycle stability at high rates.
All battery performance tests were conducted at room temperature.
Li plating/stripping stability tests were conducted using Li/Li
symmetric coin cells, where the lithium chips were separated by a
separator (Celgard 2320). About 100 μL of the respective electrolyte
was added to each cell. An apparent critical current density was
determined by the onset of the rapid voltage polarization rise when
scanned at a current increment of 0.062 mA/cm2 per cycle (1 h). To
evaluate the repetitive Li plating/stripping behavior of the electro-
lytes, the Li/Li symmetric cells were first set at rest for 6 h and then
cycled at 3 mA/cm2 with 30 min of plating and stripping (a total of 60
min per cycle) at room temperature.
2.6. Computational Modeling. First-principle density functional
theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio
Simulation Package (VASP).13 The projector augmented-wave
(PAW)14 method was employed to describe the core−electron
interactions, and the exchange-correlation effects were treated using
the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE)15 functional. A plane-wave
cutoff energy of 520 eV was used for basis set expansion. To improve
the accuracy of the calculations, we included nonspherical
contributions on PAW spheres and used an additional support grid
in the augmented sphere to reduce errors in computing force. The
energy convergence criterion of 10−5 eV and the force convergence
criterion of 0.01 eV/Å were used. Only one k-point at the center of
the Brillouin zone was used to simulate the molecules. Ionic
relaxations were performed by using the conjugate gradient algorithm
with a time step of 0.5 fs. The total energy calculations employed a
Gaussian smearing method with a smearing width of 0.1 eV.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Electrochemical Evaluation of Li−S Batteries.
Figure 2a illustrates voltage profiles of Li−S coin cells of the
NPC-S cathode/FDMB electrolyte (NPC-S_FE) and Baseline-
S/Baseline electrolyte (Baseline) during the first charge/
discharge cycle at a constant current rate of C/20 at room
temperature. The NPC-S_FE based cell attained a high
discharge specific capacity of 1580 mAh/g, which is >94%
the theoretical specific capacity of sulfur (1672 mAh/g). This
performance leads to an estimated specific energy ∼2400 Wh/
kg based on the active sulfur. Whereas the Baseline (control)
cell delivered 1100 mAh/g (66% of the theoretical) discharge
specific capacity. It is interesting to note that, unlike the
Baseline-S cell where the first-discharge voltage drops abruptly
near the end of the discharge, the NPC-S_FE cell exhibited a
gradual first-discharge voltage slope from the major plateau to
the discharge cutoff voltage which is similar to the published
work with F-ethers solvents.16 Figure S1.1 shows the evolution
of the voltage profiles as a function of cycle numbers (up to
100 cycles) where both types of the cells exhibited a relatively
gradual slope near the end of the discharge. The first cycle
Coulombic efficiencies (CEs) for the NPC_FE and the
Baseline cells are 91.9% and 94.8%, respectively. The lower
CE of the NPC_FE cell is likely attributed to the formation of
a more robust fluorinated SEI film17 that is further discussed in
Section 3.5.
Figure 2b shows cyclic voltammetry (CV) for the NPC-
S_FE and the Baseline cells at a scan rate of 0.01 mV/s
between 1.8 and 2.5 V, corresponding to 20 h per half cycle.
The Baseline cell showed three cathodic (reduction) peaks
associated with the formation of various polysulfide spe-
cies.18−21 The peak at 2.30 V corresponds to the short
discharge voltage plateau (Figure 2a) with the formation of the
high-order (long-chain) PS species (Li2Sn, 6 ≤n ≤8, such as
S8
2−and S6
2−). The peak at 2.08 V corresponds to the long
discharge voltage plateau (Figure 2a) with the formation of the
intermediate PS species (Li2Sn, 3 ≤n ≤5). The peak at 2.02 V
corresponds to the formation of the low-order (short-chain)
PS species, Li2S2, and the final product, Li2S. The NPC-S_FE
cell, on the other hand, showed only two cathodic peaks at
2.30 and 2.07 V, corresponding to the formation of the high-
order and the intermediate PS species, respectively. The lack of
the third cathodic peak of the NPC-S_FE cell near 2.0 V is
attributed to the fact that the transition from the intermediate
to low-order PS species near 2 V occurred at a more gradual
rate, as indicated by the more sloppy discharge curve from the
long plateau to the discharge cutoff voltage (Figure 1a), due to
the more thorough conversion of the intermediate PS species
to the insoluble Li2S2/Li2S final products rendering an
observed higher discharge specific capacity of the NPC-S_FE
cell, compared with the Baseline cell.
Both cells showed two anodic (oxidation) peaks, around
2.28 and 2.35 V, corresponding to the oxidation of the PS
species up to S6
2−and the elemental sulfur, respectively.20 A
closer look of the oxidation peaks showed that, while the sulfur
oxidation peaks of the two cells are in proximity, the
intermediate PS oxidation peak of the NPC-S_FE cell is 10
mV lower than that of the Baseline cell; this suggests a higher
oxidation reaction kinetics of the NPC-S_FE cell than the
Baseline cell. As a result, the NPC-S_FE cell showed larger
areas for both the anodic and cathodic scans than the Baseline.
Since the area of the CV scan is proportional to the specific
capacity, the larger area of the NPC-S_FE cell is consistent
with the higher specific capacity shown in Figure 2a, compared
with the Baseline cell. The improved conversion kinetics may
explain the disappearance of the discharge voltage dip around
2.1 V (Figure 2a) from the NPC-S_FE cell compared to the
Baseline cell.
To probe the electrochemical redox reversibility, multiple
CV cycles were carried out for the two types of cells at a higher
scan rate of 0.05 mV/s between 1.8 and 2.5 V, corresponding
to 4 h per half cycle, as shown in Figure S1.2. While both cells
exhibited overlapping of the second and the third redox peaks
after the first scan, the NPC-S_FE cell demonstrated sharper/
narrower anodic and cathodic peaks than the Baseline cell,
suggesting enhanced sulfur/PS conversion kinetics.22,23
Another way to gauge the catalytic ability is to calculate the
C2/C1 ratio, where C1 and C2 are the discharge specific
capacities at voltage plateaus of 2.3 and 2.1 V (Figure 2a)
representing the amount of high-order PSs produced and the
efficiency of reducing them to the low-order PSs and to the
final products, respectively.24 The C2/C1 ratio of the NPC-
S_FE cell is 50% higher than that of the Baseline cell,
suggesting a much improved catalytic efficiency of the NPC-
S_FE cell compared to the Baseline cell. These observations
suggest the increased redox kinetics and the higher conversion
efficiency of the sulfur species, analogous to some sort of
catalytic effect,7 helped the realization of the near theoretical
discharge specific capacity of the NPC-S_FE cell.
Figure 2c depicts the specific discharge capacity and the
Coulombic efficiency (CE) vs cycle number for the NPC-
S_FE and Baseline cells. These cells were cycled at a higher C
rate (C/10) during the long-term cycling than the initial
formation cycles (C/20). The NPC-S_FE cell demonstrated a
much-improved cycle stability maintaining a high discharge
specific capacity of 1216 mAh/g, corresponding to 84%
capacity retention after 200 cycles, compared with 425 mAh/g
(53% capacity retention) of the Baseline cell. Both types of
cells exhibited a high CE of >99% after 200 cycles, which
indicates that the oxidative side reactions during the charging
process are insignificant, possibly due to the relatively low
charge cutoff voltage of 2.5 V (vs Li/Li+) in these Li−S cells.
The excellent long-term cyclability, as well as the rate
capability, of the NPC-S_FE cell is further demonstrated in
Figure 1c where the cell was cycled at a high rate of 1 C. Even
after 500 continuous cycles, the cell was able to deliver a high
discharge specific capacity of ∼980 mAh/g, corresponding to
∼90% capacity retention or ∼0.026% capacity decay per cycle,
and ∼98% CE. A detailed analysis showed that the capacity
degradation versus cycling cannot be described with a linear
dependence over the entire cycling. Rather, it follows closely a
two-stage linear dependent pattern,25 as shown in Figure S1.3,
where the capacity fading rates at the 0.1 C cycling are 0.16%/
cycle and 0.41%/cycle during 1−100 cycles and 0.02%/cycle
and 0.08%/cycle during 101−200 cycles for the NPC-FE and
the Baseline cells, respectively. This is possibly due to the fact
that the mechanisms leading to battery degradation (e.g., SEI
formation, dissolution of PSs, and side reactions) reach a more
stable state over cycling. The capacity fading rate of the NPC-
FE cell is 3−4 times slower than that of the Baseline cell as a
result of the synergistic approach, as explained in detail in this
section. Interestingly, when cycled at 1 C rate, the NPC-FE cell
exhibited a capacity gain of 0.17%/cycle during the first 100
cycles, which is possibly due to the activation of the sulfur
active material at the high C rate cycling, before reaching a

3.2. Rate Capability, EIS, PS Adsorption, and UV−Vis
Tests. Figure 3a shows a rate capability test for the two types
of cells indicated. The NPC-S_FE cell exhibited consistently
higher discharge specific capacities at various C rates up to a 2
C rate (1 C corresponding to 1672 mAh/g) than the Baseline
cell. Specifically, the discharge specific capacities (mAh/g) for
the NPC-S_FE/Baseline cells at various C rates are 1585/
1063@0.05 C, 1481/884@0.1 C, 1262/743@0.2 C, 1056/
627@0.5 C, 859/535@1 C, 683/423@2 C, and 1451/845
upon returning to 0.1 C. Notably, the NPC-S_FE cell
demonstrated a more favorable C rate behavior by maintaining
stable capacities over five cycles at each C rate and regaining
98% of the capacity when returned to 0.1 C, compared with
96% of the Baseline cell.
Figure 3b shows the electrochemical impedance spectros-
copy (EIS) complex plane plots of the NPC-S_FE and
Baseline-S cells after being cycled for 50 cycles at 0.1 C. The
EIS spectra were analyzed using an equivalent electrical circuit,
R0 + CPE1∥R1 + CPE2∥R2 + W, where R0 is bulk resistance,
CPE stands for constant phase element, the symbol ∥
represents parallel circuit, CPE∥R represents interfacial
impedance, and W is Warburg (mass transfer) impedance at
low frequencies. The fitting parameters are given in Table S1.
The left semicircle (CPE1∥R1 corresponding to high
frequencies) is attributed to the solid-electrolyte interface
(SEI) impedance at the Li anode−electrolyte interface. The
right semicircle (CPE2∥R2 corresponding to intermediate
frequencies) is associated with charge transfer impedance at
the cathode−electrolyte interface. While the bulk resistance R0
is comparable for the two types of cells, the Baseline cell
exhibited about 1.3 and 8.0 times higher interfacial resistance
(R1 and R2, respectively) than the NPC-S_FE cell, after being
cycled for 50 cycles. The EIS data suggest a more stable SEI
(lower R1) and a higher charge transfer (lower R2)
characteristic of the NPC-S_FE cell compared with the
Baseline cell. The EIS data are consistent with the long-term
cycle performance data shown above (Figures 1c and 2c).

3.3. In Situ Raman and NMR Spectroscopies. Figure 4a
shows the in situ Raman spectra of the Baseline and NPC-

3.4. Computational Modeling. To elucidate the
experimental findings regarding PS-trapping capabilities, we
calculated the binding energies (Eb) of various polysulfides
(Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8) with binary solvent mixtures FDMB/
DOL and FDMB/DME, and the ternary solvent mixture
FDMB/DME/DOL. PS-adsorbent binding energies were
calculated using the equation, Eb = (EPS + EAds) −EFinal,
where EPS is the energy of the PS, EAds is the energy of the
adsorbent, and EFinal is the energy of the PS−adsorbent
complex. The results are summarized in Table 1, where the
average PS−adsorbent binding energies are in the order of
FDMB/DME/DOL (1.49 eV) > FDMB/DME (1.05 eV) >
FDMB/DOL (0.95 eV) > FDMB (0.38 eV). This trend is in
excellent agreement with the PS soaking results (compare
Table 1 with Table S2 and Figure 3) that show that the ternary
mixture binds strongly to the PS species.
Figure 5 shows the final relaxed structures of charge density
distributions of polysulfides bonding with the single (FDMB),
binary (FDMB/DME, FDMB/DOL), and ternary (FDMB/
DME/DOL) mixtures, respectively. The charge density
presents redistribution of the charge upon bonding, expressed
as Δρ(r) = ρsys(r) −∑iρi(r), where ρsys(r) is the total charge
density of the system, including all components (adsorbents
and polysulfides), and ρi(r) is the charge density of each
individual component (adsorbents or polysulfides). When
polysulfides bind with FDMB, charge from fluorine F transfers
to one of the two Li ions in a polysulfide, leading to a positive
binding energy. However, this process leaves the other Li ion
in the polysulfide free to bind with other electronegative
elements/molecules. In the binary mixture of FDMB/DME or
FDMB/DOL, this free Li ion binds to oxygen O in DME or
DOL, thereby increasing the binding energy further. In the
ternary mixture FDMB/DME/DOL, the binding energy
increases even more because the DME/DOL molecules tend
to bind among themselves (with a binding energy of 70 meV)
when brought sufficiently close due to the fact that the
electronegative oxygen in DME and DOL seeks to be closer to
the electropositive Li ions in polysulfides. In summary, our
calculations suggest the PS bonding in the binary mixtures
(FDMB/DOL, FDMB/DME) is driven by electrostatic
interactions between the electropositive Li ions and electro-
negative S ions in PSs, whereas in the ternary mixture (FDMB/
DOL/DME), the intermolecular interactions between the
cosolvents (DOL and DME) as well as the electrostatic
interactions between PSs and the cosolvents (the same as in
the binary mixture) drive the PS trapping.

scalable for the large-scale commercialization of Li−S batteries.

the FDMB-containing electrolyte is advantageous over the
LiNO3 additive because the fluorinated SEI films are dense and

the advantages of anodic stability and safety, the most pivotal
role FDMB plays is its strong ability to trap PSs, rendering for
the first time the observed long-term cycle stability in the
resultant Li−S batteries, compared with the Baseline electro-
lyte with LiNO3.

5. CONCLUSIONS
The novel strategy of combining the NPC nanoengineered
sulfur cathode (NPC-S) and a powerful PS-trapping capable
FDMB-based electrolyte yielded a high discharge specific
capacity ∼1600 mAh/g, approaching the theoretical limit of
sulfur, while maintaining a robust cycle stability (90% capacity
retention after 500 cycles at 1 C rate) in the resultant Li−S
batteries. Our electrochemical, analytical (chemical and
physical), and computer modeling results suggest that the
unprecedented performance is a result of the synergistic effect
where the NPC-S is responsible for boosting the near-
theoretical specific capacity due to the increased redox kinetics
and the NPC’s PS-trapping capability, while the FDMB-based
electrolyte is responsible for maintaining the near-theoretical
specific capacity over extended cycles due to the added,
powerful PS-trapping capability afforded by the FDMB-based
electrolyte. The F-ether solvent FDMB, which is applied, for
the first time, to the Li−S batteries in this study, has proven to
be an outstanding cosolvent in the electrolyte to overcome
some of the key challenges of Li−S batteries, such as PS
dissolution/shuttling, when working in concert with the NPC-
S architecture. Other unique merits of our synergistic strategy
include unprecedented performance without sacrificing energy
density, economically scalable processing, and robust Li anode
protection. The synergistic strategy used in this work is shown
to have a significant implication in advancing the promising
Li−S battery technology toward commercialization with
unprecedented high specific capacity, which can render high
energy density, robust cycle life, and enhanced safety. The
performance dependence on practical sulfur loading and the E/
S ratio requires further investigation. Leveraging the promising
strategy demonstrated in this work, future work will investigate
the advanced Li−S technology optimizing sulfur cathode
loadings and E/S ratios for practical commercialization and
applications.
