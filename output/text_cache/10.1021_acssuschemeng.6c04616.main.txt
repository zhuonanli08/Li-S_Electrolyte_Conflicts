Trade-Off between Anode Stability and Cathode Kinetics

ABSTRACT: Despite their ultrahigh theoretical energy density of
2600 Wh kg−1, the practical application of lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries is severely hindered by a kinetic dilemma: traditional elec-
trolytes fail to balance the conﬂicting demands of the sulfur cathode
(which requires strong solvation for polysulﬁde conversion) and the
lithium anode (which requires weak solvation for stability). To
address this, we propose a rational electrolyte design by introducing
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as a “molecular trigger” into a weakly
solvating THF/TTE baseline electrolyte. Multiscale characteriza-
tions and molecular dynamics simulations reveal that the precise
incorporation of DME reconstructs the primary Li+ solvation
sheath into an anion-participating conﬁguration. This optimized structure not only eﬀectively activates the deep conversion kinetics
of polysulﬁdes at the cathode but also thermodynamically drives the formation of a robust, dense, and inorganic-rich (LiF/Li2S) solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) at the anode. Consequently, the optimized electrolyte enables dendrite-free lithium deposition with a
Coulombic eﬃciency of >99% at a high current density of 4 mA cm−2, achieving stable cycling for over 1000 h in symmetric cells.
Furthermore, the corresponding Li−S full cells deliver a high reversible capacity of ∼600 mAh g−1 (∼800 mAh g−1 with LiNO3 addi-
tives) and excellent rate capability. This study provides deep fundamental insights into solvation-structure-regulated interfacial chem-
istry, presenting a viable strategy for developing high-energy, long-lifespan Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium metal anode, lithium polysulﬁdes, solvation structure, electrolytes, lithium−sulfur batteries
■INTRODUCTION
The pursuit of high-energy-density storage systems has posi-
tioned lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries at the forefront of
next-generation battery technologies, owing to their ultrahigh
theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1.1,2 However, the
practical realization of this promise is notoriously hampered
by the intertwined challenges arising from the dissolution-
precipitation chemistry of the sulfur cathode and the highly
reactive lithium metal anode.3 On the cathode side, the soluble
lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) intermediates, while essential for the
conversion reaction, induce a severe shuttle eﬀect, leading to
active material loss and rapid capacity decay.4 Concurrently,
on the anode side, these migrating LiPSs trigger parasitic reac-
tions with the Li metal, accelerating the depletion of limited
lithium reservoirs and fostering the growth of deleterious Li
dendrites.5
The electrode kinetics of LiPSs lies at the heart of these chal-
lenges, dictating both the conversion eﬃciency at the cathode
and the corrosion rate at the anode.6,7 Intriguingly, these kinet-
ics present a fundamental dilemma. Rapid cathode kinetics is
desirable for achieving high sulfur utilization and rate capability,
yet it often implies a high concentration of dissolved LiPSs,
which exacerbates the shuttle eﬀect and anode corrosion.8
Conversely, mitigating the shuttle eﬀect through weakly solvat-
ing electrolytes that limit LiPS solubility can inevitably impede
cathode reaction kinetics, leading to reduced capacity and
increased polarization.9,10 This creates a seemingly irreconcil-
able conﬂict between cathode activity and anode stability.
Recent electrolyte engineering eﬀorts have primarily bifurcated
into two paths: strong solvating power electrolytes for high
cathode kinetics and weak solvating power electrolytes for
anode protection.11,12 However, a strategy that intelligently nav-
igates the middle ground to simultaneously satisfy the stringent
requirements of both electrodes remains elusive. Most reported
weakly solvating systems, while excellent for stabilizing lithium
anodes, often lead to unsatisfactory sulfur cathode performance,

Herein, we report a fundamental discovery and a rational
design principle that resolves this kinetic dilemma. We seren-
dipitously found that a weakly solvating electrolyte based on tet-
rahydrofuran (THF) and Tetraﬂuoroethyl-tetraﬂuoropropyl
ether (TTE) endows the lithium metal anode with exceptional
electrochemical stability yet renders the Li−S battery nearly
non-functional due to the completely hindered cathode pro-
cess.14 This stark contrast serves as a perfect model system, iso-
lating the anode and cathode requirements. Guided by this
ﬁnding, we introduced a strongly solvating co-solvent, 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME), as a “molecular trigger” to precisely
tune the LiPS solvation structure. Furthermore, Li−S cells con-
taining a DME/THF-based electrolyte also exhibited improved
performance metrics when compared to cells that only con-
tained THF-based electrolyte, including a markedly improved
capacity and cycling stability (e.g., 0 vs 600 mAh g−1 after 100
cycles at 0.5 C). Our systematic investigation reveals that an
optimal, intermediate solvation strength is critical to “activate”
the sulfur cathode kinetics without compromising the superior
anode stability inherited from the THF/TTE matrix.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Material Preparation
Battery-grade ethylene glycol dimethyl ether (DME), tetrahydrofuran
(THF), lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuor-
oethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (TTE, 99.9%), and lithium
nitrate (LiNO3) were employed as dissolved substances prior to elec-
trolyte preparation. All solvents were dehydrated overnight using
molecular sieves. Subsequently, the corresponding electrolytes were
weighed according to the prescribed ratios and prepared in an argon-
ﬁlled glove box. The mixtures were stirred magnetically for 12 h to
ensure complete dissolution. The electrolyte compositions investigated
in this study were formulated based on molar ratios, with the speciﬁc
grades and molar proportions detailed in Table S1.
Materials Characterizations
The physicochemical properties of the electrolytes were characterized
using a suite of analytical techniques. Liquid-state 7Li NMR spectra
were acquired on a Bruker Avance NEO 600 spectrometer. Raman
spectroscopy was performed on a HORIBA Scientiﬁc LabRAM HR
Evolution system with a 633 nm excitation laser. Fourier-transform
infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted on a Nicolet 670 instru-
ment, scanning the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1. X-ray pho-
toelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on a
Thermo Scientiﬁc K-Alpha spectrometer. Surface morphology was
examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a TESCAN
VEGA3 SBH microscope. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was per-
formed on a CS2350H electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of
0.1 mV s−1. The cutoﬀvoltage was set to 6 V, and the measurements
were performed using Li||stainless steel coin cells.
Electrochemical Measurements
(1) Asymmetric half-cell tests. The cells underwent an activation pre-
treatment involving constant-current lithium deposition at a current
density of 0.2 mA cm−2 for 10 h (equivalent to a deposition capacity
of 2 mAh cm−2), followed by lithium stripping at the same current
density up to a cut-oﬀvoltage of 1.0 V. Subsequent cycling tests were
performed at current densities of 1 and 2 mA cm−2 with a ﬁxed areal
capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 and a discharge cut-oﬀvoltage of 1.0 V.
These measurements were used to analyze the kinetics and interfacial
behavior of lithium deposition/stripping. (2) Symmetric cell tests.
Cells were cycled at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capac-
ity of 1 mAh cm−2, with equal durations for discharge (lithium deposi-
tion) and charge (lithium stripping). The long-term cycling stability
was evaluated to assess the interfacial stability and electrochemical
reversibility of the lithium metal anode. (3) Full-cell tests. The voltage
window was set between 1.7 and 2.8 V for electrochemical perfor-
mance evaluation. For the full-cell conﬁguration, the sulfur loading of
the cathode was 1.25 mg cm−2. the N/P ratio was kept at 16.8, the
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio(E/S) was ﬁxed at 35, and the thickness of
the lithium foil was 100 μm.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As illustrated in Figure 1, the solvation structure of the electro-
lyte plays a decisive role in governing the electrochemical
behavior and interfacial kinetics of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batte-
ries. In the baseline weakly solvating electrolyte employed in
our previous work (LiFSI + THF/TTE, denoted as H1), the
voltage−areal capacity proﬁles of the full cell (Figure 1a) reveal
pronounced polarization and signiﬁcant separation between
charge and discharge plateaus during the initial cycles (compar-
ison between the 2nd and 5th cycles), accompanied by rapid
capacity decay. These features clearly indicate sluggish conver-
sion kinetics of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) and deteriorated
electrochemical reversibility within this system.15 Although con-
ventional weakly solvating electrolytes eﬀectively suppress para-
sitic reactions and stabilize the lithium metal anode by limiting
LiPS dissolution, they do so at the expense of active material
utilization at the cathode, resulting in low speciﬁc capacity
and poor cycling stability.16 To overcome this intrinsic trade-
oﬀbetween cathode and anode performance, a novel electrolyte
system with a rationally engineered solvation structure was
designed and constructed (Figure 1b,c). By precisely incorpo-
rating 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), a co-solvent with strong
solvating capability toward LiPSs, into the original weakly sol-
vating network, synergistic optimization of both electrodes
was achieved. This electrolyte design not only promotes rapid
and uniform conversion of polysulﬁde intermediates, but also
eﬀectively suppresses parasitic reactions on the anode, thereby
enabling dense and stable lithium deposition. Cyclic voltamme-
try (CV) measurements (Figure 1d) further validate this design
strategy.17 In the absence of DME, the weakly solvating electro-
lytes, regardless of concentration, exhibit broadened peaks and
signiﬁcant potential shifts, indicative of severe polarization. In
contrast, the DME-containing T1 electrolyte demonstrates
markedly enhanced redox peak currents, reduced peak separa-
tion, and sharp, symmetric peak shapes. Meanwhile, the sulfur
cathode displays two well-deﬁned peaks, conﬁrming improved
reaction kinetics and highly reversible sulfur redox processes.18
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Figure 1e,f)
results are in excellent agreement with the CV analysis. The
weakly solvating electrolyte without DME exhibits a large inter-
facial charge-transfer resistance (Rct) in the Nyquist plots. Upon
introducing varying proportions of DME (T1, T2, and
T3 systems), a substantial decrease in Rct is observed.
Notably, the T3 electrolyte with the highest DME content
shows the smallest semicircles in both high and medium fre-
quency regions, indicating signiﬁcantly accelerated interfacial
charge transfer and enhanced ionic transport capability. To fur-
ther elucidate the inﬂuence of solvation capability on the micro-
scopic electrode behavior, the dissolution and evolution kinetics
of polysulﬁdes were systematically investigated using 0.2 M
Li2S6 as a representative model (Figure 1g). At the initial stage,

whereas the weakly solvating diluent TTE is excluded from

localized high-concentration ion clusters and reconstruct the
bulk solution network.23 As the system evolves from T1 to T3
(i.e., increasing DME content), the Li+−DME interaction is sig-
niﬁcantly strengthened, competitively weakening Li+−THF
coordination. This indicates that the introduction of DME
enables precise reconstruction of the local solvation environ-
ment of Li+. Notably, despite the dynamic evolution in solvent
occupancy within the inner shell, FSI−remains ﬁrmly anchored
in the ﬁrst coordination shell of Li+, imparting a distinct anion-
participating solvation characteristic to these electrolytes.24 This
microscopic structural reconstruction directly dictates the mac-
roscopic interfacial evolution behavior. On one hand, the
strongly donating solvent DME preferentially occupies the
inner solvation shell of Li+ and cooperates with anions to stabi-
lize the local structure.25 On the other hand, the deep involve-
ment of anions leads to the formation of abundant contact
ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregated ion clusters (AGGs). Such
anion-rich solvation environments not only eﬀectively regulate
the thermodynamics of Li+ desolvation but also precisely dic-
tate the initial reduction pathways at the electrode/electrolyte
interface, thereby determining the chemical composition and
formation mechanism of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).
Theoretical binding energy calculations and adsorption con-
ﬁguration analyses (Figure 2d,e) provide energetic validation
for the above conclusions.26 All three systems exhibit favorable
aﬃnity toward lithium polysulﬁde (Li2S6) intermediates; how-
ever, the thermodynamic contributions of individual compo-
nents to the Li+ solvation shell diﬀer signiﬁcantly. Compared
with THF, DME and FSI−bind more strongly to Li+, indicating
that the ﬁrst solvation shell is preferentially constructed through
cooperative DME−anion coordination rather than THF domina-
tion. This agrees well with the RDF results and further conﬁrms
the anion-participating solvation structure. Thermodynamically,
such local coordination favors preferential anion reduction at the
interface, thereby promoting the formation of an inorganic-rich
SEI.27 Simultaneously, it enhances the spatial conﬁnement and
electronic regulation of polysulﬁde intermediates, fundamentally
improving their conversion kinetics and the uniformity of the ﬁnal
Li2S deposition. Spectroscopic characterizations further corrobo-
rate the theoretical evolution trends. Raman spectra (Figure 2f)
show that, compared with T1, the signals corresponding to
Li+−FSI−coordination are signiﬁcantly intensiﬁed in T2 and
T3, while the characteristic peaks of free solvent are relatively sup-
pressed, directly indicating that increasing DME content drives
deeper anion incorporation into the Li+ inner solvation shell. In
Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (Figure 2g), the shifts
and intensity variations of the S=O and C−O−C stretching vibra-
tions further conﬁrm the co-participation of FSI−and ether
oxygen atoms in Li+ solvation, as well as their dynamically
adjusted contributions across diﬀerent systems.28 This continuous
evolution of the local electronic environment surrounding Li+
from T1 to T3 is in excellent agreement with the structural recon-
struction revealed by theoretical simulations. Overall, through the
introduction of DME and precise compositional tuning, this work
successfully reconstructs the competitive coordination mechanism
between solvents and anions within the ﬁrst solvation shell of Li+.
This bottom-up regulation governs the degree of anion participa-
tion, the preferential interfacial reduction chemistry, and the
adsorption and conversion behavior of polysulﬁdes. The resulting

electrochemistry and highly reversible sulfur redox processes.
After elucidating the role of the DME/THF ratio in recon-
structing the local solvation environment of Li+, this work fur-
ther interrogates how such microscopic structural evolution
governs macroscopic electrode processes from the perspectives
of interfacial electrochemistry and lithium deposition kinetics.
Given that the intrinsic failure of lithium metal anodes origi-
nates from the mechanical instability of the interphase and den-
dritic lithium growth, a critical criterion for evaluating
electrolyte performance lies in whether the optimized bulk sol-
vation structure can be eﬀectively translated to the electrode/
electrolyte interface to construct a robust solid electrolyte inter-
phase (SEI).29 To directly visualize the eﬀect of solvation net-
works on lithium deposition, SEM was performed on cycled
Cu current collectors. As shown in Figures 3a and S6, the inter-
facial morphology evolves systematically with the solvation
structure. In the T1 system, lithium deposits form loose, porous
blocky/lamellar aggregates with poor interfacial continuity, indi-
cating insuﬃcient passivation in a weakly solvating environ-
ment. In T2, the deposits become more coalesced, showing
improved continuity and compactness. With a further increase
in DME content in T3, lithium deposition evolves into a uni-
form, smooth, dense, and dendrite-free layer with eﬀectively
suppressed
macroscopic
defects.
This
progressive
three-
dimensional morphological evolution strongly corroborates
the regulatory role of high donor number solvents: the rein-
forced coordination network not only homogenizes the local
interfacial current density but also kinetically suppresses den-
dritic growth, thereby enabling highly reversible and compact
lithium deposition. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
provides further insight into the systematic evolution of SEI
chemical compositions. The high-resolution F 1s spectra
(Figure 3b) conﬁrm that all three systems generate an LiF-
dominated inorganic framework; however, the T3 system
exhibits an overwhelmingly intensiﬁed LiF signal, evidencing a
substantial increase in anion-derived decomposition. The N 1s
spectra (Figure 3b) reveal that all the three systems generate
SEI components rich in Li3N, which can be attributed to the
decomposition of LiNO3 as a ﬁlm-forming additive. In addition,
SEI in T1 retains a signiﬁcant amount of partially decomposed
FSI−species, whereas in T2 and T3 systems, these species pro-
gressively convert into deeply decomposed Li−N components,
further reﬂecting the promoted involvement of anions in inter-
facial reactions induced by solvation structure reconstruction.30
The S 2p spectra (Figure 3b) indicate a more concentrated and
stable distribution of Li2S and sulfur−oxygen species in T2 and
T3 systems, pointing to a pronounced inorganic enrichment at
the interface. Notably, the C 1s spectra (Figure S7) show that
the T2 system triggers considerable solvent decomposition,

of micro-short circuits or interfacial degradation. In summary,
multiscale electrochemical analyses combined with in situ char-
acterizations conﬁrm that as the system evolves from T1 to T3,
the electrolyte network successfully transitions from a weakly
solvating regime to an anion-dominated interfacial formation
mechanism. This cross-scale structural optimization not only
signiﬁcantly broadens the high-voltage oxidative stability win-
dow but also enables the rational construction of a low-imped-
ance, highly dense, and corrosion-resistant inorganic-rich SEI.
Ultimately, it achieves precise regulation of long-term uniform
lithium deposition behavior and electrochemical longevity.
To comprehensively evaluate the long-term interfacial stabil-
ity and practical viability of the lithium anode, Li||Li symmetric
cells and Li−S full cells were systematically investigated. As a
critical indicator of interfacial kinetics and solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) stability, the voltage hysteresis of symmetric
cells was evaluated (Figure 5). Notably, the T3 electrolyte dem-
onstrates exceptional cyclic stability, delivering the lowest over-
potential with minimal ﬂuctuations over prolonged cycling. In
contrast, the T1 and T2 systems suﬀer from pronounced polar-
ization and voltage drift. Enlarged voltage proﬁles at the 1000th
and 2000th cycles further corroborate that T3 maintains an
extremely narrow voltage hysteresis, eﬀectively suppressing par-
asitic reactions and the irreversible accumulation of “dead lith-
ium.” Stepwise rate evaluations (Figure 5b) highlight the
superior interfacial kinetics of T3, which consistently exhibits
the smallest polarization and highly uniform plating/stripping
proﬁles even under harsh high-rate conditions. This under-
scores ultrafast interfacial charge transfer and eﬃcient Li+ trans-
port pathways, whereas T1 and T2 exhibit severe ion-transport
limitations. These macroscopic electrochemical behaviors align
well with the microscopic mechanisms (Figure 3): the recon-
structed solvation network in T3 enhances anion participation,
fostering an inorganic-rich SEI that underpins uniform Li depo-
sition and robust interfacial stability.

gap widens signiﬁcantly, with T3 maintaining high reversible
capacity and delayed degradation. This conﬁrms that T3 not

■CONCLUSIONS
To resolve the kinetic dilemma in weakly solvating electrolytes,
this work systematically investigated the solvation-structure-
dependent electrochemical behaviors in lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries. Although the baseline THF/TTE system can stabilize
the lithium anode, it severely suppresses polysulﬁde conversion.
