Lithium polysulfide conformer analysis in ether-
based solvents for Li–S batteries†
Javier Luque Di Salvo,
*a Guillermina L. Luque*a and Giorgio De Lucab
A fundamental understanding of polysulfide conformers in common (ether-based) solvents used in
prototype lithium–sulfur batteries is mandatory to push the technology towards practical applications. In
this work, molecular dynamics simulations were used to perform a detailed conformational analysis and
dynamics ascertainment of lithium hexasulfide Li2S6 as a representative of soluble long-chain lithium
polysulfides. This novel conformational dynamics study based on 106 structures per studied system
provides important insights into open questions such as how many Li+ coordinate polysulfides are
correlated to lithium transport and polysulfide clustering. The simulations predict that monoanionic LiS6
−in
its closed conformation is the predominant structure in 1,2-dioxolane and dimethoxyethane equimolar
mixtures. If a second Li+ approaches, that leads to the opening of the polysulfide chain and the formation
of neutral Li2S6 (open conformer). By changing the electrolyte composition, the relative distribution of
closed and open conformers varies, although closed LiS6
−remains the most abundant conformer
compared to the open one. There are three major findings from this work: the first is that there is a greater
abundance of the closed conformer, in which the closed/open aspect ratio depends on electrolyte
composition; the second is that the polysulfide charge distribution resulting from the nearby coordinating
lithium cations was determined; and the third is that the number and type of solvent molecules
coordinating the lithium polysulfide were determined. These findings, which have been missing up to now
to the best of our knowledge, provide crucial information for the design of strategies to suppress the
unwanted polysulfide shuttle effect, such as engineered separators and cathode protective layers.
1 Introduction
Lithium–sulfur electrochemistry1 is an appealing alternative
for powering portable and automotive devices, with the great
advantage of the availability of an abundant raw material
such as sulfur that can be used as an active material. It has
been stressed that the reduction of elemental sulfur (S8) to
Li2S can theoretically deliver2 2600 W h kg−1, which is three
to five times higher than that of commercial Li-ion batteries,
and
thus
meets
the
requirements
for
electric
vehicle
applications.3 Nevertheless, there are different problems
associated with these batteries that need to be solved in order
to achieve their theoretical capacity and practical use.
To begin with, both the initial reactant (S8) and the end
product (Li2S) are electrical insulators, and therefore, the
cathodes
must
be
prepared
by
mixing
sulfur
with
a
conductive material such as carbon. Although S8 and Li2S are
insoluble in the organic solvents commonly used in the

Design, System, Application
Batteries are at the heart of every cutting-edge technology aimed at attaining environmental sustainability with reliable energy storage devices that possess
greater sophistication than portable electronic devices, and can be used, for instance, with smart cities projection and massive electric transportation
systems. Among the various candidates for next-generation batteries, those based on lithium–sulfur undergo uncontrolled cathode dissolution, which leads
to a parasitic polysulfide shuttle reaction that severely hinders battery performance. In the present study, the objective was to unravel the structural features
of these shuttling species at the molecular scale, to investigate their correlated conformational dynamics at nanosecond time-scales, and to assess the effect
of solvent composition on their distribution. The insights gained in this work are directed towards the rational design of ad hoc components, such as
protective cathode additives that retain these soluble species or engineered separators that block their shuttle, with the premise of ascertaining the
structural details of the polysulfide species that need to be controlled in order to better tune their fate inside the battery.

electrolyte, the complete reduction of sulfur involves various
lithium polysulfide (PS) species as Li2Sn (4 ≤n ≤8)
intermediates, where the longer chain species are soluble
and are shuttled from the cathode to the anode, leading to a
decrease in the capacity, and causing irreversible loss of
cathode material and/or passivation of metallic lithium
anode, and low coulombic efficiency.2 As a result of the
complex
equilibria
among
different
Li2Sn
(polysulfide
speciation4,5) and their different solubilities, the volume and
viscosity of the electrolyte change during battery cycling.
During the initial steps of lithium–sulfur battery discharge,
the solute concentration in the electrolyte gradually increases
due to dissolution of PSs, producing an increase in the ion
pairs and of higher order ion aggregates, while the fraction
of free ions decreases to nearly zero.4
Despite the many efforts devoted to understand and
suppress
the
PS
shuttle6–13
from
a
theoretical
and
experimental point of view, important fundamental aspects
should still be clarified. Various studies have been performed
to modify the cathode and prevent PS dissolution in the Li–S
battery.14–18 Studies related to the electrolyte have also been
performed in order to understand the dissolution and
reaction of the PSs. At a fundamental level, some works
adopted quantum-based methods to identify lithium PS-
structured
geometries
and
to
calculate
the
energetic
differences
among
various
proposed
reaction
mechanisms.19–22
The lithium PS structures and their intertwined redox
kinetics22,23 as well as thermodynamics24 strongly depend on
the solvent. Guan-Ying Du et al. reported molecular orbital
theory analysis (HOMO–LUMO) of PS dianions and lithium
PS reduction reactions where the dielectric constant of the
media was varied,20 and they concluded that the “tendency to
form ion pairs” between Li+ and PS is fundamental for the
sulfur
reduction
mechanism.
Understanding
such
fundamental Li–S interactions in a liquid electrolyte is also
important considering PS dissolution and shuttling, and its
effect on lithium diffusion.
Using
molecular
dynamics
(MD)
simulations,
Rajput
et al.25 showed that Li+ cation can act as a bridge between
adjacent PSs and form aggregates (PS clustering). They
validated these findings by complementing NMR and XANES
spectra
measurements
with
molecular
models.26
PS
clustering is affected by the PS concentration and its chain-
length: the solubility of short-chain PSs is lower compared to
that of longer-chain PSs,2 and thus, they undergo facile
aggregation.27,28 Gupta et al.23,29 showed that PS clustering is
more important at low temperatures, and that competing
anions23 as well as cations29 can disrupt the PS cluster
network;
they
validated
these
findings
with
7Li
NMR
measurements.
Although several aspects have been analyzed in these
works, an accurate conformational analysis of representative
PSs is absent. Additionally, the number of lithium cations
that coordinate the negatively charged PSs when dissolved in
the electrolyte is not yet clear.19,21,22,24,30 This analysis, in
addition to the aforementioned characterizations, is useful
for preventing the shuttle effect, as well as to enable a more
optimal design for the lithium PS interactions within the
cathode. For instance, a higher counter-ion coordination
number could neutralize or even revert the effective charge of
the PS complex, which might decrease the ion selectivity of
negatively charged materials aimed at blocking PSs by
electrostatic repulsion.6,11,31 Furthermore, the shape and size
of lithium PS complexes, as well as their first solvation shell
on commonly used (ether) solvents, should be taken into
account in the design of separators and/or cathodes. Thus,
the conformational analysis of PSs in the electrolyte is critical
for studying strategies based on materials trapping PSs inside
the cathode,7,10,11,31,32 engineered separators for blocking
their shuttle,6,8,33 and for tailoring the electrolytes with the
most
optimal
performance
in
order
to
avoid
the
shuttle.23,29,34–36
In
this
sense,
computational
chemistry
models
can
provide
important
insights
because
they
are
able
to
investigate aspects at the molecular scale that are very
difficult to study experimentally, especially when multiple
(electro)chemical equilibria are simultaneously occurring.
Classic MD is used for this purpose to map the Li2S6
conformational space in electrolytes commonly used in Li–S
batteries composed of different compositional mixtures of
1,2-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME), along with
bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI).
We chose the force field recently calibrated by Park and
collaborators
that
reproduces
various
key
electrolyte
properties quite well.27,37 Specifics on the computational
procedure used and its validation are reported in the
Computational details section and the ESI† material. We
focused on lithium hexasulfide because it is representative of
soluble long-chain PSs, and it is produced in the electrolye at
potentials below 2.4 V vs. Li/Li+ and at higher relative
concentration among others Sn
2−(4 < n < 8).4,22 Moreover,
the mobility of Li2S6 is higher38 than that of Li2S4, with a
lower
tendency
to
agreggate
into
clusters,26,27,39
thus
highlighting its protagonist role in the PS shuttle effect.
This article is organized as follows: first, the distribution
of
hexasulfide
conformers
in
different
electrolyte
compositions is presented, which is based on the terminal
S–S
intramolecular
distance
as
the
single
molecular
descriptor
and
allows
for
a
detailed
conformational
sampling. Second, a thorough analysis is provided of the
lithium coordination number associated with the different PS
conformers. Finally, the most probable number and type of
solvent molecules around the hexasulfide is determined.
2 Results and discussion
2.1 PS conformer distribution
To perform a reliable conformational analysis of the PS
conformers, the S–S intramolecular distance between the
terminal atoms of S6
2−was chosen as a molecular descriptor.

increase in the PS concentration from 0.25 to 1 M. A change

S–S

marked difference found between the first peak and the
second and third ones, we do not expect an inversion of
peaks by the use of different parameters. We produced an
additional simulation that adopted other referenced force

with the closed conformer as the most abundant structure
(see Fig. S3†).
Due to the experimental complex distribution of PSs with
different chain lengths, it is difficult to validate this finding
versus experimental data, and thus, the results must be
compared with available theoretical works. Various first

a stable one,19–22,24,30,40,41 as evidenced by DFT geometry

which should be useful in the pursuit of suitable electrolytes
for use in Li–S batteries.23,29,34–36
2.2 Lithium coordination number
A further analysis was carried out by selecting the conformer
structures falling inside the main three peaks, where the
peak positions were obtained by fitting them with Gaussian
curves (Fig. S2†). For each conformer, all the species in the
first coordination shell were determined using a cutoff of 6.2
Å from the centre of mass of the hexasulfide dianion (see Fig.
S3†). All Li+ cations within this cutoff were counted as part of
the atomic cluster. Likewise, if an atom from a solvent
molecule
fell
within
the
considered
cutoff,
the
entire
molecule was considered as part of the atomic cluster. The
extracted atomic clusters were hence analysed in order to
obtain the number of Li+ cations and solvent molecules
involved in the first coordination shell.
The
number
of
lithium
cations
coordinating
the
hexasulfide
dianion
at
different
Li2S6
concentrations
is
reported on the histograms in Fig. 2. The vast majority of
closed conformers are coordinated by one Li+ (approximately
20k conformers), followed by conformers coordinated by two
Li+
(approximately 10k), as shown in Fig. 2a. This is
completely reverted for the open conformer (Fig. 2b), where
there
are
more
conformers
coordinated
by
two
Li+
(approximately 5k) as compared to those with only one cation
(approximately 2k). This situation was also observed in the
other
systems
studied
herein:
a
closed
conformer
is
preferentially coordinated by 1 Li+, and an open conformer is
mainly coordinated by 2 Li+ (see the histograms in Fig. S4†).
In the open conformer, the terminal S atoms have a
higher share of delocalized electron density,27 which enables
them to interact with more Li+ cations. In particular, when
the concentration of lithium inside the simulation box
increases, the number of closed conformers with only 1 Li+
decreases (Fig. 2a) and the open conformer with 2 Li+
increases accordingly (Fig. 2b). It should be noted that a high
Li+ concentration is obtained when the concentration of Li2S6
increases, or when there are extra ions from the LiTFSI salt.
In both cases, the PS encounters readily available Li+ cations
with greater frequency, which is in alignment with a higher
degree of ion association in concentrated solutions.
The de-lithiated dianion S6
2−was reported as the most
stable in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) by spectroscopic and
computational studies.22 Some theoretical works concluded
that the neutral Li2S6 prevails in pure DME19,21 and also in
DOL:DME mixtures,19,24 whereas Pascal et al.30 concluded
that monoanionic LiS6
−prevails in DOL:DME, with a second
Li+ loosely bound to the long-chain PS. Our results agree with
the latter description.30 It is interesting to note that there is
zero Li+ within the chosen cutoff in some of the open
conformers (Fig. 2b); albeit these have no statistical weight,
this singularity does not occur for the closed conformer. This
would suggest that the closed conformer exists because of a
strong interaction with one Li+ that subsequently forms a
highly stable LiS6
−monoanionic complex (the PS “tightly
wraps around the lithium cation”27). The prevalence of this
conformer
is
in
agreement
with
the
positive
effect
of
incorporating negatively charged separators in blocking the
PS shuttle.6,8,31

and

Overall, it is interesting that the analysis of the entire MD
trajectory (see the video provided in the ESI† material) shows
the following mechanism: when a second Li+ approaches the
closed conformer, it interacts with a terminal S atom (Fig.
S5†); eventually, the first Li+ is pushed out to the opposite S
terminal atom and the hexasulfide chain opens, leading to
the open conformer as shown in Fig. 3b. Although similar
Li–S minimum distances were found for both conformers
(see the distribution curves of Fig. 3a and b), in the open
conformer, a single Li+ cation interacts with only 1 or 3 sulfur
atoms, whereas the Li+ cation inside the closed horseshoe
conformer interacts with all 6 sulfur atoms.
The proposed mechanism of a second Li+-driven opening
of closed monoanionic LiS6
−to open neutral Li2S6 appears in
Scheme 1. The number of closed conformers with 2 Li+
(approximately 10k) is twice as high as compared to open
conformers with 2 Li+ (approximately 5k), (Fig. 2), which
indicates that the second lithium rattles around the closed
LiS6
−, preferentially very near to a terminal S atom (see the
distribution curve in Fig. 3b). However, the number of times
that the proximity of the second Li+ effectively leads to the
opening of the hexasulfide is lower than the times it rattles
around it; or seemingly, the open conformer closes rather
fast (see Fig. S1a†), leading to a closed conformer with a
neighbouring second Li+. As a result, we find a twofold ratio
between closed/open conformers with 2 Li+, and thus, it is
likely that the PS opening would demand breaking the
(inner)Li–S interaction favoured by the closed conformation.
Verification of this statement should be performed using
more appropriate methods that go beyond the scope of the
present work.
2.3 PS solvation
The closed and open conformers were further analyzed by
counting and identifying the number of solvent molecules
within the first coordination shell. In the case of pure
solvents, 3–4 and 5 DME molecules solvated the closed and
open conformers, respectively; whereas 6 and 7–8 DOL
molecules coordinately closed and opened structures (Fig.
S6†), respectively. Regarding this aspect, Qi Liu et al.40
performed DFT geometry optimizations starting from what
they called ‘sawtooth’ PSs (open conformers of long-chain
PSs) with two Li+ cations. In their calculations, they used six
DOL molecules for one calculation, and four DME molecules
for another, and both cases led to a cyclic structure (closed
conformer), which is in agreement with our findings of
closed conformer stability. It is important to note that in a
previous study,40 geometry optimizations did not provide
intermediate
structures.
However,
the
MD
simulations
performed herein allowed us to describe, albeit from a
qualitative point of view, the transition between closed and
open
conformers
(see
the
video
provided
in
the
ESI†
material), which is supported by the conformation analysis
carried out on a reliable set of structures.

the 0.5 M Li2S6 system in an equimolar mixture of DOL:DME.

The solvent plays a crucial role in stabilizing the Li+ cation
via electron-rich oxygen donors from ether molecules. Some
studies showed that long-chain lithium PSs crystallize with
Li+ coordinated by strong linking of solvent molecules, so
that the X-ray data does not reflect pure Li–S compounds.1,42
For the closed conformer, the solvent molecules (DME
(Fig. 5a) or DOL (Fig. 5b)) lie on the top or the bottom of the
closed conformer, i.e., parallel to the distorted horseshoe
plane containing the S atoms. For the open conformer
(Fig. 5c), both Li+ are strongly coordinated by oxygen atoms
of solvent molecules.
Andersen et al.26 described a “joint Li+ coordination by the
DOL/DME
solvent
molecules
and
(poly)sulfide
chains”,
characterized by a strong Li–S interaction19,26,27 (Fig. S3†)
along with coordination by DOL/DME oxygen atoms, which is
what we observed in the extracted clusters (see Fig. 5). In
particular, DME is more capable of coordinate Li+ due to its
flexible
structure
allowing
bi-coordination
of
lithium
compared to the cyclic DOL molecule.26,37 Lithium transport
is substantially affected by the Li+ coordination in these
systems. The authors26 concluded that ‘free’ Li+ ion transport
is a minor contribution compared to the Li+ diffusion
mediated by PS chains and solvent molecules, based on the
strong Li–S interaction.19,26,27
In addition to these effects, the insights gained in our
work provide a possible explanation obtained from an
accurate conformational dynamics distribution analysis: the
trapped lithium in the predominant LiS6
−conformer can be
released by an incoming second Li+ that opens the conformer
(see the video provided in the ESI† material). It should be
noted, however, that the real scenario is more complex due
to PS speciation, possible presence of radicals5,43 such as
S3
−˙, and disproportionation and recombination reactions.
Nevertheless, it is likely that the obtained results are valid
from the lithium coordination point of view regardless of the
transient length of the (soluble long-chain) PS.
Another aspect to take into consideration is the PS
clustering or the formation of aggregates.23,25–29 Although it
is expected to be more characteristic for short-chain PS28
given their lower solubility, soluble long-chain PS can also, at
least partially, form clusters in DOL:DME.26,27 In this work,
we evaluated the proximity among PSs by calculating the
fraction of PS–PS pairs and comparing it with the fraction of
free PS anions, and also extracting clusters centered on the
S6
2−center of mass at various cutoffs (Fig. S8†). For example,
when using 9 Å as a cutoff, 41% and 65% of the PS see
another PS when the reference anion is the closed conformer
or open conformer, respectively. This would suggest that the
open conformer is more prone to aggregate, because the
fraction of PS–PS pairs is higher compared to the closed one,
regardless of the cutoff (see Fig. S8†). Consequently, those
systems where higher second and third peaks (such as Li2S6
in a pure DOL system, Fig. 1b) appear on the distribution
curve would be more susceptible to aggregation.
Concerning this aspect, Osella et al.39 studied the effect of
ether-based solvents on the structure and diffusion of Li2S4
by means of MD simulations. They observed the formation of
a large aggregate in DOL and a more dispersed solution in
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME). Although the
systems studied herein are different from those used in the
study by Osella et al.,39 the distribution curve of Li2S6 in pure
DME presents the highest first peak corresponding to the
closed conformer (DME is a monoglyme, whereas TEGDME39
is a tetraglyme). Additionally, in pure DOL, there is an
important fraction of open conformers in comparison to
other systems. This is also supported by the Li+ histograms
in
the pure
DOL system, where
the number of
open
conformers coordinated by 2 Li+ (or even 3 Li+) is the highest
compared to the other studied systems (see Fig. S4†), which
is in agreement with the lithium PS clustering in the case of
multiple Li–S bridges.25
Lastly, it should be noted that the selection of a starting
configuration, formed by a combination of PS dianion,
lithium cations, and solvent molecules, with defined relative
orientations, is not a trivial task. See, for example, the
thermodynamic diagrams from the ab initio MD simulations
of Kamphaus and Balbuena,19 where 14 solvated structures
of Li2S6 with only 1 to 4 DME molecules were characterized
(shown in Fig. 4 of a previous study19). In this sense, the
conformational sampling performed in this work should
assist in obtaining reliable solvated lithium PS structures. As
a result, the structures extracted herein are meaningful, and
their
structural
details
should
ultimately
allow
us
to
understand their transport inside a battery.
3 Conclusions
In this work, classic MD simulations were used to map the
conformational
space
of
Li2S6,
solvated
at
different
concentrations in an equimolar mixture of 1,2-dioxolane and
dimethoxyethane,
which
are
commonly
used
in
Li–S
batteries, and in the presence of LiTFSI salt. Long simulation
times and 6 nm-large simulation boxes allowed the analysis
of more than 106 conformers, ensuring an adequate mapping
of the conformational space. The distribution curves of the
terminal
S–S
intramolecular distance
unravelled the
PS
conformers and their correlated dynamics, and indicated the
clear prevalence of the closed conformer over the open
conformer,
an
aspect
insufficiently
emphasized
in
the
literature.
The illustrated analysis shows that the closed conformer
is by far the predominant configuration, with one Li+ that
bounces around inside the distorted horseshoe hexasulfide
chain, keeping it closed due to strong Li–S coordination. The
closed
monoanionic
LiS6
−
opens
when
a
second
Li+
approaches, leading to neutral open conformer Li2S6, which

might eventually lead to the exchange of the first Li+ by the
incoming second one. Interestingly, the peak heights in the
distribution
curve
slightly
vary
when
the
electrolyte
composition is changed. In particular, a decrease in closed
conformers and a corresponding increase in the open ones
was observed in the presence of 1 M LiTFSI and with
increasing lithium hexasulfide concentration, whereas using
pure DME as the solvent produced the opposite effect. On
the contrary, using pure DOL as the solvent yielded minor
closed conformer peak height among all the studied systems,
although the closed conformer was always predominant. This
result suggests that an appropriate electrolyte composition
can be used to tune the relative distribution of open and
closed conformers, enabling different Li+ coordination and
aggregation tendencies, which are key factors that impact
lithium transport and PS shuttling between electrodes.
Overall, the conformational sampling highlights three
important aspects
that
are crucial for
the
design and
development of host–guest cathode materials and engineered
separators, and are aimed at suppressing the shuttling effect.
The first aspect is the predominance of the closed conformer
compared to the open one, and the slight variation of closed/
open aspect ratios (distribution curve peak heights) with
changing electrolyte composition. The second aspect is the
charge distribution of the PS, which is influenced by the
nearby Li+ cations and is relevant for strategies adopting
negatively
charged
separators
or
cathode-protective
electrostatic layers. The third aspect is the most probable PS
solvent coordination number and type in binary mixtures,
such as the commonly used DOL:DME. The generated
structures, which are statistically more abundant than other
possible configurations, are satisfactory starting points for
thermodynamic calculations, as well as for studying transport
mechanisms through membrane-based separators inside Li–S
batteries.
4 Computational details
The
MD
simulations
were
performed
in
GROMACS44
following the setup of Park et al.27,37 in the NVT and NPT
ensemble (p = 1 bar) using the Berendsen thermostat and
barostat, bond constraints with LINCS, 2 fs timestep, and
Verlet integrator. Before simulating the electrolyte systems
considered herein, various DOL:DME mixtures were tested,
and the density of benchmark systems was confirmed (Fig.
S9†). The electrolyte systems were composed of 1500 solvent
molecules and the corresponding amount of Li2S6 and
LiTFSI,
as
shown
in
Table
S1.†
In
order
to
achieve
equilibration, the simulation box underwent (i) gradual
heating (linear temperature increase from 0–450 K during
500 ps in NVT), (ii) 2 ns at constant T = 450 K in NVT, (iii)
gradual cooling (linear decrease of temperature from 450–300
K during 500 ps in NVT), and finally, (iv) 5 ns at constant T =
300 K in NPT. All systems rapidly achieved a stable response
of density and potential energy.
Once equilibrated, 100 ns production runs in NPT at 300
K were applied, and trajectory coordinates were saved every 2
ps. The analysis was performed over the range of 10–90 ns,
first by using the gmx mindist program44 with proper index
groups to obtain the S–S terminal distance through time, and
then
using
the
gmx
analyze
program44
to
obtain
the
corresponding distribution curve (Fig. S1†). Finally, various
bespoke bash scripts were used to extract atomic clusters
centered on the PS centre of mass from the MD trajectories
and carry out the analysis. The generated cluster structures
(in xyz format) are available upon reasonable request.
