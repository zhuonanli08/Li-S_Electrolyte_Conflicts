A B S T R A C T
Lithium metal batteries (LMBs), particularly lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, have garnered tremendous substan­
tial attention due to their high specific energy. However, severe polysulfide shuttling and continuous Li dendrite 
growth have severely hindered the lifespan and the commercial application. Here, we propose a novel solvation- 
tailoring electrolyte strategy fundamentally distinct from commercial EC-EMC-DMC carbonate electrolytes and 
reported all-fluorinated electrolytes. The strongly-solvating DMC co-solvent was incorporated into the dual- 
fluorinated FEC-FEMC to reconstruct Li⁺ primary solvation sheath via competitive coordination. The Li 
plating-stripping test of Li-Cu cells revealed that the FEC-FEMC-DMC (FFD) electrolyte delivered much better 
cycling stability than the all-fluorinated electrolytes based on FEC-FEMC (FF), and FEC-FEMC-TTE (FFT) systems. 
This enhancement is attributed to the strategic incorporation of DMC, whose stronger solvation capability 
preferentially integrates into the Li+ primary solvation sheath. By deliberately tailoring the solvation environ­
ment, the local interfacial chemistry at the anode was modulated, mitigating excessive decomposition of fluo­
rinated solvents. The FFD electrolyte also promoted the formation of a layered, F-rich SEI, which facilitated Li+
diffusion kinetics and inhibited the growth of lithium dendrites, thereby enabling the Li-SPAN batteries with 
superior rate capability and cycling stability. This work offers valuable insights for the rational design of 
advanced electrolytes in next-generation LMBs.
1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have made great progress over the past 
three decades; however, their development is approaching the theoret­
ical capacity limit, primarily constrained by the low theoretical capacity 
of the graphite anode (~372 mAh g−1). Therein, lithium metal batteries 
(LMBs) are coming back into the spotlight because of the exceptionally 
high theoretical capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and lowest electrochemical 
potential (−3.04 V vs. SHE) of lithium metal anode (LMA) [1]. For 
example, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries exhibit twice to three times 
larger energy density than the current LIBs, positioning them as prom­
ising candidates among next-generation energy storage technologies [2,
3]. Nonetheless, the intrinsic liquid-solid conversion mechanism in 
carbon/sulfur cathodes would cause the generation and dissolution of 
polysulfides, resulting in the loss of active material and progressive 
corrosion of the lithium metal anode [4–6]. The polysulfides shuttling 
significantly impedes the long-term cycling stability of Li-S cells. 
Conversely, some emerging vulcanized polymers, such as sulfurized 
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), showed negligible polysulfide shuttling phe­
nomenon during discharge, and are compatible with ester-based elec­
trolytes. This behavior is attributed to a direct solid-solid conversion 
mechanism and the formation of stable cathode-electrolyte interphases 
[7–9]. SPAN thus offers a viable strategy to stabilize the sulfur cathode, 
mitigating cathode-anode crosstalk and allowing research efforts to 
focus on the protection of the LMA.
Practical LMBs generally necessitate high cathode loading (area ca­
pacity), low negative-to-positive capacity (N/P) ratio, and low 
electrolyte-cathode (E/C) ratio to achieve 400 Wh kg−1 or higher [10,
11]. However, a large number of existing studies employ excessive 
lithium metal and flooded electrolytes, making it difficult to monitor the 
cells’ cycling performance under actual working conditions as reference. 
Critically, the low N/P ratio imply a limited lithium reservoir, rendering

Artificial

lithium from direct electrolyte contact, thereby suppressing parasitic 
reactions and promoting uniform lithium deposition [15,16]. Electrolyte 
engineering, through the rational selection of lithium salts and solvent 
systems, offers the potential to optimize the electrochemical perfor­
mance of both cathode and anode without altering the battery archi­
tecture or compromising energy density [17]. Despite these advances, 
structured anodes inevitably suffer from side reactions with electrolytes, 
and fabricating uniform and thin artificial SEI layers remains a signifi­
cant challenge for large-scale production. In contrast, electrolyte design 
presents a more straightforward and scalable approach to substantially 
enhance the performance of LMBs, contingent upon a comprehensive 
understanding of the electrochemical interactions between the electro­
lyte and the lithium metal anode.
Solvated Li-ions actively participate in the formation of the SEI on 
the metallic lithium surface through both chemical and electrochemical 
processes [18]. Typically, the SEI comprises a complex multiphase ar­
chitecture, consisting of organic matrix and inorganic components 
distributed heterogeneously [18,19]. It is widely recognized that the 
fluorine-rich interfaces can significantly enhance the electrochemical 
performance of lithium metal batteries by mitigating the accumulation 
of “dead” lithium at the anode and stabilizing the high-voltage stability 
of the cathode [20,21]. Therefore, numerous fluorinated solvents have 
been developed as electrolyte additives [22], such as fluoroethylene 
carbonate (FEC)[23], methyl (2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) carbonate (FEMC) 
[24], difluoroethylene carbonate (DFEC)[25]. The introduction of 
antisolvents, predominantly fluorinated ethers such as 1,1,2,2-tetra­
fluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE)[26,27] and bis(2,2, 
2-trifluoroethyl)ether (BTFE)[28,29], have enabled the formation of 
localized high concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) with unique solvation 
structure and anion-derived SEI. All-fluorinated electrolytes have also 
been developed to improve the performance of LMA under safety-critical 
and extreme temperature conditions [23,30–33]. These advances natu­
rally raise a pertinent and direct question: does more fluorinated sol­
vents in LMB electrolytes necessarily correlate with superior 
electrochemical performance?
In this work, we challenged the conventional paradigm that “more 
fluorine is better” by proposing a counterintuitive yet highly effective 
approach: strategically tailoring the Li+ solvation chemistry by intro­
ducing a non-fluorinated co-solvent (DMC). We demonstrated that this 
solvation-engineered electrolyte (FEC-FEMC-DMC, denoted as FFD) 
achieved superior long-term electrochemical performance compared to 
all-fluorinated counterparts (FEC-FEMC and FEC-FEMC-TTE). The FFD 
maintains the merit of fluorinated solvents for building stable F-rich SEI, 
while restraining excessive decomposition of FEC/FEMC via reducing 
coordinated fluorinated solvent molecules, breaking the flawed “more 
fluorine is better” consensus. Moreover, the FFD electrolyte is compat­
ible with Li-SPAN full cells, enabling prolonged cycling lifespan under 
high SPAN loading and low N/P ratio conditions.
2. Materials & experiment
2.1. Preparation of electrolytes
The baseline electrolyte (BL) was a commercial LIBs electrolyte 
purchased from Guotaihuarong, containing 1 M LiPF6 in EC-EMC-DMC 
mixed solutions. To prepare the fluorinated electrolytes, the salt 
(LiPF6) concentration was controlled at 1 M, and the solvents were 
mixed by a specific volume ratio. For FEC-FEMC-DMC (FFD) series, the 
volume ratio was 1:1:0, 4:4:2, 2:6:2, and 1:1:1, which were denoted as 
FF, FFD442, FFD262, and FFD, respectively. For preparing all- 
fluorinated electrolytes, DMC was replaced by a common fluorinated 
ether (TTE). The volume ratio of FEC: FEMC: TTE (FFT) was 4:4:2, 1:1:1, 
1:1:2, and 1:1:3, which labeled as FFT442, FFT, FFT112, and FFT113, 
respectively.
2.2. Preparation of electrodes and cells
The Li-Cu cells were assembled using a Li foil (thickness: 400 μm) 
and Cu foil with corresponding electrolytes (~30 μL) to evaluate the 
Coulombic efficiency of lithium plating-stripping. The Li-Li symmetric 
cells were conducted by two Li foils (diameter: 16 mm) to further 
evaluate the cycling stability of anode and electrolytes. The LiFePO4 
(LFP) electrodes contained 90 wt% active material, 5 wt% conductive 
carbon (acetylene black), and 5 wt% binder (PVDF). The LFP loading 
was around 12.3 mg cm−2 (areal capacity: ~2 mAh cm−2). The thin Li 
foils (50 μm thickness) were used as the anode in Li-LFP cells (N/P = 5). 
The working voltage range was 3.1–3.7 V (1 C = 170 mA g−1). Similarly, 
the SPAN electrodes were made and punched into 12 mm disks for coin 
cells, with a total loading of 9.0 mg cm−2, of which ~7.5 mg cm−2 was 
active SPAN. The theoretical specific discharge capacity of SPAN ma­
terials was uniformly set to 1000 mAh g−1, and the currents at various 
rates were configured accordingly. The amount of electrolyte was 
controlled to 30~40 µL (E/C = 3.5~4.7 µL mg−1). Different thickness 
lithium foils (such as 200, 100, and 50 μm) were used as the anodes in Li- 
SPAN coin cells (N/P was around 6.5, 2.2, and 0.5. See Table S6). The Li- 
SPAN cells were cycled within 1–3 V using LAND battery test system. All 
cells used Celgard-2325 as the separator.
2.3. Materials characterization and electrochemical tests
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted by JSM-7800F 
to observe the morphology of lithium anode cycling in different elec­
trolytes. The Raman and 7Li NMR of electrolytes were measured by 
NanoWizard and AVANCE III 400 MHz. The contact angel of electrolytes 
on PP separator were conducted by DSA100. The X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) of SEI were analyzed by Thermofisher Excalab 250 
Xi+. Before analysis, the SEM and XPS samples were washed with DMC 
solvent to remove the salt remains, and dried at room temperature in Ar 
filled glovebox. Low-temperature transmission electron microscopy 
(cryo-TEM, Gatan636) was applied to observe the distribution of SEI. 
The Li for cryo-TEM characterization was electro-deposited on the bare 
Cu grid without a supporting layer in coin cells under 0.2 mA cm−2 for 
20 min.
The cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spec­
troscopy (EIS) was conducted on an electrochemical workstation (Ver­
saSTAT4). To measure the ionic conductivity of electrolytes, stainless 
steel symmetric cells were assembled and characterized via EIS. The 
ionic conductivity was calculated based on the ohmic resistance 
extracted from the impedance spectra. To determine the electrochemical 
stability window of the electrolyte, Li–stainless steel cells were assem­
bled for linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements at a scan rate of 
1 mV s−1. To obtain the Tafel plot of different electrolytes, the CV of Li-Li 
symmetric cells were cycled between −0.2 V and 0.2 V with a scan rate 
of 1 mV s−1. To determine the limiting current density, Li–Li symmetric 
cells were cycled five times at 0.1 mA cm−2 initially. Thereafter, the 
current density was increased by 0.1 mA cm−2 per cycle with a fixed 
plating/stripping duration of 1 h for each cycle. To test the duration 
until short-circuit failure, Li-Li symmetric cells were galvanostatically 
charged at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 until cell short-circuiting 
occurred or the voltage reached the upper test limit. To compare the 
SPAN kinetic in different electrolytes, the Li-SPAN cells were cycled in 
1–3 V with a scan rate of 0.2 mV s−1. The EIS was obtained in the fre­
quency range from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz with an AC amplitude of 5 mV.

Three simulations systems with different compositions were con­
structed, namely FF, FFD, FFT. FF system is composed of 83 Li+, 83 PF6
–, 
572 FEC, 345 FEMC molecules. FFD system is composed of 82 Li+, 82

–, 434 FEC, 262 FEMC, 209 TTE molecules. All molecules 
mentioned above were modelled by general amber force field [34]. Van 
der Waals interactions were described by Lennard Jones (LJ) potential. 
Coulombic interactions were calculated with the PME algorithm. Clas­
sical molecular dynamics (cMD) simulations in this work were carried 
out with Gromacs software, version 2019.6[35]. Energy-minimization 
with steepest-descent algorithm was first conducted for the initial con­
figurations. Then cMD simulations with 50-ns durations were conducted 
at atmospheric pressure and room temperature. A time step of 1 fs was 
used. Leapfrog algorithm was used to integrate the Newton’s equations 
of motion. 3D periodic boundary conditions were considered.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Dual-fluorinated electrolyte design
The Li-Cu half cells were employed to evaluate the reversibility of Li 
plating and stripping in various electrolytes, as shown in Fig. 1a. The 
baseline (BL) carbonated electrolyte showed poor electrochemical per­
formance, characterized by low Coulombic efficiency (CE) values and 
rapid CE dropping after approximately 30 cycles. Specifically, the CE of 
lithium metal anode in BL electrolyte ranged from 80 to 85% during the 
initial 30 cycles. Such suboptimal CE accelerates the unnecessary Li 
consumption, rendering the electrolyte unfeasible to satisfy the rigorous 
cycling demands of LMBs. As shown in Figure S1, the BL electrolyte 
caused the Li-LFP cells with limited lithium to fail rapidly after only 20 
cycles, severely limiting the cycle life of the LMB.
The incorporation of fluorinated solvents markedly improved the CE 
of Li-Cu cells. Interestingly, only specific fluorinated electrolytes could 
ensure satisfactory long-term cycling stability. For example, the lithium 
metal anode showed lower CE values in the single FEMC or DMC solvent 
than the BL electrolyte (Figure S2). The FEC-DMC (1:1, v/v) electrolyte 
displayed larger polarization and irregular voltage profiles, attributable 
to poor wetting properties. In contrast, combining FEC with FEMC at a 
1:1 vol ratio reduced voltage polarization and improved the reversibility 
of lithium plating-stripping. Nevertheless, the FEC-FEMC (FF) electro­
lyte suffered from early CE and capacity decay in Li-Cu and Li-LFP cells, 
respectively, indicating insufficient electrochemical stability for pro­
longed cycling. Similarly, the all-fluorinated electrolyte based on the 
FEC-FEMC-TTE exhibited comparable degradation in Li-LFP and Li-Cu 
cells, which became more pronounced with increasing TTE contents 
(Figure S1 and Figure S3a). This behavior is likely due to the weaker 
solvation power of TTE, which accelerates the consumption of FEC and 
FEMC, particularly under high TTE additions. In contrast, DMC exhibi­
ted a significantly stronger solvating power towards Li⁺ than FEC and 
FEMC [36], and its coordination with Li+ mitigated the decomposition 
of FEC and FEMC solvents. Compared with other linear carbonate sol­
vents (e.g., DEC, EMC), DMC still possess the strongest solvating power 
due to the highest dielectric constant, the lowest viscosity and the 
smallest molecular size. This explains that a small addition of DMC (such 
as FFD442 and FFD262) substantially enhanced the long-term cycling 
performance of Li-Cu cells. Notably, when the content of FEC was 
decreasing, the performance of Li-Cu cells would also decline in 
advance. For the FFD-serial electrolytes, appropriately increasing the 
content of DMC further improved the CE stability in Li-Cu cells and 
obtained higher capacity retention in Li-LFP cells (Figure S1). Besides, 
when the lithium salt LiPF6 was replaced by LiFSI in FFD and FFT 
electrolytes, the electrochemical performance of Li-Cu cells became 
inferior (Figure S3).
3.2. Li-ion solvation structure characterization
To validate our hypothesis that the electrolyte’s performance is 
primarily governed by Li+ solvation chemistry, we firstly conducted 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to visualize how the introduction 
of the non-fluorinated co-solvent DMC tailors the Li+ solvation structure 
in FF, FFD, and FFT electrolytes (Fig. 2a–c). The radial distribution 
function (RDF) of Li+ to the oxygen in FEC-FEMC concluded that the FEC 
dominated the first solvation shell, consistent with its stronger solvation 
power to Li+ compared to FEMC (Fig. 2d). The average coordination 
numbers of FEC, FEMC, and PF6
−
were 4.089, 1.371, and 0.201, 
respectively (Fig. 2g). Upon the incorporation of TTE into FEC-FEMC, a 
notable shift occurred: an increased number of PF6
−anions participated 
in the Li+ solvation, whereas TTE was largely excluded from the first 
solvation shell, resulting in a localized high-concentration electrolyte 
(LHCE) solvation structure (Fig. 2f). The corresponding average coor­
dination numbers for FEC, FEMC and PF6
−changed to 3.554, 1.368, and 
0.551, respectively (Fig. 2i). On the contrary, the addition of DMC in 
FEC-FEMC significantly reduced FEMC coordination in the first solva­
tion shell while simultaneously competing with FEC for Li+ coordination 
due to its stronger solvation capability (Fig. 2e). Quantitatively, the Li+
coordination number of O-FEC and O-FEMC significantly decreased to 
2.858 and 0.368, while those for DMC and PF6
−were 2.083 and 0.294, 
respectively (Fig. 2h). FEC possesses a low LUMO energy level and is 
intrinsically prone to reduction. After the introduction of DMC, the 
formed solvation structure reduces the coordination number of FEC, 
which in turn delays the reduction reaction of FEC. Previous literature 
showed that the modified solvation environment in FFD likely inhibited 
the generation of detrimental species arising from gradual FEMC 
decomposition[36], while partially mitigating FEC decomposition. 
Meanwhile, FEC remained the dominant component in the Li+ solvation 
sheath to form LiF-rich SEI without deteriorating Li metal interfacial 
stability. Overall, these simulation results, when coupled with the 
observed superior electrochemical performance, confirmed that our 
strategy of tailoring Li+ solvation chemistry via a strongly solvating, 
non-fluorinated co-solvent is fundamentally effective. The competitive 
coordination of DMC successfully established a direct mechanistic link 
between the engineered solvation environments and enhanced electro­
chemical stability.

3.3. SEI chemistry and structure
The electrochemistry of lithium metal strongly relies on the prop­
erties of SEI, which is formed through both chemical and electro­
chemical decomposition of lithium salts and solvents. Figure S4 showed 
the F 1 s XPS spectra comparison of SEI in different electrolytes. The F of 
SEI in BL electrolyte was mainly derived from the decomposition of the 
lithium salt (LiPF6), resulting in a low F/C ratio (~0.13). Furthermore, 
Figure S4a showed two prominent peaks indicating that the content of 
LiF was lower than that of LixPOyFz. This finding indicates the inade­
quate LiF content within the SEI layer of BL electrolyte, failing to 
maintain stable long-term cycling of lithium metal. By contrast, the 
incorporation of fluorinated solvents substantially increased the F/C 
ratio in SEI. The defluorination process of fluorinated solvents (FEC and 
FEMC) contributed to the formation of LiF-rich interfaces (Figure S4b-f) 
[37]. Notably, the presence of FEMC appears to increase the formation 
of organic-rich fluorinated interfaces. Compared with FFD442 (~0.684) 
and FFD262 (~0.509), the optimized FFD electrolyte incorporates the 
highest DMC content, which effectively dilutes the fluorine concentra­
tion within the Li+ primary solvation sheath leading to lowest F/C ratio 
(~0.253) (Table S1). This compositional variation further verifies that 
the introduction of DMC successfully reconstructs the solvation envi­
ronment by reducing the proportion of fluorinated solvents in the 
electrolyte, which is consistent with the MD simulation and spectro­
scopic characterization results.
XPS sputter depth profiling were further performed to elucidate the 
vertical compositional evolution of SEI layers. Fig. 3a–d and Figure S5 
presents the elemental XPS spectra (C 1 s, O 1 s, F 1 s and P 2p) of the SEI 
formed on lithium metal in FFD and BL electrolytes. As sputtering pro­
ceeds, the inner SEI of FFD electrolyte exhibits distinct inorganic 
enrichment along the depth direction. As observed from the C 1 s spectra 
(Fig. 3a)[38,39], the FFD-derived SEI exhibits weaker C–O/C––O and 
carbonate peaks but stronger C–C organic matrix signals, proving DMC 
reduces excessive oxidation decomposition of fluorinated solvents. 
Meanwhile, the O 1 s results confirm the presence of Li2O species in 
deeper layers (Fig. 3b) [40]. As the sputtering depth increases, the F 1 s 
signal corresponding to LiF gradually intensifies and dominates the 
fluorine-containing species (Fig. 3c)[38,39]. P 2p results indicate that 
LixPFy inorganic species originated from PF6
−decomposition are mostly 
restricted to the SEI surface and are nearly absent in deeper regions 
(Fig. 3d) [40]. Such depth-dependent variations demonstrate that the 
FFD-derived SEI possesses a vertically gradient structure, in which rigid 
inorganic components (primarily LiF) are progressively enriched toward 
the anode side and stably embedded within a mild organic matrix. In 
sharp comparison, the BL electrolyte delivers a completely different SEI 
compositional gradient (Figure S5). Upon probing the inner SEI of BL, 
the overall fluorine intensity decreases significantly, accompanied by a 
drastic increase in organic C–F species and a remarkable reduction in LiF 
proportion. The C 1 s and O 1 s spectra collectively reveal that the BL 
inner SEI is heavily populated with Li2O and diverse organic fragments, 
including C–C bonds and ROCO2Li species [39]. Overall, these 
depth-resolved XPS results unambiguously distinguish the structural 
differences between the two SEI films. DMC reshapes Li+ solvation to 
mitigate over-decomposition of fluorocarbon solvents and LiPF6, con­
structing a hybrid SEI with balanced organic carbon matrix, inorganic 
LiF and Li2O for stable Li transport [41]. In contrast, the BL system forms 
a disordered inner SEI dominated by oxides and organic components, 
lacking sufficient inorganic LiF reinforcement to suppress interfacial 
degradation and dendrite growth.
Apart from the SEI chemical composition, the limited spatial reso­
lution (micron scale) of XPS constrained its ability to resolve the detailed 
SEI distribution. To overcome this, cryogenic transmission electron 
microscopy (cryo-TEM) was further employed to observe the nanoscale 
morphology and composition of the SEI. As shown in Fig. 3e–F, The SEI 
in FFD exhibited a distinct multilayer structure containing an outer 
inorganic component (predominantly Li2O) and an inner organic layer. 
It is believed that the layered SEI rather than the mosaic SEI can better 
guide uniform lithium-ion deposition, and the organic layer mitigates 
the fracture of SEI due to volume change [19,42]. Notably, multiple LiF 
microcrystalline domains were observed within the inner organic-rich 
SEI layer, which may originate from the decomposition of the fluori­
nated solvents. The defluorination process, which generate LiF, occurred 
concurrently with electro-polymerization of the amorphous organic 
matrix, resulting in a hybrid inorganic-organic SEI. Besides, the 
embedded LiF microcrystals or a heterogeneous LiF/Li2O grain bound­
aries further facilitated the Li+ diffusion within the organic SEI layer 
[29,43,44]. In contrast, the SEI formed in the BL electrolyte (Fig. 3g–h) 
was mainly comprised of amorphous matrix with lower Li+ conductiv­
ity, providing a mechanistic explanation for the divergent electro­
chemical behaviors between these electrolytes.
3.4. Cycling stability of lithium metal anode

assembled to evaluate the cycling stability of lithium metal anode. 
Limiting current density and lithium plating stripping to short-circuit 
measurements were conducted to quantitatively evaluate the Li 
dendrite suppression capability of different electrolytes. As shown in 
Fig. 4a, benefiting from the DMC-reconstructed Li+ solvation sheath, the 
FFD electrolyte delivers the highest limiting current density of 1.9 mA 
cm−1 than that of BL (1.3 mA cm−1), FF (1.6 mA cm−1) and FFT (1.7 mA 
cm−1) systems. For the FFD electrolyte, stable Li plating/stripping

anode interphase, alleviates Li⁺ concentration polarization, and signifi­
cantly delays dendrite nucleation and penetration, thereby substantially 
improving the interfacial stability and cycling durability of lithium 
metal anodes.
Fig. 4c showed the long-term cycling performance of four Li-Li 
symmetric cells. Obviously, the BL electrolyte displayed higher 
voltage polarization in the initial few cycles, followed by a sharp in­
crease after about 40 cycles, reflecting poor electrochemical stability. 
While for FF and FFT electrolyte, the cycle stability were both prolonged 
consistent with the results of CE measurements. In FFD electrolytes, the 
lithium metal anode exhibited lowest voltage polarization and most 
stable voltage profiles over 200 cycles. The competitive coordination of 
DMC strikes a favorable balance between maintaining fluorine supply 
for stable SEI and restraining excessive fluorinated solvent degradation. 
According to the Tafel curve in Fig. 4d, the FFD delivered the highest 
exchange current density (i0) than that of FFT, FF and BL electrolyte, 
indicative of faster charge-transfer kinetics and reduced voltage polar­

under harsh elevated-temperature operating conditions, galvanostatic 
cycling of Li-Li symmetric cells were also performed at 60 ◦C under same 
condition (Figure S6). Elevated temperature accelerates Li+ transport 
and boosts the kinetics of Li plating/stripping reactions, so all cells 
deliver markedly lower voltage polarization than their room- 
temperature counterparts. Nevertheless, high temperature inevitably 
exacerbates parasitic side reactions at the lithium metal interface and 
accelerates continuous electrolyte depletion, resulting in shortened 
cycle lifespans for all electrolyte systems relative to room-temperature 
tests. Nevertheless, the FFD electrolyte displays the longest stable 
cycling duration over 150 h under 60 ◦C, benefiting from its optimized 
Li+ solvation structure that reduces coordinated fluorinated solvent 
molecules and suppresses decomposition. This comparison verifies the 
favorable high-temperature stability of the FFD electrolyte system.

propensity for dendrites formation in the absence of a stable interface

Considering that differences in ionic conductivity and wettability of 
electrolytes greatly affect cell performance, we assembled stainless steel 
symmetric cells and performed EIS tests to qualitatively analyze elec­
trolyte ionic conductivity from impedance fitting. As shown in Figure S7 
and Table S3, the conductivity ranks as BL (0.843 mS cm−1), FFD (0.506 
mS cm−1), FF (0.454 mS cm−1) and FFT (0.203 mS cm−1). Such differ­
ences arise from solvent polarity and viscosity. The BL baseline elec­
trolyte is dominated by low-viscosity carbonates that accelerate Li+
migration, which accounts for its highest ionic conductivity. TTE acts as 
an inert weakly solvating diluent with an ultralow dielectric constant; it 
hardly dissociates LiPF6 and decreases the content of active solvents, 
resulting in the minimum conductivity. When DMC is added to the FF 
electrolyte, the overall viscosity declines and Li+ dissociation is 
improved, so FFD exhibits moderately higher conductivity than the 
original FF electrolyte. Besides, contact angles measured on commercial 
PP separators follow the sequence FF (50.1◦) > FFD (39.7◦) > BL (42.1◦) 
> FFT (35.9◦), as presented in Figure S8. The FF electrolyte, composed 
solely of FEC and FEMC without low-surface-tension components, pos­
sesses high surface tension and thus exhibits the largest contact angle 
and weakest wettability. In FFT, the fluorinated diluent TTE greatly 
reduces the overall surface tension, producing the smallest contact angle 
of 35.9◦Benefiting from DMC with moderate surface tension, the FFD 
electrolyte realizes balanced bulk surface tension, with its contact angle 
of 39.7◦falling between the values of FF and FFT. According to the 
above measurements, FFD exhibits neither maximum ionic conductivity 
nor the best wettability. Therefore, the enhanced performance of Li-Li 
symmetric cells is primarily attributed to the modulation of Li+ solva­
tion structures instead of electrolyte physical properties.
3.5. The performance of Li-SPAN full cells
Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was carried out to characterize the 
electrochemical stability window of different electrolytes. The FFD 
electrolyte presents the highest oxidation stability cutoff above 4.0 V vs 
Li/Li+, far exceeding the 3 V upper cutoff voltage of Li-SPAN cells 
(Figure S9). The Li-SPAN full cells with high areal loading (>5 mAh 
cm−2) were assembled to assess the applicability of the FFD electrolyte. 
The FFD electrolyte effectively reduced the internal resistance of the Li- 
SPAN cells, as evidenced by the smaller interfacial impedance including 
RSEI and Rct (Fig. 5a and Table S4). As depicted in Fig. 5b, the cyclic 
voltammetry (CV) curves displayed a positively shifted reduction peak 
and a negatively shifted oxidation peak in the FFD electrolyte, corrob­
orating the ability of the FFD electrolyte to promote the kinetics of Li- 
SPAN cells. The improved rate performance of Li-SPAN cells pro­
foundly reflected the advantage of the FFD electrolyte for promoting 
kinetics (Fig. 5c). At low current densities (such as 0.1 and 0.2 A g−1), 
the discharge capacity of Li-SPAN cells in the FFD and BL electrolytes 
were comparable; however, at a high rate of 1 A g−1, Li-SPAN cells could 
deliver a typical discharge-specific capacity exceeding 600 mAh g−1 in 
the FFD electrolyte, compared to <100 mAh g−1 in the BL electrolyte. 
Post-mortem SEM observation revealed that, in the BL electrolyte, the 
cycled Li layer expanded to fill the entire anode in the thickness direc­
tion, leaving little fresh Li for subsequent cycling (Figure S10b). The 
whole anode became thicker and more porous, and pulverization Li 
resulted in significantly increased interfacial impedance (Figure S10c). 
In contrast, the lithium metal anode in the FFD retained sufficient fresh 
Li, ensuring continued cycling (Figure S10a). In addition, the sulfur 
element was barely distributed on the lithium metal anode for both 
electrolytes (Figure S11), confirming the absence of polysulfide shut­
tling from the SPAN cathode to the anode side.
The Li-SPAN cells were assembled using 200 μm-thick lithium foils as 
the anodes, in which case the N/P ratio was around 6.5. At a current 
density of 0.5 A g−1, the Li-SPAN cells with FFD electrolyte maintained a 
stable discharge capacity of 525.7 mAh g−1 after 125 cycles and a ca­
pacity retention of 77.7% (based on the discharge capacity of 676.3 mAh 
g−1 at the third cycle) (Fig. 5d). In contrast, the specific capacity of Li- 
SPAN cells decreased continuously in the BL electrolyte. Although the 
initial discharge capacity was comparable between the BL and FFD 
electrolytes, the FFD electrolyte exhibited a higher SPAN discharge 
plateau (Figure S12a and Fig. 5e), contributing to an increased energy 
density. A more critical limitation of the BL electrolyte was the rapid 
failure of Li-SPAN cells due to the exhaustion of the metallic lithium 
anode (Figure S12b). Even after replacing a new piece of lithium foil, the 
second Li-SPAN cell displayed lower capacity than the first one and 
underwent repeated another rapid decline. Moreover, the second cell 
showed a pronounced increase in voltage polarization (Figure S12a, c), 
underscoring the crucial role of protecting the lithium metal anode to 
achieve durable and high-performance Li-SPAN cells.
Operating under a lean N/P ratio is a prerequisite for the commercial 
viability of high-specific-energy Li metal batteries. Therefore, the Li- 
SPAN cells were assembled with 100 μm-thick lithium foils, corre­
sponding to a low N/P ratio of ~2.2. The Li-SPAN cells with FFD elec­
trolyte enabled outstanding long-term cycling stability under 
asymmetric discharge/charge protocol (discharged at 0.2 A g−1 and 
charged at 0.1 A g−1) (Fig. 5f). After 175 cycles, the Li-SPAN cell still 
remained a high discharge capacity of 641.5 mAh g−1, which corre­
sponded to a capacity retention of 84.5% (based on the discharge ca­
pacity of 758.7 mAh g−1 at the third cycle). During this period, the Li- 
SPAN cells exhibited excellent cycling reversibility with an average CE 
of 99.79%. To evaluate the ultimate protective capacity of the electro­
lyte toward lithium metal anodes, an extreme lean lithium dosage of a 
low N/P ratio (~0.5, using 50 μm-thick Li foil) was employed as a 
rigorous limit verification of our FFD electrolyte’s anode protection 
capability. And the Li-SPAN cells were still able to provide >80 cycles 
(Fig. 5g). Specifically, the capacity retention reached 80.8% at 40 cycles 
(639.3 mAh g−1) and 55.6% at 85 cycles (439.5 mAh g−1 with an areal 
capacity of 3.29 mAh cm−2), based on the third-cycle discharge capacity 
of 791.1 mAh g−1. Notably, the areal capacity maintained over 4 mAh 
cm−2 before the 60th cycle. Table S6 summarized representative liter­
atures on the cycling conditions and performance of Li-SPAN cells, as 
visualized in Fig. 5h. This strongly supported that the present work

strategy is also likely transferable to other lithium metal battery systems,

way toward the commercialization of high-energy-density lithium metal 
batteries.
4. Conclusion
In summary, we demonstrated a generic and mechanism-driven 
solvation tailoring strategy to revitalize the performance of high en­
ergy density Li-SPAN batteries. Departing from the conventional reli­
ance on highly concentrated or all-fluorinated electrolyte systems, we 
strategically incorporated a strongly-solvating, non-fluorinated co- 
solvent (DMC) into dual-fluorinated FEC-FEMC electrolyte. The non- 
fluorinated DMC preferentially coordinates with Li+, forming a 
competitive primary solvation sheath. This optimized ternary solvation 
structure retains the advantage of fluorinated solvents to form layered F- 
rich SEI for fast Li⁺ transport and dendrite suppression, while mitigating 
persistent parasitic decomposition of FEC-FEMC by lowering their free 
molecular content. When paired with high loading SPAN cathode (> 5 
mAh cm−2) and thin Li foil, the Li-SPAN cells with FFD electrolyte 
enabled outstanding rate capability and cycling performance. At a low 
N/P ratio of ~2.2, the Li-SPAN cells maintained 641.5 mAh g−1 
discharge capacity and 84.5% capacity retention after 175 cycles. 
Remarkably, the Li-SPAN cell even ran over 80 cycles at extremely low 
N/P ratio of ~0.5, showing competitive performance of coin cells and 
providing a new perspective for electrolyte design in high energy density 
Li-SPAN pouch cells.
