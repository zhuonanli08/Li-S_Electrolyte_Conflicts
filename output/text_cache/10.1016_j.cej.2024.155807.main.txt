A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered as the key candidates for the next generation of energy storage 
devices. However, the practical applications of LSBs are hindered by the slow conversion of sulfur species and the 
inhomogeneous Li+ precipitation /stripping of lithium anode. Here, we report a bifunctional thiobenzamide 
electrolyte additive, 4-(trifluoromethyl) thiobenzamide (TFBCA), which synergistically improves cathode redox 
kinetics and anode stability. Polysulfides (LiPSn) are in-situ transferred to more reactive polysulfide in­
termediates (T-Sn-T), which significantly improve the liquid–solid reduction process by promoting the deposition 
dimension of Li2S. A LiF/Li3N-rich solid electrolyte interphase is formed with TFBCA, the uniform lithium 
deposition is achieved and the growth of lithium dendrites is suppressed. Correspondingly, LSBs with TFBCA 
show a high capacity (888 mAh⋅g−1 at 5C) and cycling stability (0.055 % per cycle after 600 cycles at 2C). Even 
under high sulfur loading (7.1 mg⋅cm−2) and low E/S ratio of 7, the LSBs also exhibit an areal capacity of 7 
mAh⋅cm−2 after 50 cycles, and an energy density of 314 Wh⋅kg−1 is achieved for the pouch cell. This work 
demonstrates an efficient additive strategy through molecular structure design to construct high-performance 
LSBs.
1. Introduction
With the raising popularity of 3C products and smart devices, the 
demand for high-performance power devices is daily increasing [1–4]. 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), which undergo a multiphase solid­
–liquid-solid conversion process of 16 electrons during cycling, are 
considered one of the key candidates for the next generation of energy 
storage devices due to their theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh⋅g−1) 
and energy density (2600 Wh⋅kg−1) [5]. In addition, the abundance of 
sulfur in the earth’s crust and its environmental friendliness are also 
beneficial for the development of LSBs [6–10]. However, the slow sulfur 
reaction kinetics due to the tardy liquid-to-solid conversion of lithium 
polysulfide (LiPSn) to Li2S2/Li2S hardly limits the rate performance of 
LSBs, thus reducing the utilization of sulfur species especially at high 
current densities. Besides, the growth of lithium dendrites resulting from 
the uneven deposition of Li+ which harmed the cycling stability is also a 
big hurdle for the practical application of LSBs [11–13]. To solve the 
above problems, tremendous progress has been made in the synthesis of 
high-performance electrode materials [14,15], the fabrication of modi­
fied separators [16–18] and development of advanced electrolytes 
[19,20].
The electrolyte used in LSBs plays a critical role in dissolving poly­
sulfides to ensure the reaction kinetics and the formation of solid elec­
trolyte interphase (SEI) to firm the lithium anode [21]. Since the 
conversion of LiPSn species mainly occurs in the electrolyte or at the 
electrolyte/electrode interface, adjusting composition of the electrolyte 
is an efficient way to solve the problems especially on the anode in 
lithium sulfur batteries. The introduction of homogeneous redox medi­
ators into the electrolyte will greatly improve the sulfur kinetics. Redox 
mediators generally promote the sulfur reaction kinetics mainly by the 
formation of covalent structures or orbital hybridization with LiPSn 
[22,23]. For example, Huang and coworkers [24] proposed an electro­
lyte additive diphenyl diselenide to accelerate the sulfur reaction by 
generation of lithium phenylseleno polysulfides, which enables faster

Furthermore, additives, such as organyl disulphides [25], diphenyl 
ditelluride [26] and di-isopropyl sulfide [27] have also been developed 
to promote the conversion of sulfur. At the lithium anode, the side re­
action involving highly reactive lithium metal will disrupt the homo­
geneous deposition of Li+ and causes the growth of lithium dendrites, 
resulting in lower anode stability [28–30]. The problem could be 
addressed by introducing additives with different functional groups to 
promote the formation of a more stable SEI (e.g., a LiF/Li3N-rich SEI). 
Recently, Hu and co-workers [31] proposed a 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3-hexafluor­
opropane-1,3-disulfonimide potassium salt that preferentially de­
composes on the lithium anode side to form an inorganic-rich SEI that 
suppresses the continuous corrosion from LiPSn and electrolyte, thus 
achieving highly stable Li-S pouch cells. For constructing high perfor­
mance LSBs, the issues in both cathode and anode sides should be 
overcome simultaneously. Therefore, the development of electrolyte 
additives that will synergistically improve the LiPSn kinetics and lithium 
cycling stability is of great importance for the practical application of Li- 
S batteries.
In this work, a low-cost and accessible dual-functional electrolyte 
additive 4-(trifluoromethyl) thiobenzamide (TFBCA) is investigated to 
accelerate both the sulfur conversion kinetics and uniform lithium 
deposition. As illustrated in Fig. 1, polysulfides are covalently bound to 
TFBCA, resulting in more reactive polysulfide intermediates that 
improve the redox process of Li2Sn and facilitate three-dimensional (3D) 
nucleation and growth of Li2S. On the other hand, the various hetero­
atoms and −CF3 groups in TFBCA molecule prompt a formation of LiF/ 
Li3N-rich SEI, which is beneficial for the uniform lithium deposition and 
suppresses the growth of lithium dendrites, thus improving the stability 
of Li anode. Accordingly, LSBs containing TFBCA can deliver an 
impressive rate performance of 888 mAh⋅g−1 at 5C and high cycling 
stability (over 600 cycles at a current density of 2C, 0.055% decay rate 
per cycle). The Li||Li symmetrical cells run steady for over 1000 h and 
show a very low polarization voltage (0.5 mA⋅cm−2, 0.5 mAh⋅cm−2). 
Moreover, TFBCA is still functional in Li-S pouch cells, while achieving 
gravimetric energy density of 314 Wh⋅kg−1, and the pouch cells can 
operate stably for over 30 cycles. LSBs with TFBCA show excellent 
electrochemical performance, and TFBCA has the potential for practical 
commercial applications of LSBs.

bound to the N atoms of TFBCA and the C=S double bonds are turned to 
C-S to combine with Li+, which is consistent with the calculation results 
of electrostatic potential in Fig. 2b.

increasing capacity and improving sulfur utilization due to the enhanced 
redox kinetics. The energy level of the highest occupied molecular 
orbital (HOMO) of the T-S8-T, T-S6-T and T-S3-T is higher than that of the 
Li2Sn (n=4,6,8), implying fast oxidation during charging process [33].

To verify whether TFBCA can improve the nucleation behavior of 
Li2S, the nucleation kinetics of Li2S was tested using carbon cloths as the 
working electrode and the lithium as the counter electrode, where the 
active material was Li2S8 dissolving in the bulk electrolyte with and 
without TFBCA. As shown in Fig. 3h, a higher current peak of the cell 
with TFBCA confirms the more reactive polysulfides. TFBCA reforms the 
conversion efficiency of polysulfides (Li2Sn) to Li2S. To understand the 
nucleation process of Li2S, the i-t curve in Fig. 3i was plotted based on 
the nucleation curve of polysulfides to Li2S. The theoretical 2D and 3D 
nucleation patterns (dotted lines) are based on the Scharifker Hills 
model, in which 2D progressive (2DP) nucleation and 2D instantaneous 
(2DI) nucleation are influenced by the incorporation of adatoms into the 
lattice interface, while 3D progressive (3DP) nucleation and 3D instan­
taneous (3DI) nucleation are controlled by ion diffusion in the bulk 
[32,39,40]. For the cells with TFBCA, the nucleation and growth pro­
cesses of Li2S are close to the 3DP model, which means higher deposition 
capacity and a delayed passivation layer for the sulfur cathode. The 
scanning electron microscope (SEM) image in Fig. S15 further illustrates 
the 3D nucleation of Li2S. The morphology of Li2S was more stereoscopic 
in the cells with TFBCA, while the distribution of Li2S in bare electrolyte 
was more planar. The results suggest that the addition of TFBCA can 
slow down the passivation to improve the sulfur utilization and thus the 
electrochemical performance of LSBs.

The discharged and charged products were explored to understand 
the interaction between TFBCA and polysulfides during cycling. LSBs 
with and without TFBCA as electrolyte additive were first discharged to 
1.7 V, then the cathodes were taken out and washed with 1, 2-dime­
thoxyethane (DME) for subsequent characterization. The components 
of discharged cathode were further analyzed by X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 6a, S 2p3/2 peaks located at 161.6 
eV and 160.3 eV can be assigned to the solid products Li2S2/Li2S 
[45,46]. Besides, a higher ratio of Li2S/Li2S2 was obtained in cells with 
TFBCA (Fig. S24). It suggested increased sulfur utilization from T-Sn-T to 
Li2S in the presence of TFBCA. The peaks at 162.7 eV and 163.9 eV are 
due to the C-S and N-S bonds resulting from the reaction between TFBCA 
and Li2Sn and the conversion of T-Sn-T to Li2S. The N 1s XPS results in 
Fig. 6b also confirm the existence of N-S bonds at 398.5 eV. The peaks of 
C-N and N-H bonds at 395 eV and 399.5 eV were also detected due to the 
presence of TFBCA [47,48].
The LC-MS was performed to analyze the charged products. As 
shown in Fig. 6c, the peak with an m/z ratio of 635.1 was assigned to 
polysulfide intermediate T-S7-T, which may indicate that Li2S and 
TFBCA regenerate polysulfide intermediates at the end of the charge 
instead of elemental sulfur (S8) as usual. In-situ XRD is performed to 
better understand and follow the evolution of sulfur species during the 
electrochemical process. As shown in Fig. 6e, crystalline α-S8 (~23◦) is 
rapidly converted to soluble polysulfides during the early discharge 
process both in cells with and without TFBCA; subsequently, the signal 
of Li2S (~27◦) started to appear at ~40% of the discharge depth [49]. 
Moreover, the Li2S signal is stronger in cells with TFBCA and the peak 
width is also larger, implying that the kinetics of Li2S nucleation was 
significantly promoted by the introduction of TFBCA. It is noteworthy 
that no clear signal of β-S8 was observed in cells with TFBCA during the 
charging process, which may indicate that the sulfur chain is covalently 
linked to TFBCA and functions as the active substance in the following 
cycle. Based on the above results, the functional pathway of TFBCA in 
LSBs can be summarized in Fig. 6f. The polysulfide intermediates (T-Sn- 
T) are formed in-situ from TFBCA and polysulfides, which are then 
converted to Li2S during the first discharge process. During charging, 
Li2S is oxidized to polysulfide intermediates with TFBCA as the active 
substance for the following cycles instead of the traditional sulfur 
element, significantly promoting redox kinetics and sulfur utilization.
3. Conclusion
In summary, a low-cost and efficient additive 4-(trifluoromethyl) 
thiobenzamide (TFBCA) is proposed to construct high-performance 
LSBs. The TFBCA triggered the conversion of LiPSn to more reactive T- 
Sn-T, which accelerated the reaction kinetics of sulfur species, especially 
the liquid–solid conversion to Li2S. Higher capacity was achieved by 
increasing the deposition dimension of Li2S. Moreover, the use of TFBCA 
also promoted the formation of LiF/Li3N-rich SEI, suppressed the growth 
of lithium dendrites. Accordingly, LSBs with TFBCA showed a high 
discharge capacity of 888 mAh⋅g−1 at 5C, and a 7 mAh⋅cm−2 was also 
obtained after 50 cycles at a sulfur loading of 7.1 mg⋅cm−2 and E/S of 7. 
Finally, gravimetric energy density of 314 Wh⋅kg−1 was achieved in the 
pouch cell with TFBCA. All in all, this work demonstrated a powerful 
electrolyte additive for LSBs and offered a potential strategy for the 
practical commercial application of LSBs.
