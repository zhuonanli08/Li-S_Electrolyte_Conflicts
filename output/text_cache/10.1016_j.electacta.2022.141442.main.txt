A B S T R A C T  
The commercial use of Lithium-sulfur batteries has been limited by their short service life due to the shuttle effect 
of polysulfides, which is due to the transport of polysulfides towards the anode and the formation of highly 
resistive deposits that passivate the electrodes. This problem can be solved by decreasing the transport of these 
species from the cathode to the anode and simultaneously improving the kinetics of electron transfer reactions. 
This work studies by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and cyclic and ac voltammetry the redox reactions 
of elemental sulfur in either acetonitrile (CH3CN) or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) using as a working electrode 
either pristine glassy carbon (GC) or GC modified with a thin film of poly-3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (GC- 
PEDOT) as working electrodes. Tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAPF6), 1-Butyl-3-methylimidazo­
lium tetrafluoroborate ([Bmim][BF4]) and 1-Butyl-3-methylimidazolium triflate ([Bmim][OTf]) were investi­
gated as electrolytes. It was found that the kinetics of the first reduction reaction of S8 on GC is much faster in 
CH3CN than in DMSO and when [Bmim][OTf] is used as an electrolyte. On the other hand, it was found that 
PEDOT is electrocatalytic for the first reduction reaction of elemental sulfur. Finally, by encapsulating the sulfur 
in PEDOT it is possible to increase the retention of the sulfur species inside PEDOT upon redox cycling if pure 
ionic liquids are used as electrolytes.   
1. Introduction 
The intensive use of fossil fuels to produce electricity and their use in 
transportation has negative effects on the environment and quality of 
life [1,2]. Services that make use of efficient systems of energy (i.e., low 
CO2 emissions and renewable as far as possible) are needed for the en­
ergy transition that the world must carry out in the coming decades to 
prevent the temperature of the earth from becoming so high that it en­
dangers the permanence of the human species on earth [3]. 
Solar and wind energy have emerged as alternatives in the current 
energy transition. However, the implementation of these energy sources 
needs efficient and economical storage systems that allow them to 
compete with the energy market. In this regard, lithium sulfur (Li-S) 
batteries have emerged as promising storage devices due to their high 
theoretical energy capacity (2600 Wh/kg) [4], low manufacturing cost, 
the abundance of raw materials and low toxicity [5]. The current bat­
teries that present the best-reported performances use carbon fibers with 
sulfur as the cathode and metallic lithium as the anode. Nevertheless, 
the use of this type of battery has been limited by its low life cycle, due to 
the low conductivity of sulfur (5 × 10−30 S cm−1), the formation of 
polysulfides during the discharge process [6], and the huge volume 
expansion/shrinkage of the sulfur electrode during the charge/di­
scharge process that leads to the electrode collapse and short cycle life 
[7]. The highly soluble nature of polysulfides and the low electro­
chemical reversibility of their redox reactions largely limit the practical 
use of Li–S batteries. To overcome these issues, different host materials 
[8, 9] and electrocatalysts have been studied. It has been proven that the 
solvent, supporting electrolyte, and electrode materials have a remark­
able effect on the mechanism, kinetics, and thermodynamics of the 
overall electrode reactions [10]. 
To improve the efficiency of Li-S batteries, the sulfur cathode must be 
modified with a conductive matrix that allows the electron transfer re­
actions of sulfur, which is non-conductive. On the other hand, the sol­
ubility of polysulfides in the electrolyte must be as low as possible. 
Currently, the use of conductive polymers such as poly-3,4- 
ethylenedioxythiophene [11–14], poly m-amino thiophenol [15], and

the intermediaries is by optimizing the composition of the electrolytes. 
In this regard, there are reports of the use of ionic liquids (ILs) [18,19] in 
the design of rechargeable Li-S batteries that have presented advances in 
their efficiency due to its properties such as low volatility, 
non-flammable, wide electrochemical window, and thermal stability. 
These features allow them to be used as electrolytes, thus avoiding the 
main stability problems and safety measures related to the use of most 
organic solvents as electrolytes. The first report of the use of ILs in Li-S 
batteries dates from 2006 [20]. Currently, more than twenty studies 
have been reported in which ILs have been used in the development of 
Li-S batteries [18–20]. 
The main advantages of using ILs in the development of Li-S batteries 
are the capacity to tune the solubility properties of the polysulfides and 
to improve conductivity. Currently, more than a thousand ILs have been 
proposed for applications as electrolytes [21]. The properties of these 
compounds are determined by their solubilization capacity, viscosity, 
polarity, and the chemical nature of the anions and cations used for the 
synthesis of the ILs. In the literature, the most widely used anion in ILs 
with applications in Li-S batteries is bis-trifluoromethane sulfonimide, 
and compounds derived from imidazole and pyrrole are generally used 
as cations. Some specific examples of cations are: 1‑butyl‑3-methyli­
midazolium, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium, and 1‑butyl‑1-methylpyrrol 
idinium. 
Despite the increasing interest in the use of sulfur as a cathode ma­
terial and conducting polymers as electrocatalysts to build secondary 
batteries, fundamental studies about the electrocatalytic properties of 
conducting polymers for the redox reactions of sulfur are still lacking. 
Usually, it is assumed that the conducting polymer acts as a conducting 
matrix that improves the electron transfer reactions and avoids, in some 
extension, the dissolution and losses of polysulfides from the cathode 
[14], but it has not been studied if the PEDOT is an electrocatalyst for the 
redox reactions of elemental sulfur. Even more, the composite 
rGO-PEDOT:PSS has been used as an extra layer of conventional Li-S 
battery separators, and it has been claimed that this separator led to 
lower polarization and stymied the polysulfide shuttling effect through 
the combining effects of electrostatic, physical, and chemical in­
teractions [22]. 
On the other hand, several research groups have studied how to 
encapsulate the sulfur in the cathode [23–25] and it has been proved 
that some ILs, either as electrolytes dissolved in organic solvents or as 
pure solvents, improve the performance of Li-S batteries [26,27], but it 
has not been studied the effect of the nature of the ILs on the reaction 
mechanisms and on the formal redox potentials (E0’) of redox reactions 
of elemental sulfur. 
This work studies the performance of [Bmim][OTf], [Bmim][BF4], 
and TBAPF6 as electrolytes, and PEDOT as an electrocatalyst during the 
reduction of elemental sulfur in either DMSO or CH3CN. Finally, we 
studied the retention capacity of sulfur species inside the PEDOT upon 
redox cycling using salts dissolved in organic solvents or pure ionic 
liquids as electrolytes. 
2. Experimental 
The electropolymerization of PEDOT on glassy carbon electrodes was 
carried out in acetonitrile containing 0.10 M of electrolyte using a 
classical two-compartment electrochemical cell with a platinum counter 
electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode without regard to the 
liquid junction potential. To protect the solution from chloride and 
water contamination, the Ag/AgCl electrode was placed in a separate 
compartment connected to the main cell through a Luggin capillary; a 
platinum wire in the main cell compartment connected in parallel to the 
Ag/AgCl electrode through a 22.25 µF capacitor ensured good high- 
frequency behavior [28]. A glassy carbon disk (5.47 mm2 area) was 
used as the working electrode. The glassy carbon electrode was polished 
with a succession of alpha alumina from 3 μm down to a 0.5 μm particle 
size and sonicated for 5 min after each polishing step and before use. 
Then it was activated by 20 potential cycles from 2.0 V to -1.5 V vs SCE 
(saturated calomel electrode) in 0.10 M KOH at 0.1 V s−1. Finally, using 
the chronoamperometry technique, a potential step was performed in 
the same solution from the open circuit potential to 2.0 V for 30 s in 
order to activate the surface through the formation of oxygenated 
functional groups, particularly carboxylic groups that are electro­
catalytic for many charge transfer reactions [29]. The electrochemical 
measurements were performed using a computer-controlled potentios­
tat/galvanostat CHI650E. 
It has been proven that the electropolymerization of conducting 
polymers in the presence of water yields poorly conducting films. This 
behavior can be attributed to adsorbed water molecules that can react 
with the cation radicals, inhibiting the formation of good and highly 
conducting films [28,29]. To overcome these difficulties, the polymer 
films were synthesized in the presence of molecular sieve (~20 g per 50 
mL) and before each polymerization, the glassy carbon electrode was 
cycled 20 times between −1.5 V and 0.2 V vs Ag/AgCl in the acetonitrile 
solution used for the electrosynthesis. The latter treatment decreases the 
amount of adsorbed water on the working electrode. It is important to 
clarify that 0.2 V is below the potential needed for the oxidation of the 
monomer, which is about 0.8 V. It means that during this pretreatment, 
there was no polymerization of EDOT. Then the polymer films were 
grown under galvanostatic conditions, applying a current density of 
1.32 mA cm−2 to the working electrode for 10 s. 
The electrolytes used for the synthesis of PEDOT were 0.10 M 
TBAPF6 (Merck), [Bmim][BF4] (Iolitec) or [Bmim][OTf] (Iolitec) in 
acetonitrile with 10 mM 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT, Aldrich). 
The EDOT was used as provided. In all cases, the solutions were pre­
pared with HPLC acetonitrile (Merck), and they were dried using mo­
lecular sieve 5 Å beads 8–12 mesh (CAS69912–79–4) and deaerated 
with nitrogen 99.999% (CRYOGAS) 10 min before the star of the electro- 
chemical experiments. 
The electrochemical reactions of elemental sulfur (MolLabs) were 
studied in DMSO (Merk) and CH3CN (Merk) solutions. In all cases, the 
elemental sulfur concentration was 5.0 mM and the electrolytes used 
were 0.10 M TBAPF6, [Bmim][BF4] or [Bmim][OTf]. The molecular 
solvents (CH3CN and DMSO) were dried using molecular sieve 5 Å beads 
8–12 mesh (CAS69912–79–4) before use, and the electrochemical ex­
periments with those solvents were done in the presence of molecular 
sieve (~20 g per 50 mL of electrolyte) inside the electrochemical cell in 
order to work in dry conditions during the time needed to perform the 
experiments. The molecular sieve was activated before use by using a 
microwave oven and heating it for about 1 min. On the other hand, when 
pure ionic liquids were used, they were dried using vacuum distillation 
for one hour at room temperature in order to reduce the water con­
centration as much as possible. To protect the solution from chloride and 
water contamination, the Ag/AgCl electrode was placed in a separate 
compartment connected to the main cell through a Luggin capillary, as it 
was explained before. The electrochemical cell was deaerated with ni­
trogen 99.999% (CRYOGAS). Measurements were made under ambient 
temperature conditions, 20 ± 2 ◦C. The potentials in organic solvents 
are reported vs. Ag/AgCl reference electrode, while when pure ionic 
liquids are used, these are reported vs. ferrocene/ferrocenium (Fc/Fc+). 
In order to test the electrochemical properties of S8 particles inside a 
conducting matrix made of PEDOT, a composite made of 3 layers of S8 
particles intercalated with 4 layers of PEDOT was chosen as a model. 
This composite gives reproducible data and it allows one to measure 
both capacitive and faradic currents with high precision. In this sense a 
first polymer film was synthesized for 3 s, using 1.32 mA cm−2, on the 
glassy carbon electrode, and then 10 μL of a saturated solution of sulfur 
in dichloroethane was poured on the electrode. As the solvent evapo­
rates, sulfur microcrystals are formed on the electrode. This process is 
repeated until 4 layers of polymer and 3 of sulfur are obtained, taking 
care that at the end of the process it ends up with a polymer layer

The remarkable differences in the positions and heights of the peak

with the results obtained by Park et. al. [42]. They showed that the

drastically affected by the anion nature of the IL used as an electrolyte. It 
has been observed that the tetrafluoroborate anion (BF4

-0.4 V, it is observed that the capacitances are greater when [Bmim] 
[OTf] is used as a supporting electrolyte, which suggests a high degree of 
modification of the working electrode, probably due to adsorbed poly­
sulfide species. 
It is clear that the solvent has a direct effect on the degree of 
reversibility of the sulfur redox processes and on the E0’ values. In 
CH3CN, the first reduction reaction of S8 is more reversible than in 
DMSO, but in DMSO the E0’ values are more positive than in acetonitrile 
(Table 1). 
In order to gain a better understanding of the electrode | electrolyte 
interface during the reduction of sulfur, the electrochemical impedance 
spectrum was obtained at the two formal reduction potentials shown in 
Table 1 (Fig. 2). The Nyquist plots in Fig. 2a and the values of Rct in 
Table 2 show that when DMSO is used as a solvent, the kinetics of the 
first reduction reaction of sulfur has no significant changes for the 
different supporting electrolytes. In contrast, in CH3CN, it is observed 
that the Rct values for the first reduction reaction of sulfur are lower than 
those obtained in DMSO, and that these values depend substantially on 
the electrolyte used. Finally, in DMSO, the electron transfer rate con­
stant of the redox processes of sulfur is much lower than in CH3CN, or 
the rate of the homogeneous chemical reaction coupled to the first 
reduction reaction is higher in DMSO. 
Table 2 shows that the Rct value for the second reduction reaction 
(Fig. 1, P2 peak) is much lower than the one observed for the first 
reduction reaction for all cases (Fig. 1, P1 peak) and that it is sensitive to 
the nature of the supporting electrolyte. The lowest Rct value in DMSO is 
obtained with [Bmim][OTf] and in CH3CN it is obtained with [Bmim] 
[BF4]. 
From the data obtained from the EIS simulation, it was possible to 
determine the electrode capacitance (CCPE). These values show that the 
electrode capacitance is higher during the first sulfur electrochemical 
reduction (P1) than the one observed for the second reduction signal (P2) 
in DMSO. This behavior is completely different in acetonitrile, where the 
CCPE value is higher for the second sulfur electrochemical reduction. 
This suggests that in acetonitrile, the adsorption of polysulfides on the 
working electrode is more likely than in DMSO. 
On the other hand, according to the values obtained for the [Bmim] 
[OTf] system in CH3CN (Table 2), the capacitances are higher compared 
to the other electrolytes. These facts suggest that the working electrode 
presents a high degree of modification, probably due to the adsorption of 
different species of polysulfides and/or their salts on the surface. Finally, 
it was found that the value of the Warburg element (Y0) increases when 
the redox reactions of sulfur are carried out in CH3CN, which is mainly 
related to a higher diffusion coefficient of the electroactive species in 
this solvent in comparison to those observed in DMSO. 
When it is used the glassy carbon electrode modified with a thin film 
of PEDOT as a working electrode, the electrochemical behavior of 
elemental sulfur is different than the one observed using a pristine glassy 
carbon electrode (SI-2). The presence of PEDOT shifts the E0’ of the first 
redox reaction of elemental sulfur to positive potential in all cases 
(Table 1, P1 peak), which means that PEDOT is a good electrocatalyst for 
this reaction. It is clear that in CH3CN, the first reduction process is very 
reversible (have a look at the height of the P1 peak in Fig. 3b and the 
same signal in Fig. 1b). On the other hand, it was observed that the 
solvent and the supporting electrolyte modify the electrochemical 
properties of the PEDOT films (Fig. SI-3). For example, the peak heights 
in Fig. SI-3 are higher in DMSO than in acetonitrile, the peak positions in 
DMSO shift to positive values in comparison to those observed in 
acetonitrile, the PEDOT film capacitances in the oxidized state are 
higher when acetonitrile is used as a solvent than those observed in 
DMSO, and finally, those capacitances decrease in the order [Bmim] 
[BF4] >[Bmim][OTf]> TBAPF6. (Fig. SI-3c and SI-3d). So, it is clear that 
the solvent and electrolyte are affecting both the kinetics of the S8 redox 
process and the electrochemical properties of the PEDOT.

on PEDOT and/or polysulfides affect the 3D structure of PEDOT. Table 3

(Y0) increases when the GC-PEDOT electrode is used instead of the 
pristine GC electrode, which is possibly related to an increase in the 
effective area of the electrode. 
PEDOT not only possesses excellent electrical conductivity and 
electrocatalytic properties, it can also inhibit, to some extent, the shuttle 
effect of polysulfides (observed in Li-S batteries) through the strong 
adsorption of polysulfides on PEDOT via Lewis acid-base interaction 
[50–52]. Fig. 5 shows the cyclic voltammograms obtained when solid S8 
was encapsulated inside PEDOT films. In DMSO it is observed that after 
15 cycles, the amount of sulfur species encapsulated in PEDOT is still 
significant, but the most important fact to highlight is that in the pres­
ence of TBAPF6, the retention of sulfur species inside the PEDOT is better 
than the one observed when the ILs are used. This fact suggests that it is 
possible to modify the solubility of polysulfides and alter the time in 
which they remain on the electrode. However, it is observed that the 
second reduction in S8 when using TBAPF6 is irreversible, whereas with 
ionic liquids there is a greater degree of reversibility. 
If CH3CN is used as solvent, it was observed that the sulfur reduction 
potentials are more positive when [Bmim][OTf] and [Bmim][BF4] are 
used. However, the currents were still higher when TBAPF6 was used as 
electrolyte (Fig. 5c and 5d). After 15 cycles, it was observed that the 
sulfur signals were still present in all electrolytes, obtaining higher 
currents when TBAPF6 was used (Fig. 5b and 5d). This fact suggests that 
sulfur salts formed with the TBA+ cation are less soluble than those 
formed with the Bmim+ cation in organic solvents. Furthermore, it was 
found that the sulfur signals with the ionic liquids [Bmim][OTf] and 
[Bmim][BF4], although they decrease, present a high degree of revers­
ibility and occur at more positive potentials. 
If pure and dry ILs are used as electrolytes, the redox signals of sulfur 
increase upon redox cycling even after 50 cycles at 20 mV s−1 (Fig. 6). It 
is clear that the polysulfides are quite insoluble in pure ILs, but they are 
still electroactive. The reversibility, peak shape, and position of the first 
redox reaction (Red1/Ox1) depend on the ionic liquid used (Fig. 6a and 
6b), being more positive and reversible when [Bmim][BF4] is used. The 
Red1 peak shape (in Fig. 6a) is characteristic of a kinetically controlled 
redox reaction, as expected for an electroactive solid immobilized on the 
working electrode. On the other hand, the sulfur oxidation peak (Ox1) 
shifts to more positive potentials upon redox cycling when [Bmim][Otf] 
is used. This phenomenon is probably related to the slow oxidation

The Rct value obtained in pure and dry [Bmim][BF4] is much lower 
than the one obtained in pure [Bmim][OTf], which confirms that the 
reversibility of the first reduction reaction of solid sulfur particles is 
higher in [Bmim][BF4] than in [Bmim][OTf]. The value of the Walburg 
element (Y0) is higher in the presence of [Bmim][BF4] than the one 
obtained in the presence of [Bmim][OTf], which shows that the mass 
transport processes coupled to the redox reactions are more efficient in 
[Bmim][BF4]. The low values obtained for the n exponent of the CPE1 
show the non-ideal capacitor behavior of the non-redox active islands of 
solid sulfur on the working electrode. On the other hand, the n exponent 
of the CPE2 is very close to one, which shows that the interface of the 
electroactive zones of the composite S8-PEDOT behaves as an ideal 
capacitor. Finally, the polymer, or pore, resistance (R1) is higher in 
[Bmim][BF4] than in [Bmim][OTf] (Table 4). 
As it can be seen in this communication, the complexity of the 
electrochemical reaction of sulfur is very high. Even so, we can give the 
following advice if sulfur is going to be used to build batteries using the 
chemicals studied in this paper: in the first place, the best electrolyte for 
redox flow batteries is [Bmim][BF4], and the best working electrode is 
GC-PEDOT; in the second place, PEDOT not only possesses excellent 
electrical conductivity and electrocatalytic properties, but it can also 
inhibit the shuttle effect of polysulfides, in some extension, observed in 
Li-S batteries, through the strong adsorption of polysulfides on PEDOT; 
in the third place, the reversibility of the elemental sulfur redox pro­
cesses is higher in dry electrolytes; and finally, the shuttle effect of 
polysulfides is strongly reduced if pure and dry [Bmim][BF4] is used as 
an electrolyte. 
4. Conclusions 
First of all, it was found that the formal reduction potentials of S8 and 
the kinetics of electron transfer reactions are different if either dry 
CH3CN or DMSO is used as the solvent. In second place, it was found that 
PEDOT is a good electrocatalyst for the first redox process of S8 and that 
the kinetics of electron transfer for the first reduction reaction of S8 
depends on the nature of the electrolyte. Rct increases in DMSO in the 
order [Bmim][OTf]< [Bmim][BF4]<TBAPF6 and in acetonitrile in the 
order [Bmim][BF4]<[Bmim][OTf]< TBAPF6. In the third place, by 
encapsulating the sulfur inside PEDOT films, it is possible to tune the 
retention upon redox cycling of the sulfur species in either dry CH3CN or 
DMSO by changing the electrolytes, but this effect is stronger when pure 
and dry [Bmim][OTf] and [Bmim][BF4] are used as solvents instead of 
organic solvents. Finally, if pure and dry [Bmim][BF4] is used as an 
electrolyte, the losses of electroactive material (i.e., S8 and polysulfide 
salts) upon redox cycling are very low, and the first reduction reaction of 
S8 is very reversible.
