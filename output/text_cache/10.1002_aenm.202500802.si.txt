Tuning polysulfides into clustered-states via non-coordinating molecular encapsulation to achieve an alternative kinetics in Li-S batteries

Qingyi Zheng, Leyuan Cui, Xiaojiao Zhao, Ruming Yuan, Chutao Wang, Kun Wang, Jingmin Fan, Mingsen Zheng,* and Quanfeng Dong*


Experimental Section
1.1 Chemicals and Materials
Anhydrous DME and the traditional Li-S electrolyte composed of 1M LiTFSI in DME and DOL (1:1 v/v) with 1wt% LiNO3 was purchased from DoDoChem. 1,2-dimethylbenzene (DTL) and tetrahydrofuran (THF) were purchased from Aladdin. Sulfur was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Reduced graphene oxide was purchased from the Chengdu Institute of Organic Chemistry. 4A molecular sieves were added to THF and DTL solvents and rested for 7 days to remove water. The H2O content of both THF and DTL solvents after treatment was less than 20 ppm. The lithium metal foils (≥99.9%, Ф15.6 × 1.0 mm) were purchased from China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).
1.2 Preparation of electrolyte
DTL, DME, and THF were mixed in different volume ratios, then the LiTFSI power was added as lithium salt and stirred until a clear and transparent electrolyte was obtained. The concentration of LiTFSI was 1 mol L−1. Lithium nitrate is not added to the electrolyte unless specifically mentioned. 
Preparation of electrolyte with lithium polysulfides: Different lithium polysulfides are obtained by mixing lithium sulfide with sulfur in different molar ratios in DME solvent (Li2S:S=1:3 to obtain Li2S4; 1:5 to obtain Li2S6; 1:7 to obtain Li2S8). The DME solution with a high concentration of polysulfides was mixed and diluted with other solvents (pure DME, pure DTL, pure THF) to obtain the solution containing 10 mmol L−1 Li2Sn (n=4,6,8). LiTFSI was then added to the solution to obtain electrolyte with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI and 10 mmol L−1 Li2Sn (n=4,6,8). Low concentrations are chosen to ensure that lithium polysulfide is completely soluble in different electrolytes.
Characterization
The 7Li and 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) of solution was performed by the AVANCE III 500 MHz NMR. For NMR measurements, 600 µL of electrolyte sample was placed in the outer tube of a 5 mm NMR Wilmad coaxial tube. The in-situ dynamic Li deposition behaviors were observed at 0.3 mA cm−2 for 40 min by ZOOM-0850C (SHANGHAI PUQIAN OPTICAL INSTRUMENT CO., LTD.) using Li as working electrode. The valence state of elements of the cathodes and anodes was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (PHI 5000 Versa Probe III, ULVACPHI, Japan). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the cathodes after cycles was scrutinized by field-emission SEM (HITACHI S-4800). UV-via absorption spectrometry was recorded using a Shimadzu UV 2550.
Testing of polysulfide solubility: Lithium sulfide and sulfur were mixed (1:5 by mole) and dissolved in DME solvent (1:1, v/v) to obtain 0.4 mol L−1 Li2S6 solution. Different electrolytes were prepared using the DME solution containing 0.4 mol L−1 Li2S6 (e.g., 0.35 ml of DME solution containing Li2S6 was mixed with 0.6 ml of DTL and 0.05 ml of THF to obtain the 60 DTL electrolyte) and stirred for 24 hours. The precipitated insoluble polysulfides was removed by using a filter membrane to obtain a saturated solution of lithium polysulfide. The concentration of lithium polysulfide in the saturated solution was further determined by using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The solution of 2 mmol L−1 Li2S6 in DME was prepared as the standard sample and the polysulfide concentrations of the different solutions were calculated. For DTL pure solvents, saturated polysulfide solutions of DTL were obtained by adding lithium sulfide and sulfur with stirring and then filtering by filter membranes.
1.4 Electrochemical measurements
Li-S cells assembly: Ketjen Black (Carbon ECP600JD, Lion) or rGO was mixed with sulfur with a 25:75 mass ratio and ground sufficiently for 30 min, then heated at 155℃ for 12 h to obtain the rGO/S or KB/S composites. For the cathodes with high sulfur loading, the sulfur composites, carbon nanotube, super P and LA133 (5wt% aqueous solution) were mixed in the ratio of 8:0.5:0.5:1 by weight to obtain slurry, then applied to the carbon-coated aluminum foil. The collectors were dried under vacuum for 12 h at 60℃ (0.05 Mpa). For the cathodes with conventional sulfur loading, the sulfur composites, Super P and LA133 (5wt% aqueous solution) were mixed in the ratio of 7:2:1 by weight to obtain slurry, then applied to the Al collectors (diameter of 15 mm). After being dried in vacuum for 12 h at 60 ℃, the sulfur cathodes were obtained. The mass of sulfur in each collector was about 2.4 mg, and the areal sulfur loading was about 1.2 mgs cm-2. CR2025 coin cells was assembled in an argon-filled glovebox (Vigor Tech, H2O and O2 < 1 ppm). The Li-S cells consisted of the rGO/S cathodes, Li disks (diameter of 15.6 mm), Celgard 2400 polypropylene separator, and shims with a thickness of 1mm. The electrolyte amount of conventional Li-S cells was kept at 35 μl and the E/S was 14.6 μl mgs−1. For Li-S cells with high sulfur loading, the E/S was kept at about 5.0 μl mgs−1. Li-Li2S6 cells were used with carbon paper as the cathode and Li disks (diameter of 15.6 mm) as the anode. The electrolyte containing lithium polysulfide was used as the active substance, and the amount of electrolyte was 40μl. It is noted thar the cells in the control electrolyte failed to cycle under lean electrolyte due to severe shuttle effect, so 1 wt% LiNO3 was added to the 0DTL electrolyte and 60DTL electrolyte.
Cells measurements: The Li-S batteries were galvanostatically cycled with the voltage ranging from 1.2 V to 3.2 V (Neware, Shenzhen, China). For the electrolyte with LiNO3, the charge/discharge voltage interval for lithium-sulfur batteries is 1.7 V-2.8 V (lithium nitrate decomposes below 1.7V). The cells were activated at 0.1 C for 2 cycles in advance before being texted under the high current density. CV measurements were texted with an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E and EC-lab). The EIS experiments were performed with an impedance analyzer (IM6, Zahner Elektrik, Germany), ranging the frequency from 1MHz to 100 mHz with an amplitude voltage of 5 mV. The Li-S cells after cycles were disassembled in the Air-filled glove box and the sulfur cathodes as well as anodes were washed with DME to remove the electrolyte. After drying, the electrodes were kept sealed in air-isolated containers in a glove box and quickly transferred to the SEM equipment and XPS equipment for measurements.
Potentiostatic deposition of Li2S: The Li-Li2S6 cells were discharged galvanostatically to 2.15 V at 0.1 C. After that, the deposition measurement of Li2S was performed with a potentiostatic discharge at 2.12 V until the current was lower than 0.01 mA. For the potentiostatic intermittent titration test (PITT), Li-Li2S6 cells were potentiostatically discharged from 2.24 to 1.8 V with a step size of 0.04 V. The cut-off condition at each step was that the current is less than 10 A or the time is longer than 4.0 h. The current was recorded to study the deposition process of Li2S.

Li || Li symmetric cells test: Symmetric cells were assembled by using two piece of Li disks (diameter of 15.6 mm) as working and counter electrodes. The amount of electrolyte was 40 μL. Symmetrical cells were rested for 2h before testing. For electrolytes containing saturated polysulfides, the resting time was increased to 10h. Symmetrical cells were tested under at 1 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2. The symmetric cells after cycles were disassembled in the Air-filled glove box and the Li disks were washed with DME to remove the electrolyte. 

GITT test for Li-S cells: The GITT curves were obtained by a series of galvanostatic discharge processes of 20 min at 0.1 C followed by 120 min rest at each step. All the electrochemical tests were performed on Neware multichannel battery cycler.
1.5 Computational Details
To simulate CE and CPE electrolyte systems over 17,000 atoms, we employed machine learning potentials for molecular dynamics (MLMD) simulations. The initial configurations of Li2S4, LiTFSI, DME, THF and DTL were optimized using Gaussian09 program with M062X/6-311+G(d,p). The CE and CPE electrolyte systems were prepared by randomly placing the molecules in the simulation box based on their experimental densities and molar ratios. For CE electrolyte (0DTL electrolyte), 120 LiTFSI, 74 THF, 738 DME and 30 Li2S4 molecules were added to the simulation box (60*60*60 Å3). For CPE electrolyte (60DTL electrolyte), 120 LiTFSI, 74 THF, 272 DME, 596 DTL and 30 Li2S4 molecules were added to the simulation box (60*60*60 Å3). 

The density functional theory (DFT) dataset was generated through an iterative workflow, which can be outlined as follows:
(1) Initial MD Sampling: We initiated the process by performing MLMD simulations on simplified structures comprising approximately 300 atoms. Each MLMD simulation was conducted for 20 ps with a time step of 0.5 fs.
(2) Model Training: Two independent models, differing only in their initial random seeds, were trained concurrently to assess the robustness of the predictions.
(3) Error Evaluation: After each MLMD simulation, the forces on the sampled structures were predicted using both models. Structures with prediction errors exceeding 0.2 eV/Å between the two models were identified as poorly represented by the potential model and added to a temporary dataset for further processing.
(4) Dataset Augmentation: The temporary dataset was analyzed using SOAP[1] descriptors and K-means[2] clustering to extract up to 300 structurally distinct configurations. These configurations were subjected to DFT calculations, and the resulting data were incorporated into the training dataset to enhance model accuracy.
(5) Iteration: The updated models were then used to perform another round of MLMD simulations.
Steps (3) and (4) were repeated iteratively—MLMD simulation, error evaluation, and dataset augmentation—until the prediction errors of both models were sufficiently minimized.
For the simulations, we employed the M3GNet model[3] with radial and three-body interaction cutoffs set to 5 Å and 4 Å, respectively. The loss function was formulated as a weighted sum of mean squared errors for energies, forces, and stresses, each assigned an equal weight of 1. A batch size of 4 was utilized, along with an exponential learning rate scheduler initialized at 110-3. The convergence criteria for training were set stringently, requiring forces to fall below 0.1 eV/Å. Training was performed on a Tesla A100 GPU until validation errors converged.
After training the potential function model, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on CE and CPE electrolyte systems comprising over 17,000 atoms. The simulations were carried out under NVT ensemble conditions, with the temperature maintained at 300 K, a time step of 0.5 fs, and a total simulation time of 30 ps. The initial 10 ps were designated as the pre-equilibration phase.
In addition, ESPs of molecules were calculated with Gaussian09 program at the B3LYP/def2tzvp level and visualized by GaussView.

Figure S5. Partial simulation snapshots of CPE and the length of S2-S3 bonds of Li2S4 molecules in different electrolyte. H atoms are not shown. Color of elements: C, brown; S, yellow; Li, green; N, blue; F, gray. The sulfur atoms in polysulfides are shown using larger sizes. 
In the 60DTL electrolyte, due to the encapsulation effect of DTL, the lithium atoms in Li2S4 bonds with oxygen in TFSI− anions and aggregates to form a cluster state. As a result, the free lithium polysulfide molecules are drastically reduced, and the polysulfides aggregate with TFSI− anions to form the CPE species. In addition, significantly elongated S-S bonds in Li2S4 molecule (S2-S3 atoms labeled in the Figure S4 and S5) were observed in the CPE species. According to our previous report,[4] the S-S bond at the center of Li2Sn is most easily broken, and thus the bond length of the central S-S bond determines its reactive properties. We compared the bond lengths of S2-S3 in the cluster polysulfide species in the CPE electrolyte with those in the free lithium polysulfide in the CE electrolyte, as shown in the above Figure. 
The stretching of the central S-S bonds in CPE species may be related to the mediation of TFSI−. The binding between lithium atom of polysulfides and the O atom of TFSI− increases the negative charge in polysulfides, leading to enhanced electrostatic repulsion between sulfur atoms, which in turn leads to a stretching of the central S-S bond. The bond lengths of the S2-S3 bonds of Li2S4 molecules were elongated from 1.95-2.45 Å to 2.45-2.85 Å, indicating that the Li2S4 molecules in the CPE species were activated and the reactivity was enhanced.

It is worth mentioning that a small shift of the 19F peak is also observed in CE electrolyte upon the addition of Li2S8, which may be attributed to the higher solubility and dissociation ability of the long-chain polysulfides. The strong solvation ability allows Li2S8 to influence the coordination situation of TFSI− even without the addition of DTL. However, a stronger shift of the 19F NMR peak was observed in CPE electrolyte, suggesting that the driving effect of DTL caused the aggregation of polysulfides and TFSI−.

Figure S9. UV-vis spectra of 60DTL (CPE) electrolyte and 0DTL (CE) electrolyte with 20 mmol L−1 Li2S6.
The dominant absorption at 475 nm (assigned to dissolved S62− species) and a minor peak at 620 nm (solvated Li2S6 monomers) in 0DTL electrolyte indicate free polysulfide solvation. In 60DTL electrolyte, the small absorption peak at 620 nm was disappeared and the peak at 475 nm was significantly reduced. In addition, a new small peak at around 420 nm was detected, corresponding to the formed clustered-polysulfides, confirming the transition from dissolved species to clustered polysulfides.
