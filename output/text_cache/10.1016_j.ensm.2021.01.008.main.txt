Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery, with their inherent advantages of high-energy density, low-cost, and eco- 
friendliness, shows promise as next-generation energy storage system. However, the practical Li-S batteries are 
far from achieving high-energy density and stable operation, because of the irreversible loss of active materials in 
both Li anode and S cathode. Herein, a modiﬁed diluted high concentration electrolyte (MDHCE) was designed 
for the practical Li-S batteries, which achieves a high energy density of 325 Wh kg –1 and stable cycling. Such an 
electrolyte enables a hybrid interface with high stability and fast Li-ion transport, thus improving the reversibility 
of Li anode and ameliorative kinetics of S cathode. The practicability of the MDHCE is further demonstrated in a 
0.4 Ah Li-S pouch cell, which presents no obvious capacity fading with a steady Coulombic eﬃciency of 99.6%. 
This work not only provides guidance for electrolyte design, but also shines new lights towards practical high 
energy density Li-S batteries. 
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery as a promising next-generation battery 
system holds inherited promises of high theoretical energy density (2600 
Wh kg –1 ) and low cost. Tremendous eﬀorts have been devoted to the 
development of high-performance Li-S batteries in the last decade [1- 
4] . However, most encouraging results are obtained at ideal test condi- 
tions such as low areal mass loading, ﬂooded electrolyte, and inﬁnite 
Li source, under which the energy density is far from the requirements 
of practical applications [5–7] . For constructing practical Li-S batteries 
beyond 400 Wh kg − 1 , the high-areal capacity is critical, in conjunction 
with the lean electrolyte, and limited Li excess [8–10] . Unfortunately, 
the Li-S batteries usually exhibit poor cycling and low sulfur utilization 
under such strict conditions, owing to three intrinsic challenges, includ- 
ing the dissolution of polysulﬁdes, sluggish kinetics of the thick sul- 
fur cathode, and the irreversible plating and stripping cycles of lithium 
metal anode (LMA) [11, 12] . In terms of sulfur cathode design, various 
schemes have been proposed to address the above-mentioned shortcom- 
ings of thick carbon/sulfur (C/S) cathode [13-16] . However, it remains 
a huge challenge to prevent dissolution of polysulﬁdes especially under 
practical conditions. Compared with the C/S, sulfurized polyacryloni-

the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, and negative-to-positive capacity

cm, single layer), which could operate steady without obvious capacity 
fading. This work point to the possibility towards practical Li-S batteries 
with high energy density and cycling stability. 
2. Results and Discussion 
2.1. Performance of Li-S batteries under practical conditions 
The data for the physicochemical properties of DHCE and MDHCE 
at 25°C are summarized in Table S1. It can be seen that the ionic con- 
ductivity decreases with the addition of DOL and LiTFSI to DHCE. The 
mediocre ionic conductivity (1.78 mS cm − 1 ) of MDHCE is due to the 
relatively higher viscosity (8.11 mPa • s). Hence, the wettability of MD- 
HCE is slightly poor than that of DHCE (Fig. S1). At the same time, the 
values of t Li + for these two electrolytes are as high as 0.48 and 0.46 for 
the DHCE and MDHCE, which is higher than that of traditional liquid 
electrolytes (0.2–0.3). The anodic stability of the studied electrolytes 
was evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV). It suggests that the 
anodic stability of MDHCE is higher than that of DHCE (Fig. S2). It is 
because that the free FSI - anion were reduced in MDHCE with the ad- 
ditional of DOL and LiTFSI, contributing to the higher anodic stability 
(Fig. S3). 
Conventional thin S@pPAN electrode usually shows good compati- 
bility with carbonate-based electrolyte [25, 39] . Whereas, few reports fo- 
cus on the behavior of ultra-thick S@pPAN electrode. Recently, Wang’s 
group designed a thick S@pPAN cathode with high sulfur loading up to 
6.23 mg cm –2 , which displayed excellent capacity retention of 76% 
after 120 cycles [40] . However, this result is obtained with quite large 
excess electrolyte and Li anode (N/P = 6, E/S = 5 𝜇L mg s –1 ) [38, 40] . 
Thus, to push towards practical Li-S batteries, we further raised the 
areal-mass loading and cut the excess amounts of LMA and electrolyte 
by employing a high-areal capacity Li-S@pPAN full cell assembled with 
a high areal-mass loading (26 mg cm –2 , S loading = 10.4 mg cm –2 ) Li- 
Se 0.05 S 0.95 @pPAN, a thin Li foil (200 𝜇m, 40 mAh cm –2 , N/P = 2.3), and 
limited electrolyte (2.2 𝜇L mg s –1 ). As shown in Fig. 1 a and Fig. S4, the 
practical Li-S battery using carbonate electrolyte displays a fast capacity 
decay, indicating severe side reactions between LMA and carbonate elec- 
trolyte, which is consistent with the results previously reported [28, 40] . 
In contrast, a signiﬁcantly improved cycling lifespan (30% retention 
after 60 cycles) is obtained in Li-S battery employing the ether-based 
DHCE, owing to its enhanced reversibility of Li plating/stripping. Fur- 
thermore, the Li-S battery in MDHCE demonstrates an unprecedented 
stability with a capacity retention of 80% over 60 cycles, and a steady 
CE up to 99.5%. In particular, such a Li-S battery delivers a highest 
initial areal capacity of 16 mAh cm –2 and areal energy of 24.4 mWh 
cm –2 , indicating that the employment of MDHCE not only improved the 
cycle stability of Li-S battery but also enhanced the utilization ratio of 
sulfur in the thick electrode ( Fig. 1 e). Based on the given cell parame- 
ters, the energy density of Li-S batteries employing diﬀerent electrolytes 
were estimated excluding the weight of packaging materials ( Fig. 1 b-d 
and Table S3). As shown in Fig. 1 f, the Li-S battery using carbonate elec- 
trolyte only delivers an energy density of 238 Wh kg –1 as well as limited 
capacity retention. Furthermore, the battery shows a slightly improved 
energy density of 251 Wh kg –1 in DHCE. While the hydroﬂuoroether 
(HFE) in the DHCE usually leads to a high density ( > 1.5 g cm –3 ), thus 
reducing the energy density of Li-S battery [41] . Nevertheless, Li-S bat- 
tery employing the MDHCE presents an outstanding energy density as 
high as 325 Wh kg –1 , thanks to the enhanced utilization ratio of sulfur in 
the thick electrode (Fig. S5). It must be emphasized that the value of en- 
ergy density can be further improved by using low-density diluents and 
higher-capacity Se-doped S@pPAN cathodes, which is on-going investi- 
gation in our group [37] . In brief, the MDHCE shows the dual beneﬁts 
for both cathode and anode, which leads to the improved stability of Li 
anode and high utilization ratio of S cathode. 
2.2. Compatibility with lithium anode 
The reversibility of Li plating/stripping was evaluated by the 
Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li-Cu half cells at diﬀerent current den- 
sity. At a current density of 1 mA cm –2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh 
cm –2 , the CE of LMA in carbonate electrolyte is only about 96% and the 
battery suﬀers a quick failure after about 100 cycles ( Fig. 2 a and S6a). 
In sharp contrast, signiﬁcant enhanced performance can be observed in 
the ether-based DHCE. Although an improved average CE (99%) and 
cycling stability (over 400 cycles) are obtained in DHCE, the gradually 
enlarged voltage gap suggests the unsatisfactory stability of SEI (Fig. 
S6). With the addition of LiTFSI and DOL, the average CE and cycling 
lifespan can be further improved to 99.3% and 1000 cycles, respectively, 
with steady voltage hysteresis ( Fig. 2 b). Even at a high current density 
of 3 mA cm –2 ( Fig. 2 c), the Li-Cu cell in MDHCE attains a superior cy- 
cling performance with an average CE of 99.1% for over 400 cycles. 
The morphologies of Li deposition were examined to explain the sig- 
niﬁcant eﬀect of diﬀerent electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 2 d, a disorder 
dendritic growth with loose structure is observed in the case of carbon- 
ate electrolyte, which may increase the contact area between active Li 
and electrolyte, leading to an inferior CE. While Li deposition in DHCE 
presents a compact Li stacking, which eﬀectively alleviates the side re- 
actions with electrolytes ( Fig. 2 e). Similarly, the morphology of Li in 
MDHCE reveals a dense deposition ( Fig. 2 f). Aiming to further explain 
the superior electrochemical performance obtained in MDHCE, the com- 
position of SEI after 100 cycles was characterized by X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. S7-8). The SEI generated in two electrolytes all 
contains plentiful LiF, which is derived from the decomposition of an- 
ion [42-45] . As for the C1s spectra ( Fig. 2 g-h), the Li anode cycled in

2.3. Compatibility with sulfur cathode 
A series of tests were used to investigate the reaction kinetics of the 
S cathode in two electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 3 a, the high-areal capac- 
ity Li-S battery with MDHCE shows a relatively low voltage polarization 
of 683 mV compared to that of 893 mV for DHCE. The reduced over- 
potential suggests that MDHCE can facilitate the reaction kinetics of 
discharge/charge processes in the high-areal capacities Li-S batteries. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements demon- 
strate that the Li-S battery using MDHCE displays a lower surface ﬁlm 
resistance (R 2 ) compared to that of DHCE (the ﬁrst semi-circle at high 
frequency), indicating faster ion migration through the CEI layer formed 
in MDHCE ( Fig. 3 b, Fig. S11 and Table S2) [29] . Besides, the R 2 of Li- 
S battery in MDHCE remains almost constant in the cycle, indicating 
a strong cathode stabilization provided by the CEI formed in MDHCE. 
As displayed in Table S2, the charge-transfer resistance (R 3 ) decreases 
rapidly from 44 Ω at the 1st discharge to 14.66 Ω after 10 cycles, re-

To further clarify the cathode-kinetic promotion mechanism of elec- 
trolyte, the composition and structure of CEI were deeply analyzed, 
which was crucial for the performance of cathode [31, 50] . Similar to 
the SEI composition of Li anode, the CEI of S cathode cycled in MDHCE 
also contains abundant LiF and signiﬁcantly enriched organic composi- 
tion compared to that of DHCE, according to the results of C 1s and F 
1s spectrums ( Fig. 4 a-b). It’s worth noting that metallic lithium was de- 
tected on the surface of S cathode cycled in the DHCE. This is probably 
because that trace Li dendrites puncture through the separator and is dis- 
tributed on the surface of the cathode ( Fig. 4 c) [40] . In sharp contrast, 
the S cathode employing MDHCE shows absence of metallic Li signal, 
indicating that the usage of MDHCE eﬀectively inhibits the growth of 
dendritic Li. Besides, the S 2p spectra demonstrate that the S cathode 
cycled in DHCE produces less Li 2 S/Li 2 S 2 and more Li 2 SO x compared to 
that of MDHCE. This result can be explained by the unfavorable reac- 
tions between polysulﬁdes and LiFSI to form inactive Li 2 SO x , resulting

addition of LiTFSI inhibits the above side reaction between the LiFSI and 
S cathode. Moreover, we ﬁnd that the electrolyte is greatly stabilized by 
the addition of LiTFSI. Because a highly concentrated LiFSI can trigger

2.4. Li-S pouch cell 
Based on the discussion above, the remarkable improvement of Li- 
S battery lies that the MDHCE can generate an in-situ hybrid CEI with 
high ﬂexibility and fast Li + transport illustrated as Fig. 5 a. Therefore, 
a Li-S pouch cell was assembled to evaluate the practical application 
prospects of such electrolyte at the E/S ratio of 3 𝜇L mg s –1 with limited 
LMA (200 𝜇m, 40 mAh cm –2 , N/P ratio around 3) and a high cathode 
loading of 20 mg cm –2 ( Fig. 5 b-c). The pouch cell delivers a discharge 
capacity of 0.4 Ah (areal capacity up to 10 mAh cm –2 ) with gravimet- 
ric energy density up to 238 Wh kg –1 , and could operate steady over 
40 cycles without obvious capacity fading ( Fig. 5 d, Fig. S17 and Table 
S4). The capacity gradually increases in the initial 5 cycles, suggesting a 
wetting progress. Besides, Li-S pouch cell presents a steady average CE 
of 99.6%, revealing highly reversible electrochemical conversion pro- 
cesses generated on both the Li anode and S cathode. This performance 
is also signiﬁcantly better than the control group using the conventional 
Li-S battery electrolyte (1M LiTFSI in DOL/DME + 3 wt% LiNO 3 , Fig. 
S18). Pouch cell test shows that the MDHCE has a great potential to be 
applied to the practical Li-S battery with high energy density and stable 
cycling performance.

In summary, a modiﬁed ether-based diluted high concentration elec- 
trolyte with dual eﬀects was obtained for the practical Li-S battery to

4.2. Characterization 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were carried out 
with ﬁeld-emission SEM (FEI SIRION200). TEM images were observed 
by Talos F200X (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc). Electron probe micro- 
analyzer (EPMA) were carried out using EPMA-8050G (SHIMADZU-
