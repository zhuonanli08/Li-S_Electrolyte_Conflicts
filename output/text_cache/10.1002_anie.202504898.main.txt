Promoting the Rate Performances of Weakly Solvating
Electrolyte-Based Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Tian Jin+, Xi-Yao Li+, Meng Zhao,* Shuai Feng, Zheng Li, Zi-Xian Chen, Hong-Jie Peng,
Bo-Quan Li, and Jia-Qi Huang*
Abstract: Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising next-generation energy storage devices due to their theoretical
energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1. Employing weakly solvating electrolyte (WSE) effectively alleviates the polysulﬁde
shuttle effect and improves the cycling lifespan. However, the sulfur cathode kinetics in WSE is highly sluggish to render
degraded rate performances. Herein, the sulfur cathode kinetics of WSE-based Li–S batteries is systematically investigated
to help promote the rate performances in practical pouch cells. Concretely, the sluggish cathode kinetics of the previous
charging process is identiﬁed as the main limitation for speciﬁc discharge capacity loss at high rates. Further polarization
decoupling manifests activation polarization corresponding to polysulﬁde oxidation to elemental sulfur constitutes the
dominant kinetic challenge. Accordingly, a redox mediation strategy is proposed to accelerate the charge-transfer kinetics
of polysulﬁde oxidation in WSE. 3 Ah-level pouch cells cycle stably under a high rate of 0.2 C, and 5 Ah level pouch cells
achieve an actual energy density of 470 Wh kg−1. This work deepens the comprehension of the sulfur cathode kinetics in
WSE and highlights the redox mediation strategy in achieving high-energy-density and high-rate Li–S batteries.

ronmental sustainability.[3–6] Typical Li–S batteries operate
through complicated multi-electron electrochemical reactions

To ensure sufﬁcient dissolution of LiPSs and achieve high

strong solvating power for LiPSs are usually employed.[16,17]

of mixed solvents of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-

salts.[18] Such SSE guarantees smooth sulfur redox kinetics,
and high actual energy density of up to 700 Wh kg−1 has been
achieved in practical pouch cells accordingly.[19] However,
large amounts of dissolved LiPSs in SSE inevitably diffuse
from the cathode side to the anode side, following the
concentration gradient and reacting with lithium metal. Such
side reactions between the dissolved LiPSs and lithium metal
result in serious issues regarding irreversible loss of the
active materials, uneven lithium plating and stripping, reduced
Coulombic efﬁciency (CE), and limited cycling lifespan of
Li–S batteries.[20–22]

higher CE and capacity retention that outperformed the
SSE-based Li–S batteries.[35]
Very recently, an encapsulating polysulﬁdes electrolyte
has been proposed as a new family of WSE by intro-
ducing a moderate content of weakly solvating solvents
into conventional SSE. Such electrolyte design constructs
a heterogeneous solvation structure of LiPSs, where the
inner solvation shell guarantees LiPS dissolution, while the
outer solvation shell inhibits LiPS corrosion on lithium metal
anode.[36–38] The shuttle current can be greatly reduced by
using the WSE, and the cycling lifespan of 300 Wh kg−1-
level WSE-based Li–S pouch cells can be extended to over
100 cycles.[39] Although the various WSE effectively protect
the lithium metal anode and promote the cycling stability
of Li–S batteries, the sulfur cathode kinetics is inevitably
hindered due to reduced solubility and reactivity of the
LiPSs. Such degraded sulfur cathode kinetics raises severe
challenges regarding high cathodic polarization,[40,41] reduced
speciﬁc discharge capacity,[42,43] and severely degraded rate
performances[44,45] of WSE-based Li–S batteries, especially in
high-energy-density pouch cells. Therefore, it is essential to
understand the sulfur cathode kinetics in WSE for rational
promotion strategy design to realize high-rate WSE-based
Li–S batteries.
Herein, the sulfur cathode kinetics in WSE-based Li–S
batteries is systematically investigated to guide the rate per-
formance promotion. The WSE-based Li–S batteries exhibit
longer cycling lifespan at low rates but low speciﬁc discharge
capacity at high rates. By separately evaluating the charging
and discharging processes, insufﬁcient previous charging is
identiﬁed as the primary limitation to render low speciﬁc
discharge capacity at high rates. A galvanostatic intermittent
titration technique combined with electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (GITT–EIS) method is used to decouple the
kinetics of the charging process, and excessive activation
polarization at the end of charging is found to contribute
dominantly to the kinetic sluggishness. The charge-transfer
kinetics of LiPSs oxidation to S8 is further quantitatively
evaluated using a rotating disk electrode (RDE)-based three-
electrode model system, and the electron transfer number
is intrinsically lower in WSE in comparison with that in
SSE. To address the above kinetic challenge during charge, a
redox mediation strategy is proposed to accelerate the charge-
transfer kinetics of LiPSs oxidation in WSE. As a result, 3
Ah-level Li–S pouch cells deliver 842 mAh g−1 at a high
rate of 0.2 C corresponding to a 15.3% speciﬁc discharge
capacity promotion. Furthermore, 5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cells
achieve an actual high energy density of 470 Wh kg−1 and
operate stably for over 40 cycles. This work deepens the
fundamental understanding of the sulfur cathode kinetics in
WSE and highlights the redox mediation strategy in achieving
high-energy-density and high-rate Li–S batteries.
Results and Discussion
Rate Performances of WSE-Based Li–S Batteries
To investigate the effect of WSE on lithium metal anode
protection and sulfur cathode kinetics, Li–S pouch cells based
on SSE and WSE were assembled and evaluated regarding
the cycling stability and the rate performances. The SSE is
composed of DOL/DME mixed solvents (1:3, by vol.) with
0.8 mol L−1 LiTFSI and 2 wt% LiNO3, while the WSE
incorporates 25 vol% hexyl methyl ether (HME) into the
SSE to reduce the electrolyte solvating power for LiPSs. To
evaluate the performances of the electrolytes under practical
conditions, sulfur cathode with a single-side areal loading of
7.4 mgS cm−2 and Li anode with a single-side thickness of
75 µm were employed. The electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio
was controlled as 3.5 gele gS−1 in Li–S pouch cells (Figure S1).
The long-term cycling stability at 0.05 C was ﬁrst investi-
gated. The WSE-based cell exhibits a two-plateau discharge
proﬁle similar to that of the SSE-based cell, and the initial
speciﬁc discharge capacities are close (Figure S2a). After 15
cycles, the SSE-based cell fails due to overcharging induced
by the anodic degradation, whereas the WSE-based cell
maintains a stable charge–discharge proﬁle with a speciﬁc
discharge capacity of 1096 mAh g−1 (Figure S2b). After 31
cycles, the WSE-based cell still provides a speciﬁc discharge
capacity of 856 mAh g−1, demonstrating that the WSE
effectively mitigates the shuttle effect and protects the Li
anode (Figure 1a and Figure S2c).
To evaluate the rate performances of the different elec-
trolytes, Li–S pouch cells were assembled under identical
conditions and subjected to a gradient test rate ranging from
0.05 to 0.3 C (Figure 1b). At a low rate of 0.05 C, no
signiﬁcant differences in the speciﬁc discharge capacity or the
galvanostatic charge–discharge proﬁles are observed between
the SSE- and the WSE-based cells (Figure 1c). As the current
density increases to 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2 C, both the SSE- and the
WSE-based cells maintain typical two-plateau Li–S discharge
proﬁles (Figure S3a–c). The WSE-based cell provides speciﬁc
discharge capacities of 979, 827, and 730 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.15,
and 0.2 C, respectively, which are 13%–25% lower than those
of the SSE-based cell due to the limited cathode conversion
extent. As the current density increases further, the WSE-
based cell still maintains the normal two-plateau discharge

Identifying the Limiting Factor of Rate Performances of
WSE-Based Li–S Batteries
To investigate the cause of rate-dependent speciﬁc discharge
capacity degradation in WSE-based Li–S batteries, the dis-
charge proﬁles at low and high rates were further examined,
speciﬁcally at 0.05 and 0.2 C. The WSE-based Li–S coin
cell delivers a speciﬁc discharge capacity of 1089 mAh g−1
at 0.05 C, with a total speciﬁc discharge capacity loss of
340 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C (Figure 2a). The speciﬁc discharge
capacity loss of the high plateau is 169 mAh g−1 and is close to
half of the total speciﬁc discharge capacity loss. Considering
the theoretical speciﬁc discharge capacity ratio of the high
and low plateau of Li–S batteries is 1:3,[10] the main speciﬁc
discharge capacity loss is suggested to be caused by the
process of the high plateau (Figure 2a inset). Additionally, the
initial discharge voltage of the high plateau decreases from
2.55 V at 0.05 C to 2.29 V at 0.2 C, corresponding to a loss of
approximately 143 mAh g−1 and accounting for 85% of the
overall high plateau-speciﬁc discharge capacity loss. Such a
voltage shift suggests that the speciﬁc discharge capacity loss
at the high plateau is probably due to insufﬁcient generation
of S8 in the previous charging process.
Given the interdependence of the charging and discharg-
ing processes in Li–S batteries, specialized charge–discharge
testing procedures were employed to further clarify the
limiting factor in the high plateau-speciﬁc discharge capacity
loss of the WSE-based cells, with a particular focus on
the effect of the previous charging process on the subse-
quent discharging process (Figure 2b). Speciﬁcally, different
charging rates were applied during the previous charging
step, followed by the discharging step at the same current
density. The initial discharge voltage (Region 1), the high
plateau-speciﬁc discharge capacity (Region 2), and the total
speciﬁc discharge capacity (Region 3) were compared. For
the cell discharged at 0.05 C, when previously charged at
a low current of 0.05 C (orange dashed line), the cell
provides an initial discharge voltage of 2.55 V, with a
high plateau-speciﬁc discharge capacity of 311 mAh g−1
and a total speciﬁc discharge capacity of 1089 mAh g−1

Sulfur Cathode Kinetic Evaluation of the Charging Process in
WSE
To investigate the underlying factors of the insufﬁcient
previous charging process in WSE-based Li–S batteries, a
GITT–EIS method was employed to decouple the total
polarization (ηtotal) into ohmic polarization (ηohm), activation
polarization (ηact), and concentration polarization (ηcon)
(Figure 3a).[46] Speciﬁcally, after charging the Li–S cells
galvanostatically, the current is released to allow the cells
to reach the thermodynamic equilibrium, while the voltage–
time proﬁle is recorded.[47,48] The ηtotal is represented by the
voltage difference between the initial voltage (point A) and
the thermodynamic equilibrium voltage (point C). The sum of
ηohm and ηact is determined by the voltage drop between point
A and point B. The remaining voltage difference is attributed
to the ηcon. The ηohm is calculated by multiplying the ohmic
resistance (Rohm) simulated from the EIS measurements by
the applied current (Figure 3a inset).
Based on the above method, GITT charging proﬁles were
obtained at 0.05 and 0.25 C (Figure 3b). At the end of
charging, when the kinetic voltage reaches the termination
of 2.60 V, the thermodynamic equilibrium voltage is 2.36 V
for the cell charged at 0.05 C but 2.32 V for the cell charged
at 0.25 C, conﬁrming that the LiPSs are not fully oxidized at
high rates. By further decoupling the ηtotal during the entire
charging process, the ηcon remains relatively stable, while
the ηohm is small enough to be ignored (lower than 10 mV)
(Figures S9 and S10). However, the ηact rapidly increases to
231 mV at 0.25 C at the end of charging, compared to that of
only 68 mV at 0.05 C (Figure 3c). Quantitative analysis shows
that at the end of charging, the ηact constitutes 80% of the ηtotal
and is the primary contributor to the premature termination
of the charging process at the high rate of 0.25 C (Figure 3d).
After the GITT charging, a GITT discharging procedure
was conducted, showing that the cell charged at 0.25 C
exhibits an initial thermodynamic equilibrium voltage that

To further analyze the cathodic kinetics of LiPSs oxidation
to S8 during charge, an RDE-based three-electrode model
system was constructed to determine the intrinsic kinetic
parameters (Figure 4a).[49,50] A glassy carbon electrode, a
lithium foil, and an Ag/AgCl electrode were adopted as
the working, the counter, and the reference electrodes,
respectively, with a Li2S8-containing electrolyte providing
the electrochemical reactants (Figure S14). Based on the
Koutecky–Levich (K–L) equation, linear sweep voltammetry
was performed at different rotation speeds to establish
the relationship between the limiting current density and
the rotation speed (Figure 4b and Figure S15). Based on
the Randles–Sevcik (R–S) equation, cyclic voltammetry was
conducted at different scan rates without rotation to establish
the correlation between the peak current density and the scan
rate (Figure 4c and Figure S16). By solving the K–L and R–
S equations simultaneously, the electron transfer number of
Li2S8 oxidation to S8 can be determined. In WSE, the electron
transfer number of Li2S8 oxidation to S8 is 1.13, signiﬁcantly
lower than that of 1.54 observed in SSE, indicating that
the Li2S8 oxidation process in WSE faces intrinsic kinetic
challenges that leave a substantial amount of Li2S8 unreacted
(Figure 4d).
Redox Mediation to Promote the Rate Performances of
WSE-Based Li–S Batteries
Due to the intrinsic sluggish kinetics of LiPSs oxidation to
S8 in WSE, an advanced charging redox mediator (RM)
is introduced to accelerate the cathode oxidation kinetics
of WSE-based Li–S batteries at high rates. The working
mechanism of charging RM is illustrated in Figure 5a.
First, the oxidized-state RM (RMox) chemically reacts with
Li2S8, generating S8 and the reduced-state RM (RMred).
The RMred then diffuses to the cathodic conductive surface
and is electrochemically re-oxidized to RMox. Due to the
faster oxidation kinetics of the RMox–RMred redox couple,
the constructed reaction bypass facilitates the unfavorable
direct electrochemical conversion of Li2S8 to S8. In this
work, benzo[1,2-b:6,5-b’]dithiophene-4,5-dione (BDTD) was
introduced as the charging RM (Figure S17). The oxidation
peak of BDTD is located at 2.56 V determined by cyclic
voltammetry (Figure S18), which is higher than the voltage
of Li2S8 oxidation to S8 (2.37 V, Figure S19), ensuring
the thermodynamic feasibility of the RMox in oxidizing
Li2S8 chemically. Furthermore, when Li2S8 is added to the
RM solution, the color changes and solid precipitates are
generated, conﬁrming that the RM indeed spontaneously
reacts with Li2S8 to form solid S8 (Figure S20).
To evaluate the effectiveness of the charging RM strategy,
WSE-based Li–S cells containing 10 mmol L−1 BDTD were
assembled and tested. The Li–S cell with the RM achieves
speciﬁc discharge capacities of 868, 814, and 769 mAh g−1
at 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3 C, respectively, averaging 10% higher
than the cell without the RM (Figure 5b and Figure S21).
The capacity contribution from BDTD itself is negligible
(Figure S22), indicating the capacity promotion is afforded
by redox mediation. Additionally, the initial discharge voltage
of the redox-mediated Li–S cell increases from 2.29, 2.24,
and 2.25 V to 2.42, 2.39, and 2.38 V at 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3 C,

To evaluate the feasibility of the charging RM in practical
WSE-based Li–S batteries, 3 Ah-level pouch cells were
assembled and tested with the BDTD electrolyte. The cells
featured high-sulfur-loading cathodes with a single-side areal
sulfur loading of 7.4 mgS cm−2, ultrathin lithium anodes with
a single-side thickness of 75 µm, and a low E/S ratio of
3.5 gele gS−1. The Li–S pouch cell with the RM provides
a speciﬁc discharge capacity of 842 mAh g−1 and a high
plateau-speciﬁc discharge capacity of 210 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C,
surpassing the cell without the RM by 112 and 72 mAh g−1,
respectively (Figure 5d and Figure S26). Additionally, the
initial discharge voltage increases by 30 mV, indicating that
the RM effectively enhances the sufﬁciency of the previous
charging process in practical WSE-based Li–S pouch cells
(Figure S27). The introduction of the charging RM maintains
the cycling performance, with the pouch cell retaining a
discharge capacity of 727 mAh g−1 after 18 cycles at 0.2
C (Figure S28). At the pouch-cell level, the Li–S pouch
cell with the RM outperforms many other reported sulfur
cathode modiﬁcation strategies regarding rate performances
and speciﬁc discharge capacity at a lower E/S ratio (Figure
S29 and Table S2). To further demonstrate the potential
of the RM-assisted WSE strategy to realize high-energy-
density devices, a 5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell with the RM
was assembled and tested under extremely lean electrolyte
conditions of an E/S ratio of 2.8 gele gS−1 (Table S3). The
pouch cell successfully achieves an actual energy density of
470 Wh kg−1 based on the total mass including tabs and
packages and maintains stable operation for over 40 cycles
(Figure 5e), highlighting the high potential of the charging
RM-assisted WSE-based Li–S batteries for achieving high
energy density, high rate, and long cycling stability.
Conclusion
In summary, the sulfur cathode kinetics of WSE-based Li–
S batteries is systematically investigated to enhance the
rate performances in practical pouch cells. Although the

actual energy density of 470 Wh kg−1 and operate stably for

of the sulfur cathode kinetics in WSE and underscores the
effectiveness of the redox mediation strategy in enabling
