ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are touted for their
attractive theoretical performance specifications, which greatly
surpass the limits of conventional lithium-ion batteries. However,
their commercialization is halted by Li dendrite growth and the
notorious polysulfide shuttle effect. In this study, we apply a
positively charged amino acid,
L-arginine (Arg), as an effective
electrolyte additive for stabilizing both electrodes simultaneously.
The additive combats the shuttle effect by forming a solid precipitate
with polysulfide intermediates, while also suppressing Li dendrite
growth via electrostatic shielding. As a result, symmetrical Li||Li cells
equipped with the additive display extended lifespans (>500 h, vs
∼205 h with the blank electrolyte). More notably, Li−S cells benefit
from both mechanisms, displaying improvements in capacity
retention (65% vs 51% for the blank electrolyte, after 300 cycles at 0.2 A/g). This study introduces Arg as an effective dual-
interface stabilizer for both the Li anode and the S cathode, highlighting amino acids as promising biomolecules for sustainable
energy storage.
KEYWORDS: Amino acid, Electrolyte additive, Arginine, Li−S battery, Polysulfide shuttle effect, Lithium dendrite
1. INTRODUCTION
Boasting an impressive theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh/g, a
high energy density of 2600 Wh/kg, and a sustainable, earth-
abundant cathode material, the lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery
has attracted considerable attention for applications in
consumer electronics, electric vehicles, and grid-scale energy
storage.1,2 Unfortunately, the commercial viability of Li−S
batteries is hindered by a combination of key challenges arising
from both the S cathode and the Li metal anode. In a
phenomenon known as the notorious “shuttle effect”, long-
chain polysulfide intermediates (Li2Sx, x = 8, 6, 4) formed
during discharge dissolve into the liquid electrolyte, diffuse
across the separator, and corrode the Li anode.3,4 Moreover,
the uneven deposition of Li+ on the anode during extended
cycling leads to the formation of Li dendrites and electrically
isolated Li particles.5,6 The persistent loss of active materials
from both electrodes results in severe performance limitations,
including low Coulombic efficiency (CE), unstable cycling,
and rapid capacity decay.
Researchers have gone to great lengths to remedy the shuttle
effect and Li dendrite growth. Modifying the S cathode is the
most common strategy, including spatial encapsulation of S in
porous hosts,7,8 applying multifunctional polysulfide-philic
binders,9−11 or incorporating a broad array of transition
metal compound catalysts.12−15 Less frequently reported are
modifications to the Li anode, such as artificial protective
layers,16,17 alloying,18 or various 3D hosts or frameworks19,20
which homogenize Li+ deposition or minimize parasitic side
reactions with the electrolyte. However, these single-side
approaches are unable to simultaneously address the short-
comings of both electrodes. Another common strategy is
redesigning the separator to enable dual functionalities tailored
toward both the S cathode and the Li anode. Novel Janus-
structured separators incorporating carbon nanomaterials,
polymers, inorganic compounds, and/or metal-/covalent-
organic frameworks (MOFs/COFs) have demonstrated
promising results.21−23 Despite impressive performance
improvements, these strategies tend to encounter scalability
issues or rely on methods that are incompatible with existing
battery manufacturing processes.
As the “life blood” of batteries, the liquid electrolyte makes
intimate contact with all battery components, playing a crucial
role in the performance of both electrodes. Compared to

modifications of the Li anode, S cathode, and separator,
incorporating additives into the liquid electrolyte only requires
a single fabrication step and has been shown to elevate
electrochemical performance through a variety of mechanisms.
Numerous additives have been employed to combat the shuttle
effect by reducing the solubility or mobility of polysulfides24,25
or forming protective layers on the cathode.26,27 Sulfur redox
mediators or catalysts28−33 have also seen use, accelerating the
conversion of soluble Li2Sx to solid Li2S. To stabilize the
anode, additives that inhibit dendrite growth34,35 or modify the
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) composition36−39 are
reported. While electrolyte additives have demonstrated
remarkable versatility, many are toxic or costly to synthesize.
Additionally, many of the aforementioned strategies address
only a single issue of the multitude affecting Li−S batteries.
Therefore, identifying multifunctional additives that are also
readily available and environmentally benign remains an
important and nuanced task.
Over the past decade, natural proteins have attracted
growing interest in Li−S battery research owing to their
structural/chemical diversity, ubiquity in nature, and shared
sustainability goals with the Li−S formulation itself. Most
notably, proteins intrinsically possess a variety of functional
groups, enabling various interactions with Li+ and polysulfides.
Several protein-based binders have been reported to elevate
electrochemical performance by improving sulfur retention,
active material distribution, or cathode mechanical integ-
rity.40−42 Proteins have also seen use in separator engineering
in the form of surface functionalization,43 nanocomposite
interlayers,44−46 and freestanding nanofibrous mem-
branes,47−49 which facilitate Li+ transport and polysulfide
trapping/conversion. In these studies, the large molecular size
and complexity of proteins are exploited, employing them as
superior substitutes for synthetic polymers. However, these
same characteristics pose a logistical challenge when proteins
are employed as liquid electrolyte additives. With their
tendency to aggregate in solutions with high salt concen-
trations,50 the rich functional groups of proteins are often
occluded even after stringent denaturation processes. To date,
only one study has reported a protein-based material as an
effective electrolyte additive in Li−S batteries.51
As the building blocks of proteins, amino acids offer simpler
processability while still conferring the performance-enhancing
benefits and greenness of proteins. Furthermore, their
functional groups remain fully exposed to the electrolyte,
making them appealing candidates for electrolyte additives.
With a “toolkit” of 20 unique molecules, individual amino acids
also offer a more tunable approach than complex proteins,
allowing for a stronger mechanistic understanding of their roles
in Li−S cells. In a previous study, we applied an ultralow
dosage of L-leucine, an amphiphilic amino acid, to the liquid
electrolyte. The additive facilitated electrolyte wetting and Li+
transport through the separator, while also counteracting the
shuttle effect by catalyzing the conversion of Li2Sx to Li2S.52
Although electrochemical performance improved, polysulfides
still showed high mobility in the electrolyte. More importantly,
improvements observed on the Li anode were largely a
byproduct of reduced polysulfide corrosion, and the
fundamental issue of Li dendrite growth was not directly
addressed. Incidentally, our other recent studies explored L-
arginine as a strong polysulfide-trapping agent in the cathode
and separator of Li−S batteries,53−55 whereas separate reports
on aqueous Zn-ion batteries employed
L-arginine as an
electrolyte additive to suppress dendrites.56,57 In light of
these recent developments, we propose that applying L-arginine
to the electrolyte of Li−S batteries may simultaneously
mitigate dendrite formation and directly suppress the shuttle
effect.
Herein, we employed L-arginine (Arg), a positively charged
amino acid, as an electrolyte additive for Li−S batteries. As
confirmed by visual and spectroscopic analyses, the Arg
additive binds with polysulfides to form a solid precipitate
on the cathode surface, strongly combating the shuttle effect.
Additionally, the positively charged Arg molecules compete
with Li+ and deposit onto the dendrite tips. The adsorbed Arg
molecules generate an electrostatic shielding effect, which
redistributes the flux of Li+ and inhibits dendrite growth,
enabling Li||Li cells with extended lifespans (>500 h) and
reduced overpotential. More significantly, Li−S cells with both
electrode interfaces stabilized by the Arg additive display a
higher capacity retention (65%) relative to the blank
electrolyte (51%) after 300 cycles at 0.2A/g. This study
showcases the dual effectiveness of Arg as a liquid electrolyte
additive for Li−S batteries and underscores amino acid−based
chemistry as a promising pathway toward sustainable energy
storage.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Raw Materials
L-arginine (Arg, >98%), sulfur (S, reagent grade), lithium nitrate
(LiNO3, reagent grade), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, mw = 5.34 ×
105 g/mol), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, anhydrous >99.5%),
dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous >99.5%), and 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL, anhydrous >99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich
Co., Ltd. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI,
>99%) and lithium sulfide (Li2S, >99.9%) were purchased from
Fisher Scientific Inc. Carbon black (CB, Super C45) and carbon-
coated Al foil were purchased from MTI Co., Ltd. Celgard 2325 with
a thickness of 25 μm was purchased from Beyond Battery Ltd.
Lithium metal chips, with a diameter of 15.6 mm, were purchased
from BatterySource Co., LLC.
2.2. Sample Preparation
2.2.1. Preparation of Electrolytes. The blank electrolyte
consisted of 1 M LiTFSI in a mixed solvent of DOL/DME (1:1, v/
v), with 2 wt % LiNO3. To obtain the Arg-modified electrolyte, Arg
powder (0.5 wt %) was stirred into the blank electrolyte for ∼24 h to
obtain a colorless, transparent solution. An electrolyte containing 0.1
M Li2S6 was prepared by adding appropriate quantities of Li2S and S
powders into the blank electrolyte, followed by vigorous stirring at 60
°C. The Li2S6-Arg precipitate was produced simply by mixing the Arg-
modified electrolyte with the electrolyte containing 0.1 M Li2S6,
followed by centrifuging and removal of the supernatant. The
precipitate was rinsed with DOL/DME and then dried in an Ar
glovebox prior to spectroscopic characterizations.
2.2.2. Preparation of Sulfur Cathodes. A premade mixture of S
and CB powders was combined with PVDF and an appropriate
volume of NMP to form the electrode slurry. Using a film applicator
(50 μm), the slurry was cast onto carbon-coated Al foil and then
vacuum-dried for 12 h at 50 °C. The resulting cathode sheet was
punched into 12.7 mm-diameter discs and stored in an Ar glovebox
for later use. The composition of the cathode was 67.5 wt % S, 22.5 wt
% CB, and 10 wt % PVDF, with sulfur loading in the range of 1.0−1.5
mg/cm2.
2.2.3. Cell Assembly. For all coin cells, CR2025 casings were
used. For consistency, a constant electrolyte volume of 40 μL was
applied, and all cells were crimped to 50 MPa. All cells were
assembled in an Ar glovebox.

2.3. Material Characterizations
Li anodes and S cathodes were extracted from cycled cells, rinsed 3
times with DOL/DME, and then dried in an Ar glovebox prior to
imaging. The morphology of the cycled electrodes was examined via
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Apreo VolumeScope). The
chemical compositions of Arg powder and the Li2S6-Arg precipitate
were investigated via attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform
infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, Nicolet iS50, OMNIC)
and Raman spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher DXR2XI Raman Imaging
Spectrometer). For Raman testing, the laser power was 2 mW with an
excitation wavelength of 532 nm (4 cm−1 spectral resolution), and the
exposure time was 0.33 s, averaged over 100 scans. A 10× objective
lens was used for magnification. The wetting of electrolytes on various
surfaces was characterized using an optical contact angle analyzer
(OCA 15 Plus, Dataphysics Instruments, SCA20), with a constant
droplet volume of 3 μL and a drop speed of 15 μL/s.
2.4. Electrochemical Characterization
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were
conducted in the frequency range of 0.1−106 Hz. Cyclic voltammetry
(CV) tests of Li-stainless steel cells were carried out at a scan rate of
0.05 mV/s in the voltage range of 1.8−2.8 V. Linear sweep
voltammetry (LSV) of Li-stainless steel cells was conducted in the
voltage range of 1.5−6.5 V at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. EIS, CV, and LSV
characterizations were all conducted on an electrochemical work-
station (BioLogic VSP, EC-Lab).

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The working mechanisms of the Arg additive are depicted in
Figure 1. In the cell with the blank electrolyte (Figure 1a), the
dissolution of Li2Sx during cycling results in severe polysulfide
shuttling and a persistent loss of active sulfur. Additionally, Li
nuclei on the anode surface develop into dendrites, which
continually grow due to localized Li+ deposition (Figure 1c).
The persistent growth of dendrites poses the risk of short-
circuiting, while fractured dendrites accumulate as “dead” (i.e.,
electrically isolated) Li particles. Moreover, the high surface
area of dendritic Li metal accelerates electrolyte degradation.
These combined issues contribute to the rapid capacity loss,
cycling instability, and short cycle life observed in Li−S
batteries. In contrast, when Arg is added to the electrolyte
(Figure 1b), soluble Li2Sx is rapidly sequestered by the additive
due to strong binding interactions and is retained on the
cathode surface as a solid precipitate, mitigating sulfur losses
and polysulfide shuttling. On the Li anode (Figure 1d), the
relatively larger Arg molecules compete with Li+ and adsorb
onto Li nuclei, forming an electrostatic shield, which
redistributes the flux of Li+. This effect is attributed to the
positively charged guanidinium group of Arg, which remains

The strong binding interaction between soluble polysulfide
species and the Arg additive is demonstrated through the
formation of a precipitate (Figure 2a inset). Initially, the
electrolyte containing Li2S6 is dark brown and uniform, while
the electrolyte with Arg is transparent and colorless. Upon
mixing both electrolytes, a bright orange precipitate forms on
account of the strong affinity of Arg toward Li2S6. As revealed
in previous studies,53−55,58 the positively charged guanidinium
side group (−HNC(NH2)2
+) of Arg serves as an additional
strong binding site for Li2S6, in addition to the carboxylate
(−COO−) and amine (−NH2) groups in the core structure of
the amino acid. To reveal the chemical composition of the
precipitate, Raman and FTIR spectroscopies were conducted.
Figure 2a shows the Raman spectra of pure Arg and the Li2S6−
Arg precipitate over 50−3400 cm−1. Pure Arg exhibits
characteristic peaks at ∼3359 cm−1 and ∼3298 cm−1 (N−H
stretching), ∼2917 cm−1 and ∼2867 cm−1 (C−H/N−H
stretching), 1439 cm−1 (CH2 bending/COO−deformation),
1069 cm−1 (guanidinium C−N stretching), and 985 cm−1, 879
cm−1, and 803 cm−1 (C−C/C−N skeletal modes), consistent
with reported Raman assignments of Arg.59,60 The Li2S6-Arg
precipitate displays strong S−S vibrational modes at 474 cm−1,
445 cm−1, 404 cm−1, 244 cm−1, 221 cm−1, and 154 cm−1,
characteristic of cyclo-S8 formed from polysulfide decom-
position.61−63 Several Arg fingerprint peaksincluding ∼2917
cm−1, ∼1069 cm−1, and the shifted modes at ∼1439 cm−1 and
∼814 cm−1remain visible, confirming the incorporation of
Arg in the solid. Weaker Arg features, such as the 985 cm−1
skeletal mode, are obscured by the strong fluorescence
background generated by sulfur-rich species under 532 nm
excitation.
FTIR analysis in Figure 2b further reveals the shared
moieties between pure Arg and the precipitate. In the high-
frequency region, both samples share peaks corresponding to
N−H stretching (3358 cm−1 and 3296 cm−1), carboxyl O−H
stretching (3051 cm−1), and C−H stretching (2944 cm−1 and
2858 cm−1). The broader peaks exhibited by the Li2S6-Arg
precipitate are explained by the strong interactions between
Arg and the polysulfide decomposition products or residual Li
salts. A new broad peak appears for Li2S6-Arg at 3131 cm−1,
which can be attributed to NH3
+ salts formed between Arg and
Li salt anions. In the fingerprint region, both samples share
CO and N−H stretching features between ∼1550−1750
cm−1, a −COOH feature near 1440 cm−1, and C−N features
around 1130−1190 cm−1all of which undergo shifts due to
new intermolecular interactions. The peaks appearing at 1354
cm−1 and 1082 cm−1 can be ascribed to the sulfonamide (−N−
(SO2)−) and trifluoromethyl (−CF3) groups of LiTFSI, while
the shoulder peak at ∼1065 cm−1 can be attributed to the
sulfate of Li2SO4, another oxidation product of Li2S6 in
ambient air. These spectroscopic results confirm the
polysulfide binding capability of the Arg additive.
The wetting behavior of each electrolyte on the anode,
cathode, and separator was quantified via optical contact angle
measurements (Figure S1). For all three components, only
marginal differences of <3° were observed between electro-
lytes, indicating minimal effects on wettability. Interestingly,
the Arg-modified electrolyte showed a slightly higher contact
angle on the separator. This can be attributed to the high
polarity of the additive or minor viscosity effects, which reduce
wettability on the hydrophobic polyolefin separator. To ensure
that this decrease in separator wettability did not compromise
the transport properties of the electrolyte, the ionic
conductivities of each electrolyte were measured in sym-
metrical stainless-steel cells. As observed in Figure S2, the Arg-
modified electrolyte presents an ionic conductivity of 0.649
mS/cm, which is slightly lower than the blank electrolyte
(0.855 mS/cm), consistent with the contact angle measure-
ments. Nevertheless, both electrolytes exhibit similar ionic
conductivities on the order of 1 × 10−4 S/cm, and the
performance of Li−S cells is unaffected by this minor
discrepancy.

To understand the additive’s effect on the S cathode
morphology, cycled Li−S cells were disassembled, and SEM
micrographs were taken of the extracted cathodes. For
comparison, images of the fresh cathode are also provided in
Figure 4a. Before cycling, the cathode exhibits a rough,
granular surface. After 20 cycles in the blank electrolyte, the
cathode exhibits a significantly more open and porous
structure (Figure 4b). At high magnification, several enlarged
pores with diameters of ∼5 μm are visible on the surface.
These features can be linked to the progressive loss of sulfur
during cycling, which leaves several voids in the framework of
the cathode. In contrast, the cathode cycled in the Arg-
modified electrolyte (Figure 4c) maintains a more uniform
structure without noticeable pore enlargement, suggesting
better sulfur retention during cycling. Moreover, several
flocculent deposits appear on the cathode surface, which likely
originate from the precipitation of Li2Sx-Arg. These ex-situ
observations confirm that the Arg additive effectively mitigates
sulfur losses and combats polysulfide shuttling.
The compatibility of Li metal with each electrolyte was
initially examined in Li-stainless steel cells. As shown in the
LSV scan in Figure S3, both electrolytes display similar
electrochemical stability windows, and no significant response
currents observed under ∼4 V. Similarly, CV scans of Li-
stainless steel cells show closely overlapping voltage profiles
(Figure S4). In both tests, no new peaks appear with the
introduction of Arg, indicating that the additive remains
electrochemically stable within the operating voltage range of
the Li−S cells.

metal anodes. As shown in Figure 5a, the cell with the blank
electrolyte is initially stable and maintains a steady over-

and the voltage profile fluctuates erratically, followed by cell
failure after ∼205 h. In this case, the unmitigated growth of
dendrites creates a large surface area of reactive Li, which
consumes the electrolyte excessively. On the other hand, the
cell with the Arg-modified electrolyte shows much stabler
cycling behavior. The initial overpotential is higher due to the
shielding effect of Arg, which increases the energy barrier for Li

The impedance evolution of Li||Li cells was also studied via
EIS. Figure 5b−c displays the Nyquist plots for cells with blank
and Arg-modified electrolytes recorded at the zeroth (fresh),
10th, 20th, 50th, and 100th cycle. Each spectrum was fitted to
an equivalent circuit model, shown in the inset of Figure 5b.
For direct comparisons during each cycle, conventional
presentations of Nyquist plots with fittings are included in
Figure S5. Impedance values extracted from fitting are listed in
Table S1 and their trends are summarized in Figure 5d−f. (1)
The bulk resistance (Rb) occurs in the high-frequency region
and is represented by the real-axis intercept. Rb is associated
with ohmic resistance from the electrolyte and is modeled by a
simple resistor. As displayed in Figure 5d, the Li||Li cell with
the Arg-containing electrolyte shows lower Rb values
throughout cycling, implying reduced electrolyte degradation.
(2) The SEI resistance (RSEI) is represented by the first
semicircle occurring in the midhigh frequency region and is
associated with the migration of Li+ through the SEI. For
better fitting, RSEI is modeled by two R/CPE (resistance/
constant phase elements) to account for multiple SEI phases.64
Initially, both fresh cells exhibit large RSEI values, which
decrease as the Li surface activates during cycling (Figure 5e).
The RSEI of the cell with the blank electrolyte rapidly drops by
the 10th cycle but increases again after the 20th cycle due to
unregulated Li+ stripping/plating. This is explained by high-
surface-area dendritic Li growths, which consume the electro-
lyte and produce a nonuniform SEI. For the Arg-modified cell,
RSEI decreases more slowly but continues to trend toward
smaller impedances. This suggests that Arg contributes to a
stabler and more Li+-conductive SEI. (3) The charge transfer
resistance (RCT) is represented by a second semicircle in the
midlow frequency region and is modeled by a single R/CPE
element. RCT is attributed to the kinetic barrier for Li/Li+
conversion. As seen in Figure 5f, RCT increases sharply in the
blank electrolyte due to the accumulation of dead Li particles,
which block active sites on the anode. Conversely, RCT steadily
decreases for the Li||Li cell with an Arg-modified electrolyte.
The shielding effect of the Arg additive mitigates the formation
of dendrites and dead Li, improving the charge transfer kinetics
at the anode surface. Altogether, these results reinforce the
effectiveness of the Arg additive as a dendrite-suppressing
agent.
The morphology of cycled Li anodes was investigated via
SEM. As shown in Figure S6, the surface of pristine Li metal is
flat and compact. However, after 20 cycles in the blank
electrolyte (Figure 6a), the surface of the anode becomes
“mossy” and dendritic due to inhomogeneous Li+ deposition.
By 100 cycles (Figure 6b), the surface is rough and severely
pulverized due to dendrite breakage and the accumulation of
dead Li particles. The large surface area of the anode
accelerates electrolyte degradation and leads to premature
cell failure, consistent with electrochemical characterizations
(Figure 5a). In contrast, the anode cycled in the Arg-modified
electrolyte develops a denser, plate-like morphology, with
abundant clusters of Li nuclei (diameter <0.5 μm) scattered
across the surface (Figure 6c). The shielding effect of the Arg
additive inhibits the growth of these nuclei, redistributing the
flux of Li+. The repeated interplay between nucleation and
growth suppression leads to the formation of numerous small
nuclei. Even after 100 cycles, the anode maintains a compact
and well-integrated structure due to more uniform Li+
deposition (Figure 6d). These significant improvements are
further evident on a macroscopic level. As seen in the side-by-
side comparison in Figure S7, the anode cycled in the blank
electrolyte appears darkened and corroded from parasitic side
reactions, while the anode cycled in the Arg-modified
electrolyte remains a shinier, lighter shade. Overall, these
morphological observations align well with the previous
electrochemical results and support the proposed anodic
mechanisms of the Arg additive.
Li||Cu cells were assembled to evaluate the effect of each
electrolyte on Li stripping/plating behavior. Since Li||Cu cells
are discharged to a fixed capacity at each cycle, their
Coulombic efficiency (CE) reflects the fraction of plated Li
that can be reversibly stripped during each cycle. As shown in
Figure 7a, the CE of the cell with the blank electrolyte falls
below 80% after only 40 cycles, indicating poor Li reversibility.
This decline arises from the inhomogeneous deposition of Li+,
which produces electrically isolated Li particles. In contrast, the
cell with the Arg-modified electrolyte maintains a high and
stable CE for 125 cycles. The electrostatic shielding effect of
the Arg additive promotes more uniform Li+ deposition,
contributing to reversible Li plating/stripping. The voltage
profiles in Figure 7b provide further insight into these
differences. After 200 h, the voltage profile of the blank cell
appears laterally compressed with increased overpotential,
consistent with the CE plunge at ∼40 cycles. Interestingly, the
Arg-modified cell displays higher overpotentials for the entire
test duration yet maintains stabler overall cycling. Higher
overpotentials are associated with higher nucleation energy
barriers and smaller nuclei.65 Because Arg competitively
adsorbs onto Li nuclei and redistributes the Li+ flux via
electrostatic shielding, smaller nuclei are formed, requiring
more energy to form high-surface-area Li deposits. This is in
good agreement with the SEM images in Figure 6b, where
smaller nuclei are observed on the surface of the cycled Li
metal.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we report a positively charged amino acid, L-
arginine (Arg), as an effective electrolyte additive for stabilizing
both electrodes in the Li−S system. On the S cathode, Arg
immobilizes soluble polysulfides by precipitating them from
the electrolyte, thereby combating the shuttle effect and
mitigating sulfur losses. On the Li anode, Arg adsorbs onto Li
nuclei via its positive charge, shields against uncontrolled Li+
deposition by redistributing the Li+ flux, and ultimately
protects against dendrite growth, electrolyte degradation, and
dead Li formation. The mechanisms of the Arg additive enable
improved electrochemical performance, enabling Li||Li cells to
operate with reduced overpotential and stable cycling for over
500 h. More significantly, Li−S cells equipped with the Arg-
modified electrolyte achieve higher capacity retention rates
(65% vs 50% for the blank electrolyte) after 300 cycles at 0.2
A/g. By demonstrating the dual-role efficacy of Arg, this study
paves the way for biomolecule-inspired design principles in
next-generation energy storage systems.
