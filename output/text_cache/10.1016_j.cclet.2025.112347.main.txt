Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered one of the most promising candidates for next-generation energy storage owing to 
their ultrahigh theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh/kg [1–5]. Typical Li–S batteries comprise Li metal anodes, sulfur cathodes, and 
ether-based electrolyte, operating via a dissolution–precipitation mechanism [6–12]. During discharge, the sulfur cathode produces 
soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), which are subsequently reduced to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S [13–17]. As intrinsic intermediates, the 
LiPSs lower the energy barrier of direct solid–solid conversion between elemental sulfur and Li2S, thereby ensuring high specific 
capacity and reversibility of sulfur cathodes [18–22]. Presently, practical Li–S pouch cells have achieved actual energy densities at 700 
Wh/kg level [23]. 
Despite this promise, the instability of Li metal anodes critically limits the cycling lifespan of Li–S batteries [24–27], where the 
inevitable corrosion reaction between LiPSs and Li metal serves as the main cause [28–32]. The LiPSs generated at the cathode side 
dissolve in electrolyte and diffuse to the anode side under concentration gradients [33,34]. The high-active LiPSs undergo side reactions 
with Li metal, leading to severe Li metal anode corrosion [35–37]. The as-generated Li2S2/Li2S components in solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) fail to passivate Li metal anode, instead exacerbate the instability of SEI and lead to continuous Li metal anode 
corrosion under working conditions [38–40]. Consequently, non-uniform Li plating/stripping is deteriorated and active Li depletion is 
accelerated, ultimately resulting in rapid failure of Li metal anode in Li–S batteries [41,42].

Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are widely regarded as promising next-generation high-energy-
density energy storage systems. However, their long-term cycling stability is severely restricted 
by lithium polysulfide (LiPS) corrosion on lithium metal anode. Herein, we report a strategy of 
fluorinating solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) to effectively mitigate LiPS-induced corrosion. 
Concretely, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane functions as an anion receptor to promote the 
decomposition of lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide and form a fluorinated SEI. The fluorinated
SEI effectively reduces the shuttle current by 42 % and enhances the durability of lithium metal 
anode by 45 %. Consequently, Li–S coin cells with 50 μm ultra-thin lithium anodes and 4.0 
mg/cm2 high-loading sulfur cathodes achieve promoted cycling lifespan from 81 cycles to 150 
cycles, and pouch cell delivers high initial energy density of 394 Wh/kg with stable operation for 
22 cycles. This study affords an effective method of fluorinating SEI to suppress LiPS corrosion 
and inspires rational interphase engineering for long-cycling Li–S batteries.

Constructing a stable SEI on Li metal anodes is thus an effective strategy to inhibit LiPS corrosion and enhance Li metal anode 
stability [43,44]. LiF is widely recognized as a crucial SEI component for restraining parasitic reactions and enhancing Li metal anode 
stability [45–48]. Tremendous efforts have been proposed to construct LiF-rich SEI on Li metal anodes [49,50], ranging from 
introducing fluorinated additives [51], constructing LiF-rich artificial SEI [52], designing fluorine-containing skeleton [53], and 
employing localized high-concentration electrolytes [54]. For instance, Huang and coworkers demonstrated significantly improved LiF 
content by using localized high-concentration electrolytes, thus promoted cycling stability of lithium metal anodes [55]. The enhanced 
reduction of lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) generated LiF-rich SEI, enabling a 440 Wh/kg pouch cell with 130 stable cycles. 
However, such localized high-concentration electrolytes always result in sluggish cathode kinetics and aggravated polarization [56,57]. 
Therefore, how to construct fluorinated SEI without sacrificing cathode performances remains a formidable challenge in Li–S batteries. 
Herein, we propose a strategy to promote fluorination of SEI and thereby enhance the stability of Li metal anodes in working Li–S 
batteries. Concretely, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (TPFPB) is selected as a Lewis acidic anion receptor to directly interact with LiFSI. 
This tailored interaction promotes the reduction of LiFSI by facilitating S–F bond cleavage, forming a fluorinated SEI on Li metal 
anodes. This rationally designed fluorinated SEI effectively mitigates the corrosion of Li metal anode by LiPSs, reducing the shuttle 
current by 42 % and promoting the operation durability of Li|Li symmetric cells by 45 % at 1.0 mA/cm2 and 4.0 mAh/cm2. 
Consequently, Li–S coin cells using 50 μm ultrathin Li metal anodes and 4.0 mg/cm2 high-loading sulfur cathodes achieve nearly 
doubled cycling lifespan by using the SEI fluorination strategy. Moreover, a 3 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell with the fluorinated SEI 
delivers high initial energy density of 394 Wh/kg and prolonged cycling lifespan of 22 cycles. 
To construct fluorinated SEI on the Li metal anode, LiFSI is selected as the fluorine source. Generally, high-concentration 
electrolytes are used to facilitate LiFSI decomposition via forming aggregates, thereby enriching fluorine in SEI [58,59]. However, such 
high-concentration electrolytes are incompatible with Li–S batteries due to reduced LiPS solubility, exacerbated sulfur redox kinetics, 
and limited sulfur utilization with low discharge capacity and cell energy density [60,61]. To address this limitation, 
tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (TPFPB) is introduced as a Lewis acidic anion receptor to interact with FSI− anions. Such Lewis acid–
base interaction facilitates the decomposition of LiFSI even under low salt concentration. This design allows fluorination of the SEI and 
simultaneously maintains electrolyte compatibility with the sulfur cathode in Li–S batteries. 
The interaction between LiFSI and TPFPB is first verified both theoretically and experimentally. Density functional theory (DFT) 
calculations reveal differences in reduction stability of LiFSI before and after interaction with TPFPB. The S–F bond energy in LiFSI 
decreases from 3.34 eV to 1.14 eV, and the bond length increases from 1.68 Å to 2.13 Å (Fig. 1a, Figs. S1 and S2 in Supporting 
information). Furthermore, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level is decreased from −0.16 eV in LiFSI alone to 
−2.75 eV in LiFSI–TPFPB complex (Fig. 1b). These results indicate that TPFPB facilitates S–F bond cleavage, thereby accelerating 
LiFSI decomposition and enabling fluorinated SEI formation. 
Experimentally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is employed to analyze the SEI composition. Li–S coin cells employing 
routine electrolyte (1.0 mol/L lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) and 2.0 wt% LiNO3 in mixed 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) at the volume ratio of 1:1) supplemented with 0.20 mol/L LiFSI and 4.0 mmol L−1 TPFPB (LiFSI + 
TPFPB electrolyte) were compared with the electrolyte with only LiFSI but without TPFPB (LiFSI electrolyte). After 5 cycles, the cells 
were disassembled and the Li metal anodes were analyzed. The SEI generated in LiFSI + TPFPB electrolyte exhibits significantly 
higher fluorine content, specifically 2.96 % at the surface and 3.83 % at 60 s sputtering time, over the SEI formed in LiFSI electrolyte 
(Fig. 1c). This is further confirmed by substantially enhanced LiF signals in F 1s XPS spectra (Figs. S3–S6 in Supporting information). 
To sum up, the interaction between TPFPB and LiFSI effectively promotes LiFSI decomposition and generates a fluorinated SEI that is 
expected to mitigate LiPS corrosion and enhance Li metal anode stability in Li–S batteries (Fig. 1d and Fig. S7 in Supporting 
information).

The protective effect of fluorinated SEI against LiPS corrosion is quantified by shuttle current measurements. The SEIs formed in the 
routine electrolyte and LiFSI + TPFPB electrolyte are named R-SEI and F-SEI, respectively. In Li–S coin cells without LiNO3, the 
shuttle current in the cells with F-SEI is substantially reduced compared with that with R-SEI. Within the voltage range from 2.20 V to 
2.90 V, the maximum shuttle current decreases from 0.097 mA with R-SEI to only 0.056 mA with F-SEI corresponding to a 42 % 
reduction (Fig. 2a, Figs. S8 and S9 in Supporting information). This indicates that F-SEI effectively suppresses LiPS shuttling in Li–S 
batteries. 
Further evaluation on Li metal anode stability is performed in Li|Li symmetric cells containing 1.0 molS/L Li2S8. At the current 
density of 1.0 mA/cm2 and the areal capacity of 1.0 mAh/cm2, the cells with F-SEI exhibit stable cycling for 308 h while the cells with 
R-SEI cycle for only 246 h (Fig. S10 in Supporting information). Despite similar initial voltage polarization values (0.027 V for F-SEI 
and 0.030 V for R-SEI), the cells with R-SEI suffer a sharp rise in polarization after 220 h to nearly 0.20 V, whereas the cells with F-
SEI maintain stable with minimal polarization increase to only 0.035 V (Figs. S11 and S12 in Supporting information). Cycled at higher 
areal capacity of 4.0 mAh/cm2, the cycling lifespan of the cells with F-SEI extends to 516 h compared with 356 h of the cells with R-
SEI corresponding to a 45 % increase in anode durability (Fig. 2b). The voltage polarization of the cells with F-SEI is also lower and 
more stable than that with R-SEI (Figs. S13 and S14 in Supporting information). The above results confirm that the Li metal anode with 
F-SEI is more stable attributed to inhibited LiPS corrosion. 
The morphology of cycled Li metal anodes is investigated after five cycles in Li|Li symmetric cells at 1.0 mA/cm2 and 4.0 mAh/cm2. 
Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images reveal that F-SEI endows dense and smooth plated Li (marked in pink, Fig. 2c), whereas 
R-SEI results in porous and loose Li deposition with substantial inactive Li accumulation (marked in blue, Fig. 2d). Cross-section SEM 
images confirm denser Li deposition in F-SEI than in R-SEI with a deposition thickness of 37 μm against 41 μm (Figs. S15 and S16 in 
Supporting information). The sulfur content was additionally evaluated by XPS to verify LiPS corrosion inhibition. The F-SEI exhibits 
a sulfur content of 7.64 % after 60 s sputtering, which is 18.9 % lower than that of R-SEI (Fig. 2e). These comprehensive results 
demonstrate that F-SEI inhibits LiPS corrosion on Li metal anode and facilitates homogeneous and compact Li deposition, both 
favorable for improving Li metal anode stability in working Li–S batteries.

Full-cell performances were evaluated in Li–S coin cells employing high-areal-loading sulfur cathodes (4.0 mgS/cm2) and ultrathin Li 
anodes (50 μm). As expected, the Li–S coin cells with F-SEI exhibit prolonged cycling lifespan of 150 cycles at 0.1 C (1 C = 1672 
mA/g) in comparison with only 81 cycles with R-SEI (Fig. 3a). The specific discharge capacity and charge/discharge polarization are 
similar at the 10th cycle (Fig. 3b). However, the cells with F-SEI maintain a stable second discharge plateau voltage at 2.07 V after 80 
cycles while the cells with R-SEI suffer severe polarization characterized by a sloped second discharge plateau. The polarization 
advantage of F-SEI is more pronounced upon cycling, where the accumulated polarization leads to premature cell failure of the cells 
with R-SEI (Figs. S17 and S18 in Supporting information). Such cycling lifespan contrast highlights fluorinated SEI in suppressing 
LiPS corrosion and promoting Li metal anode stability in working Li–S batteries. Notably, neither LiFSI nor TPFPB alone is able to 
promote the Li metal anode cycling stability (Figs. S19–S22 in Supporting information). The above cell performances again confirm 
that the interaction between LiFSI and TPFPB is crucial for SEI fluorination and Li metal anode protection. 
The cycled Li metal anodes were further evaluated in morphology and surface sulfur content. Li deposition in the cells with F-SEI is 
flatter and more homogeneous than in the cells with R-SEI after 5 cycles (Figs. S23 and S24 in Supporting information). Similarly, 
dense and homogeneous Li deposition is observed in the cells with F-SEI after 70 cycles (Fig. 3c and Fig. S25 in Supporting 
information), whereas dendritic Li deposition and huge cavities are observed in the cells with R-SEI (Fig. 3d and Fig. S26 in Supporting 
information). Cross-section SEM images exhibit similar plated Li thickness of 30 μm in F-SEI and 32 μm in R-SEI (Figs. 3e and f). 
However, inactive Li accumulation is markedly reduced in F-SEI (26 μm in thickness) compared with R-SEI (52 μm in thickness). 
Moreover, the sulfur content of F-SEI is only 4.76 % and significantly lower than that of R-SEI of 8.73 % (Fig. S27 in Supporting 
information). The tendency persists throughout sputtering (sulfur content of 5.94 % and 8.68 % for F-SEI and R-SEI after 60 s 
sputtering, respectively). These results corroborate that F-SEI inhibits LiPS corrosion, improves uniform Li deposition, and reduces 
inactive Li formation, contributing to stabilized Li metal anode and improved cycling lifespan of Li–S batteries.

The protective effect of F-SEI is further evaluated in practical Ah-level Li–S pouch cells (Fig. 4a). The average areal sulfur loading is 
7.4 mgS/cm2 and the Li anode thickness is 75 μm on a single side (Fig. 4b). The electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio is controlled at 3.0 
gele/gS (Table S1 in Supporting information). The Li–S pouch cell delivers an initial specific discharge capacity of 1207 mAh/g, 
corresponding to an energy density of 394 Wh/kg based on the total mass including cell package. The pouch cell undergoes stable 22 
cycles at 0.05 C with a capacity retention of 72.5 % (Figs. 4c and d), confirming the practical applicability of F-SEI in inhibiting LiPS 
corrosion and promoting Li metal anode stability in high-energy-density Li–S batteries (Table S2 in Supporting information). 
 
Fig. 4. Performance of practical Li–S pouch cell with the F-SEI. (a) Optical image and (b) design parameters of the Li–S pouch cell. (c) Cycling performance of 
the Li–S pouch cell. (d) Voltage–specific capacity profiles of the Li–S pouch cell. 
 
In conclusion, this work demonstrates a strategy of promoting SEI fluorination through TPFPB-mediated LiFSI decomposition to 
stabilize Li metal anodes in Li–S batteries. By introducing Lewis acid–base interactions between TPFPB and LiFSI, the S–F bond of 
LiFSI becomes more susceptible to cleavage, thereby facilitating the fluorination of SEI. The as-constructed F-SEI effectively mitigates 
LiPS corrosion, improves homogeneous Li deposition, and inhibits inactive Li accumulation. As a result, Li–S coin cells with F-SEI 
deliver 150 stable cycles at 0.1 C whereas the control cells can only operate for 81 cycles. This fluorinated SEI is also effective in Ah-
level pouch cells that sustain 22 cycles with an initial energy density of 394 Wh/kg. This work establishes a molecular design 
framework to achieve targeted SEI fluorination in Li–S battery systems, overcoming intrinsic challenges of Li metal anode protection 
without harming the sulfur cathode kinetics. The SEI fluorination strategy is expected to pave the way for realization of high-energy-
density and long-cycling Li–S batteries.
