Suppressing Shuttle Eﬀect via Cobalt Phthalocyanine
Mediated Dissociation of Lithium Polysulﬁdes for Enhanced
Li-S Battery Performance
Wei Zhang, Jiawen Zhu, Yadong Ye, Jiaxuan She, Xianghua Kong, Song Jin,*
Zhangquan Peng,* and Hengxing Ji*
Cationic lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) within ether-based electrolytes are
essential intermediates pivotal in instigating the shuttle eﬀect. Suppressing
the generation of cationic LiPSs is crucial for enhancing the performance of
high-energy-density Li-S batteries in practical applications. Herein, for the ﬁrst
time, it is demonstrated that cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc) can signiﬁcantly
enhance the dissociation of LiPSs, leading to a decrease in cationic LiPSs
species within the electrolyte. Employing NMR and in-situ Raman
spectroscopy, it is observed that CoPc interacts with sulfur anions within
LiPSs, modifying the dissociation equilibrium of LiPSs in 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) electrolyte. This CoPc-mediated shift toward
dissociation equilibrium reduces the concentration of cationic LiPSs,
eﬀectively suppressing the electromigration of LiPSs intermediates toward the
Li anode during the charging process of Li-S battery. As a result, the Li-S
battery enabled a high reversible areal capacity of 4.2 mAh cm−2 at a high
sulfur loading of 5.0 mg cm−2 and a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 3 μL
mgs
−1. This work aﬀords a promising strategy for tackling the fundamental
challenges regarding Li-S batteries.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries hold sub-
stantial promise for next-generation energy
storage devices due to their inherent ad-
vantages of high theoretical energy den-
sity of 2600 Wh kg−1, cost-eﬀectiveness,
and environmental friendliness. The opera-
tional mechanism of Li-S batteries involves
the interconversion between elemental S8
and Li2S, generating a range of lithium
polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) intermediates (Li2Sx, x
= 4-8) that exhibit solubility in the elec-
trolyte. Nevertheless, during the charging
process, the migration of dissolved LiPSs
from the sulfur cathode to the lithium
anode, driven by concentration gradients,
leads to chemical reduction on the Li metal.
This phenomenon, commonly referred to
as the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect, signiﬁ-
cantly contributes to a reduction in capac-
ity and ultimately premature failure of Li-
S batteries.[1,2] To address the shuttle ef-
fect, diverse strategies have been proposed,
including the use of porous carbon ma-
trices to absorb or immobilize LiPSs,[3–6]
tailoring electrolytes to decrease the solubility or mobility of
LiPSs,[7,8] designing interlayers to impede LiPSs diﬀusion,[9] and
employing catalysts to facilitate LiPSs conversion.[10–14] Although
these strategies have achieved notable advancements in partially
mitigating the polysulﬁde shuttle, the fundamental nature of the
shuttle eﬀect in the electrolyte persists. Soluble LiPSs in the elec-
trolyte can still readily accumulate to a signiﬁcant extent after a
few cycles of battery operation, triggering shuttle eﬀect.
Typically, Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, 8) exists as a neutral molecule across
a broad concentration in ether-based solvents.[15] Upon the in-
troduction of a supporting salt such as LiTFSI, the dissolved
LiPSs undergo dissociation and association reactions. Neverthe-
less, LiPSs display a pronounced aﬃnity for bonding an addi-
tional Li+ and forming a substantial amount of cationic LiPSs–
Li+ in the electrolyte.[15] However, cationic LiPSs face challenges
in reaching the cathode during the charging process, as they are
more likely to be repelled by the applied electric ﬁeld and not
easy to reach the Helmholtz plane of the sulfur cathode, which
hinders their participation in electrochemical reactions.[16] On
the anode side, electrostatic attraction of the negatively charged

from the lithium metal anode surface. As a result, these freed
ions can eﬃciently adsorb onto the cathode side under the inﬂu-
ence of an electric ﬁeld during charging, oﬀering a fundamental
means to mitigate the shuttle eﬀect. Co phthalocyanine (CoPc)
exhibits a distinctive 2D and single-molecular structure featuring
highly active Co-N4 centers, which have been shown to enhance
the kinetics of sulfur redox reactions (SRR) in both liquid and
liquid-solid processes.[18–21] For example, Hu et al. demonstrated
that Co tetranitrophthalocyanine (CoTnPc) weakens the bridging
S-S bonds in Li2S4 through increased electron donation from the
S 3pz orbital to the singly occupied Co 3dz
2 orbital, facilitating
interaction with Li2S4.[20] Wang et al. found that CoPc molecules
eﬀectively lower the energy barrier associated with the reduction
of Li2S4 to solid Li5S3, a pivotal step which governs the overall
battery reaction. To delve deeper into the role of CoPc in enhanc-
ing the kinetics of sulfur cathodic reactions, we selected it as a
model material for our investigation.[21]
In this study, we unveil a novel role of cobalt phthalocyanine
(CoPc) in facilitating the dissociation and solvation of LiPSs, lead-
ing to a reduction in the concentration of cationic LiPS species
in ether-based electrolytes. Utilizing NMR and in-situ Raman
spectra, we demonstrate a signiﬁcant interaction between CoPc
and sulfur anions within LiPSs, which modiﬁes the dissociation
equilibrium of LiPSs. This CoPc-mediated adjustment toward
dissociation equilibrium eﬀectively reduces the concentration of
cationic LiPSs, thereby mitigating the migration of LiPSs inter-
mediates toward the lithium anode during the charging phase of
Li-S batteries. Consequently, the Li-S cell exhibits an impressive
reversible areal capacity of 4.2 mAh cm−2 at a high sulfur loading
of 5.0 mg cm−2, coupled with an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1.
2. Results and Discussion

role as an intermediate product in liquid-liquid phase transitions
within Li-S batteries. This compound signiﬁcantly contributes
to the shuttling eﬀect owing to its high solubility and energeti-
cally favourable accumulation in the electrolyte.[22] A comprehen-
sive summary of all electrolyte components used here and subse-
quent experiments is provided in Table S1 (Supporting Informa-
tion). As depicted in Figure 1a, Li2S6 displays a V-shaped proﬁle,
with the minimum molar conductivity of 2.3 mS cm−1 M−1 ob-
served at a concentration of 10 mm. In the high-concentration
range of 40–400 mm, an abnormal increase in the molar conduc-
tivity of Li2S6 is observed. This phenomenon can be attributed to

To further investigate the solvation dynamics of Li2S6 in ether-
based electrolyte solutions upon the addition of CoPc, we utilized
7Li NMR spectroscopy to probe the chemical environment of Li+
within dissolved Li2S6. Utilizing LiClO4, a strongly dissociating
inorganic salt, as a reference sample, we determined the chem-
ical shift of fully dissociated Li+ in a DOL/DME 1:1 (v/v) mixed
solvent. As depicted in Figure 1b,c, the NMR spectrum reveals
negligible ﬂuctuations in the 7Li chemical shift across the LiClO4
concentration range of 8 mm to 400 mm, suggesting the complete
dissociation of Li+ and ClO4

LiClO4, the downﬁeld shift is observed in the 7Li peak of Li2S6 in
DOL/DME, indicating a reduced electron density surrounding
the Li+. Additionally, the chemical shift of the 7Li NMR spectrum
exhibited an incremental trend with increasing concentration of
Li2S6. When the concentration of LiPSs exceeded 40 mm, the 7Li
chemical shift gradually stabilized and remained constant, indi-
cating the challenge in solvation of Li2S6 in DOL/DME. However,
upon the introduction of CoPc into the DOL/DME electrolyte, the
7Li NMR spectra exhibited a downﬁeld shift across almost all the
concentrations in comparison to that of the pristine Li2S6 sam-
ples (Figure 1c; Figure S2, Supporting Information). This shift
demonstrated that CoPc can promote the solvation of Li+ in Li2S6,
indicating a substantial increase in the solvation of Li2S6 with the
inclusion of CoPc, which is consistent with Molar conducting re-
sults.

Considering that the shuttle eﬀect between cationic LiPSs and
Li metal anodes causes the loss of active material and corrosion
of the Li anode, we employed the shuttle current as a quan-
titative indicator to reﬂect the reaction rate between dissolved
LiPSs in the electrolyte and lithium metal anodes.[26,27] The shut-
tle current is linked to the oxidation of polysulﬁde species at the
cathode, while a corresponding reduction reaction occurs at the
anode due to the shuttle process. For the shuttle current mea-
surements, a sulfur/graphene composite was employed as the
cathode, and Li metal foil was employed as the anode. 1.0 M
LiTFSI and 2 wt% LiNO3 in DOL/DME solvent with or without
1 mg mL−1 CoPc was employed as electrolyte. As illustrated in
Figure 4a, the addition of CoPc results in a shuttle current of
1.1 μA, far less than that of the blank electrolyte with a shuttle
current of 7.4 μA. This signiﬁcant decrease in shuttle current
with the incorporation of CoPc implies that the reduction of poly-
sulﬁde species at the lithium metal anode is impeded, highlight-
ing the eﬀectiveness of CoPc in suppressing the shuttling pro-
cess. Soluble polysulﬁdes diﬀuse toward the anode side, where
they react with lithium metal to produce insoluble and insulat-
ing Li2S2/Li2S, leading to increased interfacial resistance. Thus,
symmetric Li metal cells were assembled to evaluate the inﬂu-
ence of the CoPc additive on the electrochemical performance of
the Li metal anode. To highlight the dissociation eﬀect of Li2S6,
0.1 M Li2S6 accompanied by 0.1 M LiTFSI in a DOL/DME sol-
vent blend were utilized as the electrolyte, and the electrolytes
with and without CoPc were both tested. As shown in Figure 4b,
the cell incorporating CoPc as an additive demonstrated a con-
sistently low potential, maintaining stability at ≈30 mV at a cur-
rent density of 2 mA cm–2 and an areal capacity of 2 mAh cm–2
for 100 hours. In contrast, the blank cell exhibits voltage ﬂuctu-
ations, accompanied by a higher overpotential exceeding 65 mV.
The cycling performance of Li║Li symmetric cells using 0.1 M
Li2S6 and 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME solvent were also evaluated
(Figure S9, Supporting Information). Initially, both cells with and
without CoPc demonstrated comparable voltage polarizations of
≈30 mV over the initial 70-hour period. However, after that, the
Li║Li symmetric cell without CoPc experienced a rapid escalation
in voltage polarization, reaching 150 mV, while the cell with CoPc
maintained a consistently low and stable voltage polarization of
20 mV even after 100 h. This was further conﬁrmed by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) images, which revealed a ﬂat surface
without mossy deposition or dendrite formation on the cycled Li
metal with the CoPc additive (Figure 4c). In comparison, moss-
like dendrites are observed on the reference Li metal cycled with-
out CoPc as shown in Figure 4d. We used X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) to determine the surface chemistry content
of the deposition side Li foil after the Li║Li symmetric cells were
cycled 50 times. As shown in Figure 4e, the S 2p XPS spectrum
of the cycled Li foil with the CoPc additive exclusively exhibited
a TFSI−doublet peak at 169.4 and 170.4 eV, along with a C–SOx
doublet peak at 167.0 and 168.0 eV originating from the decom-
position of TFSI−. No additional sulfur-related species are dis-
cerned. In contrast, additional peaks are evident at 161.6, 163.3,
162.6, and 164.3 eV in the S 2p spectra of the cycled lithium foil
with the bank electrolyte in Figure 4f, which can be ascribed to
Li2S2, and Li2S, respectively.[28] Therefore, it can be inferred that
the incorporation of CoPc facilitates the generation of polysulﬁde
anions. These anions are prone to be repelled by lithium cath-
ode in Li║Li symmetric cells, increasing the diﬃculty of access-
ing the Helmholtz plane and engaging in electrochemical reac-
tions. Conversely, while sulfur anions migrate to the lithium an-
ode and may oxidize to elemental sulfur during discharge, they
can be reversibly reduced to soluble lithium polysulﬁdes due to
their higher potential in competition with lithium ions for a sub-
sequent reduction stage, as evidenced by the absence of S8 in the
lithium surface XPS analyses.

duced capacity and high polarization, particularly under high-rate
conditions (Figure 5b). Notably, the second discharge plateau ob-
served in the coin cell incorporating CoPc exhibits signiﬁcantly
prolonged duration compared to that of the battery without CoPc,
suggesting an enhanced sulfur utilization and conversion rate
during the interconversion from Li2S4 to Li2S. Figure 5c illus-
trates the long cycling performance of coin cells with and without
CoPc. Initially, both batteries exhibited similar discharge capac-
ities of 983 and 1015 mAh g−1. However, the CoPc-free battery
experienced a sharp and continuous capacity loss, decreasing to
456 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles. Conversely, the CoPc-containing
battery maintained a preserved capacity of 797 mAh−1 after 500
cycles, demonstrating an extraordinary capacity decay rate of 0.04
% per cycle. This stands in stark contrast to the 0.18 % per cycle
observed for the CoPc-free Li-S coin cell. Remarkably, the battery
containing CoPc in the electrolyte exhibited a higher coulombic
eﬃciency (more than 99 %) compared to the CoPc-free battery
(97-98 %), indicative of reduced LiPSs shuttle. A low electrolyte-
to-sulfur (E/S) ratio (≤5 μL mgs
−1) is crucial for cost reduction
and the practical viability of high-energy-density Li-S batteries.
However, this low E/S ratio exacerbates concentration gradients
and intensiﬁes the back-and-forth migration, along with the par-
asitic issues linked to the shuttle eﬀect. Thus, to further illus-
trate CoPc’s eﬀectiveness in inhibiting the shuttle eﬀect, we con-
structed Li-S batteries with high sulfur loading and low E/S ratios
(Figure 5d). At an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1 and sulfur loadings of
5.0 and 3.5 mg cm−2, these batteries exhibited high initial capaci-
ties of 4.2 and 3.6 mAh cm−2, respectively. Moreover, they demon-
strated decent capacity retentions of 3.6 and 2.9 mAh cm−2 after
50 cycles at 0.2 C. To substantiate the practical applicability of
CoPc, we assembled and assessed Li-S pouch cells with a sulfur
loading of 2.0 mg cm−2 and a low E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1 as shown
in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). The CoPc-containing
battery exhibited an initial discharge capacity of 1223 mAh g−1 at
0.2 C and maintained a discharge capacity of 855 mAh g−1 after
100 cycles. In contrast, the Li–S pouch cell without CoPc showed
a much lower discharge capacity of 424 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles.
This comprehensive evaluation of the full-cell performance in-
disputably underscores the pivotal role of CoPc in enhancing the
electrochemical performance of high-sulfur-loaded Li-S batteries
operating in low electrolyte conditions.
3. Conclusion
In conclusion, our study highlights the eﬀectiveness of CoPc
in enhancing the dissociation of LiPSs within ether-based elec-
trolytes, thereby suppressing the harmful LiPSs shuttle eﬀect
