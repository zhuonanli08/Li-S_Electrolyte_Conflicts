A Multifunctional Additive for Long-Cycling Lithium-Sulfur
Batteries Under Lean-Ether-Electrolyte Conditions
Fengyi Yang, Xiaoqun Qi, Ruining Jiang, Xiaoyu Jin, Yan Li, Fei Zhou, Jie Ji,
Renyuan Zhang,* Pei Hu, and Long Qie*
The failure of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries operating under lean-ether-
electrolyte conditions can be ascribed to the deterioration of electrolytes,
the passivation of cathodes, and the degeneration of lithium anodes. Eﬀorts
to address these challenges by exploring alternative electrolytes beyond
commonly used ether-based systems often introduce new complications.
In this study, the utilization of a multifunctional additive, 2-Bromoacetamide
(BA), for ether electrolytes is proposed to achieve stable Li-S batteries
under lean-electrolyte conditions. These investigations reveal that the
amide bond in BA forms hydrogen bonds with sulfur atoms, promoting the
dissolution of lithium sulﬁdes (Li2S) in ether electrolytes and mediating the
conversion of lithium polysulﬁdes. This enhanced solubility of Li2S facilitates
3D deposition (vertical to the substrate) rather than the typical 2D deposition
(parallel to the substrate) of Li2S, and thus alleviates the passivation of
cathodes. Furthermore, the incorporation of BA promotes uniform and dense
lithium deposition. The use of the BA-containing electrolyte enables stable
cycling of a Li-S pouch cell for 40 cycles, even under a low electrolyte-to-sulfur
ratio of 4 μL mg−1. This multifunctional strategy oﬀers an eﬀective solution
to extend the lifetime of Li-S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions,
thereby paving the way for practical applications of Li-S battery technology.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have at-
tracted
signiﬁcant
attention
due
to
their
high
theoretical
energy
density
(2600 Wh kg−1), well above the achievable
values (250 Wh kg−1) of the commer-
cial
lithium-ion
batteries.[1]
However,
the advantage in energy density of Li-S
batteries is not fully realized, primar-
ily due to the addition of excess elec-
trolytes. For example, with an electrolyte-
to-sulfur
(E/S)
ratio
of
10
μL
mg−1,
a value lower than the ones reported in
most literature, electrolyte accounts for
>50% of the total mass of the cell.[2]
Attempts to further reduce E/S ratio al-
ways lead to early cell failure, attributable
to the following reasons. First, the ac-
cumulation of dissolved lithium polysul-
ﬁdes (Li2S4–8, LPSs) degrades the wettabil-
ity and conductivity of the electrolyte, slow-
ing down the conversion between S and
Li2S under lean-electrolyte conditions;[3]
Second, cathode passivation occurs due to
the formation of thick and electronically
insulting layer of the discharge product;
Thirdly, undesirable side reactions between
the electrolytes and lithium (Li) anodes
would consume electrolytes. Nevertheless, to retrieve the advan-
tage of Li-S batteries in energy density (>500 Wh kg−1), it is im-
perative to decrease the E/S ratio to <3 μL mg−1, coupled with a
high areal sulfur loading of >5.0 mg cm−2.[4]
Two viable strategies have been developed to minimize elec-
trolyte dosage. One is to use high-concentration or locally high-
concentration electrolytes, which could suppress the dissolution
of LPSs and mitigate LPS-induced electrolyte deterioration.[5] In
our previous research, we successfully achieved a noteworthy re-
duction in the E/S ratio to 1 μL mg−1 by employing acetonitrile-
based weakly solvating electrolytes. However, the cell only sur-
vived for three cycles.[6] The other one is to use electrolytes em-
ploying high-donor-number solvents, where increase the solu-
bility of LPSs, thereby delaying their accumulation and the re-
sulting electrolyte deterioration. However, these solvents cause
severe side eﬀects on Li anodes, doing harm to the lifetime of
the batteries.[7] The most rational approach is to modify ether-
based electrolytes to realize the feasibility of lean-electrolyte Li-S
batteries.

Here, we introduce 2-Bromoacetamide (BA) into ether-based
electrolytes to achieve stable, long cycles under lean-electrolyte
conditions. The selection of BA is based on three advantageous
functions. First, the hydrogen bonds between the amide group
in BA and the sulfur (S) atom in lithium sulﬁdes (Li2S) promote
the dissolution of Li2S, preventing the formation of an insulat-
ing Li2S layer on the discharged cathode’s surface. Additionally,
BA could react with LPSs and thus facilitate the redox process.
Thirdly, BA promotes the dense and uniform lithium deposition
by forming a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer with a higher
Li3N content. The interaction between BA and Li2S also enhances
lithium compatibility. As a result, the electrolyte with BA enables
the Li-S battery to retain 93.6% of its initial discharge capacity
after 300 cycles at 0.2C. Even with a high areal-mass-loading S
cathode (6.0 mg cm−2) and an ultralow E/S ratio of 3 μL mg−1,
the designed electrolyte provides the cell with a high reversible
capacity of 931 mAh g−1. In pouch cells, the BA-containing elec-
trolyte ensures that the Li-S battery maintains excellent speciﬁc
capacity after 40 cycles under a low E/S ratio of 4 μL mg−1.These
results suggest the potential of electrolytes with BA additive for
application in commercial Li-S batteries, particularly those target-
ing high energy density.
2. Results and Discussion
With the addition of 0.1 M BA into the blank ether-based elec-
trolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME 1:1 (v/v) with 2 wt.% LiNO3),
the ionic conductivity of the electrolytes experiences a modest in-
crease from 11.18 to 11.37 mS cm−1 (Figure S1, Supporting Infor-
mation). The eﬀect of BA on the solvation is analyzed in Figure S2
(Supporting Information), three types of solvates are formed as
solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs), contact ion-pairs (CIPs,), or
ion aggregates (AGGs,). The increased CIPs reveal more anions
interact with Li+ ions in the primary solvation sheath of Li+ and
be reduced to form an anion-derived inorganic-rich solid elec-
trolyte interface (SEI).[8] To assess the impact of additives on the
solubility of Li2S, Li2S was dissolved in electrolytes with and with-
out BA, respectively. As the inset photography in Figure 1a, the
introduction of 0.1 M BA into the electrolyte results in the trans-
formation of the turbid solution to transparency, revealing a no-
ticeable increased solubility of Li2S (from 0 to 25 mmol L−1). The
enhanced Li2S solubility could be attributed to the newly formed
hydrogen bonding interaction between the amide bonds in BA
and Li2S, which could be conﬁrmed by 1H nuclear magnetic spec-
troscopy (NMR) and Raman spectra. As the results depicted in
Figure 1b,c, the appearance of the peak ≈1.75 ppm in the 1H
spectra and the peak at ≈2775 cm−1 in the Raman spectra after
the Li2S incorporation indicates the interaction between the H
atom in amide group and the S atom in Li2S.[9] Besides, the new
peak in the 7Li NMR spectra ≈0.2 ppm reveals the interaction be-
tween the Li+ ions in Li2S and C = O of BA (Figure S3, Support-
ing Information). The dissolution of Li2S could be attributed to
both strong interactions between S2−and the amide hydrogen (S-
H), and between Li+ and carbonyl oxygen atom (Li-O) in BA. The
inﬂuence of the additive on the redox process of LPSs is also in-
vestigated. The photography of Li2S6 dissolving in the electrolytes

The interaction between LPSs and electrolytes was further in-
vestigated with phase diagram to simulate the situation in the
electrolytes (Figure S6, Supporting Information). With the same
E/S ratio, the amount of the Li2S and S is normalized, then the
curve could be divided into three part according to the discharge
proﬁle: i) the part of the two-phase area (solid S and solution)
corresponding to the upper plateau in the discharge process, ii)
the part of the single-phase area corresponding to the slope in
the discharge process, iii) the part of the two-phase area (solid
Li2S and solution) corresponding to the lower plateau in the dis-
charge process (Figure S7, Supporting Information). At the end
of the part representing the upper plateau, as shown in Figure 1f,
the dot in the electrolyte with BA contains more S than the one in
the blank electrolyte, conﬁrming the higher conversion eﬃciency
of active material after the BA addition. The interaction between
BA and LPSs promotes the generation and dissolution of LPSs,
making the S species fully reduced and boosting the utilization
of the active materials.
The liquid-solid conversion from LiPSs to Li2S was also investi-
gated with Li2S deposition tests (Figure 2a,b) and the Li2S deposi-
tion capacity was calculated using the electrodepositing potentio-
static proﬁles based on the Faraday’s law.[13] The current drop is
due to the conversion from long-chain LPSs to short-chain LPSs,
and the capacity marked by dark color is due to the deposition of
Li2S. As shown in the UV–vis absorption spectra (Figure S8, Sup-
porting Information), the peak of Li2S8 in the electrolyte in cycled
cell almost disappears, indicating the fully reaction of Li2S8. As
the calculated results shown Figure 2c, the deposition capacity of
Li2S increases from 86 to 191 mAh g−1 after the incorporation
of the BA additive, indicating the increased conversion degree
from LiPSs to Li2S. Besides, the faster (from 1708 to 1136 s) and
sharper peak (from 0.75 to 1.06 mA mg−1) after the BA addition
reveal the accelerated deposition kinetics and enhanced nucle-
ation density of the Li2S during the electrodeposition process,[14]
which contribute to the lower polarization and higher reversible
capacity, To further conﬁrm the acceleration of BA on the depo-
sition of Li2S, we operated the ex situ XRD of the cathode dis-
charged to 2.2 V, where the polysulﬁdes start to be reduced as
Li2S. As shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), the addi-
tion of BA promotes the generation of Li2S, while the cell with
electrolyte without BA does not form the Li2S at 2.2V. The X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) results conﬁrm the BA accelerates the genera-
tion of Li2S, which is consistent with the results of the potentio-
static discharge process.
The current-time curves based on the deposition proﬁles are
further calculated. As the results drawn in Figure 2d, the ﬁt curve
of the electrolyte without BA additive is similar to the 2D pro-
gressive nucleation (2DP) model while the one with BA additive
is similar to the 3D instantaneous(3DI) nucleation, indicating
the transformation of the Li2S deposition mechanism from 2D
deposition model to 3D deposition model after the addition of
the BA additive in the electrolytes.[15] The changes on the de-
position model could also be evidenced by the diﬀerent mor-
phologies of the cycled carbon ﬁber (Figure 3e,f). Compared with
the SEM image of pristine carbon ﬁber (Figure S10, Support-
ing Information), the deposition of Li2S in electrolyte without
BA additive in the form of plain plate growth, while the one in
electrolyte with BA additive in the form of ﬂaky vertical growth.
Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted to identify
the distribution of the discharge product Li2S on the deposited
CNT paper. As shown in Figure S11 (Supporting Information),
the ﬂower-like ﬂaky plate is consistent with the distribution of
the sulfur and the plain part is only composed with carbon el-
ement, proving that the vertical plate is Li2S. Combining the
morphology with the elemental distribution of the cycled carbon
ﬁber, it is obvious that the addition of the BA additive encour-
ages the ﬂaky vertical growth of the Li2S and maintains more
blank carbon exposed, ensuring the fast and suﬃcient electron
transport.
The in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)
result is shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information), the
impedance of Li-S batteries decreases with the BA additive. The
increase of impedance during the discharge process (≈5 h in
Figure S12a, Supporting Information and ≈4 h in Figure S12b,
Supporting Information) is due to the deposition of solid dis-
charge product. With the discharge product transforming from
dissolved polysulﬁdes to solid-state product, the precipitation of
solid and insulated Li2S on the cathode enlarges the impedance
of the cell. The earlier changes on the impedance also proves
the acceleration of Li2S precipitation with the BA additive (Figure
S12c,d, Supporting Information). With time going, the cell with
BA additive maintains small and stable impedance while the
cell with blank electrolyte keeps enlarging. The stable and low
impedance indicates the BA additive eﬀectively prevents the for-
mation of an insulating Li2S layer and leads to lower polarization
of the precipitation process.

The cells using the electrolytes with and without BA additive
were also tested at diﬀerent current to compare their rate perfor-
mance (Figure 3d). The result shows the BA keeps high discharge
capacity of 646 mAh g−1 even at 3C and returns to 831 mAh g−1
when the speed adjusts to 0.2C again (Figure S14, Supporting In-
formation). The cell containing BA maintains higher capacity at
diﬀerent current density because the BA could catalyze the redox
process and push the thorough reaction. Besides, the CV experi-
ments with diﬀerent scan speeds were also conducted to discuss
the changes on the redox reactions (Figure S15, Supporting In-

the ﬁrst discharge peak and the two charge peaks remain stable,

speed limitation step.
The long-term cycling performances of cells using elec-
trolytes without and with BA additive were investigated at 0.2C
(Figure 3e). The cell using electrolyte with BA additive shows an
excellent cyclability, retaining a high reversible capacity of 869
mAh g−1 after 300 cycles, 93.6% of the initial discharge capacity,
corresponding to a low capacity fading rate of 0.021% per cycle.
In comparison, the cell in blank electrolyte suﬀers from rapid
capacity fading, delivering a capacity of 640 mAh g−1 after 300
cycles, only 73.4% of the initial discharge capacity. It is also no-
ticed that the coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of the cell with BA main-
tains >99%, while the cell with blank electrolytes decreases to
96% (Figure S16, Supporting Information), which may attribute
to the mitigation of the shuttle eﬀect bringing by the additive.
Figure S17 (Supporting Information) compares the discharge–
charge proﬁles of the Li-S cells at the 300th cycle, a polarization
of 166 mV was obtained with the BA addition, while the polar-
ization of cell in blank electrolytes became 183 mV. The Li-S cell
with BA as additive maintains stable cycles for 700 cycles accom-
panying with a low decay rate of 0.064%, while the one without
additive remains only 45.6% of the initial capacity after 700 cycles
(Figure S18, Supporting Information). The electrochemical im-
provements in cyclability, rate capability, and polarization could
be attributed to the catalyzation of the redox reaction and the mit-
igation of the shuttle eﬀect brought by BA additive. Besides, the
BA additive changes the solubility of the LPSs and Li2S, thus ac-
celerates the redox kinetics and mediates the reaction process.
To investigate the inﬂuences of additive on the deposition mor-
phology and the composition of the SEI of Li anodes, we com-

Li-S cells cycled in diﬀerent electrolytes with a scanning electron

ode cycled in blank electrolytes shows a porous and loose struc-

with the BA, the cycled Li anode deposited as dense and large
plates (Figure 4b). The results of the SEM conﬁrm the improve-
ment of the lithium metal compatibility with the electrolytes after
the BA addition, which also contributes to prolonging the lifetime
of Li-S cells.

These results conﬁrm that BA additive can eﬀectively improve the
reversibility and utilization of Li metal.
In conclusion, as shown in Figure 4e, the addition of the BA ad-
ditive increases the ratio of Li2S and Li3N in the SEI layer. Li2S im-
pedes the electron tunneling from the anode into the SEI, while
Li3N ensure the fast ionic conductivity, both contribute to uni-
form lithium deposition.
The superﬂuous electrolyte counteracts the upper hand of Li-
S batteries in energy density. For the practical application of Li-
S batteries, the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries
in electrolytes with/without BA additive with high loading cath-
odes under lean-electrolyte were compared. In Figure 5a, with
a high-loading S cathode of 5.0 mg cm−1 and an E/S ratio of
5 μL mg−1, the cell with BA additive shows a high reversible ca-
pacity of 1133 mAh g−1 and maintains stable reversible capacity
of 701 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. While the capacity of the cell
with blank electrolytes decreases quickly to 364 mAh g−1 after
100 cycles. As shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information),
the charge–discharge proﬁles and impedance of Li-S cell with
BA additive maintain stable and consistent with the cycles from
20th to 100th., indicating the eﬀect of BA on the electrochemical
performance is continuous and stable. Even under an ultralow
E/S ratio of 3 μL mg−1, the cell using electrolyte with BA addi-
tive works stably and maintains at 832 mAh g−1 at the 20th cy-
cle, corresponding to of 89% of the initial capacity (Figure 5b)
without obvious polarization increase (Figure S23, Supporting
Information), while the cell using blank electrolyte fails (inset of
Figure 5b).
Pouch cell was assembled to evaluate the practical potential of
the electrolyte with BA additive. As shown in Figure S24 (Sup-
porting Information), the rate performance of the Li-S pouch
cell was tested from 0.05C to 0.5C. The pouch cell with a high
loading of 3.7 mg cm−1 under the low E/S ratio of 6 μL mg−1
could maintain a high reversible capacity of 811 mAh g−1 at
0.2C, and stable capacity of 501 mAh g−1 at 0.5C. When the
current return to the 0.05C, the pouch cell capacity could re-
turn to 877 mAh g−1. Besides the great rate performance, the
cycle performance of the pouch cells was also investigated. A
pouch cell of a loading of 3.2 mg cm−1 with a low E/S ratio of
4 μL mg−1 exhibits a high reversible capacity of 805 mAh g−1
at the 20th cycles, and stable cycles for 40 cycles, which is bet-
ter than the performance of the reported ones,[20] demonstrat-
ing the great potential of the improved electrolytes for practical
applications.

3. Conclusion
In summary, by introducing BA additive into the ether electrolyte,
we successfully increase Li2S solubility, modify the redox process,
and regulate the anode deposition. On one hand, with the medi-
ated redox reaction, the Li-S batteries boost the speciﬁc capacity
and prolong the lifetime. On the other hand, the increased Li2S
solubility provides a new way of the Li2S transportation, allowing
the 3D deposition of the Li2S and thus catalyzing the precipita-
tion process. In addition, a robust and stable SEI layer rich in
Li3N is generated on the surface of Li anodes with the assistance
of the BA, guaranteeing the stable long-term cycling stability of
Li-S batteries. The proposed electrolyte could eﬃciently increase
the capacity and maintain the cycle stability even under harsh
condition with high loading and low E/S ratio, no matter in coin
cell or pouch cell. This work opens a new possibility for the devel-
opment of practical Li-S batteries with high energy density and
long-term stability.
