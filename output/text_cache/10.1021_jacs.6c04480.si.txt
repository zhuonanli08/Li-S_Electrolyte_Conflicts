For the preparation of the electrolytes, LiTFSI (lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide, 
Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.99%) and LiNO3 (lithium nitrate, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%) were 
dried at 120 °C under vacuum overnight. DME (1,2-dimethoxyethane, Sigma-Aldrich, 
anhydrous, 99.5%), DOL (1,3-dioxolane, Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.8%) and the 
fluorinated eithers were dried with 4 Å molecular sieves for three days (8–12 mesh bead size, 
Aldrich). The sieves were first dried under vacuum in a Büchi tube at 220 °C for 72 h. The tube 
was then sealed and transferred to a glovebox, where the sealed tube was left to cool overnight 
before the sieves were used. The salt concentrations in the electrolytes are reported as moles of 
salt per liter of solvent mixture. 
The preparation of the sulfur electrodes was done by following the procedure described 
previously.1 The electrodes were made of 65% S (sulfur, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.98%), 21% KB 
(Ketjenblack, EC-600JD, NanoGrafi), 3.5% C65 (Super C65, Imerys), 3.5% CNF (carbon 
nanofibers, 20–200 nm × 100 µm, Sigma-Aldrich), 5.6% PEO (poly(ethylene oxide, Mw 
∼4000,000, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1.4% PVP (polyvinylpyrrolidone, Mw ∼360,000, Sigma-
Aldrich), where the percentages are given by weight. The sulfur and carbon additives were first 
manually mixed with pestle and mortar and then heated in a porcelain crucible with a lid, 
wrapped in foil, to 155 °C for 1-2 h to melt infiltrate the sulfur into the carbon. The resulting 
sulfur-carbon composite was then mixed with the binders, and a viscous ink was made using 
ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ) with 7.5 wt.% ethanol (Fisher Scientific, 99.7%) as the solvent, 
using 3.3 mL of solvent per gram of solids. The inks were mixed with a Thinky mixer (ARE-
250) at 1300 rpm for 5 min, then at 1800 rpm for 5 min and then at 2000 rpm for 5 min, with

S1.2. Electrochemical testing and electrode characterization 
The assembly of the electrochemical cells and preparation of the electrolytes were done inside 
an argon-filled glovebox (Unilab, MBraun, H2O and O2 content <0.1 ppm).  
The electrochemical testing was done in CR2032 coin cells (Seika Sangyo GmbH Germany) 
using a HS-HCR2 hand-operated coin crimper (Seika Sangyo GmbH Germany). First, a 15 
mm-diameter lithium disc (battery grade, China Energy Lithium Co., 100 microns thick) was 
pressed into a 0.5mm coin cell spacer and placed inside the coin cell case with a coin cell gasket 
attached. A 16 mm- diameter Celgard 2400 separator was placed on top of the lithium disc, and 
then the separator was wet with an electrolyte volume equivalent to an electrolyte-to-sulfur 
ratio of 7 µL/mg. A 14-mm diameter sulfur electrode was then placed centrally on top of the 
separator followed by an additional 0.5 mm spacer and a square wave spring. The cell was then 
sealed with the coin cell cap and crimped. After assembly, cells were introduced into a climatic 
chamber (Memmert) set at 25 °C and rested for 2 hours at OCV, followed by C/5 cycling 
between 1.8 and 2.6 V. The specific capacities were normalized by the mass of sulfur in the 
sulfur electrode. 
Note that, due to the significant empty space volume inside coin cells (known as ‘dead 
volume’), it is not possible to achieve the low electrolyte loading achievable with pouch cells.2 
This is because, during coin cell assembly, the electrolyte fills the whole cell ‘dead volume’, 
so the actual volume of electrolyte inside the cell stack (comprising the electrodes and 
separator) is much lower than the total volume of electrolyte added to the cell. Here we employ 
a low volume of electrolyte (30-35 µL) that provides high cell-to-cell reproducibility, and it 
corresponds to an electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 7 µL/mg. that is among the smallest used in coin 
cells in the literature (see Tables S1-2).  
For the SEM characterization, the cycled cells were disassembled in the glovebox and the 
electrodes were rinsed with ~3 mL of DME to remove salt and polysulfide residues. The solvent 
was then allowed to dry off in the glovebox and the electrodes were stored face up in glass 
vials. The cycled electrodes were loaded into a SemiLab sample transfer suitcase and reopened 
under vacuum inside the transfer chamber of the SEM (Zeiss Sigma 500 VP FESEM). EDX 
measurements were done using an Oxford Instruments Ultim Extreme 100 mm2 windowless 
detector that enabled the detection of elemental lithium kα X-rays, SEM images show 
secondary electron micrographs and EDX data was obtained at a 5 kV acceleration voltage 
unless stated otherwise. 
For the ex-situ NMR characterization, thin lithium deposits were prepared by galvanostatic 
plating of lithium on copper current collectors in Li//Cu coin cells (304, Cambridge Energy 
Solutions) with Celgard 2325 separators at 1 mA cm–2 for 1 h, followed by 48 h resting at OCV. 
Using Faraday’s law, the bulk thickness of the deposits is estimated to be of ca. 5 µm. Then,

The solid-state NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz 
spectrometer equipped with a 1.3 mm HFXY MAS Bruker NMR probehead. All spectra were 
collected at room temperature with 25 kHz spinning speed with a 1.3 mm ZrO2 rotor. 7Li solid-
state NMR experiments were performed using a hahn-echo pulse sequence (90°–τ–180°–τ–
acquire, with τ = 36.9 μs), a recycle delay of 5 s, a power of 100 W with a pulse length of 1.06 
μs and 256 scans. 19F solid-state NMR experiments were performed using a hahn-echo pulse 
sequence (90°–τ–180°–τ–acquire, with τ = 38.4 μs), a recycle delay of 5 s, a power of 50 W 
with a pulse length of 1.3 μs and 16220 scans. 
The operando NMR experiments were conducted on a Bruker Avance 300 MHz spectrometer 
(the Larmor frequency for 7Li being 116.6 MHz) using a solenoidal Ag-coated Cu coil. The 
spectra were recorded using an in-situ automatic-tuning-and-matching probe (ATM VT X in-
situ WB NMR probe, NMR Service) that allows for an automatic recalibration of the NMR rf-
circuit during an in-situ electrochemistry experiment. The retuning of the rf-circuit becomes 
essential in order to quantify the NMR signals when the sample conditions are changing during 
the electrochemistry.4 The probe has highly shielded wire connections to the electrochemistry 
with low-pass filters (5 MHz) attached to the probe, minimizing the interferences between the 
NMR- and the electrochemistry circuit. Overall, the in-situ setup allows for highly reproducible 
NMR measurements. Single-pulse experiments were used to collect the NMR data, with a 
recycle delay of 1 s (>5 × T1) and 256 transients recorded. This resulted in an experimental 
time of about 4.5 min. The shift of 7Li was referenced to 1 M LiCl in water at 0 ppm. The 
spectra were processed in the Bruker Topspin software using the automatic phase and baseline 
correction. Further data processing was done in R. The total intensity of the Li metal peak was 
integrated over the 7Li shift range of 310–220 ppm and normalized to the intensity measured 
at the end of plating.  
For the in-situ NMR experiments, a current density of 1 mA cm-2 was applied for 40 h. A 
capsule cell (NMR Service) made out of PEEK was used for all in-situ NMR experiments and 
has been described before.4–6 The cell was assembled with lithium metal foil electrodes, an 
Advantec glass fiber separator and a 0.5 cm thick Viton® rubber spacer. The amount of 
electrolyte added to each cell was 150 μL. Every 1 h, the current was stopped for 9 s to record 
impedance measurements using a current amplitude of 100 µA amplitude and a frequency 
range of 500-0.8 Hz, and the magnitude of the impedance was evaluated at 9 Hz. The 
impedance data are normalized by the geometrical area of the electrodes (0.5 cm2).  
 
S1.3. Computational methods 
Molecular dynamics (MD) calculations were performed with the aid of Battflow,7 an automated 
workflow for molecular dynamics simulations of liquid electrolytes. MD simulations were 
conducted with the GROMACS package, version 2021.5.8 To prepare for MD simulations, we 
constructed each simulation box of around 6 nm, with electrolyte molar ratios equal to those

used in the experiments, by randomly displacing the species using the PACKMOL software.9 
The interatomic interactions of molecules and ions were described by the Optimized Potentials 
for Liquid Simulations All-Atom (OPLS-AA) force field,10 in which the topology files were 
generated using the LigParGen server.11–13 Bonded and non-bonded parameters for the anions 
adopted here (TFSI- and NO3-), as well as partial charges, were obtained from Doherty et al.14  
The following steps were employed for MD simulations: (i) configurations were relaxed 
through 5000 steps of the steepest descent method; (ii) equilibration was done through NVT 
simulations at 300 K for 2 ns, followed by NPT simulations in which reference pressure was 
set to 1 bar; (iii) production runs of 50 ns, under the NPT ensemble, were performed using the 
V-rescale temperature coupling thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman pressure coupling barostat 
at the same conditions of the equilibration steps. Long-range electrostatic interactions were 
calculated through the particle mesh Ewald method, assuming a cut-off of 1.2 nm and a Fourier 
spacing of 0.10 nm. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. Bonds with 
hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. The leap-frog algorithm was 
used to integrate the equations of motion with a 2.0 fs time step. Steps were saved in the 
trajectory files every 20 ps. The final 25 ns of the production run were used for further analysis.

S4. Discussion about the determination of transference numbers 
The transport number of an ion is the fraction of the total current carried by that ion, but such 
quantity can only be measured when ions are fully dissociated. On the other hand, the 
transference number of an ion is the fraction of the total current carried through any species 
containing that ion. Specifically, the transference number measures the ratio between the net 
current associated with the movement of the ion, regardless of which compound containing 
the ions is producing the ion flux, with respect to the total current passed through the cell.61,62 
For example, for a M+A- salt undergoing the following ion-association reactions: 
𝑀+ + 𝐴−⇌𝑀𝐴 
𝑀+ + 𝑀𝐴⇌𝑀2𝐴+ 
𝐴−+ 𝑀𝐴⇌𝑀𝐴2
− 
The transference number of the cation and anion (TM and TA, respectively) are related to the 
transport number of the individual species (ti) as follows:61 
𝑇𝑀= 𝑡𝑀+ + 2𝑡𝑀2𝐴+ −𝑇𝑀𝐴2−  
 
 
 
 
 
 
(5) 
𝑇𝐴= 𝑡𝐴−+ 2𝑡𝑀𝐴2−−𝑡𝑀2𝐴+  
 
 
 
 
 
 
(5) 
Transference numbers can be evaluated using different techniques, including the steady-state 
current analysis method by Bruce and Vincent,63 the low frequency impedance analysis by 
Roling and coworkers,64 and the analysis of concentration and polarization cells by Newman 
and coworkers.65 However, the applicability of these methods requires fast and stable 
interfacial charge-transfer reactions, absence of convection effects and non-linear deviations, 
and a clear separation of diffusional processes in the bulk electrolyte.66–68 
An alternative approach is to evaluate the apparent transference number of an ion, which is 
defined as:61

where zi, Di and ci are the charge, diffusion coefficient and concentration of ion i. The 
diffusion coefficients, Di, can be determined, for example, from radiotracer or pulsed field 
gradient NMR experiments. Note that these measurements are sensitive to the motion of 
charged and neutral compounds containing the ion under consideration. Therefore, for the

S6. Discussion of the combined impedance and operando 7Li NMR characterization 
of lithium metal anode reactions 
The combination of impedance and operando 7Li NMR measurements of the lithium metal 
anode reactions in lithium symmetrical cells (Figure S13, which is Figure 11 in the main 
article) provide a wealth of information,5,6 and thus, they are discussed in this section in more 
detail. 
In the electrolyte containing the best performing fluorinated ether (TFMP, Figure S13, left 
panel) a nearly continuous increase in the intensity of the NMR lithium metal microstructure 
signal is observed up to a lithium plating capacity of ca. 15 mA h cm-2, after which, a 
reversible short-circuit is observed. In contrast, in the electrolytes containing the other 
fluorinated ethers, the plating capacities before the cell shorting were much smaller, of ca. 6 
and 10 mA h cm-2 for TTE and TFPP, respectively (Figure S13, middle and right panels, 
respectively), and the short-circuits lead to permanent cell failure. 
Close inspection of the evolution of the magnitude of the cell impedance at a low frequency 
of 9 Hz (Figure S14), as obtained from impedance measurements performed every hour 
during the operando 7Li NMR measurements, shows that the cell short-circuit with TFMP is 
soft, since the associated magnitude of the cell impedance (ca. 20  cm2) is not small, as 
would be expected for a hard short (note that an upper limit of 10  cm2 has been proposed 
to define hard shorts82). In contrast, with TTE, although a soft short-circuit happens after 6 
hours of plating (as identified from the plateauing in the growth of lithium microstructures 
detected by NMR), it is followed by a hard short-circuit (evidenced by a marked impedance 
drop, where the magnitude of the cell impedance becomes very small, ca. 1  cm2). Finally, 
with TFPP, there is a period (between 6 and 10 hours of plating) in which the cell is 
intermittently soft short-circuited (evidenced by the very frequent voltage drops, and the 
deceleration of the growth of the lithium microstructure NMR signal), after which the cell 
short circuit becomes hard and the cell impedance drops (to ca. 5  cm2). The occurrence of 
hard and irrecoverable short-circuits in the presence of TTE and TFPP suggests a much 
higher spatial inhomogeneity in the electrode reactions, compared to TFMP, in agreement 
with the discussion in Section 2.2 in the main article. Note that the repetitive voltage drops, 
seen in all electrolytes every hour, are due to the interruption of the current to perform the 
impedance measurements, in which the cell voltage relaxes to close to zero, as expected, 
since the two electrodes in the cell are lithium electrodes and hence their potentials are the 
same in the absence of an applied current.  
Close inspection of the evolution of the cell voltage at the beginning of the plating 
experiments (Figure S15) shows that, with TFPP, the value of the cell voltage decreases 
rapidly as the plating experiment progresses, whereas the decay is slower with TTE, and 
TFMP shows the slowest decay. Such decrease in cell voltage can be ascribed to an increase 
in electrode area resulting from the growth of lithium microstructures (e.g. dendrites/mossy 
lithium), as the lithium plating overpotential decreases when the current is distributed over a 
larger electrode area.83 Therefore, the faster voltage decay with TFPP, and to a lesser extent 
with TTE, can be ascribed to a faster growth of lithium microstructures, in agreement with 
the earlier appearance of a cell short-circuit with these fluorinated ethers. Indeed, the 
impedance results reveal that the magnitude of the imaginary component of the impedance in 
TFPP and TTE electrolytes is smaller than in TFMP, and significantly decreases as plating 
Downloaded from pubs.​acs.​org/​jacsat/​article-supplement/​5233316/​pdf/​ja6c04480_si_001/​ by TSINGHUA UNIV user on 24 September 2026

<<PAGE 29>>
29 
 
progresses (see Figure S16), which can also be ascribed to a growth of the lithium surface 
area (as the impedance of a capacitor is given by Z=-i/C,84 where i is the imaginary number, 
 is the angular frequency and C is the capacitance, and the capacitance of a capacitor 
increases with the surface area85). On the other hand, with the short-circuited cells (for 
example, after 20 hours of plating, see Figure S16) the impedance, in the 500 Hz-0.8 Hz 
frequency range used here, is dominated by the resistance of the short, and any capacitance 
contributions becomes difficult to discern.  
It is also worth mentioning that the frequent voltage jumps seen with TFPP indicate poor 
lithium SEI stability.5 The voltage fluctuations are likely a consequence of the complex 
reaction dynamics that occur in the presence of an unstable SEI; for example, fast dissolution 
of lithium microstructures can occur due to corrosion reactions, promoted by electrolyte 
reduction, and exacerbated by a non-protective SEI,86 thus resulting in short-lived cell shorts.
