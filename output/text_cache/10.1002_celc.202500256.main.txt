Joint Partial Least Squares Modeling of Experimental and
Computational Data for Electrolyte Prescreening in
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Fritz Wortelkamp, Christian Wittekind, Lilli Busch, Simon Weidner, and Ingo Krossing*
Lithium–sulfur batteries have attracted great research interest
due to the high theoretical capacity of sulfur of 1672 mAh g1.
However, they have various problems due to the shuttle current
caused by molecular sulfur dissolving in the electrolyte. Hence,
electrolyte design is a key focus when optimizing the batteries.
This study investigates the relationship between cycling data and
electrochemical properties measured with cyclovoltammetric
measurements, shuttle current measurements, and impedance
spectroscopy. Using the acquired data, a partial least squares
model to screen solvent candidates in reference to these findings
is introduced. This model is based on cycling data as well as
density functional theory-calculated Conductor-like Screening
Model for Real Solvents data of the solvents and (solvated) lith-
ium–polysulfides. The usefulness of the converged method is
demonstrated by using it to identify new possible electrolyte sys-
tems. A subset of ten selected electrolyte systems is evaluated
experimentally and their performance is reported. One of those
electrolytes, 1.4 M LiTFSI, in pimelonitrile solution and without
any further additives, displays exceptional cycling stability already
on the first attempt, reaching a state of health of 50% after 115
cycles and maintaining a Coulombic efficiency of close to 100%
during the entire cycling procedure.
1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are currently the leading technology
for mobility applications, especially for electric vehicles.[1] Even
though they have undergone continuous increases in energy
density over the years, some sectors, like long-distance transpor-
tation, have an even higher demand in energy densities.[2,3] This
means new battery technologies are needed to fulfill those
needs, and one of the promising candidates is the lithium–sulfur
battery (LiSB) owing to the high theoretical capacity of sulfur of
1672 mAh g1, the low sulfur prices, and its sustainability.[4–6] The
need to do the cell reaction in solution rather than in solid state
has led to problems with harvesting this potential, the biggest
being the shuttle current that results from dissolved sulfur react-
ing with the Li electrode, leading to a variety of problems like low
cyclic stability and self-discharge.[7] Since the cell reaction and the
shuttle effect are both taking place in the electrolyte, it has been
the subject of research, trying to find optimized properties that
suppress shuttle current while still allowing the cell to func-
tion.[8,9] Significant efforts have been made to use different clas-
ses of solvents with different solubilities of sulfur to facilitate
stable cycling.[10] It is challenging to devise an electrolyte design,
since the mechanism of the sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) is not
completely understood due to difficulties in detecting the differ-
ent polysulfides (PSs) with their close relative Gibbs free energy,
that induces equilibrium reactions between virtually all of
them.[11–14] Furthermore, there are complicated relationships
between solvent properties, the solvation structure, and lithium
plating and stripping which in turn have a great effect on
cycleability.[15–17] In addition to analytical methods, theoretical
methods like density functional theory (DFT) coupled with
molecular dynamics calculations[18] and equilibrium potential
modeling[18–20] have been employed to better grasp the cell
chemistry in LiSBs, and they have given some new insights into
the SRR and lithium plating and stripping.
Hence, our objective in the first part of this study was not to
conduct an analysis of the SRR, but rather to evaluate the impact
of electrolyte properties on battery performance. To achieve this, a
series of electrochemical measurements of cells with different elec-
trolytes were conducted and data from our own previous study[21]
was used. The results have been classified according to two distinct
categories: those pertaining to the target optimization variables,
namely discharge capacity and cycling stability, and those related
to the properties of the electrolyte system, such as electrolyte

partial least squares (PLS) regression. This approach is particularly
well suited to a system with a high degree of covariance.[22,23]
The resulting model provides insights into the relationship between

When investigating new electrolyte solvents, a large number
of candidates are available, and testing each of them is not fea-
sible. For this reason, we intend to introduce a way of sorting
through a large dataset of possible candidates using a second
model using the insights gained in the first part. Here we created
a PLS model derived from the battery performance data of bat-
teries with electrolytes containing known solvents and from data
acquired with the DFT-based Conductor-like Screening Model for
Real Solvents (COSMO-RS).[24] COSMO-RS has been used in previ-
ous studies to generate data for machine learning approaches in
the investigation of electrolytes for LiSBs. For example, it has been
employed to identify ion pairs suitable for use in ionic liquid elec-
trolytes or to classify electrolytes by solvent in salt, salt in solvent,
and solvated ionic liquids.[25,26] Based on these results, it appears
feasible to use COSMO-RS-calculated data as a substitute for
experimentally measured electrode potentials, enabling a quick
and general screening of possible electrolyte systems. In the next
step we used this model with data from COSMO-RS on a large
number of solvent candidates to predict the performance of bat-
teries that contain these solvents as part of their electrolytes.
From this evaluation, we identified and tested a subset of ten
promising candidates for new possible electrolyte solvents that
have never before been published or tested as electrolyte sol-
vents for LiSBs.
2. Choice of Electrolytes
When choosing electrolyte solvents to use for data acquisition,
we intended to select a diverse set of solvents. This is not an easy
task, as the majority of solvents used in LiSBs are ethers.[8,10] A
variety of solvents could in principle be employed for the prepa-
ration of electrolyte solutions for LiSBs. A sensible classification of
solvents can be achieved by ordering according to their polysul-
fide (PS) solubilizing capacities, resulting in three main categories:
moderately solvating electrolyte solutions, sparingly solvating
electrolyte solutions (SSEs), and highly solvating electrolyte
solutions.[27–29] An alternative classification method is based on
chemical function.[8] The objective of this study was to concen-
trate on the solvents and to obtain a comprehensive dataset that
encompasses a diverse range of solvent classes, with a particular
emphasis on the most significant solvent classes. Furthermore,
two conducting salts were selected for the purpose of creating
electrolytes. The most commonly utilized conductive salt in
LiSBs is LiTFSI (=Li[N(SO2CF3)2].[30] Additionally, Li[Al(Ohfip)4]
(=lithium
tetrakis(hexafluoroisopropoxy)aluminate)—a
very
weakly coordinating anion—has been employed in LiSBs and
was selected to facilitate the application of solvents, in which
the interaction with Liþ is minimized. This is particularly relevant
to the battery reaction, where the interaction between Liþ and
the counteranion is minimized, thus facilitating a more compre-
hensive understanding of solvent effects, i.e., in SSE solvent sys-
tems.[31] In the second part of the study, only electrolyte mixtures
using LiTFSi were used. To get meaningful data about the influ-
ence of the electrolyte solvents themselves on these properties,
no additives like LiNO3, which can suppress shuttle current and
increase the stability of the batteries,[32] have been used. The fol-
lowing sections describe the solvent systems used in part 1 of this
study.
2.1. Ether-Based Solvents
The most widely used electrolyte for LiSBs is based on a mixture
of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME), usually
mixed in a 1:1 ratio.[33] For this reason, the DOL:DME 1:1 and both
separate solvents were added to the list of solvents used.
Tetrahydrofuran (THF) has been used as a solvent for LiSBs early
on due to the high solubility of PSs and the resulting good capac-
ities.[34] Hexylmethylether (HME) has been used in mixtures with
DOL to suppress the shuttle effect.[35] Here, it was used in a mix-
ture with as well as without DOL. Bis(2-methoxyethyl) ether
(diglyme) has been used as an electrolyte solution with low flam-
mability compared to DOL:DME mixtures[36] and tetraethylene
glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME/tetraglyme) has been used in dif-
ferent setups using high concentrations of conducting salts.[37,38]
In this work, they have both been used like regular solvents to get
data points of similar solvents starting from DME, which can also
be seen as monoglyme. In a similar vein, the different polyoxy-
methylene dimethyl ethers (OME) have been of interest.
Dimethoxymethane (Methylal/OME1), which has been of interest
due to the lower PS solubility when compared to DME[39] and
OME3, which have not yet been used in LiSBs, has been included.
Lastly,

2.4. Solvents that were Excluded
Some solvents can only be used in LiSBs, when using special sul-
furized polyacrylonitrile electrodes. These electrodes suppress
the dissolution of PSs and stop side reactions and the shuttle
effect.[46] This enables the use of electrolytes like carbonates,[47]
phosphates,[48,49] or siloxanes[50] that would otherwise have
strong side reactions with the PSs, which would prevent cycling.
Hence, they were omitted in this study. Other potential electro-
lyte solvents were not included due to the scope of the study.
These include, among others, solvent mixtures, various ether sol-
vents, and sulfolane-based solvents.
3. Collecting Measurement Data
In the context of batteries, the properties that are typically of
interest are those that indicate the performance of the battery,
such as cycling stability and capacity. These properties do not
exist in isolation; rather, they are dependent on a number of more
basic properties, including electrode potentials and electrolyte
resistance. These connections are not always readily apparent,
and batteries are a complex system. Indeed, many papers focus
on a single aspect of the battery, rather than examining the bat-
tery as a whole. This is why the connections cannot be expressed
in a simple one-to-one correlation in most cases and more com-
plex models are required. The data collected here are divided into
two categories for subsequent modeling. Dependent variables
are properties of the battery that are not readily quantifiable
in isolation and that result from mechanisms that span the entire
battery. This includes most of the properties that determine
battery
performance,

those
observed
in
LiSBs.

Independent variables are those that can be measured in isola-
tion from other properties. These include values such as electrode
potentials and impedance data. The situation is not one of clear-
cut, binary decisions, as many of the data points that are classified
as independent here are in fact correlated with one another. It is,
however, essential to make this distinction for the purposes of
modeling, and PLS has been selected for this model, since it is
an appropriate method for handling data that exhibits collinear-
ity. The following sections present the methodologies utilized for
data collection and delineate the groups to which the derived
data points are assigned.
3.1. Cyclic Voltammetry
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were employed to obtain
data regarding electrode potentials in diverse electrolytes.
The potential of the electrodes is dependent upon the solvent used,
and this affects the performance of batteries.[51] In nonaqueous sol-
utions,thepotentialsreferencedagainsttheclassical(aqueous)stan-
dard hydrogen electrode are not applicable, as the shift in potential
differsforeachsubstancewhenswitchingthesolvent.Inaccordance
with the recommendations set forth by the International Union of
Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), ferrocene (Fc) was employed
as an internal standard.[52] The idea that the Fc potential is (almost)
independent ofthesolvent iscalled the“ferroceneassumption”and
has long been questioned.[53] Although the assumption is flawed, it
canbestatedthatthechangeinFcpotentialistypicallyconsiderably
less than that of the electrodes utilized in this experiment.
Consequently, the decision was made to employ the ferrocene
assumption but to acknowledge a systematic error of 40 mV in all
measured Fc potentials. This error aligns with own direct measure-
ments of the Gibbs transferenergies of the Fc/Fcþ systemmeasured
between different solvents and using the “ideal” ionic liquid salt
bridge approach.[54–57]
3.2. Li-Potential ELi
The first measurement targeted the Li-potential ELi versus Fc/Fcþ
reference. This was done by dissolving a few milligrams of Fc in
each electrolyte and using a Pt working electrode and lithium as
reference and counter electrodes. This yields a CV curve contain-
ing an oxidation and a reduction peak, the error on each of which
was the combination of measurement error and systematic error
from the ferrocene assumption. The average of these two values
was then calculated using Gaussian error propagation, which
yielded the results for Li electrode potentials (ELi) shown in
Table S1, Supporting Information.
In general, CV measurements for sulfur cathodes are a bit more
complex. Depending on the type of electrolyte, the batteries show
different reaction pathways,[20] which implies that not every CV will
have the same number of peaks. The method for data point collec-
tion was performed as reported in our previous work.[21] In general,
the expected number of peaks is four, two reductive and two oxi-
dativepeaksmirroringthetwochargeanddischargeplateauscom-
monly observed in LiSBs.[7,58] They are assigned to the reactions
shown in Figure 1a. The first is the reaction of S8 to long-chained

3.3. Shuttle Current Measurements
The shuttle current (IS) can be directly measured in 2032 coin cells
by holding the voltage at a specific potential value and observing
the current that results from this.[59] Here it was decided to do
shuttle current measurements for fully charged batteries. As
delineated in Figure 2, to get this measurement, the batteries
were discharged and then charged once to get a formation,
and when the charge was done, the open-circuit voltage
(OCV) was measured. Afterward, a constant voltage (CV) step
was started, and the voltage was set to the OCV value. Any
current that was measured during this step could be attributed
to the shuttle current, as any drop in voltage could be explained
by shuttling of soluble sulfur species to the anode and reacting
there, which then had to be balanced by a charge current to
keep the voltage constant. This data is collected in Table S4,
Supporting Information.
This IS occupies a distinctive position in the modeling process.
In examining the variables that warrant optimization of battery
performance, it is imperative to consider the role of IS as an inde-
pendent variable. The objective is not to optimize the shuttle cur-
rent in and of itself but rather to understand its impact on the
variables of interest. In this context, the reduced cycling stability
of LiSBs, a consequence of high shuttle currents IS, necessitates its
inclusion as an independent variable. Conversely, the shuttle cur-
rent represents a distinctive attribute of the LiSB, necessitating a
comprehensive understanding. Given that it cannot, like other
independent variables, be assessed in isolation, it was also
treated as a dependent variable in its own model.
3.4. Impedance Measurements
Impedance measurements were done in the same 2032 coin
cells as the shuttle current measurement before the shuttle
current was measured at a depth of discharge (DoD) of 0%.
Additionally, measurements after the shuttle current were inves-
tigated, in which the cells were discharged to DoD of 10% and
evaluated. This was intended to get a data point with minimal
dissolution of polysulfides and one with more dissolution.

4. Partial Least Squares Models
For this study, it was decided to develop a PLS model due to the
method’s strength in handling small datasets and its ability to
manage strong collinearity.[23] Collinearity in the dataset was
expected, as previous work has shown a strong linear correlation
between the sulfur and lithium potentials.[21]
4.1. Handling Missing Data
Some of the data collected in the experiments was incomplete,
which implies some thought had to be put into the handling of
missing data points. The two main ways described in the litera-
ture are deletion, pairwise or listwise, or data imputation.[62]
Excessive imputation can lead to models with low statistical rel-
evance due to an artificial reduction of variance or biases.[62,63] To
avoid missing data, it was decided to manually guess data points
by comparing them with similar points if possible. This was nec-
essary for missing CV data of HME and THF, where only data for
the first CV cycle could be measured. In these cases, the data of
the first cycle was filled in instead of an average value as the dif-
ferences between measured data and the average were, in gen-
eral, larger than that between the first and third cycles. When
data could not be inferred, the imputation method employed
was to replace the missing data with the mean value. This is
one of the most straightforward methods, yet it has the effect
of reducing variance. It should, therefore, only be employed when
there are few missing data points, representing less than 10% of
the total, which is the case in this study.[62]
5. Results and Discussion
5.1. Part 1: Identifying Important Parameters for Electrolyte
Optimization
The first goal of this work was to find out which independent var-
iables predict a good battery performance indicated by the dis-
charge capacity as well as the lifetime of the battery and
identify which properties to optimize for. Additionally, the effects
on the shuttle current were investigated. All data used was scaled
and used in its mean-centered form for the PLS regression to make
them comparable. This way, each β-coefficient indicates the rela-
tive contribution of a given dependent variable to the correspond-
ing independent variable. For CV and for impedance data, two sets
of measurements were performed. For CV data, measurements of
the first and third cycles were performed, and for impedance data,
measurements at DoD 0% and 10% were performed.
Two sets of PLS models were created, one using IS as an inde-
pendent variable and one using it as a dependent variable. For
both sets, different combination of data measurements were
tested and compared considering their explanation of variance
in the variables IS and spec. cap. d. of the first cycle to see which
yielded the best results, as detailed in Table 4.
The dataset that performed the best was the one omitting the
CV data for cycle three and the impedance data at DoD 10. It
explained 73% of the variance in IS and 48% of the variance in
spec. cap. d. The summaries of all models can be found in
Table S11, S15, Supporting Information. The three dependent var-
iables chosen for discussion here showed acceptable amounts of
variance explained. They are discharge capacity for the formation
and the first cycle and IS. These data points are well suited to dis-
cuss the performance of the batteries as the discharge capacities
are generally the most critical measure for a battery, along with its
cycling stability. One of the main reasons for poor cycling stability
in LiSBs is the shuttle effect. This implies that the cycling stability
is also covered by including IS in the discussion. When comparing
β-coefficients, specific attention should be paid to the variables
that have a large positive effect on discharge capacity but a neg-
ative effect on the shuttle current, as these are the most promis-
ing for optimizing the battery properties. Besides this, variables
that have a strong effect on the discharge capacity but a weak
effect on IS are also of interest. Figure 4 shows the relative impor-
tance of the independent variables for the model handling IS as a

can be found in the supporting data.[64]

From these results, the best way to design electrolytes seems
to be to first pay attention to suppressing the shuttle current as
far as possible without losing too much battery capacity and then

5.2. Part 2: Finding Electrolyte Solvents from a Large
Screening
The second part of this study aimed to create a method that can
quickly skim through a large dataset and find suitable candidates
for electrolytes to use in LiSBs. For this purpose, a large dataset,
which has been mostly unsorted, was necessary. This made the
measurement of electrode potentials for each electrolyte mixture
unfeasible, which is why we opted to instead use calculated
COSMO-RS data for the chemical potential of the different species
that are present in solution in the batteries.

5.3. Finding and Testing Suitable Electrolyte Mixtures
In this section promising electrolyte candidates are selected and
then tested for their viability in coin cells. First, the selection pro-
cess is outlined followed by the results and their evaluation.
5.3.1. Filtering the Dataset
The quality factor Q was calculated for the predicted data, and the
data was ordered in descending order by Q. Here, it was notice-
able that some unlikely candidates were present in the dataset
and yielded very high Q values. This included electrolyte mixtures
containing some gases like krypton, which were present due to
inaccuracies in the boiling and melting points of these gases in
the database, and solvents that would not be able to dissolve
LiTFSI in the necessary concentration, for example, alkanes.
These were present since we did not filter the original search,
and the COMSOtherm calculation was done, regardless if the
LiTFSI concentration in the investigated solvents was possible
to reach or not.
To account for this, we created an adjusted dataset for

The solvents that showed successful cycling are shown in

γ-Valerolactone (GVL) with 1.6 M LiTFSI (Figure 7a) starts at a
high specific discharge capacity, which quickly fades. It exhibits
CEs over 100% during the initial phase. This is indicative of side
reactions of PSs with the electrolyte as the PSs are formed during
discharge but are not available during charging.
Pimelonitrile with 1.4 M LiTFSI (Figure 7b) shows medium-
specific discharge capacities. However, it has exceptional stability
with the capacity showing a linear decline over the whole cycling
with CEs at almost 100%. This indicates a low amount of side reac-
tions and suppression of the PS shuttle. This has been reported
for acetonitrile which, as a sparingly solvating electrolyte, sup-
presses the PS shuttle.[44,69] When using acetonitrile, the need
for a high concentration of LiTFSI and the addition of cosolvents
to lower viscosity were reported. Both are not necessary here. A
possible explanation for this is that the bidentate pimelonitrile is
coordinating as a chelate more strongly to Liþ when compared to
the monodentate acetonitrile.
Dimethyl pimelate with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure 7c) also shows a
high discharge capacity in the formation and first cycle, followed
by a rapid decline in capacity, which probably makes this
electrolyte unusable. This is coupled with high CE values indicat-

6. Conclusion
In this study, CV data and impedance data were used in conjunc-
tion with cycling data and shuttle current measurements to cre-
ate PLS models. The data were collected from LiSBs with different
electrolyte mixtures to identify which electrolyte properties influ-
ence the discharge capacity and the shuttle current of these bat-
tery systems. The first model included IS as a dependent variable
and showed that it depends on the electrolyte properties much in
the same way as the discharge capacity. This means an electrolyte
that facilitates a good battery reaction will also facilitate a strong
shuttle current. However, when including the shuttle current as
an independent variable, some key variables could be identified
that can be optimized to improve the discharge capacity without
increasing shuttle current, namely, ES2,c1, the potential connected
to the second discharge plateau, the resistance correlated to the
charge transfer of PSs, and the carbon electrode RPS and the two
factors connected to diffusion in the battery α CPEdiff and
Q CPEdiff. This offers starting points to be considered for optimi-
zation, when purposefully designing liquid electrolytes for LiSBs.
We then present a method using PLS to predict battery per-
formance in LiSBs based on electrolyte mixtures based on these
findings. It uses electrolyte properties calculated by DFT methods
with COSMOtherm, which enables an electrolyte screening solely
based on computational data. This allows for prescreening of
many possible electrolyte mixtures that could not be screened
experimentally in a reasonable amount of time. While the method
has some weaknesses, mainly the inability to predict side reac-
tions, it fulfills its purpose of prescreening well and delivers good
results when paired with the manual selection of electrolyte
candidates.
We demonstrated the usefulness of this method by applying
it to a large set of electrolyte solvents withdrawn from the
Scifinder database and they were only chosen by their melting
and boiling points. We identified from the analysis ten possible
electrolyte mixtures with solvents that have never been pub-
lished for use in LiSBs before. Furthermore, we found and also
applied three candidates already published as electrolyte sol-
vents in LiSBs, further showing the method’s effectiveness. We
