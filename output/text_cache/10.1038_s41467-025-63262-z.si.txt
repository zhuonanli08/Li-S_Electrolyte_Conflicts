Supplementary Note 1 
AE electrolytes’ concentration optimization. 
As shown in Figure S2a, due to insufficient dissociation of Li+, the 1M LiFSI electrolyte 
possesses the lowest conductivity of 0.72 mS cm-1. The 2M and 3M LiFSI electrolytes demonstrate 
an optimal Li+ mobility of 1.68 and 1.70 mS cm-1. When the concentration increases to 4M, the 
formation of ion clusters (CIPs/AGGs) and increased viscosity decrease the conductivity to 1.43 mS 
cm-1, consistent with previous literature about concentered ether electrolytes20,21. Moreover, the 
𝑡𝐿𝑖+ in 4M LiFSI is only 0.51 (Figure S2f). By contrast, 2M LiFSI exhibited the highest 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ of 
0.64, representing efficient Li⁺ transport with minimal anion polarization (Figure S2c-f). Notably, 
the 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ are measured via the electrochemical method and determined by the following equation22: 
𝑡𝐿𝑖+ = 𝐼𝑠(∆𝑉−𝐼0𝑅0)
𝐼0(∆𝑉−𝐼𝑠𝑅𝑠) 
where ∆𝑉 is the applied voltage, I0 and R0 are the initial current and resistance, respectively, and Is 
and Rs are the steady-state current and resistance, respectively. 
Furthermore, we compared the electrochemical performance of these four concentrations. For the 
Li plating/stripping CE test, the 2M LiFSI achieved the highest CE (99.59%), surpassing 1M 
(90.03%), 3M (99.33%), and 4M (95.29%) (Figure S2b). This suggests that the optimized 
concentration of Li+ enables the formation of stable SEI and a promising Li⁺ deposition reversibility, 
a critical factor for Li metal batteries. As for the Li||Li symmetrical cell, 2M LiFSI shows the lowest 
polarization (~33 mV at 0.5 mA cm-2) and stable Li plating/stripping behaviour compared to others, 
suggesting the faster ion transference and robust SEI stability (Figure S2g).  
Based on the abovementioned experimental results, 2M LiFSI in AE electrolyte exhibits a better 
performance and has been chosen as the optimized electrolyte concentration. Moreover, our choice 
aligns with recent studies in lithium metal battery electrolytes, where 2M LiFSI in ether solvents 
has been shown to balance ion transport dynamics with interfacial stability23, further validating its 
universality.

Supplementary Note 2  
Identification of NOE effects. 
As the mixing time increases (from 200 to 400 ms), the accumulation of the (Nuclear 
Overhauser Effect) NOE signal results in the intensity increase of the 1H-19F cross-peaks (Figure 
S3a-b). When we further increase the mixing time, the cross-peak intensity decreases sharply due 
to the influence of the spin relaxation (Figure S3c). Therefore, the 1H-19F cross-peaks are raised 
from space NOE 
 accumulation instead of the coordination effect. This phenomenon may be caused by the NGP 
effect of the C=C double bond. As the double bonds coordinate with Li+ cations, the inductive effect 
of Li+ results in =CH2 carrying a less negative charge, attracting the FSI- anion via the electrostatic 
effect. Thus, the spatial proximity of AE and FSI- induces the NOE effect through magnetic dipole 
field interaction. In contrast, the 1H-7Li cross-peak intensities are constant among the three mixing 
times, demonstrating Li+ cations coordinate with AE molecules via dipole-ion interaction (Figure 
S3d-f).

Supplementary Note 3 
Calculation of electrolyte homogeneity. 
A quantitative analysis of structure factors (S(q)) is implemented to rigorously validate the 
spatial distribution characteristics of three electrolytes. The S(q) curves are obtained through the 
Fourier transformation of RDFs (provided above), where q represents the scattering vector. When q 
approaches zero, the S(q) value reflects long-range density fluctuations, with S(q) values closer to 
unity indicating higher system homogeneity24. Compared with the DME electrolyte, the quantitative 
analysis demonstrates that the AE and DPE electrolytes exhibit slightly higher S(q) values. This 
phenomenon is majorly attributed to solvation-induced local ion clusters (CIPs/AGGs), generating 
density fluctuations. In general, all three electrolytes maintain S(q) values approaching unity across 
the full q-range, which confirms the essential solution homogeneity. Therefore, all three electrolytes 
exhibit characteristic short-range ordering while preserving long-range structural uniformity.

Supplementary Note 4 
Ion transference number 
The Li+ transference numbers (𝑡𝐿𝑖+) are investigated via the electrochemical method and 
calculated based on the following equation: 
𝑡𝐿𝑖+ = 𝐼𝑠(∆𝑉−𝐼0𝑅0)
𝐼0(∆𝑉−𝐼𝑠𝑅𝑠) 
where ∆𝑉 is the applied voltage, I0 and R0 are the initial current and resistance, respectively, and Is 
and Rs are the steady-state current and resistance, respectively. 
As shown in Figure S12, the 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ are calculated to be 0.61, 0.65, and 0.56 for 2M LiFSI AE, 
DPE, and DME electrolytes, respectively. This phenomenon is attributed to the weak solvation 
structure, leading to the formation of Li+-FSI- ion clusters (CIPs/AGGs) to significantly reduce the 
mobility of FSI-. Therefore, the 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ for the weakly solvated DPE electrolyte is the highest despite 
its low overall conductivity, a phenomenon reported in previous studies25.

Supplementary Note 5 
Analysis of the polymerization of AE electrolytes during electrochemical processes 
We conducted the Attenuated Total Internal Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-
FTIR) spectroscopy to characterize the presence or absence of polymerization processes (Figure 
S23). We compared the spectra of pure AE solvent (AE), AE electrolyte, chemically polymerized 
AE solvent (poly AE), SEI on the lithium metal surface, uncycled Li chips, and LiFSI salt. The poly-
AE was synthesized through the polymerization method to provide a clear reference, where 2,2'-
Azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN) was used as an initiator. Li was deposited on Cu foil (Li||Cu) 
at 5 mAh/cm2 and 0.5 mA/cm2 for SEI formation. 
The IR spectrum comparison between poly-AE and AE exhibits the disappearance of the -
CH=CH₂ stretching vibration peak at 3094 cm-1, representing the existence of the addition 
polymerization process. Moreover, a redshift of the -CH₂ (1350 cm-1)/C=C (1650 cm-1) bending 
vibration peaks and a blueshift in the -C-O-C (1100 cm-1)/C-O (1146 cm-1) stretching vibration 
peaks are shown for poly-AE, indicating a change in the molecular environment due to 
polymerization. Nevertheless, these phenomena are negligible in the AE electrolyte, suggesting that 
LiFSI salt could not trigger the polymerization of the AE solvent.  
Furthermore, signature peaks of organic species are revealed on the cycled lithium metal 
surface, consistent with the XPS and TOF-SIMs results, indicating the decomposition of AE on the 
Li metal surface to form an organic layer of SEI. However, the absence of C=C (1650 cm-1), -CH₂ 
(1350 cm-1), and C-O (1146 cm-1) vibration peak shift demonstrates the AE electrolyte does not 
undergo a significant polymerization process at the interface, where the organic species originate 
from the decomposition products of the AE solvent.

Supplementary Note 6 
Mechanical properties of SEI formed in different electrolytes 
As shown in Figure S28, due to the involvement of the NGP effect, the SEI formed in the AE 
electrolyte at -40 ℃ exhibits significantly lower roughness (2.72 nm) than that formed in the DPE 
electrolyte (19.8 nm). The Young's modulus of the SEI for AE is substantially lower (2.5 GPa) than 
that of the SEI for DPE (18 GPa). Therefore, these variances in structural uniformity and mechanical 
properties have significant implications for lithium-ion transport and subsequent lithium deposition. 
The inhomogeneous and rigid SEI produced in the DPE electrolyte forest the formation of numerous 
lithium dendrites at -40 ℃ (Figure 3e). Moreover, the continuous decomposition and regeneration 
of this SEI layer lead to inefficient Li plating/stripping behaviour and lower CE for LMAs. 
Conversely, the SEI formed in the AE electrolyte is uniformly structured and demonstrates 
sufficient mechanical strength and toughness to endure high stress without fracturing. This 
homogeneity minimizes dendrite formation and reiterative electrolyte decomposition, thereby 
enhancing the Li plating/stripping behaviour and CE for LMAs26.

Supplementary Note 7 
Simulation of SEI formation process of DME electrolyte 
As shown in the simulation trajectories, for the initial 6 ps, the DME electrolyte components 
migrate toward the lithium metal surface without detectable decomposition. Critical reaction onset 
occurs at 8.6 ps with one FSI- anion decomposition initiated by S-F bond cleavage, yielding LiF 
compound. Subsequent progression to 12.5 ps reveals further anion breakdown to form Li2O and 
Li3N, much slower than 3 ps in the AE electrolyte, demonstrating the diminished electrochemical 
reactivity of FSI⁻ in the DME electrolyte. Notably, no additional decomposition events were 
observed beyond this stage through 20 ps. Compared with the AE electrolyte, the strongly solvated 
property of DME solvents significantly reduces anion populations within the Li⁺ solvation sheath. 
The solvent-dominated solvation structure suppresses FSI⁻ anions’ reactivity and participation, 
inhibiting the formation of an inorganic-rich inner SEI on the Li metal surface. Therefore, under the 
electric field, the unprotected Li metal surface will consistently react with the DME electrolyte to 
form a thick and organic-rich SEI layer, along with the physical characterization (Figure S20 and 
S27), significantly deteriorating the electrochemical performance.

Supplementary Note 8 
Kinetic limitation analysis for negative electrode and positive electrode 
As shown in Figure S39, the rate performance is comparable for three electrolytes at low 
current rates. At the current rate of 1C, the capacity of the full cell in 2M DPE electrolyte differs 
noticeably from 2M AE electrolyte, where the calculated geometrical current density is ~1.5 mA 
cm-2. At the current density of 1.5 mA cm-2, the Li||Li symmetrical cells demonstrate a comparable 
overpotential, demonstrating that the poor rate performance in DPE electrolyte is not majorly 
limited by the Li plating/stripping kinetic (Figure 3i). We further analyze the Li+ diffusion property 
through the electrode-electrolyte interface via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). To 
eliminate the influence of the Li negative electrode side, we apply SPAN||SPAN and Li||Li 
symmetrical cell to measure the EIS. Due to the formation of more ion clusters (CIPs/AGGs), the 
higher desolvation energy results in a higher charge transfer resistance (Rct) for the DPE electrolyte 
at the positive electrode interface (Figure 2b, S39a, and S39d). Furthermore, the reaction resistances 
and voltage hysteresis calculated from the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) 
measurements are comparable for the lithiation and delithation process in both electrolytes, 
representing the similar Li+ migration kinetic with the positive electrode materials (Figure S39h, I, 
k, and l). Moreover, due to the weak solvation property of the DPE molecules, the ionic conductivity 
of 2M LIFSI/DPE electrolyte is lower than 2M LiFSI/AE across the various temperatures and 
declines rapidly along with the decreasing temperature (Figure 4a-c and Figure S11) 
Therefore, based on the above-mentioned experimental results, the kinetic limitation for 
Li||SPAN full cell could be attributed to the low ionic conductivity in the electrolyte and the elevated 
desolvation energy at the positive electrode interface induced by the ion clusters in the DPE 
electrolyte.
