Recently, rechargeable LiS batteries, a next-generation energy storage system, are deeply studied due to their theoret-
ical speciﬁc energy density. However, to produce batteries comparable to those already available on the market some
drawbacks must be overcome, including essentially self-discharge, high internal resistance and rapid capacity fading
upon cycling. In this work the use of polysulﬁde solutions either as additives or as active material in LiS batteries is
proposed. The addition of polysulﬁdes to the electrolytic solution improves the cell performances in terms of speciﬁc
capacity and coulombic efﬁciency, passing from a capacity of about 150 mAh/g with a coulombic efﬁciency of about
0.85 to a capacity of 600 mAh/g with a coulombic efﬁciency of about 0.99 after 10 cycles. In batteries where polysul-
ﬁde solutions are used as cathodic material, the obtained performances are even higher, reaching speciﬁc capacities of
420–450 mAh/g after about 70 cycles. Moreover, the cells tested with carbon paper as electrode support shows a
greater reversibility, with a coulombic efﬁciency very close to 1. Finally, reducing the potential window from 3 to
1.5 V to 2.8–1.7 V, the cells show high stability and efﬁciency, reaching speciﬁc capacity values of about
600 mAh/g after 200 cycles.

1. Introduction
In the post-Li-ion batteries era, looking for cathodes with always higher
capacity values, sulfur represent one of the main objective of in-depth study
as electrode material [1]. Indeed, the conversion reaction from S to Li2S, in-
volving 2 mol of electrons per mole of sulfur, shows a speciﬁc theoretical
capacity of 1675 mAh/g. LiS batteries have a theoretical speciﬁc energy
of 2600 Wh/kg. Even if it is practically possible to obtain speciﬁc energy
performances of 500–600 Wh/kg, this value would still be greater than
the performances offered by the lithium-ion currently on the market [2].
Among the various problems related to the sulfur use as a cell active mate-
rial, some are intrinsically associated to the sulfur nature whose electrical
conductivity is extremely low, others are instead connected to the sulfur
use in presence of lithium. The polysulﬁdes formed during cell operations
can migrate from the cathode to the anode, through a shuttle-like mecha-
nism, causing a series of parasitic reactions. The conversion from sulfur to
lithium sulﬁde also involves morphological and structural changes. In
fact, the transformation from sulfur to sulﬁde causes a signiﬁcant increase
in volume; upon battery cycling, such increases and decreases in volume de-
termine the crushing of the electrode and the consequent contact loss. The
shuttle effect strongly limits the Coulombic efﬁciency and the battery func-
tioning over time; on the other hand, the solubility of the polysulﬁdes in the
electrolyte allows a more efﬁcient use of the sulfur. Conventional sulfur
electrodes with non-nanometric particles often show very low capacitance
values, because the formation of polysulﬁdes occurs only at the interface
with the electrolyte, while most of the sulfur is inactive. To address these
issues, in recent years, different strategies have been proposed, all aiming
to obtain cathode materials with high performances. Numerous suggestions
for developing the “lithium‑sulfur” technology consist in tuning the
chemical-physical properties and morphology of sulfur. First of all, the
aim was to reduce particle size by synthesizing nanometric sulfur. An alter-
native approach is the use of nanocomposites with different components
like carbon [3–11] or conductive polymers, besides sulfur.
This approach intends to increase both the amount of electrochemically
active sulfur and the conductivity of the cathode, by forming networks of
conductive material in intimate contact with the sulfur particles [4–7].
The addition of conductive materials obviously limits the sulfur content
in the nanocomposite, reducing the sulfur load in the cathode itself. There-
fore, current research aims to achieve the necessary balance between sulfur
load and battery performance [12]. To reduce the concentration of
polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte, so as to limit the parasitic reactions, many
porous trapping materials have been proposed, minimizing the diffusion
of the polysulﬁdes produced outside the cathode [6].Therefore the predom-
inant strategy involves the encapsulation of sulfur in conductive and porous
structures, that allow the electrolyte reaching the most sulfur possible
amount. Thus, trapping the produced polysulﬁdes into conductive network

For example, some authors [13] synthesized a hybrid three-dimensional
network sulfur composites, with a good electrical conductivity, used as
monolith cathode in LiS batteries. A reversible capacity of about 470
mAh/g after 100 cycles at a current rate of 0.2C and a good rate capabil-
ity of about 84% have been obtained at various current rates. Other au-
thors proposed [14] a sheet of natural hydratesilicate mineral, as a
powerful host for S cathodes in LiS batteries. The attraction between
the surface cations as Mg2+, K+, Li+, Ca2+, and Na+ and polysulﬁdes
anions is responsible for the suppression of polysulﬁde dissolution and
subsequent shuttle process, obtaining a good capacity above 600
mAh/g for >500 cycles at C/5. Finally, in a recent work [15] a stainless
steel wire network with high conductivity, high surface area, and stable
structure able to retain the polysulﬁdes in the cathode region during the
charge/discharge cycling, has been produced. A reversible capacity at a
low rate of 0.1C and at a high rate of 2C 1100 and 250 mAh/g respec-
tively has been reached.
Another studied approach is the so-called semi-ﬂow batteries,
[16–20] with metallic lithium as anode, while the cathode is repre-
sented by a solution of lithium polysulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte,
in this case named catholyte [21–26]. This way allows to obtain a
completely exploitable cathode material, unlike what happens with
classic electrodes, in which part of the sulfur remains inactive inside
the matrix. However, in the latter case, the presence of high concentra-
tions of polysulﬁdes in solution can cause various problems during bat-
tery operations. First, the lithium anode requires effective “protection”
to prevent the shuttle effect. Furthermore, polysulﬁde solutions can be
unstable, with precipitation of insoluble sulﬁdes and polysulﬁdes,
which tend to saturate the support pores.
Therefore, in LiS batteries, based on polysulﬁde solutions, electrolyte
containing additives must be used to allow the formation of a stable passiv-
ation layer on the surface of lithium metal anode, thus protecting it from
polysulﬁdes. The main additive used for this purpose is LiNO3 [27–29]. An-
other possible strategy is to utilize membranes permeable only to Li+ ions,
to effectively separate the catholyte from the anode [30,31]. Membranes
must be mechanically strong enough to prevent possible dendrites growth
on the anode from getting in contact with the cathode, causing safety issues.
Among the proposed solutions, it could be mentioned thick glass mem-
branes like the superionic conductor glass ceramic ﬁlms LISICON and
NASICON, [30], or solid electrolytes like Li6PS5Cl [31]. Finally, for conduc-
tive support, most of the research is focused on materials with high speciﬁc
surface area and porosity, with the aim of having a better contact surface
between the catholyte and the electrode where the redox reactions take
place. In this work, it has been proposed the use of polysulﬁde solutions
in LiS batteries either as additives or as active material. In the ﬁrst case,
polysulﬁdes were added to batteries with Li2S/carbon cathode to saturate
the electrolyte solution, thereby inhibiting the dissolution and loss of the
active material. In this battery, sulfur is formed during the ﬁrst charge. In-
stead, in the second case polysulﬁde solutions have been used as an active
material in hybrid semi-ﬂow batteries having carbonaceous materials as
support electrodes.
2. Experimental
Diffractometer Rigaku (Wilmington, MA, USA) (Cu-Kα radiation λ =
0.15418 nm) and high-resolution electronic scanning microscope Auriga-
Zeiss (SEM) were used for chemical-physical characterizations. The electro-
chemical characterizations were carried out inside a dry room at controlled
humidity (<20 ppm) and at a constant temperature of 20 °C, by using the
LIR2016 type button cells and a 54-channel Maccor Series 4000
potentiostat-galvanostat. UV–visible absorption spectroscopic data were
collected on a Beckman DU 530 UV–Vis spectrophotometer with wave-
length range from 900 to 300 nm. For UV–Vis characterization, the polysul-
ﬁde catholyte samples were diluted to 1.0 mM.
2.1. Synthesis of lithium sulﬁde
Li2S was synthesized by mixing Li3N and S in a 1: 1 weight ratio. This
ratio is equivalent, in moles, to a slight excess of lithium, which serves to
eliminate any water eventually present and adsorbed in the powders. Actu-
ally, elimination of water is crucial due to the high hygroscopicity of the sul-
ﬁde, which would degrade immediately. The mixture of Li3N and S was
loaded in a stainless steel vial, together with two stainless steel balls. The
vial was mounted in a shaker mill (SPEX 8000, CertPrep, Metuchen, NJ,
USA) and milled for 1 h, giving rise to the following reaction:

2.2. Synthesis of polysulﬁde solutions
0.5 M of polysulﬁde solutions in DOL/DME [1,3dioxolane/1,2
dimethoxyethane (1/1 v:v)] were prepared. In detail, Li2S (46 mg) and S
(224 mg) were mixed in an agate mortar and then in 2 ml of solvent at
80 °C for 7 days under magnetic stirring. The solution was then placed in
an oven at 60 °C for another 7 days. At the end of the heat treatments the
solution has a red-brown coloration, without any precipitate presence. As
already mentioned, all the material handling operations were carried out
in the absence of humidity, inside a dry room.
2.3. Electrochemical cells
When the polysulﬁdes have been used as additives, the cell for the elec-
trochemical tests consisted of a lithium anode, an electrolyte and a Li2S/C
cathode, as shown in Fig. 1a). In this case, for the cathode preparation car-
bon super P was added to Li2S sample with a weight ratio of 1: 9 respec-
tively and the mixture was ball milled for 8 h.
When the polysulﬁdes have been used as active material, the cell used,
shown in Fig. 1b), consisted of a lithium anode and a catholytic solution
containing polysulﬁdes in the form of Li2Sx (x = 4,6,8), and acting simulta-
neously as electrolytic solution and cathode.
The reduction of the catholyte takes place on a carbon-based conductive
support such as carbon cloth (E-TEK HT 1400 W) or carbon paper (Toray
TGP-H-120) which acts as support electrode. In both cases the separator
is a ﬁberglass disk. When the polysulﬁdes were used as additives, the elec-
trochemical characterization was performed using an electrode material
consisting of active electrode material, carbon and teﬂon in a ratio of 75:
17: 8. The compounds are laminated together for obtaining layers with a
thickness of about 40 μm, from which electrodes with a diameter
of 10 mm are taken by using an hollow punch. This method allows to
get electrodes with suitable electronic conductivity, plasticity and mechan-
ical properties. As regards the semi-ﬂow cells, the catholyte mixture
was prepared by adding the polysulﬁde solution to the electrolytic

3.2. UV–Vis characterization of the catholyte
The catholyte has been characterized by UV–Vis analysis to study the
stability of polysulﬁdes in solution. Fig. 3 shows the trend over time of
the UV–Vis absorption spectrum of a catholyte solution composed of LiTFSI
1 M and LiNO3 0.5 M in DOL:DME (1:1 v:v) + Li2Sxin DOL:DME,
representing the used solution composition in semi-ﬂow cells in this
work. The measurements have been performed, every each hour up to
5 h, starting from a freshly prepared solution in order to verify its stability.
The main absorption bands are located at 331 and 419 nm and are asso-
ciated with the presence of Li2S4. In the spectrum measured at the time zero
(t = 0) it is possible to observe, with a very low intensity, also the bands at
351 and 479 nm relative to Li2S6, no further distinguishable after the ﬁrst
hour. In the following hours only the bands associated with the Li2S4 are
observed, whose intensity at 419 nm slightly decreases highlighting a
small degradation by the time. Finally, the Li2S8 bands can never be de-
tected. All these observations suggest that the most stable species in the
analyzed catholyte is Li2S4, all the other polysulﬁde species rapidly convert
in it.

3.3.1. Polysulﬁdes used as additives
As a ﬁrst step, to highlight the effect of the polysulﬁdes addition to the
electrolytic solution, galvanostatic cycling tests were carried out on cells

trolyte. Fig. 4a) shows the charging and discharging voltage proﬁles as a

a greater virtual charge capacity, while during the subsequent cycles regu-
lar curves with better reversibility are observed, due to the formation of a
stable SEI, even if a decrease of speciﬁc capacity values is observed.
Fig. 4b) shows the speciﬁc charge and discharge capacity and the coulom-

3.3.2. Polysulﬁdes used as cathodic active material
Polysulﬁdes have also been exploited both as a cathodic material and
electrolyte, by using a carbon cathodic support. Carbon paper and carbon
cloth were examined to check for possible differences. Fig. 8-a) shows the
electrochemical behavior of a cell with carbon paper used as electrode sup-
port at C/10 c-rate. The battery worked for over 70 charge and discharge
cycles, with an initial speciﬁc capacity of >800 mAh/g. During the ﬁrst
about 25 cycles the charge and discharge process is not completely revers-
ible, with an average coulombic efﬁciency value equal to 0.94. Instead, in
the following cycles more stable and closer to 1values are observed indicat-
ing a high overall reversibility. However, there is a gradual decrease of spe-
ciﬁc capacity, which drops to 450 mAh/g after 50 cycles and to 400 mAh/g
after 70 cycles. Capacity retention per cycle is on average 0.6%. Fig. 8-b)
shows the electrochemical performances of a cell with carbon cloth at
C/10 c-rate.
The speciﬁc discharge capacity is rather stable over the time except for
the ﬁrst two cycles, during which it passes from 647 to 464 mAh/g. Al-
though the speciﬁc capacity remains almost constant for about 70 cycles,
the charging/discharging process does not seem to be highly reversible,
with a systematic difference between the charging and discharging pro-
cesses after 10 cycles and a resulting average coulombic efﬁciency of
0.86. It is conﬁrmed that the formation and accumulation of nonconductive
species as Li2S or sulfur on cathode surfaces and their subsequent possible
partial chemical dissolution [17] contributes to the capacity fade of LiS bat-
teries [32–34]. Thus, as aforementioned the difference in charge/discharge
capacity can be due to the shuttle effect caused by an instable anodic SEI,
but in this case it could be also due to different interaction between electro-
chemical and chemical dissolution during the charge and discharge pro-
cesses [32–34]. A change of batteries use conditions was also examined.
Generally speaking, during the operation of a LiS battery, the formation
of insoluble and poorly conductive species [35–37] occurs at the extremes
of the potential window, leading to the formation of sulfur during the
charging process and lithium sulﬁde during the discharging process.
In a Li-polysulﬁde battery the dissolved active material is present in so-
lution, therefore it is necessary to limit the reactions that lead to the forma-
tion of solid compounds. Then, because of the battery with carbon paper
presents a more pronounced capacity fade even if it has a better coulombic
efﬁciencies and reversibility, to limit this capacity fade, a reduced potential
window (2.8 to 1.7 V range) compared to the previous one, (3 to 1.5 V
range) has been tested.

4. Conclusions
In this work the effects of polysulﬁde solutions in LiS cells have been re-
ported. The polysulﬁde solutions have been used both as electrolytic addi-
tives in Li-Li2S/C cells, and as active electrode material in hybrid cells that
use carbonaceous material (either carbon paper or carbon cloth) as a cath-
ode support. In Li2S-based electrodes, the addition of polysulﬁdes to the
electrolytic solution improves the cell performances in terms of speciﬁc ca-
pacity and coulombic efﬁciency, going from a capacity of about 150 mAh/g
and a coulombic efﬁciency of about 0.85 to a capacity of 620 mAh/g and a
coulombic efﬁciency of about 0.99 after 10 cycles. In batteries where poly-
sulﬁde solutions have been used as cathodic material, the obtained perfor-
mances are even better than conventional electrodes, reaching speciﬁc
capacities of about 420–450 mAh/g after 70 cycles. In particular, the cells
tested with carbon paper showed a greater reversibility with a coulombic
efﬁciency very close to 1. Finally, the reduction of the potential window
from 3 to 1.5 V to 2.8–1.7 V leads to speciﬁc capacity values of about 600
mAh/g after 200 cycles, with high stability and reversibility of the charg-
ing/discharging process. In conclusion, the results obtained with the
semi-ﬂow hybrid LiS batteries conﬁrm the importance of the redox couple
S/S−2 as a possible candidate for cathode material in Li-ion batteries.
