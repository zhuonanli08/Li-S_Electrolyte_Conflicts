Experimental Procedures
Chemicals and reagents. α-32S (natural S, analytical reagent), HNO3 (70%), 
anhydrous NaOH (≥98%), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 
LiPF6 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. H2O2 (30%) was purchased from 
Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. α-34S (99.79%) was purchased from Cambridge 
Isotope Laboratories, Inc. CMK-3 mesoporous carbon was purchased from Nanjing 
XFNANO Materials. Carbon-coated Al foil was purchased from Guangzhou Nanuo 
New Materials Technology Co., Ltd. Super-P was purchased from Cabot. Poly 
(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) and 1-methyl-2-pyrolidone (NMP) were purchased from 
Alfa Aesar. Li metal was purchased from Tianjin Zhongneng Lithium Co. The 
reference sample of S isotope for ICP-MS analysis was purchased from National 
Institute of Metrology, China.
Preparation of 32S/34S@CMK-3 electrode. The 32S/34S@CMK-3 composite was 
prepared by mixing α-32S and α-34S powders and CMK-3 thoroughly in an agate mortar 
at a mass ratio of 194:206:600, and heating the mixture in an Ar-prefilled container at 
155 oC for 12 h. The 32S/34S@CMK-3 composite was prepared after the mixture was 
cooled down to room temperature. The 32S/34S@CMK-3 electrode was prepared by 
mixing the as-prepared 32S/34S@CMK-3 composite, super-P conductive additives and 
PVDF at a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in NMP to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was 
casted onto a carbon-coated Al foil and dried at 70 oC for 12 h. The dried foils were cut 
into electrode slices with a diameter of 10 mm with an areal mass loading of 0.8 
mgS/cm2. 
Cell fabrication and electrochemical measurements. Li-S batteries were assembled 
using the Swagelok-type cell in an Ar-filled glove box (Mikrouna, with H2O and O2 
contents of <0.01 ppm). The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1M LiPF6 in the 
mixture of DME and DOL (v:v = 4:1). 32S/34S@CMK-3 electrode was paired with a Li-
metal electrode. Glass fiber films (Whatman, GF/F, 10 mm diameter) were used as the 
separator. 150 μL of electrolyte with three GF/F films were placed between the cathode 
and Li metal. The assembled cells were rested for 4 hours and then transferred to a 
temperature-controlled chamber at 25 oC for electrochemical testing. The galvanostatic 
discharge and charge tests were carried out on a LAND system in voltage ranges of 1.5 
V to 2.8 V under 25 oC.
Sample preparation for ICP-MS tests. The cycled Swagelok-type cell was 

<<PAGE 3>>
S-3
disassembled in the Ar-filled glove box to separate cathode, anode and separator. The 
cycled Li and cathode were washed by DME for three times to remove the electrolyte. 
The cycled cathode was added into 70% HNO3 and heated at 60 °C. After complete 
oxidation, the solution is diluted to c.a. 10 μmolS L–1 with deionized water prior to test. 
The cycled Li anode and separator was dissolved in 0.01 M NaOH solution. For the 
direct measurement method, the resulting solution was directly diluted to c.a. 10 μmolS 
L–1 with 0.01 M NaOH solution prior to test. For the post-oxidation method, the 
resulting solution was added by 30% H2O2 and held at 60 oC to fully oxidize S2– and 
Sx2– to SO42–. The solution was then calibrated to a fixed volume and diluted to c.a. 10 
μmolS L–1 with deionized water and dilute HNO3 solution prior to test.
Materials characterization. Thermogravimetric profiles were collected on a Netzsch 
thermal gravimetric analyzer (TG/DTA6300) from room temperature to 800 oC at a 
heating rate of 10 oC/min under the N2 atmosphere. XPS measurements were conducted 
on Thermo Scientific ESCALab 250Xi (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The in situ Raman 
spectra were collected on a on a HORIBA spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution) with 
a 10× objective and 532-nm excitation laser. The isotopic abundance and ratio of 32S 
and 34S was examined by an ICP-MS (Agilent 8900). TOF-SIMS experiments were 
performed on a TOF-SIMS spectrometer (ION-TOF GmbH, Münster, Germany) 
equipped with a 30 keV Bi3+ primary ion gun and a 2-keV Cs+ sputter gun.

S-4
Note S1
Isotope separation factor is used to quantify the change in the ratio of two isotopes 
before and after separation. It is defined as the ratio of the isotope abundance ratio in 
the product phase to that in the initial phase and can be expressed as:
α = (XP/YP)/(XR/YR)
(S1)
where α is the separation factor, XP and YP denote the molar fractions of the two isotopes 
in the products, and XR and YR denote their molar fractions in reactants. 
As the separation factors are typically close to unity, the separation factor is often 
expressed in per mil notation to facilitate comparison, which can also be defined as:
δ = (α-1)×1000
(S2)

<<PAGE 5>>
S-5
Note S2
Assume the total molar amount of in the pristine cathode is n, and initial ratio of 32S:34S 
is 1:1. Then 32S and 34S both account for n/2.
For a cycled Li-S battery, the molar amount of 32S and 34S in the cathode are denoted 
as n12 and n14; and the molar amount of 32S and 34S in the anode and electrolyte are 
denoted as n22 and n24. The separation factor (α1) and yield (Y1) for the cathode can be 
expressed as:
α1 = n12/n14
(S3)
Y1 = (n12+n14)/n
(S4)
Similarly, the separation factor (α2) and yield (Y2) for the anode and electrolyte can be 
expressed as:
α2 = n22/n24
(S5)
Y2 = (n22+n24)/n
(S6)
Based on equation 3 to 6, we can derive:
n12 = α1nY1/(α1+1)
(S7)
n22 = α2nY2/(α2+1)
(S8)
Y1 + Y2 = 1
(S9)
Equation 9 represents mass conservation of S in a Li-S battery. According to the 
isotopic conservation of S:
n12 + n22 = n/2
(S10)
n14 + n24 = n/2
(S11)
By substituting equations 7 and 8 into equations 10 and 11, respectively, we obtain
α1Y1/(α1+1) + α2Y1/(α2+1) = 1/2
(S12)
Y1/(α1+1) + Y1/(α2+1) = 1/2
(S13)
Equations 12 and 13 can be further simplified to:
Y1 = (α1+1)(α2–1)/[2(α2–α1)]
(S14)
Y2 = (1–α1)(α2+1)/[2(α2–α1)]
(S15)
α1 = α2[(2Y2–1)–1]/[(2Y2–1) –α2]
(S16)

Note S3
For any constant 0<α1<1, by taking the first-order derivative of equation 2, we obtain:
dY1/dα2 = (1–α12)/[2(α2–α1)2] > 0
(S17)
That is to say, for any 0<α1<1, Y1 decreases as α2 decreases. Under varying conditions 
of 0<α1<1, the range of minimum values for Y1 is consistently greater than the value 
obtained at α2=1, and the maximum values for Y1 can be approached at an infinitely 
high α2; thus, we have:
Y1 > (α1+1)(1–1)/[2(1–α1)] = 1/2 + 1/(2α1) = 0
(S18)
Y1 < (α1+1)(α2–1)/[2(α2–α1)] ≈ α1/2 + 1/2
(S19)
Therefore, the allowable ranges for α1 and Y1 are as follows:
0 < Y1 < α1/2 + 1/2
(S20)
Likewise, for any constant α2>1, by taking the first-order derivative of equation S15, 
we obtain:
dY2/dα1 = (1–α22)/[2(α2–α1)2] < 0
(S21)
for any α2>1, Y2 increases as α1 decreases. The maximum value of Y2 is reached at α1=0. 
Under varying conditions of α2>1, the range of maximum values for Y2 is consistently 
smaller than the value obtained at α1=0; thus, we have:
Y2 < (1–0)(α2+1)/[2(α2–0)] = 1/2 + 1/(2α2)
(S22)
and the minimum values for Y2 can be approached when α1 approaches 1:
Y2 > (1–1)(α2+1)/[2(α2–1)] = 0
(S23)
The allowable ranges for α2 and Y2 are as follows:
0 < Y1 < 1/2 + 1/(2α2)
(S24)
