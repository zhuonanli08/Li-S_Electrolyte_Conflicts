1.Assembly and Testing of Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
The S@Fe-NC (70 wt%), Super P conductive carbon black (20 wt%), and PVDF binder (10 wt%) were mixed and dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The resulting slurry was then coated onto aluminum foil current collectors. After coating, the foils were dried under vacuum at 60°C for 12 hours to ensure complete solvent removal. The dried electrodes were subsequently punched into 12 mm diameter circular discs. The electrodes exhibited typical sulfur mass loadings of 1.3-1.9 mg cm-2. CR2032-type coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox using Celgard 2400 as the separator, lithium metal as the anode, and stainless steel cell cases. Electrochemical performance testing was conducted with current density settings and specific capacity calculations based on the sulfur mass in the cathode (1 C = 1675 mAh g-1). The cells were cycled on a Neware battery testing system within a voltage window of 1.7-2.8 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed using a CHI 650D electrochemical analyzer. CV tests were conducted at scan rates ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s-1 within a voltage window of 1.7-2.8 V. EIS measurements were carried out over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz. 2. Li2S Nucleation Experiment
To investigate the nucleation kinetics of Li2S, we employed a symmetric cell configuration comprising an S@Fe-NC cathode, a lithium metal anode, and a Celgard 2400 separator, assembled in a CR2032 coin cell. Specifically, 20 μL of a 0.2 M Li2S8/tetracarboxylic ester electrolyte solution was added to the working electrode side, while an identical volume of blank electrolyte without Li2S8 was applied to the counter electrode (anode) side. To meticulously examine the Li2S nucleation process, the cell was first discharged at a constant current of 0.112 mA until it reached 2.06 V, followed by a constant voltage discharge at 2.05 V until the current dropped below 0.01 mA, ensuring complete nucleation of Li2S.
Physical characterizations
All materials and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers (Aldrich, Canrd, Dodochem) and used without further purification. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements were conducted using a Carl Zeiss Ultra Plus scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a VG Escalab 210 instrument (VG Scientific, UK) equipped with a 300 W Mg X-ray source.
Theoretical calculations
The density functional B3LYP method within the Gaussian 09 program was employed at the 6-311G(D) basis set level, using empirically related keywords (em=gd3bj) associated with Gaussian 09. All structures were optimized and verified to have no frequencies. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), and electrostatic surface potential (ESP) were visualized using Multiwfn and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD). Thermodynamic values, including Gibbs free energy, were calculated using shermo. Intramolecular interactions were computed via the reduced density gradient (RDG) function.
