Scavenging of “Dead Sulfur” and “Dead Lithium” Revealed
by Integrated–Heterogeneous Catalysis for Advanced
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Zifeng Liu, Miao Chen, Dan Zhou, and Zhubing Xiao*
The simultaneous engineering of sulfur cathode and Li anode is critical for
electrolyte-starved high energy density Li–S batteries, in which slow
electrochemical conversions and side chemical reactions of dead sulfur are
found to be the determining factors in limiting the sulfur utilization,
corresponding to the poor reversible capacity of Li–S batteries. Herein, this
work challenges the conventional wisdom of heterogeneous and
homogeneous catalyses in Li–S batteries and proposes the concept of
integrated–heterogeneous catalysis to simultaneously scavenge the dead
sulfur and dead lithium to compensate the active materials sulfur and lithium
loss simply through adding a small amount of ZnI2 into conventional
electrolyte of Li–S cells. Regulated by integrated–heterogeneous catalysis,
over 1300 h of cycling is realized in Li||Li symmetric cells, revealing superb
compatibility of the ZnI2-incorporated electrolyte with lithium metal.
Meanwhile, the ZnI2 shows good prospects in promoting the reutilization of
dead sulfur in both theoretical calculation and experimental tests. Practically,
a high initial capacity of 1170 mAh g−1 with decent cycling stability is
achieved in electrolyte-starved and high-loading pouch cells (5.0 μL mg−1 and
5.2 mg cm−2).
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are supposed to be the most
promising energy storage system for the rapidly increasing de-
mands owing to the high theoretical energy density, as well as
low cost and environmental amity of sulfur.[1–4] However, Li–S
batteries are severely hampered by the complex multiphase and
multistep conversion reaction between elemental sulfur and Li2S
that usually leads to tardy redox kinetics, the shuttling of soluble
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPS), and a passivation of the lithium an-
ode by the formation of an unstable electrode/electrolyte inter-
face, all of which contribute to the undesired generation even en-
tanglement of inactive sulfur and inactive lithium, the so-called
“dead sulfur” and “dead lithium.”[5–7]

The mainstream strategies regarding
physical conﬁnement and chemical adsorp-
tion show limited capability to tackle above
bottlenecks mainly due to their inferior abil-
ity to promote the LiPS conversion.[8,9] En-
gineering of solid redox mediators in sul-
fur cathode for expediting the sulfur re-
duction reaction (SRR) process and sup-
pressing LiPS accumulation in the elec-
trolyte has achieved great improvements
in battery performance.[10–12] Nevertheless,
diﬀerent from intercalation-based lithium-
ion batteries, the entire discharge process
of Li–S electrochemistry is accompanied
by multiphase changing, from solid state
of elemental S to soluble LiPS and then
again to solid Li2S, in which the preceding
solid-to-liquid and the liquid-to-liquid con-
versions undergo a relatively more favor-
able pathway while the subsequent liquid-
to-solid and solid-to-solid (Li2S2-Li2S) reac-
tion processes need to surmount consid-
erable energy barrier.[13] These essences of
Li–S electrochemistry imply that the elec-
tron and energy uptakes at every conversion
stage are diﬀerent, a traditional solid redox mediator (or electro-
catalysts) in hybrid sulfur cathode with simple ability of charge
gain or loss may be incapable of propelling all the steps in SRR for
maximum impact, especially for accelerating the ﬁnal solid phase
conversion between Li2S2 and Li2S owing to the labile interface
concatenation between two or more solid compounds.[14] Recent
researches further demonstrate that Li2S2 has already existed in
the initial discharge process, meanwhile undergoing a chemical
disproportionation to produce high-order LiPS and Li2S, which
delivers no capacity contribution and leads to latent boost in LiPS
dissolution in addition to its ﬁrst occurrence in the high dis-
charge plateau and slope region.[15] That is to say, sluggish elec-
trochemical conversion and side chemical reaction of solid Li2S2
intermediate are the determining factors in limiting the sulfur
utilization and formation of dead sulfur. Although recently pro-
posed homogeneous catalysis based on tailoring the electrolyte
recipe was demonstrated to be eﬀective in regulating the sol-
ubility of solid Li2S2/Li2S products and improving the interfa-
cial stability of lithium anode, these strategies still exhibit infe-
rior eﬀect in catalyzing the reaction of shortest sulﬁde species
(Li2S2-Li2S).[16–19] Besides, most proposed homogeneous cataly-
sis systems are still plagued by the gradual accumulation of dead

Herein, we challenge the conventional wisdom of heteroge-

“integrated–heterogeneous catalysis” to realize a high-eﬃciency
and durable Li–S electrochemistry, and to simultaneously scav-

With systematic theoretical calculations and in situ/ex situ spec-
troscopic analyses, we conﬁrmed that the presence of redox cou-
ple of I−/I3
−is highly favorable for improving the utilization of
insoluble Li2S2/Li2S species and weakening the shuttle eﬀect of
soluble LiPS, signiﬁcantly mitigating the accumulation of dead
sulfur in cathode region. Meanwhile, the redox couple of I−/I3
−
not only eﬃciently eradicates the dead lithium and lithium de-
bris accumulation during the repeated Li stripping/plating pro-
cess, but also helps to recover inactive sulfur species, which con-
structs robust SEI on lithium anode upon cycling.[21,22] Beneﬁt-
ing from these integrated–heterogeneous catalysis behaviors, the
electrolyte-starved Li–S cells deliver decent electrochemical per-
formance at a relatively high sulfur loading, which is expected to
promote the practical application of energy-dense Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussions
The integrated–heterogeneous catalysis is constructed through
incorporating a small amount of ZnI2 into the conventional diox-
olane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME) electrolyte. ZnI2 is selected
due to the facts that: (1) as a nontoxic, earth-abundant, and low-
cost metal material, Zn is lithiophilic, which is conducive to re-
duce the overpotential of Li deposition and subsequently regulate
the Li stripping/plating process;[23,24] (2) I3
−can be fast formed
by the oxidization from I−anion to I2 with one more I−complex-
ing, which furnishes a faster I−/I3
−dynamic than that in other
redox couples such as Br2/Br−and Cl2/Cl−.[25]
The frontier molecular theory was employed at ﬁrst to eval-
uate the redox reaction activity of the ZnI2-incorporated elec-
trolyte components by calculating the energy level of the lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied
molecular orbital (HOMO) through Multiwfn and ORCA soft-
ware (Figure 1a).[26] It is noticed that the iodic species of I3
−and
IO3
−exhibit the lowest LUMO energy levels, that is, a higher elec-
tron aﬃnity, and is easy to reduce during the solid–electrolyte in-
terphase (SEI) formation.[22,27] Moreover, I−has highest HOMO
energy level compared to other compositions, making the oxida-
tion of I−to I3
−on the delithiated cathode side more favorable
and inhibiting the oxidation degradation of the solvent molecules
to some extent. It should be mentioned here that the IO3
−is
derived from the reaction between I3
−and Li2O as indicated in
Equation (1), which strongly scavenges the dead Li2O and sup-
presses the dendritic growth (Figure 1b). Therefore, the I−and
I3
−species recycle in the electrolyte to protect the other compo-
nents in the electrolyte from decomposition, as I3
−reacts with
dead Li and I−is oxidized prior to other component solvents and
anions. This is evidenced by the result of linear sweep votam-
metry (LSV). As presented in Figure 1c, the ZnI2-incorporated
electrolyte displays a higher onset potential and a lower polariza-
tion current, indicating a broadened electrochemical window and
reduced side reactions on account of electrolyte decomposition,
which is beneﬁcial for the compatibility between the electrolyte
and electrode. This similar phenomenon can also be found in the
previous reports.[22,28]

To further interrogate the compatibility between the ZnI2-
incorporated electrolyte and Li metal experimentally, the Li strip-
ping/plating process was ﬁrst tested using the symmetric Li||Cu
cells. As shown in Figure 1d, with the presence of ZnI2 addi-
tive, the Li||Cu cell presents superior cycling stability at the cur-
rent density of 0.32 mA cm−2, with a stable coulombic eﬃciency
(CE) of 97% over 300 cycles, meaning the signiﬁcantly enhanced
reversibility and suppressed side reactions between electrolyte
and Li metal during the Li stripping/plating process. The mor-
phologies of Li metal after 300 cycles were tested. The Li surface
for the ZnI2-free Li||Cu cells is crowded with rod-like crystals of
high aspect ratios, resulting in a thick and shaggy Li deposition
(Figure S1, Supporting Information). In sharp contrast, a much
glossier and ﬂatter surface with a rich coverage of round-edged Li
deposition is visualized, indicative of no lithium dendritic growth
after adding ZnI2 (Figure 1e). Moreover, with the presence of
ZnI2, the thickness of the Li deposited on the Cu collector is as
thin as 11.8 μm, which is far less than that in the ZnI2-free cells
(Figure S2, Supporting Information). These observations indi-
cate that the addition of ZnI2 could induce a thin and uniform SEI
layer on Li anode, which favors Li+ diﬀusion on the surface of Li
anode and reduction of the overpotential (Figure 1g,h). It should
be mentioned that Zn is thermodynamically favorable to deposi-
tion on the collector and Li anode owing to the lower standard re-
duction potential of Li (≈3.04 V versus Zn ≈0.76 V) as well as the
lithiophilicity of Zn.[23,24] The predeposition of Zn is therefore in-
clined to regulate the Li stripping and plating, which contributes
to the weakening of energy barrier of Li nucleation and growth
(Figure S3, Supporting Information).[23–24] This is further proved
by the symmetric Li||Li cell tests. As can be seen in Figure 1i, the
ZnI2-incorporated cells achieve a stable and long cycling perfor-
mance of up to 1300 h. In contrast, the ZnI2-free cell can only
operate about 650 h with a larger overpotential. All these results
demonstrate the presence of ZnI2 exerts a critical impact on Li
stripping/plating process.

Theoretical calculations were performed to further testify the
advantage of ZnI2 in Li–S electrochemistry. The systematic ki-
netic analyses based on density function theory (DFT) reveal that
the free energy for each conversion step drops, indicative of a
more energy-favorable pathway for the ZnI2-incorporated elec-
trolyte (Figure 3a). It should be especially noted that the reaction
free energy for the last step from Li2S2 to Li2S turns from pos-
itive to negative, which insinuates that the originally endother-
mic reaction becomes thermodynamically favorable. This greatly
helps promote the sulfur reduction, conﬁrming the superior-
ity of ZnI2 in raising the conversion eﬃciency of Li2Sn (n ≥2)
species, in good agreement with the above experimental results.

To implement this proof-of-concept in Li–S system, the elec-
trochemical performance of Li–S coin cells with high conduc-
tive graphene (HCG) as sulfur host were investigated due to the
nonpolar nature of HCG (see details in Supporting information
(Figures S13–S15, Supporting Information). The amount of ZnI2
additive was ﬁrst optimized, taking the strong oxidation activity
and the suppression of Li dendrites growth of ZnI2 into consider-
ation. As depicted in Figures S16 and S17 (Supporting Informa-
tion), a concentration that is lower than 0.2 × 10−3 m is insuﬃ-
cient to prevent the Li dentrite growth and further increasing the
additive concentration would possibly cause the Li surface corro-
sion. This is evidenced by the comparison in corresponding rate
performance (Figure S18, Supporting Information). In virtue of
the redox couple I−/I3
−, the cells with 0.2 × 10−3 m ZnI2 additive
show better rate performance, comparing to the cells without ad-
ditive, especially at high rates (Figure 3d). Additionally, when the
current returns to 0.5 C, the capacity recovers to 867 mAh g−1,
donating a superior reversibility. Moreover, the corresponding
charge/discharge proﬁles show depressed polarization compared
with the additive-free cells (Figure 3e, Figure S19, Supporting In-
formation), indicating faster kinetics and inhibition of polysul-
ﬁdes shuttle. The long-term cycling stability was also evaluated
(Figure 3f). With the incorporation of ZnI2, the cells deliver a high
capacity of 1047 mAh g−1 and harvest a capacity of 662 mAh g−1
over 500 cycles with an average CE of up to 99.8%, whereas the
additive-free cells tend to underperform in terms with discharge
capacity and cycling stability. When comparing to previous re-
ports utilizing other additives, the ZnI2 additive-based Li–S sys-
tem exhibits the overwhelming superiorities in terms of active
materials utilization, capacity retention, and cost (Table S1, Sup-
porting Information). The superior capacity retention as well as
the high active material utilization results from the integrated–
heterogeneous catalysis of I−/I3
−redox couple, which scavenges
the dead sulfur and dead lithium and further brings them back
to the repeated cycling. Impressively, when the sulfur loading is
sharply increased to 5.2 mg cm−2 and the electrolyte/sulfur ratio
is decreased to 5.0 μL mg−1, the cells deliver an initial capacity
of 1170 mAh g−1, corresponding to 6.1 mAh cm−2. After 100 cy-
cles, the cells still harvest 3.7 mAh cm−2 (Figure 3g), showcasing
great application prospect in advanced Li–S batteries. Consider-
ing the fact that the HCG employed is a nonpolar host material
without any elaborate mediation, we believe that there’s much
room for the improvement of electrochemical performance in
this integrated–heterogeneous system.
To further understand the superior electrochemical perfor-
mance, the Li anode surfaces reaped from Li–S cells were char-
acterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The bind-
ing energies of all the peaks were corrected through standardiz-
ing the C–C peak at 284.8 eV. It can be observed in Figure 4a,b
that the peak strength of (CH2CH2OCH2O)n, Li2CO3, CF,
and CF3 species increases while the LixC species representa-
tive of adverse reaction product is undetectable for the ZnI2-
incorporated cells, vividly conﬁrming the favorable regulation in
SEI formation.[22,28,33,34] Besides, the appreciable increase in CF
and CF3 species indicates the decomposition of TFSI−as a re-
sult of a high salt aggregation in oligoether-LiI electrolyte.[35] As
can be seen in the F 1s spectra (Figure S20a,b, Supporting In-
formation), both the contents of C–F and LiF species increase
with the presence of ZnI2, conﬁrming the formation of ﬂuorine-
enriched SEI layer.[22,33] Owing to the high surface energy, high
mechanical strength and electronic insulation, the rich forma-

Based on the XPS and in situ UV–Vis analysis together with
the electrocatalytic analysis from theoretical and experimen-
tal tests above, we can readily draw the conclusion that the
integrated–heterogeneous catalysis endowed by the ZnI2 is re-
sponsible for the durable and robust Li–S electrochemistry. With
the incorporation of ZnI2, the rapid reaction kinetics of SRR is
established, by which a fast Li2S2 transformation into Li2S in-
hibits the shuttle eﬀect as the result of disproportionation of
Li2S2. Moreover, the redox couple of I−/I3
−produced from the
disproportionation of ZnI2 scavenges the dead Li and dead sul-
fur to guarantee a more suﬃcient use of active materials, a more
uniform deposition of Li2S and Li, and a chemically stable SEI,

3. Conclusion
The integrated–heterogeneous catalysis is proposed to address
the comprehensive issues of Li–S electrochemistry and syn-
chronously scavenge dead sulfur and dead lithium simply via
adding a trace amount of ZnI2 (0.2 × 10−3 m) into conventional
electrolyte of Li–S cells. With the incorporation of ZnI2, the re-
dox couple of I−/I3
−helps to improve the utilization of insol-
uble Li2S2 species and relief the shuttling of long-chain LiPS,
which eﬀectively curtails the accumulation of dead sulfur in cath-
ode region. On the other hand, the redox couple of I−/I3
−en-
ables a stable Li stripping/plating process and eradicates the dead
lithium accumulation, meanwhile scavenging the inactive sulfur
species in SEI, which endows the Li–S system with integrated–
heterogeneous catalysis eﬀects, as substantiated by systematic
theoretical calculations and experimental studies. In virtue of
these preponderances of integrated–heterogeneous catalysis, the
Li–S batteries with practically relevant parameters (5.2 mg cm−2
and 5.0 μL mg−1) deliver decent electrochemical performances.
In light of the economy and simplicity, the motif of integrated–
heterogeneous catalysis is expected to promote the practical ap-
plication of energy-dense Li–S batteries and even other alkali
metal-sulfur batteries.
