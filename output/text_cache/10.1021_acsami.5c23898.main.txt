ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) are a promising next-generation energy storage option due to their excellent
theoretical energy density. Nevertheless, issues such as lithium dendrite growth and SEI layer instability hinder their
commercialization. This study introduced LPSP-PDMS, a polyphosphosiloxane polysulfide polymer, as an electrolyte additive to
enhance the lithium metal anode interfaces. The in situ interfacial reaction triggers the formation of a hybrid SEI, composed of
inorganic substances such as lithium chloride (LiCl) and Li−Si−O, as well as methyl-containing organic species. This structure acts
as a rigid barrier and efficient ion conductor, enabling dendrite-free lithium deposition. Among the series of materials, Li3PS8.5-
PDMS exhibits excellent properties. A lithium symmetrical cell with it has 1000 h cycle stability at 1 mA cm−2 with only 23 mV
overpotential. A lithium−sulfur full cell also has better cycling and rate capacity, retaining 710.5 mA h g−1 following 300 cycles at 1
C. This work demonstrates an effective method for designing functional electrolyte additives toward high-performance and safe
LSBs.
KEYWORDS: electrolyte additives, lithium sulfur battery, solid electrolyte interface, polysulfide-phosphosiloxane polymer,
lithium metal anodes
1. INTRODUCTION
Although lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the mainstream
choice of the current market due to their mature technology
and stable performance, their energy density has plateaued
near the theoretical limit (typically below 300 Wh kg−1). This
limitation makes it difficult to meet the urgent demands for
higher energy density, especially for applications such as long-
range electric vehicles and high-end portable electronic
devices.1,2 Consequently, there is an immediate need to create
an advanced rechargeable battery technology that delivers
superior energy storage capacity, reduced cost, and improved
eco-friendly characteristics. Among many emerging battery
technologies, lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) stand out
because of their remarkable characteristics and have become
the research focus in the field of secondary batteries. LSBs use
sulfur or its composites as the cathode material and metal
lithium (Li) as the anode. The LSBs have a theoretical energy
density of 2600 W kg−1, substantially exceeding current
commercial LIBs by 3−5 times and demonstrating significant
application promise.3−5 The theoretical specific capacity of
sulfur cathode (S8) is 1675 mA h g−1, substantially exceeding
conventional cathode materials (<200 mA h g−1). It is
plentiful, cost-effective, and ecologically sustainable globally.6,7
Simultaneously, Li metal is regarded as the best choice for the
anode material of the Li-ion (Li+) secondary battery because of
its high theoretical specific capacity of 3860 mA h g−1.8−10

Despite promising attributes of LSBs, commercial deployment
remains hindered by significant obstacles. Li metal anodes face
dendrite formation, interface instability, and polysulfide side
reactions, compromising safety and cycle longevity.11−14
Therefore, research on anode protection of LSBs has become
a key research topic in the current battery field.
As for Li anodes, a number of strategies have been adopted
to alleviate the severe problems resulting from the growth of
lithium dendrites, including the preparation of artificial solid
electrolyte interfaces (SEI),15−18 the design of the anode
electrode structure,19−21 the functional design of separa-
tor,22−24 and the use of solid electrolyte.25−28 Furthermore,
it is an effective strategy to protect Li anodes by modifying the
electrolyte owing to its simple operation, low cost, and large-
scale production.29−31 The deposition morphology of Li is
affected by the structure and composition of the electrolyte.

Through adjusting the concentration of lithium salt or
introducing specific additives, the migration of the Li-ion can
be regulated, thereby decreasing lithium polysulfide accumu-
lation.32,33 Recently, it has been found that the ideal SEl layer
has high ion conductivity, ordered nanometer-structure, and
excellent mechanical performance. These characteristics can
promote the rapid transport of Li-ion and inhibit dendrite
growth.34 Accordingly, it is necessary to design an electrolyte
additive containing both organic and inorganic components
that can satisfy the aforementioned requirements.
Polydimethilsiloxane (PDMS) is a potential candidate
material for the organic component of the SEI. Its chemical
inertness prevents side reactions with the electrolyte. More-
over, the flexible Si−O−Si skeleton can reduce the volume
expansion of the electrode and prevent the risk of lithium
dendrites penetrating the separator.35−37 However, the poor
ionic conductivity of PDMS will affect ion transport to some
extent. Lithium thiophosphate (Li3PS4) has excellent ionic
conductivity, which helps to address the problem of low
conductivity and poor discharge efficiency in LSBs. Addition-
ally, it has good compatibility in the electrolyte.38 Pang et al.
used PDMS as a flexible substrate to compact the Li3PS4 solid
electrolyte particles, thereby facilitating Li-ion transport and
enhancing deposition/stripping stability.39,40
In this paper, a polysulfide-phosphorus siloxane polymer
(LPSP-PDMS) was developed as an electrolyte additive, which
formed an organic/inorganic mixed SEI film on the surface of
the Li anode. As illustrated in Figure 1a, during Li stripping
and deposition, the methyl groups (−CH3) in the LPSP-
PDMS additive form an organic component. Meanwhile, −Si−
O−CH3 reacts on the Li anode to form an inorganic
component of Li−Si−O and lithium chloride (LiCl). Inorganic
compounds are generally distributed on the side near the Li
anode because of their excellent ionic conductivity and
chemical stability, which allow the passage of Li-ions. The
organic compositions are located in the outer layer of SEI film,
which can decrease the side reaction between anode and
electrolyte and act as a buffer. Among them, Li3PS8.5-PDMS
was the most effective one. The Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive not
only decreases the transmission resistance of Li ions but also
prevents the polysulfide shuttle. The lithium−lithium (Li−Li)
symmetrical cell assembled with the addition of Li3PS8.5-
PDMS was more than 1000 h (1 mA cm−2). It was possible to
obtain a high Coulombic efficiency (CE) of up to 98% for an
asymmetrical lithium copper (Li−Cu) cell after 280 cycles. In
addition, the lithium−sulfur (Li−S) cell with Li3PS8.5-PDMS
showed better cycling stability after 300 cycles at 1 C. All in all,
this paper provides a solution for the construction of highly
stable Li anodes, which will be helpful to the application of
LSBs in practice.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
All chemicals used in this article had not been further purified.
2.1. Materials and Instruments
Phosphorus pentasulfide (P2S5, analytically pure) was purchased from
Jizhi Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sulfur powder (S8, 99%
purity) was sourced from Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Lithium sulfide
(Li2S, 99% purity) and chlorine-terminated poly(dimethylsiloxane)
(PDMS-2Cl, 98% purity) were acquired from Merck Life Science
Network. Li foil (battery grade, 16 × 0.6 mm) was provided by
Tianjin Zhongneng Lithium Industry Co., Ltd. Li−S electrolyte
(LSE) was provided by Multireagent Network.
2.2. Preparation of the Electrolyte Containing
Polysulfide-Phosphosiloxane Polymer
The blank electrolyte, termed LSE, consists of 0.4 M LiNO3 and 0.6
M LiTFSI in 1,2-dimethoxyethane/1,3-dioxolane (v:v = 1:1). P2S5
and Li2S were dissolved in LSE and stirred steadily for 24 h to obtain
the electrolyte containing Li3PS4 additive. The Li3PS4−PDMS
additive was obtained by adding PDMS-2Cl to the obtained Li3PS4
solution and standing to make the solution clear and transparent. S8
was added to the solution containing Li3PS4 according to the molar
ratio of Li2S to S of 1:n, and the solution was stirred for 24 h to form
lithium polysulfide phosphate (LPSP, Li3PS4+1.5n). When “n” was
equal to 3 and 5, LPSP corresponds to Li3PS8.5 and Li3PS11.5,
respectively. Then, PDMS-2Cl was added to obtain the solution
containing Li3PS8.5-PDMS and Li3PS11.5-PDMS additives. At the same
time, the chlorine-terminated groups dissolved in the electrolyte in
the form of chloride ions after the reaction. The Li3PS4+1.5n-PDMS
additives were collectively referred to as LPSP-PDMS additives. The
concentration of the LPSP-PDMS additives in the electrolyte were 1
wt %. The samples used for the characterization of the additive
powder were prepared in tetrahydrofuran (THF), and the operation
steps were the same as above and dried in an oven at 80 °C. All
operations were done in an argon-filled glovebox.
2.3. Electrode Preparation
To fabricate the cathode electrode, a mixture of 70% sulfur powders,
12% super P, 8% carbon nanotube (CNT), and 10% binder (LA133)
were dispersed in solvent (n-propanol/deionized water, 1:3 volume
ratio). The slurry was coated on aluminum foil with a scraper and
dried at 60 °C overnight. The areal load is about 1.5 mg cm−2, and the
high load electrode is 4.6 mg cm−2. The copper foil was cut into 14
mm wafers, washed with dilute hydrochloric acid, and then washed
three times with deionized water and ethanol. After 10 min of
ultrasound, they were placed in the oven at 60 °C for 3 h. After that,
the electrode sheets were placed in a glovebox for later use.
2.4. Coin Cell Assembly and Characterization
The electrochemistry properties of the electrodes were investigated by
using CR2025 coin-type cells. The Li−Li symmetrical, Li−Cu, and
Li−S cells were assembled in the glovebox. A Celgard 2325
membrane was used as the separator. A 30 μL electrolyte with the
LPSP-PDMS additive was added into the separator between the
cathode and the Li foil. Before testing, the assembled batteries should
stand for 12 h. The Li−S cells was carried out by a LAND CT3001A
test system, and the voltage range was from 1.7 to 2.8 V. The
CHI660E electrochemical workstation is used for other tests. The
frequency range was 0.1−100000 Hz and the scanning rate was 5 mV.
A DDB-303A portable conductivity meter was used to measure the
conductivity of electrolytes containing different additives.
2.5. Materials Characterization
The structure of the LPSP-PDMS additive was characterized by
Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo NICOLET
IS 50) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). X-ray photoelectron spectros-
copy (XPS, PHI5000 Versa Probe III) revealed the elemental
composition of the lithium anode surface. The morphologies were
revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-IT 100). After
battery cycling tests, the Li foils were taken out, washed with 1,3-
dioxolane, and transferred under a vacuum to the specimen chamber
for SEM imaging at 15 kV.

3.2. Cycling Stability Tests Analysis for SEI Films
Li−Li symmetric cells were assembled to explore the
protective efficacy of LPSP-PDMS additives on the Li anode
and to investigate the regulation mechanism of additives on Li
ions. The voltage−time curves of distinct Li−Li cells,
measured at a current density of 1 mA cm−2, are depicted in
Figure 2a. The cell with blank electrolyte exhibited significant
voltage fluctuations and a high overpotential of 100 mV after
400 h of cycling. This was because the SEI layer was broken,
exposing the Li metal, which in turn led to rapid and uneven
growth and the disordered deposition of Li dendrites. In
contrast, Figure 2b demonstrates that the cell with the Li3PS8.5-
PDMS additive exhibited excellent stability at a low over-
potential of 23 mV for 1000 h and excellent kinetics. This
suggested that the Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive could help to form
a stable SEI layer and suppress the growth of Li dendrites. The
cells with other additives also had a better cycle stability than
the blank electrolyte. The further tests were carried out at
different current densities ranging from 0.5 to 2 mA cm−2 in
Figure 2c. As shown, the cell with the Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive
exhibited the smallest overpotential. As the current density
increased from 0.5 to 2 mA cm−2, the overpotentials reached
30, 48, and 75 mV, showing a steady increase trend. However,
other types of cells experienced a sharp increase in voltage
during the subsequent cycle. This suggested that the additive
could promote the improvement of the reaction kinetics and

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to
monitor the interfacial evolution on different electrolytes and
cycling time. The Nyquist plot conventionally comprises either
a single or a pair of coiled semicircular arcs within the medium-
to-high-frequency range and a low-frequency inclined straight
line. Starting from a nonzero intercept on the real axis (x-axis),
this intercept represents the system’s ohmic impedance (Ro),
mainly from the electrolyte and electrode materials. The
inclined line represents the Li+ diffusion impedance (Rw). The
high frequency semicircle is associated with the resistance
(RSEI) encountered by ions during their passage through the
SEI layer. Meanwhile, the semicircle at medium frequencies
indicates the charge-transfer kinetic impedance (Rct) at the
electrode/electrolyte interface. The addition of additives
obscures the demarcation between the semicircles correspond-
ing to the SEI and the charge-transfer process, and their signals
overlap to form a single semicircle, which is the interface
resistance (Ri). The impedance profile is fitted to an equivalent

In an effort to delve deeper into the charge-transfer kinetics
within the SEI layers, through meticulous analysis of the Tafel
plots, the exchange current density (I0) characteristic was
accurately determined. As illustrated in Figure 4f,g, the
exchange current density in the Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte
measured to be 0.527 mA cm−2, approximately 3-fold that of
the Li3PS4 electrolyte, which was 0.189 mA cm−2, and 8-fold
that in the blank electrolyte (0.061 mA cm−2). This result
clearly demonstrated the significantly accelerated kinetics
within the SEI layers modified by the Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive.
The enhanced electrochemical reaction kinetics had the
potential to expedite charge transfer and mitigate nonuniform
Li deposition.47,49 The electrochemical stability of electrolyte
additives is crucial for practical application. It could be
observed that the Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive exhibited a high
electrochemical window of about 4.36 V, which made it highly
appropriate for application in LSBs (Figure 4h).50 In contrast,
the electrochemical stability window of blank electrolyte was
only 4.05 V. This was because blank electrolyte led to
irreversible side reactions when in contact with Li metal.
3.3. Li−Cu Battery Performance Test
With the aim of further exploring the electrochemical stability
of the SEI film with different LPSP-PDMS additives, we carried
out a constant current charge−discharge cycle test based on
Li−Cu asymmetric cells. Figure 5a shows the CE diagram of
Li−Cu cells in three different electrolytes. It could be seen that
compared with cells using Li3PS4 and blank electrolyte, the
Li3PS8.5-PDMS-modified cell exhibited a significantly longer
cycle life. It could achieve at least 280 highly reversible cycles
at a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 (0.2 mA h cm−2). During
stable cycling, it could achieve a high CE value of >98%, which
was maintained at about 94% when approaching 300 cycles. In
contrast, Li−Cu cells with other electrolytes showed different
degrees of attenuation after 100 cycles, respectively. As shown
in Figure 5b,c, on the voltage curve of the 100th cycle under a
current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 and local magnification of
constant current charge−discharge curves, the modified cells
exhibited a higher charge capacity corresponding to the
Coulombic efficiency. From Figure 5c, it became clearly
apparent that the unmodified cell exhibited a voltage difference
of 29.7 mV between its oxidation and reduction peaks, while
the cells with Li3PS4 and Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolytes exhibited
reduced voltage differences of 25.9 and 21.9 mV, respectively.
The same result is noticed in Figure 5d. The cell in the
Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte could maintain stability for 100
cycles, which was much better than the Li3PS4 and unmodified
cells. The Li3PS8.5-PDMS cell exhibited more stable CE and
lowered voltage polarization of 28.2 mV, compared with the
cases of 45.8 and 59.4 mV in Li3PS4 and unmodified cells,
respectively (Figure 5e,f).51,52
After cycling at a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 (0.2 mA h
cm−2), the Cu foils were removed from the Li−Cu cells. SEM
was employed to explore the deposition behavior of Li+ on the
Cu foil. Within the blank electrolyte, Li that was deposited on
the Cu foil surface displayed a sparse and loose morphology
(Figure 5g). In the case of the modified electrolytes, the
surface manifested a comparatively dense and planar
morphology (Figure 5h,i). In particular, the Cu foil in the
Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte was particularly smooth, with no
obvious dendrites. This meant that the Li3PS8.5-PDMS additive
effectively homogenized Li deposition and assisted in the
formation of a stable SEI film, thereby strongly inhibiting
dendrite growth.
3.4. Li−S Battery Performance Test
After verifying the stability of the SEI film constructed by
LPSP-PDMS additives on Li−Li and Li−Cu cells, we applied
LPSP-PDMS additives to Li−S cells to further study the
electrochemical performance. Figure 6a shows the cycle
performance of Li−S cells at 0.1 C. It could be seen that the
full cells assembled with Li3PS8.5-PDMS had a first-cycle
discharge specific capacity of 1574.1 mA h g−1. After 100
cycles, it could still maintain a discharge specific capacity of
931.7 mA h g−1, which was significantly higher than that of the
unmodified cell (first cycle: 1280.4 mA h g−1; 100th cycles:
794.5 mA h g−1) and Li3PS4-modified cell (first cycle: 1324.2
mA h g−1; 100th cycles: 848.6 mA h g−1), and the CE had been
maintained at 99.8%. This implied more rapid redox kinetics
for the Li3PS8.5-PDMS-modified cell. The constant current
charge−discharge curve of the 100th cycle could clearly see a
discharge high voltage platform (QH) and low voltage platform
(QL), and the cells with Li3PS4 and Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte
provided increased discharge capacity, which were mainly due
to the extension of the low-voltage platform (Figure 6b). The
increased capacity at both high and low voltage platforms
indicated that the additives were highly beneficial for
conversion from S8 to long-chain polysulfide and then to
Li2S/Li2S2 (Figure 6c). A higher initial capacity indicated that
the additive improves the utilization of sulfur and redox
kinetics. However, the initial cycle discharge capacity usually
included a large number of irreversible activation processes,
which consumed a small amount of lithium and sulfur species
to form the initial SEI layer. Therefore, a relatively obvious
capacity decay occurred in the first 10 cycles. The rate
performance of Li−S cells was also tested. Obviously, cells with
LPSP-PDMS additives provided better reversibility than
unmodified cell. Figure 6d−f shows that cell using Li3PS8.5-
PDMS provided 1451.2, 1298.2, 1127.5, 1021.8, and 957.1 mA
h g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C, respectively. When returning
to a rate of 0.1 C, the discharge capacity of the cell was
restored to 1282.8 mA h g−1, and the capacity retention rate
was 88.4%, which was much better than that of Li3PS4 and
unmodified cells. The cell assembled in the blank electrolyte
had capacities of 805.7 and 754.9 mA h g−1 at 1 and 2 C rates,
and there was only a reversible capacity of 885.6 mA h g−1
when restored to 0.1C. All these results confirmed that the Li−
S cell using Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte had excellent reversible
capability and fast polysulfide conversion dynamics, which
could protect the Li anode more effectively and improve the
battery cycle life.

Li3PS8.5-PDMS electrolyte in the first cycle at 0.1 C (an areal
loading of 4.6 mg cm−2) was 5.1 mA h cm−2 and it still

4. CONCLUSIONS
To sum up, we have proven that the LPSP-PDMS addition in
the ether-based organic electrolyte could enhance the stability
of the Li anode and achieve superior cycle performance for
LSBs. The Li3PS8.5-PDMS outperformed nonadditive situa-
tions in SEI stability. A stable SEI was formed in situ on the Li
anode surface, reacting with it to create an organic−inorganic
hybrid layer containing Li−Si−O, LiCl, and other organics.
The SEI layer, which had a high exchange current density,
could regulate reaction kinetics and decrease the side reactions
of the Li anode and electrolyte. Therefore, the proliferation of
Li dendrites on the anode surface was efficiently restrained.
Meanwhile, a Li−Li symmetrical cell achieved a 900 h lifespan
with 240 mV hysteresis at 10 mA cm−2. A Li−Cu cell cycled
stably for 280 cycles at 0.2 mA cm−2. Not only that, when
coupled with the C−S cathode, the cell showed improved
cycling and rate performance. Hence, it provides a cost-
efficient anode interface engineering route for high-perform-
ance LSBs.
