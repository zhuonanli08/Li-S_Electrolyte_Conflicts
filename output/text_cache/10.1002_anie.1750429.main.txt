ABSTRACT
The dual regulation of sulfur redox kinetics and lithium deposition behavior represents a pivotal breakthrough toward overcoming
the performance limitations of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. Metal-based organic molecules provide an ideal solution for
rationalizing the electrolyte electrochemistry in Li–S system, featuring both nitrogen-rich properties and active metal centers.
Herein, we leverage the solubility of iron phthalocyanine chloride (FePcCl) in the electrolyte to induce phase separation. The
dissolved portion (FePcCl solute) functions as a homogeneous catalyst (Fe-Hom), whilst the insoluble fraction is uniformly loaded
onto carbon spheres to act as a heterogeneous catalyst (Fe-Het). The homogeneous/heterogeneous synergistic catalyst system (Fe-
Syg), developed through precise phase-separation engineering, enables the coexistence of mobile and immobilized active sites.
This synergy maximizes the working activity of Fe-Syg beyond the limitations of single-phase catalysts, simultaneously regulating
sulfur conversion kinetics and lithium plating/stripping behavior toward high-efficiency and robust electrodes. As a result, the
Li–S batteries incorporating Fe-Syg demonstrate favorable rate capability and operational lifespan under various conditions.
Remarkably, a pouch cell assembled with a lean electrolyte dosage of 3.5 μL mg−1 achieves a favorable energy density of 364.8 Wh
kg−1 and maintains stable cycling for 40 cycles.

Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are endowed with an ultrahigh
energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and benefit from the natural abun-
dance, environmental benignity, and low cost of sulfur, which
are advantageous compared to commercial lithium-ion batteries
[1–3]. However, their commercialization is severely hampered
related to the electrochemical behaviors of sulfur and lithium
species [4, 5]. For sulfur species, soluble lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) intermediates shuttle between the cathode and anode,
leading to rapid capacity fading [6, 7]. Moreover, the sluggish
solid-solid conversion kinetics from Li2S2 to Li2S, coupled with
the insulating nature of Li2S, result in incomplete discharge [8,
9]. For lithium species, the uncontrollable Li plating/stripping
process leads to the rampant growth of dendrites, which not
only degrades cycling stability but also poses serious safety risks
[10–12].
To address these challenges, various electrocatalysts with active
metal centers and polar surfaces have become a key focus, as
they effectively adsorb LiPSs and catalyze their conversions [13,
14]. Depending on their phase states in the reaction system, these

catalysts are categorized as heterogeneous or homogeneous [15].
Heterogeneous catalysts function at interfaces, with design strate-
gies including interface modulation [16], defect engineering [17,
18], spatial confinement [19], and size control [20]. Representative
materials encompass metal compounds [21–23] and single-atom
catalysts [24, 25]. For instance, by expanding the interlayer
spacing of Li-montmorillonite [26] and reducing the size of V2C
MXene spheres [27], enhancing the sulfur conversion kinetics
and Li+ flux. In a complementary approach, homogeneous
electrocatalysts share the same phase with the reactants, offering
intimate contact with soluble LiPSs in the electrolyte [28, 29]. The
introduction of exogenous redox mediators into the electrolyte
constructs additional electron pathways with fast kinetics, facili-
tating bidirectional sulfur redox reactions [30, 31]. Our previous
work demonstrated a binuclear copper complex formed in situ
within the electrolyte, which concurrently enabled Li2S nucle-
ation/decomposition reactions and inhibited Li dendrite growth
in a flowing manner [32]. Metal phthalocyanine (Pc) complexes
with metal–N coordination have been demonstrated to effectively
enhance LiPS conversion and stabilize the lithium anode [33–35].
Leveraging this property enables the precise design of Pc complex
catalysts offers a promising approach to overcoming the inherent
challenges of Li–S batteries. Heterogeneous catalysts are typically
immobilized within the cathode or on the separator, exposing
limited and static active sites to accelerate sulfur conversion
reactions at the solid/liquid interface and tune the Li+ transfer
kinetics [36, 37]. In contrast, incorporating homogeneous cata-
lysts into the electrolyte allows dynamic modulation of sulfur and
lithium species evolution, implying higher efficiency compared to
heterogeneous catalysts [38, 39].
In this contribution, we leverage the limited solubility of iron
phthalocyanine chloride (FePcCl) in the electrolyte of Li–S
batteries to implement phase-separation engineering. Within
this system, the dissolved fraction acts as the homogeneous
catalyst (Fe-Hom), whilst the undissolved portion serves as a
heterogeneous catalyst (Fe-Het). This synergistic engineering
overcomes the inherent limitations of homogeneous catalysts,
which are constrained by their saturation concentration, while
simultaneously addressing the low active-site utilization typically
encountered in heterogeneous catalysts. Neutron imaging, syn-
chrotron radiation small-angle X-ray scattering (SR-SAXS), and
first-principles calculations collectively reveal that the FePcCl
with the synergistic function (Fe-Syg) more effectively facilitates
Li2S nucleation/decomposition and regulates lithium deposition
than Fe-Hom and Fe-Het, hence boosting overall battery per-
formance and safety. Particularly, a 0.52 Ah-level pouch cell
incorporating the Fe-Syg achieves a high energy density of
364.8 Wh kg−1 and maintains stable operation for 40 cycles. This
work establishes phase separation as a novel pathway to engineer
catalyst systems with a homologous origin yet dual homogeneous
and heterogeneous functions, exhibiting significant potential for
Li–S batteries.

Results and Discussion
As schematically illustrated in Figure 1a,b, this study employs
phase-separation engineering to construct a homologous bifunc-
tional catalyst system for simultaneously enhancing LiPS conver-
sion kinetics and regulating Li+ flux. The procedure involves first
dispersing FePcCl into the electrolyte of Li–S batteries, followed
by filtration to separate the resulting phases. The dissolved
portion is collected as Fe-Hom, while the undissolved fraction
is uniformly immobilized onto conductive carbon spheres to
form Fe-Het. These functionalized carbon spheres are subse-
quently coated onto a polypropylene (PP) separator, generating
a hetero-catalytic layer. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM),
transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and energy-dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images in Figures S1 and S2 confirm
the homogeneous distribution of C, N, Fe, and Cl in the Fe-
Het. Furthermore, EDS mapping (Figure S3) reveals the uniform
distribution of all constituent elements (C, N, Fe, Cl, and F), sub-
stantiating the stable immobilization of compact Fe-Het spheres
on the PP separator. Figure 1c presents the x-ray diffraction
(XRD) pattern of Fe-Het, which shows the characteristic peaks
originating from both carbon spheres and FePcCl. The broad
peaks at 24.9◦and 43.3◦correspond to the (002) and (100) planes
of the carbon spheres, respectively. The sharp characteristic peak
at 27.1◦is assigned to the (002) plane of FePcCl, indicating
that the crystal structure of FePcCl is well preserved after the
loading process [40, 41]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
analysis (Figure 1d and Figure S4) reveals the surface chemical
compositions of FePcCl. The characteristic peaks of Fe 2p1/2
(723.6 eV) and Fe 2p3/2 (710.9 eV) in pristine FePcCl show slight
shifts in Fe-Het [42, 43]. Similar shifts are observed in the C
1s and N 1s spectra, which can be attributed to electron cloud
redistribution induced by π–π interactions between FePcCl and
carbon spheres. As seen in the Raman spectra in Figure 1e, the
peaks located in the range of 600–800 cm−1 correspond to benzene
ring deformations of the Pc. Peaks at approximately 1330.9 and
1512.2 cm−1 are assigned to C–H vibrations and the metal-bridged
C–N–C bond characteristic of metal Pcs, respectively [40, 44].
Notably, a significant overall attenuation of the Raman spectrum
is also observed, suggesting the interactions between FePcCl and
carbon spheres. Collectively, these findings prove that the FePcCl
is successfully and uniformly loaded onto the carbon spheres.
To determine the optimal areal loading of Fe-Het on the PP
separator surface, we systematically evaluated ion conductivity,
which is a key indicator governing interlayer functionality. Three
Fe-Het loadings were 0.30, 0.62, and 0.95 mg cm−2, yielding
corresponding ionic conductivities of 0.33, 0.53 and 0.45 mS cm−1
(Figure S5). The excessively low loading of 0.30 mg cm−2 provides
insufficient active centers to guide Li+ diffusion. Moreover, the
measured Li+ transference numbers (𝑡𝐿𝑖+) indicate that the cell
incorporating a Fe-Het mass loading of 0.62 mg cm−2 possesses
the highest value (0.47), compared to 0.40 for 0.30 mg cm−2
and 0.42 for 0.95 mg cm−2 (Figure S6). The excessively high
loading of 0.95 mg cm−2 gives rise to a remarkably increased
layer thickness, which hinders Li+ migration across the layer. In
contrast, the moderate loading of 0.62 mg cm−2 not only ensures
an adequate number of active centers on the carbon spheres
but also forms a thin layer with smooth pathways, enabling
efficient guidance of high-flux Li-ion migration. Furthermore,
the cycling performance at 2 C, shown in Figure S7, confirms
that 0.62 mg cm−2 is selected as the optimal Fe-Het loading for
Li–S batteries. The stability of Fe-Het in a practical battery was
also evaluated. We conducted post-cycling XRD characterizations
of Fe-Het layer after 10 galvanostatic cycles. As exhibited in
Figure S8, the characteristic diffraction peaks of Fe-Het remain
well-preserved, substantiating its remarkable structural integrity

The precise control of the solubility of FePcCl is critical for the
electrochemical reactions in Li–S battery systems. Along this line,
electrolyte composition and temperature as the key factors were
systematically explored. As seen in Figure S13 and Table S1, the
increase of the volume ratio of DME and DOL from 1:1 to 1.05:1
leads to the same solubility of FePcCl in the electrolyte. When
increasing the LiTFSI concentration from 1 M to 1.05 M, a similar
solubility can be observed, with the saturated concentration
around 0.26 g L−1. The effect of temperature on the solubility of
FePcCl in the electrolyte was also probed. In Figures S14 and S15
and Table S2, FePcCl exhibits a gradual increase in solubility in
the electrolyte (VDME/VDOL = 1:1, 1 M LiTFSI, and 2 wt% LiNO3) as
the temperature varies from 253 to 333 K, with the corresponding
saturated concentration changing from 0.222 to 0.278 g L−1.
As illustrated in Figure 2a, in contrast to the conventional Li–S
battery system, the immobilized Fe-Het on the separator helps to
suppress LiPS shuttling and alleviate Li dendrite growth. With
the further introduction of mobile Fe-Hom, the abundant and
uniformly exposed active sites in the electrolyte overcome the lim-
itations of fixed heterogeneous active sites, thereby enabling more
efficient Li2S deposition and enhanced Li+ transport. To verify
this mechanism, a series of electrochemical tests was performed.
In the cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles (Figure S16) obtained at
50 mV s−1, the symmetric cell with Fe-Syg supported on carbon
paper (CP) electrodes exhibits enhanced current response com-
pared to control samples, demonstrating the accelerated sulfur
redox kinetics by the synergistic catalytic effect of Fe-Syg. CV tests
were further performed within the voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V
and at scan rates ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1 to investigate
the redox behavior of sulfur species in batteries incorporating
the three catalyst systems (Figure S17). As shown in the CV
contour plots in Figure 2b–d, all catalytic systems exhibit a pair
of reduction peaks and a single oxidation peak, corresponding
to the typical transformation process of sulfur species. Notably,
the battery with Fe-Syg displays the strongest current response
associated with these three peaks among all samples, confirming
its superior electrocatalytic activity toward the sulfur reduction
and oxidation procedures. Tafel slopes were obtained from the
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves in Figure S18, measured
for Fe-Syg, Fe-Hom, and Fe-Het in Li–S batteries at a scan rate of
0.5 mV s−1 within the voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V (Figure 2e,f).
The two reduction processes occurring at approximately 2.35 and
2.10 V correspond to the stepwise conversion of sulfur species,
specifically the reduction of Li2S8 to Li2S4 and subsequently to
Li2S. The battery incorporating the Fe-Syg catalyst exhibits lower
Tafel slopes, with values of 78.9 mV dec−1 at ∼2.35 V and 28.3 mV
dec−1 at ∼2.10 V, indicating enhanced catalytic activity toward
the sulfur reduction reactions. The oxidation process observed at
∼2.02 V is attributed to the reverse reaction of Li2S4 to Li2S8, as
presented in Figure 2g. The Tafel slopes for this oxidation step
in batteries with Fe-Hom and Fe-Het are 37.7 and 56.6 mV dec−1,
respectively, both higher than that of the Fe-Syg-based battery
(28.3 mV dec−1). These results collectively confirm the favorable
bidirectional regulation capability of the Fe-Syg toward sulfur
species conversion. Furthermore, the Li+ diffusion process was
evaluated using CV tests at various scan rates. The analysis was

Fe-Syg contributes to the stabilization of Li metal anodes through
optimizing Li plating and stripping behavior. We first assembled
Li||Li symmetric cells at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a
capacity of 1 mA h cm−2 to evaluate the effects of the three catalyst
systems on Li evolution behavior. As illustrated in Figure 4a,
the Li||Li symmetric cell regulated by Fe-Syg achieves sustained
stable cycling for nearly 900 h, outperforming those with Fe-
Het and Fe-Hom. Additionally, the cell with Fe-Syg exhibits
smaller voltage fluctuations of approximately 21.0 mV (Figure
S25), indicating enhanced working stability. Furthermore, Cu||Li
cells were assembled to assess the Coulombic efficiency (CE)
of Li deposition during repeated cycling at a current density of
1 mA cm−2 and a fixed capacity of 1 mA h cm−2 (Figure 4b). It
is evident that the cell with Fe-Syg maintains a more stable CE
over 80 cycles, whereas the cell with Fe-Hom shows a signifi-
cant drop around the 60th cycle owing to unstable Li plating
and stripping. Subsequently, chronoamperometry curves and
impedance spectra were recorded before and after polarization,
as shown in Figure 4d and Figure S26. As shown in Figure 4c, the
𝑡𝐿𝑖+ of Fe-Syg-based cell reaches 0.47, significantly higher than
that for Fe-Het (0.37) and Fe-Hom (0.32), manifesting that Fe-
Syg facilitates more efficient Li+ transport. The Li plating and
stripping processes result in the formation of a SEI film on the Li
metal surface, through which Li+ migration needs to overcome
an activation energy (Ea) barrier. Along this line, temperature-
dependent impedance measurements were performed on cycled
Li||Li symmetric cells for the three catalyst systems with the
temperature varying from 298 to 318 K. As shown in Figure
S27, the cell with Fe-Syg exhibits a smaller semicircle in the
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) plot, indicative of
lower interfacial impedance. The Ea values were calculated by
fitting the data to the Arrhenius equation: the Ea value for the Fe-
Syg-based cell is 39.9 kJ mol−1, lower than that for Fe-Het (55.5 kJ
mol−1) and Fe-Hom-based cell (63.4 kJ mol−1). This reduced
Ea value further confirms that Fe-Syg facilitates Li+ transport
kinetics across the SEI film.
Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to characterize the
morphology of the cycled lithium anodes, enabling a visual
assessment of lithium dendrite growth behavior guided by the
three catalyst systems. As depicted in Figure 4f,g, the surface
profiles and 3D topographic images reveal rough Li metal
interfaces with obvious potholes and protrusions based on the Fe-
Hom and Fe-Het. In sharp contrast, the cycled Li anode based
on Fe-Syg exhibits a smoother and flatter surface, indicating
effective suppression of Li dendrite formation (Figure S28).
These observations align with the SEM characterization of cycled
lithium anodes. Cross-sectional SEM images in Figure 4h show
that after 50 cycles, the Li metal thicknesses based on Fe-Hom and
Fe-Het are greater than that based on Fe-Syg. A thinner lithium
metal layer implies reduced dendrite formation. Concurrently,
top-view SEM images in Figure S29 further demonstrate that the
Fe-Syg-enabled anode possesses a flatter surface with fewer and
smaller voids, whereas the other two catalyst-enabled anodes
exhibit rougher surfaces with more irregular cavities. These
results confirm that Fe-Syg can significantly guide the Li plating
and stripping behavior and thereby promote the generation of a
stable Li metal anode.
The spatial Li deposition behavior and the internal structures of
the Li metal anode during cycling were systematically investi-
gated using two complementary techniques with strong penetrat-
ing capabilities: neutron imaging and SR-SAXS. As illustrated in
Figure 5a, the cycled Li||Li symmetric cells were disassembled,
and lithium metal samples were collected specifically for analysis
using the two different radiation sources. Neutron imaging was
utilized to obtain macroscopic information on Li dendrites, while
SR-SAXS was applied to probe nanoscale cavity structures within
the Li metal. As above-mentioned, Li exhibits a high neutron
scattering cross-section and strong neutron absorption sensitivity,
enabling neutron imaging to non-destructively and accurately
characterize the 3D spatial distribution of lithium dendrite.
Figure 5b–d present the 2D and 3D images of lithium metal
cycled for 50 and 100 cycles based on the three catalyst systems.
A progressive deepening of image colors is observed across all
samples with increasing cycle number, indicating enhanced neu-
tron absorption due to accumulated Li and reflecting continuous
dendrite growth. In contrast, the uncycled Li metal exhibits a
uniform color distribution, corresponding to a homogeneous
Li distribution (Figure S30). Furthermore, the Fe-Syg-enabled
Li metal shows fewer blue regions and a more homogeneous
color distribution compared to Fe-Hom and Fe-Het, implying
more uniform and overall weaker neutron absorption. This
observation demonstrates that Fe-Syg effectively suppresses Li
dendrite formation. Leveraging the ultra-high x-ray flux and
spatial resolution of SR-SAXS, we further investigated the average
size of nanoscale cavities (0–100 nm) within the cycled Li
electrodes [50, 51]. As shown in the insets of Figure 5e–g and S31,
clear differences are evident in the SR-SAXS scattering intensity
matrices between cycled Li and uncycled Li. The weak scattering
signal of the uncycled Li metal indicates that cavities form in Li
metal during cycling. The radius of gyration (Rg) represents the
ensemble-averaged size of scattering entities, which reflects the
overall cavity size distribution within the Li electrode. As seen
in Figure 5e–g, we converted the gained 2D scattering patterns
into 1D scattering curves (I(q)-q), followed by fitting using the
Guinier–Porod model to extract Rg values. The resulting Rg values
follow the sequence: Fe-Syg (36.96 nm) < Fe-Het (39.44 nm) < Fe-
Hom (42.47 nm), consistent with the schematic illustrations in
Figure 5h–j. A smaller Rg value corresponds to smaller internal
cavities in the Li metal. These results collectively substantiate
that Fe-Syg enables effective regulation of Li deposition behavior,
thereby facilitating the generation of a stable Li metal electrode
interface.

3
Conclusion
In summary, this work develops a dual-phase homogeneous/
heterogeneous
catalyst
of
Fe-Syg
system
through
phase-
separation engineering from a single-source precursor (FePcCl)
for application in Li–S batteries. The strategy relies on the
saturated state of the precursor in the electrolyte, where the
dissolved fraction (Fe-Hom) acts as the homogeneous catalyst
component and the undissolved part (Fe-Het) serves as the
heterogeneous catalyst component. Notably, the mobile Fe-
Hom can diffuse into the deep regions of the sulfur cathode
to promote the deep discharge, whilst the immobilized Fe-Het
compensates for the limited active sites arising from the saturated
concentration of Fe-Hom by providing additional surface-active
sites. As such, the integration of the mobile and immobilized
dual catalytic sites significantly enhances sulfur redox reaction
kinetics and stabilizes Li plating/stripping behavior. As a result,
the battery incorporating Fe-Syg harvests high rate capacities
and favorable cycling stability. Remarkably, a 0.52 Ah-level
pouch cell with Fe-Syg achieves an impressive energy density
of 364.8 Wh kg−1 and maintains stable operation over 40 cycles.
This work presents an advanced catalyst design strategy for
high-performance Li–S batteries, offering promising potential
for commercial implementation.
