Self-Assembled Macrocyclic Copper Complex Enables
Homogeneous Catalysis for High-Loading Lithium–Sulfur
Batteries
Zhihao Yu, Xiehe Huang, Mengting Zheng, Shan-Qing Zhang, Ying Yang,* and Jun Lu*
The practical viability of high-energy-density lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
stipulates the use of a high-loading cathode and lean electrolyte. However,
under such harsh conditions, the liquid–solid sulfur redox reaction is much
retarded due to the poor sulfur and polysulﬁdes utilization, leading to low
capacity and fast fading. Herein, a self-assembled macrocyclic Cu(II) complex
(CuL) is designed as an eﬀective catalyst to homogenize and maximize the
liquid-involving reaction. The Cu(II) ion coordinated with four N atoms
features a planar dsp2 hybridization, showing a strong bonding aﬃnity toward
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) along the dz2 orbital via steric eﬀects. Such a
structure not only lowers the energy barrier of the liquid–solid conversion
(Li2S4 to Li2S2) but also guides a 3D deposition of Li2S2/Li2S. As such, with a
1 wt% electrolyte additive of CuL, a high initial capacity of 925 mAh g−1 and
areal capacity of 9.62 mAh cm−2 with a low decay of 0.3%/cycle can be
achieved under a high sulfur loading of 10.4 mg cm−2 and low
electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 6 μL mgs
−1. This work is expected to inspire the
design of homogenous catalysts and accelerate the uptake of
high-energy-density Li–S batteries.

Despite eﬀorts to improve the capacity and
cycling stability of sulfur cathode, the prac-

its infancy: current Li–S batteries fail to de-

In view of the fact that the multi-
phase conversion reaction (nS + 2e−+
2Li+ ↔Li2Sn; Li2Sn + (2n −2)e−+
(2n −2)Li+ ↔nLi2S) initiates lithium
polysulﬁde (Li2Sn; LiPSs) as intermedi-

S8 and Li2S2/Li2S,[5–7] under high load-

could accentuate the sluggish sulfur elec-
trochemistry as fewer LiPSs are accessi-
ble, stiﬂing the following conversion path-
ways and resulting in much-retarded ki-
netics. Moreover, the repeated spatially
uneven dissolution–precipitation process of the liquid–solid pro-
cess results in a rapid accumulation and large aggregates of de-
activated S/Li2S, further lowering the S utilization.
Though introducing immobilized catalysts in sulfur cathode,
such as metal oxides,[10–12] sulﬁdes,[13,14] and nitrides,[15] has been
reported to play a positive role in facilitating the conversion of
LiPSs, the limiting contact area between solid catalysts and large
S/Li2S cluster is insuﬃcient to assure high kinetics under high
sulfur loading and low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio.[17] To secure
the long-term eﬃciency of the catalyst in high-S-loading and lean-
electrolyte scenarios, an ideal catalyst should homogenize and
maximize the liquid-involving reaction. To this end, for practi-
cal Li–S batteries, catalysts should have a) large and stable “eﬀec-
tive surface area” with strong capture ability on dissolved LiPSs
and b) high catalytic activity on accelerating the reaction from
dissolved LiPSs toward solid-phase product Li2S2/Li2S. Beyond
that, it is highly promising to regulate the Li2S deposition behav-
ior to pledge high sulfur species utilization. In light of this, ho-
mogeneous catalysts capable of realizing solution-regulated re-
action pathways exhibit unique advantages over heterogeneous
catalysts in LiPSs conversion and Li2S deposition.[18,19] Most
recently, inorganic additives (such as iodide and metallocene
derivates)[20–22] and organic molecular (such as Schiﬀbases,

Compared with the inorganic additives, organic molecules show

Herein, taking advantage of the polarity of the Cu(II) and its
coordination structure, we designed an in situ self-assembled
macrocyclic Cu(II) complex (labeled as CuL) as the homoge-
neous catalyst for Li–S batteries. This CuL complex presents a
four-coordinated planar quadrilateral molecular structure: the
electron shifts from coordinated nitrogen atoms of macrocyclic
polyamine ligands to the Cu center, guaranteeing stable catalyst
ability during long-term cycling. The Cu central ions exhibit a
strong bonding aﬃnity toward LiPSs along the dz2 orbital, which
can eﬀectively weaken the bridging S-S bond, accelerate the con-
version kinetics of liquid–solid phase transitions (Li2S4 to Li2S2),
and more importantly, guide a 3D deposition of Li2S2/Li2S along
the vertical direction. Beneﬁting from this structure, with a 1 wt%
CuL in the electrolyte, the cell delivers a high discharge capac-
ity of 985 mAh g−1 and remains 766 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at
0.2C with a decay rate of 0.1% per cycle. Even under a high sulfur
loading of 10.4 mg cm−2 and low E/S ratio of 6 μL mgs
−1, stable
cycling performance with a high initial capacity of 925 mAh g−1
and area capacity of 9.62 mAh cm−2 is achieved.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of CuL
A self-assembled coordination reaction was adopted to construct
the homogeneous catalyst from Cu (II) salts and prepared
14-membered macrocyclic ligands (5,7,7,12,14,14-hexamethyl-
1,4,8,11-tetraazamacrocyclic-4,11-decadiene,
C16H32N4)
as
shown in Figure 1a. Comprehensive spectroscopy analysis
on the CuL complex was carried out to conﬁrm its chemical
structure.
The prepared CuL complex was directly dissolved in the
electrolyte (the mixed solution of dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-
dioxolane (DOL, 1:1 in volume) containing 1.0 mol L−1
LiTFSI with 1 wt% LiNO3 as an additive to form a stable
dark-purple solution after a reﬂux and recrystallization pro-
cess. A liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)
test was conducted to demonstrate the molecular formula
of the synthesized CuL. As shown in Figure 1b, the charac-
teristic peaks of m/z at 171.6, 470.1, and 623.1 correspond
to the solvated CuL as [Cu(C16H32N4)]2+ [Cu(C16H32N4)]I+
and
[Cu(C16H32N4)]C2F6NO4S+,
respectively,
which
indi-
cates the mononuclear structure of CuL complex in the
preparation.
FTIR spectroscopy aﬀords further evidence on the chemical
structure of CuL. Characteristic peaks at 3480, 3200, 2980, and
1650 cm−1 in Figure 1c can be ascribed to the stretching of N–H,
RNHR, C–H, and C≐N bonds, respectively, indicating the imine
and amine groups in CuL.[29] The broad peak at 3000–2200 cm−1
with tilted spectral baseline and the characteristic peaks at 1600–
1500 cm−1 in the spectra of ligand can be ascribed to the stretch-
ing of –NH2
+, indicating the prepared ligand crystallized in the
form of ammonium salt.[30]
The stability of CuL structure was further evaluated via the
XPS measurement. As shown in Figure 1d, the C 1s spectra
present the characteristic peaks at 286.8, 285.9, and 285.1 eV,
corresponding to imine carbon, amine carbon, and alkyl carbon,
respectively, which indicates the successful synthesis of ligands
with imine and amine structure. The additional C 1s peak at
≈284.6 eV is ascribed to the graphite carbon from the carbon
paper host. The N 1s XPS spectra shown in Figure 1e present
the characteristic peaks at ≈401 and 400 eV corresponding to
the imine nitrogen and amine nitrogen with an area ratio of
≈1:1, which coincides with the designed chemical structure of
the ligand. It is worth mentioning that both the peaks of imine
nitrogen and amine nitrogen show an evident shift of 1–2 eV
to the higher binding energy compared with the pure ligand,
manifesting the charge transfer from the coordinated nitrogen
atoms of ligand to the outer space orbitals of central Cu ion.[31]
This coordination structure and electron transfer in CuL can
stabilize the central metal ion in electrolyte for homogeneous
catalysis.
To further ascertain the chemical structure, 1H and 13C nu-
clear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments of the prepared
ligand were performed in DMSO-d6. As shown in Figure 1f;
Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the broad peak at a high
chemical shift of 8.35 ppm can be attributed to the protons
of –NH– groups. Five peaks at 3.46, 3.23, 2.62, 1.89, and
1.29 ppm can be observed which are conﬁrmed to be the alkyl
protons in diﬀerent environments.[32] And the integrals ratio
of those signal peaks is ≈1:2:2:2:3:6, which is consistent with
the designed structure in Figure 1a. As shown in Figure 1g;
Figure S2 (Supporting Information), the 13C NMR spectra show
7 peaks except for the characteristic peak of DMSO-d6 solvent,
indicating 7 diﬀerent environments of carbon atoms. The peak
at a high chemical shift of 174.7 ppm can be attributed to the car-
bon atoms linked by the double bond in imine groups, while the
peaks at 58.3, 47.2, 43.9, 41.6, 26.7, and 21.7 ppm correspond to
the alkyl carbon in diﬀerent environments.[33,34] Those observa-
tions further conﬁrm the macrocyclic structure of the ligand.
2.2. Interaction between CuL and LiPSs
To get more insights into the interaction of the designed CuL
complex with LiPSs and its stability, the density functional theory
(DFT) calculations were carried out with the optimized geometry
of CuL complex as shown in Figure 2a based on the lowest energy
principle. The charge density diﬀerence and the Mulliken popu-
lation analysis of CuL and individual ligands are performed to
prove the stability of CuL. The bond orders between central Cu
ion and four coordinated N atoms in ligand are 0.43, 0.2, 0.43,
and 0.19, respectively, based on the model shown in Figure 2b,
indicating the strong electron donation process from ligands to
central Cu ions in CuL complex and the formation of strong co-
ordination bond.
The interaction between CuL and sulfur species (S8 cluster
and polysulﬁdes Li2Sn (n = 8, 6, 4, 2, and 1)) was ﬁrst evaluated
(Figure S3, Supporting Information). With the presence of CuL,

are discouraged from highly interacting with the Cu(II) given
the existence of the methyl and methylene in the ligand, result-
ing in distortion in the optimized geometry of the adsorption
model.
Such a steric eﬀect is projected to facilitate the selective accel-
eration of the liquid–solid transition step from Li2S4 to Li2S2, the
limiting step in sulfur reduction reaction (SRR). Accordingly, the
projected density of states (PDOS) of the active center Cu ion and
S atom were calculated to understand the electronic structure of
CuL with Li2S4 absorbed (Figure 2d). The 3d orbitals of the Cu
ion overlapped the 3p orbitals of the S atom at −4 to −2 eV below
the Fermi level, indicating a strong interaction between the cen-
ter Cu ion and the S atom. After Li2S4 adsorption, the 3d orbitals
of Cu peak broadened and shifted to a lower energy level, lead-
ing to a strong covalent bonding formation with the 3p orbitals
of S, which facilitates the dissociation of the S–S bond of Li2S4
thus accelerating the liquid–solid conversion, which is in agree-
ment with the DFT-optimized structure (Figure S8, Supporting
Information).
Preferential heterogeneous-reaction catalysis is conﬁrmed by
the Gibbs free energy of the multiphase sulfur conversion (Fig-
ure 2e). The conversion steps from S8 to Li2S4 with energy bar-
riers of −3.92, −1.33, and −1.43 eV and the limited step from
Li2S4 to Li2S with an energy barrier of −1.32 and −0.52 eV,
indicating the fast homogeneous sulfur conversion but slug-
gish liquid–solid conversion in the reduction process without
catalysis. In comparison, the reaction energies of the stepwise
transformations in the system with CuL additive are respec-
tively −5.41, −1.27, −2.51, −3.10, −1.63 eV, which are ener-
getically favorable compared to those without additive. Impor-
tantly, the energy barrier of evolution from Li2S4 to Li2S2 de-
creases most signiﬁcantly (from −1.32 to −3.10 eV), indicating
an outstanding catalytic ability of CuL to break the S–S bridg-
ing bonds and the improved kinetics of liquid–solid conversion
reaction.
2.3. Catalytic Performance of CuL in LiPSs Conversion
The working potential of CuL was ﬁrst evaluated. As shown in
Figure 3a, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) proﬁle presents two re-
duction peaks at slightly higher potentials at ≈2.35 and 2.15 V
(versus Li/Li+) with CuL complex than bare sulfur (≈2.3 and
≈2.1 V for long-chain LiPSs/short-chain LiPSs and short-chain
LiPSs/Li2S redox, respectively), indicating that CuL takes prior-
ity to gain electrons, and react with both long/short-chain sulfur
species redox.
To this end, liquid–liquid conversion kinetics and liquid–solid
conversion are evaluated, separately. The liquid–liquid conver-
sion kinetics from long-chained LiPSs (Li2S6 in this test) to short-
chain LiPSs was conducted in a symmetric cell using carbon pa-
per with CuL as the electrodes. Apart from the strong anchor abil-
ity of CuL toward liquid-phase LiPSs (as shown in Figure S9, Sup-
porting Information), the CV curves recorded in Figure 3b sug-
gest that the cell with CuL exhibited a higher current of ≈15 mA,
outperforming that of the cell without CuL (≈5 mA), signify-
ing a boosted long-chain to short-chain LiPSs redox kinetics. For
the liquid–solid conversion regarding the transition from LiPSs
(Li2S8 in this test) to Li2S, the Li2S deposition tests were con-
ducted (Figure 3c,d). According to Faraday’s law, the Li2S depo-
sition capacity can be calculated based on the electrodepositing
potentiostatical proﬁles from Li2S8 to Li2S.[35] The deposition ca-
pacity increased from 130 mAh gs
−1 to177 mAh gs
−1 after the
addition of CuL. The 36% increment of the deposition capacity
implies the kinetic advantages of CuL as the catalyst. The cell
with CuL additive also exhibited a much sharper peak with a
higher peak current (0.22 mA), which suggests the accelerated
deposition kinetics and higher nucleation density from LiPSs
to Li2S during the electrochemical process. The dimensionless
current-time transient of the deposition proﬁles was further plot-
ted and revealed a 3D deposition model for the cell with CuL
additive (Figure 3e), while the conventional 2D Li2S deposition
mode was observed for the cell without additive.[36,37] A thicker
layer was uniformly covered on the surface of CNF by deposited
Li2S without aggregation under SEM (Figure S10, Supporting
Information), coinciding with the 3D deposition mode, indicat-
ing an improved solution-mediated deposition pathway by ho-
mogeneous redox mediators and a higher deposition capacity on
the vertical direction. In comparison, the disassembled cathode
from cells free of CuL additive showed severe aggregation of Li2S.
These results reveal the excellent regulation of CuL on Li2S nu-
cleation and deposition thanks to its adsorption and homogeniza-
tion properties for LiPSs in the electrolyte, which is conducive
to reducing the overpotential of Li2S conversion and improving
the sulfur utilization during the electrochemical processes. The
H-cell test conducted with the addition of CuL catalyst further
demonstrated its excellent immobilization and catalysis of LiPSs
(Figure S11, S12, Supporting Information). Overall, CuL ex-
hibits excellent catalytic properties in liquid-involving conversion
reactions.

The cycling performance of the cell with CuL additive at 0.2 C
was performed (Figure 4c), presenting a high discharge capacity
of 985 mAh g−1 and a reversible capacity of 766 mAh g−1 after 200
cycles with a decay rate of 0.1% per cycle. The cycling stability of
the cell with CuL additive was further proved by the long-term
cycling at 1 and 5 C (Figure 4d; Figure S17, Supporting Infor-
mation). The cell with CuL delivers an initial discharge capac-
ity of 780 mAh g−1 and retains 77% (601 mAh g−1) of the initial
capacity after 200 cycles at 1 C with a low decay rate of <0.1%
per cycle. It achieves an initial capacity of 660 mAh g−1 and re-
tains 535 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 5 C with a low decay rate of
0.04% per cycle (Figure S17, Supporting Information). These re-
sults suggest the stable and eﬀective catalytic ability of CuL com-
plex at varying rates.
The critical self-discharge caused by the dissolution and shut-
tle eﬀect of LiPSs is a key challenge for the practical application
of Li–S batteries. The cycling test of the cells with/without CuL
additive at 0.5 C was performed by resting the cells for 7 days. As
shown in Figure S18 (Supporting Information), high reversible
capacity retention of 92.4% of the cell with CuL additive can be
achieved after a 7 days interruption during the cycling test, while
the capacity of the cell without CuL only remains 75.9%. The
result indicates an inhibited self-discharge issue with the addi-
tion of CuL, which can be explained by an exaggerated molecular
volume and sluggish diﬀusion ability of polysulﬁdes after being
combined with CuL.
The high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte are critical fac-
tors for the practical applications of Li–S batteries, which are
still challenging and put forward a stricter requirement on the
catalytic ability. In light of this, the cycling performance of the
cells with a high sulfur loading of 10.4 mg cm−2 and low E/S
of 6 μL mgs
−1 was tested. As shown in Figure 4e, a high initial
capacity of 925 mAh g−1 is obtained and retains 750 mAh g−1
after 70 cycles at 0.871 mA cm−2 with a low decay rate of 0.3%
per cycle. The area capacity reaches as high as 9.62 mAh cm−2.
The comparison of high sulfur loading performance with other
reported work is depicted in Figure 4f and Table S3 (Support-
ing Information).[38–45] The result of the remarkably high sulfur
utilization and stable catalytic ability of CuL suggests that the
boosted kinetics of liquid–solid conversion prevents the accumu-
lation of Li2Sn in the electrolyte under such a high loading con-
dition.
To further understand the homogeneous electrocatalytic eﬀect

3. Conclusions
We have developed a homogeneous catalysis strategy in Li–S
batteries for accelerated kinetics of liquid–solid conversion and
inhibited shuttle eﬀect, by applying self-assembled macrocyclic
CuL complexes as the electrolyte additive. The intermolecular
contact between CuL additive and LiPSs homogenizes the soluble
LiPSs and leads a 3D Li2S deposition process with a higher de-
position capacity, which can eﬀectively improve the utilization of
sulfur under high loading and low E/S ratio condition. As a result,
with 1 wt% CuL electrolyte additives, the cell delivers a high dis-
charge capacity of 985 mAh g−1 and remains 766 mAh g−1 after
200 cycles at 0.2 C with a decay rate of 0.1% per cycle. Even under
a high sulfur loading of 10.4 mg cm−2 and low electrolyte/sulfur
of 6 μL mgs
−1, stable cycling performance of a high initial capacity
of 925 mAh g−1 and area capacity of 9.62 mAh cm−2 for 70 cycles
with a low decay rate of 0.3% per cycle is achieved, suggesting the
excellent homogenous catalytic ability of CuL. This work shows a
simple homogeneous catalyst design with a detailed mechanism
and is expected to solve the bottleneck of the practical applica-
tions of Li–S batteries.
4. Experimental Section
Synthesis of Materials:
12 g ethylenediamine and 10 mL methanol were
mixed in the ice bath. Then 46.5 g hydroiodic acid (55 wt%) and 30 mL ace-
tone were dropwise added into the mixture under magnetic stirring. After
further cooling in the ice bath for overnight, white crystals can be precipi-
tated. The ligand can be obtained after a ﬁltration process and washed with
acetone for several times. Then 5.36 g ligand and 2 g copper acetate were
completely dissolved in 40 mL methanol at 60 °C under heating reﬂux for
2 h. The hot solution was ﬁltered and concentrated in a water bath at 60 °C
until the formation of purple crystals. Then the solution was cooled in an
ice bath for several hours, and ﬁltered to obtain the purple copper complex
(CuL), followed by a recrystallization in methanol. Diﬀerent weight ratios
of CuL (0.5, 1, and 2 wt%) were added into the ether electrolyte (a mixed
solution of dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 1:1 in volume)
containing 1.0 mol L−1 lithium bistriﬂuoromethanesulfonimide (LiTFSI)
and 1 wt% LiNO3) to form stable solutions.
Carbon nanoﬁber (CNF) membrane was synthesized by electrospinning
and calcination using 3,3′,4,4′-biphenyl tetracarboxylic dianhydride and p-
phenylenediamine as the precursors, which was reported in the previous
work.
Material Characterizations:
The liquid chromatography–mass spec-
trometry (LC–MS) was performed using Q Exactive, Thermo with elec-
tron spray ionization source. Fourier transfer infrared radiation (FTIR)
pattern was detected by a Nicolet iS20 spectrophotometer (Thermo Sci-
entiﬁc). X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were recorded by an Escalab
250Xi Spectrophotometer. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and
13C NMR spectra were recorded at room temperature on a JEOL ECS-400
spectrometer in DMSO-d6. UV–vis absorption tests were performed with
a Hitachi U3900 Spectrophotometer. The morphologies of the samples
were observed by SU-8010 scanning electron microscope (SEM). Time-of-
ﬂight secondary-ion mass spectrometer (TOF-SIMS) was performing us-
ing TOF. SIMS 5–100 (ION-TOF GmbH, Germany). The TOF-SIMS depth
proﬁling experiments were performed by a dual-ion-beam interrogation of
the surface, alternating between an analysis beam of Bi1+ ions accelerated
at 30 keV and sputtering beam of Cs+ ions accelerated at 2 keV.
Symmetric Cell Measurements:
The symmetric cells were assembled
using carbon paper as electrodes and 0.5 mol L−1 Li2S6 solution as elec-
trolyte in an argon-ﬁlled glove box. The Li2S6 solution was prepared by
dissolving S and Li2S with a molar ratio of 5:1 into a mixture of DME/DOL
containing 1.0 mol L−1 LITFSI and 1 wt% LiNO3. The CV proﬁles of sym-
metric cells were conducted at the scan rate of 50 mV s−1 between −0.8
and 0.8 V.
Li-CP Cell Measurements:
The redox potentials of CuL were carried out
by batteries with carbon paper cathode and lithium anode. The anodic
electrolyte was composed of 20 μL DME/DOL (v/v, 1:1) with 1 mol L−1
LiTFSI and 0.1 mol L−1 LiNO3. 1 wt% CuL was added in the cathodic elec-
trolyte. The CV proﬁles were conducted from 1.8 to 2.8 V at the scan rate
of 0.5 mV s−1.
Li2S Nucleation Measurement:
0.5 mol L−1 Li2S8 solution was pre-
pared by stirring stoichiometric amounts of Li2S and S in tetraglyme by
vigorous mixing for 24 h, with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI supporting electrolyte and
0.1 mol L−1 LiNO3 additive. Lithium foils was used as anodes, carbon pa-
per was used as cathode, and Celgard 2400 membrane was used as the
separator. 20 mL above Li2S8 solution with 1 wt% CuL was applied in the
cathode side, and 20 mL electrolyte was dropped onto the lithium anode.

Theoretical Calculations:
Calculations were carried out using density
functional theory (DFT) implemented in the Cambridge Serial Total En-
ergy Package (CASTEP) software. The projector-augmented wave (PAW)
pseudopotentials and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in
the form of Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange functional were
adopted. The cutoﬀof the plane wave was set as 550 eV and a 2 × 2
× 2 k-point mesh was used in the sampling. The periodically repeated unit
cells measured 12.31 Å × 12.31 Å for CuL slabs, respectively. The samples
slabs were modeled with a vacuum width of 15 Å. In structural optimiza-
tion, all atoms fully relaxed until the force converged to 0.01 eV Å−1. The
adsorption energy (Ea) for Li2S4 with the CuL molecular was deﬁned as
Ea = ELi2S4_CuL – ELi2S4 – ECuL, where ELi2S4_CuL is the total energy of the
adsorbed system, ELi2S4 and ECuL are the energy of Li2S4 and CuL under
vacuum. The stepped reaction energy (Er) for the evolution of LiPSs cat-
alyzed by CuL was deﬁned as Er = 2ELi2Sx/2_CuL_G – ELi2Sx_CuL_G – 2ELi–
ECuL_G, where ELi2Sx/2_CuL_G, ELi2Sx_CuL_G, ELi, and ECuL_G are the total en-
ergy of the Li2Sx/2 molecule bound with CuL adsorption on the graphene
substrate, the Li2Sx (x = 8, 4, and 2) molecule bound with CuL adsorp-
tion on the graphene substrate, the chemical potential of Li, and the to-
tal energy of CuL adsorption on the graphene substrate, respectively. For
the case without catalysis, Er is deﬁned as 2ELi2Sx/2_G – ELi2Sx _G – 2ELi –
EG, where ELi2Sx/2_G, ELi2Sx _G, ELi, and EG are the total energy of Li2Sx/2
molecule adsorption on the graphene substrate, Li2Sx molecule adsorp-
tion on the graphene substrate, the chemical potential of Li, and the total
energy of graphene substrate, respectively.
