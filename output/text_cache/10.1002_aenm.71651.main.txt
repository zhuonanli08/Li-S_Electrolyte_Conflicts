ABSTRACT 
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries offer high theoretical energy density but are constrained by polysulfide dissolution, shuttling, and 
sluggish conversion. Here, a weakly solvating electrolyte comprising cyclopentyl methyl ether (CME) and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE; CME–TTE) is compared with a localized high-concentration electrolyte comprising 
diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2) and TTE (G2–TTE). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations show that G2–TTE combines 
multidentate G2 coordination with appreciable bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI− ) participation, whereas CME–TTE 
exhibits weaker, predominantly monodentate solvent coordination and stronger relative anion association. Galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique (GITT), distribution of relaxation times (DRT), square-wave voltammetry (SWV), shuttle-current 
measurements, operando ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) and Raman spectroscopy, physical-property measurements, post-cycling 
characterization, and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) link coordination to electrochemical behavior. 
Both formulations restrict extensive bulk polysulfide accumulation, but G2–TTE maintains more effective localized ion transport 
and interfacial conversion. G2–TTE cells consequently deliver capacities above 900 mAh g− 1 after 200 cycles with nearly 100% 
Coulombic efficiency. A two-cathode pouch cell delivers 600 mAh g− 1 after 50 cycles at an electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 1.37 µL 
mg− 1 . These results identify coordination connectivity, anion participation, and transport as coupled design parameters for 
quasi-solid-state Li–S batteries.

Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries stand out as promising candidates 
for advanced energy storage, offering a theoretical energy density 
( ≈ 2,600 Wh kg− 1 ) substantially exceeding that of state-of-the- 
art lithium-ion technologies ( ≈ 400–700 Wh kg− 1 based on active 
materials) [ 1, 2 ]. This benefit, combined with the earth abundance 
and low cost of sulfur, makes Li-S systems particularly attractive 
for next-generation high-energy applications. However, practi- 
cal deployment has been severely hampered by fundamental 
electrochemical barriers. Despite extensive research efforts, prac- 
tical energy densities remain limited to 300–500 Wh kg− 1 , far

100–200 cycles under typical operating conditions [ 3, 4 ]. 
Unlike intercalation-based systems, Li–S batteries operate via 
a complex multi-electron reduction mechanism that requires 
the sequential conversion of S8 into soluble lithium polysul- 
fides (LiPS; Li2 Sx , 4 ≤ x ≤ 8) and ultimately into solid Li2 S. 
This fundamental solution-mediated pathway introduces severe 
kinetic bottlenecks and multiplies opportunities for homoge- 
neous and heterogeneous side reactions [ 5–7 ]. The most promi- 
nent consequence of this mechanism is the “shut tle effect,”
whereby intermediate polysulfides dissolve in conventional liq- 
uid electrolytes and migrate uncontrollably between the positive 
and negative electrodes, acting as an internal redox mediator 
that mimics a chemical short circuit [ 8, 9 ] This continu- 
ous crossover leads to the irreversible loss of active sulfur, 
severe poisoning of the Li anode, and structural passivation of 
the positive electrode. Compounding these chemical crossover 
issues, the intrinsically insulating nature of elemental sulfur 
restricts electrical conductivity, resulting in poor active material 
utilization and sluggish reaction kinetics [ 10, 11 ]. Ultimately, 
these intertwined degradation mechanisms manifest electro- 
chemically as lowered Coulombic efficiency (CE) and severe 
capacity decay (typically 3–5% per cycle in standard ethers) 
[ 12, 13 ]. 
Various approaches have been deployed to mitigate these chal- 
lenges. Strategies for polysulfide localization include sulfur host 
encapsulation in conductive carbon matrices [ 13–16 ], advanced 
separator and interlayer designs featuring functional coatings 
[ 17, 18 ], the incorporation of protective interfacial layers [ 18–20 ] 
and heterogeneous redox mediators [ 21, 22 ]. While these struc- 
tural and catalytic interventions successfully inhibit polysulfide 
migration kinetically within the operational timeframe of a cycle, 
they still operate within fundamentally highly solvating liquid 
environments [ 23 ]. Consequently, rather than eliminating the 
intrinsic thermodynamic driving force for macroscopic polysul- 
fide dissolution into the bulk electrolyte, they largely manage 
its symptoms [ 24, 25 ]. In recent years, electrolyte molecular 
engineering has been recognized as the most direct and adaptive 
means to globally control Li-polysulfide chemistry, reaction 
pathways, and interfacial stability, offering unique advantages by 
operating within existing cell architectures and directly address- 
ing root causes of failure rather than treating symptoms [ 21, 22, 
26 ]. 
A key conceptual advance has been the recognition that con- 
trolled modulation of solvation strength can suppress polysulfide 
dissolution such that sulfur redox chemistry is fundamentally 
redirected toward quasi-solid-state (QSS) sulfur conversion [ 24, 
27 ]. In this regime, the transformation of S8 to Li2 S occurs pre- 
dominantly at solid-liquid interfaces [ 28, 29 ]. While intermediate 
polysulfides are still formed, their limited solubility confines 
their accumulation predominantly to the cathode region. Con- 
sequently, extensive bulk dissolution, diffusion and delayed 
precipitation are substantially suppressed [ 25, 30, 31 ]. 
Two complementary electrolyte-design strategies have been 
developed to restrict polysulfide dissolution: weakly solvating 
electrolytes (WSEs) and localized high-concentration electrolytes 
(LHCEs). WSEs employ solvents with intrinsically low effective 
coordination ability towards Li+ and lithium polysulfides (LiPSs). 
The reduced stabilization of solvated LiPS species increases the 
thermodynamic cost of dissolution and restricts the conventional 
dissolution–diffusion reaction pathway, while the associated ion 
pairing, solvent coordination and transport properties can also 
influence interfacial kinetics and polarization [ 27–29 ]. As shown 
in Table S1 , although cyclopentyl methyl ether (CME) exhibits a 
moderate nominal donor number (DN = 20.0) [ 32 ], its monoden- 
tate coordination geometry, together with the steric constraints 
imposed by its cyclic structure, results in a relatively low effective 
solvating power [ 27, 33, 34 ]; when paired with ultra-low-DN 
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE, DN = 
1.9) [ 35 ], the resulting CME–TTE electrolyte restricts extensive 
polysulfide dissolution. 
By contrast, localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) 
suppress LiPS dissolution through a different molecular mecha- 
nism. A high salt-to-solvent ratio incorporates most coordinating 
solvent molecules into Li+ -centred complexes, thereby reducing 
the amount and activity of free solvent available to solvate LiPSs 
[ 36–38 ]. Diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2) (DN = 19.2) [ 39 ] 
contains three ether oxygen atoms and can coordinate Li+ in 
a multidentate configuration. Under concentrated conditions, 
the limited availability of uncoordinated G2 increases TFSI− 
participation in the primary Li+ coordination environment and 
favours contact-ion-pair and aggregate formation relative to 
dilute electrolyte formulations [ 40–42 ]. A weakly coordinating 
diluent such as TTE can then reduce the macroscopic viscosity 
while largely preserving this locally concentrated solvent–salt 
structure. Thus, WSEs restrict LiPS solvation primarily through 
weak effective solvent coordination, whereas LHCEs achieve 
the same macroscopic objective through limited free-solvent 
availability and persistent locally concentrated coordination. 
Increased anion participation in LHCEs may additionally favour 
anion-derived interphase formation at the lithium-metal surface 
[ 43, 44 ]. 
Both strategies result in altered sulfur redox mechanisms, 
mitigating some of the challenges associated with polysulfide 
shuttle, while also delivering promising cycle stability and rate 
capability that are difficult to achieve in conventional systems. 
Recently, Pang et al. [ 45 ]. demonstrated a quasi-solid-state Li- 
S system achieving an initial discharge capacity of 1,408 mAh 
g− 1

0.1 C, coupled with a stable capacity of ≈ 800 mAh g− 1

current density (1 C). This performance substantially exceeds 
that of both weakly solvating alternatives and conventional 1,3-

and mechanistic understanding remain insufficiently explored. 
Explicit investigation of quantitative links between electrolyte 
systems, reaction polarization evolution, and overall improved 
battery operation is essential for predicting how molecular 
design choices translate into measurable electrochemical behav- 
ior across the full state-of-charge window, pivotal for the rational 
design of high-performance Li-S batteries [ 46, 47 ]. 
In this study, we systematically compare a weakly solvat- 
ing electrolyte (CME–TTE) and a localized high-concentration 
electrolyte (G2–TTE) as two distinct strategies for promoting 
quasi-solid-state sulfur conversion [ 46, 48–50 ]. By combining

Results and Discussion 
To determine whether these two electrolyte-design principles are 
reflected in their molecular coordination structures, MD simula- 
tions were performed for the G2–TTE LHCE and CME–TTE WSE. 
Representative local coordination clusters and simulation boxes 
are shown in Figure 1a,b for G2–TTE and Figure 1d,e for CME–
TTE. The bulk Li–O RDFs and running CNs reveal markedly 
different Li+ coordination environments (Figure 1c,f ). In G2–TTE, 
the coordination numbers at 3.0 Å are approximately 3.63 for 
O(G2) and 1.78 for O(TFSI− ), indicating that multidentate G2 
coordination coexists with appreciable TFSI− participation. By 
contrast, CME–TTE exhibits coordination numbers of approxi- 
mately 1.19 for O(CME) and 3.03 for O(TFSI− ), demonstrating 
a substantially greater relative contribution from the anion as 
a consequence of the weak and monodentate coordination of 
CME. Li–O(TTE) coordination is negligible in both systems, 
confirming that TTE is largely excluded from the primary Li+ 
coordination environment. The MD results therefore reveal that 
the two formulations restrict solvent participation through dis- 
tinct coordination structures. G2–TTE combines multidentate 
G2 coordination with appreciable TFSI− participation within a 
locally concentrated environment, whereas CME–TTE exhibits 
weaker, predominantly monodentate solvent coordination and an 
even greater relative TFSI− contribution. Thus, the distinction 
between the two systems arises from the combined effects of 
solvent denticity, solvent participation and anion association, 
rather than from the anion fraction alone. The distinction 
between the two solvation structures is retained in the presence of 
Li2 S6 . Representative environments of Li+ neighboring Li2 S6 are 
shown in Figure 1g,h . For Li+ within 3.0 Å of Li2 S6 , G2–TTE gives 
coordination numbers of approximately 1.99 for S(Li2 S6 ), 1.21 
for O(TFSI− ), and 2.02 for O(G2), compared with 2.35, 1.55, and 
0.89 for S(Li2 S6 ), O(TFSI− ), and O(CME), respectively, in CME–
TTE (Figure 1i ). Furthermore, the 2.02 G2–O contacts originate 
from only approximately 0.79 G2 molecules per selected Li+ , 
whereas approximately 0.89 CME–O contacts arise from 0.90 
CME molecules (Figure 1j ). This comparison confirms that G2 
maintains multidentate coordination near Li2 S6 , while CME–
TTE accommodates Li2 S6 through a more strongly polysulfide- 
and anion-associated coordination network. These descriptors 
provide quantitative distinctions between the modelled molec- 
ular environments experienced by Li2 S6 ; their consequences for 
dissolved-polysulfide accumulation and sulfur conversion are 
examined experimentally below. Additional bulk and Li2 S6 -local 
RDF, coordination-number and neighbouring-molecule analyses 
are provided in Figures S45–S47 . A simplified field-induced 
interfacial model of G2–TTE is also presented in Figure S48 
to illustrate qualitative electrolyte redistribution near idealized 
charged surfaces; this model is not used as a quantitative 
representation of the electrical double layer in an operating 
cell. 
The consequences of these distinct coordination structures were 
subsequently examined by galvanostatic measurements. Figure 2 
compares the galvanostatic charge-discharge profiles of Li-S 
cells employing G2-TTE, CME-TTE, DOL-DME, and DOL-DME- 
0.2 M LiNO3 under standard symmetric cycling conditions. 
Clear differences in plateau morphology are observed among 
the four electrolytes. In conventional ether systems, the upper 
voltage plateau (around 2.25 V) typically corresponds to the 
liquid-phase reduction of solid sulfur (S8 ) to long-chain soluble 
polysulfides (Li2 S8 , Li2 S6 ), while the lower, prolonged plateau 
(around 2.1 V) represents the further liquid-to-solid conversion 
into insoluble short-chain species (Li2 S4 , Li2 S2 and ultimately 
Li2 S). Although G2-TTE (Figure 2a ) exhibits two plateaus, these 
two reaction stages manifest at nearly identical operational 
potentials, presenting a severely minimized voltage gap con- 
nected by an abbreviated gentle slope. Chemically, this merging 
of plateaus signifies a deviation from the traditional solution- 
mediated pathway. Consistent with mechanistic insights from 
conceptually similar weakly solvating and high-concentration 
paradigms, the depletion of free solvent strongly suppresses the 
dissolution of long-chain intermediates, effectively forcing QSS 
conversion that bypasses massive liquid-phase supersaturation 
[ 51 ]. 
This behavior contrasts with the more conventional plateau 
distributions observed in DOL-DME (Figure 2c ) and DOL-DME- 
LiNO3 (Figure 2d ), which display a more dominant high-voltage 
contribution. In DOL-DME, the charge capacity exceeds the 
preceding discharge capacity at all three rates, with the largest 
mismatch at 0.05 C. The excess charge is characteristic of 
repetitive redox conversion of soluble polysulfides and directly 
reveals persistent shuttle behavior in the conventional electrolyte. 
Notably, the cell with the DOL-DME-0.2 M LiNO3 electrolyte at 
the extremely low rate of 0.01 C (Figure 2d ) exhibits an anomalous 
discharge capacity exceeding the theoretical limit of sulfur (1675 
mAh g− 1 ). The anomalous capacity is mainly associated with 
irreversible reduction of the nitrate additive and concurrent elec- 
trolyte decomposition during prolonged low-current operation at 
low voltage. 
The contribution from these parasitic reactions is further 
reflected by a poor initial CE of 81.2%. As widely established in 
ether-based systems, under the extended operational timeframe, 
these processes generate substantial apparent excess capacity 
[ 52 ]. Meanwhile, CME-TTE exhibits two distinct plateaus and has 
a higher overpotential. These systematic differences suggest that 
the solvation environment alters the apparent sulfur conversion 
regime even under identical operating conditions. Rather than

A close inspection of the galvanostatic discharge profiles reveals 
a fundamental shift in the sulfur reduction mechanism, high- 
lighted by the distinct presence or absence of the nucleation 
overpotential dip at the onset of the lower voltage plateau. 
In conventional ether electrolytes DOL-DME with or without 
LiNO3 , a pronounced voltage dip is clearly observed prior to 
the lower plateau. This dip is a classic kinetic signature of 
the dissolution-diffusion-precipitation pathway [ 24 ], represent- 
ing the supersaturation of highly soluble long-chain polysulfides 
in the liquid phase [ 54 ] and the subsequent activation energy 
barrier required for the homogeneous nucleation of solid Li2 S 
[ 55 ]. 
In stark contrast, the nucleation dip is absent from the discharge 
profiles of both G2–TTE and CME–TTE, indicating that their 
sulfur-reduction pathways differ from the delayed dissolution–
supersaturation–precipitation process observed in conventional 
DOL–DME electrolytes. This behavior is particularly notable 
for CME–TTE. As shown in Figure 3d , CME–TTE derives a 
substantial fraction of its initial discharge capacity from the high- 
voltage region, comparable to that of the conventional DOL–DME 
electrolyte. This sizeable high-voltage contribution confirms that 
considerable sulfur conversion occurs before the lower plateau. 
When considered together with the absence of a subsequent 
nucleation dip, the result is consistent with the initially formed 
polysulfide intermediates being retained predominantly near 
the cathode and undergoing continued interfacial conversion, 
rather than accumulating in the bulk electrolyte until delayed 
precipitation occurs. 
In these weakly solvating electrolytes, restricted dissolution 
prevents extensive diffusion and accumulation of long-chain 
intermediates in the bulk electrolyte. The surface-confined inter- 
mediates can therefore proceed directly through QSS conversion 
at the solid-liquid interface, avoiding the delayed supersaturation 
and nucleation process characteristic of conventional DOL-DME. 
This interfacial pathway maintains the initial sulfur utilization 
while eliminating the characteristic nucleation overpotential. 
To further probe the kinetic reliance of the QSS conversion 
pathway, asymmetric charge-discharge protocols were employed 
(Figure 2e,f ). Under slow-discharge/fast-charge conditions, G2- 
TTE maintains a high discharge capacity, indicating that extended 
discharge times partially compensate for kinetically hindered 
conversion by enabling prolonged interfacial transformation. 
In contrast, under fast-discharge/slow-charge conditions, the 
performance of G2-TTE converges toward that of DOL-DME, 
implying that rapid discharge imposes a kinetic bottleneck on 
solid-state or interfacial sulfur conversion, even when sufficient 
pre-charging is provided. 
Notably, CME–TTE exhibits a distinct capacity response under 
the asymmetric charge–discharge protocols. This behavior can- 
not be attributed to low bulk viscosity because CME–TTE has 
the highest measured viscosity and the lowest ionic conductivity 
among the investigated electrolytes. Its response instead reflects 
the combined effects of restricted polysulfide dissolution and rate- 
dependent bulk and interfacial transport. Although the weak, 
predominantly monodentate coordination of CME limits solvent 
stabilization of polysulfide species, the present measurements 
do not allow the contributions of ion-pair reorganization, Li+ 
desolvation, viscosity and ionic conductivity to be separated 
quantitatively [ 56, 57 ]. The influence of bulk electrolyte prop- 
erties was further evaluated through viscosity, wettability and 
apparent ionic-conductivity measurements (Tables S30–S32 and

To further quantitatively resolve the kinetic behaviors observed 
across various and asymmetric C-rates, the plateau voltages 
(Figure 3a–c ) and their corresponding capacity contributions 
(Figure 3d–f ) were analyzed. For the conventional DOL-DME 
baseline, the incorporation of the LiNO3 additive raises the charge 
plateau and produces a consistently larger full-cell voltage gap 
than the additive-free electrolyte. The lower discharge plateau 
is slightly reduced at 0.01 C and becomes comparable to DOL- 
DME at 0.05 and 0.2 C. The enhanced polarization reflects the 
combined effects of LiNO3 [ 52 ]. 
Intriguingly, the LHCE G2-TTE electrolyte, exhibits a modest 
overpotential at low C-rates, comparable to that of the LiNO3 - 
free baseline. Here, the macroscopic polarization is defined as 
the voltage gap between the median oxidation plateau and the 
lower reduction plateau. However, at 0.2C (Figure 3c ), G2-TTE 
undergoes a sharp surge in voltage hysteresis, eventually sur- 
passing the polarization of the conventional DOL-DME systems. 
This severe rate-dependent polarization is consistent with a 
QSS conversion mechanism [ 24 ]. The conventional DOL-DME 
electrolyte exhibits a low overpotential because its reactions 
proceed via a liquid-phase pathway. 
The severe rate-dependent polarization of G2-TTE is fundamen- 
tally rooted in its unique solvation structure [ 24, 28, 47 ], Unlike 
conventional ethers, G2-TTE significantly suppresses polysulfide 
dissolution. Consequently, the sulfur conversion is forced into 
a surface-mediated QSS pathway. Driving this localized, solid- 
state transformation at high currents naturally demands a higher 
activation energy to overcome sluggish charge-transfer and mass- 
transport limitations, thereby causing the observed surge in 
voltage hysteresis. 
To further quantitatively resolve the sulfur utilization and mech- 
anistic evolution across various C-rates, the total discharge 
capacities at 0.01C, 0.05C, and 0.2C were systematically deconvo- 
luted into four distinct regions: high plateau, low plateau, slope 
plateau, and non-plateau capacities (Figure 3d–f ). 
For the conventional DOL-DME-LiNO3 reference, the capacity 
distribution evolves markedly with the applied rate. During the 
initial cycle at an extremely slow discharge rate of 0.01C, up to 
49% of the discharge capacity manifests as non-plateau capacity. 
This specific capacity contribution occurs entirely within the low- 
voltage region between 1.8 V and the 1.5 V lower cutoff. This 
extensive low-voltage capacity indicates that the wide charge- 
discharge window exacerbates the irreversible reduction of LiNO3 
and associated parasitic reactions [ 58 ]. During the subsequent 
third cycle at 0.2 C, this anomalous non-plateau contribution is 
substantially reduced. The higher current shortens the residence 
time in the low-voltage region, while prior nitrate consumption 
and the associated interphase formation further limit continuous 
parasitic reduction. Together, these effects suppress the anoma- 
lous low-voltage capacity and restore a more plateau-dominated 
discharge profile. 
In contrast, the monodentate CME-TTE electrolyte shows a 
well-defined regional capacity distribution across the tested 
conditions. Its low plateau capacity dominated the overall output, 
rising from 53% at 0.01C to 65% at 0.2C. Furthermore, the non- 
plateau contribution of CME-TTE approaches zero at 0.2 C. The 
contraction of the low-voltage tail reflects increasing polariza- 
tion, which drives the cell to the lower cutoff before slower 
deep-conversion processes can proceed. Improving the cathode 
architecture through smaller sulfur particles and a more highly 
dispersed conductive network should alleviate this interfacial 
bottleneck and increase sulfur utilization at higher rates. 
The localized high-concentration electrolyte, G2-TTE, displays 
rate-dependent changes in its overall capacity and regional capac- 
ity distribution. At the intermediate rate of 0.05C, it achieves the 
highest low-plateau fraction among all systems (68%). This high 
fraction indicates an extensive deep reduction of intermediate 
species to terminal solid products (Li2 S2 /Li2 S) via a confined 
solid-state conversion pathway. At 0.2 C, the non-plateau fraction 
increases to 29%, while the low-plateau fraction decreases to 
49%, indicating that a larger proportion of sulfur conversion is 
displaced to lower potentials before reaching the cutoff. This 
redistribution is consistent with delayed formation of terminal 
Li2 S during rapid reduction. The associated increase in high rate 
overpotential arises from slower propagation of the localized 
conversion interface and increasingly restricted Li ions transport 
through progressively accumulated insulating Li2 S 
The galvanostatic discharge-charge profiles and regional capac- 
ity dissections establish clear differences in full-cell voltage 
polarization and conversion pathways. However, these bulk 
measurements only provide the global thermodynamic and mass- 
transport responses. To understand the fundamental causes of 
these rate-dependent phenomena, it is necessary to examine the 
specific reaction kinetics at the solid-liquid interface. Therefore, 
CV at varied scan rates and operando DRT analysis (Figure 4 ) 
were conducted. These techniques were used to separate the 
micro-kinetic reaction steps and to identify the individual interfa- 
cial impedance components. A closer inspection of the ultra-slow 
CV profiles (0.01 mV s− 1 , Figure 4a–d insets) unveils fundamental 
shifts in the thermodynamic reaction pathways dictated by the 
solvation environments. In the conventional DOL-DME systems 
(with and without LiNO3 ) (Figure 4c,d ), the cathodic scan 
presents two distinctly separated reduction peaks. This classical 
bifurcation signifies a highly solvated, two-step liquid-phase 
mechanism, wherein the initial formation of highly soluble long- 
chain polysulf ides is thermodynamically uncoupled from the 
subsequent liquid-to-solid Li2 S nucleation [ 59 ].

To assess the practical stability of the electrolyte at the metal 
anode interface, fundamental lithium plating/stripping mea- 
surements were strictly evaluated using standard Li||Cu half-

indicate a highly reversible lithium deposition process and a 
significant suppression of electrolyte-induced parasitic reactions 
at the anode. To further examine the interphase associated 
with this stable Li plating/stripping behavior, ToF-SIMS was 
performed on a Cu electrode recovered from a Li ∥ Cu cell after 
repeated Li plating/stripping in G2–TTE (Figures S42–S44 ). The 
two- and three-dimensional ion distributions and sputter-time

kinetics. This is confirmed by shuttle current measurements 
(Figure 5f and Figure S17 ), where G2-TTE displays near-zero inter- 
plateau leakage currents. This behavior constitutes direct macro- 
electrochemical evidence for strongly suppressed polysulfide 
crossover and the restriction of long-range active mass dissolution 
[ 66, 67 ]. Together, these decoupled half-cell and full-cell obser- 
vations validate the mechanistic picture of a solvation-regulated 
QSS architecture. The localized interfacial conversion pathways 
inherently mitigate shuttle-induced capacity decay, while simul- 
taneously improving anode compatibility, thereby highlighting 
the practical advantages of this design principle for advanced 
sulfur electrochemistry. The electrochemical observations are 
further supported by post-cycling examination of the cell compo-

therefore essential to expose and address the true interfacial mass

suffer from rapid electrolyte depletion, the G2-TTE pouch cell

the preceding kinetic characterizations, this terminal degrada- 
tion is attributed primarily to the intrinsic limitations of the 
solid-state conversion mechanism when subjected to extreme 
lean-electrolyte constraints across large geometric areas. First, as 
evidenced by the substantial voltage hysteresis (0.91 V) compared 
to coin cells, the macroscopic pouch cell operates under elevated 
internal resistance. Second, as established in the GITT analysis, 
ηcon appears to dominate the mass-transport kinetics of the QSS 
mechanism. Over prolonged cycling within the highly restricted 
electrolyte volume, localized concentration gradients gradually 
intensify. Concurrently, the inherent cyclic volume variations 
between solid sulfur and Li2 S induce progressive mechanical 
stress within the cathode matrix. The superposition of intensify- 
ing concentration polarization and structural degradation leads to 
the gradual electronic and ionic isolation of the insulating active 
materials. This loss of accessible material prematurely triggers the 
discharge cut-off voltage, manifesting as the continuous capacity 
decay observed in the late stages. Despite these geometric and 
kinetic limitations, achieving 50 reversible cycles under an E/S 
ratio of 1.37 µL mg− 1 demonstrates the feasibility of the G2- 
TTE QSS approach under lean-electrolyte pouch-cell conditions, 
although further cell engineering will likely be needed to improve 
durability.

Conclusions 
This study presents a systematic evaluation of four repre- 
sentative electrolyte formulations spanning conventional ether 
electrolytes, a weakly solvating electrolyte and a localized high- 
concentration electrolyte: two conventional highly solvating 
electrolytes (DOL-DME, DOL-DME-0.2MLiNO3 ), a WSE (CME- 
TTE) and a LHCE(G2- TTE). By integrating DRT, GITT, and SWV 
analyses, we provide quantitative evidence for how these distinct 
solvation architectures dictate interfacial mass transport and 
determine their respective kinetic limits. 
The conventional electrolyte facilitates rapid liquid-phase conver- 
sion at the cost of pronounced polysulfide shuttling. In contrast, 
both the WSE and LHCE formulations shift the reaction toward a 
QSS mechanism, strongly suppressing macroscopic shuttling and 
the supersaturation-induced nucleation overpotential. However, 
clear kinetic disparities exist between the two quasi-solid-state 
strategies. The weakly solvating electrolyte approach utilizes 
globally weak solvent-solute interactions to inhibit polysulfide 
dissolution. While effective thermodynamically, this global weak 
solvation results in sluggish bulk ion transport and provides 
a remarkably low thermodynamic driving force to extract and 
resolvate lithium ions at the interface. This severe restriction 
in interfacial ion flux fundamentally limits the reaction kinet- 
ics. Consequently, the system suffers from elevated ohmic and 
activation barriers that rapidly culminate in massive concen- 
tration polarization during operation. This overarching mass 
transport limitation prevents complete structural conversion and 
causes gradual capacity degradation over prolonged cycling. Con- 
versely, G2–TTE combines multidentate G2 coordination with 
appreciable anion participation within a locally concentrated 
environment. Although CME–TTE exhibits a greater relative 
TFSI− contribution, its weak and predominantly monodentate 
solvent coordination is accompanied by more restricted bulk ion 
transport and larger kinetic polarization. The balanced coordi- 
nation environment in G2–TTE appears to limit macroscopic 
polysulfide mobility while preserving more effective localized 
ion transport and interfacial charge transfer, as reflected by 
the transient DRT feature at log10 ( τ) ≈ − 0.4 near the 2.1 V 
conversion region. Consequently, G2–TTE sustains more effective 
QSS conversion, together with nearly 100% Coulombic efficiency 
and superior rate capability. 
Ultimately, the mechanistic superiority of the LHCE system is 
further demonstrated in scaled-up pouch cells (80 × 100 mm), 
which deliver 600 mAh g− 1 after 50 cycles under lean-electrolyte 
conditions (E/S = 1.37 µL mg− 1 ), demonstrating that the elec- 
trochemical benefits associated with the G2–TTE formulation 
can be retained under lean-electrolyte pouch-cell conditions 
under practical, electrolyte-starved configurations. Collectively,

Experimental Section 
4.1 
Electrode and Electrolyte Preparation 
Electrodes were produced using an adapted method to those 
previously reported [ 45 ] in which polyvinylidene fluoride-co- 
hexafluoropropylene (PVDF-HFP, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) was dis- 
solved in ethyl acetate (EA, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) at 60◦C at a 
ratio of 1 g per 20 mL. The active materials—sulfur (Sigma- 
Aldrich, 99%), Ketjen black EC-600JD (Nouryon, 99%), PVDF- 
HFP in EA, and Super P (SP, Fisher, 99%)—were thoroughly 
mixed with hexyl butyrate (HB, Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) at a mass 
ratio of 55:30:10:5 under continuous stirring for 2 h using a 
magnetic mixer (Camlab) at 60◦C. After incorporating the active 
material and stirring, the slurry was coated onto aluminium 
foil (MSE Supplies) at a wet thickness of 50 µm and dried in 
a controlled dry room at 60◦C for 60 min. The dried electrodes 
were cut into circular discs (12 mm diameter), and the thickness 
was measured to be 47 µm, yielding an areal mass loading 
of 1.14 mg S cm− 2 and an areal capacity of 1.90 mAh cm− 2 . 
All electrode processes were performed in an Ar-filled glove 
box. 
Electrolytes 
were 
prepared 
by 
dissolving 
lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich, 
99%) into predefined solvent combinations. The G2-TTE 
electrolyte consisted of diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2, 
Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3- 
tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) at a volume 
ratio of 1:1, with the salt ratio set to G2:LiTFSI (mol/mol) set 
to 1.5:1. For CME-TTE, cyclopentyl methyl ether (CME, Sigma-

1:1 (vol/vol) with 1 M LiTFSI. DOL-DME electrolytes used 1,3- 
dioxolane (DOL, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%) at a 1:1 volume ratio, with 1 M 
LiTFSI. DOL-DME-0.2 M LiNO3 was prepared by adding 
0.2 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3 , Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) into the 
DOL-DME electrolyte as shown in Table 2 . 
4.2 
Coin and Pouch Cell Assembly 
For coin cell assembly, Celgard 2000 separators, lithium foil (MTI, 
thickness: 0.6 mm, diameter: 16 mm, 99%), a single wave spring 
(MTI, Al-Clad stainless steel wave spring for CR2032/CR2016 
Cases), a stainless steel spacer (MTI, 15.8 mm diameter ×1.0 mm 
thickness), and coin cell casings (MTI, Al-Clad CR2032 Coin Cell 
Case) were used. Electrolyte was added at a volume of 60 µL 
per cell. For lithium plating/stripping tests, similar coin cell 
components were utilized, but the positive electrode was replaced 
with copper foil (MTI, diameter: 15 mm). 
Pouch cell fabrication was performed in a dry room (dew point 
less than − 40◦C) with semi-automated equipment to ensure 
proper alignment of electrodes. A sandwich configuration com- 
prised two single-sided sulfur cathodes and one double-sided 
lithium-plated copper foil anode (electrode size: 80 × 100 mm; 
sulfur loading: ≈ 182.4 mg). Tabs were welded with an ultrasonic 
metal welder (MTI MSK-4200 W, PI-KEM, UK): aluminium 
tabs for cathodes (welding time: 0.08 s), nickel tabs for anodes 
(welding time: 0.06 s). Electrodes were sealed in aluminium- 
plastic laminate pouches, allowing space for gas evolution during 
formation. Electrolyte (G2-TTE, 250 µL) was dispensed by vac- 
uum injection (MTI MSK-113-CP, PI-KEM, UK). the prepared 
pouch cells were placed in an Ar-filled glove box and left to stand, 
allowing the ionic conductivity and interfacial properties to reach 
equilibrium. After rest, excess pouch film, previously retained to 
allow for gas release, was trimmed using a vacuum sealer (MTI 
MSK-115A-LS, PI-KEM, UK). 
4.3

at 0.01 C for activation. Subsequently, they were tested under 
two distinct protocols within a voltage range of 1.5 to 3.5 V: 
slow-discharge/fast-charge (discharging at 0.05 C and charging 
at 0.2 C), or fast-discharge/slow-charge (discharging at 0.2 C

4.4 
Molecular Dynamics Simulations 
Classical molecular dynamics simulations were performed using 
the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator 
(LAMMPS). The second-generation General Amber Force Field 
(GAFF2) was employed to describe Li+ , TFSI− , G2, TTE and CME, 
while the sulfur-chain component of Li2 S6 was described using 
the Universal Force Field (UFF). The van der Waals and short- 
range Coulombic cut-offs were set to 1.1 and 1.2 nm, respectively, 
and long-range electrostatic interactions were evaluated using 
the particle–particle particle–mesh method. Atomic charges for 
TFSI− , G2, TTE and CME were obtained through restrained 
electrostatic potential fitting based on Becke three-parameter 
Lee–Yang–Parr (B3L YP)/def2 split-valence polarization (def2- 
SVP) calculations performed using Gaussian 16, with subsequent 
charge fitting conducted using Multiwfn [ 76–80 ]. 
For the bulk-electrolyte simulations, cubic cells with initial 
dimensions of 80 × 80 × 80 Å3 were constructed. The G2–TTE 
system contained 200 LiTFSI ion pairs, 300 G2 molecules and 
283 TTE molecules, whereas the CME–TTE system contained 100 
LiTFSI ion pairs, 429 CME molecules and 332 TTE molecules. The 
corresponding polysulfide-containing systems were constructed 
using the same electrolyte compositions with the addition of 
25 Li2 S6 units. The initial configurations were equilibrated in 
the constant-number, pressure, and temperature (NPT) ensemble 
at 600 K for 3 ns, cooled from 600 to 298 K over 3 ns, and 
further equilibrated at 298 K for 2 ns. Production simulations 
were subsequently conducted in the constant-number, volume, 
and temperature (NVT) ensemble at 298 K for 15 ns using a time 
step of 1 fs. During annealing, the pressure was maintained at 
1 bar, with temperature- and pressure-coupling time constants of 
0.1 and 10 ps, respectively.
