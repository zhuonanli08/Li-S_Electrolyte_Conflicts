Supplementary Methods: 
Preparation of electrolyte. Firstly, Cu(OH)2 nanostrands were prepared by fast mixing 
Cu(OH)2.6H2O (2.32 g in 300 mL deionized water) with aminoethanol (0.2 g in 300 
mL deionized water) and remained under ambient environment for 1 day. Then, the 
collected Cu(OH)2 film was dropped into 10 mM BTC solution (water/ethanol=1:1, 40 
mL) with 12 mg negatively charged sulfonic polymer (NSP) and stirred for 12 h. After 
filtering and drying, the Cu-BTC/PSS was mixed with 10% polytetrafluoroethylene 
(PTFE) in ethanol to prepare freestanding MOF membrane. The MOF membrane was 
pressed under 10 tons of pressure to eliminate the inter-particle pores and then 
transferred to vacuum oven (180oC) overnight for activating the MOF pores. By 
embedding ether electrolyte into MOF channel, the MOF membrane was dropped into 
1M LiTFSI/DME solution and maintained the temperature as 60oC for 1 day. Then, the 
MOF film was wiped the adhered liquid electrolyte and the dried in vacuum oven under 
60oC for 1 day. The process was repeated at three times to fully wetting all channels, 
and directedly used prior to cell assembling. The ether electrolyte was prepared by 
dissolving LiTFSI into DME solution for 1M (dilute) and 4M (saturated), respectively.  
Preparation of electrodes. S@pPAN composite was synthesized by calcining the 
mixture of sublimed sulfur with PAN (mass ratio, 8:1) in nitrogen flow under 300oC for 
400 min. The sulfur content was measured as 48% by elemental analysis. The electrode 
was fabricated by mixing S@pPAN composite, Super P carbon, guar gum/polyacrylic 
acid binder at mass ratio of 8:1:1 in water. Then the slurry was homogeneously coated 
on carbon-coated aluminum foil and transferred into air oven for drying and final 
electrode plates (11 mm in diameter) were punched out. The sulfur loading was ca. 
0.8~6.03 mg cm-2 by adjusting the thickness of slurry.  
Electrochemical measurements. The potentials depicted in this work were referenced 
to Li/Li+. The CR-2032 coin-type cells (Hohsen Corp) and pouch cells were assembled 
in Ar-filled glove box. All the cells were maintained on open circuit for 8 hours before 
the electrochemical characterizations. All the cells were characterized by battery tester 
system HJ1001SD8 (Hokuto Denko) at constant 25oC or 60oC. For the control group, 
the symmetric Li||Li, Li||Cu, Li||S@pPAN cells were assembled by Li plate (0.4 mm), 
glass fiber separator, Li plate or (Cu plate, S@pPAN electrode) with 40 uL liquid 
electrolytes. The cell with MOF-modified electrolyte, the cell was assembled by Li 
plate, MOF-modified electrolyte Li plate or (Cu plate, S@pPAN electrode) without 
extra electrolyte addition. The galvanostatic cycling of symmetric Li||Li cells was 
carried out under capacity control (1 mA cm-2/1mAh cm-2) and was evaluated by the 
voltage evolution profiles. The galvanostatic cycling of Li||Cu cells was measured by 

<<PAGE 4>>
two different protocol (See Figure S9-S10). The galvanostatic cycling and cyclic 
voltammetry measurements for Li-S cells were carried out under potential control 
(1.0~3.0 V). The pouch cells were assembled in the same operated environment by 
using MOF-modified electrolyte. The PTFE interlayer functioned as both separators to 
block between electrodes and gas diffusion layer. The inner cross structure of the pouch 
cells is also same with coin cells. The package material was Al-plastic film. The 
detailed parameters for pouch cells were provide in Table S7.  
Characterizations.  
(1) SEM characterization used to monitor the morphology change of Li metal anodes 
and cathodes. SEM images were collected from LEO Gemini Supra 35 system, working 
under condition of electron current of 50 pA with an accelerating voltage of 5kV. The 
sample (cells after cycling) were transferred into glove box and the electrodes were 
harvested from cell. Then the electrodes were rinsed three times to wash the Li salt 
precipitated on surface in dimethoxyethane (DME) solution. Prior to the final SEM 
measurements, all electrodes should dry in the glove box to fully evaporate solvent. In 
consideration of the air-sensitive samples, the sample holder with electrodes should be 
carefully sealed in the glove box and transferred to chamber within 5 seconds. We 
assumed that the morphology would not be damaged for such short-time exposure to 
air environment. Similarly, the powder samples were also carried out to obtain SEM 
images.  
(2) XPS measurement was performed using a VG scientific ESCALAB 250 
spectrometers with monochromic Al Kα Ka source (1486.6 eV) under ultra-high 
vacuum. Similarly, to prevent long-time exposure to air environment, the samples (after 
rinsing and dry) were tightly sealed into an Ar-filled bottle and then soon transferred 
into XPS chamber as quickly as possible. The XPS was equipped with etching with 
different depth to analysis the component distribution.  
(3) FT-IR measurement was carried out on IRT-5200 Infrared Microscope spectrometer 
(JASCO Corp.) with PR0450-S attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessories. Typically, 
64 interferograms were accumulated for one spectrum with a resolution of 4.0 cm-1. For 
the etching process, the cycled electrode plate was assembled onto the sputtering target 
within an auto fine coater (JFC-1200, JEOL) in a vacuum environment (10~20 Pa, 
10~15 mA current). The etching time was fixed at 10s per time. For pretreatment, the 
cycled cells were transferred into an Ar glove box once the discharge finished, and the 
cathodes were extracted from the cell and twice rinsed by DMEto wash off the 
electrolyte salt and the residual solvent, and then evaporated in a vacuum chamber, 
connected to the glove box, for ~15 min. 
(4) Raman spectra were performed by a JASCO microscope spectrometer (NRS-

<<PAGE 5>>
1000DT) and a Renishaw inVia microscope. The excitation light of an air-cooled 
He−Ne laser at 632.8 nm wavelength was focused on the electrode surface through a 
50×long working distance lens (Olympus America Inc.). The confocal slit was adjusted 
to be 4.0 μm to minimize the band broadening effect due to the contribution of non-
confocal signal. The scattered light was collected in a backscattering geometry along 
the same optical path as the pumping laser. The power of laser beam delivered to the 
electrode surface was roughly 2~10% of the maximum 30 mW laser intensity, unless 
specified, to avoid degradation to the products and/or MOF surface. The Raman 
spectrum acquisition time varied from 20~40 s with 1~2 accumulations. The confocal 
area and depth of Raman spectroscopy is micro-scale level (ca. 2.0 μm, ca. 0.5~3.0 μm, 
respectively), which is far beyond the pore size of MOF sample.  
(5) XRD measurements were used to reveal the structural S@pPAN composite. XRD 
measurements were performed on a Bruker D8 Advanced diffractometer fitted with Cu-
Kα X-rays (λ =1.5406Å) radiation. The cycled cathodes used for XRD measurements 
were also pre-treated by the same procedure. Similarly, the powder samples were also 
carried out to obtain XRD spectroscopy.

Herein, to better monitor the evolution of electrolyte solvation structure, we then 
assembled symmetric Li||Li cell with liquid electrolyte, where the cell structure was 
successively stacking Li plate, MOF membrane, glass fiber (GF) and Li plate. The 
electrolyte configuration inside MOF channels was collected from cells after 10 cycles. 
The electrochemical activation method of MOF-modified electrolyte was different from 
previous repeated soaking MOF membrane into liquid electrolyte. The potential applied 
on MOF membrane promoted ion transfer through MOF channels, which was similar 
to the heat motivated method. GF was employed as electrolyte revisor to physically 
absorb dilute electrolyte for better monitoring electrolyte solvation evolution. After 
electrochemical activation from several discharge/charge cycles, MOF membrane with 
GF was harvested for Raman spectroscopy with in-depth detection. The Raman signal 
collected from GF separator was similar to that in dilute electrolyte, which proved no 
interaction between GF separator with liquid electrolyte.

Figure S12. Space-resolution XPS spectra (Li 1s) with different etching depths (0, 
10 and 30 nm) collected from the Li anodes cycled in MOF-modified electrolyte 
(upper) and 1M LiTFSI/DME electrolyte (lower).  
 
The Li anode surface harvested from MOF-modified electrolyte was dominated 
by LiF species (56.0 eV), which was generally acknowledged to accelerate Li+ diffusion 
in the interphase. In contrast, the Li-related species from control group (GF 
separator/liquid electrolyte) mainly attributed to inorganic Li2CO3, LiOH and Li2O. 
Moreover, the etching depth indicated the SEI thickness of Li anodes from MOF-
modified electrolyte and GF were ca. 10 nm and >30 nm, respectively.

Figure S15. Voltage evolution profiles of Li coin cells with thin S@pPAN cathodes 
and different electrolytes. (a) The first cycle of cells with 1M, 4M LiTFSI/DME and 
MOF-modified electrolyte. Voltage evolution profiles of 2nd, 10th and 80th cycles of 
cells with (b) 1M LiTFSI/DME electrolyte, (c) 4M LiTFSI/DME electrolyte and (d) 
MOF-modified electrolyte. 
 
The typical voltage evolution profiles of S@pPAN cathodes (pairing with Li 
anodes) can be summarized as several points: (1) the large overpotential in the initial 
cycle can be attributed to electrochemical activation process; (2) the recovered normal 
sloping platform in the following cycles; (c) the extra capacity beyond theoretical 
specific cpacity (1672 mAh g-1) was accounted by irreversible Li insertion into anionic 
conjugated PAN backbone and film-forming process.[1] 
The S@pPAN composite exhibited superior compatibility with typical carbonate 
ester electrolyte, which not only form roubust film on cathode surface but also 
successfully inhibit the dissolution of sulfur speices. In contrast, the free DME molecule 
(or other typical ether solvents) in dilute and saturated electrolytes fail to strengthen the 
interphase of S@pPAN cathode and would dissolve sulfur species from the pPAN 
matrix. MOF-modified electrolyte effectively restrain the solvent activity by fully 
coordinated with Li+, which also benefits a benign film-forming on S@pPAN cathode. 
There, by inheriting the solid-solid phase conversion mechansim of sulfur species, the 
S@pPAN cathode can fully eliminate the dissolution of LiPS and get rid of the 
dependence on electrolyte amount.

Traditional sulfur redox reaction based solid-liquid dissolution-deposition (SSLD) 
mechanism inevitably confronted the difficult manipulation of soluble sulfur species, 
which would diffuse to the bulk of electrolyte and Li anode according to the 
concentration gradient. Then the soluble sulfur species involved a self-motivated 
reaction with Li anode, participating the SEI film forming. Some previous reports 
revealed the beneficial role of sulfide interphase, to accelerate the Li+ ion conductivity 
and diffusion. However, the uncontrolled shuttle and side-reaction on Li anode, would 
not only cause loss of active materials (capacity decline) and capacity (low CE), but 
also render thick SEI film of Li anode (capacity decline) and impair the Li capacity 
(low CE) and render thick SEI film of Li anode.  
Sulfur redox reaction based solid-solid conversion (SSC) route might confront the 
SEI aging and regeneration during the idling time. In this work, MOF-modified 
electrolyte enabled an SSC route and robust SEI film on S@pPAN cathode. Therefore, 
the CE after resting different cell potential maintained almost 100%.

Note: Comparison of sulfur cathodes  
The properties of sulfur cathodes directly influenced the electrochemical 
behaviors of Li-S batteries. Majority previous literatures of Li-S batteries focused on 
cyclo-octasulfur (cyclo-S8) cathodes, with electrolyte from ether-based electrolyte to 
the emerging solid-state electrolyte. The most conventional carbonate ester-electrolytes 
for Li-ion batteries would confront severe nucleophilic attack from intermediate lithium 
polysulfides (LiPS) and form a thick non-conductive layer on sulfur cathode. The long-
standing problem of resistive interfacial contact and the poor Li+ transfer in the bulk of 
sulfur cathode remain unsolved for the employment of solid-state electrolyte in Li-S 
batteries. The highly dependency of cyclo-S8 cathode on ether electrolyte spawned a 
wide-spectrum of innovations on host, absorption, catalysis materials. The cyclo-S8 
cathode involved a step-by-step dissolution progress, which not only activate the bulk 
sulfur (improve the sulfur utilization), but also enlarge the contact between sulfur with 
conductive carbon and liquid electrolyte (enhance the capacity). However, the 
subsequent LiPS shuttle to the bulk electrolyte and side-reaction with Li anode would 
cause significant active materials loss and low coulombic efficiency. The intrinsic 
working mechanism can be attributed to the nano or micro-scale dispersion of sulfur 
with conductive carbon matrix, which served as electronic transfer for redox reaction. 
Some minority works on small sulfur cathodes reduced the nucleophilic attack to 
carbonate ester electrolyte, owing to its redox reaction confined on carbon matrix.  
The sulfur atom in S@pPAN composite also favors a mono-dispersed molecular 
sulfur structure, which not only elevate its conductivity for Li+ transfer in the bulk 
materials, but also reduced nucleophilic attack to carbonate ester electrolytes. The 

<<PAGE 27>>
poly(acrylonitrile) (PAN) matrix provided a mutual physical segregation between sulfur 
atom and conductive matrix. The solid-solid conversion mechanism of S@pPAN can 
be attributed to mainly the unique cathode structure and its benign film-forming from 
carbonate ester electrolyte. Intermediate lithiated sulfur species has very limited 
solubility in carbonate ester electrolytes and minor dissolved species would react with 
C=O groups to participate into the interphase. The LiF-rich interphase was proved 
effective to accelerate the Li+ transfer in the interphase.3, 4 The above detailed 
discussion on sulfur cathodes and their working mechanism were summarized in Table 
S1. 
In this work, by tailoring the electrolyte solvation structure and suppressing the 
solvent activity, we harvest a novel electrolyte to compatible with S@pPAN cathode. 
This cation-solvent fully coordinated electrolyte favors a stable CEI on cathode surface 
and inherit the SSC conversion of S@pPAN cathode.

Note: The energy density calculation of this work was based on electrode mass 
(anode+cathode) and electrolyte. Benefitting from limited Li anode usage and lean 
electrolyte design, the energy density reached a satisfactory value (394 W h kg-1). 
Herein, we further discussed the correlation of several important parameters and 
pointed out the promising future of high-energy-density Li-S@pPAN battery system. 
Firstly, the sulfur loading used in this work is 6.0 mg cm-2, which is much lower than 
that in commercial LiCoO2 electrode (30 mg cm-2 each side). The mass ratio of 
electrochemical inactive components such as binder and conductive carbon can be 
further reduced. Further boosting the cathode loading by advanced electrode 
preparation technique remains a hot research topic, especially from engineering aspect. 
Secondly, the problem of irreversible Li loss during the initial cycle (low initial CE of 
cell) should be addressed, for instance, by replacing lithiated S@pPAN cathode in the 
future work. Pretreating cathode by chemical method would be a promising pathway 
owing to its high-efficiency and low-costs. Thirdly, owing to the limited lab-scale cell 
fabrication technique, one layer electrode design was performed in this work. The 
energy-density of pouch cell can be further improved by stacking the layer of electrodes.
