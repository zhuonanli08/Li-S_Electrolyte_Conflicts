A B S T R A C T  
Lithium‒sulfur (Li‒S) batteries are one of the most promising energy storage devices to achieve practical energy 
density of 400 Wh kg−1 beyond lithium-ion batteries. However, limited understanding on the failure mechanism 
of high-energy-density Li‒S batteries restricts their further development. Herein, systematic failure analysis on 
400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells is conducted. The failure of the pouch cells originates from the peak-shaped 
polarization at the second discharge plateau that renders rapid capacity decay. The cycled cathodes and an­
odes maintain their structural integrity and afford considerable residual capacity. After reinjecting electrolyte 
into the failed pouch cells, the peak-shaped polarization diminishes and the discharge capacity recovers. 
Consequently, electrolyte exhaustion is identified as the key limiting factor that renders cell failure. This work 
highlights electrolyte exhaustion as the key issue that limits the cycling lifespan of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells 
and provides guidelines for designing targeted promotion strategies to achieve long-cycling Li‒S batteries.   
1. Introduction 
Rechargeable batteries play a key role in large-scale application of 
renewable electricity [1,2]. Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have dominated 
the global market of rechargeable batteries and contributed to conve­
nient energy usage in daily life such as mobile phones, portable elec­
tronics, and electric vehicles [3,4]. However, the current LIBs have 
reached their energy density limitation and can hardly achieve an actual 
energy density beyond 400 Wh kg−1 [5–7]. Therefore, advanced 
rechargeable batteries with higher energy density are highly required to 
meet the growing demand for energy storage for a wide range of ap­
plications [8,9]. 
Lithium‒sulfur (Li‒S) batteries are considered as one of the most 
promising rechargeable batteries to achieve practical energy density 
beyond 400 Wh kg−1 [10–12]. Typical Li‒S batteries employ sulfur 
cathodes and lithium anodes and rely on the multi-electron reactions 
and multi-phase transitions between solid sulfur, dissolved lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs), and solid lithium sulfide (Li2S) to deliver and store 
energy [13–15]. In the past decade, great advances have been achieved 
in Li‒S batteries such as high specific capacity of 1660 mAh g−1, long 
cycling lifespan of 1000 cycles, and high cycling rate of 40 C in coin cell 
configuration [16–18]. 550 Wh kg−1 Li‒S primary pouch cells and 
rechargeable 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells have also been reported [19, 
20]. Nevertheless, the cycling lifespan of high-energy-density Li‒S 
pouch cells is far from satisfaction. It is highly challenging to achieve 
100 stable cycles in 300 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells, and there has not 
been a report of more than 50 cycles for 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells 
[14,21]. 
Therefore, 
understanding 
the 
failure 
mechanism 
of 
high-energy-density Li‒S pouch cells is of great significance to con­
structing practical high-performance Li–S batteries. 
Failure analysis is an important method to understand the failure 
mechanism and to propose targeted promotion strategies toward high- 
performance rechargeable batteries, which has been validated to be 
effective for LIBs and lithium metal batteries [22–28]. For Li‒S batte­
ries, Cheng et al. pioneeringly evaluated the performance of cycled 
cathodes and anodes and pointed out that the powdering of lithium 
anodes led to the failure of 300 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells [29]. Kong 
et al. discovered the nonuniform redistribution process of Li‒S pouch 
cells during cycling, which led to insufficient utilization of active ma­
terials [30]. Shi et al. further identified that the heterogeneity of active 
materials came from the heterogeneity of electrolyte distribution [31]. 
Very recently, Ye et al. evaluated the first cycle of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S 
pouch cells and found massive LiPSs in electrolyte at the end of 
discharge or charge [32]. Despite the above progress, there has not been

Understanding the failure mechanism of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells 
is expected to afford insightful guidance on rationally designing targeted 
promotion strategies to realize high-energy-density and long-cycling 
Li–S batteries. 
In this work, systematic failure analysis on 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch 
cells is conducted to reveal the underlying failure mechanism. The 
failure of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells originates from the dramatically 
increased peak-shaped polarization at the second discharge plateau that 
renders rapid capacity decay. The cycled cathodes and anodes main­
tained their structural integrity and afford considerable residual ca­
pacity and redox kinetics to support the subsequent cycles in 
reassembled coin cells. After injecting electrolyte into the failed Li‒S 
pouch cells, the peak-shaped polarization at the second discharge 
plateau diminishes and the discharge capacity recovers to 1000 mAh 
g−1. Therefore, electrolyte exhaustion is identified as the key limiting 
factor that renders the failure of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells. This 
work reveals the unique failure mechanism of high-energy-density Li–S 
batteries and identifies electrolyte exhaustion as the main limiting fac­
tor, providing essential guidance for targeted promotion strategy design 
to achieve high-energy-density and long-cycling Li‒S batteries. 
2. Results and discussion 
Li‒S pouch cells at 400 Wh kg−1 level were designed and fabricated. 
Eight double-side cathodes and nine double-side anodes were assembled 
to afford the capacity of the pouch cells at 4 Ah level (Fig. 1a). The size of 
the electrode was 4.0 cm in width and 7.0 cm in length. The areal sulfur 
loading of the cathodes was 7.3 ± 0.2 mgS cm−2 and the sulfur content 
was 63 wt.%. The electrolyte was conventional ether-based liquid 
electrolyte and the mass ratio of electrolyte to sulfur (E/S ratio) was set 
as 3.0 gelectrolyte gS
−1. The thickness of the anode was 75 μm and the 
capacity ratio of negative to positive electrode (N/P ratio) was designed 
to be 1.5, which was calculated based on assuming the discharge ca­
pacity was 1350 mAh gS
−1. The total weight of the tab and package was 
2.10 g. Following the above design parameters, 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch 
cells were fabricated (Fig. 1b), and the mass of each component in the 
Li‒S pouch cell is listed (Table S1). 
The cycling performance and failure behavior of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S 
pouch cells were evaluated. The Li‒S pouch cells were rested for 12.0 h 
and then galvanostatically cycled at the current density of 25.0 mA gS
−1 
(0.025 C, 1 C = 1000 mA gS
−1). The discharge curve of the 1st cycle 
affords well-defined first and second discharge plateaus with an average 
second discharge plateau voltage of 2.11 V and a discharge capacity of 
1365 mAh g−1. The total discharge capacity achieves 4.6 Ah and the 
total mass of the pouch cell is 21.63 g. Therefore, the actual energy 
density of the Li‒S pouch cell is 451 Wh kg−1 at the first cycle based on 
all the components of the pouch cell (Table S2). A decrease of energy 
density is observed in the initial three cycles (Fig. 1c), which is suggested 
as a result of irreversible dissolution of LiPSs in electrolyte. Then the 
discharge capacity is maintained at around 1200 mAh g−1 in the 
following 10 cycles corresponding to an actual energy density of 400 Wh 
kg−1, and the Coulombic efficiency (CE) keeps at a high value of 95%. 
The polarization curves provide further information on the cycling 
characteristics of the pouch cell (Fig. 1d). At the 4th cycle, an extra peak- 
shaped polarization appears in the front of the second discharge plateau 
and grows larger at the 12th cycle, while the average voltage of the first 
discharge plateau does not change obviously. The energy density of the 
Li‒S pouch cell starts to decrease at the 14th cycle as well as the CE, 
indicating the pouch cell enters the failure region. When the pouch cell 
is cycled to the 18th cycle, its energy density decreases sharply from 
380 Wh kg−1 at the 14th cycle to 310 Wh kg−1, and the discharge curve 
at the 18th cycle is severely distorted. The discharge polarization in­
creases in full range and especially for the peak-shaped polarization on 
the second discharge plateau. Accordingly, the discharge capacity de­
creases to around 1000 mAh g−1 at the 18th cycle. The complete failure 
of the Li‒S pouch cell happens at the 19th cycle in which the discharge 
voltage drops to the cut-off voltage of 1.70 V rapidly to render a sudden 
drop of the energy density to less than 100 Wh kg−1. To sum up, the 
failure behavior of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells is characterized as the 
rapidly increased peak-shaped polarization at the second discharge 
plateau that triggers the sudden drop of the energy density along with 
cycling. 
To analyze the underlying failure mechanism, the galvanostatic cy­
cles of the pouch cells were stopped at 1.70 V (the discharged state) or 
2.55 V (the charged state) respectively when the pouch cells failed 
following the above characteristics (Figs. S1 and S2). The failed pouch

out for detailed characterization. To reveal the actual surface of the 
cycled electrodes, the cycled electrodes were washed by solvent to 
remove residual electrolyte and surface precipitates. Diisopropyl ether 
(DIPE) or 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) was used to wash the cycled 
electrodes, respectively, according to their different solubility toward 
lithium salts and LiPSs. DIPE is used to wash the lithium salt on the 
cycled electrodes and leave the LiPSs on the surface, while DME washes 
both lithium salts and LiPSs off the cycled electrodes for full exposure of 
the surface [34]. Accordingly, three kinds of cycled electrodes were 
characterized regarding the unwashed, the DIPE washed, and the DME 
washed electrodes. 
The surface components, morphology, and structure of the cycled 
cathodes were analyzed. Most areas of the unwashed charged cathode 
are covered by golden precipitates suggested as high-order LiPSs 
(Fig. 2a). In comparison, the unwashed discharged cathode is covered by 
white precipitates suggested as Li2S as the discharge product (Fig. 2b). 
The sufur-containing species of the cycled cathode were detected 
through X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) after being washed by DIPE 
to remove the residual lithium salts. The charged cathode exhibits two 
strong signals of bridge sulfur (SB, 163.2 eV) and terminal sulfur (ST, 
161.6 eV) (Fig. 2c) [35]. The signals of ST and SB represent that LiPSs 
exist on the surface of the charged cathode. S 2p XPS spectra of the 
discharged cathode exhibit signals of Li2S (160.1 eV) as well as ST and SB 
(Fig. 2d) [36], where Li2S accounts for 45.4% and LiPSs account for 
38.9% of the sulfur content on the discharged cathode. Both LiPSs and 
Li2S are identified on the discharged cathode, which is in correspon­
dence with the previous report [32]. 
The morphology of the charged and discharged cathodes were 
observed via scanning electron microscope (SEM). The surface of the 
charged cathode is fully exposed after being washed by DIPE compared 
with the unwashed cathodes (Figs. 2e and S3), indicating that most of 
the precipitates on the charged cathode are lithium salts. On the con­
trary, the surface of the discharged cathode is covered by a layer of 
precipitates after being washed by DIPE (Fig. 2f), suggesting a large 
amount of Li2S and LiPSs accumulates within the discharged cathode. 
The surface of the cycled cathodes can be fully exposed after being 
washed by DME (Fig. S4). Plenty of the conductive surface can be 
observed which suggests well preservation of the cathode conductive 
skeleton after cycles. Volume expansion of the cycled cathode was 
further evaluated. The thickness of the charged cathode is 190 μm and is 
10 μm thicker than the uncycled cathode (Figs. 2g and S5), while the 
thickness of the discharged cathode expands to 240 μm (Fig. 2h). 
Therefore, the volume expansion is less than 30% and is much less than 
the theoretical expansion value of 79% based on sulfur and Li2S, 
demonstrating the structure of the cycled cathode is integrated. To sum 
up, the cycled cathodes of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells demonstrate 
well-maintained conductive surface and integrated structure to guar­
antee full function of the cycled cathodes. 
The morphology and surface components of the cycled anodes were 
similarly analyzed. Plenty of white metal particles is observed on the 
charged anode (Fig. 3a), while the surface of the discharged anode is 
dark which indicates the existence of massive dead lithium (Fig. 3b). The 
microscale morphology of the cycled anodes were further observed via 
SEM. To remove the precipitates on the surface of the cycled anodes (Fig. 
S6), DIPE or DME was also used individually to wash the cycled anodes. 
The surface of the discharged anode is fully exposed after being washed 
by DIPE, while the charged anode is still covered with a layer of pre­
cipitates (Fig. S7). After being washed by DME, the morphology of 
lithium metal was observed on the cycled anodes (Fig. 3c and d). The 
above results suggest the surface of the charged anode has a high content 
of precipitated LiPSs, while the surface precipitate of the discharged 
anode is mainly lithium salts. For the DME washed cycled anodes, the 
charged anode is uniformly deposited with dense lithium dendrites and 
the diameter of the dendrites is 10–20 μm. In comparison, the surface of 
the discharged anode is full of irregular folds which are suggested to be 
the left solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer after the stripping of 
active lithium. 
The volume expansion of the cycled anodes was further analyzed. 
The thickness of the charged anode is 700 μm, which indicates the 
lithium anode expands 775% during cycling (Figs. 3e and S8), and the 
thickness of the discharged anode reduces to 390 μm after lithium 
stripping (Fig. 3f). XPS was used to detect the surface component of the 
cycled anodes. C, Li, and O are the three richest surface components on 
the cycled anodes (Figs. S9 and 3g). Except for Li, the high content of C 
and O indicates the SEI of the cycled anode mainly consists of organic 
species which mainly come from solvent decomposition. After dis­
charging, the sulfur content largely increases from 3.8 to 12.5 at.%. S 2p 
XPS spectra were further deconvoluted to analyze the sulfur-containing 
species on the cycled anodes (Fig. S10). Both charged and discharged

The electrode kinetics of the cycled cathodes were further studied. 
The cycled cathodes at the charged state were reassembled in coin cells 
with uncycled lithium anodes. Rate responses were recorded to evaluate 
the redox kinetics of the cycled cathodes. The charged cathodes were 
firstly cycled at the same current density as pouch cells (25.0 mA gS
−1) for 
5 cycles and then cycled at a higher current density of 50.0 mA gS
−1 for 5 
cycles (Fig. S12). The characteristic peak-shaped polarization on the 
second discharge plateau disappears when cycled at 25.0 mA gS
−1, and 
the voltage of the second discharge plateau recovers to 2.11 V (Fig. 4d). 
When the cell is cycled at the current density of 50.0 mA gS
−1, the 
discharge curve tends to lower voltage slightly but the peak-shaped 
polarization is not observed at the second discharge plateau, either. 
Besides, high discharge specific capacities of 1000 and 800 mAh g−1 are 
delivered at 25.0 and 50.0 mA gS
−1, respectively, indicating the redox 
kinetics of the cycled cathodes are well maintained in the failed Li‒S 
pouch cells. Therefore, the cathode is not the main issue that leads to the 
failure of Li‒S pouch cells. 
The cycled anodes were reassembled in coin cells with fresh lithium 
anodes as the counter electrode for electrochemical evaluation as well 
(Fig. 4e and f). The reassembled coin cells were charged with an areal 
current density of 0.183 mA cm−2 and a cut-off voltage of 1.00 V was set 
to make sure full use of the residual lithium (Fig. S13). The charged 
anode processes a high residual areal capacity of 12.0 mAh cm−2, while 
3.8 mAh cm−2 of lithium is left in the discharged anode (Fig. 4g). 
Considering the initial areal capacity of the pristine anode is 15.0 mAh 
cm−2 and the cycling areal capacity of the Li–S pouch cells is around 9.0 
mAh cm−2, the residual anode capacity is sufficient to support the anode 
lithium usage and is not the main limitation for the capacity decay. 
Furthermore, the cycled anodes at the charged state were reassembled 
with uncycled fresh cathodes with an areal sulfur loading of 7.2 mgS 
cm−2. The cycled anodes can support 10 cycles with a specific capacity 
higher than 900 mAh g−1 and a CE of 97% in average at the current 
density of 25.0 mA gS
−1 (Figure S14). The polarization curves of the 1st 
and the 10th cycles exhibit well-defined dual-plateau signature without 
the peak-shaped polarization in the second discharge plateau (Fig. 4h). 
The stable cycles of the above coin cells indicate that the cycled anodes 
can sufficiently support a longer cycling lifespan of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S 
pouch cells. The anode is not the main issue responsible for cell failure, 
either. 
By eliminating the cathode and anode issues, we further suspect 
electrolyte exhaustion as the main issue that leads to the failure of 
400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells. To validate the above argument, addi­
tional electrolyte was injected into the failed Li‒S pouch cells (Fig. 5a). 
After injecting the mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/ 1,2-dimethoxy­
ethane (DME) solvent with the electrolyte amount equal to an E/S 
ratio = 1.0 gelectrolyte gS
−1 into the failed Li‒S pouch cells, the discharge 
capacity recovers to 920 mAh g−1 from 600 mAh g−1 and further in­
creases to higher than 80% of the initial specific capacity at the 23rd 
cycle. To further confirm the kinetics of Li‒S pouch cells, polarization 
analysis of the electrolyte-reinjected Li‒S pouch cells was conducted. 
After solvent reinjection, the characteristic peak-shaped polarization at 
the second discharge plateau disappears and the voltage of the second 
discharge plateau recovers to 2.11 V (Fig. 5b), indicating the kinetics of 
the Li‒S pouch cells is largely recovered. Therefore, solvent exhaustion 
is identified as the key limiting factor that leads to the failure of 400 Wh 
kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells. Besides, the CE of the electrolyte-reinjected Li‒S 
pouch cell is still at a low level and decreases in the following cycles. The 
un-recovered CE is suggested to be caused by the consumption of lithium 
nitrate (LiNO3). Therefore, LiNO3-contained DOL/DME electrolyte was 
reinjected into the failed Li‒S pouch cells, and the discharge capacity 
and the CE both recovered to a high level of 1200 mAh g−1 and 90% 
respectively (Fig. S15). These results indicate that the shuttle effect 
becomes more sever along the cycling of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells 
and LiNO3 is irreversibly consumed to cause the reduced CE. 
3. Conclusion 
In conclusion, the failure mechanism of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch 
cells is revealed based on systematic failure analysis. The failure of the 
400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells originates from the characteristic peak- 
shaped polarization at the second discharge plateau that renders rapid 
capacity decay. The cathodes and anodes from the failed Li‒S pouch 
cells possess integrated structure and sufficient residual capacity and 
redox kinetics to support the following cycles. When the mixed solvent 
of DOL/DME was injected into the failed pouch cells, the characteristic 
peak-shaped polarization disappears and the capacity recovers to 80% of 
the initial capacity. Based on the above comprehensive failure analysis 
results, electrolyte exhaustion, especially solvent exhaustion, is 
evidently identified as the key limiting factor that restricts the cycling 
lifespan of 400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells. Therefore, suppressing the 
electrolyte exhaustion during cycling is essential to achieve long cycling 
400 Wh kg−1 Li‒S pouch cells, such as artificial anode coating, anti- 
reductive electrolyte, and composite lithium metal anode [37–39]. Be­
sides, designing low-porosity sulfur cathode is also beneficial to achieve 
high-energy-density and long-cycling Li‒S pouch cells [40]. This work 
provides a clear guideline for developing targeted promotion strategies 
and rational design principles to achieve high-energy-density and 
long-cycling Li–S batteries.
