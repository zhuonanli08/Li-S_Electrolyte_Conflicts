ABSTRACT: The lithium sulfur battery is regarded as a
potential next-generation high-energy battery system.
However, polysulﬁdes dissolve and shuttle through the
electrolytes, causing rapid capacity decay, serious self-
discharge, and poor high-temperature performances. Here,
we demonstrate that by directly introducing glutamate into
commercial electrolytes, these issues can be tackled
simultaneously. With abundant negatively charged hydrox-
yl groups, the glutamate additive electrolyte eﬀectively
suppresses the shuttling of negatively charged polysulﬁde
ions through strong repulsive interaction up to 1.54 eV.
With glutamate additive electrolyte, the lithium sulfur battery has a capacity retention of 60% after 1000 cycles at 5.95
mA/cm2, a self-discharge rate on the order of one-third that of commercial electrolytes, and stable operation at 60 °C.
KEYWORDS: lithium sulfur batteries, electrolyte additive, polysulﬁde shuttling, charge repulsion, glutamate
L
ithium ion batteries (LIBs) have found extensive
applications in energy storage devices.1−7 Among
LIBs, the lithium sulfur (Li−S) battery exhibits great
application potential, due to the high abundance of sulfur with
a high theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh g−1.8−12 However, the
shuttle eﬀect of polysulﬁdes still challenges the commercializa-
tion of Li−S batteries. Polysulﬁdes dissolve and shuttle in the
electrolyte to react with the Li anode, leading to unstable
cycling and a short life.13−16 Extensive eﬀorts have been
centered on improving the cathodes to suppress the shuttle
eﬀect including (1) modifying cathodes with polar materials,
such as N-doped carbon,17 porous carbon,18 and polar
binder;19 (2) preparing binder-free cathodes with carbon
cloth20 and sulfur−nickel foam;21 and (3) employing
interlayers, such as metal oxides/chalcogenides22 and con-
ductive polymer interlayers.23 Although these eﬀorts inhibit
the shuttle eﬀect, the introduction of 3D porous carbon
structures and interlayers seriously reduces the volumetric
energy density of Li−S batteries.
Functional separators with abundant negatively charged
functional groups exhibit a strong repulsive eﬀect on
polysulﬁdes, which signiﬁcantly suppresses the shuttle eﬀect
without compromising the energy density.24,25 Using such
separators, thousands of cycles (>1000) with high sulfur
loadings (>3 mg) have been realized.20,24−27 The design of
charged separators to eﬀectively inhibit polysulﬁde shuttling
without compromising the Li+ ion transport is highly desirable.
However, the well-designed charged separator structure leads
to more complicated manufacturing processes, which poses
additional challenges for practical applications.
In this work, a negatively charged electrolyte is realized by
directly incorporating glutamate to electrolytes for addressing
the shuttling eﬀect and self-discharge. As shown in Scheme 1,
after adding glutamate, the negatively charged carboxylic acid
groups of glutamate eﬀectively inhibit the translocations of
polysulﬁde ions while ensuring eﬃcient transport of Li+ ions.
With a strong repulsive interaction of 1.54 eV, the self-
discharge rate of the glutamate additive electrolyte is on the
order of one-third that of the commercial electrolyte. Using the

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Physicochemical Properties of Electrolytes. As shown
in Figure 1a, the glutamate additive increases the viscosity of
the electrolyte and gelation occurs with increasing glutamate
from 2% to 10% (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The gel
electrolyte with high viscosity leads to a large particle size.28 As
shown in Figure 1b, adding 2% to 6% glutamate increases the
transference number of Li+ ions, which is attributed to the
electronegativity of glutamate in the electrolyte (Figure 1c).
The glutamate has a high zeta potential of −51.5 mV, thus
producing strong electrostatic attraction to the positively
charged Li+ ions and enhancing the Li+ migration.24
Importantly, the high zeta potential of the glutamate
electrolyte is retained after cycling (Figure S2, Supporting
Information). Glutamate forms strong electrostatic repulsion
to the polysulﬁde ions with negative charges to signiﬁcantly
inhibit the shuttling eﬀect.25,29 However, the attraction force
limits the Li+ ion transfer as the amount of glutamate additive
exceeds 6%, leading to the drop of Li+ transference number.
pH values of various electrolytes are measured and shown in
Figure S3 (Supporting Information). After the introduction of
glutamate, the acidity of the electrolyte is signiﬁcantly
enhanced, which is meaningful for matching with high-
energy-density cathodes.17,30 The electrochemical stabilities
of various electrolytes are evaluated using linear scan
voltammetry (LSV) on stainless steel electrodes (Figure S4,
Supporting Information). The electrolyte after adding
glutamate has no obvious change, indicating that glutamate
is well compatible with the electrolyte. A commercial
polypropylene (PP) separator is immersed in the electrolyte
with and without glutamate additives for 50 min. The
introduction of glutamate signiﬁcantly improves the electrolyte
uptake of the PP separator, as shown in Figure 1d. To further
investigate the solubility of polysulﬁdes in glutamate electro-
lytes, ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopic measure-
ments are performed, as shown in Figure S5 (Supporting
Information). For the DOL+DME (1,3-dioxolane and
dimethoxy ether) electrolyte, the characteristic peak in the
range of 400−425 nm corresponds to the absorption of
Li2S8.31 The peak intensity gradually decreases with increasing
glutamate, and the high proportion of glutamate gives a lighter
color of the electrolyte, which is attributed to the strong
electrostatic repulsion force that inhibits the dissolution of
polysulﬁdes. Electrochemical cycling stability of the Li anode
based on the glutamate additive electrolyte is evaluated with
symmetric Li|Li cells. As displayed in Figure 1e and Figure S6
(Supporting Information), symmetric Li|Li cells with glutamate
additive are stable over 280 h at 1.5 mA cm−2 with a ﬁxed
capacity of 1.5 mAh cm−2 and over 200 h at 3.0 mA cm−2 with
a ﬁxed capacity of 3 mAh cm−2, respectively. In comparison,
without glutamate additive, a higher voltage is observed with
cycling. The glutamate additive also decreases the polarization,
which is due to the reduced dendritic and dead Li during
cycling (Figure 3e and f). A detailed comparison of the voltage
proﬁles is also shown in the enlarged ﬁgure. With the
glutamate, the Li anode has an overpotential of ∼0.5 mV at
a current density of 1.5 mA cm−2, which only increases to ∼0.7
mV at a current density of 3.0 mA cm−2. However, a much
higher overpotential is observed without glutamate additive.
The Li metal cyclic stability in the glutamate additive
electrolyte is investigated with Li||Cu cells (Figures 1f, g and
S7, Supporting Information). A 4% glutamate additive
contributes to a lower polarization, enhanced cycling stability,
and higher Coulombic eﬃciency, as compared with the
commercial electrolyte (Figures 1f and S7, Supporting
Information). With 0.5 mA cm−2, the Coulombic eﬃciency
of the 4% glutamate electrolyte is maintained at >99% for 300
cycles, as shown in Figure 1g. The ﬂammability of various
electrolytes is evaluated with self-extinguishing time (SET). As
shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), the SET value
for the DOL+DME electrolyte is 89.7 s g−1, considerably
above that of the 4% glutamate electrolyte (70.8 s g−1).

Raman spectra combined with DFT have been used to extract
the information on polysulﬁde structures.24,36−38 In situ Raman
spectroscopy is conducted to gain further insights into the
shuttling mechanism of polysulﬁdes and the repulsive eﬀect
between glutamate and polysulﬁdes, as shown in Figure 3a−d.
The battery conﬁguration for the in situ Raman spectroscopic
analysis is shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). A
hole is made on both the negative case and the lithium foil to
allow for the direct laser irradiation on the electrolyte and the
collection of the Raman signals. Figure 3a−d show the
discharge processes of the DOL+DME electrolyte and 4%
glutamate electrolyte with the time-resolved Raman images
and Raman spectra at a conﬁrmed voltage. For the DOL+DME
electrolyte, at the beginning of the discharge, the characteristic
Raman peaks for Li2S8 at 155 and 228 cm−1 appear near the

observed for the 4% glutamate electrolyte in the entire

suppression of the polysulﬁdes. Similar phenomena occur
during the charging process (Figure S12, Supporting

Electrochemical Performances of Li−S Batteries with
Glutamate Electrolytes. LiNO3 has been the most
commonly used additive in Li−S battery electrolytes, but has
a low electrochemical stability.40 Undoubtedly, wide electro-
chemical windows of electrolytes are important for the Li−S
battery. The electrochemical stability of the glutamate
electrolyte in the electrochemical window of 1−3 V is
analyzed. As shown in Figure 4a, the capacity increases ﬁrst,
reaches the maximum with 4% glutamate, and then starts to
decrease as glutamate exceeds 6%. After glutamate is added,
battery capacity decay is suppressed and CE is enhanced, as
attributed to the repulsion eﬀect of glutamate to polysulﬁdes.
The shuttling current is investigated through the method
proposed by Narayanan.41 As shown in Figure S15
(Supporting Information), the shuttling current of 4%
glutamate electrolyte is less than one-half that of the DOL
+DME electrolyte, implying that the shuttling of polysulﬁdes is
eﬀectively reduced with the 4% glutamate electrolyte. The
electrochemical impedance analysis of the cells with diﬀerent
electrolytes shows that the charge-transfer resistance (Rct)
decreases with increasing glutamate when it is below 4%.
However, excessive glutamate leads to capacity decay owing to
the reduced Li+ ion transfer number and the increased charge-
transfer resistance when the proportion of glutamate is above
4% (Figures 1b and 4c). The rate performances of various
electrolytes are displayed in Figure 4d. The cell with 4%
glutamate electrolyte has excellent rate capacities of 998.5,
759.4, 587.6, 453.6, 300.9, 140.3, and 68.2 mAh g−1 from 0.5
to 10 C. The capacity increases to 95.3% of the initial capacity
as the current density is reduced back to 0.5 C from 10 C,
indicating excellent reversibility of 4% glutamate electrolyte. In
comparison, the speciﬁc capacities based on the original
electrolyte are considerably lower at all C rates. The excellent
rate performances and reversibility are attributed to the high
Li+ transference number of 4% glutamate electrolyte and the
robust repulsive interaction between polysulﬁdes and the
negatively charged carboxylic acid groups of glutamate, as
shown in Figures 1b and 2a. To demonstrate cyclic
performances, cells with 4% glutamate electrolyte are tested
with 5.95 mA/cm2 (2 C), as shown in Figure 4e. With an
initial speciﬁc capacity of 605.2 mAh g−1, the battery exhibits a

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, glutamate additive is directly introduced into
traditional electrolytes. With negatively charged hydroxyl
groups, the glutamate additive electrolyte eﬃciently inhibits
the shuttling eﬀect of polysulﬁdes without compromising the
transfer of Li+ ions. The special design of the glutamate
electrolyte enables high electrochemical stability, as evidenced
with the 99% Coulombic eﬃciency and the reduced over-
voltage (0.7 mV) of Li|Li symmetric cells. The glutamate
electrolyte enables stable cycling of the Li−S batteries over
1000 cycles with a high current density of 5.95 mA/cm2 (2 C)
and suppresses the self-discharge at high temperatures.
METHODS
Preparation of Glutamate Additive Electrolytes. Glutamate
additive electrolytes with the lithium salt of bis(triﬂuoromethane
sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) were prepared with 1 M LiTFSI/1,3-
dioxolane and dimethoxy ether with glutamate mass fractions of 2%,
4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%, denoted as 2% glutamate, 4% glutamate, 6%
glutamate, 8% glutamate, and 10% glutamate, respectively. The
reference electrolyte was 1 M LiTFSI in DOL+DME, named DOL
+DME. LiTFSI was heated under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h. The
DME/DOL (v/v = 1:1) mixed solvent was prepared. The electrolyte
composition was 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in DOL/DME. The improved
electrolyte composition was 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in the mixture of
DOL/DME and glutamate additive, and the mass fraction of
glutamate additive was 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%, respectively.
Preparation of S@C Composite Cathode. The mixture of
sulfur and Ketjenblack powders with a mass ratio of 8:2 was treated at
155 °C for 12 h. Using deionized water as dispersion medium,
Ketjenblack−sulfur powders were obtained. For the fabrication of S@
C electrodes, Ketjenblack−sulfur (S@C) composite, conductive
carbon, and polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) were mixed in N-methyl
pyrrolidone (NMP) with a weight ratio of 8:1:1 and stirred for 24 h.
The uniformly mixed slurry was coated on an aluminum foil with a
thickness of 12 μm. Then, the coated aluminum foil was heated under
vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h. The resulting electrodes were cut into
circular sheets with a diameter of 14 mm. The preparation of LiFePO4
electrodes followed the same procedure of the S@C electrodes. The
regular sulfur loading density of the S@C cathode was 1.35−1.45
mgs/cm2, and the sulfur content in the high-loading cathode was
3.55−3.75 mgs/cm2. The LiFePO4 loading was 1.09−1.17 mg/cm2.
Preparation of PVDF Separators. A 0.7 g amount of PVDF
powders was dissolved in 9 mL of NMP and stirred at 25 °C for 6 h.
Then the mixture was coated on a glass substrate with a scraper at a
coating rate of 1 cm s−1 and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The
PVDF separator was cut into circular sheets with a diameter of 16
mm.
Preparation of Li2S8. In an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox, 800 mg of sulfur
powders and 230 mg of Li2S were put into a three-necked ﬂask,
dissolved in 10 mL of DME, and then stirred at 60 °C for 24 h until
the solid was completely dissolved to form a brownish-red color.
Materials Characterizations. Electrolyte viscosity was measured
using a rheometer (Waters, VIS403039900, USA). The zeta potentials
of the glutamate electrolyte were tested with a microelectrophoresis
nanometer potentiometer (Nano-Z, Malvern, England). pH was
measured using PHS (PHS-25, INESA, China). The concentration of
S ions was measured with ICP (ICPMS, PerkinElmer, USA). Raman
spectra were taken with Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia,
Renishaw, England). The solubilities of Li2S8 in diﬀerent electrolytes
were analyzed through UV−vis spectroscopy (Hitachi, U-3900H,
Japan). The morphologies of Li anodes and S@C composite cathodes
after cycling were measured with SEM (Zeiss Supra, Germany). The
Li+ transference numbers for various electrolytes were studied with
Nyquist plots according to eq 1, where Re, Rint, and RD represent the
resistances of electrolyte, interface, and diﬀusion, respectively.

Electrochemical Measurements. The glutamate additive
electrolyte was assembled into CR2035 coin-type cells for electro-
chemical tests. The amount of the electrolyte was 45 μL. The
separator was Celgard 2500. Galvanostatic measurements of Li−S and
LiFePO4/Li half batteries were conducted in the voltage ranges of
1.0−3.0 and 2.5−4.2 V (vs Li+/Li). CV and EIS measurements were
carried out with a Princeton VersaSTAT electrochemical workstation.
The CV analysis of Li−S batteries was conducted in a voltage range of
1.0−3.0 V at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. EIS experiments were studied
at a frequency of 10 mV in a frequency range from 1000 kHz to 10
mHz. The self-discharge eﬃciency was calculated according to the
equation ΔR = C10th −C11th/C10th
.
Shuttle Currents Tests. Shuttle current tests were performed
through the Narayanan method.41 For the measurement of shuttle
currents, the cells were ﬁrst cycled three times with a small current
density of 1 mA cm−2 to activate sulfur electrodes. Then, the voltage
of the cells was charged to 2.7 V and remained at an open-circuit state
for 5 min. The cells were switched to a potentiostatic mode to oﬀset
the drop voltage at diﬀerent stages (2.7, 2.6, 2.5, 2.4, 2.3, and 2.2 V)
for 1 h before reaching a steady-state value. The steady-state current
was the shuttle current, as the voltage would continue to drop if there
was no external current to compromise the shuttle eﬀect.
In Situ Raman Spectroscopy Analysis. In situ micro-Raman
analysis was performed through a Renishaw InVia Raman microscope
with a 532 nm laser. The electrolyte was irradiated in a modiﬁed coin
battery through a 5 mm hole on the Li anode to obtain a Raman
signal. Li−S batteries were assembled in a DELLIX glovebox with
water and oxygen at levels both below 0.1 ppm. After the aging
process, the cell was subject to galvanostatic measurements at 0.2 C in
a voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V.
