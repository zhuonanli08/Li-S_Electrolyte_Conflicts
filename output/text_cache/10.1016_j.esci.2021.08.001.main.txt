Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered as a highly promising energy storage system due to their 
ultrahigh theoretical energy density. However, the sluggish kinetics of the complex multi-electron sulfur 
redox reactions seriously hinders the actual battery performance especially under practical working 
conditions. Homogeneous redox mediation, through elaborately designing the additive molecules, is an 
eﬀective approach to promote the sulfur redox kinetics. Herein a promoter of mixed organodiselenides 
(mixed-Se) is proposed to comprehensively improve the sulfur redox kinetics following the redox 
comediation principles. Concretely, diphenyl diselenide promotes the liquid–liquid conversion between 
polysulﬁdes and the solid–liquid conversion regarding lithium sulﬁde oxidation to polysulﬁdes, while 
dimethyl diselenide enhances the liquid–solid conversion regarding lithium sulﬁde deposition. Consequently, 
the mixed-Se promoter endows a high discharge capacity of 1002 mAh g − 1 with high sulfur loading of 4.0 
mg cm − 2 , a high capacity retention of 81.6% after 200 cycles at 0.5 C, and a high actual energy density of 
384 Wh kg − 1 at 0.025 C in 1.5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cells. This work aﬀords an eﬀective kinetic promoter 
to construct high-energy-density Li–S batteries and inspires molecular design of kinetic promoters toward 
targeted energy-related redox reactions. 
Keywords: Lithium–sulfur batteries, Sulfur redox kinetics, Organodiselenide, Lithium polysulﬁdes, Redox comediation 
1. Introduction 
Developing high-energy-density next-generation rechargeable 
batteries beyond the current lithium-ion batteries is of high priority 
for multiple energy-consuming occasions including electric vehicles, 
consumer electronics, and smart grids [1–3] . Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) 
batteries have attracted considerable attentions due to their extremely 
high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg − 1 and low cost of 
cathode materials [4] . Generally, the sulfur redox reactions undergo 
multi-electron and multi-phase processes between solid sulfur (S 8 ), 
soluble lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs, Li 2 S n , 3 ≤ n ≤ 8), and solid lithium 
sulﬁde (Li 2 S) [5,6] . The above conversion reactions endow the sulfur 
cathode with an ultrahigh theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 1672 mAh 
g − 1 . Nevertheless, full demonstration of the cathode advantages in a 
working battery is severely hindered by sluggish kinetics on the sulfur

Various heterogeneous promoters have been proposed to address the 
cathode kinetic challenges, including metal compounds [9–14] , metal 
nanoparticles [15,16] , heteroatoms doped nanocarbon [17–21] , and 
single-atom electrocatalysts [22–27] . Such heterogeneous promotors 
function by enhancing surface adsorption of LiPS intermediates through

adsorption procedure heavily depends on the limited surface active 
sites at the boundaries between the heterogeneous promoters and the 
conductive scaﬀolds [35,36] . The surface active sites can easily be

Homogeneous promoters represented by small molecular redox 
mediators (RMs) aﬀord a diﬀerent but eﬀective approach to promote the 
sulfur redox kinetics [38,39] . Typically, RMs construct an extra redox 
bypass along the targeted sulfur redox process, where the as-constructed 
bypass is supposed with similar thermodynamics but faster kinetics than 
the original one [40] . For instance, Cui et al . proposed AQT as RM to 
promote the solid–liquid conversion process regarding Li 2 S oxidation 
to LiPSs [41] . Concretely, the equilibrium potential of AQT/AQT 2 − is 
higher than the theoretical Li 2 S oxidation potential, while the actual 
potential of AQT 2 − oxidation including kinetic overpotential is lower 
than that of Li 2 S oxidation, suggesting the preferentially oxidized AQT 
chemically oxidizes the kinetically sluggish Li 2 S ( i.e. the RM-constructed 
bypass). As a result, AQT was proven to eﬀectively reduce the oxidation 
barrier of Li 2 S. Based on the similar mechanism, metallocene [42,43] , 
and anthraquinone [44] molecules were designed as RMs to overcome 
the Li 2 S oxidation barrier or promote the kinetics in the reduction of 
long-chain LiPSs. However, most RMs only focus on a single sulfur 
conversion process due to the above-mentioned mechanism following 
the thermodynamic sequence, which cannot take the full-range sulfur 
redox reactions into account or regulate the whole discharge/charge 
processes [45] . Worse still, the high reactivity between RMs and Li metal 
causes severe anode corrosion, anabatic shuttle eﬀect, and unbearably 
low Coulombic eﬃciency [46] . 
To address the above dilemma, a redox comediation strategy is 
recently proposed to comprehensively regulate full-range sulfur redox 
kinetics by fundamentally improving the redox mediating capability of 
the intrinsic polysulﬁde mediators [47] . Redox comediators (coRMs) 
are expected to chemically react with LiPSs to aﬀord products with 
higher redox mediation capabilities to promote the sulfur redox kinetics 
[48] . Based on the above principle, di-t-butyl disulﬁde has been 
ﬁrstly proven as a coRM to react with LiPSs and generate lithium t- 
butyl polysulﬁde species that accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics [47] . 
The as-generated lithium t-butyl polysulﬁde species perform better in 
reactivity and diﬀusivity, aﬀording lower polarization, higher capacity, 
and longer cycling stability. Similarly, organodiselenides with the 
highly reactive selenium element also demonstrate redox comediation 
capability to covalently modify the LiPSs and improve the sulfur redox 
kinetics [49–51] . The success of the redox comediation strategy inspires 
further optimization of the coRMs regarding advanced molecular design 
and employment in practical high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells. 
Advanced coRMs are expected to essentially promote the sulfur redox 
reactions under practical working conditions to realize Li–S batteries 
with high actual energy density and long cycling life. 
In this contribution, a promoter of mixed organodiselenides (mixed- 
Se) is introduced to accelerate the full-range sulfur redox kinetics. 
Concretely, the promoter is composed of dimethyl diselenide (Me- 
Se) and diphenyl diselenide (Ph-Se), both of which are expected 
to comediate the polysulﬁde conversions. Ph-Se is more conducive 
to promoting the kinetics of the liquid–liquid conversion between 
dissolved LiPSs and the solid–liquid conversion from Li 2 S to LiPSs, 
while Me-Se mainly enhances the kinetics of the liquid–solid conversion 
regarding Li 2 S deposition ( Fig. 1 ). Consequently, the mixed-Se improves 
full-range sulfur redox kinetics, endowing high-sulfur-loading Li–S 
batteries with high speciﬁc capacity of 1002 mAh g − 1 and long cycling 
lifespan regarding capacity retention of 81.6% after 200 cycles at 0.5 C. 
More importantly, 1.5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cells with mixed-Se deliver 
high actual initial energy density of 384 Wh kg − 1 at 0.025 C and 324 
Wh kg − 1 at 0.05 C with 50 cycles. The excellent battery performance 
validates the applicability of the mixed organodiselenides under rational 
molecular design.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectra 
(EIS) of Li 2 S 6 symmetric cells were conducted to evaluate the liquid–
liquid conversion kinetics. Analogous symmetric cells were assembled 
with identical carbon paper electrodes and 1.2 mol [S] L − 1 Li 2 S 6 
electrolyte with or without organodiselenides. The peak current of 
CV proﬁles quantitatively reﬂects the liquid–liquid conversion kinetics 
( Fig. 3 a). The cells with either organodiselenide promoter exhibited 
signiﬁcantly increased peak currents than the blank cells, illustrating 
that both Me-Se and Ph-Se can improve the redox kinetics between 
dissolved LiPSs (Fig. S1). Speciﬁcally, the cells with Ph-Se presented 
the highest peak current of 37.8 mA, indicating its superiority to Me- 
Se for the liquid–liquid conversion process. Further EIS evaluation 
and quantitative analysis on the charge transfer resistance (R ct ) 
exhibited similar tendency that both the organoselenides reduced 
the resistance while the Ph-Se aﬀorded the lowest R ct (Fig. S2 
and Table S1). The above results suggest that the liquid–liquid 
conversion kinetics can be remarkably enhanced by organodiselenide 
promoters, where Ph-Se demonstrates higher promotion capability than 
Me-Se. 
The liquid–solid conversion kinetics regarding LiPSs reduction to 
Li 2 S under the comediation of Me-Se or Ph-Se were probed via 
chronoamperometry ( Fig. 3 b). Speciﬁcally, dissolved LiPSs were poten- 
tiostatically discharged at 2.12 V to force Li 2 S nucleation and growth, 
where the peak time and the integral Li 2 S precipitation capacity of 
the current response reveal the intrinsic kinetics [52] . The peak time 
was greatly reduced with the organodiselenide promoters, 1620 or 
1290 s ahead of the blank cell regarding the addition of Me-Se or Ph- 
Se, respectively (Fig. S3). Furthermore, Me-Se aﬀorded the highest Li 2 S 
precipitation capacity of 178.2 mAh g − 1 , nearly twice as the blank cell 
and even 57.5% higher than that of the Ph-Se. Based on the above 
results, the kinetic promotion capability on the liquid–solid conversion 
process follows the sequence of Me-Se > Ph-Se > blank. As both organ- 
odiselenides promote the liquid–solid conversion kinetics, Me-Se is 
manifested to be the better choice. 
The oxidation of Li 2 S toward dissolved LiPSs plays a crucial role 
in achieving high-rate feasibility and high capacity retention during 
cycling. Nevertheless, the high energy barrier on the solid–liquid 
conversion process inﬂicts large overpotentials and reduced reversibility 
of the sulfur redox reactions by forming bulk Li 2 S particles in poor 
contact with the conductive skeleton. The above challenges can be 
generally addressed by redox mediators that even Li 2 S cathodes can be 
charged with reasonable capacity and reduced polarization [41,45] . 
The kinetic inﬂuence of the organodiselenide promoters on the solid–
liquid conversion was evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) on 
fully-discharged Li–S cells, where the onset potential, deﬁned as the 
voltage versus Li/Li + to reach the current density of 0.1 mA cm − 2 , was 
selected as the indicator to evaluate the kinetic promotion eﬃciency 
( Fig. 3 c). The onset potential of the cell with Ph-Se was 2.18 V, while 
Me-Se did not exhibit any kinetic advantages with the onset potential 
being 2.23 V, which was even higher than blank cells by 0.01 V (Fig. S4). 
As the current increased, the kinetic advantages of Ph-Se on reducing 
the overpotential were still maintained. Therefore, Ph-Se is recognized 
as the favorable promoter for the solid–liquid conversion process. 
According to the above quantitative kinetic analysis, both of the 
organodiselenides can generally promote the sulfur redox kinetics 
following the redox comediation principles as expected. However, Ph-Se 
and Me-Se demonstrate distinct kinetic promotion capabilities toward 
speciﬁc conversion processes. Concretely, Ph-Se is more favorable

The diﬀerences between Ph-Se and Me-Se are generally attributed to 
their diﬀerent organo-functional groups that aﬀord diﬀerent electronic 
eﬀects. On the other hand, the combination of the two organodiselenides 
is expected to demonstrate comprehensive kinetic advantages to 
globally promote full-range sulfur redox reactions considering their 
similarity and compatibility in structure and functions. To validate the 
above strategy, equal molar Ph-Se and Me-Se were mixed to aﬀord the 
mixed-Se promoter, whose kinetic behaviors are evaluated as follows. 
It is worth noting that both Me-Se and Ph-Se can aﬀord reversible 
capacities and serve as active materials in previous reports [53,54] . 
When used as cathodic active material and matched with lithium 
anode, both Me-Se and Ph-Se exhibit reversible redox peaks in 
CV proﬁles, but the equilibrium potential of the former is lower 
than that of the latter by 22 mV (Fig. S5), which is consistent 
with the previous DFT calculation results. To eliminate the capacity 
contributed by organodiselenides themselves, the capacities of Ph-Se, 
Me-Se, and mixed-Se electrolyte were evaluated using carbon papers 
as the cathode current collectors (Fig. S6). Obviously, Ph-Se, Me-Se, 
and mixed-Se electrolyte provided very limited discharge capacities 
of 4.0, 3.8, and 5.0 mAh g − 1 normalized to sulfur, respectively, 
which are almost negligible compared with the cycling capacity 
of routine Li–S cells. The low capacity of the organodiselenides 
is mainly attributed to their low utilized concentrations. Based on 
the above evaluation, it is concluded that the signiﬁcant capacity 
enhancement in organodiselenide-mediated Li–S cells can be identiﬁed 
as the result of kinetic promotion rather than additional active 
materials. 
The eﬃciency of the mixed-Se promoter was evaluated in Li–S full 
cells through galvanostatic discharge–charge tests at diﬀerent rates 
(1 C = 1672 mA g − 1 ). The areal sulfur loading of the Li–S full cells was 
1.2 mg S cm − 2 . As exhibited in Fig. 4 a, the Li − S cells with the organ- 
odiselenide promoters generally delivered 18% ‒31% higher capacities 
than the blank cells under diﬀerent rates. Speciﬁcally, the Li − S cells 
with the mixed-Se promoter delivered the highest initial discharge 
capacity of 1220 mAh g − 1 at 0.1 C. The capacity advantages were 
maintained when increasing the current density to 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 C 
that high speciﬁc capacities of 935, 855, and 776 mAh g − 1 were 
respectively aﬀorded. The rate responses of mixed-Se are better than 
that of individual Ph-Se or Me-Se and largely exceed that of the blank 
cells, suggesting the eﬃciency of the mixed-Se promoter under working 
conditions. 
Corresponding galvanostatic discharge–charge proﬁles further 
reveal the details on the kinetic promotion of the mixed-Se toward 
speciﬁc sulfur redox reactions ( Fig. 4 b). The discharge curves 
demonstrate typically two plateaus. The generation of dissolved LiPSs 
and liquid–liquid conversion between long-chain and short-chain LiPSs 
take place at the high-voltage plateau, while the low-voltage plateau 
corresponds to Li 2 S deposition regarding the liquid–solid conversion. 
The capacities of each discharge plateau ( Q high and Q low ) are selected 
as indicators to evaluate the kinetic promotion eﬃciency of diﬀerent 
organodiselenide promoters for the liquid–liquid and liquid–solid 
conversion processes, where their identiﬁcation is exhibited in Fig. S7. 
The cells with Ph-Se exhibited the highest Q high of 250 mAh g − 1 , in 
agreement with the kinetic evaluation results. Beneﬁtting from the Ph- 
Se content, the cells with mixed-Se promoter provided enhanced Q high

Considering the electronically and ionically insulating nature of Li 2 S, 
a Li 2 S dissolution barrier shall be overcome at the initial charge of Li–S 
cells to allow the following solid–liquid conversion to take place. The 
Li 2 S dissolution barrier also exerts signiﬁcant impact on the reversibility 
of the sulfur redox reactions. To quantitatively evaluate the organ- 
odiselenide promoters on reducing the Li 2 S dissolution barrier, the Li 2 S 
dissolution overpotential deﬁned as the potential diﬀerence between 
the initial charge voltage and the highest charge voltage is selected 
as the kinetic indicator. Both the Me-Se and blank cells suﬀered from 
high Li 2 S dissolution overpotentials ( Fig. 4 e). In contrast, the cells with 
Ph-Se or mixed-Se promoter exhibited no apparent overpotential to 
overcome the Li 2 S dissolution barrier. The kinetic advantage of the 
mixed-Se promoter is apparently inherited from Ph-Se. Based on the 
above-mentioned results, the mixed-Se promoter demonstrates com- 
prehensive kinetic promotion capabilities toward full-range sulfur redox 
reactions by rational combination of the advantages of its individual 
components. 
The durability of the mixed-Se promoter was ﬁrstly evaluated in Li–
S cells with a moderate sulfur loading of 1.2 mg S cm − 2 . The cycling 
current density was 0.5 C. The cells with mixed-Se, Ph-Se, or Me- 
Se promoters delivered an initial discharge capacity of 956, 858, 
or 902 mAh g − 1 , respectively, much higher than the blank cells of 
820 mAh g − 1 ( Fig. 5 a). Among them, the mixed-Se promoter out- 
performed the individual ones with the highest initial discharge 
capacity. After 200 cycles, the cells with mixed-Se promoter preserved 
81.6% of its initial discharge capacity, while the capacity retention 
for the Me-Se, Ph-Se, and blank cells were 73.5%, 77.9%, and 66.8%, 
respectively (Fig. S8). Obviously, the mixed-Se promoter demonstrated 
excellent durability besides kinetic promotion capabilities. Meanwhile, 
the Coulombic eﬃciency of the Me-Se cells faded after about 100 cycles, 
which was probably attributed to anode corrosion by the high electro- 
chemically active Me-Se molecule (Fig. S9). In contrast, the Coulombic 
eﬃciency remained stable for the Ph-Se and mixed-Se cells throughout 
the 200 cycles. Besides, the cells with mixed-Se promoter also showed 
excellent cycling performance with the highest discharge capacity and 
cycling stability under a high current density of 1 C (Fig. S10). 
High-sulfur-loading Li ‒S cells were further evaluated on the 
mixed-Se promoter to estimate its feasibility under practical 
working conditions ( Fig. 5 b). The areal sulfur loading was 
4.0 mg S cm − 2 and the cycling current density was 0.1 C. 
After activation for the ﬁrst two cycles, the high-loading 
cells with mixed-Se provided an initial discharge capacity 
of 1002 mAh g − 1 and maintained a discharge capacity of 
849 mAh g − 1 after 50 cycles. In contrast, the blank cells delivered 
lower discharge capacity during cycling, illustrating the pivotal role 
of mixed-Se promoter in accelerating full-range sulfur redox kinetics 
under practical conditions.

3. Conclusion 
In summary, a mixed-organodiselenide promoter is rationally 
proposed to comprehensively enhance full-range sulfur redox kinetics 
in practical high-energy-density Li–S batteries. Diphenyl diselenide and 
dimethyl diselenide respectively aﬀord kinetic advantages on speciﬁc 
sulfur redox reactions and their combination results in an overall 
kinetic promotion. The mixed-organodiselenide promoter endows Li–
S cells with high capacity retention of 81.6% after 200 cycles at 0.5 C 
and high initial capacity of 1002 mAh g − 1 at 0.1 C with high-sulfur- 
loading cathodes. More importantly, the mixed-Se promoter realizes 
1.5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cells with an actual initial energy density of 
384 Wh kg − 1 at 0.025 C and 50 cycles at 0.05 C. This work not only 
provides an eﬃcient mixed organodiselenide as the kinetic promoter 
to construct high-energy-density Li–S batteries, but also inspires further 
molecular design on kinetic promoters toward targeted energy-related 
redox reactions. 
4. Experimental section 
4.1. Materials 
All the raw materials including sulfur (S 8 ), diphenyl diselenide, 
dimethyl diselenide, carbon paper (CP), multi-walled carbon 
nanotubes (CNTs), N -methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, Sinopharm), 1,2- 
dimethoxyethane (DOL), 1,2-diamethoxyethane (DME), poly(vinylidene 
ﬂuoride) (PVDF, HSV-300), polypropylene (PP) separators (Celgard 
2400), aluminum (Al) foil, lithium (Li) foil (50 or 600 μm thick), 
lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI), and lithium nitrate 
(LiNO 3 ) were purchased from commercial sources and applied without 
further puriﬁcation. 
4.2. Material characterizations 
The morphology was characterized by a JSM 7401F (JEOL Ltd., 
Japan) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) operated at 3.0 kV. 
4.3. Electrolyte preparation 
1.0 mol L − 1 LiTFSI and 2.0 wt.% LiNO 3 dissolved in DOL/DME 
(v/v = 1:1) mixed solvents was adopted as the blank electrolyte. Me- 
Se, Ph-Se, and their equal molar mixture (mixed-Se) were added to the 
blank electrolyte to prepare corresponding electrolytes with the additive 
concentration of 100 mmol L − 1 . Meanwhile, Li 2 S 6 and Li 2 S 8 electrolytes 
were prepared for kinetic evaluation. Speciﬁcally, the mixture of 
Li 2 S and S 8 powers with the molar ratio of 1:5 or 1:7 was added 
into the blank electrolyte to prepare Li 2 S 6 (1.2 mol [S] L − 1 ) or Li 2 S 8 
(1.0 mol [S] L − 1 ) electrolytes, respectively. All the above procedures were 
conducted in an Ar-ﬁlled glove box. 
4.4. Evaluation on sulfur redox kinetics 
To evaluate the liquid–liquid conversion kinetics, Li 2 S 6 symmetric 
cells were assembled using standard 2025 coin cell conﬁguration with 
two identical CPs punched into disks with a diameter of 13.0 mm as 
the current collector for both the cathode and the anode and a PP 
membrane as the separator. 10 μL Li 2 S 6 electrolyte was added to each 
compartment of the Li 2 S 6 symmetric cell as the basis, and 10 μL blank, 
Me-Se, Ph-Se, or mixed-Se electrolyte was additionally added to the 
compartments for comparison. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) proﬁles and 
electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded on a Solartron 
1470E electrochemical workstation. Speciﬁcally, CV was performed at 
a scan rate of 50 mV s − 1 between − 0.8 and 0.8 V. EIS was collected at 
the open-circuit voltage over a frequency range from 0.1 to 100000 Hz. 
To evaluate the liquid–solid conversion kinetics, Li 2 S 8 cells were 
assembled with the CP used as the cathode current collector (13.0 mm 
in diameter), a piece of Li foil (16.0 mm in diameter and 600 μm in 
thickness) used as the anode, and a PP membrane used as the separator. 
20 μL Li 2 S 8 electrolyte and 20 μL blank, Me-Se, Ph-Se, or mixed-Se 
electrolyte were dropped onto the CP while 5 μL blank electrolyte 
was added in the anode compartment to wet the anode surface. The 
Li 2 S 8 cells were galvanostatically discharged at 0.01 mA to 2.13 V and 
then kept potentiostatically at 2.12 V on the Solartron 1470E elec- 
trochemical workstation to render Li 2 S nucleation and growth. The 
potentiostatic discharge process was ceased after 70000 s and the 
discharge capacity was determined according to Faraday’s law. The 
contribution of Li 2 S 8 /Li 2 S 6 reduction was subtracted by mathematical 
ﬁtting using a previously validated method [55] . 
4.5. Li | organodiselenide cell assembly and electrochemical testing 
To study the redox properties of diﬀerent organodiselenides, Li | 
organodiselenide cells were assembly with the CP used as the cathode 
current collector, a piece of Li foil used as the anode, and a PP membrane 
used as the separator. 20 μL 100 mmol L − 1 Me-Se, Ph-Se, or mixed- 
Se electrolyte were dropped onto the CP while 5 μL blank electrolyte 
was added in the anode compartment to wet the anode surface. The 
Li | organodiselenide cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–
2.6 V on a LAND multichannel battery cycler under current density of 
0.2 C. CV proﬁles were recorded on a Solartron 1470E electrochemical 
workstation at a scan rate of 1 mV s − 1 . 
4.6. Li–S cell assembly and electrochemical testing 
Carbon/sulfur (C/S) composite cathode was fabricated according 
to a previously reported method [56] . Speciﬁcally, C/S composite 
consisting of 70 wt.% S 8 and 30 wt.% CNT was ball-milled with PVDF 
binder at a weight ratio of 9:1 in NMP solvent to form a homogeneous 
slurry. The slurry was then coated on Al foils using doctor blades and 
dried at 60°C for 12.0 h to aﬀord the C/S composite cathodes. 
For Li–S coin cell evaluation, the C/S composite cathode 
was pouched into disks with a diameter of 13.0 mm and the 
areal sulfur loading was 1.2 mg S cm –2 for evaluation under con- 
ventional conditions and 4.0 mg S cm –2 for evaluation under 
harsh conditions. The 600 μm thick lithium foil (16.0 mm in 
diameter) and PP membranes served as the anode and the 
separator, respectively. Blank, Me-Se, Ph-Se, or mixed-Se electrolyte 
was adopted as the electrolyte for comparison. The amount of 
each electrolyte was controlled with an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) 
ratio of 15.7 or 8.5 μL mg S 
− 1 for the Li–S coin cells with 
areal sulfur loading of 1.2 or 4.0 mg S cm − 2 . The Li–S coin 
cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–2.6 V on a 
LAND multichannel battery cycler under diﬀerent C-rates (1 
C = 1672 mA g –1 ). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) tests on 
the fully-discharged Li–S coin cells were conducted on the 
Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of 
0.1 mV s − 1 .

same size as the sulfur cathodes. The thickness of the Li anode was 
50 μm (single side) on Cu current collectors. To assemble a Li–S pouch 
cell, the cathodes and the anodes were stacked layer-by-layer with 
PP membranes as the separator. All the electrodes were controlled in

4.7. Theoretical simulations 
The ﬁrst-principles calculations were conducted in Gaussian (G09) 
[71] program with Becke’s three-parameter hybrid method using the 
Lee-Yang-Parr correlation functional (B3LYP) at 6-311 ++ G(d, p) level 
[72] . The universal solvation model of SMD [73] was used to describe 
the solvation eﬀects in DME environment. The dielectric constant and 
solvent radius were set to be 7.2 and 4.19 Å, respectively. Frequency 
analysis was performed to ensure the ground state of optimized lithium 
polysulﬁdes clusters. The energy levels of lithium polysulﬁdes were 
calculated via the same methods described previously [74] .
