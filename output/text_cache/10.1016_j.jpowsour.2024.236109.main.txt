A B S T R A C T
The polysulfide shuttle is a well-known side reaction in rechargeable Lithium-Sulfur batteries with nonaqueous 
liquid electrolytes. To address this thermodynamically favorable reaction, extensive research has focused on 
developing novel electrolyte systems. Recently, sulfolane-based electrolyte has gained attention due to their 
potential to inhibit the polysulfide shuttle in Li-S batteries. In this preliminary investigation, we analyzed the 
reactivity of polysulfides with Li anode using HPLC and studied the viscosity of various electrolytes at different 
temperatures. Our findings suggested that high viscosity of the sulfolane electrolyte could contribute to its ability 
to suppress the polysulfide shuttle. Additionally, we demonstrated that the presence of a LiNO3 additive is crucial 
for mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect in sulfolane electrolytes, especially at elevated temperature.
1. Introduction
Nonaqueous liquid electrolytes are essential in lithium-based 
rechargeable batteries, as they maintain the mass transfer and the 
ionic conductivity. Those electrolytes must exhibit both chemical and 
electrochemical stability during redox reactions occurring at the bat­
tery’s cathodes and anodes [1–6]. For state-of-the-art lithium-ion bat­
teries (LiBs), the most commonly used electrolyte is lithium 
hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) in a carbonate mixture with organic or 
inorganic additives. However, carbonate-based electrolytes are chemi­
cally incompatible with lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, particularly when 
using elemental sulfur (S8) as the cathode active materials [7].
Current 
research 
has 
focused 
on 
using 
lithium 
bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSi) in a dimethoxyethane (DME) and 
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) mixture with lithium nitrate (LiNO3) as additive [3,
7]. This ether-based electrolyte has demonstrated moderate polysulfide 
solubility, good chemical and electrochemical stability, and high sulfur 
utilization, along with improved redox reversibility for Li-S batteries. As 
a result, Li-S batteries using this ether-based electrolyte demonstrate 
enhanced cycling stability and a significant reduction in the notorious 
“polysulfide shuttle effect”.
However, the cycling stability of Li-S batteries with this ether-based 
electrolyte still falls short of that observed in Li-ion batteries using 
carbonate-based electrolyte. Additionally, the continuous decomposi­
tion of the LiNO3 additive on the lithium anode raises concerns about the 
long-term stability of Li-S batteries [3].
Consequently, the search for novel electrolytes that can improve 
long-term cycling stability and minimize (or even eliminate) the “pol­
ysulfide shuttle effect” remains central focus of Li-S battery research. 
Various alternative electrolytes have been explored, including those 
based on ionic liquids [8], poly-glymes [9], ℇ-caprolactam/acetamide 
[10], fluorinated ethers [11,12], 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI) 
[13], fluorinated pyridine [14].

elevated temperature when using SL-based electrolytes. We also dis­

2. Experimental section
2.1. Chemicals
Sulfur (99.98 %), lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.98 %), lithium nitrate 
(LiNO3,99.99 %), HPLC grade methanol, HPLC grade water, methyl 
triflate, sulfolane (SL) (from Sigma), dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-diox­
olane (DOL), and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSi) 
(battery grade from 3M) were purchased and used without further 
treatment.
2.2. Sample preparation and methods
The polysulfide solutions used in this work were 0.1 M stoichio­
metric Li2S6 in various electrolytes including 1.5 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL 
(1:1), 1.0 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL(1:1) with 0.5 M LiNO3, 1.5 M LiTFSi/SL, 
1.0 M LiTFSi/SL with 0.5 M LiNO3. These solutions were prepared by 
mixing S8 and Li2S (molar ratio of 5:8, to form stoichiometric Li2S6) in 
the different electrolytes, followed by stirring for 48 h at room tem­
perature in Ar-filled glovebox (Innovative Technology).
The chemical stability of Li metal against 0.1 M Li2S6 in the different 
electrolytes were tested by adding 50 mg lithium chip into sample vials 
with air-tight caps, containing 1.0 mL of the polysulfide solution in each 
electrolyte. After vertexing the polysulfide solutions with the lithium 
chips for 5 min, the vials were stored at room temperature or at 60 ◦C for 
4 days. Finally, specific amount of the polysulfide solutions was deriv­
atized with methyl triflate in air-tight HPLC sample vials before removed 
from the glovebox for HPLC analysis.
The derivatization of the polysulfide solution was carried out as 
following: 950 μL of DME and 50 μL of methyl triflate were added into a 
2 mL air-tight HPLC sample vial. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min. 
Then 50 μL of the polysulfide solution was added and the mixture was 
vortexed for an additional 2 min. This process converted the polysulfides 
(Sn
2−) into organosulfides (CH3SnCH3) which were stable under HPLC 
mobile phase conditions and can be separated based on the chain length 
of sulfur [40–42].
A Shimadzu LC-20AD quaternary pump. equipped with a Shimadzu 
SIL-20A autosampler was used to deliver a methanol/water mobile 
phase through a Shimadzu C18 column (4.6*50 mm) at a flow rate of 
0.70 mL/min. The injection volume for the derivatized samples was 5 
μL. A binary gradient of mobile phases was used to elute the injected 
sample under the following condition: 25 % methanol (75 % water) at 0 
min, 100 % methanol at 10 min, maintained until 23 min and back to 25 
% methanol at 26 min. The entire flow from the HPLC was introduced 
into the Shimadzu SPD-M20A detector, and the full wavelength range 
from 190 to 800 nm was recorded by Shimadzu LabSolutions Lite soft­
ware. The chromatograms discussed in this work were replotted from 
collected data at 230 nm.
Sulfur cathodes were prepared by first mixing S (60%wt) with CMK3 
mesoporous carbon (20%wt) through grinding. The mixture was then 
sealed in an autoclave filled with argon and heated at 170 ◦C for 12 h. 
After cooling down to room temperature, carbon black Super C-65(10% 
wt) and PVDF binder (10%wt) were added into the mixture, with N- 
Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as solvent to form homogenous slurry. This 
slurry was coated onto an alumina foil by a doctor-blade coater, and 
vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h. Finally, the electrode sheet was punched 
into disks and transferred into an argon-filled glovebox (both oxygen 
and moisture are less than 5 ppm) for 24 h before being tested. The 
sulfur cathodes have a theoretical areal sulfur loading around 1.5 mg 
cm−2.
All battery tests were conducted in two-electrode CR2032 coin cells 
at either room temperature or at 60 ◦C. Each battery used 15 μL of 
different electrolytes, with a Celgard 3401 membrane as the separator 
and lithium chip as the anodes. After assembled in the Ar-filled glove­
box, all batteries were rested for 12 h before electrochemical testing. For 
cycling tests, a galvanostatic discharge/charge procedure was conduct­
ed between 1.7 and 2.9 V at a constant rate of 0.05 C for all batteries by a 
battery testing system (Arbin BT2000). For the elevated temperature 
test, the batteries were cycled at 60 ◦C in a temperature and humidity 
chamber (CSZ Z8 Plus).
The viscosity measurements of different electrolytes and solvents 
were performed using a viscometer (Brookfield DV-II+ Pro) with a water 
bath to control the temperature.
3. Results and discussion
As shown in Fig. 1(A), at room temperature, Li-S battery (based on 
the same S8/CMK3 composite cathodes) using 1.5 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL 
electrolyte exhibits a noticeable “polysulfide shuttle effect” due to the 
lack of LiNO3. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 1(B), the Li-S battery with 
1.5 M LiTFSi/SL electrolyte shows no significant “polysulfide shuttle 
effect” even after 200 cycles, despite not having the LiNO3 additive.
Further analysis of the chemical reactivity of lithium metal with 0.1 
M Li2S6 solutions in either sulfolane electrolyte or in DME/DOL elec­
trolyte was conducted using HPLC. The results illustrated in Fig. 2
indicated that the polysulfide solution in the sulfolane electrolyte is less 
reactive with lithium metal compared to the polysulfide solution in 
DME/DOL electrolyte. This is clearly shown in Fig. 2(B), where the 
polysulfide profile of the DME/DOL electrolyte after 4 days of contact 
with lithium metal differs significantly from the original polysulfide 
solution, as shown in Fig. 2 (A). The chromatographic peaks corre­
sponding to S3
2−and S4
2−increased while those belonging to S6
2−, S7
2−, S8
2−
and S8 decreased after the reaction [41].
On the other hand, the polysulfide profile in Fig. 2 (F) (representing 
sulfolane electrolyte after 4 days of contact with lithium metal) is quite 
similar to the original sulfolane electrolyte shown in Fig. 2 (E), with only 
a noticeable change of the chromatographic peak corresponding to S8. 
Both electrochemical cycling data (Fig. 1) and HPLC analysis (Fig. 2) 
indicate that the “polysulfide shuttle effect” in Li/S battery with sulfo­
lane electrolyte is minimized, if not eliminated, even without LiNO3 
additive.
Unfortunately, at elevated temperatures (60 ◦C), both Li-S batteries 
using sulfolane electrolyte and DME/DOL electrolyte without LiNO3 
additive exhibit the polysulfide shuttle effect, though to different ex­
tents, as shown in Fig. 1 (C) and 1 (D). In the case of the Li-S battery with 
DME/DOL electrolyte (without LiNO3), the shuttle effect is pronounced, 
preventing the battery from cycling from the start as shown in Fig. 1 (C). 
For the Li-S battery with sulfolane electrolyte (without LiNO3), the 
shuttle effect becomes noticeable after 5 discharge/charge cycles (with 
about 10 % overcharging), and the battery fails to cycle after 35 
discharge/charge cycles due to the severe and notorious shuttle effect as 
shown in Fig. 1 (D).
The HPLC analysis in Fig. 2, which examines the chemical stability of 
Li metal with polysulfide solutions in different electrolytes at 60 ◦C, 
aligns with the battery test observations. In DME/DOL electrolyte 
without LiNO3, Li metal reacted aggressively with long-chain poly­
sulfides at elevated temperature. The HPLC chromatogram (Fig. 2 (C)) 
shows only short-chain polysulfide (S3
2−) and medium-chain polysulfides 
(S4
2−and S5
2−) in very low abundance. In a cycling Li-S battery, those

other factors which could also contribute to the shuttle inhibition. For 
example, in the early work by S. Yoon et al. [23], a series of electrolytes 
with a binary solvent composition of sulfolane and DME were tested. 
The results clearly showed that when the sulfolane to DME volume ratio 
decreased to 7:3, the polysulfide shuttle effect emerged and negatively 
impacted the performance of Li-S battery. Fundamentally, the poly­
sulfide shuttle phenomenon originates from the thermodynamically 
favorable reaction between lithium metal and long-chain polysulfides. 
This makes the shuttle phenomenon inevitable, and can only be miti­
gated through kinetic approaches as demonstrated in numerous studies 
on Li-S batteries with nonaqueous liquid electrolytes [4–7].
As a result, most strategies to mitigate polysulfide shuttle effect in Li- 
S batteries focus on slowing down or restricting the reaction between 
long-chain polysulfides and Li anode kinetically. Therefore, any physical 
or chemical properties of electrolytes that influence the reaction could 
affect the polysulfide shuttle effect. For instance, the viscosity of sulfo­
lane electrolytes is much higher than that of DME/DOL electrolytes as 
shown in Table 1. This high viscosity could be a reason for the different 
cycling performance observed: the sulfolane electrolyte might kineti­
cally slow down mass transfer due to its high viscosity thereby miti­
gating the polysulfide shuttle effect even without LiNO3 additive. In 
contrast, the low viscosity of DME/DOL electrolyte could accelerate the 
mass transfer, promoting the polysulfide shuttle effect in the absence of 
LiNO3 additive.

For sulfolane electrolyte with LiNO3, at elevated temperature, the

4. Conclusion
Sulfolane electrolytes show promising nonaqueous electrolytes for 
various lithium-based rechargeable batteries. Our preliminary study 
indicates that sulfolane electrolyte can significantly reduce the poly­
sulfide shuttle effect in Li-S batteries at room temperature even without 
LiNO3 additive. One possible reason for this inhibition effect is the very 
high viscosity of sulfolane electrolyte, which could kinetically slow 
down the diffusion of polysulfides between cathode and lithium anode. 
Additionally, the difference in the SEI composition on lithium anode 
surface could also contribute to this effect. However, at elevated tem­
perature, which thermodynamically and kinetically favors the poly­
sulfide shuttle, a severe polysulfide shuttle effect was observed in the Li- 
S batteries with sulfolane electrolyte without LiNO3. Only when LiNO3 is 
added to the sulfolane electrolyte can the polysulfide shuttle effect be 
effectively mitigated at higher temperature in a Li-S battery. Therefore, 
while sulfolane electrolyte shows some advantages, it is ultimately a 
compromised solution, similar to DME/DOL electrolyte, for the use Li-S 
batteries.
