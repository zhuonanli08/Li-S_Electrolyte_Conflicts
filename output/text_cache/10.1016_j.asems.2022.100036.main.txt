A B S T R A C T
Among various energy storage devices, lithium-sulfur batteries have attracted widespread attention due to their
high theoretical energy density and speciﬁc capacity. To improve the performance and realize practical appli-
cations of lithium-sulfur batteries, it is crucial to unravel the dynamic evolution and reaction mechanism at the
electrode/electrolyte interfaces during cycling. Nevertheless, the details are still not well known despite generous
efforts, which need more in situ and non-destructive imaging characterizations. Herein, we have combined AFM
with an electrochemical workstation to dynamically visualize the morphological evolution and structural changes
of the interfacial process, which reveals the lithium iodide-mediated interfacial reactions in lithium-sulfur bat-
teries. In situ measurements showed that the electrode surface was coated by a reticular layer consists of elemental
iodine and polyether with lithium iodide additive during charging, which could effectively prevent insolube
sulﬁdes from gathering on the surface and improve the cycling performances of lithium-sulfur batteries. These
ﬁndings shed new light on the interfacial mechanism and establish design ideas for the future development of
better electrolytes for lithium-sulfur batteries.
1. Introduction
The sharp rise in both energy supply and demand has sped up the
development of the next-generation energy storage systems. Lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) batteries, with sulfur serving as the positive electrode ma-
terial and lithium serving as the negative electrode material, have un-
dergone extensive research to fully utilize their properties. Because of
their theoretical energy density of 2600 W h kg1 and theoretical speciﬁc
capacity of 1675 mA h g1, respectively, these batteries have been
regarded as promising candidates for energy storage devices [1,2]. In
addition, Li-S batteries have better prospects in a competitive market
thanks to their low cost and environmental friendliness. The poor elec-
trochemical reaction kinetics of sulfur and polysulﬁdes, however, restricts
their ability to produce high power. Furthermore, during the discharge
and charge processes, highly soluble intermediate lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs) are produced and migrate freely between the cathode and anode,
causing the notorious shuttle effect [3]. Consequently, Li-S batteries suffer
from low active material utilization, low coulombic efﬁciency and poor
cycle performance, impeding their commercialization [4,5].
So far, several strategies have been adopted to handle these problems
and enhance the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries, such as
improving sulfur cathode materials [6], optimizing alternative electro-
lytes [7] and electrolyte additives [8,9]. It is crucial to use additives in
electrolytes

cording to recent reports, lithium iodide (LiI) could induce the formation
of a protective ﬁlm on both the Li2S-based cathode and the Li anode [13,
14]. However, the current understanding of this protective ﬁlm is still in
the theoretical model stage, and there is a lack of intuitive interface
process characterization and reaction mechanism understanding to prove
this conjecture. Therefore, in situ observation of the interfacial process is
important for lithium-sulfur batteries. Extensive efforts must be made to
analyze the interfacial mechanisms to further optimize battery perfor-
mance in Li-S batteries from lab-scale operation to commercial
application.

tions and in situ imaging characterizations have been developed rapidly,
making it practical to acquire the mechanism understanding of Li-S

In this study, we successfully combined AFM with an electrochemical

iodide-mediated interfacial process at nanoscale spatial resolution. The
research established a relationship between the structure and reactivity
for Li-S batteries by providing light on the deposition, growth, and

that LiI additive could induce the formation of the reticular ﬁlm which

interface at the potential of anodic 3.50 V. Furthermore, after 10 cycles,
insoluble sulﬁdes were only partially oxidized and increased without LiI
additive, resulting in capacity fading of Li-S batteries. The above in situ
AFM interfacial investigations provide support for the exploration of the
basic mechanism of Li-S battery reaction and offer ideas for further
optimization of the battery performances.

2. Material and methods
2.1. Material preparation
1,2-Dimethosyethane (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), dimethyl car-
bonate (DMC), and lithium bis (triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI)
(purity >99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Li2S (purity
>99.9%) and LiI (anhydrous, purity >99.95%) were purchased from
Alfa-Aesar.
2.2. Fabrication of in situ AFM cells
In the Li-S model cell, freshly cleaved highly oriented pyrolytic
graphite (HOPG) (ZYH type, Bruker, 12 mm  12 mm  2 mm) was
selected as the working electrode, and the Li2S8 solution which is added
LiI or not as the catholyte. Both the counter and reference electrodes were
lithium strips. The counter electrode protruded from the small hole at the
top, and was wound into an O ring and placed in a circular groove to
increase the contact area with the electrolyte. The Li2S8 solution was
prepared by dissolving the desired amount of stoichiometric S and Li2S
with 0.5 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME solution (1:1 in volume) with 0.05 M LiI
as an additive, followed by vigorous magnetic stirring at 60 C for 24 h
[20]. The digital image of the cell for AFM monitoring was provided
(Fig. S1, Supporting Information). In situ monitoring of the Li-S reaction
on HOPG was accomplished by a potentiostat/galvanostat coupled with
AFM (Bruker Multimode 8 with a Nanoscope V controller). Since the
electrolyte, lithium anode and interface products in the lithium-sulfur
battery are all sensitive to water and oxygen, AFM was built in a glove
box (MIKROUNA, Super (1220/750), <0.1 ppm of H2O and <0.1 ppm of
O2) for testing to ensure its anhydrous and oxygen-free atmosphere. In
addition, to get rid of the inﬂuence of external micro-vibration distur-
bance during image scanning, a suspended vibration isolation platform
was built for AFM inside the glove box. In this study, each of AFM images
took 290 s with the scan rate of 0.888 Hz. Cyclic voltammetry (CV)
curves coupled with AFM scanning were measured by the electro-
chemical workstation (Metrohm Autolab, PGSTAT302 N) in the range of
1.5–3.5 V at a sweep rate of 0.3 mV s1. During the discharge process, the
potential was swept toward negative potential from open circuit poten-
tial (OCP, about 2.40 V) to 1.5 V and positive to 3.5 V for the subsequent
oxidation. HOPG surface was scanned by electrochemical AFM with an

2.3. Material characterization
Samples of products were obtained by the Li-S model cell which
discharged/charged to speciﬁc states, washed with DMC to remove the
residual electrolyte on the surface, and dried in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox for
24 h before the characterization. The sample holder which is protected
by a sample hood with a vacuum circuit on the outside was used to
transfer samples. It can protect samples from being exposed to the at-
mosphere from the time it is prepared in the glove box until it enters the
spectrometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was per-
formed on an ESCALab 250Xi (Thermo Fisher) using monochromated Al
Kα (1486.6 eV) with an energy of 150 W. The base pressure was
approximately 3  109 mbar, and the binding energies were referenced
to the hydrocarbon C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The morphology of the ob-
tained samples was examined by an SEM instrument (SU 8020) operated
at 10 kV. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measure-
ments were conducted on an electrochemical workstation (ModuLab XM
ECS) with CR2032 coin cells. They were assembled using celgard as a
separator, lithium foil as the counter electrode, S/C coating slurry (70 wt
% S) as the cathode, and 0.05 mL of the above-described electrolytes. The

3. Results and discussion
3.1. In situ AFM study of interfacial evolution
As shown in Fig. 1a, in situ electrochemical (EC)-AFM with environ-
mental control was applied to directly monitor the electrochemical
process on the cathode/electrolyte interface at nanoscale in a Li-S model
cell. Preceding the experiment, the ﬁrst cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves
of the Li-S model cell with/without 0.05 M LiI additive are displayed in
Fig. 1b. In the absence of LiI, two cathodic peaks at 2.20 V (a0) and 1.80 V
(b0), respectively, attribute to the reduction of soluble Li2S8 to interme-
diate polysulﬁdes and the further reduction of those polysulﬁdes to
insoluble sulﬁdes [21]. The anodic peak at 2.75 V (c0) is associated with
the reverse oxidation of insoluble sulﬁdes. Compared with the CV curve
obtanied without LiI, the cathodic peaks at 2.20 V and 1.80 V and the
anodic peak at 2.75 V are all signiﬁcantly enhanced, suggesting a pro-
moting effect of LiI. Besides that, when LiI is added to the electrolyte, the
CV curve consists of one redox peak centered at 3.30 V, which can be
attributed to the oxidation of I to I3
 (d00) and its reversible reduction
reaction (e00) [22]. It is worth noting that the AFM model cell exhibits
higher overpotential than the coin cell (Fig. S2), which could be due to
the large polarization of the AFM electrochemical cell.
Fig. 1c–h show the in situ AFM images of HOPG with 0.5 M LiTFSI in
DOL/DME (1:1) with LiI additive during the discharge process. Initially,
atomically ﬂat terraces of HOPG can be observed at an open circuit po-
tential (OCP) of 2.40 V (Fig. 1c), and the following discharge process
reveals the morphology evolution of reduction products, as illustrated in
Fig. 1d–h. In this work, each AFM image requires 290 s to process with
the scan rate of 0.888 Hz. When the potential sweeps negatively to 2.10
V, nanoparticles start to deposit on the electrode surface as indicated by
the yellow arrow (Fig. 1d). Holding the potential at 2.10 V for 10 min, the
nanoparticles are aggregated into large clumps (Fig. 1e). The height
distribution of discharge products on HOPG was statistically analyzed
(Fig. S5, Table S1), as shown in the Fig. 1i, indicating that the height of
nanoparticles is approximately 53.79  10.94 nm. Parallel experiments
are shown in Fig. S3, displaying similar results. Discharged to 1.80 V,
micron-sized lamellar products gradually appear and are dotted on the
electrode (Fig. 1f–h, Fig. S4). As represented by the reduction peaks in
the CV curves, the voltage of discharge products growth correlates well
with the reduction peaks of polysulﬁde intermediates to insoluble sul-
ﬁdes. Additionally, the generation of nanoparticles and lamellar dis-
charged products was also observed during the discharge process without
LiI additive (Fig. S6).
As depicted in Fig. 2, the interface topography changes during the
charge process were further observed on HOPG with LiI additive. The

3.2. Characterization of the products
To clarify the evolution of the chemical composition during the
electrochemical process, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
technique was used to analyze the surface chemical properties of the
deposited products. HOPG electrodes were taken from the Li–S model
cell at different stages of the electrochemical process. Nanoparticles and
lamellar nanosheets were obtained at 2.10 V and 1.80 V by holding the
potential for 2 h. As shown in Fig. 3, for the electrode discharged to 2.10
V, peaks at approximately 162.0 eV in the S 2p spectrum are ascribed to
Li2S2, which conﬁrms nanoparticles are Li2S2. For the electrode dis-
charged to 1.80 V, lamellar nanosheets can be certiﬁed to Li2S because of
the peak at 161.0 eV in the S 2p spectrum. The peaks at 169.0 eV, 167.0
eV, and 163.5 eV belong to –SO2, –SO3 and polysulﬁdes, respectively,
which might be from the residual electrolytes [23]. Correspondingly, the
XPS measurements of the regular system without LiI were employed and
acquired the same conclusion that nanoparticles were Li2S2 and lamellar
products were Li2S (Fig. S8). In addition, the I 3d spectra are mainly
derived from LiI, as evidenced by the I 3d5/2 peak with binding energy at
619.1 eV and the I 3d3/2 peak with binding energy at 630.2 eV, which
conﬁrm that LiI exists on the surface of HOPG. Besides, it should be noted
that when charged to 3.50 V, the minor peaks at 621.2 eV and 632.3 eV
refer to the I 3d5/2 and I 3d3/2 of elemental iodine, respectively. The I 3d
XPS peak shifts about 1.2 eV toward higher energy after the charge
process, supporting the hypothesis that LiI can convert to I2 upon
charging. As a result, it is conﬁrmed that upon charging to 3.50 V, the
surface coated by the reticular ﬁlm contains elemental iodine, which is
due to the presence of the LiI additive.
SEM and EDS analysis reveal that the presence of S element on the
discharged HOPG (Fig. S9a) further comﬁrms the generation of insoluble
Li2S/Li2S2 during the discharge process. For HOPG charged to 3.50 V,
there are a high content of the C element and I element (Fig. S9b). It has
been proposed that electrolytes containing iodide anions can engage in
DME/I oxidation processes with I neutral radicals, leading to hydrogen
abstraction by I and then results in the formation of a DME(-H) radical
and HþI [13]. By building comb-branched oligoethers, the DME(-H)
radicals can effectively initiate condensation reactions. Based on the in
situ AFM observations and ex situ characterizations, it can be concluded
that the addition of LiI to electrolytes could induce the formation of
a I-rich polyether ﬁlm on the surface of electrodes during the charge
process.
3.3. LiI–mediated reaction mechanism
To elucidate the mechanism of the system with LiI additive, in situ
AFM was carried out to observe the evolution process on HOPG after 10
cycles as illustrated in Fig. 4. The 11th cyclic voltammogram curves ob-
tained in the Li-S model cell at a sweep rate of 3 mV s1 with/without LiI
additive are shown in Fig. S10. Notably, for the system without LiI ad-
ditive, a large amount of insoluble nanoparticles with a thickness of 8.91
 3.30 nm are left on the electrode after 10 cycles (Fig. 4a), demon-
strating a terrible cycle reversibility. Fig. 4b exhibits that no obvious
changes of the system without LiI are observed until the potential sweeps
negatively to 1.50 V. At 1.50 V, ﬂocculent products with a thickness of
104.10  5.27 nm are deposited on the electrode. In contrast, for the
system with LiI, there are small amounts of nanoparticles with a thickness
of 1.42  0.53 nm left after 10 cycles (Fig. 4e) and products cover the
electrode when further discharged to 1.78 V, implying the reversible
conversion between the polysulﬁdes and insoluble sulﬁdes. Moreover,
with the charge proceeding, there are no signiﬁcant changes can be
detected in the two systems before charged to 2.80 V (Fig. S11a, c).
Products in the system without LiI start to dissolve (Fig. 4c) and

out to investigate the battery process during cycling. Fig. S12 presents the
EIS results of coin cells with/without LiI before and after 100 cycles,
respectively. The charge-transfer resistance is represented by the
depressed arc in the high frequency of the spectra, while ion dif-
fusionprocess is represented by the inclined line in the low frequency. It is
clear that it holds much smaller semi circle with LiI additive, indicating
the improved ionic conductivity and higher activity of charge transfer
reaction.
Based on the above results, the reaction mechanism is illustrated in
Fig. 5. It reveals that early in the discharge process, nanoparticle Li2S2 is
deposited and steadily accumulated. During the subsequent discharge
process, lamellar Li2S is quickly deposited, covering the underlying Li2S2.
Lamellar Li2S disappears during oxidation, while a small amount of Li2S2
is left on the HOPG surface. Besides, during the charge process, LiI can
help to form a DME-derived protective polyether ﬁlm, which prevents
insoluble sulﬁdes from gathering on the surface of the electrode after a
number of cycles.
4. Conclusions
To conclude, the real-time observation at nanoscale of the dynamic
evolution on the cathode/electrolyte interface was successfully moni-
tored by in situ EC-AFM, providing more evidence and details of the
cathode interphase layer for further development of Li-S batteries. Dur-
ing the charge process, the surface of the electrode was coated by the
reticular layer consisting of elemental iodine and polyether owing to the
presence of the LiI additive. Besides, LiI additive can improve the utili-
zation of active materials and the reversibility of the battery after
numerous cycles. This work reveals valuable insight into the LiI–medi-
ated interfacial dynamics in Li-S batteries, which gives reasonable sug-
gestions for increasing the capacity, reducing overpotential, and
suppressing side reactions in practical Li-S batteries. Such a discovery
opens up a wide range of possibilities for electrolyte additives and offers
guidance for developing Li-S batteries.
