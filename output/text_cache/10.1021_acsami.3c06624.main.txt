ABSTRACT: High-concentration lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)-
imide/1,2-dimethoxyethane (LiFSI/DME) electrolytes are promis-
ing candidates for highly reversible lithium−metal anodes.
However, the performance of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries
with a high concentration of LiFSI/DME declines because LiFSI
reacts irreversibly with lithium polysulfide, which is formed during
the charge−discharge process of Li−S batteries. Hence, to apply
high-concentration LiFSI/DME to Li−S batteries, we investigated
carbon with an appropriate pore size for use in a sulfur composite
cathode and optimized the composition of high-concentration
LiFSI/DME. The results showed that the combination of carbon
with mesopores of 2−3 nm diameter and 3 M LiFSI in DME/
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (HFE) (1:1
by vol.) provided a high-rate capability (943 mA h g−1 at a rate of 2 C). Moreover, the ratio of the 50th discharge capacity to the 2nd
discharge capacity (capacity retention) improved from 50.0 to 61.6% with HFE dilution of high-concentration LiFSI/DME. The
improved performance was achieved by suppressing the dissolution of lithium polysulfide, decreasing the viscosity of the electrolyte,
and forming a thin solid electrolyte interface on the lithium−metal anode due to HFE dilution.
KEYWORDS: LiFSI, DME, localized high-concentration electrolytes, lithium metal, sulfur, microporous carbon, lithium−sulfur battery
■INTRODUCTION
Recently, electricity generated by energy sources that do not
emit greenhouse gases such as CO2, wind, and solar power, has
been promoted as a global warming countermeasure.1,2 To
efficiently utilize clean energy, a high-energy-density storage
device is essential.3 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries with
sulfur cathodes and lithium−metal anodes have received
attention as high-energy-density storage systems.4 The
theoretical specific capacities of sulfur and lithium are 1672
and 3860 mA h g−1, respectively, providing Li−S batteries with
twice the volumetric energy density and six times the
gravimetric energy density of lithium-ion batteries.5 Therefore,
Li−S batteries are ideal storage devices for clean energy
sources. However, the practical application of Li−S batteries
faces several challenges. At the cathode, sulfur reacts with
lithium ions to form lithium polysulfide (Li2Sx) during the
charge−discharge process.6,7 In particular, Li2Sx (x = 6−8)
exhibits high solubility in most organic electrolytes.8,9 The
dissolution of Li2Sx causes loss of the active material, redox
shuttling, and pollution of the lithium anode, which hinders
the stable cycling of Li−S batteries.
To address this problem, Nazar et al. reported a method for
confining sulfur in the pores of highly ordered mesoporous
carbon.10 The dissolution of Li2Sx into the electrolyte is
kinetically suppressed owing to the interaction between the
carbon materials and Li2Sx. Consequently, the Li−S batteries
are successfully charged and discharged without a redox
shuttle. However, mesoporous carbons cannot completely
inhibit polar Li2Sx dissolution because the carbon surface is
generally nonpolar.11 Hence, surface modification of meso-
porous carbons with polymers, functional groups, or metal
compounds should be applied to impart polarity to the carbon
surface.12−15
Lithium−metal anodes also have fundamental problems.
Lithium metal reacts chemically with all electrolytes; therefore,
continuous electrolyte decomposition occurs and a thick solid
electrolyte interface (SEI) forms on the anode. This results in a
low coulombic efficiency and high cell resistance.16,17 More-
over, the lithium−metal anode undergoes repeated deposition
and dissolution during the charge−discharge process, which
induces lithium dendrite formation.18 The lithium dendrites

discharge process forms dead lithium, which loses its electronic
contact with the anode.19 Dead lithium is electrochemically

Various electrolytes and additives have been investigated to
suppress the excess decomposition of electrolytes and
formation of lithium dendrites.20,21 A high-concentration
lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide/1,2-dimethoxyethane
(LiFSI/DME) electrolyte has been reported to achieve a
stable SEI and dendrite-free lithium−metal anode.22 In
particular, a Li/Cu cell with 3−5 M LiFSI/DME showed a
high coulombic efficiency. The application of high-concen-
tration LiFSI/DME in Li−S batteries suppresses electrolyte
decomposition and short circuits, thereby improving battery
performance. However, Li2Sx dissolved from the mesoporous
carbon−sulfur composite cathode reacts
with bis-
(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI) irreversibly.23 This leads to loss
of cathode active material and reduces the coulombic efficiency
and capacity of Li−S batteries.24
Carbonate electrolytes that react with Li2Sx irreversibly
cannot be used for Li−S batteries; however, we previously
reported that Li−S batteries with carbonate electrolytes could
be cycled using a microporous carbon−sulfur composite
cathode.25 This is because the small pore size (<2 nm) of
the microporous carbon suppressed contact between Li2Sx in
the micropores and the solvent.26,27 Microporous carbon
suppresses Li2Sx dissolution more effectively than mesoporous
carbon, and Li−S batteries with carbonate electrolytes exhibit
high cycle stability. These studies suggest that carbon materials
with appropriate pore sizes can be used to realize Li−S
batteries containing high-concentration LiFSI/DME electro-
lytes with superior cycling performance.
In this study, we investigated the pore size of carbon
materials used in cathodes to achieve high-performance Li−S
batteries with high-concentration LiFSI/DME. Furthermore,
we prepared a suitable high-concentration LiFSI/DME
electrolyte for use with the micro-mesoporous carbon−sulfur
composite cathode and analyzed the effect of the electrolyte on
the cathode and anode. 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-
tetrafluoropropylether (HFE) was used for the electrolyte
optimization. Because HFE is one of the hydrofluoroethers
with the lowest lithium-solvating ability,28 dilution with HFE
significantly reduces the viscosity of high-concentration LiFSI/
DME while maintaining the locally high concentration of the
LiFSI/DME electrolyte.29 Moreover, HFE suppresses the side
reaction between Li2Sx and lithium−metal anodes, and
improves the deposition morphology of lithium−metal
anodes;30 thus, HFE is a promising solvent for electrolyte
optimization. We believe that our systematic evaluation of the
physical and chemical characteristics of the carbon materials,
electrolyte conditions, and effects of each electrolyte
component on the cathode and anode will guide the design
of high-performance Li−S batteries.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Preparation of Porous Carbon−Sulfur Composites. Three
commercial porous carbons with different pore size distributions
(Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso) were used as purchased. Porous
carbon−sulfur composites (Cmicro−S, Cmicro−meso−S, and Cmeso−S)
were prepared using the same procedure described in a previous
study.31 Additional details are provided in the Supporting
Information.
Characterization of Materials. The shapes and surfaces of the
porous carbon samples were observed using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, FlexSEM 1000 II, Hitachi High-Technologies
Co.). The accelerating voltage and working distance were set to 5.00
kV and 5 mm, respectively. The particle size distribution of the porous
carbon samples was analyzed using a laser scattering particle size
distribution analyzer (LA-950, HORIBA, Ltd.). Pore structures of
porous carbons were investigated by the N2 adsorption/desorption
method using a fully automatic gas adsorption amount measuring
device (autosorb iQ, Anton Paar Japan Co.). The specific surface area
and pore volume distribution were calculated using Brunauer−
Emmett−Teller (BET) and quenched solid density functional theory
(QSDFT) methods, respectively. The functional groups on the
porous carbon surfaces were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS, JPS-9010MC, JEOL Co.). Mg Kα radiation was
used as the X-ray source. The spectra were corrected such that the
adventitious carbon peak in the C 1s spectrum was at 284.8 eV.
Sulfur in the pores or on the surface of porous carbons was
investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Ultima IV, Rigaku Co.)
with Cu Kα radiation as the X-ray source. The accelerating voltage
was 40 kV and the tube current was 40 mA. The maximum peak
intensities of all the XRD spectra were standardized to the same value.
The sulfur content of the porous carbon−sulfur composites was
measured using thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA, DTG-60AH,
Shimadzu Co.). The rate of temperature increase was set to 5 °C
min−1, and the carrier gas was Ar. The elemental distribution of the
porous carbon−sulfur composites was investigated using energy-
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, FlexSEM 1000 II, Hitachi High-
Technologies Co.). The accelerating voltage was set to 15.0 kV.
The viscosity and ionic conductivity of various electrolytes was
investigated at 25 °C using a viscometer (VM-10A, Sekonic Co.) and
an impedance analyzer (VMP-300, Bio-Logic Science Instruments
Ltd.), respectively. The suppression effect of the electrolytes on Li2Sx
dissolution was measured using ultraviolet−visible absorption (UV−
vis) spectroscopy. Deuterium and halogen light sources were used for
measurement (DH-2000-BAL, Ocean Optics).
The effect of the electrolyte on the lithium anode surface was
analyzed using SEM and XPS (PHI Quantes, ULVAC-PHI, Inc.). The
accelerating voltage for SEM was set to 15.0 kV. The X-ray source for
XPS was Al Kα radiation. The spectra of the top surface were
corrected such that the adventitious carbon peak in the C 1s spectrum
was at 284.8 eV. After etching, the spectra were corrected such that
the peak ascribed to LiF in the F 1s spectrum was at 685 eV. The
lithium anode was removed from the charged−discharged cells after
10 cycles, washed with HFE, and analyzed. All the experiments were
performed under Ar or vacuum.
Preparation of Electrolytes. LiFSI was added to DME and
stirred overnight to prepare 3, 3.6, and 4 M LiFSI (Nippon Shokubai
Co., Ltd.)/DME (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.). To prepare electro-
lytes22 consisting of the two solvents, DME was mixed with HFE
(Daikin Industries, Ltd.) in a volume ratio of 1:1. The salt
concentration of the co-solvent was 3 M (3 M LiFSI in DME/
HFE). The molar ratio of LiFSI/DME/HFE in the 3 M LiFSI in
DME/HFE solution was 1:1.2:0.83. All operations were performed in
an Ar-filled glovebox.
Electrochemical Measurements. The cathodes with Cmicro−S,
Cmicro−meso−S, or Cmeso−S were prepared using the same procedure
described in a previous study,31 and details are provided in the
Supporting Information. The lithium−metal anode was prepared by
punching out Li foil (200 μm, Honjo Metal Co., Ltd.) with a 13 mm
diameter. The anode’s surface area was 1.33 cm2. Cells adopting a
porous carbon−sulfur composite cathode, lithium−metal anode, and
electrolyte were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. Two microporous
polypropylene membranes were used to prevent short circuits. The
separator’s surface area was 2.54 cm2. The electrolyte used for each
Li−S battery was fixed at 52.0 μL.
Constant current charge−discharge tests were conducted using a
battery tester (BTS-2004H, Nagano Co., Ltd.). The voltage range was

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of Porous Carbons. Various measure-
ments were performed to reveal the physical and chemical
structures of porous carbon materials. The particle shapes and
surfaces of the porous carbons were observed by SEM (Figure
1a). Cmicro and Cmeso exhibited rugged shapes and rough
surfaces, respectively. Cmicro−meso had a rounded shape and
smooth surface. Particle size distributions were measured to
determine the exact particle size of the porous carbons. The
median diameters of Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso were 3.49,
2.52, and 49.8 μm, respectively (Figure S1). A small particle
size increases the surface area of porous carbon−sulfur
composite cathodes and shortens the electronic and ionic
conductive paths, which improves the discharge capacity and
rate capability.32−35
N2 adsorption/desorption measurements were performed to
investigate the pore structures of the porous carbons; Figure
1b shows the isotherms. According to IUPAC classification,
the isotherms of Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso are classified as
type I(a), type I(b), and type IV(a), respectively.36 Therefore,
Cmicro mainly contains micropores, Cmicro−meso contains micro-
pores with a narrow range of slight mesopores, and Cmeso
mainly contains mesopores. Figure 1c shows the pore size
distributions of the porous carbon materials. Cmicro has a pore
diameter of 2 nm or less and is therefore a typical microporous
carbon. Although Cmicro−meso mainly has micropores, there are a
small number of mesopores of 2 nm or more and 3 nm or less.
Cmeso has a pore diameter of 2 nm or more and 50 nm or less;
therefore, it is a typical mesoporous carbon. The specific
surface areas and pore volumes of the porous carbon samples
were calculated from the isotherms (Table 1). The pore
diameter was correlated with the specific surface area and pore
volume. Carbon with a large pore volume can confine a large
amount of sulfur; thus, Cmicro−meso or Cmeso could offer the
advantage of cell energy density over Cmicro.
XPS measurements were performed to investigate the
surface composition of the porous carbons. Figure S2 shows
wide-scan XPS spectra. All the porous carbons contained only
carbon and oxygen. Narrow-scan XPS was used to analyze the
surfaces in detail (Figure 1d,e). The C 1s spectra of all porous
carbons had peaks around 284.6 and 288.6 eV, which were
attributed to C−C and −COO bonds, respectively.37 More-
over, the peaks around 532.9 eV in the O 1s spectra were
ascribed to C−O and CO bonds in the porous carbons.38
The surface composition ratios, calculated from the peak areas
of the C 1s and O 1s spectra (Table 1), revealed that Cmicro
had the highest number of oxygen functional groups and Cmeso
had the lowest number of oxygen-containing functional groups
among all carbon materials. It has been reported that oxygen
functional groups on carbon surfaces suppress the dissolution
of Li2Sx owing to its polarization.39−41 Therefore, Cmicro is

Characterization of Porous Carbon−Sulfur Compo-
sites. The sulfur content of the porous carbon−sulfur
composites was measured using TGA and DTA (Figures 2
and S3). Sulfur generally evaporates at around 155 °C. Thus,
the weight loss observed in TGA curves around 155 °C was
consistent with the sulfur content of porous carbon−sulfur
composites. The sulfur contents of Cmicro−S, Cmicro−meso−S,
and Cmeso−S were 21.4, 51.2, and 60.0 wt %, respectively.
However, the TGA curves of porous carbon−sulfur composites
showed a weight loss onset temperature slightly higher than
155 °C, indicating that additional energy was needed for the
evaporation of sulfur in the micropores.
EDX measurements were carried out to obtain elemental
mappings of the porous carbon−sulfur composites (Figure
S4). All the porous carbon−sulfur composites consisted of
carbon, oxygen, and sulfur. All the elements were uniformly
distributed. Table S1 shows the weight ratios of the elements
obtained from EDX mapping, and the trend is consistent with
that of XPS and TGA.
The crystallinity of microporous carbon−sulfur composites
measured using XRD is discussed in the Supporting
Information (Figure S5).
Electrochemical Performances of Porous Carbon−Sulfur
Composite Cathodes with 3 M LiFSI/DME. Galvanostatic
charge−discharge measurements were performed to investigate
the effect of the pore structure of porous carbons on the
performance of Li−S batteries with 3 M LiFSI/DME. Figure
3a shows the charge−discharge curves of the Li−S batteries at
the 1st and 50th cycles. Cmicro−S showed only one plateau at
low potential (∼1.6−1.9 V). This plateau was attributed to the
formation of Li2Sx (x = 1, 2).14 In contrast, Cmicro−meso−S and
Cmeso−S exhibited a plateau at a high potential (∼2.0 V) in
addition to a low-potential plateau. The high-potential plateau
was attributed to the reduction of sulfur to Li2Sx (x = 4−8).14
Figure 3b shows the cycle plots of the Li−S batteries. Cmicro−S
had high capacity retention (83.4%), which was calculated by
dividing the discharge capacity at the 50th cycle (934.5 mA h
g−1) by the discharge capacity at the 2nd cycle (1121 mA h
g−1). Furthermore, Cmicro−S showed high coulombic efficiency
(average 99.4%). This stable cycle performance could be due
to the suppression of Li2Sx dissolution, which causes loss of the
cathode active material and redox shuttling for two reasons: (i)
micropores suppress the formation of Li2Sx (x = 4−8) and42
(ii) the decomposition products of the electrolyte formed by
charging and discharging completely cover the pores, which
inhibits contact between the electrolyte solvent and the
cathode active material.27
The coulombic efficiency of Cmeso−S significantly exceeded
100% at the 11th cycle, and the Li−S battery with the Cmeso−S
cathode did not reach the charge cut-off voltage at the 14th
cycle. Qian and Fu reported that the molar ratio of Li+ to DME
in 3 M LiFSI/DME is 1:2.2, and Li+ is coordinated with five
oxygen atoms of FSI−and DME molecules.22,43 From this
electrolyte composition, they suggested that it contained
uncoordinated DME molecules. In the case of Cmeso−S, Li2Sx
(x = 6−8) would be able to dissolve in the uncoordinated
DME of the 3 M LiFSI/DME electrolyte because Cmeso has
mainly mesopores, from which Li2Sx dissolves into uncoordi-
nated DME easily. The dissolved Li2Sx in the electrolyte
removed from the cycled cell were confirmed by UV−vis
spectroscopy (Figure S6). Cmeso−S and Cmicro−meso−S had a
peak ascribed to Sx
2−at 350−500 nm; the peak intensity of
Cmeso−S was approximately four times higher than that of
Cmicro−meso−S. In addition, the Sx
2−peak was not observed in
the Cmicro−S spectrum.24,44 Thus, Cmeso−S could not reach the
cut-off voltage owing to the redox shuttle, pollution of the
lithium anode, and the irreversible reaction between dissolved
Li2Sx and LiFSI.23

Electrochemical performances of Cmicro−meso−S cath-
ode with various concentrations of LiFSI/DME electro-
lytes. The Cmicro−S/Li battery with 3 M LiFSI/DME
exhibited high cycle stability owing to the suppression of
Li2Sx dissolution. However, the sulfur content of Cmicro−S was
only 21.4 wt %, which is disadvantageous in terms of energy
density of Li−S batteries. Hence, we attempted to stabilize the
cycle performance of Cmicro−meso−S with a relatively high sulfur
content (51.2 wt %). The capacity decay of the Cmicro−meso−S/
Li battery with 3 M LiFSI/DME was due to Li2Sx dissolution
caused by a few mesopores and uncoordinated DME
molecules. Some reports have shown that high-concentration
electrolytes contain less uncoordinated DME; thus, the Li2Sx
dissolution is suppressed.24,45 High-concentration LiFSI/DME
above 3.6 M without uncoordinated DME would realize
Cmicro−meso−S/Li batteries with high energy density and high
cycle stability.
Figure 4a shows the charge−discharge curves of Li−S
batteries with Cmicro−meso−S cathodes and various concen-
trations of LiFSI/DME electrolytes at the 1st and 50th cycles.
For 3 M LiFSI/DME, two plateaus were observed. The high-
potential plateau, which is related to readily soluble Li2Sx (x =
6−8), disappeared as the concentration of LiFSI increased
(Figure S7). In the case of 3.6 and 4 M LiFSI/DME, nearly all
of the DME were coordinated to Li+ because the molar ratios
of LiFSI and DME were 1:2 and 1:1.4, respectively.22 Thus,
the dissolution of Li2Sx was significantly inhibited and the high-
potential plateau disappeared. To investigate the suppression
effect of the electrolytes on Li2Sx dissolution, solutions
prepared by mixing each electrolyte and 0.2 M Li2S8/DME
at a volume ratio of 3:1 were observed (Figure S8). As the
LiFSI concentration increased, the dark color became lighter.
This color change suggests that soluble Li2Sx (x = 6−8) was
transformed into insoluble short-chain Li2Sx.44,46,47 Therefore,
a higher electrolyte concentration suppresses the dissolution of
Li2Sx.
The morphology of the Li deposition in 3, 3.6, and 4 M
LiFSI/DME was observed using SEM (Figure S9). The
deposited lithium had a nodule-like structure with round
edges, which is consistent with previously reported results.22
As the electrolyte concentration increased, Li was densely and
uniformly deposited. Nevertheless, a higher concentration of
the electrolyte led to significant capacity decay (Figure 4b).
The capacity retentions of 3.6 and 4 M LiFSI/DME, calculated
by dividing the discharge capacity at the 50th cycle (82.3 and
161.6 mA h g−1) by the discharge capacity at the 2nd cycle
(1578.8 and 1475.4 mA h g−1), were 5.21 and 11.0%,
respectively. Furthermore, the average coulombic efficiencies
of 3.6 and 4 M LiFSI/DME were 90.8 and 92.6%, respectively.

Electrochemical Performance of Cmicro−meso−S Cath-
ode with LiFSI in DME/HFE Electrolyte. The above results
indicate that a suitable electrolyte for lithium−metal anodes
and Cmicro−meso−S cathodes requires a low viscosity and high
LiFSI concentration. Diluted electrolytes with HFE have
reduced viscosity and localized high concentrations of LiFSI
because LiFSI does not dissolve in HFE.24 Herein, 3 M LiFSI/
DME was diluted with HFE to improve the cycling
performance at high current densities and suppress Li2Sx
dissolution.
Figure 5a shows the charge−discharge curves of the Li−S
batteries with Cmicro−meso−S cathodes and 3 M LiFSI in DME/
HFE electrolytes at the 1st and 50th cycles. A high-potential
plateau, which is attributed to the Li2Sx (x = 4−8) formation
reaction, was not observed during the discharge of the Li−S
batteries with 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE. Dilution with HFE
increased the concentration of LiFSI in DME, which decreased
the amount of free DME. Therefore, the dissolution of Li2Sx
was suppressed in 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE. To investigate the
suppression effect of 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE on Li2Sx
dissolution, a solution prepared by mixing 3 M LiFSI in
DME/HFE and 0.2 M Li2S8/DME at a volume ratio of 3:1 was
observed (Figure S8), and the dark color of Li2S8 changed to
yellow because of the presence of Li2Sx (x = 1, 2).47 Thus,
dilution with HFE suppresses the dissolution of Li2Sx. Figure
5b shows the cycle plots of Li−S batteries with Cmicro−meso−S
cathodes and 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE electrolytes. The HFE
dilution suppressed the continuous capacity decay of 3 M
LiFSI/DME and improved the capacity retention rate,
calculated by dividing the discharge capacity at the 50th
cycle (732.3 and 830.4 mA h g−1) by the discharge capacity at
the 2nd cycle (1462.9 and 1347.7 mA h g−1), from 50.0 to
61.6%. Moreover, the average coulombic efficiency improved
from 97.1 to 98.5%. This improved cycling performance with
HFE dilution was due to the mitigation of the non-uniform
current distribution by decreasing the viscosity of the
electrolyte (Table 2). Furthermore, the decreased viscosity
provided 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE with superior performance
at high current density (Figure 5c).
The electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries under
practical conditions is discussed in the Supporting Information
(Figure S10).
Effect of HFE Dilution on Lithium−Metal Anode. As
noted above, HFE dilution suppressed the non-uniform
current distribution and Li2Sx dissolution from the cathode.
Herein, the effect of HFE dilution on lithium anodes was
investigated.

■CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we investigated porous carbon as a sulfur host
and the electrolyte composition suitable for Li−S batteries
with high-concentration LiFSI/DME. For improved energy
density and stable cycle performance, micro−mesoporous
carbon, which contains mesopores with high sulfur content and
micropores for the suppression of Li2Sx dissolution, was
adopted as the cathode. Moreover, to prepare a suitable
electrolyte for use with the composite cathode, a high
