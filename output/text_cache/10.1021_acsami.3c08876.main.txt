ABSTRACT: Among lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery materials,
sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has attracted substantial
attention as a cathode material owing to its potential to bypass
the problematic polysulfide formation and shuttling effect.
Carbonate-based electrolytes have been eschewed compared with
ether-based electrolytes because of their poor compatibility with Li
metal anodes. In this work, we design and study an electrolyte
comprising 0.8 M of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide,
0.2 M of lithium difluoro(oxalate)borate, and 0.05 M of lithium
hexafluorophosphate in ethyl methyl carbonate/fluoroethylene
carbonate = 3:1 v/v solution in the Li−S battery coupled with a
Li metal anode and SPAN cathode. The well-designed carbonate-
based electrolyte effectively stabilizes both electrodes, delivering
high Coulombic efficiencies with stable cyclability. Studies using operando optical microscopy and atomic force microscopy
demonstrate that dense, uniform Li deposition is promoted to suppress dendrite growth even at a high current density. Operando
Raman spectroscopy reveals a reversible Li+ storage behavior in the SPAN structure through the cleavage of disulfide bonds and their
redimerization during lithiation and delithiation. As a result, the proposed Li−S battery delivers an overall capacity retention of
73.5% over 1000 cycles, with high Coulombic efficiencies over 99.9%.
KEYWORDS: Li metal anode, electrolyte, SPAN, Li−S batteries, dendrite
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are versatile energy storage
devices that have been widely tested and employed for clean
energy over the last two decades. Compared to their early
humble beginnings in 1990 with LiCoO2/graphite, state-of-
the-art batteries employ Ni-rich layered oxide cathodes with
substantially increased electrochemical performance.1−5 How-
ever, LIBs are approaching their maximum energy density, and
alternative battery chemistries are being explored. Of these,
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been notable because of
the use of elemental sulfur for high capacity (1675 mAh g−1),
abundant supply, low cost, and environmental benignity,
surpassing the equivalent properties of LIBs.6−10
Despite their significant potential, research in Li−S batteries
has gradually decreased due to the fundamental difficulties
associated with Li−S batteries being unresolved, with the two
most notable being (i) polysulfide (Li2Sn, n = 2−8) evolution
chemistry and (ii) the electrically insulating nature of sulfur (5
× 10−30 S m−1)7,11,12 reducing active material utilization and
reversibility at high active material loadings.13−15 Encapsulat-
ing sulfur in a conductive agent or incorporating polar
mediators as absorbed agents to increase conductivity and
reduce polysulfide intermediates have prolonged the lifespan of
Li−S batteries;16−22 however, their complex and high-cost
material synthesis and ineffectiveness at high loadings reduce
the appeal of Li−S battery systems. Furthermore, the high
solubility of high-order polysulfide intermediates [Li2Sn (n ≥
4)] leads to their dissolution in the electrolyte without
conversion into insoluble Li2S2 or Li2S.23−25 These difficulties
can be circumvented when sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN)
material is employed as the cathode in Li−S batteries, as SPAN
consists of conjugated polypyridine rings containing CC and
CN double bonds, with sulfur atoms being covalently
bonded to the carbon backbone of the polypyridine rings.
Accordingly, SPAN triggers no polysulfide evolution, is
electrically conducting, and exhibits high reversibility to be
one of the most stable Li−S battery materials reported.25−31
The challenges of Li−S batteries with SPAN cathodes are
situated on their counter electrodes, though, as they employ

potential (up to 2600 Wh kg−1).32−34 Lithium metal anode
(LMA) suffers from nonuniform deposition of lithium,
uncontrollable volume changes, and surface corrosion that
results in both active Li and electrolyte consumption, followed
by a rapid decay in cyclability.35−38 Coupled with dendritic
growth, which can cause short circuiting, LMA also poses a
severe safety concern. These properties are accentuated by a
high areal capacity, current density, lean electrolyte, and
limited excess Li, which are prerequisites for a realistic Li−
SPAN battery system.39−41 Therefore, electrolyte designs using
ether- and carbonate-based electrolytes to fabricate a robust
solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer as a protective and
ionically conductive film has become the preferred approach
for addressing the challenges of LMA utilization.25,42 Of the
two electrolyte types, ether-based electrolytes, such as 1,3−
Dimethoxyethane/1,2−Dioxolane (DME/DOL), are the most
common electrolytes used in Li−S batteries, owing to their
high Coulombic efficiencies (CEs; approximately 98%),
smooth Li deposition, and stable SEI formation.31,43,44
However, ether electrolytes revert to the problematic
polysulfide chemistry and exhibit low vapor pres-
sures.9,30,31,36,45 Coupled with their near-indispensable use of
the LiNO3 salt, the use of ether electrolytes is risky for large-
scale operations. Carbonate-based electrolytes, in comparison,
are free from such problems and can utilize fluoroethylene
carbonate (FEC) to establish a LiF-rich SEI layer, which has
been a substantial focus, as LiF protects the Li metal by
forming a dense passivation layer.46−48 Therefore, the benefits
of carbonate electrolytes hold substantial value in stabilizing
the LMA of Li−SPAN systems.
In this study, we revisit a carbonate-based electrolyte
solution [0.8 M of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI), 0.2 M of lithium difluoro(oxalate)borate (LiDFOB),
and 0.05 M of lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) in ethyl
methyl carbonate (EMC)/FEC = 3:1 v/v] to establish an SEI
layer with multiple SEI-beneficial species on LMA to
outperform ether-based electrolytes in the Li−SPAN battery
system.49 Owing to its suitable compatibility with both SPAN
and metallic lithium, the 0.8 M of LiTFSI, 0.2 M of LiDFOB,
and 0.05 M of LiPF6 in EMC:FEC carbonate electrolyte
delivers a capacity of 1470.4 mAh g−1 and durable cycle life of
73.5% while maintaining higher than 99.9% CEs throughout its
1000 cycles at a 2 mg cm−2 sulfur loading. The electrolyte
encourages dense and uniform Li deposition to reduce Li loss
and dendrite growth and enables reversible Li+ storage in the
SPAN cathode.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of Electrolytes Solution. LiTFSI (99.99%),
LiPF6 (99.9%), LiDFOB, polyacrylonitrile (PAN; Mw 150,000), and
sulfur powder (S8; 99.998%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. DOL
(≥98%), DME (≥99.0%), ethylene carbonate (EC; ≥99.0%), EMC
(≥98%), diethyl ether (DEE; 99.5%), and bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl)
ether (BTFE; ≥99%) were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Inc.
Three solutions of electrolyte were prepared with the above into 1 M
of LiPF6 in EC/EMC = 3:7 v/v (denoted as LP57), 1 M of LiTFSI in
DOL/DME = 1:1 v/v, and 0.8 M of LiTFSI, 0.2 M of LiDFOB, and
0.05 M of LiPF6 in EMC/FEC = 3:1 v/v in an argon environment
glovebox with controlled oxygen and water levels.
2.2. Fabrication of the SPAN Cathode. The PAN and sulfur
powders were ball milled for 30 min at a mass ratio of 1:4. The
mixture was calcinated at 350 °C for 6 h in argon gas to obtain the
SPAN powder. Synthesized SPAN powder (80 wt %), super P (10 wt
%), and poly(acrylic acid) (dissolved in water; 10 wt %) were mixed
and slurried, followed by casting onto a carbon-coated Al foil. The
SPAN electrode was prepared by drying overnight under vacuum
conditions at 80 °C.
2.3. Electrochemical Measurements. The cell preparations
were conducted in an argon-filled glovebox (MBraun, H2O < 0.5
ppm, O2 < 0.5 ppm). The asymmetric cell of Li|Cu and symmetric cell
of Li|Li CR2032-type coin cells were conducted on Bio-Logic Science
Instruments. Li metal and Cu foil were cut into 14 mm diameter disks.
A polypropylene membrane (PP membrane; Celgard 2400) was used
as the separator, and the electrolyte volume was 50 μL. The
Coulombic efficiency was determined as the ratio of stripping to
plating of Li metal on Cu foil in an asymmetric cell. The galvanostatic
test of Li|Li and Li|Cu cells was conducted from −1 to 1 V (vs Li+|Li).

2.4. Material Characterizations. The particle morphologies and
elemental distributions of the SPAN powder were observed by using

determined by using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy
(PerkinElmer) and Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw). Thermogravi-
metric analysis (TGA) was performed using a thermal analysis
instrument (STA 449F5, Netzsch) for the characterization of SPAN
with N2 flow at a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1. The Li morphologies of
the prepared samples were analyzed via SEM, operando optical
microscopy (VHX-7000), and high-resolution atomic force micros-
copy (AFM) scanning probe microscopy (HR-AFM, Probes).
Noncontact mode (vibrating) images of the surface morphology
were obtained using Al-coated silicon AFM tips (PR-T190, Probes) at
a typical scanning rate of 0.5 Hz. The properties of the SEI layer were
determined by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with an
Ar+ beam (PHI5000, Versa Probe) and force−distance (FD) curves
(HR-AFM). The FD curves of the samples were obtained in contact
mode using a diamond-coated silicon cantilever (CDT-NCLR) with a
resonance frequency of 210 kHz and a force constant of 72 N m−1. In
situ synchrotron Raman spectroscopy was performed using an inVia
microscope spectrometer (Renishaw 2000, United Kingdom)
equipped with an EL-CELL (Optical Cell, ECC-Opto-Std). All
samples were packaged and analyzed to prevent exposure to moisture
or air.
2.5. Computational Method. Theoretical calculations of the
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels were performed using the
Gaussian 09 package. Geometry optimization of the molecules was
conducted using the closed-shell Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof general-
ized gradient approximation with the 6-311+G(d,p) basis set.50
3. RESULT AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Characterization of SPAN. The SEM image in Figure
1a shows the morphology of the synthesized SPAN. SPAN
synthesized under an Ar atmosphere was composed of
agglomerates of round particles with sizes of approximately
200 nm. SEM/EDX revealed that the carbon and nitrogen

be 47.92, 38.66, and 14.94%, respectively (Figure 1b−e). The
Raman spectra in Figure 1f showed that the synthesized SPAN

was 0.78, indicating a high degree of graphitization.
Furthermore, the FT-IR spectra of SPAN (Figure 1g) showed
peaks at 479 and 511, 668, and 940 cm−1, corresponding to S−
S stretching, C−S stretching, and C−S ring breathing,
respectively, to verify the structure of the C−S and S−S
bonds. The peaks at 1237 and 1498 cm−1, corresponding to
the symmetrical stretching of the CN and CC bonds,

3.2. Ether vs Carbonate Electrolyte for the Li metal
and SPAN. Figure 2a,b presents the charge−discharge curves
of Li/SPAN cells in 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME = 1:1 v/v
(denoted as DOL/DME) and 1 M LiPF6 in EC/EMC = 3:7 v/
v (denoted as LP57), which are representative of standard
ether- and carbonate-based electrolytes. The Li/SPAN cells
delivered 1933.6 and 2135.9 mAh g−1 specific discharge
capacities in ether and carbonate electrolytes (Figure S1),
respectively; the former shows a gradual capacity fade over 50
cycles, while the latter cycles stably but suffers from abrupt
capacity fade after the 30th cycle at a 2.0 mg cm−2 S loading
and 0.5C cycling. This difference in capacity fade behavior is
attributed to the origin of deterioration: the SPAN cathode or
metallic lithium anode. The SEM images of the deposited
lithium in the Li/Cu cells with the two electrolyte types are
shown (Figure 2c−f), and the lithium morphology has a higher
packing density favorable to deposition/stripping. Its counter-
parts in the carbonate electrolyte appeared as loosely packed
wires. The electrochemical performance of symmetrical Li/Li
cells cycled galvanostatically at a current density of 1.5 mA
cm−2 and capacity loading of 3 mAh cm−2 (Figure 2g) shows
that the DOL/DME cell cycles stably for more than 400 h,
albeit experiencing a gradual increase in overpotential. In
contrast, the LP57 Li/Li cell showed progressively increasing
overpotential curves that failed prematurely with a rapid
voltage polarization at 135 h, indicating that the sudden failure
of the Li/SPAN cell with the carbonate electrolyte was derived
from the unstable Li metal anode. However, the stability of the
ether-based electrolyte with the Li metal anode was over-
shadowed by the regression of the poor performance of SPAN
cathodes with an ether electrolyte to the solubility of Li2Sn (n
≥4) to exceed the redox conversion, leading to dissolution
and polysulfide shuttling.18,19,53 This was reflected by the
distinctive plateaus at ∼2.1 V during discharge and ∼2.4 V in
the charge−discharge curves of the Li/SPAN cell with 3.0 mg
cm−2 S loading in the sulfur chemistry, which was attributed to
polysulfide formation and shuttling (Figure S2 and Table S1).
Therefore, ether exhibited inherently poor compatibility with
the SPAN cathode, as opposed to the carbonate-based
electrolyte, which possesses the potential for improvement
through electrolyte modification for Li metal anode stabiliza-
tion.
3.3. Advanced Carbonate-Based Electrolyte. Based on
the physical properties of the HOMO/LUMO energy levels,54
the competitive oxidation and reduction behaviors of salt and
solvent molecules in electrolytes could be evaluated. The
HOMO/LUMO energy level gap represents the stability
window of the battery electrolyte,55 where low LUMO and
high HOMO energy levels, respectively, indicate propensities
for reduction and oxidation. The HOMO/LUMO energy
levels of widely used salts and solvents in rechargeable lithium
batteries, calculated via density functional theory (DFT), are
shown in Figure 3. The calculated HOMO/LUMO energy
level gaps showed that carbonate electrolytes are oxidatively
stable and highly polar, indicating high solvation of the salt and
better ionic conductivity.56 However, as carbonate electrolytes
are unstable on metal anodes, protecting the metal anode
surface with a stable SEI layer is necessary. Therefore,
LiDFOB, with a very low LUMO energy level, was used as
an additive to generate boron compounds to help form a stable
SEI layer on the anode. In addition, when FEC was used as a
cosolvent, the HOMO/LUMO energy levels were lowered due
to the electron-withdrawing ability of the highly electro-
negative fluorine. In addition, when FEC was used as a
cosolvent, the HOMO/LUMO energy levels were lowered
owing to the electron-withdrawing ability of the highly
electronegative fluorine. This increased the oxidation potential
of the electrolyte and enhanced the SEI layer stiffness on the
lithium metal anode by increasing the formation of fluorine
compounds, such as LiF, from its high reduction potential.
Adding a small amount of LiPF6 to the LiTFSI electrolyte also
prevents Al corrosion by increasing the oxidation stability of
the electrolyte owing to the characteristics of LiPF6 with a low
HOMO energy level.57

Figure 4 presents the electrochemical properties of LP57
and 0.8 M LiTFSI, 0.2 M LiDFOB, and 0.05 M LiPF6 in
EMC/FEC = 3:1 v/v (denoted as 82EF-31) with a metallic
lithium anode. The nucleation overpotential values of LP57
and 82EF-31 evaluated in Li/Cu cells were −0.280 and −0.204
V, respectively, indicating that the SEI layer formed on the
latter electrolyte provides the least resistance to Li deposition
to facilitate smooth Li deposition (Figure 4a). Similarly, a
lower voltage polarization (0.06 V) was experienced after 10
cycles for the 82EF-31 electrolyte cell compared with the 0.14
V polarization voltage of the LP57 electrolyte cell, as shown by
the first to 10th voltage profiles in Figure 4b,c. The CEs of
LP57 and 82EF-31 were evaluated in the Li/Cu cells at a cutoff
voltage of 1.0 V for 100 h during stripping. The LP57 cells
suffered from a poor starting CE of 67.7%, which decreased to
55.4% by the 14th cycle, whereas the 82EF-31 cells showed an
initial CE of 92.9%, which recovered to 96.1% after 70 cycles
(Figure 4d). When cycled in symmetrical Li/Li cells, LP57
showed an accelerated overpotential increase to result in
premature cell failure after 150 h at a current density of 1.5 mA
cm−2 and a capacity loading of 3 mAh cm−2. In contrast, the
Li/Li cell evaluated using 82EF-31 demonstrated stable Li
deposition/stripping for more than 450 h, mirroring the
enhanced compatibility of 82EF-31 with Li metal anodes.
As demonstrated by the reduced polarization, lithium
deposited on the Cu foil in 82EF-31 displayed a substantially
improved deposition morphology at a rapid charge rate of 1.5
mA cm−2 for 2 h (Figure 5a). The deposited lithium consists of
approximately 1.2 μm densely packed microwires, in contrast
to the nearly transparent and porous particle deposition
structures previously observed in the LP57 electrolyte, as
shown in Figure 2a. The disparities in the deposition
morphology became more evident on a macroscopic scale
when lithium was deposited freely at a substantially high
current density of 20 mA cm−2 for 15 min in a Li/Li optical
cell, as observed in Figure 5b. The operando optical (o-OM)
images revealed that the seeds of the dendritic structures were
observed as early as 30 s and bloomed into loose,
inhomogeneous dendrites of widely differing heights in the
LP57 electrolyte. The dendritic structure ultimately grew up to
1.5 μm to nearly contact the counter electrode. In 82EF-31
though, lithium was deposited as compact structures that
formed a more uniform surface as a homogeneous Li+ flux is
maintained. Despite 10 min of plating at a substantially high
current density of 20 mA cm−2, dendritic structure growth was
notably suppressed, and freely deposited lithium reached only
0.65 μm. Similarly, the 3D and 2D AFM topographic images
(Figures 5c and S3) and corresponding height distribution
profiles (Figure S4) further highlighted that the deposited Li
on the Cu foil in 82EF-31 was a dense and more uniform
surface than LP57, which was more concentrated in certain
regions.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, by modulating the electrolyte solution, we
introduced a rational strategy to improve the energy efficiency
and lifetime of Li−S batteries using SPAN cathodes and Li
metal anodes. The carbonate-based 82EF−31 electrolyte was
designed based on a DFT−based survey and was compatible
with the SPAN cathode. More importantly, 82EF−31
promoted dense and uniform Li deposition to suppress
dendrite growth, as demonstrated by o-OM and AFM, even
at a high current density of 1.5 mA cm−2. Its ability to stabilize
the Li metal surface can be attributed to the increased
concentration of inorganic compounds, notably LiF, which
provides physicochemical stability to the SEI layer. Con-
sequently, the stabilized Li metal anode enabled the Li/Li cell
to deliver stable deposition/stripping cycling for more than
450 h, and the Li−S cell with the SPAN cathode delivers an
overall capacity retention of 73.5% over 1000 cycles with
Coulombic efficiencies greater than 99.9%. Furthermore, the
cycling stability of Li/SPAN, when compared with a modern
ether-based electrolyte (1.8 M of LiFSI dissolved in DEE-
BTFE)62 or the same salt composition (0.8 M of LiTFSI, 0.2
M of LiDFOB, and 0.05 M of LiPF6) dissolved in DME and
DOL (Figure S7) showed enhanced cycling life and CE
stability under the same testing protocol (capacity loading of 3
mAh cm−2 and current density of 1.5 mA cm−2). Through this
work, we highlight the importance of optimal carbonate
electrolytes and their potential for Li stabilization as SPAN
cathodes for practical Li−S batteries.
