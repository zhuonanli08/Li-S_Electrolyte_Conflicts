Abstract
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) have gained remarkable attention over the past
several years, however, their inherent limitations, such as the poor interfacial
stability of their Li anodes and the severe dissolution of polysulfide, are
regarded as bottlenecks that prevent these batteries from entering commercial
markets. In this study, a 1,1-diethoxyethane (DEE), which has been functiona-
lized with acetal groups, is proposed as an electrolyte cosolvent to comple-
ment the limitations of the electrode materials of LSBs. In Li/Li symmetric cells,
the DEE cosolvent exhibited stable cycling behavior, whereas the cell with the
baseline electrolyte exhibited rapidly increasing polarization, even after 650 h.
In LSB cells, the electrolyte that contains 50% DEE exhibits the highest cycling
retention (65.0%) compared to the baseline electrolyte (49.8%) because it effec-
tively protects the interface of the Li anode, but also suppresses the dissolution
of polysulfide species.

INTRODUCTION
The rapid growth in the worldwide market for electricity
generated from renewable energy sources has been
accompanied by an exponential increase in the demand for
rechargeable batteries as the primary energy source for
electric vehicles. Unfortunately, the energy density of cur-
rent state-of-the-art lithium-ion batteries of approximately
250 W h kg1 is insufficient to satisfy the specifications of
large-scale devices.1–5 This has prompted numerous studies
over the past several years to increase the energy density
of conventional rechargeable batteries.6–12 In this respect,
lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) have gained considerable
attention as advanced-generation batteries because they
can realize energy density that is almost 10 times higher
than that of conventional rechargeable batteries, owing to
the high specific capacity of the lithium anodes and sulfur
cathode materials of which LSBs are composed.13–15
However, LSBs have critical limitations in terms of realiz-
ing stable cycling performance for prolonged periods of
time. Although sulfur cathode materials can theoretically
provide a specific capacity that is almost 10 times higher
(1675 mA h g1) than that of the conventional cathodes
used in lithium-ion batteries, a much lower specific capacity
is observed in practical cells. This is because of the low elec-
trical conductivity of sulfur (5  1030 S cm1), which hin-
ders the intensive utilization of sulfur in electrochemical
processes.16,17 In addition, the polysulfide (PS) intermediate
formed during the electrochemical reaction in a cell is con-
sidered a critical bottleneck that adversely impacts the
cycling retention of LSBs.18–20 In this regard, it should be
noted that because these PS species are highly soluble in
the electrolyte, they significantly degrade the cycling reten-
tion of LSBs. The degradation is caused by a shuttle reaction
resulting from freely migrating PS species between the
cathode and anode, which ultimately leads to the loss of
active cathode materials upon cycling.21,22 It should be
emphasized that the low electrical conductivity of active S
cathode materials and the shuttle reaction of PS are
regarded as the most challenging obstacles that need to be
overcome to improve the electrochemical behavior of LSBs.
Attempts to resolve these issues most frequently
involve the use of sulfur/carbon composites consisting of
a
mesoporous
carbon
host
infiltrated
with
sulfur.

Mesoporous carbon materials, which afford high electrical
conductivity, offer excellent opportunities to compensate
for the low electrical conductivity of active S cathode
materials.23,24 Another advantage of these carbon mate-
rials is that they effectively prevent PS dissolution by trap-
ping the PS in their mesopores.25 An alternative approach
involved the development of useful inorganic absorbents
that desirably adsorb PS species via physical interactions.
These absorbents were used as cathode additives to con-
fine the shuttle reaction of PS.26 The effectiveness of func-
tional
separators
that
contain
embedded
anionic
repulsive coating materials to control the crossover reac-
tion of PS species in LSBs has also been reported.27,28
Based on these approaches, the actual energy density of
LSBs has significantly improved over the years.29,30
In contrast to research on cathodes and separators,
the electrolyte itself has rarely been studied. This is
believed to be owing to the intrinsic characteristics of PS;
the terminal site of the PS species is composed of highly
nucleophilic sulfur anions that are known to promptly
undergo chemical reactions with electrophilic functional
groups.31 These functional groups, such as C═O functional
groups, are present in typical conventional electrolytes
(i.e., carbonate-based electrolytes), which means that
these conventional carbonate-based electrolytes cannot
be used for LSBs. This is because the PS can easily be
consumed in chemical reactions with these conventional
electrophilic electrolytes, thereby leading to drastic capa-
city fading.32 In this regard, electrolyte candidates suitable
for LSBs are extremely limited: only a few types of non-
electrophilic solvents, such as ether-based compounds,
which have the required chemical stability toward PS
species, can be used as the main electrolyte components
for LSBs. Ether-based solvents for LSBs can be classified
as either acyclic or cyclic. Acyclic ether solvents, such as
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), ethylene glycol dimethyl
ether (EGDME), triethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TGDME),
and tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME), and
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether, play key roles in the
complete dissolution of lithium salts and PS species, which
corresponds to an increase in the utilization of sulfur.33–35
However, only a limited number of cyclic ether solvents,
such as dioxolane (DOL), have been used for LSBs.36,37
DOL has a five-membered ring structure functionalized by
an acetal chemical moiety, which can improve the interfa-
cial stability of the Li anode by incorporating solid-
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers via electrochemical
reactions.38–40 Although other compounds that mimic
cyclic ethers, such as THF, have also been reported, their
effect on the stabilization of the Li anode is not reliable
compared to DOL; therefore, DOL has been solely used as
the key component of the electrolyte of LSBs combined
with an acyclic ether solvent to protect the Li anode.
Our study led to the proposal of a new solvent com-
ponent, 1,1-diethoxyethane (DEE), as a replacement for
the DOL component (Figure 1). Note that DOL, the struc-
ture of which similarly includes an acetal functional
group, is a typical solvent component for LSBs because of
its preferable behavior in terms of stabilizing the Li anode.
The chemical structure of DEE also includes an acetal
functional group, implying that DEE (with its similar func-
tional groups) is also anticipated to afford adequate SEI
layers that can inhibit undesired reactions at the Li anode.
Moreover, DEE is bulkier than DOL (because DEE has a lin-
ear structure), and the use of DEE is anticipated to effec-
tively regulate PS dissolution because the increased
viscosity of DEE-containing electrolytes can inhibit the
unrestricted migration of PS species in the cell.41 In addi-
tion, the structure of DEE is similar to that of the
additional main solvent components, such as DME, indi-
cating that DEE influences the miscibility between the
two main solvents. Based on these considerations, we
prepared electrolytes by varying the DEE-to-DME ratio
and elucidated the role of DEE in LSBs by conducting
postmodern analyses by considering the correlations
between the DEE ratio and electrochemical performance.
EXPERIMENTS
For the main solvent component, DME (Aldrich) was
selected, and 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)
imide (LiTFSI, Dongwha Electrolyte) and 0.4 M lithium

and DEE-based electrolytes were prepared by adding
appropriate amounts of DOL (Alfa Aesar) and DEE
(Aldrich) based on volume ratios of 2:8, 5:5, and 8:2 (abbre-
viated as DOL20, DEE20, DEE50, and DEE80, respectively).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Ionic conductivity measurements of the electrolytes
(Figure 2a) revealed that the baseline electrolyte (DOL20)
had an ionic conductivity of 10.3 mS cm1. The replace-
ment of DOL with DEE gave a slight decrease in the ionic
conductivity to 7.8 mS cm1 (DEE20). Moreover, a gradual
increase in the DEE content resulted in a concomitant
decrease in the ionic conductivity to 2.4 mS cm1 in
DEE80. This might be attributable to the bulkier (linear)
structure of DEE compared with that of cyclic DOL; the
incorporation of DEE could, therefore, increase the viscos-
ity of the electrolyte, which lowered the ionic conductivity
(low utilization of the S active material in the LSB).42–44
However, the appropriate amount of DEE (which is
thought to be less than 50%) does not significantly
decrease the ionic conductivity. The addition of DEE
increased the viscosity of the electrolyte; although the vis-
cosity of DOL20 was 1.5 cP, that of DEE20 was 1.6 cP.
When the amount of DEE was increased from 50% to
80%, the viscosity of the electrolyte changed from 1.7
to 1.9 cP, which follows the reverse trend compared with
the ionic conductivity. It has been reported that the vis-
cosity of the electrolyte is one of the factors that affect
the cycling performance of LSBs.45–47 When the electro-
lyte becomes more viscous, it disturbs the diffusion of PS
from the cathode to the anode, thereby preventing unde-
sired
chemical
reactions
between
the
PS
and
Li
anode.48,49 This is because increasing the viscosity of the
electrolyte can inhibit unrestricted retention in the cell.50
The electrochemical behavior of the DEE was eluci-
dated using LSV and EVS (Figure 2b–d). The LSV results

The performance of the DEE-derived SEI layers was
assessed by investigating the cycling behavior of Li/Li

electrolyte (containing DOL) after 650 h. In contrast, the
cell with the DEE-containing electrolyte exhibited stable
cycling even after 650 h, indicating that DEE offers
improved interfacial stability for the Li anode.

CONCLUSION
DEE, a surface-stabilizing cosolvent for Li metal anodes,
was proposed and its electrochemical performance was
evaluated. Although the overall ionic conductivity of
the electrolytes gradually decreased as the amount of
DEE increased, an amount of less than 50% DEE seemed
to be compatible with the LSBs, as a certain level of
ionic conductivity of approximately 5.2 mS cm1 could
still be attained. The electrochemical reduction of DEE-
incorporated organic/inorganic-based SEI layers at the
Li anode was the result of the reduction of the acetal
functional groups in the DEE structure. The Li/Li sym-
metric cell that contained DEE exhibited stable cycling
behavior, whereas the cell with the baseline electrolyte
experienced sudden fading after 500 h. The LSB cell
with DEE as the cosolvent delivered improved cycling
retention (65.0%) compared to that cycled with the
baseline electrolyte (49.8%). Furthermore, DEE50, which
was composed of 50% DEE and 50% DME, exhibited the
highest cycling retention compared to the cells cycled
by the other solvent compositions: DEE20 (55.6%) and
DEE80 (63.2%). Important to note that DEE50 increased
the viscosity of the electrolyte compared with that
composed of less DEE; therefore, it suppressed not only
the dissolution of PS species from the S/C cathode to
the Li anode, but also effectively protected the interfa-
cial stability of the Li anode. On the basis of these
results, the use of DEE as a cosolvent for LSBs was con-
cluded to be a reliable way to improve the electro-
chemical performance.
