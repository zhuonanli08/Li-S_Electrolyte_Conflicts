A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries stand promising for next-generation energy storage systems due to their high 
specific capacity and cost-effectiveness. However, their commercialization is hindered by sluggish sulfur 
reduction reaction (SRR) kinetics and polysulfide migration. To address these challenges, we introduce bis(4- 
nitrophenyl) carbonate (BNC) as a bifunctional electrolyte additive. At an optimal concentration, BNC lever­
ages its polar nature to anchor soluble polysulfides while simultaneously modifying the Li+ solvation structure at 
the molecular level, enhancing SRR kinetics. This dual functionality is confirmed through molecular dynamics 
simulations and electrochemical analyses. In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further shows 
that optimal BNC concentration reduces activation energy for polysulfides formation by 40.6%. Operando 
spectroscopic techniques, including Raman and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), demonstrate BNC’s dual 
effect, with a focus on the middle-chain polysulfides conversion, supported by detailed polysulfide quantifica­
tion. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) mapping reveals decreased sulfur deposition on lithium, indicating the effec­
tiveness of shuttle suppression. These effects contribute to outstanding cycling performance under practical 
conditions, achieving 650.93 mAh gsulfur
-1 
and coulombic efficiency of 93% over 200 cycles at a C-rate of C/2. This 
work not only offers valuable insights into the use of unconventional carbonate-based additives but also provides 
a blueprint for advancing Li-S battery designs through targeted solvation structure modifications.
1. Introduction
Renewable energy strategies such as hydrogen production, fuel cells, 
and rechargeable batteries are crucial for achieving a sustainable, low- 
carbon future [1–5]. Among these, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have 
emerged as particularly promising candidates for advancing energy 
storage technology beyond the current state-of-the-art lithium-ion bat­
teries (LIBs) [6]. With a theoretical energy density nearly five times 
greater than that of LIBs, largely due to sulfur’s exceptional capacity of 
1672 mAh gsulfur
-1
, Li-S batteries far surpass conventional cathode mate­
rials like cobalt, nickel, and manganese oxides [7]. In addition to their 
high energy density, sulfur’s environmental friendliness, non-toxic na­
ture, and widespread availability make Li-S batteries a cost-effective and 
sustainable option for applications such as electric vehicles and 
grid-scale energy storage [8,9].
However, despite these compelling advantages, significant chal­
lenges remain that hinder the commercial viability of Li-S batteries. Key 
issues include sulfur’s low electrical conductivity, parasitic reactions on 
the anode, and substantial volume changes during cycling [10–13]. 
Among these, the sluggish kinetics of lithium polysulfide conversion 
during the sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) and the notorious "shuttling 
effect", where dissolved polysulfides migrate to the anode and cause 
capacity loss, are particularly problematic [14–16]. Furthermore, the 
formation of solid sulfur byproducts exacerbates electrode passivation, 
while the volatility of lithium polysulfides raises safety concerns, 
collectively impairing battery performance and limiting both cycle life

and capacity retention [17]. These challenges necessitate innovative 
strategies to unlock the full potential of Li-S technology and pave the 
way for its practical implementation in next-generation energy storage 
systems [15,18].
To overcome the critical challenges faced by Li-S batteries, a wide 
range of strategies have been explored, including electrolyte/cathode 
modifications, single-atom catalysts, artificial solid-electrolyte in­
terfaces (SEI), and functionalized two-dimensional materials [19–25]. 
Among these approaches, carbonate-based solvents and additives have 
been largely overlooked due to their inherent polar nature, which ren­
ders them vulnerable to nucleophilic attack by sulfur atoms in poly­
sulfides [26,27]. Nevertheless, a few studies have demonstrated that 
carbonate-based electrolytes can enhance anode stability and improve 
overall safety in Li-S batteries, highlighting their potential benefits [28,
29]. Despite recent advances, the precise role of carbonate additives in 
Li-S batteries remains elusive, and the development of a bifunctional 
carbonate additive capable of concurrently enhancing polysulfide con­
version kinetics and immobilizing soluble polysulfides remains under­
explored. This study addresses this critical gap by providing a 
comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving such dual 
functionality, which is essential for fully harnessing the potential of 
carbonate-based systems in practical Li-S battery applications.
In this study, we introduce bis(4-nitrophenyl) carbonate (BNC) as an 
electrolyte additive aimed to modify the Li+ solvation structure with 
intermediate polysulfides (Li2Sx, 1<x<8) for enhanced SRR kinetics as a 
solvation-property modifier, and also to anchor the soluble polysulfides 
(Li2Sx, 6<x<8) from migration as a polysulfide mediator. BNC was first 
introduced by Yang and Goodenough in a Li-S battery study, where they 
demonstrated that BNC immobilize the soluble polysulfides [30]. 
However, our findings reveal that BNC is capable of altering Li+ coor­
dination environment and its solvation structures to enhance the SRR 
kinetics at optimized concentration. Moreover, its functionality is highly 
concentration-dependent, as excessive BNC levels result in aggressive 
and irreversible nucleophilic reactions, leading to capacity degradation. 
This work employs a combination of simulations and experiments, using 
ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and nuclear magnetic resonance 
(NMR) to validate the alterations in Li+
solvation structure. 
Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) 
spectroscopy confirm polysulfide preservation at optimal BNC concen­
trations, while extensive electrochemical experiments demonstrate im­
provements in SRR kinetics. Sophisticated operando spectroscopic 
techniques, including Raman and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), 
provide direct insights into polysulfide formation and transformation 
under the dual effect of BNC. The synergic effect of BNC culminates in an 
impressive capacity retention of 83.97%, delivering 650.93 mAh gsulfur
-1 
at C-rate of C/2 (0.08% capacity decay per cycle) with a columbic ef­
ficiency of 92.96% over prolonged cycles. This performance is achieved 
under practical conditions with a sulfur loading of 4 mg cm-2 and an 
electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 8 μL mgsulfur
-1
. This work bridges a 
critical knowledge gap by providing a fundamental understanding of the 
mechanisms underlying the carbonate electrolyte additives, paving the 
way for advancements in Li-S battery technology.

the compatibility of 0.01 M BNC in Li-S batteries, balancing moderated 
reactivity with effective polysulfide preservation.
UV–vis spectra were collected at intervals of one minute during the 
first 45 minutes and subsequently at 45-minute intervals for up to 48 
hours after the addition of BNC (Fig. 1B, S3A-D). BNC exhibits a char­
acteristic peak at 307 nm, while its reaction product shows a peak at 397 
nm (Figure S3C). By plotting the intensity of these peaks over time, the 
dissolution of BNC and the progression of the nucleophilic reaction were 
monitored (Fig. 1B). At 0.01 M, BNC dissolves rapidly during the first 45 
mins and the nucleophilic reaction intensifies gradually as these two 
peaks increase. After 45 mins, both BNC and its reaction products reach 
a plateau, indicating that the system achieves equilibrium, allowing the 
co-existence of BNC and Li2S6. In contrast, the UV–vis spectra for 0.11 M 
BNC exhibit discontinuities and sharp noise due to the rapid nucleo­
philic reaction, which results in immediate precipitation that saturates 
the instrument’s detection limit (Figure S3B). The combined FTIR and 
time-dependent UV–vis results confirm that optimizing BNC concen­
tration can effectively moderate the nucleophilic reaction while pre­
serving both BNC and Li2S6. The inset in Fig. 1B highlights the stark 
contrast in visual appearance: the 0.11 M BNC sample shows significant 
color change and precipitation at the bottom of the vial, while the 0.01 
M BNC sample shows minimal change. Additional photographs illus­
trating BNC interactions with various polysulfides are provided in 
Figure S1.
To validate the reaction pathway, FTIR and X-ray absorption near 
edge structure (XANES) analyses were performed on precipitates formed 
with various polysulfides (Figures S2C, S4A-F). XANES results reveal 
that precipitates from polysulfide retain an intact S-S cyclic structure, 
with a consistent peak at 2473.3 eV, while Li2S precipitates primarily 
exhibit Li2S with a peak at 2474.2 eV, confirming minimal BNC reac­
tivity with insoluble polysulfides (Figure S4F), the detailed peak iden­
tification and analysis can be found in SI (see Ex Situ XAS Sample 
Analysis). FTIR spectra further indicate that precipitates from Li2S8 and 
Li2S6 share identical functional groups (Figure S2C), aligning with re­
action pathways outlined in Schemes I and II in the SI and supported by 
prior studies [30,32,33].
To understand the impact of BNC at lower concentration on Li+
solvation structure, co-axial 7Li NMR experiments were conducted as 
shown in Figures S5A-C. The experiments were conducted in triplicate to 
eliminate potential errors and artifacts (Figures S6A-B). Fig. 1C shows 
the initial investigations focused on electrolytes with varying concen­
trations of BNC in the absence of Li2S6. Notably, the 0.01 M BNC elec­
trolyte displays a downfield shift with a chemical shift of 0.035 ppm, 
indicative of de-shielded Li-ion (Fig. 1C). The observed de-shielding 
phenomenon suggests enhanced interactions between Li-ions and an­
ions within the inner solvation shell due to weakened interaction be­
tween Li-ions and DME [34]. This downfield shift can be attributed to 
charge redistribution within the Li+-DME solvated cluster, induced by 
the lone electron pairs of the oxygen atoms in BNC [30,35]. This 
structural modification weakens the Li+-DME interaction, lowering the 
desolvation energy barrier for Li+ and thereby facilitating its reaction 
with lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), ultimately enhancing the sulfur 
reduction reaction SRR [31]. However, at higher BNC concentrations 
(0.11 M), the 7Li peak shifts towards lower ppm values, exhibiting a 
lesser downfield shift observed at 0.01 M. This observation is attributed 
to hydrogen bonding interactions between BNC molecules, as indicated 
by DFT calculations ranging from -0.11 to -0.17 eV (Figure S7, Table S1), 
which cause excessive BNC at 0.11 M to aggregate and form clusters 
within the Li+-DME solvated structure. These aggregates primarily 
reduce the effectiveness of BNC in weakening Li+-DME solvation 
structure and disrupts the association between Li+ and anions compared 
to the optimal 0.01 M concentration. Importantly, this result was 
observed in the absence of any LiPSs, demonstrating that even without 
the aggressive nucleophilic reaction between BNC and LiPSs, higher 
concentrations of BNC are less favorable. This underscores the critical 
importance of optimizing BNC concentration to achieve a balance be­
tween its solvation and reactivity roles in the electrolyte.
Further 7Li NMR experiments were conducted on the electrolyte 
containing Li2S6. As shown in Fig. 1D, the chemical shift at 0.01 M BNC 
remains almost unchanged compared to the case without Li2S6 (0.022 
ppm vs. 0.035 ppm), reflecting strengthened interactions between Li- 
ions and anions, including S6
2-. This heightened affinity of Li-ions holds 
promise for the SRR, where multiple Sx
2- anions play a critical role. In 
contrast, the 0.11 M BNC electrolyte exhibited a more pronounced 
downfield shift in the presence of Li2S6. This downfield shift clearly 
reflects significant changes in the LiPSs coordination environment in the 
presence of BNC. The aggressive nucleophilic reactions between BNC 
and Li2S6, as confirmed by FTIR and UV–vis (Figs. 1A-B), lead to the 
depletion of both BNC and polysulfides. The reaction products disrupt 
the original Li+ solvation structure, increasing the deshielding effect of 
Li+ in the inner solvation shell. Although this alteration seems advan­
tageous for enhancing SRR kinetics, the extensive sulfur depletion 
counteracts these benefits, ultimately causing severe capacity decay. 
Therefore, the contrasting trends observed in the 7Li NMR experiments 
with and without Li2S6 underscore the critical importance of optimizing 
BNC concentration to balance its solvation and reactivity roles, avoiding 
detrimental effects while maximizing performance benefits.
To understand the underlying mechanism of BNC on the solvation 
structure alteration, AIMD simulations was employed focusing on the 
solvation structure of the Li2S6 (in DOL/DME) as shown in Figs. 1E-F, S8 
[36]. In the absence of BNC, both DME and DOL are distributed within 
the inner solvation shell, defined as the region within 6 Å of the sulfur 
cluster’s center of mass (Figure S8), with DME showing a slight stronger 
affinity for Li2S6. This preference arises from the dipole-dipole interac­
tion strength between the solvents and LiPSs, as reflected by the donicity 
(Gutmann donor number, DN) of the solvent, where DME (24 kcal/mol) 
slightly exceeds DOL (21.2 kcal/mol) [35,37].
The introduction of BNC, however, induces a significant redistribu­
tion of the solvation structure. DME is completely expelled from the 
inner shell to the outer shell, and most of the DOL molecules are simi­
larly displaced (Figs. 1E-F). This shift is driven by the incorporation of 
BNC into the Li+ solvation structure, which alters the local charge dis­
tribution within the Li2S6-DOL/DME solvated cluster. BNC’s strong 
interaction with Li+ disrupts the original coordination environment, 
weakening the binding of DOL and DME to Li2S6. As a result, Li2S6 be­
comes more easily desolvated from the cluster, facilitating its partici­
pation in the SRR.
This shift is further corroborated by radial distribution function 
(RDF) analysis, which shows a sharp peak for DME at approximately 5.8 
Å being markedly suppressed in the presence of BNC (Figure S9). The 
bond length between oxygen atoms in DME and lithium atoms in Li2S6 
averages 1.92 Å in our AIMD simulations without BNC. However, no 
similar chemical bond forms when BNC is introduced, aligning with the 
observed solvent molecular distributions and RDF changes. This alter­
ation in the solvation structure of Li2S6 is attributed to a weakening of

2.2. Electrochemical characterization
To elucidate the electrochemical activity associated with BNC ad­
ditives, we conducted a series of cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments 
between baseline and 0.01 M BNC electrolytes. Figs. 2A and S12 depict 
the characteristics CV curves of Li-S batteries. The CV curves reveal two 
cathodic peaks and two anodic peaks, corresponding to the conversions 
between long-chain to middle-chain polysulfides and middle-chain to 
short-chain polysulfides, respectively. The similar trends observed in the 
CV curves of both the baseline and 0.01 M BNC electrolytes (Figure S12, 
Fig. 2A) under various scan rates suggest that the nucleophilic reaction 
at the optimal BNC concentration does not significantly affect the SRR. 
To gain deeper insight into the changes in SRR kinetics induced by BNC, 
we employed Randles-Sevick analysis to determine the Li-ion diffusion 
(DLi) in the baseline and 0.01 M BNC electrolytes. The absolute value of 
the slope for peak A2, corresponding to the middle-chain to short-chain 
polysulfide conversion, increased from 5.4 to 7.9 (Fig. 2B), indicating a 
46.3% improvement in the Li-ion diffusion compared to the baseline. 
This result reflects improved SRR kinetics for middle-chain polysulfides 
under the BNC additive. Randles-Sevick’s analysis of other peaks (B1, B2)

Potentiostatic discharge experiments were employed to investigate 
the nucleation and growth of Li2S, the final product of SRR. This process 
involves a liquid-solid phase transition and represents the stage with the 
highest energy barrier in the SRR, providing valuable insights into the 
kinetic impact of BNC [41,42]. As shown in Fig. 2E and 2F, the 0.01 M 
BNC electrolyte possesses a higher Li2S nucleation response current and 
earlier nucleation time compared to the baseline. Moreover, the Li2S 
nucleation capacity in the 0.01 M BNC electrolyte exceeded that of the 
baseline by approximately 30 mAh gsulfur
-1
. These observations indicate 
that BNC facilitates the nucleation and growth of Li2S, likely resulting 
from the enhanced conversion of middle-chain polysulfides. This 
enhancement underscores the significant role of BNC in improving SRR 
kinetics, particularly in overcoming the energy barrier associated with 
the liquid-solid phase transition.
Consequently, a more sophisticated in situ EIS investigation was 
executed to scrutinize the activation energy (Ea) of SRR at various DoD, 
unveiling nuanced kinetics information for each polysulfide conversion 
step. Coin cells were prepared and discharged at a C-rate of C/20 across 
temperatures ranging from 273 to 333 K at 15 K intervals. EIS mea­
surements were acquired repeatedly after discharging the cell for one 
hour until the cell reached the discharge cutoff voltage (Figures S15A- 
B). The EIS data and corresponding fitting results are depicted in 
Figures S16-S17. In this analysis, the reciprocal of charge transfer 
resistance (1/Rct) is utilized as a proxy for the SRR rate, as polysulfide 
conversion is inherently governed by charge transfer involving Li-ions 
and anions [43,44]. The Ea is subsequently determined using the 
Arrhenius equation. This approach allows for a focused investigation 
into the effect of BNC on the electrochemical reaction kinetics, inde­
pendent of electrode/electrolyte interfacial transport phenomena that 
concurrently occur during the SRR process.
Fig. 2G presents the detailed EIS data and corresponding fitting re­
sults obtained at 303 K for the two electrolytes at DoD ranging from 
59.5% to 66.1%, a region where the most sluggish kinetics was expected 
to occur. The EIS results unveil that the 0.01 M BNC electrolyte exhibits 
the lowest charge transfer resistance (Rct). The corresponding Arrhenius 
plot for the 0.01 M BNC electrolyte is presented in Fig. 2H, while the 
baseline electrolyte’s Arrhenius plot is shown in Figure S18. The Ea 
values at different DoD are summarized in Fig. 2I and Table S4, 
revealing that, for each electrolyte, Ea values at around 4.3 to 4.7%, 33.8 
to 39.4%, and 59.5 to 66.1% are higher than their adjacent positions, 
signifying these DoD as crucial steps or rate-determining steps. In fact, 
these specified DoD ranges align with the two plateaus on the discharge 
voltage profile: 4.3 to 4.7% represents the initial elemental sulfur to 
long-chain polysulfide conversion, and 33.8 to 66.1% represents middle- 
chain to short-chain polysulfides conversion—where liquid-solid and 
solid-solid phase transitions occur. The 0.01 M BNC electrolyte exhibits 
consistently lower Ea values throughout the entire DoD, highlighting the 
enhanced kinetics induced by BNC, particularly within the 33.8 to 
66.1% DoD range, where sluggish kinetics are associated with phase 
transitions.
The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) results 
further substantiate the superior kinetics, as illustrated in Figures S19A- 
B. The gap between the working voltage and the equilibrium voltage 
encapsulates the total voltage polarization, encompassing activation 
polarization (ƞact), ohmic polarization (ƞohm), and concentration polar­
ization (ƞcon). The instantaneous voltage recovery includes the cumu­
lative effect of ƞact and ƞohm because they are only present during an 
applied current as depicted in Figure S19B. The gradual voltage recovery 
over time corresponds to ƞcon, reflecting the hysteretic diffusion of 
reactive species. The EIS data reveals that the ohmic resistance (Rs) re­
mains nearly unchanged, rendering the variation of ƞohm reasonably 
negligible (Figures S16-S17, S19B). For the 0.01 M BNC electrolyte, ƞact 
manifests the lowest value of 20.66 mV, in contrast to the baseline 
electrolytes (62.45 mV), stemming from reduced hindrance in the 
nucleation and deposition of short-chain polysulfides (Figure S19B).
2.3. Operando Raman spectroscopy
Operando Raman is a practical technique for monitoring chemical 
reaction pathways without any destructive interruptions, as demon­
strated in our previous research (Figures S20A-B) [45]. Figs. 3A-C pre­
sent conventional Raman spectra of a Li-S battery using the baseline 
electrolyte. Throughout a typical discharge process, elemental sulfur 
(S8) undergoes a conversion sequence, transforming into long-chain 
polysulfides (Li2Sx, 6<x<8), followed by middle-chain polysulfides 
(Li2Sx, 3<x<5), and ultimately into short-chain polysulfides, also 
referred to as insoluble polysulfides (Li2Sx, 1<x<2) [46]. In Fig. 3A, the 
characteristic Raman shift peak of elemental sulfur is identified at 217 
cm-1 in black. The two major intermediates, long-chain and 
middle-chain polysulfides, are denoted by red and blue colors, show­
casing their characteristic Raman shifts at 400 and 453 cm-1, respec­
tively. These peak assignments align with our ex-situ samples of 
different polysulfides and electrolytes (Figures S21A-B) and previous 
studies [46]. Fig. 3A depicts the overall Raman peak changes during the 
full discharge process, with two plateaus separated by purple dash lines. 
Fig. 3B zooms into the portion before the DoD reaches 25%, emphasizing 
the change of the two intermediate polysulfides in the 1st plateau. 
Fig. 3C displays the corresponding intensity heatmap for Fig. 3A after 
normalization to the highest peak in each spectrum to enhance the 
visualization of polysulfide changes throughout the discharge process. 
The operando Raman spectra of the baseline electrolyte elucidate that 
long-chain and middle-chain polysulfides coexist throughout almost the 
entire 1st plateau region (before DoD of 25%), with middle-chain pol­
ysulfides dominating in the subsequent 2nd plateau region until DoD 
reaches approximately 88%. While the insensitivity of short-chain pol­
ysulfides (Li2Sx, x<4) to Raman shift prevents direct observation, our 
forthcoming operando XAS technique, detailed in the next section, will 
address this missing piece of the puzzle.

significant deviations in the spectra were observed compared to the 
baseline configuration. In Figs. 3D and 3E, the intensity of long-chain 
and middle-chain polysulfides is noticeably suppressed, indicating a 
diminished presence of these key intermediate polysulfides compared to 
the baseline electrolyte. The intensity heatmap (Fig. 3F) of the 0.01 M

lead to sulfur depletion and subsequent capacity shrinkage, as reflected 
in the cycling performance discussed in a later section.

hypothesis derived from AIMD simulations, reinforcing the outcomes 
observed in 7Li NMR by providing direct observations of SRR kinetics 
enhancement and polysulfide immobilization. More importantly, the 
operando Raman findings underscore the necessities of optimizing BNC 
concentration to ensure an undisturbed SRR pathway. However, they 
lack crucial insights into short-chain polysulfides (Li2Sx, x<4), which are 
essential for a comprehensive understanding of SRR kinetics.
2.4. Operando XAS
X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) is a critical tool for investi­
gating sulfur evolution in Li-S batteries, offering elemental sensitivity 
that enables the precise detection of short-chain polysulfides (Li2Sx, 
x<4), which are challenging to observe with Raman spectroscopy. 
Monitoring the formation of these polysulfides is critical for two key 
reasons. First, this phase of the SRR involves a significant phase transi­
tion that severely hinders reaction kinetics. Second, short-chain 
polysulfides are less susceptible to nucleophilic reactions with BNC, 
providing a more accurate assessment of BNC’s impact on improving 
reaction kinetics. To ensure accurate data interpretation, a compre­
hensive set of standard reference samples was prepared (Figures S23A- 
D), with detailed peak identifications provided in the SI (see Ex Situ XAS 
Sample Analysis).
Operando XAS spectra for the 0.01 M BNC electrolyte were collected 
during the first discharge cycle at a C-rate of C/10, with the cell setup 
illustrated in Figure S20C. The spectra (Fig. 4A) and corresponding in­
tensity heatmap (Fig. 4B) reveal the emergence of a peak at 2471.5 eV 
(hereby denoted as "pre-edge peak"; see SI for detailed explanation) as 
the DoD approaches 10%, signifying the formation of Li2Sx (6<x<8) 
during the 1st plateau phase of the voltage profile. Fig. 4B shows that 
this pre-edge peak intensifies as the DoD reaches 37%, indicating the 
formation and predominance of Li2Sx (3<x<5). These observations align 
seamlessly with our operando Raman spectra, yet XAS uniquely captures 
the continuous increase of the pre-edge signal from a DoD of 39% to 
75%, marking the formation of short-chain polysulfides (Li2Sx, 2<x<4), 
as confirmed by standard references in Figures S23A-D. Moreover, a 
shift of peak from 2473.3 eV (hereby denoted as "main peak"; see SI for 
detailed explanation) to 2474.2 eV is observed beyond a DoD of 92% in 
Fig. 4B, directly indicating the formation of Li2S, the final SRR product.

spectra representing mixtures of compounds, such as polysulfides, for

2.5. Polysulfides shuttling suppression
The impact of BNC on suppressing polysulfide migration was inves­
tigated using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) mapping to analyze anode sur­
faces extracted from coin cells subjected to varying cycle numbers with 
different electrolytes. Since the sulfur atoms in the LiTFSI salt commonly 
used in Li-S batteries could interfere with XRF mapping, a pre-treatment 
procedure was implemented to wash the electrodes (see Washing Pro­
cedure in SI), ensuring the removal of LiTFSI while preserving poly­
sulfides. Validation of this washing procedure is detailed in Figures S26- 
S27.
Normalized XRF mapping results reveal the sulfur accumulation 
divergence on the anodes (Figs. 5A-B and E-F). In the 0.01 M BNC 
electrolyte, sulfur deposition is consistently lower compared to the 
baseline, regardless of the cycle number, as reflected by the color in­
tensity. This result provides direct evidence of suppressed polysulfide 
shuttling in the BNC electrolyte, particularly pronounced during the 
initial cycling stages, attributed to the controlled nucleophilic reactions 
between BNC and polysulfides at optimal concentrations, as corrobo­
rated by UV–vis and FTIR results.
To gain a comprehensive understanding of sulfur composition on the 
anode, XANES analysis was conducted at two distinct locations identi­
fied from XRF maps: high sulfur accumulation (L1, green circles) and 
low sulfur accumulation (L2, cyan circles). This strategy ensures that 
sulfur composition data accounts for the inhomogeneous surface

3. Conclusions
In conclusion, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of BNC as a 
bifunctional electrolyte additive in improving SRR kinetics and sup­
pressing polysulfide migration in Li-S batteries. Unlike previous studies 
that emphasized BNC’s role in immobilizing polysulfides through 
nucleophilic reactions, our findings reveal that BNC can enhance SRR 
kinetics by altering the Li+ solvation structure at an optimal concen­
tration, as evidenced by AIMD simulations and 7Li NMR analysis. 
However, excessive BNC concentrations result in aggressive nucleo­
philic reactions that deplete sulfur and cause capacity decay.
FTIR and UV–vis analyses confirmed that BNC can coexist with Li2S6 
under moderated nucleophilic reactions, enabling dual functionalities: 
modifying the Li+ solvation structure to enhance SRR kinetics and 
immobilizing soluble polysulfides to mitigate the shuttling effect. Ki­
netic improvements were validated through comprehensive electro­
chemical experiments, including CV and Li2S nucleation studies, and 
further corroborated by in situ EIS, which highlighted reductions in 
activation energy for polysulfide conversion. Operando Raman spec­
troscopy demonstrated BNC’s synergistic effect on SRR kinetics and 
polysulfide immobilization, while operando XAS provided detailed in­
sights into enhanced kinetics, particularly for middle-chain polysulfides, 
with quantification achieved through MCR-ALS analysis. Additionally, 
XRF mapping with XANES analyses and shuttle current tests revealed 
BNC’s effectiveness in suppressing polysulfide migration, especially 
when comparing anode and cathode surfaces after varying cycle 
numbers.
These synergistic effects collectively contribute to improved cycling 
performance in high-loading configurations, achieving a sulfur areal 
loading of 4 mg cm-2 and low E/S ratio of 8 µL mg-1. The optimized 
system delivers a capacity retention of 83.97% at a C-rate of C/2, with a 
coulombic efficiency of 92.96% over 200 cycles, outperforming existing 
benchmarks in the literature. This work highlights the potential of BNC 
as a model additive for broader electrolyte modifications, paving the 
way for advanced designs that enhance the electrochemical perfor­
mance of Li-S batteries.
