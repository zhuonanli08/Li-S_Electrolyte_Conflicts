Methyl Tert-Butyl Ether Induced Synergistic Engineering of
Inner and Outer Solvation Shell for Practical Lithium–Sulfur
Batteries
Lu Wang, Dexiang Xu, Zifeng Liu, Yue Sun, Chaohui Ruan, and Zhubing Xiao*
The electrolyte-mediated electrochemistry of Li–S batteries dictates that
electrolyte modiﬁcations regarding solvation structures of lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs) are the core concerns. To accommodate the conversion kinetics of LiPSs
and interfacial stability of Li-metal anode upon lean electrolyte and high sulfur
loading, a cosolvent of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) is screened for DME-
based electrolyte. The low solvating capability of MTBE enables it to principally
construct the outer protective solvation shell of LiPSs. MTBE also promotes the
incorporation of DME and anion into the inner solvation shell, facilitating eﬀec-
tive solvation of LiPSs and a stable interfacial structure of the Li-metal anode.
Based on mutually corroborative measurements and theoretical calculations,
the superiorities of optimized LWSE electrolyte from MTBE are ﬁrmly veriﬁed in
protecting LiPSs against Li-metal corrosion and improving interfacial stability
of Li anode and electrochemical kinetics. In situ Raman/XRD results further
support that LiPSs shuttling is greatly suppressed, and highly reversible electro-
chemical conversions of sulfur species involving a “𝜶-sulfur to 𝜶-sulfur” mech-
anism are corroborated in LWSE. The physicochemical merits of LWSE enable
pouch-type Li–S full cell (N/P: 1.3) to achieve a high energy density of 385.3 Wh
kg−1 at an E/S ratio of 3.0 µL mg−1, and a high loading of 4.8 mgs cm−2.

1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are con-
sidered to be promising candidate for
next-generation energy storage system ow-
ing to the high theoretical energy den-
sity (2600 Wh kg−1), richness in natural re-
sources and environmental amity of cath-
ode sulfur,[1–6] and sulfur cathode of Li–S
system has been widely known to involve an
“solid–liquid–solid” phase-transition mech-
anism, where liquid phases of soluble
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) intermediates
(Li2Sx, 3≤x≤8) in the local electrolyte en-
vironment at electrode–electrolyte interface
play a key role in mediating the redox con-
versions between solid phases of S8 ele-
ment and lithiation product (Li2S).[2,7–13] As
a double-edged sword, LiPSs might diﬀuse
toward Li metal anode due to concentra-
tion gradients and induce parasitic reac-
tions with the anode, which further results
in the notorious shuttle eﬀect, Li metal de-
pletion, and even batteries failure.[1,10,13–18]
The LiPSs species in solvated state dictate
an electrolyte-mediated electrochemistry of Li–S systems and
electrolyte formula serves as one of the decisive aspects for the
performances advancement of Li–S batteries.[6,12,19–21] Speciﬁ-
cally, both redox conversion behaviors of sulfur species at sul-
fur cathode and the stability of Li metal anode are inextricably
correlated with solvated state of LiPSs in electrolyte,[11,13,15–17,22]
which could impact the holistic performances of electrodes and
their full cell (such as energy density).[13,16,20,21] To accommo-
date the unfavorable loss of LiPSs species and their undesired
side reactions with Li metal anode in the typical electrolyte (i.e.,
lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulphonyl) imide (LiTFSI) in 1,2-
dimethoxyethane/1,3-dioxolane (DME/DOL)), various electrolyte
modiﬁcation strategies have been proposed. High concentration
electrolytes (HCEs) or localized high concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs) could eﬀectively restrict the dissolution of LiPSs species
by compressing the amount of free ether solvent, but the com-
promised redox kinetics in HCEs or the high cost and poten-
tial environmental hazards of ﬂuorinated cosolvent diluter in
LHCEs might bring undesired implications.[14,16,23–26] Low con-
centration electrolytes (LiTFSI concentration lower than 1.0 m)
could enable the aggregation of short-chain polysulﬁdes and pre-
vent LiPSs dissolution,[27,28] while deep studies are also called for,
such as the redox conversion behaviors of sulfur species in dilute

ing capability has been extensively concerned.[2,6,19,23] Based on
the typical electrolyte (i.e., 1.0 m LiTFSI in DME/DOL), molec-
ular screen of the cosolvents with regulated solvating capability
toward LiPSs species have been reported, such as hexyl methyl
ether (HME),[30] isopropyl ether (DIPE),[18] and di-isopropyl
sulﬁde (DIPS)[19] those are adopted for outer solvation shell
with relatively weak solvating capability;[31,32] typical solvents
with high donor number and highly solvating capability have
been also studied as inner shell solvent featured with polysul-
ﬁde radicals formation, such as HMPA, DMSO, with classical
ether DME/DOL as outer solvation shell.[6,33,34] The reported
wisdom pertaining to the cosolvents for inner or outer solvation
shells of LiPSs have well resolved the necessitated solvation of
LiPSs and remarkably improved the performances of the sulfur
cathode and stability of Li metal anode. However, the solvating
capability compatibility between solvents in inner and outer
shells has been seemingly overlooked, and the solvation number
of solvents molecular in solvation shell (especially inner shell)
of LiPSs was usually evacuated by the foreign cosolvents in
reported works,[6,24,30,32,35,36] which adversely aﬀects the holistic
solvation degree toward LiPSs species, thereby compromising
the conversion kinetics of LiPSs and interfacial stability of Li
metal anode. And the cases would be exacerbated upon lean
electrolyte (with a E/S ratio<5 μL mg−1) and high sulfur loading
(>4.0 mgs cm−2).[13,16,23,37] The DOL derived electrolytes are
also challenged by the brittle SEI and unsatisfactory interfacial
stability against Li metal anode.[12,19,38] Thus, well-regulated sol-
vent conﬁgurations are highly anticipated in solvation structure
construction for Li–S batteries.
Here, based on a molecular screen strategy, a cosolvent of
methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) is optimized for DME based elec-
trolyte of 0.5 m LiTFSI in DME/MTBE (1:1 by vol.) with 0.2 m
LiNO3 (deﬁned as (LWSE). The molecular structure features en-
dow MTBE with high anti-reductive capability and low solvat-
ing ability toward both LiTFSI salt and polysulﬁdes anion, thus
MTBE principally constructs the outer solvation shell of LiPSs
(Li+ ions) and enables high electrochemical stability of LWSE.
Additionally, MTBE promotes the incorporation of DME molec-
ular and TFSI−anion into the inner solvation shell, facilitating
eﬀective solvation of LiPSs and a stable interfacial structure of
Li-metal anode.
The unique solvation structure of LWSE enables the Li||Li sym-
metrical cell stably operate for 2000 h, and the Li||Cu cell could
maintain a high Coulombic eﬃciency of 98.7% for 850 cycles.
In situ Raman/XRD results further support that, shuttle eﬀect
of LiPSs could be greatly suppressed, and highly reversible elec-
trochemical conversions of sulfur species involving a “𝛼-sulfur
to 𝛼-sulfur” mechanism have been elucidated in LWSE. The low
density of MTBE (0.74 g mL−1) also results in a lower density
of LWSE electrolyte (0.94 g mL−1) versus the conventional elec-
trolyte (1.17 g mL−1). Application of LWSE electrolyte has been
further validated in pouch type Li–S full cell with a low N/P ra-
tio of 1.3, and a high energy density of 385.3 Wh kg−1 could be
harvested under harsh conditions including a low E/S ratio of
3.0 μL mg−1 and a high loading of 4.8 mgs cm−2, much higher
than the case of conventional electrolytes (327.4 Wh kg−1). The
MTBE derived LWSE has been veriﬁed to possess great applica-
tion potential for advanced Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Screen of MTBE and its Properties
The target cosolvent for the outer shell in constructing
an EPSE for DME-derived electrolytes generally follows two
key criteria: weak solvating capability and high anti-reductive
properties,[19,20,23] and molecule structure modiﬁcation proposed
here is demonstrated in Figure 1a. It can be inferred that, re-
placing the high-polarity ethylene glycol segment with the low-
polarity propanol segment could weaken the solvating ability of
the methyl n-butyl ether (MBE) solvent while maintains its ap-
propriate chelation ability with Li+ ion,[32] and MBE could be
further modiﬁed by replacing the butyl group with a tert-butyl
group that has a greater steric hindrance eﬀect,[32,39] resulting in
the methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) solvent that is anticipated to
exhibit even lower reactivity with lithium metal despite a con-
current increase in viscosity (Table S1, Supporting Information).
The high C/O ratio, unique molecular structure, such as the
steric hindrance eﬀect from the tert-butyl group for MTEB, and
low dielectric constant (4.38 and 4.29 for MTEB and MBE, re-
spectively) (Table S1, Supporting Information) indicate that both
MTBE and MBE possess weak solvating capability.[9,19,32,40] The
electrostatic potential diagrams (ESP) and de-solvation energy
of various solvent with Li+ were further calculated to evaluate
their solvation capability.[3,16,41,42] As shown in Figure 1b–d, DME
exhibits a strong interaction with Li+ ion via its two negatively
charged O atoms (red part in the calculated electrostatic poten-
tial diagrams), resulting in a high de-solvation energy of 307 kJ
mol−1. In contrast, the less negative potential of O atoms give
rise to a much lower Li+ ion de-solvation energy of 214, 221,
and 186 kJ mol−1 for MTBE, MBE and DOL, respectively, and
the slightly higher de-solvation energy values of MTBE and MBE
than DOL are associated with their relatively ﬂexible linear struc-
ture (Figure 1b–d; Figure S1, Supporting Information).[39,42–44]
The cosolvents of MTBE and MBE were preliminarily evaluated
in Li–S batteries, as a substitute of DOL to prepare their corre-
sponding DME based electrolytes of 1.0 m LiTFSI, respectively,
both containing 0.2 m LiNO3. As shown in Figure S2 (Supporting
Information), the Li–S battery using MTBE-derived electrolyte ex-
hibits much better cyclic stability than the other two counterparts
of DOL and MBE, verifying the superiority of MTBE cosolvent for
Li–S batteries. And MTBE displays a superior ionic conductivity
to MBE (Figure S3, Supporting Information), which is more fa-
vorable for Li-ion transport kinetics. The physical/chemical prop-
erties and the favorable cost of MTBE (Table S1, Supporting In-
formation) also enable it to stand out as a protective outer solvent
shell for DME-based EPSE and robust SEI construction.

(HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecule orbital (LUMO)
were additionally calculated to reveal the thermodynamic redox

Noticeably, the peaks at ≈820 and ≈850 cm−1 assigned to typi-

better compatibility with Li metal anode, comparing with DOL.
The electrolyte structure of LWSE was also probed via wide-angle

a r value of ≈6.8 Å were determined, according to the Ehrenfest
equation Q · r = 2.46𝜋, where r is the characteristic interatomic

ity of MTBE inspired inspections of solvation structure and elec-
trochemical behaviors of LiPSs in LWSE. According to radial dis-
tribution functions around S8

The peaks at 0.364 and 0.802 nm are two concentric solvent
shells, respectively, which is denoted as shell 1 and 2 outward
from the Li+ center, respectively, in accordance with the WAXS
result in Figure 2i. As expected, DME with strong solvating capa-

the LWSE system, two concentric solvent shells were maintained

tionally, in comparison with the CE system, intensiﬁed incorpo-
ration of DME molecular into the inner shell could be observed
for LWSE system, (Figure 4b), which indicates that, adoption
of MTBE as cosolvent could promote the coordination between
DME and Li+ ions.[20,54] Apart from the MD simulations, Raman
spectra of the three solutions containing dissolved LiPSs were
further measured. As shown in Figure 4c, for 0.5 m Li2S8 (located
at 396.1, 446.7, and 519.9 cm−1) in DME/DOL, DME/MTBE and
DME/DOL/MTBE, the Raman peaks attributed to LiPSs species
well overlapped,[54–59] the signals of MTBE (725.6 cm−1) and DOL

ing to free DME at ≈847 cm−1 (Figure 4c; Figure S17, Supporting

further supporting that MTBE or DOL are located in the shell
2.[19,20,54]
The 7Li-NMR was further conducted to probe the solvation
structure of LiPSs, and the result collected in DME/MTBE was
similar with that in DME/DOL (Figure 4d), indicating that Li+ in
LiPSs interacts with the O atom through ion–dipole interactions
for both DME/MTBE and DME/DOL.[16,18,19] Noticeably, com-
pared with the case of DME/DOL, the chemical shift of 7Li-NMR
in DME/MTBE exhibits a slight upﬁeld displacement. Given that
MTBE and DOL weakly interact with Li+ and DME dominantly
occupies the inner solvent shell of LiPSs, it is rational to conclude
that the displaced chemical shift of 7Li in DME/MTBE could be
attributed to intensiﬁed coordination between DME and Li+ ions
in DME/MTBE,[16,18,23] which is in line with results from the the-
oretical calculations above. Thus, it has been ﬁrmly veriﬁed that
MTBE cosolvent could well perform as the outer shell, and en-
hance the solvation of inner DME toward LiPSs, constructing an
encapsulating structure of LiPSs with intensiﬁed inner solvation,
as demonstrated in Figure 4e. And it can be inferred that, beneﬁt-
ing from the eﬀective encapsulating of weak MTBE in the outer
solvent shell, the irreversible loss of active sulfur species can be
eﬀectively restrained in LWSE, and promote the construction of
stable SEI on Li metal anode, inhibiting the growth of lithium
dendrites, as schematically depicted in Figure 4f.
2.3. Electrochemical Performances of Li–S Batteries
To further investigate the eﬀect of MTBE as an outer encapsu-

3. Conclusion
In this work, based on molecular structure regulation, a con-
solvent MTBE possessing weak solvation capability has been pro-
posed for DME-based electrolyte of LWSE. The weak solvation
capability of MTBE enables it to principally construct the outer
solvation shell of Li+ ions as well as LiPSs species, and its physic-
ochemical properties including low polarity, low solvating abil-
ity toward both LiTFSI salt and polysulﬁdes anion, and low re-
action activity toward Li metal anode, endow the MTBE-derived
electrolyte of LWSE to well protect LiPSs species against Li metal
corrosion and improve interfacial stability of Li metal anode. The
electrochemical measurements of Li||Cu cells, Li||Li cells, and Li–
S batteries, as well as solvation structure inspections and theoret-
ical calculations, also verify the superiority of MTBE-based elec-
trolyte LWSE in improving the cyclic life and capacity delivery of
cells, in comparison with the case of CE. Application of LWSE in
Ah level pouch type Li–S full cells further prove that, the physico-
chemical merits of LWSE, such as low density, low viscosity, could
enable to harvest a high energy density of 385.3 Wh kg−1 at a low
E/S of 3.0 μL mg−1 and a high loading of 4.8 mgs cm−2, which
is retained as 327.4 Wh kg−1 after 30 cycles. In situ Raman/XRD
results further support the eﬀectively suppressed shuttle eﬀect of
LiPSs and highly reversible electrochemical conversions of sulfur
species involving a “𝛼-sulfur to 𝛼-sulfur” mechanism in LWSE.
MTBE has been solidly conﬁrmed to possess high potential to be
a promising cosolvent for practical Li–S batteries, and this work
could provide new insight into electrolyte structure constructions
in Li–S batteries.
