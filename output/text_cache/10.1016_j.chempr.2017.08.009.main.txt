Bio-inspired Self-Healing
Electrolytes for Li-S Batteries
Sinho Choi1 and Guoxiu Wang1,*
Lithium-sulfur batteries are the most promising candidates for next-generation
high-energy rechargeable batteries. However, the development of practical
lithium-sulfur batteries faces many challenges. As recently reported in the Jour-
nal of the American Chemical Society, Zhang and colleagues have developed
lithium-sulfur batteries with an ultra-long cycle life by employing polysulﬁdes
as self-healing agents.
Lithium-ion batteries currently domi-
nate the market of power sources for
portable electronic devices and electric
vehicles. However, the energy density
of lithium-ion batteries is limited by
the existing cathode and anode mate-
rials.1
The
increasing
demands
for
high-energy storage systems require
rechargeable batteries to have light
weight, high power, high energy den-
sity, and long cycle life. Therefore,
new types of battery systems such as
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) and lithium-air bat-
teries have been developed.2 In partic-
ular,
Li-S
batteries
have
attracted
extensive
interest
because
of
their
unique advantages, such as the high
theoretical energy density of 2,600
W,hr kg1, the abundance of sulfur on
Earth, and the low cost of sulfur. How-
ever, Li-S batteries also suffer from
several challenging issues, including
the low electrical conductivity of sulfur,
the dissolution and shuttle effects of
polysulﬁdes, and short cycle life.3,4
Many strategies have been employed
to overcome these drawbacks. These
include the use of porous cathode
host materials for physically conﬁning
polysulﬁdes, the addition of adsorption
agents for chemically binding polysul-
ﬁdes, and the insertion of interlayers
for preventing the migration of polysul-
ﬁdes.5–7
In a recently published paper in the
Journal of the American Chemistry So-
ciety,8 Zhang and colleagues intro-
duced
polysulﬁde-preloaded,
self-
healing electrolytes (SHEs) with the
aim of achieving a long stable cyclabil-
ity for Li-S batteries, and they proposed
mechanisms of nucleation growth and
classical super-saturation theory (the
Gibbs-Thomson effect).9 The authors
were inspired by the ‘‘self-healing’’
strategy of biological systems, such as
ﬁbrinolysis within blood vessels. The
‘‘self-healing’’ phenomenon in Li-S bat-
teries mimics two reactions in biological
processes, namely ﬁbrinolysis for the
coagulation of thrombi and plasmin ac-
tion for solubilizing thrombi in bio-sys-
tems. Although several research groups
have reported self-healing in batteries
in the past, this is the ﬁrst time that
the
self-healing
concept
has
been
applied in Li-S batteries. Polysulﬁdes
were used as the self-healing agent
because they are able to transfer solid
polysulﬁde compounds into solution,
where they subsequently re-participate
in electrochemical reaction cycles and
lead to outstanding battery perfor-
mance. In particular, this study ﬁrst
introduced crystal growth models to
explain how polysulﬁde is grown with
different spatial phenomena, including
heterogeneity and homogeneity. Ac-
cording to the theoretical model, the
heterogeneity mechanism in a routine
electrolyte (RE) results in unsynchro-
nized nucleation and growth rates of
polysulﬁdes in different regions on
cathodes. However,
in
a
SHE,
the
nucleation and growth of polysulﬁdes
on cathodes are spatially homoge-
neous and can be transferred into
the electrolyte solution for later self-
healing during the charging process.
Figure 1 shows a schematic illustration
of Li-S batteries with a RE and SHE,
elucidating the shuttle effect and self-
healing, respectively.
To understand the self-healing mecha-
nism on sulfur cathodes, Zhang and
colleagues captured real-time movies
and photos showing that polysulﬁdes
were dissolved into electrolyte and
gave no color change of the SHE. After
discharge, scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) of the nickel foam@LixS
composite electrodes showed that the
nickel foam was uniformly coated with
a ﬁlm of sulfur species, indicating the
uniform
phase-transfer
reaction
be-
tween
solid-phase
LixS
and
liquid-
phase
polysulﬁdes
in
the
SHE.
In
contrast, SEM of the other electrode
with a RE showed that micron-sized
sulfur particles produced by the recrys-
tallization still remained. To further
enhance the self-healing effect in Li-S
batteries, the authors employed sulfur
microparticle (SMiP) cathodes and car-
bon nanotube (CNT) current collectors
with high conductivity and hierarchical
porous structures. The cycling perfor-
mance of SMiP cathodes with a 0.33 M
Li2S5-SHE was dramatically extended
to 7,500 cycles at a current density of
1.2 mA cm2, whereas the initial capac-
ity was 889 mA,hr g1. The average
Coulombic efﬁciency was also above
99%,
corresponding
to
a
capacity
decay rate of only 0.010% per cycle.
Such long cycling performance has
never been reported previously.

The authors further demonstrated more
advanced Li-S batteries with a ‘‘self-
healing’’ feature. The SHE-1 M/SMiP
(with a high Li2S5 concentration and
high sulfur loading of 4.2 mg cm2)
samples exhibited ‘‘self-healing’’ be-
haviors such as the restoration of initial
speciﬁc
capacity
and
highly
stable
cycling performances after 1,000 cy-
cles. Even after a static operation for
72 hr, the capacities remained steady
with
no
decay
but
were
slightly
increased, further indicating the unique
self-healing feature. More interestingly,
the cycled Li-S batteries with SMiP/RE
exhibited a restored capacity and sta-
ble cycling performances after being in-
jected with the SHE, proving a strong
healing capability of the SHE. In addi-
tion, SEM was performed on the sulfur
electrodes for analysis of their morphol-
ogies after electrochemical cycling. In
the RE/SMiP Li-S cells, polysulﬁde parti-
cles were uniformly deposited on the
outer surface of CNTs. However, the
SHE/SMiP cells showed no aggregation
of solid phases or micron-sized parti-
cles on CNTs even after long-term
cycling over 2,000 cycles.
Furthermore,
the
authors
extended
relevant work to explore other healing
agents for practical Li-S batteries. The
redox couple of LiI/I2 was employed.
It was found that I2 can assist the oxidi-
zation of bulk Li2S to highly colloidal
sulfur
particles,
further
accelerating
the continuous solubilization of Li2S.
Moreover, I2 was re-generated during
the charging process of the Li-S battery
at
3.2
V.
Consequently,
the
LiI/I2
redox couple clearly illustrated that a
self-healing ability was attained in Li-S
batteries.
In summary, the self-healing inspired by
biological systems has been success-
fully applied in Li-S batteries via a com-
plex solid-liquid phase-transfer mecha-
nism. This self-healing strategy can help
in the design of other healing agents
for electrochemical energy storage sys-
tems. We believe that this bio-inspired
strategy,
which
is
extremely
chal-
lenging but very promising, can also
be extended to other novel recharge-
able-battery systems.
