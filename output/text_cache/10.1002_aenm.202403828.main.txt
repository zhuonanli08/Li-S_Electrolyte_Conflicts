Moderately Solvating Electrolyte with Fluorinated
Cosolvents for Lean-Electrolyte Li–S Batteries
Ilju Kim, Sejin Kim, Hannah Cho, Jinkwan Jung, Hyeokjin Kwon, Dongwoo Kim,
Yewon Shin, and Hee-Tak Kim*
To surpass the energy density limit of current Li–S batteries, attaining a long
lifespan under lean-electrolyte conditions is imperative. The persistent
challenge involves suppressing electrolyte decomposition while facilitating
sulfur electrode reaction. In this study, the solvating power of 1dimethoxy
ethane is ﬁne-tuned, the main solvent, using ﬂuorinated ether cosolvents via
H–F interactions. As the ﬂuorination degree of the cosolvent increases, the
coordination of anions around the Li-ion increases, and the solubilities of Li
polysulﬁdes decrease. By systematically varying the solvating power,
moderately solvating electrolytes are prepared that can eﬀectively suppress
the dissolution of Li polysulﬁdes without hindering the redox kinetics. The
moderately solvating electrolytes induce uniform Li deposition and reduce
electrolyte decomposition owing to the formation of anion-derived solid
electrolyte interphase. An assembled pouch-type Li–S battery containing an
electrolyte with an optimized solvation power delivers 405 Wh kg−1 at an E/S
ratio of 2.0 μL mgs
−1 with a lifespan of over 80 cycles. This study suggests a
strategy to ﬁnely tune the Li+ solvation structure for achieving well-balanced
performances of sulfur cathodes and Li-metal anodes under lean-electrolyte
conditions.

are promising next-generation batteries.[1a,b] However, to over-

ing the amount of electrolytes in these batteries is imperative.

and substantial involvement of the anion in the Li+ solvation

(LUMO) than that of the anion in the free state. Considering
the detrimental eﬀect of electrolyte decomposition at the LMAs
on the lifespans of lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries, the recent re-

trolyte development remains uncertain. To address this, it is
necessary to explore solvents with diﬀerent chemical struc-
tures and conduct systemic investigations into electrolyte de-
signs that consider the formation of LiPSs and their im-
pact on both sulfur cathodes and LMAs. Furthermore, it is
crucial to examine how these electrolytes can enhance cycle
performance by taking into account various factors such as
LiPS formation, SEI structure, Li morphology, and electrolyte
depletion.
Herein, we present a systematic investigation of ﬂuorinated
ether (FE) cosolvents that provide a moderately-solvating state
and prolong the lifespans of lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries. We re-
examined the root cause of cell failure using a conventional elec-
trolyte containing a mixture of dimethoxy ethane (DME) and 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL) under lean-electrolyte conditions to determine
the key factor aﬀecting the lifespans of these batteries. Based on
the analyses, we substituted conventionally used DOL with vari-
ous FE cosolvents that have diﬀerent ﬂuorination degrees to pre-
pare electrolytes containing a mixture of DME and FE (DME-FE
electrolytes). Subsequently, we investigated the relationship be-
tween the solvating power and performance of an Li–S battery
under lean-electrolyte conditions. Our analysis reveals that on de-
creasing the solvation abilities of DME-FE electrolytes, the Li+
solvation structure transforms from strongly solvating to weakly
solvating, which accompanies the promotion of anion coordina-
tion in the Li+ solvate and the formation of an anion-derived
SEI. Furthermore, we suggest that the speciﬁc hydrogen–ﬂuorine
(H–F) interaction between DME and FE is responsible for the
variations in the Li+ solvation structure. The density and unifor-
mity of the LMA morphology increased during cycles with the
decrease in solvating power, thereby enhancing Li reversibility.
However, the reduction in solvating power adversely aﬀected the
liquid-phase reduction reaction of the LiPSs. Therefore, moder-
ately solvating electrolytes yielded optimal performance in the
lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries. Upon comparing the residual elec-
trolytes after operation with strongly and moderately solvating
electrolytes, we identiﬁed electrolyte depletion as the root cause
of cell failure in lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries, not only in con-
ventional electrolytes but also in other types of electrolytes. This
issue was eﬀectively mitigated by the altered SEI structure and
Li morphology resulting from the changes in the solvation struc-
ture. Notably, a lean-electrolyte Li–S battery (0.5-Ah class) with a
gravimetric energy density of 405 Wh kg−1 at a challenging E/S
ratio = 2 demonstrated stable operation over 80 cycles. This con-
ﬁrms the potential application of this electrolyte design in prac-
tical Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Failure Analysis of a Lean-Electrolyte Li–S Battery
The root cause of the degradation of lean-electrolyte Li–S bat-
teries was re-examined at an E/S ratio of 5 using 1 m lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 0.2 m LiNO3, and
DME/DOL (1:1 v/v), which is considered to be a baseline elec-
trolyte for Li–S batteries.[2] As shown in Figure 1a, operat-
ing the cell at a 0.15 C discharge/charge rate signiﬁcantly de-
clines the discharge capacity after 40 cycles. At the 46th cy-
cle, an abnormal-shaped, ﬂuctuating second voltage plateau ap-
peared, indicating cell failure (Figure 1b). We conducted a post-
mortem analysis on the LMA and electrolyte after the 46th
discharge.
Li is continuously and irreversibly formed in LMBs during op-
eration owing to the disconnected Li particles and Li corrosion
by the electrolyte at the LMA surface. In Li–S batteries, Li corro-
sion can be highly signiﬁcant because of the corrosive nature of
LiPSs.[6] To quantify the amount of reversible Li remaining after
cycle failure, an Li-SUS half-cell was assembled with the LMA ex-
tracted from the Li–S battery, and Li was stripped for the LMA in
the half-cell (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The amount of
reversible Li was found to be 6.3 mAh cm−2, which is 21% less
than the theoretical capacity of the pristine 40-μm-thick LMA (8
mAh cm−2) (Figure 1c,d). The reversible capacity remained larger
than the discharge capacity of the Li–S battery (3.04 mAh cm−2),
conﬁrming that cell capacity is not limited by the remaining ca-
pacity of the LMA, consistent with the results reported in previ-
ous studies.[9] However, 21% loss in the LMA indicates signiﬁ-
cant LMA degradation over 46 cycles only, as supported by the
thick, porous layer observed in the cross-sectional images of the
cycled LMA (Figure 1e).
The NMR spectra of the baseline electrolyte before the opera-
tion and after cell failure were compared (Figure 1f; S2, Support-
ing Information). The results revealed that 33% of DME, 49% of
DOL, and 2% of TFSI−anions decomposed during the 46 cycles.
This demonstrates the signiﬁcant decomposition of the solvents,
rather than the salt anions, in the Li–S battery. The remaining
amount of electrolyte (1.76 μL mAh−1), estimated from quanti-
tative NMR (QNMR) analysis, was slightly more than that re-
quired to fully wet the S cathode and separator (1.57 μL mAh−1)
(Note S1 and Table S1, Supporting Information). However, a cer-
tain amount of liquid electrolyte must be consumed for wetting
the porous Li layer; therefore, the remaining amount of elec-
trolyte was insuﬃcient for stably operating the Li–S battery. To
conﬁrm whether electrolyte decomposition is responsible for ca-
pacity fade, we reassembled a cell with fresh electrolyte and the
electrodes of the failed cells and operated this cell. The cell was
rejuvenated owing to electrolyte exchange (Figure 1g), which con-
ﬁrms that solvent depletion is responsible for cell degradation.
The electrolyte decomposition can be increased by roughening
the LMA and trapping the electrolyte components in the porous
layer.

2.2. Solvation Structure of the DME-FE Electrolytes
Adjusting the interaction between the solvent and cosolvent is
an eﬀective method for altering the solvation structures of elec-
trolytes (Figure 2a). The main solvent with high solvating power
predominantly coordinates with Li+, increasing the solubilities
of LiPSs and aggravating Li corrosion. When a cosolvent extracts
electrons from the main solvent via solvent-cosolvent interac-
tions, the solvating power of the main solvent reduces.[15a,21] Sub-
sequently, anion coordination in the Li+ solvate increases, de-
creasing the solubilities of the LiPSs and increasing the stability
of the LMA. However, electrolytes with extremely low LiPS sol-
ubilities may hinder redox reactions at the cathode.[5b,7a] There-
fore, electrolytes with moderate solvating power may impart long
lifespans to lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.
To explore moderately solvating electrolytes applicable to Li–S
batteries, we designed DME-FE electrolytes at a molar ratio of
4.9:1.0 (DME: FE) with dual salts composed of LiFSI and LiNO3.
LiNO3 is crucial for the stable operation of Li–S batteries, while
LiFSI facilitates the formation of a stable SEI layer on lithium
metal due to the easy cleavage of S─F bonds, leading to the forma-
tion of a LiF-based SEI layer. The FE cosolvents employed in this
study are ethyl 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether (ETFE), bis(2,2,2-
triﬂuoroethyl)
ether
(BTFE),
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl
2,2,2-
triﬂuoroethyl ether (TFTFE), and 1H,1H,5H-octaﬂuoropentyl
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether (TFOFE), the structures of which
are displayed in Figure 2b. The numbers of F atoms in the
molecules follow the order ETFE (4) < BTFE (6) < TFTFE
(7) < TFOFE (12). Recent studies have revealed that F atoms
in FE cosolvents can form pseudo-H bonds with the C–H moi-
eties in DME (H–F interaction).[15a,21] Therefore, the DME-FE
interaction may vary depending on the structure of the FE.
To conﬁrm the existence of such interactions in the electrolytes
designed herein, we employed NMR analysis via 19F–1H Het-
eronuclear Overhauser Enhancement Spectroscopy (HOESY)
and identiﬁed that all the F atoms in the FE cosolvents were close
to the ─CH2 (H1) and ─OCH3 (H2) groups in DME (Figure S3,
Supporting Information). This suggests that the FE cosolvents
can inﬂuence the solvating power of DME. Comparing the 19F
NMR spectra of the FEs with and without DME revealed a down-
ﬁeld shift corresponding to the F groups on adding DME to all
the FEs (Figure S4, Supporting Information), suggesting the ex-
istence of H–F interactions. Furthermore, the H in DME (H1 and
H2) caused an upﬁeld shift in the NMR spectra in the presence
of the FE cosolvents (Figure S5, Supporting Information).[15a]
The upﬁeld shifts increased in the following order: DME-ETFE <
DME-BTFE < DME-TFTFE < DME-TFOFE (Figure 2c), indicat-
ing that the increase in DME–FE interactions followed the same
order as that of the increasing numbers of F atoms.
The DME-FE interactions suggest that the Li+ solvating power
can vary depending on the type of FE cosolvent. Using the
mixed DME-FE solvents, LiFSI- and LiNO3-containing elec-
trolytes were prepared with a molar ratio of LiFSI:LiNO3:DME:FE
= 1.9:1.0:18.5:3.8. A control electrolyte (0.75 m LiFSI and 0.4 m
LiNO3 in DME), referred to as the DME electrolyte, was prepared
for comparison. The electrolytes containing the TFOFE, TFTFE,
BTFE, and ETFE cosolvents are hereafter referred to as the DME-
TFOFE, DME-TFTFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-ETFE electrolytes,
respectively. We conducted 7Li NMR, Raman spectroscopy, and
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to understand the solva-
tion structures of the DME-FE electrolytes. The 7Li NMR analy-
sis of the electrolytes revealed that the upﬁeld shifts correspond-
ing to Li+ increased in the following order: DME < DME-ETFE
< DME-BTFE < DME-TFTFE < DME-TFOFE (Figure S6, Sup-
porting Information), indicating that the Li+ solvating powers de-
creased in the same order. As shown in Figure 2d, the Li+ solvat-
ing power, represented by the Li+ chemical shift, strongly corre-
lates with the degree of H–F interactions indicated by the chem-
ical shifts corresponding to the H in DME (H1 and H2).
Our Raman analysis for the DME-FE electrolytes indicated the
enhanced FSI−coordination in the presence of the FE cosol-
vents. The stretching of the S─N─S bond in the FSI−ions at
≈750 cm−1 was deconvoluted to correspond to solvent-separated
ion-pair structures (SSIP) (720 cm−1), contact-ion pair structures
(CIP) (734 cm−1), and aggregates (751 cm−1) (Figure S7, Support-
ing Information).[22] The addition of the FE cosolvents yielded a
blue shift irrespective of the type of FE, indicating that a stronger
signal was obtained from the CIP and aggregates than from the
DME electrolyte (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The de-
convolution of the FSI−peaks revealed that the peak ratio corre-
sponding to the CIP to the total peak increased with decreasing
Li+ solvating power (Figure 2f). Ionic conductivities, which are
sensitive to CIP and aggregate formation, of the DME-FE elec-
trolytes were investigated. The ionic conductivities decreased in
the following order: DME > DME-ETFE≈DME-BTFE > DME-
TFTFE > DME-TFOFE (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The
results align with the increasing tendency of CIP and/or aggre-
gate formation. We measured the Li+ diﬀusion coeﬃcients of the
electrolytes via 7Li diﬀusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY)-NMR;
the Li+ diﬀusion coeﬃcients were positively correlated with sol-
vating power (Figure S10, Supporting Information), indicating
an increased dissociation of the Li salt. As the solvating power
decreases, the increased formation of CIP and aggregates likely
leads to a reduction in the concentrations of free Li+ and FSI−
ions, subsequently decreasing the ionic conductivity and diﬀu-
sion coeﬃcients of Li+. These analyses collectively demonstrate
the increase in anion coordination in the Li+ solvate with the de-
creasing solvation power of the DME-FE solvents.
MD simulations were conducted to determine the Li+ solva-
tion structures in the electrolytes (Figure S11, Supporting In-
formation). The average coordination numbers of the electrolyte
components (DME, FSI−, and NO3
−) indicate that, as the solvat-
ing power of the DME-FE electrolytes decreased, the coordina-
tion of the main solvent (DME) and anions (FSI−and NO3
−) de-
creased and increased, respectively (Figure 2f; Figure S12, Sup-
porting Information). All the FE cosolvents were sparingly in-
volved in the primary solvation shell due to their sparingly sol-
vating characteristic.[23] The increased anion coordination with
decreasing solvating power can be attributed to the H–F inter-
actions with DME; with increasing H–F interactions, the Lewis
basicity of DME decreased, thereby increasing anion involve-
ment in the Li+ solvate. Recent studies have demonstrated that
solvent–solvent interactions can inﬂuence the solvating power of
electrolytes.[24] For instance, Liang et al. reported that the solvat-
ing power of a solvent with strong solvating power can be de-
creased by polarization induced by HFE solvents.[24a] Similarly,
Wu et al. found that the solvating power varies with the number
of ﬂuorine atoms of cosolvents, aligning with our ﬁndings.[24b] In

2.3. Sulfur Redox Reaction in the DME-FE Electrolytes
The FE cosolvents can alter the redox kinetics and shuttle dynam-
ics of the LiPSs. To investigate the stabilities of the LiPSs in the
DME-FE electrolytes, stoichiometric amounts of S8 and Li2S were
added to the electrolytes to form 0.2 m Li2S4. The diﬀerences in
the reddish hue among the electrolytes typically demonstrate the
impact of the FE cosolvent on the state of the LiPSs.[7a] As shown
in Figure 3a, the color of the electrolyte changes from dark red to
yellow with the decrease in Li+ solvating power. The yellow pow-
ders precipitated in the DME-TFOFE electrolyte highlight that
the formation of LiPSs was signiﬁcantly hindered owing to weak
solvating power.
We investigated the Li+ solvation structure in the presence
of LiPSs in the DME-FE electrolytes using 7Li NMR analysis
(Figure 3b; Figure S15, Supporting Information). For this, S8
and Li2S were added to the electrolytes in the amounts cor-
responding to the formation of 0.2 m Li2S4, and the super-
natants were analyzed via NMR spectroscopy. The DME elec-
trolyte showed a de-shielding shift of 0.09 ppm corresponding
to the inclusion of LiPSs, whereas the DME-ETFE, DME-BTFE,
and DME-TFTFE electrolytes exhibited relatively small shifts of
≈0.02 ppm. The de-shielding shift can be attributed to the PS
anions in the Li+ solvate replacing FSI−or DME. Such behavior
is typically observed on adding high-donor-number anions.[11,12]
Therefore, the relatively small shifts corresponding to the DME-
ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes were indicative
of lower PS solubility compared to that in the DME electrolyte.
The chemical shift corresponding to the DME-TFOFE electrolyte
remained nearly unchanged (0.0079 ppm), verifying the suppres-
sion of LiPS formation. Additionally, the maximum solubility of
LiPS was determined using UV–vis spectroscopy. After prepar-
ing electrolytes with 0.5 m Li2S4, the supernatants were diluted
to measure their absorbance, which was then compared to that
of known Li2S4 concentrations. The results indicated that the
maximum Li2S4 solubility was found to follow the order: DME
> DME-ETFE > DME-BTFE > DME-TFTFE > DME-TFOFE
which is consistent with our NMR data (Figure S16, Supporting
Information).
Raman spectroscopy conducted on the DME-TFOFE and DME
electrolytes further conﬁrms that the sparingly solvating FE co-
solvents can alter the stabilities of LiPSs. The DME-TFOFE elec-
trolyte contained a higher population of PS anions with low
charge densities (S7
−, S4
−, and S3
−) and a lower population of
PS anions with high charge densities (S4
2−and S3
2−) compared
to the DME electrolyte (Figure S17, Supporting Information).[25]
This indicates that the solvating power of the electrolyte not only
aﬀects the solubility of LiPSs but also inﬂuences the species.[7a,25]
Previous reports have demonstrated that when Li+ exists as a
strong acid due to its solvation shell within the electrolyte, strong
bases such as dianions (e.g., S4
2−) are stabilized. Conversely,
when Li+ behaves as a weak acid, weaker bases, such as monoan-
ions (e.g., S3
−), are stabilized. In our study, the increased anion
coordination around Li+ in the DME-TFOFE electrolyte is ex-
pected to reduce the acidity of Li+, thereby facilitating the sta-
bilization of weaker bases such as monoanions (S4
−, S7
−). The
contrasts clearly revealed that the DME-TFOFE electrolyte cannot
stabilize the PS species with high charge densities owing to low
solvating power. Based on the aforementioned results, we catego-
rized the DME electrolyte as strongly solvating; the DME-ETFE,

To investigate the inﬂuence of solvating power on the sulfur
cathode performance using DME-FE electrolytes, we analyzed
the discharge curves obtained from the ﬁrst cycle (0.15 C) after

and DME-TFTFE electrolytes displayed slightly higher overpo-

of the electrolytes when Li2S4 is present. In the DME electrolyte,
where Li2S4 has the highest solubility, the ionic conductivity de-
creases more signiﬁcantly, likely due to increased viscosity. As a
result, the ionic conductivities of the DME, DME-ETFE, DME-
BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes are comparable when Li2S4
is present (Figure S18, Supporting Information).

solubility, which is associated with the conversion from sulfur
to short-chain PSs.[26] The capacity of the before second plateau

sluggish LiPS redox kinetics owing to low LiPS solvating power.
The results obtained from operando galvanostatic electrochemi-

formation); the interface resistance (Rint) and charge-transfer re-

than those of the other electrolytes.[5b,29] The morphologies of
the discharged sulfur cathodes in the DME, DME-TFTFE, and
DME-TFOFE electrolytes were observed using SEM. As shown in
Figure 3i–k, interstitial pores (indicated by green lines) are clearly
visible in the SEM images of the DME and DME-TFTFE elec-

composed of sulfur, disappeared after charging or washing with

indicating that they contained undissolved LiPSs or Li2S. This
demonstrates that a weakly solvating electrolyte causes severe
LiPS aggregation, yielding a high overpotential and low discharge
capacity under lean-electrolyte conditions. Considering the bal-
ance between PS shuttle and redox kinetics, electrolytes that can
moderately solvate LiPSs are appropriate under lean-electrolyte
operations, and the use of FE cosolvents is an eﬀective means of
ﬁnely tuning the solvating power toward a moderately solvating
environment.
2.4. Reversibility of the LMA and Li–S Full Cells Containing the
DME-FE Electrolytes
We attempted to enhance the reversibility of the LMA and the
cycling stabilities of the lean-electrolyte Li–S cells containing the
DME-FE electrolytes. The reversibility of the LMA was assessed
by quantifying the Coulombic eﬃciency of an Li/Ni asymmet-
ric cell based on the Aurbach method (Figure 4a). The Coulom-
bic eﬃciencies of the DME-TFOFE, DME-TFTFE, DME-BTFE,
DME-ETFE, and DME electrolytes were measured to be 97.18,
96.81, 96.19, 95.31, and 95.42%, respectively. As the degree of
ﬂuorination in the FE increased, the compatibility with Li metal
also improved, as evidenced by comparing the Coulombic eﬃ-
ciencies of the Li/Ni asymmetric cell operated at 0.2 mA cm−2
with a capacity of 0.2 mAh cm−2 using 15 μL of each electrolyte.
The average Coulombic eﬃciency over 100 cycles was conﬁrmed
to follow the order DME-TFOFE > DME-TFTFE > DME-BTFE
> DME-ETFE > DME (Figure S26, Supporting Information).
This indicates that augmenting anion coordination to Li+ im-
proves the stability of the LMA. This ﬁnding is in accordance
with those reported in previous studies on improved Li compati-
bility using a combination of an LiFSI salt and a weakly solvating
solvent.[15]
To investigate the correlation between LMA stability and lifes-
pan of the Li–S cell under lean-electrolyte conditions, cycling sta-
bility tests were conducted on the electrolytes at an E/S ratio of 5
and a 0.15 C rate. The cycle numbers of the DME-TFTFE, DME-
BTFE, DME, DME-ETFE, and DME-TFOFE electrolytes at a ca-
pacity retention of 70% were 154, 131, 36, 32, and 31 cycles, re-
spectively (Figure 4b). Despite having the highest LMA reversibil-
ity, the DME-TFOFE electrolyte exhibited poor cycling stability,
indicating that the reversibility of the sulfur cathode determines
the cycling stability when the solvating power of electrolyte is
weak. Except in the case of the DME-TFOFE electrolyte, cell lifes-
pan is strongly correlated with LMA reversibility, conﬁrming that
LMA reversibility is a decisive factor for achieving high cycling
stability in a lean-electrolyte Li–S battery. To achieve optimal full-
cell cycling stability, sulfur redox kinetics and LMA reversibility
must be balanced, with moderately solvating electrolytes being
particularly suitable for achieving this. The cycle performance
(154 cycles) of the DME-TFTFE electrolyte was better than those
reported in previous studies (Figure 4c), highlighting the eﬃcacy
of moderately solvating electrolyte design. Another moderately
solvating electrolyte, the DME-BTFE electrolyte, exhibited a no-
tably high cycle performance (131 cycles). Additionally, in the
DME electrolyte, a slight increase in discharge capacity was ob-
served before degradation set in, likely due to LiPS dissolvoing
out of the carbon structure and allowing reactions to occur on the
outer carbon surface. In contrast, in moderately solvating elec-
trolytes, reactions were likely limited to the inner carbon surface,
preventing such capacity increases (Figure S27, Supporting In-
formation).

2.5. Chemical Structures of the SEIs in the DME-FE Electrolytes
The modulation of the Li+ solvation structure can change the
composition of the SEI because the population and reactivity of
the electrolyte components at the LMA surface vary depending on
the solvation structure.[31] By calculating the LUMO levels of each
component within the solvation structure, we can predict the de-
composed products on the Li metal surface. A higher LUMO level
makes electron acceptance more diﬃcult, suppressing reduction,
while a lower LUMO level makes electron acceptance easier, pro-
moting reduction. The comparison between the LUMO levels of
the anions and solvents determined via density functional theory
calculations (Figure 5a) reveals that these LUMO levels decreased
with the increase in coordination with Li+. This behavior origi-
nates from the electron transfer to Li+, increasing the ease with
which salt or solvent molecules are reduced.[32] Based on this con-
sideration, in the DME electrolyte, the preferential participation
of DME in the Li+ solvate will promote the cathodic decompo-
sition of DME and enrich the organic components in the SEI.
In contrast, in the moderately solvating electrolytes, the involve-
ment of the anions in the Li+ solvate may facilitate the cathodic
decomposition of the anions, forming an inorganic-rich SEI.
To verify these postulations, the chemical compositions of
the SEIs were analyzed via XPS after 36 cycles of operation
of the Li–S batteries containing the DME, DME-ETFE, DME-
BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes. The addition of the FE co-
solvents to the DME electrolyte notably reduced the carbon con-
tent by over 25% within the SEI, and the DME-TFTFE elec-
trolyte exhibited the lowest carbon content (Figure 5b). This in-
dicates substantial suppression of DME decomposition, as an-
ticipated. The DME-FE electrolytes exhibited higher Li, N, and
F contents than the DME electrolyte, indicating higher contents
of inorganic SEI components in the former electrolytes. The
abnormally high F content in the SEI in the DME-ETFE elec-
trolyte can be explained by the low cathodic stability of ETFE
owing to the localized electron distribution in this asymmetric
structure.[33]
As the NO3
−anion coordinated to Li+ is particularly suscepti-
ble to decomposition owing to the lowest LUMO level, we exam-
ined the N 1s spectra of the SEIs in the DME, DME-ETFE, DME-
BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes (Figure 5c–f, respectively).
The spectra exhibited R–NO2 (404.6 eV) and Li3N (398.2 eV)
peaks, which are formed by the partial and full reduction of NO3
−,
respectively.[12] The relative intensity corresponding to Li3N was
higher and that corresponding to R–NO2 and lower for the mod-
erately solvating electrolytes than those for the DME electrolyte,
indicating that the cathodic reaction of NO3
−to Li3N was pro-
moted by the preferred involvement of NO3
−present in the Li+
solvate.
Moreover, the S 2p spectra of the SEIs (Figure S28, Support-
ing Information) displayed that the intensities corresponding to
the LiPSs and Li2S in the DME-FE electrolytes were signiﬁcantly
lower than those in the DME electrolyte, evidencing the sup-
pressed Li corrosion owing to the LiPSs in the moderately solvat-
ing electrolytes. The signals corresponding to SO4
2−(169.2 eV)
and SO3
2−(167.2 eV),[1b] indicative of the cathodic decomposition
of FSI−, were prominent in the spectra obtained from the DME-
ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes due to the high
degrees of FSI−involvement in the Li+ solvate. Overall, the com-
positional analysis validated the formation of inorganic-enriched
SEIs and the mitigation of Li corrosion owing to the moderately
solvating electrolytes.

2.6. Post-Mortem Analysis of the LMAs After Cycling
To investigate the improvement in the lifespans of the Li–S
batteries owing to the presence of the FE cosolvent-containing
electrolytes, post-mortem analysis was conducted on the Li–S
cells containing the DME, DME-ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-
TFTFE electrolytes after 36 cycles of operation. The surface
and cross-sectional images of the cycled Li electrodes are pro-
vided in Figure 6a–h. The DME-BTFE and DME-TFTFE elec-
trolytes exhibited more uniform Li-surface and cross-sectional
morphologies than the DME and DME-ETFE electrolytes, in
good agreement with the distinguished cycling stabilities of
these electrolytes (Figure 4b). Among the electrolytes tested, the
DME-TFTFE electrolyte exhibited the most uniform and densely
packed Li-deposit morphology. Because electrolyte decomposi-
tion is aggravated owing to an inhomogeneous Li-deposit mor-
phology, the uniform Li morphology driven by the DME-BTFE
and DME-TFTFE electrolytes is beneﬁcial for achieving high re-
versibility of the LMA.
The reversibility of the LMA under Li–S cell operation was as-
sessed by determining the remaining capacity of the LMA after
36 cycles. This was achieved by constructing Li-SUS cells using
the cycled LMAs and measuring the stripping capacities of these

BTFE (5.4 mAh cm−2) < DME-TFTFE (7.2 mAh cm−2) (Figure 6i).

mity in Li morphology.

2.7. Performances of Li–S Pouch Cells Containing the Moderately
Solvating DME-FE Electrolyte
The practical applicability of our electrolyte design was assessed
by fabricating two pouch-type Li–S cells, that is, in the bi-cell for-
mat consisting of one sheet of double-side-coated cathode and
two sheets of anodes with an E/S ratio of 3.5 and in a 0.5-Ah-
class stack-cell format (10 single cells) with an E/S ratio of 2.0.
The bi-cell was cycled at 0.1-C discharge/charge and rates, and
0.05-C discharge/charge rates were applied to the stack cell. The
energy densities of the bi-cell and stack cell were 290 and 405 Wh
kg−1, respectively. The energy density was calculated based on
used active materials. If we estimate the weight fraction of pack-
age is ≈10% in the previous studies, the energy density of stack
cell would be 368 Wh kg−1. The weight fractions of the cell com-
ponents in the bi-cell and stack cell are displayed in Figure 7a,b,
respectively.
The bi-cell and stack cell yielded stable voltage curves (Figure
S33, Supporting Information). The stack cell capacity was nearly
ﬁvefold larger than that of the bi-cell in proportion to the stack cell
number, indicating successful up-scaling despite the decrease in
the E/S ratio (from 3.5 to 2.0). The bi-cell containing the DME-
TFTFE electrolyte showed 70% capacity retention at the 135th
cycle, 25% longer than that of the DME electrolyte (Figure 7c).
The DME-TFTFE electrolyte in the stack cell with a challenging
E/S ratio of 2.0 exhibited stable cycling for 80 cycles, which is over
sixfold longer than that of the DME electrolyte (Figure 7d). This
highlights the eﬀectiveness of controlling the solvent structure
to improve the lifespan performances of Li–S pouch cells under
lean-electrolyte conditions.
3. Conclusion
In this study, FE solvents with diﬀerent degrees of ﬂuorination
were used as cosolvents in Li–S battery electrolytes. By tuning
the interaction between DME and FE, we varied the Li+ solvate
structures, LiPS solubilities, and chemical compositions of the
SEIs in lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries. As the ﬂuorination degree
of the FE cosolvent increased, the H–F interactions with DME
decreased, shifting the environment from strongly solvating to
weakly solvating. Among the cosolvents tested, the DME-TFTFE
electrolyte, a moderately solvating electrolyte, exhibited appro-
priate LiPS solubility and an anion-derived SEI structure, result-
ing in a lifespan of over 150 cycles at an E/S ratio of 5. Notably,
the DME-TFTFE electrolyte eﬀectively mitigated electrolyte de-
pletion, the root cause of cell failure, thereby enabling the sta-
ble operation of a pouch-type stack cell with a high cell-level en-
ergy density of 405 Wh kg−1 and stable cycling for 80 cycles at a

4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation and Characterization:
LiFSI, ETFE, BTFE,
TFTFE, and TFOFE were purchased from TCI chemicals. LiTFSI was pur-
chased from 3M. DME (anhydrous, 99.5%) and DOL (anhydrous, 99.5%)
were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. All solvents were used after remov-
ing trace amounts of water using molecular sieves (4 Å). The electrolytes
containing the ﬂuorinated cosolvents were prepared using a molar ratio of
LiFSI:LiNO3:DME:cosolvent = 1.9:1.0:18.5:3.8, resulting in a ﬂuorinated
solvent volume fraction of ≈20% in the DME-FE electrolyte. The control
electrolyte (DME electrolyte) was prepared by dissolving 0.75 m LiFSI and
0.4 m LiNO3 in DME.
NMR analysis, except 19F-1H HOESY NMR (1H NMR, 7Li NMR, and 19F
NMR), was conducted using Bruker AVANCE III HD (9.4T). 19F-1H HOESY
NMR was conducted using Bryker AVANCE NEO (9.4T). The chemical
shifts were referenced to dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-d6 at 2.5 ppm. For
chemical shift analysis using 7Li NMR, a coaxial NMR tube (Wilmad-
Labglass) with an inner tube OD of 3.3 mm and ID of 2.34 mm, as well as
an outer tube OD of 4.97 mm, was used to exclude the eﬀects of DMSO-
d6, which was placed in an external coaxial insert. For conducting QNMR
analysis, the components of the coin cells were washed with 1.5 mL of
tetrahydrofuran to form an internal concentration reference for 1H NMR,
and subsequently, 20 μL of ﬂuorobenzene was added as an internal con-
centration reference for 19F NMR.[34] The same amount of electrolyte for
an E/S ratio of 5 (29.4 μL of electrolyte) was diluted to 1.5 mL of tetrahy-
drofuran, and subsequently, 20 μL of ﬂuorobenzene was added to measure
the component amount before operation.
Raman analysis was conducted using a dispersive Raman spectrome-
ter (ARAMIS, JY Horiba) with a 514-nm wavelength laser. The ionic con-
ductivities of the electrolytes were measured using a conductivity meter
(LAQUA twin, Horiba Scientiﬁc). The diﬀusivities of Li+ in the electrolytes
were examined via 7Li DOSY-NMR analysis.
Electrochemical Measurements:
First, 2032-type coin cells were assem-
bled in an Ar-ﬁlled glove box (H2O < 0.1 ppm) for electrochemical analy-
sis. Next, a galvanostatic discharge and charge test was conducted using
a WBC300L (Wonatech) automatic battery cycler. For the Li–Ni half-cell
test using the Aurbach method, Φ12-mm Li foil (40 μm, China Lithium),
Φ16-mm Ni foil (Welcos), and a Celgard 2400 separator were used. The
amount of injected electrolyte was 30 μL. First, 1 mAh cm−2 of Li deposit
was formed on the Ni foil at 1 mA cm−2, and subsequently, ten cycles of
repetitive strip and plate were conducted at the same current density and
a 0.2 mAh cm−2 capacity.[35] In the last cycle, the cell was charged up to
1 V to strip the remaining Li. In the Li–S cell, a high S-loaded electrode (3.8
mgs cm−2) obtained from LG Energy Solution Ltd. was used, a Φ14-mm
sulfur cathode was used as the working cathode, and Φ16-mm Li metal
(40 μm, China Lithium) was used as the counter electrode. Furthermore,
29.4 μL of electrolyte was injected to adjust the E/S ratio to 5 μL mgs−1. The
cells were precycled for two cycles at 0.05 C, and subsequent cycles used
0.1 C. After precycling, these cells were cycled at 0.15 C. Operando XRD
was conducted using an in-situ battery cell X-ray diﬀractometer, R-AXIS IV
(Rigaku) with a transmission Cu Ka X-ray source. In-situ XRD patterns were
obtained while the cells were operated at 0.1 C. All the cells were operated
in the voltage range of 1.8–2.7 V (vs Li/Li+).
Computer Simulation:
All computer simulations were conducted in
Materials Studio (Biovia, 2023). The HOMO and LUMO were calculated
using the DMOL3 modules after geometric optimization.[31] For MD sim-
ulations, all molecules were geometrically optimized using the generalized
gradient approximation method, Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and
double numerical plus polarization basis set before constructing amor-
phous cells.[29] The amorphous cells were composed of 385 DME and 113
ﬂuorinated solvent molecules, as well as 69 Li+, 38 FSI−, and 31 NO3−
ions. COMPASS III force ﬁeld was assigned to all molecules. The con-
structed amorphous cells were equilibrated at 1 atm and 298 K under the
NPT condition for 1 ns and then under the NVT condition for 1 ps. Coordi-
nation behaviors of all the Li+ ions in the amorphous cells were examined.
Post-Mortem Analysis:
The morphologies of the sulfur cathode and Li
anode were analyzed using ﬁeld-emission SEM (Magellan 400, FEI com-
pany). The cross-section of the Li metal was observed using a focused ion
beam SEM (Helios Nanolab 450 ETFE, FEI Company). The components
of the SEI were examined using XPS (K-alpha, Thermo VG Scientiﬁc). All
post-mortem analyses were conducted after 36 cycles of operation includ-
ing pre-cycles of the Li–S cell, and subsequently, the components to be
analyzed were washed with 3 mL of DME to remove residual electrolytes.
Assembly of the Li–S Pouch Cell:
The double-side-coated sulfur cathode
on Al foil with 2.5 mgs cm−2 provided by LG Energy Solution Ltd. was
used for pouch-cell assembly. To assemble the bi-cell-type pouch cell, 40-
μm-thick Li foil was cut into 4 × 6 cm2 pieces and subsequently attached
to a Cu current collector of the same size. The cathode was cut into 3 ×
5 cm2 pieces, and a polypropylene separator (Celgard 2400) was cut to
dimensions of 3.5 × 5.5 cm2. Then, 336 μL of electrolyte was injected to
adjust the E/S ratio to 3.5. The cells were compressed using a pressure
range of 1.5–2.0 bar.
A 0.5-Ah stack cell was assembled in a dry room (dew point < −40 °C).
The cathode and anode were cut to dimensions of 5 × 3.3 cm2 and 5.2
× 3.5 cm2, respectively. An Ni tab was directly attached to the LMA. The
volume of electrolyte corresponding to an E/S ratio of 2 was 0.825 mL.
The energy densities of the pouch cells were calculated based on the total
mass of the stack cell (Table S2, Supporting Information). The pouch cells
with an E/S ratio of 3.5 were precycled at 0.025 C for two cycles and 0.05
C for two additional cycles and subsequently cycled at 0.1 C. The pouch
cells with an E/S ratio of 2 were cycled at 0.0125 C for two cycles, 0.025 C
for one cycle, and subsequently cycled at 0.05 C.
