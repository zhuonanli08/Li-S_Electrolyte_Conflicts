ABSTRACT
Lithium sulfide (Li2S) is pivotal for high-energy-density lithium–sulfur (Li─S) batteries due to its high theoretical capacity,
abundant sulfur resources, and compatibility with anode-free architectures. However, its application is hindered by its intrinsically
insulating nature and sluggish redox kinetics. Furthermore, traditional 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-dimethoxyethane electrolytes cannot
withstand high voltages and pose safety hazards due to low flash points. Herein, we propose a synergistic strategy by introducing
diisopropyl dithiocarbonate disulfide (DIP) as a multifunctional redox mediator into a high-flash-point, high-voltage-tolerant
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether electrolyte system. DIP directly converts Li2S to lithium polysulfides, decreasing the activation
voltage of the first charge to 2.48 from 3.18 V. Simultaneously, DIP facilitates the formation of an organosulfur-rich solid electrolyte
interface on the lithium surface, effectively suppressing lithium dendrite formation and growth. Crucially, this system enables
stable cycling in anode-free Cu||Li2S batteries for 160 cycles at 0.3 mAh cm−2. Standard Li||Li2S cells also demonstrate superior
durability, achieving an extremely low per-cycle decay rate of 0.037% at 1C. Moreover, this strategy holds promise for other metal-
sulfur systems, such as Na─S, K─S, Ca─S, Mg─S, and Zn─S batteries, providing a feasible path for safe, next-generation energy
storage.

Introduction
The lithium-sulfur (Li─S) battery is widely regarded as a pivotal
technology for next-generation energy storage, attributable to its
superior theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) abundant
sulfur reserves and environmental friendliness compared to
conventional systems [1–5]. Among various cathode materials
employed for Li─S systems, lithium sulfide (Li2S) exhibits distinct
superiority compared to elemental sulfur (S8): as the active sulfur
species with the lowest density, it eliminates the need for pre-
reserved void space for volume expansion, enabling high-loading
designs to improve energy density [6, 7]. More critically, this
compatibility enables its coupling with anode materials suitable
for anode-free architectures, such as silicon and graphite. By
utilizing in situ lithium deposition during the initial charge, this
strategy not only achieves additional improvements in energy

density but also reduces the safety risks associated with lithium
dendrites [8–10]. These advantages position Li2S as a highly
promising cathode material for practical applications.
Despite these merits, the practical utilization of Li2S cathodes
still encounters significant hurdles. The intrinsic electronic and
ionic insulation of Li2S (electronic conductivity is approximately
10−13 S cm−1) leads to extremely sluggish redox kinetics [11].
Particularly during the initial charge stage, delithiation forms a
high-impedance solid Li2-xS intermediate phase on the particle
surface, requiring the battery to overcome a huge high activation
voltage (often approaching ∼4 V) to initiate the conversion
to soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) [12]. This enormous
overpotential not only reduces energy efficiency but also, if
insufficient, triggers severe initial polarization and “dead sulfur”
residue, greatly limiting active material utilization [13, 14]. To
reduce the activation barrier, researchers have attempted physical
strategies such as nanosizing, amorphization, carbon coating, and
introducing electrocatalysts, but these methods sacrifice energy
density or exacerbating polysulfide shuttling [15–24].
In contrast, introducing redox mediators (RMs) as “electron
shuttles” to construct additional charge transport pathways has
proven an efficient strategy for the chemical activation of Li2S
[25]. Classic RMs such as metallocenes [26], lithium iodide
[27, 28], and trithiocyanuric acid trilithium salt [29] have demon-
strated positive effects in reducing the Li2S activation energy,
providing effective approaches for mediating Li2S conversion.
However, the redox potentials of these molecules are signifi-
cantly higher than the 2.15 V (vs. Li+/Li) standard potential
for the Li2S/Li2S2 redox pair, and their reaction kinetics do not
necessarily increase with operating potential; instead, kinetic
decay may occur due to potential deviation, leading to large
initial charge overpotentials in batteries [11, 30]. Furthermore,
the high voltage required for Li2S activation exceeds the oxidative
stability window of traditional electrolytes 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME), leading to irreversible electrolyte
decomposition, which not only causes rapid capacity fade but
also fails to meet the core requirement for intrinsic safety in
storage devices due to their low flash point (≤15◦C) characteristics
[31]. Notably, tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME)
is an ideal solvent candidate. TEGDME possesses an oxidative
stability window of up to 4.5 V and a high flash point of 140◦C,
perfectly adapting to the high voltage activation requirements
of Li2S and ensuring safety [32, 33]. Furthermore, its compati-
bility with Li2S conversion has been widely validated, as it can
reduce the nucleation barrier of Li2S, promotes homogeneous
deposition of Li2S, and supports for efficient Li2S redox reac-
tions [34–36]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop an
adaptable electrolyte system that can efficiently activate Li2S
at low voltage while possessing high voltage tolerance and
safety.
To address these challenges, this study proposes a synergistic opti-
mization strategy: selecting diisopropyl dithiocarbonate disulfide
(DIP) as a multifunctional redox mediator and introducing it
into a TEGDME-based electrolyte system. The key innovation
of this work lies in utilizing the functional complementarity of
DIP and TEGDME to achieve multi-dimensional performance
improvement: on the cathode side, leveraging its distinctive
disulfide bond structure, DIP can effectively chemically oxidize
Li2S, significantly lowering the activation overpotential while
adsorbing polysulfides and catalyzing their conversion kinetics;
on the anode side, DIP participates in reactions, inducing the
formation of a stable organic sulfur-rich solid electrolyte interface
(SEI) film, thereby promoting homogeneous Li plating and
restraining the growth of lithium dendrites. This synergistic
system demonstrates exceptional capability in highly challenging
anode-free battery configurations, enabling stable cycling for over
160 cycles, which verifies the universality and reliability of the
strategy. Furthermore, Li||Li2S batteries constructed using this
system exhibit superior electrochemical performance: achieving
88.18% of the initial capacity following 250 cycles at 0.5 C, along
with a per-cycle decay rate of 0.037% across 500 cycles at 1 C.
This study provides a feasible technical path for Li2S-based Li─S
batteries and lays an important foundation for promoting the
commercialization of such batteries.

Results and Discussion
To determine the optimal concentration of DIP, we first inves-
tigated the cycling performance of Li2S batteries at different
concentrations (0.02, 0.05, and 0.08 m) (results shown in Figure
S1). The results indicated that 0.05 M is the optimal addition
amount. Therefore, subsequent experiments (unless otherwise
specified) used 0.05 m as the DIP concentration. To clarify the
activation mechanism of DIP as a RM for Li2S cathodes, Figure 1a
clearly shows the pathway through which DIP promotes Li2S
oxidation: during the charging process, DIP first chemically
oxidizes Li2S to LiPSs, getting reduced to DIP−; subsequently,
DIP−is electrochemically oxidized around 2.4 V to generate
DIP⋅(Step A), and DIP⋅further diffuses to the current collector
surface and is oxidized to DIP+ at 2.7 V (Step B), while the
discharge process is the reverse of this reaction sequence. This
mechanism allows Li2S to bypass the difficult step of forming
the high-impedance Li2-xS intermediate and directly convert into
soluble LiPSs [25, 37]. As shown in Figure 1a, the actual reaction
path corresponding to the red line is significantly shortened.
The introduction of DIP significantly reduces this shaded area
(energy barrier, its area corresponds to specific energy, obtained
by integrating specific capacity and voltage), lowering energy
dissipation throughout the reaction process. This means that
the oxidation of Li2S no longer needs to consume extra energy
to overcome the high barrier, thus achieving higher-efficiency
conversion of Li2S.
To elucidate the activation mechanism of DIP as a RM for Li2S
cathodes, we assembled Li||Li2S cells and tested their first-cycle
charge–discharge curves (Figure 1b). At a current density of
0.05 C during the first charge cycle, the Li2S battery without DIP
showed a high charge onset potential of up to 3.18 V; while after
introducing DIP, this potential was significantly suppressed to
about 2.48 V. Notably, the addition of DIP not only effectively
lowered the charge overpotential but also increased the initial
charge capacity from 921 mAh g−1 of the pristine Li2S cathode to
1159 mAh g−1, approaching the theoretical specific capacity of
Li2S. This result visually confirms that DIP successfully low-
ers the activation barrier for Li2S oxidation and substantially
improves the electrochemical utilization of Li2S. To eliminate the
possibility that DIP itself contributes to the battery capacity, we
assembled Li||carbon paper (CP) cells without Li2S for testing

After establishing the macroscopic effect of DIP on electrode
reactions, we used density functional theory (DFT) calculations
to resolve the molecular-level interaction mechanism of DIP
with sulfur species. Independent gradient model (IGM) analysis
based on Hirshfeld partitioning (Figure 2a) shows: the interaction
region between Li2S6 and DIP exhibits green characteristics,
corresponding to significant weak interaction regions, indicating
that the interaction strength between the two is dominated by van
der Waals forces, accompanied by dipole-dipole interactions. This
interaction attribute grants excellent reversibility the Li─O coor-
dination established between the DIP oxygen site and the lithium
ion of Li2S6—it can achieve dynamic bond formation and dissoci-
ation without hindering subsequent electrochemical conversion
due to excessively strong interaction [43]. The quantitative results
of the ρ-δg scatter plot (Figure 2b) further support this conclusion:
the two peak values corresponding to the green contours are
−0.02 and 0.015 a.u., respectively, among which the negative
peak value representing adsorption action is significantly higher,
which not only reflects the strong adsorption ability of DIP for
Li2S6 but also directly confirms that the adsorption of LiPSs
by DIP is achieved through lithium bonds [44]. This mode of
action centered on lithium bonds reinforces the anchoring effect
of DIP on LiPSs while retaining the electrochemical activity of
LiPSs, providing a molecular-level mechanistic guarantee for the
efficient and reversible conversion of LiPSs in the sulfur electrode.
ELF analysis (Figure 2c) further reveals the essence of the
interaction between DIP and Li2S6. The red/orange regions (ELF
≈0.8–1.0) represent highly localized electrons, corresponding to
the oxygen lone pairs of the DIP molecule and the electronic
density around sulfur in Li2S6, respectively. Notably, a yellow-
green transition region (ELF ≈0.5–0.8) appears between the
oxygen atom of DIP and the lithium ion of Li2S6, indicating
partial electron delocalization. This intuitively reflects that the
electron-rich oxygen atom in DIP provides electron density to the
electron-deficient lithium ion, forming a Li─O coordination bond
[45].
Figure 2e; Figure S4 presents the optimal geometric configu-
rations of the interaction between DIP and different lithium
polysulfides (Li2Sn, n = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) through DFT calculations
(Figure S4). From the configurational characteristics, it is clear
that polysulfides prefer to anchor at oxygen functional group
sites with higher electron density in the DIP molecule. This
selective action stems from the strong electrostatic adsorption
and coordination interaction between DIP and polysulfides,
where their electronic structure matching drives site-specific
binding [46]. The binding energy data in Figure 2d further
quantifies this interaction strength: as the sulfur chain length
in polysulfides increases, the binding energy of DIP with poly-
sulfides increases gradually (reaching −3.86 eV for Li2S8). This
high binding energy not only enhances the ability of DIP to
capture long-chain polysulfides and efficiently inhibit the shuttle
effect but also improves structural stability of the complex,
providing molecular-level assurance for improving battery cycle
stability [47, 48].
The regulation of charge transfer kinetics by DIP is elu-
cidated through frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis
(Figure 2f). During discharge, the highest occupied molecular
orbital (HOMO) of DIP serves as the source for rapid electron
transition into the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)
of Li2Sn, promoting reduction reaction of polysulfides; during
charging, electrons migrate from the HOMO of Li2S to the LUMO
of DIP, effectively lowering the electron transfer energy barrier for
Li2S oxidation. Further comparison of energy gaps reveals that
the bandgaps of intermediates formed by DIP and sulfur species
(DIP-SxLi, x = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) are significantly narrower than those
of the corresponding Li2Sx species—a narrower bandgap implies
smaller electron transition resistance, meaning higher conduc-
tivity and faster charge transfer rates for DIP-SxLi intermediates
[2, 14]. This electronic structure characteristic fundamentally
explains why DIP can significantly lower the energy barrier for
sulfur redox processes, leading to significantly enhanced reaction
kinetics.
To deeply investigate the specific catalytic function of DIP in
accelerating polysulfide conversion kinetics, we constructed and
tested Li2S6 symmetrical cells (Figure 3a). The CV results show
that compared to pure Li2S6 electrolyte, the symmetrical cells
with added DIP exhibit significantly improved current response,
indicating that DIP effectively improves the conversion kinetics of
liquid-liquid phase sulfur species. This improvement stems from
the specific interaction between DIP and LiPSs, which has been
confirmed in 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis. For
the pristine Li2S6 electrolyte, the Li resonance signal appears at
2.07 ppm, while after adding DIP, the signal shifts downfield to
2.46 ppm (Figure 3b). This is because the oxygen atoms in DIP
molecules coordinate with Li+, altering the chemical environ-
ment of lithium, thereby promoting intermolecular binding and
charge transfer [29, 14]. UV–vis absorption spectrum adsorption
experiments (Figure 3c) further support this conclusion: the Li2S6
solution with added DIP becomes lighter in color after standing
for 4 h, and the characteristic absorption peak intensity at 350 nm
decreases significantly, indicating that DIP effectively captures
polysulfides through strong adsorption [49, 50].

In situ EIS further reveals changes in interfacial behavior
(Figure 3h,i). The test procedure involved charging the battery
from open circuit voltage (OCV) to 3.4 V, discharging to 1.8 V,
and recharging to 2.8 V. Figure 3h corresponds to the system
without DIP, and Figure 3i corresponds to the system with
DIP. The high-frequency region corresponds to the interfacial
charge transfer (kinetic control), while the medium-low fre-
quency region is related to the mass transfer of Li+ and sulfur
species (mass transfer control). Comparative analysis shows that
without DIP, impedance values at various potentials are at high
levels; while after adding DIP, both charge transfer impedance
and mass transfer impedance across the entire potential range
are significantly reduced. These findings demonstrate that the
incorporation of DIP simultaneously enhances interfacial charge
transfer and facilitates the diffusion of both Li+ ions and sulfur-
based species. Consequently, this validates the capability of
DIP to accelerate the kinetic processes of the sulfur redox
reaction [57].
The catalytic role of DIP also profoundly affects the deposition
behavior, interfacial processes, and thermodynamic reaction path
involving Li2S. To verify its catalytic impact of DIP on sulfur
reduction/oxidation processes, we conducted potentiostatic Li2S
nucleation and dissolution tests. Upon potentiostatic discharge
at 2.05 V, the system without DIP (Figure 4a) shows a Li2S
deposition capacity of 73.89 mAh g−1, with its response time
reaching 1139 s; while after introducing DIP (Figure 4b), the
deposition capacity significantly increased to 106.08 mAh g−1,
and the response time shortened to 862 s, indicating that DIP
effectively promoted the growth kinetics and nucleation of Li2S.
Through the nucleation mode analysis using the Scharifker-Hills
model, we considered four typical nucleation mechanisms: 3D
instantaneous (3DI) nucleation,3D progressive (3DP), 2D instan-
taneous (2DI) nucleation and 2D progressive (2DP) (Figure 4e;
Figure S7). The system without DIP follows a nucleation mode
between 2DI and 2DP (Figure 4c), which is limited by surface
coverage and easily leads to electrode passivation. In contrast,
after introducing DIP, the nucleation mode shifts to a type
between 3DP and 3DI (Figure 4d). This mode is controlled by
ion diffusion and can achieve higher capacity deposition [36].
Combining with the insets in Figure 4c,d, it becomes evident that
DIP steers the deposition morphology of Li2S from “2D planar
coverage” prone to passivation towards “3D spherical growth”
which is more conducive to capacity release [58]. To quantify
this kinetic difference, we calculated nucleation and growth rates

energies values for various intermediate species in the system

for its improvement of battery reaction kinetics.
In this work, all cathodes employ Li2S as active material. Conse-
quently, electrochemical performance indicators such as specific
capacity of the battery, are normalized to the Li2S mass loading
(1 C = 1166 mAh g−1). We evaluated the impact of DIP on elec-
trochemical behavior through different battery configurations.
Figure 5a depicts the 0.5 C cycling test. The DIP-based battery
starts with 808.2 mAh g−1 and preserves 712.7 mAh g−1 over 250
cycles (88.18% retention), showcasing superior durability; while
the control battery without DIP has a retention rate of only 52.42%,
intuitively reflecting the significant improvement in active sulfur
utilization by DIP. Rate performance tests (Figure 5b) show that
when the current is gradually ramped up, the discharge capacities
of the control battery drop to 604, 506, 422, 375, and 307 mAh g−1
from 0.2 C to 3 C, respectively; while the battery with DIP achieves
specific capacities of 896, 757, 643, 550, and 478 mAh g−1 under
identical conditions, with capacity being restored to 683 mAh g−1
upon reverting to 0.5 C, demonstrating excellent rate response
capability. Corresponding charge–discharge curves (Figure 5c,d)
and polarization voltage statistics (Figure 5e) indicate that as
current density increases, the growth of polarization in DIP
batteries is far smaller than in control batteries, and clear charge–
discharge plateaus are maintained even at high rates. This again
confirms the dual functions of DIP as a RM: it not only accelerates
reaction kinetics but also inhibits polysulfide shuttling through
adsorption.
After verifying the performance improvement of DIP in conven-
tional batteries, we further tested its performance in long-cycle,
high-sulfur-loading, and anode-free systems to align with prac-
tical application requirements. Figure 5f shows the long-cycle
test at 1 C. The DIP-based cell demonstrates excellent durability,
preserving 91.82% capacity at the 300th cycle and delivering
517.2 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles, corresponding to 0.037% decay
per cycle. Conversely, the control battery drops to 187 mAh g−1
following 200 cycles, exhibiting a much higher decay of 0.299%
per cycle—the difference benefits from the optimized LiPSs
conversion path by DIP, endowing the battery with more stable
long-cycle performance. To further approach practical applica-
tion scenarios, we verified the performance of DIP additive in
pouch cell systems (Figure 5g). Under severe operating conditions
(8 mg cm−2 sulfur loading, 2038 mg total sulfur loading, and
4.9 µL mg−1 E/S ratio), the pouch cell exhibits excellent initial
performance: a specific capacity of 1176 mAh g−1 (0.2 C) and a
total discharge capacity of 2.09 Ah (0.05 C) (Figure S9); it can
still charge and discharge normally and maintain high specific
capacity after 12 cycles, proving the reliability and cycling stability
of DIP in pouch cells. In the case where excess lithium is not
used in the battery, anode-free full batteries are effective tools for
studying anode stability and cycling performance [63, 64]. A low
capacity of 89.5 mAh g−1 was observed for the control Cu||Li2S
battery (retention rate 14.07%) after 160 cycles under 0.2 C; in
contrast, the battery with DIP shows significantly slowed capacity
fade, still retaining 449.4 mAh g−1 (retention rate 68.53%) after
160 cycles, demonstrating superior lithium deposition/stripping
efficiency and interfacial stability (Figure 5h). The performance
comparison plot in Figure 5i, together with the detailed data
summarized in Table S2, further highlights the comprehensive
advantages of this work in balancing high cycle number and high
capacity retention, thereby highlighting the significant promise
of this strategy for advancing practical Li2S battery technologies
[40, 64–71].
Given the excellent performance of anode-free batteries, we
further investigated the protective role of DIP on lithium
metal anodes. We examined its regulation of lithium deposi-
tion/stripping behavior through Li||Li symmetric cell testing
(Figure 6a–d). The voltage curve of the control battery fluc-
tuates dramatically, with overpotential exceeding 80 mV, and
deteriorates continuously as cycling progresses, indicating severe
SEI film rupture and lithium dendrite growth. In contrast, the
battery with DIP maintains a stable voltage of about 40 mV
with no obvious fluctuations even after 800 h of cycling, fully
demonstrating the excellent cycling stability DIP confers to the
battery [72]. We disassembled cycled Li||Li symmetric cells and
observed lithium deposition morphology via SEM (Figure 6e): in
the symmetric cell with DIP, both the front (e1) and side (e3)
of the lithium anode show smooth deposition morphology with
no obvious dendrite formation, and the SEI film is compact and
homogeneous; while the lithium anode surface of the control
battery (e2, e4) is not only rough but also presents large cracks—
this is because its SEI layer is unstable, and the interface
continuously undergoes decomposition and regeneration during
cycling, ultimately triggering volume expansion of lithium metal
and irregular dendrite growth. The above results indicate that
DIP can induce a stable interfacial film formation, efficiently
suppressing dendritic growth.

Conclusion
This study successfully constructed a synergistic electrolyte sys-
tem based on the multifunctional redox mediator diisopropyl
dithiocarbonate disulfide (DIP) and the high-voltage-tolerant,
high-flash-point tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME)
solvent, addressing key challenges faced by Li2S cathodes, includ-
ing high activation barriers, sluggish kinetics, and insufficient
safety of traditional ether electrolytes. The core advantage of
this strategy is that DIP can induce Li2S to bypass the sluggish
solid-phase intermediate path via chemical oxidation, directly
converting it into soluble polysulfides, thereby significantly
reducing the initial charge activation overpotential by about
700 mV and substantially improving the utilization of active
material. Simultaneously, DIP molecules, via Li–O coordination
and van der Waals interactions with polysulfides, effectively
accelerate reaction kinetics and regulate Li2S deposition behavior
from a 2D planar mode prone to passivation to a 3D spherical
growth mode conducive to high capacity release. Furthermore,
decomposition products of DIP during cycling can participate
in situ in building a dense and stable SEI film rich in organic
sulfur, providing effective protection for the lithium metal
anode and significantly inhibiting polysulfide shuttle corrosion
and lithium dendrite growth. The DIP mediator also shows
promising potential under lean-electrolyte conditions: the DIP-
induced organic-sulfur-rich SEI and its strong adsorption toward
polysulfides effectively mitigate electrolyte depletion and sup-
press side reactions, which is crucial for extending the cycle
life of practical high-energy-density anode-free lithium-sulfur
batteries. Ultimately, benefiting from the outstanding thermal
stability of the TEGDME solvent (flash point 140◦C) and the
dual optimization effects of DIP at the cathode and anode
interfaces, this strategy enables stable operation in challenging
anode-free battery systems. Moreover, the assembled Li||Li2S
batteries exhibit outstanding long-cycle stability (500 cycles)
and high-rate performance (3 C), and the practical viability
of this system has been successfully verified in high-loading
pouch cells. The DIP-based strategy is readily scalable, and
the dual-functional mechanism of DIP is universal and can be
extended to other advanced metal–sulfur systems, including Na–
S, K–S, Ca–S, Mg–S, and Zn–S batteries, demonstrating great
potential for practical applications in next-generation high-safety
energy-storage devices.
