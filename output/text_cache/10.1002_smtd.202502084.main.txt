ABSTRACT 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are one of the most promising candidates for next-generation energy storage due to their high 
theoretical energy density and cost-effective active material. However, challenges such as polysulfide shuttling and lithium metal 
instability hinder their practical deployment. To tackle these challenges, in this study, we delve into the critical impacts of salt 
anion in the electrochemical performance of LSBs, focusing on a family of localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) 
comprising two prevalent anions [i.e., lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide 
(LiTFSI)]. While higher LiFSI content enhances ionic conductivity, SEI formation, and lithium metal compatibility, it also 
reduces sulfur utilization due to side reactions with long-chain polysulfides. Post-mortem analyses confirm the formation of 
insulating species in high-LiFSI formulations. An optimal electrolyte composition with 0.2 m LiFSI as co-salt offers excellent 
electrochemical performance, achieving enhanced lithium protection and stabilized sulfur redox reactions. These findings reveal 
the coordination-dependent, double-edged nature of LiFSI and provide key insights for electrolyte design in high-performance 
LSBs.

Introduction 
The imperative to decarbonize sectors such as industry and 
transportation, while harnessing renewable energy sources, is 
driving an escalating demand for advanced energy storage solu- 
tions. Since their commercialization in 1991, lithium-ion batteries 
(LIBs) have dominated the market, due to their high energy 
efficiency and long cycle life. However, the growing demand 
for higher energy density, coupled with the limitations imposed 
by the near-theoretical capacity of LIBs already achieved, has 
intensified the search for next-generation battery chemistries 
[ 1–3 ]. 
Among the emerging candidates, lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) 
stand out as particularly promising. With a theoretical specific 
capacity of 1672 mAh g− 1 and achievable gravimetric energy 
densities exceeding 400 Wh kg− 1 , they offer a step-change in 
performance. Moreover, the natural abundance and low cost of 
sulfur, coupled with the inherent eco-friendliness of LSBs, fur- 
ther enhance their appeal for applications requiring lightweight 
solutions, such as drones, aviation, and freight transport [ 4–6 ]. 
Nevertheless, LSBs face several intrinsic challenges that hinder 
their practical deployment. On the anode side, while providing 
high energy density, the lithium metal anode (LMA) suffers

from dendritic lithium growth, unstable solid electrolyte inter- 
phase (SEI) formation, and high reactivity with conventional 
electrolytes, leading to safety concerns and limited cycle life [ 7–9 ]. 
Simultaneously, the sulfur cathode experiences complex redox 
reactions involving the intermediate formation of soluble lithium 
polysulfides (LiPS), which are prone to migrate to the anode, 
causing the so-called shuttle effect. This leads to active material 
loss, self-discharge, and low Coulombic efficiency (CE) [ 10–12 ]. 
Electrolyte engineering has therefore emerged as a cornerstone 
strategy to overcome these challenges. The electrolyte plays 
multi-faceted roles in LSBs: it governs the solvation structure of 
lithium ions, thereby affecting ionic conductivity and interfacial 
chemistry, while simultaneously influencing the stability of both 
electrodes during cycling. Conventional ether-based electrolytes, 
although widely used due to their chemical compatibility with 
sulfur species and low viscosity, suffer from critical drawbacks. 
These electrolytes often fail to passivate the surface of the 
LMA, resulting in uncontrolled interfacial reactions, dendrite 
formation, and low CE. Additionally, with their strong sol- 
vating power, they readily dissolve LiPSs, leading to a severe 
shuttle effect, active material loss, and capacity fading during 
cycling. 
To mitigate these issues, sparingly solvating electrolytes (SSEs) 
have emerged as promising alternatives [ 13, 14 ]. Among them, 
localized highly concentrated electrolytes (LHCEs) have attracted 
particular interest. LHCEs combine the beneficial solvation struc- 
tures of highly concentrated electrolytes with reduced viscosity 
and improved transport properties by introducing inert, non- 
solvating diluents, though polar [ 15, 16 ]. This design results 
in a tailored lithium-ion coordination environment with fewer 
free solvent molecules and stronger lithium–anion interactions, 
which effectively suppresses polysulfide dissolution and pro- 
motes stable SEI formation on LMA [ 17 ]. Consequently, LHCEs 
offer enhanced cycling stability and safety compared to conven- 
tional dilute electrolytes, making them especially promising for 
LSB applications [ 18 ]. 
Within these advanced systems, the choice of the lithium salt 
plays a pivotal role in determining the physicochemical and 
electrochemical behavior of the electrolyte. Beyond serving as a 
Li+ source, the salt anion governs the ionic coordination shell, 
influences the degree of ion dissociation, and determines the 
composition and morphology of the SEI formed on the LMA 
surface. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) is 
widely used in LSBs due to its chemical stability and broad 
electrochemical window. However, it suffers from the forma- 
tion of a relatively fragile SEI, which fails to suppress lithium 
dendrite growth and irreversible consumption of active lithium 
ions, thereby compromising the long-term cyclability and safety 
of LSBs. On the other hand, lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide 
(LiFSI) has attracted increasing attention since it promotes 
thinner, denser, and more uniform SEI layers [ 19, 20 ]. These 
SEI layers, often enriched in inorganic components, such as 
LiF, contribute to improved CE, reduced interfacial resistance, 
and suppressed dendrite formation [ 21, 22 ]. However, despite 
these advantages, the use of LiFSI in LSBs remains controver- 
sial. Several studies have reported parasitic reactions between 
LiFSI and LiPSs, leading to the formation of inactive species 
and decomposition products that irreversibly consume both salt 
and active material [ 23 ]. This degradation pathway reduces the 
effective sulfur utilization and undermines long-term cycling 
performance. To balance these trade-offs, dual-salt electrolytes 
combining LiTFSI and LiFSI have been explored [ 24 ]. These salt 
mixtures aim to synergistically optimize bulk conductivity, tailor 
the Li+ solvation environment, and engineer robust SEI layers 
with favorable chemical composition. The combination allows 
fine-tuning of the anion-to-solvent interactions and enhances the 
stabilization of both LMA and sulfur redox chemistry, represent- 
ing a versatile approach to overcome the inherent trade-offs of 
single-salt formulations [ 25 ]. 
Nevertheless, a deeper understanding of how LiFSI behaves 
within LHCEs in LSBs is still needed. Although dual-salt systems 
show promise in balancing conductivity, interfacial stability, and 
compatibility with LMA, the specific concentration of LiFSI 
plays a critical role in determining the overall performance and 
long-term stability of the system. Indeed, recent studies have 
reported conflicting results regarding the use of LiFSI in LSBs, 
with some highlighting its benefits [ 26, 27 ] and others pointing 
to severe reactivity with LiPSs that leads to active material loss 
and electrolyte degradation [ 28, 29 ]. Despite these concerns, a 
clear framework to predict and control such behavior remains 
elusive. 
In this work, we perform a systematic investigation of the 
effect of LiFSI concentration in LHCEs for LSBs. By tun- 
ing the LiFSI/LiTFSI ratio within a fixed sulfolane/1,1,2,2- 
tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (SL/TTE) matrix, 
we aim to delineate the optimal conditions that combine the 
advantages of both salts, such as improved ionic transport and 
stable SEI formation, while minimizing the detrimental reactivity 
of LiFSI with LiPSs. This study provides fundamental insight 
into the coordination-dependent behavior of LiFSI in complex 
electrolyte environments and establishes clear boundaries for 
its effective application. Ultimately, our findings shed light on 
when and how LiFSI can be successfully integrated into LHCEs, 
resolving existing ambiguities in the literature and offering design 
guidelines for future electrolytes to achieve high-performance 
LSBs. 
2 
Results and Discussion 
To unravel the dual role of LiFSI in LHCEs, five different SL- 
based electrolytes, keeping the total lithium salt concentration 
constant at 1.2 m , with varying LiTFSI/LiFSI ratios (1.2 m 
LiTFSI; 0.2 m LiFSI + 1 m LiTFSI; 0.45 m LiFSI + 0.75 m 
LiTFSI; 0.6 m LiFSI + 0.6 m LiTFSI; 1.2 m LiFSI, referred 
to throughout the text by LiTFSI, DSE20, DSE45, DSE60, and 
LiFSI, respectively) were prepared and systematically evaluated. 
In this study, SL was selected as the solvating backbone for 
the LHCE because it combines an exceptionally robust safety 
profile with physicochemical properties highly favorable for Li–
S chemistry. SL is a thermally stable, non-flammable sulfone 
solvent with a high flash point and negligible vapor pressure, 
offering intrinsic safety benefits compared with conventional 
ether-based formulations. Moreover, its high dielectric constant, 
wide electrochemical stability window ( > 5 V vs. Li/Li+ ), and 
excellent oxidative resilience make it a particularly attractive 
solvent candidate for practical LSBs.

Bulk properties, including polysulfide solubility, ionic conduc- 
tivity, and cathode wettability (Figure 1 ), were first examined, 
as these parameters directly influence both shuttling effect and 
electrode kinetics. 
As shown in the visual LiPS solubility tests, the incorporation 
of LiFSI in the electrolyte formulations reduces LiPS solvation 
ability, indicating that FSI-based coordination environments 
better suppress polysulfide solvation (inset of Figure 1a ). This 
effect reflects stronger Li+ –FSI− interactions, which reduce 
the availability of free solvent molecules capable of solvating 
LiPS. Such behavior is particularly relevant to mitigate the 
shuttle effect, wherein dissolved LiPSs continuously migrate 
to the anode, causing active material loss and parasitic side 
reactions. 
To further support the visual observations shown in the inset of 
Figure 1a , UV–vis analyses were carried out on the LiPS-saturated 
electrolytes. Since all formulations share the same SL/TTE sol- 
vent environment and differ only in the nature and proportion of 
the lithium salt, the relative absorbance intensities can be directly 
compared to assess changes in polysulfide solubility. As shown 
in Figure 1a , the LiTFSI-based electrolyte displays the highest 
absorbance across the measured wavelength range, consistent 
with its pronounced ability to dissolve LiPS. Upon increasing 
the LiFSI content in the electrolyte formulation (DSE20 →
DSE45 →DSE60 →LiFSI), a gradual and systematic decrease 
in absorbance intensity is observed. This trend quantitatively 
confirms the reduced polysulfide solubility inferred from the 
optical images and reflects the limited availability of solvating 
environments in the FSI-rich systems. The combined visual and 
spectroscopic evidence thus demonstrates that LiFSI-containing 
LHCEs effectively suppress the dissolution of long-chain poly- 
sulfides, contributing to an electrolyte environment less prone to 
shuttle-induced degradation. 
Conductivity measurements further supported the beneficial 
influence of LiFSI incorporation. As shown in Figure 1b , pro- 
gressive replacement of LiTFSI by LiFSI results in an increase in 
ionic conductivity, both in pristine electrolytes and in electrolytes 
saturated with polysulfides. This enhancement is attributed to 
the smaller anionic radius and higher dissociation degree of 
LiFSI, which promotes ion mobility even in the presence of 
polysulfides. Notably, ionic conductivity retention under LiPS 
saturation distinguishes these LHCEs from conventional ether- 
based systems, where even moderate LiPSs concentrations cause 
pronounced transport losses, exacerbating polarization and lim- 
iting rate performance [ 30 ]. In contrast, the robustness of the 
currently developed LHCEs, particularly for electrolytes based 
on LiFSI, under shuttle-prone conditions suggests that these

The impact of the electrolyte formulation on electrode wetting 
was also investigated via contact angle measurements. As pre- 
sented in Figure 1c and Figure S1 , the electrolyte wettability 
on sulfur cathodes is primarily dictated by the total salt con- 
centration, instead of the feature of the salt anion, indicating 
minimal viscosity differences across formulations. Since inade- 
quate wettability can impede ion transport within the porous 
cathode structure and thereby compromise sulfur utilization, the 
confirmation that LiFSI incorporation does not negatively affect 
this property is encouraging. In summary, the combined analyses 
of solubility, conductivity, and wettability established that the 
inclusion of LiFSI in the LHCEs provides a clear benefit for ionic 
transport and LiPS suppression, without introducing penalties in 
electrode wetting. 
While these bulk electrolyte properties suggest an environment 
favorable for Li–S operation, the long-term viability of any formu- 
lation is ultimately dictated by its compatibility with LMA, which 
remains the most critical factor for LSB stability. To this end, the 
different electrolytes were evaluated in Li◦||Cu and Li◦||Li◦con- 
figurations to assess lithium deposition behavior, SEI formation, 
and electrochemical reversibility as schematically represented in 
Figure 2a . Cyclic voltammetry (CV) results presented in Figure 
S2 reveal that increasing LiFSI content enhances current density 
and reversibility, indicating a more rapid charge transfer process 
and the formation of a more stable SEI layer. Notably, the 1.2 m 
LiFSI system exhibits almost no cycle-to-cycle variation, in sharp 
contrast to the rest of the electrolytes, where weaker Li+ –TFSI− 
interactions yielded less uniform passivation layers, as reflected 
by the CE values in Table S1 . As shown in the table, the reference 
LiTFSI electrolyte delivers poor reversibility, with an average CE 
below 80 % and a slow increase over the first cycles, reflecting 
the formation of an unstable SEI and continuous electrolyte 
consumption. By contrast, even with a modest addition of LiFSI 
(DSE20), a marked improvement is produced, raising the average 
CE to nearly 90 % and achieving > 95 % already from the second 
cycle. Finally, the LiFSI formulation yields the best performance, 
as clearly seen in Figure 2b , maintaining highly reproducible 
efficiencies above 94 % from the very first cycle and reaching an 
average CE of 96.1 %.

corroborates these findings, clearly demonstrating the good 
compatibility of LiFSI with LMA. As shown in Figure S3a , 
higher LiFSI concentrations led to lower deposition overpoten- 
tials, suggesting smoother nucleation and more homogeneous Li

of LiF-rich SEI layers, which are known to combine high ionic 
conductivity with mechanical stability. These layers suppress 
dendritic growth, lower interfacial resistance, and enable highly 
reversible plating/stripping. Conversely, the electrolyte contain- 
ing only LiTFSI displays inferior performance, which evidences 
the anion’s stability toward reduction at the Li◦interface and the 
weak SEI commonly associated with TFSI− . 
To substantiate these hypotheses, scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) measurements were carried out on the initial lithium 
deposits on Cu substrates. The resulting SEM images provide 
direct morphological evidence of the impact of LiFSI on lithium 
deposition (Figure S3c ). The increase of LiFSI content in the 
electrolyte formulation affords notably smoother, denser, and 
more homogeneous lithium deposits, indicating a more uniform 
nucleation process and compact growth. These morphologies 
suggest that FSI− anions contribute to the rapid formation of 
an inorganic-rich SEI, dominated by LiF. By contrast, LiTFSI 
formulation results in highly porous and fibrillar morphologies, 
which are typically associated with large surface areas, unstable 
SEI layers, and the initiation of dendritic structures. 
To better understand the LMA compatibility behavior of the 
developed electrolytes, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
measurements to determine the chemical composition of the 
SEI formed in these electrolytes were carried out. F 1s spectra 
are displayed in Figure S3d . As shown in the figure, the LiTFSI 
sample shows only a modest contribution from LiF around 
685 eV, while higher binding energy signals ( ∼ 689 eV) from 
─CF3 moieties related to salt residuals dominate. Its poor LiF 
content is consistent with the unstable, porous deposits observed 
in SEM. In contrast, the LiFSI electrode displays a strong and 
sharp LiF peak with only minor contributions from FSI fragments 
or residuals. This shift toward an inorganic, LiF-rich SEI explains 
the lower overpotentials, higher CE, and compact morphologies 
associated with LiFSI-based electrolytes. Altogether, the XPS 
analysis confirms that FSI− promotes the formation of a robust, 
fluorine-rich passivation layer, whereas TFSI− leads to a weaker, 
less protective interphase. 
Cross-section SEM images (Figure S4 ) corroborate these observa- 
tions, revealing progressively thinner and denser lithium deposit 
layers as the LiFSI content increases, starting with an approx- 
imate thickness of 60 µm for the LiTFSI electrolyte, which is 
reduced to 25–30 µm in the case of LiFSI formulation. This mor- 
phological stabilization aligns with the previously reported data 
in Figure S3 , confirming that even a small LiFSI incorporation 
(DSE20) enhances both the thermodynamics of nucleation and 
the kinetics of stable deposition. 
The stabilizing role of LiFSI is further evidenced during extended 
Li◦plating/stripping experiments on Cu substrates, as shown in 
Figure 2c . The electrolytes containing LiFSI exhibit lower over- 
potentials and more stable voltage profiles over repeated cycles. 
The cell overpotential exhibits a modest but consistent decrease 
as the LiFSI content increases, reaching its minimum in the LiFSI 
electrolyte. This trend reflects the progressive stabilization of the 
lithium surface through the formation of LiF-rich SEI layers. In 
contrast, the TFSI-based electrolyte displays pronounced voltage 
fluctuations and polarization growth, consistent with unstable 
interfacial chemistry and progressive electrolyte consumption. 
These electrochemical trends are directly reflected in the cycled 
electrode morphology as shown in SEM images (Figure 2d ). After 
20 cycles, electrodes cycled in LiTFSI electrolyte display rough, 
porous, and dendritic deposits, evidencing uncontrolled Li◦
nucleation and growth. Such irregular morphologies drastically 
increase surface area, exposing fresh Li◦to continuous electrolyte 
decomposition and accelerating capacity loss. In contrast, these 
irregular morphologies are progressively eliminated in the dual- 
salt electrolytes as the LiFSI content in the electrolyte increases 
until reaching the LiFSI sample. This sample produces deposits 
that remain relatively smooth, compact, and uniform, indicating 
that the as-formed SEI layer effectively regulates ion transport 
and suppresses localized growth of lithium deposits. The contrast 
between the smooth surfaces in LiFSI-containing systems and the 
dendritic features in the LiTFSI electrolyte highlights the decisive 
role of FSI− in guiding uniform Li◦deposition.

applied, such as lithium symmetric cells, as schematized in 
Figure 2e . The advantages of LiFSI incorporation become even 
more evident under demanding conditions in symmetric Li◦||Li◦
cells cycled at progressively higher current densities (Figure 2f ).

test of electrolyte stability, as high current densities accelerate

In stark contrast, the LiTFSI formulation degrades rapidly as 
the current density increases. Cell polarization increases sharply 
when the applied current density is higher than 0.5 mA cm− 2 , 
and unstable voltage profiles indicate the onset of uncontrolled 
Li◦growth and interfacial breakdown. These results highlight the 
limited ability of the TFSI-derived SEI layers to regulate ion flux 
at high rates, making them prone to dendrite initiation and short-

this divergence, in line with earlier observations where increasing 
LiFSI concentration in the electrolyte progressively stabilizes the 
electrolyte-electrode interface. 
In brief, the results confirm that the incorporation of LiFSI 
in the LHCE formulations greatly improves LMA stability. The 
FSI-derived SEI, richer in LiF as shown in Figure S3d , lowers 
deposition overpotentials, enhances CE from 75.4 % of LiTFSI 
electrolyte to 98.6 % of LiFSI electrolyte, and produces compact 
deposits rather than the dendritic morphologies seen with the 
reference LiTFSI. Even under the harsh conditions of symmetric 
cells cycled up to 2 mA cm− 2 , LiFSI-rich electrolytes (DSE45, 
DSE60, and LiFSI) sustain lower polarization and more stable 
operation, highlighting their effectiveness in ensuring uniform 
and reversible lithium deposition. 
Additionally, direct visual evidence from in situ Li◦deposition 
experiments (full video available in the Supporting Information ) 
further corroborates these results. As illustrated in Figure S7 , 
the LiTFSI electrolyte displays a clear dendritic growth, whereas 
the LiFSI sample, instead, exhibits more homogeneous plating 
behavior, reinforcing the decisive stabilizing role of LiFSI at the 
electrode-electrolyte interface. 
Despite the clear benefits of LiFSI for LMA stabilization, full cell 
cycling experiments reveal the trade-off that defines its double- 
edged role in LSBs. As shown in Figure 3a , the cells cycled with 
the electrolytes containing a higher amount of LiFSI in their 
formulation (LiFSI and DSE60) exhibit lower discharge capacities 
and more rapid capacity fading, irrespective of their benefits in 
stabilizing the cycling of LMA ( cf . Figure 2 ). In particular, the 
formulation with pure LiFSI shows a quick capacity drop within 
only a few cycles. By contrast, lowering the content of LiFSI, the 
systems seem to stabilize, increasing their discharge capacities 
and also avoiding the capacity decay during cycling. Among 
all the studied samples, DSE20 achieves the highest discharge 
capacities and the most stable cycling. Furthermore, the results 
demonstrate once again that small amounts of LiFSI have a 
symbiotic effect when combined with mesoporous graphene, 
increasing initial capacities due to improved interaction between 
all components of the system [ 32 ]. These results indicate that only 
limited fractions of LiFSI can be tolerated before its detrimental 
interaction with polysulfides outweighs its stabilizing role at the 
anode. These findings align with prior reports identifying LiFSI 
as an effective additive rather than a primary salt [ 26, 32 ]. 
To clarify the origin of this degradation, activation tests have been 
performed by charging cells with LiFSI electrolytes to 4.0 V after 
five cycles. As shown in Figure S8 , no recovery in capacity was 
observed, excluding inactive Li2 S accumulation as the primary 
cause of failure. Instead, post-mortem analyses were carried out 
in order to provide direct evidence of the degradation processes 
underlying the poor sulfur utilization observed in LiFSI-rich 
systems. The samples were cycled for 8 cycles (Figure S9 ) before 
opening and post-mortem analysis using SEM. 
As shown in the SEM images (Figure 3b ), the cathodes cycled 
with the LiFSI formulation developed dense insulating surface 
layers and pronounced morphological alterations, in sharp con- 
trast with the more open and porous structure preserved in 
the LiTFSI and DSE20 cathodes. The progressive thickening 
of these surface deposits with increasing LiFSI concentration 
(Figure S10 ) points to the accumulation of electrochemically 
inactive species, which hinder sulfur accessibility and block redox 
activity, thereby explaining the observed capacity decay. High- 
resolution SEM images provided in Figure S11 further highlight 
the differences in surface morphology, with the LiFSI cathode 
showing a thicker, more obstructive layer, the LiTFSI cathode 
retaining a more accessible, porous structure, and the DSE20 
cathode demonstrating a well-balanced, stable surface layer that 
still permits high sulfur utilization. 
Chemical analysis by XPS further clarifies the origin of these 
insulating layers. The wide-scan XPS spectra of the post-mortem 
cathodes can be found in Figure S12 , providing an overview 
of the elements present in the cathode surface after cycling. 
Going deeper into the analysis, the F 1s spectra (Figure S13 ) 
reveal the presence of LiF in all analyzed electrolytes (LiTFSI, 
DSE20, and LiFSI), with a higher LiF content observed in the 
latter. The formation of LiF can be attributed to the ability of 
LHCEs to generate a cathode-electrolyte interphase via salt-anion 
decomposition, producing inorganic-rich layers that effectively

In addition to the cathode-side reactivity, the S 2p XPS spectra 
of the post-mortem LMA (Figure S15 ) reveal that sulfite and 
thiosulfate species are present at the anode–electrolyte interface

where the signals centered around ∼ 167–169 eV are significantly 
more intense. This suggests that a portion of the soluble short- 
chain polysulf ides formed during cycling migrates to the lithium

conjunction with FSI− decomposition products. These reactions 
consume metallic lithium and lead to the accumulation of sulfite-

ultimately altering the interfacial transport properties of the 
anode. 
The increased formation of sulfite and thiosulfate species in the 
LiFSI system, both at the anode and the cathode, provides a direct 
explanation for the poor performance and rapid capacity fade 
observed in LiFSI-based electrolytes. These species lead to an 
increasingly resistive and non-conductive layer at both electrodes 
and contribute to the consumption of active material. 
Interestingly, a more balanced behavior is observed on the anode 
side in the DSE20 system. The S 2p spectrum of the DSE20 
exhibits an intermediate population between single-salt LiFSI 
and single-salt LiTFSI of sulfite/thiosulfate species while still 
retaining the favorable interphase chemistry associated with 
FSI− . This environment promotes the formation of a stable SEI 
layer without excessive accumulation of insulating species. 
Taken together, these cathode- and anode-side XPS analyses pro- 
vide direct mechanistic confirmation of the synergistic behavior 
of the DSE20 electrolyte. By incorporating only a small fraction 
of LiFSI, DSE20 suppresses detrimental reactions at the cathode 
while simultaneously enabling robust, stable SEI formation at 
the anode. This delicate balance between polysulfide stability 
and interphase reinforcement explains why DSE20 consistently 
outperforms both single-salt LiTFSI- and single-salt LiFSI elec- 
trolytes, achieving improved sulfur utilization, stable cycling, and 
enhanced LMA compatibility under lean-electrolyte conditions. 
In LHCE systems, the scarcity of free solvent molecules relative 
to conventional dilute electrolytes shifts the lowest unoccupied 
molecular orbital (LUMO) from the solvent to the salt anion, 
rendering the anion more prone to reduction [ 39 ]. While this 
shift can lead to a more anion-rich SEI layer, it also makes the 
anion more susceptible to other reductive reactions. Additional 
control experiments, as summarized in Figure S16 , were carried 
out to determine whether the degradation observed in LiFSI 
systems is an intrinsic property of the FSI− anion or rather a 
consequence of its interaction with the electrolyte environment 
and the polysulfide species generated during cycling. 
When a sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathode is employed, 
which produces only short-chain polysulfides (Li2 S2 –Li2 S4 ) dur- 
ing discharge [ 40 ], the use of a LiFSI electrolyte results in 
stable capacities and extended cyclability (Figure S16a ). This 
behavior highlights that the improved performance of SPAN- 
based cathodes in LiFSI-based LHCEs results from the reduced 
solubility and reactivity of short-chain polysulfides, which are 
less prone to parasitic reactions compared to the long-chain poly- 
sulfides (Li2 S6 –Li2 S8 ) typically generated by conventional sulfur 
cathodes. This result demonstrates that the poor performance 
of conventional sulfur cathodes in the LiFSI-rich electrolytes 
(DSE60 and LiFSI) originates specifically from the interaction 
of FSI− with long-chain LiPSs, leading to the formation of 
inactive products like sulfite and thiosulfate, as shown in the 
XPS data, which reduces sulfur utilization, and not from the 
inherent instability of the lithium salt. In the environment where 
long-chain polysulfides are absent, as in the SPAN cathodes, 
the beneficial role of LiFSI becomes more evident. The anion 
stabilizes the LMA interface, promotes the formation of com- 
pact Li◦deposits, and enhances the CE, confirming that LiFSI 
incorporation can significantly improve LMA protection when its 
parasitic cathode-side reactivity is eliminated, thus underscoring 
the superior performance of SPAN-based cathodes in LiFSI-rich 
electrolytes. Further confirmation was obtained by testing LiFSI 
in a conventional ether-based 1,2-dimethoxyethane/1,3-dioxolane 
(DME/DOL) solvent mixture, named DD 3:1 LN, where the 
solvation environment differs markedly from that of the LHCEs. 
As shown in Figure S16b , a 1 m LiFSI concentration exhibits 
improved compatibility with sulfur redox processes, achieving 
higher sulfur utilization and enhanced cycling stability. This

efficiency under lean electrolyte conditions, showing that LiFSI

when carefully tuned (Figure 4c ). 
3 
Conclusion 
This work provides a comprehensive understanding of the 
coordination-dependent, dual role of LiFSI in LHCEs for LSBs. 
Through systematic variation of the LiFSI/LiTFSI ratio, we show 
that LiFSI incorporation significantly enhances ionic conductiv- 
ity, reduces polysulfide solubility, and promotes the formation of 
LiF-rich SEI layers that stabilize lithium metal deposition. These 
effects translate into smoother and denser Li◦morphologies, 
higher Coulombic efficiencies in Li◦||Cu cell configurations, 
and stable operation of symmetric Li◦||Li◦cells even under 
demanding high-rate conditions. However, these benefits come 
at the expense of cathode stability when LiFSI is used in high 
concentrations. By disentangling these competing processes, our 
study identifies a narrow compositional window, DSE20, that 
optimally balances the stabilizing role of FSI− at the anode with 
the need to preserve polysulfide redox activity at the cathode. 
This dual-salt formulation delivers improved cycling stability 
and energy efficiency under lean electrolyte conditions, showing 
that LiFSI can indeed be a powerful tool for Li–S electrolyte 
engineering when carefully tuned. 
Taken together, these findings clarify the conflicting reports on 
the role of LiFSI in LSBs and establish a mechanistic framework 
that links anion coordination to interfacial stability at both 
electrodes. Beyond resolving this long-standing ambiguity, the 
results provide actionable design principles for dual-salt and 
anion-engineered electrolytes, highlighting that rational control 
of the solvation environment is essential for enabling safe, high- 
performance Li–S systems. More broadly, this work underscores 
the importance of electrolyte design strategies that simultane- 
ously manage lithium-metal protection and sulfur redox chem- 
istry, a paradigm directly relevant for advancing next-generation 
high-energy rechargeable batteries.
