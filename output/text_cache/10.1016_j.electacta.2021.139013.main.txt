The importance of the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in lithium-sulfur 
batteries and a perspective on high-energy electrolyte/cathode design

a b s t r a c t 
The dissolution of polysulﬁdes is widely considered to be a major obstacle for developing lithium-sulfur 
batteries (LSBs) because it results in a shuttle effect. A popular strategy to address this issue is prevent- 
ing polysulﬁde dissolution, e.g., trapping/conﬁning polysulﬁdes in porous carbons. However, this conﬂicts 
with the advantage of commonly used ether-based electrolytes, i.e., the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in such 
electrolytes is beneﬁcial for delivering Li + to sulfur-based species compared to sluggish solid-state trans- 
port. Thus, a question is raised on the feasibility of the strategy to prevent polysulﬁde dissolution. Here, 
it is shown that the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in ether-based electrolytes is necessary for a high capacity, 
suggesting that it might not be right to prevent the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in such electrolytes. Thus, 
other approaches should be developed. A perspective is hence provided for designing electrolyte/cathode 
for high-energy LSBs.

1. Introduction 
Developing high-energy and low-cost rechargeable batteries has 
always been and will continue to be the trend in the history of bat- 
teries. This is becoming more and more important at the present 
time with the increasing demand for electric vehicles (EVs), grid 
storage, and consumer electronics. [ 1 , 2 ] In this context, lithium- 
sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been receiving intense research ef- 
forts because of their high theoretical energy density (2567 Wh 
kg −1 /2199 Wh L −1 , compared to 387 Wh kg −1 /1015 Wh L −1 for 
commercial LiCoO 2 /graphite batteries) and the abundance of sul- 
fur. [3–6] 
The most used electrolytes for LSBs are ether-based. [ 1 , 3 , 5 , 7 ] 
This is because some intermediates (polysulﬁdes) of sulfur cath- 
odes, produced during the charging/discharging processes, are sol- 
uble in ether-based electrolytes. This is beneﬁcial for the transport 
of Li + in sulfur cathodes and thus enhances kinetics. [7] However, 
the dissolution of polysulﬁdes results in a shuttle effect, namely, 
polysulﬁdes formed in the cathode can be dissolved in the elec- 
trolyte and diffuse to the anode, where they get reduced to lower

chain polysulﬁdes and are then transported back to the cathode, 
where they get re-oxidized and return to the anode again. [8] This 
causes poor cycling cyclability, low Coulombic eﬃciency, and self- 
discharge. 
A major strategy to suppress the shuttle effect is preventing the 
dissolution of polysulﬁdes, e.g., trapping/conﬁning polysulﬁdes in 
nanoporous carbons. [ 3 , 5 , 8 , 9 ] This has been one of the most pop- 
ular strategies and a huge number of papers have been published 
based on such a strategy to achieve improved performance. How- 
ever, there appears to be a conﬂict between preventing the dissolu- 
tion of polysulﬁdes and taking the advantage of their dissolution. 
As a major justiﬁcation to use ether-based electrolytes is to dis- 
solve polysulﬁdes to provide fast kinetics and thus a high capacity 
of sulfur, a question is raised on the feasibility of this strategy to 
prevent polysulﬁde dissolution. 
In this work, to clarify this conﬂict, we ﬁrstly show that when 
using an ether-based electrolyte with high polysulﬁde solubility, it 
is necessary for polysulﬁdes to be dissolved to have a high capac- 
ity. This suggests that the popular strategy of preventing the dis- 
solution of polysulﬁdes in ether-based electrolytes might be mis- 
leading because it results in a low capacity. Thus, other approaches 
should be developed. For this, we provide a perspective on design- 
ing a sulfur cathode and electrolyte system for high-energy LSBs.

2. The importance of the dissolution of polysulﬁdes 
To evaluate the effects of the dissolution of polysulﬁdes on the 
capacity of sulfur cathode, we varied the pore volume between 
the sulfur cathode and lithium anode for the ﬁlling of different 
amounts of electrolyte. This was realized by using different lay- 
ers of separator (Celgard 2325, 25 μm thick, porosity: 39%) and a 
porous carbon paper (TORAY, TGP-H-060, 190 μm thick, porosity: 
78%), as shown in Figure 1 a, b and c. The loading densities of sul- 
fur were in the range of 1.4 mg cm −2 to 1.7 mg cm −2 , as indicated 
in Figure 1 a, b and c. A typical ether-based electrolyte with high 
polysulﬁde solubility was used, i.e., 1 M LiTFSI/1 M LiNO 3 in 1,2- 
dimethoxyethane (DME). The amount used in each cell was 30 μL 
per mg of sulfur. The sulfur used in the electrodes was a nanosized 
S@C composite ( Figure 1 d) with 90 wt% of sulfur. More experimen- 
tal details can be found in the supplementary materials. 
The ﬁrst discharge/charge voltage proﬁles are shown in 
Figure 1 e. When one layer of separator is used ( Figure 1 a), the 
ﬁrst discharge capacity is 264 mAh g −1 . Upon increasing the lay- 
ers of separator to seven ( Figure 1 b), the ﬁrst discharge capacity 
is increased to 634 mAh g −1 . The electrolyte used in the cell is 
excessive. However, only part of the electrolyte is accessible for 
the dissolution of polysulﬁdes due to the slow diffusion rate. This 
includes both the portion ﬁlling the sulfur cathode, and the por- 
tion near the sulfur cathode, which mostly ﬁlls the pore volume of 
the separator between the two electrodes. Thus, although the same 
electrolyte amount, relative to sulfur, is used in the two cells, the 
accessible electrolyte using seven layers of separator is much more 
than the one using one layer. This means that there is more elec- 
trolyte for the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in the cell using seven 
layers of separator. This results in a higher capacity. Therefore, if 
the accessible electrolyte is not suﬃcient, most polysulﬁdes will 
remain undissolved and the capacity will be low. 
The capacity of 634 mAh g −1 is still low compared to those 
reported. [ 1 , 5 ] This should be due to the insulating property of 
the separator. Upon dissolution, the polysulﬁdes can diffuse into 
the separator before being further reduced to insoluble Li 2 S 2 and 
Li 2 S. These parts of polysulﬁdes will remain inactive and will not 
contribute to the capacity unless they are transported back to the 
cathode. To overcome such a problem, we placed a porous carbon 
paper between the cathode and separator ( Figure 1 c). With the 
conductive framework of the porous carbon paper, the polysulﬁdes 
that diffuse into the carbon paper are active. Thus, the ﬁrst dis- 
charge capacity is increased to 1002 mAh g −1 , followed by a high 
reversible (charge) capacity of 977 mAh g −1 . 
The results above give evidence for the importance of the dis- 
solution of polysulﬁdes in an ether-based electrolyte. It suggests 
that the strategy of preventing the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in 
an ether-based electrolyte might not be beneﬁcial for improving 
the performance of LSBs. It also reveals the importance of the 
high porosity of sulfur electrodes. Unlike other lithium-ion batter- 
ies (LIBs) where the electrolyte ﬁlled in the pores of electrodes is 
used only for the transport of Li + , the electrolyte in sulfur elec- 
trodes is also for the dissolution of polysulﬁdes. Thus, the porosity 
of sulfur electrodes should be much higher than that of common 
LIB electrodes. 
3. Electrolyte-dependent energy density 
As the speciﬁc capacity of sulfur depends on the dissolution of 
polysulﬁdes in ether-based electrolytes, it is favorable that all the 
polysulﬁdes are in a dissolved state. This requires a large quantity 
of electrolyte. This is the intrinsic reason why there is always a 
lot of ether-based electrolyte needed in LSBs, e.g., electrolyte/sulfur 
(E/S) ratio > 10 μL mg −1 . [ 1 , 5 , 7 ] The energy density will thus be re- 
duced drastically, i.e., the energy density of LSBs is limited by the 
amount of electrolyte. 
Figure 2 a and d show the general trend of the energy density 
of LSBs with the change of E/S ratio and areal sulfur loading den- 
sity. The parameters and equations used for the calculations can 
be found in the supplementary materials. For better discussion, 
some examples are re-plotted in 2D graphs ( Figure 2 b, c, e, and f). 
These ﬁgures clearly show the importance to minimize the elec- 
trolyte amount to achieve a high energy density. For example, for 
speciﬁc energy ( Figure 2 b and c), if the E/S ratio is higher than 4 
μL mg −1 , it is almost impossible to achieve 300 Wh kg −1 , which is 
representative of the current state-of-the-art LIBs. For volumetric 
energy density ( Figure 2 e and f), the E/S ratio needs to be as low 
as 1 μL mg −1 to compete with the current LIB technology ( > 700 
Wh L −1 ). Moreover, it is not necessary to have an extremely high 
sulfur loading. For example, at an E/S ratio of 3 μL mg −1 , the in- 
crease in energy density is rather small from a sulfur loading of 
5 mg cm −2 to 20 mg cm −2 , i.e., an increase of 36 Wh kg −1 (from 
373 Wh kg −1 to 409 Wh kg −1 ) and 28 Wh L −1 (from 410 Wh L −1 
to 438 Wh L −1 ), for speciﬁc energy and volumetric energy density,

4. Designing high-energy LSBs with polysulﬁde-soluble 
electrolytes 
Because it is necessary to reduce the amount of electrolyte 
in LSBs to achieve a high energy density, using high polysulﬁde- 
solubility electrolytes is preferable so that polysulﬁde dissolution 
can be preserved. Polysulﬁdes have high solubility in conventional 
1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/DME electrolytes. The sulfur (as polysulﬁdes) 
solubility is ∼6 M in (DOL)/DME electrolytes. [7] As the solubil- 
ity varies with different ether solvents, [2] it might be possible to 
identify other ether electrolytes with an even higher polysulﬁde 
solubility. In addition to ethers, some high donor solvents have 
been found to have a high solubility for polysulﬁdes, for exam- 
ple, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), dimethyl acetamide, and tetrahy- 
drofuran. [ 7 , 10 , 11 ] Among them, DMSO provides particularly high 
polysulﬁde solubility, > 14.3 M (sulfur solubility). This may make it 
possible to operate LSBs at a low E/S ratio. 
The porosity of sulfur electrodes should be designed in accor- 
dance with the solubility of polysulﬁdes. It needs to provide a high, 
but appropriate volume for the electrolyte which is just suﬃcient 
for polysulﬁde dissolution. The porous structure and conductive 
framework of the sulfur electrode need to be optimized for trans- 
porting electrons between the conductive framework and dissolved 
polysulﬁdes, as well as for the deposition of polysulﬁdes. 
A major disadvantage of using polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes 
is the shuttle effect. The most successful method to suppress the 
shuttle effect is perhaps the addition of LiNO 3 in the electrolyte. 
[12] The use of LiNO 3 leads to the formation of a N-containing 
solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer on the surface of lithium 
which prevents the reaction of polysulﬁdes with lithium. [13] In 
the last decade, most of LSBs reported have used LiNO 3 for im- 
provement. However, LiNO 3 is consumed continuously upon cy- 
cling. [14] Thus, the beneﬁcial effects of LiNO 3 may only last for a 
limited number of cycles. It would be very favorable to have other 
electrolyte additives or electrolytes that can form a more stable SEI 
layer to suppress the shuttle effect. 
An alternative strategy to overcome the shuttle effect is us- 
ing physical/chemical adsorption of polysulﬁdes in the sulfur elec- 
trode. This necessitates the addition of adsorption materials, such 
as graphene oxide, oxides, sulﬁdes, and copper, in the sulfur elec- 
trode. [15–18] A large amount of such additives may be needed to 
completely adsorb polysulﬁdes. For example, the weight of copper 
is four times that of sulfur for full adsorption of polysulﬁdes with 
the conversion of copper to form Cu 2 S. [18] This will not be ac- 
ceptable for high-energy LSBs. Thus, chemical and physical adsorp- 
tion of polysulﬁdes may not be useful unless an adsorbent with an 
extremely high adsorption capacity is identiﬁed, such that only a 
small amount of adsorbent is needed to adsorb polysulﬁdes com- 
pletely. 
A powerful route to fully address the shuttle effect is prevent- 
ing polysulﬁdes from diffusing to the lithium anode. In this regard, 
Li + conductive membranes are particularly attractive. Such mem- 
branes allow the transport of only Li + while blocking the diffu- 
sion of polysulﬁdes. For example, lithiated Naﬁon membranes are 
reported to be effective Li + conductive membranes for LSBs. [19–
23] However, it appears that polysulﬁdes can still slowly diffuse 
through Naﬁon membranes. [ 19 , 20 ] Additionally, Naﬁon membrane 
would be too expensive for use in LSBs. Inorganic Li + conductive 
membranes, such as LISICON, can also be used for LSBs. However, 
this type of membrane is brittle, and thus, a thick membrane is 
needed to maintain mechanical stability, typically larger than 100 
μm thick. This will signiﬁcantly reduce the energy density of LSBs. 
Hence, more advanced Li + conductive membranes or other ap- 
proaches are needed to block the diffusion of polysulﬁdes. 
5. Designing high-energy LSBs with polysulﬁde-insoluble 
electrolytes 
Although polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes are favorable for fast 
kinetics, the use of a large amount of electrolyte and the shut- 
tle effect are still two major obstacles to be overcome. With re- 
spect to these two obstacles, polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes pro- 
vide promising solutions although slow kinetics is expected. With 
polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes, the electrolytes serve only for 
Li + transport, instead of additionally for the dissolution of poly- 
sulﬁdes. Thus, the amount of electrolyte can easily be reduced to 
a commercial level as LIBs to provide a high energy density. More- 
over, since polysulﬁdes do not dissolve in the electrolyte, there is 
no shuttle effect. Among such electrolytes, carbonate and inorganic 
solid electrolytes represent the most promising ones. 
Carbonate electrolytes are one of the most desirable electrolytes 
for LSBs because of their success in commercial LIBs. Some car- 
bonate electrolytes have also been reported to be poor solvents for 
polysulﬁdes. [24–26] However, such electrolytes give very low re- 
versible capacity when used with regular sulfur electrodes. [27–

29] This is usually attributed to the chemical reactions between 
polysulﬁdes and carbonate electrolytes, which results in the loss 
of active sulfur. [ 27 , 29 ] However, advanced materials design may 
provide a feasible electrochemical mechanism for the reduction of 
sulfur to Li 2 S in carbonate electrolytes. A typical route is conﬁn- 
ing sulfur in very small pores of carbon, with pores small enough 
to allow the presence of only short-chain sulfur, i.e., S n (n < = 4), 
instead of the common long-chain S 8 . [ 30 , 31 ] In this case, it is 
reported that the reduction process does not involve the forma- 
tion of long-chain polysulﬁdes, e.g., Li 2 S 8 . Another typical route 
incorporates sulfur into a polymer structure to obtain organosul- 
fur, such as sulfur-polyacrylonitrile (PAN) compounds. [ 24 , 32 , 33 ] 
The covalent bond between the sulfur atom and polymer structure 
surely affects the reduction of sulfur. Although they can give im- 
proved performance, both routes have the same unacceptable dis- 
advantage of low sulfur content in the materials, typically < 45%. 
In addition to these two routes, some other materials have also 
been reported to work in carbonate electrolytes, including sul- 
fur/mesoporous carbons, [ 34 , 35 ] sulfur/multi-walled carbon nan- 
otubes [36] , and alucone-coated sulfur/mesoporous carbons [ 28 , 37 ] 
Although the electrochemical mechanisms for some of these mate- 
rials are not clear, they should be able to prevent side reactions. In 
addition to modifying the cathode, highly concentrated carbonate 
electrolytes, 4 – 6 M, are also reported to work for LSBs. [ 25 , 38 ] 
The improvement in highly concentrated electrolytes may be due 
to the change of electrolyte coordination structure and the forma- 
tion of an SEI layer on sulfur electrodes. These render carbonate 
electrolytes very promising for LSBs. 
Inorganic solid electrolytes can provide improved safety with 
their non-ﬂammability and serve as polysulﬁde-insoluble elec- 
trolytes for LSBs. With decades of development, the Li + conduc- 
tivity of many inorganic solid electrolytes has reached the prac- 
tical level, e.g., ∼10 −3 S cm −1 . [39] Various solid-state LSBs have 
been reported based on such electrolytes. However, maintaining 
the electronic and ionic conductivity of electrodes is a major chal- 
lenge for solid-state batteries. This becomes more serious with the 
use of sulfur cathodes, because of the large volume change ( ∼75%) 
from sulfur to Li 2 S, and the poor electronic and ionic conductiv- 
ity of both sulfur and Li 2 S. [40] To address this, it is necessary to 
design advanced sulfur based-cathodes with improved electronic 
and ionic conductivity. To improve the electronic conductivity, sul- 
fur can be combined with electronically conductive materials, such 
as carbon, or transition metal sulﬁdes (e.g., CuS, FeS 2 , and TiS 2 ). 
[41–44] If porous carbon with extra void spaces is used as a host 
for sulfur, not only will the electronic conductivity be improved, 
but also the large volume change issue will be addressed. For im- 
proving Li + diffusion through sulfur/Li 2 S, solid solutions, such as 
Li 2 S-LiI, have been reported. [45] Although these strategies can of- 
fer improved performance, it would be favorable to have one that 
can overcome both the large volume change and the poor elec- 
tronic and ionic conductivity simultaneously. 
6. Summary 
we show that it is necessary for polysulﬁdes to be dissolved 
in the electrolyte for a high capacity of sulfur electrodes when an 
ether-based electrolyte with highly polysulﬁde solubility is used. 
This suggests that the commonly used strategy of preventing the 
dissolution of polysulﬁdes in ether-based electrolytes might not be 
right for LSBs. 
For a typical LSB system used for calculations, the E/S ratio 
needs to be less than 4 μL mg −1 and 1 μL mg −1 to compete with 
the current LIB technology for speciﬁc energy and volumetric en- 
ergy density, respectively. Once the areal sulfur loading reaches a 
certain amount, e.g., ∼5 mg cm −2 at an E/S ratio of 3 μL mg −1 , 
further increasing the loading will not improve the energy density 
signiﬁcantly. Such a sulfur loading may vary when the conditions 
are different. However, there will always be a point of diminishing 
returns for sulfur loading, beyond which the energy density will 
not increase signiﬁcantly by increasing the sulfur loading. Thus, it 
is not necessary to pursue an extremely high sulfur loading. 
The development of LSBs can be divided into two routes in 
general, i.e., using polysulﬁde-soluble or -insoluble electrolytes. 
Figure 3 summarizes the strategies for designing a high-energy 
sulfur cathode and electrolyte system. When a polysulﬁde-soluble 
electrolyte is used, the electrolyte needs to have a high solubil- 
ity for polysulﬁdes to reduce the amount of electrolyte for in- 
creasing the energy density. The sulfur electrode needs to have a 
high porosity to contain the electrolyte. The shuttle effect is still a 
key issue when such an electrolyte is used. The most effective ap- 
proaches for overcoming the shuttle effect perhaps are developing 
an ionic conductive membrane and protection layer on the anode. 
Chemical and physical adsorption of polysulﬁdes may not be useful 
unless an adsorbent with an extremely high adsorption capacity is 
identiﬁed, so that only a small amount of adsorbent is needed to 
adsorb polysulﬁdes completely. 
The use of polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes has the advan- 
tage of no shuttle effect. It can also lead to the fulﬁllment of 
the lean electrolyte condition for high energy. The most promis- 
ing polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes include carbonate-based and
