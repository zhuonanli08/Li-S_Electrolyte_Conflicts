Lithium (Poly)sulfide Phase Conversion in
Working Lithium−Sulfur Batteries: The Insight
from the Equilibrium Potential Model
Yu-Tong Zhang, Zi-Xian Chen, Yun-Wei Song, Jun-Dong Zhang, Xiang Chen, Bo-Quan Li, Rui Zhang,*
and Jia-Qi Huang*

ABSTRACT: The commercialization of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries is currently
hindered by the electrochemical instability and capacity loss, due to the ultracomplex
phase conversion of lithium polysulfides (LPSs) and lithium sulfide (Li2S). Revealing the
mechanism is critical to rational regulation of the cathode reaction process so as to
improve the cycling stability and capacity retention. Herein, we develop an equilibrium
potential model to reveal the two conversion pathways under different electrolyte/sulfur
ratios of working Li−S batteries. The model predicts that the supersaturation of Li2S and
the precipitation of LPSs are responsible for the potential drop at the onset of the second
plateau and the emergence of the third plateau, respectively, deviating from the typical
discharge profiles. Furthermore, quantitative guidance for electrolyte screening and
battery assembling is presented to selectively control LPS precipitation. The description
of equilibrium potential could be used as a foundation for mechanism study and battery
monitoring system development.
L
ithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been identified as
a promising alternative to Li-ion batteries to meet the
ever-increasing demand for higher energy density
energy storage devices.1,2 The solution-mediated multielectron
reaction in the cathode contributes to the ultrahigh theoretical
specific energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1.3,4 The discharge
process starts with the reduction of solid-state sulfur (generally
S8) into a series of soluble intermediate lithium polysulfides
(LPSs), such as Li2Sn (where n = 2−8), and ends with the
precipitation of lithium sulfide (Li2S).5,6 However, the poor
cycling stability and severe capacity loss caused by the
sophisticated solid−liquid−solid conversion process of LPSs
and Li2S are the primary obstacle to practical application of
Li−S batteries.7−9
On the one hand, restriction on the dissolution of LPSs can
suppress the “shuttle effect”, thereby diminishing the loss of
active substances and improving the cycling stability.10−12 On
the other, the insulated solid-phase products result in a much
higher electron/ion transfer resistance, significantly slowing
down the redox kinetics and decreasing sulfur utilization.5,13−15
Moreover, the solubility of LPSs varies considerably depending
on the electrolyte, due to the wide range of the molecular
polarities of LPSs.16 Therefore, to optimize the performance
and realize practical application of Li−S batteries, it is essential
to gain insights into the changes in the states of lithium
(poly)sulfide in electrolyte during discharge in order to
selectively control the precipitation of LPSs.17,18
Nevertheless, isolating and characterizing the individual
polysulfide out of symbiotic polysulfide anions is especially
challenging.19,20 Furthermore, the intricate interaction of
multiple kinetic factors makes it more difficult to extract the
intrinsic conversion mechanisms from real discharging
batteries.21−24 Model development of Li−S batteries is thus
a crucial step in gaining insights into the process. There have
been some modeling works that simulate the behaviors of the
sulfur cathode well, ever since the lumped Li−S battery model
integrating thermodynamics and kinetics was proposed by
Mikhaylik et al.10 Then, Kumaresan et al. developed a more
comprehensive one-dimensional (1D) mathematical model
that incorporated the multistep sulfur reduction reactions with
both liquid and solid phases, capable of reproducing the typical
discharge curves.25 Based on their models, various improved
approaches have been proposed from different aspects, such as

transport dynamics,26,27 self-discharge,28−30 overcharge,31
impedance and cyclic voltammetry analysis,32,33 etc. Most of
these models focus on the kinetic process, yet the intrinsic
phase conversion mechanism of LPSs is rarely discussed,
despite its importance in enhancing the understanding and
improving the performances.34 Furthermore, owing to the
difficulty of measurement in the complex system, the
parameters of these models are often assumed, leading to a
lack of simulation reliability.
Herein, we propose an experimentally aligned equilibrium
potential model to investigate the intrinsic phase conversion
mechanism of Li2S and LPSs in working Li−S batteries. By
calculating the equilibrium potential at various depths of
discharge (DODs), the model excludes kinetic factors (e.g.,
current and polarization) to simulate the intrinsic conversion
process of lithium (poly)sulfides in the cathode. The discharge
process is divided into two pathways with four stages, based on
different electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio conditions with differ-
ent phase states of sulfides in electrolyte (dissolution or
precipitation), to conduct a staged simulation. Reliable
parameters (standard electrode potential Eθ and solubility
product Ksp) are used to construct the model, which are fitted
with experimental galvanostatic intermittent titration techni-
que (GITT) data under various E/S ratios. This enables the
model to achieve good correspondence between the simulation
results and the experimental data, with the mean absolute
percentage error (MAPE) less than 0.5%.
Based on the model, the solid−liquid, liquid−solid, and
solid−solid phase transitions during the entire discharge
process are explained by tracking the variations of ion
concentration and equilibrium potential throughout the
discharge process. For the first time, it is proposed that there
are two stable states existing in the Li2S precipitation process:
the supersaturated metastable state and the precipitate-
deposited stable state. The transient supersaturation of Li2S
before precipitation is the key factor leading to the voltage
drop of the second plateau. Moreover, the model predicts that
the precipitation of LPSs is the main cause of the distortions of
voltage profiles such as three-plateau. The simulated potential
curve distortion trend of the two phenomena is in agreement
with the voltage curves observed in previous experiments, thus
verifying the proposed mechanism. Moreover, the solubility
limit of the various LPSs under any given E/S ratio is
presented to selectively precipitate LPSs, thus aiding the
electrolyte screening and battery assembly. The equilibrium
potential model presents a novel perspective for the follow-up
mechanism exploration and serves as a fundamental basis for
the construction of battery working condition monitoring
systems.
Electrochemical and chemical reactions. The model is based on
the typical ether-based electrolyte system of Li−S batteries,
consisting of 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME).35 Due to the complexity of the discharge process of
the system, the five electrochemical reactions and two chemical
reactions that have been commonly used to describe the
discharge process by Li−S battery models and experiments, as
shown in Tables 1 and 2, were adopted for numerical
simplicity.14,36−38

The determination of the conversion pathway is of
importance in simulation. This work presents two pathways
with four stages during the entire discharge process,
determined by different phase states of the sulfur compounds
in different E/S ratio conditions (Scheme 1b). When the initial
E/S ratio is sufficiently high, solid-state S8(s) in the cathode
begins to dissolve and be reduced (stage I). Subsequently, all
sulfur compounds are completely dissolved (stage II), before
Li2S precipitate (Li2S(s)) appears (stage IV). When the E/S
ratio is relatively low; however, S8(s) cannot be fully dissolved,
leading to the coexistence of S8(s) and Li2S(s) (stage III). The
equilibrium potential profiles of the two pathways are exhibited
in Scheme 1b. Under a low E/S ratio, the conversion process
(I →III →IV) is distinct from the ordinary one under a high
E/S ratio (I →II →IV), which is matched with the first
plateau, slope, and second plateau of the typical discharge
curve, respectively.40,41

Investigation of the Li2S(s) precipitation mechanism is crucial
to optimize the cycling stability and capacity retention during
discharge, for approximately three-quarters of the theoretical
capacity is contributed by Li2S(s) conversion. Studies have
reported that not only the discharge curves but also the
quasistationary OCP of GITT showed a depression in the early
stage of the second platform (Figure 2a).18,43,44 Therefore, it is
necessary to explore the thermodynamic mechanism under-
lying this phenomenon.
Here we present the discharge simulation results of the
equilibrium potential model. It indicates that there are two
thermodynamic stable states theoretically when Li2S is
saturated, namely the metastable state and the stable state,
corresponding to the green dotted line and the purple solid
line in Figure 2b, respectively. In the metastable state, Li2S is
supersaturated but cannot precipitate, resulting in a lower
equilibrium potential (green region in Figure 2b). Conversely,
in the stable state, Li2S is able to overcome the nucleation
barrier (ΔG) to deposit thus reaching a higher equilibrium
potential. This phenomenon can be seen under any E/S ratio
condition (Figure 2c). Note that this model is based on the
quasi-thermodynamic experimental data; therefore, it can
prove the existence of the thermodynamic supersaturation
phenomenon but cannot provide quantitative predictions of
the potential drop, which is dominantly affected by the kinetic
factors.
A simplified mechanism based on the Gibbs−Thomson
effect, a classical supersaturation theory, is employed here.45,46
According to eq 1, supersaturation (S) is the key factor
affecting ΔG for nucleation, apart from the surface tensile (γ).
As depicted in Figure 2a, when the ion product of Li2S just
reaches Ksp,Li
d2S, the supersaturation of Li2S is low (S1), resulting
in a large ΔG1 that is difficult to overcome. This keeps Li2S
initially in the metastable supersaturated state with no
precipitation, where the equilibrium potential is relatively
low. With the increase of sulfide-ion (S2−) concentration, the
supersaturation of Li2S grows, lowering the nucleation barrier
ΔG and making it easier to overcome by little disturbance
(thermal, kinetic, or mechanical).47 As Li2S begins to
precipitate, the supersaturation decreases and the equilibrium
potential gradually increases until the system reaches a stable
precipitation state (S2, ΔG2). After that, the precipitation of
Li2S causes the Li-ion (Li+) concentration to decrease, while
that of S2−increases because of the reduction of LPSs (Figure
2d).

Apart from Li2S, deposits of LPSs can also form in the
cathodes of Li−S batteries, due to the solubility differences of
sulfides in different electrolyte systems or the lean electrolyte
conditions with a low E/S ratio, which have been observed in
previous experiments.50−53 Our model predicts that the
introduction of LPS precipitation would lead to the emergence
of a third plateau in the equilibrium potential profiles, distinct
from the two-platform norm, which is particularly obvious
under lean electrolyte conditions. Experiments have previously
observed such three-discharge platforms,54,55 and we hereby
propose a new mechanism to explain this phenomenon from
the perspective of equilibrium potential.
Taking the precipitation of Li2S2 as an example, the
simulated equilibrium potential exhibited three-platform
profiles (Figures 3a and S1), assuming the Ksp,Li
d2S
d2 is 3.5 ×
10−3. The second plateau appears earlier due to the
precipitation of Li2S2, which in turn reaches a higher
equilibrium potential (averaging 2.19 V, compared with the
average equilibrium potential of 2.18 V without Li2S2
precipitation). When Li2S begins to precipitate, the two solid
species coexist in the system with a lower potential of 2.17 V,
forming the third plateau. During this stage, the potential
remains constant due to the solid−solid transformation from
Li2S2 to Li2S, with the ions in dynamic equilibrium and no
concentration fluctuations (Figure 3b,c).
To further study the conversion of Li2S2, the change of ion
concentration and ion product was investigated. Figure 3d
shows that there is a peak value of the Li2S2 ion product that
decreases with the increase of E/S ratio. This pattern is also
observed for other LPSs (Figure S2). If the peak value of the
ion product can be attained and the Ksp,Li
d2S
dn is kept above it, the
distorted voltages induced by the precipitation of LPSs can be
inhibited theoretically. Conversely, restraining Ksp,Li
d2S
dn below
the peak value can constrain LPS precipitate in a specific range

Li2S2, Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8 during discharge under various E/

respectively. As Figure 4a shows, selecting an electrolyte

of intermediate LPSs. This result allows researchers to screen
the electrolyte system under a certain E/S ratio, or conversely,
to identify a suitable E/S ratio range for any given electrolyte.
This work presents an equilibrium potential model that
describes the two-pathway lithium (poly)sulfide phase
conversion in the cathode of working Li−S batteries under
any given E/S ratio condition. The equilibrium potential is
simulated in stages, based on the different phase states of
sulfides in the cathode during discharge. The simulation results
with high consistency with experiments were obtained by
fitting the model parameters with OCP of GITT data, which is
approximated as the quasi-thermodynamic equilibrium poten-
tial of batteries. From the perspective of equilibrium potential,
two phase conversion mechanisms are proposed: (1) The
metastable state of supersaturated Li2S contributed to the
potential drop of the initial stage of the discharge curves. (2)

Moreover, the simulated potential curves are also consistent
with previous experimental works, further confirming the
mechanisms proposed. It will be of help to the electrode
kinetic improvement and the high specific energy density Li−S
pouch cell design. This also deepens the understanding on
conversion of sulfides in the cathode, providing a novel
approach for explaining distortions in the working voltage

presented, offering quantitative guidance for electrolyte

Furthermore, the grasp of the modeling method and the
values of the standard electrode potential necessary for
simulating cell curves accurately provide the foundation for
further study of the conversion mechanism of polysulfides as
well as the kinetic model development. Meanwhile, the method
of equilibrium potential modeling can serve as the basis of the
monitor of battery working condition in future studies.
