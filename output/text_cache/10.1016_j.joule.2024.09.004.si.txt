Section 2. Computational Methods 
Supplemental Experimental Procedures. 
To evaluate chemistry with different electrolyte formulations, we performed 
density functional theory (DFT) using Gaussian161 and ab initio molecular dynamics 
(AIMD) using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)2,3. For Gaussian16, the 
hybrid density functional B3LYP/6-311++G(d,p) method was used4-6. Geometry 
optimization and vibration frequency calculations were done to calculate the Gibbs free 
energies at 298 K. For VASP, the Perdew-Burke-Enzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation 
functional7 and the projector augmented wave (PAW) method8 were used. We set the 
plane wave energy cut-off to 460 eV and used Gamma-centered Monkhorst-Pack k-
point sampling. The sampling size was adjusted and is specified later in this section. 
We relaxed geometries until the residual forces on atoms converged below 0.01 eV/Å.  
We used DFT calculations to investigate the binding energies between Li 
polysulfide (Li2S6) and electrolyte species, including LiBF4, LiTFSI, DME, and TTE 
species in gas phase. We investigated the electrolyte intrinsic stability using AIMD 
simulations. We used the Packmol code to generate the initial geometry of the liquid-
phase electrolyte9. Two electrolytes were modeled: the electrolyte developed in this 
work and a conventional DME/DOL electrolyte. The composition of the developed 
electrolyte is 1.5 M of LiTFSI in TTE/DME (10:3= v/v). The electrolyte density was 
adjusted to 1.50 g mL-1. This density includes 3 LiTFSI and 1 LiBF4 salt molecules, as 
well as 10 TTE and 3 DME solvent molecules. We neglected the 0.05 M LiBF4 because 
it makes up a negligible fraction of the total electrolyte and because it would take 

<<PAGE 4>>
 
 
 
tremendous computational resources to adjust the electrolyte density to include it. The 
composition of the conventional electrolyte is 1.0 M LiTFSI, 0.2 M LiNO3 in 
DME/DOL (1:1=v/v), and the electrolyte density is 1.26 g/ml. This density includes 4 
LiBF4 and 1 LiNO3 salt molecules, and 25 DOL and 25 DME solvent molecules. We 
then used AIMD simulations to relax the molecules. The AIMD time step was set at 1 
fs. The AIMD simulations were performed for 10 ps at 300 K using the Nosé-Hoover 
thermostat and canonical ensemble (NVT). We investigated the frontier orbitals of the 
relaxed electrolytes, including the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the 
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level, using the trajectories at 10 ps. 
We further investigated the reductive stability of the modeled electrolyte on the 
Li slab. For the unit cell of Li bulk, we referred to crystal structure in the body-centered 
cubic Im3̅m space group. The k-point sampling was set to be 7×7×7. The lattice 
constants were relaxed by DFT simulations and optimized to 3.461 Å. The optimized 
lattice constants are matched to the previous computational10 and experimental11 lattice 
constants. A Li (001) slab was used for the AIMD slab simulations and adjusted to 
represent the electrolyte density. The k-point sampling was performed at Gamma point 
only because of the high computational demands. For 1.0 M LiTFSI/0.2 M LiNO3 in 
DOL/DME electrolyte, a 6×6×3 supercell was used that had 216 Li atoms. The x-y 
dimensions of the Li (001) slab were set at 20.767 Å×20.767 Å, with a vacuum length 
of 20.0 Å. For 1.5 M of LiTFSI in TTE/DME electrolyte, a 5×5×3 supercell was used 
that had 150 Li atoms. For this case, the x-y dimensions of the Li (001) slab were set at 
17.306 Å×17.306 Å, with a vacuum length of 13.5 Å. Because of high computational 

<<PAGE 5>>
 
 
 
demands, we tested the AIMD simulation using two different plane wave energy cut-
off values (400 and 460 eV). The trajectories and charge transfer results were 
comparable (Figure S12), and thus we used 400 eV for the AIMD slab simulations. We 
further tested spin polarization effects in the AIMD. The results were similar to the non-
spin polarization result (Figure S13), and subsequently AIMD was done without spin 
polarization effects. To investigate the charge (electron) transfer between electrolyte 
and electrode, Bader charge analysis was used12-14.

Section 3. Supplementary Figures 
 
Figure S1: Schematic of interfacial chemistries in practical Li-S cells.  
(a) Issues encountered under thick S cathode and lean electrolyte conditions.  
(b) Means by which the proposed Lewis-acid additive (0.05 M LiBF4) electrolyte 
addresses these issues. The electrolyte structure is adjusted so that Li2Sn is only 
sparingly solvated, minimizing side reactions with the Lewis-acid additive, which 
would otherwise sequester sulfur. The additive contributes to formation of a F-rich, 
ionically conductive interphase that encapsulates the cathode, permitting free 
movement of Li ions through even a thick, S-rich cathode. The TTE component of the 
electrolyte forms a relatively homogeneous interphase on the anode that allows 
unimpeded flux of Li ions. 
 
Supplemental Experimental Procedures. 
Conventional electrolyte/additive mainly relies on the salt/solvent self-sacrificial 
decomposition to construct the interface film, which could randomly take place and 
cover the contact surface. However, in our proposed electrolyte strategy, the interface 
film was induced via the interaction between electrode-released sparing LiPS and 
polysulfide-incompatible additive, which can  

<<PAGE 7>>
 
 
 
achieve the in-situ formation of high-quality protective thin film covering the electrode 
surface. Moreover, in conventional electrolyte/additive strategy, LiPS compatibility is 
an important requirement for choosing electrolyte components, which screens out a 
great number of additive candidates. Nevertheless, our electrolyte design strategy can 
break through the LiPS compatibility limitation by controlling the LiPS solubility and 
the additive concentration, which significantly extends the additive choice categories 
and thus achieves unique CEI/SEI formation. 
For the cell-level heterogeneity, electrochemical reactions may take place unevenly 
throughout the cell and cause cell-level heterogeneity, which is mainly caused by the 
severe LiPS flooding and obstructed ion transport. Therefore, suppressing the LiPS 
flooding and enhancing the ion transport is important in relieving cell-level 
heterogeneity. However, for conventional electrolytes with high LiPS solubility, such 
as DME/DOL electrolyte, the intrinsic LiPS flooding issue causes severe shuttle effect 
and cell-level heterogeneity. For conventional sparingly solvating electrolyte, such as 
localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE). Although the LiPS dissolution issue 
can be effectively addressed, the sluggish ion transport inside the cathode still results 
in severe cell-level heterogeneity as demonstrated in our HEXRD full-scale scanning 
results (Figure 6D,E). 
In our electrolyte design strategy, the quasi-solid-solid cathode transition can 
effectively eliminate the LiPS flooding issue. Additionally, the polysulfide-
incompatible additive can interact with sparingly dissolved LiPS to induce the in-situ 
CEI formation, which can build the ion transport highway inside the cathode and thus 
achieve a homogeneous reaction to relieve the cell-level heterogeneity.

Supplemental Experimental Procedures. 
After introducing 0.05 M LiBF4 additive, the interaction between sparingly dissolved 
LiPS and LiBF4 can thus induce the in-situ formation of CEI to promote the Li+ 
transportation inside the cathode. Therefore, the specific capacity of KB-Se/S cathode 
increased from 1162.8 mAh g-1 to 1291.5 mAh g-1. Further double (0.10 M) and triple 
(0.15 M) the amount of LiBF4 will trigger more reaction LiPS and LiBF4, which not 
only consume more active material but also jeopardize the CEI structure. As a result, 
when adopting the polysulfide-incompatible additive, the solvation structure (LiPS 
solubility) as well as the interaction level (additive concentration) should be carefully 
modified and controlled.

Supplemental Experimental Procedures. 
As shown in Figure S7, two distinct cathodic peaks at ~ 2.3 V and ~ 1.9 V can be 
observed for the cell with DME/DOL electrolyte, which can be attributed to the 
reduction of elemental sulfur to high order LiPS (Li2Sx, 4 ≤ x ≤ 8) and the further 
reduction to low order LiPS (Li2Sx, 1 ≤ x ≤ 4), respectively. By contrast, in the case of 
LAA-0.05 electrolyte, the CV curve only exhibits one reduction peak and one oxidation 
peak, indicating the direct solid-solid conversion process.
