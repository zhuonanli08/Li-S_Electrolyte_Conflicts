Li metal stability in the fluorinated ether electrolyte 
Colour of the separator does not drastically change during cycling, staying close to white as 
shown after post-mortem analysis of the cell (Figure S2). Here there are, however, also visible 
dendrites going through the separator, while the lithium is fragile with a mossy surface, and a 
drastically changed colour on the side facing the separator (Figure S2b). 
 
Figure S2. Post mortem analysis on the high energy Li-S battery cell, a) side of the anode not 
exposed to the separator after cycling, b) Li surface facing the separator after cycling, c) 
Celgard 2400 separator with the cathode facing side, d) Celgard 2400 separator with the 
anode facing side.  
Another important indicator of poor lithium stability is impedance spectroscopy analysis on 
symmetrical Li|Li cells at OCV. Two 2 cm2 Li electrodes were separated by one layer of 
Celgard 2400 separator and wetted with 20 µL of the chosen electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI 
TEGDME:DOL 1:1 or 1 M LiTFSI TFEE:DOL 1:1). The spectra were measured over a 
period of 50 hours from 20 kHz to 10 mHz with an amplitude of 10 mV (rms). In Figure S3, 
spectra at 1 h, 15 h, 25 h, 35 h, and 45 h are shown. For the “traditional” electrolyte, the 
passive layer on Li seems to be complex with resistances reaching up to 300 Ω (high 
frequency arc). On the other hand, the resistive intercept, which indicates the electrolyte 
conductivity, does not drastically change and stays close to its initial 5 Ω. For the TFEE:DOL

High energy Li–S battery cell with different electrolyte loadings 
 
Figure S4: Discharge capacity and coulombic efficiency for a cell with 5.75 µL of 
1 M LiTFSI in TFEE:DOL 1:1 electrolyte per mg S at C/10 rate. Electrode loading was 
4 mg S/cm2. 
 
In our case the limiting factor for sulfur utilization was tested by increasing the electrolyte 
amount and studying the performance of the initial cycle (Figure S5). Besides the beneficial 
effect of increasing the first discharge capacity (to 80% sulfur utilization), increasing the 
amount of electrolyte compromises the Coulombic efficiency, likely due to a higher amount 
of DOL present in the cell. The extent of capacity fade is similar in both cases. The cells with 
more electrolyte achieve longer cycle life (the cell does not “dry out”), but are thus in reality 
less energy dense as this is due to the large reserve of electrolyte.

10 µL/mgS of 1 M LiTFSI TFEE:DOL 1:1 (v:v) and b) C/10 discharge capacities comparison. 
One layer of Celgard 2400 separator was employed, the active mass loading was 4 mgS/cm2.

Li+ solvation investigation with FTIR spectroscopy 
The Li+ solvation was investigated by applying ATR-IR spectroscopy to the solvents TFEE 
and DOL, the TFEE:DOL 1:1 (v:v) solvent mixture, and the electrolytes created by dissolving 
LiTFSI in them (Figure S6). All spectra were measured using a Bruker Vertex 80 instrument 
equipped with a Specac Silver gate ATR with a Ge crystal and a liquid nitrogen cooled 
mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector in absorbance mode at a resolution of 2 cm–1 in 
the range of 500–4000 cm–1 (256 scans). Automatic baseline correction and atmospheric 
compensation processing of the spectra were done using the OPUS version 7.0 software. 
The molar ratio of LiTFSI in the solvents was the same as for 1 M LiTFSI in TFEE:DOL 1:1 
(v:v), except for LiTFSI in TFEE where a spectrum of the saturated solution was measured, 
since the solubility of the salt was low. The spectrum of LiTFSI in TFEE showed no 
differences as compared to the pure TFEE spectrum, indicating that nearly no LiTFSI 
dissolves in TFEE (Figure S6a). For LiTFSI in DOL some additional peaks are visible, the 
most prominent at 1353 cm-1 (TFSI: νip SO2), 1330 cm-1 (TFSI: νop SO2), 1195 cm-1 (TFSI), 
615 cm-1 (TFSI: δop SO2) and 574 cm-1 (δa CF3)1 (Figure S6b – noted with dashed black lines). 
These features are also seen when comparing LiTFSI in TFEE:DOL with the pure solvents 
(Figure S6c), which supports that solvation of Li+ in TFEE is poor and is rather promoted by 
DOL.

The νas C–O–C vibration of DOL at 1085 cm-1 is one of the very few vibrations sensitive for 
the interaction of DOL with Li+ and TFEE (Figure S7a,b). In order to distinguish overlapping 
adjacent bands, the 2nd derivative of the ATR-FTIR spectra were used for a more detailed 
analysis as well. Upon solvation of LiTFSI, the band shifts to higher wavenumbers, 
1087 cm-1, whereas in the presence of TFEE it is shifted slightly to a lower wavenumber, 
1084 cm-1. The change in frequency has been verified by DFT computations using Gaussian 
09 (G09 RevB.01) at B3LYP/3-611G(d,p) level of theory with the integral equation 
formalism for the polarizable continuum model (IEFPCM) and a dielectric constant of 7 
(dielectric constant of DOL) to simulate a liquid medium. Computation were performed for 
DOL (1091 cm-1), a mono-dentate Li+-DOL complex (1097 cm-1) and a DOL-TFEE-cluster 
(1088 cm-1). Even though the absolute vibrational frequencies slightly differ, the results are

XAS experimental details 
A Si (111) double crystal monochromator was used with ca. 0.3 eV resolution at 2.5 keV. 
Higher-order harmonics were effectively eliminated by a double-flat silica mirror placed at a 
grazing angle of 8 mrad. The intensity of the monochromatic X-ray beam before the sample 
was measured by a 30 cm long ionization chamber detector, filled with a mixture of 30 mbar 
of N2 and 1970 mbar of He. The fluorescence signal was detected with a silicon drift detector 
(KETEK GmbH AXAS-M with an area of 80 mm2). The battery prepared in an in situ 
Swagelok cell with 13 µm thick Be window was mounted on the sample holder in a chamber 
placed after the first ionization detector.2,3 The chamber was filled with He at 10 % 
atmospheric overpressure. 
The absorption spectra were measured within the interval of –150 to +300 eV relative to the S 
K-edge (2472 eV). In the XANES region up to 2490 eV, equidistant energy steps of 0.2 eV 
were used, whereas for higher energies, equidistant k steps of 0.03 Å–1 were adopted, with an 
integration time of 5 s per point. The exact energy calibration was established with an 
absorption measurement on native sulfur in transmission-detection mode. The maximum of 
the edge peak was set to 2472.0 eV.2,3 Absolute energy reproducibility of the measured 
spectra was ±0.05 eV or better. 
The XAS spectra were collected in operando mode. A series of spectra were collected during 
the discharge of the Li–S battery cell at the rate of C/20. The measurement time for one 
spectrum was 52 min. The analysis of the XAS spectra was performed with the Demeter 
program package ATHENA4. The sulfur K-edge XANES fluorescence spectra were corrected 
for dead time and normalized to a unit K-edge jump.2,3 The self-absorption (SA) correction 
was not used, because the composition of the cathode (ratio between sulfur, lithium, 
electrolyte, and CB) could not be determined precisely enough.2,3 
A linear combination fit (LCF) was performed in the energy interval 2452 eV to 2502 eV in 
order to obtain the relative amount of sulfur components in the measured spectra. Number of 
independent points in the fit of each 210 spectrum and the signal-to-noise ratio in the 
measured XANES spectra is very high. Consequently the uncertainty of each component in 
the linear combination fit is ±1% or lower.
