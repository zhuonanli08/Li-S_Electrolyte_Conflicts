SUMMARY
Rational electrolyte engineering for practical pouch cells remains elusive because the correlation between
the cathode/solid-electrolyte interphase layer and cell-level reaction behavior is poorly understood. Here,
by combining multiscale characterization and computational modeling, we show that—counter to the con-
ventional perception of polysulﬁde-incompatible additives—the spontaneous reaction of sparingly solvated
polysulﬁdes with Lewis acid additives (LAAs) can induce in situ formation of a homogeneous interphase on
thick and tortuous S cathode. Multiscale synchrotron X-ray characterization consistently afﬁrms that such
interface design could effectively eliminate the notorious problems of polysulﬁde shuttle and lithium corro-
sion and, more importantly, provide an interconnected ‘‘ion transport highway’’ to alleviate the uneven ion
transport within the tortuous S cathode. Hence, this design dramatically reduces the reaction heterogeneity
of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) pouch cells under lean electrolyte conditions. This work resolves controversy around
the role of polysulﬁde-incompatible additives in high-energy Li-S pouch cells and highlights the importance
of suppressing reaction heterogeneity for practical batteries.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are promising alternatives to
commercial lithium-ion batteries for ever-growing electric
vehicle applications due to their improved energy density (2–
3 times higher), reduced cost, and lower supply chain risk.1
Signiﬁcant efforts, including cathode design,2 electrolyte engi-
neering,3,4 and lithium protection,5–7 have been devoted to
mitigating the long-standing challenges of polysulﬁde (LiPS)
shuttling and lithium dendrite growth, with notable suc-
cess—in the laboratory. When these strategies are evaluated
in practical pouch cells, however, the results are often

Several phenomena contribute to poor performance in current
Li-S battery designs. (1) A serious problem that involves the
entire battery is polysulﬁde shuttling: LiPS species form at the
cathode, consume sulfur, and migrate through the separator to
the Li anode, where (in dissolved form) they contribute to corro-
sion of the anode and thus consumption of Li. The loss of inven-
tory S and Li shortens battery life. (2) At the Li anode, Li ions can
pile up into dendrites that extend from the surface. This dendrite
growth impedes Li ions from being efﬁciently and evenly plated
on and stripped from the anode surface during charging and dis-
charging. (3) At the cathode (a sulfur-carbon composite), the
issue is the high tortuosity of the thick electrode, leading to slug-
gish ion transport. Extra electrolyte is needed to fully penetrate
the composite and provide a continuous conductive pathway
for the Li ions. (4) Finally, when a battery design is scaled up to
practical dimensions (a pouch cell), the necessary reactions
may take place unevenly throughout the cell, an issue called re-
action heterogeneity. To achieve practical performance, these
processes must be controlled while also maximizing charge den-
sity, which means using as much sulfur as possible (to increase
charge) and as little electrolyte and Li metal as possible (to
reduce weight).
The electrochemical redox reactions of sulfur—in particular,
the liquid (e.g., Li2S6)-to-solid (Li2S) step that provides three-
quarters of theoretical capacity—take place on the heteroge-
neous liquid (electrolyte)/solid (Li2S)/solid (electrode) triple-
phase boundary,9,10 while the stripping/plating behavior of
lithium metal is governed by the physiochemical properties of
the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI).11,12 Note that the underly-
ing interfacial chemistries are signiﬁcantly different depending
on whether the cell is operating under high S loading/lean elec-
trolyte conditions or low loading/ﬂooded electrolyte conditions
(Figure S1); the former conditions remain poorly understood.
High S loading increases the porosity and tortuosity of the elec-
trode,13–15 which not only requires excess electrolyte ﬁlling to
fully wet the electrode but also leads to greater inhomogeneity
of ion/electron distribution across the entire electrode.16–18
Both effects signiﬁcantly change the properties of the cathode-
electrolyte interphase (CEI). On the other hand, the repeated
formation/dissolution of the SEI leads to a heterogeneous distri-
bution of electrolyte-derived components (e.g., LiF, Li2O, and
LixN) and to increased electrode thickness, thus challenging
the stripping/plating efﬁciency and reversibility of thin lithium
metal.19 Many reported electrolytes, such as concentrated20
and localized highly concentrated electrolytes,21 ﬂuorinated
electrolytes,22 and new solvents,23 can suppress the shuttle
effect and stabilize the Li anode to some extent,20,24,25 but the
reported cell performance under high S loading and low electro-
lyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio remains signiﬁcantly unsatisfactory.
Electrolyte additives,26 such as lithium tetraﬂuoroborate
(LiBF4),27–29 lithium bis(oxalate)borate,30,31 dual salts,32 and
others, have frequently been used in lithium-ion batteries to
construct a high-quality interface ﬁlm via self-sacriﬁcial decom-
position; the resulting ﬁlm improves the long-term cycling
stability. However, these additives have long been considered
unsuitable for Li-S batteries because of their poor chemical
compatibility with LiPS.25 For example, the Lewis acid nature
of LiBF4 triggers a chemical reaction with the commonly used
ether electrolyte in Li-S batteries (i.e., ring-opening polymeriza-
tion of 1,3-dioxolane [DOL] molecules). It also causes a reaction
with LiPS (a Lewis base), as shown in Equation 1, which was pro-
posed by Zhang et al.26,33:
LiBF4 + Li2Sn / LiBSnF2 + 2LiF
(Equation 1)
This reaction causes irreversible loss of LiPS and leads to
dramatic capacity decay during cycling,34,35 which has been
validated by a series of investigations using various analytical
characterization approaches.34,36,37 As an alternative, Li-S bat-
teries have been made with polysulﬁde-compatible addi-
tives.4,38–40 However, even with these more suitable additives,
the reported cycle life is still insufﬁcient. Moreover, the effects
of SEI/CEI on the cell-level reaction behavior of Li-S batteries
containing these alternative additives remain poorly understood.
Here, by utilizing a suite of synchrotron diffraction and absorp-
tion techniques with complementary interface characterization
and computational modeling, we systematically decipher an un-
expected role of polysulﬁde-incompatible additives: they sup-
press the reaction heterogeneity of Li-S batteries under high
cathode loading and lean electrolyte conditions via interaction
with the sparingly dissolved LiPS. Although this additive has
demonstrated chemical incompatibility with LiPS intermediates,
we show that—contrary to the conventional intuition—a pre-
cisely controlled reaction between sparingly solvated LiPS
(achieved by tailoring the solvation structures of the electrolyte)
and LiBF4 additive can induce in situ formation of homogeneous
interphase layers at thick and tortuous S cathode to construct an
ion transport highway (Figure 1A). Unlike the concept of using
carbonate-based electrolyte, which is also polysulﬁde-incom-
patible but highly relies on the use of short-chain S or sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile,41 our strategy based on ether solvents has no
limitation of S form and can achieve high cathode S content. In
addition to simultaneously suppressing both the shuttle effect
and lithium dendrite growth, these attributes dramatically reduce
reaction heterogeneity at the cell level, as evidenced by high-en-
ergy X-ray diffraction (HEXRD) mapping of Li-S pouch cells.
Meanwhile, the introduction of polysulﬁde-incompatible com-
pounds extends the electrolyte additive categories for further
Li-S battery development, and the CEI optimization can be
achieved by modifying the physicochemical properties of poly-
sulﬁde-incompatible additives and applying appropriate cath-
ode structure design. Different from conventional self-sacriﬁcial
additives, we believe this work is the ﬁrst demonstration of the
use of polysulﬁde-incompatible additives to induce the CEI for-
mation in Li-S batteries and offers new additive design strategies
as alternatives to enable high-energy and long-life Li-S batteries
with suppressed spatial reaction heterogeneity.

Interphase analysis
The chemical composition of the SEI and its spatial distribution
govern the interfacial ion transport kinetics and mechanical
properties60 and, hence, plays a crucial role in the Li stripping/
plating behavior of practical Li-S pouch cells, especially for
thin Li metal anodes. We evaluated Li stripping/plating stability
in both Li-Cu and Li-Li cells containing either the DME/DOL or
the LAA-0.05 electrolyte. As shown in Figures 4A–4C, compared
with the DME/DOL electrolyte, the Li-Cu cells with LAA-0.05
electrolyte exhibit improved Coulombic efﬁciency and longer
cycling stability (from 100 to 350 cycles). Furthermore, in the
Li-Li symmetrical cell test under a higher current density of 1
mA cm2 and a higher areal capacity of 3 mAh cm2
(Figures 4D and S15), the cell with LAA-0.05 electrolyte also dis-
played much lower overpotential (25 mV) and longer cycling
stability. Additionally, since the majority of LiBF4 is consumed
for CEI formation, the LAA-0 exhibits similar Li stripping/plating
performance and overpotential (30 mV) as the case with
LAA-0.05 electrolyte (Figure 4D). Although the LiBF4 additive
may have limited effectiveness in modifying anode interphase
properties, the cathode improvement originated from involving
LiBF4 still secures its necessity for Li-S batteries.
Li stripping/plating stability under low Li excess was further
evaluated because the use of a thin Li metal anode is vital to
achieving high-energy density.61 Typically, a cell with such an
anode will suffer from low cycling stability because of the intrinsic
high reactivity of Li metal with electrolytes. As shown in Fig-
ure S16, to maintain a 2 3 Li excess during the test, 9.0 mAh
cm2 of Li was deposited on the surface of Cu at a current den-
sity of 0.5 mA cm2 (Li-Cu cell). Afterward, the continuous Li
stripping/plating test (Li-Li cell) was conducted at 0.5 mA cm2
with an areal capacity of 3.0 mAh cm2. In LAA-0.05 electrolytes,
the cell with 2 3 Li excess can be stably cycled over 550 h with a
small overpotential of 25 mV (Figure S16). The Li cycling stabil-
ity was further evaluated with only 1 3 Li excess and a higher
areal capacity of 4 mAh cm2 (Figure 4E); this cell still exhibited
stable cycling performance for over 450 h with a slightly
increased overpotential of 30 mV, conﬁrming that the good Li
cycling stability and suppressed Li side reaction are due to the
formation of a robust SEI in the presence of the LAA-0.05 elec-
trolyte. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) char-
acterizations of cycled Li metal foils from Li-Li symmetric cells
with both DME/DOL and LAA-0.05 electrolytes (Figure S17)
further validate the improved Li stripping/plating homogeneity
in LAA-0.05 electrolyte.

Pouch cell evaluation
Reaction homogeneity at the cell level is an important factor in
evaluating realistic pouch cells, but it has been signiﬁcantly over-
looked in Li-S cell development, which has led to a huge gap be-
tween laboratory research and practical cell development. We
used synchrotron HEXRD mapping (Figure 6A) to examine dis-
charged (1.2 V) Li-Se/S pouch cells with DME/DOL, LAA-0,
and LAA-0.05 electrolytes. The distribution of the peaks of Li2S
(111) and S (222) across the entire pouch cell is used to represent
reaction heterogeneity within the cell. Because LiPS is highly sol-
uble in the DME/DOL electrolyte, the S active material undergoes
a series of dissolution-migration-deposition processes during
operation. Therefore, as shown in Figures 6B and S32, a non-uni-
form distribution of Li2S was observed after only one discharge
process, with the majority of the discharge products concen-
trated on the edge of the electrode. Moreover, as shown in the
region outlined in white in Figure 6C, even at a cutoff voltage of
1.2 V, there is still a lot of S detected at the two edges of the elec-
trode (a detailed stacking plot is shown in Figure S32), indicating
the insufﬁcient reduction of S at the practical cell level. In addi-
tion, since the S residual region overlaps with some of the highly
concentrated Li2S region (two edges of the electrode), there is a
large chance that dead S will form during prolonged cycling,
which will further aggravate reaction heterogeneity and eventu-
ally affect the Li distribution on the anode side, accelerating
cell failure (Figure S33). In the case with LAA-0 electrolyte,
because of the suppressed LiPS solubility, the Li2S distribution
uniformity was greatly improved (Figure 6D) compared with
that of DME/DOL electrolyte. However, the S residual region
can still be found on the discharged cathode (Figure 6E), indi-
cating the insufﬁcient reduction of active material and severe
cathode reaction heterogeneity. By sharp contrast, due to the
synergistic cooperation of improved ion transport, suppressed
LiPS formation, and improved lithium stripping/plating enabled
by the LAA-0.05 electrolyte, the reaction homogeneity and the
uniformity of the active material distribution were greatly
improved: the Li2S signal is homogeneous distributed with no
obvious Li2S agglomeration on the discharged S cathode

DISCUSSION
In summary, through a combination of advanced multiscale di-
agnostics and computational modeling, we have discovered an
unexpected role for supposedly polysulﬁde-incompatible addi-
tives: in an appropriately designed electrolyte, they can sup-
press the reaction heterogeneity of Li-Se/S pouch cells under
high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte conditions. This behavior
emerged in the presence of an electrolyte whose structure was
tailored to suppress the solvation of polysulﬁdes, thereby mini-
mizing side reactions with the LAA. The spontaneous reaction
of the LAA—in this case, LiBF4—with the sparingly solvated pol-
ysulﬁdes induces in situ formation of cathode/solid-electrolyte
homogeneous interphases to construct the ionic transport high-
way. Furthermore, we showed that such interphase design could
address several formidable challenges of high-energy Li-S bat-
teries at one stroke, including the notorious issue of polysulﬁde
shuttle, as well as uncontrolled lithium nucleation/growth, slug-
gish ion transport within a tortuous S electrode, and severe reac-
tion heterogeneity at the practical cell level. Our work indicates
an urgent need to revisit the roles of electrolyte additives;
in particular, the newly developed polysulﬁde-incompatible
additives should be studied, rather than focusing on traditional
self-sacriﬁcial additives only. Our work also indicates that
suppression of reaction heterogeneity is required during the
cycling of high-energy Li-Se/S pouch cells to maximize the cycle
life.

additive.
