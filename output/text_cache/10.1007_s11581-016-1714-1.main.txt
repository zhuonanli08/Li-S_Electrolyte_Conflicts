SHORT COMMUNICATION
Concentrated lithium salt-monoglyme complex for restraining
polysulfide dissolution and improving electrochemical
performance of lithium-sulfur batteries
Hai Lu1 & Yan Yuan2 & Qinghao Yang1 & Zhenzhong Hou1 & Yanqing Lai3
Received: 17 December 2015 /Revised: 7 March 2016 /Accepted: 17 April 2016 /Published online: 23 April 2016
# Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2016
Abstract Concentrated lithium salt-monoglyme complex
was used as an electrolyte candidate of lithium-sulfur (Li-S)
batteries. It is found that the limited content of monoglyme
suppresses the solubility and shuttle of polysulfides in the
electrolyte. Then 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl
ether (TFTFE) was selected as a second solvent of the com-
plex, and the effect of adding TFTFE on electrolyte proper-
ties and cell performances was investigated. The results in-
dicate that the fluorinated ether further reduces the dissolu-
tion of polysulfides and improves the specific capacity and
cycle stability of the cells. Certain polarization appears in
the discharge stage if using excess TFTFE, but it is nearly
harmless to the operation of the cell at higher rates. The
combination of concentrated complex and fluorinated ether
in the electrolyte is an effective approach to achieve excel-
lent performance for Li-S batteries.
Keywords Electrolyte . Fluorinated ether . Monoglyme .
Polysulfide . Lithium-sulfur battery
Introduction
Over the past several decades, plenty of research efforts
have been devoted to developing advanced rechargeable
batteries [1]. As a special example, lithium-sulfur (Li-S)
battery has exhibited an encouraging prospect due to its
distinct advantages below. Firstly, sulfur as a kind of
common light-weight material has the merits of abundant
resource, low cost, and environmental friendliness. What
is more, the complete reaction between sulfur and lithium
can obtain a high theoretical gravimetric capacity of
1675 mAh g−1 and theoretical energy density of
2500 Wh kg−1 [2], which are several times higher than
those conventional secondary batteries. These merits
make the Li-S battery a promising candidate for energy
storage devices of electric vehicles.
However, the redox process of Li-S battery involves a
series of polysulfides (Li2Sn, 2 ≤n ≤8) as intermediate
product [3]. Most of them are soluble in common organic
electrolytes, leading to the loss of active species. The dis-
solved Li2Sn then diffuses to Li anode due to concentration
gradient and reacts with it, corroding and even destroying
the anode structure. After that, the formed short-chain Li2Sn
will come back to the cathode and be oxidized again. The
repeated Li2Sn shuttle arises thereby between the cathode
and anode [4], giving rise to the decrease in the discharge
capacity and increase in the charging capacity, that is, low
coulombic efficiency.
As Li2Sn dissolution and shuttle all happen in the electro-
lyte, the electrolyte constitution greatly determines the be-
havior of Li2Sn. Glymes (e.g., monoglyme-G1, tetraglyme-
G4) with high donor ability have been used to dissolve Li
salts widely in Li-S cells [5, 6], but Li2Sn is easily dissolved
in those glyme-based electrolytes meanwhile. Decreasing

the solvent amount is reported to be effective for restricting
the solubility of Li2Sn. For example, solvated ionic liquid
Li(G3 or G4)1X [7] controls the molar ratio of [O]/[Li+] in
the electrolyte to be 4 or 5, which is the coordination number
of Li+. Thus, the lone pairs of the glyme oxygen are all do-
nated to Li+ theoretically and no more solvent molecule
serves as dissociating Li2Sn. As a result, the dissolution
and shuttle of Li2Sn is greatly suppressed [8]. However, the
cellcapacityisstillabitlowduringcyclingforsolvatedionic
liquids [9], and the high viscosity of the solutions is always
unfavorable for the rate capability of the cell.
Another similar case is concentrated solution Li(G1)2X,
in which G1 forms a 1:2 complex with Li salt and the molar
ratio of [O]/[Li+] reaches 4 as well. The complex based on
short-chain G1 has better ion conductivity than the solvated
ionic liquids [10]. Nevertheless, it has been found that the
lifetime of complex cation Li(G1)2
+ is very short because
the ion-dipole interaction (Li+-G1) is hard to overwhelm the
coulombic interaction between Li+ and X−[10]. Therefore,
a certain amount of free G1 is present in the highly concen-
trated Li(G1)2X, which contributes to the solvation (or dis-
sociation) of Li2Sn. The Li-S cell in Li(G1)2X shows a high
initial capacity but weak capacity retention over long-term
charge-discharge cycles [9].
Herein, our work continues to employ Li(G1)2X as an
electrolyte candidate. The merits and drawbacks of the con-
centrated solution applied in Li-S batteries were examined and
discussed. In order to further restrain polysulfide dissolution
and improve cell performance, a novel fluorinated ether
named 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether
(TFTFE) was then added into the electrolyte as a second sol-
vent. The effects of TFTFE on electrolyte properties and cell
performances were investigated preliminarily in this study.
Experimental
Electrolyte preparation and cell assembly
The electrolytes for the experiment were prepared in an argon-
filled glove box, in which oxygen and water contents were less
than 1 ppm. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI,
Aldrich) and G1 (Adamas) were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:2 and
magnetically stirred overnight. Then the stoichiometric TFTFE
(Adamas) was added into the above complex Li(G1)2TFSI to
obtain Li(G1)2TFSI-xTF (LiTFSI:G1:TFTFE = 1:2:x, molar ratio,
x = 1, 6). For comparison, the solvent-rich electrolyte with a molar
ratio of 1:8 (LiTFSI/G1) was also prepared (Li(G1)8TFSI).
Sulfur/carboncomposite(79.1%sulfur,accordingtothemeth-
oddescribedpreviously[11]),carbonblack(SuperP,Timcal),and
poly(vinylidenefluoride)(PVDF,6020Solef)(80:10:10wt.)were
mixed homogeneously in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) solution.
Theelectrodewasfabricatedbyspreadingtheobtainedslurryonto
an aluminum foil and dried at 60 °C under vacuum. The typical
sulfur loading on each electrode is ∼1.2 mg cm−2(the electrode
area is ∼0.785 cm2). Li-S coin cells were assembled in the glove
box by using the above electrode as cathode, Li foil as anode, and
filter paper (GA55, Advantec) as the separator. The electrolyte
amount in each cell was controlled to be ∼16 mL g−1 (the ratio of
electrolyte volume to sulfur mass in the cathode).
Physical and electrochemical characterizations
The oxidation potentials of the electrolytes were measured by
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of stainless steel (SS)/Li
cells at an electrochemical workstation (Solartron 1470E).
The sweep rate is set as 5 mV s−1. The solubility experiments
of polysulfide in the electrolytes were conducted as follows. S
(Aldrich) and Li2S (Aldrich) with a molar ratio of 7:1 were
mixed and added in each electrolyte (the theoretical content of
Li2S8 was controlled as 0.25 mol L−1). The color changes of
the solutions with time were recorded by a digital camera.
The charge-discharge tests of Li-S cell were performed in
the potential range of 1.5∼3.0 V under a LAND CT2001A
tester. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was ex-
amined on the electrochemical workstation with a frequency
range of 105∼0.01 Hz and perturbation amplitude of 5 mV.
Results and discussion
The charge-discharge behaviors of Li-S cells in two electro-
lytes with different monoglyme contents were studied at
first. For Li(G1)8TFSI (Fig. 1a), the overcharge degree en-
larges gradually with cycle and the charge capacity exceeds
the theoretical value at the 11th cycle. The Li(G1)8TFSI
solution containing Li2S8 after standing for 2 h and separa-
tor from the cycled cell all present as dark (Fig. 1c), which
means that long-chain polysulfides are extremely inclined
to move out the cathode and dissolve into the electrolyte
with excess G1. The dissolved polysulfides generate unde-
sired shuttle reactions subsequently, deteriorating cell oper-
ation [12]. The continuous accumulation of the negative
effect during cycling leads to the overcharge out of control
eventually. By contrast, high charge-discharge efficiency is
maintained in the cell with Li(G1)2TFSI over the cycle pro-
cess (Fig. 1b). The significantly reduced the overcharge ca-
pacity originated from the decrease in solvent amount, be-
cause limited G1 dissolves polysulfides inadequately and
thus diminishes the solubility and shuttle of polysulfides
in the electrolyte, as shown in Fig. 1d. However, the cycle
performance is still unsatisfactory when the cell employs
the concentrated solution, which should be ascribed to the
presence of unstable complex cation and free G1 solvent, as
discussed previously.

Next, the fluorinated ether of TFTFE was added into the
above concentrated electrolyte. The reasons for selecting
TFTFE as the second solvent of Li(G1)2TFSI lie in its low
donor ability, high miscibility with glyme, and favorable
chemical and electrochemical stability in the Li-S system
[13]. Figure 2a exhibits the solubility experiment pictures
of Li2Sn in the electrolyte with and without fluorinated
ether. The Li(G1)2TFSI containing Li2S8 takes on yellow
at the beginning of standing and the color deepens gradually
with time. So the dissolution of Li2Sn still exists to some
extent for Li(G1)2TFSI. Once using TFTFE as the second
solvent, the Li(G1)2TFSI-xTF solution turns to be lighter
than Li(G1)2TFSI. Especially Li(G1)2TFSI-6TF is almost
colorless and has no change after standing for 6 days. It
suggests that the solubility of Li2Sn is further reduced in
the TFTFE-containing electrolyte. As we know, the fluori-
nated ether is always too fluorinated to participate in Li+
solvation [13]. Hence, TFTFE with low donor ability con-
tributes to weaker dissociation of Li2Sn compared to
Li(G1)2TFSI. The oxidative stabilities of various electro-
lytes were examined by the LSV method and the results
are shown in Fig. 2b. It can be seen that the anodic current
(caused by irreversible decomposition of the solvent [14])
shifts to positive potential as the G1 amount decreases,
reflecting the lessened activity of G1 in the electrolyte
[15]. Adding TFTFE moves the anodic limit to a low poten-
tial slightly, but the oxidation potential is held at ∼5 V vs. Li/
Li+ regardless of TFTFE content. So the presence of TFTFE
in the concentrated electrolyte meets the demand for the
operation of Li-S batteries.
The cycle performances of Li-S cells with various electro-
lytes are compared in Fig. 3a, b. For the cell with Li(G1)2TFSI,
the capacity fades rapidly from the beginning and the reserved
capacity is only 425 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles. The coulombic
efficiency sustains at ∼94 % during cycling. When adding
1:1 molar ratio of TFTFE (Li(G1)2TFSI-1TF), the cyclability
of the cell is improved effectively: an initial capacity of up to
1196 mAh g−1 and reversible capacity of ∼550 mAh g−1 after
50 cycles are obtained. In addition, the two discharge plateaus
(corresponding to two-step reduction reactions of sulfur with
metallic Li) keep quite stable over the whole cycle period
(Fig. 3c). Since TFTFE can further limit the dissolution
of Li2Sn in the electrolyte, it is reasonable that more sulfur
species are utilized and more capacities are delivered in
Li(G1)2TFSI-1TF compared with Li(G1)2TFSI. At the mo-
ment, plenty of Li2Sn should be immobilized within the cathode
and their electrochemical reactions occurred mainly in the solid
phase due to the contribution of TFTFE [16]. Continuing to
increase the TFTFE ratio in the electrolyte (Li(G1)2TFSI-6TF),
the capacity retention declines instead compared to other two
cases, even though the high initial capacity still appears and the
coulombic efficiency further grows to 97∼98 %. It is possible
that excess TFTFE decreases Li+ concentration in the electro-
lyte and thus weakens the capacity transport of the cell.
Besides, a bit lower potential plateau appears in the discharge
process for the cell with Li(G1)2TFSI-6TF (Fig. 3d), revealing

a certain polarization due to the high content of the fluorinated
ether. Therefore, it can be summarized that less dissolution of
Li2Sn or higher coulombic efficiency does not always mean
better performance for Li-S batteries [17].
To get an insight on the polarization issue caused by fluo-
rinated ether, the rate capabilities of the cells with various
electrolytes were measured. It is observed from Fig. 4a that
the specific capacities for various electrolytes at the same dis-
charge rates show little difference (the charge rate is uniformly
0.1 C). A stable specific capacity of ∼500 mAh g−1 is released
when the current is adjusted to 0.5 C in the cells with TFTFE,
a bit higher than that without it. The cycle capabilities of the
cells with various electrolytes at a charge-discharge rate of 0.3
C are also very close to each other (Fig. 4b), despite that the
polarization depresses the discharge plateau in the case of the
electrolyte containing a high content of TFTFE (Fig. 4c). On
the whole, using the fluorinated ether as the second solvent is
nearly harmless to the operation of the cell at higher rates.

Figure 4d is the EIS plots of the cell with various electro-
lytes. All of them are composed of a semicircle in the high
frequency and an inclined line in the low frequency, which
represents interface charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and
Warburg impedance (Wo), respectively [18]. The high-
frequency intercept on the real axis is associated with the
ohmic resistance (Ro), including the electrolyte and electrode
resistances [19]. The decrease in Rct for TFTFE-contained
electrolytes compared to Li(G1)2TFSI means that TFTFE fa-
vors the penetration of the electrolyte into the electrode and
improves the electrochemical environment within the cell
[20]. However, the Ro value changes little, implying that the
fluorinated ether has no adverse influence on the ionic con-
duction of the electrolyte, which should be a key reason why
the rate performances of three cells are similar.
Conclusions
Concentrated complex Li(G1)2TFSI was applied in Li-S bat-
teries. Although the electrolyte containing limited solvent of
G1 restrains the solubility and shuttle of Li2Sn, cell capacity
still fades rapidly over the repeated cycles. Adding TFTFE in
the electrolyte can further decrease the Li2Sn dissolution, thus
promoting the utilization of active material and delivering
more reversible capacity during cycling. The reserved capac-
ity reaches ∼550 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles for the cell with
Li(G1)2TFSI-1TF. The fluorinated ether gives rise to a certain
polarization within the cell, which depresses the discharge
plateau. But it almost has no effect on the rate capability of
the cell. The combination of concentrated complex and fluo-
rinated ether constitutes a novel polysulfide-restraining elec-
trolyte for advanced Li-S batteries.
