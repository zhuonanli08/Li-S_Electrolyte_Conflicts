ABSTRACT: Unclear regulation mechanisms of solid solubility
make it difficult to accurately control its concentration, posing
challenges for accelerating chemical reactions. Herein, we use a
model system to determine the effects of solvent type and ratio,
concentration, and type of lithium salt on sulfur solubility. Our
findings reveal a nonlinear relationship between sulfur solubility
and both lithium salt concentration and the ratio of different
solvents in composite solutions. Importantly, significant differences
in sulfur solubility are discovered with changes in the solvation
structure regulated by the type of salt anion. Quantitative
evaluation of nonlinear sulfur solubility is illustrated to be breaking
the threshold value of the interaction energy between the solute and solvent molecules. Based on these findings, simultaneous
regulation of sulfur and lithium polysulfide concentrations in ether electrolytes can be achieved by controlling the concentration and
type of salts. As a result, a liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway is supported for lithium−sulfur batteries with a finite amount of
electrolyte in the coin cell, and the pouch cell shows an energy density of 554 Wh kg−1, ranking at the top level of the reported
system.
■INTRODUCTION
Regulating the solubility of solid solutes has important
applications in many fields such as substance separation1−3
and reaction process regulation.4−6 It plays a crucial role in
improving separation process efficiency and reducing costs,7,8
as well as accurately controlling chemical reaction rates.9,10 In
1878, Van’t Hoff studied the relationship between solid solute
solubility and temperature and proposed the Van’t Hoff
equation. These results indicate that temperature can regulate
solute solubility by changing the intra- and intermolecular
forces between solvents and/or solutes. Compared to the
influence of external factors such as temperature and pressure,
controlling the internal chemical composition of the solution
can provide a more direct way to achieve significant changes in
solute solubility by regulating the strength of the interaction
forces between the solute and solution components. For
example, Guarr and Hickey et al. showed that the introduction
of C−H···π weak dispersion force interactions in pyridinium-
based compounds can weaken intermolecular electrostatic
interactions, thus enhancing their solubility in acetonitrile and
maximizing the energy density of redox flow battery.11
Therefore, it is very important to precisely control the solid
solute concentration to provide an important value for the
accurate control of the reaction rate. However, the influence of
solute, solvent, and ion interaction strengths on solute
solubility in solution is still needed for a deep understanding.
According to classical theories, the dissolution of solid
substances is a competitive process between entropy and
enthalpy changes.12 During the dissolution process, breaking
the interaction between solvents or solutes is an endothermic
process, while constructing the interaction bonds between
solutes and solvents is an exothermic process, where the
enthalpy change is less than zero. Therefore, reducing the
strength of intermolecular forces between solutes or enhancing
the strength of solvent and solute interactions in a solution is
key to increasing solute solubility. Based on this understanding,
strategies, including weakening solute interactions and
enhancing solvent and solute interactions, have been proposed
to regulate solute solubility in solutions. For example, Yagai et
al. demonstrated that the methylation of barbiturate π-
conjugated compounds can reduce the intermolecular hydro-
gen-bonded type of barbiturate solutes and inhibit the
formation of polycrystals, thereby improving their solubility
in nonpolar solvents.13 Rimer et al. found that the adsorption
of citrate inhibitors on the surface of calcium oxalate
monohydrate (COM) crystals weakened the interaction of
COM solute due to changes in the local lattice strain, thereby
increasing the COM concentration in solutions.14 In order to
enhance the interaction between solvents and solutes,

It should be noted that previous studies mainly focused on
the regulation of the intra- and intermolecular interaction
strength between a single solvent and solute on their solubility
from a qualitative or quantitative perspective. In complex
mixed solutions, solute solubility may be affected not only by
the solvent itself but also by salt anions and cations. The
mechanism for regulating solubility in composite solutions is
still unclear, making it difficult to accurately control the solute
concentration. The qualitative and quantitative evaluation of
chemical compositions on solute solubility in complex
solutions will provide a theoretical basis and technical means
for the precise control of the chemical reaction rate and
efficiency of substance separation.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
S8 Solubility in a Composite Solution. The solubility of
solid S8 in ether solvents, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and
1,3-dioxolane (DOL), was primarily studied at room temper-
ature because ethers are commonly used as electrolyte solvents
in Li−S batteries. Raman spectra analysis shows that the
solubility of S8 (characteristic peaks: 150, 220, and 456
cm−1)16 is 5.1 mg mL−1 in DOL and 3.65 mg mL−1 in DME
(Figure S1). With the addition of LiTFSI, the solubilities of S8
in DOL solutions containing 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 M LiTFSI
are 4.3, 4.0, 3.7, and 1.0 mg mL−1, respectively (Figures 1d and
S2). It was found that S8 solubility decreased slightly with
increasing LiTFSI concentration in the range of 0.2−0.8 M.
However, the solubility of S8 decreases sharply when the
LiTFSI concentration changes from 0.8 to 1.0 M. These results
indicate that there is a nonlinear solubility phenomenon
(Figure 1e). We also analyzed S8 solubility in DME solvent
with 1.0, 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide
(LiFSI), and a nonlinear solubility phenomenon was also
found (Figure S3). Moreover, in order to further verify the
solubility effect of temperature dependence, we tested S8
solubility at 50 °C, which still shows a nonlinear solubility
phenomenon, proving its universality (Figure S4).
To illustrate the mechanism of the nonlinear S8 solubility
phenomenon in complex solutions with solvents and salts, we
used DOL and CS2 as strongly and weakly polarized solvents,
respectively, to explore the effect of the mixed solvent ratio on
S8 solubility, in which CS2 and DOL serve as good and poor
solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively. This shows that S8
solubilities in DOL and CS2 solvents are about ∼5.1 and ∼60.0
mg mL−1 at room temperature, respectively. When the molar
ratios of CS2 and DOL change to 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, 4:6, 3:7,
and 2:8, S8 solubilities are 59, 57, 55, 51, 20, 14, and 12 mg
mL−1, respectively. This demonstrates that S8 solubility slightly
changes with a change in the ratio of CS2:DOL from 1:0 to
5:5. However, when the ratio of CS2:DOL changes to 4:6, S8
solubility decreases sharply (20 mg mL−1). When the ratio
further decreases, the solubility changes slightly (Figure 1f).
Furthermore, the measured dielectric constant of the DOL/
CS2 solution shows a nearly linear trend with regard to their
ratio, again verifying no relationship between them (Figure 1f).
To check the nonlinear S8 solubility in composite solvents, we
used classical molecular dynamics LAMMPS to simulate S8
solubility in DOL/CS2 solvents with different molar ratios.
This shows that S8 solubility in a pure CS2 solvent and mixed
CS2/DOL solvent (8:2) is close to 50 mg mL−1. When the
ratio is 5:5, S8 is still in the soluble state (50 mg mL−1). In
contrast, when mixed solvents with a ratio of 2:8 or pure DOL
are selected, a large number of S8 molecules precipitate, which
is consistent with the experimental results (Figures 1g and S5).
It is worth emphasizing that this nonlinear solubility
phenomenon is also found in other systems, for example, the
nonlinear solubility of rosin in mixed solvents of water and
ethanol (Figures 1h, S6 and S7). This confirms the broad
existence of such phenomena.
To reveal the essence of nonlinear solubility, we analyzed
the interaction energy and interaction force between the
solvent and solute molecules. As known, when a solid
transforms into a molten liquid, external input interaction
energy is needed to reduce the intermolecular forces of the
solid. For example, the interaction energy for the melting heat
of S8 from solid to liquid is 1.7175 kJ mol−1. Similarly, the
dissolution of the S8 solute in the solvent also requires the
input of interaction energy to reduce the interaction forces of
S8 molecules in the solid. When S8 solute dissolves in solvents,
the required interaction energy comes from the van der Waals
forces formed between the solvent and S8 molecules. For S8
dissolution in CS2-DOL solvents, CS2 and DOL act as good
and poor solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively. S8 solubility
will quickly decrease when the interaction force of the mixed
solvents and S8 is weaker than the interaction force of the two
S8 molecules. We use the classical molecular dynamics
simulation method (LAMMPS) to calculate the interaction
energy. According to the constructed model, we obtain the
average interaction energy of the extra input to dissolve one S8
molecule (Edis or Edis′, and the calculation details of Edis and
Edis′ are shown in Supporting Note 2). The average energy
released from the interaction of one CS2 molecule and one S8
molecule is Ea, and the energy released from one DOL
molecule and one S8 molecule is Eb (Eb < Ea). When the
average action energy of the solvent on the solute is greater
than the average energy required for the solute to dissociate a
molecule, the solubility of the solute will be high. Therefore, if
xEa + yEb < Edis (Edis′) is satisfied, S8 will precipitate. Herein, x
and y are the coordinated numbers of the CS2 and DOL
molecules on the S8 molecule. According to the calculation
results (details in Supporting Note 2), Ea is 3.28 × 10−2 kcal
mol−1, Eb is 1.1 × 10−2 kcal mol−1, and Edis is 1.2198 kcal
mol−1 (Edis′ = 1.48 kcal mol−1). Moreover, the relationship
between the average energy of one solute molecule and the
molar ratio of the two solvents can be obtained by the equation

S8 solubility in CS2 is evaluated to be ∼60 mg mL−1 at room
temperature. This means that one S8 molecule dissolves into
71 CS2 molecules. That is, the average solvation structure of
CS2 and S8 can be hypothesized to be a 71-bound structure
(71 CS2−S8). With CS2 as the pure solvent, the interaction
energy released from 71 CS2 molecules and one S8 molecule is
71*Ea (2.329 kcal mol−1). Because 71*Ea > Edis (2.329 >
1.2198) and 71*Ea > Edis′ (2.329 > 1.48) are present, the
solubility of S8 in the CS2 solvent is high. When the ratio of
CS2 to DOL is 5:5, the interaction energy released from 35.5
CS2 and 35.5 DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 35.5*Ea +
35.5*Eb (1.555 kcal mol−1). Because 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis
(1.555 > 1.2198) and 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis′ (1.555 > 1.48)
are still present, S8 solubility in the CS2/DOL (5:5) solvents is
also high (about 51 mgS mL−1), which is a little lower than that
in pure CS2. In stark contrast, when the ratio of CS2 to DOL is
4:6, the interaction energy released from 28.4 CS2 and 42.6
DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 1.40 kcal mol−1. Because
this value is lower than Edis′ (1.40 < 1.48), there is a significant
difference in S8 solubility (about 20 mg mL−1), which is much
lower than that in CS2 (Figure 1f,g). The above estimation of
input interaction energy for solute dissolution in a solvent can
explain the nonlinear solubility phenomenon well, confirming
that the enthalpy change is the core factor affecting S8
solubility in this system (Figures 1i and S8). The interaction
energy for the fast change in solute solubility, which is due to
breaking intramolecular forces of the solute through the
intermolecular action between solvent and solute, relates to the
threshold value of the interaction energy.

In one previous study of Li−S batteries at the condition of
high S loading and low E/S ratio performed by our group,17 we
found that the rate of the S ↔Li2S interconversion reaction
was controlled by the activation of S8 in the first S8 →Li2S8
step. The S8 activation ratio (Sact) can be determined by
evaluating the actual and theoretical quantity ratios of the
transferred electrons of the S8 →Li2S4 reaction (Sact = Eactual(S8
→Li2S4)/Etheorectical (S8→Li2S4)). Moreover, the S8 utilization
ratio (Suti) can be determined by evaluating the actual and
theoretical quantity ratios of the transferred electrons of the
total S8 →Li2S reaction, Suti = Eactual(S8 →Li2S)/Etheorectical(S8
→Li2S). The detailed calculation can be found in ref 17. The
changes in Sact and Suti can be used as descriptors to evaluate
the increased factor of the reaction rate for the total S
reduction reaction. Then, we calculated Sact and Suti at different
scan rates to quantitatively evaluate the increase of the reaction
rate (Figure 2d,e). At a low scan rate of 0.05 mV s−1, Sact and
Suti values were calculated to be 50 and 50% at 0.8 M LiTFSI,
respectively, which are higher than those with 1 M LiTFSI
(41.7 and 40%). At a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, Sact and Suti
values (45 and 23%) with 0.8 M LiTFSI are much higher than
those with 1 M LiTFSI (32 and 13%). These results
demonstrate that the electrolyte with 0.8 M LiTFSI can
achieve effective S dissolution and thus increase the reaction
rate (Figure 2g,h). This reaction pathway was further proven
by the S8 solubility test on a solid substrate (Figures S14 and
S15). However, note that the second Pc2 reduction peak with
0.8 M LiTFSI cannot appear well at a scan rate of 0.15 mV s−1,
and the Sact and Suti values are also very low. This indicates a
low reaction rate, which may be related to the limited Li2Sx
amount in 0.8 M LiTFSI-based electrolyte during the redox
reactions.
In order to confirm the above processes, we investigated the
disproportionation reaction rates of S and Li2S in ether
electrolytes with 1.0 and 0.8 M LiTFSI (DOL/DME = 2:1, v/
v). Due to the long reaction time and volatilization of the ether
solvent, the Li2Sx solution in DOL/DME (1:1) is highly
viscous, and it cannot accurately evaluate the disproportiona-
tion reaction and Li2Sx solubility at different time scales.
Therefore, we used DOL/DME solvent at a ratio of 2:1 (v/v)
to determine the formation rate of Li2Sx (Figure S16). The
optical photographs show that the color of the Li2Sx solution
with 0.8 M LiTFSI diluted 200 times is darker than that with
1.0 M LiTFSI in the primary state. In the ultraviolet−visible
(UV−vis) spectra, the intensity of the absorption peak of S4
2−
(420 nm) is also higher than that with 1.0 M LiTFSI. These
results demonstrate that the increase of S8 solubility accelerates
the disproportionation reaction and the generation of Li2Sx
(Figure S17). We further determined Li2Sx concentrations at
different time scales, and the higher concentration of Li2Sx
solution with 0.8 M LiTFSI was found in the primary 0−16 h.
However, after 16 h, the concentration of the Li2Sx solution
with 1.0 M LiTFSI is higher than that with 0.8 M LiTFSI
(Figures 2f and S18). It may be due to the fact that more Li+
formed Li3S6
+ with Li2S6 when the LiTFSI concentration is 1.0
M. In short, due to the insulation property of S/Li2S, the
conventional solid−liquid−solid reaction path with limited
long-range electron transfer occurs in the electrolyte with a
limited amount of unpolarized ether (1 M LiTFSI, Figure 2j).
In stark contrast, due to the improved S8 solubility in the
electrolyte with an increased amount of unpolarized ether (0.8
M LiTFSI), the liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway with an
enhanced long-range electron transfer route is constructed.
Accelerated Reaction Probed by In Situ Raman
Spectra. To confirm the liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway
initiated by the increased S8 solubility, we used in situ Raman
spectroscopy to probe the cathode reaction process of Li−S
batteries. At a discharge current density of 1C, the S8 peaks
located at 220 and 476 cm−1 appear at the open-circuit voltage
(OCV). With 1 M LiTFSI in the electrolyte, S8 peaks can still
be detected at a discharge voltage of 2.40 V. In addition, the
weak Li2Sx peaks (398 and 450 cm−1)18 can be detected at
2.35 V, and Li2Sx can still be detected at a discharge voltage of
1.86 V (Figure 3a). This suggests that the reduction reaction
rate is low and S is not completely reduced. In contrast, the S8
peak cannot be detected at 2.40 V with 0.8 M LiTFSI in the
electrolyte, and Li2Sx peaks can be detected at 2.40 V, much
earlier and stronger than those with 1.0 M LiTFSI. Moreover,
Li2Sx peaks completely disappear at 1.91 V, indicating
complete S reduction due to a greatly increased reaction rate
(Figure 3c). Compared to the discharge capacity of the battery
with 1.0 M LiTFSI (746 mAh g−1), the higher value with 0.8
M LiTFSI (928 mAh g−1) also indicates a faster reaction rate
(Figure 3b,d).
Similar to the discharge process, with 1 M LiTFSI, the Li2Sx
peaks can be detected at a potential of 2.38 V during the
charging process, relating to the high charging potential
(Figure 3e). No S8 substance was observed at the end of
charging, indicating a low reaction rate. However, with 0.8 M
LiTFSI, a strong Li2Sx peak can be detected at a charging
potential of 2.35 V, 30 mV earlier than that with 1 M LiTFSI
(Figure 3g). Moreover, the S8 peaks appear at 2.80 V. This
indicates a higher reaction rate of 0.8 M LiTFSI than that of 1
M LiTFSI, consistent with their different charging voltages
(Figure 3f,h).
To further clarify the effect of increased S8 solubility on the
reaction pathway, we performed morphological analysis to
illustrate the nucleation and growth of Li2S by using scanning
electron microscopy (SEM). With 1.0 M LiTFSI, a thin layer
of Li2S film deposits appears on the electrode surface at the
initial growth stage (Figure S19a). With the increase of
discharge depth, the film-like Li2S gradually covers the
electrode surface (Figure S19b). This indicates that Li2S
tends to grow in a two-dimensional mode in the presence of 1
M LiTFSI. In contrast, with 0.8 M LiTFSI, at the initial Li2S

To evaluate the effect of LiBr on the formation rate of Li2Sx,
we investigated the disproportionation reaction rate of S and
Li2S in the presence of 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr in the
electrolyte (DOL/DME = 2:1, v/v). The optical photographs
show that the color of the solution with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2
M LiBr after 200 times dilution is darker than that with 0.8 M
LiTFSI. In the UV−vis spectra, the intensity of the S4
2−
absorption peak (420 nm) is higher than that with 0.8 M
LiTFSI. The results show that the addition of LiBr can
accelerate Li2Sx formation during disproportionation reactions
(Figure S22). We further continued to test soluble Li2Sx
concentrations at different time scales. With an increased
reaction time, the Li2Sx amount in the presence of 0.8 M
LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is also higher than that with 1.0 M
LiTFSI, suggesting the promotion effect of LiBr on Li2Sx
formation (Figure 4k). In addition, DFT calculations show that
the solvated structures formed by LiBr and DOL can stabilize
Li2S4 through dipole interactions with a binding energy of
−123.46 kJ mol−1 (Figure S23), confirming the stabilization
effect of LiBr on Li2Sx.
To evaluate the effect of LiBr on the cathode reaction rate,
we performed a CV test with 0.8 M LiTFSI electrolyte in the
presence and absence of 0.2 M LiBr. At a scan rate of 0.05 mV
s−1 and an E/S of 5 μL mgs
−1, the CV curves are similar in the
presence and absence of LiBr. This reveals that the amount of
Li2Sx generated in the two electrolytes is high at a low current
rate to ensure the high extent of S ↔Li2S interconversion
reactions (Figure S24a). At a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, the first
Pc1 peak positively shifts, and the peak current density
increases in the presence of LiBr compared to that without
LiBr. The difference in the second Pc2 reduction peak is more
obvious. In the presence of LiBr, the peak potential shows a
positive shift of about 80 mV and the peak current increases
from 3.12 to 4.86 mA (Figure S24b). Tafel slopes and
activation energy calculations further confirm the increase of
the reaction rate (Figure S25a,b). At a CV scan rate of 0.15 mV
s−1, the introduction of LiBr accelerates the total reaction
pathway. The battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI shows only one
reduction peak, but the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M
LiBr shows two distinct reduction peaks (Figure 4l). This
proves that LiBr can increase the total reaction rate.
The above results demonstrate that an increase of S8
solubility effectively increases the reaction rate, and LiBr
with an associated ion pair can stabilize Li2Sx to further
accelerate the reaction without sacrificing S8 dissolution in the
ether electrolyte. Based on this finding, we assembled a Li−S
battery. At an S loading of 5.0 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 10
μL mgs
−1, we evaluated the rate performance (Figure 5a). The
battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr show discharge
capacities of 1328.8, 1030.4, 955.5, and 607.9 mAh g−1 at
current rates of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 C, respectively. All these
values are much higher than those obtained with 1 M LiTFSI.
The polarization voltage at different discharge rates is also
much lower than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure S26),
indicating a greatly improved reaction rate. The charge−
discharge curve (Figure 5b) shows that two typical discharge
platforms can be clearly observed for the battery with 0.8 M
LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr at a current rate of 0.5C. However, the
disappearance of the second platform of the battery with 1 M
LiTFSI (Figure S27) further indicates the lower reaction rate
of the battery with 1.0 M LiTFSI.
To further evaluate the cell performance, we measured the
cycling performance (current density of 0.5C) at an S loading
of 2.18 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 10 μL mgs
−1. The capacity
of the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is
significantly higher than that of the 1 M LiTFSI battery, and
the cycle time is about 2.3 times that of the 1 M LiTFSI
battery (Figure S28). In addition, the polarization voltage at
different cycles is lower than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure
S29). To evaluate its application under high S loading and low
E/S ratio, we further measured the cycling performance (a
current density of 0.2C) at an S loading of 4.25 mg cm−2 and
an E/S ratio of 5 μL mgs
−1 (Figure 5c). The initial discharge
capacity of the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr at
0.05C (first-circle activation) is up to 1456 mAh g−1, which is
much higher than that with 1 M LiTFSI (1251 mAh g−1). At a
current density of 0.2C, the discharge capacity of the battery
with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is 1104 mAh g−1, while that
with 1.0 M LiTFSI is 219 mAh g−1, and the second platform of
the battery with 1 M LiTFSI disappears (Figure S30). After
100 cycles, the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr still
has a discharge capacity of 883 mAh g−1, with an average
capacity decay rate of only 0.2% (Figure 5d). The second
platform was still visible after 200 cycles (Figure S31). To
further demonstrate its application under harsh conditions, we
assembled Li−S batteries with an S loading of 6.2 mg cm−2 and
an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1. The battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and
0.2 M LiBr has a high initial discharge capacity of 1129 mAh
g−1 at 0.1C and can be operated stably over 70 cycles (Figure
5e). In contrast, the second platform at 0.05 and 0.1C cannot
be observed for the battery with 1 M LiTFSI, and a capacity of
171.6 mAh g−1 is achieved at a low current of 0.05C (Figure
S32).
In order to verify the practicality of Li−S batteries with the
designed electrolyte, a pouch cell with a level of 10.77 Ah was
assembled, in which an S load of 4.6 mg cm−2 (single side) at
the positive electrode and an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1 were
selected. The discharge capacity of 1421 mAh g−1 in the first
circle at 0.1C is achieved, corresponding to an energy density
of 554 Wh kg−1 (Figure 5f,g). The decreased capacity during
cell cycling may be due to Li metal corrosion under harsh
conditions with a low electrolyte volume. We evaluated the
long cycle performance of the Li//Li symmetric battery with
0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr (Figure S33). The cells can
stably cycle, proving that LiBr has no obvious effect on the
cycling performance of the Li metal electrode. However, more
focus should be paid to Li metal protection in future research,
as we found some damage to the Li metal electrode after
battery cycling (Figure S34). Nevertheless, compared to the
recently reported literature, the high-energy density Li−S
pouch cell constructed by the present electrolyte system is at
the top level of the reported system22−27 (Figure 5h and Table
S1).
■CONCLUSIONS
The nonlinear solubility of the solid solute in complex
solutions was determined, and the S8 solubility in the ether-
based electrolyte was tuned by changing the ratio of good and
poor solvents, the concentration, and the type of lithium salt.
The unexpected phenomenon is illustrated to be breaking the
threshold value of the interaction energy between the solute
and solvent, and the enthalpy change is found to be the key
