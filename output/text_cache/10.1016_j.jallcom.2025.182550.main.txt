A B S T R A C T
As a result of their outstanding energy density and high theoretical capacity, lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) stand 
out as leading prospects for advancing energy storage technologies. However, the coulombic efficiency and 
cycling stability of lithium-sulfur batteries have been considerably diminished due to the development of lithium 
dendrites and uneven lithium deposition, and the deposition of polysulfides on the anode surface. In this work, 
graphene quantum dots coupled with ionic liquids are suggested as electrolyte additives to improve the kinetics 
of Li2S/Li2S2 and prevent the growth of lithium dendrites. Control/10wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs exhibited excellent 
battery performance at a current density of 0.2 C (sulfur loading of 1.5 mg cm−2). The discharge capacity 
remained at 797 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles. The capacity decay rate per cycle was 0.17 %, and the coulombic 
efficiency was stable at 99 %. This work demonstrates that introducing graphene quantum dots coupled with 
ionic liquids into electrolytes is a crucial tactic to increase the performance of LSBs.
1. Introduction
The dwindling supply of fossil fuels has made the transition to sus­
tainable renewable energy both a worldwide priority and an immediate 
necessity. At the heart of effectively harnessing green energy lies the 
advancement of advanced energy storage solutions. Lithium-sulfur 
batteries (LSBs) are now a prime candidate for the subsequent era of 
energy storage innovations, boasting unparalleled advantages. With an 
impressive specific theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh g⁻¹ and an 
extraordinary energy density reaching 2600 Wh kg⁻¹ , these batteries 
outperform many conventional alternatives. What sets them apart is 
their eco-friendly nature coupled with cost-effectiveness, making them 
not just powerful but also sustainable and economically viable for large- 
scale applications. These compelling benefits position LSBs at the fore­
front of rechargeable battery technology [1–3]. However, LSBs face the 
following issues: 1) inadequate electrical conductivity for sulfur and 
lithium sulfide; 2) profound shuttle effect; 3) volume expansion; 4) 
lithium dendrite growth [4]. These problems can lead to lithium anode 
corrosion, the nonreversible loss of active materials, and a reduction in 
coulombic efficiency and cycle performance. Therefore, it is of great 
importance to construct and design appropriate materials to address 
these challenges. Wu et al [5]. used graphene/carbon nanotube (CNT) 
foam as a cathode material to show excellent cycle stability and capacity 
retention at a high sulfur loading of 10.8 mg cm−2 and a low electrolyte 
sulfur ratio of 5.25 μL mg−1. Carbon nanotubes are embedded in gra­
phene to form a staggered interconnected conductive network, which 
can effectively capture the migration of polysulfides and reduce the loss 
of active materials. Han et al [6]. incorporated pyrrolidino-nitrogen into 
the carbon framework, utilizing the nitrogen-LiPSs interaction and 
inhibiting LiPSs shuttle. At a sulfur content of up to 6.0 mg cm−2, the 
N-IOP battery achieved a high initial discharge capacity of 1162 mA h 
g−1, and the capacity retention rate after 150 cycles was 64 %. Incor­
porating pyrrolidino-nitrogen into the carbon framework further im­
proves the reversibility and redox capacity of the high-load, thick sulfur 
cathode by utilizing the nitrogen-LiPSs interaction and inhibiting LiPSs 
shuttle.

various modification strategies of cathode materials. However, the

problem of lithium anodes still exists [7]. During lithium metal battery 
charging and discharging, uneven deposition of lithium anodes and the 
formation of a rough solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer occur 
because the lithium electrochemical potential surpasses the lowest un­
occupied molecular orbital of non-aqueous electrolytes [8]. The uneven 
lithium flux will lead to the accumulation and growth of Li+ at the tip, 
resulting in the polysulfide shuttling effect exacerbation, capacity decay, 
and battery safety problems. Currently, several methods can be 
employed to protect the lithium metal anode, such as artificial SEI film 
[9,10], electrolyte additives [11,12], solid electrolytes [13,14] and so 
on.
In the lithium-sulfur battery system, quantum dot materials (usually 
zero-dimensional nanocrystals with a size of less than 10 nm) show 
unique advantages in solving key problems such as polysulfide shuttle 
effect and sluggish reaction kinetics due to their significant quantum 
confinement effect, high specific surface area and adjustable electronic 
structure [15]. Quantum dot materials can be divided into two cate­
gories: metal-based quantum dots [16] and non-metal-based quantum 
dots [17]. Graphene quantum dots (GQDs) are small pieces of graphene 
with a two-dimensional lateral size (less than 100 nm), which belong to 
non-metal-based quantum dots [18]. Their unique properties are really 
turning heads in fields like biology, energy, and optoelectronics 
[19–21]. GQDs exhibit the inert characteristics of carbon materials, 
demonstrating both chemical and physical stability, along with benefits 
including consistent fluorescence characteristics, minimal toxicity, and 
excellent water solubility. The hydrogen and oxygen in GQDs mainly 
appear as hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl, and epoxy functional groups.

The exceptional attributes of GQDs, including a substantial specific 
surface area and straightforward functionalization, confer significant 
benefits in the realm of energy storage. At present, there are also some 
reports on the application of GQDs in LSBs. Park et al [24]. found that 
incorporating GQDs into the cathode significantly enhanced the utili­
zation of sulfur-active substances, achieving high performance. Through 
the induction of GQDs, the structural integrity of the S/C electrode was 
ensured, while the layered structure facilitated rapid charge transfer and 
minimized the loss of LiPSs. Hu et al [25]. pyrolyzed graphene precursor 
to generate GQDs which were uniformly dispersed into the electrolyte 
using PEO (polyethylene). The Li/Li symmetrical battery achieved a 
stable cycle of more than 500 h at a current density of 3 mA cm-2. The 
Li-S battery can be stably cycled for 200 cycles at a sulfur loading of 4 mg 
cm-2, and the coulombic efficiency is stable at 99 %. Senthil et al [8]. 
reported the synthesis of hydroxyl-rich GQDs using pyrene as a pre­
cursor. The GQDs-modified separator in the Li/Li symmetric battery 
exhibited stable cycling beyond 2000 h at 1 and 5 mA cm−2 current 
densities. Fan et al [26]. developed a versatile separator using a com­
bination of graphene quantum dots, polyacrylonitrile, and poly­
propylene. This separator enhanced sulfur cathode performance and 
lithium anode stability. The GQDs promoted even Li+ nucleation and 
deposition, reducing lithium anode passivation and avoiding battery 
short circuits. In addition, the three-dimensional network, constructed 
from electrospun fibers integrated with the functional groups of GQDs, 
effectively suppressed the notorious polysulfide shuttle effect, boosted 
the utilization of sulfur, prolonged the service life of the separator, and 
improved the stability of the battery.
In this work, the hydroxyl-rich GQDs prepared by the hydrothermal 
method are coupled with ionic liquids and introduced into the LSBs 
electrolyte. This approach not only enhances the conversion kinetics of 
low-order LiPSs but also achieves lithium anode protection through 
electrolyte modification.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Experimental materials
The active microporous carbon was purchased from Jiangsu 
XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd, 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidine tri­
fluoromethanesulfonate was purchased from Shanghai Macklin 
Biochemical Co., Ltd, and glucan was purchased from Tianjin Xiensi 
Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.
2.2. Preparation of GQDs
GQDs were synthesized using a “bottom-up” hydrothermal method 
[27]. First, 50 mg of dextran was weighed and fully dissolved in ultra­
pure water. The mixed solution was then transferred to the inner lining 
of the reactor, sealed, and placed in an oven for hydrothermal reaction at 
200◦C for 8 h. Following cooling to ambient temperature, the brown 
solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm microporous membrane to 
remove insoluble materials. The filtrate was centrifuged (10000 rpm, 
3 min) to collect the supernatant. Finally, graphene quantum dots 
(GQDs) powder was obtained by ultrasonic dispersion, dialysis for 3 
days (the molecular weight of the dialysis membrane is 2000 Da), and 
freeze-drying.
2.3. Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs electrolytes configuration
In the glove box, 1 mg of GQDs were dissolved in an appropriate 
amount of [P14][OTf]. The resulting mixture was sealed completely and 
subjected to ultrasonic dispersion and stirring at room temperature for 
24 h. The solution was then added to the Control electrolyte in a certain 
proportion and stirred for 24 h to ensure uniform mixing, yielding the 
composite electrolyte named Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs.
2.4. Cathode preparation
The mixture of active microporous carbon and sublimed sulfur was 
prepared in a 3:7 ratio, ball-milled for 2 h, and then heated at 155◦C for 
12 h to obtain a sulfur-loaded material. The sulfur-loaded material, 
PVDF, and acetylene black were combined in a 7:2:1 proportion to 
create a slurry and uniformly coated on the aluminum foil. After drying, 
cut into circular pieces with a diameter of 12 mm. The cathode sheet can 
be obtained.
2.5. Cell assembly
LSBs assembly: Lithium metal sheet was used as the negative elec­
trode, with Control, Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] and Control/10 wt% 
[P14][OTf]@GQDs as the electrolyte, the prepared electrode sheet was 
used as the positive electrode, and Celgard-2400 was used as the dia­
phragm (d=19 mm). The sulfur content of the electrode piece is 
1.0–1.2 mg cm−2, and the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio is 25 μL mg−1. 
Assemble the CR2016 button battery in a glove box (Ar atmosphere, 
oxygen and moisture content below 0.1 ppm).
The assembly of other button batteries: symmetrical batteries were 
assembled with lithium/lithium and lithium/copper, respectively.

3.2. Physical and chemical properties of electrolytes
GQDs (1 mg) were added to Control, [P14][OTf], and Control/10 wt 
%[P14][OTf] electrolytes, respectively. The three electrolytes were 
subjected to ultrasonication (30 min) and stirring (24 h) under the same 
conditions. The three electrolytes were left to stand, and the stratifica­
tion was observed. It can be observed that the three electrolytes are still 
mixed evenly after standing for one day in Fig. 3a. After standing for 7 
days, partial precipitation was observed in the Control@GQDs electro­
lyte. After standing for 30 days, apparent stratification was found in the 
Control@GQDs electrolyte, with GQDs precipitated at the bottom of the 
electrolyte, which was consistent with the reports in the literature [25]. 
Therefore, it is feasible to introduce GQDs into the Control electrolyte by 
coupling with ionic liquids.
By analyzing the infrared spectra before and after electrolyte 
coupling, it was found that the characteristic groups of GQDs such as 
stretching vibration of O-H at 3440 cm−1, bending vibration of O-H at 
1305 cm−1, the -COO group at 1386 cm−1, the C––C group at 1632 cm−1 
and the C––O group at 1705 cm−1 still exist in the infrared spectra after 
coupling. The sulfonic acid group of the ionic liquid [P14][OTf] did not 
disappear after the combination, indicating good compatibility between 
the two and the absence of side reaction occurred (Fig. 3b).

The electrochemical window of the coin cells constructed with 
lithium/electrolyte/stainless steel sheet in different electrolytes was 
tested by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), in which the electrolyte 
addition was 30 μL. The results shown in Fig. 3c demonstrate that the

operating voltage range (1.7–2.8 V). The premise of electrolyte modi­
fication is to ensure the stability of the working window of the battery. 
By increasing the redox potential, the charge and discharge window of 
the battery can be expanded, thus increasing the energy density and 
effectively inhibiting the electrolyte side reactions during the charge and 
discharge cycles in full cells and pouch cells, solving the problem of gas 
generation and swelling, and improving safety and stability.
The coupling mechanism between GQDs and [P14][OTf] is specu­
lated as follows: the hydroxyl bond of GQDs was broken under ultra­
sonication, and the GQDs as a whole exhibit a negative charge. The 
cations (1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidine) in the ionic liquid are attracted to 
the GQDs. Meanwhile, there was a sizeable π bond and hydrogen bond 
between GQDs and ionic liquids, which enabled attraction interaction 
between the molecules after ultrasonication, as illustrated in Fig. 3d.
3.3. Electrochemical performance test
Coulombic efficiency is a critical parameter to evaluate the cycling 
stability of lithium metal anodes, it’s essentially the proportion between 
the lithium stripped away from the active electrode during a cycle and 
the lithium that’s deposited through electroplating. From Fig. 4a, It is 
evident that the Li/Cu battery in Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] electrolyte 
exhibits significant fluctuations in coulombic efficiency from the 
beginning to the end of the cycling, which indicates poor reversibility of 
lithium plating/stripping in this electrolyte. In contrast, the Li/Cu bat­
tery demonstrates more stable coulombic efficiency in the electrolyte of 
Control and Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs (the Control electrolyte 
is stable at 97 %, while the Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs is stable 
at 96 % from the 5th to 50th cycle). It can be seen that the introduction 
of GQDs into Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] dramatically enhanced the 
overall cycling stability of the battery.
The effects of different electrolytes on the SEI and dendrite growth 
on the lithium metal surface were also evaluated by the lithium plating/ 
stripping cycle of the Li/Li symmetrical battery. Fig. 4b is the cycling 
performance of Li/Li symmetric battery with Control, Control/10 wt% 
[P14][OTf], and Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf]@GQDs electrolytes. It can 
be seen that after the introduction of [P14][OTf], the cell exhibits higher 
voltage throughout the overall cycling stage, indicating poor compati­
bility with the lithium sheet. However, when [P14][OTf]@GQDs is 
introduced into the Control electrolyte, although the voltage is lower 
than that of the Control electrolyte during the overall cycling process, 
both 
Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] 
and 
Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] 
@GQDs enable stable cycling of the lithium sheet for 300 h. When the 
amount of high donor electrolyte exceeds the equilibrium point, the

The formation of SEI on lithium metal is a process that occurs 
naturally, but the continuous cycling process will change its structure 
and composition. The growth of the inorganic and organic layers will 
influence the performance of lithium metal [35,36]. To further explain 
the role of GQDs in SEI formation, the battery with S@Mac cathode 
material in the three electrolytes was disassembled after 100 cycles, and 
the XPS of the lithium anodes were analyzed (Figs. 8, 9) [8]. After 
cycling, the lithium anodes in all three electrolytes contain Li, C, N, O, 
and F, but the F content of the modified electrolyte is higher than that of 
the Control electrolyte. The C 1 s spectra reveal the presence of HCO2Li 
(289.8 eV) and ROLi (288.7 eV) bonds [37]. This is due to the organic 
layer produced by the decomposition of highly oxidized reactants, and 
the relative contents of the three electrolytes are varied. In addition, the 
intensities of fluorine-based inorganics (LiF and -CF3 of LiSO2CF3) in the 
three electrolytes are also different. The nitrogen-containing functional 
groups formed by the decomposition of TFSI- and NO3
- are controlled in 
the [P14][OTf]@GQDs electrolyte system, thereby restructuring the SEI 
film and enabling uniform lithium salt deposition. By integrating the 
XPS spectra of F 1 s, it was found that the area of F bond functional 
groups on the surface of the lithium sheet in Control/10 wt%[P14][OTf] 
GQDs electrolytes is more significant, indicating that the SEI layer 
formed in the GQDs-modified electrolyte is fluorine-rich. Fluorine is 
ubiquitous in all three electrolytes. Fluorine is ubiquitous in all three 
electrolytes, the formation of fluorine-rich SEI film offers several ad­
vantages, such as a faster Li+ migration rate, a wider electrochemical 
window, and mechanically stable properties of SEI films. In the 
GQDs-modified electrolytes, Li-F or C-F bonds are also observed on the 
lithium sheet surface, contributing to the formation of a smooth surface 
with a uniform Li+ flux, thereby inhibiting dendritic lithium growth 
[38].
