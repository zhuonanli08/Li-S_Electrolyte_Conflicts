ABSTRACT: Lithium (Li) metal’s extremely high specific energy and low potential make it critical for high-performance batteries.
However, uncontrolled dendrite growth and an unstable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) during repeated cycling still seriously
hinder its practical application in Li metal batteries. Herein, we demonstrate a facile and effective approach to fabricate a flexible and
robust hybrid SEI layer using two kinds of organo-polysulfides with different sulfur chain lengths [bis(3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl)-
disulfide (Si−O−2S) and bis(3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl)tetrasulfide (Si−O−4S)] as the additives in the electrolyte. Compared to Si−
O−2S, the siloxane and the long sulfur chain in Si−O−4S are conducive to the production of LixSiSy inorganic components on
lithium metal surfaces and the formation of an organic−inorganic hybrid stable SEI layer in conjunction with LixSiOy, thereby
improving the stability of the SEI layer and inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites. Specifically, with 10 wt % Si−O−4S as the
additive, an excellent cycling lifespan (1400 h) was achieved with a low hysteresis voltage of ∼17 mV at 1.0 mA cm−2 in a Li−Li
symmetrical cell. Moreover, the lithium−sulfur battery also exhibits long cycling stability (850 mA h g−1 at 0.5 C after 200 cycles)
and good Coulombic efficiency (99.5%). This study provides an electrolyte additive strategy for the Li anode fabricating a stable SEI
layer and long cycling batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium metal battery, solid-electrolyte interface, electrolyte additives, organo-polysulfides, lithium dendrite
1. INTRODUCTION
With the thriving development of mobile Internet and new
energy vehicles, demand for high specific energy and high
power density power sources has been growing.1 The
theoretical limits of lithium-ion batteries are approaching and
they are unable to meet the increasing requirements for fast
charging capability and energy density.2,3 Therefore, lithium
metal batteries (LMBs) with extremely high theoretical specific
capacities (3860 mA h g−1) are widely seen as crucial for next-
generation high-performance energy storage and green trans-
portation.4−6 Especially, the rapid development of high specific
energy lithium−sulfur7,8 and lithium−oxygen9,10 batteries
holds great strategic significance in building a sustainable
low-carbon future energy system.11,12 However, for large-scale
commercialization of LMBs, the core scientific challenge
remains how to effectively suppress lithium dendrite growth
and construct a stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) during
cycling.
Lithium dendrite formation arises from the high reactivity of
metallic lithium. Lithium undergoes redox reactions with the
surrounding electrolyte, forming a fragile heterogeneous native
SEI.3 This SEI layer not only consumes active lithium and
electrolytes causing capacity loss during cycling, but its
repeated deposition and stripping also leads to uneven lithium
plating,13 eventually forming dendrites and “dead lithium”.14,15
Consequently, increased cell impedance and voltage polar-

and short cycle life.16 Additionally, the dendrites may puncture
the separator, causing internal short circuits and leading to
safety hazards like thermal runaway and explosions.17,18
In order to solve the above problems, researchers have
proposed various solutions. So far, lithium dendrite growth is
mainly suppressed through four approaches: (1) electrolyte
additives,19−22 (2) artificial enhancement of the anode’s
protective layer,23−25 (3) lithium metal structural design,26−28
and (4) solid-state electrolytes.29−32 Among them, electrolyte
additives have attracted widespread attention for their
simplicity and cost-effectiveness. Through suitable additives,
a dense SEI layer can be generated to suppress dendrite growth
and protect the lithium metal anode. This significantly
improves the cycling life and safety of LMBs.33
Currently, numerous electrolyte additives have been ex-
plored to optimize the SEI composition for protecting the
lithium anode. These include ester additives (vinylene
carbonate (VC)34 and fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)35);
nitrate additives (LiNO3
36 and CsNO3
37); halide additives
(LiI38 and diphenyl sulfone/bis(4-florophenyl) sulfone (DPS/
BFS)39); sulfide additives (P2S5
40 and poly(sulfur-random-
triallylamine) (PST)41); silane additives (polydimethylsiloxane
(PDMS),42 3-methacryloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (MPS)43);
etc. Different additives serve varied functions and result in
diverse SEI compositions, but they all contribute significantly
to lithium dendrite suppression and SEI stability to some
extent.
For sulfide additives, Li et al.41 reported a sulfur polymer as
an additive in ether-based electrolytes. This formed a stable
SEI layer on the lithium anode surface in situ, comprising
organic components (organic sulfides/polysulfides) and
inorganic components (Li2S/Li2S2). The organic sul-
fides41,44−46 enhanced the SEI flexibility and stability,
improving the electrochemical performance and enabling
dendrite-free lithium deposition. It also significantly increased
the CE. For silane additives, Meng et al.42 proposed end-
capped liquid PDMS with −OCH3 groups as an electrolyte
additive to inhibit dendrite formation. This grafting triggered a
mixed SEI formation, increasing LiF and Li−Si−O compo-
nents and promoting uniform lithium deposition. Wang et al.43
used a silane coupling agent as an additive. By forming
covalent bonds (Li−Si−O) firmly attached to the lithium
surface, it improved the air stability and electrochemical
performance. Most silane compounds42,43,47 can be preferen-
tially reduced due to the Si−O bonds, suppressing electrolyte
decomposition while enhancing SEI stability and inhibiting
dendrite growth.
In addition, Wang Donghai’s team,48,49 building upon their
previously developed dimethyl disulfide lithium−sulfur battery
electrolyte, has innovated a new electrolyte with added
dimethyl trisulfide (DMTS). The new DMTS-enhanced
electrolyte enables a beneficial reaction with sulfur to yield
dimethyl polysulfides while avoiding problematic long-chain
polysulfides, thereby bolstering battery cycling stability.
Additionally, the introduced DMTS participates electrochemi-
cally through cleavage of its −SSS−bonds to provide a
supplementary capacity and enhanced energy density. DMTS
also reacts with discharge phase lithium sulfides, generating
polysulfides soluble in the electrolyte solution, which cuts
irreversible capacity losses and further stabilizes cycling.
Consequently, the systematic exploration of diverse sulfur
chain architectures demonstrates considerable promise for
enabling performance improvements in lithium−sulfur bat-
teries.
Based on previous research, this study proposes using two
organo-polysulfide compounds with different sulfur chain
lengths [bis(3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl)disulfide (Si−O−2S)
and bis(3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl)tetrasulfide (Si−O−4S)] (Fig-
ure S1) as additives to investigate their effects on SEI
performance. The siloxane in the additive can bond with
lithium metal to generate a Li−Si−O-based component, which
converts to a hybrid organic−inorganic SEI under electro-
chemical cycling to protect the lithium anode (Scheme 1).
Meanwhile, the sulfide compounds can also form conductive
Li−S inorganic components on the lithium anode surface,
further enhancing the SEI stability. Attributed to these
superiorities, LMBs demonstrate a remarkable electrochemical
performance. With Si−O−4S as the additive, Li−Li symmetric
cells can achieve a superior long cycling lifetime over 1400 h
with a small plating−stripping voltage of 17 mV under 1.0 mA
cm−2. Furthermore, as matched with the sulfur cathode, the
Li−S batteries still delivered highly reversible capacities over
955 mA h g−1 (100 cycles) and 850 mA h g−1 (200 cycles) at
0.1 and 0.5 C, respectively. These encouraging results indicate
that the synergistic effect of siloxane and long sulfur chains is
expected to produce a new high-stability hybrid SEI, providing
new insights into SEI modification for lithium metal anodes.

2.2. Electrolyte Preparation. The pristine electrolyte consists of
0.6 M LiTFSI and 0.4 M LiNO3 in DOL/DME (v: v = 1:1), referred
to as the lithium−sulfur electrolyte. For comparison, modified
electrolytes were prepared by adding various contents (5, 10, and
15 wt %) of Si−O−2S or Si−O−4S to the lithium−sulfur electrolyte.
All electrolytes were prepared in an argon-filled glovebox with H2O
and O2 contents below 0.1 ppm.
2.3. Electrode Preparation. The cathode slurry was prepared by
mixing the active material (elemental sulfur), conductive agent
(acetylene black), and binder (LA133) in a weight ratio of 7:2:1. The
components were dispersed in a suitable amount of solvent
(isopropanol/deionized water, 1:3 volume ratio) and magnetically
stirred for 12 h. The resulting slurry was coated evenly on an
aluminum current collector and allowed to air-dry. After vacuum
drying at 60 °C for 24 h, the coated foil was cut into 14 mm diameter
circular electrodes using a slicer, with a sulfur loading of 1.2 mg cm−2.
2.4. Coin Cell Assembly and Characterization. CR2025 coin
cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox by using Li−Li, Li−
Cu, and Li−sulfur cathode configurations. A Celgard 2325 membrane
was used as the separator. The prepared electrolytes were added with
an electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 10 μL/mg. H2O and O2 levels in the
glovebox were below 0.1 ppm. Assembled cells were allowed to rest
for 12 h before testing. Galvanostatic charge−discharge cycling was
conducted using a Land CT3001A battery testing system at 1.7−2.8
V. The specific capacity was calculated based on sulfur. Electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using a
CHI660E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua). The
frequency range was 0.01−100,000 Hz with an AC amplitude of 10
mV.
2.5. Air Stability Testing. Lithium metal foils were soaked in the
prepared electrolytes for 12 h in a glovebox. The foils were then
removed and air-dried on clean Petri dishes for 12 h to obtain a SEI
layer on the surface. The foils were subsequently exposed to air (30%
humidity, 25 °C) and observed over time for surface morphological
changes using digital photography.
2.6. Materials Characterization. The morphologies of the Li
anode surface were revealed by scanning electron microscopy (JSM-
IT 100, JEOL Ltd., Japan). After battery cycling tests, the lithium foils
were removed, washed with 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), and transferred
under vacuum to the specimen chamber for SEM imaging at 15 kV.
For X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) studies, the samples
were sealed in a transporter in the glovebox before being quickly
transferred to the high-vacuum chamber of XPS (PHI 5000
VersaProbe III, ULVAC-PHI Ltd., Japan) for analysis.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Cycling Stability Tests Analysis for SEI Films. The
Li−Li symmetrical batteries were assembled to confirm the
optimal ratio of organo-polysulfide additive components based
on electrolytes containing organo-polysulfide additives of
different compositions. As displayed in Figure 1a,b, cycling
performance curves of Li−Li symmetrical batteries were
obtained with Si−O−2S and Si−O−4S additives of varying
mass fractions at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal
capacity of 1 mA h cm−2. For the Si−O−2S additive,
significantly improved cycling stability can be obtained when
5% Si−O−2S is added into the pristine electrolyte. However,
as the amount of Si−O−2S addition continued to rise, the
cycling life of the Li−Li symmetrical battery decreased
dramatically versus the pristine electrolyte, and its polarization
voltage (i.e., the difference of plating and stripping plateau
voltages) started to increase, as depicted in Figure S2a. This
may have been caused by the additive obstructing the ion
conduction. During repetitive stripping and plating, uneven
lithium deposition resulted in continuous dendrite formation,
leading to an unstable interface between metallic lithium and
the electrolyte, thus reducing the cycling life and raising the
polarization voltage. As shown in Figure 1b, for the Si−O−4S
additive, the cell with 10% Si−O−4S additive achieves a
superior long lifespan of more than 1400 h with a much
narrower voltage gap (17 mV) than that of pristine electrolytes
(Figure S2b). Meanwhile, the electrochemical cycling life of
cells with 5% and 15% Si−O−4S is ≈300 and ≈600 h,
respectively, before reaching a short circuit. Insufficient
addition (5%) of Si−O−4S also influences the polarization
overpotential, which is comparable to that in the pristine

To further confirm the effects of two sulfur chain lengths on
the stability of the SEI film on the metallic lithium anode,
Figure 1c shows the cycling performance curves of Li−Li
symmetrical batteries with electrolytes containing 5% Si−O−
2S and 10% Si−O−4S additives. The cycling life of the Li−Li
symmetrical battery was markedly superior in the electrolyte
with 10% Si−O−4S additive compared to that in the
electrolyte with 5% Si−O−2S additive. Additionally, the
polarization voltage was maintained within a minimal range
(inset of Figure 1c), indicating better ion conductivity and a
more stable SEI film, which could satisfy the requirements for
long cycling performance. The reason may be that the sulfur
chain of the Si−O−4S additive is longer. Upon contact with
the metallic lithium, S−S chains underwent scission. A portion
grafted onto the Li anode through Li−O−Si connections and
electron transfer reactions, ultimately forming inorganic
products like LixSiOy. Excess sulfur chains continued to
react, finally producing LixSiSy. This organic component
LixSiSy has been proven to possess good ion conductivity
and suppress lithium dendrite growth.42 Furthermore, even at
higher current densities and at practical deposition conditions,
the organo-polysulfide additive still presents excellent cycling
stability with low voltage hysteresis. As shown in Figure 1d, the
charge−discharge cycling curves were obtained for Li−Li
symmetrical batteries using 5% Si−O−2S and 10% Si−O−4S
addition at a current density of 5 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 5
mA h cm−2. More remarkably, with 10% Si−O−4S addition,
the Li−Li symmetrical battery maintained a 120 mV
polarization potential during 2500 h of cycling, much better
than the Li−Li symmetrical battery using 5% Si−O−2S
electrolyte (265 mV, 420 h) (inset of Figure 1d). These results
demonstrate that the SEI film generated in situ by adding an
organo-polysulfide additive still exhibited excellent stability
under high current densities.
The surface morphology of cycled lithium foils can also
illustrate the inhibition effect of SEI films on the lithium
dendrite growth. After 100 cycles at a current density of 1 mA
cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mA h cm−2, the lithium foils
were removed from Li−Li symmetrical batteries. According to
the SEM image in Figure S3, the lithium electrode cycled in
the electrolyte without the organo-polysulfide additive had a
rough surface with stacked lithium dendrites. This was
explained by the fact that the electrolyte-induced SEI film
was too fragile to withstand the huge volume fluctuations
during lithium plating/stripping, causing SEI rupture and Li
dendrite growth. In contrast, adding an organo-polysulfide
additive into the electrolyte could improve the surface
morphology of the cycled lithium anode. As depicted in
Figure 2a−c, the lithium anode modified by 5% Si−O−2S
addition exhibited a smoother morphology versus those
modified by 10% Si−O−2S and 15% Si−O−2S. However,
the lithium anode modified by 10% Si−O−4S addition to the
electrolyte displayed the optimal lithium deposition morphol-
ogy after 100 cycles without noticeable dendritic lithium
particles (Figure 2d−f). Clearly, adding 10% Si−O−4S to the
electrolyte played a positive role in promoting uniform lithium
deposition and suppressing lithium dendrite formation on the
lithium anode. To further verify the compactness of the SEI
layer, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was
performed. The results, as shown in Figure S4, demonstrate a
uniform distribution of key elements, supporting the formation
of a dense hybrid SEI layer, as described above. Consequently,
modifying the lithium anode by adding the organo-polysulfide
additive to the electrolyte can provide effective protection for
the lithium metal anode, enabling dendrite-free cycling after
long-term cycling, thus prolonging the life of LMBs.
Moreover, in order to further study the structural character-
istics and compactness of the SEI layer, we also carried out
scanning electron microscope cross-sectional analysis, as
shown in Figure S5. The results show significant differences
in the morphology of the lithium anode surface, highlighting
the beneficial role of the Si−O−2S and Si−O−4S additive. For
the lithium anode cycled with the Si−O−4S-containing
electrolyte, the cross-sectional SEM image reveals a smooth
and flat surface with no visible dendritic growth or
irregularities. This indicates that the SEI layer formed in the
presence of Si−O−4S is uniform and mechanically robust,
effectively suppressing lithium dendrite formation and
promoting stable lithium deposition/stripping behavior. In
contrast, the lithium anode cycled without the additive exhibits
a rough and uneven surface morphology, characterized by
obvious dendritic structures and fractured regions.

3.2. Li−Li Symmetrical Battery XPS Analysis. To
further understand the chemical composition of the SEI film
formed on the surface of the lithium anode, XPS testing was
performed on the surface of the Li−Li symmetric battery after
the above cycles. The binding energy values were all calibrated
using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The overview XPS spectra are
given in Figure S6. Figure 3 shows Li 1s, C 1s, O 1s, F 1s, S 2p,
and Si 2p energy spectra. The energy spectra of the Li metal
surface before and after the pristine electrolyte and the
addition of electrolyte additives containing 5% Si−O−2S and
10% Si−O−4S were compared. Among them, due to the
existence of Si−O bonds, the energy spectrum of batteries
containing additives has an obvious Si 2p peak (Figure 3f).
The characteristic peaks at 100.4 and 101.4 eV were attributed
to Si−C and Li−Si−O, respectively. In addition, compared to
Si−O−2S, there is a stronger Li−Si−S peak at 102.3 eV in the
energy spectrum of Si−O−4S, from which we can infer that
the SEI formed in the presence of Si−O−4S exhibits a higher
abundance of organic sulfur species LixSiSy while maintaining a
LixSiOy-rich base, which further verifies what we mentioned
above: In the generation of LixSiOy based on this inorganic
product, excess sulfur chains will continue to participate in the
reaction and eventually generate LixSiSy. At the same time, this
can also be seen in S 2p (Figure 3e). The characteristic peaks
at 163.2 and 164.8 eV were attributed to Li−Si−S and Si−S,
respectively. Due to the existence of the Li−Si−S bond, the
Si−O−4S energy spectrum has a stronger peak here. Finally,
based on the relative peak intensity comparison, it is obvious
that the corresponding peaks are weakened from Li 1s, C 1s, O
1s, and F 1s (Figure 3a−d), which means that compared with
the pristine electrolyte, the addition of organo-polysulfide
compound additives containing 5% Si−O−2S and 10% Si−O−
4S electrolyte additives has fewer electrolyte decomposition
byproducts, indicating that the additives effectively alleviate the
side reactions between the active lithium metal and electrolyte
and inhibit lithium dendrites growth. A noteworthy observa-
tion in the F 1s spectrum is the increased intensity of the −CF3
peak, which does not necessarily signify accelerated electrolyte
consumption. Correlating this with the S 2p spectrum reveals
an increase in the SOx
2−peak, indicating that the enhanced −
CF3 intensity likely stems from the further decomposition of
LiTFSI. This process results in the enrichment of −CF3 species
within the SEI layer, suggesting that, with the introduction of
sulfur-containing additives, −CF3 species may predominantly
exist in the form of −SOxCF3. Furthermore, while LiN and LiF
are recognized as essential SEI components that facilitate
uniform lithium deposition due to their superior mechanical
strength and ionic conductivity, the overall performance of
cells with Si−O−4S additives has been notably enhanced. This
enhancement highlights that the coexistence of sulfur- and
silicon-containing species compensates for the reduced LiN
and LiF content, forming a hybrid SEI layer characterized by
competitive yet synergistic interactions.

induced by the Si−O−4S additive, XPS depth profiling was

depth profile. Our depth-resolved XPS data provides
compelling evidence that supports the formation of a hybrid
SEI structure. Specifically, the XPS spectra show a distinct
variation in the distribution of organic and inorganic
components within the SEI as a function of depth. As shown
in Figure S7, we observe that the organic components

3.3. Li−Li Symmetrical Battery Impedance Test and
Analysis. The EIS of Li−Li symmetrical cells was performed
to compare the interface resistance (Ri) depending on the
cycling number and the polarization process for electrolyte
additives. The Nyquist plots typically consist of a high-
frequency semicircle (or two convolutional semicircles)
originating from a nonzero intercept on the x axis, followed
by a low-frequency inclined diffusion tail. The interfacial
resistance corresponds to the width of the high-frequency
semicircle, which usually involves contributions from the SEI
layer resistance and charge-transfer resistance between the
electrode and electrolyte. The intercept value before the
starting of the semicircle belongs to the resistance of the bulk
electrolyte (Rb).50 An equivalent circuit diagram illustrating the
electrical behavior is presented in Figure S8.
Note that both the Ri and Rb are significantly decreased for
the 10% Si−O−4S additives compared with the electrolyte
without organo-polysulfide additives after cycling (excluding
before cycling), as illustrated in Figure 4a,b. The decrease in Rb
is likely caused by the selective adsorption of Si−O−4S toward
lithium salt and therefore more facile dissociation of the ion
pair in the electrolyte (or space charge effect to release more
free charge carriers) (Figure 4c). Before cycling, Ri with 10%
Si−O−4S addition was slightly higher than the electrolyte
without organo-polysulfide compounds, likely due to an
unstable interface without battery activation. This result also
indicates that tiny Si−O−4S addition does not greatly increase
the electrolyte viscosity. The decrease in Ri is mainly due to the
generation of LixSiSy and LixSiOy after cycling, which makes
the SEI film more stable and improves the conductivity. The
interface resistance value of the Si−O−4S system is extremely
small and ranges from 13.6 to 14.1 Ω during 50−150 h (Figure
4d). It slightly increases with the progress of cycling. In
comparison, the Ri value of the control cell is roughly 1−6
times larger than that of the modified cell. All these results
signify the SEI film with 10% Si−O−4S has good stability to
support long cycling lifetimes.
3.4. SEI Film of Air Stability Analysis. The corrosion
resistance of the SEI film constructed from electrolyte additives
was further studied through air stability experiments. When
lithium foils modified by electrolyte additives were exposed to
air, the dense organic−inorganic hybrid solid electrolyte film
exhibited a positive effect on preventing air penetration. The
optical photograph in Figure S9a−c shows the corrosion
degree of lithium foils in the pristine electrolyte and lithium
foils modified by 5% Si−O−2S and 10% Si−O−4S electrolyte
additives after exposure for different periods to air, where the
humidity is about 30%. The lithium foil in pristine electrolytes
starts to lose its metallic luster and becomes darker after
exposure to air for 5 min, and it turns totally black after 15
min. Meanwhile, the lithium foil modified by 5% Si−O−2S
electrolyte addition starts to lose its metallic luster after 10
min, with most of the surface turning black after 15 min. In
contrast, the lithium foil modified by 10% Si−O−4S

the compactness of the Si−O−4S-modified layer is sufficient to
prevent a reaction between active lithium metal and air. These
results indicate that the organo-polysulfide additive exhibits
sufficient air stability to effectively impede the undesirable
reaction between active lithium and air, thereby facilitating the
establishment of a durable and stable SEI layer.

3.6. Li−S Battery Performance Test. After investigating
the stability of SEI films constructed by organo-polysulfide
additives with two sulfur chain lengths using Li−Li and Li−Cu
batteries, we applied organo-polysulfide electrolytes to Li−S
batteries for further study of the electrochemical performance.
Figure 6a presents the galvanostatic charge−discharge cycling
performance curves at a 0.1 C rate for Li−S batteries using
electrolytes with 10% Si−O−4S, 5% Si−O−2S, and pristine
electrolytes. Compared to Li−S batteries with 5% Si−O−2S
and pristine electrolytes, the 10% (Si−O−4S) battery
exhibited markedly higher discharge specific capacity and
cycling stability. Its initial discharge capacity reached 1262 mA
h g−1, remaining at 955 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles, while
capacities of the 5% Si−O−2S and pristine electrolyte batteries
dropped to only 767 mA h g−1 and 588 mA h g−1 after 100
cycles, with obvious capacity decay (Figure 6b). In the Li−S
battery with the pristine electrolyte, the shuttle behavior of
soluble polysulfides could not be eliminated, leading to the
inevitable diffusion of some long-chain soluble polysulfides to
the lithium anode side due to concentration gradients,
consequently reacting with lithium metal to form inert and
insoluble Li2S2 or Li2S. This resulted in active material loss,
irreversible lithium consumption, and diminished CE. The
subsequent inert and nonuniform SEI film formed on the
anode surface would hinder lithium-ion diffusion, inducing
uneven current density distribution and uncontrolled rapid
lithium dendrite growth, eventually degrading cycling perform-
ance and safety. The Li−S battery with 5% Si−O−2S
electrolyte additive could form an SEI film enriched in LixSiOy
versus the pristine electrolyte, improving the stability, but it
could not completely suppress lithium dendrite formation.
Thus, it did not exhibit the best cycling performance. However,
the 10% Si−O−4S electrolyte battery could not only form an
SEI film enriched in LixSiOy but also generate a more abundant
organic component LixSiSy. Their interaction formed an
organic/inorganic hybrid solid electrolyte film with excellent
ion conductivity that inhibited lithium dendrite growth,
enabling superior electrochemical performance.
Figure 6c shows the cycling performance of Li−S cells with
10% Si−O−4S, 5% Si−O−2S, and pristine electrolytes at a
high current density of 0.5 C. Consequently, the Li−S battery
with 10% Si−O−4S electrolyte additive can maintain a higher
capacity of 850 mA h g−1 after 200 cycles with a higher
capacity retention rate of about 85% than the pristine
electrolyte cell (600 mA h g−1, 75%) and 5% Si−O−2S
electrolyte cell (756 mA h g−1, 79%). The rate performance of
Li−S cells was also tested (Figure 6d). It was obvious that cells
with organo-polysulfide additives delivered a better rate of
performance than pristine electrolyte cells. In particular, using
10% Si−O−4S electrolyte additive, the capacity of the
modified battery can reach 870 mA h g−1 and 766 mA h g−1
at high current densities of 1 and 2 C and still retain a cell
capacity of 1012 mA h g−1 when reduced to 0.1 C. It is much
better than the capacity retention of 5% Si−O−2S additive
(943 mA h g−1) and the pristine electrolyte (824 mA h g−1).
All these results validate that lithium−sulfur batteries using
organic polysulfide additives have good electrochemical
performance and reversibility, which can protect the lithium
anode more effectively and improve battery cycling life.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we have demonstrated a strategy to fabricate a
flexible and robust hybrid SEI layer using two kinds of organo-
polysulfides with different sulfur chain lengths [bis(3-
(triethoxysilyl)propyl)disulfide (Si−O−2S) and bis(3-
(triethoxysilyl)propyl)tetrasulfide (Si−O−4S)] as the additive
in the electrolyte. The siloxane and the long sulfur chain in
organo-polysulfides can quickly react with Li metal to generate
organic (LixSiSy/LixSiOy) and inorganic components (Li2S/
Li2S2), in which the LixSiSy/LixSiOy function as a “plasticizer”
in the inorganic phase to make the SEI layer more flexible and
robust. This stable SEI layer is robust enough to effectively
prevent the growth of lithium dendrites and greatly prolong
the service life of the battery. Therefore, the growth of lithium
deposited in our work is more uniform and compact and has
good air stability. CE and cycle stability were also improved,
even at high magnification. When using 10 wt % Si−O−4S as
the additive, an excellent cycling lifespan (1400 h) was
achieved with a low hysteresis voltage of ∼17 mV at 1.0 mA
cm−2 in a Li−Li symmetrical cell. Moreover, the lithium−
sulfur battery also exhibits long cycling stability (850 mA h g−1
at 0.5 C after 200 cycles) and good CE (99.5%). Our finding
shows that the suitable sulfur chain length can be coupled with
organic compounds such as siloxanes and co-deposited to form
much more flexible and robust hybrid SEI layers. This study
provides new insights into designing efficient electrolyte
additives to strengthen the SEI stability for a prolonged
lifespan of LMBs. Further efforts may focus on elucidating the
reaction mechanisms and the evolution of the SEI structure
and composition upon cycling to gain an in-depth under-
standing of SEI formation and evolution. In addition,
evaluating the additives in full cells can further reveal their
versatility in various LMBs. Overall, this study holds
significance for enabling safe and high-performance LMBs.
