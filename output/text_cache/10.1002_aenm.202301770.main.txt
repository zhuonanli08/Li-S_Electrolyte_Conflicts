Deciphering the Degradation Mechanism of High-Rate and
High-Energy-Density Lithium–Sulfur Pouch Cells
Qian Cheng, Zi-Xian Chen, Xi-Yao Li, Chen-Xi Bi, Furong Sun, Xue-Qiang Zhang,
Xinzhi Ma, Bo-Quan Li,* and Jia-Qi Huang*
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are widely regarded as promising
next-generation battery systems due to their impressive theoretical energy
density of 2600 Wh kg−1. However, practical high-energy-density Li–S pouch
cells suﬀer from rapid performance degradation under high working rates.
Herein, the performance degradation mechanism of 400 Wh kg−1 Li–S pouch
cells is systematically investigated under a high cycling rate of 0.2 C. Focusing
on the reduced speciﬁc capacity and increased cell polarization, the sluggish
cathodic sulfur redox kinetics under lean-electrolyte and high-rate conditions
is identiﬁed as the main limitation. Further polarization decoupling indicates
the cathodic activation polarization contributes dominantly to the increased
cell polarization. Accordingly, a delicately designed electrolyte using dimethyl
diselenide as the kinetic promoter is proposed to enable the Li–S pouch cells
to work at 0.2 C with reduced cell polarization. This work clariﬁes the sluggish
cathodic interfacial charge transfer kinetics as the main challenge for
high-energy-density Li–S batteries at high rates and is expected to inspire
rational strategy design for achieving advanced Li–S batteries.

1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have gained
worldwide attention since their commer-
cialization in the 1990s as they have
greatly met the demand for portable
electronic devices and electric vehicles.[1]
Currently, LIBs are providing new op-
portunities for constructing a sustain-
able carbon-neutralized society based on
renewable energy sources.[2] However,
the energy density of LIBs is approach-
ing their theoretical upper limit ow-
ing to the limited speciﬁc capacity of
the electrodes based on intercalation
chemistry.[3] The state-of-the-art LIBs can
hardly achieve an actual energy den-
sity over 350 Wh kg−1,[4] which severely
restricts their application in energy-
density-sensitive occasions such as elec-
tric aircrafts and spacecrafts.[5] There-
fore, developing rechargeable batteries
with higher energy density beyond 400 Wh kg−1 is of great im-
portance to meet the rapidly growing demand.[6]
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries relying on electrochemical
conversion reactions aﬀord an ultra-high theoretical energy den-
sity of up to 2600 Wh kg−1, which breaks the energy density lim-
itation of LIBs in principle.[7] Typical Li–S batteries employ ele-
mental sulfur as the cathode material and metallic Li as the an-
ode material with conventional ether-based electrolyte.[8] During
discharge, the electron-gaining sulfur cathode undergoes multi-
electron and multi-phase transformation from solid-phase ele-
mental sulfur to soluble lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, 4≤n ≤8)
and eventually to solid-phase lithium disulﬁde (Li2S2) or sulﬁde
(Li2S).[9] Accordingly, an ultra-high theoretical speciﬁc capacity
of 1672 mAh g−1 is delivered by complete S─S bond breaking
and Li─S bond formation, and the speciﬁc capacity of the lithium
metal anode is even higher (3860 mAh g−1).[10] Encouraged by
the intrinsic high-energy-density advantages, intensive research
is focusing on practical Li–S pouch cells instead of laboratory-
level coin cells to achieve practical high-energy-density at the
cell level.[11] For instance, Gong, Li, and co-workers synthesized
S@CNT-G-TiN multidimensional composite materials to regu-
late polysulﬁde shutting, and the corresponding pouch cell ex-
hibited a high energy density of 401 Wh kg−1 (0.1 C and 0.84 mA
cm−2).[12] Xie et al. developed a freeze-drying approach to fabri-
cate sulfur cathodes with canal/capillary hierarchical pores that
reduce the amount of excess electrolyte. A high energy density of

However, most high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells are cycled
at relatively low rates (generally <0.1 C or 1.0 mA cm−2) and can-
not meet the demands of fast charging and discharging for var-
ious applications, serving as a key restriction on advanced high-
energy-density Li–S batteries in practice.[15] On the other hand,
the Li–S pouch cells with high-rate working feasibility can hardly
realize high-energy-density at the same time.[16] For example,
Xue et al. proposed an electrochemically active multifunctional
LixMo6S8 interlayer with superior electronic and ionic conduc-
tivity, extra capacity contribution, and the ability to catalyze and
prevent Li2S clogging.[17] Despite the high working rate at 0.2 C
(1.3 mA cm−2), the actual energy density of the pouch cell could
hardly exceed 300 Wh kg−1. Obviously, Li–S batteries have en-
countered a bottleneck of achieving high-energy-density under
high cycling rates at the pouch cell level. Thus, it is of paramount
importance to understand the underlying performance degrada-
tion mechanism and identify the key limitation of high-rate Li–
S pouch cells so as to guide rational strategy design and realize
high-rate and high-energy-density Li–S batteries.
Herein, the performance degradation mechanism of 400 Wh
kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells under a high rate of 0.2 C
(1.5 mA cm−2) is systematically analyzed. The failure of the
400 Wh kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells at 0.2 C is attributed to the
signiﬁcantly increased cell polarization at the beginning of the
second discharge plateau as well as dramatic capacity loss. Com-
prehensive control experiments and evaluation on the cycled elec-
trodes identify that the sluggish cathodic sulfur redox kinetics
under lean-electrolyte and high-rate conditions is the main lim-
itation that causes cell failure. Further polarization decoupling
on the sulfur redox kinetics indicates the activation polarization
determined by interfacial charge transfer dominates the cell po-
larization. Accordingly, a redox comediator, dimethyl diselenide
(DMDSe), is introduced as a kinetic promoter to improve the
cathode kinetics in high-working-rate and high-energy-density
Li–S pouch cells. The pouch cell with DMDSe maintains an ac-
tual energy density of 428 Wh kg−1 and exhibits reduced cell po-
larization of 200 mV at 0.2 C (1.5 mA cm−2) as well as much
prolonged cycling lifespan of 11 cycles in comparison of 2 cycles
of the basic cell. This work reveals the sluggish cathodic interfa-
cial charge transfer kinetics as the primary cause of performance
degradation in high-energy-density and high-rate Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
400 Wh kg−1-level Li–S pouch cells were designed and assem-
bled for evaluation under high working rates. To achieve a de-
signed cell capacity of 3 Ah, ﬁve pieces of double-side cathodes
(Figure S1a, Supporting Information) and six pieces of double-
side anodes (Figure S1b, Supporting Information) separated by
polypropylene (PP) separators were folded into an electrode pile
(7 cm in length and 4 cm in width, Figure S1c, Supporting In-
formation). The cathode sulfur content was 63 wt.% with the re-
maining composition comprising conductive carbon and binder
(Figure 1a). The areal sulfur loading was 7.5 mgS cm−2 on a single
side. The thickness of the Li metal anode was 75 μm on a single
side, corresponding to an areal capacity of 15.5 mAh cm−2. The
electrolyte was conventional ether-based electrolyte and the E/S
ratio was 2.8 gele gS
−1. The detailed masses of each cell compo-
nent are listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information), according
to which the actual energy density of the pouch cells was calcu-
lated based on all the cell components including current collec-
tors and cell package. After electrolyte injection, the pouch cells
were sealed for testing (Figure 1b).
The performance of the 400 Wh kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells
at diﬀerent cycling rates was tested and analyzed. Cell 1 was gal-
vanostatically cycled at a low cycling rate of 0.025 C (0.19 mA
cm−2, 1 C = 1000 mA gS
−1) and served as the control sample. Cell
2 with an identical cell conﬁguration as Cell 1 was discharged at
0.025 C for the 1st cycle and then galvanostatically cycled at a high
working rate of 0.2 C (1.5 mA cm−2). The performance diﬀerence
of the pouch cells at diﬀerent rates is obtained by comparing Cell
1 and Cell 2. The discharge–charge curves of the two cells are ex-
hibited in Figure 1c. The discharge-speciﬁc capacity of Cell 1 and
Cell 2 at the 1st cycle of 0.025 C is as high as 1399 and 1382 mAh
g−1, respectively, enabling an actual initial energy density of 434
and 431 Wh kg−1. Both Cell 1 and Cell 2 have accomplished the
targeted energy density of 400 Wh kg−1 and their performances
are almost identical. During the 2nd cycle, however, Cell 2 cy-
cling at 0.2 C demonstrates signiﬁcant performance degradation
in comparison with Cell 1 cycling at 0.025 C regarding cell po-
larization and discharge capacity. The discharge voltage at the
discharge capacity of 400 mAh g−1 (V400) is deﬁned as an indi-
cator to quantitively compare cell polarization at the Li2S nucle-
ation stage, which is the most kinetically sluggish stage in Li–S
batteries.[18] Speciﬁcally, Cell 1 aﬀords a V400 of 2.11 V at both
the 1st and the 2nd cycle and a capacity decay of only 120 mAh
g−1 from the 1st to the 2nd cycle (Figure 1d,e). In contrast, Cell
2 exhibits a dramatic increase in cell polarization with the V400
being 2.11 V at the 1st cycle while 1.80 V at the 2nd cycle, accom-
panied by a sharp discharge capacity decay of 340 mAh g−1 (from
1382 mAh g−1 at the 1st cycle to 1042 mAh g−1 at the 2nd cycle).
During the 3rd cycle, the discharge voltage of Cell 2 drops rapidly
to the cutoﬀvoltage to cause cell failure. In comparison, there is
little decay in Cell 1 regarding cell polarization and discharge ca-
pacity, and Cell 1 maintains a discharge capacity of 1143 mAh
g−1 even at the 10th cycle (Figure S2, Supporting Information).
To sum up, the 400 Wh kg−1-level Li–S pouch cells display se-
vere cell polarization and capacity degradation when cycling at a
high working rate of 0.2 C. The increased cell polarization and de-
graded discharge capacity lead to premature cell failure and low
working feasibility of high-energy-density Li–S batteries at high
rates.

the eﬀect of electrolyte amount on cell performance degradation
at high working rate (Figure 2a). Cell 3 and Cell 2 exhibit simi-

Cell 4 was designed to investigate the eﬀect of cycling rate
on the pouch cell performances under lean electrolyte condi-
tions. Cell 4 employed the same electrode pile and the same elec-
trolyte volume as Cell 2. Cell 4 was initially discharged at 0.025
C (0.20 mA cm−2) at the 1st cycle and cycled at 0.2 C (1.6 mA
cm−2) at the 2nd cycle following the same procedure as Cell 2 but
then the cycling rate was changed back to 0.025 C (0.20 mA cm−2)
in the 3rd and the following cycles (Figure 2d). Cell 4 demon-
strates similar performances as Cell 2 during the ﬁrst two cycles
as expected regarding both the cell polarization and the discharge
capacity (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The initial energy
density of Cell 4 is 425 Wh kg−1. However, in the 3rd cycle, the
cell polarization of Cell 4 is recovered with the V400 of 2.09 V
when the cycling rate changes back to 0.025 C (Figure 2e). The
V400 of Cell 4 at the 3rd cycle is signiﬁcantly higher than that of
Cell 2 (below 1.70 V) but very close to that of Cell 1 (2.10 V), in-
dicating the cell performance degradation at 0.2 C can be fully
recovered when the working rate is reduced. Similarly, the dis-
charge capacities of Cell 4 and Cell 1 are close of being 1168
and 1229 mAh g−1 at the 3rd cycle, respectively, which are much
higher than that of Cell 2 of 265 mAh g−1 (Figure 2f). Therefore,
high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells work smoothly under lean-
electrolyte and low-rate conditions and high-rate cycling does lit-
tle irreversible damage on the pouch cells. By systematically com-
paring Cell 1–4, it is concluded that the performance degradation
of high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells is caused by working at
both high-rate and lean-electrolyte conditions. Any single condi-
tion cannot cause characteristic cell failure, and the coupling of
high working rates and lean-electrolyte volume results in rapid
performance degradation.
The high-rate cycled Cell 2 was further disassembled and ana-
lyzed to carry out in-depth investigation on the sulfur cathodes
and Li anodes including residual capacity, electrode polariza-
tion, morphology, and the sulfur species. Speciﬁcally, Cell 2 was
stopped at the charged state and disassembled. The cycled elec-
trodes were taken out for detailed analysis (Figure S5, Supporting
Information). To quantify the electrochemical performances, the
cycled electrodes were punched into small disks with a diame-
ter of 13.0 mm, matched with brand-new counter electrodes, and
reassembled in coin cells (Figure 3a,b).
The cycled anode was matched with a fresh Li foil (600 μm)
as the counter electrode for residual capacity evaluation. The re-
assembled coin cells were galvanostatically tested at a current
density of 1.5 mA cm−2 corresponding to the cycling rate of 0.2
C in the pouch cell. A cutoﬀvoltage of 100 mV was set to ensure
complete stripping of the residual Li. The residual areal capac-
ities of the cycled single-side Li anode are 11.9 and 13.3 mAh
cm−2 under lean-electrolyte volume of 20 μL and abundant elec-
trolyte volume of 40 μL, respectively (Figure 3c; Figure S6, Sup-
porting Information). In comparison with the uncycled Li anode
(75 μm on a single side corresponding to an areal capacity of 15.5
mAh cm−2), the cycled Li anode preserves 76.8% and 85.8% of the
initial capacity, respectively, which is enough to support the fol-
lowing cycles considering the cathode areal capacity is below 10
mAh cm−2. The polarization of the Li plating/stripping process
was tested using the same cell conﬁguration at the same cycling
current density. Even under lean-electrolyte conditions of 20 μL,
the polarization of the cycled Li anode was only 32 mV (Figure 3d;
Figure S7, Supporting Information), signiﬁcantly lower than the
overall cell polarization (>400 mV) when Cell 2 failed.[19] In ad-
dition, scanning electron microscope (SEM) images reveal an ac-
ceptable anode morphology change from ﬂat and smooth Li foil
before cycling to porous chunks after cycling (Figures S8 and
S9, Supporting Information). The thickness of the anode also
increases from 170 μm (double side, before cycling) to 360 μm
(double side, after cycling) (Figure S10, Supporting Informa-
tion). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was per-
formed on the cycled Li anodes for sulfur species identiﬁcation.
Li2S (159.9 eV), Li2S2 (161.7 eV), Li2SO3 (166.3 eV), and Li2SO4
(168.6 eV) are identiﬁed in the XPS spectra of S 2p as normal sul-
fur species (Figure S11, Supporting Information), and no char-
acteristics of cell failure can be identiﬁed.[20] Consequently, the
remaining capacity of the cycled Li anode is suﬃcient to support
the subsequent cycles and Li anode polarization is not the main
limitation to cause cell failure.

3.5 μL mgS

To gain further insights into the sluggish sulfur redox kinet-
ics under lean-electrolyte and high-rate conditions, quantitative
polarization decoupling on the cathodic redox kinetics was per-
formed on the reassembled coin cells with cycled cathode using
an electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) combined gal-
vanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) method. Typi-
cally, the overall polarization can be decoupled into three compo-
nents regarding concentration polarization, ohmic polarization,
and activation polarization. Concentration polarization (𝜂con) is
primarily attributed to the disparity in the concentration of the
reactants between electrode surface and bulk electrolyte. Ohmic
polarization (𝜂ohm) is the result of low electronic or ionic conduc-
tivity in electrode or bulk electrolyte, and activation polarization
(𝜂ac) is due to the activation energy of the electrochemical redox
reaction required for interfacial charge transfer. 𝜂ohm can be cal-
culated according to the Ohm’s law, where the bulk ohmic resis-
tance can be easily acquired based on the EIS results (Figure S17,
Supporting Information). Meanwhile, 𝜂con can be distinguished
according to the GITT proﬁles (Figure 4a). Concretely, the cell
voltage drops during the discharge process and rises during the
rest process in each GITT step (Figure S18, Supporting Informa-
tion). 𝜂ac and 𝜂ohm are current-dependent according to the But-
ler‒Volmer model and the Ohm’s law, respectively, and they dis-
appear immediately when the cell is rested without applied cur-
rent. In comparison, 𝜂con follows the Nernst–Planck equation and
decreases gradually along time relaxation during the rest process.
Therefore, the gradual voltage recovery to the thermodynamic
states during the GITT rest process is identiﬁed as the 𝜂con, and
𝜂ac can be calculated accordingly after deducting 𝜂con and 𝜂ohm
from the overall polarization.

larization can be accurately quantiﬁed as the discharge process
proceeds. The GITT proﬁles exhibit large cell polarization es-
pecially at the beginning of the second discharge plateau under
lean-electrolyte of E/S ratio = 3.5 μL mgS
−1 and a high rate of 0.2
C (1.5 mA cm−2), which resembles the characteristics of Cell 2
and is believed to cause cell failure. Polarization decoupling was

50 mV when the electrolyte volume is adequate, or the working
rate is low (Figure 4c). Besides, 𝜂con is similar under the three
testing conditions to further verify diﬀusion is not the cathodic
kinetic limitation factor (Figure S22, Supporting Information).
Similar conclusion is further drawn on the polarization decou-
pling of practical pouch cells (Figure S23, Supporting Informa-
tion). The 𝜂ac increases more rapidly along discharge, contributes
to a large portion of the overall polarization (51.2% at the dis-
charge capacity of 200 mAh g−1), and causes premature cell fail-
ure (Figure 4d). The above comprehensive analysis indicates that
the cathode activation polarization corresponding to the sluggish
interfacial charge transfer kinetics constitutes the main limita-
tion to cause cell failure under lean-electrolyte and high-rate con-
ditions. Targeted strategies for promoting the cathodic interfacial
charge transfer kinetics are of great signiﬁcance to promote the
rate feasibility of high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells.
Homogeneous kinetic promoters have been veriﬁed to ef-
fectively accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics especially under
practical working conditions.[21] Particularly, redox comediators
(coRM) that react reversibly with lithium polysulﬁdes to gen-
erate lithium organo-polysulﬁdes demonstrate superior kinetic
promotion capability by facilitating the chemical disproportion-
ation and comproportionation reactions of lithium polysulﬁdes
and increasing the deposition dimension of lithium sulﬁde.[22]
Herein, DMDSe is introduced as a kinetic promoter to acceler-
ate the charge-transfer rate, overcome the enlarged activation po-
larization, and promote the working feasibility of high-energy-
density Li–S pouch cells at high rates. Cathodic polarization de-
coupling was conducted using the EIS–GITT method on the Li–
S coin cells with the DMDSe-based electrolyte (Figures S24 and
S25, Supporting Information). 𝜂ac reduces signiﬁcantly from 317
to 51 mV with the addition of DMDSe while 𝜂ohm and 𝜂con main-
tain similar (Figure S26, Supporting Information), verifying the
DMDSe kinetic promoter can signiﬁcantly reduce 𝜂ac by acceler-
ating the cathodic interfacial charge transfer kinetics.

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, the performance degradation mechanism of
400 Wh kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells under a high cycling rate
of 0.2 C (1.5 mA cm−2) is systematically analyzed. The signiﬁ-
cantly increased cell polarization at the beginning of the second
discharge plateau as well as the sharply decreased capacity are the
main characteristics for cell failure. The sluggish cathodic sulfur
redox kinetics under lean-electrolyte and high-rate conditions is
identiﬁed as the main limitation that causes cell failure. Further
polarization decoupling on the sulfur redox kinetics indicates
the activation polarization of the interfacial charge transfer pro-
cess dominates the cell polarization. To address the above issue,
DMDSe is introduced as a kinetic promoter to improve the cath-
ode kinetics in high-working-rate and high-energy-density Li–S
pouch cells. The pouch cell with DMDSe-based electrolyte main-
tains an actual energy density of 428 Wh kg−1 and exhibits re-
duced cell polarization of 200 mV at 0.2 C (1.5 mA cm−2) as well
as much prolonged cycling lifespan of 11 cycles in comparison
of 2 cycles of the basic cell. This work clariﬁes that the sluggish
cathodic interfacial charge transfer kinetics as the primary cause
of performance degradation in high-energy-density and high-rate
Li–S batteries and enlightens rational design of promotion strate-
gies to construct advanced Li–S batteries.
