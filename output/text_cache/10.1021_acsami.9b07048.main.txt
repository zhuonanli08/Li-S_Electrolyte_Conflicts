ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur batteries oﬀer a high theoretical gravimetric
energy density and low cost, but the full utilization of the sulfur electrode has
been limited by the premature passivation of insulating lithium sulﬁde (Li2S).
Anion has been one of the major parameters to improve Li−S batteries in
addition to solvent, additives, and electrode structures. Here, we reveal the
role of anion donicity on the passivation of Li−S battery and its underlying
working mechanism. We show that anions with high donicity eﬀectively
reduce the charge-transfer resistance during the cycling of Li−S cells and
alleviate the Li2S passivation by transforming the dense ﬁlm Li2S to porous
three-dimensional ﬂake Li2S. UV−vis spectroscopy revealed that anions with higher donicity exhibit higher Li2S4 solubility,
which is consistent with their stronger bonding to Li+, as revealed by nuclear magnetic resonance and density functional theory
calculations. Our study reveals the role of anion donicity in Li2S passivation and its underlying mechanism, oﬀering rational
design consideration for electrolyte salts in achieving high sulfur utilization and high energy eﬃciency for Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: anion donicity, lithium bromide, electrochemical kinetics, lithium ion solvation, lithium sulﬁde deposition,
lithium−sulfur battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are a promising energy
storage system owing to its high speciﬁc energy density
(∼2600 W h kg−1).1,2 However, the development of Li−S
batteries is hindered by low sulfur utilization, limited rate
capability, and rapid capacity fading.3−6 These challenges are
deeply related to the soluble nature of reaction intermediates
and premature electrode passivation by the insulating
discharge product lithium sulﬁde (Li2S).7−9
Manipulating the electrolyte solvents3,10−16 and addi-
tives17−24 in Li−S batteries has been shown to eﬀectively
improve the electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries.
Redox-active electrolyte additives including LiI,17−19 LiBr,20
and LiNO3
21,22,24 have been reported eﬀective in improving
Li−S batteries. For instance, LiNO3, a common Li anode
additive for suppressing redox shuttle21,24 has been reported to
catalyze the conversion of polysulﬁde to element sulfur via an
NO3 radical intermediate beyond 2.5 V.22 LiI was reported as
an eﬀective additive by Wu et al.,17 forming a protective layer
on the surface of both the Li anode and Li2S-based cathode.
Peng et al.18 reported the LiI/I2 redox couple acting as
extrinsic healing agents. I2 rapidly oxidizes bulk Li2S to sulfur
particles, further promoting the subsequent dissolution of Li2S
into polysulﬁdes (Li2S + (x −1)S = Li2Sx), in which I2 was
generated by charging to 3.2 V.18 Chen and Lu19 reported that
the presence of I−/I3
−couple increases the discharge capacity
associated with the Li2S formation, which was hypothesized
resulting from the redox interaction between the I−/I3
−and
polysulﬁde/Li2S couples. LiBr was reported as a novel
electrolyte additive oﬀering an in situ surface layer on the
cathode to prevent polysulﬁde shuttle, in which case precycling
in high voltage (2.5−3.6 V) or “activation” is needed to
achieve a robust surface layer on the cathode.20 The
enhancement was attributed to the formation of DME(−H)
radicals induced by the oxidation of Br−above 3.5 V.20
In addition to the redox interactions between the additives/
salts and polysulﬁde, the role of additives/salts on the solvation
dynamics of polysulﬁde is expected to dictate the deposition
kinetics and morphologies of insulating Li2S, which in turn
controls the achievable discharge capacity. For instance,
phosphorus pentasulﬁde (P2S5) promotes the dissolution of
Li2S by the formation of the Li2Sx/P2S5 complex.25 Chu et al.26
reported that high donor number anions can induce the three-
dimensional (3D) growth of Li2S, which was attributed to the
increased solubility of the end product Li2S. However, the role
of anions on the reaction intermediate, polysulﬁde, was not
studied, which we believe plays a decisive role in mediating the
deposition of Li2S.
In this study, we reveal the role of anion donicity on the
passivation of Li−S batteries and its underlying working

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.5%), 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL, 99.5%), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 97%),
sulfur (S, 99.98%), lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane) sulfonamide
(LiTFSI, 99.95%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99.99%), lithium bromide
(LiBr, 99.999%), and lithium chloride (LiCl, 99.99%) were received
from Sigma-Aldrich. Ketjen Black EC-600JD (KB) was received from
AzkoNobel Ltd. Super P was received from MTI Co. and vulcan
carbon (VC) was received from Cobat Ltd. Carbon paper
(HCP020N) was received from Shanghai Hesen Electric Co. Ltd.
Lithium foil was received from Shenzhen Meisen Electro-Mechanical
Co. Ltd. LiNO3, LiTFSI, LiBr, and LiCl were dried overnight under
dynamic vacuum in a glass oven (Büchi, Switzerland) at 110 °C.
2.2. Electrode Fabrication. Sulfur powder and VC were mixed at
a weight ratio of 7:3 in the DME solvent and then heated at 155 °C
overnight. The weight ratio of sulfur was measured by thermal gravity
analysis (PerkinElmer STA 6000), with the temperature increasing
from room temperature to 550 °C at 10 °C min−1. The sulfur content
of the S/VC composite was determined to be 70.3 wt %. Then, the S/
VC composites were mixed with Super P and poly(vinylidene
diﬂuoride) binder in NMP to form a uniform slurry at a weight ratio
of 8:1:1. Finally, the slurry was coated onto a piece of Φ 16 mm
carbon paper and dried at 60 °C overnight.
2.3. Preparation of LAGP Electrolyte. A NASICON-type
structured Li1.5Al0.5Ge1.5(PO4)3 (LAGP)27 lithium ion conductor
(Figure S1) was synthesized by a solid-state reaction method.
Stoichiometric amounts of LiOH·H2O (10 wt % excess), Al2O3,
GeO2, and NH4H2PO4 were thoroughly mixed by ball milling at 400
rpm for 12 h in acetone and then heated at 600 °C for 2 h. The
mixture was then cooled, ball milled, and heated at 800 °C for 6 h.
After this, the milling process was repeated, and the obtained powder
was dried and pressed into pellets and sintered at 850 °C for 6 h.
2.4. Cell Assembly. Li−S cells were assembled in a glovebox by
placing a piece of Φ 19 mm Celgard separator onto a Φ 16 mm Li
foil, followed by the addition of 40 μL of 0.5 M LiTFSI/0.4 M LiNO3
electrolyte. Then, a piece of Φ 19 mm LAGP solid electrolyte was
placed onto the Celgard separator before stacking the Φ 16 mm sulfur
cathode. A 40 μL electrolyte containing diﬀerent anions was added in
the cathode side. Above the cathode, a piece of Φ 16 mm no. 400
SS316 mesh was added as a current collector and an SS316 spring was
employed to ﬁx the electrodes and to provide electrical contact with
the top of the cell. For the cell with the Celgard separator, the
electrolytes at the anode and cathode were identical, and the assembly
diﬀerence is that the LAGP solid electrolyte is removed. The sulfur
loading is 1 mg/cm2 in the electrochemical tests but 2 mg/cm2 in the
cycle performance tests at 0.3 C. For the polysulﬁde battery assembly,
two layers of carbon paper (Φ 12 mm) were placed onto the LAGP
solid electrolyte. A 40 μL of 0.1 M Li2S8 catholyte with diﬀerent anion
compositions (Table 1) was used as the sulfur source. Li2S8 catholyte
was prepared in the same way as that of Li2S4 except for the ratio of
Li2S/S8. The cells (Figure S2) were assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox
(H2O < 1.0 ppm, O2 < 1.0 ppm, Etelux, China).
2.5. Electrochemical Measurements. All the electrochemical
characterizations were performed using a VMP3 electrochemical
testing unit (Bio-Logic). EIS measurements used sinusoidal voltage
oscillations of 20 mV amplitude at the open-circuit voltage (OCV) of
the cells. The oscillation frequencies were swept from 1 MHz to 1 Hz
with three repetitions for every test. An equivalent circuit shown in
Figure S3 was used to ﬁt all the EIS data by a nonlinear calculation
method. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT)
for the cell discharge/charge was conducted from OCV to 1.8 V and
then from 1.8 to 3 V at 0.2 C with a 5 min discharge/charge interval
and 30 min delays. Galvanostatic discharge/charge tests were
conducted with 1.8−3 V at 0.1 C and 1.4−3 V at 0.3 C. For rate
performance tests, the cutoﬀvoltage was 1.8−3 V, with the rate
ranging from 0.1 to 1 C. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) of blank
electrolytes were conducted with sulfur-free electrodes in four
electrolytes on Bio-Logic at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s−1 from 1.6 to
3.0 V. The calculation of speciﬁc discharge/charge capacities is based
on the elemental sulfur mass.
2.6. Preparation of Saturated Polysulﬁde Solution (Nomi-
nally “Li2S4”).10,28 Saturated “Li2S4” solutions were prepared by
mixing stoichiometric amounts of Li2S and sulfur in LiTFSI, LiTFSI/
LiNO3, LiNO3, and LiBr electrolytes and stirred at room temperature
for over 48 h in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox. Precipitates were observed in all
solutions to ensure the saturation concentration. We stirred the
solution at room temperature to avoid the possible reaction between
the polysulﬁde and the redox-active salt (i.e., LiBr and LiNO3) at high
temperature.
2.7. Materials Characterization. 2.7.1. X-ray Diﬀraction. X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) tests were performed using an X-ray diﬀractometer
(SmartLab) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The angle range
was from 30° to 60° at 0.5°/min. All samples were obtained after the
third discharge. Before the test, the samples were rinsed for 10−15
times with DME to dissolve the polysulﬁdes. Then, the samples were
sealed in a special CR-2025 coin cell with 10 mm Kapton windows
before the XRD test.
2.7.2. Scanning Electron Microscopy. SEM was performed using a
JSM-7800F ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL,
Japan).
2.7.3. NMR Spectroscopy. 7Li NMR spectroscopy was conducted
on a Bruker AV-400Q magnet with a 5 mm Z-gradient broad probe.
The reference sample was 3 M lithium chloride in D2O, and the
reference solution was placed in a standard 5 mm NMR tube inserted
coaxially into the sample tube. Each spectrum was repeated at least 16
times to negate the eﬀects of the ﬁeld drift.
2.7.4. UV−Vis Spectroscopy. The UV−vis spectroscope
(SEC2000; ALS; Japan) was switched on 30 min before the test.
The UV−vis data were collected every 0.5 s. The Li2S4 solution with
LiTFSI, LiTFSI/LiNO3, LiNO3, and LiBr was diluted 100 times
before UV−vis spectroscopy.
2.8. Computational Methods. DFT based ﬁrst-principles

exchange−correlation functional was dealt with by the generalized
gradient approximation of Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof type,31 and the
core electrons were modeled by the projector-augmented wave
method.32 A cubic box with the size of 40 × 40 × 40 Bohr3 was used
as the periodic cell. The cutoﬀenergy for the plane-wave basis
expansion was 20 Ha, and the k-space sampling was restricted to the

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We select a series of Li-containing salts with a span of donicity
including LiBr, LiNO3, and LiTFSI (Table 1), in which LiBr
and LiNO3 exhibit higher donicity than the electrolyte solvent
molecule (DOL/DME) to ensure the anion eﬀects are not
shuttered by the solvent molecule. All the electrolytes have the
same Li+ concentration (1.5 M) and a based salt of LiTFSI,
which has the lowest donicity and is the most commonly used
salt in Li−S batteries. The compositions of the four electrolytes
are designed with a gradual increase of anion donicity from 1.5
M LiTFSI (term “LiTFSI”), 1.1 M LiTFSI/0.4 M LiNO3
(term “LiTFSI/LiNO3”), 0.5 M LiTFSI/1.0 M LiNO3 (term
“LiNO3”), and 0.5 M LiTFSI/0.4 M LiNO3/0.6 M LiBr (term
“LiBr”). The Li−S batteries use identical electrolytes in the
anode (0.5 M LiTFSI/0.4 M LiNO3) and a solid-state
electrolyte (LAGP)27 to eliminate the capacity inﬂuence
from polysulﬁde dissolution.
The charge-transfer resistance and voltage hysteresis of Li−S
reactions reduce as the anion donicity increases. Figure 1
shows the galvanostatic discharge and charge proﬁles of Li−S
cell using the four electrolytes at 0.1 C. EIS measurements
were conducted at OCV every 2 h during discharge/charge
until reaching the cutoﬀvoltage. The Nyquist plots and the
EIS ﬁtting results are shown in Figure S3 (Supporting
Information). All the results show two depressed semicircles
followed by a sloping line. The high-frequency semicircle (1
MHz to 50 kHz) could reﬂect the interfacial contact resistance
between LAGP and the liquid electrolyte; the middle-
frequency semicircle (50 kHz to 10 Hz) can be attributed to
the charge-transfer resistance of the sulfur/electrolyte inter-
face; and the sloping line at low frequency is associated with
the resistance of the polysulﬁde diﬀusion in the cath-
ode.27,37−39 As shown in Figure 1, the charge-transfer
resistance of the sulfur/electrolyte interface and the voltage
hysteresis of Li−S reactions reduce from LiBr, LiNO3, and
LiTFSI/LiNO3 to LiTFSI, which is in direct accordance with
the anion donicity LiBr > LiNO3 > LiTFSI/LiNO3 > LiTFSI.
As the Ohmic resistance and interfacial resistance are similar
for all cases (Figure S4), the reduced overpotential can be
attributed to the enhanced charge-transfer kinetics in the
presence of high-donicity anions. The Ohmic resistance and
interfacial resistance were relatively stable during discharge and
charge. In contrast, the charge-transfer resistance continuously
increases during discharge and reaches the maximum at the
fully discharged state (Figure 1a), which is indicative of the
formation of insulating Li2S.27,40 Subsequently, the charge-
transfer resistance decreases continuously during charge, which
is attributed to the decomposition of insulating Li2S.27,40 These
observations show that the high-donicity anions alleviate the
Li2S passivation and improve the electrochemical kinetics of
the sulfur cathode. To clarify the role of Br−anion in the
discharge capacity without the inﬂuence of NO3
−in the
electrolyte, we further evaluated the discharge capacity with
varying LiTFSI/LiBr ratios. Figure S5 shows that the discharge
capacity increased from 873 to 1498 mA h/g when LiBr
increased from 0 to 1.5 M, which further conﬁrms the role of
Br−anion in increasing the sulfur utilization.
To evaluate if the reduced voltage hysteresis is mainly
caused by improved kinetics, we conducted GITT to evaluate
the quasi-equilibrium reaction potential of Li−S reactions in
the four electrolytes (Figure 2). Three GITT measurements
for each electrolyte were conducted, and the voltage hystereses
with error bars are shown in Figure S6. Interestingly, the quasi-
equilibrium potentials with diﬀerent anions are similar to each
other in the ﬁrst discharge potential at ∼2.35 V and the second
discharge potential at ∼2.15 V,41,42 conﬁrming that the
reduced voltage hysteresis can be mainly attributed to the
improved reaction kinetics.
To further understand how anions aﬀect the deposition of
the Li−S solid discharge products, we investigate the inﬂuence
of anions on the morphologies of the discharge electrodes.
First, the XRD patterns conﬁrm the formation of Li2S in all the
electrolytes (Figure 3f). Figure 3a shows the SEM image of the
pristine S/C composite with a uniform mixture of sulfur in the
carbon network. Figure 3b−e shows the discharge electrode
after full discharge in LiTFSI, LiTFSI/LiNO3, LiNO3, and LiBr
at 0.1 C. For the weak donating anions, LiTFSI, and LiTFSI/
LiNO3 electrolytes, the discharge product prefers to grow like a
two-dimensional (2D) ﬁlm covering on the surface of the
cathode, which results in poor electron transport and Li+ ion
transport because of the insulating property of Li2S and is
consistent with our pseudo-in situ EIS and GITT results. As
the anion donicity increases (from Figure 3b−e), the
morphology of the discharge product evolved from a 2D
ﬁlm-like morphology to a 3D ﬂake-like morphology, which is
most evidenced in the LiBr electrolyte (Figure 3e). The size
and thickness of the nanoﬂakes increase from LiNO3 (∼200

Consistent with the observations in Figure 3 using S/C
electrodes, Li2S formed in the presence of low anion donicity
shows ﬁlm-like morphology, whereas Li2S formed in the
presence of high anion donicity shows ﬂake-like morphology. It
clearly shows that low-donicity anions passivate the electrode
earlier and more severely than high-donicity anions. These
observations can be analogous to Li2O2 formation in Li−O2
batteries: strong donating anions or the addition of water
promote the solution-mediated processes of LiO2 intermedi-

achieving higher discharge capacity.34,43,44 The morphological
changes of Li2S support our mechanism that strong donating
anions help alleviating the surface Li2S passivation ﬁlm.
Anions’ inﬂuence on Li2S morphology can be attributed to
the modiﬁed Li+ solvation and the resulting polysulﬁde
solubility. We examine the eﬀect of anions on the solvation
environment of Li+ via 7Li NMR. Figure 4a shows that the 7Li
peak shifts downﬁeld as the donicity of the anion increases,
indicating the moiety surrounding Li+ ions exhibit stronger
electron-donating power with increasing anion donicity.45−47
On the basis of 7Li NMR chemical shifts, the degree of Li+ ion
solvation is consistent with the anion donicity in the order of
LiBr > LiNO3 > LiTFSI/LiNO3 > LiTFSI. We further evaluate
the inﬂuence of anions on the solubility of Li2S4 as it is the
reactant of the second discharge plateau (i.e., precursor of Li2S
deposition) and is typically in the saturated state in Li−S
battery cells (<0.5 M Li2S4 based on elemental sulfur, E/S =
62.4 mL/gsulfur).48 We prepared saturated polysulﬁde solution
(nominally “Li2S4”) in all salt compositions and stirred them
over 48 h. Clariﬁed liquids from all compositions were diluted
by 100 times and examined by UV−vis spectroscopy (Figure
4b). The UV−vis spectra of the background solutions without
polysulﬁde and the repeated UV−vis spectrum are shown in
Figure S10. As LiNO3- and LiBr-containing background
solutions show strong absorbance below 320 nm (Figure
S10a), we focus on analyzing polysulﬁde species at wavelengths
above 320−350 nm to avoid the interference of the salt itself.
As shown in Figure 4b, all polysulﬁde solutions show the
absorbance of S6
2−(475 nm49) and S4
2−(420 nm49−51).
Interestingly, the intensity of the polysulﬁde absorbance (e.g.,
S6
2−and S4

LiTFSI/LiNO3 > LiTFSI (repeated in Figure S10b), indicating
the highest polysulﬁde solubility for LiBr followed by LiNO3 >
LiTFSI/LiNO3 > LiTFSI, which shows the same trend as the
degree of Li+ ion solvation determined by 7Li NMR (Figure
4a). This conﬁrms that polysulﬁde solubility increases with the
donicity of the anions, which triggers the solution-mediated
mechanism for 3D ﬂake-like Li2S deposition.26,34,52 As a result,
the passivation eﬀect is greatly alleviated with the high-donicity
anions. We believe that the S4
2−anion plays a deterministic
role to facilitate the solution/chemical pathway of sulfur
reduction in the DOL/DME electrolyte tested in this work
(instead of the S3

polysulﬁde solution in all electrolytes (Figure 4b) show that
S4
2−is the major polysulﬁde species, whereas no absorbance
for the S3

consistent with the reported literature showing that S4
2−is the
dominant reaction intermediate in low-donicity solvents (e.g.,
DOL/DME, used in this study), whereas the S3
•−radical
primarily exists in high-donicity solvents (e.g., dimethyl
sulfoxide).3,10,53,54

optimized structures of Li2S4 in solutions containing LiBr,
LiNO3, and LiTFSI molecules are shown in Figure 4c−e. The
results show that LiBr binds with Li2S4 mainly through the Li−
S and Li−Br bonds, whereas LiNO3 and LiTFSI bind with it
mainly through the Li−O bond. Another interesting ﬁnding is
that LiBr tends to break the cyclic Li2S4 structure by dragging
one Li atom out of it, which then promotes the dissolution of
Li2S4. Incorporating all these factors, the average binding
energies between Li2S4 and LiBr, LiNO3, and LiTFSI are
calculated to be 1.58, 1.41, and 1.10 eV, respectively, indicating
LiBr has the strongest binding with Li2S4, whereas LiTFSI has
the weakest among the samples studied. This high binding
energy between LiBr and Li2S4 is mainly attributed to two
factors: one is the strong binding of Li−S and Li−Br bonds,
and the other is the low steric eﬀect between the neighboring
anions. Therefore, Li2S4 is predicted to have the highest
solubility in solutions with LiBr followed by LiNO3 and
LiTFSI, which is consistent with our experimental results.
The cycling stability of all electrolytes at an increased C rate
was investigated, as shown in Figure 5a. The Li−S cell with the
LiTFSI electrolyte delivers an initial discharge capacity of 771
mA h/g and maintains 774 mA h/g after 100 cycles. In
contrast, the LiBr electrolyte delivers an initial discharge
capacity of 1211 mA h/g and retains 1131 mA h/g after 100
cycles. The substantial capacity enhancement associated with
LiBr was stable over 100 cycles with a high Coulombic
eﬃciency (>99.0%) (Figure 5a,b), conﬁrming that using high-
donicity anions to alleviate passivation is eﬀective and long-
lasting in Li−S batteries. The discharge/charge proﬁles of
speciﬁc cycles are shown in Figure S12. We note that the
capacity of the LiNO3 electrolyte gradually decayed compared
to other electrolytes, which can be attributed to the
decomposition of LiNO3,21,58 as evidenced by the CVs of
the four blank electrolytes (Figure S13) showing the higher
reduction potential of LiNO3 compared to the other three
electrolytes.
We further investigate anions’ inﬂuence on the rate
capability at increasing rates to 1 C. To avoid large polarization
from LAGP at increasing rates, a Celgard separator was used in
rate capability measurements (Figure 5c,d). As shown in
Figure 5c, the capacity of LiBr is increased by 39% (240 mA h/
g) compared to that of LiTFSI at 1 C. The Li−S cell with the
LiBr electrolyte delivered discharge capacities of 1241, 1118,
1046, and 847 mA h/g at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C, respectively,
and subsequently recovered back to 1052 mA h/g at 0.2 C.
The rate capability measurements suggest that the strong
donating salt LiBr improved the electrochemical performance
(e.g., discharge capacity and voltage hysteresis) at all rates
upon cycling. For low-donicity anions, for example, LiTFSI,
the discharge capacities at the rates of 0.5 and 1 C are similar,
and both are low (675 mA h/g vs 618 mA h/g), indicating that
the electrode passivation is severe and that only a low
discharge capacity can be achieved regardless of the C rate.
The achieved capacity in this case can be attributed to the
amount of carbon surface available for Li2S deposition, which
is the same for both 0.5 and 1 C. For high-donicity anions, for
example, LiBr, the discharge capacity at 0.5 C (1046 mA h/g)
is much higher than that at 1 C (847 mA h/g), and both are
higher than that achieved with low-donicity anions at the same
rates. This suggests that the improved polysulﬁde solubility
associated with LiBr is suﬃcient to provide eﬀective mediation
at a low Li2S deposition rate (i.e., low C rate), but it becomes
less suﬃcient when the Li2S deposition rate increases at
increasing C rate, leading to a larger drop compared to the case
in low-donicity cells whose capacity is low at both low and high
C rates. Although increasing polysulﬁde solubility could beneﬁt
reaction kinetics and mitigate electrode passivation, it could
potentially lead to increasing polysulﬁde shuttle and dis-
solution59−61 if the solubility is too high. To investigate if the
irreversible loss of polysulﬁde is increased owing to the higher
polysulﬁde solubility, we examine the capacity retention in the
rate capability tests (Figure 5d) where only the porous
separator Celgard was used. As shown in Table S1, the capacity
retention from the 6th cycle (0.2 C) to the 40th cycle (0.2 C)
for all electrolytes are comparable (∼90%), suggesting that the
loss of polysulﬁde was not increased owing to the high-donicity
anions.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we combined electrochemical, spectroscopic,
morphological, and DFT investigations to study the role of
anion donicity on the passivation of a Li−S battery and its
underlying working mechanism. Pseudo-in situ EIS measure-
ments showed that anions with high donicity eﬀectively reduce
the charge-transfer resistance during the discharge and charge
of Li−S cells and alleviate Li2S passivation. SEM measure-
ments showed that high-donicity anions promote porous 3D
ﬂake-like Li2S (solution process), which is in contrast to the
dense ﬁlm-like Li2S in the low-donicity anions. These are
consistent with the higher solubility of Li2S4 and stronger Li+
solvation in the presence of high-donicity anions revealed by
UV−vis spectroscopy and NMR, respectively, which can be
attributed to the stronger binding between Li2S4 and the
anions, as revealed by DFT. A solution pathway mechanism is
proposed to explain how Li2S4 solubility aﬀects the formation
process of Li2S. Our study elucidates the role of anion donicity
in Li2S passivation and its underlying mechanism, oﬀering
rational design consideration for electrolyte salts for achieving
high sulfur utilization and high-energy eﬃciency of Li−S
batteries.
