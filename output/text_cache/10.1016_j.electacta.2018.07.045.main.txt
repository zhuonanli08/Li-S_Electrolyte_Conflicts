A rate-dependent nucleation theory is developed to analyze the dependence of the capacity of lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) batteries on discharge rates. The theory is based on expressing the nucleation rate of solid
products in terms of the surface oversaturation of the precipitating species and rewriting the classical
Kolmogorov-Avrami model in the form of a differential system of equations. By coupling this system of
equations with the multicomponent transport equations we are able to explain qualitatively well the
decrease of the speciﬁc capacity with increasing discharge rates and the characteristics of the discharge
curves under variable discharge currents in Li-S batteries. Special attention is given to accurately
determine the values of model parameters from experimental data, including the solubility constants of
the solid products and the mobilities and diffusivities of the ions. The anodes of the batteries used in our
analysis were made using Li foils and the cathodes were fabricated using freestanding carbon nanotube
foams, on which solid sulfur was uniformly distributed to maximize the dissolution rate. The experi-
mental data combined with the theoretical analysis show that the capacity limitation in our batteries is
due to surface passivation by the ﬁnal reaction products. In order to increase the speciﬁc capacity of Li-S
batteries under normal discharge rates we conclude that it is better to avoid the solid precipitation of
intermediate lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPS) by using electrolytes with a high solubility of LiPS. In addition, it
is better to use catalyst particles in the cathode that would promote the nucleation rate at speciﬁed
locations. The number of catalyst particles should not be too small to avoid electrolyte oversaturation or
too large to avoid the rapid passivation of the carbon surface with solid products.

1. Introduction
Environmentally friendly, relatively inexpensive because of the
low cost of sulfur, and having a high theoretical capacity, lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) batteries are promising candidates to replace tradi-
tional Li-ion batteries in many applications from portable elec-
tronics to electric vehicle transportation and grid storage. Li-S
batteries have a theoretical capacity of 1672 mAh/gS and, when
built using a Li metal anode, their speciﬁc energy density is 3e5
folds higher than that of Li-ion batteries [1e5]. Despite their large
speciﬁc capacity, the current Li-S batteries with liquid electrolyte
have a large electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio, which decreases their
practical capacity much below the theoretical limit [6e10].
Designing Li-S batteries with low E/S ratios will require a detailed
understanding of the physical and chemical processes that take
place inside the cell during discharge and charge. This understating
will likely come from a combination of experimental and theoret-
ical
investigations
of

Although there is much experimental research on Li-S batteries
the number of theoretical and numerical investigations is rather
limited. Most of the existing theoretical models are based on the
mathematical model proposed by Kumaresan et al. [11] and can
describe fairly well the discharge voltage under low discharge
currents. However, the existing models are somewhat contradic-
tory and often use assumptions that are either far away from the
experimentally measured data or not veriﬁed under highdischarge
rates. These models generally fail to describe the rate-dependent
capacity of conventional Li-S batteries. In this article we combine

There is much debate in the literature about the reasons for the
rate-dependence of the discharge capacity in Li-S batteries. We are
currently aware of only a few simulation attempts to model the
decrease of the discharge capacity of Li-S batteries with increasing
discharge rate [12e17]. Zhang et al. [12,13] have attributed the
decrease of the discharge capacity to the sluggish transport char-
acteristics or anions and cations in the electrolyte, which results in
a deﬁciency of sulfur ions in the cathode at high discharge rates.
The electrolyte used in Ref. [12] is sulfolane-based which is possible
to have a very low conductivity. To reproduce the voltage proﬁles at
high discharge rates the authors decreased the mobility and
diffusivity coefﬁcients of the ions by 1-to-2 orders of magnitude
below
the
values
currently
used
for
1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME):1,3-dioxolane (DOL)-based electrolytes. In this way, the
electrolyte conductivity becomes 1-to-2 orders of magnitude
smaller than the values of the conductivity reported experimen-
tally for DME:DOL (1:1 v:v). For instance, the ionic conductivity of
Li2S6 calculated using the values of the mobility coefﬁcients used in
Ref. [12] is 0.068 S/m, which is more than one order of magnitude
smaller than the experimental value of 1 S/m in DME:DOL (1:1 v:v).
Although it is possible to design electrolytes with high viscosity,
this model cannot explain the decrease of the capacity at high
discharge rates in batteries with typical electrolytes, which have a
conductivity of around 1 S/m. In addition, the model predicts a
relatively fast recovery of the capacity after the rate of the discharge
is decreased from 1 C to 0.1 C. The recovery time should be of the
order of seconds according to the model parameters, while most of
the experimental data shows that the recovery is much slower, of
the order of hours.
Thangavel et al. [15] explain the decrease of the discharge ca-
pacity by a combination of two phenomena. First, they assume that
the cathodes simulated in their analysis are made of porous parti-
cles with pore radius of 3 nm. At high discharge rates the entrance
of these pores is being clogged with Li2S, thus reducing the capacity
of the battery. This model explains qualitatively well the rate-
dependence of the total capacity, however it is applicable specif-
ically to cathodes made of porous particles, such as the ones pre-
sented in their work. If one applies the model to cathodes made of
nanoscale non-porous particles or CNT networks (like in this work),
the model predicts that the discharge capacity does not change
signiﬁcantly with the discharge rate. Second, the authors assume
that the Li2S reaction product can grow on the surface of the par-
ticles up to a maximum tunneling thickness of 5 nm. This
assumption is not supported by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images taken by other
authors, which show that the reaction product can grow in shapes
as large as 500 nm [14,18] or even 1 mm and above [19e21]. In
addition, the probability of electron tunneling across the potential
barrier of the insulating layer is negligibly small if the thicknesses
of the barrier is larger than 2 nm and the voltage drop across the
barrier is below 1 V (see, for instance [22]), which implies that the
carbon is passivated relatively fast, as soon as the Li2S starts
depositing on the surface of the carbon.
Hofmann et al. [16] use a slightly modiﬁed version of Kumar-
esan's model [11] and showed that they correctly reproduce the
ﬁnal capacities of the Li-S batteries if the consumption fraction of
solid S8(s) is carefully considered. Their model considers four re-
actions at the cathode, polysulﬁde shuttle effects, and irreversible
transport of active material from cathode to anode. Although the
model does not explain why the elemental sulfur is not fully dis-
solved and consumed during discharge, it still provides a simple
way to model capacity loss at high discharge rates.
A model that recently received increased attention in the
research community is to attribute the premature death of Li-S
batteries to surface coverage with solid Li2S [14,17,23]. To explain
the dependence of capacity on discharge rate, Ren et al. [14] assume
that the nucleation rate is rate-dependent according to an equation
initially proposed by Milchev et al. [24] for the attachment rate in
the electrolytic nucleation of metals and later used by Lau et al. [25]
to study precipitation effects in Li-air batteries. However, the rate-
dependent precipitation theory presented in Ref. [24] was devel-
oped for the electro-crystallization of metals, in which the ions in
the electrolyte attach directly to the surface of the electrode. In the
case of Li-S batteries, after the formation of the initial nuclei seeds,
the Li2S molecules diffuse in the electrolyte and precipitate on the
surface of the existing seeds, away from the surface of the elec-
trode. The growth of the solid Li2S is phenomenologically different
from the electrolytic nucleation in metals because Li2S is highly
insulating and does not contain free electrons to promote electro-
chemical deposition on its surface (like the metal-on-metal depo-
sition would do). To account for the rate of surface coverage the
authors use a two-dimensional version of the Kolmogorov phase
transformation
theory
[26],
which
results
into
an
integro-
differential system of equations. By solving these equations self-
consistently with the classical multicomponent transport equa-
tions, the authors show that the nucleation rate is faster at higher
discharge rates, which leads to a faster surface coverage and, hence,
a decreased capacity. The results provided by the model are also
supported by SEM images of the cathode coverage and the model
correctly explains the rate-dependent capacity in Li-S batteries.
In this article we develop a rate-dependent nucleation theory
appropriate for the solid reaction products in Li-S batteries and use
this theory to explain qualitatively the capacity loss at high
discharge rates. The theory is similar to the model developed by
Ren et al. [14] in the sense that it attributes that death of Li-S
batteries to surface passivation, however, in this work, the math-
ematical equations are framed entirely in the form of a system of
partial differential equations that can be implemented easily in
ordinary or partial differential equation solvers. The equation for
the nucleation rate is relatively similar to the one used in Ref. [14]
but, here, it is derived on a phenomenological basis, speciﬁcally for
the precipitation of reaction products in Li-S batteries. In this way,
the derivation for the nucleation rate can be viewed as proof for the
equation employed in Ref. [14].
Finally, one should note that ﬁnite-element models for the
simulation of Li-S batteries have also been developed by other
authors. In general, these models describe fairly well the voltage as
a function of capacity for low discharge or charge rates but do not
predict correctly or have not been tested yet for higher rates. For
instance, the model of Kumaresan et al. [11] predicts that the
discharge capacity is independent on the discharge rate for rates
below 1 C. Similarly, the models presented in Refs. [27,28] cannot
explain the decrease of the speciﬁc capacity when the discharge
rate is increasing to 1 C and beyond.
2. Experimental
The cells used in this study were made using Li foil anodes
(0.25 mm, MTI), polypropylene separators (25 mm, Celgard 2400),
and carbon nanotube (CNT) foam-based cathodes. The CNT foams
contained CNTs with an average diameter of 10 nm and a length of
2 mm, which were assembled in a freestanding three-dimensional
mesh using the method presented in Refs. [7,29]. The obtained CNT
foams had a thickness of 100 mm and a density of 125 mg/cm3. The

3. Theory
To develop a mathematical model for the discharge of Li-S
batteries we need to determine the fundamental transport and
electrochemical processes that limit the discharge of these batte-
ries. Identifying the main phenomena that take place during
discharge will enable us to build a realistic model that describes the
operation of the battery. The material parameters that enter into
the governing equations need to be determined using data taken as
much as possible from direct measurements of the material prop-
erties instead of ﬁtting the ﬁnal discharge curves with simulation
results. The model developed below is based on the classical
multicomponent transport equations in porous media, which can
accurately capture ohmic and diffusion losses in the electrolyte.
First, we focus on the role of precipitation and surface coverage in
the cathode and then we summarize the model equations. Phe-
nomena such as polysulﬁde shuttling and side reactions that take
place mostly during charging are neglected in this work.

3.1. Nucleation, precipitation, and surface coverage

reason for the active surface coverage and passivation. The model
for nucleation, precipitation, and surface coverage developed in
this section is based on the following observations:
1. Since the electric resistivity of Li2S is large and the electron
tunneling current is negligibly small for the thicknesses of Li2S
layers observed experimentally, electrochemical reactions can
take place only at the electrolyte/carbon interface and not at the
Li2S/electrolyte interface. The carbon surface underneath the

3.4. Solubility products
The solubility products of solid species in the electrolyte play a
major role on the speciﬁc capacity of Li-S batteries [7,29,44].
Therefore, we need to carefully determine the solubility products of
solid species in the cathode. In the previous reported simulations
the solubility products were not determined experimentally and
their values were simply assumed.
The solubility of elemental sulfur changes signiﬁcantly with the
concentration of the LiPS in the electrolyte. For instance, the solu-
bility of sulfur in DME is 9.96 mol/m3, while the solubility in an
electrolyte from discharged Li-S batteries is 16.6 mol/m3 [45]. In our
simulations, due to the lack of available data in the literature, we
use KS8 ¼ 10 mol/m3. This value is approximately half of the
currently assumed value of 19 mol/m3 used in most numerical
simulations [11,12,15].
To evaluate the solubility of LiPS we mix Li2S and S powder in
stoichiometric ratios of 1: n-1 to produce Li2Sn in a blank electrolyte
of DME:DOL (1:1 v:v). The solubility products of Li2Sn is measured
separately for n ¼ 4, 6, and 8 by slowly increasing the amount of
blank electrolyte until the powder is fully dissolved. For more
details on how the experiments were performed we recommend
[7]. The measured concentrations of saturation for Li2S4, Li2S6, Li2S8
are [S] ¼ 2 M, 6 M, and 7 M, respectively, which lead to the solubi-
lity products presented in Table 1. These values are in agreement
with data published by other authors for similar electrolytes. For
instance, Pan et al. [46] found that the maximum sulfur concen-
tration of the Li2S6 in DME:DOL is ~[S] ¼ 6 M.
The solubilities of Li2S and Li2S2 are negligible according to our
experiments and other published data [11,12,15,27,47e49].
3.5. Exchange current densities and reaction potentials
The voltage drop in Li-S batteries is due in part to diffusion and
conductivity losses and in part to kinetic losses. Since the diffusion
coefﬁcients and mobilities have already been determined in section
3.2 independently (i.e. not by ﬁtting the discharge curves), the
diffusion and conductivity losses are accurately accounted in our
simulations and the remaining voltage drop is due entirely to ki-
netic losses. Therefore, we can determine the exchange current
densities and reaction potentials that enter in the Butler-Volmer
equation by ﬁtting plateaus of the discharge voltage and the
slope between two plateaus with the numerical simulations. The
values of the exchange current densities and reaction potentials
determined in this way are presented in Table 2, while the reference
concentrations are presented in Table 3.

solved in the electrolyte. For this reason, if the deposition of Li2S
occurs on the surface of S8(s) it is possible that some sulfur might
remain isolated and not able to participate in the electrochemical
reactions. This fact can be another reason why the Li2S batteries
fabricated in the laboratory can hardly reach their maximum
theoretical discharge capacity. In order to avoid this phenomenon
and improve the speciﬁc capacity of Li-S batteries one should use
electrolytes with high solubility of S8(s).
Fig. 9 presents the average number of nuclei of Li2S per unit area
on the carbon, FLi2S, as a function of speciﬁc capacity. In agreement
with the discharge characteristics, we observe three main regions
on this ﬁgure. “Region 1” corresponds to the initial dissolution of
solid sulfur in the cathode. In this region, the number of nuclei per
unit area is increasing slowly but still remains relatively negligible.
In “region 2”, the concentration of Li2S molecules increases
abruptly, oversaturating the surface of the carbon. The nucleation
rate is very high in this region. When enough nuclei have been
formed and they provide enough growing seeds for the solid
product deposition, the concentration of Li2S starts decreasing and
the solid product starts growing on the surface of the cathode. This
growing process takes place in “region 3”. The oversaturation dip
labeled with letter “B” in Figs. 2 and 7 takes place at the end of
“region 2” and beginning of “region 3”.
To emphasize the importance of using the surface over-
saturation during the nucleation process, Fig. 10 presents the
average oversaturation in the transport channels (SLi2S;surf , see eq.
(16)) and on the surface of the carbon (SLi2S;surf , see eq. (15)) at the
same discharge rates as in the previous two ﬁgures. During the ﬁrst
part of the discharge (i.e. on the ﬁrst plateau) both SLi2S and SLi2S;surf
are equal to zero because the nucleation seeds have not been
formed yet. Starting at approximately 200 mAh/gS for the discharge
at 1 C and at around 300 mAh/gS for the discharge at the other rates,
both SLi2S and SLi2S;surf increase and the nucleation seeds start to
form on the surface of the carbon. Once the nuclei are formed, the
oversaturation SLi2S starts decreasing monotonically (see Fig. 10a),
however the surface oversaturation SLi2S;surf increases further (see
Fig. 10b). This behavior is due to the fact that, when the active area
of the cathode is decreasing, the reaction rate given by eq. (23)
needs to increase correspondingly to provide the same amount of
discharge current. Consequently, the nucleation rate, which is
proportional to the surface oversaturation, increases even more
during the second plateau of discharge.
It is apparent from Fig. 10 that the electrolyte needs to be
signiﬁcantly oversaturated in order for the nucleation process to
start and the battery to be discharged at a rate of 0.1 C or above.
From eq. (24) and the values of the solubility products and reaction
rates reported in Table 3 it follows that the dissolution of solid
species is a very slow process (at least a few orders of magnitude
slower than the precipitation rate under normal discharge condi-
tions). For this reason, in order to improve the speciﬁc capacity of
Li-S batteries under normal discharge conditions it is essential to
avoid the solid formation of intermediate LiPS and the only solid
material formed in the cathode to be the ﬁnal Li2S product. This
effect could be achieved by increasing the solubility of the inter-
mediate LiPS as much as possible and protecting the anode elec-
trode. A similar result was obtained experimentally by Shen et al.
[7] in Li-S batteries.

rate. By following the same procedure as in the experimental sec-

First, in qualitative agreement with the experimental data
shown in Fig. 3, the voltage of the battery during the discharge from
point “F” to point “G” is smaller than the voltage between the same
speciﬁc capacities on the 0.2 C curve (see the red dash line in

the speciﬁc capacity obtained by discharging the battery at a con-

which the nuclei appear during the 1 C region of the discharge are
different from the rate at which they are would appear if the rate
was 0.2 C; for this reason, the rate at which surface coverage
changes is different during the 1 C discharge (i.e. from point “E” to
“F”) than during discharge at 0.2 C, and the cathode becomes
passivated faster at 1 C than at 0.2 C.
Second, also in agreement with the experimental data, the ca-
pacity from point “B” to “C” in Fig. 11 (SC1) is smaller than the ca-
pacity from point “E” to “F” (SC2). This result is somewhat
unexpected because the cathode is partly covered with solid Li2S at
point “E” and we expect that the remaining capacity be smaller
than the one obtained by starting from point “B”, where the cath-
ode is completely uncovered. The explanation of this phenomenon
is due to the fact that the surface coverage increases much faster
from point “B” to “C” than from “E” to “F”. Indeed, in the case of
constant discharge at 1 C, the initial number of nuclei at point “B” is
0, which results in a large surface oversaturation and a formation of
a large number of nucleation seeds at point “B”. These seeds will
grow in size uniformly from “B” to “C” until completely covering
and passivating the surface of the cathode. If the battery is dis-
charged at 0.2 C a smaller number of nucleation seeds are formed at
point “D” because the surface oversaturation is much smaller than
at point “B”. The relatively small number of nuclei will grow slowly
from “D” to “E”. When the discharge rate is changed to 1 C at point
“E”, the cathode surface already contains a small number of
nucleation seeds that provide growing centers for further discharge
(from “E” to “F”). This phenomenon is consistent with the experi-
mental results published in Fig. 3.
Finally, the above result prompts up to the conclusion that in
order to increase the capacity of Li-S batteries it is better to artiﬁ-
cially introduce a small number of nucleation seeds in the cathode,
which will avoid reaching large surface oversaturations during the
discharge of the battery. The number of nucleation seeds should not
be too large so the nuclei will touch each other and passivate the
surface of the carbon too soon during the growth process, however
it should be large enough to allow the molecules of the solid
product to deposit easily and avoid excessive oversaturation.
Practically, this can be achieved by adding catalyst particles that
would promote the nucleation of the solid product in speciﬁc lo-
cations of the cathode. The catalyst particles should work as
nucleation seeds during the precipitation process. In addition to
increasing the speciﬁc capacity, the catalyst particles will also
eliminate the voltage dip at the beginning of the last plateau in
Figs. 3 and 7.

5. Conclusions
After reviewing the various phenomena that result in rate-
dependent speciﬁc capacities in Li-S batteries, we conclude that
the sudden death of the batteries fabricated in our study is due to
surface passivation of the active area of the cathode with solid re-
action products. To explain the discharge characteristics of the Li-S
batteries we developed a mathematical model based on the classical
Kolmogorov-Avrami
nucleation
theory.
However,
instead
of
expressing the model in integral form and keeping track of the
numberand size distribution of the nucleation seeds ateach location
inside the battery as it is usually done in the literature, we write the
surface coverage in the form of a system of differential equations
(see eqs. (17)-(19)). This system can be coupled with the classical
transport equations in multicomponent electrolytes (see eqs. (20)-
(24)) and solved efﬁciently using the ﬁnite element technique.
An important feature of our model is that it uses surface over-
saturation as the driving force for nucleation. Since the surface
oversaturation is rate-dependent, the nucleation rate depends on
the current rate of discharge. We have shown that, the nucleation
rate is higher at high discharge rates, which results in a larger
number of nuclei of relatively small size on the surface of the
cathode. At low discharge rates the nucleation rate is lower, the
number of nuclei is smaller, however, the nuclei grow in larger
sizes. Based on this model we are able to describe qualitatively
correctly the decrease of the capacity of Li-S batteries with
increasing discharge rate and explain the experimental values of
the speciﬁc capacity obtained under variable discharge currents.
The model can also be applied to the study of electrochemical
impedance spectra (EIS) of Li-S batteries by linearizing the trans-
port equation and applying the small-signal perturbation technique
to evaluate the complex impedance. EIS can provide important
information about the kinetic dynamics and relaxation times in the
battery [51e53].
