Details of neutron imaging experiments:
When imaging, the high neutron flux of 3 × 108 n cm-2 s-1 allowed for exposure time of 0.6 second per image which helped to perform tomography runs of 1000 projections per 360° in 10 minutes. This time resolution was used for the visualization of the electrolyte infiltration and redistribution at the different state of rest and charge/discharge. 209 recorded tomograms were reconstructed based on filtered back projection (FBP) algorithm. The reconstructed images and videos provide sufficient information on the dynamic distribution of the electrolyte in the different layers in the Li-S pouch cell during rest and cycling, which is beneficial to reveal the effect of electrolyte distribution on cell performances and degradation mechanisms of Li-S pouch cells under the lean electrolyte.

Figure S5. (a) Photo of the EL-Cell ECC Opto-10 cell holder of operando Raman setups after discharge/charge measurements; (b) Operando Raman spectra of Li-S batteries for the discharge and charge at 0.06 C rate.
Operando Raman spectroscopy was performed to study the electrochemical reactions of sulfur using the confocal Raman microscope and an EL-Cell ECC Opto-10 cell holder (Figure S5a).  As shown in Figure S5b, Raman analysis reveals that as the battery discharges from its initial voltage to around 2.0 V, solid sulfur is converted into soluble polysulfides (Li2Sx, x = 4–8), corresponding to the dissolution phase in Li-S batteries. During this phase, the electrolyte exhibits a 'breath-in' wetting behavior. Subsequently, between 2.0 V and 1.7 V, soluble polysulfides disappear due to precipitation, which corresponds to a 'breath-out' of electrolyte wetting. During charging, soluble polysulfides are re-formed in the range of 1.7 to 2.4 V, again indicating a breath-in process. It should be noted that the weak or absent Raman peaks for sulfur and polysulfides between 2.4 V and 2.8 V are likely due to defocusing effects caused by volume changes. Overall, these electrochemical observations strongly support the dynamic wetting evolution mechanism.


Figure S6. (a) Operando analysis of distribution of relaxation times (DRT) from EIS fitting during two cycles discharge and charge at 0.06 C and (b) followed one cycle at 0.3 C.

In addition, we performed operando electrochemical impedance measurements and conducted distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis on the Li-S pouch cells, as shown in Figure S6. 
The intermediate time constant range (0.01–0.1 s) is associated with charge transfer on electrode interfaces. As shown in Figure S6a, during discharge at 0.06 C, notably, no peaks are observed at the beginning of discharge, while prominent peaks appear during the later stages. This is accompanied by an increase in the value of γ(τ), indicating a shift from rapid charge transfer to slower interfacial mechanisms. This transition corresponds to the shift from the dissolution process of active materials to the precipitation process of lithium sulfides. In the second discharge, the amplitude of the peak becomes smaller which is assumed to the improved wetting. 
At longer timescales (1–10 s), corresponding to bulk diffusion and Warburg-type impedance, γ(τ) gradually increases with the depth of 1st discharge, which is caused by the polysulfides dissolution that results in increased polysulfides concentration, increased viscosity and reduced diffusion rate. The dramatic increase of γ(τ) approaching the end of discharge can be due to the precipitation process and breath-out behavior of electrolyte. During charging, γ(τ) decreases because of the polysulfide dissolution and breath-in behavior of electrolyte.
As shown in Figure S6b, at 0.3 C, the evolution of impedance from the different timescales is similar to those observed at 0.06 C, indicating very similar electrochemical processes. The above dynamic evolution from different time scales points to reversible structural wetting and dewetting during operation, indicative of the dissolution-precipitation electrochemical processes and electrolyte breath processes.
