ABSTRACT: In recent years, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have
become attractive owing to their high energy density and higher charge-
storage capacity than Li-ion batteries. Despite this, practical applications of
Li−S batteries remain limited by several challenges that still need to be
resolved, including Li-dendrite formation, which leads to short-circuiting.
In the present study, divide-and-conquer density-functional tight-binding
molecular dynamics (DC-DFTB-MD) simulations examine neutral cyclo-
S8, while lithium polysulfides and polysulfide anions are not included.
Increasing LiOTf concentration generally reduces the self-diffusion
coefficients because denser, more highly coordinated ionic environments
restrict translational motion. The Li+ ion preferentially coordinates with
electronegative oxygen atoms of the solvent and triflate anion, whereas
nonpolar, neutral S8 interacts mainly through induced-polarization and
dispersion forces. These competing coordination environments explain the weak direct Li+−S8 association and the coupled decline of
ionic and molecular mobility with salt loading.
■INTRODUCTION
Ion battery technology has received considerable attention
owing to its widespread use in daily-life applications.1−3
Among these technologies, the Li-ion battery is the most well-
known, and its applications range from portable electronic
devices, such as cellular phones, digital cameras, global
positioning system devices, tablets, and laptop computers.4
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are one of the most
promising batteries due to their high theoretical specific
capacity of 1675 mA h g−1 and energy density of 2600 W
kg−1.5 However, the polysulfide shuttle effect and dendrite
growth, caused by irregular Li deposition, hinder the
commercial viability of Li−S batteries.6 Mitigation of
polysulfide shuttling and lithium dendrite growth has been
tackled via various methods, most notably through separator
and cathode design. In addition to these approaches, the
optimization of the electrolyte system is an effective alternative
to simultaneously suppress polysulfide shuttling and dendrite
growth.7
Since the shuttling effect is directly tied to the diffusion of
polysulfides between the electrodes and their dissolution into
the electrolyte, suppressing polysulfide diffusion is crucial in
suppressing the shuttle effect.8 Recent work suggests electro-
lytes with high-donor-number salt anions with labile fluorine
groups are effective in suppressing lithium dendrite growth in
addition to polysulfide shuttling.9,10 One such salt anion is
trifluoromethanesulfonate ([OTf]−), which has been observed
to have greater coordination strength with Li+ in comparison
to the traditional bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide anions
([TFSI]−), leading to better electrochemical properties.11
However, the use of such electrolytes may come at the
expense of the reduced redox kinetics.12 The present model
addresses neutral elemental sulfur (cyclo-S8) and Li+-ion
transport. It does not directly simulate lithium polysulfide
transport or the shuttle mechanism. Thus, balancing the salt
concentration and different solvent systems with respect to
elemental sulfur and lithium-ion diffusion is of great
importance. Obtaining optimal conditions across different
salt concentrations and solvent types necessitates extensive
experimentation in the laboratory. Because such experiments
require considerable time and effort and may be affected by
human error, computational studies are essential for examining
the phenomena that arise when the salt concentrations and
solvent types are altered. Electrolyte phenomena as a

DFTB is a DFT-derived quantum-mechanical technique that
is frequently employed in molecular simulations because it
allows for large system sizes and extended simulation
timeframes while explicitly describing the electronic struc-
ture.13,14 When properly parameterized, DFTB can produce
DFT-comparable trends in energetics and structures at
significantly lower computing costs.15 However, its accuracy
remains dependent on the system and parameters. The second-
order self-consistent charge model (DFTB2) provides an
attractive balance between the accuracy of first-principles
electronic structure approaches and the efficiency needed for
large-scale atomistic simulations. This approach is frequently
employed as a low-cost approximation to DFT and is based on
a second-order expansion of the total DFT energy. The
repulsive potential and Slater−Koster parameters have a
significant impact on DFTB accuracy.16−18 The periodic
table basic parameters (PTBP) are one type of Slater−Koster
parameter and are presented as a comprehensive set of
fundamental parameters for all solids in the periodic table.19
Implementing PTBP for molecular systems requires improve-
ment to obtain parameters that are adequate and suitable for
electrolyte systems, as PTBP is based solely on solid systems.
This study addresses the diffusion processes of sulfur and
lithium ions in an electrolyte system and analyzes molecular
stability throughout the simulation using density-functional
theory-based tight-binding molecular dynamics simulations
with the divide-and-conquer (DC-DFTB-MD) method. This
molecular simulation process was conducted by using PTBP
parameters. We selected three types of electrolyte solvent
systems, including propylene carbonate (PC), dioxolane
(DIOX), and a mixture of ethylene carbonate and dimethyl
carbonate (EC/DMC) with varying concentrations of LiOTf.
The DC-DFTB method is computationally inexpensive as a
result of several approximations introduced in the DFTB
model and scales linearly with the system size through
fragmentation of the system, allowing for a considerable half-
cell simulation environment.20,21
Herein, the dynamic properties of Li+ ions and S8 are
analyzed to better understand the effects of high-donor-
number salt anions in Li−S batteries. Ion-pair interactions and
aggregation dynamics for the CF3 groups of the salt anion are
observed to explore the mechanisms and transport behaviors
that occur in the electrolyte. Additionally, we expect to observe
the stability and behavior of the electrolyte system within
lithium batteries at varied salt concentrations in various types
of solvents through molecular simulations utilizing the PTBP
parameters.
We intentionally exclude Li2Sx and Sx
2−because their
changing chain length, charge state, counterion stoichiometry,
and redox chemistry introduce a distinct reactive-speciation
problem. Mixing those species with neutral cyclo-S8 in the
present concentration series would prevent a controlled
attribution of transport changes to solvent and LiOTf loading.
The present results therefore provide a neutral elemental sulfur
baseline and must not be interpreted as polysulfide solubility,
diffusion, or shuttle kinetics.
■METHODOLOGY
Preparing Electrolyte Solutions
PC, DIOX, and EC/DMC were selected to contrast carbonate
and ether coordination environments used in battery-electro-
lyte research, not because all are asserted to be intrinsically
good solvents for elemental sulfur. The simulations begin from
a prescribed, molecularly dispersed cyclo-S8 composition and
do not calculate dissolution free energies, saturation concen-
trations, or phase separation. They therefore compare
transport conditional on the modeled compositions; they do
not establish macroscopic S8 solubility. All electrolyte systems
were built in the computational molecular and material design
environment (CMMDE) using the GENCAN algorithm.22
Three types of electrolyte solvents were used, namely,
propylene carbonate (PC), a mixture of ethylene carbonate
and dimethyl carbonate (EC/DMC), and dioxolane (DIOX).
These three solvent variations are representative of carbonate
and ether solvents commonly used in Li−S batteries. The
LiOTf concentrations varied for each solution, with wt % OTf−
values of 5, 20, 30, and 40%. The chemical structures of all
adopted solvents are shown in Figure 1. The solution systems
were prepared through a series of classical MD simulations that
began with energy minimization and were followed by
equilibration under microcanonical (NVE) and canonical
(NVT) ensembles to initialize the velocities of the solutions
at the target temperature of 300 K for 500 ps and 1 ns,
respectively. Finally, the mass densities of the adopted systems
were obtained from 1 ns of MD simulations under the
isothermal−isobaric ensemble (NPT) at 300 K by employing
the Berendsen barostat. Table 1 shows the system details and

Simulation Details and Analyses
The simulations used the published PTBP Slater−Koster set of
Cui et al.19 PTBP supplies the electronic and repulsive terms
for the H/C/O/F/S/Li element pairs needed here. Its
molecular-geometry transferability was checked against
ωB97X-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP structures (Supporting Informa-
tion). For PC, DMC, EC, and DIOX, the bond-length mean
absolute errors are 0.014−0.021 Å, the bond-angle mean
absolute errors are 0.86−1.07°, and the aligned Cartesian
RMSDs are 0.028−0.150 Å. Additional optimizations of one-
solvent Li+ complexes retain the DFT Li−O-bound motifs,
with heavy-atom RMSDs of 0.043−0.141 Å. The PTBP Li−O
distances are systematically shorter than the DFT values by
0.060−0.231 Å, revealing a modest overcontraction of this
contact. For a Li+−S8 cluster, the nearest Li−S distance is
2.363 Å at the DFT level and 2.411 Å with DFTB2/PTBP, a
deviation of +0.048 Å. Reproducing the solvent frameworks
and the local Li−O and Li−S contact distances provides a fit-
for-purpose validation of the geometries sampled during the
dynamics and supports qualitative and semiquantitative
comparisons across the consistently treated electrolyte series.
Molecular dynamics simulations were performed by employing
the second-order density-functional tight-binding (DFTB2)
method.23 The DFTB2 method is an approximation based on a
second-order expansion of the total density-functional theory
(DFT) energy. The published PTBP parameter set reported by
Cui et al.19 was used for every simulation. In general, the
DFTB method has been widely used for liquid-phase
simulations in many systems, including proton transfer,24−28
carbon capture and storage,29,30 and catalytic reactions.31,32
Our previous work also highlighted the effects of salt
concentration on the diffusion coefficient in a liquid-phase
electrolyte33 using the DFTB3 method. More recently, DC-
DFTB-MD was applied to characterize the graphene oxide−
ionic liquid electrolyte interface in a sodium-ion battery
system.34 All molecular dynamics simulations were performed
using the Dcdftbmd code.21
After a long preliminary equilibration at the classical
mechanics level as explained in the previous section, 10 ps
equilibrations were performed under the NVT ensemble with a
time step of 1 fs. The equilibrated atomic coordinates and
velocities were taken for 50 ps production runs under the NVE
ensemble. The simulations were repeated three times to
estimate structural and dynamical properties better. The
trajectories obtained under the NVE ensemble were subjected
to all analyses performed in this work. The mass densities
obtained from the preceding 1 ns NPT simulations are
reported for every system in Table 1. Complete mean-square
displacement (MSD) curves for Li+, S8, the solvent species, and
OTf−in all solvent and salt-concentration systems are provided
in the Supporting Information, enabling inspection of the
linear diffusion regime used to estimate the transport
coefficients. The diffusion coefficients were estimated from
the mean-square displacement (MSD) using the Einstein
equation (eq 1).
An MSD curve and its long-time slope were obtained
separately for each independent production trajectory; the
three trajectory-level diffusion coefficients were then averaged,
and their sample standard deviation is reported in Table 2.
Thus, the MSD curves were not first combined into a single
ensemble-averaged curve before fitting. This preserves
between-trajectory variability as the uncertainty estimate

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Sulfur, Solvent, and Li-Ion Diffusivities
Estimated diffusion coefficients, D, at the DFTB level are
shown in Table 2. The estimated diffusion coefficients range
from 1.61 × 10−10 to 4.04 × 10−9 m2/s. In general, Li+ ion
exhibits the slowest mobility because its high charge density
produces a persistent solvation environment containing solvent
and triflate oxygen atoms. Motion therefore requires rearrange-
ment of neighboring molecules and ion pairs. Increasing LiOTf
concentration also raises the mass density (Table 1), reduces
free volume, and increases competition for oxygen-coordina-
tion sites. These effects slow structural relaxation and generally
decrease the self-diffusion coefficients. Direct Li+−S8 inter-
action is comparatively weak because neutral cyclo-S8 lacks a
localized negative site, whereas oxygen atoms are strong Lewis
bases. S8 consequently interacts with oxygen-containing
environments mainly through dispersion and induced-polar-
ization contributions rather than stable Li−S coordination.
In dilute PC, DS and Dsolvent have comparable values, 13.01 ×
10−10 and 14.2 × 10−10 m2/s, respectively. Comparable self-
diffusion coefficients describe similar average migration rates
but do not by themselves demonstrate correlated displace-
ments or persistent pairing. Accordingly, no sulfur−solvent
complex is inferred from these values alone. Their decline
through 30% LiOTf is consistent with increasing density and
crowding. The rise in DS at 40% is nonmonotonic and is not
assigned to increased free space without supporting structural
and displacement-correlation evidence. The RDF, connection
matrix, NCI, and direct correlated-motion results below are
therefore considered together when interpreting local associ-
ation.
In DIOX, the estimated sulfur diffusion coefficient decreases
monotonically with LiOTf concentration. At 40% LiOTf, DS
and Dsolvent are numerically similar. This similarity does not
establish collective diffusion. It is consistent with both species
responding to the same increasingly dense and slowly relaxing
environment, while the structural analyses independently
identify local DIOX−S8 contacts.
In EC/DMC, sulfur diffusivity decreases from 5 to 30%
LiOTf and then increases at 40%. The self-diffusion values are
not used as evidence for correlated sulfur−solvent motion.
Although increasing salt concentration generally suppresses
sulfur mobility, it also suppresses Li+ ion mobility, revealing a
transport trade-off relevant to electrolyte design.
The complete trajectory-resolved MSD curves and quanti-
tative regime diagnostics are shown in the Supporting
Information. Over 20−50 ps, all 52 species−composition
curves have logarithmic diffusion exponents close to unity (α =
0.936−1.062), linear-fit R2 = 0.9974−0.9999, and no more
than 5.8% variation in the fitted slope when the fitting interval
is changed among 10−50, 15−50, 20−50, and 25−50 ps. The
slow ionic components satisfy the criteria independently: α =
0.981−1.062 for Li+ and 0.981−1.042 for OTf−, with
maximum fit window sensitivities of 4.0 and 2.6%, respectively.
These results demonstrate that the reported slopes sample a
stable finite-time diffusive regime, including for the slowest
species.

The contact-survival probability, S(τ), complements the
displacement correlation by quantifying the persistence of the
local environment. For example, S = 0.60 at 2.0 ps means that
60% of the pairs classified as contacts at the time origin are
again within the 5.0 Å cutoff after 2.0 ps. Because the definition
correlates the contact state only at the two end points, it is an
intermittent survival probability and can include a pair that
briefly separates and reforms. It should therefore not be
interpreted as a continuous residence probability, a binding
equilibrium constant, or direct evidence for a permanent
complex.
At 2.0 ps, S is 0.60−0.70 for PC, 0.50−0.58 for DIOX,
0.54−0.64 for EC, and 0.57−0.75 for DMC. Thus,
approximately one-half to three-quarters of the initially
neighboring S8−solvent pairs retain or recover their local
contact over this interval. PC maintains consistently high
survival across concentration, whereas the lower DIOX values
indicate more rapid exchange of its S8 contact environment. In
EC/DMC, DMC reaches the largest survival probability (S =
0.748) at 30 wt % LiOTf, while EC varies over a narrower
range. The concentration dependence is nonmonotonic for
every solvent, indicating that contact persistence is controlled
by a balance among molecular packing, salt-induced crowding,
ion coordination, and dispersion-mediated S8−solvent associ-
ation rather than salt concentration alone.
The S and ΔC values describe different aspects of the
dynamics and need not vary in parallel. For example, DMC at
30 wt % LiOTf has the largest survival probability in the table
(S = 0.748), but its excess displacement correlation (ΔC =
0.278) is smaller than the DMC values at 5 and 40 wt %. Thus,
a pair can remain within the contact shell without exhibiting
the strongest same-direction displacement. Conversely, a
positive ΔC describes directional coherence but does not by
itself establish a long-lived neighborhood. Considered together,
positive ΔC establishes preferential same-direction motion,
while finite S establishes persistence of the relevant local
neighborhood over the correlation interval. Because S remains
well below unity, the results support transient local cooperative
motion rather than rigid or permanent S8−solvent complexes.

The ensemble-averaged Mulliken-derived ESP maps in
Figures 2−4 visualize the charge distribution around Li+
solvation environments. In all systems, negative-potential
regions are concentrated around solvent and triflate oxygen
atoms and face the embedded positive Li+ center. This spatial
complementarity explains the preferential Li−O coordination
found in the RDF and connection-matrix analyses. The maps
do not support preferential Li−S8 coordination, namely,
neutral cyclo-S8 lacks the localized negative-potential oxygen
sites that stabilize Li+.
The shell composition changes with the salt concentration.
For PC, the representative shell changes from three PC plus
two triflate molecules at 5 wt % to one PC plus three triflates at
30 and 40 wt %. EC/DMC similarly changes from four
carbonate molecules plus one triflate at 5 and 20 wt % to one
carbonate plus two triflates at 40 wt %, while the shell size
decreases from five to three molecules. These changes indicate
increasing anion participation and contact-ion-pair character at
high salt loading, which increases the collective rearrangement
required for Li+ translation and is consistent with a decrease in
Li+ ion diffusivity. DIOX retains three to four solvent
molecules and one to two triflates across the concentrations,
consistent with substantial ether oxygen participation. Because
each panel represents one longest-lived contact environment,
the shell compositions are representative local motifs rather
than ensemble populations, and the color ranges should not be
compared as absolute ESP magnitudes between the panels.

for translation. Analysis of the radial distribution function

interactions between Li+ ions and sulfur atoms (OTf−), Li+

The role of weak noncovalent interactions in the system was
investigated using noncovalent interaction (NCI) analysis
based on the reduced density gradient (RDG) combined with
the sign (λ2)ρ descriptor. This approach allows the
identification and visualization of weak interactions arising
from electron-density distributions between interacting
components in the fragment system. The NCI isosurface and
RDG plot for all adopted solvent systems are illustrated in
Figures 11−13. The NCI isosurface and RDG plot for the PC
solution at various salt concentrations are shown in Figure 11.
The green surface extends across the interacting components
depicted in Figure 11. At the lowest concentration of LiOTf
(5%), as illustrated in Figure 11a, the fragments comprise one
S8 molecule and four PC molecules. The green surface exists
between the S8 molecule and PC molecules in the NCI
isosurface. This green color is confirmed in the RDG plot,
where a dominant green band indicates that van der Waals
dispersion interactions dominate the interactions between S8
and PC. Attractive interactions are present, but they are
secondary and moderate, as seen from the blue color that
appears after the green color gradation. The attractive
interaction occurs in the S8 molecule. The slight red color
that appears in the RDG plot indicates delocalization of the PC
ring, which is confirmed by the red surface in the center of the
PC ring. The NCI isosurface and RDG plot of 20% LiOTf are
represented in Figure 11b. The fragments of the system consist
of one S8 molecule, two PC molecules, and one Li+ ion. The
surface between molecules appears green, with red appearing
only in the middle of the PC ring structure. In addition, there
is a blue-green color between the oxygen atom in PC and the
Li+ ion. The RDG plot confirms the dominance of nonbonding
interactions between molecules. It shows that van der Waals
dispersion still dominates, followed by attractive interactions.
The attractive interaction is obtained between the oxygen atom
in PC and the Li+ ion, as well as from the sulfur atom in the S8
molecule. There is a slight repulsive effect, but it is minimal. As
the concentration increased to 30% LiOTf, the analyzed
system fragment consisted of one S8 molecule and three PC
molecules. The NCI isosurface showed a green surface
distribution. When confirmed by the RDG plot, it can be
seen that there is no dispersion in the attractive region (blue)
or in the repulsive region (red). Conversely, the opposite
occurs for the fragment system at the highest concentration of
40% (Figure 11d), where the RDG plot shows that the main
dominant interaction is van der Waals dispersion, and there are
indications of repulsive forces from the red peak in the positive
sign (λ2)ρ region. This may be due to the presence of two Li+
ions in the fragment system. For the 40% LiOTf concentration
fragment system, there is one S8 molecule, six PC molecules,
and two Li+ ions. Overall, the molecules surrounding S8
primarily contribute to van der Waals dispersion effects.
Repulsive and attractive interactions occur only intramolecu-
larly. For example, repulsive interactions occur within the PC
ring, while attractive interactions occur between the sulfur
atoms within the S8 molecular ring.

consists of four components, namely, one S8 and three DIOX
molecules, giving a significantly different RDG plot. The
dominant interaction is van der Waals dispersion. The second
dominant interaction is attraction (Figure 12b). For a
concentration of 30% LiOTf, the fragment system consists of
four components, namely, one S8 molecule, one OTf−ion, and
two DIOX molecules. The RDG plot in Figure 12c provides
information about van der Waals dispersion dominating the
interactions in this fragment, but not very strongly. In addition,
there is no contribution from attractive or repulsive
interactions. In contrast, a distinct pattern is shown at a
concentration of 40% LiOTf, where the RDG plot in Figure
12d shows that van der Waals dispersion contributes mainly to
the interactions in this fragment system. There is also an

The NCI isosurface and RDG plot for the EC/DMC solvent

result is shown at a concentration of 40% LiOTf, where the
RDG plot in Figure 13d shows a lower intensity of van der

fragment system consists of one S8, one EC, and three DMC.
■CONCLUSIONS
The coordination statistics provide a quantitative microscopic
link to the transport trend. Across all three solvents, raising
LiOTf from 5 to 40 wt % increases the mean triflate-O
coordination of Li by approximately 1.0−1.6, while reducing
solvent-O coordination by approximately 1.6−1.8. This ligand
substitution indicates increasing contact-ion-pair participation
and a more anion-rich first shell. Its common direction
matches the general decrease in Li, triflate, and solvent
mobility because ion translation then requires collective
rearrangement of a denser, more strongly coupled ionic
environment. The relationship is mechanistic rather than a
one-variable scaling law: nonmonotonic S8 diffusion and

structure within the PC solvent by showing that an increase in
LiOTf concentration leads to a decrease in the diffusivity of Li+
ions. The electrolyte system maintains structural stability at all
concentrations tested despite this reduction in interaction
strength. Li+ ions mainly interact with triflate (OTf−) anions
and solvents, while there is an absence of interactions with S8
molecules according to the connectivity matrix analysis.
Furthermore, van der Waals dispersion is dominant for the
interaction between the S8 molecule and other components in
fragments of the electrolyte system. The repulsion and
attraction can vary depending on the quantity of solvent and
the surrounding salt. The LiOTf, organic solvent, and S8
electrolyte system exhibit notable chemical stability with salt
concentration. Significant chemical stability is demonstrated by
the LiOTf, organic solvent, and S8 electrolyte system, and the
salt concentration plays a critical role in determining ionic
mobility, local coordination, and the overall structure of the
system.
The Mulliken-derived electrostatic-potential maps further
show that negative-potential oxygen regions orient toward Li+,
and that triflate participation in representative first solvation
shells generally increases at high salt loading. Direct displace-
ment cross-correlations demonstrate that contacting S8−
solvent pairs move more coherently than noncontact pairs.
Their finite but incomplete 2 ps contact survival identifies this
behavior as transient local cooperative motion rather than
permanent complex formation. Together, the electronic and
dynamical analyses connect preferential Li−O coordination,
salt-dependent shell composition, and dispersion-mediated
S8−solvent association to the observed transport trends.
