An Electrolyte Engineered Homonuclear Copper Complex as
Homogeneous Catalyst for Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Qin Yang, Shiying Shen, Zhiyuan Han, Guanwu Li, Dong Liu, Qingchun Zhang,*
Lixian Song, Dong Wang, Guangmin Zhou,* and Yingze Song*
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries suﬀer from severe polysulﬁde shuttle, retarded
sulfur conversion kinetics and notorious lithium dendrites, which has
curtailed the discharge capacity, cycling lifespan and safety. Engineered
catalysts act as a feasible strategy to synchronously manipulate the evolution
behaviors of sulfur and lithium species. Herein, a chlorine bridge-enabled
binuclear copper complex (Cu-2-T) is in situ synthesized in electrolyte as
homogeneous catalyst for rationalizing the Li–S redox reactions. The
well-designed Cu-2-T provides completely active sites and suﬃcient contact
for homogeneously guiding the Li2S nucleation/decomposition reactions, and
stabilizing the lithium working interface according to the synchrotron
radiation X-ray 3D nano-computed tomography, small angle neutron
scattering and COMSOL results. Moreover, Cu-2-T with the content of
0.25 wt% approaching saturated concentration in electrolyte further boosts
the homogeneous optimization function in really operated Li–S batteries.
Accordingly, the capacity retention of the Li–S battery is elevated from 51.4%
to 86.3% at 0.2 C, and reaches 77.0% at 1.0 C over 400 cycles. Furthermore,
the sulfur cathode with the assistance of Cu-2-T realizes the stable cycling
under the practical scenarios of soft-packaged pouch cell and high sulfur
loading (6.5 mg cm−2 with the electrolyte usage of 4.5 μL mgS
−1).

1. Introduction
On account of the superior theoretical ca-
pacity (1672 mA h g−1) and energy den-
sity (2600 Wh kg−1), rechargeable lithium–
sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been widely con-
sidered as one of the most promising op-
tions for future renewable energy storage
technologies.[1–3] However, several critical
issues involving low sulfur utilization eﬃ-
ciency, fast capacity degradation and safety
threat, have signiﬁcantly hindered their
commercialization.[4] These fatal problems
result from the inherent properties of sul-
fur as well as lithium, and their complex
evolutions.[5] For instance, the shuttle of
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) between the
cathode and anode gives rise to the irre-
versible sulfur loss and the ultimate infe-
rior cycling stability of Li–S batteries.[6–8]
Additionally, the nonconductive nature of
sulfur and the slow solid-solid diﬀusion
during the conversion from Li2S2 to Li2S
lead to the retarded sulfur reaction kinetics,

which restricts discharge depth and overall battery capacity.[9,10]
Moreover, unstable lithium plating/stripping processes con-
tribute to the formation of dendrites, which adversely aﬀects the
electrochemical performance and safety of Li–S batteries.[11,12]
Thus far, considerable attempts have been devoted to the de-
velopment of catalysts for comprehensively addressing the afore-
mentioned issues existing in Li–S batteries.[13–15] Linda F. Nazar
applied Ti4O7 to optimize sulfur evolution behaviors in Li–S
batteries.[16] Thereafter, catalysts have evolved from metals to
metal compounds[17–19] and downsized from particles to single
atoms[20–23] in order to facilitate eﬃcient Li–S redox reactions.
Considering the nature of catalysts, manipulating their activity
through elaborate procedures has become a research focus in the
realm of Li–S batteries. Recent investigations have reported on
conﬁnement,[24] size,[25] defect[26] and interface[27] engineering-
enabled catalysts that can tune their activity for Li–S batteries.
These advancements not only improve battery performance but
also provide valuable insights into underlying mechanisms. For
instance, cooperative heterostructure interfaces bridging two dis-
tinct components propel the “adsorption-diﬀusion-conversion”
procedure of sulfur species.[28] In addition, reducing the size of
V2C MXene spheres enhances their catalytic ability for sulfur re-
dox reactions and enables a sieving eﬀect for Li-ions.[29]
Despite the fruitful advancements, the heterogeneous nature
of these catalysts imposes limitations on their activity. The con-
cept of homogeneous catalysis is capable of further elevating
the activity and the device performance beyond the heteroge-
neous catalysis, which is crucial for large-scale Li–S battery
manufacturing.[30,31] Existing routine heterogeneous catalysts in
cathode or interlayer insuﬃciently interact with long-chain sol-
uble LiPSs in electrolyte because of inadequate interface con-
tact. In contrast, homogeneous catalysts exhibit superior cat-
alytic function on account of its ultrahigh activity, have been also
exploited in other electrochemical systems.[32] Whilst a combi-
nation of advanced characterization toolsets with advantageous
resolution, sensitivity and detection depth is desired to paint
the landscape of the homogeneous catalysts-enabled Li–S chem-
istry by identifying electrochemical species and disclosing related
mechanism.
In this contribution, we design a homonuclear copper com-
plex (Cu-2-T) in the electrolyte as a homogeneous catalyst for
Li–S batteries. Such a Cu-2-T catalyst consists of chlorine (Cl)-
bridge bonded binuclear Cu atoms with a proximal distance of
4.7 Å and possesses a unique coordination environment. Notably,
Cu-2-T with the content of 0.25 wt% approaching its saturated
concentration presents completely active sites and aﬀords the re-
markably homogeneous optimization eﬀect on the sulfur nucle-
ation/decomposition and lithium plating/stripping behaviors by
combining the characterization facilities with favorable resolu-
tion, high sensitivity, and superior examination depth. As a re-
sult, Li–S batteries incorporating Cu-2-T as a homogeneous cat-
alyst demonstrate an outstanding capacity retention of 86.3% at
0.2 C after 100 cycles and a stable cycling at 1.0 C under the sce-
nario of soft-packaged pouch cells.
2. Results and Discussion
The design and working mechanism of Cu-2-T catalyst is
schematically illustrated in Figure S1, Supporting Information.
The Cu-2 catalyst is synthesized by controlling the mole ratio of
copper (II) chloride dihydrate and 1,10-phenanthroline (phen)
ligand as 1:1. Due to the insolubility of the as-attained Cu-2
in the commercial electrolyte containing 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) solution and 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI
(Figure S2, Supporting Information), the chemical modiﬁcation
pertaining to anion displacement for realizing better solubil-
ity is conducted. Accordingly, Cu-2 powder and potassium hex-
aﬂuorophosphate (KPF6) react in deionized water and the at-
tained product is subsequently dissolved in the commercial elec-
trolyte for obtaining the ﬁnal Cu-2-T. To achieve the saturated
concentration and thus completely realize the homogeneous op-
timization function, the content of Cu-2-T in electrolyte is al-
tered from 0.1 to 0.4 wt%. It’s noteworthy that Cu-2-T is only
added to the cathode side and hardly migrate to anode side due to
the inserted one-side carbon-coated PP separator. The catalyst of
Cu-2-T is in situ generated and uniformly dissolved in the elec-
trolyte, implying its homogeneous optimization function for the
evolution behaviors of sulfur and lithium species. Aside from
the homonuclear Cu atoms and unique coordination, the as-
generated completely active sites of Cu-2-T and its suﬃcient con-
tact mainly contribute to guiding the Li2S nucleation and growth
reactions. The homogeneous catalyst of Cu-2-T can stabilize and
protect the lithium anode by guiding the dense ﬂux of Li-ions and
optimizing their adsorption, diﬀusion and deposition behaviors.
In view of the metastable states of electrochemical species and
their hard-to-acquire spatial distributions, synchrotron radiation
X-ray 3D nano-computed tomography (X-ray 3D nano-CT), small
angle neutron scattering (SANS) are combined to collect the evi-
dences and thus unravel the working mechanism of Cu-2-T in a
practically operated Li–S batteries.
The homogeneous catalyst of Cu-2-T was synthesized by expe-
riencing the stepwise replacement procedures: i) KPF6 was ﬁrst
used to occur anion replacement reaction with Cu-2 in deionized
water to generate the precipitation of Cu-2-PF6; ii) the Cu-2-PF6
after drying was dissolved in the mixture of DME/DOL contain-
ing 1.0 mol L−1 LiTFSI and 2 wt% LiNO3 to be replaced by TFSI−.
Single crystal X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) was ﬁrst utilized to exam-
ine the structures of Cu-2 and Cu-2-T. As shown in Figures 1a
and S3, Supporting Information, Cu-2 precursor possesses bin-
uclear Cu atoms with a proximal distance of 3.5 Å and connected
by a pair of Cl bridges. Cu-2-T shows a pair of homonuclear Cu
atoms with a proximal distance of 4.7 Å and connected by a sin-
gle Cl bridge. The bond length and bond angle details of Cu-2-
T are listed in Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information. More
structure details of Cu-2-T can be obtained by looking up the up-
loaded crystal structure data to the Cambridge Crystallographic
Data Centre (CCDC number: 2 243 502). The solubility of cat-
alysts in electrolyte determines their catalytic eﬀect. Therefore,
Cu-2 can only be utilized as a heterogeneous catalyst in sulfur
cathode. And the Cu-2-T can make use of its superior solubil-
ity advantage to act as a homogeneous catalyst in Li–S battery
system. The anion replacement processes were also validated by
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra. Com-
pared with the FTIR spectrum of Cu-2 (Figure S4, Supporting
Information), the peaks located at 555 and 831 cm−1 for Cu-2-PF6
in Figure 1b can be assigned to hexaﬂuorophosphate,[33] conﬁrm-
ing the ﬁrst-step atom replacement. The FTIR spectrum of Cu-2-
T shows the peaks at 778 and 1043 cm−1 which are indexed to the

An eligible LiPS adsorption capability beneﬁts to eﬀectively al-
leviating the shuttle eﬀect in Li–S batteries. Theoretical simula-
tions and electrochemical measurements were conducted to ex-

cm−2 in contrast to the control, corroborating the outstanding
LiPS capture ability of Cu-2-T in a working Li–S battery. The Cu-2-
T and electrolyte without Cu-2-T are respectively introduced into
the cathode and anode side with one-side carbon-coated PP sep-

cathode side, pledging the eﬀective suppression of shuttle eﬀect

the more easily occurred sulfur redox reaction.
To gain mechanistic insights into the essence of homogeneous
catalysis of Cu-2-T, systematic electrochemical characterizations
were implemented in sequence. Linear scanning voltammetry
(LSV) curves of the Li–S batteries were collected in the voltage
window of 1.7–2.8 V for evaluating the sulfur redox reaction ki-
netics. As exhibited in Figure S8, Supporting Information, the
peak at the high voltage range (marked as peak I) and the low volt-
age range (denoted as peak II) in the LSV curves are attributed
to the reduction of S8 to soluble Li2Sn (4≤n ≤8) and the fur-
ther conversion to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S, respectively. Note that
the formed Cu-2-T can promote the variation of the two reduction
peaks toward high voltage, conﬁrming its high catalytic activity.
And the Tafel slopes for peak I and II stemming from the ﬁtted
curves, respectively reduce from 60.5 to 53.5 mV dec−1 and 43.0
to 39.8 mV dec−1 after introducing the Cu-2-T into the battery,
further demonstrating the kinetically boosted sulfur reduction re-
action procedure by Cu-2-T (Figure 2a,b). Symmetric cells were
assembled with Cu-2-T and blank electrolyte containing 0.2 mol
L−1 Li2S6 for cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests at 10 mV s−1. It can
be observed from the CV curves in Figures 2c and S9, Support-
ing Information that the incorporated Cu-2-T endows the sym-
metric cell with the increased redox current, verifying the cat-
alytic activity of Cu-2-T. Compared with the blank electrolyte, volt-
ages at the peaks display signiﬁcant displacement in CV curves
of the symmetric cells with Cu-2-T, revealing a smaller overpo-
tential resulting from the homogeneous catalytic function of Cu-
2-T. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of sym-
metric cell with Cu-2-T manifests an obvious reduction charge-
transfer impedance (Figure S10, Supporting Information), which
is also favorable for accelerating the conversion of LiPSs. We per-
formed the CV tests of non-sulfur containing cell with Cu-2-T
electrolyte at the voltage of 1.7–2.8 V. As seen from Figure S11,
Supporting Information, a pair of reduction and oxidation peaks
can be clearly observed in the CV curve of non-sulfur containing
cell with Cu-2-T, validating the intrinsic redox activity of Cu-2-T
in the voltage window of sulfur reactions. Asymmetric Li–S bat-
teries were fabricated to gather the CV curves in the sweep rate
range of 0.1–0.5 mV s−1 and the voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V. The
contour plots of the CV patterns show a pair of cathodic peaks and
one anodic peak (Figures 2d,e and S12, Supporting Information).
It’s evident that the battery with Cu-2-T exhibits a higher cur-
rent response than the control sample. Whilst the peak current
linearly correlates with the square root of sweep rate based on
the Randles–Sevcik equation: Ip = (2.69 × 105)·n1.5·A·D0.5·CLi·𝜐0.5,
where Ip is the peak current, 𝜐represents the potential scan rate,
CLi is the concentration of lithium-ions within the cathode, n is
the charge transfer number, D stands for the diﬀusion coeﬃcient
of lithium-ions, and A is the area of the active electrode.[35] The
battery with Cu-2-T displays larger slopes for both peak A and B,
corroborating its elevated Li-ion diﬀusion kinetics (Figure S13,
Supporting Information).[36]

formed Li2S precipitations with small sizes are conducive to low-

ing the battery energy eﬃciency.[39,40]
The eﬀect of Cu-2-T on lithium metal anodes was investigated
by fabricating Cu//Li cells and Li//Li symmetric cells. As a key
parameter for evaluating the lithium plating/striping behaviors,
Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) values with regards to Cu//Li cells
were tested at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a capacity
of 0.5 mA h cm−2. The cell with Cu-2-T obtains a stable CE of
≈98.3% over 100 cycles (Figure 3a). As a contrast, the CE value
of the Cu//Li cell with blank electrolyte presents a fast degener-
ation after 50 cycles. The nucleation overpotentials pertaining to
the gap between the voltage dip and the voltage plateau are also
shown in Figure S17, Supporting Information. The Li nucleation
overpotential shows a sharp decrease from 104.4 to 33.8 mV after
using Cu-2-T. The remarkable reduction of Li nucleation overpo-
tential indicates the homogenized Li deposition by the well- de-
signed Cu-2-T. As shown in Figure 3b, at an elevated current den-
sity of 1.0 mA cm−2 and an increased capacity of 1.0 mA h cm−2,
Li//Li symmetric cell without Cu-2-T presents a wide-range volt-
age ﬂuctuation resulting from the severe polarization and uneven
Li deposition. The homogeneous catalyst of Cu-2-T guarantees
the stable lithium plating/striping behaviors upon 600 h with a
low overpotential of ≈20 mV. As shown in Figure S18, Supporting
Information, with the catalytic eﬀect of Cu-2-T, the Li//Li sym-
metric cell can stably cycle over 150 h with the plating and strip-
ping capacity of 2 mA h cm−2 at the current of 2 mA cm−2. And
the EIS curves of the Li//Li symmetric cell with Cu-2-T for dif-
ferent cycle numbers are displayed in Figure S19, Supporting In-
formation. In Figure S19, Supporting Information, the cell with
Cu-2-T remains a low value of ≈4 Ω after 25 cycles, manifest-

To precisely probe the eﬀect of Cu-2-T on the lithium evolution
behaviors throughout the long-term working process, lithium an-
odes after cycling at 1 mA cm−2 for 50 cycles were re-sealed into
two pieces of Al-plastic ﬁlm for SANS tests (Figure 4a). SANS
as a powerful facility possesses the merits of deep penetration,
high sensibility to light elements as well as non-destructivity,
and thereby can reﬂect the formed cavities in diﬀerent lithium
layers.[43] Moreover, the SANS is suitable for depicting the cavi-
ties with the size range of ≈0–100 nm. Accordingly, the collected
size information of cavities in lithium anodes can disclose the
related lithium plating and stripping behaviors during the cy-
cling. The cavities stemming from the non-uniform lithium de-
position tend to generate a strong scattering signal. The scatter-
ing intensity I(Q) to scatter vector Q plots was ﬁtted in SASﬁt
software with model independent analysis involving Guinier and
Porod approximations. For the optimized lithium anode by Cu-
2-T, the gyration radius (Rg) of cavities is 88.2 Å (Figure 4b). And
the lithium anode with the blank electrolyte shows the cavities
with a larger Rg of 100.6 Å (Figure 4c). The SANS results indi-
cate that the homogeneous guidance eﬀect of Cu-2-T leads to the
uniform lithium deposition along with the generation of small
cavities in the inner of lithium anode (Figure 4d). Such results
are in line with the observed lithium morphologies after depo-
sition by SEM (Figure S25, Supporting Information). Further,
the lithium plating states with the Cu-2-T and blank electrolyte
were explored based on ﬁnite element analysis. The lithium foil
was also observed by SEM after immersing in Cu-2-T electrolyte
(Figure S26, Supporting Information). The obtained COMSOL
results are shown in Figure 4e,f, where t0 represents the normal-
ization time, in agreement with the plating time in the cycling
procedure. For the blank electrolyte, some dendrites gradually
appear at the initial stage of lithium plating (0.1t0). Along with
the plating process ongoing, the lithium dendrites show the sit-
uation of fast growth. As a stark contrast, owing to the homoge-
neous regulation of Cu-2-T, the anode presents a relatively uni-
form lithium deposition without obvious dendrite formation, im-
plying its stable lithium working interface state.
The Cu-2-T as a homogeneous promoter to interact with sul-
fur species and guide the Li2S nucleation as well as growth, con-
tributes to its working mechanism for sulfur conversion reac-
tions. The details are summarized as follows: i) the strong bind-
ing energy of Li2S4 on Cu-2-T pledges the suﬃcient precursor
source for the Li2S nucleation and growth reactions; ii) the uni-
formly dissolved Cu-2-T in conductive electrolyte can realizes the
direct Li2S nucleation rather than long-distance migration to car-
bon mediator for indirect nucleation; iii) the homogeneous phase
nature of Cu-2-T provides ample sites for eﬃciently promoting
the Li2S nucleation and growth reactions; iv) the enhanced Li+
migration by the Cu-2-T are also beneﬁcial to the Li2S nucleation
and growth reactions; v) Cu-2-T can also lower the activation en-
ergy of the conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S (Figures S27 and S28, Sup-
porting Information). Apart from the easily-understandable ad-
sorptive and catalytic eﬀect, we explored the interaction of Cu-2-T
with LiPSs in working Li–S batteries by liquid chromatography–
mass spectrometry (LC-MS) tests. We respectively collected the
Cu-2-T electrolyte in the battery at the end of the discharge and
charge procedure prior to test. In Figure S29, Supporting Infor-
mation, isotopic Cl leads to the production of a pair of characteris-
tic peaks of m/z at 279.9 and 281.9. And both of the two peaks can
be assigned to the fragment of [Cu(phen)Cl]+ from the initial Cu-
2-T structure. After discharge, a new pair of characteristic peaks
with m/z of 582.9 and 584.9 are indexed to the chemical formula
of [Cu(phen)Cl (Li2S2)2TfSI]+. At the end of charge, two new pairs
of peaks with m/z of 566.9 /568.9 and 577.9/579.9 form and can
respectively correspond to the chemical formulas of [Cu(phen)Cl
(Li2S8)OH]+ and [Cu(phen)Cl (Li2S2)2(Li2S4)]+. The peaks of m/z
at 279.9 and 281.9 assigning to the fragment of [Cu(phen)Cl]+ al-
ways exist after the discharge and charge process. The LC-MS
results further decipher the possible working mechanism of Cu-
2-T in the practically operated Li–S batteries. Of speciﬁc note,
the existing peaks of m/z at 279.9 and 281.9 in LC-MS spec-
tra representing the fragment of [Cu(phen)Cl]+ throughout the
cycling procedure of the battery conﬁrm the stability of Cu-2-
T. In addition, we mixed Li2S6-containing electrolyte with Cu-2-
T to inspect whether precipitates generate. As in Figure S30a,
Supporting Information, no precipitates form in the mixed solu-
tion after adding the Cu-2-T. Additionally, we also observed the
color change of the mixed solution in argon-ﬁlled glovebox over
15 days. As displayed in Figure S30b, Supporting Information,
the yellow color of the mixed solution can be maintained within
15 days. These facts evidence the favorable stability of Cu-2-T in
LiPS-containing electrolyte.

3. Conclusions
In summary, an electrolyte engineered binuclear Cu complex cat-
alyst is successfully designed by ion replacement procedure for
Li–S batteries. The existing homonuclear Cu centers and unique
coordination structure imply the intrinsic high activity of the Cu-
2-T. Particularly, such an organic small molecule catalyst is uni-
formly dissolved in the electrolyte, leading to a homogeneous
guiding function for the Li2S nucleation/decomposition reac-
tions and lithium plating/stripping behaviors according to the
well discerned active species by the synchrotron X-ray 3D nano-
CT and SANS characterizations. Further, the content of 0.25 wt%
approaches the saturated concentration of Cu complex catalyst in
electrolyte, further elevating its homogeneous optimization ac-
tivity. As a result, the as-attained Li–S battery harvests favorable
durability during the long-term working process (a capacity re-
tention of 86.3%) at 0.2 C upon 100 cycles and (a capacity re-
tention of 77.0%) 1.0 C over 400 cycles. And the soft-packaged
pouch cell also holds a substantially stable performance at 1.0 C
over 200 cycles with the high retention of 84.6%. This investiga-
tion not only oﬀers a new catalysis strategy for rationalizing the
Li–S chemistry reactions to the full extent but also enriches the
characterization methodologies to decipher the related mecha-
nism. Despite of the fruitful advancement, further promoting the
sulfur reaction kinetics can be operated by synergizing the ho-
mogeneous catalysts with saturated concentration in electrolyte
