ABSTRACT: The uncontrollable Li deposition and severe lithium
polysulfide (LiPS) shuttling hinder the commercial application in
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries. Here, we propose a bifunctional
electrolyte additive, 1,4-difluoroanthraquinone (DFAQ), to be
used for lithium regulation and acceleration of sulfur redox kinetics.
DFAQ is conducive to form a rigid and smooth LiF-rich organic−
inorganic hybrid solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), realizing
reversible Li plating/stripping, restraining dendrite growth, and
shielding Li anode from the detrimental side reaction with LiPSs.
More importantly, DFAQ can be used as a redox mediator to
enable an oxygen radical-mediated catalytic cycle to dramatically
promote the conversion of LiPSs. Accordingly, the Li−S battery
with DFAQ demonstrates ultrahigh discharge capacities, enhanced high-rate performance, and outstanding long-cycle stability
(1693.4 mAh g−1 at 0.5C over 700 cycles). Even under harsh conditions including high sulfur loading (5.8 mgS cm−2), ultrathin Li
anode (50 μm), and low E/S ratio (5.0 μL mgS−1), the Li−S battery with DFAQ shows 80 cycles in comparison to just 30 cycles for
control battery. This work demonstrates a facile additive strategy originated from bond chemistry to build applicable Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: Li anode, sulfur cathode, electrolyte additive, redox kinetics, Li−S battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Due to the appealing advantages, including ultrahigh energy
density (2600 Wh kg−1), abundant sulfur resources and
environmental benignity, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are
regarded as a competitive choice for next-generation secondary
batteries.1−3 Nevertheless, the serious lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) shuttling, large volume changes encountered during
repeated lithiation/delithiation process, and dendritic lithium
growth remain as main barriers to the application of Li−S
batteries.4−7 Therefore, through rational design such as
electrode configuration,8−11 electrolyte modification,12,13 or
electrode−electrolyte interface modification,14−16 more stable
Li−S batteries are highly anticipated. For example, Yao et al.
developed a dual-functional conductive framework (TiN-VN@
CNFs) by electrospinning method,17 in which the flexible
carbon nanofibers were integrated with TiN-VN hetero-
structures. When used as an advanced host for Li−S batteries,
the obtained TiN-VN@CNF full battery shows remarkable
battery performance. Manthiram et al. reported a three-
dimensional (3D) conductive carbon nanowire framework
coupled with CoSe nanoparticles simultaneously as an efficient
host for sulfur cathode and lithium anode.18 However, the
above strategies always involve complex electrode fabrication.
Previous studies have demonstrated that suitable electrolyte
additives capable of forming durable interfaces or undergoing
electrochemical transformations on the electrode can solve the
knotty issues of Li−S batteries.19−21 Especially, the redox
mediators are successfully developed to enhance sulfur kinetics
and inhibit LiPS shuttling by regulating the chemical reactions
of sulfur species.22−24 For example, the benzenedithiols25 and
1,3,5-benzenetrithiol26 were proposed as electrolyte additives
to simultaneously inhibit LiPS shuttling and achieve reversible
Li deposition in Li−S batteries. Recently, our group has also
reported that diphenyl ditelluride can be used as an effective
additive for both sulfur and Li electrodes, resulting in Li−S
batteries with superior battery performance.27 It has been
revealed that quinhydrone complexes are reactive with lithium,
and they can be stabilized through charge-transfer interactions
between the acceptor (quinone) and donor (hydroquinone).28
Tsao et al. employed the redox chemistry of quinone to
facilitate Li2S oxidation in Li−S batteries, and the initial
charging of Li2S electrodes can occur below 2.5 V at 0.5C for
the first time.29 Dong et al. introduced quinhydrone in the Li−
S battery and demonstrated that there existed chemical

In recent years, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (S@pPAN)
shows great promise as a sulfur cathode due to its unique
short-chain sulfur structure and suitable electronic conductiv-
ity.31,32 Nevertheless, the S@pPAN cathode exhibits sluggish
reaction kinetics in ether electrolytes, which dramatically
hinders its applications in practical Li−S batteries. Huang et al.
demonstrated a quinone-based redox mediator to accelerate
the electron-transfer processes and enable strengthened
interfacial kinetics for S@pPAN cathodes.33 Our group
proposed a Se/Te-based eutectic accelerator strategy34−40
and phenyl diselenide electrolyte additive41 to accelerate sulfur
kinetics. Moreover, electrolyte design is recently adopted to
regulate the Li plating/striping behavior in Li−S batteries. For
example, a modification of nitrate ion (NO3
−) is proposed and
validated to improve the homogeneity of the solid electrolyte
interface (SEI) in practical lithium metal batteries.42 An
encapsulating LiPS electrolyte based on the solvation structure
is designed to mitigate the reaction kinetics of Li−S batteries.43
In addition, fluorinated compounds in the electrolytes are
capable of forming LiF-rich SEI with excellent stability and fast
Li+ transport on the surface of Li anodes, thus improving the
coulombic efficiency of Li deposition.44−46 Therefore, a facile
strategy combining the redox mediation ability of quinhydrone
compounds and superiority of fluorinated compounds on Li
anode is anticipated to solve issues of Li−S batteries, where
such a methodology has not been clearly explored.
Here, a commercially available fluorinated anthraquinone
compound, 1,4-difluoroanthraquinone (DFAQ), is used as a
bifunctional additive to enhance the sulfur kinetics and
improve the interfacial chemistry of Li anode in S@pPAN-
based Li−S batteries. For the lithium metal anode, a stable and
smooth LiF-rich organic−inorganic hybrid SEI is formed
through the reaction between DFAQ and Li metal, enabling a
uniform Li deposition with reduced Li nucleation over-
potential. For the sulfur cathode, due to the introduction of
DFAQ, an oxygen radical-mediated catalytic cycle can
effectively accelerate the kinetics of the sulfur cathode at the
solid−liquid interface. Consequently, the Li−S battery with
DFAQ exhibits highly efficient lithium stripping/plating and
enhanced sulfur utilization, thus resulting in an ultrahigh
reversible capacity of 1693.4 mAh g−1 and a stable cycling over
700 cycles. In addition, the bifunctional effect of DFAQ is
successfully applied in the Li−S battery under practical
conditions. It shows 80 cycles, while the control battery
affords 30 cycles before rapid decay. This work demonstrates a
bifunctional electrolyte additive to accelerate the kinetics of
sulfur and improve interfacial chemistry for advanced Li−S
batteries.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. Sulfur powder (≥99.0%), polyacrylonitrile (PAN,
Mw = 150 000), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, ≥99.0%), sodium
carboxyl methyl cellulose (NaCMC, average Mw ∼90 000), 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL, ≥99.0%), and lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99.99%) were
provided by Sigma-Aldrich. The Li bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%) was purchased from Guangzhou Tinci
Materials Technology Co., Ltd. Lithium (Li) metal foils were
provided by Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.
The polypropylene (PP) membranes and aluminum (Al) foil were
bought from Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd. Ketjen black
(KB) was provided by Lion Corp., Japan. The styrene butadiene
rubber (SBR) was bought from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co.,
Ltd. The carbon paper from NanoTechLabs, Inc. was used as a
current collector for the electrochemical test of the Li-DFAQ battery.
2.2. Material Synthesis. Preparing of DFAQ-L2S4 solution: first,
0.1 M DFAQ solution (DOL/DME with a volume ratio of 1:1 used as
a solvent) was prepared. Then, the above 2 mL of 0.1 M DFAQ
solution and 4 mL of 0.1 M Li2S4 solution (DOL/DME with a
volume ratio of 1:1 used as a solvent) were mixed. This solution was
stirred for 1 h. These operations were all performed in a glovebox.
Synthesis of S@pPAN: S@pPAN composites were synthesized
through the heating treatment method. Briefly, sulfur powder and
PAN were first mixed well by ball-milling with a weight ratio of 3:1.
Then, the above mixture was annealed in an Ar atmosphere for 3 h
with a temperature of 300 °C. Finally, S@pPAN powder was obtained
after the tube furnace was cooled to room temperature.
2.3. Material Characterization. X-ray diffraction (XRD)
measurements were done by a Rigaku MiniFlex600/600-C. Scanning
electron microscope (SEM, Sirion200) was carried out to characterize
the morphology of cycled Li anodes for symmetric Li−Li batteries
and sulfur cathodes for Li|S@pPAN batteries. UV−vis absorption
spectrometry was performed by Lambda 35 to detect LiPS species in
the Li−S batteries after cycling, in which the disassembled sulfur
cathode was rinsed by DOL. Raman spectra were carried out by a
LabRAM HR800 (Horiba Jobin Yvon). X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) was measured by AXIS SUPRA+ with the samples
cleaned with argon plasma in advance to investigate the chemical
composition of SEI in Li anodes. The elemental analysis of samples
was performed on a Vario Micro Cube to measure the sulfur content
for S@pPAN composites. Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR)
were done by a Nicolet iS50R spectrometer. 7Li nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired for Li2S4 and DFAQ-L2S4
samples by the JNM-ECA600 system.
2.4. Electrochemical Measurements. 2032 coin batteries were
assembled to test the battery performance of Li−S batteries with or
without DFAQ. For the Li−Li symmetric battery, two pieces of Li
foils were used, and 40 μL of 25 mM blank electrolyte (1.0 M LiTFSI
in DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1)) or DFAQ electrolyte was dropped onto
the two sides of a separator. Then, the above batteries were tested
under different current densities and capacity densities. For Li−S
batteries, the sulfur cathodes with low S loading (<1 mgS cm−2) were
prepared by mixing S@pPAN composites, KB, NaCMC, and SBR,
with a weight ratio of 80:10:5:5. The blank electrolytes were 1.0 M
LiTFSI in DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1) with 2 wt % of LiNO3. For the
electrolytes with DFAQ, the concentration of DFAQ was 13, 25, and
40 mM, respectively. For common measurements, the E/S ratio was
25 μL mg−1 and Li foils with a thickness of 200 μm were used. To
prepare practical Li−S batteries, the Li foils (50 μm), high sulfur
loading (>5 mgS cm−2), and low E/S ratio (5 μL mg−1) were used. In
these practical Li−S batteries, the DFAQ additive amount was 125
mM. For the preparation of sulfur cathodes with high S loading (>5
mgS cm−2), the electrodes were prepared through a previously
reported frozen-drying method.37 The above Li−S batteries were
measured by a LAND-CT2001A battery test system between 1.0 and
3.0 V. To test the electrochemical behavior of DFAQ, 25 μL of 1 M
DFAQ in the blank electrolytes was dropped onto the carbon paper
with a diameter of 8 mm, and 10 μL of blank electrolytes was dropped
onto the Li anode.
The cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles and electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were measured by electrochemical
workstation (Solartron 1470E). Galvanostatic intermittent titration
technique (GITT) test was performed by discharging or charging
batteries at a current density of 80 mA g−1 for 1 h and resting with 4
h. Using the Randles−Sevick equation (Ip = 2.69 × 105 n1.5 ADLi
+0.5
CLi
+ v0.5), the lithium-ion diffusion coefficients of the samples were
analyzed by testing CV curves under different scan rates. The n was
the number of electron transformation (2 for Li−S battery). A was the
electrode area (cm2), which was unknown because of the porosity of
the electrode. Therefore, ADLi
+ was calculated and the ratio of ADLi
+
for the sulfur cathode with and without DFAQ was used to value the
Li+ migration capability.

2.5. Theoretical Calculation. To simulate the acceleration effect
of DFAQ on the LiPS conversion, the density functional theory

semicircle at the low-frequency range ascribes to the charge-
transfer resistance (Rct) and the semicircle at the high-
frequency range stands for Li+ transport across SEI (RSEI). For
fresh DFAQ battery, it possesses lower impedances than the

Aiming to further clarify the superior Li deposition behavior
of symmetric Li battery with the DFAQ additive, the chemical
composition of the interfacial layer was characterized by XPS.
Figure 2a−f shows the F 1s, C 1s, and O 1s profiles of
symmetric Li batteries with and without DFAQ after cycling.
For the F 1s spectra, the peaks at 688.6 and 684.9 eV resulted
from the C−F in the CF3 functional group and LiF,
respectively. The CF3 species may originate from LiCF3 or
Li2NSO2CF3, which are the main decomposition products of
LiTFSI with the Li anode.47 Moreover, the peak intensity of
CF3 and LiF is much different from the above two batteries. A
ratio of LiF to CF3 reaches up to 1.85 in the symmetric Li
battery DFAQ, which is much higher than that in the control
battery (0.22). It indicates that the introduction of DFAQ
enables similar TFSI-derived SEI and generates more LiF
composition, leading to a LiF-rich interfacial layer with high
interfacial energy and good electric insulator, which can
dramatically suppress the lithium dendrite growth and inhibit
electrolyte consumption.46,48 To clarify the chemical reaction
between DFAQ and Li metal, Li foil was added to the DFAQ
solution to obtain DFAQLi. The 1H NMR spectra of fresh
DFAQ solution features several chemical shifts between 3.0
and 5.0 ppm (Figure 4b), and they all move to the higher field
range for DFAQLi, indicating that there exists a chemical
reaction between DFAQ and Li (Figure S3). Besides, the
reduction peak at 1.53 V for the CV curves of the Li-DFAQ
battery in which DFAQ was the only electrochemically active
material also proves the broken C−F in the benzene ring to
form LiF (Figure S4). In the C 1s and O 1s XPS spectra for the
two batteries as shown in Figure 2b−f, the interfacial layer is
composed of CO, C−O, C−C/C−H, and SO species,
mainly due to the side reactions TFSI−and DME with the Li
anode, and this proves the existence of organic composition in
the SEI. Moreover, the Ph−O species appear on the Li surface
for the DFAQ battery, demonstrating that DFAQ may also
participate in the formation of an interfacial layer. Therefore,
DFAQ as an electrolyte additive can greatly increase the LiF

The intrinsic accelerating reason of DFAQ on the
conversion of LiPSs was simulated by DFT calculations.
Figure 4a depicts the optimized molecular configurations and
corresponding bond dissociation energy (BDE) of LiPSs with
and without DFAQ. It is shown that the BDE values of S−O
bonds in LiSn−1OR are all much lower than the corresponding
S−S bonds in Li2Sn, suggesting higher electrochemical activity
for LiSn−1OR. Moreover, as the sulfur chain gets longer, the
BDE values of S−O bonds are decreased, indicating the weaker
chemical interaction between O and S. Figure S13 shows the
possible reaction pathways for DFAQ as an electrolyte additive
to enhance the reaction kinetics of sulfur cathodes. It has been
reported that reactive oxygen radicals are easily formed for
anthraquinone compounds through an electrochemical reduc-
tion reaction.51,52 Therefore, once discharged, DFAQ will react
with Li to generate RO• that attacks S−S of Li2S4 to form
intermediates of LiS2OR via a fast radical exchange, as proven
by our previously reported works.22,27,40 The BDE of S−O
bonds (1.22 kcal/mol) in LiS2OR is much lower than that of
S−S bonds in Li2S3 (44.16 kcal/mol). Then, the S−O bond in
LiS2OR is easily broken to generate RO• and Li2S2, which will
be continuously attacked by RO• to form intermediates of
LiSOR and Li2S. These LiSOR species will be easily reduced to
form Li2S and another RO•, making up an oxygen radical-
mediated catalytic cycle, as shown in Figure 4b. Therefore,
because of the introduction of DFAQ, an oxygen radical-
mediated catalytic cycle exists in which more electrochemically
active S−O bonds are formed to accelerate the kinetics of
LiPSs.

(68.9 mAh g−1) after 30 cycles (Figure S17). It can be seen
that the capacity contributed by DFAQ is negligible for the
Li−S battery with DFAQ. Hence, the significantly increased
capacity and great reversibility of the Li−S battery with DFAQ
are presumably rooted in the accelerated redox conversion due
to the mediation effect of DFAQ on polysulfides.
Then, the high-rate capability of two batteries was
investigated (Figure 5b−d). The Li−S battery with DFAQ
holds stable capacities of 1720.2, 1585.0, 1394.7, 1271.7,
1163.2, and 1057.2 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3C rates,
respectively, and retains excellent recovery capability as the
current goes back at 0.1C. However, discharge capacities of the
control cell fluctuate greatly as current density increases and
generally exhibits an attenuation trend. In addition, the Li−S
battery with DFAQ exhibits superior cycling stability at 1C,
while the discharge capacities of the control battery fade
rapidly as cycling proceeds (Figure 5e). Specifically, the DFAQ
battery has a high reversible discharge capacity of 1653.2 mAh
g−1 and holds 877.9 mAh g−1 after 300th, equal to a low
capacity decay of 0.16% per cycle. Therefore, benefiting from
the DFAQ mediation effect, the battery performance of S@
pPAN batteries is dramatically improved.
Finally, the efficiency of the dual-function DFAQ additive is
evaluated in the Li−S battery under harsh conditions. At 0.1C,
the DFAQ battery exhibits a high reversible discharge capacity
of 1656.4 mAh g−1 (9.6 mAh cm−2) for the 2nd cycle and
holds 1013.4 mAh g−1 (5.9 mAh cm−2) after 80 cycles (Figure
6a). However, the discharge capacity of the control battery
drops quickly after 35 cycles. This abrupt capacity drop is
mainly due to the depletion of fresh Li metal consumed
gradually through side reactions with lithium polysulfides.53 At
the initial stage, Li is adequate, while more dead Li metals
accumulate and less available Li remains as lithiation/
delithiation process proceeds. For the Li−S battery with
DFAQ, DFAQ can enable the lithium anode with a stable and
rigid LiF-rich organic−inorganic hybrid SEI, shielding the Li
anode from adverse reactions. Moreover, via rapid reaction
with the exposed Li surface, the DFAQ could be able to
dynamically repair the cracked SEI. The morphological
changes for the cycled Li anode of Li−S batteries with
ultrathin Li foil and high S loading were then investigated. For
the control cell, the Li surface is distinctly corrosive and the
deposition morphology is sparse (Figure 6b). While for the
Li−S battery with DFAQ, it displays uniform, compact, and
highly packed Li deposition (Figure 6c). As a result, with the
addition of DFAQ, the depletion of fresh Li is effectively
suppressed, resulting in a significantly improved cycle life for
practical Li−S batteries. Figure S18 shows S 2p XPS spectra of
the Li anode for Li−S batteries with/without DFAQ additive
after cycling. The Li surfaces for the two batteries all contain
the compositions of inorganic sulfides (Li2S/Li2S2), sulfites,
and thiosulfates.54,55 However, compared with the control
battery, the DFAQ battery has a much lower inorganic sulfide
content, indicating that LiF-rich organic−inorganic hybrid SEI
could mitigate side reactions of LiPSs with the Li metal.
Additionally, the sulfur cathode for the DFAQ battery also
maintains excellent structural integrity without obvious
agglomeration and irregular thick coatings during cycling
(Figure S19).
4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, a bifunctional electrolyte additive, DFAQ, is
identified for the Li−S batteries. It can simultaneously address
