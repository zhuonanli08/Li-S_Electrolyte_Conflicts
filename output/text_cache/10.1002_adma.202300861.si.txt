Supplementary Figure 6. The three-phase interfaces model in LSB with CuL

The three-phase interfaces of lithium sulfur batteries are composed of the 
following materials: S as active materials for energy storage, conductive substrate for 
electron transfer, and catalytic center for rapid conversion, as shown in 
Supplementary Figure 6. 
 
Actually, any conductive substrate can be used in the model to ensure electron 
transfer in the reaction. In our DFT calculation model, graphene, a commonly used 
conductive representative substrate, is introduced as the electron transfer substrate 
model. To clarify the influence of graphene substrate, the interaction between LiPSs 
and graphene is also calculated. The result shows that the influence is very limited as 
shown in Supplementary Figure 5.  
 
Therefore, by comparing the interaction between LiPSs and graphene as a 
substrate, the significant increase (over 400% higher than that without CuL) in 
binding energy after the addition of CuL is mainly attributed to the strong interaction 
between LiPSs and CuL.

The constant-current discharging curve presents the characteristic dual-plateaus, 
indicating the complete conversion of sulfur cathode (Supplementary Figure 11). The 
digital images of H-cell before and after discharging were shown in Supplementary 
Figure 12. There is no obvious color change in the anode electrolyte after the 
discharging process, revealing that the carbon nanofiber interlayer can inhibit the 
shuttling of both LiPSs and macrocyclic CuL complex. The liquid level of cathode 
electrolyte rises, which can be ascribed to an increased osmotic pressure by the 
dissolved LiPSs and CuL complex.

The LiPSs→S reaction occurs on the electrode interface to transfer the electrons. 
We believe that there should be a competition between the concentration of the CuL 
and the active deposition spots of sulfur. Theoretically, a suitable spatial distance is 
required to avoid the LiPS polarization by the accumulation of large size LiPSs-CuL 
on the interface and guarantee the efficient progress of the catalysis, which leads to 
excellent cycling performance. 0.5 wt%, 1 wt% and 2 wt% were tested to find out the 
smallest sufficient space between each of the LiPSs-CuL. The optimal concentration 
was determined to be 1% based on the cycling performance of LSB as shown in 
Supplementary Figure 13.

To investigate the stability of CuL to Li anode in the electrochemical process, 
Li-Cu half-cell and Li-Li symmetrical cell were assembled for further tests. The 
nucleation overpotential of ~65 mV for Li|Cu half-cells with/without CuL addition 
(Supplementary Figure 20) was observed, suggesting that the Li-ion nucleation 
behavior of the Li anode was not sensitive with CuL addition. The polarization test of 
symmetrical cell using electrolyte with CuL additive presents a stable cycle 
performance at 1.0 mA cm−2 without random fluctuation or short circuit for 2000 
hours (Supplementary Figure 21), verifying the decent interfacial stability of CuL 
additive against Li metal anodes.

CuL is formed by the coordination of a central copper ion with a ligand. The 
central Cu(II), as a catalytically active site, can strongly interact with the sulfur atoms 
in LiPSs. The ligand is a tetradentate macrocyclic structure, in which the nitrogen 
atoms have a strong interaction with lithium atoms, while the methyl and ethyl groups 
mainly serve as space occupier. In the ligand design of this work, the size of the center 
of the ligand (four nitrogen atoms) is 4.03 Å. The size of the sulfur chain in LiPSs is 
shown in the Supplementary Table 1. 
 
It is worth noting that in the adsorption structure model of CuL and LiPSs, 
long-chain LiPSs suffer from severe geometric distortion due to the strong attraction 
of the central Cu(II) and the spatial hindrance of the ligand, as shown in 
Supplementary Figure 3. The adsorption energy of the ligand to LiPSs has a clear 
correlation with the length of the sulfur chain, while the Cu(II) exhibits relatively high 
adsorption energy to almost all LiPSs. The result indicates that the reactants can be 
screened by the ligand of CuL based on their molecular weight and chain geometry, 
which is defined as steric hindrance effect in this paper. Only LiPSs with sizes 
matching the CuL catalytic active site could contact Cu(II) sufficiently and achieve 
the highest catalytic efficiency.   
In this work, the size of ligand is designed to be between Li2S4 and Li2S6, which can 
effectively accelerate the decisive step (Li2S4 → Li2S2) of lithium-sulfur battery.

The strong interaction between CuL and LiPSs from S8 to Li2S in each reaction 
step guarantees superior catalytic performance under high S loading. 
 
In this work, the steric effect is manifested in two aspects: (1) In the geometric 
optimization of the interaction between CuL and LiPSs in DFT calculation, it can be 
observed that when the size of LiPSs exceeds that of the ligand, serious geometric 
distortion occurs due to steric hindrance, which affects the adsorption between CuL 
and LiPSs. (2) By calculating the adsorption energies of CuL, Cu(II), and ligands on 
LiPSs, it is found that Cu(II) exhibits high adsorption energy for all LiPSs.  
 
We evaluate the contribution of steric hindrance to catalytic performance by 
comparing the adsorption energies of ligand/CuL on LiPSs with different sizes. As 
shown in Supplementary Table 2, the ratios of adsorption energy on ligand/CuL are 
13.9%, 19.9%, -1.6%, 44.2%, 57.8%, and 58.9%, respectively.
