ABSTRACT: With extremely high specific capacity and high energy density, lithium−sulfur
batteries (LSBs) have attracted enormous interest as promising candidates for energy storage
devices. However, several problems, such as the shuttle effect and sluggish redox kinetics,
hinder the successful realization of LSBs on an industrial scale. Therefore, designing an
efficient electrode material to inhibit the shuttle effect and improve the reaction kinetics of
polysulfides (LiPS) is of utmost significance. Herein, a bifunctional additive with excellent
polysulfide adsorption and superior catalytic behavior is developed using the phthalocyanine-
tetrasulfonic acid nickel complex tetrasodium salt (Ni-PCTs) additive. Ni-PCTs provide
effective trapping of LiPS due to their abundant sulfonic acid groups. Moreover, Ni-PCTs
exhibit effective catalytic conversion of LiPS due to the presence of N atoms in the
phthalocyanine ring as well as the central Ni atoms. Consequently, the as-assembled LSBs,
with a 10 wt % Ni-PCTs additive, exhibit a significant increase in specific capacities, such as
the high initial specific capacity of 1283 mA h g−1 at 0.15 mA/cm2 and a stable specific
capacity of 623 mA h g−1 after 400 cycles. The current study demonstrates the promise of metal phthalocyanines for sulfur cathodes,
opening up avenues for further research and development of LSBs.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, additive, bifunctional, adsorption, catalytic
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) are promising candidates for
energy storage systems due to their superior theoretical specific
capacity and energy density.1−4 However, the following
intractable challenges should be addressed for the successful
realization of LSBs: (1) the shuttle effect originating from the
dissolution and diffusion of long-chain polysulfides (Li2S4−
Li2S8) in ether-based electrolytes;5−8 and (2) the sluggish
redox kinetics and severe cathode passivation originating from
insulating Li2S during the discharge reaction.2,9,10 The shuttle
effect from Li-based polysulfides (LiPS) leads to self-discharge,
loss of active material, reduced coulomb efficiency, rapid
capacity decay, and fast Li corrosion during cycling.11−13
Moreover, the insulating products of the discharged Li2S result
in sluggish redox kinetics, leading to severe cathode passivation
and diminishing overall sulfur utilization.14,15
Therefore, effective trapping of LiPS to suppress the shuttle
effect is the main research focus to improve the performance of
LSBs. To date, various strategies have been explored to achieve
better affinity for LiPS, including the utilization of carbona-
ceous materials (porous/hollow carbon,16−18 carbon nano-
fibers/nanotubes, and graphene19,20), various mediators
(heteroatom-doped carbonaceous materials,21 metal com-
pounds,22 nonmetallic polymers,23 and conducting poly-
mers24,25), and a wide range of polymeric binder (chitosan,26
soy protein isolate27). Although these tactics have improved
the performance of LSBs to a certain degree, these results are
still unsatisfactory. One should note that the saturation of
surface adsorption sites is inevitable, resulting in uncontrolled
dissolution and accumulation of LiPS and reducing sulfur
utilization.28 The accumulation of soluble LiPS at the cathode
is due to the sluggish reaction kinetics of conversion from Li2S4
to the final Li2S, which inevitably diffuses from the cathode to
the anode. Besides, the dissolution and accumulation of LiPS
result in the precipitation of insoluble Li2S on the cathode
surface, forming large particles that are difficult to reuse during
subsequent reactions.29 Therefore, promoting the redox
kinetics of adsorbed LiPS is highly desirable to improve the
overall sulfur utilization. Recently, catalytic LiPS conversion of
the discharge products (S/Li2S) has been demonstrated as an
effective strategy to reduce the amount of LiPS in the
electrolyte and further elevate the sulfur utilization of the
cathode. For example, transition metal phosphides, such as
FeP, CoP, MoP, and NiP, nanocatalysts; such as metal oxides
and metal sulfides; and transition metal phthalocyanine
compounds (M = Fe, Co, Cu, and Zn), have rendered
excellent electrocatalytic properties in energy storage devices,

Therefore, it is extremely important to suppress the shuttle
effect and improve the reaction kinetics of polysulfides to
obtain high-performance LSBs.
Despite the substantial improvement of LSBs from the
viewpoints of adsorption and catalytic conversion, there remain
some shortcomings in the application that a single substance
always achieves a single side of functionality. In order to realize
the dual functions of adsorption and catalysis, two different
types of substances are added at the same time, leading to a
complex production process and high costs. Moreover, the
addition of two different substances reduces the active amount
of sulfur, which is not conducive to the development of LSBs
with a high energy density. Although few materials exhibit by-
functionality, the preparation process often requires high-
temperature pyrolysis, which is not conducive to large-scale
applications.30 Therefore, it is very important to find a
substance with a low price, a simple production process, a
low dosage, superior LiPS trapping, and excellent catalytic
properties.
Herein, we demonstrate a bifunctional additive of
phthalocyanine-tetrasulfonic acid nickel complex tetrasodium
salt (Ni-PCTs) for sulfur cathodes, providing effective LiPS
trapping at the cathode and promoting sulfur redox. In
particular, the sulfur cathode is prepared by simply mixing Ni-
PCTs with an active material (sulfur) and a conductive agent
[carbon black (CB)]. The simple production process and low
dosage of Ni-PCTs are highly conducive to large-scale
applications.
Ni-PCTs are formed by the coordination of the
phthalocyanine ring with nickel and sodium tetra sulfonate.
The molecular formula of Ni-PCTs is shown in Figure S1, and
the mechanisms of adsorption and conversion of LiPS at Ni-
PCTs additive are presented in Figure 1. Ni-PCTs are a highly
effective redox catalyst owing to the active metal-N4 centers,
which can accelerate the reduction of polysulfides, reduce the
Gibbs free energy, promote the formation of lithium sulfide,
and promote the electrochemical reaction.36 Besides, the
pyrrolic N atoms on the Ni-PCT rings are not only lithiophilic
but also facilitate the catalytic conversion of LiPS.36,37
Moreover, Ni-PCTs are rich in sulfonic acid groups, which
can effectively adsorb LiPS and enhance the electrochemical
performance of the sulfur cathode. Last but not least, Ni-PCTs
exist in the form of sodium salt, leading to excellent water
solubility. On the one hand, it avoids the utilization of organic
solvents during electrode production, and on the other hand,
Ni-PCTs are suitable for the preparation of sulfur cathode by
forming a well-dispersed uniform slurry (Figure S2).
Owing to the ideal chemical structure, LSBs assembled with
10% Ni-PCTs additive exhibit a remarkable improvement in
electrochemical performance, rendering a high initial specific
capacity (1282.98 mA h g−1 at 0.15 mA/cm2) and stable long-
term cycling (623.12 mA h g−1 after 400 cycles at 0.15 mA/
cm2). However, the LSBs without Ni-PCTs exhibit a low initial
discharge capacity of 993.38 mA h g−1 and poor cyclic
performance (292.66 mA h g−1 after 400 cycles at 0.15 mA/
cm2). In addition, the deposition of lithium sulfide shows that
Ni-PCTs possess excellent catalytic performance for lithium
polysulfides, and the deposition of lithium sulfide corresponds
to a capacity of 206 mA h g−1, which is significantly higher
than that of the LSBs without Ni-PCTs (140 mA h g−1).
Moreover, high specific capacity and stable cyclic performance
under a high sulfur loading of 4.7 mg cm−2 have also been
demonstrated due to the presence of Ni-PCTs. Hence, the
proposed strategy is a novel route for LiPS adsorption and
conversion with metal phthalocyanines to exploit high-
performance LSBs.

2.2. Preparation of Ni-PCTs@S/C Composite and S/C
Composite. In a typical experiment, Ni-PCTs were mixed with
sulfur by hand milling for 20 min, and then CB (Super P/ketjen black
= 1:1) was added and ground for 30 min, with a ratio of 1:6:3. After
mixing, the mixture was put into the tank of the reactor and dried for
2 h to remove the water absorbed in the grinding process. Finally, the
inert gas was replaced in the glovebox; the reactor was locked and
taken out, transferred to vacuum heating at 155 °C for 12 h, and then
cooled to room temperature to obtain Ni-PCTs@S/C composite
(name as 10% Ni-PCTs). The preparation process of the S/C
composite (named 0% Ni-PCTs) was the same as that of the Ni-
PCTs@sulfur carbon composite, except that Ni-PCTs were not added
into the grinding process.
2.3. Preparation of Sulfur Cathodes. The cathode material was
prepared by mixing Ni-PCTs@S/C composite or S/C composite and
PAA binder at a mass ratio of 9:1 and stirred by a high-speed ball mill
with deionized water as a solvent to form homogeneous slurries.
Carbon cloth with a diameter of 12 mm was used as the collector. The
sulfur loading of the electrode was between 1.7 and 2 mg cm−2.
2.4. Preparation of LSBs. For the half cell, CR2025 coin cells
were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox to study the electro-
chemical properties of LSBs. The electrolyte was an LSBs-based
electrolyte (1.0 m LiTFSI and 1.0% LiNO3 were dissolved in the
mixed solution of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) with the volume ratio of 1:1). Celgard 2500 was the
separator. The additive amount of electrolyte is 25 μL mg−1 of sulfur.
For the full cell, CR2025 coin cells were assembled with Li deposited
on a copper-coated carbon cloth as the anode. The experimental steps
are as follows: (1) the copper-coated carbon cloth was cut into a 12
mm diameter and shape to serve as the working electrode, and a
lithium foil was used as the counter electrode. The electrolyte is 1 M
LiTFSI in a mixture of DOL and DME (1:1, vol ratio) with a 1 wt %
LiNO3 additive. (2) the cell was first cycled five times at 0−1 V and 1
mA cm−2 to activate cuprous oxide in the cladding metal layer, and
then the required Li metal was deposited inside copper-coated carbon
cloth at 0.5 mA cm−2 to obtain the anode. In the full cells, the N/P
ratio (negative/positive capacity ratio) for the full-cell was 5:1. The
electrolyte is 15 μL mg−1 of sulfur for the LSBs full cell, and the
electrolyte/capacity ratio is 12 μL mA h−1.
2.5. Adsorption Capacity of LiPS. Typically, sulfur and Li2S
with a molar ratio of 1:5 in a mixture solution of DOL/DME (1:1 v/
v) was stirred at 45 °C for 48 h to prepare a LiPS (average formula
Li2S6) solution. To investigate the adsorption capacity of the Ni-PCTs
to lithium polysulfides (LiPS), 0.8 mM LiPS (average formula Li2S6)
was prepared. Soak the Ni-PCTs or CB at 20 mg in a 3 mL LiPS
solution to research the adsorption of LiPS. UV−visible (vis)
spectrograms were obtained via a Shimadzu UV-240 spectropho-
tometer. The absorbance was tracked every 5 min within the
wavelength of 350−550 nm by a UV−vis spectrometer.
2.6. Electrochemical Measurements. The symmetrical cell
slurry was prepared by mixing Ni-PCTs, super P, and PAA binder at a
mass ratio of 3:1:1 and stirred by a high-speed ball mill with deionized
water as solvent (acidified carbon cloth as a collector). The cathode
and anode used the same electrode (the load and size were
consistent). The electrolyte of the symmetrical cell was composed
of 0.2 M Li2S6, 1.0 M LiTFSI, and 0.2 M LiNO3 dissolved in DOL
and DME (V/V = 1:1). The additive amount of electrolyte is 50 μL.
After packaging, the electrochemical performance was tested at 30 °C
for 8 h. In the control sample, the Ni-PCTs were replaced with the
same proportion of CB, and the other preparation methods were the
same as those of Ni-PCTs.
For the Li2S deposition experiments, sulfur and Li2S with a molar
ratio of 7:1 were dissolved in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (with
1 M LiTFSI) to form a 0.3 M Li2S8 stock solution. The mass ratio of
Ni-PCTs (or CB) to super P was 4:1, and the mixture was mixed by a
high-speed ball mill with deionized water as a solvent to obtain
uniform cathode electrodes for the lithium sulfide nucleation battery.
The commercial carbon paper was a collector, and the load of the
cathode electrodes was less than 0.5 mg cm−2. 20 μL of a portion of
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether without Li2S8 was used as the
cathode electrolyte, and 20 μL of a 0.3 M Li2S8 solution was used as
the anode electrolyte. CR2025 coin cells were assembled using the 12
mm diameter cathode electrodes and 14 mm diameter Li foil discs.
For Li2S deposition, the cells were first galvanostatically discharged to
2.06 V at a current of 0.112 mA and then continuously discharged to
2.05 V with a cutoff current of 0.01 mA.

These results confirm that LiPS is effectively captured by the
Ni-PCTs additive, thereby alleviating the shuttle effect. The
strong adsorption capacity of LiPS is mainly due to the sulfonic
acid groups of Ni-PCTs and nitrogen elements in the
macrocycle.38 The reduction in the shuttle effect of LiPS is
also conducive to the subsequent catalytic conversion of LiPS
into lithium sulfide, remarkably enhancing the utilization of
sulfur and the cyclic performance of LSBs.39
In LSBs, accelerating the reaction kinetics of LiPS plays an
important role in promoting sulfur retention at the cathode
side and improving overall utilization of sulfur, which
significantly reduces the saturation of surface adsorption sites
and improves electrochemical performance, i.e., specific
capacity and cycling stability.39 To evaluate the catalytic
activity of Ni-PCTs, CV was adopted by using a symmetric
cell, which consisted of two identical Ni-PCT-based electrodes
or pure CB electrodes. The CV curves were recorded in the
voltage range of −1 to 1 V at a scan rate of 0.3 mV s−1. As
shown in Figure 4a, the CV curves of 10% Ni-PCTs-based cells
clearly exhibit one pair of redox peaks with high reversibility,
which is similar to most of the reported catalysts.40 The CV
curves of 10% Ni-PCTs exhibit two obvious peaks at 0.22 and
−0.22 V, which are related to the conversion of Li2S6 in the
presence of two electrodes.41,42 We also ran a control
experiment to confirm that CB does not exhibit any redox
reactions, as shown in Figure 4b. One should note that the
generated current density of the Ni-PCTs host is 0.02 A, which
is significantly higher than the CB host (<0.01 A).
Moreover, the formation of solid Li2S from liquid LiPS is
considered to be another conversion kinetic index during the
redox process of cathodic sulfur.42 It is worth noting that three-
quarters of the LSBs’s theoretical capacity originates from the
slow liquid−solid transition of soluble Li2S4 into insoluble Li2S.
The catalytic sulfur redox during the solid−liquid−solid
conversions in the presence of the Ni-PCTs host was further
revealed by the potentiostatic deposition of Li2S along with the
nucleation behavior. The test cells were composed of Ni-PCTs
or CB-based cathodes (without sulfur), lithium foil (anode),
and 0.3 M Li2S8 (catholyte), as detailed in the Experimental
section. The potentiostatic discharge curves were obtained by
integrating the fitted peak of Ni-PCTs or CB electrodes at 2.05
V, as shown in Figure 4c,d, respectively. To quantify the
precipitation of Li2S, mathematical modeling was carried out
according to the two-dimensional growth mechanism of Li2S
on CF, as proposed by Fan et al., distinguishing the
precipitation of Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S phases.43 According to
the corresponding relationship between the mass of sulfur in
the electrolyte and the current integral calculation, the
integrated specific capacity of Li2S deposition was calculated.
In the case of Ni-PCTs and CB-based LSBs, the measured
current profiles can be divided into contributions from the
initial reduction of Li2S8, background reduction of Li2S6, and
deposition of Li2S.44 Compared with the CB-based LSBs, the
Ni-PCTs-based LSBs exhibited a sharp and intense Li2S
deposition peak in the current profile, reaching the highest
value of Li2S potentiostatic current in only 4345 s, which is
much faster than the CB-based cell (6575 s). The lower Li2S
precipitation time indicates that the presence of Ni-PCTs
significantly promoted the nucleation density and growth rate
of Li2S. Besides, the Li2S precipitation capacity in Ni-PCTs-
based LSBs is found to be 206 mA h g−1, which is also higher
than the CB-based LSBs (140 mA h g−1). These results suggest
that the utilization of the Ni-PCTs additive in the sulfur
cathode can greatly facilitate the conversion of LiPS into Li2S.
The deposit morphology after potentiostatic discharge of the
electrode was confirmed by direct imaging using SEM. The
morphology of the working electrode from the CB-based LSBs
retained most of the features of the original CF with only
scattered Li2S islands (Figure S4b), whereas a thick and solid
Li2S2/Li2S layer was formed at the working electrode from the
Ni-PCTs-based LSBs (Figure S4a). This is consistent with the
potentiostatic deposition curve, as mentioned in Figure 4c,d.
These results conclusively demonstrate that the Ni-PCTs
additive yields faster polysulfide conversion kinetics, especially
for converting LiPS into Li2S. The strong catalytic activity of
the Ni-PCT host is mainly due to the nitrogen and nickel in
the phthalocyanine rings. This geometry utilizes the Ni
unoccupied DZ orbitals as electron superexchange highways
to promote sulfur redox, accelerating the reduction of lithium
polysulfide and promoting the formation of lithium sulfide.36
Hence, the Ni-PCTs additive can accelerate the reaction
kinetics of LiPS and improve the electrochemical performance
of LSBs.
To study the effect of the additive content on electro-
chemical performance, we conducted impedance tests on
batteries with different additive contents. In all tests, Li−S
batteries containing 10 wt % Ni-PCTs exhibit the best
performance (Figure S5). In order to study the electrochemical
performance and reaction kinetics of LSBs, CV and EIS were
performed on coin cells. The comparison of CV curves of Ni-
PCTs and CB-based LSBs in the voltage range of 1.8 and 2.8
V, at the scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, is presented in Figure 5a.
During the cathodic scan, the CV curves exhibit two
characteristic cathodic peaks around 2.3 and 2.0 V,
corresponding to the reduction of elemental sulfur to long-
chain polysulfide (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) and further reduction to
Li2S2 and Li2S, respectively.45 During the anodic scan, two
oxidation peaks in the voltage range of 2.3 to 2.4 V can be
ascribed to the conversion of Li2S into long-chain LiPS and
elemental sulfur, respectively.46 To figure out the catalytic
activity of Ni-PCTs toward polysulfide conversions, the Tafel
slopes were calculated based on CV curves. As shown in Figure
5b,c, the Tafel slopes at the reduction peak are 183.01 and
197.15 mV dec−1 for 10% Ni-PCTs and 0% Ni-PCT sulfur
cathodes, respectively. Meanwhile, those calculated from the
oxidation peak are 72.50 and 57.02 mV dec−1. Which
demonstrates that Ni-PCTs have better electrocatalytic
efficiency in promoting bilateral polysulfide conversions.47

Moreover, EIS was carried out to explore the influence of
the Ni-PCTs-based sulfur cathode on Li-ion diffusion and
charge transfer. Figure 5e,f shows the Nyquist curves of Ni-
PCTs and CB-based LSBs after the zeroth and 100th cycles.
The Nyquist plots are mainly composed of a semicircle and an
inclined line in the medium-to-high-frequency and low-
frequency regions, respectively, where the starting point of
the first semicircle represents the ohmic resistance, the radius
of the first semicircle indicates the charge transfer resistance,
and the inclined line refers to the resistance during mass
transfer. It is obvious that the ohmic resistance and charge-
transfer resistance of the 10% Ni-PCTs-based LSBs are
significantly lower than 0% Ni-PCTs-based LSBs both before
and after 100 cycles (Tables S1 and S2). Moreover, the mass
transfer resistance of 10% Ni-PCTs-based LSBs is significantly
higher than that of the CB-based LSBs after 100 cycles. The
low ohmic resistance and effective charge transfer of Ni-PCTs-
based LSBs can be ascribed to the catalytic activity of Ni-
PCTs, which enables the rapid transmission of ions and
electrons during the electrochemical process. The sulfonic acid
groups in the Ni-PCTs additive exhibit excellent ionic
conductivity, promoting Li-ion transport and accelerating
charge transfer within the sulfur cathode. The excellent
anchoring efficiency of polysulfides by the Ni-PCTs composite
reduces the deposition of solid electrolyte interphase films on
the anode, improving the mass transfer process of LSBs. The
low impedance and high mass transfer of Ni-PCT-based LSBs
also demonstrate the synergistic influence of Ni-PCT hosts on
the adsorption and catalytic conversion of LiPS.
Furthermore, the electrochemical characterization was
carried out to demonstrate the application prospects of Ni-
PCT additives in LSBs. During the prolonged cycling test
(Figure 5g), the initial capacity of the Ni-PCTs-based LSBs
was found to be 1282.98 mA h g−1 at 0.15 mA/cm2, and it
remained at 623.12 mA h g−1 after 400 charge/discharge
cycles, corresponding to a capacity attenuation of only 0.12%
per cycle and an average coulomb efficiency of 98.3%. In
contrast, the Ni-PCTs-free-based LSBs delivered a much lower
initial discharge capacity of only 993.37 mA h g−1, and the
fading capacity per cycle was 0.18%. Furthermore, the
discharge specific capacity decreased to 292.66 mA h g−1
after 400 cycles. Moreover, the galvanostatic charge/discharge
profiles of the 10% Ni-PCTs and 0% Ni-PCTs cathodes at
second cycles are presented in Figure 5h. The results reveal
that, compared with the charge/discharge profiles of the 0%
Ni-PCTs-based LSBs, the 10% Ni-PCTs-based LSBs exhibit
longer and flatter plateaus with lower voltage polarization,
indicating that the sulfur host material with 10% Ni-PCTs can
speed up the reaction kinetics of polysulfides and thereby
enhance the electrochemical performance of LSBs. This is
consistent with CV results, showing improved sulfur utilization
and redox kinetics.
Moreover, the galvanostatic charge/discharge curves of the
10% Ni-PCTs and 0% Ni-PCTs-based LSBs are presented in
Figure S7a,b to further understand the cyclic stability. It can be
clearly observed that the Ni-PCTs-based sulfur cathode can
still maintain characteristic electrochemical reactions and
display a flatter voltage platform after 400 cycles, indicating
that the redox reaction occurred with high electrochemical
stability. Moreover, each plateau capacity contributing to the
total discharge capacities of the 10% Ni-PCTs and 0% Ni-
PCTs-based LSBs are shown in Figure S7c,d, respectively. In
the case of Ni-PCTs-based electrodes, both high-plateau and
low-plateau contributions are more stable and higher than the
CB-based LSBs. These observations confirmed that Ni-PCTs
could suppress polysulfide dissolution. In general, the capacity
and cyclic stability of the Ni-PCTs-based electrode were higher
than those of the Ni-PCTs-free electrode.
To further characterize the significance of Ni-PCT additives
for utilization in high-energy-density LSBs, increasing the
loading of active sulfur in the cathode is necessary. Figure 5j
presents the discharge curve of Ni-PCTs-based LSBs with a
sulfur loading of 4.7 mg cm−2. One should note that at the
current rate of 0.4 mA cm−2, the initial capacity reached 899
mA h g−1, corresponding to a considerable areal capacity as
high as 4.2 mA h cm−2. Accordingly, the initial gravimetric
energy densities reach 260 W h kg−1 (without including the
electrolyte). After 100 cycles, a capacity of 535.5 mA h g−1
(areal capacity as high as 1.7 mA h cm−2) is still provided,
demonstrating the excellent potential of Ni-PCTs for real-
world application in energy storage systems. The average
coulomb efficiency during the charge/discharge process was
maintained at >97%. Besides, the charge/discharge curves
demonstrate that the Ni-PCTs additive can maintain the two-
plateau discharge profiles even with high sulfur loading (Figure
S8), indicating the excellent stability of the redox reactions.
Figure S9 presents the discharge curve of 10% Ni-PCTs-based
LSBs with higher sulfur mass loadings (>10 mg cm−2). The
additive amount of electrolyte is 10 μL mg−1 of sulfur. In the
first cycle, its discharge capacity can reach 8.64 mA h cm−2
(Figure S9a). Besides, the battery, with a high sulfur loading of
10.22 mg cm−2, its capacity can still reach 5.42 mA h cm−2
after 17 cycles. This demonstrates that the sulfur cathode with
Ni-PCTs additive exhibits excellent cyclic performance in high-
sulfur-loaded LSBs.
Lastly, Ni-PCTs-based full cells were assembled to
demonstrate the potential of a newly developed bifunctional
additive in practical energy storage devices, as shown in Figure
5i. The sulfur loading was 1.63 mg cm−2, and the capacity of
the Li anode was 13.6 mA h cm−2, corresponding to an
oversize of about 600%. At a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2,
the as-assembled full-cell provided a decent initial capacity of
1.95 mA h cm−2. After 100 cycles, the capacity of the full-cell
decreased to 1.03 mA h cm−2, and the average Coulombic
efficiency remained at >97%. Besides, Figure 5i shows an LED
powered by the as-assembled Ni-PCTs-based LSBs, indicating
the great potential of this material. The aforementioned
electrochemical measurements demonstrate that Ni-PCTs are
an effective and feasible additive for LSBs.

In summary, a CB cloth was decorated with Ni-PCTs using a
simple synthesis route and further applied as a bifunctional
additive for highly efficient sulfur hosts in LBSs. The as-
prepared Ni-PCTs additive enabled the effective trapping of
