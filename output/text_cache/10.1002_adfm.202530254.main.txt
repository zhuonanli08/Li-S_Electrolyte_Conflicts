ABSTRACT
To develop high-energy-dense lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs), improving cycle performance by suppressing electrolyte depletion
and stabilizing the anode–electrolyte interphase under lean-electrolyte conditions is essential. Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is regarded
as an effective additive that reduces electrolyte decomposition and stabilizes the interphases of the cathode and anode in LSBs,
however, it also increases cell overvoltage, limiting rate performance. Herein, by exploring various metal nitrates, we found that
cesium nitrate (CsNO3) improves both rate and cycle performance in lean electrolyte LSBs. Cs+, a soft acid, stabilizes polysulfides
(a soft base) in the form of solid precipitate, while NO3
−suppress electrolyte decomposition by forming a sulfate-based solid
electrolyte interphase. As a result, the CsNO3 additive enables stable LSB operation over 300 cycles at an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S)
ratio of 5 µL mgs
−1 and a current rate of 0.3C due to the suppressed electrolyte depletion. A pouch-type LSB with CsNO3 delivered an
energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 at a challenging E/S ratio of 2.0 µL mgs
−1. Our work inspires the development of additives with dual
functionalities—polysulfide stabilization and interphase stabilization to enable high-energy-density LSBs with extended lifespans.

Introduction
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered among the most
promising next-generation energy storage systems due to their
high theoretical gravimetric energy density exceeding 2600 Wh
kg−1 and the natural abundance of sulfur [1–4]. These features
make LSBs particularly attractive for urban air mobility (UAM)
applications, where lightweight energy storage is required. To
achieve practically viable energy densities, it is essential to
develop LSBs with a low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio (<5 µL
mgs
−1) [5, 6]. However, under such lean electrolyte conditions,
concentrated lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) inevitably form [7,
8], leading to challenges such as electrolyte gelation, which
hinders redox reactions at the cathode [9], lithium metal anode
corrosion, and non-uniform lithium-ion (Li+) flux that promotes
“dead lithium” growth and accelerates electrolyte decomposition
[10, 11]. Furthermore, the power performance of LSBs remains
insufficient for UAM applications due to the sluggish kinetics of
sulfur redox reaction (SRR) [12].
To facilitate SRR, significant efforts have been made to improve
both the sulfur cathode and the electrolyte. The use of catalysts
as electrode additives (e.g., metal oxides [13], nitrides [14], phos-
phides [15]) or of electrolyte additives that form in situ catalysts
(e.g., carbon/Ni compounds [16], calcium triflates [17]) acceler-
ates SRR by promoting the adsorption of polysulfide (PS) and
modulating their electronic structure. To address the issue of slow
SRR caused by the increased viscosity of the electrolyte medium
due to PS dissolution, low-salt-concentration electrolytes with
lower viscosity have been proposed [18]. Although these strategies

can mitigate LiPS dissolution and enhance the kinetics of SRR,
this effectiveness becomes less apparent over cycling because of
electrolyte decomposition at the lithium metal anode [19].
Recent studies have shown that electrolyte depletion—resulting
from continuous reactions between lithium metal and the
electrolyte—is the primary cause of LSB cell failure [20]. This
is supported by the finding that adding fresh electrolytes to a
degraded cell can restore stable cycling [21]. Consequently, form-
ing a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) at the lithium metal
anode is crucial for preventing electrolyte loss. In lithium metal
batteries, electrolytes with lower solvating power or higher salt
concentrations can improve Li metal stability, which is attributed
to an increase of anion coordination in the Li+ solvation shell and
consequent formation of an inorganic-rich SEI. In LSBs, weakly
solvating or high-concentration electrolytes reduce the solubility
of LiPSs, thereby suppressing the PS shuttle but leading to a
large overvoltage for SRR due to reduced Li+ conductivity arising
from the decreased solvating power [22]. Therefore, there is an
urgent need to develop advanced electrochemical frameworks
that enhance SRR while mitigating the electrolyte decomposition
at the lithium metal anode.
LiNO3 has been recognized as an indispensable electrolyte addi-
tive for stabilizing the lithium metal anode and suppressing
electrolyte decomposition, which is attributed to the formation
of an inorganic-rich SEI through its reductive decomposition or
its redox reaction with PSs [23]. Because of its higher electron
donicity compared to DME and FSI−, NO3
−preferentially coor-
dinates with Li+, thereby increasing the proportion of DME and
FSI−existing in a free state that is less prone to reduction [24,
25]. Moreover, it facilitates the formation of SEI components
with lower solubility such as Li2SO4, LiNxOy, Li2O at the Li
metal anode [26]. Since it is inevitably consumed during cycling,
increasing the concentration of LiNO3 can prolong cell lifespan
[27]. However, this also induces significant overpotentials that
compromise rate performance. To address the long-lasting trade-
off, we explored various alkali metal nitrates (KNO3, RbNO3, and
CsNO3) as NO3
−suppliers and introduced them to the cathode
instead of the electrolyte for high doses of NO3
−
. Including
additives directly in the cathode can be beneficial, as it allows
incorporation of amounts exceeding their solubility limits in the
electrolyte. We found that the Rb+ and Cs+ cations, owing to
their larger ionic radius and greater softness, stabilize soft-base
PS anions, promoting PS precipitate formation. This reduces the
concentration of dissolved PS in the electrolyte, thereby improv-
ing SRR kinetics and rate capability. Furthermore, the high
dose of NO3
−effectively suppresses electrolyte decomposition
and stabilizes the lithium metal electrode for extended cycling.
Using CsNO3, 0.8C operation (3.48 mA cm−2) was achievable
at an E/S ratio of 5 µL mgs
−1, which is a two-fold increase
over the control system. For CsNO3 additive, which shows the
most promising performance among the nitrates tested, cycling
stability is improved by two-fold at a lean electrolyte condition
(E/S = 5 µL mgs
−1) and at 0.3C due to the increased supply of
NO3
−. At higher NO3
−concentrations, electrolyte decomposition
was suppressed due to the modified solvation structure and the
formation of a stable SEI layer, such as Li2SO4, induced by
reactions between NO3
−and PS anions. A pouch-cell prototype
with CsNO3 additive demonstrated an energy density about
408 Wh kg−1. Our findings highlight the strong potential of
alkali metal nitrates—particularly CsNO3—for addressing the
dual challenges of sluggish SRR and poor cycling stability in lean
electrolyte LSBs.

Results
2.1
LiNO3 Additives as the Double-Edged Sword
Between Cycle and Rate Performance
To clarify that electrolyte depletion is the primary failure mecha-
nism of LSBs under lean electrolyte conditions, we tracked the
consumption of various constituents during cycling at E/S =
5 µL mgs
−1 using an electrolyte containing 1 m LiFSI and 0.3 m
LiNO3 (DME (1,2-dimethoxyethane):2MF (2-methyl furan): BTFE
(Bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) ether) = 7:2:1 v/v/v), which has moder-
ate Li+ solvating power adequate for LSB operation (Figure 1a,b).
Cycling tests were performed at 0.3C after three pre-cycles at
0.05C, 0.1C, 0.15C, and 0.2C for each current rate (see experi-
mental section). The coulombic efficiency was calculated as the
discharge capacity of the (n + 1)th cycle divided by the charge
capacity of the nth cycle. Consequently, the initial coulombic
efficiency may appear unstable due to changes in C-rate during
the pre-cycling process. The amount of DME was measured by
1H QNMR, FSI by 19F QNMR [21], and NO3
−by ion chromatog-
raphy (see experimental, Figures S1–S3). Approximately 60% of
NO3
−was consumed during the first 25 cycles, and noticeable
decomposition of FSI−was observed after 50 cycles. Despite
the relatively stable discharge capacity, the coulombic efficiency
gradually decreased beyond 50 cycles due to the reduced Li salt
concentration in the electrolyte. In contrary, the main solvent,
DME, did not show significant decomposition. Notably, NO3
−—
the key anion for the durability of LSB—exhibited the most severe
depletion. After 100 cycles, NO3
−fell below the detection limit
(<0.015 m).
We confirmed that this anion depletion leads to cell degradation
by cycling the cells until failure (∼150 cycles) and subsequently
replenishing them with either fresh electrolyte or a lithium
salt-free solvent mixture. The cell refilled with the lithium-salt-
containing electrolyte maintained stable operation for more than
70 additional cycles, whereas that refilled with the solvent-only
mixture experienced immediate overcharging from the second
cycle (Figure S4). Furthermore, cycling stability tests with vary-
ing LiNO3 concentrations showed a strong positive correlation
between LiNO3 content and cycling stability: 31, 90, and 155
cycles at 0.0, 0.1, and 0.3 m LiNO3, respectively (Figure 1c). These
findings corroborate previous reports highlighting the pivotal role
of NO3
−in extending the lifespan of LSB by stabilizing both the
cathode and anode interphase [28, 29].
Despite its interphase-stabilizing capabilities, NO3
−compromises
rate performance (Figure 1d,e). At E/S = 5 µL mgs
−1, we tested
the rate performance using the electrolytes with different LiNO3
concentrations and found that the threshold current density,
at which the lower voltage plateau was sustained, decreased
with LiNO3 concentration: 0.7C (3.05 mA cm−2), 0.6C (2.62 mA
cm−2), 0.4C (1.74 mA cm−2), and 0.2C (0.87 mA cm−2) for 0.0,
0.1, 0.3, and 0.6 m LiNO3, respectively (Figure 1d; Figure S5).
A comparison of voltage profiles at the 14th cycle under 0.3C
operation further revealed that higher LiNO3 concentrations

that is capable of improving the cycling stability and the rate
capability. We hypothesize that metal cations with strong binding

2.2
PS-Capturing Effect of Metal Cations With
Different Hardness
To probe the PS capturing ability of different metal cations,
we examined the PS solubility in electrolyte as a function of
the cation species. A 0.5 m Li2S4-electrolyte was prepared by
dissolving stoichiometric amounts of S8 and Li2S in the control
electrolyte, followed by the addition of 0.3 m MNO3 (M = Li+, K+,
Rb+, Cs+). After filtering out the precipitates, the PS concentra-
tion in the supernatant was quantified using UV–vis spectroscopy
(Figure 2a; Figure S9). Compared to the control (0.3 m LiNO3),
0.6 m LiNO3-containing electrolyte resulted in a slight increase
in LiPS solubility. However, the 0.3 m KNO3, RbNO3, and CsNO3-
containing electrolytes exhibited a diminished PS solubility, and
it decreases in the order of LiNO3> control > KNO3> RbNO3>
CsNO3. This suggests that the alkali metal cations softer than Li+
can capture solvated PS anions, reducing PS solubility.
The observation highlights the role of the hardness of the cation.
The hardness of metal cations decreases as the ionic radius
increases due to charge density decrease. To validate it, we
estimated Pearson’s absolute hardness values for the cations
(Li+, K+, Rb+, and Cs+) and anions (PS species, FSI−, NO3
−) by
using density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Figure 2b;
Figure S10) [32]. As expected, cation hardness decreases with
increasing ionic radius. On the other hand, PS anions (S8
2−,
S6
2−, S4
2−) exhibit lower hardness than FSI−and NO3
−, despite
being dianions. According to the Hard–Soft Acid–Base (HSAB)
principle, soft cations (Rb+, Cs+) can strongly associate with soft-
base PS anions, forming M–PS complexes and thereby lowering
the PS concentration [33, 34]. In contrast, the harder Li+ is less
effective in combining with PS anions than Rb+ or Cs+, resulting
in higher PS concentration. The correlation between the hardness
of the cation and PS solubility showed to be strong (Figure 2c).
Next, we examined the influence of M–PS complex formation
on Li+ transport by measuring the ionic conductivity of the
supernatants from 0.5 m Li2S4 electrolytes containing MNO3 with
different cation hardness (Figure 2d). As the cation hardness
decreased, the ionic conductivity of the supernatants increased.
In general, the ionic conductivity of LiPS-containing electrolyte
decreases with increasing LiPS solubility due to higher viscosity
[19]. The observed trends, consistent with the change in LiPS
solubility change, indicate that pairing NO3
−with a soft cation
suppresses LiPS dissolution and lowers viscosity, thereby enhanc-
ing the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte medium during Li–S
cell operation.
Increasing Li+ transport by reducing PS solubility can also
enhance the PS redox kinetics. To investigate the effect of metal
nitrates on PS redox kinetics, we assembled Li2S4 symmetric
cells using carbon paper electrodes loaded with MNO3 (M =
Li+, K+, Rb+, Cs+; 0.004 mmol cm−2) and 0.5 m Li2S4 electrolyte
[35]. The activation energy for the charge-transfer reaction was
determined from impedance measurements at different temper-
atures (Figure 2e; Figure S11). The activation energy decreased
in the order LiNO3 (0.76 kJ mol−1) > control (0.61 kJ mol−1) >
KNO3 (0.69 kJ mol−1) > RbNO3 (0.58 kJ mol−1) > CsNO3 (0.43 kJ
mol−1), indicating that soft acid cations accelerate charge-transfer
kinetics by suppressing PS solubility. Moreover, cations can also
affect the thermodynamic driving force for LiPS reduction. To
understand the effect of soft acid cations on PS reduction, we
calculated ΔG for the Li2S4 →Li2S2 conversion (Equation (1)),
which is considered the rate-limiting step in SRR, with different
metal cations using DFT calculations.
Li2S4(sol) + 2M+ + 2e−→Li2S2(s) + M2S2(s) (M = Li, K, Rb, and Cs) (1)
The value for ΔG shifted to a more negative value with decreasing
the hardness (ΔG = +2.803, −0.502, −0.561, and −1.153 kcal
mol−1 for K+, Rb+, and Cs+, respectively), showing that SRR is
thermodynamically more favored with softer cations (Figure 2f).
Despite the advantages of RbNO3 and CsNO3, their low solubility
in electrolyte (∼0.1 m) limits the applicable dose in LSBs (Figure
S12). To overcome this limitation, we incorporated these additives
into the sulfur cathode during fabrication using water as a solvent.
As exemplified by CsNO3, the addition of MNO3 to the cathode
slurry did not significantly alter the electrode morphology (Figure
S13). This strategy enables the gradual dissolution of the additives
into the electrolyte during cycling, within their solubility limit.
The molar ratio of incorporated MNO3 to sulfur was adjusted to
1.0:2.8, allowing the electrode to supply an additional 0.3 m NO3
−
under an E/S ratio of 5 µL mgs
−1. Assuming complete dissolution
of MNO3 during operation, the total NO3
−concentration in the
MNO3-containing cell would reach 0.6 m (0.3 m LiNO3 in the
electrolyte and 0.3 m MNO3 from the electrode).
To evaluate the impact of this strategy, we measured cell polar-
ization using GITT for pristine and MNO3-containing sulfur
cathodes. Similar to the case of MNO3-containing electrolytes,
both ohmic and activation polarizations decreased in the order:
LiNO3> KNO3> Control > RbNO3> CsNO3 (Figures S14 and
S15), reflecting enhanced PS-capturing ability of softer cations.
Especially, Li2S nucleation overpotential around DOD of 0.25
was decreased as the cation hardness decreased (Figure 2g).
These reduced polarizations translated into improved rate per-
formance, as evidenced by the maximum discharge current
density sustaining a stable lower-voltage plateau: LiNO3 (0.2C,
0.87 mA cm−2) < KNO3 (0.3C, 1.31 mA cm−2) < Control (0.4C,
1.74 mA cm−2) < RbNO3 (0.6C, 2.62 mA cm−2) < CsNO3 (0.8C,
3.48 mA cm−2) (Figure 2h; Figure S16). Among these, CsNO3
exhibited the best performance and was selected for further
studies.

2.4
Enhanced Cycling Stability and Li Metal
Electrode Reversibility With the
CsNO3-Incorporated Sulfur Cathode
The cycling stability of Li-S cells with the CsNO3-incorporated
electrode was evaluated at an E/S ratio of 5 µL mgs
−1 using a
sulfur loading of 2.5mgs cm−2. (Figure 4a). In the control cell
without CsNO3, the initial discharge capacity at 0.3C was 713
mAh gs
−1, and the discharge capacity remained above 570 mAh
gs
−1 (80% of the initial capacity) after 159 cycles. In contrast, the
CsNO3-containing cell maintained a discharge capacity above 570
mAh gs
−1 for 332 cycles, nearly doubling the cycling stability. The
apparent decrease in discharge capacity during the first ∼10 cycles
originates from the formation cycles conducted sequentially at
0.05 C, 0.1 C, and 0.2 C for three cycles each prior to cycling
at 0.3 C. Meanwhile, the discharge capacity of the control
electrode was slightly higher than that of the CsNO3-containing
electrode. This behavior can be attributed to differences in
LiPS solubility and the resulting electrode passivation behavior
under lean-electrolyte conditions [21, 24]. When LiPS solubility
is relatively high, LiPSs can dissolve out of the porous carbon
framework, allowing the active materials to redistribute over
a larger electrode surface area. This widespread redistribution
delays the passivation of conductive surfaces by insulating Li2S
deposits, leading to an apparent increase in discharge capacity.
In contrast, when LiPS solubility is suppressed by CsNO3, LiPS
redox reactions are largely confined to the immediate vicinity
of the porous carbon host. This spatial confinement leads to
more rapid surface passivation due to localized Li2S precipitation,
resulting in a reduced effective reaction area and consequently
a slightly lower discharge capacity under these specific coin-cell
conditions.
Consistent with this interpretation, the control electrode exhibits
a gradual increase in discharge capacity during cycling, accom-
panied by accelerated degradation due to intensified Li metal
corrosion caused by dissolved LiPSs. In contrast, such behavior is
much less pronounced in the CsNO3-containing cell. As a result,
despite the slightly lower discharge capacity, the introduction of
CsNO3 significantly enhances long-term cycling stability under
lean-electrolyte conditions. Furthermore, the cycle performance
achieved by introducing CsNO3 is superior to or comparable
with other Li–S systems tested under lean electrolyte conditions
(E/S < 5 µL mgs
−1) (Figure 4b) [5, 21, 22, 24, 25, 36–46]. The
beneficial effect of CsNO3 was also evident in a widely used
baseline electrolyte (1 m LiTFSI, 0.2 m LiNO3, DME/DOL 1:1 v/v)
(Figure S25).
To investigate the effect of CsNO3 on electrolyte stability, we ana-
lyzed the residual NO3
−in the electrolyte after 60 and 100 cycles
using the ion chromatography technique (Figure 4c; Figures S3
and S26). In the control cell, 68% of NO3
−was consumed within
60 cycles, and after 100 cycles, the NO3
−concentration fell below
the detection limit (< 0.015 m). This depletion correlated with the
abrupt capacity fade and the coulombic efficiency decay observed
at around 100 cycles. Conversely, in the CsNO3-containing cell,
the NO3−concentration remained at ∼0.32 m after 60 cycles and
at 0.26 m after 100 cycles. These results clearly demonstrate that
CsNO3 incorporated into the sulfur cathode serves as a reservoir
of NO3
−, sustaining a high NO3
−concentration in the electrolyte
phase. To measure the remaining amount of FSI−and DME
during operation, Q-NMR analysis was conducted (Figure 4d,e;
Figure S27). [21] In the control cell, 35% of FSI−decomposed
within 60 cycles, whereas in the CsNO3-containing cell, the
decomposition was reduced to less than 10%. DME depletion
also diminished in the presence of CsNO3. The Q-NMR analysis
highlights that the supply of CsNO3 mitigates the degradation of
electrolyte components.
DFT and Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations suggest that
the reduced electrolyte decomposition is attributed to changes
in Li+ solvation. Among the electrolyte components, NO3
−has
the largest binding energy to Li+ (Figure S28). Therefore, NO3
−
preferentially coordinates with Li+, increasing the fraction of
free DME and FSI−(Figure S29). Since the LUMO levels of
the uncoordinated species are higher than those of the Li+-
coordinated states (Figure S30), introducing additional NO3
−
via CsNO3 effectively suppresses the reductive decomposition
of DME and FSI−. Previous studies have also reported that
the incorporation of NO3
−modifies the Li+ solvation sheath,
thereby mitigating the decomposition of other electrolyte com-
ponents and improving cycle life [24]. This is consistent with the
experimental observation that increasing the LiNO3 concentra-
tion in the electrolyte delays degradation caused by electrolyte
decomposition (Figure 1c).
The reversibility of Li metal anode was evaluated using Li–
SUS cells with the cycled Li metal electrodes. After 60 cycles,
the stripping capacity was 6.40 mAh cm−2 for the con-
trol and 7.22 mAh cm−2 for the CsNO3 cell; at 100 cycles,
these values decreased to 3.70 and 5.85 mAh cm−2, respec-
tively (Figure 4f; Figure S31). FIB–SEM cross-sectional images
revealed a porous Li metal electrode morphology in the control
(Figure 4g) and a dense, uniform morphology in the CsNO3-
containing cell (Figure 4h). It was confirmed that control-
ling the solvation shell suppresses electrolyte decomposition
and enhances the retention of electrolyte components, lead-
ing to a denser Li metal morphology and improved Li metal
reversibility.
The chemical structure of SEI affected by the Li+ solvation shell,
strongly influences the reversibility of Li metal electrode. To
investigate SEI components, XPS analysis was conducted after
100 cycles (Figure S32). In the CsNO3-containing cell, the carbon
content, indicative of organic SEI from DME, was lower than in
the control, while the fluorine content from FSI−decomposition,
was slightly reduced. By contrast, the oxygen signal associ-
ated with NO3
−decomposition was higher, indicating greater
involvement of NO3
−in SEI formation. These observations are
in good agreement with the Q-NMR (Figure 4d,e) and ion
chromatography analysis (Figure 4c).

at 0.3C revealed distinct differences between the control and

2.5
Pouch-Cell Validation
To assess the practical applicability of the additive, we assembled
0.45 Ah-class pouch-type Li–S cells (12 single-cell stack, E/S
= 2.0 µL mgs
−1) and cycled them at a 0.05C discharge/charge
rate. At the early stage of operation, the control cells delivered
∼402 Wh kg−1, while the cell with the CsNO3-sulfur cathode
delivered ∼408 Wh kg−1 (based on the total weight of cell
components excluding housing). Although the total cell mass of
the CsNO3-containing cell was slightly higher (∼6%), stable oper-
ation achieved higher energy density, accompanied by a slight
increase in capacity and a reduction in discharge overpotential
(Figure 5a; Figure S34, Table S3). Notably, the discharge capacity
reduction observed for the CsNO3-incorporated electrode in coin-
cell configurations was not observed in the pouch cells. This
difference can be attributed to the much more stringent lean-
electrolyte condition (E/S = 2 µL mgs
−1), under which the limited
electrolyte volume suppresses LiPS dissolution and minimizes
redox reactions occurring on the out of the porous carbon
framework. Under such conditions, the kinetic enhancement of
Li2S formation induced by CsNO3 becomes the dominant factor
governing cell performance, outweighing any capacity variations
associated with changes in reaction surface area. Moreover, the
CsNO3-containing pouch cell exhibited significantly improved
cycling stability compared to the control (Figure 5b), clearly
demonstrating the efficacy of the CsNO3 additive at the full-cell
level.
3
Conclusion
In this study, we reexamined Li–S battery degradation under lean
electrolyte conditions and confirmed that electrolyte depletion is
the primary failure cause. While LiNO3 is crucial for prolonging
cycle life, its excessive use increases overpotential and reduces
rate capability—a classic double-edged sword. To address this
trade-off, we suggest a family of metal nitrate additives in the
electrode. Among them, CsNO3 is highly effective because Cs+
(soft cation) stabilizes PS anions (soft base), reducing PS solubil-
ity, increasing Li+ conductivity and boosting redox kinetics. The
beneficial effects of CsNO3 are consistently observed when it is
incorporated into the sulfur cathode, resulting in significantly
reduced polarization and approximately twofold improvement
in cycling stability compared to the additive-free control (over
330 cycles at E/S = 5 µL mgs
−1 and 0.3 C). In contrast to pre-
vious approaches that improve cycling stability by lowering the
solvating power of electrolytes-often at the expense of Li+ ionic
conductivity and rate performance-the present strategy achieves
PS immobilization without altering the intrinsic solvating power
of the electrolyte. This dual-functional approach, which com-
bines effective PS capture by Cs+ with suppression of electrolyte
decomposition by NO3
−, enables simultaneous enhancement
of rate capability and cycling stability through improved ionic
conductivity and interphasial kinetics. We anticipate that further
integration of such electrode-level additives with electrolytes
that possess both high solvating power and strong Li metal
stabilization will open new pathways toward Li–S batteries with
substantially higher energy and power densities.
