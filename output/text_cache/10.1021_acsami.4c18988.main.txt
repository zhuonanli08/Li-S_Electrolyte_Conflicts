ABSTRACT: The electrochemical reduction of sulfur has the capability to deliver high energy
density in lithium−sulfur batteries; however, its effectiveness is lessened by the requirement of
excessive electrolyte for high performance. The potential-limiting step of this reaction was
previously proposed to be the reduction of the final soluble intermediate Li2S4 to solid Li2S2/Li2S.
In this work, this step was further investigated. It was discovered that nominal Li2S4 solutions with
concentrations relevant to battery operation disproportionate into Li2S6 in the solution phase and
Li2S in the solid phase. Li2S6 is the more likely reactant for the observed electrochemical reduction,
which manifests two discernible steps. The first reduction step occurs at 2.2 V vs Li/Li+. Its specific
capacity is almost invariable with scan rate, but its relative contribution to the total reduction
capacity decreases with reactant concentration. The product is a semisoluble polysulfide such as
Li2S4 or Li2S3, which can be further reduced in the second step or oxidized at 2.3 V. The second
reduction step, with Li2S as the product, occurs at 2.1 V and is electrochemically more irreversible
than the first reduction. Its specific capacity declines significantly with increased scan rate but is less impacted by concentration.
KEYWORDS: Li−S batteries, potential-limiting step, lithium polysulfides, Li2S4 disproportionation, voltammetry
■INTRODUCTION
Electrochemical energy storage is critical to the leveraging of
renewable energy sources and proliferation of other emerging
technologies such as unmanned aerial vehicles, electric cars,
power tools, and wearable electronics.1−5 Lithium-ion batteries
have matured alongside these fields but now pose as a
bottleneck due to their limited gravimetric energy density.6,7
Alternative battery systems are being explored in order to
supply 500 Wh/kg or greater.8,9 The lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
system shows great potential based on the complete reduction
of S8 to Li2S, providing a theoretical specific energy of 2600
Wh/kg. This reaction process includes multiple lithium
polysulfide intermediates and phase changes.10,11 Optimization
and adherence to compatibility constraints have led most lab-
scale Li−S cells to comprise a high-surface-area carbon-
supported sulfur cathode, a low-donor-number ether-based
electrolyte, and a lithium metal anode.12,13 There have been
many studies of the reduction of sulfur to polysulfides in ether
electrolytes over the past few decades. The general consensus
is that S8 is reduced to Li2S8, which is then reduced to Li2S4 in
solution before being reduced to a mixture of solid Li2S2 and
Li2S (Figure 1).14−24 There is debate on the existence and role
of another intermediate, Li2S6, in this process.25−29
Maintaining polysulfides in solution has shown to improve
reaction kinetics and increase the discharging voltage in
comparison to solid-state conversions.30 Conversely, in order
to achieve high energy density and displace Li-ion batteries, the
amount of electrolyte must be minimized.31−34 As the
electrolyte volume is lowered to a point where not all of the
sulfur content is soluble in polysulfide form, the reduction
becomes especially challenging.35,36 This point is known as the
lean electrolyte condition.37 Our group previously observed
that the liquid-to-solid reduction of Li2S4 is the potential-
limiting step in the process under lean electrolyte conditions.38
At an electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio of 20 μL/mg, the
galvanostatic reduction of S8 to high-order polysulfides and
eventually Li2S2/Li2S followed the typical two-voltage-plateau
behavior. When this ratio was lowered to 6, the reduction

could not be performed at all and cells could deliver at most a

The reasoning for this larger barrier to Li2S4 reduction under
lean electrolyte conditions remained unclear considering that
other reduction and oxidation steps associated with the
charging and discharging of the Li−S cell were not as much
impacted. This motivated us to focus on this step specifically
and investigate the electrolyte composition and surface
reactions occurring at this point in the sulfur reduction
reaction at increasingly concentrated conditions.
Herein, the potential-limiting step of sulfur reduction is
further investigated utilizing nominal Li2S4 solutions. We
highlight the low solubility of Li2S4 in the mixed ether solvent
used for Li−S battery electrolyte. At concentrations higher
than 2.5 mM, Li2S4 spontaneously disproportionates into
soluble Li2S6 and solid Li2S, as revealed by induction coupled
plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). Consequently, Li2S6 is the major
polysulfide species to be reduced. In the reduction of Li2S6,
two distinguishable electrochemical steps are observed. The
first step results in a semisoluble lower-polysulfide product and
the second step generates solid Li2S. The first step has more
rapid kinetics and delivers a greater fraction of the total
reduction capacity at lower reactant concentration, while the
second is kinetically slower but less dependent on concen-
tration.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Preparation of Nominal Polysulfide Solutions and Cath-
olyte.
In

(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, TCI) was dried at 80 °C
for 12 h. 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL, Sigma-Aldrich) were dried with activated molecular
sieves. To produce nominal Li2S4 polysulfide solutions, S8 (99.9%,
Alfa Aesar) and Li2S (99%, Alfa Aesar) were combined in a molar
ratio of 3:1 in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of DOL and DME and allowed to
react for 2 days at 90 °C with vigorous stirring. Blank electrolye was
prepared by dissolving LiTFSI in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of DOL and
DME to reach a concentration of 1 M. Catholytes were produced
following the same procedure as the polysulfide solutions using blank
electrolyte instead of the solvent mixture. Catholytes and polysulfide
solutions were allowed to sit at room temperature for 1 day before
use. For electrochemical analysis, catholyte was extracted from the top
of the vials with any precipitate remaining on the bottom unless the
precipitate was explicitly included by agitating the mixture before-
hand. In additional cases as explicitly stated, filtration of polysulfide
solutions or catholyte prior to analyis was performed with a syringe
filter having a pore size of 20 nm (Whatman Anotop).
Cell Fabrication and Electrochemical Testing. All electro-
chemical tests were performed with CR-2032 type coin cells. Coin
cells were fabricated with a lithium chip anode (MSE Supplies), a
polypropylene separator (Celgard 3501), and a carbon fiber paper
(CFP) working electrode (Spectracarb 2050A-0850). A lithium chip
was pressed onto the anode case and 20 μL of catholyte was dropped
onto its surface. A separator, extra catholyte (20 μL), a CFP cathode,
a stainless steel spacer, a spring, and a cathode case were placed
sequentially on top of the lithium chip and then sealed with a
hydraulic press. Electrochemical tests were performed on a BioLogic
VMP3 potentiostat. Unless specified otherwise, the scan rate used for
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) or cyclic voltammetry (CV) was 0.2
mV/s.
Characterization. XPS was performed with a PHI Versaprobe II
Scanning XPS Microprobe with an aluminum Kα anode. Samples were
transferred directly into the instrument from the glovebox with an
airtight sample transfer vessel. Liquid nitrogen cooling of the sample
holder allowed the chamber to reach sufficient vacuum for analysis.
For XPS analysis of the solution phase of the nominal polysulfide
solutions, filtered polysulfide solution was drop-casted onto a silicon
wafer and dried in vacuum to form a red colored gel on the surface.
Precipitate was scraped from the filter surface and dried by vacuum
before XPS analysis. ICP-MS was performed on a PerkinElmer ICP-
MS Elan DRC-e with an oxygen reaction cell to facilitate sulfur
determination. For sulfur and lithium determination of the polysulfide
solutions (containing no additional Li salt), 1 mL of each polysulfide
solution and a Teflon-coated stir bar were sealed in a round-bottom
flask with a rubber septum in an argon filled glovebox. In a fume
hood, 15 mL of 35 wt % ammonium hydroxide aqueous solution was
added to the flask through a syringe. After 5 min the septum was
removed and 10 mL of 30 wt % hydrogen peroxide aqueous solution
was slowly added to the flask while stirring. The digestion mixture was
heated in an oil bath at 60 °C for 30 min at which point bubbles were
no longer forming and no solid could be observed. The cooled
solution was diluted to 250 mL with deionized water in a volumetric
flask. Prior to ICP-MS measurement, aliquots of the digested solution
were further diluted to an expected sulfur concentration of 50 ppb
with 1 wt % trace metal nitric acid aqueous solutions. Scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) were performed with a Hitachi SU8230 UHR microscope with
a BRUKER XFlash 5060FQ annular EDS detector.

To clearly identify the compositions of these nominal Li2S4

Comparison of S and Li contents through ICP-MS confirms
that as the nominal concentration of Li2S4 is increased, the
overall composition of the solution phase changes to resemble
Li2S6 stoichiometry (Figure 2c), consistent with the XPS
results. Only the lowest concentration 2.5 mM shows the
expected Li2S4 stoichiometry. This marks the solubility limit
for Li2S4 in DOL/DME and shows that Li2S6 is the more stable
polysulfide in solution at concentrations relevant to battery
operation. This aligns with a very recent study which found the
solubility of Li2S4 to be below 25 mM in DOL/DME and a
solution composition of Li2S5.5 for Li2Sx (3 ≤x ≤5) systems

complete Li2S composition of the precipitate.41 Our study,
which benefits from direct measurements of S and Li
concentrations and oxidation states in both phases, comple-

To study the electrochemical reduction behavior pertaining
to the potential-limiting step of Li−S batteries, we prepared
nominal Li2S4 catholytes. The base electrolyte was 1 M LiTFSI
in 1:1 DOL/DME and the nominal Li2S4 concentration was
adjusted. LSVs were performed with a CFP electrode against a
Li metal foil in a coin cell using the solution phase of the
catholyte. The voltammograms show two distinct reduction
peaks at approximately 2.1 and 1.95 V vs Li/Li+, referred to as
Peak 1 and Peak 2, respectively (Figure 3a). Peak 2 is absent at
low Li2S4 concentrations and becomes increasingly apparent
above 12.5 mM. The peak current change is quantified in
Figure 3b where Peak 2 increases more rapidly than Peak 1 as
the Li2S4 concentration increases. Above 25 mM, Peak 2
becomes dominant. We note that the current increases almost
linearly with the concentration for Peak 2 whereas Peak 1
manifests a change in slope between 12.5 and 25 mM, which
correlates with the dissolved polysulfide composition shifting
from Li2S4 to Li2S6.
We gain further insights by normalizing the LSV curves to
the sulfur mass in the expected Li2S4 preparation (i.e., the
sulfur element involved in the final solution phase and
precipitate). The mass specific current is by far the greatest
at the lowest concentration which is notably dominated by
Peak 1 (Figure 3c). As concentration is increased and Peak 2
becomes prevalent, there is less obvious change in specific
current with concentration. The areas beneath these curves
provide the specific capacity of these catholyte cells (Figure
3d). Full reduction of Li2S4 into Li2S should give a capacity of
1254 mAh/gsulfur. The achieved capacity of the 2.5 mM cell is
closest to this, in line with the observation that a larger E/S
ratio leads to higher specific capacity in Li−S cells.10 As
concentration is increased, the specific capacity continues to
fall. The areas beneath the LSV curves were partitioned at the
trough between the two peaks to provide an approximation of

The overall reduction path of Li2S6 varies with concentration,

The most common representation of sulfur lithiation under
potential scanning or galvanostatic conditions involves two
current peaks or voltage plateaus, corresponding to the
reduction of solid S8 to liquid polysulfides, and then the
reduction of liquid polysulfides to solid Li2S2/Li2S.42−45 The
additional polysulfide reduction peak seen here is not just a
result of beginning discharge with catholyte. It can be
replicated using a S8 cathode with blank electrolyte which
produces three distinct reduction peaks (Figure S5). The peak
at 2.34 V is attributed to the reduction of S8 and Li2S8 while
the other two are described throughout this manuscript.15,17
This additional reduction peak also occasionally appears in
other studies, whether or not it is addressed, likely due to
similar sulfur concentrations and experiment time scales
allowing it to be resolved from the larger nearby reduction
peak.14,25,46−48 The precipitate found in the concentrated
catholytes is also not the cause of either reduction peak. Three
nominal 250 mM Li2S4 catholytes with significant precipitate
formation were either mixed into suspension, or separated
from the precipitate by decanting or filtration. The CV profiles
of the three cells nearly trace over each other, meaning the
precipitate is not electrochemically active in the conditions
relevant to this study (Figure S6).
The solid products of the reduction peaks were charac-
terized through XPS. Cells of the 250 mM Li2S4 catholyte were
discharged by LSV to the maximum of Peak 1 or past Peak 2,
disassembled, washed with DME, and dried in vacuum. The S
2p XPS spectrum of the CFP electrode discharged to Peak 1
shows polysulfide remaining on the electrode surface with a
ratio of Li2S3.6 and notably no Li2S (Figure 4a). SEM and EDS
confirm the presence of small sulfur-containing particles and a
coating on the surface of the carbon fibers (Figures S7 and S8).
The S 2p XPS spectrum of a clean CFP electrode soaked in the
same catholyte shows that polysulfides can adsorb to the
surface and survive the DME washing step (Figure S9). A
combination of adsorbed polysulfides and solid Li2S2 would be
capable of producing the polysulfide ratio of Li2S3.6 observed
on the surface. This aligns with the coating (adsorbed
polysulfides) and particle (Li2S2) morphology observed by
SEM. Li2S2 formation in this step could also explain the large
capacity delivered in Peak 1 for the nominal Li2S4 catholytes
up to 25 mM without formation of Li2S (Figure 3d). For the
2.5 mM catholyte with actual Li2S4 stoichiometry of the
solution phase, the product of the first peak must be a
polysulfide shorter than Li2S4. The specific capacity of 509
mAh/g attributed to Peak 1 is near the 418 mAh/g required
for complete reduction to Li2S2. The 12.5 and 25 mM
catholytes (with solution phase between Li2S4 and Li2S6
stoichiometry and some precipitate) also deliver 282 and
190 mAh/g respectively in terms of nominal sulfur content. In
these cases, Peak 1 must therefore also involve reduction to an
average polysulfide chain shorter than Li2S4 as they deliver
more than the required 139 mAh/g to entirely reduce Li2S6 to
Li2S4. The fully discharged (after Peak 2) electrode surface
contains a mixture of Li2S and polysulfide of Li2S2.6
composition. The molar ratio of Li2S to Li2S2 is about 9, in
line with previous results from galvanostatic discharging.38
Clearly the second step involves the formation of Li2S which is
not present at Peak 1. SEM images of the fully discharged
electrode also show large flaky particles which completely
cover the surface of the carbon fibers (Figure S7).
A full CV of the 250 mM Li2S4 catholyte shows the two
reduction peaks described previously and a large oxidation
peak at about 2.5 V vs Li/Li+ which is ascribed to the oxidation
to S8 and designated as Peak 2′.46 On the return sweep of the
CV, the beginning of the S8 and Li2S8 reduction peak is
observed (Figure 4b). To assess the reversibility of reduction
Peak 1, the CV lower switching potential was moved up to
2.10 V (at the maximum of Peak 1), to avoid any contribution
of Peak 2. This results in an oxidation peak at approximately
2.25 V which is not present when the lower cutoff potential is
beyond the second reduction peak. This oxidation reaction is
therefore believed to involve the product of Peak 1 and is
designated Peak 1′. To prove that this oxidizable product is not
already present in the catholyte, a CV scan with the same
bounds as the blue curve in Figure 4b was performed but
beginning in the direction of oxidation (Figure S10). Here,
only Peak 2′ is seen. The lack of Peak 1′ in a typical CV cannot
be explained by the diffusion of the product away from the
electrode surface during the extra time required to sweep down
to 1.7 V and back up to 2.25 V. To prove this, the potential
was swept to the maximum of the first peak and then the cell
was allowed to rest at its open circuit voltage (OCV) for the
extra amount of time described in the previous sentence. The
potential was then scanned upward from the cell’s new OCV to
2.7 V. In this case, Peak 1′ is still present at 2.25 V, albeit
slightly smaller (Figure S11). Some of the reduction product of
Peak 1 either diffused away or underwent a chemical reaction
during this time but clearly the rest could still be oxidized
electrochemically. With the oxidation peak at 2.25 V attributed
to the products of reduction Peak 1, the products of reduction
Peak 2 along with other remaining polysulfides must be
oxidized around 2.5 V to S8. This further shows how
irreversible Peak 2 is, having a separation of about 550 mV
at this scan rate compared to Peak 1 with a separation of about
150 mV. When CV is performed on a 2.5 mM Li2S4 catholyte
beginning in the oxidative direction, an additional oxidation
peak also occurs at 2.36 V (Figure S12). An additional CV
cycle confirms the absence of this oxidation peak for a fully
reduced solution. This additional oxidization peak can be
assigned to the soluble Li2S4 initially present in the electrolyte.
This supports the assignment of Peak 1′ in Figure 4b to lower
order polysulfides. However, this peak is 110 mV more positive
than Peak 1′, implying these two oxidation events may not be
exactly the same.

of 1.7 V and the other was cut at 2.05 V to maximize Peak 1

The kinetics of the most concentrated 250 mM nominal
catholyte were further investigated through varied scan rates of
linear potential sweeps. Each curve belongs to a brand-new cell
to avoid the influence of irreversible deposition on the
electrode surfaces (Figure 5a). Peaks 1 and 2 are visible
across all scan rates with maxima at 2.16 and 2.09 V for the
lowest scan rate of 0.01 mV/s. Their heights relative to each
other vary with scan rate, similar to their dependence on Li2S4
concentration discussed earlier (Figure 3). The current
maximum of Peak 2 is greater than Peak 1 for scan rates
below 0.8 mV/s, however as the scan rate is increased, Peak 1
overcomes Peak 2 (Figure 5b). The slope for both peaks in the
logarithmic plot is greater than 0.5 so neither process is purely
diffusion controlled and adsorption must be involved in both
electrochemical steps.49 The specific capacities delivered by
each peak calculated in the same manner as Figure 3d are
shown in Figure 5c with the hatched areas designating peaks
not completed in the potential window. The specific capacity
of Peak 1 remains fairly constant at all measured scan rates at
around 70 mAh/g, showing that this step of the reaction
involves fast kinetics. On the contrary, Peak 2’s specific
capacity falls greatly as the scan rate is increased from 0.01
mV/s to 0.2 mV/s showing that it is kinetically hindered.
Finally, we present one reaction pathway that can possibly
explain the observations discussed above for the electro-
chemical reduction of a nominal Li2S4 catholyte of relatively
high concentration, whose solution phase is essentially Li2S6.
The expected reaction of Peak 1 involves Li2S6 which is
reduced to a semisoluble polysulfide such as Li2S4 or Li2S3 at
about 2.16 V vs Li/Li+. This reaction in the solution phase has
faster kinetics and must yield a polysulfide not present in a
great concentration prior to the reaction. This product can be
readily oxidized at 2.25 V as shown in Figure 4b or further
reduced at 2.09 V in the second reduction peak. Because of its
limited solubility, the formed Li2S4 or Li2S3 must either
precipitate or disproportionate. A possible outcome of this is
the disproportionation process to form Li2S6 and Li2S2. This
would align with the polysulfide composition below Li2S4
found on the surface after Peak 1. The Li2S6 that is re-formed
in solution can be reduced to Li2S at a potential below 2.09 V
seen in the second peak (Scheme 1).
■CONCLUSIONS
The findings of this work show that the most prevalent
dissolved lithium polysulfide species in relatively concentrated
nominal Li2S4 ethereal solutions is Li2S6. These polysulfides are
reduced through two distinct steps as revealed by LSV. The
first step is more dominant at lower concentrations and higher
scan rates. It generates a polysulfide product which can be
oxidized separately from the former Li2S6. The next step
involves conversion to Li2S with slower kinetics.
