ABSTRACT 
High-performance lithium-sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) batteries require an electrolyte that simultaneously delivers 
robust lithium metal stability and efficient bulk ionic transport. However, existing electrolyte engineering strategies generally 
improve interfacial stability at the expense of bulk transport properties. Herein, by introducing 1-methyl-1-propylpyrrolidinium 
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (PY13FSI), we achieved a reconstruction of the ionic network, coupled with the electrostatic shielding 
effect at the interface, breaking the traditional trade-off between interphase stability and bulk transport kinetics. While forming 
inorganic-rich LiF/Li3 N interphases on both the cathode and anode to block lithium polysulfide shuttling and homogenize lithium 
deposition, the electrolyte simultaneously delivers a high ionic conductivity approaching 10 mS cm− 1 and an impressive Li+ 
transference number of 0.71. Consequently, Li||SPAN cells deliver outstanding long-term cycling stability and rate capability, 
retaining 94.1% capacity retention after 1100 cycles at 1 C. Meanwhile, stable cycling performance is observed at 60◦C. 
Commercially viable full cells (with a thin Li anode and high-loading cathode) still demonstrate exceptional performance with 
a capacity retention of 90.8% after 200 cycles. This work presents an effective strategy to break the conventional trade-off 
between interphase stability and bulk ionic transport, offering fresh insights into the future design of advanced electrolytes for 
high-energy-density battery systems.

Introduction 
The high theoretical specific capacity, cost-effectiveness, and 
nontoxicity of sulfur endow lithium-sulfur (Li ─S) batteries as an 
attractive candidate for next-generation energy storage systems 
[ 1–4 ]. Nevertheless, the solid-liquid-solid multiphase conversion 
mechanism inherent to conventional elemental sulfur cathodes 
leads to the dissolution and migration of lithium polysulfide 
(LiPS) intermediates, which induces rapid capacity decay and 
low Coulombic efficiency [ 5, 6 ]. Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile 
(SPAN) has been recognized as a promising sulfur-based cath- 
ode material that effectively immobilizes sulfur species by

chemically anchoring them within a polyacrylonitrile skeleton 
[ 7 ]. In carbonate electrolytes, the SPAN cathode follows a com- 
plete solid-phase conversion mechanism, which entirely elimi- 
nates the detrimental polysulfide shuttle effect. This stems from 
the formation of a polycarbonate cathode-electrolyte interphase 
(CEI) layer on the SPAN surface, derived from the decompo- 
sition of cyclic carbonate solvents [ 8 ]. Nevertheless, carbonate 
electrolytes are incompatible with the lithium metal anode, 
compromising stable full-cell performance [ 9 ]. Conversely, when 
utilizing ether-based electrolytes that are compatible with lithium 
metal, the issue of polysulfide dissolution re-emerges [ 10, 11 ]. 
Consequently, the performance of Li||SPAN batteries is strongly 
tied to the electrolyte system. 
Recent progress has underscored the benefits of constructing 
electrolytes with an anion-rich solvation structure [ 12–15 ]. Rais- 
ing the lithium salt concentration or decreasing the solvent 
polarity can elevate the proportion of anions within the solvation 
sheath. This promotes the formation of robust, inorganic-rich 
interphases on both the cathode and anode. However, the massive 
aggregation of ion clusters inevitably leads to sluggish bulk 
transport kinetics. When paired with the poorly ionic-conductive 
SPAN cathode, this severe kinetic mismatch drastically exac- 
erbates electrochemical polarization, thereby severely compro- 
mising the overall energy density. Ionic liquids (ILs), a class of 
room-temperature molten salts consisting solely of cations and 
anions, offer exceptional chemical, electrochemical, and thermal 
stability [ 16, 17 ]. Their anions can participate in the lithium- 
ion coordination structure, thereby modulating the interfacial 
chemistry and facilitating the construction of inorganic-rich 
interphases. Concurrently, the bulky organic cations can form 
an electrostatic shielding layer on the lithium metal anode, 
inducing uniform lithium deposition [ 18, 19 ]. Furthermore, the 
bulky cations can effectively disrupt the bulky solvation clusters 
and competitively coordinate with Li+ , thereby significantly 
enhancing the ion transport kinetics [ 20 ]. Previous studies have 
demonstrated that CEI layers constructed from inorganic anions 
and organic cations can effectively inhibit polysulfide dissolution 
[ 21 ]. However, the high viscosity and limited ionic conductivity at 
room temperature of ILs constrain their practical application. 
In this study, we introduce the ionic liquid 1-methyl-1- 
propylpyrrolidinium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (PY13FSI) into 
the typical ether solvent (1,2-dimethoxyethane, DME). By 
optimizing their ratio, a balance between an anion-rich solvation 
structure and efficient ion transport was achieved, yielding 
an optimized electrolyte of 2 m LiFSI in a DME/PY13FSI (5:5, 
v/v) mixture (named D5-IL5, according to the volume change 
between DME and IL, the others are D3-IL7 and D7-IL3) for 
Li||SPAN batteries. Experimental characterization reveals that 
a solvation structure predominantly featuring contact ion 
pairs (CIPs), as distinguished from solvent-separated ion pairs 
(SSIPs) or aggregates (AGGs), is more conducive to promoting 
the electrochemical stability of the SPAN cathode and the 
highly reversible plating/stripping of the lithium metal anode. 
Benefiting from the synergistic effect between the bulky cations 
and the identical anions, a simultaneous enhancement in both 
interfacial stability and ion transport kinetics is successfully 
achieved (Which will be rigorously proven in the following 
chapters). 
2 
Results and Discussion 
2.1 
Electrolyte Design and Properties 
As a typical ether-based electrolyte solvent, DME is widely 
employed in lithium-sulfur batteries. The strong solvating abil- 
ity results in a solvent-dominated solvation structure for the 
DME-based electrolyte (DE) even at a salt concentration of 
2 m (Figure 1 ). Meanwhile, the low viscosity of DME endows 
the electrolyte with excellent ion transport kinetics, which is 
beneficial for improving the rate performance. However, this 
solvent-dominated solvation structure leads to severe issues. 
The plentiful free DME solvent accelerates the dissolution of 
polysulfide intermediates into the electrolyte (Figure S1 ), trig- 
gering a pronounced shuttle effect that causes irreversible active 
sulfur loss and deteriorates the cycling stability of the battery 
[ 13 ]. More importantly, a solvent-dominated solvation structure 
not only faces a high interfacial desolvation energy, leading 
to sluggish interfacial kinetics, but also fails to form stable 
electrode–electrolyte interphases [ 22 ]. Most current electrolyte 
modification strategies rely on high-concentration or localized 
high-concentration electrolytes to induce anion-rich solvation 
structures, thereby optimizing the interphase. However, these 
approaches inevitably compromise bulk ionic transport, lead- 
ing to sluggish ion-transfer kinetics [ 21 ]. In response to these 
limitations, we propose a hybrid electrolyte by combining DME 
with PY13FSI ionic liquid. Through precise ratio modulation, 
the system achieves an optimal equilibrium between an anion- 
rich solvation structure and favorable ion transport properties, 
yielding a tailored electrolyte for Li||SPAN cells. 
Ionic conductivity is a key macroscopic parameter reflecting 
ion transport kinetics. We measured the ionic conductivities of 
electrolytes with different solvent ratios at various temperatures, 
as shown in Figure 2a . As the proportion of PY13FSI in the 
electrolyte increases, the ionic conductivity gradually decreases 
and shows a positive correlation with temperature. Benefiting 
from the excellent electrochemical stability of PY13FSI, the 
electrochemical stability window of the electrolyte widens with 
increasing PY13FSI content (Figure S2 ). Raman spectra were 
employed to reveal solvation structure evolution with different 
electrolytes. The spectral region of 680–780 cm− 1 corresponding to 
the S-N-S stretching vibration of the FSI− anion was selected for 
analysis. As shown in Figure 2b,c , with the gradual substitution 
of DME by PY13FSI in the electrolyte, more anions coordinate 
with Li+ , and the enhanced Li+ -FSI− interaction leads to a 
blueshift of the S-N-S stretching mode of FSI [ 23 ]. Furthermore, 
a quantitative analysis of the solvation structures was conducted 
(Figure 2d ). The DE electrolyte mainly consists of solvent- 
separated ion pairs (SSIPs, 57.3%), which stems primarily from the 
strong solvating ability of DME solvent molecules [ 24 ]. Because 
strongly solvating DME exhibits a strong affinity for coordinating 
with LiPS, an SSIP-dominated solvation structure can effectively 
stabilize dissolved LiPS intermediates during subsequent charge–
discharge processes. This stabilization thermodynamically favors 
the persistence of soluble LiPS species following S ─S/C ─S bond 
cleavage, thereby promoting the LiPS shuttle effect, causing active 
sulfur loss and ultimately reducing sulfur utilization [ 25, 26 ].

2.2 
Compatibility Characterization of Electrolyte 
With Li Anode 
Since the desolvation behavior of Li+ is predominantly governed 
by ion-solvent interactions, we initially performed Nuclear Mag- 
netic Resonance (NMR) analysis to investigate the electronic 
environments of both the solvents and anions. As can be seen 
in Figure 3c , within the chemical shift range of 52.0–53.0 ppm, 
the 19 F NMR peak is assigned to the fluorine atoms within 
the sulfonyl group of the FSI− anion. As the proportion of 
PY13FSI in the electrolyte increases, the gradual downfield shift 
in the 19 F NMR indicates enhanced Li+ -FSI− coordination. The 
enhanced interaction reduces the electron cloud density around 
the FSI− anion, inducing a deshielding effect [ 23 ], consistent 
with the Raman spectra. Furthermore, changes in the solvent 
coordination environment were investigated by analyzing the 1 H 
NMR of DME in the different electrolytes. The 1 H NMR signals 
within the chemical shift range of 3.2–3.6 ppm are assigned to 
the hydrogen atoms of the ─CH2 and ─CH3 groups in the DME 
molecule, respectively (Figure 3b ). At the early stage of PY13FSI 
addition, the 1 H peaks show an upfield shift, which accounts 
for the weakened Li+ -DME coordination and the consequently 
enhanced shielding effect on the hydrogen atoms [ 27 ]. However, 
as the proportion of PY13FSI continues to increase, a downfield

favorable electrolyte/electrode affinity and sufficient penetration

The lithium metal anode and SPAN cathode were separately 
evaluated using different electrolytes to clarify the merits of the 
predominance of CIPs in the solvation shell for electrochemical 
performance. First, using the Aurbach method at 0.5 mA cm− 2 , 
we obtained Coulombic efficiency measurements (Figure 3g ). 
The results indicate that the D5-IL5 electrolyte, characterized 
by a CIP-dominated solvation structure, achieves the highest 
Coulombic efficiency (98.83%), significantly outperforming DE 
(97.57%), IL (94.57%), and the other hybrid electrolytes (97.99% 
and 98.15%). The excellent compatibility of the D5-IL5 electrolyte 
with the lithium metal anode was further validated by the 
long-term performance of Li-Cu half cells (Figure 3h ). Sub- 
sequently, Li||SPAN half cells were cycled at 1 C. After 600 
cycles, the cell containing the D5-IL5 electrolyte exhibited no 
capacity decay, demonstrating superior electrochemical stability 
(Figure S7 ). Consequently, among the tested formulations, D5- 
IL5 was identified as optimal for Li||SPAN batteries. For further 
investigation of the interfacial stability of the lithium metal anode 
in the DE and D5-IL5 electrolyte systems, cycling durability of 
Li symmetric cells was conducted at 1 mA cm− 2 and 1 mAh 
cm− 2 (Figure 3i ). Compared to DE, the D5-IL5 electrolyte exhibits 
a higher overpotential due to its lower ionic conductivity, yet 
it stabilizes lithium metal for over 1100 h of stable cycling. 
Morphology analysis of lithium deposition is also an essential 
method for evaluating the compatibility between lithium and 
the electrolyte. Lithium deposition experiments using copper foil 
were performed at 2 mA cm− 2 . The results (Figure 3j ) show that a 
loose structure with pronounced dendrites is observed for lithium 
deposited in the DE electrolyte. In contrast, the D5-IL5 electrolyte 
achieves more dense and uniform morphology without dendrite 
formation. To provide a more intuitive demonstration that bulky 
ionic liquid cations can equalize Li+ flux via the electrostatic 
shielding effect, in situ optical microscopy was employed to 
observe the temporal evolution of lithium deposition morphol- 
ogy. To amplify this dynamic effect, a high current density of 
2.5 mA cm− 2 was applied during deposition. As illustrated in 
Figure S8 , in the DE electrolyte under high current density, 
localized protrusions initially emerge due to surface defects and 
other non-uniformities. Subsequently, driven by the tip effect, 
the local electric field at these protrusions becomes significantly 
intensified, causing subsequent Li+ to preferentially deposit at 
these sites and ultimately leading to explosive dendrite growth. In 
contrast, in the D5-IL5 electrolyte, the bulky ionic liquid cations 
preferentially adsorb onto the tips of the protrusions to form a self- 
healing electrostatic shield. This layer effectively homogenizes 
the local Li+ flux and suppresses preferential Li+ deposition at 
the tips, ultimately yielding a smooth and uniform deposition 
morphology. This favorable structure helps mitigate parasitic 
reactions occurring at the lithium/electrolyte interface, thereby 
enhancing the safety of lithium metal batteries. 
2.3 
Electrochemical Characterization of 
Li||SPAN Cells 
To further evaluate the benefits of the CIP-dominated Li+ solva- 
tion structure for the performance of the SPAN cathode, Li||SPAN 
half cells were assembled and tested. The sulfur content in the 
SPAN we use is about 49% (Table S1 ). When cycled at 1 C at 
room temperature (Figure 4a ), the SPAN cathode in the DE 
electrolyte exhibits rapid capacity decay, retaining only 50.7% 
capacity retention after 1100 cycles. This rapid capacity fade stems 
from polysulfide dissolution and shuttling in the DE electrolyte, 
which also accounts for the anomalous Coulombic efficiency [ 29 ]. 
Conversely, the cell employing the D5-IL5 electrolyte demon- 
strates significantly enhanced cycling stability, maintaining a 
high reversible capacity of 1265.8 mAh g− 1 after 1100 cycles, which 
corresponds to 94.1% capacity retention. Furthermore, the D5-IL5 
electrolyte results in lower polarization (Figure 4b and Figure S9 ), 
indicating a more favorable interface for the SPAN cathode in this 
electrolyte system [ 30 ]. To further demonstrate the advantages 
of the D5-IL5 electrolyte over conventional sulfur cathode elec- 
trolytes, a Li||SPAN half-cell using 1 m LiFSI in DME/DOL was 
evaluated as a reference. As shown in Figure S10 , the DME/DOL- 
based electrolyte exhibits obvious polysulfides dissolution and 
inferior cycling stability, accompanied by an obvious solid–liquid 
conversion platform during cycling. Figure 4c compares the rate 
performance of the DE and D5-IL5 electrolytes. The D5-IL5 
electrolyte delivers high reversible capacities of 1540.2, 1424.9, 
1368.0, 1319.1, and 1225.5 mAh g− 1 at rates ranging from 0.1 
to 2 C. Upon switching the current density back to 0.2 C, a 
reversible capacity of 1484.73 mAh g− 1 is recovered, followed 
by 180 cycles with virtually no capacity loss. This excellent rate 
capability is attributed to the low desolvation activation energy 
associated with the CIP-dominated solvation structure of the D5- 
IL5 electrolyte. The charge–discharge profiles (Figure S11 ) reveal 
that the SPAN cathode in the DE electrolyte displays a distinct 
solid–liquid conversion voltage plateau, which leads to active 
sulfur loss and exacerbates parasitic reactions at the electrode–
electrolyte interface. Conversely, the reduced content of free DME 
solvent in the D5-IL5 electrolyte effectively suppresses polysulfide 
dissolution, thereby avoiding the solid–liquid conversion phe- 
nomenon. Concurrently, the stable interface structure formed 
in the D5-IL5 electrolyte further mitigates capacity fade and 
polarization.

the CIP-dominated D5-IL5 electrolyte maintains excellent cycling 
stability at 60◦C and preserves the solid–solid conversion reaction 
of SPAN (Figure 4d ). Although the elevated temperature helps 
alleviate the capacity loss of the DE electrolyte during initial 
cycling, its cycle life remains significantly limited due to the poly- 
sulfide shuttle effect (Figure S12 ) [ 31 ]. To evaluate the practicality 
of the electrolytes in high-energy-density batteries, the perfor- 
mance of Li||SPAN full cells was assessed with a high-loading 
SPAN cathode ( ∼ 5.4 mg cm− 2 ) combined with a lean electrolyte 
usage (E/SPAN ratio = 8.4 µL mgSPAN 
− 1 ) under practical operating 
conditions (Figure 4e ). The cells were subjected to two pre-cycling 
cycles at 0.1 C, followed by testing at 0.2 C. When using the 
D5-IL5 electrolyte, the cell exhibits stable cycling performance, 
delivering a discharge capacity of 1343.4 mAh g− 1 after 200 cycles. 
The corresponding capacity decay rate is as low as 0.047% per 
cycle. In contrast, the DE electrolyte exhibits rapid capacity decay 
due to aggravated polysulfide dissolution and interfacial parasitic 
reactions under high loading. Furthermore, an Ah-level Li||SPAN 
pouch cell was assembled to evaluate the scalability of the D5-IL5 
electrolyte. The pouch cell exhibited promising cycling, while the 
charge–discharge profiles maintained the characteristic solid–
solid conversion behavior, highlighting the practical feasibility 
of the proposed electrolyte system. (Figure 4f,g and Table S2 ). 
These results demonstrate that tailoring the solvation structure 
of the electrolyte and reducing the content of strongly solvating 
free solvents can significantly mitigate the polysulfide shuttle 
effect, thereby comprehensively enhancing the electrochemical 
behavior of Li||SPAN batteries.

2.5 
Characterization of Cycled Cathode and 
Anode 
As demonstrated above, the CIP-dominated electrolyte system, 
formulated with PY13FSI ionic liquid as a co-solvent, exhibits 
excellent interfacial compatibility with both the SPAN cathode 
and the lithium metal anode. Numerous studies have established 
that ion transport kinetics and the extent of detrimental para- 
sitic reactions are strongly dependent on the physicochemical 
properties of the electrode–electrolyte interface [ 37, 38 ]. There- 
fore, to elucidate the interfacial mechanisms underlying the 
performance disparities between the two electrolyte systems, the 
electrode–electrolyte interfaces formed in Li||SPAN batteries after 
50 cycles in the DE and D5-IL5 electrolytes were systematically 
characterized. 
First, the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer morphology 
on cycled SPAN cathodes was observed using transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM). As presented in Figure 6a,b , after 
cycling in the DE electrolyte, the SPAN surface developed a 
thick and uneven CEI layer, with thicknesses ranging from 4.1 
to 9.5 nm. In contrast, the CEI layer formed in the D5-IL5 
electrolyte system is considerably thinner and more homoge- 
neous, only 2.4 to 4.5 nm in thickness. This disparity is primarily 
ascribed to the D5-IL5 electrolyte promoting the formation of 
an inorganic-rich CEI. In contrast to their organic counterparts, 
these inorganic constituents possess superior electronic insula- 
tion properties. The dense interfacial layer constructed from these 
inorganic components effectively passivates the electrode sur- 
face, thereby suppressing continuous parasitic reactions between 
the electrolyte and the electrode thereby promoting interfacial 
stability [ 22 ]. 
To gain further insight into the mechanism of the CEI layer, 
x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to investigate 
the chemical constituents on the surface of SPAN cathodes 
cycled in different electrolytes (Figure 6c ). The C 1s spectra 
reveal that organic components originating from DME solvent 
decomposition, including C ─O bonds ( ∼ 286.5 eV) and C ═ O 
bonds ( ∼ 288.7 eV), are present on the SPAN cathode surfaces 
cycled in both the DE and D5-IL5 electrolytes [ 39 ]. Notably, 
the SPAN surface cycled in the D5-IL5 electrolyte exhibits an 
enhancement of Li ─C bonds ( ∼ 283.7 eV), which is conducive 
to enhancing the ionic conductivity of the interfacial layer [ 32 ]. 
F 1s spectral analysis indicates a higher abundance of LiF 
components detected in the D5-IL5 electrolyte system, a species 
derived from the preferential decomposition of FSI− anions. It 
has been previously confirmed that LiF within the CEI layer 
can effectively impede polysulfide diffusion, thereby suppressing 
the shuttle effect [ 29 ]. Furthermore, in the N 1s spectra, the 
D5-IL5 electrolyte system displays three characteristic peaks at 
∼ 402.5, 399.7, and 398.1 eV, corresponding to PY13+ cation, FSI− 
anion, and their decomposition product Li3 N, respectively [ 20 ]. 
In contrast, the -N- component attributable to PY13+ is not 
detected in the DE electrolyte, providing direct evidence for the 
participation of the ionic liquid’s cation in the process of CEI 
layer formation. Li3 N, as a key component of the interface layer, 
has garnered considerable attention owing to its excellent ionic 
conductivity and high mechanical strength. Its enrichment in the 
D5-IL5-derived CEI layer is conducive to the construction of an 
interface structure that combines rapid ionic transport capability 
and physical barrier function [ 40 ]. S 2p spectra further elucidate 
the effectiveness of the CEI layer in mitigating the loss of active 
material. Compared to the DE electrolyte, the intensity of sulfur 
species detected on the SPAN cathode surface is significantly 
lower in the D5-IL5 electrolyte, indicating that the dense and 
homogeneous CEI layer greatly reduces the loss of active sulfur 
components from SPAN [ 25 ]. 
The above results indicate that the solvation structure predomi- 
nantly composed of SSIPs triggers severe solvent decomposition 
at the cathode surface, failing to form an effective passivation 
layer. Conversely, the introduction of an ionic liquid comprising 
both cations and anions as a co-solvent in the D5-IL5 electrolyte 
engenders a unique inorganic-rich dual-ion-derived structure 
of the CEI layer formed at the interface. Benefiting from this 
structure, polysulfide diffusion is significantly suppressed, and 
interfacial parasitic reactions are substantially mitigated, ulti- 
mately leading to an effective enhancement in sulfur utilization. 
The morphology of the lithium metal anodes after cycling was 
analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As displayed 
in Figure 6d,e , cycling in the DE electrolyte results in a loose 
and porous structure with distinct cracks and corrosion on the 
lithium anode surface. This rough surface morphology signifi- 
cantly increases electrolyte contact, intensifying the occurrence 
of interfacial side reactions and the consumption of active 
lithium [ 41 ]. Conversely, the lithium anode cycled in the D5-IL5 
electrolyte displays a dense and smooth deposition morphology, 
without obvious dendrites or cracks. This optimized interfacial 
structure effectively alleviates side reactions by limiting lithium- 
electrolyte contact, and thus mitigates the irreversible loss of 
active lithium and prolongs the cycle life of the battery.

(SEI) layer directly governs the reactivity and ionic conductivity

3 
Conclusion 
In summary, by introducing an ionic liquid sharing the same 
anion as the lithium salt, this work couples ionic network 
reconstruction with interfacial electrostatic shielding. The bulky 
organic cations reconstruct the ionic network to establish a 
CIP-dominated solvation structure, allowing sufficient anion par- 
ticipation in solvation while preserving fast bulk ionic transport, 
as reflected by the high ionic conductivity and Li+ transference 
number. Simultaneously, their preferential adsorption on the 
lithium surface generates electrostatic shielding that homoge- 
nizes Li+ flux and stabilizes lithium deposition. Thus, Li||SPAN 
cells employing this optimized electrolyte deliver outstanding 
cycling stability with 94.1% of the initial capacity retained after 
1100 cycles at 1 C. Moreover, steady operation is maintained 
even at a high temperature of 60◦C. Notably, when paired with 
a 50 µm lithium foil and high-loading SPAN cathode ( ∼ 5.4 mg 
cm− 2 ), the full cell achieves stable operation over 200 cycles 
while maintaining 90.8% capacity retention. More importantly, 
beyond regulating lithium deposition, this solvation-structure 
engineering strategy can be extended to sulfur redox chemistry: 
the transition from SSIP to CIP reduces DME participation in 
the local solvation environment, thereby mitigating polysulfide 
dissolution, while the resulting anion-derived interphases further 
stabilize both electrodes. By simultaneously regulating bulk ion 
transport, interfacial stability, and sulfur conversion, this strat- 
egy decouples interphase stability from bulk transport kinetics, 
thereby overcoming the conventional trade-off. These findings 
establish a transferable electrolyte design principle that extends 
solvation and interfacial engineering from Li-metal deposition 
to sulfur conversion, offering a new paradigm for electrolyte 
engineering in high-energy-density battery systems.
