SUMMARY
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) battery affords an ultrahigh theoretical energy
density of 2,600 Wh kg1 as a promising next-generation energy
storage technique, whose actual performance is heavily dependent
on the sulfur redox kinetics. Lithium polysulﬁde intermediates play a
decisive role on the complex sulfur redox reactions but are unfortu-
nately insufﬁcient to afford rapid kinetics, rendering reduced spe-
ciﬁc capacity especially at high rates. Herein, a redox comediation
strategy is proposed and an organopolysulﬁde (di-t-butyl disulﬁde,
DtbDS) is introduced as a model comediator to accelerate the sulfur
redox kinetics. Concretely, DtbDS reacts with lithium polysulﬁdes
and provides a kinetically favorable pathway for polysulﬁde conver-
sion. Consequently, high-rate (4 C), high-sulfur-loading (5 mg cm2),
or lean-electrolyte (5.0 mL mgS
1) Li–S batteries are realized. The
redox comediation strategy demonstrates a novel approach to pro-
mote the sulfur redox kinetics for high-performance Li–S batteries
and inspires more redox comediators represented by DtbDS.
INTRODUCTION
The pursuit for high-energy-density battery systems drives grand exploration of
emerging chemistries beyond the current energy storage technique based on
lithium-ion intercalation.1–4 In the past decades, lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries have
attracted extensive attention due to their ultrahigh theoretical speciﬁc energy
(2,600 Wh kg1) and advantages of earth abundance, low cost, and environmental
friendliness of cathode sulfur.5,6 Generally, the sulfur redox reactions refer to the
conversion from solid sulfur to dissolved lithium polysulﬁde (Li2Sn, 3 < n % 8) to solid
lithium sulﬁde (Li2S). In the Li–S system operating upon multi-phase and multi-elec-
tron conversion, the deposition of Li2S from dissolved lithium polysulﬁdes accounts
for at least three-quarters of the theoretical capacity, i.e., 1,254 mAh gS
1.7,8 How-
ever, the sluggish deposition kinetics and ﬁlm-like two-dimensional (2D) growth of
insulating Li2S in routine ether electrolyte often result in severe irreversible loss of
active sulfur and rapid capacity decay.9–11 Therefore, regulating the deposition
and dissolution behavior of Li2S is critical to achieve high-capacity, high-rate, and
long-cycling Li–S batteries especially under practical conditions.
Generally, the deposition/dissolution behavior of Li2S is regulated by soluble lithium
polysulﬁdes. Serving as intrinsic redox mediator (RM), lithium polysulﬁdes partici-
pate in the chemical comproportionation and disproportionation processes (refer-
ring to the redox mediation process) parallel to their electrochemical reduction
and oxidation and directly determine the deposition rate and growth dimension
of Li2S on conductive substrates. However, the intrinsic RM of lithium polysulﬁde
is restricted to the dynamic battery evolution regarding its species and concentra-
tion and therefore fails to execute the mediated process timely and thoroughly,

To overcome the above issues, numerous heterogeneous mediators, such as
metal,16 metal oxides,17 sulﬁdes,18 and nitrides,19,20 have been designed and intro-
duced to facilitate the deposition/dissolution of Li2S via chemisorption and/or sur-
face catalysis of lithium polysulﬁdes.21–27 Despite the enhanced battery perfor-
mance realized by heterogeneous mediators, such a mediation process heavily
relies on the conductive surface28 that the heterogeneous mediators are easily
covered by insulating Li2S and lose their functions especially at higher depth of dis-
chage.29 Meanwhile, modulating the solvation structure of the polysulﬁde anions
would relieve the dependence of the regulation strategy on conductive sur-
faces.30,31 Typically, high-dimensional Li2S deposition on limited surfaces can be
achieved via tuning the composition and concentration of electrolyte solvent32–34
and/or supporting salts.35 Nevertheless, such solution is merely effective at low cur-
rent densities or high temperature required for smooth lithium polysulﬁde conver-
sions, reducing the application potentials of practical Li–S batteries.36–39
Considering the insufﬁcient redox mediation capability of the intrinsic lithium poly-
sulﬁde RMs, it is a rational strategy to introduce extrinsic RMs to assist and
strengthen the homogeneous redox mediation process.40,41 Taking the Li2S depo-
sition process as an example, the extrinsic RM chemically reduces lithium polysul-
ﬁdes to Li2S in the electrolyte to maximize the usage of favorable surfaces and in-
crease the deposition dimensions of Li2S with higher discharge speciﬁc capacity,
while the oxidized RM+ is further reduced on conductive substrates with inherent ki-
netic advantages.42 Given an appropriate thermodynamic potential and favorable
kinetics, the above-mentioned redox mediation scheme is generally effective for
other polysulﬁde-involving processes, including Li2S dissolution and elemental sul-
fur formation.43 However, in essence, the redox mediation capability of the intrinsic
lithium polysulﬁde RMs remains unchanged and poor. Furthermore, according to
the thermodynamic potential sequence, an extrinsic RM can only regulate a single
electrochemical process where other processes at different potentials remain kinet-
ically sluggish. In addition, extrinsic RMs often result in severe corrosion of lithium
anode through their own shuttling.42,44 Therefore, to promote the kinetics of the
intrinsic lithium polysulﬁde RMs for higher redox mediation capability is highly re-
garded to essentially facilitate the multi-phase and multi-electron sulfur redox reac-
tions and improve the Li–S battery performances with higher speciﬁc capacity, better
rate responses, and longer cycling lifespan.
Inspired by the mechanism of coenzyme in bio-systems, a redox comediation strat-
egy is proposed to accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics for high-performance Li–S
batteries in this contribution. Compared with coenzyme that interacts with corre-
sponding enzyme to promote the catalytic activity, a redox comediator (coRM)
chemically modulates the intrinsic lithium polysulﬁde RMs to promote their redox
mediation capability with faster kinetics (Figure 1B). As a proof of concept, an orga-
nopolysulﬁde, di-t-butyl disulﬁde (DtbDS), is introduced as a coRM. Concretely,
chemically reactive DtbDS spontaneously reacts with lithium polysulﬁdes to
generate lithium t-butyl polysulﬁde (LitbPS) species through the reaction indicated

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of the Reactions between Lithium Polysulﬁde RMs and
DtbDS CoRM
Generally, coRMs are expected to interact with the intrinsic RMs to promote their
functions of accelerating sulfur redox kinetics. Therefore, the reaction of DtbDS
with lithium polysulﬁdes is investigated in the ﬁrst place.
DtbDS and Li2S6 with equimolar amount were mixed in 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-dime-
thoxyethane (v:v = 1/1) and characterized comprehensively to gain insights into their
reactions. Li2S6 was selected because it is recognized as the primary RM among
various lithium polysulﬁdes in working Li–S batteries. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis)
spectroscopy was ﬁrst conducted to evaluate the lithium polysulﬁdes qualitatively.45

(Figure 2A). After the reaction, the peaks of the Li2S6 + DtbDS mixture appeared at
l = 332 nm, which corresponds to (CH3)3C–Sn–Li assigned to the LitbPS mentioned
above. Similarly, the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of 7Li also conﬁrm
the exchange reaction. The chemical signal of Li2S6 downshifted from 3.3 to 2.8
ppm due to the chemical reaction between DtbDS and Li2S6 (Figure 2B).

Kinetic Evaluation of DtbDS-Comediated Polysulﬁde Conversion
Considering organopolysulﬁdes can serve as cathode active materials in several
cases, electrochemical evaluation on DtbDS itself was performed to exclude its
contribution to capacity as an interference. Cells with lithium metal as anode and car-
bon paper (CP) as cathode were assembled, and the electrolyte contained addi-
tional 100 mmol L1 DtbDS corresponding to a theoretical discharge capacity of
about 4% of sulfur in each cell (calculated as sulfur loading of 1.2 mgS cm2). Within
the electrochemical window of routine Li–S cells (1.7–2.8 V versus Li/Li+), no redox
current peak belonging to DtbDS appeared in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve
(Figure S2A). In addition, the discharge capacity of DtbDS was close to 1 mAh gS
–
1 throughout 200 cycles, which can be virtually negligible as expected (Figure S2B).
Therefore, DtbDS alone possesses no electrochemical reactivity, and any increase in
capacity observed in the following experiments is mainly attributed to the redox co-
mediation mechanism endowed by DtbDS.
The as-formed lithium t-butyl polysulﬁdes by the introduction of DtbDS coRM are ex-
pected to exhibit enhanced redox mediation capability than the original lithium pol-
ysulﬁde RMs. In order to investigate the sulfur redox kinetics with or without DtbDS,
potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT) was conducted to determine
the actual potential and capacity of Li2S deposition and dissolution. Generally, the
PITT discharge curves of Li2S8 catholyte can be divided into two regions.53,54 The
ﬁrst is the liquid-liquid conversion region, corresponding to the electrochemical
conversion from long-chain polysulﬁdes to short-chain polysulﬁdes, while the sec-
ond region undergoes the liquid-solid conversion recognized as the deposition of
Li2S. The ﬁrst process continued until 2.04 V for the pristine Li2S8 catholyte but
was advanced to 2.06 V with DtbDS (Figure 3A), implying the presence of LitbPS
altered the electrochemical equilibrium and promoted the kinetics of the liquid-solid
conversion. Speciﬁcally, in the ﬁrst region, the initial current responses increased by
14%–34% at each potentiostatic step with DtbDS added, suggesting improved
liquid-liquid conversion kinetics. In the second region, the Li2S deposition peak
time for the cell with DtbDS was earlier corresponding to faster deposition rate.29
The Li2S deposition capacity was also higher with DtbDS (270 mAh gS

exhibited a total discharge capacity of 600 mAh gS

The above discrepant kinetic evaluations evidently prove DtbDS as an effective
coRM to facilitate the kinetics for Li2S deposition, Li2S dissolution, and the conver-
sion between lithium polysulﬁdes. First-principle calculations further reveal the
possible reason why LitbPSs possess better kinetics than intrinsic lithium polysulﬁdes
at the atomic level. Figure S3A exhibits the optimized geometrical structures of
different LiPSs and LitbPSs molecules. The presence of tert-butyl induces a charge
redistribution on the sulfur atoms and changes the lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energies of
the original LiPSs (Figure S3B). The change of the LUMO and HOMO energy levels
leads to different redox capabilities of LitbPSs and accordingly improves the redox
kinetics.

To reveal the growth behavior following Li2S nucleation, the cells were ceased with

in 3D growth with higher discharge capacity.10 Under the redox comediation of
DtbDS, it is speculated that the chemical Li2S deposition pathway is facilitated by
modifying the pristine lithium polysulﬁdes into lithium t-butyl polysulﬁdes, thereby
realizing the 3D deposition of Li2S and achieving higher capacity.
Electrochemical Performance of Li–S Batteries
The introduction of DtbDS beneﬁts the sulfur redox kinetics in model experiments,
where the actual performances in Li–S batteries were further examined to evaluate
the redox comediation strategy. Figure 5A exhibits the rate performances. At low
current densities, the Li–S cells with DtbDS comediation provided the speciﬁc capac-
ity of 1,136, 939, 863, and 810 mAh gS
1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C (1 C = 1,675 mA
gS
1), respectively, 14% higher than those of the cells that rely only on intrinsic poly-
sulﬁde mediation. Such higher speciﬁc capacities are probably brought by the 3D
deposition of Li2S endowed by DtbDS. Under higher discharge rates of 2 C and 4
C, the Li–S cells with DtbDS maintained high discharge capacity of 741 and 566
mAh gS
1, respectively, whereas the discharge capacity was dramatically decreased
in absent of DtbDS. Recalling the previous kinetic studies, DtbDS signiﬁcantly
improved the sulfur redox kinetics regarding both liquid-liquid and liquid-solid con-
version, thus enabling Li–S batteries with high-rate performances. DtbDS also en-
dowed Li–S batteries with satisfying cycling stability. The DtbDS-comediated cell
still maintained a discharge capacity of approximately 600 mAh gS
1 after 300 cycles
at 0.5 C (Figure S4). In contrast, the blank cell suffered violently ﬂuctuating
Coulombic efﬁciency after only about 180 cycles, probably due to anode corrosion
and failure caused by too much lithium polysulﬁdes that could not be converted on
the cathode timely. The capacity decay rate of the Li–S cells with DtbDS at 4 C
achieved 0.25% per cycle throughout 150 cycles (Figure 5C). However, the capacity
of the Li–S cells lacking DtbDS decreased to almost 0 mAh gS
1 after 8 cycles due to
the sluggish kinetics.

mAh gS
1 and that of the liquid-solid conversion also enhanced by 32% with the
DtbDS comediator.
To validate the practicality of DtbDS in Li–S batteries, a 1.5 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell
with DtbDS was assembled (Figure 5E), wherein the sulfur loading of the cathode
was 4.9 mg cm2 (single side) and the E/S ratio was controlled at 4.0 mL mgS
1.
The DtbDS-comediated pouch cell delivered a discharge capacity of 1,313 mAh
gS
1 at 0.05 C corresponding to an energy density of 300 Wh kg1 at the cell level
and maintained stable cycling for 40 cycles with a speciﬁc capacity of 1,135 mAh
gS
1 (256 Wh kg1). It is worth noting that the above energy density was calculated
based on the mass of the entire pouch cell, including current collectors, package,
and tabs. Through fabricating large-capacity pouch cells and reducing the mass of
the non-active components, the actual energy density can be further improved.
Overall, the reaction of DtbDS comediator and the intrinsic polysulﬁde redox medi-
ators generated kinetically favorable lithium t-butyl polysulﬁdes and further
improved the performance of Li–S batteries. Similar organopolysulﬁdes are ex-
pected to exhibit resemble comediating effects, as long as these molecules: (1)
are able to chemically react with lithium polysulﬁdes, (2) afford reactants with higher
redox mediation capability to accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics, and (3) are chem-
ically stable but not severely corrosive to metallic lithium. To further reveal the crite-
rions for effective redox comediators, additional organodisulﬁdes were evaluated in
Li–S cells, including diphenyl disulﬁde (DPDS), diallyl disulﬁde (DADS), and dipropyl
disulﬁde (DprDS). Among the above organodisulﬁdes, the cells with DPDS only pro-
vided a discharge capacity of less than 400 mAh g1 (Figure S7A); the DADS-cell pro-
vided higher discharge capacity in the initial cycles, but a rapid decline in capacity
and a sharp ﬂuctuation in Coulombic efﬁciency occurred afterward (Figure S7B);
DprDS also exhibited a rapid capacity and Coulombic efﬁciency degradation after
50 cycles. First-principles calculations were further conducted to reveal the differ-
ences among the above-mentioned organodisulﬁdes at the atomic level. Figure S8A
shows the optimized geometry of the four organodisulﬁdes and the corresponding
charge distribution on the sulfur atoms based on natural bond orbital (NBO) ana-
lyses. The LUMO energies of DPDS, DADS, DprDS, and DtbDS increase in turn (Fig-
ure S8B), which indicates that DtbDS has the best reductive stability among all while
the others are more reactive with reductive components in batteries, such as Li metal
anode. The high reactivity of DPDS, DADS, and DprDS with reductive components in
the battery results in a quick degradation of the battery capacity and cycling stability.
Therefore, only DtbDS can persistently comediate the long-term electrochemical
cycling processes to afford desirable battery performances.
In summary, a redox comediation strategy is proposed to assist the intrinsic lithium
polysulﬁde redox mediators with higher capability to regulate the sulfur redox ki-
netics. DtbDS, an organopolysulﬁde as comediator, spontaneously reacts with
lithium polysulﬁdes to generate lithium t-butyl polysulﬁdes as active redox media-
tors with kinetic merits. The DtbDS comediation accelerates the deposition and
dissolution of Li2S and alters the Li2S deposition mode from 2D to 3D with higher
discharge capacity. Consequently, the Li–S batteries with DtbDS as comediator
exhibit superb rate performances of 566 mAh gS

Kinetic Evaluation
Liquid-Liquid Conversion Kinetics
Li2S6 symmetric cells were assembled to verify the role of the redox comediators in
the liquid-liquid conversion process. Both electrodes of the symmetric cell adopted
CP as the conductive substrate and PP as the separator. CP was cut into disks with a
diameter of 13.0 mm and then ultrasonically washed with ethanol and dried at 60C.
The electrolyte was 20 mL 0.5 mol L1 [S] of Li2S6 in DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1) with or
without 50 mmol L1 DtbDS. The electrolyte also contained 1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI.
CV and EIS were conducted on a Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation. CV
was performed at a scan rate of 50 mV s1 between 0.8 and 0.8 V. EIS was per-
formed using the potentiostatic mode at the open-circuit potential. A sinusoidal
voltage with an amplitude of 10 mV and a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz
were applied.
Liquid-Solid and Solid-Liquid Conversion Kinetics
Li | CP/polysulﬁde cells were assembled using CP as the cathode conductive
substrate, Li foil (16 mm in diameter) as the anode, and PP as the separator.
The catholyte was 40 mL 0.5 mol L1 [S] Li2S8 and 1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI in DOL/
DME (v/v = 1:1) with or without 50 mmol L1 DtbDS. The anode side was added
with 5 mL blank electrolyte (1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI in DOL/DME [v/v = 1:1]) to wet
the anode surface. The total sulfur content in each cell was controlled to be
around 0.64 mg. PITT was conducted on the Solartron 1470E electrochemical
workstation. For discharge PITT evaluation, the above cells were ﬁrst rested at
the open-circuit voltage for 2.0 h, and the cutoff condition for a given step
was the current being less than 30 mA or longer than 4.0 h. For charge PITT
evaluation, the above cells were ﬁrst galvanostatically discharged to 1.7 V at
0.05 C and then potentiostatically discharged at 1.7 V for 4 h to ensure that
all the electroactive polysulﬁdes were converted into Li2S. The subsequent steps
are similar to the discharge PITT, except that the set voltages were gradually
increased.
Li2S Nucleation and Growth Tracking
Similar to the cells for PITT evaluation, Li | CP/polysulﬁde cells were assembled
to observe the nucleation and growth of Li2S. For the cells without DtbDS, the
catholyte was consisted of 40 mL 0.5 mol L1 [S] of Li2S6 in DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1)
with 1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI. For the cells with DtbDS, DtbDS was added to the cath-
olyte with a concentration of 50 mmol L1. All the cells were discharged at a
current of 0.1 mA. In order to observe the nucleation and subsequent growth
behavior of Li2S, the cells were terminated when the discharge capacity reached
0.07 and 0.14 mAh (or the voltage dropped to 1.7 V in cases without DtbDS),
respectively. After that, the cells were immediately disassembled, and the CPs
were taken out and cleaned with DME for scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
observation. The SEM was conducted on JSM 7401F (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) oper-
ated at 3.0 kV.
Electrochemical Performance of DtbDS
Li | CP/DtbDS cells were assembled using CP as the cathode conductive sub-

0.1 C (0.2 mA cm2).

Li | S Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Performance Evaluation
Carbon-sulfur composites were prepared by ball-milling. Concretely, 30 mg
multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and 70 mg S were ball-milled with PVDF
binder at a weight ratio of 9:1 in NMP solvent to form a slurry. The slurry was
then coated on Al foils, dried at 60C for 12.0 h, and punched into small
disks with a diameter of 13 mm as sulfur cathodes. The areal sulfur loading was
1.2 or 5 mgS cm2 for the routine and harsh condition measurements. The sulfur con-
tent in the composite was 70 wt % (63 wt % within the cathode). Electrochemical
measurements were conducted using standard 2025-coin-type cells, where a piece
of Li foil with a diameter of 16.0 mm was used as the anode and a piece of PP mem-
brane served as the separator. 1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI and 2 wt % LiNO3 dissolved in
DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1) solvents was adopted as the control electrolyte. Extra DtbDS
was added to the above blank electrolyte with the concentration of 100 mmol L1.
The E/S ratio was 15.6 and 5 mLelectrolyte/mgS for cells with 1.2 and 5 mgS cm2,
respectively. Similarly, for the electrochemical tests of other organosulﬁdes, the
electrolyte consisted of 100 mmol L1 organosulﬁdes (DPDS, DADS, and DprDS,
respectively), 1.0 mol L1 LiTFSI, and 2 wt % LiNO3 in DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1). The
E/S ratio of cells with organosulﬁdes was 15.6 mLelectrolyte/mgS with sulfur loading
of 1.2 mgS cm2. The cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–2.8 V on the
Neware multichannel battery cycler under different C-rates (1 C = 1,672 mA g1).
For Li–S pouch cell evaluation, the well-mixed slurry was double-side coated on Al
foil and then dried at 60C for 12 h. Then, 4 3 7 cm2 electrodes with a single-side
areal sulfur loading of 4.9 mg cm2 were cut. The Li anode sheets were also cut
with the same size as sulfur cathode. The thickness of the Li anode was 50 mm (single
side) on Cu current collector. To assemble a pouch cell, the cathode and anode elec-
trodes were stacked layer by layer with PP separators. All the electrodes were
controlled in parallel connection through an electrode lug terminal. The composi-
tion of the electrolyte was the same as the coin cells, and the E/S ratio was controlled
as 4 mLelectrolyte/mgS. The cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–2.55 V on
the Neware multichannel battery cycler under 0.05 C (1 C = 1,672 mA g1).
Theoretical Simulations
The ﬁrst-principle calculations were conducted in Gaussian (G09)56 program with
Becke’s three-parameter hybrid method using the Lee-Yang-Parr correlation func-
tional (B3LYP) at 6-311G++G(d, p) level.57 The universal solvation model of SMD58
was used to describe the solvation effects in DME environment. The dielectric con-
stant and solvent radius were set to be 7.2 and 4.19 A˚ , respectively. Frequency anal-
ysis was performed to ensure the ground state of optimized lithium polysulﬁdes clus-
ters. Both linear and cyclic structures were considered for the clusters and the latter
has a lower total energy than the former.
