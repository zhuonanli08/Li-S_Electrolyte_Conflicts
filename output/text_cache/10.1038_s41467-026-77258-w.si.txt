Supplementary Fig. 2 Compatibility of Se2Cl2 with the Designed-Cl electrolyte. 
 
Supplementary Note 1 To evaluate the stability of Se2Cl2 under our electrolyte conditions, we 
examined the compatibility between Se2Cl2 and the Designed-Cl electrolyte. After liquid Se2Cl2 
was introduced into the Designed-Cl electrolyte, the reddish-brown Se2Cl2 phase remained at 
the bottom of the electrolyte because of the density difference, with only slight diffusion 
observed over time. This result suggests that Se2Cl2 exhibits reasonable compatibility and 
stability in the Designed-Cl electrolyte environment.

Supplementary Note 2 To evaluate the adsorption ability of KJ toward Se2Cl2, 50 μL of Se2Cl2 
was added into 8 mL of electrolyte and uniformly dispersed by shaking (Supplementary Fig. 4). 
A carbon-cloth electrode coated with KJ was then immersed in this solution. After 0.5 h, the 
solution color became noticeably lighter, and after 12−24 h, the solution became almost 
colorless, indicating that Se2Cl2 was effectively adsorbed by the KJ host. In addition, SEM/EDS 
characterization of the charged positive electrode after 50 cycles shows that the Se/KJ 
composite remains uniformly distributed on the carbon cloth, while the presence of both Se and 
Cl signals confirms that the charged products are still retained on the positive electrode side 
after extended cycling (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicate that the synergistic effects 
of the electrolyte and positive electrode host help confine Se2Cl2 and suppress its diffusion 
during electrochemical cycling.

Supplementary Fig. 7 XPS spectra of the Li metal negative electrode collected at the charged 
state after 10 cycles in the Li||Se cell. 
 
Supplementary Note 3 The stability of the Li metal negative electrode is critical for the overall 
performance of Li||Se cells, especially in halide-rich electrolytes. To address this concern, we 
have performed post-cycling characterization of the Li negative electrode after 10 cycles in the 
Li||Se cell, including SEM, EDS, and XPS analyses. 
After disassembling the cycled cell, the Li foil was washed with DMI and dried before 
characterization. The Li surface showed only slight darkening, and no reddish-brown Se2Cl2-
like species were observed (Supplementary Fig. 6a). SEM/EDS analysis of the region facing 
the Se positive electrode revealed the presence of N, O, F, and S species, which can be attributed 
to the SEI components derived from the decomposition of LiTFSI, LiFSI, and LiNO3. 
Importantly, no detectable Se or Cl signals were observed in the EDS mappings, indicating that 
the charged Se2Cl2-related species do not significantly accumulate on the Li metal negative 
electrode (Supplementary Fig. 6b). 
Consistently, the XPS spectra of the cycled Li negative electrode show no obvious Cl 2p 
or Se 3p signals, further confirming the absence of detectable Se2Cl2-derived species on the Li 
surface (Supplementary Fig. 7). These results suggest that the halide-rich electrolyte does not 
induce severe Se/Cl-containing deposition or passivation on the Li metal negative electrode 
under our testing conditions, thereby supporting the observed cycling stability of the Li||Se cells.

Supplementary Note 5 The Li||Se cell cycled within 1.5−2.3 V exhibits two distinct discharge 
plateaus at 1.98 and 1.78 V in the first cycle, delivering an initial discharge capacity of 646.6 
mAh g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 33a). However, the capacity decays rapidly during subsequent 
cycling, retaining only 152.3 mAh g−1 after 80 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 
23.6%. Compared with the cell involving only the high-valent Se0/Se+ reaction (Supplementary 
Fig. 32), the cell limited to the low-valent Se2−/Se0 reaction shows much faster capacity fading, 
indicating that the capacity decay of the three-electron Li||Se system mainly originates from the 
low-valent Se2−/Se0 redox process.

Supplementary Note 6 The ionic conductivities of electrolytes with different chloride contents 
are 1.92 mS cm−1 (0.5 M), 1.82 mS cm−1 (1.0 M), and 1.60 mS cm−1 (2.0 M), respectively. With 
increasing chloride content, the electrolyte viscosity gradually increases, leading to a decrease 
in ionic conductivity. However, the electrochemical performance of the Se positive electrode 
does not show a corresponding decrease with reduced ionic conductivity. This result suggests 
that the electrochemical behavior of the Se positive electrode is governed by the combined 
effects of chloride-ion content and ionic conductivity, rather than ionic conductivity alone.

Supplementary Note 7 The three-electrode Li||Se cells exhibit charge-discharge profiles 
similar to those observed in the corresponding two-electrode cells, confirming that the main Se

S24 
redox processes are preserved in the three-electrode configuration. Moreover, the cells deliver 
even higher reversible discharge capacities, further supporting the effective utilization of Se 
under the designed electrolyte conditions. More importantly, the corresponding dQ/dV curves 
clearly display the characteristic redox peaks associated with both the Se2−/Se0 and Se0/Se+ 
reactions. In the Designed-Cl electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 10), the Se0/Se+ redox process 
occurs at approximately 2.57 V, while in the Designed-Br electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 38), 
this process shifts slightly to approximately 2.60 V. This trend is consistent with the calculated 
results, which suggest that Br-assisted Se0/Se+ conversion occurs at a slightly higher potential 
than the Cl-assisted process. In addition, the potential separation of the Se0/Se+ redox couple is 
larger in the Designed-Br electrolyte than in the Designed-Cl electrolyte, indicating relatively 
higher polarization for Br-assisted high-valent Se conversion.

Supplementary Note 8 S powder was ball-milled with KJ and thermally infiltrated to ensure 
uniform distribution within the porous carbon scaffold, thereby enhancing electronic 
percolation and providing physical confinement for soluble intermediates. In the Designed-Cl 
electrolyte, the Li||S cell exhibits a distinct oxidation feature at 2.71 V during the first anodic 
scan (Supplementary Fig. 41a), which is attributed to the activation of a high-valent S0/S+ 
process. This couple becomes reversible in subsequent cycles, appearing at 2.74/2.59 V. 
Consistently, during the first charge at 400 mA g⁻1 (Supplementary Fig. 41b), an evident S0/S+ 
contribution is observed, delivering a first-charge capacity of 318 mAh g⁻1. With high-valent 
S0/S+ reaction activated, the Li||S cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1575 mAh g⁻1, 
but decays to 593 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 41d), which can be caused by 
the higher solubility and migration propensity of sulfur-containing intermediates.

Supplementary Fig. 43 CV curves, Charge-discharge profiles, and the corresponding dQ/dV

Supplementary Note 9 To further validate the broader applicability of our electrolyte design, 
we performed a benchmark comparison of Li||SeS2 cells in the Baseline electrolyte 
(Supplementary Figs. 43a-c) and in the Designed-Cl electrolyte (Supplementary Figs. 43d-f). 
In the Baseline electrolyte, Li||SeS2 cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1035 mAh g⁻1 
(92% of the theoretical value, corresponding to an ~5.5-electron reaction) and fades to 482 mAh 
g⁻1 after 100 cycles. By contrast, in the Designed-Cl electrolyte, the Li||SeS2 cell displays richer 
redox features (Supplementary Fig. 43f), indicative of an expanded redox manifold in which 
S0/S+ and Se0/Se+ processes are simultaneously activated. The initial discharge capacity is 1175 
mAh g⁻1 and, after an activation period, reaches a maximum of 1726 mAh g⁻1 (corresponding 
to an ~9.2-electron reaction). Notably, this cell retains a reversible capacity of 1070 mAh g⁻1 
after 200 cycles, demonstrating substantially improved capacity retention under the halide-rich 
electrolyte environment.
