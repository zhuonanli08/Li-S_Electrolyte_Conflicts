Rechargeable lithium–sulfur batteries offer a promising route to 
high-energy storage using abundant sulfur, but their energy density 
is constrained by low operating voltage, sluggish redox kinetics and 
polysulfide shuttling. Raising sulfur to higher oxidation states could 
increase cell voltage, yet reversible high-valence sulfur chemistry in 
lithium batteries remains difficult because chloride species bind Li+ and 
halogen-mediated reactions consume electrolyte. Here we show that a 
free-chloride-rich ionic liquid electrolyte enables a Li||S2Cl2 chemistry that 
addresses these limitations by reversibly converting Li2S to S2Cl2 through 
a three-electron sulfur redox process. The electrolyte functions as an ionic 
mediator with only a minor capacity contribution. This chemistry increases 
the average operating voltage from 2.05 to 2.54 V at 25 °C and 0.2C, raises 
sulfur-specific capacity by 58% and delivers an electrode-level specific 
energy above 1,700 Wh kg−1 with cycling over 100 cycles.
Rechargeable lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising alternatives 
to lithium-ion batteries because of their high theoretical energy density 
and low cost1,2. Advances in conductive and catalytic hosts, electrolytes 
and separators have improved sulfur utilization and mitigated polysulfide 
shuttling3–6. Nevertheless, major challenges remain, particularly the 
low redox potential of the S2−/S0 conversion and the severe electrolyte 
requirements associated with practical high-areal-capacity operation7,8. 
Because sulfur electrodes operate at only ~2.0 V, reaching energy densi-
ties comparable to ~4.0 V layered oxides requires roughly twice the areal 
capacity9. This demand requires electrolytes that sustain high critical 
current densities while suppressing lithium dendrites and polysulfide 
shuttling8, beyond current Li–S electrolytes10–13. In addition, highly coor-
dinated electrolytes that form stable LiF-rich interphases often restrict 
ion mobility and increase viscosity14,15. Thus, no existing electrolyte simul-
taneously satisfies high ionic conductivity, high critical current density 
and low polysulfide solubility for practical low-voltage Li–S batteries.
Exploring high-valence sulfur redox chemistry provides a prom-
ising strategy to address these limitations by increasing the sulfur 
redox potential beyond the conventional S2−/S0 couple, thereby 
simultaneously improving operating voltage and energy density while 
alleviating the stringent electrolyte requirements associated with 
low-voltage sulfur chemistry16. Sulfur can access multiple valence 
states ranging from −2 to +6. Oxidation to higher valence states such 
as S1+ therefore offers an attractive route towards higher-energy sulfur 
batteries. Stabilizing oxidized sulfur species requires highly elec-
tronegative elements, limiting viable candidates primarily to oxygen 
and halogens. Sulfur–oxygen compounds such as SO2 have long been 
used in primary batteries17, while reversible sulfur oxidation has been 
explored in open Li–SO2 systems18. However, these chemistries either 
exhibit limited reversibility or fail to fully utilize sulfur through mul-
tielectron transfer. Halogen stabilization provides a more attractive 
route for high-valence sulfur chemistry. Heavy halogens such as bro-
mine and iodine reduce the capacity advantage of sulfur due to their 
large atomic masses, whereas sulfur fluorides are gaseous at room 
temperature and therefore unsuitable for rechargeable systems19,20. 
By contrast, chlorine offers a balanced combination of high theoretical 
capacity and favourable liquid-phase properties among the halogens 
through disulfur dichloride (S2Cl2)21,22.

Among the systems evaluated, sulfur is particularly attractive 
because of its high theoretical capacity, low cost and natural abun-

tronegative elements (X = F, O, Cl, Br, I, S or N; Fig. 1b) that can support

Among them, chlorine is especially promising because it enables

Overall, the Li||S2Cl2 chemistry involves a net three-electron 
redox process per sulfur atom, in contrast to the conventional 
two-electron sulfur redox chemistry in Li–S batteries. Based on the 
combined mass of the positive-electrode material (S2Cl2) and lithium 
metal negative electrode (Li), the S2Cl2 chemistry theoretically deliv-
ers a specific capacity of ~910 mAh g−1 at an average operating volt-
age of ~2.65 V versus Li+/Li, corresponding to a theoretical specific 
energy of ~2,400 Wh kg−1. Figure 1c schematically illustrates the 
sulfur redox pathway from S2− to S1+ through the reversible forma-
tion of S2Cl2.
Despite these advantages, achieving reversible S2−/S1+ conver-
sion in lithium batteries remains challenging. Strong Li+–Cl− inter-
actions suppress chloride reactivity, making previously reported 
chlorine-mediated sulfur oxidation mechanisms difficult to translate 
into Li battery systems23,24. In addition, most reported Cl−-assisted 
sulfur oxidation chemistries operate through dual-ion mechanisms in 
which the electrolyte directly participates in charge storage, thereby 
lowering the practical energy density and causing sustained electrolyte 
consumption25. Developing a lithium-compatible chlorine-mediated 
sulfur chemistry that enables reversible sulfur oxidation without con-
tinuous electrolyte depletion therefore remains a critical challenge for 
high-energy-density Li–S batteries.
Here, we demonstrate a reversible S2−/S1+ conversion in lithium 
batteries through a Li||S2Cl2 chemistry in which S and LiCl serve as the 
initial positive-electrode materials. A free-Cl−-rich ionic liquid electro-
lyte enables sulfur oxidation to S1+ at ~3.4 V through dynamic chloride 
exchange between the electrolyte and the electrode, resulting in an 
overall reversible three-electron sulfur redox process. The Li||S2Cl2 
chemistry increases the average operating voltage by 24% compared 
with conventional Li–S chemistry and delivers a reversible capacity 
of ~1,838 mAh gS
−1 at an average discharge voltage of ~2.54 V. The elec-
trode achieves a specific energy of 1,700 Wh kg−1 and retains 78% of its 
capacity after 100 cycles. These results establish a chlorine-mediated 
strategy for enabling multielectron sulfur redox chemistry towards 
high-specific-energy Li–S batteries.

Enabling reversible sulfur oxidation through free 
chloride ions
To enable the oxidation of sulfur from S0 to S1+ through chloride-mediated 
reactions, LiCl was mixed with sulfur–carbon composite (S–C, 80 wt% 
sulfur; Supplementary Fig. 1) at a S:Cl molar ratio of 1:1 to prepare the 
positive electrode. The electrochemical performance of Li||S–LiCl cells 
was first evaluated using a conventional Li–S electrolyte consisting 
of 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) dissolved 
in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME). As shown in Fig. 2a, the voltage profiles of the S–LiCl electrode 
were nearly identical to those of the S–C electrode, indicating that little 
or no S0/S1+ conversion occurred in the DOL–DME electrolyte. Scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) analyses revealed poor physical contact between S and LiCl within 
the electrode (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, the low solubility of 
LiCl in the DOL–DME electrolyte limited interactions between sulfur 
and LiCl primarily to solid–solid interfaces.
An effective strategy to improve the reaction kinetics is to shift 
from a solid–solid to a solid–liquid reaction by designing electrolytes 
capable of partially dissolving LiCl. Among the strongly solvating 
solvents evaluated, N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMA) exhibited the high-
est LiCl solubility (>2 M; Supplementary Fig. 3). Consequently, 0.9 M 
LiTFSI in DMA was selected as the base electrolyte, with 0.1 M lithium 
difluoro(oxalato)borate (LiDFOB) added to suppress polysulfide shut-
tling28. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV; Supplementary Fig. 4) showed 
that the electrolyte remained stable up to ~4.5 V, confirming its suitabil-
ity for Li||S2Cl2 chemistry. As shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5, 
the first discharge profile of the S–LiCl electrode in the DMA electrolyte 
exhibited a polysulfide plateau at ~2.6 V, slightly higher than the ~2.3 V 
plateau observed in DOL–DME electrolytes. This shift is attributed to 
alternative reaction pathways involving S3
•− species29. The discharge 
capacity was comparable to that obtained in DOL–DME electrolytes. 
The charge profile also displayed a plateau near ~2.6 V. However, only 
a small oxidation plateau (~10 mAh gS
−1) appeared at the expected S0/
S1+ oxidation voltage of ~3.4 V (Fig. 2b, inset), indicating limited S0/S1+ 
conversion under these conditions.
To further enhance Cl− activity, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium chlo-
ride (EMIMCl, ~1 M solubility in DMA) was introduced to increase the 
Cl− concentration, while the lithium salt concentration was reduced 
to maintain a total solute concentration of 1.0 M. The LSV results con-
firmed that the DMA electrolyte containing 1 M EMIMCl remained 
electrochemically stable up to ~4.0 V (Supplementary Fig. 4). Figure 2c 
shows the charge profiles of fully lithiated electrodes (Li2S + LiCl) 
in DMA electrolytes containing (1 − x) M LiTFSI and x M EMIMCl at 
different x values. At x = 0.1 and 0.3, the charge profiles resembled 
those without EMIMCl, exhibiting only a weak plateau near 3.4 V. As x 
increased to 0.5 and 0.7, the oxidation plateau became progressively 
more pronounced and extended. At x = 0.9, a well-defined plateau 
appeared, suggesting that a sufficiently high concentration of active 
Cl− species facilitates sulfur oxidation.
To gain insight into the electrolyte design, molecular dynamics 
(MD) simulations were performed on the (1 − x) M LiTFSI + x M EMIMCl 
DMA electrolytes to examine the local solvation structure of Cl−. Analy-
sis of Li–Cl radial distribution functions and coordination numbers 
(Supplementary Fig. 6a) showed that at x = 0.1, nearly all Cl− ions were 
coordinated with Li+ ions (Fig. 2d), indicating little or no free Cl− spe-
cies (Fig. 2e). As the Cl/Li ratio increased, free Cl− species gradually 
emerged. At x = 0.5, uncoordinated Cl− species became detectable, 
whereas at x = 0.9, ~80% of Cl− ions were uncoordinated, generating a 
Cl−-rich environment in which each Li+ was coordinated by ~2.5 Cl− ions 
and ~1.3 DMA molecules (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Nuclear magnetic 
resonance (NMR) and Raman measurements further supported the 
MD results (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). As the Cl/Li ratio increased, 
the 7Li NMR signal shifted to higher ppm values, indicating progressive 
replacement of DMA molecules by Cl− within the Li+ solvation shell. 
Consistently, the characteristic Raman peak associated with Li+–DMA 
coordination nearly disappeared at x = 0.9. A higher Cl/Li ratio there-
fore favours the release of Cl− from Li+ coordination, promoting sulfur 
oxidation (Fig. 2f).
Although the strategy above facilitates sulfur oxidation dur-
ing charging, the high-voltage plateau observed in the x = 0.9 elec-
trolyte exhibits limited reversibility during discharge. As shown in 
Supplementary Fig. 9, only partial reduction from S0 to S2− is observed, 
whereas reduction of S2Cl2 is absent. This behaviour suggests that 
the generated S2Cl2 readily dissolves in DMA and subsequently shut-
tles between the positive electrode and lithium metal. The extended 
charge plateau in the x = 0.9 electrolyte, which exceeds the theoretical 
capacity, further supports this interpretation. To suppress this shuttle 
effect, the highly solvating DMA electrolyte needs to be replaced with 
a phase-separating electrolyte designed to immobilize S2Cl2 through 
liquid–liquid immiscibility and thereby prevent its diffusion to the 
lithium metal.
LiTFSI and EMIMCl can directly form a binary ionic liquid without 
DMA at high EMIMCl content30. (1 − x) LiTFSI–x EMIMCl mixtures were 
therefore prepared. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, mixtures with 
x = 0.7 and 0.9 remained liquid at 45 °C. Differential scanning calorim-
etry (DSC) profiles of pure EMIMCl and the 0.1 LiTFSI–0.9 EMIMCl 
mixture are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11. Mixing LiTFSI with EMIMCl 
markedly altered the melting behaviour of EMIMCl: from a distinct 
melting point near 87 °C to a gradual melting process completed near 
50 °C. This decrease in melting temperature is consistent with the MD 
simulation finding that homogeneous phases form only when the Li+ 
coordination environment is oversaturated with Cl−. The LiTFSI–
EMIMCl ionic liquid can therefore be used as the electrolyte system 
either directly or diluted with fluorinated ethers such as 
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) to reduce 
viscosity and suppress S2Cl2 dissolution. Two electrolytes were subse-
quently prepared: a diluted ionic liquid of LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE (1:9:10; 
Supplementary Fig. 12) and a pure ionic liquid of LiTFSI–EMIMCl (1:9). 
As shown in Supplementary Figs. 13–15, the diluted electrolyte exhib-
ited higher ionic conductivity (~3.5 mS cm−1), mainly because of its 
lower viscosity, and a higher Li+ transference number (tLi+) ≈ 0.35 than 
the pure ionic liquid.
The dissolution behaviour of S2Cl2 was examined in several sol-
vents, including TTE, DMA and DME. S2Cl2 readily dissolves in DMA and 
DME but exhibits negligible solubility in TTE, where clear phase separa-
tion is observed (Supplementary Fig. 16a). S2Cl2 also remains immiscible 
in the LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 16b). In this 
electrolyte, TTE functions primarily as a diluent to reduce the viscosity 
of the ionic liquid, while the electrolyte simultaneously satisfies several 
key requirements: dissolving Cl− species, oversaturating the Li+ coordi-
nation environment with Cl−, suppressing high-order polysulfide disso-
lution and phase-separating from S2Cl2 (ref. 31). MD simulations of the 
LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE (1:9:10) electrolyte further showed that ~67% of 
Cl− ions remain uncoordinated and available to participate in sulfur oxi-
dation (Supplementary Fig. 17). Because electrolytes containing Cl− can 
corrode aluminium current collectors at elevated voltages32, Li||Al cells 
were evaluated using the LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE electrolyte. No notice-
able Al corrosion was observed below 3.8 V (Supplementary Fig. 18a), 
confirming stable operation within the designed voltage range. In 
addition, Li||carbon cells only exhibited a small reversible capacity of 
~20 mAh gC
−1 (Supplementary Fig. 18b), indicating negligible electro-
chemical activity of the electrolyte between 1.6 V and 3.6 V.
As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the S2Cl2 generated during charging is 
initially retained within the mesoporous carbon matrix but readily dis-
solves in DMA-based electrolytes because of its high solubility. The dis-
solved S2Cl2 subsequently diffuses towards the lithium metal, leading to 
self-discharge and loss of reversible capacity. By contrast, in the LiTFSI–
EMIMCl–TTE electrolyte (Fig. 3b), S2Cl2 is largely retained within the 
mesoporous carbon electrode, probably because of its immiscibility

Revealing reversible Li2S-to-S2Cl2 conversion
A series of characterizations were conducted to probe the 
three-electron redox process between Li2S–LiCl and S2Cl2. X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed 
on electrodes collected from Li||S–LiCl cells after being held at 1.8, 
3.0 and 3.6 V for 4 h (Fig. 4a). Before measurement, the electrodes 
were subjected to Ar sputtering for 600 s to minimize contributions 
from residual LiTFSI and decomposition products (Supplementary 
Figs. 21 and 22). At 1.8 V, corresponding to the fully discharged state, 
the S 2p spectra exhibited characteristic peaks associated with Li2S 
and Li2Sx (ref. 34), whereas the Cl 2p spectra were dominated by 
LiCl signals. After charging to 3.0 V, the Li2S peaks disappeared and 
signals associated with polysulfides and elemental sulfur became 
dominant, while the Cl 2p spectra remained largely unchanged. Upon 
further charging to 3.6 V, the Li2S and polysulfide signals nearly van-
ished, and new features corresponding to S2Cl2 emerged in both the 
S 2p and Cl 2p spectra, consistent with the reference S2Cl2 spectrum 
(Supplementary Fig. 23). Similar electrochemical and spectroscopic 
behaviour was also observed using an S-free LiPF6–EMIMCl–TTE 
(0.6:9:10) electrolyte, further excluding possible sulfur contributions 
from the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Cell performance of Li||S–LiCl batteries
The redox mechanism of the S–LiCl electrode in the LiTFSI–EMIMCl–
TTE electrolyte has been established. However, the Li||S–LiCl cell 
exhibits limited cycling stability, retaining only ~20% of its initial 
capacity after 50 cycles (Fig. 5a, blue dots). To improve cycling per-

cell with the LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE–A electrolyte was first subjected 
to five formation cycles at a charging rate of 0.2C and a discharging 
rate of 0.1C (1C = 700 mAh g−1) within a voltage window of 1.8–3.55 V, 
followed by cycling at 0.3C for charging and 0.2C for discharging.

Conclusion
A reversible three-electron sulfur redox process between S2− and S1+ 
has been demonstrated in the S–LiCl positive electrode. Enabled by a 
free-Cl−-rich ionic liquid electrolyte, the S–LiCl electrode delivered a 
reversible capacity of 713 mAh g−1, corresponding to 1,838 mAh gS
−1, at 
an average operating voltage of 2.54 V. This high-voltage sulfur chemis-
try achieves an electrode-level specific energy exceeding 1,700 Wh kg−1 
with only a minor energy contribution from the electrolyte. Structural 
and spectroscopic characterizations support that sulfur oxidation 
proceeds through the reversible formation of S2Cl2 during charging. 
The Li||S–LiCl pouch cell retained 78% of its initial capacity after 100 
cycles. These results establish a chlorine-mediated strategy for enabling 
multielectron sulfur redox chemistry and provide a route towards 
high-energy-density lithium batteries.

Electrode preparation
Electrodes were prepared by coating active-material slurries onto Al 
foil or carbon paper. The S–LiCl electrode slurry consisted of 39 wt% 
S–C, 41 wt% LiCl, 10 wt% KB and 10 wt% PVDF, with NMP added to adjust 
the viscosity. The slurry was coated onto Al foil and vacuum-dried at 
60 °C overnight. The S–C electrode was prepared using 80 wt% S–C, 
10 wt% KB and 10 wt% PVDF in NMP, followed by coating onto Al foil and 
vacuum-drying at 60 °C overnight. Unlike the other electrodes, the S–C 
electrode could be prepared in ambient air. The Li2S–LiCl@C composite 
was prepared by high-energy ball milling a mixture of 48 wt% Li2S, 8 wt% 
mesoporous carbon and 44 wt% LiCl. The resulting composite was then 
mixed with 10 wt% KB and 10 wt% PVDF in NMP to prepare the slurry, 
which was coated onto Al foil and vacuum-dried at 100 °C overnight. 
The S2Cl2 electrode was prepared by hand milling S2Cl2 with KB at a 
mass ratio of 1:10. The resulting composite was sandwiched between 
two carbon-cloth layers to provide continuous electronic pathways.
Unless otherwise specified, all electrode preparation and handling 
procedures were performed in a dry room or an argon-filled glovebox 
to minimize moisture exposure. The electrode slurries typically had 
a liquid-to-solid ratio of ~3:1 by weight, and the wet coating thickness 
was generally 150–200 μm. Based on the measured electrode thickness 
and mass, the electrode porosity was estimated to be ~60–70%. Circular 
electrodes (10 mm diameter) were punched from the dried sheets, 
yielding a typical areal capacity of ~1 mAh cm−2. The typical S–LiCl 
electrode mass loading was ~1.4 mg cm−2, corresponding to a sulfur 
loading of ~0.54 mg cm−2, unless otherwise specified.
Electrochemical measurements
Unless otherwise specified, electrochemical measurements were con-
ducted at 25 °C. CR2032-type coin cells were assembled using lithium 
metal as the negative electrode and two layers of cellulose separator 
placed between the positive and negative electrodes to reduce cell 
resistance (Supplementary Fig. 41). An aluminium foil was inserted 
between the positive electrode and the stainless-steel cathode case to 
suppress corrosion. Unless otherwise specified, each coin cell con-
tained 20 μl of electrolyte, corresponding to an electrolyte-to-sulfur 
ratio of ~50–60 ml g−1 sulfur. Pouch cells were assembled using a similar 
configuration, except that only one separator layer was used and the 
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio was reduced to 10 ml g−1 sulfur. 
Three-electrode coin cells were assembled similarly to the 
two-electrode cells, except that a lithium reference electrode was 
introduced using an enamelled copper wire coated with molten lithium 
metal. The lithium-coated end was wrapped with a separator and posi-
tioned near the positive electrode, while the other end extended out-
side the cell casing as the reference terminal. GITT measurements were 
performed using a LAND battery testing system. For GITT measure-
ments, cells were tested at a specific current of 100 mA g−1 with a pulse 
duration of 20 min followed by a 20-min rest period. LSV, CV and EIS 
measurements were conducted using a Gamry electrochemical work-
station. EIS measurements were collected over a frequency range from 
106 to 0.1 Hz with an amplitude of 25 mV. For Li||Li symmetric cells, a 
single cellulose separator and ~20 μl electrolyte were used per cell. The

Characterization
SEM and EDS measurements were performed using a Hitachi SU-70 
field-emission scanning electron microscope equipped with a Bruker 
X-Flash 6/60 EDS detector. XPS measurements were conducted using 
monochromatic Al Kα radiation. To minimize air and moisture expo-
sure, samples were transferred directly from the glovebox to the XPS 
instrument. A neutralizer was used during data collection, and Ar 
sputtering was performed at an ion energy of 200 eV for 600 s. All 
spectra were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. 7Li NMR spectra 
were recorded on a Varian Mercury 400-MHz NMR spectrometer at 
room temperature. In situ and ex situ Raman spectra were collected 
using a Horiba Jobin Yvon LabRAM Aramis system with a HeNe laser 
(632.8 nm). The laser power was maintained at ~0.5 mW to minimize 
sample heating. Spectra were collected with an integration time of 
10 s and averaged over three accumulations. In situ and ex situ sulfur 
K-edge and ex situ chlorine K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy 
(XAS) measurements were carried out at beamline 8-BM of the National 
Synchrotron Light Source II, Brookhaven National Laboratory. Spectra 
were collected in fluorescence mode, and air-sensitive samples were 
prepared in an argon-filled glovebox. XAS data were processed using 
the Demeter software package41. For in situ measurements, modi-
fied coin cells were used in which a 4–6-mm hole was drilled into the 
cathode case and sealed with Kapton tape (for XAS) or thin glass (for 
Raman). The electrode slurry was coated onto carbon paper instead 
of Al foil, with the coated side facing the incident beam. Thermogravi-
metric analysis was performed using a TA SDT Q600 at a heating rate 
of 5 °C min−1 from 25 °C to 500 °C. DSC measurements were conducted 
using a Discovery Pressure DSC 25P at 2 °C min−1 from 25 °C to 100 °C. 
DART–MS measurements were performed using a JEOL AccuTOF LC 
time-of-flight mass spectrometer over an m/z range of 100–250 with 
He as the ionization gas and a gas-heater temperature of 400 °C. For 
DART–MS measurements, both the S2Cl2 reference sample and the 
extracted cathode supernatant were diluted in toluene before analysis.
Computational details
MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS v2022.4 package. 
Five LiTFSI–EMIMCl–DMA systems with different LiTFSI/EMIMCl ratios, 
together with a LiTFSI–EMIMCl–TTE (1:9:10) system, were simulated at 
303 K and 1 bar. Initial molecular configurations were generated using 
Packmol with a minimum intermolecular distance of 0.2 nm in a cubic 
simulation box. Each system was first energy-minimized for 50,000 
steps with a 1-fs time step, followed by 50-ns equilibration under the 
NPT ensemble using a 2-fs time step. Temperature and pressure were 
controlled using the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and Parrinello–Rah-
man barostat, respectively. After equilibration, an additional 12-ns 
production run was performed for structural analysis. Force-field 
parameters for EMIM+, TFSI−, DMA and TTE were adopted from previ-
ously reported models42–45. Li+ and Cl− ions were described using the 
OPLS-AA parameters opls_406 and opls_401, respectively46–50. The 
fraction of free Cl− ions was quantified from the MD trajectories using 
an in-house Python script. A cutoff distance of 0.3 nm, determined 
from the first minimum of the Li–Cl radial distribution function, was 
used to distinguish free Cl− ions from Li+-coordinated Cl− species. Force 
field parameters and MD simulation files are also available via Zenodo 
at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20259878 (ref. 51).
