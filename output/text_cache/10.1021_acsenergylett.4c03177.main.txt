Long-Cycling Lithium−Sulfur Batteries
Enabled by Reactivating Inactive Lithium
Li-Ling Su, Mu-Xuan Wu, Shu-Yu Sun, Chen-Xi Bi, Zhao Zheng, Meng Zhao, Bo-Quan Li,

ABSTRACT: High-energy-density lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are attractive but
hindered by short cycle life. The formation and accumulation of inactive Li deteriorate
the battery stability. Herein, a phenethylamine (PEA) additive is proposed to reactivate
inactive Li in Li−S batteries with encapsulating lithium-polysulfide electrolytes (EPSE)
without sacrificing the battery Coulombic efficiency (CE). PEA undergoes reactions with
inactive Li to form organic polysulfide R-LiPEA in a reduction state at the anode side
and subsequently reacts with the S cathode to generate O-LiPEA in an oxidation state. R-
LiPEA and O-LiPEA engage in redox cycles to reactivate inactive Li that accumulates in
batteries. Compared with 122 cycles based on EPSE without PEA, Li−S batteries with
PEA additive in EPSE achieve 185 cycles under demanding conditions and maintain
higher CE of 96% with PEA than 93% without PEA. This work elucidates the ingenious
modification of amino-based additives to reactivate inactive Li by an organic polysulfide
redox mechanism for advanced Li−S batteries.
T
he burgeoning demand of portable electronics and
electric vehicles has stimulated the research of high-
energy-density batteries.1−3 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries, characterized by an impressive theoretical energy
density of 2600 Wh kg−1, emerge as a potential next-generation
battery system.4−6 In order to ensure practical high energy
density of Li−S batteries over 400 Wh kg−1, ultrathin Li anodes
(<50 μm), high-loading S cathodes (>4.0 mg cm−2), and lean
electrolytes (<3.0 μL mgS
−1) are necessary.7−11 However, the
cycle life of practical Li−S batteries is very poor, hindering their
applications.6,12,13
The rapid failure of Li metal anodes mainly accounts for the
poor cycle life of Li−S batteries.14,15 The deterioration of Li
metal anodes is primarily attributed to the incessant formation
and accumulation of inactive Li.16−20 The nonuniform plating/
stripping of deposited Li leads to the formation of inactive Li, in
which Li is wrapped and electrically isolated by the solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI).21−27 In addition, highly reactive
lithium polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates irreversibly react with
Li metal anodes, inducing the loss of active Li and LiPSs.28−30
Accordingly, encapsulating lithium-polysulfide electrolyte
(EPSE) has been developed to improve Li deposition uniformity
and suppress the parasitic reactions of LiPSs on Li metal anodes
by encapsulating LiPSs with weakly-solvating-power and high-
reduction-stability solvents, which notably prolongs the cycle
life of Li−S batteries.28,31,32 In the EPSE, LiPSs are encapsulated
within a bilayer solvation shell. The inner shell is composed of
solvents with strong solvating power, such as 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), which ensure the
dissolution and electrochemical conversion of LiPSs. Solvents
with weak solvating power and high reduction stability are
introduced to form the outer solvation shell, such as 1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE), which
kinetically impedes the reactions between LiPSs and Li metal
anodes and mitigates the shuttle effect of LiPSs. Nevertheless,
the formation of inactive Li remains an inevitable issue in
EPSE.32,33 Consequently, it is imperative to reactivate the
accumulated inactive Li to further improve the cycle life of Li−S
batteries with EPSE.34−36
The 2-aminophenylacetonitrile (APA, Figure S1a) additive
was first demonstrated to reactivate inactive Li in Li−S
batteries.34 Nonetheless, the use of APA additive results in
lower Coulombic efficiency (CE, 80%) compared with conven-
tional ether-based electrolyte (97%) under practical conditions,
which diminishes the energy efficiency of Li−S batteries.34 The
low CE is correlated with the high reactivity of APA, which is
caused by the direct conjugation of the electron-donating amino
group (−NH2) with the phenyl. Especially, owing to the
conjugation effect between −NH2 and the phenyl, the electron

reactivity of −NH2 against Li metal.37,38 Moreover, the cyanide
group (−CN) is not favorable for Li metal.39 Nonetheless,
−NH2 acts as an indispensable function in sequentially reacting
with inactive Li and S to form organic polysulfides.
Consequently, molecular modification is necessary to develop
a novel additive capable of reactivating inactive Li while
simultaneously maintaining a high CE, thereby optimizing the
overall performance and CE of Li−S batteries.
Herein, phenethylamine (PEA, Figure S1b) is introduced as
an additive to reactivate inactive Li in Li−S batteries with EPSE
through an organic polysulfide redox mechanism while
maintaining a high CE. PEA participates in sequential reactions
with inactive Li and S and generates R-LiPEA (reduction state)
at the anode side and O-LiPEA (oxidation state) at the cathode
side. The redox cycle of R-LiPEA and O-LiPEA contributes to
reactivating inactive Li accumulated in Li−S batteries. The
reactivation and recycling of inactive Li decrease the
consumption rate of active Li, further prolonging the cycle life
of Li−S batteries from no more than 122 cycles to 185 cycles
under demanding conditions using Li anodes with a thickness of
50 μm, S cathodes with a sulfur loading of 4.3 mg cm−2, and an
electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 6.6 μL mgS
−1. Moreover, the
CE of the battery with the electrolyte containing PEA is
maintained at 96%, which is higher than that of the electrolyte
without PEA (93%).
PEA is selected to reactivate inactive Li and maintain high CE
by avoiding the side effect of APA. In order to control a
moderate reactivity with Li metal, the −NH2 group should not
be directly connected with the phenyl. Moreover, the −CN
group should be abandoned. Based on the above considerations,
benzylamine (BA, Figure S1c) and PEA are reasonable
candidates to substitute for APA. However, the cycle life of
the conventional ether electrolyte with the BA additive is inferior
to that with the PEA additive (Figure S2). Therefore, PEA is
further comprehensively evaluated in EPSE. To prove the
effectiveness of PEA in Li−S batteries with EPSE, TTE-based
EPSE is chosen as the baseline electrolyte.31 The baseline
electrolyte consists of 1.0 M Li bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI) and 2.0 wt % LiNO3 dissolved in a solution of
TTE/dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) with the
volume ratio of 1:2:2 (marked as w/o PEA). The PEA
electrolyte is prepared by adding 1 vol % PEA to the baseline
electrolyte (marked as with PEA).
The capability of PEA to reactivate inactive Li is first evaluated
by a model experiment (Figure 1). To simulate the
accumulation of massive inactive Li in Li−S batteries, the Ni
foil was disassembled from Li|Ni cells after discharging/charging
5 times and final low current charging to ensure that the active
lithium is fully extracted (Figure 1a,b). As shown in the inset of
Figure 1b, the prepared Ni foil is covered with black inactive Li.
The Ni foil with inactive Li was then employed as the anode in
a Li−S battery, termed an inactive Li−S (i-Li−S) battery, to
preliminarily evaluate the ability of PEA to reactivate inactive Li.
The i-Li−S batteries underwent one cycle to observe their
charge capacity. The i-Li−S battery with PEA exhibits a charge
capacity of 0.017 mAh (Figure 1c). The surface morphology of
the deposits on Ni foil at the end of the charging process (point
A) was observed directly or by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM). After being charged, the Ni foil transforms into a bright
silver color, indicating that the black inactive Li disappears (inset
of Figure 1d). The deposits on Ni are uniformly distributed with
spherical morphology, which are consistent with the morphol-
ogy of Li deposited in an electrolyte containing LiNO3 (Figures
1d and S3a).40,41 In contrast, the i-Li−S battery without PEA
demonstrates negligible charge/discharge capacity of no more
than 10−3 mAh (Figure 1e). The morphology of the Ni foil
disassembled at point B (the inset of Figure 1f) is identical with
the morphology of the prepared inactive Li, and a porous and
filamentous morphology is observed by SEM (Figures 1f and
S3b). The i-Li−S battery with PEA exhibits charge capacity,
indicating new redox reactions are introduced during the
charging process. Subsequently, the recycling of inactive Li was
directly verified by carrying out continuous discharge/charge
tests on the i-Li−S battery. The i-Li−S battery with PEA is able
to sustain continuous operation (Figure S4). The notable
difference in charge/discharge capacity between the batteries
with and without PEA, as well as the distinct morphology of the
anodes at the end of charging, directly indicates that PEA is
capable of reactivating and recycling inactive Li in Li−S
batteries.
The real active species and specific process of redox reactions
of PEA participating in reactivating and recycling of inactive Li
were investigated by in vitro visual experiments (Figure 2).
Before that, the role of the S cathode in reactivating inactive Li
was investigated, and thus the i-Li|carbon paper (i-Li−CP)
batteries were designed. Regardless of the presence of PEA, the i-
Li−CP batteries exhibit a mere 0.001 mAh charge/discharge
capacity (Figure S5). The i-Li−CP battery model experiment
indicates that PEA acts as a precursor and works only in the
presence of the S cathode. Inactive Li or elemental S was

The electrochemical properties of each intermediate were
investigated through model experiments employing i-Li−CP
batteries. Both the O-LiPEA and R-LiPEA exhibited charge/
discharge capacities exceeding 0.010 mAh (Figures 2d, S6, and
S7). Conversely, LiPEA presented no measurable charge/
discharge capacity, further confirming that O-LiPEA and R-
LiPEA have electrochemical reactivity and LiPEA has no
electrochemical reactivity.
The distinct properties of PEA, LiPEA, O-LiPEA, and R-
LiPEA were further investigated, revealing the conversion
reactions of PEA undergone upon its addition into Li−S
batteries. In the
1H NMR spectrum of PEA in dimethyl
sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6), a peak at 1.3 ppm is observed,
attributable to the amino group (−NH2) in PEA (Figure
3a).42−44 In contrast, the 1H NMR spectrum of LiPEA in
DMSO-d6 has no −NH2 peak, indicating that inactive Li reacts
with the −NH2 group in PEA to form −NLi2. The peaks of 1H
NMR spectra of O-LiPEA and R-LiPEA remain unchanged in
the subsequent reaction process, implying the stability of the
molecular structure in subsequent reaction processes, excluding
the reactions of the −NH2 group (Figure S8). The subsequent
reaction process was analyzed via 7Li NMR and 13C NMR
spectra (Figures 3b and S9). After the reaction with S, the 7Li
NMR peak of LiPEA at around −0.9 ppm corresponding to
−NLi2 is observed to shift upfield to −1.0 ppm in the 7Li NMR
spectrum of O-LiPEA. Subsequently, the 7Li NMR chemical
shift of R-LiPEA, formed by the reaction of O-LiPEA with
inactive Li, remains identical to that of O-LiPEA at −1.0 ppm,
indicating the similar chemical environment of Li ion in R-
LiPEA and O-LiPEA molecules. The 7Li NMR chemical shift of
the O-LiPEA and R-LiPEA resembles the soluble Li2S6 in Li−S
batteries. Subsequently, cyclic voltammetry test was performed.
The cyclic voltammetry profiles of the polarized-states of O-
LiPEA and R-LiPEA exhibit two distinct reduction peaks, which
are highly similar to that of Li2S6 (Figure 3c). The similarity in
redox properties indicates the presence of long-chain and short-
chain polysulfides in the molecular structures of O-LiPEA and
R-LiPEA, respectively. Moreover, the redox of O-LiPEA was
demonstrated to be reversible (Figure S10). Further evidence

Based on model experiments, visual experiments, NMR, CV,
and UV−vis tests, the reactivation mechanism of inactive Li by
PEA is summarized (Figure 4). The reactive group −NH2 in
PEA initially reacts with inactive Li to produce the group −NLi2
in LiPEA. The intermediate, Ph−NLi2 (LiPEA), then
participates in a reaction with S to form organic long-chain
polysulfides, Ph−N−(Sx−Li)2 (O-LiPEA, 4 ≤x ≤8).
Subsequently, Ph−N−(Sx−Li)2 (O-LiPEA) undergoes a
reaction with inactive Li accumulated in the battery to form
organic short-chain polysulfides (Ph−N−(Sy−Li)2, R-LiPEA, 4
≤y < x ≤8), thereby activating the inactive Li. Finally, Ph−N−
(Sy−Li)2 (R-LiPEA) is oxidized, converting back to organic
long-chain polysulfides, O-LiPEA at the cathode, establishing a
recurring cycle of the organic polysulfide redox mechanism for
reactivating inactive Li. Thus, the inactive Li generated and
accumulated during cycles is continuously reutilized through
organic polysulfide redox reactions, thereby recovering the
active Li inventory and enhancing the cycle performance of Li−S
batteries.
The effectiveness of PEA in the reactivation and recycling of
inactive Li was further evaluated in Li−S coin cells under
demanding conditions (Figure 5). An ultrathin Li anode (50
μm) and a high-loading S cathode (4.3 mg cm−2) were
employed, along with a low E/S ratio of 6.6 μL of mgS
−1. TTE-
based EPSE was used as the baseline electrolyte to confirm the
effectiveness of PEA. The Li−S battery with PEA maintains a
high average CE of 96% within 100 cycles compared with that of
93% without PEA (Figure 5a). The discharge capacity of Li−S
batteries without PEA rapidly decays to 340 mAh g−1 after 122
cycles, and the CE significantly decreases to less than 17%
(Figure 5a,b). Conversely, the Li−S battery with PEA maintains
a stable performance over 185 cycles, showing a slow decay rate
in both discharge capacity and CE (Figure S11). After 122
cycles, the Li−S battery with PEA shows a discharge capacity of
676 mAh g−1 and a CE of 90% (Figure 5a). At the 100th cycle,
the specific capacity and charge/discharge profiles of batteries
with and without PEA is similar (Figure 5b). The Li−S battery
with PEA holds a second discharge plateau above 2.05 V during
122 cycles (Figures 5b and S12). In contrast, the battery without
PEA displays a discharge curve with markedly increased
polarization, with the second discharge plateau dropping to
2.00 V at the 122nd cycle.
SEM was applied to further reveal the morphology of Li
deposition after cycles. The deposited Li in the Li−S battery
with PEA is smooth and dense, as opposed to the pronounced
porous Li deposition observed in the battery without PEA
(Figure 5c,d). At the 100th cycle, the average thickness of
residual Li in the battery with PEA (59 μm) exceeds that
observed in the battery without PEA (42 μm, Figure S13).
Additionally, the performance of PEA at different concen-
trations was demonstrated (Figure S14). The cycle performance
of Li−S batteries is enhanced by using various concentrations of
PEA, with the optimal concentration being 1 vol %. Overall, Li−
S batteries employing the PEA additive demonstrate remarkably
improved cycle stability, which is attributed to the reactivation of
inactive Li enabled by the redox between O-LiPEA and R-
LiPEA.
In conclusion, PEA additive is introduced in EPSE to
reactivate and recycle inactive Li in Li−S batteries without the
compromise on lowering battery Coulombic efficiency. The
reactivity of the −NH2 group is moderate when it is not directly
connected to phenyl. PEA undergoes reactions with inactive Li
and S cathodes in sequence to form O-LiPEA and R-LiPEA.
These organic polysulfide species engage in redox cycles to
reactivate and recycle inactive Li produced and accumulated in
the batteries, prolonging the cycle life of Li−S batteries. When
employing PEA in Li−S batteries under demanding conditions,
the cycle life of Li−S batteries with TTE-EPSE can be extended
from 122 to 185 cycles, and a high CE of 96% was preserved
meantime. This work presents a rational modification of amino-
based additives to enable the reactivation of inactive Li by an
