Triethyl phosphate (TEP) electrolytes hold significant promise for high-safety lithium metal batteries
(LMBs) due to their eco-friendliness and intrinsic nonflammability. However, parasitic reactions with
lithium metal, coupled with sluggish reaction kinetics, hinder their practical deployment in LMBs. Hence,
we propose a sustainable TEP-based localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) by molecularly
regulating the coordination ability and reduction chemistry of anisole diluents, thereby simultaneously
overcoming the thermodynamic and kinetic limitations associated with high-concentration electrolytes
and conventional LHCEs. The optimized p-methylanisole (pMA) diluent modulates Li–TEP coordination
and
facilitates
anions
to
enter
primary
solvation
sheath
through
Hd+–Od
hydrogen-bonding
interactions, while the weak ion–dipole interaction between Li+ and pMA promotes pMA participation in
interfacial reactions and preserves the cation-hopping transport mechanism. This strategy yields robust
LiF/Li2O-rich interphases and accelerates reaction kinetics, enabling lithium metal to achieve a high
average coulombic efficiency of 98.7% over 650 cycles and an ultralong-lifespan exceeding 1600 h.
When deployed in LMBs paired with 2.5 mAh cm2 sulfurized polyacrylonitrile cathodes, the batteries
demonstrate an extended lifespan over 600 cycles with an average capacity decay of only 0.03% per cycle.
Furthermore, the molecular-level design of diluents is broadly applicable to other alkali–metal batteries,
offering a new pathway toward the development of high-energy LMBs.
Broader context
Lithium metal batteries (LMBs) employing sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) or sulfur cathodes are considered promising candidates to achieve the target
energy density of 500 Wh kg1 set by the Battery500 Consortium. Unfortunately, currently available electrolytes fail to simultaneously ensure the stable
operation of lithium metal anodes and SPAN cathodes. Moreover, most reported electrolyte formulations are based on flammable organic solvents such as
ethers and carbonates, which pose severe safety risks under conditions of collision or overcharging. Triethyl phosphate electrolytes hold significant promise for
high-safety LMBs due to their eco-friendliness and nonflammability. However, parasitic reactions with lithium metal, coupled with sluggish reaction kinetics,
hinder their practical deployment in LMBs. In this work, we rationally designed a functional diluent for nonflammable phosphate electrolytes based on the
electron push–pull effect. The proposed pMA diluent accelerates Li+ desolvation and charge-transfer kinetics while promoting the formation of a robust LiF/
Li2O-rich interphase. The designed electrolyte enables lithium metal to achieve a high average coulombic efficiency and an ultralong-lifespan. Meantime, the
LMBs paired with 2.5 mAh cm2 SPAN cathodes demonstrate an extended lifespan over 600 cycles. Furthermore, the molecular-level design of diluents is
broadly applicable to other alkali–metal batteries, offering a new pathway toward the development of high-energy LMBs.

Introduction
The gravimetric energy density of commercial lithium-ion
batteries (LIBs) based on intercalation chemistry is approach-
ing a theoretical upper limit of 300 Wh kg1.1–3 Lithium metal
batteries (LMBs) employing sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN)
cathodes are considered promising candidates for achieving
the Battery500 Consortium target of 500 Wh kg1, owing to the
high theoretical specific capacity of SPAN (B1000 mAh g1) in
combination with the high redox potential of the lithium metal
anode (3.04 V vs. SHE).4–7 Beyond energy density, stringent
requirements for safety and cycle life are critical, particularly
for applications in electric vehicles.8,9 Unfortunately, currently
available electrolytes fail to simultaneously ensure the stable
operation of lithium metal anodes and SPAN cathodes.10 More-
over, most reported electrolyte formulations are based on
flammable organic solvents such as ethers and carbonates,
which pose severe safety risks under conditions of collision or
overcharging.11–13 These challenges have become major obsta-
cles to the practical development of LMBs, significantly restrict-
ing their broader applications.
To mitigate battery safety hazards, functional solvents with
intrinsic nonflammability such as water, ionic liquids, and
phosphates are developed as alternatives. However, aqueous
electrolytes are constrained by a narrow electrochemical win-
dow,
while
ionic
liquid
electrolytes
remain
prohibitively
expensive.14,15 In contrast, phosphates oﬀer distinct advan-
tages, including low cost, eco-friendliness, wide electrochemi-
cal window, and excellent salt solubility, making them the most
promising nonflammable solvents.10 Recent studies have
further shown that phosphate/carbonate electrolytes can facil-
itate solid–solid sulfur conversion reactions, thereby extending
the lifespan of SPAN cathodes under low mass loading. Never-
theless, parasitic reactions between phosphates and lithium
metal often lead to unstable solid electrolyte interphases (SEIs),
resulting in poor compatibility with lithium anodes.7,10,16 Con-
sidering that lithium metal anodes favor anion-derived SEIs,
the development of localized high-concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs) based on high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) has
greatly promoted ether-based electrolytes. When combined
with phosphate solvents, however, halogen atoms with high
electronegativity can accelerate the chemical and electrochemical
corrosion of lithium metal anodes through intense electron
exchange reactions.17 Moreover, decomposition of hydrofluor-
oether diluents generates excessive LiF with intrinsically low ionic
conductivity, thereby increasing Li-ion diffusion barriers.18,19
Although triethyl phosphate (TEP)-based LHCEs incorporating
fluorine-containing diluents have been reported to reduce polar-
ization compared with HCEs, the cycling lifespans of symmetric
and half cells remain limited, rarely exceeding 1000 h.20–22 These
observations suggest that the applicability of fluorine-containing
diluents in such electrolytes requires critical reassessment.
Traditional hydrofluoroether diluents, exemplified by bis(2,2,2-
trifluoromethyl)ether (BTFE) and 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-(fluoro-
methoxy)propane (HFP), exhibit high minimum electrostatic
potential (ESPmin) values, which hinder cation-hopping conduction
and thereby reduce ionic conductivity.23 Several studies have
sought to overcome this limitation by exposing or introducing
coordination sites to develop weakly solvating diluents, which
facilitate diluent incorporation into the solvation structure, thereby
enhancing ionic conductivity and tuning the SEI composition.24
However, most existing LHCEs are still formulated with fluorinated
ethers, fluorinated carbonates, or fluorinated alkanes as diluents,
which inevitably pose environmental and health risks. Moreover,
fluorinated diluents are generally costly and possess high densities,
sharply increasing battery manufacturing costs.25–27 To the best of
our knowledge, the molecular configuration and composition of
diluents strongly influence their electronic structure, which in turn
governs intermolecular interactions such as diluent–anion and
diluent–solvent coordination, ultimately modulating the solvation
structure of electrolytes. Therefore, rational design of suitable
diluents for TEP-based LHCEs and elucidation of their role in
solvation regulation are of paramount importance.
To address the above challenges, we systematically evaluated
a series of anisole-based diluents, including p-methylanisole
(pMA), phenetole (MA), anisole (AN), difluoroanisole (DFA), and
p-fluoroanisole (pFA), to elucidate the role of the electron push–
pull effect in nonflammable TEP-based LHCEs. Among them,
pMA was identified as the optimal diluent, exhibiting a favor-
able balance of physicochemical properties such as low bio-
toxicity, sustainability, high boiling and flash points, low
density, and appropriate ESPmin and the lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level. Specifically, the pMA
diluent regulates Li–TEP coordination and promotes more
anions to enter the primary solvation sheath through Hd+–Od
hydrogen-bonding interactions, while its weak ion–dipole inter-
action with Li+ facilitates participation in interfacial reactions.
Distinct from the LiF-rich SEI layer formed in LHCEs with
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl
ether
(TTE,
denoted as LH-TTE) and in high-concentration TEP electrolyte
(H-TEP), the pMA-based LHCE (denoted as LH-pMA) generates
a uniform LiF/Li2O-rich SEI layer on lithium metal anodes,
effectively suppressing lithium corrosion. Benefiting from
simultaneous enhancement of reaction kinetics and interfacial
stability, LH-pMA delivers a highly reversible coulombic effi-
ciency of 98.7% over 650 cycles and a lifespan exceeding 1600 h
for lithium plating/stripping. When paired with high-loading
SPAN cathodes (3.5 mg cm2), LMBs with LH-pMA achieve an
extended lifespan of over 600 cycles. Furthermore, LiFePO4-
based LMBs (5.5 mg cm2) with LH-pMA exhibit an impressive
initial coulombic efficiency (ICE) of 94.7% and maintain stable
operation for over 300 cycles without noticeable capacity fading.

In addition, the increased molecular polarizability improves
compatibility with high-polarity solvents, enabling homoge-

strategies regulate the electron density of diluent molecules
through inductive and conjugation effects. Electron-donating
methyl groups in pMA increase electron density, thereby low-
ering electron affinity and reducing reduction reactivity.28,29

Intermolecular interactions and electrolyte solvation structures
Density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations were employed to elucidate the dominant influ-
ence of anions, solvents, and diluents on the solvation struc-
tures of H-TEP, LH-TTE, and LH-pMA. As shown in Fig. 2a, the
calculated binding energies of pMA, MA, AN, DFA, pFA, TEP,
and FSI with Li+ are 2.017, 2.015, 1.930, 1.912, 1.829,
2.521, and 5.898 eV, respectively. The electron-donating
–CH3 group in pMA enhances the electron cloud density around
oxygen atoms, reflected in its higher binding energy compared
with other aromatic ethers. Such moderate binding strength
ensures weak coordination with Li+, thereby preserving cation-
hopping conduction and enhancing ionic conductivity. The
spectroscopy characterization further corroborates the regula-
tory influence of pMA on solvation chemistry. As shown in
Fig. 2b, the Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum dis-
plays the characteristic P–O stretching vibration (1259 cm1) of
TEP, which undergoes a slight blueshift in H-TEP.10 Upon the
introduction of pMA, two additional peaks emerge at 1293 and
719 cm1, relating to C–O stretching and out-of-plane C–H
bending vibrations of pMA, while the P–O stretching vibration
of TEP exhibits a pronounced blueshift.32 This shift is primarily
attributed to the influence of pMA. Subtle variations in solva-
tion structures directly alter the electronic environments of
oxygen and hydrogen atoms in solvents, as evidenced by 1H and
17O NMR spectra (Fig. 2c and d). In TEP, the –CH2– and –CH3
resonance peaks appear at 3.25 and 0.52 ppm, respectively, but
shift to 4.11 and 1.34 ppm after dissolving LiFSI, due to dipole–
dipole interactions between Li+ and oxygen atoms in TEP,
which reduce the electron cloud density of adjacent hydrogen
atoms through an inductive effect.33 With the addition of pMA,
the two characteristic peaks of TEP shift to 3.97 and 1.18 ppm,
pointing to an increased electron cloud density around hydro-
gen atoms in TEP. Compared with H-TEP, the
17O NMR
spectrum of LH-pMA shows a clear upfield shift, signifying a
reduced electron cloud density around oxygen atoms in TEP.
This behavior is attributed to hydrogen-bonding interactions
between oxygen atoms in TEP and protons in pMA, which in
turn enhance the interaction between Li+ and FSI.34–36 As
shown in Fig. S3, the Raman signature of free pMA remains
visible in the LH-pMA electrolyte, suggesting relatively weak
Li+-pMA coordination. Meanwhile, LH-pMA and H-TEP display
similar proportions of solvation species corresponding to the
FSI coordination, confirming that the essential characteristics
of a LHCE are retained.
The MD simulation results further reveal the spatial config-
urations and mass transport processes. As shown in Fig. 2e, f
and Fig. S4, H-TEP exhibits highly aggregated clusters at high
salt concentration, whereas the introduction of pMA and TTE
diluents loosens the solvation structure. Owing to the distinct
coordination geometries between Li+ and the diluents, LH-pMA
facilitates the formation of Li+–FSI–Li+ coordination networks,
which sustain rapid structural (hopping) Li+ transport. In
contrast, in LH-TTE, the aggregated clusters are separated by
noncoordinating TTE molecules, leading to a vehicular Li+
transport mechanism. This striking difference in transport
behavior is further confirmed by radial distribution functions
(RDFs), where FSI and TEP primarily occupy the first solvation
shell (B1.78 Å), while pMA resides in the second solvation shell
(B3.54 Å). TTE scarcely enters the inner solvation shells due to
its low binding energy arising from strong intramolecular
fluorination. As illustrated in Fig. 2g, h and Fig. S5, the
coordination numbers of Li+–FSI are 4.17, 4.52, and 4.74 in
H-TEP, LH-pMA, and LH-TTE, respectively. Correspondingly,
the coordination numbers of Li+–TEP decrease from 0.56 (H-TEP)
to 0.46 (LH-TTE) and 0.51 (LH-pMA), consistent with the spectro-
scopic results. After 1000 ps of simulation, all electrolyte models
maintain stable densities, in which LH-pMA exhibits a signifi-
cantly lower density (1.18 g cm3) compared with LH-TTE
(1.56 g cm3) and H-TEP (1.48 g cm3) (Fig. 2i). This substantial
reduction in density is advantageous for improving the gravi-
metric energy density under fixed electrolyte volume conditions.
Furthermore, mean squared displacement (MSD) calculations
distinguish the diffusion behaviors of Li+ and FSI. As shown
in Fig. 2j and Fig. S6, Li+ diffusion is markedly enhanced in
LH-pMA due to the cation-hopping conduction mechanism.
Specifically, the Li+ diffusion coefficient in LH-pMA reaches
3.95  104 mm2 s1, which is 14.36 times higher than that in
H-TEP (2.75  105 mm2 s1). The LH-TTE electrolyte exhibits
noticeable MSD fluctuations around 900 ps, which can be
attributed to the transport mechanism transition induced by
the disruption of the percolated Li+ conduction network upon
incorporation of the high-content TTE diluent.37,38

LSV was conducted in Li8Cu cells at a scan rate of 0.01 mV s1

Beyond its low chemical reactivity and stable SEI, the low
desolvation energy of LH-pMA facilitates charge-transfer pro-
cesses, typically reflected by a higher exchange current density
( j0). As shown in Fig. 3g, LH-pMA delivers a j0 value of 0.24 mA
cm2, which is significantly higher than those of LH-TTE (0.16
mA cm2) and H-TEP (0.15 mA cm2). Consistently, the Li+
ionic conductivity of LH-pMA reaches 1.41 mS cm1, surpassing
LH-TTE (0.96 mS cm1) and H-TEP (0.64 mS cm1) (Fig. 3h).
The superior conductivity in LH-pMA arises from enhanced
intermolecular interactions between Li+ and the –OCH3 group
in pMA, while the high viscosity of H-TEP hinders ion transport.
Although the addition of TTE lowers viscosity, its inability to
coordinate with Li+ disrupts ion-hopping networks, thereby
limiting transport kinetics. Activation energies (Ea) for charge
transfer, calculated from charge-transfer resistance (Rct) values
using the Arrhenius equation (Fig. 3i and Fig. S16), further
confirm this trend. The Ea value of LH-pMA is 12.7 kJ mol1,
slightly lower than those of LH-TTE (12.9 kJ mol1) and H-TEP
(13.4 kJ mol1), consistent with the faster kinetics of LH-pMA.
Morphological analysis further validates these findings. SEM
images (Fig. 3j–l) reveal that lithium deposits in H-TEP appear
granular with rough edges, while LH-TTE exacerbates uneven
deposition and dendrite formation. In sharp contrast, LH-pMA
produces homogeneous and dense lithium deposits, highlight-
ing its excellent compatibility with lithium metal. The discre-
pancy between deposition morphology and kinetic parameters
underscores the critical role of interfacial characteristics in
governing lithium deposition behavior.
Interfacial chemistry evolution
Under an applied electric field, solvated Li+ clusters migrate to
the lithium metal surface and undergo reduction to form the
SEI layer. The SEI composition critically aﬀects lithium deposi-
tion behavior. In Fig. 4a–c and Tables S3–S5, the depth-
dependent SEI compositions generated in different electrolytes
were studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). After a
short etching time of 30 s, the F content of the SEI formed in
LH-pMA is only 33.8%, compared with 50.2% in H-TEP and
52.9% in LH-TTE. In contrast, the O content in the LH-pMA-
derived SEI reaches 40.1%, significantly higher than that in H-
TEP (26.9%) and LH-TTE (27.4%). Strikingly, the SEI in LH-pMA
consistently exhibits lower F and higher O contents across all
depths, indicating that pMA serves as an oxygen source, thereby
suppressing continuous FSI decomposition. C 1s spectra
(Fig. 4d–g and Fig. S17) reveal the presence of organic compo-
nents such as C–C, C–O, C–F, and CF2. The SEI derived from
LH-pMA shows pronounced C–O signals at all depths, suggest-
ing that the coordinated pMA diluent actively participates in
SEI formation. In contrast, the abundant CFx species detected
in the LH-TTE-derived SEI highlight the nontrivial electroche-

Electrochemical performance of practical full cells
To assess the practical applicability of the designed electrolytes,
Li8LFP and Li8SPAN full cells were evaluated, with the initial
two cycles conducted at 0.2C for activation. The mass loadings
of the LFP and SPAN cathodes were 5.5 and 3.5 mg cm2,
respectively. LSV measurements (Fig. S21) show that LH-pMA
and LH-TTE exhibit oxidation potentials of 4.03 and 4.17 V,
respectively, ensuring compatibility with the operational vol-
tages of both LFP and SPAN. Moreover, the leakage-current test
demonstrates that the LH-pMA electrolyte does not induce
parasitic reactions at 3.0 V on the SPAN cathode (Fig. S22),
confirming that the corrosion-resistance advantage of pMA
mainly refers to the lithium metal anode. As shown in Fig. 5a
and b, the Li8LFP full cell with LH-pMA demonstrates nearly
100% capacity retention after 300 cycles, accompanied by an
impressive initial coulombic eﬃciency (ICE) of 94.7%. In
comparison, the Li8LFP cell with H-TEP presents a lower
discharge capacity and a reduced ICE of 85.93%, which can
be attributed to sluggish reaction kinetics limited by poor ionic
conductivity and persistent CEI formation.
A similar trend is observed in Li8SPAN full cells, where ICE
values are 78.6% for H-TEP and 82.4% for LH-pMA (Fig. S23).
The Li8SPAN full cell with LH-pMA exhibits a markedly lower
voltage polarization of 0.62 V compared with 1.03 V for the H-
TEP-based cell, indicating significantly improved intrinsic
solid–solid conversion kinetics. After the activation process,
the Li8SPAN cell in LH-pMA exhibits a single discharge plateau,
delivering a high discharge capacity of 757.82 mAh g1 at 0.5C
and superior cycling stability, with 71% capacity retention after
600 cycles (average capacity decay of only 0.03% per cycle)
(Fig. 5c). In contrast, the H-TEP electrolyte results in lower
capacities, while the poor compatibility of LH-TTE with lithium
metal induces severe capacity degradation (48% retention after
510 cycles, Fig. S24). Differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis
(Fig. 5d and Fig. S25) further confirms that sulfur conversion
in both LH-pMA and H-TEP is highly reversible, as evidenced by
overlapping dQ/dV profiles over 50 cycles, confirming the
polysulfide-free conversion feature of the pMA diluent.7,53 The
rate capabilities of Li8SPAN full cells are shown in Fig. 5e and f
and Fig. S26. As the C-rate increases from 0.2 to 0.5, 1, 1.5, and
2C, the reversible capacities of the Li8SPAN cell with LH-pMA
gradually decrease to 716.58, 675.00, 616.04, 554.12, and 481.23
mAh g1, respectively. By contrast, the Li8SPAN cell in H-TEP
delivers only 76.41 mAh g1 at 2C. Moreover, the LH-pMA
electrolyte maintains stable operation for B100 cycles without
noticeable capacity fading. Clearly, Li8SPAN full cells with LH-
pMA exhibit significantly superior rate capability and long-term
stability compared with those employing H-TEP, which can be
ascribed to the enhanced reaction kinetics.
To elucidate the diﬀerences in cycling performance, galva-
nostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was implemen-
ted to analyze the reaction kinetics of Li8SPAN full cells in LH-
pMA, LH-TTE, and H-TEP (Fig. 5g and Fig. S27). Ohmic polar-
ization and charge-transfer impedance originate from the con-
tact resistance of components and the charging/discharging of
the interfacial double layer, with response times on the order of
milliseconds to nanoseconds. The diﬀusion process arises from
concentration polarization and scales proportionally with the
square root of time. Thus, the voltage step stems from ohmic and
charge-transfer resistances during GITT relaxation, while the
subsequent slow shift originates from ion diﬀusion.54 During
the discharge process between 1.85 and 2.00 V, the ohmic and
charge-transfer resistance (step I) decreases sequentially from
30.8 mV in H-TEP to 22.5 mV in LH-TTE and further to 21.5 mV
in LH-pMA. Likewise, the diﬀusion resistance of ionic species
(step II) markedly decreases from 111.4 mV in H-TEP to 98.7 mV
in LH-TTE and ultimately to 88.9 mV in LH-pMA. The SPAN
cathode in LH-pMA consistently exhibits the lowest overpotential
among the three electrolytes, attributable to enhanced ‘‘solid–
solid’’ sulfur conversion kinetics. Operando distribution of
relaxation times (DRT) analysis further clarifies the effect of
LH-pMA on sulfur redox dynamics. Based on the characteristic
relaxation times, P5 corresponds to Li+ diffusion within SPAN,
P3–P4 are assigned to the interfacial reaction region, and P1–P2
represent interparticle contact (Fig. 5h and i). The LH-pMA cell
maintains reduced and stable resistances across all regions,
confirming its enhanced sulfur conversion kinetics enabled by
improved interfacial stability. The Li8SPAN full cell employing
LH-pMA exhibits decreased and stabilized resistance across all
regions with increasing depth of discharge, indicating improved
Li+ transport and interfacial charge-transfer kinetics enabled by
the formation of a robust CEI.

the shuttle eﬀect and ensure stable operation of Li8SPAN full
cells. Elemental analysis further highlights the diﬀerence

Conclusions
In summary, we rationally designed a functional diluent for
nonflammable phosphate electrolytes based on the electron
push–pull effect. The designed pMA diluent, with an optimized
LUMO energy level and ESPmin, effectively regulates solvation
structures through hydrogen-bonding and ion-dipole interac-
tions. This dual functionality accelerates Li+ desolvation and
charge-transfer kinetics while promoting the formation of a
robust LiF/Li2O-rich interphase. Therefore, LH-pMA delivers an
outstanding average CE of 98.7% over 650 cycles and an ultra-
long cycling lifespan exceeding 1600 h. Compared with LH-TTE
