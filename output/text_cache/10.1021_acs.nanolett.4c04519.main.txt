Regulating Polysulfide Clustering with an Anion Acceptor for Low-
Temperature Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: The performance of the lithium−sulfur battery at harsh
conditions of lean electrolyte and low temperature is impeded by
nanometric clustering of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), which
inherently hinders sulfur reaction kinetics. Here we describe a
cluster-disrupting strategy wherein a fluorinated borane anion
acceptor, tris(perfluorophenyl)borane, is exploited to regulate the
LiPSs solvation structure and to promote their reactions under harsh
conditions. The electron-deficient anion acceptor molecules bond
with the electron-rich LiPSs via soft Lewis acid−base interactions,
which disrupts LiPSs’ clustering and promotes Li2S solvation,
facilitating a three-dimensional Li2S growth via solution-mediated
mechanism at low temperatures. With the borane anion acceptor, the
cell shows a significantly improved capacity at −25 °C. Impressively,
this strategy enables stable cycling of a 1.83 Ah pouch cell at 0 °C retaining 96.0% capacity over 50 cycles. Our study provides a
general methodology for manipulating the fundamental electrolyte chemistry to regulate LiPSs clustering and improve sulfur
batteries at harsh conditions.
KEYWORDS: Li−S battery, nanometric clustering, lithium polysulfides, anion acceptor, coordination structure
L
ithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery remains attractive for high-
energy and low-cost energy storage, particularly for
specialized weight-sensitive applications, owed to its high
theoretical specific energy and high sulfur abundance.1−3
Substantial progress has been made in electrode and electrolyte
design to improve the utilizable capacity and long-term cycling
stability.4,5 Yet, achieving high energy density with lean
electrolyte remains challenging.6 Further, low-temperature
performance is crucial for specialized fields like space
exploration and subsea operations, but remains under
explored.7
In Li−S batteries, both sulfur and lithium sulfide (Li2S) are
electron-insulating, inherently impeding the interfacial charge
transfer.8−10 Fortunately, the reactions are facilitated by the
solvated lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in ether-based electro-
lytes, enabling rapid solution-mediated charge transfer and
high sulfur utilization in flooded electrolytes.11,12 However, the
sulfur capacity is highly associated with the physiochemical
properties and the volume of electrolyte.13 To achieve an
energy density of over 400 W h kg−1, the electrolyte/sulfur (E/
S) ratio (μLelectrolyte mg−1
sulfur) must be below 5 μL mg−1.6,14
Under such harsh conditions, the insufficient solvation for
LiPSs and their susceptibility to clustering (bridged by Li+) at
medium-to-high concentrations greatly hinder the solution
mediated reactions.15 Even worse, such kinetic disadvantage is
also considered the primary capacity limiting mechanism at
low temperatures, more than other factors such as lower ion
conductivity or higher viscosity.16 Therefore, the LiPSs
clustering significantly impedes sulfur utilization and the
reversibility under these harsh conditions, challenging the
promises of Li−S batteries.17,18
The solvated structure of LiPSs is beyond being free Li+−
Sx
2−ion pairs in that the interactions between LiPSs and the
electrolyte solvent/salt incur a range of coordination
structures.17,19−22 The polysulfide anions bond easily with
Li+ through short-range electrostatic interactions at high
concentrations, leading to aggregation into higher-order
clusters.10 For instance, with a low E/S ratio of 3 μL mg−1,
corresponding to a high Li2S4 concentration of 2.6 M, reaction
kinetics are found to be significantly suppressed by the Li2S4

clustering.22 Salient efforts have been made to alleviate the
detrimental clustering by introducing highly polarizing anions
to compete in bonding with the Li+ ions via electrostatic
interactions.23 Moreover, strongly coordinating salt anions
have been used to enhance electrochemical behavior at low
temperature.24−26 However, there is still a gap toward meeting
practical requirements, particularly in terms of low-temperature
cycling stability which has been historically deemed impossible
for Li−S batteries.
In this study, we describe a polysulfide clustering disrupting
strategy by a fluorinated borane anion acceptor molecule to
enable a high-capacity and stable low-temperature Li−S
battery with ethereal electrolytes. Our experimental evidence
and theoretical calculations confirm that the tris-
(perfluorophenyl)borane exhibits soft Lewis acid−base inter-
action with LiPSs through bonding of electron-deficient boron
with the electron-rich sulfur in LiPSs, effectively disrupting the
LiPSs’ clustering at lean electrolyte and low temperatures.
Notably, the anion acceptor not only promotes full solvation
for LiPSs and hence their enhanced conversion reaction
kinetics but further assists partial solvation for Li2S, resulting in
a 3D island-like growth of Li2S, which is critical for high sulfur
utilization. As a result, the Li−S cell shows a greatly increased
capacity of 1,000 mA h g−1 at a low temperature of −25 °C.
Furthermore, with the LiPSs cluster-disrupting strategy, we
demonstrate a 1.83 Ah Li−S pouch cell with steady cycling
over 50 cycles at 0 °C.
The lone electron pairs in sulfur atoms makes LiPSs soft
Lewis bases, and the typical anion acceptors borates/boranes
with electron-deficient boron centers are Lewis acids. We thus
propose that the boron-based compounds can bond with the
polysulfide anions through Lewis acid−base interactions and
thus regulate their coordination structure.
We investigated two types of anion acceptors, a borane of
tris(perfluorophenyl)borane (TPFPB) and borates of triphenyl
borate (TPB) and trimethyl borate (TMB), as proof-of-
concept molecules for regulating the solvation and clustering of
LiPSs. Theoretical calculations were performed to understand
the thermodynamic origin of their interactions with LiPSs,
wherein Li2S6 and Li2S4 are used as the representative LiPSs.
The binding energy (energy gain of binding, ΔE) of TPFPB
with one Li2S6 molecule is −1.37 eV, and −1.11 eV with one
Li2S4 molecule, indicating a rather strong interaction with
LiPSs (Figure 1a). The optimized coordination geometries
reveal B−S and Li−F bond formation in the Li2S6-TPFPB and
Li2S4-TPFPB complexes (Figure 1a). In comparison, the TPB
and TMB also spontaneously coordinate with Li2S4 with ΔE
values of −1.10 and −1.16 eV, but the origin is the Li−O
rather than B−S bonding (Figure S1). We speculate that the
strong electronegativity of oxygen encourages Li−O bonding

The altered chemical coordination of LiPSs was further
confirmed by UV−visible (UV−vis) and 7Li nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The characteristic UV−vis
peak of Li2S6 at 310 nm undergoes a blue shift and narrowing,
resulting from the coordination with TPFPB (Figure 1c).27,28
Meanwhile, the formation of the Li2S6-TPFPB complex leads
to enhanced electron shielding around Li+ as confirmed by the
upfield shifting of the 7Li NMR chemical shift (Figure 1d).22,29
Notably, the complex formed through the soft Lewis acid−
base interaction exhibits highly reversible redox behavior, as
evidenced by the cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements
(Figure 1e). The enhanced reversibility with TPFPB contrasts
with the nucleophilic attack of LiPSs by carbonate, which
produces electrochemically inert products.30 Importantly, such
Lewis acid−base interaction also greatly enhances the
solubility of Li2S in the ethereal electrolytes (Figure S2b),
which is critical for regulating the deposition behavior of Li2S,
as we will discuss below. Therefore, the solvation structure and
hence the solubility of LiPSs and Li2S can be regulated with
the electron-deficient anion acceptors, promising improved
sulfur reaction kinetics under harsh conditions.
We now discuss the molecular mechanism of the polysulfide
clustering behavior. The medium-chain Li2S4 was used for
theoretical calculations to evaluate the binding energy (ΔE) of
clustered Li2S4 molecules with/without TPFPB (Figure 1f).
Without TPFPB, multiple (two and four) Li2S4 molecules
prefer to aggregate with a rather negative ΔE, indicating a high
susceptibility to clustering at high population density (Figure
1f), which impedes its diffusion and further redox reaction.
Consequently, the conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S would exhibit
slow kinetics, becoming the rate- and capacity-determining
step at low E/S ratio and low temperature conditions.31
Remarkably, with the presence of TPFPB, the cluster is
suppressed, wherein the multiple Li2S4 bonds stay isolated by
explicitly interacting with the TPFPB through the B−S and
Li−F bonds (Figure 1f). Indeed, the more negative ΔE values
for 2(Li2S4)-TPFPB (−2.04 eV) and 4(Li2S4)-2TPFPB (−5.86
eV), respectively, compared to (Li2S4)2 (−1.69 eV) and
(Li2S4)4 (−5.67 eV) confirms a thermodynamically driven
binding process (Figure 1f).

We investigated the impact of the disrupted LiPSs clustering
and enhanced Li2S solubility by analyzing the Li2S growth
behavior and kinetics (Figure S2b). A potentiostatic electro-
deposition of Li2S was conducted on Li|Li2S6 cells with a Li2S6
catholyte on a carbon paper current collector (Figure 2). At 25
°C, following predischarging to 2.09 V at 0.05 C, the cells are
subjected to a potentiostatic discharge at 2.05 V and the
current response is recorded. Typically, under such testing
conditions, the current first decreases from a maximum due to
the Faradaic reaction of residual long-chain LiPSs, then
increases as the Li2S nucleation and growth occur, and
eventually fades due to the reduced active surface area by the
deposited nuclei and the overlapping of diffusion hemispheres
(Figure 2a).32 Comparing the chronoamperogram curves
demonstrates a high Li2S deposition capacity of 307 mA h
g−1 in the TPFPB electrolyte (Figure 2b), nearly double that in
the blank electrolyte (156 mA h g−1, Figure 2a).
The Li2S nucleation mechanism in different electrolytes can
be clarified by constructing dimensionless I/Im −T/Tm (Im:
the maximum current, Tm: the specific time) curves and
comparing them with four classical models of electro-
deposition.33,34 The Li2S deposition mode in the blank
electrolyte is closely aligned with the two-dimensional
instantaneous (2DI) model, in which the Li2S growth is
predominantly governed by surface diffusion resulted from the
restricted Li2S solubility and diffusion on the surface (Figure
2c).35−37 In contrast, the current response curve of the TPFPB
cell aligns with the three-dimensional progressive (3DP)
model, indicating the solution-mediated mechanism facilitated
by the partially solvated Li2S (Figure 2c).35,37
The Li2S growth morphologies were further studied by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging. A 2D film-like
Li2S morphology is observed in the blank electrolyte (Figure
2a, inset), which can impede the electrochemical reaction as
Li2S gradually covers the surface. In contrast, the TPFPB
electrolyte produces small Li2S granules on the electrode
surface (Figure 2b, inset), aligned with the growth modeling
(Figure 2c). The Li2S deposition capacity in TPB and TMB
electrolytes approaches about 150 mA h g−1 (Figure S3a,d),
with growth model closely following the 3DP model, forming a

beneficial to improving the sulfur utilization at harsh

Lithium plating/stripping stability is crucial for long cycle
life in Li−S batteries when designing a new electrolyte.38
Previously reported highly electron-donating electrolyte
solvents or salts that aimed to promote the LiPSs’ solvation
tend to react heavily with the Li metal anodes (LMAs) and
thus impede the long-term cycling life.39−41 Here, instead of
introducing electron-donating molecules, the proposed TPFPB
is an electron-deficient anion acceptor, avoiding extensive side
reactions with LMA. The Li|Cu cell with the 25 mM TPFPB
electrolyte exhibits stable cycling for 250 cycles with an average
Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 98.8%, whereas the one with the
blank electrolyte degrades quickly after 180 cycles (Figure 3a,
Figure S4). Furthermore, the symmetric Li|Li cell measure-
ments demonstrate stable lithium stripping/plating in the 25
mM TPFPB electrolyte for 500 h, in contrast to the premature
short-circuiting at 115 h in the blank electrolyte (Figure 3b).
Although the cell using TPFPB electrolyte shows a slightly
higher polarization, likely due to the slightly lower ionic
conductivity or different solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
(Figure S5), it results in a more stable overpotential during
long-term cycling. Furthermore, we note that further increasing
the TPFPB concentration to 60 mM results in reduced CE and
a shorter lifespan of the Li|Cu cell and continuous voltage
increase of the symmetric Li|Li cell (Figure 3a,b).

To evaluate the influence of TPFPB on the sulfur reactions
in Li−S cells, comprehensive electrochemical studies were
performed using sulfur cathodes prepared with plain carbon
without sophisticated engineering (Kejten Black, 70 wt % S;
KB/S). The CV measurements demonstrate that the TPFPB
leads to less-polarized cathodic/anodic peaks than does the
blank electrolyte at all scan rates (Figure S8). At 25 °C, the cell
with TPFPB electrolyte exhibits a high initial capacity to 1,158
mA h g−1 at 0.2 C, maintaining 898 mA h g−1 over 300 cycles,
outperforming the one without TPFPB (Figure 4a,b).
Although the cell exhibits capacity decay during early cycles
due to the dissolving of polysulfides and its cross-reaction with
Li metal, the TPFPB ultimately enhances sulfur reaction
kinetics and improves sulfur utilization by regulating the LiPSs’
clustering behavior. Indeed, the in situ EIS measurements show
that TPFPB reduces the interfacial and charge transfer
impedances over the course of cycling (discussed in Figure
S9). The rate capability test confirms higher sulfur utilization
at high rates for the cell with TPFPB, highlighting the essence
of facilitated polysulfide reaction kinetics and promoted 3D
Li2S deposition (Figures S10−S12). With a sulfur loading of
2.4 mg cm−2, the cell with TPFPB electrolyte shows a
discharge capacity of 1,164 mA h g−1 with very stable cycling
over 100 cycles (Figure S13). The cells with TPB and TMB
additives show limited enhancement on the stability (Figures

The mechanism of the LiPSs clustering disruption with the
presence of TPFPB was particularly effective at low temper-
atures. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique
(GITT) measurements at −25 °C reveal large overpotential
during the reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S and a low capacity of 920
mA h g−1 for the cell with the blank electrolyte (Figure 5a). In
contrast, the cell with the TPFPB electrolyte exhibits a high
capacity of 1,325 mA h g−1 at −25 °C, indicating greatly
facilitated formation of Li2S with the 3D growth (Figure 5a).
In addition, the quasi-diffusion coefficient of Li+ ions evaluated
by GITT indicates that TPFPB particularly improves the Li2S4-
to-Li2S conversion at low temperatures (Figure 5b). Further,
the cell with TPFPB electrolyte enables a high specific capacity
of 1,000 mA h g−1 at −25 °C, with an initial activation cycle,
retaining 676 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles at 0.1 C (Figure 5c,d).
In contrast, the cell with the blank electrolyte shows a capacity
of only 260 mA h g−1, and of particular note, the second
plateau that corresponds to the Li2S4-to-Li2S conversion does
not appear in the applied voltage window, indicating an
extremely sluggish reaction at −25 °C. In addition, the cell
with the TPFPB electrolyte exhibits a rather impressive rate
performance, reaching a specific capacity of 714 mA h g−1 at
0.3 C (Figure 5e). The huge difference at low temperature
clearly points to the essence of disrupted LiPSs’ clustering and
promoted Li2S solubility afforded by the TPFPB anion
acceptor (Figures 5c). Further discussions of the low-
temperature performance are provided in Figure S16.
To further validate the proposed strategy in facilitating sulfur
reaction under harsh conditions, we assembled an Ah-level Li−
S pouch cell with aggressively configured electrodes (cathode
with sulfur loading of 4.0 mg cm−2; 125 μm LMA for two-
sided; negative/positive capacity ratio of 1.87) and a lean
electrolyte with 4 μL of mgsulfur
−1 (Figure 5f; detailed in the
Experimental Methods). The 2.54 Ah Li−S pouch cell exhibits
a low-polarization voltage profile and delivers a high energy
density of 304 W h kg−1 (based on the mass of the pouch cell)
at 25 °C (Figure S17). Notably, the cell could deliver a high
capacity of 1.83 Ah with an average voltage of 2.07 V at 0 °C
(Figure 5g) and exhibits steady cycling over 50 cycles with
96.0% capacity retention and a rather high CE of 99.7%
(Figure 5h). The outstanding performance demonstrates the
prospect of the anion acceptor in regulating LiPSs’ clustering
and improving sulfur utilization.
In this work, we demonstrated a strategy of regulating LiPSs
clustering by introducing a fluorinated borane anion acceptor.
The TPFPB molecule shows strong interaction with the
polysulfide anions by the electron-deficient boron bonding
with sulfur and thus disrupts the clustering behavior of LiPSs
that would occur at thermodynamically harsh conditions of
high concentration and low temperatures. We show that the
promoted solvation for LiPSs not only enhances their reaction
kinetics but also further regulates the deposition morphology
of Li2S favoring a 3D island-like growth behavior. Further, the
electron-deficient nature of the TPFPB participates in the
secondary solvation shell of Li+, and results in an inorganic-rich
Li metal SEI (LiF, Li3N and Li2O). The developed electrolyte
demonstrates outstanding performance at low temperatures,
achieving a specific capacity of 1,000 mA h g−1 at 0.1 C and
−25 °C. Notably, the 1.83 Ah Li−S pouch cell demonstrates
steady cycling over 50 cycles with 96.0% capacity retention at 0
°C. Our study contributes to the understanding of polysulfide
solvation behavior and provides insights on how to manipulate
the fundamental electrolyte chemistry to improve Li−S
batteries at harsh conditions.
