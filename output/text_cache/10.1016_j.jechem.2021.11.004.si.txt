Supplemental experimental section
Chemicals and Materials
Sulfur (99.5 wt%, S), lithium sulfide (99.98%, Li2S), lithium foil (99.9 wt%, Li), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (99.95 wt%, LiTFSI), lithium iodide (99.9%, LiI), potassium ferricyanide (K3Fe(CN)6, 99 wt%), potassium hexacyanocobaltate (K3Co(CN)6, 97 wt%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 99.9 wt%), 1,3–dioxolane (DOL, anhydrous, 99.8 wt%), 1,2–dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous, 99.5 wt %), tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (>99.9 wt%, G4), lithium nitrate (99.99 wt%, LiNO3), acetonitrile (ACN, anhydrous, 99.8 wt%), poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO, average Mw ~4000000), poly (vinyl pyrrolidone) (PVP, average Mw ~360000), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 99.5 wt%), 1–methyl–2–pyrrolidinone (99.5 wt%, NMP) from Sigma Aldrich; carbon nanotube (CNT) from Carbon Solutions, Inc.; and Super–P carbon (SP, 99.5 wt%) from Timcal; were all used as received. Tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether was dried Mover 4 Ångström molecular sieves for 5 days before use.
Preparation of Li2S6 catholyte
The Li2S6 catholyte was prepared by mixing Li2S and S powders in 1:5 stoichiometric ratio in the tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether solvent. The mixture was vigorously stirred at 60 °C until the powders were completely dissolved.
Preparation of the Li3PS4 protected lithium metal anode
The Li3PS4 protected lithium metal was prepared according to Ref. 1[1]. In a typical preparation, 0.445 g P2S5 was added to 10 mL 0.1 M Li2S6 catholyte to form a clear solution. A piece of polished lithium metal was equilibrated in the solution at room temperature for 24 h, followed by washing with DME thrice and drying in vacuum for 2h before use.
Materials characterization
Powder X–ray diffraction (XRD) was measured by a BRUKER D8 ADVANCE (Germany) diffractometer using a Cu Kɑ source. X–ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were collected on a Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD surface analyzer using a monochromatic Al K source at 15 kV (1486.71 eV). Binding energies were corrected by referencing the C 1s peak of adventitious carbon to 284.5 eV. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken by a JEOL JSM–6700F scanning electron microscope. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were based on a JEOL 2100F transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV accelerate voltage. UV–vis spectroscopy was performed on a Shimadzu UV–2450. Cyclic voltammetry, potentiostatic measurements and electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded using an Autolab type III electrochemical workstation. EIS measurements were carried from 1 MHz to10 mHz. All tests were conducted at room temperature.

Figure S3. Wetting test of (a) a Nor–S cathode with the PVDF binder and (b) a Gel–S cathode with the PEO–PVP binder. The wetting test was performed on a contact angle equipment with video capture (VCA optima, AST Products, Inc.). The results show that the contact angle of the Gel–S cathode with PEO–PVP binder was much smaller than that of the Nor–S cathode with PVDF binder.

Figure S5. Potentiostatic polarization curves of (a) the sulfur reduction reaction and (b) the Li2S oxidation reaction of Gel–S, Nor–S and C–S cathodes at 0.1 mV s–1 with the derived Tafel plots as insets. A smaller Tafel slope implies a faster reaction kinetics. The results show that the Gel–S cathode had the highest reaction kinetics, while the C–S cathode had the lowest reaction kinetics.

Figure S8. Electrochemical impedance spectra of Nor–S and Gel–S cells, recorded at the open–circuit voltage after 100 cycles. Under the lean electrolyte condition, the accumulation of insulating Li2S on the sulfur cathode after repeated battery cycling could lead to electrode passivation and polarization. The semicircle in the high–frequency region indicates the formation of a passivating layer on the electrode surface (corresponding to R1), and the semicircle in the mid–frequency region is related to the charge transfer resistance (corresponding to R2).[2,3]


Figure S9. Characterization of a Li3PS4–protected Li anode from the Gel–S–LiI cell after 100 cycles. (a) Li 1s, (b) P 2p and (c) S 2p XPS spectra. There was no significant difference between the XPS spectra of the cycled and pristine Li3PS4–protected anodes, suggesting that the Li3PS4 protection layer after 100 cycles still retained most of its pristine chemical structure. The XPS spectra of pristine Li3PS4–protected Li anode were almost the same as those in our previous study [4].

Figure S15. UV–vis spectra of two Gel–S–LiI cathodes after charging to 2.8 V and 3.2 V respectively. These electrodes were recovered from disassembled cells in the glovebox and equilibrated in DME. The DME solutions were then analyzed by UV–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy. There was no presence of S62– and S82– after charging to 2.8 V, but clear presence of I3– at ~295 and ~366 nm after charging to 3.2 V. This supports the assignment of the voltage plateau at ~3V in Figure 4a to the I–/I3– redox couple.




Figure S16. SEM images of (a) a Nor–S electrode and a Gel–S electrode. It was found that the as–fabricated Gel–S cathode had a smooth surface texture without any crack, as a result of the good gelation ability of the PEO–PVP binder in the presence of a small amount of electrolyte. On the contrary, cracks were found in the Nor–S cathode surface, which are adverse to its cycling performance.
