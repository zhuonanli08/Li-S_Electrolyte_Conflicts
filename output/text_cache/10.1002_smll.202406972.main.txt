Organic Thiolate as Multifunctional Salt for Rechargeable
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Pengfei Sang, Shuai Tang, Fengli Li, Yubing Si, and Yongzhu Fu*
The practical application of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries is hindered by the
severe shuttle eﬀect of soluble polysulﬁde intermediates and the unstable
lithium anode interface. Conventional lithium salts (e.g., LiPF6, LiTFSI) just
serve as conducting salts to provide necessary free lithium cations for internal
ion transport, lacking full utilization of the anions. Herein, lithium
4-ﬂuorobenzenethiolate (F-PhSLi) as a multifunctional salt for rechargeable
Li–S batteries, which is able to chemically react with sulfur to alter the redox
pathway of sulfur cathode, accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics, and inhibit the
shuttle eﬀect of polysulﬁdes is reported. Meanwhile, due to the redox activity
of F-PhSLi, the reactive electrolyte can oﬀer additional capacity. In addition, it
also can construct a stable LiF-rich solid electrolyte interface layer on the
lithium metal anode. Such reactive electrolyte endows Li–S batteries with
ultrahigh discharge speciﬁc capacity, improved sulfur utilization, long-term
storage ability, enhanced rate capability, and outstanding low-temperature
performance. This work presents a new solution for developing high
performance Li–S batteries.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered as one of the
promising candidates to lithium-ion batteries due to the advan-
tages of high theoretical speciﬁc energy (2600 Wh kg−1), envi-
ronmental friendliness, and low cost.[1] However, several chal-
lenges exist hindering the practical application of Li–S batteries,
such as 1) the shuttle eﬀect of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs)
intermediates; 2) the unstable interface between electrolyte and
lithium metal anode, resulting in the continuous decomposi-
tion of electrolyte.[2] Numerous attempts have been proposed to
address these problems, such as use of polar host materials,[3]
development of polymer cathode materials,[4] modiﬁcation of

As the “blood” of batteries, the elec-

explored, which enable enhanced sulfur utilization under low
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratios.[11] Current electrolyte engineering
is mainly focused on the optimization of solvents, with fewer re-
ports on new lithium salts due to their more complex design and
synthesis. However, salt has a signiﬁcant impact on cell perfor-
mance via anion decomposition involved in the construction of
the interface layer and composition regulation. The exploration
of lithium salts with functional anions is of great signiﬁcance
in broadening the lithium salt types and understanding anionic
chemistry.
Organic thiolate, lithium 4-ﬂuorobenzenethiolate (F-PhSLi)
with low dissociation in 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DOL/DME) solvents has been conﬁrmed to have an anion-
dominated solvation structure at conventional concentration,
which is able to induce the formation of stable anion-derived in-
terfacial layers.[12] Here, we report the F-PhSLi as a multifunc-
tional salt for constructing high-performance rechargeable Li–
S batteries (Figure 1). Concretely, the F-PhSLi electrolyte could
react with sulfur cathode and participate in the electrochemical
redox process of sulfur to provide additional capacity. The gen-
erated organopolysulﬁdes show improved redox kinetics, sup-
pressed shuttle eﬀect, and increased deposition dimension of
Li2S. Meanwhile, F-PhSLi electrolyte could also help construct

2. Results and Discussion
To investigate the reactivity of F-PhSLi with sulfur, visualized
experiments were conducted. The yellow F-PhSLi electrolyte in-
stantly turned to reddish–brown when mixed with sulfur powder
(Figure 2a), indicating that the reaction between F-PhSLi and ele-
ment sulfur occurred. Raman spectroscopy was used to reveal the
reaction. Compared with the F-PhSLi electrolyte, the new scat-
tering peaks at 396, 452, and 530 cm−1 are attributed to the vi-
brations of sulfur─sulfur (S─S) bonds (Figure 2b),[13] suggesting
the formation of polysulﬁdes in the mixture. The sulfur powder
is insoluble in the ether-based electrolyte (Figure S1, Supporting
Information), which excludes the possible interference of the vi-
bration of the S─S bonds in the element sulfur and further con-
ﬁrms the chemical reaction between sulfur and F-PhSLi. Mean-
while, the mass spectroscopy (MS) of the mixture corresponds
well with the spectroscopic result. As shown in Figure 2c, the
peak of the mass/charge fraction (m/z) at 158.9718 belongs to
the formation of lithium organopolysulﬁde (F-PhSSLi). In addi-
tion, the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of 7Li also
veriﬁes the chemical reaction. The F-PhSLi electrolyte shows an
apparent resonance at 0.75 ppm (Figure 2d). The mixture shows
the chemical downshift signal of F-PhSLi due to the increased
electron density of Li in the formed F-PhSSLi.
The Li–Li symmetric cells were assembled to evaluate the be-
havior of Li plating/stripping in the F-PhSLi and LiTFSI elec-
trolytes. At a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and an areal ca-
pacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2, a stable voltage curve with low voltage
hysteresis (30 mV) is obtained during 300 h with the F-PhSLi
electrolyte, while the cell with LiTFSI electrolyte shows a larger
voltage hysteresis (Figure 3a), which conﬁrms that the F-PhSLi
electrolyte has good adaptability to the lithium metal anode. The
enlarged proﬁles of the corresponding plating/stripping curves
are shown in Figure 3b. The morphology of the cycled lithium
metal in the F-PhSLi and LiTFSI electrolytes at diﬀerent cycles
was investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As
shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information), the lithium metal
cycled in the F-PhSLi electrolyte displays a more uniform and
dense structure than that in the LiTFSI electrolyte. Meanwhile,
the thickness of the deposited lithium layers was measured by
cross-sectional SEM (Figure S3, Supporting Information) and the
one using the F-PhSLi electrolyte is thinner than that of using the
LiTFSI electrolyte, indicating the formation of denser SEI layer
on the lithium metal and reduced side reactions between lithium
metal and electrolyte.
The results of the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
(EIS) conform to the diﬀerence of the voltage hysteresis. Figure
S4 (Supporting Information) shows the impedance evolution of
the Li–Li symmetric cells with the two electrolytes. Obviously,
the cell with the F-PhSLi electrolyte exhibits lower interfacial re-
sistances both in the fresh state and 10th cycle compared with
those with the LiTFSI electrolyte, which suggests the F-PhSLi
electrolyte is able to form a stable SEI layer with fast kinetics.
In addition, the cell with the F-PhSLi electrolyte also represents
excellent rate performance as the current density increases from
0.1 to 1.0 mA cm−2 (Figure S5, Supporting Information), and the
voltage hysteresis at 1.0 mA cm−2 is only ≈50 mV. X-ray photo-
electron spectroscopy (XPS) with an Ar+ sputtering depth analy-
sis was carried out on the cycled lithium metal in the Li–Li sym-
metric cell using the F-PhSLi electrolyte after 100 cycles at 0.5 mA
cm−2 to reveal the composition of the SEI. To avoid the poten-
tial inﬂuence of the cleaning solvents, the surface XPS spectrum
is not included. The peaks in the S 2p spectra (Figure 3c) can
be attributed to SOx (167.2 eV),[14] F-PhS−(164.0 eV),[12] PhS−
(162.0 eV),[15] and S2−(160.5 eV).[16] And the peaks in the F 1s
spectra (Figure 3d) can be attributed to C-F (687.4 eV) and Li-F
(685.3 eV).[10b,17] They are all derived from the decomposition of
F-PhSLi. Meanwhile, a high LiF content is observed in the SEI,
which is beneﬁcial to reduce the diﬀusion barrier of Li+ and im-
prove the migration of Li+ at the surface.[18] With the increase
of the etching depth, the composition and content of S and F
elements change negligibly, indicating that a uniform and sta-
ble organic–inorganic hybrid SEI layer is formed in the F-PhSLi
electrolyte.

charged to each desired potential (Figure 4a; and Figure S5a, Sup-
porting Information) for consistency of the test due to the reac-
tivity between the F-PhSLi electrolyte and sulfur, and EIS was

To provide a better understanding of the kinetics properties
of the electrolytes, the ground states of the active intermediates
were calculated by density functional theory (DFT). As shown
in Figure S8a (Supporting Information), for both kinds of ac-
tive intermediates of Li2S4-8 and F-PhS4-8Li, the lowest unoccu-
pied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies decrease with the sul-
fur atom number increases, while the highest occupied molec-
ular orbital (HOMO) energies have minor ﬂuctuations around
−6.00 and −5.70 eV, respectively. It is worth to note that, all
the F-PhS4-8Li intermediates show lower LUMO and higher
HOMO energies than those of Li2S4-8 intermediates, and thus
having narrowed energy gaps, better electron transfer kinetics,
and enhanced electrochemical reactions (Figure S8b, Supporting
Information).[20]
In addition, since the deposition format of Li2S is closely re-
lated to the kinetics of sulfur cathode,[21] current-time transients
(CTTs) tests were performed to reveal the nucleation and growth
of Li2S in both electrolytes. As shown in Figure 4d, no peaks are
observed in the LiTFSI cell due to the ultrafast nucleation/growth
rate,[22] while an obvious peak appears in the F-PhSLi cell corre-
sponding to the nucleation and growth process of Li2S. Four clas-
sical electrochemical deposition models were used to ﬁt the CTTs
proﬁles, and the result in the F-PhSLi electrolyte is consistent
with the 3D instantaneous (3DI) nucleation (Figure 4e). Mean-
while, the cathode morphology of the cells after the CTTs tests
was investigated by SEM. Consistent with the results of CTTs,
the deposition of Li2S presents a 3D model around the CNTs sub-
strate in the F-PhSLi electrolyte and 2D model in the LiTFSI elec-
trolyte (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The 3D deposition
format is able to reduce the passivation of the conductive frame-
work surface, thereby improving the electrode reaction kinetics
and the utilization of the active sulfur.[11c,23]
The oxidation reaction kinetics of the cathode during the
charging process was estimated by potentiostatic intermittent
titration technique (PITT). Speciﬁcally, the fully discharged Li–
S cells were potentiostatically charged from 2.10 to 2.60 V with a
step size of 20 mV, and the cut-oﬀresponse current of each step
is less than 20 μA (Figure 4f). In the LiTFSI electrolyte, the oxida-
tion of Li2S begins at 2.20 V. However, an earlier response current
occurs at 2.10 V in the F-PhSLi electrolyte, indicating the faster
oxidation reaction kinetics, which is supposed to be related to the
3D deposition morphology of Li2S and the activation of Li2S by
the organosulﬁdes.[23] The capacities at each voltage step were
calculated and summarized in Figure S10 (Supporting Informa-

The electrochemical processes of the LiTFSI and F-PhSLi cells
were explored by cyclic voltammetry (CV). As shown in Figure
5a, the F-PhSLi cell exhibits diﬀerent redox process compared
with the LiTFSI cell and blank cathode using F-PhSLi electrolyte
(blank cell). Among them, the LiTFSI cell shows typical redox
peaks of elemental sulfur, and the blank cell shows a pair of redox
peaks related to the cleavage and recombination of S─S bonds,
indicating F-PhSLi electrolyte can not only conduct lithium ions
but also provide additional capacity contribution. While three re-
duction peaks can be observed in the F-PhSLi cell, which prob-
ably correspond to the cleavage of the middle S─S bonds in
the organopolysulﬁdes (F-PhSnPh-F) (F-PhSnPh-F + Li →F-
PhSn/2Li), the formation of F-PhSLi and short-chain LiPSs (F-
PhSn/2Li + Li →F-PhSLi + Li2Sx), and the conversion of LiPSs to
Li2S (Li2Sx + Li →Li2S), respectively.[16b,24] The oxidation process
is reversed. Therefore, F-PhSLi changes and participates in the
redox pathway of sulfur. Meanwhile, the presence of cathodic el-
emental sulfur content after the reaction with F-PhSLi electrolyte
was measured. Concretely, 20 μL 1.0 m F-PhSLi electrolyte was
dropped on the sulfur cathode with a sulfur loading of 1.2 mg
and then washed by DME solvent; the washed sulfur cathode was
assembled with lithium metal anode using LiTFSI electrolyte.

involved in the reaction at low sulfur loading. The cycling perfor-

The charge–discharge curves of both cells at second cycle are
shown in Figure S14a (Supporting Information), and the F-PhSLi
cell exhibits a lower initial charge potential barrier, which is
consistent with the previous PITT results. Meanwhile, the over-
lapped charge–discharge curves of the F-PhSLi cell at diﬀerent
cycles indicate the excellent reversibility of the redox reactions
(Figure S14b, Supporting Information). To explore the redox
mechanism of the F-PhSLi cell, MS was introduced to conﬁrm
the recharge/discharge products. Figure S15a–c (Supporting In-
formation) show the m/z of the recharged products, correspond-
ing to the structure of F-PhS2Ph-F, F-PhS3Ph-F, and F-PhS4Ph-
F. Figure S15d (Supporting Information) presents the m/z of the
discharged products, corresponding to the protonated F-PhSH,
which is the form of F-PhSLi in the MS analysis in the atmo-
spheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) mode. From these
results, we propose that elemental sulfur is ﬁrst transformed
into F-PhS2Li after adding F-PhSLi electrolyte, and the initial
discharged products are F-PhSLi and Li2S, while the recharged
products are F-PhSnPh-F (n = 2,3,4). Subsequent redox process
is based on the above-mentioned reversible discharge-recharge
products (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
The excellent rate performance of the F-PhSLi cell is shown in
Figure 5c. Concretely, the discharge capacities of the F-PhSLi cell
at 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.5 C, and 1.0 C rates are 1542.7, 1454.8, 1332.5,
and 1226.7 mAh gS
−1, respectively. When the rate returns to 0.1
C, the discharge capacity is 1505.7 mAh gS
−1, which is higher
than that of the LiTFSI cell, suggesting improved reaction kinet-

low self-discharge rate during a long-term idling period of 180
days and the capacity loss is only 2.4% owing to the inhibition of
shuttle eﬀect (Figure S17, Supporting Information).

cyclability of F-PhSLi cell under various conditions were studied.
The cycling performance of the F-PhSLi cell at 1.0 C rate is shown
in Figure S18 (Supporting Information), which delivers a capac-
ity of 1258.8 mAh gS
−1 after 100 cycles. Even at high rate of 2.0
C (Figure 5d), the F-PhSLi cell could still deliver a discharge ca-
pacity of 966.8 mAh gS
−1 with a capacity retention of 72.5% after
200 cycles. Figure S19 (Supporting Information) displays the cy-
cling stability of the F-PhSLi cell with a sulfur mass loading of
2.8 and 3.8 mg cm−2 at 0.1 C rate. The F-PhSLi cells show an ini-
tial capacity of 1250.5 and 903.1 mAh gS

In addition, F-PhSLi cells with diﬀerent salt concentrations (0.5,
1.0, and 2.0 m) were explored (Figure S20, Supporting Informa-
tion). They all deliver stable performance and the discharged spe-
ciﬁc capacity increases with the increase of the active F-PhSLi salt
concentration. Furthermore, the cycling performance at −20 °C
was also performed. As shown in Figure S21 (Supporting Infor-
mation), the F-PhSLi cell shows a gradually increasing speciﬁc
capacity during the preliminary cycles and then stabilizes at over
1300 mAh gS
−1 after 50 cycles. The excellent low temperature per-
formance can be attributed to the fast kinetics, suppressed shuttle
eﬀect of polysulﬁdes, and stable electrolyte–electrode interface.
When the F-PhSLi electrolyte was applied in a single-layer Li–
S pouch cell, additional 0.5 m LiTFSI was added for suﬃcient
ion transport and the pouch cell also remains decent cycling per-
formance with an initial capacity of 1000.2 mAh gS
−1 and a ca-
pacity retention of 81.7% after 30 cycles (Figure 5e; and Figure
S22, Supporting Information). The above electrochemical perfor-
mance indicates the great application potential of the reactive F-
PhSLi electrolyte in Li–S batteries, and some recent reports on
the electrolytes of Li–S batteries are compared in Figure S23 and
Table S1 (Supporting Information).
3. Conclusion
In summary, a thiolate-based (F-PhSLi) reactive electrolyte is pro-
posed to construct high-performance Li–S batteries. On the cath-
ode side, F-PhSLi is highly reactive to sulfur, which not only acts
as the conductive salt to provide necessary ion transport, but also
changes the redox pathway of sulfur to inhibit the shuttle eﬀect,
regulate the deposition of Li2S, improve the redox reaction kinet-
ics, and contribute additional capacity as an active component.
On the anode side, the F-PhSLi electrolyte can help construct a
stable LiF-rich SEI layer on the lithium metal anode and suppress
the lithium dendrite growth. Due to the multifunctionality of the
F-PhSLi electrolyte, the Li–S cells show ultrahigh speciﬁc capac-
ity of 1618.9 mAh gS
−1, ultralong storage capability of low self-
discharge rate with a capacity loss of only 2.4% after 180 days
idling, and excellent low-temperature performance of over 1300
mAh gS
−1 at −20 °C. This work will inspire more exploration of
conductive salt anion design in electrolyte engineering, and the
reactive electrolyte strategy is expected to be applied to other bat-
tery systems, such as Na-S and Li-O2.
