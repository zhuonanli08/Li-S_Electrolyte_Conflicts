The design of an eﬃcient catalyst is crucial for sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) in lean electrolyte lithium- 
sulfur (Li–S) batteries. However, most existing catalysts primarily focus on polysulﬁde-catalyst interac- 
tions and generally demonstrate high activity only with an excessive electrolyte. In this study, we propose 
high-entropy alloy (HEA) composite to enhance the SRR under lean-electrolyte conditions by optimizing 
the solvent-catalyst interaction. As evidenced by in situ spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), 
density functional theory (DFT) calculations, and experimental investigations, the strong interaction be- 
tween the solvent and HEA catalysts enables the Li–S battery to achieve a high capacity of 1007.9 mAh/g 
with a low E/S ratio of 10 μL/mg, and capacity retention of 77 % after 200 cycles at lower E/S ratio of 
4.5 μL/mg. The enhanced SRR performance in lean electrolyte conditions is primarily attributed to d–d 
electron interactions among the constituent metals and the presence of multiple active sites with op- 
timized energy barriers in the HEA catalysts. This work underscores the signiﬁcance of solvent-catalyst 
interactions in regulating the SRR of lean electrolyte Li–S battery and offers insights into the rational 
design of HEA catalysts to advance Li−S batteries.

Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries hold immense promise for next- 
generation energy storage due to their ultrahigh theoretical energy 
density (2600 Wh/kg) and cost-effectiveness [ 1–5 ]. Conventional 
Li−S batteries utilize a sulfur-based cathode of elemental sulfur 
(S8 ), coupled with a lithium metal anode. These batteries depend 
on an ether-based electrolyte to facilitate ionic transport during 
electrochemical cycling [ 6–9 ]. During discharge, the conversion of 
S8 to lithium sulﬁde (Li2 S) occurs through a solid-liquid-solid re- 
action mechanism, which is mediated by soluble lithium polysul- 
ﬁde (LiPS) intermediates [ 10–12 ]. These soluble LiPSs circumvent 
the substantial reaction barrier associated with the direct solid- 
solid conversion between S8 and Li2 S, facilitating eﬃcient energy 
delivery in Li−S batteries [ 13–15 ]. Consequently, LiPSs play a cru- 
cial role in bridging the conversion between solid S8 and Li2 S, mak-

LiPSs are solvated by solvent molecules such as 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and their electrochemical 
behaviors are signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by the electrolyte amount 
corresponding to the concentration of LiPSs [ 19 ]. Typically, the sul- 
fur reduction reaction (SRR) shows high activity when there is an 
excess of electrolyte present (low LiPS concentration), which en- 
sures high sulfur utilization and excellent rate capability in Li−S 
batteries [ 20 , 21 ]. However, an excessive amount of electrolyte can 
hinder the full realization of the high-energy-density advantages of 
Li–S batteries [ 22–24 ]. Consequently, it is crucial to minimize the 
dosage of electrolytes used in Li–S batteries. Unfortunately, the SRR 
becomes markedly sluggish under conditions of a lean electrolyte- 
to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, resulting in increased viscosity of the LiPSs- 
contained electrolyte, reducing ion conductivity and slowing down 
lithium-ion (Li+ ) diffusion [ 25–27 ]. As a result, the performance of 
Li–S batteries deteriorates, leading to decreased discharge capacity 
and limited cycling rates [ 28 ]. Furthermore, it has been overlooked

This depletion results in capacity decay and, ultimately, battery 
failure [ 31–33 ]. These issues are exacerbated when the usage of

lyst that enhances the sulfur reduction activity in lean electrolyte 
conditions while mitigating electrolyte loss to improve cycle stabil- 
ity simultaneously. 
Most reported catalysts are designed to facilitate the SRR in 
lean electrolytes, including various metals [ 6 ] and their com- 
pounds, such as sulﬁdes [ 34 ], nitrides [ 35 ], oxides [ 36 ], and 
carbides [ 37 ] that possess strong anchoring sites [ 38 ]. These 
solutions including our previous work [ 39 ] typically focus on con- 
trolling the interactions between the catalyst and LiPSs [ 40–42 ]. 
However, it is essential to note that the solvation of LiPSs occurs 
before the electrochemical reduction on the catalyst surface [ 19 ]. 
Consequently, the local solvent environment around the catalyst 
plays a pivotal role in LiPS conversions, which are determined by 
solvent aﬃnity for the catalyst surface [ 29 ]. The oxygen atoms 
in these solvent molecules possess exposed 2p-orbital electrons, 
which readily hybridize with the d-orbitals of metal catalysts [ 43 ]. 
By elevating the metal d-band center, the antibonding orbitals 
(d-p∗) formed through d-p hybridization are shifted to higher 
energy levels, thereby strengthening the interaction between the 
catalyst and solvent molecules [ 44 ]. Thus, a catalyst with a high 
d-band center and an increased number of solvent molecule ad- 
sorption sites is expected to promote LiPS conversion and mitigate 
electrolyte loss in lean electrolyte conditions. 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have garnered signiﬁcant interest as 
emerging catalytic materials in recent years [ 45 , 46 ]. HEAs consist 
of ﬁve or more elements to form a solid solution phase, resulting 
in several unique characteristics of HEAs [ 47 ]. Firstly, the different 
composition allows for effective regulation of microstructure and 
electron distribution, resulting in surfaces with numerous distinct 
binding site environments [ 48 ]. This leads to a near-continuum 
distribution of adsorption energies, thereby enhancing the kinet- 
ics of the SRR [ 49 ]. Secondly, the high entropy effect contributes 
to the thermodynamic stability of HEAs [ 50 ]. Thirdly, the lattice 
distortion optimizes the electronic structure, promoting ion dif- 
fusion [ 51 ]. Lastly, the “cocktail” effect enhances catalytic activity 
through unexpected synergies among the various elements [ 50 ]. 
In short, these intrinsic characteristics ensure that HEA materials 
exhibit good activity under lean electrolyte conditions and mini- 
mize electrolyte loss, positioning them as promising catalysts for 
application in Li−S batteries. Therefore, it is essential to develop 
effective HEA catalysts to explore strong interaction between elec- 
trolytes and catalysts, as well as to enhance the SRR under lean- 
electrolyte conditions. 
In this study, we selected Ni, Fe, Co, and Cu as doped metal- 
lic elements due to their high abundance, similar atomic radii, and 
individual catalytic capacities, which can likely form a single and 
stable solid solution with Pt. Based on this premise, we fabricated 
the hollow carbon sphere (HCS) supported PtCuFeCoNi HEA cata- 
lyst (referred to as HEA/HCS), which demonstrates strong binding 
with DOL and DME molecules by density functional theory (DFT) 
calculation and experimental study and serves as a highly active 
catalyst for the SRR under lean electrolyte conditions. The cathode 
kinetics is systematically evaluated by decoupling the kinetic polar- 
izations under working conditions to identify the key kinetic limit- 
ing factor of Li2 S nucleation process in lean-electrolyte Li−S batter- 
ies. The HEA/HCS exhibits a high speciﬁc capacity of 1007.9 mAh/g 
at 0.2 C under a low E/S ratio of 10 μL/mg, maintaining stability 
over 200 cycles with an 80 % retention rate. Furthermore, it de- 
livers a high reversible capacity of 455 mAh/g and retains stability 
over 200 stable cycles under a lean E/S ratio of 4.5 μL/mg. DFT cal- 
culations conﬁrm that the strong d–d interactions among the con-

illustrated in Fig. 1 . X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is performed 
to elucidate the crystal structure of the as-synthesized HEA, with 
detailed results presented in Fig. 2 a. The three distinct diffraction 
peaks observed at 2 θ angles of 41.8 °, 49.1 °, and 71.6 ° correspond 
to the (111), (200), and (220) facets of Cu3 Pt (PDF #65–3247) 
[ 52 ], respectively. This suggests a homogeneous face-centered 
cubic (FCC) conﬁguration within the HEA, analogous to that of the 
Cu3 Pt alloy. The morphology of the HEA/HCS is explored using 
a transmission electron microscope (TEM), which reveals that 
the shape of the HCS remains intact while its surface is adorned 
with numerous small HEA nanoparticles ( Fig. 2 b). The high-angle 
annular dark ﬁeld scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) image further 
conﬁrms the FCC nanocrystalline structure of the HEA ( Fig. 2 c).

To investigate the interaction between solvent molecules and 
HEA/HCS electrodes, contact angle measurements were performed. 
The lower contact angle observed on the HEA/HCS electrode in- 
dicates a greater aﬃnity of the electrolyte solvent for the HEA 
surface (Fig. S5 in Supporting information). In situ Raman spec- 
troscopy was utilized to further characterize the local solvent envi- 
ronment and solvent retention on the catalyst surface. The volatile 
nature of DOL/DME leads to their easy evaporation from the ma- 
terial surface. As shown in Fig. S6a (Supporting information), the 
time-dependent Raman spectra of the HEA/HCS electrode surface 
with DOL/DME adsorption were recorded, and the corresponding 
contour patterns are illustrated in Fig. 2 h. The peaks at 747, 1454, 
and 2916 cm-1 are attributed to DOL/DME molecules [ 29 ]. Notably, 
the strong binding between the HEA/HCS electrode and the solvent 
results in a prolonged retention time of 12 min. In contrast, solvent 
molecules on the Pt/HCS and Cu3 Pt/HCS electrode exhibit retention 
times of only 4 and 8 min ( Figs. 2 i and j, Figs. S6b and c in Sup- 
porting information). These ﬁndings conﬁrm that the HEA catalyst 
possesses a signiﬁcant aﬃnity for solvent molecules and demon- 
strates a strong adsorption capacity, effectively reducing electrolyte 
consumption. 
The quantitative analysis of the adsorption capacity of three 
catalysts with solvent molecules is essential for a comprehensive 
evaluation of the catalyst activity. Consequently, ultraviolet-visible 
spectroscopy (UV-vis) was employed to quantify solvent adsorp- 
tion on the different catalysts. Fig. 3 a displays a digital photograph 
of standard solutions containing DOL/DME solvent, while Fig. 3 b 
illustrates the corresponding UV-vis absorption spectra. The peak 
observed at 218 nm corresponds to the C-O-C bonds in DOL/DME 
[ 54 ], enabling the establishment of a standard correlation between 
solvent volume and UV-vis absorbance. Consequently, the UV-vis 
absorbance demonstrates a proportional relationship with solvent 
volume ( Fig. 3 c). From Fig. 3 d, the supernatant of HEA/HCS exhibits 
the lowest peak intensity after adsorption of DOL/DME, indicat- 
ing the strongest interaction between HEAs and solvent molecules. 
Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 3 e, the HEA/HCS is quantiﬁed 
with a solvent adsorption capacity of 35.93 μL/mg, which signif- 
icantly exceeds the capacities of 27.18 and 22.32 μL/mg for the 
Cu3 Pt/HCS and Pt/HCS, respectively. 
To further investigate the solvent molecule capture ability of 
the three catalysts at the atomic level, ﬁrst-principles calculations 
were conducted to determine the adsorption energies of DOL/DME 
on each catalyst. Based on XRD data, we constructed an atomic 
model of the (111) crystal plane of the FeCoNiCuPt HEA, which 
serves as the active surface for DOL/DME adsorption and polysul- 
ﬁde catalysis. Considering that the oxygen 2p-orbitals in solvent 
molecules readily hybridize with the d-orbitals of metal catalysts 
to form metal-oxygen (M−O) bonds, we prioritized this bonding 
scenario when constructing the adsorption model. To identify the 
optimal adsorption sites in the HEA and Cu3 Pt, adsorption mod- 
els for DOL at different metal sites were constructed (Fig. S7 in 
Supporting information). As illustrated in Fig. 3 f, the adsorption 
energies of DOL on Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Pt sites are 2.16, 2.10, 
2.06, 1.91, and 1.77 eV, respectively. This data indicates that the Fe 
site, exhibiting the highest adsorption energy, preferentially forms 
bonds with oxygen, thereby establishing it as the most active site 
for DOL/DME adsorption. The slightly lower adsorption energies of 
the Co and Ni sites imply that they also act as signiﬁcant active 
sites for adsorption. Similarly, Cu serves as the best active site in 
Cu3 Pt. Based on this, we further constructed models for the opti- 
mal DME adsorption sites in both the HEA and Cu3 Pt ( Fig. 3 g and 
Fig. S8 in Supporting information). For pure Pt, we built models 
for DOL/DME adsorption at the Pt site (Fig. S9 in Supporting in- 
formation) and calculated their corresponding adsorption energies, 
which are shown in Fig. 3 f. It can be observed that the adsorption 
energy of the HEA catalyst (2.16/2.45 eV) for DOL/DME is higher 
than that of the Cu3 Pt (1.69/2.08 eV) and Pt (0.90/1.24 eV) cat- 
alysts. This suggests that the HEA catalyst demonstrates a strong 
anchoring ability for DOL/DME, effectively preventing the loss of 
solvent molecules and thereby enabling high capacity and long cy- 
cle life for Li−S batteries under lean electrolyte conditions. 
To understand the origin of the high adsorption activity of HEA 
in lean electrolytes, we calculated the total density of states (TDOS) 
and d-band center of the three catalysts. As shown in Figs. 3 h- 
j, the presence of ferromagnetic elements such as Fe, Co, and Ni 
induces an asymmetric distribution of spin-up and spin-down d 
orbital electrons within the HEA. The high-energy spin-up or spin- 
down d orbitals, located near the Fermi level ( Ef ) play a crucial role 
in enhancing the interaction between the catalysts and DOL/DME 
molecules [ 55 ]. Compared to the spin-up d orbitals (d-band center: 
−2.24 eV), the spin-down d orbitals of HEA (d-band center: −1.20 
eV) occupy higher energy levels, making a dominant contribution 
to adsorption activity. In comparison to Cu3 Pt (−1.87 eV) and Pt 
(−2.28 eV), HEA (−1.20 eV) exhibited a higher d-band center, sug- 
gesting that the upward shift of the d-band center may lead to the 
formation of more empty anti-bonding states, resulting in stronger 
binding energy ( Fig. 4 a). 
Exploring the role of each element in the HEA is crucial for 
gaining a deeper understanding of the intrinsic mechanism gov- 
erning the interaction between HEA and DOL/DME. The partial 
density of states (PDOS) and d-band centers of each element 
in the HEA are calculated. As illustrated in Fig. 4 b and Fig. S10 
(Supporting information), the 3d orbitals of the ﬁve elements sig- 
niﬁcantly overlap, indicating strong interactions between the d-d 
orbital electrons of the metals in the HEA. This strong interaction 
not only facilitates electron transfer between different metal sites

To gain insight into the role of “cocktail” effect in DOL/DME ad- 
sorption with the HEA, charge density analyses were conducted for 
the HEA, Cu3 Pt, and Pt catalysts, both before and after their inter- 
actions with DOL/DME. As shown in Figs. S11a and b (Supporting 
information), the adsorption of DOL/DME on Pt and Cu3 Pt results 
solely in a decrease in charge density at the adsorption site. In 
contrast, in the case of the HEA, the charge density at most sites 
on the catalyst surface signiﬁcantly decreases, especially at the Fe, 
Co, and Ni, which act as electron-deﬁcient sites (Fig. S11c in Sup- 
porting information). These ﬁndings suggest the presence of elec- 
tronic synergistic effects among the multi-metallic sites within the 
HEA, which not only enhance the adsorption of DOL/DME solvent 
molecules but also expedite the conversion of polysulﬁdes under 
lean electrolyte conditions. 
Under lean electrolyte conditions, the stability of the DOL/DME 
solvent system is crucial for the performance of Li−S batteries. The 
crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) method was employed 
to analyze the inﬂuence of the catalyst on the internal structural 
stability of the solvent molecules [ 29 ]. We followed the conven- 
tional approach of displaying −COHP, where regions with −COHP 
> 0 indicate bonding contributions, while regions with −COHP < 
0 signify antibonding contributions. We calculated the COHP for all 
C–O and C–C bonds marked in Fig. 3 g, which are shown in Fig. 4 c 
and Fig. S12 (Supporting information). The populations of bonding 
orbitals ﬁlling the C–O and C–C bonds on HEA below the Ef are 
signiﬁcantly larger than that of the antibonding orbitals. This 
observation indicates that these bonds within DOL/DME on the 
HEA exhibit strong stability, allowing the DOL/DME to maintain a 
stable state, thereby ensuring the stability of the lean electrolyte 
solvent system. Moreover, we calculated the corresponding energy 
values of the COHP integrated up to the Ef (ICOHP) to quantita- 
tively evaluate the strength of C–O and C–C bonds in DOL/DME 
on different catalysts. As shown in Tables S1 and S2 (Supporting 
information), the ICOHP values for the C–O and C–C bonds on the 
HEA are more negative than those on Pt and Cu3 Pt, indicating that 
the internal bonds of the solvent molecules adsorbed on the HEA 
are more stable. Additionally, a comparison of the ICOHP values 
for the C–O and C–C bonds before adsorption, and after adsorption

Under low E/S ratio conditions, the high viscosity of the 
LiPS-contained electrolyte diminishes the ionic conductivity and 
impedes Li+ diffusion, which is a key factor affecting the SRR [ 59 ]. 
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) at variable scan rates was performed 
on different cathodes ( Fig. 5 a, Figs. S14a and b in Supporting 
information). The peak currents and their corresponding scan 
rate square roots for the various peaks in the CV proﬁles were 
subjected to linear ﬁtting, allowing for determining the Li+ diffu- 
sion coeﬃcient ( DLi+ ) using the classical Randles–Sevcik equation 
(Note S1 in Supporting information) [ 60 ]. As shown in Fig. 5 b and 
Fig. S14c (Supporting information), the HEA/HCS cathode exhibits 
consistently steeper slopes compared to the Pt/HCS and Cu3 Pt/HCS 
cathodes, indicating its superior ability to facilitate Li+ diffusion 
and signiﬁcantly enhanced SRR even in low electrolyte condi- 
tions. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5 c and Fig. S14d (Supporting 
information), the estimated DLi+ values for cells with HEA/HCS 
cathode during the Li2 Sn → Li2 S are 3.04 × 10−8 cm2 /s (S8 → Li2 Sn : 
0.99 × 10−8 cm2 /s), which are higher than those for cells with 
Cu3 Pt/HCS (1.55 × 10−8 , 0.56 × 10−8 cm2 /s) and Pt/HCS cathodes 
(0.84 × 10−8 , 0.44 × 10−8 cm2 /s). The high Li+ diffusion coeﬃcient 
under lean-electrolyte conditions can be attributed to three key 
factors that work synergistically. (1) First, the hollow carbon 
sphere substrate provides a continuous conductive network that 
facilitates electron transfer while simultaneously offering abundant 
channels for ion transport. (2) Second, the unique electronic struc- 
ture of the HEA catalyst, characterized by strong d-d interactions 
among the constituent metals, creates multiple active sites that 
effectively lower the energy barrier for Li+ migration. (3) Most 
importantly, the HEA/HCS exhibits exceptional solvent retention 
capability, which helps maintain a stable solvation environment 
for Li+ even under electrolyte-starved conditions. This ﬁnding 
further substantiates the higher electrocatalytic activity of HEAs, 
which accelerates the reduction reaction kinetics of LiPSs under 
low electrolyte conditions. 
The discharge process can often be attributed to three stages: 
sulfur dissolution, Li2 S nucleation, and Li2 S growth [ 7 ]. To pin- 
point the key kinetic rate-determining step in lean-electrolyte Li−S 
batteries, the kinetic voltages of the sulfur cathode were system- 
atically evaluated across varying E/S ratios during the discharge 
process ( Figs. 5 d-f). For the Pt/HCS and Cu3 Pt/HCS cathodes, the 
kinetic voltage during the sulfur dissolution stage decreases by 
approximately 210 and 110 mV as the E/S ratio changes from 
30 μL/mg to 4.5 μL/mg ( Fig. 5 g). In contrast, the Li2 S nucleation 
stage exhibits a decrease of around 270 and 130 mV, suggesting 
that the kinetics of Li2 S nucleation are signiﬁcantly affected by the 
reduction in electrolyte quantity ( Fig. 5 h). Furthermore, the kinetic 
voltage of the Li2 S growth stage exhibits a decrease of 250/120 mV 
( Fig. 5 i), suggesting that the progressively reduced E/S ratio ulti- 
mately impairs the reaction kinetics of the Li2 S growth process. For 
the HEA/HCS cathode, the kinetic voltages for the three stages are 
reduced by 50, 60, and 50 mV, respectively. All values are smaller 
than those of the other two cathodes. In summary, the kinetic 
challenges associated with lean-electrolyte Li−S batteries primarily 
result from sluggish cathode kinetics at the Li2 S nucleation stage 
during the discharge process. The strong interaction between the 
HEA catslyst and the solvent signiﬁcantly enhances the SRR kinet- 
ics under lean-electrolyte conditions, particularly during the Li2 S 
nucleation stage. 
To verify the enhanced catalytic activity of HEA/HCS in regu- 
lating Li2 S nucleation with a lean electrolyte, Li2 S nucleation tests 
were conducted with the addition of only 15 μL of electrolyte. The 
nucleation curves are presented in Figs. 5 j-l, the nucleation of Li2 S 
using HEA/HCS electrode demonstrates a higher Li2 S precipitation 
capacity (232.1 mAh/g) compared to Cu3 Pt/HCS (106.3 mAh/g) and 
Pt/HCS (76.6 mAh/g) electrodes. This ﬁnding substantiates the su- 
perior capability of HEAs to catalyze the conversion of LiPSs into

Additionally, the high catalytic activity of the HEA/HCS un- 
der lean-electrolyte conditions is further evidenced by its lower 
charge/discharge overpotentials compared to those of batteries us- 
ing Cu3 Pt/HCS and Pt/HCS cathodes. To reveal the source of the 
increased polarization under lean-electrolyte conditions, the dis- 
charge polarizations were decoupled and analyzed systematically 
by galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) curves ( Figs. 6 d-h). 
During the discharge process, a slight increase in the total overpo- 
tential ( ηtotal ) is observed at the sulfur dissolution stage, attributed 
to the diffusion of LiPSs into the electrolyte, consequently increas- 
ing its viscosity ( Fig. 6 i). Conversely, at the Li2 S nucleation and 
growth stage, ηtotal signiﬁcantly increases due to the deposition of 
insulating Li2 S on the electrodes. In comparison to Cu3 Pt/HCS and 
Pt/HCS cathodes, the HEA/HCS composite exhibits a lower ηtotal , 
demonstrating that the strong interaction between the HEA com- 
posite and the solvent largely mitigates kinetic challenges under 
lean-electrolyte conditions. Furthermore, the ηtotal was decoupled 
into ηac , ηcon , and ηohm using a GITT–electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) method ( Fig. 6 j, Figs. S16a and b in Supporting 
information) [ 7 , 62 ]. ηohm is the loss of voltage due to ion conduc- 
tion in the electrolyte. ηcon values for the three catalysts remain 
close and stabilize during discharge (Fig. S16c in Supporting infor- 
mation). ηcon arises from differences in concentration of the reac- 
tion species between the electrode surface and the electrolyte. In 
contrast, the ηac signiﬁcantly increased during the Li2 S nucleation 
stage ( Fig. 6 k). ηac represents the polarization required for charge 
transfer at the interface, reﬂecting the activation energy of the SRR. 
Thus, the rapid rise of ηac at Li2 S nucleation stage is considered to 
be the key kinetic limiting factor under lean-electrolyte conditions. 
Additionally, the ηac of the HEA/HCS cathode is lower than that 
of the Pt/HCS and Cu3 Pt/HCS cathodes, further demonstrating that 
the strong interaction between the HEA and solvent effectively re- 
duces ηac under lean electrolyte conditions.

74.1 % for Cu3 Pt/HCS and 67.3 % for Pt/HCS cathodes (Fig. S17 in 
Supporting information). This observation indicates that these cat-

In summary, by introducing HEA/HCS, Cu3 Pt/HCS, and Pt/HCS 
to interact with DME/DOL molecules, we demonstrate that the 
catalyst-solvent interaction signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the lean- 
electrolyte SRR activity, electrolyte consumption, and stability of 
Li−S batteries. The strong interaction between the HEA catalyst 
and DOL/DME molecules enhances lean-electrolyte SRR activity, 
resulting in faster Li+ diffusion, increased Li2 S deposition, and 
reduced polarization. Furthermore, DFT calculations reveal the 
strong d–d interaction in constituent metals can regulate the 
electronic structures of the different metals and create multi- 
active sites within the HEA catalyst. This study suggests that
