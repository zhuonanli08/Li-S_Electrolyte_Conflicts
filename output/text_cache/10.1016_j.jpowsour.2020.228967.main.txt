Promoting the conversion of Li2S by functional additives phenyl diselenide 
in Lithium–Sulfur batteries

H I G H L I G H T S 
• Phenyl diselenide (PDSe) is added into the electrolyte of Li–S batteries. 
• Se–Se bond in PDSe is broken and Li–S bond in Li2S is elongated induced by PDSe. 
• PDSe promotes the oxidation of Li2S. 
• PDSe greatly reduces the deposition of Li2S particles in the lithium anode. 
• Thus, the electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries is significantly improved.

A B S T R A C T  
Lithium–Sulfur (Li–S) battery is a promising device in the field of electrochemical energy storage since its high 
specific capacity and low cost. However, the low insoluble Li2S conversion efficiency and uncontrollable 
deposition on the electrodes lead to low sulfur utilization, poor reversibility and rapid capacity decay. Conse­
quently, the practical application of Li–S battery is limited. Herein, phenyl diselenide (PDSe) is added into the 
conventional electrolyte try to improve the batteries performance. The results show that the Se–Se bond in PDSe 
is broken and the Li–S bond in Li2S is elongated induced by PDSe. Moreover, PDSe promotes Li2S conversion and 
reduces Li2S deposition on the electrodes. As a result, the batteries using the electrolyte with PDSe exhibit 
enhanced discharge capacity and improved rate performance. Particularly, the batteries with 5.0 wt% PDSe 
additive exhibit a high initial discharge capacity of 1585.6 mAh g−1, even after 300 cycles at 0.5 C, the batteries 
still maintain 916.5 mAh g−1, which is much higher than conventional batteries of 618.7 mA h g−1.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries have become one of the most prom­
ising high-energy-density batteries due to the high theoretical specific 
capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and high theoretical energy density (2600 W h 
kg−1) [1–4]. Also, sulfur has advantages of rich in resources, low in 
price, and environmentally friendly [5,6]. Unlike intercalation-based 
lithium-ion batteries, Li–S batteries operate by multistep conversion of 
elemental sulfur (S8) and lithium sulfide (Li2S). During the multistep 
conversion, the dissolution of polysulfide species with different chain 
lengths in the electrolyte and the irreversible deposition of insulating 
Li2S on the electrodes result in poor cycle performance [7]. To solve 
these problems, great efforts have been made including design of porous 
cathode material [8,9], polysulfide-blocking interlayers [10–12], and 
functional electrolyte additives [13–15]. For example, dimethyl disul­
fide (DMDS)-containing [16] and dimethyl trisulfide (DMTS)-containing 
[17] electrolyte promote an alternate electrochemical reaction pathway 
without intermediate polysulfide. Therefore, the batteries exhibit 
significantly boosted electrochemical performance. 
However, the key issues in lithium-sulfur batteries have not been 
completely solved. The insulated and insoluble short-chain polysulfides 
(Li2S2/Li2S) in electrolyte result in poor kinetics of the second-plateau 
reaction (corresponding 3/4 of the theoretical specific capacity). The 
passivation of the electrode is related to repeated and uncontrollable

deposition of insulating Li2S, which decreases electrode interface ac­
tivity and causes battery failure. In addition, the high initial reaction 
over-potential (up to 4 V) is an obstacle to Li2S as cathode material in 
lithium batteries [18]. Therefore, the insolubility and insulation of the 
discharge products (Li2S) lead to a series of serious problems, such as 
severe polarization, low utilization of active materials, low specific ca­
pacity and poor cycle stability in Li–S batteries [19,20]. 
As reported, selenium element has higher electronic conductivity (1 
× 10−3 S m−1), compared with sulfur element (5 × 10−28 S m−1) [21], 
and each selenium atom can take up two Li+ and two e−on electro­
chemical reduction in the lithium batteries [22]. Phenyl diselenide 
(PDSe) has been studied as a cathode material in lithium batteries, 
delivering a theoretical specific capacity of 171.7 mAh g−1 based on the 
2e−reduction reaction [23]. However, its solubility in liquid organic 
electrolytes results in low specific capacity and poor cycling perfor­
mance. In order to increase its specific capacity, PDSe was mixed with 
sulfur in a molar ratio of 1: 1 and 1: 2 to generate phenyl selenosulfide 
(PhSe-S-SePh) and phenyl selenodisulfide (PhSe–SS–SePh), respectively. 
The hybrid materials exhibit stable cycling performance due to the 
reduced solubility of the discharge products, but the initial discharge 
specific capacity of 252 mAh g−1 and 330 mAh g−1 is still very low 
compared to Li–S batteries [21]. For given cathode consisted by Li2S and 
PDSe, it has found that the Li in Li2S was attracted by Se in PDSe, and 
this phenomenon was defined as “inductive effect” [24]. As a result of 
inductive effect, Li–S bond in Li2S is elongated and Se–Se bond in PDSe is 
breakage. The lower activation energy of Li2S caused by elongated Li–S 
bond promotes the oxidation of Li2S in lithium metal battery and leads 
to the decrease of the charging over-potential of Li2S. Inspired by the 
inductive effect of PDSe and Li2S, besides, due to the good conductivity 
and solubility of PDSe in electrolyte, PDSe was added into electrolyte in 
this work expected to promote Li2S oxidation and thus reduce the 
deposition on electrode surface. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Preparation of S/C cathode 
The melt diffusion method was used to produce the sulfur-carbon 
composite used in this experiment. Sublimed sulfur (Aladdin) and 
porous carbon were mixed uniformly in a ratio of 6.5:3.5. The CS2 sol­
vent was added to the mixture, and the mixture was hand milling again 
until the CS2 had completely evaporated. The mixture was placed in a 
sealed Teflon vessel at 160 ◦C for 12 h in an argon-filled environment to 
obtain the sulfur-carbon (S/C) composite material. 80 wt% S/C com­
posite (65.0 wt% S), 10 wt% acetylene black and 10 wt% polyvinylidene 
fluoride (PVDF) were dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) to 
form uniformly mixed slurry. The slurry with good uniformity was 
coated on an aluminum foil and dried under vacuum at 60 ◦C for 12 h to 
prepare the S/C cathode having sulfur loading of 1.8–2.0 mg cm−2. The 
S/C cathode was cut into disks with an area of 1.33 cm2 by a tablet press. 
The cathode composition of the Li–S pouch cells is 86% S/C, 8% SP and 
6% PVDF. 
2.2. Preparation of electrolytes 
The conventional electrolyte was 1 M lithium bis (tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) and 2.0 wt% LiNO3 in a solvent 
of 1, 2-dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v/v = 1:1). 
Different amounts (1.0 wt%, 2.0 wt%, 5.0 wt%, 8.0 wt%) of diphenyl 
diselenide (PDSe, purchased from Bailingwei Science and Technology 
Co) were added to the conventional electrolyte as the experimental 
electrolyte. 
2.3. Preparation of Li2S-PDSe 
Li2S solution was prepared by dissolving 0.1 g of Li2S in absolute 
ethanol, and PDSe solution was prepared by dissolving 0.14 g of PDSe in 
DOL/DME (v/v = 1:1), and then the two solutions were mixed to form 
Li2S-PDSe solution (The Li2S: PDSe weight ratios = 1:1.4, which was 
approximately equal to the weight ratio in a Li–S coin cell with 5.0 wt% 
PDSe). The mixed solution was dried at room temperature inside the 
glove box. 
2.4. Electrochemical measurements 
The CR2025 coin cells were assembled in a glove box with oxygen 
and water content less than 0.01 ppm and used for electrochemical 
detection. The Li/Cu batteries were assembled by copper foil (13 mm 
diameter), lithium sheet and a polypropylene separator (PP2400, Cel­
gard) in the middle. The assembled Li/Cu batteries were allowed to 
stand for 12 h, and they were tested on a LANDAN test system (Wuhan, 
China). Li was deposited onto Cu at 1 mA/cm2 to a 1 mA h/cm2 depo­
sition capacity. In the Li–S batteries, a polypropylene separator 
(PP2400, Celgard) was used as the separator between the lithium anode 
and the S/C cathode. The electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio in the final pole 
piece (13 mm diameter) was 20 μLmg−1. The assembled Li–S batteries 
were allowed to stand for 12 h, after that they were charged and dis­
charged at a potential of 1.7–2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+) on a LANDAN test system 
(Wuhan, China). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were per­
formed on an electrochemical workstation (Solartron 1470 E) in a 
voltage range of 1.5–3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) with a scan rate of 0.05 mV s−1.

2.5. Characterization 
Lithium anodes were collected from recycled Li–S coin cells, washed 
with fresh DME solvent to remove lithium salts and dried in a glove box 
full of Ar. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to observe the 
morphology of the Li anode before and after the battery cycle, and an 
energy dispersion spectrum (EDS) was used to obtain the sulfur distri­
bution in the cross section of the lithium anode. X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the chemical composition of the 
surface of the lithium anode. The dried KBr powder was mixed with Li2S 
and Li2S-PDSe, respectively, and then pressed into a sheet by a Fourier 
transform infrared (FTIR) mold. Sheet samples were used for FTIR 
testing to analyze changes in chemical bonds between Li2S and Li2S- 
PDSe. 
3. Results and discussion 
Different amounts of PDSe (1.0 wt%，2.0 wt%，5.0 wt%，8.0 wt%) 
as electrolyte additives were added to the conventional electrolyte. The 
cycling performance and coulombic efficiency of Li–S batteries with 
PDSe were shown in Fig. 1a. The discharge capacities of Li–S batteries 
were boosted significantly by additive of PDSe. In particular, the bat­
teries with 5.0 wt% PDSe additive delivered an initial discharge capacity 
of 1662 mAh g−1, which is even close to the theoretical value of 1675 
mAh g−1. Further, after 100 cycles, the discharge capacity of batteries 
with 1.0 wt%, 2.0 wt%, 5.0 wt% and 8.0 wt% PDSe are 924 mAh g−1, 
991 mAh g−1, 1075 mAh g−1, 1160 mAh g−1, respectively. However, the 
discharge capacity of Li–S batteries without PDSe is 789 mAh g−1, which 
is lower than that with PDSe additive. Although the 8.0 wt% PDSe- 
containing batteries deliver high specific capacity, the low coulomb 
efficiency and poor cycle stability are undesirable. However, the batte­
ries with 5.0 wt% PDSe exhibit higher coulomb efficiency and relatively 
better cycle stability. Even after 300 cycles, the batteries still maintain 
relatively high specific capacity of 949 mAh g−1, as it shown in Fig. S1.

The electrochemical behavior of the batteries with S/C cathode was 
detected by cyclic voltammetry (CV), and the results without and with 
PDSe additives were shown in Fig. 2a and Fig. 2b, respectively. The 
batteries without PDSe show a large difference between the first and 
third cycle. The third cycle was chosen for discussion due to the large 
polarization of the first cycle. In the cathodic reduction process, the two 
representative reductive peaks are located at 2.30 V and 2.03 V, 
respectively. The first peak is associated with the reduction of sulfur to 
long chain polysulfides, and the second one is because the formation of 
short-chain Li2S2/Li2S [16,18]. Similarly, the batteries with 5.0 wt% 
PDSe also show two reductive peaks at 2.32 V and 2.02 V, respectively. 
This suggests that PDSe has little effect on the battery discharge process. 
In the anodic segment, oxidation of Li2S2/Li2S to intermediate poly­
sulfides result in the anodic peak at 2.37 V, and the anodic peak at 2.42 V 
is attributed to the conversion from polysulfides to sulfur [25]. How­
ever, the batteries containing 5.0 wt% PDSe exhibit two oxidation peaks 
at 2.34 V and 2.39 V, respectively. The negative shift of the oxidation 
potential indicates that the additive PDSe promotes the conversion of 
Li2S2/Li2S to polysulfide and reduces the polarization of the reaction. In 
addition, CV of the batteries with sulfur-free cathode and 5.0 wt% PDSe 
additive was carried out. The results, as it shown in Fig. 2c, show two 
reduction peaks in the cathodic scan from 3 V to 1.5 V. The reduction 
peak at 2.12 V indicates that 2e−
and 2Li+ are attracted by a PDSe 
molecule to form DhSeLi [21,23]. The other reduction peak at 1.72 V 
may be due to the intercalation of Li+ into porous carbon [20]. In the 
following anodic scan from 1.5 V to 3 V, only single oxidation peak at 
2.21 V is seen. It can be seen from Fig. 2d that, compared to the capacity 
(the area enclosed by the red line or the green line) of a lithium-sulfur 
battery 5.0 wt% PDSe provides very little capacity (the area enclosed 
by the black line) in a sulfur-free batteries. But the additive PDSe has a 
great influence on the reaction process of Li–S batteries. The batteries 
with PDSe do not show the PDSe oxidation peak at 2.21 V, but a 
continuously increasing current. The addition of PDSe causes the initial 
potential of Li2S2/Li2S reduction to shift from 2.27 V to 2.15 V, and the 
reaction peak potential shift from 2.37 V to 2.34 V, which broaden the 
entire low-potential reaction range (shaded). In addition, different from 
the conventional electrolyte batteries (Fig. S3), the batteries with 5.0 wt 
% PDSe (Fig. S4) show a lower charge over-potential, especially at the 
beginning of the charge, over the entire course of charging. As shown in 
Fig. 2e, there is no obvious charge activation barrier on the initial 
charging curve in the battery containing 5.0 wt% PDSe. However, a 
significant over-potential of up to 2.3 V is observed in the battery 
without PDSe. In addition, even at a rate of 0.2 C and 1 C, the battery 
containing PDSe still has a lower charging over-potential during 
charging (Figs. S5 and S6). The lower charge over-potential is consistent 
with the CV test results. It can be concluded from the small potential 
difference (Fig. 2e) between the charging platform and the discharging 
platform that the addition of PDSe can reduce polarization [24]. The 
results of CV (Fig. 2d) and charge-discharge voltage profiles (Fig. 2e) 
confirm that the PDSe sufficiently promotes the oxidation of Li2S and 
improves the reversibility of the batteries. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of batteries with and 
without PDSe is shown in Fig. 2f. Both of them show a semicircle in the 
high frequency region and a slopping line in the low frequency region. 
The former represents the interfacial resistance at the electrode- 
electrolyte and the latter is associated with the mass transfer process 
[25–27]. The participation of PDSe in the electrolyte causes to the 
reduction of the interfacial resistance between liquid electrolyte and 
electrodes, which is due to the rapid transformation of the insoluble 
Li2S, good charge transport and electrolyte infiltration. The accelerated 
oxidation of Li2S may be related to the “inductive effect” [24] between 
PDSe and Li2S confirmed by density functional theory. “Inductive effect” 
i.e. the breakage of Se–Se bond and the elongation of Li–S bond caused 
by the attraction of Se in PDSe to Li in Li2S. The elongated Li–S bond 
indicates lower activation energy of Li2S, which reduces the initial

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) and X-ray photo-electron spec­
troscopy (XPS) were performed to investigate the inductive effect be­
tween PDSe and Li2S in current conditions. Due to the out-of-plane 
phenyl ring twist of PDSe [23], there is an distinct peak pointed by 
the black arrow in Fig. 3a. However, as it shown in Fig. 3b, it is failed to 
detect the peak at 453.6 cm−1 of PDSe mixed Li2S. The disappearance of 
the peak at 453.6 cm−1 means there is no out-of-plane phenyl ring twist, 
i.e. the rupture of Se–Se bonds in PDSe-Li2S. The results of XPS, as it 
shown in Fig. 3c, the Se 3 d peak of PDSe shifts from 55.9 eV to 55.2 eV 
by mixing Li2S. The shift of the peak position indicates that Li2S induces 
the break of the Se–Se bond. In Fig. 3d，the binding energy of Li1s in 
Li2S is 55.3ev, and the binding energy of PDSe-Li2S is 56.2ev. The 
increased binding energy of Li1s indicates that the electron density 
around Li decreases in PDSe-Li2S. This is due to the attraction of Se 
atoms to Li atoms, leading to the elongation of the Li–S bond. 
Theoretical models based on density functional theory (DFT) are 
used to further explore the interaction between PDSe and Li2S. The 
calculations are carried out by the software ORCA [28]under the func­
tional b3lyp [29] and basis set 6–311 + g (d) [30,31]level. DFT-D3 [32] 
dispersion correction is used to obtain more accurate calculation results, 
and the result is shown in Fig. 3e. The Li–S bond length in Li2S is 2.233 Å 
(Figs. 3e–1), and the Se–Se bond length in PDSe is 2.391 Å (Figs. 3e–2). 
When PDSe and Li2S coexist in the computing system, the system will be 
optimized towards the lowest energy (that is, the most stable state). The 
final state after optimization is shown in Figs. 3e-3. The contact between 
PDSe and Li2S (Figs. 3e-3) caused the Se–Se bond to break (the Se–Se 
distance was 5.039 Å) and the Li–S bond was extended to 2.394 Å. 
Moreover, the Gibbs free energy (△G) of Equation (1) is −57.5 kJ/mol 
<0, which indicates that the stretching of the Li–S bond is valid 
(Figs. 3e–1 to Figs. 3e-3). This result is consistent with the theoretical 
model. The rupture of the Se–Se bond and the elongation of the Li–S 
bond reduce the activation energy of Li2S. As a result, lower initial 
charge over-potential and accelerating oxidation of Li2S are obtained.

The surface morphology of Li metal anode after 100 cycles at 0.5 C 
with and without PDSe is shown in Fig. 4a and b, respectively. Because 
of the corrosion of the lithium anode by the electrolyte and the 
dissolution-deposition of lithium during the cycle [14,33], the surface of 
the lithium anode change from smooth and dense surface of the fresh Li 
(Fig. S7) to distinctly rough and loosely stacked deposits in conventional 
electrolyte, as shown in Fig. 4a. Fortunately, the surface of Li anode 
shows a continuous, uniform, and highly packed morphology (Fig. 4b) 
by addition of 5.0 wt % PDSe. In addition, Li/Cu batteries containing 
5.0 wt% PDSe show longer cycle life and better cycle stability (Fig. S8), 
which further confirms that the addition of PDSe ensures a smooth and 
compact lithium deposition. The results may be due to the fact that PDSe 
can promote the reduction of Li2S and reduce the deposition of insoluble 
low-order polysulfide (Li2S2/Li2S). 
The compositions of the Li anodes surface with and without PDSe 
were detected by XPS. The S 2p peak is equipped with spin-orbit 
coupling 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, with a resolution of 1.18 eV and a charac­
teristic peak area ratio of 2: 1 (Fig. 4c) [34]. The S2p3/2 peak binding 
energy of 169.0 eV represents the S–O bond of –SO2CF3 species, which 
come from the decomposition of LiTFSI salt [5,35]. And the 167.0 eV 
(S2p3/2) band is assigned to thiosulfate, which is thought to be the 
product of lithium polysulfide oxidized by LiNO3 [20,36]. Two strong 
S2p3/2 peaks at 161.7 eV and 160.5 eV indicate large irreversible Li2S2 
and Li2S covering the surface of Li anode [24,37]. While, S2p3/2 peaks at 
161.7 eV and 160.5 eV drastically decrease with 5.0 wt% PDSe additive, 
representing a decrease in the insoluble Li2S2 and Li2S deposited on the 
surface of lithium anode. The decrease of sulfur content indicates that 
PDSe accelerates the oxidation of Li2S and improves the reaction kinetics 
of the charging process, thereby reducing the loss of active materials and 
increasing the specific capacity. Fig. 4d–g shows the SEM image and EDS 
mapping of the cross section of the lithium foil. The S content (0.1%) of 
lithium foil with PDSe additives is lower than the S content (0.3%) of 
traditional electrolytes, as shown in Table S1. Further, the EDS mapping 
suggests that the deposition thickness of Li2S on the surface of lithium 
foil decrease with the addition of PDSe. In short, PDSe promotes the

In order to confirm the effect of PDSe, the cycle performance of the 
batteries at a high current density of 1 C was further tested, and the 
results are shown in Fig. 5a (the first three cycles are at 0.2C). Compared 
to the batteries without PDSe, the batteries with 5.0 wt% PDSe additive 
still achieve a better cycle performance. Even after 100 cycles, the bat­
teries with 5.0 wt% PDSe addition maintains a high specific capacity of 
1046.8 mAh g−1, which is still much higher than 827.6 mA h g−1 of the 
batteries without PDSe. In addition, the PDSe-containing pouch batte­
ries (Fig. S9) also show excellent cycle performance (Figs. S10,S11,S12) 
under high sulfur loading (5 mgcm−2) and low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio 
(E/S = 3 μLmg−1). After 60 cycles, the capacity retention of the con­
taining 5 wt% PDSe is 72.4%, which is significantly higher than 55.2% 
of the pouch batteries without PDSe. The additive of 5.0 wt% PDSe 
improves the rate performance at 0.1 C–2 C of Li–S batteries, as shown in 
Fig. 5b. For the batteries without PDSe, the specific capacities of 1066 
mAhg−1 (fifth discharge), 888.1 mA h g−1, 806.4 mAhg−1, and 756.2 
mAhg−1 are delivered at 0.2 C, 0.5 C, 1 C, and 2 C, respectively. By 
comparison, the batteries with 5.0 wt% PDSe deliver higher discharge 
capacity of 1541.5 mAhg−1, 1367.6 mAhg−1, 1265.8 mAhg−1, 1142.4 
mAhg−1, respectively. After returning to 0.2 C, the capacity of the bat­
teries containing PDSe comes back to near the initial value, which in­
dicates better stability and reversibility. The improved cycling 
performance and rate performance of batteries attribute to the addition 
of PDSe, which causes to good reaction kinetics and decreased deposi­
tion of insulating Li2S. 
4. Conclusion 
PDSe as an electrolyte additive is reported in this work. The induc­
tive effect between PDSe and the final discharge product (Li2S) in Li–S 
batteries leads to the elongation of Li–S bonds in Li2S and the rupture of 
Se–Se bonds in PDSe. Thus, PDSe additives promote the oxidation of Li2S 
and greatly reduce the deposition of insoluble and insulating Li2S par­
ticles in the lithium anode. The batteries with 5.0 wt% PDSe additives 
provide an initial capacity of 1585.6 mAh g−1 and maintain 916.5 
mAhg−1 after 300 cycles at 0.5 C showing excellent electrochemical 
performance. We propose a way to reduce the activation barrier of Li2S 
by adding PDSe. This work is expected to stimulate more interest in 
using similar compounds to achieve high-performance Li–S batteries.
