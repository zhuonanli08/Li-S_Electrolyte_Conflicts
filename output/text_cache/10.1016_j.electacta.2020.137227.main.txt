a b s t r a c t 
The HPLC method including derivatisation was used for the determination of polysulﬁde species concen- 
tration during the operation of lithium-sulfur battery cells. A comparison was made between a glyme 
based electrolyte, which exhibits high polysulﬁde solubility, and a ﬂuorinated ether based electrolyte, 
which was proposed to reduce the dissolution and diffusion of polysulﬁdes. A separate analysis was con- 
ducted on porous carbon cathodes and separators obtained from cycled battery cells. The determination 
of the species concentration trends allowed a deeper understanding of the differences in battery cell op- 
erating mechanism. We have determined that polysulﬁde species are formed in similar concentrations in 
both examined electrolytes, but they remain trapped in the cathode pores in the ﬂuorinated ether based 
electrolyte lithium-sulfur battery cells. The polysulﬁde concentration in the separators of the ﬂuorinated 
ether cells was below the limit of detection, which indicates that the solubilities of polysulﬁdes in the ﬂu- 
orinated electrolyte are below 50 μM. This results in absence of disproportionation or coproportionation 
reactions between the polysulﬁde species in solution. This different mechanism inﬂuences the change in 
length and potential of the voltage plateaus of the battery cells during galvanostatic cycling.

1. Introduction 
One of the approaches for improving the performance of 
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries includes the use of electrolytes, 
which exhibit low polysulﬁde solubility. Examples of such elec- 
trolytes include ionic liquids [1–3] , highly concentrated elec- 
trolytes [ 4 , 5 ] and the use of ﬂuorinated co-solvents [6–9] . The 
latter possibility seems to be the most promising alternative 
mostly due to their low viscosity, although a complicated syn- 
thesis and high cost of ﬂuorinated solvents do remain their ma- 
jor drawbacks. It has been proven that although the polysulﬁde 
solubility is around 10 0 0x lower in the ﬂuorinated ether 1,2- 
(1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethoxy)ethane (TFEE) based electrolyte than in 
the tetraglyme (TEGDME) based one (estimated 1 mM for TFEE 
vs. 1 M for TEGDME for Li 2 S 8 ), polysulﬁdes are still formed during 
sulfur reduction. Likewise, lithium sulﬁde (Li 2 S) is detected in the 
discharge after the voltage reaches the lower voltage plateau [7] . 
There is nevertheless still a gap in understanding how ﬂuorinated

Basic understanding of the sulfur reduction mechanism includes 
investigation of sulfur species present at different stages of bat- 
tery cell discharge or charge. Various ex-situ and in-situ / operando 
analytical techniques are used to better understand mechanism 
during battery cycling [ 10 , 11 ]. These include: X-ray techniques 
(i.e. ex-situ and in-situ / operando X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) 
[ 12 , 13 ], X-ray absorption (near-edge) structure (XANES) [ 14 , 15 ], 
full-ﬁeld and scanning transmission X-ray microscopy (FF-TXM 
and STXM), X-ray absorption tomography, X-ray ﬂuorescence mi- 
croscopy (XRF)), UV-Vis spectrometry [14] , Raman spectrometry 
[ 13 , 16 ], NMR spectrometry [16] , in-situ atomic force microscopy 
(AFM), in-situ / operando Fourier transformed infrared spectrome- 
try (FTIR) and galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) 
[ 10 , 11 ]. Recently, operando techniques have gained increased at- 
tention for the investigation of the reaction mechanism of Li-S 
batteries [11] . Here, electrode information is obtained with vari- 
ous characterisation techniques as the battery is cycling. Zhu and 
co-workers used in-situ Raman spectrometry and XRD for the 
study of polysulﬁde species in the sulfur cathode and in the elec- 
trolyte during the cycling of Li-S batteries. Based on the identi-

Li et al. used in-situ UV-Vis spectrometry and XAS for elucida- 
tion of mechanism when cathode of Li-S battery was stabilized by 
the covalently binding of sulfur to carbon nanoﬁbers [14] . Robba 
et al. demonstrated that normalized area of the pre-edge from 
operando high-energy resolution ﬂuorescence detected (HERFD) 
XAS can be a reliable ﬁngerprint of the Li 2 S x mean chain length 
and thus can be used to differentiate mean polysulﬁde composi- 
tion [15] . Nakanishi et al. employed (among others) pulsed-ﬁeld 
gradient (PFG)-NMR, Raman spectrometry and single-crystal X- 
ray structure analysis for the study of transport properties and 
Li-ion coordination of sulfolane-based highly concentrated elec- 
trolytes with lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)amide [16] . Op- 
timally, such analysis should include the concentration of every in- 
dividual species formed, but due to the complexity of the system, 
techniques capable of giving such information are limited. In order 
to differentiate between polysulﬁdes with different chain length, 
an analytical method that includes separation of species and en- 
ables low limit of detection should be employed, e.g. HPLC. Accord- 
ing to our literature survey, HPLC is currently the only technique 
capable of determining the concentrations of individual polysul- 
ﬁdes of different chain length [17–26] . 
Since polysulﬁde species are air sensitive [ 27 ] as well as prone 
to redox reactions among themselves (disproportionations, com- 
proportionations) [ 28 ], for accurate HPLC determination derivati- 
sation of polysulﬁde species as part of the sample preparation 
is required. Derivatisation in this sense means a reaction with a 
proper reagent producing a new compound with properties suit- 
able for HPLC analysis. The basic structure of the initial com- 
pound remains similar, with only speciﬁc functional groups chang- 
ing in order to make the compounds more stable, detectable and 
analysable. That enables to ﬁx the polysulﬁde species in their cur- 
rent balance. Derivatisation should also be fast [ 20 ] in order to 
not change the polysulﬁdes speciation during the reaction. Chro- 
matographic investigations have already been performed on Li-S 
batteries. Barschasz et. al. employed methyl triﬂate derivatisation 
reagent [ 20 , 21 ] and demonstrated its application for detection of 
polysulﬁde species in glyme based electrolyte. Battery cell samples 
were prepared by chromoamperometry, while only the electrolyte 
component was used for the analysis [22] . Kawase et. al published 
a detailed study with determination of polysulﬁde species in the 
cathode matrix using a different reagent, benzyl chloride. Polysul- 
ﬁdes were benzylated, enabling sensitive mass spectrometry de- 
tection, but, on the other hand extended time was needed for 
the derivatisation reaction (several hours), inﬂuencing on the er- 
ror associated with polysulﬁdes stability [23] . Several studies are 
also available from D. Qu group: the solubility of sulfur in differ- 
ent solvents and electrolytes was determined and demonstrated 
that sulfur can be super-saturated in the Li-S battery cell [24] . 
A novel derivatisation reagent (4-(dimethylamino) benzoyl chlo- 
ride) for mass spectrometry determination was synthesised [17] , 
enabling shorter reaction times than the one previously investi- 
gated. Further publications nevertheless report the use of methyl 
triﬂate reagent used in conjunction with UV-Vis determination of 
methylated polysulﬁde compounds. Reactions between Li and poly- 
sulﬁdes and the inﬂuence of LiNO 3 on consumption of polysulﬁdes 
was studied [25] . Similarly, stability of Li solid electrolyte inter- 
phase (SEI) and polysulﬁde reactions with electrolyte salts were 
investigated [26] . In-situ derivatisation was employed for determi- 
nation of initially formed polysulﬁde species upon reduction of sul- 
fur, which proved to be S 4 2- and S 5 2 −[19] . Additionally, polysulﬁde 
speciation in the electrolyte was determined through the ﬁrst dis- 
charge and charge of a beaker type Li-S battery cell with a large 
electrolyte volume [18] . 
In summary, all of the investigations with fast reacting derivati- 
sation reagents used large electrolyte volume Li-S battery cells 
and focused on the analysis of polysulﬁdes in liquid electrolyte. 
In order to investigate the Li-S battery cell operation with an 
electrolyte, where polysulﬁdes are poorly soluble (1 M LiTFSI in 
TFEE:dioxolane 1:1 v:v mixture, in continuation “ﬂuorinated elec- 
trolyte”), the same setup (i.e. sampling of only the electrolyte solu- 
tion) would only give partial information, since most of the poly- 
sulﬁdes are expected to be trapped in the carbon cathode pores. 
Hence, we studied the chromatographic determination of polysul- 
ﬁdes using battery cell setup, with a thin separator and relatively 
limited electrolyte volume (in comparison with the beaker cell de- 
sign), which had the added beneﬁt of being closer to the one vi- 
able for commercialisation. Furthermore, the amount of polysul- 
ﬁdes was separately determined in the cathode matrix and elec- 
trolyte trapped in the separator pores, providing important insight 
into the operation of ﬂuorinated ether based electrolyte. Since a di- 
rect comparison with the already published investigations on more 
conventional glyme based electrolytes was not possible due to the 
described differences, the same study was performed on 1 M LiTFSI 
in TEGDME:dioxolane 1:1 v:v electrolyte battery cells (in continu- 
ation “conventional electrolyte”). Obtained results were also used 
for comparison with the already published data and evaluation of 
our method. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Battery cell conﬁguration 
The sulfur cathodes were prepared by conventional slurry- 
casting method with a doctor blade applicator. Sulfur/carbon com- 
posite was obtained by ball milling sulfur (66.7 wt. %) and ENSACO 
350 G carbon (33.3 wt. %, 780 m 2 g −1 , Imerys) at 300 rpm for 
30 min. After milling, the mixture was heated to 155 °C for 5 h 
with a 0.2 °C min −1 heating ramp under argon ﬂow. The cathode 
slurry was prepared by mixing 80 wt. % sulphur/carbon composite, 
10 wt. % Printex XE2 carbon (Degussa), and 10 wt. % PVdF binder 
(Sigma-Aldrich, average molar mass 543.0 0 0) in a planetary ball 
mill (PM100, Retch), using NMP (Sigma-Aldrich, Chromasolv, ˃99%) 
as solvent. Obtained slurry was cast onto carbon coated aluminium 
foil using doctor blade applicator (200 μm spacer) and dried at 
50 °C overnight. Cathodes with a diameter of 14 mm were cut out. 
The sulfur loading varied from 1.4 to 1.6 mg cm −2 and was selected 
due to similar performance of both electrolytes with selected load- 
ing. 
The solvents tetraglyme (TEGDME, Acros) and 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL, Sigma-Aldrich) used for electrolyte preparation were dried 
in a multi-step process using molecular sieves, K/Na alloy and 
distillation [7] . 1,2-(1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethoxy)ethane (TFEE, 99.1%, 
Apollo scientiﬁc) solvent was dried using molecular sieves. LiTFSI 
salt (Sigma-Aldrich) was dried at 140 °C under vacuum overnight. 
After drying all the electrolyte components were stored in an 
argon-ﬁlled glovebox with oxygen and water content below 1 ppm 
(MBRAUN, LABmaster). 1 M LiTFSI was dissolved in TEGDME:DOL 
1:1 (v:v) mixture (termed conventional electrolyte) and TFEE:DOL 
1:1 (v:v) mixture (termed ﬂuorinated electrolyte). The ﬁnal water 
content was determined by Karl Fischer titration in the glovebox. 
The solvents were dried to water content below 1 ppm, while both 
electrolytes had water content approx. 10 ppm, most of which was 
introduced with the salt. 
All battery cells were assembled in an argon-ﬁlled glovebox us- 
ing 100 μm lithium foil (FMC, used with no pretreatment, 14 mm 
in diameter) and Celgard® 2320 separator (16 mm in diameter) 
which was wetted with a normalized quantity of the electrolyte 
(20 μL mg −1 of sulfur). The amount of electrolyte added has been 
selected to ensure the suﬃcient battery cell performance for both 
electrolytes.

2.2. Electrochemical tests 
All electrochemical tests were performed using pouch cells with 
nickel current collectors. The cells were cycled galvanostatically be- 
tween 3.0 and 1.5 V vs. Li/Li + at a ﬁxed current rate of 0.1 C us- 
ing VMP3 galvanostat/potentiostat (Bio-Logic). Average discharge 
capacity for the conventional electrolyte was 10 0 0 mAh g −1 and 
for the ﬂuorinated electrolyte 1100 mAh g −1 . Cells were stopped 
at different states of charge (SOC) and immediately transferred 
to the glovebox for derivatisation. We estimate the maximum 
time between stopping the current and sample derivatisation to 
be 15 min. In order to determine the thermodynamic potential 
of voltage plateaus observed during galvanostatic discharge and 
charge, GITT (galvanostatic intermittent titration technique) exper- 
iment was conducted by discharging a cell with 0.05 C, stopping 
the current every hour and leaving the cell to rest until the condi- 
tion of dE/dt < 15 mV h −1 was met. 
2.3. Derivatisation 
For methylation of sulfur species present in the battery cell, 
methyl triﬂuoromethanesulfonate (Sigma-Aldrich) as derivatisation 
agent was used. The derivatisation agent was used as received and 
was kept in the refrigerator when not in use. Acetonitrile was used 
as a solvent for the derivatisation and was dried with molecu- 
lar sieves and kept in the glovebox. The battery cell at a selected 
SOC was transferred to an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. Then the cathode 
and separator were taken from the cell and individually immersed 
in a freshly prepared mixture of 50 μL methyl triﬂuoromethane- 
sulfonate in 1 mL of acetonitrile and shook for 45 s in closed 
10 mL vials. The solution was ﬁltered through 0.22 μm PTFE ﬁlters 
(13 mm, RESTEK) to HPLC vials, which were closed and the caps 
of which were additionally protected with paraﬁlm. The ﬁltered 
solutions were analysed with HPLC in the time frame of 6 h af- 
ter derivatisation. It has been estimated the uncertainty associated 
with the derivatisation steps is similar for all investigated polysul- 
ﬁde species and smaller than the uncertainty associated with the 
speciﬁc capacity obtained for individual battery cells. 
2.4. HPLC analyses 
HPLC was performed for simultaneous determination of methy- 
lated polysulﬁde species and sulfur. The analyses were performed 
on an Agilent HPLC system 1100 (Palo Alto, USA) equipped with a 
degasser, a binary pump, an auto-sampler and a diode array detec- 
tor (DAD) set at 230, 254 and 280 nm. The separation was achieved 
on Waters Symmetry C18 column (Milford, USA) with the dimen- 
sions 4.6 × 50 mm and particle size 5 μm. The ﬂow rate of the 
mobile phase was 0.7 mL/min and injection volume was 4 μL. The 
separation was carried at 25 °C using two eluents: A, ultra-pure 
water; B, methanol. A discontinuous gradient was run as follows: 
0–10 min, 25–100 % B; 10–20 min, 100 % B; 20–21 min, 100–25 
% B. The retention times were determined by injection of standard 
solution of methylated S 2 −, S 2 2 −and S 3 2 −species (all from Sigma 
Aldrich) and sulfur (Honeywell) prepared in acetonitrile at 0.5 mM 
concentration. Other sulfur species were identiﬁed according to 
published data and obtained UV spectra. The retention times for 
methylated species were as follows: 2.1 min (S 2 −), 6.0 min (S 2 2 −), 
8.6 min (S 3 2 −), 9.6 min (S 4 2 −), 10.5 min (S 5 2 −), 11.1 min (S 6 2 −), 
11.6 min (S 7 2 −), 12.1 min (S 8 2 −) and 13.0 min (sulfur).

Formation of different polysulﬁde and sulfur species and their 
concentration was determined through the ﬁrst and second dis- 
charge and charge of a conventional electrolyte containing LiTFSI

the TEGDME solvent was replaced with ﬂuorinated ether TFEE. The 
ﬂuorinated electrolyte is reported to have lower polysulﬁde solu- 
bility [7] . Presence of polysulﬁde species in Li-S battery cells cycled 
with this type of electrolyte has therefore already been proven, 
while it is currently not fully understood through which chemical 
mechanism the reduction of sulfur proceeds or how the reduced 
solubility affects the ongoing processes. Although the calculations 
estimate a 10 0 0x lower Li 2 S 8 solubility in the ﬂuorinated elec- 
trolyte than in the conventional one, there are no reports of actual 
chemical analyses that determine the concentrations of individual 
polysulﬁde species in the investigated electrolyte. As discussed in 
the introduction, this is due to the limitations of currently devel- 
oped analytical techniques, of which only HPLC is capable of sepa- 
ration and identiﬁcation of polysulﬁdes of different chain lengths. 
3.1. Determination of polysulﬁde species 
UV-Vis detection of individual polysulﬁdes and sulfur after 
HPLC separation was performed at 230 nm, a wavelength deter- 
mined according to experimental spectra of methylated polysul- 
ﬁdes and sulfur obtained with DAD detector ( Fig. 1 ). Although the 
absorption maxima of polysulﬁde species move to higher wave- 
lengths with the chain length, all investigated species have accept- 
able absorbance at 230 nm. 
The samples removed from battery cell setup were derivatised 
immediately after battery cell was stopped (in maximum 15 min) 
and analysed no more than 6 hours after derivatisation. The sta- 
bility of the derivatised species was relatively low according to in- 
vestigation in the 100 h range ( Fig. 2 ). In the selected time frame, 
higher concentrations of sulfur were observed, which can be at-

The occurrence of individual polysulﬁde species and their con-

and charge cycle using the previously deﬁned conventional and 
ﬂuorinated electrolyte. This was initially done in order to inves- 
tigate the differences between the initial and subsequent battery 
cell cycle. Obtained results do not indicate signiﬁcant differences 
from the ﬁrst to the second cycle, however with the extended data 
set it can be conﬁrmed that the trends we are discussing are rele- 
vant and repeatable. Although the results do not provide informa- 
tion brought on due to cell aging or extended cycling, they do give 
important information about the mechanism of operation and the 
differences between the Li-S battery cells with the two different 
electrolytes employed. 
3.2. Polysulﬁde species in various electrolytes 
In order to provide data, which as closely as possible mimics 
a Li-S battery cell with a commercially viable cell setup, we used 
pouch cells for sample preparation. The catholyte trapped in the 
cathode and in the Celgard separator pores were separately deriva- 
tised and analysed. Each data point at different SOC was therefore 
from a different cell, with two samples coming from each of it –
one for the separator and one for the cathode. Each determination 
was performed at least twice. A rather large standard deviation of 
obtained results was recognised, which can be attributed to inde- 
pendent battery cell for each measurement point. The polysulﬁde 
equilibrium could also be shifted due to a non-negligible time pe- 
riod between stopping the current through the cell and derivati- 
sation of the existing poylsulﬁdes. Nevertheless, we estimate that 
only battery cell samples with a small electrolyte amount and a 
thin separator can give data relevant for optimisation of Li-S cell 
performance and mechanism understanding. Furthermore, in order 
to be able to detect the amount of polysulﬁdes in a low-solubility 
electrolyte, this was the optimal experimental setup. In order to 
validate our method to some extent, we compare our data with 
already published analyses of the conventional electrolyte in con- 
tinuation. We also estimated the total uncertainty of the results, 
which was calculated to be 30% for the conventional electrolyte 
and 25% for the ﬂuorinated one. We estimate most of this stems 
from the change in sulfur active mass in the cathode and devia- 
tions in obtained speciﬁc capacity between the cells. 
3.3. Conventional electrolyte 
Chromatograms of methylated sulfur species and elemental sul- 
fur from the corresponding SOC for the cells built with 1 M LiTFSI 
in TEGDME:DOL 1:1 (v:v) (conventional) electrolyte are shown in 
Fig. 3 . The chromatographic peaks for Me 2 S (2.1 min) and Me 2 S 2 
(6.0 min) are due to clarity excluded from the chromatograms pre- 
sented in the Fig. 3 , but can be accessed in the supporting informa- 
tion ( Fig. S1 ). Chromatograms for the samples obtained from the 
cathodes and the chromatograms of the separators are shown in 
the Fig. 3 b and Fig. 3 c, respectively. The alteration in the polysul- 
ﬁde oxidation state during the two cycles of discharge and charge 
are clearly visible through the intensity of individual peaks. Inten- 
sity of sulfur peak (13.0 min) decreases towards the end of dis- 
charge and increases again in charge, while intensities of short 
polysulﬁde peaks follow the opposite trend with S 2 −detected af- 
ter the start of the low voltage plateau ( Fig. S1 ). The results are 
in line with already published data on the formation of sulfur and 
polysulﬁde species in Li-S battery cell [ 18 , 22 , 23 ]. 
Analogous to data for cathode samples, separator samples show 
the expected alteration in the polysulﬁde oxidation state during 
the two analysed cycles. A clear difference between the cathode 
and separator samples is in the concentration of individual species. 
Chromatograms of separator samples show peaks with signiﬁcantly 
smaller peak area, indicating that the concentration of polysulﬁde 
species and sulfur is lower in the electrolyte in the separator than 
in the cathode. The concentrations are about twice as high in the 
cathode than in the separator. This is important, since we can es- 
sentially detect that 1/3 of polysulﬁdes are lost in the separator. 
The capacity achieved in described cells in our study is also in 
line with the result, since the average obtained ﬁrst discharge ca- 
pacity was 10 0 0 mAh g −1 , approx. 2/3 of theoretical capacity. The 
difference in the obtained capacity is likely a consequence of the 
fact that the diffusional distances in the separator (several μm) 
are larger in comparison to those in the cathode pores (several 
nm) [29] and some amount of these polysulﬁdes cannot be uti- 
lized during battery cell discharge due to limited diffusion. This 
result clearly supports the theory that entrapment of polysulﬁde 
species in the separator is the most important factor contributing 
the lower discharge capacity compared to theoretical one of Li- 
S battery cells built with conventional electrolytes [29] . Although 
lowering the current rate would be beneﬁcial in enabling more 
polysulﬁde utilisation in the case where no other side reactions are 
taking place in the battery cell, it would result in adverse effects 
on the performance of battery cells of our current design. Namely, 
enabling longer times of cell discharge also increases the concen-

Additional difference between the cathode and separator sam- 
ples can be observed with formation of sulfur (according to peak 
area close to the end of charge). In that region, sulfur forms by

consequently its concentration in the electrolyte decreases as it 
was observed in the separator samples. The intensity of the sulfur 
peak in the cathode remains high, demonstrating that our sam-

on the carbon cathode matrix. With the same argumentation, one 
would also expect to see a similar discrepancy between cathode 
and separator signals for Me 2 S species, while this species was not

ity limit of the species, which is (for the electrolyte in use) es- 
timated to be several orders of magnitude lower for Li 2 S (in the 
range of 10 −15 M – 10 −21 M) than for sulfur (mM) [33] . 
3.4. Fluorinated electrolyte 
Results for the cathode samples from the ﬂuorinated electrolyte, 
where lower solubility of polysulﬁde species is the case, are pre- 
sented in Fig. 4 b (see also Fig. S2 for full chromatograms showing 
also the short chain polysulﬁdes). In general, the alteration in the 
oxidation state of sulfur species is similar to the one from the con- 
ventional electrolyte. A XANES study [7] has already shown that 
both polysulﬁdes and Li 2 S are formed during operation of cells 
with this electrolyte at similar points during discharge. This tech- 
nique is unfortunately limited, since due to lack of standard mate- 
rials for polysulﬁdes of different chain lengths only differentiation 
between sulfur, polysulﬁde species and Li 2 S is usually possible. Our 
purpose was to investigate the difference of the individual poly- 
sulﬁde amount between the conventional and the ﬂuorinated elec- 
trolyte by HPLC analysis, which enables determination of individual 
species. Furthermore, it is rather interesting that although the ﬂu- 
orinated electrolyte is supposed to have low polysulﬁde solubility, 
we detect a relatively large amount of polysulﬁdes in cathode sam- 
ples, even higher comparing to the conventional electrolyte sam- 
ples. 
The separator samples from the ﬂuorinated electrolyte cells on 
the other hand show an interesting lack of the polysulﬁde sig- 
nals, except for very small peaks for elemental sulfur present in 
some of the samples. Comparison of sulfur peak areas in both elec- 
trolytes shows that sulfur concentrations in the ﬂuorinated ether 
electrolyte are 25x lower, supporting calculated values of sulfur 
solubility, which is 1–4 mM for the TEDGME based electrolyte and 
0.1–1 mM for the TFEE based one [7] . This result demonstrates that 
the use of ﬂuorinated ether electrolytes can decrease the degree of 
Li-S battery cell self-discharge phenomenon, which takes place due 
to dissolution of sulfur from an oxidised cathode. 
Since the separators also appeared uncoloured upon disassem- 
bly of the cells (in contrast to intense yellow/orange colour from 
the conventional electrolyte separators – see supporting informa- 
tion, Fig. S3 ), these results are in line with our expectations and 
with the claim that in this electrolyte solubility of polysulﬁde 
species is very low. Since no polysulﬁdes were detected in the elec- 
trolyte in the separator pores, the higher peak areas observed with 
polysulﬁdes detected in the cathode samples can be explained.

3.5. Comparison of conventional and ﬂuorinated electrolytes 
The peak areas of individual species presented on Figs. 3 and 
4 were compared in order to have a more detailed information 
about these species in both examined electrolytes. According to lit- 
erature [22] , a linear relation is obtained when peak areas are plot- 
ted versus the overall sulfur concentration. Since the sulfur mass 
loading in the cathode was always the same, the sulfur content 
in our samples is expected to be constant. Consequently the sum 
of all peaks of sulfur species in the chromatograms is also ex- 
pected to be constant for a given electrolyte. If the sulfur content is 
calculated from the concentrations of elemental sulfur, polysulﬁde 
species and Li 2 S in the battery cells by taking into account the sul- 
fur oxidation state, the value should be constant regardless of the 
state of charge, as all sulfur has been added in the form of cathode 
active mass during cell assembly. This hypothesis was tested by 
plotting the total peak area as a function of state of charge ( Fig. 5 ). 
Fig. 5. Total peak area of methylated polysulﬁde species and sulfur in a chro- 
matogram as a function of SOC. The uncertainty was estimated to be on the order 
of the differences between the curve for the ﬂuorinated electrolyte and the conven- 
tional electrolyte curve, which corresponds to the total sum of both cathode and 
separator concentrations. 
For both electrolytes, the peak areas for species in the cathode 
and separator were summed. Total peak area was clearly not con- 
stant for all the investigated samples. There was a large decrease 
at the ends of discharge or charge. There was, on the other hand, 
a remarkable resemblance between the trends for the ﬂuorinated 
and conventional electrolyte. This led us to deduce that the ob- 
served (large) variation was not likely to be due to poor stability 
of the individual samples. Namely, any instability would certainly 
show as a more local outlier - e. g. 8 th sample for the conventional 
electrolyte shows an anomalously larger total peak area, which was

attributed to possible unwanted solvent evaporation. The increases

our investigation and the one in ref [22] can explain why the to-

Smaller values for SOCs, where elemental sulfur is the predomi-

sample solvent, which has a limited solubility of elemental sulfur 
[24] . A decrease at the ends of discharge, on the other hand, is 
likely a consequence of our study including poorly soluble short 
chain polysulﬁdes in the analysis. These exhibit lower absorption 
of light at the 230 nm wavelength ( Fig. 1 ), so cannot be assumed 
that the total peak area would be constant at substantial amount 
of short-chain polysulﬁdes. The similarity of the trend of total sul- 
fur content in both electrolytes (compare red and black full curves 
with solid dots) also shows that although the large variation in the 
achieved capacity of the tested cells was noticed, the ﬁnal uncer- 
tainty of the HPLC analysis seems to be smaller, i.e. in the mag- 
nitude of the observable differences between the two plots dis- 
cussed. 
In order to therefore take into account the sample properties 
and short-chain polysulﬁde detection limitations, chromatographic 
peaks for sulfur, Me 2 S 2 and Me 2 S were not further processed. To 
remove outliers due to any instability of the methylated polysul- 
ﬁdes, data from the ﬁrst and second battery cell cycle were aver- 
aged. This kind of data processing give more reliable results ac- 
cording to amounts of different polysulﬁde species. 
Fig. 5 also illustrates one of the most important differences be- 
tween the two examined electrolytes. Concentrations of species 
determined in the cathode samples are higher for the ﬂuorinated 
electrolyte. The origin of this difference has already been com- 
mented on as stemming from entrapment of sulfur species close 
to the cathode matrix. The difference is also in line with higher 
capacities reached with the cells using ﬂuorinated electrolyte. As 
already discussed, results from HPLC analyses provided concrete 
proof that polysulﬁde entrapment in the separator pores is the 
main reason for non-theoretical discharge capacities achieved in 
conventional electrolytes, while no such conclusion can be made 
for the electrolyte with a reduced polysulﬁde solubility. On the 
contrary, the data shows that polysulﬁde dissolution is highly un- 
likely to be the cause contributing to poor capacity utilisation 
(1100 mAh g −1 still corresponds to only 66% of theoretical capac- 
ity) in the ﬂuorinated electrolyte Li-S battery cells. As such, limi- 
tations should be searched for in other processes taking place (e. 
g. Li metal incompatibility, passivation of the cathode surface, side 
reactions, etc.). 
Fig. 6 shows the concentration trends of the polysulﬁde species 
for cathode and separator samples for the conventional electrolyte. 
As already stated, the results for cathode samples (open circles) 
are in agreement with previously published literature [ 18 , 22 , 23 ]. 
Polysulﬁde species with higher oxidation state have a maximum 
concentration at the beginning of the discharge, while the shorter- 
chain length polysulﬁdes reach the highest concentration after- 
wards. The opposite can be observed for charge. The average poly- 
sulﬁde species oxidation state therefore ﬁrst gradually decreases 
and later increases through charge. This is noticed as the two lo- 
cal concentration maxima (one in the discharge and one in the 
charge), which are closer together when going from more oxidised 
polysulﬁde species towards more reduced polysulﬁdes (compare 
for example cathode lines in Figs. 6 b, 6 d, and 6 g). 
The trend of maximum concentration getting closer is not visi- 
ble for species in the separator. To rephrase, concentration change 
in the separator during cycling is similar for all the determined 
species. This means that the ratios between different sulfur species 
in the separator is always similar and is not an indication of the 
state of charge. This feature is likely a result of disproportionation

Another difference between the two electrolytes is at the posi- 
tion just before the end of discharge. This position corresponds to 
the unexplained third voltage plateau in the ﬂuorinated ether elec- 
trolyte cell ( Fig. 7 a), therefore the difference in the polysulﬁde spe- 
ciation and concentrations is expected, but its origin is not under- 
stood. The observed differences are rather minor and in the range 
of the estimated uncertainty and conclusions could not be made 
based on this result alone. 
Lastly, during the ﬁnal stages of the charge deviation for all the 
measured species is observed. In the conventional electrolyte the 
concentration maximum is reached then the concentrations rel- 
atively fast decreases, while the maximal concentrations of poly- 
sulﬁdes in the ﬂuorinated electrolyte form plateau. This observed 
lower variation of polysulﬁde concentrations in the ﬂuorinated 
electrolyte can be closely linked to the fact that the voltage in 
charge is more constant. Namely, the ﬂuorinated electrolyte ex- 
hibits a single voltage plateau during galvanostatic charge ( Fig. 7 a). 
In order to understand, whether this ﬂat plateau actually corre- 
sponds to only one thermodynamic equilibrium, GITT experiment 
was conducted. The red lines on Fig. 8 a show the well-known two 
plateau discharge and charge mechanism of operation for a Li- 
S cell with a conventional, glyme based electrolyte. Due to high 
polysulﬁde solubility, this electrolyte also shows overcharging and 
a smaller capacity compared to the ﬂuorinated electrolyte. During 
discharge the TFEE based electrolyte ( Fig. 8 b) exhibits the already 
discussed features – a shorter high voltage plateau (the fact that 
this potential is at a lower voltage can be explained with reduced 
polysulﬁde solubility [ 7 , 35 ]) and a third, unexplained plateau at 
low SOC. 
During charge, although the operational potential (black) seems 
ﬂat, the GITT experiment reveals there are at least two thermo- 
dynamic plateaus present. Given their position regarding SOC, we 
deduce that the lower plateau corresponds to the process of the 
unexplained third voltage plateau during discharge. Since most of 
the observed differences are at the later stages of discharge ( Fig. 7 ), 
this feature will be for present purposes disregarded. That is not 
to claim that further research should not be focused on the origin 
of this difference, but that this unidentiﬁed electrochemical pro- 
cess simply has no observable inﬂuence on the concentration and 
equilibria of soluble polysulﬁde species. The two voltage plateaus 
evident in the charge of the conventional electrolyte are therefore 
merged into a single one in the ﬂuorinated electrolyte at 2.25 V vs. 
Li/Li + , explaining the plateau in polysulﬁde concentrations. Lithium 
sulﬁde dissolution and sulfur precipitation during charge are there- 
fore less resolved in the ﬂuorinated electrolyte in comparison to 
the conventional electrolyte. This likely stems from the deviation 
in potentials of the two processes, which are closer together in the 
ﬂuorinated electrolyte, a consequence of solubility differences [7] . 
4. Conclusions 
The formation of polysulﬁde species through the ﬁrst cycles of 
Li-S battery cell operation was determined by HPLC. Two different 
electrolytes were employed, a more conventional electrolyte based 
on TEGDME solvent and a reduced polysulﬁde solubility electrolyte 
prepared from ﬂuorinated ether TFEE. 
The data obtained for the TEGDME based electrolyte is in line 
with previously published data, while separate determination of 
the polysulﬁde content in cathode and separator part of battery 
cell setup allowed deeper understanding of battery cell operation. 
We show additional evidence that entrapment of polysulﬁdes in 
the separator leads to poor performance, as the concentration of 
polysulﬁdes in the separator accounted for about 1/3 of the total 
polysulﬁde content, a value that is directly correlated with the ca- 
pacity. The samples obtained from the separators of the conven- 
tional electrolyte battery cells show a similar polysulﬁde distribu- 
tion regardless of SOC. The absence of large alteration is attributed 
to the disproportionation reactions and because precipitated sulfur 
and short polysulﬁde species remain on the cathode. Polysulﬁdes 
with a higher number of sulfur atoms are more likely to remain 
trapped in the separator of the conventional electrolyte, probably 
due to the higher solubility compared to more reduced polysul- 
ﬁdes. 
The concentration of polysulﬁdes detected in the cathode of 
the ﬂuorinated electrolyte is higher than in the conventional elec- 
trolyte, indicating that our analytical methods allow the detection

differences in the operating mechanism between the electrolytes 
were detected: (i) a shorter high voltage plateau during charging 
for TFEE is most likely a consequence of sulfur re-formation in the 
TEGDME based electrolyte due to disproportionation reactions; (ii) 
differences in the polysulﬁde trends in second part of the discharge 
also show signs of polysulﬁde solution reactions in the conven-

in TFEE has no effect on the concentration of soluble polysulﬁde 
species; (iv) the charge shows a ﬂatter plateau for the ﬂuorinated 
electrolyte – lithium sulﬁde dissolution and sulfur precipitation are 
less resolved, resulting in less variation in polysulﬁde concentra- 
tion trends. 
In summary, the conducted study shows the difference in the 
mechanism of operation between the chosen high and low poly- 
sulﬁde solubility electrolytes. The changes stem from a decrease of 
interactions between polysulﬁdes in solution (disproportionation, 
comproportionation) in ﬂuorinated ether based electrolyte, produc- 
ing changes in electrochemical performance. These results could 
likely be generalised to other Li-S battery electrolytes, where poly- 
sulﬁdes are formed but cannot be dissolved in the electrolyte in a 
substantial amount.
