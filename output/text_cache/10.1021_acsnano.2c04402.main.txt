“Dual Mediator System” Enables Efficient and
Persistent Regulation toward Sulfur Redox
Conversion in Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: Li−S batteries present great potential to realize high-energy-
density storage, but their practical implementation is severely hampered by
the notorious polysulfide shuttling and the sluggish redox kinetics. While
rationally designed redox mediators can optimize polysulfide conversion, the
efficiency and stability of such a mediation process still remain formidable
challenges. Herein, a strategy of constructing a “dual mediator system” is
proposed for achieving efficient and durable modulation of polysulfide
conversion kinetics by coupling well-selected solid and electrolyte-soluble
mediators. Theoretical prediction and detailed electrochemical analysis
reveal the structure−activity relationships of the two mediators in
synergistically optimizing the redox conversions of sulfur species, thus
achieving a deeper mechanistic understanding of a function-supporting
mediator system design toward sulfur electrochemistry promotion.
Specifically, such a dual mediator system realizes the bridging of full-range
“electrochemical catalysis” and strengthened “chemical reduction” processes of sulfur species as well as greatly suppressed
mediator deactivation/loss due to the beneficial interactions between each mediator component. Attributed to these
advantageous features, the Li−S batteries enable a slow capacity decay of 0.026% per cycle over 1200 cycles and a desirable
capacity of 8.8 mAh cm−2 with 8.2 mg cm−2 sulfur loading and lean electrolyte condition. This work not only proposes an
effective mediator system design strategy for promoting Li−S battery performance but also inspires its potential utilization
facing other analogous sophisticated electrochemical conversion processes.
KEYWORDS: lithium sulfur batteries, dual mediator system, polysulfide mediation, high-efficiency, long-term stability
O
wing to their ultrahigh theoretical energy density
(2600 Wh kg−1), together with the superiority of
sulfur element such as low cost, earth abundance, and
environmental benignity, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have
been regarded as one of the most promising candidates for the
next-generation energy storage systems.1−5 Nevertheless, the
electrochemical behavior of sulfur in Li−S chemistry is a
multiple-electron reaction process accompanied by complex
solid−liquid−solid transformations, which brings inherent and
inevitable hurdles toward the practical applications of Li−S
batteries. In particular, the outward diffusion of soluble lithium
polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates from the sulfur cathode
causes a severe shuttling effect, leading to low Coulombic
efficiency and poor cycling lifespan of the batteries. Moreover,
the sluggish conversion kinetics of LiPS intermediates in the
widely used ester electrolyte and the two-dimensional (2D)
layered deposition of lithium sulfides (Li2S) further give rise to
irreversible loss of active sulfur, limited rate capability, and
inferior sulfur utilization efficiency.6−10 Therefore, rational
management of the shuttling and conversion behaviors of
LiPSs is crucial in the pursuit of high-performance Li−S
batteries especially for those with increased sulfur loading and
reduced electrolyte content, i.e., low electrolyte/sulfur/ (E/S)
ratio.
The use of solid mediators such as metal nitrides,11−13
oxides,14−16 sulfides,17−20 and their heterostructures21−24 with
the capability of capturing and electrochemically catalyzing
LiPS conversion has shown potential to boost the performance

diffusion−conversion process is inevitably required for the
solid mediators during their catalysis process.25−27 However, as
illustrated in Figure 1a, the immobilization and catalytic
conversion of LiPSs merely occur on the surface of solid
mediators. The slow diffusion rate of LiPSs in the bulk
electrolyte, together with the limited specific surface areas of
solid mediators, significantly restrict the conversion efficiency.
Moreover, the insulating film-like Li2S precipitations tend to
form a dense layer on the cathode surface, which prevents the
further exposure of underneath active sites of solid mediators
to LiPSs and weakens their catalytic efficacy. This passivation
phenomenon is more severe for Li−S batteries with high sulfur
mass loadings and large depths of discharge. More recently,
cells comprising dual solid mediators with different functions
were also reported. For example, iron phthalocyanine (FePc)
and octafluoronaphthalene (OFN)-decorated graphene (Gh)
were utilized as a dual electrocatalytic regulation system to
accelerate the liquid−liquid and liquid−solid conversions of
LiPSs.28 In another work, two pseudocapacitive oxides with
suitable redox potentials were selected as dual solid chemical
mediators to promote the sulfur electrochemical reaction
kinetics by coupling spontaneous chemical reactions during the
discharging and charging processes.29 Although these dual
solid mediator systems have demonstrated encouraging
performance improvement of Li−S batteries, there are still
challenges in increasing the sulfur mass loading and reducing
the amounts of mediators required for efficient operation of
cells.
In comparison with the solid mediators, the electrolyte-
soluble redox mediators possess dynamic and more abundant
contact interfaces with the sulfur species, endowing them with
much enhanced LiPS mediation capability during battery
operation.30,31 The soluble redox mediators can function in
either the charging or discharging process of Li−S batteries,
depending on their redox potentials. Taking the discharging
process as an example, the soluble redox mediators in liquid
electrolyte with a slightly lower redox potential than that of
electrochemical formation of Li2S can chemically drive the
reduction of LiPSs to solid Li2S,32 which leads to uniform and
three-dimensional (3D) deposition of Li2S at the entire
cathode−liquid electrolyte interfaces and a higher discharging
capacity as well (Figure 1b). Moreover, the oxidized redox
mediators at the electrolyte−cathode interfaces can also be
reduced through catching electrons from the cathode substrate
upon further discharge, which recovers their chemical
mediation capability and enables them to function in a
continuous manner. Recently, several electrolyte-soluble redox
mediators such as an organometallic compound,33 ethyl
viologen diperchlorate,34 and anthraquinone derivatives35
have been developed to mediate the deposition and dissolution
processes of Li2S. Unfortunately, similar to LiPSs, most of the
soluble redox mediators still suffer from internal shuttling
issues during cycling due to their uncontrolled mobility, which
not only leads to gradually decreased mediation capability for
LiPS conversion but also causes strong corrosion of the lithium
anode (Figure 1b). As a result, the battery cycling lifespan is
severely restricted.
Based on the above considerations, due to the inherent
functional limitation, it is difficult for an individual mediator to
achieve full-scale enhancement toward the sophisticated sulfur
conversion processes, which involve multistep solid−liquid−
solid electrochemical transformations and associated chemical
comproportionation and disproportionation reactions of LiPSs
occurring in the electrolyte. Nevertheless, considering the
different mediation pathways of solid and soluble mediators,
the rational coupling of them with function-oriented selection
and design is highly considered to be a reasonable strategy to
comprehensively promote the electrochemical performance of
working Li−S batteries. Specifically, the ideal coupling mode of

process, the CoS1.097 NPs in CoSNC catalyze the polysulfides
electrochemically, while the CoCp2 chemically reduces the
shuttling polysulfides in the electrolyte, synergistically con-

configuration of Li2S mediated by CoCp2 during discharging
can in turn guarantee the smooth charge transfer with the

oxidation process on CoS1.097 NPs, greatly alleviating the
deactivation risk of a solid mediator. Meanwhile, the porous
nitrogen-doped carbon sheets in CoSNC with high chemical
affinity to CoCp2 can favorably restrain them in the cathode
region, thus achieving persistent polysulfide mediation function
and suppressed lithium anode corrosion. Attributed to these
advantageous function-supporting features, the dual mediator
system endows Li−S batteries with a long-term cycling stability
with a capacity retention of over 67% after 1200 cycles at 2 C.
Even with the raised sulfur loadings of 3.7 and 8.2 mg cm−2, an
impressive cycling stability with the capacity retention of 71%
over 300 cycles at 0.5 C and a high areal capacity of 8.8 mAh
cm−2 at a low E/S of 8 are also achieved. This work elucidates
the structure−activity relationships for efficiently manipulating
sulfur redox conversions based on a function-supporting dual
mediator system, which offers a promising strategy for
promoting Li−S batteries approaching practical applications.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The CC@CoSNC was prepared through a two-step process, as
illustrated schematically in Figure 2a. First, uniform ZIF-67
nanosheet arrays were in situ grown on the surface of carbon
cloth (denoted as CC@ZIF67) via the coordination reactions
of cobalt ions and 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2) in aqueous
medium (Figure S1a),36 and then the CC@ZIF67 reacted with
the pyrolysis products of thioacetamide (TAA) in an Ar/H2
mixture to form CC@CoSNC (Figure S1b). Four-point probe
measurement shows that the CC@CoSNC exhibits a high
electrical conductivity of 3246 S m−1. The carbon cloth (CC)
substrates not only provide excellent electron conduction and
ion diffusion pathways but also accommodate the volume
variation of the cathode effectively during cycling, particularly
under the high sulfur loading conditions.
The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of CC@
CoSNC with different magnifications are shown in Figure 2b
and c. The vertically grown CoSNC nanosheet arrays with a
leaf-like morphology are uniformly distributed on the surface
of CC. The N2 adsorption−desorption result shows that the
CC@CoSNC sample has a much larger specific surface area
(SSA) of 109 m2 g−1 than that of the pristine CC, due to the
growth of CoSNC arrays (Figure S4). The enlarged SSA of
CC@CoSNC is beneficial for capturing the LiPSs and
electrolyte-soluble redox mediator during battery operation.
The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of a
typical CoSNC nanosheet clearly shows that CoS1.097 NPs of
∼5 nm in size are evenly embedded in the 2D carbon matrix
(Figure 2d). The corresponding selected area electron
diffraction (SAED) pattern (inset in Figure 2d) verifies that
the CoS1.097 NPs are randomly oriented. Close observation by
high-resolution TEM (Figure 2e) reveals clear lattice fringes
with a d-spacing of 0.25 nm, which corresponds to the (220)
planes of CoS1.097 and matches well with the X-ray diffraction
(XRD) (Figure S2) and SAED patterns.37 From the energy-
dispersive spectrum (EDS) mapping images, C, N, Co, and S
elements are found to be dispersed in the entire CoSNC
nanosheet, demonstrating the homogeneous distribution of
CoS1.097 NPs in the nitrogen-doped carbon matrix (Figure S5).
In the catalytic process of LiPSs, the adsorption of LiPSs by
mediators is essential to their subsequent conversion to Li2S.
To evaluate the chemical interaction between the solid
mediator and LiPSs, the static Li2S6 adsorption tests were
conducted, as shown in Figure 3a. It is noted that the Li2S6
solution with CC@CoSNC emerging becomes completely
colorless, while the solution with only CC remains a deep
yellow color. The test demonstrates the strong adsorption
ability of CC@CoSNC toward LiPSs. To further explore the
adsorption mechanism, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) was conducted to characterize the corresponding
surface states and adsorption sites of CC@CoSNC. The Co
2p3/2 peaks located at 778.1 and 780.8 eV shift to higher
binding energies, i.e., 779.2 and 781.5 eV, respectively, after the
adsorption of Li2S6. Similarly, the Co 2p1/2 peaks at 793.1 and
796.8 eV also shift by 1.2 and 0.8 eV, respectively, toward
higher binding energies after capturing Li2S6 (Figure 3b).37 In
the corresponding Li 1s spectra in Figure S6, a new peak
located at 55.8 eV is observed, which is ascribed to the Li−N
binding after the adsorption of Li2S6.38,39 Density functional
theory (DFT) calculation was also performed to disclose the
interfacial interaction between CC@CoSNC and LiPSs at the
atomic level. As shown in Figure 3c, the binding energy of
Li2S6 on the surface of CoS1.097 with optimized adsorption
configuration is −4.30 eV, much higher than those of pure
carbon (−0.53 eV) and nitrogen-doped carbon (−0.87 eV) in
previous literature, convincingly demonstrating the CoS1.097
NCs in CC@CoSNC are the main polysulfide-anchoring
sites.40 The above results collectively elucidate the strong
chemical interactions between CoSNC and LiPSs, which is
helpful to immobilize sulfur species within the cathode and
thus reduce their shuttling during the cycling process.
Similarly, the binding ability of CoSNC to CoCp2 was also
evaluated. As presented in Figure 3d, in comparison with
CoCp2 solution with CC, the solution merged with CC@
CoSNC exhibits an obviously lighter color, intuitively
indicating the strong adsorption capability of CoSNC toward
CoCp2. XPS analysis was further performed to reveal the
underlying interaction between CoSNC and CoCp2. In the N
1s spectra, the peaks of pyridinic N located at 398.3 eV and
pyrrolic N located at 400.1 eV shift to a higher binding energy
of 398.5 and 400.3 eV, respectively, after CoCp2 adsorption
(Figure 3e). This can be attributed to the strong dipole−dipole
electrostatic interaction between the active N sites and
CoCp2.41 Such interactions can also be verified by the DFT
calculation results, as shown in Figure 3f and Figure S7, in
which both pyridinic N and pyrrolic N on the carbon surface
show an obvious adsorption ability toward CoCp2. The above
results demonstrate that the solid mediator (CoSNC)
facilitates the chemical immobilization of both LiPSs and
CoCp2, which effectively retards the shuttling loss of sulfur and
soluble mediator as well as the consequent corrosion of the
lithium anode during battery operation.

Furthermore, to gain insight into the mediation capability of
different redox mediators for the kinetics of LiPS conversion,

scanning rate of 20 mV s−1, as shown in Figure 4a. Two

kinetics for polysulfide conversion can also lead to decent

A potentiostatic Li2S precipitation test in different mediation
systems was conducted to evaluate the impact of redox
mediators on the LiPS conversion process (Figure 4g−j).43,44
The cells for the test were assembled by using a Li2S8/
tetraglyme solution as the catholyte. Obviously, the Li2S
precipitation in the CC@CoSNC/CoCp2 cell is most effective,
i.e., delivering the much higher precipitation current and
precipitation capacity of 152.1 mA h g−1 (versus 60.3 mA h g−1
for CC, 102.4 mA h g−1 for CC@CoSNC, and 89.7 mA h g−1
for CC/CoCp2). The morphologies of Li2S precipitates were
further characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
(inset of Figure 4g−j). For the CC cell, only a thin Li2S layer
and scattered Li2S sediments are observed on the surface of the
carbon fiber. In contrast, the amount of deposited Li2S
increases significantly on CC@CoSNC, indicating the strong
capability of CoSNC in mediating the deposition process of
Li2S. On the other hand, when CoCp2 is introduced in the
electrolyte, a 3D porous Li2S layer is deposited on the carbon
fiber as compared with the case without CoCp2, implying that
the CoCp2 also enables the increase of the deposition of Li2S
through promoting sulfur conversion in the electrolyte and
thus the improvement of the discharging capacity. By
combining the mediation effects of both solid CoSNC and
soluble CoCp2, a further increased Li2S precipitation is
achieved. The observation of a 3D deposition of porous Li2S
also suggests CoCp2 molecules more likely attain a dynamic
adsorption−desorption equilibrium state on the nitrogen-
doped carbon sheets in CoSNC, forming a CoCp2-enriched
zone close to the scaffold surface, which is consistent with the
mediation feature of CoCp2 dissolved in electrolyte. These
results demonstrate that the synchronous reinforcement of
electrochemical and chemical conversion toward LiPSs by the
dual mediator system favors both enhanced reaction kinetics
and greatly improved sulfur utilization during the discharging
process. In addition, the oxidation of precipitated Li2S was
further probed by applying a potentiostatic charging process at
2.35 V (Figure S14a−d). The oxidation current and
dissolution capacity are much higher for the CC@CoSNC/
CoCp2 cell in comparison with the other three cases, which is
consistent with the CV results and further demonstrates a
more efficient Li2S dissolution process. The higher Li2S
dissolution efficiency is believed to be mainly due to the
porous 3D configuration of Li2S precipitates mediated by
CoCp2 during discharging, which in turn favors the catalytic
oxidation of Li2S on the surface of the CC@CoSNC
framework during the process of charging and leads to a
greatly improved Li2S dissolution capacity.
Li−S cells with different mediators, denoted as CC@
CoSNC−S, CC/CoCp2−S, and CC@CoSNC/CoCp2−S,
were assembled, and their rate and cycling performance were
evaluated. The cell without mediators (CC−S) was also
assembled as a control sample. Note that the concentration of
CoCp2 in electrolyte was optimized based on the electro-
chemical performance of cells as shown in Figure S15, and 20
mM of CoCP2, which delivered the highest capacity and the
best stability, was selected for cell assembly. Based on the
immobilization capability of CoSNC to CoCp2, a too high
CoCp2 concentration (e.g., 80 mM) may still have the internal
shuttling problem of CoCP2, leading to an inferior cycling
stability. As shown in Figure 5a, the dual mediator system
renders superb rate capability for working Li−S cells; that is,
the CC@CoSNC/CoCp2−S cell delivers significantly higher
capacities of 1540, 1289, 1162, 1005, 816, and 665 mAh g−1 at
0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 C (1 C = 1675 mA gS
−1), respectively,
as compared with other cells. As the current density is switched
back to the initial 0.1 C, the discharging capacity can be largely
recovered to 1255 mAh g−1 (∼81.5% of that obtained at the
initial current density of 0.1 C), suggesting a high reversibility
and rapid reaction kinetics. Moreover, based on the charging
and discharging plateau at 0.1 C, the CC@CoSNC/CoCp2−S
cell is calculated to have a smaller voltage variation (ΔE = 166
mV), as compared to the ΔE values of 186, 198, and 232 mV
for CC@CoSNC−S, CC/CoCp2−S, and CC−S, respectively
(Figure S16a). Besides, the second discharge plateau of the
CC@CoSNC/CoCp2 cell is also about 21 mV higher than that
of the CC@CoSNC cell (Figure S16b). As illustrated by the
charge−discharge curves of the CC@CoSNC/CoCp2−S at
various current densities (0.1−4 C) in Figure 5b, two typical

To gain an in-depth understanding of the “dual mediator

DFT calculations were performed to investigate the conversion
of Li2S4 to Li2S2 and further to Li2S, as these conversion steps
require higher activation energy than the former reactions (i.e.,
S8 to Li2S4) and are the main rate-determining steps during the

shown in Figure 5e. Based on the calculated results, the Gibbs
reaction energy of the conversion from Li2S4 to Li2S2 in the
CoS1.097@CoCp2 system is 0.56 eV, that is, lower than that in
CoS1.097 system (0.70 eV), which demonstrates that the
reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S2 in the dual mediator system is more
favorable. For the reduction of Li2S2 to Li2S, which is the
kinetically slowest step during the discharging process, the
Gibbs reaction energy in the CoS1.097 system (1.10 eV) is
much higher than that needed for conversion in the CoS1.097@
CoCp2 system (0.90 eV). These findings indicate that the
reduction processes of sulfur species are more thermodynami-
cally favorable in the CoS1.097@CoCp2 dual mediator system
because such a system can significantly decrease the reaction
barrier of LiPSs and facilitate the Li2S precipitation for
improved sulfur utilization.

CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, the cooperative dual mediator system,
constructed by coupling solid CoSNC and electrolyte-soluble
CoCp2, is found to be an effective method to attain high-
efficiency and durable regulation toward LiPSs conversion in
Li−S batteries. The combination of “electrochemical catalysis”
and “chemical reduction” synergistically optimizes the
conversion behaviors of LiPSs with accelerated redox kinetics
and desired Li2S deposition morphology, offering superior rate
capability and improved sulfur utilization. In addition, the
CoSNC with a porous architecture and increased active surface
facilitates the spatial confinement and chemical immobilization
of both LiPSs and soluble CoCp2, which effectively retards the
shuttling loss of sulfur and soluble mediator as well as the
consequent corrosion of the lithium anode during battery
operation. As a result, the Li−S batteries with an ultralow
capacity fading of 0.026% per cycle over 1200 cycles at 2 C and
an excellent rate capability up to 665 mAh g−1 at 4 C are
achieved. Even under lean-electrolyte conditions of a low E/S
of 8, a high areal capacity of 8.8 mAh cm−2 with a sulfur
loading up to 8.2 mg cm−2 is also demonstrated. This work
suggests a promising strategy through a mediation system
design to improve the performance of Li−S batteries toward
practical applications.
METHODS AND EXPERIMENTAL
Raw Materials. All raw materials, including sulfur power (S),
lithium sulfides (Li2S), cobaltocene (CoCp2), cobaltous nitrate
hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O), 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2), car-
bon cloth (CC, areal mass: 10.5 mg cm−2), thioacetamide (TAA),
blank Li−S electrolyte, 1,3-dioxetane (DOL), 1,2-diamethoxyethane
(DME), tetraglyme, deionized (DI) water, ethanol, carbon disulfide
(CS2), Super P, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), N-methyl-2-
pyrrolidinone (NMP), polypropylene (PP) separator (Celgard
2400), and lithium (Li) foil are purchased from commercial sources.
Preparation of the Soluble Redox Mediator Containing
Electrolyte. The CoCp2 was directly dissolved into the blank Li−S
electrolyte (1 M bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with 1
wt % LiNO3 in a DME and DOL mixture (1:1 vol)). The electrolytes
with different CoCp2 concentrations (20, 40, and 80 mM) were
obtained after 12 h of stirring.
Synthesis of the Solid Mediator. The CC@ZIF-67 was
synthesized by a facile room-temperature crystallization and
deposition strategy. In a typical preparation, an aqueous solution
containing 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2, 40 mL, 0.4 M) was quickly
added into the aqueous solution of Co(NO3)2·6H2O (40 mL, 0.05
M). After thorough mixing, a piece of CC was immersed into the
above mixed solution. After reaction for 6 h at room temperature, the
as-obtained sample was then taken out, washed with deionized water
and ethanol three times, and finally vacuum-dried overnight.
The synthesis of CC@CoSNC was achieved by a simultaneous
high-temperature sulfidation and carbonization process. Typically, a
crucible containing 2 g of TAA power was located at the upstream
position of the tube furnace, while the CC@ZIF-67 sample was
placed in the downstream position. The synthesis reaction was
conducted under the Ar/H2 mixture (95:5) flow at 30 sccm, and the
furnace temperature was maintained at 500 °C for 2 h with a ramp
rate of 2 °C min−1, followed by naturally cooling to ambient
temperature. Then the sample was cleaned with deionized water and
ethanol three times and vacuum-dried overnight to obtain the final
CC@CoSNC. The mass loading of CoSNC on CC was about 0.8 mg
cm−2, resulting in a proportion of ∼7% of the CoSNC in CC@
CoSNC.
Materials Characterization. The morphologies of the as-
prepared materials, Li2S deposition, and Li metal anode after cycling
were characterized by the scanning electron microscope (Philips
XL30 FEG). The microstructure of samples was analyzed by TEM
(JEM-2100F, Japan). XRD patterns were obtained by using a Rigaku
D/MAX 2500/PC diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 100
mA, λ = 0.154 nm) to investigate the crystal structure of the samples.
Raman spectra were measured by a multiwavelength micro-Raman
spectrometer (LabRAM HR800, HORIBA Jobin Yvon, Japan) using
an Ar-ion laser of 532 nm. The SSA of samples was calculated by the
Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) equation according to the N2
adsorption−desorption isotherms tested by a Micromeritics ASAP
2020 analyzer at 77 K. XPS (VG ESCALAB 220i-XL) was used to
detect the chemical states of as-prepared materials and their
interaction with polysulfides and CoCp2.
Li−S Cell Assembly. Two kinds of substrates (CC and CC@
CoSNC) were used to load sulfur as composite cathodes. To
uniformly distribute sulfur on the substrates, 0.5 g of sulfur powder
was dissolved in 10 mL of CS2. The CC or CC@CoSNC substrate
was completely soaked in the CS2 solution for 5 min and then dried at
ambient temperature for 12 h. Finally, the composites were placed in
the tube furnace under N2 protection and heated at 155 °C for 12 h to
obtain the uniform composite cathodes. The average sulfur loading is
about 1.2 mg cm−2.
The composite cathodes with high sulfur loading were fabricated as
in the following procedures. First, the carbon/sulfur composite was
prepared by homogeneously mixing sulfur power, Super P, and PVDF
binder (90:5:5 by weight) in NMP. Next, the composite slurry was
uniformly pasted to the CC@CoSNC substrate and dried at 55 °C in
a vacuum oven overnight to obtain the composite cathodes with a
sulfur loading of 3.7 and 8.2 mg cm−2, respectively.
Electrochemical Characterization. Common CR2032 coin cells
were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. The CC−S or CC@
CoSNC−S composite cathodes were cut into small pieces with a
diameter of 12 mm. The diameter of each separator is 16 mm. A
lithium metal foil was used as the counter electrode. The amount of
blank electrolyte or electrolyte containing CoCp2 was precisely
controlled with an E/S ratio of 15 μL (electrolyte)/mg (sulfur) for
composite cathodes with sulfur loadings of 1.2 and 3.7 mg cm−2, and
that of 8.2 mg cm−2 was employed for the lean electrolyte test with an
E/S ratio of 8. The cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7 and
2.8 V (vs Li+/Li) on a Neware battery tester. An Ivium electro-
chemical workstation was used for CV measurements over the
potential range of 1.7−2.8 V (vs Li+/Li) at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1.
The Li−S cells with a sulfur loading of 1.2 mg cm−2 were
disassembled after 300 cycles, and the lithium disks were taken out
and washed to observe the morphology.
In Situ EIS Testing. The cells for in situ EIS testing were
assembled with CC−S and CC@CoSNC−S as cathodes and lithium
metal as the anode, together with the adding of blank electrolyte or
electrolyte containing CoCp2. The testing was carried out at a
constant current of 0.2 C for the discharging process between 2.8 and
1.7 V. Based on the first discharge process, different depths of
discharging (DOD) were selected for EIS testing until the cells were
discharged to 1.7 V. There was a 30 min settling before measuring
each impedance. The frequency range of the testing was between 100
kHz and 0.01 Hz with a 5 mV amplitude.
Visualized Adsorption of Polysulfides and CoCp2. A
polysulfide solution (0.1 M Li2S6) was prepared by mixing sulfur
and lithium sulfide (Li2S) at a molar ratio of 5:1 in DOL/DME (v/v =
1:1) under vigorous stirring. Afterward, the disks of CC or CC@
CoSNC with the same diameter of 16 mm were added in sealed vials
containing Li2S6 (30 μL, 0.1 M) diluted with another 5 mL of the
above solvents. The digital pictures of absorption efficiency for Li2S6
by different samples were taken after 24 h.

Experiments for the Deposition and Dissolution of Lithium
Sulfide. The Li2S8 solution was prepared by combining sulfur power
and Li2S powder at a molar ratio of 7:1 in tetraglyme under vigorous
stirring for 24 h. The CC or CC@CoSNC disk with a diameter of 12
mm was used as the current substrates to assemble the coin cells, and
a piece of lithium foil was used as the counter electrode. A 25 μL
amount of Li2S8 (0.2 mol L−1) solution was dropped on the cathode
side, and then 20 μL of blank electrolyte with or without CoCp2 was
dropped on the separator. For the Li2S deposition test, the above cells
were first galvanostatically discharged at 0.112 mA to 2.06 V and then
kept at 2.05 V until the current was below 10−5 A. As for the Li2S
dissolution test, the cells were first galvanostatically discharged at
0.112 mA to 1.7 V and then potentiostatically discharged for another
3.0 h to ensure thorough conversion of Li2S. Afterward, the cells were
potentiostatically charged at 2.35 V until the charge current was below
10−5 A for the complete dissolution of Li2S.
Theoretical Calculations. The DFT calculations were performed
using a Dmol3 module of Materials Studio 2017. A surface model was
cleaved from this supercell to simulate the (0 0 1) surface of CoS1.097.
The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method with the
Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) function was employed to describe
the interactions between the core and electrons. In the vertical
direction, a vacuum layer of about 10 Å in thickness was introduced
for the surface. For the decomposition energy and adsorption
conformation simulations, the DFT-D functional included the
physical van der Waals interaction, which was very important in the
simulation of Li2Sx adsorption. The force and energy convergence
criterion were set to 0.002 Ha Å−1 and 10−5 Ha, respectively.
