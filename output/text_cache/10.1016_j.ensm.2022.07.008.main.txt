A B S T R A C T  
With their high energy density and environmental friendliness, lithium/sulfur (Li/S) batteries represent a 
promising candidate for the post-lithium-ion era. However, complex degradation mechanisms and a lack of 
microscopic insight into the molecular structure of the polysulfide (PS) and ether-based electrolyte materials 
have hindered the broad commercialization of this energy storage system so far. Here, neutron scattering ex­
periments combined with electronic-continuum corrected molecular dynamics simulations of typical ether-based 
electrolytes for lithium/sulfur batteries were used to elucidate the complex electrolyte and PS structure. We find 
that a delicate balance between ion-ion and ion-solvent interactions steers the ordering and undesired clustering 
of Li+ and PS ions at moderate concentrations (> 1 M). In particular, ‘solvent-separated’ Li+ ions and PS/anions 
induce a cation and anion short-ranged alternating structure. At these moderate concentrations, we show the 
onset of the decoupling of diffusion processes of Li+ ions into caged and jump diffusions within the 3D TFSI−
networks. Our study and the presented combination of methods are extendable to other electrolytes and com­
positions and provide a significant step forward in the modeling and understanding of next-generation battery 
materials.   
1. Introduction 
Driven by the ever-growing electric vehicle sales and renewable 
energy generation, the demand for energy storage systems increases. 
Highly efficient and economically attractive battery technologies are 
expected to play a crucial role in transitioning to a sustainable energy 
system. Lithium-sulfur (Li/S) batteries are a promising candidate for 
next-generation energy storage systems because of their low cost, safety, 
and high theoretical specific energy of 2600 W h kg−1 [1]. 
However, the commercial applications of Li/S batteries are still 
hindered by several intrinsic problems, such as the dendrite formation 
and the reactive Li-metal electrode, the significant volume change of the 
active materials, the insulating properties of S8 and Li2S, and the poly­
sulfide (PS) shuttle during the charging. Among these issues, the PS 
shuttle is considered to be the primary factor causing rapid capacity 
fading and low Coulombic efficiency [2]. The shuttle effect is caused by 
highly soluble intermediate PS ions, which escape from the cathode side, 
and "shuttle" between the cathode and anode during charging, resulting 
in parasitic reactions on the Li metal anode. Subsequently, it forms 
insulating layers on the Li metal surface, leading to fast capacity fading 
in Li/S batteries. 
Various strategies have been attempted to address this shuttle effect.

They include the physical confinement of sulfur into a highly ordered 
porous carbon [3–5], the chemisorption between the host materials and 
sulfur [6–9], interlayer materials [10,11], or sulfurized-conductive 
polymers [12,13]. From the electrolyte side, highly concentrated elec­
trolytes [14], ionic liquids [15], restricting the polysulfide solubility, or 
the use of sparingly solvating electrolytes [16–20] have been explored. 
Recently, the opposite direction of maximizing the solubility of poly­
sulfides by using high donor solvents or salts has also been actively 
studied in the context of the low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio [21–24]. 
The formation of the ionic pairs or aggregates strongly depends on 
the ionic strength, concentration, polar/non-polar, donor ability, and 
the shapes of ions and solvents [2,18]. Such ion pairs or aggregates 
affect ionic conductivity, diffusion, and solubility of Li+ and PS ions in 
Li/S electrolytes. The shuttling PS species during charging can be 
influenced by the Li+-Sn
2– association or clustering [25]. 
Although the properties of electrolytes for Li/S batteries are critical 
to the electrochemistry [26] and the shuttle effect [15,18,23,27,28], a 
deeper understanding of the structure of Li/S electrolytes is still 
controversial. Previously, density functional theory calculations [29,30] 
revealed that Li/PS clusters or networks are more favorable than simple 
Li2Sn. Studies with classical force-field based molecular dynamics (MD) 
simulations [31,32] also reported aggregated PSs in the solutions. 
Andersen et al. [32] found that the computationally calculated NMR and 
X-ray absorption spectroscopies for the aggregated PSs chains agree with 
experiments. Meanwhile, our previous study [25] showed that the ten­
dency to cluster decreases with PS chain length, though single PS units 
prevail. Up to now, several experimental and theoretical studies have 
been investigating the stable PS species in the Li/S battery electrolytes. 
Nevertheless, the structure of the liquid PS electrolytes is still not fully 
understood. 
In this study, we use a combination of neutron scattering experi­
ments and the electronic continuum corrected (ECC) method [33–40], 
‘ECC-MD’ simulations to elucidate the liquid PS electrolyte structure. 
Neutron scattering experiments can provide a total structure factor for 
liquid electrolytes, a robust experimental benchmark for solution 
structure. At the same time, MD simulation trajectories can be used to 
obtain the total structure factor. As shown previously, ECC-MD can 
provide consistent insight into electrolyte structure and dynamics in 
organic and aqueous solvents [25,34,39,41]. Combining neutron ex­
periments and MD simulations provides direct insights into the liquid 
structural properties of electrolytes for Li/S batteries and elucidates the 
complex interaction of the lithium salts with the PS. 
2. Experimental section 
All chemicals were used without further purification and were pur­
chased from Sigma Aldrich except for the deuterated dimethoxyethane 
(d-DME), which was ordered from Eurisotop. Lithium sulfide and sulfur 
were mixed in the respective ratios to obtain the desired stoichiometric 
ratio for the different concentrations of PS solutions (0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 
M). The two fully deuterated solvents used are tetrahydrofuran (d-THF) 
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (d-DME). THF was used instead of the mainly 
used 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) because of its more straightforward deutera­
tion procedure and its chemical similarity. The various solutions were 
mixed with PS, lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide lithium salt 
(LiTFSI), and LiNO3. In order to gain a clearer picture of the liquid 
structure and the functionality of real electrolytes for PS batteries, we 
prepared an electrolyte solution (‘EL’), which was utilized in Li/S bat­
tery experiments [42,43]. The investigated chemical compounds are 
shown in Fig. 1 and their solutions summarized in Table 1 (detailed 
computational setup is tabulated in Table S1 in the Supporting Infor­
mation (SI)). 
Neutron diffraction data were collected using the Near and Inter­
Mediate Range Order Diffractometer (NIMROD) instrument [44] at the 
ISIS spallation neutron source (Harwell, U.K.). The liquid samples were 
prepared in a glovebox filled with argon atmosphere, and null scattering 
Ti0.68Zr0.32 alloy cells were used, as this avoids potential issues with 
cell background subtraction from Bragg peaks. These flat sample con­
tainers had internal dimensions of 35 × 35 × 1 mm and were loaded into 
an automatic sample changer. All measurements were conducted at 
room temperature, which was around 24 ◦C. The neutron scattering of 
the samples was measured with a beam size of 30 × 30 mm for at least 2 
h. The measured neutron scattering was reduced to the differential 
scattering cross-section F(q) using the program GudrunN [45,46]. This 
program summarizes the time-of-flight scattering from all detectors to a 
single q-scale, normalizes to a 3 mm VNb plate calibration standard, 
subtracts the scattering from the sample container and empty instru­
ment, and applies corrections for beam attenuation, multiple scattering, 
and inelastic scattering [47]. 
3. Molecular dynamics simulations

THF and DME solvents, Dang parameters for Li+ ions [49], the DREID­
ING force field for the S atoms [50], and the CL&P force field for TFSI– 
ions [51]. Lennard-Jones interaction parameters (σij and εij) between 
different species (i and j) were calculated using the geometric combi­
nation rules.

MD simulations were carried out with the GROMACS simulation 
package using the velocity Verlet algorithm for integrating Newton’s 
equations of motion [52]. Initial simulation structures of ions in the 
solvent were constructed with the PACKMOL package [53]. The mole­
cules were randomly inserted into the simulation box and then first 
equilibrated by the energy minimization procedure, followed by an NPT 
simulation (fixed number of particles, pressure, and temperature) using 
the Berendsen et al. [54] and Parrinello and Rahman [55] barostats (the 
equilibrated cubic box size L was ~7 nm). The production simulations 
were carried out with a time step of 2 fs in the NVT ensemble (fixed 
number of particles, volume, and temperature) at 298 K (see Table S1). 
The atom positions were stored every 6 ps. The temperature was 
controlled by the Nos´e-Hoover chain thermostat [56] with a time con­
stant of 0.8 ps. The cutoff of the non-bonded interactions was set to 1.2 
nm, with a standard dispersion correction for energy and pressure. The 
particle mesh Ewald method with a Fourier spacing of 0.12 nm and a 1.2 
nm real-space cutoff was used for calculating electrostatic interactions 
[57]. The LINCS algorithm was used for all bond constraints [58]. Pe­
riodic boundary conditions were used to approximate infinite bulk 
behavior. The equilibration was quantified by monitoring the average 
molecular cluster size <N> of PS, see Fig. S1 in the SI. A cluster was 
defined as a group of PS ions whose at least one terminal S atom ap­
proaches a terminal S atom of another PS ion within at least 0.53 nm 
[25].

In general, the structure factor F(q) in Fig. 2 features two peaks: the 
main one at around 1.4 Å−1 and the low q peak between 0.4 and 0.9 Å−1. 
The main peak can be attributed to the size and packing of the THF 
molecules and is therefore present in all samples (see the RDF analysis 
and the partial structure factor in Figs. S4 and S5 in the SI. Note that 
peaks in F(q) and real-space correlation lengths are related via r = 2π/q). 
The low q peak develops by adding LiTFSI salt or PS into the THF solvent 
(LT1-PS00, Fig. 2b, and Fig. S5 in the SI). Partial structure factor analysis 
reveals that adding LiTFSI in LT1-PS00 increases the contributions from 
the C-D and D-D atom pairs (carbon ‘C’ and deuterium ‘D’) to the low q 
peak (shown in Fig. S5 in the SI). Even though the Li-Li and N-N pairs 
also feature the same low q peak, their absolute contribution is negli­
gible (see Fig. S6). Namely, the weighted structure factor cicjbibjSij(q) is 
dominated by the carbon ‘C’ and deuterium ‘D’ species from solvent 
molecules. Meanwhile, as the LiTFSI ion pairs feature a long-range 
correlation and also affect the solvent structure (‘C’ and ‘D’ species), 
they contribute to the low q peak. 
Similar observations regarding LiTFSI were reported by Aguilera 
et al. [60], stating that the low q peak between 0.8 and 1.0 Å−1 in 
LiTFSI/tetraglyme (G4) electrolytes is due to a charge alternation of 
Li+/G4 complexes and TFSI anions. Namely, the long carbon and oxygen 
chain in G4 embraces (chelates) Li+ ions, forming solvent-separated ion 
pairs. These Li+/G4 complexes act as positive ions, and together with 
TFSI anions, they form alternating structures reminiscent of ionic liquids 
[60]. Although the chelate effect is not expected to occur in our systems 
with the THF solvent, we attribute the low q peak in the presence of 
LiTFSI salt to an alternating structure of Li+ and TFSI−ions. In addition, 
the angular distributions of TFSI-Li-TFSI formations within the second 
solvation shell of Li+ ions present preferential angles around 90 degrees 
(see Fig. S7 in the SI). Namely, instead of random configurations of Li+

Additional insight into the diffusion dynamics can be gained from 
mean squared displacements (MSD) of Li+ ions (see Fig. S14). Very 
generally, MSD enters the normal diffusion regime (MSD ∝ t) after a 
sufficient lapsed time. The normal diffusion regime is commonly pre­
ceded by a sub-diffusion regime (MSD ∝ tα with α < 1). We observe that 
the crossover time into the normal diffusion regime is longer for higher 
PS concentrations, culminating in the LT1-PS10 system. 
Note that in glass-forming supercooled liquids and dense colloidal 
suspensions, sub-diffusive regimes can be extremely long, resulting in a 
quasi sub-diffusive plateau [61,62]. 
Moreover, as shown by our analysis with the van Hove functions (see 
Fig. S15), the MSDs in weakly concentrated systems (LT1-PS00, LT0- 
PS05) are nearly Gaussian distributed. However, they start to deviate 
from Gaussianity in EL, LT0-PS10, LT1-PS05, and particularly in LT1- 
PS10 (see the inset of panel f in Fig. S15). In the latter systems, the 
tails of the distributions are ticker (nearly exponential) than in the 
Gaussian distribution. The exponential tails are a universal signature of 
the jump (or hopping) diffusion: the particle dwells most of the time in a 
local cage and performers larger jumps into a new cage after a large 
enough thermal fluctuation. In our case, the entrapment of Li ions is due 
to the association with PS ions. 
As we have seen from RDFs in Fig. 3, the first Li+ solvation shell is 
mainly dominated by solvent molecules, followed by PS. Thus, Li+ ions 
diffuse with their solvating molecules, termed a vehicle diffusion. By 
adding LiTFSI salt into the PS solution (e.g., LT1-PS10), a short-ranged 
alternating structure among TFSI and Li within the second solvation 
shell of Li+ starts to form, which is also seen in the enhanced RDF peak at 
around 10 Å. The diffusion coefficients of Li+ and TFSI−in LT1-PS10 
(Fig. 6) and the autocorrelation function (Fig. S17) imply that their 
motions are fairly synchronized. We suppose that the ‘semi-ordered’ 
anion structure within the second solvation shell of Li+ influences the 
diffusion of Li+ in such a way that the Li+ diffusion process is composed 
of caged and jump diffusions. 
5. Discussion 
We have shown that the main peak and the low q peak in Fig. 2 are 
related to the packing of solvent molecules and the alternating structure 
of cations and anions. ECC-MD simulations indicate that this alternating 
structure occurs only when ions are separated by solvent molecules. 
Fig. 7 shows representative snapshots of ion distributions in different 
systems. The dashed lines depict the connectivity of Li+ ions in the 
distance 7-12 Å (which corresponds to the low q peak in F(q)). In LT0- 
PS10, Li+ and S 2−
6 
ions are uniformly distributed over space (Fig. 7a), 
but S 2−
6 
ions are able to contact Li+ ions via terminal S atoms instead 
(see Fig. 3d), forming weak Li+-S6 networks. Although the electrostatic 
force from Li+ ions attract anions, Li+ and S 2−
6 
ion pairs are frequently 
disrupted by solvent molecules (reflected by the coordination number of 
the terminal S 2−
6 
in Fig. S12c). This is attributed to the relatively lower 
electron density at terminal S atoms of S 2−
6 
ions compared to shorter PS 
chains [25]. 
Adding LiTFSI into the solution (cf. LT0-PS10 and LT1-PS10) in­
creases the packing of ions, and Li+ ions are more uniformly distributed 
in LT1-PS10 (Fig. 7b) than in LT0-PS10 (Fig. 7a). Similar to S 2−
6 
ions in 
LT0-PS1, the binding affinity of S 2−
6 
and TFSI−to Li+ions is not intense 
enough to entirely replace solvent molecule in the first solvation shell of 
Li+ ions. The delicate balance between the ionic and solvent interactions 
leads to a regular distance between the ionic species, as shown in the 
RDFs in Fig. 3 and the alternating net charge in Fig. 5. 
It can be conceived that the intensity of the low q peak observed in 
Fig. 2 can also be related to the binding affinity or the donor ability of 
ions and solvent molecules. Depending on the binding affinity, ionic 
species can be categorized as a ’solvent-separated ion pair’ (SSIP), 
’contact ion pair’ (CIP), or aggregate. By classifying ionic species for PS 
electrolytes, we can have an approximate ranking for increasing ionic

Meanwhile, the strong ionic interactions alter the solvation structure 
of Li+ ions and PS clustering to a large degree. Recent studies show that 
high donor number solvents and salts can facilitate Li2S nucleation 
growth and its kinetics [21,63]. The nucleation and growth of Li2S are 
controlled by the diffusion of PS in the solutions. We assume that the 
repulsive interaction between the high donor anions and PSs can in­

anions, such as Br−, CF3CO −
3 , and I−
ions, are expected to exhibit 
similar solvation structures as Li+ −NO−
3 observed in this study. This 
result is surprising because the actual motivation for adding LiNO3 to the 
electrolyte is the passivation of the lithium anode [64]. This 
cluster-breaking effect, therefore, represents a second positive effect 
against cluster formation. 
We suppose that the strongly bound NO −
3 ions to Li+ ions are very 
closely related to the chemistry of the solid-electrolyte interface (SEI) 
layer [65]. As the NO −
3 ions are smaller than the PS ions, it is more likely 
to find a Li+ −NO−
3 pair near the electrode surface, which also decreases 
the presence of Li-PS pairs at the same spot. It is known that closely 
associated 
cations 
and 
anions 
shift 
the 
lowest 
unoccupied 
molecular-orbital energies of anions [65], consequently, anions will be 
reduced to form inorganic compounds for SEI layers. Therefore, the 
quality of the SEI layer can be partly deduced from the Li+ ion solvation 
structure. 
To reduce the polysulfide shuttle effect, highly concentrated TFSI−
electrolytes are often used [66,67]. As the result of the high ionic 
strength and steric effects, TFSI−ions localize within the second solva­
tion shell of Li+ ions in the investigated systems in this study. Having 
shown that Li+ and TFSI−
ions form ‘semi-ordered’ structures, the 
character of the alternating structure will be enhanced at higher TFSI−
concentrations or lower temperatures. A recent study on transport 
properties of Li+ ions in “solvent-in-salts” systems found relatively fast 
diffusion of water and Li+ ions in highly concentrated TFSI−
systems 
with a slow relaxation time [68]. Likewise, the diffusion of Li+ is 
strongly affected by the solvation and shape of solvent molecules and the 
anionic structure within the second solvation shell. We suppose that 
high donor ability and large solvent molecules tend to trap Li+ ions in 
fairly ordered anionic networks, resulting in a jump diffusion process. 
On the other hand, for small solvent molecules, such as water, the 
effective radius of the Li+ ion solvation shell is expected to be small. As a 
consequence, vehicle diffusion would be possible within the anionic 
networks. With this hypothesis, small solvent molecules will assist fast 
diffusion of Li+ ions and increase the transference number in these 
anionic networks. 
6. Conclusion 
By comparing neutron scattering experiments and force-field based 
electronic-continuum corrected MD simulations, we investigate the 
structure and clustering behavior of PS in THF solutions. Neutron scat­
tering data show pronounced low q peaks between 0.4 and 0.9 Å−1 in 
LT0-PS10 and LT1-PS10. MD simulations reproduce the structure factor 
F(q) very well, along with the low q peak. The latter is attributed to the 
long-range Li+-Li+ and anion-anion correlations as an alternating 
structure of cations and anions in the solutions. This observation in­
dicates that the anions with low donor numbers (i.e., TFSI−
and S 2−
6 
ions) form SSIPs with Li+ ions. SSIPs in moderate concentrations (> 1 M)

observe that adding LiNO3 salt into the PS solutions reduces the clus­
tering of PS. Highly coupled NO −
3 and Li+ ions prevent PS from forming 
larger clusters by screening electrostatic potentials. The relative posi­

alternating structure, especially among TFSI and Li ions. Meanwhile, 
the SSIP interaction between Li+ and S 2−
6 
ions shows prevalent mono­
mer PS units instead of large clusters of PSs. 
The self-diffusion coefficients of the species in the investigated so­
lutions with low ionic strength are inversely proportional to viscosity. 
Yet, the solutions with high ionic strength (LT1-PS05 and LT1-PS10) 
show a long sub-diffusive regime, and the diffusion coefficients 
deviate from the Stokes–Einstein relation. Based on the van Hove 
analysis, the diffusion process of Li+ ions can be regarded as caged and 
jump diffusions in these solutions. The long relaxation times of Li+ and 
its surrounding anions within the second solvation shell imply that their 
motions are correlated, which affects the diffusion process. 
MD simulations reveal that a delicate balance between ion-ion and 
ion-solvent interactions influences the ordering and clustering of ions. 
These ion-ion and ion-solvent interactions are crucial in low E/S ratios in 
porous environments to control the PS chains’ solubility while maxi­
mizing the transport of Li+ ions. 
The combination of neutron scattering experiments and MD simu­
lations is expected to play a vital role also in further studies, e.g., to shed 
light on atomic-level solvation structures of various donor number salts, 
or the effects of variations of solvent types and compositions for Li/S 
battery electrolytes or other energy storage devices.
