Supporting Discussion 
 
a. The polysulfides were not saturated in the IL. 
Long-chain polysulfides in the electrolyte have not reached saturation. Evidence 
is the following. 
The right end of the carbon nanotubule (CNT) was connected to a large chunk of 
ionic liquid (IL, Figure S10a, b), and the IL in the CNT was well connected to the IL 
reservoir on the right. Evidently there was efficient "communication" between the IL in 
the CNT and that in the IL reservoir, as when the nanobattery was switched to open 
circuit, the amount of precipitated sulfur due to the disproportion of the polysulfides 
(e.g. Figure 2d) was much less than the initial sulfur (e.g. Figure 2a), implying that 
some of the previously formed polysulfides have diffused or flowed to the large IL 
reservoir on the right. Because the as-formed polysulfides could diffuse or flow 
efficiently to the large IL reservoir on the right, and considering the limited amount of 
sulfur in the CNT, the polysulfides would not saturate.  
We have direct experimental evidence showing the flow of the IL in the CNT to 
the IL reservoir on the right. In many instances, the CNT was only partially filled with 
sulfur, leaving a long hollow segment on the right (e.g. Figure S2a). As soon as the 
partially hollowed CNT contacted the IL reservoir on the right, the IL flowed into the 
hollow of the CNT instantly due to capillary force (show a movie S1 of the IL filling 
process). Frequently we observed bubble formation in the IL filled CNT, and the 
bubbles were highly mobile in the CNT (e.g. Figure S11d-g), proving unambiguously 
that the IL in the CNT was flowing.

b. The S/IL interface is a solid/liquid interface rather than a solid/solid interface.  
 
An IL/S interface in IL electrolyte different from a solid/solid interface in solid-
state Li-S battery. 
As shown in Figure S2a, we frequently observed only partially sulfur filled CNT 
with a long hollow segment. As soon as the CNT contacted the IL reservoir on the right, 
the IL flowed into the hollow of the CNT, forming an IL/S interface (Figure S2b). Upon 
applying a negative potential to the S-CNT cathode vs. Li, the IL/S interface started to 
move from the IL side towards the sulfur side due to initiation of the discharge reaction. 
The formation of polysulfides is clearly demonstrated in Figure 2, in which the IL/S 
interfaces migrated from the IL side towards the sulfur side (Figure 2a-c), as soon as 
the system was switched to open circuit, numerous semispherical sulfur nanoparticles 
precipitated out from the IL as a result of the disproportion reaction of the polysulfides 
(Figure 2d). Figure 1 essentially exhibits similar results to Figure 2. 
These results have two important implications: firstly, the polysulfides, once 
formed, dissolved into the IL instantly, which disproportionated into sulfur at open 
circuit; secondly, the IL/S is a solid/liquid interface rather than a solid/solid interface, 
as the precipitation of semispherical sulfur nanoparticles is a clear indication of the 
liquid nature rather than a solid in the IL side.  
The discharge product in the IL near the reaction front is polysulfides rather than 
Li2S, as the latter is insoluble to the IL (e. g. Figure S3). The formation of polysulfides 
upon discharge is also backed up by several other experiments, such as in situ Raman 
spectroscopy (Figure 4). The IL/S interface being a solid/liquid interface rather than a 
solid/solid interface is further supported by e-beam irradiation experiments (Figure S4). 
When we placed the e-beam on the IL/S interface, a spherical bubble emerged in the IL 
(Figure S4e) possibly due to the gas release resulting from the e-beam induced IL 
decomposition. We normally do not observe such large spherical bubble formation in a 
solid. The results further testify the liquid nature in the IL side. 
In short, the IL/S interface is a solid/liquid interface rather than a solid/solid 
interface.
