In situ Raman microscopy was used to study polysulﬁde speciation in the bulk ether electrolyte during the discharge and charge of a Li-
S electrochemical cell to assess the complex interplay between chemical and electrochemical reactions in solution. During discharge,
long chain polysulﬁdes and the S3−radical appear in the electrolyte at 2.4 V indicating a rapid equilibrium of the dissociation reaction
to form S3−. When charging, however, an increase in the concentration of all polysulﬁde species was observed. This highlights the
importance of the electrolyte to sulfur ratio and suggests a loss in the useful sulfur inventory from the cathode to the electrolyte.

Secondary lithium sulfur (Li-S) batteries have been heavily stud-
ied since the start of the 21st century due to their high theoretical
capacity and speciﬁc energy density. While lithium intercalation bat-
teries have revolutionized society’s ability to store energy, they are
reaching their limit with a theoretical capacity almost ﬁve times lower
than that of lithium sulfur.2 The viability of Li-S battery chemistries
rely on overcoming several key difﬁculties, including the solubility
of polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte, the insulating nature of sulfur and
Li2S, the volume change of the cathode, and reactivity of the lithium
anode.3–5 If the aforementioned obstacles can be overcome, electric
vehicles and personal electronics will have extended performance on
a single charge.
These difﬁculties manifest through inefﬁcient sulfur utilization in
the battery, thus decreasing speciﬁc energy density and shortening
cycle life.3 Increasing sulfur utilization, in turn, requires control of
polysulﬁde chemistry and transport within the electrolyte. For this
reason, we focus here on the mobility and reactivity of the polysulﬁdes
within the electrolyte.
Shuttling of the polysulﬁdes during the charge cycle is one of the
most detrimental reactions for battery performance. Shuttling occurs
when polysulﬁdes diffuse through the electrolyte, driven by a con-
centration gradient, to the anode where they are reduced. The newly
reduced polysulﬁdes then shuttle back to the cathode where they are
oxidized. This results in inefﬁcient operation of the battery and can
lead to inﬁnite recharging. Additionally, the electrolyte is host to
several chemical side reactions including dissociation and dispropor-
tionation reactions that inﬂuence the discharge characteristics of the
battery.3,6 A better understanding of polysulﬁde speciation in the bulk
electrolyte is needed to address losses in efﬁciency stemming from
polysulﬁde solubility, mobility, and side reactions.
To this end, several characterization techniques have been used
to assess Li-S batteries, ranging from optical absorption and chro-
matography to X-ray diffraction.7 An ideal method would measure
sulfur concentration and speciation, in solid and solvated form, in
situ with good spatial resolution. Since no single characterization
method exhibits these characteristics, a combinatorial approach must
be used to fully study the Li-S battery. Several techniques—including
ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis), Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR),
X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), X-ray absorption near edge
structure (XANES), and Raman spectroscopies—identify the differ-
ent polysulﬁde chain lengths, but all suffer from the inability to deﬁni-

standard spectral responses for a given polysulﬁde species remain un-
known. Owing to these constraints, understanding of the polysulﬁde
chemistry within the electrolyte remains incomplete.
For example, since polysulﬁdes absorb strongly in the UV, UV-Vis
spectroscopy provides a means to quantify their concentration.8,9 It
has therefore been utilized to assess the time evolution of polysulﬁde
speciation and its dependence upon electrolyte,8 while also giving
insight into its adsorption on high surface area carbons.9 However,
UV-Vis does not provide any spatial resolution and is used predomi-
nately in the examination of liquids.
A combination of ex situ, high performance liquid chromatography
(HPLC) and UV-Vis provided a means to separate and identify chain
lengths leading to insights into the disproportionation reactions and
the reduction of S8.10 HPLC was also used in conjunction with elec-
tron spray ionization (ESI) mass spectroscopy to identify derivatized
polysulﬁdes removed from a lithium sulfur cell.11 Although HPLC
mass spectroscopy provides exact identiﬁcation of the polysulﬁde
chain lengths, it requires the use of polysulﬁdes in varying polar and
nonpolar solvents, that likely alter the polysulﬁde speciation from that
existing in the electrolyte.
Batteries are multistate systems requiring characterization of both
solids and liquids as well as the interfaces between them. In Li-S
batteries, there is a particular need to observe both insoluble S8 and
Li2S as well as soluble polysulﬁde intermediates. Li NMR fulﬁlls
these requirements with the ability to distinguish between solvated
and precipitated polysulﬁdes.12 However, convoluted spectra resulting
from peak overlap limits NMR’s capability to identify polysulﬁde
chain length.
Synchrotron based X-ray methods, in contrast, can analyze poly-
sulﬁde oxidation states within the electrolyte itself and specify chain
length. XAS, for example, provided evidence that carbonate-based
electrolytes chemically react with solvated polysulﬁde species, re-
ducing battery performance. This helped lead to ether electrolytes
more commonly used today.13 Using polysulﬁde reference standards,
XANES demonstrated S8 and Li2S dissolution and deposition during
cycling,14 providing evidence for the initial stepwise reduction of S8
to Li2S6 and Li2S4. A combination of XAS and XANES has also been
used to study the condition of the electrode and quantify polysulﬁde
concentrations during cycling.15 Over just three cycles, an irreversible
loss of sulfur to the electrolyte and deterioration of the sulfur cathode
micro structure were observed.
In operando ATR FT-infrared spectroscopy has added to the slate
of techniques used to probe the Li-S battery. Speciﬁcally, Saqib et al.
developed an in operando cell and studied the cathode and polysulﬁde

speciation during cycling.16 They tracked the mean polysulﬁde order

Raman spectroscopy supplements these characterization tech-
niques with control of spatial resolution (on the order of 1 μm or
less)17 and the identiﬁcation of differing chain lengths of polysul-
ﬁdes, which are typically Raman active in the 100–600 cm−1 range.18
Raman spectroscopy is also commonly available and relatively easy to
use. Despite these beneﬁts, Raman is limited by the spectral overlap
between differing polysulﬁde species and the lack of spectral stan-
dards for these compounds. It also has an inherently weak signal that
can be obscured by ﬂuorescence or photochemical reactions. These
limitations are partially offset, however, by the technique’s sensitiv-
ity to the coordinating cation19–23 and state of the polysulﬁdes,19–21
as well as the electrolyte solvent,24–28 which each affect the Raman
spectrum. Thus, Raman provides a sensitive probe to the polysulﬁde
chemistry within the electrolyte.
For example, Raman spectroscopy has been used for both in-1,18,29
and ex-situ30 studies of Li-S batteries in TEGDME and DOL elec-
trolytes. Theoretical methods, including density functional theory
(DFT) and ab initio molecular dynamic simulations, aided in de-
termination of peak positions of various polysulﬁdes. Partovi-Azar
et al. focused on the theoretical Raman spectra of Li2S2 clusters
and described the effect of cluster size on peak intensity.31 Hagen
et al.1 used density functional theory (DFT) to calculate theoreti-
cal vibrational modes of lithium polysulﬁdes1 comparing predictions
with chemically synthesized lithium polysulﬁde standards, previous
reports, and in situ measurements. Contrary to in situ results and pre-
dictions, only the S3−radical was observed in the standards, which is
a possible result of their storage under air. Wu et al.18 and Hannauer
et al.29 performed similar studies of polysulﬁdes as a function of state
of charge. However, Hannauer et al. successfully decreased the elec-
trolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio to 60 μL/mg whereas Wu et al. retained
a larger electrolyte volume of 20 mL. Both the E/S ratio and solvent
change the speciation,18 so the study of polysulﬁdes in various solvent
systems adds to the Raman library of polysulﬁde peaks.
Most in situ Raman measurements examining polysulﬁde specia-
tion have sampled near the electrode-electrolyte interface. Dispropor-
tionation and dissociation reactions occur not only at this interface
but in solution as well.8 Furthermore, battery performance is known
to be dependent upon the quantity and type of electrolyte.3,32 It is
therefore necessary to investigate the polysulﬁde speciation within
the bulk electrolyte itself in various solvents. Here, in situ confocal
Raman spectroscopy examines the bulk electrolyte of a Li-S T-cell.
Speciﬁcally, chemically synthesized standards were produced under
argon in an attempt to provide reference spectra for the in situ cell for
a series of expected polysulﬁdes. Spectra were then acquired during
the charge and discharge of the cell. During the discharge cycle, the
appearance of S3−and long chain polysulﬁdes was observed at 2.4 V,
which is consistent with previous reports.1,18 During charge, however,
the reversal of speciation was not observed. Instead, the concentration
of all observed polysulﬁde species in the bulk electrolyte increased.
These results provide insight to the speciation of the bulk electrolyte
and indicate the importance of the electrolyte to sulfur ratio in the
operation of Li-S batteries.
Experimental
Materials preparation.—For all experiments, the electrolyte con-
sisted of 1:1 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME): 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) with
1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) and 0.25 M
LiNO3. 1,2-dimethoxyethane anhydrous, 99.5%, inhibitor free and
1,2-dioxolane anhydrous, 99.8%, containing ∼75 ppm BHT as in-
hibitor were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Electrolyte salts included
LiNO3 99.999% (Alfa Aesar and Johnson Matthey Material Tech-
nology UK) and LiTFSI 99.95% (Sigma Aldrich) or 99.99% (Os-
sila). Alumina 99.5% (Sigma Aldrich) was activated under vacuum at
150◦C for 20 hours and used to dry the electrolyte by allowing them
to mix overnight. To remove the activated alumina from solution, the
electrolyte was ﬁltered using HPLC-certiﬁed GHP Acrodisc 25 mm
syringe ﬁlters with 0.45 μm GHP membranes from Life Sciences.
Raman standards were synthesized by a procedure similar to that
of Hagen et al.1 except the entire synthesis was performed under
argon. Stoichiometric ratios of S8 99.5% (Sigma Aldrich) and Li2S
99.98% (Sigma Aldrich) were mixed together in 5 mL of full elec-
trolyte, including LiTFSI and LiNO3. The Raman spectrum of each
component is provided in supplemental Figures S3 and S4. This corre-
sponded to 0.012 ± 0.003 g Li2S for all standards and 0.056 ± 0.002 g,
0.040 ± 0.002 g, and 0.024 ± 0.002 g S8 to theoretically make 50 mM
of Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4 respectively. For simplicity, these standards
will be labeled as above even if their speciation does not actually fol-
low the stoichiometric expectation. Dissociation and disproportiona-
tion chemical side reactions, as have been observed previously,14 are
presumed to cause this variation.
The solutions were mixed in 20 mL scintillation vials on a hotplate
set at 60◦C and 600 RPM within an argon-ﬁlled glove box with water
and oxygen content < 1 ppm and < 10 ppm respectively. Once the
reaction was initiated, the standards were mixed for ∼15 hours. The
standards (3 mL) were then moved into Starna quartz cuvettes with
silicon-PTFE tape seals. The standards remained sealed under argon
and were covered with aluminum foil, to exclude light, once removed
from the glove box. All Raman spectra were obtained within a dry
room with a dew point less than −40◦C. Raman spectra were obtained
1–2 times daily for 7 days. The sealed cuvettes were covered with
aluminum foil and kept in an opaque ﬂammables cabinet when not
being actively analyzed.
The in situ cell cathodes were composed of 60:30:10 sulfur:
Super P carbon (TIMCAL): poly(acrylonitrile (LI133) and styrene-
butadiene rubber (SBR) 8 mil wet ﬁlm thickness coating on a carbon-
coated aluminum current collector. The average cathode loading was
2.75 ± 0.09 mg sulfur/cm2. Cathodes were produced by ﬁrst grinding
together sulfur and carbon in the ratio of 80:20 using a mortar and
pestle. The mixture was fused at 150◦C and 50 mTorr for 12 hours.
After fusing, the mixture vented overnight in a dry room before being
ground into a ﬁne powder again. The powder was then further pro-
cessed on a Turbula T2F overnight with ∼30, 3–5 mm zirconia beads
(BYK Gardner) to disperse clumps.
The fused carbon-sulfur was added to a mixture of 2.5 wt% LI133
and 2.5 wt% SBR suspended in water. Super P carbon was added to
create a ratio of 60:30:10 and mixed overnight at 600–1000 RPM.
The slurries were then cast onto carbon-coated aluminum using a
doctor blade Elcometer (4340 Automatic Film Applicator) to cre-
ate the desired wet ﬁlm thickness. The coatings dried overnight and
then were placed in a vacuum oven at room temperature for at least
12 hours.
Characterization.—A Swagelok PTFE 0.5” T-cell with a quartz
viewing window on one side (Supplemental Figure S5) facilitated
in situ Raman measurements. Three, 0.5” diameter stainless steel rods
with 0.25” electrode contacts were used as leads to the electrodes. A
0.39” diameter, 25 mil thick lithium metal (Foote Mineral Corporation
high purity Li foil under argon), anode was cold welded to one of the
current collector leads with a carbon-sulfur cathode on the opposite
lead. The lithium was mechanically abraded to remove surface oxide
prior to use. The ﬁnal lead was left as bare stainless steel. All connec-
tions were prepared within the dry room with ﬁnal assembly in the
glove box.
The cathode had a diameter of 0.25” and was hand-cut to have
tabs that were secured to the stainless-steel rods with 0.25” PTFE
tubing. The tabs were gently cleaned with a knife to remove all active
material. The electrodes were physically separated by a distance of
0.8 mm rather than using a separator. The cell was ﬁlled with 2 mL of
electrolyte, which corresponds to a large E/S ratio of 1850 mL/gram
sulfur. The in situ system was sealed with a #013 DuPont Kalrez
O-ring and a 3D-printed pressure plate.
The T-cell was cleaned prior to each use to avoid contamination
that could affect the electrochemistry of the cell or induce increased
ﬂuorescence that could obscure the Raman signal. All components

excess of 130 μL of the same 1:1 DME: DOL electrolyte and were

For the in situ cells, each voltage step was held until the current decay

dt | ≤0.015 μA/sec.
The quantity of sulfur in the cathode was calculated by ﬁrst taking
the average, total mass of a 0.25” diameter cathode without tabs. The
current collector mass was subtracted and 60% of the remaining mass
represented the mass of sulfur. The T-cell current and voltage were
controlled using a Bio-Logic SP-200 potentiostat.
Raman spectra were obtained at room temperature using a WiTec
Alpha300M with a back-illuminated charge-coupled device detector
Peltier cooled to −60◦C. The grating period and accuracy for the sys-
tem were 600 l/mm and ± 1 cm−1, respectively. A 532 nm frequency
doubled Nd:YAG laser was used to collect all spectra and was fo-
cused using a Zeiss EC ‘Epiplan’ DIC 10x objective with a numerical
aperture of 0.25. The power, integration time, and number of accu-
mulations were material-dependent, ranging from 0.5 mW to 8 mW
with the acquisition times spanning from 30–300 seconds. This laser
power was chosen to optimize signal without damaging the sample as
shown in the supplemental material Figure S6.
For the polysulﬁde standards, Raman spectra were obtained from
the bulk of the solution, 50 μm below the solution-argon interface.
Due to the absorptivity of the polysulﬁdes, obtaining spectra a greater
distance into the solution was not possible. Raman spectra were nor-
malized to the most intense LiTFSI peak at approximately 740 cm−1.
Electrolyte peaks were subtracted to more clearly highlight those
originating from polysulﬁdes. In situ spectra underwent the same data
processing as the standards, but were also smoothed. For further in-
formation on data processing see the supplemental material.
Results and Discussion
Figure 1 depicts the Raman spectra of standards synthesized under
argon. The dotted and solid lines, representing mono- and di-anions,
respectively,1 indicate that polysulﬁdes were successfully generated.
Even with differing stoichiometric ratios, peak locations were com-
mon among all samples indicating similar speciation. However, differ-
ences in the relative peak intensity and solution color are observed be-
tween the samples, suggesting that different equilibria containing the
same species were obtained for each stoichiometry. Chemical side re-
actions, including dissociation and disproportionation reactions, likely
shifted the equilibrium away from the intended speciation, which is
consistent with previous results.1,14
Here, this speciation was observed even though the system was
maintained in an inert environment. This, in turn, suggests chemical
reactivity that is intrinsic to the system apart from the intended electro-
chemical processes. Speciﬁcally, as the standards do not equilibrate to
the intended stoichiometry, speciation is relatively uncontrolled. The
processes are self-limiting as speciation remains for up to one week.
As shown in Figure 2, the peak intensities for the dominant poly-
sulﬁde peaks remained approximately constant for one week when
stored under inert conditions. As expected, this stability is not ob-
served when stored in air (Figures S8-S10). The effects of air can be
seen both in the speciation of the polysulﬁdes as well as the change
in background of the electrolyte alone. Taken together, these results
highlight the complex interplay between the bulk electrolyte and the
polysulﬁdes, as well as the need to investigate their interaction during
battery operation.
Recognizing the prevalence of chemical side reactions and the
importance of maintaining an air-free system, in situ Raman measure-
ments of the electrolyte within the T-cell were collected following
the discharge proﬁle given in Figure 3. Figure 4 shows a representa-
tive comparison between the in situ spectrum at 2.4 V and the Li2S8
standard with peak intensities normalized relative to the 533 cm−1
feature. In the Li2S8 spectrum, there is a mode located at 510 cm−1
corresponding to S4−/S7−1 that is absent from the electrochemical
system. In addition, the relative intensity of the S3−, 533 cm−1 peak
is greater in the battery than the standard. This indicates that S3−is
more energetically favorable than other species in the battery, whereas
the opposite is true in the purely chemical system. These differences
highlight the variation in chemical versus electrochemical processes
that change the equilibrium of polysulﬁde speciation.
There is also electrochemical evidence suggesting the presence of
chemical side reactions, as shown by the nonzero equilibrium cur-
rents in Figure 3. In order to maintain a constant voltage, a nonzero
current was required to offset the presence of chemical side reac-
tions. This behavior is not the result of an abnormal cell, as the T-cell
discharged normally under constant-current conditions as shown in
Figure S12.
To compare the prevalence of side reactions and those intended
for battery operation, polysulﬁde speciation was tracked as a function
of potential. Four polysulﬁde peaks are predominant in the Raman
response of the T-cell bulk electrolyte at energies 365 (S82−),1 390 (S7−
and S72−),1 450 (S32−, S42−, and S4−),1 and 533 (S3−)14,20,22,25,cm−1 as
listed in Table I. Lower intensity polysulﬁde peaks were also observed
and are listed in Table I. The changes in spectra during discharge as a
function of potential are shown in Figure 5 and are quantiﬁed for both
charge and discharge in Figure 6. Polysulﬁde speciation as a function
of state of charge for the discharge cycle is given in Figure S13.
Before the start of discharge, a feature at 365 cm−1, corresponding
to S82−, is present indicating self-discharge of the cell. Consistent with
this interpretation, the open circuit potential decreased from > 3 V
to ∼2.9 V versus lithium for the period between their fabrication and
the experiment’s commencement (∼12 hours). Phenomenologically,
it is possible that, although largely insoluble, a small amount of S8
dissolved into the electrolyte prior to the ring opening of the Li+ attack
of the S8 ring. Once solubilized, S8 diffused to the lithium anode and
reacted to form Li2S8 which is highly soluble and was observed via
Raman in the bulk electrolyte. As this occurs, a mixed potential at the
cathode is created, decreasing the observed voltage.
As discharge begins, at 2.4 V, all four peaks begin to increase
from their initial intensity as shown in Figure 6a. All polysul-
ﬁde chain lengths are therefore present during the pseudo-steady
state and are continually present throughout discharge. The peaks
corresponding purely to longer chain polysulﬁdes, 365 (S82−) and

Upon charging (Figure 5b), the expected reversal of the discharge
process was not observed. The concentration of all polysulﬁde species
studied increased. This increase in concentration in the bulk elec-
trolyte shows that sulfur was not fully oxidized back to its insoluble
S8 form. Furthermore, since the soluble polysulﬁdes contain fewer
electrons and remain in the bulk electrolyte and not near the electrode
surface, less charge will be available for the next discharge cycle.
This irreversibility may be the result of the large electrolyte to sulfur
ratio.

Conclusions
The speciation in the bulk electrolyte of Li-S batteries as a result
of chemical and electrochemical reactions was examined by com-
paring the polysulﬁde Raman response in the electrolyte solution.
Chemical side reactions are prevalent as multiple polysulﬁde species
were observed within the electrolyte regardless of the solution’s initial
stoichiometry. The effect of air on the polysulﬁde speciation was com-
pared to that under argon; the standards under inert conditions were
found to be stable for at least 1 week. The chemically driven speci-
ation is further modiﬁed by the electrochemical environment as was
evidenced by in situ measurements. Speciﬁcally, prior to discharge,
the presence of S82−indicated self-discharge of the battery. With dis-
charge, stepwise reduction of long chain to short chain polysulﬁdes
was conﬁrmed, albeit with an equilibrium presence of several poly-
sulﬁdes throughout. In particular, an intense S3−signature appeared
early at 2.4 V, and was sustained throughout discharge suggesting a
rapid equilibrium favoring this radical over S62−. With charging, ir-
reversibility was apparent as not all polysulﬁdes were removed from
solution, most likely due to the large electrolyte to sulfur ratio and
focusing the sampling point in the bulk electrolyte, centered between
the electrodes. This work provides support for the persistence of the
S3−radical and the negative effects of a large E/S ratio. As well as
expands the library of polysulﬁde peaks in ether solvents and provides
stability information in the case of chemically synthesized standards
for use in characterization.
