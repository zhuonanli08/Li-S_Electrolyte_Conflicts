a b s t r a c t 
Rechargeable lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered promising next-generation energy storage 
systems owing to their high theoretical energy density, but their application is hindered by the shuttle 
effect arising from dissolved lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs). Herein, we design an optimized electrolyte to 
achieve long-term stability by employing an appropriate low-polarity solvent. A combination of diethyl 
ether (DEE) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) was selected to improve Li metal stability even in the pres-
ence of LiPSs. The DEE/DME electrolyte not only suppresses parasitic reactions between Li and LiPSs but 
also promotes uniform Li deposition. Moreover, operando optical microscopy was employed to directly
visualize electrolyte stability and dendrite evolution in real time, while quantitative analysis was con-
ducted via normalized hue index and contour image mapping. The enhanced anode stability of the
DEE/DME electrolyte enabled excellent cycling performance, retaining 80.14% of its initial capacity after
300 cycles at 3 C, while maintaining superior performance under practical conditions with high sulfur
loading and a low E/S ratio. These ﬁndings highlight that solvent properties critically inﬂuence Li metal
stabilization in Li–S batteries and underscore the signiﬁcance of solvent engineering in electrolyte design.

1. Introductio n
The development of next-generation battery systems is becom-
ing increasingly crucial to meet the growing demand for electric 
vehicles (EVs), driven by efforts to reduce fossil fuel consumption 
in pursuit of a carbon–neutral society. Moreover, the rising interest 
in urban air mobility (UAM) has led to an urgent need for light-
weight, high-capacity batteries. Particularly, the UAM industry
requires batteries that achieve not only high energy density but
also high power output, extended range, and long cycle life. Conse-
quently, Li–S batteries, which utilize lightweight lithium and sulfur
as the active materials and offer a theoretical energy density of
approximately 2,600 Wh kg−1 , have attracted signiﬁcant attention
[1,2].

Li–S batteries operate through multi-electron redox reactions 
based on a conversion reaction. During the discharge process, 
insoluble sulfur (S8) at the cathode is converted into soluble LiPSs 
of varying chain lengths, which are further reduced to insulat ing
lithium sulﬁde species (Li2S2/Li2S) [3]. These soluble LiPS interme-
diates play a crucial role in determining the performance of Li–S 
batteries. However, their dissolution in the electrolyte causes 
issues such as the shuttle effect, low sulfur utilization, and Li anode 
corrosion arising from their high reactivity with Li metal [4,5]. To 
mitigate such parasitic reactions between Li metal and polysul-
ﬁdes, extensive research has focused on both modifying cathode 
structures and developing electrolyte components that regulate 
polysulﬁde solubility while simultaneously s tabilizing the Li metal
interface [6,7]. Nevertheless, overly suppressed polysulﬁde solubil-
ity can hinder redox kinetics, thereby negatively impacting the

<<PAGE 2>>
discharge capacity. Therefore, the key strategy is designing an elec-
trolyte that regulates polysulﬁde solubility sufﬁciently to sustain
sulfur redox kinetics while minimizing undesirable reactions with
the Li metal anode [8,9].

To achieve rational electrolyte design, recent studies have 
explored various strategies, including highly solvating electrolytes 
(HSEs), sparingly solvating electrolytes (SSEs), encapsulating 
lithium polysulﬁde electrolytes (EPSEs), and weakly solvating elec-
trolytes (WSEs). HSEs enhance sulfur redox kinetics by using sol-
vents or additives with high polysulﬁde solubility [10,11]. In 
contrast, SSEs limit polysulﬁde solubility, creating environments 
similar to those in Li metal batteries [12]. EPSEs modulate the sol-
vation structure of polysulﬁdes, suppressing their direct interac-
tion with Li metal [13,14]. WSEs improve Li metal stability by 
weakening solvent–p olysulﬁde interactions through the use of
diluents [15,16]. While these strategies have been explored to con-
trol polysulﬁde solubility, a deep understanding of solvent proper-
ties that govern solvation interactions with polysulﬁdes is crucial
for ﬁne-tuning solubility levels [17,18]. To design electrolytes that 
optimize both cathode reaction kinetics and Li metal protection, it 
is essential to investigate solvent effects through a fundamental
understanding of electrolyte behavior in Li–S battery systems.
In this study, we propose DEE as a promising solvent candidate 
for stabilizing Li metal due to its high chemical stability in the 
presence of polysulﬁdes. However, DEE alone cannot deliver sufﬁ-
cient discharge capacity, as the extremely limited polysulﬁde dis-
solution leads to sluggish reaction kinetics. To overcome this 
limitation, DME was introduced as a co-solvent to ensure adequate 
polysulﬁde solubility while maintaining compatibility with DEE. 
The solvation structure of the DEE/DME electrolyte was systemat-
ically investigated using Raman spectroscopy, density functional 
theory (DFT) calculations, and molecular dynamics (MD) simula-
tions. Furthermore, polysulﬁde solubility and its impact on real-
time electrochemical behavior were evaluated through operando 
optical microscopy with quantitative analysis. The optimized
DEE/DME electrolyte effectively suppresses parasitic reactions at
the Li metal surface, exhibiting only a 2.4% reduction in dendrite
area even after a 10-hour rest following charging. In addition to
its stable interfacial characteristics, the system also maintains
favorable sulfur redox kinetics, achieving 80.14% capacity retention
after 300 cycles at 3 C and demonstrating excellent performance
under practical conditions with high sulfur loading and a low E/S
ratio. This study established a clear correlation between solvent
properties and Li metal stabilization, providing new insights into
electrolyte design strategies for high-performance Li–S batteries.
2. Experimen tal
2.1. Electrolyte preparati on
The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1.0 M lithium bis(f 
luoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiFSI; anhydrous, 98%, TCI) and 
0.2 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3; anhydrous, 99%, Alfa Aesar) in 
selected solvents. For the typical electrolyte, the solvent mixture 
consisted of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL; anhydrous, 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich) 
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME; anhydrous, 99.5%, Sigma Aldrich)
in a 1:1 vol ratio. For DEE/DME electrolyte, to optimize the solvent
ratio, diethyl ether (DEE; anhydrous, 99.7%, Sigma Aldrich) and
DME were mixed in various volume ratios of 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 2:3,
1:2, 1:3, 1:5 and 1:10.
2.2. Electrode preparation
All electrochemical performance tests were conducted using 
sulfur cathodes prepared by mixing sulfur (99.5%, Alfa Aesar),
Super P (average particle size = 400 nm, speciﬁc BET surface 
area = 62 m2 g−1 , Timcal), and polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) in 
N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP; 99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) in a weight 
ratio of 70:20:10. For the cyclability test at 3 C and under practical 
conditions, carbon-based sulfur composite cathodes were prepared 
using multi-wall carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs; diameter ≈ 20 nm, 
length ≈ 5 lm, speciﬁc BET surface area = 100–700 m2 g−1 , Carbon 
Nano-material Technology Co.) as the sulfur host. A mixture of
80 wt% sulfur and 20 wt% MWCNTs was placed in a sealed stainless
steel (SUS) container and heated at 155 °C for 12 h. Electrodes were
then prepared by mixing the as-prepared S/MWCNT composite,
Super P, and PVDF in NMP with a weight ratio of 75:15:10. The
resulting slurry was cast onto carbon paper (Avcarb EP40, AvCarb
Material Solutions) and dried at 60 °C overnight.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Feasibility and optimization of DEE-based electrolytes for Li–S
batteries
The application of Li–S batteries inherently involves the use of 
Li metal, suggesting that electrolyte systems developed for Li 
metal batteries may be effectively extended to this ﬁeld. In this 
context, DEE represents a promising candidate, as its stabilizing
effect on Li metal has been previously reported [25–27]. DEE has 
been utilized as an electrolyte solvent for Li metal batteries due 
to its low viscosity and density, which ensure excellent wetting 
ability. In addition, its low freezing and boiling points make it 
suitable for low-temperature applications. Furthermore, as a less 
polar ether, DEE can weaken the interaction between Li+ and sol-
vent molecules. However, in Li–S batteries, the inﬂuence of LiPS 
dissolution must be considered, as it governs the sulfur redox 
reactions. To examine the potential of DEE as an electrolyte sol-
vent for Li–S batteries, a 0.1 M Li2S6 solution was prepared by
mixing Li2S and sulfur in DEE at a stoichiometric ratio of 1:5,
and its stability was monitored over time (Fig. 1a). Typically, LiPS 
dissolution leads to a reddish-brown coloration. In contrast, the 
DEE solution retained its initial light yellow color even after one 
day, indicating its extremely low LiPS solubility. When DEE was 
used as the sole solvent in a Li–S battery, the delivered capacity
was severely limited, with a ﬁrst discharge capacity of only
312.21 mA h g−1 (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1). Electrolytes with low LiPS sol-
ubility are generally considered favorable, as they suppress the 
shuttle effect. However, since LiPS acts as intrinsic mediators in 
the sulfur redox process, excessively low solubility impedes
redox efﬁciency by introducing a large overpotential associated
with solid-to-solid conversion [28,29]. A schematic illustration 
of these issues associated with using DEE alone in Li–S batteries
is shown in Fig. 1c. To exploit the excellent Li metal compatibility 
of DEE while ensuring efﬁcient redox reactions, it is essential to 
blend DEE with anoth er ether-based solvent that can enhance
LiPS solubility.
When designing an electrolyte solvent for Li–S batteries, the 
most critical factor to consider is its interaction with LiPSs. In the 
electrolyte, Li salts form solvation structures around Li+ through 
coordination with solvent molecules and anions. LiPS dissolution 
occurs via Li exchange with solvent molecules released from sol-
vated Li+ . Since the solvation structures of LiPSs are primarily gov-
erned by interactions with solvent molecules, solvent properties 
play a decisive role in determining LiPS dissolution behavior. A 
fraction of the solvent molecules form solvation shells around Li+ , 
while the remaining free molecules interact wi th LiPSs to facilitate
solvation. The extent of LiPS dissolution varies signiﬁcantly with
solvent polarity [30]. Due to their high nucleophilicity, LiPSs inter-
act strongly with electrophilic solvents of higher polarity, leading 
to increased solubility and greater stability in solvation. Con-
versely, in lower-polarity solvents, these interactions are much 
weaker, resulting in markedly reduced solubility (Fig. 1d). Given 
the strong correlation between solvent polarity and LiPS solubility, 
polarity is a crucial factor in electrolyte solvent selection. Several 
parameters reﬂect solvent polarity, including the carbon-to-
oxygen (C/O) ratio, dielectric c onstant (er), and steric hindrance
(Fig. 1e). According to solvation theory and the Born equation, 
the solvation energetics of Li salts and other ionic species are pri-
marily determined by electrostatic interactions between Li+ and 
surrounding solvent molecules, which are strongly inﬂuenced by 
the er. When er is below 10, even a small change can result in a pro-
nounced difference in LiPS solubility [31,32]. Therefore, increasing 
the C/O ratio is an effective strategy to lower the er of ether-based 
solvents. Furthermore, steric hindrance around the coordinating 
oxygen atoms can structurally restrict Li+ coordination and is

In Fig. S3, DEE was mixed with various solvents at a 1:1 vol ratio.

electrolyte showed both capacity activation and retention despite

batteries. To resolve the immiscibility issue and identify the opti-

3.2. Investigation of electrolyte solvation structure and
electrochemical compatibility
To analyze the physicochemical properties and solvation envi-
ronment of the prepared electrolytes and evaluate their intrinsic 
performance in the absence of LiPSs, a comparative study was con-
ducted. DOL/DME (1:1 vol%) was used as the control electrolyte,
representing a conventional solvent system for Li–S batteries.
The wetting ability of the electrolytes was assessed through con-
tact angle analysis to examine the afﬁnity between the electrolyte
and separator (Fig. S4). The average contact angles were 43.8° for 
DOL/DME and 31.65° for DEE/DME (2:3). As discussed above, the 
lower viscosity of DEE leads to a smaller contact angle, indicating
superior wetting ability. The improved wettability reduces interfa-
cial energy between the separator and electrode, thereby enhanc-
ing electrochemical reaction efﬁciency.
MD simulations were employed to investigate the solvation 
structures of different electrolyte systems. In all systems, Li+ spe-
cies are closely bound to Li2S8 (Fig. S5). In DOL/DME electrolytes, 
Li2S8 exhibits a characteristic two-shell solvation structure with
an inner shell (4.4 Å) dominated by DME and an outer shell (6.6
Å) containing both DME and DOL (Fig. 2a–c). In DEE/DME systems, 
the inner shell maintains the same structure with DME molecules 
at 4.4 Å, but the outer shell experiences reorganization, shifting to
6.2 Å and incorporating both DEE and FSI−(Fig. 2d–f). Notably, pure 
solvent systems further demonstrate the unique solvation behav-
ior of DEE. While pure DME maintains typical DME-dominated sol-
vation structures, pure DEE systems show the simultaneous
presence of DEE molecules and FSI−around Li2S8 (Fig. S6). The 
appearance of FSI− in the outer shells of DEE-containing systems 
(absent in DME-only systems) indicates that the weak solvating 
power of DEE promotes FSI −participation in the solvation struc-
ture of LiPSs, which further promotes the formation of inorganic-
rich SEI at the Li metal surface.

Raman spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the fundamen-
tal coordination structure of the electrolyte (Fig. 2g–i). Upon disso-
lution of LiFSI, the coordination between Li+ cations and FSI− 
anions decreases, accompanied by enhanced Li+ –solvent interac-
tions. This redistribution manifests as a redshift in the anion peak 
originating from the Li salt. In the S–N–S band, a slight shift toward
higher wavenumbers suggests incomplete dissociation of LiFSI,
implying the prevalence of contact ion pair (CIP) and aggregate
(AGG) structures [33,34]. As shown in the percentage of solvation
structure (Fig. 2g, h), both DOL/DME and DEE/DME electrolytes 
predominantly exhibit solvent-separated ion pair (SSIP) structures, 
which effectively promote LiFSI dissociation and enable fast Li+ 
migration. However, DEE/DME electrolytes contain a higher pro-
portion of CIP and AGG structures than DOL/DME, which is beneﬁ-
cial for forming a more stable anion-derived SEI layer on the Li
metal anode. The relative distributions of these solvation struc-
tures are summarized in Fig. 2i. While the overall compositions 
of DEE/DME (2:3) and DEE/DME (1:2) electrolytes are similar, 
increasing the DEE content leads to a higher proportion of AGG 
structures. This compositional dependence aligns with observa-
tions from single-solvent systems: pure DEE predominantly forms
AGG structures with minimal free solvent, whereas DME primarily
exhibits SSIP structures with relatively few CIP and AGG structures
(Fig. S7). The relationship between the electrolyte solvent and Li 
salt was further examine d using NMR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR
spectra (Fig. S8) conﬁrmed the chemical structures of the various 
electrolytes, with clear and distinct peaks indicating successful 
mixing of DEE and DME in the electrolyte. To investigate the chem-
ical environment of Li+,
7Li NMR spectroscopy was performed
(Fig. S9). Minimal differences in peak positions were observed 
among the electrolytes, which can be attributed to the coexistence 
of SSIP, CIP, and AGG in both DOL/DME and DEE/DME systems,

in Fig. 2i. 
To evaluate the compatibility of the electrolytes with Li metal, 
Li symmetric cells were fabricated. The DEE/DME (2:3) electrolyte 
demonstrated stable cycling over 400 h with a minimal voltage 
hysteresis of ∼23 mV at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 . In contrast, 
the DOL/DME electrolyte showed considerable voltage ﬂuctuations

The morphology of the Li metal anode after 100 cycles in Li–S

In contrast, the DEE/DME electrolyte produced more uniform and
densely packed dendrites with consistent Li grain sizes. This homo-

SEI layer, as discussed in Section 3.2. Detailed Li dendrite mor-

in Fig. S14. Even in the single-solvent DME system, dendrites 
formed unevenly, exhibiting a morphology similar to that in the

and uniform Li plating is strongly inﬂuenced by the presence of
the DEE solvent. To evaluate the thickness of the SEI layer formed
on the Li metal surface, cryo-TEM was conducted (Fig. 3f, g, and
Fig. S15). The average SEI thickness was 32.07 nm in the DEE/ 
DME electrolyte and 45.84 nm in the DOL/DME electrolyte, which 
is 1.43 times thicker. The thinner and more homogeneously dis-

uniform Li+ ﬂux and Li deposition, thereby enhancing Li metal sta-

XPS results (Fig. 3h, Fig. S16). The atomic contents (at%) of F and

that in DOL/DME, while the N content was 1.6 times higher. Addi-

inhomogeneous accumulation of insoluble Li2S2/Li2S species on the 
Li surface reduces the availability of active Li sites, thereby exacer-
bating irregular Li plating and stripping behavior.
In Fig. 3i, the shuttle current, representing corrosion between 
the Li metal anode and LiPS, was measured at a ﬁxed potential of 
2.35 V. This faradaic current is required to balance the polysulﬁde
shuttle from the cathode to the anode and reﬂects the extent of
concentration gradients within Li–S cells [40,41]. To ensure consis-
tency, the sulfur loading was ﬁxed at 1.9 mg. The DEE/DME elec-
trolyte maintained a highly stable shuttle current close to zero
(approximately 0.004 mA), whereas the DOL/DME electrolyte
exhibited a value about ﬁve times higher (approximately 
0.02 mA). These results conﬁrm that the DEE/DME electrolyte 
markedly mitigates the shuttle effect, thereby minimizing parasitic
reactions between LiPSs and the Li metal surface.

3.4. Evaluation of electrolyte stability and dynamic interfacial
behavior
As noted i n Section 3.3, the most signiﬁcant difference in the 
XPS S 2p spectra appears in the Li2Sx species region. In the DOL/ 
DME electrolyte, the proportion of Li2Sx species reached 56.1%, 
whereas in the DEE/DME electrolyte, it was signiﬁcantly sup-
pressed to 4.73%. This difference likely arises from the extent to
which polysulﬁde species remain stable within the electrolyte dur-
ing real-time operation. To investigate this behavior under actual
Li–S battery operating conditions, operando optical microscopy
was employed to visualize morphological and chromatic changes
during current application. Fig. 4a, b present time-lapse images 
after discharging the cells to 10% depth of discharge (DOD), fol-
lowed by a rest period. As shown in Fig. 4a(i), b(i), both electrolytes 
initially exhibited a similar color after 1 h of rest. However, as time 
progressed, the DOL/DME electrolyte gradually changed from deep 
red to yellow. This suggests that long-chain polysulﬁdes chemi-
cally react with the Li metal during rest, leading to their reduction
into short-chain polysulﬁdes [42,43]. In contrast, the DEE/DME 
electrolyte retained its initial red color with markedly less chro-
matic variation, implying a substantially suppressed interfacial
reactivity between polysulﬁdes and the Li metal surface.
Although the difference was clearly discernible by visual obser-
vation, a more quantitative metric was required to analyze the evo-
lution of electrolyte color. The hue index, which represents color in 
the HSV (hue, saturation, value) color space, serves as a suitable 
indicator. In this study, we focused on the speciﬁc transition from 
red to yellow observed in the electrolytes. To capture this relevant 
range, a normalized hue index was introduced, deﬁning standard 
red (RGB: 255, 0, 0) as 1 and standard yellow (RGB: 255, 255, 0) 
as 0. Moreover, since the concentration gradient of LiPSs can vary 
spatially within the electrolyte, it was necessary to consider posi-
tional dependence. To address this, the central region of the elec-
trolyte was divided into ﬁve vertical sections, with a total height 
o f 300 lm from Section 1 to Section 5. This approach enabled spa-
tially resolved and quantitative analysis of color variation across
the electrolyte (Fig. 4c). As shown in Fig. 4d an d Fig. S1 7, the DOL/ 
DME electrolyte exhibited a rapid decrease in normalized hue index 
during the early stages, with the average value dropping by 55.08% 
after 20 h compared with the initial state (1 h after rest). This trend 
reﬂects a progressive chromatic transition from red to yellow. In 
contrast, the DEE/DME electrolyte maintained a relatively stable 
hue index, retaining 74.84% of its initial value after 20 h, indicating 
superior temporal stability. To evaluate spatial uniformity, the mean 
and standard deviation of the hue index were calculated for each 
section. At all time points, the DEE/DME electrolyte consistently 
exhibited lower standard deviation values than the DOL/DME elec-
trolyte, suggesting more uniform color change and superior spatial 
homogeneity. At 20 h, the relative standard deviation (RSD) of 
DOL/DME was 4.6%, compared with 1.5% of DEE/DME, conﬁrmin g
a more inhomogeneous hue distribution in DOL/DME. To further
explore spatial evolution, the sectional gradient of the normalized
hue index was analyzed (Fig. 4e, f). By calculating the hue index dif-
ference between adjacent sections, we quantiﬁed the color shift 
slope across each region. In DOL/DME, a sharp gradient appeared 
between Sections 4 and 5 during the initial stages, and this transi-
tion continued to intensify until approximately 8 h, suggesting a fas-
ter color transition near the anode side. As time progressed and the 
color shifted toward yellow, the gradients between sections gradu-
ally diminished, indicating improved spatial homogeneity. In con-

<<PAGE 9>>
trast, the DEE/DME electrolyte maintained gradients below 0.01 
throughout, demonstrating highly uniform color transitions across 
all regions. This quantitative analysis further corroborates the supe-
rior electrochemical stability of DEE/DME, revealing its enhanced 
resistance to chromati c variation and reduced reactivity toward dis-
solved polysulﬁdes.

4. Conclusion s
In this study, a low-polarity electrolyte strategy was proposed for 
solvent selection in Li–S batteries, focusing on regulating polysulﬁde 
dissolution and stabilizing the Li metal interface. Although DEE 
alone exhibits poor compatibility with sulfur species due to its 
extremely low polarity, the incorporation of a suitable co-solvent 
is necessary to facilitate the sulfur redox reactions. Accordingly, 
DEE was blended with DME, and the composition was optimized 
to achieve superior electrochemical performance. The solvation 
structures and polysulﬁde interactions were investigated using 
Raman spectroscopy and theoretical calculations, revealing that 
the optimized electrolyte promoted the formation of a stable SEI 
enriched with LiF and Li3N. This interphase effectively suppressed 
the accumulation of insulating Li2S2/Li2S species on the Li metal sur-
face and enabled uniform Li deposition. To further evaluate the elec-
trolyte stability and internal morphological evolution under real-
time electrochemical operation, operando optical microscopy and 
quantitative image analysis were conducted. The DEE/DME elec-
trolyte exhibited high chemical stability against polysulﬁdes, effec-
tively mitigating parasitic reactions at the Li metal interface. By 
facilitating a rapid liquid-to-solid conversion process, the electrolyte 
also achieved excellent high-rate capability and long-term cycling 
stability, retaining 80.14% of its initial capacity after over 300 cycles 
at 3 C. This superior performance was further maintained under
practical conditions, with a high sulfur loading (4.5 mg cm−2) and
a low E/S ratio (6 lL mg−1). The insight into solvent effects demon-
strated in this study is expected to inspire further electrolyte inno-
vations across emerging battery systems, addressing persistent
challenges such as polysulﬁde dissolution and Li metal instability.
