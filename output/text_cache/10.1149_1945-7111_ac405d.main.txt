An Improved Cycling Performance of Different Types of
Composite Sulfur-Carbon Cathodes with the Use of Lithium
Polysulﬁdes Containing Electrolyte Solutions
E. Markevich,1,z
G. Salitra,1,z H. Yoshida,2 S. Sawada,2 and D. Aurbach1,*
1Department of Chemistry and BINA—BIU Institute for Nano-Technology and Advanced Materials, Bar-Ilan University,
Ramat Gan 5290002, Israel
2R&D Division, Nichia Corporation, Anan, Tokushima 774-8601, Japan
We report on stabilization of Li–S cells with different types of composite sulfur cathodes using ethereal LiTFSI/LiNO3/DOL/DME
electrolyte solutions containing a-priori 0.1 M Li2S8. These electrolyte solutions enable an improved cycling behavior for Li–S
cells compared to Li2S8-free electrolyte solutions, thanks to the presence of LiSx species from the beginning of operation. We show
that Li anodes cycled in Li∣S cells with solutions containing Li2S8 possess ﬂatter and more uniform surface, higher dimensions of
the surface structures in average and, as a result, a lower surface area. This surface morphology ensures a low rate of parasitic
surface reactions of the electrolyte components on the Li anodes’ surface, slower depletion of the electrolyte solution in the cells
and stabilization of the cells cycling. Besides, the presence of Li2S8 maintains a better integrity of composite sulfur/carbon/PVdF
cathodes, ensuring a better electronic contact between the particles in the composite cathodes. We believe that we outline herein a
logical approach for practical Li–S batteries, emphasizing high energy density, cost effectiveness and relatively simple production
procedures.

Li–S batteries with metallic lithium foils as the negative
electrodes and elemental sulfur as the positive electrodes’ active
mass are electrochemical systems with the potential for extremely
high gravimetric energy density. The theoretical speciﬁc capacity of
elemental sulfur cathodes is 1675 mAh g−1
S and the theoretical
energy density of Li–S cells is 2600 W h kg−1, which is much higher
than that of Li-ion batteries (about 415–670 W h kg−1 based on
graphite anodes and Li[MnNiCo]O2 or Li[NiCoAl]O2 cathodes).1–3
Besides, sulfur is abundant, cost-effective, and environmentally
friendly cathode material. However, commercialization of Li–S
batteries is constrained by several serious problems:
1.
A shuttle side reactions of lithium polysulﬁde (LPS) intermedi-
ates (formed by S reduction) between the working cathode and
anode, lead to the disability to fully charge the sulfur cathodes.4
2.
The passivation of the Li anodes is not completed; there are Li
surface side reactions that lead to inevitable loss of active
materials.
3.
Non uniformity of the Li anodes’ surface due to surface
reactivity and the presence of the surface ﬁlms leads to non-
uniform current distribution, thus to Li dendrites growth, what
causes safety issues and the consumption of the electrolyte
solution.5,6
Several LiSn species (Li poly-sulﬁdes, LPS) intermediates
formed by sulfur reduction in Li–S cells are soluble in the
conventional ethereal electrolyte solutions suitable for these cells.
They can be reduced directly by the Li anodes to LPS species of
higher Li content. LiN(SO2CF3)2 (LiTFSI) is commonly used as the
major electrolyte and LiNO3 serves as an additive that promotes
formation of highly protective surface ﬁlms on the Li anodes thus
reducing the shuttle side reactions. Numerous strategies were
proposed to prevent shuttle process by effective conﬁnement or
trapping of soluble LPS by components of the S composite cathodes
or by putting special interlayers between S cathodes and Li metal
anodes.2,7–10 Many efforts were made to prevent contact of LPS in
solution with Li anode, aiming at isolating its surface from these
species, and a great deal of different ingenious solutions were
proposed to minimize the contact of LPS with Li anodes. In parallel
to such efforts, a stable performance of Li–S cells with sulfur-carbon
composite cathodes and LPS-containing catholytes was demon-
strated in numerous works, suggesting a reasonable compatibility
of Li metal anodes with LPS-containing electrolyte solutions.11–13
Indeed, it was show that some reactions between lithium
polysulﬁde species and Li metal anodes can enhance passivation
and effectively suppress the growth of Li dendrites and to increase
the Coulombic efﬁciency of the process of Li plating/stripping on
stainless steel or copper substrates.14–17 According to Ref. 14, a
stable and uniform solid electrolyte interphase layer is formed due to
a synergetic effect of both LPS and LiNO3 as additives in ether-
based electrolyte solutions. Besides, it was shown that using Li2S6 as
an additive to electrolyte solutions in Li–S cells additive enhances
wetting of dense composite sulfur cathodes by in situ generating
electrolyte diffusion channels through direct chemical reactions
between sulfur and LiSn moieties.18 Xu et al. replaced the commonly
used salt LiTFSI with LPS species as the electrolytes, and showed
stable behavior of composite sulfur cathodes for 50 cycles with this
ethereal LPS electrolyte solutions.19 In our recent work we demon-
strated the positive effect of the initial presence of Li2S8 as an
additive in standard electrolyte solutions (comprising DOL,DME,
LiTFSI and LiNO3) on the stability and cycling efﬁciency of both
Li∣Li symmetric and full Li–S cells cycled with practical cycling
parameters (in terms of areal loading of electrodes, 100% DOD, high
current densities and a minimal content of the electrolyte solution in
the cells).20 With the aim to exclude the effect of different
components of composite sulfur cathodes on the obtained results
we used model binder free composite S/carbon electrodes. The
initial presence of LPS in solution as an additive was shown to allow
decreasing the amount of the electrolyte solution in the cells, yet
keeping their cycling performance. In the present work we demon-
strate the effect of the presence of Li2S8 as an additive in the
electrolyte solution with two different types of practical sulfur
composite cathodes. The ﬁrst one is comprised of sulfur encapsu-
lated in microporous carbon (MPC), carbon black conductive

Experimental
Materials.—Li metal foils (FMC Chemicals Limited (U.K.) with
the thickness of 0.25 mm which were kept in argon ﬁlled glovebox
were used without any pretreatment. Micrometer-sized activated
microporous
carbon
(MPC)
powder
(Mead
Westvaco,
USA)
(Fig. 1a) was used for the preparation of S-C composite cathodes.
Cathode sheets comprising elemental sulfur (70 wt. %), carbon black
(20 wt. %) and PVdF (10 wt. %) with the areal loading of 3.27 mgS
cm−2 were obtained from NEI Corporation (USA). The electrolyte
solutions were 1,3-dioxolane (DOL):1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) v/
v with 10 wt% LiTFSI and 2 wt% of LiNO3. Liquid Li2S8 was added
to this electrolyte solution to obtain 0.1 M Li2S8 solutions.
Preparation of S-MPC composite cathodes.—MPS was heated
at 950 °C for 4 h under 95% Ar 5% H2. Elemental sulfur (Aldrich)
and carbon powders were mixed by grinding them in mortar and
Vortex mixer with sulfur loading of 50 wt. %. Then the powders
were sealed in a stainless steel vessel (Swagelok) in an argon-ﬁled
glovebox and heated for 10 h at 155 °C and after that for 3 h at
200 °C. The composite MPC-sulfur powders, carbon black—Super P
(Superior Graphite) and PVdF in a weight ratio of 80:10:10, were
mixed with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). Electrodes were pre-
pared by coating the slurry onto carbon coated aluminum foils
(Exopack, USA) using a doctor-blade technique, dried overnight at
room temperature in dry air and after then were heated overnight at
60 °C in vacuum.
Cells assembly and electrochemical tests.—For galvanostatic
tests of Li∣S cells Li-metal foil disk electrodes and composite sulfur
cathodes with diameter of 14 mm were assembled using coin-type
cells (2325, NRC, Canada). The cells were cycled between
1.9–2.7 V. The cells were tested using a BT2000 multichannel
battery cycler (Arbin Instruments, USA).
Characterization of materials and electrodes.—Thermal gravi-
metric analysis (TGA) was carried out by a TA Instruments
apparatus (1GA Q500 model) up to 800 °C (10 °C min−1, N2
atmosphere, 10 ml min−1. The samples were pre-heated at 60 °C
under nitrogen in order to lower their total moisture content.
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained with a D8
Advance system (Bruker Inc.) using Cu Kα radiation operated at
40 mA and 40 kV.
Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were measured using Autosorb
iQ2 (Quantachrome) at 77 K.
SEM images were obtained with Environmental Scanning
Electron Microscope, Ouanta FEG 250 (FEI). Air sensitive samples
were transferred from the Ar ﬁlled glove box to the chamber of the
microscope using a homemade vacuum tight transferring cell.21
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used for the surface
analysis of the cycled Li metal anodes. Photoelectron spectra were
obtained with a Thermo Scientiﬁc Nexsa spectrometer (USA) using
monochromatized Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.68 eV). Binding
energies (BEs) were corrected with respect to the BE value of the
C1s peak at 285 eV. Air sensitive electrodes after cycling were
transferred from the glove box to X-ray photoelectron spectrometer
without contact with ambient air.
Prior to the XPS and SEM measurements, the cycled cells were
disassembled in the Ar ﬁlled glove box; cycled Li metal anodes were
washed four times with pure dry DOL.
Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the gaseous pro-
ducts from cycled cells were measured using a Magna 860 spectro-
meter (Nicolet) in a glovebox in an H2O- and CO2-free atmosphere.
The cycled cells were punctured under vacuum in a homemade
metallic FTIR cell equipped with two KBr windows.
Results and Discussion
A SEM image of a pristine MPC is presented in Fig. 1a.
Figures 1b and 1c show SEM images and EDX mapping of sulfur
distribution in sulfur-carbon composite electrodes.
X-ray diffraction patterns of the MPC pristine powder, elemental
sulfur powder and composite MPC-sulfur composite powders are
shown in Fig. 1d. XRD patterns of the sulfur powder exhibit its
crystalline structure. XRD patterns of pristine MPC powder reﬂect
the totally disordered structure of MPC carbon. Parameter R deﬁned
as the ratio of the height of (002) diffraction peak to the background
is close to approximately ∼1 for this powder22 due to almost
randomly oriented graphene sheets arranged as single layers No
sulfur peaks were detected in the MPC-sulfur composite powder
revealing that sulfur is in highly dispersed state inside the pores.
Figure 1e shows the TGA curves of the MPC-sulfur powder with
50 wt% sulfur, with a weight loss exactly corresponding to the sulfur
content in this composite powder.
The N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms of MPC before and after
sulfur impregnation are shown in Fig. 1f. The type I isotherm
obtained for a pristine MPC pristine powder indicates the micro-
porous structure of this carbon (⩽2 nm).23,24 The total pores volume
of the MPC powder before and after impregnation with sulfur at
P/P0 = 0.90 was around ∼1 cc g−1 and 0.2 cc g−1, respectively. The
BET speciﬁc surface area decreases signiﬁcantly due to sulfur
impregnation from an initial value of 2100 m2g−1 to 130 m2g−1.
The cumulative pore size distributions of pristine MPC and MPC-
sulfur composite shown in Fig. 1g demonstrate signiﬁcant reduction
of the micropores volume in the MPC-sulfur composite.

Figure 3 shows SEM images of Li metal anodes’ surfaces after
133 galvanostatic cycles in Li∣S cells with the two electrolyte
solutions with a current density of 0.14 mA cm−2. The surface
morphology of Li anodes cycled with both electrolyte solutions used
herein is characterized by a smooth, non-dendritic Li deposition.
However, for Li anodes cycled with LPS additive ﬂatter and more
uniform Li surface is observed. Besides, for LPS-containing
electrolyte solution the surface structures possess higher dimensions
in average and, as a result, a lower surface area. The lower surface
area of the Li metal anodes cycled with solutions containing the LPS
additive, leads to a lower rate of parasitic reactions of the electrolyte
components on the Li anodes’ surface, slower depletion of the
electrolyte solution in the cells and stabilization of the cells during
cycling.
SEM images of MPC/S composite cathodes cycled in Li∣S cells
in the two electrolyte solutions are shown in Fig. 4. One can see
regions with a low amount of binder on the surface of the electrodes
after prolonged cycling in LPS free electrolyte solution. Probably,
the presence of LPSs maintains better integrity of MPC/S-PVdF-
carbon black composite, especially on the top layer of the cathodes
and ensures a better electronic contact between MPC/S particles.

Note that the surface composition of the surface ﬁlms on the Li
metal anodes is highly dependent on the cycling conditions. XPS
characterization of the surface ﬁlms which were formed on Li metal
anodes after cycling in symmetric Li∣Li cells for more than 300
cycles with much higher current density (1.5 mA cm−2) revealed the
presence of both LiF and CF3-containing species. Obviously, the
current density and/or the presence of Li/S cathodes affect the
mechanism of the reductive decomposition of LiTFSI salt.14
Sulfur compounds in the surface ﬁlms are presented mainly as
Li2SO3, Li2S2 and LiSn polysulﬁdes. Li2SO3 is obviously the main
S-containing product, which is formed from LiTFSI salt according to
reaction (1). Besides, according to Ref. 17, the formation of Li2SO3
occurs as a result of an oxidation reaction of LPSs by LiNO3.
All the data presented above demonstrate the advantages of the
use of LPS-containing electrolyte solution for cycling of Li∣S cells
with composite MPC-S cathodes with sulfur encapsulated in the
pores of MPC powder. In our previous work we demonstrated
enhanced cycling stability of composite S-carbon cathodes prepared
with binder free activated microporous carbon cloth when the
electrolyte solutions contained LPSs.20
It is important to emphasize that that positive effect of LPS an
additive is relevant when it presents in solutions a-priori, when Li is
brought in contact with the electrolyte solutions at the ﬁrst time. In
such circumstances, the presence of LPS affects dominantly and
positively the surface chemistry of the Li anodes (leading for the
improved performance). In cells containing only conventional
electrolyte solutions (DOL, DME, LiTFSI, Li-nitrate), LPS species
are also formed by sulfur reduction, but in these cells, they are
formed too late to affect positively the Li surface chemistry, which is
dominated mostly by DOL and TFSI anions reduction.

Li∣S cells with a different type of composite sulfur cathodes
comprised of 70% elemental sulfur, PVdF binder and carbon black
conductive agent (so called, open system) with practical sulfur
loading of 3.27 mA h cm−2. Figure 6 shows an improved cycling of
these composite electrodes for 100 cycles with LPS-containing
compared to LPS free standard electrolyte solution. The amount of
the electrolyte used was only 9.2 μl mg−1

We demonstrate herein an important directions and compromise
related to practical Li-sulfur batteries. These battery systems can
provide the highest energy density that can be extracted from
rechargeable battery systems, if we use thin Li metal foil anodes,
simple composite sulfur cathodes containing 70% sulfur by weight,
and an optimized low amount of suitable electrolyte solutions
comprising DOL, DME, LiTFSI, LiNO3 and LiSn (e.g. Li2S6,
Li2S8). What we propose herein is cost effective, relatively safe,
Li-sulfur battery systems which production procedure is the sim-
plest.
High energy density and cost effectiveness are achieved with
these systems on the account of cycle life. However, for uses such as
unmanned aviation and transportation such Li–S battery systems
maybe very attractive.
Conclusions
Our results conﬁrm that the addition of LPSs to standard electrolyte
solution LiTFSI/LiNO3/DOL/DME have a positive effect on the
capacity retention and cycling efﬁciency of Li∣S cells with different
types of composite sulfur cathodes. SEM analysis revealed that the
addition of LPSs to the electrolyte solution has a positive effect on the
surface morphology of both Li metal anodes and composite MPC/S
cathodes. The surface of Li anodes cycled with LPS containing
solutions was ﬂatter and more uniform. Composite sulfur cathodes, in
turn, preserve their integrity and better contact between their MPC/S
particles. XPS analysis revealed the absence of CFx-containing moieties
in the surface ﬁlms which were formed on Li anodes, and LiF was
detected as the only F-containing products of LiTFSI salt decomposi-
tion. Obviously, in Li∣S cells the mechanism of LiTFSI reduction
depends on the cycling conditions. The obtained results show that the
addition of LPSs to the electrolyte solutions containing LiNO3 is a very
promising strategy of developing advanced Li∣S batteries
