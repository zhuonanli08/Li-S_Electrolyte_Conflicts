ABSTRACT: Despite the several advantages of Li−S batteries compared to Li-ion batteries, their real-world application is limited
because of the rapid capacity fading associated with the low electronic conductivity of sulfur and polysulfide formation. Herein, we
present a high-performance Li−S battery enabled by an organic−inorganic hybrid cathode−electrolyte interface (CEI) on a sulfur−
carbon composite cathode. This hybrid surface film is formed by the in situ reaction of the citric acid (CA) cathode additive with the
electrolyte solution and by unavoidable LiTFSI/LiNO3 decomposition during the charge−discharge process. The Li−S battery
cathode (SCB55-CA5) containing an optimized CEI composition exhibits high specific capacity (1207 mAh g−1 at 50 mAh g−1), rate
performance (406 mAh g−1 at 1 A g−1), and cycling stability (405 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 500 mAh g−1, 0.075% loss per cycle)
compared to the reference cathode (38 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles at 500 mAh g−1, 0.33% loss per cycle). Impedances of the hybrid
CEI-containing Li−S batteries are also considerably reduced due to the formation of hybrid CEI, leading to an increased shelf life.
The technique presented in this work enabled the use of an inexpensive carbon matrix for high-performance Li−S batteries, which is
a crucial factor for their commercialization. This study also illustrates the crucial role of the CEI in the electrochemical performance
of Li−S batteries.
■INTRODUCTION
Since their discovery in the 1990s, rechargeable Li-ion batteries
have become one of the most attractive technologies for
electrochemical energy storage.1−3 Due to their several
advantages, they have been dominating the rechargeable
battery market. Lithium-ion batteries possess higher energy
and power densities than Pb-acid, Ni−Cd, and Ni-MH
batteries. Nevertheless, the development of Li-ion batteries
has reached a point where they cannot meet the ever-
increasing energy-density demands of next-generation electric
vehicles.4,5 Consequently, new high-performance battery
systems capable of large-scale energy storage have been
investigated recently. Li−S batteries are widely considered
one of the most attractive alternatives to Li-ion batteries.6,7
Sulfur is an interesting cathode material for Li−S batteries due
to its high theoretical capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and theoretical
energy density (2600 Wh kg−1). Additionally, sulfur has a
significantly lower cost (∼300 USD/metric ton) compared to
transition-metal oxide cathodes used in conventional Li-ion
batteries. Natural abundance (∼0.05% of Earth’s crust) and the
nontoxic nature of sulfur are also highly appealing for next-
generation secondary batteries.8−12
However, real-world applications of Li−S batteries are
limited by their mediocre electrochemical performance
resulting from the poor electronic conductivity of sulfur (5 ×
10−30 S cm−1 at 25 °C), large volume change (70−80%)
during charge−discharge, and polysulfide formation/shuttling
during cycling.13 Subsequently, numerous approaches such as
conductive polymer coatings, insertion of a porous carbon
interlayer, use of superconcentrated electrolytes, electrolyte
additives, metal oxide coatings on cathodes, etc. were
developed to mitigate the above-mentioned drawbacks of the

Li−S battery system.14−21 Coating a sulfur−carbon composite
cathode with various materials that can effectively prevent the
formation or trap polysulfides is one of the widely used
techniques. Improved specific capacity, rate performance, and
cycling stability are identified in the case of sulfur cathodes
coated with metal oxides such as Al2O3, TiO2, Co3O4, and
SiO2.22,23 Hong et al. recently demonstrated improved
electrochemical performance of a graphene-sulfur hybrid
cathode coated with Al2O3.24 Nevertheless, ex situ cathode
protection has been proven not to be an effective method from
an economic point of view.25 This is mainly due to the use of
sophisticated and expensive techniques such as chemical vapor
deposition and atomic layer deposition. However, many
technologically relevant materials cannot be deposited due to
the complex nature of these coating techniques.26 Therefore,
alternative methods need to be developed for the efficient and
inexpensive protection of sulfur cathodes. Designing and
fabricating inexpensive and high-performance sulfur cathodes
thus remains a challenge for the commercialization of Li−S
batteries.
Solid−electrolyte interfaces (SEI) also play a key role in the
electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries.27 In recent
years, cathode−electrolyte interface (CEI) engineering has
emerged as a critical strategy to enhance the performance of
lithium−sulfur batteries. Over the past two years, significant
progress has been made in developing multifunctional CEI
designs.28,29 Protective coatings and ultrathin interfacial layers
have been introduced to suppress polysulfide shuttling and
improve cycling stability. Functional electrolytes, including
ionic liquids and redox mediators, have been employed to
stabilize the CEI and reduce interfacial resistance. More
recently, integrated CEI designs that simultaneously regulate
polysulfide chemistry, enhance ionic conductivity, and stabilize
electrode surfaces have been highlighted as a promising
development trend. These advances demonstrate a clear shift
from single-function coatings toward holistic CEI engineering
approaches.30,31
Formation of a stable CEI capable of blocking polysulfide
leaching is key to enhancing the cycling stability of sulfur
cathodes.32,33 One of the well-known strategies for CEI
engineering is the formulation of electrolyte compositions
containing surface film-forming cosolvents or salt addi-
tives.34−36 For instance, Mikhaylik reported enhanced electro-
chemical performance of sulfur cathodes in electrolyte
solutions containing LiNO3 additive.37 Kaskel’s group reported
in situ formation of passivation layers on both cathodes and
anodes due to the electrochemical reduction of lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) salt at 60 °C without the
need for any electrolyte additives. While the cathode SEI
originated from the interaction between FSI−anions and
LiPSs, surface film formation at the Li-anode was caused by
LiF deposition. The synergistic effect of both phenomena
resulted in a remarkable enhancement of the overall perform-
ance in terms of Coulombic efficiencies and cycling stability.38
Similarly, Markevich et al. proved improved CEI formation
and electrochemical performance in a LiFSI-based ionic liquid
(IL) electrolyte.32 Wu et al. also reported the use of lithium
bromide (LiBr) as an additive in a highly concentrated
LiTFSI/DME electrolyte.34 Due to LiBr oxidation and DME
decomposition at elevated potentials, a precycling stage in the
range of 2.5−3.6 V prevented the formation of dendrites at the
Li electrode and induced the formation of a polymerized
protective layer on the cathode surface. Lee et al. demonstrated
the use of fluoroethylene carbonate additive to form a
protective layer on the cathode at a potential close to 0.1 V.
Capacity loss in this case was 0.023% per cycle, and that kept
the Coulombic efficiency at almost 100% for 1000 cycles.39
Another approach is the use of superconcentrated electrolyte
solutions without the use of additives. Despite these advances,
most of the demonstrated methods are not scalable. In
addition, they often use expensive additives and electrolyte
compositions.40−47 Moreover, the use of electrolyte additives
such as LiNO3 results in the self-discharge issue, which
seriously affects the shelf life of Li−S batteries. Hence, it is
crucial to develop alternative, inexpensive, and scalable
approaches for the real-world application of Li−S batteries.
Herein, we demonstrate a high-performance Li−S battery
based on an organic−inorganic hybrid CEI on the sulfur−
carbon hybrid electrode. This hybrid surface film is formed by
the in situ reaction of citric acid (CA) cathode additive with
the electrolyte solution and unavoidable LiTFSI/LiNO3
decomposition during the first charge−discharge cycle. This
work not only regulates polysulfide behavior but also maintains
the SEI stability on the lithium anode, thereby offering a
balanced and effective interfacial design. Commercially
available, inexpensive, and most commonly used carbon
black and elemental sulfur are used as cathode components
in this case, considering real-world application and scaling up
of the technology. CA, an additive used in this case, is also
environmentally friendly, and cathode protection through
hybrid CEI formation can be further optimized by implement-
ing a variety of carboxylic acids. The demonstrated approach of
stabilizing Li−S batteries via organic−inorganic hybrid CEI
formation is advantageous for the design of next-generation
high-performance Li−S batteries.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Material Synthesis
All reagents were used as received from the manufacturer without
further purification. A sulfur−carbon composite (SCB55) was
prepared by mixing sulfur (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and carbon black
(CB, MTI Corporation) in an S/C ratio of 1.25:1. High-energy ball
milling for 4 h was used to reduce and homogenize particle size. The
resulting powder was then subjected to a melt-diffusion process where
the mixture was heated up to 200 °C for 12 h in a sealed vessel under
an Ar atmosphere. CA-modified sulfur cathodes were prepared by
adding 2 wt % (SCB55-CA2), 5 wt % (SCB55-CA5), and 10 wt %
(SCB55-CA10) CAs, followed by mixing.
Material Characterization
An Empyrean diffractometer (PANalytical) fitted with a Cu Kα
radiation source (λ = 1.54 Å) and a Lynx detector was used to record
the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (2θ = 10−80°) of the samples.
Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR)
spectra were acquired using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer. Sulfur
content in the composite (55%) was determined by thermogravi-
metric analysis (TGA) using a Q50 analyzer (TA Instruments) in the
temperature range of 25−400 °C under a N2 atmosphere (heating
rate of 10 °C min−1). A typical 6 mg cathode used in this study
consists of 3.3 mg/cm2 sulfur loading. Textural properties of the
carbon−sulfur composite powder were investigated using a Micro-
metrics ASAP 2020 surface area analyzer. FEI Helios Nano-Lab 600i
Dual-Beam equipment was used to record scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX)
mapping of the samples. High-resolution transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images were recorded using an FEI-Talos
F200X microscope operating at 80 kV. A Thermo Scientific Multilab
2000 spectrometer fitted with a dual-anode X-ray source (Mg Kα,
1253.6 eV and Al Kα, 1486.7 eV) was used for collecting the X-ray

Electrochemical Measurements
Carbon−sulfur composite cathodes were fabricated by mixing SCB55
composite with different amounts of CA (2, 5, and 10 wt %) and 10
wt % polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 99.5%, MTI Corporation) in N-
methylpyrrolidone (NMP, 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich). The doctor blade
technique was used for coating the slurry on the aluminum foil. Coin
cells (2032 type) were assembled by using sulfur−carbon composite
cathodes, Li−metal foil (99.9%, MTI Corporation) anode, and glass
fiber separator (GF/C, Sigma-Aldrich). Electrolyte consisted of 1 M
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%, Sigma-
Aldrich) solution in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99%,
Sigma-Aldrich) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.5%, Sigma-
Aldrich). Lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as
an additive (0.2 M). The electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio used in this
case is 10 μL/mg S. Coin cells were fabricated inside a glovebox filled
with ultrahigh purity argon (99.999%), and the content of oxygen
(O2) and water (H2O) was kept below 1 ppm. The Neware BTS4000
multichannel battery tester system was used for galvanostatic charge−
discharge rate performance (50−1000 mA g−1) and long cycling tests
of Li−S batteries, in the voltage range of 1.8−2.7 V. A ZIVE SP1
electrochemical workstation was used for cyclic voltammetry (CV)
and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements.
Potentiostatic EIS measurements were performed at open-circuit
voltage (OCV) in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 10 mHz. All
potentials presented are vs Li/Li+, and the specific capacity
measurements are within a 5% error limit. All electrochemical
measurements were performed at room temperature (∼25 °C).

Quantification of sulfur content in the sulfur−carbon
composite (Figure 1c) is performed using TGA. In the case
of composite powder SCB55, single-step weight loss of 55 wt
% around 250 °C confirms the homogeneity of sulfur
particles.49 Such a homogeneous distribution of nanosulfur in
the composite is crucial to achieve superior electrochemical
performance. Textural property analysis (Figure 1d) of SCB55
powder through N2 adsorption−desorption analysis is
performed to investigate the incorporation of sulfur. A
noticeable (∼50%) reduction in the micropores and BET
surface area of CB (133.5 m2 g−1 in AB vs 70.7 m2 g−1 in
SCB55) verified successful infiltration of sulfur within the
carbon matrix. Although micropores are partially filled (22.0
m2 g−1 in CB vs 9.2 m2 g−1 in SCB55), sulfur infiltration in said
micropores has been preferential, since there is a lower
reduction in the external surface area (111.4 m2 g−1 in CB vs
61.5 m2 g−1 in SCB55). Barrett−Joyner−Halenda pore-size
distribution of SCB55 (Figure 1d inset) demonstrates the
presence of micro-, meso-, and macropores, which reflects a
wide pore-size distribution.
A lack of well-defined porosity can be expected for
commercial CB, where pore-size distribution is an uncon-
trolled parameter during the fabrication process. A broad pore-
size range facilitates the accommodation of sulfur during the
melt-diffusion process of SCB55. This is due to the small size
of micropores that provides high surface area, and the large size
of meso- and macropores provides high pore volume.50
Preferential filling of sulfur in the micropores of SCB55 favors
the partial inhibition of LiPS dissolution, considering that the
narrow size of micropores impedes solvent penetration and
ensures a solid−solid lithiation/delithiation of confined sulfur
molecules.51 Mesopores and macropores allow optimal contact
between the CB matrix and electrolyte, thus improving the
charge transfer kinetics.52 Morphological characterization
through SEM (Figure 1e) and TEM images of SCB55
confirms that the morphology of CB particles is preserved
after ball milling, heat treatment, and sulfur melt diffusion. The
presence of sulfur was also observed on the carbon particle at
different magnifications, which also signifies successful sulfur
infiltration into the CB matrix. EDX elemental mapping of
carbon and sulfur (Figure 1f,g) in SCB55 demonstrates a
homogeneous distribution of sulfur without covering the entire
surface of carbon particles, ensuring optimal electronic
conductivity of the sulfur−carbon composite. The TEM
image of the sulfur−carbon composite powder (Figure 1h)
also verified the existence of individual carbon particles with a
low degree of agglomeration, which is in line with the SEM
image. These characteristics are beneficial for improved
electrode-electrolyte contact and reduced charge-transfer
resistance of the sulfur−carbon composite cathode.

oxidation peak. Interestingly, no further reduction peaks can be
observed in the following cycles, meaning that the electro-
chemical reduction of CA additive is completed during the first

electrolyte components, CV tests of 50 wt % CA-containing
cathodes are performed. No electrochemical responses are
observed in the LiNO3-free electrolyte solution (Figure S2a).
However, an intense reduction peak can be observed at ∼3.5 V

that the OCV increase is only due to the electrochemical
reaction between LiNO3 and the CA additive. This finding is in
agreement with previous reports of electrochemically induced
reduction between CA and lithium-containing salts.56−58
Lithium-ion storage performance of CA-free and CA-
containing cathode compositions has been tested in 2032-
type coin-cells containing a Li−metal anode and LiTFSI/
LiNO3-DOL/DME electrolyte solution. This is crucial for
investigating the effect of the CA additive on electrochemical
performance and optimizing its loading in the carbon−sulfur
hybrid cathodes. Galvanostatic rate performance of the sulfur−
carbon hybrid cathodes (Figure 2f) confirmed superior specific
capacities of CA-containing cathodes, especially at low current
densities. This should be expected due to the slow and
controlled reaction between CA and LiNO3 that results in the
in situ formation of high-quality SEI on the cathode (CEI)
surface. This is highly beneficial, as slow CEI formation leads
to compact, uniform deposition of individual components. The
SCB55-CA5 cathode exhibits a specific capacity of 1000 mAh
g−1 at 50 mA g−1 current density, which is superior to those of
SCB55-CA2 (910 mAh g−1), SCB55-CA10 (770 mAh g−1),
and SCB55 (900 mAh g−1). Nevertheless, cycling at a high rate
of 1 A g−1 leads to reduced differences in specific capacities.
These results demonstrate that excess CA additive is
detrimental to the electrochemical performance of sulfur−
carbon composite cathodes. At low current densities, SCB55-
CA10 delivers specific capacities that are ∼15% lower
compared to the CA-free reference cathode SCB55. Although
higher carbon content could improve the electronic con-
ductivity, SCB55-CA10 exhibits low specific capacities. This
suggests that a higher CA content could lead to thicker CEI
formation that can block the accessibility of the electrolyte to
the active material, which negatively affects the cathode
performance.59
The effect of CA additive on the long-term cycling stability
has also been studied at a current density of 200 mA g−1
(Figure S3). Sulfur−carbon hybrid cathodes containing CA
additive are initially cycled at 20 mA g−1 to promote better
CEI formation, which is crucial for long-term cycling stability.
All electrodes delivered specific capacities matching the rate
performance tests, validating the obtained results. It is worth
mentioning that SCB55-CA2, SCB55-CA5, and SCB55-CA10
show consistent cycling and evince that the slow electro-
chemical preconditioning stage really improves their cyclabil-
ity. However, the absence of preconditioning at 20 mA g−1
results in cycling instability within the first 15 cycles and leads
to a 30% capacity loss, delivering only 552 mAh g−1 after 100
cycles. The SCB55-CA5 cathode exhibits the best capacity
retention, being able to maintain ∼90% (687 mAh g−1) of its
initial specific capacity after 100 cycles. The cathode
containing lower CA content (SCB55-CA2) shows similar
performance compared to SCB55, confirming 2 wt % CA is
insufficient to enhance the electrochemical performance.
These results manifest that a low CA content might lead to a
thinner CEI that is unable to prevent LiPSs from leaching.
Hence, a 5 wt % CA loading within the cathode composition
has been established as optimal for longer galvanostatic tests,
which have been performed at higher rates to evaluate the
electrochemical stability of the cells at different cycling
conditions. While comparing the long cycling performance of
SCB55 and SCB55-CA5 at a high current of 500 mA g−1
(Figure 3a), after preconditioning at 20 mA g−1, it is shown
that faster rates lead to better capacity retention when the CA
additive is present in the cathode. Despite the performance
differences, the voltage profiles of SCB55 and SCB55-CA5
(Figure 3b,c) are similar.
It should be noted that there is a third plateau at ∼1.8 V,
only during the first cycle discharge curve of the SCB55
cathode (Figure 3b). It is widely known that the irreversible
reduction of LiNO3 occurs at about 1.8−1.5 V, only during the
first discharge.60,61 Therefore, this plateau is directly related to
the irreversible reduction of LiNO3. However, LiNO3
reduction in the case of the SCB55-CA5 cathode is negligible
due to the efficient formation of a CEI layer in the first cycle,
which limits further contact with the electrolyte solution.
Galvanostatic cycling tests and corresponding voltage profiles
of SCB55 and SCB55-CA5 show that SCB55 only requires 150
cycles to lose around 50% of its initial specific capacity (0.33%
loss per cycle), delivering 38 mAh g−1 with extremely low
Coulombic efficiency (68%) after 400 cycles, representing total
capacity fading of the battery. Meanwhile, the SCB55-CA5
cathode shows superior Coulombic efficiency (97%) and high
electrochemical stability, delivering 405 mA g−1. Capacity
retention of 70% after 500 cycles represents a 0.075% capacity
loss per cycle, indicating that LiPS leaching and shuttling can
be efficiently mitigated by the in situ formed CEI layer. Hence,
increased specific capacity and superior cycling stability are
solely attributed to the addition of CA as a CEI-forming agent.
It is worth noting that our cathode design, consisting of the
modification of a standard sulfur−carbon composite CA
functional additive, can compete well with previously reported
complex cathode architectures.28,61−63
EIS measurements are performed to further investigate the
effect of CA loading on the electrochemical performance of
carbon−sulfur hybrid cathodes.64−66 The Nyquist plot (Figure
3d,e) consists of high-, medium-, and low-frequency
components corresponding to electrolyte resistance (Re),
charge-transfer resistance (Rct), and Warburg impedance
(W), respectively.67 Since carbon and sulfur content are
identical in SCB55 and SCB55-CA5 cathodes, any electro-
chemical differences result from the effect of CA on the
electrochemical reactions during the charge−discharge process.
EIS results in Figure 3de, and Figure S4 reveal that CA
addition increases the initial Rct of the SCB55-CA5 cathode
compared to SCB55 (310 Ω vs 103 Ω, respectively). Although
the initial cell cycling leads to a huge drop in the Rct of both
electrodes (10 Ω and 7 Ω for SCB55 and SCB55-CA5,
respectively), the Rct of the SCB55 electrode increased to 26 Ω
after 20 galvanostatic cycles, whereas the Rct of the SCB55-
CA5 cathode remains stable upon continuous cycling and
retains 14 Ω after 20 cycles. After the first cycle, SCB55 and

Li−S batteries containing SCB55 and SCB55-CA5 cathodes
were prepared and stored without any externally applied
potential to methodically investigate the self-discharge
phenomenon. The self-discharge rate has been monitored
over time by studying the changes related to cell impedance
(Figure 4a,b) and cell OCV (Figure S5). By observing how the
OCV of the noncycled cells evolves, we can state that the
severe voltage drop within the first 5−10 days is due to the
inherent self-discharge rate of Li−S batteries and the fast
tendency of the redox system to quickly form LiPSs. This can
be clearly observed in the case of Li−S batteries containing the
SCB55 cathode, where the voltage drops to 2.4 V within the
first 1−2 days. In contrast, rapid voltage fading is mitigated in
the case of the SCB55-CA5 cathode, where voltage decreases
less abruptly during the first 10 days of storage, and eventually
stabilizes at 2.5 V, which avoids the conversion of sulfur into
long-chain LiPSs. Although no external potential is applied, the
CA additive slowly reacts with the LiNO3 salt of the electrolyte
solution, minimizing the voltage drop during the first 10 days
of storage and preventing the rapid LiPS formation. The slow
rise in voltage to ∼2.8 V in the case of the SCB55-CA5
cathode can be attributed to the redistribution and re-
equilibration of soluble polysulfide species in the electrolyte.
During the standstill, polysulfides undergo redox equilibrium
shifts, which can temporarily increase the OCV. The presence
of CA additive modifies the polysulfide chemistry, likely
stabilizing intermediate species and slowing their relaxation
kinetics compared to conventional Li−S systems. Higher
impedance values are observed in the case of the SCB55-CA5
cathode (275 Ω) compared to SCB55 (89 Ω), which signifies a
difference in the electrochemical process during storage, even
in the absence of an externally applied potential. Even in the
absence of applied potential, Nyquist plots of the SCB55-CA5
cathode revealed gradual interaction between CA additive and
LiNO3, which drops the cell’s impedance by ∼25% (204 Ω)
after the first 10 days of storage. The Li−S battery containing
the SCB55 cathode experienced an increase of ∼55% (139 Ω)
in the same time period. Even though the impedance of the
SCB55-CA5 cathode manifests a clear decrease in impedance,
the lack of applied potential prevents the CA additive from
totally fulfilling its role as a CEI modifier. These results also
suggest that the CA additive is only partially able to provide
cathode protection, and external potential is required for its
reaction with LiNO3 electrolyte additive. Slight changes can be
observed from day 10 to day 100 for the SCB55 cathode,
where OCV monitoring indicates the slow, stable formation of
long-chain LiPSs at 2.4 V. Even after 100th day of storage,
similar characteristics corresponding to sulfur’s first reduction
stage are observed with no changes in the cell impedance (136
Ω). In the case of the SCB55-CA5 cathode, the reaction
between CA additive and LiNO3 starts to dominate the sulfur
redox process after 10 days, and the cell voltage slowly
increases. During 100 days of storage, the voltage of Li−S cells
containing the SCB55-CA5 cathode continuously increased to
2.80 V, preventing the formation of a 2.4 V plateau where
sulfur undergoes its first step reduction. Additionally, EIS
measurements from day 10 to 100 confirmed a slow reaction
between CA additive and LiNO3 and the corresponding
inhibition of sulfur reduction to Li2S6, thereby reducing cell
impedance to 200 Ω (Figure 4b).

Finally, we have implemented postcycling SEM analysis to
investigate the hybrid CEI formation. Surface and cross-
sectional images of SCB55-CA5 and SCB55 cathodes evince
clear morphological differences. Surface morphology of these
CEI’s on CA-containing and CA-free cathodes does not show
significant differences (Figure 7b,c). However, considerable
variations are observed in the cross-sectional images. The
SCB55 cathode without CA additive formed a thick and
porous CEI (Figure 7d), which is to be expected due to the
uncontrolled decomposition of LiTFSI and LiNO3. This is also
in line with the high impedance and rapid capacity fading on
continuous cycling associated with polysulfide leaching. On the
other hand, a cross-sectional image of SCB55-CA5 confirmed
the formation of a compact and thin CEI (Figure 7e), which is
in line with the low impedance and excellent cycling stability
due to limited polysulfide shuttling. EDX mapping of the
SCB55-CA5 cathode (Figure 7f−i) exhibited uniform
distribution of C, S, O, and F, which further substantiates
the formation of a uniform high-quality CEI in the presence of
the CA additive. It is thus clear that the CA additive in the
cathode plays a crucial role in the CEI formation process.
Formation of a thin and compact CEI on the SCB55-CA5
electrode that effectively protects the sulfur−carbon composite
cathode from electrolyte attack can be explained by the crucial
role of the CA additive in the surface film formation. As
observed from the electrochemical, spectroscopic, and micro-
scopic results, the CA additive results in a significant difference
in the performance and CEI morphology/chemical composi-
tion.
Surface film formation in the absence and presence of the
CA additive can be explained as follows. In the case of a
sulfur−carbon composite cathode without CA additive, LiTFSI
and LiNO3 decomposition results in the formation of CEI
composed of LixNOy, LixSOy, Li2NSO2CF3, etc. This is in line
with the surface chemical studies of sulfur−carbon composite
cathodes reported earlier. Uncontrolled decomposition of the
electrolyte solution in this case can be credited to the
formation of a thick, porous, and nonuniform CEI that
facilitates polysulfide leaching and shuttling. In the presence of
a CA additive, its electrochemical reaction with LiNO3 during
the first charge−discharge cycle results in the formation of
LiCit. This organic CEI layer could act as an initiator for the
subsequent deposition of inorganic CEI components (LixNOy,
LixSOy, Li2NSO2CF3, etc.) formed by the decomposition of
LiTFSi and LiNO3 (Scheme 1). Additionally, the reactivity of
the electrolyte solution toward the sulfur−carbon composite
cathode can be considerably reduced due to the initial
formation of a LiCit organic layer, which is clearly reflected
in the formation of a compact CEI on cycled cathodes
containing CA additive.
It should be noted that an organic−inorganic hybrid CEI is
formed in the case of CA additive-containing cathodes,
whereas a conventional inorganic CEI is formed in the case
of CA-free sulfur−carbon composite cathodes after charge−
discharge cycling. Differences in the chemical composition and
microstructures of CEIs are also reflected in the EIS studies,
where the organic−inorganic hybrid CEI exhibited improved
Li-ion diffusion and reduced charge-transfer resistances. These
differences in the CEI properties eventually resulted in the
significantly improved electrochemical performance of Li−S
batteries containing CA-modified sulfur−carbon composite
cathodes. The inorganic CEI formed in the absence of CA
additive results in the increased reactivity of sulfur with the
electrolyte solution, followed by the leaching and shuttling of
polysulfides, which causes inferior electrochemical perform-
ance. On the other hand, the preconditioning stage of SCB55-
CA5 at a low current density of 20 mA g−1 modifies the CEI by
acting as an initiator for the subsequent deposition of SEI
components due to electrolyte decomposition (LixNOy,
LixSOy, Li2NSO2CF3, etc.). The CA additive strategy
demonstrated here could also be extended to high-loading
cathodes, where its ability to regulate polysulfide chemistry and
stabilize interfaces may help achieve high energy density while
maintaining an excellent cycling stability. In conclusion, the
demonstrated strategy of the in situ formation of an organic−
inorganic hybrid CEI is highly promising for the development
of next-generation cost-effective Li−S batteries with excellent
rate performance, cycling stability, and shelf life.
■CONCLUSION
In summary, we presented a high-performance lithium−sulfur
battery enabled by an organic−inorganic hybrid CEI on a
sulfur−carbon composite cathode. The use of a CA additive in
this case resulted in the formation of lithium citrate due to the
reaction with LiNO3 of the electrolyte solution. Inorganic
components of the CEI are composed of LixNOy, LixSOy,
Li2NSO2CF3, Li2CO3, LiF, etc., formed by the LiTFSI and
LiNO3 decomposition. Hybrid CEI resulted in enhanced
specific capacity, rate performance, cycling stability, and shelf
life compared to reference Li−S cells without CA additive. The
superior electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries
composed of CA-containing cathodes is credited to the
formation of an organic−inorganic hybrid CEI that consid-
erably reduces polysulfide shuttling and capacity fading. The
