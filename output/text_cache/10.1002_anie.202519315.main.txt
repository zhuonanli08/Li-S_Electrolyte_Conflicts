Advanced Self-Phase-Separating Electrolytes for High-Performance
Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Xu Yao+, Zhicheng Wang+,* Suwan Lu+, Haifeng Tu, Jingjing Xu, Xingdong Ma, Kun Liang,
Yongbin Lin, Anqi Chen, Sicheng Xu, Ju Ren, Ke Wang, Fengrui Zhang, Jieyun Zheng,
Xiaodong Wu,* and Hong Li*
Abstract: Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries face a critical challenge in synergistically optimizing high-sulfur-loading
redox kinetics and suppressing soluble lithium polysulﬁde (LiPSs) shuttle effects. Herein, we propose a self-phase-
separating electrolyte design strategy based on heterogeneous LiPSs dissolution characteristics, using co-solvents of
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME) to induce spontaneous phase separation during LiPSs
dissolution through solvation disparity. The constructed electrolyte system facilitates formation of a strong-solvation region
at the cathode to maintain rapid sulfur redox kinetics while establishing a weak-solvation region at the anode to form a
stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), thereby achieving dual objectives of “kinetics promotion and shuttle suppression”
via a spatially partitioned dual-zone synergistic mechanism. This strategy enables steady cycling above 170 cycles of single-
layer Li-S pouch cells with ultra-thin Li anodes (50 µm) and high-sulfur-loading cathodes (4.3 mgs cm−2). Moreover,
under practical lean-electrolyte conditions (6.2 mgs cm−2 sulfur loading, 50 µm Li, 3 µL mgs−1 electrolyte), a 1.8 Ah multi-
layer pouch cell delivers 323 Wh kg−1 energy density with stable cycling above 50 cycles. This work provides an effective
solution for resolving the critical trade-off between rapid sulfur conversion kinetics and stable anode interfacial behavior
in metal-sulfur batteries.
Introduction
Amidst the urgent global demand for energy transition
and carbon neutrality, developing novel energy storage
technologies with ultrahigh energy density has emerged
as a frontier challenge in energy science.[1,2] The lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) battery system, leveraging its unique dual-high-
capacity electrode characteristics of sulfur cathode (1675
mAh g−1) and Li anode (3860 mAh g−1), demonstrates a
superior theoretical gravimetric energy density of 2,600 Wh
kg−1, positioning it as a transformative system to surpass
current energy storage limits.[3,4] The competitiveness of
Li-S batteries stems from resource substitution of critical
metals, compared to layered oxide cathodes reliant on low-
abundance cobalt/nickel, sulfur offers a disruptive advantage
through its high natural abundance and low cost.[5,6] However,
the polysulﬁde (LiPSs) shuttle effect creates orders-of-
magnitude gaps between theoretical and practical cycling
stability/discharge capacity, constituting the primary technical
barrier to industrialization.[7,8]
Distinct from the intercalation mechanism in conventional
lithium-ion batteries, the unique solid (S8)-liquid (LiPSs)-
solid (Li2S2/Li2S) multiphase reaction pathway of Li-S
batteries dictates dual regulation characteristics of redox
kinetics.[9,10] In the electrolyte with high LiPSs solubility,
while rapid liquid-phase polysulﬁde conversion enhances
sulfur
cathode
kinetics,
it
inevitably
intensiﬁes
LiPSs
diffusion toward the Li anode, triggering dual detrimental
consequences. The continuous chemical parasitic reactions

Although above studies establish a theoretical framework
for LiPSs solvation regulation, the weakened solubility of
LiPSs in the homogeneous electrolyte inevitably reduces the
conversion kinetics of the sulfur cathode, resulting in the
adverse passivation of partial sulfur site during long-term
cycling.
Emerging phase-separation electrolyte strategies over-
come conventional design constraints by creating non-
equilibrium microenvironments. Ren et al. proposed a bipha-
sic system exploiting polarity differences to induce solvent
stratiﬁcation, high-polarity tetramethylene sulfone (TMS)-
based electrolyte formed a Li+-enriched layer at the anode
for optimized deposition kinetics, while low-polarity dibutyl
ether (DBE) with polymeric ion conductors constructed
polysulﬁde-blocking barriers at the cathode.[23] Despite syn-
ergistic interfacial stabilization, complex gradient fabrication
(requiring preformed gel layers and precise phase-interface
control) increased manufacturing costs. Recently, Kang et al.’s
self-phase-separating electrolyte (SPSE) in aqueous zinc bat-
teries offers a new paradigm, water/non-aqueous spontaneous
separation via solvent polarity regulation enables hydrophilic
phases ensuring fast cathode kinetics and hydrophobic
phases forming ﬂuorinated interphases suppressing hydro-
gen evolution/dendrites.[24] This thermodynamics-driven self-
organization simpliﬁes preparation while achieving adaptive
electrode-kinetics matching via phase-boundary modulation,
providing valuable insights for Li-S systems. However, there
are few reports on the application of SPSE in Li-S batteries.
It is of great signiﬁcance to conduct an in-depth analysis of
its phase separation behavior and mechanism in real-time
battery operation, and to promote its application in practical
Li-S pouch batteries under harsh conditions.
Herein, we propose a dynamic self-phase-separating elec-
trolyte, with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and cyclopentyl
methyl ether (CPME) co-solvents, exploiting solvation dis-
parity to counter LiPSs shuttle effects in conventional ether
electrolytes. Unlike homogeneous dissolution and shuttling
of LiPSs in conventional ether-based electrolyte (Figure 1a),
the CPME/DME system utilizes sterically hindered weakly
solvating CPME and strongly solvating DME to induce
spontaneous phase separation via differential LiPSs solubility
(Figure 1b). During discharging process, DME preferentially
coordinates LiPSs forming cathode-localized redox-active
zones ensuring efﬁcient sulfur conversion, while CPME
migrates to the Li anode interface forming in situ protective
layers with dual physical-blocking and interfacial-stabilization
functions. Li-S batteries with CPME/DME electrolyte exhibit
exceptional performance, single-layer pouch cells (S load-
ing = 4.3 mgs cm−2, 50 µm Li, E/S = 6 µL mgs−1) deliver
851.7 mA h g−1 after 170 cycles. Even under harsh conditions
(S loading = 6.2 mgs cm−2, 50 µm Li, E/S = 3 µL mgs−1), 1.8
Ah multi-layer pouch cells achieve 323 Wh kg−1 initial energy
density and steady cycling above 50 cycles.
Results and Discussion
In conventional 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/DME (1:1, v/v) sol-
vents, the system maintains a single liquid phase before
and after saturated Li2S8 dissolution (Figure 1c,d), indicating
homogeneous LiPSs solvation characteristics. In contrast,
within the CPME/DME (1:1, v/v) solvents, steric hindrance
from cyclopentyl groups substantially weakens the oxygen
coordination capability of CPME molecules, reducing overall
solvation power.[25] Notably, although CPME introduction
was expected to decrease polysulﬁde solubility, experimental
observations reveal no signiﬁcant solubility reduction post
Li2S8 dissolution. Instead, spontaneous liquid-liquid phase
separation occurs, forming a transparent light-red up phase
and brick-red down phase (Figure 1e,f), with identical phe-
nomena observed for Li2S6 and Li2S4 dissolution (Figure S1).
In order to ﬁgure out the reasons for the aforemen-
tioned phenomenon, dissolution characteristics of Li2S8 in
DME, DOL, CPME, and their mixtures were systematically
evaluated (Figure 1g). The solubility of S was detected by
inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry
(ICP-OES), demonstrating that DME exhibits highest sulfur
solubility (7.88 mol L−1), followed by the down phase of
CPME/DME (3.42 mol L−1), DOL/DME (2.17 mol L−1),
DOL (0.51 mol L−1), while the up phase of CPME/DME
(0.26 mol L−1) and pure CPME (0.16 mol L−1) show signiﬁ-
cantly lower solubility. Concurrently, the densities of solutions
with saturated Li2S8 rank as DOL (1.21 g cm−3) > DOL/DME
(1.20 g cm−3) > DME (1.15 g cm−3), with down phase
(1.09 g cm−3) and up phase (0.89 g cm−3) of CPME/DME
approaching pure DME (1.15 g cm−3) and pure CPME (0.88 g
cm−3), respectively. Cross-comparison conﬁrms the sulfur
content (3.42 mol L−1) and density (1.09 g cm−3) of down
phase of CPME/DME align with pure DME, while these

superior rate capability in Li-S batteries compared to
DOL/DME electrolyte (Figures 3a and S7), delivering

86.9% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 0.2C with 99.9%

system shows only 66.8% retention and 98.1% Coulombic
efﬁciency under identical conditions. Initial discharge curves
post-activation (Figure S9) show that despite similar capacity
utilization at the ﬁrst plateau, rapid polarization at the
second discharge plateau in DOL/DME system results in
signiﬁcantly lower capacity than CPME/DME system. This
may be attributed to Li2S nucleation kinetics, quantiﬁed by
the potential gap between nucleation onset and discharge
plateau.[29] This gap measured 32.8 mV in DOL/DME system
versus only 16.6 mV in CPME/DME system, demonstrating
that the dynamic phase-separation structure effectively
enhances polysulﬁde conversion kinetics. This optimization
originates from regulation of LiPSs redox pathways by
the cathode-side DME-enriched phase, promoting rapid
Li2S
nucleation
deposition.
Electrochemical
impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) before and after cycling (Figure 3c) also
conﬁrmed that the CPME/DME system exhibits lower total
impedance, correlating with its formation of more uniform
and conductive SEI layer, along with low viscosity and high
ionic conductivity after LiPSs dissolution (Figure S10).
At a high charge/discharge rate of 0.5C, Li-S coin cells with
CPME/DME electrolyte still demonstrate excellent cycling
stability (Figure 3d), achieving 76.7% capacity retention
after 400 cycles. Under harsh conditions in Li-S coin cells
(50 µm ultrathin Li anode, 7 µL mgs−1 lean electrolyte,
4.3 mgs cm−2 high-loading sulfur cathode), the CPME/DME
electrolyte still exhibits superior electrochemical performance
(Figure 3e and Figure S11), delivering a high discharge
capacity of 1152.4 mA h g−1 during initial 0.05C activation.
After three activation cycles, at 0.1C cycling rate, discharge
capacity reaches 848.6 mA h g−1, maintaining 832.0 mA h
g−1 after 100 cycles (98.0% retention). With conventional
DOL/DME electrolyte, initial discharge capacity at 0.1C
was only 799.9 mA h g−1, decreasing to 532.7 mA h g−1
after 100 cycles (66.6% retention), signiﬁcantly lower than
CPME/DME system. Charge/discharge curves at the 1st
and 100th cycles (Figure 3f) indicate that the DOL/DME
system exhibits rapidly increasing polarization in discharge
proﬁles, signifying the consumption of active substances and
the deterioration of the electrode interphase. In contrast,
Li-S batteries with CPME/DME electrolyte demonstrate
slower polarization growth rate, suggesting reduced parasitic
reactions.[30] In CPME/DME system, comparative cycling
performance and charge-discharge curves further reveal
slightly increased polarization, reduced Coulombic efﬁciency
and capacity under high sulfur loading (4.1 mg cm−2 versus
1.1 mg cm−2) and thin Li (50 µm versus 400 µm) after
equivalent cycling (Figure S12), indicating the feasibility
of this electrolyte in practical Li-S batteries under harsh
conditions.
To clarify the suppression of parasitic reactions between
LiPSs and Li anodes by CPME/DME electrolyte, shuttle
currents were ﬁrst measured in Li-S batteries. To amplify
the shuttle effect, constant potentials were applied to cells
with LiNO3-free electrolytes[31] (Figure S13). It is found that
the DOL/DME system exhibits a pronounced shuttle current
peak at 2.4 V (versus Li+/Li) (0.26 mA cm−2), indicating
LiPSs reduction at the anode. In contrast, CPME/DME
system maintains peak current densities below 0.05 mA cm−2
throughout the potential window, signiﬁcantly lower than that
of DOL/DME system. This conﬁrms that the CPME phase
effectively blocks LiPSs diffusion to the anode via physical
barrier effects, substantially weakening parasitic reaction
kinetics.
Interfacial morphology characterization of cycled Li
anodes (50 cycles) reveals parasitic reaction differences
between electrolytes (Figure 4a). Optical images show the
CPME/DME electrolyte forms a dense, uniform SEI layer
on Li metal surface without structural defects, whereas
DOL/DME electrolyte exhibits extensive SEI cracking and
delamination, exposing underlying pulverized Li metal.

The phase separation behavior and mechanism of the
electrolyte in the Li-S battery in real time were observed by
in situ Li-S visible cell and in situ Raman spectroscopy. First,
in situ Li-S visible cells demonstrated the existence of the
phase separation phenomenon and its inhibitory effect on the
shuttle effect of LiPSs as well as the corrosion of the Li anode.
The initial discharge curve of Li-S cell with CPME/DME
electrolyte was shown in Figure 5a, four typical time points
were selected to observe the phase separation behavior of
the electrolyte under different LiPSs conditions. As shown
in Figure 5b, in the presence of long-chain LiPSs, the color
of the electrolyte in S/C cathode side turns yellow due to
the dissolution of LiPSs (stage A). The phase separation
leads to a distinct distribution of LiPSs. Speciﬁcally, a dark
brown phase rich in LiPSs has accumulated near the S/C
cathode side, while on the Li anode side, a light yellow phase
with less LiPSs has formed (stage B). As the subsequent
discharge progresses, the phase separation phenomenon of
the electrolyte weakens, and the colour also becomes lighter
as the long-chain LiPSs transform into solid-state short-
chain LiPSs (stages C and D). In contrast, the Li-S cell
using DOL/DME electrolyte maintained a uniform colour
throughout the discharge process and no phase separation
was observed (Figure 5c). Meanwhile, on the Li metal anode
side, due to the presence of phase separation behavior in the
CPME/DME electrolyte, less change in colour was observed
compared to the DOL/DME electrolyte, which proved the
inhibition of LiPSs shuttle. By comparing the state of the Li
metal electrode after discharge, it was found that the Li metal
in the DOL/DME electrolyte turned signiﬁcantly black, while
in the CPME/DME electrolyte, the Li metal still maintained
its metallic luster. This further proves the beneﬁcial effect of
the CPME/DME electrolyte in inhibiting the corrosion of the
Li metal electrode.
However, due to the relatively large E/S ratio and the
considerable distance between the positive and negative
electrodes in the aforementioned H-type battery, the phase
separation behavior of the electrolyte may be subject to
certain disturbances. Therefore, further application of in situ
Raman spectroscopy can provide a more accurate analysis of
the microscopic structural changes of the electrolyte caused
by phase separation. As shown in Figure 5d, in situ Raman
spectra of the CPME/DME electrolyte near the S/C cathode
side were collected at various discharge states. During the
initial stage of battery discharge, the characteristic peaks of
CPME solvent near the S/C cathode gradually disappeared,
while the typical Raman peaks of DME coordinated with
LiPSs strengthened.[14] This indicates that DME aggregated
near the cathode while CPME was repelled, suggesting
the occurrence of electrolyte phase separation induced by
the generation of long-chain soluble LiPSs. As the battery
continues to discharge, the soluble long-chain LiPSs gradually
deposited into short-chain LIPSs. The Raman signal of the
coordinated DME on the cathode side weakens, while the
Raman signal of CPME reappears and gradually strengthens.
This indicates that during the depth discharge, the decrease in
soluble LiPSs concentration allows the electrolyte to restore
its initial homogeneous state. Furthermore, the characteristic
Raman peaks of TFSI−also demonstrated the dynamic pro-
cess of electrolyte phase separation.[34] The weak solvation
effect of CPME in the homogeneous phase facilitated the
coordination between TFSI−and Li+. After phase separation
occurred, the reduction of CPME promoted the coordination
between DME and Li+, thereby releasing more free TFSI−.
Therefore, in our opinion, the reasons for the formation
of the separated phases in this work mainly stem from
the difference in solubility of LiPSs in the two different
solvents, which leads to differences in polarity and density
of the mixed system, and subsequently results in the phase
separation phenomenon. Furthermore, additional thermody-
namic driving forces, such as electric ﬁelds and chemical
potential gradients, collectively contribute to the observed
phase separation process.

Conclusion
This study addresses the intrinsic conﬂict between sulfur
cathode kinetics and LiPSs shuttling in Li-S batteries by inno-
vatively proposing a dynamic phase-separation electrolyte
system regulated by solvation disparity. Constructing a binary
solvent of CPME and DME leverages heterogeneous LiPSs
dissolution to achieve spatial decoupling of cathode/anode
interfacial microenvironments. At the sulfur cathode, the
strongly solvating DME-enriched phase maintains efﬁcient
sulfur redox kinetics. At the Li metal anode, the weakly
solvating CPME phase suppresses LiPSs diffusion via physical
blocking while inducing inorganic-rich SEI formation, sig-
niﬁcantly enhancing Li deposition uniformity and interfacial
stability. Therefore, the Li-S batteries with CPME/DME
electrolyte demonstrate exceptional performance even under
practical conditions. Single-layer Li-S pouch cells (50 µm Li,
4.3 mgs cm−2 high S loading, 6 µL mgs−1 lean electrolyte)
show stable cycling performance over 170 cycles. Multi-layer
1.8 Ah Li-S pouch cells (50 µm Li, 6.2 mgs cm−2 high S
loading, 3 µL mgs−1 lean electrolyte) achieve 323 Wh kg−1
initial gravimetric energy density and sustain 50 stable cycles.
By spatially reconstructing solvation microenvironments, this
work overcomes the mutual exclusion of sulfur kinetics and Li
