Summary
A 1D model is developed for the Li‐S cell to predict the effect of critical cathode
design parameters—carbon‐to‐sulfur (C/S) and electrolyte‐to‐sulfur (E/S) ratios
in the cathode—on the electrochemical performance. Cell voltage at 60% depth
of discharge corresponding to the lower voltage plateau is used as a metric for
calculating the cell performance. The cathode kinetics in the lower voltage pla-
teau is defined with a single electrochemical reaction; thus, the model has a sin-
gle apparent kinetic model parameter, the cathode exchange current density (i0,
pe). The model predicts that cell voltage increases considerably with increasing
carbon content until a C/S ratio of 1 is attained, whereas the enhancement in
the cell voltage at higher ratios is less obvious. The model can capture the effect
of the C/S ratio on the cathode kinetics by expressing the electrochemically
active area in the cathode in carbon volume fraction; the C/S ratio in the
cathode does not affect i0,pe in the model. On the other hand, the electrolyte
amount has a significant impact on the kinetic model parameter such that
increasing electrolyte amount improves the cell voltage as a result of increasing
i0,pe. Therefore, in the model, i0,pe needs to be defined as a function of the
electrolyte volume fraction, which is known to have a crucial effect on reaction
kinetics.

INTRODUCTION
Li‐S batteries have received major attention as a promis-
ing candidate for energy storage systems beyond Li‐ion
batteries due to their high theoretical specific energy of
2567 Wh kg−1.1 In addition, sulfur is naturally abundant,
nontoxic, and inexpensive.1-3 Li‐S batteries typically
contain an Li‐metal anode, a porous separator, and a
sulfur‐carbon porous cathode filled with an organic elec-
trolyte. The overall reaction for the Li‐S battery with a
standard potential of U0 = 2.2 V (vs Li/Li+) is shown in
Equation 1.1,4
16Li þ S8 →8Li2S
(1)
Although Li‐S batteries are highly attractive, they
suffer from major challenges, which lead to rapid capacity
fading and limitations in cycle life. First of all, the
precipitation of the insulating sulfur and Li2S at the
cathode surface decreases the electronic conductivity,
thus the utilization of the active material.1 Second, the
polysulfide shuttle mechanism that takes place due to
the migration of the soluble polysulfides in the electrolyte
could decrease the Coulombic efficiency significantly.1,4-6

Lastly, Li metal is highly unstable; the surface area
increases significantly with cycling, which could cause
Li or electrolyte depletion in the cell.1,7-9
In Li‐S batteries, cell design has a critical effect on
both electrochemical performance and systems‐level‐
specific energy and energy density.1 The carbon‐to‐sulfur
(C/S) ratio in the cathode is one of these key cell design
parameters. Due to the insulating nature of sulfur and
Li2S, carbon should be incorporated into the cathode to
increase the surface area for e−transfer and electronic con-
ductivity, which would enhance the reaction kinetics. The
effect of the C/S ratio on the electrochemical performance
of an Li‐S battery has been studied previously in the lit-
erature.2-6 Ding et al2 stated that there is an optimum
ratio of carbon to sulfur in the cathode that maximizes
the specific capacity of the cathode. Brückner et al3 also
investigated the effect of carbon amount and showed that
60 wt% S gives the highest cathode discharge capacity.
Xu et al4 confirmed that the C/S ratio in the cathode
determines the initial discharge capacity as well as the
cell voltage.
The electrolyte‐to‐sulfur (E/S) ratio in the cathode is
another important parameter that defines the reaction
and polysulfide shuttle mechanisms through the polysul-
fide electrolyte concentration on the cathode surface.
Polysulfide electrolyte concentration controls the precipi-
tation/dissolution reactions and shuttle mechanism, and
it determines the active material utilization at the cath-
ode. There are previous studies in the literature investigat-
ing the effect of the E/S ratio in the cathode on Li‐S cell
performance.2,3,7,10-14 Ding et al2 studied the effect of elec-
trolyte amount on the initial discharge capacity of an Li‐S
cell and concluded that increasing the E/S ratio in the
cathode increases the capacity significantly. However, a
large amount of excess electrolyte decreases the energy
density at the cell and systems level. Zhang10 stated that
the E/S ratio in the cathode should be optimized for high
discharge capacity and good capacity retention and deter-
mined that 10 mL g−1 gives the best performance. Zheng
et al11 and Kim et al12 both concluded that an E/S ratio
of 20 mL g−1 leads to better Coulombic efficiency and
cycle life and higher discharge capacity. All these studies
in the literature confirm that C/S and E/S ratios in the
cathode have a critical effect on the cell and systems level
performance of an Li‐S battery.
Electrochemical modeling of the reaction and shuttle
mechanisms in the Li‐S battery has also been studied
to a great extent. Mikhaylik and Akridge15 developed a
model that focuses on the shuttle phenomena in Li‐S bat-
teries. Kumaresan et al16 introduced a comprehensive
model, including both electrochemical and precipitation
reactions as well as the transport phenomena in the elec-
trolyte. Other models based on the study of Kumaresan
et al16 have also been proposed.17-24 Neidhardt et al17
developed a 2D model framework describing the multi-
phase processes in the Li‐S cell. Fronczek and Bessler18
applied this framework to a 1D model of the Li‐S cell
and predicted the EIS response of the battery. Hofmann
et al19 also used this framework to obtain a 1D model
accounting for the polysulfide shuttle mechanism and
the precipitation of the active material at the anode.
Ghaznavi et al20-22 performed a sensitivity analysis to
identify the most important parameters affecting the cell
performance, such as the applied discharge current, pre-
cipitation and electrochemical reaction kinetics, sulfur
content, and transport properties. Yoo et al23 included
the solubility and diffusivity of the polysulfides in the
electrolyte in their model to predict the cycling perfor-
mance of an Li‐S cell. Zhang et al24 provided a 1D model
to demonstrate the transport‐limitation mechanism of the
Li‐S cell. Risse et al25 introduced a linear 4‐state model
predicting the capacity fade of an Li‐S battery. Zhang
et al26 accounted for the concentration dependence of
the electrolyte conductivity in their mechanistic model.
Ren et al27 incorporated the Li2S precipitation kinetics
into their model. Al‐Mahmoud et al28 described the self‐
discharge of an Li‐S cell via a simple, experiment‐based
model. Marinescu et al29 developed a zero‐dimensional
model based on the electrochemical reactions proposed
by Mikhaylik and Akridge15 to describe the voltage
response of an Li‐S cell during discharge/charge. Finally,
Thangavel et al30 presented a comprehensive Li‐S dis-
charge model describing the cathode, which is composed
of mesoporous carbon particles with sulfur impregnated
in‐between pores, in multiscale. Their model considers
the complicated multistep cathode kinetics, as well as
the precipitation of Li2S.30 To conclude, comprehensive
electrochemical models describing the detailed reaction
and shuttle mechanisms in the Li‐S cell have been
reported extensively, as summarized above. However,
only a few of these studies focus on modeling the effect
of key cell design parameters, such as the C/S and E/S
ratios in the cathode on the electrochemical performance
of an Li‐S cell.
In this study, a 1D electrochemical model has been
developed to predict the effect of C/S and E/S ratios in
the cathode on the Li‐S cell performance. As is known,
complicated physical‐based models for the Li‐S cell
require a number of assigned model parameters. How-
ever, in our model, the cathode kinetics in each of the
two voltage plateaus (high or low discharge plateau) is
described with a single electrochemical reaction; thus,
the model contains a single apparent kinetic parameter
in the cathode for each discharge region. Importantly, this
novel feature of our model allows a direct comparison of
the model with the experimental data. For instance, the

MODEL DESCRIPTION
In this study, a 1D concentration‐independent electro-
chemical model based on a previous model1,31 is proposed
to investigate the effect of key cathode design parameters
—C/S and E/S ratios in the cathode—on the electrochem-
ical performance of an Li‐S cell. The model is developed
for a constant‐current, isothermal discharge of an Li‐S cell
composed of an Li‐metal anode, a porous separator, and a
porous cathode containing carbon, sulfur, binder, and an
organic
electrolyte
(Figure
1).
As
is
known,
the
electrolyte, binder, and carbon type have a considerable
effect on the electrochemical performance of the Li‐S cell.
However, in the scope of this work, the effect of the
binder, carbon, and electrolyte type has not been investi-
gated. The properties for the most typical ones have
been used and assumed constant throughout the study.
Physicochemical properties of the electrolyte are taken
for 1 M LiTFSI and 2 wt% LiNO3 in DOL:DME
(1:1 vol%), neglecting the variation in the physicochemi-
cal properties of the electrolyte with changing polysulfide
electrolyte concentration.1 For the binder in the cathode,
polyvinylidene difluoride at a constant concentration of
10 wt% is used.1 The BET surface area and density for
the carbon are taken for the super P carbon.1
In the development of the model, concentration gradi-
ents in the cell have been neglected. This assumption is
based on the previous modeling studies discussing that
at low current densities, concentration profiles do not
show significant gradients across the thickness of the cell,
especially at the discharge depths corresponding to the
lower voltage plateau.16,21 Therefore, the inhomogeneity
within the cell resulting from the accumulation of Li2S,
clogging of the pores, or high variations of the polysulfide
concentration in the electrolyte that are typically reported
at high discharge rates or near the end of discharge20,30
have been disregarded. Another major simplification in
the model is that each of the two discharge plateaus is
dominated by a single electrochemical reaction.15,29 This
model assumes symmetric discharge and charge reactions
and does not account for the polysulfide shuttle mecha-
nism. The current‐voltage relationships for the anode,
separator, and cathode are considered separately in the
electrochemical model, as described below.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Due to the complexity of Li‐S chemistry, cell design has a
crucial impact on the electrochemical performance of the
battery. The C/S ratio and electrolyte amount in the cath-
ode are two of those critical design parameters that have a
significant effect on both cell‐ and systems‐level perfor-
mance. In the proposed model, the complicated sulfur
redox mechanism at the cathode is simplified into a 2‐step
reaction chain composed of a single electrochemical reac-
tion in each of the two discharge plateaus, as previously
discussed. Thus, the cathode kinetics in each discharge
region is defined with a single apparent kinetic model
parameter, cathode exchange current density (i0,pe) that
can be estimated through a direct comparison with the
experimentally measured impedances. To study the effect
of carbon and electrolyte amounts in the cathode on the
electrochemical performance of the Li‐S cell by using this
model, the cathode exchange current density is deter-
mined as a function of DOD and cycle number, as
described below.
The exchange current density at the cathode is calcu-
lated comparing the total ASI of the Li‐S cell predicted
by the model with the cell resistances measured experi-
mentally by using the EIS method in the literature.38-40
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is one of the
most useful characterization techniques that clarify the
complicated electrochemical processes in the battery dur-
ing charge and discharge. The electrochemical behavior of
an Li‐S battery has been previously investigated by using
EIS technique in the literature.38-43
In Figure 2, the effect of DOD on the cathode kinetics
can be seen by means of the model calculations for the
cathode exchange current density for different discharge
depths. It is apparent from the figure that i0,pe increases
with increasing DOD to 60% and starts to decrease at fur-
ther DOD. Mechanistic studies in the literature suggest
that at the early stages of discharge first sulfur dissolves
into the electrolyte. Then soluble polysulfides in the elec-
trolyte are reduced via the multistep electrochemical
reactions. Finally, precipitation of Li2S becomes predomi-
nant toward the end of discharge.38,39,41 Because both S
and Li2S are insulating solids, accumulation of these on
the cathode surface results in poor electronic conductivity
and limited electrochemically active surface area. Previ-
ous EIS studies show that there is a significant increase
in the resistances associated with the formation and
growth of Li2S film toward the end of discharge.38,39
Therefore, it may be suggested that cathode kinetics are
enhanced up to a certain DOD as the charge transfer resis-
tance at the cathode decreases as a result of S dissolution,
leading to higher conductivity and thus better accessibil-
ity of the active material. However, cathode kinetics is
worsened as the accumulation of Li2S becomes significant
toward the end of discharge. Because in this model, an
apparent kinetic parameter is proposed for the cathode
kinetics, the trend in Figure 2 can be simply explained
with the previous discussion. In fact, the kinetic parame-
ter proposed in the model accounts not only for the
rate of charge transfer but also for the S dissolution and
Li2S deposition that reaches a maximum toward the
half‐discharge. Furthermore, the cathode exchange cur-
rent densities are estimated ranging from 3 × 10−7 to
6.3 × 10−7 A cm−2 as shown in Figure 2, which are com-
parable with the previous studies in the literature.1,31
Figure 3 presents the change of i0,pe as a function of
cycle number projected by the model by using experimen-
tal EIS data.40 The figure shows a significant decrease in
the cathode exchange current density within the first
few cycles. Similar to the discussion above, this falling
may be attributed to the irreversible accumulation of
Li2S on the cathode surface. The EIS results show that
there is a noteworthy increase in the Li2S film resistance
with increased cycling.38 In fact, the predominant reason
of the capacity fade of a Li‐S battery with cycling has been

3.1 | Effect of carbon‐to‐sulfur ratio in the
cathode
Because of the insulating nature of S, high C/S ratios are
typically required in the Li‐S cathode. Higher carbon
amounts lead to an enhancement in both electronic
conductivity and the electrochemically active surface
area in the cathode. As a result, utilization of the active
material and thus the useable specific capacity of the cell
increase significantly. However, a recent technoeconomic
analysis of the Li‐S battery shows that there is an opti-
mum C/S ratio in the cathode at which the energy den-
sity is maximized and the cost is minimized at the
systems level.1 Increasing the C/S ratio up to this opti-
mum level enhances the useable specific capacity, thus
the systems‐level energy density. However, beyond this
point, the cathode is not kinetically limited anymore
and increasing the amount of carbon, which is an inac-
tive material in the cell, ends up decreasing the sys-
tems‐level energy density. Consequently, the C/S ratio
is one of the most critical cathode design parameters that
have a vital effect on both electrochemical performance
of the cell and the systems‐level performance of the
battery. Therefore, the effect of the C/S ratio in the cath-
ode on the cathode kinetics is investigated by using the
developed model and presented in Figure 4 for different
current densities.
In Figure 4, the change of cell voltage of the Li‐S cell
as a function of C/S ratio can be seen for current densities
of 0.1 to 1 mA cm−2. Electrochemical performance of the
cells is compared through the calculated cell voltages at
60% DOD corresponding to the second plateau voltage of
the Li‐S battery. It is evident from the figure that below
C/S ratios of 1, there is a significant increase in the cell
voltage with increasing carbon amount. However, at
higher C/S ratios, the improvement of the cathode kinet-
ics is less obvious. In addition, this trend appears to be
independent of the current density applied. Cell voltage
varies less with the C/S ratio at lower current densities,
as expected. This trend of the impact of the C/S ratio in
the cathode on the cell voltage predicted by the model is
in agreement with the experimentally observed trends.4,5
In addition, the projected trend in this study is similar to
the previous modeling results.21
To validate the model, cell voltages calculated at 60%
DOD as a function of C/S ratio in the cathode are com-
pared with the experimental data in the literature
(Figure 1 in Xu et al4 and Gao and Abruña5) as shown in
Figure 5. The model is fed with the available experimental
parameters such as the current density and C/S and E/S
ratios in the cathode, and estimated cell voltages are com-
pared with the experimentally measured values at 60%
DOD that corresponds to the second plateau voltage of
the cell. It can be seen in Figure 5 that for the available
data, the model and the experimental results show a good
agreement. As mentioned previously, in the proposed
model, the electrochemically active area, a, is specified
in terms of the carbon volume fraction in the cathode
(Table 1). Therefore, it can be concluded that the effect
of C/S ratio on the cathode kinetics can be captured in
the model by the change in the reaction area in the cath-
ode without varying the kinetic model parameter i0,pe.

3.2 | Effect of electrolyte‐to‐sulfur ratio in
the cathode
Li‐S cells usually contain a large amount of excess electro-
lyte for good capacity retention and improved cyclability;
E/S ratios higher than 10 mL g−1 are typical in the litera-
ture.2,3 First, an excess of electrolyte in the cell is neces-
sary to prevent electrolyte depletion, which may happen
due to the enhanced side reaction between the lithium
metal and the electrolyte as a result of the morphology
change at the anode. An excess of electrolyte is also criti-
cal at the cathode by defining the concentration of the
polysulfides at the cathode surface. Both reaction and
shuttle mechanisms are highly dependent on the polysul-
fide concentration in the electrolyte; high concentrations
may result in enhanced polysulfide shuttle mechanism
or limited sulfur utilization. Therefore, high excess of
electrolyte is mostly required for high useable specific
capacity
and good capacity retention.
However,
as
the electrolyte amount in the cell increases, the systems‐
level energy density decreases significantly.1,2 A recent
technoeconomic
analysis
concludes
that
electrolyte‐
starved cathodes are required in Li‐S batteries for high
energy density and low cost.1 Subsequently, the E/S ratio
in the cathode is one of the most important design factors
in the Li‐S cell affecting critically both the cell‐ and sys-
tems‐level performance.
The effect of E/S ratio on the cathode kinetics is inves-
tigated by the proposed model and presented in Figure 6
for different current densities. It can be seen in the figure
that the cell voltage decreases with increasing E/S ratio
for all current densities, even though the decrease is less
obvious at lower i. As discussed, in the developed model,
electrolyte volume fraction affects the cathode kinetics
first through the electrochemically active area; increasing
porosity reduces a by decreasing the carbon volume frac-
tion at the cathode. The trend seen in the figure can be
explained by this decrease at the reaction area. Electrolyte
amount also affects the effective ionic and electronic con-
ductivities in the model through the Bruggeman's expres-
sion, but this effect is less significant.
The trend seen in Figure 6 is unexpected considering
the previous studies showing that cathode kinetics is
improved with increasing electrolyte amount.2,3,7,10-14
This is presented in Figure 7, in which cell voltages pre-
dicted at 60% DOD are plotted against the experimental
data in the literature (Figure 3 in Ding et al2) as a function
of E/S ratio in the cathode. As previously described,
experimental current density and C/S and E/S ratios in
the cathode are fed into the model, and model predictions
are compared with the experimentally measured cell volt-
ages at 60% DOD corresponding to the second plateau
voltage of the cell. It can be seen in the figure that the
experimental trend cannot be captured with the model.
This suggests that E/S ratio affects the cathode kinetics
not only through the electrochemically active area but
also through the kinetic model parameter. Because E/S
ratio is known to have a great impact on the reaction
and shuttle mechanisms, this is not unexpected. It can
be seen that when i0,pe in the model has been changed

may be stated that a better way to compare the cathode

predictive model that could capture the effect of these
parameters on the cell kinetics. In addition, a direct
comparison with the experimental data is achievable

CONCLUSIONS
C/S and E/S ratios in the cathode are critical in
determining the electrochemical performance as well
as the systems‐level energy density of a Li‐S battery. In
this study, the effect of these two key cathode design con-
siderations on the electrochemical performance of an Li‐S
cell was modeled. Each of the two discharge plateaus is
defined with a single electrochemical reaction in the cath-
ode; thus, the cathode kinetics was defined by using a sin-
gle apparent kinetic parameter in the model, cathode
exchange current density, for each discharge region. With
this simplification, a direct comparison of the model pre-
dictions with the experimental data was possible. Cathode
exchange current density was estimated matching the ASI
of the cell predicted by the model with the total cell resis-
tances measured experimentally by EIS. This analysis
showed that cathode kinetics is improved until 60%
DOD, which may be explained by an enhancement in
the electronic conductivity and surface area due to the dis-
solution of the insulating sulfur. However, i0,pe starts to
decrease at further DODs mainly because of the Li2S accu-
mulation on the cathode surface. Irreversible accumula-
tion of Li2S was also predicted to be the reason of the
significant decrease of i0,pe with cycling. The effect of the
C/S ratio in the cathode on the Li‐S cell performance
was investigated for different current densities through
the predicted cell voltage at 60% DOD, which corresponds
to the second plateau voltage. Cell voltage increased sig-
nificantly with carbon amount until a C/S ratio of 1,
mainly because of the enhanced cathode kinetics with
increasing electrochemically active surface area. The
improvement in the cell voltage was less significant at
higher C/S ratios. Validation with the experimental
results implied that the model can capture the effect of
the C/S ratio on the cathode kinetics by determining the
electrochemically active area in the cathode; the kinetic
model parameter i0,pe does not depend on this ratio. How-
ever, for the E/S ratio, the model could predict the
enhancement in the cathode kinetics with increasing elec-
trolyte amount only when the cathode exchange current
density was varied. Therefore, it was concluded that i0,pe
