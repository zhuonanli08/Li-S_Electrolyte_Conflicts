High-Temperature Formation of a Functional Film at the Cathode/
Electrolyte Interface in Lithium–Sulfur Batteries: An In Situ AFM
Study
Shuang-Yan Lang, Yang Shi, Yu-Guo Guo, Rui Wen,* and Li-Jun Wan*
Abstract: Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been attracting
wide attention for their promising high specific capacity. A
deep understanding of Li–S interfacial mechanism including
the temperature (T) effect is required to meet the demands for
battery modification and systematic study. Herein, the inter-
facial behavior during discharge/charge is investigated at high
temperature (HT) of 608C in an electrolyte based on lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl) imide (LiFSI). By in situ atomic force
microscopy (AFM), dynamic evolution of insoluble Li2S2 and
Li2S is studied at the nanoscale. An in situ formed functional
film can be directly monitored at 608C after Li2S nucleation. It
retards side reactions and facilitates interfacial redox. The
insight into the interfacial processes at HT provides direct
evidence of the existence of the film and reveals its dynamic
behavior, providing a new avenue for electrolyte design and
performance enhancement.
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are prospective candidates
for low-cost and large-scale energy storage with a high
specific capacity (1675 mAhg@1).[1] Issues inherent in the
electrochemical cell chemistry include the low conductivity of
sulfur and solid reduction products (Li2S and Li2S2), the
“shuttle effect” originating from soluble polysulfides (PS),
and large volumetric expansion, limiting practical applica-
tions of Li–S batteries.[2] Temperature (T) has impressive
effects on many of the key Li–S processes, such as PS
dissolution, ionic transport, and formation of passivation
layers on both electrodes.[3] Dual influences exist at elevated
temperature. An optimum balance is required concerning the
high ionic conductivity and severe PS shuttling.[4] To achieve
a significant breakthrough, a fundamental investigation of
reaction mechanism and properties at electrode/electrolyte
interface is needed. However, although various in situ char-
acterization techniques have been applied to study the
electrode processes,[5] the direct data on the nanoscale
structural evolution and interfacial dynamics at high temper-
ature (HT) in the Li–S battery is still largely missing.
Electrolytes
based
on lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)
imide
(LiFSI) salt have shown potential as an alternative in Li–S
batteries.[6] Recently Yushin et al. reported that enhanced
performance in Li–S cells with LiFSI-based electrolyte can be
realized at elevated temperature of 608C, whereas, severe
capacity decay at room temperature.[7] Nevertheless, details of
the direct interfacial observation and reaction mechanism
have not been revealed so far. Herein we present deep insight
into high-temperature effects on the interfacial processes at
nanoscale in Li/PS semi-liquid batteries with LiFSI-based
electrolyte provided by an in situ atomic force microscopy
(AFM) study. At a high temperature of 608C, in situ
formation of an amorphous film can be directly monitored
during discharge. This film appeared after Li2S nucleation and
coated the sediment surface afterwards. Owing to the high-
temperature decomposition of LiFSI, a LiF layer was formed
at the interface, acting as a dense network with both physical
confinement and chemical anchoring effects, which enable the
capture of soluble PS intermediates and then the film formed
upon discharge. This film formation is of benefit to constrain
sulfides from the electrolyte and limit parasitic reactions on
the Li anode, protecting the Li–S rechargeability and electro-
chemistry from electrical passivation on both electrodes.
Besides that, the entrapment of PS intermediates diminishes
the Li+ transport distance and decreases the phase separation
between lower-order and higher-order sulfides, as evidenced
by the improvement of reversibility of the oxidation process
and electrode conductivity conservation after repeated cycles.
The in situ observations elucidate intrinsic Li–S electrochem-
ical processes in the LiFSI-based electrolyte, specifically the
functional film formation at elevated temperature (608C),
giving insight into the interfacial mechanism and guidance for
optimizing the electrolyte components in Li–S batteries.

Integrating the ex situ analyses with in situ
AFM observations, schematic of interfacial Li–S
electrochemical processes is provided in Figure 4.
For the cell at room temperature, Figure 4a shows
typical HOPG basal plane with atomically flat
terraces under OCP. Reduction from Li2S8 to
soluble intermediate PS occurs at the beginning of
discharge. Li2S2 NPs deposit on decreasing the potential as
shown in Figure 4b. Li2S nucleates, grows and accumulates as
reaction proceeds (Figure 4c). A few LiF NPs generated from
LiFSI also deposit along with Li2S. Upon charge, Li2S sphere-
like products are oxidized via bottom-up dissolution and Li2S2
NPs reemerge (Figure 4d). Li2S2 cannot be decomposed
completely and increases after cycles (Figure 4e), because
the conversion of Li2S2 to soluble PS is harder than the solid–
solid conversion of Li2S to Li2S2.[15] The deposition of Li2S2
and LiF after cycles overspreads the surface and inhibits
effective charge transfer at the interface, which directly
correlates to the rapid capacity fading at room temperature in
Li–S cells with LiFSI-based electrolyte.[16] Enhanced interfa-
cial behavior can be revealed at 608C. Figure 4 f depicts the
atomically flat terraces of bare HOPG at OCP. The reaction
of Li2S2 and Li2S deposition goes faster compared with that at
room temperature as shown in Figure 4g,h. An in situ formed
amorphous film grows after Li2S nucleation (Figure 4h).
Experiments in electrolyte without Li2S8 provide clues that
the film formation cannot be linked to solvent decomposition
or DOL gelation. Besides that, dense scattered LiF NPs are
accumulated on HOPG with nano-cavities similar in size to
PS intermediates. Considering no other materials are present
in the electrolyte, we identify the amorphous film as a film of
intermediate sulfides captured by the LiF net. QC calcula-
tions probe the chemical interaction between LiF and
intermediates, which indicates Li bond formation. The
entrapment of intermediates can attenuate side reactions on
the anode, as well as PS shuttling in the Li–S systems for
practical application, assuring electrochemical processes and
high capacity during battery cycling. It is in good agreement
with the calculation results published by Yushin et al. which
propose an in situ surface coating for PS protection on the S
cathode.[7] In addition, the film accommodates intermediates
attached to the sediments on the cathode, shortening Li+
diffusion paths and further benefitting the reversible oxida-
tion of insoluble lithium sulfides on charging (Figure 4i).
However, the LiF net is broken in the meantime with sulfides
oxidation and chain growth, which in turn guarantees the
benign conductivity of the HOPG/electrolyte interface after
cycles (Figure 4j). In order to evaluate the temperature effect
properly, it should be clarified that the high-temperature

In summary, in situ AFM measurements combined with
ex situ analyses were utilized to investigate the Li–S dis-
charge/charge processes in LiFSI-based electrolyte. Direct
visualization of the functional film was realized at the
elevated temperature of 608C. At room temperature, Li2S2
NPs deposit at an early stage of discharge. Li2S sphere-like
products grow with negatively shifting the potential, these
products gradually increase and stack on HOPG. Upon
oxidation, Li2S decomposes, while some amount of insoluble
Li2S2 remains and accumulates after cycles with a little LiF
degraded from LiFSI, suppressing electrical conductivity at
the interface. Interestingly, an in situ formed film can be
detected after Li2S nucleation upon discharge at 608C. It is
concluded that the LiF NPs produced by LiFSI decomposi-
tion can form a net with both physical confinement and
chemical anchoring effects on PS intermediates, which leads
to the film formation. Moreover, this film formation at the
interface reduces Li+ transport distance between lower-order
and higher-order sulfides, facilitating Li2S and Li2S2 oxidation
upon charge and interfacial morphology maintenance after
several cycles. The high-temperature and in situ formation of
the functional film sheds a new light on issues of benign
interface conservation and performance optimization in
LiFSI-based cells. Our findings show in situ observation of
Li–S interfacial morphology evolution as well as fundamental
understanding of the reaction mechanism at both room
temperature and 608C, indicating the potential application of
the LiFSI salt and novel approaches for battery modification
in Li–S system.
