ABSTRACT: Equimolar mixtures of lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)amide
(LiTFSA) and tetraglyme (G4: CH3O−(CH2CH2O)4−CH3) yield the solvate (or
chelate) ionic liquid [Li(G4)][TFSA], which is a homogeneous transparent solution at
room temperature. Solvate ionic liquids (SILs) are currently attracting increasing
research interest, especially as new electrolytes for Li−sulfur batteries. Here, we
performed neutron total scattering experiments with 6/7Li isotopic substitution to
reveal the Li+ solvation/local structure in [Li(G4)][TFSA] SILs. The experimental
interference function and radial distribution function around Li+ agree well with
predictions from ab initio calculations and MD simulations. The model solvation/local
structure was optimized with nonlinear least-squares analysis to yield structural
parameters. The reﬁned Li+ solvation/local structure in the [Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL
shows that lithium cations are not coordinated to all ﬁve oxygen atoms of the G4
molecule (deﬁcient ﬁve-coordination) but only to four of them (actual four-
coordination). The solvate cation is thus considerably distorted, which can be ascribed to the limited phase space of the
ethylene oxide chain and competition for coordination sites from the TFSA anion.

similar systems composed of LiTFSA and glymes,8 and SAXS,
Raman and DFT calculations studies were performed on SILs

been probed using NMR diﬀusion studies, Raman spectra

the macroscopic properties of the electrolyte solutions. These
studies clariﬁed how the properties of SILs diﬀer from those of
ordinary concentrated salt solutions10−22,27,34 and developed
rules for classifying SILs as “good” or “poor”. In good SILs,
such as [Li(G4)][TFSA], the metal ion is practically
coordinated with all of the glyme contained in the SIL and
has properties that are similar to those of ordinary ionic liquids.
In a poor SIL, the anion eﬀectively competes for metal ion
coordination sites, which weaken interactions with the glyme,
and has properties that are similar to those of concentrated salt
solutions.
The results from NMR diﬀusion and Raman spectroscopic
studies explain the resistance of glymes to oxidization at SIL
electrolyte/electrode interfaces and the lower free-glyme
concentration both in the bulk electrolyte solution and at the
interface.10−18 Moreover, MD simulations20,21 and ab initio
calculations22 agree with the experimental diﬀusion coeﬃcients
and the observed Raman spectra for the SILs, respectively. On
the other hand, Li+ local structures in aprotic ionic liquids have
been elucidated by Raman and X-ray scattering techniques with
the aid of theoretical calculations.23−26 Very recently, Atkin’s
group performed neutron scattering experiments with the
empirical potential and structure reﬁnement (EPSR) analysis to
elucidate the bulk nanostructure of the good and poor SILs
[Li(G4)][TFSA] and [Li(G4)][NO3].27 However, the Li+ local
structure in the SILs is still needed from an experimental
perspective as this structure is critical for the Li+ ionic
conduction mechanism in the bulk and the reduction/
oxidization mechanisms at the SIL electrolyte/electrode
interface. Although Raman spectroscopic studies and MD
simulations have been often used to clarify the Li+ solvation in
solutions,28−30 neutron scattering experiments, particularly
those with the 6/7Li isotopic substitution technique,31,32 are
some of the most powerful tools and have successfully revealed
Li+ solvation structures in aqueous and nonaqueous solvent
solutions.33
Here the Li+ local structure in the [Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL is
determined using neutron total scattering experiments with
6/7Li isotopic substitution. Usually, hydrogeneous (nondeu-
terated) samples where a part of the system (usually the solute
or solvent) is not deuterated are generally unsuitable for
neutron measurements. Here we demonstrate that
6/7Li
isotopic substitution is useful even for samples employing
hydrogenous G4.

The radial distribution function GLi(r) obtained by Fourier
transformation of the ΔLi(Q) results is shown in Figure 3. The
ﬁrst and the second peaks of GLi(r) can be assigned to the
positions of atoms close to the Li+ cations. Such close
neighbors can be unambiguously found at about 2 and 3 Å,
respectively. It should also be noted that the broad valley found
in the GLi(r) function at around 3.5 Å can be ascribed to the
solvated G4 hydrogen atoms of negative neutron scattering
length. The presence of the valley suggests that most of the Li+
in the SIL is solvated by G4 molecules, which is consistent with
the previous NMR and Raman studies.10−18,34
Before proceeding with a quantitative structural analysis, the
observed neutron data are compared with theoretical
calculations. Figure S1 in the Supporting Information shows
optimized geometries and the corresponding stabilization
energies for the Li+−G4 complexes in the isolated gas phase
at the MP2/6-311G(d,p)//HF/6-31G(d) level of theory from
ref 22. The radial distribution functions for all 31 geometries
were evaluated and are depicted in Figure S2 (Supporting
Information). The ﬁgure shows that the radial distribution
functions based on more stable structures are in better
accordance with the observed results. Recently, we reported
the Li+ local structure in a HFE (1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-
tetraﬂuoropropyl ether)-diluted [Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL by means
of Raman and HEXTS (high-energy X-ray total scattering)
experiments with the aid of MD simulations.34 Figure S3 in the
Supporting Information compares the MD-derived interference
functions with those from the neutron and X-ray experiments.
This ﬁgure reveals good agreement between the three sets of
results. In the MD simulations, the Li+ cations exhibited, on
average, ﬁve-coordinated structures: 4 and 1 coordinations by
oxygen atoms from the G4 molecule or the TFSA anion,
respectively.
Finally, we performed quantitative analysis of the neutron
scattering data based on a local structure model. In general,
most solvent molecules, including water, have only one
coordination site and are modeled as monodentate solvents.
In the nonlinear least-squares ﬁtting analysis, the possible
adjustable parameters are the Li+−X bond length (X: a
coordination atom), the coordination number, a thermal factor
(Debye−Waller), Li+−X−Y bond angles (Y: an atom next to
the X), and Li+−X−Y−Z torsion angles (Z: an atom next to the
Y).31 Recently, for more-ﬂexible dimethyl carbonate, intra-
molecular torsion angles were employed as reﬁnement
parameters to yield adequate structural analysis, and solvent
molecules could change their intramolecular structure upon
coordinating to the central metal ion.32 In the case of the G4
molecule, ﬁve oxygen atoms can potentially coordinate to the
Li+ ion (although in solution the Li+ prefers a four-atom-
coordinated structure). Therefore, the ﬁtting parameters
considered here were the Li+−G4 coordination number, the
Li+−O bond lengths, their thermal factors, the Li+−O−C bond
angles, and 12 signiﬁcant torsion angles along the backbone of
the G4 molecule and ∠C3O2C1Li+. Details of the least-squares
ﬁtting data analysis are described in the Supporting
Information. The results obtained are listed in Table 1,
accompanied by data for the second-sphere Li+−TFSA
correlation and the long-range term. The GLi(r) function
calculated based on the obtained structural parameters of the
model structure is shown in Figure 3 as a solid line. As shown
in the ﬁgure, the model agrees with the experimental results. An
atomistic depiction of the model structure is shown in Figure 4.
Li+ solvation structures are well established for aqueous
solutions.33 In monodentate solvent solutions, Li+ prefers four-
coordinated structures, although coordination numbers can
increase up to six depending on the salt concentration, the
counterion, and the solvent. Therefore, it is worth comparing
the structural parameters obtained in this work with those
reported for ordinary solvent solutions. A coordination number
of 0.95 was obtained for the Li+−G4 solvate complex, which is
in good agreement with the Raman spectroscopic results,11

An optimized bond length of 2.06 Å for the Li+−O2 distance
in [Li(G4)][TFSA] liquid is shown in Table 1, but the ﬁve
oxygen atoms in G4 have their own characteristic distances:
2.06, 2.09, 2.13, 1.93, and 2.24 Å for O2, O5, O8, O11, and
O14, respectively. Clearly, 2.24 Å is signiﬁcantly larger than the
others. The average distance of 2.05 Å (if the O14 distance is
not considered) is still signiﬁcantly larger than values of 1.95−
1.97 Å usually found in aqueous solutions. However, the
distances are similar to values for cyclic31 and acyclic32
carbonates (2.04−2.08 Å) and for the poly(ethylene oxide)
solutions (2.07−2.1 Å).35−37 Longer bond lengths in non-
aqueous media are probably due to weaker Li+−solvent
interactions, stronger contact ion pair formation in solvents
of low-dielectric and/or poor electron pair donating ability, and
steric eﬀects. In fact, contact ion pair formation has been
observed in the LiPF6 dimethyl carbonate solutions by Raman
spectroscopy.32 Small but still signiﬁcant contact ion pair
formation between Li+ and TFSA−has also been found in
[Li(G4)][TFSA] by Raman spectroscopy.6,8,14,34
The 2.24 Å Li+−O14 bond is considerably longer than other
Li+−glyme oxygen bonds. According to Shannon38 and
Bondi,39 the ionic radius for the four-coordinated Li+ and van
der Waals radius for the oxygen atom are 0.59 and 1.52 Å,
respectively. The sum of these two values, 2.11 Å, is close to the
Li+−O average distance of 2.05 Å found for the O2, O5, O8,
and O11 oxygen atoms. On the other hand, the 2.24 Å distance
is considerably larger than 2.11 Å. Our previous MD
simulations suggested that the terminal oxygen atoms never
fully coordinate to the Li+ but rather undergo successive
coordination/uncoordination stages.20,21,34 Figure 5 shows pair
correlation functions g(r) and their integral curves N(r) for the
Li+−O (G4) and the Li+−O (TFSA) in the simulated
[Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL and the corresponding potential mean
forces (PMFs). As shown in Figure 5a, the ﬁrst peak in g(r) for
the Li+−O8 (G4) is the shortest and sharpest, which suggests
that the central oxygen O8 inertly coordinates to the Li+. As the
oxygen atoms locate closer to the terminal, the ﬁrst peak in g(r)
shifts further with broadening. The smallest coordination
number of about 1.4 for O2 and O14 of the terminal oxygen
atom with the longest position and the broadest width is
consistent with the present neutron experiments. This means
that on average one of the terminal oxygen atoms in the glyme
molecules is not fully coordinated to the Li+, which supports
the longer 2.24 Å distance for the Li+−O14 predicted by the
model. This situation is revealed by the Venn diagram in Figure
9b of ref 20, where a relatively large proportion of the glyme
molecules are coordinated to Li+ by four of their oxygen atoms,
leaving one of the terminal atoms uncoordinated. PMF in
Figure 5c,d is also insightful. Though rough, the diﬀerence in
height between the nearest minimum and maximum at around
3.0−3.5 Å should be related to the activation free energy for
coordination/uncoordination of the oxygen atom. With G4, the
diﬀerence (apparent activation free energy) becomes smaller
from the central oxygen to the terminal one and is almost
identical to that of the TFSA oxygen. This indicates that the
terminal oxygen of G4 and those of TFSA are more labile than
the other oxygen atoms in G4, which also supports the
interpretation of neutron experiments.
Figure 4 shows that the coordination shell of the Li+ ion in
[Li(G4)][TFSA] has a rather distorted structure, which agrees
with structures optimized by ab initio calculation22 and MD
simulation.20,21,34 Such a distorted structure can be ascribed to
the constrained conﬁgurational phase space experienced by the
oxygen donor atoms of the glyme molecules, which is limited
by the intercalated ethyl moieties. The distortion can also be
caused by the competition of the TFSA anion for one of the
coordination sites (as also shown in previous MD stud-
ies).20,21,34 Recently, highly concentrated electrolyte solutions
have been studied where the number of solvent molecules is
insuﬃcient to solvate the Li+ with a coordination number of
4.40,41 Although such highly concentrated electrolyte solutions
bear some similarities to the glyme-based SILs, the underlying
processes of solvation, ion pair formation, and oxidization/
reduction reactivity should be rather diﬀerent in chelate glyme-

The Li+ solvation/local structure in the [Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL
was studied by means of neutron total scattering experiments
using the 6/7Li isotopic substitution technique. The diﬀerence
interference function ΔLi(Q) and radial distribution function
GLi(r) around the Li+ in the SIL were successfully evaluated.
Both ΔLi(Q) and GLi(r) agree well with previous ab initio
calculations and MD simulations. The parameters for the model
structure of lithium ions coordinated by G4 molecules were
optimized with a nonlinear least-squares technique and yielded
a model for the Li+ solvation/local structure in [Li(G4)]-
[TFSA] satisfactorily. According to the model, one G4
molecule coordinates to the Li+ by four of its oxygen atoms
with an average Li+−O bond length of 2.05 Å, while one of its
terminal oxygen atoms remains at a larger distance (2.24 Å).
This can be related to the more labile ligand character of the
terminal oxygen atoms of the G4 molecule. Moreover, the Li+
solvation/local structure in [Li(G4)][TFSA] SIL is rather
distorted. Such distorted structure may be a characteristic for
the chelate systems based on glyme solvents due to the
semirigidity of the ethylene oxide chain.
