ABSTRACT: Practical lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries demand rapid
sulfur redox kinetics and high Li-metal compatibility simultaneously,
which cannot achieve this objective using current electrolyte based on
1,2-dimethoxyethane and 1,3-dioxolane. Herein, we propose a solvent
screening criterion using the dielectric constant (εr) and donor number
(DN) as a synergistic descriptor. Guided by this εr−DN screening
criterion, 75 solvents were classified as strong, midstrong, middle, weak,
and nonsolvation solvents, respectively. The combination of midstrong/
weak solvation solvents endows a distinctly gradient lithium polysulfides
(LiPSs) solvation structure, suppressing their shuttle effect and
enhancing Li-metal compatibility. This bestows the Li−S coin cell
with an extremely high areal capacity of 9.4 mAh cm−2 under a high S
mass loading (8.6 mg cm−2) and a low E/S ratio (3.6 μL mg−1). As a
proof-of-concept, a 3 Ah-level pouch cell delivers a high energy density
of 369 Wh kg−1.
L
ithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, featuring an ultrahigh
theoretical energy density (up to 2600 Wh kg−1) and low
cost, abundant S as the cathode material, are considered as the
leading candidates for next-generation energy storage systems.1,2
However, their industrialization has been persistently hindered
by critical challenges, among which electrolyte design and
optimization play a pivotal role.3 The electrolyte serves as not
only an ion-transport medium but also a core component, which
profoundly governs the dissolution, diffusion, and conversion of
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and critically impacts the stability of
electrode−electrolyte interfaces.4 Benefiting from the prior
lithium-ion battery technology, early research predominantly
employed ether-based solvents, such as 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as the baseline electrolytes.5,6
Although ether solvents exhibit high solubility for LiPSs, which
ensures favorable sulfur conversion kinetics, this property also
causes the shuttle effect of LiPSs.7,8 Dissolved LiPSs species
migrate between the cathode and anode, causing irreversible
active material rapid loss and lithium metal anode (LMA)
corrosion.9,10
Recently, various electrolyte strategies have been explored to
suppress the shuttle effect of LiPSs.11,12 For instance, employing
weak-solvating-power solvents, e.g., carbonates,13 single-oxygen
ether,14 and hydrofluorinated ether,15 results in incomplete
sulfur redox reactions and easy formation of passivation layer on
the cathode surface. Introducing ionic liquids or highly
concentrated electrolytes effectively restricted LiPSs dissolution,
however also leads to high viscosity, low ionic conductivity, and
high costs.16,17 Although these approaches have achieved
incremental progress, they often inevitably face a dilemma of
trade-offs: suppressing shuttle effect of LiPSs comes at the
expense of compromised conversion kinetics or interfacial
stability.18 Then, to achieve a high S utilization under lean
electrolyte condition, a series of Gutmann’s donor number
(DN) solvents with strong-solvating-power are proposed to
boost the solubility of LiPSs leading to a rapid conversion
kinetics based on a solid−liquid−solid S redox process.19,20
From the perspective of electrolyte engineering, a fundamental
understanding of the LiPSs’ solvation is essential for the
industrialization of Li−S batteries.
Further, in designing new electrolyte systems, the reliance on
the empirical trial-and-error approaches and the lack of
comprehensive theoretical guidance are the primarily reasons
of the puzzling relationship between screening solvents and the
solvation of LiPSs.21−23 Recently, several key parameters with
great potential have recently been proposed to understand this
relationship, as following: (i) The Gutmann’s DN of a solvent
dictates Li+ coordination strength: high DN solvents dissolve

imperative to bridge the fundamental gap between the
nanoscopic screening criteria and macroscopic electrochemical
performance.

and evaluated by using seven critical electrochemical perform-

voltage, cycle life of Li||Li symmetric cells, LiPSs shuttle current,
Li2S nucleation overpotential and deposition capacity, and
average capacity and average CE of Li−S coin cells. The
electrolyte formula combining midstrong solvation (2,5-
dimethyl-1,2,5-thiadiazolidin 1-oxide, CSA) and weak solvation
(2-methyltetrahydrofuran, MeTHF) solvents outperforms all

According to the Born−Haber cycle (Figure 1a), the
dissolution of a Li salt in a solvent requires a negative Gibbs
free energy change (ΔG = ΔH −TΔS < 0). This is a
thermodynamic prerequisite for forming a homogeneous and
stable electrolyte system. The dissociation of a crystalline Li salt
first involves its endothermic ionization into anions and cations,
which is a process primarily governed by the lattice energy
(ΔUlat). Then, the dissociated ions are solvated by solvent
molecules, forming a coordination structure in an exothermic
process dominated by the solvation enthalpy (ΔHsol).
Electrolytes in Li−S batteries, which typically operate via a
solid−liquid−solid conversion mechanism, usually contain
three main Li salts: lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI), LiNO3, and LiPSs (Figure 1b). Using Li2S6 as a
representative LiPSs, its dissolution and precipitation are
correlated with the solvation structure and the conversion
equilibrium. Specifically, the solvation structure of LiPSs can be
modulated by high εr solvent with enhancement of a strong
electrostatic shielding effect for ions. This leads to more free ions
with increased entropy (Figure 1c) and favors the dissociation of
LiPSs. Moreover, based on the theory of Lewis acids and bases,
the coordination environment of Li+ can be regulated with high
DN solvent, resulting in lower solvation enthalpy change (ΔHsol;
Figure 1d; further discussion is provided in Supplementary Note
2.1).29

≤εr < 100), including most carbonate solvents, shows ultralow
solubility for LiNO3.26,28 Due to the high DN value of NO3

rapid active material loss and severe side reactions at the cathode
(Figures S2−S4 and Supplementary Note S2.2).30 Region II (25
≤DN < 40 kcal mol−1 and 1 ≤εr < 20) contains polar protic
nitrogenous solvents, which may undergo deprotonation

In this work, five representative solvents were selected to
design binary solvent electrolyte formulations (Figure 2a and
Table S9): dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from Region S, 2,5-
dimethyl-1,2,5-thiadiazolidin 1-oxide (CSA) from Region M-S,
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) from Region M, 2-methyltetrahy-
drofuran (MeTHF) from Region W, and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluor-
oethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) from Region N.
CSA was synthesized via a nucleophilic substitution reaction
(Figure S6) and confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy
(Figures S7 and S8). Density functional theory (DFT)
calculations revealed that calculated the lowest negative
electrostatic potential (i.e., ESPmin) of the five solvents follows
the strength trend: DMSO > CSA > DME > MeTHF > TTE
(Figure S9), indicating a reduced trend in solvation capability.
Further, the electrolyte was prepared using an optimal molar
ratio of LiTFSI:CSA:MeTHF = 1:4:6, with the detailed
optimization shown in Figures S10 and S11. Using this
formulation, Li||Li2S6 cell exhibits a capacity retention of 93%
after 60 cycles with high average CE of 99.4% (Figure S12). All
other formulas were prepared with an optimal molar ratio of
LiTFSI: solvent 1: solvent 2: LiNO3 = 1:4:6:0.2, as detailed in
Table S10. Due to the insolubility of LiNO3 in hydrofluoroether
solvents, LiNO3 was omitted from the TTE-containing
electrolyte formulations. Additionally, a conventional electrolyte
of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 in DME:DOL (1:1 by volume;
denoted as DD) was employed as the reference electrolyte. The
density, viscosity, and ionic conductivity of these electrolytes
were measured and are summarized in Table S11.
Li+ solvation structures in all electrolyte systems were
investigated by using Raman spectroscopy (Figure S13).
Characteristic peak shift in the binary solvent electrolyte
systems reveal a consistent solvation strength trend (DMSO >
CSA > DME > MeTHF > TTE), confirming the reliability of
solvent selection criteria. In the DMSO/CSA (S/M-S) electro-
lyte, the C−S−C stretching vibration of DMSO exhibits a
significant blue shift, indicating strong coordination between
DMSO and Li+ (Figure S13a). Meanwhile, the N−S−N
stretching vibration of CSA shows a slight blue shift, indicating
partial coordination between CSA and Li+. This suggests a
competitively coordinated Li+ solvation structure in DMSO/
CSA. Similar competitive coordination was observed in CSA/
DME (M-S/M), DME/MeTHF (M/W), and DME/DOL
(DD) (Figure S13b−d). In contrast, DMSO/DME (S/M),
DMSO/MeTHF (S/W), CSA/MeTHF (M-S/W), DMSO/
TTE (S/N), CSA/TTE (M-S/N), DME/TTE (M/N), and
MeTHF/TTE (W/N) exhibit a different Li+ solvation structure
(Figures S14 and S15), which features only one coordinated
solvent and another free solvent (noncoordinated). This
confirms that, besides coordination capability of a single solvent,
the combination of two solvents is another key factor for binary
solvent electrolytes to regulate the Li+ solvation structure.
First, the LMA compatibility with each electrolyte formula
was evaluated by Li||Li symmetric cells, whose polarization
voltage could reflect the kinetics during the Li deposition and
stripping process. Both polarization voltage and cycle life of Li||
Li symmetric cells using various electrolytes were selected as the
primary two screening parameters for the binary solvent
electrolytes, which was evaluated using Li||Li symmetric cells
cycled at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2 (Figures 2b, S16, and
S17). Only the M-S/W, M/W, M/N, W/N, and DD electrolytes
simultaneously met the demands of polarization voltage below
50 mV and cycle life exceeding 800 h, demonstrating the
excellent compatibility with LMA. Scanning electron micros-
copy (SEM) images of cycled LMAs, using M-S/W, M/W, M/
N, W/N, and DD electrolytes, revealed a clear morphology-
performance correlation of small polarization voltages, long
cycle life, and smooth morphologies. In contrast, those that
induced large polarization voltages and short cycle life, using S/
M-S, S/W, S/M, S/N, M-S/M, and M-S/N electrolytes, resulted
in cracked, porous, mossy deposited morphologies. These
results can be attributed to the distinct solvent properties: (1)
DMSO with strong solvation ability accompanies intrinsic
corrosion, leading to short cycle life. (2) CSA, with midstrong
solvation ability, offers a balance between strong donor ability
and compatibility toward Li metal. (3) TTE, as a nonsolvation
solvent, contributes to low ionic conductivity and high viscosity,
which could aggravate Li dendritic growth.
Given that the multispecies and multiphase of LiPSs in Li−S
batteries, their shuttle current (SC) is adopted as the third key
screening parameter, which directly reflects the loss of active
materials induced by the corrosion reaction between LiPSs and
LMA.11 The SC of LiPSs was measured via potentiostatic tests
between 2.2 and 2.9 V (Figure S18). Previously reported work
has demonstrated that an antisolvent-derived encapsulation
solvation structure can effectively suppress the LiPS shuttle.32,33
In this work, only S/N, M-S/W, M-S/N, M/N, and W/N
electrolytes show an average SC below 10 μA cm−2 based on Li||
Li2S6 coin cells (Figure 2c).
Thanks to the solid−liquid−solid conversion mechanism in
the S cathode, both nucleation overpotential (ΔVnuc) and
deposition capacity of lithium sulfide (Li2S) are critical rate-
determining steps,34 representing the energy barriers for Li2S
nucleation and growth. Consequently, both ΔVnuc and Li2S
deposition capacities from Li−S coin cells were used as the
following two key screening parameters for these electrolytes. As
shown in Figures 2d, S19, and S20, only S/M, M-S/M, and M-S/
W simultaneously met the demand of ΔVnuc below 30 mV and a
Li2S deposition capacity exceeding 600 mAh g−1. This implies
that enhanced solvation capability effectively accelerates the
conversion kinetics of sulfur species. Moreover, average
discharge capacity and average CE over 100 cycles were
proposed as the last two key parameters for assessing
practicality. As shown in Figures 2e, S19, and S20, only the
M-S/W electrolyte achieved an average discharge capacity
exceeding 900 mAh g−1 and an average CE of 99.9% over 100
cycles at 0.2 C, indicating high S utilization and excellent cycling
stability.

The conventional DD electrolyte, with moderate LiPSs
solubility, exhibits a typical solid−liquid−solid conversion
process (Figure S21). This process prior forms a two-
dimensional (2D) Li2S deposition morphology, leading to low
S utilization and significant corrosion with LMA.35 Coupling a
strong solvating solvent with LiPSs could regulate the LiPSs
solvation structure, promote a three-dimensional (3D) Li2S
deposition morphology, and provide an additional conversion
pathway. However, this approach often exacerbates LiPSs
corrosion with LMA.36 Conversely, employing a solvent with a
low LiPSs solubility can create a sparingly solvated structure that
mitigates LMA corrosion. Yet, this strategy also results in 2D
Li2S deposition and sluggish conversion kinetics.37
Given the critical role of the LiPSs solvation structure, we
further explored the solvent−solvent interactions in the M-S/W
electrolyte to elucidate the underlying structure−property
relationships in this binary solvent electrolyte. The solubility
of LiPSs was investigated in Figure S22. Ten mM Li2Sx (x = 2, 4,
6, 8) in CSA:MeTHF (4:6 by molar) exhibits uniform a dark-
brown color, indicating high LiPSs dissolubility. In contrast, 10
mM Li2Sx in DME:DOL (1:1 by vol.) shows visible precipitate
(the dotted box), indicating poor dissociation and low
dissolubility of polysulfides (Sx
2−) species. The ionic con-
ductivity of 0.1 M Li2Sx in CSA:MeTHF is higher than that in
DME:DOL (Figure S23). This enhanced Li+ solvation and
promoted Li2Sx dissociation could be attributable to CSA with
high εr and DN. The sulfur atomic concentration ([S]) in a
saturated Li2S6/CSA solution reaches approximately 18 mol L−1,
which is significantly higher than that in DME (9 mol L−1;
Figure S24). Conversely, [S] in a saturated Li2S6/MeTHF
solution is only 0.6 mol L−1, lower than that in DOL (1.0 mol
L−1).
17O NMR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the
LiPSs solvation structure (Figure 3a−d). The 17O chemical shift
of 0.1 M Li2S6 in CSA exhibits a significant upfield shift of −0.16
ppm, whereas that of 0.1 M Li2S6 in DME shows a smaller
upfield shift (−0.11 ppm). This difference confirms a stronger
coordination between CSA and Li+ than with DME, resulting in
a CSA-dominated Li2S6 solvation structure. The 17O chemical
shifts of 0.1 M Li2S6 in DOL and MeTHF exhibit slight upfield
shifts (−0.03 and −0.01 ppm, respectively), indicating weak
coordination with Li+. This suggests that MeTHF is mostly
located in the outer shell of Li2S6 solvation structure, forming a
gradient distribution of mixed solvents. 0.1 M Li2S6 in the mixed
solvents (CSA:MeTHF and DME:DOL) were further analyzed
by comparative 7Li NMR spectroscopy (Figure 3e). The shield
effect of Li+ in CSA:MeTHF is stronger than in DME:DOL due
to the stronger coordination capability of CSA compared to
DME. This verifies the efficacy of CSA:MeTHF in regulating the
LiPSs solvation structure.

using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. To simulate the
local environment around LiPSs in Li−S batteries, 0.2 M Li2S6
was introduced into the electrolyte of 1.0 M LiTFSI in
CSA:MeTHF and DME:DOL, respectively. From the result of
radial distribution function (RDF) in Figure S25a, the S6

Furthermore, 1H−1H correlation spectroscopy (COSY) was
conducted to elucidate the solvent−solvent interaction in the
LiPSs solvation structure. In the COSY spectra (Figure 3f,g),
both the abscissa and ordinate represent the 1H NMR chemical

1H−1H
coupling between protons, which occurs through chemical

The solid−liquid−solid conversion in Li−S batteries involves
a unique multistep, multielectron, multispecies, and multiphase
(termed as “4M”) conversion redox process. Within this process,
Li2S deposition behavior and its conversion kinetics are critical
for achieving a high S utilization and long-term cycling stability.
To investigate this, Li2S deposition and dissolution experiments
were conducted using both electrolytes, with the results shown
in Figures S28−S32. These results confirm that the primary
inner solvation sheath of the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte enables
the formation of a three-dimensional (3D) Li2S deposit.
Additionally, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests at various scan
rates demonstrate that CSA/MeTHF electrolyte with a gradient
LiPSs solvation structure strongly facilitates rapid LiPSs
conversion kinetics (Figures S33−S37). Then, electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted to evaluate the
dynamic interfacial chemistry of the S cathode. Li||Li2S6 coin cell
with a S loading (MS) of 1.4 mg cm−2 was assembled and tested
at 0.1 C. As shown in Figures 4a−c, at the initial states-of-charge
(SoC) (i), high-order LiPSs dissolve in the CSA/MeTHF
electrolyte, leading to a smooth surface of CF with low
impedance. At SoC (ii), where high-order LiPSs transform
into low-order species, tiny Li2S particles begin to gradually
electrodeposit on the CF surface with a slightly increased
impedance. The Li2S particle becomes nonuniform at SoC (iii),
showing higher impedance than that at SoC (ii). At SoC (iv), an
island-like Li2S morphology forms. The abundant triple-phase
interface among Li2S, the CF substrate, and the electrolyte
facilitates efficient Li+ transport and diffusion. During the
discharge process, the Li2S particles become small with low
electrical impedances at the SoC (v), resulting from the
oxidation of Li2S to LiPSs. At SoC (vi), Li2S is fully converted
to S, resulting in a smooth film with white insulating regions on
the CF surface. This morphology indicates a highly reversible
conversion process. From the EIS result in Figures S38a,b,
increased impedances are remarkably observed at SoCs iii, iv,
and v using DD electrolyte. This is mainly attributed to the
limited LiPSs solubility in DD electrolyte, which induces 2D
growth of Li2S with a film-like morphology on the CF surface
(Figure S38c). Further, this premature and compact passivation
severely hinders subsequent LiPSs reduction reactions, resulting
in low S utilization and sluggish conversion kinetics.
Moreover, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) method
based on the EIS result was conducted to analyze the interfacial
evolution during the redox conversion reaction. As shown in
Figures S39 and S40, the DRT profiles acquired at different
stages of charge−discharge reveal seven characteristic peaks
across high to low frequency range.44 Each peak corresponds to
a distinct physical or chemical process with a specific time
scale.45 These peaks are assigned as follows: interparticle
resistance (P1), double-layer relaxation (P2), solid−electrolyte
interphase (P3), positive electrode charge transfer (P4), positive
electrode charge transfer (P5), polysulfide diffusion (P6), and
general diffusion (P7). During cycling, the interfacial evolution
using CSA/MeTHF electrolyte is primarily governed by P7 peak
(canary area), especially at SoC iv (Figure 4d). In contrast, the
cell using DD electrolytes exhibits dominance of both P6 and P7
peaks at SoCs (iii, iv, and v) in Figure 4e. In addition, XPS and
morphological analyses reveal stark differences between the two
electrolytes (Figures S41−45). The CSA/MeTHF electrolyte
enables 3D porous Li2S cathode deposition and a smooth LMA
surface enriched with protective high-valence sulfur species
(SO4
2−/ SO3
2−), demonstrating excellent LMA compatibility. In
sharp contrast, the DD electrolyte produces a compact 2D Li2S
film on the cathode and a mossy LMA surface dominated by
corrosive low-valence sulfur species (Li2S, Li2S2, and LiPSs),
highlighting severe polysulfide-induced LMA degradation.

Understanding the origin of these distinct interfacial
chemistries is crucial for advancing fundamental issues of Li−
S battery electrochemistry. The galvanostatic intermittent
titration technique (GITT) was applied for decoupling the
electrochemical polarization during the 4 M conversion
reaction. Electrochemical polarization is defined as the deviation
of the electrode potential from its equilibrium value. The Li||S
cells with a lean electrolyte of 4 μL mg−1 and high MS of 4.5 mg
cm−2 were subjected to GITT measurements, including
galvanostatic discharge or charge at 0.05 C for 20 min and
following a relaxation time of 1 h for each GITT step (Figures
4f,g and S46). Usually, the total polarization voltage (ηtotal) is
defined as the gap between the working voltage and the
equilibrium voltage. This can be decoupled into three
components: ohmic polarization (ηohm), concentration polar-
ization (ηcon), and activation polarization (ηact).14 The values of
ηcon and ηohm + ηact could be distinguished from the GITT
profile, while the ηohm value is calculated using Ohm’s law from
the EIS result (Figures S47). The relative contributions of each
polarization component at different depths of discharge (DoDs)
are shown in Figures 4h and S46c.The contribution of ηohm
remains minimal throughout the discharge process, indicating
that it is not the dominant factor determining the S-based
reduction rate. After the DoD of 25%, ηact becomes the
dominant polarization in the DD electrolyte (Figures S46c and
S48), indicating sluggish conversion kinetics governed by
interfacial chemistry. These kinetics are limited not only by
the LiPSs maximum concentration but also by the protracted
liquid−solid conversion process. Although, for the CSA/
MeTHF electrolyte, the proportion of ηcon exceeds 50% after
the DoD of 25%, all three polarization components maintain
consistent trends throughout low voltage plateau. This
demonstrates that CSA/MeTHF electrolyte induces stable
interfacial chemistry that sustains rapid concentration-domi-
nated conversion, particularly for the shorter order LiPSs.32
Thus, these results demonstrate a clear correlation between
interfacial chemistry and electrochemical polarization during the
4 M conversion process, highlighting the critical role of
electrolyte engineering.
To verify the suppressed shuttle effect and boosted
conversion kinetics of LiPSs resulting from the gradient
solvation structure of the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte, Li−S coin
cells were first assembled and tested in Figure 5. With a MS of 1.1
mg cm−2 and E/S ratio of 16.7 μL mg−1, a Li||S coin cell using
CSA/MeTHF electrolyte delivers an initial specific capacity of
1037 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C (Figure 5a), verifying efficient S
utilization. And the capacity retention achieves 70% after 300
cycles, corresponding to a low decay rate of only 0.05% per cycle.
The average CE during cycling reaches 99.8%, indicating
effectively suppressed LiPSs shuttle effect. With an increased MS
of 3.0 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7.4 μL mg−1, the initial
capacity using CSA/MeTHF achieves 992 mAh g−1 with a small
polarization voltage at the low plateau (ΔElow) of 0.232 V
(Figures 5b and S49a). After 130 cycles, the capacity retention
remains at 88% with a high average CE of 99.5% (Figure 5c),
while ΔElow shows no obvious change (Figure S49b). In
contrast, using DD electrolyte exhibits a significantly large ΔElow
in the first cycle, which sharply increased after 130 cycle (Figure
S49c).
Since lean electrolyte and high MS are prerequisites for
practical high-energy-density Li−S batteries, the electrochem-
ical performance using CSA/MeTHF electrolyte was further
evaluated under harsh condition: an E/S of 4.2 μL mg−1 and a
MS of 6.3 mg cm−2 in Figures 5d and S50. The initial capacity
reached 1211 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C, corresponding to an areal
capacity of 7.6 mAh cm−2. After 50 cycles, the capacity is
retained at 909 mAh g−1 (a capacity retention of 67%),
demonstrating excellent cycling stability using high MS and lean
electrolyte conditions. With a MS of 8.6 mg cm−2 and an E/S
ratio of 3.6 μL mg−1, the coin cell using the CSA/MeTHF
electrolyte delivers an initial capacity of 1094 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C,
corresponding to an areal capacity of 9.4 mAh cm−2 (Figures 5e
and S51). After 30 cycles, the areal capacity remains at 7.7 mAh
cm−2, significantly exceeding that of commercial lithium-ion
batteries (∼4 mAh cm−2). The outstanding performance under
harsh conditions (MS ≥3 mg cm−2 and E/S ≤8 μL mg−1)
demonstrates a competitive advantage over most of reported
electrolytes for state-of-the-art Li−S batteries (Figure 5f and
Table S13).33,36,41,46−53
Finally, a 3 Ah-level Li−S pouch cell was assembled to validate
the practicability of this electrolyte screening approach. The
detailed parameters are shown in Figure S52 and Table S14. As
shown in Figure S53a, the Li−S pouch cell using the CSA/
MeTHF electrolyte shows an initial discharge capacity of 3.12
Ah and an initial energy density of 369 Wh kg−1 at 0.03 C, based
on the total mass of the pouch cell. After 15 cycles, the pouch cell
still works, and the energy density is maintained at 307 Wh kg−1
(Figure S53b). It should be noted that this cycling lifespan of 3
Ah-level Li||S pouch cell is only proof-of-concept.
To validate the screening criteria, a new solvent, 4,4,4-
trifluoro-N,N-dimethylbutanamide (TFBA) was synthesized
(Figures S54−S57), and paired with DOL, forming a gradient
solvation structure (TFBA/DOL) that effectively suppresses the
LiPSs shuttle effect (Figures S58 and S59). The TFBA/DOL
electrolyte enables stable Li||Li cycling for over 1000 h and
delivers 1051 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C with 92% capacity retention after
100 cycles (Figures S60 and S61), confirming the broad
applicability of this screening strategy for Li−S batteries.
In conclusion, we developed a screening methodology
comprising 75 solvent classifications based on εr and DN, and
electrolyte evaluation using seven critical electrochemical
performance parameters. The CSA/MeTHF electrolyte,
combining midstrong and weak solvation solvents, exhibits a
distinct solvent−solvent interaction that forms a gradient
solvation structure, as evidenced by multiple spectroscopic
techniques. Furthermore, the correlations among Li2S electro-
deposition, electrode interfacial chemistry, and LiPSs con-
version kinetics are demonstrated. The Li||S coin cell achieves an
initial capacity of 1094 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C with a MS of 8.6 mg
cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 3.6 μL of mg−1. Three Ah-level Li−S
pouch cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 3.12 Ah and an
initial energy density of 369 Wh kg−1. Additionally, the TFBA/
DOL electrolyte is designed to validate the universality of this
screening criterion engineering. This work for binary solvent
electrolytes provides new insights for promoting practical high-
energy-density Li−S batteries.
