a b s t r a c t
A new in operando spectro-electrochemical Li-S cell has been demonstrated. The novel design allows
investigations of the liquid electrolyte phase, in a commercial coin cell geometry, at C rates much higher
than conventional in situ cells. We use ATR FT-IR spectroscopy, coupled with a previously developed
polysulﬁde diagnostic to quantify the evolution of lithium polysulﬁdes during the discharge and charge
cycles of a Li-S cell. The trends observed in the polysulﬁde order and concentration with respect to state
of charge are consistent with prevailing understanding of the electrochemical mechanisms of Li-S battery
operation. During discharge, we observe the reduction of elemental sulfur to dissolved Li2S8 polysulﬁdes,
and their cascading conversion to smaller polysulﬁdes until insoluble species (Li2S2 and Li2S) are formed.
During cell charging, we observe the oxidation of insoluble polysulﬁdes to larger, soluble polysulﬁdes
(Li2Sn, n > 3), and infer an eventual recovery of crystalline sulfur, from changes in polysulﬁdes. Long-term
evolution of polysulﬁdes is observed over 7 discharge/charge cycles. Capacity fading is evident in the
decay of polysulﬁde order and concentration at the same state of charge between cycles. Sulfur is not
recovered by charging the cell in the latter cycles, and the active material is lost as solid Li2S.

1. Introduction
Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) batteries have the potential to fulﬁll the
performance requirements of the next generation of rechargeable
lithium cells [1e6]. Due to their high energy density, Li-S cells are
very attractive for portable electronics and electric vehicle appli-
cations. Sulfur is a promising cathode material because: it has a
high theoretical capacity, more than 5 times higher than traditional
lithium battery cathodes; it is earth abundant and consequently
inexpensive;
and,
it
is
environmentally
benign
[3,7,8].
Yet
commercialization of Li-S batteries remains elusive, due to the
problems of low electrical conductivity of sulfur, its volume
expansion upon discharge, and the dissolution of lithium poly-
sulﬁdes in the electrolyte [2,9,10]. The overall result is that Li-S cells
suffer from accelerated aging [8]. While many novel carbon based
nano-structural designs have been demonstrated to signiﬁcantly
improve cycle life, state of the art Li-S cells still struggle to maintain
a high and stable capacity beyond 100 cycles, whereas advanced Li-
ion batteries can deliver more than 1000 cycles [3,11,12]. In order to
make the lifetimes of Li-S cells competitive with existing Li-ion
technology, a fundamental understanding of the processes within
a Li-S cell that contribute to its fast aging is needed.
The electrochemical processes in a Li-S battery involve multiple
intermediate species, which can be difﬁcult to characterize and
have a strong dependence on the electrolyte composition and cell
conﬁguration [8,13,14]. The reaction mechanisms are the subject of
an ongoing scientiﬁc debate. However, the following steps are

There is also a general agreement that, during discharge, lithium
polysulﬁdes undergo a “shuttle” mechanism between the elec-
trodes. Long-chain lithium polysulﬁdes, formed at the cathode,
dissolve in the electrolyte and diffuse to the lithium anode. At the
anode, these long polysulﬁdes undergo a parasitic reaction with
lithium to form lower-order polysulﬁdes which are able to diffuse
back to the cathode, setting up a shuttle mechanism [8,16e21].
Eventually, the shuttling leads to electrode corrosion and loss of
active material. Researchers have proposed several ways to restrict
the dissolution and movement of polysulﬁdes in the battery [22],
for example: using a LiNO3-modiﬁed electrolyte to generate a
protective ﬁlm on the anode, which prevents the direct contact of
lithium with dissolved polysulﬁdes [23]; encapsulating sulfur into
micropores of carbon spheres to constrain the movement of poly-
sulﬁdes [12]; and anchoring polysulﬁdes by adsorption on a
phosphorene monolayer [24]. In many cases, characterization of
these novel cells are carried out ex situ [8]. Ex situ studies may lead
to debatable ﬁndings for Li-S batteries because preparation of the
target material by opening, washing, drying, etc. may lead to the
creation of artifacts [8,22]. Therefore, to avoid any misinterpreta-
tion, in operando studies, where cells are characterized while being
cycled, are critical to understand the phenomena taking place in-
side Li-S cells. In operando studies can also speed battery devel-
opment on account of being less time and labor intensive.
To date, several in operando studies of Li-S cells have been re-
ported. Patel at al. used ultravioletevisible (UVeVis) spectroscopy
for quantitative and qualitative determination of polysulﬁdes dur-
ing the ﬁrst discharge and charge cycles in a pouch cell [25,26].
Hagen et al. reported a Raman spectroscopy study in a glass cell to
observe a polysulﬁde equilibrium that moved toward shorter
chains during discharge and longer chains during charge [27].
Wang et al. used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
to capture the evolution of the soluble and precipitated lithium
polysulﬁdes during electrochemical cycling and the irreversible
accumulation of Li2S on the Li metal anode [4]. Gorlin et al. studied
the mechanism of the charging process and obtained spatially
resolved information about both solution and solid phase in-
termediates using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) [28].
Vizintin et al. coupled XAS with UVeVis spectroscopy to demon-
strate direct oxidation of Li2S to sulfur in a Li-S cell using a novel
cathode material [29]. Nelson et al. described the use of both in
operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) and in operando transmission X-
ray microscopy (TXM) to study the structural and morphological
changes in Li-S cells during operation [30]. XRD was also used by
Ca~nas et al. to observe consecutive reduction of sulfur to Li2S during
discharge and sulfur recrystallization during charge [31]. Marceau
et al. observed polysulﬁde dissolution in a solid polymer electrolyte
(SPE) using in operando scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [8].
Sun et al. demonstrated a in operando Li-S cell designed for direct
visualization of soluble polysulﬁde species using an optical micro-
scope, and their temporal and spatial distribution during a
discharge/charge cycle [32].
Generally, modifying an electrochemical cell for in situ studies
limits operation to relatively low charging or discharging rates
((C
=
10), due to suboptimal geometries for optical access, large
electrolyte volumes (pouch cells), or very long sampling times (as
in NMR). Matsui et al. reported a in operando Fourier transform
infrared (FT-IR) spectro-electrochemical cell for Li-ion batteries
that can be operated at charging-discharging rates of up to 1 C [33].
In operando studies at high C rates are useful for studying the
performance of cells over many cycles, and it can replicate rates
seen in a variety of applications. FT-IR spectroscopy is particularly
appealing because it is an established technique that can reveal
useful structural information of the molecules [34]. It is also fairly
inexpensive and straightforward compared to XRD or XAS, which
may require synchrotron sources typically only available at a
handful of national research facilities. In operando FT-IR spectros-
copy can also be used to study electrolyte decomposition in Li-S
batteries by monitoring the molecular bonds of the electrolyte
[33], which is not trivial in Raman or UVeVis techniques for Li-S
cells. We have previously demonstrated a optical diagnostic for
measuring lithium polysulﬁdes dissolved in electrolytes using
attenuated total reﬂection (ATR) FT-IR spectroscopy [34]. The
diagnostic uses the position and area of a characteristic infrared
absorption band to determine the order and concentration of pol-
ysulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte.

2. Experimental
The spectro-electrochemical cell is illustrated in Fig. 2. The
bottom part of the cell is a commercial ATR FT-IR accessory: Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc XR Diamond Crystal Plate for Smart iTX. We place
on the stainless steel plate a 50mm thick PTFE disc and a 50mm Cu
foil disc, with a 2 mm hole cut in the center of both discs to
accommodate the raised diamond crystal. The Cu foil acts as the
current collector. The Li-S CR2032 cell is then assembled on top of
these layers.
As an infrared beam undergoes a total internal reﬂection within
the diamond crystal, an evanescent wave is formed at the bound-
ary, which penetrates the medium beyond the crystal (Fig. 2). The
depth of penetration of the wave depends on: the incident angle of
the IR beam, the refractive indexes of the crystal and the medium,
and the wavelength of the incident light. In the spectral range of
interest for lithium polysulﬁdes, the effective penetration depth of
the evanescent wave is 20mm, which is on the order of typical
cathode thickness in Li-S coin cells [37,38]. Therefore, to effectively
measure the electrolyte phase of the Li-S cell using ATR FT-IR
spectroscopy, the cathode placed on the diamond crystal must be
porous, with the pores accommodating the electrolyte. The spectral
attenuation of the reﬂected beam is used to determine the poly-
sulﬁde order and concentration.
Cathode preparation begins by dissolving 5 wt% polyvinylidene
ﬂuoride
(PVDF,
pellets,
Sigma-Aldrich)
in
1-methyl-2-
pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) at 40C. Sulfur (pow-
der, 99.5%, Alfa Aesar) and carbon black (acetylene, 99.9%, Alfa
Aesar) with a 2:1 wt ratio were then added to make a slurry. The
weight ratio of carbon black to PVDF in the slurry was 1:1. The well-
mixed slurry was spread on a 25mm thick Celgard separator ﬁlm to
make a 50mm layer of separator and slurry. The slurry was allowed
to dry overnight before cutting into discs. The thickness of the dried
cathode layer was 15mm. The sulfur loading on each cathode disc
was 0.033g cm2. Before battery assembly, the cathode/separator
disc was soaked in 10mL of electrolyte. The electrolyte used was a
1 M solution of bistriﬂuoromethane sulfonimide lithium (LiTFSI,
99.95%, Sigma-Aldrich) in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether
(TEGDME, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich). The anode disc was cut out of a
0.38 mm thick lithium ribbon (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were
assembled in an argon-ﬁlled glove box. The stainless steel cell
casing shown in Fig. 2 (a) is a commercially available CR2032 casing
with an attached EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer)
rubber gasket. The Li-S battery was sealed by applying pressure
from above using the Smart iTX sample anvil. The Smart iTX, with a
spectro-electrochemical cell mounted on top, was ﬁnally installed
on a Nicolet iS50 (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc) FT-IR spectrometer for
in operando spectroscopy. Spectra were collected using a deuter-
ated triglycine sulfate (DTGS) detector with a resolution of 4 cm1
and 32 scan average. Spectra collection took about 50 s and was
repeated every 2 min.
3. Results and discussion
Fig. 3 shows the infrared absorbance peaks for lithium poly-
sulﬁdes measured in the Li-S cell. The ﬁrst discharge and charge
cycles are reported. The cell was cycled at 1 C and voltage proﬁles
can be found in Fig. 4(a). During discharge the peak diminishes and
shifts to lower wavenumbers, and the reverse is true during
charging of the cell. In order to get a clearer picture of the poly-
sulﬁde evolution, the aforementioned diagnostic was used to
resolve the infrared peaks to a mean order and concentration of
polysulﬁdes [34]. Peak area was calculated by integrating the
absorbance spectra between 480 and 522 cm1. The results are
presented in Fig. 4. The shape of the voltage curves and the initial
capacity are consistent with cells of similar geometry and sulfur
loading [9,37e40]. It is also noteworthy that gaining optical access
by making a hole in the current collector did not lead to any sig-
niﬁcant changes in the electrochemical performance of the cell.
This was veriﬁed by comparing capacity values against cells con-
structed without a hole in the current collector.

Charging the cell results in a fairly steady polysulﬁde order and
sharp increase in concentration during the ﬁrst third of the cycle.
During this time, Li2S and Li2S2 are oxidized to soluble polysulﬁde
species, which continue to form longer polysulﬁdes as the cell
charges. Towards the end of the cycle the concentration decreases
slightly. This suggests a recovery of crystalline sulfur from the
electrolyte, i.e. Eqn. (1) is reversed (Li2S8/2Li þ S8).
Since the ATR FT-IR measures polysulﬁdes through a porous
layer rather than a liquid ﬁlm, the concentration reported by the
diagnostic (Eqn. (6)), which was calibrated by wetting the diamond
crystal with a drop of polysulﬁde solution, will be undervalued. The
“true” concentration can be found by dividing the apparent con-
centration by the porosity. To correct the concentration values, we
soaked a cathode disc without any sulfur in a known polysulﬁde
standard solution and compared the resulting polysulﬁde absor-
bance peak area to that obtained by directly wetting the ATR crystal
with the same polysulﬁde standard. Sulfur was removed from the
cathode disc in order to maintain the same polysulﬁde equilibrium.
SEM images showed that the absence of sulfur did not have a sig-
niﬁcant impact on the porosity of the cathode layer due to the much
higher density of sulfur compared to carbon black. The ﬁnal results
showed that cell measurements under-predict the polysulﬁde
concentration by 50%. Therefore the true concentration of poly-
sulﬁdes in the cell at any given time is twice that obtained by the in
operando diagnostic. Note that the y-axis label in Fig. 4(c) reports
the corrected polysulﬁde concentration.

The spectro-electrochemical cell was used to study evolution of
lithium polysulﬁdes over 7 discharge/charge cycles. The polysulﬁde
order and concentration after every charge cycle are shown in
Fig. 5(a), and the capacity fading is shown in Fig. 5(b). At the end of
the 7th cycle 35% of the initial capacity was retained. The capacity
fading of the cell can be observed by the decay in polysulﬁde order
and concentration. As cell capacity is lost between cycles, the pol-
ysulﬁde concentration at the end of the charge cycle decreases. The
rate of capacity loss is highest initially. In the latter cycles, the
polysulﬁde order after the cell is fully charged drops to around 6.
This is also indicative of the irreversible loss of active cathode
material, which shifts the polysulﬁde equilibrium in the electrolyte
from Li2S8, initially, to Li2S6, at the end of the 7th charge cycle. The
polysulﬁde order at the end of each discharge cycle was measured
to be around 4. This, however, is attributed to the lower detection
limit of polysulﬁdes using our diagnostic.
4. Conclusions
A in operando spectro-electrochemical Li-S cell capable of
tracking the evolution of lithium polysulﬁdes has been demon-
strated. The cell uses ATR FT-IR spectroscopy to measure poly-
sulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte. The ATR technique is ideal for
sampling thin electrolyte ﬁlms and small electrolyte volumes
allowing
in
operando
measurements
of
commercial
battery
geometries and high C rates for cell cycling. The porous nature of
the cathode layer allows optical access to the electrolyte. Using a
novel diagnostic, we can quantitatively measure polysulﬁde order
and concentration from the in operando FT-IR spectra. This allows
us to quantify dissolved polysulﬁdes as a function of a Li-S cell's
state of charge. During the ﬁrst discharge cycle, we observe the
dissolution of sulfur to Li2S8, and cascading reduction to smaller
polysulﬁdes. We also observe sulfur being recovered from the
electrolyte at the end of the ﬁrst charge cycle. Over multiple cycles,
capacity fading can be observed in terms of polysulﬁde order and
concentration. Crystalline sulfur is not recovered during charging in
latter cycles and the active material is lost as insoluble Li2S.
The spectro-electrochemical cell can be used for in operando
studies to determine the efﬁcacy of novel techniques proposed to
mitigate polysulﬁde shuttling and improve Li-S cycling efﬁciency.
Since the cell geometry involves very few departures from a typical
coin cell, in operando spectroscopy is easy to integrate. The cell can
also be adapted to a multiple-reﬂection ATR for increased detection
limits, or a variable angle ATR for adjustable penetration depth. The
cell design can easily accommodate other in situ techniques, like
Raman spectroscopy, to detect solid-phase polysulﬁdes. Species
speciﬁc to the anode side can also be studied by modifying the
anode layer to be porous.
