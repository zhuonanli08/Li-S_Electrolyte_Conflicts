A B S T R A C T
The practical application of lithium–sulfur battery, one of the most promising batteries close to market, is
hindered by its poor cyclibility. Herein, Tris(trimethylsilyl) phosphite-Vinylene carbonate (TMSP-VC) is ﬁrstly
investigated as a duplex-component additives to prolong the cycle life and enhance the rate performance of Li-S
batteries. TMSP and VC function synergistically to modify the interfacial ﬁlm of sulfur cathode and Li anode,
and accelerate the lithium ion diﬀusion. Using traditional carbonate electrolyte with 1 wt%TMSP-2 wt%VC
additives, S@pPAN cathode delivers a reversible capacity of 1243 mA h g−1 over 800 cycles at 1 C, and a
discharge capacity of 990 mA h g−1 at the extremely high rate of 20 C.
1. Introduction
Due to high energy density, low cost and long cycle life, lithium ion
batteries (LIBs) have been successfully commercialized in electric
portable devices and electrical vehicles. However, the state-of-the-art
Li-ion battery cannot satisfy society's demands for batteries with longer
cycle life and higher energy density [1–3]. Accordingly, for a remark-
ably high theoretical capacity of 1675 mA h g−1 for sulfur cathode, high
speciﬁc energy density of 2600 W h kg−1 and low cost, lithium-sulfur
(Li-S) batteries show great potential for the next generation of “beyond
lithium ion battery” and have recently attracted intense interest [4–7].
Li-S batteries greatly reduce overcharge dangers compared with
conventional LIBs through utilizing “integration chemistry” instead of
insertion chemistry [8]. Nonetheless, the practical application of Li-S
batteries is hindered by several critical problems to be resolved,
including the poor electronic/ionic conductivity of sulfur and its
discharge products, the expansion/shrinkage of sulfur during cycling
(i.e., ∼76%), and the shuttling phenomenon resulted from the
solubility and diﬀusivity of polysulﬁde intermediates. These lead to
low sulfur utilization, low Coulombic eﬃciency and poor cycle stability
of Li-S batteries [9–11].
Therefore, continuous eﬀorts have been conducted to enhance the
electrochemical performances of Li-S batteries via optimizing the
conductive sulfur composites, anode or electrolyte composition to solve
aforementioned problems. In particular, through the syntheses of nano
sulfur composite material with unique conductive framework including
carbon, graphene, conductive polymer and mental oxide, the electro-
chemical performances of Li-S cells have been signiﬁcantly improved
[12–14]. In previous research, Wang et al. ﬁrst synthesized molecular-
level composite materials S@pPAN, in which single molecular sulfur Sn
(1 ≤n ≤8) was mono-dispersed and encapsulated in pyrolyzed
polyacrylonitrile (PAN) matrix to be a semiconductor, possessing an
electrical conductivity of ca. 10−4 S cm−1 [15,16]. The composite S@
pPAN eﬀectively prevented the polysulﬁde from dissolving and ob-
tained up to 95% sulfur utilization, showing excellent compatibility
with common carbonate electrolyte without obvious discharge/charge
plateau. Moreover, the S@pPAN cathode delivered high electrochemi-
cal activity and nearly 100% Coulombic eﬃciencies except the ﬁrst
cycle in common carbonate electrolyte. Compared with the frequently
used ether electrolytes in Li-S batteries, carbonate electrolytes shows
relatively lower vapor pressure, which is another advantage of S@
pPAN composites [17–19]. However, the common carbonate solvents
with LiPF6 impede the robust electrochemical performances of S@
pPAN composite materials after about 200 cycles due to the side-
reaction of electrolyte and the continuous growing cathode electrode
interface. To achieve long-term cycle stability and excellent rate
performance, various strategies have been reported to stabilize the
interface between electrodes and electrolyte.
So far, plenty of functional additives [20–23], binary lithium salts
[24,25] and new liquid electrolyte [26,27] are employed in Li-S battery
to improve its electrochemical properties. As one of the most facile and
economic solution, introducing functional additive into common
electrolyte systems could eﬀectively inhibit the electrolyte decomposi-
tion and participate in the formation of SEI [28–32]. For common
elemental sulfur cathodes, LiNO3 was widely adopted to form a
passivation layer on lithium anode surface to alleviate the shuttle

2. Experimental section
2.1. Preparation of sulfur cathode
16 g elemental sulfur and 2 g polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Aldrich) was
homogeneously mixed by ball milling and then heat-treated at 300 °C
in nitrogen for 450 min, resulting in S@pPAN black powder. The sulfur
cathodes were fabricated by mixing S@pPAN with 41.3 wt% S, Super P
and carbonyl-β-cyclodextrin (C-β-CD) binder [45] in the ratio of 8:1:1
to form slurry. After stirring for 6 h, the slurry was uniformly coated
onto carbon coated aluminum foil and dried at for 70 ℃under vacuum
overnight. The cathode was cut into circular discs area of 1.13 cm2 with
the average composites mass loading of about 1 mg cm−1.
2.2. Preparation of electrolyte
Lithium hexaﬂuorophosphate (LiPF6) in ethylene carbonate (EC)
and dimethylcarbonate (DMC) (1:1, v/v) was used as basic electrolyte.
The experimental functional electrolyte was obtained by adding
adequate
amounts of
Tris(trimethylsilyl) phosphite
(TMSP)
and
Vinylene carbonate (VC) into the common electrolyte, and further
stirring for 12 h at room temperature. It is noted that the electrolyte
was prepared and stored in an argon-ﬁlled dry glove box (MB-10
compact, MBRAUN) containing less than 1 ppm water and O2.
2.3. Physical characterizations
Ionic conductivity tests of the electrolyte were carried out with a
DDS-307A conductivity meter at ambient temperature. The morphol-
ogy of the sulfur cathode and Li anode were observed using SEM (FEI
Nova Nano-scanning electron microscope). Before examination, the Li-
S coin cells in the full-charged state after 100 cycles were disassembled
in an argon-ﬁlled glove box and then obtained electrodes were rinsed
with dimethylcarbonate (DMC) thoroughly. It is noted that the dried Li
anode must be sealed in a Ar-ﬁlled container and transferred quickly
into the SEM equipment to avoid oxidation. Surface composition of the
electrode after 100 cycles in electrolyte with and without additives was
analyzed by the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), using a
Kratos Axis UltraDLD spectrometer (Kratos Analytical-A Shimadzu
Group Company) with monochromatic Al Ka source (1486.6 eV) under
ultra-high vacuum. Also, the samples should be thoroughly washed
with DMC to remove any electrolyte salt residuals. The obtained
spectra after calibration were ﬁtted via using XPS peak software
(version 4.1).
2.4. Electrochemical measurements
The testing CR2016-type Li-S coin cells were assembled in an
argon-ﬁlled glove box, using the as-prepared sulfur electrode as
cathode, pure lithium foil as anode, and Celgard2300 ﬁlm as separator.
Also, to evaluate the eﬀects of TMSP and VC in Li-S batteries,
electrolytes with and without additive were employed, respectively.
The galvanostatic discharge/charge tests were carried out on a LAND-
CT 2001A Cell Test System (Wuhan, China) in the voltage range of 1.0–
3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+). The rate performance of cells was evaluated using the
same equipment at the discharged current density of 0.5 C, 1 C, 3 C,
5 C, 7 C, 9 C, 10 C, 12 C, 15 C, 20 C, 30 C, respectively, and charged at
0.5 C (1 C equal to 1672 mA g−1 for sulfur). Alternative current
impedance spectroscopy of Li-S cells with diﬀerent electrolyte were
measured using AUTOLAB PGSTAT302N (Metrohm) at full-charged
state over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with an
amplitude of 5 mV. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were performed on
a CHI760E Electrochemical Workstation (Shanghai, China) with the
three-electrode cell equipment between 1 V and 3 V. The working
electrode was a sulfur-based electrode (area: 1.13 cm2), the counter
and the reference electrodes were lithium metal anodes.
3. Results and discussion
To evaluate the eﬀect of TMSP on Li-S batteries, a comparison of
the cycling performance of S@pPAN cathode with various contents of
TMSP additive at 1 C is demonstrated in Fig. 1. It is clear that the cycle
stability of the cells was remarkably improved with the addition of
TMSP, especially with 1 wt% TMSP. The S@pPAN composites in blank
electrolyte showed gradually continuous capacity decrease before 150
cycles but suﬀered a fast decay later. After 400 cycles, the capacity
retention was only 57.7%, as calculated against the 2nd cycle. On the
contrary, the cell using electrolytes with 1 wt%TMSP maintained a
reversible discharge capacity of 1250.1 mA h g−1 in 400th cycle with a
fading rate of 0.036% per cycle, indicating that the capacity retention
was up to 85.6%. However, when the concentration of TMSP additive
increased to 10 wt%, the capacity decreased to 455.5 mA h g−1 which

cyclibility of S@pPAN composite cathode, and the introduction of 1 wt
% TMSP into electrolyte signiﬁcantly prolonged its cycle life.

lithium ion batteries by eﬀectively improving anode [42–44]. The

Besides the excellent cycle stability when using 1 wt%TMSP-2 wt%
VC additives, the cell also manifested a satisfactory high power rate
performance, as shown in Fig. 2c. To overcome the relatively slow
charge kinetics, the rate performance test was conducted using slow
constant charge rate 0.5Cwhile dischage at increasing rate. At 10 C, the
S@pPAN composites cathode in the blank electrolyte delivered merely
71.4% of the initial capacity of 1420 mA h g−1, while the capacity of
cathode in the electrolyte with 1 wt% TMSP and 2 wt% VC increased to
1300 mA h g−1 and 1240 mA h g−1, respectively. It is worth noting that
in the functional electrolyte 1 wt%TMSP-2%VC additives, the cell
exhibited no obvious capacity decay and still retained extremely large
capacity of 1400 mA h g−1 even discharged at 10 C. Also, a favorable
capacity of 1440 mA h g−1 was restored when the current density rate
returned to 0.5 C. In addition, Fig. S3 shows that the functional
electrolyte could signiﬁcantly suppress the voltage polarization at high
current density. Remarkably, with the increasing discharge rate, about
a capacity of 990 mA h g−1 could be restored in the electrolyte with 1 wt
%TMSP-2 wt%VC additives at 20 C, and Coulombic eﬃciencies ap-
proached 100% (Fig. 2d). Moreover, the cell using TMSP-VC contain-
ing electrolyte maintained capacity retentions of 91%, 94.9% and
83.4% after 500 cycles at 2 C, 3 C and 4 C, respectively, as shown in
Fig. 2e. This result further demonstrated the high rate cyclibility of S@
pPAN cathode in functional electrolyte with TMSP-VC duplex-compo-
nent additives.
Furthermore, we conducted cyclic voltammograms (CVs) with a
scanning rate of 1 mV S−1 to identify the inﬂuence of additives on the
electrochemical reaction. Fig. 3a and b both displayed one cathodic
peak at around 2.65 V and one anodic peak at around 1.40 V, meaning
that the addition of TMSP-VC hardly aﬀected the charge/dischage
reaction. It is worth mentioning that, during cycling, the oxidation peak
potential increased but the reduction peak potential decreased in blank
electrolyte, leading to the increasing polarized voltage gaps. In contrast,
the CV in TMSP-VC containing electrolyte presented almost constant
oxidation/reduction peak potential. More interestingly, the 5th and
10th CVs in Fig. 3b were complete overlap, suggesting that the cell
using proposed electrolyte obtains excellent stability. This result is in
coincidence with galvanostatic discharge/charge tests.

Meanwhile, as the structural fracture of sulfur cathode can directly
limit its cycle life, to understand the additives eﬀect in the interface
structure of the electrode, we examined the morphology of the sulfur
cathode after 100 cycles by SEM, respectively. It can be seen from
Fig. 4a that, after 100 charge-discharge process in blank electrolyte, the
surface of the cathode displayed visible cracks and rough agglomerated
particles, which lead to capacity decay. In contrast, no obvious
pulverization or fracture in Fig. 4b was observed on the S@pPAN
cathode when cycled in standard electrolyte with 1 wt%TMSP-2 wt%
VC, thus maintaining integrated cathode structure and obtaining a
superior long-term cycle performance. This observation conﬁrms that
the additive we used was beneﬁcial for structural uniformity and
integrity of sulfur cathode.
The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) presented in Fig. 5
also explains the impact of TMSP-VC on the electrochemical properties
of sulfur cathode. All EIS measurements were conducted with an AC
amplitude of 5 mV. Fig. 5a and b show the impedance spectra of the Li-
S cells in various electrolytes before and after cycling at full-charged
state. The EIS consisted of one semicircle at high frequency and a
slopping line at low frequency, corresponding to the interfacial
resistance (Rf) and electron transfer resistance (Re), respectively. The
cell using diﬀerent electrolytes owned similar interfacial impedance
before cycling, as shown in Fig. 5a. Though the addition of TSMP-VC
additives didn’t cause any obtrusive change in Re after cycling, a
variation in Rf of the electrolytes appeared 10 cycles. Meanwhile, the
EIS were measured at a discharged state (1.7 V). Two superimposed
semicircles and a sloping line were observed in Fig. 5c, representing Rf,
the electrochemical reaction resistance (Rct) and Re. The impedance
spectra were ﬁtted by the equivalent circuits presented in Fig. S4, and
the ﬁtted EIS data were listed in Table S1. The cell using electrolytes
containing 1 wt%TMSP-2 wt% VC exhibited smaller Rf and Rct in
diﬀerent testing states than in blank electrolyte. These results provided
evidence that the addition of TMSP-VC additives might take part in
interfacial reactions to form a SEI with less impedance, thus accelerat-
ing the electrochemical reactions and being in coincidence with the
diﬀusion coeﬃcient of diﬀerent electrolytes.
On the other hand, the layer on Li anode would also retard Li+
transport and negatively aﬀect cell performance. The impedances of
symmetric Li/Li cells were also measured and shown in Fig. 5d and e.
The impedances were composed of the SEI resistance at high frequency
region and the charge transfer resistance at middle frequency range. By
the equivalent circuits given, the ﬁtted EIS data were listed in Table S1.
It conﬁrms that, compared to the Li anode in blank electrolyte, the
anode impedance in proposed electrolyte was much lower, especially
after cycling. It is quite likely that additives form dense SEI with less
impedance on lithium metal anode.

In the C 1s spectra in Fig. 6a, the peak located at 289.8 eV is
assigned to Li2CO3 species resulted from the electrolyte decomposition,
of which the intensity in TMSP-VC containing electrolyte was much
weaker than that in blank electrolyte. However, the intensities of C=O
at 288.5 eV and C-O at 286.3 eV increased in proposed electrolyte [46].
It reveals that the major electrolyte decomposition products varied for
the addition of electrolyte additives. Furthermore, the obviously
stronger peak for C-C at 284.4 eV corresponding to graphite [46]
suggested that the deposits on the cathode cycled in TMSP-VC contain-
ing electrolyte was thinner.
Fig. 6b shows two characteristic F 1s peaks for LiF (at 685.3 eV) and
LixPOyFz (at 687.4 eV) in the SEI on the cathodes after several cycles [46].
As we know, the following reactions exist when trace amounts of water are
present in the electrolyte: LiPF6 + H2O→OPF3 + LiF + 2HF, OPF3 +
3H2O→O4PF3 + 3HF [45]. The product HF would cause irreversible loss
of Li+ by forming LiF, a component of SEI on the cathode. Here, the
intensity of LiF discernibly decreased when cycled with TMSP-VC
additives. Therefore, we speculate that the addition of additives could
remove the resulting HF during cycling to suppress LiF formation.
In the P 2p spectra after 100 cycles(see in Fig. 6c), LixPOyFz peak at
133.8 eV showed signiﬁcant increases in proposed electrolyte [46],
while the intensity of LixPFy peak decreased. The decreasing intensity
of LixPFy peak conﬁrms that there were more electrolyte degradation
products in blank electrolyte during cycling, which contributed to the
capacity decay. The enhanced LixPOyFz peak (F–P–O) might be
ascribed to the decomposition of TMSP.
In addition, Si element on S@pPAN cathode was detected to further
conﬁrm that TMSP take part in the formation of the SEI layer. As
shown in Fig. 6d, Si 2p spectrum located at 102.0 eV appeared when
cycled with TMSP-VC additives, suggesting that the formed SEI
contained the decomposition of TMSP.

Based on the above results, it indicates that TMSP-VC additives were
beneﬁcial for forming protective interfaces on the cathode and anode,
respectively. We proposed the schematic illustration of functions of
additives as shown in Fig. 7. The formation process of SEI layer in the
role of TMSP improved the electrode/electrolyte interface, resulting in
better electrochemical performance. Table S3. shows the HOMO levels,
LUMO levels, Oxidation Potential (OP) values and Reduction Potential
(RP) values [47]. It should be mentioned that the protective cathode ﬁlm
simultaneously prevented the oxidation of VC, owning to the higher OP
value of VC. Not only that, it has been reported that TMSP possess the
relatively highest reactivity with HF, resulting in less nonconductive LiF
products and smaller interfacial impedance of Li-S cells. Consequently,
S@pPAN dissolution and lithium depletion were eﬀectively restrained
when cycling in TMSP-VC-added electrolyte. Beside that, during repeated
cycling, due to its higher RP value than EC, VC additive was reduced
predominantly on the lithium anode to form polymeric surface ﬁlms
containing -OCO2Li groups and C=C bonds, as already reported in
previous literature that this ﬂexible interface beneﬁted lithium electro-
deposition and suppressed interfacial by-reactions, resulting in stable
cycling of lithium metal anode [42].
4. Conclusion
In summary, TMSP-VC were ﬁrstly applied as duplex–component
additives in carbonate electrolyte for Li-S batteries with S@pPAN
composite cathodes. During the charge/discharge process, TMSP was
prior oxidized at the cathode, participated in the interfacial interac-
tion and scavenged HF derived from the degradation of electrolyte.
Thus, a protective and stable SEI layer with less impedance was
formed,
remarkably
enhancing
the
Li-ion
diﬀusion
coeﬃcient.
Meanwhile, in the role of additives, the interfacial ﬁlm on the anode
was also obviously modiﬁed by VC additive. Under the synergistic
eﬀect of TMSP and VC, Li-S battery exhibited extraordinary cyclibil-
ity and high rate capability. The S@pPAN cathode in carbonate
electrolyte with 1 wt%TMSP-2 wt%VC additives maintained capacity
retention of 84.6% over 800 cycles, and manifested extremely large
speciﬁc capacity of 1400 mA h/g and 990 mA h/g discharged at 10 C
and 20 C, respectively. We hope that this duplex–component addi-
tives presented here could promote the development of high-perfor-
mance Li-S batteries.
