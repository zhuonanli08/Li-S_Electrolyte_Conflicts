Experimental Procedures 
Materials
1,2-Dimethoxyethane (99.5%), 1,3-dioxolan (99.5%), lithium perchlorate (LiClO4, 99%), lithium sulfide (99.9%), lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6, 99.9%), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (99%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Sulfur powder (sublimated, 99.5%), lithium nitrate anhydrous (LiNO3, 99%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) were obtained from Alfa Asear. Lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiOTf, 98%) were purchased from TCI America. Lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI, 99.9%), lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, 99.9%), lithium difluoro(oxalato)borate (LiDFOB, 99.9%), lithium bis(oxalato)borate (LiBOB, 99.9%), lithium tetrafluoroborate (LiBF4, 99.9%) were purchased from Dodochem. Li foils were purchased from China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. 

Electrode Preparation
Sulfur cathodes were prepared using a melting diffusion method. Sublimated sulfur powders and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) were mixed in a mass ratio of 7:3, then heated in an oven at 155°C for 24 hours in a sealed glass vial filled with argon. After cooling to room temperature, the sulfur/CNT mixture was mixed with additional CNT and PVDF in a mass ratio of 8:1:1, using N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as the solvent. The binder concentration was 20 mg g-1. The slurry was mixed with a magnetic stirrer overnight and then cast onto carbon-coated aluminum foil with a thickness of 250 µm. The electrode was dried at 60°C for 24 hours under vacuum to remove the NMP solvent. The electrode was then cut into discs with a diameter of 12 mm before use.
For high-loading cathodes, LA133 binder (15% aqueous solution) was used, with a mass ratio of S/CNT:LA133 of 90:10. Deionized water and ethanol (1:1 volume ratio) were used as the solvent. The concentration of binder solution used for slurry was 30 mg g-1. The slurry was then coated onto carbon paper (TGP-H-060, Toray) following the same procedure, with thicknesses of ~800 µm (5-6 mg cm-2) and ~1200 µm (11-12 mg cm-2). Electrodes were dried and cut using the same procedure.
For the S-S symmetrical cell, the electrodes consisted of ethylene black, with 10% PVDF as the binder (9:1 mass ratio). The slurry preparation followed the same procedure as that of the sulfur cathode, using ethylene black as the material. All electrodes were pre-selected to ensure uniform mass loading (1 mg/piece) for the CV test.

Electrolyte Preparation
The LS005 electrolyte, containing 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 in a DME/DOL (1:1 vol), was purchased from Dodochem and used as received. The 5NB electrolyte was prepared using 0.75 M LiNO₃ and 0.25 M LiBOB in a DME/DOL mixture with a 1:1 molar ratio (6:4 volume ratio). For high-loading sulfur cathode cells, 50 mM LiI was added as an additive. Other electrolytes were prepared by adding the appropriate amounts of salts and solvents into glass vials and stirring at room temperature for more than 24 hours before use. For LiPF6, LiBF4, and LiDFOB containing electrolytes, the preparation was done in polypropylene (PP) vials following the same procedure.

LiPS Solution Preparation
Li2S and S were added to a DME/DOL (1:1 vol) solution in a stoichiometric ratio of 1:5 to prepare the LiPS solution. The concentration of the Li2S6 solution was set at 1 M. The solution was heated on a hot plate at 60°C for 48 hours. For the catholyte used in S-S symmetrical cells, the 1 M Li2S6 solution was diluted to 50 mM or 0.2M by adding DME and DOL solvents. Subsequently, 0.5 M of various salts were added to the solution. The final solution was stirred for more than 24 hours at room temperature before use. In the visualization test, a 0.2M salt concentration was used to ensure a clear color comparison between different samples. A 0.5M salt concentration could also be used and will give the same result except for LiPF6. The 0.5M LiPF6 solution presents challenges due to electrolyte gelation.

Theoretical calculations
The molecular geometries of various species were optimized using Density Functional Theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) level. Subsequently, overall energy, HOMO/LUMO energy, and electrostatic potential surfaces were computed using DFT at the B3LYP/6-311G+(2d,2p) level. All DFT calculations were conducted with Gaussian161 on the Digital Research Alliance of Canada platform.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the GROMACS package2. For the LS005 electrolyte, the simulation box (50 × 50 × 50 Å3) contained 100 Li, 87 TFSI, 13 NO3, 421 DME, and 626 DOL molecules, based on the approximate density. For the 5NB electrolyte, the simulation box (55 × 55 × 55 Å3) included 100 Li, 75 NO3, 25 BOB, 573 DME, and 579 DOL molecules, also according to the approximate density. Packing Optimization for Molecular Dynamics Simulations (PACKMOL)3 was used for packing the molecules.
Force-field parameters for DME, DOL, and BOB- were generated by Sobtop 1.04 using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF)5, with restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) atomic partial charges calculated using the RESP2 method6. The structure optimizations were done under vacuum and IEFPCM solvation model with acetone parameters. Force-field parameters for Li+, TFSI-, and NO3- were sourced from previous studies7,8. Visualization and analysis of the results were carried out using VMD software.
All the initial configurations were energy-minimized using the steepest descent method with a convergence criterion of 450 kJ mol-1 nm-1. Production runs of 2 ns for 1,000,000 steps in the NPT ensemble were performed using the V-rescale thermostat9 and Parrinello-Rahman barostat10, with a time constant of 0.2 ps at 298.15 K. The last 10 ns of simulations were used for radial distribution function (RDF) analysis, ensuring a fully equilibrated system. Van-der-Waals interactions were calculated with a cutoff distance of 1.2 nm. The particle-mesh Ewald (PME)11 method with a 1.2 nm real space cutoff was used to calculate the electrostatic interactions.

Material characterization
7Li-NMR spectra were acquired using an Agilent/Varian Inova two-channel 400 MHz spectrometer at ambient temperature, using 10 vol% DMSO-d6 as the reference solvent. Raman measurements were conducted with a Renishaw InVia Raman Microscope equipped with a 532 nm laser. The setup of the in-situ cell is illustrated in Figure S39. For NMR and Raman measurements, the 5NB electrolyte contained 0.25 M LiBOB and 0.75 M LiNO3 in DME/DOL. However, a single-salt 0.75 M LiNO3 solution is not fully soluble in DME/DOL. Thus, for Raman measurements, we used a saturated LiNO3 solution (~0.65M) in DME/DOL. In the case of NMR, adding 10 vol% DMSO-d6 which is necessary for the test, caused re-precipitation of LiNO3 from the electrolyte solution, making it difficult to maintain identical concentrations across samples. Finally, after several trials, to avoid precipitation, we used 0.25 M LiNO3 for NMR, ensuring a stable and homogeneous electrolyte composition with DMSO-d6.
The morphology of the Li metal anodes and sulfur cathodes was observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Zeiss EVO M10). For in-depth X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements, cells were cycled at 0.1C until fully discharged and charged. The cells were then disassembled and washed with DME to remove residual electrolytes. Samples were transferred to and sealed in a transfer vessel within an argon-filled glovebox. XPS profiles were collected using the XPS Versa Probe III (PHI 5000), and all binding energy values were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. 


Electrochemical measurements
All electrochemical tests were conducted using 2032-type coin cells, fabricated in an argon-filled glovebox with a single layer of Celgard 2500 as the separator. The electrolyte amount added to each cell was dependent on the cathode sulfur loading, with the E/S ratio indicated in each figure.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed on a Biologic VMP3 electrochemical workstation. Symmetrical CV tests utilized 40 µL of 0.2 M Li2S6 solution with 0.5 M of various salts in DME/DOL (1:1 vol), with ethylene black electrodes serving as both working and counter electrodes. The scan rate was 20 mV s-1, and each cell underwent three cycles (Figure S1). 
Li||S, Li||Li, Li2S||Ti cells were tested using a Neware battery testing system, operating in C-rate mode with a specific capacity of 1672 mAh g-1 of sulfur. Testing conditions are detailed in each figure. For the CE test, the cutoff charging capacity is 1672 mAh g-1 sulfur. For self-discharging test, cells are cycle at 0.5C and rest for 1,3 and 7 days at fully charged state before discharging. Li2S||Ti anode-free cells were prepared by fully discharging a sulfur cathode against lithium, then reassembling it against a piece of Ti foil.

Note S1: Discussion on symmetric cell CV and Randles-Sevcik equation
The peak current can be explained using the Randles-Sevcik equation, where the peak current (iP) and peak potential (EP) are related to the parameters of the electrochemical system. The equation is given as12:


Where n is the number of electrons involved in the electrode reaction, A is the area in cm2, D is the diffusion coefficient in cm2 s-1; subscript O stands for the oxidized form of the standard system O + ne ⇌ R; C* is the bulk concentration in mol cm-3; v is the scan rate in V s-1. E1/2 is the half-wave potential in voltammetry in V; R is the ideal gas constant; T is temperature in K; and F is the Faraday constant.
From Equation S1, the peak current is linearly proportional to the v1/2, while the peak potential is independent of scan rate. In practice, however, peak potential shifts occur due to polarization at the electrode. The Randles-Sevcik equation is derived from the Nernst equation under boundary conditions and assumes semi-infinite linear diffusion. Additionally, this equation is valid only when the system is diffusion-controlled, specifically when the electron transfer at the electrode surface (i.e., the reaction kinetics) is sufficiently rapid. If the reaction is kinetically controlled, the Randles-Sevcik equation no longer applies, and the Butler-Volmer equation must be used instead. In such cases, the peak current becomes proportional to v rather than v1/2. 
In Figure 1b, the peak current of the CV is presented, where LiOTf exhibits the highest peak current among all the salts. This can be attributed to faster ion diffusion in the LiOTf system, as described by the Randles-Sevcik equation, which relates peak current to diffusion coefficient. In contrast, the diffusion of LiTFSI and LiPF6 is slower, resulting in lower peak currents.
In Figure S6, the CV profile under different scan rates (5, 10, 15 and 20 mV s-1) are provided. The LiTFSI, LiFSI, LiClO4 and LiPF6 system exhibit typical characteristics of a reversible, diffusion-controlled process, as indicated by the linear correlation between peak current and v1/2. 
For LiOTf, the first cycle shows two distinct oxidation and reduction peaks. However, after cycling under the same scan rate, the second peak at ~0.6V diminishes while the first peak at ~0.2V increases. This suggests that the conversion from LiPS to Li2S is suppressed. Despite LiOTf showing the highest current response in the initial cycles (Figure 1b), this suppression could be attributed to side reactions between LiOTf and LiPS, or an unfavorable deposition morphology of Li2S that inhibits further reactions.
In the case of LiNO3, a different pattern emerges. The first peak increases with the scan rate, indicating a diffusion-controlled process. However, the second peak diminishes as the scan rate increases. This may be due to the slow kinetics of Li2S deposition or the low ionic conductivity of the LiNO3 electrolyte.
In contrast, cell polarization (reflected in the peak potential separation) is related to the half-wave potential in voltammetry, the number of electrons transferred and the temperature (Equation S2). As the reaction and the temperature are constant across the tested systems, the changes in peak potential arise from differences in half-wave potential. Factors influencing half-wave potential include ionic conductivity, reaction kinetics, diffusion, electrode materials, the nature of redox species, and scan rate. With the last three factors constant in our tests, the primary contributors to variations in peak potential are ionic conductivity and reaction kinetics. The measured ionic conductivities are shown in Figure S5.
In summary, the peak current mainly reflects the diffusion coefficient, while cell polarization is influenced by ionic conductivity, reaction kinetics, and diffusion.

Note S4: Mechanism of the Reaction Between LiPS and LiBOB
The reaction mechanism between LiPS and BOB- can be explained as follows. Ethylene carbonate (EC) undergoes a typical ring-opening reaction under nucleophilic attack, and polysulfide anions, being strong nucleophiles, can react with EC as reported in previous literature. Similarly, the boron center in the BOB- anion is partially positively charged due to its coordination with four electron-rich oxygen atoms. This makes it an active site for nucleophilic attack, leading to a comparable ring-opening reaction as proposed in the main text.

Note S5: Salt selection for electrolyte
Firstly, one of the reacting salts was selected for the battery. The reacting salts form a CEI that act as a diffusion barrier for LiPS shuttle and improves cycling stability. Among all the reacting salts, LiBOB and LiBF4 exhibit the fastest reaction with LiPS. This is crucial because if the reaction is too slow, LiPS will have enough time to diffuse into the electrolyte before reacting with the salt, leading to precipitation in the electrolyte phase rather than on the electrode surface. This undesired reaction consumes more LiPS and electrolyte, ultimately harming cell performance. Additionally, LiBF4 does not function effectively in this regard, as the solvent remains transparent (Figure 1a), indicating that it does not form solid-phase products when reacting with LiPS or produces only a minimal amount. This contradicts the purpose of forming a CEI to suppress the shuttle effect. Furthermore, LiDFOB reacts more slowly with LiPS, while LiFSI exhibits an even slower reaction.
Secondly, LiNO3 was chosen as the second salt for the electrolyte. A non-reacting salt is necessary to maintain ion transport and ensure sufficient ionic conductivity. Among all the non-reacting salts, LiNO3 is essential for maintaining the high Coulombic efficiency of the cells. LiTFSI, a commonly used salt in Li-based systems, was found to be less favorable due to its slower kinetics in facilitating sulfur redox reactions, leading us to exclude it from our final electrolyte formulation. As a result, only two salts, LiBOB and LiNO3, were selected for this system.

Note S6: The effect of solvent on LiPS dissolution and lithium metal anode corrosion
Figure S36 illustrates the underlying mechanism of the phenomenon observed in Figure 4a. Initially, elemental sulfur dissolves in the ether-based solvent/electrolyte at a very low concentration. The dissolved sulfur then diffuses to the lithium metal anode, where it reacts with lithium to generate LiPS, such as Li2S4. Due to their high solubility, these LiPS dissolve back into the electrolyte.
After resting, the dissolved LiPS diffuse back to the cathode, where they undergo a disproportionation reaction with elemental sulfur, producing longer-chain LiPS, such as Li2S8. These soluble LiPS continue to react with the lithium metal anode, creating a chain reaction that accelerates the reactions due to the continuous generation of highly soluble LiPS. In the pure solvent, shuttling and corrosion are more severe, as the solubility of elemental sulfur is higher. Adding lithium salts, such as LiTFSI, can limit this solubility, reducing the initial dissolution of elemental sulfur. Consequently, the reaction proceeds more slowly in the LS005 system compared to the pure solvent.
In the 5NB electrolyte, elemental sulfur could also react with lithium metal on the anode, generating LiPS on the surface of the lithium foil. Due to the presence of LiBOB in the electrolyte, a rapid reaction occurs between LiBOB and LiPS, forming a SEI layer on the lithium metal surface. This SEI layer protects the lithium and prevents further reactions between S8 and Li. Thus, the chain reaction observed in the LS005 electrolyte does not occur in this situation. As a result, after 30 days of rest, the 5NB electrolyte remains clear, while the LS005 electrolyte turns a dark orange color.
Therefore, the choice of lithium salts in the electrolyte significantly impacts the stability of the lithium metal anode. While LiTFSI offers better protection for the lithium metal compared to pure solvents, it is still insufficient. By selecting appropriate salts, the corrosion of the lithium metal anode and the self-discharging effect in Li||S cells can be greatly suppressed.

Note S7: Discussion on CEI Formation and GCD Curves
The cycling performance and galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves are presented in Figure S46. The discharge plateaus can be divided into two parts: the higher plateau, corresponding to the conversion from S8 to long-chain lithium polysulfides (LiPS, Li2Sn, 4 ≤ n ≤ 8), and the lower plateau, corresponding to the reaction from LiPS to Li2S.
1. Discharge Plateau Voltage:
When comparing the discharge plateaus of the two systems, the 5NB system shows a higher voltage at the upper plateau and a lower voltage at the lower plateau compared to the LS005 system. This difference is attributed to the donor number of the electrolyte. A higher donor number increases interactions with LiPS, making the Li2S deposition more difficult, which results in a lower second plateau Due to the high donor number of LiNO3,13 the LiPS may interact more strongly with NO3- anions, hindering Li2S deposition and causing the observed voltage differences between the systems.
2. Higher Plateau vs. Lower Plateau:
The capacity ratio of the two plateaus differs between the systems. Typically, a longer lower plateau (or a higher Qlow/Qhigh ratio, where Q represents capacity) indicates a faster and more complete sulfur reduction reaction. The theoretical capacity of the first high plateau is 418 mAh g-1, and the lower plateau is 1254 mAh g-1, indicating that the lower plateau has three times the capacity of the higher one. However, in the first cycle of the 5NB system, the higher plateau exceeds the 418 mAh g-1 limit. This is due to the elemental sulfur generated during the CEI formation reaction, where part of the reduced LiPS is consumed, producing S8 (Figure 5n). This results in an additional capacity contribution to the higher plateau while decreasing the second one. This observation supports the hypothesis that elemental sulfur is generated during the reaction of LiBOB and LiPS, as discussed in Equation 1 of the main text.
3. Capacity Decay from 1st to 2nd Cycle:
Comparing the capacity decay from the 1st to the 2nd cycle, the 5NB system shows a relatively larger decrease (306 mAh g-1) than the LS005 system (221 mAh g-1). This is because part of the sulfur active material is consumed during the CEI formation, as discussed in Equation 1. Consequently, the first charging plateau also has a lower capacity than the discharge plateau (Dch: 1224 mAh g-1, Ch:1116 mAh g-1). The consumed sulfur, which forms the CEI, cannot participate in the sulfur redox reaction anymore, resulting in a decrease in the charging capacity from the 1st to the 2nd cycle, further indicating the occurrence of the CEI reaction. In contrast, the LS005 system exhibits almost identical charging and discharging capacities in the first cycle (Dch: 1096 mAh g-1, Ch: 1085 mAh g-1). Although the decay from the 1st to the 2nd cycle is higher in the 5NB system due to CEI formation, it provides a higher discharge capacity and better capacity retention than the LS005 system.
Overall, the GCD curve analysis further confirm the formation of the CEI and help exploring the possible reaction products generated by the interaction between LiPS and LiBOB.
