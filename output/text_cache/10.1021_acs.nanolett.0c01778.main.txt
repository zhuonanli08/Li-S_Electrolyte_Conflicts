ABSTRACT: Polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀects, active material losses,
formation of resistive surface layers, and continuous electrolyte
consumption create a major barrier for the lightweight and low-
cost lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery adoption. Tuning electrolyte
composition by using additives and most importantly by
substantially increasing electrolyte molarity was previously shown
to be one of the most eﬀective strategies. Contrarily, little attention
has been paid to dilute and super-diluted LiTFSI/DME/DOL/
LiNO3 based-electrolytes, which have been thought to aggravate
the polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttle eﬀects. Here we challenge
this conventional wisdom and demonstrate outstanding capabilities
of a dilute (0.1 mol L−1 of LiTFSI in DME/DOL with 1 wt. %
LiNO3) electrolyte to enable better electrode wetting, greatly
improved high-rate capability, and stable cycle performance for high sulfur loading cathodes and low electrolyte/sulfur ratio in Li−S
cells. Overall, the presented study shines light on the extraordinary ability of such electrolyte systems to suppress short-chain
polysulﬁde dissolution and polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀects.
KEYWORDS: Li−S, Super-Diluted, Electrolyte, Sulfur Cathode, Battery
■INTRODUCTION
As the world faces a rapidly growing demand to move to
renewable energy sources and to drastically reduce carbon
emissions, battery technologies need to oﬀer improved energy
storage for electric cars, busses and trucks, drones, electric
planes, and the grid.1−4 For large-scale transportation and
many mobile applications, fabrication of lightweight and stable
batteries is particularly important, while reducing cathode
toxicity remains imperative for low environmental impact.2,5
The lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery system oﬀers a combina-
tion of a very low weight, low toxicity, low cost, and high
speciﬁc energy. With one of the highest theoretical capacities
(1675 mAh·g−1) of any conversion-type cathode, the pursuit of
long-lasting and stable Li−S batteries remains a global research
focus for the past decade.2,3,6−8
In the Li−S conversion chemistry studies, the most
commonly used electrolyte comprises ∼1 M lithium bis-
(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) salt solution in
distilled dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DIOX)
(1:1, v/v) solvent with 1−2 wt. % of LiNO3 additive.9−11
The reduction of S typically proceeds through a formation of a
series of polysulﬁde species, which are for the most part soluble
in common organic solvents and electrolytes. Successful
commercialization of Li−S batteries is severely hindered by
polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀects and strict requirements of low
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S: μL/mg) ratio and high S mass loading
cathodes in commercial cells.12−14 The polysulﬁde dissolution
and the migration of redox-active species cause the loss of
active S and thus reversible capacity, self-discharge, low
Coulombic eﬃciency (CE), and unfavorable layer formation
on both S cathode and Li anode that induces resistance
growth. The lack of a passivating surface layer results in the
electrolyte being continuously consumed until it is exhausted
to the battery death. To achieve high energy densities of Li−S
batteries, the simultaneous employment of rather low E/S ratio
and high S mass loading cathode becomes essential.13−15 Such
requirements further increase the demands for improved
cathode stability against shuttle eﬀects, better electrolyte
wetting, faster reaction kinetics, and even more stable surface
layers to achieve long cycle life and reasonable charging rates.
In previous studies, composite particle design2,16−22 and
electrolyte modiﬁcation7,23−25 have been the most eﬀective
complementary strategies to overcome known challenges.

Porous carbons and polar inorganic compounds with diﬀerent
nanoarchitectures have been widely employed as S or Li2S
hosts.21,26−33 Optimizations of both liquid and solid electro-
lytes have proved to be particularly eﬀective in enhancing the
cyclability of a broad range of S/Li2S cathodes.23,34−37
So far, organic liquid electrolytes and their fundamental
studies dominate the mainstream due to their high ionic
conductivity. To protect active material from shuttle eﬀects,
functional additives (or Li salts) have been reported to induce
in situ formation of solid electrolyte interphase to enhance the
stability and performance of S/Li2S cathodes.25,38−41 In
addition, −NH or NH4
+ based additives in electrolytes have
been reported to adjust the solubility of Li2S or Li2S2, aiming
to improve the conversion reaction kinetics.42,43 For
preventing the polysulﬁde shuttle in Li−S cells, a novel
electrolyte solution of 0.2 mol·L−1 containing low-ion-pairing
salt (Li[Al(Ohﬁp)4]) and a triﬂamide solvent (N,N-dimethyl
triﬂimide, DMT) have been developed.44 In addition, 0.2 mol·
L−1 solution of novel salt Li[B(OTfe)4] in standard solvents
(DME/DOL) has been investigated in Li−S cells.45 Since
2013, ultrasaturated salt-solvent mixtures have become a
popular avenue to combat the polysulﬁde dissolution due to
smaller fraction of solvent molecules not participating in Li+
and TFSI−solvation and available for solvating the
polysulﬁdes.46−49 However, such electrolytes typically display
high viscosity, low Li+ mobility, and poor wetting on S
cathodes, resulting in low capacity utilization, low rate
capability, and reduced practically attainable energy density,
particularly for low E/S ratio and high mass loading
cathodes.9,50 In contrast, one literature recently showed that
0.1−0.5 M LiTFSI with 0.4 M LiNO3 in DME/DOL
demonstrated better rate capability and smaller voltage
hysteresis but worse cycle stability in Li−S cells than those
in 1 M standard electrolytes.9 Overall, especially for the
standard electrolyte system of LiTFSI/LiNO3/DME/DOL,
little attention has been paid to dilute and ultradilute
electrolytes, which have been thought to aggravate the
polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttle eﬀects because, according
to a solution theory, a higher fraction of free solvents for
solvation should result in stronger and faster solvency.
Here we challenge this conventional wisdom and demon-
strate outstanding capabilities of dilute (0.1 M) electrolytes to
enable better electrode wetting, greatly improved high-rate
capability, and stable cycle performance for high active mass
loading S cathodes and low E/S ratio in Li−S cells. We prepare
such dilute (0.1 M) electrolyte using LiTFSI salt and solvents
(DME/DOL, 1:1) with 1 wt. % LiNO3 and yet attain rather
unique and unexpected properties. Using a powerful
combination of post-mortem analyses and molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations, we explain our discovery of abnormal
reductions in short-chain polysulﬁde dissolution and migration
of soluble sulfur species in dilute electrolytes. As additional
beneﬁts, the dilute electrolytes are less expensive, lighter, and
less viscous than both standard 1 M and high-M electrolytes
which may open new avenues for the exploration and
overcoming challenges of other battery systems.

The shape of the discharge/charge proﬁles reveals the
voltage curves typical for Li−S chemistry with two plateaus
corresponding to S8 →Li2S4 (the ﬁrst, shorter, higher voltage
plateau) and Li2S4→Li2S (the second, longer, lower voltage
plateau).52 In a standard 1 M electrolyte, the S cathodes
displayed high capacity (1265 mAh·g−1) and high second
galvanostatic discharge voltage plateau (∼2.1 V vs Li/Li+;
voltage hysteresis ∼0.15 V) at a slow rate of C/10, gradually
reducing to ∼50% of the initial capacity (650 mAh·g−1 at 2 C)
and ∼1.8 V vs Li/Li+ (voltage hysteresis of ∼0.65 V) at 2 C
(Figure 1a), indicating a limited rate performance, as may be
expected for relatively thick and unprotected from dissolution/
shuttle eﬀects CC-S cathodes. Increasing electrolyte concen-
tration to 2 (Figure 1b), 3 (Figure S5), and ﬁnally to 5 M
(Figure 1c) noticeably reduced capacity utilization and
increased voltage hysteresis at high rates. In fact, cells with
concentration electrolytes showed very low speciﬁc capacity
(100−300 mAh·g−1) at 1 C and even smaller (50−100 mAh·
g−1) at 2 C (Figures 1b and 2c). We could not even distinguish
two discharge plateaus in such cells at 1−2 C rates, indicating
rather poor reaction kinetics in concentrated electrolytes.
Quite remarkably, reducing LiTFSI salt concentration to 0.1 M
resulted in both high accessible capacity and dramatically
improved rate performance (∼75% of the initial capacity or
956 mAh·g−1 at 1 C and 828 mAh·g−1 at 2 C) with noticeably
smaller distortions of the voltage curves and smaller voltage
hysteresis growth with increasing rates (voltage hysteresis of
only ∼0.4 V at 2 C) when compared to cells with 1, 2, 3, and 5
M electrolytes (Figure 1a−e).
Besides suﬃciently high rates, here again low molarity
electrolytes showed superior performance (Figure 1e,f). When
cells were cycled at moderate C/2 rate for 200 cycles, both 1
and 2 M electrolytes resulted in a visible capacity degradation

Post-mortem analysis of the electrodes revealed a dramatic
impact of salt concentration on the morphologies of both Li
anodes and CC-S cathodes. Compared to the SEM images of a
fresh CC-S cathode in Figure S8a, the surface of a cycled CC-S
electrode in 1 M electrolyte becomes rough and fully covered
by a thick surface layer containing reprecipitated particles
(Figure 2a), as could be expected from prior-art studies.25,39 In
addition, the cycled Li metal anode in 1 M electrolyte shows a
rough and highly uneven surface covered with nonuniform
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer (Figure S8c)
comprising precipitated Li2S nanoparticles and large Li metal
grains emerging through the SEI/Li2S layer (Figure 2c). The
dissolved and reprecipitated polysulﬁdes are known to be
associated with the formation of Li2S in the anode (Figure 2c),
the losses of active material and capacity decay (Figure 1f), as
well as the increased cell resistance (Figure 2e).7,52 In sharp
contrast, the CC-S cathode cycled in the 0.1 M electrolyte
shows only marginal surface changes and the absence of
unfavorable particles (or layer) after cycling (Figures 2b and
S8b), in agreement with the previously discussed cycling
stability results (Figure 1f). Meanwhile, the Li foil cycled in a
0.1 M electrolyte exhibits a very smooth surface morphology
with homogeneous large Li grains and thin and dense SEI layer
without any visible precipitates and vertical dendrites (Figures
2d and S8d), conﬁrming that the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀects
have been successfully suppressed. These ﬁndings are also
consistent with no visible increase of charge-transfer resistance
in the 0.1 M electrolyte cell (Figure 2f). The cycled electrolytes
of both 0.1 and 1 M were collected to identify possible color
changes (Figure S9). As expected, the color of a cycled 1 M
benchmark electrolyte turned into visible yellow that is much
deeper than the color of a cycled 0.1 M electrolyte, also
indicating that the polysulﬁde shuttle and S loss were
noticeably reduced in the 0.1 M electrolyte.

In order to reveal the underlying mechanism(s) for superior
performance of dilute electrolytes in Li−S batteries, we
analyzed physical properties and interactions with polysulﬁdes
and conducted MD simulations. When compared to standard
(1 M) and concentrated (2, 3, 5, 7 M) electrolytes (some of
which are often called “solvent-in-salt” electrolytes), a dilute
(0.1 M) electrolyte utilizes much less salt and oﬀers a smaller
density (Figure 3a,c), which are beneﬁcial for achieving low
cost and higher speciﬁc energy in Li−S batteries. Through the
ionic conductivity studies of 0.1, 1, 3, 5, and 7 M electrolytes,
electrolytes with diﬀerent molarities indeed display diﬀerent
ionic conductivities that are aﬀected by both ion concentration
and ionic mobility. As shown in Figure 3b, the ionic
conductivity of 0.1 M electrolyte (1.78 mS·cm−1) is lower
than that of 1 M (12.16 mS·cm−1). Since the rate performance
of a cell is determined by Li+ solvation/dissociation and the
subsequent Li+ migration,54 both the electrolyte viscosity and
wettability of the electrode by the electrolyte are important
factors for cell manufacturing rate and cell cycling rate. We
investigated the impact of salt concentration on electrolyte
viscosity and the CC-S cathode wetting (Figure 3d,e). The
dilute (0.1 M) electrolyte enables extremely small viscosity
(∼0.6 cP or mPa·s), which correlates with high Li ion mobility
(see Table S2). The wetting of the CC-S cathode by this (0.1
M) electrolyte is also excellent (Figure 3d) with the contact
angle of a mere 15.8° at 0.06 s, while standard 1 M and
moderately concentrated 3 M electrolytes display contact
angles of 24.1° and 72.1°, respectively. The good wetting, low
viscosity, and high Li ion mobility of this dilute electrolyte may
explain fast the Li+ transport at the solid−liquid interface and
higher capacity utilization and lower voltage hysteresis at high
C rates (Figure 2).
MD simulations of short-chain polysulﬁde Li2S2 placed in a
dilute (0.1 M) electrolyte show a very strong aggregation of
Li2S2 (Figure 4a and Figure S11), suggesting a very low Li2S2
polysulﬁde solubility.11 LiTFSI is largely dissociated in low
LiTFSI concentrations with TFSI−anions being well dispersed
(Figure 4a) while the Li+ cations are strongly bound to the
S2
(2‑) anions (Figure S12a), thus bridging the Li2S2 into a well-
deﬁned domain (Figure 4a) that indicates a strong phase
separation of short-chain polysulﬁde in the electrolyte.
Increasing the LiTFSI salt concentration from 0.1 to 1 M
increases Li-TFSI aggregation (see higher peak for Li−
O(TFSI) in Figure S12). As a result, the TFSI−anions more
frequently become binding to Li+ and thus screening the
desired Li+ and polysulﬁde interactions. This screening

electrolytes. According to previous reports, the formation of

explanation, fundamentally the lattice energy of the salt Li2S2 is
higher than that of Li2S4, Li2S6 and Li2S8 (larger anion size).
Thus, the long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sx, x = 4, 6, 8) have
higher solubilities in DOL/DME. Yet, the situation changes if
one compares the solvating properties of the 0.1 and 1.0 M
LiTFSI electrolytes. Although the solvents are the same
(DOL/DME), the ionic strength of the solution, which
corresponds to the capacity to dissolve smaller ions with
higher lattice energies, increases from 0.1 to 1.0 M. This easily
explains the reduced solubility of the smaller Li2S2 compound
in the lower ionic strength electrolyte (0.1 M). In addition, this
explanation is in agreement with the MD simulations that also
showed an excellent agreement with NMR (see Figure S14).

In Figure 5, the lithium polysulﬁde dissolution experiments
were conducted in pure solvent, dilute (0.1 M), standard (1
M), and concentrated (3 M) electrolytes to see the color
changes with time (which are indicative of the formation,
dissolution, and diﬀusion of mixed long-chain polysulﬁdes).
Similar to previous results (Figure 4) and prior art studies,47
the lithium polysulﬁde formation, dissolution, and diﬀusion
into the bulk of the electrolyte are somewhat limited in
concentrated electrolytes (3 M electrolyte in Figure 5) when
compared with 1 M electrolyte. In a comparison among the
results in pure solvents, 0.1, and 1 M solutions, the observed
deep yellow color indicates the rapid formation of long-chain
polysulﬁdes (might be the mixture of S8
2−, S6
2−, and S4
2−) at
room temperature and demonstrates the strong solvency of
long-chain lithium polysulﬁdes in three solutions. However, in
the pure solvent and 0.1 M solutions the dissolved S species
diﬀuse even more slowly, not only when compared with 1 M
but also with 3 M electrolytes (Figure 5), which means the
polysulﬁde shuttles eﬀects are largely reduced in the 0.1 M
electrolyte. More LiTFSI salt in 1 M electrolyte aggravates the
diﬀusion of polysulﬁdes. This unexpected behavior (sup-
pressed polysulﬁde diﬀusion/shuttle) is correlated with a
signiﬁcantly more pronounced aggregation of Li2S2 and their
mixtures with Li2S4 observed in MD simulations of dilute
electrolytes (Figure 4). Slower polysulﬁde diﬀusion leads to
better cycle stability (Figure 1f,g), smooth surface morphology
of cycled electrodes (Figure 2b,d), and no change of charge-
transfer resistance during cycling (Figure 2f) in the 0.1 M
electrolyte.
In summary, we have demonstrated an unexpected ability of
the dilute electrolyte (0.1 mol·L−1 of LiTFSI in DME/DOL
with 1 wt. % LiNO3) to oﬀer outstanding performance in high-
energy Li−S batteries. Because of very low viscosity, excellent
wetting on sulfur cathodes, and dramatically reduced short-
chain polysulﬁde dissolution and polysulﬁdes shuttle eﬀects,
the carbon−sulfur cathodes displayed much higher rate
capability and better cell stability (in terms of both capacity
retention and the lack of growth in charge transfer resistance)

polysulﬁde dissolution experiments conﬁrmed a signiﬁcantly

MD simulations shine more light on the superior performance
of dilute LiTFSI-based electrolytes: at low salt concentrations
(0.1 M LiTFSI) Li+ cations bridge Li2S2 into large phase-

tion into the bulk of the electrolyte; aggregations of Li2S4 via
participation in the Li2S2 aggregates (forming Li2S2−Li2S4
clusters) lead to slowing down of not only Li2S2 diﬀusion
but also Li2S4 diﬀusion at 0.1 M (vs 1 M LiTFSI). Therefore,
dilute electrolytes may become more suitable for the next-
generation Li−S batteries and possibly other conversion-type
cathode chemistries. We call for future studies of dilute and
ultradilute electrolytes on high sulfur loadings and low E/S
ratios to be carried out in Li−S pouch cells.
