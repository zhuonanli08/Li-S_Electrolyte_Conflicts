Synergistic Effect of High Donor
Electrolyte and Catalytic Separator for
Practical Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Yongxin Xiao†, Qianhui He†, Lin Peng, Jingwen Liu, Xiaoxian Guan, Luyi Chen*, Yue-Peng Cai* & Qifeng Zheng*
School of Chemistry, Guangzhou Key Laboratory of Materials for Energy Conversion and Storage, South China Normal University,
Guangzhou, 510006 Guangdong
*Corresponding authors: chenluyi@m.scnu.edu.cn; caiyp@scnu.edu.cn; Qifeng.Zheng@m.scnu.edu.cn; †Y. Xiao and Q. He contributed
equally to this work.
Cite this: Renewables 2024, 2, 364–374
DOI: 10.31635/renewables.024.202400065
The commercialization of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries has
been hampered by the low utilization of S, resulting in low
practical energy density and the severe shuttle effect of
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) that leads to poor cycle life.
Herein, a combined strategy of electrolyte engineering and
separator functionalization was proposed to solve the con-
ﬂict between high S utilization and cycling life. We have
demonstrated that the high donor electrolyte regulates
the solvation of LiPSs with the formation of S3•−to induce
radical-assisted efﬁcient conversion pathway and three-
dimensional (3D) Li2S deposition, which greatly enhanced
the S utilization while exacerbating the shuttling of LiPSs.
Fortunately, the carbon nanosheet-based modiﬁed separator
with abundant Zn-Co diatomic metal sites efﬁciently inhib-
ited the shuttling of LiPSs by catalyzing the conversion
reaction of LiPSs. Hence, the resulting Li–S battery delivered
a remarkably high capacity of 1300 mAh g−1 with a high
average coulombic efﬁciency of 99.4% during cycling. Even
at a high S mass loading (9.3 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte
condition (E/S = 3 μL mg−1), the Li–S battery still delivered a
high capacity of 1088 mAh g−1, representing a signiﬁcant
advancement in designing practically high energy Li–S bat-
teries with long cycle life.
Keywords: Li–S battery, high donor electrolyte, catalytic sep-
arator, sulfur utilization, synergistic effect
Introduction
With the rapid development of portable electronic devices,
electric vehicles, and large-scale energy storage, it is urgent to
develop the next generation of advanced rechargeable batteries
with higher energy density. Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries with
Li metal as anode and S as cathode could achieve a remarkable
energy density of up to 2600 Wh kg−1, based on the complete
conversion from S (solid-state) via liquid-state intermediates
(lithium polysulﬁdes, LiPSs, partially dissolved in electrolytes) to
the ﬁnal discharged product of Li sulﬁde (Li2S, solid-state).1–3
However,
this
multielectron,
multistep,
and
multiphase
conversion reaction (denoted as “3M”) during the charge/dis-
charge process brings critical challenges for the practical appli-
cation of Li–S batteries. First, the electronic and ionic insulating
nature of both S and Li2S results in sluggish conversion kinetics,
which causes large polarization and low S utilization during
cycling, leading to practically low capacity and energy density.
Second, the notorious shuttle effect of LiPSs not only causes the
rapid loss of S active material but also continuously reacts with
Li-metal anode, which gives rise to the depletion of limited Li
reservoir and accumulation of dead Li, leading to low Coulombic
efﬁciency (CE) and rapid failure of Li–S batteries.4–7

In the past few decades, intensive research efforts have been
proposed to address those challenges with improved battery
performance such as structural tailoring of S cathodes,8–10
passivation of Li-metal anodes,11–13 functionalization of separa-
tors,14–17 and optimization of electrolytes.18–20 In particular, a
radical-based conversion pathway has recently been proposed
to enable high S utilization by engineering electrolytes with high
Gutmann donor number (DN) solvents or anions, including
dimethyl acetamide (DMA),21 dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO),22 1,3-
dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone,23 and bromide (Br−) anion.24 These
high donor electrolytes not only promote the solubility of LiPSs
but also alter the conversion pathway with the formation of tri-
sulfur radical intermediate (S3•−), which signiﬁcantly accelerates
the conversion kinetics and boosts the S utilization, even in
instances of lean electrolyte and high S loading.21,25 Furthermore,
in contrast to the two-dimensional (2D) Li2S ﬁlms formed in low
donor electrolytes that inevitably passivate the cathodes, the
high donor electrolytes endow the Li2S with three-dimensional
(3D) deposition morphology, which keeps the conductive elec-
trode surface available for repeated charge/discharge over cy-
cling without passivation, further enhancing the utilization of S.
Although the effective solvation of LiPSs, S3•−-based conversion
pathway, and 3D deposition of Li2S favors S utilization and
conversion kinetics, the high donor electrolytes aggravate the
shuttle effect of LiPSs, which exacerbates the corrosion of
Li-metal anode, leading to low CE and short cycle life with at
most dozens of cycles. For instance, by using a high DN solvent of
DMA, an initial capacity of more than 1300 mAh g−1 was
achieved; however, the capacity rapidly failed after only ﬁve
cycles.21
In an attempt to suppress the shuttle effect of LiPSs, numer-
ous kinds of functional materials with trapping effect merits,26–28
shield effect,29–31 catalytic effect,32–34 or sieve effect35,36 for LiPSs
have been introduced to design functionalized separators for
Li–S batteries. The above-mentioned strategies to suppress the
shuttle effect of LiPSs were mainly realized through intermolec-
ular interactions such as Van der Waals force,26,37 chemical
bonding force,38,39 electrostatic force,40,41 fast charge transfer
pathway,42 and ion-sieving.43–45 Also, introducing a high-efﬁcien-
cy catalyst within a functionalized separator to facilitate the
conversion process of sulfur species has been seen as one of the
most
effective
strategies.46,47
Moreover,
the
functionalized
separators have been demonstrated that could also suppress
the growth of Li dendrites via homogenizing electric ﬁeld or ion
ﬂux, which effectively stabilizes the Li-metal anodes and extends
the battery cycle life.48–50 Unfortunately, up to now, how to
maintain the prolonged cycle lifespan while achieving a high
S utilization (i.e., high capacity) remains an unsolved challenge
for current Li–S battery design.
Herein, inspired by the electrolyte engineering and separator
functionalization, a combined design conﬁguration was pro-
posed for a high energy density Li–S battery with a long cycle
life by using a high DN cosolvent and carbon nanosheet-based
modiﬁed separator simultaneously. In this concept, tetramethy-
lurea (TMU, DN = 31.0 kcal mol−1)51 with high solubility for LiPSs
was chosen as a cosolvent to initiate S3•−-based conversion
pathway, endowing high S utilization and rapid conversion
kinetics. On the other hand, the carbon nanosheet-based modi-
ﬁed separator with abundant Zn-Co diatomic metal sites was
adopted to further boost the conversion kinetics and suppress
the shuttle effect of LiPSs, which signiﬁcantly extended the cycle
lifetime of Li–S battery with high S utilization. Furthermore, the
interrelation of accelerated redox kinetics of LiPSs with S utiliza-
tion and shuttle effect was comprehensively studied. Beneﬁtting
from the radical-assisted conversion pathway in TMU-based
high DN electrolyte and suppression of LiPSs shuttling by carbon
nanosheet-based modiﬁed separator, the resulting Li–S battery
delivered a remarkably high capacity of 1300 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C;
additionally, it demonstrated a high capacity retention of 76%
after 100 cycles at 0.3 C with a high average CE of 99.4%.
Results and Discussion
Design principle of high-energy Li–S batteries with
electrolyte engineering and separator
functionalization
In consideration of the 3M conversion reaction in conventional
ether-based electrolyte, how to overcome the low capacity
resulting from low S utilization and rapid loss of active S
material, as well as sluggish reaction kinetics remain severe
challenges for practical Li–S batteries with high energy density.
Owing to its high solubility to LiPSs and formation of radical
intermediate (i.e., S3•−) that results in different conversion path-
ways with enhanced kinetics, a series of high DN electrolytes
deliver great potential to achieve high S utilization. TMU, a
representative solvent with a high DN of 31.0 kcal mol−1 and
good compatibility for Li-metal anode, was chosen as the cosol-
vent to regulate the electrolyte solution properties and acceler-
ate the conversion reaction of LiPSs (Supporting Information
Figure S1). To suppress the aggravated shuttle effect of LiPSs in
TMU-based high DN electrolytes, a carbon nanosheet-based
modiﬁed separator with abundant diatomic metal catalytic sites
was developed to provide catalytic and sieving effects for LiPSs.
Therefore, in combination with electrolyte engineering and
separator functionalization, high S utilization and extended
cycling lifespan could simultaneously be implemented (Figure 1).
As shown in Figure 2a, the conventional ether solvents of 1, 2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1, 3-dioxolane (DOL) (1:1 by volume,
denoted as DD-sol) could dissolve long chain Li2Sx (6≤x≤8) to a
certain extent, while their solubility for short chain Li2Sx (2≤x≤4)
was extremely low. By introducing the high DN solvent of TMU
to DD-sol, the resulting TMU/DME/DOL mixed solvents (1:1:1 by
volume, denoted as TDD-sol) exhibited much higher solubility
for all LiPSs intermediates (Li2Sx, 2≤x≤8) than that in DD-sol,
indicating that TDD-sol had a much stronger solvation ability for
Li+ than DD-sol. The solvation ability was further veriﬁed by
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. As shown in
the 7Li NMR spectra, the TDD-sol displayed a lower chemical
shift, indicating that TMU had a stronger solvation ability for Li+
than DD-sol, which could promote the dissolution of LiPSs
(Figure 2b). Generally, the insoluble LiPSs tended to precipitate

the high DN solvent could effectively stabilize the S3•−, which
offered an additional radical-assisted conversion pathway for
promoting the 3M conversion reaction that largely increased S
utilization, especially during the disproportionation of long-
order LiPSs,29 representing the conspicuous advantage of the
TMU-based high DN electrolyte for high energy density Li–S

In light of the high solubility of LiPSs in TDD-sol, it is note-
worthy that their shuttle effect might be exacerbated in such
high DN electrolytes. Therefore, extra measures to suppress the
shuttle effect should be taken, of which a type of carbon
nanosheet with a zinc-cobalt catalytic center (denoted as
ZnCo-CN), as reported in our earlier work,52 was employed to
modify the polypropylene (PP) separator in this study. The ZnCo-
CN was obtained through the carbonization of Zn-Co complex-
based precursor, which was synthesized through the reaction of
4-tertbutyl-2,6-diformylphenol, 1,2-benzenediamine, cobalt chlo-
ride, and zinc chloride.The resulting ZnCo-CN exhibited a distinct
peak of amorphous carbon-based X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern
(Supporting Information Figure S4) with remarkable lamellar
morphology (Supporting Information Figure S5). As shown in
Supporting Information Figure S4, a small amount of Zn-Co
diatomic metal sites was observed, resulting from the structural
inheritance and high carbonization of Zn-Co complex, as well as
partly evaporated Zn preventing the agglomeration of Co atoms
at high temperatures (800 °C).53 Since Zn has a lithophilic effect
and Co has a sulfurophilic effect, the Zn-Co bimetallic site was
very conducive to promoting the adsorption and conversion of
LiPSs. Additionally, the elemental mapping result displayed an
abundance and uniform distribution of N (Supporting Informa-
tion Figure S6). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area
of as-prepared ZnCo-CN was up to 447 m2 g−1 with a uniform
microporous size of 0.39 nm (Supporting Information Figure S7).
The as-prepared ZnCo-CN might offer the following merits for
Li–S batteries: (1) the readily accessible noncarbon elements
could serve as the chemisorption sites for LiPSs, (2) the ZnCo
diatomic metal sites could provide a catalytic center for the
efﬁcient conversion of LiPSs, and (3) the microporous structure
could also prevent the shuttle effect of LiPSs via sieving effect.
The physical adsorption of the micropores of ZnCo-CN towards
LiPSs was ﬁrst investigated. After adding 0.8 mg ZnCo-CN
powder to the 0.2 mM Li2S6 solution of TDD-sol, the solution
quickly turned colorless within 1 h (Supporting Information
Figure S8), suggesting that ZnCo-CN could effectively adsorb
the LiPSs. Then the as-prepared ZnCo-CN was uniformly coated
on the surface of the PP separator to fabricate the ZnCo-CN
modiﬁed separator (namely, ZnCo-CN/PP). As shown in Support-
ing Information Figure S9, the separator displayed a ﬂat surface
with a coating layer thickness of ∼43 μm. Even after bending, the
separator demonstrated good integrality, demonstrating its
excellent ﬂexibility. Besides, the ZnCo-CN/PP exhibited a smaller
contact angle towards TMU/DME/DOL electrolyte (denoted as
TDD) than that of PP with DME/DOL electrolyte (denoted as DD),
indicating its good wettability (Supporting Information Figure
S10), attributed to the high BET surface area and polar group of
the ZnCo-CN layer.
To verify the suppression of the shuttle effect of ZnCo-CN, the
permeation experiment of LiPSs was carried out in a customized
device. As shown in Figure 2e, for the Li2S6 solution of DD-sol, the
LiPSs quickly permeate through the PP separator after 4 h as the
external pure DD-sol turns yellow, proving that the PP separator
was unable to block the LiPSs. An even darker yellow color was
observed for the permeation testing in TDD-sol with a PP
separator (Figure 2f), revealing the severe shuttle of LiPSs in
high DN solvent (i.e., TDD-sol). By replacing the PP with a ZnCo-
CN/PP separator, the shuttle effect of LiPSs was signiﬁcantly
suppressed as the external pure TDD-sol remained colorless after
6 h (Figure 2g), beneﬁcial for reserving the S active material on
the cathode side and boosting the utilization of S to achieve a
high capacity.
Fast conversion kinetics of LiPSs
According to the 3M reaction mechanism of Li–S batteries, the
reaction from soluble Li2S6 to solid Li2S is primarily responsible
for three-quarters of the capacity. This reaction also has been
regarded as the most crucial step to achieve high S utilization
during the LiPSs redox process. The LiPSs redox process was ﬁrst
explored by assembling the symmetrical cells using electrolytes
with 0.2 M Li2S8 or Li2S6. As shown in Supporting Information
Figure S11, compared with that in DD, the cyclic voltammetry (CV)
curves (i.e., blue in color) of both Li2S8 and Li2S6 in TDD displayed
an intensive pair of redox peaks, indicating the improved con-
version kinetics. This could be attributed to the formation of S3•−
in TDD that initiated an efﬁcient conversion pathway. Moreover,
together with the ZnCo-CN/PP separator, the pair of redox peaks
were further enhanced with lower overpotential, suggesting
that ZnCo-CN could serve as an effective catalytic material for
the fast conversion of LiPSs in the system with high DN electro-
lyte. Subsequently, to explore the actual reaction kinetics of
various cells during operation, Li||Li2S6 cells with DD, TDD, DD
+ZnCo-CN, and TDD+ZnCo-CN were assembled for the CV test.
As shown in Supporting Information Figure S12, the Li||Li2S6 cell
with TDD+ZnCo-CN showed the highest current value and the
largest area of redox peaks, indicating the fastest conversion
kinetics and highest S utilization. Additionally, the Tafel curves
derived from linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) tests were further
analyzed to explore the electrochemical conversion efﬁciency of
LiPSs. Normally, the smaller Tafel slope and higher exchange
current density indicated the faster conversion kinetics. As
shown in Supporting Information Figure S13, with the aid of
high DN electrolyte and catalytic ZnCo-CN/PP separator simul-
taneously (i.e., TDD+ZnCo-CN), the cell exhibited the largest
exchange current density of 0.36 mA cm−2. Furthermore, the
Tafel slopes of the cells with DD, TDD, and TDD+ZnCo-CN were
93.2, 56.6, and 46.5 mV dec−1, respectively. This result indicated
that the conversion of LiPSs could be efﬁciently accelerated by
combining electrolyte engineering with high DN solvent and
separator functionalization with the catalytic property.

Electrochemical deposition and morphology of Li2S
As the ﬁnal discharge product, Li2S, its electrochemical deposi-
tion behavior has been recognized as the key to achieving high
capacity of Li–S batteries, of which the deposition could be
divided into the process of nucleation and growth. As shown
in Figure 4a–c, the 1st discharge proﬁles of Li||Li2S8 cells with DD,
TDD, and TDD+ZnCo-CN exhibited two different plateaus. The
discharge capacity at the high plateau (near 2.4 V) and the low
plateau (at about 2.1 V) were denoted as QHigh and QLow,
respectively, which QHigh represented the transformation process
of S8 into long chain LiPSs (Li2Sx, 6≤x≤8), and QLow was ascribed
to the conversion process of short chain LiPSs (Li2Sx, x≤6) to
Li2S2/Li2S. In theory, QLow represented 75% of the total theoretical
capacity of S, which was about 1256 mAh g−1.
Notably, QLow was related to the deposition of Li2S and has
been identiﬁed as one of the primary parameters to assess the
utilization of S in Li–S batteries.54 Unfortunately, the discharge

LiPSs was further accelerated, which resulted in large particle-
like Li2S deposits (Figure 4i), promoting the rapid migration of
electrons and ions, thereby enabling high S utilization with a
high capacity (Figure 4f).

S16. At 0.1 C, the ratio of QLow/QHigh (2.4) in TDD+ZnCo-CN was
close to that in TDD (2.3), both of which were much higher than
that in DD (only 0.5), proving that radical-assisted pathway
offered by high DN electrolyte could lead to more effectively
Li2S deposition and improve the utilization of S. Meanwhile, as
shown by QHigh+QLow in Supporting Information Figure S16, the
modiﬁed separator could further increase the total discharge
capacity to 1300 mAh g−1.
The long-term cycling performance of the Li||Li2S6 cells was
then evaluated at 0.3 C. As shown in Figure 5b, the initial
capacity of the cell with DD was only 386 mAh g−1, which was
largely improved to 881 mAh g−1 for the cell with high DN
electrolyte (i.e., TDD). However, owing to the enhanced LiPSs
solubility that might have caused aggravated shuttle effect and
Li-metal corrosion, the capacity of the cell with TDD decayed
rapidly with only 43% retained after 100 cycles. Markedly, the
initial capacity of the cell with TDD+ZnCo-CN was increased to
1035 mAh g−1, and the cell demonstrated very stable cycling with
more than 76% capacity retained after 100 cycles, representing
one of the best cycling performances for Li–S battery with high
capacity of ≥1000 mAh g−1 (Figure 5c and Supporting Informa-
tion Table S2).19,21,23,55–60 The average CE during cycling was
nearly 100% (i.e., 99.4%), indicating that the severe shuttle of
LiPSs in TDD was largely preserved by the catalytic and sieving
function of the ZnCo-CN. Moreover, according to the charge-
discharge proﬁle in Figure 5d, the cell with TDD+ZnCo-CN also
exhibited a very low ΔE of only 0.27 V, much lower than that
with DD (0.38 V) and TDD (0.42 V) (Supporting Information
Figure S17), owing to the rapid conversion kinetics in TDD+
ZnCo-CN.

Given that LiPSs have high reactivity with Li-metal, we envis-
aged that their enhanced solubility in TDD might worsen the
issue. Therefore, the Li-metal anode after cycling was analyzed.
As shown in Supporting Information Figure S18a,b, for the
Li-metal cycled in DD or TDD, the surface turned yellow due to
the contamination of LiPSs. Furthermore, porous and loose Li
deposits were observed in the SEM images, as an indication of
dead Li resulting from the corrosion of LiPSs. In contrast, a silver
and clean appearance was found for the Li-metal cycled with
TDD+ZnCo-CN, where the dense and compact Li deposition
morphology was validated in the SEM image (Supporting Infor-
mation Figure S18c), again demonstrating that the ZnCo-CN
could suppress the shuttle of LiPSs and the corrosion of Li-metal,
even in high DN electrolytes.
To validate the effectiveness of this rationally combined strate-
gy in practical Li–S batteries, a Li||S cell was assembled using a
carbon nanotube/sulfur composite cathode (CNT/S) with an S
loading of 1.3 mg cm−2. As shown in Supporting Information
Figure S19, the initial discharge capacity of the cell with TDD
+ZnCo-CN is up to 1170 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, corresponding to the S
utilization of 70%, much higher than those with DD and TDD.
Furthermore, the cell with TDD+ZnCo-CN delivered the lowest ΔE
value of 0.20 V and the lowest fc value of 0.60. When the S mass
loading was increased to 4.0 mg cm−2, the cell with TDD+ZnCo-CN
still exhibited a high initial capacity of 1126 mAh g−1 (correspond-
ing to the S utilization of 67%) and was able to maintain more
than 77% capacity after 45 cycles at 0.1 C (Supporting Information
Figure S20). As well known, both lean electrolyte and high S
loading mass are important to achieve high energy density for Li–
S batteries. Therefore, to further demonstrate the superiority of
this combined strategy, the Li||S cell with a high S mass loading of
9.3 mg cm−2 and a low E/S ratio of 3 μL g−1 was assembled, which
still delivered a high capacity of 1088 mAh g−1 (Supporting
Information Figure S21), corresponding to a high areal capacity
of 10.2 mAh cm−2 and a high S utilization of 65%.
Conclusion
We herein propose a combined strategy of electrolyte engineer-
ing and separator functionalization for Li–S batteries to over-
come the longstanding contradiction between high S utilization
and cycling life. This systematical studies have demonstrated
that the high DN electrolyte greatly enhanced the solvation of
LiPSs, as well as stabilizes the S3•−to induce radical-assisted
efﬁcient conversion pathway and 3D Li2S deposition, promoting
the S utilization whereas aggravating the shuttle effect of LiPSs.
Furthermore, the carbon nanosheet with abundant Zn-Co di-
atomic metal sites coated on the separator further boosted the
conversion kinetics and suppressed the shuttle effect of LiPSs,
which prevented the corrosion of the Li-metal anode while
preserving high capacity. As a result, this combined strategy
not only endowed the Li–S battery with a high capacity of
1300 mAh g−1 but also retained more than 76% capacity after
100 cycles with a high CE of 99.4%. Even at a high S mass loading
(9.3 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte condition (E/S = 3 μL mg−1), the
Li–S battery still delivered a high capacity of 1088 mAh g−1.
Therefore, this work demonstrates the effectiveness of employ-
ing high DN electrolyte and catalytic separator synergistically to
enhance both S utilization and cycling life of Li–S batteries,
thereby representing a signiﬁcant advancement in promoting
Li–S batteries with practically high energy density.
Methods
Materials characterization
XRD patterns were conducted by a diffractometer (D8 Advance,
Bruker) at 40 kV and 40 mA with a Cu target tube and a graphite
monochromator. The morphologies and element distributions of
materials were investigated using SEM (TESCAN Brno, s.r.o.
MAIA3) with an energy dispersive spectrometer (Oxford). The
speciﬁc surface area and pore size distribution were determined
using N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm (Belsorp-max). The
solubility of LiPSs was tested by a UV–vis absorption spectro-
photometer (Shimadzu, UV-2700). Raman spectra were obtained
by a QE Pro spectrometer (Ocean Optics) with an emission
wavelength of 532 nm. The viscosity of the electrolyte was
measured by a viscometer (SVM 3001, Anton Paar). The contact
angle was tested by a surface tensiometer (JC2000D3P,
Powereach).
Electrochemical measurements
Using an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox, Li||S and Li||Li2S6 cells were assem-
bled using CNT/S or Li2S6 as cathode and Li-metal as anode in a
standard 2032-type coin-cell conﬁguration. The amount of the
electrolyte (TDD or DD) was 30 μL with a PP or ZnCo-CN/PP
separator. The active mass loading of S was 1.3, 4.0, or 9.3 mg
cm−2 (for a high-loading test). The galvanostatic charge–
discharge tests were carried out on a LAND CT200A instrument
(Wuhan Jinnuo Electronic Co. Ltd.) at different current densities
within a voltage range of 1.6–2.8 V. The CV of symmetrical cells
