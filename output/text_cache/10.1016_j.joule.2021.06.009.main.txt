SUMMARY
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries promise high energy density for next-
generation energy storage systems, yet many challenges remain. Li-
S batteries follow a conversion chemistry, which radically differs
from intercalation-based lithium-ion batteries. Recently, it has
become clear that the chemistry of electrolyte solutions and their
ability to stabilize polysulﬁde Li2Sx species formed by sulfur reduc-
tion have a critical effect on energy density and cycling perfor-
mance. This review evaluates the key role of solution properties
and polysulﬁde solvation. Factors that determine the solvation are
discussed, including the solvent, salt, concentration, and interaction
with Li-polysulﬁde species. Three fundamental types of electrolyte
solution—moderately (conventional), sparingly, and highly solvat-
ing—are presented along with a multi-dimensional analysis of solu-
tion chemistry, polysulﬁde solubility, sulfur reaction pathway, Li2S
deposition, and solution quantity. The stability of lithium metal an-
odes with these solutions is discussed with respect to side reactions,
protective surface ﬁlm formation, and dendritic Li deposition.
Emphasis is placed on options to reduce the electrolyte solution/sul-
fur ratio and prolong battery cycle life. The advantages and disad-
vantages of the three systems are compared in accordance with
the multifaceted requirements. In conclusion, we offer our perspec-
tive for future development of Li-S batteries.
INTRODUCTION
Energy storage is becoming increasingly important for our technology-driven soci-
ety. Among various technologies, lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries play a critical role in
powering the rechargeable world, from portable devices to electric vehicles, and,
on a larger scale, in grid-scale energy storage for renewable energy resources
such as tidal and wind power.1 However, restricted by Li+ insertion/extraction chem-
istry, Li-ion batteries are approaching their energy density boundaries (250–300 Wh
kg1) and hardly meet the ever-increasing demand for energy in speciﬁc markets,
such as drones and long-distance transportation.2–4 Alternative systems that go
beyond the state-of-the-art Li-ion batteries are therefore of great interest.4
Elemental sulfur—which is abundant, cheap, and non-toxic—possesses a high spe-
ciﬁc capacity of 1,672 mAh g1 as a cathode material for lithium batteries.5,6 The
coupling of sulfur and lithium offers the highest theoretical energy density for any
pair of solid elements—up to 2,600 Wh kg1 or 2,800 Wh L1.5,7,8 In the past several
decades, great efforts have been made to tackle the intrinsic problems of lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) batteries, namely, insulating sulfur/lithium sulﬁde, the electrode volume

Arguably, one of the biggest challenges is the choice of a suitable electrolyte solu-
tion and solvation of the polysulﬁde moieties therein for Li-S batteries. The nature of
the solution plays a more important role in Li-S batteries than in conventional Li-ion
batteries, as it not only serves as an ionic conductor for mass transport but also par-
ticipates extensively in the conversion reactions of both lithium and sulfur. Lithium
stripping/plating, dendrite growth, effective passivation by solid electrolyte inter-
phase (SEI), and interference by side reactions are all inﬂuenced by the identity of
the electrolyte solution (Figure 1A). Importantly, polysulﬁde solvation is a major
manipulator dictating the sulfur reaction pathway. In the conventional ether-based
solutions, i.e., 1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.2 M
LiNO3 in equivolume 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)—
termed DOL/DME throughout this review—lithium polysulﬁdes are moderately dis-
solved to form the catholyte. Such a Li-S cell is in fact a liquid electrochemical system
where the reaction of solid S8/Li2S proceeds via dissolved polysulﬁdes (catholyte or
dissolution-precipitation mechanism).34,35 The effective solvation of polysulﬁdes fa-
vors sulfur utilization and reaction kinetics, but brings the notorious ‘‘shuttle effect,’’
which hampers the charging process back to elemental sulfur, limiting the practical
speciﬁc capacity of the sulfur cathode, and increasing lithium corrosion, with
concomitant low coulombic efﬁciency and short cycle life due to failure of the
corroded Li anode (Figure 1B).36,37
More profoundly, it is now widely realized that minimizing the quantity of the electrolyte
solution is essential for high energy density.38–41 For a Li-S cell to achieve 500 Wh kg1/
700 Wh L1, a low electrolyte solution/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 1 mL mg1 is required.42 How-
ever, the reported E/S ratios are usually higher than 5 mL mg1 in coin cells and approx-
imately 2–3 mL mg1 in pouch cells.41,43 Operation of Li-S cells in lean electrolyte systems
(<3 mL mg1) presents a daunting challenge, as the polysulﬁde solubility in moderately
solvating electrolyte solutions (MSEs) is limited to 4.7 mL mg1 (6.6 M Li2S8) in DOL/
DME.44 Polysulﬁde saturation results in high viscosity and low ionic conductivity, and
leads to sluggish kinetics for Li2S electrodeposition, with large polarization and inferior
capability.45,46Thesulfurredoxreactionsarethushighlydependentontheelectrolyteso-
lution volume; it is almost impossible to decrease the E/S ratio without altering the cath-
olyte mechanism. Furthermore, Li-S cells comprising a lean electrolyte catholyte are
complicatedbytheaggravatedshuttleandlithiumanodecorrosionthatformsLi2S,which
exaggerates anode pulverization, promotes degradation of the electrolyte solution, and
incurs premature cell failure.47–49
It is thus evident that polysulﬁde dissolution is a crucial dictator for the Li-S mecha-
nism and its performance in lean electrolyte systems. Therefore, long-lived and efﬁ-
cient operation of Li-S cells based on such systems requires a careful redesign of the
electrolyte solution with respect to polysulﬁde solubility. To this end, two concepts
were recently developed—sparingly solvating electrolyte solutions (SSEs) and highly
solvating electrolyte solutions (HSEs), featured respectively by suppressed and pro-
moted polysulﬁde dissociation.50–52

SSEs enable a fundamental alternation of polysulﬁdes from fully to sparingly sol-
vated,51 overcoming the drawbacks of catholyte cells with desired outcomes such
as alleviated shuttle and stable lithium anodes (Figure 1C). More importantly, the
alternation of polysulﬁde solubility changes the reactant/product distribution and
enables a different reaction mechanism.53–58 For example, in saturated-ether-based
electrolyte solutions, the sulfur conversion no longer follows a catholyte mechanism,
but rather a quasi-solid or solid-solid mechanism.54,59 In parallel work, it was shown
that a single-voltage plateau response of composite sulfur electrodes was observed
also in ﬂuorinated-alkyl-carbonate-based electrolyte solutions (LiPF6) and in ionic
liquid electrolytes with bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (FSI) anions. In these solutions, for-
mation of SEI-type surface ﬁlms on sulfur/carbon cathodes is responsible for the real-
ization of the quasi-solid-state mechanism in the electrochemical reactions of Li ions
with sulfur encapsulated in microporous or mesoporous (2–3 nm) carbons.16,56,57,60
Similar to Li-ion electrodes that react only with solids, the required amount of elec-
trolyte solution is decoupled from the sulfur mass and reaction paths, allowing cell
operation at lean electrolyte solution conditions. Notwithstanding the merits,
SSEs face daunting challenges such as high viscosity, low ionic conductivity, and

HSEs provide a higher polysulﬁde solubility, aimed at dissolving more polysulﬁdes
with less electrolyte (Figure 1D). HSEs can possibly reduce the E/S ratio, and also

In view of the importance of electrolyte systems in Li-S cells, we highlight the critical
role of manipulating polysulﬁde solvation for tuning the performance of Li-S batte-
ries. In this review, key factors affecting polysulﬁde solvation in non-aqueous electro-
lyte solutions are discussed, including solvent/salt properties and polysulﬁde chain
length. The three types of electrolyte systems—MSEs, SSEs, and HSEs—are re-
viewed to clarify the intrinsic relationship between the solution composition/struc-
ture, polysulﬁde solubility, reaction mechanism, and electrochemical performance,
with a special focus on understanding the varied redox reaction pathways at the
atomic/molecular level via advanced in situ and operando techniques. In particular,
the inﬂuence of the electrolyte solution on the stability of the lithium anode is high-
lighted, and the strengths and weaknesses of several solutions are presented and
compared. Perspectives for the improvement of Li-S batteries are provided in
conclusion.
FACTORS THAT GOVERN THE SOLUBILITY OF LITHIUM POLYSULFIDE
MOIETIES (Li2Sx)
In this section, we focus on the behavior of lithium polysulﬁde (Li2Sx) species in a va-
riety of non-aqueous electrolyte solutions, ignoring the compatibility of some of the
solutions discussed herein with Li metal anodes in order to address several funda-
mental aspects of non-aqueous electrochemical systems in relation to the Li2Sx moi-
eties. In the ﬁnal choice of electrolyte solution for practical Li-S batteries, they are
optimized for the best compatibility with both the anode and the cathode sides.
Sulfur atoms have a high tendency to form homoatomic rings or chains because of
the strong S–S covalent bond (265 kJ mol1).64,65 In Li-S batteries, S8 rings open
upon lithiation and generate a series of Li2Sx (1 % x % 8), which easily dissolves
into the polar electrolyte solution. These polysulﬁde anions with different chain
lengths coexist in equilibrium through disproportionation as a result of very close
values of free energy of formation.66,67 Given the complicated chemical equilibria,
the polysulﬁde solubility is generally described by using the concentration of sulfur
atoms rather than speciﬁc species. Furthermore, the dissociation of dianions can
generate the radical monoanion Sx

The solubility of the Li2Sx species depends on the solvent, the salt, and their chain

vent.69,70 The ability of a solvent to dissociate a given solute can be roughly esti-
mated by polarity parameters, such as the dielectric constant (ε) and the donor

to higher solubility. When several solutes are included in a multiplex solution, their
dissolution equilibria are interrelated.
For dissolution of Li2Sx species, experiments show that high ε/DN solvents, such as
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), N, N - dimethylacetamide (DMA), and tetramethylurea
(TMU), can highly dissolve Li2Sx species because of their strong capability to coordi-
nate with Li+.50,70,71 On the contrary, low solubility is seen in low ε/DN solvents such
as ionic liquids with relatively large anions (e.g., [N(SO2CF3)2] (TFSI)) and hydro-
ﬂuoroethers, which are weak donors.72–76 Ethers (glymes and DOL as examples) usu-
ally display a moderate capability in dissociating Li2Sx. Proper selection of the sol-
vent is a primary consideration for constructing SSEs or HSEs for Li-S batteries.
When a supporting salt is introduced, the Li2Sx dissolution can be ﬁnely tuned ac-
cording to the coordination effect or the common ion effect. At low concentration,
the dissociated ions of a supporting salt might promote dissolution by inducing a
complex-forming ligand with the Li2Sx species,70 which is known as the coordination
effect. Supporting salts with more strongly coordinating anions allow higher solubi-
lity of Li2Sx.77 When the concentration of a supporting salt exceeds a threshold, the
Li2Sx solubility is reduced by the common ion effect or by limited solvent molecules
for coordination,78 with examples including highly concentrated LiTFSI in ethers59,79
and exceptions including electrolyte solutions with lithium triﬂate (LiTf) or LiNO3.80

Solubility of long versus short-chain Li2Sx
The solubility of nominal Li2Sx shows a high dependence on molecular chain length,
although Li2Sx disproportionation and dissociation equilibria are ubiquitous. Longer
chain polysulﬁdes are in general more soluble.94,83 One possible reason for the
different solubility is the binding force between lithium and sulfur in the molecular
structure. In short-chain Li2S and Li2S2, the electrostatic interactions between Li+
and Sx
2 are too strong to be dissociated by organic solvents.95 Another
possible reason relates to the molecular structure of the shorter-chain Li2Sx,
which favors thermodynamically stable dimers or clusters in speciﬁc solution
systems.94,95–97 According to the mechanism proposed by Vijayakumar et al., the
dissolution is initiated by lithium exchange between the polysulﬁde solute and
solvent molecules.94 Dimerization or clustering hinders the bridge lithium from
interacting with solvent molecules, resulting in lower solubility.
MODERATELY SOLVATING ELECTROLYTE SOLUTIONS
The DOL/DME formulation, as an MSE representative, is compatible with both the
sulfur and lithium electrodes. The moderate solvating capability for polysulﬁdes
enables good sulfur utilization and reaction kinetics. The LiNO3 additive can
suppress the shuttle by forming robust passivating surface ﬁlms on the lithium an-
ode’s surface consisting of inorganic LiNxOy, organic ROLi, and ROCO2Li, as well
as lithium sulﬁde/sulfates (products of Li2Sx).98,99 Although DOL/DME is the most
widely used solvent in electrolyte solutions for Li-S batteries to date, challenges
still exist regarding the lean electrolyte requirements, which we discuss in this
section.

Challenges for MSEs at lean electrolyte conditions
The generation of intermediate polysulﬁdes is associated with both positive and
negative effects. The dissolution enhances sulfur utilization by providing rapid elec-
tron transfer and access to Li+ ions and preventing passivation by Li2S/Li2S2 precip-
itation. The overpotential of Li2S/Li2S2 oxidation also decreases.68 However, disso-
lution of polysulﬁdes in MSEs means that the reaction requires a large number of
solvent molecules to solvate the ions, which greatly reduces the energy density. In
fact, the energy density and cycling stability are intricately intertwined, and the
ll

Review

<<PAGE 10>>
E/S ratio has a big impact. A low E/S ratio incurs a high polysulﬁde concentration in
the electrolyte solution (Figure 3A). Working under lean electrolyte conditions gen-
erates a ‘‘polysulﬁde-saturated’’ solution, which brings several challenges:
(1) Increased viscosity and lower ionic conductivity (Figure 3B): The restricted
mass transport increases cell polarization, decreases sulfur utilization, and
lowers the rate capability.40,45,136 Chiang et al. revealed that Li2S deposition
is concentration-dependent and that a larger deposition overpotential is
required at a low E/S ratio.46 The Li2S deposition and growth even cease at
a ratio of 2.4 mL mg1 (Figure 3C). A low ratio leads to island nucleation
and up to a 75% lower growth rate than a high ratio (Figure 3D).46, Further-
more, at a low E/S ratio, part of the sulfur remains undissolved (dead
sulfur).137
(2) Aggravated polysulﬁde shuttle: Polysulﬁde diffusion is essentially driven by
the concentration gradient. A low E/S ratio increases the concentration
gradient and aggravates the back-and-forth migration as well as the parasitic
problems associated with the shuttle, such as sulfur loss, lithium corrosion,
low coulombic efﬁciency, and capacity decay. This situation becomes more
serious when cathodes with high sulfur loading are employed.47 Furthermore,
shuttling under lean conditions triggers endless overcharge in many
instances.
(3) Lithium anode corrosion and consequent electrolyte solution depletion:
although polysulﬁdes participate in benign SEI formation,138,139 the negative
impact at high concentration is noticeable. Highly concentrated polysulﬁdes
lead to more serious lithium corrosion,47 as the protection of the LiNO3-
derived surface layer is only temporary (LiNO3 is consumed on both elec-
trodes).140,141 After repetitive deposition/stripping, lithium metal becomes
powdery and forms irreversible ‘‘dead lithium’’ (Figure 3E), which means a fail-
ure of the cells (Figure 3F).48,142 Another serious problem is the electrolyte so-
lution decomposition because of continuous reactions with Li metal.28 Un-
doubtedly, lean electrolyte conditions result in faster depletion of more
reactive electrolyte solutions in the cells.
(4) Poor sulfur reaction kinetics with high polysulﬁde concentration at low E/S ra-
tio: recent works show that electrocatalysts (such as MoP and La0.8Sr0.2MnO3)
or mediators (such as cobaltocene and di-t-butyl disulﬁde) have enabled Li-S
cells to work with only 4–5 mL mg1 electrolyte solution by enhancing the sul-
fur redox kinetics.23,143–145 This deserves further effort and might beneﬁt from
learning from the versatile electrocatalysis ﬁeld. Nevertheless, the E/S ratio
demonstrated is still hard to meet the requirement of less than 3 mL mg1.
Designing new electrolyte solutions beyond those based on DOL/DME sol-
vent mixtures has lately shown the potential for long-lived Li-S cells in lean
electrolytes.
Sparingly solvating electrolytes
SSEs refer to solution systems where the reactant has a certain but very low solubility.
The application of SSEs is demonstrated by some commercialized secondary batteries
such as lead-acid and sodium iron chloride batteries. Sparing solubility is not precisely
deﬁned and can vary from system to system. For Li-S batteries, speciﬁc solvents such
as ionic liquids, hydroﬂuoroethers, concentrated electrolyte solutions, and speciﬁc
lithium salts can suppress the dissolution of polysulﬁdes. In these solutions, the Li2S8 sol-
ubility ranges from 1 to 100 mM (in sulfur atom). The electrolyte solution composition
and its correlation with the sulfur reaction pathway, lithium metal compatibility, and bat-
tery performance are discussed in the following subsections.

High-concentration electrolyte systems
The structure of the electrolyte solution can be tuned by controlling the salt concen-
tration, which in most cases is around 1 M, wherein only a small fraction of solvent
coordinates to salt ions, whereas most remain in a free state (Figure 6A, left). Higher
concentrations reinforce the interactions between the solvent and salt anions/cat-
ions, forming contact ion pairs and aggregates (AGGs), one anion coordinating
with two or more Li+ (Figure 6A, middle).191 This leads to a dramatic decrease in
free-state solvent molecules, even to none when the salt-solvent ratio is above a
certain value. In such a high-concentration electrolyte (HCE) system, polysulﬁde
dissolution is restricted due to the lack of free-state solvent molecules. Because of
the strong solvent-ion interactions, HCEs possess some unique properties, such as
high Li+ transference numbers, wide electrochemical windows, ﬂame retardance,
and reduced volatility, as well as thermal stability.192 Nevertheless, HCEs experience
low ionic conductivity and high viscosity.55,78,79 To circumvent this challenge without
destructing the solvation structure, diluents with low viscosity and weak solvating
power are usually incorporated to form diluted electrolytes or localized high-con-
centration electrolyte (LHCE) systems (Figure 6A, right).54,55,193

Reactions in various HCE systems
It is interesting to note that the discharge proﬁles of sulfur cathodes in HCE vary from
system to system, including typical two-plateau proﬁles,78,91,208 single-slope pro-
ﬁles,55,198 and one-plateau proﬁles with low voltage,53,195,202 as well as ‘‘equipoten-
tial bi-plateau’’ voltage proﬁles,59,88 which indicate different reduction mechanisms.
The primary cause for this variation appears to be the polysulﬁde solubility that dictates
the reaction pathway, which is thermodynamic in nature. The solubility is governed
mainly by the number of free Lewis base sites (O or N). Unsaturated electrolyte solutions,
for example, 5 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME78 and Li(G4)1-TFSI, 86 where G4 molecules have
extra uncoordinated O atoms, can still dissolve a sufﬁcient amount of polysulﬁdes to
trigger the dissolution-precipitation pathway, thereby giving rise to the typical two-
plateau voltage proﬁle. In non-solvent electrolyte systems such as saturated 12 M lithium
bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in DME54 and 6 M LiTFSI in EC/DEC,53 polysulﬁdes barely
dissolve, and the reactions proceed through solid-solid pathways, resulting in sloping
voltage proﬁles. If polysulﬁdes are sparingly solvated, the limited but non-negligible dis-
solved polysulﬁdes can act as redox mediators, inducing the ‘‘equipotential bi-plateau
proﬁles’’ exempliﬁed in the G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1)59 and (ACN)2-LiTFSI88 electrolyte systems
at elevated temperatures. Nevertheless, the exact reaction pathways in the HCE systems
are not trivial and require further investigation.
Another reason for voltage proﬁle variations in HCE systems is the sulfur distribution
in the composite cathodes and/or the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte solution,

The effect of HCEs on lithium metal anode stability
The main advantage of Li-S batteries is their high energy density, likely the highest
that can be achieved by any practical rechargeable battery system. For this, the use
of metallic Li anodes is highly important, especially given that the redox voltage of
sulfur cathodes is relatively low. Therefore, the lithium metal anode stability is a key
factor that determines the performance of Li-S cells as a rechargeable battery sys-
tem, which deserves a thorough discussion. The basic stabilization of Li metal an-
odes is achieved by complex surface reactions that form protective surface ﬁlms
that behave like a solid electrolyte interphase (the famous SEI model). However,
the development of a good SEI on a Li metal anode is not sufﬁcient to avoid detri-
mental situations like dendrite formation. Here, we focus mainly on the impact of
the electrolyte solution structure on the performance of Li anodes in Li-S cells.

In the model proposed by Chazalviel and Brissot et al.,209,210 when the applied cur-
rent exceeds a certain threshold, the anion concentration near the cathode surface
decreases to zero after a speciﬁc period (Sand’s time). The cations then preferentially
deposit onto surface protrusions, leading to a self-amplifying process of dendritic
growth.211 It is anticipated that high concentration and transference number of Li+
enable a prolonged Sand’s time and possibly eliminate dendrites by suppressing
the generation of local space charge and electric ﬁeld.210
In this regard, high salt concentration favors a more stabilized lithium, as evidenced
in electrolyte systems such as 7 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME and 12 M LiFSI in DME and
G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1) (Figures 8A–8C).54,59,79 Such stabilized lithium anodes show only
small polarization variation during stripping/plating, indicative of a low electrode
surface area that reduces parasitic reactions with the electrolyte solution (Fig-
ure 8D).59 The stabilization leads to high coulombic efﬁciency of lithium stripping/
plating processes (Figures 8E and 8F).59 The enhanced solvent-ion interactions—
contact ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregates (AGGs)—greatly decrease the activity of sol-
vent molecules, alleviating solvent decomposition and preventing battery dry-
out.53,59 The high concentration also alters the SEI composition. A multilayer SEI
consisting of outside-organic and inside-inorganic compositions (e.g., LiF species)
was detected on Li anodes in concentrated LiFSI/DME solutions (Figures 8G–
8J).54 The high interface energy of ionic Li compounds such as LiF with Li metal is
believed to suppress dendrite growth upon Li deposition (charging).
The anion species of the lithium salt affect the composition and stability of the SEI
layers on the Li anodes. For instance, in the 3 M electrolyte solutions (DOL/DME
as solvent), LiFSI induces LiSOx-dominated surface ﬁlms on Li anodes, whereas
LiTFSI generates LiSx-containing surface ﬁlms on Li anodes.212 In turn, in spite of
the unique properties of solutions containing (ACN)2-LiTFSI complexes described
earlier, they fail to support the long-term cycling of Li-S cells because of poor
compatibility of ACN with lithium metal.59
Hydroﬂuoroether-based electrolyte solutions
Hydroﬂuoroethers (HFEs), a family of ﬂuorinated ethers, are considered as "nonsol-
vents" for polysulﬁdes (i.e., they cannot dissolve them) because of the weak donor
ability of their oxygen atoms imparted by the strong electronegativity and steric hin-
drance of the ﬂuorine atoms.213 Therefore, HFEs are used not only in combination
with SSEs such as RTILs, SILs, and HCE acting as diluents that lower viscosity but
also with other ethereal solvents such as DOL, in order to tune the polysulﬁde solu-
bility (thus controlling detrimental shuttle effects).
In the early 2010s, Amine et al. reported on the operation of Li-S cells in hybrid
electrolyte solutions containing the ﬂuorinated ether 1,1,2,2-teraﬂuoro-3-(1,1,2,2-
teraﬂuoroethyoxy)-propane and DOL.214 The improved capacity and cycle life
are attributed to the low polysulﬁde solubility. Most recently, the same group pro-
posed a selection rule for the mono-ether HFE Rf1(b)-Rf2(a)-O-Rf3(a0)-Rf1(b0)-R as a
cosolvent by evaluating its solvating power and shuttle factor for polysulﬁdes.215
Mandal et al. further examined the di-ether HFE R1-O-CH2-CH2-O-R2 and found
that the –CF3 group lowers the viscosity as compared with the –CF2CF2H group,
and that the polysulﬁde solubility is highly correlated with the percentage of ﬂuo-
rine atoms in the ether molecules.216 The limited dissolution of polysulﬁdes makes
HFEs good solvents to inhibit self-discharge of Li-S batteries for a long shelf
life.217,218

Lithium stability in electrolyte solutions with HFE
HFEs can form SEI-type surface ﬁlms that protect lithium anodes from reacting with
polysulﬁdes and other solution components.226–228 SEI formation is ascribed to
decomposition of ﬂuorinated molecules on the lithium surface. Manthiram et al.
pointed out that the SEI is composed of LiF that reinforces the SEI and sulfate/sul-
ﬁte/sulﬁde species that serve as self-limiting layers suppressing parasitic reac-
tions.228 A LiF-rich SEI means a denser and more stable passivation layer, which
helps to suppress dendrite growth and dead Li formation, enhancing lithium
plating/stripping efﬁciency (Figure 9E).224
To better elucidate the interplay between the molecular structure of HFE and the
coulombic efﬁciency, Zhang et al. conducted a systematic investigation of HFE mol-
ecules with different amounts and ﬂuorination levels.219 Increasing the number of
ﬂuorine atoms enhanced the options of reactions between HFE and lithium that
form LiF-rich SEI-type protective surface ﬁlms. In particular, the low volatility and
the nature of the reactions between HFEs and lithium metal prevent the formation
of gaseous products, enabling us to demonstrate gas-free pouch Li-S cells.89
Decreasing the solubility by tuning the lithium salt
Other than the solvent, the lithium salt can also tune the polysulﬁde solubility
because of the anion interactions that affect the chemical equilibrium of the polysul-
ﬁdes. A typical example is the use of lithium triﬂuoromethyl-4,5-dicyanoimidazole
(LiTDI) in DOL/DME solutions, which promotes the dissociation of Li2S8 into two
Li2S4 moieties that dimerize into Li4S8-like clusters.85 This situation prevents polysul-
ﬁde dissolution, leading to very low Li2S8 solubility (0.67 M in sulfur).
In addition, as discussed in the effect of solvents and salts, anions with low Lewis ba-
sicity decrease the polysulﬁde solubility. For example, Nazar et al. reported that
combinations of sulfonomide solvents, i.e., N,N-dimethyl triﬂamide (DMT) or N,N-
dipropyl triﬂamide (DPT), and low ion-pairing salts, such as Li[Al(Ohﬁp)4], behave
like non-solvent systems toward polysulﬁdes at room temperature.63 At elevated

Improved Li2S deposition in HSEs
Regulation of the deposition of Li2S is critical as premature Li2S formation would
passivate the cathode surface, leading to low sulfur utilization, large polarization,
and inferior rate capability.61,77,130 Liu et al. showed that Li2S deposition is highly
associated with the electrolyte solution properties, especially the Li2S solubility
therein.236 In solutions containing low-DN TMS solvent (DN = 14.8), polysulﬁdes
prefer to precipitate uniformly and induce a thin ﬁlm-like morphology (Figure 12A).
Such a situation can be termed a bottom-up deposition mechanism, in which elec-
trons are transferred from the cathode to the solution species during discharging,
leading to an immediate deposition of Li2S on the surface. This might block further
effective charge transfer (leading to a limited speciﬁc capacity). In medium DN DOL/
DME-based solutions (DN z 20), polysulﬁdes have suitable solubility to balance the
nucleation and subsequent particle growth, leading to a ﬂower-like deposition of
Li2S (Figure 12B). Solutions containing high-DN DMSO (DN = 30) as a cosolvent pro-
vide a higher polysulﬁde solubility capability and form strong coordination with Li+-
or Li+-containing species (Li2Sx) and therefore favor particle-like deposition (Fig-
ure 12C).236 Such a situation can be termed a top-down precipitation mechanism,
leading to high speciﬁc capacity cathodes. The discharging process can be fully
completed as the cathode is not blocked by Li2S deposits.
It is important to emphasize that when dealing with high-DN solvents such as DMSO
or DMA, we focus mainly on the sulfur cathode side and the fate of the Li2Sx reduc-
tion products. Understanding the possible inﬂuence of such high-DN solvents on the
sulfur cathode reactions provides an important basic background for further prac-
tical developments. There is always a serious open question about the compatibility
of such reactive solvents with Li metal anodes, which is discussed brieﬂy in the next
section.
A more detailed description of possible pathways for Li2S deposition during
discharge processes was recently given by Lu et al. They pointed out that Li2S depo-
sition is an interplay of electrode kinetics, Li2S dissolution and polysulﬁde/Li2S diffu-
sion, which are governed by the electrolyte solution donicity, polarity, and viscosity,
respectively.61 Four classical deposition models are provided (Figure 12G). First is
the Bewick, Fleischman, Thirsk (BFT) model consisting of (1) 2D progressive nucle-
ation and (2) 2D instantaneous nucleation, both of which are followed by incorpora-
tion of atoms into the lattice interface. Second is the Scharifker-Hills (SH) model con-
sisting of (3) 3D progressive nucleation and (4) 3D instantaneous nucleation, both of
which are followed by a volume diffusional controlled growth.61 In high-DN solu-
tions, Li2S nucleation is slow due to the stabilization of long-chain polysulﬁdes.
This results in a low nuclei density with a hemispherical diffusion-controlled growth.
Accordingly, Li2S deposition follows the 3D SH model and forms deposits of a quasi-
spherical granular shape. In contrast, in low-DN solvent, Li2S deposition is more
likely to follow a 2D model to form a plate or sheet-like morphology because of
the fast nucleation rate and low solubility of Li2S.
A subsequent study by Kim demonstrated that in low-DN DOL/DME, the 3D Li2S
deposition can also be obtained as long as soluble S2 anions are available (Fig-
ures 12D–12F).77,230 As mentioned earlier, high-DN anions (Tf, Br, and NO3
)
can promote a partial solvation of Li2S.77,230 The binding energy between S2
and deposited Li2S (3.6/2.48 eV, regardless of facets) is higher than that between
S2 and carbon (1.39 eV). Consequently, the soluble S2 anions can move freely in
the electrolyte solution throughout the whole reduction process and hence prefer
to deposit on the polar Li2S surface to form a three-dimensional morphology,

Challenges for highly solvating electrolyte solutions (HSEs)
HSEs promise a higher sulfur utilization in the initial discharge because of the pres-
ence of S3
$ and the less passivated cathode surface. The high polysulﬁde solubility
also enables the use of low E/S ratios, thereby increasing the cell energy density.
However, challenges exist with respect to the long-term cycling performance.
One problem is the aggravated polysulﬁde shuttle. The dissolution of polysulﬁdes is
the root of the detrimental shuttle phenomenon, which induces parasitic sulfur loss,
capacity fading, low coulombic efﬁciency, and corrosion of lithium anodes. A second
problem is the lithium metal instability. Unfortunately, high-DN solvents (DMSO,
DMA, and DMF) are usually highly reactive with Li metal, and their reactions with
the active metal cannot form stable, SEI-type, passivating surface ﬁlms.50,77,237,238

METRICS OF LI-S CELLS CONTAINING MSEs, SSEs, AND HSEs
A spider (multifaceted) chart compares the performance of Li-S cells containing
different electrolyte solutions with respect to various requirements (Figure 14A).
The most noticeable difference is the solvation power for dissociating polysulﬁdes,
which is the key property for manipulating sulfur electrochemistry. Low polysulﬁde
solubility is more preferred in the long term as it renders shuttle-free Li-S batteries
for better cycling. In this regard, cells using SSEs might perform better than cells us-
ing HSEs and MSEs. However, the reported Li-S cells with SSEs have not realized the
long cycle life yet. The underlying reason might be the sluggish sulfur reactions and
the serious Li2S passivation, which currently lacks reports and needs further research
in the future. The polysulﬁde solvation characteristics of SSEs and HSEs promise bet-
ter operation of Li-S cells under lean electrolyte conditions. Another property
related with polysulﬁde solvation is the rate capability. For MSEs and HSEs, a higher
solubility generally means faster reaction kinetics and hence a better rate capability.
Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that, even with a catholyte mechanism (MSEs and
HSEs), a high-rate performance can only be obtained with adequate/ﬂooded elec-
trolyte solutions. Therefore, rate capability is, in fact, a common challenge.

(g/g) would provide a better description of the electrolyte solutions’ quantity.
CONCLUDING REMARKS AND PERSPECTIVES
The success of Li-S batteries as a fully practical reality requires holistic advancements
involving cathodes, anodes, and the electrolyte systems as a whole. The history of
identifying mixtures of alkyl carbonate solvents with LiPF6 as the most suitable elec-
trolyte solution for Li-ion batteries using graphite anodes teaches us that unsuccess-
ful electrolyte solution identiﬁcation might signiﬁcantly delay research and develop-
ment of new battery systems. Yet, the choice of suitable electrolyte solutions for Li-S
batteries is apparently much more important, as it plays additional roles—in passiv-
ating highly reactive Li metal anodes, solvating intermediate polysulﬁde moieties,
stabilizing radical species, and channeling reactions and precipitation mechanisms.
To avoid repeating the Li-ion battery path and perhaps seek a shortcut to realize the
full potential of practical Li-S batteries, a re-examination of the whole issue of elec-
trolyte solutions is needed. We believe that appropriate manipulation of polysulﬁde
solubility can be a game changer, as the solubility of the variety of sulfur reduction
products inﬂuences nearly every aspect, including reaction mechanisms, sulfur utili-
zation, lithium anode stability, electrolyte solution quantity, and eventually the en-
ergy density and long-term cycling capability.

battery performance in lean electrolyte conditions. Development of stable additives
that enhance the stabilization and passivation of Li metal anodes is also required in
order to ensure their long-term protection.
The advent of SSEs, offers to fundamentally alleviate the dependence of the electro-
lyte solution quantity on the sulfur reaction pathway, thereby potentially enabling
the use of a low volume of electrolyte solution. Nevertheless, SSEs still suffer from
high viscosity and low ionic conductivity. These issues deserve more attention in or-
der to tackle the large polarization and poor rate capability. Further development
might involve exploration of new systems and a deeper understanding of the sulfur
reaction mechanism as well as options for effective Li anode passivation. Theoretical
work using machine learning and big data could be tremendously useful to rapidly
screen relevant solvents and salts.
By reinforcing polysulﬁde dissolution, highly solvating electrolyte solutions, HSEs,
provide an alternative strategy to decrease the E/S ratio. However, the incompatibil-
ity between metallic lithium and high-DN solvents leads to fast capacity fading and
battery failure. In order to use HSEs and beneﬁt from their advantages, it is important
to develop new Li metal anode passivation approaches. Creative design related to
solvents, salts, and additives is required to maintain polysulﬁde solubility and lithium
stability. Further reinforcement of Li anode passivation in these systems by the use of
appropriate additives should also mitigate the parasitic shuttle reactions.
In the past few years, what greatly delayed the rapid development of Li-S batteries
was the lack of understanding of the electrochemistry in lean electrolyte conditions
where the sulfur reaction mechanisms (liquid, quasi-solid, or solid-solid), reaction ki-
netics, reactant distribution, and lithium stability greatly differ from those in ﬂooded
cells. In this regard, utilizing advanced spectroscopy/microscopy techniques is
extremely important to elucidate these fundamental issues. The in-depth under-
standing of Li-S chemistry will in turn provide insightful guidance to the design of
electrolyte solutions and optimization of electrodes.
Notably, pursuing all solid-state batteries represents a parallel research direction for uti-
lizing active metal anodes by reducing the surface activity and suppressing dendrite for-
mation. The deployment of solid-state electrolytes might also eliminate problems
related to polysulﬁde dissolution, shuttle mechanisms, and safety concerns. Break-
throughs in reaching high ionic conductivity and low interfacial impedance between
solid electrolytes and electrodes are expected to further boost Li-S battery develop-
ment. The discovery of new materials for electrolyte systems as discussed throughout
this review is expected to continuously contribute to the further development of Li-S bat-
tery systems and eventually open the doors for their eventual commercialization.
