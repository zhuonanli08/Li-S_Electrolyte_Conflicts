for lean-electrolyte Li-SeS2 batteries

A B S T R A C T  
The “solid-liquid-solid” reaction mechanisms of lithium-sulfur/selenium (Li-S/Se) batteries in ether-based elec­
trolytes, which rely on the soluble polysulfides (Li2S4–8, LPSs)/polyselenides (Li2Se4–8, LPSes) intermediates, 
prevent further reduction of the electrolyte dosage and thus offset the advantages of Li-S/Se batteries in energy 
density. Using sparingly-solvating electrolytes, in which the dissolution of LPSs/LPSes is suppressed, could 
reduce the electrolyte dosage. Nevertheless, such electrolytes bring sluggish reaction kinetic and poor compat­
ibility with lithium (Li) anodes. Herein, by regulating the electrolyte structure, we found the weakly-solvating 
electrolytes, which have slightly higher dissolubility of LPSs/LPSes than the sparingly-solvating ones, could 
facilitate electrochemical reaction and stabilize Li anodes. The newly formulated electrolyte makes high active 
materials utilization under conditions of high current densities, high areal mass loadings, and low electrolyte-to- 
active-material (E/A) ratio possible. The cell with a SeS2 cathode realizes a high discharge capacity of 1047 mAh 
g−1, 93.2% of its theoretical value (1123 mAh g−1), at 0.1 C. Even with a high areal-mass-loading SeS2 cathode 
(9.5 mg cm−2), the as-designed electrolyte endows the cell with a high reversible capacity of 713 mAh g−1 under 
a low E/A ratio of 1 μL mg−1. The as-proposed strategy provides an effective electrolyte-regulation solution to 
practical high-energy Li-S/Se batteries.   
1. Introduction 
Li-S/Se batteries have been regarded as competitive candidates for 
next-generation rechargeable batteries due to their low cost, high 
theoretical energy density, and environmental friendliness [1]. 
Ether-based electrolytes, which have high ionic conductivity, superb 
compatibility with the lithium anodes, and the capability to dissolve the 
LPSs/LPSes intermediates and decrease the reaction barrier, have been 
the most popular electrolytes for Li-S/Se batteries [2]. Nevertheless, our 
recent finding demonstrated the ether-based electrolytes significantly 
reduce the wettability and conductivity of the electrolytes during the 
electrochemical process under conditions of low E/A ratios due to the 
accumulation of the soluble LPSs, which increases the internal contact 
and charge-transfer resistances of the cells and thus terminates the 
conversion between S and Li2S [3]. The above reasons constrain the 
development of the lean-ether-electrolyte Li-S/Se batteries and sacrifice 
the advantages of Li-S/Se batteries in energy density for practical 
applications. 
A class of sparingly-solvating electrolytes, which suppress the 
dissolution of the LPSs/LPSes intermediates, was proposed to overcome 
the challenge [4]. One of the examples is the concentrated acetonitrile 
(AN)-based electrolytes, which use AN as solvent, high-amount lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as salt, and 1,1,2,2-tetra­
fluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) as non-disrupting 
dilution [4d]. In this electrolyte, the AN molecules are effectively 
blended with the LiTFSI salt, and the absence of the free solvent mole­
cules suppresses the dissolution of the LPSs/LPSes intermediates, which 
makes the high-S/Se utilization in Li-S batteries under low E-A ratios 
possible. However, the reported AN-based sparingly-solvating electro­
lyte with an AN-to-salt molar ratio of 2 (AN2) shows a low Li+ con­
ductivity and poor compatibility with Li anodes. The above drawbacks 
lead to sluggish reaction kinetic and poor cycling stability of Li-S bat­
teries. In a pioneering work, the charge current density of Li-S batteries 
in AN2 electrolyte is suggested to as low as C/10 to minimize the

What’s worse, due to the lack of dissolved LPSs, the S redox species 
show very limited availability/mobility in AN2 electrolyte during the 
electrochemical processes. Such a solid or quasi-solid conversion be­
tween S and Li2S requires a more electronic conductive cathode to 
facilitate the electrochemical process. In our previous work, we 
demonstrated the use of Se, a congeneric element of S but with higher 
electronic conductivity, as active material delivered a low polarization 
and long cycling stability in AN2 electrolyte [5]. While the theoretical 
capacity of Se is only 675 mAh g−1, in this context, the use of a 
composition of S and Se, which has a higher theoretical specific capacity 
than Se and better electronic conductivity than S, as active material 
instead of pure Se or S seems a solution for the cells in low-solvating 
electrolytes [6]. On the other hand, how to improve the stability of 
the Li-metal anode in the low-solvating electrolytes also remains chal­
lenging [7]. 
Here, using AN-based electrolytes as an example, we demonstrated 
that the reaction kinetic and cycling stability of the cells with SeS2 
cathodes could be significantly improved by introducing more AN sol­
vent into the sparingly-solvating AN2 electrolyte, which changes the 
electrolyte structure. The fine regulation of the AN-to-salt molar ratio 
from 2 to 3 slightly increases the solubility of LPSs/LPSes in the elec­
trolytes and transforms the sparingly-solvating electrolyte into a weakly- 
solvating one (AN3), which not only enhances the availability/mobility 
of the active redox species and facilitate the conversion between S/Se 
and Li2S/Li2Se, but also induces the formation of a robust solid elec­
trolyte interface (SEI) layer on the Li-metal anodes. With this newly-

lation strategy paves the way to practical high-energy Li-S/Se batteries. 
2. Results and discussion 
To employ AN, which is highly reactive with Li metal, as the solvent 
for the electrolytes of Li-metal batteries, the typical solution is to in­
crease the salt concentration and thus coordinate the free AN molecules 
with Li+ ions [8]. To determine how much salt is needed to guarantee 
the reductive stability against Li metal, we immersed fresh lithium foils 
into AN solvent and AN solutions (ANx, here, x refers to AN-to-LiTFSI 
molar ratios) and monitored the color change of the solutions. As the 
results shown in Fig. S1 in Supplementary Information, the pure AN 
solvent turned into light yellow after 1 h and the dilute solution (AN4) 
became yellow after 1 day, and the color change is due to the reaction 
between Li and AN molecule. In contrast, the Li foil-immersed AN2 and 
AN3 maintained transparent even after 10 days. In addition, the Li 
plating/stripping curves of the symmetrical cells (Fig. S2 in Supple­
mentary Information) and the cycling performance of Li-SeS2 cells 
(Fig. S3 in Supplementary Information) confirmed the advantages of 
AN3, so x = 3 is chose as our experimental formulation. 
Compared to AN2, which is the typically used formula in previous 
publications, AN3 shows lower Raman spectra peak for the TFSI−
coordinating to Li+ cations (749 cm−1) while stronger free-AN peak (as 
shown by the νC–––
N stretching vibration band at 2258 cm−1) [8,9], 
indicating there are more free Li+ cations and AN molecules in AN3 
(Fig. 1a). Such change in electrolyte structure endows AN3 with higher 
ionic conductivity at different temperature (Figs. 1b and S4 in Supple­
mentary Information), which are beneficial to the high-rate capability of 
the cells. In addition, with more free AN molecules, AN3 shows better 
wettability with the separator and SeS2 cathode (Fig. 1c). As shown in 
the Fig. S5 in Supplementary Information, the morphologies of lithium 
foils after immersed in different electrolytes for 15 days do not show 
apparently different, indicating the high compatibility of AN3 with 
lithium metal. All the above physicochemical characterizations confirm 
the superiority of AN3 as the electrolyte for Li-metal batteries. 
The additional free AN molecule also endows AN3 other advantages 
as electrolytes for Li-S(Se) batteries. One obvious example is its higher 
solubility to LPSs/LPSes, which is considered beneficial to the reaction 
kinetics. To investigate the solubility difference and its influences on the 
electrochemical behaviors between AN2 and AN3, we designed a 
cuvette cell (Figs. 1d and S6a in Supplementary Information), and in-situ

that in AN2, we classify these electrolytes into the sparingly-solvating 
electrolyte (AN2) and the weakly-solvating electrolyte (AN3) to make 
a distinction between them. The sparingly solvating electrolytes (such as 
AN2) represent saturated electrolytes, in which the intermediates are 
hard to be dissolved. The weakly-solvating electrolytes (such as AN3) 
represent concentrated electrolytes contain more coordinated AN than 
free AN, in which the intermediates could be slightly dissolved. 
Here, we chose an S and Se composition (SeS2) as the active material 
because Se has much higher electric conductivity (10−5 S cm−1) than 
that of S (5 × 10−30 S cm−1) [6a], and the introduction of Se into S 
cathodes can increase the conductivity of electrodes, which is critical to 
promote the reactions of Li-S(Se) cells, especially when low-solvating 
electrolytes are used [6c,13]. The SeS2 composition was prepared by 
heating the S and Se powders at 260 ◦C for 6 h [13,14]. The as-obtained 
SeS2 composition shows a distinguishing X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern 
compared with those of Se (PDF: 04–009–6204) and S (PDF: 
01–071–0396), confirming a new phase was formed after the heating 
process (Fig. S7 in Supplementary Information) [6b]. The SeS2 compo­
sition was further confined into the Ketjenblack (KB) carbon (Fig. S8 in 
Supplementary Information) with a content of active materials in 
SeS2@KB composites of 62 wt% as detected by the thermogravimetric 
analysis (TG, Fig. S9 in Supplementary Information). Benefiting from the 
incorporation of high conductive Se into S electrodes, the Li-SeS2 cell 
shows a lower discharge-charge polarization (0.28 V) than that of Li-S 
cell (0.70 V) in the AN3 electrolyte (Fig. S10 in Supplementary Infor­
mation). In addition, we also noticed that the Li-SeS2 cell demonstrated 
better cycling stability than that of the Li-S cell in the AN3 electrolyte, 
even though its discharge capacities are lower in the first 40 cycles 
(Fig. S11 in Supplementary Information). Such a remarkable improve­
ment in cycling stability could be ascribed to the improved stability of

the cathode conductivity, but also allows more polyselenfides dissolu­

The electrochemical kinetics of Li-SeS2 cells in AN2 and AN3 was 
first investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) with a scanning speed of 
0.1 mV s−1. Compared with the CV curve in AN2, the cell in AN3 shows 
an extra reduction peak located at 2.16 V (Fig. 2a), which is due to the 
formation and dissolution of LPSs/LPSes intermediates in AN3 [4d,5b, 
15]. Such results are consistent with the in-situ UV–vis characterizations 
(Fig. 1d-f). In contrast, due to the low solubility of LPSs/LPSes in AN2, 
the Li-SeS2 cell in AN2 shows only one discharge plateau, implying a 
solid-solid conversion mechanism (Fig. 2b). The introduction of free AN 
solvent alters the sparingly solvating AN2 electrolytes into weakly sol­
vating AN3 electrolytes and allows part of the active materials to be 
involved in the dissolution-precipitation reaction during the electro­
chemical processes, which reduced the reaction polarization from 0.32 
to 0.28 V without capacity loss. The improved reaction kinetics of the 
Li-SeS2 cells in AN3 was further confirmed by the electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS), as the Nyquist plots shown in Fig. S13 in 
Supplementary Information, the Li-SeS2 cell in AN3 shows significantly 
lower impedances compared with those of the cell in AN2. Potentiostatic 
experiments were further conducted by potentiostatically discharging at 
2.1 V to understand the solvation process from solid SeS2 to the 
LPSs/LPSes intermediates during discharge [6e,16a,b]. The cell in AN3 
shows a potentiostatic discharge time of 46,135 s with a capacity of 256 
mAh g−1, much higher than those in AN2 (8672 s and 64 mAh g−1). In 
addition, compared with AN2, an obvious reduction current peak at 
21481s is observed for the cell in AN3, confirming the increased sol­
vating process during discharge for the Li-SeS2 cells in AN3 (Fig. 2c). 
The increased solvation of LPSs/LPSes intermediates in AN3 renders 
the super rate capability of Li-SeS2 batteries. Fig. 2e compares the rate 
performance of the Li-SeS2 cells in AN2 and AN3 from 0.1 C to 2.0 C (1 C 
= 1123 mA gSeS2
−1 ). In AN3, the Li-SeS2 cell exhibits a high revisable 
capacity of 1047 mAh g−1, corresponding to 93.2% of the theoretical 
value, at 0.1 C. Even when the current density was increased to 2.0 C, a 
stable capacity of 851 mAh g−1 could still be maintained (Fig. 2d). In 
contrast, the cell in AN2 electrolytes undergoes a fast capacity decay at 
2.0 C. Meanwhile, the regulation of the electrolyte from sparingly- 
solvating AN2 to weakly-solvating AN3 decreases the charge-discharge 
polarization, especially at high C rates. With AN3, the polarization Li- 
SeS2 cell was decreased from 1.05 V to 0.65 V at 2.0 C with the shape of 
the discharge-charge curve well-maintained (Fig. 2e and f). 
The long-term cycling performance of Li-SeS2 cells in AN2 and AN3 
was investigated by cycling the cells at a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2. 
As the results depicted in Fig. 2g, the cell in AN3 shows an excellent 
cyclability, retaining a high reversible capacity of 800 mAh g−1 after 135 
cycles, corresponding to a low capacity fading rate of 0.25% per cycle. In 
comparison, the cell in AN2 suffers from rapid capacity fading, deliv­
ering a capacity of 490 mAh g−1 after 75 cycles and 223 mAh g−1 after 
100 cycles, respectively. It is also noticed that the Coulombic efficiency 
(CE) of the cell in AN3 maintained > 99%, indicating the negligible 
“shuttle” of the intermediates even though more LPSs/LPSes were dis­
solved in weakly-solvating AN3 during the electrochemical process. 
Fig. 2h compares the discharge–charge profiles of the Li-SeS2 cells in the 
50th cycle, a polarization of 0.31 V with a capacity of 1071 mAh g−1 was 
obtained in AN3, and the polarization is even lower than that of the first 
cycle in AN2 (0.32 V). Meantime, the polarization of the cell in AN2 
increased from 0.32 V in the first cycle to 0.37 V after 50 cycles, the 
deteriorated voltage polarization may explain the lost capacity (from 
1079 mAh g−1 in the first cycle to 781 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles) of the 
cell in AN2. The electrochemical improvements in polarization, rate 
capability, and cyclability could be attributed to increased solubility of 
LPSs/LPSes in AN3, which promotes the solvating of the intermediates 
during the electrochemical process and improves the reaction kinetic. 
To investigate the influences of electrolytes on the stability of Li 
anodes, we disassembled the cycled Li-SeS2 cells in AN2 and AN3 and 
compared the morphology of the cycled Li anodes with a scanning 
electron microscope (SEM). As the results shown in Figs. 3a-b and S14 in 
Supplementary Information, the Li anode cycled in AN2 shows a porous 
and loose structure after only three cycles (Fig. S14a), while in AN3, the 
cycled Li anode deposited as smooth and large plates without lithium 
dendrites. After 100 cycles, the Li anode in AN3 remains dense (Fig. 3b), 
whereas the lithium was deposited as separate particles in AN2 (Fig. 3a). 
The above results confirm that the cycling stability of the Li anode is 
significantly improved by tuning the solvating property of the electro­
lyte from sparingly-solvating to weakly-solvating, which also contrib­
utes to the long-term stability of the Li-SeS2 cells. To figure out how the 
electrolyte influences the SEI formation on the Li anodes, we checked 
the elemental distribution of the cycled Li anodes with energy dispersive 
spectroscopy (EDS). As the results shown in Fig. 3c, the EDS of the cycled 
Li anode in AN3 shows stronger Se peaks, corresponding to a higher Se 
content (5wt%) compared with the value (1wt%) in AN2 in Fig. S15 in 
Supplementary Information. The increased content of Se element could 
be ascribed to the increased soluble LPSes in AN3, and the Se-rich SEI 
layer is always regarded to be beneficial to the stability of the Li anodes 
[17]. 
The SEI compositions of the cycled Li anodes were further charac­
terized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Fig. 3d compares the 
Li 1 s, S 2p, C 1 s, and N 1 s spectra of the SEI formed in sparingly- 
solvating AN2 and weakly-solvating AN3 electrolytes. The Li 1 s of the 
cycled Li anodes in both electrolytes exhibit peaks corresponding to the 
Li-Se bond at 56.7 and 54.9 eV (attributing to Se 3d3/2 and Se 3d5/2, 
respectively) [6e]. The atomic concentration of Se on the cycled Li 
surface in AN3 according to the peak area ratio is much higher than that 
in sparingly-solvating AN2 electrolyte (78% vs. 49%, Fig. S16 in Sup­
plementary Information). The sulfur element in electrolytes may be 
assigned from LiTFSI salts and the soluble LPSs, whereas the selenium 
element could just be found from the soluble LPSes in electrolytes. Thus, 
it could be confirmed again that the higher Se content of the Li anode 
cycled in AN3 is due to the increased soluble intermediates from 
sparingly-solvating AN2 to weakly-solvating AN3 electrolyte. 
Another distinct difference between the Li anodes cycled in two 
electrolytes can be found in the S 2p spectra. The signal intensity of the 
peak assigned for SO4
2−in the SEI formed in AN3, which is a product 
resulting from the oxidization of the polysulfides [18], is higher than the 
one formed in AN2. LiSO4 could effectively protect Li surface from AN 
molecules’ corrosion, leading to a prolonged lithium lifetime. The 
Li2SO4 component is desirable for a stable SEI on lithium metal, and 
important to the cyclability of the battery [19]. The content of Li2SO4 in 
the SEI of cycled Li anode in AN2 was calculated according to the peak 
area ratio, and the atomic concentration of S from Li2SO4 is 59%, almost 
twice of that in AN2 (Fig. 3e). While for the Li anode cycled in AN2, the 
SEI contains more CO3
2−, which is due to the continuous reaction be­
tween the Li anodes and AN2 electrolyte, inducing gas formation and 
destruction of the SEI [19], and thus bad cycling stability. For N 1 s 
spectra, the SEI in AN3 contains more Li3N (398.6 eV), which not only 
increases the mechanical strength to suppress the Li dendrite but also 
exhibits high Li-ion conductivity, contributing to the uniform and fast 
Li-ion flux [20]. Such results suggest the dense and smooth SEI layer in 
AN3 formed by the co-reactions between the free AN molecule and 
dissolved LPSs/LPSes. With more Li2SO4 and Li3N, the SEI layer formed 
in AN3 is smoother and more compact, and the uniform and fast Li 
plating/stripping was realized. To further confirm the function of the SEI 
layer, asymmetrical Li||Li cells were assembled (Fig. S17 in Supple­
mentary Information). The Li||Li cell with fresh Li anodes shows a huge 
voltage fluctuation after only 15 h, which indicates the drastic parasitic 
reaction between the Li anode and AN3. However, with the Li anodes 
disassembled from the cycled Li-SeS2 cells in AN3 as electrodes, the Li|| 
Li cell shows an overpotential of < 0.1 V even after 100 h, indicating the

Schematic diagrams are present in Fig. 3f and g to explain the SEI 
formation in sparingly-solvating AN2 and weakly-solvating AN3 elec­
trolytes. In AN2, all solvent molecules are effectively blended with 
LiTFSI and a Li+(AN)2TFSI−complex is formed [4d,8]. Due to the lack of 
free AN molecules, the dissolution of the LPSs/LPSes intermediates be­
comes difficult and thus results in a ‘solid-solid’ reaction pathway in 
AN2 for Li-SeS2 cells. In such sparingly-solvating electrolytes, the SEI 
layer formed on the surface of Li anode is loose and unstable, leading to 
poor cycling stability of the cells. While in AN3, the increased free AN 
molecule allows the dissolution of more LPSs/LPSes intermediates, the 
Li-SeS2 cells undergo an LPSs/LPSes mediated conversion process. 
However, due to the low solubility of LPSs/LPSes intermediates in AN3, 
the “shuttle effect” of the cell is negligible (Fig. S18 in Supplementary 
Information). The dissolved LPSs/LPSes intermediates facilitate the 
conversion between S (Se) and its discharge products and help to form a 
robust and stable SEI layer on the surface of Li anodes, thus leading to 
prolonged cycle life with improved rate capability. 
For the practical applications of Li-S(Se) batteries, one of the ob­
stacles is the use of superfluous electrolyte, which counteracts the 
advantage of Li-S(Se) batteries in energy density. The electrochemical 
behaviors of Li-SeS2 batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions were 
investigated in AN2 and AN3. As the results shown in Fig. 4a, with high- 
loading (9.5 mg cm−1) and high-active-material-content (72wt%) SeS2 
cathodes, the Li-SeS2 battery in AN3 obtains a reversible capacity of 844 
mAh g−1, corresponding to an active material utilization of 79%, at an 
E/A ratio of 2 μL mg−1. Even under a harsh test condition with an ul­
tralow E/A ratio of 1 μL mg−1, a reversible capacity of 713 mAh g−1 
could still be achieved without obvious polarization increase or capacity 
decay after 3 cycles (Fig. 4b), and an energy density of 370 Wh kg−1 
could be achieved when considering the mass of cathode, cathode col­
lector, anode, and electrolyte (Table S1 in Supplementary Information). 
When the E/A ratio was increased to 3 μL mg−1, a stable cycle capacity 
of 734 mAh g−1 was obtained after 12 cycles with a SeS2 cathode of 8.0 
mg cm−2 (Fig. S19 in Supplementary Information), while the cell in AN2 
died at 6th cycle due to the irreversible charging process (Fig. S20 in 
Supplementary Information). With an ultrahigh loading SeS2 cathode 
(15.0 mg cm−2), a specific capacity of 670 mAh g−1, corresponding to an 
area capacity of > 10 mAh cm−2 was obtained for 11 cycles at 0.25 mA 
cm−2 at 2 μL mg−1 (Fig. S21 in Supplementary Information). In contrast, 
the Li-SeS2 batteries in AN2 could not finish the charging process at an 
E/A ratio of 1 μL mg−1, while the one in ether-based electrolytes with 
LiNO3 failed at 3 μL mg−1 (Fig. S22 in Supplementary Information). We 
further evaluated the electrochemical performance of Li-SeS2 batteries 
in AN3 with a pouch cell. As shown in Fig. 4c, a high capacity of 662 
mAh g−1 with a capacity fading rate of 0.5% was attained after 70 cycles, 
demonstrating the great potential of AN3 for practical applications.

3. Conclusion 
In summary, by introducing more free AN molecules into the 
sparingly-solvating AN2 electrolyte, we successfully regulated the 
structure and solvating behaviors of the AN-based electrolytes. The new- 
formulated weakly-solvating electrolyte increases the solubility of the 
LPSs/LPSes intermediates and promotes the discharge-charge reactions 
of Li-SeS2 batteries. In addition, with the assistance of the dissolved 
intermediates, a robust and stable SEI layer was generated on the surface 
of Li anodes, which guarantees the long-term cycling stability of Li-SeS2 
batteries. Even under a harsh test condition of ultralow E/A ratio (1 μL 
mg−1) and high active loading/content (9.5 mg cm−1, 72 wt%), the Li- 
SeS2 cell could still deliver a high reversible capacity of 713 mAh g−1. 
This work may open up new possibilities for the development of 
competitive Li-S(Se) batteries with high energy density and long-term 
stability.
