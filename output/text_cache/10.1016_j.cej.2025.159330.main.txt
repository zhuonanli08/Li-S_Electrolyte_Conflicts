A B S T R A C T
Li-S batteries are attractive due to its high energy density, while its application is severely hindered by the 
sluggish kinetics and notorious side reactions between the soluble polysulfides and lithium metal anode. Herein, 
the concept of steric tuning electrolyte is proposed to facilitate the sulfur redox reaction and maintain a superb 
metallic Li compatibility via utilizing suitable methyl propyl ether as co-solvent for electrolyte-starved energy- 
dense Li-S batteries. By combining ab-initio molecular dynamics simulations, spectral characterization, and in- 
situ measurements of the electrochemical cell, it is confirmed that the stereochemistry manipulation endows 
the electrolyte with a weakened solvation ability to enhance the reaction kinetics and forms an encapsulated 
polysulfide solvation structure to prohibit the anode parasitic reactions from polysulfides. In virtue of the 
optimized local environment of STE, the Li-S cells deliver significantly improved electrochemical performance 
under lean electrolyte conditions. Moreover, the Li-S cells could stably operates under −30 ◦C for 100 cycles at 
0.5C, which may promote the practical application in cold environment.
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have emerged as a promising energy 
storage system, capturing significant attention in recent years. Their 
immense appeal stems from the impressive theoretical capacity and the 
use of sulfur–a low-cost, abundant, and environmentally benign mate­
rial [1–4]. Nevertheless, the pathway to their commercialization is 
fraught with challenges. Among these, the infamous “shuttle effect” 
stands out, where soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPS) migrate between 
the electrodes, leading to rapid capacity decay and self-discharge, 
especially for the cell operation under lean electrolyte condition 
[5–8]. Moreover, the inherent sluggish kinetics associated with the 
multi-step conversion of sulfur redox reaction (SRR) poses a significant 
hurdle to achieving high rate performance [9–12]. Additionally, in­
compatibility between the electrode materials and electrolyte compro­
mises both the cell’s overall efficiency and its lifespan [13]. These issues, 
while being actively researched, remain critical barriers that need to be 
conquered to boost the implementation of Li-S batteries [14–16].
Traditional strategies of physical confinement or chemical adsorp­
tion focus too much on the sulfur cathode [17–21], failing to solve the 
intertwined issues of Li metal anode, especially for the Li/electrolyte 
compatibility and the parasitical side reactions between the polysulfides 
(PS) and Li metal, both of which incur the loss of active materials, rapid 
capacity decay, and even safety concerns [22–24]. Electrolyte engi­
neering including solvent recipe modification and salt additive optimi­
zation provides a new platform to manipulate both the Li-S chemistry 
and solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) structure, which generally fall 
within the categories of sparingly solvating electrolytes (SSE) and highly 
solvating electrolytes (HSE). In the SSE class, the solvation of LiPS is 
largely ameliorated in the limited solvent and/or selective solvent, 
which requires the supporting salt to solvate more readily over the LiPS. 
However, it also faces daunting challenges such as high viscosity, low 
ionic conductivity, high cell polarization, and sluggish redox reaction 
kinetics. Adopting the opposite strategy, the HSE system provides a 
higher LiPS solubility at less electrolyte to achieve faster reaction ki­
netics. But the high reactivity of PS with Li metal anode requires special 
protection for the anode side and robust construction of SEI to avoid the 
cell dry-out and failure [16,25–27]. It can be found that the adaptations 
in the two motifs often fall short of meeting rigorous standards for lean 
electrolyte usage in high-energy–density Li-S batteries. Under this 
backdrop, focusing on regulating the LiPS solvation structure, the 
concept of encapsulated polysulfide electrolytes (EPSE) was proposed

meanwhile maintaining decent kinetics of SRR as well as durability in 
energy-dense Li-S cells, which to some extent solves the dilemma in SSE 
and HSE strategies. However, owing to the heterogeneous double-shell 
solvation structure constructed by the two concentric solvents, the 
EPSE-based Li-S batteries usually suffer from large cell polarization, 
poor rate performance, unsatisfactory utilization of active materials, and 
finally inferior energy density at the pouch cell level, failing to meet the 
ever-increasing energy demand [28–31]. Therefore, more exquisite 
solvation structure engineering and mechanistic understanding of the 
relationship between the solvation structure of LiPS and the reaction 
kinetics are urgently needed for synchronously addressing the cathode 
and anode issues towards practically available Li-S batteries.
As a proof-of-concept study to address the above-mentioned chal­
lenges in electrolyte engineering of Li-S batteries, herein, we propose the 
concept of steric tuning electrolyte (STE) to substantially accelerate the 
cathode sulfur reaction kinetics and maintain a superb metallic Li 
compatibility as well as mechanically robust SEI layer under lean elec­
trolyte condition via incorporating suitable methyl propyl ether (MPE). 
With the manipulation of stereochemistry, the steric hindrance effect of 
ether solvent molecules and the LiPS solvation structure are dramati­
cally optimized, which results in a stronger protection of the Li metal 
anode and promoted kinetics of SRR. Benefiting from the ameliorated 
local environment of STE, the electrolyte-starved Li-S cells deliver 
decent electrochemical performance at high sulfur loading, which is 
expected to promote the practical application of energy-dense Li-S 
batteries. Moreover, the Li-S cells could still operate well under the gelid 
condition, which may promote the practical application in cold 
environment.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials and electrode preparations
The high conductive graphene (HCG) was purchased from Suzhou 
Tanfeng Graphene Technology Co. Ltd. For the HCG/S preparation, the S 
was infiltrated into HCG using a melt-diffusion strategy at 160 ◦C for 24 
h, forming well-contacted and homogeneous HCG/S composites. The 
cathode materials were prepared by mixing HCG/S, acetylene black, and 
polyvinylende fluoride (PVDF) binder in a weight ratio of 8: 1: 1 in N- 
methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The slurry was then coated onto the 
aluminum foil and dried at 50 ◦C for 12 h. Afterwards, the dried coated 
aluminum foil was punched into disks with a diameter of 14 mm for Li-S 
coin cell use, and the loading of the active-material was ~ 1.1 mg cm−2.
2.2. Preparation of electrolyte
Conventional electrolyte (CE), i.e., 1.0 M LiTFSI in 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL)/ dimethoxyethane (DME) (1: 1, by vol.), and STE, i.e., 1.0 M 
LiTFSI in MPE/DOL/DME (4: 3: 3, by vol.), and 2 wt% LiNO3 was 
employed. The electrolyte was prepared in an Ar-filled glove box.
3. Results and discussion
With an aim to screen a target solvent with relatively low solvation 
ability and relatively higher reduction stability, one of the O atoms in 
the dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent was tentatively substituted with a C 
atom to eliminate the chelation effect and provide the electron feedback 
to the H atoms, which results in the conceptual molecule of methyl butyl 
ether (MBE) (Fig. 1a) [32,33]. However, the high flexibility of long- 
chain C enables MBE solvent with higher steric hindrance effect that 
should otherwise be avoided for achieving better reaction dynamics. To 
this end, one C atom is further eliminated from the MBE molecular, 
which readily reaps the target of MPE. Comparing with the traditional 
solvent of DME and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), the MPE solvent carries a 
shorter chain and the decrease in O atom endows the H atom with a 
better ability to avoid the nucleophilic attack of PS with high reduction 
reactivity (Fig. 1a), which are highly favorable for propelling the reac­
tion kinetics of Li-S chemistry (vide infra). Meanwhile, the shrinkage in 
molecular volume would endow MPE with less steric effect to accelerate 
Li+ de-solvation process and block the Li+-ether complex, thus 
increasing the reaction kinetics [32]. Moreover, as an extra bonus, the

[34,35]. The MPE solvents, characterized by their smaller molecular size 
and reduced molecular polarity, stand out due to the uniquely tailored 
steric effects they present. These inherent molecular attributes translate 
into a significant reduction in viscosity when compared to traditional 
DOL/DME electrolyte. To qualitatively assess these viscosity properties, 
we employed the GROMACS simulation package to perform the viscosity 
simulations [36,37]. And its real value was tested by rheometer. The 
incorporation amount of MPE (40 vol%) was first investigated to realize 
the best kinetic performance (Fig. S1). The viscosity simulation results 
and experimental test results jointly validate that the MPE-incorporated 
electrolyte exhibits ultra-low viscosity (Fig. S2), which facilitates the 
ionic mass transfer within electrolyte, thus enhancing the overall effi­
ciency of the electrochemical processes involved in Li-S cells. Addi­
tionally, this ultra-low viscosity would benefit the penetration of 
electrolyte into electrode, as evidenced by the diminutive contact angle 
between the electrode and MPE-incorporated electrolyte (Fig. S3). 
Theoretically, better wettability of the electrolyte on the material’s 
surface leads to improved interfacial kinetics and less electrolyte 
required to maintain the same reaction kinetics, which is advantageous 
for achieving better electrochemical performance under lean electrolyte 
condition, thus increasing the energy density of cell [38].
The inherent molecular properties of MPE were systematically 
investigated for an understanding of the function of target solvent. By 
calculating the electrostatic potential (ESP), it is found that the electron 
density isosurface of MPE is more neutralized (Fig. 1b), indicating that 
the H atoms of MPE possess enhanced electronegativity while the O 
atom possess weakened electropositivity. The ameliorated molecular 
polarization should benefit from the reduced O atom number. Since the 
long-range secondary electrostatic interactions can be negligible in MPE 
molecule [39], the ESP behavior of MPE predicts that it not only helps 
reduce the side reactions brought by the disastrous nucleophilic attack 
by the PS species with high reduction reactivity but also contributes to a 
better compatibility between the electrolyte and Li anode. We further 
utilized the Multiwfn software package to get a quantification analysis of 
the electrostatic reactions of the given solvent [28,40]. As can be seen in 
Fig. 1c, the areas of MPE solvent whose ESP energy is closer to zero 
account for a larger contribution percentage, consistent with the above 
direct visual results (Fig. 1b). More interestingly, we found the lowest 
unoccupied molecular orbit (LUMO) energy level of MPE is elevated 
distinctly (Fig. S4), which insinuates the improved reduction stability 
and better compatibility with both the PS species and Li anode [41,42], 
according to the frontier orbital theory. This is also substantially vali­
dated by the reduction stability tests, where the MPE-incorporated salt- 
free electrolyte shows an ameliorated side reaction currents comparing 
with its counterpart (Fig. 1d).
After a systematic analysis of the stability and compatibility of MPE 
at a molecular scale, the solvation structures of electrolyte were further 
probed with quantum chemistry and molecular dynamics (MD) utilizing 
ORCA and GROMACS software, respectively. Naturally, the elimination 
of chelation effect in MPE weakens the Li+ cation binding capability as 
can be vividly demonstrated by the binding energy calculation (Fig. 2a). 
Though MPE solvent has a stronger binding energy relative to DOL, the 
MPE would still have weakened solvation capability because the two 
adjacent O atoms in DOL endows MPE with elevated possibilities to bind 
with Li+ [43]. As confirmed by the MD simulations (Fig. 2b), the 
collected radial distribution function (rdf) signifies that MPE solvent has 
a much smaller g(r) peak value comparing to DME and DOL solvents 
(Fig. S6), which means that the solvation ability of Li+ follows the trend: 
DME > DOL > MPE. The rdf results verify our hypothesis that the sol­
vation ability is related to both the binding energy and the numbers of 
atoms that coordinates to the Li+ [44]. The increased g(r) peak value of 
Li+-TFSI−after adding the MPE solvent further proves our hypothesis as 
an increased g(r) of the cation–anion can be traced to the weakened 
solvation ability of the electrolyte (Fig. 2c). Given that the MPE solvent 
in electrolyte participates in the dissociation of lithium salts (Fig. 2c), 
there is a reasonable prospect that the MPE competitively participate in

As witnessed in Fig. 2d, the MPE mainly exists in the second shell of the 
solvation structure, reaffirming the weak solvation ability of MPE

Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate the solvation 
structure of STE (Fig. 2e). Peaks observed at approximately 820 and 850 
cm−1 are attributed to the unbonded oxide bonds of isolated DME. 
Remarkably, the intensity of these peaks associated with free DME di­
minishes upon the addition of the weakly coordinating MPE, suggesting 
that Li+ has a stronger affinity for DME in comparison with that in the 
DME/MPE mixed solvents [34,35]. Additionally, the Raman peak 
centered at 740 cm−1 arising from the S-N-S stretches of TFSI−
[46]
shows a clear blue-shift with the presence of MPE, upon which the 
interaction of Li+ of TFSI−is less impaired (Fig. S7). These Raman results 
confirm the fact that the solvent activity of DME solvent is substantially 
decreased by the addition of the weakly solvating MPE, meanwhile 
LiNO3 and LiTFSI would be preferentially reduced on the lithium sur­
face, which is underpinned by the 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance 
(NMR) measurements. When the Li2S8 was added into pure solvent 
mixtures of MPE/DME, the 7Li peak exhibits an appreciable down-shifts 
from −0.060 to −0.035 ppm (Fig. S8), contributed by reduction of the 
overall binding energy of Li+ ions with solvent molecule [29]. This 
sparingly solvating power to a Li+ ions dictates the recruitment of MPE 
into the secondary sheath of a Li+ ions, which decreases the amounts of 
DME and DOL that are supposed to preferentially interact with Li+ ions, 
thus inducing the deshielding of Li+ ions. Hence, the MPE molecules 
tend to distribute in the outer solvent shell around PS and the inner shell 
is mainly composed of DOL and DME [28], by which PS are encapsulated 
in anti-reductive solvent shells to decrease its reactivity towards Li 
anode, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2f.

ture and the Li de-solvation dynamics in STE, the numbers of Li+ ions 
with different solvation structure configurations were counted using MD 
simulations [47]. The snapshots obtained from MD simulations for 
conventional electrolyte (CE) and STE are displayed in Fig. 3a and 3b.

in electrolyte, we first examined each Li+ in the simulation systems and

Fig. 3a and 3b. To simulate the actual state of electrolyte working in Li-S

Correspondingly, the de-solvation free energy of each solvation struc­
ture was systematically determined by DFT calculations. It can be found 
in Fig. 3d and Fig. S9 that the Li300 solvation structure possesses the 
highest de-solvation energy and this solvation structure only exists in the 
CE system (Table S2, S3). Moreover, when we extracted the main sol­
vation structure from the data recorded in tabulation, we found that the 
main solvation structure is transformed from Li200 to Li100 (Fig. 3e, 
Fig. S10), a typical symbol of decrease in the average de-solvation en­
ergy (ADE). Based on these derived statistical results, the ADE in CE and 
STE was further calculated using weighted average methods. As depic­
ted in Fig. 3f, the de-solvation free energy is markedly decreased with 
the presence of MPE, which is consolidated by our experimental mea­
surements according to fitting the temperature-dependent electro­
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) shown in Fig. S11 [48]. The 
above results indicate that the incorporation of MPE could optimize the 
electrolyte structure, in which the MPE molecules serve as the building 
blocks of the outer solvent shell where the parasitic reactions between 
the PS and Li metal are kinetically obstructed by the anti-reductive shell, 
thus promoting the kinetics of electrode reaction and mitigating its 
exposure to fresh Li.
The Li compatibility performances were further investigated by the 
Li||Cu and Li||Li cycling tests. As shown in Fig. 3h and Fig. S13, with the 
incorporation of MPE, the Li||Cu cell presents superior cycling stability 
and smaller overpotential with a stable coulombic efficiency of 98.5 % 
over 300 cycles, meaning the significantly enhanced reversibility and 
suppressed side reactions between electrolyte and Li metal during the Li 
stripping/plating process. The morphologies of Li metal after 300 cycles 
were further tested. A rough surface full of bumps and hollows are 
visualized on the Li surface for the CE system, whereas the Li surface in 
the STE is tight and smooth (Fig. S14). A more uniform Li deposition 
insinuates the great potential of good Li compatibility imparted by MPE 
solvent, which favors Li+ diffusion on the surface of Li anode and 
amelioration of the overpotential. This is also reflected by a thinner 
thickness of the deposited Li on Cu collector (Fig. S15). Additionally, 
irrespective of the low or high current density, the symmetrical Li||Li 
cell in the STE works with a smaller overpotential in contrast with the 
unstable stripping/plating potential observed in the CE (Fig. S16a, b, d), 
substantiating the superior Li stripping/plating process. When the cur­
rent density increases to 3 mA cm−2, the cell with CE shows obvious 
short-circuit phenomenon, while STE can still work normally. Mean­
while, it can be clearly observed that after adding MPE, the over­
potential decreases significantly (Fig. S16c). The satisfactory 
performances of Li||Li cells further confirm the encouraging application 
prospect of MPE in constructing highly stable and effective interface. 
Moreover, the observed Li foils surface morphology in symmetrical Li|| 
Li cells reaffirms the regulation effect of MPE on the Li anode (Fig. S17). 
Furthermore, finite element analysis was performed to probe how the 
MPE would influence the Li morphology and we found that the Li 
deposition morphology shows a more obtuse angle with the presence of 
MPE (Fig. 3g, Fig. S12), suggesting that the MPE could realize a more 
favorable Li striping/platting process. In addition, a more uniform 
electrolyte concentration distribution is observed in the finite element 
simulation, indicative of a relieved polarization.
Besides the compatibility between the STE and Li anode, the kinetics 
of SRR was further studied by the ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) 
simulation [44]. As can be seen in Fig. 4a, the free energy surface (FES) 
derived from the AIMD results in the CE system performs a ragged shape 
while the STE shows a smoother one. Notably, when inspecting the Li+
diffusion route into the PS monomer, we found there are so many local 
minima and energy barriers in the FES where the Li+ cations are easy to 
get trapped into the local minima, meaning the decelerated SRR process. 
In sharp contrast, Li+ can easily enter the PS monomer in the STE, 
vividly demonstrating the accelerated kinetics of SRR process (Fig. 4b). 
The FES simulation results strongly prove that the decrease in carbon- 
chain length induces a reduced steric hindrance for the accessibility of 
Li+ to the PS monomer, which accelerates the sulfur transformation 
during SRR process. The accelerated SRR dynamics is also proved by the 
in-situ EIS measurements (Fig. 4c, d, Fig. S18). The charge transfer 
resistance of the cells in STE is lower than that in CE, indicative of faster 
overall reaction kinetics. In a detailed examination, at a 60 % depth of

Considering the fast SRR dynamics originated from the better de- 
solvation kinetics, lower viscosity, and inhibited side reactions in the 
STE system, the performances of STE in gelid conditions were evaluated 
to further exploit its potential in wide-temperature applications 
(Fig. S26). The cells with STE can still operate well under −30 ◦C even at 
3C while the cell with CE almost stagnates when the current density is 
elevated to 1C. Especially, the cell with CE exhibits a sloping and short 
second discharge plateau in the charge/discharge profile (Fig. S27a), 
which can be indicative of the inhibited pathway of solid–liquid-solid 
transformation in SRR owing to the tardy Li+ diffusion and SRR process 
[53,54]. In comparison, the cell with STE still exhibits the traditional 
discharge behavior, albeit with the appreciable polarization voltage 
(Fig. S27b), clearly manifesting the enhanced redox kinetics in STE. 
Importantly, the cells with STE could stably operates under −30 ◦C for 
100 cycles at 0.5C (Fig. 5e), whereas the cells with CE exhibit signifi­
cantly degraded capacity attainment, incarnating the great application 
potentials over wide-temperature conditions for STE.
To better understand the appealing superiorities of STE in Li-S 
electrochemistry, we conducted ex-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) mea­
surements to track the speciation evolution of the solid sulfur species 
during the discharge/charge process. As the discharge proceeds, the Li2S 
and S8 signal in the CE show an inappreciable variation (Fig. 6a) 
[55,56], signifying the slow transformation from S8 to Li2S. In contrast, 
the Li2S signal in the STE gradually increases, accompanied by the 
gradual decreases in S8, indicating an ideal S transformation and ener­
getically favorable SRR process (Fig. 6b). Moreover, this alternant 
emergence and disappearance of S8 and Li2S species is more pronounced 
in the subsequent charge process for STE system compared with that in 
CE. Although the PS dissolution is suppressed, the Li-S dynamics in STE 
still outperforms the CE due to the reduced steric effect which reduces 
the steric hindrance to construct the accessible Li+ diffusion channel to 
the PS. Given that the morphology of the cathode plays a crucial role in 
reflecting the intricate interplay between the electrolyte and the elec­
trode, the cells after cycling in Li-S batteries were disassemble to analyze 
the impact of STE on the interface. In the case of CE, the cathode exhibits 
a bulky and aggregated appearance (Fig. S28a, c). This is primarily

The Li anode surface of the cycled Li-S cells was also analysed by X-

the enhanced electrochemical performance. In the F 1 s spectra, we

abundant LiF species is instrumental to the passivation of Li surface and 
mitigation of adverse parasitic reactions between PS and Li anode, both 
of which contribute to the effective Li anode protection and inhibition of 
dead lithium [45]. In addition to the increased LiF content, we also 
detected a significant presence of Li3N species on the cycled Li surface in 
cells with STE (Fig. 6e, f). Given the higher ionic conductivity and sur­

LiF reasons the formation of a compact and smooth SEI layer, thus 
effectively inhibiting the corrosion and consumption of Li anode, in 
congruence with our observations in Li||Li and Li||Cu cells (Fig. S14, 
S15, S17). We further analyzed the S 2p spectra to examine the distri­

4. Conclusions
The stereochemistry-directed solvent molecular design aiming at 
modulating the local environment of the electrolyte to coordinate 
weakly with Li+ was proposed to address the comprehensive issues of Li- 
S electrochemistry. With weak solvation ability and lowered steric effect 
of MPE, the STE guarantees a significantly alleviated parasitic reactions 
between PS and Li anode as well as a promoted kinetics of SRR process. 
In virtue of these preponderances of STE, the Li-S batteries with prac­
tically relevant parameters (5.2 mg cm−2 and 5.0 μL mg−1) deliver 
decent electrochemical performances. Moreover, benefiting from the 
better desolvation kinetics, the cell with STE exhibits substantial po­
tential in the application of gelid conditions. In light of the economy and 
simplicity, the motif of STE is expected to promote the practical appli­
cation of energy-dense Li-S batteries and even other alkali metal-sulfur 
batteries.
