ABSTRACT: In this study, a fluorine-free aromatic cosolvent
strategy is presented to regulate electrolyte solvation chemistry in
Li−S batteries with sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathodes.
Assisted by the Uni-ELF AI tool and experimental validation,
toluene is identified as an optimal weakly solvating cosolvent. Its
incorporation compresses the Li+ solvation sheath and induces an
anion-dominated solvation structure, thereby enhancing interfacial
ion transport and sulfur redox kinetics through controlled π−π
interactions with polysulfides. Consequently, Li || Li symmetric
cells exhibit stable cycling for over 1,000 cycles at a current density
of 1 mA cm−2. Meanwhile, Li−S cells employing high-loading SPAN cathodes retain more than 75% of their initial capacity after 250
cycles at −10 °C. Additionally, a practical pouch cell with high SPAN loading and a low electrolyte-to-SPAN ratio of 3 μL mg−1
delivers an initial capacity of around 600 mAh gSPAN−1, underscoring the potential of fluorine-free electrolytes for practical metal−
sulfur batteries.
W
ith the escalating global demand for high-energy-
density and cost-effective energy storage systems,
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have emerged as a promising
next-generation technology.1 Their exceptionally high theoreti-
cal gravimetric capacity (1,672 mA h g−1) highlights the
significant energy-density advantages of Li−S batteries. In
addition, the intrinsic low density, cost-effectiveness, and earth
abundance of sulfur render Li−S batteries an appealing
alternative to conventional lithium-ion chemistries, particularly
for large-scale energy storage and weight-sensitive applications.
Nevertheless, the practical deployment of Li−S batteries
remains severely constrained by the dissolution and long-range
migration of lithium polysulfides, a phenomenon commonly
known as the shuttle effect. This process induces continuous
loss of electrochemically active sulfur species and accelerates
the degradation of lithium−metal anodes, particularly under
elevated-temperature conditions. Furthermore, the sluggish
sulfur conversion chemistry impedes their operation under
low-temperature conditions. Therefore, the development of
practical Li−S batteries necessitates addressing several critical
challenges, including ensuring stable operation over a wide
temperature range, effectively suppressing the polysulfide
shuttle effect, and maintaining robust electrochemical perform-
ance under stringent cell configurations such as low electrolyte-
to-sulfur ratios (E/S) and high active-material loadings.
Toward practical Li−S battery applications, sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has emerged as a compelling sulfur-
containing active material.2,3 By anchoring sulfur to the carbon
backbone through covalent bonding, SPAN may reduce the
formation of long-chain polysulfides. Nevertheless, polysulfide
shuttling remains a critical challenge during the sulfur
conversion process, as it typically follows a solid−liquid−
solid pathway, particularly in ether- based electrolytes.4−6
Furthermore, high-load SPAN cathodes often exhibit sluggish
sulfur redox kinetics, because of the complex interfacial
interactions between the electrolyte and the electrode.
Consequently, the development of a tailored electrolyte for a
practical Li−S battery with SPAN cathodes is imperative to
facilitate a mechanistic shift from solid−liquid−solid to quasi-
solid−solid sulfur conversion. Such a design not only mitigates
shuttling and optimizes reaction kinetics but also ensures
robust electrochemical stability against the lithium−metal
anode.7
The electrolyte functions as the cation carrier that sustains
cell operation and bridges the electrochemical reactions
between the cathode and the anode. In particular, electrolytes
play a pivotal role in dictating the electrochemical performance
of sulfur-based batteries.8 They not only govern the kinetics of
sulfur redox conversion but also determine the severity of the
polysulfide shuttle effect. Recent advances in electrolyte
engineering for Li-SPAN batteries have focused on regulating
the electrode−electrolyte interphase to achieve enhanced cell

(WSEs),11−13 and additive strategies.14,15 The central design
principle involves restructuring the Li+ within the first solvation
shell to incorporate a greater proportion of anions. This
increased Li+−anion interaction reduces solvent decomposi-
tion and promotes the formation of an anion-derived inorganic
solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI), ensuring both high ionic
conductivity and reliable electronic passivation at the anode
side.16,17 Notably, in sulfur-based systems, the strengthened
Li+−anion clustering within the electrolyte effectively reduces
polysulfide solubility, thereby mitigating the active material
leakage and the shuttle effect during cycling.18
Fluorinated ethers have traditionally served as prototypical
solvents for regulating solvation structures in electrolytes,
owing to their intrinsically low donor ability and low dielectric
permittivity.19 However, fluorine-containing compounds,
commonly identified as polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS),
present serious environmental and occupational health risks.10
In addition, their inherently high density and considerable cost
further exacerbate these limitations. Consequently, the
dependence on fluorinated solvents imposes substantial
constraints on their viability for large-scale and practical
applications. Intriguingly, aromatic solvents exhibit weak
solvating ability toward Li+ ions, attributed to the limited
π−π interactions of the benzene ring.20,21 Moreover, the
electron density of the benzene ring in aromatic molecules can
be finely modulated by altering the substituents, thereby
influencing their physicochemical properties and consequently
affecting the lithium plating behavior and SEI formation.
Furthermore, most aromatic solvents exhibit moderate density
and are costless compared to fluorinated ethers, implying their
high application potential in the electrolyte field.
To the best of our knowledge, a systematic discussion of
fluorine-free aromatic solvents as solvation regulators in Li−S
systems, particularly in terms of tailoring Li+ coordination and
electrochemical stability through substituent-dependent elec-
tronic effects, has not been previously reported.
Herein, we introduce a fluorine-free aromatic cosolvent into
the electrolyte for practical Li−S batteries with a SPAN
cathode, particularly under wide-temperature (−10−60 °C)
operating conditions. This tailored electrolyte strategy anchors
and modulates lithium polysulfides while enhancing sulfur
conversion kinetics through controlled π−π interactions.
Initially, the Uni-ELF AI tool, a novel multilevel representation
learning framework to advance electrolyte design, was
employed to screen potential electrolyte cosolvent candidates
through an assessment of their basic physical properties and
electrochemical stability. Then, a set of well-defined aromatic
molecules, such as toluene, anisole, and benzyl chloride, is
introduced as electrolyte cosolvents and comprehensively
evaluated with long-term cycling measurements and simulation
analyses. Toluene is subsequently identified as the most
suitable electrolyte cosolvent for the Li−S system. We
demonstrate that its incorporation effectively compresses the
solvation sheath and tunes the solvation behavior through its
weak solvating ability toward Li ions, which in turn influences
the electrode−electrolyte interphase, electrochemical perform-
ance, and sulfur conversion reactions. Consequently, this
compressed electrolyte formulated with the optimized fluorine-
free aromatic cosolvent enables Li || Li cells to maintain stable
lithium stripping and plating behavior for over 1,000 cycles at a
high current density of 1 mA cm−2. Low-temperature Li−S
half-cells also exhibit markedly improved cyclability at −10 °C,
retaining more than 75% of their capacity after 250 cycles at a
C/5 rate. Furthermore, a pouch cell incorporating a high-
loading SPAN cathode (5 mg cm−2, 24 cm2) and a low
electrolyte-to-SPAN (E/S) ratio of 3 μL mg−1, together with
the toluene-based electrolyte, delivers an initial specific
capacity of around 600 mAh gSPAN−1 at C/5. This indicates
that incorporating a fluorine-free aromatic cosolvent into the
electrolyte not only lowers the overall cost of Li-SPAN

In contrast to conventional lithium/sodium-ion batteries,
the presence of polysulfide intermediates in sulfur-based
systems gives rise to a more intricate electrolyte environment
during cycling, exerting a pronounced influence on both the
cathode and the anode processes. Moreover, the electro-
chemical stability of electrolyte solvents is critical because they
may undergo decomposition during cycling. Although high
oxidation stability is not essential for Li−S batteries, sufficient
reduction stability is necessary due to the strong electro-
chemical reactivity between the alkali metal anode and the
electrolyte.22,23
In addition, the physical properties of the solvents, such as
the boiling point and melting point, strongly affect the overall
cell operation. To preliminarily screen suitable solvent
candidates for the Li−S system, the Uni-ELF AI tool was
employed to evaluate a range of aromatic solvents based on
their key physical properties (Table S1), including dielectric
constant, density, boiling point, melting point, and viscosity.24
It is worth mentioning that the predicted values of density,
boiling point, and melting point obtained from the Uni-ELF AI
tool match well with the experimental values, indicating the
reliability of this AI tool. Moreover, the intrinsic oxidative and
reductive tendencies of those solvents were assessed through
ionization and electron-affinity calculations. The vertical and
adiabatic ionization potentials (VIP and AIP) quantify the
energy required to remove an electron under fixed and relaxed
geometries, respectively.25,26 A larger AIP signifies a higher
energetic barrier toward electron removal, which correlates
with superior oxidative stability at elevated potentials.
Conversely, the vertical and adiabatic electron affinities (VEA
and AEA) describe the thermodynamic preference for
accepting an additional electron. Smaller or negative AEA
values indicate a diminished inclination for electron capture
and, therefore, reflect enhanced resistance to reductive
degradation at the Li−metal interface. Based on the screening
results obtained from the Uni-ELF AI tool, three aromatic
solvents, including toluene, anisole, and benzyl chloride, were
selected for systematic investigation as shown in Figure 1a. All
three candidates exhibit promising wide-temperature oper-
ability. Among them, toluene possesses a relatively low
dielectric constant, which efficiently reduces the interactions
between Li+ ions and solvent dipoles and consequently
enhances the Li+−FSI−interactions. It also demonstrates
superior reduction stability compared with the other solvents.
These characteristics motivated a detailed examination of the
three aromatic systems to elucidate how the reduction stability
influences the interfacial behavior of the Li−metal anode.
Accordingly, to more directly assess the suitability of
aromatic solvents, Li-SPAN cells were assembled with various
electrolytes containing different fluorine-free aromatic solvents
(FFASs). The designed electrolytes include 4 M LiFSI in THP
(tetrahydropyran) as well as 2 M LiFSI in THP/toluene,
THP/anisole, and THP/benzyl chloride (all with a 2:3 volume
ratio), denoted as 4M-THP, Tol, Anisole, and ClBen,
respectively. Under room-temperature conditions (Figure
1b), cells with 4M-THP exhibit gradual capacity fade after
100 cycles at C/2. This behavior indicates that THP is
compatible with both the SPAN cathode and Li−metal anode,
as confirmed in our previous study.27 However, the high
concentration of 4 M LiFSI in pure THP increases the
viscosity of the electrolyte, thereby hindering ionic con-
ductivity and resulting in a slight capacity decay during cycling
at a higher C rate of C/2. In contrast, cells with ClBen- and
anisole-based electrolytes suffer pronounced degradation
during the initial cycles and after 150 cycles, suggesting
possible side reactions between these solvents and both the
SPAN cathode and the Li−metal anode. This indicates that the
electrochemical stability of the solvent strongly influences the
cycling performance of the cell, particularly the reduction
stability that is critical in the Li−S system. This trend is
consistent with the predictions provided by the AI screening
tools. Notably, cells paired with the Tol-based electrolyte
deliver highly stable cycling with 85% capacity retention after
250 cycles at C/2 rate, even under practical conditions with a
high-loading SPAN cathode (4.5 mg cm−2) and a lean
electrolyte supply (6 μL mgSPAN−1). This finding implies
that toluene exhibits strong compatibility with both the SPAN
cathode and the Li−metal anode. Moreover, it may effectively
tune the electrolyte to form a compressed solvation structure,
even at a relatively low salt concentration of 2 M LiFSI.
In the following section, we further elucidate the role of
toluene as a cosolvent in the electrolyte. As mentioned,
substituents on the aromatic ring modify the electron density
distribution, thereby influencing the electrochemical properties
of the molecules. To elucidate the effects of various
substituents, theoretical density functional theory (DFT)
calculations were performed. The surface electrostatic potential
(ESP) mapping was first conducted to characterize the
electron density distribution of the solvent molecules. As
illustrated in Figure 1c, aromatic molecules exhibit a more
neutral electron density distribution than THP, reflecting their
low polarity.22 Furthermore, the oxygen atom in anisole
contributes a more negative charge density due to its electron-
donating nature, while the negative charge density in benzyl
chloride originates from the chloride substituent. Notably,
toluene exhibits a negative charge density arising from the
conjugation of the aromatic ring, which implies a stable charge
distribution and a lower reduced reactivity. Furthermore, the
dipole moment of toluene (0.4059 D) is much lower than that
of anisole (1.3965 D) and benzyl chloride (2.3482 D), which
suggests weaker interactions with Li+ in the electrolyte and
favors the formation of a compressed solvation structure.28,29
This is further confirmed by binding energy calculations
(Figure 1c). Whereas THP exhibits the strongest binding
energy with Li+ (−44.78 kcal/mol), all of the selected FFASs
display weaker interactions. Also, the substituents on the
aromatic ring significantly modulate the binding strength. For
instance, toluene (−41.34 kcal/mol) and anisole (−41.79 kcal/
mol) bearing electron-donating methyl and methoxy groups
show slightly higher binding energies, whereas benzyl chloride
(−40.34 kcal/mol), with an electron-withdrawing chloro
group, exhibits a lower value. However, binding energy alone
cannot fully determine the solvent effect in electrolytes. It is
worth noting that the highest occupied molecular orbital
(HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)
energy levels critically influence the solvent stability within the
electrolyte. These energy levels may shift due to electron
density redistribution induced by substituent groups.30 The
frontier orbital energies play a decisive role in determining the
electrochemical stability of the molecules and, consequently, in
governing the formation of the electrode−electrolyte interface.

lower LUMO energy levels than toluene, with benzyl chloride,

Moreover, the solvation structures within 4 and 6 Å of the
Li+ ion in both electrolytes, as obtained from MD simulations,
are depicted in Figure 2d,e. In the inner solvation structure of
the 4M-THP electrolyte, more THP molecules surround the

Tol electrolyte, there is a lower ratio of THP molecules
surrounding the Li+ ion at a closer distance. The Li+ ion
interacts with a higher density of anion (FSI−) species.
Moreover, the overall solvation structure of Li+ in the Tol
electrolyte exhibits a more compressed coordination environ-
ment than that in 4M-THP, indicating a pulling effect of the
toluene cosolvent that confines the anion-rich solvation
structure within the inner solvation shell.31 Accordingly, both
DFT calculations and MD simulations substantiate the
enhanced electrochemical stability of toluene compared to
others as well as its capacity to modulate the solvation
structure and compress the solvation sheath.

The interfacial stability between electrolytes and lithium−
metal anodes governs Li+ plating behavior, desolvation
processes, and transport kinetics, thereby dictating the long-
term cycling stability of Li−S batteries. To evaluate the effect
of electrolytes on lithium−metal stability, Li || Li symmetric
cells with two different electrolytes were first tested.
As shown in Figure 3a,b, the cells with 4M-THP at 1 mAh
cm−2 exhibit an increased overpotential after 300 h, whereas
those with Tol maintain a stable and low plating/stripping
overpotential of approximately 50 mV. Likewise, under a
higher current density of 2 mAh cm−2, the Tol-based cells still
demonstrate superior cycling stability compared to 4M-THP,
highlighting the beneficial role of toluene as a cosolvent in
stabilizing Li+ plating behavior. Similarly, the Li+-ion plating
behavior of these electrolyte was assessed through Li−Cu cells
at 1 mA cm−2/1 mAh cm−2 (Figure S4). Accordingly, the Li−
Cu cells employing the Tol electrolyte exhibit more stable and
continuous Li+ stripping/plating behavior compared to those
with 4M-THP. The Tol-based Li−Cu cells maintain a high
Coulombic efficiency of >99% over 500 cycles.

Accordingly, practical Li−S batteries must be engineered to
operate not only under ambient conditions but also across a
wide temperature spectrum to satisfy the stringent require-
ments of practical applications. Furthermore, enhancing the
energy density of these systems necessitates rigorous cell
configurations such as minimized electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S)
ratios and maximized active-material loadings. As a result, to
evaluate the electrolyte under practical conditions, Li−S cells
employing high-loading (4.5−8.5 mg cm−2) SPAN (∼46% S
content in SPAN) cathodes and a restricted electrolyte-to-
SPAN ratio of 4−6 μL mg−1 were assembled and tested over a
wide temperature range in this study.
Generally, the polysulfide shuttling effect exerts a more
pronounced influence at elevated temperatures due to the
increased solubility of the polysulfide species. However, the
tuned solvation sheath effectively stabilizes the cation−anion
coordination environment within the electrolyte, thereby
preventing undesirable interactions between polysulfide species
and solvent molecules. As illustrated in Figure 4a, Li-SPAN
cells with 4M-THP and Tol electrolytes both operate
efficiently at 60 °C, maintaining discharge capacities above
600 mAh gSPAN−1 after 150 cycles at C/2 rate. This behavior
is attributed to the anion-rich solvation structures of both
electrolytes, which effectively suppress the formation of soluble
polysulfide species within the electrolyte. Moreover, both
electrolytes exhibit similar ionic conductivities under high-
temperature conditions, thereby displaying stable cycling
performance.
A key challenge for Li-SPAN batteries is the sluggish sulfur
conversion kinetics, especially at low temperatures and high C
rates. Notably, the electrolyte plays a crucial role in modulating
the Li-ion desolvation behavior, which further influences the
sulfur conversion procedure and sulfur utilization.
As shown in Figure 4b, the cells with 4M-THP exhibit a
more rapid capacity decay at 0 °C under a C/2 rate, whereas
those employing the Tol-based electrolyte maintain a high
capacity retention of 75% after 150 cycles. Furthermore, even
at a lower temperature of −10 °C (Figure 4c), the Tol-
electrolyte cells still deliver an outstanding capacity retention
of 74% after 250 cycles at a C-rate of C/5.
In addition, impressive rate capacities of 598, 573, 550, 535,
520, 495, 473, and 420 mAh gSPAN−1 were obtained at C/10,
C/4, C/2, 3C/4, 1C, 3C/2, 2C, and 3C rates, respectively
(Figure 4d), underscoring the crucial role of fast Li+
desolvation in enabling superior rate performance. Moreover,
cells employing a high-loading SPAN cathode (>8.5 mg cm−2)
(Figure 4e) demonstrate excellent cycling stability, maintaining
an areal capacity of ∼5 mA h cm−2 after 100 cycles. Notably,
the cathode with over 8 mg cm−2 SPAN in this work
demonstrates outstanding electrochemical performance and
compares favorably with previously reported systems (Table
S3). This indicates that the incorporation of toluene effectively
tunes the solvation structure within the electrolyte, thereby
facilitating Li+ desolvation and accelerating sulfur redox
kinetics, particularly under low-temperature conditions and
in cells employing high-loading cathodes.

investigate the electrochemical transformations and kinetic
behavior of the SPAN cathode, thereby elucidating the
influence of toluene on the cycling performance. As shown
in Figure 4f,g and Figure S5, the cells with 4M-THP and
toluene-based electrolytes exhibit similar CV profiles, with an

To further validate the practical advantages of the toluene-
based electrolyte, 24 cm2 single-layer pouch cells were
fabricated with a high SPAN cathode loading of 5 mg cm−2
and a low electrolyte-to-SPAN (E/S) ratio of 3 μL mg−1 as
shown in Figure 4i,j. The pouch cell employing the toluene-
based electrolyte delivers a high initial specific capacity of
approximately 600 mA h g−1 at a high rate of C/5. After 55
cycles, it maintains stable cycling behavior with a high capacity
retention exceeding 70%. In addition, to further demonstrate
the relevance of our electrochemical cycling parameters, the
material-level energy density (including the mass of the
cathode, electrolyte, and Li metal) was calculated to be 150
W h kg−1. This result indicates that the toluene-based
electrolyte effectively enhances the cycling stability of the
pouch cell, owing to its compressed solvation structure and
lower viscosity. Consequently, even under practical operating
conditions, the toluene-based system enables the pouch cell to
deliver outstanding cycling performance.
To gain deeper mechanistic insights into electrolyte-
dependent SEI evolution, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) was employed to interrogate the electrode−electrolyte
interphases formed on lithium−metal anodes and SPAN
cathodes. As shown in Figure 5a,b, the S 2p and F 1s spectra
were examined to elucidate the chemical composition of the
SEI layer on the lithium−metal surface. The S 2p spectra for
lithium−metal anodes in both 4M-THP and Tol electrolytes
exhibit nearly identical profiles, indicating a consistent
decomposition of LiFSI. The detection of peaks at around
169.0 and 167.0 eV confirms the presence of insulating SO4
2−
and SO3
2−species.33,38,39 Moreover, the minimal intensity of
signals associated with polysulfide components, such as S0 (S
2p3/2 ∼163.7 eV), S1−(S 2p3/2 ∼161.7 eV), and Li2S (S
2p3/2 ∼160.1 eV), suggests that both electrolytes can
effectively mitigate polysulfide shuttling from the cathode to
the lithium−metal anode. Also, the anion-dominated solvation
sheath in both 4M-THP and Tol electrolytes promotes the
formation of an inorganic-rich SEI. Within this interface, LiF
constitutes a higher proportion compared with the S−F
components, reflecting the specific decomposition pathways of
the concentrated electrolyte systems. Notably, a greater
proportion of LiF is observed on the cycled lithium−metal
anode surface within the Tol electrolyte than in its 4M-THP
counterpart. These findings suggest that the densely
coordinated solvation sheath in the Tol electrolyte facilitates
a higher degree of anion degradation.
To further evaluate the solvent stability in the electrolyte,
the C 1s spectra of the cycled cathodes were examined in detail
(Figure 5c). The cathode with both electrolytes exhibits four
distinct peaks located at 284.8 eV (C−C/C−H), 286.4 eV
(C−O), 288.4 eV (CO), and 283.7 eV (Li−C−O). It is
possible that toluene undergoes partial decomposition during
initial cycles, and the corresponding CC related species may
overlap with the C−C signals due to the limitations of XPS
analysis. However, the intensity of the C−O and Li−C−O
peaks in the 4M-THP system is significantly higher than that in
the Tol electrolyte. This implies that THP molecules in the
4M-THP electrolyte undergo more severe decomposition
during the cycling process compared to those in the Tol-
based system.40
Moreover, the cycled lithium anodes from the Li-SPAN cells
were examined by SEM, as shown in Figure 5d. In contrast to
the 4M-THP system, lithium−metal anodes operated in the
Tol electrolyte develop markedly smoother and more
homogeneous surface morphology. This advantage originates
from the synergistic effects of a compact solvation environment
and accelerated Li+ desolvation kinetics, which together
facilitate regulated interfacial processes. As a result, cells
employing the Tol electrolyte deliver superior electrochemical
performance, particularly under low-temperature conditions, as
manifested by prolonged cycling durability, enhanced rate
capability, and a high reversible capacity.
In summary, we presented the rational design of a
nonfluorinated electrolyte system incorporating toluene as a
strategic cosolvent for high-performance Li-SPAN batteries.
Leveraged by AI-driven screening and density functional
theory calculations, toluene was identified for its optimal
physical properties and robust electrochemical stability. The
introduction of toluene reconfigures the electrolyte into a
compact and anion-rich solvation sheath, which effectively
suppresses polysulfide shuttling and facilitates the formation of
a resilient inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase on the
lithium−metal anode. This tailored solvation structure
enhances ionic conductivity under subzero conditions and
enables Li- SPAN cells to retain more than 75% of their initial
capacity after 250 cycles at −10 °C. Furthermore, the practical
viability of this system was demonstrated in high-loading
pouch cells with a low electrolyte-to-active material ratio of 3
μL mg−1, delivering an initial specific capacity of around 600
mAh g−1 with over 70% retention after 55 cycles. This work
provides fundamental insights into fluorine-free aromatic
cosolvents and establishes a scalable pathway for the
development of cost-effective and high-energy-density metal−
sulfur batteries.
