Spatial Progression of Polysulfide Reactivity
with Lithium Nitrate in Li−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: LiNO3 is a common electrolyte additive in Li−S
batteries, but its stabilizing effect is not well-understood due to the
complex electrolyte chemistry. This complexity often hampers the
clear characterization and interpretation of data. Herein, we explore
the LiNO3 reactivity with polysulfide through in operando sulfur K-
edge spectroscopy, using a sulfur-free electrolyte with LiNO3 as the
sole salt. We reveal a spatially progressing chemical reaction influenced
by the polysulfide concentration gradient. Polysulfides are electro-
chemically generated near the sulfur cathode, leading to a high local
concentration. As a result, they are incompletely oxidized by LiNO3 to
sulfites, which are gradually further oxidized into sulfonates and
sulfates. Conversely, polysulfides near the anode side have a lower
local concentration as they are diffused from the cathode side, thus
leading to more highly oxidized species like sulfonates and sulfates. These reaction products are stable during electrochemical
cycling, suggesting their capabilities to passivate the electrodes and contribute to the cycling stabilities of Li−S batteries.
L
i−S batteries have garnered renewed attention owing to
the high capacity and energy density that can be
achieved by simply utilizing sulfur that is cheap and
abundant.1−3 However, a significant challenge faced by Li−S
batteries, particularly those using elemental sulfur as cathodes,
is the issue of polysulfide dissolution.4,5 As the battery
operates, long-chain polysulfides are formed as intermediates
on the sulfur cathodes.3 These polysulfides have high solubility
in ether-based electrolytes which are commonly used in Li−S
batteries due to their compatibility with the Li metal anode.6,7
The dissolved polysulfides then migrate through the electrolyte
to the anode side, where they react with the Li metal to form
Li2S and Li2S2.8 This interaction known as polysulfide shuttling
is irreversible, which leads to the consumption of active
material from the sulfur cathode and shortens the cycle life of
Li−S batteries.9,10
LiNO3 is widely utilized as an additive in electrolytes for Li−
S batteries to prevent polysulfide shuttling.11,12 However, the
working mechanism of LiNO3 remains a topic of intense
debate.13−15 One popular mechanism is that LiNO3 forms a
protection layer on the surface of the Li metal anode and
prevents irreversible interaction of polysulfide with the Li
metal.16 LiNO3 is suggested to make a robust electrical double
layer on the interface of the Li metal prior to the solid-
electrolyte interphase formation.17 Such an electrical double
layer allows the formation of a passivating interphase rich in
LiNO3 decomposition products that can modulate the Li metal
reactivity. It is also believed that LiNO3 participates in the
formation of the solid-electrolyte interphase by oxidizing
polysulfide into LixSOy-like species, while itself undergoing
reduction to LixNOy (y < 3).14 Both the oxidized sulfur species
and the reduced LixNOy become part of the SEI, which
minimizes direct interaction of dissolved polysulfide with the
Li-metal anode and thus prevents polysulfide shuttling.
Another proposed mechanism suggests that LiNO3 is first
electrochemically reduced, and then the reduced products
react with polysulfide to form a passivating layer. This prevents
the direct irreversible interaction between polysulfides and
lithium, leading to better cyclability.15 The synergistic effect of
LiNO3 and polysulfides has been shown by Cui and co-
workers.18 They utilized polysulfides and LiNO3 as dual
additives, and that leads to more reversible Li metal plating/
stripping.18 Compared with the well-known protection
functionality of LiNO3, either alone or together with
polysulfides, the working mechanism of such protection is
not clear yet. One of the main reasons is the typical Li−S

In this study, we apply in operando sulfur X-ray absorption
spectroscopy (XAS) to a model system to elucidate the LiNO3
protection mechanism. The electrolyte is designed to be sulfur-
free such that sulfur XAS can faithfully track the evolution of
polysulfide and their derived species during charge−discharge.
By probing the electrolyte on both the cathode side and anode
side, we can obtain a more accurate understanding of the
reactivity of LiNO3 and its role in stabilizing Li−S batteries.
The commonly used electrolyte formulation for Li−S
batteries is lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTF-
SI) dissolved in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME) with LiNO3 as the additive. LiNO3 is
understood to react with polysulfide (PS) to form products
that stabilize the battery from polysulfide shuttling. However,
precisely determining the LiNO3 reactivity is nontrivial
because the salt and PS both contain sulfur. To simplify the
electrolyte formulation and to accurately determine the
working mechanism of the LiNO3 additive, we formulated an
electrolyte using LiNO3 as the only salt dissolved in DOL and
DME in 1:1 volume ratio (Figure 1a). Hence, any sulfur
species found in the electrolyte must be from the dissolved
polysulfide or its derived products. The concentration of
LiNO3 chosen for detailed study is 0.5 M because this
concentration is widely studied in the literature.20−22 In terms
of weight percentage, it is equivalent to around 3.5 wt % in a 1
M LiTFSI in DOL/DME electrolyte. The measured ionic
conductivity (Figure S1) and Coulombic efficiency (Figure S2)
are typical for LiNO3-containing electrolytes. Switching from
LiTFSI to LiNO3 does not change the fundamental electro-
chemical behavior which is the conversion reaction from S8 to
Li2S during discharge and vice versa during charge (Figure
S3).23 The charge−discharge profile is not influenced by the
concentration of LiNO3 either (Figure S4). Long-term cycling
of Li−S batteries assembled with the 0.5 M LiNO3 dissolved in
DOL/DME electrolyte deliver good electrochemical perform-
ance, maintaining more than 80% of initial capacity even after
100 cycles (Figure 1b). While it is tempting to attribute such
good performance to the presence of LiNO3 only, comparison

Only after introducing a sulfur source can a stable interphase

To understand the reason behind this, we performed in
operando S K-edge spectroscopy to determine the reaction
mechanism between LiNO3 and the sulfur source.
In operando sulfur K-edge spectroscopy was performed in
the sulfur-free electrolyte with two different coin cell
configurations. Holes were cut in the electrodes so that the
electrolyte can be directly probed. In the first configuration, the
sulfur cathode side faces the beam. A hole was cut in the sulfur
cathode so that the beam can pass through the sulfur cathode
and probe the electrolyte on the separator near the cathode
side (Figure 1d). In the second configuration, the Li metal side
of the coin cell faces the beam. A hole was cut in the Li metal
anode so that the beam can pass through the Li metal and
probe the electrolyte on the separator near the Li metal anode
side (Figure 1e). The reason to probe the electrolyte on two
different sides of the cell is to capture the spatial progression of
the PS reactivity with LiNO3 across the cell. Since the only way
PS can reach the Li metal anode side is through shuttling, there
might exist a concentration gradient of PS across the cell from
the point of origin, which is the sulfur cathode, to the Li metal
anode. Hence, a PS concentration-dependent reactivity with
LiNO3 may be observed. The two in operando coin cell set up
discussed is designed to test this hypothesis.
The in operando cells were charged and discharged at a rate
of C/10. Spectrum acquisition started 1 h after the start of the
cycling. Sulfur-derived species were detected from the
beginning of the data acquisition. The sulfur K-edge spectra
have several features. Peaks marked by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6
originate from polysulfides, elemental sulfur, Li2S, sulfites,
sulfonates, and sulfates, respectively.22 The top panel shows
the in operando XAS of the cell in which the side of the
separator that is close to the sulfur cathode is probed. The
intensity of the polysulfide peak increases during discharge as
expected (top panel of Figure 2a). This is due to the
electrochemical generation of polysulfide species on the sulfur
cathode and their subsequent dissolution. At the late stage of
the first discharge, PS converts into Li2S as indicated by the
broadening of the peak at around 2471 eV. In addition to PS
and Li2S, other species like sulfites and sulfonates (peaks 4 and
5) are also observed. The fact that these oxidized forms of
sulfur appear during discharge when the S electrode is

free of sulfur, sulfur-derived species are also observed on the
separator (bottom panel of Figure 2a). The presence of PS and
Li2S should be due to the shuttling of PS from the cathode
side. No oxidized sulfur species are observed in the beginning
of the discharge. However, as the cell is more deeply
discharged, oxidized sulfur species such as sulfonate and
sulfate begin to appear (peaks 5 and 6). The key distinction
from the observation on the cathode side is the lack of sulfite.
As sulfur in sulfonate and sulfate is more oxidized than that in
sulfite, such difference indicates PS near the Li metal anode is
reacted more completely than that near the sulfur cathode.
During charge, PS near the cathode side keeps getting
reacted to oxidized sulfur species (top panel of Figure 2b).
Sulfite peak intensity rises, and a new peak appears marked by
6 at higher energy due to the sulfate species because of the
progressive oxidation of PS as the cycling progresses. PS
species are also observed in the electrolyte near the Li metal
anode side during charging (bottom panel of Figure 2b), which
can be attributed to the diffusion of PS from the cathode side
to the anode side. Sulfonate and sulfate species continue to
prevail. When the cycling enters the stage of second discharge,
PS species are replenished in the electrolyte again near the
sulfur cathode (top panel of Figure 2c). Further oxidation of
PS leads to a higher concentration of oxidized sulfur species
(top panel of Figure 2c). Meanwhile, oxidation of PS near the
anode side leads to a higher concentration of more completely
oxidized sulfur products, such as sulfate (bottom panel of
Figure 2c). In general, the common trend of oxidation of PS is
observed throughout the cell, irrespective of the applied
voltage and the vicinity of the PS to the type of electrodes.
Another important point is the persistent presence of these
species near both the electrodes, especially near the Li metal
anode. This indicates these species are chemically stable within
the presence of the Li metal. Eventually, these oxidized species
will become part of the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI)
and the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) since the solubility of
Li sulfonate and sulfate in the ether-based electrolyte is low.14
Indeed, the presence of oxidized sulfur species can be found in
both CEI (Figure S5) and SEI (Figure S6) in addition to the
electrolyte (Figure 2). These species in the interphase will lead
to the formation of inorganic-rich interphases, stabilizing the
interphases and likely preventing PS shuttling. Furthermore,
once formed and accumulated on the interphases, these species
are stable over long-term cycling, as they can be found in the
solid-electrolyte interphase after several cycles (Figure S7).
Hence, they are expected to contribute toward long-term
interphasial stability. Another point is that a stable interphase
can be formed with these oxidized sulfur species, which is free
of LiF. LiF is regarded as a key component for interphasial
stability.24 This may open new avenues for engineering
fluorine-free electrolyte and LiF-free interphase. Cui and co-
workers demonstrated such a concept where they utilized
polysulfide and LiNO3 additives to form an oxidized sulfur-rich
interphase that promotes reversible Li metal plating and
stripping.18 The positive influence of the oxidized sulfur-rich
interphase can also be observed in the electrochemical
performance of the Li−S cell. Over time, the interfacial
resistance of the Li−S cell decreases, which can be partially
attributed to the formation of a Li-ion conductive interface
contributed by the oxidized sulfur rich interphase components
(Figure S8).
A common trend of PS oxidation across the electrolyte
indicates that the reaction leading to their formation is
unaffected by the applied voltage and is more chemical in
nature. Like in any chemical redox reaction, the oxidation of
PS requires an oxidizer, which has to be NO3
−, the only
available oxidizer in the electrolyte. Consequently, NO3
−
should be reduced to lower oxidation N species. Nitrogen K-
edge spectra support this point, showing NO2
−species being
observed along with the NO3
−in the cathode-electrolyte
interphase (Figure 3a), electrolyte (Figure 3b), and the anode-
electrolyte interphase (Figure 3c).25 NO2
−is the reduced
product formed because of the chemical oxidation of PS by
NO3
−. The chemical nature of the reaction is further supported
by the consistency of the peak intensity of NO2
−and NO3
−
observed in the N K-edge, irrespective of the type of battery
components investigated (Figure 3).
The reactivity of PS across the cell can be best explained by
the distribution of PS in the cell. The source of PS in the
electrolyte is the cathode, which produces PS during cycling.
The high solubility of PS in ether-based electrolytes allows PS
to leave the cathode and enter the electrolyte. PS then diffuses
across the electrolyte and reaches near the Li metal anode
where it can react to form Li2S on the Li metal interphase.
Since the diffusion is happening from the cathode side, the
electrolyte near the cathode should always have a higher
concentration of PS unless the concentration is homogenized
across the cell. However, the continued operation of the
battery will prevent homogenization since the cycling of the
sulfur cathode will always produce more PS that can dissolve in
the electrolyte. The higher concentration of PS near the sulfur
cathode side can prevent their full oxidation, as there is an
abundance of PS available for reaction. Hence, the PS near the
cathode side undergoes partial oxidation to sulfite and
sulfonate. Eventually, fully oxidized sulfate can form but sulfite
is prevalent (see the top panel of Figure 2c−e). Meanwhile, the

In summary, we studied the detailed reactivity of LiNO3 in
Li−S batteries through utilizing a sulfur-free electrolyte
containing only LiNO3 as the salt in DOL and DME solvents.
LiNO3 undergoes a chemical reaction with the dissolved PS,
resulting in the formation of oxidized S species. However, the
progression of the reaction is spatial in nature, determined by
the concentration gradient of polysulfide across the cell. The
cathode side of the electrolyte contains less oxidized sulfur
species in comparison to the anode side. NO3
−is subsequently
reduced to NO2
−because of the redox reaction. The oxidized
sulfur products such as sulfite, sulfonate, and sulfate become
part of both interphases, passivating the interphases and
preventing PS shuttling.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Electrochemistry. Sulfur cathodes were prepared from a
slurry of elemental sulfur (70 wt%), poly(vinylidene difluoride)
(PVDF) binder (20 wt%), and carbon black (10 wt%) using
N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as the solvent and casting the
slurry on aluminum foil. Cathode loading was 2 mg/cm2. Li
metal (250 μm thick) was used as the anode. 0.5 M LiNO3 was
dissolved in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) (1:1 volume ratio) to prepare the electrolyte. Coin
cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox with the
elemental sulfur cathode, Li metal anode, and the prepared
electrolyte using a glass microfiber separator. Cells were cycled
at C/10 charge/discharge (current density calculated using
1000 mAh/g base capacity). Ionic conductivity of the
electrolytes was measured in a Pt||Pt symmetric cell through
measuring the resistivity of the electrolyte by electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy within 1 Hz to 5 MHz range. The
following equation was used for the conductivity calculation:26
