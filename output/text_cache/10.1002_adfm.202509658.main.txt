Methylation Design on Weakly Solvating Ethers for
Wide-Temperature Li–SPAN Battery
Chaoqiang Tan, Zeyu Shen, Shichao Zhang, Zhijun Wu,* Shengnan He, Hongge Pan,
Hao Cheng,* and Yingying Lu*
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) recently emerges as a prospective cathode
for lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries owing to its high S utilization, remarkable
cycling stability, and liberty from shuttle eﬀect. Despite weakly solvating ether
electrolytes exhibiting simultaneously compatibility for SPAN cathode and
lithium (Li) anode, they universally suﬀer from low ionic conductivity and
narrow liquid-phase temperature range. However, conventional ether-based
electrolytes with excellent ionic conductivity cannot realize reversible
solid–solid conversion of SPAN cathode due to Li polysulﬁde intermediates
(LiPSs) dissolving and shuttling. Here, a series of weakly solvating ethers are
evaluated by selectively methylating dimethoxymethane (DMM) to ﬁne-tune
their solvation capability and liquid-phase temperature range. Enlightened
from weakly solvating ether electrolytes, the contact ion pair (CIP)-dominated
electrolyte is designed for satisfactory ionic conductivity of electrolyte and
reversible solid–solid conversion of SPAN cathode over wide temperature
range. The reinforcement mechanisms of the CIP-dominated electrolyte for
the Li anode and SPAN cathode are comprehensively elucidated through
experimental characterizations and molecular dynamics simulations. Based
on the optimized electrolyte, Li||SPAN full cells can steadily operate over wide
temperature range from −20 to 60 °C. Furthermore, the 120 mAh Li||SPAN
pouch cell can exhibit 85.5% capacity retention after 45 cycles at 0.2 C.

1. Introduction
Commercial rechargeable lithium-ion bat-
teries (LIBs) have enabled the widespread
use of mobile electronic devices, electric ve-
hicles, and renewable energy storage.[1]
However,
the
capacity
limitations
of
graphite/silicon–carbon
anodes
and
lithium–iron phosphate/ternary compound
cathodes hinder the further development
of long-range electric tools.[2] To increase
the
energy
density
of
electrochemical
batteries, lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
have emerged as a promising candidate
for
next-generation
mobile
solutions.[3]
In Li–S batteries, Li-metal anodes oﬀer
more than ten times the theoretical speciﬁc
capacity of graphite anodes (3860 vs 372
mAh g−1), while S cathodes provide a
high speciﬁc capacity of up to 1675 mAh
g−1 and are cost-eﬀective.[4] However, the
electrochemical performance of Li–S bat-
teries is severely impacted by signiﬁcant
volume expansion of both the S cathodes
and Li-metal anodes, the shuttle eﬀect of Li
polysulﬁde intermediates (LiPSs), and the
rapid, irreversible consumption of active
materials and electrolytes, leading to issues
like capacity decay and short-circuit risks.[5]
By adjusting the electrolyte formulation, it is possible to simul-
taneously regulate the Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li deposi-
tion/stripping, the physicochemical stability of the Li/electrolyte
interface, and the redox reaction pathways of the S cathode.[6] Re-
garding solvent selection, it is important to note that LiPSs and Li
metal tend to react with carbonate solvents, whereas ether-based
solvents with excellent reduction stability are compatible with Li-
metal anodes.[7] However, the focus of electrolyte adjustment for
the Li-metal anode and S cathode is not aligned, limiting the
comprehensive electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries.[8]
On the one hand, high-concentration electrolytes (HCE), locally
HCE (LHCE), and weakly solvating electrolytes (WSE) have been
proposed to construct anion-derived solid electrolyte interphases
(SEI) on Li-metal anodes by adjusting the solvation environment
of Li+, rather than the solvent-derived SEI formed in conven-
tional electrolytes.[9] On the other hand, the focus is on analyz-
ing and regulating the reaction pathways and solvation environ-
ment of LiPSs, which directly determine the S utilization and ca-
pacity retention of the widely used S/carbon composite cathodes
in Li–S batteries.[10] Although ether-based electrolyte regulation

strategies like HCE, LHCE, and WSE can eﬀectively improve the
CE of Li deposition/stripping, these strategies struggle to simul-

as both are related to the solubility of LiPSs in the electrolyte but
exhibit opposing trends.[6,11] Therefore, there is an urgent need
to avoid LiPSs’ shuttling and improve S utilization by regulating
the reaction pathways.
In this impasse, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has gar-
nered attention in recent years, where the short sulfur chains are
covalently bonded to the conjugated pyridine backbone.[12] In car-
bonate electrolytes, the nonsoluble solid–solid conversion mech-
anism of the SPAN cathode enables good cycling stability while
achieving both high CE and high S utilization.[13] Therefore, after
mitigating the adverse eﬀects of the cathode, Li inventory con-
sumption and dendrite growth become the key limiting factors
for the long-cycle performance of Li–SPAN batteries.[14] Com-
pared to carbonate electrolytes, ether-based electrolytes, which
are more chemically compatible with Li metal, can provide
a smooth Li morphology and high reversibility of Li deposi-
tion/stripping. However, SPAN faces severe capacity degradation
in conventional ether-based electrolytes due to the formation of
soluble LiPSs and the shuttle eﬀect.[15] Fortunately, there are re-
ports that ether-based HCE, LHCE, and WSE, by adjusting the
Li+ solvation structure, can be simultaneously compatible with
both Li anodes and SPAN cathodes.[16] For ether-based HCE, re-
placing 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with dibutyl ether (DBE) as
the sole solvent helps to improve the cycling stability of Li–SPAN
batteries by inhibiting LiPSs’ dissolution in DBE.[17] However,
HCE is not only costly but also has high viscosity and low ionic
conductivity. To reduce viscosity, diluents are introduced into
HCE to formulate LHCE, maintaining anion-rich solvation envi-
ronment for Li+. Furthermore, advanced LHCE was prepared by
formulating 1.8 m (mol L−1) Li bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)
in a mixture of diethyl ether (DEE) and bis(2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl)
ether (BTFE), and the constructed Li–SPAN battery delivered a
speciﬁc capacity of 579 mAh g−1 with no capacity decay over 1200
cycles.[18] Although the introduction of diluents reduces the Li-
salt content in LHCE, the diluents are mainly hydroﬂuoroethers
(HFEs), which are complex to process and have high density. To
overcome the limitations of LHCE, a more promising alterna-
tive has emerged, WSE. Ether-based WSEs are formulated with
moderate concentrations of Li salts in a single weakly solvating
ether, eﬀectively addressing challenges related to viscosity, den-
sity, and cost.[19] However, the traditional ether-based WSE, dom-
inated by aggregated anion group (AAG)-based solvation struc-
tures, mostly sacriﬁces Li salt dissociation. With the guarantee
of ether-based WSE compatible for SPAN cathode, it is worth-
while to optimize ion transport in the ether-based WSE through
exploring appropriate solvation structure with better ion trans-
port ability. Therefore, it is essential to simultaneously enhance
ionic conductivity of ether-based WSE and the wide temperature
performance of Li–SPAN batteries through solvent molecular en-
gineering and solvation structure regulation, paving the way for
their practical application.
Here, we evaluate a series of weakly solvating ethers by se-
lectively methylating dimethoxymethane (DMM) to ﬁne-tune
their solvation capability and liquid-phase temperature range.
Among them, 1,1-diethoxyethane exhibits the broadest liquid-
phase temperature range, maintaining intact SPAN during
electrochemical cycling rather than the collapse of SPAN ob-
served in traditional ether-based electrolytes. However, the WSE
formulated with 1,1-diethoxyethane exhibits low ionic conductiv-
ity due to the steric eﬀects enhanced by methylation. Under the
same conditions of 2 m LiFSI, we propose a binary mixed ether
electrolyte with higher ionic conductivity than the WSE formu-
lated with 1,1-diethoxyethane, which can also prevent the LiPSs’
shuttling during the electrochemical reaction of SPAN. Experi-
mental characterizations and molecular dynamics (MD) simula-
tions reveal that compared to the AAG-dominated solvation struc-
ture, the contact ion pair (CIP)-dominated solvation structure can
promote stable electrochemical reactions of SPAN and improve
ionic conductivity of the electrolyte. Consequently, the optimized
electrolyte enables the Li||Cu half cells to achieve an average CE
of 98.7% after 370 cycles at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 at 30 °C,
an average CE of 98.6% after 300 cycles at 60 °C, and no freezing
at −20 °C with an ionic conductivity of 1.61 mS cm−1. Beneﬁt-
ing from the high reversibility of the Li anode in the electrolyte,
the Li||SPAN full cell composed of a 45 μm Li anode and a SPAN
cathode (2 mAh cm−2) retain 80% of its capacity after 200 cycles
at a current density of 0.5 C. Designing new solvents with weak
solvation capabilities and wide liquid-phase temperature range
through selective methylation is an eﬀective and feasible strat-
egy. Inspired by the electrolyte regulation of SPAN, we develop a
promising paradigm to balance ion transport and the anion-rich
Li+ solvation structure in the electrolyte, and contributing to the
wide temperature performance of Li–SPAN batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Electrolyte Design and Characterizations
Molecular design of electrolyte solvents presents a promising
strategy to regulate the solvation structure and physicochemical
properties of electrolytes. Unlike simply increasing the molar ra-
tio of Li salt to solvent in HCE, the distribution of three Li+ sol-
vation states (solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIP), CIP, and AGG)
can be ﬁnely tuned by altering the interactions between solvents
and ions. In brief, SSIP, CIP, and AGG correspond to Li+ sol-
vation structures involving 0, 1, and 2 or more anions, respec-
tively. By shortening the central alkyl chain of DME, WSE formu-
lated with DMM enables the Li–SPAN battery to cycle steadily at
−40 °C, owing to its low desolvation energy and anion-rich Li+
solvation structure.[20] Notably, methylation of solvent molecules
not only further reduces their solvation ability due to steric hin-
drance but also provides an opportunity to broaden their liquid-
phase temperature range by enhancing van der Waals forces.[21]
Based on this methylation strategy, diethoxymethane (DEM),
and 1,1-dimethoxyethane are produced by methylating the ter-
minal and central carbons of DMM, respectively, while 1,1-
diethoxyethane results from simultaneous methylation of both
terminal and central carbons of DMM (Figure 1). These solvents,
DMM, DEM, 1,1-dimethoxyethane, and 1,1-diethoxyethane, are
labeled as solvents A, B, C, and D, respectively. Referring to the
WSE previously reported for SPAN, four binary mixed ether elec-
trolytes formulated from DME and either of these four ethers all
enable SPAN to undergo a solid–solid conversion reaction with-
out LiPSs’ shuttle eﬀect (Figure S1, Supporting Information).

Li+─DME interaction, which disappears entirely when DME is
fully replaced by solvent D (Figure 2c).
Further insights are obtained using NMR spectroscopy, which
is sensitive to the solvation environment in electrolytes. As
shown in Figure 2d, the 17O peak within a chemical shift range
of 160–180 corresponds to the sulfonyl 17O of the FSI−anion,
and its upﬁeld shift represents a stronger shielding eﬀect on the
17O due to closer coordination between Li+ and FSI−.[24] With the
increasing proportion of solvent D, the 17O peak of FSI−shifts
progressively upﬁeld, indicating stronger Li+─FSI−interaction
(Figure 2e). Additionally, the 7Li chemical shift, which reﬂects the
shielding eﬀect caused by the lone pair electrons of DME around
Li+ in the 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte,[25] shifts downﬁeld as the
proportion of DME decreases, demonstrating reduced electron
density around Li+ and a transition toward more FSI−coordina-
tion (Figure 2f).
Based on these experimental results, the solvation structure
evolves monotonically and continuously from a solvent-rich to
an anion-rich Li+ solvation structure as solvent D substitutes
DME in varying proportions. Although the anion-rich Li+ solva-
tion structure is conﬁrmed in the 2 m LiFSI/D electrolyte, its ionic
conductivity is signiﬁcantly lower than that of the 2 m LiFSI/DME
electrolyte. Given the poor ion transport in WSE, it is crucial to
identify a mixed ether electrolyte that combines favorable ionic
conductivity with an anion-rich Li+ solvation structure, thereby
improving the CE, cycling lifespan, maximum applied current
density, and tolerable areal capacity of Li plating/stripping. As
shown in Figure 2g, the ionic conductivities of the 3:7, 5:5, and
7:3 electrolytes are markedly superior to that of the 2 m LiFSI/D
electrolyte, due to the introduction of DME as a cosolvent. How-
ever, the ionic conductivity of the 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte de-
creases signiﬁcantly due to its freezing at −20 °C (Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information). The plunge in conductivity occurs because
electrolyte freezing leads to an insuﬃcient amount of free sol-
vent to dissociate the Li salts and facilitate the transport of Li+
through the solvated solvent. As a result, Li+ transport may pri-
marily occur through the solid phase in the frozen electrolyte.[26]

ether electrolytes on the SPAN electrochemical reaction is an-

SPAN exhibited LiPSs’ shuttle eﬀects in the 3:7 electrolyte due
to the excessive DME, while the 5:5 electrolyte eﬀectively sup-

cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2) improve progressively as the electrolyte

Microscopic analysis reveals that the behavior of Li deposition
under diﬀerent conditions in the 5:5 electrolyte is closely asso-
ciated with the composition and morphology of the SEI, which
is derived from electrolyte decomposition. A robust and thin SEI
can mitigate side reactions between the electrolyte and the Li an-
ode, thereby improving the reversibility of Li plating/stripping.
Since the thickness of SEI ﬁlms is typically less than 25 nm, X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with argon sputtering is
employed to characterize the chemical composition of the SEI
ﬁlm. Overall, XPS results indicate that the SEI ﬁlms formed in
the three electrolytes (2 m LiFSI/DME, 5:5, and 2 m LiFSI/D)
have similar compositions. As shown in Figure 4b and Figure
S16 (Supporting Information), the signals for S and F elements,
which are derived from FSI−decomposition, increase as sol-
vent D gradually replaced DME in the electrolytes, validating
the hypothesis that an anion-rich solvation structure can induce
more FSI−decomposition. However, excessive FSI−decompo-
sition in the 2 m LiFSI/D electrolyte is detrimental to Li plat-
ing/stripping, as conﬁrmed by electrochemical data (Figure 2h).
Thus, the CIP-dominated Li+ solvation structure in the 5:5 elec-
trolyte demonstrates its advantage in constructing an inorganic-
rich SEI through moderate FSI−decomposition.
For the SEI formed in the 5:5 electrolyte, the XPS signal for
Li ﬂuoride (LiF) slightly increases, and the weak signal for C–Fx
completely disappears after the ﬁrst etching, indicating a mosaic
SEI structure with an organic-rich outer layer and an inorganic-
rich inner layer.[27] Furthermore, the SEI formed in the 5:5 elec-
trolyte is characterized by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). As
shown in Figure 4c, the SEI exhibit a mosaic structure, with a uni-
form amorphous organic layer encapsulating internal inorganic
nanoparticles, identiﬁed as LiF by lattice fringe patterns (Figure
S17, Supporting Information). According to the literature, the
high Young’s modulus (70 GPa) and wide bandgap (10.9 eV) of
LiF contribute to an SEI with high mechanical strength and excel-
lent electronic insulation, which inhibits Li dendrite growth and
prevents electrolyte decomposition.[28] Additionally, the transport
of Li+ in the SEI is facilitated by surface diﬀusion of LiF nanopar-
ticles with low energy barrier, consistent with the low Ea1 of the
5:5 electrolyte observed in EIS results.[29]
In comparison, the SEI formed in the 2 m LiFSI/DME elec-
trolyte displays an inhomogeneous morphology, with Li carbon-
ate (Li2CO3) nanoparticles irregularly embedded in the amor-
phous organic layer of the SEI (Figure 4d; Figure S18, Supporting
Information). Although some inorganic species are present, the
uneven morphology of the SEI in the 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte
could not eﬀectively prevent side reactions caused by direct con-
tact between the electrolyte and Li metal, and loose distribution
of Li2CO3 nanoparticles in the SEI with poor electronic insula-
tion cannot eﬀectively block the electronic tunneling to suppress
the electrolyte decomposition.[30] The morphology and chemical
composition of the SEI formed in the 5:5 electrolyte underscore
its unique advantages, revealing the structure–eﬀectiveness rela-
tionship between SEI and solvation structure.
2.3. Compatibility Mechanisms for Electrolyte and SPAN Cathode
The chemical composition of our fabricated SPAN is shown
in Table S1 (Supporting Information), and Raman and Fourier
transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) results of SPAN powder
are consistent with the previous studies in Figure S19 (Support-
ing Information).[31] SPAN cathode is prepared as described in
the “Experimental Section,” and the morphologies of SPAN parti-
cles and cathode are observed by utilizing SEM (Figure S20, Sup-
porting Information). Although stable electrochemical cycling
of SPAN is achieved in the 5:5 electrolyte, the reaction mecha-
nism of SPAN in this electrolyte remains unclear. To address this,
high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) is
used to observe the morphologies of SPAN after cycling in var-
ious electrolytes. Pristine SPAN particles exhibit an amorphous
structure without lattice fringe patterns, as conﬁrmed by X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) results (Figure S21, Supporting Information).
However, after cycling in 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte, the SPAN
morphology appears to collapse, with Li sulﬁde (Li2S) nanoparti-
cles forming within the degraded SPAN (Figure 5a,b). This col-
lapse is attributed to the dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs in
the 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte, leading to irreversible by-products
such as Li2S, as veriﬁed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) elemental mapping (Figure S22, Supporting Information).
Speciﬁcally, the structural nitrogen in the collapsed SPAN shows
signiﬁcant loss compared to the pristine SPAN (Figure S23, Sup-
porting Information).
In contrast, SPAN cycled in the 5:5 electrolyte maintains its
intact morphology without interfacial by-products, and no dis-
cernible loss of structural nitrogen is detected in the EDS results
(Figure 5c,d; Figure S24, Supporting Information). Due to the
amorphous nature of SPAN particles, the interface between the
cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) and SPAN is challenging to
distinguish in HRTEM images. To further investigate the inter-
facial chemical composition of SPAN after cycling in diﬀerent
electrolytes, XPS is employed. In the S 2p spectra, the presence
of LiPSs is detected on the surface of SPAN cycled in the 2 m
LiFSI/DME electrolyte, indicating LiPSs’ dissolution from SPAN
(Figure 5e). In contrast, no LiPSs are observed on the surface of
SPAN in the 2 m LiFSI/D and 5:5 electrolytes, suggesting that the
solvation structure modulated by solvent D eﬀectively suppresses
LiPSs’ dissolution from SPAN. However, inorganic species such
as LiF and Li2CO3 are detected on the surface of SPAN cycled in
all the electrolytes, indicating that these inorganic species alone
are insuﬃcient to prevent SPAN morphology from degrading.
This suggests that the electrochemical reaction of SPAN is closely
related to the solvation structure of the electrolytes.

2.4. Electrochemical Performance of Li||SPAN Full Cell
Through rigorous and comprehensive investigation, the rein-
forcement mechanisms of the CIP-dominated Li+ solvation
structure for both the Li anode and SPAN cathode are visually
depicted in Figure 6a. On the one hand, the uneven and brit-
tle solvent-derived SEI formed in the SSIP-dominated electrolyte
is ineﬀective at suppressing Li dendrite growth, while the uni-
form and robust anion-derived SEI formed in the CIP-dominated
electrolyte enables conformal Li deposition. On the other hand,
in the SSIP-dominated electrolyte, the SPAN structure collapses
due to the dissolution of LiPSs, whereas in the CIP-dominated
electrolyte, solvent D eﬀectively blocks LiPSs’ dissolution, pre-
serving the initial state of SPAN. As a result, the Li||SPAN full
cell, composed of a 500 μm Li anode and a high-loading SPAN
cathode (2 mAh cm−2), exhibits superior cycling stability in the
5:5 electrolyte, delivering a speciﬁc capacity of 1025 mAh g−1
with 81.8% capacity retention after 450 cycles (Figure 6c; Figure
S28b, Supporting Information). In contrast, the performance of
the Li||SPAN full cell severely degrades in the 2 m LiFSI/DME
electrolyte within just 30 cycles. Due to the severe capacity degra-
dation of the SPAN cathode in the 2 m LiFSI/DME electrolyte,
we are only able to compare the rate performance of Li||SPAN
full cells in the 2 m LiFSI/D and 5:5 electrolytes. Compared to
the 2 m LiFSI/D electrolyte, the 5:5 electrolyte enables Li||SPAN
full cells to achieve higher capacity at the same rate. Owing to
the higher ionic conductivity of the 5:5 electrolyte, Li||SPAN full

3. Conclusion
We evaluate a series of weakly solvating ethers by selectively
methylating DMM to ﬁne-tune their solvation ability and liquid-
phase temperature range. In terms of electrolyte design, we de-
velop a promising approach to balance ion transport with an
anion-rich Li+ solvation structure. Based on the liquid-phase tem-
perature range, 1,1-diethoxyethane is selected to modulate the Li+
solvation structure, ensuring stable electrochemical reactions for
SPAN and enhancing the ionic conductivity of the electrolytes
over a wide temperature range from −20 to 60 °C. Ultimately,
the 5:5 electrolyte with a CIP-dominated Li+ solvation structure
is chosen and tested in Li–SPAN batteries. Through comprehen-
sive evaluation, the 5:5 electrolyte eﬀectively enhances the reac-
tion kinetics of both Li anode and SPAN cathode, demonstrating
its superiority for future high-power applications. The reinforce-
ment mechanisms of the CIP-dominated electrolyte for the Li an-
ode and SPAN cathode are comprehensively elucidated through
experimental characterizations and molecular dynamics simula-
tions. Moreover, our work presents a universal strategy to sup-
press LiPSs’ dissolution from SPAN by modulating the Li+ sol-
vation structure in mixed ether electrolytes, and the collabora-
tive design of solvent molecules and solvation structures shows
promise for advancing practical Li–SPAN batteries.
4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation:
The solvents DMM, diethoxymethane (DEM),
1,1-dimethoxyethane, and 1,1-diethoxyethane were purchased from Al-
addin. Battery-grade DME and LiFSI were purchased from Dodochem.
Electrolytes were prepared by dissolving 2 m LiFSI in a single solvent or
mixed solvents in an argon-ﬁlled glovebox (H2O < 0.01 ppm and O2 <
0.01 ppm).
SPAN Cathode Preparation:
SPAN was synthesized as follows: poly-
acrylonitrile (Sigma–Aldrich, Mw = 150 000) and elemental sulfur (Sigma–
Aldrich) were ball-milled for 2 h at a mass ratio of 1:3, and the mixed pow-
ders were annealed in an argon atmosphere at 350 °C for 6 h with a heat-
ing rate of 5 °C min−1. SPAN cathode was prepared as follows: SPAN,
carbon nanotube (CNT), and polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) were mixed
in a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) through a high-
speed homogenizer. The resulting slurry was coated onto aluminum foil
and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. By modulating the coating thickness,
the SPAN cathode with diﬀerent loading could be obtained.
Material Characterization:
The cycled electrodes were rinsed with
DME prior to subsequent characterization. NMR spectra were obtained by
a 600 MHz NMR instrument (Agilent DD2-600). Raman spectra (Raman)
were obtained by Horiba Jobin Yvon LabRAM HR Evolution. XPS was ob-
tained by Escalab 250Xi. SEM images were obtained by ﬁeld emission SEM
(Hitachi SU-8010) at the accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Cryo-EM characteri-
zation was carried out by depositing 0.5 mAh cm−2 of Li metal on the Cu
mesh at a current density of 1 mA cm−2. Cryo-EM images were then ob-
tained by FEI Talos F200C in low-dose mode at an accelerating voltage of
200 kV.

with a thickness of 500 or 45 μm and a SPAN cathode with an areal capac-
