ABSTRACT: Accelerating Li2S2/Li2S reduction through catalysis to
enhance the apparent conversion kinetics has emerged as an
innovative paradigm for a highly efficient lithium−sulfur battery.
Nonetheless, the sluggish kinetics of the solid−solid conversion from
solid-state intermediate product Li2S2 to the final discharge product
Li2S (corresponding to the last 50% of the theoretical capacity) leads
to the premature end of discharge and low discharge capacity output
and sulfur utilization. Here, we add 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidine
trifluoromethanesulfonate ([P14][OTf]) to a typical ether electro-
lyte, which in turn promotes the slow Li2S2/Li2S conversion kinetics
by increasing the solubility of Li2S/Li2S2. The three-electrode
experimental and characterization studies disclose that the exchange
current densities of Li2S2 reduction, Li2S oxidation, and Li2S2
oxidation are up to 2.28 times, 4.6 times, and 1.04 times than those of the control electrolyte, respectively. Furthermore, the
modified electrolyte could activate “dead Li2S”, thus providing more reactive sites and inducing small particle Li2S/Li2S2 deposition.
Accordingly, the LSBs containing the Control/5 wt % [P14][OTf] electrolyte exhibit a significantly improved capacity rate of 40%
compared with the traditional electrolyte at 0.5 C for 500 cycles (corresponding to a fading rate 0.1% per cycle). We believe that
increasing Li2S2/Li2S solubility via electrolyte modification provides practical opportunities toward long-life batteries.
KEYWORDS: Li2S2/Li2S, three-electrode electrochemical system, ionic liquid, depth-profiling, Li−S battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Battery technologies play a key role in transforming societal
development in a more sustainable way.1 The rapid develop-
ments of portable electronic devices and electric vehicles have
brought about urgent needs for high-energy-density recharge-
able batteries. The lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) are
considered one of the most promising successors to next-
generation energy storage devices because of their high
theoretical energy density (2600 W h kg−1).2−4 However,
practical applications of LSBs are currently hindered by the
complex multistep electrochemical and chemical reaction
process between sulfur and lithium sulfide. This makes the
study of the electrochemical reaction kinetics very difficult,
while the “shuttle effect” of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in
ether electrolyte further accelerates the loss of sulfur and
reduces the actual cycling performance of the battery.5,6
The multiphase reaction interface formed between the
electrolyte and the conductive carrier serves as the main site
for the reaction of LSBs actives. In the typical ether electrolyte
system, the active sulfur mainly undergoes the solid−liquid−
solid multiphase multistep electrochemical reaction in addition
to a portion of the chemical reaction during the discharge
process. As it is known that liquid to solid conversion reaction
from Li2S4 to Li2S provide the 75% of the theoretical capacity
(1256 mA h g−1).7 However, among the latter two stages of
liquid−solid (Li2S4 to Li2S2) and solid−solid (Li2S2 to Li2S)
conversion, the deposition of solid-phase products and the low
solubility of the actives cause concentration differences and
electrochemical reaction polarization. Then, high polarization
overpotentials lead to the premature end of the discharge and
reduce sulfur utilization, resulting in a huge deviation of the
actual delivered capacity from the theoretical value.8,9
At present, some researchers are committed to the idea of
electrocatalysis. The researchers focused on accelerating the
kinetics of the conversion between polysulfides to address the
shuttle effect. Such as metal nitrides,10,11 metal sulfides,12,13
metal selenides,14,15 and some metal single atom materials,16,17
which accelerate the conversion reaction by decreasing the
overpotential of polysulfides, resulting in enhancing sulfur

cycle life. Similarly, the researchers accelerated the kinetics of
the Li2S2 →Li2S conversion reaction by introducing a catalyst.
Remith et al. modified the hollow carbon spheres with
nanoscale Ru and used the carbon spheres as the sulfur host.
As the Ru nanoparticles could significantly reduce the
interfacial resistance of the sulfur cathode/electrolyte and
thus improve the ion diffusion ability, the Ru nanoparticles
acted as the catalytic center of Li2S2 →Li2S, which improved
the reaction kinetics considerably.18 Jin et al. improved the
Li2S2 →Li2S reaction kinetics by designing “speed-pulp-peel’’
ultrafine nanostructures, which effectively accelerated the
conversion kinetics and significantly improved the sulfur
utilization by shortening the Li+/e−transport distance and
providing abundant active sites through the ultrafine
structure.19 Yang et al. used amorphous sulfide (CoS3) as a
catalyst to reduce the dissociation energy of Li2S2, which can
promote the electrochemical conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S at
high sulfur loading (3−10 mg cm−2), resulting in an increase in
the Li2S/Li2S2 ratio from 1/1.63 to 5.60/1 in the final
discharge product, and improve the sulfur utilization rate by
about 20%.20 Yang et al. improved the sulfur utilization rate by
adding MoP with electrocatalytic properties. Due to the faster
conversion kinetics and more reversible sulfur conversion, the
overpotential of the charge/discharge reaction was greatly
reduced, which improved capacity, rate performance, and cycle
stability of the battery.21
Nonhomogeneous catalytic processes can speed up the
conversion of liquid LiPSs, but they are unable to prevent the
solid product Li2S/Li2S2 from depositing on the catalysts’
active sites under long-cycle conditions. This reveals problems
such as large reaction barriers, slow solid phase diffusion and
poor solid-to-solid contact interfaces, which will negatively
impact the catalysts’ efficiency in the solid−solid conversion
process. Controlling the cathode material to improve the
solid−solid conversion kinetics is a challenging task. Therefore,
reactivation of “dead Li2S/Li2S2” to increase more reactive sites
can provide an important initiative for long-cycle LSBs.
Ma and other researchers22 verified that higher capacity and
rate performance are determined by Li2S2/Li2S conversion
kinetics. The experimental results show that the ideal
electrolyte requires higher S8 and Li2S/Li2S2 solubility by
means of a three-electrode electrochemical system in the
mobile phase, which removes the effect of mass diffusion. Yang
et al.23 proved by means of a three-electrode electrochemical
system that an increase in the solubility of sodium polysulfide
(especially Na2S2/Na2S) can improve the capacity and cycling
performance of RT Na−S batteries. This shows that increasing
the solubility of Li2S/Li2S2 is the key to enhancing the
performance of Li−S batteries.
In the current typical LSBs ether-based electrolyte system (1
M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and
0.2 M LiNO3 in an equal-volume mixture of DOL/DME),
DME facilitate the solvation of polysulfides, while DOL
possesses well compatibility with lithium cathode.24 The
maximum polysulfide solubility in this electrolyte at room
temperature is ∼6 molS L−1 (E/S ratio of ∼5.2 μL mg−1). For a
practical LSBs with an energy density beyond 500 W h kg−1
(E/S ratio of ∼3 μL mg−1).25,26 This E/S ratio corresponds to
a high polysulfide solubility of 10.4 molS L−1 which is far
beyond the solubility limit of the DOL/DME electrolyte
system.27 For regulating the dissolution state of LiPSs in the
electrolyte, there are two research thoughts: one dedicated to
finding electrolyte solvents with higher polysulfide solubility,
such as dimethyl sulfoxide, N,N-dimethylacetamide, and 1-
methylimidazole (MeIm). These electrolytes are known as
highly solvating electrolytes (shorted for HSEs), which can
provide higher solvation capacity for the electrolytes in poor
liquid systems and accelerate the conversion kinetics of LiPSs
to Li2S. Nevertheless, the high reactivity of such active solvents
with metallic lithium requires a special protection techniques
for the anode side.28,29 Another approach is to use sparingly
solvating or nonsolvating electrolytes (shorted as SSEs), such
as ionic liquids, hydrofluoroethers, other highly concentrated
electrolytes and solid-state electrolyte to inhibit polysulfide
dissolution.30 However, the application of the SSEs mechanism

Ionic liquids are usually molten salts with a melting point
below 100 °C. Most ionic liquids consist of organic molecular
ions, which are referred to as room-temperature ionic liquids
(RTILs). RTILs are promising electrolyte contenders for
energy storage systems due to their important properties of
extraordinary electrochemical stability, low volatility, good
thermal stability, nonflammability, and moderate ionic
conductivity.33 Despite the many excellent properties of
RTILs, they cannot be used as the main bulk of LSBs
electrolyte systems because of the high viscosity and cost.
According to Masayoshi Watanabe’s group,34 the anionic
structure in ionic liquids affects the solubility of LiPSs. In
particular, the solubilities of the various polysulfides in
[P14][OTf] are 1 order of magnitude higher than those in
other ionic liquids. It would have been better served if we
controlled a proper amount of [P14][OTf] to the typical ether
electrolyte to avoid the more serious “shuttle effect”.
Meanwhile, transforming the low-order solid−solid reaction
into a liquid-phase reaction is expected to improve the kinetics
of Li2S/Li2S2 conversion and thus improve the performance of
LSBs.
In this work, Li2S/Li2S2 solubility is improved by adding a
pyrrolidine-type ionic liquid ([P14][OTf]) to the usual ether
electrolyte for LSBs. It is verified that the increase in the
solubility of Li2S/Li2S2 can substantially enhance the exchange
current densities of Li2S/Li2S2 by a three-electrode system.
During the battery discharge process, it can induce the
deposition of generated Li2S/Li2S2, alleviate the passivation of
the sulfur anode surface, enhance the utilization rate of the
active sulfur species, and reduce the polarization of the battery.
The improvement effect is more significant at higher rates. In
addition, the generation of “dead Li2S/Li2S2” can be reduced
during the cycling process, which improves the utilization of
active sulfur species in the battery (Figure 1). This study
provides a new method for accelerating the solid−solid
reaction kinetics and obtaining high-energy Li−S batteries.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. Without additional purification, all chemicals are of
an analytical standard and are used. The chemical reagents such as
active microporous carbon (≥purity: 90%), sublimed sulfur
(≥99.5%), 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidine trifluoromethanesulfonate
ionic liquid (≥99%), lithium−sulfur electrolyte (LS-002, Suzhou
DodoChem), and Super P (≥99%), are purchased from XFNANO,
Aladdin, Machlin reagent, Suzhou Dodochem, and Cyber Electro-
chemical Network, respectively.
2.2. Preparation of Control/[P14][OTf]. 1 M lithium bis
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide with 2 wt % lithium nitrate
(LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/ethylene glycol dimethyl
ether (DME) (volume ratio of 1:1) served as the blank electrolyte
(named as Control electrolyte). Control/5 wt % [P14][OTf] is
obtained by adding 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidine trifluoromethanesul-
fonate ([P14][OTf]) ionic liquid to the Control electrolyte and
stirring in the glovebox for 24 h.
2.3. Preparation of Saturated Li2S/Li2S2 Electrolytes. The
preparation of the Li2S2 electrolytes is based on the following reaction

2.4. Characterizations. The morphology and structure of the
samples are characterized by scanning electron microscopy (FSEM,
JSM-6700E). Raman tests are conducted in a Thermo Scientific DXR
3Xi HR Evolution at an excitation wavelength of 532 nm.
Transmission spectra of FI-TR are obtained on a Thermo Scientific
Nicolet iS20. Depth-profiling chemistry is performed by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo scientific K-Alpha). The
sulfur content of the samples is determined by thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA, HCT-1). X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, RINT2000
apparatus) is used to obtain the composition of the material, the
structure, and morphology. UV Absorption Spectroscopy (UV/vis) is
carried out on different electrolyte dispersion of S and Li2S.
Viscosity is measured by a NiRun NDJ-8S. Conductivity is
measured by an ALALIS CD400.
2.5. Three-Electrode Electrochemical Testing. The Li2S/Li2S2
redox kinetics is tested by a three-electrode system in which the
electrolytic cell is an H-type electrolytic cell. The counter electrode is
Li plate, the working electrode is a carbon cloth with a diameter of 12
mm, the reference electrode is Ag+/Ag (RE, an Ag wire immersed in
methyl cyanide with 0.01 M AgNO3 and 0.1 M ammonium tetrabutyl
perchlorate), and the electrolyte is saturated Control or Control/5 wt
% [P14][OTf] electrolytes for Li2S/Li2S2 are tested in a glovebox.
The workstation is a Chenhua CHI 660. During the LSV test, the
voltage range of the Li2S oxidation process is −1.0 to −0.2 V, and the
Li2S2 reduction and oxidation process is the voltage range is −1.1 to
−0.8 V, scan rate is 1 mV s−1.
2.6. Symmetrical Cell Assembly. The CR2016 cells are
assembled using carbon cloth as positive and negative electrodes,
and the separator is a Celgard 2400 polypropylene membrane,
electrolyte is saturated Li2S2 Control or Control/5 wt % [P14][OTf]
electrolyte. Then, cyclic voltammetry (CV) test is carried out, and the
voltage range is −1 to 1 V, and the scan rate is 1 mV s−1.
2.7. Electrodes Preparation. Microporous activated carbon
(Mac) and sublimated sulfur (mass ratio of 7:3) are mixed ball milled
at 400 rpm. Thereafter, sulfur containing hybrids are kept at 155 °C
for 12 h to obtain the S@Microporous activated carbon (S@Mac)
composite are mixed with Super P and poly(vinyl difluoride) (mass
ratio of 7:2:1, respectively) in the N-methyl pyrrolidone solution to
form a slurry. The ground oily slurry is spread evenly on aluminum
foil. The aluminum foil coated with the slurry is vacuum-dried at 60
°C for 12 h and cut into electrode sheets with a diameter of 12 mm,
and then, water and oxygen contents in the glovebox are controlled
below 0.1 ppm to assemble a coin cell (CR2016). The prepared
electrode sheets are the positive electrode, the Celgard 2400
polypropylene microporous membrane is used as the separator, and
the lithium metal sheet is used as the negative electrode. A positive
electrode case, a positive electrode sheet, an electrolyte, a separator, a
lithium metal sheet, a gasket, and a negative electrode case are
assembled from top to bottom. Galvanostatic charge/discharge curves
are analyzed using a program-controlled test system (LAND
CT2001A) in the range of 1.7−2.8 V. CV measurements are
performed using the Autolab electrochemical workstation
(PGSTAT302N) at voltages of 1.7−2.8 V in scanning speeds of
0.1−0.5 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is
performed at 2.1 V with a voltage amplitude of 5 mV in the frequency
range of 10 mHz to 100 kHz. If unspecified, the sulfur load on the
electrode is 1.0−1.2 mg cm−2, and the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio is
25 μL mg−1.

3.2. Optimization of [P14][OTf] Ionic Liquids Addi-
tives. The solubility of different species of LiPSs is regulated
by optimizing the addition quantity of [P14][OTf] ionic
liquids to avoid more severe “shuttle effects”. Figure 3a shows
the cycling performance of the batteries with various additions
of 1, 5, and 10 wt % [P14][OTf] in control electrolyte. After
activation at 0.2 C for two cycles, the initial capacities of the
cells in Control electrolytes and Control/1, 5, and 10 wt %
[P14][OTf] additions are 1355.8, 1248.1, 1462.5, and 1427.6
mA h g−1 at 0.5 C, respectively. The capacities of 368.2, 683.1,
982.3, and 877.5 mA h g−1 are maintained after 200 cycles,
respectively, corresponding to the decay rates of 0.36, 0.22,
0.16, and 0.19% per cycle. So, it can be proved that the optimal
addition of ionic liquids is 5 wt %. The cycling performance of
Li/Li symmetric batteries in Control and Control/5 wt %
[P14][OTf] electrolytes at a current density of 0.25 mA cm−2
and an aggregate cycling time of 300 h with a single cycling
time of 0.5 h are displayed in Figure 3b. Although the
compounded electrolyte’s voltage is higher at the start stage, it
is only marginally higher than the control electrolyte
throughout the entire cycle. Nevertheless, both electrolyte
systems are capable of enabling the lithium anode to achieve
stable cycling for 300 h. The introduction of [P14][OTf] does
not make the electrolyte less compatible with lithium anodes.41
The Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency in the Li/Cu
cell of Control and Control/5 wt % [P14][OTf] is displayed in
Figure 3c. Both electrolytes contain LiNO3, which plays a key
role in stabilizing the Coulombic efficiency.42

sulfur” in the Control/5 wt % [P14][OTf] cell covers the
cathode surface to a small extent and leaves more pores as
nucleation sites. Inversely, the bulk “sulfur species” in the
Control cell passivates the material surface more severely and
reduces the nucleation site for continued reaction, leading to
increased polarization (as demonstrated by the multiplicative
polarization difference in Control electrolyte batteries) and low
active material utilization. Consequently, smaller flake stacks
allow for reduced polarization, a high active material usage
rate, and exceptional cycling stability since they cover less of
the material surface while maintaining enough reactive sites.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, the Li2S2/Li2S solubility is enhanced by adding
[P14][OTf] ionic liquids to the typical lithium−sulfur
electrolyte, which in turn promotes the slow low-order
Li2S2/Li2S conversion kinetics. This provides benefits in
terms of improving sulfur utilization and battery cycling
stability. Additionally, the addition of 5 wt % [P14][OTf] to
the Control electrolyte significantly increases the solubility of
Li2S2/Li2S and induces the formation of Li2S deposition. This
facilitates the reduction of passivation at the material interface,
improves the diffusion process at the surface of the material,
and reduces the polarization of the battery, especially at higher
rates. Meanwhile, the high solubility of Li2S2/Li2S can recycle
the “dead Li2S2/Li2S” on the material surface, further
facilitating the utilization rate of the battery active substance.
This work offers a new route to improve the Li2S2/Li2S solid−
solid conversion kinetics of high energy density LSBs and
contributes to the future commercialization and practical
application of LSBs.
