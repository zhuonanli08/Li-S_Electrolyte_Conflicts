ABSTRACT: Achieving high energy densities for lithium−sulfur
batteries remain elusive. Largely limited by the volume of electrolyte
used, lean electrolyte conditions (electrolyte/sulfur mass ratio <3)
present enormous challenges that have led to very poor specific
capacity and rate performance. Previous studies have identified that
the high concentration of polysulfide is responsible for the poor
discharge voltage. However, there still lacks sufficient understanding
of the processes occurring at lean electrolyte conditions. In this work
we uncovered a polysulfide concentration regulating mechanism that
operates through the precipitation and redissolution of solid sulfur-
based species (reversibly precipitated sulfur species, RPSS). This
occurs in a concerted manner in a global sense through the cathode and can be measured using impedance spectroscopy. It was
found that the more RPSS that is formed, the higher the energy density of discharge. We propose that high concentration of
polysulfide tends to supersaturate, which impeded the formation of RPSS. Employing an electrolyte with low Li ion concentration
along with using poorly dissociating lithium salts allowed for more RPSS formation and ultimately enabled discharge at >2.0 V at
0.05 C, at E/S = 2.5, and at room temperature without the use of an engineered cathode.
■INTRODUCTION
The lithium−sulfur battery is an attractive next-generation
battery technology due to the natural abundancy of sulfur and
high theoretical energy density.1−4 However, the practically
achievable energy density (volumetric and gravimetric) is
highly dependent on the volume/mass of electrolyte used.5,6 At
relevant electrolyte conditions (electrolyte/sulfur mass ratio,
E/S < 3),7 the cell experiences enormous overpotential that
results in extremely poor rate performances (often well below
0.05 C).8,9 An increasingly popular and reasonably robust class
of strategies centers around moving away from the traditional
solid−liquid−solid conversion of a lithium−sulfur battery to a
solid−solid conversion. This is achieved by either employing
sparingly soluble electrolytes10,11 and/or covalently-bonded
sulfur polymer.12−14 With almost every aspect of this class of
strategies appealing, its most undermining property is the
intrinsically lower operating potentials due to a fundamental
change in the reaction pathways.15 A study by Eroglu et al.
presented a rapid decline in projected pack-level energy density
when the cell-level voltage is <2.0 V.16−18 Since the operating
voltage of lithium−sulfur batteries hovers around 2.0−2.2 V,
the pack level energy density of the lithium−sulfur battery is
particularly sensitive to any decrease/increase of cell voltage. A
low cell voltage can drastically increase the number of cells
required to reach often pre-established device-level operating
voltages. This would effectively increase the mass of “dead-
weight” components such as current collectors, electrolyte, etc.,
that do not scale well even with a high single-cell-level energy
density. While the solid−solid-based systems can reach the
necessary lean electrolyte conditions, they are typically
operated at a much lower average cell voltage i.e., ∼1.5 V.
As part of a long list of requirements for commercial success,
maintaining a >2.0 V average discharge voltage while also
operating at an appreciable current density, room temperature,
high sulfur loading, and all under lean electrolyte conditions
(≤3.0 μL mgs) remains to be a monumental task.
Many alternative strategies have been explored to achieve a
low E/S ratio while still maintaining a solid−liquid−solid
conversion mechanism. These include the use of macroporous
carbon that assists in temporarily relieving the overpotential by
allowing more local electrolyte access and slowing the onset of
large overpotential;19,20 the use of polysulfide adsorptive
binders can be useful in taking polysulfide out of the bulk
solution to lower the overall impedance but still lacks
significant progress.21 Increasing the solubility of polysulfide
has also been a very popular direction of study. Solvents such
as dimethylacetamide,22 tetramethylurea,23 and 1,3-dimethyl-2-
imidazolidionne24 that can promote more polysulfide dis-

improving the specific discharge capacity. However, these
solvents tend to have a high viscosity and are also incompatible
with Li metal. The reduction in the concentration of Li ion salt
has also been pursued, where the proportion of free solvents

solubility of polysulfide by the common-ion effect, which, in
turn, reduces the overpotentials. Improvements in discharge
capacity and voltage were observed but only at a relatively high

were either discharged at very low current densities (<0.0025

°C)28 to function at >2.0 V.
The large overpotential often observed at the LEC usually
manifests itself as a large dip at the onset of the second
discharge plateau. This has been termed the Li2S nucleation
overpotential.29 A particularly troublesome property of this dip
is its tendency to chronically persists for the remainder of the
second discharge plateau, i.e., cell voltage never recovers. This
leads to average cell voltages well below 2.0 V. The cause for
the overpotential can be reliably traced to increases in the
charge transfer resistance. However, this is still a rather
ambiguous source of overpotential that can be caused by many
cathode processes. These include poor polysulfide mass
transfer limitations (from high viscosity),30 polysulfide
clustering/gelation,31,32 passivation of conductive surfaces,19
or a combination thereof. Deconvolution of these sources of
high overpotential is a difficult task that might also lead to
varying conclusions depending on the specific LSB cell system
studied. Accordingly, it is difficult to devise effective strategies
for LSB operation under LEC.
It might be instructive to study the underlying processes that
are occurring in configurations that have been already shown
to allow for relatively higher performing LEC-LSB. There exist
clear configurations19 where cell at LEC experienced large
overpotential at the onset of the second discharge plateau but
can reliably recover its potential. Accordingly, there must exist
some type of mechanism at the cathode that can help the cell
recover from the voltage dip and avoid a chronically depressed
discharge.
In this work, we uncover a polysulfide concentration
regulating mechanism that exists under lean electrolyte
conditions in a traditional solid−liquid−solid conversion type
LSB by employing operando and relaxation-based electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis. We discov-
ered a strong relationship between the interfacial resistance
(high frequency semicircle, centered at ∼103−105 Hz) at a
specific depth of discharge (DOD) and the discharge material
level energy density. Cells with more than one interfacial
resistance increasing event as a function of DOD and larger
magnitude of such an event were found to deliver higher
discharge energy density. We attributed this to sulfur-based
species that are reversibly deposited as deduced from a
relaxation-based EIS study where changes in the phase angle
were tracked. The formation and importance of reversibly
precipitated sulfur species (RPSS) are discussed. Promotion of
RPSS formation led to significant improvements in cell
potential and capacity. Using a highly Li ion deficient
electrolyte with poorly dissociating anion and a standard
“nonengineered” sulfur electrode based on commercially
available micrometer-sized sulfur particles at 3.5 mg cm−2
(60% sulfur content), we were able to achieve a high discharge
specific capacity (∼850 mAh g−1) with an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL
mg−1 at a rate of 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA g−1), at room
temperature, and with a second plateau well above 2.0 V.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The poor performance of lean electrolyte conditions (LECs)
during a single discharge can be succinctly described by two
important deficiencies associated with the discharge process:
(1) the existence of a large overpotential/nucleation “dip” and
(2) a chronically depressed discharge potential following the
dip. The potential of the dip often determines whether the cell
can proceed into the second discharge plateau (depending on
the cutoff voltage). In the case where the cell can sustain a
second discharge plateau, the depressed discharge potential
determines the operating voltage and energy density of the cell.

polysulfide,31−35 reducing the reaction kinetics. Interestingly,
while a some studies have reported a near full recovery of
discharge potential after the initial potential dip,19 most studies

In principle, as the cell discharges, the polysulfide mass
should be converted to insoluble species (Li2S/Li2S2), and
thus, the global concentration of polysulfide (averaged across
the entire electrolyte volume) should decrease. Shown in
Figure 1b, an “effective E/S ratio” was estimated as the
reciprocal of the expected amount of sulfur mass remaining in
solution and plotted against the depth of discharge. It was
assumed that all sulfur species were consumed to form either
insoluble Li2S or soluble Li2S2. As one discharges past the
second plateau, the polysulfide concentration decreases and
the “effective E/S ratio” increases. If Li2S2 is the main discharge
product, at ∼450 mAh g−1, the cell is expected to have a global
polysulfide concentration that is similar to the highest solvated
sulfur concentration experienced in an “equivalent cell” with an
injected E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1. If Li2S is considered as the
main product, the cell reaches an effective E/S ratio of 5 μL
mg−1 at ∼700 mAh g−1. Based on literature reports, a cell with
an E/S ratio of ∼5 μL mg−1 can be expected to run relatively
unhindered at 0.1 C even when using a relatively routine
carbon nanotube/sulfur composite.36 Accordingly, a decrease
in sulfur content in the electrolyte from discharging should
impart some relief to any high polysulfide-concentration-
derived overpotentials experienced by the cell. Yet, this is often
not observed with the chronic voltage depression persisting
well into the second plateau and often never recovering at all.
Therefore, it appears that it is not the high concentration of
polysulfide that is directly responsible for the chronically
depressed second plateau under LEC, but some long-lasting
negative effect that is caused by the mere temporary existence
of a high polysulfide concentration.
This chronic lack of recovery in overpotential can be clearly
demonstrated from operando electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) analysis over the course of discharge. A
series of operando EIS measurements were performed on cells
using different Li salts and at varying concentrations. A highly
depressed discharge profile is observed during operando EIS
(Figure 2a) employing an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL mgs

standard electrolyte composition: 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M
LiNO3 in DOL/DME 1:1 v/v) and a sulfur loading of 3.5 mgs

recover the overpotential experienced during the dip, and in
fact, the impedance continuously increased. The fitted

the discharge time. Re and Rint peaked at the onset of the

the higher Li ion concentration cell continued to drop in
voltage, while the low Li ion concentration cell was able to
maintain a stable voltage.
The ability of the low Li ion concentration cell to avoid a
continued voltage drop is an indication that there must exist
some mechanism for the cell to manage and regulate the high
concentration of polysulfide. To investigate such a regulation
mechanism, we look toward understanding the surface
condition of the cell at various depths of discharge (DOD)
by observing the relaxation processes. As the viscosity and
polysulfide concentration are expected to be very high under
LEC, we suspect that much of the diffusional and dissolution-
based relaxation processes (that can be used to infer the
surface conditions of the cathode) occur over long time scales
and even though the cell appears to have to have reached
equilibrium. To uncover the relaxation processes occurring at
slower time scales, cells discharged to four different DOD were
rested at OCV while their impedance was sampled
intermittently (Kramers−Kronig analysis was employed to
confirm that the EIS spectra were causal, Figure S1). Four
different DODs were selected and defined as follows: DOD-1:
discharged to 8 h, Figures 3a−c; DOD-2: discharged to 9 h,
Figures 3d−f; DOD-3: discharged to 11 h, Figures 3g−i;
DOD-4: discharged to 13 h,Figures 3j−l.
Confirming our suspicions, the impedance elements changed
significantly in magnitude even when rested at OCV. As the
impedance metrics tended to decrease (beside Rint), it cannot
be due to the Li metal corrosion which tends to increase
linearly with t0.5.38,39 Therefore, the relaxations are likely
cathode-based processes. Moreover, the relaxation trends
change dramatically from DOD-1 to DOD-2 to DOD-3 with
DOD-4 being relatively like DOD-3. This means that
conditions (surfaces, electrolyte composition) of the cell
prior to rest vary significantly as a function of DOD. Generally,
across all DODs, Rint tended to decrease monotonically over
time while Re tended to increase monotonically. The most
interesting phenomenon lies in Rct, which exhibited a rather
surprising nonmonotonic trend for the cell rested from DOD-
1. Right after the current was halted, Rct increased for a few
hours, followed by a sharp decrease. If discharge was halted at
DOD-2, Rct initially held constant for a period of ∼2 h

Changes in Rct can be interpreted as a surface area change or
polysulfide concentration change (Figure S2). To delineate
which one of these is dominating, we refer to the transient
behavior of the Bode-phase plots during relaxation. The peak
position in the Bode-phase plots can be used as an indication
of the characteristic frequency (fct) of the observed process. A
change in Rct caused by a process dominated by polysulfide
concentration variations should experience a change in its fct,
whereas a change in Rct dominated by surface area variations
will tend to maintain a constant frequency peak position. This
can be rationalized if we first approximate the time constant of
these processes as τ = RctC, where τ is the time constant (1/fct)
and C is the capacitance. Since capacitance C is proportional to
the surface area and Rct is inversely proportional to the surface
area, together, the impact of any surface area change will be
likely nullified as they cancel each other.35,40 In other words, a
decrease in Rct without any frequency change can be
interpreted as a process mostly caused by an increase in the
electronically active surface area. On the other hand, a change
in polysulfide concentration is not directly proportional to
capacitance, which would be reflected in a change in τ.
Accordingly, a decrease in Rct accompanied by a change in τ
can be interpreted as a phenomenon mostly dominated by an
increase in concentration of redox active species, i.e.,
polysulfide concentration. Using this analysis, we can deduce
the events occurring during relaxation.
At DOD-1 (8 h of discharge, Figure 3a), the phase-peak
associated with Rct (∼102 Hz) initially shifts to a lower
frequency. Once Rct stops increasing and begins to decrease,
the frequency stops shifting and begins to maintain a constant
value of ∼30 Hz. This suggests that during the initial 3 h of
relaxation, Rct increases due to a decrease in near-surface
polysulfide concentration (likely diffusion to the bulk electro-
lyte) as illustrated in Figure 4a,b, whereas the subsequent
decrease in Rct can be interpreted as an exposure of more
surface area (Figure 4c). As DOD-1 is at the early stages of the
second discharge plateau, there likely exists a very high near-
surface polysulfide concentration. Upon resting, the polysulfide
surface concentration dissipates into the bulk electrolyte,
decreasing the exchange current and increasing the Rct. The
lowering of local polysulfide concentration likely triggered
some sort of dissolution event, exposing the surface area (Rct

DOD-2 appears to represent a turning point between a near-
surface polysulfide concentration that is too high for RPSS
dissolution and a concentration low enough to begin
redissolving RPSS. Intriguingly, the discharge percentage of
DOD-2 aligns nicely with features pertaining to how Rint
evolves over the course of discharge. Looking at the impedance
vs DOD evolution for the high Li ion concentration electrolyte
(Figure 2b), Rint was found to increase up to ∼60 ohm
(denoted as Rint‑P1, peak 1 of Rint), followed by a decrease to
∼27 ohm, which then increases back up to ∼37 ohm (denoted
as Rint‑P2, peak 2 of Rint). There seem to be multiple Rint
increasing/decreasing events, which we refer to as oscillations.
Similar trends were reported in another study but not
discussed.21 As a confirmation, this oscillating trend of Rint vs
DOD can even be seen without the assistance of equivalent
circuit modeling; the existence of Rint‑P2 can be observed
directly on the Nyquist plot (Figure S3). More importantly,
while the first peak of Rint is relatively similar between the high
and low Li ion concentrations (∼61 and ∼57 ohm,
respectively), there appears to exist a much larger difference
in the second Rint peak (∼67 vs ∼35 ohm, respectively).
Overall, this potentially indicates a degree of importance to the
level of Rint and RPSS as surface films. From Figure 3a,d,g,j),
we can also associate DOD-1 as before Rint‑P2, DOD-2 is near
Rint‑P2, and DOD-3 and -4 are post Rint‑P2. We postulate that
the phenomenon that is responsible for the differing relaxation
characteristics among the 4 DODs can be tracked by Rint.
With a magnitude comparatively lower than that of Rct, Rint
has been rarely discussed even though it has important
implications on the surface processes in the cathode. The
interpretation of Rint has been rather complex. Miller et al.41
attempted deconvolution of the high- to mid-frequency regime
using distribution of relaxation time analysis. In their study,
four distinct time constants were identified in the frequency
regime typically considered Rint (103−106 Hz). This was done
by using a regularization parameter of 10−4. Only one of the
four time constants was found to match that of a Li/Li
symmetric cell, i.e., the solid electrolyte interphase impedance.
Using a series of symmetric cells containing various
combinations of sulfur cathodes, carbon-only electrode, and
lithium polysulfide-containing electrolytes, the other three time
constants were all assigned to cathode processes: interparticle
resistance, double layer relaxation, and sulfur-based reactions.
All such cathode processes can be affected by the formation of
insulating sulfur-based surface layers, suggesting Rint can be
used as a probe for the cathode’s surface impedance. We
propose in the case of LEC, the main contribution of Rint is
dominated by surface films in the form of RPSS. Furthermore,
in contrast to our recent reports of precipitation of polysulfide

To further understand the importance of Rint, a series of cells
with different electrolyte compositions (Table S1) were
subjected to the same in operando EIS analysis collected in
Figure 2. Since LEC negatively affects both the discharge
capacity and potential, we utilized the active material level
energy density (Wh kgs

interest to correlate against the impedance contributions in the
cell. As expected, the energy delivered by the first discharge
appears to show a reasonably strong negative correlation with
the Rct magnitude taken at 6 h of discharge. The higher the
observed Rct, the lower the energy density of the first discharge
(Figure 5a). It should be noted that 6 h was chosen as the
representative Rct because it was roughly at 50% DOD in all
cases. No significant correlation was found between the
electrolyte resistance (Re) peak near the end of the first
discharge plateau and the energy of the cell as shown in Figure
S4a. This is somewhat unexpected given its known dependence
on the polysulfide concentration43 and suggests that a high
viscosity of the electrolyte stemming from polysulfide
dissolution might not be a great sole predictor of performance.
From initial inspection, no correlation was observed
between Rint‑P2 and Rint‑P1 with the energy density (Figures
S4b and S4c, respectively). However, we found that the ratio
between the second and first Rint peak (Rint‑P2/Rint‑P1, Figure
5b) correlated rather strongly (R2 = 0.98) with the discharge
energy density up to a ratio of ∼1. After Rint‑P2 reaches a similar
magnitude as Rint‑P2, it appears that the energy density
decreases and does not change and becomes unrelated to the
Rint peak ratio. We speculate that perhaps there exists a
minimum threshold value of Rint‑p2 for the cell to produce a
high energy density. This finding is surprising first because a
higher impedance generally does not yield higher energy
density and second, the magnitude of Rint is rather small in
comparison to Rct. Although Rint makes little contribution to
the total impedance, from our relaxation experiments (Figure
3), Rint can be considered as a probe for the surface condition
of the sulfur cathode, which then has a strong impact on the
charge transfer resistance. Furthermore, as the trend appears
only after normalizing Rint‑P2 by Rint‑P1, the degree of the
second surface film formation event is important only in
relation to the first surface film formation event.
Cells with a higher Rint‑P2/Rint‑P1 performed better, indicating
that the precipitation of these solid-sulfur species likely help to
alleviate the high concentration of the cell discharged under
LEC. These interim solid sulfur-based species give the
electrolyte a “mass sink” to temporarily store the polysulfide
content as some form of RPSS (Figure 5c) This mechanism
ultimately provides the cell with an alternative pathway to
rapidly regulate the polysulfide concentration without relying
on the slow kinetics of lithiating a high concentration of
polysulfide into solid Li2S/Li2S2. Consequently, cells that do
not have a high Rint,P2 (when normalized to Rint,P1) are unable
to form such redissolvable species (i.e., RPSS) and unable to
eliminate/avoid the sources (clustering/aggregates/gelation)
of chronic overpotentials.
In order to promote more formation of RPSS, we must first
understand how they are limited. It is known that lithium
polysulfide tends to form clusters at high concentrations or low
temperature.44 It is possible that such clusters cannot form
RPSSs and are very stable. One possible explanation is that the
more stable the clusters, the higher the degree of super-
saturation and the poorer its ability to precipitate out as an
RPSS. This is especially true for polymeric species. Clusters/
aggregates in solution have been previously shown to
substantially increases the solubility of a polymer.45 In contrast,
electrolytes with less stable polysulfide clusters will tend to
precipitate out of solution readily, forming the desired RPSSs.
To show this is the case, we investigated the stability of the
polysulfide/electrolyte solution to temperature.
Upon cooling, the solubility of the polysulfide is expected to
decrease, which should cause precipitation. Polysulfides were
mixed with electrolyte at high and low Li ion salt
concentrations and studied using differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC). Because of the high polysulfide concen-
tration, a slight compositional change from evaporation of
DOL/DME (high vapor pressure at room temperature) could
induce premature precipitation prior to testing, nullifying the
results of the experiment. The solvent was therefore switched
to tetraglyme (TEGDME), which has a much lower vapor
pressure. DSC shows that cooling of high concentration
polysulfide (8 mols L−1 nominally in the form of Li2S8) in an
electrolyte solution of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M LiNO3 in
TEGDME did not produce any exotherms (Figure 6a,b).
When the Li ion concentration was lowered, a clear exothermic
peak was found with an abrupt increase, characteristic of
supersaturation. We attribute this peak to the precipitation of

between the polysulfide and TEGDME molecules stabilizing

A corollary of such a finding is that a stronger interaction
between solvent and lithium polysulfide and lithium salts could
provide an unwanted stabilizing effect of polysulfide clusters in
solution during discharge. This is likely detrimental to LEC
LSB, which is in contrast to the common notion of increasing
the solubility limit of polysulfide by using more polar
solvents.23,46 Stronger solvating solvents will tend to interact
more strongly with polysulfide and Li ions. As expected, when
solvents with higher solvating power such as 1,3-dimethyl-2-
imidazolidinone, hexamethylphosphoramide, or dimethyl-
acetamide were used at E/S of 2.5 μL mgs
−1, no notable
performance improvement was observed (Figure S8). Argu-
ably, the performance was slightly worse. However, in flooded
cells, where the concentration regime of polysulfide is much
lower, notable performance enhancements have indeed been
previously reported.47
From our results, the problem of lean E/S ratio (high
concentration of polysulfide) does not depend on merely the
solubility but is more related to the existing concentration of
polysulfide in the electrolyte and the proclivity of aggregation/
clustering. If clusters are formed, a higher polysulfide
concentration can be achieved but might cause a higher
charge transfer resistance. Such clustering of polysulfide limits
the formation of RPSSs and the polysulfide concentration
cannot be regulated or remedied over the course of discharge.
When polysulfides are not stabilized in solution by clustering,
RPSSs can form, acting as a temporary “mass sink” for the
electrolyte to help mediate the polysulfide concentration.
Manthiram et al. have previously discussed that at low
temperatures,44 polysulfides tend to form overpotential-
inducing aggregates and found that using high donor anion
salts could be beneficial. Poorly dissociated Li salts could
disrupt the formation of these lithium polysulfide clusters, as a
significant portion of Li ions are already paired with their
original anion and cannot participate in cluster formation. With
non-Li salts and high donor number anions, they were able to
achieve an E/S ratio of 4 μL mgs
−1.48 Zhang et al. described
the possibility that cationic polysulfides (over-lithiated) are
formed that hinder the discharge process. It was proposed that
a decrease in Li ion concentration will benefit the performance,
citing an increased discharge capacity when using an E/S ratio
of 3.5 μL mgs
−1.49
At a very lean E/S ratio of <3.0 μL mgs
−1, we observe that
lowering Li ion concentration still exhibits a large over-
potential. Figure 7a shows the discharge voltage profile when
an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL mgs
−1 was used at a loading of 3.5 mg
cm−2. On the first and second cycles, these cells tend to
produce unstable and ill-defined voltage plateaus at 0.05 C.
Notably, there is significant difference between the perform-
ance in Figure 7a and Figure S4i (operando EIS) even though
they possessed similar concentrations. This is likely due to the
beneficial impact of the resting step throughout the operando
EIS discharge. Nevertheless, the discharge voltages in both
cases are <2.0 V. However, we found that the addition of
lithium salt with high donor number anion (triflate) and under
Li ion-starved conditions yielded a synergistic effect that
substantially reduced the overpotential (Figure 7b) to a
satisfactory voltage of well >2.0 V (albeit at 0.05 C). Cathodic
linear sweep voltammetry (Figure S9) using sulfur cathode at
3.5 mg cm−2 as the working electrode and Li metal as the
reference/counter electrode at an E/S ratio of 2.5 also supports
this conclusion. When the typical electrolyte salt composition
of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M LiNO3 was used, only a weak
reduction peak was observed. The second peak was not
observed likely to the poor kinetics of the reduction process
under Li ion-rich conditions, which corroborates nicely with
the galvanostatic discharge (Figure 2a). However, when the Li
ion content was reduced to 0.05 M LiTFSI and 0.025 M
LiNO3, we begin to observe two distinct peaks (∼2.25 and
∼1.95 V), indicating a better ability of the cell to reduce sulfur
polysulfide and also to reduce polysulfides to Li2S/Li2S2. The
peaks further become clearer and more distinct when 0.015 M
LiTF is added to the electrolyte composition, yielding a higher
peak current density.
We attribute this to the ability to form more RPSS as the
lack of free Li ions limits the formation of stabilized polysulfide
aggregates. It should be noted that Li ion diffusion can also
substantially influence the discharge. One might suggest that
the addition of LiTF could disrupt the polysulfide clustering
and significantly increase the diffusion coefficient of the Li
ions, causing the observed impact in performance. To explore
this possibility, we performed 7Li diffusion ordered spectros-
copy NMR to estimate the diffusion coefficient of Li.50 Three
electrolyte compositions were used to make lithium polysulfide
solutions at different sulfur concentrations: high Li ion
concentration (1 M LiTFSI + 0.25 M LiNO3), low Li ion
concentration (0.05 M LiTFSI + 0.025 M LiNO3), and low Li
ion concentration with LiTF addition (0.05 M LiTFSI + 0.025
M LiNO3 + 0.015 M LiTF). The attenuation curves of all
samples are shown in Figure S10. The estimated diffusion
coefficients are shown in Figure S11 plotted against the sulfur
concentration [S]. The diffusion coefficient drops with
increasing polysulfide concentration, which is expected due
to increases in viscosity and clustering. Interestingly, even with
the same polysulfide concentrations, there are substantial
differences between the three electrolyte compositions tested,
indicating the importance of Li ion concentration. The Li
diffusion coefficient increased by 2.56 times (from 2.82 ×

Unfortunately, the benefit of this electrolyte system is
limited to 0.05 C. At an E/S ratio of 2.5, the cell was unable to
enter the second discharge plateau under a higher rate of 0.1 C.
However, at a slightly higher E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs
−1, we
varied the discharge rate from 0.05 to 0.2 C with an E/S ratio
of 3 μL mgs
−1 (Figure S12). It appears that at 0.1 C, the cell
was able to mostly discharge above 2.0 V. At 0.2 C the cell
once again cannot enter the second discharge plateau with a
cutoff voltage of 1.5 V. Nevertheless, there still appears to be
substantial benefit over just using low Li ion concentration
composition (Figure 7a). The triflate salt seemed to have a
trade-off and yielded a decreased capacity. The use of another
high donor number salt, 0.375 M of lithium nitrate without
LiTFSI (Figure S4e,h,j), also resulted in improved discharge
profile over the standard 1 M LiTFSI + 0.25 M LiNO3
electrolyte. While the voltage of 0.375 M LiNO3 (Figure
S4e,h,j vs Figure S4c) was lower than that of Li ion-starved +
high donor number salt (Figure 7c), the total capacity was
higher for 0.375 M LiNO3. The discharge voltage plateaus
using our strategy is markedly higher than that of similar E/S
ratio studies employing fluorinated solvents which tends to
depress the discharge voltage.14 Directly increasing the LiTF
while maintaining a higher Li ion concentration also yielded a
higher voltage, but there still a depression in the discharge
profile <2.0 V after the first cycle (Figure S13). Overall, there
appears to be some dependency of the discharge energy
density (product of voltage and specific capacity) with the type
of anion used and concentration of the salt, especially at the
LEC regimes, and should be investigated in future studies.
Finally, we note that the use of low Li ion concentration
electrolytes might lead to problems such as the lowered Li ion
conductivity in the electrolyte. While this might be partially
alleviated by the dissolution of lithium polysulfides, it is
unlikely to be sustainable at high current densities and near
low and high SOCs. Furthermore, use of low amounts of
LiNO3 is likely to hinder cyclability as the Li metal will be less
passivated against polysulfide shuttling. This is evidenced by
the short cycle life, where we observed stable cycling for only 9
cycles (Figure S14). It is foreseeable that a combination of
sulfophillic surfaces,51,52 salts with poorly dissociating anions,
and moderately lowered Li ion concentration can provide
significant improvements in performance at LEC and can be
the topic future investigations.
■CONCLUSION
Lean electrolyte conditions and high sulfur loading along with
a cell-level discharge voltage >2.0 V are critical for achieving a
commercially relevant lithium−sulfur battery. Decreasing the
E/S ratio to 2.5 can have a drastic influence on the cell that
typically either prevents discharge entirely or produces a
discharge profile that is very unstable and depressed. While the
existence of intermediate passivating films has been previously
acknowledged in lithium−sulfur batteries,53 our findings
suggest a seemingly concerted nature in this process and that
the precipitated intermediate can be redissolved back.
Furthermore, it seems that the higher the magnitude of this
process (taken as the Rint, mid-frequency impedance), the
higher the discharge energy density. We propose that these
reversibly precipitated sulfur species (RPSS) in fact serve as a
beneficial mass sink for temporarily storing polysulfide and
transferring it out of the electrolyte solution. Ultimately, this
serves as a means for the cell to regulate the polysulfide
concentration, reducing high polysulfide concentration-in-
duced overpotentials. High concentration polysulfide solutions
that are too stable against precipitations are likely the cause of
the poor performance observed at LEC. It was found that the
addition of lithium salts with high donor number salts in
addition to an overall decrease in Li ions can promote
formation of RPSS as deduced from the large Rint‑P2/Rint‑P1
ratio and increase the discharge energy density while
maintaining an operating voltage of >2.0 V at room
temperature and at 0.05 C.
