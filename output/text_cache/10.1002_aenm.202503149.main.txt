Synergistic Trimethylsilyl-Engineered Ether and
Weakly-Coordinating Diluent Enables Compact Solvation for
Robust Interphases in Practical Li-SPAN Batteries
Junjie Liu, Ruopu Zhao, Zhiwei Ni, Yuan Li, Zhengran Wang, Suyun Liu, Huizi Zhang,
Chen Yang, Shenglin Xiong, Baojuan Xi, and Jinkui Feng*
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) is a highly applicable cathode material in
lithium-metal batteries for its low cost and high theoretical capacity. However,
the polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttle eﬀects in ether electrolytes pose a
challenge to its long-term stability. Here, based on the strategy of molecular
structure design, an ion-compact electrolyte is engineered through
synergistically combining bis[2-(trimethylsilyloxy)ethyl] ether (BTEE) with a
weakly coordinating diluent diﬂuorobenzene (DFBn). The bulky trimethylsilyl
(TMS) groups of BTEE impose steric hindrance to weaken solvent dipole
interactions, while DFBn further tailors the solvation environment, collectively
driving the formation of an ion-rich solvation structure. This unique solvation
chemistry not only accelerates Li+ transport but also promotes the in situ
construction of robust LiF-rich interphases on both electrodes. As a result, the
Li-SPAN cells employing employing dipole modulated compact electrolyte
DMCE demonstrate exceptional long-term performance with 86.2% capacity
retention over 800 cycles, and maintain stable cycles even under demanding
operational conditions characterized by high cathode loading (≈3.88 mAh
cm−2) and lean electrolyte conﬁguration (E/S ratio = 4.55) at both room
temperature and 60 °C. This research conﬁrms the feasibility of modulating
the solvation environment by altering the ion-dipole interaction through
molecular structure design, giving new insights into the ether electrolyte
design for practical Li-SPAN batteries.

1. Introduction
While lithium-ion batteries have dom-
inated commercial applications span-
ning portable electronics, electric vehi-
cles, and grid-scale energy storage sys-
tems, their inherent energy density lim-
itations persistently drive the pursuit
of next-generation energy storage tech-
nologies with superior speciﬁc energy
capabilities.[1–4] Lithium metal batteries
have received widespread attention in the
new generation of battery systems due
to the low oxidation potential (3.04 V
vs the standard hydrogen electrode) and
high theoretical speciﬁc capacity (3860
mAh g−1) of metallic lithium.[5,6] How-
ever, the cathode materials matched with
metal lithium are mostly layered transi-
tion metal compounds, such as NCM,
LCO, etc., which can only exhibit rela-
tively high energy density at high cut-
oﬀvoltage and are equipped with high
cost, while the diﬃcult recycling char-
acteristics bring greater environmental
pressure.[7]
Sulfur has a high energy density
(2600 Wh kg −1) and low cost, making
it a promising cathode material for applications.[8–10] However,
the problem of the low electronic conductivity and shuttle ef-
fect of Lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) present in Li-S batteries usu-
ally leads to lower coulombic eﬃciency and short cycle life of
the batteries.[11–13] The researchers found that a simple heating
method of covalently bonding reactive sulfur atoms to the con-
ductive backbone of PAN could eﬀectively alleviate the above
problems.[14,15] As a result, Li||Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile cells
with traditional carbonate electrolytes achieve high coulombic ef-
ﬁciencies because the low solubility of the ester electrolyte for
LiPSs allows for a solid-solid conversion mechanism for sulfur
only.[16,17] Nonetheless, the poor compatibility of carbonate elec-
trolytes with the lithium metal anode will still lead to a short
cycle span.[18] In contrast, ether electrolyte has better inertness
with lithium metal anodes; however, the high solubility of LiPSs
in ether electrolytes also contributes to the abysmal cycling per-
formance of Li-SPAN batteries.[19] Hence, the development of a
comprehensive electrolyte that stabilizes both the Li metal anode

and the SPAN cathode is of paramount signiﬁcance for the Li-
SPAN battery.[20–22]
Among the various electrolyte improvement strategies, high
concentration electrolytes (HCEs), localized high concentration
electrolytes (LHCEs), and weakly solvated electrolytes (WSEs) for
utilization in Li-SPAN batteries have been reported in recent
years.[23–25] The strategy of raising the salt concentration or in-
troducing diluents in a single step actually reduces the number
of free solvent molecules in the solvation structure, which suc-
cessfully addresses the problem of poor compatibility between
solvents with Li anode,[26] but does not solve the intrinsic issue
of high solubility of LiPSs to the solvent,[27] and thus also fails
to obtain the desired electrochemical performance for Li-SPAN
cell. Furthermore, the inevitably high viscosity of HCEs brings
slow battery dynamics problems, and the addition of ﬂuorinated
ether in LHCEs raises the density of the electrolyte, leading to
low energy densities and short cycle life under lean electrolyte
conditions.[28–30] Relatively speaking, WSEs balance the above is-
sues well, but for lower polysulﬁde solubility, usually at the sac-
riﬁce of low salt concentration, typically greater than 2 mol L−1,
resulting in higher costs.[31–33] Also, ethers with weak solvation
energies usually present low boiling points, limiting their use in
practical environments.[34]
In this work, we engineered a novel Li+-anion compact
electrolyte with low salt concentration and low density utiliz-
ing the strategy of designing molecular structure to modu-
late ionic dipole interactions, which contains 0.8 m lithium
bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in bis[2-(trimethylsilyloxy)ethyl]
ether (BTEE)/diﬂuorobenzene (DFBn) (1:4 v/v) and is denoted
as DMCE (Dipole modulated compact electrolyte). This elec-
trolyte is distinguished from conventional WSEs in that we have
achieved a shift from strong solvation to weak solvation by reduc-
ing the ion-dipole interactions between solvent molecules and Li+
through the group introduction strategy.[35] Meanwhile, unlike
conventional hydroﬂuoroether-based LHCEs, we introduced the
relatively strong polar diluent of DFBn, which can participate in
the inner solvation sheath through weak coordination ability with
Li+, further optimizing the solvation structure into Li+/anion-
rich solvation clusters (Figure 1a). In addition, we demonstrated
that the solvation cluster is conducive to the fast transport kinet-
ics of the bulk phase and the interface, and also achieves stable
anode and cathode interfaces. To verify the optimality of this in-
novative electrolyte, the electrolytes with diﬀerent modiﬁcation
strategies were prepared they are 0.8 m LiFSI in Diglyme/DFBn
(1:4 v/v), 0.8 m LiFSI in Diglyme/1,1,2,2-Tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-
tetraﬂuoropropylether (TTE) (1:4 v/v), and 2 m LiFSI in Diglyme,
and denoted as LCE (Loose contrast electrolytes), LHCE, and
HCE, respectively. In comparison, DMCE achieves superior
lithium metal compatibility, while its low polysulﬁde dissolu-
tion realizes a “solid-solid” conversion mechanism in ether elec-
trolytes. Based on DMCE, the Li||Li symmetric battery realizes
stable and impressive plating/stripping performance at 1 mA
cm−2 for ultra 1300 h. Meanwhile, the Li-SPAN cell displays out-
standing cyclic stability over 800 cycles with retention of 86.2%
and average coulombic eﬃciency of 99.9%. Even under the severe
conditions of 50 μm lithium, the Li-SPAN cell shows 450 steady
cycles with 87.9% capacity retention and 120 cycles at high cath-
ode loading (3.88 mAh cm−2) and low E/S ratio of 4.55. Simulta-
neously, we have validated its application under extreme condi-
tions of 60 °C, where the DMCE-enabled full cell demonstrates
stable cycling over 70 cycles under demanding operational pa-
rameters, including high-loading cathode and ultrathin Li metal
anodes (50 μm). This study systematically evaluates the merits
and constraints of various electrolyte engineering approaches for
Li-SPAN systems while establishing fundamental electrolyte de-
sign principles through solvation dipole modulation.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Electrolyte Design Strategies and Solvation Structure
Analysis of DMCE
Modulation of Li+ solvation structure via synergistic inter-solvent
hydrogen bonding network and ion-solvent inter-dipole within
the solvation sheath layer has a signiﬁcant impact on the charge
transfer kinetics, interfacial stability, polysulﬁde solubility, and
electrochemical performance of Li-SPAN batteries.[36,37] Engi-
neering molecular structures is an essential way to weaken ion-
dipole interactions, which is generally achieved by decreasing
the number of strongly electronegative binding sites of the sol-
vent molecule or by increasing the steric hindrance eﬀect of the
terminal groups of the molecule.[38,39] The BTEE molecule can
be obtained by introducing the trimethylsilyl (TMS) groups at
both ends of the Diglyme molecule, which is capable of limit-
ing the ability of O atoms to solvate Li+ and gives it a lower
dipole moment[40] (Figure S1, Supporting Information). We cal-
culated the electrostatic potentials (ESP) of Diglyme and BTEE
molecules by theoretical density functional theory (DFT) to ver-
ify the changes in ion-dipole interactions. It can be seen from
Figure 1b that there are three oxygen atoms with a very low
ESP of −2.96 eV in the Diglyme molecule,[41] which proves the
strong interaction between the Diglyme molecule and Li+. In
contrast, the BTEE molecule exhibits a relatively high ESP of
−1.93 eV, suggesting that the polarities and solvation ability of the
molecule are attenuated by the intervention of steric-hindrance
eﬀects. We have also demonstrated this by calculating the bind-
ing energies of Li+ with BTEE and Diglyme, respectively, as −1.46
and −2.47 eV (Figure 1c). However, BTEE exhibits a very lim-
ited ability to solvate Li+, which originates from dual molecular-
scale constraints: 1) The pronounced electronegativity disparity
between silicon (𝜒= 1.90) and oxygen (𝜒= 3.44) induces 𝜎-
electron delocalization and redirects oxygen lone-pair electrons
toward silicon via hyperconjugation, signiﬁcantly weakening co-
ordination sites[42,43]; 2) The bulky TMS substituent imposes se-
vere steric hindrance, physically blocking Li+ access to oxygen
coordination centers[40] (Figure S2d,e, Supporting Information).
For this reason, we introduced the DFBn diluent into the BTEE
solvent, which has a high electronegativity and weak coordina-
tion ability, resulting in higher lithium salt solubility in the mixed
solvent system[44] (Figure S3a, Supporting Information). In or-
der to reveal the solvent interactions, we studied the 1H spec-
tra of mixed solvents containing DFBn and TTE diluent by nu-
clear magnetic resonance (NMR), respectively. As can be seen
from Figure 1d, after the introduction of DFBn, the H peaks at
3.5 ppm belong to the weakly acidic hydrogen atom neighboring
the ether oxygen, have a more pronounced downshift compared
to the mixed solution of BTEE and TTE. Besides, the F peaks as-
signed to DFBn shifted to lower ﬁelds after mixing BTEE with

Figure S6, Supporting Information). We quantitatively compared

reduce the solvation ability of BTEE and Diglyme, ﬁll the molec-

solvation structures.[18] Apart from being capable of participating
in the solvation structure of Li+, DFBn also exhibits promising
F-donor qualities. The HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the
involved solvents are shown in Figure 1i based on DFT calcula-
tions. Compared to TTE, DFBn exhibits a lower LUMO energy
level, suggesting its priority decomposition trends on the anode
side. 7Li NMR was further employed to compare the diﬀerences
of solvation structures. To exclude the eﬀect of the anion on the
chemical environment of Li, we adjusted the lithium salt concen-
tration to 0.1 mol L−1 for the test, and the results are shown in
Figure 1j. In DMCE, as the shielding eﬀect of the mixed solvent
molecules on Li is weak, it exhibits a small upﬁeld shift, verifying
the weak ion-dipole interaction of BTEE and more AGGs-type sol-
vation structures in DMCE. In contrast, the LCE exhibits a large
upﬁeld shift owing to the strong solvation ability of the Diglyme
molecule. We noticed that LHCE actually exhibits a larger chem-
ical shift than LCE. This is likely a result of the involvement of
DFBn in the solvation environment of Li+, which reduces the
number of Diglyme molecules in the solvation sheath and causes
a relatively small shielding eﬀect on the Li+. Molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations were performed to deepen the understanding
of coordination diﬀerences in solvation structures under dipole
modulation. Comparing the solvation conﬁgurations of DMCE
and LCE, we noticed that the inner solvation sheath is shifted
from the original Li-solvent-dominated coordination to Li-anion-
dominated coordination, which conﬁrms the shift of the solva-
tion structure toward anion-rich type by weakening the ion-dipole
interaction (Figure 1k–n). Moreover, comparing LHCE, the coor-
dination peak of DFBn appears in the primary solvation sheath (r
<2.75 Å), whereas TTE can be surrounded only in the outer sol-
vation sheath, which is consistent with our previous results that
DFBn’s strong polarity, small spatial site resistance, and weak
coordination ability with Li+ allow it to engage in solvation struc-
tures to compete for coordination (Figures S7 and S8, Supporting
Information).
2.2. Analysis of Electrolyte Transport Kinetics
The energy density of Li-SPAN batteries is quite sensitive to the
weight of the electrolyte, and the same volume of electrolyte with
a diﬀerence in density of ≈0.4 g mL−1 can cause a diﬀerence in
energy density of ≈30–40 Wh kg−1.[46] First, we compared the
densities of the four electrolytes (Figure 2a), and thanks to the low
density of the BTEE molecules[30] (0.8838 g ml−1), DMCE exhibits
a relatively low density of 1.193 g mL−1. Compared to LCE, LHCE
exhibits a high density of 1.521 g mL−1 due to the high F content
of the TTE diluents. And to ensure the practicality of the elec-
trolyte in full batteries, we analyzed the antioxidant properties of
the electrolyte by linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) (Figure 2b)
that DMCE exhibits a wider electrochemical window satisfying
the working conditions of the Li-SPAN battery. Subsequently, we
investigated the kinetic behavior of Li+ in the bulk phase elec-
trolyte and at the electrolyte-electrode interface. Among the four
electrolytes, DMCE exhibited the highest ion transference num-
ber of 0.756, which is beneﬁcial for the rapid diﬀusion of Li+ in
electrolytes and low polarization during battery charging and dis-
charging processes (Figure 2c,d; Figure S9, Supporting Informa-
tion). The diﬀusion behavior of Li+ at the interface was studied
by exchanging current densities (i0) using the simpliﬁed Butler–
Volmer equation.[47] It demonstrates the high i0 of 0.204 mA
for DMCE, and 0.089, 0.132, and 0.094 mA for LCE, LHCE,
and HCE, separately (Figure 2e; Figure S10, Supporting Infor-
mation). Also, the high i0 value we obtained by ﬁtting the Tafel
plot yielded consistent experimental results (Figure 2f). However,
more important than the diﬀusion rate in the bulk electrolyte, Li+
desolvation from the Li+ solvation shell and diﬀusion through the
solid-state electrolyte interface (SEI) are rate-determining steps
during the deposition/stripping process.[48] Therefore, we ob-
tained activation energies (Ea) related to two kinds of rates using
the temperature-dependent electrochemical impedance spectra
of the Li symmetric cell (Figure S11, Supporting Information).
It can be seen that DMCE has the lowest desolvation barrier of
42.07 KJ mol−1 (Figure 2g), which is probably attributed to the
anion-dominated solvation structure and the weak ion-dipole in-
teraction of DMCE. Meanwhile, DMCE exhibits the lowest Ea2 of
57.33 KJ mol−1, much lower than the LHCE of 59.80 KJ mol−1,
LCE of 63.99 KJ mol−1 and 59.46 KJ mol−1 for HCE (Figure 2h).
Therefore, it can be hypothesized that the unique solvation struc-
ture of DMCE electrolyte derives an inorganic-rich SEI with a low
ion diﬀusion barrier at the interface, which enables a high diﬀu-
sion rate of Li+. Overall, since molecular dipole modulation plays
a crucial role in electrolyte design, the designed ion-compact sol-
vation structure results in DMCE with low density, high Li+ trans-
ference number, and low desolvation energy (Figure 2i).

2.4. Mechanisms of Active Species Transformation and Interface
Kinetics
In addition to considering the compatibility of the electrolyte
with the Li metal anode, we further tested the correlation of the
electrolyte with the SPAN cathode. First, the SPAN cathode was
synthesized by a simple solid-phase process, of which relevant
parameters are displayed in Figure S21 (Supporting Informa-
tion). We evaluated the compatibility of the electrolyte with the
SPAN utilizing a long cycle of Li-SPAN cells at 1C (1C = 700
mAh g−1) (Figure 4a). Because of the intramolecular dehydro-
genation and sulfur loss of SPAN, irreversible capacity reduc-
tion is observed in the ﬁrst cycle. Due to the high solubility of
the ether electrolyte for polysulﬁdes, we observed a rapid capacity
decay in HCE, which decayed to a capacity minimum after only
200 cycles. In the presence of diluents, the free solvent binding
to polysulﬁdes is diminished, and their corresponding LCE and
LHCE maintain a reversible capacity of 290.5 and 200.41 mAh
g−1 with average coulombic eﬃciencies of 101.45 and 99.31%,
respectively, after only 200 cycles. In comparison, DMCE main-
tains an 86.2% reversible capacity and a high average coulombic
eﬃciency of 99.9% after 800 cycles (Figures S22 and S23, Sup-
porting Information). Dissolution of polysulﬁdes and the subse-
quent resulting shuttle eﬀect are the main reasons for the fail-
ure of the sulfur-based cathode. We then tested the solubility of
LiPSs in four electrolytes (Figure 4b). 0.5 mol Li2S6 was added to
each of the four electrolytes for 48 h at 60 °C. It can be seen that
from DMCE-HCE, the color in the glass bottle gets progressively
darker, and the typical reddish-brown color of LiPSs is shown in
HCE. What’s more, DMCE still retains relatively high clarity af-
ter a month of standing, allowing evidence of extremely low poly-
sulﬁde solubility (Figure S24, Supporting Information). We then
employed DFT to further demonstrate the diﬀerence in solubil-
ity of the electrolyte for polysulﬁdes. From Figure 4c,d, it can be
seen that the BTEE molecule exhibits a small absolute value of
the binding energy for LiPSs compared to Diglyme, which can
evidence the considerably weak polysulﬁde solubility due to the
low polarity and weak dipole eﬀect (Figure S25, Supporting In-
formation). Subsequently, the sulfur transformation mechanism
within the electrolyte was systematically investigated through CV
at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, with electrochemical analysis eluci-
dating reaction kinetics and phase evolution (Figure 4e,f; Figure
S26, Supporting Information). Obviously, DMCE shows reduc-
tion peaks at ≈1.7 and ≈2.1 V, matching the solid-solid conver-
sion, which corresponds to the conversion of S to Li2S/Li2S2. And
the oxidation peak ≈2.4 V correlates with the reversible conver-
sion of the two.[22,52] Moreover, the reduction peaks at diﬀerent
voltages correspond to the electron transfer of S, and the appear-
ance of the double reduction peaks in DMCE implies a faster elec-
tron transfer compared to the single reduction peak that occurs
in the conventional ester electrolyte. Nevertheless, we found alter-
nate reduction peaks at 2 and 2.4 V corresponding to the solid-
liquid conversion of S to LiPSs and a corresponding oxidation
peak at 2.5 V in the HCE electrolyte,[14] which leads to the disso-
lution of polysulﬁdes, the main cause of rapid cell decay.
The kinetic behavior of four electrolytes in Li-SPAN batteries
was studied by contour plots of the CV patterns from scans be-
tween 0.05 – 0.5 mV s−1 (Figure 4g,h; Figure S27, Supporting In-
formation). At the anodic peak, DMCE exhibits a higher current
response even at lower sweep speeds, especially in the conver-
sion region of Li2S/Li2S2 to S. At the cathodic peak, HCE simi-
larly exhibits a polysulﬁde conversion peak, which is also in ac-
cordance with the above single-sweep speed results. The trends
of diﬀerent response peaks are also systematically presented in
Figure S28 (Supporting Information), where the double reduc-
tion peaks in the LHCE and LCE gradually combine into a sin-
gle reduction peak as the sweep rate becomes larger, implying a
slowing down of the electron transfer rate. On the contrary, even
at a sweep rate of 0.5 mv s−1, we still observed a clear double re-
duction peak in DMCE, implying a fast migration and transfer
kinetics. According to the Randles–Ševc equation, the peak cur-
rent is positively correlated with the square root of the sweep rate,
and its slope is usually used to respond to the diﬀusion kinetics
at the electrode.[53] As shown in Figure 4i, in the oxidation ki-
netic curve, DMCE exhibits a maximum slope of 4.37 compared
to the other three electrolytes, and slopes of LCE, LHCE, and HCE
were 3.57, 2.99, and 0.97, respectively. And in the reduced kinetic
curve, DMCE still maintains the largest slope absolute value of
3.3 (Figure 4j). The anion-rich solvation structure of DMCE en-
sures faster desolvation and diﬀusion processes, leading to faster
oxidation/reduction kinetics. Additionally, we found that HCE ex-
hibits very poor electrode kinetics, which may be attributed to the
high viscosity of the electrolyte aﬀecting the diﬀusion behavior in
the bulk phase electrolyte.

DMCE can develop a dense and robust CEI on the SPAN surface,
which is an important factor in realizing the solid-solid transition
mechanism of the ether electrolyte. Interfacial impedance also
supports a better understanding of the excellence of the interface,
which is closely related to the polarization of the cell dynamics.
We assembled SPAN||SPAN cells with diﬀerent electrolytes, and
the electrochemical impedance was obtained by EIS after an ini-
tial cycle (Figure 5c). The resistance at high and mid frequencies
is closely related to the transport at the electrode interface and
charge transfer. The four electrolytes exhibit similar RCEI because
only the initial cycle was performed, and thin CEI was generated
on the SPAN cathode. However, the HCE exhibits a large charge
transfer impedance, partly originating from the large desolva-
tion barrier of the Diglyme, and partly due to the poor compat-
ibility of the HCE with the cathode. In contrast, DMCE exhibits
the smallest Rct, meaning that DMCE has a low desolvation bar-
rier and best compatibility with the cathode, forming an interface
with excellent ionic conductivity. The diﬀerence in impedance is
also reﬂected in the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) im-
age (Figure S34, Supporting Information), from which it can be
seen that HCE produces a very large impedance at 1–10 s. It is
noteworthy that the apparent polarization resistance, which lies
between 0.1 and 1 s, is related to the diﬀusion of polysulﬁdes, sug-
gesting that polysulﬁdes dissolve from SPAN, and DMCE shows
very slight polysulﬁde dissolution.
To better understand the distinction between CEI derived
from diﬀerent electrolytes, more information about eﬀective
SPAN cathode protection can be acquired from XPS analysis
(Figure 5d–g). Through C1s spectra, we detected higher levels
of organic components such as C─C/C─H, C─O, and C═O in
all electrolytes except DMCE. This is mainly due to the massive
decomposition of the solvent, which has low ionic conductivity
and is not conducive to the homogeneous plating/stripping of
lithium. Meanwhile, the highest LiF content appears in the F1s
spectra of DMCE (Figure S35, Supporting Information), corre-
sponding to the decomposition of FSI−and DFBn, which is ben-
eﬁcial to inhibit the dissolution of polysulﬁdes. This is mainly
owed to the synergistic eﬀect of weak dipole interaction at the
interface and inner solvation sheath with diluent participation.
Also, we detected the ineﬀective CEI species of S0 and S1−ap-
pear least in DMCE, which means that the generated CEI have
high ionic diﬀusivity and electronic insulating properties. More-
over, we detected characteristic peaks of Li2S in both LCE, LHCE,
and HCE as a result of inactive species brought about by the
dissolution of polysulﬁdes.[8] The amount of its content also
echoes the solubility test in Figure 3b. Eventually, we made a
tangible result argument by analyzing the SEM of the SPAN
that had cycled for 20 cycles (Figure 5h; Figure S36, Support-
ing Information). The diﬀerent degrees of cracks as well as bro-
ken cathode particles on the SEM images of LCE, LHCE, and
HCE, as well as the surface of the cathode particles of LCE and
HCE, are covered with a layer of non-conductive species, which
may be attributed to the dissolution of LiPSs, causing insoluble
Li2S.
Overall, it is proved that DMCE designed through molecular
structure design and diluent modulation beneﬁts from its unique
ion-compact solvation structure, forming a dense and steady elec-
trode electrolyte interface (EEI) with high ion diﬀusion rate and
insulating ability and realizing the solid-solid conversion mecha-
nisms in ether electrolytes, which is favorable for long-term sta-
ble cycling of Li-SPAN batteries (Figure 5i).
2.6. Electrochemical Performance of Li-SPAN Full Cells
To further verify the practicality of the electrolyte, Figure 6a–h
demonstrates the cycle performance under real conditions us-
ing coin cells assembled with a thin Li anode (50 μm) and SPAN
cathode with diﬀerent loadings. Under the test condition of the
SPAN loading of ≈2 mg cm−2 and a rate of 0.4 C, as the cycle pro-
ceeds, DMCE gradually forms a stable interface at the electrodes
and cell with DMCE, demonstrate growing discharge speciﬁc ca-
pacity and stable cyclability more than 450 cycles. In contrast,
the cell with HCE electrolyte shows a 685 mAh gSPAN
−1 initial
discharge capacity, but rapid decay to under 100 mAh gSPAN
−1
after only 50 cycles (Figure S37, Supporting Information). This
awful stability is mainly due to the high incompatibility of the
electrolyte with the cathode. Interestingly, although LHCE and
LCE exhibit very large variations in compatibility with Li metal
anodes, the Li||LCE||SPAN and Li||LHCE||SPAN exhibit similar
decay trends, with capacity retention of 56.9 and 60.4% after 300
cycles, respectively. Also, during the cycles, both of them show
highly unstable deposition stripping as well as short circuit im-
punity (Figure 6a–c). We hypothesized that the stability of the
cathode plays an even more important key role in the cycling of
the Li-SPAN full battery. However, as the temperature rises, the
consumption rate of lithium and dissolution of polysulﬁdes ac-
celerate, leading to faster battery capacity degradation.[6,55] The
cells with LCE and HCE electrolytes go bad after less than 50
cycles because of poor compatibility with the anode and cath-
ode, respectively (Figure 6d; Figure S38, Supporting Informa-
tion). Due to the extremely low solubility of LiPSs and excellent
lithium metal compatibility, the Li||DMCE||SPAN displays a sta-
ble cycling of 290 cycles and reversible capacity of 565.72 mAh
gSPAN
−1 at the extreme condition of 60 °C (Figure 6e). Thanks
to good lithium metal deposition, LHCE also cycle stably for 80
cycles, but then exhibited rapid cell failure (Figure 6f). The E/S
ratio, an important parameter in Li-S batteries, directly aﬀects
the cost, real energy density, and electrochemical performance
of the battery. Therefore, we further explored the performance
of DMCE under lean electrolyte conditions (Figure 6g,h). Under
lean electrolyte conditions, as the charging and discharging pro-
cess occurs, the electrolyte is gradually consumed until it dries
out, resulting in a continuous capacity degradation.[56] However
larger electrolyte volume resulting from lower density helps com-
bat electrolyte drying out, partly attributed to the low density of
DMCE. The cell with DMCE achieved 120 stable cycles with a
capacity retention of 67.7% at the low E/S condition of 4.55. Sim-
ilarly, it also achieves 70 times steady cycles with a 0.09% attenu-
ation rate per cycle at high temperatures (Figure S39, Supporting
Information). In order to explore the reasons for the diﬀerences
in the cycle performances of diﬀerent electrolytes, we performed
impedance analysis on cells with diﬀerent cycling times. From
Figure S40 (Supporting Information), we can ﬁnd that DMCE
exhibits the lowest diﬀusion resistance and, with the increase of
the cycling time, the impedance of the DMCE is getting progres-
sively smaller due to the inorganic-rich species formed at the
interface. While the other electrolytes also exhibit progressively

3. Conclusion
In summary, we proposed a strategy for the design of molecu-
lar structures to modulate dipole interactions to realize the Li+-
anion compact solvation structure. We demonstrate that the Li+-
solvent interaction can be diminished by introducing the TMS
group, which greatly reduces the generation of LiPSs, mean-
while, the weakly coordinating ability of DFBn gives it the prop-
erty of internal solvation sheath coordination. The two synergisti-
cally construct a compact solvation structure rich in Li+ and FSI−,
which features low desolvation energy and superb Li+ transport
kinetics, and promotes the formation of a stable EEI. Beneﬁting
from the multifunctional design of DMCE, Li-SPAN cells achieve
exceptional cycling stability with 86.2% capacity retention after
1000 cycles at 1C rate, alongside remarkable temperature adapt-
ability demonstrated through stable operation at both 25 and
60 °C. This performance persists even under demanding condi-
tions of high SPAN loading (3.88 mAh cm−2) and lean electrolyte
conﬁguration (E/S ratio = 4.55). More importantly, we have pro-
vided a new insight into the application of ether electrolytes in
Li-SPAN batteries, achieving both a robust interface and stable
cycles.
