Low Concentration Electrolyte Enabling Anti-Clustering of
Lithium Polysulﬁdes and 3D-Growth of Li2S for Low
Temperature Li–S Conversion Chemistry
Zengqiang Guan, Xuanfeng Chen, Fulu Chu, Rongyu Deng, Sisi Wang, Jiamin Liu,
and Feixiang Wu*
The development of low-temperature lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB) has been
suppressed by rather poor sulfur utilization and cycle performance, caused by
planar Li2S growth, hindered lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) transformation, and
poor stability of the anode. Recently, low-concentration electrolytes (LCE) have
been employed as promising solutions to solve the above issues. However,
aggregation and deposition behavior of polysulﬁdes have been rarely studied.
In this work, by comparing the performance of 0.1 and 1 m LiFSI electrolytes,
LCEs are proven beneﬁcial for low-temperature LSBs via new fundamental
insights. According to growth pattern analyses by both morphology
observation and theoretical models, Li2S nucleation in LCEs switch into
progressive mode with less initial nuclei, which favors the vertical growth of
Li2S, resulting in a more complete lithium–sulfur conversion reaction under
cold conditions. Through visual experiments, computational simulation, and
progressive electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, the ability of LCEs to
suppress LiPSs clustering is supported and this anti-clustering ability
eﬀectively enhances the Li–S conversion reaction kinetics. Moreover, in LCEs
a protective SEI with LiF and Li2S/Li2S2 is likely to form on and stabilize the
anode. As a whole, the boosting eﬀect and the mechanism of LCEs enlighten
future designs on low-temperature electrolytes for high-performance
cryogenic LSBs.

fast ion transport channels as well as accommodation of volume

So far, above studies on the eﬀect of LCE on low tempera-
ture LSB mainly focus on the reactions on the anode, includ-

demonstrated by visual experiment and computational simula-
tions, showing the anti-clustering ability of LCE. These eﬀects
support the fast cathodic kinetics of LSB using LCE under cold
conditions.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Investigation of Li2S Nucleation and Growth
The planar growth of Li2S and the consequent early inactivation
of cathode is a signiﬁcant cause of the performance deterioration
of LSB in cold environment. Li2S deposition pattern depends on
the density of nucleating sites and the relative rates of nucleation
and growth, each of which is manipulated by many factors in-
cluding overpotential, substrate properties, and solvent choice.[17]
Therefore, it could be deduced that the concentration of elec-
trolyte plays an important role in Li2S deposition because it not
only directly determines the density of reaction sites, i.e., nucle-
ation sites, but also can aﬀect the nucleation and growth rates by
altering the ion structure and migration.
To evaluate the reduction degree of sulfur, a commonly used
chronoamperometry test was conducted. Carbon cloth was used
as cathode and lithium as anode. The electrolyte in the cathode
side was added with 0.2 m Li2S8 to imitate the shuttle eﬀect. The
cells were initially discharged at a constant current of 0.1 C to
2.06 V (0.1 m) and 2.0 V (1 m) in order to reduce all S8 and
higher-order polysulﬁdes to Li2S4 (nominally). Then the current
was monitored with the cells under potentiostatic discharge at
1.98 V (0.1 m) and 1.90 V (1 m), respectively. Figure 1a,b show
the ﬁtting of the chronoamperometry proﬁles of 0.1 and 1 m cell.
The blue and green parts represent the reduction of Li2S8 and
Li2S6 and the left part is the precipitation of Li2S. The integral
area of each part is the amount of charge transfer for correspond-
ing reaction. The precipitation capacities of Li2S in 0.1 and 1 m
are 640.84 and 430.88 mAh g−1 respectively. Ideally, S is meant to
fully deposit as Li2S on cathode under potentiostatic discharge. In
actual situation, however, the deposition ceases once the surface
of cathode is completely covered by insulated Li2S. The distinct
precipitation capacity may origin from diﬀerent growth pattern
in two electrolytes.
The diﬀerent tendency of nucleation in these two electrolytes
may provide an insight into this question. Four classical mod-
els of electrochemical deposition were introduced to ﬁt the
current-time proﬁle in chronoamperic test. They are Scharifker–
Hills models: 1) 3D instantaneous (3DI) and 2) 3D progressive
(3DP) nucleation models, which are followed by a bulk-diﬀusion-
controlled growth,[18] and Bewick, Fleischman, and Thirsk mod-
els: 2D instantaneous (2DI) and 2D progressive (2DP) models,
whose following growth is controlled by the incorporation of
adatoms into lattice.[19] (see detailed equations in Table S1, Sup-
porting Information and curves in Figure S1, Supporting Infor-
mation).
The time and response current are converted into dimension-
less indexes and plotted in the same format as the models, as
shown in Figure 1c. At least two conclusions can be drawn. First,
Li2S growth is governed by bulk diﬀusion, rather than surface
adatoms, in −20 °C. This is well in line with the idea that the
decelerated ion transfer in electrolyte is the main contribution
to the slow kinetics of LSB in low temperature. Second, despite
the deviation, Li2S nucleation ﬁts better with the 3DP model in
0.1 m cell, and 3DI model in 1 m cell. This means the initial nu-
cleation rate in 1 m electrolyte (instantaneous) is higher than in
0.1 m (progressive). In other words, Li2S in 1 m electrolyte tends to
form more initial nuclei, which inevitably leads to smaller grains
and thinner layer when the deposition ends. While in 0.1 m elec-
trolyte, less initial nuclei are formed, allowing larger grain and
deferred coverage of Li2S on cathode.
To observe the Li2S deposition behavior more intuitionally, im-
ages of surﬁcial morphology of cathodes (carbon paper in this
case) at diﬀerent state of charge were obtained. Cells were dis-
assembled to collect cathodes at speciﬁc points as tagged in the
discharge proﬁle in Figure 2. The cathodes were then imaged by
SEM. The Li2S deposition behaviors in the two electrolytes diﬀer
since the early stage of nucleation (Point 1). The Li2S in 1.0 m
cell consists of many ﬁne nuclei and presents a rough appear-
ance like crawling moss. While in 0.1 m cell it is formed by large
gathering islands. At the midway of the lower plateau (Point 2),

2.3. Morphology and Composition Analysis of Cycled Electrodes
For LSB, the loss of capacity mainly derives from two aspects.
One is the renowned “shuttle eﬀect”. That is to say, the dissolved
intermediate LiPSs diﬀuse through the separator to reach an-
ode and directly react with it, provided that the SEI is lack of
protectability, causing the loss of active sulfur and corrosion to
anode. The other is the irreversible transformation of S in the
cathode side. Lithium salts like LiFSI and LiNO3 (the additive in
our formular) can potentially oxidize sulfur compounds to higher
state such as LixSOy, which lose the ability to return back to active
mass.[22] In terms of these issues, the characterization of cycled
electrodes will provide more valid proofs for the better perfor-
mance of LCE.
Firstly, the cross section and surface morphology of anodes
cycled in diﬀerent electrolytes at 0.5 C and −20 °C were ob-
served by SEM as in Figure 5a–f. The deposited lithium layer
in 1 m cell (Figure 5d) is recognizably thicker than in 0.1 m
cell (Figure 5a), indicating loose inner structure. From the sur-
face morphology it can be concluded that the anode surface in
0.1 m cell (Figure 5b,c), despite several holes on the Li particles,
is smoother than in 1 m cell (Figure 5e,f). The anode surface in
1 m cell has more slacks as well as edges in small particles. These
may increase the contact surface between lithium and electrolyte,
exacerbating the side reactions between Li and solvent and LiPSs.
The key to the inhibited capacity loss lies in SEI. By detecting
the components of the surface of cycled electrodes, the cycling
stability of LCE is further veriﬁed. Figure 5g–j show the S 2p X-
ray photoelectron spectra of discharged anode and cathode of 0.1
and 1 m cell after 50 cycles at 0.5 C and −20 °C. C 1s and F 1s
spectra as well as atomic ratio are illustrated in Figures S5 and
S6 (Supporting Information). The whole spectra are corrected by
C─C peak at 284.8 eV. The C 1s spectra in both electrolytes have
peaks ≈284.8, 286.2, 288.6, and 290 eV, corresponding to C─C,
C─O, C═O and CO3
2−bond or component.[23] In term of anodes,
it is worth noting that F is richer in 1 M (Figure S5g,h). Since
LiFSI is the only source of F in the system, higher content of F
means larger amount of LiFSI consumption on the anode sur-
face, suggesting the malfunction of SEI. Moreover, the higher ra-
tio of F–C (≈688.3 eV) to F–Li (≈684.9 eV)[24] bond in 1 m cell than
0.1 m cell indicates more unfavorable transformation of LiFSI to
LiCF3 and Li2CSO2CF3 instead of LiF, which is known to be able
to suppress the lithium dendrites growth.[25] Another prominent
diﬀerence is the amount and state of sulfur element. Sulfur ex-
ists in multiple forms in SEI. In S 2p spectra, characteristic peaks
appear as dual peaks. Peaks below 166 eV correspond to nega-
tive valence sulfur, including Li2S (≈160.2 and 161.3 eV), Li2S2
(≈161.7 and 162.8 eV) and LiPSs (≈163.1 and 164.3 eV). While
peaks above 166 eV are positive valence sulfur, including ─SO2
(≈166.8 and 167.9 eV), −SO3 (≈168.9 and 170.0 eV), and −SO4
(≈170.3 and171.4 eV).[24,26] There are two sources of sulfur: the
decomposition of LiFSI and the side reaction between lithium
anode and LiPSs. Since sulfur element in the anode in 1 m cell
is richer (Figure S5g,h, Supporting Information) and mostly in
high-valence state (Figure 5h), it can be concluded that larger
amount of LiFSI and LiPSs decompose here, which should be the
result of the lack of protection of SEI in 1 m. Instead, the sulfur in
0.1 m cell not only is less in quantity, but also exists mainly in the
form of Li2S and Li2S2 (Figure 5g). The presence of Li2S/Li2S2 on
the surface of anode signiﬁes the synergetic eﬀect of LiPSs and
LiNO3, in which a small amount of LiPSs are sacriﬁced to be re-
duced to Li2S/Li2S2 to serve as a protective layer to suppress the
decomposition of lithium salt and further degradation of LiPSs
on anode,[24] which is conﬁrmed by the smaller atomic ratio of
sulfur in 0.1 m.
As for cathodes, the sole Li2S characteristic peaks in 0.1 m
cell convincingly conﬁrms the complete reduction of sulfur
(Figure 5i). While in 1 m cell, sulfur is not only small in con-
tent, but in various form ranging from Li2S to high valence sul-
fates (Figure 5j), which means irreversible oxidation of sulfur by
lithium salt does happen on cathode. According to this, combined
with the fact that signiﬁcantly large amount of F and more C is
detected in the surface of cathode of 1 m cell (Figure S6h, Sup-
porting Information), it can be concluded that lithium salt easily
decompose in 1 m electrolyte, which violates the stability of the
cell.
To sum up, in LCE, LiFSI is more likely to decompose to favor-
able LiF on anode, which, together with an inert Li2S/Li2S2 layer,
builds a protective SEI on anode. While on cathode, LiPSs can
fully reduce to Li2S and only little signal of salt decomposition is
detected. LCE ensures stable cycles by inhibiting side reactions
on both electrodes.
2.4. Low-Temperature Electrochemical Performance
To verify the above points, −20 °C cycling performance of cells
using 0.1 and 1 m LiFSI electrolytes (shorted as 0.1 cell and
1 m cell) were compared as in Figure S7 (Supporting Informa-
tion). 0.1 m cell outperforms 1 m cell in both speciﬁc capacity
and capacity retention. As in Figure S7a–c (Supporting Informa-
tion), when cycled at −20 °C and 0.2 C, the discharge feature
of 1 m cell changes obviously. The visible voltage hysteresis of
the second plateau and the curving of the line indicate sluggish
cathodic kinetics in the transformation of sulfur from LiPSs to
Li2S. 0.1 m cell, on the other hand, suﬀers only slight overpo-
tential. With the operating condition getting harsher, no matter
increasing the current density to 0.5 C or raising the sulfur mass
loading of cathode from 1 to 2.3 mg cm−2, the second plateau
of 1 m cell completely disappears, while that of 0.1 m cell under-
goes relatively small changes. When the discharge rate elevates to
0.5 C (Figure S7d–f, Supporting Information), 1 m cell loses the
characteristic second plateau, leaving only a steep slope. The liq-
uid to solid transformation of sulfur does not appear even when
the voltage is down to 1.5 V, resulting in a low capacity of only
≈250 mAh g−1. While in 0.1 m cell, the feature of discharge proﬁle
of LSB is basically preserved. When increasing the cathode sulfur
loading to 2.3 mg cm−2, at 0.2 C, the same fate of disappearance
of second plateau befalls 1 m cell (Figure S7g–i, Supporting In-
formation). Still, 0.1 m cell presents stable discharge capacity of
≈600 mAh g−1. Only when combining the above harsh conditions
(larger current density and higher cathode mass loading), will
there be an essential change in the discharge behavior of 0.1 m
cell, as shown in Figure S7j–l (Supporting Information). But even

3. Conclusion
At low temperature, LSB performance deteriorates drastically,
which is accounted for by sluggish cathodic kinetics caused by
LiPSs clustering, planar deposition of Li2S, and worsened elec-
trode stability. With the temperature decreasing to −20 °C, cou-
pled with elevated current density or cathode loading, the second
discharge plateau of LSB may disappear. By using low concentra-
tion electrolyte, both cycling stability and speciﬁc discharge ca-
pacity of LSB at cold condition are improved. LCE takes into eﬀect
mainly in three aspects. First, LCE is capable to switch the Li2S
nucleation into progressive mode, decrease the amount of initial
nuclei, promote the vertical nucleation, and avoid the too-early
ending of Li2S deposition. Second, by reducing the concentra-
tion of lithium salt, the bridging Li+ in electrolyte decreases, and
thus the clustering of LiPSs is suppressed, ensuring the success-
ful reduction of LiPSs to Li2S. Third, in LCE, the synergic eﬀect
of LiPSs and LiNO3 is more prominent, which helps to build a
dense SEI to protect the anode against the attack of lithium salt
and dissolved LiPSs. Therefore, a stable and high-capacity LSB
for low temperature is achieved.
