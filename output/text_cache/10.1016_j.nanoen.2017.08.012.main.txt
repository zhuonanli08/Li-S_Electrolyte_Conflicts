A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries suﬀer from the dissolution of its intermediate charge products (polysulﬁdes) in
organic electrolytes, which limits the utilization, rate performance and cycling stability of S cathode materials.
Formation of protective surface coatings on S cathodes may eﬀectively overcome such a challenge. Here, we
explored a simple, low cost, and widely applicable method that oﬀers in-situ formation of a protective coating on
the S-based cathode by using lithium bromide (LiBr) as a novel electrolyte additive. Quantum chemical (QC)
studies suggested that pre-cycling a S cathode at high potentials is needed to oxidize the Br- and induce for-
mation of DME(-H) radicals, which are involved in the formation of a polymerized protective layer of a solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) on a S cathode at high potentials. Experimental studies with a LiBr additive con-
ﬁrmed that 3 pre-cycles in a voltage range of 2.5–3.6 V are suﬃcient to achieve the formation of a robust Li ion
permeable SEI on the cathode, eﬀectively preventing the dissolution of polysulﬁdes into electrolyte. As a result,
almost no degradation was observed within 200 cycles, compared to more than 40% of capacity loss in the
benchmark control cells without LiBr or the pre-cycles. Post-mortem analysis on both the cathode and anode sides
of the LiBr-comprising cells further provided evidence for the in-situ SEI formation on the cathode and the lack of
polysulﬁdes’ re-precipitation. In addition, such studies showed smooth surface on the cycled Li metal anode, in
contrast to the rough Li SEI with dendrites and polysulﬁdes in the benchmark cells.
1. Introduction
The lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery is a promising energy storage
system for demanding applications in lightweight consumer electronics,
electromobility, and aerospace. Because of the high theoretical speciﬁc
and volumetric capacities of both the Li anode and S cathode, this
electrochemical couple oﬀers an opportunity to increase the gravi-
metric energy density above what is achievable in the well-established
intercalation chemistry-based Li-ion batteries [1–4]. Moreover, the high
abundance, low-cost, and low environmental impact of elemental S may
provide additional economic and ecologic beneﬁts. However, com-
mercialization of Li-S cells is hindered by severe drawbacks, which have
to be solved before the Li-S chemistry can compete with state-of-the-art
technology. The major problem is linked to the formation and high
solubility of polysulﬁdes, the reaction intermediates of the S (dissolu-
tion-precipitation) cathode chemistry, in the often used DME/DOL
electrolyte system [5]. As a consequence of such a dissolution, S is ir-
reversibly lost for further electrochemical transformations, which leads
to rapid capacity fading. The situation is aggravated by the so-called
“polysulﬁde shuttle” [3,6], which leads to a self-discharge. Besides, Li
corrosion and Li2S precipitation on the surface of electrodes slow down
Li ion transport in the cell and deteriorate both the electron transfer and
the charge-transfer kinetics, reducing the power performance and in-
creasing cell resistance [7–9]. We should note that the reduced or
oxidized state of sulfur-rich environments have a reasonable environ-
mental impact, which would cause severe metal corrosion problems.
In the last years, signiﬁcant eﬀorts went into the development of
sophisticated nano-architectures with tailored surface and/or pore
characteristics in order to anchor polysulﬁdes inside the cathode scaf-
fold. Success was obtained progressively by exploiting hierarchical
porous carbons [10–18], carbon hollow spheres [19–21], carbon na-
notubes [22–24], and graphene [25–28], as well as polarity tuning

N-, B- or S [31–37], and synthesis of various other (nano)composites

gering in the state of academic research and prototyping. A crucial
reason might be, that the electrolyte system, including additives, which
may constitute the most critical factor in terms of controlling poly-
sulﬁde saturation concentration and cathode (anode) protection against
polysulﬁde precipitation (attack), was not given a proper attention for a
long time.
More recently, several new electrolyte concepts based on highly
concentrated (equimolar) glyme/salt mixtures, sometimes referred to as
“solvent-in-salt” approach, began to exploit the “common ion eﬀect”
aiming for a millimolar level polysulﬁde solubility [16,46,47]. The
corresponding electrolyte systems not only showed room temperature
ionic liquid-like behavior featuring a high transference number for Li
ions as well as smooth Li plating, but also aﬀected the coulombic eﬃ-
ciency (CE) and S utilization positively, at least if combined with a
suitable cathode architecture. However, even at a minimal polysulﬁde
solubility, the solvation of S active material due to direct contact with
the electrolyte solvent still posed an imminent threat for slow poly-
sulﬁde leakage from the cathode in case of prolonged cell operation
[45]. Consequently, research focused on the encapsulation of S-C
composites by Li+ conducting layer coatings [48,49]. Since the ex-situ
deposition of the protective ﬁlms was found to be often unsuitable from
the cost prospective and the eﬀectiveness of the applied layers was
additionally limited by remaining defects and inhomogeneities, novel
electrolyte additives were introduced in order to induce the ﬁlm-
forming reactions in-situ. Such additives included phosphorus penta-
sulﬁde (P2S5), which was said to possess a function in alleviating the
capacity loss by increasing the solubility of Li2S and passivating the
lithium metal surface against polysulﬁde attack likewise [50]. LiNO3 is
another popular additive for improving passivation of the Li surface but
it is gradually consumed on the Li anode, which leads to a decrease in
the protection eﬃciency, and it is a strong oxidative agent causing
safety concerns if used at high concentrations, especially at high tem-
perature [51]. Since the polysulﬁde dissolution on cathode side was not
addressed by this approach, fast capacity fading and CE degradation as
well as formation of a thick and mossy Li surface layer remained a
challenge. Kim et al. demonstrated that the electrochemical reduction
of LiFSI in DME electrolyte, initiated by the generation of FSI(-F) anion
radicals close to the sulfur cathode operation potential at elevated
temperatures (60 °C), can form an eﬃcient long-term protection layer
containing ether-based polymer and LiF on the cathode and LiF and FSI-
decomposition products on the anode surfaces [48]. Cycle stability tests
conducted at both room temperature and 60 °C, after SEI formation at
60 °C, showed an average CE close to 100% over 1000 cycles as well as
high speciﬁc capacity and slow capacity fading after stabilization, lar-
gely surpassing the control cell including LiTFSI. The approach's ef-
fectiveness was later conﬁrmed by Aurbach at al., who cycled highly
disordered microporous carbon inﬁltrated with sulfur in 0.5 M LiFSI/
MPPFSI IL electrolyte solution showing that the performance and re-
action mechanism of the Li–S cell was controlled by the N-, S- and O-
rich SEI ﬁlms forming on the surface of both electrodes [52]. However,
in contrast to Kim et al. the authors emphasized that a repeated charge/
discharge to low potentials of 1.4 V and 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+, respectively,
at 30 °C under irreversible consumption of capacity was required as a
pretreatment to initiate ﬁlm growth in the initial cycles. Successful
encapsulation and quasi-solid-state reaction under solvent deﬁcient
conditions, a direct consequence of Li ion de-solvation and limited S-
electrolyte contact because of the dense surface ﬁlm, was evidenced by
a characteristic single voltage plateau and stable cell operation with
high CE in subsequent cycles. Aurbach et al. also reported on the use of
a ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC), an additive typically used to enhance
the cycling eﬃciency of Si anodes through growth of polymeric,
ﬂuorine containing SEI ﬁlms, forming surface layers on S/C electrodes
if initially discharged to low potential of 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ [53]. A similar
conclusion was drawn by Lee et al. [49], who found that a deep dis-
charge to 0.1 V vs. Li/Li+ was even more eﬃcient in ensuring the long-
term stability of the protective layer. Liang et al. previously mentioned
the lithium halides including (LiI and LiBr) in their patent using as
additives for Li-S batteries, which would induce enhanced cycling
performance [54,55]. Wu et al. previously reported on the ability of
lithium iodide (LiI) to form a smooth, uniform protective coating con-
taining polymer-like ﬁlms on the surface of the cathode particles, a
process induced in-situ by LiI equilibrating with radical former iodine
(I·) at oxidation potentials around 3.0 V vs. Li/Li+ followed by elec-
trolyte degradation and DME polymerization [9]. This SEI layer eﬀec-
tively prevented the diﬀusion of polysulﬁdes from the cathode to the
anode side (shuttling inhibition). Additionally, the rate capability as
well as electrochemical activity of Li2S (low overpotential for Li2S
oxidation in ﬁrst charge cycle) was improved signiﬁcantly.
In this paper, we elucidate the application of lithium bromide (LiBr)
as a promising electrolyte additive, which oﬀers in-situ protective
coating on the S-based cathode in an appropriate pre-cycling regime.
Quantum chemical (QC) studies revealed coating formation mechan-
isms and provided guidance on the pre-cycling potentials required for
the process to be eﬀective. Experimental studies in combination with
post-mortem analysis conﬁrmed eﬀectiveness of this approach and vi-
sualized the in-situ SEI formation on both cathode and anode sides.
2. Result and discussion
The principle capability of LiBr to form Li permeable, mechanically
stable, polymeric coatings on the cathode surface was ﬁrst investigated
using QC calculations that provided insight in the mechanisms of ﬁlm
forming reactions [9,48,56–58]. In order to simulate the Br- oxidation
and associated degradation reactions as well as interactions with sol-
vent molecules, the g09 Gaussian package [59] including implicit sol-
vent SMD solvation model with “ether” parameters (ε=3.0473) was
employed in all calculations. The composite methodologies G4 and
G4MP2 were utilized because they were previously demonstrated to
accurately predict ionization energies, electron and proton aﬃnities
and enthalpies of formation with the mean absolute deviations of
0.73–1.29 kcal/mol [60]. Oxidation potential were calculated ac-

As is well known, porous carbon hosts with sophisticated structures
are often utilized in Li-S batteries to overcome the challenges of poly-
sulﬁde dissolution and low conductivity. Although the results of elec-
trochemical performance tests were promising, the use of special hard
or soft templates combined with multiple synthesis steps typically in-
volved in the construction of those porous carbon architectures limited
their success substantially when it came to mass production and cost
considerations. In order to circumvent such restrictions and also de-
monstrate a broad applicability of our approach, we decided to test our
novel LiBr additive with cathodes prepared from commercially avail-
able porous carbon black that was melt-inﬁltrated with S. In line with
our previous publication, we used Ketjenblack EC-600JD (KB) as the
host because of its low cost, high conductivity and intrinsic multimodal
porosity even in non-activated form [65]. In the Fig. 2a, the KB host
showed uniform sphere-like nanoparticles with size of 20–50 nm and
connected with each other inducing good conductivity. After melt-in-
ﬁltration, the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of KB-S shown
in Fig. 2b still displayed sphere-like particles, but appeared aggluti-
nated due to some amount of S covering the outer surface of KB na-
noparticles. A similar particle size and agglomeration was also proven
by TEM in Fig. 2c, whereas the existence of (amorphous) sulfur inside
the KB-S nanocomposite was conﬁrmed by XRD in Fig. 2d. Thermo-
gravimetric analysis (TGA) studies in Fig. 2e further characterized the S
content of the KB-S nanocomposite to be approx. 66 wt%. In the Fig. 2f,
N2 physisorption isotherms measured at 77 K including changes of the
speciﬁc surface area (BET) and pore-size distributions calculated for
samples before and after S melt-inﬁltration are shown. They indicate a
considerable diﬀerence between pristine KB and the KB-S composite,
for which micropores completely disappeared, and the share of free
mesopores was largely reduced, as was the speciﬁc surface area, due to
S inﬁltration. Overall, a high content of elemental S was successfully
embedded into the KB host.
Guided by the QC studies discussed previously, pre-cycles to po-
tentials above 2.5 V were conducted on both electrolytes with and
without LiBr additive (Fig. 3(a-c) and Fig. S2) under exclusion of any
lithiation and de-lithiation of S. Without LiBr, the charge and discharge
capacities are small and reversible, indicating that there are no irre-
versible electrochemical reactions contributing to the SEI formation on
the cathode (Fig. S2). If we look at Fig. 3(a-c), using the LiBr additive in
the electrolytes, the 1st charge capacity increases drastically at poten-
tials higher than 3.5 V vs. Li/Li+ with large amounts of charge and low
CE. This is an indication for Br- oxidation, followed by DME decom-
position and consumption for ﬁlm-forming reactions during the pre-
charge processes to high voltages, in agreement with the QC studies
(Fig. 1, S1). If we do a comparison within the Fig. 3(a-c) we observe
that charging to higher charge voltage cutoﬀof 3.6 V (close to the
theoretical redox potential of Br-), the irreversible capacity of 1st cycle
steadily grows, highlighting that the increased electrochemical activity
of Br- is a strong driving force for the SEI formation on the S cathode.
Due to the relatively large speciﬁc surface area of the nanoparticle
morphology of the cathode, pre-charging the cells to higher voltage
would induce more uniform and homogeneous SEI formation on the S
cathode. As we can see in Fig. S3, the pre-charged S cathode using LiBr
additive was covered by a smooth SEI. These results are also in ac-
cordance with the diﬀerence in CVs recorded on metal electrodes
(blank Al foil versus Li foil) in electrolytes with and without LiBr ad-
ditive (Fig. 3d). In the cell using regular electrolyte, there is no visible
redox peak in the CV curve. However, as soon as LiBr is present in the
electrolyte, several pronounced redox peaks become visible. Higher
voltage cutoﬀthereby induced higher oxidation current peaks and
larger diﬀerence between oxidation and reduction peak areas, which is
due to accelerated “side reactions” taking place on both electrodes. In
order to check the SEI formation on the cathode, we did the post-
mortem SEM study on the cycled metal Al foil after CV scan between
2.5 and 3.6 V for 3 cycles. According to the SEM images (Fig. 3e), the
top surface of the cycled Al foil was uniformly covered by a dense SEI
layer pre-formed at these very high potentials.
To examine the chemical nature of the surface coatings, we per-
formed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization of the
cycled Al foil (Fig. 3f-h). XPS studies on empty and clean Al foil provide
clear insights on the SEI composition. After pre-cycles, there is no Al-2p
peaks in cycled Al foil, which means the cycled Al foil was uniformly
covered by the SEI layer. The SEI layer with thickness above 10 nm can
block the XPS signal of Al-2p (Fig. 3f). The C-1s spectra provide further
details of the SEI content. The peak of C-1s spectra at 286.3 eV is at-
tributed to C‒O‒C bonds from SEI-polymerized DME (a linear or comb-
branched oligoethers). The peak at 284.8 eV is due to the C‒C bonds
from adventitious carbon and ‒CH2‒CH2‒ bonds from polymerized
DME (Fig. 3g). The major peak of the O-1s spectra at 532.6 eV conﬁrms
the existence of C‒O‒C bonds from polymerized DME (due to bonds of
CH3‒O‒CH2‒ and ‒CH2‒O‒CH2‒) as SEI layer (Fig. 3h).
After pre-cycling studies, we conducted the comparison of cycling
performance of diﬀerent cells (areal S loading ca. 1 mg/cm2) within
200 cycles at room temperature (~ 24 °C) using both electrolyte sys-
tems and diﬀerent pre-charging conditions (Fig. 3i). Without pre-cycles,
both regular and LiBr-comprising cells showed poor capacity retentions
of ~61% and ~ 22%, respectively, which are mainly due to the poly-
sulﬁde dissolution. Under such “regular” cycling conditions, the de-
gradation was even worse when we used LiBr additive due to the cor-
rosion on the unprotected Al current collector induced by LiBr [54]. By
applying the pre-cycling strategy, the cycling stability of cells was en-
hanced considerably with capacity retention of ~ 69% (3.3 V), ~ 95%
(3.5 V) and ~ 104% (3.6 V) after 200 cycles and the coulombic eﬃ-
ciency was increased and closed to 100% (Fig. S4), which proved the
positive eﬀect of pre-charging to 3.5 V and 3.6 V, respectively. How-
ever, when pre-charging the cell to 3.6 V, the capacity utilization was
reduced (Fig. 3i) because the formed SEI created an additional physical
barrier for Li+ transfer, which increased the charge-transfer resistance
of the cell (see Fig. S5a and b). Therefore, for a given application an
optimal thickness of the LiBr-induced SEI should be found as a balance
between the eﬀectiveness against a polysulﬁde dissolution and a pos-
sible impedance growth when the overall thickness becomes excessive.
Considering that capacity loading and elevated temperature op-
eration are critical parameters for future commercial applications, the
LiBr additive studies were furthermore conducted on cells with higher
sulfur mass loading electrodes (~ 1.5 and ~ 2.0 mg cm−2) at a slightly
elevated temperatue of 30 °C. Comparing the pre-cycles studies be-
tween Fig. 4a and b, the irreversible 1st cycle charge capacity induced
by LiBr was over 200 mAh g−1 based on the mass of S, which is a about
30 times higher compared to the regular electrolyte, consequently
forming a thicker SEI on the cathode at the elevated temperature.
After pre-cycling studies, the cycling stabilities of cells using higher
mass loadings were further studied. For the regular samples, pre-cycling
at higher voltages was not beneﬁcial, but rather detrimental for the
cycling performance. In contrast, with LiBr and pre-cycling to 3.6 V,
both S cathodes with mass loadings of 1.5 and 2.0 mgs cm−2, respec-
tively, showed remarkable improvements in cycling stability. Following
initial formation (~ 10 cycles), the capacity fading slowed down to just
~5% from cycle 10–200. After 200 cycles at C/5, the cells still exhibited
~ 870 mAh g−1 (1.5 mgs cm−2) and ~ 710 mAh g−1 (2.0 mgs cm−2).
Under a higher C rate of C/2 in 100 cycles, the cycle stability was
visibly improved by combination of pre-charge and LiBr additive (Fig.
S6). If we look at the voltage proﬁles in the Fig. 4d-f, the voltage hys-
teresis is smaller for samples with LiBr additive and with the SEI formed
in high voltage pre-cycles (Fig. 4d–e). In fact, the cells with the higher
mass loading electrodes, pre-cycled in a LiBr-comprising electrolyte
showed smaller voltage hysteresis than LiBr-free cells with much lower

Post-mortem analysis of the electrodes revealed a drastic eﬀect of
LiBr on the morphologies of both Li and S cathodes. The SEM pictures in
Fig. 5a and b show S cathodes derived from cells disassembled after 200
cycles with a regular (LiBr-free) electrolyte. Compared to the SEM
images of a fresh S cathode in Fig. S7a and b, the electrode was fully
covered by ﬂake-like and crystalline Li2S and Li2S2 particles, which is a
clear indicator for a heavy polysulﬁde dissolution and undirected re-
precipitation caused by the shuttle of polysulﬁdes between the cathode
and anode sides. This ﬁnding is also consistent with the poor cycling
properties in Figs. 3i and 4c. Similarly, there was no change in the
morphology of a cycled cathode in case when we performed the pre-
cycling procedure in a regular electrolyte - it was still covered by ﬂake-
like and crystalline particles (see Fig. S8a and b). Moreover, just the
addition of LiBr without conducting high voltage pre-cycles did not
change the surface properties of the cycled cathode (Fig. S9a and b),
supporting the conclusion drawn from the QC calculations that SEI
growth on the cathode was not induced by LiBr at potentials below
2.8 V. As a consequence, the cycle stability in such a cell remained
poor. As well known, the layer formed by reaction products (Li2S or
Li2S2) block the transfer of electrons and Li ions during the charge and
discharge likewise, which leads to the increased cell resistance ac-
companied by a reduced cell capacity (S utilization). However, once we
did the pre-cycles at the high voltage range of 2.5–3.6 V in a LiBr-
comprising electrolyte, the morphology on the cathode after cycling
tests diﬀered remarkably from all the other samples. As visible in
Fig. 5c and d, the cathode surface became coated with a very smooth
polymer-like ﬁlm, which is in a signiﬁcant contrast to the rough surface
of the fresh electrode (compare Fig. 5(c and d) and S7(a and b)). No
crystalline precipitates were observed, further suggesting that poly-
sulﬁde diﬀusion was signiﬁcantly impeded by the protective ﬁlm
formed in-situ after pretreatment at high voltage.
Since the degree of the polysulﬁde dissolution correlates with the
irreversible mass loss of S from the cathode, we determined the S/S0
weight ratio in the cathodes after 200 cycles by energy dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS) measurements. As the result shown in Fig. 5e re-
veals, as much as ~ 60% of S is extracted from the cathode in a regular
(baseline) electrolyte, whereas the addition of LiBr into the electrolyte
and conducting pre-cycles reduced the S losses to only ~ 16% after 200
cycles.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we have reported for the ﬁrst time the use of LiBr as
promising electrolyte additive for Li-S batteries leading to multiple
performance improvements via formation of favorable surface layers
under suitable conditions. Quantum chemical (QC) studies suggested
that in order for the LiBr to be eﬀective, one needs to pre-cycle S
cathodes at elevated potentials in order to induce oxidation of Br- fol-
lowed by the decomposition of DME forming DME(-H) radicals needed
for the growth of the comb-branched polyether-based protective SEI
ﬁlm on the cathode. With the guidance of QC, experimental studies of
the Li-S cells comprising LiBr into electrolyte and pre-cycled at the
voltage range of 2.5–3.6 V showed excellent performance character-
istics with minimal capacity fading within 200 cycles. Charge-discharge
and cyclic voltammetry measurements conﬁrmed the irreversible re-
actions induced by LiBr at high potentials, resulting in the polymer-like
SEI formation on the cathode, which successfully prevented polysulﬁde
dissolution. Post-mortem studies of the cycled electrodes revealed a
dramatic eﬀect yielded by LiBr additive and pre-cycling at an elevated
potential range. Control cells (without LiBr or without pre-cycling)
showed rough dendrite-containing SEI on the Li anodes and precipita-
tion of insulative crystalline Li2S and Li2S2 on the cathodes. In contrast,
the pre-cycled LiBr – enhanced cells showed a very smooth polymer-like
SEI on the cathode and very smooth surface of the Li metal anode with
large Li grains. These ﬁndings provide further evidence of the dramatic
reduction in the polysulﬁde dissolution upon the LiBr-induced protec-
tive ﬁlm formation. Therefore, the presented results emphasize the
great promise of our low-cost approach to form an eﬀective protective
layer on the S-based cathode surface in-situ and additionally suggest
possible advantages of using LiBr for dendrite-free cycling of Li anodes
in ether-based electrolytes.
4. Materials and methods
4.1. Composite preparation
A Ketjenblack/sulfur (KB/S) composite was prepared by thoroughly
milling sulfur and Ketjenblack EC600JD in a weight ratio of 1:2 (KB:S),
followed by melt-inﬁltration of the sulfur in a pre-heated oven at 155 °C
for 30 min in air.
4.2. Structural characterization
The pore structure and particle morphology of KB and KB/S was
probed using a JEOL JSM-7800F scanning electron microscope (SEM)
as well as JEOL JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope (TEM).
The surface of both fresh and cycled anode and cathode samples was
visualized with a ﬁeld-emission LEO 1530 microscopy (Zeiss, Germany)
at a working distance of 3–6 mm and accelerating voltage of 2–3 kV.
EDS analysis was conducted at a distance of 10 mm and accelerating
voltage of 10 kV. Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) under nitrogen at-
mosphere (heating rate: 5 K min−1) on a Netzsch STA 409 PC Luxx
instrument conﬁrmed the sulfur content of the KB/S composite. A
PANalytical X´Pert PRO was applied to identify crystalline phases of
pristine sulfur, KB and KB/S samples via powder X-ray diﬀraction (Cu-
Kα radiation). Nitrogen physisorption isotherms were measured at 77 K
using a Microtrac BELSORP-max apparatus. Whereas the KB sample was
degassed under vacuum at 200 °C overnight, the KB/S composite was
activated under vacuum at room temperature to prevent sulfur loss. The
pore size distribution was calculated using the QSDFT adsorption
branch kernel for nitrogen on carbon (slit/cylindrical pores). The cu-
mulative pore volume at 2 nm pore width represents the micropore
volume. The total pore volume of both samples was obtained at p/
p0 > 0.99 and their speciﬁc surface area was calculated via multi-point
BET for p/p0 between 0.05 and 0.2. It should be noted that all values
related to the KB/S composite were normalized to the carbon share in
the composite. XPS analysis was done using the Thermo K-Alpha (Al Kα
peak). The vacuum transfer module was used to prevent air exposure.
The cycled electrodes including fresh Al foil were washed by fresh DME
4 times before XPS measurement. For the characterization of cycled
electrodes, the electrodes were drip-washed using fresh DME solvent
(4 mL) by 6 times to remove the lithium salts. Then, the sample was
sealed in vacuum box during the transfer. Finally, we put the TEM/SEM
sample holder in 10 s into the machine.
4.3. Electrochemical measurements
For cathode preparation, the KB/S composite powder was mixed
with carbon nanoﬁber conductive additive (5 wt%) (PR-25-XT-HHT,
Sigma-Aldrich) and ball-milled for 10 min at 25 Hz frequency. After
adding an aqueous binder solution consisting of 2.8 wt% poly(ethylene
oxide) (Mw > 5,000,000, Alfa Aesar), 0.7 wt% poly(vinylpyrrolidone)
(Mw = 360,000, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1.5 wt% styrene-butadiene-
rubber (15 wt% in water, Targray Inc.) and additional water to adjust
the viscosity, the slurry was ball-milled for another 15 min. In order to
improve the wetting properties, 1 mL EtOH was mixed into the slurry
before casting it on battery grade Al foil via doctor-blade technique.
After drying the cathode sheets at 80 °C for 1.5 h in air, circular samples
with a diameter of 1.5 cm and sulfur loadings of ca. 1.0 mg/cm2,
1.5 mg/cm2 and 2.0 mg/cm2, respectively, were punched for electro-
chemical tests in CR2032 coin cells. Lithium metal foil punched into
circular chips was used as counter and reference electrode in the two
electrode setup. A thin, porous polyoleﬁn ﬁlm was used as the se-
parator. The electrolyte consisted of 2.4 M LiTFSI salt dissolved in
DME/DOL (1:1, v-v) solvent with either no LiBr (2.4 M LiTFSI) or sa-
turated LiBr (99.998%, ultra
dry, Alfa Aesar) (2.4 M LiTFSI /
0.15MLiBr) additive and was applied with a pipette on the cathode
prior to cell stacking. The volume of electrolyte injected in coin cells is
set as about 30 μL per 1 mg of sulfur. The whole assembly process took
place inside an Ar glovebox (O2 and H2O levels < 0.2 ppm).
All cells were cycled in galvanostatic mode between 1.2 and 2.8 V
vs. Li/Li+ at a rate of C/5 using an Arbin battery test system or BaSyTec
CTS system (1 C equals 1672 mA/g). In selected cases, a formation (pre-
cycles) was performed by charging/discharging at a rate of C/20 be-
tween 2.5 and 3.3 V (pc-3.3 V), 3.5 V (pc-3.5 V) or 3.6 V (pc-3.6 V) for
3–5 cycles prior to the regular cycling protocol, in order to probe the
impact of pre-charge on the ﬁlm formation. To identify the electrolyte's
stability window as well as LiBr induced redox peaks, cyclic voltam-
metry (CV) was performed for cells containing a blank Al foil as
working electrode versus Li plus 2.4 M LiTFSI and 2.4 M LiTFSI / LiBr
electrolyte, respectively (Li//electrolyte//Al). A multi-channel Ivium n-
Stat potentiostat was used to cycle the cells with a sweep rate of
0.1 mV/s in a voltage window equivalent to the pre-cycles (pc-3.3 V,
pc-3.5 V or pc-3.6 V).
