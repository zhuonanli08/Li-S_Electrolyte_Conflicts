Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy Analysis of Dendrite
Growth on the Lithium Metal Surface in Polysulﬁde-Insoluble
Electrolytes
Rikuya Ito,1 Hiroki Nara,2,*,z
and Toshiyuki Momma1,2,*,z
1Graduate School of Advanced Science and Engineering, Waseda University, Shinjuku-ku, Tokyo 169-8555, Japan
2Waseda Research Institute for Science and Engineering, Waseda University, Shinjuku-ku, Tokyo 169-8555, Japan
Lithium has been widely investigated owing to its high theoretical speciﬁc capacity and low electrochemical potential. This is
required for high-energy-density lithium batteries such as lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. Recently, Li–S batteries with polysulﬁde-
insoluble electrolytes, such as sulfolane (SL) and triglyme (G3), have attracted research attention because they suppress the
dissolution of lithium polysulﬁde intermediates. However, lithium dendrite growth on the Li metal anode during the charging-
–discharging process causes an internal short-circuit, which may lead to serious accidents. To realize a Li–S battery, a fail-safe
system to prevent short-circuits is essential. In this study, we investigated the cycle degradation mechanism of a Li metal anode in
SL and G3 electrolytes using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The changes in charge transfer resistance (Rct) and solid
electrolyte interphase resistance (RSEI) of Li–Li symmetrical cells in SL and G3 electrolytes was measured under charge–discharge
cycling in detail down to internal short-circuits. Consequently, in both the electrolyte systems, the RSEI and Rct behaviors were
disparate during cycling, and a mechanism for the short-circuit process was proposed. In addition, before the short-circuit process
occurred, the change in the trend of Rct from stable to increasing was indicative of an imminent short-circuit.

Lithium metal has attracted attention as a promising anode
candidate
owing
to
its
high
theoretical
speciﬁc
capacity
of
3861 mAh g−1 and lowest electrochemical potential (−3.045 V vs
the standard hydrogen electrode).1–3 Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery
(LSB) employing Li metal anode is one of the most promising Li
batteries. It has a high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1.4–7
However, practical applications of LSB are hindered by the degrada-
tion of the anode and cathode through processes such as the dendritic/
mossy growth of Li on the Li metal anode and the dissolution of
lithium polysulﬁdes (mainly Li2S4 and Li2S8) into electrolytes.5,8
Recently, the application of polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes, such as
sulfolane (SL) and triglyme (G3), has been proposed to suppress
polysulﬁde dissolution,9,10 but internal short circuits due to Li dendrite
growth remain a serious issue.
To address this issue, many studies related to the suppression of
Li dendrite growth have been reported.1,11–20 The factors that
promote Li dendrite growth are the roughness of the electrode
surface and the inhomogeneity of the composition and thickness of
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).11,12 Thus, pre-treatments such
as electrochemical polishing and etching in a naphthalene–-
tetrahydrofuran solution
for the
generation of
a smooth Li
surface11–13 and the application of a uniformly protective layer on
the Li surface14–18 have been reported. In addition, dendrite free
anode materials such as Li-In alloy19 and amorphous silicon
polymer20 have been reported. Also, many studies have been
conducted on the Li deposition mechanism and SEI formation and
growth mechanisms, with various analytical methods having been
used to analyze these phenomena.21–27 In particular, electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) can be used to separate and simulta-
neously evaluate the charge transfer resistance and the SEI-derived
resistance.21–23 Each resistance change is dependent on the electrode
surface area and morphology, as well as the SEI thickness;14,28 thus,
EIS is useful for analyzing the cycle degradation mechanism of the
Li metal anode that arises due to dendrite growth and SEI
thickening. However, there have been few studies that have detailed
the cycle degradation of a Li metal anode during cycling using this
method,29 and there have been no studies that have evaluated the
changes in charge transfer resistance and SEI resistance during
cycling in detail down to internal short circuits. In addition, the cycle
degradation mechanism of the Li metal anode in the SL and G3
electrolytes has not been reported, although the SEI composition and
Li deposition morphology are known to change depending on the
electrolyte. Therefore, it is necessary to elucidate the cycle degrada-
tion mechanism of Li metal anodes in the SL and G3 electrolytes for
the practical application of LSB. In addition, even if LSB are
commercialized, it is essential for them to feature a fail-safe system
to circumvent short circuits to prevent serious accidents.
Here, the cycle degradation mechanism of a Li metal anode in the
SL and G3 electrolytes was studied using EIS. The impedance of the
Li–Li symmetrical cells in the SL and G3 electrolytes was measured
during charge–discharge cycling. Based on the temperature depen-
dence of the impedance and focused ion beam scanning transmission
electron microscopy (FIB-STEM) measurements, an equivalent
circuit of the Li metal anode was designed and a ﬁtting analysis
was performed. Furthermore, based on various structural and
chemical characterizations, a cycle degradation mechanism com-
prising three processes was proposed. In addition, before the
occurrence of the short-circuit process, the change in the trend of
the charge transfer resistance from stable to increasing is indicative
of an imminent short circuit, suggesting that the internal short-circuit
process of the cell can be predicted by tracking these changes.

using an electrochemical workstation (VSP; Biologic, France).

Fitting analysis method.—Fitting analysis of the Nyquist plots
was conducted using the Microsoft Solver add-in. The ﬁtting error
was evaluated as the sum of the absolute value of error ratio of
absolute impedance |Z| and phase shift θ, which was calculated from
observed and calculated data at each frequency. Regression calcula-
tion was performed to minimize the sum of the values at all
frequencies.
Characterization methods.—SEM, FIB-SEM, and FIB-STEM
images of the Li electrode were obtained using a FIB-STEM
instrument (Hitachi High-Technologies NB5000). The cells were
ﬁrst disassembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox and the Li electrode was
rinsed with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) to remove the electrolyte,
followed by drying under vacuum. The electrode was then trans-
ferred to an FIB-STEM instrument using an airtight transfer vessel.
The SEM and FIB-SEM images were obtained at an accelerating
voltage of 5 kV. The FIB-STEM images were acquired at an
accelerating voltage of 30 kV. Etching was performed using a Ga
ion beam after the deposition of a protective ﬁlm of carbon and
tungsten on the samples.
The XPS analysis of the Li electrode was conducted using a PHI-
VersaProbe II (Ulvac-phi) spectrometer. Prior to the measurements,
the cell was disassembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox and the Li
electrode was rinsed with dimethyl carbonate and DME to remove
the electrolyte, followed by drying under vacuum. The sample was
then transferred to an XPS instrument using an airtight transfer
vessel. XPS measurements were conducted using an Al-Kα
(1486.7 eV) X-ray source.
Results and Discussion
Conﬁrmation of internal short circuit.—The internal short
circuit of the Li–Li symmetrical cells with SL or G3 electrolytes
was conﬁrmed. The charge–discharge curves of the cells with the SL
and G3 electrolytes are shown in Figs. 1 and S1, respectively. An
internal short circuit occurs at the point where the overvoltage drops
rapidly during the charge–discharge process. In the cells with the SL
electrolyte, the diffusion overvoltage gradually increased, and a
short circuit occurred during the 5th discharge process (approxi-
mately 64 h). In the other sample, a short circuit was observed
around the 5th cycle. After the short circuit, the overvoltage
remained ﬂat at low values. In contrast, a micro short circuit, which
reached a short circuit in several cycles, occurred during the 9th
charge (approximately 113 h) of the cells containing the G3
electrolyte. In the other samples, a short circuit was observed around
the 9th cycle. Subsequently, a rapid increase or decrease in over-
voltage was repeatedly observed after the short circuit occurred [Fig.
S1]. Therefore, the short circuit recovers more easily in the cell with
the G3 electrolyte compared to that with the SL electrolyte, and the
overvoltage increases and decreases each time it recovers and shorts.
Change in the Nyquist plots during charge–discharge cy-
cling.—The potential-controlled impedances of the cells with SL
and G3 electrolytes were measured before and after charging.
Nyquist plots of the cells with SL and G3 electrolytes are shown in
Figs. 2a–2c and S2a–S2c, respectively. For both electrolyte systems,
two semicircles are observed in the Nyquist plots, where the

charge transfer resistances, respectively.14,16,18 The semicircle of the
SEI can be separated into two semicircles with charge–discharge
cycling, as shown in Figs. 2c and S2c. The impedance of the cells
decreased after the short-circuit process, as conﬁrmed by the
charge–discharge curves shown in Figs. 1 and S1. The results
suggest that the current ﬂow at a part of the short circuit decreases
cell impedance. Additionally, in the cells with the SL electrolyte, the

organic SEI layer (SEI2). The third RC circuit, which can be
observed in the low-frequency region (10–1 Hz), corresponds to a
charge-transfer process. Hereafter, the changes in the impedance
parameters during charge–discharge cycling are discussed individu-

Changes in the impedance behavior during charge–discharge
cycling.— Charge transfer resistance.—The changes in the charge
transfer resistance (Rct), double layer capacitance (Cdl), and time
constant of the charge transfer process (τct) of the Li metal anode
with the SL and G3 electrolytes are shown in Figs. 5a–5c and
S4a–S4d, respectively. The same behavior for each parameter was
observed between the early, middle, and late systems during cycling.
First, τct, which is theoretically unchanged against a particular
electrochemical process, increases. This change may be due to the
SEI/Li interface resulting from changes in the SEI composition and
immersion in the electrolyte. However, the characteristic frequency
(f = 1/2πτ) was within the range of 10−1 Hz. Therefore, it can be
assumed that the charge transfer process is unchanging, meaning that
a change in the morphology of Li can be discussed according to the
changes in Rct and Cdl. Subsequently, the entire cycle, down to the
internal short circuit, can be divided into three stages according to
the changes in Rct. The stage in which Rct decreased was deﬁned as
the early stage, that in which Rct remained stable was deﬁned as the
middle stage, and that in which Rct increased was deﬁned as the late
stage. In the early cycles, Rct decreased, and Cdl increased. This
result indicates that the electrode surface area increases with dendrite
growth. Subsequently, both Rct and Cdl leveled off in the middle of
the cycling, suggesting that the growth of the dendrites was
suppressed by stress because they reached the separator. In later
cycles, Rct increased and Cdl decreased. The electrode surface area is
expected to decrease as the regrown dendrites merge with each
other. In contrast, the impedance behavior after a short circuit differs
between the systems. In the SL electrolyte, Rct decreases to almost
zero after the short circuit, indicating that most of the current ﬂows
in the short circuit, and the electrochemical reaction does not
progress at all. Moreover, Rct slightly decreases after the short-
circuit process in the G3 electrolyte, indicating that the electro-
chemical reaction is still in progress owing to the micro short-circuit
process. Therefore, comparing the SL and G3 electrolytes, the
behavior of Rct and Cdl after the short circuit differed, which may
be attributed to the difference in the short-circuit conditions.
To conﬁrm this difference, SEM images of the Li metal anode
after the 1st charge in the SL and G3 electrolytes were recorded
[Figs. 6a and 6b]. The dendrites in the SL electrolyte are thicker than
those in the G3 electrolyte. FIB-STEM images of the Li metal anode
after the 1st charge in the SL and G3 electrolytes were also recorded
[Figs. 6c and 6d]. In both systems, the dendrites merged with each
other. Based on the results, early in the cycling, it is thought that the

surface area of the electrode increases due to the growth of dendrites
and that the area then decreases due to the merging of dendrites at
the same time. However, the decrease in Rct suggests that the former
had a greater effect. In contrast, late in cycling, the increase in Rct

The formation of thicker dendrites in the SL electrolyte is related to
overvoltage. The overvoltage for nucleation during the 1st charge
was approximately 70 mV in the SL electrolyte and approximately
threefold higher in the G3 electrolyte at 200 mV [Figs. 1 and S1]. Li
nucleation with a larger nuclear radius results in lower nucleation
overvoltage.36 Therefore, larger Li nuclei were formed in the SL
electrolyte, leading to the subsequent growth of thick dendrites.
Therefore, the lower nucleation overvoltage in the SL electrolyte can
be attributed to Li+ conductivity. Although the following are results
that do not include HFE, Li(SL)2TFSI exhibits lower ionic con-
ductivity (σion) than that of Li(G3)TFSI (Li(SL)2TFSI: 0.42 mS
cm−1 vs Li(G3)TFSI: 1.1 mS cm−1). However, the Li+ transference
number (tLi) of Li(SL)2TFSI is 0.68, which is much higher than that
of the Li(G3)TFSI (tLi = 0.028).34,37 Therefore, the Li+ conductivity
(σLi) of Li(SL)2TFSI is also higher than that of Li(G3)TFSI
(Li(SL)2TFSI: 0.29 mS cm−1 vs Li(G3)TFSI: 0.031 mS cm−1),
suggesting that the same trend is observed in a system with HFE.
SEI2 (organic layer of the SEI) resistance.—The changes in the
SEI2 resistance (RSEI2), capacitance of the SEI2 layer (CSEI2), and
time constant of SEI2 (τSEI2) of the Li metal anode in the SL
electrolyte are shown in Figs. 5d–5f, respectively. RSEI2 exhibited
the same behavior for Rct throughout the entire cycling process,
which can be attributed to dendrite growth and inhibition and short-
circuit conditions. Meanwhile, CSEI2 barely changed throughout the
cycling, and τSEI2 showed a trend similar to that of RSEI2. This is
expected to be due to changes in the SEI composition derived from
the SL electrolyte. In contrast, the changes in the RSEI2, CSEI2, and
τSEI2 values of the Li metal anode in the G3 electrolyte are shown in
Figs. S4e–S4h. τSEI2 is constant for the G3 electrolyte as opposed to
that in the SL electrolyte. Additionally, RSEI2 and CSEI2 exhibited the
same behavior for Rct and Cdl early in the cycling, in the middle of
the cycling, and after the short circuit occurred. This can be
attributed to dendrite growth and inhibition and short-circuit condi-
tions, similar to those of the SL electrolyte. Meanwhile, RSEI2 and
CSEI2 exhibit different behaviors for Rct and Cdl only late in cycling;
RSEI2 decreases and CSEI2 increases. This results from a decrease in
the effective thickness of the SEI2 layer at the sites of dendrite
growth, whereas a decrease in the electrode surface area is expected
late in cycling, as discussed above.
FIB-SEM images of the Li metal anode in the SL electrolyte after
the 1st and 2nd charges were obtained, as shown in Figs. S5a and
S5b. FIB-SEM images of the Li metal anode in the G3 electrolyte
after the 1st and 10th charge cycles were also obtained, as shown in
Figs. S5c and S5d. From these results, the thickness of the SEI layer,
which is considered to consist mainly of an organic layer, decreases
throughout the cycling in both systems. In addition, late in the
cycling, the area of the SEI2/SEI1 interface decreases owing to the
merger of dendrites, as discussed above, and the thickness of the
SEI2 layer decreases simultaneously. However, the increase in the
RSEI2 in SL electrolyte suggests that the former had a more
signiﬁcant effect. In contrast, the decrease in RSEI2 in the G3
electrolyte indicated that the latter had a more signiﬁcant effect.

To evaluate the SL-derived SEI composition changes and discuss
the relationship between the SEI composition and τSEI2 changes, as
described above, XPS spectra of the Li metal surface before cycling
and after the 1st, 2nd, and 5th charges were measured. The O1s and
S2p spectra were analyzed with reference to previous reports35,38 to
determine the atomic and peak area ratios of Li2O and Li2S [Fig. S6
and Table S1]. From these results, the content rates of Li2O and Li2S
in SEI2 increased until the 2nd charge and then decreased after the
5th charge. Considering the EIS results, when the RSEI2 decreases
and increases, theoretically the CSEI2 should increase and decrease,
respectively. However, the change in CSEI2 was small compared with
that in RSEI2. This can be attributed to the effect of the content rate of
Li2O and Li2S as the dielectric constants of Li2O (εr = ∼11) 39 and
Li2S (εr = ∼16) 40 are lower than that of SL (43.4).41 Thus, the
increase in the content of Li2O and Li2S leads to the replacement of
the SL contained in the pores in SEI2, decreasing CSEI2. Therefore,
the change in τSEI2 can be attributed to changes in the Li2O and Li2S
content rates in the SEI2 (organic) layer. The increase in Li2O and
Li2S corresponds to dendrite growth early in cycling, which can be
attributed to the further reduction of Li2SO3 and Li2S2O4 as the
reductive decomposition products of TFSI−and SL. In addition, the
decrease in Li2O and Li2S also corresponds to dendrite growth late
in the cycling, which is considered to promote the reduction of
TFSI−and SL mainly and increase Li2SO3 and Li2S2O4.
SEI1 (inorganic layer of SEI) resistance.—Changes in the SEI1
resistance (RSEI1), capacitance of the SEI1 layer (CSEI1), and time
constant of SEI1 (τSEI1) of the Li metal anode in the SL and G3
electrolytes are shown in Figs. 5g–5i and S4i–S4k, respectively. The
same behavior for each parameter was conﬁrmed between the
systems throughout cycling: RSEI1 increased, CSEI1 decreased, and
τSEI1 leveled off. The results show that the composition of the SEI1
(inorganic) layer does not change signiﬁcantly, but thickens. By
contrast, the impedance behavior after a short circuit was completely
different between the systems. For the SL electrolyte, RSEI1
decreased; however, unlike Rct and RSEI2, RSEI1 was ∼10 Ω even
after the short-circuit process. These results imply that current ﬂows
through the inorganic layer of the SEI during the short-circuit
process. However, τSEI1 remained stable after a short circuit. In other
words, Li+ conduction through the inorganic layer was maintained.
Therefore, it is also expected that the tunneling current does not
simply ﬂow through the SEI1 layer, but that Li dissolves from one
dendrite at the part of the short circuit, and Li+ is conducted through
the SEI1 layer to the other dendrite, resulting in Li deposition. For
the G3 electrolyte, an increase in RSEI1, a decrease in CSEI1, and
leveling off of τSEI1 after the short circuit were also observed,
indicating that the electrochemical reaction is still in progress and
the inorganic layer thickens.
To determine whether e−or Li+ conduction occurred through the
SEI1 layer, EIS measurements were conducted under low-tempera-
ture conditions42 after the short-circuit process. To evaluate the
temperature dependence of each resistance component, Nyquist plots
for each cell temperature in the SL electrolyte are shown in Fig. S7.
In contrast to the drastic change in the intercept of the x-axis at high
frequencies, which mainly represents the ionic resistance in the cell,
the diameter of the semicircle derived from SEI1 shows no change
from 25 °C to 15 °C. This result indicates that the SEI1 resistance
after a short circuit barely depends on temperature, suggesting that a
tunneling current ﬂows through the SEI1 layer at part of the short
circuit.

Cycle degradation mechanism.—Based on the results discussed
in the previous section, a mechanism is proposed for the cycle
degradation of the Li metal anode in both electrolytes [Fig. 7]. In the
SL electrolyte, during early cycling, it is thought that an increase in
the electrode surface area (SEI2/SEI1 interface) and a decrease in the
thickness of the organic layer of SEI due to the stretching of the
layer is caused by dendrite growth. In the middle cycles, the growth
of dendrites was suppressed by stress because they reached the
separator. Later in cycling, the electrode surface area is expected to
decrease because the re-grown dendrites merge with each other,
followed by a short circuit. In addition, the SEI2/SEI1 interface area
decreases because of the merging of re-grown dendrites, as
discussed above, and the thickness of the SEI2 layer decreases
simultaneously owing to the stretching of the layer. However, the
former had a more signiﬁcant effect in the SL electrolyte. The
inorganic layer of the SEI thickens throughout cycling. After the
short circuit, most of the current ﬂows through the inorganic layer of
the SEI at the thicker dendrite of the short-circuit part and the
electrochemical reaction does not progress. In the G3 electrolyte,
degradation progressed similarly to that in the SL electrolyte in the
early, middle, and late cycles, as discussed above. In contrast, in
terms of the organic layer of the SEI, the decrease in the thickness of
the layer had a more signiﬁcant effect than the decrease in the SEI2/
SEI1 interface area later in the cycling. In addition, the short-circuit
conditions are different between the electrolytes, and it is indicated
that the electrochemical reaction is still in progress in the G3
electrolyte owing to a micro short circuit with thinner dendrites and
thicker inorganic layer. Hence, in both systems, Li dendrite growth
mainly progressed early and late in the cycling, and the organic layer
of the SEI degraded owing to Li dendrite growth. In contrast, the
inorganic layer of the SEI thickened throughout cycling. A compar-
ison of the cycle degradation mechanisms in both electrolytes
suggests that there are differences in their degradation rates during
cycling and under short-circuit conditions. In addition, the RSEI2 and
Rct behaviors were disparate in early-, middle-, and late-stage
cycling. Before the short circuit in the SL electrolyte, the changes
in the trends of RSEI2 and Rct from stable to increasing indicate an
imminent short circuit. In the G3 electrolyte, the changes in the
trends of Rct from stable to increasing and the change in RSEI2 from
stable to decreasing indicate an imminent short circuit. Therefore, an
internal short circuit can be predicted by tracking the changes in
each type of resistance.
Facile internal short-circuit prediction method.—Based on the
above discussion, the internal short circuits of the cells can be
predicted by tracking the changes in RSEI2 or Rct. In addition, the
resistance at 10 Hz exhibits similar changes in RSEI1, RSEI2, and Rct.
Changes in resistance at 10 Hz can be tracked as a facile method for
predicting internal short circuits. Only one point at 10 Hz was
measured every 30 min during charge–discharge cycling. The
changes in the real part of the impedance Re(Z) and the absolute
value of the impedance |Z| of the Li–Li symmetrical cells in SL and
G3 electrolytes are shown in Figs. 8 and S8, respectively. In both
systems, the same behavior of Re(Z) and |Z| was conﬁrmed in early-,
middle-, and late-stage cycling. Early in cycling, Re(Z) and |Z|
decreased and leveled off in the middle of cycling. Then, late in
cycling, Re(Z) and |Z| increased. The behavior in the early and
middle cycles can be attributed to the behavior of RSEI2 and Rct in
both the electrolytes. In contrast, the late cycling behavior can be
attributed to the increase in RSEI1, RSEI2, and Rct in the SL
electrolyte, and the increase in RSEI1 and Rct in the G3 electrolyte.
Therefore, before the short-circuit process, the change in the trend of
the resistance at 10 Hz from stable to increasing is an indication of
an imminent short circuit as well as for Rct, suggesting that the
internal short-circuit process of the cells can be versatilely predicted
by tracking only one point of the resistance at 10 Hz.

To evaluate the applicability of the method for predicting internal
short-circuit to LSB, the current-controlled impedances of the Li–S
cells with SL electrolytes were also measured during the charge–-
discharge process, similar to Li–Li symmetrical cell. Fisrt of all, the
charge–discharge curves of the Li–S cell with the SL electrolyte are
shown in Fig. S10, and an internal short circuit occurred during the
32th charge process. Then, Nyquist plots of the cells with SL
electrolytes under current-controlled conditions during charging and
discharging are shown in Figs. S11a–S11d, respectively. In the
Nyquist plots during charging, the two semicircles are observed,
where the semicircles at high and low frequencies correspond to SEI
and charge transfer resistances derived from Li and polysulﬁde
species, respectively.16,43 In contrast, the three semicircles are
observed in the Nyquist plots during discharging, where the
semicircles at high, middle and low frequencies correspond to SEI
and charge transfer resistances of Li and that of polysulﬁde species,
respectively.43 The semicircle of the SEI was not separated into two
semicircles with charge–discharge cycling, as opposite to Li–Li
symmetrical cell. This result suggests that, in the Li–S cell, a
different composition of the SEI is formed due to the inﬂuence of
trace amounts of polysulﬁdes species that have leached out in SL
electrolyte, compared to the Li–Li symmetrical cell [Fig. 2]. Then,
the charge transfer resistance of the polysulﬁde species during
charging is small and overlaps with the charge transfer resistance
of Li, making it difﬁcult to separate them. Therefore, by using the
equivalent circuit [Fig. S12], the ﬁtting analysis of the Nyquist plots
during discharging shown in Fig. S11c was performed. The changes
in the SEI resistance (RSEI), charge transfer resistance derived from
the Li metal anode (Rct, Li) during discharging are shown in Figs.
S13a, S13d, respectively. RSEI continued to decrease even just prior
to the short circuit, in contrast to the results obtained from the Li–Li
symmetrical cell. This behavior is likely attributable to the inﬂuence
of the polysulﬁdes species discussed above. On the other hand, the
charge transfer resistance of Li exhibited an increase just before the
short circuit, similar to the behavior observed in the Li–Li
symmetrical cell. Therefore, it is expected that the internal short
circuits can be predicted by tracking the resistance at 10 Hz. The
changes in the real part of the impedance Re(Z) at 10 Hz of the Li–S
cells in electrolyte during discharging is shown in Fig. S14. From the
result, the resistance at 10 Hz continued to decrease even just prior to
the short circuit, the changes in the trends from stable to increasing
was not observed, similar to Rct, Li. The cause of this behavior can be
attributed to the signiﬁcant charge transfer resistance of polysuliﬁde
species (Rct, PS) at 10 Hz, as suggested by Fig. S11(e). The S/KB
cathode used in this study is a prototype at the lab-scale, rather than
a commercial product, resulting in a higher resistance. In the future,
as the resistance of the S/KB cathode decreases during the product
development process, similar to the behavior observed in Li–Li
symmetric cells, the change in the trend of the resistance at 10 Hz
from stable to increasing will be observed of an imminent short
circuit. This is expected to enable the prediction of short circuits of
LSB.
Conclusions
To develop a method for predicting the internal short-circuit
processes in LSB in polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes, the cycle
degradation of a Li metal anode in SL and G3 electrolytes was
analyzed using EIS measurements. The semicircle for the SEI of the
Nyquist plots was separated into two semicircles during charge–-
discharge cycling. EIS measurements under low-temperature condi-
tions and FIB-STEM images revealed that the semicircles were
derived from the two-layered structure of the SEI, which comprised
organic and inorganic layers stacked in the depth direction. Based on
this, an equivalent circuit was designed for the Li metal anode
comprising three RC circuits and a ﬁtting analysis was performed.
The RSEI2 and Rct behaviors were disparate in the early, middle, and
late stages of cycling and after the short-circuit process in both
systems. Furthermore, based on FIB-SEM, FIB-STEM, and XPS
measurements, a cycle degradation mechanism comprising of three
processes was proposed. In both systems, Li dendrite growth mainly
progressed early and late during cycling, and the degradation of the
organic layer of the SEI was affected by Li dendrite growth. In
contrast, the inorganic layer of the SEI became thicker over the
course of the cycling. A comparison of the cycle degradation
mechanisms in both electrolytes suggested that there were differ-
ences in the degradation late in the cycling and under short-circuit
conditions. In addition, before the short-circuit process occurs, the
change in Rct from stable to increasing is indicative of an imminent
short circuit, suggesting that the internal short circuit of the cell can
be predicted by tracking this change. Based on this, as a facile
method to predict the internal short-circuit processes in batteries, the
changes in resistance at 10 Hz were tracked, which was similar to the
change in Rct. Consequently, before the short-circuit process, the
change in resistance at 10 Hz from stable to increasing indicates that
the short circuit is imminent, as is the case for the charge transfer
resistance, suggesting that the internal short circuit of the cell can be
versatilely predicted by tracking only one point of the resistance at
10 Hz. However, the resistance at 10 Hz of Li–S cell continued to
decrease even just prior to the short circuit, the changes in the trends
from stable to increasing was not observed because the S/KB
cathode used in this study is a prototype at the lab-scale, rather
than a commercial product, resulting in a higher resistance. In the
future, as the resistance of the S/KB cathode decreases during the
product development process, similar to the behavior observed in
Li–Li symmetric cells, the change in the trend of the resistance at
10 Hz from stable to increasing will be observed of an imminent
short circuit. This method is expected to be applied to LSB to safely
shut down the cell before an internal short circuit occurs.
