Polysulﬁde Shuttle Suppression by Electrolytes with
Low-Density for High-Energy Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Christine Weller, Jonas Pampel, Susanne Dörﬂer, Holger Althues,* and Stefan Kaskel
A low-density electrolyte composition is introduced for lithium–sulfur (Li–S)
batteries with intrinsic and effective polysulﬁde shuttle suppression. Hexyl
methyl ether (HME) is used in combination with 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) as a solvent
for the Li–S battery electrolyte. The choice of solvent limits the dissolution of
polysulﬁdes, leading to successful suppression of the parasitic polysulﬁde
shuttle. Hence, high coulombic efﬁciencies of 98% can be obtained in coin cells
for over 50 cycles. The impact of the speciﬁcally adapted electrolyte solvent is
studied systematically by varying solvent combinations in order to enable the
development of light innovative shuttle suppressing electrolytes. In contrast to
concepts relying on hydroﬂuoro ether dilution, the presented electrolyte features
a signiﬁcantly reduced mass density at 2 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfo-
nylimide) (LiTFSI) conductive salt enabling signiﬁcant weight reduction on the
Li–S prototype cell level, thus, allowing energy densities up to 400 Wh kg1.
1. Introduction
The lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery has attracted enormous attention
over the past decade as one of the most promising next generation
battery systems to replace established lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).
The high theoretical speciﬁc capacity of sulfur (1672 mAh gS
1)
and its high theoretical energy density of 2500 Wh kg1 when
coupled with a Li metal anode, as well as the abundance of sulfur,
makes the Li–S battery system a possible lightweight and low-cost
alternative for applications in drones, high
altitude pseudo satellites and aerospace
technology.[1]
Besides numerous promising features of
sulfur as cathode active material, few inher-
ent challenges still hamper the widespread
commercialization of the Li–S battery sys-
tem. Technological challenges, especially,
regarding Li anode corrosion, polysulﬁde
shuttling, active material loss and, as a con-
sequence, rapid capacity fade hinder the
comprehensive application.[2] Much effort
has been undertaken to alleviate the a
fore-mentioned problems of the system.
Several approaches have been published
focusing on the suppression of the para-
sitic polysulﬁde shuttle reaction by cath-
ode adaptation. In the past years, a huge
effort was put into the design of tailored
carbon materials to restrain polysulﬁdes in the carbon scaffold.[3]
Polysulﬁde dissolution into the electrolyte is reduced by adapted
cathode structures including, e.g., highly porous carbon materials,
metal oxides and functionalized polymers.[4] Furthermore, polysul-
ﬁde repelling separator coatings were employed to hinder polysul-
ﬁde diffusion to the Li metal anode.[5] Beyond the design of
tailored carbon structures and the application of polysulﬁde bar-
riers, the most effective attempts to intrinsically suppress the
shuttle so far have involved the adaptation of the employed elec-
trolyte system.[6–10] The current state of the art (SOTA) electrolyte,
comprising 1 M
lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) conductive salt dissolved in 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) in equal volumetric ratio,
results in a catholyte-type Li–S battery with high polysulﬁde sol-
ubility.[11,12] In this system, the active material is almost fully dis-
solved in the electrolyte and can easily migrate to the Li anode,
thus, entering the shuttle mechanism. In order to countervail
the shuttle effects, LiNO3 is added. LiNO3 improves the formation
of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and enhances the cycle
stability and coulombic efﬁciency (CE).[13–15] Thus, the shuttle
problem can be prevented since an interfacial layer is formed
on the lithium anode through reduction of electrolyte compo-
nents.[15] The major drawback of the nitrate additive is its steady
consumption resulting in ﬁnite shuttle suppression. Due to the
high polysulﬁde solubility, the reaction intermediates involved
in the electrochemical redox process are present in the dis-
solved state. Therefore, the total electrolyte volume is directly
connected to the sulfur utilization and, thus, the discharged
capacity.[16] Large electrolyte volume is required to achieve
sufﬁcient cycling performance in terms of capacity and cycle-
life.[6,13] However, to achieve Li–S cells with high speciﬁc

unusual high LiTFSI concentrations up to 7 M in glyme sol-
vents.[6–8,10,19–21] The so called “solvent-in-salt” approaches
often suffer from the high viscosity of the electrolyte caused
by the high concentration of the conductive salt. Thus, chemi-
cally inert, non-solvating hydroﬂuorinated ethers (HFE) were
introduced as diluents to the “solvent-in-salt” or “sparingly sol-
vating” electrolytes.[21–23] A recurring hydroﬂuoro ether is
1,1,2,2,-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (TTE)
as diluent for glyme based and acetonitrile based highly concen-
trated electrolytes as well as sulfolane comprising shuttle sup-
pressing electrolyte.[9,10,23–25] The approach of restricting the
polysulﬁde solubility is typically based on the high concentrations
of the conductive salt and the accompanied “common ion
effect” in the pre-saturated electrolytes.[19] The electrolytes with
restricted polysulﬁde solubility combine several beneﬁts. Hence,
they are a highly promising research ﬁeld for future generation
Li–S batteries. First of all, the polysulﬁde shuttle, which is directly
related to the dissolution of polysulﬁdes, is effectively inhibited
without addition of highly reactive salts. This prolongs the long-
term cycle stability compared to electrolytes utilizing LiNO3 as
additive. LiNO3 is consumed throughout cycling by the permanent
reformation of the SEI on the lithium metal anode, thus, leading
to ﬁnite shuttle suppression. Second, high salt concentrations
enhance the electrolytes oxidative stability, therefore, increasing
the applicable operating voltage window.[10,26] Third, by changing
the charge/discharge process from a catholyte-type discharge, uti-
lizing dissolved intermediate polysulﬁde species, into a quasi-
solid-solid conversion with minimized concentration of dissolved
polysulﬁdes, the sulfur utilization becomes less dependent of the
employed electrolyte volume. The latter is a remarkable progress
in operating Li–S batteries as a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio is
essential for the design of energy density optimized prototype
cells.[11,17,27] As mentioned before, sparingly solvating electrolytes
suffer from high viscosity and low ionic conductivity, thus,
TTE is often added as inert solvent. Even though dilution with
non-coordinating HFE solvents improves the electrolyte properties,
the optimal operating temperatures often above room temperature
are required.[21] Furthermore, HFEs are generally hampered by
their high mass density due to the 18-fold increased atomic mass
of ﬂuorine compared to hydrogen. The frequently applied TTE for
example has a high mass density of 1.53 g mL1 (20 C).[28] In con-
trast, the typically employed solvents DME and DOL have low mass
densities of 1.065 g mL1 (20 C) and 0.867 g mL1 (25 C), respec-
tively. Hence, the ﬁnal electrolyte mass density of HFE-based sys-
tems typically exceeds that of DOL/DME based electrolytes, even at
comparable salt concentration.[29]
Nowadays, several publications deal with shuttle suppression
based on restricted polysulﬁde dissolution by basically adapting
the common ion effect in order to minimize the E/S volume
ratio.[7,18,21,25] However, none of these approaches actually consider
the intrinsic mass density of the electrolyte itself. Therefore, we
introduce in the following for the ﬁrst time a low-density electrolyte
with restricted polysulﬁde solubility, based on a low-density
ether (hexyl methyl ether) with only one oxygen donor function.
Systematic evaluation employing the galvanostatic intermittent
titration technique (GITT) and S–Li2S symmetrical cells, are used
to conﬁrm the distinct discharge mechanism in such hexyl methyl
ether (HME) based electrolytes.[30]
The adapted HME based electrolyte is herein employed in coin
cells under lean electrolyte conditions and with reasonable high
sulfur loading (5 μL mgS
1, 2.2 mgS cm2) utilizing solvent-free
processed sulfur cathodes.[21,31,32] Thus, the results presented are
particular promising for Li–S cells with high speciﬁc energy.[11,17]
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Solubility of Lithium Polysulﬁdes in Different Electrolytes
The solvent’s ability to dissolve polysulﬁdes is determined by
the solvent’s donor number and its dielectric permittivity.[12,33]
Therefore, we decided to employ hexyl methyl ether (HME) as a
suitable medium with expected low permittivity.[34] The coordi-
nation number is limited due to the sterical demand of the hexyl
groups and their interference with each other. In order to inves-
tigate the ability of HME to dissolve polysulﬁdes, the solubility
of a stoichiometric ratio of 0.1 m Li2S8 is tested in different HME-
based electrolytes (Figure 1).

After stirring Li2S and sulfur in the molar ratio Li/S ¼ 2/8 for at
least 2 weeks at elevated temperatures (45 C) no dissolution is
visible for 0.1 m Li2S8 in HME (Figure 1a). Sulfur is suspended
in the solvent. However, the white (Li2S) and yellow (S8) solid gen-
erally appear unchanged. No yellow or orange coloration, indicat-
ing the formation of polysulﬁdes, is observed. The reduced
polysulﬁde dissolution should effectively suppress the polysulﬁde
shuttle. Similar effects are visible for the HME electrolyte contain-
ing 2 M LiTFSI (Figure 1b). It needs to be considered that the inhi-
bition of polysulﬁde formation also considerably reduces the
reduction kinetics and can as well impede the sulfur reduction
to some extent. In order to enhance the electrochemical kinetics,
various HME/DOL ratios by volume were evaluated. Eventually,
the addition of 10 Vol% DOL and 2 m LiTFSI showed the best
performance in terms of cycle stability, overpotential and sulfur
utilization. The addition of DOL enables the solubility of a small
proportion of polysulﬁdes (Figure 1c–e). The maximum dissolved
concentration of stoichiometric Li2S8 in 2 M LiTFSI in HME/
DOL (9:1) is 50 mM as determined by UV/VIS measurements
(Figure S1, Supporting Information). Thus, the concentration
of dissolved atomic sulfur [S] equals 0.4 m in 2 m LiTFSI in
HME/DOL (9:1), which is considerably lower than in DOL/
DME electrolytes ([S]  6 m).[35]
2.2. Electrochemical Investigation of a Li–S Cell
The galvanostatic cycling results of a Li–S cell employing the pris-
tine electrolyte 2 m LiTFSI in HME is presented in Figure 2.
A wide voltage window (1–3 V versus Li/Liþ) was chosen at ﬁrst
to ensure that full S–conversion is possible as a high overpotential
was expected due to the very low solubility of Li2S8 in the pristine
LiTFSI/HME electrolyte (compare Figure 1a). The obtained data
represents a proof-of-concept for ether based electrolytes with a
classical ether solvent comprising only one oxygen donor atom
per molecule. So far, only glycol ethers like DME or cyclic ethers
like tetrahydrofuran (THF) or DOL were used as ethers for elec-
trolyte solvents in Li–S batteries. The main advantage of the
herein presented electrolyte is the low mass density (1.12 g cm3)
based on the solvent’s extremely low mass density (0.77 g cm3).[36]
Comparable electrolytes with intrinsic shuttle suppression show
higher mass densities. Hence, a sulfolane comprising electrolyte
(TMS/TTE) is characterized by 1.51 g cm3.[25] A sparingly solva-
ting electrolyte based on an equal molar ratio of diglyme and
LiTFSI has a similar mass density of 1.48 g cm3 without taking
into account the TTE additive.[18] The DME/DOL electrolyte shows
a comparable mass density of 1.13 mg cm3. However, due to the
strong relation between a good cell performance and the necessary
high electrolyte volume, the advantage of the low mass density is
lost on cell level (Figure S2, Supporting Information). As visible
from the coulombic efﬁciency and the charge proﬁle, the shuttle is
sufﬁciently suppressed with HME-based electrolyte (Figure 2a,b).[37]
A capacity of 1100 mAh gS
1 was initially discharged, equal to
a moderate sulfur utilization of 66%. The discharge capacity
quickly degrades in the ﬁrst 7 cycles and remains stable at about
900 mAh gS
1 for the following 16 cycles. Linear capacity loss is
observed after cycle 23. Unlike to the cycle stability, the CE,
calculated as quotient of discharged/charged capacity, remains
unstable until cycle 20 with values above 100%. The latter implies
irreversible capacity losses. As soon as the capacity starts to fade,
the CE stays at 100%. The irreversible discharge capacity indi-
cated by CE > 100% is ascribed to electrolyte decomposition on
the cathode, most presumably on the high speciﬁc surface area of
the carbon material. However, a decomposition of the Li-activated
ether molecules by a nucleophilic attack of the polysulﬁdes cannot
be excluded. When CE stays at 100% the irreversible discharge
capacity is reduced. However, at that point overall cell degradation
starts. Hence, it is correlated that Li2S oxidation is impeded in the
electrolyte with low polysulﬁde solubility after formation of a SEI
on the cathode surface. The sometimes appearing deviations to
values below 100% are most likely due to electrolyte depletion
and/or wetting issues indicated by charging peaks and unstable
charging behavior. If a polysulﬁde shuttle occurs, a constant voltage
plateau should be observed at 2.4 V versus Li/Liþ during charge,
corresponding to the continous oxidation of shuttled long-chain
polysulﬁdes. In contrast, the steep voltage increase indicates recov-
ery of sulfur at the end of the charging step (Figure 2b). The dis-
charge proceeds via two voltage plateaus at about 2.1 and 1.8 V
versus Li/Liþ in the ﬁrst cycle. Thus, discharge seems to be shifted
to lower voltages compared to the SOTA DME/DOL electrolyte
(2.4 V versus Li/Liþ and 2.05 V versus Li/Liþ for ﬁrst and second
plateau, respectively). The lower discharge plateaus indicate a high

reasons. In the present system, the effect can be mainly ascribed to
increased internal resistances in the cell. Due to the low dissolu-
tion ability of the electrolyte towards sulfur and (poly)-sulﬁdes, the
reduction of active material is kinetically hindered. A similar effect
is assumed for the charging procedure. Insulating Li2S-particles
precipitate on the carbon surface throughout discharge. Resolva-
tion and oxidation of these particles is also kinetically hindered
due to the low solubility of Li2S in the electrolyte. This is in stark
contrast to electrolyte recipes published before, relying on sol-
vents with high donor ability and sufﬁcient solubility for Li2S,
e.g., tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME), acetonitrile
(ACN) or DME/DOL. In the SOTA electrolyte, intermediate and
long chain polysulﬁdes facilitate the dissolution of Li2S by redox
mediation.[38] The low solubility of all reaction intermediates
and solid products in electrolytes with restricted polysulﬁde sol-
ubility lead to the growth of an insulating layer of active material
on the cathode surface. S8 and Li2S precipitation and accumu-
lation on the carbon surface impedes the charge transfer from
the carbon scaffold to the active material. Furthermore, pore
blocking effects appear upon accumulation, therefore, reinforc-
ing active material loss.[39] As a result, the internal resistance is
believed to gradually increase resulting in higher overpotential
during charge and discharge. At ﬁrst, a relatively wide voltage
window (1–3 V versus Li/Liþ) is chosen to compensate these
effects in order to extract most of the capacity and allow sufﬁ-
cient reversibility of cycling (see Figure 2). However, the larger
voltage window used can induce severe side reactions and electro-
lyte depletion. As a result, the overall cycle stability is reduced.
As known from literature, a slight increase in polysulﬁde solubility
can signiﬁcantly improve the redox behavior and the reaction
kinetics.[25] Thus, DME or DOL were added as a modulating
solvent in the herein studied electrolyte. DOL proved to be more
suitable than DME in terms of electrochemical stability. Moreover,
DOL is known to polymerize on the metallic lithium anode, there-
fore, supporting the formation of a stable SEI.[13] Furthermore,
the cycling behavior is adapted to enhance the cycle-life and reduce
the overpotential.
Several volumetric ratios of HME:DOL were evaluated and an
amount of 10 vol% of DOL to yield HME/DOL (9:1, by volume)
was eventually found to enhance the solubility of the active spe-
cies in the electrolyte, without simultaneously creating shuttling
polysulﬁdes (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The initial sulfur
utilization is slightly increased to 1150 mAh gS
1 and the CE
remains at 98% (Figure 3). Intriguingly, the addition of
10 vol% DOL distinctly raises the discharge voltage of the sec-
ond plateau and decreases the charge voltage (Figure 3b). As
energy density is calculated by multiplying the average discharge
voltage and discharge capacity, the observed increase in discharge
voltage while preserving the capacity can be directly translated into
an increase of energy density with respect to the pristine HME
system. The lowered voltage difference between charge and dis-
charge is ascribed to the enhanced solubility of (poly)-sulﬁde
intermediates which are known to act as redox mediators
and activate Li2S agglomerates for oxidation.
To further improve the cycle stability, the voltage range is
reduced to 1.5–2.5 V for galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling.
Hence, side reactions and electrolyte depletion can be mini-
mized, thus, improving the cycle-life (Figure 3a,b). In order
to allow nonetheless a reasonable conversion to sulfur during
the charging process, a constant voltage step is implemented
during charging at 2.5 V versus Li/Liþ. Generally, the polysul-
ﬁde shuttle is promoted by a constant voltage procedure at the
end of charge. Thus, constant voltage steps have not been used
for charging typical Li–S batteries comprising non-encapsulated
sulfur, so far. However, as the electrolyte approach presented
herein sufﬁciently suppresses polysulﬁde dissolution and the
connected shuttle, the constant voltage step improves the sulfur
recovery and cycle stability. Moreover, the lowered charge cut-
off voltage reduces electrolyte consumption. A low charging
current of 0.05 C is employed for the galvanostatic cycling until
reaching the cut-off voltage of 2.5 V versus Li/Liþ in order to
support the re-oxidation of the Li2S species, followed by the dis-
cussed constant voltage step until reaching a cut-off current of
0.01 C. If the polysulﬁde shuttle occurs, an elongated charging
plateau would be visible and the current would not reach the
threshold value during the constant voltage period due to the
steady redox shuttle of polysulﬁdes. Furthermore, a shuttle if
present would lead to a vast drop in CE as the electrical charge
injected is unlimited or at least only terminated by time

Insights into the electrochemical stability of the HME/DOL
electrolyte were obtained by cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Figure 3c).
The CV results conﬁrm that the addition of DOL leads to a decrease
in overpotential. Without DOL two reduction peaks at 2.0 and
1.4 V versus Li/Liþ are observed with CV (Figure S4, Supporting
Information). The voltage limits for the cyclic voltammetry of
the Li–S cell based on DOL-containing electrolyte 2 m LiTFSI in
HME/DOL (9:1, by volume) are extended throughout the cycling
procedure, starting with 1.5–2.8 V versus Li/Liþ in the ﬁrst
cycle. Two reduction peaks and one oxidation signal are detected
(Figure 3c). The reduction peaks are observed at 2.1 and 1.7 V
versus Li/Liþ, thus being located at lower values with respect to
the classical behavior in DME/DOL electrolyte. This effect can
be ascribed to the inherent characteristics of electrolytes with
low polysulﬁde solubility, e.g., the increased overpotential.
Extending the voltage range to 1.5–3.0 V versus Li/Liþ veriﬁes
the electrolyte’s oxidative stability at least up to 3.0 V. Moreover,
the increased upper voltage limitation reveals that S8-oxidation is
incomplete at 2.8 V. Reductive electrolyte decomposition below
1.2 V is observed when the lower voltage limitation is decreased
to 1.0 V. The reduction of the lower voltage threshold further inﬂu-
ences the oxidation behavior of polysulﬁdes: The onset for the
oxidation to S8 is raised from 2.6 to 2.8 V (Figure 3c, red). The
increased oxidation potential can be connected to increased
amounts of precipitated and agglomerated Li2S and a resulting
increased overpotential upon oxidation. At voltages below 1.2 V
versus Li/Liþ another reduction signal becomes apparent. On such
low voltages irreversible electrolyte decomposition processes are
induced. Therefore, Liþ ion mobility decreases and reduction
products precipitate on the cathode surface, thus, increasing
charge transfer resistances. Both processes enforce the overpo-
tential leading to an increase in oxidation potential offset.
2.3. Rate Capability
The electrolyte’s rate capability for 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C is presented
in Figure 4. The obtained discharge capacity is marginally reduced
when the discharge rate is doubled from 0.1 to 0.2 C. However, a
sharp decline in capacity is observed for discharge rates ≥0.5 C.
The discharged capacity drops below 200 mAh mgS
1 at 0.5 C.
For the 1 C discharge, no considerable capacity is obtained. A full
recovery, similar to cycle 20 (950 mAh mgS
1), can be regained
by lowering the discharge rate to 0.1 C after the high rate discharge

concentrated systems (2 m versus 5–7 m), the observed low Li-ion
conductivity can not be linked to the viscosity. Considering the
low permittivity of a similar ether, diethyl ether (εr ¼ 3.06 at
25 C),[40] the ionic interaction in the present electrolyte is driven
towards ionic association to form contact ion pairs. This is con-

2 m LiTFSI in HME electrolyte and 2 m LiTFSI in HME/DOL

The presented charge/discharge voltage proﬁles show a steady
increase in overpotential upon continuous increase of the dis-
charge current rate. The second plateau is lowered to 1.7 V versus
Li/Liþ for 0.2 C discharge. Nonetheless, a reasonable capacity
is obtained until the lower cut-off voltage of 1.5 V is reached
(Figure 4b). A further increase of the discharge rate appears
to be detrimental for the discharge behavior. No distinctive dis-

without any plateau formation is observed. The related charging

charge (SoC) subsequent to higher discharge rates (Figure 4c, 0.5 C

rates indicating the absence of solid discharge products. Thus, no
overpotential inducing Li2S is precipitated. Contrarily, the charge
proﬁle following a 0.1 C discharge shows a distinctive overpotential
which is related to the Li2S resolvation. A faster discharge (0.2 C)
affects this charging overpotential, the initial voltage increase is
lowered and the overpotential is less distinctive. For 0.5 C no initial
overpotential is observed which is in accordance to the very low
discharge capacity indicates the absence of solid Li2S.
The herein employed cell setup, S/C-cathode versus Li-metal
anode, does not allow the discrimination between anode
and cathode degradation. Therefore, a symmetrical cell setup is
used to investigate cathode-electrolyte interactions without the
interference of the metallic Li-anode. Hence, inﬂuences of the
cathode on rate capability can be determined.
2.4. Cathode-Electrolyte Interface
A crucial technique to measure intrinsic cathode performance
without superimposed degradation effects of the metallic Li-anode
is symmetrical cycling of S versus Li2S.[30] This setup enables
the study of the cathode-electrolyte interface without interfering
anode related effects, e.g., dendrite formation or electrolyte reduc-
tion. An initially discharged, thus, pre-lithiated electrode, serves
as Li-reservoir in the symmetrical setup. The same electrolyte is
used for the pre-lithiation and symmetrical cycling to avoid addi-
tional washing and drying steps that might falsify the results. For
reasons of comparability, both electrodes have a sulfur loading of
2.2 mgS cm2 and a density of 0.5 g cm3 in order to keep them as
identical as possible. The cycle stability test (Figure 5a) reveals deg-
radation of the Li-metal free cell. The CE is slightly above 100%,
which can be linked to the imperfect capacity balancing of the
studied electrodes. During the pre-lithiation step, a sulfur utiliza-
tion of 70% S was achieved. Therefore, even though S-loading is
equal in both electrodes, the symmetrical cycling is limited by the
areal capacity of the pre-lithiated Li2S electrode. Considering the
areal capacity, the S-electrode is initially overbalanced compared
to the Li2S-electrode. The unbalancing is mirrored in the ﬁrst
discharge of the symmetrical cell (negative voltage, Figure 5b).
The lower voltage limit (1 V) is not reached during sulfur
reduction in the ﬁrst cycle. As the Li-concentration is solely depend-
ing on the pre-lithiated electrode, a full conversion of S to Li2S
is not possible in the ﬁrst cycle (Figure 5b). Reduction of the
S-electrode stops at higher voltages of 0.16 V which correspond
to the second plateau in a half-cell setup. Therefore, the reduction
presumably stops at a mixture of intermediate polysulﬁdes, e.g.,
Li2S4 and Li2S (Figure S7 and S8, Supporting Information).
The symmetrical cycling procedure includes a ﬁrst discharge
of the pristine sulfur electrode with a low current rate of 0.05 C in
order to simulate the ﬁrst discharge in a Li–S cell and enable a
comparable formation step on the pristine sulfur electrode as
already accomplished on the pre-lithiated electrode. However,
the slow current inﬂuences the electrodes’ potential, thus, no
steep voltage increase or decrease might be monitored for the

2.5. Basic Mechanistic Insights into the Discharge Process
Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was con-
ducted with sulfur-cathodes versus Li metal anodes to get further
insights into the kinetics and thermodynamics during dis-
charging.[22] All experiments are conducted in coin cells in
order to prepare and evaluate an electrolyte system for eventual
application in high energy pouch cells. Hence, evaluation of
internal resistance changes by electrical impedance spectros-
copy (EIS) is beyond the scope of this work. However, impor-
tant insights into the kinetically limiting processes are obtained
by GITT. The open circuit potential (OCP) is monitored during
discharge for 2 h equilibration time. The equilibrium voltage is
plotted versus the discharged capacity (Figure 7). The difference
between the voltage during the discharge while employing a con-

kinetics of polysuﬂide conversion in the electrolyte system during
discharge.

3. Conclusion
The electrolyte system introduced herein provides a working con-
cept of intrinsically restricted polysulﬁde solubility with low mass
density in combination with a state of the art cathode system. The
latter is a crucial requirement for applications in cells with high
speciﬁc energy. Using the non-ﬂuorinated (HME) based electro-
lyte, a discharge mechanism suppressing polysulﬁde dissolution
is induced resembling a quasi-solid-solid sulfur-lithium sulﬁde
conversion. Due to the change of reaction mechanism, very
low E/S ratios are expected for future pouch cell designs with
reduced dead volume and high active mass. The new electrolyte
was herein combined with an easy scalable commercial carbon
based cathode without sulfur encapsulation. Thus, the concept of
applying common ether solvents in Li–S batteries is proven.
Based on this approach, solvents with high mass density and
viscosity, which are commonly used for restricted polysulﬁde
solubility in Li-S batteries (e.g., TEGDME, TMS), can be avoided.
Despite the formation of long-chain polysulﬁde intermediates
was effectively suppressed, further optimization of the cathode
structure adapted to the new mechanism is required in the
future. This individual adaptation of the cathode structure
depending for the chosen electrolyte is promising for pouch cells
with high speciﬁc energy surpassing 400 Wh kg1.
4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation: Electrolyte preparation and cell assembly was per-
formed in a glove box under argon atmosphere (H2O and O2 < 0.01 ppm).
LiTFSI was dried prior to usage. 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL) was stored over
molecular sieve (3 Å). Hexyl methyl ether (HME) was purged with Ar, dried
over molecular sieve (3 Å) at least three times, and, ﬁnally, stored over
molecular sieve. For electrolyte preparation, the deﬁned molarity of
conductive
salt
Lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI)
was dissolved in HME or HME/DOL (9:1, by volume) and stirred over-
night. Electrolyte density was determined using a 5 mL volumetric ﬂask
under argon atmosphere. The ﬁlled amount of electrolyte was weighed
in a threefold determination at 25 C. Ionic conductivity was measured
using a LF400 graphite measurement electrode coupled to a GMH 5430
measurement device. The temperature was monitored with an internal
temperature sensor. Various temperatures were stabilized using an external
silicon bath.
UV/Vis spectroscopy was performed as described earlier.[25] Standard
samples for calibration were prepared in an argon ﬁlled glove box (H2O
and O2 <0.01 ppm) by ﬁrst mixing Li2S and elemental sulfur to yield stoi-
chiometric 0.5 m Li2S8 in DOL. The polysulﬁde stock solution was stirred
for 2 weeks at 50 C until no precipitates were visible. A certain amount of
the polysulﬁde solution corresponding to the desired molarity was added
to a solution of 2 m LiTFSI in HME and diluted with additional DOL to yield
a volumetric 9:1 mixture of the solvents and a conductive salt concentra-
tion of 2 m with 5, 7.5, 10, and 20 mM additional lithium polysulﬁdes,
respectively. The prepared mixtures were stirred for 12 h days at room
temperature. Three-fold determination was carried out for all samples.
The electrolyte samples for Raman spectroscopy were, as well, prepared
under argon atmosphere and sealed in a vial. A laser with 514 nm wave-
length was used for excitation.
Electrode Preparation: The cathodes used herein were prepared using a
solvent-free roll-press procedure as described elsewhere.[32] Sulfur was
ﬁrst melt inﬁltrated into carbon black (Ketjenblack EC600JD) for 30 min
at 155 C under ambient conditions. The as-prepared C/S composite
was then mixed with multi-walled carbon nanotubes (Nanocyl MWCNT)
and poly(tetraﬂuoroethylene) (PTFE) in a weight ratio of 90:7:3 (C/S:
MWCNT : PTFE) to give a sulfur content of 60 wt% in the ﬁnal electrode.
After homogenization and intensive grinding, electrode sheets were pre-
pared using shearing forces. The free standing electrode ﬁlms were then
laminated onto a primer coated aluminum current collector for electro-
chemical characterization. The electrodes were punched into circular disks
(15 mm in diameter) having a typical sulfur loading of 2.2 mgS cm2 and a

full discharge is completed.
