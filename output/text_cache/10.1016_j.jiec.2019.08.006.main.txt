A B S T R A C T
Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) has been reported as a novel additive to improve the cycling performance of Li/S
batteries because LiNO3 suppresses the polysulﬁde shuttling problem. However, several studies indicate
that LiNO3 instead decreases the battery performance due to the formation of irreversible products at the
cathode. In this study, we investigated the role of LiNO3 in irreversible product formation. To elucidate the
effect of LiNO3, electrolytes with an excess concentration of LiNO3 were employed in the Li/S cell for
discharge and cyclic voltammetry tests. In the discharge test, a distortion of the discharge proﬁle near the
end of the discharge was clearly observed for the Li/S battery using a 0.8 M LiNO3 electrolyte. The third
plateau (distortion) representing the irreversible reaction was signiﬁcantly more pronounced when a
poor cathode that was not subjected to heat-treatment was used. In addition, cyclic voltammetry tests
showed an extra cathodic peak at 1.5 V corresponding to the irreversible reaction. It was conﬁrmed that
the irreversible product can be partially recovered to high-order polysulﬁdes and elemental S8 by
applying a potential of over 2.9 V. Finally, to provide a more detailed explanation, we carried out a
computational simulation of the irreversible reaction. The simulation indicated that the reaction was
related to the formation of crystallized lithium sulﬁde, and the simulation results successfully
reproduced the third plateau (distortion) in the discharge proﬁle.

Introduction
In recent decades, Li-ion batteries have revolutionized portable
electronic devices such as laptops, cell phones, etc., due to their
high energy and power density. However, their practical energy
density is still limited to 200 Wh/kg [1], which is insufﬁcient to
meet the current demands of the automotive industry and the
portable electronics market. For example, the driving range of the
Nissan Leaf, one of the most popular electric vehicles (EV), is
limited to 200 km despite employing a state-of-art Li-ion battery
[2]. Therefore, as the EV market continues to grow, the demand for
next-generation batteries that can surpass the performance of Li-
ion battery continues to increase. Among the myriad candidates,
Li/S batteries have received particular attention as alternative
energy storage devices, since the obtainable energy density of the
Li/S battery is three times that of commercial Li-ion batteries,
which would allow the driving range of EVs to be extended to
400 km.
Despite the high expectations for Li/S batteries, their practical
application is still hindered by many obstacles, such as their short-
term cycling ability, overcharge problem, and the loss of active
sulfur in the cathode. The problems are mainly attributed to the
soluble polysulﬁdes during the multistage reaction at the sulfur
cathode. While octa-sulfur (S8(s)) and Li2S(s) are mostly insoluble in

Mathematical model

Li/S battery. The general formulations of the ion transport, electron
transfer reaction, and charge conservation (see Appendix) are
based on our previous study [19]. In this study, an additional

drop due to Li2S(S) is modeled as an exponentially increasing

Numerical method
Inthiswork, aﬁnite volumemethodwasusedtoconstructalinear
algebraic equation for the numerical modeling of the Li/S battery.
Discretized algebraic equations were obtained using an upwind
scheme for both the mass conservation and charge conservation
equations since the diffusive term is dominant. The time-dependent
equations were solved by an implicit method for numerical stability.
The Thomas algorithm was used to solve the tri-diagonal system of
linear algebraic equations. The tolerances for the mass and charge
conservation equations were 105. The numerical simulations were
performed using an Intel Xeon 2.2 GHz CPU.
Experimental
The activated-carbon/sulfur-graphene oxide (AC-Sulfur) nano-
composite powder used as the active material for the sulfur
cathode in this study was synthesized following the method of
Song et al. [20]; the procedure is brieﬂy described here. The S-GO
nanocomposite was obtained from a mixture of sodium sulﬁde
nonahydrate (Na2S9H2O)/sulfur (1:3 mol ratio), activated carbon
(AC), and an aqueous solution of CTAB. Here, the acetone was
dispersed with intensive sonication. The aqueous solution was
added to a 2 M HCOOH solution and ﬁltered using a GHP
membrane. The resulting AC-Sulfur composite was heat-treated
at 155 C for 12 h in a tube furnace under an Ar atmosphere.
The sulfur electrodes were prepared from the S-GO nano-
composite by mixing the S-GO with carbon black (Super C65) and
polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) at a weight ratio of 70:20:10 in the
solvent N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The resulting slurry was
applied to pure aluminum foil using a doctor blade (MTI Micrometer
Adjustable Film Applicator) with sulfur loading of 1.5 mg/cm2 and
then dried in a vacuum oven at 50 C for 48 h. The electrode was cut
into circular pieces with diameters of 12.7 mm for cell assembly.
For the electrolyte, a 1 M solutions of lithium bis(triﬂuorome-
thylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich) in a mixture of the
organic solvents 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME)
(1:1 by volume) were prepared with various concentrations of
lithium nitrate (LiNO3) from 0.1 to 1.0 M to evaluate the effect of
LiNO3 on the irreversible reaction in the Li/S cell.
Li/S coin cells (CR2032-type) were assembled in an argon-ﬁlled
glove box using a lithium metal foil (99.98%, Cyprus Foote Mineral)
and the sulfur electrode as the anode and cathode, respectively.
Celgard-2340 was used as the separator. The discharge and charge
tests (constant-current load condition) of the coin cells were
carried out using a battery cycler (Biologics BCS-805) at a load
current density of 0.1C (1C = 1675 mA/g) between 1.5 and 2.8 V. The
cell capacity was normalized by the weight of sulfur. Cyclic
voltammetry (CV) tests were also conducted using a potentiostat
(Biologics SP-300) over a potential range from 1.2 to 2.8 V vs. Li+/Li
at a scan rate of 0.5 mV/s. All the electrochemical characterizations
were conducted at 25 C inside a temperature-controlled chamber.
Results and discussion
Discharge characteristics
In this section, the discharge proﬁles of the Li/S cell for different
concentrations of the additive LiNO3 are discussed. As described
above in the experimental section, the electrolytes were synthe-
sized using DOL/DME (1:1) as the solvent, the salt Li-TFSI, and the
additive LiNO3. In this study, the LiNO3 concentration was varied
from 0.0 to 1.0 M (Case-1: 0.0 M / Case-2: 0.2 M / Case-3: 0.5 M /
Case-4: 0.8 M / Case-5: 1.0 M). Fig. 3 shows that insoluble lithium
nitrate was present on the bottom of the vial for the 1.0 M case,
indicating that up to 0.8 M of LiNO3 could be dissolved in the
electrolyte. First, we investigated the Li/S cell for Cases 1–4. Fig. 4
shows the discharge and charge proﬁles for the different LiNO3
concentrations. In Case-1, a traditional discharge proﬁle with a ﬁrst
(2.3 V) and second (2.1 V) potential plateau was observed. A
speciﬁc capacity of 800 mA h/g speciﬁc capacity was obtained for
this case; the low capacity was attributed to sulfur loss and
shuttling problems. As Watanabe et al. reported, organic solvent-
based electrolytes such as DOL:DME show poor cell performance
without additives to suppress polysulﬁde shuttling. The charge
proﬁle demonstrates that the Li/S cell could not be fully charged,
and an inﬁnitely long charging time was observed (Fig. 2, black
dash-dot-dot line). However, in Case-2 with 0.2 M of LiNO3, the
speciﬁc capacity during the discharge operation improved from
800 to 900 mA h/g, and the cell was fully charged (Fig. 2, red dash-
dot-dot line). The concentration of LiNO3 in the electrolyte was
further increased to 0.5 M to (Case-3). The discharge-charge proﬁle
and speciﬁc capacity were similar to those of Case-2, but a small
distortion of the second plateau was observed due to the reactions
described in Eq. 2-d/e and 3-b/c. Furthermore, a clear distortion
was seen with 1.0 M LiNO3 (Case-4). In this experiment, the
distortion was signiﬁcantly greater, and a discharge potential of
1.9 V was detected at 85% depth of discharge (DOD). Therefore, we
attributed the distortion of the discharge proﬁle and the decreased
potential to the negative effect of LiNO3 on the Li/S cell.
From these observations, we postulated that the distortion of
the second plateau in the Li/S battery would be more clearly
apparent for a bulk sulfur-carbon cathode because the negative
effect of LiNO3 on the ﬁnal stage reaction of the reduction and
precipitation process would be more strongly induced due to the
poor activity of this cathode. For this purpose, we investigated the
discharge proﬁles for the bulk sulfur cathode with varying LiNO3
concentrations (0.2 M and 1.0 M). We prepared the two types of
cathodes that consisted of a sulfur-activated carbon mixture and
Super C-45 (conductive carbon black). The quality of the sulfur
cathode was determined by whether or not it was subjected to the
heat treatment process. In this experiment, the activated sulfur
was obtained from sodium sulfate and deposited on the activated
carbon. After the chemical process, the activated carbon was
impregnated with sulfur via the heat treatment method known as
the melt-diffusion process. The heat treatment process produced a
high-quality sulfur cathode. The poor cathode obtained without
the heat treatment process induced a high reaction resistivity as
well as sulfur loss.
Fig. 5 shows the voltage proﬁles of the Li/S cell with the two
types of cathode for varying concentrations of LiNO3 in the
electrolyte. To show the variance clearly, we used electrolytes in
which 0.2 M and 1.0 M LiNO3 were dissolved. No evident distortion
of the second plateau in the voltage proﬁle was observed with the
0.2 M LiNO3 electrolyte for either cathode. However, a lower initial
cell potential (2.27 V) was observed for the Li/S cell using the heat-
treated cathode. This was attributed to high concentration
polarization [19], since the nanostructure of the sulfur-impregnat-
ed carbon alleviates octa-sulfur dissolution in the electrolyte. On
the other hand, the cell potential proﬁles were signiﬁcantly
distorted in the Li/S cells when the 1.0 M LiNO3 electrolyte was
used. An unexpected potential drop was observed at 70% DOD.
Furthermore, a lower speciﬁc capacity was obtained with the 1.0 M
LiNO3 electrolyte for the untreated cathode. Hence, we concluded
that LiNO3 negatively affected the battery discharge by interfering

concentration of LiNO3 in the electrolyte, a cyclic voltammetry

anode protective layer [21], it was postulated that the retardance
of the reduction reaction due to LiNO3 would be observed more
clearly if the aging process were omitted. Hence, two Li/S coin

peak was observed clearly for the non-aged Li/S cell at 1.5 V

Conclusions
In this study, the effects of LiNO3 on Li/S batteries were
investigated. We postulated that excessive LiNO3 would induce
the irreversible reaction at cathode; to test this, the electrical
performance of Li/S batteries employing electrolytes containing
0.1–0.8 M 
of 
LiNO3
were 
investigated. 
First, 
an 
additional
discharge plateau near the end of discharge was clearly observed
only for the Li/S battery with a high concentration LiNO3
electrolyte. Furthermore, a similar test was carried out using
an inferior cathode to address the irreversible reaction in the
electrode. In the test, two types of sulfur cathode (with/without
heat treatment) were prepared for the Li/S battery, and we
observed a clear discharge distortion in the discharge test for Li/S
battery employing the inferior sulfur cathode. Additionally, cyclic
voltammetry data indicated that the irreversible reaction resulted
from excessive LiNO3. In this test, two types of Li/S batteries
(aged/non-aged) 
were 
prepared 
for 
comparison. 
Based 
on
previous reports indicating the LiNO3 contributes to the forma-
tion of an anode protective layer (APL) and is consumed during
the reaction, it was expected that the non-aged Li/S battery would
clearly show the irreversible reaction. Indeed, we observed an
additional current peak in the CV charge for the non-aged Li/S
battery. Finally, a multi-step reaction for lithium polysulﬁde
crystallization related to the irreversible reaction was proposed,
and a numerical simulation was carried out. In the simulation, we
obtained a similar distortion proﬁle for an Li/S battery with
excessive LiNO3 dissolved in the electrolyte. Thus, based on both
the numerical and experimental investigations, the irreversible
reaction related to the formation of lithium polysulﬁde at the
cathode side was attributed to the dissolution of an excessive
amount of LiNO3 in the electrolyte.

Boundary condition
At the anode-electrolyte interface, lithium ions are supplied by
the oxidation reaction, and the ﬂux can be formulated from the
load current (NLiþ ¼ iload=F), while zero ﬂux (Ni ¼ 0) is applied to
the cathode-current corrector interface. Similar, the magnitude of
the current corresponds to the load current at the anode-
electrolyte interface (lr’l ¼ iload) while it is zero (r’l ¼ 0) at
the cathode-current corrector interface. On the other hand, the
electrical potential of the electrode is deﬁned only in the solid
electrode, so that the zero current (r’s ¼ 0) is deﬁned at the
separator-cathode side with the load current (sr’s ¼ iload) at the
cathode-current corrector interface.
