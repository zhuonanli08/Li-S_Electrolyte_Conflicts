ABSTRACT: The liquid electrolyte in a lithium−sulfur battery
is important for the dissolution−deposition reaction through
the solubility of polysulﬁdes. To get insight into the sulfur
chemistry, fundamental understanding of the dissolved poly-
sulﬁdes should be explored. In this work, the dissolved forms of
Li2S6, as prototypical polysulﬁdes, in 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) were studied by ab initio calculations. The calculated
ﬁrst-order and second-order dissociation constants are 3.52 and 17.61, respectively, for Li2S6 dissociated into LiS6
−and S6
2−.
Simultaneously, Li2S6 can also dissociate into LiS3
• radicals with dissociation constant of 7.80. The experimental ultraviolt−
visible spectrum of stoichiometric 2 mM Li2S6 in DME was interpreted by the individual contributions of various polysulﬁde
species using time-dependent density functional theory calculations. The concentrations of Li2S6 and the dissociated LiS6
−,
S6
2−, and LiS3
• were further calculated for diﬀerent electrolyte-to-sulfur ratios. It is found that neutral Li2S6 is dominant in
DME, while polysulﬁde anions and radicals are rare.
L
i−S batteries are one of the most promising candidates
for rechargeable energy storage devices, because of a
high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh·kg−1 due to
to the multiple electron transfer of the cathode active material
cyclooctasulfur (S8).1,2 The total reaction in a Li−S battery is
S8 + 16Li+ + 16e = 8Li2S, with a total of 16 electron transfers.3
During discharge, S8 is not directly reduced to Li2S but
undergoes a multistep reduction process, accompanied by the
generation of soluble intermediate lithium polysulﬁde species
(Li2Sn, n = 3−8) toward insoluble Li2S2 and Li2S.4,5 Therefore,
it is the dissolution−deposition chemistry of sulfur that
smoothly promotes the electrode process.6,7 Meanwhile, the
soluble polysulﬁde species, on the other hand, can diﬀuse to
the lithium anode through the electrolyte and react with
lithium.2 Such an undesired “shuttle eﬀect” leads to the loss of
the sulfur active material and thus causes the fast capacity
decay of the cathode as well as serious corrosion and cracking
of the lithium anode upon cycling.8,9 Consequently, it is
diﬃcult to take advantage of the high theoretical energy
density of the Li−S battery.
To suppress the shuttle eﬀect, many eﬀorts have been
employed mainly with the focus on various host materials,
including nonpolar carbon nanomaterials10−13 and polar
compounds.14−18 Beneﬁting from the physical conﬁnement
eﬀect of the nonpolar carbon nanomaterials and chemical
aﬃnity/catalytic eﬀect of the polar compounds, the electro-
chemical performance of the sulfur cathode can be improved.
Besides, the electrolyte can be optimized by designing solvents
and regulating support salts.19 In particular, ethers are
considered as suitable solvents in a Li−S battery, including
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL),
because of the compatibility with polysulﬁdes.20 Generally,
binary mixtures of electrolytes are usually utilized to obtain
good electrochemical performance, e.g., mixture of DME and
DOL by 1:1 volume, which has been widely adopted as an
electrolyte in Li−S batteries to achieve low viscosity and high
ionic conductivity.6 However, the shuttle eﬀect still exists
because of the high solubility of Li2Sn, resulting from the Lewis
basicity from the oxygen lone pair electrons of ethers and
Lewis acidity from the Li atoms of Li2Sn.21 It is believed that a
high solubility of the polysulﬁde species is welcome in a Li−S
battery in order to increase the utilization of sulfur active
materials for a high-loading sulfur cathode,22,23 as long as the
shuttle eﬀect can be depressed. Therefore, the understanding
of the dissolved forms of the polysulﬁde species in the
electrolyte should be investigated in detail, including the
valence, concentration, and size dimension. This is of

Experimental studies have been conducted to reveal the
dissolved forms of Li2Sn in electrolytes, such as Raman
spectroscopy,24 Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectros-
copy,25 X-ray diﬀraction (XRD),26 ultraviolet−visible (UV−
vis) spectroscopy,27 nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR),28
and X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES).29 Indeed,
in situ experiments can identify the time-dependent products of
polysulﬁde species, but it is still diﬃcult to verify the valence of
the dissolved polysulﬁdes (e.g., neutral molecules Li2Sn,
monoanions LiSn
−, or dianions Sn
2−) and the stoichiometric
species.30 Given the assumption that the dissolved polysulﬁde
species are the neutral molecules, various host materials are
induced to adsorb neutral Li2Sn via lithiophilic31 and sulﬁphilic
hosts32 or the synergistic eﬀect of both.33 On the contrary,
another viewpoint is that the dissolved polysulﬁde species are
mainly LiSn
−monoanions or Sn
2−dianions.24,30,34,35 Thus,
highly concentrated lithium salts are used to suppress the
dissolution of polysulﬁde species via the co-ion eﬀect.19,35−37
With the same assumption, the dissociated Li ions (Li+) and
polysulﬁde anions, together with primary supporting salt (e.g.,
LiTFSI), are regarded as the dominant ionic species that
contribute the total ionic conductivity.37,38 Nevertheless, the
dissolved polysulﬁde anions may cause a more severe shuttle
eﬀect than the neutral Li2Sn that is driven only by the
concentration gradient, owing to additional electric migra-
tion.38 In order to suppress the shuttle of the dissolved
polysulﬁde anions, a permselective membrane with negatively
charged functional group was adopted on the cathode side by
Coulombic repulsion.39,40 It is worth mentioning that radicals,
in particular LiS3
• and S3
• −,21,29,41 have been also widely
studied as chemical mediators41 or electrochemical intermedi-
ates.42 They are generally considered to be the products from
the entropy-driven homolysis of Li2Sn or Sn
2−under chemical
equilibria, which depend on the forms of polysulﬁdes in the
electrolyte.21
Computational methods are a powerful tool to study the
structure, stability, and reactivity of Li2Sn in diﬀerent
solvents.43 In terms of the valence of the polysulﬁde species,
Chen et al.44 found that neutral Li2Sn are energetically more
favorable than their charged counterparts. Similarly, in ab initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations and free energy
calculations, Kamphaus and Balbuena45 found that the
dissociation of Li2Sn is not favored in the DME solvent.
Thus, the shuttle eﬀect may be caused by the neutral species in
a Li−S battery, with the driving force merely from the
concentration gradient of the dissolved neutral Li2Sn, instead of
the charged LiSn
−or Sn
2−that also migrates via the electric
ﬁeld. However, this was challenged by Steudel and Chivers,21
because the cell dimensions in the AIMD simulations may be
too small to allow the solvation of the cations and anions, if
they do exist. Besides, Andersen et al.46 found that partial
dissociation from Li2Sn is plausible, which results in the
dynamic aggregation and shuttle processes of polysulﬁde
species in electrolytes. Experimentally, the dissolved poly-
sulﬁde species have been intensely studied via redox titration,
and the average oxidation of individual sulfur atoms can be
measured.47 However, the speciﬁc valence states of individual
polysulﬁde species with accurate dissociations have not been
unambiguously determined.
Our endeavor of this study is to elucidate the dissociation of
Li2S6 in DME solvent by ab initio calculations and UV−vis
spectra. The reasons for choosing Li2S6 as prototypical
polysulﬁde species are (1) Li2S6 has been widely studied in
terms of the solubility,47 transport properties,37 and adsorption
characteristic on host materials48 in the electrolyte of Li−S
batteries; (2) experimental preparation of Li2S6 with acceptable
toleration for the ratio of sulfur to lithium;49 (3) Li2S6 is
believed to be a representative intermediate species with higher
solubility during cycling at high-voltage plateau in a Li−S
battery;4 and (4) there is a debates whether the dissolved form
of Li2S6 in the ether-based electrolyte is the molecule, anion, or
radical.30 The solvation Gibbs free energies (ΔG*) of Li2S6,
Li+, LiS6
−, S6
2−, and LiS3
• were ﬁrst calculated by ab initio
calculations using density functional theory (DFT) at the
M06-2X/6-31+G(d) level of theory,50 with a cluster−
continuum solvation method.51 Then, the dissociation
constants (pKd1, pKd2, and pKdr) were calculated by the
Gibbs free energies of thermodynamic cycles. We found that
the neutral Li2S6 molecules are the dominant species in DME,
with a small amount of LiS6
−anions because of the moderate
pKd1, while S6
2−anions and LiS3
• radicals hardly exist because
of the very high pKd2 and pKdr. These results were compared
with the experimental UV−vis spectrum of stoichiometric 2
mM Li2S6 DME solution, with the contributions of the
individual dissolved species calculated by a time-dependent
density functional theory (TD-DFT) method.52,53 Considering
the co-ion eﬀect of Li salt in the electrolyte of a Li−S battery,
the concentration of Li2S6 and dissociated LiS6
−, S6
2−, and
LiS3
• at diﬀerent electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratios were also
calculated. We hope this study contributes an in-depth
understanding of the dissolution−deposition chemistry and
shuttle eﬀect in Li−S batteries.

Experimental and Computational UV−Vis Spectrum of Li2S6 in
DME. The experimental UV−vis spectrum of the prepared
stoichiometric 2 mM Li2S6 in DME is shown in Figure 2a. The
reason for choosing 2 mM concentration is that it is generally
adopted in the UV−vis measurements,23,69 because the
concentrations of the dissociated species vary with stoichio-

As a result, the concentration of the dissociated species is very
small. In particular, [LiS6
−] and [S6
2−] can be almost ignored if
further taking into account the co-ion eﬀect of concentrated
Li+ introduced by the supporting electrolyte. Besides, even if
the dissociation reaction of Li2S6 into LiS3

the co-ions eﬀect, the negligible [LiS3
•] suggests that LiS3
• is
also hardly produced in DME electrolyte because of the
relatively high pKdr = 7.80 in Table 2. Therefore, the dominant
species in the DME electrolyte is the neutral Li2S6 molecules,
rather than LiS6
−or S6
2−anions. In other words, the neutral
molecules mainly participate in the redox conversion in the
electrolyte and also lead to the diﬀusion and shuttle of active
material merely by concentration gradient.37 In order to
suppress the diﬀusion and shuttle of the neutral Li2S6
molecules, two strategies can be adopted: (1) captured by
sulfur host materials and (2) hindered by separators. It is noted
that the dissolution of the neutral Li2S6 is unavoidable but
necessary because of the fast liquid−liquid conversion and
mass transport in the solution phase under a ﬂooded
electrolyte. However, employing a low E/S ratio below 5 μL·
mg−1 (to be much lower considering the porosity of cathode
and separator, and the gap between them) is usually advocated
to achieve the high gravimetric and volumetric energy density
demand of Li−S batteries,78 but it greatly constrains the
dissolution of neutral Li2S6 molecules and thus limits the sulfur
utilization, redox kinetics, and dissolution−deposition chem-
istry of Li−S batteries. Besides, a low E/S ratio also leads to
high electrolyte viscosity because the dissolution of the neutral
Li2S6 molecules produces large Li2S6(DME)2 clusters, which
increase the solution resistance and restrict the mass transport.
Although the dissolution of the neutral Li2S6 molecules is
essential in the solution-mediated conversion,80 the resulting
shuttle eﬀect needs to be inhibited. Because the main dissolved
species is the neutral Li2S6 molecules in a typical electrolyte of
supporting [Li+] = 1.0 M in a Li−S battery as shown in Figure
3, the practical strategy to depress the shuttle eﬀect is to
conﬁne the lithium polysulﬁde in the sulfur host materials and/
or to prevent it from passing freely across the separator.
Because of the insulating nature of lithium polysulﬁde, it seems
to be diﬃcult to achieve high loading of active sulfur materials
through the former strategy. In that sense, a separator of well-
designed pore size may be a good choice.81 Here, the volumes
and diameters of the polysulﬁdes investigated in this study
were calculated by the Monte Carlo method with a 0.001 e·
Bohr−3 density envelope using Gaussian 16,60 with the number
of points per Bohr3 set to 2000. The calculated volumes of
Li2S6(I), Li2S6(II), LiS6
−, S6
2−, and LiS3
• are 195.43, 198.39,
203.40, 212.79, and 107.17 Å3, respectively, with the diameters
of 7.20, 7.24, 7.30, 7.41, and 5.89 Å by assuming spherical
species. The larger sizes of the anions are attributed to the
higher electron diﬀusion. Considering the dominant compo-
nent of Li2S6 molecules, it is desirable to have a separator of
pore size less than 7.20 Å in order to eﬀectively inhibit the
shuttle eﬀect of Li2S6.
In summary, we employed ab initio methods to study the
ﬁrst-order and second-order dissociations of Li2S6 in DME to
form LiS6
−and S6
2−anions, and the homolytic dissociation of
Li2S6 in DME to form LiS3
• radials, according to the
thermodynamic cycles. The results are pKd1 = 3.52, pKd2 =
17.61, and pKdr = 7.80 for the ﬁrst-order, second-order, and
homolytic dissociations, respectively. The pKdi (i = 1, 2, r) was
adopted to estimate the contents of the individual dissolved
polysulﬁde species in a stoichiometric 2 mM Li2S6 DME
solution. The weighted sum of the calculated UV−vis
spectrum by TD-DFT calculation shows reasonable agreement
with the experimental measurement. Subsequently, the
contents of the individual dissolved polysulﬁde species were
further calculated at various E/S ratios in the presence of 1 M
Li salt supporting electrolyte, which is often adopted in Li−S
batteries. The result shows that the main dissolved species is
the neutral Li2S6 molecules of order 1 M under lean electrolyte
of low E/S ratio, with a tiny amount of LiS6
−anions and LiS3
•
radicals of order 10−4−10−5 M, while the content of the
dissolved S6
2−anions can be completely ignored in the ether-
based electrolyte. Given the current study, the shuttle eﬀect is
associated with the concentration gradient of the dissolved
neutral lithium polysulﬁde. We hope this study can provide in-
depth knowledge on the polysulﬁde species in the electrolyte,
which is highly important for designing high energy density
Li−S batteries, including selection of electrolyte solvent/
lithium salts, controlling of separator pore structure, and
optimization of catalytic/polar sulfur host materials. Further
studies on chemical equilibrium of polysulﬁde species in
diﬀerent solvents are in progress.
