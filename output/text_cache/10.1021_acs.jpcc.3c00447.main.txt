ABSTRACT: Practical use of lithium−sulfur batteries can be realized by using “sparingly
solvating electrolytes” such as [Li(G4)][TFSA] (G4, tetraglyme; TFSA, bis-
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)amide) solvate ionic liquid, superconcentrated electrolyte solutions,
and their hydrofluoroether-diluted electrolytes. On the other hand, the battery performance
such as C-rate characteristics of lithium−sulfur batteries is different depending on the
electrolyte used. In order to investigate the relationship between the discharge reaction and the
battery performance for lithium−sulfur batteries with 1,1,2,2-tertafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-
tetrafluoropropyl ether-diluted [Li(G4)][TFSA], in situ electrochemical impedance spectros-
copy has been conducted. During discharge at a high C-rate, a voltage drop was observed in the
discharge curve at DOD = 20−35%. The charge transfer resistance of the lithium negative
electrode remarkably increased when the voltage drop occurred. Moreover, this increase was
only observed for in situ measurements. The charge transfer resistance of the negative electrode
is related to the resistance of the Li+ ion dissolution/deposition reaction. During discharging at
a high C-rate, the Li+ ion is abundant at the negative electrode interface owing to the changes in the Li+ ion solvation structure,
suppressing the Li+ ion dissolution reaction.
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LiSB) are promoting as one of the
next-generation batteries using elemental sulfur (S8) as a
positive electrode active material, which is easily available and
inexpensive. Elemental sulfur has a large theoretical capacity of
1672 mAh g−1. However, there are some issues toward its
realization; obtaining the theoretical capacity is difficult as
lithium sulfide (Li2S) which is a reduced species and S8
exhibitlow electric conductivities. In addition, the inter-
mediate lithium polysulfide (Li2Sm: 2 ≤m ≤8), which forms
during the discharge reaction, dissolves into the conventional
ether-based electrolyte solution, causing a shuttle phenomen-
on.1 Ji et al.2 reported that the discharge capacity was
improved by using a sulfur−mesoporous carbon composite
electrode. To suppress the shuttle phenomenon, transition
metal compounds (oxide, sulfide, nitride, phosphide, carbide,
and MXene) that adsorb Li2Sm have been added to the positive
electrode.3−5 For the electrolytes, lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is
used as an electrolyte additive. A passivation layer such as
LixNOy and LixSOy is formed on the lithium negative electrode
surface, preventing the polysulfide shuttling. However, the
passivation layer is destroyed by repeated charging and
discharging.6,7 Solid electrolytes consisting of polymer or
inorganic electrolytes have been proposed to physically
suppress the polysulfide dissolution,8−12 whereas it is difficult
to control the electrolyte/electrode interface where the
electrochemical reaction occurs.
Aprotic ionic liquids (APILs), which are composed of a
weak Lewis acid and a weak Lewis base, have been reported as
a polysulfide-insoluble electrolyte.13,14 However, APILs have
the disadvantage that the Li+ diffusion rate is slow.15 A Li−
glyme-based solvate ionic liquid (Li-Gn SIL, n = 3 or 4), which
consists of a solvated ion and its counter ion, exhibits a specific
Li+ transport mechanism.16 Ueno et al.17,18 found that lithium
polysulfide did not dissolve in Li-Gn SIL and applied the Li-Gn
SIL to LiSBs. Furthermore, adding hydrofluoroether reduced
the electrolyte viscosity, improving the battery performance.18
Recently, superconcentrated electrolyte solutions (SCESs) are
being reported to exhibit several advantages in battery
performance.19−21 In the SCESs, the solvent solvating to Li+
is insufficient, leading to suppression of lithium polysulfide
dissolution.22−24 So far, sulfone (SL)-based24 and acetonitrile-
based23 SCESs have been applied to LiSBs. The SILs and
SCESs are known as “sparingly solvating electrolytes”.25
Various battery materials have been proposed for LiSBs; on
the other hand, the discharge behavior strongly depends on the
battery materials. Both the Li-Gn SIL and SL-based SCESs are

reduction pathway of S8 may be different depending on the
electrolytes. Most of these studies are on the positive
electrode; however, the reactions of both positive and negative
electrodes affect the battery performance. Therefore, informa-
tion about both positive and negative electrodes is required.
The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) meas-
urement is a method that provides the knowledge of electrode
reactions because the electrode reactions can be separated by
the characteristic time constant. There are a lot of reports
using EIS to analyze the charge−discharge behavior of the
LiSB.30−34 Waluś et al.35 reported that the solution resistance
reached the maximum between the first and second plateaus,
where the concentration of Li2Sm was maximum. Drvarič
Talian et al.36 found that the battery performance of the LiSB
was restricted at the end of the discharge owing to the decrease
of the Li2Sm concentration in the electrode. However, in the
aforementioned research, impedance includes information
about both the charging and discharging because EIS
measurements are carried out in an equilibrium state of the
battery.37 Thus, an impedance method to evaluate the
operating battery is necessary to reveal the discharge behavior
of the LiSB. We reported that in situ EIS could acquire
charge−discharge curves and impedance simultaneously, for
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).38−40 In the in situ EIS, the
impedance spectra during charging or discharging can be
obtained because a small AC current signal is superimposed on
the charge−discharge current.37 Moreover, the instantaneous
impedance can be measured at a specific SOC by using an
algorithm developed by Stoynov41 that compensates for the
changes occurring over time.
In the present work, we applied in situ EIS to the LiSB with
the Li-G4 SIL to reveal the relationship between the discharge
reaction and battery performance such as C-rate character-
istics. Subsequently, an in situ multi-EIS measurement with a
three-electrode cell was carried out to identify the electrode
that affected the rate performance. In addition, we compared
the impedance spectra during discharging with those in an
equilibrium state. Finally, we propose the electrode/electrolyte
interface reaction within the LiSB using the Li-G4 SIL
electrolyte during the discharge.

Cell Preparation and Electrochemical Measurements.
The CR2032 coin cell and the three-electrode cell were used
for the EIS measurements. A lithium metal foil (16 mm

electrode, the negative electrode, a glass separator (GA-55,

metal foil was used as a reference electrode. The ratio of the
electrolyte volume to sulfur weight is defined as E/S [μL/mg].
The electrolyte volumes in the coin cell and the three-electrode

In situ EIS measurements were conducted between 1.0 and
3.3 V at different C-rates under constant-current charging/
discharging conditions at 298 K using an electrochemical
measurement system (SP-150, Bio-Logic, France). The DC
current value of the first charge−discharge cycle was 168 μA
(C-rate of 0.05C), and the current values after the second cycle
were 336 μA (C-rate of 0.1C) and 84 μA (C-rate of 0.025C).
In the present work, the C-rates of 0.1C and 0.025C are
defined as the high C-rate and low C-rate, respectively. The
initial discharge−charge tests at 0.05C were conducted to
stabilize the cell. Figure S1 shows the initial charge−discharge
profiles. Although the charge−discharge curves were different
between the two cells due to film formation and cell
stabilization, the effective capacities are almost the same.
Hence, the effect of the C-rate can be compared using these
cells. The instantaneous impedance spectra were determined
using the second cycle discharge data. A small AC current was
superimposed on DC current adjusted so that the response
voltage amplitude did not exceed 10 mV. The measurements
were conducted in the frequency range from 500 kHz to 10
mHz, and the number of integrations was one. The measured
impedance spectra during discharging/charging include the
contribution of changes over time. Accordingly, the instanta-
neous impedance spectra at an arbitrary time were determined
using the method to compensate for the changes over
time.38,41
The polarization and EIS measurements for the lithium

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 1 shows the charge−discharge profiles of the LiSB with
Li-G4 SIL electrolyte during in situ EIS measurements. As
shown in Figure 1, the charge−discharge curves did not exhibit
any distortion due to superposition of the AC signal. A typical
discharge profile of the lithium−sulfur battery has two
discharge plateaus at 2.3 and 2.1 V, which represent the
conversions of S8 to Li2S4 and Li2S4 to Li2S, respectively. In
addition, at the end of discharge, Li2S is formed as the voltage
drops. The discharge curves exhibit two voltage regions at
approximately 2.3 V (first plateau voltage) and 2.0 V (second
plateau voltage), which is consistent with the previous
studies.18 In the discharge curves measured at the high C-
rate, the voltage drops are observed at the capacity range from
150 to 200 mAh g−1 (corresponding to DOD = 20−35%);
subsequently, the voltage slightly increases and becomes
constant. By contrast, the curves measured at the low C-rate
have no voltage drop and the voltage is constant in the capacity
range from 150 to 200 mAh g−1. Although the voltage change
should be small at the plateau voltage region, a large voltage
change is observed in the second plateau region (correspond-
ing to the DOD of 20−35%) at high-C-rate discharge. This
voltage drop is often observed in the discharge profiles of the
LiSBs with the Li-Gn SIL electrolyte42 but not in the discharge
profiles of the LiSBs with the SL-based SCES, the same
polysulfide-insoluble electrolytes.
Figure 2a−d shows the second discharge curve measured at
a high C-rate and the corresponding instantaneous impedance
spectra at different DODs. Two capacitive semicircles and a
straight line are observed for all of the spectra. A semicircle is
represented by an RC parallel circuit. For the electrode/
electrolyte interface, R and C are the charge transfer resistance
and electric double-layer capacitance, respectively. RC is the
time constant (relaxation time) of the electrode reaction
process and can be expressed as RC = 1/(2πf max), where f max is
the peak frequency of the semicircle. Hence, the LiSB with the
Li-G4 SIL electrolyte has at least two time constants. The
semicircle in the high-frequency range (>15 Hz) is asym-
metrical, as if two capacitive semicircles are overlapped. In the

In many cases, the impedance of a battery is measured in an
equilibrium state. The charge−discharge test is stopped at an
arbitrary DOD (or SOC: state of charge), and the impedance
is subsequently recorded after waiting for relaxation to an
equilibrium state. In the present study, this method is defined
as ex situ EIS. The LiSB with the Li-G4 SIL electrolyte was
discharged at a C-rate of 0.1C (corresponding to 336 μA) for
30 min, and the impedance was measured after waiting for 3 h
of relaxation to the equilibrium state. Figure 6a shows the cell
voltage and the potentials of the positive and negative
electrodes during ex situ EIS as a function of the DOD. The
initial discharge capacity of the cell was 1338 mAh g−1, and the
DODs were determined by eq 1. As shown in Figure 6a, the
cell voltage is lower than the positive electrode potential, and
the negative electrode potential increases at DOD = 10−30%.
In addition, the difference between the cell voltage and the
positive electrode potential becomes smaller at DOD ≥50%.
This suggests that the characteristic discharge profile at a high
C-rate, which is the voltage drop followed by a slight voltage
increase, is also observed during ex situ EIS. Figure 6b−d
shows the impedance spectra of the entire cell and the positive
and negative electrodes, respectively. For the impedance
spectra of the entire cell and negative electrode, the diameter
of the capacitive semicircle with the peak frequency at 457 Hz
increases at DOD = 25%, being consistent with the impedance
behavior obtained via in situ EIS. However, the sizes of the
capacitive semicircle with the peak frequency at 666 mHz
related to the positive electrode increase in the impedance
spectra of the entire cell and positive electrode. This behavior
is not observed during discharging. The impedance data
corresponding to the equilibrium state include information
related to both the discharging and charging processes. By
contrast, in situ impedance data acquired during discharge
includes only information related to the discharging process.
When the impedance corresponding to the equilibrium state is
different form that corresponding to the polarized state, ex situ
EIS may not evaluate the batteries accurately. Therefore, ex situ
EIS measurements cannot reveal the cause of the voltage drop
observed during discharging at a high C-rate.

diffusion rate of G4 near the negative electrode, depletion of
free G4 to coordinate the Li+ ion occurs at the electrode
interface. A contact ion pair (CIP) and an aggregate ion pair
(AGG) are formed when free G4 is depleted due to electrode

the Li+ ion dissolution rate increases with increasing C-rate, the
formation of CIPs and AGGs may occur in the negative
electrode/electrolyte interface. As a result, G4 to coordinate
the Li+ ion is insufficient and the Li+ ion dissolution is
suppressed, increasing the charge transfer resistance of the
negative electrode. For the positive electrode interface, with
increasing C-rate, the concentration gradient becomes larger
and the concentration of Li+ ions on the interface decreases. In
addition, the supply of Li+ ions becomes insufficient due to the
suppression of dissolution at the negative electrode. Thus, the
positive electrode reaction may become a diffusion-controlled
reaction, resulting in the voltage drop.
■CONCLUSIONS
In situ EIS was applied to a LiSB with the Li-G4 SIL electrolyte
to reveal the relationship between the discharge reaction and
rate performance. During discharging at a high C-rate, the
voltage dropped at the capacity range from 150 to 200 mAh
g−1 (corresponding to DOD = 20−35%) and then increased
slightly. This behavior was not observed during discharging at
a low C-rate. The capacitive semicircle with the peak frequency
at 457 Hz appeared in the instantaneous impedance spectra of
the LiSB during discharging. The diameter of this semicircle
increased as the voltage dropped and decreased with increasing
voltage. According to multi-EIS and linear sweep voltammetry
experiments, the semicircle with the peak frequency at 457 Hz
was assigned to the time constant related to the charge transfer
resistance and the double-layer capacitance of the negative
electrode. Curve fitting was applied to the instantaneous
impedance spectra of the LiSB with the Li-G4 SIL during
discharging. When the voltage dropped at high-C-rate
discharging, the charge transfer resistance of the negative
electrode Rct,N and the constant related to the diffusion
condition σ of the positive electrode remarkably increased. The
charge transfer reaction on the negative electrode corresponds
to the Li+ ion dissolution/deposition reaction. The change in
the liquid structure from the SSIP to the CIP or AGG at the
negative electrode/electrolyte interface may suppress the Li+
ion dissolution reaction, resulting in insufficient supply of Li+
ions at the positive electrode. This interface change causes the
increases in the Rct,N and σ, resulting in the voltage drop.
