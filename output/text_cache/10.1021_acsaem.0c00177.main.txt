ABSTRACT: Due to the strong electron-withdrawing ﬂuorine groups, the aromatic ring
of hexaﬂuorobenzene (HFB) becomes more electron-deﬁcient and strongly interacts with
the lone-pair electrons of the medium. When used as an electrolyte additive for Li−S cell,
HFB preferably coordinates with low-order lithium sulﬁdes Li2S and Li2S2 and
subsequently reacts and converts both into active aromatic sulﬁdesbis-
(pentaﬂuorophenyl) disulﬁdes and bis(pentaﬂuorophenyl) polysulﬁdes in situthus
facilitating the utilization of the electronically insulating Li2S and Li2S2 and improving the
cycling stability of the Li−S cell. This research opens a new avenue to tackle the inherent
issues associated with the Li−S chemistry.
KEYWORDS: hexaﬂuorobenzene additive, activation of low-order polysulﬁdes, DFT simulations, improved capacity retention,
lithium sulfur battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Li-ion battery (LIB) has been one of the most competitive
energy storage systems due to its high theoretical energy
density and long calendar life.1 Nevertheless, the practical
energy density of most commercial LIBs is around 200−300
Wh/kg,2 which has already been a limitation when applying
them in pure electric vehicles. Therefore, to achieve the
requirement of higher energy density, lithium sulfur (Li−S)
battery is considered to be a promising chemistry to replace
the traditional LIB as next-generation energy storage3,4 due to
its high theoretical capacity (1675 mAh/g), which is an order
of magnitude higher than that of the traditional insertion-type
oxide cathodes (e.g., LiCoO2, LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2, and LiFe-
PO4),3 and the fact that sulfur is currently the cheapest solid
state cathode material.
However, there are several technical issues associated with
Li−S chemistry such as low sulfur utilization, redox shuttling
eﬀect, severe self-discharge, poor cycle life, and low speciﬁc
energy due to the excess amount of electrolyte. Under normal
conditions, sulfur atoms form cyclic octatomic crowns with a
chemical formula S8. During the discharge (lithiation), sulfur
accepts electrons and is reduced stepwise to a series of high-
order polysulﬁde intermediates (Li2Sx, 3 < x < 8) and ﬁnal
reduction product Li2S.5 The former, which are soluble in the
ether-based electrolyte, diﬀuse to the lithium anode side,
accept the electron, and get reduced to lower-order
polysulﬁdes Li2Sx (2 < x < 3) (staying dissolved in the
electrolyte) and Li2S which nucleates and precipitates from the
electrolyte. The lower-order polysulﬁdes diﬀuse back to the
cathode and are reoxidized to high-order ones. This process
continues and causes a severe shuttling issue6 with a
consequence of extremely low Coulombic eﬃciency and
rapid capacity decay.7 In addition, excess electrolyte has to
be used in order to enable the complete reduction of S8 to
Li2S, which compromises the energy density of the Li−S cell.
Tremendous research eﬀorts are focused on constraining the
dissolution of the high-order polysulﬁdes by new material
design using mesoporous carbon,8 strong absorption poly-
mers,9 carbon interlayers,10 and metal oxide hosts.11
LiNO3 is an indispensable electrolyte component for Li−S
cells. It could eﬀectively eliminate the shuttling eﬀect via
passivating the lithium anode and improve the Coulombic
eﬃciency.12−14 However, the capacity of the Li−S cell still
fades rapidly despite nearly 100% Coulombic eﬃciency.15,16
Xiong et al. introduced an additive lithium bis(oxalato)borate
(LiBOB), suggesting a lithium surface ﬁlm rich in organic
components rather than the inorganic NOx species17 would be

observed, but with increasing cycling, the cells without
additives showed the same performance as those with LiBOB
additive. Phosphorus pentasulﬁde, biphenyl-4,4-dithiol, and

Lithium polysulﬁdes themselves were also used as additives to
improve the compatibility of the electrolyte with the lithium
metal anode.19 Recently, a number of works20−24 reported that
organic disulﬁdes such as dimethyl disulﬁde (DMDS) could
enhance cell capacity and capacity retention due to the
electrochemically active nature of the S−S bond. However, the
capacity of the Li−S cell cannot be accurately determined since
the S−S bond of the disulﬁdes contributes to the overall cell
capacity. This leads to an artiﬁcially inﬂated speciﬁc capacity of
the cell. So far, no additives are reported to tackle the issues
associated with the crystallization/precipitation of Li2S/Li2S2,
thus preventing the active material loss which is believed to be
the key to the stable cyclability of Li−S cells.
In this work, we report for the ﬁrst time such an additive,
hexaﬂuorobenzene (HFB) (Figure 1a), to tackle the above
issue. The designed idea is based on the fact that HFB has an
electron-deﬁcient (Lewis acidic) core due to the six electron-
withdrawing ﬂuorine groups which strongly interacts with
polysulﬁdes Sx
2−(Lewis basic), especially the stronger Lewis
base Li2S/Li2S2 forming a new complex which could be
recharged back to the system, thus enabling the reutilization of
isolated active materials. Our experimental results support our
initial hypothesis that HFB could curtail the loss of the active
material and improve the performance of the Li−S battery.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations shed light on the
energetics of the HFB-Sx
2−coordination and possible reaction
pathways.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Materials. 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL), 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), hexaﬂuorobenzene (HFB, 99%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3,
99.99%), lithium sulﬁde (Li2S, 99.9%), and lithium bis-
(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%) were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich. Elemental sulfur (Alfa Aesar), pentaﬂuorothio-
phenol (Oakwood Chemical), and anhydrous diethyl ether (HPLC
grade, Fischer Scientiﬁc, 99.9%) were purchased from their respective
suppliers, respectively. HFB was distilled under reduced pressure
before drying. Water content was measured by Karl Fischer titration
method and controlled <20 ppm for all electrolyte solvents and
additives. The baseline electrolyte is 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (1:1
volume ratio) + 0.1 M LiNO3. A ﬂuorinated ether-based electrolyte,
−1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/TTE (1:1 volume ratio)containing 1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (TTE, Synquest Labo-
ratories) as cosolvent, was used to verify the impact of HFB additive.
2.2. Li−S Cell Testing. 2032-coin cells were assembled in an
argon-ﬁlled glovebox with O2 level < 5 ppm. Lithium chips were used
as the anode (15.6 mm in diameter, MTI), Celgard 2325 (16.0 mm in
diameter) as separator with various electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratios.
Sulfur loading is 1.4−1.8 mg/cm2. After cell assembly, the cells were
transferred to a battery cycler (Maccor Test Unit 4100) and cycled at
a C/10 current (1C = 1675 mA/mg) with a cutoﬀvoltage of 3.0−1.6
V.
2.3. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy. Electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured by Solartron
Cell Test 1470E with ZView software. A frequency range from 1 MHz
to 0.1 Hz and an amplitude of 10 mV were applied for performing the
measurements.
2.4. Post-test Analysis. Cycled lithium anodes were rinsed twice
with 2 mL of dry DME and then airtight transferred to the chamber of
the SEM instrument for surface morphology analysis. FT-IR spectra
were collected on a Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher
Scientiﬁc) using an attenuated total reﬂectance (ATR) attachment
placed in an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. Cycled electrolyte was recovered
from cells after disassembly by soaking the cell components in
anhydrous diethyl ether (1 mL) to both dissolve analytes and desalt
the electrolyte materials. The resulting supernatant was then passed
through a 0.2 μm ﬁlter and used for GC-MS analysis without further
dilution.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
HFB was selected as a neutral molecule receptor which is
capable of binding lone-pair-bearing molecules including
anions through multiple pair−π interactions. HFB is
susceptible to nucleophilic attack, and this type of reaction
has been widely studied with a variety of nucleophiles. Sulfur-
containing nucleophiles of the type of Sx
2−have been
somewhat neglected. Since a series of polysulﬁdes are
generated during the discharge and charge process of Li−S
battery and the low-order ones are known to be easily isolated
and electrochemically inactive, we were curious about the
impact of the HFB−Sx
2−coordination/reaction on the cell
performance. Figure 1b illustrates the strong pair−π inter-
actions between HFB and Sx
2−by a solubility test. Apparently,
when HFB was added, the suspension of Li2S in DME turned
to a dark solution from the opaque suspension indicating a
reaction occurs between HFB and S2−anion; similarly, HFB
also coordinates and reacts with the high-order polysulﬁdes

3.1. Cell Performance Improvement. As shown in
Figure 1c, the HFB cell suﬀers from severe shuttling eﬀects due
to the strong interaction of HFB with the discharged
polysulﬁde products. To examine the positive eﬀect of HFB
on the cell performance, LiNO3 was added to the electrolyte to
minimize the shuttling mechanism so that the regular cycling
of HFB cell could be achieved as shown in Figure 2a.
Surprisingly, during the charging process, an extra small
“plateau” was shown on the voltage proﬁle for all cells
containing HFB additive, indicating a new species formed
likely from the complex of HFB with the lithium polysulﬁdes.
This plateau remains persistent even after the 50th cycle, as
shown in Figure 2b.
Compared with the baseline electrolyte, the delivered
capacity is more stabilized for the 3% HFB cell and maintains
high retention after 20 cycles as shown in Figure 2c. It is worth
noting that all of the sulfur electrodes used in this test were
made by simplymixing commercial sulfur powder, carbon
black, and PVDF binder and not optimized or specially
designed.
However, further increase in HFB concentration (from 3%
to 20%) deteriorates the cell performance in both capacity
retention and the Coulombic eﬃciency, indicating the
additional HFB participates in the parasitic reaction and
causes deterioration of the cell performance (Figure 2d).
Possible reactions of HFB with the polysulﬁde anions were
proposed in Figure 3. HFB preferably interacts with Li2S, a
stronger nucleophile compared with polysulﬁde anions Sx
2−,
via a nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) reaction
generating lithium pentaﬂuorophenyl sulﬁde (1) and LiF
(route a). Compound 1 can be reversibly oxidized (or
charged) to bis(pentaﬂuorophenyl) disulﬁde (3) and bis-
(pentaﬂuorophenyl) polysulﬁde (4) and reduced (or dis-
charged) back to 1 in the cell following route d. The use of
dimethyl disulﬁdes (DMDS) and alkyl polysulﬁdes to activate
the dead lithium sulﬁdes has been previously reported by other
groups20−23 without clear understanding of the mechanism.
In an attempt to potentially identify these species,
compound 1 and compound 3 were synthesized ex situ and
the synthesis procedures and spectroscopic characterizations
(Figures S1−S4) were provided in the Supporting Information
. Synthesized compounds 1 and 3 were then added to the
baseline electrolyte and tested in the cell. As shown in Figure
S5, the charge voltage proﬁles of HFB, compound 1, and
compound 3 cells have exactly the same shoulder shown in the
voltage range from 2.63 to 2.85 V during the charging process,
conﬁrming compounds 1 and 3 as the interaction products of
HFB and Sx
2−. Further evidence for the formation of the above
proposed species was provided by the post-test analysis of the
cycled electrolyte. A trace amount of compound 3 generated
by the reaction of HFB and Li2Sx during the cycling was
observed by gas chromatography−mass spectrometry (GC-
MS) analysis; i.e., the mass spectra (MS) of the ex situ
synthesized compound (3) (Figure S4) and the cycled
electrolyte (Figure S6) (peak 3 separated in the gas
chromatography) are identical.
However, when additional HFB are present, it interacts with
high-order polysulﬁdes as shown in route b, generating lithium
pentaﬂuorophenyl polysulﬁde (2). Compound 2 can be further
reduced to give 1 and Li2S, resulting in active material loss and
the capacity fade.
Figure 4 presents the Nyquist plots of the cells before
cycling and after one hundred discharge−charge cycles. All
cells showed similar R1 (Ohmic resistance of the cell) that
ranges from 2.5 to 4.0 Ω before cycling. After cycling, both R1
and Rint became smaller for the low HFB content cells
compared with both baseline and 20% HFB cell, indicating the
reutilization of the low-order Li2S and Li2S2 in the sulfur
electrode. The biggest diﬀerence is the charge transfer
resistance (Rct) shown in Figure 4b caused by the high
concentration of HFB. Rct for the 20% HFB cell increases
dramatically after 100 cycles. This result is well correlated to
the cell data showing that excess amount of HFB leads to the
deterioration of cell performance and the possible mechanism
was proposed in Figure 3.
The strong interaction of HFB−Li2Sx was conﬁrmed by the
solubility data (Figure 1b) and the electrochemical data
(Figures 1c and 2a−d). However, due to the increased
solubility of the sulﬁde species in the electrolyte, LiNO3
additive is mandatory to be present in the base electrolyte in
order to evaluate its impact on the cell performance.
Apparently, side reactions of HFB with LiNO3 thus altering
the SEI chemical composition and morphology on Li anode
become problematic. With increasing HFB concentration from
1% to 20%, the chemical composition and morphologies of the
Li SEI dramatically changed (FT-IR spectra in Figure S7 and
SEM/EDS in Figure S8 in the Supporting Information) with
20% HFB cell resembling that of the SEI formed in the LiNO3-
free baseline electrolyte (Figure S8h).

To verify the impact of HFB additive in LiNO3-free
electrolyte, a ﬂuorinated ether-based electrolyte 1.0 M LiTFSI
DOL/1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether
(TTE) was used as a new baseline electrolyte. TTE as an
electrolyte cosolvent has been thoroughly studied by our
group25−27 and other groups,28−30 and signiﬁcant improve-
ment in Li−S battery performance has been reported due to its
low lithium polysulﬁde solubility and excellent lithium surface
passivation capability. Therefore, no LiNO3 additive is
required for this electrolyte to eliminate the redox shuttling
eﬀect caused by the reactivation of the low-order lithium
polysulﬁdes by HFB and the subsequent dissolution of HFB-
Li2Sx species. Compared with the DOL/DME and DOL/TTE
electrolyte cell, the DOL/TTE + HFB cells showed much
improved capacity and capacity retention, especially for the
low HFB concentration as evidenced in Figure 5a. Counter-
intuitively, the higher HFB concentration actually slightly
lowered both the capacity retention and the Coulombic
eﬃciency. It is speculated that higher HFB content increases
the solubility of the active sulﬁdes, which detracts from the
function of TTE cosolvent to suppress the solubility of sulﬁde
species.
Density functional theory calculations were then carried out
to determine the mechanism by which the HFB might
coordinate with low-order lithium sulﬁdes and subsequently
reacts and converts Li2S/Li2S2 into active aromatic sulﬁdes,
thus facilitating the sulfur utilization and the cell performance.
Calculations of the potential energy surfaces were performed
with Gaussian 09, Revision E.01, with the B3LYP/6-311+
+G** method. Zero-point energies were scaled by 0.9877. The
polarizable continuum solvation method was used for implicit
solvation calculations of solvation energies. The calculation
results show that the nucleophilic aromatic attack of Li2S with
HFB was found to have a barrier of 0.52 eV for conversion
from an encounter complex C6F6···Li2S to a pentaﬂuorophenyl
sulﬁde−dilithium ﬂuoride structure (C6F6S−Li2F) via a
transition state. The free energy and the predicted structure
are illustrated in Figure 6. Compared with Li2S, the reaction of
HFB with Li2S2 was found to have a slightly high barrier of
0.75 eV for conversion from an encounter complex C6F6···
Li2S2 to a pentaﬂuorophenyl disulﬁde−dilithium ﬂuoride
structure (C6F6S2−Li2F). The same calculations were also
performed for high-order polysulﬁde anions (Li2Sx
2−, x = 3, 4,
6, 8), and the results are summarized in Table 1. The higher
polysulﬁdes had even larger energy barriers and were much less
favorable compared with the low-order ones. This result is
consistent with the mechanism by which HFB acts as an
additive to preferably coordinate and activate the low-order

4. CONCLUSIONS
A new functional electrolyte additive hexaﬂuorobenzene
(HFB) was reported for the ﬁrst time for lithium−sulfur
batteries. The impact of HFB was veriﬁed experimentally both
in 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/DME + 0.1 M LiNO3 and 1.0 M
LiTFSI DOL/TTE electrolyte cells. Although high-concen-
tration (>20%) additive causes detrimental eﬀect on the
former cell performance probably due to the interference with
LiNO3, low concentration improved the capacity retention
especially at the later stage of the cycling. DFT calculations
indicate that HFB preferably coordinates with low-order
polysulﬁdes Li2S and Li2S2 over higher order Li2Sx and further
reacts forming redox-active species, thus reactivating the
electronically isolated active materials back to the system and
reducing the gradual capacity loss during later cycles. The
reaction mechanism was proposed, and possible species were
identiﬁed. This research opens a new approach to tackle the
fundamental issue of the Li−S chemistry.
