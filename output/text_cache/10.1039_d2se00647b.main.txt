A novel mixed ether-based electrolyte for lithium–
sulfur batteries with Li anode protection by dual
salts†
Xirui Kong,a Yichen Kong,b Xiaobin Liao,
c Sheng Liu*a and Yan Zhao
*a
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB) are regarded as one of the next-generation energy storage systems with
a high energy density. However, the shuttle eﬀect of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) and irreversible
consumption of the Li anode prevent the practical application of Li–S batteries. In this work, a mixed
tetrahydrofuran (THF) and dipropyl ether (DPE) based electrolyte has been optimized for Li–S batteries.
Due to the common-ion eﬀect, the LiPS solubility has been signiﬁcantly inhibited when the sulfur
cathode is operating in a concentrated electrolyte containing an optimized solvent system with weak
dissolving power. And no self-discharge eﬀect occurred in the cells with these electrolytes during a 10
day storage. Besides, the coulombic eﬃciency (CE) for Li deposition/stripping has been improved by
optimizing the ratio of dual lithium salts. Impressively, LikCu half-cells can run stably for more than 500
cycles with a high CE (>99.3%). For these reasons, a Li–SeSPAN cell with a L1L2TE-1382 electrolyte
demonstrates an excellent capacity retention (>94% after 500 cycles) with an ultrathin (50 mm) Li metal
anode. With a good compatibility of the optimized electrolyte for both the Li metal anode and sulfur
cathode, this work provides a new design strategy for the electrolyte for lithium–sulfur batteries.
1.
Introduction
The development of clean energy technologies, for example,
thermal power, hydroelectric power, nuclear energy, solar
power, and wind power, is imperative for the survival and
sustainable development of human society. For the utilization
of these clean energy resources, advanced energy storage
devices play a key role for applications in portable electronic
devices and electric vehicles in this power-demanding world.
Among various advanced energy storage devices, metal–sulfur
batteries, including Li–S, Na–S and Mg–S batteries, are espe-
cially attractive due to their high specic energy. With a high
theoretical energy density of about 2500 W h kg1, lithium–
sulfur batteies (LSBs) have been considered as one of the most
promising candidates of next generation rechargeable devices.1
In a traditional lithium–sulfur battery, soluble lithium pol-
ysuldes (LiPSs) would form during sulfur cathode cycling in
ether-based electrolytes, and they tend to dissolve in these
electrolytes and react with the Li anode, which leads to low
coulombic eﬃciency (CE) and rapid loss of active materials.2,3
To make matters worse, the low CE of lithium plating/stripping
caused by dendrite lithium and dead lithium restricts the
commercialization of lithium metal batteries (LMBs) using
conventional
ether-based
electrolytes.4
The
formation
of
a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) by carefully regulating
lithium salts and solvents is an important solution to handle
these problems.5–7 In order to restrain the LiPS dissolution,
many studies have developed high concentration electrolytes
(HCEs) and localized high concentration electrolytes (LHCEs)
with limited ether solvents for LSBs.8,9 However, the highly
concentrated lithium salts and excessive uorinated ethers
result in lower conductivity and high cost.10 Wei et al. proposed
a “solid–solid” conversion mechanism during discharge and
charge processes, which is an eﬀective strategy to avoid the
shuttle eﬀect and high reversibility during cycling.11 Sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), reported by Wang and co-workers in
2002, has been proposed as a promising cathode for LSBs,12 in
which active sulfur is covalently bonded to the pyrolyzed poly-
acrylonitrile (PAN) chains. Recently, Xie et al. optimized the
structure of SPAN by doping selenium (SeSPAN).13,14 In general,
SPAN and SeSPAN cathodes have been proved to work well in
carbonate ester-based electrolytes with minimal LiPS dissolu-
tion and nearly 100% coulombic eﬃciency. However, compared
with ether solvents, carbonate-based electrolytes are even less
compatible with a Li anode, and cannot be employed in LSBs for
practical application.15

Since the solubility of LiPS and the reactivity with lithium
metal can be inhibited by low-polar ether solvents with a high C/
O ratio, methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), diisopropyl ether
(DIPE), dipropyl ether (DPE) and dibutyl ether (DBE) have been
prudently selected as low-polarity solvents in electrolytes for
LSBs.16,17 Chen et al. mixed DOL–DME (1,3-dioxolane and 1,2-
dimethoxyethane) with DIPE or MTBE to give a limited solu-
bility for LiPS and suitable conductivity for the electrolyte.18 The
mixed-DIPE electrolyte also has an improved compatibility with
the lithium anode, resulting in signicantly improved cycling
stability.
Zhou
et
al.
introduced
a
4
M
lithium
bis(-
uorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) electrolyte with a DBE solvent for
LSBs. Despite having suﬃcient solubility of lithium salts, this
pure ether-based electrolyte delivers a low Li+ ionic conduc-
tivity.19 Both lithium bis(triuoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI)
and LiFSI salts are common lithium salts used in lithium–sulfur
batteries. Compared with LiTFSI, LiFSI salt has enhanced ionic
conductivity and abundant labile uorine, which can partici-
pate in forming a more robust and stable solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI) on the Li anode.20–23 Unfortunately, the solu-
bility of LiFSI is very limited in the DOL solvent.24 Therefore,
a new mixed-solvent system needs to be developed for LSB
electrolytes.
In order to achieve the purpose of eliminating the shuttle
eﬀect and parasitic reaction on the Li anode, we have designed
herein a novel THF–DPE based dual-salt electrolyte for Li–S cells
with a limited lithium anode (lithium foil with a thickness of 50
mm). Unlike the DME–DOL solvent system, the THF–DPE system
delivers a better performance, including low volatility, a high Li+
transference number and good compatibility with electrodes. In
detail, with a high CE of Li deposition/stripping (99.3%), stable
cycling for more than 500 cycles was achieved in LikCu cells
with the L1L2TE-1382 electrolyte. And the life span of Li–
SeSPAN cells with these optimized electrolytes are prolonged to
500 cycles with a high retention of capacity (>90%). Further-
more, dense morphology and low resistance of cycled Li metal
anodes were identied as advantages of the higher ratio of LiFSI
in electrolytes for Li anode protection.
2.
Experimental section
2.1
Materials
Li metal (4 16 mm, 600 mm, China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.,
China), lithium–copper composite tape (Li, 50 mm, China
Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., China), Cu foil (100 mm, Sinopharm
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Al foil (15 mm, Nippon Light
Metal Co., Ltd., Japan), a separator (2325, Celgard, USA).
lithium bis(triuoromethanesulfon)imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%),
1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99.8%, with 75 ppm BHT inhibitor), 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.5%), and polyacrylonitrile (PAN,
average Mw  150 000, 99%) were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich, Inc., USA. Lithium bis(uorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI,
99.9%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd.,
Japan. Tetrahydrofuran (THF, anhydrous, 99.9%) was obtained
from Innochem Co., Ltd., China. Dipropyl ether (DPE, 98%),
elemental sulfur (S8, 99.8%), selenium (Se powder, 99.99%),
ethanol (spectral purity, 99.8%), and propanol (99.5%) were
purchased from Aladdin, Inc., China. All the solvents used for
electrolytes were stored with 4 ˚A molecular sieves in an argon
glove box before use. Conductive carbon was acetylene black
(Denka Black Li 250, Lion Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan), and the
LA132 binder (solid content  15%) was purchased from Indigo
Technology Co., Ltd., China. RO-pure water was self-made
through a water purier (Smart-N UV, Heal Force).
2.2
SeSPAN electrode preparation
Before preparing the cathode, sulfur, selenium and poly-
acrylonitrile powders were ball-milled at a weight ratio of
15 : 1 : 3 with ethanol used as the dispersant. The resulting
mixture was dried at 60 C for 6 h and then heated to 350 C for
6 h in a nitrogen atmosphere to obtain the SeSPAN composite
with a sulfur content of 41.8% (Table S1, ESI†). The sulfur
content was determined by elemental analysis (Vario EL cube,
Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany). The SeSPAN
cathode slurry was prepared by mixing SeSPAN, acetylene black,
and an LA132 binder at a weight ratio of 7 : 2 : 1, which was
then spread on Al foil. Aer the evaporation of the solvents in
a vacuum oven at 60 C for 12 h, the cathode coating pasted on
Al foil was rolled to 30 mm thickness. The areal loading of sulfur
in the as-fabricated cathode is about 2.0 mg cm2 in all cases.
2.3
Electrolyte preparation
All electrolytes were prepared in an argon-lled dry glove box
(H2O < 0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm). First, LiTFSI and LiFSI were
mixed with diﬀerent molar ratios and dissolved in THF and
then stirred for 30 min. A specied amount of DPE was added to
the above solutions and stirred for 6 h at room temperature and
the total concentration of salts was about 2.5 M. Diﬀerent
solutions, including 1 M LiTFSI/DME–DOL (LDD) and two THF–
DPE based LiTFSI solutions with diﬀerent THF–DPE molar
ratios, namely LTE-4X0 (LiTFSI : THF : DPE ¼ 4 : 10 : 0) and
LTE-482 (LiTFSI : THF : DPE ¼ 4 : 8 : 2) have been prepared.
These solutions were stirred with stoichiometric amounts of
lithium sulde (Li2S) and sulfur (S8) for 24 h to form saturated
Li2S6 solution and were then stored for 7 days at room
temperature.
2.4
Battery fabrication
In an argon glove box, 2025 type and 2016 type coin cells were
fabricated using a Celgard 2325 separator. The coulombic eﬃ-
ciency of plating/stripping performance of lithium was exam-
ined by using CukLi coin cells (CR2016) with 20 mL electrolyte.
The electrochemical window of electrolytes was examined by
using Liksteel coin cells (CR2016) with 20 mL electrolyte. The
galvanostatic cycling performance of Li deposition–dissolution
was tested by using LikLi symmetric cells (CR2025) with 30 mL
electrolyte. The Li–SeSPAN cells were assembled by using
CR2016 coin cells with 20 mL electrolyte.

3.
Results and discussion
3.1
Physicochemical properties of electrolytes
As the solvent serves as a lithium ionic conductor for mass
transport, the properties of solvents and their dosage play an
important role in electrolyte. The key characteristics of elec-
trolyte solvents for the LSB are that they should have limited
LiPS solubility and low reactivity with metallic lithium. Both
DME and THF, having the same Gutmann donor number (DN ¼
20) and equivalent dielectric constants, are widely used in ether-
based electrolytes to provide enough conductivity as needed.
Unlike a bidentate ligand (such as DME), THF is a monodentate
ligand solvent possessing a lower solvating power for lithium
salts and LiPSs. As a weak solvent, inert DPE acted more as
a diluent added in electrolyte to reduce the viscosity and inhibit
the dissolution of LiPSs. However, excessive introduction of
DPE in HCE would result in immiscibility of the solution. The
digital photos of the solutions (LDD, LTE-4X0 (X ¼ 10) and LTE-
482) with saturated Li2S6 are shown in Fig. 1a. Owing to the high
solubility of Li2S6 in LDD electrolyte, the color of the upper uid
turned to a red-brown color immediately and became opaque in
2 min during stirring. In contrast, with a relatively higher salt

The electrochemical window of these electrolytes was
investigated by the linear potential sweep method. As shown in
Fig. 1b, the onset of oxidation of the electrolytes shis from
4.1 V to 4.6 V when the molar ratio of LiFSI/LiTFSI increases.
Although THF was found to breakdown at a voltage above 4 V vs.
Li/Li+, the medium-concentration Li salt with a large molar ratio
of LiFSI/LiTFSI can help improve the electrochemical stability of
THF.26 These electrolytes are adequate for the Li–SeSPAN
battery with an operating voltage of 1–3 V. The conductivity and
lithium-ion transference number are two important parameters
to characterize the ion transport performance in electrolyte.
Fig. 1c shows the inuence of salts on the conductivity and the
Li+ migration number. With the increase of LiFSI, the conduc-
tivity of electrolyte has increased from 1.05 mS cm1 (L1L2TE-
4082) to 3.99 mS cm1 (L1L2TE-1382), which explains the
cells delivering a higher capacity at high rates when using
L1L2TE-1382 electrolyte. Due to the high lithium concentration
and the small molecule solvents, the Li+ migration number of
the series electrolyte is maintained at about 0.8. Because of
traditional ether-based solvents having low boiling and ash
points, the battery containing these ether-based electrolytes
would face great risks when they are operating in hot environ-
ments. In the volatility test of these electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI/
DME–DOL (LDD) lost about 30% of its mass in 5 minutes and
50% in 30 minutes at 25 C (Fig. 1d). In contrast, thanks to
a higher concentration of Li salts, the L1L2TE electrolyte lost
only 10% of its mass in 30 minutes, which exhibits less volatility
even when containing more volatile solvents.
3.2
Lithium plating/stripping cycling CE and stability
In order to assess the lithium plating/stripping behavior in
these electrolytes, the galvanostatic cycling experiments were
conducted in LikCu half-cells with an areal capacity of
1 mA h cm2 at a current density of 0.5 mA cm2. With the LDD
electrolyte,
the
LikCu
half-cells
exhibited
an
unstable
coulombic eﬃciency (CE), which increased from 77% to 98%
within 50 cycles and then dropped rapidly to 40% in 100 cycles
(Fig. 2a and S1a, ESI†). As shown in Fig. 2b–d, by increasing the
molar ratio of LiFSI/LiTFSI, the CE of the rst cycle has
improved from 91% to 95% with a polarization voltage drop-
ping from 0.039 V to 0.028 V. The CE of lithium plating/
stripping in dual-salt electrolytes has increased to 99.3% aer
10 cycles and is maintained over 500 cycles (2000 h). It is worth
noting that the polarization voltage of the L1L2TE-1382 half-cell
is very stable and lower than 40 mV even at the 500th cycle. The
LikCu half-cells using single-salt electrolytes (L1L2TE-4082 and
L1L2TE-0482, Fig. 2e and f) exhibited a reduced CE aer 100
cycles, which indicates that single-salt electrolytes cannot
eﬀectively prevent the side-reactions on a metallic lithium
electrode. The long-term cycling stability of a Li anode was
further examined in symmetrical cells with an areal capacity of
1 mA h cm2 at a current density of 1 mA cm2 (Fig. S1b, ESI†).
The cells with the L1L2TE-3182 and L1L2TE-2282 electrolytes
experienced a short circuit aer a brief increase of the polari-
zation voltage around 500 and 600 cycles respectively. More
importantly, the symmetrical cell in L1L2TE-1382 electrolyte
initially displayed the lowest overpotential of 40 mV and deliv-
ered a remarkable lifespan of more than 1000 cycles (2000 h)
with a stable voltage of about 60 mV. These results demonstrate
that the optimized LiTFSI–LiFSI/THF–DPE (L1L2TE-1382) is
more compatible with the lithium anode, and eﬀectively
suppresses excessive consumption of the electrolyte.

3.3
Electrochemical performances of Li–SeSPAN cells
The electrochemical performances of Li–SeSPAN cells with
diﬀerent electrolytes (LDD and L1L2TE-3182/2282/1382) were
also investigated for comparison. The preparation process of
the SeSPAN electrode is given in the ESI.† As shown in Fig. 4a,
all these cells have gone through the rst discharge at 0.05 A g1
(0.075C rate) and ve formation cycles at 0.1 A g1 (0.15C rate)
before long-time cycling (0.2 A g1, about 0.3C). Because the
shuttle eﬀect occurs in the charge process, the charge capacity
in LDD electrolyte is obviously higher than the discharge
capacity (Fig. S2a, ESI†). And the specic capacity of the LDD
cell dropped rapidly from 570 mA h g1 to 160 mA h g1 within
100 cycles with low CE. The cells in L1L2TE electrolyte delivered
superior long-term stability and a high initial discharge capacity
of about 700 mA h g1. As shown in Fig. S2b–d,† the long-term
cycling performance is signicantly enhanced by increasing the
molar ratio of LiFSI/LiTFSI. The cell with the L1L2TE-1382
electrolyte delivers a high capacity of 670 mA h g1 aer acti-
vation (the theoretical capacity is 729 mA h g1). Aer 500
cycles, the cell retains an excellent capacity retention of more
than 94% (650 mA h g1, Fig. S2d, ESI†), corresponding to
a capacity fade as low as 0.006% per cycle.
The rate performances of Li–SeSPAN cells are shown in
Fig. 4b and S3 (ESI†). With continuous loss of the active
substance, the capacities of the LDD cell decreased signicantly
during charge–discharge cycles with a low coulombic eﬃciency.
In contrast, the cell delivered a reversible capacity with the
L1L2TE electrolytes. The stable capacity of cells with L1L2TE-
2282/1382 electrolyte have recovered to about 670 mA h g1
when the current density returned to 0.2 A g1 (0.3C). As shown
in Table S3,† due to L1L2TE-1382 having the highest conduc-
tivity, the cell with this electrolyte delivered the highest capac-
ities of 705, 667, 646, 616, 575 and 300 mA h g1 with a current
density of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 A g1 (0.15, 0.3, 0.75, 1.5, 3,
and 7.5C) respectively, corresponding to the utilization ratios of
96.7%, 91.5%, 88.5%, 84.5%, 78.8% and 41.1% of the theoret-
ical capacity (729 mA h g1). These results indicate that THF–
DPE-based electrolytes with higher molar ratios of LiFSI/LiTFSI
is benecial to enhancing the cycling stabilities and rate
performances of the Li–SeSPAN cell.

4.
Conclusions
In summary, we have demonstrated a novel electrolyte that
enables a dendrite-free Li anode and eliminates LiPS shuttling
in Li–SeSPAN batteries, simultaneously allowing for a long
lifespan of 500 cycles with a capacity retention of 97%. The
common-ion eﬀect of medium concentration dual-salt (LiTFSI–
LiFSI) and low polarity of the mixed solvent (THF–DPE) prevent
lithium polysuldes from dissolving in electrolyte. Therefore,
the capacity of the cells with the L1L2TE electrolytes has no self-
discharge eﬀect during a 10 day storage. This work provides an
eﬀective electrolyte design strategy toward Li anode protection
and inhibition of the “shuttle eﬀect”, which is expected to
promote the long-cycle life of LSBs with an ultrathin Li anode.
Some ethers (such as THF) under heat and oxygen-rich envi-
ronments tend to form peroxides, which would be replaced with
some safer ether solvents in subsequent research.
