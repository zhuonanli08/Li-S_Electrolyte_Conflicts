A B S T R A C T
The “shuttle effect,” slow sulfur redox kinetics, and low utilization of active materials have become challenges 
that lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries must currently address. To tackle these issues, this study employs 2-chloro- 
1,4-benzoquinone (MCBQ) as an electrolyte additive. MCBQ, with its carbonyl oxygen structure and halogen 
substituents, can react with polysulfides to form a cyclic insoluble organic sulfur intermediate that contains Li–O 
bonds and C–S bonds. During discharge, it alters the conversion pathway of sulfur species. Taking Li2S6 as an 
example, the original path “Li2S6 → Li2S4 → Li2S2 → Li2S” is optimized into a new pathway “MCBQ- Li2S6 → 
MCBQ-2Li2S3 → MCBQ-2Li2S” simplifying the route to enhance sulfur redox kinetic efficiency. Moreover, MCBQ 
also forms an organic-inorganic hybrid interfacial protective layer on the lithium anode side, effectively miti­
gating the corrosion of the lithium anode by polysulfides. The battery with the MCBQ additive maintains a 
Coulombic efficiency of 100 % at a current density of 0.2C, with an initial discharge capacity reaching 812 mAh 
g−1; even after 120 cycles, the capacity decay rate per cycle is only 0.42 %. The introduction of MCBQ not only 
accelerates sulfur redox kinetics but also enhances the utilization of active materials and overall battery stability.
1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs) represent a promising candidate for 
next-generation energy storage systems, owing to their unique combi­
nation of metallic lithium and elemental sulfur. With a theoretical spe­
cific energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, LSBs present an exceptional 
solution for applications in autonomous vehicles and aerospace tech­
nologies [1,2]. In comparison to conventional lithium-ion batteries 
(LIBs), LSBs generate discharge capacity through the reaction between 
elemental sulfur and Li+ during charge-discharge cycles, forming 
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and facilitating reversible conversion 
among these species. This mechanism offers significant advantages, 
including abundant raw material availability and cost-effectiveness 
[3,4]. The fundamental reaction processes in LSBs can be categorized 
into two primary mechanisms: (1) the two-phase transformation at the 
cathode interface (Scheme 1a), encompassing S8 (s)↔Li2Sn (l) (4 ≤n ≤
8) and Li2Sn (l) ↔Li2Sx (l) (2 ≤x < 4), ultimately yielding solid-state Li2S 
[5]; and (2) the deposition and dissolution of lithium at the anode [6]. 
However, this multi-step reaction mechanism inherently contributes to 
decreased cycle life in LSBs [7–10]. Furthermore, the extensive disso­
lution of soluble polysulfides in the electrolyte induces the “shuttle ef­
fect,” which not only depletes the lithium anode but also promotes 
undesirable lithium dendrite formation. This may pierce the battery 
separator, resulting in reduced active material utilization and severe 
self-discharge issues, potentially leading to short-circuit events [11,12].
To mitigate the inherent “shuttle effect” and sluggish redox kinetics 
in LSBs, researchers have implemented various strategies to immobilize 
and promote the conversion of LiPSs [3,13–16]. The primary approach 
focuses on enhancing the adsorption and catalytic conversion efficiency 
of sulfur species through improvements in cathode materials [17], 
including the utilization of porous carbon materials [18–21], specific 
additives [22–24] and metal atom doping [25–27], all aimed at 
achieving more effective LiPSs fixation and conversion. Li et al. [28] 
proposed a method combining transition metals with carbon matrices as 
sulfur host materials, effectively confining LiPSs to the cathode side 
during discharge, thereby reducing the “shuttle effect” and achieving

covalent sulfur fixation. However, the limited capacity of single-atom 
metal active sites to fix LiPSs prevents this approach from fundamen­
tally resolving the “shuttle effect” issue [29–33]. Another widely 
employed strategy involves constructing a cathode-electrolyte inter­
phase (CEI) protective layer. Wu et al. [34] demonstrated that N-bro­
mosuccinimide (NBS) as an electrolyte additive can form a CEI through 
in-situ deposition, effectively suppressing LiPS dissolution while main­
taining internal sulfur conversion processes, thereby significantly 
enhancing battery cycling stability and rate performance. However, 
most of the CEI layers have poor conductivity, and it is still necessary to 
explore the most direct method to improve the slow kinetics.
Modifying the redox pathway of sulfur species represents another 
viable strategy. For example, Chen et al. [35] demonstrated the use of 
dimethyl disulfide (DMDS), a liquid organic sulfur compound, as an 
additive for LSBs. This additive undergoes a reaction with sulfur to 
generate soluble methyl-terminated polysulfide intermediates (DMPS), 
which subsequently reduce to lithium organosulfides and Li2S during 
discharge, thereby significantly shortening the conversion pathway of 
LiPSs (see Scheme 1b). Furthermore, this intermediate not only con­
tributes additional capacity to the battery but also acts to stabilize the 
sulfur cathode. However, the use of DMDS as an additive presents a 
challenge due to its corrosive effect on the lithium anode. Consequently, 
there remains a need to develop novel methods that can simultaneously 
protect the sulfur cathode and enhance the conversion of sulfur species.
In this study, 2-chloro-1,4-benzoquinone (MCBQ) is employed as an 
electrolyte additive due to its ability to react with LiPSs, forming 
insoluble organosulfur intermediates. These intermediates serve dual 
purposes in enhancing battery performance. First, they generate a 
conductive CEI layer on the cathode surface, which effectively immo­
bilizes sulfur and mitigates the accumulation of insulating Li2S. This 
mechanism preserves electron transfer pathways within the sulfur ma­
trix, thereby optimizing active material utilization. Furthermore, MCBQ 
facilitates an alternative pathway for LiPS conversion: MCBQ-Li2S6 
initially converts to MCBQ-2Li2S3, followed by transformation into 
MCBQ-2Li2S. This process shortens the sulfur chain length and promotes 
the efficient conversion of sulfur species. Moreover, MCBQ contributes 
to the formation of an organic-inorganic hybrid SEI protective film at the 
anode, which effectively shields the lithium anode from LiPS-induced 
degradation. These synergistic mechanisms collectively enhance the 
battery's overall performance and long-term stability.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of Fe-NC
First, 0.03 mmol of Fe(NO3)3 and 1 g of peptone were dissolved in 10 
mL of deionized water. The resulting solution was then placed in a 
refrigerator and frozen for 12 h. After freezing was complete, the sample 
was transferred to a freeze dryer for lyophilization. The freeze-dried 
sample was subsequently mixed with 10 g of NaCl and ball-milled 
together for 3 h to ensure thorough mixing. Next, under a protective 
atmosphere of nitrogen (N2), the mixture was heated to 900 ◦C at a rate 
of 5 ◦C/min and maintained at this temperature for 1 h. Finally, the 
treated sample was washed clean with water and dried for later use.
2.2. Preparation of S@Fe-NC
Fe-NC and sublimed sulfur were first mixed in a mass ratio of 3:7 and 
ground for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. The mixture was then 
transferred into a glovebox under an argon atmosphere and subse­
quently placed in an oven at 155 ◦C for a reaction period of 12 h. 
Through this process, S@Fe-NC was ultimately obtained.
2.3. Preparation of Li2S6
Li2S and sublimed sulfur were mixed in a molar ratio of 1:5 and 
added to a solvent mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane 
(DME) in a volume ratio of 1:1. This mixture was stirred continuously at 
60 ◦C for 24 h, resulting in a 0.2 M Li2S6 solution. Using the same 
method, a 5 mM Li2S6 solution was also prepared.
2.4. Preparation of electrolyte additives
Initially, a blank electrolyte was prepared as the base electrolyte for 
lithium‑sulfur batteries. This base electrolyte comprised 1.0 M lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.1 M LiNO3 dissolved 
in a 1:1 (v/v) binary solvent mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 
dimethoxyethane (DME). Subsequently, p-benzoquinone (PBQ) and 2- 
chloro-1,4-benzoquinone (MCBQ) were dissolved into the base electro­
lyte at concentrations of 60, 70, and 80 mM. Following comprehensive 
performance evaluations, the 70 mM concentration demonstrated 
optimal performance. Consequently, all subsequent experiments were 
conducted using samples containing 70 mM of additives. The sulfur 
loading was 1.3–1.9 mg cm−2, and the ratio of electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) 
was about 40 μL mg−1.
3. Results and discussions
To elucidate the binding mechanisms of polysulfides with quinone- 
based electrolyte additives, we present a detailed analysis using two 
representative additives: PBQ and MCBQ. Calculations of reduced den­
sity gradient (RDG) [36–38] and electrostatic potential surface (ESP) 
[39–41] was performed for both additives, as illustrated in Fig. 1a and 
Fig. S1a. The quinone additives possess two carbonyl oxygen moieties in 
para positions. These carbonyl groups function as electron-withdrawing 
substituents, depleting electron density from the benzene ring and 
creating reactive sites. In PBQ, the carbonyl oxygens exhibit the most 
negative electrostatic potential (−29.84 kcal mol−1) and serve as reac­
tion centers during discharge, forming Li–O bonds with polysulfides to 
generate insoluble intermediate 1 (2PBQ-2Li2S6). Thermodynamic cal­
culations [42] reveal a Gibbs free energy change of −78.1 kJ mol−1 for 
this transformation, confirming its spontaneous nature. PBQ primarily 
contributes to polysulfide immobilization as a component of the

token. Figs. 2a-c and S3a illustrate the addition of 0.7 M Li2S6 to freshly

insoluble precipitates, transitioning from a clear bright yellow to a red-

chemical reactions between the blank electrolyte and Li2S6. Conversely, 
the benzoquinone-containing electrolytes exhibited increased precipi­

Li2S6. UV–Vis spectroscopic analysis was subsequently performed on the

To investigate the chemical bond structures of the two insoluble 
intermediates, infrared (IR) spectroscopy analysis was performed. The 
IR spectrum of MCBQ (Fig. 2d) revealed three vibration peaks at 3050.4, 
2927.4, and 2859 cm−1, attributable to the stretching vibrations of ar­
omatic hydrogens both within and outside the ring. The stretching vi­
brations of C––O and C––C bonds on the benzene ring were observed at 
1676.7 and 1586.4 cm−1, respectively. In the fingerprint region, two 
bending vibration peaks at 908.2 and 822.9 cm−1 characteristic of ar­
omatic hydrogens indicated a trisubstituted benzene ring. The presence 
of a chlorine substituent was confirmed by a peak at 673 cm−1 [46].The 
IR spectrum of intermediate 2 exhibited peaks at 1632.5 and 1511.1 
cm−1, confirming the retention of the benzene ring structure through 
C––O and C––C bond stretching vibrations. Notably, the replacement of 
the C–Cl bond with a C–S bond on the benzene ring suggested nucle­
ophilic substitution between polysulfides and MCBQ. A peak at 515.3 
cm−1, corresponding to S–S stretching vibrations [47], indicated the 
presence of sulfur species in intermediate 2. The IR spectrum of inter­
mediate 1 (Fig. S3b) displayed only the S–S stretching vibration peak, 
suggesting the interaction between carbonyl oxygen and polysulfides.
To validate the aforementioned analysis, Raman spectroscopy was 
performed on both insoluble intermediates. The Raman spectra of MCBQ 
and intermediate 2 are presented in Fig. 2 f. A vibration peak at 463 
cm−1 was observed, corresponding to the benzene ring's bending vi­
bration [48]. The C–Cl stretching vibration peaks typically appear 
within the 700–800 cm−1 range [49,50], and accordingly, the peak at 
775 cm−1 is attributed to the C–Cl stretching vibration at the benzene 
ring's 2nd carbon position. Notably, intermediate 2's Raman spectrum 
showed no C–Cl stretching vibration peak; instead, a peak at 882 cm−1, 
characteristic of C–S bond stretching vibration, was found. This 
observation provides further evidence of the nucleophilic reaction be­
tween MCBQ and polysulfides, resulting in C–S bond formation. The 
peak at 1371 cm−1 can be attributed to both C––O stretching vibration 
and C–C breathing vibration [51,52]. Analysis of intermediate 1's 
Raman spectrum (Fig. S3c) revealed only characteristic polysulfide vi­
bration peaks, indicating carbonyl oxygen-polysulfide combination. X- 
ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted on the two insoluble in­
termediates (Figs. 2g and S3d). The detection of additive-related peaks 
in the precipitate suggests spontaneous reactions between additives and 
polysulfides. These analyses confirm that intermediate 2 maintains its 
quinone group structure while forming new bonds with polysulfides, 
aligning with our theoretical computational findings.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted on 
cathode sheets cycled 50 times at 0.2C to examine the composition of the 
cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) film formed on the sulfur cathode in 
lithium‑sulfur batteries. The results are presented in Figs. 2h-i and S3e- 
g. The O 1 s XPS spectrum (Fig. 2 h) reveals characteristic peaks at 531.9 
eV and 533.2 eV, corresponding to C–O and C––O bonds in the MCBQ 
sample, along with a distinctive Li–O bond peak at 530.65 eV [53,54]. 
This observation is confirmed by the Li 1 s spectrum (Fig. S3e), which 
exhibits a peak at 55.05 eV attributable to Li–O bonds, indicating the 
interaction between polysulfides and carbonyl oxygen. The Li 1 s spec­
trum also shows a characteristic Li–Cl bond peak at 56.5 eV [55], 
corresponding to Li–Cl bonds at 198.6 eV and 202 eV in the Cl 2p 
spectrum (Fig. S3f). This suggests a nucleophilic substitution reaction 
between MCBQ and polysulfides, where sulfur from polysulfides re­
places chlorine to form Li–Cl and C–S bonds, a phenomenon further 
supported by the C 1 s spectrum (Fig. S3g).
The S 2p spectrum (Fig. 2i) displays peaks at 162.5 eV and 163.45 eV, 
indicative of Li2S [56], while peaks at 164.25 eV and 165.2 eV corre­
spond to S–S bonds [44]. Notably, new peaks at 166.2 eV and 166.9 eV 
represent carbon‑sulfur bonds formed through the reaction between 
MCBQ and polysulfides [57], consistent with the C 1 s spectrum data. 
Additionally, peaks at 167.4 eV and 168.1 eV are attributed to thiosul­
fate, a discharge byproduct formed from the reaction between poly­
sulfides and lithium nitrate [58–61]. The peaks at 168.95 eV and 170.3 
eV correspond to S–O bonds, likely resulting from lithium salt decom­
position. These spectroscopic findings provide compelling evidence for 
the formation of Li–O and C–S bonds through reactions between the 
additives and polysulfides.
To investigate the potential impact of the two insoluble in­
termediates on electronic conduction within the working electrode, 
conductivity measurements were performed. The results, presented in 
Figs. 2j and S3h, demonstrate that both intermediates exhibit measur­
able conductivity (σMCBQ-Li2S6 = 2.5 × 10−6 S mm−1，σ2PBQ-2Li2S6 = 1.7 
× 10−5 S mm−1). During the discharge process, this conductivity enables 
efficient electron transport through the CEI layer and into the sulfur 
core, facilitating effective electron transfer within the sulfur and miti­
gating the adverse effects of insulating Li2S. The observed conductivity 
of these intermediates serves two crucial functions: maintaining elec­
tronic conduction pathways within the electrode materials and reducing 
the electronic conduction barriers associated with Li2S formation. These 
findings demonstrate that the strategic incorporation of appropriate 
additives can significantly enhance both the operational efficiency and 
long-term stability of lithium‑sulfur batteries.

a composite organic-inorganic SEI layer on the Li anode surface, which 
mitigates polysulfide-induced degradation. To elucidate this mecha­
nism, XPS analysis was conducted on the Li anode following 50 cycles at 
0.2C, with results presented in Fig. S4. Analysis of the S 2p XPS spectrum 
and peak deconvolution (Fig. S4a) revealed characteristic peaks at 
158.5 eV and 159.35 eV, corresponding to Li2S2/Li2S species. This 
observation indicates limited polysulfide shuttling to the Li anode sur­
face, where reduction reactions occur, leading to anode degradation.

containing organic compounds, which likely represent intermediates 
formed through polysulfide-MCBQ interactions, confirming the pres­
ence of the organic SEI component. The Li 1 s spectrum (Fig. S4b) ex­
hibits a peak at 54.9 eV, characteristic of Li–O bonds, which is

Based on the theoretical and experimental results, we present a 
schematic diagram in Fig. 3a. The insoluble conductive intermediates 
formed by MCBQ additives and polysulfides adhere to the cathode sur­
face, addressing two key issues: they prevent the reduced utilization 
efficiency of active materials caused by insulating Li2S accumulation on 
the cathode surface, and they significantly simplify the polysulfide 
conversion pathway from “Li2S6 → Li2S4 → Li2S2 → Li2S” to “MCBQ- 
Li2S6 → MCBQ- 2Li2S3 → MCBQ-2Li2S”. To evaluate the reversibility and 
stability of the chemical reactions between MCBQ and polysulfides, 
cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed. Fig. 3b shows that at a 
scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, the CV curve exhibits a higher oxidation peak 
potential during the initial activation cycle, followed by stabilization in 
subsequent cycles. This pattern suggests that during initial cycles, pol­
ysulfides undergo spontaneous discharge before the additive fully acti­
vates. CV testing of the Li2S6 symmetric cell (Fig. 3c) reveals that 
samples containing MCBQ exhibit significantly larger redox peak areas 
compared to the blank sample. Additionally, the number of redox peaks 
decreases from three pairs to two pairs, indicating that MCBQ modifies 
the redox pathways of sulfur species. The adjustment of the reaction 
pathway led to a smoothing of the peak current distribution and a 
reduction in the overall current response. In the blank sample without 
MCBQ, Li2S6 exists within the electrolyte, and its oxidation and reduc­
tion reactions rely on the rapid adsorption and electron transfer of free 
polysulfides on the electrode surface. This often results in sharp current 
peaks due to drastic fluctuations in local concentration. In contrast, after 
MCBQ binds with Li2S6, the stability of the complex significantly im­
proves, and the concentration of free Li2S6 decreases substantially. 
Under these conditions, the reaction proceeds stepwise via MCBQ-Li2S6 
→ MCBQ-2Li2S3 → MCBQ-2Li2S, leading to a decrease in the overall 
reaction rate manifested as reduced peak currents. This stepwise reac­
tion minimizes the overlap of multiple peaks, resulting in a more uni­
form current response with respect to potential changes and 
consequently a naturally smoother peak profile. To further investigate 
the impact of MCBQ additive on battery performance, CV curves of three 
samples were compared at 0.1 mV s−1 (Fig. 3d). The MCBQ sample 
demonstrates two distinct pairs of redox peaks, sharper peak currents, 
and the smallest voltage difference (ΔE = 259 mV), suggesting highly 
reversible and stable reactions between MCBQ and polysulfides. We 
performed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests to assess 
the effect of the additive on Li + diffusion rate (Fig. 3e). Both additives

discharge cycles, an additive-Li cell was assembled. After 30 cycles, 
the sulfur-free battery maintained almost all its initial capacity (Fig. S5b- 
c), demonstrating the sustained effect of benzoquinone-based additives. 
The discharge specific capacities contributed by the additives were 
negligible (PBQ: 0.024 mAh, MCBQ: 0.030 mAh). These findings 
confirm that the enhanced battery performance results from the inter­
action between additives and polysulfides rather than from the additives 
themselves.
To investigate the influence of insoluble conductive intermediate 2 
on the cathode interface structure, the cathode morphology after 50 
cycles at 0.2C was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
for both blank and benzoquinone-based additive samples (Figs. 4a-f and 
S7a-c). The blank sample's cathode surface exhibited extensive, deep 
cracks, indicating significant surface roughness. In contrast, the MCBQ- 
treated cathode surface displayed fewer cracks and enhanced smooth­
ness, suggesting that intermediate 2 provides protective effects during 
cycling, thereby reducing Li2S encapsulation of active materials. Ex­
amination of cathode cross-sections (Figs. 4g-h and S7d) revealed the 
formation of a CEI layer across all samples. Notably, the MCBQ-treated 
cathode (Fig. 4h) exhibited the most favorable CEI characteristics, with 
a thickness of only 12.5 μm compared to 29 μm and 25 μm for blank and 
PBQ samples, respectively. This observation provides strong evidence 
that the surface-adherent insoluble intermediate 2 effectively minimizes 
active material loss, thereby enhancing electrode stability and 
performance.
To investigate the influence of insoluble intermediates on the Li 
anode's interface structure, the morphology of the lithium metal elec­
trode in symmetric Li||Li cells after 50 cycles at 1 mA cm−2 was analyzed 
by SEM (Fig. S8a-c). with the PBQ sample exhibited an irregular lithium 
metal surface characterized by large cracks and surface roughness. 
Cross-sectional analysis (Fig. S8j) revealed a loose, thick, and irregular 
SEI layer (77 μm), attributed to continuous destruction and regeneration 
of the interfacial layer formed by lithium nitrate reactions during 
discharge, resulting in irregular SEI formation and volumetric expan­
sion. In contrast, cells containing electrolyte additives (Fig. S8d-i) 
demonstrated significantly improved morphology: the lithium metal 
surface was free of visible dendrites, and the SEI layer was dense and 
uniform, with the MCBQ sample showing optimal performance 
(Fig. S8h-i). This enhancement is attributed to MCBQ's ability to bind 
with migrated polysulfides, forming insoluble organic sulfur in­
termediates that adhere to the lithium metal surface, thereby estab­
lishing a protective organic SEI layer (Fig. S8k) that effectively isolates 
polysulfides. Furthermore, sulfur from the polysulfides substitutes 
chlorine to form LiCl, facilitating the formation of an inorganic SEI layer, 
ultimately resulting in a composite organic-inorganic SEI layer that 
effectively protects the lithium anode. The catalytic effect of benzoqui­
none additives on the nucleation process of Li2S was investigated using 
chronoamperometry under constant potential (Fig. 4(i-k)). The experi­
mental results demonstrate that cells containing benzoquinone additives 
exhibited higher specific capacities (346.41 mAh g−1 for PBQ and 
505.84 mAh g−1 for MCBQ) compared to the blank cell (336.03 mAh 
g−1). Furthermore, the reduction in nucleation time (3330 s for MCBQ 
<5580 s for PBQ < 9150 s without additive) further confirms the sig­
nificant role of benzoquinone additives in promoting Li2S nucleation. 
These findings reveal that benzoquinone additives effectively enhance 
the kinetics of the catalytic conversion by accelerating nucleation and 
precipitation processes.
Fig. 5a-b illustrates the initial charge-discharge profiles of the three 
samples at rates of 0.1C and 0.2C. Using the 0.1C rate as a representative 
example, batteries containing benzoquinone-based additives demon­
strated enhanced discharge specific capacities compared to the sample 
with blank electrolyte, indicating superior electrochemical perfor­
mance. The battery with the MCBQ additive achieved the highest 
discharge specific capacity (1149 mAh g−1), followed by the PBQ- 
containing battery (965 mAh g−1). These results suggest that MCBQ 
addition effectively facilitates polysulfide conversion reactions, thereby 
significantly enhancing the battery's discharge specific capacity.
To investigate the reversibility and stability of the batteries, rate 
performance tests were conducted at various current densities, ranging 
from 0.1C to 2C. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, the additive-free battery 
demonstrated relatively low discharge specific capacities and sulfur 
utilization rates across the tested current densities, achieving only 23.1 
%–51.7 %. However, the addition of benzoquinone-based additives 
substantially enhanced sulfur utilization. The battery containing PBQ 
additive exhibited sulfur utilization rates of 30.1 %–63.7 % under 
identical conditions, while the MCBQ-containing battery achieved su­
perior performance with utilization rates of 43.7 %–77.6 %. These re­
sults clearly demonstrate the superior performance of MCBQ-modified 
batteries under comparable conditions. Notably, when the current 
density was restored to 0.2C following high-current density testing, the 
MCBQ-modified battery maintained a discharge specific capacity of 958 
mAh g−1, with an impressive capacity retention rate of 89.4 %. This 
observation confirms that MCBQ significantly enhances the battery's 
reversibility and stability. Comparative analyses of charge-discharge 
curves at varying current densities (Figs. 5d and S9a-b) revealed that 
MCBQ-modified batteries preserved the characteristic charge-discharge 
plateaus of lithium‑sulfur batteries even at elevated rates. In contrast, 
additive-free batteries exhibited substantial polarization voltages that 
intensified with increasing current density, confirming that MCBQ en­
hances sulfur redox kinetics. To assess long-term stability, cycling tests 
were conducted at 0.2C (Fig. 5e) and 1C (Fig. 5f). At 0.2C, the MCBQ- 
modified battery exhibited a minimal decay rate of 0.42 % after 120 
cycles, significantly outperforming the other batteries. These findings 
consistently demonstrate that MCBQ not only enhances discharge spe­
cific capacity but also substantially improves cycling stability. The cell 
performance was further assessed under high sulfur loading (6.0 mg 
cm−2), low electrolyte conditions (E/S = 5.0 μL mg−1), and a high 
current density of 0.5C. The results indicate that the discharge specific 
capacity of the reference cell exhibited an initial increase followed by a 
sharp decline. In the early stages, the sparse electrolyte failed to fully 
infiltrate the dense sulfur electrode, resulting in low specific capacity. As 
cycling progressed, the electrolyte gradually penetrated the electrode, 
activating previously unreacted sulfur and improving the utilization rate 
of active material, which led to a gradual increase in specific capacity. 
However, in later cycles, a large amount of polysulfides accumulated at 
the interface due to insufficient dissolution. The unconverted poly­
sulfides diffused to the lithium anode, causing loss of active material and 
corrosion of the lithium metal, ultimately leading to reduced coulombic 
efficiency and rapid capacity decay. In contrast, the cell with MCBQ 
additive delivered a high initial discharge specific capacity of 645.41 
mAh g−1 and maintained 501.08 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles, corre­
sponding to a capacity retention of 77.6 %. This significant improve­
ment is attributed to the ability of MCBQ to accelerate sulfur conversion 
and suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect. The LiCl interface layer 
formed by MCBQ effectively mitigates polysulfide shuttling and elec­
trolyte consumption under high sulfur loading, significantly enhancing 
cycling stability. As a result, no capacity fluctuations were observed, and 
a high capacity retention rate was achieved.

In summary, density functional theory (DFT) calculations (ΔG =

that insoluble intermediate 2 comprises quinone groups, C–S bonds, 
and Li–O bonds. This intermediate serves dual functions: first, as a 
conductive CEI layer adhering to the cathode surface (σMCBQ-Li2S6 = 2.5

minimal capacity decay (0.42 % per cycle) after 120 cycles at 0.2C, 
substantially improving battery stability. Thus, MCBQ demonstrates 
considerable potential as an additive for enhancing lithium‑sulfur bat­
tery performance, offering a promising solution to current challenges in 
this field.
