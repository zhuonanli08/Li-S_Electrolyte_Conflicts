Abstract
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising candidates for next-generation
energy
storage
systems,
but
practical
use
is
limited
by
polysulfide
(PS) shuttling and Li metal anode instability. Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is widely
used to mitigate these issues; however, its interfacial effects across the anode,
electrolyte, and cathode during operation are not fully understood. Here,
operando optical microscopy with a custom side-by-side cell enables simulta-
neous monitoring of the Li anode, liquid electrolyte, and sulfur cathode in a
single field of view under conditions with and without LiNO3. In the absence
of LiNO3, the Li surface undergoes rough stripping and fragmented, non-
coalescent deposition, accompanied by PS-induced corrosion and accumula-
tion of parasitic byproducts at the anode-electrolyte interface. Redness Inten-
sity (RI), introduced to quantify electrolyte-phase PS dynamics, indicates
sustained transport toward the anode and delayed conversion to elemental sul-
fur. By contrast, LiNO3 induces uniform Li stripping and the growth of aggre-
gated, interconnected deposits, while mitigating PS crossover and promoting
efficient sulfur crystallization at the cathode. Complementary SEM-EDS, UV–
vis, XPS, TXM, and CT analyses corroborate these observations. By elucidating
the multifunctional role of LiNO3, this study clarifies the interfacial dynamics
that govern Li–S battery performance.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
2.1
|
LiNO3 as electrolyte additive in
lithium–sulfur batteries
LiNO3 is widely adopted in Li–S batteries, playing a key
role in their development with its broad applicability.26–30
The electrochemical behavior of Li–S cells differs mark-
edly depending on the presence of LiNO3 (Figures 1A,B
and S1). With or without LiNO3, a typical two-plateau
discharge profile is observed, corresponding to the reduc-
tion of S8 to Li2S4, and subsequently to Li2S. During sub-
sequent
charging,
cells
without
LiNO3
exhibit
an
extended voltage plateau at 2.4 V (vs. Li+/Li), indicative
of PS shuttling. In this stage, soluble long-chain PS spe-
cies diffuse to the Li anode, where they are reduced to
short-chain PS and return to the cathode, repeating the
redox cycle. This shuttle effect prolongs charging, causes
active material loss, and reduces Coulombic efficiency
(CE).31 Remarkably, even at low concentrations, LiNO3
effectively suppresses the PS shuttle with CE up to
99.98%. This has motivated extensive studies of its mech-
anistic roles in improving cycling stability and reversibil-
ity, as summarized in Figure 1C. In 2009, Aurbach et al.
first reported that LiNO3 reacts at the Li surface to form
insoluble LixNOy and LixSOy species, yielding a stable
passivation layer that inhibits PS-induced side reac-
tions.32 Subsequent studies have further reported that
LiNO3 regulates the SEI, reducing gas evolution, PS shut-
tling,
and
self-discharge,
thereby
enhancing
sulfur
utilization.33–51 Xiong et al. demonstrated that a composi-
tionally uniform SEI formed in the presence of LiNO3
enables homogeneous Li+ transport and suppresses den-
drite growth.52 Using DFT calculations, Duangdangchote
et al. proposed that LixNOy clusters in the SEI can immo-
bilize PS species through dipole–dipole interactions,
thereby inhibiting parasitic reactions with Li metal.53
Computational studies have explored LiNO3 decomposi-
tion pathways and its strong interactions with PS species,
providing valuable insights into its complex roles in Li–S
systems.54
Beyond SEI regulation, LiNO3 also significantly alters
the solvation environment of the electrolyte. In the con-
ventional
electrolyte
consisting
of

 anion, which has a high donor
number (22.2 kcal mol1), preferentially coordinates with
Li+ and forms a NO3
-rich solvation shell.55 Based on its
strong electron-donating ability, NO3

2.2
|
Operando optical imaging for real-
time visualization of lithium–sulfur cells
For direct observation of Li anode, sulfur cathode, and
electrolyte, the electrodes are placed side-by-side with an
approximate spacing of 500 μm (Figures 2A and S2). The
bright metallic region at the bottom corresponds to the Li
metal anode, whereas the dark area at the top is the sul-
fur cathode on Al current collector. The interelectrode
gap is filled with liquid electrolyte, and the electrodes are
physically isolated by a separator and immobilized by
compressive sealing during assembly to prevent displace-
ment and electrolyte leakage. This optical cell is designed
for in situ observation of working Li–S cells, enabling
visualization of Li metal morphological changes, PS dis-
solution and diffusion, and sulfur crystallization within a
single field of view, although it does not exactly replicate
a conventional coin cell geometry. Figure 2B,C present
the electrochemical profile, along with the corresponding
operando optical images of a Li–S cell using base electro-
lyte containing LiNO3. Video S1 provides time-resolved
operando images. Figure 2C shows the optical images
taken at the red and blue circles marked on the voltage
profile. The D- and C-labels in each image indicate the
depth of discharge (DOD) and state of charge (SOC) dur-
ing the discharge and charge process, respectively. This
optical cell was cycled at 0.1 C within a voltage range of
1.7–2.8 V (vs. Li+/Li). Before the current was applied, the
electrolyte in the interelectrode gap remained bright and
transparent (D0). Upon discharge, Li began to dissolve,
while S8 was reduced to Li2S8 at the cathode. At 2.32 V
(D0.7), the first voltage plateau emerged, accompanied by
a brownish-red coloration of the electrolyte, indicating
the dissolution of long-chain, soluble PS from the
cathode surface. As the discharge progressed, these PSs
gradually diffused toward the anode through the liquid
electrolyte driven by a concentration gradient, yielding a
uniformly colored solution. Simultaneously, continuous
stripping of the Li anode was observed, and the reddish
coloration intensified, reaching a maximum near D17. At
the onset of the sloping voltage region (D20), the electro-
lyte shifted from brownish-red to orange and then to pale
yellow by D50, consistent with reduction of long-chain
PS to lower solubility, short-chain species.63,64 As the sec-
ond voltage plateau near 2.0 V emerged and discharge
approached completion (D100), the electrolyte progres-
sively returned toward its initial appearance, indicating
that most PS had migrated back to the cathode. Notably,
the Li surface remained smooth and uniform with
numerous fine pits after discharge—markedly different
from the severe pitting observed without LiNO3, as will
be discussed below. During the subsequent charge, Li
nucleated on these pits (C1). Up to C30, the voltage
remained around 2.26 V, while Li nuclei gradually grew
into larger particles. Meanwhile, Li2S was oxidized to
short-chain PSs, slightly darkening the electrolyte. As the
voltage increased, long-chain PS reappeared in the elec-
trolyte around C52 (2.4 V), followed by their oxidation
into elemental sulfur with gradual brightening of the
electrolyte. As the charge progressed, metallic Li deposits
continued to grow, and by the fully charged state at
C100,
large,
electrically
connected
Li
grains
were
observed without obvious formation of electrically iso-
lated dead Li. It is noted that the voltage profile differs
from that of a conventional coin cell due to wider elec-
trode spacing and excess electrolyte fully filling the
interelectrode gap, both affecting the electrochemical
response.

markedly different morphological evolution, as seen in

behavior during discharge was similar to that with
LiNO3. At D10, the electrolyte exhibited its darkest red-
dish color. As the voltage entered the sloping region at

corrosion.

Effect of LiNO3 on the
morphological evolution of Li metal in
lithium–sulfur cells
The influence of LiNO3 on the morphological evolution
of the Li metal anode in Li–S batteries is pronounced
during the stages of (i) stripping, (ii) nucleation, and sub-
sequent (iii) growth. Figure 4A highlights the surface
evolution at each stage in Li–S cells with and without
LiNO3. Each image is annotated with the specific capac-
ity at the corresponding point (e.g., D200 denotes
200 mA h g1 during discharge). During stripping, the Li
anode dissolves continuously, forming pits. At D200, in
the absence of LiNO3, these pits are deeper and larger,
yielding a rough, uneven surface. By contrast, with
LiNO3, numerous smaller stripping sites form and the
surface remains relatively smooth as discharge proceeds.
In the early stage of charging, Li begins to deposit on the
anode surface. The subsequent evolution differs markedly
depending on the presence of LiNO3. Without additive,
from C10 to C30, the surface becomes covered with fine
Li particles that remain discrete and loosely packed,
resembling fine sand grains with little coalescence. In
contrast, with LiNO3, Li grows by adhering to pre-
existing nuclei, producing aggregated, cohesive struc-
tures. This distinction becomes even more pronounced
during the growth phase. As charging proceeds, Li
continues to deposit; however, without LiNO3, the non-
agglomerated particles stack irregularly, pushing previ-
ously deposited Li into the electrolyte and causing sub-
stantial active Li loss. This behavior persists until long-
chain PS species reappear and react with Li. With LiNO3,
no Li detachment is observed. Although the deposits may
appear more mossy or bulky in optical images, they are
well-connected and electrochemically active, growing
continuously from nucleation sites and maintaining inti-
mate contact. This densely interconnected morphology
minimizes Li detachment, prevents Li loss into the elec-
trolyte, and ensures efficient ion-transport pathways,
thereby supporting higher Li utilization and stable,
reversible cycling. To further demonstrate the role of
LiNO3 in stabilizing Li metal anodes in the presence
of PS, we compared the behavior of Li metal under four
different conditions: with and without LiNO3, and with
and without PSs. For the PS control, LijjLi symmetric
cells (without PSs, Figure 4B (i)) were compared with Li–
S cells (with PSs, Figure 4B (ii)). Figure 4B (i) presents
the Li plating on previously stripped surfaces in the LijjLi
symmetric cells with and without LiNO3. Images were
acquired at three representative points—P1 (before plat-
ing), P2 (after 3 h), and P3 (after 6 h)—as marked on the
voltage profiles. The LijjLi cells were cycled at a current

deposits in LijjLi cells. To gain further insight into Li cor-

of Li deposits exposed to PS were monitored. In Li–S
cells, the current was interrupted at 2.5 V during charge,
and the cell was held at open circuit to track spontaneous
PS-induced corrosion of pre-formed Li deposits. As
shown in Figure 4B (ii), images were obtained at the ini-
tial point (O1), after 30 min (O2), and after 1 h (O3).
Without LiNO3, the Li surface at O1 was already covered
with a byproduct layer, and areas marked in red show
previously metallic active Li undergoing PS-induced reac-
tion over time. By contrast, when LiNO3 was present, the
Li deposits observed at O1 remained unchanged in mor-

active Li was preserved throughout the OCV period.
These operando images reveal distinct differences in mor-

LiNO3 and point to a critical interplay between LiNO3
and dissolved PS in determining Li growth and stability.
The efficacy of LiNO3 was further corroborated across an
expanded voltage range (Figure S5).

Quantitative analysis of PS species
in the electrolyte
Operando optical imaging enabled real-time visualization
of dynamic electrolyte coloration associated with dissolu-
tion and diffusion of PS species during charge and dis-
charge. Figure 5A,B, show the electrolyte color evolution
at corresponding voltages during discharge and charge

tems present comparable coloration trends, and detailed
comparison is limited by the inherently qualitative nature
of visual observation. To quantify spatial–temporal PS
dynamics, a colorimetric image parameter, Redness
Intensity (RI), was introduced to track PS evolution dur-
ing cycling. Electrolyte coloration can arise from changes
in dissolved PS concentration, chain-length distribution,
or both.67 RI was calculated as the red channel fraction
relative to the total RGB intensity in a given region and
normalized by setting the initial value to zero prior to
detectable
PS
dissolution.
Five
red-marked
regions
between the electrodes in Figure 5A,B, were selected for
analysis. Each region was divided into 10 equal sections
from section 1 (cathode side) to section 10 (anode side),
and the average RI of each section was monitored
throughout the discharge–charge process. Figure 5C,E,
present RI evolution in section 1 of region 1 in the
absence and presence of LiNO3, respectively, together
with the corresponding electrochemical profiles. At the
onset of discharge, RI sharply rose on the first voltage
plateau due to the dissolution of long-chain PS. As the
voltage entered the sloping region, further reduction to
short-chain PS led to a gradual RI decrease, followed by a
rapid drop on the second plateau to a minimum at the
end of discharge. Upon charging, RI increased again with
the formation of short-chain PS, then rose sharply
beyond 2.4 V as long-chain PS was regenerated and
finally decreased toward the end of charge consistent
with the formation of elemental sulfur. This analysis was
extended from section 1 to section 10 to probe spatial var-
iations across the electrolyte and repeated over the five
regions (regions 1–5) to assess reproducibility and posi-
tional consistency. As shown in Figure 5D,F, location-

less of LiNO3. In all regions, RI changes were steeper in

through
the
electrolyte.

Effect of LiNO3 on sulfur formation
behavior
As an electrolyte additive, LiNO3 affects not only the Li
metal anode but also the distribution of PSs within the
electrolyte, which in turn governs sulfur formation at
the cathode. Figure 6A,B, present the conversion of solu-
ble Li2Sn to crystalline S8 during the final stage of charg-
ing (50%–100% SOC). Each image corresponds to the
cathode surface at the SOC indicated on the voltage pro-
file. The XRD patterns of charged cathodes confirm the
formation of crystalline sulfur in both the LiNO3-free and
LiNO3-containing electrolytes (Figure S10). In both cases,
sulfur nucleation began at 2.45 V. Without LiNO3
(Figure 6A), prolonged shuttling delays conversion to S8
and slows crystallization. This extended process allowed
sustained sulfur growth, forming larger, more crystalline
particles. From S75 onward, bulky crystals appeared,
growing preferentially on pre-existing nuclei rather than
forming uniformly across the surface. These crystals are
irregular in shape and size, aggregating into coarse,
aggregated domains due to excessive growth in specific
directions. In contrast, with LiNO3, the voltage rose
sharply near the end of charge, indicating accelerated
conversion. Rapid crystallization promotes abundant
nucleation sites while limiting individual particle growth,
producing densely packed, uniformly distributed fine sul-
fur domains over the cathode. This behavior was corrobo-
rated in the coin cell configurations where electrodes face
each
other.
Transmission
x-ray
microscopy
(TXM)
tracked sulfur formation during charging (Figure S11).
Consistent with the optical images, the LiNO3-free cell
exhibited heterogeneous nucleation confined to specific
cathode regions, forming large, irregularly shaped crys-
tals (50–150 μm). With LiNO3, crystals (<20 μm) were
uniformly distributed across the cathode, reflecting regu-
lated crystallization and redistribution. X-ray CT images
taken after full charge further confirm LiNO3-mediated
regulation of sulfur crystals at the electrode scale.
Figure 6C–E show 3D reconstructions of the pristine
cathode and charged cathodes in LiNO3-free and LiNO3-
containing electrolytes, respectively. Without LiNO3, sul-
fur deposits are highly uneven, with bright yellow spots
indicating localized crystal aggregation. By contrast, with
LiNO3, sulfur is more evenly distributed with signifi-
cantly smaller particles, highlighting the role of LiNO3 in
guiding sulfur crystallization and spatial redistribution.
Figure 7 schematically summarizes the comprehensive
effect of LiNO3 in Li–S cells during the discharge and
subsequent charge.

CONCLUSION
This study elucidates the multifaceted role of LiNO3 in
Li–S batteries using operando optical microscopy to visu-
alize the Li-metal anode, liquid electrolyte, and sulfur
cathode in real time. Without LiNO3, the Li surface
exhibits rough stripping and discrete, non-coalescent
deposition, accompanied by PS-induced corrosion and
accumulation of byproducts. Furthermore, prolonged PS
migration delays sulfur crystallization and yields hetero-
geneous sulfur deposits. By contrast, LiNO3 enables uni-
form
Li
stripping
and
compact,
interconnected
deposition, mitigates PS crossover, and promotes rapid,
finely dispersed sulfur crystallization. Quantitative image
analysis with the RI parameter confirms that LiNO3
limits long-chain PS transport toward the anode, localiz-
ing redox reactions near the cathode. SEM, UV–vis, XPS,
TXM, and x-ray CT corroborate these observations,
revealing distinct differences in Li morphology, SEI
chemistry, and sulfur formation behavior depending on
the presence of LiNO3. Overall, LiNO3 simultaneously
regulates redox processes and stabilizes interfaces at both
electrodes, highlighting its multifaceted role. Moreover,
operando optical microscopy
offers direct, real-time
access to multiple-interface evolution, thereby enabling
detailed tracking of reaction pathways under operating
conditions
and
complementing
conventional
characterization.
