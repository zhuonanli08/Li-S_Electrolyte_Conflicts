Solvent-Dictated Lithium Sulfur Redox Reactions: An Operando UV−
vis Spectroscopic Study
Qingli Zou and Yi-Chun Lu*
Electrochemical Energy and Interfaces Laboratory, Department of Mechanical and Automation Engineering, The Chinese University
of Hong Kong, Shatin, N.T. 999077, Hong Kong SAR, China

S Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Fundamental understanding of solvent’s inﬂuence on Li−S redox
reactions is required for rational design of electrolyte for Li−S batteries. Here we
employ operando UV−vis spectroscopy to reveal that Li−S redox reactions in high-
donor-number solvents, for example, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), undergo
multiple electrochemical and chemical reactions involving S8
2−, S6
2−, S4
2−, and
S3
•−, where S3
•−is the most stable and dominant reaction intermediate. In low-
donor-number solvents, for example, 1,3-dioxolane:1,2-dimethoxyethane, the
dominant reaction intermediate, is found to be S4
2−. The stability of these main
polysulﬁde intermediates determines the reaction rates of the disproportionation/
dissociation/recombination of polysulﬁdes and thereby aﬀects the reaction rates of
the Li−S batteries. As an example, we show that dimethylformamide, a high-donor-
number solvent, which exhibits stronger stabilization of S3
•−compared with DMSO,
signiﬁcantly reduces Li−S cell polarization compared with DMSO. Our study
reveals solvent-dependent Li−S reaction pathways and highlights the role of
polysulﬁde stability in the eﬃciency of Li−S batteries.

two to three times higher speciﬁc energy than conven-

Implications in the Lithium−Sulfur Batteries. We have
previously showed that the electrode kinetics of sulfur
reduction and oxidation is much faster than the current density
applied in the Li−S batteries in both DMSO and DOL:DME.25
We suggested that the rate limitation of the Li−S batteries lies
in the disproportionation/dissociation/recombination reactions
of polysulﬁdes to form electrochemically reducible (redox-
active) polysulﬁdes.25,53 Therefore, solvents that facilitate the
formation of redox-active polysulﬁdes (e.g., S8
2−and S3
•−in
DMSO or S8
2−and S4
2−in DOL:DME) may improve the
reaction rates of the Li−S cells. Here we investigate how the
degree of stabilization of S3
•−inﬂuences the polarization of the
Li−S cells by examining Li−S catholyte cells with two high-DN
solvents, namely, DMSO and DMF, where S3
•−is expected
(and shown here) to be the main reaction intermedi-
ates.19,23,54,55
First, we characterize the stability of S3
•−in both DMSO and
DMF via UV−vis spectrometer. Figure 4a shows the UV−vis
spectra of chemically synthesized 2.0 mM nominal S6
2−in
DMSO and DMF. As expected, both solvents show large
absorbance at 617 nm, indicating the formation of S3
•−in both
solvents. Interestingly, the absorbance of S3

•−is more

cell reactions in a Li−S catholyte cell, where 4.0 mM S8 in

(Figure 4b), the same amount of elemental sulfur (i.e., 4.0 mM)

the stability of S3
•−(in high-DN solvents) and S4
2−(in low-
DN) to the polarization characteristics and eﬃciency of the Li−
S batteries.
We note that donor number should only be used as a ﬁrst
pass ﬁlter to identify which solvents will have a high tendency
to solubilize the trisulfur species. Other solvent properties such
as viscosity, dielectric constant, and sulfur solubility may play a
role on the redox behavior in addition to the donor
number.24,25 Dielectric constant describes the solvent’s
tendency to partly cancel the ﬁeld strength of the electric
ﬁeld of a charged particle immersed in it or its ability to reduce
the solute’s eﬀective internal charge; however, the inﬂuence of
dielectric constant typically cannot be easily separated from the
inﬂuence of donor number because high-DN solvents typically
have high dielectric constant (ε) (e.g., DNDMSO = 29.8, εDMSO =
47/DNDMA = 28, εDMA = 38/DNDME = 20, εDME = 7.2).56 To
examine the role of dielectric constant on the stability of the
trisulfur species and distinguish it from the inﬂuence of DN, we
conducted UV−vis measurements on the polysulﬁde (S8
2−) in
an alternative solvent, triethanolamine (TEA), which has
exceptionally high DN (61) but low dielectric constant
(2.42)56 (Figure S7). Interestingly, we found that the trisulfur
radical cannot be stabilized in TEA, suggesting that moderate
or high dielectric constant is required to stabilize the trisulfur
radical, even for high-DN solvents. Clearly, other solvent
property such as viscosity, and sulfur solubility may play a role
in the redox behavior in addition to the donor number.
Additionally, trisulfur radical was found in the work of
Barchasz et al. in TEGDME31 but not in this study in
DOL:DME. Considering that these ether solvents have largely
similar donor number and dielectric constant (DNTEGDME = 17,
εTEGDME = 7.8, DNDOL = 18, εDOL = 7.3, DNDME = 24, εDME =
7.1),56 it suggests that other factors, in addition to donor
number and dielectric constant, must play a critical role in the
stability of trisulfur.24 We note that solvent’s donor property
inﬂuences the structure, stability, and behavior of the cation
(e.g., solvent solvated-Li+), which then inﬂuences the behavior
of the anions (e.g., trisulfur radical). The donor number is one
way to describe the donor property of solvents. It is deﬁned as
the negative enthalpy value for the 1:1 adduct formation
between a Lewis base and the standard Lewis acid antimony
pentachloride (SbCl5) in dilute solution in the noncoordinating
solvent 1,2-dichloroethane;57 however, it is also recognized that
DN does not consider speciﬁc interactions between certain
individual donor−acceptor species.58 According to Viktor
Gutmann in Coordination Chemistry in Non-Aqueous Solutions,58
it was explained that the steric factor becomes important when
small ions (e.g., Li+ in this study) are to be coordinated by
several large or bulky solvent ligands and that the results
obtained may not be in agreement with the donor number of
the particular solvent.58 An example was given that the
interactions of the VO+2 ion are stronger with acetonitrile
(DN = 14.1) than with propanediol-1,2-carbonate (DN = 15.1)
and trimethylphosphate (DN = 23), which is not in agreement
with their donor number.58 This is because the coordination
compounds of acetonitrile and the cation have octahedral
symmetry, while those with propanediol-1,2-carbonate and
trimethylphosphate show a lower symmetry with higher π-
bonding contributions to the VO bond58 (i.e., weaker
interaction). Inspired by this example, we believe that the
diﬀerence in coordination structure of DME/DOL-solvated Li+
(Li+-DME/DOL) and TEGDME-solvated Li+ (i.e., Li+-
TEGDME) gives diﬀerent level of stabilization to the trisulfur
radical despite having similar DN. Consistently, Abraham et
al.59 and Olsher60 suggested that TEGDME solvates the Li+
cation by forming a caged structure around Li+, which is more
stable than the DME-solvated Li+. Accordingly, we hypothesize
that the trisulfur radical is better shielded inside the caged (Li+-
TEGDME) complex compared with the (Li+-DME) complex,
which is a natural analogy to superoxide radical (O2
−•) in Li-
oxygen chemistry reported by Abraham et al.59 To directly
compare the stability of the trisulfur in various ethers, we
conducted UV−vis measurement of dissolved nominal ‘S8
2−’ in
DME (monoglyme), diglyme, triglyme, and tetraglyme
(TEGDME); all have similar DN and dielectric constant
(Table S1). As shown in Figure S8, the intensity of the trisulfur
in DME is lower than that in diglyme and triglyme, followed by
tetraglyme. This provides a direct experimental correlation
between the stability of trisulfur radical and the structure of the
glyme, which resonates well with the improved stability of
solvated Li+ with higher number of solvating oxygen atoms of
the ethers.61−63 This indicates that, in addition to donor
number and dielectric constant, the stability and conforma-
tional structure of the solvent−cation solvating complex will
play an important role in the stability of trisulfur radical in the
Li−S redox reactions. Future work on systematically controlling
various solvents’ properties and examining how each parameter
inﬂuences the reaction kinetics/behavior will provide further
insights into the solvent development for Li−S batteries.
In summary, we examined Li−S redox reactions via operando
UV−vis spectroscopy and revealed solvent-dependent Li−S
redox pathways. We report that Li−S reactions in high-DN
solvents, for example, DMSO, undergo a series of electro-
chemical and chemical reactions involving S8
2−, S6
2−, S4
2−, and
S3
•−, where S3
•−is the most stable and dominant reaction
intermediate. In contrast, Li−S reactions in low-DN solvents,
for example, DOL:DME, mainly involve S4
2−. We investigated
the inﬂuence of the stability of the major polysulﬁde
intermediates on the Li−S cell reactions. We show that
DMF, a high-DN having stronger stabilization of S3
•−
compared with DMSO, signiﬁcantly reduces the Li−S cell
polarization compared with DMSO. This suggests that solvents
that facilitate the formation of redox-active polysulﬁdes can
