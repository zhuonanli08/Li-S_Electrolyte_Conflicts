Highly Reversible Lithium-Metal Anode and Lithium−Sulfur
Batteries Enabled by an Intrinsic Safe Electrolyte
Jiahang Chen, Huijun Yang, Xuan Zhang, Jingyu Lei, Huiming Zhang, Huanhuan Yuan, Jun Yang,
Yanna Nuli,
and Jiulin Wang*
Department of Chemical Engineering, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai 200240, China

S Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Rechargeable lithium-metal batteries have
gained signiﬁcant attention as potential candidates of energy
storage systems; however, severe safety issues including
ﬂammable electrolyte and dendritic lithium formation hinder
their further practical application. In this work, we develop a
novel intrinsic ﬂame-retardant electrolyte, which enables a
stable and dendrite-free cycling with lithium plating/stripping
Coulombic eﬃciency of up to 99.1% over 500 cycles. Raman
spectra indicate that no free molecular solvent exists, and X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy reveals the LiF-rich interphase
on the Li-metal anode. When coupled with sulfurized
pyrolyzed poly(acrylonitrile) cathode, it shows a benign
electrochemical reversibility with the areal capacity of up to
3.41 mAh cm−2 after 70 cycles. To further check its compatibility with sulfur cathode, a higher sulfur content (51.6%) is
examined with the areal capacity of 3.92 mAh cm−2 and sulfur utilization of 81.7%. This work provides an alternative for safe
and high-performance Li−S batteries via a novel electrolyte strategy.
KEYWORDS: ﬂame-retardant electrolyte, lithium-metal anode, Coulombic eﬃciency, dendrite-free, lithium−sulfur battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Recently, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been widely used in
portable electronic products and hybrid electric vehicles.1,2
However, the development of LIBs has gradually reached the
eventual maturation limited by the energy density (300 Wh
kg−1).3,4 Accordingly, rechargeable lithium-metal batteries
(LMBs) have attracted extensive attention as the next
generation of high-energy storage system due to the ultrahigh
speciﬁc capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and the lowest redox
potential (−3.04 V vs standard hydrogen electrode) of Li,
which is regarded as the “holy grail” in the battery
community.5 Numerous strategies have been implemented to
modify the electrochemical behavior of LMBs.6−8 However,
safety issues remain to restrain their further development and
practical application:9,10 (1) the volatile and ﬂammable nature
of the conventional electrolyte solvents like esters or
carbonates cause them to easily ignite, resulting in thermal
runaway and explosion; and (2) the Li metal is highly reactive
and forms undesirable lithium dendrite to induce short-circle.
Besides, detrimental side reactions occur in the unstable
interface between Li and electrolyte during repeated cycles,
which induce a low Coulombic eﬃciency (CE).11,12
Electrolytes play one of the vital roles in aﬀecting the
performance of LMBs,13,14 but conventional electrolytes such
as ether-based electrolytes and carbonate electrolytes are
highly volatile and ﬂammable and can be ignited at room
temperature, attributed to the low ﬂash points and boiling
points.15 Under extreme conditions, like over voltage, over
current, and over temperature, the electrolyte may break down
to release ﬂammable gas and the build-up pressure induces a
possible rupture. Once the ﬂammable gas comes in contact
with the oxygen released from cathode breakdown or in air, it
is likely to burn or even explode.16−18
Many diﬀerent strategies have been employed to address the
safety problems, and the research on safe electrolytes appears
to be eﬀective.19,20 Solid-state electrolytes have been proved to
be nonﬂammable, but they suﬀer from native limitations of
solid-state electrolytes, such as the low ionic conductivity at
room temperature and high interface resistance between the
electrolyte and electrode.21−24 The ionic liquid electrolytes
have also attracted abundant attention for their thermal
stability as well as excellent ionic conductivity, but they show
high viscosity and poor compatibility with conventional
electrode materials.25,26 Meanwhile, numerous phosphate
solvents have been studied as additives and co-solvents to
form an eﬃcient and eﬀective ﬂame-retardant electrolyte, such
as triethyl phosphate (TEP) and27,28 trimethyl phosphate
(TMP).29,30
It has been demonstrated that high-concentration electro-
lytes can improve the interfacial stability owing to their unique

More importantly, the high-concentration electrolytes can

Moreover, inert solvents with minimal eﬀect on Li+ solvation
were used as co-solvents to improve the viscosity and ionic

According to the reported literature, artiﬁcial Li3PO4 layer
possesses a high Young’s modulus and an enhanced Li+
transport capacity, while employing phosphate solvents directly
may achieve the same eﬀect.37 Yamada’s group38 reported the
use of “concentrated + diluted” lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)-
amide (LiFSA)/TMP−1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetra-
ﬂuoropropyl (HFE) to achieve both nonﬂammability and
excellent cycle performance of carbonaceous negative electro-
des. Yang et al.39 employed TEP solvent to form an intrinsic
ﬂame-retardant electrolyte system for lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries. A comparison of the two ﬂame-retardant solvents
TEP and TMP shows that the latter has a higher dielectric
constant (21.6),40 which guarantees better physical property
like higher ionic conductivity of the electrolyte. Moreover,
TMP may react preferentially with lithium based on the
molecular structure.
Inspired by this idea, herein we reported a novel ﬂame-
retardant electrolyte consisting of saturated lithium bis-
(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in a mixture of TMP and
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl (HFE) in
which the LiFSI concentration ranged from 3.6 to 1.3 M
diluted by HFE as the co-solvent (corresponding to TMP/
HFE volume ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4). Particularly, the
1.6 M LiFSI/TMP−HFE (1:3) electrolyte can enable a high-
CE (>99%), dendrite-free, and stable cycling of lithium-metal
anodes. Furthermore, coupled with common and high-loading
cathode, it exhibited excellent cycling stability and rate
performance. Especially the high-loading cathodes with a
higher sulfur content displayed remarkable areal capacity,
which profoundly favors the practical application of the Li−S
battery.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Electrolyte Preparation and Characterization. The high-
concentration electrolytes and proposed electrolytes were prepared by
dissolving LiFSI into the solvent mixtures of TMP and HFE in the
volume ratios of 1:0 (pure TMP), 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4, respectively,
until they were saturated. Then, the corresponding molar concen-
trations were calculated as 5.0, 3.6, 2.3, 1.6, and 1.3 M. All electrolytes
were stored in glass bottles. The pure trimethyl phosphate (TMP)
was made by distillation from a lower-concentration TMP
commercially purchased from Aladdin Shanghai China and then
quickly transferred to an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. 1,1,2,2-Tetraﬂuor-
oethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl (HFE) (purity > 99.9%) was kindly
provided by Braunway Technology Co., Ltd. TMP and HFE were
dried using 4 Å molecular sieves prior to use. The conventional
battery-grade electrolyte of 1.0 M lithium hexaﬂuorophosphate
(LiPF6) in a mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC)/dimethyl carbonate
(DMC) (1:1 by volume) was purchased directly from Company
Xiaoyuan. Furthermore, all chemicals were stored and experiments
conducted in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (MB-10 compact, MBRAUN) with
almost no O2 and H2O (<1 ppm).
The ignition tests were carried out using a glass ﬁber inﬁltrated by a
conventional carbonated electrolyte and proposed electrolytes. The
ﬂammability of diﬀerent electrolytes was contrasted through the
phenomena when ignited as well as after the removal of 1exogenous
ﬁre.
Raman spectra were recorded at room temperature with a DXR
Raman microscope (Thermo Scientiﬁc) using a HeNe laser with a
wavelength of 780 nm as the excitation source with microspatial
resolution of <2 μm. The tested electrolytes consisted of pure TMP,
pure HFE, 1.3 M LiFSI/TMP, 5.0 M LiFSI/TMP, 3.6 M LiFSI/
TMP−HFE (1:1), 2.3 M LiFSI/TMP−HFE (1:2), 1.6 M LiFSI/
TMP−HFE (1:3), and 1.3 M LiFSI/TMP−HFE (1:4); all of the
samples were sealed in a capillary after the removal of the Ar-ﬁlled
glovebox.
The wettability tests were carried out via dropping the same
amounts (4 drops) of conventional carbonated electrolyte, high-
concentration electrolyte, and experimental electrolyte onto the PE
separator. Then, contrast the degree of inﬁltration and the time
needed.
2.2. Sulfurized Pyrolyzed Poly(acrylonitrile) (S@pPAN)
Cathode Preparation. The sulfur contents of S@pPAN cathode
depended on the time of pyrolysis process, where the sulfur content of
44.6 and 51.6% corresponded to the pyrolysis time at 300 °C for 450
and 360 min. All the cathodes were prepared by the mixture of S@
pPAN, guar gum, polyacrylic acid, and super P at the proportion of
8:0.5:0.5:1 by mass. The electrode used carbon-coated aluminum as a
current collector and was coated by a common doctor-blade coating
method. The mass loading depended on the coated thickness. Then,
after drying at 70 °C under vacuum for 6 h to totally remove water,
the electrode was cut into pieces with the circular disks of 1.0 cm2 and
sequentially dried at the same condition again. Additionally, all the
electrodes were pressed under 10 MPa before use.
2.3. Electrochemical Measurements. All CR2016 coin-type
cells used in this work were assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox and
tested under room-temperature condition. Li|Cu cells consisted of a
Li foil (thickness: 100 μm) and Cu foil as electrodes and ENTEK
ET20-26 (PE, thickness: 20 μm) as a separator. To investigate the
Coulombic eﬃciency, the Li|Cu cells were tested under the constant
current density of 0.2 and 0.5 mA cm−2 for 2 or 5 h, respectively, and
then the same stripping current density was applied until the cutoﬀ
voltage of 1.2 V vs Li/Li+ was achieved. The Li|Li symmetric cells
were assembled with the Li foil used both as the working and counter
electrodes. The deposition/stripping test was conducted under 0.5
and 1.0 mA cm−2 for 1 or 2 h, respectively. The Li−S battery used the
Li foil as the anode and the as-prepared sulfur electrode as the
cathode. The electrolyte/sulfur ratio was controlled at 20 mL gsulfur
−1
and negative/positive capacity ratio at 4.8. The galvanostatic
discharge/charge tests were carried out on a LANDCT 2001A cell
test system (Wuhan, China) in the voltage range of 1.0−3.0 V (vs Li/
Li+). The alternating current impedance spectroscopy was measured
using AUTOLAB PGSTAT302N (Metrohm) in the frequency
ranging from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz with an amplitude of 5 mV.
2.4. Electrode Characterizations. A DDB-303A conductivity
meter (INESA INSTRUMENT) was used to measure the
conductivity of the electrolytes at room temperature. The morphology
of Li deposition on Cu foils was observed using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) (FEI Nova Nano-scanning electron microscope).
Before the examination, the Li|Cu coin cells were disassembled in an
argon-ﬁlled glovebox and then the obtained Cu foils were immersed
in dimethoxyethane thoroughly to remove the remaining salt. It is
noted that the dried Li deposition on the Cu foils must be sealed in an
Ar-ﬁlled container and transferred quickly into the SEM equipment to
avoid oxidation. The surface composition of the electrode cycled in
1.0 M LiPF6/EC−DMC (1:1) and 1.6 M LiFSI/TMP−HFE (1:3)
electrolyte was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
using a Kratos Axis ultraDLD spectrometer (Kratos AnalyticalA
Shimadzu Group Company) with a monochromatic Al Kα source
(1486.6 eV) under ultrahigh vacuum.
2.5. Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations. The
density functional theory (DFT) with the LDA/VWM operator was
employed in the dmol3 method in Materials Studio 7.0. The set was
used for C, H, O, and P elements, which is component in both TMP
and TEP. Visualization of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(LUMO) was done with Materials Studio View.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To examine the nonﬂammability of the proposed electrolyte, a
direct ignition test was carried out. The incombustible glass
ﬁbers, which absorbed enough conventional carbonated
electrolyte and experimental electrolytes, were ignited directly
to compare the ﬂammability. As shown in Figure 1a,
conventional carbonated electrolyte burned up as soon as
ignited and did not stop burning even after the removal of
exogenous ﬁre. In contrast, the experimental electrolyte
showed no ﬂame burning and a distinct white smoke when
ignited. The mechanism lies in the released phosphate radical
of TMP after gasiﬁcation can trap the hydrogen and hydroxyl
radicals to terminate the chain reaction in the ﬂame zone.25,41
Furthermore, in comparison with the 5.0 M LiFSI/TMP
electrolyte (0.58 mS cm−1), the 1.6 M LiFSI/TMP-3HFE
electrolyte exhibited a higher ionic conductivity (1.36 mS
cm−1), greatly reduced viscosity, and improved wettability.
Moreover, to illustrate the benign wettability of the proposed
electrolytes, the wettability test was carried out via dropping
the same amounts (4 drops) of electrolytes of the conventional
carbonated electrolyte, high-concentration electrolyte, and
experimental electrolyte onto the PE separator. As shown in
Figure S1, the high-concentration electrolyte exhibited a poor
wettability ascribed to the pretty high viscosity, whereas the
experimental electrolyte delivered remarkable wettability as the
separator was completely inﬁltrated immediately.
Raman spectra were recorded to analyze the solvation
structures of pure TMP, pure HFE, 1.3 M LiFSI/TMP, 5.0 M
LiFSI/TMP, and LiFSI/TMP−HFE in diﬀerent salt concen-
trations. As shown in Figure 1b, the free TMP molecules
displayed absorption peaks at 736 and 752 cm−1, which were
assigned to the stretching vibration of the P−O−C group in
TMP.42 The diluted 1.3 M LiFSI/TMP electrolyte showed
broad peaks between 726 and 760 cm−1 owing to the fact that
most of TMP molecules existed in a free state.32 However,
when the salt concentration increased to 5.0 M, the vibration
bands shifted up to higher wavenumbers and the vibration
band at 752 cm−1 disappeared, indicating that most TMPs
were coordinating with Li+ rather than in a free state. After
being diluted by HFE, the electrolytes displayed the same
vibration band at 1225 cm−1 (the stretching vibration of SO
in uncoordinated FSI−), revealing that the Li+-coordinated
TMP solvation structure was well-preserved after being diluted
by HFE.39,43 In addition, the consistent presence of the 594
cm−1 vibration band indicated that HFE did not engage in the
solvation of Li ions. Consequently, the diluted LiFSI/TMP−
HFE electrolytes possessed the localized high-concentration
LiFSI/TMP pairs and sequentially enabled the unique
functionalities, similar to those of the high-concentration
electrolyte and stable with the lithium-metal anodes.
To investigate the unique electrochemical performances of
these electrolytes, Li|Cu cells were tested using a relatively
accurate approach, consisting of one cycle for passivation, nine
cycles in a ﬁxed capacity, and ﬁnally an exhaustive stripping
(Figures 2a and S2).44 Compared with the low CE (77.6%) of
the conventional carbonated electrolyte, the CE of 5.0 M
LiFSI/TMP reached as high as 94.5%, attributed to the high-
concentration LiFSI/TMP pairs. More signiﬁcantly, all the
diluted LiFSI/TMP−HFE electrolytes exhibited obviously
improved CEs of nearly 99.1%, which conﬁrmed the excellent
reductive stability against Li (Figure S2a).
Besides the high CE, long-term cycling stability of lithium-
metal anodes is also vital for rechargeable LMBs. The
evolution of voltage proﬁles and CEs of Li|Cu cells during Li
plating/stripping was further examined at a current density of
0.2 mA cm−2 (Figures 2b and S3). For the conventional
electrolyte, the CE was generally low (<88%) and declined
rapidly after 80 cycles, ascribed to the existence of a large
amount of free solvent molecules and aggressive parasitic
reactions with the highly reactive lithium metal. In contrast, the
Li|Cu cells using high-concentration electrolyte showed a
higher CE of 95.2% in the former cycles but became wavering

Meanwhile, the interfacial reaction kinetics of the cells were
investigated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).
The impedance spectra for the Li|Cu cells using diﬀerent
electrolytes before and after cycling are presented in Figure
3a,b, respectively. Before cycling, the Li|Cu cells using
concentrated electrolyte showed a larger electrolyte resistance
and interfacial charge transfer resistance than that using
conventional and dilute electrolytes due to the high salt
concentration and high viscosity. After cycling for 25 cycles at
a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 with the areal capacity of 0.4
mAh cm−2, the Li|Cu cells using conventional electrolyte
displayed an increased interfacial resistance, indicating the
detrimental parasitic eﬀect and an undesirable deposition on
the Li metal surface. In contrast, the cells using dilute
electrolytes deliver similar and even lower interfacial resistance
owing to the formation of a thinner and more robust solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) ﬁlm on the surface of the lithium-
metal anode. The results demonstrate that with the dilution of
HFE, the proposed electrolytes signiﬁcantly improved the
interfacial reaction kinetics, thereby enhancing the electro-
chemical performance of LMBs.
The Li metal deposited on Cu was observed by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figures 4a−c and S5,
in all of the TMP-based electrolytes, lithium depositions were
even and smooth, whereas highly porous and loose structures
with extensive dendritic Li growth were observed in conven-
tional electrolyte. The most striking diﬀerence came up when
using the 1.6 M LiFSI/TMP−HFE. Instead of detrimental
dendrites, much bigger and more uniform Li particles appeared
(Figure 4c), which could greatly mitigate the possibility of
penetrating through the separator. The robust SEI of lithium
anode, which will be further discussed by XPS shielded the
bulk Li against the electrolyte attack and restrained the
interfacial reactions with electrolytes, sequentially contributing
greatly to the high CE, long-term cycling, and safety of battery.
The long-term stability and compatibility against the
lithium-metal anode were further investigated using Li|Li
symmetric cells at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. The Li|Li
cell with conventional electrolyte showed a rapidly increased
voltage polarization over 0.6 V, which led to cell failure in less
than 1000 h. In contrast, the Li|Li cells with the proposed
electrolyte showed an overwhelming cycling stability for 1600
h with a relatively low electrode polarization of less than 0.2 V
(Figure S6a). Furthermore, the superiority became more
obvious as the current density increased to 1 mA cm−2 (Figure
S6b). The Li|Li cells with the conventional electrolyte broke
down quickly in less than 100 h, while the proposed electrolyte
showed signiﬁcant stability over 350 h. Besides, the rate
performance was also studied up to high current density
(Figure S6c). Although the proposed electrolyte held an
inferior ionic conductivity (nearly an order of magnitude)
compared with conventional electrolyte, the battery still
exhibited stable cycles under 4.0 mA cm−2 within the limited
polarization ascribed to the speciﬁc local coordination
structure and the formed LiF-rich SEI. When the current
density returned, the polarization also recovered totally,
revealing the benign sustainability of the high current density.
Overall, the excellent long-term stability and limited polar-
ization increase represented an eﬃciently improved interfacial

To further ascertain the mechanism, XPS analysis was
performed to study the chemical composition of the SEI layer
formed on the Li-metal anodes. The contents of Li, C, O, S, N,
P, and F were tested (Figure S7a), while the selected elements
of S, N, P, and F were summarized. The proposed electrolyte
showed unique S 2p and N 1 s spectra, which were absent in
the conventional electrolyte (Figure S7b,c). The S 2p spectra
presented peaks corresponding to Li2S, S−N, and SO at
160.7, 166.9, and 168.7 eV, respectively.45−47 The N 1 s peak
at 398.1 eV was most likely from the N−SOx species generated
by the incomplete decomposition of FSI−anions, and the peak
at 396.3 eV was attributed to the Li−N species.47−49
Moreover, the P 2p and F 1 s spectra in Figure 4d,e show
various SEI layer components using diﬀerent electrolytes.
Using the conventional electrolyte, the P 2p spectra presented
peaks corresponding to LixPOyFz at 133.6 eV and LixPFy at
137.3 eV, which could originate from the side eﬀect of LiPF6
during the cycles.50,51 In contrast, for the proposed electrolyte,
the P 2p spectra peak corresponded to the PO at 134.5 eV,
indicating the decomposition of TMP.25,50 In the F 1 s spectra,
diﬀerent from containing both LiF at 684.7 eV and LixPOyFz at
687.4 eV in the conventional electrolyte,14,52−54 the peak only
corresponded to LiF, and simultaneously the peak intensity
was much stronger in the proposed electrolyte, indicating the
formation of LiF-rich SEI layers. Besides, the atomic ratio of
every element in the formed SEI is listed in Figure S8 for a
visual comparison. The XPS results revealed that the SEI layers
formed in the proposed electrolyte contained unique S and N
elements, which can lead to a higher ionic conductivity.47,55
Furthermore, LiF-rich SEI layers were formed because (1)
more FSI−anions participate in the formation during cycles
where the S−F bond of FSI−is more inclined to reduce to
form LiF56,57 and (2) the solvent HFE in the proposed
electrolyte reacts with lithium to form LiF as a perﬂuorinated
ether.58,59 Thereby, the generated LiF with a low diﬀusion
energy and a high surface energy for Li+ can eﬃciently enhance
surface diﬀusion of Li+ and sequentially suppress the Li
dendrite growth.53,60

4. CONCLUSIONS
We proposed an eﬀective ﬂame-retardant electrolyte that
rendered dendrite-free and stable cycling performance of
lithium-metal anodes with a high CE of 91% after 550 cycles.
The excellent electrochemical performance beneﬁted from the
facts that no free molecular solvent exists and a LiF-rich
interphase is generated on the Li-metal anode. When applied
in Li−S batteries, the S@pPAN cathodes delivered a greatly
enhanced cycling stability with a high capacity and superior
rate performances. Furthermore, the proposed electrolyte
showed good aﬃnity with the high-loading and high-sulfur-
content cathode. In sum, this work provides a new perspective
for developing highly safe and stable electrolyte systems.
Meanwhile, it also attracts more attempts on electrolyte
compatibility to enable high performance with Li−S batteries.
