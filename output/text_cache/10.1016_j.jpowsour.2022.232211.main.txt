A new ether-based medium-concentrated electrolyte for lithium–sulfur 
battery with lean Li anode

H I G H L I G H T S 
• Diisopropyl ether is a cheaper co-solvent for lithium-sulfur battery electrolyte. 
• The electrolyte with dual-salt shows better compatibility with Li anode. 
• The new electrolyte effectively inhibits the dissolution of lithium polysulfide.

A B S T R A C T  
A new ether-based electrolyte with tetrahydrofuran (THF) and di-isopropyl ether (DIPE) is proposed for Lith­
ium–Sulfur battery (LSB). By employing the common ion effect and the weakly-solvating solvent (such as DIPE), 
the dissolution of lithium polysulfides and the self-discharge effect are effectively inhibited. Besides, the LiFSI has 
be used as the co-salt in the LiTFSI-based electrolyte to improve the ionic conductivity and inhibit the side re­
action on Li metal anode. It is worth mentioning that, the L1L2TD-1382 electrolyte (LiTFSI: LiFSI: THF: DIPE = 1 
: 3: 8 : 2, molar ratio) ensures a stable cycling for more than 500 cycles with a high Columbic efficiency (CE) of Li 
deposition/stripping (>99.3%). Impressively, with selenium doped sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SeSPAN) cath­
ode, the Li–S cell has delivered a high capacity of 626 mAh g−1 in 500th cycle with an excellent capacity 
retention no less than 93%. This work provides a new insight to electrolyte design strategy of advanced elec­
trolytes for Li–S batteries.   
1. Introduction 
With a theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh Kg−1, lithium–sulfur 
battery (LSB) has been considered as one of most promising next gen­
eration rechargeable batteries [1,2]. However, serious intrinsic prob­
lems of LSB, including the shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) 
and high reactivity of Li with electrolyte, still remain to limit the cycling 
performance of the cell. With a sulfur cathode operating through “sol­
id-liquid-solid” mechanism, the conventional LSBs would produce a 
large amount of soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in charging and 
discharging processes [1,3]. Unfortunately, the dissolved LiPSs shuttling 
between the electrodes would get lithiumized on anode and delithiu­
mized on cathode, which aggravates self-discharge effect and 
irreversible capacity attenuation [4]. 
Many efforts have been devoted to preventing the “shuttle effect” 
and inhibiting the degradation of metallic Li anode. In 1 M Lithium bis 
(trifluoromethanesulfon)imide (LiTFSI) in dimethyl ether (DME)/1,3- 
dioxolane (DOL) electrolyte, LiNO3 has been commonly used as an ad­
ditive to block the redox shuttle of the LiPSs [5]. However, once LiNO3 is 
depleted, the “shuttle effect” would reappear in the following cycles. 
Although both ether solvents (i.e., DME and DOL) and LiTFSI possess a 
low reactivity with lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), the dendrite lithium 
growth in plating process and dead lithium formation in stripping pro­
cess are unavoidable issue in anode cycling in traditional ether-based or 
ester-based electrolytes [6]. Fortunately, assembling the cathodes 
following “solid-solid” conversion mechanism for Li–S cells is an

mentioning that, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has been reported 
can be used as a potential sulfur cathode to deliver high capacity and 
robust reversibility in the cells [8]. Although these sulfurized poly­
acrylonitrile cathodes exhibit an excellent cycling performance in 
half-cells with the carbonate-based electrolyte, the life span of the full 
cells coupled with lean Li anode is limited due to the continuous 
depletion of metallic lithium [9–11]. Even the traditional ether solvents 
have better compatibility with Li metal, shuttle effect still happens when 
SPAN cells containing these ether-based electrolytes [12,13]. 
The solubilities of LiPSs are low in the ethers with high C/O molar 
ratios, and these ethers are also more compatible with lithium metal, so 
they are good candidates for the solvents in the liquid electrolyte for 
LSBs [14]. Zhou et al., prepared high concentrated LiFSI (4 M) in dibutyl 
ether (DBE) to effectively extend the lifespan of LSBs [15]. However, all 
the pure DBE based electrolytes have low ionic conductivities. Chen 
et al., has built ternary solvent systems (DME-DOL-DIPE and 
DME-DOL-MTBE) containing 1 M LiTFSI to meet the requirements of 
high conductivity, low solubility and good compatibility with Li anode. 
The electrolytes based on di-isopropyl ether (DIPE) have been shown to 
be able to effectively suppress the dissolution of LiPSs and significantly 
improve the performance of the LSBs [9]. Although the localized 
high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) with low concentration of 
lithium salt have been investigated in LSB, the diluent solvents with high 
volatility (such as 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl 
ether, TTE) are not suitable for the batteries working in hot environ­
ment [16]. As a co-salt, lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) has 
abundant labile fluorosulfonyl group, which can preferentially be 
reduced on Li anode and participated in the formation of a robust and 
stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) [6,17]. 
In the present work, we have developed medium-concentrated 
electrolytes for LSBs based on the THF-DIPE dual solvents of lower po­
larity than DME and DOL. Compared to DPE, DIPE has low-cost and is 
more competitive as the weak-solubility cosolvent for LSBs electrolyte. 
With the optimized solvent combinations and dual salts, the electrolytes 
are compatible with Li-metal anode and demonstrate excellent cycling 
stability of Li-SeSPAN battery. 
2. 2．Experimental section 
2.1. Materials 
Li metal (φ 16 mm, 600 μm, China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., China), 
Lithium-copper composite tape (Li–Cu–Li, 50-15-50 μm, China Energy 
Lithium Co., Ltd., China), Cu foil (100 μm, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent 
Co., Ltd., China), Al foil (15 μm, Nippon Light Metal Co., Ltd., Japan), 
separator (2325, Celgard, USA). Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfon) 
imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%), polyacrylonitrile (PAN, average Mw ~ 150,000, 
99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., USA. Lithium bis(fluo­
rosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, 99.9%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical 
Industry Co., Ltd., Japan. Tetrahydrofuran (THF, anhydrous, 99.9%) 
was obtained from Innochem. Co., Ltd., China. Di-isopropyl ether (DIPE, 
98%), elemental sulfur (S8, 99.8%), selenium (Se powder, 99.99%), 
Ethanol (spectral purity, 99.8%) were purchased from Aladdin, Inc., 
China. All the solvents used for electrolytes were stored with 4 A mo­
lecular sieves in an argon glove box. Conductive carbon was Acetylene 
black (Denka Black Li 250, Lion Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan), and the 
LA132 binder (solid content ~ 15%) was purchased from Indigo Tech­
nology Co., Ltd., China. RO-pure water was self-made through a water 
purifier (Smart-N UV, Heal Force). 
2.2. Materials preparation 
All electrolytes were prepared in an argon-filled dry glove box (H2O 
< 0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm). LiTFSI and LiFSI of different molar ratios 
were mixed in THF and then stirred for 30 min. A specified amount of 
DIPE was added to the above solutions and stirred for 6 h at room 
temperature and the total concentration of salts in electrolytes was 
about 2.5 M. For SeSPAN composite preparation, sulfur, selenium, and 
polyacrylonitrile powders were ball-milled at a weight ratio of 15:1:3 
with ethanol used as the dispersant. The resulting mixture was dried at 
60 ◦C for 6 h and then heated to 350 ◦C for 6 h in nitrogen atmosphere to 
obtain the SeSPAN composite with a sulfur content of 41.8% (Table S1, 
Supporting Information). The sulfur content was determined by an 
elemental analysis (Vario EL cube, Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, 
Germany). The SeSPAN cathode electrode was prepared by mixing 
SeSPAN composites, acetylene black, LA132 Binder at a weight ratio of 
7:2:1, which has sulfur loading of about 2 mg cm−2. The slurry was 
spread on Al foils and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 ◦C for 12 h. 
2.3. Electrochemical measurements 
The coulombic efficiency (CE) of plating/stripping performance of 
lithium was examined by using the Li || Cu coin cells (CR2016) with 20 
μL electrolytes and the Li || Li symmetric cells (CR2025) with 30 μL 
electrolytes. The electrochemical windows of electrolytes were exam­
ined by using Li || steel coin cells (CR2016) with 20 μL electrolytes. The 
Li-SeSPAN full batteries (CR2016) contain 20 μL electrolytes. The E/S 
ratio the Li-SeSPAN cell is about 7.5 μL mg−1. The electrochemical 
windows of these electrolytes were examined with linear sweep vol­
tammetry (LSV) by using an electrochemical workstation (IVIUMSTAT, 
Netherlands). In order to investigate the CE of plating/stripping process 
of lithium, the constant current density for the Li || Cu cell was 0.5 mA 
cm−2. During each cycle, 1 mAh cm−2 of Li metal was deposited on the 
Cu substrate and then stripped until the potential reached 1.0 V vs. Li/ 
Li+. The current density of Li || Li symmetric cells was 1 mA cm−2 with 
the charging of 1 mAh cm−2 for each cycle. The discharge and charge 
measurements for Li || Cu cells and Li || Li cells were carried out on a 
NEWARE CT-4008T5V10mA Battery Test System (Shenzhen, China). 
The Galvanostatic charge/discharge for Li-SeSPAN cells were also con­
ducted on the NEWARE Battery Test System with the fixed voltage range 
of 1.0 V–3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) at room temperature. The FTIR spectroscopy 
was performed in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) configuration and 
on a Thermo Scientific-Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer. 
2.4. Electrode characterization 
The morphologies of lithium metal electrodes were investigated by a 
scanning electron microscopic (SEM, FESEM Phenom LE, USA). The Li- 
SeSPAN cells were dissembled in the glove box and the electrodes were 
thoroughly washed three times with anhydrous THF solvent to remove 
residual electrolyte before drying. In order to avoid exposure to air, the 
dried samples were sealed in an air-isolating container with Ar gas and 
transferred quickly into the SEM equipment. X-ray photo electron 
spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spec­
trometer (ESCALAB250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a 
monochromatic Al Ka source (1486.6 eV). 
2.5. Computation details 
All the calculations were performed by employing the BIOVIA Ma­
terial Studio 2020. The COMPASS III force field was employed in Forcite 
module for the classical molecular mechanics simulations [18]. The 
M06–2X hybrid meta exchange-correlation functional (with Basis set 
DND-4.4) was used in DMol [3] module for the density functional theory 
(DFT) calculation [19,20]. The ESP charges of lithium ion and a solvent 
molecule were calculated by the DFT mothed, and then used as charges 
in Forcite module for initial geometry optimization. The final solvation 
structures were obtained by the DFT optimizations.

3. Results and discussion 
The ratio of different solvent (THF and DIPE) in electrolyte has a 
great influence on the cycling performance of the Li anode. In order to 
determine the optimal solvent ratio, we firstly prepared pure-LiTFSI 
based electrolytes with various molar ratio of ether solvents (THF and 
DIPE). All the code names and formulations of the pure-LiTFSI electro­
lytes are listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The Long-time 
cycling test of lithium plating/stripping process in Li || Li symmetry- 
cells at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and with a capacity of 1 mAh 
cm−2 is shown in Fig. S1 (Supporting information). Since DIPE cannot 
dissociate Li+ from the TFSI−efficiently, the polarization voltage has got 
increased with the more ratio of DIPE in the mixed solvent system. As 
same as the one using L1L2TD-40X0 (X = 10) electrolyte, the symmetry- 
cell with the L1L2TD-4082 electrolyte presented lowest polarization 
voltage and the longest lifespan, demonstrating that the optimized dual- 
solvent system (THF: DIPE = 4 : 1) exhibits a better compatibility with Li 
anode. 
Therefore, THF and DIPE with a molar ratio of 4 : 1 was employed as 
the mixed-ether solvent for LSB electrolytes in the following work. All 
the code names and formulations of the electrolytes are listed in Table S3 
(Supporting Information), where the four digits at the end of each code 
name denote the molar ratio of each formulation. For example, L1L2TE- 
1382 is the code name of the formulation of LiTFSI: LiFSI: THF: DIPE = 1 
: 3: 8 : 2 (molar ratio). The conventional LSB electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI in 
DME/DOL (v/v = 1 : 1), was named as “LDD” in the present study. The 
DIPE solvent has similar properties to DPE, but has much lower cost 
($15/100 ml for DIPE vs $460/100 ml for DPE). Therefore, by replacing 
DPE with DIPE, the cost of electrolytes can be reduced by 80% (Fig. 1a). 
With concentrated lithium salt in the LiTFSI-LiFSI/THF-DIPE (L1L2TDs) 
electrolytes, most of the ether molecules have coordinated with lithium 
ions, which effectually enhances their oxidation resistance. As shown in 
Fig. 1b, all these electrolytes have wide electrochemical window with a 
same oxidation potential higher than 5.0 V, which could be applicable to 
both sulfur cathode and other cathodes with higher operating voltage. 
The lithium plating/stripping behavior were investigated in Li || Cu 
half-cells with areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 at a current density of 0.5 
mA cm−2 (Fig. 1c). The cells cycling with pure-LiTFSI electrolyte 
(L1L2TD-4082) delivered an unstable coulombic efficiency (CE), which 
increased from 87% to 97% within 100 cycles and dropped quickly to 
60% after 240 cycles. In contrast, the Li || Cu half-cells with LiFSI- 
containing electrolytes kept a CE higher than 99.3% for over 500 cy­
cles. The Voltage-Capacity profiles of lithium plating/stripping process 
in these electrolytes are shown in Fig. S2 (Supporting information). With 
the increasing molar ratio of LiFSI in L1L2TDs, the polarization voltage 
for these Li || Cu half-cells decreased from 0.055 V to 0.029 V at the 
100th cycle. The polarization voltage of the half-cells with L1L2TD- 
1382/0482 electrolyte have been stable at 4 mV after 200 cycles. The 
cycling performances of Li || Li symmetry cells with these electrolytes 
(excluding L1L2TD-4082) were investigated at the current density of 1 
mA cm−2 for 1 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 1d). The polarization voltage of lithium 
plating/stripping decreased with the increased molar ratio of LiFSI in 
the THF-DIPE based electrolytes. Especially, the symmetry cell with the 
L1L2TD-1382 electrolyte presented the lowest polarization voltage and 
the longest lifespan for more than 1000 cycles (2000 h) without short- 
circuit. With the optimized molar ratio of lithium salts, the proposed 
L1L2TD-1382 electrolyte exhibited an excellent compatibility with Li 
anode.

An ideal LSB electrolyte should have low solubilities to LiPSs to 
prevent the “shuttle effect”. Fig. 2a, the saturated Li2S6 solutions in LDD, 
LTE-4X0 (LiTFSI: THF = 4 : 10, molar ratio) and LTE-482 (LiTFSI: THF: 
DIPE = 4 : 8: 2, molar ratio) revealed different colors. The LDD con­
ventional electrolyte containing DME and DOL can dissolve a large 
amount of LiPSs, the color of LDD solution (Li2S6 in LDD) is red-brown. 
By contrast, benefited by using concentrated salt and monodentate 
ligand solvent (THF), the LTE-4X0 solution has effectively suppressed 
the dissolution of Li2S6, and has shown a light-yellow color for a week. 
Furthermore, twenty percent of THF was replaced with DIPE, the LTE- 
482 solution (saturated Li2S6 in LTE-482) exhibited an obviously ligh­
ter color than LTE-4X0, demonstrating that less LiPSs dissolved in LTE- 
482. The solvation structures of lithium ion with different number of 
solvent molecules were calculated by DFT. Figs. S3–S8 (Supporting In­
formation) exhibited the solvation structures Li+ in DME, THF, DOL, 
DPE, DIPE, respectively. The total energies (E) of these solvation 
structure models are listed in Table S4 (Supporting Information). As 
more solvent molecules coordinated with the lithium ion, the coordi­
nation to Li+ for the subsequent solvent molecule is hindered. Based on 
the optimized geometries of the solvation clusters, Li+ can coordinate 
with three DME molecules to form Li+(DME)3 (Fig. S4), and with five 
THF molecules to form Li+(THF)5 (Fig. S5). With the electron-withdraw 
effect between two oxygen atoms in DOL, only four DOL molecules can 
coordinate with Li+. Besides, due to their larger molecular volume, one 
lithium ion can only coordinate with three DPE or DIPE molecules to 
form Li+(DPE)3 or Li+(DIPE)3 (Figs. S6 and S7). Fig. 2b lists the 
sequential binding energies of these molecules (ΔEsolvent (n th)) contact 
with lithium ion in turn, which can be expressed as follows:  
ΔEsolvent (n th) = ELi+(solvent)(n) - ELi+(solvent)(n-1) - Esolvent                            
where n is the number of solvent molecules mixed with lithium ions and 
Esolvent is the total Energy of single solvent molecule. 
The DME and THF solvents have a same Gutmann Donor number 
(DN = 20) and similar dielectric constant, which show significantly 
higher binding energy in these five solvents. Because there is only one 
oxygen atom in the molecule as an electron donor, the coordination 
between THF and Li+ is weaker than that of DME [21]. Unlike DME, the 
two oxygen atoms are too close together in the DOL molecule cannot 
coordinate with the lithium ion at the same time. As compared to THF, 
the electron-withdraw effect between two oxygen atoms weakens the 
solvation ability of the DOL molecules. Therefore, the corresponding 
sequential binding energy is much lower than those of DME and THF. 
Due to the steric hindrance of the isopropyl fragment, it is difficult for 
more than two DIPE molecules to coordinate with lithium ion at the 
same time, and the average length of Li–O bonds is longer than that in 
Li+(DPE)n (n > 2) structure (Table S5, Supporting Information). The 
maximum oxygen coordination number to Li+ (Li–O bond) with 
different ether solvents is shown in Fig. 2c. These trends indicate that the 
dissolution of LiPSs is suppressed significantly with the common ion 
effect of high salt concentration and the use of solvents with low polarity 
[21]. 
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy has been employed 
to understand the ion solvation structure in the solution. Fig. S9 (Sup­
porting Information) show the spectra of the association of the ether 
solvents with the LiTFSI salt (marked as “L1”) in low concentration so­
lution (1 M LiTFSI in THF, marked as “1 M L1-THF”), LiTFSI/THF so­
lution (LiTFSI in THF with molar ratio of 4 : 10, marked as L1TE-4X0), 
and LiTFSI/THF-DIPE solution (LiTFSI in THF-DIPE with molar ratio of 4 
: 8: 2, marked as L1TE-4X2). With the Li+ concentration increases, the 
ring stretching vibration assigned to the peak about 1080–1050 cm−1 is 
evidently shifted to low wavenumber direction. The signal for the ring 
stretches in THF molecules (the peak around 1043 cm−1) changed from 
a shoulder peak to a strong single peak due to the participation in Li+
solvation. This trend can be seen for the C–O stretching mode around 
930-870 cm−1, the peak at 890 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching in THF-Li+
complexes) appeared and enhanced with the weakening of the peak at 
910 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching in free THF) when the Li concentration 
increases. After replacing part of THF with DIPE, a subtle change has 
occurred in the coordination structure.

In order to compare self-discharge effect of the Li-SeSPAN cell with 
different electrolytes, all the cells would discharge to 1.9 V and then 
have a rest for 10 days and continue discharge to 1.0 V. Due to the 
“shuttle effect”, the discharge capacity of the cell with LDD electrolyte 
has reduced by a quarter (Fig. S10 a, Supporting Information). In 
contrast, the cell with L1L2TD-1382 electrolyte delivered an almost 
equal and unattenuated capacity after long-time storage (Fig. 4a), and 
the cells with other L1L2TD electrolytes also perform well (Fig. S10 b-d, 
Supporting Information). To further demonstrate that these optimized 
electrolytes have an enough ability for LiPSs inhibition, we also per­
formed the self-discharge test of the Li-SPAN cells with L1L2TD-1382 
electrolyte and the result has been presented in Fig. S10 e in 
Supporting Information. As same as the cell with SeSPAN cathode, there 
has no capacity fading in the Li-SPAN cell after undergoing a 10-day rest.

The chemical composition of the SEI has been analyzed by X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in the S 2p spectra 
(Fig. 7a–b and Fig. S12, Supporting Information), the peaks at 163.0 eV 
and 161.4 eV can be assigned to the bridge-sulfur (SB) and terminal- 
sulfur (ST) in Li2SX, respectively. Li2SX has formed in the SEI of the 
anode cycled in the LDD electrolyte, which reveals that an obvious 
“shuttle effect” has occurred in the LDD electrolyte [26]. In contrast, the 
Li2SX content was significantly reduced in SEI formed in L1L2TD elec­
trolytes (e.g. L1L2TD-1382), which is consistent with the result of 
self-discharge test, confirming that the dissolution of LiPSs in the 
L1L2TD electrolytes has been effectively inhibited. With a higher molar 
ratio of LiFSI in the electrolyte formulation, the signals for Li2SO4 and 
Li2SO3 have increased in the SEI (Fig. S12, Supporting Information), 
which have beneficial effects for fast Li ion transfer in the SEI layers 
[27]. 
As for the F 1s spectra shown in Fig. 7c–d, the peaks at 688 eV and 
684.5 eV are attributed to C–F bonds and LiF, respectively. Unlike the 
SEI formed in LDD, most of the F atoms are presented as LiF in SEI 
formed in the L1L2TD electrolytes, and LiF plays an important role for 
suppressing Li dendrite formation [28]. Since the FSI−ions are prone to 
decompose and form nitrides on the Li surface, the two large peaks 
around 399 eV and 397 eV in the N 1s XPS spectra (Fig. S13, Supporting 
Information) are associated with Li2N2O2 and Li3N, respectively. With 
the increase of molar ratio of LiFSI in the L1L2TD electrolyte, the signals 
for the F(SO2)2N fragment (peak at 400 eV), Li2N2O2 and Li3N increase 
in the SEIs, and previous works have shown that Li2N2O2 and Li3N 
would improve the Li ionic conductivity of the SEI [29,30]. 
4. Conclusions 
In the present study, we have developed a novel electrolyte system 
which enables dendrite-free Li anode and suppresses the “shuttle effect” 
in Li-SeSPAN batteries. The Li-sulfur cells with the optimized L1L2TD- 
1382 electrolyte (LiTFSI: LiFSI: THF: DIPE = 1 : 3: 8 : 2, molar ratio) 
can have a long lifespan of 500 cycles with a capacity retention of 93%. 
In addition, the capacity of the cells with the L1L2TD electrolytes have 
no self-discharge during a 10-day storage. Benefited from the concen­
trated Li salt and solvents of low polarity, the proposed L1L2TD elec­
trolytes have a low volatility as compared to the conventional LDD 
electrolyte, which improves the safety of LSBs operated in the hot 
environment. Although some ethers (such as THF) tend to form perox­
ides under heat and oxygen-rich environments, this electrolyte optimi­
zation strategy proposed in this work is expected to promote the long- 
cycle life of LSB with lean Li anode. The disadvantage can be over­
come by replacing THF with robust solvents in our future research.
