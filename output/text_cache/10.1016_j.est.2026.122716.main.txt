A B S T R A C T
Although lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries possess an exceptional theoretical specific capacity, their practical 
application has long been hindered by the severe shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and sluggish redox 
kinetics. To address these core challenges, this study proposes an electrolyte additive strategy based on nitrogen- 
containing heterocyclic molecules, systematically investigating the regulatory effects of pyridine, pyrazine, and 
1,3,5-triazine on LiPS interfacial chemistry. All three additives are capable of anchoring LiPSs through Li–N 
coordination and effectively modulating their frontier molecular orbital energy levels, thereby accelerating LiPS 
conversion and promoting the formation of three-dimensional (3D) Li2S deposition structures. Among them, 
pyrazine exhibits unique advantages due to its para-dinitrogen configuration: it not only achieves potent 
chemical anchoring but also reacts in situ with LiPSs to generate organosulfur intermediates, synergistically 
suppressing the shuttle effect and significantly enhancing sulfur utilization. Most importantly, this molecule 
achieves an optimal balance between coordination strength, orbital modulation capability, and interfacial sta­
bility. Consequently, the Li–S cell utilizing the pyrazine additive delivers an initial discharge capacity of 709.48 
mAh g−1 at 1C. Under practical conditions of high sulfur loading (3.0 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte (E/S = 13.3 
μL mg−1), the cell maintains a high reversible capacity of 527.06 mAh g−1 after 120 cycles at 0.2C, highlighting 
its superior cycling stability and potential for practical application.
1. Introduction
Recently, lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been recognized as 
important candidates for next-generation high-energy storage systems 
due to their ultrahigh theoretical energy density (approximately 2600 
Wh kg−1). This advantage stems from their electrode active materi­
als—elemental sulfur (theoretical specific capacity 1675 mAh g−1) and 
metallic lithium (3860 mAh g−1)—which combine multiple benefits 
including low density, high specific capacity, abundant resources, low 
cost, and environmental friendliness [1–3]. However, the shuttling 
behavior of soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) between the sulfur 
cathode and lithium anode (i.e., the “shuttle effect”) severely constrains 
the development of LSBs [4,5]. Specifically, the dissolution and shut­
tling of LiPSs cause irreversible loss of sulfur species from the cathode 
[6–8], sluggish kinetics of LiPS conversion reactions [7,9], and kinetic 
limitations during Li2S deposition and decomposition processes 
[10–12], collectively leading to severe capacity fading during cycling. 
These issues result in low active material utilization, rapid capacity 
decay, and poor Coulombic efficiency of the sulfur cathode, seriously 
impeding the practical application of LSBs. More critically, the sluggish 
kinetics of liquid-liquid conversion of LiPSs and liquid-solid deposition 
to insoluble Li2S further limit sulfur utilization and rate performance, 
constituting a core bottleneck hindering the commercialization of LSBs.
To address these challenges, researchers have proposed surface 
chemistry-initiated strategies aimed at constructing interfaces capable 
of forming strong interactions with LiPSs. Among these, nitrogen- 
containing functional materials have attracted considerable attention 
due to their unique electronic structure and coordination ability, which 
promote better electrochemical contact between polysulfides and 
conductive surfaces [13–18]. For example, Sun et al. [19] demonstrated

improve conductivity, and alleviate volume expansion, significantly

which not only helps mitigate the shuttle effect but also reduces the loss 
of cathode active material. Furthermore, N atoms transfer electron

orbital energy levels and thereby promoting polysulfide conversion ki­
netics. Yang et al. [21] introduced pyrrole into the electrolyte, where its 
N atoms not only facilitate the formation of a polypyrrole protective 
layer on the sulfur cathode but also form strong interactions with Li+ in 
cycling-generated LiPSs, confining LiPSs to the cathode region and 
fundamentally suppressing the “shuttle effect.” This protective layer, 
possessing conductive, adsorptive, and blocking functions, serves as an 
efficient polysulfide trap, effectively inhibiting their shuttling and 
enhancing cathode performance. The aforementioned results indicate 
that the effectiveness of nitrogen-containing additives fundamentally 
depends on the lone pair coordination capability and electron delocal­
ization characteristics provided by nitrogen atoms. Such additives ach­
ieve chemical anchoring of LiPSs through Li–N bond formation, thereby 
effectively suppressing their shuttle effect. Meanwhile, nitrogen- 
containing structures (such as heterocycles and conjugated systems) 
can modulate the local electron transfer environment, accelerating the 
redox conversion kinetics of LiPSs. Additionally, nitrogen-containing 
derivatives or nitrogen-rich interfacial layers constructed at the elec­
trode/electrolyte interface help stabilize the cathode structure, miti­
gating the negative effects of active material loss and volume expansion.
Based on this understanding, this study proposes a strategy for the 
precision design of electrolyte additives at the molecular level. We 
systematically investigate the efficacy of three structurally well-defined 
six-membered N-heterocyclic compounds with an increasing number of 
nitrogen atoms—pyridine (mono-aza), pyrazine (1,4-diaza), and s- 
triazine (1,3,5-triaza)—in modulating the interfacial chemistry of 
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs). By correlating the number and spatial dis­
tribution of nitrogen atoms with their coordination behavior, the results 
reveal that pyridine provides limited single-site interaction, while the 
densely distributed nitrogen atoms in triazine may induce steric hin­
drance and reduce effective adsorption [22–24]. In contrast, the para- 
positioned dual‑nitrogen configuration of pyrazine enables a favorable 
bidentate coordination mode, allowing strong Li–N interactions with 
lithium polysulfides and promoting the formation of stable complexes. 
Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations confirm that the pyrazine- 
LiPSs complex exhibits an elevated HOMO level and a lowered LUMO 
level, markedly narrowing the HOMO-LUMO energy gap (Egaₚ). This 
electronic modulation substantially boosts redox activity and acceler­
ates the bidirectional conversion kinetics of polysulfides. Li–S batteries 
utilizing pyrazine as an additive deliver a specific capacity of 459 mAh 
g−1 at a high rate of 2C and maintain 64.9% capacity retention after 600 
cycles at 1C.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
All raw materials were purchased from suppliers and used without 
further purification or treatment. Specifically, pyridine, pyrazine, and s- 
triazine (analytical grade, AR) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin 
Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sublimed sulfur (chemical grade, CP) 
was purchased from Tianjin Kaixin Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. Poly­
vinylidene fluoride (PVDF), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), and lith­
ium‑sulfur (Li–S) battery electrolyte (AR) were sourced from 
Guangdong Zhuguang New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. Lithium sulfide 
(Li2S, AR) was obtained from Energy Chemical Co., Ltd. Carbon paper 
and lithium metal (AR) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Re­
agent Co., Ltd.
2.2. Preparation of sulfur cathodes
Carbon black (XC-72) and sublimed sulfur were mixed at a mass ratio 
of 2:3 and ground for 30 min. The mixture was then heat-treated at 
155 ◦C for 12 h under an argon (Ar) atmosphere to achieve sulfur 
infusion. The resulting sulfur‑carbon composite (70 wt%), Super P 
conductive carbon black (20 wt%), and PVDF binder (10 wt%) were 
dispersed in NMP and stirred thoroughly to form a homogeneous slurry. 
The slurry was subsequently coated onto an aluminum foil current col­
lector and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 ◦C for 12 h to ensure complete 
solvent removal. The dried electrode was punched into circular discs 
with a diameter of 12 mm for battery assembly.
2.3. Preparation of conventional electrolytes
In an Ar-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm), specified 
amounts of pyridine, pyrazine, or s-triazine were dissolved in 1 mL of 
blank electrolyte. The solutions were ultrasonicated for 10 min to ensure 
complete dissolution, yielding electrolytes with varying additive con­
centrations. The blank electrolyte consisted of 1.0 M lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.1 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) 
dissolved in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) (1:1 by volume).
2.4. Preparation of Li2S6 electrolyte
In a glovebox, Li2S6 and sublimed sulfur were added to a DOL/DME 
(1:1 v/v) solvent at a molar ratio of 1:5. The mixture was stirred 
continuously at 60 ◦C for 24 h to obtain the Li2S6 electrolyte.
2.5. Battery assembly and electrochemical measurements
All cells were assembled in a high-purity Ar-filled glovebox (H2O <
0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm) using CR2032-type coin cells. The compo­
nents were stacked from bottom to top in the following order: stainless 
steel anode case, lithium metal anode, Celgard 2400 separator, sulfur 
cathode, spacer, spring, and stainless steel cathode case. Each side of the 
separator was infused with 20 μL of either the blank (as reference cell) or 
additive-containing (e.g., pyrazine) electrolyte before the cell was 
sealed. The electrolyte-to‑sulfur (E/S) ratio was controlled at approxi­
mately 40 μL mg−1. The same assembly procedure was applied for cells 
under high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte conditions. Galvanostatic 
charge-discharge (GCD) tests were performed using a Neware battery 
testing system within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V. Specific capacities 
were calculated based on the mass of active sulfur in the cathode (1C =
1675 mA h g−1). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical imped­
ance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted on a CHI 650D electrochemical 
workstation. CV was performed at a scan rate of 0.1 mV S−1 within the 
1.7–2.8 V range. All tests were carried out at room temperature. The Li+
conductivity was derived from the EIS fitting results. The ion diffusion 
resistance was calculated according to Eq. (1-1):

2.6. Theoretical calculations
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using 
the Gaussian 16 software package. The geometric and electronic struc­
tures of the molecular systems were optimized using the B3LYP density 
functional with the 6-31G(d) basis set. Binding energies, Highest 
Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) levels, Lowest Unoccupied Mo­
lecular Orbital (LUMO) levels, and electrostatic potential (ESP) distri­
butions were obtained at the same level and corrected for zero-point 
energy (ZPE). The adsorption energy (Eb) between lithium polysulfides 
(LiPSs) and additive molecules was defined as:
Eb = EAB + E(BSSE) −(EA + EB)
where EAB is the total energy of the complex, and EA and EB are the 
energies of the isolated additive molecule (A) and the LiPS (B), respec­
tively, E(BSSE) = Energy of the Basis Set Superposition Error.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to 
evaluate the binding energies between the organic N-heterocyclic ad­
ditives and Li2Sx. Fig. S2 illustrates the most stable optimized geometries 
of the interactions between Li2Sx (x = 1, 4, 6 and 8) and the N-hetero­
cycles. The N-heterocycles adsorb lithium sulfide and polysulfides 
through the formation of stable Li–N bonds, resulting in the creation of 
stable coordination complexes. The corresponding binding energies are 
presented in Fig. 1d, calculated using the following equation: Eb = ELi2Sx- 
NHR - ELi2Sx–ENHR, where ELi2Sx-NHR represents the total energy of the 
Li2Sx and NHR (N-heterocyclic receptor) interaction system, while ELi2Sx 
and ENHR denote the energies of the isolated Li2Sx and N-heterocyclic 
molecules, respectively. By this definition, a more negative binding 
energy value indicates a more stable configuration. The results 
demonstrate that pyrazine possesses a superior adsorption capacity for 
Li2Sx compared to pyridine and s-triazine. Furthermore, In this work, the 
binding energy of the Li–N interaction is approximately-24 to −19 kJ 
mol−1, which falls within the typical energy range of hydrogen bonds 
(about 10–40 kJ mol−1), suggesting that the formation and dissociation 
of the Li–N bonds are reversible. This ensures that the interaction is 
sufficient for anchoring without hindering the electrochemical conver­
sion kinetics of the polysulfides.
To further investigate the specific reaction mechanisms between 
organic N-heterocycles and LiPSs, first-principles calculations were 
performed on the N-heterocycles and their corresponding complexes. 
Their electronic structures were systematically analyzed using frontier 
molecular orbital (FMO) theory. The highest occupied molecular orbital 
(HOMO) is associated with electrophilic reactions and reflects the 
electron-donating capability; a higher HOMO energy indicates a greater 
propensity for the material to lose electrons. Conversely, the lowest 
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is related to nucleophilic re­
actions and represents the electron-accepting capability, where a lower 
LUMO energy signifies a higher affinity for electron acceptance [26–30]. 
In this work, DFT calculations were performed in the gas phase without 
the inclusion of an implicit solvation model. The energy difference be­
tween the HOMO and LUMO is defined as the energy gap (Egap), which 
characterizes the ease of electron transition from occupied to unoccu­
pied orbitals and serves as a critical descriptor of molecular chemical 
reactivity. Generally, a smaller Egap indicates higher chemical reactivity. 
Fig. 2a presents the calculated HOMO and LUMO energy levels for 
pyridine, pyrazine, and s-triazine. The results reveal that pyridine pos­
sesses a higher HOMO level (−6.873 eV), while s-triazine exhibits a 
lower LUMO level (−1.579 eV). Notably, pyrazine displays the nar­
rowest energy gap (5.507 eV). The HOMO and LUMO energy levels, 
along with the corresponding Egap, for pyridine, pyrazine, and s-triazine 
are listed in Table S1. This dataset serves as a quantitative supplement to 
the computational results in Fig. 2, further elucidating the frontier 
orbital profiles of these molecules. Based on these findings, it can be 
inferred that pyrazine facilitates electron transitions more readily, 
making it the most chemically active candidate for intermolecular re­
actions, followed by pyridine and s-triazine. Taking the coordination of 
Li2S6 as a representative case, the resulting complexes (Prd-Li2S6, Prz- 
Li2S6, Trz-Li2S6) exhibit elevated HOMO levels and lowered LUMO 
levels compared to pristine Li2S6. Consequently, these complexes can 
more easily accept electrons as oxidants and donate electrons as re­
ductants, thereby enhancing the redox capability of Li2S6 and acceler­
ating the reaction kinetics. The adsorption capacities of the different 
additives toward Li2S6 were investigated via visualization experiments. 
To validate our findings, comparative calculations under identical co­
ordination environments were conducted (Fig. S3a–b). Consistent trends

As shown in Fig. 3a–c, the catalytic activities of organic nitrogen- 
heterocyclic additives toward LiPSs were evaluated using 0.2 M Li2S6 
as the electrolyte. In the absence of Li2S6, the current response is 
negligible (approaching zero), represented by a flat line. Upon the 
addition of Li2S6, the symmetric cells exhibit significant current re­
sponses, indicating that the redox reactions of LiPSs are the primary 
contributors to the observed current [31]. At a scan rate of 1 mV s−1, the 
cells containing organic nitrogen-heterocyclic additives display highly 
symmetric profiles with two distinct pairs of redox peaks. Notably, the 
pyrazine-based symmetric cell yields the largest peak area and highest 
peak current density; this superior performance is maintained even at a 
high scan rate of 50 mV s−1 (Fig. S5). These results demonstrate that 
organic nitrogen-heterocyclic additives effectively accelerate the liquid- 
liquid conversion of LiPSs, exhibiting enhanced catalytic activity for 
Li2S6 transformation.

To further investigate the influence of different additives on Li2S 
nucleation, a dimensionless transient current analysis was performed 
based on the Scharifker-Hills model. Generally, the rates of two- 
dimensional progressive (2DP) and instantaneous (2DI) nucleation are 
governed by the crystal phase [34], whereas three-dimensional pro­
gressive (3DP) and instantaneous (3DI) nucleation are diffusion-con­
trolled—a mechanism that significantly accelerates Li2S nucleation rates 
[35,36]. As shown in Figs. 3g–i and S9, Li2S nucleation in the reference 
cell follows the 2DP mode, while the cell with the s-triazine additive 
exhibits the 2DI mode. In contrast, the addition of pyridine and pyrazine 
shifts the Li2S nucleation process toward 3DP and 3DI modes, respec­
tively. These results indicate that the nucleation process in the presence 
of these additives is independent of crystal planes, facilitating easier 
nucleation and growth. This further confirms that organic nitrogen- 
heterocyclic additives optimize redox kinetics and promote efficient 
Li₂S deposition.

fluctuations in subsequent cycles, indicating rampant lithium dendrite 
growth and eventual short-circuiting [40]. Conversely, the pyrazine- 
added cell maintains a stable polarization voltage. This superior stabil­

interfacial adsorption strength and catalytic activity; it is strong enough 
to suppress the shuttle effect without being so excessive that it hinders 
the participation of LiPSs in cathode reactions, thereby effectively pro­
tecting the lithium anode.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to 
analyze the interfacial transport processes of the cells. As shown in the 
equivalent circuit model (Fig. 5a), cells containing organic nitrogen- 
heterocyclic additives exhibit significantly lower charge transfer resis­
tance (RCT) compared to the reference cell (60.1 Ω), with the pyrazine- 
added cell achieving the minimum RCT (24.8 Ω). This reduction is 
attributed to the suppressed migration of LiPSs and enhanced ionic 
transport, which collectively accelerate LiPS conversion kinetics. As 
illustrated in Fig. 5b, the rate performance was evaluated at current 
densities ranging from 0.1 to 2C (1C = 1675 mAh g−1). The pyrazine- 
added cell demonstrates superior sulfur utilization (30%–62.8%) 
compared to the pyridine-added (25.1%–56.1%), s-triazine-added 
(22.6%–53.5%), and reference cells (19.6%–44.1%). Notably, the 
pyrazine-added cell maintains a high specific discharge capacity when 
the current density is restored to 0.2C after high-rate cycling. These 
results highlight the excellent electrochemical reversibility and high 
stability of pyrazine as a redox mediator. The galvanostatic char­
ge–discharge profiles at various rates are shown in Fig. 5c. As shown in 
Fig. S10a–b, consistent capacity attenuation is observed across different 
additive systems with varying rates. Notably, the pyrazine sample de­
livers superior specific capacity and voltage stability at high rates, 
providing further evidence of its effectiveness in promoting reaction 
kinetics and improving rate capability. The pyrazine-added cell retains 
the characteristic discharge plateaus of LSBs even at high current den­
sities, confirming its robust rate capability. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was 
used to characterize the electrochemical reaction kinetics, reflecting the 
reversibility and stability of the system. To exclude the capacity 
contribution of nitrogen-heterocyclic additives during the galvanostatic 
charge-discharge process, sulfur-free cells were assembled and tested 
(Fig. S11a–c). The results indicate that the trace amount of additives 
provides only negligible capacity, confirming that their contribution to 
the overall cell capacity is insignificant. In Fig. 5d, the CV curve of the 
pyrazine-added cell shows higher redox current densities and a nar­
rower peak-to-peak separation (314 mV), indicating that pyrazine fa­
cilitates the rapid transformation between sulfur and lithium sulfide. 
The nearly overlapping CV curves after four cycles (Fig. S12a) demon­
strate high electrochemical reversibility. During discharge, two char­
acteristic reduction peaks appear at 2.3–2.4 V and 1.9–2.1 V 
(Fig. S12b–c), corresponding to the formation of soluble LiPSs and 
insoluble Li2S, respectively. The oxidation peak between 2.3 and 2.6 V 
corresponds to the decomposition of Li2S during charging. The pyrazine- 
added cell exhibits higher current densities than the reference cell, 
particularly for the reduction of Li2Sn to Li2S2/Li2S and the reverse 
oxidation process, signifying accelerated redox kinetics. The Tafel slopes 
were calculated from the magnified CV regions (Fig. 5e, f). During the 
lithium dissolution process, the Tafel slopes for the cells with pyridine, 
pyrazine, and s-triazine additives are 53.21, 44.01, and 46.72 mV dec−1, 
respectively, substantially lower than the reference cell (120.62 mV 
dec−1). Similarly, during lithium deposition, the additives yield lower 
Tafel slopes (68.17–83.37 mV dec−1) than the blank electrolyte (114.21 
mV dec−1). Given that the Tafel slope is inversely proportional to elec­
trochemical kinetics, these findings indicate that organic nitrogen- 
heterocyclic additives, especially pyrazine, enhance sulfur utilization 
and accelerate redox reactions. As shown in Fig. S13, the peak currents 
exhibit a linear relationship with the square root of the scan rate. The

cell delivered an initial capacity of only 470.03 mAh g−1 with rapid 
degradation and an average CE of only 96.87%, highlighting the pivotal 
role of pyrazine in suppressing capacity decay and enhancing stability. 
Optimizing electrochemical performance under high sulfur loading and 
lean electrolyte conditions is essential for the practical development of 
LSBs. Cell performance was further evaluated under challenging con­
ditions: a high sulfur loading of 3.0 mg cm−2, a lean electrolyte ratio (E/

pyrazine-added cell exhibited an initial discharge capacity of 752.72 
mAh g−1 and retained 527.06 mAh g−1 after 120 cycles (70.0% capacity 
retention), whereas the control cell's retention was only 53.5%. The 
cycling stability under high sulfur loading (5.3 mg cm−2) and lean 
electrolyte (E/S = 5.6 μL mg−1) is shown in Fig. S15. An initial rising 
trend in capacity is observed due to sulfur activation, followed by 
attenuation induced by polysulfide shuttling and lithium anode corro­
sion. By anchoring lithium polysulfides through Li–N coordination, the 
nitrogen-heterocyclic additives mitigate the shuttle effect and stabilize 
the electrochemical interface. Consequently, the pyrazine system de­
livers a reversible capacity of 740.48 mAh g−1 with a minimal decay rate 
of 0.05% per cycle and a steady Coulombic efficiency of 97.6%. Fig. 5k 
showcases the cycling performance with a high sulfur loading of 5.6 mg 
cm−2 and lean electrolyte. At 0.1C, the cell achieved an areal capacity of 
4.72 mAh cm−2, which remained at 4.26 mAh cm−2 after 50 cycles, 
surpassing the typical areal capacity of commercial lithium-ion batteries 
(4.0 mAh cm−2). In summary, organic nitrogen-heterocyclic additives 
significantly enhance LSBs performance, with pyrazine demonstrating 
the most remarkable optimization effects among the studied additives.

4. Conclusions
This study systematically investigates the underlying mechanisms of 
three nitrogen-containing heterocyclic molecules as electrolyte addi­
tives in LSBs and their subsequent impact on electrochemical perfor­
mance. The results demonstrate that these additives interact with LiPSs 
through the formation of stable Li–N coordination bonds, achieving 
effective anchoring of LiPSs. By modulating the molecular orbital 
structures and reaction pathways, these additives promote the 3D 
nucleation, deposition, and reversible decomposition of Li2S, thereby 
significantly mitigating the shuttle effect and enhancing reaction ki­
netics. Notably, pyrazine achieves an optimal balance between LiPSs 
adsorption strength, electronic structure regulation, and interfacial 
stability, attributed to its symmetric bidentate coordination structure 
formed by the para-positioned nitrogen atoms. Theoretical calculations 
reveal that pyrazine possesses a low electrostatic potential distribution, 
enabling it to efficiently capture Li+ and spontaneously form stable 
organic‑sulfur intermediates with LiPSs, which effectively inhibits LiPSs 
diffusion. Simultaneously, the regulation of the LiPSs energy level 
structure by pyrazine significantly reduces the reaction energy barrier 
and accelerates the redox kinetics of sulfur species. Electrochemical 
performance testing further verifies the varying optimization effects of 
the different additives. Overall, pyrazine exhibits the most superior 
performance: at a current density of 1C, it delivers an initial specific 
discharge capacity of 720.04 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of 
68.32% after 600 cycles. Under demanding conditions of high sulfur 
loading (2.9 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte (E/S = 10.3 μL mg−1), it 
maintains a discharge capacity of 527.06 mAh g−1 after 120 cycles at 
0.2C, demonstrating exceptional high-loading adaptability and cycling 
stability. This research indicates that pyrazine, as a low-cost and highly 
efficient additive, offers distinct advantages in improving cycling sta­
bility and sulfur utilization under high-loading and lean-electrolyte 
conditions, underscoring its significant potential for practical applica­
tion. Furthermore, the study explores the influence of the number and 
spatial arrangement of nitrogen atoms in heterocyclic molecules on 
LiPSs adsorption and catalytic behavior, providing a crucial theoretical 
foundation for the rational design of high-performance electrolyte ad­
ditives. In summary, this work not only reveals the outstanding perfor­
mance of pyrazine as an additive but also provides a new strategy for 
developing next-generation LSBs, highlighting the importance of mo­
lecular design in optimizing battery performance.
