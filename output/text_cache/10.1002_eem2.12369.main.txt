Dual-Functional Organotelluride Additive for Highly
Efﬁcient Sulfur Redox Kinetics and Lithium Regulation in
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Wei Zhang, Fenfen Ma, Qiang Wu, Ziqi Zeng, Wei Zhong, Shijie Cheng, Xin Chen, and
Jia Xie*
1. Introduction
Widespread development of electric vehicles, intelligent electronic
devices, and grid–scale electricity storage calls for urgent needs of high
energy density battery system.[1,2] Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery has
tremendous attracted attention because of its ultrahigh energy density
(2600 Wh kg1), abundant reserve of sulfur, and low cost.[3–6] How-
ever, some knotty issues related to both sulfur cathode and lithium
anode dramatically hinder practical applications
of Li–S batteries.[7–10] For cathode side, the
shuttle effect of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs), huge volume change (about 80%) of
cathode and insulating nature of S and Li2S
would lead to low sulfur utilization, sluggish
reaction kinetics, and fast capacity decay.[11–13]
For the Li anode side, the extremely high chem-
ical reactivity of metallic Li presents two main
challenges including inhomogeneous deposi-
tion of lithium that forms disordered Li den-
drites, and fragile SEI that aggravates irreversible
electrolytes consumption.[14–16] Many strategies
have been reported to solve these problems,
such as advanced electrode conﬁguration,[17–20]
electrolyte regulation,[21–23] and electrode–elec-
trolyte interface modiﬁcation.[8,24,25] With the
development of electrode materials, many elec-
trolyte additives has been proposed to solve
problem of Li–S battery.[26,27] Electrolyte addi-
tives with certain functional groups are able to
form stable interfacial layers and inhibit LiPSs
shuttling. For instance, Fu et al. recently pro-
posed that introduction of 1,3,5–benzene-
trithiol (BTT) and benzenedithiols (BDTs) as electrolyte additives in Li–
S battery could reduce dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs and enable
reversible lithium deposition/stripping, resulting in much improved
battery performance.[28,29]
Because of ultrahigh volumetric capacity of 2621 mAh cm3, tellu-
rium (Te), in the same element group with S, has been reported as a
promising cathode material for lithium ion batteries.[29,30] Compared
with S, Te possesses much higher electronic conductivity. Therefore, it
can greatly enhance conductivity and electrochemical activity of sulfur
cathodes by introducing Te in sulfur cathodes.[31] Xie et al. recently
have proposed a eutectic accelerator strategy of introducing little Te in
sulfur cathodes to signiﬁcantly accelerate the redox kinetics.[32–37]
Moreover, Manthiram et al. discovered that using Te as cathode additive
could form a tellurized and sulﬁde-rich SEI on the surface of Li anode,
realizing dendrite-free Li deposition in Li–S battery.[38] Redox media-
tors with reversible redox couples can enhance the battery perfor-
mances by regulating chemical reactions of the sulfur species.[20,39,40]
Huang et al. have proposed that the organodiselenide, diphenyl disele-
nide (DPDSe), is used as a redox comediator to enhance kinetics of sul-
fur cathode, in which DPDSe reacts with LiPSs to improve the redox
mediation capability, thus endowing the Li–S batteries with excellent

High energy density and low cost made lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
appealing for the next-generation energy storage devices. However, their
commercial viability is seriously challenged by serious polysulﬁde shuttle
effect, sluggish sulfur kinetics, and uncontrollable dendritic Li growth. Herein,
a dual-functional electrolyte additive, diphenyl ditelluride (DPDTe) is
reported for Li–S battery. For sulfur cathodes, DPDTe works as a redox
mediator to accelerate redox kinetics of sulfur, in which Te radical-mediated
catalytic cycle at the solid–liquid interface contributes signiﬁcantly to the
whole process. For lithium anodes, DPDTe can react with lithium metal to
form a smooth and stable organic–inorganic hybrid solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI), enabling homogeneous lithium deposition for suppressing
dendrite growth. Consequently, the Li–S battery with DPDTe exhibits
remarkable cycling stability and superb rate capability, with a high capacity
up to 1227.3 mAh g1 and stable cycling over 300 cycles. Moreover, a Li–S
pouch cell with DPDTe is evaluated as the proof of concept. This work
demonstrates that organotelluride compounds can be used as functional
electrolyte additives and offers new insights and opportunities for practical
Li–S batteries.

Herein, DPDTe is investigated as an appealing dual-functional elec-
trolyte additive for both highly efﬁcient sulfur cathode and dendrite-
free Li anode. On one hand, introducing DPDTe enables a Te radical-
mediated catalytic cycle that is responsible for effectively enhancing
redox kinetic of LiPSs and achieving controllable growth of Li2S. On
the contrary, DPDTe reacts with lithium metal to form a uniform,
dense, and stable organic–inorganic hybrid SEI that can reduce Li nucle-
ation overpotential and facilitate homogeneous lithium deposition, thus
effectively preventing Li dendrite growth. Consequently, DPDTe as
additive helps to increase sulfur utilization and realize highly reversible
Li stripping/plating, which leads to Li–S batteries with excellent rate
performance (611.4 mAh g1 at 5 C rate) and high cycling stability
over 300 cycles at 1 C rate. Moreover, DPDTe can be successfully
applied under harsh conditions and in Li–S pouch cell. Overall, this
work presents a facile strategy of using an organotelluride as electrolyte
additive to improve battery performance of Li–S batteries, which can
guide further exploration for practical high-energy-density systems.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Heading Electrochemical Evaluation of Li–S Batteries
To demonstrate the effect of DPDTe on battery performances of Li–S
batteries, DPDTe was introduced as electrolyte additive in a blank elec-
trolyte for electrochemical test. Structural characterization identiﬁes the
typical structures of the carbon–sulfur composite which serves as the
active sulfur cathodes (Figures S1 and S2, Table S1, Supporting Infor-
mation). Figure 1a compares the CV curves of Li–S batteries with and
without DPDTe at 0.5 C rate. While they possess generally similar oxi-
dation and reduction peak features, the Li–S batteries with DPDTe show
not only better redox reversibility but also higher peak current density.
Cycling stability and rate performance for the Li–S batteries with differ-
ent additive concentrations of DPDTe are displayed in Figures S3 and
S4, Supporting Information. It shows that Li–S battery with 50 mM
DPDTe exhibits the best battery performances, which was used in
throughout our work without further optimization. The cycling perfor-
mance of Li–S batteries with and without DPDTe at 0.5 C rate show
drastic difference (Figure 1b and Figure S5, Supporting Information).
The control battery, starts with an initial discharge capacity of 1120.9
mAh g1 and initial coulombic efﬁciency (ICE) of 90.28%, and holds
253.1 mAh g1, corresponding to a capacity retention of 22.58%.
However, the Li–S battery with DPDTe exhibits much higher initial dis-
charge capacity of 1227.3 mAh g1 and increased ICE of 97.18%. After
300 cycles, it still remains 639.0 mAh g1 corresponding to a capacity
retention of 52.06%, much higher when compared to the control bat-
tery. Additionally, the capacity contribution of DPDTe to the whole bat-
tery was investigated. It is worth to note that the Li–DPDTe battery has
good redox reversibility and the discharge capacity is well retained
without obvious decay, which suggests that the effect of DPDTe is last-
ing (Figures S6 and S7, Supporting Information). Moreover, the capac-
ity contributed by DPDTe can be negligible when compared with that
of Li–S batteries, which is higher than 1000 mAh g1. Therefore, the
improved cycling stability of Li–S batteries with DPDTe should mainly
result from the mediation effect of DPDTe. Figure 1c shows the
charge/discharge curves of Li–S batteries with/without DPDTe at 0.5 C
rate. Two voltage plateaus are observed for the control battery, indicat-
ing multi-step conversion reactions of lithium polysulﬁdes under
dissolution–deposition mechanism.[42,43] When DPDTe is added to Li–
S battery, there is a slight voltage change at 2.11 V for the discharge
voltage curve, which mainly results from the reduction of DPDTe.
Because the redox peaks of DPDTe are greatly overlapped with that of
Li–S battery (Figure 1a and Figure S6, Supporting Information), and
the amount of DPDTe additive added in Li–S battery is much low, just
50 mM. Therefore, the discharge–charge voltage proﬁles of the two
batteries generally possess similar voltage features, which is also in
accordance with the CV results (Figure 1a). The charge/discharge
curves of Li–S battery with DPDTe for 200 and 300 cycles are basically
overlapped, conﬁrming superior cycling stability. More importantly,
the Li–S battery with DPDTe also delivers excellent cycling performance
at high current density of 1 C (Figure S8, Supporting Information).
The EIS measurement was performed to explore impedance change
of Li–S batteries with/without DPDTe (Figure 1d, Figures S9 and S10,
Supporting Information). When compared to the control battery, the
Li–S batteries with DPDTe exhibits much lower resistance, suggesting
that the introduction of DPDTe beneﬁts redox kinetics of sulfur cath-
odes and stabilization of Li anode. For control battery, the impedance
spectrum just shows a phase element, resulting from the migration of
Li+ in the rough solid–liquid interfaces. While the Li–S battery with
DPDTe features two semicircles, in which the semicircle in high-
frequency region results from fast Li+ migration through sulfur cath-
odes and SEIs formed via chemical reaction of DPDTe with lithium
metal on the anode side. This suggests that the chemical reaction hap-
pens immediately once DPDTe is in contact with lithium anode. With
sufﬁcient electrolyte inﬁltration and several charge and discharge cycles,
a uniform interface is gradually formed in Li–S battery with DPDTe.
The semicircle in low-frequency region is due to charge transfer resis-
tance of interface between the electrode and electrolyte. After either 10
cycles or 100 cycles, the Li–S battery with DPDTe shows much lower
resistance than that of the control battery (Figures S8 and S9, Support-
ing Information), which can be attributed to the faster Li+ migration in
sulfur cathode and uniform SEIs on Li anode with addition of DPDTe.
In addition, GITT test was exploited to investigate polarization potential
of the electrochemical reaction. Compared with control battery, the Li–
S battery with DPDTe exhibits smaller polarization and higher capacity
in the whole cycling process (Figure 1e).
The Li–S battery with DPDTe also delivers much improved rate per-
formance than that of control battery (Figure 1f and Figure S11, Sup-
porting Information). Speciﬁcally, the Li–S battery with DPDTe shows
high discharge capacities of 1161.2, 835.9, 777.3, 666.3, and 611.4
mAh g1 at 0.1, 0.5, 1, 3, and 5.0 C rate, respectively. When the cur-
rent is back to 0.2 C rate, discharge capacity returns to 818.5 mAh g1,
demonstrating extraordinary rate capability. In contrast, the Li–S battery
without DPDTe exhibits inferior rate performance as capacity ﬂuctuates
greatly with the increasing current densities and follows a quick attenu-
ation trend. It should mainly result from the sluggish sulfur kinetic and
anode corrosion due to excessive LiPSs that are not timely converted on
the cathode side. The efﬁciency of DPDTe electrolyte additive under
harsh conditions (high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte) in Li–S bat-
teries was further evaluated. The Li–S battery with DPDTe delivers a
high discharge capacity of 1142.2 mAh g1 and maintains 640.4
mAh g1 after 100 cycles, corresponding to a capacity decay rate of
0.44% per cycle (Figure 1g). To validate practicality of DPDTe electro-
lyte additive, a Li–S pouch cell with DPDTe was also assembled

2.2. Properties of Li Anode with Organotelluride-Containing SEI
To gain insight into the effect of the organotelluride-containing SEI on
the behaviors of Li plating/stripping, the symmetric Li batteries were
assembled in which DPDTe was used as electrolyte additive. Blank ether
electrolyte was used in a control battery for comparison. Figure 2a,b
show Li plating/striping curves of the two batteries at current density
of 2 mA cm2 and capacity of 2 mAh cm2. The polarization voltage
of symmetric Li battery with DPDTe remains steady at about 61 mV
over 1000 h. However, the polarization voltage of control battery
increases to 120 mV after 200 h and ﬁnally failure happens. In addi-
tion, the symmetric Li battery with DPDTe also show a stable
polarization voltage of about 48 mV over 800 h at current density of
1 mA cm2 and capacity of 1 mAh cm2 (Figures S12 and S13, Sup-
porting Information). Figure 2c depicts the plating and stripping curves
of Li symmetric battery with and without DPDTe at various currents.
The DPDTe enables symmetric Li battery with a regular increase in volt-
age hysteresis as current density increases. By contrast, the control bat-
tery shows an unstable voltage proﬁle with ﬂuctuation of voltage
hysteresis. Moreover, Figure 2d,e demonstrate SEM images of Li anode
with/without DPDTe additive after cycling. For control battery, the Li
foil shows a corrosive and scraggy surface, which results from side
reactions of Li anode with soluble LiPSs. While for the Li anode with
DPDTe, the Li deposition presents a compact and smooth morphology
due to the organotelluride-containing SEI. This interfacial layer can real-
ize uniform Li deposition with signiﬁcantly suppressed dendrite
growth, which greatly improves the cycling stability of symmetric Li
batteries.

2.3. Properties of Sulfur Cathode with DPDTe Mediator
The inﬂuence of DPDTe on the electrochemical performance of sulfur
cathode was explored in more detail. First, the chemical reactions
between polysulﬁdes and DPDTe were investigated. Equal molar
amounts of DPDTe and Li2S6 were mixed to obtain a Li2S6 + DPDTe
solution. Figure 4a compares the UV–visible spectra of DPDTe, Li2S6,
and mixed Li2S6 + DPDTe solution. The Li2S6 features a signiﬁcant
absorption peak at 285 nm, and DPDTe exhibits strong absorption in
the wavelength range of 200–500 nm (Figure S16, Supporting Infor-
mation). However, the peak intensity of Li2S6 + DPDTe sample at
280 nm far exceeds the sum of the pristine Li2S6 and DPDTe, indicat-
ing the spontaneous exchange reaction between Te–Te and S–S bonds.
The results of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra for 7Li and
13C also conﬁrm the exchange reaction.[48] The 7Li NMR spectra of
Li2S6 features an apparent chemical shift at 2.81 ppm (Figure 4b), and
it moves to 2.28 ppm after the exchange reaction, which is mainly
resulted from electron transfer from electron-rich Te to Li via Te–S
bonds. The 13C NMR further proves the breaking of Te–Te bonds in
DPDTe after mixed with Li2S6 (Figure S17, Supporting Information).
Moreover, the existence of exchange reaction between DPDTe and Li2S6
was clariﬁed by Raman spectroscopy. DPDTe shows a series of peaks

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, we have designed a dual-functional electrolyte additive,
DPDTe, as both an efﬁcient LiPS mediator and a lithium regulator for
high-performance Li–S batteries. The fundamental interface chemistry
and mediation mechanism of DPDTe are systematically investigated. For
the cathode side, a Te radical-mediated catalytic cycle in the solid–liquid
interface can effectively accelerate the transformation of LiPS, thereby
suppressing shuttle effect and promoting kinetics of sulfur. For the Li
anode side, DPDTe helps enable a uniform organic–inorganic hybrid
SEI, thus realizing uniform Li deposition with dramatically suppressed
dendrite growth. Beneﬁting from the advantageous synergy, the Li–S
batteries with DPDTe exhibits impressive cycling stability and rate per-
formance. This study provides a simple and effective route to address
the issues for both S cathode and Li anode to achieve high-performance
Li–S batteries. Additionally, with help of computations, this strategy
could be adopted to design and screen out other compounds with simi-
lar chemical structures to solve the intrinsic problems of metal–S
battery.
