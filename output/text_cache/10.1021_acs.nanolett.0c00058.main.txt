ABSTRACT: Understanding of lithium polysulﬁde (Li-PS)
formation and the shuttle phenomenon is essential for practical
application of the lithium/sulfur (Li/S) cell, which has superior
theoretical speciﬁc energy (2600 Wh/kg). However, it suﬀers from
the lack of direct observation on behaviors of soluble Li-PS in
liquid electrolytes. Using in situ graphene liquid cell electron
microscopy, we have visualized formation and diﬀusion of Li-PS
simultaneous with morphological and phase evolutions of sulfur
nanoparticles during lithiation. We found that the morphological
changes and Li-PS diﬀusion are retarded by ionic liquid (IL)
addition into electrolyte. Chronoamperometric shuttle current
measurement conﬁrms that IL addition lowers the experimental
diﬀusion coeﬃcient of Li-PS by 2 orders of magnitude relative to
that in IL-free electrolyte and thus suppresses the Li-PS shuttle current, which accounts for better cyclability and Coulombic
eﬃciency of the Li/S cell. This study provides signiﬁcant insights into electrolyte design to inhibit the polysulﬁde shuttle
phenomenon.
KEYWORDS: lithium sulfur cells, lithium polysulﬁdes, ionic liquid, in situ transmission electron microscopy, graphene liquid cell
O
ver the last couple of decades, advancements in lithium-
ion based rechargeable cells have made great contribu-
tions to the commercialization of portable electric devices.
Nevertheless, there is still a great demand for developing the
next generation of rechargeable cells with high speciﬁc energy
and low material cost.1−4 One of the most promising candidates
is the lithium/sulfur (Li/S) cell. It has a superior theoretical
speciﬁc energy of 2600 Wh/kg (S + 2Li+ + 2e−→Li2S, 2.15 V vs
Li/Li+)5,6 and low price (<$200/ton) due to the abundance of
sulfur. However, the practical application of the Li/S cell has
been hampered by cell performance degradation due to its
unique reaction chemistry, in addition to the practical issues
associated with minimizing the weight of cell components that
do no store energy, especially the weight of the electrolyte
(electrolyte volume to sulfur weight ratio of <4 μL/mg is highly
desired). This involves the formation of lithium polysulﬁdes (Li-
PS, Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) through a complex soluble pathway and
their shuttle phenomenon.7,8
During lithiation of sulfur, thermodynamically stable Li-PS
are formed and dissolved in organic liquid electrolyte, followed
by diﬀusion of Li-PS toward the lithium metal electrode and its
reduction to form an insoluble dilithium sulﬁde (Li2S). The
increase of high-order Li-PS (Li2Sx, x > 4) concentration at the
lithium electrode leads to a reverse diﬀusion of high-order Li-PS
toward the sulfur electrode, the so-called Li-PS shuttle
phenomenon.9−11 The continuous Li-PS shuttle phenomenon
causes loss of active sulfur from the positive electrode and
degrades the capacity retention and Coulombic eﬃciency of the
cells within a few cycles. To mitigate the Li-PS shuttle eﬀect,
electrolyte modiﬁcation, such as including functional additives
in the electrolyte, has been strongly investigated.12−18 However,
a main obstacle for rational design of the electrolyte is due to the
lack of insight regarding the formation and diﬀusion behavior of
Li-PS in liquid electrolytes. Recently, the ionic liquid has been
investigated as a functional additive in the liquid electrolyte,
which plays an important role not only to limit the Li-PS shuttle
phenomenon13,14 but also to protect the lithium electrode by
forming passivation layer on the surface.19 However, the eﬀect of
ionic liquid in suppressing Li-PS shuttle was only suggested by
improved cycle retention and Coulombic eﬃciency of the cells
and has not been experimentally proved.
Recently, various microscopy or spectroscopy techniques
have been utilized to demonstrate the Li-PS behavior.20−24

According to Fick’s ﬁrst law, the ﬂux of the diﬀusing species (Li-
PS in the electrolytes in this case) is determined by the diﬀusion
coeﬃcient and initial concentration of Li-PS in the electrolyte
and they strongly depend on the viscosity and Li-PS solubility of
the electrolyte. Thus, it is expected that the addition of IL to the
liquid electrolyte can mitigate Li-PS shuttle due to its low Li-PS
solubility and high viscosity.
To electrochemically verify our hypothesis accounting for the
eﬀect of the IL-containing electrolyte, the Li-PS shuttle current
of the Li/S cells with the IL-containing electrolyte (50% of IL by
volume) and the IL-free electrolyte was chronoamperometri-
cally measured. As shown in the potential vs time plot (SI, Figure
S3), the Li/S cells were ﬁrst galvanostatically discharged to 1.7 V
(vs Li/Li+) and then charged until the charge capacity reached
80% relative to the discharge capacity obtained, where the
soluble high-order polysulﬁdes are favorably formed. After the
targeted state of charge was reached, the cell was kept at open
circuit for the voltage relaxation process to reach the equilibrium
potential and then the chronoamperometric Li-PS shuttle
current measurement was conducted, while holding the cells
at the measured equilibrium potential. If a continuous redox
reaction of Li-PS at the electrodes by Li-PS shuttle phenomenon
occurs during the test, a steady-state current should be
measured. As shown in Figure 1a, the IL-containing electrolyte
cell showed almost no current for 40 h with negligible charge
obtained (17 mAh/gS, Figure 1b), whereas the IL-free
electrolyte cell exhibited a signiﬁcant shuttle current (7−10
μA) that corresponded to a speciﬁc capacity of 430 mAh/gS,
(Figure 1c).

According to the results discussed above, the suppression
mechanism of the IL against Li-PS shuttle can be described in
the inset schemes in Figure 1a. With the existence of the IL in the
electrolyte, the reduction of Li-PS at the lithium electrodes as
solid Li2S was prohibited due to the lack of Li-PS diﬀused from
the sulfur electrode to the lithium electrode, whereas the
continuous reduction of Li-PS to the lithium electrode occurs
due to Li-PS shuttle eﬀect that keeps supplying Li-PS to the
lithium electrode during the measurement. With respect to this
Li-PS shuttle protection mechanism, the lithium electrodes
cycled with IL-containing and IL-free electrolytes were collected
from the Li/S cells and investigated under a SEM. The lithium
electrodes after the shuttle current tests with the IL-containing
electrolyte showed a smoother surface than that cycled with the
IL-free electrolyte (SI, Figure S4a,b). It implies that the Li-PS
reduction at the lithium surface is successfully prevented by
using IL-containing electrolyte. Also, the sulfur nanoparticles in
the sulfur electrode cycled with IL-containing electrolyte
maintain their spherical shape (SI, Figure S4c) compared to
the particles cycled with IL-free electrolyte (SI, Figure S4d).
EDS elemental analysis for the Li electrodes cycled using the IL-
containing electrolyte (SI, Figure S5) shows signiﬁcantly smaller
sulfur EDS peak compared to that of the lithium electrode cycled
using the IL-free electrolyte, indicating a smaller amount of Li2S
deposited on the lithium electrode. The more yellowish color of
the IL-free electrolyte than the IL-containing electrolyte after
the cycling (inset ﬁgure of SI, Figure S5) is the evidence of the
higher content of Li-PS in the cycled IL-free electrolyte, which
attributes to larger sulfur EDS peak of the lithium electrode
cycled using the IL-free electrolyte.
To investigate the eﬀect of the IL-containing electrolyte on
the electrochemical performance of the Li/S cells with a more
conventional cell conﬁguration, a cetyltrimethylammonium
bromide modiﬁed sulfur-graphene oxide composite consisting
of thin graphene oxide (GO) ﬂakelike particles with a thin sulfur
layer on the surface was prepared as an active electrode material
(SEM and TGA results of the S-GO-CTA composite are
available in SI, Figure S6).16,17 The prepared S-GO-CTA
composite is then made into a slurry and cast onto a porous
aluminum foam current collector and galvanostatically tested
(S-GO-CTA, 2 mg S/cm2) in 2032 type coin cells with the IL-
containing and the IL-free electrolytes. As an electrochemical
test, the S-GO-CTA electrode was cycled with the IL-containing
electrolyte and it exhibited better capacity retention and
improved Coulombic eﬃciency of more than 99% for 100
cycles. Although the higher speciﬁc capacity of the IL-free
electrolyte cell was demonstrated in early cycles, which could be
due to low viscosity of the IL-free electrolyte, the speciﬁc
capacity and Coulombic eﬃciency of the IL-free electrolyte cell
gradually decayed, and then the cell ﬁnally shorted after 85
cycles (Figure 1e). The improved electrochemical cell perform-
ance resulted from the IL addition that suppresses the Li-PS
shuttle due to its high viscosity and low Li-PS solubility,12−18
and the formation of solid−electrolyte interphase on the lithium
electrode that prevents dendrite formation during cycling.19

containing and the IL-free electrolytes using the in situ GLC-EM
technique and visualized the Li-PS behavior in the two
electrolytes. For GLC fabrication, two graphene sheets are

the sulfur nanoparticles in the IL-free and the IL-containing
electrolytes, respectively. As lithiation progresses, the spherical
sulfur nanoparticle in the IL-free electrolyte collapses and the
generated Li-PS intermediates surrounding the sulfur nano-

containing electrolyte (SI, Movie S2) maintains its original
morphology during lithiation process and the diﬀusion of the Li-

sulfur nanoparticle. By tracing boundaries, the lateral morphol-

expansion of the Li-PS region (dashed line) are examined

the diﬀusion of Li-PS in the IL-free electrolyte are much faster
than those in the IL-containing electrolyte under identical
experiment conditions (SI, Figure S8), implying that the
formation and diﬀusion of soluble Li-PS are eﬀectively
suppressed in the IL-containing electrolyte.

nanoparticle in the IL-containing electrolyte and the IL-free
electrolyte is not due to the diﬀerent thickness of the electrolyte
volume in the GLC, thickness calculation of the liquid
electrolyte pocket using the STEM-EELS thickness computing

obtained thickness proﬁle, liquid electrolyte pocket adjacent to
the sulfur nanoparticles are very similar regardless of the particle

electron beam-induced eﬀects is negligible. In addition, it was
found in our previous study30 that the lithiation rate in the GLC-

similar lithiation behavior of the sulfur nanoparticle is

that the thickness of the liquid pocket in the GLC is not
inﬂuential in the lithiation behavior of the sulfur nanoparticle.

inﬂuences in the image contrast was found (SI, Figure S15).

In summary, with the in situ GLC-EM technique, we
visualized the lithiation dynamics of sulfur nanoparticles in
liquid electrolytes in real time and demonstrated the eﬀect of the
IL on the formation and diﬀusion of Li-PS during the lithiation
process. During the lithiation process of sulfur nanoparticles in
the conventional IL-free electrolyte, the morphology of sulfur
nanoparticles changes from spherical to irregular due to the
formation and dissolution of Li-PS. In contrast, the sulfur
nanoparticle in the IL-containing electrolyte maintains its
pristine morphology during the lithiation process because the
low solubility of Li-PS and high viscosity of the IL-containing
electrolyte prevent the formation and diﬀusion of Li-PS. The
results of electrochemical Li-PS shuttle current measurements
are in accordance with the in situ GLC-EM observation, where
the Li-PS shuttle was successfully inhibited by the presence of
the IL in the electrolyte and an approximately 2 orders of
magnitude lower experimental diﬀusion coeﬃcient was obtained
for the IL-containing electrolyte compared to that of the IL-free
electrolyte. Consequently, the Li/S cell employing the IL-
containing electrolyte exhibited improved cyclability and
Coulombic eﬃciency over 100 cycles than the IL-free electrolyte
cell. We believe that this work provides signiﬁcant insights into
the electrolyte design for preventing the Li-PS shuttle
phenomenon in the Li/S cells.
