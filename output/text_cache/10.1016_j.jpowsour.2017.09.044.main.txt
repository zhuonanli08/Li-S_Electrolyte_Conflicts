Dissolved polysulﬁdes, formed during Li-S battery operation, freely migrate and react with both the Li
anode and the sulfur cathode. These soluble polysulﬁdes shuttle between the anode and cathode e the
so-called shuttle effect e resulting in an inﬁnite recharge process and poor Columbic efﬁciency. In this
study, water present as an additive in the Li-S battery electrolyte is found to reduce the shuttle effect in
Li-S batteries. Batteries where water content was below 50 ppm exhibited a substantial shuttle effect and
low charge capacity. Alternatively, addition of 250 ppm water led to stable charge/discharge behavior
with high Coulombic efﬁciency. XPS results show that H2O addition results in the formation of solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) ﬁlm with more LiOH on Li anode which protects the Li anode from the
polysulﬁdes. Batteries cycled without water result in a SEI ﬁlm with more Li2CO3 likely formed by direct
contact between the Li metal and the solvent. Intermediate quantities of H2O in the electrolyte result in
high cycle efﬁciency for the ﬁrst few cycles which then rapidly decays. This suggests that H2O is
consumed during battery cycling, likely by interaction with freshly exposed Li metal formed during Li
deposition.

1. Introduction
The lithium-sulfur (LieS) battery is among the most widely
explored next-generation Li battery, mainly due to its high cathode-
based theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh g1-sulfur which several
times higher than the theoretical capacity of the LiCoO2 cathode
(~272 mAh g1) used in conventional Li ion batteries [1e6]. In
addition, sulfur is also a naturally abundant element, nontoxic and
inexpensive [1,3].
One of the challenges attendant Li-S batteries is the dissolution
of partially discharged species which diffuse from the cathode
resulting in capacity loss [6,7]. These diffusing species can be
reduced at the Li metal anode and then reoxidized at the cathode
resulting in the so-called shuttle effect, which strongly limits bat-
tery efﬁciency [3,8]. A large volume expansion (~80% from S) of
cathode material occurs at the end of discharge process as a result
of the formation of insoluble lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) which also
causes detachment of cathode materials from the electrode [9,10].
Finally, an additional concern with LieS batteries relates to the use
of Li metal anodes. In particular, non-uniform deposition of Li re-
sults in Li dendrite formation, the presence of which can result in
safety issues [11e13]. Additionally, Li reacts with electrolyte and
forms a highly resistive solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer on
the Li surface, leading to low Coulombic efﬁciency of Li deposition
and stripping during repeated cycling [11].

A wide variety of strategies have been developed to amelio-
rate issues regarding the corrosion of Li metal and Li dendrite
formation [11e20]. One of the most popular involves addition of
LiNO3 to the electrolyte, resulting in formation of a protection
layer on the Li anode [18,19]. Hydroﬂuoric acid as an additive was
used to modify the SEI layer. Which mainly composed of Li2O,
LiOH, Li2CO3, and LiF [21,22]. Another method to prevent corro-
sion of the Li anode in a Li-S battery is the use of artiﬁcial pro-
tection layer such as Li3N which is successfully fabricated on the
surface of the Li anode at room temperature [16]. The protected Li
anode was then tested into a Li-S cell which exhibits high sta-
bility in the electrolyte compared with the primitive Li anode
[16]. Solid-state electrolytes or polymer electrolytes have been
used to block polysulﬁdes shuttling back and forth between S
cathode and Li [6,23e27]. The low concentration Csþ cation
formed electrostatic shield around the initial growth tip of the
protuberances was used to change the deposition process and
eliminates dendrite formation in Li metal [15,28]. The use of
highly concentrated electrolytes such as 4 M lithium bis(-
ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide in 1,2-dimethoxyethane enables the high-
rate cycling of a lithium metal anode at high Coulombic efﬁ-
ciency without dendrite growth [12].
One issue not well-studied is the role of water as an additive for
Li-S batteries. Indeed, many of the additives discussed above are
strongly associated with water. Water has been well-studied with
regard to its role in Li-ion batteries and Li metal electrodes in
general [11,20e22,29e32]. Togasaki et al. reported that trace
amount of H2O accompanied with CO2 can signiﬁcantly improve
the cycling performance of Li metal anode in the electrolyte con-
taining LiPF6/ethylene carbonate and diethyl carbonate [31]. Li
dendrite growth was suppressed in the 1 M LiPF6-propylene car-
bonate electrolyte with trace amounts of H2O (25e50 ppm), mainly
due to the formation of LiF-rich SEI layer which leads to a uniform
distribution of electric ﬁeld on the substrate surface and forms the
uniform and dendrite-free Li deposition [20]. The role of H2O in the
LiPF6 carbonate-based electrolyte used for Li-ion batteries has been
explored [20,29e31]. However, the effect of H2O on Li-S batteries is
not clear.
In this work, we used galvanostatic cycling, Cyclic Voltammetry
(CV) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to study the effect
of H2O as an additive in a Li-S cell. We show that different amounts
of H2O result in profoundly different behaviors in the battery,
particularly with regard to the shuttle effect. Long term cycling and
detailed measurements provide insight into the effect of H2O on the
Li-S battery construct.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Electrolyte preparation
The electrolyte was 1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl)
imide (LiTFSI) in 1:1 (v/v) solution of 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME,
anhydrous, 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, anhy-
drous, Sigma-Aldrich). DME and DOL were distilled and stored
with molecule sieves. The LiTFSI salt purchased from Sigma
Aldrich was further dried at 130 C under vacuum for 8 h [33].
The water content of the as-prepared electrolyte changes from
200~400 ppm to ~15 ppm after the distillation and drying pro-
cesses. Milli-Q water (>18 MU cm) was then added in the as-
prepared electrolyte to prepare “wet” electrolyte. The water

estimated to be accurate to ±15%.
2.2. Sulfur-carbon slurry and two-electrode Swagelok-cell
preparation
The cathode materials were made from a slurry consisting of
60 wt% sulfur (99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich), 30 wt% carbon black (Super-
P Li, Timcal Inc.), and 10 wt% polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF, Kynar
2801) binder mixed with anhydrous N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone
(NMP, Sigma-Aldrich) and stirred overnight [34]. The sulfur loading
was 0.5e0.9 mg-sulfur/cm2. The electrode diameter is one inch. We
used 0.3 mL electrolyte in the batteries. The ratio of S to electrolyte
volume is ~7 g/L. The separator is Whatman glass ﬁber separator
(GF/F) and Li metal (0.75 mm thick) is from Alfa Aesar. Lithium-
sulfur batteries were assembled in a modiﬁed Swagelok tube
ﬁtting apparatus (nylon, 100 inner diameter, Chicago Fluid System
Technologies) described previously [7]. Charge and discharge pro-
cesses in the Swagelok cell were carried out at the rate of 0.15 C
(calculated using the weight of sulfur) by using an Arbin Battery
Tester (Model BT 2043, Arbin Instruments Corp., USA). Li metal is
the counter/reference electrode in the two-electrode Swagelok cell,
and potentials are given with respect to Li/Liþ.
2.3. Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) measurement
Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) of the sulfur-carbon cathode was per-
formed using a 760C or 760D CH Instruments potentiostat in the
two-electrode Swagelok cell at scan rates of 0.1 mV/s. The current
density was estimated by using the mass of sulfur in the cathode.
2.4. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements
XPS measurements of the top layers of surface (~10 nm) were
performed using a Kratos AXIS Ultra spectrometer with a mono-
chromatic Al Ka (1486.6 eV) X-ray source operated at a base pres-
sure of 109 Torr. Ion sputtering experiments were performed using
a Physical Electronics PHI 5400 spectrometer equipped with a Al Ka
X-ray source also operated at a base pressure of 109 Torr. The ion
etching was carried out by using an Arþ beam (accelerating voltage
3 kV, 3  3 mm2 raster, and an ion current density of 15 mA/cm2).
The binding energy scale was referenced to the aliphatic hydro-
carbons at 285.0 eV. To avoid exposure to air, Li samples were
transferred to the XPS chamber using a sealed Ar-ﬁlled vessel.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. The effect of water on the electrochemical behavior of the
sulfur-carbon cathode
We ﬁrst study the effect of water-containing electrolyte on the
electrochemical behavior of the Li-S cell. Fig. 1A shows the initial
discharge and charge curves of the sulfur-carbon cathode cycled at
a 0.15 C rate between 3.2 V and 1.8 V. The electrolyte is 1 M LiTFSI
DME/DOL (1/1, v/v) with 15 ppm water referred to as “dry” elec-
trolyte (black line) and 250 ppm water referred to as “wet” elec-
trolyte (red line).
The discharge proﬁle of Li-S battery obtained in the dry elec-
trolyte shows two plateaus at ~2.37 V and ~2.05 V. The two plateaus
are associated with the typical sulfur reduction found in a Li-S
battery [7,35e39]. The discharge plateau at ~2.37 V is associated
with the formation of long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sx, x > 4) as a
consequence of opening the S8 ring. These polysulﬁde chains are
then shortened as the long-chain polysulﬁdes are further reduced
in the second plateau (~2.05 V) and form ﬁnal products such as Li2S
[40e42]. The charge proﬁle shows two plateaus at ~2.25 V and
~2.4 V associated with the oxidation of polysulﬁdes to elemental
sulfur. The ﬁrst charge plateau at ~2.25 V is associated with the

formation of short chain polysulﬁdes (Li 2Sx, x 4). The second
charge plateau at ~2.4 V gives an apparent charge capacity of
>2000 mAh/g, a non-physical number associated with the so-called
‘shuttle effect’ wherein oxidized, soluble long-chain polysuﬁdes
dissolve in the electrolyte, migrate to the Li metal anode, and are
reduced to short chain polysulﬁdes which are then oxidized again
at the cathode, starting the shuttle again [3,4]. The discharge-
charge proﬁle exhibiting the pronounced strong shuttle effect is
in a good agreement with previous studies [19,43,44].
The discharge curves obtained in the wet electrolyte exhibit
sulfur reduction plateaus shifted slightly (~2.35 V and ~2.1 V) from
those obtained in the dry electrolyte. The similarity between the
discharge proﬁles in the wet and dry electrolyte suggests that
similar sulfur reduction processes are operative. The ﬁrst charge
plateau in the wet electrolyte is at ~2.25 V which is the same with
the ﬁrst charge plateau obtained in the dry electrolyte. The short
second charge plateau at ~2.35 V and the vertical charge proﬁle
showing that the battery can be fully charged suggests that the
shuttle effect is substantially diminished in the wet electrolyte.
Fig. 1B shows the CV of the as-prepared cathodes obtained at a
scan rate of 0.1 mV/s between 3.2 V and 1.8 V in the electrolyte with
15 ppm water (dry electrolyte, black line) and 250 ppm water (wet
electrolyte, red line). The negative scan of the sulfur-carbon cath-
ode obtained in the dry electrolyte shows the typical reduction
peaks at ~2.4 V and ~1.95 V and oxidation peaks at ~2.35 V and
~2.5 V [7,35]. The redox peaks correspond to the discharge and
charge plateaus shown in Fig. 1A. The CV obtained in the dry
electrolyte exhibits a larger second oxidation peak at ~2.5 V and
higher anodic current between 2.7 V and 3.2 V relative to the CV
obtained in the wet electrolyte. In the dry electrolyte, the oxidation
wave never reaches zero, a behavior consistent with the shuttle
effect described above. In addition, we found that there are slight
changes in the discharge curves and voltammetry with water
addition. These changes could relate to the effect of water on the Li
anode and polysulﬁde species.
Fig. S1 shows the effect of water concentration on the capacity of
Li-S cell. When the water concentration is above 1000 ppm, the
potential of typical two discharge plateaus changes and the ca-
pacity of Li-S cell decreases drastically. We speculate that a reaction
between polysulﬁde and water could be the origin of the decreased
capacity with the greater water concentration.
We next study the effect of water on the long term cycling
performance of the Li-S battery. Fig. 2 shows the cycling perfor-
mance of the Li-S cell including discharge capacity and Columbic
efﬁciency in the DME/DOL electrolyte containing 50 ppm and
250 ppm H2O operated at a 0.15 C rate. We used 50 ppm H2O rather
than the 15 ppm H2O discussed in Fig. 1A because the shuttle effect
prevents battery charging with the lesser amount of H2O. In order
to construct a battery that could be cycled, we utilized an electro-
lyte containing 50 ppm H2O. The discharge-charge curves of sulfur-
carbon cathode obtained in the DME/DOL electrolyte with 50 ppm
H2O exhibits typically two discharge plateaus at 2.37 V and 2.03 V
(Fig. S2) which is similar with the discharge curve obtained in the
DME/DOL electrolyte with 15 ppm H2O.
Fig. 2A shows that the battery with 50 ppm H2O exhibits an
initial discharge capacity of ~800 mAh/g-sulfur. This capacity fades
in the following cycles, consistent with previous studies, albeit
where H2O content was not controlled. The capacity is ~300 mAh/g
after 100 cycles with the S:electrolyte ratio ¼ ~7 g/L. As compared
to the previous studies [45], the low S:electrolyte ratio used in this
study results in poor battery performance, mainly due to the large
polysulﬁde dissolution during cycling. Fig. 2B shows that the
Columbic efﬁciency decreases from 93% to below 10% after 15 cy-
cles, due to the reappearance of a substantial shuttle effect. This
result suggests that the effect of added H2O at this level is dimin-
ished as a function of cycle number.
The Li-S battery cycled with 250 ppm H2O exhibits a similar
initial capacity (~900 mAh/g-sulfur) relative to the battery operated
with 50 ppm H2O and fades in the following cycles to ~300 mAh/g-
sulfur after 100 cycles. The Columbic efﬁciency is found to be
91 ± 2% during the 100 cycles. The Columbic efﬁciency of sulfur-
carbon cathode is depending on whether S8 is encapsulated
[3,46], the use of a solvate electrolyte [47,48], or the presence of
LiNO3 [19,43,49]. With any of these additional features present in
the battery, the Columbic efﬁciency can be as high as 99.9%. To
examine the effect of H2O, we did not use any of these additional
treatments to elevate the Columbic efﬁciency of the battery. Our
91 ± 2% Columbic efﬁciency number is consistent with earlier ap-
proaches absent enhancements above [44,49]. The battery perfor-
mance obtained with different amount of H2O suggests that water
can reduce the shuttle effect.

These results suggest that the major difference in the Li surface
between the wet and dry electrolyte is the presence of carbonate on
the Li surface formed in an electrolyte with less water. There are
several possible origins of the carbonate. First, it could result from
solvent breakdown at the electrode surface. Indeed, prior results
suggest DOL/DME can break down under reducing conditions [18].
The second possible origin of the carbonate is from transfer of the Li
electrode into the XPS chamber [53]. In this scenario, the Li surface
formed in the dry electrolyte would be more susceptible to deco-
ration by adventitious CO2 relative to the surface formed in wet
electrolyte. However, the presence of other similar species between
the two suggests that differential adventitious decoration is not the
origin of the carbonate found. The major component of the SEI
found in the XPS measurements, rather, is LiOH.
The presence of LiOH in the wet electrolyte, likely associated
with H2O decomposition on the Li electrode, leads to a passivated Li
electrode which cannot support the polysulﬁde reduction found
with the shuttle effect. The formation of LiOH on the Li surface
could increase lithium ion transport which results in better cycling
performance. On the other hand, the presence of carbonate in the
dry electrolyte suggests that bare Li sites are available with which
to reduce the polysulﬁde. In systems with intermediate amounts of
H2O, such as the 50 ppm system described in Fig. 3, sufﬁcient H2O is
present to initially form a passivation layer on the Li metal elec-
trode. After a limited amount of cycling, however, the water pre-
cursor for the passivation layer is consumed, polysulﬁdes can be
reduced, and the shuttle effect commences.
4. Conclusions
The effect of H2O additive on the Li-S cell was evaluated with
galvanostatic cycling, CV and XPS. The recharge proﬁles obtained in
the conventional electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME, with different
amount of H2O are different, suggesting the changes in the shuttle
mechanism. The CV obtained in the dry electrolyte exhibits a larger
second oxidation peak at ~2.5 V and higher anodic current between
2.7 V and 3.2 V relative to the CV obtained in the wet electrolyte,
suggesting that the H2O additive inhibits the shuttle effect. The wet
DME/DOL electrolyte allows the Li-S batteries to reach the full state
charge during the charge process which reduces the shuttle effect
and yields better Columbic efﬁciency. Using XPS, we observed that
the amount of LiOH on Li anode increases in the wet electrolyte. The
composition of SEI ﬁlm can be tailored to yield beneﬁcial cycling
behavior in the H2O -containing electrolyte. Based on the changes
in the shuttle effect obtained with different amount of H2O, H2O in
the solvent and cathode materials should be taken into account
when optimizing battery cycling performance. Additionally, the
effect of water is diminished as a function of cycle number, sug-
gesting that the Li deposition and stripping process likely consumes
water. The results presented here suggest that the adventitious
effects of water addition must be considered when optimizing
battery performance.
