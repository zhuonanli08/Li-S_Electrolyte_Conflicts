Unfortunately, Li–S batteries also experienced significant 
capacity attenuation at LTs, presenting a considerable barrier to 
implementing them in a broader range of environments, par-
ticularly in special working conditions like polar/space explo-

explanation for the capacity loss of Li–S batteries in LT envi-
ronment is the inherent sluggish redox kinetics caused by 
the aggregation of intermediates, lithium polysulfides (LPSs), 
and the deposition of insoluble LPS, which insulated the elec-
trode surface and further obstructed the mass transfer of the 
charge carrier.[10] For this reason, several strategies have been 
devised. For example, materials with catalytic activity were 
introduced into the sulfur cathode to promote the LPS con-
version kinetics;[11–13] some electrolyte additives were used to 
inhibit the formation of LPS clusters[14,15] or change the cathode 
reduction way.[16] However, the de-solvation process of Li+ in 
Li–S batteries hold promise for pushing cell-level energy densities beyond 
300 Wh kg-1 while operating at low temperatures (LTs, below 0 °C). However, 
the capacity release of existing Li–S batteries at LTs is still barely satisfactory, 
and there is almost no verification of the practicability of Li–S batteries at 
LTs in the Ah-level pouch cell. Here, antecedent molecular dynamics (MDs) 
combined with density functional theory analysis are used to systematically 
investigate Li+ solvation structure in conventional Li–S batteries at LTs, which 
unprecedentedly reveals the positive correlation between lithium salt concen-
tration and Li+ de-solvation barrier, indicating dilute electrolytes can enhance 
the Li+ de-solvation kinetics and thus improve the capacity performance of 
cryogenic Li–S batteries. These insights derived from theoretical simulations 
invested Li–S batteries with a 67.34% capacity retention at −40 °C compared 
to their room temperature performance. In particular, an Ah-level Li–S pouch 
cell using dilute electrolytes with a high sulfur loading (5.6 mg cm-2) and 
lean electrolyte condition is fabricated, which delivers a discharge capacity 
of about 1000 mAh g-1 and ultra-high energy density of 350 Wh kg-1 at 0 °C, 
offering a promising route toward a practical high-energy cryogenic Li–S 
battery.

1. Introduction
Lithium-based batteries power virtually all modern portable 
devices and electric vehicles, and their ubiquity continues 
to grow.[1,2] Recently, great attention has been paid to the per-
formance of batteries at low temperatures (LTs), as emerging 
applications in energy storage require increasing tolerance to

the electrolyte, as another pivotal factor affecting the kinetics 
performance,[17] is rarely paid attention to in cryogenic Li–S 
batteries. Theoretically, the sluggish Li+ de-solvation process at 
the electrode/electrolyte interface leads to increased Li+ mass 
transfer impendence from the bulk electrolyte to the active 
materials (LPSs), significantly affecting the overall extent of 
sulfur cathode reaction.[18–20]
Herein, the antecedent molecular dynamics (MDs) com-
bined with density functional theory (DFT) analysis provide 
theoretical guidance that Li+ de-solvation energy barrier is 
positively correlated with LiTFSI concentration, which indi-
cates that the reduction of salt concentration can enhance the 
Li+ de-solvation kinetics and thus improve the capacity perfor-
mance of cryogenic Li–S batteries. Guided by the theoretical 
simulation results, we prepared Li–S battery electrolytes using 
conventional solvents (DOL/DME, 1:1) and various concentra-
tions of LiTFSI and studied their LT performance. As expected, 
reducing LiTFSI content in electrolytes promotes the discharge 
capacity of Li–S batteries at LTs effectively. And Li–S batteries 
with 0.5  m electrolyte retain 67.34% capacity at −40  °C when 
compared with that at 25 °C. In particular, a 2.5 Ah Li–S pouch 
cell using 0.5 m with a high sulfur loading (5.6 mg cm−2) and 
lean electrolyte condition (the mass ratio of electrolyte to sulfur, 
E/S, is only 4 mL g−1) was fabricated, which demonstrated ultra-
high energy density (350 Wh kg−1) at 0 °C and verified the prac-
tical application of this dilute electrolyte.
2. Results and Discussion
Recently, the magic of electrolyte concentration has aroused 
widespread interest. The low-concentration electrolytes (<1 m), 
which not only reduce the cost but demonstrate some unique 
characteristics, have illustrated certain advantages in some 
battery types such as LIBs and sodium-ion batteries, but the 
mechanism of diluted electrolytes on LT performance of the 
battery is not clear. Previous studies have indicated that the de-
solvation behavior is a direct result of the inherent solvation 
structure of the electrolytes, which is pivotal in describing the 
LT performance discrepancy.[17,21,22] Therefore, MD simulations 
combined with DFT analysis were first applied to investigate 
the Li+ solvation structure in conventional Li–S electrolytes with 
various LiTFSI concentrations (0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 m) and explore 
the potential relationship between salt concentration and Li–S 
battery’s LT performance. The snapshots obtained from MD 
simulations of three electrolytes were displayed in Figure 1a–c.
In the electrolyte, the solvent-shell structure around Li+ is 
dynamic and diverse. Therefore, we first examined each Li+ in 
the simulation systems and determined their solvated structure 
with a radius of 0.3 nm as the solvation shell boundary by ana-
lyzing the MD trajectories. To simulate the actual state of the 
electrolyte working in Li–S batteries, we introduced a certain 
amount of electrolyte additive (LiNO3) and LPS into the simu-
lation box. All the results were summarized in Table S1, Sup-
porting Information, which realized a balance between cation/
solvent and cation/anion. Specifically, the Li+/anion binding 
has generally been neglected in the de-solvation energy calcu-
lations of previous studies due to the repulsion from the sig-
nificant negative polarization of the electrode (Figure 1d).[17,23] 
Therefore, Li+ de-solvation process can be further simplified. 
Figure 1f lists the proportion of various Li+ solvated structures 
in electrolytes with different LiTFSI concentrations. It can be 
found that the coordination structure of DME12 (meanings one 
Li+ coordinate to the two oxygens in DME) appeared most fre-
quently in both three electrolytes, indicating that lithium ions 
in the electrolyte are inclined to be solvated by DME. The de-sol-
vation free energy of each solvation structure is determined sys-
tematically by DFT calculations. Figure 1e lists the free energy 
of the seven primary solvation structures in the electrolyte at 
298.15 K. All de-solvation free energy values for every solvation 
structure can be found in Tables S2–S4, Supporting Informa-
tion. Based on the statistical results in Figure  1f, the average 
de-solvation energy in each electrolyte with various LiTFSI con-
centrations can be calculated using weighted average methods 
(Figure  1g), demonstrating a positive correlation between Li+ 
de-solvation energy barrier and lithium salt concentration in 
the electrolyte, and this positive correlation remains same 
both at 298.15 and 233.15 K. The above results indicate that the 
decrease of LiTFSI concentration could reduce the energy bar-
rier of the Li+ de-solvation process, promoting the kinetics of 
electrode reaction. Therefore, this faster de-solvation process 
brought by low LiTFSI concentration would promote the cryo-
genic performance of Li–S batteries.
To investigate the Li+ solvation structure of conventional 
Li–S electrolytes with different LiTFSI concentrations in the 
experiment, we measured spectral analysis on the electrolytes 
with a systematic variation of LiTFSI concentrations. Figure 2a 
displays Raman spectra results, where the S–N–S bending peak 
of the TFSI− at 747 cm−1 undergoes redshift when dissolved in 
DOL/DME solvents.[17,24] There was no corresponding signal in 
0.1  m electrolyte due to the ultra-low concentration of TFSI−. 
However, compared with 0.5 m electrolyte, the S–N–S bending 
peak in 1 m electrolyte undergoes a smaller shift, from 747 cm−1 
in the pure salt to 731 cm−1, indicative of much stronger cation-
anion interaction in the electrolyte with higher salt concentra-
tion.[25] In the 7Li NMR spectra results (Figure 2b), the 7Li peak 
in electrolyte shifts upfield upon increasing LiTFSI concentra-
tion from 0.1 to 1 m, which is indicative of increased Li+−TFSI− 
ion coordination at a higher salt concentration.[26] The above 
spectra result is consistent with the tendency obtained by MD 
simulation in which the coordination number of TFSI− around 
Li+ increases with higher salt concentration (Figure S1, Sup-
porting Information). Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) of 
six electrolytes with a systematic variation of LiTFSI concen-
trations (from 0 to 1 m) were further conducted to determine 
the solvation environment at different temperatures (25, 0, 
and −20  °C). Figure S2a, Supporting Information, summa-
rized the 2D scattering patterns of these six electrolytes meas-
ured at 25 °C, and their SAXS profiles were demonstrated in 
Figure S2b, Supporting Information. As the LiTFSI concentra-
tion increases in the electrolyte, the intensity of characteristic 
peaks around 15  nm−1 gradually decreases. In contrast, the 
intensity in the low Q region increased, making the overall peak 
wider. To reflect the influence of LiTFSI concentration increase 
on SAXS spectrum and obtain a more accurate peak signal, a 
peak deconvolution procedure is used to fit multiple Gaussian 
peaks to the scattering data and a term for the B-spline base-
line.[27] And the fitting results are summarized in Figure 2c. As

performance at low temperatures, contrary to the subjective 
impression because a decrease in salt concentration accompa-
nies a decrease in ionic conductivity. In Figure S7, Supporting 
Information, the electrolyte containing 1  m LiTFSI exhibited 
the highest Li+ conductivity from −40 to 25  °C. Besides, the 
ultra-low viscosity has always been an inherent advantage in 
DME/DOL-based electrolytes, especially under dilute salt con-
centration conditions (<1 m).[33,34] When LiTFSI concentration 
increases from 0.1 to 1  m, the electrolyte’s overall viscosity 
increases slightly from 0.00116 to 0.00237 Pa s−1 (Figure S8, Sup-

cosity of electrolyte is not the critical issue affecting the kinetics 
of Li–S battery when salt concentrations are below 1 m, and the 
decrease of Li+ de-solvation energy barrier in dilute electrolytes 
is the primary reason for the performance enhancement of

MD simulation results. It should be noted that LiTFSI con-

To further demonstrate the potential of employing dilute 
electrolytes under harsh conditions and validate the practicality 
in cryogenic Li–S batteries, an Ah-level Li–S pouch cell with 
0.5 m electrolyte was fabricated (Figure 4a). The sulfur loading 
of the cathode is up to 5.6 mg cm−2, and E/S is only 4 mL g−1. 
The overall weight of the pouch cell is only 15.52 g. Figure 4c 
was the charge/discharge profiles of the Li–S pouch cell cycled 
at 0 °C, which exhibits a typical double discharge platform in 
Li–S batteries, and delivers a high specific capacity of about 
1000 mAh g−1. The pouch cell maintained stable cycling for 
30 cycles with a capacity of about 2.5 Ah. Based on the mass 
of the entire pouch cell, including electrode tabs, current col-
lectors, and package, the corresponding energy density of Li–S 
pouch cell at 0  °C as up to 350  Wh kg−1 revealed the great 
potential for practical application (Figure 4d). We also further 
conducted a demonstration experiment to verify the scale-up 
application of dilute electrolytes. The Li–S pouch cell can power 
a small-scale electric vehicle, demonstrating the excellent reli-
ability and strong power of dilute electrolytes. (Figure 4b and 
Video S1, Supporting Information)
3. Conclusions
In summary, the conventional Li–S electrolytes with different 
LiTFSI concentrations were systematically investigated by MD 
simulations combined with DFT analysis, which surprisingly 
revealed a positive correlation between Li+ de-solvation energy 
barrier and LiTFSI concentration in the electrolyte. The Li+ de-
solvation process in a dilute electrolyte environment exhibits 
faster kinetics and promotes the discharge capacity of Li–S 
batteries at low temperatures. In the environment of exces-
sive electrolyte, Li–S battery with 0.5 m electrolyte delivered a 
high specific capacity of 674.1 mAh g−1 at −40 °C, which is up 
to 67.34% of the capacity at room temperature. In particular, the 
Ah-level Li–S pouch cell with 0.5 m electrolyte under practical 
working conditions realized a high specific capacity of about 
1000 mAh g−1 and a high energy density of 350 Wh kg−1 at 0 °C, 
offering a promising route toward practical high-energy cryo-
genic Li–S battery.
