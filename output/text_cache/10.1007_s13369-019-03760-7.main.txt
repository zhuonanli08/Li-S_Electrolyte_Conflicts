Effect of Ether-Based and Carbonate-Based Electrolytes
on the Electrochemical Performance of Li–S Batteries
Longyan Li1
· Lifan Wang1 · Ruiqi Liu1
Received: 2 August 2018 / Accepted: 2 February 2019 / Published online: 11 March 2019
© King Fahd University of Petroleum & Minerals 2019
Abstract
Lithium–sulfur batteries undergo a complicated electrochemical process while functioning. Their performance varies with
electrolytes and the electrode–electrolyte compatibility, and it is not possible yet to assert which kind of electrolyte could lead
to the optimum performance of lithium–sulfur batteries. In this work, different sulfur/carbon composites are prepared in both
crystalline and amorphous states by heating. The products are systematically studied as electrodes in lithium–sulfur batteries
containing two types of electrolytes: ether-based electrolyte and carbonate-based electrolyte. The amorphous composite
exhibits less polarization than the crystalline one in both electrolytes and can achieve higher utilization of sulfur in ether-
based electrolyte at a low current density and better cycle stability in carbonate-based electrolyte at a high current density.
Furthermore, the kinetics, to be speciﬁc, the surface activation energy and lithium ion diffusivity of the electrode in those two
electrolytes are presented to properly shed light on the electrochemical performance of the composites being investigated.
Keywords Lithium–sulfur battery · Sulfur/carbon composite · Ether-based electrolyte · Carbonate-based electrolyte · Kinetics
1 Introduction
Among various types of rechargeable batteries, Li–S bat-
teries have attracted increasing attention due to their high
theoreticalspeciﬁccapacity(1675mAhg−1)andenergyden-
sity (2600Wh kg−1), which are several times greater than
those of common lithium ion batteries [1–3]. Since sulfur is
low cost, abundant and nontoxic, Li–S batteries have great
potential applications in high energy density devices [4,5].
Nevertheless, the practical development of Li–S batteries
is hindered by some speciﬁc issues, namely, the dissolu-
tion of lithium polysulﬁdes into liquid electrolyte and its
resulting adverse “shuttle effect,” and the dendrite formation
on Li metal anodes [6–8]. Efforts have been carried out to
address these issues, among which electrolyte research plays
a crucial role since electrolytes directly inﬂuence the electro-
chemical performance of Li–S batteries [9–13]. Although the

It was reported that DOL (dioxolane) was ﬁrst applied as

various kinds of electrolytes containing DOL as the basic
solvent, such as LiCF3SO3 or LiTFSI as the electrolyte salt,
have been widely used in Li–S batteries. However, those fre-
quently used DOL-based electrolytes suffer from low boiling
and ﬂash points, which raises the safety risk when batter-

Wan and co-workers conﬁned sulfur as small molecules

carbon with a major portion of ultramicropore (< 0.7 nm)
as a host for sulfur cathode in carbonate electrolyte, which
exhibited good cycling stability and rate capability [24]. In

cycling performance with around 0.03 % capacity loss per

strong nucleophilic reactions [26,27]. However, it is favor-
able to use carbonate electrolyte when sulfur is restrained in
the “quasi-solid-state” so that the formation of polysulﬁdes
and their contact to carbonate are inhibited [28–30].
At present, the most commonly used cathode material for
Li–S batteries is the sulfur/carbon composite in which carbon
material serves as the matrix to hold sulfur. This is because
carbon will effectively help to improve the conductivity of
the electrode and the utilization of sulfur and accommodate
the volume expansion of sulfur [31–37]. According to our
previous work, we noticed that the sulfur/carbon composite
can be generally divided into two categories in accordance
with the XRD characterization: One presents the obvious
crystalline state with all characteristic peaks in XRD pat-
tern attributed to sulfur and the other shows the amorphous
state of carbon matrix. To study the compatibility of com-
posites and electrolytes, the impact exerted by those two
different sorts of electrolytes, carbonate-based electrolyte
and ether-based electrolyte, on the speciﬁc electrode was
systematically investigated. The amorphous-state composite
exhibited good compatibility with both electrolytes, while it
showed higher speciﬁc capacity at a low current density in
ether electrolyte and better cycle stability at a high current
density in carbonate electrolyte. The kinetics of electrodes
in different electrolytes was further carried out to understand
the electrochemical performance.
2 Experiment
2.1 Material Preparation and Characterization
Preparation of the crystalline-state composite: Sublimed sul-
fur and Ketjen black (EC-600JD) with certain weight ratio
were mixed by ball milling for 30min with ethanol as the
medium. Then, the mixture was dried at 50 ◦C to remove
ethanol. The obtained sulfur/carbon composite was further
heated at 155 ◦C for 6 h in a sealed reactor to obtain the ﬁnal
crystalline-state sulfur/carbon composite.
Preparation of the amorphous-state composite: The dried
sulfur/Ketjen black precursor was ﬁrstly obtained from the
same wet ball milling method illustrated before. Then, the
precursor was moved into a tube furnace for the melting pro-
cedure under argon atmosphere, to be speciﬁc, 155 ◦C for
6 h and then 300 ◦C for a certain time (15, 30, 45, 60, 75,
90, 105, 120 min, respectively). Among these heat-treated
materials, the ﬁnal amorphous-state composite was selected
through XRD (Rigaku D/max-2500) characterization.
The sulfur content of all those composites was measured
by the Carbon/Sulfur Analyzer (CS-902, Wanlianda Xinke,
Beijing, China). The crystalline-state and the amorphous-
state composites with the same sulfur content (60%) were
chosen for further electrochemical test.
2.2 Electrochemical Characterizations
The cathode slurry was prepared by mixing 80wt% the as-
prepared S/C composite, 10wt% acetylene black and 10wt%
PVDF binder in N-methyl-pyrrolidinone (NMP) solvent. The
slurry was then spread onto aluminum foil substrate and dried
under vacuum at 60 ◦C for 24h. The dried electrode was fur-
ther punched into sheets with the diameter of 14mm. CR2032
type coin cells were assembled in an argon atmosphere glove
box with H2O and O2 contents below 1 ppm. The carbonate-
based solution, 1 M LiPF6 EC+DMC (V:V, 1:1), and the
ether-based solution, 1 M LiTFSI DOL+DME (V:V, 1:1),
wereemployedaselectrolyte,respectively.Celgard2400was
used as the separator, and lithium foil was used as anode. The
galvanostatic charging–discharging tests of Li–S cells were
operated on a LAND CT2001 (Neware Co. Ltd) battery tester
between 1.0 and 2.8V versus Li+/Li at room temperature.
The cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed on the
Autolab Electrochemical Workstation with the voltage range
of 1.0–2.8V and the scanning rate of 0.1mV S−1. The AC
impedance spectroscopy was measured over the frequency

3 Results and Discussion
Figure 1 shows the XRD spectra of the S/C composite heated
at 155 ◦C for 6 h. It can be seen that the S/C composite gener-
ally presents the compound state with characteristic peaks at
2θ = 15.58◦, 23.27◦, 26.05◦, 26.89◦, 27.92◦, 28.86◦and
31.59◦which are identical to the standard one of elemental
sulfur (JCPDS#08-0247). The broad peak around 25 depicts
a typical amorphous form of carbon. Thus, the XRD pat-
tern of the whole composite exhibits a superposition state of
crystalline elemental sulfur and amorphous carbon matrix.
As we know, the molten sulfur at 155 ◦C shows the lowest
viscosity and the best ﬂuidity, which is in favor of sulfur
molecule entering into the inner pore of Ketjen black. After
being heated at 155 ◦C for 6 h, sulfur that attaches to the
surface of the Ketjen black particle will recrystallize into
crystalline state during the cooling process, resulting in the
obvious characteristic peaks of crystalline sulfur in the XRD
spectra for the whole S/C composite.
The composites after being heated at 155 ◦C are further
heat treated at 300 ◦C for 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 105 and
120 min, respectively. The Carbon/Sulfur Analyzer was used
to measure the sulfur content of those composites, and the
results are shown in Fig. 2. As characterized in our previous
work [38], the Ketjen black consists of spherical particles
with a diameter of 10–25 nm and has large speciﬁc sur-
face area and pore volume with a pore diameter generally

0247 of elemental sulfur
of around 3–5.5 nm. On the basis of the distribution in the
composite, sulfur can generally be divided into three types.
The ﬁrst type is the sulfur on the surface of the Ketjen black
particle. This kind of sulfur simply attaches to the surface of
carbon particle without any restriction, making them most
prone to lose in vapor form with the rise of temperature. The
second type is the sulfur under the surface and in the shallow
part of carbon particle. This part of sulfur, which distributes
within the carbon pore, is more difﬁcult to lose versus the
increase in temperature, due to the restriction by pore wall
as well as the adsorption force from micropores. The third
type is the sulfur in the deep interior of particle. Deﬁnitely,
this part of sulfur is the most difﬁcult to lose because of the
strongest constraint by the abundant micropores in the inner
part of carbon particle.
As shown in Fig. 2, the sulfur content in the composite
is dropping along with heating due to evaporation. Never-
theless, it can be noticed that according to the curve slope,
the loss rate of sulfur changes during the heating process.
Notably, the slope value from 15 to 45 min is the highest,
indicating the fastest evaporating rate of sulfur. From 45 to
105 min, the slope value slightly decreases, meaning that the
evaporating rate of sulfur becomes slower. From 105 to 120
min, the loss rate of sulfur further slows down in accordance
with the smallest curve slope value. Based on the previous
analysis, the sulfur which evaporates from 15 to 45 min is
those on the surface of Ketjen black, and the sulfur lost from
45 to 105 min and 105 to 120 min can be attributed to those
located in the shallow part and the deep interior of carbon par-
ticle, respectively. The sulfur on the surface of carbon particle
has a larger contact area with electrolyte, which means that
it is easier to dissolve into electrolyte, leading to the loss of
active materials.

The XRD characterizations were carried out to further
studytheimpact of heatingtimeonthecrystallinestateof car-
bon/sulfur composite. As shown in Fig. 3, composites heated
under 300◦C for 15 min and 45 min all present the XRD pat-
tern superposed by crystalline sulfur and amorphous carbon,
while the diffraction intensity of the characteristic peaks of
sulfur decreases. Moreover, it can be seen that composites
transform from crystalline state to amorphous state as the
heating time increases. It is well known that at room temper-
ature, sulfur exists in the crystalline state of S8 and porous
carbon is in the amorphous state. Therefore, with a large
amount of crystalline sulfur adhering on the surface of Ketjen
black, it makes the composite present the crystalline state as
sulfur. As for the amorphous composite, sulfur mainly exists
in the internal pores of carbon particle and is restricted by
the irregular pore walls so that the amorphous composite can
beneﬁt from the conﬁnement effect of matrix. Additionally,
the sulfur content of the amorphous composite heated for 105
minand120minis61.3%and60.9%,respectively,beneﬁting
from the large speciﬁc surface area of Ketjen black.
The crystalline composite and the amorphous one with the
same sulfur content (60%) were chosen to study the inﬂu-
ence of different electrolytes, ether-based electrolyte (Fig. 4)
and carbonate-based electrolyte (Fig. 5), on the electrochem-
ical performance of those two kinds of composite in Li–S
batteries. Figure 4a, c shows charge/discharge curves and
cyclic voltammetry test results for the initial three cycles of
crystalline-state composite in ether electrolyte, respectively,
and Fig. 4b, d shows those of amorphous-state composite in
ether electrolyte. As we can see, the crystalline-state compos-
ite and the amorphous one show similar charge and discharge
proﬁles. In general, the discharge curves of both composites
in ether electrolyte present two obvious plateaus at around
2.3 V and 2.0 V. The plateau at around 2.3 V can be assigned
to the reduction of S8 to higher-order Li polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn,
n ≥4), and the plateau at 2.0 V is related to the further reduc-
tion in those higher-order polysulﬁdes to the ﬁnal Li2S. In
charge curves, the broad plateau at around 2.3 V is related
to the conversion of Li2S to S8. However, it can be noticed
that in discharge curves, the plateau, especially the second
plateau at around 2.0 V, of crystalline composite is shorter
than that of amorphous composite, indicating the dissolu-
tion of polysulﬁdes. Compared with amorphous composite,
crystalline composite has more sulfur attached to the surface
of the carbon particle which suffers the weakest restriction.
Once this part of sulfur is reduced to soluble polysulﬁdes,
they will easily dissolve into electrolyte, leading to capac-
ity fading. The cycle performance of those two composites
in ether electrolyte at 400 mA g−1 shown in Fig. 4e further
demonstrates that the amorphous composite delivers better
cycle stability than the crystalline one.
As for the corresponding cyclic voltammetry test, it can
be clearly seen that in ether electrolyte, the CV curve of
the crystalline cathode (Fig. 4c) is different from that of the
amorphous cathode (Fig. 4d). To be speciﬁc, the reduction
peaks of crystalline cathode at around 2.3 V and 2.0 V are
broad and not that evident. Additionally, the shape and the
position of its oxidation peak change a lot along with cycling,
indicating critical polarization with the crystalline electrode.
On the other hand, the amorphous cathode presents much
sharper reduction and oxidation peaks, with less changing
for the shape and position along with cycling, manifesting
less polarization in the amorphous cathode. As analyzed
before, for the crystalline-state composite there exists sul-
fur bulk deposited on the surface of carbon particle. Due
to its low conductivity for both electrons and ions, it will
make the composite undergo a nonuniform conducting pro-
cess for electrons and ions, resulting in the polarization of the
crystalline composite. However, after removing the surface
sulfur, the ﬁnal amorphous composite has a more homoge-
nous distribution of sulfur within the inner paths and pores of
the carbon particle. Therefore, the polarization of amorphous
composite can be reduced to a certain extent.
Comparatively, Fig. 5 presents characterizations of com-
posites in carbonate electrolyte. As shown in Fig. 5a (crys-
talline) and Fig. 5b (amorphous), there is no stable plateau
in charge/discharge curves of composites in carbonate elec-
trolyte compare to charge/discharge proﬁles of composites
in ether electrolyte. The discharge voltage slowly attenuates
from about 1.9 to 1.0V, corresponding to the whole reduction
of S to Li2S, which is in accordance with any other reference
of Li–S batteries based on carbonate electrolyte [39–42]. In
the case of CV results presented in Fig. 5c (crystalline) and
Fig. 5d (amorphous), both cathodes show one broad reduc-
tion peak and one broad oxidation peak. Notably, there exits
shift with both reduction peak and oxidation peak for crys-
talline cathode (Fig. 5c), while there is no obvious shift with
those of amorphous cathode (Fig. 5d), indicating better cycle
stability and less polarization in amorphous cathode. The
cycle performance depicted in Fig. 5e further proves that in

the electrode in carbonate electrolyte.
4 Conclusion
It is well known that sulfur cathode undergoes complicated
electrochemicalreactionsindifferentelectrolytespairedwith
lithium anode. According to XRD results, sulfur/carbon
composite can be divided into crystalline state, present-
ing diffraction peaks which are all attributed to sulfur, and
amorphous state. In this work, the crystalline composite
and the amorphous composite with the same sulfur con-
tent were chosen to study the electrochemical performance
in two sorts of electrolyte, the ether-based electrolyte and
the carbonate-based electrolyte. We can conclude that the
crystalline composite suffers from more serious polarization
and less stable cycle performance than the amorphous one
in either electrolyte. Furthermore, amorphous composite can
achieve a higher utilization of sulfur in the ether-based elec-
trolyte at a low current density and a better cycle stability in
the carbonate-based electrolyte at a high current density. The
kinetics study on the surface activation energy of the inter-
face between electrode and electrolyte as well as the Li+ ion
diffusivity of electrode helps to understand the electrochem-
ical performance properly. This work might give a hint on
the selection of electrolytes for Li–S batteries used in differ-
ent conditions. It would be better to choose ether electrolytes
when the battery is used for devices running under a low
current density, say, small-scale electronic equipment, while
the carbonate electrolyte would be more suitable for Li–S
batteries working under a high current density.
