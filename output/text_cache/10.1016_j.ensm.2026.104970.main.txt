A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries with sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathodes hold promises for high energy 
density but face critical challenges in conventional ether/ester electrolytes, including polysulfide dissolution, 
shuttle effects and incompatibility with lithium metal anodes. To address these issues, a systematic siloxane 
electrolyte screening strategy serves as the core of this work. Here, we propose a siloxane-based localized high- 
concentration electrolyte (4 M LiFSI PTTS/TTE (7:3 by volume), PT73) to address these issues. By leveraging the 
unique D-p orbital conjugation of siloxanes and tailored steric hindrance from propyl terminal groups, PT73 
weakens Li⁺-solvent coordination while promoting anion-dominated solvation structures, thereby forming a 
robust inorganic-rich SEI and suppressing polysulfide dissolution. Electrochemically, Li||Cu cells with PT73 
achieve 98.7 % average Coulombic efficiency (CE) and retain 98.2 % CE over 720 cycles. Li-SPAN full cells (4.4 
mg cm-2 sulfur loading) maintain 91.1 % capacity retention after 120 cycles. This screening paradigm provides a 
rational framework for siloxane electrolyte design, accelerating high-energy-density Li-S battery development.
1. Introduction
Current research in the battery industry is intensely focused on 
achieving higher energy densities, motivating the development of 
innovative electrochemical systems to overcome the limitations of 
conventional lithium-ion intercalation mechanisms [1,2]. Among 
emerging candidates, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, combining the high 
energy density of lithium metal anodes with sulfur’s high specific ca­
pacity, exhibit remarkable theoretical energy density. The full-cell-level 
are the theoretical specific energy of Li-S batteries are as high as 2600 
Wh kg-1, while the cathode composite level theoretical specific energy 
(considering sulfur and conductive carbon matrix) typically ranges from 
600 to 700 Wh kg-1, highlighting their great potential for 
high-energy-density energy storage systems [3,4]. Nevertheless, the 
intrinsic redox chemistry of Li-S systems inevitably leads to the forma­
tion of insulating discharge products (Li2S2 /Li2S) during electro­
chemical cycling [5]. These crystalline deposits progressively passivate 
electrode surfaces through epitaxial growth mechanisms, thereby dete­
riorating interfacial charge transfer kinetics. Concurrently, the dissolu­
tion and electromigration of polysulfide intermediates within the 
electrolyte bulk phase instigate characteristic shuttle phenomena, 
systematically degrading Coulombic efficiency (CE) through sustained 
parasitic redox cycling that disrupts the sulfur reduction pathway [6–8].
To address these challenges, researchers have explored sulfurized 
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) as a novel cathode material for Li-S batteries. 
During SPAN synthesis, the C–H bonds in PAN are converted into C = N 
bonds, resulting in the formation of a cyclized structure. Subsequently, 
the cleavage of S-S bonds in sulfur facilitates the grafting and anchoring 
of short-chain sulfur molecules into the cyclized framework via C-S and 
S-S bonds [9]. Endowed with exceptional conductive properties and 
theoretical solid-to-solid transformation reactions, the resulting com­
posite material offers novel insights into resolving the inherent limita­
tions of sulfur electrode [10]. What is intriguingly noteworthy is that, as 
research progresses, it becomes evident that the SPAN cathode does not 
achieve absolute solid-to-solid conversion across different electrolytes. 
In ether-based electrolytes with strong solvation capabilities, the 
dissolution of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) dissolution persists, leading to 
severe shuttle effects and rapid capacity fading [11,12]. Although SPAN 
cathodes exhibit a reaction mechanism distinct from the conventional 
dissolution-deposition process in carbonate-based electrolytes (e.g., 
diethyl carbonate (DEC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC)), the inherent 
chemical incompatibility between carbonate solvents and lithium metal

fundamentally limiting the practical application of carbonate electro­

electrolyte solvents necessitates dual optimization criteria rooted in 
interfacial chemistry mechanisms [16,17]: (1) Regulation of Li+ solva­
tion structure: the composition of the solvation sheath fundamentally 
dictates the decomposition pathways at lithium metal anodes. Specif­
ically, the preferential coordination of anions over solvent molecules 
facilitates the formation of anion-derived, inorganic-rich solid electro­
lyte interphases (SEI) layers, effectively suppressing dendritic growth 
and electrolyte consumption. (2) Suppression of LiPSs dissolution: 
strategic solvent selection should leverage ε manipulation and weak 
Lewis basicity to establish a thermodynamically unfavorable environ­
ment for LiPSs dissolution. Concurrently, optimized solvents promote 
the formation of cathode-electrolyte interphases with selective Li+
permeability, thereby kinetically inhibiting the shuttle effect through 
physical confinement and chemical passivation [18].
Compared to ether and ester solvents, siloxanes exhibit a unique 
advantage as potential ideal electrolytes for Li-SPAN batteries due to the 
silicon atom’s additional vacant 3d orbital, which enables D-p orbital 
conjugation with adjacent atoms [19–22]. The lower electronegativity 
of siloxanes further reduces solvent polarity, while hyperconjugation 
involving LP(O)→σ*(X-C, where X=Si, C) in siloxanes releases oxygen 
lone pairs into the antibonding orbitals of siloxanes, thereby lowering 
the electrostatic potential of coordinating oxygen atoms [23–27]. These 
factors synergistically weaken the coordination strength between Li+
and solvent molecules, enabling precise modulation of the Li+ solvation 
structure. Given that the strong binding energy of ether-based solvents 
(such as DME) with LiPSs often contributes to the shuttle effect in the 
Li-SPAN batteries [11,28], and the compatibility of ester-based solvents 
with lithium metal anodes remains suboptimal, siloxanes combine the 
advantages of both solvent types, making them promising candidates for 
Li-SPAN electrolytes. However, their potential as ideal solvents neces­
sitate further experimental validation to assess their performance and 
applicability in practical battery systems. Moreover, the relationship 
between the molecular structure of siloxanes and Li-S battery perfor­
mance remains unclear, lacking systematic molecular design principles 
and quantitative descriptors for structural screening and pattern anal­
ysis. Herein, we propose a set of siloxane molecular design principles to 
evaluate the rationality of selected siloxanes: (1) Sufficient lithium salt 
solubility. The designed siloxane molecules should possess appropriate 
polarity and chemical structures capable of coordinating with Li+ to 
facilitate the dissolution of lithium salts [29]. (2) Weaker solvation in­
teractions with Li+. Promoting the entry of more anions into the first 
solvation shell of Li+ and encouraging their preferential decomposition 
to form an SEI rich in inorganic compounds, such as LiF, is crucial for 
enhancing the compatibility of the solvent with the lithium metal anode 
[30]. (3) Inhibition of LiPSs dissolution. The design of siloxane mole­
cules should focus on reducing the coordination strength between the 
solvent and LiPSs, limiting their diffusion within the electrolyte, thereby 
suppressing the dissolution and shuttle effect of LiPSs. Based on the 
proposed design principles, we introduced the “LiPSs aggregation de­
gree in the electrolyte” as a novel indicator to evaluate the electrolyte’s 
ability to suppress LiPSs dissolution. This approach follows solvent 
screening based on dielectric constant (ε) [31,32], donor number (DN) 
[33,34], electrostatic potential (ESP) [35,36], and Li⁺-solvent binding 
energy, providing critical insights for the application of siloxanes in Li-S 
electrolytes. As a proof of concept, a 4 M LiFSI triethoxypropylsilane 
(PTTS)/1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether 
(TTE) 
(7:3 by volume) electrolyte (hereafter referred to as PT73) was devel­
oped by utilizing the localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) 
strategy [37]. Consistent with predictions, the triethoxysilane group in 
siloxane solvents ensures weak Li⁺-solvent binding, thereby enabling 
excellent compatibility with lithium metal. Simultaneously, by modu­
lating the alkyl chain length at the terminal group, the triethoxysilane 
molecule achieves appropriate steric hindrance, effectively suppressing 
the dissolution of LiPSs (Fig. 1). As a result, the Li||Cu cell with PT73 
electrolyte achieved a high average CE of 98.7 % and demonstrated 
long-term cycling stability with a CE of 98.2 % over 720 cycles. 
Furthermore, the Li-SPAN battery with a sulfur loading of 4.4 mg cm-2 
retained an exceptional capacity retention rate of 91.1 % after 120 stable 
cycles.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. Design of ideal Li-SPAN electrolyte siloxane solvent molecule
In the design of various siloxane solvent molecular structures, it is 
crucial to consider not only the balance between the solvent’s ability to 
dissolve lithium salts and its coordination capacity with Li+, but also 
ensure that, while facilitating adequate coordination between Li+ and 
anions, there is an effective reduction in the binding of Li+ with LiPSs. 
Consequently, the conceptualization of siloxane molecular solvent 
structure modulation is primarily divided into two components: altering 
the binding sites of Li+ with the solvent and modifying the spatial steric 
hindrance of the solvent molecules. Incorporating the proposed design 
strategy for siloxane molecular structures, we have crafted eight exem­
plary, well-ordered siloxane molecules for in-depth exploration 
(Fig. 2a): Ethoxytrimethylsilane (ETTS), Dimethyldimethoxysilane 
(DMMS), Diethoxydimethylsilane (DMES), Tetraethyl Orthosilicate 
(TEOS), Methyltriethoxysilane (MTES), Triethoxyethylsilane (TTOS), 
Propyltriethoxysilane (PTTS), and Butyl(triethoxy)silane (BTTS).
Alkoxy groups function as coordination sites for Li+, with their 
quantity influencing the distribution of negative charge and inducing 
spatial modifications at Li+ binding sites [38]. This, in turn, modulates 
the coordination strength of Li+. In Li-S electrolyte systems, DN, a core 
molecular descriptor characterizing the solvent’s electron-pair donating 
ability that quantitatively reflects the coordination tendency between 
the solvent and Li+, is widely utilized to assess the solubility of lithium 
salts in solvents and the strength of their interactions with LiPSs (Fig. 2b, 
S1). The introduction of solvents with high ε, a key indicator for gauging 
solvent polarity whose value directly dictates the solvent’s electrostatic 
shielding capacity, effectively shields the coulombic attraction between 
Li+ and FSI-, weakening their intermolecular interactions and promoting 
lithium salt dissociation. As illustrated in Fig. 2c, the siloxane series 
dominated by triethoxysilane (namely MTES, TTOS, PTTS, and BTTS) 
exhibit relatively higher ε and DN values compared to other siloxane 
molecules, indicating superior solubility for lithium salts [31]. These 
findings suggest that a lower minimum electrostatic potential (| 
ESPmin|), an important parameter reflecting the surface charge distri­
bution of solvent molecules whose absolute value is positively correlated 
with the solvent’s electrostatic attraction to Li+, and reduced Li-solvent 
binding energy contributes to a diminished coordination capacity and 
strength for Li+. Among these solvents, triethoxysilanes demonstrate the 
weakest |ESPmin| and Li-solvent binding energies. It is hypothesized 
that the three ethoxy groups disperse the electron cloud density around 
oxygen atoms, thereby reducing the negative electrostatic potential 
(Table S1). Lower |ESPmin| and Li-solvent binding energy values are 
associated with weakened coordination strength with Li⁺, facilitating the 
preferential occupation of anions in the first solvation shell. Compre­
hensive analysis of these molecular descriptors identifies triethox­
ysilanes as the optimal candidates within the siloxane series. These 
triethoxysilanes not only fulfill the requirements for efficient electrolyte 
ion transport but also enhance the solvation structure of Li⁺.
The steric hindrance of solvent molecules significantly influences the 
coordination behavior of LiPSs. Through experimental validation, it has 
been found that PT73 electrolyte exhibits a remarkable inhibitory effect 
on the dissolution of LiPSs (Fig. S2). By systematically adjusting the 
chain length at the termini of the siloxane solvent molecules, the solu­
bility of LiPSs can be precisely modulated. To evaluate the applicability 
of selected triethoxy series siloxanes in Li-S electrolytes, we employed 
the “aggregation degree of Li2S4 in the electrolyte” as a quantitative

2.2. Solvation structure of various electrolytes
The electrochemical compatibility between battery electrodes and 
electrolyte, along with the stability of SEI, is fundamentally governed by 
the solvation structure of Li+ within the electrolyte system, this solva­
tion structure especially when dominated by salt anions also plays a 
pivotal role in suppressing LiPSs dissolution, because the anion-enriched 
Li+ solvation sheath reduces the availability of free solvent molecules to 
form strong coordination complexes with LiPSs species, thereby 
lowering the solubility of LiPSs in the electrolyte and impeding their 
migration from the cathode to the anode, which is a key mechanism for 
mitigating the shuttle effect in Li-SPAN batteries. To elucidate these 
critical relationships, a multi-technique spectroscopic approach was 
implemented to investigate the anion coordination environment in both 
PTTS-based and conventional DD11 (1 M LiFSI DME: DOL 1:1 by vol­
ume) electrolytes across varying concentrations (Fig. 3a-d). 1 M DD11 
was selected as the control because it is a well-recognized classic base­
line electrolyte in the Li-S battery field with extensive industrial refer­
ence value, and LiNO3 was excluded from this system to eliminate 
additive interference for an accurate comparison of intrinsic solvent 
performance. Raman spectral deconvolution of the FSI- vibration modes 
(650–780 cm-1) enable the quantitative differentiation between solvent- 
separated ion pairs (SSIPs), contact ion pairs (CIPs), and aggregate 
clusters (AGGs) through characteristic peak assignments at approxi­
mately 720 cm-1732 cm-1, and 745 cm-1 respectively [43,44]. Notably, 
at an equivalent 1 M LiFSI concentration, the DD11 electrolyte exhibits a 
significantly higher SSIPs content (21.7 % vs. 9.97 % in PTTS), sug­
gesting weaker anion coordination in the conventional electrolyte sys­
tem (Fig. 3e). This disparity became more pronounced at elevated 
concentrations, where the PT73 electrolyte demonstrates dominant 
contributions from CIPs/AGGs (93.3 % combined) compared to dimin­
ished presence of SSIPs (6.7 %), indicative of anion-rich solvation shells. 
Complementary Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis reveal 
distinct Li+-FSI- interaction signatures at 1386 cm-1 (coordinated FSI-) 
and 1365 cm-1 (free FSI-). The peak at 1386 cm-1 signifies the interaction

2D NMR analysis provides reliable insights into the overall exchange 
rates and diffusion kinetics of various species within liquid solutions, 
serving as a tool to investigate the interactions between LiPSs and sol­
vents [56]. First, the 1H NMR analysis illustrates the markedly different 
intermolecular interactions between lithium polysulfides and different 
solvent molecules (Fig. 4d). In this context, two-dimensional 
diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) 1H NMR is employed to probe 
the self-diffusion properties of the solvents (Figures S11, S12). Given 
that DOL and TTE localize in the outermost solvation shells in MD 
simulations and show minimal reactivity toward LiPSs, the analysis is 
focused on probing the interplay between the dominant solvents (PTTS 
and DME) and LiPSs. The self-diffusion coefficients (D values) for PTTS 
and DME are determined to be 1.63 × 10–9 and 3.6 × 10–9 m2 s-1, 
respectively. Upon the addition of 0.02 M Li2S6, the self-diffusion of 
PTTS decreased by 6.7 %, reaching 1.52 × 10–9 m2 s-1, whereas DME’s 
self-diffusion diminished by 19.1 %, reaching 2.91 × 10–9 m2 s-1(Fig. 4e, 
F). This more pronounced reduction in self-diffusion further corrobo­
rates the preference of LiPSs to complex with the strongly polar solvent 
DME, while the interaction with PTTS is significantly diminished due to 
its substantial steric hindrance and weaker coordination ability, effec­
tively suppressing the solvation of LiPSs. The addition of Li2S6 to DME

to the coordination of Li2S6 with DME through Li-O bonds. This coor­

Conversely, the addition of Li2S6 to PTTS resulted in a shift in the

PTTS and the significant steric hindrance from its long-chain terminal

2.4. Characterization of the electrodes and interspatial chemistry
Since the chemical composition of the SEI is central to understanding 
its protective effect on lithium metal anodes, we first employed depth- 
resolved X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to characterize the 
SEI’s gradient chemical structure.The tailoring of Li⁺ solvation archi­
tecture exerts profound control over the morphological evolution and 
compositional heterogeneity of the SEI, dictating lithium deposition 
modalities through interface-driven electrochemical kinetics. To estab­
lish structure-property correlations, depth-resolved XPS analysis with 
Ar+ sputtering is conducted to probe the gradient chemical landscape of 
cycled lithium metal anodes. In the C 1 s profile of 4 M LiFSI-PT73 
systems (Fig. 5a), the organic content in the SEI is relatively low, 
while the F 1 s profile of 4 M LiFSI-PT73 systems (Fig. 5b) shows pro­
nounced Li-F bonding signatures (684.3 eV), confirming LiF-dominated 
interfacial chemistry with gradient inorganic-rich architecture. Notably, 
while LiF exhibits intrinsically low Li⁺ conductivity (activation energy 
~0.9–1.2 eV), its nanocomposite integration with organic moieties en­
ables defect-assisted ion transport through localized barrier reduction 
(<0.4 eV). This synergistic configuration effectively prevents electrolyte 
decomposition by electrochemically isolating metallic lithium from the 
bulk electrolyte. Depth-dependent quantification reveals a significant 
increase in Li-F intensity (>300 % at 50 nm depth), contrasting sharply 
with the LiF-deficient SEI in 1 M LiFSI-DD11 systems. Coupled with O 1 s 
and Si 2p spectral analysis (Figure 5c, S16b, S17c, d), the lithium metal 
anodes treated with 4 M LiFSI PT73 shows an increase in the intensity of 
the peak at 528.2 eV, attributed to Li2O, as the etching depth increases. 
Another peak located around 531.9 eV corresponds to -O-Si-O- in the Si 
2p spectrum, positioned at 101.4 eV, indicating that trace amounts of 
PTTS molecules participate in the formation of the interfacial layer. The 
presence of Li2O and -O-Si-O- within the EEI significantly enhances the 
resilience and flexibility of the SEI. The C 1 s analysis further reveals that 
the organic content in the SEI formed on the lithium metal anodes sur­
face is minimal. With increasing etching depth, the content of LiCOx in 
the 4 M LiFSI PT73 electrolyte substantially surpasses that in the ether- 
based electrolyte. Based on the XPS results, the proportions of lithium- 
containing species at different etching times are evaluated (Fig. 5d). 
By analyzing the different atomic ratios within the SEI layers of the 4 M 
LiFSI PT73 and 1 M LiFSI DD11 electrolytes, it is evident that the 
siloxane electrolyte’s high F atomic ratio is fully corroborated in the F 1 s 
spectrum (Figure S18). In contrast, the DD11 electrolyte exhibits a 
notably high C atomic ratio, implying that the Li+ solvation structure, 
predominantly composed of DME/DOL, decomposes at the lithium 
metal interface, resulting in an SEI enriched in organic matter[60]. 
Complementary XPS characterization of the SEI formed after the first 
cycle further verifies the intrinsic difference in interfacial chemistry 
between the 4 M LiFSI PT73 and 1 M LiFSI DD11 systems, which is 
prominent right from the initial stage of SEI formation. Specifically, the 
4 M LiFSI-PT73 system enables the formation of an inorganic-rich initial 
SEI with LiF and Li2O as the dominant components during the first cycle. 
In sharp contrast, the 1 M LiFSI-DD11 system undergoes rapid decom­
position of its solvation structure at the lithium metal interface, leading 
to an initial SEI with a distinctly organic-dominated composition. This 
compositional divergence at the early stage of SEI formation fully 
demonstrates the critical regulatory effect of electrolyte solvation ar­
chitecture on the initial SEI formation behavior and directly establishes 
an intrinsic correlation between the initial interfacial chemical charac­
teristics and the interfacial stability of batteries during long-term cycling 
(FigureS19, S20).
These compositional differences of the SEI identified by XPS are well 
corroborated by subsequent scanning electron microscopy (SEM) ob­
servations of lithium deposition morphology in Li||Cu cells, where gal­
vanostatic lithium plating was performed at 0.5 mA cm-2 with a fixed 
areal capacity of 2.5 mAh cm-2, and these observations revealed sig­
nificant disparities in deposition behavior between the two electrolyte 
systems. The 4 M LiFSI-PT73 electrolyte demonstrates exceptional 
performance, resulting in densely packed lithium deposits with a uni­
form surface morphology (Fig. 5e). This optimized deposition behavior 
originates from three synergistic mechanisms: (1) The preferential 
decomposition of LiFSI salt generates a mechanically resilient SEI 
dominated by inorganic LiF components, exhibiting high fracture 
toughness and low electronic conductivity; (2) The LiF-rich SEI effec­
tively mitigates volume fluctuations during lithium plating while pro­
moting a homogeneous distribution of Li⁺ flux; (3) Weakly solvated 
siloxane molecules reconfigure the Li⁺ solvation sheath, significantly 
limiting direct contact between reactive lithium metal and electrolyte 
components. This multifunctional synergy suppresses localized current 
density gradients, inhibits dendritic nucleation and propagation, and 
curtails electrolyte decomposition pathways, thereby establishing the 
critical role of electrolyte design in stabilizing lithium metal interfaces. 
Conversely, the DD11 electrolyte system exhibits severely compromised 
deposition characteristics, producing irregular lithium morphologies 
with dendritic protrusions and mossy structures (Figure S21 a). These 
factors collectively account for the observed low CE (<92 %) in DD11- 
based cells. Quantitative cross-sectional analysis highlights the 
morphological contrast, showing compact lithium layers (12.6 μm) in 
PT73 electrolytes versus porous deposits (20.5 μm) in DD11 electrolytes 
(Figure. 5f, S21b). The 62 % increase in deposit thickness directly re­
flects the reduced packing density in DD11 electrolytes. Complementary 
in situ optical microscopy observations under accelerated plating con­
ditions (5 mA cm-2, 20 min) provide dynamic confirmation, revealing 
significantly fewer needle-like protrusions in PT73 electrolytes 
compared to the DD11 electrolytes (Figure. S22). This temporal reso­
lution of deposition dynamics underscores the kinetic advantages of 
siloxane electrolytes in maintaining interfacial stability during high-rate 
operation. The convergence of evidence from multiscale characteriza­
tion establishes a mechanistic framework for electrolyte-mediated 
lithium deposition and delivers critical design criteria for advanced 
lithium metal battery systems.
Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) was 
employed to assess the chemical composition and spatial distribution of 
the as-formed solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) films in different elec­
trolytes (Fig. 5g). Inorganic species (F-, Li3N-) are uniformly distributed 
on the surface of the lithium metal anodes, and their signal peaks 
maintain a relatively high intensity even after sputtering treatment. This 
observation indicates that an SEI film enriched in LiF and Li3N is formed 
in the PT73 electrolyte system.
As observed from the 3D distribution maps, a relatively high- 
intensity signal of CHO⁻ (aldehyde/hydroxyl anions), which is associ­
ated with solvent decomposition was still detected on the Li metal 
following sputtering treatment (Figure S23). This result demonstrates 
that DD11 undergoes decomposition, and the abundant organic prod­
ucts generated from its decomposition take part in the formation of the 
SEI film.
The cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) plays a critical role in 
governing the electrochemical performance of Li-SPAN batteries by 
regulating interfacial stability and ion transport kinetics. Prior to eval­
uating electrochemical behavior, fundamental investigations into the 
microstructure and chemical composition of the SPAN-electrolyte 
interface are essential, as these parameters dictate the dynamics of CEI 
formation and its evolutionary pathways. Notably, SPAN particles 
experience substantial volumetric fluctuations and mechanical stress 
during electrochemical cycling, attributed to the density disparities 
between reactants and products (Li2S: 1.66 g cm-3 vs S: 2.07 g cm-3). 
These cyclic dimensional changes induce pressure accumulation at the 
interface, potentially compromising CEI integrity through fracture or 
delamination. This mechanical degradation mechanism is particularly 
pronounced in low-elasticity CEI films, underscoring the critical 
importance of elastic modulus optimization for interfacial stability 
maintenance. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) coupled with force curve 
analysis reveals significant disparities in the mechanical properties of 
cycled SPAN cathodes. The CEI formed in PT73 electrolyte

severe active material loss and LiPSs dissolution, aligning with the 
design concept that PTTS-based electrolytes can suppress LiPSs 
dissolution.

2.5. Electrochemical performance of cells
The stability of lithium metal is crucial for achieving long-term 
cycling in Li-SPAN batteries. To evaluate the compatibility of various 
electrolytes with lithium metal anodes, multiple tests are conducted to 
elucidate the reversibility of lithium metal deposition and stripping. Li|| 
Cu cells are assembled and tested using an improved Aurbach method to 
determine the CE of lithium deposition and stripping at a current density 
of 0.5 mA cm-2. As depicted in Fig. 6a, batteries utilizing the PT73 
electrolyte (CE~98.7 %) and PTTS electrolyte (CE~98.3 %) demon­
strate superior cycling performance compared to the baseline ether- 
based electrolyte (CE~94.3 %), achieving remarkable cycling stability 
with a CE of 99.46 %. The long-term cycling stability of electrolytes in 
Li||Li symmetric cells is investigated at current densities of 0.5 mA cm-2 
and 0.5 mAh cm-2 to explore their stabilizing effect on lithium metal 
(Fig. 6b). After only 440 h, cells employing DD11 electrolyte fail. In 
contrast, PTTS-based electrolytes exhibit better compatibility with 
lithium metal, with overpotentials of approximately 44 mV and 45 mV 
for 4 M LiFSI PT73 and 4 M LiFSI PTTS electrolytes, respectively, and 
more effectively stabilizing lithium metal to enable stable cycling for 
over 800 h. Moreover, the Li||Cu asymmetric cell with PT73 electrolyte 
can stably run over 720 cycles without obvious fluctuations and the 
average CE is 98.7 % under the conditions of 1 mA cm-2 and 1 mAh cm- 
2(Fig. 6c).
Through the analysis of Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Tech­
nique (GITT) and Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS), the 
polarization ratio in the full cell can be evaluated, with cathode polar­
ization serving as a key indicator to assess the electrolyte’s solvation 
ability towards LiPSs and its impact on battery performance [61]. By 
combining EIS analysis with GITT, cathode polarization can be divided 
into Ohmic polarization (ηohm), concentration polarization (ηcon), and 
activation polarization (ηact), allowing for the precise quantification of 
each type of polarization (Figure 6d, e, S27). It is evident that in Li-SPAN 
full cells assembled with PTTS-based and DD11 electrolytes, the differ­
ence in ηcon is significant, with 4 M LiFSI PT73 and 4 M LiFSI PTTS 
exhibit ηcon around 80 mV, while 1 M LiFSI DD11 reaches a much higher 
ηcon of 180 mV. In Li-S battery systems, high cathode concentration 
polarization leads to a decrease in LiPSs concentration near the cathode, 
which promotes LiPSs shuttling between the anode and cathode 
(Fig. 6f). This results in the loss of active material and limits the 
reduction reaction of LiPSs, ultimately reducing both the discharge ca­
pacity and cycling life of the battery. Based on the performance of the 
Li-SPAN full cells, it can be deduced that PTTS-based electrolytes not 
only enhance the cycling life of the lithium metal anode but also effec­
tively suppress the dissolution of LiPSs, thereby mitigating the shuttle 
effect in the Li-SPAN system. In this study, the electrochemical perfor­
mance of Li-SPAN full cells utilizing different electrolytes under prac­
tical conditions is investigated. Specifically, SPAN with a normal loading 
(S loading= 2.26 mAh cm-2) is matched with a 50 µm ultrathin lithium 
foil and tested under charge-discharge conditions at 0.5C (1 C = 1675 
mAh g-1) (Fig. 6g). In the full cell employing 1 M LiFSI DD11 electrolyte, 
the initial discharge capacity (second cycle) is 1300 mAh g-1, but it 
rapidly declined to 1000 mAh g-1 after only 200 cycles. This poor sta­
bility can be attributed to the dissolution of polysulfides and poor 
lithium cycling reversibility. To verify the intrinsic solvent effect under 
the same high-concentration condition, the 4 M LiFSI DD11 electrolyte 
was tested as well, showing improved performance over the 
low-concentration counterpart yet still suffering significant capacity 
fading due to persistent polysulfide dissolution and still inferior inter­
facial performance to PT73 (Figure S28, S29, S30). In contrast, the 4 M 
LiFSI PTTS electrolyte exhibits superior cycling stability, maintaining 98 
% of its capacity after 320 cycles. The 4 M LiFSI PT73 electrolyte 
simultaneously stabilizes both the SPAN cathode and lithium anode, 
thereby improving cycling performance. After 870 cycles, an initial 
discharge capacity retention of 87.3 % and an average CE of 98.3 % were 
achieved. Notably, the battery assembled with the 4 M LiFSI PT73 
electrolyte in a full cell with a loading of 4.6 mAh cm-2 (approximately 
three times the area capacity of commercial electrodes) maintains 91.1 
% of its capacity after 120 cycles, with an average CE of 98.89 % 
(Fig. 6h). Compared to recently published Li-SPAN electrolyte systems, 
especially under high loading conditions of SPAN cathode, batteries 
assembled with PTTS-based electrolytes exhibit superior capacity 
retention and cycling stability (Fig. 6i). The double-layer pouch cell 
assembled by a 50 μm lithium metal anodes and double thick SPAN 
cathode with 8.12 mAh cm-2 can still show remarkable cyclability, as 
shown in Fig. 6j. A high-capacity retention of 80 % is attained after 70 
cycles under demanding conditions, and the calculated EV is up to 560 
Wh L-1, this confirms the feasibility of the practical application of this 
electrolyte. Compared with many current experiments, the PT73 elec­
trolyte system also maintains a good competitiveness (Fig. 6k).
3. Conclusion
In summary, we have demonstrated a versatile design strategy for Li- 
SPAN silicone electrolytes. Building upon the screening of solvent 
molecules using conventional molecular descriptors, we have employed 
the “aggregation degree of LiPSs in the electrolyte” as an indicator for 
suppressing the dissolution of LiPSs. This approach ensures that the 
electrolyte exhibits lithiophilicity while effectively mitigating the 
dissolution of LiPSs. As a conceptual validation, a localized high- 
concentration silicone electrolyte, 4 M LiFSI PTTS: TTE (7:3), has been 
rationally designed. The unique D-p conjugate interaction within PTTS 
leads to a weak electrostatic interaction between the coordination ox­
ygen and Li+, resulting from the transfer of lone pair electrons from the 
oxygen. Moreover, the distinctive propyl segments in PTTS contribute to 
excellent cycling performance while inhibiting the dissolution of LiPSs. 
Consequently, the designed silicone electrolyte guarantees excellent 
performance in Li-SPAN batteries. Specifically, it enables the Li-SPAN 
full cell (2.26 mAh cm-2) to achieve an ultra-long cycle life of over 
870 cycles. Even under more practical conditions, our electrolyte dem­
onstrates stable cycling performance, maintaining 91.1 % capacity 
retention after 120 cycles when paired with a Li-SPAN full cell featuring 
a high-loading cathode of 4.6 mAh cm-2 This design philosophy provides 
valuable insights for subsequent applications of siloxanes in Li-S elec­
trolytes, thereby accelerating the development of next-generation high- 
energy-density battery electrolyte designs.
