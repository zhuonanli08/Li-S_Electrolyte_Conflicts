Construction of Organic-Rich Solid Electrolyte Interphase
for Long-Cycling Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Zheng Li, Yuan Li, Chen-Xi Bi, Qian-Kui Zhang, Li-Peng Hou, Xi-Yao Li, Jin Ma,
Xue-Qiang Zhang, Bo-Quan Li,* Rui Wen,* and Qiang Zhang*
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries promise great potential as high-energy-density
energy storage devices. However, the parasitic reactions between lithium
polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) and Li metal anodes render limited cycling lifespan of
Li–S batteries. Herein, an organic-rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) is
constructed to inhibit the LiPS parasitic reactions and achieve long-cycling
Li–S batteries. Concretely, 1,3,5-trioxane is introduced as a reactive co-solvent
that decomposes on Li anode surfaces and contributes organic components
to the SEI. The as-constructed organic-rich SEI eﬀectively inhibits the LiPS
parasitic reactions and protects working Li metal anodes. Consequently, the
cycling lifespan of Li–S coin cells with 50 μm Li anodes and 4.0 mg cm−2
sulfur cathodes is prolonged from 130 to 300 cycles by the organic-rich SEI.
Furthermore, the organic-rich SEI enables a 3.0 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell to
achieve a high energy density of 400 Wh kg−1 and stable 26 cycles. This study
aﬀords an eﬀective organic-rich SEI to inhibit the LiPS parasitic reactions and
inspires rational SEI design to achieve long-cycling Li–S batteries.

high-active LiPSs dissolved in electrolyte chemically react with
Li metal anodes.[9] The parasitic reactions between LiPSs and
Li metal anodes generate disadvantageous components in solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) and simultaneously destroy the
SEI through continuous corrosion.[10] Consequently, inhomoge-
neous Li deposition is aggravated and limited Li reservoirs are
depleted, which induces unstable Li metal anodes during cycling
and rapid failure of Li–S batteries.[11] Moreover, the parasitic re-
actions and anode instability are severely aggravated under de-
manding conditions, such as using ultrathin Li anodes and high-
areal-loading sulfur cathodes that are necessary to construct high-
energy-density Li–S batteries.[12] Therefore, suppressing the par-
asitic reactions between LiPSs and Li metal anodes is of prior ne-
cessity to stabilize Li metal anodes and prolong the cycling lifes-
pan of Li–S batteries.
Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate the parasitic
reactions between LiPSs and Li metal anodes.[13] Sparing solvat-
ing electrolytes are particularly eﬀective in inhibiting the disso-
lution of LiPSs and thus relieving Li metal anode corrosion.[14]
Typical sparing solvating electrolyte systems include ionic liq-
uid electrolytes,[15] solvated ionic liquid electrolytes,[16] high-
concentration electrolytes,[17] and localized high-concentration
electrolytes.[18] Very recently, Zhang and co-workers proposed
an encapsulating LiPS electrolyte (EPSE) to simultaneously sup-
press the parasitic reactions while maintain satisfactory cathode
conversion kinetics by LiPS solvation structure design.[19] The
Li anode stability and battery cycling lifespan can be eﬀectively
improved in 300 Wh kg−1-level Li–S pouch cells with various
EPSEs.[20] In addition to electrolyte design, the regulation of SEI
plays a critical role in mitigating the parasitic reactions between
LiPSs and Li metal anodes as well.[21] Numerous eﬀorts have
been made to construct advantageous SEI for suppressing the
parasitic reactions and stabilizing Li metal anodes.[22] LiNO3 ad-
ditive and LiNxOy-containing SEI constitute a milestone that has
signiﬁcantly promoted the Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) by LiPS
shuttle inhibition.[23] Similarly, oxysulﬁde-rich SEI was recently
proposed to improve the uniformity of SEI and realize homoge-
neous Li deposition, thus suppressing LiPS corrosion on Li metal
anodes and promoting the lifespan of Li–S batteries.[24] Many
other inorganic-rich SEIs have also been veriﬁed to inhibit the
LiPS parasitic reactions, such as LiF,[25] Li2SnS3,[26] and Li 1,4-
benzenedithiolate.[27] In addition to inorganic-rich SEI, organic
SEI components are also essential for Li anode protection.[28]
Organic SEI components provided by 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) are
generally considered necessary to protect Li metal anodes.[29]
However, it remains challenging to eﬀectively construct favorable
organic-rich SEI for Li metal anode protection in Li–S batteries.
Novel methodology on organic-rich SEI construction is expected
to innovate protection strategies on Li metal anodes and prolong
the cycling lifespan of Li–S batteries under practical working con-
ditions.
In this contribution, an organic-rich SEI is constructed to sup-
press the parasitic reactions of LiPSs for constructing stable Li
metal anodes and long-cycling Li–S batteries. Concretely, 1,3,5-
trioxane (TXA) is introduced as an active co-solvent that decom-
poses on Li anode surface and contributes organic components to
the SEI. The as-constructed organic-rich SEI eﬀectively mitigates
the LiPS parasitic reactions and stabilizes Li metal anodes. Con-
sequently, the cycling lifespan of Li–S coin cells is prolonged from
130 to 300 cycles with 50 μm Li metal anodes and 4.0 mg cm−2
sulfur cathodes by the organic-rich SEI. Moreover, a 3.0 Ah-level
Li–S pouch cell with the organic-rich SEI achieves a high initial
energy density of 400 Wh kg−1 and stable 26 cycles.
2. Results and Discussion
As a routine co-solvent in Li–S batteries, DOL can decompose
on the Li metal anode surface and contribute organic compo-
nents to the SEI.[29a] The reactivity of DOL originates from the
degradation of the acetal structure.[30] The C atom bonding with
two O atoms in the acetal structure is highly positively charged,
which enhances the instability of the C–O bonds and the reac-
tivity of DOL to aﬀord organic SEI components. However, DOL
has only one acetal structure per molecule, which is insuﬃcient
to construct organic-rich SEI (Figure S1a, Supporting Informa-
tion). To enhance the capability to construct organic-rich SEI,
TXA is herein proposed as a substitute co-solvent. With three ac-
etal structures per molecule, TXA is expected to be more reactive
than DOL to contribute more advantageous organic SEI compo-
nents for Li metal anode protection (Figure S1b, Supporting In-
formation).
The preferential reductive decomposition of TXA was ﬁrst in-
vestigated through electrochemical tests. Linear sweep voltam-
metry (LSV) was conducted, where DOL and TXA were dis-
solved in hexyl methyl ether, a reductively stable blank sol-
vent (Figure 1a). TXA exhibits an obvious reduction peak at
≈1.5 V versus Li+/Li and a maximum reduction current den-
sity of 11.4 μA cm−2. In contrast, DOL exhibits no obvious re-
duction peak and a maximum reduction current density of only
3.8 μA cm−2. The LSV results indicate inherent preferential re-
ductive decomposition of TXA.
To further explore the capability of TXA in forming SEI, real-
time monitoring of the electrode process during discharge was
performed by in situ electrochemical atomic force microscopy
(AFM). Figure 1b exhibits the schematic diagram of the in situ
electrochemical cell, where highly oriented pyrolytic graphite
(HOPG) was selected as the working electrode (WE). Both the
counter electrode (CE) and the reference electrode (RE) were Li
wires. Lithium iodide (LiI) was employed as the Li salt in elec-
trolyte to eliminate the eﬀects of Li salt reduction. As displayed
in Figure 1c,g, atomically ﬂat terraces of HOPG are observed at
the open circuit potential (OCP) in DOL and TXA based elec-
trolytes, respectively. In the DOL based electrolyte, there is no
obvious topography change of the HOPG electrode during the
cathodic sweep from OCP to 1.19 V (AFM image from cathodic
1.49 to 1.19 V is presented in Figure S2a, Supporting Informa-
tion). During the cathodic scan from 1.19 to 0.99 V, as indicated
by the yellow arrows in Figure 1d, random nanoparticles with
the height of 3.0 ± 0.2 nm start to deposit on the HOPG elec-
trode when discharging at 0.99 V. Holding the potential of ca-
thodic 0.99 V, nanoparticles slowly expand (Figure 1e) and even-
tually disperse sporadically on the electrode surface after 1 h
(Figure 1f). According to the LSV results, the random nanopar-
ticles are speculated to be the slight decomposition products of
the solvents, thus hardly contributing to the formation of SEI.[31]
Comparatively, in the TXA based electrolyte, no visible change
of the HOPG electrode is observed during the cathodic sweep
from OCP to 1.14 V (AFM image from cathodic 1.43 to 1.14 V is

In order to investigate the chemical components of the SEI
formed by TXA, post analyses were conducted on the cycled Li
metal anodes of Li–S cells. EPSE was selected as the basic elec-
trolyte system to help enhance the cycling performance of Li–S
batteries. DOL and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) are mixed with
hydroﬂuoroether (HFE) (2:2:1, by vol.) as the control electrolyte,
in which the SEI formed is named as R-SEI as the control sam-
ple. The mixture of TXA/DME (1:2, by mol.) is further mixed
with HFE (4:1, by vol.) as the experimental electrolyte, in which
the SEI formed is named as O-SEI. X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the SEI components of
the cycled Li metal anodes in Li–S cells without LiNO3. The wide-
scan XPS spectra of R-SEI and O-SEI on the Li metal anode sur-
face are shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information) and the
corresponding atomic content of diﬀerent elements is shown in
Table S1 (Supporting Information). The C atomic content of R-
SEI and O-SEI is 24.7% versus 61.7% on the surface and 2.5% ver-
sus 34.0% after 60 s sputtering, indicating much higher C con-
tent of O-SEI than R-SEI (Figure 2a). The higher C content of
O-SEI indicates more organic components contributed by TXA.
The C-containing species, including C–C, C–O, C–SOx, and C–F
are observed on the surface of O-SEI and an extra species, C–Li,
emerges after 60 s sputtering (Figure 2b; Figure S7, Supporting
Information). The R-SEI exhibits similar C-containing species
with O-SEI on the surface and after 60 s sputtering (Figure S8,
Supporting Information). Both C–C and C–O are generally con-
sidered as organic SEI components while the other species are
considered to mainly exist in the form of inorganic components.
The ratios of each species were obtained by integration (Figure 2c
and Table S2, Supporting Information). The organic species ra-
tio, including C–C and C–O in O-SEI is obviously higher than
that in R-SEI both on the SEI surface (89.6% vs 82.1%) and after
60 s sputtering (77.3% vs 67.7%). In addition, similar F contents
of ≈3.3% are observed in both R-SEI and O-SEI (Figure S9, Sup-
porting Information). The F-containing components in SEI, in-
cluding C–F and LiF and the corresponding species ratios are also
similar in R-SEI and O-SEI, indicating the unchanged inorganic
components in SEI (Figure S10, Supporting Information). The
above results suggest that more organic components are gener-
ated in O-SEI due to the preferential reductive decomposition of
TXA.
The organic-rich SEI formed by TXA was further investigated
by time-of-ﬂight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS).
C2H2O−fragments were detected to analyze the organic com-
ponents in SEI (Figure S11, Supporting Information). The O-
SEI exhibits higher intensity of C2H2O−compared with R-SEI.

The eﬀect of organic-rich SEI on mitigating the parasitic re-
actions between LiPSs and Li metal anodes was further investi-
gated. Shuttle current was measured at diﬀerent voltages to quan-
titatively characterize the parasitic reactions. Negligible shuttle
current (< 0.02 mA cm−2) is observed in the cell with O-SEI in
the range from 2.34 to 2.60 V. In comparison, the shuttle cur-
rent is ≈0.14 mA cm−2 in the cell with R-SEI, indicating the par-
asitic reactions between LiPSs and Li metal anodes are mitigated
by the organic-rich SEI (Figure 3a). Li | Li symmetric cells con-
taining 1.0 m[S] Li2S8 were further tested at a current density of
1.0 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 4.0 mAh cm−2 (Figure 3b).
Despite similar initial voltage polarization, the symmetric cell
with O-SEI exhibits a longer cycling lifespan of 300 h compared
with 180 h of the cell with R-SEI. The prolonged cycling lifespan
with O-SEI indicates stabilized Li metal anodes, which originates
from mitigated parasitic reactions by the organic-rich SEI.
The suppression of the LiPS parasitic reactions was further
veriﬁed in Li–S coin cells without LiNO3. The initial speciﬁc dis-
charge capacity of the cell with O-SEI is similar to that with R-SEI
(Figure S14, Supporting Information). However, a higher capac-
ity retention of 81% after 10 cycles is exhibited in the Li–S cell
with O-SEI, while the capacity retention is only 68% in the Li–
S cell with R-SEI. The CE of the cell with O-SEI is also higher
than that with R-SEI during cycling. The cell with O-SEI exhibits
a higher CE of 79.9% than 65.8% of the cell with R-SEI at the
third cycle (Figure 3c). Furthermore, the cycled Li–S cells were
disassembled to analyze the cycled Li metal anodes after ﬁve cy-
cles. According to the scanning electron microscope (SEM) im-
ages, the deposited Li exhibits a loose and porous morphology
with massive Li dendrites in R-SEI (Figure 3d). In contrast, O-
SEI enables dense and smooth Li deposition (Figure 3e), indicat-
ing a stabilized Li metal anode. Moreover, the S atomic content of
O-SEI is lower than that of R-SEI (1.45% vs 1.73%) by XPS analy-
sis (Figure S15, Supporting Information). Therefore, the parasitic
reactions between LiPSs and Li metal anodes are signiﬁcantly in-
hibited by the organic-rich SEI formed by TXA.

Figure S21, Supporting Information). The pristine Li metal an-
ode before cycling is 50 μm in thickness. The Li metal anode with
O-SEI is composed of bulk Li of 40 μm and deposited Li of 23 μm
with a low volume expansion ratio of 26%, where abundant bulk
Li remains due to the inhibition of the parasitic reactions between
LiPSs and Li metal anodes. In contrast, the Li metal anode with
R-SEI is composed of bulk Li of 32 μm and deposited Li of 79 μm
with a higher volume expansion ratio of 122%, where massive
bulk Li is utilized to form loose and porous deposited Li with mas-
sive accumulation of inactive Li. Therefore, the organic-rich SEI
eﬀectively mitigates the parasitic reactions between LiPSs and Li
metal anodes to form homogeneous and compact Li deposition.

eﬀect of organic-rich SEI. The Li–S cell with O-SEI delivers an
initial areal capacity of 5.0 mAh cm−2 and a prolonged cycling
lifespan of 191 cycles with a capacity retention of 64% at 0.1 C
while the cell with R-SEI only undergoes 48 cycles followed
by a sharp capacity drop (Figure 4g). The characteristics of
the voltage–speciﬁc capacity proﬁles are highly consistent with
the Li–S cells using 4.0 mg cm−2 sulfur cathodes (Figure S22,
Supporting Information). The voltage polarization with R-SEI
and O-SEI is similar at the 10th cycle while greatly diﬀerent at
the 50th cycle. The stable voltage polarization with O-SEI after
50 cycles originates from the suppressed parasitic reactions of
LiPSs and stabilized Li metal anodes aﬀorded by the organic-rich
SEI.
To fully validate the feasibility of the organic-rich SEI in prac-
tical high-energy-density devices, a 3.0 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell
was assembled and evaluated. The Li–S pouch cell was assem-
bled with a size of 4 × 7 cm2 and stacks of double-sided Li metal
anodes and sulfur cathodes (Figure 5a). Ultrathin Li metal an-
odes of 75 μm in thickness and high-areal-loading sulfur cath-
odes of 7.8 mg cm−2 were used in the pouch cell with a low
E/S ratio of 3.0 g gS
−1. The parameters, including the mass of
each component and electrochemical performances of the Li–S
pouch cell are listed in Figure 5b. The initial energy density is
400 Wh kg−1 based on the total mass of the pouch cell. The Li–S
pouch cell delivers an initial speciﬁc capacity of 1280 mAh g−1
and undergoes stable 26 cycles with a high capacity retention
of 73% (Figure 5c). The voltage–speciﬁc capacity proﬁle displays
typical two discharge plateaus at the ﬁrst cycle (Figure S23, Sup-
porting Information). A dip at the second discharge plateau is ob-
served and gradually scales up during cycling due to electrolyte
exhaustion.[32] Furthermore, the organic-rich SEI enables supe-
rior performance of the Li–S pouch cell in terms of energy density
and cycling lifespan compared with other reported Li–S pouch
cells (Figure 5d).[20b,33] Therefore, the organic-rich SEI formed
by TXA is veriﬁed to be feasible in practical high-energy-density
devices to mitigate the parasitic reactions between LiPSs and Li
metal anodes and achieve long-cycling Li–S batteries.

actions of LiPSs for stabilizing Li metal anodes and achieving
long-cycling Li–S batteries. Concretely, TXA is employed as an
