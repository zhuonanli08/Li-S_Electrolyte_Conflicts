A B S T R A C T
The commercialization of Li-S batteries is constrained by the unstable interface, severe shuttle of polysulfides, 
and sluggish kinetic conversion reactions. Electrolyte regulation is a straightforward and efficient way to tackle 
those mentioned issues. Herein, a multifunctional electrolyte additive of 2,5-Dimercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole 
(DMTD) is introduced in the ether-based electrolyte, which can be lithiated into Li2-DMTD when contacted 
with the Li or Li2S during the discharge process. The generated Li2-DMTD can operate as a highly ionic- 
conducting protective coating on the Li surface, inhibiting the formation of Li dendrites, and increasing inter­
face stability. Then, it is reversibly oligomerized with Li2S, forming two organosulfur oligomers during the

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered an ideal candidate for 
the next generation storage devices because of their low cost and 
extremely high theoretical specific energy density (2510 Wh kg−1) 
[1,2]. However, there are still several critical issues that need to be 
addressed, including rapid lithium dendrites growth, severe shuttle ef­
fects, and sluggish reaction kinetics of sulfur (S) conversion towards the 
LSBs commercialization [3–5]. Furthermore, uneven Li+ deposition/ 
stripping is prone to causing the formation of lithium dendrites, 
potentially piercing the separator, and generating thermal runaway in 
the batteries. The Li-S chemistry involves a discharged-liquid-solid 
conversion process, resulting in intermediate lithium polysulfides 
(LiPSs, Li2Sx, x = 4 ~ 8) that are very soluble in ether-based electrolytes 
[6–8]. They detach from the cathode and diffuse to the Li anode along 
with the electrolyte, resulting in Li corrosion and irreversible capacity 
loss, which is known as the notorious shuttle effect [9]. Finally, and most 
importantly, due to the insulating property and low solubility of the 
discharge product of lithium sulfide (Li2S) in the electrolyte, the 
oxidation of the Li2S is incomplete under the normal charge voltage 
range or at a high rate [10,11]. The electrode can be easily passivated by 
the residual Li2S during the repeated discharge–charge processes, 
enhancing the polarization voltage and increasing the irreversible ca­
pacity loss of the cell.
Many efforts have been made to tackle these critical problems. Some 
strategies mainly include interface modifications [12,13], electrode 
designs [14,15], and electrolyte regulations [16–18]. Interface modifi­
cations, such as designing 3D conductive current collectors or covering 
the protective film on the Li surface, could alleviate the Li dendrites 
growth by changing the Li deposition behaviors [12]. However, it could 
not prevent the dissolution of polysulfides or enhance the reaction ki­
netics at the cathode. Introducing effective adsorbents on S cathode to 
restrict the dissolution of polysulfides has been widely studied [19,20]. 
Although the polysulfide dissolution is alleviated at the initial cycling 
period by applying this strategy, the shuttle effect is still obvious as the 
charge–discharge proceeds because of the gradual passivation of the S 
cathode. Alternatively, organosulfurs with high electrochemical activity 
have recently been widely employed as the cathode material in LSBs 
[21]. It can firmly anchor S atoms through covalent bonds with S or C- 
contained substituents to form copolymers. Therefore, the char­
ge–discharge process does not involve the formation of polysulfide in­
termediates, thus significantly suppressing the shuttle effect of 
polysulfides [22].
These methods typically focus on individual electrodes and may not 
simultaneously address issues occurring within the cells. Among these, 
electrolyte regulation is regarded as the most direct and effective 
approach to enhance the interface stability of LSBs [23,24]. Except for 
the commonly used forming additives aimed to improve the interface 
stability of Li metal surface, many benzenedithiol derivatives have been 
recently employed as electrolyte additives in LSBs to modify the SEI 
composition and alleviate the shuttle effect of polysulfides by changing 
the conversion routes of the S cathode [25]. Xie et al. reported an 
organosulfur additive of di(tri)sulfide polyethylene glycol (PESn) to 
reversibly realize the redox process of S species by converting small PSs 
into large organosulfur species. The interface stability and capacity were 
obviously improved in LSBs [26]. Then, 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl) 
thiophenol (BTB) was employed as a SEI-forming additive to protect 
the Li anode from the soluble LiPS by forming Ph-S−component in the 
SEI film [25]. The polymerization of 2,5-dimercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole 
(DMTD) as a cathode material for lithium metal batteries has been 
extensively studied [27–29]. Early research suggested poor recharge­
ability of DMTD-based polymer electrodes, attributing the inferior per­
formance to their sluggish kinetic behavior. However, the real culprit 
was improper electrolyte system selection [30]. Most of these organic 
sulfur cathodes employ carbonate-based electrolytes, which are sus­
ceptible to nucleophilic attacks by radicals formed during the operation 
of sulfur and organic sulfur cathodes [31]. Furthermore, carbonate- 
based electrolytes are also considered incompatible with alkali metal 
anodes [32]. In 2022, Manthiram’s team demonstrated excellent 
reversible capacity (611 mAh g−1) and cycling stability by pairing 
synthesized DMTD-based polymer cathode materials with ether-based 
electrolyte systems [33]. Although the above-mentioned efforts effec­
tively suppress polysulfide shuttle, significant battery polarization and 
incomplete formation of the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) on the 
anode indicate that further optimization of additives is required to 
enhance the stability of both cathode and anode interfaces 
simultaneously.
In this work, we propose a promising multifunctional electrolyte 
additive of 2,5-Dimercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole (DMTD), which contains 
two thiol groups (−SH) grafted on the thiadiazole ring. DMTD acts as a 
strong hydrogen donor that can easily react with the Li to produce a 
high-ionic conducting Li2-DMTD component on the SEI film. Mean­
while, the preformed Li2-DMTD can react reversibly with Li2S, gener­
ating two ring-like organosulfur oligomers during the subsequent 
electrochemical process. Such a work route not only immobilizes the S 
atoms, and suppresses the shuttle effect, but also activates the inert Li2S. 
The charge voltage of the Li2S cathode is greatly decreased from 3.6 V to 
2.8 V with the DMTD additive, enhancing the utilization of the active 
material and the Columbic efficiency of the LSBs. As a result, the cell 
with DMTD shows a capacity retention from 45.7 % to 81.2 % at 1C rate 
after 320 cycles compared to the control cell without DMTD. Therefore, 
such work demonstrates the multifunctional roles of the organosulfur 
additives with −SH groups, which provides new guidance in designing 
electrolyte additives to synchronously stabilize the interface of elec­
trodes and accelerate the reaction kinetics of the S cathode.
2. Methods and experimental methods
2.1. Materials
The typical Li-S electrolyte, composed of 1.0 M LiTFSI in DME/DOL 
(V:V = 1:1) with 2 % LiNO3 was purchased from DoDoChem and used as 
a standard electrolyte. The Graphene was purchased from The Six 
Element Inc. 2,5-Dimercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole (DMTD) was purchased 
from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. DMTD-based electrolyte 
refers to the reference electrolyte with 2 % DMTD by weight. In control 
experiments, 0.5 %, 2 %, and 4 % were prepared separately. As shown in 
Fig. S1, electrolytes with 2 % DMTD by weight show the best cycle 
performance. Sublimated S, Ethanol absolute,1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidinone 
(NMP) and Lithium sulfide (Li2S) were purchased from Sinopharm 
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‑d6) 
was purchased from Jinan Manalab Co., Sodium Ligninsulfonate,

2.2. Preparation of S@C composite
In a typical preparation, sodium lignosulfonate 2 g, melamine 1 g, 
and NaCl 8 g were consecutively added to 190 mL deionized water and 
stirred until completely dissolved to obtain a mixed solution. The above 
solution was then freeze-dried to remove water and obtain a dry powder 
precursor. After that, the obtained precursor was pressed into flakes 
under the pressure of 30 MPa, and then carbonized at 800 ◦C for 2 h 
under an argon atmosphere with a heating rate of 2 ◦C min−1 to obtain 
porous carbon as the host of S. Then, the S powder was mixed with the 
prepared porous carbon at a weight ratio of 4:1. The mixtures were 
transferred into a sealed stainless steel reaction vessel and heated at 
155 ◦C for 12 h to obtain the S@C composite.
2.3. Preparation of Li2S@G composite electrode
Graphene 0.1 g and Li2S 0.2 g were mixed and ball-milled for 10 h to 
obtain the Li2S@G composite electrode.
2.4. Material characterization
The morphologies of Li anodes were characterized by the field 
emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Gemini 500). The 
elemental composition and chemical state of the electrode surfaces were 
characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS, ESCA­
LAB250Xi). Raman results of the powdered S and DMTD, as well as the 
electrodes after cycling were measured by LabRAM HR Evolution from 
HORIBA JOBIN YVON within a range of 100–1500 cm−1 and a 532 nm 
laser at 5 % ND filter. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Agilent 
Technologies, VNMRS600) was used to detect the chemical shifts of the 
DMTD and its reaction products with Li2S, with Deuterated dimethyl 
sulfoxide (DMSO‑d6) as the deuterated reagent. The Ultraviolet–visible 
light absorption spectrum (UV–Vis, CARY 5000) was used to analyze the 
polysulfide species in electrolytes with or without DMTD. The mass/ 
charges of the charge and discharge products were analyzed by the Mass 
Spectrograph (MS) with a Liquid chromatography-quadrupole electro­
static field orbital well mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 
Vanquish Q Exactive Plus). The Young’s modulus of the surface film of 
lithium sheets was analyzed by the Atomic Force Microscope (Dimen­
sion Icon). The surface morphology of lithium sheets was measured by 
White Light Interferometry (Chotest Technology Inc Super View W1).
2.5. The cell assembling and electrochemical measurements
The S cathodes are prepared as follows: S@C composite, Super P 
(SP), and polyvinylidene fluoride (in NMP solvent mass ratio) were 
mixed at a weight ratio of 7:2:1 and ground with NMP diluent into a 
cathode slurry. Then the slurry was evenly coated on the aluminum foil 
and dried at 55 ◦C for 10 h. The dried cathode sheet was punched into 
disks with 14 mm in diameter. The sulfur loading is about 1.2 mg cm−2 
and 2.4 mg cm−2. Cells were assembled by using 40 μL standard elec­
trolyte or DMTD-based electrolyte in a glove box. The preparation of the 
Li2S cathode is similar to that of the S cathode, with attention to 
ensuring that the entire process is operated in a glove box. Li2S@G 
cathode preparation: Replace the above S@C composite material with 
the Li2S@G composite material while keeping all other conditions 
consistent. Note that Li2S reacts with moisture in the air to generate H2S 
gas, so it is crucial to handle and prepare the Li2S@G cathode material in 
a glove box.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV, 0.1 mV s−1) and electrochemical imped­
ance spectra (EIS, 100 kHz-0.1 Hz) measurements of the cells were 
carried out on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. The galvano­
static charge–discharge of the coin cells was measured on Neware bat­
tery testers (BTS4008).
3. Results and discussion
As shown in Fig. 1a, the chemical structure of DMTD reveals two 
−SH groups attached to the thiadiazol ring. Due to the hydrogen- 
bonding donor ability of its groups, DMTD exhibits a weak acidity, 
which allows it to react readily with lithium metal. To analyze the re­
action, two pellets were soaked in DME solutions with and without 
DMTD, respectively. As expected, the reaction proceeded slowly with 
some bubbles emerging on the Li surface, and the solution turned cloudy 
in the presence of DTMD. The lithiated DMTD may be formed after its 
mild reaction with Li. A dense and white protective film was observed on 
the Li surface. Then both of the Li pellets were taken out and exposed to 
the air. The Li foil without DMTD treatment immediately oxidized with a 
black color change on its surface. While there is almost no visible change 
for the DMTD-treated Li foil because of the protective layer on its sur­
face, it could prevent corrosion. Additionally, the mechanical properties 
of films formed on lithium sheets were investigated using atomic force 
microscopy (AFM), revealing a higher Young’s modulus of 663 MPa for 
lithium sheets treated with DMTD, as shown in Fig. S2. A scanning 
electron microscope (SEM) is used to observe the morphology of the Li 
foil after DMTD treatment. As shown in Fig. 1b, a dense and uniform 
surface was observed after treatment. Elements of C, S, and N are 
dispersed unevenly on the Li surface, indicating that the reaction 
product of DMTD is fully covered on the Li surface, which could protect 
from corrosion. X-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS) was used to 
analyze the composition of the Li pellet in Fig. 1c, The S2p3/2 peak at 
163.75 eV and 162.05 eV are assigned to the C–S–C and C–S–Li bond, 
respectively. The peak area ratio was close to 1:2, confirming the lith­
iation product of Li2DMTD was formed. The high-resolution S 2p XPS 
spectra of the Li foil from Li||Li symmetric cells further confirmed the 
above conclusion (Fig. S3). When the reaction products were scraped 
from the Li surface, the corresponding mass was tested by Mass Spec­
trometry. A clear signal peak at the m/z of 163.04 was detected (Fig. 1d), 
further verifying the formation of Li2-DMTD. Luckily, the formed Li2- 
DMTD-based protective film has a high ionic conductivity.
The Li||Li symmetric cells were assembled with different electrolytes 
and the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were carried out. As 
shown in Fig. 1e, a charge transfer impedance of 35 Ω was obtained for 
the cell with 2 % DMTD additive in the electrolyte, which was much 
lower than that with the standard electrolyte (85 Ω). To evaluate the 
interface stability of the cell with DMTD, the Li plating/stripping be­
haviors were evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 1f, the Li||Li symmetric cells 
could stably cycle for more than 1000 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 current density 
and a lower polarization voltage of about 50 mV was gained with DMTD 
electrolyte (Fig. S4a). This means the excellent reaction kinetics and 
interface stability for the DMTD-based cell were consistent with the EIS 
results. However, the base cell could only cycle for 650 h with a sudden 
short circuit. When the cell was operated under a high current density of 
5 mA cm−2, it could only run for 60 h with fluctuating polarization 
voltage in the later stage (Fig. 1g and Fig. S4b). In contrast, the DMTD- 
based cell cycled stably for more than 250 h, with the cyclic life elon­
gating to about 5-fold than that of the base cell, demonstrating that the 
formed Li2-DMTD film could significantly prevent the Li dendrites 
growth and stabilize the interface. The morphologies of the Li metal 
after cycling for 50 cycles were characterized, as shown in Fig. 1h–i. A 
uniform and flat surface was observed for the DMTD-based anode after 
cycling. In contrast, the base cell exhibited a number of cracks and 
dendrites for the based Li anode due to the inhomogeneous Li plating/ 
stripping behaviors.

The charge–discharge curves and CV results demonstrate a decreased 
charge overpotential for the oxidation of Li2S, which may be adapted to 
the changed reaction routine. Aiming to investigate the reaction kinetics 
between Li2-DMTD and Li2S, the Li||Li2S cells were assembled. The 
initial galvanostatic charge/discharge profiles of Li||Li2S cells with 
different electrolytes are shown in Fig. 3a. As the inherent electronic 
insulation and insolubility of Li2S, a high activation voltage is needed to 
overcome the kinetic barrier. It shows one charge plateau at about 3.6 V 
with 1090 mAh g−1 capacity for the control cell, corresponding to the 
oxidation of Li2S to S8. Two discharge voltage plateaus II’and III’ 
correspond to the reduction of S8 to Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8) and Li2Sx to Li2S, 
respectively. On the contrary, for the DMTD-based cell, the initial acti­
vation voltage of Li2S was substantially decreased. The plateau I and 
plateau II are attributed to the oligomerization reactions between Li2- 
DMTD and Li2S. The subsequent plateau III corresponds to the poly­
merization between oligomers and S radicals with a superpolymer as the 
charge product. The plateau IV and plateau V correspond to the 
decomposition of the superpolymer to oligomers, and the plateau VI and 
plateau VII are attributed to the conversions of oligomers to Li2S, 
respectively [35,36]. The charge specific capacity of the DMTD-based 
cell could reach about 1200 mAh g−1 which contains the capacity of 
Li2-DMTD, indicating the introduction of DMTD accelerates the con­
version of Li2S at a lower potential. Cyclic voltammetry analysis of the 
Li||Li2S cells with different electrolytes was further performed 
(Fig. 3b–c), and the anodic and cathodic peaks are consistent with the 
charge/discharge plateaus. The activation voltage of Li2S was drastically 
reduced from 3.11 V to 2.56 V after the addition of DMTD, further 
confirming that the reaction kinetics between Li2-DMTD and Li2S is 
faster than the conversion of Li2S itself.
To further evaluate the multifunctionality of the DMTD in LSBs, the 
long-term cycling performances of Li-S cells were evaluated. As shown in 
Fig. 4a, the cell with DMTD delivered a discharge capacity of 1036.2 
mAh g−1 and remained 817.8 mAh g−1 after 140 cycles with a capacity 
retention of 78.9 % at 0.5C, which was much higher than that of the 
control cell with an initial discharge capacity of 538.9 mAh g−1 and 
55.7 % capacity retention. Compared to the S cathode, the added DMTD 
in the electrolyte almost provides no capacity, the performance 
improvement of the cell mainly contributed to the synergistic effects of 
the DMTD, which not only produced the stable protective film on anode, 
but also changed the conversion routine of the Li2S, avoiding the shuttle 
of polysulfides and improving the utilization of Li2S. To confirm whether 
the reaction product of MDTD and Li2S contributes a significant capacity 
when used as an electrode material, a battery was fabricated using the 
product. The results indicate that the capacity provided by the reaction 
product is negligible compared to sulfur, with a capacity of less than 20 
mAh g−1 (Fig. S8). The charge/discharge profiles of the Li-S cells at the 
50th cycle are shown in Fig. 4b. The cell with DMTD had a polarization 
voltage of only 0.27 V, which was significantly lower than the control 
cell’s 0.5 V, indicating a lower charge transfer impedance for the 
inversible oligomerization reactions of Li2S and Li2-DMTD than the 
typical transformation processes of the single Li2S. After 50 cycles, EIS 
was performed on the cells. As shown in Fig. 4c, both of the cells have 
two semicircles corresponding to the SEI film impedance (Rsei) in the 
high-frequency region and the charge transfer impedance (Rct) in the 
low-frequency region, respectively. The fitted data of Nyquist plots are 
presented in Table S1. The Rsei and Rct values of the blank cell are 95.6 Ω 
and 51.6 Ω, respectively, while the Rsei and Rct values of the DMTD cell 
are substantially lower, 64.9 Ω and 12.9 Ω, respectively. These are 
attributed to the high ionic conducting ability of the Li2-DMTD-based 
SEI on the anode and less accumulation of the insulating Li2S or side 
products at the cathode. To verify this, Fig. 4d shows the high-resolution 
S2p XPS spectrum of the charged cathode after cycling. It can be seen 
that except for the S was detected with the S2p3/2 peak located at 164.3 
eV, many residual Li2S and Li2S2 with the S2p3/2 peaks appeared at 
160.42 eV and 162.47 eV were found on the cathode of the control cell, 
which was derived from the incomplete transformations of the Li2S 
[37,38]. In contrast, C–S bonds with the S2p3/2 peak appeared at 162.1 
eV, originating from the oligomers, were detected for the DMTD-based 
cathode, and the intensity of the oxidation products of S was much 
lower than that of the control cell, indicating the improved interface 
stability in the presence of DMTD.
SEM is further used to analyze the morphologies of the cycled Li 
anodes in Li-S cells. As shown in Fig. 4e–f. The Li anode from the control 
cell was severely corroded by polysulfides with a concave and uneven 
surface (Fig. 4e). With the help of DMTD, the redox pathway was 
changed, and the shuttle of polysulfides was significantly decreased. 
Consequently, it exhibited a smooth and dense surface for the Li anode, 
confirming the shuttle and dendrites suppression effects of DMTD 
(Fig. 4f). The long-term cycle performance of Li-S cells was performed at

found to be 0.066 µm and 0.087 µm (Figs. 5c and S10a), respectively, 
significantly lower than those treated with standard electrolyte, which 
were 1.701 µm and 2.36 µm (Figs. 5d and S10b). This lower roughness 
implies smoother surfaces, which is advantageous for the battery’s sta­
bility and longevity.
Regarding the mechanism of DMTD improving Li-S battery cycle 
performance, the proposed hypothesis is shown in Fig. 5, which can be 
attributed to several key points: i) Ionic Conductivity Improvement: The 
lithiation products of Li2-DMTD exhibit high ionic conductivity, which 
effectively suppresses the growth of lithium dendrites and corrosion of 
the electrolyte [33]. This improved conductivity contributes to 
enhanced battery stability and longevity. ii) Suppression of Shuttle Ef­
fect: DMTD facilitates the reversible oligomerization with Li2S, forming 
organosulfur oligomers during the electrochemical process as shown in 
Fig. 6a. These oligomers immobilize the sulfur species, thereby sup­
pressing the shuttle effect where polysulfide species shuttle between the 
electrodes [27,39]. This prevents the loss of active sulfur species and 
degradation of the battery performance over multiple cycles. iii) 
Accelerated Electron Transfer Rates: Compared to the direct oxidation of 
Li2S, the oligomerization reaction between Li2-DMTD and Li2S has a 
lower energy barrier [27,40]. This lower barrier accelerates the rates of 
electron transfer within the battery, improving its electrochemical ki­
netics as shown in Fig. 6b. As a result, the battery exhibits enhanced 
cycling performance with improved capacity retention over many 
charge–discharge cycles. We would like to clarify that our in-situ Raman 
spectroscopy results shown in Fig. 6a indicate that no S–S bonds are 
detected during the charging phase, which indicates that the sulfur 
oligomers do not participate as active materials.
4. Conclusion
In essence, the addition of DMTD, featuring two thiol groups on the 
thiadiazole ring, shows promise in enhancing the performance of Li-S 
batteries. Experimental findings demonstrate that lithiation products 
of Li2-DMTD exhibit high ionic conductivity, effectively mitigating the 
growth of Li dendrites and electrolyte corrosion. Symmetric Li||Li cell 
longevity with DMTD is bolstered by approximately fivefold under 5 mA 
cm−2 current density compared to the base electrolyte. Moreover, Li2- 
DMTD forms reversibly oligomerized with Li2S during subsequent 
electrochemical processes, immobilizing sulfur species and curbing the 
shuttle effect confirmed by in-situ Raman spectra. This oligomerization 
reaction, with a lower energy barrier than direct Li2S oxidation, accel­
erates electron transfer rates and enhances cell electrochemical kinetics. 
Consequently, cells with DMTD display improved cycling performance, 
with capacity retention increasing from 45.7 % to 81.2 % after 320
