Qualitative Determination of Polysulfide Species in a Lithium−Sulfur
Battery by HR-LC-APCI-MSn with One-Step Derivatization

ABSTRACT: Lithium−Sulfur (Li−S) batteries are one of the most
promising next-generation batteries due to their ultrahigh energy density
up to 500 W h kg−1. However, despite the steady progress during the last
several decades, there have been significant challenges for practical appli-
cations and commercialization. One of the major issues is controlling
the lithium polysulfide (LiPS) shuttling process, which causes premature
cell failure. To better understand the mechanism of the LiPS shuttling
chemistry, a qualitative and quantitative analysis on polysulfide species in
Li−S cell has profound significance for realizing highly efficient sulfur electrochemistry. Here we report a qualitative determination of
the derivatized polysulfides in the electrolyte of a custom-made Li−S pouch cell with a high-resolution liquid chromatography-
atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometry method for the first time. The ionization efficiency of the
methylated polysulfides was affected by the tune parameters such as the corona discharge current, the vaporizer temperature, and the
source capillary temperature. It was found that the source capillary temperature was the dominant parameter to increase the peak
intensity of CH3S7
−ion, which was the smallest peak in the spectrum. An unusual and unique ionization pattern for methylated
polysulfides detected in atmospheric pressure chemical ionization negative mode was elucidated by using first-principles calculations.
KEYWORDS: atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI), lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery, lithium polysulfide (LiPS),
high resolution liquid chromatography-atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometry (HR-LC-APCI-MSn)
1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
Lithium ion batteries (LIBs) have dominated the electric
vehicle market as rechargeable power sources due to their high
energy density and capacity. Particularly, as next-generation
energy-storage devices, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have
been achieved in promoting the performances since they were
first developed in the 1960s.1−3 An electrochemical redox con-
version mechanism between sulfur and catalytic sulfides offers
us the possibility to realize the ultrahigh theoretical energy
of 2600 W h kg−1 and energy density up to 500 W h kg−1.
In addition, sulfur, one of the most abundant elements on
earth, makes Li−S batteries more cost-effective than other
LIBs.
However, Li−S batteries have several critical issues that are
interrupting to practical applications, and their performance is
limited within the laboratory-level model system as of now.4
Elemental sulfur (octasulfur, S8) undergoes structural and
morphological changes to the smaller polysulfide (PS) during
a charge/discharge process, and soluble lithium polysulfide
LiPS, Li2SX (3 ≤X ≤8) enters into the ether-based elec-
trolyte. Dissolved LiPS increases the electrolyte viscosity and
significantly reduces the ionic conductivity. Simultaneously,
LiPS intermediates shuttle back and forth between the cathode
and the anode, resulting in severe active material loss, lithium
metal anode corrosion, and columbic inefficiency. In addition,
insoluble LiPS (Li2S2/Li2S) produced by a longer PS dispro-
portionation process makes porosity loss in a cathode and
separator. Premature precipitation of Li2S2/Li2S on both of the
electrodes gives impedance increasing and dramatically rapid
battery failure. This sequence of events, that is, the so-called
“LiPS shuttle effect”, triggers and accelerates the self-discharge
process of the Li−S rechargeable batteries. However, inversely,
mobility of the PS anions from shuttling relocates inert sulfur
in the cathode, allowing the electrode to have uniform sulfur
distribution. Easier access of electrons to sulfur generates a fast
sulfur redox reaction through the enhanced kinetics.5,6
According to aforementioned disadvantages and advantages,
maintaining the appropriate degree of shuttle effect becomes a
major issue in enhancing the cell performance.7−15 There have
been tons of studies using electron spin resonance (ESR),16
UV/vis spectroscopy,16−19 cyclic voltammograms,19 X-ray dif-
fraction and transmission,20−22 X-ray microscopy, Raman,23,24
X-ray absorption including XANES,25−27 HPLC/UV,16,17,28,29

The most basic and important factor of these studies is the
prerequisite identification of PS species in the battery system,
especially in the electrolyte. Liquid chromatography/mass
spectrometry (LC/MS) enables one to present the way to
distinguish the each polysulfide ion with a different sulfur chain
length.29,30 Zheng et al. assigned derivatized polysulfide
mixtures and suggested a polysulfide reduction mechanism in
the first cell operation using a liquid chromatography-atmospheric
pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometry
(LC-APCI-MS) method.28,29 But for qualitatively analyzing the
PS species, they used a linear relation between the logarithm of
retention time and sulfur number in Me2Sn (3 ≤n ≤8); that is,
an indirect method was used. And the PS ion from S8 was not
detected. So here, we present the more effective and infallible
way to identify methylated PS species (Me2Sn, 3 ≤n ≤8) with
elemental composition using high-resolution liquid chromatog-
raphy-atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass
spectrometry (HR-LC-APCI-MSn) for the first time. In con-
junction with other in situ spectroscopic tools, ex situ char-
acterization with HR-LC-APCI-MSn will play an important role
in explaining the PS behavior within Li−S cell operation.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Sulfur (99.998%, Sigma-Aldrich), methyl trisulfide (Me2S3,
98%, Sigma-Aldrich), methanol (high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) grade, ≥99.9%, Honeywell Interna-
tional Inc.), methyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (methyl triflate,
≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich), anhydrous dimethoxyethane (DME,
99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99.5%, Sigma-
Aldrich), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%,
Sigma-Aldrich) were purchased and used without further
treatment.
The Li−S pouch cell was assembled and operated in an
Ar-filled glovebox. All materials were handled in an Ar-filled
glovebox (H2O, O2 < 0.1 ppm), also. The cell consisted of a Li
foil (99.9, MTI Corp.) anode, a porous polypropylene separator
(Celgard), a sulfur-composite cathode (65% sulfur, 25% carbon
black, 10% poly(ethylene oxide) coated on an aluminum current
collector), and a DOL/DME-based electrolyte with the
geometric area of 15 cm2.
The electrolyte consisted of 0.75 M LiTFSI, 5 wt % LiNO3
in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of DOL/DME solvents. Charge−
Discharge cyclings were performed three times with 0.1 C-rate
at 25 °C, and the cutoff voltage range was 1.8−2.5 V.
The electrolyte was extracted by soaking the opened pouch
cell in DME for 48 h using a sealed bottle. And the polysulfide
anions in the extract were derivatized with the methylated
form, (CH3)2Sn, according to reaction (1).28,29,31

A derivatization procedure was constructed as follows: DME
(450 μL) containing methyl triflate (0.2 mmol) was added into
a vial and vortexed for 1 min; 200 μL of the electrolyte extract
derived from the Li−S cell was then added and vortexed for
5 min; the mixture was put in an HPLC vial and sealed with an
airtight cap. The derivatized polysulfide samples in the airtight
HPLC vials were taken out of the glovebox for an immediate
HPLC analysis. All of the procedures were performed in an
Ar-filled glovebox (H2O, O2 < 0.1 ppm).
For HPLC/UV analysis, a Waters e2695 Alliance HPLC
system and a 2998 photodiode array detector were used. The
separation was performed using a CAPCELLPAK C18 column
(50 mm× 4.6 mm I.D., 3 μm; OSAKA SODA), and the
column was maintained at 40 °C. Eluent A was purified water
obtained from a Milli-Q system (18.2 Mω, Millipore), and
eluent B was methanol (HPLC grade, ≥99.9%, Honeywell
International Inc.). The flow rate was 0.7 mL/min, and the
gradient mobile phase condition was as follows: 0 min: 25 vol %
B (75 vol % A), 0−10 min: linear change from 25 vol % to
100 vol % B, 10−20 min: isocratic with 100 vol % B. The total
run time was 20 min, and the sample injection volume was
10 uL. HPLC/UV chromatograms were obtained in a full
wavelength range (190−800 nm) and plotted at 254 nm.
Empower 3 software was used for chromatogram recording.
Experiments on HR-MS were performed with a Thermo
LTQ Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer with APCI interface
that was coupled to a Dionex UltiMate 3000 HPLC system
(Thermo Fisher Scientific). For sample introduction and
separation, a CAPCELLPAK C18 column (50 mm× 4.6 mm
I.D., 3 μm; OSAKA SODA) was used, and the gradient mobile
phase condition was identical with that of HPLC/UV experi-
ments as above. Methylated polysulfides were detected in the
Orbitrap at 240 000 resolution, and the scan mass range was
set to cover m/z from 50 to 300. Internal calibration was
performed using the ion signal of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at
m/z 112.985 587 as a lock mass.
The negative APCI mode was used to detect PS ions and to
tune the parameters, which were optimized as follows: the
sheath and auxiliary gases of 50 and 5 arbitrary units, respec-
tively, the capillary voltage of 6 kV, the corona discharge
current of 15 μA, the vaporizer temperature of 150 °C, and the
source capillary temperature of 150 °C.
It was found that the dominant tuning parameters that affect
the ionization efficiency of PS ions were the corona discharge
current, the vaporizer temperature, and the source capillary
temperature. Tables 1 and 2 show the intensities of the PS ion
detected for each of the tuning conditions. For the optimi-
zation of the corona discharge current and the vaporizer tem-
perature, the peak intensity of the CH3S3
−ion (m/z 111) was
used as the evaluation factor to determine the ionization
efficiency. The peak intensity of the CH3S3
−ion showed little
dependence on the source capillary temperature, but the peak
intensity of the CH3S7
−ion (m/z 239) increased as the source
capillary temperature decreased. Therefore, the source capillary
temperature was optimized to increase the peak intensity of the
CH3S7
−ion.

The multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was
operated by tracing the transition of the specific precursor
ion. The Orbitrap mass spectrometer was operated using the
LTQ Tune software.
All calculations were performed using the Gaussian16
computational package.32 We employed the density functional
theory (DFT) of Becke’s three parameters hybrid functional
for the exchange part and the Lee−Yang−Parr correlation
functional (B3LYP) using 6-311++G(d,p) basis sets.33,34
Frequency calculations were performed with the optimized
geometries, all of which were characterized as local minima.
Thermodynamic enthalpies and free energies were evaluated at
the condition of gas phase under 1 atm and 150 °C, which are
near to experimental environments.

is analyzed by HPLC/UV. The chromatogram is shown in

The mass spectra for the methylated PS species, from Me2S3
to Me2S8, are shown in Figure 2. The methylated PS species
are identified in the unusual ion form of [MeSx−1]−(labeled as
▼in Figure 2) using the negative ion APCI mode. It is worth
noting that Me2S8 is identified for the first time using the
LC-APCI-MS method. The methylated PS ions of longer chain
lengths, especially Me2S8, are hard to detect because they are
prone to redistribution. For this reason, other previous works
have indirectly assigned Me2S8 by an extrapolation of the
calibration curve and have not shown mass spectrum data of
Me2S8.29,31 In this work, [MeS7]−ion of m/z 238.828 52, which
is the ionized form of Me2S8, is directly detected and confirmed
to ±3 ppm accuracy by applying a specifically optimized APCI
tune parameter (Figure 2f). The experimental isotopic
distribution pattern of [MeS7]−ion matches with its theoretical
isotopic distribution pattern, as shown in Figure 3. The MS/MS
spectra for the methylated PS species, from Me2S4 to Me2S7, are
shown in Figure 4. The MS/MS spectra of Me2S3 and Me2S8 was
not acquired because, for Me2S3, the mass-to-charge ratio of the
Me2S3 fragment is under 50, which is the lower limit range of the
Orbitrap analyzer, and for Me2S8, the intensity of the precursor
ion was insufficient (intensity of m/z 239 = 7.97E3).
Theoretical calculations based on the DFT methods were
conducted to confirm the unusual ion form detected under an
APCI condition. As a result, one of the two S−S bonds of
Me2S3 ([CH3−S3−CH3]) stretches from 2.102 to 2.784 Å after
deprotonation from a methyl group (Figure 5). The dissociation
process of the stretched S−S bond of Me2S3 ([CH3−S3−CH2]−)
is predicted to be exergonic (ΔG0 = −3.89 kcal/mol) though it is
endothermic (ΔH0 = +8.22 kcal/mol), which shows the bond
readily dissociates due to the entropy effect (Figure 6).
Furthermore, one of the fragments from the above dissociation,
CH2S, can be protonated by H3O+ due to the stronger proton
affinity of CH2S than that of H2O.
4. CONCLUSION
Remarkable advances have been made on Li−S batteries in the
past decades with significant understanding and cutting-edge
research. However, we are still far from realizing an energy
density over 500W h kg−1 beyond the current lithium ion
batteries. To achieve a significant development, there needs to
be full understanding of the fundamental electrochemistry in
lithium−sulfur batteries.
Focusing on the shuttle effect of LiPS, this study addresses
the importance of a qualitative identification for the PS species
in a Li−S cell electrolyte. Using the HR-LC-APCI-MSn method
and one-step derivatization, we identified the structural assign-
ment of Me2Sn (3 ≤n ≤8) clearly for the first time. [MeSn−1]−,
the rarely detected analyte in the usual APCI process, was
justified by theoretical calculations.
These results are expected to contribute to the in-depth
understanding of the Li−S cell operation mechanism,
especially the LiPS redox mechanism. This will soon be used
