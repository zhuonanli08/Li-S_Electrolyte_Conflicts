1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) battery possesses the advantages of low 
cost, high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg–1), and eco-
friendly, which is deemed as an attractive potential alternative 
for Li-ion batteries.[1] However, the premature passivation of 
lithium sulfide (Li2S) discharge products, associated with poly-
sulfide shuttling and sluggish kinetics, seriously compromised 
the conversion efficiency and stability of ether-based Li–S bat-
teries.[2] The morphology and structure of Li2S products are key 
factors that determine the discharge capacity, rate capability, 
and reversibility of Li–S batteries.[3]
Passivation of the sulfur electrode by insulating lithium sulfide (Li2S) restricts 
the reversibility and sulfur utilization of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. 
Although electrolytes with high donor number (DN) solvents induce tri-sulfur 
radical intermediate thus 3D nucleation of Li2S with fast kinetics can be 
achieved, their catastrophic reactivities with Li metal hinder practical applica-
tions. Here, the use of high DN solvent as an additive instead of as co-sol-
vent to solve their incompatibility between cathode and anode is proposed, 
by adopting N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as a proof-of-concept. Such a 
strategy is accomplished by the unique solvation structure of the NMP added 
electrolyte, where the preference of NMP-Li+ coordination squeezes out 
partial 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) molecules while enriching 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL) molecules in the first solvation sheath of Li+ ions. It affords the robust 
SEI on Li metal from corrosion either by NMP or the dissolved polysulfides. 
Spectral analyses (Raman and UV–vis) also verify that the coordinated 
NMP additive preserves its S3•− radicals stabilization ability as it does as a 
co-solvent, which effectively improves the sulfur conversion kinetics and 
reversibility. This approach enables competitive capacity retention and a 
stable cycling performance of 340 cycles, which is one of the longest lifespans 
known for the high DN solvent involved Li–S batteries.
Many strategies have been proposed to 
address those challenges in the past few 
decades with the aim of improving the 
electrochemical performance of Li–S bat-
teries.[4] Confinement of polysulfide to the 
host materials could mitigate the cathode 
passivation to a certain extent, either by 
trapping them physically in host materials 
such as various carbons[5] and conductive 
polymers,[6] or by chemically adsorbing 
them onto the specific cathode struc-
ture including transition metal chalcoge-
nides,[7] MXene-based complex materials[8] 
and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs).[9] 
These approaches enhance the sulfur uti-
lization and suppress the polysulfide (PS) 
shuttle in a sense, however, the electrode 
passivation issue is yet to be completely 
resolved during repeated cycles.[10]
The lithium polysulfides (Li2S4) in reg-
ular ether electrolytes, serve as intrinsic 
redox mediators to guide the growth of 
insulating Li2S passivation film on the 
electrode,[11] which greatly restrains the 
liquid-solid conversion efficiency. Great 
progress has been made to modulate the passivation issue of 
Li2S deposition.[7b,12] Specifically, the electrolyte recipe strategy 
is attractive for its tunable solvent coordination structure that 
could effectively regulate the LiPS speciation and conversion, 
which further affects the Li2S deposition morphology.[13] Tri-
sulfur radical anions (S3•−) were involved in the electrolyte with 
high donor number (DN) solvent,[14] where the sulfur speciation 
pathway was significantly altered, thus resulting in 3D growth 
of the Li2S final product.[14a,i] Such distinct Li2S nucleation 
manner decouples the dependence between capacity and con-
ductive surface,[13c,14b] otherwise a host material with a particu-
larly high surface area is essential for a decent capacity delivery. 
It is therefore consequential to create an eligible atmosphere 
that could propagate and stabilize the radicals for the high effi-
ciency of the multi-phase and multi-electron conversion of the 
sulfur-based electrochemistry. Nonetheless, the high-DN sol-
vents are well-known for their catastrophic reactivity with Li 
metal anode, such as DMSO,[14c] DMA,[14b] DMF,[14e,i] etc, which 
limits their practical application in Li–S batteries. Previous 
trials of high-DN solvents for Li–S batteries typically embrace 
the use of protected Li metal, however, limited lifespan with fast 
capacity decay was reported, i.e., 400 mAh g–1 after 180 cycles 
for the TMU-DOL hybrid electrolyte and 680 mAh g–1 after 
50 cycles for the DMI-LiNO3 blending.[14a,g] It is therefore vital 
to explore an advanced electrolyte formula that is compatible

In this work, we propose the use of high-DN solvent in the 
form of an additive rather than as a co-solvent to the conven-
tional ether electrolyte, by using NMP as a proof of concept. 
The Gutmann’s donor numbers of NMP, DOL, and DME sol-
vents are listed in Table S1, Supporting Information, showing 
the highest DN for NMP. Spectral characterizations indicate 
that the NMP additive preserves its high-DN characteristics 
as a free solvent that triggers the tri-sulfur radical pathway. It 
induces the formation of porous-network Li2S to postpone the 
passivation therefore enhanced reaction kinetic and sulfur utili-
zation are achieved. Moreover, lithium metal anode is compat-
ible with such 1 vol% NMP added electrolyte, as conveyed by 
the combination of multiple electrochemical analyses and sur-
face acquisition of the Li anode. This is attributed to the unique 
solvation structure of Li+ ions in the electrolyte, where NMP 
molecules are intensively involved in the first solvation sheath. 
It leads to the squeezing out of partial DME molecules while 
enriching the DOL molecules in the solvation sheath. Such a 
solvation structure not only suppresses the reactivity between 
NMP and Li metal but also endows the DOL-rich coordina-
tion for the robust SEI formation on Li metal. Consequently, 
the Li–S battery with 1 vol% NMP in ether electrolyte delivered 
high reversible capacity (1250 mAh g–1 at 0.05 C), outstanding 
cycling stability (340 cycles at 0.3 C with high-capacity reten-
tion of about 700 mAh g–1), and excellent rate capability of 
620 mAh g–1 at 1 C. Even with harsh conditions of high areal 
sulfur loading (4.2  mg cm–2) and lean electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) 
ratio (7.1 µL mg–1), a high discharge capacity of 750 mAh g–1 was 
maintained after 50 cycles in the 1 vol% NMP electrolyte. This 
work opens a new avenue toward the choice of electrolyte addi-
tive for Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
We note that the NMP additive significantly impacted the per-
formance of Li–S batteries. In order to obtain the optimal ratio 
of NMP additive to DOL/DME solvents, the electrochemical 
performances of Li–S batteries with various content of NMP 
additive were firstly studied. The Li–S cells with 0%, 1%, 5% 
and 10 vol% NMP additives deliver the specific discharge 
capacity of 550, 896, 654, and 540 mAh g–1 after 100 cycles at 
0.1 C, respectively (Figure 1a). It is clear that the 1 vol% NMP 
additive-based battery exhibited the highest capacity retention. 
The detrimental capacity retention with higher NMP additive 
might be attributed to the drastic reactivity between the exces-
sive NMP additive (>1 vol%) with Li metal anode. Thereafter, 
long-term plating/stripping stability and interface resistance of 
the Li–Li symmetric cells were tested. There is no significant 
difference between the 1 vol% NMP electrolyte and the blank 
electrolyte for the plating/stripping voltage profile and the elec-
trochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) spectrum of the Li–Li 
symmetric cells (Figure  1b,c, Supporting Information). The 
interfacial resistance of Li–Li symmetric cells with 1 vol% NMP 
electrolyte is even lower than that of the blank electrolyte (see 
details in Table S2, Supporting Information), implying a stable 
Li anode interface with a trace of NMP additive. However, the 
electrolytes with higher NMP concentration (5–30 vol%) either 
give rise to voltage fluctuating or high polarization during Li 
plating/stripping (Figures 1b, S1 and S2, Supporting Informa-
tion). It indicates that the electrolyte with 1 vol% NMP additive 
is compatible with lithium metal anode. Moreover, the ionic 
conductivity of electrolyte with 1 vol% NMP additive still keeps 
a relatively high level of about 7.4 mS cm–1, which is comparable 
to that of the blank electrolyte (Figure S3, Supporting Informa-
tion). The electrolyte viscosity increases with the increase of the 
NMP content, where the 1 vol% NMP electrolyte is nearly equal 
to that of the blank electrolyte (Table S3).
In order to clarify the underlying principle of the compat-
ible lithium metal anode interface with 1 vol% NMP additive, 
Raman analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of 
NMP on the Li+ solvation structure of electrolytes. As the NMP 
additive ratio increased from 1 to 20 vol%, the peak intensity 
of the solvated DME (coordinated-DME, 870 cm–1) decreases, 
along with the strengthening of the free-DME peak (850 cm–1), 
as shown in Figure  1d. The characteristic C–C–C symmetric 
stretch band of NMP molecule (926 cm–1) emerges concur-
rently with the increase of NMP ratio, nevertheless, it is absent 
in the electrolyte with 1 vol% NMP additive. Moreover, the 
Raman signal of the coordinated DOL molecules also strength-
ened after the introduction of NMP additive (Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information). This might be attributed to the different 
coordination structures of DME and DOL solvents with Li+.[15] 
Specifically, the linear DME molecule prefers to chelate Li+ 
atoms as (2O–Li) interaction, while the cyclic DOL or NMP 
molecules have a more rigid backbone structure for point coor-
dination (O–Li). It indicates that NMP additive extrudes partial 
coordinated DME out of the inner solvation sheath of Li+ ions 
and reconstructs it with the cooperating residual coordinated 
DME/DOL.
The solution structure of electrolyte is further demon-
strated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, as shown in 
Figures 1e and S5, Supporting Information. The proportion of 
coordinated solvent molecules in the first solvation sheath of Li+ 
is clearly altered with the introduction of NMP, which is con-
firmed by the radial distribution function (RDF) of Li+ to the elec-
tronegative oxygen of DME (Li+-ODME), DOL (Li+-ODOL), NO3–  
(Li+-ONO3–), TFSI– (Li+-OTFSI–) and NMP (Li+-ONMP). NMP mole-
cules exhibit a shorter distance to Li+ (1.900 Å) compared with 
the ether solvents, suggesting that NMP molecules are involved 
in the first solvation sheath (Table S5, Supporting Information). 
The coordination numbers of solvent molecules and salt anions 
around the Li+ are shown in Figure  1f and S6, Supporting 
Information, respectively. The introduction of NMP additive 
does not alter the coordination number of anions (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information). However, it decreases the coordi-
nation number of DME, which is consistent with the Raman 
analysis. More importantly, NMP molecules are not only closely 
packed in the solvation shell, but also intensify the coordina-
tion of DOL molecules to Li+ ions. Enrichment of DOL in the 
first coordination sheath is beneficial for robust SEI formation 
because of its ring-opening polymerization during cycling.[16] 
Solvent closely packed to Li+ ions possesses an increased 
LUMO energy (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) compared 
to their free state, which was one of the most effective strate-
gies to regulate the electrochemical window of the electrolyte,

polysulfide solution from yellow to blue-green when adding the

To understand the Li2S nucleation growth behavior in dif-
ferent electrolytes, the related I–t curves (2.05 V) were analyzed 
based on the Bewick, Fleischman, and Thirsk (BFT) models 
and Scharifker-Hills models (SH) (Figure  4g).[14i,25] 3DI and 
3DP mean three-dimensional nucleation mechanisms with 
instantaneous (I) and progressive (P) growth rates controlled by 
ion diffusion, respectively. 2DI and 2DP suggest a two-dimen-
sional nucleation mechanism with a growth rate controlled 
by lattice incorporation. According to the related equations 
(in Supporting Information) and Figure 4g, it is clear that the 
Li2S growth model in NMP electrolyte is a 3DP model, while 
it is a 2DP model for the blank electrolyte. This is the essential 
reflection that NMP additive could induce the formation of 
internal redox mediator (S3•− radicals), which significantly mod-
ulates morphology of Li2S and decouples the dependence of 
Li2S deposition process and conductive interface.
In short summary, the use of high DN solvent as an addi-
tive instead of as co-solvent could decouple the tri-sulfur radical 
favorable high-DN solvents from their incompatibility with the 
Li metal anode. The proposed bifunctional NMP additive pre-
fers to coordinate with Li+ ions due to its strong polarity, which 
modulates the solvation structure of Li+ ions with enriched DOL 
and NMP coordination sheath. Such a unique solution struc-
ture enables the robust SEI formation thus leading to improved 
interface stability of Li metal. Moreover, the coordinated NMP 
molecules preserve their high DN properties about stabilizing 
the tri-sulfur radical, which affords 3D Li2S nucleation with fast 
conversion kinetics that improves the electrochemical revers-
ibility (Figure 4h and 4i). Such a strategy is affordable for other 
high DN solvents, where the application of 1 vol% DMSO or 
DMF in the electrolyte significantly improved the cycling sta-
bility of Li–S batteries (Figure S19, Supporting Information).
The effectiveness bifunctional NMP additive was further cor-
related to the cycling performance of the Li–S battery. S/ketjen 
black (KB) composite prepared by the melt-diffusion method 
was used as the cathode. In addition to the elevated potential for 
the first discharge plateau, the 1 vol% NMP also enabled a much 
lower overpotential (voltage gap between charge/discharge) than 
that of the blank electrolyte (Figure 5a). It is notable that the 
overpotential of the cell in NMP electrolyte kept steady for 200 
cycles, whereas it was increasing for the cell with blank electro-
lyte. Such overpotential reached 0.308 V for the blank electrolyte 
after 200 cycles indicative of compromised energy efficiency.

performance of Li–S batteries with both high sulfur loading 
and lean electrolyte condition. The Li–S battery with NMP elec-

sulfur loading (4.2 mg cm–2) with E/S ratio of about 7.1 µL mg–1

demonstrating a higher sulfur utilization due to more favorable 
free radical reaction paths and 3D porous network Li2S deposi-
tion morphology. On the contrary, the blank electrolyte capacity 
showed fast capacity decay at an identical condition.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we introduced 1 vol% of high-DN number NMP 
as an electrolyte additive, which decouples the tri-sulfur radical 
favorable high-DN solvents from their catastrophic reactivity 
with the Li metal anode. Such strategy was accomplished by 
the unique solvation structure of the NMP added electrolyte, 
where NMP prefers to coordinate with Li+ ions due to its strong 
polarity. It squeezes out partial DME molecules while enriches 
DOL molecules in the first solvation sheath of Li+ ions, which 
afford the robust SEI on Li metal composed by O-CH2-O/Li3N/
LiF organic-inorganic components. In conjunction with the 
decreased reactivity of the coordinated NMP toward Li metal, 
such SEI formation could suppress the Li metal corrosion from 
the dissolved polysulfide in the electrolyte, thus the shuttling 
effect of LiPS was significantly alleviated. Spectral (Raman 
and UV–vis) also indicated that the coordinated NMP additive 
could effectively stabilize S3•− radicals in ether-based solvent 
thus inducing the generation of a porous-network Li2S, as it did 
when employed as a solvent in the electrolyte, which effectively 
postpones the passivation of the sulfur cathode. Such distinct 
polysulfide speciation and Li2S nucleation manner afford fast 
conversion kinetics which greatly improved the electrochemical 
reversibility. Consequently, the 1 vol% NMP electrolyte of the 
Li–S battery showed a reversible capacity of about 700 mAh g–1 
after 340 cycles at 0.3 C, which is one of the longest lifespans 
known for the high DN solvent involved electrolyte in Li–S 
batteries (see the comparison of the lifespans of the literature 
with high DN electrolytes, Table S7, Supporting Information). 
Even with high areal sulfur loading (4.2  mg cm–2) and lean 
E/S ratio (7.1  µL mg–1), the Li–S battery delivered a high dis-
charge capacity of 750 mAh g–1 after 50 cycles. We believe that 
our comprehensive investigation on the role of high-DN solvent 
as an additive instead of as co-solvent, considering both on the 
cathode and anode, could offer guidance for exploring advanced 
electrolyte formulas with high DN molecules for Li–S batteries.
