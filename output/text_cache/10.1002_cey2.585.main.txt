Abstract
Carbonate‐electrolyte‐based lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries with solid‐phase
conversion offer promising safety and scalability, but their reversible
capacities are limited. In addition, large‐format pouch cells are paving the
way for large‐scale production. This study demonstrates the in situ formation
of a solid‐electrolyte interphase (SEI) as a protective layer using vinylene
carbonate (VC), highlighting its industrial adaptability. A high reversible
capacity is achieved by the lithiated poly‐VC SEI formed inside the cathode
particles as a nanoscale ionic conduction path, along with the traditional
surface protective layer. Furthermore, the severe dissolution of poly‐VC is
mitigated by LiF derived from fluorine ethylene carbonate as a co‐solvent,
enabling high rate performance and a long cycle life. A large 8 Ah pouch cell is
successfully developed, which shows a high energy density of 400 Wh kg−1
based on the cell weight. This work demonstrates the high performance of
large‐scale Li–S batteries with the in situ formation of a protective layer as a
scalable technique for future applications.

INTRODUCTION
To meet global targets for the alleviation of greenhouse
gas emissions, the electric vehicle market is growing
rapidly, which is accompanied by increasing demand for
lithium‐ion
batteries
(LIBs).1
To
produce
electrical
vehicles currently replacing gas‐powered vehicles, large
amounts of raw materials are mined and processed,
considerably affecting the environment.2 For the sustain-
able production of energy storage devices, one option is
to use materials from natural resources.1 Lithium–sulfur
(Li–S) batteries using an earth‐abundant elemental sulfur
cathode coupled with a lithium anode have attracted
considerable research attention.3 Sixty million tons of
elemental sulfur are annually produced as a by‐product,
the majority of which is stored in powder form or as solid
bricks.4 In addition, Li–S batteries have the potential to
realize electrical aircraft because of their higher energy
density (>500 Wh kg−1) compared with that of LIBs and
lithium‐metal
batteries
(<500 Wh kg−1).5,6
However,
Li–S batteries have not yet been implemented because
of the gap between academic and industrial research.
Thus, high‐performance Li–S batteries must be produced
using the materials and mature manufacturing processes
developed for LIBs to avoid further research expenditures
and save time on developing new production processes
and
know‐how,
in
addition
to
improving
battery
performance.7 The insights that prompted us to conduct
this study, aiming to facilitate a smooth transfer from
academic research to industry, are shown in Figure 1. We
focused on two main points, namely performance and
scalability, to narrow down the selection of various Li–S
technologies. In addition, we controlled sulfur content,
sulfur utilization, and the electrolyte (mL)/sulfur (mg)
ratio (E/S) as indices for achieving a high energy density.
For scalability, feasible material supplies and processes
were selected to reduce overall costs from the viewpoint
of commercialization.
Li–S batteries undergo two electrochemical reactions:
a liquid–solid two‐phase conversion and a solid‐phase
conversion.8 In the liquid–solid two‐phase conversion,
solid sulfur is reduced to soluble lithium polysulfides
(LPSs) at high voltages of 2.4 V and above, and the solid
discharge products of lithium disulfide (Li2S2) and
lithium sulfide (Li2S) form at and below 2.0 V.9 The
liquid–solid two‐phase conversion demonstrates a high
reversible capacity (900–1500 mAh g−1), even with a high
sulfur content (70–90 wt%) and a low E/S (2.8–3.5).8,10,11
As a result, Ah‐class pouch cells have delivered high
energy densities of over 324–460 Wh kg−1, which is
beyond the energy density of LIBs.8,10,11 However, a
low E/S under 3 is required, as well as a high sulfur
content
over
70 wt%
and
high
sulfur
utilization
(>1300 mAh g−1) to further increase the energy density
of Li–S batteries (500 Wh kg−1).6,8,10,12 In contrast, solid‐
phase conversion involves the direct transfer of sulfur to
Li2S and avoids the formation of soluble LPSs, which can
decrease the E/S (<2).13,14 Host carbons with a pore size
of less than 1 nm have been utilized to prevent electrolyte
penetration, which confines the small S2–4 molecules to

the pore volume of the microporous carbons is generally
limited, resulting in sulfur contents below 50 wt%.16
Thus, mesoporous carbons capable of confining more
than 60 wt% sulfur are necessary for use in the solid‐
phase conversion reaction. All‐solid‐state Li–S batteries
based on sulfide solid electrolytes have demonstrated
solid‐phase conversion with an extremely low E/S (<2) in
the cathode, a high sulfur content (>70 wt%), and high
sulfur
utilization
(>1500 mAh g−1).14,17
However,
Li
plating and stripping cause short circuits in solid‐state
batteries, and achieving a high areal capacity through
plating and stripping remains a challenge.18,19 Mean-
while, the development of practical all‐solid‐state pouch
cells has paved the way for large‐scale production.20,21
Liquid‐electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries can be manu-
factured using conventional LIB production processes
and equipment, and therefore, we believe that liquid‐
electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries with solid‐phase conver-
sion offer promising scalability and energy density
capabilities. High‐concentration electrolytes and micro-
porous/mesoporous
carbon
have
been
proposed
to
realize liquid‐electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries with a
solid‐phase conversion reaction.13,22–25 However, highly
concentrated electrolytes (e.g., 5.5 M LiFSI dimethyl
carbonate [DMC]) would be six times more expensive
than commercial electrolytes (1.0 M LiPF6 EC‐DMC),
even if the cost of LiFSI is the same as that of LiPF6.26 In
addition, concentrated electrolytes are 10 times or more
viscous than conventional ones, which would dramati-
cally extend the wetting time during the conventional
manufacturing process.26 Therefore, mesoporous carbon
(>2 nm) and electrolytes with conventional concentra-
tions (<2 M) that can achieve acceptable performance
would be more suitable for the mass production of Li–S
batteries with solid‐phase conversion reactions.
Li et al.27,28 prepared sulfur–carbon (SC) cathodes,
an artificial solid‐electrolyte interphase (SEI), to utilize
conventional
carbonate‐based
electrolytes
by
using
molecular layer deposition (MLD). They demonstrated
reversible cycling in practical Li–S pouch cells using
carbonate‐based electrolytes, resulting in an energy
density of over 200 Wh kg−1.28 However, the MLD
process is effort‐intensive, which increases the battery
cost. Therefore, from an economic perspective, the in
situ formation of a protective layer is preferred because
it is more facile and scalable. An industrial‐scale
technology that forms an SEI passivation layer through
in situ polymerization is already available for graphite
anodes in LIBs and is promising for the mass production
of Li–S batteries. Similar to the SEI layer formation on
the graphite anode in LIBs, fluoroethylene carbonate
(FEC) and vinylene carbonate (VC) as additives and co‐
solvents form a Li‐polycarbonate‐based SEI on an SC
cathode and demonstrate solid‐phase conversion in
conventional‐concentration electrolytes such as ether
electrolytes and electrolytes with localized high concen-
trations.29–33 However, the supply chains for battery‐
grade ether and fluorocarbon‐based solvents are insuffi-
cient for widespread adoption today. In addition, the
reversible capacities are approximately 1000 mAh g−1,
which is insufficient for achieving target energy densi-
ties. Therefore, to smoothly transition from lab‐scale
Li–S
batteries
to
practical
ones,
we
believe
that
carbonate‐electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries with solid‐
phase conversion are promising in terms of both energy
density and scalability.
In this study, we directly utilized VC and FEC as
electrolyte solvents to form an in situ protective SEI layer
for fully carbonate‐electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries. The
Li–S batteries with VC‐based electrolytes described
herein showed high reversible capacities (>1200 mAh
g−1) with solid‐phase conversion, even though the sulfur
content was over 60 wt%. We first revealed that a
lithiated poly‐VC‐based SEI formed not only on the
surface but also inside the SC particles, which acted as a
nanoscale ionic conductive path for high sulfur utiliza-
tion. In addition, cells with electrolytes containing a
mixture of VC and FEC demonstrated a reversible
capacity of over 578 mAh g−1 even at a rate of 10 C,
which originated from the thin SEI layer that formed
because of the adhesiveness of the LiF derived from FEC.
Finally, we demonstrated practical 8 Ah Li–S pouch cell
batteries operating with the solid‐phase conversion
mechanism and delivering an energy density of 400 Wh
kg−1. This study provides new insights and a strategy for
achieving practical high‐energy‐density Li–S batteries,
which is a breakthrough in traditional Li–S batteries and
will
accelerate
the
practical
application
of
next‐
generation batteries with a high energy density and
scalability.

The sulfur content ratios were calculated by dividing the
sulfur loading (mg cm–2) in the SC cathode after charging
by that before discharging, as summarized in Figure 2B.
The sulfur content ratio in the SC cathode of the Li–S/
DME was only 4.5% after charging. Interestingly, Li–S/
DMC also showed a sulfur content ratio of 5.44%. One of
the reasons for the poor capacity of Li–S batteries with
carbonate‐based
electrolytes
was
the
dissolution
of
thiocarbonates produced by reactions between DMC
and LPSs,35 in addition to passivation layers derived from
EC and DMC on the separator and cathode.36 In contrast,
with electrolytes based on PC and VC, which are cyclic
carbonates, the sulfur content ratios were maintained at
75% and 82%, respectively, even after charging and
discharging. The color of the VC‐based electrolyte was
checked before and after discharging. The photograph of
electrolytes is shown in Figure S4. The electrolyte was
discolored after discharging and characterized using
Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR). The
FT‐IR spectrum of VC‐based electrolytes after dischar-
ging was exactly the same as that before discharging
(Figure S5). In particular, the peaks attributable to
thiocarbonate (1450–1750 cm−1)37 formed by reacting
between carbonate solvents and LPSs were not observed.
Thus, the dissolution of LPSs was not confirmed by the
electrolyte and the discoloration would have originated
by a subtle decomposition product of VC formed during
the electrochemical reaction of Li–S batteries. The
mitigated dissolution of sulfur is due to the formation
of PC‐ or VC‐based polymers by reacting with LPSs.
Based on the discharge voltage, the VC‐based polymer
has lower ionic resistance than PC‐based polymer, which
would be originated by the polymer structure with ring‐
opened38 or cyclic carbonates derived from PC or VC,
respectively. Among the Li–S batteries examined with
carbonate solvents, those with the VC‐based electrolyte
exhibited the highest reversible capacity of 1380 mAh g−1
at the second cycle. In addition, no drastic capacity decay
was observed over 15 cycles (Figure 2C); therefore, VC is
a promising solvent for carbonate‐based electrolytes.
Before discharging, the S K‐edge XAS spectrum
showed a peak at 2472 eV attributable to sulfur.39 The
peak intensity of Li2S (2475 eV) increased when fully
discharged at 0.5 V, indicating that sulfur was converted
to Li2S. After fully charging, the intensities of the peaks
attributable to Li2S and sulfur were decreased and
increased, respectively. The changes in S K‐edge spectra
indicated that sulfur in host carbon functions as an active
material. Based on the results of S K‐edge spectra and
reversible capacity, 1.6 mol Li+ ions diffuse by the
maximum length of 1 µm in SC cathode particles with
a size of 2 µm during discharging and charging. However,
to achieve the discharge capacity of 1000 mAh g−1 at
about 0.05 C in solid‐phase conversion, the diffusion
length of Li+ ions has to be designed to 5 nm from the
surface to inside sulfur based on a simulation using the
resistivity of Li2S and Li2S2.40 There is a large gap of Li+
ion diffusion length between the experimental result and
the simulation. To fill the gap, it is necessary to consider
the existence of an ionic conduction path to shorten the
Li+ ion diffusion length inside SC particles used in this
experiment. In sulfide‐based all‐solid‐state Li–S batteries,
it is experimentally confirmed that not only nanoscaled
electronic conduction path by conductive additive but
also nanoscaled ionic conduction by solid electrolytes is
crucial for high sulfur utilization of solid‐phase redox.41
Based on the results and papers40,41 as shown above, we
built a hypothesis that the high sulfur utilization
obtained in this study originated from the in situ
formation
of
ionic
conduction
inside
the
particle.
However, the ionic conduction path inside the particle
in solid‐phase redox of sulfur with liquid electrolytes has
not been discussed in previous studies.29–33 Therefore,
the ionic conduction path inside the SC composite
particle was first characterized by a solid‐phase redox
reaction of sulfur.

indicates that the SC cathode is covered by a protective

The peak at 290 eV is clearly observed in the C K‐edge

attributed to the carbonyl C=O (CO3) functional group of

Mitigating the severe dissolution
of poly‐VC
After the first cycle, Li–S/VC was disassembled, and
the
separator
was
observed
and
characterized.
Figure 5A shows a photograph of the separator after
the first cycle of the Li–S/VC. The separator turned
yellow on the side facing the sulfur electrode. In the
elemental analysis using EDX, the intensity of the peak
attributable to oxygen increased drastically compared
to that of other elements, such as carbon, fluorine, and
sulfur
(Figure
5B).
Figure
5C
presents
the
FT‐
IR spectra of the separators before and after the first
cycle. New peaks appeared at 764, 972, 1062, 1170,
1250, 1350, 1650, and 1800 cm−1 in the spectrum of
separator after the first cycle, which are attributed to
the C–H of C=C,52 C–O stretching of cyclic carbon-
ate,53 C–O–C in ester,54 C=O of lithium alkyl carbon-
ate,55,56 and C=O of cyclic carbonate,54 respectively.
These results indicate that the poly‐VC that formed
during
the
discharge
process
was
dissolved
and
deposited on the separator. The dissolution of SEI
components, such as lithium alkyl carbonates, has been
revealed in previous studies.57,58 In the SEI formation
mechanism based on molecular dynamics simulations,
the oligomers of SEI components form on the electrode
surface, dissolve in the electrolyte, aggregate near the
electrode
surface,
and
adsorb
onto
the
electrode
surface.59
In
addition,
the
LiF
generated
by
the
decomposition of FEC acts as an adhesive to adsorb
the dissolved SEI oligomers, which results in the
formation of a compact SEI.60 Thus, FEC was mixed
with VC as a cosolvent to mitigate the dissolution of the
VC‐derived oligomers from the cathode.

The rate performances of the Li–S/VC and Li–S/
VC–FEC are shown in Figure 5H. The Li–S/VC exhibited
discharge capacities of 972, 750, 667, 217, 0, and 0 mAh
g−1 at 0.1 C, 1, 2, 5, 7, and 10 C, respectively. In contrast,
the Li–S/VC–FEC demonstrated discharge capacities of
1055, 989, 967, 856, 789, and 578 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 1, 2, 5, 7,

the Li–S/VC–FEC showed a higher discharge capacity
(930 mAh g−1) than that with the VC‐based electrolyte
(872 mAh g−1) and maintained a discharge capacity of
600 mAh g−1 even after 300 cycles (Figure S11). Thus,
owing to its superior cycling and rate capabilities, the
VC–FEC‐based electrolyte was selected for application in
the Li–S pouch cells.

lithium metal. The size of the battery was 8.5 cm
(length) and 11 cm (width). An SC coated on both sides
of metal foil and a lithium metal on Al and Ni foils

seven sheets of SC cathodes and eight sheets of Li
anodes

The
detailed
parameters,
weights, and values of components for the Li–S pouch
cell are summarized in Tables S2, S3, and S4. The
charge–discharge characteristics were evaluated at a
current density rate of 0.1 C. The first and second
charge–discharge curves of the 8 Ah pouch cell are
shown in Figure 5K. The pouch Li–S cell exhibited first
discharge and charge capacities of 1760 and 1351 mAh
g−1, respectively, and the irreversible capacity could be
attributed to the formation of lithiated poly‐VC on the
surface and inside SC particles. The pouch Li–S cell
was discharged with a capacity of 1342 mAh g−1
(8.35 Ah) in the second cycle. The practical Li–S pouch
cell successfully demonstrated an energy density of
401 Wh kg−1 based on the cell weight. These values are
the largest capacity and highest energy density of Li–S
batteries with carbonate‐based electrolytes reported
thus far (Table S2 and Figure 5L). The cycling area
capacity of Li metal is about 12 mAh cm−2, corre-
sponding to a theoretical thickness change of 61 μm
per cell and 0.85 mm in the pouch cell. However, a
total thickness change of the pouch cell was not
observed because the S cathode expands when the Li
anode dissolves during discharge (and vice versa
during charging). The total thickness change was
offset by these factors and was negligible in the first
few cycles. Over the course of the cycles, however, the
cell thickness tended to increase gradually. Our future
study will focus on the capacity decay, thickness
changes, and gas generation of Li–S pouch cells using
carbonate‐based electrolytes for practical use.

CONCLUSION
Aiming to exploit mature materials and technologies, we
focused on carbonate‐electrolyte‐based Li–S batteries
with
a
solid‐phase
conversion
of
sulfur.
Among
carbonate‐based electrolytes, VC‐based electrolytes en-
able the solid‐phase conversion of sulfur, which origi-
nates from the lithiated poly‐VC SEI formed on the
surface of the SC particles. Further, it was revealed that
lithiated poly‐VC formed inside SC particles and acted as
a nanoscale ionic conduction path to sulfur as the origin
of
the
high
sulfur
utilization.
However,
a
severe
dissolution of the poly‐VC oligomer from the SC cathode
was observed, and it resulted in a low capability and
cycle life. VC–FEC‐based electrolytes were investigated
to address these issues. The FEC‐derived LiF absorbed
the poly‐VC‐based oligomer at the interface of the
electrolyte and SC cathode. Therefore, a thin poly‐VC‐
based SEI was formed, enabling high rate performance
and long cycle life. Finally, using the VC‐FEC‐based
electrolyte, the 8‐Ah‐class Li–S pouch cell successfully

METHODS
4.1
|
Preparation of SC composites and
electrolytes
Sulfur (>99%; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.) was
mixed with micromesoporous carbon in a sulfur/carbon
weight ratio of 65:35. The host carbon had a pore size of
<4 nm and a total pore volume of more than 1.5 cm3 g−1.
The SC composites were prepared by sealing the
precursors in a stainless‐steel container and heating
them at 155°C for 6 h in air. For the electrolyte, LiTFSI
(1 M; Nippon Shokubai Co., Ltd.) was added into VC, PC,
DMC, or DME and dissolved by stirring in an Ar‐filled
glove box. The electrolyte for preventing the dissolution
of SEI was prepared by dissolving 1 M LiTFSI in a
mixture of VC and FEC in a 50:50 volume ratio. All
carbonate solvents were purchased from Kishida Chemi-
cal Co., Ltd.
4.2
|
Characterization
The pore structures of the porous carbon were investi-
gated using an N2 adsorption/desorption method with a
fully automatic gas adsorption amount measuring device
(Autosorb® iQ; Anton Paar Japan Co.). The specific
surface area and pore volume distribution were calcu-
lated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method and
quenched solid density functional theory, respectively.
The structure of the sulfur, carbon, and SC composite
was investigated using XRD (Ultima IV; Rigaku Co.) with
Cu Kα radiation as the x‐ray source. The morphologies
and compositions of the SC composites were character-
ized using SEM‐EDX (FlexSEM 1000 II; Hitachi High‐
Technologies Co.). The sulfur content of SC composites
was measured using thermogravimetric analysis (DTG‐
60AH; Shimadzu Co.) at 5°C min−1 under flowing Ar.
The amount of sulfur in the SC cathode of Li–S cells with
various electrolytes during discharging and charging was
measured using XRF (ZSX Primus IV; Rigaku Co.). The
semiquantitative
analysis
fundamental
parameter
method was used for composition analysis in SC
cathodes because the light elements, such as proton
and oxygen, were included. The chemical states of the
SEI were characterized by XPS (PHI Quantes; ULVAC‐
PHI Inc.). Al Kα radiation was used as the x‐ray source.
The accelerating voltage was 40 kV, and the tube current
was 40 mA. To characterize the electronic states of sulfur,
carbon, and oxygen in the SC cathode in Li–S cells during
discharging and charging, XANES analysis was carried
out at the BL1N2 and BL6N1 beamlines of the Aichi
Synchrotron Radiation Center in Aichi, Japan. The x‐ray
absorption spectra were recorded in TEY and PFY modes
at room temperature. The spectral data were processed
using Athena software. The C K‐edge XANES spectra
were simulated using the QMAS computational code for
implementing PAW calculations with a generalized
gradient approximation for the exchange‐correlation
energy function. Furthermore, core–hole effects were
incorporated through a supercell containing the target C
atom with a PAW pseudopotential constructed for an
excited atom with a core hole in the 1s orbital.46 Cross‐
sectional images and elemental maps were obtained
using SEM‐EDX (JSM‐IT800; JEOL). The cross sections
of the SC cathode were prepared using a cryo‐Ar beam
cross‐sectional polisher (IB‐19520CCP; JEOL). Nanoscale
cross‐sectional
images
and
elemental
maps
were
obtained using STEM (JEM‐ARM200F; JEOL) and EELS
(GIF Quantum ER; Gatan Inc.). Cross sections of the SC
cathodes were prepared for STEM‐EELS at −140°C using
a cryo‐focused ion beam (VERSA 3D; FEI Co.). FT‐IR
spectroscopy (ALPHA II; Bruker) was performed in an
attenuated total reflectance mode to analyze the decom-
position of the separator. Sulfur cathodes were extracted
from the Li–S cells discharged and charged at voltages of
1.7, 1.5, 0.5, 2.3, 2.5, and 3.0 V to characterize sulfur
cathodes during discharging and charging. The sulfur
cathodes were washed with DMC (50 mL) three times
and dried under vacuum for more than 5 h before
characterization.

Fabrication and electrochemical
measurements of 8 Ah Li–S batteries
Double‐coated cathodes composed of an SC composite on
aluminum were cut into 7 × 9 cm2 pieces and dried at
70°C under vacuum. An Al foil and an Al tab were
welded together using a welding machine. Double‐coated
lithium anodes on copper were cut into 7.5–9.5 cm2 and
welded together with a Ni tab. Dry cells with 10 cathodes
and 11 anodes were assembled using a propylene
separator. The Al plastic film was cut into 15 × 6 cm2.
The two edges of the Al plastic were sealed using a hot
pressure machine. The dry cells were then placed in an
Al package to assemble a dry pouch cell. The LiTFSI
VC–FEC electrolyte (10 mL, 1 m) was added to the dry
pouch cell, which was finally sealed under vacuum.
