A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur batteries offer the highest theoretical energy density among enclosed rechargeable batteries. 
However, there are various issues that need to be addressed before their practical adoption, such as the disso­
lution of intermediate lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn) into the electrolyte solution, which causes rapid degradation of 
the battery. Herein, we used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and in-situ magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 
to visualize the dissolution of the intermediate species formed during the discharge–charge process in Li-S 
batteries. A strong enhancement in the MRI signal was observed when the cell was first discharged, which is 
associated with the dissolution of intermediate polysulfides. To determine the origin of this signal enhancement, 
1H NMR spectra, T1 relaxation time, and electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements were performed on 
electrolyte containing polysulfides. The origin of the strong enhancement of the MRI signal by the dissoluted 
polysulfides is mainly attributed to the paramagnetic interaction due to the formation of S3
*- and S4
*- radicals in the 
dissoluted polysulfides. The results demonstrate that 1H MRI is an indirect and effective way to study and 
visualize the intermediate species formed during Li-S battery operation.   
1. Introduction 
Electrochemical energy storage devices, such as batteries and 
supercapacitors, are vital for decreasing fossil fuel consumption and 
developing a low-carbon and sustainable society. Lithium-ion chemistry 
has accelerated the use of batteries in stationary power storage, portable 
electronics, and electric vehicles owing to the high gravimetric and 
volumetric capacities of such batteries. However, the ever-increasing 
demand and necessity to increase battery performance requires the 
development of advanced energy storage systems that are better than 
current generation lithium-ion batteries [1]. 
Among the candidates being investigated, batteries based on lithium- 
sulfur (Li-S) chemistry are promising owing to their high theoretical 
energy density (1675 mAh/g), which is approximately-four times that of 
Li-ion batteries [2]. Moreover, the high abundance and low toxicity of 
sulfur favors the development of Li-S batteries [3]. An ether-based sol­
vent (i.e., 1, 3 dioxolane (DOL), dimethoxy-ethane (DME), or tetra­
ethylene glycol dimethyl ether (tetraglyme or G4)) with a lithium salt, 
for example, lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) dis­
solved in it, is usually used as electrolyte in lithium-sulfur batteries, 
while sulfur is employed as the cathode [4]. In Li-S batteries, the solvent 
in the electrolyte plays a key role in the electrochemical performance, 
whereas no significant effects are observed for lithium salts [5]. 
Lithium-sulfur battery chemistry is a complex mechanism in which 
sulfur is reduced to form various polysulfides with lithium during the 
discharge and charge cycles. The electrochemical reduction of sulfur to 
form soluble lithium polysulfides as intermediate species is a critical 
factor in reaping the high energy stored in the system [6–8]. Conversely, 
these intermediate long-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2S4 to Li2S8) tend 
to dissolve/elute into electrolytes, especially in ether-based electrolytes, 
resulting in low coulombic efficiency during cycling and rapid capacity 
fading. The polysulfide intermediates dissoluted into the electrolyte 
cause a continuous loss of active materials at the cathode [9,10]. To 
achieve the maximum capacity from Li/S cells and to address the 
dissolution problem, it is necessary to understand the dissolution 
mechanism by determining the starting point of the reaction, especially 
focusing on the positive electrode. Furthermore, it is important to derive 
an overall picture of the electrochemical reaction occurring at the 
electrode–electrolyte interface. 
The chemical kinetics involved during battery operation, that is, the 
formation of polysulfides at particular potentials and their contribution 
to cell performance, has long been studied [11,12]. Different in-situ

spectroscopic techniques, including X-ray diffraction [13,14], X-ray 
absorption [4,11], UV/Vis [15], infrared [16,17], Raman [18,19], and 
electron spin resonance (ESR) [20] spectroscopy, have been employed to 
monitor and identify the electrochemical reactions in Li-S battery sys­
tems. However, the identification of the exact polysulfide species formed 
during the discharge process through various operando characterization 
techniques has been reported to be very difficult [11,12]. 
Alternatively, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is a 
suitable technique to investigate the intermediate species in Li-S battery 
systems because both lithium (6,7Li) and sulfur (33S) are NMR-active 
nuclei. Several studies have used 7Li NMR to identify the reaction 
mechanism and discharge products in Li-S systems [6,21–25]. More­
over, the identification of intermediate polysulfides using in-situ 7Li 
NMR is challenging because the reduction of cyclic S8 into polysulfides 
does not occur in a discrete step-by-step manner [6] and mixed species 
are present at any stage. On the contrary, direct measurement of the 
polysulfide species using 33S NMR is restricted because of the low nat­
ural abundance (0.75 %) and quadrupolar nature of the 33S nucleus (I =
3/2), resulting in longer acquisition times and broader signals, partic­
ularly in non-symmetric polysulfides. 
In addition to using 7Li and 33S NMR to directly study the interme­
diate species, 1H and 7Li longitudinal (T1) and transverse (T2) relaxation 
time measurements of the solvent molecules can be used to identify the 
dissoluted polysulfide species in the electrolyte, as they are sensitive to 
the presence of different dissoluted species [25–27]. In addition, ex-situ 
7Li T2 relaxation time measurements on sulfur cathodes discharged at 
different voltages showed a decrease with respect to the intermediate 
discharge products [25]. This decrease in relaxation time can be used to 
measure magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and to study the spatial 
distribution of the intermediate products. In recent years, MRI has 
proven to be an efficient way to non-invasively study the spatial distri­
bution of lithium ions and to investigate dendrite growth in lithium 
batteries [28,29]. Moreover, MRI can provide detailed information 
about the chemical species, cell component behavior, and failure 
mechanism in a non-invasive manner, which is crucial for understanding 
fundamental electrochemical processes [30,31]. 
However, the direct visualization of the intermediate lithium- 
containing species formed during cell operation using 7Li MRI is 
limited owing to the rapid disproportionation of the species and low 
sensitivity of Li, thereby requiring a longer acquisition time, which 
hinders the visualization of the intermediate species. On the contrary, 
owing to its high abundance and sensitivity, 1H MRI can be utilized to 
investigate the formation of intermediate species in Li-S cells. 
Besides, 1H MRI has been used in secondary battery systems to study 
the corrosion/dissolution of metal ions from electrodes into the elec­
trolyte [32–36]. In our earlier work, we showed that 1H MRI is an 
effective technique to indirectly visualize manganese dissolution from a 
LiMn2O4 cathode material in carbonate-based electrolytes [37]. 1H MRI 
takes advantage of the change in the T1 and T2 relaxation times of the 
solvent molecules in the presence of dissoluted species owing to the 
change in the coordination structure and magnetic properties of the 
dissoluted species. Taking advantage of the solubility of polysulfides in 
the electrolyte, we demonstrate for the first time that in-situ 1H magnetic 
resonance imaging technique can be used efficiently to visualize the 
intermediate polysulfide species in a Li-S cell and to understand the 
reaction mechanism. 
2. Experimental 
To identify the origin of the effect of intermediate polysulfide species 
on the electrolyte, 1H and 7Li NMR and T1 relaxation measurements 
were performed. The electrolyte was prepared by mixing stoichiometric 
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonylimide) (LiTFSI) in tetraglyme and 
1:1 vol% dioxolane: dimethoxyethane (DOL:DME) (Wako Chemicals, 
Japan) mixtures separately inside a glove box and it was subsequently 
used for the measurements. Stoichiometric lithium polysulfides 1 M 
Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4 were prepared chemically by mixing Li2S and S in 
tetraglyme or DOL:DME and added to the electrolyte. 
Ex-situ 1H and 7Li NMR and T1 relaxation measurements were made 
using Bruker Avance 400 NMR spectrometer equipped with a diff60 
probe. The standard one-pulse sequence and inversion recovery pulse 
sequence were used for NMR and relaxation time measurements. 2 vol% 
Tetramethylsilane (TMS) in CDCl3 and 1 M lithium chloride (LiCl) in 
D2O solution were used as external standard for referencing 1H and 7Li 
NMR chemical shift (δiso = 0.0 ppm). For the in-situ MRI measurements, 
a Li-S cell was constructed using 0.13 mm thick Li metal and a composite 
sulfur electrode composed of acetylene black and polyvinylidene fluo­
ride (PVdF) in a mass ratio of 8:2:1 coated on a carbon-coated aluminum 
sheet as anode and cathode, respectively. In-situ 1H MRI measurements 
were carried out using Bruker Avance 400 NMR spectrometer equipped 
with 1H mini imaging probe with a triple axis gradient. A multi-spin 
multi-echo pulse sequence was used to acquire two dimensional 1H 
MRI images. The experimental parameters (TR and TE) were chosen 
based on the corresponding relaxation times (T1 and T2) of the elec­
trolyte under study and were fixed at 500 and 10.5 ms for TR and TE, 
respectively. The 1H MRI images were acquired using an average of four 
scans, and the total acquisition time was approximately 5 min. For the 
in-situ measurements, we used a homemade cell, as shown in Fig. 1. The 
distance between the two electrodes is 5 mm and the active area of the 
electrodes is 0.1925 cm2. The initial 1H MRI image acquired using this 
cell is shown in Fig. 1. In-situ MRI measurements were performed during 
galvanostatic discharge–charge measurements at a rate of ± 64 µA using 
Toho Giken PS-08 potentiostat/galvanostat equipment in the potential 
range 2.6–1.3 V. ImageJ software was used to measure the intensity 
profile from the acquired images. 
To investigate the origin of the relaxation mechanism, electron spin 
resonance (ESR) measurements were performed on the prepared stoi­
chiometric lithium polysulfides using JEOL X330 ESR spectrometer with 
an operating frequency in the range of 8.75–9.65 GHz. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1.
1H NMR 
The solvation structure of the electrolyte in the presence of poly­
sulfides was studied using ex-situ 1H and 7Li NMR (Figs. 2–3). The 1H 
NMR results of 1 M LiTFSI in tetraglyme and DOL:DME electrolytes in 
the presence of stoichiometric lithium polysulfides Li2Sn (n = 8,6,4) are 
shown in Fig. 2a–b. The 1H NMR signals observed close to 3.5, 3.7, and 
3.8 ppm in Fig. 2a are assigned to protons in the end-chain methyl (6H), 
ethyl group near the end chain (4H), and the ethyl groups in the inner 
chains (12H) of tetraglyme, respectively. In the presence of polysulfides, 
the peak corresponding to the methyl and ethyl protons at the terminal 
of tetraglyme shifts toward lower frequencies (higher ppm). On the 
contrary, a slight or no shift was observed in the peaks corresponding to 
the ethyl protons located in the inner chain of the tetraglyme. It is well 
known that in LiTFSI tetraglyme mixtures, Li ions are solvated by the 
oxygen atoms of tetraglyme to form a cage structure [38,39]. Therefore, 
in the presence of polysulfides, the ion–dipole interactions between 
polysulfides and tetraglyme molecules are stronger in the terminal 
protons. As a result, a larger shift is observed for the end-chain methyl 
protons in the 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 2a and Table 1). 
1H NMR spectra of 1 M LiTFSI DOL:DME electrolyte in the presence 
of lithium polysulfides are shown in Fig. 2b. The NMR signals observed 
close to 4.0 (4H), 5.0 (2H) 3.6 (6H), and 3.75 (4H) ppm are assigned to 
the protons from the DOL and DME moieties, respectively. From Fig. 2b, 
a shift toward lower frequencies is observed for the peak associated with 
the DOL molecule in the presence of polysulfides. However, there is 
almost no shift in the peaks associated with DME moieties. This indicates 
that the DOL molecules are mainly affected by the ion–dipole in­
teractions with the polysulfides. This result is well supported by earlier 
reports based on NMR and molecular modeling studies on DOL:DME,

3.2.
7Li NMR 
The 7Li NMR spectra of 1 M LiTFSI in tetraglyme and DOL:DME 
electrolytes in the presence of stoichiometric polysulfides are shown in 
Fig. 3a and 3b. 7Li NMR peak was observed at negative ppm (higher 
frequencies) in both the electrolytes, indicating strong electron shielding 
around the Li nuclei. The appearance of a single narrow peak indicates 
that the Li ions experienced a similar environment in all the samples. 
The 7Li NMR signals became broader with decreasing polysulfide chain 
length owing to the increase in the viscosity of the electrolyte. Similar to 
the proton NMR results, the NMR signal shifted to lower frequencies in 
the presence of polysulfides. The shift increases in the order Li2S4 >
Li2S6 > Li2S8 in both solvent systems [21], which is attributed to an 
increased deshielding effect. The observed 7Li NMR chemical shifts in 
the tetraglyme and DOL:DME electrolytes are listed in Tables 1 and 2.

of lithium polysulfides. 1H T1 relaxation time for the tetraglyme-based

ysulfides (Li2S8) and increase with a decrease in the polysulfide chain

in ether-based solvents and exist as anions (e.g., S8
2- and S6
2-). In the 
polysulfide anion S8
2-, the negative charge density is highly delocalized

2- 
anion and protons of the tetraglyme molecules, the T1 relaxation time 
decreases. With decreasing chain length, the negative charge density on 
the terminal sulfur atoms becomes higher, and the dipolar interaction 
decreases. Therefore, the T1 relaxation time increases with decreasing 
polysulfide chain length. Similar results were observed for 7Li T1

with the intermediate species formed during the cycling process. 
In the discharge–charge profile, 12 different potentials (indicated by 
alphabet symbols in Fig. 4a) were chosen, where intermediate poly­

(D)) near the cathode. Upon further discharging of the cell, the MRI 
signal observed near the cathode decreased gradually and disappeared

was observed near the cathode (Fig. 4b (J)). As the charging process 
continued, the MRI signal observed near the cathode gradually 
decreased and disappeared (Fig. 4b (K-L)). [A video of the complete

The calculated total MRI signal intensity includes the contribution 
from the electrochemical convection effect observed near the Li metal

The dissolution of polysulfides into the electrolyte occurs in two stages. 
Stages 1 and 2 are indicated by the light-yellow and purple areas in 
Fig. 5c. In stage 1, a gradual increase in the MRI signal intensity was 
observed (plateau region, light-yellow area in Fig. 5c). During discharge, 
the electrochemical reduction of sulfur is accompanied by the formation 
of long-chain polysulfides. These polysulfides get dissoluted into the 
electrolyte because of their high solubility in ether-based solvents [42]. 
The T1 relaxation of the protons was confirmed to decrease in the 
presence of long-chain polysulfides from 1H NMR and T1 relaxation time 
measurements. This decrease in the T1 relaxation time of the protons is 
reflected as an increase in the MRI signal intensity. 
In the second stage of discharge (area marked in purple), a sudden 
increase was observed in the MRI signal, which decreased before 
reaching 2.30 V. In this region (2.42 ~ 2.35 V), long-chain polysulfide 
(S8
2-) is disproportionated to short-chain polysulfide (S6
2-). These S6
2- ions 
are in equilibrium with the S3
•- radicals, which possess a strong electron 
magnetic moment. Owing to the paramagnetic interactions between 
these radicals and the protons of tetraglyme, a sharp increase in the MRI 
signal was observed at approximately 2.38 V. As the discharge pro­
ceeded further below 2.35 V, the MRI signal intensity decreased. In this 
region (<2.35 V), the long-chain polysulfide is disproportionated to 
short-chain polysulfide of chain length Li2Sn (n < 4). In short-chain 
polysulfides (e.g., S4
2-and S2
2-), the negative charge is higher in the ter­
minal sulfur atom, and there is a strong electrostatic force between Li+
and Sn
2- (n < 4), leading to their poor solubility [42,43]. Therefore, the T1 
relaxation time is not significantly affected, which is reflected as a 
decrease in the MRI signal intensity. Beyond 2.3 V, there is no change in 
the MRI signal intensity near the cathode region. This result correlates 
well with our T1 relaxation time measurement results, where T1 was 
found to increase in the presence of short-chain polysulfides.

results were reported by He et al., who confirmed the ~ 4 e- transfer 
process in 1 M LiTFSI DOL:DME mixtures using rotating disk electro­
metric measurements [42]. However, the exact reason for the higher 
electron transfer is yet to be clearly understood. 
Beyond 2.35 V, the MRI intensity calculated near the cathode region 
is observed to decrease throughout the discharge cycle where short- 
chain polysulfides are formed. With decreasing chain length of the 
polysulfide molecules, the negative charge is strong on the terminal 
sulfur and the polysulfides exist as Li2Sn, leading to the formation of a 
mixed cluster of DOL/Li2Sn [24]. The formation of these mixed clusters 
in the DOL:DME system with lithium polysulfides could be the reason for 
the observed increase in T1 relaxation time and, therefore, a decrease in 
the MRI signal intensity. These results are in good agreement with our T1 
relaxation time measurement results, where an increase in relaxation 
time was observed in the presence of short-chain polysulfides.

Based on the above results, it can be concluded that the intermediate 
polysulfide reaction species in lithium-sulfur batteries can be indirectly 
visualized using 1H MRI. The observed change in MRI intensity is suf­
ficiently accurate to validate the reaction mechanism in Li-S batteries. 
Even though a different behavior is observed in the DOL:DME system, 
this result suggests that 1H MRI is a useful technique to study the in­
termediate reaction species in electrolytes. 
4. Conclusion 
Herein, 1H magnetic resonance imaging was used for the first time to 
visualize the intermediate reaction species in a Li-S battery. 1 M LiTFSI 
in tetraglyme and DOL:DME were used as the electrolytes. The shift in 
1H NMR spectra of 1 M LiTFSI in tetraglyme with different polysulfides 
established that the primary location of coordination of the polysulfides 
is the end-chain methyl group of tetraglyme. For 1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME 
with different polysulfides, the shift in 1H NMR spectra confirmed that 
the interaction between DOL and polysulfides is predominant. The T1 
relaxation time decreased in the presence of long-chain polysulfides and 
increased with decreasing polysulfide chain length. During the in-situ 
measurement of MRI of the Li-S battery, a strong enhancement in 1H 
MRI signal was observed in the discharge process, which was accom­
panied by dissoluted long-chain polysulfides. The formation of poly­
sulfide radicals during the disproportionation of long-chain polysulfides 
was confirmed by the strong increase in the MRI signal intensity, which 
is due to the paramagnetic interaction between the polysulfide radicals 
and protons in the electrolyte. Further, this work is the first to report the 
observation of the of sulfur radical participation using NMR/MRI, which 
until now has been presumed to be reason for the unexplainable shift in 
the NMR signals. Based on these results, we have demonstrated that 1H 
MRI is an indirect and effective way to study and visualize the inter­
mediate species formed during Li-S battery operation.
