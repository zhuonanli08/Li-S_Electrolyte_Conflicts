ABSTRACT: Although Li−S batteries offer significant promise as
replacements for conventional lithium-ion batteries in terms of
sustainability and performance, their practical implementation
remains challenging. Designing cathodes with high sulfur loading
(≥5 mg/cm2) under lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ≤5 μL/mg)
presents specific obstacles, including sluggish reaction kinetics,
poor sulfur utilization, and increased interfacial resistance, often
driven by the saturation of lithium polysulfide species in the
electrolyte. To overcome these issues, the development of solvents
and additives that can enhance sulfur utilization/dissolution while
mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect has become crucial. To
address this, we introduce a boron-based anion receptor,
tris(trimethyl)silyl borate (TMSB), as an electrolyte additive for Li−S batteries to improve the dissolution of polysulfide species
in the electrolyte. Due to its Lewis-acid ability, TMSB was found to dissolve more anionic polysulfide species (Lewis bases) in DOL/
DME-based electrolytes. Surface analysis of cycled cathodes revealed that TMSB also aids in scavenging decomposition products at
the cathode surface by coordinating with anions, including sulfates and thiosulfates. At high-loading (5 mg/cm2) and lean electrolyte
(E/S = 8 μL/mg) conditions, results show that 5 wt % TMSB in the electrolyte reduced the overpotential by 850 mV. Further, the
addition of TMSB also enabled stable cycling at a 0.2C rate for 100 cycles compared to the intermittent cycling in the blank
electrolyte. Hence, this study shows that TMSB as an electrolyte additive helps in improving sulfur utilization and reducing
interfacial resistance for high-loading lithium−sulfur batteries under lean electrolyte conditions.
KEYWORDS: anion receptor, electrolyte, high-sulfur-loading, E/S ratio, polysulfide dissolution
■INTRODUCTION
There is a critical requirement for next-generation high-energy
battery systems beyond lithium-ion batteries, and one of the
most promising and potential candidates is lithium−sulfur
(Li−S) batteries owing to their high theoretical capacity of
1675 mAh/g.1,2 Li−S batteries can provide a high theoretical
energy density of 2600 kWh/kg, which is over ten times higher
compared to the maximum energy density threshold of
conventional lithium-ion batteries (240 kWh/kg).3−5 How-
ever, Li−S batteries suffer from several shortcomings that
hinder them from achieving their maximum potential.6,7 The
low electronic conductivity of sulfur and the end products of
discharge, Li2S/Li2S2, inhibit the complete utilization of the
active materials, which yields decreased capacity from the Li−S
batteries.8,9 The other major challenge is caused by the
shuttling of lithium polysulfides, which are intermediates of the
charge−discharge process and dissolve in the electrolyte,
leading to the loss of active material by involving themselves in
parasitic reactions at the lithium anode.10 Apart from these, the
lithium anode undergoes dendrite propagation, and there exist
electrolyte compatibility issues with the lithium anode and
polysulfide species.11 Various design strategies have been
implemented to overcome the above issues, such as using a
high percentage of functional carbon materials12,13 in the
cathode to increase the conductivity of the cathode, electro-
catalysts that boost the electrochemical reaction kinetics and
retrieve the lithium polysulfides within the cathode to prevent
the shuttling mechanism,14−16 and electrolyte formulations
that form a passive solid-electrolyte interface (SEI) at the
lithium anode to suppress dendrites and unwanted reac-
tions.17,18 These solutions to the issues mentioned above
greatly improve the performance of Li−S batteries, but most of
these strategies have been implemented under ideal conditions
of low sulfur loading and electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S ratio),
which without doubt results in low energy density of Li−S
batteries as opposed to their theoretical numbers. E/S ratios
above 10 μL/mg constitute over 50 wt % of the whole cell,

heavily affecting the practical energy density of Li−S batteries,
which operate at a low average discharge voltage compared to
that of lithium-ion batteries.19 Hence, achieving a low E/S
ratio and high sulfur loading are two major requirements for
the practical application of Li−S batteries. The reduction of
the E/S ratio and increase of sulfur loading bring forth a
significant problem of sluggish redox kinetics of the Li−S
electrochemical system resulting from high viscosity caused by
increased dissolution of polysulfides in the electrolyte.20 This,
in turn, leads to exacerbated polarization of the cell, which
ends in under-utilization of sulfur, thus making it a problem
that needs to be specifically addressed for the practical
application of Li−S batteries.19
Anion receptors (ARs) are electron acceptors or Lewis acids
that constitute an acid center that has the ability to specifically
complex by accepting an electron pair from other molecules or
anions.21,22 Several boron-based ARs have been employed as
electrolyte additives for Li-ion batteries to improve the ionic
conductivity of electrolytes by lowering the ion interaction
between Li+ ions and the anions using ARs. In boron-based
ARs, boron serves as the acidic center, which is bonded to
three alkyl or alkoxyl groups (σ bonds) and contains an empty
π orbital, ready to accept electrons to form a π−π coordination
bond with a Lewis base.23,24 The formation of the π−π bond
delocalizes the negative charge of the anion to the AR,
decreasing the negative charge density on the anion, and as a
result, the ion interaction between the Li+ ion and anion
decreases.25 This facilitates higher ionic conductivity and
results in an increased lithium-ion transference number in the
electrolytes.26 These anion receptors have been used in
lithium-ion batteries as additives to dissolve insoluble inorganic
SEI components by solvating anions like F−, OH−, and O2−to
alleviate the impedance contributed by the large quantities of
these compounds and to retain better capacity over
cycling.27−32 In Li−S batteries, in addition to the anions
from the lithium salts, the intermediate polysulfides exist as
anions, which are considered soft Lewis bases in terms of hard
and soft acids and bases (HSAB) theory.33 In high-loading and
low E/S ratio conditions, the concentration of polysulfides in
the electrolyte becomes saturated, and there is a need to
improve the redox kinetics and sulfur utilization by promoting
the dissolution of more polysulfides into the electrolyte.
Herein, we use an AR as an electrolyte additive for lithium−
sulfur batteries in an attempt to achieve better sulfur utilization
and decrease the polarization of high-sulfur-loading cells by
employing an AR to dissolve anion species in the electrolyte
and the decomposition products on the electrode−electrolyte
interface. In this work, we explore the effect on polysulfide
species solubility and stability from the addition of a boron-
based AR, tris(trimethyl)silyl borate (TMSB), by using UV−
visible spectrophotometry and nuclear magnetic resonance
(NMR) spectroscopy. The performance of the electrolytes
with AR was also investigated for lithium−sulfur batteries with
low and high sulfur loading at different E/S ratios. The cycled
electrodes were studied by using X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) to determine the effect of the AR on the
cathode-electrolyte interface (CEI).
■EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
1.5 M Li2S6 (1.5 M) was used for absorption and NMR studies
and was prepared by adding stoichiometric amounts of S8 and
Li2S in 1:1 v/v 1,3-dioxolane and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL/
DME) by constant stirring overnight at 60 °C. All the
chemicals for the lithium polysulfide preparation were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. A Jasco V-770 UV−vis-NIR
Spectrophotometer was used to perform absorption studies on
the addition of TMSB to the lithium polysulfide solution. 5 wt
% TMSB was added to 5 mL of the Li2S6 solution and was
constantly stirred overnight. The solutions were allowed to rest
for 3 h. After the rest time, 0.5 mL of the supernatant solution
of each sample was removed and diluted in the ratio of 1:10
with 1:1 v/v DOL/DME. The samples were added to the
cuvettes and sealed inside an Ar glovebox. Lithium salt was not
added to the samples to avoid interference. Li7 NMR
spectroscopic studies were performed using a Varian 400
MHz NMR spectrometer. 10 μL of the supernatant solution of
each sample was added to vials and diluted with deuterated-
dimethyl sulfoxide (d-DMSO) in the volume ratio of 1:8. The
prepared solutions were collected in NMR tubes, and the tubes
were sealed with a polyethylene cap inside the glovebox.
The blank electrolyte was prepared by adding 1 M lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.2 M
lithium nitrate (LiNO3) to 1:1 v/v DOL/DME with constant
stirring overnight. Two different electrolyte formulations were
prepared by adding 2 and 5 wt/vol % of TMSB to the blank
electrolyte, and these were allowed to stir constantly overnight.
Solid sulfur cathode slurry was prepared by grinding carbon/
sulfur composite (purchased from MSE, 75 wt % S),
conductive carbon (C65), and polyvinylidene fluoride
(PVDF) binder in the weight ratio of 90:4:6, respectively,
using a mortar and pestle. The slurry was then coated onto a
carbon-coated Al foil using a doctor’s blade at different
thicknesses. The areal sulfur loadings used in this study were
∼1 mg/cm2 and ∼5 mg/cm2 for the low- and high-loading
cathodes, respectively. The coin cells for all electrochemical
studies were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox with the
fabricated sulfur cathode and lithium metal as the anode. The
electrolyte quantity in the low-sulfur-loading cells was 20 μL.
Varying E/S ratios were used for high-sulfur-loading cells and
are mentioned in the main text.
A Biologic SP300 bipotentiostat was used to cycle the cell at
a scan rate of 0.2 mV/s in the range of 1.7 to 3 V for cyclic
voltammetry studies. An Arbin cycle life tester was used to
perform galvanostatic charge−discharge between 1.7 and 3.0
V, unless or otherwise the voltage range is specified. The X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on cycled
cathodes at charged and discharged conditions using a Nexsa
G2 surface analysis system. The XPS samples were prepared by
rinsing the cycled electrodes with DME solvent before
transferring them to the instrument using a vacuum transfer
module in the glovebox with no exposure to the ambient
atmosphere.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
TMSB has been widely used as an electrolyte additive in Li-ion
batteries to improve performance by modifying the CEI with
B- and Si-based compounds that form a thinner and more
stable CEI.21,22,34 Moreover, TMSB aids in scavenging
parasitic molecules like LiF and HF from the cathode surface
and the electrolyte, respectively, to provide better capacity
retention, and TMSB does so by complexing with anions to
dissolve them in the electrolyte. The polysulfides in Li−S
batteries are dianions, and the higher polysulfides readily
dissolve in the electrolyte. However, in the case of high sulfur
loading and E/S ratio, the concentration of polysulfides in the
electrolyte is saturated, as mentioned earlier, hindering

uniform, and this is mainly due to the presence of different
polysulfide species that exist in the solution as a result of
increased lithium polysulfide solubility on the addition of

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed to study the effect of
the TMSB on the redox activity of the Li−S system. One of the
main reasons for using TMSB can be owed to its high
electrochemical stability and its ability to increase the energy of
the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the
electrolyte, stabilizing the cathode in Li-ion batteries. However,
Li−S batteries operate at a lower voltage range compared to
Li-ion batteries, and the cell undergoing CV was monitored for
any degradation at lower voltages. Figure 2a shows the cyclic
voltammogram of lithium−sulfur cells with lithium as the
anode and low-loading carbon/sulfur as the cathode in the first
and second cycles. The cathodes containing precisely the same
loading were used for cyclic voltammetry experiments to
evaluate the resulting current peaks. No excess current was
noted for the cell containing TMSB at lower voltages, and
hence, it is assumed that TMSB does not undergo any
degradation process at the cathode in the defined voltage
range. The cells have two reduction peaks during the first cycle
at ∼2.3 and ∼2.0 V, corresponding to the conversion of long-
chain polysulfides to short-chain polysulfides and the reduction
of short-chain polysulfides to Li2S, respectively.39 The
oxidation peak at ∼2.36 V represents the oxidation of Li2S
to short-chained polysulfides,40 followed by the conversion of
polysulfides to S8 at ∼2.4 V. The overall current during the first
cycle appears higher for the cell tested with the TMSB additive
compared to the blank cell, which shows that the TMSB
additive facilitates increased sulfur utilization. The second
reduction peak for the cell containing TMSB has a minor
negative shift compared to that of the blank cell. This shows
that the energy required for reduction in the case of the cell
containing TMSB is lesser, which likely results from changes in
the thermodynamics and kinetics of the redox reactions. The
onset of the second current peak during the negative sweep for
both the cells occurs at the same voltage, showing that the
negative peak shift of the TMSB-containing cell takes place
majorly due to kinetic effects, such as reaction kinetics,
diffusion of the redox species, or interfacial properties, rather
than solely thermodynamics. Further, it was noted that the
current in the second oxidation peak for the cell containing
TMSB increases in the second cycle, with the overall current
being lower than that in the first cycle. This indicates the
propagation of the polysulfide shuttle mechanism in the cell
containing TMSB, which is an expected outcome of the
increased polysulfide dissolution in the electrolyte. The
decrease in overall current in the second cycle in the presence
of TMSB can be the result of the loss of active material due to
the shuttling of polysulfides. This proves that the addition of
TMSB to the electrolyte improves the dissolution of
polysulfides and the utilization of sulfur.
To further ensure that the shuttling effect increases due to
the effect of TMSB in the electrolyte, galvanostatic charge−
discharge studies were carried out with two different TMSB
additive concentrations and blank electrolytes. Figure 2d−f
shows the voltage profiles of the first five cycles of low sulfur-
loading Li−S cells containing electrolyte with no AR, 2 wt %
TMSB, and 5 wt % TMSB in the E/S ratio of 20 μL/mg cycled
at a rate of 0.1C. The variation in the first discharge profile of
the cell with 2 wt % TMSB likely occurred due to factors such
as minor changes in electrode wetting or initial testing. The
cells shown in Figure 2b,f were fabricated and tested together
to avoid any variations. The 2 wt % TMSB cell was fabricated
and tested in the same conditions to ensure reproducibility of
the performance trends. It was noted that the cells containing
TMSB had a higher capacity contribution from the second
charge plateau in the consecutive cycles compared to the blank
cell. The charge capacity increased with the addition of TMSB,
indicating the progression of a drastic shuttle effect as a
consequence of higher polysulfide dissolution in the electrolyte
resulting from the effect of TMSB addition. It was also
observed that the cell with 5 wt % TMSB underwent increased
capacity fade and experienced lower Coulombic efficiency
throughout cycling in comparison to the other two cells, which
indicates the consequence of increased polysulfide shuttling
due to more polysulfide dissolution in the cell with 5 wt %
TMSB. While the cell with 2 wt % TMSB reached a higher
specific capacity than the blank cell in the initial cycles, the
capacity fading experienced by the former was higher
compared to the latter due to the effect of shuttling. Hence,
from Figure 2b,c, we can see that the higher the concentration
of TMSB in the electrolyte, the higher the capacity fade and
the lower the Coulombic efficiency of the Li−S cell. Although
no major changes were observed in the discharge plateaus,
additional detail was observed at the end of the discharge and
the start of the charge. The tail at the end of discharge signifies
the nucleation of Li2S at the cathode, and the overvoltage at
the start of charge marks the voltage barrier for the conversion
of Li2S to liquid lithium polysulfides. These two details
gradually faded with the increase in TMSB concentration,
putting forth questions regarding the formation and the state of
the end product of discharge, solid Li2S. Through the voltage
profiles and CV, we know that the presence of TMSB did not
influence different reaction pathways but promoted the
dissolution of lithium polysulfide species, proving our initial
findings with NMR spectroscopy and UV−vis spectropho-
tometry. However, from the minor changes, it is a question of
whether Li2S forms as a solid or liquid, and how the conversion
from and to Li2S does not include any potential barriers.
To investigate the effect of TMSB on the cathode-electrolyte
interface (CEI) and the disappearance of the Li2S voltage
barriers in the presence of TMSB, X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) was performed for cathodes cycled with
blank electrolyte and electrolyte containing 2 wt % TMSB at
two different states: the end of third discharge and the 10th
discharge. Initial cycles were chosen for the XPS studies to
avoid the influence of shuttling effect on the CEI composition
of the cell containing TMSB and 2 wt % TMSB was chosen for
the XPS studies due to the lower shuttling effect experienced
by 2 wt % TMSB in comparison to 5 wt % TMSB. S 2p XPS
spectra for the cathode samples are shown in Figure 3. Across
all S 2p XPS spectra, the splitting of the S 2p signal into two
components, S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, with a 1.2 eV gap and a 2:1
intensity ratio arises from the spin−orbit coupling. The S 2p
peaks from 166 to 159 eV for the sample at the end of third
discharge containing TMSB were deconvoluted into three S
2p3/2 peaks at 163, 161.5, and 160.5 eV corresponding to
polysulfides (Sx
2−), Li2S2 and Li2S, respectively, as shown in
Figure 3b.41 This indicates that despite the changes observed
at the end of discharge during cycling as in Figure 2e, Li2S is
present on the cathode surface as a solid as the washing of the
cathodes before the XPS analysis would have removed any Li2S
on the cathode surface if it was in a liquid state. The other
samples, even in the absence of TMSB, had all three peaks
except the blank sample at the end of the 10th discharge,
showing no signs of Li2S. All the cells show the signatures of
sulfate (SO4
2−, S 2p3/2 at 169 eV) and thiosulfate (S2O3
2−, S

In conditions of high sulfur-loading and low E/S ratios,
lithium−sulfur cells undergo sulfur under-utilization and high
polarization as the dissolution of lithium polysulfides in the
electrolyte is limited, which in turn affects the redox kinetics of
the Li−S system. There is a need to dissolve more polysulfides
in the electrolyte under these conditions for improving the
sulfur utilization, and ARs like TMSB are potential candidates
as electrolyte additives for enhancing polysulfide solubility in
lean electrolyte conditions by lowering the interfacial
resistance. Galvanostatic charge−discharge was performed in
high sulfur-loading and lean electrolyte conditions with TMSB
as an electrolyte additive to understand the effect of the AR
more effectively in dissolving negatively charged species. Figure

mg cycled at a rate of 0.05C with and without TMSB in the
range of 1.7−3.0 V. The blank cell experienced high
polarization after the first plateau, and the cell barely reached
the second plateau in the first discharge. However, the cell
polarization increased in the second discharge, causing the cell
to cut off at 1.7 V after the voltage dip following the first

section. Since the end-product of the second discharge was not
Li2S, the second charge only involved the oxidation of the
intermediate products back to sulfur, resulting in suppressed
specific capacity values. The cell containing 2 wt % TMSB had
lower cell polarization in the same conditions compared to the
blank cell, and the polarization after the first plateau decreased
further in the second discharge due to enhanced polysulfide
solubility resulting from the removal of interfacial products at
the cathode and facile lithium polysulfide dissolution by
TMSB. However, in the same conditions, both the cells
suffered extreme overpotential after the first discharge plateau
when a faster C-rate (0.2C) was used. This was most likely
caused by the sluggish redox kinetics of high concentrations of
polysulfides at the cathode interface, owing to rapid mass
transfer in the electrolyte when a faster C-rate is employed,
which is expected at low E/S conditions. To further study the
effect of the TMSB additive at faster rates and to avoid partial
discharge−charge of the cells, cells with a higher E/S ratio of 8
μL/mg were used with the same S-loading, and the charge−
discharge profiles are shown in Figure 4b,c. These cells were
initially allowed to rest for 24 h to ensure proper wetting of the
cathode and were first cycled three times at a 0.05C rate for
formation to take place, as shown in Figure S3. After the first
three cycles, the cells were tested at a rate of 0.2C, and similar
to the previous case, the discharge for the cell with no additive
was cut off at 1.7 V after a high voltage dip following the first
plateau of discharge in the second cycle. However, the cell with
2 wt % TMSB had significantly lower polarization since the
formation cycles, and it underwent stable charge−discharge at
a faster rate despite reduced Coulombic efficiency during the
formation cycles. It was noted that the first discharge plateau of
the first formation cycle was missing for the cell with the
TMSB additive, as shown in Figure S3b, and this might have
resulted from the formation of short-chained lithium
polysulfides that were formed in the cell due to the long rest
time. Upon consecutive cycling, it was found that both cells
suffered from cutoff issues after the voltage dip due to the
electrolyte and loading conditions.
The cutoff voltage for discharge was set as 1.7 V for all the
cells mentioned before due to the use of LiNO3 salt in the
electrolyte, which stabilizes the solid-electrolyte interface (SEI)
and inhibits unwanted reactions from taking place on the Li
anode.45 However, discharging the Li−S cell beyond 1.7 V
causes the decomposition of LiNO3 at the cathode surface and
leads to the formation of undesirable products at the cathode−
electrolyte interface (CEI).46 To evaluate the extent of the
overvoltage during discharge, the cutoff voltage was reduced to
1.4 and 1.2 V for formation cycles at 0.05C and cycling at 0.2C
rates, respectively. Therefore, no LiNO3 salt was used in the
electrolyte for the further galvanostatic charge−discharge
studies, meaning the SEI was not stable, and there were
consequences from the shuttle effect on the voltage profiles of
the formation cycles, as shown in Figure S4b,c. In the absence
of LiNO3, the cells containing TMSB were overcharged in the
initial formation cycles (0.05C), which is distinctive of the
shuttle effect and is very similar to the charge profiles of Li−S
cells when high donor number (DN) solvents like dimethyl
sulfoxides are used. This suggests that, similar to high DN
solvents,47 TMSB also facilitates the dissolution of higher
polysulfide concentration in the electrolyte as a result of a
different mechanism compared to high DN solvents, which
accelerates the shuttle effect by dissolving polysulfide species
and anions in the CEI instead of Li+ ions in the absence of an
SEI-stabilizing additive like LiNO3. However, the most
significant observation was the decreasing voltage dip following
the first discharge plateau, which is proportional to the amount
of TMSB additive in the electrolyte. The overpotential
experienced by the cells was calculated by finding the
difference in voltage between the lowest point in the voltage
dip and the point of onset of the voltage dip following the first
discharge plateau in the fourth (0.2C rate) cycle for all the
cells, as shown in Figure 5a-c. There was a prominent

very low Coulombic efficiency (<30%) when the (n + 1)th

the shuttling of polysulfides, which can also be attributed to the
absence of LiNO3 in the electrolyte. A closer observation of

the discharge profile of the cell containing 5 wt % TMSB. The
existence of two voltage plateaus after the voltage dip with 5 wt

and low E/S conditions, which shows that this behavior arises
due to changes in redox kinetics taking place in the cell. The
investigation of the change in redox kinetics that influences
voltage profile variations in Li−S batteries is an area of ongoing
interest and will be further explored in our future work
involving ARs.
■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, TMSB greatly reduces the overpotential caused
and enhances utilization in high sulfur-loading cells under lean
electrolyte conditions by scavenging the interfacial products at
the cathode surface and improving polysulfide dissolution. The
cathodes cycled with TMSB as an additive contained relatively
fewer decomposition products on the surface compared to the
cathodes cycled in the blank electrolyte, thereby exposing the
end-products at the cathode surface. Hence, it showed that
TMSB helps break down oxidized products of the electrolyte
not only in high-voltage cathodes but also in lithium−sulfur
cells. However, the effect of the TMSB additive on the Li
anode surface in the presence of polysulfides remains a
question that we intend to investigate in our future work.
Further, we believe that ARs, similar to TMSB, can be
designed based on organic side groups to selectively scavenge
decomposition products that lead to high interfacial resistance
and promote utilization of the active materials in high-loading
and lean electrolyte conditions. Moreover, the loss of active
materials during consecutive cycling due to the shuttle effect
under these conditions also needs to be addressed for the
successful commercialization of lithium−sulfur batteries.
