Among these components, new anionic compounds (counte-

molecular compounds (solvents and additives), mainly due to 
the much higher difficulty associated with their designing and 
synthesis. Hence, how anions contribute to interphases remains 
little understood, although a few successful examples of anion-
derived interphases, as represented by LiBOB and LiDFOB in 
nonaqueous and LiTFSI in aqueous electrolytes, suggests that 
the protections provided by such interphases often outperform 
their molecular counterparts. In particular, for the interphase 
protecting Li-metal anode, the anion-derived interphase seems to 
be more effective than those formed by solvents or additives.[20–23]
One challenge presented for anion-derived interphases is 
their negatively charged nature, which discourages their pres-
ence in the inner-Helmholtz layer at anode surface.[24] In the 
case of water-in-salt electrolytes (WiSE), such “anode challenge” 
was partially offset by superconcentration, which compresses 
the anions into the inner-Helmholtz layer and forces an inter-
phasial chemistry that is otherwise impossible in diluted 
aqueous electrolyte. The astonishing benefit brought by such 
anion-derived interphase is an electrochemical stability window 
of 3–4  V.[25–26] Superconcentration concept has also been 
applied on both Li-metal anode [27] and sulfur-cathode,[28] in the 
hope that the interphasial chemistries formed by the existing 
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered promising candidates for 
the next-generation energy-storage systems due to their high theoretical 
capacity and prevalent abundance of sulfur. Their reversible operation, 
however, encounters challenges from both the anode, where dendritic and 
dead Li-metal form, and the cathode, where polysulfides dissolve and become 
parasitic shuttles. Both issues arise from the imperfection of interphases 
between electrolyte and electrode. Herein, a new lithium salt based on an 
imide anion with fluorination and unsaturation in its structure is reported, 
whose interphasial chemistries resolve these issues simultaneously. Lithium 
1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3-hexafluoropropane-1, 3-disulfonimide (LiHFDF) forms highly 
fluorinated interphases at both anode and cathode surfaces, which effectively 
suppress formation of Li-dendrites and dissolution/shuttling of polysulfides, 
and significantly improves the electrochemical reversibility of LSBs. In a 
broader context, this new Li salt offers a new perspective for diversified 
beyond Li-ion chemistries that rely on a Li-metal anode and active cathode 
materials.

1. Introduction
Among the diversified beyond-Li-ion chemistries explored, 
lithium–sulfur battery (LSB) is generally accepted as a prom-
ising candidate due to its high theoretical specific capacity

salt anions can simultaneously suppress both lithium den-

in high-concentration LiTFSI electrolyte, LiNO3 is reduced to 
LiNO2, high-concentration LiTFSI can promote LiNO2 pre-

formation of protective layers on both the cathode and anode, 
which can suppress both polysulfide dissolution/shuttling 
and lithium dendrite.[30] However, superconcentration always 
results in high viscosity, which significantly reduces reaction 
kinetics and limits the electrode active species utilization.[31] 
Therefore, on fundamental level, it is of high scientific impor-
tance to develop a new salt anion that can contribute to inter-
phasial chemistries at both anode and cathode even when used 
at dilute concentrations.
Herein, we report such a novel new anion for lithium salt, 
1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3-hexafluoropropane-1, 3-disulfonimide (LiHFDF). 
When dissolved in nonaqueous solvent, it can simultaneously 
form a LiF-rich solid electrolyte interface (SEI) on Li-metal 
anode and a LiF-containing cathode–electrolyte interface (CEI) 
on sulfur-cathode. The former is a good electronic insulator 
of high mechanical strength, which guides the preferential 
Li-growth in a parallel rather than vertical manner to the Li 
surface. The latter effectively confines the active sulfur species 
from the bulk electrolyte, thus minimizing the dissolution and 
shuttling of polysulfides. The new SEI and CEI chemistries 
derived from the novel anion HFDF significantly suppress the 
parasitic reactions in the very challenging Li-sulfur system, 
leading to excellent reversibility as evidenced by both cou-
lombic efficiency and cycling stability. In a 
broader context, this new anion is expected 
to offer new perspective to diversified battery 
chemistries based on Li-metal anode.
2. Results and Discussion
One of the major restrictions imposed on the 
electrochemical performance of LSBs is the 
inevitable formation of lithium dendrites and 
dead lithium. Previous research has revealed 
that the anion of Li salts have an impact far 
greater than solvents and additives on the 
battery performance, as the inner-layer of 
SEI close to Li-metal surface, which is the 
key interphase components functioning as 
protective barrier, mainly originates from the 
reduction of salt anions,[20–21,23] this explains 
why lithium dendrite growth can be par-
tially suppressed in superconcentrated elec-
trolytes, with lithium bis(trifuoromethane 
sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) as a prominent 
example. Compared with other lithium salts 
with fluorinated anions, LiTFSI offers high 
solubility with relatively lower viscosity, fast 
ion conduction, and most importantly, rich 
fluorine source readily accessible to electro-
chemical reduction and interphase forma-
tion. Amongst all electrolytes used for LSBs, 
LiTFSI is doubtlessly the most favored salt 
thanks to these merits. To design a better anion than TFSI, we 
aim to maintain the skeleton structure of imide that ensures 
excellent delocalization of negative charge, and the fluoroalkyl 
moiety that provides chemical source for interphase. To make 
the new anion more prone to electrochemical reduction, we 
also attempt to introduce an element of unsaturation, which 
takes the form of a cyclic structure in the case of LiHFDF. The 
calculation reveals that the energy level of the lowest-unoccu-
pied-molecular-orbital (LUMO) for HFDF (3.92  eV) is lower 
as compared with LiTFSI (4.97  eV), suggesting that HFDF-
reduction would occur at a potential higher than TFSI.[32] 
More interestingly, its energy level of the highest-occupiedmo-
lecular-orbital (HOMO) (−3.57 eV) is also higher than LiTFSI 
(−3.67 eV) (Figure 1a), suggesting an easier oxidation of LiHFDF 
at cathode surface as well. Comparing the structures between 
TFSI and HFDF, one would attribute the lower LUMO and 
higher HOMO energies of HFDF to its ring structure, which 
introduces instability against either electrochemical reduction 
or oxidation, this is confirmed by the cyclic voltammetry (CV) 
measurements of LiTFSI and LiHFDF electrolyte on stainless 
steel electrodes (Figure  S1, Supporting Information). Consid-
ering its fluorine-rich structure, one would also expect abun-
dant LiF-content in both SEI and CEI. The presence of LiF has 
been credited for many benefits in different interphases,[33] 
mainly because of the extreme properties associated with the 
elements sitting at two extremities of the electrochemical spec-
trum, i.e., Li and F, which include electrochemical stability 
against both reduction and oxidation, small lattice constant,

These characteristics make LiF the ideal interphasial 
component for SEI with merits such as high electronic 
insulation to prevent electrolyte irreversible reactions, high 
mechanical strength against breach of interphase due to 
electrode volume change, and most importantly, the ability 
to guide Li-deposition in a parallel rather than vertical direc-

energy.[36] On the cathode side, the new CEI chemistry rich 
in LiF possess similar merits in effectively preventing the 
polysulfides dissolution and shuttling as well as the volume 
change during the repeated lithiation/de-lithiation cycles, 
which has been the main cause for the persistent capacity 
fading of LSBs using electrolyte based LiTFSI dissolved 
ether solvents.[37–38] The active sulfur are hence physically 
separated from bulk electrolyte by this CEI, reduced the dis-
solution of polysulfides in electrolyte during the subsequent 
charging/discharging processes, and maintained the elec-
trochemical stability and mechanical integrity of the sulfur 
electrode. How LiHFDF suppresses both Li dendrite growth 
and polysulfide dissolution and shuttling is schematically 
illustrated in Figure 1b.
The effect of new Li-salt on the morphologies of sulfur 
cathode is revealed under transmission electron microscope 
(TEM). After 5 and 20 cycles, a CEI film was observed on the 
sulfur surface recovered from the LiHFDF-containing electro-

elements (Figure  S2, Supporting Information) that were gen-

LiTFSI is used as salt, indicating the general absence of a

In sharp comparison with LiHFDF, the LiTFSI-based 
electrolyte produced a rather clean sulfur cathode surface 
(Figure  2e,f), in absence of Li2O, LiF, and Li3N as indicated 
by O 1s (Figure  S4f, Supporting Information), F 1s spectra 
(Figure 2h) and Li 1s (Figure 2g) or N 1s[42] (Figure S3b, Sup-
porting Information). This absence of CEI is the consequence 
of high electrochemical stability of TFSI anion as shown in 
Figure  1a, which can remain thermodynamically inert at the 
oxidation potential of sulfur active materials (<3.0 V).
It has been suggested earlier that LiF and Li3N are the 
favored species to form uniform, high flexibility, mechanically 
durable, electrochemically stable, and dense interphases, 
which could physically confine the sulfur and its many forms 
of lithiation products to the bulk cathode, thus physically sepa-
rating them from diffusing into bulk electrolyte.[41,44] Moreover, 
high flexibility, mechanically durable, and dense interphase, 
which makes the sulfur electrode surface maintain the stability 
and integrity against the volumetric change of sulfur particles, 
and thus strongly inhibit the dissolution of polysulfides.[38] 
Furthermore, Li3N have ionic conductivities far above 
10−4 S cm−1, which reduces interfacial impedances toward cell 
reactions.[45–46] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
was conducted to evaluate the resistance and reversibility of 
lithium–sulfur batteries using electrolytes with both LiTFSI 
and LiHFDF (Figure  S6, Supporting Information). Interfacial 
resistance (Rf) and charge transfer resistance (Rct) for LiHFDF-
containing electrolyte are apparently higher than those with 
LiTFSI after the first discharge, this may be due to LiF-content 
CEI as high electronic insulation is formed on the surface of 
electrode, which increases the Rf and Rct of electrode. After 
5 and 60 cycles, the Rf and Rct for LiTFSI electrolyte exhibit an 
obviously high increase, this may be due primarily to sulfur-
content passivation layer as a poor ionic conductivity and high 
electronic insulation is formed on the surface of electrode 
during cycling. The repeated breakdown-reconstruction process 
of sulfur-content passivation layer results in a gradually thick-
ened passivation layer on the electrode surface upon prolonged 
cycling, which results in continuous increase of Rf and Rct.[38] 
However. these resistances for LiHFDF-containing electrolyte 
exhibit little increase after 60 cycles, probably because of both 
LiF for its mechanical rigidity and electrochemical stability, and 
Li3N for its ion conduction. The synergistic effects between 
LiF and Li3N ensure the reversibility and integrity of sulfur 
cathode.
Sulfur confinement by CEI was confirmed by in situ ultra-
violet (UV) absorption spectra in a quartz vessel cell. The 
spectra of Li2Sn and corresponding digital images of quartz 
vessel lithium sulfur cells are compared between LiTFSI 
(Figure 2i,k) and LiHFDF (Figure 2j,k) electrolytes during the 
discharge process at 0.2 C. In sharp comparison with LiTFSI-
based electrolyte, only peaks in weak absorption intensity was 
observed in the UV spectra of Li2Sn in 1 m LiHFDF electrolyte, 
indicating the bare solubility of polysulfides in LiHFDF electro-
lyte (Figure  2j). Support also comes from optical observation, 
as when the cell discharge to 1.7 V (Figure 2k), the electrolyte 
using LiTFSI turns into deep yellow in the quartz vessel, in 
strong contrast to the only faint yellow displayed by LiHFDH 
electrolyte. Digital images of separators used in LiTFSI, 
LiHFDF, and LiHFDF-0.2 electrolytes after 5 and 60 cycles 
(Figure S7, Supporting Information) lead to the same conclu-
sion. Note that in LiHFDF-0.2, the new salt was used only as a 
minor salt at 0.2 m, which already provides sufficient stabiliza-
tion as LiHFDF-based electrolyte.
On the anode side, how LiHFDF affect the surface mor-
phology of Li-metal was investigated using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) conducted on Li-metal electrodes recovered 
from symmetric Li–Li cells after cycling in both LiTFSI- and 
LiHFDF-based electrolytes. Li-metal surface from LiHFDF 
appears to be both dense and smooth after 2 cycles (Figure 3a,c), 
while after 20 cycles still no dendritics lithium can be observed 
(Figure 3b). This is mainly due to that LiF-rich interphase was 
formed on the surface of lithium anode, which is a good elec-
tronic insulator of high-strength that guides the Li-growth in a 
parallel rather than vertical manner to the Li surface. In com-
parison, cracks are clearly visible on the surface of the Li elec-
trode recovered from LiTFSI after only 2 cycles (Figure 3e,g). 
After 20 cycles obvious dendrites in loosely packed form have 
formed (Figure 3f). Similar conclusion can be drawn from the 
examination of Li electrodes recovered from actual LSB cells 
rather than Li–Li symmetric cells (Figure S8, Supporting Infor-
mation). XPS were performed on Li-metal electrodes recov-
ered from symmetric Li–Li cells, which provide information 
regarding surface element species, their relative abundances 
as well as the valence state of Li. The intensity of Li-F signal 
(684.8 eV), which is the fully reduced product of HFDF− anion, 
is stronger than that of CF (688.6 eV) that arises from frag-
mented anion not fully reduced (Figure 3d), indicating LiF as 
the main ingredient in the SEI originated from LiHFDF-based 
electrolyte. The reversal was observed for LiTFSI-based electro-
lyte (Figure 3h), suggesting that most TFSI anions on surface 
are only partially broken down before being reduced to LiF. A 
LiF-rich interphase on lithium metal anode apparently plays 
the key role in maintaining a smooth lithium metal surface 
during repeated Li-deposition and stripping while preventing 
Li-dendrite growth.[36,43]

potential addictive. The superior stabilization effect of LiHFDF 
is also confirmed by the sulfur cathode cycling tests at 0.5 C 
in LiHFDF, LiTFSI, and LiHFDF-0.2 electrolyte without LiNO3 
additive (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
3. Conclusion
In summary, we report a new Li salt, which can simultane-
ously form new SEI and CEI chemistries that can stabilize 
both Li metal anode and sulfur-cathode. The cyclic and highly 
fluorinated anion of this new salt LiHFDF introduces LiF-rich 
interphases that effectively suppress the Li-dendrite growth as 
well as construct a durable physical barrier consisting of both 
LiF and Li3N that confine sulfur species to the cathode bulk. 
Such combined stabilizations at both Li-metal anode and sulfur 
cathode leads to significant improvements in electrochemical 
performance of LSBs. In a much broader context, this new 
salt LiHFDF should find extensive applications in any battery 
that employs Li-metal anode and other aggressive cathode 
chemistries.
4. Experimental Section
Material and Electrode Preparation: Elemental sulfur and K carbon 
were mixed at a weight ratio of 3:1 by milling for 1 h, and then the 
mixture was transferred to a tube furnace and treated at 155 °C under 
argon atmosphere for 12 h, resulting in C/S-64 composites (Figure S17, 
Supporting Information). Working electrodes were prepared by 
mixing a slurry containing 90% active material, 10% polymer binder 
(polyvinylidene difluoride, PVDF) with N-methyl pyrrolidone on an 
aluminum foil, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The 
sulfur electrodes were punched into 0.6 cm disk. The sulfur loading was 
1–1.5 mg cm−2, and electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 20 µL mg−1. 3D current 
collector electrodes were prepared by mixing the slurry on the carbon 
cloth with sulfur loading 5–11  mg cm−2, corresponding metal lithium 
anode were obtained by pressing lithium foil on the stainless steel 
back-up plate with lithium loading 2–5 mg cm−2, and electrolyte/sulfur 
ratio of 15 µL mg−1.
Preparation of Electrolytes: The base electrolyte with 1.0 m LiTFSI and 
1 wt% LiNO3 in DME and DOL (1:1 by volume) was purchased from 
Suzhou Qian min Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. LiHFDF was purchased 
from TCI (Shanghai) Development Co., Ltd. The LiHFDF electrolyte was 
prepared by dissolving 1.0 m LHFDF, 1 wt% LiNO3 in DOL and DME 
(1:1 by volume). LiHFDF-0.2 electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 
1 m LiTFSI and 0.2 m LiHFDF, 1 wt% LiNO3 in DOL and DME (1:1 by 
volume).
Electrochemical Testing: Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were 
carried out at Newware battery tester between 1.5 and 3  V versus 
Li+/Li. All tests were performed at 30  °C. EIS was performed using 
a PARSTAT4000A electrochemical workstation (Princeton Applied 
Research, USA) in the frequency range of 1  MHz to 100  Hz at open 
circuit potential with an alternating spectrum (ac) perturbation of 10 m. 
Cyclic voltammetry measurements of LiTFSI and LiHFDF electrolyte 
on stainless steel electrodes were used to confirm the electrochemical 
stability window of the electrolyte salt, and carried out on PARSTAT4000A 
electrochemical workstation (Princeton Applied Research, USA) at a 
scan speed of 5 mV s−1 in the voltage of 1.5–7.0 V.
In Situ Batteries Testing: In situ quartz vessel cell was assembled in a 
microquartz cuvette with 1 mL volume, Li metal was used as anode and 
copper foil or sulfur electrode was used as counter electrode. All of the 
sulfur electrodes were cut to the same size. Cegard 2325 (Hefei Kejing 
materials technology co., LTD) was used to insulate the cathode from 
anode. All of in situ batteries testing were used with the electrolyte with 
the same volume.
Characterizations: Morphology of samples was investigated by a SEM 
(JSM- 6360) and a TEM system (JEOL, JEM-2100). Thermogravimetric 
(TG) analysis was carried out on a Perkin-Elmer 7 instrument to 
determine the weight ratio of S to Carbon. The surface chemistry of 
electrode was characterized by XPS system (Thermo Fisher, USA), 
samples were washed by dimethyl carbonate and held in the vacuum 
chamber overnight to get rid of electrolyte and organics residues.
