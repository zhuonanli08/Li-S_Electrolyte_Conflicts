Suppressed Li2Sx Solubility to Achieve Stable Li−S Pouch Cells with

ABSTRACT: The endurance of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) cells depends on the
stability of lithium (Li) metal anodes and their consistent efficiency during
extended Li dissolution and deposition cycles. Electrolytes containing Li[N-
(SO2F)2] (Li[FSA]) have shown potential in enhancing Li anode reversibility by
promoting the formation of a favorable inorganic-rich solid-electrolyte interphase
(SEI) on the Li metal electrode. However, the use of Li[FSA] as the primary
electrolyte salt in Li−S batteries is hindered by the spontaneous side reactions of
[FSA]−anions with soluble lithium-polysulfides (Li2Sx, 2 ≤x ≤8). To overcome
this challenge, we have developed a localized high-concentration electrolyte
(LHCE) with reduced Li2Sx solubility, composed of Li[TFSA0.8LiFSA0.2]
([TFSA]: [N(SO2CF3)2]) binary salts dissolved in sulfolane (SL) and diluted
by 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE). This LHCE
solution demonstrates superior stability of [FSA]−anions, due to the restricted dissolution of Li2Sx within the LHCE. We
experimentally evaluated the critical factors affecting reversibility of Li dissolution/deposition in electrolytes containing
Li[TFSA0.8LiFSA0.2]. Increased salt concentration, combined with HFE dilution, widens the reduction potential gap between the
anion and Li+, which thermodynamically promotes anion reduction, controls SEI composition, and improves Li reversibility. We
demonstrate the operation of a Li−S pouch cell under practical conditions with a high sulfur loading of 5.5 mg(S) cm−2 and an
extremely low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 3.0 μL mg(S)
−1. The battery delivers a high energy density of 280 Wh kg−1. Our
findings provide insights into the critical factors for achieving prolonged Li dissolution/deposition reversibility, particularly under
practical Li−S pouch cell conditions, through electrolyte formulation design.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, lithium dissolution/deposition reversibility, anion reduction, solvation structure,
solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI)
1. INTRODUCTION
Rechargeable lithium metal batteries (LMBs), including
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) and lithium−air (Li−O2) batteries,
hold immense promise as next-generation energy storage
systems due to their extremely high theoretical energy
densities.1−3 This potential primarily arises from the high
gravimetric capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and the lowest standard
electrode potential (E°Li/Li+ = −3.04 V vs SHE) of metallic
lithium.1 However, the practical realization of LMBs faces a
significant challenge due to the thermodynamic instability of Li
anodes when paired with nonaqueous electrolytes. In the
presence of organic electrolytes, spontaneous reactions
between the Li anode and electrolyte components lead to
the gradual formation of passivating solid-electrolyte inter-
phase (SEI) layers, which consist of Li+-insulating side
products.4,5 This layer hinders reversible Li dissolution and
deposition, resulting in continuous reductive decomposition
and ultimately leading to high-impedance failure of LMBs.4
Furthermore, cyclic operation induces the formation of dead
lithium and lithium dendrites on the Li anode, compromising
both the longevity and safety of LMBs.6
Efforts to optimize electrolyte compositions through the
inclusion of suitable SEI-forming additives have emerged as a
promising approach.7 Incorporating specific lithium salts into
electrolytes plays a crucial role not only in ensuring high ionic
conductivity but also in significantly influencing the
composition of SEIs on Li anodes.8−11 For example, high
concentration electrolytes (HCEs) based on Li[FSA] have
shown improved LMB performance by promoting the

formation of a desirable inorganic-rich SEI on the Li anode,
which offers low interfacial resistance and inhibits continuous
electrolyte decomposition.12,13 Some previous studies have
reported remarkable Coulombic efficiencies of approximately
99% during Li deposition/dissolution using Li[FSA]-based
electrolytes.14−19 However, despite their excellent Li Coulom-
bic efficiency and high ionic conductivity, the application of
Li[FSA] salts in Li−S batteries remains limited. The relatively
weak S−F bond in [FSA]−anions readily participates in
parasitic reactions with electrolyte-soluble Li-polysulfides
(Li2Sx), leading to the formation of insulating lithium sulfate
(LiSOx).20
Previous studies have demonstrated stable Li−S batteries
using highly concentrated Li[TFSA], Li[FSA], or binary
Li[FSA]/Li[TFSA] electrolytes dissolved in specific solvent
mixtures, significantly enhancing cycling stability.21,22 How-
ever, these studies are typically accompanied by low sulfur
loadings (1 mg(S) cm−2), which suggests the continued
occurrence of undesired reactions between [FSA]−anions
and polysulfides.20 Castillo et al. realized an improved cycling
performance of Li−S battery with graphene-based sulfur
cathode (4 mg(S) cm−2) using dual salt (Li[FSA]/Li[TFSA])
SL-based sparingly solvating electrolytes with an E/S of 7.0 μL
mg(S)
−1.23 However, to achieve Li−S batteries with a high
practical energy density of 300 Wh kg−1, it is crucial to ensure
an S-loading above 4 mg(S) cm−2, an E/S ratio below 5 μL
mg(S)
−1, and a low negative-to-positive capacity ratio.24
Consequently, the need for Li[FSA]-based electrolytes
compatible with high-sulfur-loading cathodes under lean
electrolyte conditions remains a major challenge in the
development of high-energy-density, durable Li−S batteries.
To address this, our study explores Li[FSA]/Li[TFSA]
binary-salt electrolytes with suppressed Li2Sx solubility, based
on our electrolyte research,25−27 aiming to improve long-term
Li dissolution/deposition reversibility during extended cycling
in Li−S batteries. We were the first25 to propose a sparingly
solvating electrolyte for Li2Sx in Li−S cells and to use HFE as a
diluent in HCEs to create LHCEs. By incorporating a 20%
molar ratio of Li[FSA] into Li[TFSA], [Li(SL)2]-
[TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE electrolytes exhibit favorable cycling
performance by suppressing side reactions between Li2Sx and
[FSA]−anions, ensuring a high degree of mutual compatibility.
Utilizing a fabricated pouch cell with a high sulfur-loading
cathode (5.5 mg(S) cm−2) and a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S)
ratio (3.0 μL mg(S)
−1), our investigation demonstrates a high
energy density of 280 Wh kg−1. Furthermore, we systematically
evaluate key electrolyte properties influencing lithium reversi-
bility. This work holds promise for the development of
practical high-energy-density Li−S batteries and advances the
understanding of electrolyte design for LMBs.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. Purified sulfolane (SL) was supplied by Kishida
Chemical. 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether
(HFE) was purchased from Daikin Industries and used as received.
Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)amide (Li[TFSA]) and lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)amide (Li[FSA]) were obtained from Kishida
Chemical and vacuum-dried at 120 °C for 48 h before being
transferred into an Ar-filled glovebox. Elemental sulfur (S8) was
acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, Ketjen black (KB) from Lion
Corporation, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC2200) from Daicel
FineChem, and styrene−butadiene rubber (SBR) from JSR
Corporation. Carbon-coated aluminum foil (thickness = 15 μm)
was procured from Showa Denko and used as the cathode current
collector.
2.2. Preparation of Electrolytes. Electrolyte solutions with
specific molar ratios of Li[TFSA], Li[FSA], and solvents were
prepared by dissolving the required amounts of salts into SL. For
example, a highly concentrated [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2] electrolyte
was prepared by mixing SL, Li[TFSA] and Li[FSA] at a molar ratio of
2:0.8:0.2 to form a homogeneous solution. For LHCEs with a final Li-
salt concentration of 1 mol dm−3, calculated amounts of HFE were
added to the electrolytes. In the case of [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]
electrolyte, HFE was added to the above concentrated electrolyte to
obtain a homogeneous mixture of [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-4.5HFE.
The basic properties of the aforementioned electrolyte solutions used
in this study are presented in the Supporting Information (Table S1).
For comparison in pouch cell evaluation, a traditional electrolyte
consisting of 1 mol dm−3 Li[TFSA] and 0.3 mol dm−3 Li[FSA] in a
1:1 volume ratio of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), with 0.2 mol dm−3 LiNO3 as an additive, was used.
Additionally, for Raman measurements, an electrolyte consisting of 3
mol dm−3 Li[FSA] in a 1:1 volume ratio of DOL/DME was also
utilized. All electrolytes were prepared in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 <
0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm) at room temperature.
2.3. Fabrication of Sulfur Cathodes. A sulfur/carbon composite
was prepared by heat-treating S8 and KB in a 3:1 weight ratio at 155
°C for 12 h, followed by fine powdering. An electrode slurry was made
by dispersing 93.5 wt % of the S/KB composite and 3 wt % of
titanium black28 (Mitsubishi Material Corporation) in an aqueous
solution of CMC2200 (1.5 wt %) and SBR (2 wt %). Titanium back is
an amphiphilic functional additive to obtain high S-loading
cathodes.28 The slurry was coated onto a carbon-coated aluminum
foil and dried at 40 °C for 48 h. The coated foil was then cut into
circular disks (for coin cells) or rectangular shapes (for pouch cells).
Prior to use, the electrodes were further dried in a vacuum oven at 45
°C for 12 h. The average areal loading of sulfur in the S/KB cathodes
was adjusted to 4.2 mg(S) cm−2 for coin cells and 5.5 mg(S) cm−2 for
pouch cells.
2.4. Electrochemical Cells Assembly and Characterization.
Electromotive force (EMF) measurements for a concentration cell
with the configuration [Li metal|1 mol dm−3 Li[TFSA] in Triglyme
(G3)|test electrolyte solution|Li metal] were conducted to investigate
the potential of Li/Li+ in test electrolyte solutions, following a
procedure similar to that in our previous work.19 To examine the
reduction behavior of the electrolytes, cathodic linear-sweep
voltammetry (LSV) measurements were carried out using a three-
electrode cell with a copper (Cu) disk (3 mm dia.) as the working
electrode, a Li metal foil as the counter electrode, and another Li
metal foil immersed in 1 mol dm−3 Li[TFSA] in G3 as the reference
electrode. The Cu disk electrode was polished with high-purity
alumina suspensions (Buehler) with particle sizes of 1, 0.5, and 0.03
μm successively before each experiment. A Vycor glass frit was used to
separate the reference electrode from the electrolyte solutions during
the EMF and LSV measurements. The LSV measurements were
performed on a BioLogic VMP-3 instrument.
To evaluate lithium deposition/dissolution reversibility, Li||Cu coin
cells (2032-type, Hohsen Corp.) were assembled using a Cu foil disk
(16 mm dia.) as the substrate for lithium electrodeposition, paired
with a Li metal disk (Honjo Metal, 14 mm dia., 200 μm thick) as the
anode. The Cu foil was cleaned by immersing it in 1 mol dm−3 HCl
for 10 min, followed by repeated rinsing with ultrapure water (∼18
MΩ·cm, Milli-Q Integral 3), washing with acetone several times, and
rapid drying under vacuum before use. Celgard 3501 membranes (17
mm dia.) soaked with the appropriate amount of electrolyte were used
as separators. The total electrolyte volume for each Li/Cu coin cell
was approximately 70 μL. The Li deposition/dissolution efficiency
(Coulombic efficiency, CE) in the Li||Cu coin cells was assessed using
an established protocol:29,30 (1) an initial formation cycle with Li
deposition of 5 mA h cm−2 on Cu and stripping to 1 V; (2) deposition
of 5 mA h cm−2 on Cu again as a Li reservoir; (3) repeated
dissolution/deposition of Li at 1 mA h cm−2 for 20 cycles; and (4)
complete stripping of Li to 1 V. The current density for all steps was

sulfur on the cathodes, with a theoretical capacity of 1672 mAh g−1.

temperature of 30 °C.
2.5. Post-Cycling Characterization. After charge/discharge
cycling, the Li−S batteries were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox
to investigate electrode morphology and analyze chemical composi-
tions. The cycled electrodes of interest were thoroughly washed with
HFE before characterization. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) was performed using a PHI Quantera II Scanning X-ray
Microprobe (ULVAC-PHI) to analyze the chemical composition of
the samples. XPS data fitting was conducted using XPSPEAK 4.1
software. Raman spectra of the electrolyte solutions were obtained
using a Raman spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser (RMP-
330, JASCO), with a spectral resolution of 4.5 cm−1. The instrument
was calibrated with a polypropylene standard. The electrolyte
solutions were sealed in a glass capillary tube inside the glovebox
prior to analysis.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Lithium Metal Anode Deterioration in Li−S
Batteries with [Li(SL)2][TFSA]-2HFE Electrolyte. To assess
the impact of Li anode stability on the cycling performance of
Li−S batteries, cathodes composed of a KB/S composite with
[Li(SL)2][TFSA]-2HFE electrolyte were cycled at 0.1C
against Li metal foils of two different thicknesses (70 or 200
μm) in coin cells. Figure 1a illustrates the cycling performance
of both cell configurations. Both configurations exhibited rapid
capacity decay within the initial 20 cycles, primarily due to the
structural changes caused by significant volume fluctuations in
the sulfur cathodes.31 Subsequently, both cell types reached
relatively stable capacities. However, Li−S cells with a 70 μm-
thick Li anode exhibited a gradual capacity decline after 60
cycles, retaining only 43% of the initial capacity after 80 cycles,
with an average capacity decay rate of 0.71% per cycle. In
contrast, Li−S cells with a 200 μm-thick Li anode
demonstrated superior cycling stability, retaining 62% of the
initial capacity after 100 cycles, with an average capacity decay
rate of 0.38% per cycle.
Figure 1b,c compare the charge/discharge profiles of cells
using 200 and 70 μm Li anodes, respectively. Both
configurations displayed two-step discharge profiles, with an
initial capacity of approximately 1000 mAh g−1, corresponding
to the reduction of S8 to Li2S via Li2Sx intermediates.32 The
first-step capacity of approximately 100 mAh g−1 is significantly
lower than that observed in traditional DOL/DME-based
electrolytes,26,27,33 indicating greatly suppressed solubility of
Li2Sx in this electrolyte. Notably, the discharge curves for the

For a comprehensive comparison, optical images of cycled Li
electrodes with a 70 μm thickness are presented in Figure S1.
Initially, the Li electrode displayed a shiny and smooth surface
(Figure S1a). However, after 50 cycles, the electrode exhibited
a blackened surface, indicative of powdery Li deposition on the
current collector (Figure S1b). Continued cycling led to
further Li loss and detachment from the collector after 80
cycles (Figure S1c). This detachment, caused by the formation
of inactive or “dead” Li, coupled with Li loss due to side
reactions, contributes to the substantial decrease in capacity. In
contrast, for an optical image of a Li electrode with a 200 μm
thickness after 100 cycles (Figure S1d), the surface appeared
blackened, indicating the formation of powdery lithium
deposits. However, detachment of Li from the collector, as
seen in Figure S1c, was not observed. The continuous supply
of fresh lithium from the excess source likely prevents
significant deterioration in the Li−S coin cell (Figure 1a),
though the depth of powdery lithium deposition appears to
increase progressively. Consequently, the primary cause of
long-term cycling failure in Li−S batteries can be attributed to
issues with the lithium metal anode.
To further confirm this, a Li−S cell using a 70 μm-thick Li
anode was disassembled after the 50th charging cycle, and the
cycled electrodes were reassembled with fresh counterparts.
The reassembled cells were cycled again at 0.1C, and the initial
charge−discharge curves are compared in Figure 1d. Notably,
when reassembled with a fresh anode, the cycled cathode
exhibited an increased discharge capacity, rising from 700 mAh
g−1 to 800 mAh g−1 at 0.1C. In contrast, the cell reassembled
with the cycled anode and a fresh cathode achieved only 600
mAh g−1, with no significant improvement. Therefore, the
replacement of the anode significantly enhances the perform-
ance of cycled cells, highlighting the critical role of the lithium
metal anode in determining the overall battery performance.
In short, the degradation of long-term lithium−sulfur (Li−
S) battery performance under practical conditions can be
primarily attributed to uneven lithium deposition and
irreversible loss of active lithium. Addressing these challenges
necessitates a fundamental understanding of lithium reversi-
bility.
3.2. Li/Li+ Reversibility with Li[TFSA]/Li[FSA] Binary
Salt Electrolytes. The beneficial role of Li[FSA] in enhancing
lithium reversibility is well-established.16−19,35−38 To further
validate this, we conducted an investigation using a series of
highly concentrated electrolytes with compositions of [Li-
(SL)2][TFSA1−xFSAx] (where x ranges from 0.1 to 0.4). For
comparison, we also examined the behavior of a pure
[Li(SL)3][FSA] electrolyte (due to the solubility limit of
Li[FSA], three times the amount of SL relative to Li[FSA] in
molar ratio was necessary). Figure 2a illustrates the potential
evolution during the deposition/dissolution processes for these
solutions. Our previous studies indicated that electrolytes
containing pure Li[TFSA] salt experienced premature failure
before the test completion, regardless of the lithium salt
concentration.19 In contrast, cells using partially Li[FSA]-
substituted solutions demonstrated consistent cycling perform-
ance, indicating stable deposition/dissolution of Li onto Cu
substrates.
Furthermore, Figure 2b presents the measured CE as a
function of Li[FSA] molar fraction (x) in the binary salts. As
expected, solutions with higher Li[FSA] mole fractions showed
improved CE. Notably, a substantial increase in CE was
observed at a 20 mol % Li[FSA] content. Specifically, the CE
for [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.9FSA0.1] was 92.4%, which significantly
improved to 98.2, 98.4, and 98.7% for [Li(SL)2]-
[TFSA0.8FSA0.2], [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.7FSA0.3], and [Li(SL)2]-
[TFSA0.6FSA0.4], respectively. Considering the potential side
reactions between Li2Sx and Li[FSA], previous studies have
restricted the use of Li[FSA] in Li−S battery applications.20−22
Therefore, in the following section, we discuss the factors
influencing lithium reversibility, with a particular focus on the
20 mol % substituted Li[TFSA0.8FSA0.2] electrolytes.

Li[TFSA] and Li[FSA] solutions.19 Building on this
foundation, the current study employed EMF and LSV
measurements for various Li[TFSA0.8FSA0.2] solutions.

The above results confirm that the reduction potentials of
chemical species in the solution follow the order of ELi/Li+ <
ESL < Eanion (Figure 3a). To clarify, we define the potential gaps
between ESL and ELi/Li+ as δ1, and those between Eanion and
ELi/Li+ as δ2. Illustrating potential gap variations based on
lithium salt concentration, we found that δ1 decreases and δ2
increases with higher Li concentration (Figure 3b). This trend
thermodynamically indicates easier reduction of anions and
suppressed reduction of the solvent (SL) at higher lithium salt
concentrations. Further analysis of the effect of HFE dilution,
presented in Figure S5, shows a decrease in δ1 and an increase
in δ2 due to HFE dilution. The rise in δ2 by dilution implies
easier anion reduction due to increased anion involvement in
the Li+ solvation shell within the electrolytes,19 which is also
supported by the Raman shift of [TFSA]−/[FSA]−anions, as
discussed earlier (Figure S4).

XPS spectra of F 1s and C 1s comparing LCE, HCE, and
LHCE are shown in Figures 4a,b, respectively. As depicted in
Figure 4a (F 1s spectra), the formation of LiF may result from
the electrochemical reduction of −CF3 and S−F functional
groups present in Li[TFSA] and Li[FSA], respectively.40
However, due to the higher bond strength of C−F in
Li[TFSA] compared to S−F in Li[FSA], LiF formation is
likely primarily due to the decomposition of [FSA]−
anions.38,41 Peaks associated with −SO2F and −SO2CF3
species correspond to the partial electrochemical reduction
of [FSA]−and [TFSA]−anions, respectively, with their
proportions varying across different electrolytes. In LCE, the
larger area under the −SO2CF3 peak relative to −SO2F
suggests a predominant decomposition product from
[TFSA]−. Conversely, in HCE, a significant decrease in the
−SO2CF3 peak area, coupled with an increase in −SO2F,
indicates a greater contribution from the decomposition of
[FSA]−.
It is worth noting that the high salt concentration in HCE
([Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]) leads to predominant anion-
coordination with lithium ions, resulting in the formation of
contact ion pairs and aggregate clusters (Figure S4).19,42,43
This coordination facilitates the reduction of [FSA]−. Addi-
tionally, as shown in Figure 4b, the C 1s XPS spectra exhibit a
peak assignable to −SO2CF3 around 292 eV for all electro-
lytes.44 The intensified −SO2CF3 peak observed in LHCE
indicates a substantial increase in anion decomposition
products on the electrode surface. Moreover, the surface
passivation film formed in LHCE did not display SL-derived
organolithium compounds or lithium carbides, unlike in LCE
and HCE, suggesting suppressed SL decomposition.14,19
The chemical change in the SEI can be exemplified by the
shift in elemental composition from LCE (C: 49.65%, N:
0.42%, F: 2.47%) to HCE (C: 26.74%, N: 3.82%, F: 10.77%)
and further to LHCE (C: 25.37%, N: 6.08%, F: 12.60%)
(Table S2). The distinctive decrease in carbon content and the
increase in fluorine and nitrogen content further demonstrate
reduced SL decomposition and enhanced anion decomposition
with increasing salt concentration, which is further amplified by
HFE dilution. This observation aligns well with the potential
diagram.
To understand the correlation between SEI composition,
resulting from the reduction potential changes, and lithium
reversibility in these electrolytes, we employed the same
method as used in Figure 2 to assess the CE of lithium
deposition/dissolution. Solutions with higher lithium salt
concentrations exhibited improved cycling performance,
achieving higher final stripping capacities (Figure S6a).
Additionally, HFE dilution further reduced overpotential
throughout the measurement period, indicating decreased
resistance at the Li/electrolyte interface (Figure S6b). The CE
shown in Figure S6c demonstrates enhanced lithium
reversibility facilitated by higher lithium salt concentrations
and HFE dilution, primarily due to the formation of anion-
derived SEI films.
In summary, our study effectively correlates the potential
diagram with SEI composition, offering a comprehensive
understanding of electrolyte behavior. Electrolytes with larger
reduction potential gaps between Eanion and ELi/Li+ (δ2) exhibit
accelerated anion reduction, which enhances Li anode
reversibility through the formation of an anion-derived SEI
enriched with inorganic species. This potential diagram visually
represents changes in thermodynamic parameters of the
electrolyte components, providing valuable insights into the
intricate interplay of components influencing lithium reversi-
bility.
3.4. Li−S Battery Performance Under Practical
Conditions. After observing enhanced lithium reversibility,
particularly in LHCE solutions, we aimed to investigate its
influence on the longevity of Li−S batteries. To explore this,
we used a binary salt LHCE solution of [Li(SL)2]-
[TFSA1−xFSAx]-2HFE (x: 0−0.3) as the electrolyte. As
previously mentioned, the contribution of the Li metal anode
to battery degradation is often overlooked due to the
continuous supply of fresh lithium from an excess lithium
source.45 Moreover, many studies on Li−S cells have been
conducted using coin cells, which typically feature low S-
loading cathodes (∼1 mg(S) cm−2), an excess of Li anode, and
an excess of electrolyte (E/S > 10 μL mg(S)
−1). These
conditions can sometimes lead to overestimated performance
and differ significantly from those in practical Li−S batteries,

3.5. Restricted Side Reactions Between Li[FSA] and
Li2Sx in HCE. To evaluate the possible reaction of Li2Sx with
[FSA]−anion, we conducted Raman experiments on electro-
lytes after the introduction of Li2Sx. Li2Sx (nominally Li2S6)
was generated by mixing a stoichiometric molar ratio of Li2S
and S8 into the electrolyte and was allowed to be equilibrated
at an elevated temperature of 60 °C for 5 days. To eliminate
potential interference from the HFE solvent in the Raman
spectra, we investigated two HCEs, [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]
and 3 mol dm−3 Li[FSA] in DOL/DME, both with
approximately the same lithium salt concentrations.
Figure 6a,b show the Raman spectra changes over time for
the above electrolytes in contact with Li2Sx. In the 3 mol dm−3

period (Figure 6a). Additionally, the absence of the SO2

DME solution after 5 days further suggests a significant
reaction between Li2Sx and [FSA]−anion. In contrast, the
[Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2] solution with added Li2Sx exhibits a
broad Li2Sx peak in the wavenumber range of 450−500
cm−1.50−53 Even after 5 days at elevated temperature, this peak
remains almost unchanged, indicating the stability of Li2Sx in
this solution (Figure 6b).

appearance of these electrolytes in contact with Li2Sx after
storage at 60 °C for 5 days. The 3 mol dm−3 Li[FSA] in DOL/
DME solution exhibited an orange color due to the high
solubility of Li2Sx. Meanwhile, the [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]
and [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE solutions appeared tur-

limited solubility, along with the small fraction of Li[FSA],
likely prevented significant reactions between [FSA]−and
Li2Sx in the [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-based HCE and LHCE.

a DOL/DME electrolyte, we fabricated a pouch-type Li cell

DOL/DME electrolyte showed gradually decreasing efficiency,
dropping to around 85% after 30 cycles.
This study focuses on the influence of Li[FSA] on
enhancing lithium reversibility in LHCEs and its impact on
the cycling performance of Li−S batteries. Cells using LHCEs
containing [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2] demonstrated superior
stability in cycling life compared to other electrolyte
compositions. This improved stability is attributed to the
formation of an inorganic-rich SEI and the limited side
reaction between the [FSA]−anion and Li2Sx due to its
restricted solubility. The changes in SEI composition resulting
from variations in salt concentrations and HFE dilution were
reasonably interpreted using a reduction potential diagram of
chemical species in the electrolytes.
4. CONCLUSIONS
Li−S batteries are still facing challenges of cycling stability,
particularly under practical conditions, where a high S-loading
cathode, limited amount of electrolyte (a low E/S ratio), and
low N/P ratio (thin lithium anode) are essential. To overcome
these issues, we have developed SL-based HCE and LHCE
containing LiTFSA to suppress Li2Sx solubility, as well as
better transport property. However, Li−S batteries using these
electrolytes are still suffered from large capacity decay due to
the poor lithium dissolution/deposition reversibility. In this
study, we implemented a strategic approach by formulating a
binary salt mixture of Li[TFSA] and Li[FSA] in an SL solvent
to enhance the lithium reversibility, while keeping suppressed
Li2Sx solubility. Through systematic evaluation of reduction
behavior of chemical species in the electrolytes, we developed a
reduction potential diagram for the LCE, HCE, and LHCE,
and revealed a direct correlation with the SEI compositions,
which greatly affected lithium reversibility. Based on the
accumulated knowledge, we fabricated a Li−S pouch cell under
practical conditions with high sulfur loading, a low N/P ratio,
and an extremely low E/S ratio, which exhibited an energy
density of up to 280 Wh kg−1 and high CE. These insights
would be pivotal in advancing the development of novel
electrolytes suitable for practical application in Li−S batteries.
