Predicting the Solubility of Sulfur: A COSMO-RS-Based Approach
to Investigate Electrolytes for Li–S Batteries
Steffen Jeschke* and Patrik Johansson[a]
Abstract: Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are, in theory, con-
sidering their basic reactions, very promising from a specific
energy density point of view, but have poor power rate ca-
pabilities. The dissolution of sulfur from the C/S cathode in
the electrolyte is a rate-determining and crucial step for the
functionality. To date, time-consuming experimental meth-
ods, such as HPLC/UV, have been used to quantify the corre-
sponding solubilities. Here, we use a computational fluid-
phase thermodynamics approach, the conductor-like screen-
ing model for real solvents (COSMO-RS), to compute the sol-
ubilities of sulfur in different binary and ternary electrolytes.
By using both explicit and implicit solvation approaches for
lithium bistrifluoromethanesulfonimidate (LiTFSI)-containing
electrolytes, a deviation of <0.4 log units was achieved with
respect to experimental data, within the range of experi-
mental error, thus proving COSMO-RS to be a useful tool for
exploring novel Li–S battery electrolytes.
Introduction
The lithium–sulfur battery is considered as a possible next-
generation battery for energy storage in electric vehicles.
Using sulfur as the active electrode material is advantageous
as it is abundant, environmentally friendly, and cheaper than
any currently used cathode materials. Also, it has a high theo-
retical capacity of 1672 mAhg@1 and an energy density of the
order of 2500 Whkg@1, assuming complete reduction to Li2S.[1,2]
Due to its insulating nature, the cathode is often a combination
with a porous carbon material (C/S). During discharge, sulfur is
reduced to various intermediate polysulfides, such as S8
2@, S6
2@,
S4
2@, and S2
2@, before S2@ is reached. This process is ideally re-
versible and the sulfides are oxidized back to sulfur during
charging. However, Li–S batteries suffer from poor cyclability,
mainly due to the solubility of elemental sulfur and polysul-
fides in the electrolyte causing loss of active material. This re-
sults in capacity fading, increase in internal resistance, poor
coulombic efficiencies, and self-discharge. Assuming that ele-
mental sulfur dissolves prior to reduction and hence energy
production, suppressing and controlling the solubility of sulfur
should
clearly
offer
an
approach
to
improve
cycling
stability.[2–4]
In order to quantify the solubility of sulfur in various conven-
tional Li–S battery liquid organic electrolytes, often binary or
ternary systems,[4] high-performance liquid chromatography
coupled with a UV detector (HPLC/UV)[5,6] has been used. The
application of supportive computational methods and predic-
tive models is useful in reducing the experimental work
needed for further and optimized solvent selection. However,
to the best of our knowledge, this has not yet been reported
for the screening of electrolytes for Li–S batteries.
In this context, we report herein the application of the con-
ductor-like screening model for real solvents (COSMO-RS), de-
veloped by Klamt,[7] to compute the physical properties of
mixed liquid phases, such as vapor pressure,[8,9] partition and
activity
coefficients,[10]
phase
equilibria
prediction
of
pKa
values,[11,12] and redox potentials,[13,14] for Li-S battery solvents
and electrolytes. COSMO-RS combines a quantum chemical
(DFT)-based continuum solvation approach for a single mole-
cule with statistical thermodynamics, and has already proven
to be an effective and reliable screening tool in relation to
pharmaceutical drugs,[15,16] industrial separation thermodynam-
ics,[17] and ionic liquids.[18–20] With regard to Li-based battery de-
velopment, and Li-ion and Li-air systems in particular, COSMO-
RS has so far mostly been used for screening large datasets
(102–105) of small molecules as potential electrolyte sol-
vents.[21–26] Basic electronic properties,[21] such as ionization po-
tentials and electron affinities, as well as physicochemical prop-
erties,[22–26] such as viscosity and melting and boiling tempera-
tures, were assessed as selection criteria. The predictions have
provided a sufficient qualitative ranking for a multi-level ap-
proach[22–24,26] or have been used in quantitative structure–
property relationship (QSPR)-based methods[22,25] to find new
electrolytes.

a tool for quantitative computational screening in the design

An essential prerequisite for this approach is a reasonable
molecular structure of the solute, known for sulfur but not for
the various polysulfides formed during Li–S battery operation.
Furthermore, the polysulfides are also salts that release an ad-
ditional and dissociation-dependent concentration of Li+ ions
into the electrolyte. Therefore, the conceptual and computa-
tional approaches for the prediction of polysulfide solubilities
are very different and beyond the scope of this study.
As COSMO-RS was originally developed for neutral com-
pounds or organic ions, such as the bistrifluoromethanesulfoni-
midate (TFSI) anion, alkali ions such as Li+, with a high charge
density, might result in significant errors as the polarization
charge density cannot be compensated by the polarization
charge of the solvent, ultimately causing a misfit charge in the
COSMO-RS thermodynamics.[27] Furthermore, the long-range in-
teractions between ions in dilute electrolytes, as considered in
Debye–Heckel theory, are in reality also contributing, but are
not accounted for in COSMO-RS.[28,29] To resolve these short-
comings, we used organic solvents in step I and two different
methods to describe the binary and ternary electrolytes in
step II: A) an implicit solvation of Li+, and B) an explicit solva-
tion of Li+, in which it is completely replaced by correspond-
ing solvent- and anion-based clusters. In this way, we could
also evaluate the effect of the description of the electrolyte at
the molecular level, both in terms of accuracy and computa-
tional effort.

Referencing
As COSMO-RS has been developed to compute miscibility and
physical properties solely of liquid phases, the calculation of
the solubility of sulfur was divided into two steps: a) virtual
transfer of solid sulfur to a supercooled liquid, and b) mixing
of the supercooled liquid sulfur with the solvent. For step a,
the molar free energy of fusion (DfusG) was required. For
step b, the chemical potentials of pure sulfur (mS) and sulfur
solvated by a solvent (msolv

Benchmarking
Binary electrolytes
In order to verify the reliability of the COSMO-RS approach, the
solubilities of cyclo-S8 in binary electrolytes containing 0.1m
and 1.0m LiTFSI were computed and compared to literature
values.[5] For the implicit solvation approach (approach A),
three components; solvent, Li+, and TFSI, were considered and
their mole fractions were directly calculated using the density
of the corresponding organic solvent (Table 1).
The explicit solvation approach B reduces the peak of the
“free” cation and spreads out the s profile closer to the
s range of @0.02 ea@2 (Figure 2). To maintain constant solven-
t:LiTFSI ratios in both approaches A and B, the contents of the
different clusters in B were predicted from an intermolecular
contact statistics calculation for A. Li+ is exclusively contacted
by a solvent molecule in the 0.1m LiTFSI electrolytes, which
corresponds to a fully dissociated LiTFSI salt (Table 2). The con-
tact statistics calculation was, however, based on the pairwise
combination of molecular surface segments and therefore de-
scribed the formation of [Li(solvent)]+ clusters rather than the
full cation solvation shell. Accordingly, the formation of an
[Li(TFSI)] cluster becomes more likely for the 1.0m LiTFSI elec-
trolytes. Moreover, the formation of [Li(solvent)]+ clusters is
more likely for DMF, DMSO, or Pyr as solvents than for DME,
G2, or PC, due to the charge distributions; the mesomeric >
N+=C@O@ form of DMF, the >S+@O@ form of DMSO, and the
localized lone pair on the nitrogen atom in Pyr, each represent-
ing predominant interaction sites for the lithium cation. Any
potential bidentate coordination of Li+ by DME or G2 is not
accounted for in the contact statistics calculations. Subse-
quently, the mole fractions of all components in a binary elec-
trolyte were recalculated and the cyclo-S8/binary electrolyte
solid–liquid equilibrium was computed to obtain the maximum
cyclo-S8 mole fraction solubility xS.
Table 3 shows the significant improvement in calculated sol-
ubilities for the 0.1m LiTFSI binary electrolyte by using the ex-
plicit solvation approach, reducing the RMSD from 0.8 to
0.4 log units. For the 1.0m LiTFSI binary electrolyte, both
approaches are similarly accurate, but with a slightly better
performance of the implicit solvation approach. For most elec-
trolytes, the cyclo-S8 solubility tends to be overestimated, an
exception being the DMSO-based electrolytes. For neat DMSO,
a cyclo-S8 DfusG value of 9.0 kJmol@1 was determined above,
which is about 3 kJmol@1 lower than the mean value used.
Hence, the solubility might be underestimated due to the
greater energy required for the virtual melting process.
The solubility of cyclo-S8 increases as a function of tempera-
ture for all organic solvents and electrolytes, showing the pro-
cess of sulfur dissolution to be endothermic (Figures 3 and 4,
using the lowest RMSD solvation approach for each).
The solubility trend is: Pyr > DME & G2 > DMF > PC &
DMSO. It is also clear from Figures 3 and 4 that a higher LiTFSI
concentration reduces the sulfur solubility.

All resulting thermodynamic data from Equations (2)–(5) are
listed in Table 6. During the dissolution process, solvent–sol-
vent and solute–solute interactions are broken and solvent–
solute interactions are formed. The positive values for DsolS in-
dicate an overall increase in the degree of freedom. However,
with a contribution of %xH>80% for all of the investigated
solutions, the dissolution process of elemental sulfur is clearly
enthalpy-driven, but endothermic (DsolH>0) and non-sponta-
neous (DsolG>0). As a result, and in accordance with the calcu-
lated absolute solubilities of cyclo-S8, the dissolution process is
broadly favored in the following order: Pyr > G2 & DME >
DMF > DMSO/PC (the order of DMSO and PC varies with the
LiTFSI concentration). For the 0.1m electrolytes, lower DsolG
values were obtained compared to the neat organic solvents,
indicating that the addition of LiTFSI first favors the solubility
of cyclo-S8 and then inhibits it upon further increasing the salt
concentration. This might be attributed to the problems asso-
ciated with computations on dilute electrolytes with COSMO-
RS. However, the experimental solubilities[5] for the neat organ-
ic solvents (Table 1) and the 0.1m LiTFSI electrolytes (Table 3)
differ only marginally (except for Pyr).
A similar analysis of the ternary 1.0m LiTFSI TMS:DME elec-
trolytes revealed an inhibiting effect of TMS on the dissolution
process of cyclo-S8 (Table 7).
Conclusion
Using experimentally determined sulfur solubilities as referen-
ces, the solubility of cyclo-S8 in binary and ternary electrolytes
has been shown by COSMO-RS simulations to be an endother-
mic process facilitated by DME as solvent and increased tem-
perature, but to decrease as a function of LiTFSI concentration.
From a methodological perspective, for salt concentrations
<1m, the explicit solvation approach performs more accurate-
ly, whereas at >1m the implicit solvation approach is more fa-
vorable. Overall, the computed solubilities differ from the ex-
perimental values by <0.4 log units, which is within experi-
mental error and hence indicates an accurate and reliable per-
formance of the COSMO-RS simulations. We thus surmise that
the approach presented here is useful for screening and under-
standing at the molecular level.

package. Geometries were optimized using the BP86 function-

via the COSMOthermX GUI.[45] The solvent screening module was
used to determine DfusG of cyclo-S8, which represents elemental
