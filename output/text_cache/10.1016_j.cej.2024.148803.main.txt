Hit two birds with one stone: A bi-functional selenium-substituted 
organosulfur polymer additive for high-performance 
lithium-sulfur batteries

A B S T R A C T  
Sluggish reaction kinetics of sulfur cathode and high reactivity of lithium anode hinder the commercialization of 
Li-S batteries. Employing the electrolyte additive to regulate the states of active material is a facile and effective 
way to solve the above issues. Herein, the bifunctional selenium substituted lithium polythioctic acid (PTA-Se) 
was prepared as an additive of electrolyte via in-situ polymerization and substitution method. Such additives can 
promote the conversion of polysulfides and facilitate the homogenization of Li-ion flux. The active selenium sites 
provide a kinetically favorable pathway for polysulfide conversion. The introduction of additives also facilitates 
the formation of elastic and uniform solid electrolyte phase interface (SEI) film on the surface of lithium anode. 
As results, Li-S batteries assembled with PTA-Se additive demonstrates a high initial capacity of 1319 mAh g−1 at 
0.2C with 1000 mAh g−1 remaining after 150 cycles, corresponding to a 0.16 % capacity decay per cycle. At a 
high rate of 5C, the cell still achieves a reversible capacity of 517 mAh g−1. This work opens a new avenue for 
designing effective electrolyte additive and achieving high-performance Li-S batteries.   
1. Introduction 
The pursuit of an electrochemical energy storage system with high 
energy density has stimulated research focusing on various new battery 
chemistries beyond lithium-ion technology [1–3]. Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) 
batteries are among the most promising candidates due to the high 
theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1and the low cost of sulfur 
[4–7]. Nevertheless, the practical application of Li-S battery is severely 
hindered by the low electrical conductivity of sulfur species, the disso­
lution and shuttle effect of the intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), 
as well as the uncontrollable dendrite formation on Li anode surface. To 
overcome these intractable obstacles, various strategies have been pro­
posed to prosper the development of Li-S battery [8–10]. The compo­
sition and structure of the electrolytes will play a key role in the 
conversion path of sulfur and the deposition morphology of lithium. The 
electrochemical performance of both the cathode and anode can be 
improved simultaneously through reasonable regulation of the electro­
lyte. The modification methods of electrolytes mainly include adjusting 
the type and concentration of lithium salts and solvents, and introducing 
additives. Among them, adding additives is a feasible and efficient 
approach. A minimal amount of additive can greatly improve the 
rechargeability of the cathode or the anode, which is an important 
means to build high-performance Li-S batteries. 
Electrolyte additives in Li-S batteries are mainly used to promote the 
redox kinetics of sulfur or to form a protective layer for Li metal. In the 
field of Li protection, the most well-known additive is lithium nitrate 
(LiNO3). It can form a stable passivation film on Li surface, and thus 
suppresses the parasitic reactions between the dissolved lithium poly­
sulfides and Li metal and in the meanwhile inhibit the growth of lithium 
dendrites [11–13]. The similar functions are also corroborated in a 
number of other film-forming additives such as Li2S6-P2S5 [14], sulfur- 
containing polymers [15], fluoroethylene carbonate [16], SiCl4 [17], 
nitro-C60 [18], PFPN [19], crown ether [20], etc. Besides, some elec­
trolyte additives can protect lithium metal via forming an electrostatic 
shield [21], regulating the solvation sheath of Li ions [22], and reac­
tivating dead Li by shuttle effect [23].

provide an alternative chemical path for the redox reaction of sulfur to

regulate the dissolution process of polysulfides. Recent advances reveal 
that 1,3,5-benzenetrithiol (BTT) can be used as an electrolyte additive in 
Li-S batteries to realize an exchange reaction on the Li-metal anode and 
electrochemical polymerization on the sulfur cathode [24]. The reaction 
between BTT and sulfur can change the redox path of the sulfur cathode, 
leading to enhanced performance. Similarly, a class of functional ben­
zenedithiols (BDTs) as functional electrolyte additives are investigated 
[25]. The results show that 1,4-BDT is hardly to be self-polymerized due 
to the steric hindrance effect, thus it can combine more sulfur radicals to 
cause oligomerization, suppressing the shuttle effect and stabilize the 
interfaces simultaneously. Apart from that, electrolyte additives like 
sulfur-containing polymer [26] and some organic small molecules 
[27–32] are also proved to react with LiPSs spontaneously as well as 
promote its conversion. However, few studies using sulfur-Selenium- 
coexisting polymers are reported as an electrolyte additive for Li-S 
battery, which might bring out unique effects on the electrochemical 
process. 
In this contribution, a selenium-substituted organosulfur polymer 
additive is introduced to the Li-S battery to accelerate the redox re­
actions of sulfur and render the uniform deposition of lithium simulta­
neously (Fig. 1). Concretely, the polythioctic acid (PTA) is first 
introduced to the Li-S system via the in-situ polymerization of thioctic 
acid in the electrolyte. Then, the long-chain lithium polysulfides are 
brought into the electrolyte to spontaneously form lithium polythioctic 
acid (PTA-S). On this basis, the selenium-substituted lithium poly­
thioctic acid (PTA-Se) is obtained through the substitution of Se atoms 
into the polysulfide chain. On the cathode side, PTA-Se can directly 
change the reaction pathways through in-situ solidification of poly­
sulfides into large organosulfur polymer species, thus immobilizing the 
polysulfides within the cathode side effectively. In the meanwhile, the 
active selenium sites can provide a kinetically favorable pathway for the 
conversion of polysulfide by accelerating the redox transformation of 
the intermediates and promoting the Li2S deposition. The additive 
introduction also facilitates the formation of robust solid electrolyte 
phase interface (SEI) film on the surface of the lithium anode. Conse­
quently, the Li–S cells with PTA-Se additive achieve a significant ca­
pacity retention of 85.6 % for 500 cycles with a reversible capacity of 
667.7 mAh g−1. This strategy illuminates a new approach towards high- 
performance Li-S batteries via introducing electrolyte additives. 
2. Results and discussions 
2.1. Material structural characterization 
The effects of PTA additives with different concentrations were 
compared. As shown in Figure S1, with the increase of the concentration 
from 0 M to 0.03 M, the color of electrolyte is transformed from light 
yellow to dark yellow. The Li/Li symmetric cell with 0.02 M PTA ad­
ditive shows a longest lifespan up to 1000 h and a smaller hysteresis of 
≈18 mV compared to the cells with other proportions of additives 
(Figure S2 and Table S1). Therefore, 0.02 M PTA additive was utilized in 
the subsequent experiments. Fig. 2a shows obvious color contrast be­
tween blank ether electrolytes and the electrolytes with PTA-S/PTA-Se 
additives. The colorless transparent commercial ether electrolyte 
turned yellow after adding additives, and the color of the PTA-Se elec­
trolyte became lighter compared to the PTA-S electrolyte, which can be 
attributed to the substitution of Se atoms into the chain segment of PTA. 
Raman spectroscopy was used to measure the possible molecular 
structure of additives (Fig. 2b). Both PTA-S and PTA-Se electrolytes 
show typical scattering peaks at 445 and 470 cm−1, which can be 
attributed to the S-S bond of PTA. While the characteristics of Se-Se 
bonds at 240 cm−1/587～684 cm−1 and Se-S bonds at 387 cm−1 are 
clearly detected in PTA-Se [29,33], indicating that selenium atoms have 
also been stably introduced into the additives. In addition, solid-state 7Li 
NMR spectroscopy reveals that the chemical shift signals shift toward 
the right after adding the additives (Fig. 2c), which is probably due to 
the interaction between the carboxyl group in thioctic acid and the Li- 
ions. 
To explore the effect of additives on the sulfur cathode, the elec­
trochemical performance of KB/S cathodes with different electrolyte 
were systematically investigated. Typical CV profiles were obtained 
within a potential window of 1.7––2.8 V at various scan rates ranging 
from 0.1 to 0.6 mV s−1 (Figure S3). It can be observed that the shape of 
CV curves based on different electrolyte systems is almost identical, 
whereas there are some shifts in the peak position. According to the

the introduction of selenium atom is beneficial to enhance the internal

polysulfides and lithium sulfide. The phase composition and the ele­

are studied by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The result 
from EDS mapping (Figure S4) indicates the homogeneous dispersion of 
C, F, O, S, and Se elements. It can be inferred that a selenium-containing 
cathode electrolyte interface (CEI) film has been formed on the surface

important role in promoting the transport of Li-ions at the interface and 
maintaining the structural stability of the cathode. Cyclic voltammetry 
(CV) of Li2S6 symmetric cells with different electrolytes was conducted 
to evaluate the conversion kinetics of LiPSs. Fig. 2e shows that the cells 
with either additive exhibit significantly increased peak currents than 
the blank cells, indicating that both PTA-S and PTA-Se additives can 
effectively enhance the redox kinetics. Besides, the cells with PTA-Se 
electrolyte present the highest peak current, illustrating that PTA-Se is 
more powerful than PTA-S in promoting the liquid–liquid conversion of 
LiPSs. Moreover, the cells with PTA-Se electrolyte also display the 
lowest charge transfer resistance in the corresponding electrochemical 
impedance spectra (EIS)test (Fig. 2f), which agrees with the CV results. 
Apart from facilitating redox kinetics of polysulfide in the liquid 
phase, PTA-Se electrolyte also enhances the polysulfide reduction into 
Li2S. The Li2S precipitation profiles were collected to evaluate the ki­
netics of different samples, and the capacity of Li2S conversion was 
calculated based on the quantity of electric charge according to Fara­
day’s law, as shown in Fig. 3. Obviously, the cells with PTA-Se afford the 
highest Li2S precipitation capacity of 179 mAh g−1, whereas the ca­
pacities of the cells with PTA-S and blank electrolyte are 142 and 93 
mAh g−1, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further 
showed the details of the Li2S deposition. In the blank electrolyte and the 
PTA-S electrolyte, the morphology of Li2S deposited on the carbon paper 
is rough and uneven. In contrast, when PTA-Se electrolyte is applied, 
most Li2S can be deposited on the surface of the carbon matrix tightly 
and uniformly. Based on the above results, the kinetic promotion 
capability on the transformation from soluble LiPSs to solid Li2S follows 
the sequence of PTA-Se > PTA-S > blank. 
To visually demonstrate the capability of additives to suppress the 
polysulfide diffusion, an adsorption test was further performed by 
equally adding Li2S6 catholyte into the electrolyte with and without 
additives. Photos were taken after thorough mixing and standing for 24 
h respectively (Figure S5). Unlike the blank electrolyte became dark red 
after adding Li2S6 catholyte, a yellowish color was observed in the 
electrolytes with additives. In addition, solid precipitation appears in the 
solution after standing in the latter. The precipitation may be caused by 
the chemical reaction between the LiPSs and the formation of a solid 
organosulfur polymer. Through the in-situ solidification of polysulfides, 
the shuttle effect can be effectively inhibited and the integrity of the 
sulfur cathode can be well retained. Further observation denotes that in 
the PTA-Se electrolyte, the color is lighter and the amount of solid 
organosulfur polymer is higher compared to its PTA-S counterpart 
electrolyte. All these results manifest that the conversion of polysulfides 
is faster and more complete in the PTA-Se system. 
2.2. Electrochemical reaction mechanism 
To explore the effect of electrolyte additives on lithium metal anode, 
the electrochemical performance of Li plating/stripping is investigated 
by Li-Li symmetric cells. First, the symmetric cells with different elec­
trolytes were tested at 5 mA cm−2, and 20 mAh cm−2 (Figure S6). The 
cells with the PTA-Se and PTA-S electrolyte show a prolonged plating/ 
stripping lifetime of 3500 and 2500 h respectively with a relatively 
stable polarization voltage, whereas, in the blank electrolyte, the cells 
underwent rapidly increased polarization and short-circuit, which might 
be caused by the dead or dendritic lithium. When the current density 
and areal capacity are increased to 8 mA cm−2 and 40 mAh cm−2, as 
shown in Fig. 4a, the symmetrical cell with PTA-Se electrolyte exhibits 
stable voltage profiles with a small overpotential of 60 mV and an 
ultralong lifespan of 4500 h. In contrast, the counterparts of blank 
electrolyte and PTA-S electrolyte show a fluctuant voltage profile with a 
much larger overpotential, which could be attributed to the unstable SEI 
layer and repeated growth/corrosion of Li dendrites. To survey the 
evolution of the voltage profiles in detail, the 400th and 4000th cycles 
were magnified as the insets in Fig. 4a. Obviously, the symmetrical cell 
with PTA-Se electrolyte displayed flat charge–discharge profiles with 
much smaller overpotential than that of symmetrical cells with PTA-S 
electrolyte or blank electrolyte. These results indicate the substantially 
improved kinetics and reversibility by adding PTA-Se additive. The 
surface and cross-sectional morphology of cycled Li metal anodes were 
further investigated via ex situ SEM (Figure S7). After 40 mAh cm−2 Li 
deposition, a porous and dendritic texture is observed on the Li surface 
in the blank electrolyte. For the Li anode in the PTA-S electrolyte, the 
surface remains flat but presents a linear loose crimp morphology after 
Li plating/stripping. In contrast, the surface of the Li electrode in the 
PTA-Se electrolyte remains uniform and compact. Besides, it shows a 
more uniform and compact cross-section after lithium deposition 
compared to the others. Thus, the PTA-Se electrolyte additive is bene­
ficial to suppress the lithium dendrite formation throughout extended 
cycles. 
A series of tests were used to study the electrochemical nature of the 
PTA-Se electrolyte. The ionic conductivities for the different electrolytes 
were tested in symmetric coin cells with stainless steel (SS) foils as 
electrodes (Fig. 4b). They are calculated as 2.11, 1.38, and 1 mS cm−1 
for the PTA-Se electrolyte, PTA-S electrolyte, and blank electrolyte, 
respectively. The ionic conductivity of the PTA-Se electrolyte is more 
than twice that of the blank electrolyte, which might originate from the 
lithium-rich sites in the additives which promotes the fast Li-ion trans­
fer. Furthermore, the Li-ion transferences are calculated from the I-t 
curves and the impedance spectrums before and after polarization for 
different electrolytes (Figure S8 and Table S2). The PTA-Se electrolyte 
demonstrates the highest Li-ion transference number of 0.67, much 
higher than those of 0.56 for the PTA-S electrolyte and 0.53 for the blank 
electrolyte. The enhancement might come from the formation of a 
beneficial SEI layer, which enables fast Li-ion transport. Tafel plots 
(Figure S9) were employed to evaluate the kinetic difference of Li 
deposition in different electrolytes. The corresponding exchange current 
density (j0) obtained from the intercept demonstrates a higher j0 for the 
PTA-Se electrolyte (2.66 mA cm−2) and PTA-S electrolyte (1.61 mA 
cm−2) than that for the blank electrolyte (0.06 mA cm−2), indicating the 
PTA-Se can promote the Li deposition kinetics effectively. 
To verify the previous conjecture, the Li-Cu cells were assembled and 
cycled under different current densities. From Figure S10, at a current 
density of 1 mA cm−2 with an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2, the Li-Cu 
cells in PTA-Se electrolyte delivers more stable electrochemical 
cycling and longer cycle life for near 600 cycles. When the deposition 
capacity goes up to 4 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 4c), the Coulombic efficiency (CE) 
in PTA-Se remains at 98.7 % after 400 cycles, whereas the CE in the 
blank electrolyte and PTA-S electrolyte quickly drops below 80 % after 
only 60 cycles and 170 cycles, respectively. The same pattern is noticed 
in a higher current density of 2 mA cm−2, as displayed in Fig. 4d. The Li- 
Cu cells in PTA-Se electrolyte can maintain stability for 100 cycles, 
which is much better than that of the PTA-S electrolyte and blank 
electrolyte. More intuitive shreds of evidence for traits of the PTA-Se 
electrolyte were present in the morphological change after cell opera­
tion (Figure S11). Compared with the counterparts, the cycled Li anode 
in the PTA-Se electrolyte shows a uniform surface without apparent 
dendrite formation. The contrast is even more obvious in the cross- 
section SEM image. After 4 mAh cm−2 Li deposition, the anode in the 
PTA-Se electrolyte demonstrates a compact and thinner Li layer with a 
thickness of 20 µm, which is much thinner than those of the PTA-S 
electrolyte (29 µm) and the blank electrolyte (58 µm). This could be

The Li deposition evolution in different electrolytes was visualized 
by in situ optical microscopy, as illustrated in Figure S12. In the blank 
electrolyte, due to the uneven distribution of Li-ion flux, the Li-ion will 
gather locally on the Cu substrate, and gradually grow into fluffy bulk 
lithium. After 60 min, obvious dendrites are observed in the blank 
electrolyte. In the PTA-S electrolyte, the uniformity of lithium deposi­
tion is substantially improved, but a small amount of cluster lithium still 
can be observed. As to the PTA-Se electrolyte system, benefited from the 
smoothing effect and protective SEI layer, the Li surface remain smooth 
and free of dendrites. The result directly corroborates the regulation 
effect of the PTA-Se additive on Li-ion deposition, which well matches 
the electrochemical performance. 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is further employed to 
investigate the components of the SEI film formed on the lithium anode 
surface in different electrolytes during cycling. According to the C 1s and 
O 1s spectrums (Figure S13), after adding PTA-Se, the C-O species peak 
is apparently strengthened, indicating the selenium-substituted orga­
nosulfur polymer takes part in the generation of SEI. The elastic polymer 
chain segment is beneficial to enhance the interfacial stability. Mean­
while, in the O 1s and Li 1s spectrums (Fig. 4e), the SEI film produced in 
the blank electrolyte contains abundant Li alkyl carbonates (ROCOOLi) 
and carbonate (Li2CO3) components that originated from the decom­
position of the electrolyte. After introducing the PTA-Se additive, the 
alleviation of the Li2CO3 signal as well as the enhancement of the Li3N 
signal indicate the newly formed SEI layer can effectively reduce the 
interfacial side reaction, and the augment of LiF content is instructive to 
impede the growth of lithium dendrites [34]. Moreover, the new signal 
of SeO3
2−(165 eV and 58.4 eV) appears in the S 2p + Se 3p and Li 1s + Se 
3d spectrums (Fig. 4f) [35], which is mainly come from the oxidation of 
PTA-Se. This phenomenon further verifies that the additives participate 
in the SEI formation, and the introduction of Se is also conducive to the 
improvement of conductivity in sulfur-rich SEI. Notably, in addition to 
optimizing the Li growth behavior, PTA-Se can also affect the SEI for­
mation via adjusting the composition, giving rise to the uniform Li 
deposition. 
To explore the roles of the functionalized additives in the electro­
chemical performance improvement of Li–S batteries, CR2032-type coin 
cells with different electrolytes were assembled and evaluated. The 
traditional KB/S cathode and the STI composite cathode were prepared 
with a sulfur content of 70 wt% and 89 wt%, respectively, as determined 
by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; Figure S14). Figure S15 shows the 
charge/discharge behavior of the carbon paper in electrolytes with ad­
ditives. Obviously, the additives boost the discharge ability and 
reversibility of the sulfur species, but they do not contribute to the 
overall capacity. Fig. 5a shows the cycling performance of the Li-KB/S 
batteries with different electrolytes (0.5C). It can be clearly noticed 
that the cell in the PTA-Se electrolyte delivers the highest cycling sta­
bility with a capacity retention of 667.7 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 
0.5C, corresponding to an ultralow capacity fading rate of 0.036% per 
cycle, which outperforms the results of recent work of additives in Li-S 
batteries (Table S3). By contrast, the cells in PTA-S electrolyte and blank 
electrolyte can only retain 542.1 (fading rate of 0.103 %) and 362.7 mAh 
g−1 (fading rate of 0.166 %) respectively after 500 cycles, which reveals 
the largely improved sulfur utilization and reaction efficiency in the 
PTA-Se electrolyte. Fig. 5b presents the rate performances of the KB/S 
cathodes which can deliver reversible capacities of 1284.3, 1181.4, 
994.6, 879.2, 689.9, and 517.8 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3C, 
respectively. However, the KB/S cathodes using the blank electrolyte 
and PTA-S electrolyte deliver much lower reversible capacities at 
different rates. The corresponding charge–discharge curves under 
different rates are presented in Figure S16. The cell based on the PTA-Se 
electrolyte maintains the typical two-plateau voltage profile even at 
high rates, while with the other electrolytes, suffers severe profile 
deformation upon an increase in the current rate.

3. Conclusion 
A multifunctional PTA-Se organosulfur polymer, acting as an addi­
tive, is constructed to stabilize both the S cathodes and Li anodes via a 
facile in situ polymerization and substitution method. In the cathode 
side, the PTA-Se can not only chemically react with LiPSs to generate 
solid organosulfur polymer, which changes the redox path of sulfur and 
prevents the sulfur shuttle effect, but also the Se atoms promises faster 
diffusion kinetics and improves rate capability. In the anode side, the 
PTA-Se containing electrolyte features a high ionic conductivity and fast 
Li-ion diffusion kinetics, which can facilitate the fast transport of Li ion 
on the interface and guarantees uniform Li deposition. In the mean­
while, the organic component in additive decomposes and forms a 
robust and resilient SEI layer on Li surface that accommodates the 
electrode volume variation. As a result, the Li anode with PTA-Se ad­
ditive enables superior Li plating/stripping performance in symmetric 
cells that sustain long-term cycling of more than 4500 h at a high areal 
capacity of 8 mAh cm−2; the Li–S batteries protected by PTA-Se additive 
realizes a remarkable cycling performance with a capacity of a capacity 
of 569.8 mAh g−1 after 900 cycles at 2C, and a great rate capability up to 
5C. Even at a low E/S ratio of 7 μL mg−1, the high sulfur content cathode 
with a S loading of 5.1 mg cm−2 still exhibits a high capacity of 688.2 
mAh g−1.This work affords an ingenious interface reaction strategy for 
the development of advanced electrolyte additive in Li–S batteries, 
which is referential and instructive to the cost-efficient electrolyte en­
gineering technique that allows for stable battery operation in practical 
scenarios.
