battery
Zhenyu Chen1 · Zhenyu Du1 · Tianxiang Wang1 · Lei Chen2

Abstract
Lithium-sulfur (Li/S) batteries using tetrahydrofuran (THF) as an electrolyte solvent have high discharge capacity. In this 
study, we investigate the effect of THF as an electrolyte solvent on the performance of Li/S batteries. Charge/discharge tests 
show that Li/S batteries employing THF as an electrolyte solvent have higher discharge specific capacity than those employ-
ing 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethyl ether (DME) at low current densities. The reaction rate of S with Li metal and the 
shuttle effect of polysulfide ions is lower in THF than in DME. Compared to DME and DOL, THF has a higher dielectric 
constant, and its solvation ability is weaker than that of DME, which is more conducive to the deposition of lithium ions. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy shows that the charge transfer resistance of symmetric Li metal batteries in THF is 
lower than that in DOL/DME. THF forms a gel layer that restrains the shuttle effect during battery discharge and increases 
battery impedance. The addition of DOL to THF can effectively inhibit the formation of a gel layer and improve the coulombic 
efficiency of the system. Finally, ternary mixed solvents are prepared, and batteries with the new electrolyte solvent reveal 
better discharge capacity and higher coulombic efficiency than those with the original solvents. Taking the results together, 
THF appears to be a promising solvent for Li/S batteries.
Keywords  Energy storage and conversion · Lithium-sulfur batteries · Electrolyte · Solvent · Tetrahydrofuran
Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li/S) cells represent a promising recharge-
able battery system with energy densities higher than that 
of conventional Li-ion batteries. Elemental S has a high 
theoretical specific capacity of approximately 1675 mAh/g 
at 2.1 V relative to Li/Li+, which corresponds to an energy 
density of 2600 Wh/kg based on weight [1, 2]. Despite its 
theoretical advantages, however, Li/S also presents certain 
drawbacks. A major disadvantage of this material combina-
tion is the dissolution of intermediate polysulfides during the 
charge/discharge process and their reaction with the Li metal 
anode, which results in low coulombic efficiency and rapid 
capacity fading [3, 4]. Many studies have focused on C/S 
composites to absorb polysulfides and increase their electric 
conductivity; several polymers have also been investigated 
[5–8]. Another approach is to form an interlayer or protec-
tive layer in the cell system to mitigate the reaction of Li 
anode and polysulfides. The formation of an interlayer or 
protective layer in the cell system is one approach used to 
mitigate the reaction of Li anodes and polysulfides [9, 10]. 
The development of an electrolyte formulation that could 
inhibit polysulfide dissolution and the redox shuttling effect, 
such as electrolyte additives, Li salts, electrolytes consisting 
of ionic liquids, solid-state electrolytes, and new organic 
solvents, has been proposed [11, 12]. While these efforts 
have improved the performance of Li/S batteries, they still 
cannot solve the problems described above.
Solvents play a key role in the electrochemical proper-
ties of Li/S batteries [13, 14]. Many studies have been 
conducted to understand the roles of solvents. However, 
the effects of solvent components on the electrochemical 
properties of Li/S system remain unclear. The electrolyte 
formulation used for Li-ion batteries is unsuitable for 
Li/S batteries because carbonates react with polysulfides 
[15, 16]. The main electrolyte solvents used in today’s

Li/S cells are 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxy ethane 
(DME). DOL can form a stable solid electrolyte interface 
(SEI) on the surface of Li metal, with using ­LiNO3, which 
can immensely inhibit the shuttle effect [17, 18]. Poly-
sulfides are highly soluble in DME, which provides good 
kinetics to improve the cathode operation [19, 20]. The 
combination of two electrolyte solvents results in syner-
gistic effects on S utility and cycle life. However, these 
combinations present some disadvantages. For example, 
DOL has low kinetics and presents poor polysulfide solu-
bility. Moreover, the discharge product in DOL-rich sol-
vents is ­Li2S2, which results in a low S specific capacity 
[21]. DME is reactive toward Li metal and depleted dur-
ing the charge/discharge process [22]; its high polysulfide 
solubility also leads to a significant shuttle effect. The use 
of tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the main electrolyte solvent 
in Li/S cells has been investigated in previous studies [23, 
24]. Researchers have found that the discharge efficiency 
of S exceeds 95% in THF-toluene solution [25]. Li metal 
is kinetically stable in THF owing to the properties of the 
corrosion products [26]. However, THF solvent presents 
a number of drawbacks, such as low rate capability and 
the shuttle effect [25]. When THF is used as the solvent 
and ­LiClO4 is used as the Li salt, high electrolyte viscos-
ity is obtained [25]. When DOL and DME are used as the 
electrolyte solvent in Li/S cells, and bis(trifluoromethane)
sulfonimide Li (LiTFSI) and ­LiNO3 are introduced to the 
electrolyte, high S utility and coulombic efficiency are 
achieved [27, 28], Previous studies have focused on the 
dielectric constant of solvents. In recent research, however, 
interest has shifted to the solubility of solvents.
THF has long been utilized in the research of lithium-
sulfur batteries, with a particular focus during the 1970s 
to 1980s. However, as DME and DOL emerged in lith-
ium-sulfur battery research, THF was quickly overshad-
owed and largely forgotten. Yet, with the progression of 
research, the shortcomings of these solvents have become 
evident. DME can lead to severe shuttle effects and is not 
stable against metallic lithium, being continuously con-
sumed during the battery’s charge–discharge cycles, which 
is especially detrimental at low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratios. 
On the other hand, DOL can suppress the shuttle effect and 
provide excellent protection for metallic lithium, but its 
excessive use is not conducive to enhancing sulfur utiliza-
tion. In this study, we evaluated the differences between 
THF and mixtures of DOL/DME when used as electrolytes 
in Li/S batteries. THF, with its high dielectric constant, 
can improve the discharge specific capacity of sulfur at 
low current densities without causing excessive shuttle 
effects. We believe that these advantages position THF 
to be used in conjunction with other solvents like DME 
and DOL as a co-solvent to enhance the performance of 
lithium-sulfur batteries.
Experimental
The electrolyte used for cell testing was composed of 
1 mol/L LiTFSI (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in solvent 
composed of one organic solvent or a mixture of organic 
solvents with ­LiNO3 as an additive. DOL, DME, THF, 
and ­LiNO3 were purchased from Aladdin. The solvents 
were dried over 4 Å molecular sieves for 1 week prior to 
their use, and the electrolyte was prepared in an Ar-filled 
glove box.
The cathode slurry was prepared by mixing S powder 
(Sigma-Aldrich) with Super P Li (TIMCAL) and LA132 
(Indigo) at a mass ratio of 6:3:1 in water using a ball-
milling machine. The slurry was painted on an Al foil and 
dried at 50 °C in a vacuum for 4 h. Then, the Al foil was 
cut into round pieces and used as the cathode for the Li/S 
batteries. The sulfur areal loading is 3 mg/cm2; the thick-
ness of the electrode is 80 µm, which does not include 
the thickness of the aluminum foil. In an Ar-filled glove-
box, a CR2032 coin cell was used to assemble a half-cell; 
here, metallic Li was used as the counter electrode, and 
a Celgard 2400 microporous membrane was used as the 
separator. The charge/discharge performance of all half-
cells was evaluated by using a BTS-5 V/5 mA battery test 
system (Neware) at the voltage range of 1.8–2.8 V. Elec-
trochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed 
over frequency sweeps between 1 MHz and 10 mHz by 
using an electrochemical workstation (CIMPS-2, Zahner) 
and an amplitude of 5 mV.
MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS 
simulation package [29]. The Optimized Parameters for 
Liquid Simulations all-atom force-field (OPLS-AA) was 
applied [30]. The molecules are randomly inserted into 
the simulation box and undergo an energy minimization, 
followed by a simulated maintenance by the Berendsen 
thermostat at 298 K within a time interval of 2 ns in the 
NVT ensemble. In the NPT ensemble, the simulations are 
carried out at a constant pressure of 1 bar and temperature 
298 K. The pressure is controlled by the Parrinello–Rah-
man barostat with the time constant of 5 ps. The produc-
tion simulations are carried out at a constant pressure of 
1 bar with the time constant of 100 ps, whereas the tem-
perature is controlled by the Nose–Hoover thermostat at 
298 K.

that of THF, the two oxygen atoms of the former can form 
bidentate coordination, when lithium salt is dissolved, the 
solvent molecules expended in the coordination are less. 
Therefore, DME mixed solvent can dissolve large amounts 
of ­LiNO3 or Li polysulfides. By comparison, as only one 
oxygen atom exists in the THF molecule, many solvent 
molecules are expended during coordination with Li ions; 
thus, the solubility of ­LiNO3 in THF is low. To achieve a 
higher coulombic efficiency, we opted for a lithium nitrate 
concentration of 0.4 mol/L in the design of other electro-
lytes. However, due to the limited solubility of lithium 
nitrate in THF, we were constrained to use a concentration 
of only 0.2 mol/L.
Charge/discharge tests are conducted using TE01 and 
DDE01 electrolytes to compare the effect of these solvents 
on the discharge performance of the batteries. As shown 
in Fig. 1, the discharge capacities of cells in batteries with 
two different electrolytes are approximately identical at the 
current density of 100 mA/g. At 25 mA/g, the discharge 
capacity of TE01 is significantly higher than that of DDE01. 
The discharge capacity of the battery using DDE01 is 1081 
mAh/g, while that of the battery using TE01 is 1388 mAh/g. 
This difference could be attributed to the presence of DOL in 
the former system. In the present experiment, the electrodes 
used do not employ special carbon materials or other com-
posite materials; thus, they do not form a high liquid-rich 
state. Because DOL can inhibit the discharge depth of S, part 
of the ­Li2S2 cannot continue to discharge to produce ­Li2S 
[32]. This effect is evident when a high liquid-rich state is 
not formed in the cathode. Therefore, the discharge capac-
ity of the DDE01 electrolyte battery is lower than that of 
the TE01 battery at low current density. At a current den-
sity of 200 mA/g, the initial discharge voltages of the bat-
teries using two different electrolytes were both relatively 
low. However, there was an improvement in the discharge 
voltages during the second cycle for both. In the second 
cycle, the battery with DDE01 electrolyte achieved a dis-
charge specific capacity of 825 mAh/g, while the battery 
with TE01 electrolyte reached a discharge specific capacity 
of 963 mAh/g. Utilizing THF as the electrolyte solvent can 
more effectively enhance the battery’s discharge specific 
capacity at high current densities.
The shuttle effect is another factor that should be inves-
tigated. We add S and Li to different solvents at a 3:1 molar 
ratio and place them for 2 h or 2 days to observe the activity 
in different solvents, as shown in Fig. 2. The reaction rate 
of S and Li occurs fastest in pure DME. The solution color 
remarkably darkens after 2 h, indicating that high concentra-
tions of Li polysulfides are produced. Although dielectric 
constant and donor number of THF are higher, the reaction 
rate of S and Li metal in THF is lower than that in DME but 
higher than that in the DOL/DME mixed solvent; among 
the solvent systems investigated, DOL produces the slowest 
reaction rate between S and Li. DOL can form an SEI film 
on the surface of Li metal, thereby delaying the reaction rate 
between S and the metal. In the DOL/DME mixed solvents, 
DME has strong solubilizing ability because of its bidentate 
property, which helps increase the discharge capacity of S. 
DOL can thus inhibit the shuttle effect, weaken Li corrosion, 
and improve the coulombic efficiency and cycle stability of 
the batteries.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the specific capacity of the battery at 
the first discharge is basically identical in both electrolytes. 
In the subsequent charging process, however, the charging 
capacity of DE01 is higher, and the discharge capacity of this 
electrolyte in the second cycle is lower than that of TE01. 
This finding shows that a more serious shuttle effect occurs 
in DE01. During the charge/discharge cycle, the coulombic 
efficiency of DE01 is constantly less than 100%, as shown 
in Fig. 3b, and the efficiency of the first cycle is 91.25%. In 
TE01, the coulombic efficiency of the first seven cycles is 
constantly higher than 100%. The coulombic efficiency of 
the batteries with the two electrolytes is 97% in the 13th 
cycle, which shows that, under the same ­LiNO3 concentra-
tion, the shuttle effect in THF is easier to control, although 
this effect is obvious in the two solvents.
We believe that the rate of the shuttle effect during the 
charging process is primarily determined by the concentra-
tion of polysulfides. At a given concentration of polysulfides, 
or at a specific state of charge, the speed of the shuttle effect 
for a particular battery is fixed. With this understanding, 
we conducted continuous charging tests at different cur-
rent densities within the same unit, with each charging time 
exceeding the theoretical capacity of the battery. A portion 
of the charging capacity is consumed by the shuttle effect, 
while another part is used to increase the state of charge of 
the cathode. For a given battery, we consider the speed of 
the shuttle effect to be constant. Only when the charging 
rate significantly exceeds the shuttle effect can the battery 
be fully charged in a shorter time. Utilizing this principle, 
we incrementally increased the battery’s charging current 
density to determine the current density at which the battery 
can be quickly charged. Through this approach, we assess 
the strength of the battery’s shuttle effect. We conduct a 
charge test with variable currents to compare the shuttle 
effect in the two solvents and show the results in Fig. 3c. 
The electrolytes used in the comparison are DE02 and TE02. 
No ­LiNO3 is used in the two electrolytes. In the absence of 
­LiNO3 and DOL, the charging capacity of the Li/S batter-
ies is consumed by the shuttle effect, causing difficulties in 
filling them. The batteries can be fully charged when the 
charging current is greater than the shuttle effect. In the 
present test, the batteries are discharged to 1.8 V at a cur-
rent density of 100 mA/g (relative to S) and then charged at 
different current densities. In this case, charging cannot be 
used as a quantitative measurement of the shuttle effect but 
can effectively reflect the strength of the shuttle effect in two 
different electrolytes. The test results show that the shuttle 
effect in DME is significantly stronger than that in THF, 
consistent with the results in Fig. 2. The use of THF as an 
electrolyte solvent yields good discharge specific capacity at 
low current densities, and the shuttle effect in THF is lower 
than that in DME. The strong shuttle effect in DME could 
be attributed to the strong dissolving ability of DME due to 
its bidentate nature. High DOL contents in the electrolyte 
are required to inhibit the shuttle effect.

The solvation structures of the two electrolytes at a salt 
concentration of 1.0 mol/L were also examined. Due to the 
distinct solvation abilities of the solvent molecules, the ratio 
of solvent molecule oxygen to anion in the primary solvation 
shell varies considerably (as shown in Fig. 5c and d). In the 
case of the TE01 electrolyte, the anion count is considerably 
higher than that of solvent oxygen, suggesting a stronger ten-
dency for lithium ions to coordinate with anions. In contrast, 
in the DDE01 electrolyte solution, Li + ions predominantly 
coordinate with solvent molecules. DME, with its chelat-
ing coordination ability, has a higher solvation capacity for 
lithium ions (as depicted in Fig. 5e), which is beneficial for 
achieving higher ionic conductivity. However, the strong 
interaction between DME and Li + ions impedes the des-
olvation process of Li, preventing uniform lithium deposi-
tion. Due to its weaker solvation capability, THF is outcom-
peted by anions during the solvation process (as indicated in 
Fig. 5f). This reduced solvation strength is more favorable 
for the desolvation process during lithium deposition.

literature [33] indicates that THF as an electrolyte solvent 
is polymerized during ring opening of the anode to form 
a gel layer. The arc in the AC impedance curve represents 
the passage of Li ions through the gel layer. This gel layer 
helps inhibit the diffusion of polysulfide ions to the negative 
electrode but increases the diffusion impedance of Li ions.
Through the ring-opening polymerization of THF, a gel 
layer is formed; this layer attenuates the shuttle effect and 
increases the impedance of electrochemical reactions. DOL, 
characterized by its propensity to undergo ring-opening, 
forms a dense SEI (solid electrolyte interphase) film, which 
protects the lithium metal anode and suppresses the shuttle 
effect. We aim to combine the advantages of THF and DOL 
to obtain an electrolyte solution with an even weaker shuttle 
effect. Consequently, we prepared TDE01 and assessed the 
electrochemical performance of coin cells using TDE01 to 
validate this concept. Figures 7a and b present the results of 
EIS measurement of batteries using TDE01. The addition 
of DOL cannot effectively reduce the charge transfer resist-
ance of the battery but can effectively weaken the gel layer. 
As shown in Fig. 7c, the coulombic efficiency of the battery 
significantly increases to approximately 100% after the addi-
tion of DOL. This finding indicates that SEI films formed 
by DOL and THF can effectively prevent the shuttle effect. 
After the addition of DOL, the capacity decay rate of the 
battery during the first 40 cycles significantly decreases. In 
subsequent cycles, the capacity is reduced to a level identical 
to that in batteries using TE01. Thus, the addition of DOL 
decreases the solubility of THF and inhibits the discharge 
depth of TDE01.

cycle capacity of Li/S batteries. DME is added to a THF 
and DOL mixture as a cosolvent., and the electrochemical

Nyquist and Bode diagrams of the battery. AC impedance 
results show that the addition of DME does not disturb 
DOL’s inhibition of the gel layer formed by THF and effec-

TDE01; moreover, the coulombic efficiency of the former
