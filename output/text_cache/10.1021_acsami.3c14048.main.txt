ABSTRACT: By enhancing the stability of the lithium metal
anode and mitigating the formation of lithium dendrites through
electrolyte design, it becomes feasible to extend the lifespan of
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries. One widely accepted approach
involves the utilization of Li[N(SO2F)2] (Li[FSA]), which holds
promise in stabilizing the lithium anode by facilitating the
formation of an inorganic-dominant solid electrolyte interface
(SEI) film. However, the use of Li[FSA] encounters limitations
due to inevitable side reactions between lithium polysulfides
(LiPSs) and [FSA] anions. In this study, our focus lies in precisely
controlling the composition of the SEI film and the morphology of
the deposited lithium, as these two critical factors profoundly influence lithium reversibility. Specifically, by subjecting an initial
charging process to an elevated temperature, we have achieved a significant enhancement in lithium reversibility. This improvement
is accomplished through the employment of a LiPS sparingly solvating electrolyte with a restricted Li[FSA] content. Notably, these
optimized conditions have resulted in an enhanced cycling performance in practical Li−S pouch cells. Our findings underscore the
potential for improving the cycling performance of Li−S batteries, even when confronted with challenging constraints in electrolyte
design.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, lithium metal anode, solid electrolyte interphase, lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)amide,
sparingly solvating electrolyte
1. INTRODUCTION
Over the past few decades, substantial progress has been
achieved in the advancement of lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries.
However, as energy demands continue to rise in sectors such as
long-distance electrified transportation and the stationary
storage of sustainable energy, Li-ion batteries are approaching
their limits in terms of energy density and struggling to keep
up.1 Therefore, the pursuit of alternative systems that can
outperform conventional Li-ion batteries has become crucial.
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, presenting various advan-
tages as potential alternatives, have emerged as a promising
solution due to the following factors. Elemental sulfur, which is
abundant, cost-effective, and nontoxic, boasts a high specific
capacity of 1672 mAh g−1. This makes it an attractive choice as
a cathode active material for lithium batteries.2,3 Furthermore,
the combination of sulfur and metallic lithium electrodes yields
the highest theoretical energy density among solid electro-
active materials, reaching up to 2600 Wh kg−1 and 2800 Wh
L−1.4 These characteristics position Li−S batteries as a
promising option for more efficient and durable energy storage
systems. However, despite their immense potential, the
progress of Li−S battery technology has been impeded by
significant challenges. These challenges stem from the
instability of the lithium anode, the lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) shuttle effect, the insulating nature of sulfur and its
discharge products, and the volumetric changes of the
electroactive material during charge/discharge cycles.5−8
Overcoming these obstacles is pivotal to fully unlocking the
potential of Li−S batteries.
The electrolyte solution assumes a crucial role in Li−S
batteries, actively participating in the conversion reactions of
both lithium and sulfur and effectively addressing the
challenges mentioned above. Specifically, concerning the
lithium anode, the electrolyte solution plays a paramount
role in curbing dendrite growth, facilitating effective
passivation through the formation of a solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI), and minimizing the impact of side reactions.
All these factors are crucial for ensuring the long-term cycling
performance of Li−S batteries.9,10 Therefore, researchers are
dedicated to enhancing the stability of the lithium metal anode
and mitigating the aforementioned challenges by thoughtfully

selecting and designing the electrolyte solution. This endeavor
involves the exploration of novel electrolyte compositions,
additives, and solvents that have the potential to enhance the
overall performance and durability of Li−S batteries.11−14
Our research group has introduced a solution involving a
highly concentrated lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
amide (Li[TFSA]) in the solvent sulfolane (SL), denoted as
[Li(SL)2][TFSA], which forms a sparingly solvating electrolyte
for polysulfides.15 This electrolyte exhibits a remarkably low
solubility for lithium LiPSs. Additionally, by introducing the
cosolvent 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl
ether (HFE) and diluting the solution, the solubility of LiPS
can be further minimized.15 Nonetheless, it is imperative to
address the inherent irreversible side reaction between SL and
the lithium anode, which leads to poor lithium reversibil-
ity.16,17 To enhance performance, the utilization of a “magic
salt” known as lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)amide (Li[FSA]) as
the electrolyte salt has shown improved results due to the
beneficial formation of an SEI through Li[FSA] decom-
position.18 We have clarified that the reaction of SL with a
metallic lithium electrode yields tetrahydrothiophene in the
electrolyte and lithium oxide (LiO2) on the lithium surface by
online electrochemical mass spectroscopy and cryogenic
transmission electron microscopy-electron energy-loss spec-
troscopy (TEM-EELS) measurements.17 Such side reactions
are greatly suppressed when Li[FSA] is used as a salt,
compared with Li[TFSA].17 Furthermore, reduction potential
and current of imide anions (especially [FSA] anion) are
greatly upshifted and enlarged, respectively, in an SL-based
high-concentration-electrolyte (HCE) and the HCE diluted by
HFE (localized high-concentration-electrolyte, LHCE).19 The
[FSA] anion reduction (SEI formation) in the first sweep was
accelerated at 60 °C, compared with that at 30 °C, which was
revealed by slow-scan voltammetry. The SEI formation was
repeatedly followed in the following cycles.19 This facilitates
the formation and maintenance of inorganic-rich SEI and
enhances the reversibility of lithium deposition/dissolution
reactions for the SL-based HCE and LHCE containing
Li[FSA].19 These behaviors are reproduced by DFT-MD
simulation in terms of the projected density of states (PDOS)
calculations and the anion decomposition reaction simula-
tion.19
An increase in the composition of Li[FSA] in Li[TFSA]/
Li[FSA] binary salt electrolytes would enhance lithium
reversibility and extend the cycle life by continual reduction
of the [FSA] anion during subsequent cycling. However, it is
important to note that an excessively high concentration of
[FSA] anions is not practical due to potential side reactions
with LiPSs.20,21 For instance, certain prior studies employed an
electrolyte with a molar ratio of Li[FSA]/Li[TFSA] set at 1:8,
resulting in improved Li coulombic efficiency (CE) compared
to that of Li[TFSA] electrolytes. Yet, an increase in the
Li[FSA] content within the binary salt leads to rapid capacity
decay.22 Therefore, it becomes crucial to explore methods that
enhance lithium anode stability while working within the
confines of the available Li[FSA] quantity.
In this study, we utilized a sparingly solvated polysulfide
solution with a limited proportion of Li[FSA] as the electrolyte
system ([Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE). Through meticu-
lous optimization of the SEI film and the manipulation of
deposited lithium morphology via an initial charging process at
an elevated temperature, we successfully attained enhanced
cycling performance in practical Li−S pouch cells.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Fabrication of S/MCND Cathodes. A ternary composite
was prepared by mixing 75 wt % elemental sulfur (S8, Sigma-Aldrich)
and 25 wt % mesoporous carbon nano-dendrite (MCND, Nippon
Steel Chemical & Material) using an agate mortar and pestle. The
resulting composite was then subjected to heat treatment at 155 °C
for 12 h within an Ar-sealed borosilicate glass vial. Subsequently, an
aqueous slurry was prepared by mixing 96.5 wt % S/MCND
composite, 2 wt % styrene−butadiene rubber (SBR, JSR Corpo-
ration), and 1.5 wt % carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC2200, Daicel
FineChem) with a requisite quantity of deionized water. This
resulting slurry was blade-cast onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil
(thickness = 15 μm, Showa Denko) and then dried overnight at 40
°C. The dried cathode obtained was then cut into circular disks (for
coin cells) or rectangular shapes (for pouch cells) and further dried at
45 °C for 10 h under a vacuum before usage. The average areal
loading of sulfur in the S/MCND cathodes was adjusted to be 5.0 mg
cm−2.
2.2. Preparation of Electrolytes. For the preparation of
electrolytes, an initial step involved the preparation of a highly
concentrated electrolyte by dissolving Li[TFSA] (Solvay) and
Li[FSA] (Nippon Shokubai) in a molar ratio of 0.8:0.2 within twice
the molar amount of SL (Kishida Chemical). This concentrated
electrolyte is denoted as [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]. Following this,
HFE (Daikin Industries) was introduced as a diluting agent to the
concentrated electrolyte. The mixture underwent stirring for a
duration of 24 h to yield the final electrolyte solution, designated as
[Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE. All electrolytes were meticulously
prepared within an Ar-filled glovebox (VAC, dew point <−80 °C, O2
<1 ppm).
2.3. Coin Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Study. To
assess the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution, 2032-type coin
cells (Hohsen) were utilized. Li||Cu coin cells were constructed within
an Ar-filled glovebox. The assembly consisted of a cathode of Cu foil
(16 mm in diameter), an anode of Li foil (Honjo Metal, 14 mm in
diameter, 200 μm thick), an electrolyte (70 μL), and a Celgard 3501
separator. The Cu foil underwent cleaning through immersion in 1
mol dm−3 HCl for 10 min, followed by thorough rinsing with
ultrapure water (∼18 MΩ cm, Milli-Q Integral 3), multiple acetone
washes, and rapid vacuum drying before use. Galvanostatic
deposition/dissolution experiments for the Li/Cu coin cells were
conducted by using a battery tester (HJ1001SD8, Hokuto Denko,
Japan). The cutoff voltage ranged between −1 and 1 V, with a current
density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2.
To construct practical Li−S pouch cells, a layer-by-layer technique
was employed. These cells involved the lamination of cathodes (two
sheets of one-side cathode coated on an Al current collector),
separators, and an anode. Li metal attached onto both sides of Cu foil
(Li thickness = 70 μm, Honjo Metal) was used as the anode. The Li−
S pouch cell underwent cycling between 1.4 and 3.2 V, utilizing a
protocol of 0.05 C charge and 0.08 C discharge through a battery
tester. The amount of electrolyte ([Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE)
was reduced to 3.7 μL mg(S)
−1. Specific capacities were calculated
based on the areal mass loading of sulfur and the theoretical capacity
of sulfur (1672 mAh g−1). Charge−discharge experiments were
carried out at 30 °C.
2.4. Characterization. Subsequent to testing, coin cells were
disassembled within the glovebox to retrieve the Cu substrate and Li
foils. These electrodes underwent a thorough rinse with a fresh
anhydrous HFE solvent to eliminate any residual electrolyte.
Subsequently, they were dried in an Ar atmosphere for further
measurements. Morphological observations were conducted through
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM-6390. To
analyze SEI films, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measure-
ments were performed with a PHI Quantera II Scanning X-ray
Microprobe (ULVAC-PHI). A focused monochromatic Al Kα X-ray
source was utilized. For depth profiling, an Ar ion source was
employed at a 45° incident angle with a 2 kV acceleration voltage.
The XPS data were fitted using Casa XPS software, employing

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Temperature Effects on Li Deposition/Dissolu-
tion Reversibility with Li/Cu Cells. To explore the impact
of temperature on Li reversibility, we conducted experiments
using a coin cell setup that paired a Li foil with a Cu foil under
lean electrolyte conditions (70 μL). The utilized electrolyte
was an LHCE solution ([Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE).
Our findings underscore the significant influence of temper-
ature on the performance of Cu/Li cells. Specifically, Li/Cu
cells cycled at a higher temperature of 60 °C (referred to as Li/
Cu cells at 60 °C) exhibited a notably superior coulombic
efficiency (CE) compared to cells cycled at room temperature
(referred to as Li/Cu cells at 30 °C), as depicted in Figure 1a.
For instance, after 20 cycles, the CEs for Li/Cu cells at 30 °C
and Li/Cu cells at 60 °C were measured to be 97.5 and 98.6%,
respectively. These results are consistent with previous studies
that have reported a similar trend.23,24 Furthermore, Figure 1b
illustrates a reduction in the overpotential gap between Li
deposition and dissolution with rising temperature. This
phenomenon can be attributed to the accelerated ionic
mobility and improved charge transfer kinetics at elevated
temperatures. However, it is important to note that, despite the
higher temperature, a significant enhancement in long-term
cycling stability was not observed compared to cycling at 30 °C
(Figure 1a), which diverges from our initial expectations. Prior
research has also indicated that, while Li metal demonstrates
remarkably high CE and long-term cycling stability at elevated
temperatures in ether-based electrolytes, adverse effects of high
temperature are observed in additive-free carbonate electro-
lytes due to variations in the evolution of the SEI composite.23
To comprehensively investigate the effect of temperature, we
conducted a thorough evaluation of the electrochemical
performance of Cu/Li cells using an HCE solution of the
[Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2] electrolyte at 60 °C without the
dilution by HFE (Figure S1). Intriguingly, although the cycling
lifetimes in this electrolyte system were extended compared to
the system with HFE (Figure 1a, red circle), short circuiting
was observed after 80 cycles (Figure S1). Our prior research
has indicated the accelerated decomposition of the SL solvent
at elevated temperatures, especially in [TFSA]-based electro-
lytes lacking an effective initial SEI film to curb subsequent
solvent decomposition.17,19 Furthermore, the limited content
of [FSA] anions in this study was insufficient for sustaining
long-term cycling. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that
solvent decomposition and SEI maintenance at the electrode/
electrolyte interface could negatively impact the cycling
performance at higher temperatures. To address the
detrimental effects of solvent decomposition as much as
possible, we adopted an alternative approach.25−27
In this approach, cells were initially charged/discharged at
60 °C and subsequently cycled at room temperature (30 °C),
denoted as Li/Cu cell (60 °C →30 °C). Interestingly, this
strategy yielded significantly prolonged cycling life compared
to the Li/Cu cell at 30 °C. This was accompanied by a high CE
of 97.4% at the 80th cycle (Figure 1c). These outcomes
indicate that the initial cycle under elevated temperature
conditions in the SL-based LHCE has a positive influence on
the reversibility of lithium deposition and dissolution in
subsequent cycles. Furthermore, comparing the deposition/

3.2. SEI Film Characterization. The formation of the SEI
film on a Cu electrode within a Cu||Li cell, prior to lithium
deposition (voltage >0 V), was investigated by measuring the
electricity for SEI formation under a relatively low current
density of 0.1 mA cm−2 (under slow reaction conditions). This
investigation revealed distinctive processes between cells
measured at 30 and 60 °C (Figure S3). Notably, both the
reductive potential onset and electricity for SEI formation were
higher at 60 °C compared to 30 °C. This indicates that the SEI
formation, primarily attributed to [FSA] anion decomposition,
occurs more readily at 60 °C, resulting in a robust SEI
compared to that formed at 30 °C.28 Our cyclic voltammetry
study has also confirmed that anion reduction occurs at
approximately 2∼1 V vs Li/Li+.19 To gain deeper insights into
the SEI composition, we conducted surface analysis using XPS
on Li electrodeposits onto a Cu foil (1 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 mA
cm−2). Figure 2a illustrates the normalized F 1s spectra for the
Cu electrode deposited in the LHCE solution, [Li(SL)2]-
[TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE, at 30 and 60 °C. In both spectra, two
distinct peaks centered at binding energies of 685 and 688 eV
are observed. The peak at 685 eV corresponds to lithium
fluoride (LiF), while the peak at 688 eV signifies residual or
incomplete decomposition products of the anions.29 Impor-
tantly, at 60 °C, the intensity of both the LiF and residual
peaks is heightened, indicating an increased level of anion
decomposition at elevated temperatures. Additionally, the
corresponding decomposition products of N 1s (solely from
anion decomposition) presented in Figure 2b closely mirror
the F 1s spectra, affirming a significantly elevated anion
reduction at higher temperatures. Consequently, the initial
lithium deposition at elevated temperatures contributes to the
augmenting formation of the anion-derived SEI film.
In Figure 2c, the normalized O 1s spectra for both electrodes
cycled at 30 and 60 °C are presented. An intriguing peak at
approximately 532 eV is evident, which can be attributed to the
S−O bond. This bond potentially arises from the decom-
position of either SL or anions. Additionally, both electrodes
exhibit a peak at 528 eV, corresponding to the presence of
Li2O.30,31 Our prior investigations have indicated that Li2O is a
common decomposition product of SL upon reaching the
lithium metal surface.16,17 Thus, the notably diminished
intensity of the Li2O peak at 60 °C suggests that the
heightened formation of the anion-derived SEI under elevated
temperatures effectively restrains subsequent SL decomposi-
tion. Analysis of the C 1s spectra (Figure 2d) does not reveal
significant differences in terms of the decomposed product
peaks. However, the heightened intensity of the CF2 peak at 60
°C, attributed to [TFSA] decomposition, aligns with the F 1s

3.3. Morphological Evolution of Li Deposits during
Cycling. As discussed earlier, the character of the SEI film
holds a substantial influence over the processes of lithium
deposition/dissolution, particularly via the fracturing and
swelling of the SEI film. Moreover, the morphology of the
deposited lithium significantly contributes to shaping the
subsequent stability of the SEI film. Figure 4a,b presents the
time-evolution of overpotential during galvanostatic lithium
deposition (0.5 mA cm−2) at 30 and 60 °C, respectively. The
corresponding morphology of the lithium deposits on Cu foils
(1 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2) is displayed in Figure 4c,d. The
recorded potential profiles reveal two crucial overpotentials:
the nucleation overpotential (ηn), characterized by a voltage
spike at the commencement of lithium deposition, and the
lithium-growth overpotential (ηp), which represents the
plateau overpotential subsequent to nucleation. It is note-
worthy that the deposition of lithium onto existing nuclei is
more favorable compared to deposition onto a different metal,
such as a Cu substrate. This results in a significantly smaller ηp
value compared to ηn. The magnitude of ηn depends upon the
thermodynamic free energy barrier for nucleation, influenced
by the composition and nature of the pre-existing SEI. Hence,
ηn plays an instrumental role in determining the morphology of
the lithium deposits.33,34
Clearly, Li deposition at an elevated temperature of 60 °C
exhibits a notably diminished ηn in comparison to that of
deposition at 30 °C. This observation is further substantiated
by the morphologies of the deposited Li, as depicted in Figure
4c,d. At 30 °C, the Li metal particles are diminutiveand exhibit
noncircular shapes, showing needle-like particles on the
deposition surface. In contrast, at 60 °C, the Li particles are
larger and possess well-defined edges, showing island-like
morphologies. These disparities in the morphology of the
deposited Li underscore the marked impact of temperature on
the process of lithium nucleation during the initial deposition
phase. Importantly, the initial SEI film is more prone to
persisting on the larger Li particles formed at high temper-
atures, given that smaller lithium particles offer relatively larger
surface areas, consequently leading to more frequent breakage
of the formed SEI.33

3.4. Enhanced Battery Performance Demonstrated
by Practical Li/S Pouch Cells. To further illuminate the
advantageous impacts of a well-managed SEI film and lithium
morphology on battery performance, we embarked on a
practical exploration employing pouch-type Li−S batteries
with the [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.8FSA0.2]-2HFE electrolyte. The
configuration of the cell is visually depicted in Figure S4, as
explained in the Experimental Section. The importance of
lithium metal anode consumption resulting from parasitic
reactions and its subsequent role in battery deterioration are
often overlooked due to the ready availability of unused
lithium. This perspective prompted us to conduct a
comprehensive and meticulous validation of the lithium−
sulfur cell.35 For this purpose, we deliberately opted for a
limited amount of lithium (70 μm, theoretically 15 mAh cm−2)
as the anode. Additionally, to expedite the occurrence of
parasitic reactions on the lithium anode side, we adopted a
challenging pouch cell design. This design features a low N/P
ratio of 1.7, accompanied by a sulfur loading exceeding 5 mg
cm−2, and a reduced quantity of electrolyte (E/S = 3.7 μL
mg(S)).
In Figure 5a, the charge−discharge profiles of the Li−S
battery cycled at 30 °C are depicted. The discharge curves
exhibit two plateaus corresponding to the reduction of S8 to
long-chain LiPSs and the subsequent reduction to Li2S.36 The
charge profiles stem from the one-step oxidation of the
terminal discharge products to S8.36 Upon observation of the
curves, the initial discharge capacity of the cell was 989 mAh
g−1. With the progression of cycling, the discrepancy between
the lithiation and delithiation voltage plateaus grew notably,
reaching a typical value of 700 mV by the 10th cycle. As
elucidated earlier, the XPS spectra reveal that the SEI film
formed at 30 °C lacks robustness and is primarily derived from
solvent decomposition. It predominantly consists of organic
species, rendering it susceptible to swelling. Furthermore, the
presence of small-sized and nonuniformly distributed lithium
nucleation results in a substantial surface area and uneven
current flux, contributing to heightened overpotential during
cycling. Moreover, the porosification of lithium might
inherently absorb an insufficient amount of electrolyte from
the cathode, potentially rendering the electrochemical reaction
of sulfur species less kinetically favorable. Regarding the cycling
longevity, the battery managed to maintain a capacity of 757
mA h g−1 at the 40th cycle (Figure 5b). However, a marked
capacity decline occurred subsequently, potentially indicating
significant depletion of both the lithium anode and electrolyte.

Recognizing the advantageous effects of the anion-derived
SEI film and larger lithium particles observed in Li/Cu cells at
60 °C, we embarked on exploring their potential benefits in
Li−S cells. However, it is important to note, as illustrated in
Figure S5, that discharging at 60 °C accelerates the dissolution
of LiPSs in the electrolyte, resulting in an increased discharge
capacity for the first plateau.37,22 Moreover, the elevated
temperature also expedites the shuttle effect and inevitable side
reaction between Li[FSA] and LiPSs, as evidenced by the
continuous fluctuations in the charging curve (Figure S5).
Therefore, to counteract these effects, we devised a two-step
strategy. Initially, the cell was discharged at 30 °C, followed by
charging at 60 °C, with the charging process restricted to
match the capacity discharged initially. As depicted in the
charge/discharge curves in Figure 5c, there were no
significantly enlarged polarizations in the subsequent discharge
processes compared to the initial discharge process, and the
overpotential remained stable at 610 mV by the 10th cycle.
This stability was attributed to the SEI film derived from
anions, primarily composed of inorganic species. This film
exhibited low swellability toward the electrolyte, thereby
inhibiting solvent decomposition. Furthermore, the suppressed
porosification of the lithium anode ensured a relatively even
current flux and prevented electrolyte loss from the cathode,
resulting in a low overpotential. The cycling longevity, as
demonstrated in Figure 5d, shows a consistent discharge
capacity, with the cell delivering a capacity of 722 mAh g−1
after 50 cycles. This corresponds to a retention of 71.8%
compared with the initial discharge process. In contrast, the
retention for the cell cycling at 30 °C throughout was only
39.7% (Figure 5b). These findings underscore the viability of
the two-step strategy in enhancing the cycling stability and
performance of Li−S cells. The majority of studies on Li−S
batteries have been carried out under mild conditions,
characterized by a high N/P ratio, high E/S, and low S-
loading, primarily utilizing coin cells.38 These mild conditions
readily demonstrate artificially stable cycling behavior of Li−S
batteries.38 However, reports on cycling under such demanding
conditions are limited to only several tens of cycles.38 In our
Li−S full cell tests (pouch cell tests) presented here, we
adopted challenging harsh conditions and illustrated an
enhancement in the cycling stability for up to 50 cycles.
Finally, we proceeded to examine the morphologies of the
lithium anodes in the fully charged state after we completed 50
cycles of pouch cell testing. Figure 6a visually represents the
morphology of the lithium metal electrode when cycled at 30
°C. While no prominent dendritic formations were observed,
notable structural collapse and uneven morphology were
apparent in certain regions, leading to a visibly porous
structure. This outcome can be attributed to the repeated
rupture of the SEI film during cycling, which accelerates side
reactions on the lithium metal surface during the subsequent
cycles. This ultimately results in the premature depletion of the
lithium anode, especially under the low N/P ratio with less
lithium excess. In such cases, the anode cannot sustain the
continuous irreversible loss of active lithium, ultimately leading
to a significant capacity drop after 40 cycles (Figure 5b).
Conversely, the cell initially charged at 60 °C exhibited a
markedly smooth surface and compact structure (Figure 6b).
This favorable result can be attributed to the formation of the
anion-derived SEI film and lithium nucleation facilitated by the
elevated temperature. Consequently, during subsequent
cycling processes, the SEI film could maintain its integrity
without swelling, preserving its initial morphology. The
stability of the SEI film contributes to higher lithium
reversibility and excellent cycling performance.
To summarize, our findings underscore the considerable
distinctions in the behavior of cells cycled with initially
controlled SEI films and lithium morphology in Li−S batteries.
It is evident that, through meticulous optimization of these
factors, we can significantly enhance the overall performance
and extend the cycling life of Li−S batteries, particularly under
challenging conditions.
■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, our study highlighted the substantial advantages
gained from the initial charging of Li−S batteries at elevated
temperatures, leading to improved lithium reversibility. This
strategy results in the formation of an anion-derived SEI film
enriched with inorganic constituents, even when a limited
amount of Li[FSA]. Moreover, the elevated temperature
facilitates favorable lithium nucleation, yielding a smooth and
densely packed lithium surface. These combined effects
effectively counteract the fracture and swelling of the SEI
film, leading to enhanced CE during Li deposition and
dissolution, ultimately extending the cycling lifespan. Fur-
thermore, we successfully demonstrated the practical benefits
of these insights in pouch-type Li−S batteries, designed with
challenging conditions such as high sulfur loading (5 mg
cm−2), low N/P ratio (1.7), and reduced electrolyte quantities
(E/S = 3.7 μL mg(S)
−1). Through this investigation, we
underscore the significance of comprehending SEI film
formation and controlling lithium morphology to achieve
notable enhancements in the performance and durability of
Li−S batteries, particularly in practical operational scenarios.
Our findings contribute to the ongoing progress in Li−S
battery technology, setting the stage for more effective and
long-lasting energy storage solutions in the future while taking
