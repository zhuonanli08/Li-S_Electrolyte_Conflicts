EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials of electrolyte. All solvents and salt used in this work are purchased from 
Suzhou DoDochem without further treatment. EGDBE (DoDoChem, 99.5% purity, 20 
ppm H2O max), TTE (DoDoChem, 99.8% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), DME 
(DoDoChem, 99.9% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), EGDEE (DoDoChem, 99.8% purity, 
20 ppm H2O max), and LiFSI (DoDoChem, 99.9 % purity, 100 ppm H2O max).
Preparation of electrolyte. The solvent of LSSE was prepared via mixing EGDBE and 
TTE with a volume ratio of 3:2. Then LiFSI salt was dissolved into the as-prepared 
solvent with a concentration of 1 M. LMSE was prepared via following the same 
procedure while EGDBE was substituted by DME. With respect to the formation of 
LHCE, the salt concentration was elevated to 3 M. M/B was prepared via dissolving 
LiFSI in the mixed solvent of DME and EGDBE with a volume ratio of 1:1. Note that 
the concentration of DPDX (i.e., DPDS, DPDSe, and DPDTe from Sigma Aldrich) was 
fixed to 10 mM. The thus-derived electrolytes are abbreviated as LHCE+DPDX.
Preparation of S/NG electrode. Firstly, sulfur powder and nitrogen-doped graphene 
(NG, purchased from Xianfeng Nano) were mixed and ground with a mass ratio of 3:1. 
The obtained mixture was heated with a temperature of 155 ℃ for 12 h. Then, S/NG 
composite (80 wt%), SuperP (10 wt%) and LA133 binder (10 wt%) were mixed and 
ball-milled to form a homogeneous slurry, which was casted onto a piece of clean Al 
foil. The slurry was dried at 60 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven to remove H2O and O2

precipitation process. With regard to the reversible Li2S dissociation process, the 
assembled cells were fully discharged to 1.7 V, which were then potentiostatically 
charged from 2.2 V to 2.38 V with 20.0 mV per interval.

Figure S1. Li+ binding configuration of with (a) DME, (b) EGDBE, (c) TTE, (d) 
EGDEE, (e) DPE, and (f) DBE.
Five analogous ether solvents were taken into consideration for fabricating LSSE with 
low salt concentration as well as low diluent usage and hence low electrolyte cost. To 
begin with, it was found that with respect to such dual-denticity molecules with two 
ethereal atoms, their rotated former is more stable when interacting with Li ions rather 
than initial linear former. In other words, all the ethereal solvents with dual-oxygen 
sites will form a chelation effect for the Li-ion solvation process. It is intriguing that 
there is a slight difference of binding energy among five molecules, which confirms 
that the solvation ability cannot be reflected by the binding energy with respect to such 
chelated coordination configuration. In this respect, the C/O atomic ratio plays a 
decisive role in influencing the solvation effect for chelation ether molecules. For one 
thing, Li ions have less chance to coordinate with the ethereal oxygen atoms due to the 
high proportion of C atoms. In other words, the solvation structure needs to 
accommodate more extra C and H atoms, thus reducing the Li-ion coordination number. 
For another, it will reduce the number of solvent molecules and hence the solvation 
effect in real electrolyte due to the higher molar mass. Therefore, DME (C/O atomic 
ratio:2) and EGDEE (C/O atomic ratio: 3) show an apparently stronger solvation ability 
than that of other ether molecules. Note that the steric hinderance might have a crucial 
influence if the C/O atomic ratio is same. Hence the DBE is anticipated to have a weaker 
solvation effect than that of EGDBE due to a larger steric hindrance of the t-butyl group

to rich electron-withdrawn F atoms. Taken together, it is understandable that TTE 
always serves as the diluent for fabricating LHCE, which can stay outside the Li-ion 
solvation sheath without direct coordination with Li ions.

DPE and DBE are not commercially available accompanied by the complicated 
synthetic process, which will lead to substantial increase of electrolyte cost. Hence 
DME, EGDEE, and EGDBE with a facile alkyl chain adjustment were employed to 
fabricate low-concentration LSSE. With respect to the 0.02 M Li2S6 solution, the 
polysulfide solubility follows an order DME > EGDEE> EGDBE. Apparently, only 
EGDBE can achieve a sufficient restriction of polysulfide dissolution, which is 
anticipated to enable a quasi-solid-phase reaction pathway. In response, two discharge 
plateaus can be observed in the GCD curves when using DME or EGDEE as the main 
solvent, further confirming that they can not fundamentally suppress the polysulfide 
diffusion and shuttling. Taken together, EGDEB was employed to fabricate LSSE with 
the aim to cope with the key issues of both sides in Li–S batteries.

Figure S4. (a) 13C, (b) 1H, and (c) 17O NMR spectra of M/B-E and M/B-S. (d) 7Li, 
and (e) 19F NMR spectra of M/B-E.
13C and 1H NMR spectra of MB-S evidence the successful mixture of binary solvent 
containing DME and EGDBE, whose characteristic peaks are labelled. With the 
addition of LiFSI, the 1H NMR exhibits an apparent upfield shift, which confirms that 
the nuclei are shielded by dense electron clouds and the change in the solvent 
environment upon Li+ solvation process. In the meanwhile, 17O NMR signals for both 
DME and EGDBE broaden, indicating the formation of Li+–O bond with respect to the 
ion-dipole interaction. Besides, the 17O NMR peak of DME displays a more obvious 
change, which is attributed to the stronger solvation effect of DME as compared to that 
of EGDBE. In the 7Li NMR spectrum of M/B-E, the chemical shift of Li-ion resonance 
exhibits a more negative value than that of LSSE and LMSE, indicative of strong 
shielding effect. It is understandable that the strong Li+–O binding from DME and 
absence of TTE’s electron withdraw effect will lead to the increase the electron cloud 
density around Li ions. In stark contrast, 19F resonance of FSI− exhibits a downfield 
shift. Such a deshielding effect suggests that more FSI− anions enter the inner Li+ 
solvation sheath due to the more proportion of weakly solvating EGDBE solvents in 
MB-E in comparison with other two as-prepared electrolytes.

work, which manifests that Li-ion transport will be retarded due to the non-coordination 
feature of fluorinated ether as a diluent.

LSSE+DPDTe with three concentrations (i.e., 5 mM, 10 mM, and 20 mM) were 
prepared to explore the optimized co-mediator concentration. For one thing, cathode 
kinetics can scarcely be elevated with a fairly low concentration of 5 mM. It can be 
observed that the discharge capacity is almost the same as that of the pure LSSE. 
However, for another, when the concentration is increased to 20 mM, it will be harmful 
to the stability of Li metal anode. In this respect, the CE of Li-Cu half cell will suffer 
from a rapid decay upon the 150th cycle. In conclusion, to concurrently facilitate the 
sulfur reaction kinetics and ensure high compatibility with Li anode, 10 mM DPDTe 
was employed to serve as a promising electrolyte additive.
