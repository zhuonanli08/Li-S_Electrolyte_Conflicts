ABSTRACT: Electrocatalysis is a powerful approach to accelerate
sulfur redox kinetics in lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries. However, in
practical high sulfur loading and thick cathodes, the severe
concentration polarization induced by the rapid depletion of local
lithium ions (Li+) greatly restricts catalysis and battery performance,
representing an engineering challenge in a closed microelectrochemical
reactor. Here, an electrolyte-dispersible Li+-reservoir catalyst is
proposed to sustain the local Li+ concentration to ensure the
continuous electrochemical reaction in the battery with an energy
density of over 400 Wh kg−1. Such a catalyst is realized by anchoring
single cobalt atoms onto uniformly dispersed carbon quantum dots (Co-CQD). The negatively charged CQD strongly attracts and
enriches Li+ around the Co catalytic sites by robust electrostatic interactions, ensuring a continuous Li+ supply during the catalytic
reactions. Moreover, Co-CQDs are homogeneously dispersed in the electrolyte and distributed in thick electrodes, promoting bulk-
phase Li+ distribution and effectively eliminating concentration polarization. As a result, this strategy lowers the sulfur conversion
activation energy in thick cathodes from 1.27 to 0.72 eV and enables the battery to maintain a high reversible capacity of 13.5 mAh
cm−2 under a high sulfur loading of 13 mg cm−2, outperforming conventional catalysts under identical conditions. Moreover, an Ah-
level pouch cell delivers a high energy density of 513 Wh kg−1, offering a scalable strategy to overcome ion transport bottlenecks in
thick cathodes for practical Li−S batteries.
1. INTRODUCTION
In chemical reactions, the diffusion process plays a
fundamental role and can serve as the rate-limiting step,
particularly in systems where reactants are not uniformly mixed
or exist in different phases. When diffusion is slow, it constrains
the overall reaction rate irrespective of the intrinsic kinetics of
the chemical transformation.1,2 Electrochemical energy storage
devices, such as lithium-ion batteries, can be regarded as
electrochemical reactors that couple microscale processes of
mass, charge, and heat transport with electrochemical
reactions.3−5 The coordinated and efficient transport of
electrons and ions to electrochemical reaction sites is critical
to ensuring stable battery reactions, particularly in emerging
high-energy-density batteries requiring the high lithium ions
(Li+) flux in the charge/discharge process.6,7 A prime example
is the lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery, which offers an
exceptionally high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1)
based on the sulfur redox reactions, making it a strong
candidate for next-generation energy applications.8,9 However,
the redox pathway of sulfur involves multiple soluble and solid
intermediates, giving rise to sluggish and complicated ion and
electron transfer kinetics.10,11 Catalysis, as a core approach in
chemical engineering production, can also play a critical role in
Li−S batteries to accelerate interfacial charge transfer and the
sulfur reaction kinetics.12−15 Nevertheless, under the practical
conditions involving thick electrodes and high sulfur loadings,
the significant capacity decay and poor fast charging/
discharging performance still exist.16,17 This behavior cannot
be attributed solely to electron-transfer dominated kinetics, but
rather to the growing dominance of ion-transport limitations,
particularly the local coordinated transfer of ions and electrons
around the catalytic sites.18,19 The classical Butler−Volmer
model shows that the overpotential exhibits a logarithmic
dependence on the local Li+ concentration near the reaction
sites (Supplementary Note S1). This implies that a substantial
drop in local Li+ concentration can cause an exponential rise in
overpotential, ultimately leading to reaction termination
(Figure 1a).20,21 Unfortunately, due to the inherently closed
and static nature of battery systems, the enhancement of ion
transport in thick electrodes cannot rely on traditional
chemical engineering approaches, such as intensified stirring

Regulating the porous structure, such as increasing
porosity22 and constructing hierarchical23 or vertically aligned
channels,24 is commonly used to enhance the diffusion of Li+
in a thick electrode. These structural designs effectively
enhance the bulk ion diffusion across the electrode, but
guaranteeing continuous Li+ access to the local reactive sites is
still a great challenge. Another strategy to improve Li+
accessibility near the reaction sites is to design bifunctional
catalytic centers with both lithiophilic and sulfiphilic character-
istics.25,26 For example, Park et al. designed polymeric
phthalocyanine-coated carbon nanotubes27 and ionically
structured polymeric sulfurs28 as redox-mediated sulfur hosts,
providing adsorption sites for both sulfur species and Li+ to
accelerate sulfur reaction kinetics. However, since this
approach does not fundamentally alter the diffusion pathway
of Li+ from the bulk electrolyte, it still suffers from depth-
dependent transport disparities in thick electrodes, leading to
heterogeneous Li+ distribution and severe polarization. Thus,
ensuring a sufficient local supply of Li+ around catalytic sites is
essential for alleviating ionic depletion and enabling persistent
sulfur redox reactions under practical conditions.
Herein, we propose an electrolyte-dispersible catalyst with a
local Li+ enrichment function, which realizes the uniform Li+
concentration across the electrode and local coordination of
Li+ and catalytic electron transfer (Figure 1b). The catalyst
design is realized by introducing the atomically dispersed
cobalt sites on the carbon quantum dot (CQD) (denoted Co-
CQD). The negatively charged surface of CQD helps maintain
a high local Li+ concentration by the strong electrostatic
interaction with Li+. The CQDs also show excellent
dispersibility in the electrolyte, which can establish a uniform
Li+ distribution across the electrode from nanoscale to
microscale, even deep within thick electrodes. Simultaneously,
Co single atoms embedded within the CQD matrix
significantly accelerate the redox kinetics of sulfur species,
reducing the activation energy of the rate-determining lithium
polysulfide (LiPS)-to-Li2S conversion step from 1.27 to 0.72
eV in thick cathodes, sharply decreasing the battery polar-
ization, and increasing the sulfur utilization by over 40%,
reaching 65.7% of the theoretical capacity. Additionally, the
battery with Co-CQDs delivers a high reversible areal capacity
of 13.5 mAh cm−2 even under an ultrahigh sulfur loading of 13
mg cm−2. The energy density of the assembled pouch cell is up
to 513 Wh kg−1 with a low E/S ratio of 1.6 μL mg−1, providing
a novel paradigm to address multiscale transport challenges in
practical sulfur cathodes.
2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
2.1. The Uniform Dispersion of Li+ Enabled by Co-
COD. Co-CQD catalysts were prepared by a pyrolysis method,
and the details could be found in the experimental section.29
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show that
Co-CQD and CQD have average sizes of about 2 nm. The
uniformly distributed nitrogen heteroatoms provide electron-
rich coordination sites for atomically dispersed Co atoms
(Figures 2a and S1). No characteristic peaks of Co metal or
compounds are observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD)
patterns (Figure S2), and the homogeneous distribution of Co
atoms (partially indicated by red circles) is shown by
aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning
transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) (Figure
S3). The inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-
MS) result suggests that the Co metal loading is 2.19 wt %.
The coordination environments and chemical states of Co
atoms were analyzed using X-ray absorption near edge
structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine
structure (EXAFS). The XANES in Figure 2b demonstrates
that the absorption edge of Co-CQD is between CoO and
CoOOH, indicating the simultaneous presence of Co2+ and
Co3+, resulting from the incomplete reduction of Co3+ during
the preparation process. X-ray photoelectron spectrum (XPS)
in Figure S4 also verifies this conclusion. In the Fourier
transform (FT) k3-weighted extended EXAFS spectra (Figures
2b and S5), the Co−Co peak at 2.5 Å observed in the standard
Co foil is hardly visible in Co-CQD, thus confirming the
atomic dispersion of Co. In addition, the R space distribution
of Co-CQD (Table S1) demonstrates a predominant peak
situated at approximately 1.9 Å, signifying that Co atoms are
firmly anchored in CQD with a square-plane Co−N4
coordination structure (Figures S6 and S7).29−31 In contrast
to CQD, Co-CQD displays a distinct negative shift in the N 1s
XPS spectra, evidencing electron donation from the atomically
Co sites to coordinated nitrogen species, which underpins the
formation of a Co−N interaction (Figure S8).
CQD and Co-CQD are added into the DOL/DME (1:1 v/
v) for electrophoresis test, and the ζ potentials are determined
to be −13 mV and −11.3 mV, respectively, owing to similar
negatively charged oxygen-containing functional groups
(Figures 2d and S9), which enhance colloidal stability and
Li+ electrostatic affinity.32,33 The electrical double layer (EDL)
capacitance quantitatively reflects the extent of electrostatic ion
accumulation at the electrode−electrolyte interface, serving as
a sensitive indicator for ion adsorption capability under non-

2.2. Enhanced LiPS Conversion Kinetics Enabled by
Coupled Adsorption of Li+ and Sn
2−. Motivated by the
remarkable catalytic activity of atomically dispersed Co sites,37
we further investigated the adsorption of Co-CQD toward
polysulfide anions (Sn
2−), and examined how its coupled
enrichment of Li+ within the reactive microenvironment affects
the LiPS conversion kinetics. Supercapacitors were assembled
using carbon electrodes as cathodes and lithium foils as anodes
to measure the sulfur adsorption factor (fsulfur) (Supplementary
Note S3).38 As demonstrated in Figures 3a and S18, after
introduction of CQD and Co-CQD into the electrolyte, the
corresponding value increases from 0.02 to 0.06 and 0.13,
respectively. In addition, the optical photos in Figure 3b show
that the Li2S6 solutions containing CQD and Co-CQD exhibit
progressively lighter color after standing for 6 h. This
observation is corroborated by the marked attenuation of the
characteristic S6
2−peaks in the UV−vis adsorption spectra,
suggesting the ability of Co-CQD to simultaneously adsorb Li+
and Sn
2−, which is helpful to sulfur redox under practical
conditions. Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) tests were
subsequently carried out to quantify the adsorption of Li+ and
S6
2−by CQD and Co-CQD.39 Methanol (CH3OH) was
chosen as the solvent, owing to its weak adsorption capacity.
As shown in Figure 3c, the mass change of Li+ and S6
2−
adsorbed by the carbon electrode is 8.4 ng μg−1, while the
corresponding values increase significantly to 17.5 and 22.1 ng
μg−1 in the presence of CQD and Co-CQD, respectively,
verifying the coupled enrichment of Li+ and Sn
2−by Co-CQD.
NMR tests were performed on Li2S6 solutions. The 7Li peak
in Figure 3d exhibits a pronounced positive shift after the
addition of CQD and Co-CQD, indicating their interaction
with Li2S6. As shown in the XPS spectra (Figures 3e), the
terminal sulfur (ST) of Li2S6 shifts from 161.5 to 161.3 eV
following interaction with Co-CQD, while the bridge sulfur
(SB) remains unchanged (163.3 eV).40 Moreover, the
proportion of Co2+ to Co3+ decreases from 65.6% to 54.3%
(Figure S19), suggesting the formation of the Co−S bond
between Li2S6 and the Co site of Co-CQD, resulting in the
observed shift in the NMR peak.41 Density functional theory
(DFT) calculations were further conducted to assess the
binding energies of Li2S6 on the CQD and Co-CQD surface,
revealing the significantly enhanced adsorption of Co-CQD
toward Sn
2−via Co−S bonds (Figures 3f and S20).
Additionally, as revealed by the differential charge density
analysis in Figures S21 and S22 and Table S3, pronounced
interfacial charge redistribution occurs upon the binding of
Li2S6 to the Co-CQD, indicating a strong electronic
interaction. This interfacial charge delocalization highlights

To systematically evaluate the role of Co-CQD in
overcoming thickness-related kinetic limitations, a series of
electrochemical characterizations were conducted under differ-
ent sulfur loadings (1 and 5 mg cm−2). The corresponding
electrode thicknesses are 24 and 96 μm, respectively (Figure
S23). We first determined the activation energy (Ea) for sulfur
conversion in Li−S batteries with a low sulfur loading of 1 mg
cm−2. As illustrated in Figure S24, the cathode with CQD
exhibits a low Ea of 0.319 eV for the solid−liquid conversion,
compared to the additive-free cathode (0.354 eV), while the
Co-CQD cathode shows a much lower Ea (0.183 eV). To
probe the underlying kinetic limitation, a distribution of
relaxation time (DRT) analysis was conducted under open-
circuit conditions (Figures 4a and S25). The introduction of
CQD and Co-CQD brings about 22.3% and 20.7% decline in
the ion diffusion resistance, respectively, and Co-CQD
additionally reduces the charge transfer resistance by 36.6%,
outperforming CQD (14%). Co-CQD with negatively charged
surfaces adsorbs Li+ and enriches it near catalytic sites, thereby
enhancing local ion transport. This is further evidenced by the
CV analysis at different scan rates, where Co-CQD markedly
increases the Li+ diffusion coefficient (Figure S26).42 These
findings indicate that under low-loading (thin cathode)
conditions charge transfer constitutes the primary kinetic
bottleneck, which is effectively mitigated through the
adsorption of Sn
2−by Co-CQD.
When the sulfur loading is increased to 5 mg cm−2, DRT
analysis shows that the increase in ion-diffusion resistance is
much more pronounced than that of charge-transfer resistance
in comparation with low-loading batteries (Figure S27).

Co-CQD maintains a consistently low Ea throughout the

2.3. Electrochemical Performance of Li−S Batteries
with Co-CQD. Li−Li symmetric cells were assembled to
elucidate the impact of electrolyte-dispersible Co-CQD on Li
metal anodes. The negatively charged surface of Co-CQD
adsorbs Li+, facilitating a more homogeneous ion distribution
near the anode−electrolyte interface, which promotes smooth
and compact Li deposition and thereby markedly enhances
cycling stability (Figure S41). Li−S batteries were further
assembled with sulfur cathodes, lithium anodes, and ether-
based electrolytes without/with additives. The electrochemical
performance is optimized by changing the content of Co-CQD
(20, 100, and 200 ppm), with the optimal addition of 100 ppm
(Figure S42). As demonstrated in Figure 5a, the optimized
battery with Co-CQD has exceptional rate performance, with a
reversible capacity of 1202 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and 645 mAh g−1
at 5.0 C, considerably higher than those of the additive-free
battery (996 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and 302 mAh g−1 at 5.0 C) and
the battery with CQD (1098 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and 424 mAh
g−1 at 5.0 C). The enhanced sulfur reaction kinetics of the
battery with Co-CQD results in a significantly lower
polarization voltage (132.7 mV) in comparison to the
additive-free battery (152.2 mV) and the battery with CQD
(183.9 mV) (Figure S43). The charge and discharge curves at
varying rates demonstrate that the battery with Co-CQD
exhibits a higher low-voltage plateau with a stable overpotential
even at 5.0 C. Conversely, the corresponding values of other
batteries display a substantial increase, thereby indicating a
decreased battery polarization enabled by the coupled Sn
2−and
Li+ adsorption ability of Co-CQD (Figures 5b and S44).
Moreover, the Co-CQD catalyst can improve the cycling
performance of Li−S batteries. The battery with Co-CQD
exhibits a reversible capacity of 724 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at
0.5 C but 563 and 467 mAh g−1 for the battery with CQD and
without additives, respectively (Figure S45). The distinguished
double-platform discharge behavior is observed even at a high
rate of 3.0 C for the battery with Co-CQD, with a long cycling
life of 2000 cycles and a low-capacity decay rate of 0.03% per
cycle. In contrast, both the battery with CQD and the additive-
free battery exhibit rapid capacity decay, indicating a severe
loss of active species (Figure 5c). The dQ/dV curves in the
first 500 cycles (Figures 5d and S46) reveal that the battery
with Co-CQD exhibits larger redox peak currents and smaller
overpotentials, suggesting that the conversion of soluble LiPS
was effectively enhanced by Co-CQD.
High-loading Li−S batteries with thick cathodes were
assembled to evaluate the effect of lean-electrolyte conditions
(5 μL mgS
−1) on catalytic activity and battery performance.
The initial discharge curve in Figure 5f exhibits higher area
capacity (6.6 mAh cm−2), platform voltage (2.103 V) and
sulfur utilization (65.7%) for the battery with Co-CQD than
the battery with CQD (5.2 mAh cm−2, 2.101 V, and 51.7%)
and the additive-free battery (4.6 mAh cm−2, 2.086 V, and
45.7%), which is consistent with the COMSOL simulation
results. And the capacity remains approximately 4 mAh cm−2
after 150 stable cycles at 0.1 C. In contrast, the battery with
CQD and the additive-free battery deactivate after tens of
cycles, due to the worse concentration polarization (Figure
5e). Under a sulfur loading of 9 mg cm−2, the battery with Co-
CQD exhibits an areal capacity of 7.8 mAh cm−2 after 100
cycles. When the thickness of the cathode further increases
(the sulfur loading of 13 mg cm−2), the battery with Co-CQD
still works stably for 60 cycles, indicating the enhanced
performance resulted from the coupled enrichment of Li+ and
Sn
2−by Co-CQD (Figure S47).
To demonstrate the potential of Li−S batteries for practical
applications, pouch cells were assembled using Co-CQD
catalysts. The Ah-level stacked pouch cell with a sulfur loading
of 1.2 g has an initial capacity of 1.48 Ah at an E/S ratio of 3
μL mgS
−1 and exhibits a stable cycling capability for over 60
cycles at 0.05 C (Figure 5g). Furthermore, when the E/S ratio
is reduced to 1.6 μL mgS
−1 and the sulfur loading is increased
to 2.86 g, an ultrahigh energy density of 513 Wh kg−1 is
achieved based on the total mass of the full cell (Figure S48,
Supplementary Note S4, and Table S4). In addition, the
previously reported performance of Li−S pouch cell is
summarized in Figure 5h, indicating that this work exhibits
excellent levels of various parameters, including cycling life,
energy density, E/S ratio, and capacity.47−58
3. CONCLUSION
In summary, we propose an electrolyte-dispersible Li+-reservoir
catalyst that simultaneously regulates the ion and electron
transport kinetics in Li−S batteries. Such an approach
orchestrates a Li+-rich microenvironment via the negatively
charged interface, effectively buffering Li+ concentration
fluctuations during the continuous discharge process. Mean-
while, the strong interaction between Co catalytic sites and
LiPS promotes effective charge redistribution, achieving rapid
electron transfer. The coupled effect significantly lowers the Ea
for the liquid−solid conversion from 1.27 to 0.72 eV in thick
cathodes, sharply decreasing the battery polarization and
