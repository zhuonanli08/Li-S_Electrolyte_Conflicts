A B S T R A C T  
The reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution onto/from a copper substrate is analyzed herein for fabricating 
anodeless Li2S-cathode-based batteries using sulfolane (SL)-based sparingly solvating electrolytes to prevent 
lithium-polysulfide dissolution. The composition of electrolytes—binary lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) 
amide/lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)amide (LiTFSA/LiFSA; 9:1 molar ratio) in SL solvent—considerably affects the 
reversibility; a high-concentration electrolyte (HCE) and a localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE: HCE 
diluted by non-coordinating hydrofluoroether) exhibit superior reversibility than that of 1 M solution. Solid 
electrolyte interphases (SEI) with high F compositions and relatively low O concentrations are formed on the 
HCE- and LHCE-deposited Li surfaces. SEI formation via FSA decomposition is promoted in the HCE and LHCE, 
whereas SL decomposition is prohibited by the fluorine-rich SEI. The Li-deposit morphology is significantly 
affected by the nucleation overpotential at the initial deposition, which is influenced by the nature of the formed 
SEIs. Small-surface-area deposits with a low nucleation overpotential suppress the electrolyte decomposition and 
the formation of dead Li, resulting in high reversibility. Optimal reversibility is achieved by performing low- 
current-density initial deposition at 60 ◦C with the LHCE. The charge/discharge characteristics of anode-less 
Cu||Li2S batteries are determined under the reversibility-optimized conditions; 75 charge/discharge cycles 
and a 50-cycle capacity retention rate of 47.5% are achieved.   
1. Introduction 
Li-ion batteries (LIBs) are extensively used in portable devices and 
electric vehicles because they have the highest energy density among 
commercial secondary batteries as well as excellent cyclability [1–4]. 
However, state-of-the-art LIBs are approaching the energy density limit 
of LIBs (~250 Wh kg−1) [3,4]. Therefore, Li–metal batteries (LMBs) 
have attracted considerable attention as alternatives to LIBs [5–9]. The

higher than that of graphite (a widely used LIB anode), which can 
facilitate the production of high-energy-density batteries. Moreover, 
anode-less (or anode-free) batteries, which comprise a cathode and an 
anode current collector, are attracting research attention [10–12]. Un­
like conventional composite anodes, the anode current collector in 
anode-less batteries is not porous, which helps reduce the amount of 
electrolyte used in the batteries. Besides, because the anode becomes 
lighter, anode-less batteries can exhibit considerably improved energy 
densities. However, only the cathode material acts as the Li source for 
battery operation. Therefore, a low Li-deposition/dissolution efficiency 
onto/from the current collector seriously deteriorates the capacity. 
Thus, increasing the deposition/dissolution efficiency of Li is crucial for 
realizing anode-less batteries. Investigations on improving the 
Li-deposition/dissolution reversibility in anode-less batteries have pri­
marily targeted either electrolytes [13–29] or current collectors 
[30–33]. In several studies conducted on electrolyte solutions, the use of

been found to promote uniform deposition of Li [32]. A 50-cycle ca­
pacity retention rate of 78% has been achieved using graphene-coated 
copper foil as a current collector [32]. Moreover, the application of a 
thin liquid metal layer to copper foil has been found to promote the 
alloying reaction with Li, thereby facilitating uniform Li deposition 
[33]. A 50-cycle capacity retention rate of 84% and an energy density of 
420 Wh kg−1 have been achieved using a full cell with 
liquid-metal-coated copper foil and LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 cathode [33]. 
In particular, the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution can be 
improved using high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) and localized 
high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs), as mentioned above [14–25]. 
In electrolyte solutions, the solvation structures of the salt vary 
depending on the salt concentration as well as the nature of the salt and 
solvent (Fig. S1) [14–18]. The following structures are typically 
considered in this context: completely dissociated ions and 
solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs), in which Li+ ions and anions are 
separated by solvents; contact ion pairs (CIPs), in which cations and 
anions establish one-to-one contact; and aggregates (AGGs), in which 
multiple cations and anions are aggregated. In conventional organic 
electrolyte solutions with a concentration of ~1 M, the major ionic 
species are SSIPs, in which only the solvent molecules are coordinated to 
the cation and a sufficient number of free solvent molecules exist. When 
these electrolyte solutions are in contact with the Li anode, a solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) is formed at the Li anode primarily via 
decomposition (reduction) of the solvent molecules because the anions 
cannot be in the vicinity of the negative electrode owing to electrostatic 
repulsion [14–25]. However, increasing the salt concentration decreases 
the molar ratio of the coordinating solvent to Li+, which simultaneously 
reduces the amount of the free solvent, resulting in coordination of the 
anions to Li+ (Fig. S1) [14–18]. Under these conditions, CIP- and 
AGG-type ionic species primarily exist in the electrolyte solution. In 
LHCEs, the solvation structure around Li+ remains unchanged from that 
in HCEs because of the non-coordinating nature of a diluent, such as 
hydrofluoroethers (HFEs). When anions are coordinated to Li+, they can 
approach the anode interface along with Li+ via cathodic polarization. 
The reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution can be enhanced by sup­
pressing the reductive decomposition of the solvent and by forming an 
effective anion-derived SEI [14–25]. In particular, the decomposition of 
anions such as bis(fluorosulfonyl)amide (FSA−; [N(SO2F)2]−) is 
considered to occur more readily than that of solvents [14–25]. There­
fore, in addition to the solvent molecules, the anions can be reduced and 
decomposed to form an SEI at the anode interface. Furthermore, LiFSA 
can be reduced and decomposed at the Li anode to form an effective SEI 
with a high LiF content [14–25]. For instance, 4 M LiFSA in dimethox­
yethane—an HCE system—provides >99% Coulombic efficiency for 
hundreds of Li-deposition/dissolution cycles [23]. 
Li–S batteries are next-generation LMBs that use a high-capacity 
sulfur cathode (CS = 1672 mA h g−1) [34–36]. The energy density of 
Li–S batteries can be enhanced by increasing the sulfur loading on the 
cathode and reducing the electrolyte content. Recently, the importance 
of using sparingly solvating electrolytes, which have an extremely low 
lithium-polysulfide (LPS) solubility, has been recognized for increasing 
the energy density of Li–S batteries [37–40]. This is because the amount 
of electrolyte can be principally decoupled from the capacity of Li–S 
cells with the use of sparingly solvating electrolytes, resulting in a high 
energy density and high Coulombic efficiency. In our previous study, 
HCEs with glyme or sulfolane (SL) as a solvent were found to act as 
sparingly solvating electrolytes for Li–S batteries [37,38,41–44]. 
Furthermore, the solubility of LPSs was further suppressed in LHCEs 
diluted with an HFE [38,41,42]. Compared with the glyme-based elec­
trolytes (solvate ionic liquids), the SL-based HCE and LHCE enabled 
much higher rate capabilities, which could be ascribed to the much 
higher Li+-transference number [41,42]. Consequently, a Li–S battery 
with an energy density of >350 Wh kg−1 was fabricated by increasing 
the sulfur loading on the cathode and reducing the amount of electrolyte 
using an SL-based sparingly solvating electrolyte [44]. 
Li2S (CLi2S = 1166 mA h g−1) has been proposed as a sulfur battery 
cathode that can permit the utilization of anodes other than Li metal, 
such as graphite and Si [45–49]. Li2S/C composites were synthesized by 
reducing Li2SO4 with carbon, and a high-capacity Li2S cathode was 
fabricated [50–53]. Furthermore, a battery with the graphite|sparingly 
solvating electrolyte|Li2S assembly was found to exhibit excellent 
characteristics [48]. In this study, to explore the possibility of realizing 
anode-less batteries with a Li2S cathode, the enhancement of the 
Li-deposition/dissolution efficiency was investigated in SL-based spar­
ingly solvating electrolytes applicable to sulfur batteries. SL is a solvent 
with high oxidative stability [54–56], however, it exhibits poor reduc­
tive stability upon contact with a Li–metal anode [57,58]. It was found 
by ab initio-MD simulation [57] and electrochemical experiments [58] 
that SL reacts with metallic Li to form tetrahydrothiophene in the 
SL-based electrolytes. However, these side reactions were greatly 
reduced by using LiFSA, compared with LiTFSA [58]. Actually, high Li 
deposition/dissolution reversibility was reported in the SL-based HCE 
and LHCE containing LiFSA [24]. However, LiFSA cannot be used alone 
in sulfur batteries because of the side reactions with LPSs [59,60]. 
Therefore, the Li-deposition/dissolution reversibility was investigated 
in this study in dual-salt electrolyte solutions (LiTFSA and LiFSA) with 
the SL solvent. Furthermore, Li2S-cathode-based anode-less batteries 
were fabricated under optimal reversibility conditions, and their char­
ge/discharge characteristics were examined.

2.2. Li deposition/dissolution tests 
The copper foil used as the substrate for Li deposition/dissolution 
was first washed by sonication in 1 M HCl for 20 min. After replacing the 
1 M HCl several times with ultrapure water, the copper foil was washed 
several times with acetone and then dried overnight under a vacuum at 
room temperature. A copper foil punched to ø-19 mm, two pieces of 
Celgard 3501 punched to ø-17 mm (the separator), and a Li foil punched 
to ø-15 mm were layered in an Ar-filled glove box, and an electrolyte 
solution (75 μL) was added to prepare a 2032-type coin cell (Hohsen). 
The prepared Cu||Li cell was analyzed under galvanostatic conditions 
using a charge/discharge tester (HJ-SD8, Hokuto Denko). To investigate 
the Li-deposition/dissolution reversibility, 5 mAh cm−2 of Li was 
deposited at an arbitrary current density, and part of the deposited Li 
was then dissolved at 0.5 mA cm−2 for 2 h (1 mAh cm−2). The deposi­
tion/dissolution process was then repeated at 1 mAh cm−2. To estimate 
the Coulombic efficiency, the Li remaining on the copper foil after 20 
cycles was completely dissolved to determine its amount, and the 
average Coulombic efficiency for 20 cycles was calculated [61]. 
2.3. Analytical measurements 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/energy-dispersive X-ray spec­
troscopy (EDX) measurements of the surface of the deposited Li were 
performed using a field-emission SEM apparatus (SU8010, Hitachi High- 
Technologies). The coin cell subjected to the Li deposition/dissolution 
test was disassembled in a glove box, and the surface was washed with 
the HFE and then transferred to the SEM apparatus using a transfer 
vessel. 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of the surface 
of the deposited Li were performed using a Quantera SXM instrument 
(ULVAC-OHI). The coin cell subjected to the Li deposition/dissolution 
test was disassembled in a glove box, and the surface was then washed 
with the HFE; subsequently, the sample was then moved from the glove 
box to the XPS sample chamber using a transfer vessel. 
Thermogravimetry/mass spectroscopy (TG-MS) was performed 
using a Bruker TG-MS setup to simultaneously monitor the decrease in 
weight and obtain mass spectra of the evolved gas component. The 
temperature ramp rate and range were 10 ◦C min−1 and 40–1000 ◦C, 
respectively. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using an X- 
ray diffractometer (Ultima IV, Rigaku) in an airtight cell to suppress the 
reaction of Li2S with water. 
2.4. Synthesis of Li2S/KB composite 
Li2S/KB composite was prepared by carothermal reduction of Li2SO4 
[50–53]. Li2SO4 monohydrate (0.6 g) and KB (1.6 g) were mixed for 30 
min in an agate mortar. The prepared Li2SO4/KB mixture was placed in a 
tube furnace (KTF347N1, Koyo Thermosystems) in an Ar atmosphere 
and sequentially subjected to 40, 200, and 800 ◦C for 1, 2, and 2 h, 
respectively, at a ramp rate of 10 ◦C min−1. Li2SO4 was reduced by KB to 
yield a Li2S/KB composite owing to the addition of excess KB. The ob­
tained composite was immediately transferred to an Ar-filled glove box 
and stored. The Li2S/KB composite had a 69.6:30.4 composition by 
weight. 
2.5. Fabrication of Li2S/KB composite cathode 
The preparation of an Li2S cathode sheet in an Ar-filled glove box 
equipped with a drying oven is described henceforth. A conditioning 
mixer (ARE-310; Thinky) was used to prepare the slurry. Low- 
molecular-weight PVP K30 (0.09 g) and NMP (0.60 g) were added to a 
vial, and the mixture was stirred at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The MWCNTs 
(0.03 g), AB (0.143 g), VGCFs (0.137 g), and Super C (0.09 g) were 
successively added while stirring at 2000 rpm. The viscosity of the slurry 
was adjusted by adding an appropriate amount of NMP. The Li2S/KB 
composite (2.0 g) and NMP (1.2 g) were subsequently added while 
stirring at 2000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, high-molecular-weight PVP K90 
(0.21 g) and NMP (0.60 g) were added and stirred at 2000 rpm for 10 
min to prepare a slurry. The composition of the final slurry with Li2S/KB, 
VGCFs, AB, Super C, MWCNTs, PVP K30, and PVP K90 was 74.1/5.1/ 
5.3/3.3/1.1/3.3/7.8 by weight, respectively. The prepared slurry was 
applied to a carbon-coated aluminum foil using an applicator (MC20, 
Hohsen) to prepare the cathode sheet, which was dried in an oven in a 
glove box for 16 h at room temperature and then vacuum-dried at 80 ◦C 
for 16 h Li2S-loading on the composite cathode was 4.3 mg cm−2. 
2.6. Charge/discharge tests of anode-less Li2S cell 
To prepare a 2032-type coin cell, the composite Li2S cathode was 
punched to ø-13.8 mm and layered with two pieces of the Celgard 3501 
separator (ø-17 mm) and a copper foil anode (ø-16 mm), and an elec­
trolyte solution (75 μL) was added. The produced anode-less cell was 
charged and discharged under galvanostatic conditions using a charge/ 
discharge tester (HJ-SD8, Hokuto Denko). The initial charging was 
conducted at a low current of C/48 with a cutoff voltage of 4.2 V, and the 
subsequent discharging/charging processes were performed at a con­
stant current of C/24 with cut-off voltages of 1.1 and 3.3 V, respectively. 
A rest period of 10 min was employed between each charging and dis­
charging step. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Effect of electrolyte composition on reversibility of Li deposition/ 
dissolution 
Because Li deposition on a Cu substrate occurs after the SEI is 
formed, the composition and structure of the SEI affects the morphology 
of the deposited Li. The ratio of the binary salt (LiTFSA/LiFSA) was fixed 
at 0.9:0.1 (mol/mol) in this study, and the effect of the electrolyte 
composition on the reversibility was monitored, as shown in Fig. 1. For 
the 1 M Li[TFSA0.9LiFSA0.1]/SL electrolyte, the time evolution of the 
change in potential during deposition/dissolution became unstable 
within 10 cycles. In contrast, the use of the LHCE ([Li(SL)2] 
[TFSA0.9FSA0.1]-2HFE) led to a dramatically improved stability and 150 
h of reversible deposition/dissolution. This difference was presumably 
caused by the variation in the electrolyte-dependent composition of the 
SEI. 
The surface of the Li deposited on the Cu foils (5 mAh cm−2) was 
investigated by XPS to determine the actual difference in the SEI com­
positions (Fig. 2). The elemental compositions calculated by XPS are 
shown in the upper-left corners of Fig. 2 images, in addition to the re­
sults of the spectral deconvolution and assignments of the peaks. First, 1 
M Li[TFSA0.9LiFSA0.1]/SL and [Li(SL)2][TFSA0.9FSA0.1] (HCE, 3.0 M) 
were compared to determine the effect of concentration (Fig. 2a and b, 
respectively). The peak intensity of the F1s spectrum increased when the 
electrolyte concentration was increased. Moreover, the surface F 
composition increased significantly from 2.50% to 12.62%. As described 
in the Introduction, the anions coordinated to Li+ in the HCE can 
participate in the formation of the SEI, and consequently, the F1s peak 
intensity can increase. Because the formation of LiF was suppressed in an 
HCE consisting of LiTFSA and SL [58], the LiF was presumably formed 
by the reduction of LiFSA. In the O1s XPS profiles, the low- and 
high-energy peaks (red and blue) were assigned to Li2O and SOx,

3.2. Effect of initial Li-deposition conditions on the reversibility of Li 
deposition/dissolution 
When Li is deposited on a copper foil (another metal), the 
morphology of the deposit is known to significantly affect the revers­
ibility of the Li deposition/dissolution [10,62]. A thermodynamic 
free-energy barrier exists for the nucleation of a new solid phase on 
another metal layer, which corresponds to the thermodynamic costs for 
forming a critical cluster of atoms. Pei et al. reported two important 
overpotentials that can be experimentally observed during galvanostatic 
Li deposition: the nucleation overpotential (ηn), which is the magnitude 
of the voltage spike at the beginning of Li deposition, and the Li-growth 
overpotential (ηp), which is the plateau overpotential following nucle­
ation [10]. Because the deposition of Li onto existing nuclei is more 
favorable than that onto another metal (such as a Cu substrate), ηp is 
considerably smaller than ηn. Therefore, ηn significantly affects the 
morphology of the deposited Li. Furthermore, the smaller the ηn at 
deposition, the larger and more uniform the size of the deposited Li 
nuclei [10]. In contrast, as ηn increases, the critical nuclear radius de­
creases (r* ∝ ηn
−1), and the areal density increases (N ∝ ηn
3) [10]. Addi­
tionally, the thermodynamic free-energy barrier for nucleation is 
significantly affected by the surface of the depositing substrate, that is, 
the composition and nature of the SEI formed prior to Li deposition. In 
the nucleation and growth mechanism of Li deposition, mechanically 
unstable SEIs can be readily fractured owing to localized stresses, which 
facilitates dendrite growth during deposition/dissolution cycles. 
Furthermore, side reactions during the nucleation and growth in an 
electrolyte solution increase the electrolyte consumption, leading to 
electrolyte depletion, short circuit, and cell failure [62]. The side re­
actions with the electrolyte must be considered in analyses of 
Li-deposition/dissolution cycles. Thus, large and uniform Li deposits, 
accompanied by a small ηn, result in a small surface area, thereby 
minimizing the SEI fracture and side reactions with the electrolyte as

The morphologies of the Li deposited on the Cu foils under different 
Li-deposition conditions were examined by SEM. Fig. 3a–c shows the 
differences among the three different electrolytes: the 1 M solution, 
HCE, and LHCE. Fine-needle-shaped moss-like Li was deposited when

Investigation of the change in the overpotential during galvanostatic 
deposition (Fig. 3d) revealed that increasing the electrolyte concentra­
tion (from the 1 M solution to the HCE) significantly reduced the ηn 
during deposition. Furthermore, in the case of the LHCE, ηn became 
considerably smaller, and the potential of the subsequent Li-growth 
process (ηp) also decreased. Because the magnitude of the ionic con­
ductivity at 30 ◦C followed the 1 M solution (2.77 mS cm−1) > the LHCE 
(1.06 mS cm−1) > the HCE (0.60 mS cm−1), the correlation between ηn 
and the deposit size was valid even if the contribution of iR drop on the 
ηn was taken into consideration. Therefore, in terms of morphology, the 
deposited Li became larger and more uniform with decreasing ηn. 
Subsequently, the effects of the current density and temperature used 
for galvanostatic deposition on the morphology of the deposits were 
examined for the LHCE. Fig. 4a and b shows SEM images of the Li 
deposited at 0.5 mA cm−2 at two different temperatures (30 and 60 ◦C), 
respectively, whereas Fig. 4c and d shows those corresponding to 0.1 
mA cm−2 at 30 and 60 ◦C, respectively. The morphology of the deposited 
Li was found to differ considerably upon changing the deposition tem­
perature and current density. The size of the deposits increased with 
increasing deposition temperature and decreasing deposition current 
density. The deposits obtained at 60 ◦C and 0.1 mA cm−2 were granular 
aggregates with several tens of micrometers in size. The dependence of 
ηn on the aforementioned deposition conditions was subsequently 
investigated (Fig. 5a). A clear correspondence was observed between the 
size of the deposits and ηn, with smaller ηn values leading to larger de­
posits, as reported in the literature. The ηn values from Fig. 5a are 157.8, 
45.5, 72, and 30.4 mV (gray, pink, blue, and green profiles; 0.5, 0.5, 0.1, 
and 0.1 mA cm−2; 30, 60, 30, and 60 ◦C, respectively). According to the 
XPS results (Fig. S2), increasing the deposition temperature increased 
the elemental composition of F and S and reduced that of C. 
Additionally, the surface N composition increased from 6.62% to 7.78% 
by the temperature increase. Because the SEI formation was initiated at 
potentials higher than that of the Li deposition, the change in voltage 
related to SEI formation prior to Li deposition was investigated (Fig. 5b), 
which indicated that a higher amount of electricity was used for SEI 
formation at 60 ◦C than that at 30 ◦C. An SEI with thicker and greater 
mechanical stability was formed at 60 ◦C than that at 30 ◦C. 
Fig. 6 shows the surface morphologies of the LHCE-based Li deposits 
after 20 dissolution/deposition cycles. The initial deposition (5 mAh 
cm−2) was performed under different conditions, as described in Fig. 6 
images; however, the subsequent dissolution/deposition cycles (0.5 mA 
cm−2 for 2 h) were all conducted at 30 ◦C. A comparison between the 
SEM images in Figs. 4 and 6 revealed that the size of the initial deposit 
was maintained more or less even after 20 cycles under the identical 
cycling conditions. The average Coulombic efficiency over 20 cycles, as 
shown in Fig. 6, was 92.9% for the initial deposition at 30 ◦C and 0.5 mA 
cm−2, 95.3% at 60 ◦C and 0.5 mA cm−2, 95.3% at 30 ◦C and 0.1 mA 
cm−2, and 95.9% at 60 ◦C and 0.1 mA cm−2. It is worth noting that the 
initial deposition conditions and the form of the deposit significantly 
affected the form of the subsequent deposit and Coulombic efficiency. 
The larger the initial deposits, the higher the efficiency. In the case of 
galvanostatic Li deposition, an instantaneous nucleation event occurs, 
followed by the continuous growth of the nuclei, which results in a 
relatively static nuclear density [10,62]. Therefore, the initial nucle­
ation overpotential (Fig. 5a) directly governs the nuclear density, and 
consequently, the size and homogeneity of the deposits. Small inho­
mogeneous Li deposits have a large surface area, which causes repeti­
tious 
fracture/regrowth 
of 
SEI 
layers, 
increased 
electrolyte 
consumption, and the formation of inactive (dead) Li during Li deposi­
tion/dissolution cycles. The preservation of large uniform Li deposits 
assists in maintaining the initial SEI, which enhances the Coulombic 
efficiency of the Li deposition/dissolution.

electrolyte solutions and the initial deposition conditions significantly 
affected the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution on Cu foil. The 
HCE and LHCE achieved greater reversibility than that of the 1 M so­
lution. Furthermore, the reversibility was affected by the initial depo­
sition conditions, with the reduction in current density and increase in 
deposition temperature enhancing the reversibility. The morphology of

3.3. Characterization of Li2S/KB composite 
The synthesized Li2S/KB composite was characterized by TG-MS and 
XRD, as shown in Fig. 7a and b. The weight loss at approximately 100 ◦C, 
corresponding to the gas evolution at m/z = 18 (blue line), was due to 
the desorption of crystalline water and adsorbed water from Li2SO4. The 
weight loss at approximately 800 ◦C was attributed to the following 
reaction:  
Li2SO4 + 2C → Li2S + 2CO2 ↑,                                                              
because of the large weight loss and gas evolution at m/z = 44 (orange 
line). Therefore, the heat treatment of the Li2SO4/KB mixture at 800 ◦C 
was confirmed to yield a Li2S/KB composite. Fig. 7b shows the XRD 
patterns of the synthesized Li2S/KB composite (blue line), with diffrac­

the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (311) planes of cubic Li2S (JCPDS 23- 
0369). 
The prepared Li2S/KB composite cathode sheet was subjected to SEM 
and EDX analyses (Fig. 7c and d, respectively); the blue and yellow 
colors in Fig. 7d represent the presence of carbon and sulfur (Li2S), 
respectively. Li2S crystals that were several micrometers in size were 
found to be uniformly distributed in the carbon matrix on the electrode 
surface. 
3.4. Properties of anode-less Cu || Li2S battery 
Anode-less Cu||Li2S cells were prepared using the LHCE, which 
provided optimal results in terms of the reversibility of Li deposition/ 
dissolution. Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed on 
cells consisting of a Li2S/KB composite cathode sheet and a copper foil 
anode. The initial charging was conducted at C/48, after which the 
discharging and charging steps were repeated at C/24. Fig. S3 shows the 
charge/discharge curves obtained when charging/discharging was 
performed at 30 ◦C, including the initial charging (activation of Li2S). A 
high overpotential is required to convert crystalline cubic Li2S to S8 in 
terms of the activation of Li2S, which caused the charge curve to oscil­
late. This high overpotential can be reduced using strategies such as 
reducing the Li2S crystal grain size, increasing the charging temperature, 
and reducing the charging current density [53]. Fig. 8a shows the 
charge/discharge curves obtained when the initial charging and subse­
quent discharging/charging were performed at 60 and 30 ◦C, respec­
tively. The overpotential was significantly reduced by performing the 
initial charging at 60 ◦C. It is worth noting that the initial charging also 
corresponded to the deposition of Li onto the copper anode and that Li 
deposition at an elevated temperature (60 ◦C) provided superior Li 
deposition/dissolution reversibility, as discussed in Section 3.2. Fig. 8b 
shows the cycling characteristics and Coulombic efficiency of the 
anode-less batteries obtained under these two conditions (initial 
charging at 30 and 60 ◦C). The anode-less batteries could be charged and 
discharged for 75 cycles, although capacity decay was apparent. No 
Li-dendrite-induced short circuit was observed. The average Coulombic 
efficiency over 75 cycles (Fig. 8b) was 96.7%, and no difference was 
observed with respect to the initial charging conditions. However, the 
average capacity retention per cycle were 98.0% and 98.6% for the 
initial charging at 30 and 60 ◦C, respectively, possibly reflecting the 
morphological differences in the Li deposits. The larger granular size 
and smaller surface area of the Li deposited at 60 ◦C than those at 30 ◦C 
presumably assisted in reducing the capacity decay. The capacity 
retention rates after 50 cycles were 38.4% and 47.5% for the initial 
charging at 30 and 60 ◦C, respectively. The achieved Coulombic effi­
ciency for the Li deposition/dissolution in this study is 95.3% and 95.9% 
for the first deposition at 30 ◦C and 60 ◦C, respectively, as shown in 
Fig. 6, by which the capacity decay is understandable. Regarding the

have been a few previous reports that utilize conventional LPS soluble 
ether-based electrolytes (1,3-dioxolane/dimethoxyethane, 1/1 by vol­
ume) electrolytes containing LiTFSA and LiNO3) [63–65]. The capacity 
retention after 50 cycles ranged from ~36% to ~66%, however, it was 
greatly improved by using Cu/Au anode instead of Cu anode [63], by 
adding Te into the Li2S cathode [64], and by replacing the liquid

4. Conclusions 
In this study, the possibility of realizing an anode-less battery with a 
Li2S cathode was investigated. To date, HCEs with SL solvent can 
function as sparingly solvating electrolytes in Li–S batteries [40–42]. 
Furthermore, the solubility of LPSs can be further suppressed by LHCEs, 
that is, HCEs diluted with a non-coordinating HFE solvent [40–42]. 
Additionally, the preparation of a Li2S/carbon composite by the reduc­
tion of Li2SO4 with carbon has been investigated by several groups 
[50–52]. LiFSA/SL-based HCEs and LHCEs are known to exhibit excel­
lent reversibility in Li deposition/dissolution reactions [24]. However, 
with respect to sulfur-based battery operation, LiFSA causes side re­
actions with LPSs, indicating the inapplicability of LiFSA alone [59,60]. 
Therefore, binary LiTFSA/LiFSA salts (9:1 molar ratio) in SL solvent 
were used in the present study as electrolyte solutions to realize an 
anode-less Cu||Li2S battery, and the reversibility of the Li deposi­
tion/dissolution reactions was examined. Furthermore, anode-less Cu|| 
Li2S batteries were fabricated under the conditions optimized for 
reversibility, 
and 
their 
charge/discharge 
characteristics 
were 
determined. 
The composition of the electrolyte solutions significantly influenced 
the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution. The HCE ([Li(SL)2] 
[TFSA0.9FSA0.1]) and LHCE ([Li(SL)2][TFSA0.9FSA0.1]-2HFE) exhibited 
higher reversibility than that of the 1 M solution (1 M Li 
[TFSA0.9LiFSA0.1]/SL). SEIs with high F compositions and relatively low 
O compositions were formed on the Li surfaces deposited from the HCE 
and LHCE. SEI formation by the decomposition of anions, particularly 
FSA−, was promoted in the HCE and LHCE, whereas the decomposition 
of SL was prohibited by the formation of the fluorine-rich SEI. The 
morphology of the deposited Li was considerably affected by the 
nucleation overpotential at the initial deposition, which was influenced 
by the nature of the formed SEIs. For the electrolyte solution with 
adequate reversibility, the nucleation overpotential was low, the num­
ber of Li nuclei was small, the grown Li was uniform and large, and the 
surface area was consequently small. Because repetitive Li deposition/ 
dissolution reactions involve fracture/regrowth of the SEI layers, 
deposition of Li with a small surface area, accompanied by a low 
nucleation overpotential, suppressed the decomposition of the electro­
lyte solution, particularly the solvent, and the formation of dead Li, 
resulting in high reversibility. The highest Coulombic efficiency in this 
study was achieved using the LHCE when the initial deposition was 
performed at 60 ◦C at a low current density. Based on these results, 
anode-less Cu||Li2S batteries were fabricated using a Li2S/KB composite 
sheet cathode with the LHCE, which resulted in 75 charge/discharge 
cycles. The capacity retention of the cell with the initial charging reac­
tion (8Li2S →16Li + S8) at 60 ◦C was superior to that at 30 ◦C. The initial 
deposition of Li was found to be crucial in determining the Li deposi­
tion/dissolution reversibility, as well as in influencing the performance 
of the anode-less battery with the Li2S cathode.
