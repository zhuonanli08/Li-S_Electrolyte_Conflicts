A B S T R A C T
For lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs), transition region metals offer d electron counts from 1 to 10, making them an 
ideal platform to study electronic configuration and catalytic performance. This study selects zinc and cobalt 
because their 3d orbitals lie at two extremes: Zn2+ has a fully filled [Ar]3d10 configuration, while Co2+ has an 
unsaturated [Ar]3d7 configuration. Such extreme differences reveal a long overlooked hidden variable: the 
transition region d electron count. Using Zinc(II) Bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Zn(TFSI)2) and Cobalt bis 
(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (Co(TFSI)2) as model additives for LSBs, we demonstrate that the decisive factor 
is not the metal type but the 3d orbital occupancy. This hidden variable leads the two systems toward distinct 
pathways in catalyzing polysulfide conversion and suppressing lithium dendrites.In the d10 system (Zn2+), an 
electric field drives Zn2+ to react with polysulfides, forming an in situ ZnS nanocatalytic interface. This interface 
accelerates sulfur redox and suppresses shuttling, but the fully filled d orbital causes strong electron localization, 
offering limited dendrite suppression. In the d7 system (Co2+), the higher d band center induces strong orbital 
hybridization, generating ultrafine CoS nanocrystals with abundant defects for targeted polysulfide capture. A 
reversible Co2+/Co3+ redox cycle lowers the reaction barrier and accelerates conversion kinetics. Moreover, the 
unsaturated 3d7 configuration enables an electron/ion dual conductive interface, significantly inhibiting lithium

1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs) have emerged as promising alterna­
tives to conventional lithium-ion batteries owing to their low cost and 
high specific capacity (1672 mAh g−1) [1]. However, the insulating 
nature of sulfur and its discharge products, electrochemical instability of 
lithium metal anodes, and lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) shuttle effect 
remain major barriers to their commercialization [2]. Soluble LiPSs 
readily migrate to the anode through the shuttle effect, where they react 
with metallic lithium, while insoluble short-chain sulfides (Li2S/Li2S2) 
deposit on the anode surface. The continuous loss of active material and 
persistent shuttling collectively result in reduced sulfur utilization, low 
Coulombic efficiency, lithium corrosion, and rapid capacity fading [3]. 
Strategies proposed to address these issues include advanced sulfur 
cathode design [4], electrolyte optimization [5], separator modification 
[6], and binder development [7]. Nevertheless, modifications to cath­
odes, separators, and binders remain confined to localized catalytic in­
terfaces, where active sites are easily passivated by the accumulation of 
Li2S/Li2S2 [8]. As the core component permeates the entire electrode, 
electrolytes provide essential ionic conduction pathways [9]. Conse­
quently, electrolyte optimization represents one of the most cost- 
effective approaches for enhancing battery performance [10].
Electrolyte properties can be modified by incorporating additives 
without altering the primary constituents. A conventional example is 
LiNO3, which enables the in situ formation of a stable solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) on lithium anodes, thereby suppressing the LiPSs 
shuttle effect [11]. However, most additive studies have investigated 
mechanisms indirectly, relying on macroscopic performance indicators 
such as capacity and cycle life. These assessments often focus on 
chemical bond formation, the incorporation of redox mediators, and the 
reduction of reaction barriers, while providing limited insight into how 
atomic and electronic configurations dictate catalytic behavior and 
overall battery performance [12–16].To address this gap, the present 
study introduces orbital energy level theory (widely applied in advanced 
sulfur cathode design [17,18]) and selects Zn2+ and Co2+ with distinct 
electron configurations as electrolyte additives to examine their influ­
ence on LSBs performance from the perspective of outer electron 
structure.
The fundamentally different 3d-orbital electron configurations of 
Zn2+ ([Ar]3d10) and Co2+ ([Ar]3d7) directly dictate their catalytic be­
haviors toward LiPSs. The d-electron count, as a “hidden variable,” 
differs from traditional descriptors such as d-band center or electro­
negativity by governing the coordination mode with LiPSs and the for­
mation pathway of in-situ catalytic interfaces. Specifically, with fully 
occupied 3d orbitals, Zn2+ primarily drives the in-situ formation of ZnS 
nanoparticles through reactions with LiPSs, thereby enhancing catalytic 
activity. In contrast, the unsaturated 3d7 orbitals of Co2+ provide 
abundant electronic states that promote targeted adsorption of LiPSs via 
strong coordination and d-p orbital hybridization, enabling the in-situ 
formation of CoS catalytic interfaces that act as redox mediators to 
accelerate LiPSs conversion. These distinct electrocatalytic behaviors, 
dictated directly by electronic structure, govern the contrasting capa­
bilities of Zn2+ and Co2+ in suppressing the shuttle effect and improving 
sulfur cathode conversion efficiency, ultimately reflected in battery 
performance. Furthermore, both cations promote solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) formation on the anode, suppressing side reactions 
between polysulfides and lithium metal.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Preparation of electrolyte
The materials and solvents were purchased from commercial sup­
pliers (Aldrich, RHAWN, Dodochem). The baseline electrolyte consisted 
of 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 2.0 wt% 
LiNO3, and a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3- 
dioxolane (DOL). Zinc(II) Bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Zn 
(TFSI)2, >98%) or Cobalt bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (Co(TFSI)2, 
>98%) was added at 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 wt% and stirred until homogeneous. 
All preparations were carried out in an argon-filled glove box with O2 
and H2O levels below 0.01 ppm.
2.2. Preparation of sulfur cathode
A sulfur‑carbon composite was prepared by blending 3 g of Super P 
conductive carbon (AR, >99.5%) with 7 g of sublimated sulfur (AR, 
>99.5%) (3:7 weight ratio). The mixture was ground for 30 min, then 
heated in an argon-filled tube furnace at 155 ◦C for 12 h. The resulting 
composite was mixed with additional Super P and PVDF binder at a mass 
ratio of 7:2:1, dissolved in 1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, >99.5%), 
coated onto aluminum foil, and vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 8 h. The dried 
electrodes were punched into 12 mm discs (sulfur content: 49%). Cel­
gard 2500 was used as the separator, lithium metal as the anode, and 
2016-type coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box using 
stainless steel casings.
2.3. Electrochemical measurements
During electrochemical testing, current rates and specific capacity 
were referenced to the sulfur mass in the cathode (1C = 1672 mA g−1). 
Cycling, cyclic voltammetry (CV) (0.1 mV s−1, 1.7–2.8 V), electro­
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) (100 kHz–0.01 Hz, 10 mV) and 
galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) (0.2 mA pulse, 
120 s) were measured using Land, LANHE, Autolab, and Neware sys­
tems. The Li+ diffusion coefficient was obtained from the Randles-Sevcik 
relationship. For Li2S nucleation tests, CR2016 cells (Li|Li2S8) with 
lithium anode, carbon paper cathode, and Celgard 2500 separator were 
used. Control cells contained 20 μL 0.1 M Li2S8 + 15 μL baseline elec­
trolyte; modified cells used 1 wt% Zn(TFSI)2 or Co(TFSI)2 in baseline. 
Cells were discharged to 2.11 V at 0.112 mA, then held at 2.09 V until 
current dropped below 10−5 A.
3. Results and discussion

light purple. With increasing additive concentration, light yellow and 
black solid precipitates were observed (Fig. S1a). The significant color 
changes indicated that reactions occurred between the additives and 
LiPSs. To further verify these interactions, Raman spectroscopy and 
UV–visible absorption spectroscopy of the supernatant were performed. 
In the Raman spectra, the intensity of the long-chain LiPSs peaks 
decreased, while that of the short-chain LiPSs peaks increased, demon­
strating that both additives accelerated the conversion of long-chain

this process, the local environment of hydrogen atoms in ether solvent 
molecules was analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 1c). The 1H NMR 
spectra indicated that the characteristic peaks of ether solvent molecules 
shifted upfield, confirming their release from the solvation shell layer of 
long-chain Li2S6/Li2S8, thus inhibiting the shuttle effect [19,20]. In 
addition, a symmetrical battery devoid of any lithium source was 
assembled using the supernatant, and CV measurements were conducted 
(Fig. 1d). No distinct peaks were observed for pure Li2S6, whereas 
electrolyte additives yielded larger peak areas and higher peak in­
tensities, which demonstrated enhanced LiPSs conversion kinetics [21]. 
Notably, the CV curve for Co(TFSI)2 exhibited obvious fluctuations. This 
can be attributed to the variable valence of Co2+ (Co2+/Co3+ redox pair) 
originating from its unsaturated 3d7 orbital, as well as the strong 
adsorption-desorption behavior of polysulfides on the carbon paper 
electrode, reflecting the dynamic reconstruction of the catalytic inter­
face. In contrast, the Zn(TFSI)2 system showed a smooth curve due to the 
fully filled 3d10 configuration of Zn2+, which lacks variable valence and 
strong interaction-induced fluctuations.
To investigate the role of excess precipitates in LSBs, the cleaned 
precipitate was added to commercial electrolytes, and batteries were 
assembled for CV testing (Fig. 1e). The results suggested that the peak 
currents of both the oxidation and low-voltage reduction peaks in the 
first cycle CV curves increased, indicating that the precipitates provided 
additional catalytic sites and promoted LiPSs conversion. However, the 
peak current of the high-potential reduction peak was lower than that of 
the control group, and the polarization voltage difference increased, 
suggesting that the conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S was inhibited 
during the reduction process. The excess accumulation of solid catalyst 
particles not only occupied the effective surface area available for Li2S 
deposition but also obstructed ion and reactant diffusion pathways. 
Consequently, Li2S4 was unable to effectively reach catalytic sites, while 
the generated Li2S2/Li2S struggled to deposit at suitable sites and form 
conductive pathways. In addition, precipitates obtained from the Zn2+
−and Co2+-containing electrolyte systems exhibited distinct differences 
due to the contrasting 3d orbital electronic structures of Zn2+ (3d10 
closed shell) and Co2+ (3d7 high spin). The Co2+-containing system 
showed a significantly higher first-cycle peak position with a negative 
shift, followed by gradual recovery (Fig. S1b). Compared with the Zn2+- 
containing system, the Co2+-containing system exhibited higher first- 
cycle peak currents, indicating the presence of more catalytic sites and 
suggesting that its catalyst particles were finer in size. The negative peak 
displacement and subsequent recovery further indicated that the con­
ductivity of cobalt-containing catalysts was superior to that of zinc- 
containing catalysts. To confirm the impact on capacity, identical bat­
teries were assembled and cycled at a current density of 0.2C for 100 
cycles. The cobalt- and zinc-containing systems both presented 
increased capacities compared with the control (Fig. 1f), confirming the 
improved performance of batteries incorporating these precipitates and 
reflecting the catalytic contribution of the precipitate, which was 
consistent with the CV results.
The composition of the nanocatalysts was further confirmed through 
XPS multi-channel analysis of the precipitates (C 1 s, O 1 s, Zn 2p, Co 2p, 
and S 2p) (Fig. 1g–j, S1c–f). The combined analysis of the Zn 2p and Co 
2p spectra with the S 2p characteristic peaks confirmed the formation of 
ZnS and CoS catalysts. In cobalt-containing systems, the Co 2p spectrum 
also revealed the coexistence of Co2+ and Co3+, confirming the active 
involvement of the Co2+/Co3+ redox couple. High-resolution trans­
mission electron microscopy (HRTEM) further characterized the pre­
cipitates (Fig. 1k, l, S2a–f, S3a–f). The lattice fringe spacing of 0.31 nm 
corresponded to the (111) crystal plane of ZnS, while the 0.26 nm 
spacing matched the (101) plane of CoS. These results confirmed the 
successful establishment of crystalline ZnS and CoS phases, demon­
strating the in situ construction of the nanocatalysts.
To investigate the distribution characteristics of the nanocatalysts, 
XPS compositional analysis was first performed on the sulfur cathode 
after five cycles at a current density of 0.5C (Fig. 2a, b, S4a–f, S5a–f), 
confirming the presence of in situ-generated nano-catalytic particles on 
the electrode surface. Further characterization by energy-dispersive X- 
ray spectroscopy (EDS) surface scanning (Fig. 2c) revealed that Zn, Co, 
and S were uniformly dispersed across the electrode surface, indicating 
that the catalysts achieved global regulation of LiPSs conversion ki­
netics.Notably, the relative atomic abundance of cobalt was significantly 
higher than that of zinc, which is attributed to the smaller particle size 
and superior dispersion of CoS nanoparticles (Fig. S6a, b). In the GITT 
test, the relaxation voltage drop in the charge-discharge curve (arrows in 
Fig. 2d–f) effectively characterized the evolution of interface impedance 
during Li2S nucleation and oxidation [22,23]. Batteries incorporating 
electrolyte additives exhibited lower relaxation voltage drops at these 
stages, confirming reduced electrode polarization and improved reac­
tion kinetics. The Ip-v1/2 linear relationship was further established 
through variable sweep rate CV testing (Fig. 2 g–i, S6c–e), and lithium- 
ion diffusion coefficients were calculated using the Randles-Sevcik 
equation (Table S1) [24]. Reduction peak 1 corresponded to the con­
version from Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S. The lithium-ion diffusion coefficient in 
the zinc-containing system was 5.05 × 10−8 cm2/s, while that in the 
cobalt-containing system was 3.84 × 10−8 cm2/s. Reduction peak 2 
represented the conversion of Li2S8 to Li2S4, where the zinc-containing 
system exhibited a diffusion coefficient of 7.36 × 10−8 cm2/s, whereas 
the cobalt-containing system demonstrated a significantly higher value 
of 1.68 × 10−7 cm2/s. These results indicated that the cobalt-containing 
electrolytes facilitated a targeted catalytic mechanism during the Li2S8- 
to-Li2S4 conversion, while the diffusion coefficient decreased during the 
Li2S4-to-Li2S2/Li2S step due to the strong adsorption effect of unsatu­
rated 3d7 orbitals of Co2+, which hindered polysulfide desorption during 
the liquid-solid transition. Consequently, short-chain sulfur in the solid 
phase cannot diffuse independently, which may lead to the occupation 
of active sites. Oxidation peak 3 represents the oxidation process of 
Li2S2/Li2S. The lithium-ion diffusion coefficient in the additive-free 
system was 5.06 × 10−8 cm2/s, whereas the values increased to 1.15 
× 10−7 cm2/s with zinc additives and 1.59 × 10−7 cm2/s with cobalt 
additives. Furthermore, the diffusion coefficient of the cobalt-containing 
system was consistently higher than that of the zinc-containing system. 
This contradictory phenomenon confirmed that the accelerated solid- 
liquid conversion was enabled by reversible Co2+/Co3+ redox cycling.

(Fig. 2j-l). Compared with the additive-free system, which delivered a 
deposition capacity of 361.22 mAh g−1, the zinc-containing system 
achieved 385.12 mAh g−1, and the cobalt-containing system reached 
596.04 mAh g−1. Both systems also exhibited shorter nucleation 
response times. These results demonstrated that the incorporation of 
electrolyte additives significantly promoted the conversion of Li2Sx (4 ≤

Building on this foundation, first-principles calculations based on 
Density Functional Theory (DFT) were performed to elucidate the reg­
ulatory mechanisms of cobalt-containing electrolyte additives at the 
atomic and electronic levels. The results indicated that the unique un­
saturated 3d7 electronic configuration of Co2+ enabled strong orbital 
hybridization. Under the driving force of an external electric field during 
battery operation, Co2+ interacted directly with polysulfide ions, 
resulting in the in-situ formation of a structurally stable and catalytically 
active CoS (101) facet at the cathode interface. This CoS (101) surface 
not only provided abundant active sites for polysulfide adsorption and 
conversion but also markedly reduced the energy barrier for subsequent 
reactions owing to its distinctive electronic structure.
To quantitatively evaluate the affinity of the CoS (101) surface for 
polysulfides, the adsorption energies for lithium polysulfides with 
different chain lengths were systematically calculated (Fig. 3a–g). The 
results demonstrated that CoS (101) exhibited strong chemisorption of 
all long-chain polysulfides. The adsorption energy values were signifi­
cantly higher than the threshold for physisorption (generally < −0.8 
eV), confirming that the interactions exceeded the van der Waals forces 
and represented chemisorption driven by strong electronic interactions.

Remarkably, the adsorption energy of the CoS (101) surface for Li2S6 
reached −7.865 eV, whereas that of Li2S8 was −9.159 eV. These findings 
theoretically confirm the excellent targeted capture capability of CoS 
interfaces toward long-chain LiPSs.
To further clarify the electron redistribution behavior during 
adsorption, differential charge density analysis was performed 
(Fig. 3h–j). Significant electron rearrangements were observed between 
the CoS (101) surface and LiPS molecules. The electron depletion region 
(blue) was localized near the Li–S bonds in the LiPS molecules, indi­
cating reduced electron cloud density in these areas due to surface in­
teractions. In contrast, electron accumulation (yellow) was concentrated 
in the newly generated bonding regions between Co and S atoms, 
demonstrating directional electron transfer from the CoS surface to LiPS 
molecules and establishing an effective charge migration channel. This 
direct evidence strongly indicates that the interaction between CoS 
(101) and LiPSs is not only attributed to simple physical adsorption or 
polarization but also involves substantial charge donation and sharing. 
This contributes to the generation of strongly coupled interfacial 
chemical bonds. This process effectively activated Li–S bonds and 
significantly lowered the energy barrier for subsequent polysulfide 
conversion. In summary, DFT calculations revealed that the unique 
electronic structure of Co2+ induced strong orbital hybridization, facil­
itating the in situ formation of a highly active CoS catalytic interface. 
This interface not only enabled the targeted anchoring of long-chain 
polysulfides via strong chemisorption but also functioned as an effi­
cient electron bridge, promoting interfacial charge transfer and accel­
erating the redox kinetics of polysulfides. These computational results 
theoretically confirm the essential role of cobalt-containing additives as 
redox mediators and provide valuable insights and design strategies for 
the development of high-performance electrolytes for lithium‑sulfur 
batteries.
To evaluate the regulatory effects of electrolyte additives on the 
lithium anode interface, EIS was conducted. Compared with the 
additive-free system, batteries containing electrolyte additives gener­
ated SEI layers on the lithium anode surface (Fig. 4 g), as evidenced by a 
significant membrane impedance (RSEI) signal. Concurrently, all 
additive-containing systems exhibited reduced charge-transfer imped­
ance, indicating accelerated interfacial kinetics. To further assess the 
protective role of the additives, the surface morphology of lithium metal 
after five cycles at 0.5C was examined (Fig. 4a–f). In the absence of 
additives, lithium surfaces displayed cracks and dendrites caused by 
LiPS shuttling and unstable SEI formation. In contrast, the surfaces in 
additive-containing systems were smooth and covered with dense SEI 
layers, which exhibited an inhibitory effect. Notably, the dendrite 
coverage in the Co(TFSI)2 system was markedly lower than that in the Zn 
(TFSI)2 system, and the dendrites appeared sparsely distributed, 
requiring longer times to fully cover the electrode. This difference is 
attributed to the strong bonding capacity of the unsaturated d orbitals in 
cobalt, which generates components with delocalized electronic struc­
tures [26]. The enhanced electronic conductivity within the SEI ho­
mogenizes the surface electric field, resulting in uniform lithium-ion 
flux, consistent deposition, delayed dendrite formation, and suppression 
of preferential dendrite growth.
The regulation of the SEI is critical for suppressing lithium dendrite 
growth in LSBs. As the SEI originates from the reduction products of 
electrolyte components, solvent decomposition products and solvated 
ions collectively constitute its primary constituents. To examine the SEI 
composition on the lithium metal surface after cycling, lithium anodes 
subjected to five cycles at a current density of 0.5C were analyzed, and 
the interfacial composition and valence evolution were investigated in 
depth using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The SEI composi­
tion was determined from the spectra of C 1 s, O 1 s, Li 1 s, Zn 2p, Co 2p, 
F 1 s, S 2p, and N 1 s (Fig. 4 h, i, S8a–f, and S9a–f), with the results 
summarized in Fig. 4i. Analysis revealed that the zinc-containing elec­
trolyte additive system produced a Zn/LiZn phase at the lithium anode 
interface, 
whereas 
the 
cobalt-containing 
system 
generated 
multifunctional composite phases of Co, CoNx, and CoSx. These differ­
ences originate from the high bonding ability of the unsaturated 3d7 
orbitals of Co2+, which facilitates the generation of metallic CoSx with 
delocalized electronic structures and highly ionic CoNx [27]. Overall, 
these phases establish an electron/ion dual-conductive interface that 
effectively suppresses dendrite growth. In contrast, Zn2+ is restricted to 
the formation of simple metallic or alloy phases owing to the localization 
of its fully occupied d-orbitals [28]. Although this represents an 
improvement over additive-free systems, its dendrite inhibition effect 
remains markedly inferior to that of cobalt-containing electrolytes. 
These results highlight the decisive role of d-orbital electron arrange­
ments in optimizing SEI composition.
To thoroughly evaluate the protective effect of additives on lithium 
anodes, sulfur-free Li‖Li symmetric cells were assembled (Fig. 4j, k). 
These cells exhibited low and stable deposition/stripping over­
potentials, confirming that dissolved Zn2+ and Co2+ ions effectively 
promoted uniform Li deposition and reversible stripping, thereby 
enhancing the stability of the anode interface [29,30]. On this basis, the 
real-world environment of polysulfide shuttling was simulated by 
replacing the base electrolyte with a polysulfide solution to assemble a 
symmetric battery. It was compared with a system in which the additive 
was dissolved in the polysulfide supernatant (Fig. S10a–d). The latter 
system displayed a lower deposition overpotential, which revealed the 
negative influence of excess precipitates generated by additive poly­
sulfide reactions on the lithium anode interface. These precipitates 
served as physical barriers, disrupted the lithium-ion flow uniformity, 
and compromised SEI integrity, which caused increased interfacial 
impedance, dendrite formation, and weakened interfacial protection 
[31]. On the other hand, when the additive remained in the dissolved 
ionic form in the polysulfide supernatant, its beneficial effect on lithium 
deposition kinetics was preserved. This finding further confirms that the 
presence of excess precipitates is detrimental to the interfacial stability 
of the lithium anode.
The aforementioned studies have revealed the accelerated mecha­
nism of polysulfide conversion kinetics and the distinct mechanisms of 
lithium anode protection arising from the differences in the d-orbital 
electron configurations of metal cations. Hence, this study further 
investigated the effect of Zn/Co-TFSI2 additives on the overall electro­
chemical performance of LSBs. At current densities of 0.2C and 0.5C, 
batteries containing 1 wt% Zn(TFSI)2 and 1 wt% Co(TFSI)2 electrolytes 
exhibited optimal electrochemical performance (Fig. S11a–d). After 200

system without electrolyte additives was only 49.4%, reflecting severe 
active material loss and accumulated shuttle effect. In contrast, the zinc- 
containing electrolyte additive system and cobalt-containing electrolyte 
additive system displayed significantly higher capacity retention rates of 
57.6% and 63.5%, respectively (Fig. 5c). This improvement directly 
substantiated the proposed mechanism, in which the superior retention 
rate of the cobalt-containing electrolyte additive system demonstrated 
its dual-functional advantage. Specifically, it provided chemical 
confinement and interfacial catalysis through CoS nanocrystals while 
simultaneously mediating the sulfur redox process via the reversible 
Co2+/Co3+ couple. However, at a high current density of 0.5C, the 
specific capacities of both systems nearly converged after 400 cycles 
(Fig. 5a). This behavior was mainly attributed to the intensified driving 
force for directed S2−
migration under strong electric fields, which 
induced the formation of large amounts of ZnS and CoS. Although the 
increased number of ZnS active sites improved the catalytic activity, the 
excessive generation of ultra-fine CoS particles led to electrode pore 
blockage, thereby hindering the full realization of catalytic benefits.

CV testing further elucidated the effect of electrolyte additives on the 
redox capacity of LSBs (Fig. 5e). Two reduction peaks were observed in 
the negative scan region, with a high-potential peak corresponding to 
sulfur reduction to polysulfides and a low-potential peak corresponding 
to polysulfide reduction forming Li2S2/Li2S [37]. In the positive scan 
region, a single oxidation peak was detected, corresponding to the 
oxidation of Li2S2/Li2S to polysulfides and their subsequent conversion 
back to sulfur [38,39]. Compared with batteries without electrolyte 
additives, the CV curves of batteries with electrolyte additives exhibited 
higher peak currents and smaller polarization voltage differences, 
indicating enhanced polysulfide redox kinetics. As anticipated, batteries 
containing electrolyte additives demonstrated superior rate perfor­
mance at all current densities (Fig. 5g, h). Even after the high-current- 
density testing and subsequent return to a low current density of 0.1C, 
LSBs with electrolyte additives retained the excellent electrochemical 
reversibility and high stability, thereby confirming the effectiveness of 
electrolyte additives in promoting improved redox kinetics and revers­
ibility in LSBs.
4. Conclusions
This study confirms that the transition region d electron count is 
precisely the overlooked hidden variable behind catalysis and dendrite 
suppression in LSBs. We selected two extreme representatives: Zn2+
(3d10, fully filled) and Co2+ (3d7, unsaturated). The difference in 
d orbital electronic configuration dictates their divergent behaviors. The 
unsaturated 3d7 orbital of Co2+ endows a variable valence state, 
enabling dynamic catalysis via the Co2+/Co3+ redox pair, and eventu­
ally surpasses the Zn2+ system in liquid liquid and solid liquid conver­
sions. However, the strong adsorption induced high desorption barrier 
limits Co2+ efficiency in liquid solid conversion. This trade off precisely 
reflects the complex regulatory role of the hidden variable. On the anode 
interface, the high bonding capability of Co2+ induces the formation of 
metallic CoSx and highly ion conductive CoNx, which together construct 
an electron/ion dual conductive interlayer to significantly inhibit 
lithium dendrites. In contrast, Zn2+ with its fully filled d orbital and 
strong electron localization can only form simple alloy phases, showing 
far inferior dendrite suppression. In summary, the d electron count is not 
a trivial atomic parameter but a core hidden variable that deeply couples 
catalytic pathways and interfacial chemistry. Understanding this vari­
able opens a new avenue for rationally designing high performance 
electrolyte additives for LSBs. And this concept is expected to be 
generalizable to other transition metals and other rechargeable battery 
systems, e.g., Na–S, K–S, and metal-air batteries.
