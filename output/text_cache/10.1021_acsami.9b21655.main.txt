ABSTRACT: The shuttle eﬀect of polysulﬁde and the ﬂammability of the
conventional electrolyte are the two major obstacles restricting the development
progress of lithium−sulfur batteries. Exploring highly eﬃcient electrolyte
components coupled with the conventional electrolyte is a reliable strategy to
solve these issues. However, the current electrolyte components usually relieve these
issues at the expense of the sacriﬁced electrochemical performance. Herein, a novel
zwitterionic ionic liquid named as TLTFSI is reported, which exhibits a high ionic
conductivity of 3.7 × 10−3 S cm−1, a wide electrochemical potential window from
1.51 to 4.82 V at 25 °C, and a high thermal decomposition temperature of 275 °C.
The optimized TLTFSI-based electrolyte is nonﬂammable and performs superior
electrochemical performance in terms of larger capacity, better rate capability, and
longer cyclic life compared with the conventional organic electrolyte. The robust
performance is attributed to the high intrinsic ionic conductivity, the suppressed
polysulﬁde dissolution/diﬀusion, and the high interface compatibility toward the lithium anode of the TLTFSI-based electrolytes.
This present work represents the ﬁrst demonstration of the zwitterionic ionic liquid to eﬃciently improve the overall electrochemical
performance and the safety of lithium−sulfur batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, zwitterionic ionic liquid, electrolyte, nonﬂammability, long cyclic life
1. INTRODUCTION
To meet today’s ever-growing demands for chemical power
supplies with a larger energy density than the current Li-ion
batteries, a great deal of eﬀort has been focused on developing
new battery chemistries enabling higher electrochemical
window and/or larger capacity. Among all the potential
choices, lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have caught the
worldwide interest and are considered as one of the most
promising candidates to achieve the goal of high-energy-
density battery chemistry through the usage of earth-abundant
and high-capacity sulfur positive electrode coupled with
metallic lithium anode (the theoretic speciﬁc capacity is
1675 mAh g−1 based on the mass of sulfur).1,2 Unfortunately,
two main obstacles limit the development progress of LSBs
toward commercial application. One is the shuttle eﬀect of
lithium polysulﬁde, and the other is the safety issue. The
shuttle eﬀect of lithium polysulﬁde of LSBs can directly lead to
the short cyclic life of LSBs. The reason for the shuttle eﬀect is
mainly due to the high solubility of lithium polysulﬁdes in the
organic electrolyte.3 Many eﬀorts have been devoted to
mitigate this issue from the aspects of the cathode,4−6
electrolyte,7−12 and separator.13,14 The safety issue originated
from the ﬂammability of the commonly used electrolyte
solvents for LSBs, for example, the most popular 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME). Although the results
of the nail penetration test suggested that the organic
electrolytes used in LSBs had no thermal runaway unlike the
typical lithium-ion battery,15 the organic electrolytes used are
still ﬂammable in nature, which might be on ﬁre under certain
circumstances. The ﬂammable organic electrolyte provides
most of the burning energy of the batteries,16 implying the
signiﬁcance of exploring novel electrolyte systems or additives
that can embrace the nonﬂammable advantage and fast Li+
diﬀusion simultaneously.
In this context, developing advanced electrolyte systems play
a critical role in alleviating the above two challenges. Several
new types of electrolyte systems like solid-state electrolytes and
ionic liquids (ILs) have been designed to decrease the
ﬂammability and the solubility of lithium polysulﬁdes for
LSBs. For instance, Xu et al.17 reported a solid-state electrolyte
Li7P2.9Mn0.1S10.7I0.3, which had a high ionic conductivity of 5.6
× 10−3 S cm−1 at room temperature and a wide voltage
stability up to 5.0 V versus Li/Li+. The obtained LSBs with the
Li7P2.9Mn0.1S10.7I0.3 electrolyte showed higher capacity reten-
tion than those with liquid electrolyte. However, such all-solid-
state LSBs performed inferior rate capability and large

suppress the dissolution/diﬀusion of polysulﬁde because of the

Thus, the cyclic stability of LSBs can be improved to some
extent with the aid of ILs. However, the high viscosity and the
low dissolution of polysulﬁde also limited the speciﬁc capacity
(600−800 mAh g−1) and rate capability of LSBs (no more

mixture of ILs with organic liquid was proposed.16,20−24
Through optimizing the mixture ratio, the speciﬁc capacity and
cyclic performance of LSBs have been improved eﬀectively.
For example, Liao et al.24 constructed a mixture of IL (N-
methyl-N-propylpyrrolidinium bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)-
imide ([MPPY][TFSI])) and sulfone (methylisopropylsulfone
[MIPS]). With the mixture of 1.0 M LiTFSI, 40 vol % MIPS,
and 60 vol % [MPPY][TFSI] as the optimum electrolyte, the
obtained LSBs exhibited a high speciﬁc capacity of 1265 mAh
g−1 at the ﬁrst cycle and 655 mAh g−1 after 50th cycle at 0.05
C.24 However, the rate capability is still an intractable issue for
these IL-based LSBs. The development of more eﬃcient
electrolyte components should be highly impressive for safe
and long cycle life LSBs.
Zwitterionic ILs, ﬁrst pioneered by Ohno and co-workers, is
a kind of IL with the capability of tethering the anion and
cation together.25 With this unique characteristic, two
advantages can be gained. The ﬁrst is the less mobility. One
reason is the increased interaction between cations and anions
since two ion pairs exist in the system. The other reason is the
inhibited electro-migration due to pinging the cation and anion
in one molecule. The less mobility implies the less diﬀusion of
polysulﬁde when zwitterionic ILs employed as LSBs electrolyte
composition. The second is the higher Li+ conductivity. The
carrier ion in common ILs can hardly migrate dominantly
because all of the component ions are majority in number and
have high mobility.26,27 In the zwitterionic ILs system, the
mobility of the counterpart of carrier salt is inhibited and the
number of mobile cation and anion are synthesized in an equal
stoichiometric ratio.28,29 In general, zwitterionic ILs can
provide higher Li+ conductivity and more ability to suppress
the diﬀusion of polysulﬁde than the common ILs, suggesting
that the zwitterionic ILs would be a fascinating electrolyte
component. With this in mind, we synthesized a novel
zwitterionic IL, tris(dioxa-3,6-heptyl)aminesulfonate coupled
with bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (named as
TLTFSI), for LSBs. The TLTFSI exhibited a robust ionic
conductivity of 3.7 × 10−3 S cm−1, a wide electrochemical
potential window from 1.51 to 4.82 V, a high viscosity of 0.262
Pa·s, and a high thermal decomposition temperature of 275 °C.
When used as an LSBs electrolyte component, the mixture
ratio of TLTFSI and conventional organic electrolytes was
systematically investigated. The optimal electrolyte T70D30
consisted of 1 wt % LiNO3, 70 vol % TLTFSI, and 30 vol %
DDL that contained 50 vol % 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), 50 vol %

These results show an attractive demonstration that the novel
zwitterionic IL acts as the functional electrolyte component,
simultaneously improving the safety and overall electro-
chemical performance of LSBs.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. Sodium 2-bromoethanesulfonate (SBES; J&K,
98.5%), tris(dioxa-3,6-heptyl)anime (TDA; J&K, 95%), bis-
(triﬂuoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI; Aladdin, 99%),
lithium hydroxide (LiOH; J&K, 99%), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL; Aladdin,
99.8%), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME; Aladdin,99.5%), sulfur powder
(Alfa, 99.5%), and lithium sulﬁde (Li2S; Macklin, 99.9%) were used
without further puriﬁcation.
2.2. Preparation of Lithium 2-Bromoethanesulfonate
(LBES). Lithium 2-bromoethanesulfonate (LBES) was prepared by
the ion exchange technology using the cation exchange resin. Brieﬂy,
3.00 g of SBES (14.2 mmol) was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized
water, and the solution was passed through the cation exchange
column with 500 mL of deionized water slowly until the eﬄuent
liquid drop was neutral. LiOH (0.34 g, 14.2 mmol) was then added
into the collected solution and stirred for 5 min. Afterward, the
collected solution was dried by rotary evaporation. The obtained
white solid (2.59 g, 13.3 mmol) was LBES.
2.3. Preparation of Zwitterionic Ionic Liquid TLTFSI. The
zwitterionic ionic liquid TLTFSI was prepared via the quaternization
reaction of tris(dioxa-3,6-heptyl)anime (TDA) and lithium 2-
bromoethanesulfonate (LBES) followed by the anion exchange
reaction with LiTFSI. In the ﬁrst step, the LBES (6.81 g) and TDA
(12.45 g) with a LBES/TDA mole ratio of 1:1.1 were mixed with 35
mL of deionized water and 7 mL of ethanol in a round-bottom ﬂask,
which were then reﬂuxed at 100 °C for 72 h. The dull red mixture was
then subjected to rotary evaporation to remove the solvents like water
and ethanol. Afterward, 30 mL of deionized water and 30 mL of
dichloromethane were added into the remained liquid. The aqueous
phase was collected and subjected twice to extraction by 30 mL of
dichloromethane again. The aqueous phase was then dried to get rid
of the water. After drying, 15.82 g of liquid products named as TLBr
were obtained. In the second step, 8.28 g of LiTFSI and 5 mL of
deionized water were added into the obtained TLBr (15.82 g), and
the mixture was layered. The lower liquid layer was collected, giving
the ﬁnal product of the zwitterionic ionic liquid TLTFSI. The
molecular weight and density of TLTFSI are 718.43 g mol−1 and
1.367 g cm−3, respectively. The concentration of LiTFSI in TLTFSI
was calculated to be 1.9 M. The presence of Br−ions in TLTFSI was
checked by adding AgNO3 aqueous solution into a small portion of
TLTFSI. No precipitate of AgBr was observed, indicating that
TLTFSI did not contain Br−ions. The 4 Å molecular sieves were used
to remove the trace water in TLTFSI so that it could be used as
electrolyte in LSBs. The TLTFSI and corresponding mixed
electrolytes were stored in the glovebox before use. In order to
conﬁrm that there is no trace water in the TLTFSI and DDL after
adding 4 Å molecular sieves, a piece of Li metal was directly placed in
TLTFSI and DDL electrolytes to make sure that there is no reaction
between Li metal and possible trace water.
2.4. Preparation of TLTFSI-Based Electrolytes. An electrolyte
named as DDL, which was usually used for LSBs, was ﬁrst prepared
by mixing 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and
bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI). The volume
ratio of DOL and DME was 1:1, and the ﬁnal concentration of
LiTFSI was 1.9 M. As a result, the obtained DDL contained 50 vol %
DOL, 50 vol % DME, and 1.9 M LiTFSI. To prepare TLTFSI-based
electrolytes, we mixed the given volumes of TLTFSI and DDL

2.5. Preparation of C/S Complex and Cathode. The C/S
complex was prepared as follows: First, 50 mg of sublimed sulfur
powder and 50 mg of Super P were mixed and grinded in a mortar.
Second, the mixture was put into a pipe furnace and heated to 150 °C
with a heating speed of 5 °C min−1.7 After holding at 150 °C for 6 h,
the obtained C/S complex was slowly cooled to room temperature,
and the sulfur content in the C/S complex was 41.3%. The cathode
was made with the C/S complex, Super P, and PVDF in a weight ratio
of 7:2:1 and aluminum foil as the current collector. The cathode was
dried in the vacuum at 50 °C for 5 h and then transferred to a
glovebox before use. The diameter and area of aluminum foil were 12
mm and 1.13 cm2, respectively, and the sulfur content in the cathode
was 0.95 ± 0.02 mg cm−2.
2.6. Physical Characterization. The thermal stability and
properties of TLTFSI were characterized by thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA) and diﬀerential scanning calorimetry (DSC) using a
TGA Q50 instrument with the heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under a
N2 atmosphere and DSC Q20 with a heating and cooling rate of 10
°C min−1 ranging from −80 to 50 °C under a N2 atmosphere,
respectively. The 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400
MHz spectrometer using dimethylsulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6) as the
solvent. The viscosities and densities of the electrolytes were
measured by using the Anton Paar AMVn and DMA 5000M at 25
°C, respectively. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were
recorded by the Bruker TENSOR II spectrometer. The solubility of
lithium polysulﬁdes in TLTFSI and T0D100 electrolytes were
characterized by the following steps: First, 70 mg of sulfur powder
(0.27 mol) and 14.5 mg of Li2S (0.32 mol) were added into 1 mL of
TLTFSI and 1 mL of T0D100, respectively. The mixtures were
vigorously stirred at 25 °C for 12 h. The mixtures were then
transferred to the centrifugation tubes and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm
for 30 min.3 Finally, the supernatants were taken for ultraviolet−
visible (UV−vis) absorption measurements by using a Cary 100 UV−
vis spectrometer. The mass spectrum of TLTFSI was recorded on a
Thermo LTQ XL mass spectroscopy with a capillary temperature of
280 °C and methanol as the solvent.
2.7. Electrochemical Characterization. The CR2032-type coin
cells were prepared in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox for the following
experiments, and the amount of TnDm electrolyte added into each cell
was 100 μL. The electrochemical stabilization window of TLTFSI was
carried out via a Bio-logic VMP3 multichannel potentiostatic-
galvanostatic system, and linear sweep voltammetry measurements
were tested from the open-circuit voltage to 5.5 V and 1.0 V versus
Li/Li+ with a potential scan rate of 1 mV s−1. The conductivities (σ)
of TLTFSI with various temperatures were measured by the same
instrument with a CR2032-type coin cell, of which both the anode
and cathode were stainless steel pistons. A polarization voltage of 10
mV was given to the [Li metal anode | TLTFSI electrolyte | Li metal
cathode] and [Li metal anode | T70D30 electrolyte | Li metal cathode]
cell by the Bio-logic VMP3 multichannel potentiostatic-galvanostatic
system for 8 h to characterize the Li+ transference number of TLTFSI
and TnDm electrolytes. The impedances of the cells were characterized
by the same instrument in the frequency ranging from 100 kHz to 100
MHz with a perturbation amplitude of 5 mV.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The zwitterionic ionic liquid, TLTFSI, was synthesized via the
quaternization reaction of tris(dioxa-3,6-heptyl)anime (TDA)
and lithium 2-bromoethanesulfonate (LBES) followed by the
anion exchange reaction with LiTFSI, as shown in Scheme 1.
The TLTFSI IL contained countercation Li+ and counteranion
TFSI−itself, that is, LiTFSI, one of the most promising lithium
salts used in lithium-based battery systems, is contained in
TLTFSI. The chemical structure of TLTFSI was conﬁrmed by
1H NMR, and the detailed H attribution is shown in Figure S1.
Figure S2 shows the FTIR spectra of TDA and TLTFSI. The
vibration of C−O−C band on TDA is around 1117 cm−1,
while the C−N band appears at 1199 cm−1 (Figure S2a). For
the FTIR spectrum of TLTFSI (Figure S2b), the bands at
1196, 1058, 617, and 514 cm−1 are attributed to SO3
−
vibration, and the band at 1107 cm−1 corresponds to C−O−
C vibration because of the association of Li+ and O atom. The
bands at 1353 and 1228 cm−1 are attributed to the vibration of
SO and CF3 groups of TFSI−, respectively. Figure S3 shows
the mass spectra of TLTFSI. Only fragment ion peaks were
observed because the capillary temperature of 280 °C was
higher than the decomposition temperature of TLTFSI (i.e.,
275 °C; see below). The strong fragment ion peaks of TLTFSI
could be identiﬁed as N(CH2CH2OCH2CH2OCH3)3 with H+
(m/z = 324.3), two molecules of CH2CH2SO3 with a molecule
of methanol (m/z = 248.2) and CH2CH2OCH2CH2OCH3
(m/z = 103.0) in positive mode, N(SO2CF3)2 (m/z = 279.9),
N(SO2CF3)(SO2) (m/z = 210.9), and N(SO2CF3) (m/z =
146.9) in negative mode. No ion peak of Br−was observed,
suggesting the complete ion exchange of TFSI−. The
photograph of the synthesized TLTFSI in Figure 1a displays
its liquid feature. The viscosity of TLTFSI was measured to be
0.262 Pa·s. Figure S3 shows the UV−vis spectrum of TLTFSI.
There are no absorption peaks in the range of 400−760 nm.
We also added AgNO3 aqueous solution into a portion of
TLTFSI and no precipitate of AgBr was observed, indicating
that TLTFSI did not contain any Br−ions. The brown color of
TLTFSI probably comes from its chemical structure that has
both a quaternary ammonium cation and sulfonate anion,
which is similar to that of dodecyl-2-hydroxy-1-propyl
sulfobetain that shows a yellow color. The diﬀerential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) measurement further proves its low phase
transition temperature (−67 °C) (Figure S5a). In addition, its
thermal decomposition temperature is conﬁrmed to be 275 °C
by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure S5b).
To explore whether the TLTFSI was suitable for being used
as the electrolyte for LSBs, the electrochemical potential
window of TLTFSI was ﬁrst investigated. Figure 1b,c shows

the TnDm electrolyte were then tested with the current density
of 0.5 C at 25 °C, and the result is shown in Figure S6. It is
found that the LSBs with the TnDm electrolyte perform higher
speciﬁc capacities during the 100 cycles than LSBs with DDL
plus 1 wt % LiNO3 (deﬁned as T0D100) in most n/m ratio
(Figure S6a). Furthermore, Figure S6b shows that the
Coulombic eﬃciency of LSB with the T0D100 electrolyte is
larger than 100%, indicating the existence of the “polysulﬁde
shuttle eﬀect” in the charging process.7 For the rest of the
electrolytes with TLTFSI studied here, the Coulombic
eﬃciencies of the corresponding LSBs are about 96−99%,
suggesting the signiﬁcant suppression of “polysulﬁde shuttle
eﬀect”. The solubilities of lithium polysulﬁdes in TLTFSI and
T0D100 were tested, and the results are shown in Figure S7.
Note that the lithium polysulﬁdes were simulated by mixing
Li2S and sulfur powder according to the literature report.3 It
can be clearly observed that the lithium polysulﬁdes can
dissolve in the T0D100 electrolyte, leading to the strong
adsorption peaks in the UV−vis spectrum of T0D100 with
dissolved lithium polysulﬁdes (Figure S7C). However, the
solubility of lithium polysulﬁdes in TLTFSI was much lower
than that in the T0D100 electrolyte. In other words, TLTFSI
can indeed suppress the dissolution of lithium polysulﬁdes.
Among all the TLTFSI-based electrolytes studied here,
T70D30 is found to perform the best performance. Therefore,
the rest of the investigation was focused on the T70D30
electrolyte. As shown in Figure S8a,b, the T70D30 has a stable
electrochemical potential window from 1.36 to 4.44 V, close to
that of the commonly used T0D100 electrolyte (1.51−4.56 V).
Figure 1d shows that the T70D30 electrolyte possesses an
excellent ionic conductivity of 4.1 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 25 °C.
Furthermore, the CV curves of LSBs with the T70D30
electrolyte exhibit two elegant reduction peaks and one
oxidation peak, well corresponding to the discharge and
charge plateaus of the LSB (Figure S8c). Figure S8d shows the
CV curve of TLTFSI in the range of −0.3−1.5 V. The result of
Figure S8d suggests that TLTFSI would be decomposed when
the voltage was scanned from 1.5 to −0.3 V at the stainless
steel electrode, which was consistent with the result shown in
Figure 1c. The almost same capacity between discharge (1371
mAh g−1) and charge (1459 mAh g−1) processes (Figure S8e)
proves the high reversibility of LSBs with the T70D30
electrolyte. The Coulombic eﬃciencies are 94.0 and 102.0%
for the LSBs with T70D30 and T0D100 electrolytes, respectively,
indicating that the performance of the suppressed “polysulﬁde
shuttle eﬀect” is better for the T70D30 electrolyte than that of
the T0D100 electrolyte. Since the TLTFSI has the ability to
reduce the dissolution of polysulﬁdes, the T70D30 electrolytes
are the mixed electrolytes of DDL and TLTFSI. Therefore,
T70D30 electrolytes can also suppress the dissolution of
polysulﬁdes to some extent. As a result, the generated
polysulﬁdes would stay in the catholyte and adhere to the
Super P rather than dissolve in the T70D30 electrolyte. On the
other hand, the T70D30 electrolyte has a relatively higher

level (838 mAh g−1 vs 968 mAh g−1). In contrast, the discharge
capacity of LSBs with T0D100 is lower than the value of LSBs
with T70D30 for all the tested current densities, and the
diﬀerence becomes larger with the increase in the current
density until 2 C (Figure S9a). It should be noted that when
the current density is increased to 5 C, the speciﬁc capacity of
the LSBs with the T70D30 electrolyte decreases more obviously
and is close to the corresponding value of the LSBs with the
T0D100 electrolyte. To explain this result, the Li+ transference

+ of
TLTFSI, T0D100, T10D90, T30D70, T50D50, T70D30, and T90D10

content of TLTFSI in the mixed electrolyte, the tLi
+ ﬁrst
increases and then decreases. The reason is probably due to
the diﬀerent solvated property of the cation and anion in an
aprotic solvent. As we know, Li+ is solvated in an aprotic
solvent, while the anion is not. Once TLTFSI was added, Li+ is
solvated by the excess solvent molecules, forming the mobile
[Li+(solvent)n], while the cation site in a zwitterionic molecule
can only be partially shielded by the solvent. In this context,
when the ratio of TLTFSI is low, there are suﬃciently free
solvent to form [Li+(solvent)n], ensuring the mobility of Li+.
In contrast, the mobility of the introduced TFSI−is limited by
the electrostatic interaction between TFSI−and cation in a
zwitterionic molecule. Therefore, the tLi
+ increases in this
situation. With the increase in TLTFSI, the amount of free
solvent molecules decreases, leading to the increased viscosity
of the solution and the reduced mobility of [Li+(solvent)n].
Oppositely, the mobility of TFSI−is unchanged. In this screen,
the tLi
+ is then decreased. Besides the eﬀect of Li+ diﬀusion, the
large iR drop at the initial stage of discharge curve that is
contributed from bulk resistance, the surface passivation of the
sulfur cathode and mass transfer of dissolved lithium
polysulﬁdes also aﬀect the rate performance of the LSBs with
the T70D30 electrolyte.31 The results of Figures S6 and S10 also
indicate that there is no clear relationship between the Li+
transference number (tLi+) of TnDm electrolytes and the
performances (like discharge speciﬁc capacity) of the
corresponding LSBs with TnDm electrolytes. Furthermore,
the low tLi
+ (0.21) of the T70D30 electrolyte might lead to
signiﬁcant capacity reduction of LSBs at the high charge−
discharge current density like 5 C than those with T0D100 as
the electrolyte. Note that tLi
+ of T0D100 was 0.56 and much
higher than that of T70D30. As a result, the LSBs with T70D30 or
T0D100 as the electrolyte had similar capacities at 5 C, although
the LSBs with the T70D30 electrolyte had much higher
capacities than those with the T0D100 electrolyte at lower
charge−discharge current densities like 0.2 and 0.5 C.
The cycling performance of the LSBs with the T70D30
electrolyte with diﬀerent current densities was then inves-
tigated, and the result is shown in Figure 3. The discharge
speciﬁc capacities of the obtained LSBs are 1429, 1371, 826,
710, and 425 mAh g−1 at the ﬁrst cycle and decay to 605, 562,
451, 223, and 111 mAh g−1 after the 100th cycle with the
current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C, respectively. The
corresponding capacity retentions are about 42.3, 41, 54.6,
31.4, and 26.1% with the current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1,
and 2 C, respectively. These results are better than those
previously reported LSBs with mixed electrolytes and similar
cathode, as shown in Table 1.16,21 It should be noted that the
Coulombic eﬃciency of the LSBs with the T70D30 electrolyte at
0.1 C is larger than 100%, indicating the existence of the
“polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect” in the charging process at 0.1 C. At
a lower C-rate at 0.1 C, the lithium polysulﬁde has more times
to diﬀuse from the cathode to anode, which compromises the
suppression of “polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect” and results in the
Coulomb eﬃciency over 100%. This result indicates that the
suppression of “polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect” of the T70D30
electrolyte is not strong enough at the lower rate of 0.1 C.

speciﬁc capacity of the LSBs with T70D30 as the electrolyte

Additionally, the rate capability and long-term cyclic stability
of LSBs with the T70D30 electrolyte at a high temperature (60
°C) were also investigated. As shown in Figure S9b, the LSBs
with T70D30 exhibit constant higher capacities than that of the
LSBs with T0D100 during the continuous increase in the
current density from 0.2 to 2 C, except 5 C. The phenomenon
is similar to the situation at 25 °C. Figure 4b shows the long-
term cycle performances of LSBs with T70D30 and T0D100 as
electrolytes under 0.5 C at 60 °C. The discharge speciﬁc
capacity of the LSBs with the T70D30 electrolyte decreases from
869 mAh g−1 at the ﬁrst cycle to 433 mAh g−1 at the 100th
cycle and 198 mAh g−1 at the 500th cycle. For the LSBs with
T0D100 as the electrolyte, the discharge speciﬁc capacity
decreases from 630 mAh g−1 at the ﬁrst cycle to 376 mAh g−1
at the 100th cycle and 130 mAh g−1 at the 500th cycle. The
discharge speciﬁc capacity of LSBs with T70D30 is 52.7% larger
than the corresponding value of LSBs with T0D100 after 500
cycles. The capacity retention of LSBs with T70D30 is 22.8%
after 500 cycles, also higher than that of LSBs with T0D100
(20.6%). These results suggest the superior electrochemical
performance of LSBs with T70D30 at a high temperature.
Furthermore, the T70D30 electrolyte is nonﬂammable. As
shown in Figure 5a and Video S1, the degreased cotton after
dipping into the T70D30 electrolyte can hardly be lighted.
However, the degreased cotton after dipping into the T0D100
electrolyte can be easily lighted and burned to ashes (Figure 5b
and Video S2). This result proves the high safety of the T70D30
electrolyte for LSBs. Thus, the T70D30 electrolyte solves the
safety issue of LSBs and enhances the overall performance in
terms of the speciﬁc capacity, rate capability, and long-term
cyclic stability of LSBs.
To gain the compatibility of the T70D30 electrolyte with the
Li anode, the charge−discharge cycling test of a lithium
symmetrical cell at 25 °C was performed. As shown in Figure
6a, no sudden voltage ﬂuctuation can be observed during the
test at 0.1 mA cm−2 for 100 cycles, indicating that the stable
solid electrolyte interface (SEI) ﬁlm is formed on the Li metal
anode. Figure 6b shows the corresponding impedance spectra
of the lithium symmetrical cell before and after running for 100
cycles. It can be seen that the bulk resistance (Rb) of the
T70D30 electrolyte is 1.74 Ω and almost unchanged after 100
cycles, indicating the high stability of the T70D30 electrolyte
toward the lithium anode. Furthermore, the interfacial
resistance (Ri) is reduced from initial 33.3 to 23.1 Ω after
100 cycles. The great compatibility of the T70D30 electrolyte
with lithium metal should be one of critical roles for the
superior electrochemical performance of the TLTFSI-based
LSBs.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, a novel zwitterionic ionic liquid TLTFSI has been
synthesized to be employed as the electrolyte component for
lithium sulfur batteries. The TLTFSI has a high ionic
conductivity of 3.7 × 10−3 S cm−1, a wide electrochemical
potential window from 1.51 to 4.82 V, and a high thermal
decomposition temperature of 275 °C. An optimal TLTFSI-
based electrolyte T70D30 is obtained, which consisted of 1 wt %
LiNO3, 70 vol % TLTFSI, and 30 vol % DDL that contained
50 vol % DOL, 50 vol % DME, and 1.9 M LiTFSI. The T70D30
electrolyte is nonﬂammable and has a robust ionic conductivity
of 4.1 × 10−3 S cm−1, a wide electrochemical potential window
from 1.36 to 4.44 V, and a Li+ transference number of 0.21. In
consequence, the lithium−sulfur batteries with the T70D30
