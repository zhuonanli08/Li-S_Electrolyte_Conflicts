Unifying the clustering kinetics of lithium
polysulﬁdes with the nucleation behavior of Li2S in
lithium–sulfur batteries†
Abhay Gupta
and Arumugam Manthiram
*
Within the lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery, a wide variety of soluble lithium polysulﬁde intermediates form
during operation. Under lean-electrolyte or low-temperature conditions, the solution coordination of
polysulﬁdes dynamically shifts to highly clustered states, which is subsequently accompanied by inhibited
electrochemical kinetics. In fact, it has been shown that the tendency for polysulﬁdes to strongly
aggregate is one of the dominant kinetically limiting obstacles towards achieving adequate utilization of
active material under such conditions. While this association has been noted before, it is not explicitly
understood what mechanism intrinsic to polysulﬁde clustering curtails the electrochemical utilization of
active material, particularly during the conversion to insoluble Li2S. Here, we perform a series of
investigations to unify and link the kinetic constraints that arise from polysulﬁde clustering to the
nucleation and growth behavior of Li2S. We ﬁnd that there is a drastic decrease in polysulﬁde diﬀusion
coeﬃcient arising from the advent of clustering, and that this decline functionally matches that seen for
the nucleation and growth rate constants for Li2S deposition. Additionally, it is found that there is a less
favorable minimization of energy during Li2S nucleation, arising from the altered solvation stability of
polysulﬁde clusters. This knowledge expands our understanding of the Li–S materials chemistry and the
primary factors dictating the electrochemical behavior.
Introduction
There has been a rising global eﬀort to decarbonize a wide-
range of energy-intensive sectors, most immediately recogniz-
able in the surge of growth in applications like electric vehi-
cles.1–3
The
lithium-ion
battery
has
been
the
largest
technological driver behind this growth, both helping to inte-
grate renewable energy sources into the electric grid and
enabling the advent of electried transportation.4,5 However,
there are a wide-range of energy-intensive applications whose
eventual electrication will necessitate battery chemistries with
the ability to store more energy per unit mass.6 Lithium–sulfur
(Li–S) batteries, which possess a theoretical specic capacity an
order of magnitude higher than lithium-ion batteries, have
been a promising technology for further research and devel-
opment in this regard.
The chemical mechanisms underlying the behavior of
lithium–sulfur batteries are highly distinct from lithium-ion
batteries, predominantly due to the presence of soluble
lithium polysulde intermediate species (Li2Sx, 2 < x # 8) that
form at the cathode during operation. While active material
dissolution generally presents an array of challenges to cycle life
and
self-discharge,
the
solubility
to
lithium
polysuldes
enabled by the liquid electrolyte is actually highly critical and
essential to the electrochemical utilization of the sulfur active
material.7–11 Lithium polysuldes can enable facile charge-
transfer in solution; given the highly insulating nature of both
sulfur and the Li2S discharge product, Li–S batteries rely upon
these solution-mediated reaction mechanisms during charge
and discharge.12–14 This contrasts with lithium-ion batteries,
which rely upon intercalation, as well as most other conversion
anode and cathode chemistries, which rely upon solid-state
lithiation and conversion.15,16
In many respects, the active material and electrolyte within
the Li–S batteries are linked and inextricable, and thus must be
considered in conjunction to understand the materials chem-
istry underlying electrochemical behavior.17 This property is
readily apparent in the stark dependence of electrochemical
behavior on the quantity of electrolyte present within a Li–S cell,
a factor that is metricized in the research literature by the
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S, in mLelectrolyte mgsulfur
1).7,18,19 The
eventual implementation of Li–S batteries is dependent on the
attainment of high specic energies, and thus it is desired to
construct Li–S cells with low E/S ratios to minimize inactive
weight in the cell. However, the solution chemistry under lean

Within the Li–S electrolyte environment, lithium poly-

understood.22 The nature of bulky aggregates could present
a range of steric, electronic, or mass-transfer limitations to the
electrochemical reduction and oxidation of active material, but
it is not clear which if any of these factors plays a dominant role
in inhibiting the reaction kinetics.22 Understanding these exact
mechanisms is critical for both our underlying knowledge of Li–
S materials chemistry as well as for future engineering devel-
opment. In this work, we are motivated to explore this behavior
in more detail in the hopes of providing a leap forward in our
understanding of the dynamic Li–S redox chemistry.
Experimental
Polysulde solution preparation
Polysulde solutions were prepared inside an argon lled glo-
vebox for studies employing FTIR as well as for electrochemical
studies using liquid catholyte. For the FTIR solution, an amount
of Li2S and sulfur powder (stoichiometrically equivalent to
0.2 M Li2S6) were mixed in a 1 : 1 volume mixture of DOL and
DME solvents, along with 1 M lithium triate (LiTF) and 0.2 M
LiNO3. salts. The catholyte was the same composition, with 1 M
LiTFSI instead of 1 M LiTF.
Li–S coin cell fabrication
Li–S cells for single cycle discharge/charge tests at various
temperatures were assembled in CR-2032 type coin cells. Each
cell contained a sulfur cathode, lithium-metal foil anode, elec-
trolyte, and a Celgard 2325 separator. For E/S variation tests, the
sulfur cathodes had loadings of 3.1 mg cm2 and consisted of
63% sulfur, 28% carbon black, and 9% binder (polyethylene
oxide and polyvinylpyrrolidone, 4 : 1 by mass). For variable
temperature tests, the sulfur cathodes had loadings of 3.6 mg
cm2 and consisted of 70% sulfur, 15% carbon black, and 15%
polyvinylidene uoride binder cast onto aluminum foil, along
with a carbon ber paper placed between the cathode and
separator as a free-standing interlayer.25 The cathode area was
1 cm2 in both cases. The electrolyte contained 1.0 M LiTFSI in
DOL/DME (1 : 1, by vol), of which 60 mL was used in each cell (E/
S ¼ 17 mL mg1) for variable-temperature tests. In the E/S
variation tests, 0.2 M LiNO3 was also used in the electrolyte.
For

cyclic
voltammetry
and

tained 80 mL of catholyte and a buckypaper (60 gcarbon m2)
cathode substrate with an electrode area of 1.6 cm2 and elec-
troactive area of 30.2 m2 g1.26
Galvanostatic cycling
For E/S variation tests, cells were rested for 1 h and cycled in
a window of 2.7 and 1.7 V at a rate of C/10 (with 1C ¼
1672 mA g1 of sulfur in the cathode). For variable-temperature
tests, cells were rested for 8 h at a given temperature in an Espec
BTZ-133 environmental chamber prior to cycling. An Arbin
battery cycler was used to galvanostatically discharge cells at
a rate of C/20. Cells were cycled between a voltage window of 2.8
and 1.5 V.
Cyclic voltammetry studies
Voltammetry studies were conducted on a BioLogic VSP
potentiostat. For low-temperature studies, potential was swept
from open circuit voltage to 1.8 V and then back to 2.4 V at a rate
of 0.05 mV s1. The anodic sweep was terminated at a low
potential of 2.4 V to prevent catalytic oxidation of the electrolyte
by the buckypaper that begins to occur around 2.45 V.
Temperature was maintained during tests via an Espec SH-241
bench-top environmental chamber. For the E/S ratio study,
similar parameters were used, except the potential was swept
back up to 2.8 V given cast-cathodes were used instead of
buckypaper.

also placed and used as an external reference during scans.

Results and discussion
While lithium polysuldes are generally envisioned as being
solvated by the 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), and lithium bistriimide (LiTFSI) components consti-
tuting the liquid electrolyte, the polysulde active material can
in fact exhibit exacerbated ion-pairing and coordination with
other
polysuldes
under
low
E/S
ratios,
as
shown
in
Fig. 1a.20,30,31 Correspondingly, under lean electrolyte condi-
tions, Li–S batteries exhibit signicantly curtailed kinetic
behavior and capacity attainment, as shown in Fig. 1b, pre-
senting a sizeable barrier to practical deployment.19,32–34 In fact,
it has been recently shown that this change in the electrolyte
environment at low E/S conditions is the predominant failure
mechanism for sulfur cathodes under lean electrolyte amounts,
with dissolved polysuldes transitioning from a liquid state to
a semi-solid gel-like state as electrolyte amounts are reduced.23
The tendency for lithium polysuldes to coordinate, cluster,
and form large aggregates in solution is thus highly conse-
quential and serves as one of the most inuential factors
dictating the electrochemical kinetic behavior of Li–S batteries.
Furthermore, polysulde clustering frequently occurs in
scenarios extending beyond the scope of just low E/S condi-
tions. At subzero low-temperature conditions, for example,
lithium polysuldes exhibit similar coordination and clustering
tendencies.22,35 At low-temperature conditions, Li–S batteries
tend to perform quite poorly in terms of capacity attainment,
rate capability, and cyclability, despite the excellent low-
temperature conductivity of the DOL/DME electrolyte.22,24,36–38
In a prior work, we showed that the rise of polysulde clustered
aggregates was the dominant cause of this counterintuitive
behavior at low temperatures, as opposed to simply the stunted
ionic conductivity and heightened charge-transfer resistances
that plague lithium-ion batteries at low-temperature condi-
tions.22,39 This transition in electrolyte environment is evident
from Li–S electrochemical behavior at low temperatures, like
that shown in Fig. 1c. Specically, there is a signicant depre-
cation in the low-voltage plateau conversion of polysuldes to
insoluble Li2S. This decrease in performance at low tempera-
tures consistently occurs despite the impressively low freezing
points and high ionic conductivities of the DOL and DME
solvents making up the liquid electrolyte.
The eventual implementation of Li–S batteries is dependent
on the battery chemistry being able to outperform lithium-ion
batteries under specic niche use-cases, such as aerospace
applications that need high specic energy and recurring low-
temperature resilience.6,35,40,41 However, separately under both
low E/S ratios and low temperatures, polysuldes tend to cluster
and aggregate, which serves as one of the most critical and
dominant factors limiting performance at such conditions. It is
notable that a shared underlying phenomenon seems to debil-
itate electrochemical performance under the seemingly dispa-
rate and unrelated scenarios of low E/S ratios and low
temperatures. However, in the case of low E/S ratios, this eﬀect
is primarily related to increased overpotential in the second
plateau, while in the case of low temperatures, this eﬀect also
diminishes the second plateau capacity. While the aggregation
of active material does seem to be a common inhibitor in both
cases, it is notable that this eﬀect proceeds in a distinct manner
in each case.
Here, we aim to specically deconvolute and assess the
distinct contributions to poor kinetics arising from polysulde
clustering, specically evaluating diﬀusional mass-transfer and
chemical interfacial stability limitations. Additionally, we use
the application of low-temperature conditions as both a toggle
and a proxy to, respectively, vary the degree of and understand
the impacts of lithium polysulde coordination. The diﬀusional
limitations arising from the clustering of polysuldes was rst
assessed using voltammetry. Li–S cells were constructed with
a prepared catholyte consisting of 1 M LiTFSI, 0.2 M LiNO3, and
0.2 M Li2S6 in DOL/DME solvent (1 : 1, by vol). Meanwhile,
commercial buckypaper served as the cathode substrate in
order to eliminate any eﬀects arising from a polymeric binder
on the assessed liquid diﬀusional properties of lithium poly-
suldes. Cyclic voltammograms of Li–S cells were collected at
a series of descending temperatures, aer which the diﬀusional
properties of the electroactive species were interrogated with

While there is strong evidence for diﬀusional mass-transfer
limitations to underlie the poor kinetics associated with poly-
sulde clustered aggregates, it is possible that other factors may
also contribute to this trend. One such factor is the stability of
the solvated polysulde complex, as well as the interfacial
stability of nucleated Li2S arising from the reduction of poly-
sulde aggregates. This can be contextualized via Pearson's
hard–soacid–base (HSAB) theory, an eﬀective lens to quali-

Within
the
Li–S
electrolyte,
the
coordination

surrounding each Li+ atom is made up by components exclu-
sively belonging to the electrolyte (DOL, DME, and LiTFSI) as
well as polysulde dianions.20,47 However, the partially negative
charged oxygen-bearing elements of DOL/DME (soLewis base)
only weakly coordinate with Li+ (hard Lewis acid), which is more
likely to coordinate with immediately adjoined polysulde
dianions (harder base than DOL/DME) with greater frequency.14
During the galvanostatic conversion from dissolved polysuldes
to Li2S nuclei, energy is released at a potential of 2.1 V vs. Li/
Li+. However, this equilibrium potential changes as a function
of the solvation environment in the electrolyte. For example, in
electrolytes made up of high-donor number solvents, like
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and dimethylacetamide (DMA), the
solvent (hard base) more eﬀectively coordinates with Li+,
leading to a weaker coordination state with polysulde anions
present in solution. This “shielding” of Li+ makes the entire
cationic complex a soer acid than weakly solvated Li+, leading
to a weaker coordination to S2 during the conversion to Li2S.
This has been extensively discussed in past works,9,14 and is
reected by a reduced potential of the second plateau conver-
sion in solvents like DMSO and DMA that takes place at less
than 2.0 V vs. Li/Li+.7,9 In a similar manner, the advent of pol-
ysulde clustering alters the solvation dynamics that normally
takes place in the Li–S electrolyte, which may aﬀect the equi-
librium potential during electrochemical conversion.

Therefore, there are two apparent mechanisms by which the
advent of polysulde clustering and aggregation may curtail the

The rise of polysulde clustering behavior signicantly inhibits
the diﬀusion of active material within the electrolyte. This
mass-transfer limitation drastically diminishes the advanta-
geous diﬀusion-based reaction mechanisms that active material
would formerly rely upon during charge and discharge. Mean-

coordinated state from the perspective of each Li+ cation, with
heightened association and shielding by a greater number of
surrounding Sx

energetic favorability for the conversion reaction from poly-
suldes to Li2S.

which one is the dominant limiting factor. However, each may
play a part in dictating the deposition and utilization of sulfur-
containing active material. Thus, evaluating the distinct kinetic
behavior of Li2S electrodeposition as a function of the degree of
clustering can provide a perspective into which factor is most

of the role that solvation stability of aggregated polysuldes
plays in dictating Li–S battery performance. Since the dominant
kinetic descriptors during discharge seem to be primarily
regulated by diﬀusional controls, it would be expected that the
solvation stability of polysulde aggregates plays a relatively
minor role in comparison. Given the studies detailed in Fig. 3
and the rationale provided, however, it is logically consistent

nucleation and growth rate of Li2S is signicantly curtailed in
scenarios where polysuldes are highly aggregated, there is
support for the hypothesis that this multi-plateau behavior
stems from a change in the solvation stability of polysulde

Conclusions
The chemical environment contained within a Li–S cell
demonstrates a rich and tremendously complex diversity of
inactive and active species and coordination states. As the
system is modulated from equilibrium conditions, there is
a dynamic transition from individually solvated lithium poly-
sulde units to large, clustered polysulde aggregates, partic-
ularly at low E/S ratios and subzero low temperatures. The rise
of these highly aggregated states has been shown to be the
dominant behavior inhibiting performance under such condi-
tions, though the exact mechanism by which they do so has
remained unclear.22,23 Here, we have shown that occurrence of

polysuldes is also altered. The greater stabilization of each Li+

reduces the equilibrium voltage potential at which the conver-
sion to Li2S takes place, which is further amplied by the
diﬀusional overpotentials presented from the severe mass

Li–S chemical environment opens a critical juncture from
which new mechanism-guided improvements to the system can
be developed. Building oﬀthis scientic investigation, there is
a strong need to improve the diﬀusion kinetics and polysulde
solvation environment at both low-temperature and lean elec-
trolyte conditions. These scenarios are especially critical as they
are the use-cases most practically suited for the future imple-
mentation of Li–S batteries. The most eﬀective manner by
which to overcome these hurdles is to altogether prevent and
delay the formation of coordinated polysulde clustered
networks in solution, likely through the implementation of
novel
electrolyte

The

of

bringing the deployment of Li–S batteries ever closer to fruition.
