A novel class of functional additives for lithium sulfur batteries was studied. In particular, the facile oper-
ation of adding LiCoO2 (LCO) to the sulfur cathode achieves a remarkably improved cycling performance
i.e. an ultralow capacity degradation of 0.034% per cycle at a 2C rate over 1200 cycles with a LCO
addition of only 3 wt%.
With increasing demand for energy consumption, lithium
sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been regarded as one of the most
promising types of energy storage device for next generation
power systems, owing to their high gravimetric and theoretical
capacity (2.6 kW h kg−1 and 1675 mA h g−1, respectively).1–3
However, Li–S batteries suﬀer from poor electrode cyclability
ascribed to the large volumetric expansion of sulfur during
lithiation coupled with polysulfide (PS) shuttling and low util-
ization of active material resulting from the poor ionic and
electronic conductivities of S and its lithiated form (Li2S).4–8
During the charging process of Li–S batteries, higher-order
PSs diﬀuse to the lithium electrode where they react directly
with the lithium to recreate lower-order PSs. Then, these
species diﬀuse back to the sulfur electrode to generate the
higher forms of PS again, thus creating the PS shuttle mecha-
nism, leading to low coulombic eﬃciency. Once insoluble Li2S
is formed at the lithium electrode, capacity degradation occurs
concomitantly. Therefore, the inhibition of PS diﬀusion is the
key to enhancing electrode cyclability.9–12
Physically encaging and chemically absorbing PSs are two
eﬀective ways to suppress PS shuttling. Carbon based sulfur
electrodes, such as a reduced graphite oxide-S cathode, are
successful examples of physically encaging PS.13–21 Diﬀerent to
carbon materials, polar additives are widely used for the settle-
ment of PS due to their relatively strong chemical binding
(such as TiO2,22 CoS2
23 and Ti4O7
24). However, most of these
polar materials are insulators that cannot transport electrons
to the trapped species. Thus, rational design and construction
of hybrid structures from conductive macroporous carbon and
polar additives should be a promising approach to eﬃciently
inhibit PS shuttling behavior.25–27 Undoubtedly, there is still
an urgent need to develop a facile and cost-eﬀective procedure
to yield adaptive hybrid structures for improving the electro-
chemical performance of Li–S batteries.
Herein, we report a novel and easy way to improve the
cycling performance of Li–S batteries by the facile addition of
conventional lithium ion cathode materials (LiCoO2 (LCO),
LiFePO4 (LFP), LiMn2O4 (LMO)) to the sulfur cathode. The
eﬀect of LCO addition on the cyclability enhancement was
investigated. A remarkably improved cycling performance was
achieved; a capacity of over 720 mA h g−1 at 1C, even after 250
cycles, corresponding to a high capacity retention of 85%.
Additionally, the battery maintained a capacity of around
400 mA h g−1 over 1200 cycles at a 2C rate with an ultralow
capacity degradation of 0.034% per cycle with a LCO addition
of only 3 wt%.

Moreover, CV tests were further carried out at diﬀerent
sweep rates to explore the reaction kinetics behaviour of dispa-
rate cathodes, as shown in Fig. 2a and b. The kinetics data
were characterized by analyzing the CV data according to
i ¼ av b
where the i value is measured current, which obeys a power
law relationship with sweep rate v. The b value is positively
related to Li+ diﬀusion, and can be obtained from the slope of
the curve of log i vs. log v. When the scan rate ranges from 0.2
to 1 mV s−1 (Fig. 2c and d), log v vs. log i retains a good linear
relationship. Additionally, the plot gradually deviates when the
sweep rate increases beyond 1 mV s−1, which is ascribed to a
polarization eﬀect. It is apparent that the b values of peak I (bI)
are larger than the b values of peak II (bII), indicating that the
solid–liquid reaction is faster than the liquid–solid reaction.
After the introduction of LCO, the b values significantly
increase with about a 6.8% improvement for bI and 2.7% for
bII. Therefore, these results suggest that the introduction of
LCO could eﬃciently promote the lithiation kinetics.32

Furthermore, the diﬀerent electrodes were disassembled
after cycling, and SEM measurements were carried out to
characterize the surface morphologies. Fig. 4b shows that a
porous network was formed outside the MPC pores in the
S/MPC electrode after 50 cycles compared with that before
testing (Fig. 4a). Conversely, there was barely any change for
the S/MPC + LCO electrode before and after 50 cycles; the dis-
charge product was a uniform distribution and LCO still
existed in the physical mixing state with S/MPC. Moreover, the
XRD patterns show the same strong peaks corresponding to
pure LCO both before and after cycling, verifying the stability
of LCO during battery operation (Fig. S2†). These results also
confirm that LCO addition improves the reversibility of the
battery, resulting in an enhancement of cyclability.
Visual discrimination was carried out to explore the capa-
bility of LCO to adsorb PS anions by applying Li2S4 as a repre-
sentative lithium polysulfide. As shown in Fig. S3,† the color of
the Li2S4 solution was dark yellow at first and then converted
to buﬀafter 24 h. Meanwhile, the chemical PS adsorption was
further studied by XPS analysis (Fig. S4†). From the S 2p spec-
trum, the peaks clearly shift to a higher binding energy after
adsorption compared with pure PS, indicating the chemical
interactions between LCO and PS.
In order to more fully exert the beneficial eﬀect of the LCO
additive with limited addition amount, ball-milled nanoscale
LCO particles (Fig. 5a and b) with increased surface area
(70.789 m2 g−1, Fig. S5†), which is higher than that of LCO
before ball-milling (4.294 m2 g−1), were utilized to provide
more contact surface for interaction with PS, and a smaller
additive percentage for higher electrode energy density. The
electrochemical performances are depicted in Fig. 5c. The elec-
trodes showed similar cycling performance even when decreas-
ing the ratio of LCO. However, when the capacity was calcu-
lated based on the mass of the cathode, a lower percentage of
additive revealed significantly higher energy density. The elec-
trode with just 3 wt% ball-mixed LCO delivered a capacity of
340 mA h g−1 based on the cathode after 150 cycles at a 1C
rate, which is much higher than the values for those materials
with higher LCO content (318.8 mA h g−1 for 5 wt% LCO,
315.9 mA h g−1 for 10 wt% LCO and 285.8 mA h g−1 for
20 wt% LCO). Therefore, the percentage of the additive was
chosen to be 3 wt% in further research.
Further electrochemical performances were measured to
illustrate the validity of the LCO additive. Fig. 6a shows the
rate performance of the S/MPC-LCO electrode at various rates
from 0.2C to 5C. At the rate of 0.2C, the cell exhibited an
initial capacity of 1179.6 mA h g−1. As the rate increased
higher to 0.5C, 1C and 2C, the cell could also maintain high
capacities of 853 mA h g−1, 782 mA h g−1 and 727 mA h g−1,
respectively. Remarkably, when further increasing the rate to
5C, the cell could still achieve a large reversible capacity of
630 mA h g−1. Moreover, as the rate was restored back to 0.2C,
the cell could nearly completely return to its original capacity.
It should be noted that the battery still suﬀered from slight
capacity decay attributed to the unavoidable dissolution of
polysulfides into the electrolyte. An initial capacity of 848
mA h g−1 was obtained at 1C after two activating cycles, as
shown in Fig. 6b, and then the capacity faded but stabilized at
over 720 mA h g−1 during the following cycles, corresponding
to a high capacity retention of 85% after 250 cycles. When the

Conclusions
In summary, we have developed a novel class of additives to
enhance the cyclability of the sulfur cathode in lithium sulfur
batteries by simply adding conventional lithium ion cathode
materials. The resultant Li–S battery with 3 wt% LCO addition
to the cathode showed an ultralow capacity degradation rate of
0.034% per cycle at a 2C rate over 1200 cycles. Moreover, LCO
addition in the cathode decreased sulfur lithiation impedance,
thus increasing the rate capacity as high as 630 mA h g−1 at
5C.
