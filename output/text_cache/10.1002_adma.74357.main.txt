ABSTRACT
A crucial step toward widespread electrochemical energy storage is the design of lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) that integrate high
energy density with robust safety. Realizing practical LSBs demands an electrolyte that possesses high interfacial stability, excellent
ion-environment regulation capability, and high electrocatalytic activity. Although current electrolyte technologies have improved
the cycling performance of LSBs, preparing electrolytes that simultaneously deliver high energy density, high cycling stability, and
high safety remains a significant challenge. Here, we report an electrolyte design strategy aimed at achieving high-performance and
high-safety LSBs. This is primarily accomplished by incorporating symmetric ionic plastic crystals into the electrolyte to construct a
slimmed solvation structure (Li+) regulated by suppression of anion aggregation (Sn
2−and TFSI−) during discharge. The electrolyte
exhibits high ionic conductivity, low desolvation energy barrier, high electrocatalytic activity, and interfacial stability. This design
acts simultaneously on the cathode and anode interfaces, enabling stable and rapid cycling of LSBs, with high energy density
(pouch cell: 704 Wh kg−1) and high stability (average capacity decay rate per cycle of 0.018% over 600 cycles). Our solvation structure
model design provides a feasible approach for realizing high-performance and high-safety LSBs.

Introduction
Currently, with the development of the low-altitude economy
and the electric transportation, there is an increasingly urgent
demand for power batteries with high energy density and
enhanced safety [1–4]. Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) possess
ultra-high theoretical specific capacity and energy density [5–8].
However, their practical energy density and safety are largely
limited by issues such as shuttle effect, sluggish kinetics under
lean electrolyte conditions, and lithium dendrite growth [9, 10].
Researchers have discovered that under lean electrolyte condi-
tions, the substantial formation and dissolution of polysulfides
during the discharge process significantly increases the viscosity
of the electrolyte, which severely hinders ion transport [11, 12].
This further aggravates the sluggish conversion of polysulfides,
preventing the effective utilization of active materials and making
it difficult to improve energy density. Although an increase
in the amount of electrolyte can partially alleviate the kinetic
limitations, it leads to a significant rise in the mass of individual
pouch cells (with electrolyte mass accounting for over 50% of the

total weight) [13]. Researchers have devoted considerable efforts
to addressing the kinetic issues under lean electrolyte conditions.
For instance, functional materials such as heteroatom-doped
carbon, transition metal compounds, and single-atom catalysts
have been employed in the cathode, while redox mediators and
catalytically effective additives have been introduced into the
electrolyte [14–16]. These approaches have successfully estab-
lished rapid conversion active sites at the electrode–electrolyte
interface, facilitating the conversion of Li2Sx to Li2S by lowering
the reaction activation energy. However, the intrinsic kinetic
constrains arising from restricted ion transport in lean electrolyte
has not been fully resolved.
Moreover, suppressing lithium dendrite growth is a decisive
factor affecting battery safety and cycling stability. Representative
research achievements include (localized) high-concentration
electrolytes, solvent-in-salt electrolytes, and modified fluorinated
electrolytes [17–22]. These advancements enable the formation
of a uniform, robust solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) rich in
anion-derived inorganic components. Although these methods
improve the cycling performance of LSBs, the lithium-ion solva-
tion structure formed by the aggregation of excess anions (Sn
2−)
often constrains the capacity in practical applications. Under lean
electrolyte conditions, the increased polysulfide concentration
during discharge impedes ion transport, while the desolvation of
large lithium-ion clusters becomes more difficult. These effects
are particularly problematic at low temperatures due to severe
anion aggregation. Regardless of whether high solvating or spar-
ingly solvating electrolytes are used, little attention has so far been
paid to the ion transport issues caused by high-concentration
polysulfides under lean electrolyte conditions (Figure 1a) [23–
28]. To address the above issues, it is essential to optimize
the cathode, anode, and electrolyte separately. However, this
will inevitably introduce numerous new challenges during the
scale-up process in industrial production, posing significant diffi-
culties for component compatibility and integration. Therefore,
a comprehensive solution that simultaneously achieves high
energy density, safe cycling, scalable manufacturing, and high
compatibility is urgently needed for the practical application and
industrialization of LSBs.
Here, we report a general method for establishing a slimmed
solvation structure (SSS) model. Guided by this concept, we
developed a novel electrolyte utilizing an ionoplastic crystal
material with a symmetric molecular structure. By regulating the
outside of the solvation shell through strong ion-pair association,
a unique slimmed solvation structure is formed (Figure 1b).
This structure can effectively reduce the desolvation energy
barrier while significantly suppressing the issue of slow ion
transport caused by anion aggregation (Sn
2−and TFSI−) dur-
ing discharge under lean electrolyte conditions. The positively
charged symmetric molecular structure forms an aligned cation
layer under the influence of an electric field, which induces
anions to self-assemble on the lithium anode, forming a uniform
hybrid inorganic-organic SEI layer (Figure 1c). Additionally, this
symmetric anion facilitates polysulfide conversion kinetics at
the cathode interface through cation-anion interaction at the
conversion sites. The establishment of this solvation structure
model, coupled with the simultaneous regulation of both cathode
and anode interfaces, enables low desolvation energy, fast ion
transport capability under low E/S ratios, rapid polysulfide
conversion kinetics, and a safe, stable anode interface. This
simple electrolyte formulation simultaneously overcomes the key
limitations restricting the energy density, safety, and stability of
LSBs, while exhibiting strong potential for scalable application.
Small-batch production of 20 Ah large-format pouch cells has
been achieved, demonstrating energy densities of over 704 Wh
kg−1 at 0.1 C and 663 Wh kg−1 at 0.2 C. These values represent
the highest reported levels to date, with third-party test reports
provided for verification. Additionally, the cells exhibit a capacity
retention rate of 93.6% after 100 cycles at 0.2 C (E/S = 2), and
an average capacity decay rate of 0.018% per cycle over 600
cycles at 1 C. This pouch cell continues to function normally
after puncture, with virtually no change in temperature and
voltage. Our solvation structure model design provides a fea-
sible approach for achieving high-performance and high-safety
LSBs.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Slimmed Solvation Structure
First, we synthesized a variety of structural analogs. Among
ionic liquids with varying ion-pair association strengths, E-Pn-
S exhibits strong ion-pair interactions, which are sufficient to
form a slimmed solvation structure (SSS) [29, 30]. Next, we
formulated electrolytes consisting of different ionoplastic crystal
materials combined with DOL and DME, and evaluated the
electrochemical properties of these electrolytes. E-Pn-S meets
the requirements of non-flammability, high ionic conductiv-
ity, low desolvation energy barrier, high reaction promotion
capability, and interfacial stability, thereby enabling LSBs that
simultaneously achieve high safety and high energy density
(relevant synthesis and characterization results of ionoplastic
crystal materials are displayed in Figures S1–S5). The results
preliminarily show that lithium-ion solvation structure plays a
key role in controlling ionic conductivity.

compositions, we verified the formation of the solvation

Through nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), we further investi-
gated the actual Li+ solvation environment in electrolytes with
different compositions, as well as the influence of polysulfide
incorporation during the discharge process on the Li+ solvation
structure [32, 33]. The 7Li NMR peaks of the electrolyte shift
downfield in the order of E-Pn-B, E-Pn-A, E-Pn-S, and E,
while the 19F NMR peaks associated with TFSI−shift upfield
(Figure 2e,f). This is primarily due to the weakened Li+ and
TFSI−coordination, which reduces the shielding effect of the
anions on Li+. The positively charged symmetric molecule
interacts with TFSI−, increasing the relative distance between
Li+ and TFSI−. After polysulfide incorporation, nearly all the
7Li NMR peaks of the electrolytes shift upfield, while the 19F
NMR peaks shift downfield. This indicates that the introduction
of polysulfides brings a large number of polysulfide anions,
which participate in the Li+ solvation and induce a severe
anion aggregation phenomenon. The extent of this effect follows
the order of E-Pn-S<E-Pn-A<E-blank, further confirming the
inhibitory effect of E-Pn-S and the slimmed solvation structure on
anion aggregation under high polysulfide concentrations. This is
key to maintaining high ionic conductivity under lean electrolyte
conditions.
To directly verify the impact of the aforementioned solva-
tion structure model on ion-transport behavior, we employed
diffusion-ordered spectroscopy NMR (DOSY-NMR) analysis to
quantify the diffusion coefficients of ions (including pyrroli-
dinium cations, TFSI−, and Li+) in the electrolyte and under
high polysulfide concentrations [32, 34]. Compared to E-Pn-B
and E-Pn-A, E-Pn-S exhibits the highest Li+ diffusion coefficient
(12.96 × 10−11 m2 s−1) (Figure 2g and Figure S6). Even under high
polysulfide concentrations, E-Pn-S still maintains a relatively
high Li+ diffusion coefficient (12.51 × 10−11 m2 s−1), while the blank
electrolyte plummets from 13.65 × 10−11 m2 s−1 to 8.161 × 10−11
m2 s−1, and E-Pn-A drops further 7.1851 × 10−11 m2 s−1. This
is because the positively charged symmetric molecule forms
a slimmed solvation structure by weakening the coordination
between anions and Li+, thereby stabilizing the relative propor-
tions of SSIP, CIP, and AGG under lean electrolyte conditions.
This directly confirms the improvement of Li+ migration by E-
Pn-S under low E/S ratio and high polysulfide concentration
conditions. Correspondingly, the F−diffusion coefficient of E-
Pn-S is (2.947 × 10−11 m2 s−1), and remains nearly unchanged
(2.932 × 10−11 m2 s−1) even at high polysulfide concentrations
(Figures S7 and S8). In contrast, the blank electrolyte plummets
from 4.148 × 10−11 m2 s−1 to 1.8 × 10−11 m2 s−1, while Pn-A registers
only 1.626 × 10−11 m2 s−1. The trends in the F−diffusion coefficient
and the symmetric cation diffusion coefficient are consistent with
the Li+ diffusion results (Figure S9 and S10).
The inhibition of E-Pn-S on the aggregation of polysulfide anions
was further verified via theoretical calculations and small-angle
x-ray scattering (SAXS). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
reveal that in the E-Pn-S electrolyte system, the coordination
number of polysulfide anions around Li+ is lower than that in the
E electrolyte after polysulfide addition (Figure 2h,i and Figures
S11 and S12). SAXS results show that the size of lithium-ion
clusters in the E-Pn-S system is larger than that in the blank
electrolyte E but smaller than those in the E-Pn-A and E-Pn-B
systems (Figure 2j). After polysulfides incorporation, Li+ cluster
sizes in E-LS, E-Pn-A-LS and E-Pn-B-LS all increase significantly,
while those in E-Pn-S-LS remain stable [35]. The above results are
consistent with the Raman spectroscopy, 7Li and 19F NMR, DOSY-
NMR, and theoretical calculation results, further confirming the
inhibition of polysulfide anion aggregation and the modulation
of Li+ cluster size by E-Pn-S. In summary, this slimmed solvation
structure (SSS) not only lowers the desolvation energy barrier
but also inhibits polysulfide anion aggregation, which is crucial
for resolving the kinetic mismatch between reaction rate and ion
transport in LSBs.

which force DME into the inner solvation layers through spatial
effects. This restructuring markedly reduces Li+ ion mobility
and increases the desolvation energy barrier. These findings
prove that one of the actual causes of the sharp capacity
drop under lean electrolyte conditions is the alteration of the
solvation structure induced by polysulfide anion aggregation,
which leads to slow ion transport. In other words, it reveals a

The real-time evolution of this solvation structure was further
observed through in situ electrochemical infrared spectroscopy
(Figure 3g). A downward peak indicates the depletion of sub-
stances compared to the initial state, while an upward peak
corresponds to the formation of substances. For the blank elec-
trolyte (on the far right), as the discharge and charge processes
proceed, the peaks appear downward first and then upward. This
is due to the decomposition of the electrolyte (including both
the solvent and the salt) occurring at the electrode interface
during discharge (Figure 3h–k). The resulting heterogeneous and
partially insulating decomposition layer on the electrode surface
leads to persistent upward peaks which remain unchanged upon
cycling, indicating its irreversible nature. In contrast, the E-Pn-
S electrolyte exhibits a negative signal with stable intensity and
no noticeable enhancement, due to the process of “desolvation
+ solvent returning to the bulk phase,” which reduces the
interfacial concentration. Moreover, a signal recovers at the
end of both discharge and charge, indicating that this state
is reversible. Notably, the S═O peak of TFSI−and the C–O
peak of DME show a distinct shift toward higher wavenumbers
during both discharge and charge, particularly when polysulfides
are extensively dissolved in the electrolyte. As the polysulfide
concentration decreases, the peaks shift back to original values,
further revealing the evolution of the solvation structure under
the influence of polysulfides. Li–Li symmetric cell cycling tests
confirm the high stability against lithium of the electrolyte with
this solvation structure. E-Pn-S enables long-term stable cycling
(∼3500 h) with a steady voltage polarization of approximately
32 mV (Figure 3k,l and Figure S14). In contrast, the batteries
with E-Pn-A and E-blank undergo fast failure (less than 1200 h).
Measurements under different current densities further verify the
aforementioned findings (Figures S15 and S16). The high lithium
stability originates from the low desolvation energy barrier of the
slimmed solvation structure and the uniform, stable SEI formed
by the self-assembly of symmetric cations on the lithium surface.
2.3
Anode Interface Modulation for SEI
Formation
Next, we investigated the effects of E-Pn-S, E-Pn-A, and E-
Pn-B in the electrolyte on the evolution of the electric double
layer using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-
SIMS), given that the electric double layer plays a critical role
in SEI formation [37, 38]. The research results indicate that all
three cationic salts facilitate close packing and suppress self-
aggregation [39, 40]. This closely packed fixed layer is capable
of microscopically attracting anions in the solvation structure,
thereby forming an inorganic-rich and uniform SEI. To verify this
mechanism, we characterized the post-cycled anodes of batteries
using E-Pn-S, E-Pn-A, E-Pn-B, and the E-blank to analyze the
SEI structure. The 3D ion mapping of TOF-SIMS illustrates
the specific spatial distribution of SEI components (Figure 4a–
d). Benefiting from the slimmed solvation structure and the
electric field-induced compact positively charged fixed layer, E-
Pn-S forms the most uniform SEI. Depth profiling analysis shows
that various components such as C2HO−(organic layer), F−(LiF),
O2−(Li2O), LiN−(Li3N), and Pn+ are uniformly distributed across
different depths (Figure 4a–d and Figures S17 and S18). This
highly uniform organic-inorganic SEI exhibits high electrochem-
ical stability and flexibility, effectively passivating and protecting
the lithium metal. Due to the presence of the positively charged
compact fixed layer, both Pn-A and Pn-B also demonstrate a more
uniform SEI compared to the blank electrolyte.
Furthermore, the local chemical distribution and nanostructural
arrangement of the SEI were directly visualized using cryogenic
transmission electron microscopy. The SEI of E-Pn-S exhibits
the most abundant and uniform distribution of inorganic com-
ponents (Li2O and LiF) (Figure 4e–g). In contrast, the SEI of
E-Pn-A and the blank electrolyte shows relatively sparse and
unevenly distributed inorganic components. Li2O and LiF were
distinguished and identified through detailed analysis of lattice
fringes (Figure S19). Their uniform distribution within the SEI
ensures a homogeneous and ordered Li+ flux on the lithium metal
surface, guaranteeing the safe and stable battery operation [18].
After the discharge process, the anode surface cycled in E-Pn-S
exhibit uniform lithium deposition, while those cycled in E-Pn-
A and E-blank show spiky lithium dendrite growth (Figure 4h–j
and Figure S20). SEM images of lithium anodes cycled in E-Pn-
S and E-blank electrolytes further confirm that a stable, smooth
SEI layer can effectively suppress the corrosion of lithium metal
(Figure S21). A stable and homogeneous SEI layer can effectively
suppress lithium dendrite growth and prevent side reactions
between the anode and the electrolyte, which is crucial for the
cycling stability and safety of LSBs.
2.4
Cathode Interface Modulation for Li2S
Deposition
A stable SEI at the anode interface ensures excellent cycling
stability and safety, while the polysulfide conversion kinetics
at the cathode interface largely determine the resulting energy
density. In situ electrochemical Raman tests were conducted on
the E-Pn-S and E-blank electrolytes, with the Raman measure-
ments primarily focused on the cathode interface (Figure 5a)
[41]. Compared to the control group, the E-Pn-S exhibits stronger
polysulfide signals during the mid-discharge stage, but these
signals rapidly diminish in the later stage (Figure 5b,c). This is
primarily attributed to the solvation structure, which promotes
the formation of long-chain polysulfide at the interface and
the enhancement of short-chain polysulfide conversion kinetics.
The Li2S nucleation test further confirmed that the strong ion-
pair interactions of symmetric cations promote the Li2S4 to Li2S
conversion kinetics (Figure 5d and Figure S22). Cells assembled
with the E-Pn-S electrolyte exhibit the shortest nucleation peak
time and the highest nucleation capacity (4925 s and 381.25 mAh
g−1), whereas those with E-Pn-A show only 8389 s and 297.78 mAh
g−1, and those with E-blank merely 10240 s and 223.86 mAh g−1.
This is primarily attributed to the strong interaction between the
cations and polysulfide anions, which elongates the Li─S bonds
and lowers the barrier for bond cleavage.

2.5
Universal Applications in Safe, Practical
LSBs
To achieve the practical application of lithium–sulfur batteries,
we assembled coin cells with three different electrolytes (E-Pn-S,
E-Pn-A, and blank electrolyte E) for electrochemical performance
testing. The rate performance test results (Figure 6a) indicate
that the E-Pn-S electrolyte exhibits the highest discharge capacity
across various current densities. At 0.2 C, its discharge capacity
reaches 1292.45 mAh g−1. Even at 5 C, the initial discharge
capacity remains at 705.18 mAh g−1, while that of E-Pn-A is only
501.09 mAh g−1, and the E-blank merely 302.71 mAh g−1. The
cycling performance results reveal that cells assembled with the
E-Pn-S exhibit excellent cycling stability. At 1 C, the capacity
reaches 1111.09 mAh g−1 (Figure 6b), and after 600 cycles, the
capacity retention rate remains as high as 88.4%, with an average
capacity decay rate of only 0.019% per cycle. In contrast, E-Pn-
A only achieves an initial cycle capacity of 840.1 mAh g−1 with
a capacity retention rate of 40.2%, while the E-blank exhibits
the low capacity of merely 731.88 mAh g−1 with 27.5% retention.
Consistent with the charge/discharge profiles of E-Pn-S after
different cycles at 1 C, it was observed that the curves nearly
overlap within a certain region, further confirming its high
cycling stability (Figure S28). Meanwhile, the SEI film derived
from E-Pn-S effectively stabilizes the lithium metal electrode,
exhibiting negligible resistance changes between 100 and 500
cycles (Figure 6d and Figure S29). The equivalent circuit diagram
is shown in Figure S30. In contrast, the E-Pn-A system shows
significant fluctuations in SEI resistance during cycling due to
unstable SEI formation, sluggish kinetic conversion, and internal
short circuits.
Next,
we
extended
the
experimental
conditions
to
low-
temperature and high-loading environments to verify the
universal applicability of LSBs designed based on this slimmed
solvation structure under different conditions. At -40◦C, the
battery assembled with the E-Pn-S electrolyte still maintains a
stable capacity of 950.3 mAh g−1, while those with E-Pn-A and
the E-blank only achieves 783.55 and 683.28 mAh g−1, respectively
(Figure 6c). Meanwhile, the capacity retention rate remains at
86.1% after 50 cycles. This is primarily attributed to the ability
of symmetric cations to effectively suppress severe polysulfide
anion aggregation under low-temperature conditions. Under
the condition of E/S = 5, the lithium–sulfur battery assembled
with the E-Pn-S electrolyte exhibits a capacity as high as 1,459.1
mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, with a high-capacity retention rate of 95.8%
after 50 cycles (Figure 6e). In contrast, under the conditions of
E/S = 5 and 0.2 C, E-Pn-A only achieves a capacity and cycle
retention rate of 1,138.43 mAh g−1 and 40.8%, respectively, while
the blank electrolyte E reaches merely 1,089.58 mAh g−1 and
35.7%. Furthermore, when the E/S ratio is reduced to 2, E-Pn-S
still achieves a capacity of 1,293.4 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, maintaining
stable cycling over 50 cycles with a capacity retention rate as
high as 93.6%. This strongly confirms that the slimmed solvation
structure regulates ion transport under practical lean electrolyte
conditions, ensuring rapid conversion kinetics of polysulfides
in real scenarios and achieving LSBs that balance high capacity
with high stability.
Subsequently, we expanded the application scope of E-Pn-S
to various conventional battery electrolyte systems to verify
the universal applicability of this solvation structure regulation
design for practical LSBs. We blended Pn-S with other solvent
systems, respectively. All electrolytes with Pn-S as the functional
component enable LSBs to deliver excellent cycling performance.
Under the conditions of relatively low E/S ratio, the batteries
show negligible capacity decay after 50 cycles, with a specific
capacity as high as 1300–1500 mAh g−1 (Figure 6f). This result
confirms that the design of this solvation structure exhibits
broad applicability to various electrolytes suitable for practical
application scenarios.

3
Conclusion
In summary, we propose a slimmed solvation structure and
successfully realize this concept through symmetric organic salts.
The positively charged symmetric structural molecules exhibit
strong ion-pair interactions. Through remote modulation of
anions, they increase the distance between anions and Li+ in
the outer solvation shell, suppressing the extensive aggregation
of anions under lean electrolyte conditions, thus realizing real-
time lightweight optimization of the Li+ solvation structure.
This structure features the ability to suppress the decay of ion
mobility under high polysulfide concentrations, ensuring high
utilization of active materials and high energy density under
lean electrolyte conditions. In addition, the positively charged
symmetric molecules form a fixed layer on the anode surface
under the electric field, effectively and uniformly adsorbing
anions, thereby yielding a stable SEI rich in uniformly distributed
inorganic components. The rapid conversion of polysulfides in
the lean electrolyte and the formation of a stable SEI at the
anode interface have enabled the simultaneous realization of
high energy density and cycling stability for LSBs. This solvation
structure model is widely applicable across various electrolytes,
endowing batteries with high energy density (704.6 Wh kg−1),
excellent rate capability (5 C current density), outstanding cycling
stability (only 0.019% average capacity decay per cycle over 600
cycles), and enhanced safety (passing nail penetration tests with
minimal voltage fluctuation). Overall, this design enables key
advances in both safety and performance, pointing toward a
promising future for long-range LSBs.
