Controlling the Wettability between Freestanding Electrode and
Electrolyte for High Energy Density Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Jang-Yeon Hwang,= Hee Min Kim,= and Yang-Kook Sun∗,z
Department of Energy Engineering, Hanyang University, Seoul 04763, South Korea
The present study demonstrates how to improve the electrochemical performance and energy density of lithium-sulfur batteries based
on controlling the wettability between the freestanding electrode and the electrolyte. First, to improve the volumetric energy density,
we reduced the thickness of freestanding electrode by the applying pressure. Second, in order to improve electrolyte conductivity
and wettability for pressed freestanding electrode, we reduced the viscosity of electrolyte solution by reducing the LiTFSI salt
concentration. Through these controls, the Li-S cell is constructed from a pressed freestanding carbon nanotube-sulfur cathode, 0.5
M LiTFSI in DME/DOL with 0.4 M LiNO3 as a low-viscosity electrolyte (0.72 cP), and Li-metal foil as an anode. As a result, we
can obtain a high discharge capacity of 1,400 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C and excellent cycle retention of 95% after 80 cycles at 0.5 C in
coin-type cell. In addition, scale-up (3 by 5 cm) pouch-type Li-S cell delivers a high discharge capacity of 500 mAh at 0.1 C with high
gravimetric (954.4 Wh kg−1cathode and >170 Wh kg−1cell) and volumetric (1,150 Wh L−1cathode and 515 Wh L−1cell) energy densities.

In order to mitigate the global environmental issues associated
with the use of fossil fuels and the resulting greenhouse gas emissions,
clean and sustainable energy production and storage technologies are
urgently needed.1,2 In this context, lithium-ion battery (LIB) tech-
nology has progressed considerably, with continuing applications in
potential energy storage devices. However, current LIBs have limited
applications in electric vehicles, hybrid electric vehicles, and power
grids, owing to their low theoretical energy densities.3–6 Therefore,
for mid-to-large-scale applications, it is imperative that we explore
alternatives that offer the possibility of going beyond the limits of the
intercalation chemistry of current Li-ion technology.7
The lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery has emerged as a next-generation
rechargeable battery, owing to the involvement of multiple electrons
during the redox reactions between Li and S, which provides high the-
oretical energy density (2,600 Wh kg−1) and speciﬁc capacity (1,675
mAh g−1).8,9 In addition, due to abundance of elemental sulfur and
its environmental compatibility, Li-S battery has been considered the
much more attractive power sources. Despite these advantages, current
Li-S battery have various disadvantages such as poor electrical con-
ductivities of sulfur and Li2S,10,11 undesirable shuttle reactions,12,13
and huge volume changes of sulfur (∼80%) upon cycling,14 which
have severely delayed the commercialization of Li-S batteries.
In order to overcome these problems, various strategies have been
implemented to improve the design of both the electrode and the
electrolytes materials.15–23 A freestanding electrode is one of attractive
choice as the cathode for a Li-S battery, because it is fabricated without
a binder or any powdery conductive materials, thereby allowing higher
percentages of active material (S8) in the cathode.24 In addition, by
taking advantage of their porous structure, the use of freestanding
electrodes cannot only improve electrolyte accessibility and better
absorbability of sulfur species, but also provide better electron and Li+
pathways, signiﬁcantly improving the electrochemical activity.24–27

First, the three-dimensional interconnected porous structure of free-

structure, a large amount of electrolyte is required, resulting in low
gravimetric energy density. In order to solve these shortcomings, vari-
ous studies have been carried out such as reducing the thickness of the
electrode and/or using a small amount of electrolyte in freestanding
electrode cell.34,35 However, such approaches could lead to inadequate
ﬁlling/penetration of electrolyte inside the pores. In addition, excess
of lithium polysulﬁde formed from charge-discharge process makes
the electrolyte solution more viscous, which impedes the electrode
wettability and increases the resistance of the cell.36,37 Therefore, to
improve the both electrochemical performance and energy density of
Li-S battery using a freestanding electrode at same time, wettability
between electrode and electrolyte by changing an electrode thickness
and electrolyte viscosity must be controlled.
Herein, we have fabricated a 3D interconnected freestanding
porous electrode consisting of carbon nanotubes and a sulfur com-
posite, which can be further pressed to reduce the thickness of the
electrode, thereby increasing the volumetric energy density. We have
also engineered the viscosity of the electrolyte by reducing the lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)-imide (LiTFSI) salt concentration from
1 M to 0.5 M, resulting in improved wettability between freestanding
electrode and electrolyte as well as increased electrolyte conductivity.
Moreover, to mitigate the dissolution of excess lithium polysulﬁdes
as well as increase the gravimetric energy density, we also reduced
electrolyte/sulfur ratio under 9.5 in the Li-S cell. After initial study in
a coin-type cell, we further fabricated and tested a pouch-type Li-S
cell with a high electrode stack (four stacks), which demonstrated
excellent cycling stability and high energy density.
Experimental
Preparation of the freestanding MWCNT-S nanocompos-
ite cathode.—A multiwall carbon nanotube-sulfur (MWCNT-S)
nanocomposite was synthesized by the impregnation of sulfur into
MWCNT (Hanwha Chem.) in a mass ratio of 6:4 (w/w) using a
mixer-mill machine. A freestanding ﬁlm of MWCNT-S composite
was prepared via a two-step vacuum ﬁltration process. In brief, 0.26 g
of MWCNT-S was ﬁrst dispersed in a mixture of deionized (DI) water
(200 mL) and isopropyl alcohol (IPA; 20 mL) (DUKSAN) with soni-
cation for 10 min. The solution was then ﬁltered by vacuum ﬁltration
through a membrane (Advantec No. 2) to form the ﬁrst layer of the
MWCNT-S composite ﬁlm. Next, a suspension of 0.04 g MWCNT in
a mixture of DI water (250 mL) and IPA (10 mL) was ﬁltered on the

Material characterization.—The surface morphology and thick-
ness of the prepared electrodes were visualized by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM; NOVA NANO SEM 450, FEI). Thermogravimet-
ric analysis (TGA; TG 209 F3, Netzsch) was performed under a
nitrogen atmosphere to determine the weight (%) of sulfur in the
MWCNT matrix. The surface areas and pore volumes of the bare and
pressed electrodes were measured by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller
(BET) method. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD; Rigaku, Rint-2000)
was performed in the 2θ range between 10◦and 80◦with a step size
of 0.03◦using Cu Kα radiation. Contact angles were measured by the
sessile drop method using an optical contact angle (OCA) goniometer
(Dataphysics, USA) at room temperature (30 ◦C). The optical images
were taken with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera attached to
a goniometer (Olympus BH61 with an Olympus DP70 RGB CCD
camera).

cathodes, Li metal foil was used as an anode, and a micro-porous
polypropylene ﬁlm membrane (Celgard 2400) was used as a separa-
tor. Electrolyte solutions were prepared by dissolving two different
concentrations, 0.5 M and 1 M, of LiTFSI (Sigma-Aldrich) in a mix-
ture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL; Sigma-Aldrich) and dimethoxyethane
(DME; TCI Chemicals) (1:1 v/v) with 0.4 M LiNO3 (Sigma-Aldrich)
as an additive. The as-prepared 0.5 M and 1 M electrolyte solutions
are referred to in this work as the 0.5 M LiTFSI electrolyte and 1
M LiTFSI electrolyte, respectively. The diameter of the cathode was
14 mm, and the total mass loading of sulfur was 3.1 mg cm−2. A
CR 2032 coin cell was fabricated in an argon-ﬁlled glove box, and
galvanostatic cycling was performed at a constant rate of 0.5 C (1 C
= 1,675 mA g−1) with a potential range of 1.9–2.8 V versus Li/Li+ at
30◦C. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed using
a multi-channel potentiostat (Bio-Logic, VMP3) over the frequency
range of 1 MHz to 1 Hz. A pouch cell was also fabricated, in which
the micro-porous polypropylene ﬁlm separator was sandwiched
between the dual-sided MWCNT-S cathode and the Li metal foil
anode. We stacked four sets of the cell, which were ﬁlled with
electrolyte and sealed in the aluminum pouch. The size of the pouch
cell was 3 × 5 cm, and the mass loading of sulfur in each electrode
was 3.1 mg cm−2. Both CR 2032 coin cell and pouch cell used same
ratio of electrolyte/sulfur (E/S ratio = 9.5).
Results and Discussion
Preparation of freestanding MWCNT-S.—Scheme 1 shows the
detailed synthesis procedure for the freestanding bare and pressed
MWCNT-S cathodes in Experimental section. First, the MWCNT-S
composite was synthesized by grinding, followed by the two-step vac-
uum ﬁltration process. In the ﬁrst step of ﬁltration, a known amount of
MWCNT-S composite in suspension was ﬁltered. Further, MWCNT
suspension without any sulfur was dispensed into the ﬁltration unit
to obtain an additional layer during the second step of ﬁltration. This
additional layer provides a medium for scavenging the lithium poly-
sulﬁdes and preventing them from diffusing to the anode side, which
triggers undesirable shuttle reactions.26 After completion of the ﬁl-
tration, the resulting product was dried in the oven to obtain a free-
standing ﬁlm. The as-prepared ﬁlm was then compressed at constant
pressure and dried to form the freestanding pressed-MWCNT-S com-
posite cathode.
The surface morphology of the MWCNT-S composite was charac-
terized by SEM, as shown in Figures 1a,1b. The ﬁber-like morphology
of MWCNT could be clearly visualized, with embedding of elemental
sulfur. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Figures 1c,1d)
with elemental mapping further conﬁrmed the uniform distribution of
elemental sulfur (S8) into the MWCNT matrix. The crystalline phases
of elemental sulfur, MWCNT, and the MWCNT-S composite were
determined by XRD (Figure 2a). The diffraction peak obtained at
26◦could be attributed to the (002) reﬂection plane of MWCNT.15,38

Electrolyte control.—The controlling the wettability between the
electrode and the electrolyte interface is an important factor to deter-
mine the electrochemical performances.37,39,40 In that regard, we in-
vestigated the effects of wettability induced by changing the viscosity
of the electrolyte. First, in order to conﬁrm the wettability of between
the electrolytes on the pressed MWCNT-S electrode, we measured the
contact angles. When applied the most commonly used 1 M LiTFSI
concentration based electrolyte in current Li-S batteries, the contact
angle was measured as 36◦, 14◦, and 12◦, after 0.5, 1, and 1.5 sec.,
respectively (as shown in Figure 5a). According to a beneﬁcial effect
of low viscosity electrolyte for wetting rate in previous literatures, we
lowered the viscosity of electrolyte by reducing LiTFSI concentration
to 0.5 M.41 By reducing the high molecule LiTFSI salt, the viscosity
values of 0.5 M LiTFSI electrolyte (0.72 cP) was decreased in half
than 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte (1.54 cP) at 30◦C (Table I). As expected
from viscosity data, the contact angle of the 0.5 M electrolyte dras-
tically was reduced to 20◦at 0.5 sec. After 1 sec., all the electrolyte
was absorbed, which indicate the complete wetting of the electrode
surface. Although the pressed MWCNT-S electrode advantageous to
improve the volumetric energy density, lower voids in its structure
than bare MWCNT-S electrode result in low wetting rate in Figure 5a.
On the other hand, by lowering the viscosity of the electrolyte solu-
tion, wettability between the electrode and electrolyte could be greatly
improved. Furthermore, to determine the electrolyte conductivity, the

Electrochemical performances in a coin cell.—Figure 7 shows
the electrochemical properties of the pressed MWCNT-S cathode us-
ing the 0.5 M and 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte in a Li-S coin cell. The cyclic
voltammetry (CV) plots of each electrolyte are recorded at the scan
rate of 0.1 mV s−1 (Figure 7a). For both electrolytes, the CV curves
exhibit multiple oxidation/reduction peaks, which are associated with
the electrochemical processes between sulfur and lithium. During the
ﬁrst discharge process, the reduction peak appears at 2.5 V, which
indicates the formation of high-order lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sx, 4 ≤
x ≤8), while the other two reduction peaks appear between 2.24 and
1.95 V, and correspond to the formation of low-order lithium polysul-
ﬁdes (Li2S2, Li2S). During the charging process, the CV curve shows
two oxidation peaks at 2.4 and 2.47 V, indicative of a reversible reac-
tion process. Figure 7b shows the ﬁrst galvanostatic charge–discharge
proﬁle of two different electrolyte concentrations (0.5 M and 1 M
LiTFSI electrolyte) at 0.1 C-rate. During the ﬁrst discharge, the cell
using the 0.5 M LiTFSI electrolyte delivered higher speciﬁc capaci-
ties of 1,400 mAh g−1 than 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte (1,300 mAh g−1),
which could be mainly attributed to high electrolyte conductivity, and
good wettability of low viscosity 0.5 M LiTFSI electrolyte. In addi-
tion, the high accessibility of electrolyte solution into electrode inside
by using the lower viscous 0.5 M LiTFSI electrolyte also improved
the solubility of the polysulﬁde, thereby the sulfur utilization was
drastically increased. In contrast, as shown in Figures 4c and 4d, the

low void space (macropore). From above results, we can conclude that
the reducing the viscosity of electrolyte is highly efﬁcient to enhance
sulfur utilization, and enable better electrochemical performance for
pressed MWCNT-S electrode.

V (0.1–0.2 C) and 1.8–2.7 V (0.5 C). During the initial cycle at 0.1

a high discharge capacity of 500 mAh (1,370 mAh g−1). Despite of
increasing the current density at 0.5 C, the pouch-type Li-S cell still
deliver a high discharge capacity of 425.9 mAh (1,167 mAh g−1) with
low voltage polarization (V = 110 mV) and excellent cycling sta-

coin and pouch cells in electrochemical performances.48,49 However,
interestingly, the electrochemical performance of our stacked pouch-
type Li-S cell is almost similar to that of small-scale coin-type cells in
Figures 7b,7c. These results indicate that our approach, based on con-
trolling wettability between electrode and electrolyte, could be helpful
in developing practical Li-S batteries. Finally, energy density is one of
the key issues in the successful commercialization of Li-S battery for
mid- to large-scale applications. Our pouch-type Li-S cell can deliver a
capacity of 500 mAh at the average working potential of 2.15 V. Based
on overall weight/volume of cathode (pressed MWCNT-S cathode and
Al-foil), the Li-S pouch cell delivers high gravimetric energy density
of 954.4 Wh kg−1 and volumetric energy density of 1,150 Wh L−1.
Although there still remains the challenge to reduce the weight of
the electrolyte use,50 our Li-S cell also shows the relatively high gravi-
metric energy density in pouch-type cell compared to recent works in
literature on freestanding electrode (Table III).51–58 Considering the
total electrode weight/volume, including a cathode (with Al-foil), sep-
arator, electrolyte, and Li-metal anode (in Table IV), the pouch-type
Li-S cell could deliver high energy density values (>170 Wh kg−1
and >515 Wh L−1).
Conclusions
In summary, we investigated the Li-S battery performance based
on the effect of wettability between the freestanding electrode and the
electrolyte, by changing the thickness of the electrode and viscosity of
the electrolyte. Although the macropore (void space) was reduced after
pressuring, the pressed MWCNT-S electrode had a similar surface area
with a reduced thickness compared with that of the bare MWCNT-S
electrode, without compromising its mechanical integrity and surface
