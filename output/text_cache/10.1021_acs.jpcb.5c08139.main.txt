ABSTRACT: Lithium tetrasulfide is the final product of sulfur reduction at
the high-voltage plateau of the discharge curve of lithium−sulfur batteries
(LSBs). When dissolved in an electrolyte system, lithium tetrasulfide binds
solvent molecules into the solvate shells of lithium cations. Therefore, the
properties of lithium tetrasulfide solutions in electrolyte systems significantly
affect the performance of LSBs. This study examines the effect of the
concentration of the supporting salt (lithium perchlorate in sulfolane) on the
properties (density, ionic conductivity, and self-diffusion coefficients) and
structure of lithium tetrasulfide solutions by using molecular dynamics
simulation. The range of lithium tetrasulfide concentrations in lithium
perchlorate solutions in sulfolane (SL) is limited by a Li2S4/SL molar ratio of
1/10 to 1/1. It was found that in solutions of lithium tetrasulfide in sulfolane increasing the concentration of Li2S4 salt decreases the
solvation number of the lithium cation with respect to sulfolane and increases that of the tetrasulfide anion. When the Li2S4
concentration reaches 1 M, the solvation numbers of the lithium cation with respect to SL and S4
2−become equal and lithium
tetrasulfide exists predominantly in a molecular form and/or as neutral clusters.
It was shown that the introduction of lithium perchlorate into
the Li2S4−LiClO4−SL system leads to cross-linking of the
tetrasulfide anions by lithium cations, as indicated by a shift in
equilibrium toward the formation of positively charged clusters
and an increase in their size. At a Li2S4 concentration in
LiClO4·4SL greater than 5 M, all tetrasulfide anions are cross-
linked by lithium cations and form a single positively charged
cluster, which leads to a sharp decrease in the self-diffusion
coefficients of lithium cations and the ionic conductivity of the
electrolyte system.
2. INTRODUCTION
The structure and properties of lithium polysulfide solutions in
electrolyte systems based on aprotic dipolar solvents and
lithium salts are of great interest in connection with the
development of a new generation of energy-intensive electrical
energy storage deviceslithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs).1,2
Lithium−sulfur batteries are classified as batteries with a liquid
cathode, since elemental sulfurthe active material of the
positive electrode and the intermediate products of its
electrochemical reductionlithium polysulfidesdissolve in
the electrolyte.3
The oxidation−reduction potential (ORP) of lithium
polysulfides depends on the length of the polysulfide chain.4
The difference in the ORP of lithium polysulfides results in the
appearance of clearly distinguishable signals from each redox
pair in voltammograms and chronopotentiograms.
However, the discharge and charge curves of lithium−sulfur
cells show only two platforms connected by a descending
curve, and the voltammograms show only two peaks at anodic
polarization and one peak at cathodic polarization.5 The
complex shapes of the charge−discharge curves and voltammo-
grams of lithium−sulfur cells indicate a two-stage electro-
chemical reduction of sulfur. On the high-voltage platform,
sequential reduction of long-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn,
n > 4) occurs. On the low-voltage platform, the discharge
voltage of lithium−sulfur cells remains virtually unchanged
during their discharge. It is likely that under the conditions of
cathodic and anodic polarization of the positive electrodes of
lithium−sulfur cells, electrochemical reduction/oxidation of
one form of lithium polysulfide occurs on the low-voltage
platform, while other forms of lithium polysulfides are
converted into it in disproportionation reactions.
The reduction of sulfur during the discharge of lithium−
sulfur cells can be described by the following scheme.6 Sulfur is
electrochemically reduced to lithium octasulfide (eq 1), which
dissolves in the electrolyte system as it is formed. In this case,
the solvent molecules are bound in the solvate shell of the
lithium cations of lithium octasulfide.7−9 The concentration of
lithium octasulfide gradually increases and reaches a maximum

As the lithium−sulfur cell (LSC) discharge continues,
lithium tetrasulfide is electrochemically reduced to form
insoluble lithium sulfide and smaller lithium polysulfide, such
as lithium trisulfide. Therefore, during the electrochemical
reduction of lithium tetrasulfide, the overall concentration of
lithium polysulfides and, consequently, the number of bound
solvent molecules, remains constant. Only the concentrations
of individual forms of lithium polysulfide change: the
concentration of lithium tetrasulfide decreases, while that of
short-chain lithium polysulfides increases. The number of
solvent molecules bound in the solvation shells of the lithium
cations contained in the polysulfides remains unchanged. Only
at the end of the discharge, when poorly soluble lithium
disulfide is formed, will the concentration of lithium
polysulfides in the electrolyte solution decrease and the
number of free solvent molecules increase.
A large number of studies have been published describing
the study of factors influencing the depth of electrochemical
reduction of sulfur in LSB.3,11−16 Various research groups have
found that limiting the amount of the electrolyte and
increasing the concentration of the background salt increases
the overvoltage of electrode processes and decreases the depth
of electrochemical reduction of sulfur and lithium polysulfides,
which leads to a decrease in the length of the high-voltage and
low-voltage platforms on the discharge curves of LSB.17−21 At
very low electrolyte solution contents in LSB, the low-voltage
plateau in the discharge curves disappears.
It is known that, when dissolved in an electrolyte system,
lithium polysulfides can significantly alter its physicochemical
properties and influence the mechanisms and depth of
electrochemical processes in the LSB.7−9 We believe that the
properties of lithium tetrasulfide solutions in electrolyte
systems determine the possibility of deep electrochemical
reduction of sulfur and thus play a key role in achieving the
maximum specific energy of the LSB.
Much attention has been paid by researchers to the
physicochemical properties of lithium polysulfide solutions in
LSB electrolyte systems.10,22 Lithium polysulfide solutions are
typically prepared by the direct interaction of lithium sulfide
and sulfur in pure solvents and electrolyte solutions.
Unfortunately, experimental methods have failed to produce
solutions with lithium polysulfide concentrations above 0.5
M.17 To study the properties and structure of lithium
polysulfide solutions with higher concentrations, researchers
use molecular dynamics simulations.23−26
The aim of this study was to investigate, using molecular
dynamics simulation, the effect of lithium tetrasulfide
concentration on the physicochemical properties of an
electrolyte system comprising an aprotic dipolar solvent, a
lithium salt, and lithium tetrasulfide (Li2S4, PS4). Sulfolane
(tetramethylsulfone, SL) and lithium perchlorate (LiClO4, PC)
were chosen as model objects for the calculations.
Lithium perchlorate solutions in sulfolane were chosen as
the objects of study because they are promising electrolyte
systems for LSB. The attention of researchers to sulfolane is
due to its unique physicochemical properties: high boiling
point (287.3 °C), flash point (162−165 °C), dielectric
constant (43.3−44), high oxidative stability, and low toxicity.
Thermal degradation of lithium salt solutions in sulfolane does
not result in the formation of gaseous or volatile compounds.
Sulfolane has high dissolving and ionizing properties with
respect to alkali and alkaline earth metal salts. In sulfolane-
based electrolytes, the hopping mechanism makes a significant
contribution to ion transport, which significantly increases
their specific ionic conductivity.27 An important property of
sulfolane-based electrolytes is the increase in the transport
numbers of lithium cations with the increasing concentration
of lithium salt solutions, which leads to an increase in the
cationic conductivity of sulfolane electrolytes, including highly
concentrated ones.1 In28, it was shown that LSCs with a
sulfolane-based electrolyte retain their functionality up to 160
°C.
Lithium perchlorate is of interest because its solutions in
aprotic solvents exhibit a high ionic conductivity. Lithium
perchlorate did not undergo hydrolysis. When oxidation-
resistant solvents, such as sulfolane, are used, perchlorate
solutions are stable even at elevated temperatures.
3. COMPUTER SIMULATION
3.1. Molecular Dynamics Simulation of Solutions
Classical molecular dynamics simulations of lithium tetrasul-
fide solutions in sulfolane and lithium perchlorate solutions in
sulfolane were performed using the Gromacs version 2019.1
software package.29 The OPLS-AA force field was used in the
study.30 This force field is well suited for modeling the
properties of lithium perchlorate in sulfolane.31−33

The parameters of the sulfolane molecule and perchlorate
anion were identical to those in34. The parameters of the
tetrasulfide anion (bond lengths, bond and tetrahedral angles,
charges) were calculated using the Gaussian 09 software
package35 at the B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory. Output
files with optimized anion structures were converted to the files

At the initial moment, the atoms were given random

the kinetic energy of the system corresponded to the specified
temperature. For each object, at least 15 sequential simulations
were performed to calculate the data and estimate errors. In
sequential simulations, the results are statistically independent
and are normally distributed. The error in calculating the
density did not exceed 0.5%, while the error in calculating the
ionic conductivity and diffusion coefficients did not exceed
15%.
In this study, charges were derived by using the Mulliken
population analysis and the RESP method. A comparison with
experimental charge densities obtained for sulfolane solutions
revealed that the Mulliken method offers a better description
of the electronic structure (Tables S5 and S6). The partial
charge on the lithium cations was +0.80.
The correctness of the selected model is confirmed by the
correspondence of the calculated density and specific electrical
conductivity with those measured experimentally (Table S7).
Visualization of the calculations was performed using the VMD
program.40
3.2. Calculation of Coordination Numbers
To calculate the coordination number of the lithium cation for
lithium tetrasulfide, we first constructed the radial distribution
functions (RDFs) of each sulfur atom in the anion around the
lithium cation. Each calculated RDF was then integrated. The
calculations were performed by using modeling packages
implemented in the Gromacs software package.
The coordination number of the lithium cation with respect
to the sulfur atom was taken as the value of the integrated RDF
corresponding to the position of the minimum in the RDF
following the first maximum. The coordination number of the
lithium cation was calculated as the ratio of the sum of all of
the coordination numbers of the sulfur atoms to the number of
lithium cations in the lithium tetrasulfide molecule.
The coordination number of the lithium cation for
perchlorate and sulfolane was calculated similarly but using
oxygen, sulfur, and chlorine atoms.
The PS4 coordination numbers were calculated using PS4 as
a function of the PS4 coordinate system. The RDFs of PS4 and
its integral forms were plotted. The value of the radial
distribution function corresponding to the position of the
minimum in the radial distribution function following the first
maximum was taken as the PS4 coordination number using
PS4.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1. Structure of the Solvation Shell of the Lithium Cation
in Lithium Tetrasulfide Solutions in Sulfolane
The RDF describes a two-dimensional probability distribution
of the location of a particle (solvent molecules, ions, or
individual atoms) at a distance r from a reference particle. The
position of the first RDF maximum of the solvent molecule or
anion atoms around lithium cations corresponds to the
distance between the lithium cation and the donor atom of
the ligand located in the first solvation shell. It is known that
the first solvation shell of the lithium cation is typically located
at a distance of 0.17−0.21 nm.45
To study the structure of the first solvation shells of lithium
cations in lithium tetrasulfide solutions in sulfolane in the
presence and absence of lithium perchlorate, the RDFs of the
sulfur atoms of the tetrasulfide anion (Figure 2a), the oxygen
and sulfur atoms of sulfolane (Figure 2b), and the oxygen and
chlorine atoms of the perchlorate anion (Figure 2c) around the
lithium cation were calculated and plotted. Since most of the
RDF curve shapes do not change with variations in the
composition of the systems under study, to save space, the
curves for only one composition are shown.
Since the terminal sulfur atoms of the tetrasulfide anion are
equivalent to each other (Figures S1−S4), and the internal
ones (Figures S2 and S3) are equivalent to each other, the
RDFs are presented only for one sulfur atom of each type. It is
interesting to note that the maxima in the RDF of the terminal
and internal sulfur atoms are close and are 0.214 and 0.218 nm
(Figure 2a). The distance between the sulfur atoms in the
lithium tetrasulfide anion is 0.209 nm; i.e., both atoms can
simultaneously coordinate to the lithium cation within its first
solvation shell. It should be noted that the RDF peak shapes of
the terminal and internal sulfur atoms of the tetrasulfide anion
differ. The RDF peak of the terminal sulfur atom of the
tetrasulfide anion is clear and narrow, slightly asymmetrical,
and broadens with increasing distance to the lithium cation
(the length of the peak base is approximately 0.08 nm). The
RDF peak of the inner sulfur atom is broader and
asymmetrical. The peak base length is approximately 0.13
nm. The RDF maximum for R(Li+−S1) is approximately three
times lower than the RDF maximum for R(Li+−S2). The more
distorted shape of the RDF peak for the second sulfur atom of
the lithium tetrasulfide anion indicates that the lithium

5. CONCLUSION
A study examining the effect of lithium perchlorate as a
supporting salt in lithium tetrasulfide solutions in sulfolane
allowed us to identify the key patterns that determine their
physicochemical properties. It was shown that lithium
tetrasulfide in a Li2S4 solution in sulfolane without a supporting
salt does not dissociate completely, even in the first step,
regardless of the molar ratio of the components. Moreover, the
lithium cation exhibits virtually no coordination with the
perchlorate anion.
It has been shown that with increasing Li2S4 concentration,
the coordination number of the lithium cation with respect to
sulfolane decreases, while with respect to the tetrasulfide anion
it increases. Upon reaching a Li2S4 concentration of ≈1 M, the
solvation numbers equalize and lithium tetrasulfide exists
predominantly in a molecular form and/or as neutral clusters.
Further increases in the Li2S4 concentration enhance the
tendency toward clustering, and the presence of lithium
perchlorate shifts the equilibrium toward the formation of
cationic clusters, facilitating ionic cross-linking of the
tetrasulfide anions by lithium cations.
The transport properties of the system are directly affected
by the cluster formation and changes in the solvation shell.
Increasing the Li2S4 concentration leads to an increase in
density and a significant decrease in the diffusion coefficients
and ionic conductivity. Modeling showed that at high Li2S4
concentrations (>5 M) in solutions with LiClO4, a single
positively charged cluster is formed, in which all tetrasulfide
anions are bound by lithium cations. This causes a sharp drop
in ionic conductivity to values of approximately 10−6−10−4 S·
cm−1. Such low ionic conductivity explains the absence of a
low-voltage discharge plateau in LSB, since the resulting Li2S4
is unable to undergo effective electrochemical reduction. Thus,
the physicochemical properties of the studied systems are
determined by the composition of the lithium cation solvation
shell and the structure of the resulting clusters.
