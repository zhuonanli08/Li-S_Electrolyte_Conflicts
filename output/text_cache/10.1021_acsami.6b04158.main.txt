Ammonium Additives to Dissolve Lithium Sulﬁde through Hydrogen
Binding for High-Energy Lithium−Sulfur Batteries
Huilin Pan, Kee Sung Han, M. Vijayakumar, Jie Xiao, Ruiguo Cao, Junzheng Chen, Jiguang Zhang,
Karl T. Mueller, Yuyan Shao,* and Jun Liu*
Joint Center for Energy Storage Research, Paciﬁc Northwest National Laboratory, Richland, Washington 99354, United States
*
S Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: In rechargeable Li−S batteries, the uncontrol-
lable passivation of electrodes by highly insulating Li2S limits
sulfur utilization, increases polarization, and decreases cycling
stability. Dissolving Li2S in organic electrolyte is a facile
solution to maintain the active reaction interface between
electrolyte and sulfur cathode, and thus address the above
issues. Herein, ammonium salts are demonstrated as eﬀective
additives to promote the dissolution of Li2S to 1.25 M in
DMSO solvent at room temperature. NMR measurements
show that the strong hydrogen binding eﬀect of N−H groups
plays a critical role in dissolving Li2S by forming complex
ligands with S2−anions coupled with the solvent’s solvating
surrounding. Ammonium additives in electrolyte can also
signiﬁcantly improve the oxidation kinetics of Li2S, and therefore enable the direct use of Li2S as cathode material in Li−S battery
system in the future. This provides a new approach to manage the solubility of lithium sulﬁdes through cation coordination with
sulﬁde anion.
KEYWORDS: lithium sulfur batteries, lithium sulﬁde, solubility, ammonium additive, NMR
■INTRODUCTION
High-energy battery technologies are highly desired for modern
society.1 Li−S battery is one of the promising candidates that
has attracted intensive interest in recent years. The conversion
reaction chemistry between Li and S is fundamentally diﬀerent
from the intercalation reaction in Li-ion batteries.2−5 This
diﬀerence leads to superior energy storage capability of Li−S
batteries.6,7 Meanwhile, it also makes the Li−S system more
complicated because of the involvement of intermediate
diﬀerent chain length of polysulﬁde species.3,8 On one hand,
highly soluble long-chain polysulﬁde species have been viewed
as the main obstacle in Li−S batteries, leading to gradual
capacity loss and low eﬃciency.9,10 A number of concepts have
been proposed to address this issue.6,11−16 Nevertheless, the
real application of Li−S batteries is still largely unreachable
currently.17−19 Meanwhile, long-chain polysulﬁdes have also
been proposed as additives or catholyte to improve the kinetics
and stability of sulfur cathode and lithium anode.20−22
Therefore, the soluble polysulﬁdes seem a double-edged
sword for Li−S batteries; understanding and controllable
management of polysulﬁde solubility are needed to achieve
high performance of Li−S batteries. On the other hand, short-
chain polysulﬁdes, e.g., Li2S2/Li2S, are insoluble and highly
insulating, which accounts for the poor kinetics of the second-
plateau reaction (corresponds to 3/4 of the theoretical
capacity).23,24 Especially, the chemistry of end discharge
product of insoluble Li2S is closely related to the sulfur
utilization and polarization in Li−S batteries,25 but rarely
investigated in literature. The passivation of electrode (carbon
host) surface by repeated and uncontrolled deposition of
insulating Li2S during cycling will continuously reduce the
active interface and cause cell failure.26 This is quite similar to
the electrode passivation process by insulating Li2O2/Li2O in
lithium−air batteries.7,27,28 Besides, the large initial activation
potential (up to 4 V) for Li2S is also the main obstacle to
directly use Li2S as cathode for future Li metal free sulfur
battery.29
Enhancing the solubility of Li2S in organic electrolyte is a
direct and feasible approach to sustain the active reaction
interface between electrolyte and sulfur cathode and increase
sulfur utilization.30 However, crystalline Li2S (Scheme 1) is
merely soluble in commonly used organic electrolytes.30−32 In
the past, the dissolution chemistry of polysulﬁdes has been
tuned through manipulating the coordination between Li+
cation and solvent molecular and/or special anion groups.33
For example, triﬂate anion (CF3SO3
−) ,which has strong ionic
association strength, has been reported by a few groups

propose a completely diﬀerent and new approach to tune Li2S
solution chemistry through the coordination between sulﬁde
anion S2−and ammonium additives and solvent molecules. This

smallest atom hydrogen (37 pm). In N−H groups, highly
electronegative nitrogen atom bonded with small hydrogen
atom pulls electrons away from the hydrogen atom, resulting in
tightly concentrated positive charge on hydrogen atom and an
unusual dipole−dipole interaction. This is the reason for the
extreme polar strength of N−H bonds in ammonium-based
additives, which facilitates the dissolution of ionic Li2S crystal
through the strong interaction between NH4

■EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES
Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) and deuterated DMSO-
d6 (99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich), lithium sulﬁde (Li2S, Alfa,) and Lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, BASF) were used as
received. NH4NO3 (Sigma-Aldrich) and NH4I (Sigma-Aldrich) were
dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight prior to use.
(DMSO)50(NH4TFSI) solution was obtained by mixing stoichiometric
NH4I and AgTFSI (Sigma-Aldrich) in DMSO solvent and ﬁltering the
formed AgI precipitation. Li2S solutions were obtained by dissolving
Li2S powder into (DMSO)n(NH4X) or (DMSO-d6)n(NH4X) (n = 5,
10, 20, 50, ∝; X = NO3
−, I−or TFSI−) at room temperature. 1H, 17O
and 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained
using single pulse excitation on a 600 MHz NMR spectrometer
(Agilent, USA) equipped with 5 mm liquid NMR probe at 25 °C for
the solutions of Li2S dissolved in (DMSO-d6)n(NH4NO3). The
observed
1H,
17O and
7Li NMR shifts were calibrated using
tetramethylsilane (TMS), H2O and 1 M LiCl in H2O as an external
standard, respectively. Li/dissolved Li2S cells were assembled using 0.2
M Li2S and 1.5 M LiTFSI in (DMSO)50(NH4NO3) as catholyte,
carbon paper (FuelCellStore) as current collector, celgard 3501 as
separator, Li metal as anode. Cyclic volytammogram (CV) was
performed on CHI600D (CH Instrument) within a voltage range of
1.7−3 V at a scanning rate of 0.1 mV s−1. Charge/discharge was
performed on LANHE tester during a voltage range of 1.7−2.9 V.
Quantum chemistry calculations were carried out using the
Amsterdam Density Functional (ADF-2014) program. All DFT
calculations were performed under hybrid-Generalized Gradient
Approximation (GGA) based B3LYP function with recent Grimme
dispersion correction (DFT-D3). The TZP (triple Z, polarization
functions, all electrons) basis set with Slater type functional
implemented in the ADF program is used for both geometry
optimization and binding energy calculations. The DFT calculations
were carried out using both explicit (8 DMSO molecules) and implicit
conductor-like screening model-based model with DMSO as solvent
system.■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 1a shows the solubility of Li2S in DMSO with diﬀerent
contents of NH4NO3 additive. Despite the high polar nature of
DMSO solvent, pure DMSO barely dissolves ionic compound
Li2S (<0.015 M). The addition of NH4NO3 can signiﬁcantly
enhance the dissolution of Li2S without any additional heating
procedure. The color of Li2S solutions changed from light blue
to light green when increasing the concentration of Li2S and
NH4NO3 (Figure S1a). The color change might reﬂect the
diﬀerent coordination status. With increasing the amount of
NH4NO3, the solubility of Li2S ﬁrst increases, and then slightly
decreases, with the maximum solubility of 1.25 M at solvent/
additive ratio of 10. The slight decrease in the solubility of Li2S
at higher content of NH4NO3 is probably due to the decrease
of free NH4
+ group and DMSO solvent. Besides, the NH4NO3
additive also greatly modulates the dissolution chemistry of
other short-chain polysulﬁde species, e.g., Li2S2 and Li2S3
(Figure 1b). Meanwhile, the dissolution of highly soluble
long-chain polysulﬁde species, e.g., Li2S8 and Li2S6 are
suppressed with the NH4NO3 additive (Figure S2). Further-
more, other ammonium salts such as NH4I and NH4TFSI can
also increase in solubility of crystalline Li2S (Figure S1b). This
indicates that the additive with ammonium group must play a
crucial role for the dissolution of Li2S.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is used to
investigate how the molecular interaction tunes the dissolution
process of Li2S in DMSO solvent with the addition of
NH4NO3. DMSO-d6 was used as a solvent for all NMR
measurements for the suppression of the
1H signal from
DMSO solvent itself. The 1H NMR spectrum of pure (DMSO-
d6)10(NH4NO3) in Figure 2a shows the one peak located at
7.74 ppm from the NH4
+ ions and one small peak of 3.0 ppm
from impurity protons in DMSO-d6 (99.8% purity) solvent.38
The peak position of 1H resonance from impurities in solvent
appeared at ∼3 ppm varied negligibly with the concentration of
the dissolved Li2S (Figure 2a). However, with increasing the
concentration of dissolved Li2S from 0 to 1.2 M, the
1H
resonance of NH4
+ group linearly shifts to upﬁeld (lower
frequency) in both (DMSO-d6)10(NH4NO3) and (DMSO-
d6)50(NH4NO3) (lower concentration of NH4NO3) as shown
in Figure 2a-2b. The upﬁeld shift of 1H resonances is due to the
strong interaction between negatively charged S2−(from Li2S)
and tightly concentrated positively charged H+ (from NH4
+),
resulting in the increase in electron shielding on H atom.39
Furthermore, the linear ﬁtting slopes of 1H chemical shift in
(DMSO-d6)50(NH4NO3) is about 5 times larger than that in
(DMSO-d6)10(NH4NO3), indicating stronger average inter-
action between H+ of NH4
+ group and S2−of Li2S in solution
with lower NH4NO3 concentration. The average interactions in
(DMSO-d6)10(NH4NO3) is 5 times diluted with respect to that
in (DMSO-d6)50(NH4NO3) as a result of more NH4
+ groups,
causing weaker average interaction. Then it is concluded that
the interaction of the proton of NH4
+ group with S2−of Li2S is
responsible for the dissolution of Li2S. This is further conﬁrmed
from 7Li NMR spectra (Figure 2c, d), which show that the
resonances linearly shifted to downﬁeld (higher frequency) as
increasing the dissolution of Li2S. It is known that 7Li NMR
peak shifts to the higher frequency when dissolved Li+ and S2−
concentrations increase.40 This shift is probably due to the less
shielding on Li ions in S-rich solvation surroundings and the
coordination with the O atom of DMSO and/or NO3
−anion
groups (to be discussed later and in Figure S3).
The 17O NMR signals were further obtained to understand
the dissolution chemistry of Li2S in DMSO with ammonium
additive. The 17O NMR shift from 5 through 15 ppm and from
420 through 426 ppm are from DMSO-d6 solvent and NO3
−
anions, respectively (Figure 3).41,42 The 17O NMR shifts exhibit
a two-stage feature. At stage I (Li2S concentration <0.6 M), 17O
NMR signals in DMSO-d6 show a linear shift to the lower
frequency with the increase of Li2S concentration in both
(DMSO-d6)10(NH4NO3) and (DMSO-d6)50(NH4NO3) sol-
utions. Meanwhile, 17O NMR signals in NO3
−anions stay
around 423 ppm, whereas at stage II (Li2S concentration >0.6
M), 17O NMR signal shifts from DMSO-d6 reach a stable value
at around 9 ppm. In contrast, 17O NMR signal in NO3
−shifts
to the lower frequency with increasing Li2S concentration.

Typically, lower frequency (upﬁeld) shift of 17O NMR is due to
excess electron density at the oxygen nuclear site,43 indicating
the interaction between Li+ cations and O atom in DMSO
molecules and/or NO3
−anions. The two-stage feature of 17O
NMR signals indicate more complicated dissolution chemistry
of Li2S regarding the coordination status of Li+ ionsa
coordination with Li+ between DMSO at lower Li2S
concentration, and participation of both DMSO and NO3
−at
higher Li2S concentration.
For a better understanding on the dissolution chemistry,
density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed
to reveal the detailed molecular structure of dissolved Li2S.
Li2S−NH4NO3−8DMSO system was used to simulate the
above dissolution process. As shown in Figure 4, DFT
calculation predicts two sets of molecular coordination
structures with similar binding energy of Eb = −465.1 eV
(complex I) and −466.0 eV (complex II), respectively,
indicating that both are thermdynamically favorable states. In
the former structure (complex I), S2−anion has a strong
interaction with NH4
+ cation through hydrogen bond and Li+
cation has an interaction with DMSO solvent (NO3
−anion is
free), whereas in complex II, besides the hydrogen bond
interaction between S2−and NH4
+, both DMSO and NO3
−
anion participate in the interaction with Li+ because of the less-
available DMSO molecule at increased Li2S concentration.
DFT calculation well-explains the observation of chemical shifts
from 1H (NH4
+), 7Li (Li2S), and 17O (DMSO and NO3
−)
NMR signals as discussed above. Diﬀerent from previous
understanding on the sulﬁde dissolution (Li+ ion exchange
between solvent/anion and sulﬁde anion),42 these ﬁndings
provide new concepts and understanding to eﬀectively tune the
sulﬁde chemistry.
The electrochemistry of dissolved Li2S in DMSO with
ammonium-based additive (NH4NO3) was investigated in a
coin cell. Two anodic peaks located at 2.45 and 2.75 V and two
cathodic peaks at 2.68 and 2.05 V are shown in the
representative cyclic volytammogram (CV) of Li2S dissolved
in DMSO solution (Figure 5a). They are attributed to the
oxidation from Li2S to polysulﬁde Li2S4 and from Li2S4 to Li2S8
and sulfur sequentially, and the reverse process, i.e., reduction
from sulfur to polysulﬁde, and then to Li2S.20 The voltage
diﬀerence in the redox peaks with respect to traditional
DOL:DME system can be attributed to the diﬀerent solvating
surrounding of sulfur species in diﬀerent solvents.44 The
following cycles reveal two almost identical main pairs of redox
peaks as the initial cycle. This solvation of Li2S by ammonium
salt and DMSO does not change the electrochemical behavior
of Li2S (Figure S5). The additive actually facilitates stable and
fast reaction kinetics of Li2S with great alleviation of initial large
activation potential due to the enhanced dissolution of Li2S
with ammonium groups (Figure S4). The charge/discharge
proﬁles of Li/dissolved Li2S cell shows two plateaus, in good
agreement with CV curves. It delivers a capacity of 730 mAh
g−1 at C/2 (Figure 5b). The cycling performance of Li/
dissolved Li2S cells are also shown in Figure 5c with slight
capacity increase in initial 5 cycles but capacity fades in
following cycles. One possible reason for the deterioration of
capacity might be the increasing passivation of undissolved
large sulfur species on the low-surface-area carbon current
collector (0.5 m2 g−1) used here (Figure S6). Of note, without
concentrated supporting salt (1.5 M used here) and LiNO3
additive, the Coulombic eﬃciency of the Li/dissolved Li2S cell
can be as high as 95% after 100 cycles despite the intrinsically
unstable interface between DMSO and Li anode.45 This
probably beneﬁts from solvation molecular structure of Li2S in
this work, reducing the available free DMSO solvent to attack
Li metal. This result provides promising insight on the direct
application of Li2S as cathode material in Li−S batteries with
improved reaction kinetics and stability with Li anode in the
future.

■CONCLUSION
In summary, the fundamental details regarding the dissolution
chemistry of the ﬁnal discharge product Li2S in Li−S battery
was carefully investigated in this work. Ammonium salt has
been identiﬁed as a strong coordinating functional group to
dissolve ionic crystalline Li2S in DMSO solvent. NMR
measurements show that strong interaction between NH4
+
and S2−(NH···S2−) via hydrogen bond coupled with the
solvating by DMSO solvent and NO3
−anion plays a crucial role
in dissociating crystalline Li2S. Furthermore, ammonium
additives can greatly promote the oxidation kinetics of Li2S,
which is a new approach to enabling the direct use of Li2S
cathode for high-energy Li−S battery. The understanding of the
additive functioning mechanism and solubility chemistry of Li2S
provides foundation to modulate the polysulﬁde chemistry in
Li−S system.
