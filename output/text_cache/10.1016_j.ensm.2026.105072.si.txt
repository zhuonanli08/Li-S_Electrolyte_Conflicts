Highly Polarizable Solvents Enable Adaptive Electrolytes for Practical
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries

Methods
Raw materials
Sublimed sulfur (S), lithium nitrate (LiNO3), Lithium sulfide (Li2S) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China); 1,2-dimethyl ether (DME), 1,3-diamethoxyethane (DOL), bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide lithium salt (LiTFSI), bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide lithium salt (LiFSI) and lithium nitrate (LiNO3) were purchased from Dodochem (Suzhou, China); N,N-Dimethyltrifluoromethanesulfonamide (DMTMSA) and N, N-Dimethyltrifluoroacetamide were purchased from Adamas. All reagents and starting materials were used without further processing.
Preparation of carbon/sulfur cathodes
The porous carbon and S8 (S) were mixed and grinded at a mass ratio of 1:3, and then heated at 155 °C for 12 hours under vacuum to obtain the sulfur porous carbon composite (S-PC). Carbon fiber (10 wt%), S-PC powder (80 wt%) and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (10 wt%) as a binder were dispersed in Nmethyl-2- pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry, and then coated onto an aluminum (Al) foil and dried using a coating machine with different coating thicknesses and dride at 60 °C for 12 h under vacuum.
Electrolyte and polysulfide preparation
1.0 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.3 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxetane (DOL)/ 1,2- diamethoxyethane (DME) (v/v=1:1) mixed solvents was adopted as the blank electrolyte. 0.5 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI), 0.5 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide and 0.3 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxetane (DOL)/ 1,2- diamethoxyethane (DME) (v/v=1:1) mixed solvents was adopted as the LiFSI electrolyte. 0.5 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI), 0.5 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide and 0.3 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxetane (DOL)/ 1,2- diamethoxyethane (DME)/ N,N-Dimethyltrifluoromethanesulfonamide (DMTMSA) (v/v=4.5:4.5:1) mixed solvents was adopted as the DMTMSA electrolyte.
The mixtures of lithium sulfides (Li2S) and sulfur (S) with a molar ratio of 1:3 and 1:7 were added into DOL/DME mixed solvents. Then the powder was heated under an argon (Ar) atmosphere at 60 °C to prepare Li2S4 and Li2S8.
Cell assembly and electrochemical measurements
In a glove box filled with argon (<0.1 ppm of O2, <0.1 ppm of H2O), the Li-S battery (CR2025) was assembled using SC/S electrodes with different loading as the cathode, lithium foil as the counter electrode, and PP membrane (Celgard 2500) as separator, respectively. The cathode diameter is 12 mm, Li metal diameter is 16 mm, lithium metal thickness is 450 μm. The E/S ratios were about 20, 15, and 5 µL/mg for the electrodes with sulfur loadings of 1, 2, and 4 mg/cm2, respectively. The galvanostatic discharge/charge measurements were conducted using a Neware Battery test station (CT-4008T) battery test system. The cathode specific capacities were normalized only by the mass of sulfur, as per common practice. The charge-discharge voltage range was 1.7-2.6 V (vs. Li/Li+) at room temperature (1 C=1675 mA/g).
To measure the shuttle currents, the cells were galvanostatically charged to 2.90 V and then discharged potentiostatically to 2.60, 2.45, 2.43, 2.40, 2.39, 2.38, 2.37, 2.36, 2.35, 2.34, 2.25, and 2.20 V step by step. The shuttle current was determined by the steady-state current at different potentiostatic voltages.
 Fabrication of Li || Carbon paper (CP) cells. Specifically, carbon paper was punched into circular disks with diameters of 12 mm as the cathode, and 10 µL of blank electrolyte was added into the anode side and 20 µL electrolyte containing 100 mmol/L Li2S8 and 1.0 M LiTFSI with or without 10 vol% DMTMSA as catholyte. Lithium foil as the reference/counter electrodes, PP membrane (Celgard 2500) as separator, in an Ar-filled glove box (<0.1 ppm of O2, <0.1 ppm of H2O), respectively. At 25 °C, the cells were pre-discharge to 2.07 V at 0.05 C, followed by a potentiostatic hold at 2.02 V to allow for the nucleation and growth of Li2S. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses were carried out in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV at the open-circuit voltage. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed at a scan rate of 0.1~0.4 mV s−1.
Material characterizations
The field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, SU8220) was used to analyses the microstructure of the Li metal anodes and the Li2S deposition. The XPS characterization was carried out on a high-resolution Kratos AXIS 165 X-ray photoelectron spectrometer using monochromic Al Kα radiation. Prior to analysis, the electrodes were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox and then transferred to the XPS using an air-tight transfer system to prevent exposure to air or moisture. Raman spectroscopy was carried out on a Micro Raman imaging spectrometer (DXRxi) employing a 532 nm laser for excitation. The size of the laser spot was 10 μm-2, and the spatial resolution was 3 μm. The NMR measurements of the electrolytes was performed on a Bruker AV-400 NMR spectrometer using coaxial NMR tube. The FTIR characterization was carried out on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR Spectrometer.
In-situ Raman spectroscopy
A Li-S cells with a perforated stainless steel positive case and a quartz window was used for in situ Raman spectroscopy monitor under a 532 nm laser. Each spectrum was acquired for 10 s with the frequency of 10%. The cells were tested at a current rate of 0.5 C (sulfur loading: 1.0 mg cm-2, 100 μL electrolyte).
Theoretical Calculation
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations for geometry optimization were conducted by Gaussian 16 software package.[3] Geometry optimizations and energy calculations were performed using B3LYP/6-311G(d,p)[4], and the convergence tolerance quality was the default. The solvation effect was considered with the universal solvation model of SMD[5]. 
All the classic molecular dynamic simulations conducted in this work were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS, http://lammps.sandia.gov). OPLS-AA[6] parameters with 1.14*CMIA partial atomic charges were generated by LigParGen[7-9] for the organic solvent molecules.[10] The OPLSAA force field developed by Jorgensen was used for Mg2+.[11] To get the electrolyte structure, isothermalisobaric (NPT) ensemble runs were first performed at 328 K for 5 ns and then 298 K/328 K for 5 ns to ensure the equilibrium salt dissociation. Then, 5 ns long canonical (NVT) ensemble runs were conducted and the last 0.5 ns were used to obtain the structure of electrolyte.

(e) Performance of Li||Li symmetric cells with N,N-Dimethyltrifluoroacetamide, exhibits poor compatibility with lithium metal.
Discussions on Figure S4. Symmetric batteries containing 10% N, N–Dimethyltrifluoroacetamide demonstrate stable operation for about 200 hours under 1 mA/cm2 and 1 mAh/cm2 current condition, while symmetric batteries containing 10% DMTMSA demonstrate stable operation for over 400 hours. The fluctuations at the 30th and 60th hours are caused by the switching of current magnitudes (0.1mA, 0.5mA). Moreover, 10% DMTMSA notably enhances the Coulombic efficiency (CE) of Li||Cu batteries. While the CE of the control batteries drops rapidly after fewer than 100 cycles, batteries with 10% DMTMSA maintain stable cycling for more than 400 cycles with nearly 100% CE, achieved the best result across various addition levels.

Discussions on Figure S14 and S15. The difference in charge density between TFSI– and FSI– also leads to varying degrees of polarization in DMTMSA. When LiTFSI and LiFSI were separately introduced into the Li2S4 solution, the increased Li+ concentration reduces the free solvent, and combined with the common ion effect leading to a decrease in the solubility of Li2S4. However, the Li2S4 concentration remained higher in the LiFSI added solution compared to the LiTFSI added one (Figure S14). The FSI– exhibits a higher charge density than TFSI–, which helps to facilitate the polarization of DMTMSA, thereby further enhancing the solubility of Li2S4.[12-14] The deshielding effects of 1H and 19F NMR spectrum demonstrate the more intense intramolecular electron transfer occurring in DMTMSA induced by FSI– (Figure S15).

Discussions on Figure S31. From the results shown in Figure S31, it can be observed that DMTMSA does not exert its maximum effect at lower concentrations. Conversely, when the content is excessively high, the solubility of Li2S8 becomes too low, causing the conversion to favor a quasi-solid-state form. This excessive suppression of the liquid-phase-mediated pathway is detrimental to battery performance. Therefore, there exists an optimal moderate concentration for DMTMSA.
