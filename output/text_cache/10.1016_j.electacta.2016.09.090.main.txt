A B S T R A C T
Lithium sulfur (Li-S) batteries are very promising electrochemical storage system due to their high
gravimetric energy density and low cost. Enormous efforts have been put on Li-S battery to achieve its
commercialization. The function of electrolyte is a key issue in achieving the high performance of Li-S
system, and several additives have been tried. But very few works have been working on the electrolyte
optimization method with the consideration of over-all performance, including cycling stability, rate
capability, especially self-discharge prevention ability, and the consideration of loading condition. In this
work, we focus on the incorporation of room temperature ionic liquid (IL) as co-solvent, and the effect of
IL in mitigating the polysulﬁde dissolution via systematical mechanism study of self-discharge
phenomena. Moreover, the optimization of IL incorporation ratio is discussed for the sake of the better
over-all electrochemical performance of Li-S cell by considering cycling stability, rate performance and
self-discharge prevention ability, which is found to vary with loading condition. The improved
understanding of the effect of IL on battery performance will help the development of electrolyte for Li-S
batteries.

1. Introduction
Li-S batteries have become one of the most promising
candidates for next generation energy storage systems, due to
their high theoretical capacity, low cost and environmental
friendly properties [1–4]. They have been listed as the “closest
to market” energy storage system among all in the recent Nature
news [5]. However, the practical application of Li-S batteries has
been hindered by several shortcomings, including the poor cycling
stability, low coulombic efﬁciency and the severe self-discharge
during battery storage, etc., compared to other system [6–10]. Most
of these problems stem from the polysulﬁde dissolution in Li-S
batteries during cycling [11,12]. Severe shuttle effect is resulted,
along with active material loss and high self-discharge capacity
loss. Several issues need to be addressed to further improve the
performance of Li-S batteries, including the design of sulfur
cathodes with polysulﬁde retention strategies [9], the design of
new electrolyte with reduced polysulﬁde solubility, and other Li-
electrode protection methods, etc. The novel ideas in electrode
structure design include retaining the sulfur species inside the
encapsulate structure [13–16], improving the afﬁnity between the
electrode surface and the dissolved polysulﬁde via the functional-
ity of the binders [16–20] or chemical modiﬁcation of electrode
[15,21,22]. 
Specially 
designed or commercialized 
conductive
binders are also incorporated to improve the conductivity and
sulfur utilization of the Li-S electrode [20]. The majority of
electrolyte design focused on the incorporation of co-solvent to
reduce the dissolution capability of polysulﬁde, such as room-
temperature ionic liquid (IL) [12,23–25] and other organic co-
solvent [6,26].
The design of a variety of room temperature ILs has been
performed in the Li-S batteries, because of their low ﬂammability,
negligible volatility, high thermal stability, acceptable conductivi-
ty, and a wide electrochemical potential window [27,28]. Different
ILs have been tried out by Park et al., with good analysis of the

efforts have been put on using IL as co-solvent with organic solvent
to achieve faster Li+ ion transport property as well as the
suppression of polysulﬁde dissolution [28–31]. Also, Zheng et al.
has proposed that the improvement of performance is mainly
attributed to IL-enhanced passivation ﬁlm on the lithium anode

electrode is a key parameter to achieve the high energy density and

by the solubility of polysulﬁde in the electrolyte, the diffusion rate
of polysulﬁde in the electrolyte, and passivation of SEI at Li
electrode surface [33]. Although self-discharge performance is a
critical property for energy storage system, it has just arouse
people’s attention very recently [19,34–37] and the research of
self-discharge phenomena is specially needed with systemic study
of electrolyte composition.
Huge efforts have been put in the cell-level application of Li-S
batteries with novel electrode design to further increase the sulfur
loading of the electrode [21,38–40], while very few have been put
on the study of electrolyte composition with consideration of
loading condition Li-S system, except for a few works focused on
optimization of the electrolyte with the sulfur ratio [3,41,42]. The
incorporation of IL or other organic solvents usually aims at
reducing the polysulﬁde dissolution in electrolyte design. Howev-
er, in Li-S system with higher loading, larger amount polysulﬁde
dissolution is required in the limited volume of electrolyte for the
reaction, and higher demand for ion transport capability is
required. Therefore, the research in balancing between the
reduction of polysulﬁde dissolution and improvement of ion
transport capability is in urgent need.
The low Li+ ion transport ability is commonly known as a
limiting factor for the application of IL for electrolyte. The
incorporation of organic solvent into IL electrolyte can dilution
the high viscosity and the solvate IL can still serve as supporting Li-
salts in the electrolyte [43,44]. In this work, electrolyte design for
Li-S batteries is performed by incorporating room-temperature IL
as co-solvent, and the systematical optimization of the composi-
tion is performed with balancing between the solubility of
polysulﬁde and the conductivity of Li+ ion in the electrolyte. The
improvement in over-all performance is evaluated with consider-
ation of four critical aspects: cycling stability, rate capability, self-
discharge prevention ability, as well as loading condition. Self-
discharge performance is a most direct reﬂection of the reduction
of polysulﬁde dissolution, a systematic study of self-discharge

considering that the different solubility of polysulﬁde species

Furthermore, in order to minimize the effect of the SEI-passivation

the series IL-electrolyte design, the electrochemical impedance

performed. It is found that the over-all performance needs to be
optimized with the consideration of the reaction resistance, ion
diffusion ability, and polysulﬁde solubility, etc. Also, when taking
loading condition into consideration, a much less IL ratio is
optimized in the electrolyte for higher sulfur loading Li-S batteries.
Therefore, a general idea about the IL-electrolyte optimization
method in Li-S batteries with consideration of over-all battery
performance is conceived in this work, with loading condition of
sulfur electrode specially addressed.

2.2. Cathode fabrication
The micrometric sulfur powder is used as active material in this
work. The PEDOT/PSS is ﬁrst diluted in de-ionized water to achieve a
5 wt% ratio of PEDOT. Sulfur powder and AB are added into the
binder-solvent solution. The weight ratio of these three components
is: 50% sulfur, 10% PEDOT, 40% AB. The mixture is mixed by ball-
milling method for overnight to obtain uniform slurry. The laminate
is then made by coating the slurry on a 30-mm-thick battery-grade
aluminum current collector with a Mitutoyo doctor blade and an
Elcometer motorizedﬁlm applicator. After the laminate is fully dried,
it is further dried in avacuum oven at 50 C for overnight. Sulfur mass
loading of electrode is about 0.4 mg cm2 and 1.5 mg cm2.
2.3. Cell assembly and testing
Li-S batteries are tested with 2325-type coin cells (National
Research Council Canada). The cells are assembled in an argon-
ﬁlled glove box with oxygen content less than 0.1 ppm. The size of
the sulfur electrode is 1/2-inch OD, and the size of the lithium
metal counter electrode is 11/16-inch OD. The Li foil is purchased
from FMC-Lithium Co. The separator used is polypropylene ﬁlm
(Celgar 2400). Galvanostatic cycling tests are performed on a
Maccor series 4000 cell tester (Maccor, Inc.). The cell voltage
window is 1.5–2.6 V. 50 mL electrolyte is added for the low loading
cell (0.4 mg cm2) and 70 mL electrolyte is added for the higher
loading cell (1.5 mg cm2).
Two sets of self-discharge test procedures are designed for the
fresh electrode and cycled electrode respectively. The “fresh
electrode self-discharge test” is taken by doing the 60 hour self-
discharge rest at 70% state of charge (SOC) in the ﬁrst discharge
process for a fresh cell. The “cycled electrode self-discharge test” is
taken after 10 cycles at C/10 to get a relatively stable performance.
Then the self-discharge test is performed by charging the cells to
their fully charged state and then discharge to different SOC before
performing the self-discharge test for 60 hours. The SOC state for
self-discharge test is 100%SOC, 90%SOC, 50%SOC and 30%SOC,
respectively. The discharge to precise SOC is controlled by the
galvanostatic discharge time measured from the previous cycle. All
cells are tested in the thermo chamber at 30 C. All experiments
set-up has 2-3 samples for conﬁrmation. The rate test for cells with
different IL-electrolyte is performed at C/10, C/5, C/3, C/2,1C, 3C, 6C
and C/10, with 5 cycles at each rate. The EIS is tested at 50% depth of
discharge in the frequency 10 mHz 100 KHz.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. The effect of IL-electrolyte on Self-discharge Performance in Li-S
cell
Good self-discharge prevention ability is one of the most critical
properties for all energy storage system, but the systematic
mechanism study of self-discharge is scarcely mentioned in
previous Li-S works. In Li-S batteries, the self-discharge phenom-
ena takes place when polysulﬁde species are dissolved in the
electrolyte, diffuse to the counter electrode and then react with Li
metal. It is expected that the self-discharge capacity loss could be
decreased when the polysulﬁde dissolution and transport ability in
the electrolyte is reduced, or a physical barrier could be built at the
Li-anode surface (e.g. SEI layer). The effect of IL-electrolyte on self-
discharge performance is systematical studied at a series of SOC
(100%SOC, 90%SOC, 50%SOC and 30%SOC) in the Li-S system,
considering that the different solubility of polysulﬁde species
(Li2Sx, x = 1-8) at each SOC may lead to performance changes. A
series of IL-electrolyte is designed in this work which is composed
of DOL/DME with different ratios of IL as co-solvents. Volume
ratios between DOL/DME and IL are set to 1:0, 3:1, 1:1, 1:3, 0:1 and
named as 0% IL, 25% IL, 50% IL, 75% IL and 100% IL, respectively. A
high viscous PEGDME is used as solvent for reference. Commer-
cialized PEDOT/PSS as conductive binder with micrometric sulfur
is specially selected for this work, which has demonstrate good
performance with all commercial materials and easy processing
method [20].
Self-discharge test procedures are designed for the fresh
electrode and cycled electrode respectively. For fresh electrode,
the protocol is taken by setting a 60 hour rest at 70% state of charge
(SOC) in the ﬁrst discharge of a fresh electrode, as illustrated in
Fig. 1(a), named as “fresh electrode self-discharge test”. The 70%
SOC corresponds to the end of the ﬁrst plateau, where the cell has
the largest amount polysulﬁde dissolution before precipitation
takes place. The sulfur composition here is a mixture polysulﬁde of
different chain lengths, with an average Li to S ratio around 2:4
(nominally Li2S4) [45]. The design of “fresh electrode self-discharge
test” aims to minimize the effect of the SEI-passivation-induced
difference on Li electrode surface during the self-discharge test.
Although the SEI might still form from the reaction between the
polysulﬁde/electrolyte and the Li metal during the short-time
discharge from 100%SOC to 70%SOC, but since the continuous
dissolution of Li exists on the fresh surface during discharge and
the surface is kept refreshed; we suspect that the inﬂuence from
electrolyte is minimal compared to the reaction between polysul-
ﬁde and Li metal during 60-hour long reaction time. Therefore, the
effect of self-discharge is ﬁrstly discussed in the “fresh electrode
self-discharge test” with elimination of SEI-passivation-induced
difference. The self-discharge capacity loss factor for the fresh
electrode (Qsd-fresh) is quantiﬁed by comparing the test cell with
another cell that has the same cell conﬁguration but without rest
step during discharge.

The self-discharge capacity loss ratios Qsd-fresh during fresh

There are several phenomena worth discussing in these charts.
First, at each SOC, the self-discharge capacity loss and the
irreversible capacity loss of the cycled cells with each IL-electrolyte
follow the similar trend as the fresh electrode self-discharge test in
Fig. 1(b). The cells with the 0%IL and 25%IL show largest self-
discharge loss, followed by PEGDME; and the 50%IL and 75%IL
show much smaller self-discharge loss. This is the result of the
reduced polysulﬁde dissolution ability with increasing IL ratio in
electrolytes, from 0%IL to 75%IL. With the increasing of IL ratio in
the electrolytes, gradually stronger “solvent effect” and the
reduction in polysulﬁde dissolution are resulted [43,44]. Less side
reactions with lithium and less shuttle effect can exist when the
polysulﬁde is in the solvated state than the dissolved state [44].
Therefore, more stabilized polysulﬁde can be achieved by the
solvent molecules in the electrolytes in high ratio IL-electrolytes
and a much improved self-discharge performance is resulted. Also,
the 75%IL show much less self-discharge capacity loss in self-
discharge test of the cycled electrode than the test for the fresh
electrode. This is because in cycled self-discharge test, a much
more stable passivation layer is formed on the lithium metal
surface in 75%IL compared to others during the initial 10 cycles, the
inhibition of the redox shuttle is resulted, and a much improved
self-discharge prevention capability is observed [43]. Second,
when comparing the capacity loss of self-discharge test for fresh
electrode (70%SOC) and the test for cycled electrode (90%SOC), we
can ﬁnd that the self-discharge capacity loss ratio is very similar for
each electrolyte. Third, for each IL-electrolyte, the self-discharge
loss decreases with lower SOC, except for 50%IL and 75%IL. This is
because that the majority of polysulﬁde species changes in Li-S cell
at different SOC and the dissolution ability of polysulﬁde varies
according to the changes of the chain length of polysulﬁde
[23,24,44]. At 50%SOC and 30%SOC, the polysulﬁde gradually
precipitated as solid phase because of the smaller solubility of the
shorter chain polysulﬁde (Li2Sx, x = 1-4). From higher SOC to 50%
SOC and 30%SOC, the short chain polysulﬁde gradually precipitated
as Li2S2/Li2S2 on the cathode surface, and the remaining amount of
polysulﬁde in the electrolyte decrease. Therefore, a much smaller
self-discharge loss is observed at these two SOCs. Fourth, an
interesting phenomenon of capacity recovery is observed with
some of the electrolyte (50%IL, 75%IL and PEGDME) at 50% SOC or
30%SOC. This capacity recovery may come from the re-activation of
some isolated solid phase lithium sulﬁde (Li2S). When viscosity of
the electrolyte is high, some of the polysulﬁde and sulfur (Li2S/S8)
has more chance of being electronically isolated in the electrode
and being kept away from sufﬁcient charge/discharge reaction.
Especially in the case of solvate ILs, when the solubility of
polysulﬁde are low, the polysulﬁde are supposed to remain
trapped and predominantly react in solid-state [44]. The isolation
of active material is part of the reason for the capacity decay and
insufﬁcient sulfur usage in Li-S batteries. During the 60-hour self-
discharge rest, the soluble polysulﬁde has enough time to
penetrate and react with the isolated Li2S/S8 and the sulfur
species with higher solubility is achieved. Therefore, capacity
recovery is observed in the self-discharge process. This phenome-
non is more dominate when the viscosity of the electrolyte is high
(75%IL than 50%IL and PEGDME). Fifth, comparing Fig. 3(a) and (b),
it is found that irreversible capacity loss contributed to most of the
self-discharge capacity, which means that the elimination of self-
discharge via electrolyte design can greatly prohibit the active
material loss.
In all, the 75%IL shows the best self-discharge prevention
ability among all IL-electrolytes followed by 50%IL. Detailed
mechanism study of the self-discharge performance is per-
formed, which stresses the reduction of polysulﬁde dissolution by
IL. Some capacity recovery is observed during the self-discharge
process with 75%IL and 50%IL. It gives further evidence of the
improvement in incorporating the IL in Li-S electrolyte, and
necessity in the electrolyte optimization to achieve good over-all
performance.

The increase in IL ratio can greatly improve the cycling stability

kinetics. The reaction kinetics for 0%IL is fast as well, but much
faster capacity decay is observed in both Fig. 4(a) and (b), which is
the result of high polysulﬁde dissolution.
The PEGDME shows a faster decay and a poor rate performance
with less than 400 mAh g1 at C/2. This indicates that in PEGDME,
due to the high viscosity and slow ion transportation, the Li-ion
diffusion and migration is not fast enough to facilitate the reaction
at rate higher than C/2. However, as checked from reference data
source, the viscosity of PEGDME is very near to the 50%IL, while
50%IL can perform well even at 6C. Therefore the high viscosity
does not necessarily lead to the poor rate performance. With the
co-solvent of x%IL (x = 25-75), although a high viscosity is
inevitable, the high demand for Li-ion transportation can be
compensated through the good ionic transport property, so the
good rate performance can be ensured. The overall trend of
capacity loss in Fig. 5a is very similar as the trend in Fig. 4 with
minor departure. And the minor difference may result from the
high rate test, during which some of the systems can’t provide
efﬁcient charge transport to support the high rate reaction kinetic
and the higher capacity loss is resulted. This phenomenon is very
prominence in the case of Li-S cell with PEGDME electrolyte. The
cells cycled at 1C can also be used to well demonstrate the high rate
performance of the electrolytes of x%IL (x = 0-75) compared to
PEGDME, shown in Fig. 5(b). The cycling became stable after the
initial drop due to the high rate cycling.
Therefore, although the best cycling performance at lower C-
rate and self-discharge prevention ability is obtained with 75%IL,
the optimization of the IL-electrolyte composition under certain
circumstance should be trade-off regarding to the long term
cycling performance and the cycling rate required. In the
circumstance of extreme high rate (higher than 1C) is required,
the 50%IL will be better choice.
3.3. Further Optimization of IL-electrolyte with Loading Consideration
High sulfur loading design of the electrode has drew most
attention in current Li-S battery researches, but the electrolyte
design for Li-S system with consideration of loading condition has
been scarcely mentioned. The incorporation of IL or other organic
solvents usually aims at reducing the polysulﬁde dissolution.
However, in Li-S system with higher loading, larger amount
polysulﬁde dissolution in the limited volume of electrolyte and
faster reaction kinetics are required for the reaction, therefore the
balancing between this polysulﬁde dissolution reduction and ion
transport capability need to be re-evaluated. In this work, the
discussion of IL-electrolyte is performed with 1.5 mg cm2 sulfur
loading of Li-S batteries, three times of the loading of the previous
section. In the Li-S cells with higher sulfur loading, higher
requirement exists for reaction kinetics, such as the ion transport
capability, etc. In Fig. 6, cells with 0%IL and 25%IL show the highest
speciﬁc capacity of over 1000 mAh g1 in the ﬁrst two cycles, while
cells with 50%IL and 75%IL show lower initial capacity. In the long
term cycling, the cell with 50%IL shows the best cycling stability
followed by cell with 25%IL. The speciﬁc capacity for cell with 75%IL
is relatively low. This trend is different from the previous case with
the low loading (Fig. 4), in which 75%IL shows the best
performance. With increased sulfur loading, the current density
is higher at a given C-rates for Li-S cells and the charge transport
capability of the electrolyte will become the limit for the cell
performance. The optimized composition should be somewhere
between 25% IL and 50%IL for the cells with higher loading.
Therefore, the optimal performance needed to make a good trade-

4. Conclusion
In this work, the IL is incorporated as co-solvent in electrolyte to
achieve good overall performance of the Li-S batteries, including
the self-discharge prevention ability, the cycling stability and the
rate capability, with the consideration of loading condition. The
addition of IL in electrolyte can greatly improve the stabilization of
the polysulﬁde via solvent effect; the side reactions and shuttle
effect in Li-S cells are reduced. The reduction of polysulﬁde
dissolution and the improved stabilization in high IL ratio
electrolyte leads to improvements in self-discharge prevention
ability, and noticeable capacity recovery is observed in high IL ratio
electrolyte in self-discharge test at low SOC. With consideration of
over-all performance, an optimized IL-electrolyte composition is
achieved to maximize self-discharge elimination performance,
cycling performance and rate capability. This optimized composi-
tion of IL-electrolyte has been found to highly depend on the
application circumstance, such as different rate requirement and
the loading requirement, due to trade off between polysulﬁde
solubility, ion transport capability and the reaction kinetics. In this
way, this work can provide useful insights into the effect of the IL as
co-solvent, and give important guidelines to the development of
electrolyte for Li-S batteries.
