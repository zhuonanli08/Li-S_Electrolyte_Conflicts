Practical lithium-sulfur batteries stipulate the use of a lean-electrolyte and a high Coulombic efﬁciency
of the lithium metal anode. Herein, we employ 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl ether as a
co-solvent in the electrolyte of Li-S batteries to meet the demands. The co-solvent fosters the formation
of a LiF-rich solid electrolyte interphase on lithium metal anode, as revealed by Ab initio molecular
dynamics and Femtosecond Stimulated Raman spectroscopy. The co-solvent results in an average
Coulombic efﬁciency of 99.4% for lithium plating/stripping cycles. Full cells with a capacity ratio of 2
between lithium anode and the S@PAN cathode (3 mgs cm2) exhibited a stable cycle life over 100
cycles at an electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 2 mL mg1, validating the high Coulombic efﬁciency of the
lithium anode and demonstrating the compatibility of the electrolyte with both electrodes. To enhance
the energy density, we prepared a hybrid cathode composed of 45 wt.% VS2 mixed with ZnS-coated
Li2S@graphene as the cathode. Based on the mass of both electrodes and the electrolyte, the full cell
delivers an energy density of 483 Wh kg1, which demonstrates viable Li-S batteries with the lean-
electrolyte.
Introduction
Lithium-sulfur
(Li-S)
batteries
represent
a
promising
next-
generation high-energy battery technology [1]. Recently, a
plethora of examples of high-performing Li-S cells have been
demonstrated [2–4]. However, commercialization of Li-S batter-
ies still confronts several major technical challenges, including
the shuttling of polysulﬁde species [5], rapid capacity fading of
the sulfur cathode under lean-electrolyte conditions [6], and
the low Coulombic efﬁciency (CE) of plating/stripping of the
lithium metal anode (LMA) [7], where the latter two issues can
be the real showstoppers but have received meager effort, to date
[8–13].
Remarkable advances have been made in polysulﬁde immobi-
lization by, for example, intricate carbon/sulfur nano-composites
[14,15], coated separators [16,17], and highly concentrated elec-
trolytes [18,19]. These strategies are efﬁcacious in electrolyte-
ﬂooded Li-S systems; however, the superlative results often fail
to be translated under the lean-electrolyte conditions, where
the sulfur cathode accentuates much lower capacity and fast fad-
ing [20]. Note that the inconveniently necessary but extravagant
usage of electrolyte tanks the speciﬁc energy of the Li-S cells and
raises the manufacturing cost [21,22]. Besides the challenge to
secure the high performance of cathode, the lean electrolyte
needs to facilitate the impeccably reversible plating and stripping
cycles of LMA. Unfortunately, conventional electrolytes used by
the LIB industry is unstable with LMA, both thermodynamically
and kinetically, resulting in continuous corrosion of LMA and
the formation of lithium dendrites [7]. Therefore, it is pivotal

Most recently, strides of progress have been made to enhance
the performance of sulfur electrodes under lean-electrolyte con-
ditions, i.e., at an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 5 (mL mg1) or
below [25–28]. Hollow current collectors [29], swellable sulfur
cathode [30], and ZnS-coating [31] were employed for the
enhanced electrolyte uptake. Modiﬁcations of electrolytes have
also been made. For instance, dimethyl disulﬁde is used as a
co-solvent for a higher discharge capacity [32]; catholyte com-
prising Li2S6 facilitated electrolyte permeation into thick sulfur
electrodes [33]; a salt of NH4TFSI served as a redox enhancer to
alleviate Li2S passivation, thus promoting the S/Li2S utilization
[20]. Electrocatalysts such as vacancy-rich Ni3FeN were utilized
to improve surface reactions of sulﬁde species [34].
Electrolyte represents the centrality to improve the CE of
LMA. Recently, multiple localized high concentration elec-
trolytes (LHCE) were introduced [35–39]. These low-viscosity
electrolytes are usually ﬂuorine rich, which facilitates the forma-
tion of SEI passivation layers on LMA. For instance, Nazar and
coworkers
developed
a
LHCE
that
contained
1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (HFE) in diglyme
(G2), which delivered a CE of 96.2% for LMA, and in this elec-
trolyte the S/CMK-3 cathode exhibited 100 cycles at an E/S ratio
of 5 at 55 C [35]. Liang and coworkers used a mixture electrolyte
comprising hexa-ﬂuoroisopropyl methyl ether (HFME), ethylene
carbonate (EC), and diethyl carbonate (DEC), which achieved a
CE of 99.5% for LMA and 50 cycles for the S/Ketjen black cath-
ode at an E/S ratio of 1.5 [36]. In a similar fashion, Wang and
coworkers employed a mixture of 1H,1H,5H-octaﬂuoropentyl-1
,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether (OFE) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) with the volume ratio of 95:5 and an E/S ratio of around
4.8, where the CE of LMA reached 99.3%, and the sulfur cathode
retained a discharge capacity of 775 mAh g1 at the 150th cycle
with the active mass loading of 1–2 mgs cm2 [37]. To date, lean
electrolytes have been reported in half cells either for the sulfur
electrode or for the LMA separately. However, it is rarely reported
that under lean-electrolyte conditions, an electrolyte can facili-
tate both high CE of LMA and stable cycling of the Li-S batteries
at the full-cell level.
Herein, we report a new electrolyte formula for Li-S batteries
under lean conditions, where 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,2-
triﬂuoroethyl ether (TFTFE, Scheme 1) is employed as a co-
solvent added to the electrolyte of 6 M lithium bis(ﬂuorosul-
fonyl)imide (LiFSI) in DME. TFTFE outperforms other recently
reported ﬂuorinated ethers in safety, and its cost and purity are
competitive (Table S1). TFTFE was used as an electrolyte for Li-
S batteries by Liu and co-workers owing to the its unique inert-
ness against polysulﬁdes [40]. TFTFE was later reported as a co-
solvent mixed with the electrolytes of ﬂuorinated carbonates in
a lithium metal battery with a NMC811 cathode [41]. In this
study, we demonstrate that the TFTFE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte is
highly compatible with LMA, where the average plating/strip-
ping CE reaches 99.4% over 350 cycles, and the sulfur cathode
delivers stable cycling performance under the lean-electrolyte
conditions with E/S ratios of as low as 2. Furthermore, femtosec-
ond stimulated Raman spectroscopy (FSRS) and Ab initio molecu-
lar dynamics (AIMD) simulations reveal that the TFTFE addition
does not alter the solvation structure of the electrolyte salt but
profoundly affects the electrochemical performance of both elec-
trodes. When a S@PAN cathode and a limited mass of LMA with
the anode/cathode capacity (N/P) ratio of 2 are assembled in full
cells with the lean electrolyte at an E/S ratio of 2, the full cell with
the S@PAN cathode delivered 100 cycles without a glitch. In
addition, pairing the electrolyte with a hybrid sulfur cathode that
has 45 wt.% vanadium disulﬁde (VS2) added into a cathode of
ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, the same full cell conﬁguration
demonstrated
one
of
the
highest
energy
densities
of
483 Wh kg1 based on the mass of both and electrolyte. Lastly,
this electrolyte exhibits low viscosity for excellent electrode wet-
ting and anti-ﬂammable properties for enhanced battery safety.
Materials and methods
Electrolyte and cathode preparation
The baseline electrolyte (BE) is 6 M LiFSI (99%, Oakwood Chem-
icals) in DME (99.5%, anhydrous, Sigma Aldrich). The salt was
dried at 95 C under Ar to remove trace water before use. The
mixture of salt and solvent was stirred for 3 h. The diluent, TFTFE
(99%, SynQuest Labs), was added to the as-prepared BE (BE/
TFTFE, v/v 1:2 or 1:5) to prepare hybrid electrolytes, HE-2 and
HE-5, respectively.
S@PAN cathode was synthesized as previously reported [42].
Brieﬂy, acrylonitrile (99%, Sigma Aldrich) and sublimed sulfur
(99.98%, Sigma Aldrich) were mixed in a mass ratio of 1:4 and
ball milled for an hour. Homogeneously mixed powder was
annealed at 450 C for 3 h under N2 at a ramping rate of
5 C min1.
A hybrid cathode powder was synthesized in three steps and
all moisture/oxygen sensitive syntheses were conducted in a
tube-furnace housed in a glovebox under Ar: (1) Li2S@graphene
nanocapsules were synthesized as reported [43]. Brieﬂy, lithium
foil (0.38 mm in thickness, Alfa Aesar) was placed in the tube fur-
nace, and CS2 liquid in a ﬂask was bubbled by Ar to form a mix-
ture gas containing CS2 vapor. This gas passed through the
quartz tube as ramping up the temperature to 650 C, and this
temperature was held for ﬁve hours, followed by naturally cool-
ing down to room temperature. (2) The obtained Li2S@graphene
was ﬁnely ground using mortar/pestle, then it was gently pressed
to form a pellet. The Zn precursor suspension, a mixture of 50 mg
zinc acetylacetonate and 5 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF),
was dropped on the pellet, which was dried under vacuum for
5 h to remove solvent before it was transferred to a tube furnace.

Electrode preparation
For the S@PAN electrode, slurry was made by mixing the active
material, conductive carbon (C-45), and polyvinylidene ﬂuoride
(PVdF) binder at a mass ratio of 80:10:10. A hybrid cathode slurry
was prepared by mixing the VS2, ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, C-
45, and PVdF at a mass ratio of 45:45:5:5 with N-Methyl-2-
Pyrrolidone (NMP) as a solvent. Slurry was casted on the
carbon-coated Al foil (15 mm thickness, MTI) and dried at 65 C
overnight. The dried electrode ﬁlm was punched into circular
disks with an area of 0.76 cm2.
Cell fabrication
The 2032-type coin cells were used for Li||Cu cell and Li||Li cells.
In a Li||Cu cell, Li metal foil was used as the counter and refer-
ence electrode, and Cu foil (MTI) was used as working electrode.
In both cells, regular polypropylene separators (25 mm, Celgard
2400) were employed for HE-2, whereas hydrophilic polypropy-
lene separators (25 mm, Celgard 3501) were applied for BE to
enhance wettability. All cell fabrication was conducted inside
Ar-ﬁlled glovebox, where O2 and H2O concentrations are below
10 ppm.

Electrochemical tests
A Maccor battery tester (Maccor) was used to perform all electro-

Li||Cu systems and galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) for
half-cell and full cell tests in coin cells. For plating, a current den-

current density until the potential reached 1.0 V. In a Li||Li sym-
metric cell test, current densities of 0.5 mA cm2, 1.0 mA cm2,
and 2.0 mA cm2 were applied for 20 cycles with a capacity of
1.0 mAh cm2. Then it was switched back to 0.5 mA cm2 for
another 20 cycles.
Theoretical calculations
AIMD calculations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio
simulation Package (VASP) [47,48]. Generalized gradient approx-
imation
(GGA)
with
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof

the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials. Initial
geometries of solutions were generated by randomly mixing salt
and solvent molecules using the Packmol software [50]. Number
of atoms, volume of a virtual cell, and temperature at 363 K
(NVT) were maintained during the calculation. An energy cutoff

analysis. The radial distribution function (RDF) was generated
using VMD [52].
Results and discussion
We set 6 M LiFSI in DME as the baseline electrolyte (BE) and
mixed it with TFTFE in two volume ratios, v/v 1:2 and 1:5, result-
ing in two hybrid electrolytes, referred to as HE-2 and HE-5.
When adding a co-solvent, the ﬁrst question is whether it partic-
ipates in the solvation structures of electrolyte ions. To learn the
interactions between the electrolyte species, we conducted FSRS
measurements (Figure 1a). Pure DME exhibits integrated vibra-
tion modes at 820 and 850 cm1 that are ascribed to the free
ethylene oxide [53], and pure TFTFE shows vibrations at

648 cm1 and 850 cm1 remain non-shifted in HE-2 and HE-5
compared to its pure liquid, suggesting that TFTFE, indeed, serves
as a diluent, not playing any solvation role with ions in the
electrolytes.
AIMD was conducted to further understand the coordination
structures for the electrolytes of BE and HE-2. Solvent molecules,
Li+, and FSI ions were randomly placed in the unit cell with
their concentrations consistent with experiments. Then, the unit
cell was relaxed for 13 ps, in which the time between 10 ps and
15 ps is deemed sufﬁcient to use for analysis (Figure S1). Besides,
we applied a higher energy cutoff of 600 eV for a good descrip-
tion of electron wavefunction [58,59]. Figure 1b illustrates the
two distinctive ﬁrst-coordination shells of Li+ sorted out of the
entire simulation cell. Li+ in both electrolytes is bound with three
to four oxygens from DME and FSI, where the ﬁrst coordination
shells are analogous to the previously reported solvation struc-
tures with inert co-solvents [58,60]. To be speciﬁc, a high con-
centration of LiFSI renders Li+ more likely to bind with FSI
rather than DME, and the oxygen atoms of TFTFE in HE-2 does
not participate in the Li+ solvation. The ions and DME already
form the stable solvation structures before TFTFE is added, where
less dielectric TFTFE barely perturbs the Li-O (FSI) coordination.
Furthermore, the radial distribution function (RDF) and the
quantitative analysis of Li-O bonds also suggest that TFTFE func-
tions as a diluent without involving in the coordination chem-
istry (Figure S2 and Table S2). The AIMD results thereby
corroborate the above Raman spectroscopy interpretation of
unchanged solvation upon TFTFE addition.
The new electrolyte is designed to improve the reversibility of
LMA. We measured CE for each plating/stripping cycle by calcu-
lating the ratio of the stripped lithium over a ﬁxed amount of
plated lithium, i.e., 1.0 mAh cm2, where the current density is
0.5 mA cm2
.
Cu foil was used as a working electrode and 10 cycles
of electrochemical sweeping treatment from 0.01 V to 1.0 V (vs.
Li+/Li, hereafter) at 0.05 mA cm2 was applied before lithium is
initially plated (Figure S3). For HE-2, the ﬁrst plating and stripping
overpotentials were respectively 35 mV and 27 mV (Figure 2a),
where the CE of the initial cycle reached 96.3%. It gradually rose
to 99.6% by the 30th cycle with a reduced overpotential of
25 mV and 25 mV upon plating and stripping, respectively. In
contrast, BE exhibited an erratic behavior of plating/stripping, as
shown in Figure 2b. Initial plating/stripping overpotentials of BE
were 54 mV and 45 mV, respectively, and the ﬁrst CE was
93.7%, which gently increased to 97.7% by the 10th cycle, but it
started to ﬂuctuate during the following cycling. Especially at
the 26th cycle, a severe overcharge was observed with the attained
capacity of 1.4 mAh cm2, 40% more than the plated amount in
the same cycle. We postulate that the extra capacity of 0.4 mAh
cm2 came from the stranded Li mass over the previous cycles,
which somewhat got re-wired back to the current collector in this
cycle. Figure S4 shows the GCD proﬁles of the six cycles, where the
CE exceeds 100%. Short circuit is typically associated with abrupt
potential drops, which was not observed in these plating/stripping
proﬁles; therefore, the high CE here is unlikely due to short circuit.
Furthermore, we tested CE of LMA in HE-2 at 2.0 mA cm2, a
quadrupled current density, where dendrites may more likely
form (Figure 2c). LMA exhibited an increased extent of polariza-
tion with an initial overpotential of 190 mV that stabilized to
130 mV after 30 cycles, which is a marginally lower overpotential
than the heavily ﬂuorinated electrolytes (FEC/FEMC/HFE) and
the engineered Li2S8/LiNO3 electrolyte, but slightly higher than
that of using 10 M LiFSI in EC/DMC and the ternary-salt of
LiFSI-LiTFSI-LiNO3 [41,61–63]. The cell showed stable potential
proﬁles with the CE increased to 98.7% at the 30th cycle. We
tested CE over 350 cycles, and the arithmetic mean CE values
are 99.4% and 99.3%, at 0.5 mA cm2 and 2.0 mA cm2, respec-
tively, in HE-2 (Figure 2d).
The cycling stability of LMA in BE and HE-2 was also investi-
gated in Li||Li symmetric cells at various current densities. As
observed, BE displays a greater overpotential upon plating and
stripping, i.e., 31 mV, than that of HE-2, 15 mV, at 0.5 mA cm2.
The stability advantage of HE-2 is more evident at higher current
densities of 1.0 mA cm2 and 2.0 mA cm2. The results suggest
the utility of TFTFE in protecting the surface of lithium metal
(Figure 2e and f).
In order to understand how TFTFE as an electrolyte diluent
enhances Li cycling, we investigated the surface morphology of
the cycled LMA and the SEI compositions. After 20 cycles of plat-
ing/stripping at 0.5 mA cm2 with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm2 in
Li||Li symmetric cells, LMA in HE-2 displays smooth surface with
tightly bound Li grains, whereas BE shows relatively smaller Li
islands with conspicuous cracks, where neither of the two elec-
trolytes grows dendrites on LMA’s surface, as shown in Figure 3a
and b. The ratios of Li-OFSI-, Li-ODME, and Li-OTFTFE suggested by
RDF analysis (Table S2) show that Li+ prefers to bind FSI over
DME in both BE and in HE-2, which suggests that FSI may be
preferably reduced on the LMA surface than DME.
We studied the SEI compositions by depth-proﬁling X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on the cycled LMA with Ar+
sputtering that removes the top layer up to 10 nm thick beneath
the surface (Figure 3c). The N 1s spectra reveal the degree of FSI
reduction on the surface of LMA in electrolytes. The peaks at
396.8 eV and 399.1 eV can be attributed to Li-N and N-SOx,
respectively, and these signals could still be found at 10 nm deep
in LMA for HE-2. However, those peaks were not detectable for
BE when the top SEI layer was removed by 5 nm thick, which
indicates the preferential FSI reduction on LMA in HE-2.

The advantage of using TFTFE as a co-solvent also lies on its
unique physical properties (Table S5), particularly the ﬂammabil-
ity resistance, owing to its high ﬂash point. Flammability tests
were conducted by placing a ﬁre source to the electrolyte-
soaked glass ﬁber separators. A separator soaked in HE-2
remained without ﬁre after burning it for ﬁve consecutive sec-
onds, and when the lighter was taken away, it showed car-
bonized trace on the surface (Figure S12a). This indicates that
the TFTFE addition enhances battery safety. In contrast, the BE-
soaked separator was ignited right after the lighter approached
and maintained its red-yellow ﬂame until the electrolyte was
burned out (Figure S12b). Owing to the highly combustible char-
acteristics of DME, high salt concentration alone could not elim-
inate the ﬂammability of the BE electrolyte (Video S1 and Video
S2). In a similar fashion, TFTFE also acted as a diluent, where the
viscosity was drastically lowered from 107.72 mPas for BE to
11.34 mPas for HE-2. Likewise, ionic conductivity decreases
from 11.53 mS cm1 for BE to 4.59 mS cm1 for HE-2 because
TFTFE does not solvate Li-ions [74] (Table S6). The conductivity
results suggest that both the diffusivity of diffusion and the
mobility under the electric ﬁeld of polysulﬁdes in HE-2 and
HE-5 should be smaller than in BE. This is due to the diluent
characteristics of TFTFE—a weak solvent with low permittivity,
and TFTFE does not perturb the original Li+ solvation structure.
Thus, the presence of TFTFE sterically narrows the effective path-
ways of ions, thus lowering their movability in the electrolyte,
including both Li+ and polysulﬁdes. Thus, the diluent may help
curtail the polysulﬁde shuttle effect.
Conclusion
In summary, we developed an electrolyte formula for the Li-S
battery under lean-electrolyte conditions. The addition of TFTFE
as a co-solvent to LiFSI/DME promotes the formation of LiF-
abundant SEI, where the LMA exhibits a high CE of 99.4%.
The TFTFE addition also alters the physical properties of the elec-
trolyte, where the electrolyte wets the carbon/sulfur composite
cathode better, which promotes the electrolyte uptake under
the lean-electrolyte conditions. When the new electrolyte, i.e.,
HE-2, is paired with S@PAN cathode at a low E/S ratio of 2 and
a N/P ratio of 2, the full cell can be cycled for 100 cycles. This
is a pioneering example of a full cell design, where an electrolyte
facilitates excellent performance and reversibility of both elec-
trodes under lean-electrolyte conditions. To increase the energy
density, we tested this electrolyte with a hybrid cathode, where
45 wt.%
VS2
is
mixed
with
the
composite
of
ZnS-
Li2S@graphene, which yielded an energy density of 483 Wh kg1
based on the mass of both electrodes and electrolyte. The results
here represent a new milestone achieved for the safe and high-
energy density Li-S battery with a lean-electrolyte.
