Introduction
Due to the continuously increasing global energy demand 
and growing scarcity of fossil fuel resources, the devel­
opment of efficient and clean energy storage technologies 
has become particularly urgent. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) bat­
teries have attracted significant research attention owning 
to their environmental friendliness, cost-effectiveness, and 
abundance of sulfur as an active material [1–4]. With an 
energy density far exceeding that of traditional lithium-ion 
batteries, Li-S batteries enable long-distance electric vehi­
cles travel and prolonged operation of electronic devices, 
promoting transformative changes in the renewable energy 
sector [5, 6]. However, Li-S batteries still face challenges 
that hinder their commercial use; their main limitations are 
applicable to both the cathode and anode [7–9]. On the cath­
ode side, due to the inherently low electrical conductivity

Abstract
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered promising candidates for next-generation energy storage technologies due to 
their high theoretical energy density. However, the instability of the lithium anode and the sluggish redox kinetics of sulfur 
significantly hinder their practical application. To overcome these challenges, the development of electrolyte additives with 
dual functions, protecting the lithium anode and promoting sulfur redox reactions has emerged as a key strategy for break­
ing through current performance bottlenecks. In this study, the effect of 6-bromohexanenitrile (BHN) as a bifunctional 
electrolyte additive on Li-S battery performance is investigated. The findings reveal that the bromo terminal group in BHN 
participates in the formation of the SEI layer on the lithium anode, generating inorganic compounds such as LiBr, thereby 
enhancing interface stability, suppressing lithium dendrite growth, and protecting the lithium anode from side reactions 
with polysulfides. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that the BHN additive can effectively 
modulate the molecular orbital energy levels of lithium polysulfides, resulting in reduced molecular orbital energy gaps 
and enhanced redox activity. This accelerates polysulfide interconversion and effectively improves interfacial reaction 
processes. Li-S cells incorporating BHN exhibit an initial discharge specific capacity of 1360 mAh g− 1 at a 0.1 C rate. 
Moreover, at a current density of 0.2 C, the cells demonstrate excellent cycling stability, retaining 80.4% of their capacity 
after 120 charge-discharge cycles. This work presents a new strategy for the design of high-performance lithium-sulfur 
battery electrolytes and provides valuable theoretical insights into the development of bifunctional electrolyte additives.

internal resistance persists during cycling, resulting in slow 
reaction kinetics and affecting the rate performance of the 
battery [10–13]. Additionally, the high solubility of polysul­

leads to continuous loss of active sulfur and rapid capacity

ited during repeated cycling, forming dendritic structures 
which consume active lithium and may cause safety risks, 
such as internal short circuits [19–23]. Collectively, these 
issues significantly impede the practical applications of Li-S 
battery technology.
The incorporation of organic small molecules into Li-S 
battery electrolytes has emerged as an effective strategy to 
address the challenges described above. These molecules, 
characterized by diverse functional groups, are flexible and 
easily tunable, which allows for structural optimization to 
improve performance of Li-S batteries. Li et al. [24] suc­
cessfully anchored sulfur species using strong chemical 
interactions between anthraquinone molecules and lith­
ium polysulfides, substantially extending the cycle life of 
high-loading sulfur cathodes. Zheng et al. [25]. introduced 
2,4-bis(p-tolylthio)−1,3-dithia-2,4-diphosphacyclobutane-
2,4-disulfide into Li-S batteries as a functional molecule 
that undergoes nucleophilic substitution with polysulfides to 
form organic phosphorus polysulfides in situ, which remain 
fixed at the cathode due to their low solubility in the electro­
lyte, effectively suppressing the shuttle effect. Benzyl chlo­
ride (BzCl) was used as an electrolyte additive [26] to form 
a dense and robust solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer 
rich in LiCl, LiF and Li2O on the lithium metal surface, 
accelerating ion transport and preventing decomposition 
of solvents and lithium polysulfides. Wang et al. [27] used 
crown ether-based electrolyte additive, dibenzo-24-crown-8 
(D24C8), to address lithium anode degradation by reversing 
the lithium electroplating/stripping behavior, suppressing 
dendrite growth and reducing harmful side reactions with 
polysulfides.
However, current research focuses mostly on resolving 
individual electrode problems with few studies addressing 
both the sulfur cathode and the lithium anode simultane­
ously. To further improve the overall performance of Li-S 
batteries, there is an urgent need to develop multifunctional 
additives that can stabilize both the cathode and anode 
interfaces.
In this study, 6-bromohexanenitrile (BHN) is proposed 
as an electrolyte additive for Li-S batteries. Experimental 
results and theoretical calculations reveal the mechanism of 
protective layer formation by BHN on the sulfur cathode 
surface that promotes interaction with lithium polysulfides, 
thereby accelerating their redox kinetics. Simultaneously, 
LiBr deposits on the lithium anode surface form an inter­
face protection layer, significantly reducing anode corrosion 
by the electrolyte. At a low rate of 0.1 C, the BHN-contain­
ing battery cell achieves a high capacity of 1360 mAh g− 1; 
while under a higher rate of 0.2 C, it maintains excellent 
cycling stability, with a capacity retention of 80.4% after 
120 cycles. These results demonstrate the potential of BHN 
as a bifunctional additive that enhances both redox kinetics 
and interfacial stability in Li-S batteries.
Results and discussion
To investigate the surface composition and chemical states 
of the sulfur cathode in the presence of BHN as an addi­
tive, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was 
performed on the electrodes after 50 cycles at room tem­
perature (Fig. 1). The fitted C 1 s spectrum, as shown in Fig. 
1d, revealed two new characteristic peaks at 286.0 eV and 
291.1 eV, which were absent in the spectrum of the refer­
ence cell without BHN (Fig. 1a). The peak at 286.0 eV is 
attributed to the C ≡ N bond vibration, consistent with the 
nitrile functional group in the BHN molecule [28], indi­
cating the participation of the additive in the formation of 
the interfacial protection layer. The peak at 291.1 eV cor­
responds to a Co-C bond [29], suggesting that the cyano 
group in BHN coordinates with cobalt atoms in the cathode 
material to form Co-C bonds. The presence of Co-C bonds 
enables stable adsorption of BHN onto the sulfur cathode 
surface, to form a protective film that effectively suppresses 
the diffusion of polysulfides into the electrolyte.
In Co-NC, nitrogen atoms react with polysulfides to form 
Li-N bonds, thereby immobilizing the polysulfides and 
preventing their diffusion. N 1s spectra, shown in Fig. 1b 
and e, confirms the presence of Li-N bonds with a charac­
teristic peak at 398.7 eV [30], which is further supported 
by the Li 1s spectrum (Fig. 1c). A new peak at 401.3 eV 
is observed in the spectrum of the electrode material after 
cycling with BHN (Fig. 1e), corresponding to the C ≡ N 
bond. The appearance of C ≡ N features in both C 1 s and N 
1 s spectra provides strong evidence for the involvement of 
BHN in the interface layer formation on the sulfur cathode. 
Comparing the spectra in Fig. 1c, f and a noticeable shift 
of the Li 1s peak toward higher binding energy is observed 
in the presence of BHN, which can be attributed to the sp 
hybridization of nitrogen atoms in the cyano group, which 
have a higher electron cloud density. Upon combination of 
the cyano group with lithium atoms, nitrogen attracts elec­
trons from lithium, resulting in reduced electron cloud den­
sity consequently shifting the Li 1s peak to higher binding 
energy. Furthermore, the enhanced Li-S peak is attributed to 
the formation of BHN-SxLi species produced by the reaction

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to 
examine the microstructural changes in the sulfur cathode 
after repeated cycling. Figure 2a and d show schematic dia­
grams of the top and side views of the sulfur cathode. SEM 
images of the cathode after 50 cycles in both the reference 
cell and the BHN-containing cell are presented in Fig. 2b, c, 
e and f. After 50 cycles, significant cracking is observed on 
the surface of the sulfur cathode in the reference cell. This 
degradation is attributed to large volume change between 
elemental sulfur and lithium sulfide during cycling, lead­
ing to the electrode structural instability, manifesting itself 
in sharp decline in battery performance and potential safety 
concerns. In contrast, the cathode surface of the BHN-con­
taining cell remained smooth and uniform after the same 
number of cycles (Fig. 2c and f). This is attributed to the 
coordination between BHN and cobalt (Co) in the carbon 
framework, facilitating the formation of a stable cathode 
electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer, which effectively sup­
presses the shuttle effect of polysulfides, significantly 
extending the cycle life. This protective layer effectively 
mitigates structural damage during battery cycling, slows 
down cell capacity decay and improves the safety of the 
Li-S battery.

The improvement in polysulfide conversion kinetics can pri­
marily be attributed to the faster redox behaviour of BHN-
SxLi intermediates formed through the reaction between 
BHN and lithium polysulfides. These intermediates pos­
sess more favourable redox properties than Li2Sx, thereby 
improving polysulfide conversion efficiency. In addition 
to the optimization of polysulfide conversion mechanism, 
BHN also effectively immobilizes polysulfides, prevent­
ing their dissolution and diffusion into the electrolyte and 
thereby suppressing the shuttle effect. This mechanism not 
only reduces the loss of active sulfur but also minimizes 
side reactions between lithium polysulfides and the lithium 
anode by limiting their direct contact, thus protecting the 
latter from corrosion. As a result, the BHN-containing cell 
exhibits significantly improved discharge specific capacity 
and cycle life.

is insufficient to effectively enhance cell performance, while 
excessive amounts hinder Li+ diffusion. Therefore, achiev­

The interaction between BHN and lithium polysulfides 
was further investigated using density functional theory 
(DFT) calculations. Using Li2S6 as a representative species, 
its reaction with BHN was found to produce BHN-S6Li and 
LiBr. Thermodynamic analysis yielded a Gibbs free energy 
of −1391.2 kJ mol− 1, confirming the spontaneous nature of 
this reaction. To understand the superior redox kinetics of 
BHN-SxLi (x = 1,2,4,6,8) compared to Li2Sx, the frontier 
molecular orbital (FMO) energy levels of these interme­
diates were calculated. As shown in Fig. S3b, BHN-SxLi 
exhibits narrower band gaps relative to Li2Sx. The lower 
energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) 
implies a higher electron affinity, while the elevated energy 
of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) reflects 
a stronger electron-donating capability. Therefore, it can be 
concluded that BHN-SxLi intermediates possess superior 
redox kinetics and charge transfer properties, compared to 
Li2Sx, resulting in enhance electrochemical performance of 
the BHN-containing cell.

Conclusion
In summary, the incorporation of BHN as a bifunctional 
electrolyte additive substantially enhanced the electro­
chemical performance of Li-S batteries. First, BHN reacts 
more readily with lithium polysulfides than Li2Sx forming 
BHN-SxLi intermediates that lower the activation energy for 
polysulfide redox reactions, thereby significantly improving 
the discharge specific capacity. Second, BHN effectively 
immobilizes lithium polysulfides at the sulfur cathode via 
C-S bond formation, suppressing their dissolution and dif­
fusion into the electrolyte, mitigating the shuttle effect and 
reducing the loss of active sulfur. Finally, the stable inter­
facial layer formed in the presence of BHN and contain­
ing LiBr offers dual benefits: it prevents corrosion of the 
lithium anode and inhibits dendrite growth. As a result, Li-S 
cells containing BHN as a functional additive demonstrated 
excellent cycling stability, maintaining a high capacity 
retention of 86.2% after 120 cycles at a current rate 0.2 C. 
This study demonstrates that the introduction of bifunca­
tional additives into the electrolyte is an effective strategy 
for simultaneously addressing the critical challenges associ­
ated with both sulfur cathodes and lithium-metal anodes in 
Li-S batteries.
