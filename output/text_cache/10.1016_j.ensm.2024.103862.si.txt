As is shown in Figure S3b, the peaks of Li-I bond (2.4 Å), I-F bond (2.5 Å), and Li-F bond (2.6 Å) prove that I- is in the solvation sheath of Li+ for LiI additive. I- ions interact with FSI- via I-F bonds. The smaller radial distance of the I-F interaction in the case of LiI indicates (Figure S3e) that I- is near F (from FSI-) in the presence of LiI compared to ZnI2. This can be confirmed from the 19F NMR spectrum in Figure 2d with a more shielded F for LiI as compared to ZnI2. Unlike LiI, the Li-I- peak in the presence of ZnI2 additive is observed at a larger radial distance of 3.3 Å indicating that I- might not be in the primary solvation sheath of Li+. Moreover, the higher intensity of the I-F bond in comparison to Li-F further suggests the preferential interaction between I and F (in FSI-). Since ZnI2 is dissolved into the base electrolyte (STD-1M LiNO3), the presence of an additional solvation sheath for Zn2+ ions were confirmed. The Zn2+ radial distribution function is showed in Figure S2b. It is very clearly that the strong peak of Zn-ONO3 and Zn-I bond shows that the Zn2+ interact with NO3- with O atom and I- ions are in the Zn-ions solvation sheath. The peak of Zn-OFSI (2.4 Å) indicates that the presence of FSI- ions in the Zn solvation sheath through the Zn-O bond rather that Zn-N bond (7 Å). The lower intensity peak of Zn-ODME shows that DME is not in the primary solvation sheath of Zn2+.

It can be seen from Figure 4a that the Li metal surface becomes black after dropping ZnI2 additive on the surface of Li metal. This indicates that the chemical reaction between Li metal and ZnI2. From EDS image in Figure 4a, Zn, N, and F element distribution shows a particle-like morphology. This indicates that the decomposition products of electrolyte are attached together. Since the reduction potential of LixZn is lower that the reduction potential of the solvent and anions in the electrolyte [2], the formation of LixZn promotes the decomposition of electrolyte. The N, F, and S are the decomposition products from NO3- and FSI-. After dropping 20 ZnI2 additive electrolyte on the surface of Li metal, Zn metal and LiI (From Figure S7b-c) is formed by the reaction between Li metal with ZnI2. The intensity of XRD peak around 36.1 confirmed with Li metal decreased with the adding amount of ZnI2 additive electrolyte. This indicates that the Li metal react with ZnI2 and the products covered on the surface of Li metal. The increased amount of LiN3 (Figure S7b) is observed on the surface of Li metal. The formed Zn metal promotes the reduction of LiNO3 because of the strong binding energy of Zn-ONO3 in Zn solvent structure. Small peak of ZnS for S cathode and LiZn for Li metal are observed from the XRD curves in Figure S 7d.

Similar morphology of Zn@LiZn hybrid SEI could be observed in Figure S8 after 5 cycles both charge and discharge process, indicating that the alloy hybrid SEI is formed during cycles and the structure of alloy hybrid SEI is stable. The formation of Zn and LiZn alloy is covered by organic components and rich inorganic components. The deposited Zn is stable due to inhibited electron transfer through the SEI laye while the Li ion conductivity is improved by the rich inorganic components.

Supplementary Figure 27. a) Map sum spectrum analysis of the ZnS nanosheet. b) XPS Li 1s, Zn 2p, S 2p, I 3d, N 1s and F 1s spectra of pure ZnS and the ZnS with polysulfides adsorption.
Only Zn and sulfur can be seen from the map sum spectrum of TEM. The detailed value can be seen in table S2. The Atomic percentages for Zn and S are 35.19 and 64.81. The excess S can be contributed to the Li2S4 due to the equivalent element content in ZnS.
ZnS with absorbed polysulfides samples was prepared by mixing 0.05M STD – 1M LiNO3 0.05M ZnI2 mixed with 0.1M Li2S6 DME solvent. In order to verify the strong chemical interaction between ZnS and polysulfides, XPS was used to investigate the chemical interaction between ZnS and polysulfides. Pure commercial ZnS particles show classical peak at 1045.1eV and 1022.2eV of Zn 2p spectra and 161.7 eV and 162.9 eV of S 2p spectra, respectively. After adsorption, the peak value of Zn 2p is shifted to to lower binding energies by 1.08 eV in Figure S27b, indicating that binding energy decreases after adsorption of Li2S6. The decrease in binding energy indicates electron transfer from polysulfides to Zn atom in ZnS, illustrating the strong interaction. The The presence of LiFSI (688 eV), LiF (684.8 eV) in Figure S26b and S27b, and SO3- in Figure 6c, for ZnS@Li2S6 samples, suggests the possibility of LiF and SO3- deposition on the S-cathode from the incomplete removal of LiFSI.

Supplementary Figure 30. EIS plots of Li || Li and Li-S full cells with different electrolytes. a) Li || Li cells before cycle, b) Li || Li cells after 2 cycles, c) Li-S cells before cycles, and d) Li-S cells after 5 cycles.
For Li||Li batteries in Figure S28a-b, one semicircle related to Rct in high frequency region was observed. After 2 cycles, the Rct (5.113 Ω) become lower for ZnI2 additive electrolyte. This indicates fast Li charge transfer for plating/stripping process. 
For LSB batteries before cycles in Figure S28c, the Nyquist curves for base and LiI additive electrolyte consisted of one semicircle in high and medium frequencies and a straight line in low frequencies, which represents charge transfer resistance (Rct) and lithium diffusion impedance (Zw) into a solid cathode, respectively[4]. As for ZnI2 additives, the semicircle in high-frequency region results from Li+ migration through sulfur cathodes and SEIs formed via the chemical reaction of ZnI2 with lithium metal on the anode side[5]. The semicircle in low-frequency region is the charge transfer resistance at the interface between the electrode and electrolyte. This suggests that the chemical reaction happens immediately once ZnI2 additive electrolyte is in contact with the lithium anode. Compared with base electrolyte before the cycle, the Rct of LiI and ZnI2 additive electrolyte is bigger. This may be caused by the formation of Poly DME with the help of I-[6].
After 5 cycles in Figure S28d, a uniform interface is gradually formed in LSB with ZnI2 additive electrolyte. Compared to the base and LiI additive electrolyte for LSB after 5cycles, the ZnI2 additive electrolyte exhibits much lower Rct (25.19 Ω), suggesting that the introduction of ZnI2 benefits redox kinetics of sulfur cathode via formation of ZnS nanosheet and stabilization of Li anode. 



Supplementary Figure 31. Galvanostatic charge and discharge plot of different electrolytes. Charge and discharge curves with various electrolytes a) at 0.1C 1st cycles, b) at 0.5 C after 0.1 C 1st cycles, c) with STD-0.05M ZnI2 at 0.1 C for 1st and 2ed cycles cutting off at 3000 mAh g-1, and d) with STD-0.05M ZnI2 at 0.1 C for 1st and 2ed cycles cutting off at 9000 mAh g-1. 
From the charge and discharge plot in Figure S31a, the shuttle effect occurs for STD-0.05M ZnI2 additive. Both STD and STD 0.05M ZnI2 electrolytes showed similar discharge platforms (S8 to Li2S4 process) with the same discharge capacity, which indicates that fewer LPSs form during the discharge process. For STD-0.05M LiI electrolyte, the first platform is larger than the other electrolytes. This indicates that I- ions promote the conversion from S8 to Li2S4. The bigger charge capacity compared with the discharge capacity shows the shuttle effect in the LSB. Li2S6 was not observed at the 2.4 V voltage for ZnI2 additive in Figure S31b. This indicates that ZnI2 reacted with Li2S6 during the discharge process, which caused the decreased capacity of sulfur.
From Figures S31c-d, the discharge capacity is obviously lower in the 2ed cycle when the charging step was stopped at a larger capacity (9000 mAh g-1). This indicates that the long charge platform was caused by more LPSs in the electrolyte.
