Abstract
Liquid battery electrolytes are utilized in most battery systems to date as they
provide improved electrode contact and ionic conductivity compared to solid
electrolytes; however, they pose major challenges regarding safety. Being
highly flammable, toxic, and volatile, leakage of such a liquid electrolyte is
always considered a major safety risk. Hence, the improvement of liquid
electrolytes remains an important goal, especially for high gravimetric energy
battery systems like the lithium–sulfur battery (LSB), which is considered a
suitable battery type to enable fully electric‐powered aviation. Here, a study on
the effects of a variation of the electrolyte media and salt was conducted to
establish an inexpensive alternative liquid electrolyte system to the state‐of‐
the‐art DOL/DME electrolyte of LSB. The combination of DEGMEE and LiFSI
led to the best cycling performance showing an increase in cycling stability
(110 cycles at 97% Coulombic efficiency) and specific capacity (~500 mAh g−1
in the 110th cycle) at a moderately high C‐rate of 0.25 C, which for our coin
cell system translates to a moderate current of ~1.8 mA (~1.2 mA cm−2).

INTRODUCTION
With the need for batteries with higher specific capacity in
the current state of societal transformation to sustainable
energy supply, the use of pure lithium (Li) metal as active
material in commercial batteries is one of the most desired
targets. It is considered as an ideal anode material because
of the most negative standard potential of Li of −3.02 V
versus standard hydrogen electrode, its high theoretical
specific capacity (3860 mAh g−1), and its low density.1
However, the commercial application of Li metal is greatly
impeded by several problems. First, Li metal is highly
reactive and its reaction with gases and liquids creates a
passivation layer, preventing it from further reaction.
Nevertheless, this passivation layer is not entirely stable
and will be consumed upon galvanostatic cycling.2 In
batteries, Li metal is very prone to form dendrites upon
charging, representing a high safety risk due to possible
short circuit formation. The experience shows that Li metal
is difficult to handle and cannot be combined with many
types of solvents and thus, also electrolytes. From the early
stages of Li‐ion battery (LIB) commercialization in the
1990s, carbonates
have been successfully applied as
electrolyte media.3 These electrolytes were mainly opti-
mized for the specific case of graphite‐based LIBs. However,
the more reactive Li metal anodes quickly exposed the

limits of carbonates being readily consumed to a high
degree for solid electrolyte interface (SEI) formation which
results from the instability of the Li metal passivation layers
in these electrolyte media.4 Solvents with a low oxidation
potential like ethers are rather advisable to use due to the
high anodic reactivity of Li metal.5 A comparison of
carbonate‐ and ether‐based liquid electrolytes by Lui et al.
revealed a superior cycling performance for the ether in
metal Li/Li symmetrical cells as well as a higher capacity
output for Li metal NMC811 cells.6 To ensure a stable
environment for the Li metal, aprotic solvents are necessary
to prevent the formation of highly flammable H2. Besides
the carbonate solvent type, ethers also fulfill this require-
ment. Ether‐based liquid electrolytes find widespread
application and are one of the most used classes of
electrolyte media for Li‐based batteries.7 This is not
surprising as due to the inert behavior of ethers toward the
reaction with alkaline and alkaline earth metals, they are
also widely utilized in organometallic chemistry. The
stabilization of organo‐magnesium compounds in Grignard
reactions is one example that shows the great potential of
this solvent class. Thereby, the metal atom of the organo‐
metal coordinates with the oxygen of the ether, forming an
organo‐metal ether complex.8 Single metal ions can be
stabilized in solution using a crown ether.9 Another
application for the controlled reaction of metal in ether
solvents is the well‐known drying of ethers under inert
conditions.10 In that process, only the remaining moisture
is reacting with the alkaline metal (mostly sodium), while
the metal can safely be stored under inert conditions (argon
atmosphere, excluding oxygen and H2O mainly) in such
ether solvents without being decomposed. Furthermore,
most ether solvents possess a low viscosity and low density,
which
makes
them
adequate
candidates
for
high‐
gravimetric
energy‐density
battery
technologies
like
lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs). Although LSBs promise
high application potential particularly for aviation, they
are facing a number of challenges; mainly, the polysulfide
shuttle effect that is associated with electrolyte propert-
ies.11–14 Hence, for such systems, the utilization of a safe
liquid electrolyte providing favorable interactions with
polysulfides is highly desired. Besides the named advan-
tages for the application of ether‐based electrolytes in Li
metal batteries there are also challenges involved with the
use of this solvent class. The state‐of‐the‐art electrolyte
in LSB is a mixture of 1,3‐dioxolan (DOL) and 1,
2‐dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1, v‐v). Both ethers have a
very high vapor pressure, which is the reason for the quick
evaporation of the electrolyte medium even at room
temperature resulting in an early electrolyte dry‐out of
battery cells.15,16 Combined with their high toxicity further
application becomes more challenging as higher safety
precautions are necessary. These properties are very
common for most ether‐type media. Nevertheless, applica-
ble ethers have been identified that combine the beneficial
properties of a low density, viscosity, and stability against Li
under inert conditions, while at the same time possessing a
very low vapor pressure and lower toxicity. In 2012,
Barchasz et al. demonstrated the great potential of
diethylene glycol diethyl ether (DEGDEE) due to its
improved Li metal passivation, and polyethylene glycol
dimethyl ether (PEGDME) having a high polysulfide
solvation ability and thereby reducing the polysulfide
precipitation leading to a delay of electrode passivation.
In both cases, a high initial specific capacity was observed
which was superior to a mixture of DOL/DME.17 The
electrolytes contained 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesul-
fonyl)imide (LiTFSI) conducting salt which is also present
in the state‐of‐the‐art LSB electrolyte. Another option is the
use of lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) that is also
similar in structure. A comparison of the two conducting
salts in DOL/DME for use in LSB shows better initial
capacity but poor long‐term cycling for LiFSI, making
LiTFSI the preferred choice.18 However, in the case of
higher salt concentrations LiFSI seems to come out on top
regarding cycling performance.19
This work investigates in more detail the application
of LiFSI as conducting salt in DEGDEE and PEGDME,
and introduces a new electrolyte medium, diethylene
glycol methyl ethyl ether (DEGMEE), which has not
been previously reported for the use as electrolyte in LSB.

2.1

Electrolyte preparation
Each electrolyte solution was prepared in a threaded glass
vial. The empty vial and a magnetic stirring bar were
heated to 100°C, kept for 30 min, and directly put into the
vacuum chamber of the glovebox (H2O < 1 ppm; GS
GLOVEBOX Systemtechnik GmbH, Germany). Inside the
glovebox, 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI, 99.95%, Sigma‐Aldrich) or lithium bis(fluorsulfo-
nyl)imide (LiFSI, 98%, BLD Pharmatech GmbH) and 0.25 M
lithium nitrate (LiNO3, ≥98%, Merck KGaA) were dissolved
in 2 mL of electrolyte medium using a magnetic stirring bar
at 500 rpm for 24 h. As electrolyte medium either a mixture
of 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL; 99%, contains ~50 ppm BHT as
inhibitor, Sigma‐Aldrich) and 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME;
≥99%, inhibitor‐free, Sigma‐Aldrich) (1:1, v‐v) or diethylene
glycol methyl ethyl ether (DEGMEE; ≥96% (GC), Sigma‐
Aldrich) or diethylene glycol diethyl ether (DEGDEE; ≥98%
(GC), Sigma‐Aldrich) or polyethylene glycol dimethyl ether
(PEGDME; average mn ~ 250, Sigma‐Aldrich) was used.

Materials characterization
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, Helios G4 CX Dual‐Beam) was performed to
investigate the morphology of the Li anode and S

room
atmosphere

to

SEM holder that opens in high vacuum.20 The S
cathodes were prepared in the same dry room onto
usual SEM holders and put into a box which was then
sealed under argon in an aluminum bag. To maintain a
low exposure to the surrounding atmosphere, the S
cathode samples were opened and quickly put into the
SEM directly after the chamber was opened. The whole
process took less than 1 min.

Electrochemical measurements
Coin cells (GELON, 2032 type) were assembled in an
argon‐atmosphere glovebox (H2O < 1 ppm; GS GLO-
VEBOX Systemtechnik GmbH, Germany), with a Li
metal anode (Li foil, 500 μm thickness, Goodfellow), a
Celgard2500 separator (thickness of 25 μm, Celgard
LLC) and a sulfur‐based electrode which was the same
S cathode as used in our previous research.20 The
electrolyte is described in Section 2.1. was added to
each cell (50 μL) in different manners depending on
the spacer setup. Current spacer setup: electrolyte (five
drops) was distributed onto the S cathode. Afterward,
one layer of the separator was laminated onto the S
cathode, followed by the application of the remaining
electrolyte, then the Li anode which was pressed onto a
0.5 mm spacer and finally the 1 mm spacer. Old spacer
setup: two drops of electrolyte were put onto the Li
anode which was then pressed onto the 0.5 mm spacer,
followed by the
lamination of one
layer of the
separator, then the remaining electrolyte was added.
Afterward, the S cathode and the 1 mm spacer were
placed on top. A detailed scheme of the different spacer
setups is presented in the Supporting Information
section. After cell building, the coin cells were cycled
on a battery cycler (Series 4000, Maccor Inc.) at 20°C in
an environmental chamber (Model KB 720, BINDER
GmbH) between 1.7 and 2.8 V starting with five cycles
of 0.1 C, followed by a continuous C‐rate of 0.25 C.
Only for the preliminary galvanostatic cycling experi-
ment in Section 3.1. A C‐rate test (0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5,
and 1; three cycles each) was conducted after the five
initial cycles of 0.1 C. Electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS; Biologic, VSP300) was performed
within a frequency range of 7 MHz to 100 mHz before
the
cyclic
voltammetry
(CV)
measurements.
CV
(Biologic, VSP300 was carried out at a voltage range
of 1.5–3 V applying different scanning rates of 0.05, 0.2,
0.5, 1, and 2 mV s−1.

3.1

Preliminary galvanostatic cycling
examination
In our past research, we assembled Li metal sulfur coin
cells that utilized a simple standard sulfur electrode
consisting of only conductive carbon, CMC binder, as
well as sulfur (3 mg/cm²), and was produced via a
water‐based
process.
Besides
this
sulfur
cathode
(S cathode), the coin cells consisted of a Li metal anode,
one layer of Celgard2500 separator, and the state‐of‐the‐

LiNO3. This system is highly prone to polysulfide (PS)
shuttling, already giving a poor Coulombic efficiency

cycle number.20 However, a low CE in LSB is not only

might have the most impact on the CE, there are also
reasons like electrolyte consumption over a long galva-
nostatic charge–discharge period as well as side reactions
of the conducting salt and additives with the electrodes
that can have an effect on the CE. Figure 1 depicts the
molecular structure of the applied electrolyte media and
the utilized salts.

rating for DEGMEE, DEGDEE, and PEGDME was
found;
the

existing
classification

for

To achieve a more in‐depth insight, a postmortem
analysis of the preliminary galvanostatic cycling was
elaborated. Supporting Information: Figure S2 shows the Li
anode, separator, and S cathode of one coin cell for each
system. The references (REF_TFSI and REF_FSI) were
completely dry after opening the cell and showed a black
surface on the Li anode and separator; in the case of
REF_TFSI, the black layer was largely detached from the
separator and fell off. This black area is considered to be
“dead Li” formed by Li plating, creating separated Li
particles in the micron regime with a nanometer‐thick SEI
that kinetically hinders the transfer of electrons and thus
oxidation.28,29 A closer look at the black surface of the
separator or Li anode shows brownish and yellowish content
derived from PS shuttling. For all investigated cells with
other electrolyte media, always still wetted cell compart-
ments were found. The beneficial physicochemical propert-
ies of significantly lower vapor pressure are the reason why
these electrolyte media do not partially evaporate during cell
building or diffuse into dead space of the cell during a long
experiment or even upon longer storage. The poor electro-
chemical cycling behavior of the reference DOL/DME
systems can thus also partially derive not only from
electrolyte consumption for SEI‐build up but also from
partial evaporation during cell building and diffusion during
the experiment. DEGMEE_TFSI suffered from Li plating
and the PS shuttle effect, as separator and Li anode showed
black, yellow, and brownish content on the surface. In
contrast, the DEGMEE_FSI seems not to be affected by
“dead Li” plating as no black surface was observed on the Li
anode and separator, but only small amounts of a brownish
residual were spotted. On the separator, the majorly
yellowish layer, which is still liquid, could also stem from
the electrolyte itself, as the LiFSI‐containing electrolytes
always formed a yellowish solution, which clearly is in
contrast to the LiTFSI‐based electrolytes which always
formed a colorless solution. In that sense, it could be
possible that no severe PS shuttle occurred for DEGMEE_F-
SI, explaining the superior CE. A similar result was obtained
for DEGDEE‐based electrolytes. Since this cell ran for more
than 250 cycles at 0.25 C, the outcome of such a high current
can be observed as Li only plated back onto preferred spots
creating this specific pattern. Surprisingly, “dead Li” from Li
plating did not seem to occur as the plated Li was still shiny.
To a small extent, a darker brownish and reddish coloration
is observed, which can be ascribed to PS shuttling or an
incomplete reaction as the more reddish coloration might
stem from S4 radicals.30,31 These findings match with the CE
trend of DEGDEE_FSI, as after ~200 cycles, the CE sharply
decreases and incomplete reactions are more likely. Moving
on, also the comparison of PEGDME‐based electrolytes
showed a more prominent reddish coloration, supporting the
assumption of an incomplete reaction with PEGDME‐based
electrolytes indicated by the CV experiments. PEGDME was
thus declared as insufficient electrolyte medium for our
setup and was not further investigated.

due to the smaller standard deviation, an averagely
higher specific capacity output. Each single cell can be

in Supporting Information: Figure S3A,C. A similar

including the preliminary cycling experiment data. Only
one outlier (REF_TFSI_3) showed significantly lower
capacity and poor cycling behavior. With DOL/DME

DOL/DME, DEGMEE, and DEGDEE
From the previous results, the improved electrochemical
performance of DEGMEE and DEGDEE electrolyte media
was believed to originate from a facilitated PS formation
during
cycling,
followed
by
a
more
homogeneous
distribution of sulfur and PS on the S cathode. A simple
LiPS synthesis to form Li2S6 was conducted to get a better
understanding of the dissolution and formation of PS in
various media. A strong brownish color was already
initially observed for DEGMEE (see Figure 9 initial) which
intensified over time. This coloration is characteristic for
LiPS formation. Finally, a black/brown solution was
obtained after 1 h of reaction for DEGMEE, while in
DEGDEE and DOL/DME, the reaction and thus, the color

DEGDEE always exposed a darker coloration than the

formation allows a more complete and reversible reaction,

sulfur species on the S cathode. The CE is a good quality
indicator for these findings, as it was almost at 100% for

CONCLUSION
Different electrolyte media and salts have been tested for Li
metal sulfur coin cells under long‐term cycling conditions
applying a moderately high current (0.25 C, ~1.2 mA cm−2).
A comparison of the electrolyte salts LiTFSI and LiFSI
revealed better reproducibility and CE for the LiFSI
configuration using conventional DOL/DME electrolyte
media. LiFSI outperformed LiTFSI in DEGMEE and
DEGDEE as well regarding the CE, cycle life, and overall
capacity outcome, except for the initial discharge capacity
which was higher for LiTFSI but quickly experienced a
strong fading for each electrolyte system in the preliminary
cycling experiment. A possible explanation for the superior
cycling behavior of LiFSI‐based electrolytes might be the
formation of a more suitable SEI as well as more favorable Li
stripping and plating processes, as indicated by the revealed
shiny metal surface of Li during postmortem analysis in the
case of the DEGMEE and DEGDEE samples. Moreover,
DEGMEE and DEGDEE electrolytes surpassed the electro-
chemical performance of the reference system DOL/DME
with LiFSI. Thereby, a CE close to 100% for DEGMEE and
DEGDEE was achieved. This high CE implies an effective
mitigation of the PS shuttle. Further improvements in terms
of cycle life (cycles before malfunctioning: DEGMEE ~ 120,
DEGDEE ~ 250, DOL/DME ~ 90) and specific discharge
capacity (in the 75th cycle: DEGMEE ~ 575, DEGDEE ~ 400,
DOL/DME ~ 300 mAh gS
−1) were reached. Under these
experimental conditions, the achieved high CE, moderate
specific capacity, and cycle life for DEGMEE and DEGDEE
need to be pointed out as exceptional, as only a very standard
LSB system being highly prone to PS shuttling and Li
dendrite formation was utilized. In SEM‐EDX‐supported
postmortem analysis, small pointed Li plateaus were only
observed for the DOL/DME Li anode, whereas broader
structures and a more homogeneous surface were obtained
for the DEGMEE and DEGDEE Li anodes, explaining an
inferior cycling stability of the reference. Additionally, a
more homogenous (re)deposition of cycled sulfur was found
on the S cathodes with DEGMEE and DEGDEE electrolyte
media. An adequate dissolution of PS and elevated reaction
kinetics are believed to be the underlying reasons. In
agreement with these results, DEGMEE and DEGDEE
media revealed elevated reaction kinetics toward PS forma-
tion. However, when high currents are used, DEGDEE
apparently does not withstand these conditions as shown by
CV; also for postmortem analysis unused pristine Li and
always a deep red coloration of electrolyte remains was
found for DEGDEE indicating an incomplete reaction being
a reason for the poorer capacity outcome compared to
DEGMEE. The different arrangement of process steps in cell
building and different spacer setups had a severe impact on
reproducibility. Having active cell compartments in between
the spacers significantly improved the reproducibility of
discharge capacity and CE.
All in all, it has been demonstrated that on the same LSB
system changes to the electrolyte medium and conducting
salt highly impacted the cell performance. By the optimum
choice of electrolyte medium, a high CE was achieved even
at a considerably high current density. These findings were
mainly attributed to advanced mechanisms of PS formation,
including subsequent homogenous distribution of S‐related
species on the cathode without clogging the ionic and
electronic pathways and the formation of a suitable SEI on
the Li anode site that mitigates reaction with PS. For future
liquid‐, Li metal‐related LSB research, it is thus advised to
target improved LiPS dissolution using ether‐based electro-
lyte media.
