A B S T R A C T
Herein, we offer two electrochemical models, a zero-dimensional (0-D) and a one-dimensional (1-D) model, to
mechanistically explore the impact of the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio and electrolyte properties on the
discharge behavior of a lithium-sulfur (Li-S) cell. The electrochemically active area in both models is defined
based on the carbon weight fraction and reference porosity, so the influence of the carbon loading and E/S ratio
on the reaction kinetics is included. Mass transfer of the species must be considered to model the reliance of the
discharge capacity on the current density. Instead, the 0-D model is more responsive to the influence of the E/S
ratio on the discharge capacity. The effect of electrolyte properties can be modeled implicitly through model
parameters. The 0-D model is less sensitive to the variance in model parameters than the 1-D model in terms of
the predicted capacity.
1. Introduction
The interest in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries has dramatically
increased in the last couple of decades since developing technologies
such as electric vehicles suffer from the moderate capacities and high
prices of the current battery systems. Li-S batteries are considered to be
one of the most promising technologies for the future because they have
a significantly high theoretical specific energy [1–6]. In addition, sul-
fur’s natural abundance as a cathode active material in Li-S batteries is
expected to make these batteries cheaper than commercial ones. Besides
their cost, Li-S batteries are relatively eco-friendly due to sulfur being
non-toxic. So, Li-S batteries may play a critical role in diminishing
vehicle carbon emissions [7].
Although Li-S batteries are promising candidates for energy storage
in the future, critical challenges must be dealt with before the
commercialization of these batteries. The primary obstacle Li-S batteries
face is limited cycle life as a consequence of irreversible capacity fading.
This is first because of the polysulfide shuttle mechanism, resulting from
the dissolution of polysulfides, which forms due to the complex reaction
mechanism between Li and S in the electrolyte. The polysulfide shuttle
mechanism gives rise to deformation on the anode surface, loss of the
active material in the cathode, and reduced coulombic efficiency
[8–11]. The passivation of the cathode surface due to the accumulation
of insulating S and Li2S and the uncontrolled increase in the Li surface
area depleting the electrolyte in the cell are other mechanisms
contributing to the fast capacity fade of the Li-S battery.
The multi-step electrochemical and dissolution/precipitation re-
actions and polysulfide shuttle mechanism involving soluble poly-
sulfides in the electrolyte lead to electrolyte design being one of the most
crucial factors in improving the Li-S battery performance. Both the
amount and the properties of the electrolyte system control the
discharge capacity, cycle life, and energy density of the battery. The
electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio is a commonly used metric to quantify
the electrolyte amount in the cell. This is a significant design parameter
due to its vast effect on electrochemical performance and energy den-
sity. The E/S ratio must be sufficiently high to ensure complete solvation
of the produced polysulfides and adequate wetting of the cathode to
facilitate the reaction kinetics and, thus, the sulfur utilization [12].
Increasing the E/S ratio typically improves the cell voltage and capacity,
whereas it adversely impacts the energy density of the batteries at the

0-D and 1-D models are compared based on the projected discharge
profiles regarding the C-rate, E/S ratio, and electrolyte properties. With
this analysis, we can understand the most critical factors in modeling the
impact of electrolyte design on the Li-S battery performance.
2. Model development
2.1. Zero-dimensional (0-D) model development
A concentration-independent and time-dependent, zero-dimensional
electrochemical model based on Zhang et al. [32] is developed in this
study. The dissolution reaction of S8(s), which is not taken into account
in Zhang et al. [32], is added to the presented model because defining
the mass fraction of sulfur in the solid matrix is crucial to be able to
control the design parameters such as sulfur loading, carbon-to-sulfur
ratio (C/S), and electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S) in the model. Further-
more, in contrast to the Zhang et al. [32] model, the electrochemically
active area in the cathode is defined based on the carbon mass fraction
and a reference electrolyte volume fraction. By doing so, it is possible to
control the influence of the carbon amount (or the C/S ratio, in other
words) and the initial porosity (or the E/S ratio, in other words) on the
electrochemically active area in the cathode.
There are several assumptions in this model. Firstly, the isothermal
discharge temperature of the Li-S cell is 298 K. Secondly, as there is no
noteworthy effect of the overpotential of the anode and separator on the
cell voltage, they are ignored in this model. Also, at the end of the
discharge, the precipitation of only one polysulfide, Li2S, is assumed to
take place, while the polysulfide shuttle mechanism is not considered.
Thus, five electrochemical reactions and two precipitation/dissolution
reactions of elemental sulfur, S8, and lithium sulfide, Li2S, are assumed
for the discharge of the cell, given in Eqs. (1)–(5) and (6), (7),
respectively.

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 represents the discharge profiles predicted by the two models.
It can be seen in the figure that both models are successful in capturing
the characteristic features of a Li-S cell discharge curve; there are two
discharge plateaus and a voltage dip between them. The first discharge
plateau is at around 2.35V, and the second discharge plateau is at
around 2.2V. Predicted discharge capacities at 0.1C are about 1000 mAh
g-1. As evident in the figure, the 0-D and 1-D models can project almost
the same discharge profile at a given C-rate, indicating that the con-
centration dependence of the species is not critical to capturing the
characteristic discharge behavior.
Fig. 2 illustrates how the discharge profiles change with varying C-
rates for both models. As also discussed in the previous studies in the
literature, predicting the right trend on the effect of current density on
the discharge performance is not trivial. This is apparent in the figure;
the 0-D and 1-D models calculate different behavior. For instance, the C-
rate does not affect the discharge capacity for the 0-D model, whereas
increasing the C-rate leads to a decrease in the discharge capacity for the
1-D model. This reduction in the discharge capacity becomes significant,
especially at higher current densities. The predicted discharge capacity
not being responsive to the C-rate at low C-rates is not entirely reason-
able; however, this is well-known for the electrochemical models based
on Kumaresan et al. [27,33]. Yet, the mass transport of the species must
be considered to model the dependence of the discharge capacity of a
Li-S cell on the current density.
Even though the concentration-independent model cannot predict
the change in the discharge capacity with changing C-rate, both models
successfully project the decrease in the voltage of the plateaus (espe-
cially for the second plateau) as current density increases, as expected
from the experimental trends [34–39]. This is anticipated since both
models consider the reaction kinetics in a similar manner. On the other
hand, the voltage decrease with increasing C-rate in the 0-D model is
higher than that of the 1-D case; this can be stemmed from the ohmic
overpotential explicitly included in the 0-D model (Table S1). For
instance, the voltage of the 2nd discharge plateau of the 1-D model is at
2.1V, whereas it is at 1.95V for the 0-D model at a C-rate of 4C.
Fig. 3 presents the change in the calculated discharge capacities with
the E/S ratio. As discussed above, in both models, we modified the
definition of the electrochemically active area to account for the influ-
ence of the initial porosity, which depends on the given E/S ratio, on the
reaction kinetics. With this alteration, we can account for the improved
cathode wetting at higher E/S ratios (Fig. S1). As seen in Fig. 3, elevated
E/S ratios lead to higher discharge capacities and voltages, as expected
from the experimental trends [7,15,40,41]. Low E/S ratios result in high
overpotentials and low active material utilization due to hindered
cathode kinetics [11,40,42]. Both models successfully capture this
anticipated behavior. Yet, the change in capacity with changing elec-
trolyte amounts is more evident in the 0-D model than in the 1-D model.
Surprisingly, by neglecting the mass transfer of the species and thus the
concentration change within the cathode, the predicted discharge per-
formance becomes more sensitive to the E/S ratio. For instance, the 0-D
model projects a significant decrease in the capacities of both plateaus
with a decreasing E/S ratio, while the upper voltage plateau capacity

Our previous discussions clearly show that the models can directly
express the impact of the E/S ratio on the discharge profile. On the other
hand, modeling the influence of the electrolyte type and properties is not
straightforward. A reasonable approach would be to capture it implicitly
through the model parameters. Redox chemistry of the Li-S batteries is
affected by the physiochemical properties of the electrolyte, such as the
donor number, dielectric constant, and acceptor number, which are
related to the solubility and precipitation/dissolution of the species
forming in the electrochemical reactions [39]. Since the electrolyte has a
crucial impact on the reaction mechanisms and kinetics, one way to
model this dependence would be through the exchange current densities
[43]. Figs. 4 and 5 display the dependence of the calculated discharge
profiles on the exchange current densities for the 0-D and 1-D models,
respectively. We observe similar trends in both figures; none of the ex-
change current densities alter the discharge capacity for both models.
When the first reaction exchange current density, i01, is lowered, the
upper discharge plateau slightly shifts downward in the 0-D model. On
the other hand, i01, i02, and i03 do not affect the discharge profile of the
1-D model in Fig. 5a, b, and c, respectively. In the 0-D model, lowering
i03 leads to a slightly elevated capacity of the lower voltage plateau.
However, these changes are subtle. The exchange current densities for
the fourth and the fifth reactions lead to the most prominent change in
the discharge profiles; a decrease in i04 and i05 in both models results in a
downward shift in the second discharge plateau as higher overpotentials
are required at hindered kinetics. Increasing none of these kinetic pa-
rameters results in a change in the predicted performance. To sum up,
the presented models cannot project the impact of the electrolyte type
and properties on the discharge capacity through the kinetic model
parameters.

The sensitivity analysis results on these model parameters are pre-
sented in Figs. 6 and 7 for the 0-D and 1-D models. An increase in kLi2S
leads to an upward shift in the second discharge plateau for both models
while decreasing kLi2S results in an opposite effect in Figs. 6a and 7a; yet,
this parameter does not impact the predicted discharge capacity for both
models. In contrast, the 0-D and 1-D models predict opposite trends for
the sulfur precipitation rate constant in Figs. 6b and 7b, respectively.
Increasing kS8 doesn’t lead to a capacity change in the 1-D model, while
it leads to a decrease in the capacity in the 0-D model. Oppositely,
decreasing kS8 in the 1-D model results in a capacity reduction, while it
does not affect the capacity in the 0-D model. This is rather surprising as
lower S80 concentrations and, thus, lower first discharge plateau ca-
pacities are expected at worse sulfur dissolution kinetics. Moving on to
the solubility products, increasing KspLi2S reduces the second plateau
voltage for the 0-D model in Fig.6c, whereas it does not lead to any
voltage and capacity change for the 1-D model in Fig. 7c. Lowering KspS8
for the 0-D model reduces the discharge capacity slightly in Fig.6d; on
the other hand, a decrease in KspS8 significantly lowers the discharge
capacity for the 1-D model in Fig. 7d. To summarize, the 0-D model is
less sensitive to the parameters related to the precipitation kinetics, and
the discharge capacity can only be adjusted through the S8 precipitation
rate constant. On the other hand, the impact of the electrolyte type on
the discharge capacity can be controlled through the S8 precipitation
rate, both through kS8 and KspS8, for the 1-D model.
The next parameter to consider to model the influence of the elec-
trolyte on the discharge performance would be the electrolyte conduc-
tivity, σ. As seen in Fig. 6e, the electrolyte conductivity only impacts the
calculated cell voltage and does not lead to a capacity change for the 0-D
model. The whole discharge profile shifts downward with decreasing σ,
which may be explained by the direct impact of the conductivity on the
Ohmic overpotential through the electrolyte resistance in the cell
voltage calculation for the 0-D model (Table S1).
The impact of diffusion coefficients is also investigated for the 1-D
model in Fig. 7e, as the electrolyte type and properties have a crucial
influence on mass transport. As shown in the figure, increasing the
diffusion coefficients doesn’t lead to any significant change, whereas
decreasing the diffusion coefficients results in a prominent reduction in
the discharge capacity. Up to 0.01-fold, the first plateau capacity doesn’t
change; however, further decrease also leads to a decline in the first
plateau capacity. Consequently, an apparent advantage of the 1-D model
is controlling the discharge capacities through the diffusion coefficients
of the species.
To sum up, the 1-D model shows superiority over the 0-D model as
there are more model parameters to be controlled for capturing the in-
fluence of electrolyte properties on the discharge capacity, in addition to
the fact that it projects the impact of the C-rate more accurately. How-
ever, as discussed above, the predicted discharge capacities vary less
significantly with changing E/S ratios in the 1-D model. In fact, the
degree of the dependence of the capacity on the electrolyte amount also
differs with the electrolyte type. Since the model projections are highly
responsive to the changes in kS8, KspS8, and Di0, we investigated the
change of the voltage profile with E/S ratio for different values of these
model parameters for the 1-D model. Figs. S2–S4 show that the degree of
sensitivity of the discharge capacities on the E/S ratio can be controlled
in the 1-D model by altering kS8, KspS8, or Di0. For lower assumed values
of these parameters, the predicted discharge capacities decrease more
significantly with decreasing E/S ratios.

4. Conclusions
Two electrochemical models, a zero-dimensional and a one-
dimensional model, were proposed to mechanistically investigate the
influence of the electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio and electrolyte properties on
the discharge performance of a Li-S battery. Both models include five
electrochemical and two dissolution/precipitation reactions. In the 0-D
model, the mass transfer of species and, thus, the concentration variance
within the cell is ignored, while the 1-D model accounts for the transport
phenomena and calculates the change of concentrations concerning
both time and space. Both models project the electrochemically active
area as a function of carbon weight fraction and reference porosity in the
cathode. With this modification, the change in the area and, thus,
cathode kinetics with changing carbon loading and E/S ratio are
considered. The 0-D and 1-D models are compared based on the pre-
dicted discharge profiles as a function of the C-rate, E/S ratio, and
electrolyte properties. Here are the main conclusions:
• The 0-D and 1-D models predict very similar discharge profiles at a
given C-rate, indicating that the concentration dependence of the
species is not essential to capture the characteristic discharge profile
of a Li-S cell.
• Both models successfully predict the decrease in the plateau voltages
(specifically the second plateau) as the C-rate increases. However,
the discharge capacity is only affected by the C-rate in the 1-D model
and not in the 0-D model, indicating that considering transport
phenomena is crucial to predicting the impact of current density on
the capacity.
• Both models effectively capture the fact that both the cell voltage and
capacity are improved with increasing E/S ratio. However, the pre-
dicted discharge capacities vary more significantly with changing E/
S ratios in the 0-D model.
• The influence of the electrolyte type and properties on the discharge

the discharge capacities.
