ABSTRACT: Sulfur-based redox materials are promising next-generation energy
storage solutions. Identifying electrode and electrolyte properties that facilitate
polysulﬁde reduction reactions is critical for rational material designs for sulfur-
based batteries. In this study, we reveal that the eﬀectiveness of the polysulﬁde
reduction is governed by the resolved binding strength of polysulﬁde on the
electrode surface, which is dictated by the competition between electrode’s
polysulﬁde chemisorption strength and solvent’s polysulﬁde solvation strength.
Using titanium-based model compounds (TiX) as examples, we show that the
polysulﬁde reduction kinetics and sulfur utilization increase with increasing
polysulﬁde chemisorption strength of TiX, which can be associated with the
decreasing electronegativity of nonmetal element (X). Strong coordinating solvent
reduces catalyst’s eﬃcacy by reducing the binding strength between polysulﬁde and
the catalysts, highlighting that a weak solvent coordination is a critical selection
criterion for eﬀective catalysis in Li−S batteries. Our study reveals physical origins controlling the catalytic processes of
polysulﬁde reduction reactions and unravels the interplay of solvent−polysulﬁde−catalyst competition for achieving higher-
energy and reversible sulfur-based energy storage.
1. INTRODUCTION
Sulfur-based redox chemical reactions are promising candi-
dates for next-generation energy storage systems owing to the
natural abundance, high theoretical capacity, low cost, and
environmental friendliness of sulfur.1−4 The energy eﬃciency
and utilization of sulfur-based redox chemistry highly depend
on the activity of polysulﬁde reduction to metal sulﬁde (or
polysulﬁde conversion).5,6
Intensive eﬀorts have been devoted to catalyst development
for polysulﬁde reduction to solid product metal sulﬁde.5,7−9
For instance, noble or transition metals (e.g., Pt10 or Co11),
metal nitrides (e.g., VN12,13 and TiN14,15), and borides (e.g.,
MgB2
16 and TiB2
17) were exploited to catalyze polysulﬁde
conversion reactions owing to their strong chemical bonding
with polysulﬁde. Additionally, surface defects like edges or
dangling bonds of polar host materials were also found to serve
as the catalytic center in some oxides (e.g., TinO2n−1,18,19
WO3−x
20), sulﬁdes (e.g., WS2,21 MoS2,
21−23 and Co3S4
24), and
phosphorus,25 triggering recent research eﬀorts in tuning the
aspect ratio or creating defects to increase the catalytically
active sites. Tao et al.9 suggested that a balance of strong
adsorption ability and low diﬀusion barrier gave optimum host
materials. Liang et al.26 proposed that the eﬃcacy of metal
oxides in binding polysulﬁde was based on the formation of
insoluble thiosulfate, which could be dictated by the redox
potential. Peng and co-workers27,28 suggested that polarity and
conductivity were important criteria for eﬀective host materials
that provided high polysulﬁde conversion activities. Recently,
Zhou et al.29 indicated the importance of p-bands in cobalt-
based compounds for the charge transfer reaction in Li−S
batteries.
Identifying electrode and electrolyte properties that dictate
the eﬀectiveness of polysulﬁde reduction to metal sulﬁde will
yield critical insights into materials design for sulfur-based
batteries. In this study, we show that transition-metal
compounds’ (MX)s’ polysulﬁde adsorption strength and
polysulﬁde reduction activity increase as we decrease the
electronegativity of nonmetal element X.30,31 Using titanium
(Ti)-based model compounds (i.e., TiX, X = B, C, and O) as
examples, we show that TiB2 exhibits the highest polysulﬁde
reduction activity followed by TiC and TiO2, which directly
correlates to the chemisorption strength revealed by UV−vis
spectroscopy (TiB2 > TiC > TiO2). This observation can be
explained by the enhanced reactivity between metals (M,
electron acceptors) and S-containing adsorbates (electron
donors) when the electronegativity of the nonmetal (X) is
reduced (Figure 1a). In addition, we show that the
eﬀectiveness of the polysulﬁde conversion is governed by the

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Material Preparation. All chemicals were used as received.
Lithium sulﬁde (Li2S, 99.98%), sulfur (S8, >99.5%), bis-
(trifuromethane) sulfonamide lithium salt (LiTFSI, 99.95% trace
metal basis), lithium perchlorate (LiClO4, 99.99%), and lithium
nitrite (LiNO3, 99.99%), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous,
99.5%), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO,
99.9%), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99.5%), and tetraethylene
glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME, ≥99.0%) were all received from
Sigma-Aldrich and stored in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (Etelux, <1.0 ppm
of H2O and <1.0 ppm of O2). Li2S, LiTFSI, and LiNO3 were dried
under vacuum overnight at 110 °C in a glass oven (Büchi,
Switzerland), respectively. Sulfur was dried at 75 °C for 1 day before
transferring into the glovebox. Solvents (DME, DOL, DMSO, and
TEGDME) were stored with 4 Å molecular sieves (Sigma-Aldrich)
for more than 1 week before use, and the water content was
determined by Karl Fischer titration (TitroLine 7500 KF, SI
Analytics) to be less than 20 ppm. Lithium foil (16 mm in diameter,
1.58 mm in thickness) was received from Shenzhen Meisen
Electromechanical Co. Ltd. Poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF,
HSV900) binder was received from Taiyuan Lizhiyuan Batteries
Co. Ltd., China. Titanium diboride nanopowder (TiB2, >95%, 58 nm,
hexagonal), titanium carbide nanopowder (TiC, >99%, 40−60 nm,
cubic), and titanium oxide nanopowder (TiO2, anatase, 99.9%, 18
nm) were received from US Research Nanomaterials, Inc. and dried
under vacuum at 80 °C before use. Celgard 2325 (Φ19 mm, LLC
Corp.) was dried under vacuum at 70 °C overnight before use. Quartz
ﬁber (Φ19 mm, QM-A, Whatman) was dried under vacuum at 110
°C for one day before use.

2.3. Computational Methods. All density functional theory
(DFT)-based ﬁrst-principles calculations were carried out by
ABINIT32,33 code. The exchange−correlation functional was dealt
with by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew−
Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) type.34 The electron−ion interactions were
coped with by projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials.35 The
plane wave energy cutoﬀwas set to be 24 Ha to ensure a satisﬁed
convergence, and the spacing between k-point grids was all set to be
less than 0.05 Å−1. Ti-terminated TiB2(001) surface, Ti-terminated
TiC(111) surface, and the most stable TiO2(101) surface were
chosen as the slab models based on the XRD and XPS results. The
bottom two atomic layers were ﬁxed, and others were fully relaxed for
all the slab models. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was added in all of the
slab models to avoid the image−image interaction. The force
convergence criterion for self-consistent-ﬁeld cycles was 4.0 × 10−5
Ha/Bohr and that for structural relaxation was 6 × 10−4 Ha/Bohr.

2.4. Electrochemical Measurements. The bulk sulfur cathode
was prepared by employing S-Vulcan XC-72 carbon (Cobot co.,
USA) composite/Super P (MTI Co., Ltd.)/PVDF = 80:10:10 in
NMP and drop-casted onto carbon paper (HCP010N, Shanghai

carbon = 7:3 and sonicating in DME for 30 min, and then, it was
heated at 155 °C overnight for the inﬁltration of liquid sulfur into the
pores of Vulcan carbon. The weight ratio of sulfur was measured by
thermal gravity analysis (PerkinElmer STA 6000) before use. The
temperature was controlled from room temperature to 550 °C at 10
°C min−1. The chemical composition of S/Vulcan carbon composite
was determined to be 71.1 wt % sulfur and 28.9 wt % carbon. The
electrodes were dried under 60 °C overnight to remove NMP. The
sulfur loading for routine Li−S batteries was controlled to 1.1 mgS
cm−2 on Φ16 mm carbon paper. Titanium-based compound-

(X = B, C, and O)/Super P/PVDF = 8:1:1 in NMP onto carbon
paper (Φ16 mm × (0.1 ± 0.01) mm) and dried at 60 °C overnight to
evaporate the solvent. The total surface areas of the catalysts per piece
were ﬁxed at 0.1 m2 by controling the weight based on the measured
BET surface area (Table S1).
2.4.1. One-Compartment Li−S Batteries. CR2025 coin cells were
used for Li−S batteries with both electrolytes (i.e., 1.0 M LiTFSI and
0.4 M LiNO3 in DOL/DME and DMSO, respectively). In a cell with
DOL/DME as the electrolyte, one layer of Celgard 2325 was used as
the separator and a piece of lithium foil was used as the anode. The
galvanostatic test of the routine loading cell with the electrolyte/sulfur
(E/S) ratio of 25 mL g−1 was conducted with the voltage cutoﬀof 1.7
V for discharge and 3.0 V for charge processes. Routine cell
conﬁguration was also used for cyclic voltammetry tests. The voltage
range was 1.7−3.0 V, and the scanning rate was set to 0.1 mV s−1.
Potentiostatic deposition of polysulﬁde to Li2S from 20 μL of 0.5 M
Li2S8 catholyte was conducted with stepping the potential of the cell
to certain potentials (2.09 and 2.11 VLi) from the open-circuit voltage
(OCV) and the cutoﬀof the current was set to 10 μA on Bio-Logic
VMP3. Catholyte with 0.5 M Li2S8 were made by dissolving
stoichiometric Li2S/S = 1:7 into DOL/DME under vigorously
stirring at the controlled temperature of 25 °C till no solids can be
observed. In a cell with DMSO as the electrolyte, one piece of quartz
ﬁber was used as the separator instead of Celgard due to the wetting
problem. Lithium foil was pretreated with propylene carbonate (PC,
anhydrous, 99.7%, Sigma-Aldrich) to avoid quick reaction of DMSO
and lithium. Electrolyte (80 μL) was utilized to keep good wetting
between each layer. All galvanostatic tests were conducted on the
battery testing system (LAND, CT2001A, Wuhan LAND electronics
Co., Ltd.) and rested for 2 h before tests.
2.4.2. Two-Compartment Li−S Batteries. Two-compartment cells
were assembled inside the glovebox with the two compartments
separated by a polymer-sealed NASICON-type structured
Li1.5Al0.5Ge1.5 (PO4)3 (LAGP, Φ19 mm × 300 μm). A lithium foil
was used as the anode. One layer of Celgard was applied to prevent
the direct contact between the lithium anode and LAGP. TEGDME
(40 μL) with 1.0 M LiTFSI and 0.4 M LiNO3 was added as the
electrolyte in the anode compartment. The potentiostatic intermittent
titration technique (PITT) tests during the discharge process were
conducted with a bulk sulfur electrode (1.1 mgS cm−2) as a cathode
and a functional interlayer with TiB2, TiC, and TiO2 with the same
total surface area (0.1 m2). Voltage step was set to 10 mV from OCV
to 1.8 V, and the reduction current cutoﬀfor a given step was 20 μA
(C/100) on Bio-Logic VMP3.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Inﬂuence of the Nonmetal Element on the
Polysulﬁde Adsorption Strength. The polysulﬁde adsorp-
tion strength increases with decreasing electronegativity of the
nonmetal element (X) of the titanium-based compound (TiX)
(Figure 1a). Figure 1b,c shows the UV−vis spectra of 1.0 mM
Li2S8 electrolytes before and after soaking each of the three Ti-
based catalysts with the same equivalent surface area (i.e., TiX,
X = B, C, and O, calculated from the BET surface area in
Figure S1 and Table S1) in DOL/DME and DMSO. We
control the total surface area of these compounds instead of
their mass loading since the surface area is the major scaling
factor for chemisorption.36 First, in both solvents, TiB2 exhibits
the strongest polysulﬁde adsorption ability followed by TiC
and TiO2, as evidenced from the reduction in UV−vis
absorbance compared to that of the blank. These observations
show that reducing the electronegativity of the nonmetal
facilitates polysulﬁde adsorption. Second, the degree of
polysulﬁde adsorption by the Ti-based compounds is much

We exploited X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to
examine the changes in surface chemistry of TiB2 and TiO2 as
two extreme examples after polysulﬁde adsorption. Figure 1d,e
shows the evolution of Ti 2p XPS of TiB2 and TiO2 before

polysulﬁde from the catalysts before XPS measurements. For
TiO2 (Figure 1d), no major change can be identiﬁed after

chemisorption of polysulﬁde on the TiO2 surface. In contrast,
the surface chemistry of TiB2 changed signiﬁcantly compared
to its pristine state (Figure 1e). Pristine TiB2 exhibits two sets
of peaks in the Ti 2p spectrum with a characteristic energy
separation of ∼5.7 eV due to Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 spin−orbit
levels,37 representing the Ti−B bond (454.9 eV) and Ti−O
bond (459.3 eV, due to partial surface oxidation), respec-
tively.38,39 After soaking in polysulﬁde in both solvents, an
additional peak located at 455.8 eV appeared, which can be
attributed to the Ti−S bond (e.g., TiSx).40 The formation of
the Ti−S bond as well as the divergence of the original Ti−B
bond suggest that the chemisorption of polysulﬁde cleaves Ti−
X bonds. This conﬁrms the stronger chemical reactivity of TiB2
toward polysulﬁde than that of TiO2. These observations
support our hypothesis that lowering the electronegativity of
the nonmetal (X) in the TiX facilitates the chemisorption of
polysulﬁde on TiX. We further exploited S L-edge XANES to
examine the changes in the electronic structure of sulfur after
polysulﬁde adsorption onto the Ti-based compounds (Figure
S3). The result conﬁrms that TiB2 exhibits the strongest
polysulﬁde adsorption ability and TiO2 has the weakest.
One major factor aﬀecting the order of polysulﬁde
adsorption strength of the TiX model compounds can be
attributed to the diﬀerence in the electronegativity of the
nonmetal element (X) and the resulting diﬀerences in ionicity/
covalency, which aﬀect the chemical reactivity toward
polysulﬁde. Mizuno and co-workers exploited ﬁrst-principles
molecular orbital calculations to show that lowering the
electronegativity of nonmetal (X) increases the covalency of
the metal compounds (MX) regardless of diﬀerent crystal
structures and local coordinations.30 They showed that the
total covalent bond density simply increases with decreasing
electronegativity diﬀerences between metal and nonmetal
elements despite diﬀerent crystal structures and local
coordinations of the TiX.30 In our study, this aspect can be

the reaction rate per surface atom of the catalyst could vary
when the particle size going below 5 nm42) or exposed

(20−40 nm from XRD, SEM, and TEM characterizations,

size eﬀect.
To further identify the anchoring behavior of TiX to Li2S8,
the ﬁrst-principles study is then conducted. Here, the Ti-
terminated TiB2(001) surface, Ti-terminated TiC(111) sur-
face, and the most stable TiO2(101) surface, as shown in
Figure S6, are chosen as the representative slab models based
on the experimental XRD (Figure S5) and XPS results (Figure
1d,e). The most stable structures of Li2S8 adsorbed on TiX
substrates are exhibited in Figure 1f. It is found that Li2S8
adsorbs on the Ti-terminated TiB2(001) and TiC(111)
surfaces from the formation of the Ti−S bond, while adsorbs
on the TiO2(101) surface from the formation of the O−Li
bond, which is consistent with the strong Ti−S peak observed
in the XPS tests for TiB2 and TiC samples but no obvious peak
for TiO2. It is also found that the Li2S8 decomposes with no
energy barrier on both TiB2(001) and TiC(111) surfaces to
single S atoms bonded with exposed Ti surface as the breakup
of the cyclic Li2S8 structure and small LiSx (x < 4) molecules
was observed, while keeps its initial structure on TiO2(101)
surface. These observations indicate the high polysulﬁde
adsorption ability (Figure 1) as well as high catalytic eﬀect
to promote the conversion of longer-chain polysulﬁde to Li2S
(Section 3.2) of TiB2 and TiC owing to this non-
electrochemical interaction between TiX and polysulﬁde. The
calculated adsorption energies for Li2S8 on TiB2(001),
TiC(111), and TiO2(101) are −28.78, −24.14, and −1.73
eV, respectively. We note that these values are several times
higher than Li2S8−solvent interaction (e.g., the binding energy
of Li2S8 with DMSO is only −0.96 eV, Figure S7), which
suggests that the solvent environment would not inﬂuence the
trend of the adsorption energy. The larger adsorption energies
on TiB2(001) than those on TiC(111) can be ascribed to the
higher electronegativity of C (2.55) than that of B (2.04), as
evidenced by the lower average Bader charge on the top Ti
surface for TiB2(001) (0.81|e|) than that for TiC(111) (1.03|
e|).
3.2. Correlation between Polysulﬁde Adsorption
Strength and Polysulﬁde Reduction. The polysulﬁde
reduction reaction is facilitated with increasing polysulﬁde
adsorption strength. Figure 2a,b shows the ﬁrst galvanostatic
charge/discharge voltage proﬁles of Li−S batteries using a
carbon interlayer decorated with various Ti-based compounds
in DOL/DME and DMSO, respectively. Diﬀerential capacity
curves (dQ/dV, Figure 2c,d) were evaluated by numerical

3.4. Evolution of B K-Edge XANES of the TiB2 during
the 1st Cycle of Li−S Batteries. To further understand the
evolution of the local structure and chemical state of nonmetal
elements (e.g., boron) at diﬀerent stages of Li−S batteries,
synchrotron-based B K-edge XANES was conducted on the
TiB2 catalyst at various discharge and charge stages, as shown
in Figure 4. Figure 4b,c shows the changes of B K-edge of the
TiB2 catalyst during the 1st galvanostatic discharge/charge
cycle in DOL/DME (voltage proﬁle in Figure 4a) under total
electron yield (TEY, Figure 4b) and ﬂuorescent yield (FY,
Figure 4c) modes. Spectra collected under TEY mode provide
surface information with a detection depth of ∼10 nm, while
FY mode could probe deeper changes with a depth of ∼70 nm.
During the discharge process from 2.35 to 1.8 V, all of the
signals related to the original reduced B in the TiB2 structure
decreased (i.e., edge jump at ∼187.5 eV and sharp peak at
192.4 eV; standard reference spectra in Figure S16)
accompanied by the increase of threefold-coordinated oxidized
B (3c-B, a sharp peak at ∼194 eV and a broad peak centered at
203−204 eV; standard reference spectra in Figure S16).70−73
Upon charge, the changes were reversed (Figure 4b,c). Figure
S17 shows the evolution of the relative contents of reduced B
and oxidized B (3c-B) during electrochemical operation in
DMSO, in which a similar reversible evolution process was
observed (Figure S18). This shows that TiB2 is oxidized upon
discharge and reversibly recovered upon charging, demonstrat-
ing that the TiB2 catalyst is oxidized when bonded to
polysulﬁde and recovered upon polysulﬁde desorption.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we revealed that the eﬀectiveness of the
polysulﬁde conversion is governed by the resolved binding
strength of polysulﬁde on the electrode surface, which is
dictated by the competition between electrode’s polysulﬁde
chemisorption strength and solvent’s polysulﬁde solvation
strength. Using titanium-based model compounds as examples
(TiX), we show that TiX’s polysulﬁde adsorption strength
increases with decreasing electronegativity of the nonmetal
element (X) on the order of TiB2 > TiC > TiO2 via UV−vis
spectroscopy and demonstrate that the polysulﬁde reduction
kinetics (via PITT) and sulfur utilization increase with the
increasing polysulﬁde chemisorption strength of the catalyst.
We further revealed that strong coordinating solvents (e.g.,
DMSO) reduce catalyst’s eﬃcacy by reducing the binding
strength between polysulﬁde and catalysts owing to stronger
solvation, highlighting the importance of reducing solvent
solvating strength to improve the eﬃcacy of the catalyst. Our
study reveals the key factors dictating the eﬀectiveness of
polysulﬁde reduction and unravels the interplay of solvent−
polysulﬁde−catalyst competition, providing eﬀective design
criteria for rational development of catalysts for sulfur-based
energy storage technologies.
