Multi-Eﬀect Ionic Liquid Additives Achieve High Cycle
Stability Lithium-Sulfur Batteries by Constructing an
Electrostatic Shielding Layer and Eliminating ‘Dead Sulfur’
Yong Jiang, Yalan Liao, Jiaqi Yu, Xue Li, Tianyu Jin, Yi Xu, Wenrong Li,*
Shoushuang Huang, Shuixin Xia,* Bing Zhao,* Xueliang Sun, and Jiujun Zhang
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered the most promising alternative for
energy storage, however, their practical applications are still limited by lithium
dendrites growth, slow lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) conversion kinetics, shuttle
eﬀect, and deposition of “dead sulfur” at Li anode surface. Herein, a novel ionic
liquid tetrabutylammonium triiodide (TBAI3) is adopted as a multi-eﬀect elec-
trolyte additive to solve low coulombic eﬃciency and short life issues of Li-S bat-
teries. A series of in situ characterization technologies, theoretical calculations,
potentiostatic Li2S deposition experiments, and diﬀerent kinds of symmetric
and asymmetric cells are conducted to reveal the multifunctional electrochem-
ical work mechanism. It is found that the TBA+ cations can coordinate with
solvent molecules, reduce desolvation barrier, and accelerate Li+ transport ki-
netics; they can also form a dynamic electrostatic shielding layer at the lithium
protrusions and induce uniform lithium deposition. The I3
−/I−redox pairs
continuously eliminate “dead sulfur” by transforming Li2S deposits into soluble
LiPSs and release active substances during cycling, while the reduzate I−can
be electrochemically rejuvenated into I3
−when charged to 2.89 V. Therefore,
Li-S batteries with TBAI3 additives exhibit a ultra-long cycle performance of
503 mAh g−1 at 2 C after 1000 cycles with an average coulombic eﬃciency
of 99.99%.

Based on the “solid-liquid-solid” conversion of Li-S batteries,
which is from solid S element to soluble LiPSs, and then to
solid Li2S/Li2S2. Among them, the conversion of solid-liquid and
liquid-liquid is relatively an easy way, but the subsequent liquid-
solid reaction process needs to overcome a very large energy bar-

ent electronic insulation and insoluble shortcomings of Li2S, re-

sulfur” formation.[10]

can be revealed by redox mediator (RM).[11] RMs are a series
of molecules, ions, polymers or compounds that can be re-
versibly oxidized and reduced in an electrochemical cycle. They
act as electron-hole transfer agents without changing the ﬁnal
product.[12] Lai et al.[13] constructed RM of phloroglucinol-2,6-
diaminoanthraquinone (PG-DAAQ) under-going a two-electron
redox reaction to reduce overpotential and improve sulfur uti-
lization, and form an inorganic rich SEI on the Li anode
surface. Sun et al.[14] proposed an activated cisplatin (cis-Pt) as a
mediator to improve the sulfur reduction reaction kinetics. Ex-
tensive research has been dedicated to introduce RMs includ-
ing cathode host,[15] separator modiﬁcation,[16] and electrolyte
additive.[17] As the most simple and economical strategy, elec-
trolyte additive has made a major breakthrough in solving the
problem of Li−S batteries system.[18] The addition of electrolyte
additives could improve the redox kinetics of sulfur species,
while provide anode engineering to shield lithium anodes and
inhibit the generation of lithium dendrites.[19] Among various
electrolyte additives/solvents, ionic liquids (ILs) have excellent
stability in terms of chemicals, electrochemical properties, non-
ﬂammability, low vapor pressure, and excellent ionic conduc-
tivity, which are capable of enabling batteries to exhibit high
coulombic eﬃciency and excellent cycle stability.[20] In particu-
lar, the structural diversity and adjustability of ILs allow for ﬁne
molecular design and further dual-eﬀect solution to the issues
of both positive and negative electrodes.[21] As a trace additive, it
can also regulate the solvation structure of lithium ions, which
can be used to overcome the inherent challenges associated with
Li metal anodes.
Herein, we develop a multi-eﬀect system of cyclic removal of
“dead sulfur” and Li anode interface protection by using tetra-
butyltriiodonium triiodide (TBAI3) as an additive in a standard
LiTFSI/(DOL+DME) electrolyte solution to achieve eﬃcient and
durable Li-S batteries. TBAI3 is an ionic liquid, and its molecu-
lar formula is C16H36I3N. The organic pyrrolidine cation (TBA+)
can self-assemble into a protective layer on the Li anode by elec-
trostatic shielding eﬀect. As shown in Figure 1a, it can eﬀectively
inhibit the dendritic tip eﬀect and the growth of lithium den-
drites. Moreover, the long fat chain has a sulfur-repellent eﬀect,
which inhibits the reaction of polysulﬁdes and other oxidizing
substances with Li metal. The positively charged cations form
a competitive solvation structure, which reduces the interaction
between Li+ and DOL/DME solvent molecules, reduces the de-
solvation barrier of lithium ions, and accelerates the kinetics of
Li+ transport. At the same time, as shown in Figure 1b, I3
−re-
acts with the Li2S2/Li2S deposition at the Li anode surface to
form soluble LiPSs, while the reduzate I−can be electrochemi-
cally recovered into I3
−when charged to 2.89 V. The I3
−/I−redox
pair acts as a redox mediator to eliminate “dead sulfur”, acceler-
ating the conversion kinetics of the sulfur cathode and the recov-
ery rate of the active sulfur in the battery, resulting in prolonged
battery cycle performance. Through in situ optical microscopy
and Raman spectroscopy, combined with density functional the-
ory (DFT) calculations and ﬁnite element simulation (FES), it is
clearly revealed that the electrostatic shielding eﬀect of TBA+ and
redox-mediator (I3
−) are beneﬁcial to improve the utilization of
insoluble Li2S species and weaken the shuttle eﬀect of polysul-
ﬁdes, signiﬁcantly reducing the accumulation of “dead sulfur” on
the Li anode side. Beneﬁting from the dendrite-free Li deposition
behavior, the symmetrical Li battery can perform a stable cycle of
up to 3400 h at a current density of 1 mA cm−2. The Li-S battery
exhibits outstanding electrochemical performance at 2 C with a
high reversible capacity of 503 mAh g−1 and an average coulom-
bic eﬃciency of 99.99% after 1000 cycles, which provides strong
evidence for the key role of ionic liquid TBAI3 in the practical
application of Li-S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
First, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) were conducted to reveal
the reduction stability of TBAI3 at the interface of Li anode.
As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the inﬂection
point at −3.56 V (vs SHE) is considered the reduction potential
of TBA+, which is signiﬁcantly lower than the reduction poten-
tial of lithium (−3.04 V, vs SHE), suggesting the electrochemical
stability of TBA+ in Li-S battery system. In contrast, the majority
of the previously reported cations with high reduction potential
are readily reduced during lithium deposition, which leads to the
failure of long-term cycle stability.[22] DFT was carried out to re-
veal the relationship between the special chemical structure of
TBA+ and the reduction potential. Figure S2a (Supporting Infor-
mation) presents the distribution of the molecular electrostatic
polarity (ESP), with the red region indicating the attraction po-
tential to electrons, and the blue region indicating the repulsion
potential. Based on the electron distribution states of diﬀerent
atoms on TBA+ (Figure S2b and Table S1, Supporting Informa-
tion), the center N atom has a very low potential that it does not
readily gain electrons to be reduced, thereby reducing the total
molecule’s reduction potential.[23]
Subsequently, the morphology of Li anodes during Li plating
process in the Blank electrolyte and TBAI3-containing electrolyte
was observed by in situ electrochemical optical microscopy to ver-
ify the electrostatic shielding eﬀect.[24] As shown in Figure 2a,
loose-like dendrites appeared clearly in the Blank electrolyte with
10–20 min of deposition at 3 mA cm−2, the deposited lithium
becomes a more fragmented porous structure with the exten-
sion of deposition time. In contrast, the morphology of lithium
deposited in the electrolyte with TBAI3 is denser and smoother
(Figure 2b). There are no obvious lithium dendrites appeared
even after 40 min of deposition in the electrolyte with additive.
To further demonstrate the inﬂuence of the electrostatic shield-
ing eﬀect in diﬀerent electrolytes on Li deposition morphology,
the Li+ ion concentration distribution with increasing current re-
sponse duration (0–600 s) was calculated based on ﬁnite element
simulation (FEM) on COMSOL software. It can be observed from
Figure 2c that in the Blank electrolyte, as the deposition proceeds,
the layer on the lithium surface will progressively thicken due
to the concentration of both the electric ﬁeld and lithium ions
at sharp edges with a smaller radius of curvature (“tip eﬀect”).
Consequently, more lithium will be preferentially deposited at
the tips rather than in the smooth areas of the lithium negative
electrode after 600 s.[25] Upon introduction of the TBAI3 addi-
tive, TBA+ adaptively aggregates at the tip of the semi-elliptical
lithium protrusion, which would not be reduced during lithium
plating due to the lower reduction potential than lithium.[26] The
enriched TBA+ produces a strong electrostatic shielding eﬀect on

ric cells with diﬀerent TBAI3 concentrations (from 0.1%-TBAI3

is the best TBAI3 additive content, and the follow-up studies are
carried out with 1%-TBAI3 electrolyte.
The frontier molecular orbital theory was applied to under-
stand the redox property of TBAI3 additive in Li-S battery sys-
tem. As we know, a lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)
energy level enhances a molecule’s likelihood of accepting elec-
trons, facilitating reduction reactions. And a higher occupied
molecular orbital (HOMO) means the priority to oxidation. As
shown in Figure 4a, the LUMO and HOMO of each component
in the electrolyte are calculated by DFT method. The iodic species
of I3
−and TBAI3 exhibit the lowest LUMO energy levels, which
indicates that they have a higher electron aﬃnity for Li2S/Li2S2
deposits on the Li anode surface in the forms of SEI, during
which the “dead sulfur” are recovered to soluble LiPSs species.[34]
Upon comparative analysis of the binding energies between I3
−
and various polysulﬁdes, as illustrated in Figure 4b, it is evident
that the binding energy associated with the interaction of I3
−
and Li2S exhibits the lowest value, indicative of the highest likeli-
hood for this particular redox reaction to occur. It is worth noting
that the binding energy of I3
−with various chain-length LiPSs
(Li2S-Li2S8) is stronger than that with DME and DOL solvent. Es-
pecially, the insoluble short-chain Li2S/Li2S2 present the higher
binding energy. The above theoretical calculations show that I3
−
is more likely to react with Li2S than other polysulﬁdes and sol-
vents, thus eﬀectively eliminating the inactive sulfur produced by
the shuttle eﬀect.

3. Conclusion
In summary, an eﬃcient multifunctional ionic liquid TBAI3
electrolyte additive was proposed for Li-S battery. The dynamic
electrostatic shielding eﬀect of TBA+ cations, the regulation on
the solvation structure, redox-mediated conversion kinetics and
the unique electrochemical work mechanism were investigated
systematically by series of in situ technologies, theoretical cal-
culations, potentiostatic Li2S deposition experiments and dif-
ferent kinds of symmetrical and asymmetric cells. TBA+ acts
as competitive cations to form a dynamic shielding layer to
suppress the tip eﬀect, coordinate with the solvent molecules,
and reduce the lithium-ion desolvation barrier. The I3
−/I−re-
dox couple eﬀectively eliminates the accumulation of “dead
sulfur” on Li anode surface by transforming the deposited
Li2S/Li2S2 into soluble LiPSs and relief the shuttling eﬀect.
Due to the multi-eﬀect advantages of TBAI3 additive, Li-S bat-
teries based on the additive exhibit minimal polarization, high
cyclic coulombic eﬃciency (99.99%) and ultra-long cycle perfor-
mance at high current (ca. 503 mAh g−1 at 2 C after 1000 cy-
cles). This simple strategy of multifunctional ionic liquid addi-
tive will provide an eﬀective strategy to solve the inherent prob-
lems associated with the positive and negative electrodes of Li-S
batteries.
