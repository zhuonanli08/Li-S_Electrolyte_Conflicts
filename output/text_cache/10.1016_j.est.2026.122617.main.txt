A B S T R A C T
Lithium‑sulfur (Li-S) batteries are regarded as one of the most promising next-generation energy storage systems 
due to their high theoretical energy density and cost-effectiveness. However, the practical application is hindered 
by sluggish redox kinetics, polysulfide shuttling, and low active material utilization, especially under high sulfur 
loading and lean electrolyte conditions. Herein, we report a dual-reaction interlayer composed of 2, 5-dimer­
capto-1, 3, 4-thiadiazole (DMTD) and conductive carbon (Ketjen Black/single-walled carbon nanotube) to 
address these issues. This dual-reaction interlayer not only strongly anchors lithium polysulfides but also pro­
motes their in-situ polymerization into short-chain solid-state sulfur-rich intermediates. This process transforms 
the conventional “solid-liquid-solid” sulfur redox pathway into a hybrid “solid-liquid-solid” and “solid-solid” 
conversion route, which significantly lowers the energy barrier of polysulfide conversion and enhances the 
completeness and reversibility of redox reactions. Specifically, it achieves a capacity of 1292 mA h g−1 at 0.1 C, 
822 mA h g−1 at 3 C and 705 mA h g−1 after 50 cycles at 0.2 C under a sulfur loading of 3.5 mg cm−2, electrolyte/ 
sulfur ratio of 8 μL mg−1. This work provides insights into the regulation of reaction kinetics of lithium poly­
sulfide reduction, contributing to the development of advanced practical Li-S batteries.
1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur (Li-S) batteries have attracted great attention due to a 
high theoretical specific energy of 2600 W h kg−1, and are considered to 
have great potential as next-generation batteries [1]. Unfortunately, the 
energy density of practical Li-S cells is far from the theoretical values 
from excessive use of electrolytes [2]. Sluggish reaction kinetics of sulfur 
cathode under lean electrolyte lead to low output capacities and large 
polarizations [3–5]. Current strategies to address kinetic limitations, 
involving highly efficient electrocatalysts or redox mediators, are mostly 
focused on cathode and electrolyte additives [6–11]. However, the role 
of interlayer materials in regulating reaction kinetics under lean elec­
trolyte conditions has often been overlooked.
Since the concept of an “interlayer” was introduced by Manthiram’ 
group in 2012 [12], interlayer materials have evolved from simple 
conductive carbon-based barriers to complex polar catalytic materials 
[13–21]. However, most of these electronic conductive and electro­
catalytic interlayers exhibit high activity and efficiency only under ideal 
conditions, featuring low sulfur loading cathode (< 2 mg cm−2) and 
flooded electrolytes (electrolyte/sulfur ratio, E/S ratio, typically 
exceeding 20 μL mgS−1) [22–26]. Interlayers show low activity and ef­
ficiency with the increase of sulfur loading and decrease of electrolyte 
[23,55,56]. Furthermore, the extra mass and thickness of interlayers 
increase electrolyte consumption and decrease the overall energy den­
sity of Li-S cells [23]. These limitations highlight the need for ultrathin, 
lightweight interlayers that remain catalytically effective under high

The fundamental bottleneck is that the kinetics of the sulfur reduc­
tion reaction (SRR) are highly dependent on solvent quantity. The 
standard solid-liquid-solid reaction process relies on soluble lithium 
polysulfides to circumvent the high activation barrier between sulfur 
and Li2S and guarantee smooth energy delivery of Li-S batteries 
[9,28–33]. However, their concentration and electrochemical behaviors 
are strongly governed by the amount of electrolyte [9,34]. Under lean 
electrolyte conditions, high concentrated lithium polysulfides dramati­
cally increase the viscosity of electrolyte, resulting in restricted ion 
diffusion and sluggish electrochemical reaction kinetics [35–37]. 
Crucially, when the E/S ratio is below a threshold value (approx. 3.2 μL 
mg−1), lithium polysulfides approach their solubility limit in ether 
electrolyte, transitioning into a ‘semi-solid’ state that further hinders the 
conversion to Li2S. Active electrolyte additives can promote solid-phase- 
involved conversions and can be broadly classified into two types: 
chemical polysulfide-scission reagents and redox-active mediators 
[10,38–40]. However, both types typically offer only single function­
ality, are ineffective in continuously promoting the complete conversion 
of polysulfides to Li2S [38,41], and may aggravate lithium-metal 
corrosion and electrolyte decomposition [10,38–42]. To date, there 
have been few reports of an additive that efficiently integrates dual 
functions without lithium metal corrosion or electrolyte decomposition. 
Finding alternative additives, such as interlayer additives, especially 
electrolyte insoluble additives, has emerged as a promising solution to 
overcome the challenges. However, research on interlayer additives, 
particularly regarding their effects on SRR kinetics, remains relatively 
limited. A deeper understanding of their mechanistic role in SRR could 
provide critical insights for improving the electrochemical performance 
and long-term cycling stability of Li-S batteries operating under lean 
electrolyte conditions. Therefore, there is an urgent need to pursue a 
suitable interlayer additive that accelerate deep conversion of poly­
sulfides to Li2S and reduce the concentration of soluble polysulfides.
Herein, a 2, 5-dimercapto-1, 3, 4-thiadiazole (DMTD)-Ketjen black 
carbon (KB)/ single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) layer with both 
polysulfide-scission/redox-active mediator functions, while also exhib­
iting high electronic conductivity and catalytic ability toward poly­
sulfides conversion is proposed as a dual-reaction interlayer for high 
sulfur loading/lean electrolyte Li-S batteries. Unlike conventional in­
terlayers that rely primarily on physical adsorption or surface catalysis, 
dual-reaction interlayer fundamentally reconstructs the SRR pathway by 
inducing in-situ polymerization of soluble lithium polysulfides into 
solid-state sulfur-rich active mediators. This transformation converts 
viscosity-limited liquid-phase reactions into hybrid “solid-liquid-solid” 
and “solid-solid” conversion processes, effectively decoupling sulfur 
utilization from electrolyte volume limitations. Owing to its ultrathin, 
lightweight architecture, abundant C––N bonds and thiol groups, and 
high electronic conductivity, dual-reaction interlayer strongly immobi­
lizes 
polysulfides 
and 
undergoes 
reversible 
polymerization- 
depolymerization during cycling, enabling sustained promotion of SRR 
kinetics and enhanced polysulfide utilization. Consequently, Li-S cell 
with dual-reaction interlayer achieves a capacity of 1292 mA h g−1 at 
0.1 C, 822 mA h g−1 at 3 C and 705 mA h g−1 after 50 cycles at 0.2 C 
under a sulfur loading of 3.5 mg cm−2, E/S ratio of 8 μL mg−1. 
Furthermore, the role of this dual-reaction interlayer in enhancing pol­
ysulfides utilization and promoting the polysulfides reduction reaction 
kinetics are systematically explored.
2. Results and discussion
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the dual-reaction interlayer is composed of 2, 
5-dimercapto-1, 3, 4-thiadiazole (DMTD), Ketjen-black carbon (KB) and 
single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT). DMTD is a typical vulcaniza­
tion agent of halogenated rubber and redox-active materials widely used 
in chemical industrial fields [43]. With two carbon nitrogen double 
bonds (C=N), four carbon sulfur bonds (C-S) and two thiol groups (S-H), 
DMTD molecule is an ideal interlayer additive with both polysulfide- 
scission and redox-mediator functions. Besides, the polar C––N bonds 
adsorb lithium polysulfides strongly and the S-H bonds convert the 
lithium polysulfides into solid-state active mediators, then fast convert 
to Li2S. This process accelerates deep conversion of polysulfides to Li2S 
and reduces the concentration of soluble polysulfides in electrolyte. 
However, the electronically insulating nature impedes the application of 
DMTD as an interlayer additive. Fortunately, SWCNT and KB are the 
most efficient electronic conductive substrates for sulfur species [23,44]. 
Homogeneously mixing DMTD with KB and SWCNTs can significantly 
increase the electronic conductivity and reutilize lithium polysulfides 
effectively under lean electrolyte conditions. Moreover, this composite 
exhibit reversible interactions with lithium polysulfides during charge- 
discharge cycles, leading to a repeatable utilization and a sustained 
promotion of SRR kinetics.
The dual-reaction interlayer was uniformly coated on a Celgard 2400 
membrane (PP separator) by casting process. The front side of the 
membrane appeared black due to the dual-reaction layer (Fig. 2a), while 
the back side remained white, indicating the uncoated PP surface 
(Fig. 2b). No irreversible destruction could be observed after folding/ 
unfolding tests (Fig. 2c, d), indicating a stable structure. The morphol­
ogies of the separator with a dual-reaction interlayer coating and the 
pristine separator were characterized by SEM. The surface of dual- 
reaction interlayer was uniformly covered by a network of cross- 
linked SWCNTs and spherical particles of KB/DMTD with diameters of 
several tens of nanometers (Fig. 2e, g). In contrast, the pristine PP sur­
face (Fig. 2h) exhibited slit-like pores with widths of several hundred 
nanometers. These pores serve as pathways for electrolyte transport, but 
lack ion selectivity, allowing both lithium ions and polysulfides shuttle 
during cycling. While in dual-reaction interlayer, the C––N bonds of 
DMTD molecules can adsorb lithium polysulfides strongly and the thiol 
groups are capable of in-situ polymerizing them into solid-state sulfur- 
rich active mediators. Meanwhile, the conductive KB/SWCNTs network 
facilitates rapid electron transport, promoting both the adsorbed poly­
sulfides and the in-situ polymerization products into Li2S. Finally, the 
cross-sectional SEM image reveals that the thickness of dual-reaction 
interlayer coating is ~4.66 μm (Fig. 2f).

Li2S6 electrolyte solution (Li2S6 dissolved in DOL/DME, v/v = 1:1) and 
then left to stand for 4 h in Ar atmosphere (Fig. 3a). Two identical 
containers were filled with the same concentration of Li2S6 solution, and 
a certain amount of solid DMTD powder was added to the left container. 
After standing for 4 h, the Li2S6 solution containing DMTD exhibited a 
visibly lighter color with solid precipitation at the bottom of the 
container than the control group. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) 
spectroscopy was subsequently performed on both the pristine DMTD

To demonstrate the properties of dual-reaction interlayer, Li-S coin 
cells were assembled using an SP/S cathode, a lithium metal anode and 
the separators including DRI@PP, KB/SWCNT@PP, p-DMTD@PP, and 
PP. The electrochemical performance of these cells was evaluated. All 
cells were first activated at 0.1 C for 3 cycles and then subjected charged- 
discharged tests at different rates. The initial charge-discharge profiles 
(Fig. 4a) and cycling performance (Fig. 4b) of the cell with dual-reaction 
interlayer were compared with those of the control groups. All cells 
exhibited two typical discharge plateaus within the voltage range of 
2.02–2.34 V, corresponding to the conversion of elemental sulfur to 
long-chain lithium polysulfides and their subsequent reduction to Li2S2/ 
Li2S, respectively [46]. Conversely, the charging process shows a 
plateau at 2.38 V, representing the reversible conversion of Li2S2/Li2S 
back to elemental sulfur [46,47]. These features are characteristic of the 
redox reactions of elemental sulfur in Li-S batteries [47].
Although the first plateau discharge capacity (C1) of DRI@PP cell 
showed no significant improvement compared to the control group, the 
second discharge plateau capacity (C2) of DRI@PP cell exhibited a 
markedly higher plateau voltage and a significantly enhanced capacity 
compared to the other groups. This indicates that the dual-reaction 
interlayer facilitates more efficient adsorption, in-situ polymerization 
and conversion of long-chain polysulfides [48]. At 0.1 C rate, the initial 
discharge capacities of cells with DRI@PP, KB/SWCNT@PP, p- 
DMTD@PP, and PP were 1292, 1002, 983, and 1059 mA h g−1, 
respectively (Fig. 4a). After 100 cycles at 0.2 C, the cell with DRI@PP 
retained a discharge capacity of 978 mA h g−1, while those with KB/ 
SWCNT@PP and PP retained 817 and 432 mA h g−1, respectively, 
indicating the superior cycling stability of the cell with DRI@PP 
(Fig. 4b). The cycling performance of the p-DMTD@PP cell is shown for 
~20 cycles, as its rapid capacity decay in the initial cycles is sufficient to 
reflect its intrinsic limitations. The absence of a conductive network 
leads to poor electronic conductivity and unstable sulfur redox re­
actions, resulting in fast performance degradation [58]. In contrast, the 
DRI@PP integrates conductive carbon with DMTD, enabling both effi­
cient charge transfer and effective polysulfide regulation, which signif­
icantly improves cycling stability. Notably, after 200 cycles, the cell with 
DRI@PP still maintained a high capacity of 881 mA h g−1 with a low- 
capacity fading rate of 0.16% per cycle, whereas the cell with PP 
dropped to 404 mA h g−1, corresponding to a much higher fading rate of 
0.31% per cycle.
The fluctuations of the Coulombic efficiency and charge-discharge 
capacities of cells with KB/SWCNT@PP and p-DMTD@PP during the 
initial three cycles (Fig. 4b) are mainly related to the different electro­
chemical roles of conductive carbon and p-DMTD in the two interlayers.

For KB/SWCNT@PP, the conductive carbon network enables relatively 
high sulfur utilization in the initial cycles, resulting in higher capacities. 
However, the weak chemical confinement toward lithium polysulfides 
causes severe shuttle behavior, leading to unstable Coulombic efficiency 
and even values exceeding 100% in the first three cycles [57]. For p- 
DMTD@PP, it shows lower and rapidly decaying capacities and 
Coulombic efficiency because the absence of conductive carbon results 
in poor electronic conductivity, incomplete sulfur conversion and irre­
versible side reactions [58]. In contrast, DRI@PP exhibits significantly 
higher charge-discharge capacities than both control groups during the 
initial three cycles, indicating that the combination of conductive car­
bon and DMTD can simultaneously ensure efficient electron transport 
and strong polysulfide regulation. The discharge capacity in the second 
cycle is even slightly higher than that in the first cycle, suggesting a 
progressive activation process of the dual-reaction interlayer. During 
this process, DMTD gradually participates in reversible polymerization- 
depolymerization reactions with polysulfides, while the conductive KB/ 
SWCNT network facilitates charge transfer and sulfur utilization. 
Although the initial Coulombic efficiency is relatively low, it gradually 
increases and stabilizes in subsequent cycles, indicating that the sulfur 
redox pathway becomes more reversible and stable after activation. 
These results further demonstrate the synergistic effect between DMTD 
and conductive carbon in regulating sulfur conversion reactions.
To further investigate the rate performance, the electrochemical 
properties of coin cells with either DRI@PP or PP separator were eval­
uated under various current rates. The cells were tested at 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 
0.5 C, 1 C, 2 C, and 3 C (Fig. 4c). The discharge capacities of cell with 
DRI@PP are 1189, 1156, 1015, 937, 839, and 822 mA h g−1 at each rate, 
respectively, whereas the PP-based cell only achieved 1012, 666, 574, 
528, 479, and 380 mA h g−1, indicating enhanced electrochemical re­
action kinetics and the superior rate capability enabled by dual-reaction 
interlayer. Notably, when the current rate was returned to 0.1 C, the 
DRI@PP cell recovered a high reversible discharge capacity of 1058 mA 
h g−1, surpassing the 590 mA h g−1 recovered by the PP cell. This result 
further confirms the critical role of dual-reaction interlayer in promoting 
efficient adsorption, in-situ polymerization, and conversion of long- 
chain lithium polysulfides.
High sulfur loading and lean electrolyte Li-S coin cells were assem­
bled using DRI@PP to evaluate its performance under conditions of high 
polysulfide concentrations. Fig. S1 (Supporting Information) shows the 
cycling performance of a DRI@PP-based cell with an areal sulfur loading 
of 2.0 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 8 μL mg−1 which was activated at 0.1 
C for three cycles and then cycled at 0.2 C. This cell exhibited a high 
initial discharge capacity of 1099.5 mA h g−1 and retained 873 mA h g−1 
after 50 cycles. As shown in Fig. 4d, DRI@PP-based cell with an areal 
sulfur loading of 3.5 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 8 μL mg−1 was acti­
vated at 0.1 C for 3 cycles, followed by cycling at 0.2 C for 60 cycles. 
Although the initial discharge capacity was relatively low (454 mA h 
g−1), it gradually increased during cycling and reached 705 mA h g−1 
after 50 cycles. Such an upward trend in discharge capacity is commonly 
observed in Li-S cells under high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte 
conditions, primarily due to the initially poor wetting of the thick 
cathode and the limited electrolyte volume [23]. Repeated charge- 
discharge processes can facilitate electrolyte penetration and redistri­
bution within the cathode, activate sulfur species and improve ionic/ 
electronic conductivity, thus leading to a progressive rise in discharge 
capacity [59]. The Coulombic efficiency was initially unstable but 
gradually stabilized with continued cycling. The unsteady Coulombic 
efficiency during the initial cycles is mainly attributed to the activation 
process [60]. The gradual wetting of the sulfur cathode and the pro­
gressive participation of DMTD in polymerization-depolymerization 
reactions lead to evolving sulfur redox pathways. This results in tem­
porary fluctuations in Coulombic efficiency, but with continued cycling 
and the stabilization of the system, the Coulombic efficiency becomes 
steady during subsequent cycling. It should be noted that the control 
group cells cannot deliver stable electrochemical performance under 
high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte conditions. Severe polysulfide 
shuttling and sluggish redox kinetics result in extremely low capacities 
and unstable cycling, making reliable data collection difficult.

The interaction mechanism between dual-reaction interlayer and 
lithium polysulfides during cycling was investigated by Li-S cells 
assembled with DRI@PP separators. The cells were disassembled in an 
argon-filled glovebox at two specific states: after the first discharge to 
1.7 V (point ‘a’ in Fig. 5a) and after the first charge to 2.8 V (point ‘b’ in 
Fig. 5a). The retrieved DRI@PP separators were immersed in acetoni­
trile for 2 h, and the resulting supernatants were collected for LC-MS 
analysis. Upon immersion, the DRI@PP separator from the discharged 
cell (1.7 V) immediately turned blue-green (sample a, Fig. 5a), while 
that from the charged cell (2.8 V) appeared grass green (sample b, 
Fig. 5a). The positive-ion mode LC-MS analysis of the discharge-state 
supernatant (a1) showed a major peak at m/z = 596.8557, corre­
sponding to an octamer of DMTD molecules (Fig. 5b). In contrast, the 
negative-ion mode LC-MS spectra of the charged-state supernatant 
exhibited two major peaks. The first peak (b1), at m/z = 566.868, was 
assigned to a trimeric DMTD‑sulfur polymer featuring evenly distributed 
-S-S- linkers (Fig. 5c). The second peak (b2), at m/z = 853.7794, was 
attributed to a similar structure consisting of a pentameric DMTD‑sulfur 
polymer with terminal -S-S-S- chains (Fig. 5d). These results suggest that 
during the initial discharge process, DMTD molecules undergo a self- 
polymerization process while encapsulating long-chain lithium poly­
sulfides into short-chain sulfur units within the polymer matrix. As 
discharge proceeds, the sulfur chains confined within the polymer 
network will gradually reduce to Li2S and a lithiated DMTD-based 
polymeric discharge product. Upon charging, the lithiated polymer 
undergoes delithiation and depolymerization, partially recombining 
with Li2S to form DMTD-short sulfur chains-based oligomers. These 
oligomers subsequently act as short-chain organic sulfur polymer active 
materials, participating in reversible electrochemical reactions. Funda­
mentally, dual-reaction interlayer enables a transformation of the 
typical “solid-liquid-solid” sulfur redox mechanism into a hybrid process 
involving both “solid-liquid-solid” and “solid-solid” conversions. By 
combining the advantages of both mechanisms and mitigating their 
limitations, the dual-reaction interlayer maximizes the electrochemical 
performance and stability of the Li-S batteries.
To further assess the ability of dual-reaction interlayer to immobilize 
long-chain lithium polysulfides and promote their conversion, in-situ X- 
ray diffraction (in-situ XRD) measurements were conducted using Li-S 
cells equipped with either DRI@PP or PP separators (Fig. 5e, f). The 
discharge-charge profile and corresponding in-situ XRD contour map of 
the DRI@PP-based cell are shown in Fig. 5e. During discharge, the first 
plateau ended at ~130 min, accompanied by the disappearance of the 
characteristic α-S8 peaks at 22.9◦, 25.7◦, 26.6◦, and 27.6◦, and the 
emergence of a new peak at 25.5◦, which is attributed to polysulfide 
species. This indicates that the solid-phase sulfur (S8) was reduced to 
long-chain lithium polysulfides during the first discharge stage [49,50]. 
In comparison, the cell with a PP separator (Fig. 5f) completed the first 
discharge plateau earlier at ~102 min, and the lithium polysulfide peak 
appeared even before this time. Moreover, the intensity of the lithium 
polysulfide peak was significantly stronger than that in the DRI@PP- 
based cell, suggesting that dual-reaction interlayer effectively adsorbs 
and in-situ polymerizes long-chain lithium polysulfides during 
discharge.
From 130 to 540 min, corresponding to the entire second discharge 
plateau, the intensity of the lithium polysulfide peak at 25.5◦in the 
DRI@PP-based cell progressively decreased, while a broad Li2S related 
peak appeared between 26.5◦and 27.0◦, indicating a further reduction 
of long-chain polysulfides and dual-reaction interlayer confined sulfur 
chains into Li2S2/Li2S. Compared to the PP-based cell, the DRI@PP cell 
exhibited a longer time period of Li2S deposition, confirming that dual- 
reaction interlayer not only confines polysulfides but also facilitates 
their conversion to solid discharge products [49,50].
Upon charging, the DRI@PP-based cell completed the full charge 
process at 1020 min, which corresponded to the disappearance of the 
broad Li2S peak (26.5◦-27.0◦) and the reappearance of a β-S8 peak at 
25.8◦, confirming the reversible transformation from Li2S back to β-S8. 
In contrast, the PP-based cell maintained a strong lithium polysulfide 
peak throughout the charge process, indicating that a substantial 
amount of lithium polysulfides remained unconverted at the end of 
charge. This result suggests poor utilization of active materials in the PP- 
based cell. Conversely, DRI@PP promotes Li2S to convert into short- 
chain sulfur polymers that are utilized in subsequent cycles, thus 
enhancing the reversibility of Li-S cells and promoting the utilization of 
active materials during cycling.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to 
explore the interactions between Li2S6 and DMTD, using graphene as a 
representative of carbon-based materials for comparison. The charge 
density differences and binding energies between a Li2S6 molecule and 
different substrates are illustrated in Fig. 6a and b. The DMTD shows a 
more negative binding energy of −1.09 eV (Fig. 6b) than the graphene 
(−0.81 eV, Fig. 6a), indicating its superior affinity for Li2S6.
Detailed charge analysis provides insight into this enhanced 
adsorption. While the Li2S6-graphene (Fig. 6a) shows only weak in­
teractions, the Li2S6-DMTD (Fig. 6b) reveals profound charge redistri­
bution. Specifically, significant electron accumulation on the N atoms of 
DMTD indicates a strong coordination interaction between the lith­
iophilic N sites and Li atoms in Li2S6. These results clearly show the good 
affinity and strong chemical anchoring of polar DMTD for polysulfides 
[51]. In addition, the non-polar carbon-based material in DRI@PP can 
also facilitate the redeposition of sulfur during charging process [51].
The thermodynamic feasibility of lithiation reaction can be evalu­
ated by calculating the reaction energy of the lithiated sulfur products 
[52]. Generally, a more negative reaction energy indicates a stronger 
thermodynamic driving force for the conversion [52]. The S8 reduction 
pathways of cells with PP separator and DRI@PP were investigated, 
focusing on the reversible formation and decomposition of Li2S from S8 
and Li+. During discharge, S8 first undergoes a two-electron reduction 
with Li+ to form Li2S8, which subsequently reduces to three intermedi­
ate lithium polysulfides (Li2S6, Li2S4, and Li2S2) before ultimately con­
verting to Li2S [53,54]. The reaction energies were calculated for the S8- 
Li2S6-Li2S process using PP separator and DRI@PP. The optimized 
structures of the intermediates and their reaction energy profiles are 
illustrated in Fig. 6c. From S8 to Li2S6, both PP separator and DRI@PP 
show the same reaction energy changes (−6.06 eV). After the formation 
of Li2S6, DMTD in DRI@PP undergoes an in-situ reaction with Li2S6, 
forming a polymerized reactive intermediate (C16N16S31Li2). This step is 
highly exergonic, with a reaction energy is −13.31 eV. Subsequently, 
during the continued discharge process, C16N16S31Li2 is further con­
verted into the final discharge products (C16N16S24Li2 and Li2S), with a 
reaction energy of −3.41 eV. In contrast, without DMTD, the control 
group (PP separator) exhibits a direct reduction of Li2S6 to Li2S with a 
reaction energy of 2.75 eV. These results suggest that the dual-reaction 
interlayer alters the conventional S8 to Li2S discharge pathway and 
significantly enhances the thermodynamics of sulfur reduction [53].
During charge process, the oxidation of Li2S to S8 was investigated 
using both PP separator and DRI@PP (Fig. 6d). With PP separator, the 
oxidation of Li2S to S8 requires a reaction energy of 3.31 eV. In contrast, 
DRI@PP introduces an alternative reaction pathway wherein Li2S reacts 
with the polymeric intermediate (C16N16S24Li2) to form two depoly­
merized products (C6N6S12Li2 and C10N10S18Li2), with a lower associ­
ated reaction energy of 3.13 eV. Thus, DRI@PP modifies the 
conventional charge reaction route and reduces the reaction energy for 
Li2S oxidation, thereby promoting the decomposition of lithium sulfides 
and enhancing the electrochemical reaction kinetics. The structures of 
the reactive intermediates involved in these theoretical calculations are 
consistent with the MS results (Fig. 5b-d). Additional details regarding

3. Conclusion
In summary, a dual-reaction interlayer composed of DMTD-KB/ 
SWCNT was rationally designed to address the key challenges of slug­
gish sulfur redox kinetics and polysulfides shuttling in Li-S batteries with 
high sulfur loading and lean-electrolyte conditions. The dual-reaction 
interlayer not only strongly anchors lithium polysulfides but also 
enable their in-situ polymerization into short-chain solid-state sulfur- 
rich intermediates. Furthermore, it facilitates a hybrid sulfur redox 
pathway involving both “solid-liquid-solid” and “solid-solid” conver­
sions, thereby enhancing active material utilization and reducing the 
energy barrier of the Li2S6 reduction reaction. The incorporation of 
conductive carbon (SWCNT and KB) ensures high electronic conduc­
tivity, facilitating rapid charge transfer and accelerating sulfur redox 
kinetics even under lean electrolyte conditions. Consequently, the dual- 
reaction interlayer significantly improves cell performance under lean 
electrolyte conditions (E/S ratio of 8 μL mg−1) and increases the C2/C1 
ratios across various current rates, demonstrating its effectiveness in 
promoting polysulfide conversion and maintaining reaction kinetics 
under practically relevant conditions.
