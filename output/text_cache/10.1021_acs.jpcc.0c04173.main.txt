ABSTRACT: Reduction potentials of electrolyte molecules in the lithium−sulfur (Li/S)
battery and their variations in several solvent environments are studied using the density
functional theory method with Dunning’s triple-ζ correlation consistent basis set. Reliable
reduction potential values are key for electrolyte additive design needed for suppressing
polysulﬁde dissolution shuttle mechanism, resulting in poor cycle performance and severe
self-discharge of the Li/S battery. Although isolated electrolyte molecules have reduction
potentials outside the operating voltage range of the Li/S battery, complexation with other
electrolyte species enables the electrolyte molecules to be reduced within the operating
voltage range. Among the electrolyte species considered in this study, bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)-
imide (FSI−) and ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC) yield reduction potentials within the
expected range, suggesting the development of ﬂuorine-containing additives as a promising
line of research.

energy density of ∼2500 Wh/kg obtained from the following
reduction reaction of S8

The practical capacity of a battery cell can be achieved by
25−33% of the theoretical speciﬁc capacity, considering the
weight of other battery components such as separator,
conducting materials, binders, tabs, pouch, or case materials.
Thus, the probable speciﬁc capacity of a Li/S battery is about
∼600 Wh/kg although the commercially available Li/S
battery from OXIS Energy has a speciﬁc energy of 400
Wh/kg now.2,5 However, even these practical values that are
lower than the theoretically predicted ones are still higher
than the currently highest-speciﬁc-energy electric vehicle
(EV) LiB cell equipped to Tesla model S, which was
calculated to ∼270 Wh/kg.6 In addition, the use of abundant
and cheap sulfur (S) rather than cobalt and nickel enables us
to reduce the cost of producing battery cells, compared to
that of LiB.
Despite these advantages of the Li/S battery over LiB, the
Li/S battery has several limitations to be overcome for the
EV application, including the low electric conductivity of S,
the large volume expansion of S, and the polysulﬁde (PS)
shuttle.4 Among these limitations, the PS shuttle aﬀects the
cycle performance of the Li/S battery, and the commercial
Li/S battery cells from OXIS Energy also reach 80% of the
capacity at the beginning of life (BoL) in 60−100 cycles.5 To
prevent the PS shuttle, there have been several investigations
in terms of cathode material designs, functional separators,
anode protection, and electrolyte types (see refs 4 and 7−10
and references therein). For the electrolyte studies, most
research groups have taken only electrolyte solvents and the
types of electrolyte into account, and few researchers have
investigated the eﬀect of electrolyte additives on generating a
passivation layer on the cathode surface, hindering the
dissolution of soluble long-chained PS into the electrolyte
and ﬁnally the PS shuttle.11−15
In our previous study, we calculated the oxidation
potentials of electrolyte solvents, Li salts, and the additives
aimed to protect the cathode surface as well as two anionic
additives whose purpose is to generate a passivation layer on
the cathode in LiB,16 since most of the studied additives were
expected to be decomposed on the cathode surface by
oxidation during charge. Yushin and co-workers, however,
investigated the eﬀect of ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC) on
the cathode passivation by reducing FEC.14 They suggested
that FEC would be reduced on the surface of the lithiated S
cathode and participate in forming the solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI) around S particles. This SEI layer on S
particles acts as a barrier to the PS dissolution into the
electrolyte and the PS shuttle, resulting in the improvement

2. COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS
We employed the same procedures as in our previous study
for the oxidation potential calculations. Boese and Martin’s τ-
dependent hybrid functional (BMK) DFT method18 with the
cc-pVTZ basis set19 was utilized, and LanL2DZdp eﬀective
core potential (ECP)20,21 was used for the iodine atom.
Although the augmented basis set would produce more
accurate reduction potential than cc-pVTZ for the negatively
charged systems in the reduced state, the cc-pVTZ basis set
rather than the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set was considered in this
study to directly compare the computation results with
previous oxidation potential calculations. In addition, it was
also reported that Dunning’s triple-ζ basis set produces
similar results regardless of the use of diﬀuse functions.22
(See the Supporting Information (Table S1 and discussion)
for the benchmark calculation performed to select the DFT
functional and the basis set in this study.) The solvation
eﬀect was mimicked by the SMD model23 with the
tetrahydrofuran (THF) solvent. For some anionic complexes,
the empirical dispersion correction was included using
Grimme’s D3BJ method,24 as indicated in the next sections.
The charge model 5 (CM5)25 was employed in comparing
the charge changes by reduction, and the natural bond orbital
(NBO) method26 was utilized in analyzing the lowest
unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMOs) of electrolyte
complexes. All computations were performed by using the
Gaussian 09 program.27

3.1. Reduction Potentials of Isolated Electrolyte
(Solvent and Additive) Species. The calculated reduction
potentials of DME_ttt and DME_tgt are −1.26 and −1.24 V,
respectively, indicating that DME is not easily reduced. Park
and co-workers reported the reduction potential of DME to
be −1.68 V using the B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) theoretical level
with the polarized continuum model (PCM) for the solvation
eﬀect, which is lower than our result by about 0.40 V.35 The
diﬀerences may be attributed to the diﬀerences in functional
and solvation method. According to our benchmark
calculation, B3LYP was the worst performer in this set of
calculations. Based on this, our calculated potentials may be
more realistic. In addition, we note that these negative
reduction potential values mean that the reduction of the
isolated DME is not thermodynamically favorable. Indeed,
DME reduction was not observed in ∼20 ps of ab initio
molecular dynamics simulations,36,37 where the solvent was in
contact with the highly reactive Li metal surface, in contrast
to other molecules such as FSI−and TFSI−anions that easily
reduce at the Li surface. This suggests that the uncoordinated
DME solvent is relatively stable and requires electron-rich
environments for its reduction.38−40 Table 1 indicates that
the reduction potential becomes positive when the molecule

The reduced geometry of DME is shown in Figure 1a

The calculated reduction potential of DOL is −0.72 V, and
this is much higher than the computed reduction potential
from Park and co-workers by about 0.76 V.35 The calculation
also shows that DOL is more stable to reduction than Li+ but
is more easily reducible than DME. The optimized geometry
in the reduced state of DOL displays a ring opening through
breaking a bond between one of C−O bonds (C1−O10 in
Figure 1b). According to its spin density, atom C1 has an
unpaired electron, and atom O10 is part of an alkoxide as
shown by the partial charge of −0.63 e. This is also
consistent with the NBO analysis, where the LUMO is the
antibonding between atoms C1 and O10. Aurbach and co-
workers also observed this alkoxide formed by the reductively
decomposed DOL through infrared (IR) spectroscopy
experiments.42

linear sweep voltammetry, Passerini and co-workers reported

Li salts are not reduced at a voltage larger than 0.0 V.43 The

The calculated reduction potential of EDOT is −0.60 V,
and the optimized geometry in the reduced state does not
show dramatic changes in bond lengths. C1−S5−C4 in the

hardly reduced. This shows that the electrochemical polymer-

FEC is predicted to have a reduction potential of 0.52 V,
which is much lower than those from experiments (Table 1).
The optimized geometry has a broken bond between atoms
C2 and O5, as shown in Figure 1g. That is also shown in the
experiment by Gustafsson and co-workers,30 and Wang and
co-workers,29 while Nakai and co-workers claimed that the
bond between atoms C1 and O6 is broken by reduction.44 In
this study, we found that the C2−O5 broken one is more
stable than the C1−O6 broken one by 6.6 kcal/mol
(potential energy) and by 6.4 kcal/mol (free energy) in the
absence of Li+. Atom C2 has an unpaired electron from the
spin density of 0.75, and larger partial charges for atoms O5
and O8 than for atom O6 indicate that the negative charge is
delocalized over these O atoms (Table S2). From the NBO
analysis, the LUMO is predicted to be the antibonding
orbital of the CO bond, which indicates the bond breakage
in the carbonyl group; however, the resultant reduced
geometry shows that the bond between atoms C2 and O5
is broken. Then, a bond cleavage mechanism can be
explained as shown in Scheme 3, where the electron added
to the carbonyl group is moved to atom O5, leading to the
dissociation between atoms O5 and C2.
Iodide is predicted to have a reduction potential of −3.53
V, which is the lowest reduction potential among the
electrolyte additive molecules considered in this study and
also indicates that it is the least reducible reagent. This low
reduction potential enables us to anticipate that iodide works
as a reductant in electrolyte complexes.
The two borate anions, BOB−and DFOB−, have calculated
reduction potentials of −0.09 and −0.27 V, respectively,
which are much lower than experimental potentials listed in
Table 1. The LUMOs of BOB−and DFOB−are predicted to
be the antibonding orbital of the CO bond in the oxalate
group from the NBO analysis, which is consistent with the
spin density in the reduced states, as shown in Figure 1h,i
and Table 1. The bond distances between atoms C1 and O11
and between atoms C2 and O10 increase from 1.19 to 1.24 Å
by reduction, and those between atoms C1 and O9 and
between atoms C2 and O8 also increase from 1.31 to 1.39 Å
in BOB−. The carbonyl CO bonds in DFOB−also have an
elongated bond length from 1.20 to 1.24 Å, and the C−O
bond lengths also increased from 1.31 to 1.38 Å by
reduction. In addition, the two B−F bonds were also
elongated from 1.38 to 1.42 Å.

4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
Reduction potentials of electrolyte molecules are investigated
using the DFT method and Dunning’s triple-ζ correlation
consistent basis set (LanL2DZdp for I atom) not only in
isolated molecules but also in complexes with Li+, anions, Li
salts, solvent, S8 and pyrene, and PS species. The variations of
reduction potentials, where each electrolyte molecule is
reduced by complexation with other electrolyte species in
this study, are displayed in Figure 12. Figure 12 illustrates
how the reduction potentials of electrolyte species change by
the presence of other electrolyte molecular species around
them. Almost all of the electrolyte molecules cannot be
reduced in their isolated states with negative reduction
potential values; however, when complexed with other
electrolyte molecules, many of the negative reduction
potentials turn to positive.
Considering the operating voltage range of the Li/S
battery, TFSI−and most of the additives tested in this
study are not appropriate for generating a protection layer on
the cathode surface by reduction, leading to the prevention of
the PS dissolution and the PS shuttle mechanism from our
calculations. For example, although EDOT, pyrrole, and I−
were reported as promising additives for this purpose by
oxidative polymerization12,15 and DME oxidation,13 the
reduction of EDOT and pyrrole is not favorable and the
role of I−as a reducing agent is also limited from the current
study.
However, the reduction of FSI−shows the production of
F−and also generates LiF in the presence of Li+. LiF is one
of the main inorganic components of the SEI layer on the
anode surface.49 The reduction potentials range from 1.36 to
2.36 V in complexes with other electrolyte molecules, which
indicates that FSI−can be decomposed in the initial few steps
of discharging and generates a protective layer on the cathode
surface. FEC also displays promising reductive decomposi-
tions at a lower potential when complexed with Li+, and the
decomposed FEC can also react with other electrolyte
molecules and produce a passivation layer on the cathode
surface. These are also observed experimentally by Yushin
and co-workers,14,17 and this study proves the usefulness of
these compounds and ﬂuorine-containing compounds in the
prevention of the PS dissolution. Fluorine-containing
additives have been studied in the LiB system for high-
voltage batteries and for silicon anodes, and they also showed
improved cycle performances by protecting the LNMO
cathode surface and the silicon anode surface.30,50−52 In the
Li/S battery system, although ﬂuorine-containing ether
compounds have also been studied to suppress the PS
dissolution by using them as cosolvent,53−55 the research on
the ﬂuorine-containing additives is rare. The results in this
study strongly suggest that the development of ﬂuorine-
containing additives for Li/S battery electrolytes aimed for
the prevention of the PS dissolution and the PS redox shuttle
mechanism.
