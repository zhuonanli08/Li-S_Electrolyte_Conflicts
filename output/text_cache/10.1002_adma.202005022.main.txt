New High Donor Electrolyte for Lithium–Sulfur Batteries

The attainable specific energy of Li-S 
cells is largely affected by the electrolyte-
to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, with a low value[5] 
thereof being prerequisite to achieve a 
competitive energy density for a practical 
cell. However, the most commonly used 
electrolyte, a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) 
(i.e., v/v  = 1:1), exhibits low Li2S8 solu-
bility of at most 0.7 m at room tempera-
ture.[6] This limited solubility constitutes 
an obstacle in the way of the full-depth 
utilization of sulfur under so-called lean 
electrolyte conditions. Although it could 
be overcome by using a large amount of 
electrolyte, this would severely impair the 
volumetric energy density. This notwith-
standing, the DOL/DME system and its 
analogues served as standard electrolyte 
solvents in the early stages of investiga-
tion, because the stable environment 
they offer to Li metal anodes contributed greatly to enhancing 
the cycle life. Nevertheless, identifying electrolyte conditions 
that allow effective operation at low E/S ratios (i.e., below 
2 μLelectrolyte mgsulfur−1 in practice) is essential for practical 
cells.[7] Therefore, the development of alternative electrolyte sol-
vents with high polysulfide solubility remains highly desirable.
Electrolytes with a high Gutmann donor number, such 
as N,N-dimethyl acetamide (DMAc),[7a,8] dimethyl sulfoxide 
(DMSO),[6,7,9] 
N,N-dimethyl 
formamide 
(DMF),[10] 
and 
N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)[9c] are good candidates for lean 
electrolyte conditions in Li-S batteries. High donicity affords 
an environment that promotes interaction with electrophilic 
cations, implying that the solvation of Li ions can facilitate the 
solubility of polysulfides. For example, high donor electrolytes 
were reported[6] to readily dissolve more than 1.6 m of Li2S8. In 
the same line, the utilization of sulfur can be enhanced for the 
given amount of electrolyte introduced in the cell. Apart from 
the solvation capability, high donor electrolytes confer 3D mor-
phology for the final discharging product, namely Li2S, contrary 
to low donor electrolytes that give rise to a 2D film-like mor-
phology. The 3D morphology is beneficial in that it leaves the 
conductive electrode surface available for repeated discharge–
charge over cycling without passivation.[11] In addition, high 
donor electrolytes activate reaction routes that involve S3•− spe-
cies (Figure 1a),[8a,12] the availability of which represents diverse 
reaction pathways for discharge to further enhance the utiliza-
tion of sulfur.
Despite these remarkable advantages, high donor electro-
lytes are known to have a short cycle life mainly because of 
their catastrophic reactivity with the Li metal anode.[8a,11c] This 
problem was subsequently pinpointed by Gupta  et  al.,[7a] who 
The unparalleled theoretical specific energy of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries has 
attracted considerable research interest from within the battery community. 
However, most of the long cycling results attained thus far relies on using 
a large amount of electrolyte in the cell, which adversely affects the specific 
energy of Li–S batteries. This shortcoming originates from the low solubility of 
polysulfides in the electrolyte. Here, 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI) is 
reported as a new high donor electrolyte for Li–S batteries. The high solubility 
of polysulfides in DMI and its activation of a new reaction route, which engages 
the sulfur radical (S3•−), enables the efficient utilization of sulfur as reflected 
in the specific capacity of 1595 mAh g−1 under lean electrolyte conditions of 
5 μLelectrolyte mgsulfur−1. Moreover, the addition of LiNO3 stabilizes the lithium 
metal interface, thereby elevating the cycling performance to one of the highest 
known for high donor electrolytes in Li–S cells. These engineered high donor 
electrolytes are expected to advance Li–S batteries to cover a wide range of 
practical applications, particularly by incorporating established strategies to 
realize the reversibility of lithium metal electrodes.
The ever-growing demand for rechargeable batteries with 
increased energy densities calls for new approaches to raise 
the performance of promising post-lithium-ion batteries (post-
LIBs) in realistic cell conditions.[1] Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) bat-
teries, which are one of these post-LIB candidates, have strong 
potential to surpass the energy densities of current LIBs.[2] 
Their superior energy density arises from the choice of elec-
trode materials on both the cathode and anode sides: elemental 
sulfur and metallic lithium (Li) possess high theoretical capaci-
ties of 1675 and 3860  mAh  g−1, respectively, leading to high 
theoretical gravimetric energy density of 2600  Wh  kg−1 at the 
cell level when the mass of the active materials is exclusively 
taken into account. Furthermore, elemental sulfur is naturally 
abundant and easily accessible as it is produced as a byproduct 
during the refinery process of natural gas and petroleum. 
Intensive research efforts have brought substantial progress 
in the cell performance, such as energy density as high as 
400 Wh kg−1[3] and a lifetime longer than one thousand cycles.[4] 
Nonetheless, the operating conditions and cell specifications 
would need to be carefully evaluated and improved to ensure 
successful dissemination of the relevant technology in practice.

be the origin thereof. Although various high donor electrolytes 
have been reported over the years,[6–10] the corrosion of the Li 
metal counter electrode was severe and beyond an acceptable 
level even in normal salt concentrations near 1 m. Only a few 
strategies, such as salt-in-solvent electrolytes[6,13] and modified 
separators,[8a] reached at most dozens of cycles.
In an attempt to find a new high donor electrolyte, we paid 
attention to the molecular structure of an electrolyte solvent 
that is commonly employed in LIBs, ethylene carbonate (EC). 
EC secures a certain level of compatibility with Li metal but its 
reactivity with polysulfides interrupts the designated cascade of 
the discharging reaction.[14] This reactivity is ascribed to the high 
electrophilicity of the carbon atom in the carbonyl group toward 
sulfide anions. Replacement of the two oxygen atoms adjacent 
to the carbonyl group in EC by a strong electron donor such as 
nitrogen would be expected to pacify the reactivity with lithium 
polysulfides owing to the stabilization of the carbonyl group. 
Based on this rationale, herein, we introduce 1,3-dimethyl-2-im-
idazolidinone (DMI) in which the neighboring oxygen in EC 
is replaced by nitrogen as a new high donor electrolyte for Li-S 
batteries. DMI is characterized by high donicity and reasonable 
compatibility with the Li metal anode but is unreactive toward 
polysulfides, possessing properties that are altogether desirable 
for robust cycling under lean electrolyte conditions. As a result, 
by employing lithium nitrate (LiNO3) as an additive, almost full 
sulfur utilization was realized even at low E/S ratios, along with 
decent cyclability. Our study unveils promising opportunities for 
high donor electrolytes for Li-S batteries that meet the required 
practical specifications when the electrolyte is engineered to 
secure its compatibility with the Li metal anode.
Before discussing the electrochemical results of Li-S cells in 
lean electrolyte conditions, we first present the basic physico-
chemical and electrochemical properties of DMI. The appro-
priate physical properties of DMI enables it to serve as an 
electrolyte solvent in terms of its viscosity (1.9 cP at 25  °C), 
dielectric constant (37.6 at 25  °C), melting point (8.2  °C), 
boiling point (225  °C), and oxidation stability (up to 4.1  V 
versus Li/Li+) (Figure S1, Supporting Information). DMI also 
has a high donor number of 29.0  kcal  mol−1, similar to that 
of other high donor solvents reported for Li-S battery electro-
lytes: DMSO (29.8 kcal mol−1), DMAc (27.8 kcal mol−1), NMP 
(27.3  kcal  mol−1), and DMF (26.6  kcal  mol−1) (Figure  1b,c). 
These high donor solvents have much higher solvation 
abilities than those of solvents commonly used for LIBs 
and post-LIBs, including DME (DN = 20.0  kcal  mol−1), EC 
(DN = 16.4 kcal mol−1), sulfolane (TMS, DN = 14.8 kcal mol−1), 
and acetonitrile (ACN, DN = 14.1 kcal mol−1).

were chosen as high donor solvents along with DMI, and 
DOL/DME (v/v  = 1/1) and EC/dimethyl carbonate (DMC)

prepared by chemically reacting Li2S and elemental sulfur, 
the solubility of Li2S6 in the different solvents was clearly 
distinct depending on the donicity (Figure  2a). Although the 
high donor solvents, DMI, DMSO, and DMAc, were capable 
of dissolving 1.5 m of nominal Li2S6, DOL/DME exhibited a 
clear precipitation because of its limited dissolution ability. 
In the case of EC/DMC, compounds produced from the reac-
tion of polysulfide with EC/DMC, together with unreacted ele-
mental sulfur and Li2S, precipitated in the bottom of the vial. 
It should be noted that the polysulfide concentration of 1.5 m 
was selected by considering the energy density of 500 Wh kg−1 
targeted for commercial Li-S cells.[7a] The poor ability of DOL/
DME to dissolve polysulfides points to its limited utilization of 
sulfur under lean electrolyte conditions.
Next, UV–vis spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the 
polysulfide species resulting from the exposure of 1 mm Li2S6 
to various solvents (Figure  2b). Notably, high donor DMI, 
DMAc, and DMSO exhibited a strong peak at 618 nm indicative 
of the sulfur radical (S3•−). The detection of S3•− is attributed to 
the disproportionation reaction of S62−, followed by stabilization 
of S3•− by high donor solvents. DMSO exhibited another peak 
at 475  nm that was assigned to S62−,[7a,12] therefore implying 
the co-existence of S62− and S3•−. By contrast, DMAc had a low-
intensity peak at 475 nm, indicating the dominant presence of 
S3•− over S62−, which is consistent with the literature.[7a] The 
result for DOL/DME was the exact opposite in that it did not 
exhibit the peak corresponding to S3•− at all; instead, it dis-
played a strong peak at 420 nm as a sign of S42−.
The presence of the S3•− species was detected even when Li2S 
was dissolved in the high donor electrolytes (Figure 2a, bottom). 
When various solvents were filtered in the presence of 0.1 m 
Li2S, DMI and DMSO turned green, and DMAc turned blue, 
both of which reflect the presence of S3•− species.[7a,12] By con-
trast, when subjected to the same treatment, DOL/DME and 
EC/DMC did not undergo any color change. After removing the 
precipitants by filtration, the distinct presence of the S3•− spe-
cies among the different solvents was again verified by UV–vis 
spectroscopy (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Although 
DMI, DMAc, and DMSO each exhibited a peak at 618  nm 
attributable to the S3•− species, DOL/DME and EC/DMC did 
not show such a peak at all. In addition, the reaction time scale 
for the formation of S3•− and polysulfides was much shorter 
for the high donor solvents than for the low donor ones. The 
dropwise addition of DMI to a vial containing elemental sulfur 
and Li2S led to the immediate observation of the formation 
of S3•− and polysulfides (Figure  2c and Movie S1, Supporting 
Information), whereas the formation of polysulfides in DOL/
DME was much slower such that the reaction took more than 
1 h (Movie S2, Supporting Information). The rapid reaction 
of Li2S to form soluble polysulfides in high donor solvents is 
indeed a conspicuous advantage for Li-S batteries because the 
fast reaction kinetics represents the accelerated conversion of 
solid Li2S to liquid polysulfides in the charging process. From 
the viewpoint of the utilization of sulfur, as discussed above, 
enhanced utilization is enabled by the additional chemical and 
electrochemical reaction pathways that are absent in low donor 
systems (Figure 2d). These advantages could also lead to inac-
tive Li2S or S8 being diminished and consequently to the Cou-
lombic efficiency (CE) approaching 100%.

The key results derived from the CV analysis can be corre-
lated with the galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles of both 
the DMI- and DOL/DME-based cells that were obtained for an 
E/S ratio of 30 μL mgsulfur−1 with 1 m LiTFSI. All galvanostatic 
tests were performed at 0.1C (Figure S4, Supporting Infor-
mation) after pre-cycling at 0.03C. The voltage profile of the 
DMI-based cell exhibited a sloping shape in the potential range 
of 2.7–2.3 V, corresponding to the conversion reaction of solid 
sulfur to long-chain liquid polysulfides. This profile sharply 
contrasts that of conventional DOL/DME systems, which usu-
ally show a clear plateau in the given potential range. The 
sloping profile seemingly indicates a one-phase-like reaction 
without a clear phase boundary.[16] This interpretation is also 
related to the excellent ability of DMI to dissolve polysulfides, 
which enables the solid-liquid phase transition to occur without 
a clear boundary. As in solid-state phase transitions in other 
electrode materials,[17] one-phase-like behavior is more condu-
cive to fast reaction kinetics, as indeed demonstrated in the 
chemical reaction (Figure  2c). A small shoulder-like profile 
at 2.1 V then appeared which is attributed to the reduction of 
long- to medium-chain polysulfides engaging S3
•− as a reaction 
intermediate. A lower discharge plateau subsequently appeared 
at around 2.0 V, instead of at 2.1 V as in the conventional DOL/
DME case. This greater polarization of the lower discharge 
plateau is ascribed to the fact that the high solvation energy of 
DMI impedes the nucleation of Li2S upon its precipitation on 
the conductive electrode surface.[11b]
The galvanostatic electrochemical discharge–charge cycle 
was repeated to assess the cycling sustainability of the DMI-
based electrolyte. Although inferior to the DOL/DME-based 
counterpart (Figure S5, Supporting Information), DMI alone 
exhibited significantly improved cycling stability (Figure  4b) 
compared to that of any high donor electrolyte reported to 
date,[6–10] revealing the promising ability of DMI to stabilize the 
Li metal interface. Furthermore, the DMI-based cell showed an 
initial CE of 102.4%, which is even closer to 100% than that of 
the DOL/DME-based cell (109.3%). For reference, the excess CE 
above 100% refers to the extent of overcharging and thus the 
shuttling process.[18] During the initial ten cycles, the CE of 
the DMI-based cell increased and its capacity decayed owing 
to the unstable interface with the Li metal anode. This ten-
dency of the cycling and CE performance re-emphasizes the 
importance of stabilizing the Li metal interface as a task that 
remains to be accomplished to advance high donor electrolytes.
In an attempt to improve the cycling performance of the 
DMI-based cell, we implemented LiNO3 as an electrolyte addi-
tive by benchmarking its established role in Li-S batteries: pas-
sivating the Li metal surface to mitigate the shuttling effect.[19] 
To determine the effect of the LiNO3 additive, galvanostatic tests 
were performed under the following electrolyte conditions: 
i) 1 m LiTFSI in DMI, ii) 1 m LiTFSI+0.2 m LiNO3 in DMI, 
and iii) 1 m LiTFSI+0.5 m LiNO3 in DMI, which are abbrevi-
ated to DMI-LNO-0m, DMI-LNO-0.2m, and DMI-LNO-0.5m, 
respectively. The cycling performance was evaluated by galva-
nostatically cycling each cell at 0.1C after pre-cycling at 0.03C. 
During

Figure 5a displays the cycling behavior of the three cells for 
50 cycles. During this cycling period, the initial capacities of

improved by increasing the LiNO3 content. In the first cycle at

three cells was close to 100.0% (102.4, 96.6, and 97.1%, respec-

increased such that after 50 cycles they reached 119.0, 103.2,

revealed the effect of LiNO3. The CE of the DMI-LNO-0m cell 
rose drastically above 100% in the first ten cycles, in reflec-

unstable Li metal interface. Figure S7, Supporting Information 
shows the voltage profiles of the three cells at the tenth and 
50th cycles. The above-mentioned excessively high CE of the 
DMI-LNO-0m cell is reflected in its abnormally long charging 
plateau at approximately 2.6 V.

Further confirmation of the stabilization of the Li metal by 
the addition of LiNO3 was obtained by evaluating Li-Li sym-
metric cells by applying a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 with a 
specific capacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2 for each charge and discharge 
(Figure 5c). The effect of the added LiNO3 was dramatic. In the 
case of DMI-LNO-0m, the reversibility of Li plating and strip-
ping was very poor in such a way that the overpotential sur-
passed 1.0 V after only seven cycles. In stark contrast, cycling 
in the DMI-LNO-0.2m and -0.5m cells was extended to 45 and 
215 cycles until their overpotentials exceeded 1.0  V. As far as 
we could establish, this cyclability is superior to those of any 
symmetric Li-Li cells based on high donor electrolytes.[7a,21] 
Figure S9, Supporting Information shows magnified views 
of the overpotential in the second to seventh cycles for all the 
three cells. Remarkably, the overpotential decreased from 157 
to 107 to 86  mV for DMI-LNO-0m, -0.2m, and -0.5m, respec-
tively, implying that the interface formed by engaging LiNO3 is 
beneficial for facile Li+ ion transport, aside from improving the 
cyclability. This advantageous ionic transport is also reflected 
in the semi-circles of the EIS results (Figure S10, Supporting 
Information). The reversibility and interfacial stability during Li 
plating and stripping were also tested in a Li-Cu asymmetric 
cell setting (Figure S11, Supporting Information), for which the 
same cycling trend was observed among the cells.
To elucidate the origin of the distinct interfacial stability 
among the cells, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) anal-
ysis was conducted (Figure S12, Supporting Information). We 
particularly paid attention to the C 1s and N 1s bands, and the 
deconvoluted sub-peaks were interpreted by focusing on the 
decomposition of the electrolyte solvents and salts. The organic 
CO, CN, and CO in the C 1s band and N(CO) in the 
N 1s band originated from the decomposition of the electrolyte 
solvent (DMI), whereas the decomposition of the salts (LiTFSI 
and LiNO3) was revealed by the appearance of the CF3 peak in 
the C 1s band and the Li3N peak in the N 1s band. In the case of 
DMI-LNO-0m, the electrolyte solvent-derived organic products 
were more prominent in its SEI layer (Figure S12a, Supporting 
Information). On the other hand, upon adding LiNO3, the salt-
derived bond signatures grew relative to the electrolyte solvent-
derived ones (Figures S12b and S13, Supporting Information). 
This effect by LiNO3 is consistent with the literature[22] in that 
LiNO3 mitigates the decomposition of high donor electrolytes 
by passivating the Li metal surface.
We then conducted further electrochemical analyses to 
compare the DMI-based cells with their well-known DOL/

In addition, DMI-LNO-0.5m was tested at different electrolyte

about 800 mAh gsulfur−1 or 4 mAh cm−2 was maintained when 
the E/S ratio was 8 μLelectrolyte mgsulfur−1 or above.
Based on the rationale that the amount of electrolyte is critical 
for the specific energy of a Li-S cell, we evaluated the solubility 
of Li2S8 in various solvents (Figure 6d). DOL/DME, of which the 
donicity is relatively low, dissolves only 0.8 m of Li2S8, whereas 
the high donor solvents DMSO, DMF, DMAc, and DMI dis-
solve 3.5–4.5 m of Li2S8. Especially, DMI is able to dissolve as 
much as 4.5 m. By taking this solubility into account, the specific 
energy of a cell was estimated (Figure 6e) based on the assump-
tion that sulfur loading at the cathode is 10  mg  cm−2 and the 
minimum amount of electrolyte required to dissolve polysulfide 
is introduced. Based on these results, the theoretically possible 
E/S ratio was extracted (Figure S17a, Supporting Information), 
and the attainable specific energy density was calculated accord-
ingly (Figure S17b, Supporting Information). Considering the 
theoretical capacity of sulfur, 1675  mAh  g−1, DOL/DME could 
achieve specific energy of 485 Wh kg−1, whereas DMI would be 
able to reach 1241 Wh kg−1. Assuming the typical sulfur capacity 
of 1000 mAh g−1, DOL/DME could achieve a specific energy of 
297 Wh kg−1, whereas DMI could deliver 791 Wh kg−1 (Figure 6e). 
These series of energy densities dictate the necessity of using 
high donor electrolytes to achieve the commercial target for the 
specific energy, namely 500 Wh kg−1.
Taking full advantage of the high theoretical capacity of ele-
mental sulfur in a practical cell setting of Li-S batteries would 
require various cell parameters, such as the amount of electrolyte, 
to be carefully designed. In this direction, high donor electrolytes, 
in which polysulfides are highly soluble, could be an invaluable 
approach to pursue as they permit lean electrolyte conditions, yet 
they allow sulfur to be fully utilized. The present study identified 
DMI as a promising high donor electrolyte solvent and demon-
strated its reasonable compatibility with the metallic Li counter 
electrode. As a demonstration of the feasibility of DMI-based elec-
trolytes, the addition of LiNO3 improves the cyclability markedly 
even under lean electrolyte conditions of 5 μLelectrolyte mgsulfur−1. We 
expect a variety of revolutionary approaches that recently emerged 
from research in the area of Li metal batteries to be implemented 
to further stabilize the Li metal interface and therefore improve 
the cycling performance of DMI-based Li-S cells.
