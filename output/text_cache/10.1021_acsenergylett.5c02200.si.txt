I. Experimental Section
1. Raw materials
1, 3-dioxolane (DOL), 1,2-dimethyloxyethane (DME), lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), lithium nitrate (LiNO3) and lithium sulfide 
(Li2S) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Sulfur (S8) was purchased from Alfa. 1,1,2,2-
terafluoro-3-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyoxy)-propane (TTE) was purchased from Synquest Labs. 
1,2-bis(2,2-difluoroethoxy) ethane (F4DEE) was purchased from Chemfish Tokyo. The 
solvents were dried overnight with activated 4 Å molecular sieves before usage. Li chips 
(15.6 mm in diameter and 200 µm in thickness) was purchased from American Elements.
2. Electrolyte preparation
The solvent mixtures of DOL/DME (1:1 v/v), DOL/F4DEE (4:1 v/v) and DOL/TTE (4:1 v/v) 
were prepared in advance. Four kinds of electrolyte solutions were then prepared: (1) For Li+ 
solvation study, 1.0 M LiTFSI was dissolved in each single/mixed solvent; (2) For LiPS 
solvation study, Li2S and S8 were mixed with a molar ratio 8:5 and then dissolved in varied 
solvents to reach Li2S6 nominal concentration of 0.1 M; (3) For Li||Carbon paper cell 
construction, 1.0 M LiTFSI and 0.1 M LiNO3 were dissolved in the solutions prepared in (2); 
(4) For Li||S cell construction, 1.0 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 were dissolved in varied 
solvents. All electrolyte preparation processes were performed in an argon-filled glove-box 
with controlled moisture and oxygen level below 1 ppm.
3. Characterization
19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy measurements were performed on 
Bruker Ultrasheild300. 1.0 wt% hexafluorobenzene (HFB, Sigma-Aldrich) in 
tetrahydrofuran-d8 (THF-d8, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as internal standard and sealed in 
coaxial capillary tubes. The samples were inserted out of the capillary. 
To construct Li||Cu cells, copper (Cu) foils were punched into disks with diameter of 15 mm, 
and Li chips were punched into disks with diameter of 12.7 mm. Li||Cu cells were assembled 
as 2032-coin cells with W-scope separator (single-layer PP) and an electrolyte amount of 50 
µL, and were then tested on a Neware BTS4000 battery cycler system. Li||Cu cells were 
tested with the Aurbach method, in which a Li plating/stripping formation cycle was first 
performed at 0.5 mA cm-2 for 10 h, followed by plating the Li reservoir at the same condition. 
Then 1 mAh cm-2 Li was stripped/plated for ten cycles before all the Li reservoir was fully 
stripped at the same current density. All the electrochemical stripping processes were 
conducted with cutoff voltage of 1 V vs. Li/Li+. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
tests, we constructed Li||Cu cells and plated 5 mAh cm-2 Li on Cu foil with a current density 
of 0.5 mA cm-2. Then the cells were disassembled in the argon-filled glovebox, and the Cu 
electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with anhydrous DME and allowed air-dry before examined 
on JSM-6610LV SEM system.

For X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests, a special design was applied (Figure S14). 
Two Cu disks with diameter of 3 mm were loaded on Li anode surface and cycled against 
sulfur cathode for 2 formation cycles at 0.05 C. Then the Cu disks were peeled off and rinsed 
with 50 μL DME for XPS analysis. XPS analysis was conducted on a PHI 5000 VersaProbe 
II system (Physical Electronics) with a base pressure of ∼2 × 10−9 torr. The spectra were 
obtained using an Al Kα radiation (hυ = 1486.6 eV) beam (100 μm, 25 W), with Ar+ and 
electron beam sample neutralization, in Fixed Analyzer Transmission mode with a pass 
energy of 11.75 eV. Subtracting a Shirley background and then fitting the spectra to multiple 
Gaussian peaks was performed on all spectra using CasaXPS from Casa Software Ltd. The 
area under the XPS peaks (the sum of the Gaussian components) was adjusted using 
manufacturer-calibrated relative sensitivity factors and normalized to obtain elemental 
concentrations. Binding energy was calibrated by aligning the C 1 s peak of C–C/C–H 
environments at 284.7 eV. 
Titration gas chromatography (TGC) was applied to measure the Li metal corrosion with 
LiPS. The calibration curve was obtained in advance following the reported procedure.1 Li 
metal chips with varied mass were weighed and seal in flasks with constant volume. We then 
injected 3 mL methanol into the sealed flask to digest Li metal. After complete reaction, 25 
μL gas in the flask was collected and injected into the GC column. The measured H2 peak 
area (proportional to H2 concentration) can be plotted versus Li mass and linearly fitted to get 
the calibration curve equation. For LiPS corrosion quantification, Li metal (whose pristine 
mass was weighted in advance) was soaked in solutions containing 0.1 M Li2S6 and varied 
solvents for 25 days. Then the soaked Li was taken out, and the remaining Li0 can be 
quantified using the same TGC procedure and the calibration curve equation.
4. Electrochemical evaluation
S cathodes were prepared with S/carbon/titanium nitrate (TiN) composite (at a weight ratio of 
78:11:11), carbon black (C45) and polyvinylidene fluoride at 80:10:10 weight ratio. The 
powder was dispersed in 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) solvent. The slurry was coated 
onto carbon-coated aluminum and dried. The cathode contains 62.2 wt% sulfur, with an 
average loading of 3.5 mgS cm-2.
The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) experiments were conducted on 
Neware cycler. For Li||Carbon paper (CP) GITT, 2032-coin cells were constructed with Li 
metal as anode, carbon paper (GDL340, punched into disks of 14 mm in diameter) as cathode 
and W-scope as separator. Electrolyte used in Li||CP GITT test contained 1.0 M LiTFSI, 0.1 
M LiNO3 and 0.1 M Li2S6 with varied solvents. 50 μL electrolyte was added in each cell. The 
GITT step includes a 6-minute galvanostatic discharging (or charging) at 0.12 mA cm-2, 
followed by a 30-minute rest. The cells were cycled first with GITT discharging steps until 
1.6 V, then with GITT charging steps until 2.6 V, finally with GITT discharging steps again 
until 1.6 V. For Li||C/S GITT, S cathodes with diameter of 14 mm were used as the working 
electrode. 2032-coin cells were assembled with W-scope separator, and certain amount of 
electrolyte (without LiPS) was added to control E/S = 10 µL mg-1. The GITT testing protocol

II. Computational Methods
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on the Cray cluster Theta of the 
Leadership Computing Facility at Argonne National Laboratory. The simulations were 
carried out in a high-performance mode with version 2.14 of NAMD2, a greatly scalable 
molecular dynamic program used to render an atom-by-atom representation of biomolecules, 
created at the University of Illinois. All electrolyte simulations adopted GAMMP force field3 
generated through GAAMP server on Laboratory Computing Resource Center at Argonne 
National Laboratory. The molar ratios of each molecular species in DOL-DME, DOL-F4DEE 
and DOL-TTE electrolyte with and without the existence of 0.1 M Li2S6 are listed in Table 
S1. The number of atoms for the simulated systems range between 81,830 and 100,000, and 
the dimension of each simulation cell is ~150Å×150Å×150Å. All simulations adopt Periodic 
Boundary Condition under constant NVT condition. The equations of motion were integrated 
with a 2-fs time step, using Langevin dynamics at a temperature of 300 K. To overcome the 
slow dynamics due to the high viscosity of the simulated systems, Hamiltonian simulated 
annealing method (HSA)4 was employed to accelerate the equilibration of MD trajectories. In 
each HSA simulation, 64 independent trajectories were generated, and all of them were used 
to get the statistical average of structural properties. Each trajectory lasts 20 ns, and the last 
10 ns was adopted to do statistical average. Snapshot of each trajectory was sampled at an 
interval of 100 ps, and thus for each simulated system, 6400 snapshots were adopted to 
guarantee high-fidelity sampling.

Note S1 
The PFG structure of F4DEE determines its high LiTFSI solubility but moderate Li+ 
solvation
LiTFSI solubility increases in solvents that either disperse LiTFSI more effectively or interact 
with Li+ more strongly. Specifically, solvents with high dielectric constant (ε) promote the 
dispersion of LiPS due to their stronger polarizability, whereas solvents with high donor 
number (DN) solvate LiPS strongly. 5, 6 Therefore, solvents with higher ε or DN tend to 
exhibit higher LiPS solubility. 
Compared to DME, the extra fluorine atoms in F4DEE show strong electron withdrawing 
effect, dispersing the electron density on oxygen coordination centers. Therefore, F4DEE 
shows lower electron donicity, resulting in weaker Li+ solvating power than DME. The 
electron density on TTE oxygen is even more dispersed because of even higher fluorination 
degree than F4DEE. Together with the absence of chelating effect from the glyme (-
OCH2CH2O-) backbone, the solvating power of TTE is further decreased (Figure S9a). 
These features determine the moderate Li+ solvating power of F4DEE. Meanwhile, the 
electron withdrawing effect of fluorine on -CF2H terminal groups increases the local polarity 
of F4DEE,7 resulting in increased LiTFSI solubility (Figure S9b). In all, the PFG structure 
endows F4DEE with high electronic polarizability but low electron donicity, resulting in high 
LiTFSI solubility but moderate Li+ solvation in F4DEE.

In agreement with previous studies, Li+ conductivity shows a volcano-shape relationship with 
LiTFSI concentration as demonstrated in DOL/DME solvent (Figure S9a).8 When LiTFSI 
concentration is relatively low, Li+ conductivity increases because of the increasing Li+ 
charge carriers. However, raising LiTFSI concentration closer to its solubility limit (like 3.0 
M) renders weakened Li+ solvation that decreases Li+ conductivity. Therefore, LiTFSI 
concentration is optimized to be 1.0 M to balance the Li+ concentration and Li+ solvation 
effects on ionic conductivity.
Figure S9. Effect of LiTFSI concentration and Li+ solvation on bulk electrolyte ionic

DOL/TTE.
The Li+ conductivities of electrolytes with 1.0 M LiTFSI and varied Li+ solvation reveal that 
Li+ conductivity generally increases with stronger Li+ solvation (Figure S9b). Other factors 
also influence Li+ conductivity. For example, the F4DEE single solvent electrolyte shows the 
lowest Li+ conductivity because of its high viscosity7 albeit similar Li+ solvating power as 
DOL. Despite these disparities, all electrolytes in our study possess high Li+ conductivity of > 
1.0 mS cm-1, already ensuring fast Li+ transport. Thus, the impact of Li+ conductivity in our 
study is relatively small, which will be further discussed in Note S3.

Note S3
Li anode and bulk electrolyte resistance show trivial impact on Li anode cyclability
Besides the SEI composition, there are other factors such as high bulk electrolyte Li+ 
conductivity and fast Li|electrolyte interfacial kinetics that influence Li stripping/plating 
stability. However, their contributions to Li cyclability are comparatively insignificant.
(1) Bulk electrolyte conductivity. All electrolytes exhibit Li+ conductivity above 1 mS cm-1 
(see Note S2), which is sufficient to support facile Li+ transport. Notably, the DOL/DME 
electrolyte, despite its superior Li+ conductivity, shows poorer Li anode cyclability than 
DOL/F4DEE. This indicates that the bulk conductivity is not the primary reason for the 
observed Li stability differences.
(2) Interfacial charge transfer kinetics. We assessed the Li|electrolyte interfacial kinetics 
using Li||Cu stripping/plating experiments. Figure S17 summarizes the voltage profiles with 
the DOL/DME, DOL/F4DEE, and DOL/TTE electrolytes, obtained via the Li||Cu Aurbach 
method. During the ten highlighted stripping/plating cycles (1 mAh cm-2, 0.5 mA cm-2), a 
lithium reservoir consistently remains on the Cu electrode. Therefore, the observed 
overpotential is attributed to the Li|electrolyte interfacial processes. All three electrolytes 
exhibit similarly low overpotentials (less than 20 mV), indicating that the impact of 
interfacial kinetics is negligible during lithium stripping/plating. 
Compared to the above effects, the effect of SEI composition on Li anode is significant. LiF 
is well known to regulate Li deposition because of its electrochemical stability and 
mechanical strength. Therefore, the LiF-rich SEI layer formed by the DOL/F4DEE 
electrolyte effectively suppresses Li dendrite growth, leading to enhanced Li cyclability.7, 9

Note S4
Suppressed lithium anode chemical corrosion with weak LiPS solvation
LiPS in Li-S batteries, especially long-chain species (x = 5 ~ 8 in Li2Sx), can chemically 
corrode Li anode, causing irreversible Li inventory loss.10, 11 Here we quantified the effect of 
LiPS solvation on this parasitic reaction via titration gas chromatography (TGC). Li metal 
chips were soaked in 1.0 mL LiPS solutions (0.1 M Li2S6 dissolved in DOL/DME, 
DOL/F4DEE or DOL/TTE) for 25 days. Given that ether solvents themselves are highly 
stable against Li metal, the Li corrosion loss during soaking should completely originate from 
parasitic chemical reactions between Li metal and LiPS. The corroded Li chips were then 
taken out to quantify the remaining Li mass via TGC method (see Experimental Section for 
more details).1 The standard calibration curve of Li metal mass versus H2 peak intensity from 
ethanol digestion was measured in advance (Figure S18a).

There are two major differences between the GITT voltage plateaus in Li||CP cells and 
Li||C/S full cells.
(1) The Li||CP cell does not have the discharge plateau at 2.35 V (stage I). 
(2) The Li||CP cell displays an extra discharge plateau at 1.8 V (stage III).
Difference (1) arises from the distinct initial active materials: LiPS in the Li||CP cell but S8 in 
the Li||C/S cell. The 2.3 V discharging plateau corresponds to the reduction of S8 to long-
chain LiPS (Eq. S3).12 This cannot occur in the Li||CP first discharging because of the 
absence of S8. 
S8 (s) + 2 Li+ + 2 e- → Li2S8 (sol)   (Eq. S3)
Difference (2) arises from the liquid-solid reduction of short-chain LiPS to Li2S (Eq. S4). 
Li2Sx (sol) + (2x-2) Li+ + (2x-2) e- → x Li2S (s) (x = 2 ~ 4)  (Eq. S4)
We propose that the cathode surface chemistry plays a pivotal role in this reaction because it 
provides the necessary sites for Li2S deposition. Solid products, either S8 or Li2S, always 
exist on the C/S cathode surface. These insulating species partially block the active sites, 
resulting in rapid polarization after the 2.0 V plateau. In comparison, fresh CP possesses 
more active sites for Li2S deposition, promoting the reduction reaction Eq. S4 at 1.8 V.
To illustrate how the surface chemistry of the CP cathode influences Li-S reaction dynamics, 
we performed a second GITT discharge on the Li||CP cell following a complete discharge 
and charge cycle (Figure S19). The second discharge begins with the 2.3 V plateau (stage I), 
which corresponds to the reduction of S8 to long-chain LiPS (Eq. S3). This is expected, as the 
final product after the first GITT charging is S8. Meanwhile, the 1.8 V plateau (stage III) 
turns into a slope-like feature during the second discharging, indicating the CP surface active 
sites are partially deactivated. Arguably, part of the Li2S irreversibly remain on the cathode 
surface after the first GITT cycle. This “dead” Li2S is insulating to both Li+ and electrons.13 
Therefore, the liquid-solid reduction on stage III is impeded during the second discharging, 
resulting in a diminished plateau.14 These features demonstrate that Li||CP and Li||C/S cells 
basically follow the identical redox pathway. However, the extent of the redox reactions is 
dictated by the initial active materials and the cathode surface chemistry.

strong LiPS solvation.

Li||CP and Li||C/S GITT results, the thermodynamic and kinetic features of LiPS redox 
remain consistent. Therefore, Li||CP GITT provides valuable insights that can be applied to 
deepen the fundamental understanding of Li||C/S full cells.
