ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries using Li2S and Li-
free anodes have emerged as a potential high-energy and safe
battery technology. Although the operation of Li−S full batteries
based on Li2S has been demonstrated at room temperature, their
effective use at a subzero temperature has not been realized due to
the low electrochemical utilization of Li2S. Here, ammonium
nitrate (NH4NO3) is introduced as a functional additive that
allows Li−S full batteries to operate at −10 °C. The polar N−H
bonds in the additive alter the activation pathway of Li2S by
inducing the dissolution of the Li2S surface. Then, Li2S with an
amorphized surface layer undergoes the modified activation
process, which consists of the disproportionation and direct
conversion reaction, through which Li2S is efficiently converted into S8. The Li−S full battery using NH4NO3 delivers a
reversible capacity and cycling stability over 400 cycles at −10 °C.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, low-temperature, Li2S cathode, full cell, electrolyte additive
E
lectric vehicles (EVs) using rechargeable batteries have
emerged as one of the primary technologies for the
2050 carbon neutral strategy of the international
communities toward a sustainable and green society.1 To
date, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) using intercalation-type
electrode materials such as nickel-rich LiNixCoyMzO2 (x ≥
0.6, M = Mn or Al) cathodes and graphite anodes have
dominated the EV battery market.2,3 Based on intercalation
chemistry, current LIBs deliver a high energy and excellent
lifetime with good safety. However, those intercalation-type
nickel-rich LiNixCoyMzO2 cathode materials have a relatively
low theoretical capacity limit of 274 mAh g−1. In addition, the
use of rare elements in the Earth’s crust, such as Ni and Co
metals, incurs sustainability and stability issues as a cost of their
increased energy density. Therefore, it is reasonably expected
that theoretical and/or practical limits of current LIBs
technologies will be reached in the near future.4−8
To mitigate these concerns as well as secure public demand
for EV battery markets, the development of a high-energy,
environmentally friendly, and low-cost battery system is
necessary. Based on the conversion reaction chemistry (2Li
+ S + 2e−↔Li2S), lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, as one of
the most promising alternatives to LIBs, are receiving rapidly
growing research interest owing to their high energy density,
low toxicity, and expected low price.9−14 Both elemental sulfur
(S8) and its fully lithiated state, Li2S, can be utilized as cathode
materials for Li−S batteries. The conventional Li−S batteries
using S8 as a cathode and metallic Li as an anode demonstrated
a high gravimetric theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh g−1 and
energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, more than 3 times higher
than that of current LIBs.15,16 However, conventional Li−S
batteries still cannot be free from safety issues because of the
use of metallic Li as an anode.17 In contrast, Li2S can be used
by pairing with metal-free anodes in Li−S batteries, which can
avoid the safety problems caused by employing a Li anode.18
In addition, Li2S as a fully lithiated state of S8 offers a
comparable high theoretical capacity of 1167 mAh g−1 and
shows an even more stable microstructure without the extent
of volume expansion during the charge−discharge process.18
However, the Li2S activation to S8 in the first charging process
turns out to be a formidable problem since it requires a high
overpotential due to the electrically insulating nature of Li2S
and loss of active mass by forming soluble lithium-polysulfides
(LiPSs) into the electrolyte solution.19−22 To mitigate those

issues, significant research efforts have been devoted to
identifying advanced Li2S-based cathodes for high-performance
Li−S batteries. In 2017, Zhou et al. introduced a series of solid
metal sulfide catalysts that can lower the energy barrier for Li2S
oxidation and simultaneously trap LiPSs during first charging
process.23 Recently, Tan et al. fabricated Li2S@graphene
nanocapsules by burning the lithium foil in CS2 vapor and
showed its potential use as a high Li2S loading cathode
material.24 Recently, our group designed an innovative
electrode design of the pelletized Li2S/graphene composite
cathode for high-performance and practical Li−S full
batteries.25 Based on the enhanced electronic conductivity by
graphene nanosheets and the formation of a metastable phase
under high-pressure application, Li2S in the composite cathode
can be effectively activated with minimal formation of soluble
lithium-polysulfides. Even under a high Li2S loading of over 10
mg cm−2, as a result, the composite cathode demonstrated
excellent electrochemical activities in both Li−S batteries using
metallic lithium and a graphite anode. Although impressive
advances in electrode design have been achieved so far, the
overall impression from all the previous works on Li2S-based
cathodes is limited to operating only at room temperature;
thus, they are not very encouraging for practical operation over
a wide temperature range.26−29 For the practical use of Li−S
batteries in EV applications, low-temperature operation is an
essential requirement anywhere around the world, regardless of
atmospheric temperature. Because the predominant electrolyte
solvents used in current Li−S batteries, such as 1,3-dioxolane
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane, have low freezing points, the Li2S-
based cathode can be utilized for low-temperature operation
once Li2S can be safely activated at the first delithiation
process. However, compared to room temperature, the low
temperature further retards the charge-transfer reaction
kinetics of Li2S at the first delithiation process, which
eventually makes it difficult to utilize and operate Li−S
batteries using a Li2S-based cathode. Due to the sluggish
delithiation kinetics, electrochemical full activation from Li2S
to S8 in conventional Li−S batteries has not yet succeeded at
−10 °C.
Herein, we introduce the electrolyte additive NH4NO3,
which plays a critical role in enabling the electrochemical
utilization of Li2S even at −10 °C. NH4
+ in the NH4NO3
additive strongly interacts with Li2S, modifying the delithiation
pathways, which consist of two-step characteristic processes; it
is interestingly observed that the first delithiation step involves
a transient disproportionation reaction and the second
delithiation step proceeds through direct conversion reaction.
These features can highly lower the activation potential barrier
of Li2S and subsequently trigger the effective utilization of Li2S
within the electrochemical stability window of the ethereal
electrolytes, even at −10 °C. As a result, the proposed Li−S
batteries with the NH4NO3 additive can survive and
demonstrate good capacity and cycling performances at −10
°C (Figure 1).
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Fabrication of Li−S Batteries with NH4NO3. Due to the
insulating nature of solid-phase Li2S, electrical conductivity is a
key parameter for enhancing the intrinsic kinetics of electron
transfer at the Li2S-based cathode to improve the electro-
chemical activities of the Li−S battery. Hence, the poor first
delithiation reaction of Li2S to S8 in the first charging process
(called first activation) is challenging for Li2S-based cathodes
to improve the electrochemical properties of Li−S batteries.
Undoubtedly, advanced cathode design critically affects the
delithiation kinetics of Li2S in the first cycle and the
subsequent electrochemical utilization of sulfur. In our
previous studies, we designed the Li2S/graphene composite
cathode through the high-pressure application of 1 GPa.25 The
compact 2D geometry, having an enhanced electrical network,
is the signature of the Li2S/graphene composite cathode. In
this study, we prepared a Li2S/graphene composite cathode

equilibrium of NO3
−anions in the electrolyte solution, we
prepared the modified electrolyte by adding the 0.25 M
NH4NO3 salt while simultaneously decreasing the LiNO3
concentration to 0.55 M in the baseline electrolyte (Figure
S4). In this study, we fabricated the Li−S cells comprising the
Li2S/graphene composite as cathode and 0.5 M LiTFSI + 0.55
M LiNO3 + 0.25 M NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 as
electrolyte with metallic Li or graphite as anode.
Functionality of NH4NO3 Additive: Computational
Studies. To gain insight into the functionality of using an
NH4NO3 additive in the proposed Li−S batteries, theoretical
studies using density functional theory (DFT) calculations
were performed. First, to understand the dissolution chemistry,
we simulated the dissolution process using 324 atoms that
consist of NH4NO3, Li2S, DME, DOL, and LiTFSI molecules,
and the results are shown in Figure 2a. The binding energy of
NH4+ and Li2S with the molecules was calculated to be −5.44
eV, indicating that the system is thermodynamically favorable.
We can observe the hydrogen bond interaction between NH4
+
and the surrounding molecules. Furthermore, oxygen-contain-
ing molecules contribute to the interaction with Li+. This
suggests a tendency of the solute and additive to dissolve in the
solvent. Valuable information related to the molecule
interactions was also obtained using molecular electrostatic
potential (MESP) map calculations. Particularly, the MESP
map may provide an understanding of the nature of chemical
addition, i.e., which molecule is most likely to exhibit
electrophilic or nucleophilic tendencies. Initially, we calculated
the MESP map for NH4NO3 after molecule relaxation (Figure
2b). The negative (red) and positive (blue) electrostatic
potential areas indicate strong tendencies for electrophilic and
nucleophilic reactions, respectively. It can be observed that
NO3
−tends to attract cations and the H+-containing molecule
prefers to bind with the anion. We then added the Li2S
molecule to NH4NO3 for further analysis (Figure 2c).
Interaction of the NO3
−anion with Li+ and S2
−with H+ was
observed. Furthermore, it is also expected that the interaction
between Li and S is weaker than that of their respective
interactions with the surrounding molecules, which in turn
facilitates Li−S bond breaking and finally leads to the
dissolution of the Li2S particle surface from the solid phase
to the liquid phase. Photographs of the dissolution test of Li2S
in the electrolyte solution (Figure S5) clearly show the
different colors because of the formation of liquid-phase Li2S

Functionality of NH4NO3 Additive: Experimental
Studies. To experimentally verify the functionality of
NH4NO3 improving the electrochemical properties, the Li−S
cells using a Li metal anode and the composite cathode with a
Li2S loading of 5 mg cm−2 were assembled with 0.5 M LiTFSI
+ 0.8 M LiNO3 (baseline) or 0.5 M LiTFSI + 0.55 M LiNO3 +
0.25 M NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 (v/v)
electrolytes (modified). The Li−S cells were initially tested
at a room temperature of 30 °C. Figure 3a and b show the
cyclic voltammogram (CV) of the Li−S cells at the first cycle
in the voltage range of 1.8−3.6 V. For the baseline electrolyte,
only one oxidation peak after 3.2 V, which corresponds to the
delithiation of Li2S in the composite cathode via direct
conversion reaction, can be observed21,22,25 (Figure 3a),
whereas upon using 0.25 M NH4NO3, two distinctive
oxidation peaks were identified during the first anodic scan
(Figure 3b). The first oxidation peak around 2.3 V in the
modified electrolyte was clear because of the charge transfer
reaction of the chemical oxidation of liquid-phase Li2S along
with the electrochemical reaction of Li2S. Subsequently, a
broad range of activation peaks for the electrochemical
oxidation of Li2S via direct conversion were observed (around
2.8 V) in the composite cathode. Here, it is interestingly
observed that the Li−S cell using the modified electrolyte also
participated in the direct conversion reaction. However, the
direct conversion with 0.25 M NH4NO3 showed a relatively
lower activation potential than the baseline electrolyte in the
first charging voltage profile (Figure 3c and d); this is because
a part of Li2S, which is an insulator, was readily activated in the
former step. In the following cathodic scan to 1.8 V (CV plots
in Figure 3a and b), the composite cathode showed the typical
electrochemical activities of sulfur reduction reaction with Li-
ions by forming long-chain (2.23 V) and short-chain lithium
polysulfides (1.85 V). Next, operando optical microscopy (o-
OM) was used to confirm the function of the NH4NO3
additive in forming liquid-phase Li2S and subsequently
triggering the activation at the low potential of the Li2S/
graphene composite cathode during delithiation. The Li2S/
graphene composite cathode was loaded on an aluminum mesh
and assembled using a customized electrochemical cell to
investigate optical characterization in the reflective mode
(Figure S6). The cells were electrochemically charged from
open circuit voltage (OCV) to 3.6 V (vs Li/Li+) at 1/3 C (1 C
= 1167 mAh g−1). Figure 3c−f show snapshots obtained from
the o-OM movie with the voltage profiles of the pelletized
Li2S/graphene composite cathode during the first charging
process. Without NH4NO3 additive, the composite cathode
experienced a high initial activation potential at approximately

TEM Analysis. Morphological changes in Li2S with/
without NH4NO3 additive during the first activation process
were investigated at the microscopic level using TEM (Figure
5). The Li2S/graphene composite cathodes were retrieved
from the Li−S cells with/without NH4NO3 additive after
galvanostatic charging to the marked point in Figure S8 and
Figure 6 under 0.1 C rate at room temperature and low
temperature (30 and −10 °C). (Because Li2S was not activated
at −10 °C without the additive as shown in Figure 6b and d,
Li2S charged with the additive was solely present.) As seen in
Figure 5a and c, Li2S charged in the baseline electrolyte
displayed the crystalline phase; however, Li2S charged in the
modified electrolyte at both 30 and −10 °C had an
amorphized surface owing to the activation of the liquefied
Li2S (Figure 5b, d, and e). These modified crystallographic
characteristics symbolize the delithiation process of Li2S with
the NH4NO3 additive by the formation of an amorphous LiPS
layer. The different spots for the TEM observation of the Li2S
surface mediated by NH4NO3 are presented in Figures S9−
S11, showing uniform chemical or electrochemical reactions.
Electrochemical Properties. Owing to the sluggish
reaction kinetics of the electrochemical conversion of Li2S to
S8 at the first activation charging process, the practical
utilization of low-temperature Li−S batteries using Li2S as
the cathode material has been an unsolved challenge so far. By
using a 0.25 M NH4NO3 additive, the successful operation of
the composite Li2S/graphene composite cathode at an
extremely low temperature of −10 °C (Figure 6a) highlights
the functionalities of the additive for practical Li−S batteries.
Figure 6 shows a comparison of the low-temperature (−10 °C)
electrochemical properties of the composite cathode with and
without 0.25 M NH4NO3 additive. Similar to the RT (30 °C)
data, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data
of the Li2S/graphene composite cathode with the NH4NO3
additive showed a lower resistance in the OCV state at −10 °C
(Figure S12). It can be reasonably inferred that interfacial
dissolution of Li2S through the chemical interaction with the
NH4NO3 additive lowered the resistance values of the Li2S
cathode, facilitating the initial delithiation from the Li2S
surface. From the CV plots at 0.1 mV S−1, no oxidation/
reduction reactions of the composite cathode were detected in
the baseline electrolyte during the initial two cycles (Figure
6b). In contrast, the composite cathode exhibited high
electrochemical activities with distinctive oxidation/reduction
peaks in the 0.25 M NH4NO3 containing electrolyte (Figure
6c). Even at −10 °C, strikingly, the 0.25 M NH4NO3 additive
enabled the two-step delithiation reaction of Li2S to S8 during
the first anodic scan. In the following first cathodic scan and
second anodic/cathodic scan, electrochemical sulfur reduc-
tion/oxidation reactions with Li+ ions were observed. As
shown in Figure 6d, the composite cathode exhibits no
electrochemical reactions in the baseline electrolyte. In
contrast, in the case of the 0.25 M NH4NO3 containing
electrolyte, the charge−discharge voltage profiles of the
composite cathode were almost identical (two-step delithiation
reaction) at −10 °C to that at room temperature (Figure 6e).
In addition, the Li2S particle after 25% charging (first
delithiation step), once the Li2S in the composite cathode
was activated to S8 by using the 0.25 M NH4NO3 containing
electrolyte, delivered a high discharge capacity of 978 mAh g−1
at 0.1 C owing to its physicochemical and structural
advantages. Although the electrochemical utilization of S8
was relatively low owing to the kinetic challenges at low
temperatures, the composite cathode exhibited a high
discharge capacity of 726 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C and long-term
cycling stability with a capacity retention of 78% over 300
cycles (Figure 6f and Figure S13). Notably, despite the
sluggish kinetics at −10 °C, the Li2S cathode with the modified
electrolyte using 0.25 M NH4NO3 was fully activated within
the stable voltage window; otherwise, capacity would not be
delivered or undesirable side reactions at high voltage (>4 V)
would have occurred38,39 (Figure S14). Meanwhile, the
common catalytic additive (e.g., TiS2 as demonstrated in the
previous studies23,40,41) can lower the energy barrier for Li2S
oxidation; however, it could not be effectively utilized due to
the contact limitation between the solids in the high-energy
Li2S cathode, in which the maximum amounts of Li2S are
placed in the minimum conductive matrix (Figure S15).
Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2e, the optimized structure of
Li2S adsorbed on the TiS2 surface with NH4NO3 suggests
favorable Li2S dissociation by NH4NO3 compared to TiS2. We
explored the superior function of the additive by calculating
the charge density difference (CDD) of the absorbed Li2S on
the TiS2 surface with the NH4NO3 additive. The significant
CDD surrounding the Li2S and NH4NO3 additives indicates
possible facile charge transfer and sturdy binding between Li
and O and N-containing molecules that readily accept
electrons. The 1.96 Å Li−O bond distance also indicates
stronger binding than that of Li−S with a 2.4 Å bond distance.
This can be attributed to the favorable dissociation of Li2S by
NH4NO3. Additional DFT calculations on the interaction of
Li2S with NH4NO3 and TiS2 (Figure S16) also suggested facile
Li2S dissociation by the NH4NO3. We report the successful
construction and operation of a safe Li−S full battery using a
composite cathode and graphite anodes that can survive the
extremely low operating temperature of −10 °C, making the
Li−S battery ready for field operation for practical applications.
For the Li−S full battery, we used the 2.5 M LiTFSI + 0.15 M
LiNO3 + 0.25 M NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 as
electrolyte solution.25,42 Surprisingly, the Li−S full battery
delivered a high discharge capacity of 736 mAh g−1 and stable
cycling with a high Coulomb efficiency over 400 cycles at 0.1 C
(Figure 6g). Overall, this finding is a breakthrough discovery
because Li2S can be activated and utilized even at −10 °C
directly through simple modulation of the electrolyte by
addition of 0.25 M NH4NO3. Meanwhile, as expected from the
better electrochemical utilization of Li2S to S8 in the first
charging process by the NH4NO3 additive, the composite
cathode showed a higher discharge capacity of 1250 mAh g−1
(0.1 C) and 1130 mAh g−1 (0.5 C) at 30 °C in the 0.25 M
NH4NO3 additive containing electrolyte (Figure S8). On the
other hand, the optimal composition of the NH4NO3 additive
was simply screened throughout the galvanostatic cycling test.

CONCLUSIONS
We have demonstrated the successful operation of Li−S full
batteries at −10 °C, through the introduction of the NH4NO3
additive in an electrolyte solution. The proposed NH4NO3
additive in a conventional electrolyte solution was possibly able
to induce homogeneous catalytic reactions in the entire Li2S
cathode. The key role of NH4NO3 is to trigger the formation
of liquid-phase Li2S, which has a relatively weaker interaction
between Li and S than that of the solid phase because of the
strong interaction between NH4
+ and S2
−. This modifies the
delithiation pathways, which in turn facilitates Li−S bond
breaking and eventually results in facile Li2S oxidation at −10
°C. Consequently, the Li2S/graphene composite cathode
delivered a high energy of 1075.7 Wh kg−1 (calculation details
are in Table S1) and exhibited stable cycling at −10 °C in both
half and full batteries. This is a breakthrough discovery because
a small fraction of NH4NO3 additive completely activates the
Li−S battery to survive under subzero temperatures, which has
never been reported previously. We believe that these findings
provide insights into the practical viability of Li−S batteries
that can be used anywhere in the world, regardless of
atmospheric temperature.
EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Electrolyte Preparation. Bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide
(LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.95%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, Alfa
Aesar, 99.999%), and ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3, Sigma-Aldrich,
99.999%) were dried at 110 °C under vacuum for 3 days before use.
Dimethoxyethane (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%) and dioxolane (Sigma-
Aldrich, 99.8%) were purified using a sieve (Sigma-Aldrich, 4 Å) until
the water content was below 10 ppm, as measured using a Karl
Fischer titrator (Mettler Toledo, C20). The baseline electrolyte
consisted of 0.5 M LiTFSI and 0.8 M LiNO3 dissolved in DME:DOL
= 1:1 (v/v), and the modified electrolyte consisted of 0.5 M LiTFSI +
0.55 M LiNO3 + 0.25 M NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 (v/
v). To alleviate the Li metal issues in high-loading Li−S batteries,
relatively high contents of LiNO3 were used to compose the baseline
electrolyte as per our previous research.25,43,44 For comparison,
electrolytes with different compositions of 0.5 M LiTFSI + 0.7 M
LiNO3 + 0.1 M NH4NO3 and 0.5 M LiTFSI + 0.4 M LiNO3 + 0.4 M
NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 (v/v) were prepared.
Fabrication of Li2S/Graphene Composite Cathode. The Li2S
powder (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.98%) and graphene (Acros, single layer)
were dried at 150 °C under a vacuum for 24 h before use. To prepare
the Li2S/graphene composite, powders with a mass ratio of 75:25
(Li2S:graphene) were homogeneously mixed for 30 min using the
mechanical ball milling method in an Ar atmosphere. The prepared
composite powder was pelletized at 1 GPa for 30 min in a mold with a
diameter of 10 mm. The target loading was 5 mg cm−2, and the
margin of error toward the target was below 0.1 mg cm−2. All the
cathode fabrication processes were conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox
with O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm. For the pouch-type cell,
the same procedure was conducted to prepare the cathode using a
rectangular mold with a size of 3 cm × 5 cm.
Fabrication of the Graphite Anode. A slurry comprising
graphite (POSCO Chem. Inc.), carbon black (Super P, TIMCAL),
and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA, Sigma-Aldrich, average Mv ∼450,000 g
mol−1) in a mass ratio of 92:1:7 was coated onto the Cu foil and fully
dried at 110 °C under a vacuum for 12 h before use. The N/P ratio
was set to 1.2:1.
Fabrication of the Pouch-Type Cell. A Li2S/graphene
composite cathode with a size of 3 × 5 cm was attached to the Al
foil current collector. Li foil (Honzo, 200 μm) with a size of 3.1 × 5.1
cm on Cu foil was used as an anode. An Al tap for the cathode and a
Ni tap for the anode were used. The cell assembly was performed by
using the winding method. The amount of electrolyte to the Li2S
weight ratio was controlled at 12 μL mg−1. All the fabrication
processes were performed in an Ar-filled glovebox.
Electrochemical Tests. For the typical cycling and CV test, a
CR2032 coin-type cell made up of a Li2S/graphene composite
cathode with a Li2S loading of 5 mg cm−2, polypropylene (PP)
separator (Celgard 2400, Celgard), and Li foil (Honzo, 200 μm) was
used as the anode. For the full cell using a graphite anode, the
electrolyte composed of 2.5 M LiTFSI + 0.15 M LiNO3 + 0.25 M
NH4NO3 dissolved in DME:DOL = 1:1 (v/v) was prepared. To
ensure the stable cycling of the graphite anode in ether-based
electrolyte, highly concentrated salt was used to weaken the Li+−
solvent interaction and thus hinder the co-intercalation of Li+−solvent
into the graphite anode.42 The electrolyte amount to Li2S weight ratio
was controlled at 12 μL mg−1 for typical coin-type cell tests. To assess
the feasibility of the proposed concept, the electrolyte amount to Li2S
weight ratio was controlled at 6 μL mg−1, and these values are also
denoted in each figure. All the coin-type cell fabrications were
performed in an Ar-filled glovebox. For the galvanostatic cycling test,
the cells were initially charged to 3.6 V for activation of Li2S and
subsequently discharged to 1.9 V (at 30 °C) and 1.8 V (at −10 °C).
The cycling cutoff voltage was 1.8−2.8 V for the half cell test and
1.6−2.8 V for the full cell test. Each cycling condition of the C-rate (1
C: 1675 mAh g−1) and cutoff voltage is denoted in the figures. The
galvanostatic cycling tests were conducted by using a TOSCAT-
3100U apparatus (TOYO SYSTEM). The specific capacity and
current density were calculated based on the weight of sulfur. CV tests
were conducted using VMP3 (BioLogic Science Instruments) in the
voltage range of 1.8−3.6 V during the first cycle and 1.8−2.8 V during
the second cycle at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. In situ EIS tests were
performed by using VMP3 in the OCV state and during the first
charging process. The applied frequency range was 100 kHz to 0.05
Hz, and the amplitude was set to ±5 mV.
Characterization. The morphologies of the materials and the
cross-section of the cathode were analyzed using SEM (Verios G4UC,
Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TEM (NEO ARM, JEOL). All the
samples were prepared in an Ar-filled glovebox and carried using an
Ar-filled container to prevent contamination. The samples were
unsealed and quickly transferred to the chamber for the SEM and
TEM analysis. High-resolution XRD (HR-XRD, Empyrean, PAN-
alytical) was performed in the 2θ range of 10−80° with a step width
of 0.03°. The XRD samples were sealed using ketone tape to avoid
deterioration during analysis.

performed using a specialized cell with an optical window (ECC-
Opto-Std, EL-CELL). The inspection via high-resolution OM (VHX-
7000) and Raman spectroscopy with a 532 nm laser (DXR3xi,
Thermo Fisher Scientific) was conducted through the window on the

Computational Method. First-principles calculations based on
DFT were performed using the Quantum-Espresso program with the
projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential and Perdew−

cutoff energy of 49.69 Ry (675 eV) with C.pbe-n-kjpaw_p-
sl.1.0.0.UPF, H.pbe-kjpaw_psl.1.0.0.UPF, F.pbe-n-kjpaw_p-
sl.1.0.0.UPF, N.pbe-n-kjpaw_psl.1.0.0.UPF, Li.pbe-sl-kjpaw_p-
sl.1.0.0.UPF, O.pbe-n-kjpaw_psl.1.0.0.UPF, and S.pbe-nl-kjpaw_p-
sl.1.0.0.UPF pseudopotentials for C, H, F, N, Li, O, and S,
respectively, were used. For NH4NO3 and Li2S electrostatic potential
map calculations, a cutoff energy of 30 Ry (408 eV) was used with the
pseudopotentials of O.pbe-rrkjus.UPF, N.pbe-rrkjus.UPF, H.pbe-
rrkjus.UPF, Li.pbe-n-van.UPF, and S.pbe-van_bm.UPF for the
pseudopotentials of O, N, H, Li, and S, respectively. The molecules
were optimized using the Broyden−Fletcher−Goldfarb−Shanno
(BFGS) algorithm with a convergence threshold of 0.5 × 10−6 Ry,
and the Brillouin zones were sampled using a gamma point, allowing
the atoms to move. To simulate the melted Li2S structure, ab initio
molecular dynamics implemented in the Vienna ab initio Simulation
Package (VASP) code was used.47 Initially, a crystalline Li2S supercell
model containing 64 and 32 atoms of Li and S, respectively, was built.
The supercell was then annealed to 2000 K by using an NVT
ensemble with a Nose−Hoover thermostat for a simulation time of
150 fs. Finally, to visualize the calculation results, the VESTA program
was used.48
