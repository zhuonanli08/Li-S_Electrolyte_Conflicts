A B S T R A C T
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs), with their ultra-high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), are considered 
one of the most attractive high-energy batteries. However, the “shuttling effect” of polysulfides and the un­
controlled growth of lithium (Li) dendrites pose significant challenges to the practical implementation of LSBs. 
Herein, a lightweight and eco-friendly diethoxydimethylsilane (DEMS) is chosen as a multi-functional solvent for 
LSBs. The low polarity of DEMS promotes the “solid–solid” conversion of sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) 
during charge–discharge processes, eliminating the shuttle effect of polysulfides. Moreover, the DEMS-based 
electrolyte enables highly reversible Li plating/stripping processes across a wide temperature range spanning 
from −20 to 60 ◦C. As a result, the Li/SPAN batteries using the DEMS electrolyte exhibit excellent cyclic stability 
at 0.2 C with retainable capacities of 524.4 mAh/g after 200 cycles, 598.8 mAh/g after 100 cycles, and 318.6 
mAh/g after 50 cycles at 26, 60 and −20 ◦C, respectively. This study aims to identify low-cost and highly stable 
electrolytes through solvent screening to enhance the electrochemical performance of LSBs.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) have emerged as a compelling tech­
nology in the realm of energy storage due to their ultra-high energy 
density of 2600 Wh kg−1 and the availability, cost-effectiveness, and 
environmental friendliness of sulfur [1–5]. However, the implementa­
tion of LSBs remains challenging due to a spectrum of issues, including 
the insulating nature of sulfur and lithium sulfides, the large volume 
changes of sulfur cathodes upon charging/discharging, the shuttling 
effect of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), and the uncontrolled growth of Li 
dendrites [6–8]. Various approaches have been proposed to address 
these challenges and enhance the performance of LSBs, including the 
construction of advanced hosts for sulfur [9–18], the deployment of 
multifunctional separators or interlayers [19–22], the formulation of 
novel electrolytes [8,16,23–30], and the development of stable lithium 
metal anodes [31–35].
Among various sulfur cathodes, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) 
stands out due to its high electronic conductivity, easy scalability in 
preparation, and low production cost [36]. Notably, short-chain sulfur 
species are covalently bonded to the conductive backbone of pyrolyzed 
PAN in SPAN, ensuring an excellent sulfur utilization rate compared to 
other sulfur cathodes [37]. Despite these advancements, utilizing SPAN 
as the cathode material for LSBs poses a significant challenge because of 
the incompatibility between existing electrolytes and both the Li anodes 
and SPAN cathodes. In conventional glyme-based ether electrolytes, the 
generation and migration of polysulfides during the conversion of SPAN 
initiate unfavorable side reactions, leading to rapid capacity attenuation 
during charge/discharge processes, even though these electrolytes 
exhibit good compatibility with Li metal [4,38]. While SPAN demon­
strates excellent cyclic stability in carbonate electrolytes, the poor 
reversibility of Li and the pronounced growth of Li dendrites not only 
degrade the battery’s electrochemical performance but also introduce 
safety risks [39]. Hence, there is a pressing need to develop cutting-edge 
electrolytes capable of stabilizing both the Li anode and sulfur cathode.
Recently, considerable efforts have been dedicated to advancing 
LSBs through the engineering of liquid electrolytes[40,41]. High- 
concentrate electrolytes (HCEs) and localized high-concentration elec­
trolytes (LHCEs) have been intensively researched because they cannot

dendrite growth but also reduce the solubility of LiPSs [42,43]. Despite 
their ability to deliver promising performance, these electrolytes exhibit 
notable drawbacks in practical applications. Specifically, the extensive 
use of Li salts and the increased viscosity render HCEs challenging to 
implement. Similarly, the adoption of heavily fluorinated diluent in 
LHCEs results in a significant rise in electrolyte costs and imposes 
environmental burdens. Moreover, both HCEs and LHCEs have a high 
density (>1.4 g mL−1), which compromises the overall energy density of 
LSBs [44]. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop novel electrolytes 
that are environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and still possess good 
compatibility with both Li metal anode and sulfur cathodes to advance 
the commercialization of practical LSBs.
Herein, we present a weakly solvating electrolyte with low cost and 
density for Li|SPAN cell, utilizing an eco-friendly, cost-effective, and 
lightweight solvent, diethoxydimethylsilane (DEMS). DEMS not only 
exhibits low solvating power due to its low dielectric constant of 3.2, but 
also features low melting (−87 ◦C) and high boiling (113 ◦C) points, as 
well as a low density (0.865 g cm−3), making it a promising solvent for 
LSB electrolytes [45]. The electrolyte of 2 M LiFSI-DEMS (referred to as 
DEMS), comprising a single salt and single solvent, enables the forma­
tion of a stable SEI on Li metal to improve Li plating/stripping revers­
ibility. Furthermore, it effectively stabilizes the SPAN cathode by 
inhibiting the dissolution of LiPSs. As a result, the DEMS electrolyte 
enables high Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiencies (CEs) of 
approximately 99.0 %, 98.7 %, and 96.5 % at 26 ◦C, −20 ◦C, and 60 ◦C, 
respectively, under a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 
1.0mAh cm−2. The assembled Li|SPAN cells deliver a reversible capacity 
of 524.1 mAh/g over 200 cycles without capacity degradation at 0.2 C 
and 26 ◦C. Additionally, the DEMS electrolyte is also compatible with 
both Li metal anodes and SPAN cathode at low (−20 ◦C) and high 
(60 ◦C) temperatures.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of electrolytes
Dimethoxyethane (DME) and lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide 
(LiFSI) were purchased from DodoChem. DEMS, ethylene carbonate 
(EC), and propylene carbonate (PC) were provided by Aladdin. Before 
the preparation of various electrolytes, LiFSI was pre-dried at 80 ◦C for 
12 h, and the specific solvents including DEMS, DME, and EC/PC (v/v =
1/1) were dehydrated using 4 Å molecular sieves. All electrolytes were 
prepared in Ar-filled glove box (O2 < 0.01 ppm, H2O < 0.01 ppm). The 2 
M electrolyte solutions were prepared by dissolving 2.0 mmol LiFSI salt 
in 1.0 mL of corresponding solvents at 26 ◦C. The electrolytes of 2 M 
LiFSI-DEMS, 2 M LiFSI-DME, and 2 M LiFSI-EC/PC are denoted as DEMS, 
DME, and EC/PC, respectively.
2.2. Characterizations
The solvation structures of different electrolytes and the chemical 
structure of sulfur and SPAN were analyzed using Raman spectroscopy 
(DXR2xi). The morphologies of deposited Li on Cu foil in various elec­
trolytes were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
Hitachi 8100). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements 
were carried out on ESCALAB 250 Xi (Thermo Fisher). The sulfur con­
tent of SPAN was examined using an elemental analyzer (Vario Micro 
cube).
2.3. Electrochemical measurements
All batteries were assembled in CR2025-type coin cells for electro­
chemical tests. All cells were assembled inside a glove box filled with 
ultrapure Ar (≥99.999 %). A piece of Celgard 2500 was employed as the 
separator, and 75 μL of the specific electrolyte was used. Linear sweep 
voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on a CHI 760 electrochemical 
workstation at a scan rate of 1 mV/s using Li/stainless steel (SS) cells. 
The charge–discharge processes of Li/Cu, Li/Li, and Li/SPAN cells were 
tested using the LANHE CT3001A battery testing system. The ionic 
conductivities of different electrolytes were measured by electro­
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) within a frequency range from 
100 kHz to 0.1 Hz using a symmetric coin cell blocked with SS elec­
trodes. The results are calculated based on the following equation: σ =
d/(R*S), where σ, d, R, and S are the ionic conductivity, the thickness of 
the separator, the area of the electrode, and the impedance, respectively. 
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of Li||SPAN cells were measured using 
an Admiral Squidstat Plus electrochemical workstation between 1.0 and 
3.0  V at a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s.
2.4. DFT calculations
All quantum chemical calculations in this work were conducted 
using density functional theory (DFT) with the B3LYP functional with 
the 6–311G++(d,p) basis set via Gaussian 09 software. The binding 
energy (Eb) of complexes was determined using the following equation: 
Eb = Etotal – EA – Esolvent, where Etotal represents the total energy of the 
complexes, EA denotes the energy of the particular species (Li+ or Li2S4), 
and Esolvent accounts for the solvent molecules. The Counterpoise 
keyword was employed to eliminate basis set superposition error [46].
3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Physicochemical and electrochemical properties
The characteristics of selected solvents, such as EC, PC, DME, and 
DEMS, have been studied to gain insights into their fundamental char­
acteristics. Fig. 1a illustrates the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the 
target solvents, namely, EC, PC, DME, and DEMS. The proximity of the 
LUMO and HOMO energy levels of DEMS to those of DME suggests the 
high compatibility of DEMS with Li metal. For comparison, the lower 
LUMO energy levels of EC and PC compared to DME and DEMS indicate 
that the conventional carbonate solvents exhibit greater reactivity with 
the Li anode, potentially contributing to the inherently low CEs achieved 
in carbonate electrolytes. The solvating capabilities of the solvents of 
interest were evaluated using DFT. The electrostatic potential (ESP) 
analysis of EC, PC, DME, and DEMS reveals that oxygen sites exhibit 
higher electronegativity for binding with Li+ in all solvents (Fig. S1). 
Moreover, the binding energies (Eb) for EC–Li+, PC–Li+, DME–Li+, and 
DEMS–Li+ complexes are calculated to be −2.24, −2.28, −2.78, and 
−2.70 eV, respectively (Fig. 1b).
As a crucial parameter for an electrolyte, the ionic conductivities of 
DEMS electrolytes at varying salt concentrations were measured. LiFSI 
was chosen for its high solubility even in weakly solvating solvents. As 
shown in Fig. 1c and Fig. S2, the 2 M electrolyte exhibits the highest 
ionic conductivity of 1.35 mS cm−1. Therefore, the concentration of 
LiFSI was kept at 2 M across all electrolytes of interest to ensure a 
consistent comparison. LSV measurements were conducted to investi­
gate the electrochemical windows of DEMS, DME, and EC/PC electro­
lytes using Li|SS cells (Fig. 1d). Among these, the DME electrolyte 
displays the earliest decomposition with an onset potential of 4.66 V, 
whereas DEMS and EC/PC electrolytes exhibit enhanced oxidation 
resistance, reaching 4.86 and 4.98 V. Given that the electrolyte typically 
constitutes the heaviest component in practical LSBs, the densities of the 
three electrolytes were measured. As shown in Fig. S3, the densities of 
DEMS, DME, and PC/EC electrolytes were recorded at 1.10, 1.13, and 
1.43 g mL−1, respectively. Although HCEs and LHCEs have notably 
improved LSB efficacy, their densities surpassing 1.4 g mL−1 inevitably 
compromise the energy density of LSBs [44].
The solvation structures of three electrolytes were studied using 
Raman measurements (Fig. 1e). The S–N–S bending peaks of the FSI−
anions in the EC/PC and DME electrolytes are located at 720.8 and

722.6 cm−1, respectively, indicating that a substantial portion of the 
FSI−anions are not coordinated with Li+ ions. Therefore, in the presence 
of strongly solvating solvents like DME, PC, and EC, Li+ ions tend to 
primarily associate with the solvent molecules, fostering the formation 
of solvent-separated ion pairs within the primary solvation structure 
[47], in agreement with the DFT calculations. In contrast, the S–N–S 
peak of FSI−anions displays a noticeable blue shift to 749.2 cm−1 in the 
DEMS electrolyte. This shift can be attributed to the weak solvating 
capability of the DEMS solvent, which inadequately dissociates LiFSI, 
resulting in the prevalence of contact ion pairs and aggregates within the 
electrolyte. It is worth noting that a high degree of Li-anion association 
significantly enhances the initiation of the reduction potential of the Li+- 
anion complex, thereby contributing to the anion-derived SEIs [37].
In addition to the aforementioned characteristics, an ideal electrolyte 
for LSBs should also inhibit the appreciable dissolution of LiPSs. 
Therefore, the solubility of 0.1 M Li2S6 in DEMS, DME, and EC/PC sol­
vents was investigated by dissolving/dispersing stoichiometric quanti­
ties of Li2S and S in these solvents. As depicted in Fig. 1f, the DEMS 
solution maintained its colorless and transparent appearance after 24 h, 
with yellow sediments composed of a Li2S and S blend. Conversely, the 
DME control sample quickly transitioned to a yellowish hue after 6 h 
(Fig. S4), darkening to a deep red-brown shade after 24 h, indicating 
significant dissolution of LiPSs. The stark contrast suggests that the 
DEMS solvent can effectively impede the dissolution and reaction of 
LiPSs. The PC/EC solution remained transparent, suggesting that LiPS 
formation was neglectable in conventional carbonate solvents. To 
further understand the interaction of LiPSs with different solvents, the Eb 
values of Li2S4 with different solvents were calculated using DFT 
(Fig. 1g). Notably, the Eb of the Li2S4–DME complex was calculated to be 
−1.25 eV, which is more negative than that of Li2S4–EC, Li2S4–PC, and 
Li2S4–DEMS counterparts. These findings confirm that LiPSs are more 
prone to dissolution in DME, consistent with the polysulfide solubility 
test.
3.2. Li plating/stripping performance
Li plating/stripping CEs were examined using Li/Cu cells at a current 
density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2. As depicted in 
Fig. 2a, the CEs in the EC/PC electrolyte were approximately 95 % 
within the first 50 cycles, sharply decreasing to < 50 % after 80 cycles 
(Fig. S5). This significant fluctuation in CEs can be ascribed to the un­
even plating/stripping of Li metal, resulting in the formation of massive 
dead Li [48]. For comparison, both the DME and DEMS electrolytes 
exhibited excellent Li plating/stripping reversibility, evidenced by high 
CEs approaching 99 %. It is noteworthy that the initial CE achieved with 
the DME electrolyte was only 73.0 %, considerably lower than the 96.1 
% obtained with the DEMS electrolyte (Fig. 2b and Fig. S6). These 
outcomes underline the outstanding compatibility between the Li metal 
anode and the DEMS electrolyte, facilitating high and consistent Li 
plating/stripping CEs. Moreover, Li/Li symmetric cells with different 
electrolytes were also evaluated to investigate interfacial stability at 0.5 
mA cm−2 with a cutoff capacity of 0.5mAh cm−2 (Fig. 2c). The Li/Li cell 
with the DEMS electrolyte exhibited excellent cyclic stability over 1000 
h and maintained a stable polarization voltage of 38 mV, surpassing the 
EC/PC counterpart. Of note, the DME electrolyte also showed excellent 
compatibility with Li metal, as evidenced by the stable charge–discharge 
curves. Therefore, the DME electrolyte can be an ideal control group to 
study the side effects of LiPS shuttling, as will be discussed later.

different electrolytes were examined via SEM. A Li deposition of 2 mAh

3.3. Electrochemical performance of Li/SPAN batteries
The electrochemical performance of LSBs using the proposed DEMS 
electrolyte and the SPAN cathode was further assessed. SPAN was pre­
pared based on our previous work [38], which possesses an average size 
of approximately 100 nm (Fig. S7). Fig. S8 shows the Raman spectra of 
SPAN and sulfur powder, confirming the successful fabrication of SPAN. 
In addition, elemental analysis (Table S1) indicates the SPAN contains 
~ 39.4 wt% sulfur. The cyclic performance was measured at a char­
ge–discharge rate of 0.2 C (1 C = 1000 mA g−1) and 26◦C following three 
formation cycles at 0.1 C. As shown in Fig. 3a, the cell with the DEMS 
electrolyte delivered an initial capacity of 513.4 mAh/g and maintained 
524.4 mAh/g after 200 cycles at 0.2 C, accompanied by remarkably high 
CEs of approximately 99.9 %. In stark contrast, the Li/SPAN batteries 
employing the DME electrolyte showed rapid capacity decay, dropping 
from 428.1 mAh/g at the 1st cycle to 148.1 mAh/g at the 25th cycle. 
Considering the excellent Li reversibility in the DME electrolyte, the 
dramatic capacity degradation can be attributed to the continuous 
dissolution of LiPSs during charge–discharge processes. While the Li/ 
SPAN cells using the EC/PC electrolyte displayed good cyclic stability 
with a capacity retention rate of 94.3 %, the reversible capacity at the 
200th cycle was around 466.6 mAh/g. Fig. 3b-d shows the char­
ge–discharge profiles of the 1st, 100th, and 200th cycles. Notably, sig­
nificant capacity degradation was observed in the DME electrolyte, 
accompanied by a large voltage hysteresis. Conversely, cells using the 
DEMS and EC/PC electrolytes maintained good reversibility, with 
voltage gaps of 0.43 and 0.57 V, respectively, at the 200th cycle.

Fig. S9 compares the fifth CV curves of Li|SPAN full cells using 
different electrolytes at a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s. Full cells with EC/PC 
and DEMS electrolytes exhibited a typical “solid–solid” transition, 
characterized by two reduction peaks at ~ 2.00 and 1.70 V, and an 
oxidation peak at ~ 2.40 V. In contrast, the Li|SPAN cell using DME

the formation of insoluble Li2S/Li2S2. Importantly, the oxidation peak at 
~ 2.53 V suggested the transition from polysulfides back to sulfur, 
indicating the occurrence of “solid–liquid” conversion in the DME 
electrolyte. Therefore, Li/SPAN batteries employing DEMS electrolyte 
not only have a high specific capacity but also exhibit superior cyclic 
stability at room temperature (26 ◦C). The rate capability of Li/SPAN 
cells using the DEMS electrolyte was also tested, as presented in Fig. 3e 
and 3f. The cells employing the DEMS electrolyte were found to output 
535.1, 510.4, 463.6, and 275.3 mAh/g at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 C, 
respectively (Fig. 3f). To further assess the feasibility of DEMS in prac­
tical applications, the cyclic performance of Li/SPAN full cells with a 
limited N/P ratio of 4 was evaluated at 0.5 C. As shown in Fig. S10, the 
full cell using the DEMS electrolyte maintained a high capacity retention 
rate of 80.0 % after 100 cycles at 0.5 C. In sharp contrast, the 
corresponding retention rates for EC/PC and DME electrolytes were only 
34.1 % and 22.3 %, respectively.
3.4. Wide-temperature performance
Regarding the wide liquid range of the DEMS solvent, the DEMS 
electrolyte is anticipated to be well-suited for applications spanning a 
diverse temperature spectrum. Fig. 4a and 4b showcase the CEs of Li 
plating/stripping at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 
1.0 mAh cm−2 at 60 and −20 ◦C, respectively. Notably, the DEMS 
electrolyte exhibited remarkable CEs exceeding 99 % with a minor 
voltage hysteresis of ~ 0.1 V over 150 cycles at 60 ◦C (Fig. S11). 
Meanwhile, the DEMS electrolyte also maintained stable CEs of 
approximately 96 % after 50 cycles at −20 ◦C (Fig. S12). Furthermore, 
the interfacial stability of Li/Li symmetric cells employing the DEMS 
electrolyte was further evaluated at a current density/capacity of 0.5 mA 
cm−2/0.5 mAh cm−2. As displayed in Fig. 4c, the DEMS electrolyte 
facilitated stable Li plating/stripping at 60 ◦C for 500 h, accompanied by 
a minimal polarization voltage of around 20 mV. At −20 ◦C, the Li/Li 
symmetric cells operated reliably for 250 h with an overpotential of 
0.11 V (Fig. 4d). Additionally, the cyclic performance of Li/SPAN cells at

4. Conclusion
In conclusion, a DEMS electrolyte has been developed for Li/SPAN 
batteries to operate across a wide temperature range. The weakly sol­
vating nature of the DEMS solvent facilitates an anion-rich solvation 
structure within the electrolyte, hence achieving impressive plating/ 
stripping CEs of approximately 99 % at both 26 ◦C and 60 ◦C. In addi­
tion, the DEMS solvent, known for its low polarity, demonstrates limited 
polysulfide solubility, effectively mitigating the shuttling effect of LiPSs 
and extending the lifespan of Li/SPAN batteries. As a result, the 
assembled Li/SPAN cells with the DEMS electrolyte maintained a 
reversible capacity of 524.4 mAh/g after 200 cycles at 0.2 C and 26 ◦C, 
exhibiting negligible capacity degradation. Additionally, the DEMS 
electrolyte yielded remarkable Li plating/stripping CEs and high ca­
pacities for Li/SPAN cells at both low (−20 ◦C) and high (60 ◦C) tem­
peratures. Our work provides a practical electrolyte design to advance 
high-energy–density and long-life LSBs.
