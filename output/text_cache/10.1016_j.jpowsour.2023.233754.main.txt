Tuning the localized dissolution environment of polysulfides for practical 
Li-S cells

A B S T R A C T  
The shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides is one of the main reasons for the poor cycling performance, which greatly impedes the practical application of lithium-sulfur 
(Li-S) batteries. In this context, we report a novel design of sulfur cathode structure by regulating the distribution of PEO on the surface of C/S particles to extend the 
stable cycle life of practical Li-S cells. The in-situ dissolution of PEO changes the localized solution environment around C/S particles and suspends the diffusion of 
polysulfides (LiPSs), while the large number of mesopores retained inside C/S particles can ensure the good infiltration of electrolyte and act as a container for LiPSs. 
With this strategy, the cycle performance of the high sulfur loading (~4 mg cm−2) Li-S pouch cell is significantly improved, with a discharge-specific capacity above 
900 mAh g−1 and a capacity retention rate of 91.68% for 70 cycles. In addition, the rate performance of pouch cells is quite significant due to the promotion of local 
kinetic conversion (736.4mAh g−1 at 2C). At last, the Ah-scale Li-S pouch cell exhibits excellent capacity (above 1000 mAh g−1), low fading (0.16% per cycle), and 
high power capability. This work offers a new perspective for the design of sulfur cathode structures and promotes the scale-up of lithium-sulfur cells for practical 
applications.

1. Introduction 
In recent years, with the maturity of lithium-ion battery technology, 
the development of electric vehicles and mobile electronics has 
advanced rapidly. However, due to the limited energy density of tradi­
tional lithium-ion cells based on ion insertion mechanism, the further 
development of emerging electronic devices is severely delayed. In this 
context, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery with high theoretical energy den­
sity (2600 Wh kg−1), is called upon to appear on the stage as the next 
generation of the energy storage system [1]. However, its commercial­
ization still faces a series of challenges such as the shuttle effect of sol­
uble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs, Li2Sx, 2< x ≤8) [2,3], insulation 
properties of S and insoluble sulfides (Li2S2 and Li2S) [4,5], and inherent 
dendrite growth of lithium metal anodes [6–12]. 
Among the mentioned challenges, the shuttle effect is major obstacle 
that contribute to the continues capacity loss in Li-S batteries. Currently, 
the mainstream Li-S cells use an ether electrolyte with DOL and DME as 
binary solvents. Based on the “solid-liquid-solid” reaction processes in 
such electrolyte system, LiPSs will continuously migrate between the 
positive and negative electrodes and a series of complex side reactions 
will occur, nominating, the shuttle effect [13,14]. Although soluble 
LiPSs positively promote reaction kinetics, their constant shuttling leads 
to a continuous loss of positive substances and passivation of negative 
anode, which greatly decrease the capacity and lifetime of Li-S batteries. 
Typically, the formation of the shuttle effect involves the following five 
steps: (1) formation of long-chain polysulfides, (2) detachment of pol­
ysulfides from the cathode conducting surface, (3) dissolution of poly­
sulfides into the electrolyte, (4) migration of polysulfides to the lithium 
anode side, and (5) reaction of polysulfides with lithium anode [15,16]. 
Numerous strategies have been reported targeting the different 
stages of the shuttle effect. For example, the use of micro mesopores [17, 
18] or cathode coating [19–21] can physically limit the diffusion of 
long-chain LiPSs, while introducing chemical adsorption sites [22–28] 
into the cathode can prevent the detachment of LiPSs from the positive 
conductive surface. From a different angle, reducing E/S [29] or opti­
mizing electrolyte solvent [30,31] are effective ways to reduce the 
amount of LiPSs dissolved. In addition, the use of sulfur-containing 
polymers [32] as cathode active materials is another very promising 
strategy to avoid the shuttle effect. Finally, optimizing SEI or con­
structing artificial SEI to prevent contact reactions between Li and LiPSs 
serves as the last roadblock for the shuttle effect [33]. 
Compared with conventional coin cells, in the pursuit of high energy 
density pouch cells, with the increase of sulfur load, N/P decrease, and 
E/S decrease, most strategies mentioned above that work well in coin 
cells cannot be directly extended to pouch or the suppression of shuttle 
effect in pouch cells is no longer as effective [34,35]. In recent years, 
with the continued enthusiasm for Li-S battery research, researchers pay 
more attention to the research of l Li-S pouch cells that are more in line 
with practical applications and solve the key technical problems that 
hinder their practical application. 
In this context, we deliberately designed a sulfur cathode polymer 
coating strategy with a " Sugar-coated” structure, as shown in Fig. 1b, in 
which, PEO polymer does not enter the inner of C/S composite particle 
(ECP600-JD&S), but only distributes in the outer layer (nominated as 
PEO@C/S), without the need to introduce a large number of polymers. 
Such structure is different with that shown in Fig. 1c (nominated as PEO- 
C/S), in which the prolonged slurry mixing process will cause the 
blocking of inner channels by PEO. When the electrolyte infiltrates the 
cathode, the dissolved PEO provides a PEO-rich electrolyte environment 
around the C/S, and the resistance of LiPSs to migrate away from the 
cathode to the anode is greatly increased due to the reduced mobility 
caused by the increased viscosity of the local electrolyte environment 
and the interaction between PEO molecules and LiPSs. In addition, since 
PEO is distributed only on the outside of the C/S composite, the large 
number of mesopores retained inside the C/S composite still remains, 
ensuring that the entire interior of the C/S composite is well wetted by 
the electrolyte. The mesopores can also serve as containers for the LiPSs 
conversion and accommodate the volume expansion of sulfur. Due to the 
synergistic effects within the designed coating structure, the Li-S pouch 
cell exhibits high sulfur utilization, significantly improved rate perfor­
mance and cycling stability at a sulfur loading of 4 mg cm−2, paving the 
way towards future applications.

2.2. Localized solvation environment 
According to the BET study above, we further investigate the influ­
ence of the dissolved polymer to the localized solvation environment of 
LiPSs. To visualize the process, we made a polymer film and immersed it 
into the solvent, demonstrating the fast dissolution of PEO when con­
tacting the electrolyte (Fig. S4). To mimic the localized solvation envi­
ronment after dissolution, we conducted the viscosity and ion 
conductivity tests using electrolytes with and without 2 wt% PEO. As 
shown in Table S2, the viscosity of the W/O PEO electrolyte is only 1.62 
cP with the ion conductivity of 1.108 mS cm−1, while that of the PEO 
dissolved electrolyte is 25.9 cP with the slightly lower ion conductivity 
of 0.969 mS cm−1. To gain a deeper understanding of the influence of 
introducting PEO towards the solvation and migration of LiPSs in the 
electrolyte, Raman spectroscopy was used to characterize different LiPSs 
containing electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 3a and Fig. S5, the electrolyte 
containing LiPSs exhibits characteristic peaks at 535, 400 and 367 cm−1,

2.3. Electrochemical performances 
Encouraged by the above observations, we proceeded to validate the 
practicality of the PEO@C/S cathode by assembling pouch cells with 
dimension of 50 mm × 60 mm, as shown in Fig. 4a. The thermal 
gravimetric analysis (TGA) results show that the sulfur content in the C/ 
S composite after thermal treatment is 80% (Fig. S6). Unlike the coin cell 
configuration with massively excessive electrolyte (E/S ratio higher 
than 30), the Li-S pouch cell features much reduced electrolyte 
requirement (E/S ratio lower than 10). However, the reduced amount of 
electrolyte dramatically increases the localized concentrations of LiPSs, 
resulting in large concentration gradient as impetus for a more severe 
shuttle effect. As expected, the controlled sample without PEO (Fig. 4b) 
shows rapid capacity decay from 998.7 mAh g−1 (after 3 activation 
cycles) to 662.3 mAh g−1 within 41cycles (corresponding to 63.5% 
retention, and a decay rate of 0.89% per cycle). By contrast, the 
PEO@C/S cathode shows much improved cycle performance for over 70 
cycles (94.8% retention) with a capacity decay of 0.074% per cycle. In 
addition, other cathodes with different PEO content were tested. As 
shown in Fig. S7, the PEO0.5@C/S and PEO1.5@C/S cathodes with the 
same preparation process as PEO@C/S(PEO1.0@C/S) show inferior 
performances than PEO@C/S. Furthermore, the pouch cell with 
PEO@C/S cathode exhibits exceptional rate performance. As shown in 
Fig. 4c, the Li–S pouch cell with PEO@C/S cathode exhibits specific

2.4. Performances in practical applications 
To demonstrate the practicality of the proposed strategy, we also 
assembled multi-layer Ah-scale lithium-sulfur pouch cells with our pilot- 
production line, as shown in Fig. 5a and Fig. S16, with detailed 
parameters in Table S3. These cells inherit the outstanding performance 
observed in the single-layer pouch cells, exhibiting the typical double 
discharge plateau characteristic of Li-S batteries during cycling at 0.2C, 
showing a high specific capacity of 1084.9 mAh g−1 at 4th discharge 
(Fig. 5b). The pouch cell demonstrates stable cycling for over 50 cycles, 
with a capacity retention rate of 89.15% (Fig. 5c). Such pouch cell can 
be used as the power source for drones, supporting the climbing, hov­
ering and landing operations (Supplementary Video S1). Here, the 
average power for the drone is 17.9 W, while the peak power reaches 
18.58 W, giving a power density of 1133 Wh kg−1(Fig. S17 and Sup­
plementary Video S2). In addition, for different applications, we also 
assembled 376.6 Wh kg−1 energy density pouch cells for cellphone 
charging (Fig. 5e and Fig. S18). The designed cathode can also be used in 
18650 cylindrical cells, as shown in Fig. 5f, which exhibited high ca­
pacity and great rate performance even the E/S rate is as low as 3.3 
(Fig. S19). The capacity of the cell was 2.08Ah (0.05C) and can work at 
0.4C. The exceptional performance of both the Ah-scale lithium-sulfur 
pouch cell and the 18650 cylindrical lithium-sulfur cell highlights their 
significant potential for practical applications. 
3. Conclusions 
In summary, a PEO@C/S cathode with PEO-coated C/S composite 
particles was prepared by incorporating a certain amount of PEO during 
the cathode slurry preparation process followed by short-term blending. 
When contacting electrolyte, the dissolution of external PEO in the 
PEO@C/S cathode not only exposes the abundant internal voids 
retained within the C/S composite particles, promoting thorough elec­
trolyte infiltration throughout the cathode, but also increases the 
localized electrolyte viscosity and reduces its fluidity. Moreover, the 
interaction between PEO molecules and LiPSs significantly hinders the 
diffusion of LiPSs. Through the combined effect of these two aspects, the 
high-loading lithium-sulfur pouch cell employing the PEO@C/S cathode 
demonstrates excellent performance in terms of high capacity, high rate 
capability, and long-term cycling stability. Notably, such strategy is 
highly suitable for large-scale production, as demonstrated by our pilot- 
production line which supports Ah-level pouch cells for practical ap­
plications. Therefore, this novel design for sulfur cathodes holds great 
promise as a strategy for high-performance application-oriented Li-S 
batteries.

4. Experimental section 
4.1. Materials synthesis 
Preparation of cathodes: S powder and ECP600 JD were mixed in a 
mass ratio of 7:3 or 8:2 (7:3 for coin cell and 8:2 for pouch cell and 
18650 cylindrical cell, respectively) and underwent hydrothermal 
treatment at 155 ◦C for 10 h to form the S/C composite. All cathodes 
were prepared by casting the slurries onto carbon-coated aluminum foils 
and first dried in a convection oven at 65 ◦C for 30 min to remove the 
majority of the solvent. Subsequently, it is transferred to a vacuum oven 
set at 50 ◦C and left overnight for further drying. The slurry of cathodes 
without PEO was prepared by mixing S/C composite and 5 wt % PVDF 
solution (NMP as solvent) for 1 min first, and then adding NMP to adjust 
the viscosity and followed by further mixing for 9 min. The mass ratio of 
S/C composite and PVDF in the mixture is 10:1.5. The slurry of PEO@C/ 
S cathodes was prepared by mixing S/C composite, 5 wt % PEO solution 
(acetonitrile as solvent), and 5 wt % PVDF solution for 1 min first, and 
then add NMP to adjust the viscosity and continue mixing for 1 min. The 
mass ratio of S/C composite, PVDF and PEO in the mixture is 10:1.5:1. 
The preparation of PEO-C/S cathodes is similar to PEO@C/S, except that 
the 2nd mixing time is 9 min. The areal sulfur loading is about 1 mg 
cm−2 for coin cells and 3.5–4.5 mg cm−2 for pouch cells. 
BET test: Powder samples are scraped from the corresponding pos­
itive electrodes, then underwent vacuum drying at 50 ◦C for 12 h to 
eliminate moisture content. For the washing experiments, after being 
immersed in the DOL + DME (1:1) mixed solvent for 24 h, the PEO@C/S 
and PEO-C/S samples were washed three times with the DOL + DME 
mixed solvent before proceeding to the vacuum drying step, in order to 
effectively remove the dissolved PEO. 
Preparation of polysulfide solutions: 0.2 M Li2S8 were prepared 
by mixing Li2S and S8 powder in particular molar proportion of 8:7. The 
powder mixture was then added to DOL + DME mixed solvent and 
stirrer for 24 h at 60 ◦C. 
Preparation of PEO film: First, dissolve PEO in acetonitrile solvent to 
obtain a 10 wt% solution, then pour it into a polytetrafluoroethylene 
square dish, and then peel the PEO film off from the polytetrafluoro­
ethylene dish after vacuum drying at 50 ◦C. 
Cell assembly, electrochemical measurements and applications. 
Electrochemical test: The assembly of lithium-sulfur coin battery is 
carried out in an argon-filled glove box with O2 and H2O contents below 
0.1 ppm, and using a 2025 coin battery, polypropylene (PP) separator 
(Celgard 2400), lithium metal anode (thickness 500 μm), and 40 μL 
electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2- 
dimethoxyethane (DME) (1/1 v/v) with 2% LiNO3). All cells were 
stood for 8 h before operation. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were 
measured by a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation within a voltage 
window from 1.5 to 2.7 V at a scan rate of 0.04 mV s. 
The Li-S pouch cells contained Celgard 2400 as the separator, 160 μm 
lithium foils as anodes and the size of the electrode is 5 cm × 6 cm. The 
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio was 6 mL g−1. The Ah-level Li–S pouch cell 
adopts a multi-stack form, in which the electrode size is 5 cm × 8 cm, the 
lithium metal thickness is 100 μm, and the E/S is 5. All the assembly 
processes are carried out in a dry room (dew point < −60 ◦C). The 
galvanostatic discharge and charge of cells between 1.7 and 2.5 V at 0.1, 
0.2, 0.5C, and 1.5–2.7 V at 1C, 2C and 3C on a LAND CT2001A battery 
test system (Wuhan, China). The Li–S pouch cells were standing for 20 h 
before operating. All performances were carried out at 25 ◦C. The spe­
cific capacity was calculated according to the mass of sulfur. Electro­
chemical impedance spectrum (EIS) of Li-S pouch cells was examined 
using the CHI 604E electrochemical workstation in the frequency range 
of 10−1–105 Hz by applying a 5 mV ac oscillation. When using lithium- 
sulfur pouch cells as the power source for drones, considering the 
drone’s rated voltage of 4.2V, we connected two pouch cells in series to 
form a battery pack with a voltage of 4.6V to power the drone. But, in the 
case of charging a cellphone using pouch cells with an energy density of 
381.2 Wh kg−1, we first employed a DC boost module (DM13–4.2) to 
step up the voltage from 2.3V to 4.2V, enabling the charging of the 
mobile phone. 
Characterization: Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were 
captured by Hitachi S-4800. XRD of electrode samples was conducted on 
a HighResolution Powder X-Ray Diffractometer (Smartlab, RIGAKU) 
with a Cu Kα radiation source at a scan rate of 5◦min−1. Surface-element 
analysis was performed by means of an energy-dispersive X-ray fluo­
rescence spectrometer (EDX). The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) 
were collected with Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 spectrometer from 
400 to 4000 cm−1. The specific surface areas were studied using the 
Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) equation based on the N2 adsop­
tion–desorption measurement (ASAP2460). The pore-size distribution 
was calculated by the Barret-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. In the BET 
test, Powder samples are scraped from the corresponding positive 
electrodes, then underwent vacuum drying at 50 ◦C for 12 h to eliminate 
moisture content. For the washing experiments, after being immersed in 
the DOL + DME (1:1) mixed solvent for 24 h, the PEO@C/S and PEO-C/ 
S samples were washed three times r with the DOL + DME mixed solvent 
before proceeding to the vacuum drying step, in order to effectively 
remove the dissolved PEO. The viscosity test is measured by the rota­
tional viscometer Brookfield DV-II+ Pro. The Raman spectra were 
recorded with a Raman microscope (Horiba iHR550, 622 nm, 200–1200 
cm−1). TGA was tested by a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG 209 F1, 
NETZSCH, Germany) ranging from 25 to 800 ◦C with a heating rate of 
10 ◦C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere.
