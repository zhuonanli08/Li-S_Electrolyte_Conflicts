Additives in the electrolytes of Li-S batteries aim to increase overall capacity, improve Liþ ion conductiv-
ity, enhance cyclability, and mitigate the shuttle effect, which is one of the major issues of this system.
Here, the use of water as an additive in the commonly used electrolyte, 1.0 M LiTFSI/1.0% (w/w) LiNO3
and a 1:1 mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) was investigated. We used
Co2Mn0.5Al0.5O4 (CMA) as an electrocatalyst anchored on an activated carbon (AC) electrode with added
sulfur via a melt-diffusion process. The structural analysis of CMA via Rietveld reﬁnement showed inter-
atomic spaces that can promote ionic conductivity, facilitating Liþ ion migration. Electrochemical tests
determined 1600 ppm as the optimal water concentration, signiﬁcantly reducing the shuttle effect.
Post-mortem XPS analysis focused on the lithium metal anode revealed the formation of Li2O layers in
dry samples and LiOH in wet samples. Better capacity was observed in wet samples, which can be attrib-
uted to the superior ionic conductivity of LiOH at the electrode/electrolyte interface, surpassing that of
Li2O by 12 times. Finally, Operando FTIR experiments provided real-time insights into electrolyte degra-
dation and SEI formation, elucidating the activity mechanisms of water and Li2CO3 over the cycles. This
work presents results that could aid future advancements in Li-S battery technology, offering possibilities
to mitigate its challenges with inexpensive and scalable additives.

1. Introduction
The ongoing global advancements in technology are driven by
the increasing need for improved energy storage solutions in por-
table electronic devices, electric cars, and smart power grids [1]. In
this context, high-speciﬁc energy batteries, such as the lithium-
sulfur (Li-S), have garnered more interest over the past decade
because of their impressive theoretical capacity of 1675 mA h g-1
[2–4]. Notably, this remarkable capability surpasses existing
cathode materials used in lithium-ion batteries. It typically ranges
from about 100 to 250 mA h g-1 [4]. Sulfur also exhibits impressive
properties, including natural abundance, ecological properties, and
proﬁtability [5]. However, Li-S batteries have some disadvantages,
such as low conductivity and slow electrochemical kinetics, which
affect their energy storage properties. In addition, during the
charging and discharging process, the anode of Li-S batteries is
usually quenched rapidly due to the formation of high- and
medium-order lithium polysulﬁde in the electrolyte (LPS) (for

increase [1,6]. Another problem in Li-S batteries is the shuttle
effect observed when low-order LPS (Li2Sx, 2 ≥x ≥1) is deposited
on the separator and cathode material [7,8]. The shuttle effect in Li-
S batteries involves the movement of polysulﬁde ions (formed dur-
ing the discharge process) from the cathode to the anode. These
polysulﬁde ions can dissolve into the electrolyte and diffuse to
the anode, where they may react with lithium and form solid
lithium sulﬁde. When the battery undergoes the next charge cycle,
these solid lithium sulﬁde deposits may not fully dissolve into the
electrolyte. Instead, they can form undesirable structures, such as
dendrites or deposits on the lithium anode.
The term shuttle effect was pioneered by Akridge et al. [9]. The
authors conducted a thorough quantitative examination of the
shuttle effect on Li-S batteries. In this work, experimental results
were used to develop a model to describe the charging process,
capacity during charge and discharge cycles, safeguards against
overcharging, thermal inﬂuences, and self-discharge mechanisms.
Papers then published previously described the shuttle effect
mainly in a qualitative manner [10,11]. However, evidence of such
an effect has already been observed. Chronologically, Giggenbach
et al. [12] examined the equilibrium distribution and the optical
spectra of polysulﬁde ions in a water-based solution at the temper-
ature of 20 C. According to the authors [12], the pentasulﬁde ion
was the sole species capable of dissolving in water without under-
going disproportionation. Soon after, Brummer et al. [13] studied
the formation of lithium polysulﬁdes in aprotic media. According
to the authors [13], in speciﬁc aprotic environments, the produc-
tion of Li2S can be achieved through either the electrochemical
reduction of S8 or through the direct in situ reaction of S8 with Li
or Li2S. In addition, the order of dissolved polysulﬁdes is inﬂuenced
by the dielectric constant of the solvent, with lower dielectric con-
stant values promoting the formation of long chains. Both studies
[12,13] focused attention on spectroscopy rather than electro-
chemical study. Rauh et al. [14] ﬁrst studied electrochemically a
lithium dissolved sulfur battery using organic electrolyte. Accord-
ing to the authors [14], the poor discharge efﬁciencies were related
to some soluble polysulﬁde, S2
n , which ‘‘escaped” from the current
collector of the cathode and underwent self-discharge on the
lithium anode. Dan et al. [15] ﬁrst studied the water contamination
in rechargeable Li batteries. According to the authors [15], hydro-
gen gas is produced in Li batteries when the electrolyte solution
is contaminated with water at the cathode. They also observed that
discharging the cell with a wet cathode stops hydrogen evolution.
When the concentration of water surpasses 1000 ppm, the stability
of lithium in a solution is insufﬁcient to reliably hinder a substan-
tial reaction between lithium and water, resulting in the liberation
of hydrogen. Given that the volume of the solution in practical bat-
teries is typically limited, contamination with H2O can exceed the
critical concentration, leading to the passivation of the lithium
anode. Consequently, the cells may experience a signiﬁcant
build-up of H2 pressure. Other authors have also investigated the
effect of doped water levels in Li batteries, such as the enhancing
effect of H2O on the charge and discharge cycle performance of Li
metal anodes [16] and lithium deposition without dendrite forma-
tion by incorporating minimal amounts of water as an electrolyte
additive [17]. However, none of these studies considered the effect
of water additive levels on Li-S batteries. The effect of aqueous
electrolytes on Li-S batteries was ﬁrst reported by Wu et al. [18].
According to the authors [18], the charge and discharge proﬁles
from the conventional electrolyte (1.0 mol L-1 LiTFSI DOL/DME),
varying water concentrations are different. This suggests that there
is a relation between water dopping and alterations in the shuttle
effect. Using XPS, the authors [18] noted the formation of lithium
hydroxide (LiOH) on the surface of the lithium anode within wet
electrolytes. Additional researchers indicated the formation of this
layer due to the presence of water on the electrolyte of Li-based
batteries [19]. The composition of the SEI ﬁlm can be customized
to achieve advantageous cycling behavior in the electrolyte-
containing H2O. Based on the decrease of the shuttle effect from
different amounts of H2O, it is imperative to consider water pres-
ence on the solvent and cathode materials when optimizing bat-
tery cycling performance. Other mechanisms for overcoming the
shuttle effect are described in the literature, such as the engineer-
ing of advanced membranes and separators [11,20,21].
The importance of water in inhibiting the shuttle effect in Li-S
batteries, as well as its water level, was described previously. To
the best of our knowledge, only one article [18] described the study
of water-containing electrolytes on Li-S batteries. However, to
obtain a decreased shuttle effect, the water-containing electrolyte
strategy can synergistically be coupled with a catalyst dispersed
into cathode Li-S electrode materials. Miranda et al. [22] pioneered
the study, the synthesis, and reactor application of Co2Mn0.5Al0.5O4
spinel oxide dispersed into carbon nanotubes (CNT) cathode elec-
trode materials. The Co2Mn0.5Al0.5O4 demonstrates high efﬁciency
in catalyzing H2O2, achieving a maximum performance (>96%)
when utilizing 70% H2O2. Additionally, this catalyst has shown pro-
mise in Li–O2 batteries, exhibiting improved cyclability at lower
overpotentials and high activity in peroxide decomposition. Addi-
tionally, Santos et al. [23] investigated the performance of CMAs
applied to lithium-sulfur batteries, employing activated carbon
(AC) KURARAY 80F as carbon electrode hosts. These electrodes
were treated via a melt-diffusion process to incorporate sulfur S8
into their structure, thus forming the activated carbon sulfur elec-
trodes (AC-S). A distinctive feature of the AC as a host material is its
high surface area, which is approximately 2366 m2/g, making it
ideal for CMA anchoring [8]. Therefore, the rational design of Li-S
battery using water additive level coupled to Co2Mn0.5Al0.5O4 spi-
nel oxide dispersed into AC-S cathode electrode material is pivotal
from a fundamental and application point of view.
Considering the above, this paper presents, for the ﬁrst time, the
rational and optimal water additive level combined with spinel-
based electrode materials to enhance the suppression of the shut-
tle effect in Li-S batteries. Here, the electrochemical activity of the
Li-S battery was investigated, and the formation of chemical spe-
cies was conﬁrmed by operando FTIR electrochemical analysis.
Furthermore, structural and crystallographic characterizations
were performed to unveil the phenomena behind the observed
performance shifts.

2.3. Operando FTIR characterization
The operando Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy
experiment was performed using a commercial FTIR spectrometer
(Cary 670, Agilent Technologies) in the mid-IR range (4000–
400 cm-1) with IR illumination from a Globar source and a Peltier
cooled DLaTGS detector at the Imbuia beamline in the Brazilian
Synchrotron Light Laboratory (LNLS)/Brazilian Center for Research
in Energy and Materials (CNPEM). Each spectrum was acquired
from 256 averages and with 16 cm-1 spectral resolution. In the
operando analysis, 205 spectra were collected at a rate of ∼98 s
per spectrum. The measurements were carried out in transmission
mode with a cell case with two ZnSe windows. The Li-S battery
(LSB) was composed by a porous carbon paper working electrode
(Toray Industries, Inc), glass-ﬁber separator (Whatman®), 30 lL
of 1.0 mol L-1 LITFSI + 1.0% LiNO3 in DOL-DME electrolyte and
Li-metal as anode. The battery was tested in a cyclic voltammo-
gram from 1.7 to 2.7 V at a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s. In this way,
the complete experiment comprised 102 cathodic and 103 anodic
datapoints.

their performance as electrocatalyst.

3.2. Electrochemical results
Galvanostatic discharge curves (GDC) play a crucial role in the
study of Li-S batteries. In this sense, galvanostatic discharge curves
obtained for AC-S-CMA, AC-S-CMA1200, AC-S-CMA1600, and AC-S-
CMA2000 electrode materials can be noticed in Fig. 3(a).
In its open-circuit state, the Li-S battery demonstrates its peak
voltage, referred to as the open-circuit voltage (Uoc∼2.7 V), as
depicted in the inset of Fig. 3(a). This voltage aligns directly with
the discrepancy between the electrochemical potentials of the Li
anode (la) and the S cathode (lc). Upon discharge, sulfur (S)
undergoes a two-electron reduction process with lithium (Li),
resulting in the formation of polysulﬁde intermediates (Li2Sx,
x=2–8), culminating in the production of Li sulﬁde (Li2S) at dis-
charge’s conclusion. A concurrent rise in cathode and electrochem-
ical potential occurs until the battery reaches its terminal voltage
(U, typically < 1.5 V). Charging, the reverse process, is initiated
by applying an external electric ﬁeld with a speciﬁc potential dif-
ference, prompting the decomposition of Li2S back into Li and S.
Throughout this phase, the cathode’s electrochemical potential
gradually declines until the battery voltage returns to Uoc. How-
ever, due to exhaustive examination and lack of mechanistic study
of the shuttle effect, only the discharge process was analyzed.
Under standard conditions, sulfur exists as ring-like octatonic
molecules with a chemical formula of S8, as depicted in the inset
of Fig. 3(a). During discharge, cyclo-S8 undergoes a stepwise reduc-
tion by Li, manifesting in three distinct segments [49,50]. Segment
I (from Uoc to approximately 2.0 V, featuring a minor plateau at
around 2.35 V) corresponds to the conversion from S8 to Li2S4
[50–54].

It was observed in Fig. 3(a) that increasing the water-doped
amount up to 1600 ppm (AC-S-CMA1600) minimizes the shuttle
effect. However, the role of water in the shuttle effect in Li-S bat-
teries is multifaceted and not fully understood. Water can inﬂu-
ence the shuttle effect through its impact on various aspects of
the electrochemical processes of the battery such as the solubility
of polysulﬁdes. High solubility of polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte can
increase the shuttle effect. These soluble species can migrate
between the electrodes, leading to capacity loss and reduced cycle
life. Moreover, the reaction between lithium polysulﬁdes and
water can result in the formation of lithium hydroxide LiOH. LiOH
is an insulating material that can accumulate on the surface of the
electrodes, creating a barrier that hinders the efﬁcient movement
of ions during battery cycling. This can contribute to increased
impedance and reduced overall battery performance. In addition,
water may contribute to the corrosion of certain electrode materi-
als in Li-S batteries. Corrosion can accelerate the degradation of
electrodes and increase the likelihood of the shuttle effect by pro-
moting the release of soluble polysulﬁdes. In this sense, cyclic
voltammograms were performed to study the whole electrochem-
ical process related to Li-S batteries [17,18,57–64].
The cyclic voltammograms of AC-S-CMA, AC-S-CMA1200, AC-S-
CMA1600, and AC-S-CMA2000 electrode materials are depicted in
Fig. 3(b). The redox process corresponds with the CVs of a typical
Li-S battery [17,18,57–61]. Towards anodic sweep, the oxidation
peaks (anodic AI = ∼2.35 V and AII = ∼2.45 V) for AC-S-CMA corre-
spond to the cell charge process. In the next step, there is a rever-
sible formation of sulfur in the solid state [8]. Along the cathodic
sweep, the reduction peak (cathodic CI = ∼2.30 V) in AC-S-CMA
electrode materials results from the solid-state reduction of the
sulfur present in the electrode, subsequently converting to the liq-
uid state forming the lithium polysulﬁde in the electrolyte (e.g.,
Li2Sx, where 8 ≥x ≥4) [65]. This stage is marked by an increased
solubility of sulfur species and a decrease in viscosity. The voltam-
metric peak CII around ∼1.96 V is proposed to originate for the
reduction of high-order polysulﬁdes to intermediate polysulﬁdes
(e.g., Li2Sx, where 4 ≥x ≥2), indicating the potential for multiple
faradaic reactions involving various sulfur species in the presence
of Li-ions [3]. The redox band observed at approximately 1.7–2.0 V
is commonly linked with the formation of low-order polysulﬁdes,
indicating the generation of insoluble species during discharge.
As a result, a viscosity two-phase solution is formed, known as
catholyte [7].

5. Conclusions
Aiming to enhance the performance of Li-S batteries, our study
investigated the inﬂuence of water additives at various levels as a
strategic means to suppress the shuttle effect. Additionally, we
explored the impact of incorporating the electrode composite
material Co2Mn0.5Al0.5O4 (CMA) as an additive. The investigation
commenced with the analysis of the morphological properties of
the electrode and the structural properties of the CMA spinel using
Rietveld reﬁnement, followed by electrochemical tests and ﬁnally,
post-mortem and operando FTIR analyses to elucidate degradation
mechanisms and water activity. Based on our study, we highlight
the next key ﬁndings: The analysis of electron density maps,
speciﬁcally for the hkl-(311) lattice parameter, revealed the
absence of electrons between the Mn and Al atoms. This absence
of electron density presents a promising avenue for Liþ-diffusion
into CMA spinel crystal structures, establishing CMA as an ionic
conductor. These channels are signiﬁcant for Liþ migration during
battery cycling, resulting in a notable increase in system capacity.
Electrochemical tests determined 1600 ppm as the optimal water
level, showing a substantial reduction in the shuttle effect. Higher
water levels showed the potential to further enhance the system’s
capacity. Post-mortem XPS analysis highlighted distinct trends,
with dry samples favoring the formation of Li2O and wet samples
favoring the formation of LiOH. The high ionic conductivity of
LiOH, which exceeds that of Li2O by a factor of 12, supported the
observed increase in capacity, especially at the 1600 ppm water
concentration. operando FTIR provided real-time insights into the
mechanisms governing electrolyte degradation, chemical changes,
SEI formation, and other pertinent phenomena. This experimental
investigation illuminated the activity and mechanism behind the
formation and decomposition of water and Li2CO3 throughout
the cycles, offering valuable insights for future advancements in
Li-S battery technology.
