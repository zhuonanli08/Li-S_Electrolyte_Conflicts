A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, with their high theoretical specific capacity and energy density, hold the promise 
of transforming energy storage. However, challenges such as polysulfide shuttling and lithium anode instability 
hinder their commercialization. This study introduces an innovative electrolyte additive, iron bromide dime­
thoxyethane (FeBr2DME), which catalyzes polysulfide conversion, enhances reaction kinetics, and stabilizes 
lithium deposition. In Li-Li symmetric cells, FeBr2DME enabled stable cycling for 1000 h at 1 mA cm−2. Li-Cu 
cells maintained high coulombic efficiencies (~99 %) over 200 cycles. Adding 0.5 mM FeBr2DME to the elec­
trolyte resulted in a specific discharge capacity of 519.3 mAh g−1 at 4C, with 90.3 % capacity retention after 100 
cycles at 0.2C. Even with high sulfur loading (3.1 mg cm−2) and a low electrolyte/sulfur ratio (5 μL mg−1), the 
batteries achieved a discharge specific capacity of 425.8 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles at 0.2C, showcasing excep­
tional cycling stability. These findings highlight FeBr2DME’s potential as a dual-function electrolyte additive, 
offering a viable solution to key challenges in Li-S batteries and paving the way for their commercial viability.
1. Introduction
The rapid progress of smart electronic devices and electric vehicles 
has dramatically boosted the need for green and efficient energy storage 
systems (Qian et al., 2024; Service, 2018). Among the various potential 
candidates, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries stand out as a top contender 
for next-generation energy storage,thanks to their impressive theoretical 
specific capacity (1672 mAh g−1) and high energy density (2600 Wh 
kg−1) (Nakamura et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020; Guo et al., 
2024). The appeal of Li-S batteries is further enhanced by sulfur’s low 
cost, environmental friendliness, and abundant supply. Despite these 
advantages, Li-S batteries still face significant hurdles that hinder their 
commercial viability.
A major challenge is the complicated multi-step conversion process 
in Li-S batteries. During charge–discharge cycles, soluble lithium poly­
sulfides (LiPSs) are generated, which migrate between the cathode and 
anode. This migration leads to the notorious shuttle effect, resulting in 
poor utilization of active sulfur, rapid capacity decay, low Coulombic 
efficiency, and corrosion of the lithium anode (Li et al., 2023; Li et al., 
2020). The slow conversion of soluble LiPSs to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S ex­
acerbates these issues, particularly during long cycles and with high 
sulfur loadings (Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021; 
Dong et al., 2018). Additionally, the reactive and potentially unsafe 
nature of lithium metal anodes, which can react with the electrolyte to 
form an unstable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer, pose further 
challenges to the widespread adoption of Li-S batteries (Guo et al., 2024; 
Wang et al., 2020).
Recent research have made significant strides in addressing issues 
with sulfur cathodes. One effective method is using porous, high- 
conductivity carbon materials as sulfur carriers to alleviate the sub­
stantial volume expansion (80 %) during cycling (Wang et al., 2020; 
Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, introducing polar materials can trap 
dissolved LiPSs on the cathode side, reducing the shuttle effect through 
pphysical confinement or chemisorption (Dong et al., 2018; Wang et al., 
2018; Mori, 2023; Gao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020; 
Zhang et al., 2021). However, these methods alone cannot fully resolve

the shuttle problem, necessitating the introduction of catalysis to ensure 
strong adsorption and rapid conversion of LiPSs (Yang et al., 2021; 
Pongilat and Nallathamby, 2018; Han et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Zou 
et al., n.d).
Besides improving cathode materials, optimizing the electrolyte 
composition is another strategy to inhibit LiPSs diffusion. Some studies 
propose using electrolyte additives to enhance sulfur redox reactions 
and promote polysulfide transformation. For example, cobalt dichro­
cene (Co(Cp) 2) has been used as a redox medium to accelerate sulfur 
redox kinetics (Zhao et al., 2020); and dimethyldiselenide (DMDSe) has 
also been introduced as an efficient additive to promote sulfur redox 
kinetics in Li-S batteries (Liu et al., 2023). Current additives primarily 
stabilize the lithium anode, induce lithium deposition, and form SEI 
films (Cheng et al., 2017; Han et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2016; Hou et al., 
2021; Wang et al., 2019; Nulu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; Lu et al., 
2023; Jin and Xie, 2014; Liu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020; Fan et al., 2019; 
Kim et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2024). Notably; metal halides as electrolyte 
additives can create stable SEI layers on lithium metal anodes. For 
instance, Sollee Kim’s group added metal iodides (LiI, MgI2, AlI3, TiI4, 
SnI4) to ether-based electrolytes, resulting in stable SEI layers, better 
control of polysulfide diffusion, and maintaining 61 % or 64 % capacity 
after 50 cycles (Kim et al., 2021). Similarly, copper phthalocyanine as an 
additive forms a stable Cu2S/CuS layer, which help to regulate lithium 
deposition, prevent polysulfide corrosion, and stabilize the lithium 
anode (Hu et al., 2024). Other additives, such as SOCl2 (Li et al., 2019); 
copper acetate (Zu and Manthiram, 2014); and La(NO3)3 (Liu et al., 
2016); also offer positive protection for the lithium anode. Therefore, 
adjusting the electrolyte with additives is a simple and scalable way to 
significantly improve the performance of Li-S batteries.
In this research context, this study introduces an innovative 
approach by incorporating an organometallic salt, iron bromide- 
dimethoxyethane (FeBr2DME), as an electrolyte additive for Li-S batte­
ries. Dimethoxyethane (DME) serves as a ligand, making the inorganic 
salt FeBr2 more soluble in the organic electrolyte commonly used in 
ether-based electrolytes for Li-S batteries. The dual function of the 
FeBr2DME additive helps to regulate LiPSs redox reactions and stabilize 
the lithium anode. The metal cation Fe2+ interacts strongly with poly­
sulfide anions, inhibiting the shuttle effect and accelerating the con­
version of LiPSs. Additionally, the interaction between Br−
and Li+
forms a stable SEI layer containing LiBr on the lithium anode surface, 
facilitating uniform lithium plating/stripping and preventing dendrite 
formation. Thus, the dual benefits of Fe2+ and Br−in FeBr2DME effec­
tively inhibit polysulfide shuttling, promote LiPSs conversion, and pro­
tect the lithium anode.
As expected, adding 0.5 mM FeBr2DME to the electrolyte achieved a 
specific discharge capacity of 519.3 mAh g−1 at 4C and maintained a 
high capacity retention rate of 90.3 % after 100 cycles at 0.2C. Even 
under high sulfur loading (3.1 mg cm−2) and low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S 
= 5μL mg−1), the Li-S batteries retained a discharge specific capacity of 
425.8 mAh g−
1 after 100 cycles at 0.2C, demonstrating excellent 
cycling performance. Therefore, this study highlights the potential of 
FeBr2DME as a bifunctional electrolyte additive, offering a promising 
strategy for addressing key challenges in Li-S batteries.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Preparation of sulfur cathodes
120 mg of sublimed sulfur powder, 40 mg of conductive carbon black 
(Super P), 20 mg of carbon nanotubes, and 20 mg of binder (PVDF) were 
mixed in an agate ball mill tank with an appropriate amount of NMP as 
the solvent. The mixture was evenly mixed and ball-milled for 4 h. The 
resulting paste was applied to carbon-coated aluminum foil using an 
automatic coating machine. The foil was then dried in a vacuum oven at 
60 ℃ for 24 h and pressed to produce a pure sulfur cathode sheet with a 
diameter of 12 mm and a sulfur loading of approximately 1.1 mg cm−2.
2.2. Preparation of Dual-Function electrolyte
The ether electrolyte comprises 1 M bis(trifluoromethane) lithium 
sulfonimide (LiTFSI) and 0.2 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) in 1,2-dime­
thoxy-ethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (volume ratio 1:1). 
FeBr2DME (Aladdin, 98 %) was dissolved in the ether-based electrolyte 
at concentrations of 0.3 mM, 0.5 mM, and 0.8 mM, forming a stable and 
uniform solution after stirring at room temperature in a glove box for 8 
h.
2.3. Preparation of polysulfide lithium solution
2.3.1. Preparation of Li2S6 solution
Experiments were conducted in a glove box filled with argon gas (O2, 
H2O < 0.1 ppm). Sulfur powder and Li2 S (molar ratio 5:1) were mixed in 
1, 2-dimethoxy-ethane (DME) and 1, 3-dioxolane (DOL) (volume ratio 
1:1), and stirred at 25 ℃ for 12 h to prepare a 0.2 M Li2 S6 solution.
2.3.2. Preparation of Li2S8 solution
Similar procedures were conducted with sulfur powder and Li2 S 
(molar ratio 7:1) mixed in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether, and 
stirred at 60 ℃ for 24 h to prepare a 0.2 M Li2 S8 solution.
2.4. Electrochemical experiment tests
2.4.1. Li-S batteries
The Li-S battery comprised a prepared sulfur cathode, a commercial 
polypropylene diaphragm (PP, Celgard-2400; diameter 19 mm), and a 
lithium foil anode (diameter 16 mm; 0.6 μm thickness), assembled using 
an ether-based electrolyte with or without FeBr2DME.
2.4.2. Li-Cu batteries and Li-Li symmetrical batteries
Li-Cu batteries consisted of copper foil (diameter 12 mm), a PP 
separator, the prepared ether-based electrolyte, and lithium foil. Li-Li 
symmetrical batteries used lithium metal as the working electrode, the 
counter electrode, along with the ether-based electrolyte.
2.4.3. Li2S6 symmetrical batteries
Graphite felt served as the positive and negative electrodes, and 0.2 
M Li2S6-DOL/DME solution was used as the electrolyte. Each graphite 
felt received 20 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6 solution and 10 μL of ether-based 
electrolyte, added to the separator to prepare the batteries.
2.4.4. Deposition and dissolution of Li2S
Carbon paper acted as the positive electrode, and lithium foil as the 
negative electrode, with 0.2 M Li2S8-tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether 
solution as the electrolyte. Each carbon paper received 20 μL of 0.2 M 
Li2S8 solution, and 20 μL of the prepared ether electrolyte was added to 
the separator, forming a battery for testing Li2S deposition and 
dissolution.
The electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT 2273) conducted elec­
trochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) 
tests. EIS measured the frequencies from 100 kHz to 10 mHz, with an AC 
voltage amplitude was 5 mV. The voltage range for Li2 S6 symmetrical 
batteries was −0.8 to 0.8 V, with CV curves tested at a scan rate of 5 mV 
s−1. The deposition process discharged batteries at 0.112 mA to 2.06 V, 
then held at 2.05 V until the current fell below 10−5 A. The dissolution 
process reduced the voltage to 1.8 V at 0.10 mA and 1.7 V at 0.01 mA, 
ensuring complete conversion from LiPSs to solid Li2 S. The batteries 
were then charged at a constant potential of 2.35 V until the current was 
below 10−5 A, ensuring complete dissolution of Li2 S.
2.5. Material characterization
The morphology of the experimental samples was characterized by 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Quanta 200FEG FEI).

3. Results and Discussion
To investigate the interaction between FeBr2DME and polysulfides, 
we performed an adsorption test. As depicted in Fig. 1a, we introduced a 
0.1 M Li2S6 solution into an electrolyte containing FeBr2DME and 
another with equal amounts of DME/DOL. Upon standing for 48 h 
(Fig. 1b), the FeBr2DME-Li2S6 solution exhibited a noticeable lightening 
of color, attributed to a chemical interaction between FeBr2DME and 
Li2S6. This interaction led to a decrease of the FeBr2DME concentration 
within the solution, evidenced by a color change. The observed phe­
nomenon suggests the molecular dispersion of FeBr2DME and Li2S6 
within the electrolyte matrix, which was maintained post-adsorption, 
thereby effectively inhibiting condensation. This adsorption process is 
anticipated to enhance the catalytic conversion of polysulfides and 
improve the reaction selectivity between FeBr2DME and soluble LiPSs 
(Tan, 2023). The UV–visible spectrum of FeBr2DME-Li2S6 and DME/ 
DOL-Li2S6 solutions(Fig. 1c) revealed that the absorption peaks of 
FeBr2DME-Li2S6 were significantly reduced compared to DME/DOL- 
Li2S6., indicating that FeBr2DME interacts with Li2S6, thereby dimin­
ishing LiPSs accumulation in the electrolyte and facilitating uniform Li2S 
nucleation (Luo et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020).
To further investigate the impact of FeBr2DME (0.5 mM) on poly­
sulfides, we assembled Li2S6 symmetric batteries for testing (Wang et al., 
2022; Shao et al., 2020). Fig. 2a illustrates that at a sweep rate of 5 mV 
s−1; batteries with FeBr2DME additive exhibited higher peaks and more 
distinct redox peaks, signifying effective regulation of liquid–liquid 
conversion of polysulfides (Li2S6-Li2S4). Fig. 2b presents the CV curve of 
Li-S batteries at a sweep rate of 0.1mV s−1, revealing two reduction 
peaks at 2.27 V and 2.02 V, corresponding to the reduction of S8 to LiPSs 
and LiPSs to Li2S/Li2S2, respectively. The oxidation peak at 2.41 V 
represents the oxidation of Li2 S to LiPSs. Compared to the blank elec­
trolyte, the reduction peak shifted positively, and the oxidation peak 
shifted negatively, demonstrating that FeBr2DME accelerates the redox 
reactions of LiPSs. We further examined the solid–liquid conversion of 
polysulfides by testing the deposition and dissolution of Li2S (Fig. 2c–f) 
(Qiu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2017,7.; Chen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 
2020). The addition of FeBr2DME increased the deposition capacity of 
Li2S from 123.8mAh g−1 to 459.5mAh g−1; accelerating both the 
deposition time and capacity, and promoting uniform nucleation of Li2S. 
In the dissolution experiment, FeBr2DME enhanced the dissolution ca­
pacity from 573.7 mAh g−1 to 666.5 mAh g−1 with a higher response 
current value. These results demonstrate that FeBr2DME exhibits bidi­
rectional catalytic conversion during charge–discharge cycles, effec­
tively regulating the redox processes of LiPSs. Post-experiment analysis 
showed that the deposition of Li2S on carbon paper was higher with 
FeBr2DME (Fig. S1,S2), the SEM image of large area Li2S deposition with 
FeBr2DME, which also proves that FeBr2DME enhances the redox reac­
tion of LiPS, and further confirms its catalytic effectiveness (Wang et al., 
2020).
To further explore the kinetic influence of FeBr2DME, we tested 
different CV curves at sweep rates of 0.1–0.5 mV s−
1 (Fig. S3) to 
characterize redox kinetics and lithium-ion diffusion characteristics of 
LiPSs. The peak shape with FeBr2DME remained stable with increasing 
sweep rates, whereas it broadened without FeBr2DME, indicating slower 
LiPSs conversion to Li2S. Fig. S4 plots the square root of the current 
versus the scanning rate, enabling the calculation of lithium diffusivity 
using the Randles-Sevcik equation. The results show that Li-S batteries 
with FeBr2DME had higher Li+ diffusion coefficients at various redox 
stages, enhancing Li+ diffusion rates and redox kinetics of LiPSs.
To investigate FeBr2DME’s protective mechanism for lithium anodes, 
we assembled Li-Li symmetric batteries. The polarization potential of 
these batteries (Fig. 3a) with FeBr2DME at various current densities (0.1 
to 4mA cm−2) was consistently lower than those without FeBr2DME. 
This difference was particularly notable at higher current densities (3- 
4mA cm−2). Long cycle tests (Fig. 3b and Table S1) at a current density 
of 1mA cm−2 showed that Li-Li symmetric batteries with FeBr2DME 
maintained a stable polarization potential of 50 mV for 1000 h, whereas 
those without FeBr2DME stabilized for only 100 h. The increase in 
lithium anode voltage without FeBr2DME was due to lithium dendrite 
formation, leading to short circuits (Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; 
Koo et al., 2019). Fig. 3c demonstrates that the batteries with FeBr2DME 
additive maintained high and stable coulomb efficiency (~99 %) after 
200 cycles at the current density of 1 mA cm−2, while batteries without 
FeBr2DME CE dropped sharply after 100 cycles (Adams et al., 2018; Li 
et al., 2024). Fig. 3d shows lower nucleation overpotentials and higher, 
stabl coulomb efficiencies (~99 %) with FeBr2DME, indicating stable 
SEI formation and lithium anode protection.
SEM analysis (Fig. S5) of lithium anodes after 100 cycles revealed 
that surfaces with FeBr2DME were flat and smooth, while those without 
FeBr2DME were rough and porous with many cracks. The lithium cross- 
section with FeBr2DME maintained a stable and compact SEI layer 
(~27.2 μm), inhibiting dendrite growth and stabilizing the lithium 
anode interface. In contrast, the SEI layer formed without FeBr2DME 
was loose, porous, and thick (~68.0 μm), leading to instability and 
potential short circuits. Surface element mapping confirmed the pres­
ence of Fe and Br, stabilizing lithium deposition and forming a more 
stable SEI layer. Li-Cu batteries tested under lithium plating/stripping. 
The above results confirmed that adding FeBr2DME in the electrolyte 
can reduce the polarization potential of the batteries and maintain stable 
coulomb efficiency under long cycles. This also shows that FeBr2DME 
applied in batteries can promote the stable formation of SEI layer and 
protect the lithium metal negative electrode.
To further confirm the practical application, this work continues to 
investigatethe electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries by adding 
different concentrations of FeBr2DME to the electrolyte. The effects of 
FeBr2DME concentrations (0.3, 0.5, and 0.8 mM) on the batteries were 
examined. Fig. 4a shows the rate performance of Li-S batteries with and 
without FeBr2DME additives. Batteries with 0.5 mM FeBr2DME exhibi­
ted superior specific discharge capacities of 1005.5, 854.9, 774.6, 710.1, 
630.9, 570.4, and 519.3mAh g−
1 at rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4C, 
respectively. These values are significantly higher than those without 
FeBr2DME, which showed capacities of 968.7, 721.5, 614.5, 552.3, 
483.5, 457.5, and 424.2 mAh g−1 at the same rates. Notably, particularly 
at the elevated current density of 4C, the specific discharge capacity of 
the 0.8 mM FeBr2DME solution was measured at 443.8mAh g−1, 
significantly lower than the 519.3mAh g−1 observed for the 0.5 mM 
solution. This discrepancy in specific discharge capacities across 
different concentrations can be attributed to the relatively high

demonstrate the catalytic capability of FeBr2DME, we calculated the 
ΔQ2/ΔQ1 values (Yao et al., 2021) (Fig. 4c). ΔQ1 represents the sol­

We also explored the cycling performance of Li-S batteries at 
different rates (Fig. 4d). At 0.2C, batteries with FeBr2DME maintained a 
discharge specific capacity of 751.5 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, with a 
capacity retention rate of 90.3 %, demonstrating more stable cycling 
performance at low current densities (Table S2). At 0.5C (Fig. S7), 
batteries with 0.5 mM FeBr2DME showed a higher initial discharge ca­
pacity of 783.2 mAh g−1 and after 200 cycles a lower capacity decay rate 
of 0.17 % per cycle compared to those without FeBr2DME. The discharge 
capacity with FeBr2DME remains at 353.5 mAh g−1 after 500 times 
when C-rate increased to 1C, with a capacity decay rate of 0.72 % per 
cycle. Continuing to test the long cycle of high sulfur load, we can see 
that even in high sulfur loadings (3.2 mg cm−2) and low electrolyte/ 
sulfur (E/S = 5μL mg−1), the initial capacity of batteries with FeBr2DME 
at 0.2C was 801.9 and 624.8 mAh g−1, respectively, maintaining good 
stability after 100 cycles (Chung, 2018; Rana et al., 2019; Wang et al., 
2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Further analysis using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) (Fig. S9) revealed that lithium surfaces with 
FeBr2DME were flat and smooth, while those without FeBr2DME 
exhibited large cracks and rough textures. This finding supports the role 
of FeBr2DME in stabilizing the lithium surface and enhancing the overall 
battery performance.
To further understand the mechanism of transition metal bromide 
dimethoxy-ethane in Li-S batteries, we continued to study the effect of 
cobalt dimethoxy-ethane bromide (CoBr2DME) on polysulfides. 
Through the adsorption test with Li2S6, it can be seen that Co2+ can 
interact with polysulfides to form Cobr2DME-Li2S6 precipitate. At the 
same time, the solution is taken for UV–visible spectrum experiment, 
and the peak value of the solution with CoBr2DME is significantly 
reduced in Fig S10. The positive effect of CoBr2DME on enhancing 
polysulfide reaction kinetics was further demonstrated (Zhang et al., 
2024; Yao et al., 2024). (Fig S11) The introduction of CoBr2DME into the 
electrolyte also improves the electro-chemical performance of Li-S bat­
teries. We continued to test the influence of Li-Li symmetrical batteries 
with CoBr2DME additive on lithium deposition. As shown in Fig S12 and 
Fig S13, 1000 cycles at the current density of 1mA cm−2 showed stable 
cycle performance. Combined with SEM characterization of lithium 
surface and lithium cross section after deposition, it is further proved 
that CoBr2DME has advantages in stabilizing lithium deposition. The 
above experiments show that the positive effect of metal iron ions on 
enhancing reaction kinetics and stabilizing lithium deposition can also 
be applied to other transition metal cations (such as Co2+).
Through density functional theory (DFT) calculation, the adsorption 
energy of FeBr2 on DME, S8, Li2S6 and Li2S8 was revealed. As shown in 
Fig. 5a, the adsorption energy of FeBr2 on DME, S8, Li2S6 and Li2S8 was 
−0.76 eV, −1.02 eV, −1.21 eV and −2.29 eV in sequence. The adsorp­
tion capacity of FeBr2 on polysulfide is higher than DME, and FeBr2 
interacts with polysulfide preferentially, which further proves the bidi­
rectional catalytic effect of FeBr2DME on polysulfide redox reaction 
(Fig. 5b). These results suggested that the metal cation Fe2+ interacts 
strongly with polysulfide anions, inhibiting the shuttle effect and 
accelerating the conversion of LiPSs. Additionally, the interaction be­
tween Br−and Li+ forms a stable SEI layer containing LiBr on the lithium 
anode surface, facilitating uniform lithium plating/stripping and pre­
venting dendrite formation (Fig. 6). Therefore, the dual benefits of Fe2+
and Br−in FeBr2DME effectively inhibit polysulfide shuttling, promote 
LiPSs conversion, and protect the lithium anode, contributing to the 
development of robust Li-S batteries.

incorporating FeBr2DME exhibited stable cycling for 1000 h at 1 mA

FeBr2DME demonstrates considerable potential as an electrolyte addi­

for other transition metal compounds. The strategy of incorporating 
small amounts of electrolyte additives offers a viable approach to 
addressing critical challenges in Li-S batteries, thereby advancing their 
prospects for commercialization.
