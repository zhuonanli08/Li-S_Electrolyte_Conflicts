A Fluorinated Lewis Acidic Organoboron Tunes Polysulﬁde
Complex Structure for High-Performance Lithium–Sulfur
Batteries
Siyuan Gao, Bomin Li, Qijia Zhu, Jingtian Yang, Jiayi Xu, Bowen An, Cong Liu, Qin Wu,
Qian Liu, Zhengcheng Zhang,* and Yingwen Cheng*
Many challenges in lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are associated with the
radical change in lithium polysulﬁde (LPS) solubility during cycling, but
chemical approaches to address such inconsistency are still lacking. Here, the
use of a strong Lewis acidic ﬂuorinated organoboron, tri(2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl)
borate (TFEB), is reported as a multi-functional mediator to simultaneously
overcome multiple technical barriers in practical Li–S batteries. TFEB acts as
an anion acceptor and forms strong molecular complexes with Lewis basic
LPS. The TFEB-LPS complexes have consistent solubility across the full
polysulﬁde spectrum and deliver several times improved better redox kinetics,
unlocking a true redox catalytic mechanism that covers the majority of redox
events in thick sulfur cathodes. As a result, Li–S batteries evaluated under
practical conditions exhibit signiﬁcantly improved discharge capacity, rate
capability, and cycling stability with the addition of the TFEB additive. More
importantly, TFEB also contributes to the stabilization of lithium anode in the
presence of polysulﬁdes by generating strong interfacial ﬁlm. These attributes
signiﬁcantly improve the cycling stability of practical Li–S pouch cells, which
are assembled with a unit energy density of 219 Wh kg−1. The results provide
new molecular insights on the design of unlocking solvation networks of
practical Li–S systems.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur
(Li–S)
batteries
are
among the most promising techniques
toward reaching the 500 Wh kg−1 target
at reduced cost and are being heav-
ily studied as next-generation storage
batteries in energy-density demanding
applications such as electric vehicles
and electric aviation.[1] However, un-
locking their full potentials requires
stable cycling of thick sulfur cathodes
(>5 mAh cm−2) under lean electrolyte
conditions
(electrolyte-to-sulfur
ratio,
E/S < 4.0 μL mg−1
s) in combination with
thin Li anodes.[2] Unfortunately, batteries
under such conditions typically suﬀer
from very low speciﬁc capacity and rate
capability, along with fast capacity decay
and severe fast self-discharge. Failure
mechanisms analysis often identiﬁes
pathways associated with two critical
factors: 1) radical changes of polysulﬁde
concentration during cycling that start
with highly soluble long-chain polysul-
ﬁdes and change to sparsely soluble
short-chain polysulﬁdes, which induces Li corrosion, shuttling,
and sulfur loss; 2) low sulfur utilization and formation of passi-
vating Li2S layers due to the poor electron and ion conductive na-
ture of sulfur redox intermediates, which necessitates the aggres-
sive amount of additives for conducting electrons (e.g. carbon)
and ions, with the latter often requiring separate ion diﬀusion
channels, usually provided either by solid ion conductors or addi-
tional liquid electrolytes.[3] As a result, high speciﬁc discharge ca-
pacities are usually limited to thin electrodes under ﬂooded elec-
trolyte conditions at the sacriﬁce of cell-level energy density, mak-
ing Li–S batteries unable to compete with lithium-ion batteries.[4]
As such, new ways of modulating the solvation and redox be-
havior of LPS under practical conditions are critical for enabling
high-performance Li–S batteries.
The importance of modulating LPS conversion has motivated
the design of advanced sulfur hosts,[5] modiﬁed separators,[6]
cathode architectures, and electrolyte formulations,[7] with the
latter being more promising due to their tunability in modulating
solvation and chemical interactions of polysulﬁde with both sul-
fur cathode and Li anode.[8] Sacriﬁcial additives such as LiNO3

and lithium bis(oxalate)borate have been shown to suppress Li
corrosion in polysulﬁde, but they are unable to remedy cathode
issues and have short cycle life due to gradual depletion.[9] On
the other hand, functional redox mediator incorporated either
in cathode or electrolyte emerged as a new approach to modu-
late LPS conversion, such as pentamethyl-ferrocene, cobaltocene,
and anthraquinone.[10] However, these mediators have narrow re-
dox potentials and are unable to cover the whole spectrum of poly-
sulﬁde conversion, making their eﬀectiveness inherently limited.
In addition, the redox-active nature of these soluble mediators
also means that they suﬀer from shuttling between the cathode
and anode. These limitations can potentially be addressed by har-
nessing soluble LPS as intrinsic redox mediators, for example,
solvated Li2S4 can chemically react with the otherwise inert S
(e.g. Li2S4 + 2S →Li2S6) for high-depth discharging,[11] but this
approach is frustrated by the very low solubility of short-chain
polysulﬁdes and the associated diﬃculties in expediting the Li2S-
Li2S2 redox couple.[12] As such, developing novel additives to in-
crease the solvation of short-chain polysulﬁdes while suppressing
long chain polysulﬁdes’ solvation could mitigate the otherwise
radical changes in solvated polysulﬁdes concentration during cy-
cling, and as such can potentially overcome multiple challenges
in practical Li–S batteries.
We outline here the use of a strong Lewis acidic ﬂuorinated
organoborane tris(2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl) borate (TFEB) as a multi-
functional mediator for high-performance practical Li–S batter-
ies. We discovered that TFEB forms strong intermolecular com-
plexes with all lithium polysulﬁdes and the resulting complexes
exhibit several times enhanced redox activity compared with na-
tive polysulﬁdes. More importantly, these new intermolecular
complexes exhibit similar solubilities across the entire polysul-
ﬁde spectrum by improving short-chain LPS solvation while sup-
pressing long-chain LPS solvation, and as such unlocked the con-
sistent presence of solvated polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte dur-
ing battery cycling. This invokes a true redox catalytic mecha-
nism that harnesses the intrinsic redox activities of TFEB-LPS
complexes for high-capacity discharge of sulfur cathodes while
also mitigating the polysulﬁde-induced Li corrosion on the an-
ode side. As a result, practical Li–S batteries equipped with TFEB-
modiﬁed electrolytes delivered signiﬁcantly improved cycling sta-
bility while still maintaining suﬃcient unit-level energy densities
both under coin cell and pouch cell conﬁgurations.
2. Results and Discussion
The molecular structure of tris(2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl) borate
(TFEB) is shown in Figure 1a. It is a strong Lewis acid due to the
strong electron-withdrawing eﬀect of the three ﬂuorinated ethyl
groups on the central boron atom that already has an empty va-
lence orbital, making the B more acidic compared with its non-
ﬂuorinated counterpart, triethyl borate (TEB). In fact, our den-
sity functional theory (DFT) calculation suggests a ≈2.0 eV lower
LUMO energy level for TFEB compared with TEB, making TFEB
a very strong electron acceptor (Figure S1, Supporting Informa-
tion). This, when combined with the gradually increased HOMO
energy level for shorter chain lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sx), sug-
gests that TFEB would form strong binding with the lone-pair-
bearing nucleophilic Li2Sx, as demonstrated with the potential
maps (Figure 1a). This is validated with the strong TFEB-Li2Sx
binding energies especially for the short chain Li2Sx and the fur-
ther enhanced binding energy when a second TFEB molecule
was complexed with the TFEB-Li2Sx complex (Figures S2 and S3,
Supporting Information for stable conﬁgurations), especially for
the Li2S that exhibits −4.09 and −2.65 eV binding energies for the
formation of Li2S-2TFEB and Li2S-TFEB complexes. On the other
hand, the complexation of Li2S with the ﬂuorine-free counterpart
triethyl borate (TEB), although also exothermic, is much weaker
compared with TFEB (Figure S4, Supporting Information).
The strong intermolecular TFEB-Li2Sx interactions were cor-
roborated with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) characteriza-
tions where a pronounced ≈0.8 ppm chemical shift in 19F NMR
(Figure 1a) and ≈0.5 ppm shift of 1H NMR (Figure S5, Support-
ing Information) of TFEB were observed after complexation with
Li2S or Li2S8. These complexes appeared fairly stable during bat-
tery cycling as electrolyte samples harvested from cycled cells
exhibited the same singlet peak at −76.9 ppm in 19F NMR. Ra-
man analysis, similarly, also reveals evident shifts in Li2S res-
onance peaks after complexation with TFEB (Figure S6, Sup-
porting Information). Furthermore, the TFEB-Li2Sx mixture so-
lution exhibits dramatically changed absorption properties as ev-
idenced from the nearly disappeared polysulﬁde absorption peak
at ≈420 nm for Li2S8 solvated in dioxolane/dimethoxyethane
(DOL/DME, 1:1 v/v) after addition of 2 eq. TFEB (Figure 1b), also
conﬁrms the formation of these complexes.
The formation of TFEB-polysulﬁde complexes has signiﬁ-
cant impacts on the solubility of the polysulﬁde as shown in
Figure 1c. Unlike un-complexed polysulﬁdes that exhibited dra-
matic changes in solubilities when moving from long- to short-
chain polysulﬁdes, solubilities for TFEB-Li2Sx complexes were
much more consistent. This includes a more than 100 times in-
creased solubility for short-chain Li2S/Li2S2 that are otherwise
hardly soluble, and a nearly 10 times decreased solubility for long-
chain Li2S8. This smoothing in solubility leads to the consistent
presence of polysulﬁdes in electrolytes, which is critical for more
durable battery cycling. In particular, the signiﬁcantly reduced
long-chain polysulﬁde solubility could mitigate issues related to
sulfur loss and polysulﬁde-induced lithium anode corrosion, and
the improved solvation of short-chain Li2Sx avoids formation of
passivating Li2S/Li2S2 deposits (to be discussed below). In ad-
dition, we also analyzed the polysulﬁde-TFEB conﬁguration via
DFT as long polysulﬁde chains break down to short chains. A
change of binding mode was observed, such that Li2S8, Li2S6,
and Li2S4 bind with TFEB mainly via F–Li interaction whereas
Li2S2 and Li2S bind with TFEB via Li–F, Li–O, and S–B inter-
actions. The HOMO of Li2S8-2TFEB was populated on Li and
S atoms adjacent to these Li atoms and almost buried by poly-
sulﬁde chains, while it gradually shifted to surrounding S atoms
as the polysulﬁde chain breaks down and hence became less af-
fected by the steric hindrance (Figure S2, Supporting Informa-
tion). These trends are consistent with the solubility results of
the LiPS-2TFEB complex, and therefore it appears that better ac-
cessibility of solvent molecules to the HOMO of complexes con-
tributes to a higher solubility.

teries due to irreversible Li consumption and electrolyte de-
pletion from spontaneous chemical reactions with dissolved
Li2Sx.[13] The conventional LiNO3 additive that has been eﬀective
in batteries with thin S cathode and ﬂooded electrolyte becomes

These mediated processes promote the complete discharge of
sulfur cathodes by activating the otherwise “dead” short-chain
polysulﬁde deposits that typically passivate the electrodes un-

and solid redox mediators that are only compatible with one
or two polysulﬁde redox couples due to narrow redox match-

one or two polysulﬁde conversion reactions), the TFEB- poly-
sulﬁde mediators have the unique features of covering the en-
tire redox spectrum of sulfur cathodes both during charging and
discharging.
The promotion of TFEB on the high-capacity discharge of thick
sulfur cathodes was conﬁrmed in the model experiments using
coin cells assembled with polysulﬁde catholyte and carbon pa-
per cathode. Under low sulfur loading conditions (0.5 mg cm−2,
Figure S16, Supporting Information), TFEB only slightly in-
creased the discharge capacity compared with the additive-free
cells and these cells exhibited similar voltage proﬁles, suggest-
ing that the complexation with TFEB did not dramatically modify
the redox characters of polysulﬁdes in such battery environment.
This is further supported with XRD analysis of discharged sulfur
cathodes that points to Li2S as the only discharge product in elec-
trolytes with and without TFEB additive (Figure S17, Supporting
Information). The comparison of high sulfur-loading catholyte
cells (2.5 mg(s) cm−2), on the other hand, conﬁrms the nearly full
discharge of sulfur with the addition of TFEB (Figure 3e,f). The
discharge capacity in the TFEB containing cell was over twice
of that in the additive-free cells (1196 vs. 540 mAh g−1). The in-
creased capacity was mostly contributed by the discharge plateau
at ≈2.1 V (i.e., liquid-to-solid transition of short-chain Li2S4 to
Li2S/Li2S2), highlighting the beneﬁcial roles of the TFEB-Li2Sx
redox complexes on catalyzing the continuous discharging and
full discharging of sulfur under aggressive conditions.

We then explored the application of TFEB in practical Li–S bat-
teries assembled with high-capacity sulfur electrodes and lean
electrolytes (electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio, E/S = 4.0 μl mg−1). It is
well known that batteries under these conditions are constrained
with the interplay of sluggish ion diﬀusion and electron diﬀu-
sion within the cathode, as well as the formation of passivating
Li2S layers under these conditions.[4] Figure 4a,b compares the
initial voltage proﬁles and cycling stability of Li–S cells contain-
ing 5.0 mg cm−2 sulfur cathode and gradually increased TFEB
content up to 500 mM (all E/S = 4.0, at 0.1C). The results reveal
an optimal performance observed at the TFEB concentration of
250 mM where the discharge plateau at 2.1 V can be observed
only. As expected, the baseline electrolyte exhibited very sluggish
kinetics and only delivered an initial capacity of 678 mAh g−1,
and the initial Coulombic eﬃciency was only 71.8% due to poly-
sulﬁde shuttling. However, the addition of TFEB evidently im-
proves the discharge capacity, discharge voltage, and Coulombic
eﬃciency, enabling 1063 mAh g−1 and 95.5% Coulombic eﬃ-
ciency at the initial cycle when 250 mM TFEB was added, along
with a lower overpotential of 326 mV (vs. 554 mV in TFEB-free
cell).
In addition to higher initial capacity, TFEB also signiﬁcantly
improved stability on both discharge capacity and Coulombic ef-
ﬁciency for all cycles (Figure 4b). Unlike the TFEB-free cell, the
cell with 250 mM TFEB cell retained 63.3% of the initial 1084
mAh g−1 speciﬁc capacity after 200 cycles compared with an ini-
tial speciﬁc capacity of 716.6 mAh g−1 and 49.8% capacity lost
within only 20 cycles in an additive-free cells. The optimal content
of TFEB corresponds to an S8 to TFEB molecular ratio of 3.9:1.
Further increase in TFEB concentration to 500 mM deteriorates
the cell performance both in capacity retention and the Coulom-
bic eﬃciency, which is consistent with Figure 3a where the addi-
tion of 4 equiv. TFEB signiﬁcantly compromise redox reversibility
and almost completely eliminated the anodic peak. The improved
cycling stability was also conﬁrmed with the impedance analy-

The cycled batteries were dissembled and analyzed to under-
stand how the TFEB addresses the critical factors responsible
for battery failure. Based on prior investigations, polysulﬁde-
induced failure of practical Li–S batteries is often linked to 1)
sulfur loss associated with shuttling of high-chain polysulﬁde to
the anode, which also induces Li corrosion and passivation; 2)
growth of passivated Li2S/Li2S2 ﬁlms on the cathode that are in-
active and block utilization of the otherwise active underneath
sulfur. In accordance, we compared the weight of the cycled sul-
fur cathode at the fully charged state after careful washing and
drying. The weight loss in cells without TFEB was ≈2.1 mg,
which accounts for 36% of the initial sulfur present in the elec-
trode, assuming all weight loss was due to sulfur (Figure 4c).
Such sulfur loss in the TFEB-containing cell was only ≈0.7 mg
(12% of initial loading), consistent with the signiﬁcantly re-
duced solubility for the shuttling-responsible long-chain poly-
sulﬁde after complexing with TFEB. The SEM analysis of cy-
cled sulfur cathodes revealed that with TFEB, the electrode re-
tained the general surface porosity whereas the TFEB-free cell
exhibited the typical electrode passivation commonly observed
under lean conditions. This reduced sulfur loss also mitigated
the polysulﬁde-induced Li anode corrosion, as supported by the
lack of Li2S diﬀraction peaks observed in the cycled Li anode
where more compact SEI layers formed by TFEB (Figure 4d).
Such diﬀerences, in combination with the electrochemistry re-
sults, conﬁrm that TFEB could assist the generation of much
more electroactive Li2S that can be easily recharged. These
beneﬁts are further conﬁrmed by the fact that the improve-
ments from TFEB become more pronounced as sulfur loading
increased from 1.0 to 10.0 mg cm−2, as challenges associated with
sulfur management become more problematic under such con-
ditions (Figure 4e).
An important implication with the suppressed polysulﬁde
shuttling with TFEB-Li2Sx complexes is the signiﬁcantly reduced
self-discharge rate of Li–S batteries, which is rather fast in real-
istic applications and making it impractical.[16] Figure 5a,b com-
pares the resting and self-discharge behavior for cells with and
without TFEB additive (sulfur loading = 5.0 mg cm−2, E/S ra-
tio = 4.0). The comparison of voltage proﬁles for the charging
of these cells, as well as their 7 days room temperature resting
and the subsequent discharging at 0.5 mA g−1, suggests that the
cell equipped with TFEB exhibited much more stable and higher
resting voltages, presumably due to much less solvated polysul-
ﬁdes in the electrolyte. In accordance, the discharge capacity after
resting 7 days for the TFEB cell was 73.2% of the initial charg-
ing capacity, much higher than that of 33.7% of the TFEB-free
cell.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we report TFEB as a new multi-functional ad-
ditive to unlock high-performance Li–S batteries under practi-
cal conditions. Both experimental and DFT examinations con-
ﬁrm that TFEB coordinates with all polysulﬁde and form strong
and more reactive complexes. These complexes have similar sol-
ubility across the whole spectrum of lithium polysulﬁde un-
like the native counterparts, and as such improves the solva-
tion of short-chain polysulﬁde while suppressing long-chain
polysulﬁde, permitting the stable presence of solvated polysul-
ﬁdes during battery cycling. Such presence unlocks a true re-
dox mechanism with the solvated polysulﬁdes function as re-
dox mediators for catalyzing the high capacity cycling of the
otherwise sluggish short-chain polysulﬁde Li2S4-Li2S conver-
sion, especially under the high capacity and lean conditions.
The reduced long-chain polysulﬁde solubility, in combination
with the more resistant interfaces formed on the Li anode with
TFEB, also contribute to the extension of the cycle life of Li an-
ode under realistic battery environment. These attributes col-
lectively improve the cycling stability of lean electrolyte bat-
tery. Li–S pouch cells with a gravimetric energy density of
219 Wh kg−1 can be achieved by employing a TFEB additive
that exhibits signiﬁcantly improved stability for more than 200
cycles.
4. Experimental Section
Electrode and Electrolyte Preparation:
All chemicals were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich and were used as received. The baseline electrolyte
was 1.0 m LiTFSI dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1 volume ratio) with 0.4 m
LiNO3 additive. Appropriate amounts of TFEB or TEB were added to this
baseline electrolyte inside an Ar-ﬁlled glove box to reach the designated
concentration as speciﬁed in the manuscript.
Sulfur cathode was prepared using the method outlined in our previ-
ous publication, and is characterized as having 65 wt.% sulfur, 10 wt.%
N-doped carbon, 15 wt.% of Mo6S8 and 10 wt.% PEO10-LiTFEI binder.
Typical sulfur loading is 5.0 mg cm−2.[18] Sulfur-free carbon electrodes for
polysulﬁde catholyte studies were prepared by rolling press the mixture of
65 wt.% Ketjenblack carbon black, 15 wt.% super-P carbon black; 10 wt.%
of carbon nanotube, and 10 wt.% of PTFE binder as thin ﬁlms.
Polysulﬁde Solutions Preparation:
The polysulﬁde solutions were pre-
pared by stoichiometric reactions of Li2S and S in DOL/DME inside a glove
box. As an example, a 200 mm Li2S8 catholyte can be prepared by react-
ing 92.0 mg Li2S and 448.0 mg elemental sulfur in 10 mL DOL/DME, the
solutions were stirred at 50 °C typically overnight prior to use. The poly-
sulﬁde solubility quantiﬁcation was performed by gradual dilution of over-
concentrated solutions with DOL/DME solvent until complete dissolution
of solid residues.
Physical and Electrochemical Characterizations:
The morphological and
structural characters of the materials were examined by XRD (Rigaku Mini-
ﬂex diﬀractometer using Cu K𝛼radiation), SEM (Zeiss Merlin FE-SEM),
EDS (Bruker), UV–vis absorption spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Lambda
XLS+ spectrometer), Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw InVia with 532nm
laser) and XPS (ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc using an Al Ka source). NMR
spectra were acquired using a 300 MHz Bruker spectrometer, with sam-
ples prepared by sealing electrolyte solutions in a 3.0 mm PTFE liner with
acetone-d6 added as an external standard.
Electrochemical Studies and Battery Testing:
Type 2025 coin cells were
used for all coin cell evaluations. The cells were assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled
glove box with a Celgard separator. Cycling of coin cells was performed us-
ing Neware CT-4008T battery analyzers. Cyclic voltammograms (CV) and
electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded using a CH In-
struments CHI660E electrochemical workstation. EIS measurements were
conducted at the open-circuit voltage with the frequency range of 100 kHz
to 100 MHz.
Electrodes for post-cycling electron microscope analysis were harvested
by dissembling cycled cells and rinsing the electrodes with fresh DOL and
then dried inside the glovebox. The samples were mounted to an airtight
sample holder in the glove box.
Pouch cells were fabricated in the dry room at the Cell Analysis, Mod-
elling, and Prototyping (CAMP) Facility at Argonne National Laboratory.
The size is 46.0 × 32.4 mm for anode, and 45.0 × 31.2 mm for cathode.
The areal sulfur loading on cathodes is 5.0 mg cm−2 and the Li metal foil
has a thickness of 200 μm. An Al tap was riveted to the cathode and a Ni
tab was riveted on the anode. The electrodes were stacked together with
a Celgard separator in between and 282 μL electrolyte was injected (E/S
ratio = 4.0 μL mg−1), followed by vacuum sealing and subsequent testing.
The energy density was calculated using the following equation:
Energy density = Capacity × Average operating voltage
Total weight of components
(1)
The average operating voltage was 2.19 V calculated from the
charge/discharge proﬁle. The total weight of the components is 602 mg.
Based on the equation, the total energy of the contained pouch cell is
131.8 mWh, accounting for energy density of 219 Wh kg−1. The total en-
ergy of the coin cell was 13.49 mWh, accounting for an energy density of
287 Wh kg−1 (Table S1, Supporting Information).
Density Functional Theory Calculations:
The calculations were carried
out using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The projector-
augmented (PAW) method was used to describe the wavefunctions of the
ionic cores. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) PBE functional
was employed to account for the electron exchange-correlation eﬀects.
