A B S T R A C T
The high theoretical capacity of Li–S batteries makes them attractive for next-generation energy storage. 
However, achieving prolonged cycle life is a bottleneck currently for commercial adoption. Upon discharge, 
sulfur and lithium produce polysulfides that dissolve in the electrolyte and transfer from the cathode to the 
anode, causing capacity fading. To prevent this, micro/mesoporous carbon hosts have been proposed to trap 
polysulfides effectively. To understand the synergistic effect of melt-infiltration temperature, sulfur chain length 
and carbonate electrolyte, we performed various electrochemical studies, including long cycle GCD, transient 
experiments like PITT and GITT, and in-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy followed by DRT analysis. 
Our study shows, the infiltration temperature influences sulfur confinement, loading, and different allotropes 
formation, and demonstrates a critical interplay between melt-infiltration temperature and electrolyte compo­
sition. The proposed system shows a suitable electrode/electrolyte system to study further, and offers a valuable 
insight to develop stable Li–S batteries.
1. Introduction
The state-of-the-art lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have achieved 
remarkable commercial success, their energy density is approaching 
theoretical limits, and reliance on critical transition metals such as co­
balt and nickel raises sustainability and cost concerns. Other side, lith­
ium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have emerged as one of the most promising 
next-generation energy storage systems, owing to their exceptionally 
high theoretical energy density coupled with the natural abundance, low 
cost, and environmental benignity of sulfur. However, the practical 
realization of Li–S batteries remains elusive. The electrochemical con­
version of sulfur involves soluble lithium polysulfides that migrate be­
tween electrodes, causing active material loss, self-discharge, and poor 
cycle stability. In addition, the insulating nature of both sulfur and its 
discharge products, coupled with significant volume expansion during 
cycling, leads to poor kinetics and mechanical degradation.
Recent advances in advanced materials and cell design, including 
nanostructured sulfur hosts, catalytic interfaces, solid-state electrolytes, 
and engineered lithium anodes, have significantly mitigated these 
challenges. [1–5] Nevertheless, achieving high energy density, long- 
term stability, and scalability simultaneously continues to demand 
innovative strategies, along with a deeper understanding of the coupled 
redox, transport, and interfacial phenomena governing Li–S batteries is 
therefore critical to reveal their full practical potential.
As a practice including new battery startups, carbon-based frame­
works consisting of nanometre-sized pores are used in Li–S batteries 
and proven to enhance electrical contact and adsorb the lithium poly­
sulfides to mitigate the electrode loss problem. [6] Optimised carbon 
hosts consisting of both micropores to confine sulfur and mesopores to 
reduce charge transfer polarization, have demonstrated high sulfur 
utilization and improved high-rate capability [7,8] Despite these 
advanced hosts, complete suppression of the polysulfide shuttling in 
ether-based electrolytes remained elusive due to the high solubility and 
mobility of long-chain polysulfides.
To address these issues associated with ether-based electrolytes, 
persistent efforts are being devoted to the tuning of advanced electro­
lytes that severely limit polysulfide solubility. This has been achieved by 
increasing the concentration of lithium salts or introducing weakly

solvation environments get modified, and hence the amount of “free”

others incorporate fluorine-rich components such as fluoroethylene 
carbonate (FEC) [16] and lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)

Furthermore, with the introduction of carbonate-based electrolytes, 
a significantly enhanced cycle life and stability have been witnessed, as 
demonstrated in Table S2.There are two different explanations for this 
improved performance: one attributes this to confinement of short sulfur 
allotropes that follow a direct conversion of trapped sulfur to Li2S2/Li2S, 
leading to near absence of soluble long-chain polysulfides [20]. Another 
alternative hypothesis centres around the formation of a passivation 
layer due to nucleophilic reaction between long-chain polysulfides and 
carbonate solvents that limits the contact between the electrolyte and 
active material [16,21]. Both hypothesis confirms this by the absence of 
a redox plateau at ~2.4 V vs. Li+/Li in the first cycle, leading to trans­
formation of the redox mechanism from the solid(S)-liquid(Li2Sx(8 ≤x 
≤4)- solid(Li2S2/Li2S) to solid(S)-solid(Li2S2/Li2S). [20–22] However, 
trapping small sulfur allotropes at a temperature of 155 ◦C–200 ◦C 
during melt-infiltration is challenging and therefore, when the same 
cathodes are subjected to perform in ether-based electrolyte, they switch 
the redox mechanism back to a solid-liquid-solid mechanism, confirm­
ing the presence of long sulfur allotropes [22]. Although some re­
searchers have used very high infiltration temperature (upto 600 ◦C) to 
confine short sulfur molecules [23] within the porous host, the com­
bined influence of systematically varying the infiltration temperature 
and employing carbonate-based electrolyte has not yet been investi­
gated. [24]
Therefore, it will be interesting to study the synergistic effects of 
infiltration temperature that ensure the formation of different sulfur 
allotropes generated at different infiltration temperature and carbonate- 
based electrolyte solvent chemistry within the micro/mesoporous hosts, 
posing a critical knowledge gap of optimization and the solid-solid 
conversion mechanism in Li–S batteries. While melt-infiltration of 
sulfur into the carbon hosts is typically carried out at ~155 ◦C, the 
infiltration temperature is a critical factor, as increasing this tempera­
ture can intensify sulfur catenation (leading to the formation of long 
sulfur allotropes), and temperatures beyond 444.6 ◦C increase the con­
centration of short sulfur allotropes [2,25,26]. Therefore, in an attempt 
to fill the above-mentioned gap, we carried out an extensive study by 
infiltrating sulfur into the micro/mesoporous host at 250 ◦C (to ensure 
long-chain sulfur allotrope confinement) and at 450 ◦C (to ensure short 
sulfur allotrope confinement). Herein, we used a commercially available 
carbonate electrolyte consisting of 1 M LiPF6 in Ethylene Carbonate (EC) 
and Dimethyl Carbonate (DMC) solvent (in 1:1 v/v). We used various 
electrochemical techniques—including an in-depth analysis of the 
electrochemical performance via in-situ electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) followed by DRT, GITT, and PITT- to study the in­
fluence of melt-infiltration temperature on the performance of these 
Li–S cells containing carbonate electrolyte.
2. Experimental section
The synthesis of the host was done by following the procedure 
mentioned elsewhere [20]. First, the functionalization of CNT was car­
ried out. To prepare a batch, 50 mg of the as-treated CNT was dispersed 
in 25 ml of DI water using sonication. 2 g of glucose was added to the as- 
prepared solution, and finally, 5 mg of SDS was added. It was sonicated 
till a uniformly dispersed solution was obtained. It was then transferred 
to a Teflon-lined autoclave for hydrothermal synthesis. The autoclave 
was heated at 190 ◦C for 15 h to get a blackish-brown precipitate. The 
obtained precipitate was washed with DI water and then dried out at 
80 ◦C. After drying, the precipitate was heated at 5 ◦C min−1 to reach 
800 ◦C, and the holding time was set to 2 h. Finally, the host was 
obtained.
SMC 250 and SMC 450 were synthesized by taking host and sulfur in 
a 50:50 weight ratio in a pestle. Further, the material was ground to 
obtain a uniform mixture. The mixture was then transferred into a sealed 
ampoule and heated at 250 ◦C and 450 ◦C temperatures in a furnace. The 
obtained material was heated at 200 ◦C to ensure the removal of any 
sulfur adsorbed on the surface of the host.
To analyze the crystallinity of the host and uniform dispersion of 
sulfur in the host, powdered X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out 
using a Rigaku Smartlab X-ray diffractometer with source wavelength λ 
= 1.54 Å. To study the morphology of the prepared material Scanning 
electron Microscope (Zeiss/Ultra 55) and Transmission electron micro­
scope (FEI Tecnai G2, F30) were used. The thermo-gravimetric analysis 
of SMC 250 and SMC 450 was carried out using Hitachi NEXTA STA300 
in Ar atmosphere. For porosity studies, N2 adsorption and desorption on 
the samples were performed using St 2 on NOVA touch 4LX. Using the 
BET (Brunauer- Emmett-Teller) method specific surface area and DFT 
pore volume of pores having pore size lying in the range of 0 - 40 nm was 
calculated. The X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy was performed at the 
BL-10 beamline of Indus-2, using SPECS Phoibos 150 electron energy 
analyzer.
For fabrication of working electrode, a slurry was prepared by add­
ing composite material, Super-P (conducting carbon) and PVDF (binder) 
in an 8:1:1 weight ratio using NMP solvent. The obtained slurry was 
casted onto to Al-foil such that the loading was ~1 mg cm−2. The casted 
electrode was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 ◦C for 12 h. A circular disc of 
12 mm diameter was cut out of the dried electrode. The coin cell was 
assembled with the as-prepared electrode as the working electrode and 
lithium foil as the reference as well as a counter electrode separated by a 
Glass fiber separator. The Separator was soaked with LP30 electrolyte 
containing 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate and dimethyl carbonate 
solvent (in 1:1 v/v). The assembly was carried out under <0.5 ppm level 
of O2 as well as <0.5 ppm level H2O inside an Argon filled glove box 
(MBraun). These cells were used for various electrochemical tests such 
as cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD), in- 
situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), GITT and PITT. CS 
electrode were made from slurry consisting of 80 wt% sulfur, 10 wt% 
conducting carbon (super-P) and 10 wt% binder (PVDF). Symmetric 
cells such as Li||Li and CS||CS were fabricated using LP30. Further 
symmetric cell was prepared by hosting polysulfides such as Li2S6 and 
Li2S4 on carbon cloth (CC) termed as CC_Li2S6|| CC_Li2S6 and CC_Li2S4|| 
CC_Li2S4 respectively. Two cells of each polysulfide were prepared, one 
containing conventional ether based electrolyte (i.e., 1 M LiTFSI in DME: 
DOL (1:1 v/v)) and another containing LP30 in order to analyze the 
interaction of polysulfide with the electrolyte.
All the electrochemical cell testing was carried out at room tem­
perature, 27 ◦C ± 2 ◦C. Biologic VMP-3 was used to carry out cyclic 
voltammetry (CV) at a scan rate of 0.05 mV/s within a potential range of 
1–3 V vs. Li+/Li. Arbin BT-1000 and Neware Battery testing system was

3. Results
The melt-infiltration technique is one of the most facile techniques 
used widely for the infusion of sulfur [7,27]. As per theory, at the two 
chosen temperatures, i.e., 250 ◦C and 450 ◦C, encapsulation of long- 
chain sulfur and short-chain sulfur should take place, respectively. 
Simultaneously, the presence of carbonate and polysulfides formed 
during the lithiation of sulfur catalyzes a reaction near the pore's 
opening, leading to the formation of thiocarbonates. To utilize this re­
action to our favour, we need to develop a porous host with minimal 
pore width, i.e, smaller than 2–3 nm [18] that can hold the sulfur allo­
tropes and minimize the contact by forming a passivation layer.
Here, the porous host was engineered such that it can conduct 
electrons while providing an adequate pore structure for sulfur infil­
tration as demonstrated in Fig. 1 (a). Herein, we achieved this by coating 
highly conducting carbon nanotubes (CNTs) with an amorphous carbon 
derived from glucose termed as GMPC (i.e., glucose-derived micro/ 
mesoporous carbon). This resulting host was termed as GMPC@CNT 
hereafter. The formation of an amorphous coating over CNT was 
confirmed by powdered XRD and other microstructural analysis. As 
shown in Fig. S1, the transformation of sharp crystalline Bragg's 
diffraction peaks of CNT corresponding to the plane (100) and (002) to 
wide hump-like peaks indicates a layer of amorphous carbon that had 
deposited above the CNT. Furthermore, as can be seen from the high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images (Fig. 1
(b-d)), the host appears like a concentric cable-like structure, with CNT 
functioning as a core structure and amorphous glucose-derived carbon 
(GMPC) as a sheath. The pore size distribution analysis is done by the 
BET experiments, which indicate the presence of micropores and mes­
opores (Fig. S2 (a,b)). These pores enable the sulfur confinement upon 
melt-infiltration. Moreover, the morphological characterization of the 
host through SEM reveals rod-like morphology (Fig. S2c).
Heating of sulfur at 250 ◦C results predominantly in polymeric chains 
but with a viscosity below the peak viscosity that occurs at ~200 ◦C. 
However, heating beyond this temperature leads to an increase in the 
fragmental sulfur allotropes with short sulfur chain length [28]. 
Therefore, melt infiltration at the two predetermined temperatures, i.e., 
250 ◦C and 450 ◦C was carried out. In the scenario where the sample is 
heated at 250 ◦C, we anticipate the confinement of higher concentra­
tions of long-chain sulfur allotropes. Conversely, when the sample is 
heated at 450 ◦C, a significant concentration of shorter chain allotropes 
is anticipated. The higher the temperature, the lower the viscosity of 
sulfur and based on this assumption, we were expecting the confinement 
of sulfur to take place as indicated in Fig. 2a. Further, the transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) results of SMC 450 and SMC 250 samples 
indicate that melt infiltration didn't modify the morphology of the host 
(Fig. 2b and g). The host's surface does not exhibit any agglomerated 
solid mass, suggesting almost no sulfur is present above the surface. The 
line scan of the sample SMC 250 and SMC 450 along the line (Fig. 2c and 
h) demonstrates a dip near the core, indicating sulfur has been infused 
predominantly into the pores of the sheath of the host [29]. Further, the 
elemental mapping of carbon and sulfur indicates that carbon is present 
throughout the host, whereas the intensity of sulfur is higher along the 
sheath and lower along the core. The elemental mapping of the samples 
indicated a uniform dispersion of sulfur over the sheath of carbon 
(Fig. 2d, e, i and j). Moreover, it can be seen that carbon is present as an 
outline to sulfur in the layover image of SMC 450 (Fig. 2k). This in­
dicates that sulfur has been predominantly filled deeper inside the host's 
pores at this temperature.

Further, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were collected 
to understand the binding strength of the sulfur present in our host. In 
Fig. 2p and q, the S 2p region spectrum of SMC-250 and SMC-450 is 
demonstrated, respectively. Each S 2p XPS was fitted with 3 peaks. The 
first peak ~164 eV and the second peak ~165 eV arise due to the spin- 
orbit coupling of S 2p electrons corresponding to S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2, 
respectively. For simplicity, the variations in S 2p peaks will be 
explained by variations in S 2p1/2. The last peak ~167 eV is due to the 
oxidized sulfur, i.e., sulphates, sulphites, etc. [34] This mainly arises due 
to impurities and oxidation of sulfur. In the case of SMC 250, as long- 
chain polymerized sulfur covered at the surface of the pores, the bind­
ing energy of S 2p1/2 is at 164.01 eV [35] i.e., nearly the same binding 
energy as that of the elemental sulfur having S–S bond, whereas the 
same peak is found at 163.74 eV in case of SMC 450 [34,36]. This shift in 
SMC 450 spectra is attributed to the weak bond formed due to the 
entrapment of sulfur inside the pores of the host structure. Since the 
melt-infiltration temperature is high in SMC 450, most impurities escape 
from the surface; therefore, a smaller intensity of the impurity peak was 
observed in the case of SMC 450. Moreover, deconvoluted C 1 s spectra 
show three peaks, one near 284.6 eV corresponding C–C bond, another 
around 286 eV corresponding to the C-S/C-O bond, and lastly, one peak 
around 290 eV corresponding to the O-C=O bond [37] However, there is 
a minute difference in the peak position of C 1 s spectra of SMC 450 and 
SMC 250 (Fig. S3), indicating there might be some impact of melt- 
infiltration temperature on the host. To confirm this, Raman Spectros­
copy of the host, SMC 250 and SMC 450, was carried out. (Fig. S4). The 
Raman spectroscopy demonstrated two peaks corresponding to first- 
order Raman scattering. One peak is attributed to the in-plane stretch­
ing vibration of sp2 carbon corresponding to the G-band (i.e., E2g sym­
metry) around 1580 cm−1, whereas the other peak is attributed to the 
breathing mode of disordered graphite-like carbon rings, corresponding 
to the D-band (i.e., A1g symmetry) around 1350 cm−1 [38,39]. A similar 
G-band is seen for pure graphite sample containing sp2 carbon, and a D- 
band around ~1350 cm−1 is witnessed in disordered graphitic structures 
[40]. This disorder leads to the breaking of translational symmetry, 
giving rise to disorder-induced Raman scattering. The coexistence of 
both G- and D-bands suggests that the host contains carbon domains that 
exhibit a partial graphitized domain, along with significant amorphous 
regions that align well with our objective of designing a conductive host 
structure. Furthermore, the ratio between the intensity of the G-band 
and D-band provides the degree of disorder present in the sample. The 
Id/Ig ratio of the host (1.073) is greater than SMC 250 (1.107), which in 
turn is greater than SMC 450 (1.148). This trend implies that with 
increasing temperature, improved infiltration of sulfur into the host led 
to an increase in disorder.
From all the physical analyses above, we can conclude that SMC 450 
has a comparatively lower total sulfur loading, but it has a higher 
amount of sulfur inside the pores, whereas in SMC 250, sulfur largely 
resided near the opening of the pores (Table S1). This shall influence the 
electrochemical performance, as good confinement will minimize the 
polysulfide shuttling and will help to retain the contact of insulating 
sulfur with the electron-conducting host. This has been further explored 
in electrochemical experiments and structure-property correlations.
Fig. 3 demonstrates the galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) profile 
and cyclic voltammetry of SMC 250||Li and SMC 450||Li cells containing 
LP30 electrolyte which is composed of 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DMC (1:1 in v/ 
v). These cells were run at a room temperature of 27 ◦C ± 2 ◦C. The GCD 
profile during the 1st discharge of SMC 250 demonstrates a clean 
plateau at ~2.4 V and another plateau at ~1.77 V vs. Li/Li+. The first 
plateau corresponds to a reduction of sulfur at the opening of the pores 
(surficial sulfur) to long-chain polysulfides, and the second plateau 
corresponds to solid-solid conversion of sulfur, which resides inside the 
pores [41]. Unlike SMC-250, SMC-450 has a negligible amount of sur­
ficial sulfur, giving one prominent plateau at ~1.8 V vs. Li+/Li [8]. The 
presence of any surficial sulfur in the as-prepared sample leads to direct 
contact between elemental sulfur and the carbonate, leading to the 
formation of long-chain polysulfides (PS) upon lithiation. As the 
discharge proceeds, these long-chain PS react with the electrolyte, and 
those not in direct contact with the electrolyte get lithiated to form short 
polysulfides upon arrival of the Li+ ion and de-solvated at the opening of 
the pore due to steric hindrance [35]. During the second plateau, the 
reduced short polysulfides as well as confined sulfur gets converted to 
Li2S2 /Li2S. Such conversion of solid S8 to long-chain PS and long-chain 
PS to short polysulfides, such as Li2S2/Li2S, is seen in the SMC 250 
sample in the first cycle, which is typically witnessed in an ether-based 
electrolyte. As in the Li–S cell consisting of ether-based electrolytes, S8 
gets lithiated to form Li2Sx (8 ≤x ≤4)) at ~2.4 V and finally to solid 
(Li2S2/Li2S), demonstrating a similar two-step discharge curve. [42]
Further, in SMC-250, an activation polarization of ~33 mV was 
observed, whereas in the case of SMC-450, it is ~14 mV (Fig. 3c.). This 
implies that even the type of sulfur allotrope confined might be different 
and hence created a need to study in detail. Since the long-chain PS are 
nucleophilic, it attacks the electrophilic carbonate solvent to create a 
passivation layer that breaks the direct contact between the electrolyte 
and electrode [13]. To test the film formation on the cathode, a Carbon 
-Sulfur (C–S) cell was used. This cell contained Carbon (Super-P) – 
sulfur composite (50:50 wt%) as active material in the cathode, lithium 
as anode, and a separator soaked in LP 30 electrolyte. Then, the GCD of 
this cell was carried out for 5 cycles at 30 mA gcomp

. After 5 cycles, the 
cathode was obtained from the cell, and its surface characterization was

Henceforth, from the second cycle onward in both SMC 450 and SMC 
250, only solid-solid conversion occurs, leading to a continuous phase 
change that provides us with a sloppy charge-discharge voltage profile. 
Furthermore, the SMC-250 sample demonstrates comparatively higher 
activation polarization (i.e., ~12 mV), whereas it becomes almost 
negligible in the case of SMC-450 (Fig. 3d.). The overpotential due to the 
concentration polarization during the 2nd cycle is higher for SMC-250 
than SMC-450 (i.e., 360 mV vs. 230 mV). This reduction in the 
voltage gap between the charge and discharge profile during the 2nd 
cycle, both in the GCD and in the CV for both the cells, compared to the 
1st cycle, suggests that a rearrangement of active materials has taken 
place in the carbon host [8]. Similar to the GCD, the 1st cycle from the 
CV profile also demonstrates two cathodic peaks and one anodic peak 
for SMC-250, whereas there is only one cathodic and one pair of redox 
peaks for SMC-450, respectively. The anodic peak is associated with the 
formation of Li2S2/Li2S, and it is sharper and occurs at a higher reduc­
tion potential for SMC-450 than in SMC-250. The greater extent of sulfur 
confinement provided a faster electron-conducting pathway and a lesser 
overpotential, thereby enhancing the kinetics of SMC-450.

The DRT analysis on both SMC 250 and SMC 450 cells after 1st 
discharge, demonstrated 7 different local maxima (Fig. S6a, S6b). Each 
maximum is attributed to one individual electrochemical process that 
take place in the cell with different time constants. The peak P1 occurs at 
a fast relaxation time and changes minutely with charge-discharge 
process. It is associated with the interparticle resistance faced by 
charged particles, most likely electrons [47]. The peak P2 lies in the time 
range of 10−5 s and is attributed to the resistance faced due to the 
double-layer formed at the electrode surface [48,49]. It is predomi­
nantly present in the DRT of CS||CS symmetric cell in Fig. S6f. The peak 
P3 is found in the range of 10−4 s, and it is attributed to the solid elec­
trolyte interphase formed on the electrodes [48]. In our case, it is visible 
in the Li||Li symmetric cell containing LP30 electrolyte (Fig. S6c). Even 
in CC_Li2Sn||Li2Sn_CC (n ≥4) symmetric cells (with LP30 electrolyte), 
there is a presence of peak between 10−4 s and 10−3 s, indicating a 
passivation layer is formed on the electrode surface due to reaction 
between long PS and carbonate solvent which shows properties similar 
to the SEI form on the anode (Fig. S6d, S6e). This implies that peak P3 
demonstrates the process of film formation on the electrodes due to 
some irreversible changes. The peak P4 occurs around 10−3 s time scale 
and it has been attributed to ionic-pore resistance as suggested by Gerle 
et al. specifically for microporous electrodes [47]. The range of time 
constants in which peak P5 and peak P6 lie is the range of relaxation 
time constants where reactions due to charge transfer takes place 
[47–49]. The peak P5 has a time constant of 10−2 s, similar to the peak 
found in CC_Li2S4||Li2S4_CC cell with Li2S4 polysulfide in the ether-based 
solvent (Fig. S6g). Hence, it is assigned to the presence of short PS 
formed from either short sulfur allotropes' lithiation or from long PS 
reduction. Similarly, P6 has a time constant as same as that of in 
CC_Li2S6||Li2S6_CC cell with Li2S6 polysulfide in the ether-based solvent 
(Fig. S6h). Even Song et al. in their DRT analysis found a similar peak 
between 10−2 s – 1 s [50] and concluded it to be the initiation of lith­
iation of sulfur to form polysulfides. Hence, peak P6 is allotted to PS 
formed by the lithiation of resistive elemental sulfur. The slight devia­
tion may be due to limited contact between the electrolyte and the 
confined sulfur. Lastly, the peak P7 is allotted to the diffusion process of 
lithium-ions in various literature [48]. Herein, this case, the resistance 
associated with the peak reduces as the infinite diffusion layer becomes 
a finite passive layer due to the formation of insulating Li2S [49].
Even before the discharging, the freshly prepared Li–S cells con­
taining ether-based electrolyte tend to form lithium polysulfides due to 
the direct contact between the electrolyte and sulfur [51]. Hence, to 
confirm this, EIS followed by DRT of the freshly prepared two cells SMC 
250||Li and SMC 450||Li with LP30 at OCV were carried out (Fig.S7a, 
S7b). At OCV, unlike peak P6, all other peaks are common in both the 
DRT. The presence of peak P1 associated with interparticle resistance, 
peak P2 associated with double-layer, peak P3 associated with solid 
electrolyte interphase, and peak P4 associated with ionic pore resistance 
are valid. The presence of peak P6 in the DRT of SMC 250 only, indicates 
the formation of lithiated polysulfides, implying the presence of direct 
contact of sulfur and electrolyte, whereas absence of P6 in SMC 450 
indicates negligible direct contact. This implies that the confinement of 
sulfur is better in the case of SMC 450 sample rather than SMC 250.
Further, to analyze the mechanism of SMC-250 and SMC-450, EIS at 
different state of charge on the 1st and 2nd plateau of the 1st cycle in 
both cells were carried out, followed by their DRT analysis. These points 
can be seen in Fig. S7c and S7d. Fig. 4. demonstrates the variation of 
DRT peaks as a function of charge-discharge voltages, elucidating the 
following electrochemical transformations taking place.
During the discharge process, the peaks associated with charge 
transfer reactions leading to the initiation of the conversion of sulfur to 
polysulfides and further conversion to short-chain polysulfides like Li2Sn 
(2 ≤n ≤4) witness a dependency on voltage i.e., peak P6 and peak P5. 
Due to the onset of polysulfide formation around 2.3 V–2.4 V, an in­
crease in the intensity of peak P6 and the simultaneous emergence of 
peak P5 (Fig. 4a, 4b) were observed. The co-existence of these peaks 
highlights the multi-step redox pathways usually witnessed in Li–S 
chemistry. In Fig. 4c and 4d, we witnessed noticeable broadening and an 
increase in intensity of the peak P5, indicating increased formation of 
short-chain polysulfides. Moreover, the observed relative increase in 
peak intensity of P6 is higher for SMC 250 (~ 37 Ω) at 1.71 V than SMC 
450 (~ 17.5 Ω) at 1.68 V. An increase in the area corresponding to a 
particular peak P6 indicates either the presence of a larger concentration 
of the same resistive species or the presence of a species that is more 
comparatively resistive in nature. Around 1.7 V, sulfur confined inside 
the pores begins to get lithiated. Since SMC-450 exhibits higher sulfur 
confinement in comparison to SMC 250 (as determined by TGA), then 
SMC 450 should have higher resistance associated with peak P6 than 
SMC 250, but the case is otherwise. This discrepancy confirms that the 
nature of sulfur trapped in SMC-250 is more resistive than that trapped 
in SMC-450. According to the work of Fedyaeva et al.; short-chain al­
lotropes, such as S2 and S4 are lesser resistive in comparison to longer 
chain sulfur allotropes, i.e., Sn>6 [52]. Therefore, it is confirmed that 
SMC 250 has successfully confined a higher concentration of longer- 
chain sulfur allotrope, whereas SMC 450 has confined a comparatively 
short-chain sulfur allotrope; hence, SMC 450 is less resistive to Li-ions.
As the discharge proceeds, further reduction of short-chain poly­
sulfides to Li2S2/Li2S takes place; therefore, the area under P5 increased, 
whereas the area under peak P6 diminishes, indicating consumption of 
lithiated sulfur. Furthermore, at the low frequency regime, the trans­
formation of the Warburg diffusion tail to a semicircle towards the end 
of the discharge takes place. Therefore, the intensity of P7 reduces as the 
film formation proceeds due to growth and nucleation of insoluble 
lithium sulfide species (Li2S2 /Li2S) that takes place upon reaching the 
2nd discharge plateau. The peak reduces till the elemental sulfur gets 
converted to solid lithium sulfide species. Lastly, the presence of a 
relatively high intensity of peak P6 in SMC 250 compared to SMC 450 
towards the end of discharge implies SMC 250 has comparatively less 
sulfur utilization, i.e., partial conversion to Li2S2/Li2S.
Moreover, the variation of peak P3 while discharging from OCV till 
1.71 V for SMC 250 and 1.68 V for SMC 450 is due to the formation of a 
passivation layer due to the reaction between long-chain polysulfides 
and carbonate electrolytes. After this point, the passivation layer for­
mation slowed down, as a result of which the intensity of peak P3 re­
mains almost constant. While discharging proceeds from OCV to 1 V, the 
intensity magnitude of peak P3 in the case of SMC 250 shows an 
increasing trend of ~27 Ω, whereas the magnitude of intensity of peak 
P3 in SMC 450 witnessed only an increase of ~15 Ω. The difference 
implies again that the passivation layer formed on SMC 250 is thicker 
and more resistive compared to SMC 450. This is due to the presence of a 
higher amount of sulfur at the opening of the pore in the case of SMC 250 
compared to the SMC 450 sample. The thicker passivation layer can 
further give rise to a sluggish reaction kinetics for SMC 250, leading to 
higher overpotential. To mimic this, we designed two symmetric cells 
with different concentrations of Li2S6 and carried out their EIS. The cell 
consisted of stainless steel blocking electrodes separated by a separator. 
Separator of one cell consisted of 1 M Li2S6 (50 μl) and LP30 electrolyte, 
and another 0.5 M Li2S6 (50 μl) and LP30. The EIS of the cells are 
demonstrated in Fig. S8. These EIS concluded a higher concentration of 
lithium polysulfide developed higher resistance, (i.e., the resistance 
increased from 6 Ω to 35 Ω).Further, to confirm the stability of this 
passivation layer, we tested pretreated SMC 450 and SMC 250 in con­
ventional ether-based electrolyte and lithium metal as anodes. These 
cells demonstrated solid-solid conversion mechanism even in the pres­
ence of ether based solvents (in Fig. S9) [21].

The relation between characteristic time constant (t), resistance (R) 
and capacitance (C) due to the process is given by t = RC. However, to 
take into account for the microscopic inhomogeneities and complex 
process, constant phase element (Q1/α) is used and the relation modifies 
into τ = RQ1/α. The peak P1, P3 and P4 of SMC 250 and peak P2 of SMC 
450 have high resistance as well as higher relaxation time τ. However, 
peak P5 and P6 of SMC 450 have a higher relaxation time τ (i.e.,0.483 s 
vs. 0.459 s for peak P6 and 1.15 s vs. 0.97 s for P5 peak) despite having 
lower resistance, implying they must higher capacitance. Since the 
sulfur has a higher degree of confinement in SMC 450, these peaks are 
also slightly broadened in comparison to SMC 250. The high capacitance 
at a deeper extent of confinement indicates increased area of electro­
chemically active sites. Hence, proving SMC 450 sample a faster ki­

the 1st cycle to the 2nd cycle i.e., from ~79.5 Ω to ~83.2 Ω whereas in 
SMC 250, it decreased from ~120 Ω to ~114.7 Ω. The increase in net 
resistance of SMC 450 was due to increased resistance of peak P2 and P7, 
implying an increase in interparticle resistance and formation of Li2S2/ 
Li2S, respectively (Fig. 4u, 4v). In SMC 250, the resistance associated 
with peak P1 and P4 has increased but the height of peak P7 has reduced 
in the 2nd discharge process indicating less formation of Li2S2/Li2S 
(Fig. 4r, 4 s.). The higher formation of Li2S2/Li2S implies higher sulfur 
utilization of SMC 450. These changes in resistance, broadening and 
relaxation time clearly communicate that the SMC 450 electrode has a) a 
thinner passivation layer formed on the cathode, b) better diffusion, and 
c) lesser resistive sulfur allotropes. Further, parallel experiments were 
carried out to confirm the above facts.
As the sulfur allotropes confined in the two samples and having 
different resistances, the initiation of their redox reactions should take 
place at different potentials [53,54]. Here, the Potentiometric Inter­
mittent titration technique (PITT) was used to precisely identify the 
reduction potential of the sulfur allotropes confined inside SMC 250 and 
SMC 450. In this method, the potential was increased/decreased in small 
consecutive steps of 30 mV, and was held for 30 min at each step. Before 
approaching the redox potential, the current response decayed 
completely to zero within the holding time frame. However, upon 
approaching the redox potential, the consumption of charge became 
substantial, and the current response didn't decay to zero within that 
holding time frame. This potential at which the decay of current 
response begins to take a longer time marks the initiation of the reaction 
[55]. We analysed this deviation of the current response to mark the 
reduction potential at which the initiation of the reduction of sulfur 
allotropes present inside the pores takes place. The initial current 
response against each applied step potentials is due to the capacitive- 
type discharging process (Fig. 5a,b). Such deviation in SMC 250 first 
takes place at ~2.48 V potential indicating initiation of sulfur to long 
polysulfides further at ~1.833 V vs. Li+/Li, indicating the beginning of 
the formation of Li2S2/Li2S whereas in SMC 450 such deviation is wit­
nessed directly at ~1.955 V for SMC 450, implying direct conversion of 
short-chain sulfur allotrope to formation of Li2S2/Li2S. Moreover, in the 
case of SMC 450, the conversion of Li2S to higher PS during charging, 
takes place at a lower voltage (i.e., 1.351 V) in comparison of SMC 250 
(i.e., 1.696 V) against Li+/Li. This revealed that the commencement of 
short PS is relatively faster in SMC 450 sample due to the lower charge- 
transfer resistance. Further, the sulfur utilization in both cells is ach­
ieved by estimating the area under the peak near 1.8 V vs. Li+/Li in each 
case. The bar graph indicating the sulfur utilization in the SMC 450 
sample was high throughout the 1st and 2nd cycles (Fig. 5c). This could 
be due to better distribution of sulfur at 450 ◦C having access to a well- 
connected electron conducting channel, a lesser resistive passivation 
layer or lesser activation overpotential and better reaction kinetics. 
Hence, to acquire information about the factors that are responsible for 
such behavior is kinetically favorable or thermodynamically favorable, 
we carried out Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique (GITT) of 
SMC 450 and SMC 250. It is evident from Fig. 5d, that the phase con­
version attained during the 2nd plateau of the 1st discharge curve for 
SMC 450 has a higher thermodynamic potential than SMC 250, con­
firming the presence of different sulfur allotropes as rest other param­
eters such as pore size of host and amount of electrolyte employed for 
the cells were kept same in both cases. These imply that thermodynamic 
factors should receive a fair share of credit for the better electrochemical 
performance of SMC 450.
Further, DRT data hinted that SMC 450 has better Li-ion diffusion 
and a thinner passivation layer, and hence lesser overpotential. To 
confirm this, factors like overpotential and lithium-ion apparent diffu­
sion coefficients of both the samples were estimated from GITT. Fig. 5e,f. 
demonstrates the apparent diffusion coefficients, and Fig. 5g and h 
demonstrates the overpotential associated with SMC 450 and SMC 250, 
respectively. The apparent diffusional coefficient of SMC-450 estimated 
at these potentials was larger than SMC-250, indicating SMC-450 has 
better kinetics. The overpotential of SMC-250 was higher than SMC-450, 
especially at the beginning of the discharge process, but as it approached 
the second plateau, the overpotentials of both the cells became almost 
similar, indicating lithiated sulfur species are less resistive in compari­
son to elemental sulfur. However, the high overpotential of SMC-250 
cathode at 2.3 V vs. Li+/Li can be attributed to the presence of a 
comparatively higher resistive sulfur allotrope as discussed above.

4. Discussion
This study aimed to enhance the performance of Li–S cells by 
exploiting a facile parameter, like melt-infiltration temperature in the 
presence of commercial carbonate-based electrolyte, i.e., LP30. A set of 
characterization and electrochemical techniques was carried out to 
understand the influence of these parameters.
XRD data indicated the absence of crystalline sulfur on the surface of 
the host upon melt infiltration but it did not reveal any significant in­
formation about the phase change in sulfur cathode at the two chosen 
melt infiltration temperatures i.e., 250 ◦C and 450 ◦C. However, SEM 
analysis of SMC 250 and SMC 450 demonstrated no agglomeration of the 
sulfur on the surface of the host, implying sulfur was successfully infused 
into the host. Complementarily, TGA data informed us that lower tem­
perature enhanced the sulfur loading; however, it reduced the sulfur 
infiltration depth as SMC 450 has a higher amount of sulfur in the pores 
as compared to SMC 250. This indicated that with an increase in tem­
perature, the extent of confinement increased from near the opening of 
the pores to deep inside the pores. Further, XPS analysis demonstrated 
that sulfur infiltrated deep into the pores as seen in the case of SMC 450, 
forms a weak bond between the host and sulfur whereas sulfur infil­
trated just near the opening of pores demonstrates bond strength similar 
to that of molecular sulfur. Moreover, the Raman analysis of the host, 
SMC 250 and SMC 450 showed changes in the degree of disorder. The 
ratio Id/Ig increased in the order Host < SMC 250 < SMC 450. This 
suggested that the lower infiltration temperature of SMC 250 resulted in 
a good loading, whereas the higher infiltration temperature of SMC 450 
provided better confinement. We believe that higher temperature shall 
generate a higher concentration of short sulfur allotrope, which will 
have comparatively lesser resistance, and not only that the greater 
extent of confinement should provide an enhanced electron conduction 
network to the insulating sulfur. Lastly, this confinement should 
improve the cycle life of the cells by confining the as-generated poly­
sulfides in the host. Therefore, comparatively higher sulfur utilization, 
faster kinetics and reduced overpotential is expected out of SMC 450.
Further, electrochemical analysis, such as CV and GCD, was carried 
out to understand the impact of sulfur loading and confinement inside 
the host. Herein, the electrode mass was kept almost same, and current 
density was provided on the basis of the composite so that analysis of 
SMC 250 and SMC 450 as a composite can be carried out fairly. CV 
analysis demonstrates an improved redox reaction for SMC 450. The 
charge-discharge profile of SMC 450, presented a lesser activation 
overpotential and a lower voltage separation between the charge- 
discharge plateaus during the 1st as well as 2nd cycle profiles. More­
over, the GCD profile demonstrated SMC 250 has higher irreversible first 
cycle capacity loss compared to SMC 450, i.e., 218 mAh gcomp
−1 
vs. 174 
mAh gcomp
−1
. These outcomes indicate SMC 450 is a better candidate in 
comparison to SMC 250. To investigate the reason behind improved 
performance and understand the mechanism of both the cells, in-situ 
EIS, followed by its DRT analysis of the first cycle, was specifically 
carried out.
In time dependent DRT analysis of SMC 250, the presence of peak P6, 
even at OCV, confirmed the presence of direct contact between surficial 
sulfur and carbonate solvent, leading to the formation of long-chain 
polysulfides. Both in SMC 250 and SMC 450, as the discharge reaction 
proceeded, the peak P3 intensity kept on increasing till the peak P6 
attained a minimum resistance. This indicated that the presence of direct 
contact between long-chain polysulfides and carbonates led to side re­
actions. The increment of resistance associated with P3 indicated that 
the byproducts led to the formation of a passivation layer on the cath­
ode, as reported in the literature. The increase in the peak P3 is higher in 
SMC 250 as compared to SMC 450, indicating that the passivation is 
higher in SMC 250. This can also be confirmed from the presence of a 
bigger plateau at ~2.3 V vs. Li+/Li in the first cycle of GCD profile of 
SMC 250. This plateau was completely absent in the second cycle. This 
indicates that all the non-confined long polysulfides reacted with the 
electrolyte, leading to a higher 1st cycle loss. As the discharge pro­
ceeded, peaks associated with short-chain polysulfide i.e., P5 increased 
and the resistance associated with peak P7 reduced due to the formation 
of Li2S, leading to the conversion of infinite diffusion length to semi- 
infinite diffusion length. Further, upon charging, the reverse trend was 
seen.
Further, from the second cycle onwards, instead of a two-step 
discharge profile, only one sloping plateau was witnessed in the GCD 
profile. Thi confirmed that the direct contact between the polysulfide 
and electrolyte is broken, but the presence of peak P6 in the DRT 
analysis of EIS acquired at the end of 2nd discharge implied long-chain 
polysulfides are still present, although in minute quantities. Moreover, it 
also implied that the utilization of sulfur is high in the case of SMC 450. 
To determine whether the performance is thermodynamically controlled 
or kinetically controlled, PITT and GITT analyses were done. The faster 
initiation of oxidation and reduction of sulfur near ~2 V in SMC 450 
implied it has better electrode kinetics. To understand the thermody­
namic angle, GITT analysis were carried out. The higher thermodynamic 
sulfur reduction potential near ~2 V vs. Li+/Li in the GITT analysis of 
the 1st discharge cycle is comparatively higher for SMC 450, i.e., ~2.1 V 
vs. ~2.05 V for SMC 250. This indicated that sulfur species confined in 
SMC 250 are different from those in SMC 450. Further, the lower 
overpotential and higher lithium-ion diffusion of SMC 450 during the 
discharging state indicate SMC 450 has even better kinetics.
From the above results, it is evident that higher infiltration temper­
ature enhances the Li–S battery performance by improving sulfur uti­
lization, extent of sulfur confinement, reducing the long-chain sulfur 
allotrope concentration and increasing the small-chain sulfur concen­
tration, and as smaller sulfur allotrope has lesser resistance and a lesser 
tendency to form passivating layer, thereby lowering overall total 
resistance, and increasing Li-ion mobility. Notably, this performance is 
comparable to other carbonate based Li–S systems given in Table S2, 
without the need of very high sulfur infiltrating temperature (~600 ◦C) 
often employed to form short-chain sulfur species, thereby reducing the 
input energy. However, higher sulfur utilization leads to Li2S accumu­
lation in SMC 450, which shows its effect after 40 cycles, because of 
which there is initiation of capacity fade followed by a linear capacity 
decay. This can be avoided by careful engineering of the host structure 
by the introduction of weakly polar surface functional groups or defect- 
rich regions, such that nucleation and growth of Li2S get dispersed or by 
the addition of a catalyst that can promote efficient conversion of Li2S to 
long-chain polysulfides.
All these results are consistent and imply that the SMC 450 composite 
cathode sample is a promising candidate with respect to confined active 
material (i.e., mAh gs
−1). These advantages are achieved at a relatively 
low sulfur mass fraction in the composite. Hence, to attain the advan­
tages of SMC 450 cathode, it is essential to increase the sulfur loading 
while preserving the confinement of short-chain sulfur state. In this 
work, melt infiltration was adopted to obtain these cathodes as it is 
experimentally straightforward and scalable, but it did not provide the 
sulfur loading required for practical Li–S while preserving the short- 
chain sulfur state. However, in future, other infiltration protocols such

Moreover, this work emphasizes on the mechanistic analysis of Li–S 
cells employing carbonate electrolyte, wherein we witnessed confine­
ment of short-chain sulfur along with limited electrolyte and active 
material contact, resulting in solid-solid S to Li2S conversion leading to 
better sulfur utilization and reduced polysulfide dissolution. However, 
upon translating key design principles, namely formation of stabilized 
ionically conducting passivation layer between the electrode and elec­
trolyte interface, suppression or confinement of reactive polysulfide 
species and tunning the availability of polysulfide dissolving solvents 
can help us achieve similar solid-solid conversion. In the meantime, the 
same system can be translated to a conventional ether-based electrolyte, 
but the high solubility of polysulfides in these electrolytes can sub­
stantially modify the formation of intermediate species, thereby varying 
the reaction pathway and the rate-limiting step. Therefore, our current 
results should be viewed as a mechanistic insight specific to carbonate- 
based electrolyte, while a detailed analysis of the system in an ether- 
based electrolyte is left for future work.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we studied the direct impact of melt-infiltration tem­
perature on confining sulfur into the microporous host. High tempera­
ture not only reduced the viscosity but it also developed short sulfur 
allotropes, which, upon being confined, formed a weak bond with the 
host. The low viscosity of sulfur at 450 ◦C led to confinement of sulfur 
deep inside the pores, whereas in the case of SMC 250, a large amount of 
sulfur resided at the opening of the pore. Further, the usage of carbonate 
electrolyte helped in the formation of a passivation film resulting from 
the reaction of nucleophilic polysulfide and carbonate solvent. Since 
SMC 450 had a minimum amount of sulfur at the opening, it led to the 
formation of lesser resistive film in comparison to SMC 250.
Moreover, the confirmation of the presence of sulfur having different 
resistance was obtained using non-destructive EIS analysis. Higher 
extent of confinement, lesser resistive sulfur allotrope, and lesser resis­
tive passivation provided SMC 450 with fast charge transfer kinetics and 
comparatively high sulfur utilization. However, higher sulfur utilization 
led to higher formation of insulating Li2S2/Li2S in SMC 450, which 
resulted in sudden capacity decay after 30 cycles, whereas no such rapid 
decay was witnessed in SMC 250. Therefore, higher melt-infiltration 
temperature can improve the electrochemical performance of Li–S 
cell to a greater extent, but to sustain this performance we need to assure 
better conversion Li2S to long-chain polysulfides. Although a lower melt- 
infiltration temperature can give a stable cycle life but a lesser specific 
capacity. Hence, by modifying the temperature of melt-infiltration alone 
we can tune the performance of the Li–S batteries.
