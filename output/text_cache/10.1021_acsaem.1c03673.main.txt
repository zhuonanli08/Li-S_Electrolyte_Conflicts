ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur is a “beyond-Li-ion” battery chemistry attractive for
its high energy density coupled with low-cost sulfur. Expanding to the MWh
required for grid scale energy storage, however, requires a diﬀerent approach for
reasons of safety, scalability, and cost. Here we demonstrate the marriage of the
redox-targeting scheme to the engineered Li solid electrolyte interphase (SEI),
enabling a scalable, high eﬃciency, membrane-less Li−S redox ﬂow battery. In this
hybrid ﬂow battery architecture, the Li anode is housed in the electrochemical cell,
while the solid sulfur is safely kept in a separate catholyte reservoir and electrolyte
is pumped over the sulfur and into the electrochemical cell. Electrochemically
facile decamethylferrocene and cobaltocene are chosen as redox mediators to kick-
start the initial reduction of solid S into soluble polysulﬁdes and ﬁnal reduction of
polysulﬁdes into solid Li2S, precluding the need for conductive carbons. On the anode side, a LiI and LiNO3 pretreatment strategy
encourages a stable SEI and lessens capacity fade, avoiding use of ion-selective separators. Complementary materials characterization
conﬁrms the uniform distribution of LiI in the SEI, while SEM conﬁrms the presence of lower surface area globular Li deposition and
UV−vis corroborates evolution of the polysulﬁde species. Equivalent areal loadings of up to 50 mgS cm−2 (84 mAh cm−2) are
demonstrated, with high capacity and voltage eﬃciency at 1−2 mgS cm−2 (973 mAh gS
−1 and 81.3% VE in static cells and 1142 mAh
gS
−1 and 86.9% VE in ﬂow cells). These results imply that the fundamental Li−S chemistry and SEI engineering strategies can be
adapted to the hybrid redox ﬂow battery architecture, obviating the need for ion-selective membranes or ﬂowing carbon additives,
and oﬀering a potential pathway for inexpensive, scalable, and safe MWh scale Li−S energy storage.
KEYWORDS: ﬂow battery, energy storage, grid scale, lithium, sulfur, redox targeting
■INTRODUCTION
There is a pressing need for inexpensive, safe, and reliable
batteries with a high capacity for grid storage applications.
According to Bloomberg, the average cost for Li-ion cells
ranged as low as $100−130/kWh in 2021. This can be thought
of as a threshold needed to be economically competitive. The
high capacity (1675 mAh gS
−1) and low cost (∼$0.1 kg−1) of S
make Li−S batteries ideal for this application as their
theoretical energy density is 2600 Wh kg−1.1−3 Expanding
Li−S to MWh-GWh grid scale capacities, however, necessitates
a change in cell architecture. Li−S battery cathodes are
commonly made as ﬁlms, where S/carbon slurries are cast onto
metal foil current collectors. As the areal S loading, and thus
theoretical capacity, of a cell is increased beyond 4−5 mg
cm−2, there are diminishing returns on the energy density of
the cell due to the decreased accessibility of the S in the back
of the ﬁlm, although higher S loadings have been demonstrated
at lower capacities.4,5 At grid scale, decreasing the nonactive
materials could signiﬁcantly reduce costs. Moreover, in ﬁlm
implementations, the anode and cathode are separated by <0.1
mm, preventing eﬀective control of energy release during
possible thermal runaway events.6
Redox ﬂow batteries (RFBs), on the other hand, oﬀer a safe,
easily scalable architecture amenable for grid scale energy
storage.7,8 Physical separation of anode and cathode limits
thermal runaway concerns, while power generation can be
scaled independently of the energy storage capacity of the
system. Nonaqueous RFBs, in particular, oﬀer the ability to
access higher voltages outside the water voltage stability
window and are compatible with ultrahigh energy density alkali
metal anodes.9,10 Unfortunately, the relatively high cost of
nonaqueous solvents, compared to water, necessitates increas-
ingly high concentrations, ideally ≥5 M, of redox-active species
to be cost-competitive.11 One strategy to increase concen-
tration involves pumping semisolid slurries of known battery
chemistriesLiCoO2, LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4, Li4Ti5O12, S, and
electronically conductive carbonsthrough ﬂow cells, increas-
ing eﬀective concentrations >10 M.12,13 While exemplary
performance has been seen, removing conductive carbons
could decrease pumping costs and self-discharge concerns over
long times.
Another strategy to enhance RFB solubility, dubbed “redox-
targeting,” leverages soluble redox-active molecules (redox

materials, the mediators being recharged in the RFB’s
electrochemical cell.14,15 Like the carbon slurry approach, the
eﬀective concentration of active materials can easily exceed
>10 M, but electronically conductive carbon is not
needed.16−18 Regardless, RFBs still exhibit several limiting
factors, with the performance of ion-exchange membranes
being the most widely cited.19−22
Li−S batteries can beneﬁt from RMs as well. Li2S is
electronically insulating, has a high charge transfer resistance,
and is insoluble, so the redox mediation of Li2S oxidation has
been demonstrated to enhance the active material utilization in
static Li−S cells.23 Additionally, the intermediate polysulﬁdes
(PSs) that form during charge and discharge of Li−S batteries
have been used as mediators for LiTi2PO4 in a ﬂow cell.24 In
fact, PSs have been demonstrated as high-performing, low cost
redox-active species for ﬂow batteries.25 Many of these studies
use solid state separators to prevent crossover, while also
limiting the voltage range to keep the S mainly in the soluble
PS phase, which limits the capacity of the battery.26−28 Others
have successfully used multiple polychalcogenides to extend
the capacity of the S, but still use a solid state separator.29
Elimination of the solid state separator or ion-selective
polymeric membrane would decrease cost and decrease
complexity, as fewer, potentially unstable, interfaces are present
in addition to increasing the ionic conductivity, enabling faster
cycling.30−32
Pure Li metal anodes have been used in ﬂow battery
systems, though not to the same extent as PS.10 Instead, many
reports that employ Li-anodes use static cells, as is common in
this research area.33 Yang et al. demonstrated that intentionally
limited use of PS capacity is possible with a Li anode, LiNO3
additives, and no ion-selective membrane in coin cells.34
Using lessons learned from the Li-metal anode community, a
membrane-less Li-metal RFB might be obtained, implementing
a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) coating and
minimizing dendritic growth.34−37 The addition of LiNO3 to
the electrolyte has been shown to both improve the Li
deposition and mitigate PS shuttling.34,38 Presoaking the Li in
LiI has also been shown to enable the formation of a stable
SEI.39
We believe that the innovative ideas from the Li-metal
anode and RFBs communities can be combined to make
something greater than the sum of its parts, creating a high
performing mediated Li−S RFB. Indeed, this opportunity has
been recognized by Lee’s group, who have used engineered
sulfur/polymer composites and a complex hybrid graphite/Li-
metal anode to demonstrate the viability of a redox-mediated
Li−S battery to access <50% the capacity of available S.40,41 In
these reports, however, it is not clear what the predominant
failure mechanism is, nor how the chosen mediator will always
enable complete reduction of S into Li2S. Many related ideas
have been presented across the literature, but to our knowledge
none have attempted to use all of (1) Li metal anode, (2) PS
cathode, (3) mediators to access nearly all S capacity, (4) a
practical ﬂow cell (e.g., not a coin cell) without an ion-selective
membrane, and (5) long discharge times. The combination of
all these attributes represents both a potential increase in Li−S
performance and demonstration at a relevant size and time
scale necessary for grid scale energy storage. In this report, we
identify suitable redox mediators (RMs) for commercially
available S and couple these with a stabilized Li SEI via LiI
pretreatment and a LiNO3-containing electrolyte. The
eﬃcacies of these processes are veriﬁed in static cells via
electrochemical and spectroscopic methods, and failure
mechanisms identiﬁed. Subsequent application is demonstra-
ted over 2−50 mgS cm−2 in a lab-scale membrane-less ﬂow cell
where solids are actively ﬁltered, and discharge times exceed 66
h. Performance characterization suggests a promising path
forward for safe, scalable, and energy dense grid-scale energy
storage.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. Li metal foil (0.75 or 1.5 mm thick, Alfa-Aesar, 99.0%)
was cut to size, scraped with a razor blade, and rolled smooth with a
polyethylene rod prior to use. Lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane)-
sulfonimide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.95%) and LiNO3 (Alfa
Aesar, anhydrous, 99%) were dried at 125 °C and <10 mTorr for
24 h. 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL, Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.8%, ∼75
ppm BHT as inhibitor) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-
Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.5%, inhibitor free) were dried over activated
3A sieves (Alfa-Aesar, 1−2 mm beads). The sieves were activated as
follows: sieves were loaded into a Schlenk ﬂask, evacuated to <10
mTorr on an all-glass Schlenk line (no rubber tubing was used), and
heated to 175 °C for 24 h. The heat was removed, and the ﬂask was
allowed to cool under vacuum until <100 °C. The warm ﬂask was
sealed and directly loaded into the glovebox antechamber. LiI (Alfa-
Aesar, anhydrous, 99.95%) and bis(cyclopentadienyl cobalt) (CoCp2,
Alfa-Aesar, 99%) were ampouled under argon by the manufacturer
and opened in the glovebox. Sulfur (S, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.98%), and
decamethylferrocene (DmFc, Alfa-Aesar, 99%), were separately
evacuated to <10 mTorr on a Schlenk line and transferred into the
glovebox.
Cell Design. All electrochemical testing occurred in an argon-ﬁlled
glovebox with <0.1 ppm of H2O and <0.5 ppm of O2. Both static and
ﬂow cells contained Li metal (0.75 mm thick for static cells and 1.5
mm thick for ﬂow cells) as the anode and carbon felt (Alfa Aesar,
99.0%) as the counter electrode. Electrolyte in all cells was 1 M
LiTFSI in 1:1 (v/v) DOL:DME. To enhance SEI stability, chemical
additives were employed. First, Li foil was soaked in 20 mM LiI in 1:1
(v/v) DOL:DME overnight. The Li foil was removed from solution,
rinsed with DME, and dried under vacuum for 10 min. Additionally, 1
wt % LiNO3 was added to the 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte during testing.
“No additive” cells did not employ LiI or LiNO3.
Initial testing was conducted in small (1.237 cm diameter) or large
(1.872 cm diameter) static cells sealed to prevent solvent evaporation,
Figure S1a. The cathode side consisted of a glass tube with a Au-
plated W rod glass sealed into one end, and an O-ring joint at the
other end. Carbon felt surrounded the Au/W rod. The Li anode was
placed on a Cu plate for support and was sealed to the glass tube with
a Kalrez or EPDM O-ring (McMaster-Carr). In this design there is a
gap of about 1 cm between the C felt and Li anode; no separator of
any kind was used. The diameter of the O-ring determined the
available surface area of the Li: 1.20 cm2 for the small static cell and
2.75 cm2 for the large static cell.
For the small static cell, S and RMs were predissolved in electrolyte
to a concentration of 13.6 mM S (0.99 mg cm−2) and 1.36 mM for
each CoCp2 and DmFc. In this case, the presence of the RMs is
important because they reduce the S to allow it to form polysulﬁdes
enabling it to fully dissolve in the electrolyte. For the large static cell, a
higher S loading (78 mM, 4.4 mg cm−2) was added in powder form
into a pocket created in the C felt, without any binder or additional
conductive carbon. The RMs were predissolved at 7.8 mM. As the
solubility of S in the electrolyte is on the order of 10 mM, signiﬁcant
amounts of solid S remained undissolved in the large static cell.
To minimize the solvent evaporation that may occur in traditional
graphite block ﬂow cells, an all-metal ﬂow cell was designed, Figure
S1b. Here the Li foil was placed on a 1/4 in. thick copper plate. An
EPDM O-ring was used to seal the Li to a 1/2 in. thick 316 stainless
steel plate into which a conventional (open) ﬂow ﬁeld was machined.
A Celgard 3501 microporous separator and carbon felt were
compressed between the Li and stainless steel. The Celgard separator

Electrochemical Testing. To test the cell performance, both
static and ﬂow cells were galvanostatically cycled with an Arbin
LBT21084 at 0.5 mA cm−2 between 1.75 and 3.40 V. Select cells
employed a voltage hold where a constant voltage step was added at
either 1.75 V for discharge or 3.40 V for charge until the current
dropped to less than 50 μA. This technique was suggested by Goulet
and Aziz as a way to examine the diﬀerent mechanisms for capacity
fade in redox ﬂow batteries.42 Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were
taken using a Gamry Interface 1010E in a three-electrode cell with a 3
mm diameter glassy carbon working electrode (Bioanalytical
Systems), platinum wire counter electrode, and Li reference electrode
with scan rates as noted. Coulombic eﬃciency was calculated as CE =
Qd/Qc*100%, where Qd is the discharge capacity and Qc is the charge
capacity. Voltage eﬃciency was calculated as VE = Vd/Vc*100%
where Vd is the average voltage on discharge and Vc is the average
voltage on charge. Details of the calculations for the diﬀusion and
kinetic parameters are given in the Supporting Information.
Materials Analysis. UV−vis spectra were recorded in the
glovebox using an Ocean optics PX2 pulsed xenon light source and
USB400 detector with a 1.3 neutral density ﬁlter. One M LiTFSI with
1 wt % LiNO3 in 1:1 DOL:DME was used as a blank. Aliquots of
electrolyte were removed from the cells and diluted with 1 M LiTFSI
in 1:1 (v/v) DOL:DME to 2.0 mM S (based on initial S content) to
keep the absorbance in the range of the detector.
Chemical analysis was performed on the Li anodes from the large
static cells. XPS was performed using a Kratos Axis Supra XPS system
with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source using a large analyzer spot
size. Samples were removed from the cell, rinsed with DME, and dried
under vacuum and then transferred to the XPS using an inert transfer
arm to prevent air exposure. Spectra shown in this work is of the
pristine surface without sputtering. XPS data was analyzed using
CasaXPS software using the Kratos relative sensitivity factors for an X-
ray source at 90° and corrected to sp3 C at 284.8 eV. Scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) were performed with a Zeiss Gemini Supra 55VP using an
accelerating voltage of 3 kV for imaging and 10 kV for EDS.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In this study, we combine aspects of static Li−S batteries with
redox ﬂow batteries and redox targeting to develop a redox
mediated Li−S ﬂow battery. Targeting the redox mediation of
S, speciﬁc RMs were chosen and electrochemically charac-
terized. By coupling these RMs with protective Li additives,
high S utilization was achieved in static cells. The evolution of
the sulfur species in the catholyte was evaluated with UV−vis
spectroscopy, and the eﬃcacy of the Li additives on Li anode
chemistry and morphology was conﬁrmed with XPS and SEM.
This system was then successfully scaled up to a ﬂow cell with
S loadings as high as 50 mgS cm−2, demonstrating the
feasibility of using this redox-mediated Li−S ﬂow system.
In a standard Li−S battery, Li and S are converted through a
series of PSs to Li2S during discharge and then back to Li and
S during charge. While the intermediate PSs are soluble in
many organic solvents including DOL/DME, the beginning
and end species, S and Li2S, are not. This problem has been
addressed in ﬂow cells by adding conductive carbon to the
catholyte to form a S/C slurry that can be ﬂowed through the
cell.43,44 Other studies have limited the voltage of the cell to
keep the S in soluble form, cycling only between Li2S8 and
Li2S4.34 Both approaches limit the theoretical energy density of
the battery, so we used a third approach, adding RMs to
oxidize and reduce the S/Li2S in solution.23 This removes the
need for carbon additives and allows full cycling of these
insoluble species rather than limiting cycling to the soluble
regime, which greatly increases the theoretical energy density
of the battery from 170 Wh kg−1 for a cell limited to Li2S4 to
2500 Wh kg−1 for the full reaction to Li2S.34 However, unlike
completely insoluble cathodes such as LiFePO4, the
intermediate PSs are soluble and can act as additional RMs,
which improves the voltage eﬃciency.43 The addition of a RM
enables the reduction and oxidation of S to occur in solution
while the RM (either CoCp2 or DmFc in this case) is oxidized
or reduced at the counter electrode according to the following

the RMs must be within 420 mV of the Li−S reaction. (2)

diﬀusion to enable fast charge and discharge. Ferrocene,

Static Cell Cycling. We then evaluated the performance of
the mediated Li−S in a small static cell for proof of concept.
The static cell enabled us to evaluate the impact of the RMs
and additives in a simple, more controlled system than the full
ﬂow cell. A Li metal anode and carbon felt cathode current
collector were immersed in 1 M LiTFSI in 1:1 (vol %)
DOL:DME with 1.2 mg S (13.6 mM, 0.99 mg cm−2) and 1.36
mM each CoCp2 and DmFc. No ion conductive membrane or
separator of any kind was used in these cells. Full details of the
cell design are given in the Experimental Section and Figure
S1a. As expected, cycling this cell with “no additives,” shown in
Figure 3, produced inferior results, with poor S utilization and
rapid capacity fade.
To improve capacity retention, LiNO3 and LiI additives
were used. The addition of LiNO3 has been widely shown to
protect the Li surface from PS shuttling and reduce the
formation of dendrites, which improves the capacity and
capacity retention.34,38 This is a necessity if a solid-state
separator is not used because otherwise high surface area
dendrites will form decreasing cell eﬃciency and battery life. In
addition to adding LiNO3 to the electrolyte, we presoaked the
Li anode in a 20 mM solution of LiI to form a LiI-rich SEI,
which has been shown to be helpful at blocking PS
shuttling.39,47 Both of these additives function by promoting
the formation of a stable SEI that passivates the Li surface.
This passivating SEI both physically blocks PSs from reaching
the Li metal and also promotes uniform, low surface area Li
deposition.39,48 XPS and EDS conﬁrmed the presence and
uniform distribution of I in the form of LiI in the SEI before
cycling, Figure S3.49 When used without LiNO3, the LiI beneﬁt

Li Anode Surface Characterization. To understand how
the chemistry and morphology of the Li anode evolved during
cell cycling, we examined the Li anode from the large static cell
using XPS and SEM. XPS of the cycled Li anodes also supports
the idea that the loss of S is causing capacity fade. In addition
to the expected decomposition products from LiTFSI,55 the
XPS spectra, shown in Figure 5, reveal peaks for S, S−, and S2−
on the Li anode after charge, indicating that the PSs are
shuttling and depositing on the anode.56 In contrast, the
absence of either Co or Fe, Figure S12, on the Li surface
suggests that the RMs are stable and not reacting or being
consumed at the Li anode. This is supported by the stable
capacity coming from the RMs throughout cycling, Figure S13.
SEM suggests the beneﬁcial impact of the LiNO3 on the Li
morphology. SEM of the treated Li electrode after 50 cycles
shows mostly globular rather than ﬁlament like deposition,
Figure 6, which is expected with the LiNO3 added to the
electrolyte.38,57 The lower surface area globular deposition
morphology decreases the capacity lost to side reactions and
reduces the Li available for reaction with PSs.
In addition to the expected morphology, we also see LiNO2,
and Li2SO3 in the SEI after cycling as we would expect from
LiNO3, although there is no Li2O present, Figure 5, indicating
the successful incorporation of LiNO3 into the SEI.57,58
However, the LiI peak vanishes after 50 cycles, Figure 5e,
indicating that the original SEI is either covered with new ﬁlm
(a possibility given the disappearance of the metallic Li peak,
Figure 5b) or damaged during the cycling process. The lack of
I in the SEI after 50 cycles would suggest that the original
protective eﬀects of the LiI are no longer present at long
cycling times, explaining the decrease in capacity after 10
cycles when no LiNO3 is present. It is also possible that
although LiNO3 is incorporated into the SEI, the LiNO3 in
solution has been consumed and is no longer acting as
protection for the Li. This is likely given other studies that have
shown that LiNO3 is consumed during cycling.57 XPS shows
other changes to the SEI composition occur during cycling as
well. Speciﬁcally, there is a loss of Li2O and increases in
Li2CO3. In sum, XPS conﬁrms successful initial incorporation
of LiI into the SEI, and reveals thickening of SEI during
cycling, with the disappearance of I, and PSs, LiNO3, and
LiTFSI incorporated into the SEI after cycling. It also reveals
the presence of polysulﬁdes on the anode after cycling,
indicating a likely mechanism for the capacity fade observed.
Flow Cell Cycling. A ﬂow battery design oﬀers a safe, easily
scalable architecture for grid scale energy storage, enabling the
scale-up of the Li−S chemistry to the MWh−GWh grid scale
capacity. The electrodes in nonﬂowing Li batteries have
limited possible architectures making it diﬃcult to fully utilize
the active material in the cathode at very high mass loadings.4
In standard LIB cathodes, diﬀusion of Li ions must occur from
the front of the ﬁlm to the back, however, this is not the case
with ﬂow cells. In a ﬂow cell with the active material free in the
electrolyte, diﬀusion of Li+ is not limited to two dimensions.
After the success of the proof-of-concept static cell, we scaled
up the system into a ﬂow cell. The components were the same
as the stationary cell with the addition of a polypropylene
Celgard separator between the Li and carbon felt to prevent
shorting of carbon ﬁbers on the Li anode, but the Celgard does
not hinder ion movement. We tested three diﬀerent S loadings
in the ﬂow cell to examine how an increase in active material
aﬀected the capacity of the cell: low, 2.4 mg cm−2; medium, 5
mg cm−2; and high S loading, 50 mg cm−2. The ﬂow cells show
improved voltage eﬃciency and capacity compared to the
static cells, as seen in Figure 7.
The lowest S loading had an initial gravimetric capacity of
807 mAh gS
−1 increasing to 1142 mAh gS
−1 on the second
cycle. This is excellent considering the low RM concentration
used (0.625 mM) and may even be further improved with
higher RM concentration. This is also an improvement over
the initial gravimetric capacity of the static cell (965 mAh gS
−1,
second cycle), indicating better utilization of the S active
material in the ﬂow cell than the static cell. Note that the
similar ﬁrst and second cycle capacities for the static cell may
be due to the fact that the S was predissolved in the electrolyte

■CONCLUSION
The Li−S chemistry was successfully adapted for use in a
membraneless, mediated RFB by combining the Li−S
chemistry from static cells, redox mediation, and a ﬂow cell
architecture. DmFc and CoCp2 were chosen as RMs to kick-
start the initial reduction of solid S into soluble polysulﬁdes
and ﬁnal reduction of polysulﬁdes into solid Li2S, as veriﬁed by
UV−vis spectroscopy. On the Li anode, a LiI and LiNO3
pretreatment strategy encouraged a stable SEI and lessened
capacity fade, without the use of ion-selective separators. Due
in part to the self-mediating PSs, high capacity and voltage
eﬃciency was demonstrated at 1−2 mgS cm−2: 973 mAh gS
−1
and 81.3% VE in static cells and 1142 mAh gS
−1, and 86.9% VE
in ﬂow cells. Capacity fade was attributed to consumption of S
and PS species by the Li anode. These results imply that the
fundamental Li−S chemistry and novel SEI engineering
strategies can be adapted to the hybrid redox ﬂow battery
architecture. Admittedly, increasingly higher S loadings are
necessary for this chemistry to realize its true promise, but such
loadings require excessive cycling times at lab scale. The
performance of these cells could be further increased with
optimization of the cell design and Li anode. This could be
done by improving the uniformity of the ﬂow ﬁeld, which
should improve the contact between the catholyte and carbon
counter electrode and promote more uniform Li deposition on
the Li anode. Increasing the eﬀective surface area of the Li
anode by using a 3D scaﬀold should also improve the
uniformity of the Li deposition as well as enabling cycling at
faster charge rates and enable testing of even higher S loadings.
