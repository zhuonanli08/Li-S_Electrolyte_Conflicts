ABSTRACT: Lithium polysulfides are intermediate products formed
during the discharge and charge of lithium−sulfur batteries and have
good solubility in electrolyte solutions. Therefore, the properties and
structure of solutions of lithium polysulfides in electrolyte solutions
affect the energy characteristics of the lithium−sulfur battery. In this
work, the structure and physicochemical properties (density, ionic
conductivity, and self-diffusion coefficients) of solutions of lithium
disulfide, tetrasulfide, and octasulfide in lithium perchlorate tetrasolvate
with sulfolane in a wide range of concentrations (0.2−12 M) were
studied using the molecular dynamics method. With increasing
concentrations of lithium polysulfides, the proportion of sulfur atoms
in the coordination sphere of the lithium cation increases and the
proportion of sulfolane molecules decreases. It has been established
that the ability of the polysulfide anion to act as a bridging ligand leads to the formation of clusters, including lithium perchlorate,
lithium polysulfides, and sulfolane. It has been shown that the tendency to form clusters increases with an increasing number of
sulfur atoms in the polysulfide anion. At high concentrations of lithium polysulfides, regardless of their size, the electrolyte system
becomes a single cluster. With an increase in the concentration of lithium polysulfides in the electrolyte system, its density increases
and the ionic conductivity and diffusion coefficients of the system components decrease.
■INTRODUCTION
The physicochemical properties of electrolyte systems based
on sulfolane, lithium salts, and lithium polysulfides of various
sizes are of great interest in connection with the development
of lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs).1,2 The specific energy of
the lithium−sulfur electrochemical system is 2600 W h/kg,3
which is approximately 5 times the specific energy of lithium-
ion electrochemical systems. A comparison of the energy
characteristics of lithium−sulfur and lithium-ion electro-
chemical systems suggests the possibility of creating batteries
with a specific energy that is at least 2−3 times larger than the
specific energy of the best examples of lithium-ion batteries
(LIBs).4,5 However, despite significant efforts, it has still not
been possible to create LSBs with characteristics that meet the
requirements of the modern market.6 The specific energy of
the best samples of lithium−sulfur cell prototypes barely
reaches 400−450 W h/kg with a cycling duration of no more
than 100−150 cycles.7 (see Table 1)
The difficulties in developing LSBs are due to the feature of
the lithium−sulfur electrochemical system. Sulfur in the solid
state is a dielectric and therefore has low electrochemical
activity. However, sulfur is soluble in electrolyte systems based
on aprotic dipolar solvents (ADSs) and can be sorbed from
solutions on the surface of electronic conductors, for example,
on the surface of various carbon materials, carbon black,

When discharging and charging LSBs, intermediate products
of the electrochemical reduction of sulfur are formed that are
highly soluble in electrolytes−lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn).8,9
The solubility of sulfur and lithium polysulfides in electrolytes
causes several negative consequences: high self-discharge rates,
short cycling times, and low specific energy of LSBs.
Electrochemical reduction of sulfur proceeds through the
sequential reduction of lithium polysulfides of various sizes
(Table 1). The end product of the electrochemical reduction
of sulfur is lithium sulfide.10
When the resulting polysulfides are dissolved in electrolytes,
the molecules of electrolyte solvents bind in the solvation
shells of the lithium cations included in their composition and
the formation of solvation complexes occurs.10,11 The binding
of solvent molecules occurs before the formation of lithium
tetrasulfide. The electrochemical reduction of lithium
tetrasulfide is not accompanied by the binding of additional
solvent molecules since as a result of reduction lithium sulfide
is formed, which is released from the solution in a solid form.
When the solubility limit of LPS in electrolytes is reached, their
solvate complexes are released into a separate phase.
Consequently, the amount of electrolyte solution required
for the complete reduction of sulfur is determined by the
solvate number of the lithium cation included in the
composition of lithium tetrasulfide. To ensure deep electro-
chemical reduction of sulfur during LSB discharge or oxidation
of lithium sulfide during their charging, the electrolyte must be
sufficient for solvation of the lithium cation, included in the
composition of lithium tetrasulfide.
In the limiting case, during the discharge and charge of
LSBs, all solvent molecules find themselves bound in the
solvation shells of lithium cations, which are part of both
background salts and lithium polysulfides. This leads to the
formation of a solvate complex mixture of lithium polysulfides
and lithium salts. With a minimum electrolyte content in LSBs,
lithium cations can be solvated not only by the anions of
lithium salts and solvent molecules but also by sulfur atoms
that are part of the polysulfide anions. In addition, the
formation of lithium polysulfide clusters is possible as a result
of the interaction of the lithium cation with the sulfur atoms of
the neighboring polysulfide chains.
The stability and composition of lithium polysulfide
complexes with solvent molecules included in electrolyte
systems affect the energy characteristics of LSBs.10−12
Normally, the amount of electrolyte in the LSB should be
reduced to achieve high specific energy. In many cases, the
electrolyte/sulfur weight ratio in LSBs lies in the range of 20−
3. To achieve acceptable specific energy, the electrolyte/sulfur
ratio in LSBs should not exceed 2.0.7
As LSB electrolytes, as a rule, one molar solutions of lithium
salts in individual or mixed ADSs are used.13 In most studies,
the solvent/lithium salt molar ratio is in the range of 12−10.14
If we take the solvate number of the lithium cation in an
electrolyte solution to be equal to four, then the concentration
of free (not participating in solvation) solvent molecules will
be about 6−8 M. From simple calculations, it follows that if the
solvate numbers of the lithium cation included in the solvate
complexes of lithium salts and lithium polysulfides are equal,
the electrolyte/sulfur weight ratio is in the range of 7−10.
One of the methods to reduce the amount of electrolyte is to
reduce the solubility of the resulting lithium polysulfides in
electrolyte systems. Most often, this is achieved by introducing
weakly solvating nonpolar solvents into the electrolyte
composition, which are most often fluoroethers of various
structures.15
Recently, solvate ionic liquids have attracted increasing
interest as LSB electrolytes.16 They are highly concentrated
solutions of lithium salts that do not contain free solvent
molecules. By their chemical nature, such systems are solvate
complexes of lithium salts with ADSs. Lithium polysulfides can
dissolve in these systems, despite the absence of free solvent
molecules. However, the mechanisms of polysulfide dissolution
and the structures of the resulting systems are not clear.
For the targeted development of electrolyte systems for
LSBs, it is necessary to understand the interaction mechanisms
of various sizes of lithium polysulfides with the components of
electrolyte systems: solvents and anions of background salts.
It was previously shown17 that lithium polysulfides in
electrolyte solutions undergo self-association, or clustering,
that occurs due to the interaction of the two terminal sulfur
atoms of the polysulfide anion and the lithium cation.
Understanding the mechanisms of self-association of lithium
polysulfides is extremely important for optimizing the
composition of electrolyte systems for LSBs.
Clustering studies were carried out using both experimental
and theoretical methods.15,17−22 The properties of lithium
polysulfides (Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8) and their mixtures in 1,3-
dioxolane and 1,2-dimethoxyethane were studied using MD
and DFT methods.18 It has been shown that short-chain
lithium polysulfides (Li2S4) tend to form larger clusters,
especially at high concentrations, compared to long-chain
lithium polysulfides (Li2S8). The reason is the localization of
charge on the terminal sulfur atoms in short-chain polysulfide
anions. These results are consistent with the results of work on
the structure and solubility of lithium polysulfides in dimethyl
sulfoxide.22 It has been shown that lithium tetrasulfide Li2S4
predominantly forms dimers while Li2S8 is in the form of
monomers.
The properties and structure of long-chain lithium
polysulfides (Li2S6 and Li2S8) in dimethoxyethane and 1,3-
dioxolane were studied using molecular dynamics (MD) and
quantum chemistry methods.21 It has been shown that lithium
polysulfides practically do not dissociate in solutions. The
authors determined the first solvation shell for the lithium
cation Li+, as well as for lithium polysulfides (Li2S6, Li2S8
closed, and Li2S8 linear) in pure solvents and in solvents
containing a background salt. Simulations in the presence of
additional lithium cations showed that the polysulfide anion is
capable of coordinating more than two lithium cations.
The influence of background salts on conductivity, diffusion
coefficients, structures of solvation complexes, and clustering in
electrolyte solutions containing lithium polysulfides was also
studied using MD methods in the works of Rajput et al.17 and
Park et al.20 They showed that the self-diffusion coefficients of
Li+, polysulfide anions, and solvents (1,3-dioxolane and 1,2-
dimethoxyethane) decrease with the addition of 1 M LiTFSI.

The physicochemical properties and structure of a solution
of lithium polysulfides in 1,2-dimethoxyethane in the presence
of LiTFSI salt and bis-2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether were studied.15
It was shown that the addition of ether led to an increase in the
clustering of lithium polysulfide resulting in a decrease in its
solubility.15 Thus, the dissolution of lithium polysulfides in
electrolyte solutions of LSBs leads to a change in their
physicochemical properties: density, viscosity, and ionic
conductivity. To optimize the composition of LSB electrolytes,
it is important to understand how the content of lithium
polysulfides in electrolyte solutions, their composition, and the
composition of the clusters they form affect the physicochem-
ical properties of electrolyte solutions.
Sulfolane-based solvate ionic liquids are promising electro-
lyte systems for LSBs.23 The advantages of sulfolane are a high
boiling point (287.3 °C), a high flash point (165 °C), and a
high dielectric constant (43.3−44), as well as good solubility of
lithium polysulfides. Sulfolane has low toxicity, which is very
important when used as an electrolyte solvent in batteries.24
LSBs with sulfolane-based electrolytes remain operational up
to 160 °C.25 In electrolytes based on sulfolane, a significant
contribution to ion transport is made by the hopping
mechanism, which significantly increases their specific ionic
conductivity.26 An important property of sulfolane-based
electrolytes is an increase in the transfer numbers of lithium
cations with increasing concentrations of lithium salt solutions,
which leads to an increase in the cationic conductivity of
sulfolane electrolytes, including highly concentrated ones.1
Sulfolane-based electrolytes for LSBs are of great interest.2,27
MD studies were carried out in highly concentrated solutions
of lithium polysulfides in sulfolane.1,28 Of great interest as a
promising electrolyte for LSBs is lithium perchlorate
tetrasolvate with sulfolane (LiClO4·4SL).23 This electrolyte
has a high ionic conductivity (1.7 × 10−3 ohm−1 cm−1 at 30
°C) and is in a liquid phase over a wide temperature range. In
this work, the physicochemical properties and structure of
various lithium polysulfide solutions (Li2S2, Li2S4, Li2S8) in
lithium perchlorate tetrasolvate with sulfolane were studied
using the MD method in a wide range of concentrations to
assess the possibility of using LiClO4·4SL as an LSB
electrolyte.
■METHODS
Experiment. Preparation of Lithium Polysulfide Solu-
tions. In this work, we used sulfur (S8, ≥99.0%, Sigma-
Aldrich), lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.98% trace metals basis,
Sigma-Aldrich), lithium perchlorate (LiClO4, battery grade,
99.995%, Aldrich), and sulfolane (99%, Aldrich). S8 and
LiClO4 were dried in a vacuum oven at 40 and 70 °C,
respectively, for at least 7 days. Sulfolane (99%, Aldrich) was
purified and dried by vacuum double distillation.
2.4 M solution of lithium perchlorate in sulfolane was
prepared by dissolution of LiClO4 in sulfolane. The water
content in the 2.4 M LiClO4 in sulfolane was ≤50 ppm. A
coulometric titration in Fischer reagent medium using a
Titroline7500 KF trace automatic titrator (SI Analytics) was
used to estimate the water content into solutions.
Lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn) in sulfolane solution were
obtained by the reaction of sulfur and lithium sulfide in 2.4 M
solutions of LiClO4 in sulfolane. Sulfur and lithium sulfide
were dissolved in 2.4 M solutions of LiClO4 in sulfolane with
continuous stirring (200 rpm) on a magnetic stirrer for 7−9
days until full dissolution of the reagents at 30 °C. The molar
ratio of sulfur and lithium sulfide was 7:1, 3/1, and 1/1. The
completeness of the dissolution was controlled visually: it was
considered that all solid particles were completely dissolved if
after centrifugation of the solution at a speed of 2500 rpm for
15 min, no solid phase was observed at the bottom of the glass
vessel.

Measurements of Conductivity and Density. The ionic
conductivity and density of lithium salt solutions in sulfolane
were measured at 303 K in an air thermostat and the accuracy
of temperature stabilization was ±0.1°.
The ionic conductivity of lithium polysulfide solutions was
measured in hermetic conductometric cells with platinum
black electrodes calibrated by standard KCl solution at a
frequency of 1 kHz. The error in measuring the ionic
conductivity did not exceed 3%.
Density was measured by a standard pycnometric method in
Biron-type pycnometers with a graduated scale of 1 mL. The
error of measurement did not exceed 2%.
All work on preparing lithium polysulfide solutions and
filling pycnometers and conductometric cells was carried out in
a glovebox in an argon atmosphere (oxygen and water content
less than 1 ppm).
Molecular Dynamics Simulation. The modeling of
lithium polysulfide solutions in tetrasovolvate of lithium
perchlorate with sulfolane by the classical MD method was
carried out in the Gromacs version 2023.1 software package.29
We used the nonpolarizable OPLS-AA force field.30 Modeling
objects are listed in Figure 1. The parameters of the sulfolane
(SL) molecule were identical to the work.31 The parameters of
polysulfide anions (bond lengths, bond and tetrahedral angles,
and charges) were calculated in the Gaussian 09 software
package32 using the B3LYP density functional theory method33
using the aug-cc-pVTZ basis set.34 Partial charges are
summarized in Table 1S. The force field describing constants
were calculated using the LigParGen service of the Jorgensen
group.35 A specified number of lithium cations (Li+),
polysulfide anions (S2

previously conducted MD simulations,31,36 the results of which
are in good agreement with experimental data on density and
ionic conductivity.
After energy minimizing with a convergence criterion of
1000 kJ/mol/nm, the system was equilibrated using an
annealing procedure from 500 to 303 K. Next, modeling was
sequentially carried out in the isobaric−isothermal ensemble
(NPT) for 20 ns to calculate the density of the system and in
the isochoric-isothermal ensemble (NVT) for 40 ns to
calculate the self-diffusion and ionic conductivity coefficients.
The simulation time was chosen based on previously
performed MD calculations of the physicochemical properties
of solutions of electrolyte solutions by our research
group31,36,37 and others.38,39 A constant temperature was
maintained by a Nose−Hoover thermostat40,41 with a
parameter τ = 0.2 ps. Temperature T = 303 K. The pressure
was maintained constant [10,1325 Pa (1 atm)] by a Berendsen
barostat42 with a parameter τ = 0.2 ps. The integration step
was 1 fs. At the initial moment, the atoms were given random
(in magnitude and direction) velocities and normalized in such
a way that the kinetic energy of the system corresponded to a
given temperature.
The works43−45 state that in nonpolarizable force fields,
interionic interactions are overestimated. This leads to an
underestimation of the ionic conductivity and self-diffusion
coefficient calculated values. To level out this effect, the
charges of cations and anions of dissolved salts are usually
reduced by 10−30%.43−45 Previously,31 in order to more
accurately describe the properties and structure of a 1 M
solution of lithium perchlorate in sulfolane, solutions were
simulated by MD using this approachthe charges of the
lithium cation and perchlorate anion were reduced to 70, 80,
and 90%. The most optimal results were obtained when taking
into account the counterion charges of 80%.31 Therefore, in
this work, we performed MD simulations with 80%
consideration of the counterion charge. For each four-
component system, several sequential simulations were
performed to calculate the data and estimate uncertainties.
In sequential simulations, the results are statistically
independent and are normally distributed. The error in parallel
calculations of density did not exceed 0.1% and the coefficients
of self-diffusion and ionic conductivity −10%.
Coordination Number of the Lithium Cation. Initially,
radial distribution functions (RDFs) of each sulfur atom
included in the anion around the lithium cation were
constructed to calculate the coordination number of the
lithium cation over the polysulfide anion (N(PSn)). Each
calculated RDF was integrated. Calculations were performed
by using modeling packages implemented in the GROMACS
software package. The coordination number of the lithium
cation at the sulfur atom was taken to be the value of the
integral distribution function corresponding to the position of
the minimum on the RDF following the first maximum.
The coordination number of the lithium cation to the
perchlorate anion (N(ClO4
−)) and sulfolane (N(SL)) was
calculated similarly. RDFs and cumulative distribution
functions are given in Supporting Information (Figures S2−
S4).

The RDF describes the two-dimensional probability
distribution of finding a particle (solvent molecules, ions, or
individual atoms) at a distance r from the reference particle.
The first maximum position of the RDF of solvent molecules
or anion atoms around lithium cations corresponds to the
distance between the lithium cation and its first solvation shell.
It is known that the first solvation shell of the lithium cation is
usually located at a distance of 0.17−0.21 nm.50,51
Previously, we discussed in detail the forms of the RDFs of
oxygen, sulfur, and chlorine atoms of sulfolane molecules and
the lithium perchlorate anion around the lithium cation,31,36 so
we will not provide a detailed analysis in the presented work.
We only note that based on the analysis of the shape of the
RDF of the S and O atoms of the sulfolane molecule relative to
the lithium cation (Figure 2a), we can conclude that the
lithium cation interacts with only one oxygen atom of
sulfolane, and the second oxygen atom does not participate
in the solvation of the same lithium cation and is inverted in
the opposite direction from him. The second oxygen atom of
the sulfolane molecule can coordinate with the second lithium
cation and act as a bridging ligand.36
We have previously shown that the lithium perchlorate
anion interacts very weakly with the lithium cation with only
one oxygen atom at a molar ratio of lithium perchlorate and
sulfolane less than 1/10.36 This is indicated by low peaks in the
curves of the RDFs of the perchlorate anion oxygen atoms
around the lithium cation (Figure 2b).
The RDFs of the disulfide anion of sulfur atoms around the
lithium cation and their integral forms are identical (Figure
3a). In the R(Li−S(PS2)) curves, only one peak is observed,
the maximum of which is located at 0.214 nm. One lithium
cation simultaneously coordinates both sulfur atoms of PS2
disulfide anions.
The terminal sulfur atoms of the tetrasulfide anion are
equivalent to each other (Figures S1 and S4), and the internal
ones (Figures S2 and S3) are equivalent to each other;
therefore, the RDFs are presented only for one sulfur atom of
each type. It is interesting to note that the maxima on the RDF
of the terminal and internal sulfur atoms are close and amount
to 0.214 and 0.218 nm (Figure 3b). The distance between
sulfur atoms in the PS4 anion is 0.209 nm, i.e., both atoms
enter the first solvation shell of the lithium cation
simultaneously. It should be noted that the shapes of the
peaks of the RDF of the terminal and internal sulfur atoms of
the tetrasulfide anion is different. The RDF peak of the
tetrasulfide anion terminal sulfur atom is clear and narrow,
slightly asymmetrical, and broadens with increasing distance to
the lithium cation (the length of the peak base is approximately
0.08 nm). The RDF peak of the internal sulfur atom is broader
and asymmetrical. The length of the peak base is approximately
0.13 nm. The maximum RDF R(Li+−S1) is approximately
three times lower than the maximum RDF R(Li+−S2). The
more distorted peak shape of the RDF of the lithium
tetrasulfide anion second sulfur atom indicates that the

The concentration of the lithium octasulfide solution affects
the shape of the RDFs R(Li−S(PS8)). For Li2S8 solutions with
a concentration of less than 0.7 M, two clear peaks with

dilute solutions, the lithium cation coordinates only with the
terminal sulfur atoms of the polysulfide chain (Figures S2−S28
and 5c).
For solutions of lithium octasulfide in lithium perchlorate
tetrasolvate with a concentration of Li2S8, broad overlapping
peaks appear for the sulfur atoms, as shown in Figures S3 and
S4. For example, the RDFs of R(Li−S3(PS8)) exhibit two
broadened overlapping peaks with maxima at 0.234 and 0.266
nm (Figure 3d). The RDFs of R(Li−S4(PS8)) exhibit two
broadened overlapping peaks with maxima at 0.236 and 0.27
nm. The distances between the positions of the first maxima on
the curves of the RDFs of the first, second, third, and fourth
sulfur atoms of the octasulfide anion do not exceed 0.05 nm,
which is significantly less than the length of the S−S bond in
the anion. The complex shape of the RDFs of sulfur atoms of
the octasulfide anion indicates the ability of the lithium
octasulfide anion to coordinate with one lithium cation
simultaneously by all sulfur atoms. Such coordination is
possible only if the octasulfide anion coils around a lithium
cation or simultaneously coordinates the sulfur atoms of two
octasulfide anions into a lithium cation.

The sulfur atoms of the octasulfide anion have the least
solvating ability. The solvating ability of sulfur atoms included
in the composition of di- and tetrasulfide anions is almost the
same. The perchlorate anion has a weaker solvating ability
compared with sulfolane and polysulfide anions. To the
greatest extent, the perchlorate anion solvates the lithium
cation at high concentrations of lithium polysulfides. This is
especially pronounced in solutions of lithium octasulfide.
In general, the coordination number of the lithium cation at
low concentrations of lithium polysulfides is close to 5 and
increases as the concentration of polysulfides increases. In
solutions of lithium disulfide, the coordination number of the
lithium cation is practically independent of the concentration
(Figure 4d). As the length of the polysulfide chain increases
and the concentration of polysulfides in the system increases,
the total coordination number of lithium cations increases
[N(PS4) ≈5, N(PS8) ≈6].
This occurs because the lithium cation, in addition to
coordinating with the four sulfur atoms of one tetrasulfide
anion, can coordinate with the terminal sulfur atom of another
tetrasulfide anion. Also, one lithium cation can simultaneously
coordinate with the two terminal sulfur atoms of two different
tetrasulfide anions and the terminal and terminal sulfur atoms
of a third tetrasulfide anion (Figure 5).
The octasulfide anion can take on various conformational
formslinear and cyclic. In this case, the lithium cation can be
located both inside the polysulfide ring, coordinating all sulfur
atoms with different contributions (so that in total Ntotal = 6),
and along the terminal sulfur atoms of different octasulfide
anions (Figure 5c).
The effect of the length of the polysulfide chain on the value
of the average coordination number of the lithium cation can
be explained by the increase in the conformational flexibility of
the polysulfide chain as its length increases. The disulfide anion
has the greatest rigidity; therefore, at a high content of lithium
disulfide in the system, the lithium cation is predominantly
coordinated with four sulfur atoms of two disulfide anions
(Figure 5a). The oxygen atoms of the sulfolane molecule and
the perchlorate anion additionally solvate the lithium cation
until the average solvate number is close to 5.

It is also worth noting that one polysulfide anion can
coordinate with two lithium cations. This fact is consistent
with the data from.21 Instantaneous MD images of dilute
solutions of lithium polysulfides in lithium perchlorate
tetrasolvate [ratio of n(Li2Sn)/n(LiClO4·4SL) = 1/10] show
the formation of structures in which disulfide and tetrasulfide
anions interact with several lithium cations (Figure 5d,e). For
the octasulfide anion at this ratio of the components of the
system, such interactions are not observed (Figure 5e).
The proportion of free lithium cations, calculated by eq 4,
decreases with the increasing concentration of lithium
polysulfide due to an increase in the degree of lithium
polysulfide clustering (Figure 6). In the range of lithium
polysulfide concentrations less than 1 M, the proportion of free
lithium cations does not depend on the degree of polysulfide.
As the concentration of lithium polysulfides increases to more
than 1 M, the proportion of free lithium cations decreases with
increasing degree of polysulfide.
Clustering of Lithium Polysulfides. To estimate the size
and composition of the resulting polysulfide clusters, functions
of the radial distribution of polysulfide anions relative to each
other were constructed. In the RDF curves for all types of
polysulfide anions (Figure 7a−c), sharp peaks with a maximum
at 0.35−0.36 nm are observed. With an increasing concen-
tration of lithium polysulfides, the height of the peaks
increases, which indicates an increase in the intensity of
interaction of polysulfide anions with each other. From the
integral functions of the radial distribution of polysulfide
anions relative to each other (Figure S6), it is clear that with
increasing length of the polysulfide anion, the number of
contacts increases. Analysis of the RDF curves showed that
regardless of the length of the polysulfide chain, the distance
between PS anions is approximately 0.45 nm (Figure 7). This
distance was taken as the minimum distance between
polysulfide clusters when analyzing the structure of lithium
polysulfide solutions of various sizes in sulfolane tetrasolvate of
lithium perchlorate.
We also studied the interaction of polysulfide anions with
sulfolane molecules (Figure 7d−f). Several sharp peaks are
observed in the RDF curves of sulfolane molecules relative to
polysulfide anions for dilute solutions. With an increase in the
concentration of lithium polysulfides, a broadening of the
peaks is observed as well as a decrease in their intensity, which
is associated with a decrease in the number of PS and SL
contacts (Figure 7). This is also evident from the integral
functions of the radial distribution of sulfolane molecules
relative to polysulfide anions (Figure S7): with the increasing
concentration of lithium polysulfides, the step height decreases.
It is also interesting to note that with an increase in the
length of the polysulfide anion on the RDFs of polysulfide
anions relative to each other and their integrals (Figures 7 and
S7), there is an increase in the gap between the curves with
concentrations of 2.18 and 5.65 M for PS2, 2.02 and 4.76 M
for PS4, and 1.76 and 3.50 M for PS8. This is because, at a
high concentration of lithium polysulfide (5.65−12.58 M for
PS2, 4.76−9.01 M for PS4, and 3.50−5.44 M for PS8), a high
degree of clustering is observed. Accordingly, there are more
contacts between polysulfide anions than in dilute solutions
(∼0.2−2.0 M).
Cluster analysis, carried out using Gromacs program
algorithms, shows the distribution of sizes (number of
atoms) of clusters formed in the systems under study,
depending on their composition (Figure 8). From an analysis
of the number of atoms in a particular cluster, it follows that
both neutral and charged (cationic and anionic) clusters of
lithium polysulfides can form in the solutions we study.
From the cluster distribution diagrams (Figure 8), it follows
that in highly concentrated solutions (purple marker), ionic
cross-linking occurs, as a result of which all polysulfide anions
and lithium cations form a single cluster (Figure 8). With a
decrease in the concentration of lithium polysulfides, cationic
and anionic clusters of different compositions are formed. First,
a lithium cation unbound with polysulfide anions begins to be
present in the solution (Figure 8a,d,g). Second, lithium
polysulfides are predominantly present in the form of charged
cationic and anionic clusters, rather than neutral (Li2Sn)y
clusters. This fact confirms our calculations of the coordination
numbers of the lithium cation on the polysulfide anions: more
than 2 lithium cations can be coordinated with the polysulfide
anion. It should be noted that the complete binding of
polysulfide anions and lithium cations into a single cluster for
lithium polysulfides of various compositions occurs at different
concentrations. Thus, in the Li2S2−[LiClO4·4SL] system, all
disulfide anions and lithium cations are bound into a single
positively charged cluster at a lithium disulfide concentration
of 12.58 M, in the Li2S4−[LiClO4·4SL] system at a
concentration of −9.0 M, and in the Li2S8−[LiClO4·4SL]
system at a concentration of 5.13 M. Thus, we can conclude
that with increasing degree of polysulfide, the tendency of
lithium polysulfides to form clusters increases. Thus, studies of
the clustering processes of lithium polysulfides have shown that
reducing the amount of solvent in the system leads to the
binding of lithium polysulfides into a single cluster.

■CONCLUSIONS
Our research group52 has shown that it is impossible to obtain
concentrated solutions of lithium polysulfides by directly
reacting sulfur with lithium metal or lithium sulfides in
solutions. Experimental data on ionic conductivity were
obtained only for lithium polysulfides in a solution of lithium
perchlorate in sulfolane with a concentration of no more than
0.5 M.52 During the discharge process, the concentrations of
lithium polysulfides can reach higher concentrations. In this
regard, we studied the physicochemical properties of lithium
polysulfides in lithium perchlorate solutions over a wide range
of concentrations. Since this cannot be done by experimental
methods, we applied theoretical methods, namely, the MD
method.
Modeling the structure and physicochemical properties of
solutions of lithium polysulfides of various sizes in the solvate
complex of lithium tetrasulfide with sulfolane using the MD
method showed that polysulfide anions are capable of
coordinating the lithium cation. With an increase in the
concentration of lithium polysulfides, the composition of the
solvation shell of the lithium cation changes: the number of
sulfur atoms of polysulfide anions increases and the number of
sulfolane molecules decreases. The perchlorate anion has the
weakest solvating properties.
Polysulfide anions are capable of acting as bridging ligands
and interacting with several lithium cations. Lithium cations
are capable of cross-linking polysulfide chains, which ultimately
leads to the formation of polysulfide clusters. The tendency of
lithium polysulfides to form clusters increases with the number
of sulfur atoms in the polysulfide chain. At high concentrations
of lithium polysulfides, the electrolyte system turns into a
single cluster. The formation of clusters has a significant effect
on the physicochemical properties of lithium polysulfide
solutions. With an increase in the concentration of lithium
polysulfides in the electrolyte system, its density increases, and
the ionic conductivity and diffusion coefficients of the system
components decrease.
