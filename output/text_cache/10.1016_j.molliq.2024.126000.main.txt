A B S T R A C T
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, which have a high theoretical capacity and can be prepared using low-cost 
activation materials, are a promising alternative for realizing high-energy-density battery systems. However, 
the stability of the electrolyte and the shuttle effect caused by dissolved lithium polysulfides in the electrolyte 
prevent their practical application. Due to its non-flammability and high oxidation stability, sulfolane shows 
good promise for improving the oxidation stability and weakening the shuttle effect of Li–S electrolytes. In this 
work, the effects of a sulfolane additive on a Li–S battery electrolyte were revealed by density functional theory 
calculations and molecular dynamics simulations. The molecular orbitals, thermodynamics, and dynamics of the 
oxidation reaction were analyzed from the electron level. The addition of sulfolane lowers the HOMO energy 
level and the configuration energy difference, which leads to improved oxidation stability. Furthermore, sulfo­
lane shows affinity to the S atoms close to Li, which correspond to low-order polysulfides. This affinity is 
confirmed by binding energy data and indicates that the addition of sulfolane inhibits the diffusion of Li2S4, Li2S6 
and Li2S8. This work provides an analysis of the effects of sulfolane as an additive from the electron and mo­
lecular levels, which will promote a better understanding of Li–S battery systems and enable the design of 
improved electrolytes.
1. Introduction
As interest in sustainable green energy rapidly increases, batteries 
with much higher energy densities are in demand as stable energy 
storage and conversion systems [1–5]. Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, 
which have a high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), are non- 
toxic, and can be prepared with low-cost materials, have attracted 
extensive attention as promising battery systems [6,7]. However, the 
poor conductivity, large volumetric change, and the diffusion back and 
forward of lithium polysulfide (Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) between anode and 
cathode (named “shuttle effect”) still hamper the practical application of 
Li–S batteries [7–10]. Designing the electrodes to control the kinetics 
and transformation of polysulfides in one solution [11,12]. While, the 
electrolyte, which can dissolve and react with active electrode materials, 
is a key component that must be improved to enhance the safety and 
achieve the high utilization of active materials in Li–S batteries [13,14]. 
A notable challenge preventing the utilization of Li–S batteries is “sol­
id–liquid–solid” conversion being common in the ether-based electro­
lytes of low flash point, which can cause thermal runaway or even 
explosion. This problem is common in Li–S batteries with ether-based, 
ionic liquid-based, and polymer-based electrolytes [15,16].
A suitable Li–S electrolyte should have good oxidation stability to 
resist decomposition reactions. Moreover, electrolytes should inhibit the 
shuttle effect of polysulfides, which leads to poor cycle performance 
[17]. Mixed electrolytes can exhibit enhanced sulfur utilization and 
cycle stability if the benefits of each component in the electrolyte lead to 
a synergistic effect that improves performance [13,17,18,7,8]. Mixtures 
containing dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxypolyalkane (DOL) (v/ 
v = 1/1) are the most frequently used ether-based electrolytes in Li–S 
batteries. The use of fluorinated DME in electrolytes has been reported 
to suppress the shuttle effect and Li dendrite formation [19]. The 
addition of a co-solvent is an effective and economic strategy for 
providing synergistically improved battery performance. Many solvents, 
such as dimethyl disulfide (DMDS), glutamate, and phenyl diselenide, 
have been added to improve the stability of electrolytes and weaken the 
shuttle effect [20–22]. Sulfolane (SL), which is non-flammable and has 
high oxidation stability, has been shown as an excellent electrolyte ad­
ditive for Li-ion, Li-metal, and Li–S batteries [18,23,24]. Moreover, SL

In this study, microscopic and theoretical calculations were 
employed to investigate the effects of SL as an additive on a Li–S battery 
electrolyte. The molecular orbitals of the sulfolane molecule were 
analyzed to study electrolyte stability from the electron level. Further­
more, the thermodynamics and kinetics of the oxidation of different 
electrolytes were investigated to understand the combination effect of 
the SL additive. Molecular-level simulations revealed the microscopic 
structure and influence of the SL additive on the polysulfide shuttle ef­
fect. By focusing on unraveling the effects of the sulfolane additive on 
the electrolyte of a Li–S battery, this work provides an in-depth under­
standing of the function of electrolyte additives.
2. Computational details
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed by 
using the Dmol3 code [28,29]. The PW91 functional within the gener­
alized gradient approximation (GGA-PW91) [30] with OBS dispersion 
correction parameters [31] was chosen to describe the exchange- 
correlation energy. GGA-PBE generalized function has also been tested 
and the same trend for the binding energy can be obtained (Table S1). 
The solvation effect (water) was included in the conductor-like 
screening model (COSMO). DFT semicore pseudopotentials and the 
double-numeric basis with polarization (DNP) functions were used for 
all atoms. The global real-space orbital cutoff of the atomic basis sets 
was set to 5.1 Å. The convergence criteria for structure optimization and 
energy calculation were 1.0 × 10−6 for self-consistent field calculations, 
1.0 × 10−5 Hartree for energy, 2.0 × 10−3 Hartree/Å for maximum 
force, and 5.0 × 10−4 nm for maximum displacement.
The LAMMPS software package [32,33] was used to perform the 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Packmol [34] was employed to 
construct the initial model of electrolytes. The electrolyte model con­
sisted of 486 DME, 486 DOL, and 108 lithium polysulfide molecules, 
which provide ~1.1 M lithium as in the conventional liquid electrolytes. 
Then, 48 SL molecules, with the mole fraction of ~4 % as cosolvent, 
were added to introduce the additive effect on the electrolyte. The rigid 
lithium polysulfide configurations were considered to guarantee the 
integrity of molecules, while the other molecules (DME, DOL, and SL) 
were flexible during the simulation. The force parameters for all mole­
cules are reported in Tables S2–5. Simulations were executed under 
periodic boundary conditions. The long-range electrostatic interactions 
were taken into account by the particle-mesh Ewald summation. The 
cutoff distance was set to 16 Å, and the time step was 1 fs. Initially, a 10 
ns simulation using the NPT ensemble was performed. Then, an 
annealing procedure (300–400 K) was employed to completely relax the 
structure. After annealing, another 10 ns NPT simulation with well 
equilibrated system (Fig. S1) was performed for production. And 5 in­
dependent runs with different initial configurations were employed to 
compute the diffusion rate of polysulfides.
3. Results and discussion
The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoc­
cupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of the three solvent 
molecules are shown in Fig. 1. The molecular orbital of SL is different 
compared to those of DME and DOL. The HOMO energy level of SL 
(−6.78 eV) is substantially lower than those of the DME (−5.80 eV) and 
DOL (−5.95 eV) molecules. This indicates that the SL additive is more 
difficult to oxidize than the DME or DOL solvents. Meanwhile, the LUMO 
energy levels of the DME (1.14 eV) and DOL (1.18 eV) solvents are much 
higher than that of the SL additive (0.05 eV), implying that SL will be 
reduced before the DME/DOL solvents. Therefore, according to these 
molecular orbital (MO) calculations, the use of SL as an additive will 
improve the oxidation stability of the Li–S battery electrolyte.
To further evaluate the oxidation stability of the electrolyte, the 
change in energy of complex M caused by ionization and solvation was 
calculated according to the free energy cycle using Eq. (1) [35–37]. 
ΔE = ΔEg(M→M+) + ΔEs(M+) −ΔEs(M)
(1) 
where ΔE is the energy required for the oxidation of an electrolyte 
molecule, ΔEg(M → M+) is the gas-phase ionization energy, ΔEs(M+) is 
the solvation energy of the oxidized (M+) electrolyte molecule, and 
ΔEs(M) is the solvation energy of the initial (M) electrolyte molecules. 
And ΔEg(M → M+), ΔEs(M+) and ΔEs(M) can be obtained by employing 
Eqs. S1–3 and data in Table S6.
The calculated oxidation energies of the electrolyte molecules are 
shown in Fig. 2. The corresponding oxidation potentials were calculated 
from these energy values using Eq. (2). With an oxidation energy of 
~7.5 eV, the SL molecule shows stronger antioxidant properties than the 
DME and DOL molecules. The addition of SL to DME or DOL to form 
bimolecular or trimolecular systems causes a slight reduction in oxida­
tion energy. Further, the oxidation energy value of DME + DOL + SL

DME + DOL: 6.18 eV). An electrolyte molecule with a low oxidation 
energy has weak oxidation stability. Considering the common electro­

where Vox is the oxidation potential of an electrolyte molecule, Vox(Li) is

In addition to thermodynamics, the oxidation reaction kinetics of the 
electrolyte molecules should be considered. Eq. (3) shows that ioniza­
tion energy and change in configuration energy contribute to the 
oxidation energies of the electrolyte molecules. Ionization energy is 
related to electron transfer, while the change in configuration energy 
only considers the energy variation due to geometric structure relaxa­
tion. The ionization energy ΔE(M→M+) was calculated using Eq. (1). 
Then, the configuration energy difference ΔEv was obtained by sub­
tracting ΔE(M→M+) from ΔE. Employing the Arrhenius equation, the 
reaction rate was calculated using Eqs. (4) and (5). The configuration 
energy difference ΔEv was significant for directly evaluating the oxida­
tion reaction kinetics. 
ΔE = ΔE(M→M+) + ΔEv
(3) 
ΔEa = (ΔE0 −ΔEv)2/(−4ΔEv)
(4) 
K = Ae(−ΔEa/RT)
(5) 
where ΔE(M→M+) is the ionization energy, ΔEv is the change in 
configuration energy, ΔEa is the oxidation activation energy, ΔE0 is the 
standard change in energy, K is the reaction rate constant, R is the 
universal gas constant, and T is the temperature.
The ΔEv values of the electrolyte molecules were calculated using 
Eqs. (1) and (3), as shown in Fig. 3. The negative values in Fig. 3 indicate 
that the structural relaxation that occurs during oxidation is an energy 
reduction process. From a configuration transformation point of view, 
the oxidation of the electrolyte molecules is thermodynamically pref­
erential. The configuration energy difference ΔEv can weaken the ioni­
zation energy ΔE(M → M+), which is the main contributor to oxidation 
stability.
As displayed in Fig. 3, the DOL molecule has the highest configura­
tion energy difference, while the SL molecule has the lowest configu­
ration energy difference. This indicates that the structure of the DOL 
molecule is quite flexible during oxidation. However, SL and DME are 
more rigid than DOL. Thus, oxidation stability can be improved by the 
addition of SL or DME to the electrolyte. Compared to the DOL system, 
the absolute value of the configuration energy difference declines by 
~35 % in the SL-DOL system. The further addition of DME to form a 
triple-component electrolyte results in a ~70 % decline in the DOL 
configuration energy. Compared to the ΔEv value of the DME-DOL 
cosolvent electrolyte, that of the SL-DME-DOL electrolyte is about 30 
% smaller. These electron-level analyses demonstrate that the addition 
of SL improves the oxidation stability of the Li–S battery electrolytes 
(Table 1).
Next, electrolytes with and without the SL additive were investigated 
at the molecular level to reveal the microscopic structure and transport 
properties of polysulfide species, which can cause severe capacity fading 
in Li–S batteries (Fig. 4a). Fig. 4(b) and S2–4 show the radial distribution 
functions (RDFs) of Li(polysulfide)-solvent pairs. The Li atoms of the 
polysulfide preferentially interact with the oxygen atoms of DME 
(intense RDF peak at 1.95 Å) compared to the oxygen atoms of DOL and 
SL. The computed binding energies (Table 1) confirm that Li (poly­
sulfide) and O (DME) have the strongest binding energy. In contrast, the 
oxygen atoms of SL and Li (polysulfide) have relatively weak binding 
energies. The energy values indicate that DME molecule preferentially 
occupy the position closing to Li atoms of polysulfide and other mole­
cules (DOL and SL) mainly approach to S atoms of polysulfide.
Due to the preferential binding between DME and the polysulfide Li 
atoms, the DOL and SL solvents will mainly interact with the polysulfide 
S atoms to suppress the shuttle effect. Fig. 5 and S5–8 show the RDFs of 
the S (polysulfide)–solvent pairs. The S atoms are divided into Sp1, Sp2, 
Sp3, and Sp4 according to their distance from the Li atoms. The carbon 
atoms of SL and DOL preferentially interact with the S atoms of the 
polysulfide molecules. In particular, strong RDF peaks (~4.0 Å) corre­
sponding to SL and S atoms close to Li (Sp2-3) are observed (Fig. 5). 
Moreover, the RDF peak of DOL and the S atoms far from Li (Sp3-4) is 
relatively strong.
Mean square displacements (MSDs) were employed to study the 
dynamics of polysulfide molecules in different electrolytes (Fig. 6), and 
the self-diffusion rates of polysulfide molecules were calculated using 
the MSDs (Table 2). It can be seen that the small molecule (Li2S4) can 
diffuse fast and the large molecules (Li2S6 and Li2S8) own relatively slow 
diffusion rates. And the addition of SL suppresses the diffusion rates of 
Li2S4 (0.13 times lower), Li2S6 (0.25 times lower) and Li2S8 (0.17 times 
lower). While, Li2S8 clearly diffuses faster than Li2S6 after SL is added to 
the electrolyte.
To understand the effect of SL additive on polysulfide shuttling, the 
binding energies between the solvent molecules (SL and DOL) and the 
polysulfide S atoms were calculated by DFT (Fig. 7). With increasing 
Li–S distance (Sp1-4), the SL-S interactions slightly increase and then 
rapidly decrease. In contrast, the DOL-S interactions keep increasing 
until Sp3. Compared to DOL, SL strongly interacts with the S atoms (Sp1, 
Sp2, and Sp3) closer to Li. This explains the reduction in the diffusion 
rates of all polysulfides when SL is added to the electrolyte. However, 
the DOL molecule preferentially binds with Sp4, which only appears in 
the Li2S8 molecule. Thus, the added SL molecule, partly as replacement 
for the DOL molecule, actually weakens the interactions with the Li2S8 
molecule. It can be responsible for the faster diffusion of Li2S8 than that 
of Li2S6 in the electrolyte with SL. Overall, the shuttle effect of all pol­
ysulfides can be suppressed owing to the addition of SL in the electro­
lyte. This analysis reveals the microscopic effects of using SL as an 
additive on the polysulfide shuttle effect in Li–S batteries.
4. Conclusion
In conclusion, DFT calculations and molecular dynamics simulations 
were employed to reveal the effects of using sulfolane as an additive on 
Li–S battery electrolytes. The sulfolane molecule has a low HOMO en­
ergy level, which provides it with excellent anti-oxidation performance. 
The change in configuration energy was determined to study the 
oxidation reaction kinetics. The addition of sulfolane lowers the

exhibit improved oxidation stability. The RDF results show that sulfo­
lane molecules preferentially interact with the polysulfide S atoms, 
while DME molecules can anchor the polysulfide Li atoms. Compared to 
DOL, sulfolane shows an affinity to the S atoms close to Li (corre­
sponding to low-order polysulfides). Molecular-level analysis shows that 
the addition of sulfolane suppresses the Li2S4, Li2S6 and Li2S8 diffusion 
rates by about 13 %, 25 % and 17 %, respectively. The large binding 
energy between sulfolane and the S atoms close to Li is responsible for 
the strong inhibition of polysulfide diffusion by the sulfolane additive. 
This work unravels the effects of using sulfolane as an additive on the 
electrolyte of Li–S batteries at the microscopic level and is expected to 
inspire the design of electrolytes with improved performance.
