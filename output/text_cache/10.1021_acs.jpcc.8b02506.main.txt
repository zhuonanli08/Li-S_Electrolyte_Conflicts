Polysulﬁde Speciation and Electrolyte Interactions in Lithium−
Sulfur Batteries with in Situ Infrared Spectroelectrochemistry
Caitlin Dillard, Arvinder Singh, and Vibha Kalra*
Chemical and Biological Engineering Department, Drexel University, 3141 Chestnut Street, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19104,
United States
*
S Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Understanding redox mechanisms as well as interactions
between redox species and electrolyte is critical for rational design of
electrolyte/cathode systems for Li−S batteries. Here, we demonstrate in situ
FT-IR with attenuated total reﬂection (ATR) to monitor both polysulﬁde
(PS) speciation (Sx
2−, 2 ≤x ≤8) and triﬂate anion (electrolyte)
coordination state while simultaneously discharging/charging a full battery
coin cell. We report the concentration of various PS species as a function of
voltage during cell discharge. In addition, we found that molecular-level
changes occurred in the electrolyte salt anion in response to PS speciation.
During discharge, PS dissolution increases total solute concentration,
inducing anion interactions between low coordination state complexes
ion pairs and free ionsto form aggregate complexes. Under fast cyclic
voltammetry sweep, less progressive formation of all PSs, due to diﬀusion limitations, resulted in a higher concentration of
aggregates and PSs even upon completion of discharge. This new application of in situ FT-IR oﬀers direct insight into dynamic
interactions between electrolyte salt and polysulﬁdes fundamental in developing Li−S systems.
■INTRODUCTION
In the near future, the energy capabilities of lithium-ion (Li-
ion) batteries will no longer fulﬁll the energy storage
requirements of emerging technologies. Electric vehicles, for
example, require much higher energy density than conven-
tional, intercalation-based batteriessuch as Li-ionto reach
the target range of >250 miles (∼400 km) per charge while
keeping the same weight/volume. The conversion-reaction-
based lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery is one of the most
promising, closest-to-market technologies available, oﬀering a
theoretical energy density of 2600 W h kg−1about ﬁve times
greater than Li-ion. However, several challenges must be
addressed to develop a high-energy-density design that oﬀers
long cycle life, high stability, safe operation, and aﬀordability.
The main challenges include the electronically insulating
nature of both starting active material (elemental sulfur, S8)
and ﬁnal reduction products (Li2S/Li2S2), volumetric change
from reactants to ﬁnal products (∼80%), the complex
electrochemically reversible redox reactions of S8 to Li2S/
Li2S2, and the high solubility of intermediate polysulﬁde
species into the electrolyte (loss of active material). During the
initial discharge (>2.4 V), a solid−liquid reduction occurs, in
which insoluble S8 reduces to soluble S8
2−in high-dielectric
solvents and proceeds to reduce to S6

Monitoring these highly soluble intermediate species is critical
in understanding the fundamental electrochemistry at the
electrolyte−electrode interface. However, only a handful of
studies have demonstrated real-time monitoring of Li−S redox
with nondestructive spectroscopy to probe an electrochemical
cell during operation. For example, X-ray absorption (XAS),5
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR),6 electron paramagnetic
resonance (EPR),7 X-ray diﬀraction (XRD),8,9 Raman,10 and
UV−vis11 have been implemented in Li−S research to
understand the reaction mechanisms during electrochemical
testing. While these in situ techniques are nondestructive, most
are limited to particular species (i.e., soluble vs insoluble,
crystalline species only, radical species only), require complex
instrumentation and cell assembly, have long acquisition times
(limiting the electrochemical testing to very slow charge/
discharge times or C-rates), and/or rely on a suboptimal
electrochemical cell design adapted to the spectrometer.

Among these techniques, XAS oﬀers the most detailed
information, encompassing nearly all redox species; however,
it is highly sophisticated equipment with a complex cell design
and requires a synchrotron radiation source, rendering this
technique inaccessible for quick evaluation of new electrolyte−
electrode systems. Overall, to observe real-time molecular-level
changes under more representative cell operating conditions, in
situ techniques should ideally oﬀer the following: easily
accessible spectrometer or equipment, simple operation,
straightforward integration of a full 2-electrode cell assembly
into a spectrometer, rapid spectral acquisition time, and the
ability to detect multiple species.
In addition, current in situ reports on Li−S do not
investigate molecular-level changes to the electrolyte in
response to polysulﬁde evolution. While polysulﬁde speciation
represents the dynamic redox of Li−S cells, understanding the
interaction mechanisms between polysulﬁdes and electrolyte
species is critical to developing new electrolytes or additives.
The electrolyte system is one of the most inﬂuential factors
governing polysulﬁde evolution and redox kinetics.12,13 For
example, polysulﬁde shuttling occurs in the common binary
electrolyte solvent1,2-dimethoxyethane and 1,3-dioxolane
(DME:DOL)which has been highly regarded owing to its
low viscosity and high lithium-ion mobility but also exhibits
high polysulﬁde solubility.14 Thus, signiﬁcant recent research
has focused on electrolyte additives to improve capacity,
Coulombic eﬃciency, or reversibility as an alternative to
complex polysulﬁde-trapping nanocomposite electrodes.15
Some additivessuch as LiNO3,16 LI,17 and P2S5
18can
form a protective layer at the lithium anode, limiting the
shuttle eﬀect to improve capacity retention. Another approach
utilizing additives aims to create a concentration gradient or
barrier at the electrode/electrolyte interface to limit the
solubility of polysulﬁdessuch as thiol-based19 or imidazole-
based20 additives. Unfortunately, few studies demonstrate an
understanding of the molecular-level interactions between
electrolytes and electrodes under diﬀerent electrochemical
operating conditions. In work by Wu et al., the interactions of a
new additive were investigated with in situ Raman and in situ
UV−vis to understand the direct formation of additive−
polysulﬁde complexes to shed light on the enhanced capacity
attributed to the altered reaction mechanisms.19 However, this
study was speciﬁc to an additive that directly bonds with
polysulﬁdes, which would not be generally applicable to other
functional electrolytes and additives that indirectly suppress
polysulﬁde shuttling. Zhu et al. investigated electrolyte−
electrode interactions in situ with Raman and X-ray diﬀraction
(XRD) to demonstrate polysulﬁde formation kinetics in an
ionic liquid electrolyte.21 Unfortunately, the Raman cells were
discharged and charged at extremely slow rates of C/48−C/
60, which limits the ability to measure changes at faster rates;
meanwhile, XRD is limited to crystalline species (S8 and Li2S)
and required ∼3.5 h to acquire a spectrum.
Thus, establishing an in situ technique that detects both PS
species and electrolyte interactions, in addition to meeting the
design criteria above, would be instrumental for understanding
the direct interactions and kinetics of electrolyte−electrode
systems for designing and improving Li−S batteries. Fourier
transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) is a well-established
spectroscopic technique that is low cost with simple operation
but has yet to be implemented in Li−S batteries under
electrochemical evaluation to monitor Li−S batteries. Recent
work by Saqib et al. established that FT-IR could be used to
detect diﬀerent lithium-polysulﬁde species based on the S−S
vibrational mode.22 This study combined computational
simulations and static experimental FT-IR measurements of
pre-prepared polysulﬁde solutions.
Herein we report a simple in situ technique to probe
polysulﬁde evolution and electrolyte interactions via Fourier
transform-infrared spectroscopy with attenuated total reﬂec-
tion (FT-IR ATR). During the preparation of this manuscript,
a paper on in situ monitoring various polysulﬁdes and their
mean concentrations during charge−discharge of the Li−S cell
in TEGDME-based electrolyte was reported.23 To our
knowledge, this is the ﬁrst study to demonstrate an in situ
spectroelectrochemical technique to simultaneously evaluate
both the redox active species and the electrolyte salt in Li−S
batteries. The in situ cell assembly closely replicated a coin cell
without altering the key battery components, such as
electrolyte volume, cathode, sulfur loading, etc., which was
made possible with the ATR accessory. In this work, we
monitored the evolution of polysulﬁdes and salt anion
coordination states in the lithium triﬂate electrolyte system
(LiCF3SO3/DME:DOL) paired with a free-standing, bind-
erless nanoﬁber cathode design. In this work, we electrospun
carbon nanoﬁber-based sulfur cathodes (S-CNF) such that the
need for a current collector was completely eradicated and the
free-standing S-CNF cathode could be directly probed with the
ATR accessory. During cyclic voltammetry (CV) testing,
polysulﬁde evolution (Sx
2−, 2 ≤x ≤8) was monitored with IR
via the S−S vibrational frequency around ∼500 cm−1.
In addition to monitoring polysulﬁde speciation, the IR
showed cyclic changes in the triﬂate anion coordination, via
the SO3 symmetric vibrational mode ∼1050−1030 cm−1. The
trend showed that the concentration of anion aggregates
drastically increased with the increasing polysulﬁde concen-
tration in the electrolyte. Furthermore, when the CV scan rate
was increased by an order of magnitude, ion aggregates and
soluble polysulﬁdes showed a delay in formation and remained
in the electrolyte solution even at the end of discharge due to
incomplete diﬀusion-limited redox. This technique could be
vital for developing new electrolyte additives as a practical
method for directly studying the PS-additive interactions. For
example, additives incorporated to limit polysulﬁde solubility
could be investigated with in situ FT-IR by detecting
polysulﬁde speciation while determining the degree of
dissolution into electrolyte based on the degree of anion
coordinationspeciﬁcally aggregate formation. Furthermore,
monitoring the coordination states of electrolyte ions could be
invaluable for understanding transport properties for develop-
ing ionic liquid, polymer, and solid electrolyte systems.

electrolyte surrounding the electrolyte−electrode interface

experiment with detailed information on the molecular-level
changes of polysulﬁdes, particularly the polysulﬁde evolution
under diﬀerent conditions, such as scan rate or diﬀerent
electrolyte environments. Independently, Saqib et al. have
demonstrated the use of in situ FT-IR in investigation of
polysulﬁde evolution in another electrolyte system.23
In conventional liquid electrolytes, most polysulﬁdes are
highly soluble, and redox takes place in the electrolyte phase at
the electrode surface. Therefore, in addition to monitoring
polysulﬁde evolution, we have also investigated the impact of
polysulﬁde dissolution on the electrolyte medium. Lithium
triﬂate (electrolyte salt used in this work) consists of two
molecular groups that absorb at varying vibrational frequen-
cies. Since the Li+ cations interact with the −SO3 molecular
group directly, the opposite −CF3 group we believe will exhibit
very little change in vibrational frequencies during CV/
polysulﬁde evolution. Hence the 517 cm−1 band attributed
to the −CF3 bending mode shown in Figures 3 and 4 does not
vary during CV. Although it seems to “disappear” while
discharging, its absorption intensity remained constant, while
the absorption intensities of polysulﬁdes overpowered the 517
cm−1 band. See the evolution of the raw FT-IR spectra in
Figures 3a and 4a for clarity. In the following section, we
discuss the spectroscopic information from the −SO3 group
that details the dynamic structural changes of the triﬂate anion
during discharge and how it coincides with polysulﬁde
evolution.
Dynamic Spectroscopic Changes in the Ion Coordi-
nation States (1050−1030 cm−1). Lithium triﬂate salt
absorbs at various bands corresponding to vibrational modes of
either the −CF3 or −SO3 molecular groups (shown in Table
1). While the former shows negligible changes during
electrochemical testing, the latter exhibits a symmetric
vibrational frequency υs(SO3) at around 1040 cm−1 that is
known to be extremely sensitive to surrounding media.31,33 We
collected two reference FT-IR spectra, A: DME:DOL (1:1 v:v)
and B: LiCF3SO3 + DME:DOL (1:1 v:v) systems, and
subtracted spectra A from B as shown in Figure S4. We can see
from Figure S4 that the “B−A” spectrum exhibits three
distinctive contributions appearing at ∼1050.2, 1039.2, and
1030.8 cm−1. These three positions were assigned to three ion
coordination states of the υs(SO3) band: coordinated ion pairs
(1040 cm−1), free triﬂate ions (1030 cm−1), or ion aggregates
(1050 cm−1) in the literature (see Scheme 1).7,33,34 Figure 5
shows the absorbance spectra around the υs(SO3) band during
the discharge cycle at a slow scan rate (0.05 mV s−1). At the
start of discharge at 2.8 V, there are three peaks attributed to
the three triﬂate ion coordination states as mentioned above.
Deconvolution of these peaks shows that the three bands are
similar in area, which is proportional to concentration, with the
ion pairs and free ions being slightly more favorable than ion
aggregates at equilibrium at relatively low electrolyte
concentrations (see Figure S5 in Supporting Information).
As shown in Figure 5b, the relative peak areas show a dramatic
shift at the ﬁrst reduction peak from 2.35 to 2.32 V. The 1030
cm−1 band rapidly becomes negligible, while the 1050 and
1040 cm−1 bands increase. This could be attributed to the
reduction of elemental S8 to long-chain polysulﬁdes that
dissolve readily into the narrow pockets of electrolyte between
nanoﬁbers in the cathode. Thus, triﬂate anions begin to
aggregate to spatially compensate for the addition of “new
solutes” (soluble polysulﬁdes) in the electrolyte solvent. By the
beginning of the second reduction peak (∼2.1 V), the relative
amount of ion aggregates reaches a maximum, which directly
corresponds to the formation of shorter-chain polysulﬁdes,
Sx
2−(3 ≤x < 8). At the end of this reduction step, soluble Sx
2−
(x > 2) species are consumed to form solid polysulﬁdes (Li2S2/
Li2S), which deposit on an electrode surface. Thus, the
concentration of dissolved species drastically reduces causing
the number of ion aggregates to decrease toward the
equilibrium state. Likewise, the ion pairs and free ions
approach their equilibrium values after the electrochemical
reduction is completed.

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Cathode Fabrication. For the preparation of CNFs, a 10%
polyacrylonitrile (PAN) (Sigma-Aldrich, MW = 150 000) in
N,N-dimethyl formamide (DMF) (Sigma-Aldrich) solution
was prepared and electrospun at a ﬂow rate of 0.2−0.4 mL h−1
and a constant applied voltage of ∼9 kV. The distance between
the nozzle tip and aluminum foil collector was kept at ∼15 cm.
Free-standing PAN nanoﬁber mats were then stabilized to 280
°C for 5 h in air and carbonized to 1000 °C for 1 h in nitrogen.
The ultrarapid sulfur technique to incorporate sulfur into an 11
mm CNF disc is reported in our previous unpublished work.32
In brief, commercial sulfur was peppered across the surface of
the CNF disc and placed in a heat press that was preheated to
∼140 °C. The sulfur + CNF was pressed to ∼200 psi for only
5 s, removed, and weighed and was directly used in the S-CNF
cathode without any further treatment. The sulfur loading of
the S-CNF cathodes in this work was ∼2.0 mg cm−2.
Synthesis of Reference Polysulﬁde Solutions. Three
reference lithium polysulﬁde solutions, viz., Li2S8 (Li2S:S; 1:7),
Li2S6 (Li2S:S; 1:5), and Li2S2 (Li2S:S; 1:1) (concentration: 10
mmoL), were synthesized by adding sulfur and Li2S in a
stoichiometric ratio in DME:DOL (1:1) solvents and
subsequently stirring and heating at ∼60 °C for 96 h. The
Li2S8 solution was turned dark brown, and Li2S6 and Li2S2
solutions became light brown and green-yellow, respectively,
after 96 h. We did not see any residual/settled powder after 96
h.
FT-IR Characterization. A Fourier transform infrared (FT-
IR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo-Fisher Scientiﬁc) was
used to collect all spectra using an extended range diamond
ATR accessory. All infrared spectra were collected under
ambient conditions using a deuterated triglycine sulfate
(DTGS) detector at a resolution of 16 scans per spectrum at
4 cm−1, resulting in spectrum collection intervals of ∼20 s. All
spectra were corrected with a background subtraction of the
ATR crystal spectrum, followed by a baseline correction, and
advanced ATR correction in the Thermo Scientiﬁc Omnic
software package and Omnic Macros Basic. Polysulﬁde peaks
at diﬀerent voltages were ﬁtted using the Peak Resolve feature
in which 7 Gaussian peaks were automatically ﬁtted to the
active spectra in the 510−470 cm−1 region. The area under
each curve was normalized by subtracting the minimum area
across all voltages (which was repeated independently for each
peak center location) to obtain the relative changes in
individual species concentrations. For collecting ATR FT-IR
spectra of reference solutions, a diamond puck was transferred
into the glovebox. We put ∼2 μL of the given reference
solution on the diamond crystal and then sealed it using a cap
of the 2032 coin cell and vacuum grease.
Electrochemical Characterization. To build the in situ
FT-IR cell, the extended range diamond crystal ATR accessory
was transferred into an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. A coin cell
(CR2032) assembly was replicated by placing the S-CNF
cathode directly on the diamond ATR crystal. Along with a
Celgard separator, ∼30 μL of electrolyte, a pure lithium-foil
anode, and a nickel-foam spacer were assembled on top of the
cathode. The negative CR2032 cap (with insulated gasket) was
secured on the top of the assembly to seal it from ambient air
when removed from the glovebox. The pressure anvil on the
FT-IR compressed the cell components to ensure good
contact, both electrochemically between cell components and
spectroscopically between the diamond crystal and the sample.
The electrolyte composition was ∼1.85 M lithium triﬂuor-
omethanesulfonate (lithium triﬂate) (Acros Organics) and 0.1
M lithium nitrate (Sigma-Aldrich) in a mixture of 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1,3-dioxolane (Acros
Organics) in a 1:1 volume ratio.
Prior to electrochemical testing, cells were allowed to rest at
open-circuit potential for 30 min. Cyclic voltammetry was
conducted with a potentiostat (Gamry reference 1000) at scan

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we demonstrated a novel, in situ spectroelec-
trochemical technique using FT-IR ATR to investigate both
polysulﬁde evolution and triﬂate anion coordination states for
Li−S batteries. Relative to other in situ spectroscopy
techniques, FT-IR oﬀers several advantages: it is a simple
and accessible equipment, does not require special radiation
sources, and delivers extremely rapid acquisition times. In
addition, the cell assembly closely replicated a true coin cell,
which eliminated excessive electrolyte volume or a three-
electrode setup. We demonstrated this technique on the
lithium triﬂate/DME:DOL electrolyte system to determine
how triﬂate anions change coordination states in response to
the electrochemical Li−S redox reactions at diﬀerent CV scan
rates. More importantly, the symmetric vibrational mode of the
triﬂate anion is highly sensitive to its coordination states (free
ions, ion pairs, and ion aggregates) which could be used to
indicate the change in overall concentration of electrolyte
specieswhere inﬁnitely dilute electrolyte would exhibit
mostly “free” ions and highly concentrated electrolytes would
show mostly ion aggregates. For example, the relative
concentration of ionic aggregate complexes reached a
maximum corresponding to the point of discharge when the
concentration of polysulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte
reaches a maximum. The relationship between polysulﬁde
dissolution and anion coordination changes can provide two
key insights for the future development of electrode or
electrolyte systems: (i) As for the lithium salt, the ion
distribution and ion association play an important role in the
electrolyte transport propertiessuch as ionic diﬀusivity,
mobility, and transference number.43 For example, ion pairs
or aggregates decrease the number of charge carriers. In situ
monitoring could provide insight into the transport kinetics,
especially in polymer or ionic liquid electrolytes.44 (ii) The
degree of polysulﬁde dissolution can be detected. While
previous in situ works detect polysulﬁdes, they have yet to
study its response on the electrolyte. This can be critical for
directly probing electrodes or additives designed to conﬁne
sulfur or suppress dissolution. In this type of application,
polysulﬁdes may be detectable, while no electrolyte changes
would be observed during cyclingwhich would indicate that
polysulﬁdes are successfully retained at the cathode surface
such that the lack of dissolution leaves the electrolyte
unaltered. Therefore, the simple application of the in situ
FT-IR technique in Li−S batteries could be instrumental in the
development and design of electrolytes, additives, and new
systems, including polymer and solid electrolytes, to create a
fundamental understanding of polysulﬁde evolution and
interaction with shuttle-mitigating materials.
