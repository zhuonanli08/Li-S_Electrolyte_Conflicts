ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur batteries possess favorable
potential for energy-storage applications because of their
high speciﬁc capacity and the low cost of sulfur. Intensive
understanding of the interfacial mechanism, especially the
polysulﬁde formation and transformation under complex
electrochemical environment, is crucial for the buildup of
advanced batteries. Here, we report the direct visualization of
interfacial evolution and dynamic transformation of the
sulﬁdes mediated by the lithium salts via real-time atomic
force microscopy monitoring inside a working battery. The
observations indicate that the lithium salts inﬂuence the
structures and processes of sulﬁde deposition/decomposition
during discharge/charge. Moreover, the distinct ion interaction and the diﬀusion in electrolytes manipulate the interfacial
reactions determining the kinetics of the sulﬁde transformation. Our ﬁndings provide deep insights into surface dynamics of
lithium−sulfur reactions revealing the salt-mediated mechanisms at nanoscale, which contribute to the profound understanding
of the interfacial processes for the optimized design of lithium−sulfur batteries.
1. INTRODUCTION
Since the conventional Li-ion batteries are approaching their
theoretical energy density limits, new electrochemical energy
storage systems are urgently demanded for various applications
with high-energy density and long service life. Lithium−sulfur
(Li−S) batteries have been widely considered as one of the
most promising candidates because of the high theoretical
speciﬁc energy of 2600 Wh kg−1 and because of the nontoxicity
and low cost of sulfur.1,2 However, successful commercializa-
tion is still hindered by several obstacles which predominantly
relate to the complicated interfacial reactions. On the
cathode−electrolyte interfaces, the cathode reduction experi-
ences a solid−liquid−solid phase transformation from sulfur to
intermediate polysulﬁdes and Li2S2/Li2S. The dissolving of
polysulﬁdes will cause the internal shuttle and, thus, will give
rise to the deposition of the “dead” sulﬁdes, nonsoluble and
irreversible Li2S2/Li2S on both electrodes, leading to the high
interfacial resistance and continuous capacity degradation on
cycling.3−5
Studies have been focused on the stabilization of the
interfaces between the Li metal and electrolytes. Various
electrolyte additives, such as LiNO3,6 Cs+ or Rb+ ions,7 and
LiF,8 have been explored to improve the solid−electrolyte
interphases (SEI) protection. Eﬀects of the Li salts in
electrolytes have been thoroughly investigated on Li metal
surfaces via practical tests9 and theoretical simulations.10 In
addition, concepts of the solid-state11 and solvent-in-salt12
electrolyte are also proposed toward the eﬀective suppression
of dendrite formation and polysulﬁde dissolution. In spite of
the eﬀorts on overcoming the issues of Li metal anodes, Li−S
batteries still face various interfacial challenges on cathodes.
The substantial aggregation of inactive Li2S2/Li2S passivates
the cathodes and inhibits the interfacial charge transfer during
discharge/charge, dramatically deteriorating the capacity and
long-range stability.

Therefore, electrolyte design should include the under-
standing and control of the reactions on cathodes, such as the
precipitation/dissolution and the kinetic processes of the
sulﬁdes. Pan et al. recently compared the morphologies of Li2S
obtained among a range of solvents, indicating that the
solvents with intermediate donor numbers balanced the
nucleation and growth of the polysulﬁdes inﬂuencing the
electrochemical performance.13 Similarly, solvating additives in
electrolytes were reported to favor the deposition of Li2O2
toroids in Li−O2 batteries at the interfaces between the oxygen
cathode and the electrolyte, providing basic guidance to the
interfacial design and capacity improvements.14,15 The anions
in Li salts play a key role in the ion−solvent interactions in
electrolytes and the polysulﬁde dissolution on cathode−
electrolyte interfaces.16 However, few researchers have studied
the Li salt eﬀects on the interfacial evolution of the sulﬁdes on
cathodes. Moreover, though the electrolyte design becomes a
consensus for the further advancements of Li−S batteries, the
real-time dynamic transformation at the cathode−electrolyte
interfaces is still unknown, which is limited by the in-situ
techniques with both high temporal and spatial resolution.
Four-dimensional (4D) insights involving the time scale into
the underlying mechanisms of the electrolyte-dependent
sulﬁde reactions are urgently required through real-time
monitoring, including the morphology evolution, structural
transition, and kinetic conditions under the electrochemical
environments.
Here, we provide direct insights into the diﬀerent dynamic
processes of Li−S electrochemical reaction at cathode/
electrolyte interfaces correlated to the properties of the anions
in Li salts and propose the corresponding interfacial
mechanisms upon discharging/charging. Using in-situ atomic
force microscopy (AFM), we monitor the dynamic evolution
of solid sulﬁdes on a highly oriented pyrolytic graphite
(HOPG) cathode in a lithium polysulﬁde (LPS) semiliquid
battery, where the codeposition of lamellar and spherical Li2S
is imaged with the binary-salt electrolyte of lithium bis-
(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide
(LiFSI)
and
lithium
bis-
(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI). The in-situ mon-
itoring provides evidence for the mediating eﬀects of the Li
salts over the interfacial precipitation of Li2S. The oriented
reaction direction of <200> dominates the routes and
behaviors of Li2S decomposition, which is strongly correlated
with the electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries.
Furthermore, the ion diﬀusion and interfacial reactions can
be manipulated by Li salts, determining the reaction kinetics
under distinct salt environments. Understanding the electrolyte
dependency of Li−S electrochemistry is essential for the
interfacial research, oﬀering a fundamentally new perspective
for designing high-capacity Li−S and other rechargeable
technologies.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of Materials. 1,2-Dimethosyethane (DME),
1,3-dioxolane (DOL), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) (purity >99.5%) were
from Sigma-Aldrich. Lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) was
acquired from Suzhou Fluolyte Co. (purity >99.95%). Li2S (purity
>99.9%) and LiNO3 (purity >99.98%) were purchased from Alfa-
Aesar. All the materials were used as received. In the electrochemical
cell, both the counter and the reference electrodes were lithium strips.
To form the passivation layer on the surface, the lithium strips were
soaked in 0.5 M LiTFSI−LiFSI (1:2) binary-salt electrolyte in DOL/
DME solution (1:1) with 1 wt% LiNO3 additive for 1.5 h.
The LPS semiliquid was studied in our research, and the lithium
polysulﬁde (Li2S8) acted as the liquid cathode. The Li2S8 was
prepared by mixing stoichiometric amounts of Li2S and sulfur in 0.5
M LiTFSI−LiFSI (1:2) binary-salt electrolyte in DOL/DME (1:1) at
60 °C overnight. In this study, 5 mM solution was used. LPS coincells
were also tested with the 5 mM Li2S8 catholyte in LiTFSI−LiFSI
(1:2) for cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests, cathodes of which were the
8:1 weight ratio mixture of carbon (Ketjenblack) and poly(vinyl
diﬂuoride) binder, mixed as slurry in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone and
dried at 60 °C overnight. In addition, the freshly cleaved HOPG
(ZYH type, Bruker Corp.) was used as the working electrode for the
in-situ AFM cell.
2.2. Electrochemical AFM. The three-electrode LPS cell was
used for electrochemical AFM experiments. Lithium strips were
employed as the counter and reference electrodes, and HOPG was
selected as the working electrode using Li2S8 as the catholyte. The
digital image of the cell for AFM monitoring was provided (Figure
S1). The port for electrolyte entrance, the support post for counter
electrode, and the entry hole for reference electrode were integrated
into the cell as marked in Figure S1. The electrochemical AFM cell
was then assembled on the commercial AFM system (Bruker,
Multimode 8 with Nanoscope V controllers) coupled with a
potentiostat for in-situ electrochemical AFM experiments. HOPG
was placed at the bottom of the cell as the working electrode, which
was carefully cleaved by adhesive tape before use. The schematic
diagram of the electrode and cell designs for AFM monitoring was
depicted in our previous report.17 An O ring with the diameter of 15
mm was used to seal the cell. All experiments were carried out in an
argon-ﬁlled glovebox (MIKROUNA, Super 1220/750, H2O < 0.1
ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm). All potentials were referred to Li+/Li. During
the discharge process, the potential was swept toward negative
potential from open circuit potential (OCP, about 2.44 V) to 1.5 V
and positive to 3.0 V for the subsequent oxidation. HOPG surface was
scanned by electrochemical AFM with an insulating triangular silicon
nitride AFM tip (force constant of 3−26 N m−1) in peak force
tapping mode. In this study, each of the AFM images took 3 min 15 s.
Additionally, the CVs in LPS cells with various electrolytes were
measured by the electrochemical workstation (Methrohm Autolab,
PGSTAT302N) in the range 1.5−3.0 V at diﬀerent sweep rates.
2.3. Material Characterization. HOPG electrodes were
separated from their respective cells via constant potential control
at speciﬁc stages and were rinsed by DMC solution to remove the
residual electrolyte on the surface. It was dried in an argon-ﬁlled
glovebox before the ex-situ analyses. The X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on an ESCALa-
b220i-XL electron spectrometer (VG scientiﬁc) using 300 W Al Kα
radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). The base pressure was about 3 × 10−9
mbar, and the binding energies were referenced to the hydrocarbon C
1s peak at 284.8 eV. X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) experiments were
performed on an Empyrean instrument (PANalytical) using a step
size of 0.0260° at 120 s per step over a 2θ range of 5−90°. High-
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were
characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM
2100F) with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. High-angle annular
dark-ﬁeld (HAADF) images, element mapping, and EELS were
captured on a JEOL ARM200F transmission electron microscope
equipped with double CS correctors for the condenser lens and
objective lens.
2.4. Theoretical Calculation. The density functional theory
(DFT) calculations performed in this study are carried out with the
Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)18,19 within the projector
augmented-wave (PAW) approach.20,21 The generalized gradient
approximation is adopted in the parametrization of Perdew, Burke,
and Ernzerhof (PBE)22 to describe the exchange-correlation func-
tional. For all calculations, the cutoﬀs of wave function and charge
density are 500.0 and 756.3 eV, respectively. At ﬁrst, the relaxed
molecular structures of Li2S, LiFSI, and LiTFSI are obtained
individually. Then, three models with one Li2S molecule absorbed
on graphene layer are established, in which the eﬀects of LiFSI and
LiTFSI are considered by bonding Li2S with each of the salt molecule.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. In-Situ AFM Study of Lithium Sulﬁde Deposition.
To real-time monitor the interfacial deposition of lithium
sulﬁdes during discharge, LPS semiliquid battery is constructed
for the in-situ AFM experiments where liquid polysulﬁde is
used as the catholyte. Figure 1a presents the typical cyclic
voltammetry (CV) of LPS cells. Three cathodic peaks can be
detected at a slow sweep rate for the carbon electrode with no
embedded sulfur.23 As the active material, 5 mM Li2S8 is added
in the electrolyte with 0.5 M LiTFSI−LiFSI (1:2) binary salts
in 1,2-dimethosyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (1:1).
Three cathodic peaks at 2.38, 2.07, and 1.97 V can be
attributed to the reduction of long-chain polysulﬁdes and the
formation of insoluble Li2S2 and Li2S. The subsequent anodic
curve displays a peak at 2.42 V and a shoulder one at 2.48 V,
indicating the reversible oxidation of the short-chain sulﬁdes.24
A three-electrode LPS electrochemical cell is employed for in-
situ AFM experiments, with HOPG as a working electrode, Li
metal as counter and reference electrodes, and the identical
Li2S8 solution for CV as the catholyte. Atomically ﬂat terraces
of HOPG are observed at open circuit potential (OCP) of ca.
2.44 V (Figure 1b), and the discharging process features the
morphology evolution of the deposition of reduction products
as shown in Figure 1c−f. Nanoparticle (NP) nuclei with 1−2
nm thickness (Figure S2a) form at 2.1 V (Figure 1c), followed
by the growth of spherical sediments at 2.0 V marked by yellow
arrows in Figure 1d. As measured in Figure S2b, the average
height of the spherical products is about 26.5 nm at the initial
state. With the discharge proceeding, lamellar products begin
to deposit since slice edges can be discovered at the end of
Figure 1e (marked by the green arrows). It is obvious that the
codeposition of both spherical and lamellar structures can be
observed clearly in Figure 1f at the potential of 1.5 V. The
voltage range, corresponding to the participation of NPs (2.1
V) and the spherical/lamellar sediments (2.0−1.75 V), consists
of Li2S2 and Li2S formation as marked in the CV of Figure 1a,
respectively, providing clues for the analyses of the chemical
information on these reduction products.
3.2. Characterization of the Lithium Sulﬁdes. The X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in Figure S3 shows a
weak peak at 684.5 eV attributed to LiF.25 The ratio of LiF
increases a little from 8.91% to 18.22% after discharge to 1.5 V,
revealing the little decomposition of the anions. In Figure S4,
the CV does not show signiﬁcant anion reduction compared
with that of the sulﬁde reactions. Neither NPs nor spherical
and lamellar products exist overspreading during discharge in
bare electrolyte without Li2S8 (Figure S5), representing that
the little decomposition of the anions cannot be attributed to
the observed spherical/lamellar sediments at the cathode−
electrolyte interfaces. Thus, the deposits could be related to the
insoluble sulﬁdes (Li2S2 and Li2S) reduced by polysulﬁdes.
The S 2p spectra show an additional peak at 162.0 eV of the
NPs related to Li2S2 (Figure 2a) when compared with that of
the sample soaked in electrolyte (Figure S6).26 The obvious
peak at 161.2 eV occurs in Figure 2b after the deposition of
spherical and lamellar products, which can be identiﬁed as the
growth of Li2S.27,28 Scanning transmission electron microscopy
(STEM) is performed to further conﬁrm the formation of Li2S.
Because of the ultrasonic dispersion before STEM inves-
tigation, the cluster can be recognized as the spherical and
lamellar samples in the high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld
(HAADF)-STEM image in Figure 2c. Uniform element
distribution of S can be observed (Figure 2d), demonstrating
the attribution of the cluster to the reduced sulﬁdes.29,30

3.3. Decomposition Pathways of Li2S. The interfacial
decomposition of the lamellar and spherical Li2S is further
investigated by in-situ AFM, as shown in Figure 4. We focus on
the lamellar structures marked as 1 and 2 in Figure 4a, and no
morphological change is observed up to 2.4 V. At 2.65 V, edges
of the layers start to decompose, as depicted clearly in the
bottom left corner of lamella 1 (Figure 4b) and on the right
side of 2 (Figure 4c) marked by yellow arrows. Maintaining the
potential at 2.65 V, the top layers stay smaller accompanied by
the collapse from the underlayer decomposition (Figure 4d).
The decomposition continues from the edge to center, and
slices of diﬀerent layers are hard to recognize in Figure 4e. At 3
V, the lamellar structures collapse completely with several
fragments remaining (Figure 4f). Since the image height is
enlarged by the lamellar structures, sharp images of the
spherical product decomposition have to be obtained
separately as depicted in Figure 4g−l. The scan range is
reduced in Figure 4g. Interestingly, at 2.65 V, nanoscale
processes of decomposition are directly monitored as two dark
holes start to appear on the centers of spherical products A and
B (Figure 4h). The holes become larger with the increasing of
charging time, and new ones are dug and even connected as
can be seen on the spherical product C in Figure 4i and
product A in Figure 4j, k. The “crater” positions are smoothed
with the enhanced ion ﬂux promoting the decomposition at
those spots (Figure 4l),32 which is presented clearly in the
three-dimensional (3D) image in Figure S9f. The interfacial
decomposition of the spherical products occurs from the
center to edge, and only boundaries are left after charging at 3
V.
3.4. Salt-Mediated Reaction Mechanism. As illustrated
schematically in Figure 5, the full cycle reaction processes in
LiTFSI−LiFSI binary-salt electrolyte are proposed in terms of
the experimental measurements. Figure 5a depicts the dynamic
evolution during discharge. The long-chain polysulﬁdes can be
ﬁrst reduced to sulﬁde intermediates which are solved in the
electrolyte (step 1). Insoluble Li2S2 nucleates and grows in the
shape of NPs (step 2). Spherical Li2S deposits on decreasing
the potential, and thereafter, the lamellar Li2S occurs as shown
in step 3. Figure 5b displays the product oxidation upon
charge. The lamellar Li2S exhibits two-dimensional (2D)
decomposition mode from the edge to center, while the
spherical Li2S oxidation starts from the holes appearing from
the center. In step 4, edges of the lamellar Li2S start to
decompose, while holes appear on the centers of spherical Li2S.
As the charge proceeds, layers collapse for the lamellar Li2S
accompanied by the holes becoming larger and connected for
the spherical Li2S (step 5). Thus, fragments remain from the
middles and boundaries of the lamellar and spherical Li2S,
respectively (step 6). Figure 5b1 and Figure 5b2 illustrates the
detailed decomposition process of a single lamellar and
spherical Li2S for clear expression. The atomic arrangements
on Li2S (200) and (220) planes are provided in Figure 5c and
d. On the basis of the interfacial processes of Li−S
electrochemical reaction, the salt-mediated mechanism of the
structure evolution can be discussed as follows.
1. During discharge, the LiFSI causes 3D spherical Li2S
growth, and LiTFSI induces 2D lamellar Li2S, which is
provided according to the in-situ AFM experiments in

at the “craters” of these holes which promotes the
decomposition and hole connection. Therefore, boundaries
remain upon charging because of the center-to-edge
decomposition pathway of the spherical Li2S.
The interfacial structure and dynamic transformation are
strongly correlated with the battery performance, which is
critical for the radical understanding and designing of advanced
batteries with optimized electrolyte. As shown in Figure S11,
the total volume of the spherical Li2S (2693 μm3) is ∼1.7 times
larger than that of the lamellar Li2S (1567 μm3) at the same 50
μm × 50 μm scale as analyzed by the image-measuring tool.
This volume diﬀerence is inherent to the structures of sphere
and lamella, which might be one of the important factors
explaining the larger initial capacity of the Li−S battery in
LiFSI-based electrolyte.33 Besides that, the reversibility of the
spherical and lamellar Li2S is compared upon charging under
the same circumstance with the LiTFSI−LiFSI electrolyte. We
shifted the potential from 2.4 to 3.0 V at the rate of 1 mV s−1
and stayed at 2.65 V for about 20 min to record the
decomposition. The volume of both spherical and lamellar Li2S
at 2.4 and 3.0 V can be obtained as indicated in Figure S12. It
is shown that 52.1% of the lamellar Li2S and 74.6% of the
spherical one remained upon charging at 3.0 V, reﬂecting that
more active product was oxidized in the former case than in
the latter one. Accordingly, the combination of the lamellar
and spherical Li2S on cathode can take advantage of both of
them, leading to the relatively high reversibility and capacity of
the Li−S batteries in LiTFSI−LiFSI binary-salt electrolyte in
contrast to those in individual-salt electrolytes (Figure S13).
These results reveal that the salt-mediated interfacial reactions
on cathodes, including the sulﬁde structure evolution and
growth/decomposition processes, should be highly considered
when analyzing the battery performance in various electrolytes,
even though the previous research always places emphasis on
the conductivity, the viscosity of the electrolytes,38 and the SEI
formation on anodes.16,33,39 Moreover, the understanding of
the preferential decomposition in Li2S <200> direction can in
turn help speciﬁcally build crystalline cathodes in Li2S-based
batteries, providing signiﬁcant guidelines for the eﬃcient
activation and utilization of the Li2S material.
In addition, roles of the Li salts in mediating the kinetics of
the Li−S electrochemistry are further elucidated and compared
by the CV analyses as shown in Figure S14. The current
densities (i) of the reduction peaks are plotted versus the scan
rates (v) (Figure S15). By ﬁtting the data to the equation i =
avb, the kinetic factor that dominates the reaction can be
identiﬁed, as b = 0.5 represents a diﬀusion-controlled reaction
and b = 1 represents a surface reaction controlled one.40,41 The
corresponding b values are labeled in the insets of Figure S15.
Figure S15a presents the plots of the polysulﬁde-transition
reaction (peak 1) with the scan rates in three environments.
The b values in LiTFSI, LiFSI, and LiTFSI−LiFSI binary-salt
environments are 0.87, 0.92, and 1.02, respectively, indicating
that the kinetics is transferring from a mix-controlled reaction
to the surface reaction governed one. This variation can be
attributed to the change of Li−S interactions and electrolyte
viscosity in diﬀerent Li salt electrolytes. The TFSI−anions
added in the electrolyte can interact actively with Li ions and
weaken the Li−S networks in sulﬁdes, accelerating the surface
reaction as reported recently.42 In LiTFSI−LiFSI binary-salt
electrolyte, the higher ionic conductivity with lower viscosity38
is beneﬁcial for the Li ion diﬀusion and promotes the
interfacial reactions governing the kinetics. The Li ions in
LiFSI-based electrolyte can coordinate with not only O but
also F atoms from the solvents and salts,10 indicating the
weaker Li−S networks16 and the further accelerated surface
reaction. However, the increased ionic conductivity and the
decreased viscosity of the LiFSI-based electrolyte accelerate
the diﬀusion as well.38 Thus, both the interfacial reaction and
the diﬀusion act and mediate the reaction kinetics in LiFSI-
based electrolyte. As shown in Figure S15b, c, the b values of
the short-chain sulﬁde reactions (peaks 2 and 3) decrease in
these three electrolytes, indicating that the kinetics is impeded
by the slow solid-state diﬀusion. Because of the strong Li−S
network and less interaction with the solvent molecules, the
short-chain sulﬁdes tend to exist as dimers or clusters in
electrolyte.43 The solid-state reaction nature limits the Li ion
diﬀusion during the short-chain sulﬁde transitions, resulting in
the decline of the b values.
4. CONCLUSION
In conclusion, via in-situ AFM monitoring, we have
successfully disclosed the interfacial mechanism of the impact
of Li salts on the dynamic evolution of the Li−S electro-
chemical reactions. It is discovered that Li salts play a key role
in governing the growth pathways of Li2S at nanoscale, which
determine the corresponding deposition morphology and
decomposition modes upon discharge/charge. LiTFSI salt
