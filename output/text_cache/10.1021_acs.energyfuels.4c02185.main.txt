ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur batteries hold great promise for energy storage
because of their high theoretical capacity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental
friendliness. However, their commercialization has been hindered by the low
conductivity of the discharge product lithium sulfide (Li2S) and its high oxidation
energy barrier, resulting in low sulfur utilization, poor reversibility, and rapid
capacity decay. This study investigates the addition of diethyldiselenide (DEDSe)
into conventional electrolytes to mitigate Li2S deficiencies. The result
demonstrates the breakage of the Se−Se bond in DEDSe and the elongation of
the Li−S bond in Li2S. Moreover, DEDSe reduces the oxidation energy barriers
and accelerates the oxidation kinetics of Li2S. Quantitative analysis confirms the
decrease of irreversible Li2S deposition at the electrode. As a result, batteries with
DEDSe contents exhibit excellent electrochemical performance. Specifically, cells
with 2.0 wt % DEDSe added show a high initial discharge capacity of 1314.2 mAh
g−1 in the high sulfur-loading pouch cell, maintaining 992.5 mAh g−1 after 34 cycles, which is significantly higher than conventional
electrolyte cells at 562.7 mAh g−1. This study offers innovative perspectives on addressing practical challenges and improving the
cycling stability of lithium−sulfur batteries.
1. INTRODUCTION
The evolution of energy storage technologies has been driven
by the pursuit of safer, more efficient, and environmentally
friendly alternatives to conventional lithium-ion batteries. In
this context, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have emerged as a
promising candidate for next-generation energy storage
systems.1−4 Unlike traditional lithium-ion batteries, which
rely on intercalation chemistry,5−9 Li−S batteries store energy
through a conversion reaction between lithium and sulfur.10
The appeal of Li−S batteries lies in their high theoretical
energy density, which significantly surpasses that of lithium-ion
batteries. Furthermore, sulfur is abundant, low-cost, and
environmentally friendly, providing both economic and
ecological benefits compared to other battery chemistries.11,12
Despite their significant advantages, the practical application of
lithium−sulfur batteries is still hindered by several obstacles,
notably the notorious shuttle effect of highly soluble lithium
polysulfide (LiPSs) intermediates and the slow sulfur redox
kinetics during cycling.13 This continuous shuttling of LiPSs
leads to electrolyte depletion, increased self-discharge, and
capacity degradation.14−16
In addressing these challenges, researchers have undertaken
persistent endeavors. These efforts encompass the design and
synthesis of host materials with varied structures to physically
confine polysulfides,17−20 enhancing redox kinetics through the
absorption and catalytic transformation of LiPSs using
nitrides,21,22 sulfides,23−25 oxides,26 and other strategies.27−29
Moreover, high-entropy compounds30 and quantum dots31−33
have been introduced to enhance catalysis. Additionally, some
researchers have successfully isolated LiPSs by alleviating their
shuttle effect through separator modifications or functional
interlayers.34−36 These endeavors led to a notable enhance-
ment in battery performance. However, most researchers have
focused on mitigating the shuttle effect, specifically through the
adsorption and catalysis of LiPSs, while neglecting the issue of
nonconductive and insoluble Li2S. Li and his collaborators
identified the formation and accumulation of nonconductive
Li2S and Li2S2 films on the cathode surface as the primary
cause of rapid capacity decay in Li−S batteries.37 Sang-Eun
Cheon also observed that even at 100% charging depth, soluble
polysulfide hardly oxidizes into solid sulfur, and some Li2S
remains on the carbon matrix resulting in the active substance
not being fully utilized.38 Additionally, Miran found that
during the later stages of discharge, the Li2S exhibits poor
electronic and ionic conductivity, and its deposition on the
electrode interface inhibits the conversion of LiPSs to Li2S,
ultimately leading to capacity loss.39 Furthermore, Ali
Eftekhari’s research indicates that due to the faster ion

in electrode passivation, and the excessive oxidation energy
barrier hinders its full participation in the reaction, resulting in
the loss of active substances.40 Therefore, to advance the
development of Li−S batteries with high specific energy and
extended cycle life, it is essential to address the low reactivity of
Li2S that leads to incomplete conversion during the charge−
discharge cycles, contributing to rapid capacity decay and
limiting the practical application.
Selenium (Se), recognized for its substantially higher
conductivity relative to sulfur, has garnered significant
attention as a prospective cathode component in lithium-ion
batteries. Recent research conducted by Kim et al. demon-
strates that selenium, when doped with sulfur in the cathode,
can significantly reduce battery polarization. This enhancement
is attributed to selenium’s ability to improve the electronic
conductivity of the electrode material, thereby facilitating more
efficient charge transfer and reducing overall resistance within
the battery system.41 Additionally, Zhang’s research illustrates
that selenium in lithium polysulfide-Se (LiPS-Se) config-
urations facilitate electron provision during discharge, leading
to the reduction of numerous LiPSs, while selenium in the
resulting lithium disulfide-Se (Li2S−Se) acts as an electron
acceptor, aiding in the rapid decomposition of Li2S during
subsequent charging cycles.42 Furthermore, Fu proposes that
phenyl diselenide (PDSe) can effectively reduce the charge
overpotential of Li2S through an inductive effect.43 Given the
advantages of Se, this study explores the addition of
diethyldiselenide (DEDSe) as an electrolyte additive to Li−S
batteries and investigates its impact on battery performance.
The findings indicate that the inclusion of DEDSe lowers the
oxidation energy barrier of insoluble Li2S due to the inductive
effect between Se and Li2S, thereby enhancing the oxidation
kinetics of Li2S and significantly improving the overall battery
performance. Quantitative analysis shows that after a single
charge, the residual Li2S in the battery decreases from 1.7 to
0.55%, effectively reducing irreversible deposition and loss of
Li2S. Consequently, the battery containing DEDSe exhibits
outstanding performance, with its pouch cell configuration
achieving a capacity of 1314.2 mAh/g at 0.1 C.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Different weight fractions (1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 8.0 wt %) of DEDSe
were incorporated into conventional electrolytes. The cyclic
performance at a current density of 0.5 C, along with the
energy and Coulombic efficiency, are depicted in Figure 1a.
The initial discharge capacity of the conventional electrolyte
was 1032.0 mAh g−1, and after 100 cycles, the specific
discharge capacity decreased to 658.7 mAh g−1, with a capacity
retention rate of only 63.82%. The battery with DEDSe
exhibited superior specific discharge capacity compared to the
conventional electrolyte, with the most significant enhance-
ment observed at 8.0 wt %. The initial discharge capacity of the
DEDSe-containing battery increased significantly to 1386.0
mAh g−1; after 100 cycles, the specific capacities of batteries
containing 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, and 8.0 wt % DEDS were 881.7, 891.9,
882, and 876.1 mAh g−1, respectively, surpassing those of the
conventional electrolyte system. Additionally, Figure 1b
illustrates the polarization curve indicating that the cells with
DEDSe additive exhibit a smoother discharge curve and
reduced polarization. However, the batteries with 8.0 wt %
DEDSe exhibited noticeable capacity degradation, low
Coulombic efficiency, and poor cycling stability, and the
specific capacity after 100 cycles did not differ significantly
from other DEDS-containing batteries. Notably, the 2.0 wt %
DEDSe battery demonstrated excellent cyclic stability and high
Coulombic efficiency, with a capacity retention rate of 73.66%.
Therefore, adding 2.0 wt % is considered the most suitable
amount, leading to the selection of a 2.0 wt % DEDSe battery
for further in-depth study.
Electrolyte additives typically containing S and Se groups
contribute to the battery capacity during cycling, as illustrated
in Figure 1c. The cycling results of an electrolyte containing
2.0 wt % DEDSe in a carbon cathode material devoid of sulfur
reveal that DEDSe contributes to a specific capacity of 40−90
mAh g−1 as an electrolyte additive, indicating its involvement
in an electrochemical reaction. Figure 1d depicts the battery’s
cycle performance after accounting for the capacity provided
by 2.0 wt % DEDSe at 0.5 C. The initial specific discharge
capacity of the 2.0 wt % DEDSe battery is 1102 mAh g−1, with
the specific capacity remaining at 854.4 mAh g−1 after 100
cycles, significantly higher than the 658.7 mAh g−1 specific
capacity without DEDSe. Its capacity retention rate of up to
77.53% suggests that the introduction of the DEDSe additive
significantly enhances the cycling performance of the batteries.
It is speculated that the induction effect of the selenium bond
in the DEDSe additive on Li2S reduces the oxidation
overpotential of Li2S, accelerates oxidation kinetics, and
mitigates the irreversible deposition of Li2S, thus reducing
the loss of active substance.
Figure 1e illustrates the rate performance comparison of Li−
S batteries with conventional electrolyte and 2.0 wt % DEDSe
additive at 0.1−1 C. In the conventional electrolyte, after 10
cycles, the specific capacities at 0.1 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C are 864.1,
746.9, 718.6, and 674.4 mAh g−1, respectively. Conversely, for
the battery containing 2.0 wt % DEDSe, the specific current
capacities are 1315.3, 1115.3, 940.4, and 780.9 mAh g−1,
respectively. Upon returning to a discharge rate of 0.2 C, the
capacity of the DEDSe-containing battery reaches 1046.6 mAh
g−1, comparable to the maximum capacity of 1115.3 mAh g−1
at the initial 0.2 C. Furthermore, Figure 1g demonstrates that
the cycle curve exhibits minimal fluctuation and a consistently
smooth discharge curve across all current densities. The cyclic
performance of the battery under a high current density was
further assessed to validate the practical utility of the electrode
material. Initially, a precycling procedure was conducted using
a low current of 0.2 C for 3 cycles to ensure full electrolyte
saturation and activation of the active material. Subsequently,
the battery was subjected to testing at a high current of 1 C,
and the cyclic performance is illustrated in Figure 1f. The
inclusion of 2.0 wt % DEDSe in the batteries resulted in
notable specific capacity and enhanced cycle performance
under high current conditions. In comparison, the conven-
tional electrolyte exhibited an initial discharge capacity of
814.4 mAh g−1, which decreased to 511.7 mAh g−1 after 100
cycles, with a retention rate of only 62.8%. Conversely, the 2.0
wt % DEDSe battery demonstrated an initial specific discharge
capacity of 1257.9 mAh g−1, with a capacity of 882.0 mAh g−1
after 100 cycles, achieving a retention rate of 70.1%. The
addition of DEDSe significantly mitigates the irreversible
deposition of electrochemically inert Li2S and reduces the
reaction rate during charging, consequently decreasing the loss
of active substances in each cycle and enhancing the
performance and stability of lithium−sulfur batteries.
Figure 2a,b displays the cyclic voltammograms (CV) of a
Li−S battery using a conventional electrolyte and 2.0 wt %
DEDSe-containing electrolyte. In the cathodic reduction
process, two reduction peaks at 2.30 and 2.03 V correspond
to the reduction of sublimed sulfur to long-chain polysulfides
and the formation of short-chain Li2S. Similarly, the battery
with a conventional electrolyte shows reduction peaks at 2.24
and 1.76 V. In the anodic process, Li2S oxidizes to lithium
polysulfide and then converts to S8, producing peaks at 2.57 V
due to polysulfide conversion. However, with 2.0 wt % DEDSe,
peaks shift to 2.42 V, indicating DEDSe promotes conversion
and reduces reaction polarization. Furthermore, the potential
difference (ΔE) between the oxidation and reduction peaks is
commonly referred to as polarization. Minimal polarization
promotes the electrochemical reaction rate, enhancing the
redox process of sulfur and improving battery performance. A
reduction in the potential difference (ΔE) indicates that the
addition of DEDSe mitigates internal battery polarization,
enhancing electrolysis and improving the reversibility of the
response. The CV curves of the battery with DEDSe addition
closely match those of the battery, indicating favorable
electrochemical reversibility. In addition, the changes of
DEDSe in the charge and discharge processes are also
explored. The CV test of the sulfur-free electrode with 2.0
wt % DEDSe is presented in Figure 2c, and the reduction peak
at 2.28 V suggests that DEDSe molecules attract 2e−and 2Li+.
Another reduction peak at 1.72 V may result from the
decomposition of LiNO3, which diminishes gradually with an
increasing number of cycles. During the anodic scan, only an
oxidation peak at 2.31 V is observed, indicating the restoration
of DEDSe.

To explore the “inductive effect” between DEDSe and Li2S,
we employed Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)
and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) for in-depth
study as shown in Figure 3. The absence of peaks at around
900 cm−1 indicates the lack of a characteristic DEDSe peak in
DEDSe-Li2S, suggesting the cleavage of the Se−Se bond in
DEDSe-Li2S. XPS results are depicted in Figure 3b,c, revealing
a shift in the Se 3d peak from 55.4 to 55 eV upon mixing
DEDSe with Li2S. This shift suggests that Li2S initiates the
breakage of the Se−Se bond. Additionally, the binding energy
of Li2S is observed at 55.3 eV in Li 1s, whereas that of DEDSe-
Li2S is recorded at 55.7 eV. The increase in binding energy
indicates a decrease in electron density around Li in PDSe-
Li2S, attributed to the Se atom’s attraction to Li, thereby
elongating the Li−S bond. This alteration aids in lowering the
oxidation energy barrier of Li2S, accelerating oxidation kinetics,
minimizing the deposition loss of Li2S, and enhancing the
utilization rate of active materials.
Scanning electron microscopy was employed to investigate
the effect of additives on the lithium anode by examining the
surface morphology of lithium metal after 100 cycles with or
without the addition of DEDSe. In the electrolyte containing
2.0 wt % DEDSe, after 100 cycles, the surface of the lithium
anode remains flat, dense, and uniform, displaying a
continuous, uniform, and highly stacked structure (Figures
4a and S5). Conversely, in the conventional electrolyte, the
surface of the lithium anode becomes markedly rough
compared to the fresh lithium metal (Figure S4), with
noticeable uneven deposition (Figures 4d and S6), and this
roughness arises from the shuttle effect of LiPSs dissolved in
the electrolyte and reaction with the lithium anode, resulting in
the formation of insoluble Li2S2 and Li2S.
The observation of sulfur on the anode cross-section showed
that the erosion depth of sulfur on the anode in the DEDSe
electrolyte was significantly reduced compared with the
conventional electrolyte (Figure 4b, c, e, f). For a more
accurate analysis of lithium metal erosion caused by sulfur, the
anode is sectioned for linear scanning EDS, as illustrated in
Figure 4g, j. It is evident that the section of the cathode with
added DEDSe appears flatter, while that of the anode with a
conventional electrolyte is rough and porous. A linear scan of
the cross-section for sulfur elements, depicted in Figure 4h, k,
reveals that the anode with added DEDSe experiences erosion
of less than 100 μm, whereas the anode with a conventional
electrolyte endures erosion significantly deeper (approximately
200 μm). Moreover, XPS analysis was conducted to examine

163.2−160.5 eV, suggesting a reduction in Li2S2 and Li2S
deposition on the lithium anode surface. This may be due to

In order to illustrate the advantages of DEDSe in improving
the redox kinetics of sulfur, we utilized established techniques
for precise separation and quantitative detection of sulfur-
containing components to analyze the sulfur status and content
in batteries under different conditions,44 as depicted in Figure
5a−c. After the initial discharge, the DEDSe-containing battery
exhibited a significantly higher Li2S content (84.74%)
compared to that of the conventional electrolyte (71.12%),
while the LiPSs content was notably lower (13.35% versus
26.34%). The higher Li2S content and lower LiPSs content
suggest that DEDSe can expedite the sulfur reduction process.
Figure 5b illustrates the distribution of Li2S in the discharged
state, predominantly deposited at the cathode with minimal
deposition at nonconductive sites such as the separator and cell
shell. Upon charging, the Li2S content in the DEDSe-
containing battery decreased to only 0.55%, significantly
lower than that in the ordinary electrolyte battery (1.7%),
with lower Li2S content observed on each component
compared to the ordinary electrolyte. The low Li2S content
after charging indicates that DEDSe is conducive to the
oxidation process of Li2S. These alterations in sulfur-
containing components indicate that the inclusion of DEDSe
expedites the redox kinetics of sulfur, and by reducing the
oxidation barrier of Li2S, DEDSe diminishes the Li2S content
in the charged battery, thereby minimizing active substance
loss.
To bridge the gap between laboratory research and
industrial applications and assess the viability of integrating
DEDSe additives into the industrial production of Li−S
batteries, we examined their impact on the cyclic performance
of pouch cells. The pouch cells incorporating DEDSe featuring
high sulfur loading (5 mg cm−2) and a low electrolyte-sulfur
ratio (E/S = 8 μL mg−1) as shown in Figure 5d, exhibited
outstanding cycling performance compared to normal batteries
as illustrated in Figure 5e. The initial specific capacity of the
pouch cells with 2.0 wt % DEDSe additive was 1314.2 mAh
g−1; after 34 cycles, the specific capacity reached 992.5 mAh
g−1, with a capacity retention rate of 75.5%. In comparison, the
initial specific capacity of the conventional electrolyte pouch
cells stood at 1075.2 mAh g−1, which decreased to 562.7 mAh
g−1 after 34 cycles, with a retention rate of only 50.3%.
Additionally, pouch cells containing 2.0 wt % DEDSe additives
showed lower initial charge potentials compared to those with
conventional electrolytes. Moreover, the discharge platform of
the DEDSe battery remained relatively consistent throughout
the cycle, while the polarization of the conventional battery
gradually increased, resulting in a decrease in discharge voltage
(Figures 5f and S7). These findings are consistent with button
battery results, re-affirming that DEDSe additives facilitate the
oxidation of the discharge product Li2S in lithium−sulfur
batteries, thereby improving the reaction kinetics and cyclic
stability.
Figure S8 shows the postcycling cathode surface with
conventional electrolyte demonstrating extensive cracking,
with unoxidized Li2S clearly visible under magnification.
Conversely, the cathode surface of the battery with the
addition of DEDSe exhibits significantly fewer cracks,
indicative of a more uniform and complete reaction, with no
residual Li2S. The inclusion of DEDSe reduces the oxidation
energy barrier of Li2S, minimizes inactive sulfur deposition,
and improves battery performance. In batteries utilizing
conventional electrolytes, prolonged accumulation and in-
advertent oxidation of Li2S lead to considerable volume
changes, resulting in surface cracks. These cracks tend to
propagate during subsequent cycling, compromising the
battery’s reaction uniformity and shortening its lifespan.
Therefore, the incorporation of DEDSe markedly enhances
the battery’s performance and longevity.
Overall, the incorporation of DEDSe significantly enhances
the battery oxidation kinetics by inducing interactions with
Li2S, thereby reducing the irreversible deposition of Li2S
(Figure 6). Consequently, this improvement enhances the
utilization rates of active materials, reduces battery polar-
ization, and mitigates lithium metal erosion, resulting in
remarkable performance. DEDSe emerges as a pivotal additive
for enhancing the performance of Li−S batteries by leveraging
selenium-containing compounds.
4. CONCLUSIONS
This study proposes the utilization of DEDSe as an electrolyte
additive in Li−S batteries to mitigate irreversible deposition
and enhance the reactivity of the final discharge product Li2S.
The interaction between DEDSe and Li2S results in the
elongation of the Li−S bond in Li2S and the breakage of the
Se−Se bond in DEDSe, thereby reducing the oxidation energy
barrier of Li2S. Consequently, the DEDSe additive accelerates
the oxidation kinetics of Li2S, facilitates the dissolution of
insulating products deposited on the cathode surface, mitigates
the erosion of lithium metal, minimizes polarization, and
improves electrode stability. Benefiting from these advantages,
the pouch cell containing 2.0 wt % DEDSe exhibits an initial
capacity of 1314.2 mAhg−1 and maintains 992.5 mAhg−1 after
34 cycles at 0.1 C, demonstrating outstanding electrochemical
performance. We propose a method to reduce the oxidation
energy barrier of Li2S by incorporating DEDSe into the
electrolyte, thereby enhancing the utilization of active materials
and improving the cycle stability. This study aims to broaden
researchers’ perspectives on optimizing Li−S battery perform-
ance to achieve high specific energy and longevity.
