A B S T R A C T  
Numerous efforts can improve reversible capacity and long cycling stability for lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries, 
however, challenges remain in achieving high sulfur utilization and suppressing the shuttle effect, especially for 
cells with a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio. In this research, we utilize tetrathiafulvalene (TTF) as multi­
functional catalyst for high-performance Li–S batteries through optimizing electrolyte chemistry for the first 
time. With TTF additive in electrolyte, it acts as π-electron donor molecule to improve the electron transport of 
Li2Sx due to the van der Waals interaction, and promote LiNO3 to Li3N to form highly conductive passivation 
layer on lithium anode (Li-anode). Furthermore, TTF functions as a catalyst to improve the redox kinetics of Li2Sx 
thus slowing down the “shuttle effect”, improve the utilization of sulfur, and protect the Li-anode. As a result, the 
cell with TTF exhibits excellent performances with high discharge capacity (1359 mAh g−1 at 0.1C), long-term 
cycling stability (509 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at 0.5C), and excellent cycling performance even at low E/S ratio 
(5.5 μL mg−1) (initial discharge capacity of 1129 mAh g−1 with a capacity decay rate of 0.20% per cycle at 0.1C 
for 200 cycles).   
1. Introduction 
Li–S batteries are considered as a promising next-generation energy 
storage candidate and intensively investigated to meet the needs of 
higher energy applications due to sulfur’s high theoretical energy den­
sity (2600 Wh kg−1), low cost (~$100 ton−1), natural abundance, and 
non-toxicity [1–3]. Despite its outstanding advantages, the real appli­
cation of Li–S batteries is still limited by their performance drawbacks 
such as low practical energy density, low Coulombic efficiency, and fast 
capacity degradation. These challenges originate from the poor con­
ductivity of sulfur and their discharge products (Li2S/Li2S2), the sulfur 
loss, serious corrosion of Li-anode caused by the “shuttle effect” of 
lithium polysulfide (LiPSs), and the irreversible deposition of Li2S [4–9]. 
Many ingenious methods have been proposed to overcome these 
challenges, among them, advanced carbon materials with hollow porous 
structures as host for sulfur are particularly extensive [7–13]. Carbon

essential problem, especially for the passivation of redox-active sites and 
the shuttle effect of LiPSs upon long-term cycling with low E/S ratios 
[14–19]. Therefore, additional approaches have emerged to improve 
cycling performance along with the development of advanced sulfur 
cathode materials. The approaches include catalysts utilization [20–23], 
optimizing electrolyte with additives or low polysulfide solubility 
[24–34], polysulfides blocking interlayers or separators [35–39], 
Li-anode protection [40], and solid-state electrolyte [41]. 
Electrolyte plays as a crucial component by affecting the lithium-ion 
transport and conductivity [19]. Additive can stabilize the electro­
de/electrolyte interfaces, reduce the solubility of polysulfide in the 
electrolytes, and protect the Li-anode. Hence, the introduction of suit­
able additives has been considered as a simple and effective way to 
improve the electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries [24–34]. 
Among them, catalysts additive can further improve the performance of 
batteries by catalyzing the conversion of polysulfide thus accelerating 
the electrochemical kinetics of the batteries [32,34]. At the same time, 
some catalysts can improve electron transfer process, which will further 
improve the performance of Li–S batteries [24]. Hence, it is necessary to 
develop a low-cost, highly-efficient, and reliable catalyst as electrolyte 
additive to improve the practical performance of Li–S batteries from 
different procedure. 
TTF as a π-electron donor molecule has been widely used in 
rechargeable lithium battery fields [42–45]. Herein, we utilized TTF as a 
Li–S battery electrolyte additive for the first time in order to achieve 
stable cycling of Li–S batteries at high rate. Combining theoretical and 
experimental design, our results demonstrate that TTF can accelerate 
electron transfer process through van der Waals forces with Li2Sx (x = 1, 
2, 4, 6, 8) and facilitate the oxidation of Li2Sx. Improved capacity 
retention of Li–S batteries over 500 cycles at 0.5C proved that utility of 
TTF after the first cycle is sustained. Importantly, the Li metals are 
effectively protected from passivation in Li–S batteries. As a result, the 
Li–S batteries with a low E/S ratio of 5.5 μL mg−1 still exhibit a capacity 
of 670 mAh g−1 over 200 cycles at 0.1C. This research has offered an 
insight into the development of a multifunctional catalyst additive for 
Li–S batteries.

2.2. Characterization methods 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were step-scan recorded to analyze 
the composition and crystal structure on RigakuDmaX2500 diffrac­
tometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 154.06 p.m.) with steps of 10◦min−1. 
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) data were collected with a TG 
analyzer (Henven T15-114) under an N2 atmosphere to 800 ◦C with a 
heating rate of 10 ◦C min−1 to determine the sulfur content of the ma­
terial. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-5500) coupled 
with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to deter­
mine the morphologies and element analysis of the products. 
2.3. Synthesis of the CMK-3/S cathodes and the electrolytes 
Sulfur and CMK-3 (7:3 by mass) were mixed uniformly and sealed in 
a 50 mL autoclave and heated to 155 ◦C for 20 h to prepare CMK-3/S 
composite material. The weight loss of CMK-3/S determined by TGA is 
69.72%, as shown in Fig. S1 in Supporting Information (SI). CMK-3/S, 
super P, and PVDF were mixed with NMP in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 to 
make a slurry. The slurry was then coated onto an aluminum foil with a 
diameter of 14 mm. After the NMP evaporation at 60 ◦C overnight in a 
vacuum oven, the cathode was thus obtained. The active sulfur loading 
for each cathode is about 2.1 mg cm−2. The LiTFSI and LiNO3 as solutes 
were dried under vacuum at 120 ◦C for 12 h, DOL and DME (1:1 by 
volume) as solvent was dried using activated molecular sieves. 0.1 M 
LiNO3 in 1 M LiTFSI/DOLDME was prepared as a pure electrolyte. TTF 
as functional additive was added in pure electrolyte by different con­
centration (0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 M) to prepare optimized 
electrolyte. 
2.4. Cell assembly and electrochemical tests 
All coin-type (CR2032) cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove 
box (VIGOR, LG1200/750 TS, H2O < 1.0 ppm, O2 < 1.0 ppm). The Li–S 
cells were assembled with a Li foil anode (China Energy Lithium Co., 
Ltd.), one-layer polyethylene (PE) separator (HongTu Separator Co., 
Ltd.) soaked in electrolytes described above, and a cathode mentioned 
above. The Li || Li symmetric cells were assembled with Li foils as both 
the cathode and anode and a single-layer PE separator soaked with 
electrolytes mentioned above. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements 
were performed on a PARSTAT 4000 potentiated at a scanning rate of 
0.1 mV s−1 in a voltage range of 1.6–2.8 V vs. Li+/Li and 2.8–3.8 V vs. 
Li+/Li. The galvanostatic discharge/charge measurements of the 
assembled Li–S cells were conducted on a LAND system (CTA2001A, 
Wuhan land Electronic Co., Ltd.) at a galvanostatic current with cut-off 
voltages of 1.6–2.8 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
measurements were also carried out on the PARSTAT 4000 with a 
voltage amplitude of 5 mV in the frequency range 10−1-105 Hz.

2.6. Computational details 
The essential reason that TTF promotes the electrochemical reaction 
is revealed by density functional theory (DFT) calculation. All the 
computations were performed with the Gaussian 09 program package. 
Full ground state geometry optimizations of Li2Sx compounds before and 
after binding to TTF were carried out at the wb97xd/6-311+g(d,p) level. 
3. Results and discussion 
The effect of TTF on Li2Sx was systematically investigated by DFT 
calculation, in order to understand the role of TTF in the charge/ 
discharge reaction process of Li–S batteries. No covalent bond is formed 
between TTF and Li2Sx, indicating that the interaction between them is 
noncovalent. Meanwhile, low-gradient isosurfaces (RDG) (s = 0.5 a.u.) 
for the compounds were plotted in Fig. 1, illustrating, strong attraction, 
van der Waals interaction, and steric hindrance. As shown in Fig. 1, color 
images are the gradient isosurfaces (RDG) of the compounds, which are 
used to determine the strength of the interactions depending on whether 
there are green areas between TTF and Li2Sx. It is found that the non­
covalent interaction regions of all compounds are filled as green or light 
brown, which confirms an overwhelming preponderance of the van der 
Waals interactions. Although the presence of TTF does not directly 
participate in the transformation reaction of Li2Sx, the existence of this 
interaction relationship allows TTF to improve the electron transport of 
Li2Sx [46]. 
The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoc­
cupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are associated with the oxidation 
process and reduction process, respectively. Therefore, energy levels of 
the HOMO and the LUMO, as well as the energy difference (Egap) be­
tween HOMO and LUMO of all compounds were obtained at wb97xd/6- 
311+g(d,p) level, shown in Fig. 2. The Egap values of all the Li2Sx (Li2Sx 
with TTF, x = 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8) compounds are in the range of 6.20–8.05 
eV. Interestingly, the Egap values of TTF-Li2Sx are smaller than that of the 
corresponding Li2Sx, because TTF increases the HOMO energy levels of 
TTF-Li2Sx. Therefore, TTF-Li2Sx can accept electrons as an oxidant more 
easily. Additionally, it can be found that the HOMO of TTF-Li2Sx (x = 1 
and 2) compounds localized mostly on the Li2Sx units (Fig. 2), showing 
that the oxidation of both compounds takes place at the Li2Sx units. For 
TTF-Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, and 8) compounds, the HOMOs are delocalized over 
the TTF units, indicating that the introduction of TTF has a significant 
regulating effect on the oxidation ability of Li2Sx units. 
Li || Li cells have been constructed with electrolytes containing TTF 
or without TTF to explore the effect of TTF as an additive on electrolyte 
for Li–S batteries. At a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 1 
mAh cm−2, the Li || Li cell with TTF presents stable cycles for more than 
450 h, and the overpotential is rather small (around 15 mV) (Fig. S2a in 
SI). In contrast, the Li || Li cell without TTF exhibits a large polarization 
voltage and becomes short-circuit after 250 h (Fig. S2a in SI). This be­
comes more obvious when Li2S6 is added in the Li || Li cells assembly 
process. As shown in Fig. S2b in SI, the Li || Li cell without TTF only 
cycles around 150 h but the cell with TTF can cycle more than 400 h. It 
can be concluded that the addition of TTF is favorable to reduce and 
suppress the interfacial reaction between Li-anode and electrolyte. 
The electrochemical performance of Li–S cells assembled with elec­
trolytes containing TTF or without TTF to verify the above phenomenon. 
Specific capacity of the cells with different concentrations of TTF addi­
tive were collected by the galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) test 
(Fig. S3 in SI) to optimize the amount of TTF additive. It shows that the 
cell with 0.025 M of TTF additive cycled at 0.1C rate gave the maximum 
initial discharge specific capacity (1359 mAh g−1). In the following 
discussions, all data are collected with electrolyte with 0.025 M TTF 
added (abbreviated as “cell with TTF”), and the cell without TTF as a 
comparison. 
Fig. 3a displays the initial CV curves of the Li–S cells with electrolytes 
containing TTF and without TTF. Both cells exhibit typical characteristic 
redox peaks of Li–S batteries. However, oxidation peaks of the cell with 
TTF (2.25 and 2.38 V) are lower than the cell without TTF (2.35 and 
2.45 V), and the reduction peaks of the cell with TTF (2.02 and 2.33 V) 
are higher than the cell without TTF (2.01 and 2.26 V). Meanwhile, the 
cell with TTF has a higher peak current. The positive shift in the 
reduction peaks and negative shift in the oxidation peaks of the cell with 
TTF together with higher peak current are evidence of the improvement 
of the polysulfide redox kinetics by TTF, in accordance with the theo­
retical calculation results. Furthermore, the CV curves overlapped well 
during subsequent cycles (Fig. S4b in SI), demonstrating that the cell 
with TTF exhibits a good reversibility of the electrochemical reaction. 
None of the characteristic peaks of TTF are presented in Fig. 3a. In fact, 
its characteristic peaks at 3.21 and 3.62 V are much higher than the 
operating voltage of the Li–S battery (Fig. S4c in SI). Moreover, as 
described by the above theoretical calculation results, TTF does not 
directly participate in the transformation reaction of Li2Sx. 
However, the TTF added in electrolyte significantly impacts elec­
trochemical performance: the initial GCD curves of the Li–S cells 
without and with TTF cycled at 0.1C rate, as presented in Fig. 3b, the 
initial discharge specific capacity of the cell with TTF is 1359 mAh g−1, 
larger than the cell without TTF (1222 mAh g−1). The utilization of 
active sulfur for the cell with TTF is 80.9%, which is higher than that of

To visually demonstrate the positive effects of the TTF on the sup­
pression of the irreversible sediment sedimentation, the electrodes were 
obtained by disconnecting the cells after 100 cycles (the cells are fully 
charged). Through SEM measurement, the surface of the cathode and Li- 
anode in two different cells are remarkably different. Compared with the 
pristine cathode (Fig. 5a), small particles are deposited on the surface of 
the cathode materials in the cell without TTF after 100 cycles (Fig. 5b), 
and the deposition of sulfur is not uniform. However, as shown in 
Fig. 5c, there is almost no small particle deposition on the surface of the 
cathode materials of the cell with TTF, and the sulfur deposition is 
uniform. The XRD spectra of the cathode materials for the cells without 
and with TTF are shown in Fig. S7 in SI. The characteristic peaks at 44.8 
and 53.2◦, which belongs to the Li2S (JCPDS No.77-2145) appears on the 
XRD spectra of the cathode materials of the cells with and without TTF. 
No substance other than sulfur and Li2S are detected. Thus, the main 
component of CEI formed on the surface of the cathode material is Li2S 
[51,52]. TTF can effectively inhibit Li2S deposits on the surface of the 
cathode by promoting the oxidation reaction of Li2S and this is helpful to 
improve the utilization rate of active sulfur and Li–S battery cycle life. 
Usually, the pristine Li-anode surface presents relatively flat and 
smooth morphology (Fig. 5d). After 100 cycles in the Li–S cell without 
TTF (Fig. 5e), the surface of the Li-anode undergoes heavy corrosion and 
the representative “mosslike” lithium was observed on the surface of the 
Li-anode [53,54]. In addition to the uneven electrochemical dis­
solution/deposition process of the lithium anode and the consumption 
of electrolytes, surface corrosion is more severe due to the erosion of 
LiPSs. In comparison, as shown in Fig. 5f, the Li-anode in the Li–S cell 
with TTF exhibits a relatively smooth surface indicating less erosion 
effect by LiPSs, which is confirmed by Li||Li cell test result. TTF plays a 
positive role in maintaining the surface integrity of Li-anode by accel­
erating the conversion of LiPSs to slow down corrosion, which improve 
the lifespan of Li–S batteries under practical conditions.

characterized by XPS to analyze the composition of SEI on the surface of

The results have shown that the introduction of TTF reduces the 
deposition of Li2S on the surface of the electrode and promotes the 
construction of a high ion conductive SEI. A possible modified mecha­
nism of TTF in a Li–S cell during cycling is proposed by the scheme 
(Fig. 7). During cycling, Li2S is continuously formed and deposited on 
the surface of the cathode. Meanwhile, the “shuttle effect” of LiPSs 
causes the Li-anode corrosion and formation of Li2S on the surface, 
which can seriously affect the cycling performance of Li–S batteries. The 
oxidation reaction of Li2S is promoted on the cathode side by TTF, thus 
reducing the deposition of the irreversible Li2S during the cycling of the 
Li–S cells with TTF. In contrast, the “shuttle effect” of long-chain LiPSs 
can be relatively slowed down by accelerating the conversion rate of 
LiPSs. And under the promotion of TTF, LiNO3 is more converted into 
Li3N to form SEI with high ion conductivity. In addition, the improve­
ment of charge transfer brought by TTF can improve the utilization rate 
of sulfur. This process can effectively protect Li-anode from corrosion 
and reduce the formation of Li2S on the Li-anode surface. Therefore, the 
regulating effect of TTF for Li2Sx is key to improving cell performance. 
TTF can not only improve the stability of the electrode interface but 
also effectively promote electron transfer during cycling. Therefore, the 
cell still exhibits an excellent performance even at a low E/S ratio (5.5 
μL mg−1) when TTF as a multi-functional additive is added to the elec­
trolyte. As shown in Fig. S9 in SI, the cell with TTF demonstrates a stable 
cycling performance and a reversible capacity of 670 mAh g−1 after 200 
cycles at 0.1C, with a smaller capacity decay rate of 0.20% per cycle than 
that without TTF (0.29%). It can be attributed to the characteristic 
(electron conductor) of TTF and the regulation ability for the oxidation 
ability of Li2Sx. 
4. Conclusions 
Regulating Li2Sx conversion is critical to improve the performance of 
Li–S batteries under real application condition. In this research, we 
optimized the normal electrolyte with TTF additive through facilitating 
the electron conduction, stabilizing electrode/electrolyte interface, and 
improving lithium-ion transport, as well as reducing the irreversible 
deposition of Li2S. Our results illustrated that the oxidation ability of 
Li2Sx can be regulated by introducing TTF. Both electrodes are protected 
simultaneously, as opposed to traditional method by solving problems in 
only one electrode. The irreversible Li2S deposition on the surface of the 
cathode decreases after cycling, thus helping to improve the utilization 
rate of active S and to increase the electrical conductivity of the cathode. 
Last but not least, the side reaction of Li-anode with LiPSs is greatly 
suppressed by TTF additive, which reduces electrolyte depletion during 
cycling. A high-performance Li–S battery is realized even at a low E/S 
ratio of 5.5 μL mg−1 (670 mAh g−1 over 200 cycles at 0.1C, as opposed to 
550 mAh g−1 over 100 cycles for the normal electrolyte). This novel 
research is expected to stimulate the wider application of Li–S batteries 
in the future.
