First-Principles Investigations to Evaluate NiCl2/NiF2 as
Cycle-Catalyzed Electrolyte Additives to Improve Li–S
Batteries’ Performance
Bingqian Wang, Shuhan Lu, Panyu Zhang, Xiaoli Jiang, Xinxin Zhao, LiLi Wang,
ZhiXiang Yin, and Jianbao Wu*
1. Introduction
The energy crisis has forced people to focus on renewable energy
such as solar energy and wind energy. In this case, high-energy
density rechargeable batteries have attracted widespread atten-
tion from academia as an efﬁcient energy storage device.[1–3]
Lithium-ion batteries have been widely used in portable equip-
ment, electric vehicles, and other energy storage equipment.
However, as the actual energy density of lithium-ion batteries
gradually approaches its theoretical energy density, the demand
for higher energy density drives people to focus on lithium-sulfur
batteries (LSB).[4–6] Generally, elemental sulfur is used as the
positive electrode of LSB, and lithium metal sheet is used as
the negative electrode. The reaction mechanism of LSB is differ-
ent from that of lithium ion battery, but it is electrochemical
mechanism. Compared with the actual energy density of
600 Whkg1 of lithium-ion batteries, the theoretical speciﬁc
capacity of LSB up to 2600 mAhg1 undoubtedly has great advan-
tages. In addition, the sulfur cathode used by LSB has the
advantages of rich production, environ-
mental protection, and low toxicity, which
is also one of the reasons why it has
become the most promising secondary bat-
tery system.[7]

improves the electron transport of Li2Sx through van der
Waals interaction, and promotes the formation of a highly con-
ductive passivating layer from LiNO3 to Li3N on the lithium
anode (lithium anode), which protects the lithium anode. Due
to the slow conversion rate of soluble LiPSs to insoluble Li2S
or sulfur, simple adsorption of polysulﬁde is difﬁcult to funda-

functions of electrolyte additives are also being widely studied.

The potential application of two electrolyte additives, NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME, in
lithium–sulfur batteries is investigated by density functional theory calculations. The
results show that both electrolyte additives can effectively adsorb soluble lithium
polysulﬁde (LiPSs) in the electrolyte. With the progress of the charging–discharge
reaction, NiCl2 and NiF2 separate from Li2Sn when it is converted to S8 and
recombine with 1,2-dimethoxy-ethane (DME) to achieve the purpose of cyclic
adsorption of polysulﬁde in the electrolyte. At the same time, NiF2 and NiCl2 also
have a catalytic effect in the cycling process. When adsorbed with Li2Sn, the bond
length of the Li–S bond in Li2Sn is elongated, which causes the reduction of the Li2Sn
activation energy, making it easier to be oxidized, and accelerating the conversion
speed of Li2Sn and reducing Li2S, Li2S2 deposition on the surface of the electrode.
Therefore, NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME are ideal dual-function electrolyte additives.

At present, there have been a lot of studies on electrolyte addi-
tives, but there was still a lack of research on the reaction mech-
anism and action mechanism of electrolyte additives in the
battery environment. More theoretical and experimental studies
were needed to improve the interaction between electrolyte addi-
tives and polysulﬁde as well as its catalytic effect. Based on the
earlier analysis, we decided to choose as the research target
the product of combining the synthesized metalide NiCl2 with
the organic solvent DME. Through theoretical calculation and
analysis, the performance of NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME in
improving the shuttle effect and accelerating the conversion of
polysulﬁde in LSB was compared.
In this article, the stable conﬁgurations of Li2Sn (n = 1, 2, 4,
6, 8) molecules, S8 molecules, and electrolyte additives NiCl2-
DME and NiF2-DME were calculated and analyzed using density
functional theory. The calculation results showed that Li2Sn
(n = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) molecules can be effectively adsorbed by
NiCl2 and NiF2 molecules in the electrolyte. The adsorption of
Li2Sn (n = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) was accompanied by separation of
NiCl2 molecules or NiF2 molecules from DME molecules. The
adsorption of NiCl2 molecules or NiF2 molecules continued with
the conversion of polysulﬁde to pure sulfur and NiCl2 molecules
or NiF2 molecules dissociated from S8 and recombine with DME
at the ﬁnal stage. This process constitutes a complete closed loop
of cyclic adsorption of LiPSs, which can inhibit the shuttle effect.
The results also revealed that the two electrolyte additives have
catalytic effects. After adsorption of Li2Sn (n = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) by
two electrolyte additives NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME, Li–S bond
lengths changed signiﬁcantly and regularly. The highest occupied
molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LOMO) levels of Li2Sn were calculated before and after
the reaction with NiCl2 or NiF2 molecules. The results showed that
these two additives had an “induction effect” with polysulﬁde, and
it promoted the oxidation of polysulﬁde and insoluble and insu-
lating discharge products Li2S, thus avoiding a series of serious
problems of LSB. In this study, two kinds of bifunctional electro-
lyte additives combining metal compounds with organic solvents
were proposed, which provided a new solution for LSB electrolyte
additives in terms of adsorption of polysulﬁde and catalysis.
2. Computational Methods
All the calculations of Gibbs free energies presented in this arti-
cle were performed using the Gaussian 16 code. We used the
B3LYP hybrid functional along with the 6–31 þ G* basis set
for the C/H/O/Cl/F and LALN2DZ effective core potentials
(ECPs) for the Ni atom for all of the calculations, which were
widely used to calculate the organo-sulﬁdes.[23] The optimized
structures were thermally stable without negative frequency.
The solvation model based on density (PCM)[25] was performed
to describe the solvent environment of 1,2-dimethoxythane
(DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL). The solvation effect of DME/
DOL (1:1, v/v) was widely used in the theoretical and experimen-
tal research of batteries.[26] In addition, the static permittivity was
set to ε = 7.07. In order to verify the stability of Li2Sn after bind-
ing with NiCl2 and NiF2 and the possibility of further conversion,
“alloy-like” diagram[27,28] was used to analyzed the stability and
possibility. The Gibbs free energy difference (ΔG) was used to
analyze the relative stability and possibility of further reactions
of NiCl2-Li2Sn and NiF2-Li2Sn, the formation energies were

The “alloy-like” diagram[27,28] was used to analyze the thermody-
namic stability of the overall structure of the two catalysts after
reacting with Li2Sn or S8. If a compound is above the convex
envelope, it will be unstable and decompose into two nearby com-
pounds. As shown in Figure 2 that the structures from Li2S to
Li2S8 are thermodynamically stable, in keeping with the stability
of polysulﬁde clusters. The thermodynamic stability of Li2Sn-
NiCl2 and Li2Sn-NiF2 is also consistent with Li2Sn. Another
advantage of the “alloy-like” diagram is to determine whether
the reaction can continue. As the content of S increases, the for-

3.3. Binding of NiF2 and NiCl2 to Li2Sn
During the discharge of a lithium-sulfur battery, Li2Sn is the
result of lithium on the sulfur cathode. The optimized geometry
and bond length of Li2Sn were demonstrated in our previous
studies. The interaction between NiCl2/NiF2 and S8/Li2Sn
(n = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) was investigated in order to elucidate the bind-
ing behavior of NiCl2/NiF2 with efﬁcient homogeneous catalysis.
Typically, a heterogeneous catalyst that interacts with the elec-
trode surface, for example, a transition metal dihydride (TMD)
or a monatomic catalyst (SAC)-based material,[34] interacts with
Li2Sn in one of two binding conﬁgurations. Li binds to nonme-
tallic atoms, while transition metal atoms on the base covalently
bind to sulfur in LiPSs. In transition metal-based SAC, polysul-
ﬁdes regulate the LiPSs adsorption behavior and electrocatalysis
by adsorbing S by TM (where S comes from LiPSs). In order to
ﬁnd the most favorable binding conﬁguration of NiCl2/NiF2
homogeneous catalyst, we ﬁrst locate Li2Sn near the Ni atom
in NiCl2/NiF2. The most stable geometry after relaxation is
shown in Figure 1. The Li2Sn anchored to the NiCl2/NiF2
through covalent bond formation via a single NiS bond exhibits
stronger interactions compared with the weak van der Waals
interaction between S8 and the NiCl2/NiF2. The Gibbs free ener-
gies of the two organometallic nickel salts and S8/Li2Sn clusters
were calculated to compare the adsorption effects of the two
organometallic nickel salts on S8/Li2Sn clusters and their cyclic
abilities. The free energy between S8/Li2Sn and NiCl2/NiF2 rep-
resents the exothermic adsorption process, as shown in Figure 3.
The exothermic binding between S8/Li2Sn and NiCl2/NiF2 indi-
cates a good binding interaction between them. As shown in
Figure 1, only S interacts with Ni in the S8 cluster, and the defor-
mation of S8 is very small. The calculated Gibbs free energy of S8
is 0.40 and 0.48 eV, respectively, indicating that the interaction
with NiCl2/NiF2 is weak. On the contrary, with the addition of
Li atom, the free energy of soluble long-chain Li2Sn (such as
Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4) is 2.28/2.91, 2.20/2.87, 2.35/2.99 eV after
binding with NiCl2/NiF2 via Ni-S bond. Compared with
soluble polysulﬁdes, insoluble Li2S2 (2.92/3.24 eV) and Li2S

3.5. Catalysis of Li2Sn by NiF2 and NiCl2
The insolubility and insulation of the discharge products
(Li2S, Li2S2) lead to a series of serious problems such as serious
polarization, low utilization of active materials, low speciﬁc
capacity, and poor cycle stability of LSB.[37] Therefore, the
catalytic utility of these two electrolyte additives in the battery
environment was also investigated after the ability to adsorb free
Li2Sn in the electrolyte was discovered. The length of Li–S bond
was calculated after Li2Sn was bound to NiCl2 and NiF2, respec-
tively. The acceleration of sulfur reduction during discharge is
evident from our previous calculations. This observation sug-
gests that the NiCl2/NiF2 catalyst studied can effectively acceler-
ate the reduction of soluble high-order polysulﬁdes to insoluble
sulﬁdes (Li2S2 and Li2S). The length of the Li–S bond of Li2Sn
combined with NiCl2 and NiF2, respectively, was calculated.
As shown in Figure 6, the Li–S bond length of Li2Sn increased
signiﬁcantly after binding to NiCl2 and NiF2. After comparison, it
was found that the length of Li–S bond growth after Li2Sn bind-
ing to NiF2 was signiﬁcantly longer than that after Li2Sn binding
to NiCl2. The Li–S bond length of Li2S increased from 2.25 to
2.40 Å after the reaction with NiF2. Obviously, the Li–S bond
length of Li2Sn has a critical change after the reaction with elec-
trolyte additives. This phenomenon is due to the fact that Cl and
F attract Li in Li2Sn with the addition of NiCl2 and NiF2, and this
phenomenon is deﬁned as the “induction effect”.[38,39] Due to the
induction effect, the Li–S bond in Li2Sn is elongated, which leads
to the reduction of Li2Sn activation energy and promotes the oxi-
dation of Li2Sn in lithium metal batteries. The addition of these
two electrolyte additives not only accelerates the conversion rate
of Li2Sn but also increases the conversion rate of insoluble dis-
charge products such as Li2S and Li2S2, so that their deposition
on the electrode surface is reduced. In terms of the effect on
bond length, NiF2 electrolyte additive is better than NiCl2 elec-
trolyte additive.

accelerate the conversion of Li2Sn, including Li2S and Li2S2,
reduce its deposition on the conductive skeleton and electrode
surface, and avoid the extreme loss of capacity after the addition
of electrolyte. The LUMO and HOMO energy levels of Li2Sn
(n = 1,2,4,6,8) and NiCl2 and NiF2 were calculated, respectively.
As shown in Figure 7, after the original energy bandgap of Li2Sn
reacts with these two organometallic nickel salts, the LUMO
level decreases signiﬁcantly, while the HOMO level does not.

products such as Li2S and Li2S2 on the electrode surface and in
the battery skeleton. This phenomenon supports the “induction
effect” and proves that the two organometallic nickel salts do
have catalytic effects to promote the deposition of Li2S and
Li2S2 on the conductive skeleton and electrode surface.
4. Conclusions
To conclude, two organometallic nickel salt additives have been
thoroughly studied in terms of inhibition of shuttle effect and
catalysis by density functional theory calculation. The simulation
results showed that both NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME can effec-
tively adsorb Li2Sn, and the free energy of NiCl2 and NiF2 and
Li2Sn can reach 2.99 to 4.63 and 4.08 to 5.71 eV, respectively.
By comparing the free energy between NiCl2, NiF2, and DME,
it was found that this additive can play the role of cyclic adsorp-
tion of Li2Sn in the battery. Through Gibbs free energy calcula-
tion and “alloy-like” graph analysis, we found that NiCl2, NiF2,
and Li2Sn can exist relatively stable and did not affect the next
reaction of Li2Sn. The catalytic effects of these two additives were
also investigated. The Li–S bond length of Li2Sn was compared
with that of NiCl2-Li2Sn and NiF2-Li2Sn, and it was found that an
“induction effect” would occur between the additive and Li2Sn.
The Li–S bond length of Li2Sn was compared with that of
NiCl2-Li2Sn and NiF2-Li2Sn, and it was found that an “induction
effect” would occur between the additive and Li2Sn. The Li–S
bond length would be elongated due to the addition of additives,
and the growth of Li–S bond would lead to the reduction of Li2Sn
activation energy. The oxidation of Li2Sn in lithium metal batter-
ies was accelerated, and the deposition of Li2Sn on the electrode
surface was ﬁnally reduced. The calculation results showed that
the HOMO-LUMO difference of Li2Sn decreases to different
degrees with the reaction with NiCl2 and NiF2, which also sup-
ports the catalytic effect of the two electrolytes. Theoretical anal-
ysis showed that NiCl2-DME and NiF2-DME can recycle stable
adsorbed Li2Sn in electrolyte, accelerate the oxidation of adsorbed
Li2Sn, reduce the deposition of Li2Sn on the electrode surface, and
thus improve the cycling performance of LSB. The shuttle effect
caused by Li2Sn in LSB has been plaguing researchers in LSB. We
hope that this work will lead to further experimental research,
which will hopefully make organometallic electrolyte additives
a backup electrolyte additive to solve this problem.
