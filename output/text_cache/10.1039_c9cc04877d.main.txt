Trace ethanol as an eﬃcient electrolyte additive
to reduce the activation voltage of the Li2S
cathode in lithium-ion–sulfur batteries†
Xin Liang,a Jufeng Yun,a Kun Xu,a Pengcheng Shi,a Yi Sun,a Chunhua Chen
b and
Hongfa Xiang
*a
Trace ethanol is applied as a cheap and eﬀective electrolyte additive
for reducing the activation voltage of Li2S cathodes in lithium-
ion–sulfur batteries. Because Li2S can dissolve in ethanol, the solid–
solid phase reaction in the first stage of the activation process
converts into a liquid–solid phase reaction, which expedites the
transport of electrons and lithium ions and reduces the activation
voltage of Li2S. This strategy can be extended to other solvents that
can dissolve Li2S.
Rechargeable batteries play important roles in the field of new
energy applications.1–7 Among them, lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
have attracted wide attention recently, because of the high
theoretical specific capacity (1675 mA h g1), environmental
friendliness and low price of the sulfur cathode.8–13 However,
the volume expansion of sulfur (up to 80%) during the charge
and discharge process and the use of a metallic lithium (Li)
anode can cause serious safety problems, which has prevented
their practical application over the past 40 years.14,15
Li-ion–S batteries with Li2S as the cathode can retain the main
advantages of Li–S batteries, but can avoid several disadvantages
of the typical Li–S batteries with a S cathode. Li2S can provide a
theoretical capacity of 1166 mA h g1 and can be coupled with Li
metal-free anode materials, such as silicon, graphite, and so on.
Therefore, it can avoid the safety problems caused by Li dendrite
growth in Li–S batteries with a S cathode.16 Furthermore, Li2S
can be handled at high temperatures due to its high melting
point (B938 1C). Besides, Li2S has a larger volume than S with
the same weight, which enables much more stable surface
modification of Li2S. Nevertheless, the Li2S cathode still suffers
from several problems to be overcome. Like the S cathode, Li2S
also has to face the problems of poor electronic conductivity and
the ‘‘shuttle effect’’.17–22 Moreover, compared to elemental S,
Li2S has a defining disadvantage which is its electrochemical
inactivity.1 Li2S, as the fully lithiated state, needs a high voltage
(up to 4 V) to overcome the thermodynamic and kinetic barriers
to be oxidized to elemental S during the first charge process,
which is attributed to the high charge transfer resistance,
the intrinsic electronic insulating properties and the insoluble
nature of Li2S caused by the strong crystal structure of bulk Li2S.
Until now, many effective methods have been applied to solve
this problem. The commonly used method is to reduce the size
of Li2S particles. The reduced Li2S particles with high surface
area can increase the contact area among the Li2S, conducting
agent and electrolyte. This can significantly speed up the kinetic
process of Li2S oxidation.22 Ball milling commercial Li2S parti-
cles and synthesizing fresh Li2S by reduction of Li2SO4 are the
two main approaches to reduce the particle size and the activa-
tion voltage of Li2S. Li et al. reported that when Li2S was ball
milled with carbon, its activation voltage reduced to 3.55 V.1
However, as Li2S is sensitive to air and moisture, the ball milling
process requires harsh experimental conditions. Wang et al.
used glucose-thiourea resin as a carbon source to react with
Li2SO4 at high temperatures to synthesize Li2S particles in situ
and the activation voltage of the Li2S cathode reached 2.7 V.20
This method is very effective to reduce the activation voltage, but
the synthesis process is very hard to handle. It not only requires
high temperature and inert gas protection, but also has a very
low produce ratio. Therefore, it is not conducive for industrial
production. Adding a specific additive to the electrolyte is
another effective approach to help Li2S to overcome the activa-
tion barrier during the first charging process.23–25 Meini et al.
effectively reduced the Li2S activation voltage to as low as 2.9 V
by adding redox mediators to the electrolyte.23 Redox mediators,
as reversible redox couples, could oxidize the surface of Li2S

Based on the discussion above, it is found that shortening
the electron transmission path and speeding up the electron
transfer is one of the critical approaches to reduce the activa-
tion voltage during the first charging process. As the interface
between Li2S particles is a solid–solid interface, and Li2S
particles are insulated, the kinetic barrier is very high. In this
work, we propose a new way to overcome the Li2S activation
barrier via changing the solid–solid interface into a solid–liquid
interface by taking advantage of the solubility of Li2S in certain
solvents. It is well known that ethanol is an ideal solvent to
dissolve Li2S, as it is cheap and environmentally friendly.26
Therefore, trace ethanol is chosen and applied as the electrolyte
additive to convert the solid–solid reaction in the first stage of
the activation process into a solid–liquid phase reaction to
reduce the reaction barrier in this work.
Fig. 1 presents the CV curves of the first four cycles of the
Li2S cathodes cycled in electrolyte with 500 ppm and without
ethanol. In Fig. 1a, the first cycle of the Li2S cathode cycled in
the electrolyte with 500 ppm ethanol was measured from 1.8 V
to 3.2 V at a rate of 0.1 mV s1. For comparison, the test range
of the cell cycled in the electrolyte without ethanol has been set
from 1.8 V to 3.8 V to ensure complete activation of Li2S, as
shown in Fig. 1b. In Fig. 1a, during the first cycle, the Li2S
cathode charge peaks at 2.85 V and 3.1 V represent the oxida-
tion of Li2S to elemental S. This corresponds to the activation
of the Li2S cathode.22 In contrast, when the cell cycled in the
electrolyte without ethanol was charged to 3.6 V, a significant
broad oxidation peak appeared (shown in Fig. 1b), which can be
ascribed to the poor electron and ionic conductivity of Li2S.27
After the first charge process, both the cells cycled in the
electrolyte with ethanol and without ethanol showed two
reduction peaks at 2.03 V and 2.35 V. This represents the
reduction of elemental sulfur to long chain polysulfides
(Li2S8–Li2S6)
and
short
chain
polysulfides
(Li2S4–Li2S3),
respectively.28 Therefore, after the first cycle of charging, the
Li2S is successfully oxidized into elemental S.29 This indicates
that 500 ppm ethanol can reduce the activation voltage of the
Li2S cathode by 0.8 V.
In order to clarify the eﬀect of ethanol on the Li2S particles
in the electrodes, 6 ml of DME solution containing 5% ethanol
was dropped on Li2S electrodes and dried at 60 1C for 3 hours.
Subsequently, the SEM and TEM images of the Li2S electrodes
after ethanol modification were measured, which are presented
in Fig. 2. For comparison, the SEM and TEM images of the Li2S
cathode without ethanol treatment are also tested, which are
presented in Fig. 2a and b. It is shown that without ethanol
modification, there is a large amount of Li2S particles on the
electrode, and the particle size is very large (2–3 mm). While, the
particle size of Li2S on the ethanol-modified electrode (Fig. 2c
and d) becomes much smaller (B1 mm). This can be ascribed to
dissolution of Li2S particles in ethanol. The agglomerated larger
Li2S particles were dissolved by ethanol to form small particles. At
the same time, the originally smaller Li2S particles are completely
dissolved by the ethanol and diffused in the electrolyte to form
finer particles distributed between the graphene sheets. Similar
results can also be found from the SEM and TEM images at lower
magnification in Fig. S1 (ESI†). It is interesting that, as shown in
Fig. S1d (ESI†), the dissolved Li2S is distributed uniformly along
the groove formed by bent graphene. Therefore, the modification
of a trace amount of ethanol significantly reduces the size and
number of Li2S particles on the electrode and improves the
contact area between the Li2S and the conductive materials,
which facilitates the transport of Li ions and the derivation of
electrons.

Li2S electrodes with electrolytes containing 1000 ppm, 500 ppm,

respectively. This matches very well with the results discussed in
Fig. 1. The first discharge specific capacity of cells containing
500 ppm and 250 ppm ethanol are as high as 1144 mA h g1 and
1052 mA h g1, while the first discharge capacity of 0 ppm and

As discussed above, a trace amount of ethanol, as low as
500 ppm, can significantly reduce the activation voltage of the
Li2S cathode and improve the electrochemical performances,
which can be ascribed to the strong solubility of Li2S in ethanol
(Fig. S6, ESI†). From the XPS spectra of Li2S/graphene cathodes
treated by ethanol, as shown in Fig. S7 (ESI†), it is clear that
ethanol has only played a simple physical solution eﬀect on the
surface, with no other chemical side reactions. In order to
better understand the possible working mechanism of the
reduction of activation voltage by adding ethanol in the elec-
trolyte, a schematic diagram is provided in Fig. 4. Yang et al.
found that Li2S had a high charge transfer resistance (B120 Ohms)
before reaching the barrier top, and the corresponding reaction
was a process in which Li2S solids were converted into Li2XS
solids.22 This is the main reason why Li2S generates a high
activation barrier during the initial charge progress. Li2S in
contact with the conductive material is oxidized to Li2XS to
generate both electrons and Li ions, and the reaction proceeds
further only when the electron and Li ion transport paths are
unobstructed. Li ions can be transported through the electrolyte
to the Li negative electrode, but electrons can only be trans-
ported through the conductive carbon material. According to
Fig. 4a, Li2S particles have a large particle size and there is only a
small solid–solid interface to contact with the conductive material
without ethanol treatment. In this case, the electrons and Li ions
in Li2S particles cannot be efficiently transferred during the
reaction, which will hinder the conversion of Li2S to Li2XS. In
addition, the Li2S particles in the intermediate position cannot be
in contact with the electrolyte and the conductive material, so that
the activation process cannot be completed. However, when
ethanol is added into the electrolyte, the large particles of Li2S
are dissolved into small particles of Li2S and liquid Li2S, which

In summary, we have applied trace ethanol as a simple and
eﬀective electrolyte additive to reduce the activation voltage of
the Li2S cathode for Li2S based Li-ion S batteries. The addition
of ethanol made the interface between the Li2S particles become
a solid–liquid mixed state, instead of a solid–solid interface,
which greatly increases the contact area with the conductive
carbon material, and raises the electron transfer speed, thereby
effectively reducing the activation barrier of the Li2S cathode.
As the usage of Li2S increases with the addition of trace ethanol,
the electrochemical performances have been improved. It is also
found that the amount of ethanol is also critical. When the
amount of ethanol increases to 1000 ppm, it will have side
effects on the Li metal anode in the battery. It is concluded that
the optimal amount of ethanol is 500 ppm. This work has
proposed an effective and generalized mechanism to reduce
the activation voltage of Li2S, in which ethanol can be replaced
by other solvents that can dissolve Li2S. Therefore, this work
has provided a new direction for the research of Li2S and shows
great potential towards the industrial application of Li2S based
Li-ion–S batteries.
