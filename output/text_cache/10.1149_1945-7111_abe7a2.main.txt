Assessment of Li-S Battery Performance as a Function of
Electrolyte-to-Sulfur Ratio
H. Merve Bilal and Damla Erogluz
Bogazici University, Department of Chemical Engineering, Istanbul 34342, Turkey
Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) battery performance is greatly sensitive to cell design as a result of the highly complex reaction and shuttle
mechanisms within the cathode. Electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio is one of the key design parameters that have a great impact on the
performance of Li-S batteries. Here, an integrated research methodology coupling experimental characterization and electro-
chemical modeling is applied to forecast the relation between the E/S ratio and the discharge capacity, cycling performance and
cell- and system-level speciﬁc energy and energy density of the Li-S battery. The highest initial discharge capacity is achieved with
an E/S ratio of 20 μl mg−1, whereas, the best capacity retention is observed for 13 μl mg−1. This experimentally obtained link
between the E/S ratio and the discharge performance is taken into consideration in the proposed cell- and system-level performance
models. Lower E/S ratios lead to higher battery performance at the cell and system level. Consequently, an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1
presents the best performance as the impact of E/S ratio not only on the peak discharge capacity and capacity retention but also on
the speciﬁc energy and energy density at the cell and system level are all considered.

Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) batteries have attracted signiﬁcant atten-
tion as a promising candidate for the next-generation batteries owing
to their high theoretical speciﬁc energy (2567 Wh kg−1)2–4 and
capacity (1675 mAh g−1).5,6 In addition to those beneﬁts, sulfur also
has the advantages of non-toxicity, abundance and low cost.7,8 The
overall reaction between S and Li+ is S8 + 16Li+ + 16e−→8Li2S
with a standard potential of U0 = 2.2 V (Li/Li+).9–12
Li-S batteries have made great improvement in recent years; yet,
their performance is still critically linked to cell design13–18 due to an
absence of a full comprehension of the complex reaction mechan-
isms in the cathode and the related equilibria in a working Li-S
system19,20 and the negative outcomes of the polysulﬁde shuttle
phenomenon.21 Cathode design parameters such as the electrolyte-
to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, carbon-to-sulfur (C/S) ratio, and sulfur loading
are examples of key factors to be optimized in the Li-S batteries to
achieve high performance.22,23
E/S ratio, which is one of the key design parameters in the cell,
has a great impact on the electrochemical performance of the Li-S
battery since it affects the viscosity of the electrolyte and the
polysulﬁde dissolution in the cell.24,25 The electrolyte plays a critical
role in the Li-S battery performance as it dissolves the polysulﬁdes,
which are the reaction intermediates in the cathode, and carries the
lithium ions within the cell. Consequently, it has a great impact on
the reaction and polysulﬁde shuttle mechanisms in the cell. High E/S
ratios are required for enhanced sulfur utilization and thus high
initial discharge capacity.17,26 Nevertheless, polysulﬁde dispropor-
tionation should be reduced to achieve good cyclability.27 As a result
of the insulating nature of sulfur and the generation of polysulﬁdes,
understanding the reaction and shuttle mechanisms at low electrolyte
amounts is a vital challenge that needs to be resolved for the
practical use of Li-S batteries.28
Moreover, increasing the electrolyte amount impacts the system-
level performance by decreasing the speciﬁc energy and energy
density of the battery.18 Excessive electrolyte amounts, or high E/S
ratios in other words, cause a signiﬁcant increase in the total weight
of the cell, which leads to a decrease in the gravimetric energy
density.24 One of the biggest challenges in the Li-S battery literature
is to achieve high speciﬁc capacity and cyclability with low
electrolyte amounts hence low E/S ratios, which are required for
high energy density at the system level.29 As a consequence of this
contradiction, the E/S ratio should be optimized by not just
considering the electrochemical performance of the Li-S cell but
also the cell- and system-level energy density of the battery, which
has not gained enough attention in the literature.
The effect of E/S ratio on the Li-S battery performance has been
investigated experimentally by many studies in the literature.30–33
E/S ratio is higher than 10 μl mg−1 in more than half of the
investigated studies. Zheng et al. presented that E/S ratio should be
20 μl mg−1 to achieve optimal sulfur utilization and cycling stability
with reduced shuttle reactions.34 Low electrolyte amounts typically
cause inadequate wetting of the electrode surface and continuous
electrolyte depletion during discharge-charge; thus, only 5 percent of
the studies reported E/S ratios lower than 4 μl mg−1.35 Recently,
different approaches are taken in the literature to achieve high
capacity and improved cycle life at low E/S ratios (<4.5 μl mg−1):
designing a cathode with oxygen-rich dense carbon host to increase
the interaction between carbon and sulfur,36 adding polymer gel into
the cell as a reservoir to trap polysulﬁdes,37 and developing a solid-
state Li2S4 cathode to overcome the issue of surface coverage.38
To sum up, the majority of the studies in the literature focus on
enhancing the speciﬁc capacity and cycle life of Li-S cells by
utilizing very high E/S ratios in the cell. However, as mentioned
above, low E/S ratios are required for high energy densities at the
cell and system level. For instance, it was discussed that the E/S ratio
should be at most 4 μl mg−1 to pass beyond the energy density of the
Li-ion batteries.32 Hagen et al.24 also reported that E/S ratio should
be 2 μl mg−1 to excel Li-ion batteries with an energy density of
500 Wh kg−1. For this reason, to achieve high performance Li-S
batteries, the inﬂuence of E/S ratio not only on the speciﬁc capacity
and cyclability but also on the energy density must be studied.39
Even though there are many studies in the literature that
developed electrochemical models for the Li-S batteries,2,40,41
models investigating the impact of cell design on the energy density
of the Li-S battery are much more limited. Eroglu et al.18 examined
the impact of design parameters on the cell- and system-level
performance of Li-S batteries by developing a techno-economic
model. The effect of E/S ratio on the speciﬁc energy and energy
density of the Li-S cell is also explored previously by McCloskey.25
Moreover, Emerce and Eroglu15 explored the effect of E/S ratio on
the cell- and system-level performance of a Li-S battery by
developing a performance model. However, in these previous
models the experimental effect of E/S ratio on the cell capacity
and cycle life of the Li-S battery has not been considered thoroughly.
Subsequently, there is still a gap in the literature for understanding
the critical link between the E/S ratio with the electrochemical
performance and the cell- and system-level energy density of the
Li-S battery.
In this study, we present an integrated research methodology
combining electrochemical characterization and modeling techniques
to characterize the impact of E/S ratio on the cell- and system-level
performance of the Li-S battery. First, we demonstrate an experimental
investigation of the electrochemical performance of the Li-S cells,
namely the initial discharge capacity and cycling performance, with
varying E/S ratios. Then, cell- and system-level performance models
predicting the impact of E/S ratio on the energy density and speciﬁc
energy of the Li-S battery are developed based on our experimental
observations. The investigation of the cell- and system-level perfor-
mance of the Li-S batteries as a function of E/S ratio coupling a
comprehensive material-to-system analysis and electrochemical char-
acterization essentially makes this study unique. Therefore, we believe
that it will make a signiﬁcant contribution to the Li-S literature.
Experimental
Cathode and cell fabrication.—Cathodes were prepared by
adjusting carbon/sulfur/binder weight ratios as 45/45/10. First,
conductive carbon black (Super C65, MTI) and sulfur (Sigma
Aldrich) were mixed in a mortar and pestle for 5 min. Afterwards,
polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF, MTI) was added to the mixture as a
binder. N-methyl-2-pyrolidone (NMP, MTI) was then mixed with
the solid mixture until a homogenous medium with a NMP/solid
mixture ratio of 7:1 wt/wt was obtained. In order to achieve a well-
mixed solution, the slurry was ﬁrst mixed in a magnetic stirrer
overnight, then heated to 50 °C for 2 h and then mixed in an
ultrasonic mixer for 2 more hours. Next, the solution was cast onto
an aluminum foil (MTI) with a doctor blade. The cathode slurry
casted on the aluminum foil was dried overnight and then at 50 °C
for 4 h in oven for the removal of the NMP. Thickness of the
cathodes after drying was around 90 μm. Finally, the cathode ﬁlms
were cut as 2.01 cm2 disks by using a puncher.
In order to prepare the electrolyte, 1,3-Dioxalane (DOL, Sigma
Aldrich) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma Aldrich) were mixed
in equal volumes in a glove box. Then, 1 M lithium bis-triﬂuor-
omethanesulfonimide (LiTFSI, Sigma Aldrich) and 0.1 M lithium
nitrate (LiNO3, Sigma Aldrich) were added to the solution. Two-
electrode CR2032 coin cells were constructed with the prepared
cathode, a Celgard polymeric separator (3.1 cm2, 25 μm, MTI), a

So as to study the effect of E/S ratio on the electrochemical
performance of the battery, Li-S cells with E/S ratios of 6–35 μl mg−1
were prepared. The cathode composition and thickness thus the sulfur
loading was kept constant for all Li-S cells with varying electrolyte
amounts. Table I presents the experimental design parameters used in
this study.
Electrochemical cycling measurements.—Galvanostatic cycling
measurements were performed at room temperature to assess the
electrochemical performance of the coin cells by using a Neware
battery cycler. First, each cell was rested for 16 h at its open circuit
voltage. The cells were then cycled for 100 cycles at a C-rate of 0.1C
(C-rate was calculated based on the theoretical capacity of the Li-S
cell). The lower and upper cut-off voltages were set as 1.5 and 3 V,
respectively. Each experiment was replicated three times and all
results are reported as the average of the three replicates.
Model development.—Following the experimental part, the
effect of E/S ratio on the cell- and system-level performance of a
Li-S battery was investigated by developing performance models as
described next. Design parameters (E/S ratio, S, C and binder wt%,
S loading) used in the experiments as well as experimentally
measured cell capacities and voltages were fed into the models to
capture the dependence of the electrochemical performance of the
Li-S cell on the E/S ratio in the performance models.
Cell-level performance model.—First, the impact of E/S ratio on
the cell-level energy density and speciﬁc energy is examined by
developing a cell-level performance model. The Li-S cell in the
model is composed of the positive and negative current collectors,
the separator, and the positive and the negative electrodes. In the
calculation of the speciﬁc energy and energy density, cell capacity
and the mass and volume of each cell component are normalized to
the cathode area.15,25

set by the battery performance and life is typically deﬁned as the

Therefore, it is assumed that thicker cathodes than the ones used

In the model, after the cell is designed according to the energy
and power requirements, the components of packaging and thermal
management are also calculated to predict the system-level perfor-
mance of the Li-S battery. Subsequently, the system-level energy
density and speciﬁc energy of a Li-S battery is attained as a function
of the E/S ratio by considering all of the crucial components in the
battery pack. The parameters used in the electrochemical and
system-level models are presented in Table III.
As it will be discussed in the next section, the impact of E/S ratio
on the system-level performance is also investigated for different
maximum cathode thicknesses and C/S ratios using the developed
model. As the scope of this work is studying the effect of E/S ratio
on the battery performance, the effect of cathode thickness and C/S
ratio is not examined experimentally in this study and the same
cathode speciﬁc capacities are fed into the model for all electrode
thicknesses and C/S ratios. Consequently, the inﬂuence of these two
critical design factors are investigated by changing the maximum
cathode thickness limitation (100, 150 and 200 μm) or the weight
percentages of carbon and sulfur (20:70, 30:60, 45:45, 60:30, 70:20)
in the system-level performance model. Examining the impact of
cathode thickness and C/S ratio experimentally and feeding the
model with the experimental speciﬁc discharge capacities for
different thicknesses and carbon amounts will be of signiﬁcance
and considered in the future studies.
Results and Discussion
Experimental characterization of the effect of E/S ratio on the
Li-S cell performance.—Electrolyte amount has a great impact on
the electrochemical performance of a Li-S cell since it provides a
medium for the transportation of the Li ions between the electrodes.
As mentioned above, adequate wetting of the surface and accelerated
charge transfer can be achieved with high amounts of electrolyte.
The effect of E/S ratio on the cycling behavior and discharge
capacity of a Li-S cell is examined for E/S ratios of 35, 20, 13 and
6 μl mg−1. The initial discharge curves for the respective cells with
varying E/S ratios and their cycling performance are demonstrated in
Figs. 1 and 2, respectively.
As seen in Fig. 1, the highest initial capacity is obtained with an
E/S ratio of 20 μl g−1. It is clearly illustrated that both the cell
capacity and voltage increase up to a certain level with increasing

However, a further increase in the E/S ratio leads to a decrease in the

The electrolyte amount in the cell is highly related to the
polysulﬁde amount in the medium as stated before. Although the
initial capacity is high in cells with high electrolyte content, a sharp
decrease in the capacity is observed for these cells as the number of
cycles increases (Fig. 2). At a higher level of E/S ratio with a
relatively excess electrolyte amount capacity declines rapidly, which
can be explained by that the polysulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte
diffuses into the cell’s dead corners during cycling resulting in poor
capacity retention.27 Consequently, the best cycling performance is
obtained for the Li-S cell with an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1.
Even though, the Li-S cell with the lowest E/S ratio of 6 μl mg−1
shows a similar initial discharge proﬁle with the others, the cycling
performance of this cell is signiﬁcantly bad. The capacity fades
completely after the 20th cycle as seen in Fig. 2a. This can be explained
by the fact that when the electrolyte amount is insufﬁcient in the cell,
polysulﬁde concentration increases considerably leading to a highly
viscous medium and inadequate wetting of the surface of the carbon/
sulfur composite cathode; hence, the reaction kinetics is hindered.24,34 It
should be noted that due to the low sulfur loading used in this study,
10 μl electrolyte is used in the cell to achieve the targeted E/S ratio of
6 μl mg−1. This electrolyte amount is fairly low to attain sufﬁcient
wetting of the electrolyte and the separator in a coin cell; this may also
contribute to the extremely poor cycling performance obtained for this
E/S ratio. Yet, the trends observed in Fig. 2a on the effect of E/S ratio
on the cycling performance are expected to be independent of the sulfur
loading of the cathode, as it will be discussed below in Fig. 2b.
Another factor contributing to the capacity fade observed in Fig. 2a
may be the irreversible reduction of LiNO3 forming a passivation layer
on the cathode at low cut-off voltages (<1.7 V).45–47 It is previously
reported in the literature that narrowing the voltage window leads to a
better cycling performance of the Li-S batteries.45–47 Nevertheless, the
impact of E/S ratio on the cycling performance is expected to be
independent of the voltage window chosen for the cycling measure-
ments.

higher E/S ratios. This may be explained by the presence of excess
LiNO3 in the cell at higher electrolyte amounts.48 It is also apparent

In Fig. 2b, the cycling performance of Li-S cells with a higher
sulfur loading is shown for two different E/S ratios. Similar conclu-
sions to the ones stated above can be drawn from the ﬁgure; while the
peak discharge capacity of the cell with an E/S = 35 μl mg−1 is
comparable to the one with an E/S = 13 μl mg−1, the capacity
retention for the lower E/S ratio is much better. This trend seems to be
independent of the sulfur loading of the cathode; consequently, it can
be discussed that the conclusions we report in this study can be
extended for Li-S cells with higher sulfur loaded cathodes. The impact
of sulfur loading on the performance is also apparent in the ﬁgure. It is
clear that the capacity fade becomes more prominent at higher sulfur
loadings. On the other hand, the effect of sulfur loading on the peak
discharge capacity is less pronounced. Lastly, lower sulfur loadings
lead to slightly higher Coulombic efﬁciencies as seen in the ﬁgure.
In Fig. 3, the discharge behavior of the Li-S batteries with different
E/S ratios is demonstrated at the 1st, 10th and 100th cycle. It is apparent
in the ﬁgures that in almost all E/S ratios there is a considerable
capacity decay at the 10th and 100th cycles. Especially, the cell with an
E/S ratio of 35 μl mg−1 displays a signiﬁcant decrease in the capacity
through cycling since shuttle mechanism increases at elevated electro-
lyte amounts, which may cause corrosion on the surface of the lithium
anode. As expected from Fig. 2a, the best capacity retention is achieved
with an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1. As the electrolyte amount decreases,
discharge proﬁles are relatively well preserved; the negative effect of
polysulﬁde shuttle mechanism over the capacity retention may be
diminished with a limited amount of dissolved species.34
Figure 4 summarizes the experimental results obtained on the
inﬂuence of the E/S ratio on the Li-S cell performance. As also
discussed above, the amount of electrolyte has a considerable impact
on the discharge capacity (Fig. 4a). It can be observed in the ﬁgure that
increasing E/S ratio increases the discharge capacity up to a certain
level. Figure 4b illustrates the relation of the cell voltage at 50% DOD
with the E/S ratio; increasing E/S ratio leads to a sharp increase in the
cell voltage, which can be explained by the domination of kinetic
limitations at low E/S ratios. However, since kinetic limitations
become more submissive at higher E/S ratios, cell voltage does not
change considerably with further increase in the E/S ratio.
Model predictions on the effect of E/S ratio on the Li-S battery
performance.—As mentioned previously, the majority of the
literature focuses on the effect of E/S ratio on the discharge capacity
and cycling performance of the Li-S battery. However, one should
also consider the speciﬁc energy and energy density at the cell and
system level while optimizing the electrolyte amount. In order to do
so, both cell- and system-level performances of the Li-S battery are
projected here, as discussed next.
Cell-level performance.—Figure 5 demonstrates the speciﬁc
energy and energy density of a Li-S cell as a function of the E/S
ratio. It can be seen in the ﬁgure that at very high E/S ratios speciﬁc
energy and energy density at the cell level decreases signiﬁcantly.
Even though our experimental results clearly present that the speciﬁc
capacity is the highest for an E/S ratio of 20 μl mg−1, the best cell-
level energy density and speciﬁc energy are achieved at lower E/S
ratios. This observation suggests that the increase in the cell weight
and volume with increasing E/S ratio has a more prominent effect on
the performance than the improvement in the discharge capacity.
This critical discussion proves that the E/S ratio should be optimized
not only based on the discharge capacity but also on the energy
density.
Since sulfur loading is another key design parameter affecting the
performance of Li-S batteries considerably, its effect on the
performance is also discussed in Fig. 5. It is clear that when sulfur
loading increases cell performance is enhanced remarkably. High
sulfur loadings are required to attain higher speciﬁc energies and
energy densities as also predicted in the literature.27,30 It can be also
discussed that the enhancement in the cell performance with
increasing sulfur loading is speciﬁcally pronounced at low sulfur
loadings; Li-S cells present similar energy densities at higher sulfur
loadings. Another interesting observation in Fig. 5 is that at low
sulfur loadings (including the experimental sulfur loading in this
study), the effect of discharge capacity on the speciﬁc energy is more
pronounced as the highest speciﬁc energies are obtained for an E/S
ratio of 13 μl mg−1. On the other hand, energy densities are not as
much as affected; it may be discussed that the best energy densities
are projected for the lowest E/S ratio for almost all sulfur loadings.
In Fig. 6, the effect of E/S ratio on the cell-level performance of
Li-S cells with varying sulfur loadings is investigated by using the
50th cycle discharge capacities. This is mainly done to compare the
performance of the cells based on their reversible capacity rather than
the peak one and thus to include the cycling performance of the cells
in the model projections. Similar trends are obtained with Fig. 5, but
this time the predicted speciﬁc energies and energy densities are the
highest for an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1 for all sulfur loadings.
Here, it should be emphasized that in the predictions reported in
Figs. 5 and 6, the variation of the discharge capacity with sulfur

System-level performance.—As stated previously, higher electro-
lyte amounts lead to better battery capacity, yet it should be
optimized by considering the overall mass and volume of the battery.
Figures 7 and 8 show the predicted speciﬁc energy and energy
density of a Li-S battery at the system level as a function of the E/S
ratio in the cell. Similar to our discussions in the previous part, both
speciﬁc energy and energy density decrease signiﬁcantly with
increasing electrolyte amount in the cell. This suggests that the
increase in the speciﬁc capacity of the cell with increasing E/S ratio
is less inﬂuential on the system performance compared to the
increase in the pack weight and volume.
The effect of maximum cathode thickness on the system-level
performance is also investigated in Fig. 7. The ﬁgure exhibits that
when the cathode thickness increases, the speciﬁc energy and energy
density of the Li-S battery also increase. This is expected based on
our discussion on the impact of sulfur loading; higher sulfur loadings
can be achieved with increasing cathode thicknesses. Thus, higher
cathode thicknesses result in higher cell capacities, thereby, better
system-level performance. It is also seen in the ﬁgure that the
inﬂuence of E/S ratio on the speciﬁc energy and energy density is
clearer at higher cathode thicknesses due to prevailing kinetic
limitations at these high cathode thicknesses.

performance is also considered. Carbon is needed in the cell in
excess amounts due to its good electronic conductivity. Increasing

Conclusions
In this study, the impact of E/S ratio on the cell- and system-
level performance of a Li-S battery was examined by applying a
combination of theoretical and experimental methods. Electrochemical
characterization of Li-S cells with varying E/S ratios was performed and
the experimental results were fed into the developed cell- and system-
level performance models to project the effect of E/S on the speciﬁc
energy and energy density of the Li-S battery. Experimental results
demonstrated that even though the highest initial discharge capacity was
obtained for an E/S ratio of 20 μl mg−1, the highest capacity retention
was achieved with an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1. Cell- and system-level
energy density and speciﬁc energy projections clearly suggested that
lower E/S ratios were better for higher performance. The impact of sulfur
loading, cathode thickness and C/S ratio on the energy density and
speciﬁc energy was also investigated along with the E/S ratio with the
developed performance models. To conclude, when the inﬂuence of E/S
ratio not only on the initial discharge capacity and cycling performance
but also on the speciﬁc energy and energy density at the cell and system
level were considered, an E/S ratio of 13 μl mg−1 showed the best
performance. Our approach has clearly shown that achieving enhanced
cycle life at low E/S ratios and high sulfur loadings is crucial to attain

current collectors as reported in our previous models.15,17 In the
model, the ASI and the overpotential are calculated at a speciﬁed
DOD for each component of the cell -cathode, anode, current
collectors and porous separator- individually.
