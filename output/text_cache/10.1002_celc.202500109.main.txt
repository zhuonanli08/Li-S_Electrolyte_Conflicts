Electrolyte-Dependent Electrode Potentials in
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Fritz Wortelkamp, Christian Wittekind, Lilli Busch, and Ingo Krossing*
Lithium–sulfur batteries are promising for future energy storage
due to their high specific capacity. However, challenges like low
cycling stability and the shuttle effect hinder their practical use. A
better understanding of the underlying processes is crucial. This
study examines the correlation between lithium potential (ELi)
and sulfur reaction potentials, referenced against the ferroce-
nium/ferrocene couple as an (almost) solvent-independent refer-
ence. Sulfur reactions are divided into two main stages during
discharge and charge. Most sulfur reactions show a good linear
correlation with ELi (R2 up to 0.87), except for the initial reduction of
S8 to Li2S8, which shows no significant correlation. This suggests
electrolyte design should address individual sulfur reactions rather
than treating them collectively. Additionally, the correlation
between ELi and the Raman-active symmetric S–N–S stretching
mode vs(SNS) in the Li[N(SO2CF3)2] electrolyte anion is analyzed
across different electrolyte mixtures. A good linear correlation
(R2 = 0.73) indicates that both the Raman peak position and ELi
reflect the strength of the interaction between Liþ and the electro-
lyte solvent and hence the Liþ ion activity. These findings provide
valuable insights for optimizing lithium–sulfur battery electrolytes.
1. Introduction
The hunt for the postlithium battery that will be able to replace
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) in the future is ongoing. A strong can-
didate for applications that demand a high energy density is the
lithium–sulfur battery (LISB). While at comparatively low voltages,
it offers an exceptionally high theoretical cathode capacity of
1672 mAh g1 coupled with a low price and low environmental
impact of its primary cathode material, sulfur.[1–3] While their cath-
ode discharge capacity is significantly lower, with, for example,
a theoretical capacity of 274 mAh g1 for LiCoO2, LIBs offer
exceptional cyclability and efficiencies, sometimes higher than
99.9 %.[4–7] These qualities are still lacking in LISBs due to various
factors, like the dissolution of sulfur species and their shuttling to
the negative electrode side, followed by undesired side reac-
tions.[8,9] For lithium–metal batteries, it has been shown that
the lithium potential in different electrolytes is a good indicator
for the reversibility of the battery system, with lower potentials
correlating with lower Coulombic efficiencies (CEs).[10] While the
potential window stays the same, the potential of Li/Liþ changes
with the electrolyte, and lower potentials seem to indicate a
stronger tendency for side reactions, including those with the
electrolyte. Such side reactions are among the most substantial
contributors to lower Coulombic efficiencies and poor cycling
performance.[11,12] This implies that a thoughtful choice of elec-
trolyte regarding these effects can result in better battery perfor-
mance. Accordingly, the effect of electrolytes on the battery
reaction and unwanted side reactions for LISBs has been a focus
of research, since the battery reaction primarily takes place in the
electrolyte and not in the bulk material.[13–15] Compared to the LIB
case, the number of species involved in LISBs is way higher due to
sulfur undergoing a multistep reduction from S8 to Li2S as part of
the battery reaction.[16,17]
The goal of this study is to shine a light on how the choice of
electrolytes affects all the electrochemical potentials of the dif-
ferent species involved in the LISB battery reaction. It was shown
that the potential of lithium (ELi), which is highly dependent on
the interaction between the Liþ-ion and the electrolyte solvent
(i.e., its activity), correlates with the potentials at which different
reduction and oxidation peaks can be measured in the Li–S sys-
tem if measured versus ferrocene as an internal standard and an
(almost) solvent-independent reference. This correlation is not
the same for all sulfur reactions, indicating that different effects
influence the Li electrode potential ELi and the potentials asso-
ciated with the sulfur reduction ES1, ES2, ES3, and ES4. Additionally,
the position of the Raman-active SNS stretching mode vs(SNS) in
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) was investi-
gated. This is an indirect measure for the Liþ-ion activity and
has been shown to correlate with ELi for lithium bis(fluorosul-
fonyl)imide (LiFSI), and a similar correlation for LiTFSI, which
is predominantly used in LISBs, was shown.[9,10] The goal of this
step was to verify whether ELi is overly dependent on side
reactions involving reactive solvents at the surface in these
systems. The position of vs(SNS) is independent of these
effects, senses the Liþ cation activity, and is therefore well-suited

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Lithium Potentials and vs(S–N–S) Peak Positions
A total of 23 electrolyte solutions were prepared to be used in
this study. They consist of a solvent and LiTFSI as a conducting
salt. The complete list can be found in Table S1, Supporting
Information. This list comprises solvents and solvent mixtures
used in LISBs before, plus dimethyl carbonate (DMC), which can-
not be used in LISBs due to its reaction with lithium polysulfides,
but is a common solvent in other types of batteries, which is why
its’ use has been limited to this first part of the study. The stan-
dard concentration used was 1 M LiTFSI, but for some solvents,
an additional measurement at 0.2 M LiTFSI was added to collect
more data points.

2.1.2. Raman Measurements
They were performed for the same electrolyte systems, and the
peak positions of the symmetric SNS stretching mode of the TFSI
anion between 740 and 750 cm1 are marked in Figure 1a–c. The
individual data can be found in Table S3, Supporting Information.
The ELi data was then plotted versus the Raman peak position of
vs(S–N–S) in Figure 1d, and a linear regression was performed,
showing a strong relationship between these measurements.
This effect is due to the coordinating ability of any given solvent
affecting the potential/activity of Liþ in solution. Additionally,
strong coordination of solvent and Liþ results in solvent-
separated ions with a weaker interaction between TFSI and Liþ,
which then affects the position of the vs(S–N–S) peak in the
Raman measurement and shifts it to lower wave numbers as indi-
cated in Scheme 1.[10] By contrast, mono- or bidentate coordina-
tion of Liþ polarizes the S–N–S unit positively and leads to a
blueshift/larger S–N–S stretching frequency. This implies that
the position of the vs(S–N–S) band strongly correlates with the
chemical potential and the Liþ-ion activity in solution, and, since
no metallic lithium is present during the Raman measurement, it
is not affected by electrode overpotentials or similar effects. The
good correlation between vs(S–N–S) and ELi implies that the lith-
ium potential ELi can be seen as an indicator for the interaction
strengths of Liþ and the solvent molecule present in the electro-
lyte. Low values of ELi signify a stronger interaction with the elec-
trolyte, and higher values of ELi signify a weaker interaction with
the electrolyte. Compared with the distribution of the vs(S–N–S)
peak positions observed for electrolytes containing FSI as an
anion, the distribution of values measured here is smaller.
The distribution for FSI spans a range of about 60 cm1, while
the data measured here is only spread over 10 cm1.[10] This
observation aligns with the considerable differences in the peak
position change upon switching solvents between TFSI and
FSI reported elsewhere.[26]

steps start with a negatively charged PS in solution, this is the
only step where sulfur (= molecular S8) transitions from a solid
form to a dissolved form. Interestingly, the highest correlation
can be observed for the inverse reaction four, where the product
is solid sulfur. This indicates that only the dissolution and activa-
tion of sulfur are strongly dependent on the solvent, while the
oxidation and precipitation are less dependent on it. This results
in the potential of the fourth reaction being dominated by the
same effects determining the lithium potential. A stronger corre-
lation during the first cycle compared to later cycles was
expected, as the first cycle represents a state in which sulfur spe-
cies have not yet dissolved in the electrolyte, thereby minimizing
their influence on the measured potentials. Interestingly, the
strength of the correlation remained largely unchanged between
the first and third cycles, although the absolute positions of the
potentials did shift. This was interpreted as an indication that, at
least in the solvents investigated here, side reactions, such as the
reaction of sulfur with the Li electrode, are indeed present, but
either affect the different systems in a similar manner or have a
significantly smaller impact than the differences between the
electrolyte systems. Although no confirmation via Raman meas-
urements could be performed for the sulfur potentials, the
observed correlations with ELi suggest that these measurements
yield consistent and reliable values, even in the presence of side
reactions such as the shuttle effect, which cannot be captured in
the measurements performed here and which is a weakness of
this method. The data presented here shows that the different
processes occurring in LISBs must be considered individually
when designing electrolytes. Optimally, an electrolyte can be
chosen that offers favorable conditions for the PS reactions that
occur in the solution, while also supporting the dissolution reac-
tions starting with solid sulfur. When considering unwanted side
reactions in the battery, this also opens up the possibility of tun-
ing an electrolyte to favor specific reactions in the sulfur battery
while suppressing others. While the data presented here does not
contain information about the quality of a battery cell built with
each of the electrolytes, it provides insight into the working prin-
ciples of LISBs.
3. Conclusion
In this study, it was demonstrated that the positions of the
Raman-active S–N–S stretching mode vs(S–N–S) in electrolyte sol-
utions with the LiTFSI conducting salt correlate with the lithium
potential ELi in different electrolyte mixtures and follow a previous
report for the related LiFSI electrolyte salt. This implies that ELi, as
well as the position of the vs(S–N–S) mode, are good indicators for
the interaction between Liþ and the electrolyte, i.e., the Liþ-ion
activity. The correlations between the different peaks observed
in the sulfur CV and ELi contain information about whether this
interaction between electrolyte and Liþ plays a significant role in
sulfur redox chemistry. Here, it was found that the first observed
CV peak position of sulfur, the reaction of molecular S8 to long-
chained PS, showed no correlation with ELi. The peak assigned to
the second reaction, the reduction of long-chained PS, showed a
moderate correlation. The peaks for the third and fourth reac-
tions, which are the oxidation reactions occurring during battery
charging, showed good correlations with ELi. This implies that the
dissolution of sulfur during the discharge of the LISB is dominated
by other effects, most likely the solubility of sulfur and the poly-
sulfides. Additionally, the correlation between the third and
fourth sulfur peaks with ELi also indicates that they both depend
on the same electrolyte characteristics. This opens the door for a
better understanding of LISBs. When designing electrolytes with
battery performance in mind, the information presented here
provides the ability to make an informed decision about the
solvent properties that influence the battery. This also opens
the door for further studies investigating the relations between
battery performance and the potentials of lithium and the sulfur
reactions in different solvents. While such investigations are
beyond the scope of this work, currently, a follow-up study that
includes performance measurements and shuttle current meas-
urements alongside the potential measurements discussed here
is being conducted. The goal is to evaluate how the individual
potentials of the sulfur reactions relate to overall battery perfor-
mance. This will be followed by a large-scale screening of 3000
solvents using chemical potential data of different species in elec-
trolyte mixtures calculated with COSMO-RS and solvent proper-
ties, aimed at identifying and testing potential new electrolyte
mixtures for use in lithium–sulfur batteries.
