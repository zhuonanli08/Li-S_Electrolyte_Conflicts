Electrolyte Additive for Regulating Redox Kinetics in Lithium−Sulfur

ABSTRACT: Sluggish sulfur redox kinetics and incessant shuttling of lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs) greatly influence the electrochemical properties of
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries and their practical applications. For this
reason, ammonium thiosulfate (AMTS) with effective redox regulation
capability has been proposed as a functional electrolyte additive to promote
the bidirectional conversion of sulfur species and inhibit the shuttle effect of
soluble LiPSs. During discharging, the S2O3
2−in AMTS can trigger the rapid
reduction of LiPSs from long chains to short chains by a spontaneous chemical
reaction with sulfur species, thereby decreasing the accumulation of LiPSs in
the electrolyte. During charging, the NH4
+ in the AMTS enhances the
dissociation/dissolution of Li2S2/Li2S by hydrogen-binding interactions, which
alleviates the electrode surface passivation and facilitates the reversible
oxidation of short-chain sulfides back to long chains. The enhanced
bidirectional redox kinetics brought about by AMTS endows Li−S cells with high reversible capacity, excellent cycle stability,
and rate capability even under lean electrolyte conditions. This work not only illustrates an effective redox regulation strategy by an
electrolyte additive but also investigates its catalytic reaction mechanism and Li corrosion behavior. The crucial criteria for screening
additives that enable bidirectional redox mediation analogous to AMTS are summarized, and its application perspectives/challenges
are further discussed.
KEYWORDS: electrolyte additive, ammonium thiosulfate, polysulfide, redox kinetics, lithium−sulfur battery, catalytic effect
■INTRODUCTION
With the rapid development of advanced portable devices and
electric vehicles, it is difficult for traditional lithium-ion
batteries (LIBs) to meet the increasing market demand due
to their limited energy density.1 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries possess ultrahigh theoretical energy density (∼2600
Wh kg−1),2,3 which is significantly superior to that of state-of-
the-art LIBs. Besides, the battery system has the merits of
abundant material resources, low cost, and good environmental
friendliness, which is generally regarded as a promising
candidate for next-generation energy storage devices. However,
there are several obstacles that seriously hinder their practical
application. On the one hand, the insulating nature of sulfur
limits its electrochemical activity. For this reason, various
conductive carbon materials are usually introduced into the
cathode as the sulfur host.4,5 On the other hand, the long-chain
intermediates, lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), easily dissolve in
conventional liquid electrolytes and then shuttle between two
electrodes to produce insoluble Li2S2/Li2S, which is difficult to
reuse.6,7 This leads to continuous active material loss, severe Li
corrosion, and low Coulombic efficiency.8 The carbon host
always has a nonpolar surface with a relatively low affinity for
polar LiPSs and thus is insufficient to restrict the redox
shuttle.9,10
Since dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs are closely
associated with the liquid electrolyte, the optimization of
electrolyte composition is considered to be a reasonable
approach to tackle the above problems, e.g., altering the Li
salt/solvent type, regulating the electrolyte amount, or
introducing functional additives.11−13 The employment of
weakly polar or nonpolar solvents, such as fluorinated ether,
makes LiPSs poorly soluble in the electrolyte conducive to
restraining the shuttle.14 However, the prepared electrolytes
are generally highly viscous and poorly ion-conductive, and the
insoluble LiPSs give rise to sluggish conversion kinetics, which

leads to low utilization of the active material.15 In this sense,
the dissolution of LiPSs to some extent is necessary. At this
moment, the restriction of diffusion and shuttle of dissolved
LiPSs can rely on some film-forming additives, such as LiNO3,
which is capable of preventing the Li metal from the attack of
LiPSs by constructing a robust protection layer.16 Never-
theless, the protection function of these additives would be
degraded gradually by continuous consumption on the
electrodes during cycling.17
Another promising route for restraining LiPS shuttles by
electrolyte engineering is to accelerate the LiPS conversion to
solid Li2S2/Li2S and then decrease their accumulation in the
electrolyte.18−20 For this purpose, some special additives with
catalytic conversion functions have been developed in recent
years.21−24 For instance, dipentamethylenethiuram tetrasulfide
(DPTT) has been reported as an electrolyte additive to slow
down the accumulation and shuttle of long-chain LiPSs by
promoting their reduction.24 However, the consequent result is
the generation of a large amount of Li2S2/Li2S, changing the
reversible accumulation of long-chain LiPSs in the electrolyte
into the irreversible deposition of short-chain sulfides on the
electrode surface. This is because the conversion of insoluble/
insulated Li2S2/Li2S back to LiPSs requires large activation
energy, which is worsened by their aggregation during the
formation process.25 Moreover, these insoluble Li2S2/Li2S have
a high electrical resistivity that seriously passivates the
electrodes with a high interface resistance. Therefore, the
regulation of a single sulfur reduction process is insufficient. It
is urgently necessary to further develop electrolyte additive
technology for mediating bidirectional redox kinetics between
LiPSs and Li2S2/Li2S.26
Recently, a class of so-called “organic co-mediator” (such as
DMDS, DMTS, DtbDS)27−30 has emerged as a promising
alternative to achieve the redox mediation for Li−S batteries,
which is featured by the spontaneous chemical reaction with
sulfur species, accompanied by the generation of new highly
active intermediates (e.g., soluble organopolysulfides) for
subsequent reduction to short-chain sulfide. The altered
reaction pathway is considered to accelerate polysulfide
conversion kinetics as well as induce 3D and uniform Li2S
deposition favorable for higher utilization of sulfur species.27
Moreover, the regeneration of the active intermediate and
disappearance of its reduction product upon charging were
observed, indicating that the process is reversible and that the
additive mediation function can be recovered.28 However,
most of these co-mediators (including their intermediates) are
also highly reactive with reductive components in batteries,
such as Li metal anodes, with latent self-discharging hazards.
Especially, their amounts are usually high (e.g., 50 vol %)28 in
order to better boost the cell capacity (employed as a cosolvent
and not an additive), which further aggravates the Li corrosion
issue. In addition, the sluggish solid−liquid conversion caused
by insoluble sulfides has not been solved effectively in these
studies.
Herein, a novel electrolyte additive, ammonium thiosulfate
((NH4)2S2O3, AMTS), was first introduced in Li−S batteries
to achieve bidirectional redox regulation. Different from the
above “organic co-mediator”, the AMTS proposed in this study
is an ion-type mediator consisting of S2O3
2−and NH4
+. As a
functional additive, AMTS is relatively limited in amount (only
0.5 wt %, which is advantageous to reduce the weight load of
the electrolyte); however, it delivers a strong catalytic
capability for redox mediation by in situ chemical reaction
with long-chain sulfur species. The reaction process is verified
to be highly reversible as well. Importantly, the side reaction
between the AMTS and Li metal is controllable. Besides, the
NH4
+ in AMTS can further promote the dissociation/
dissolution of short-chain sulfides, which facilitates their
subsequent oxidation. These merits well reflect the difference
and advantage of the ion-type additive over previously reported
“organic co-mediators.” Consequently, the Li−S cell with
AMTS exhibits an increased reversible capacity, improved
cycle stability, and rate capability even under lean electrolyte
conditions.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Electrolyte Preparation. A baseline electrolyte was prepared by
dissolving 1 M bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSi) and 2 wt
% lithium nitrate (LiNO3) in a solvent mixture of 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v/v = 1:1). Then, different
mass fractions of AMTS (Macklin, 99%) were added to the baseline
electrolyte and stirred at 25 °C for 72 h to obtain the experimental
electrolyte. All of the above operations were conducted in a glovebox
filled with argon.
Electrochemical Performance. The elemental sulfur and
mesoporous carbon (CMK-3, XFNano) were mixed uniformly at a
mass ratio of 7:3, sealed in a muffle furnace, and then heated to 155
°C for 20 h to obtain the S/CMK-3 composite. The sulfur cathode
was made of the S/CMK-3 composite, conductive carbon (Super P,
Timcal), and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Solef) in a weight
ratio of 8:1:1 coated on a piece of Al foil. The typical sulfur loading on
the cathode was ∼2.2 mg cm−2.
The CR2025-type Li−S cell was assembled in a glovebox, which
was composed of a sulfur cathode and a Li metal anode separated by a
polypropylene separator. The cell was cycled galvanostatically on a
LAND tester in the voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V. Cyclic voltammetry
(CV) was performed on an electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT
MC) at different scan rates. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS)
were measured in the frequency range from 105 to 0.01 Hz at a
perturbation amplitude of 5 mV.
For the redox kinetics study, a symmetric cell was employed with
two identical carbon papers as electrodes and 30 μL of 0.5 M Li2S6
solution as the electrolyte. Another 10 μL of the electrolyte with/
without AMTS was dropped onto the separator. CV measurements of
the symmetric cell were carried out in the voltage range of −1.0 to 1.0
V at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1.
The Li2S deposition experiment was conducted in a coin cell using
carbon paper as the cathode and Li foil as the anode. In the assembly
process, 20 μL of 0.2 M Li2S8 solution was added on the cathode side,
and another 20 μL of the electrolyte with/without AMTS was
dropped onto the separator. The cell was first discharged at a constant
current of 2.06 V, and then the potential was kept at 2.05 V until the
current was below 10−5 A for nucleation and deposition of Li2S.
Characterization. UV−visible absorption spectroscopy (UV-
3600) and Raman spectroscopy (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution)
were employed to examine the changes of sulfur species in the
solution. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo
Scientific Nicolet 6700) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS,
Thermo Scientific Escalab 250XI) were used to further study the
interaction between AMTS and Li2S6 in the solution. X-ray diffraction
(XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) was performed to examine the
precipitation products from a mixed solution of AMTS and Li2S6.
The sulfur cathode and Li anode were extracted from the Li−S cells
and washed with DME solvent. The surface and cross-sectional
morphologies of the electrodes were investigated by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300). The chemical composition
differences on the electrode surfaces were analyzed by an X-ray
photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha). The
fitted results of the S 2p peak were adjusted by using two equal full
width half-maximum S 2p doublets with a 2:1 area ratio and splitting

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Interaction with LiPSs. The color change of the LiPS
solution with and without AMTS was recorded, and the
supernatant liquid was extracted for UV−visible absorption
spectroscopy (employing Li2S6 as a representative of LiPSs).
As shown in Figure 1a,b, the color of the Li2S6 solution after
the addition of AMTS obviously changes from yellow to
colorless, and the intensities of the S6
2−absorption peaks at
264 and 278 nm are significantly reduced. This indicates a
decreased Li2S6 content in the solution, suggesting that the
AMTS additive can interact with unstable LiPSs. The Raman
spectra in Figure 1c also demonstrate a decline of peak
intensities for S6
2−and other sulfur species (originating from
the disproportionation reaction and complex equilibrium)
when AMTS was added to the solution. In addition, the FTIR
absorption peaks located at 542, 582 and 591 cm−1 in the Li2S6
solution (Figure 1d) represent the stretching vibrations of the
SS bond. These characteristic absorptions slightly shift
toward lower wavenumbers with weakened strength in the
presence of AMTS, further confirming the chemical interaction
between AMTS and LiPSs. In the Li 1s XPS (Figure 1e), the
characteristic peaks at 54.5 and 55.3 eV ascribed to Li2S6 also
demonstrate the reduced intensity in the solution with AMTS
compared to its counterpart. The precipitation product from
the Li2S6 solution with AMTS was collected for XRD analysis,
and the results are shown in Figure 1f. Interestingly, some of
the characteristic peaks attributed to Li2S appear in the
precipitation mixture, accompanied by several additional peaks
belonging to the residual AMTS. These results suggest that the
AMTS additive may be involved in the consumption of Li2S6
via a spontaneous chemical reaction.
Reaction Mechanism. To probe the reaction mechanism
of AMTS with LiPSs, ex situ XPS analyses of the sulfur cathode
at specific discharge/charge states were conducted, and the
results are shown in Figures 2 and S1. The fresh sulfur cathode
(Figure S1a) exhibits a weak S8 contribution, since most of the
elemental sulfur permeated into the porous CMK-3 during the
S/C composite preparation by the melting strategy, and was
surrounded by other electrode components, such as SP and
PVDF, which are difficult to detect by XPS. When the sulfur
cathode was immersed in the baseline electrolyte resting for 2
h at an open-circuit voltage (OCV), a pair of S−O peaks (S
2p3/2 at 169.8 eV) appeared on the electrode surface (Figure
S1b), which is usually associated with −NCF3SO2 in the
LiTFSi salt33,34 as well as sulfate species caused by air exposure
during the XPS sample loading process.31 The vanished S8
signal could be attributed to the surface passivation of these
species.
In contrast, the sulfur cathode immersed in the AMTS-
containing electrolyte exhibits an additional three doublet
peaks responsible for Li2S2 (S 2p3/2 at 162.6 eV), Li2Sx (S 2p3/2
at 164.0 eV), and thiosulfate (S 2p3/2 at 167.4 eV), respectively
(Figure 2a). Thiosulfate is usually regarded as the reaction
product between LiNO3 and Li2Sx,24,35 which exists in the
AMTS-containing case but is difficult to detect in its
counterpart without the additive. This reveals that there is
another source that mainly endows the thiosulfate peak, more
likely, S2O3
2−in the AMTS adsorbed/embedded on the
cathode. Note that the S−O peak also becomes obviously
stronger than the baseline electrolyte, which can be ascribed to
the additional contribution from the produced polythionate
complex (due to partial peak overlapping).31,36 Meanwhile, a
strong Li2S2 peak (together with longer-chain Li2Sx) is
observed on the cathode immersed in the AMTS-containing
electrolyte. The simultaneous generation of large amounts of
the polythionate complex and sulfide is considered strong

When the cell was discharged to 2.05 V (approximately the
beginning of the second discharge plateau, Figure 2b), the S−
O and Li2Sx peaks were moderately weakened due to the
electrochemical reduction of the polythionate complex and
long-chain Li2Sx. Further discharging to 1.7 V (the end of the
discharge, Figure 2c) led to a significant decrease in the S−O
peak and even vanishing of the Li2Sx peak, accompanied by an
increase in the Li2S2 contribution and the existence of a new
Li2S peak (S 2p3/2 at 161.2 eV). Meanwhile, the thiosulfate
contribution almost disappears as well, implying complete
consumption of S2O3

Based on the above XPS analyses, a credible reaction
mechanism between Li2Sx and AMTS is summarized in Figure
2e. In this process, the long-chain Li2Sx is linked to thiosulfate
by insertion in the S−S bond to generate the polythionate
complex and short-chain sulfide. In other words, the
transformation of thiosulfate to a polythionate complex is
accompanied by the conversion of LiPSs to Li2S2/Li2S.31,37
Then, the polythionate complex as an active intermediate is
capable of further reduction to release the capacity originating
from the inserted S in the S−S bond. Consequently, an
alternate reaction pathway for sulfur reduction is created owing
to the existence of AMTS, which probably helps to latently
catalyze the LiPS conversion.
Moreover, the XPS spectrum of the sulfur cathode recharged
to 2.4 V indicates the regeneration of the thiosulfate along with
polythionate complex and Li2Sx (Figure 2d, similar to that at
the initial discharge in Figure 2a). The regeneration process
can be observed constantly even when the sulfur cathode was
repeatedly cycled exceeding 200 cycles (Figure S2). The
results reveal the above reaction process is highly reversible
without a discernible decrease in AMTS contribution due to
the irreversible consumption, favorable for the long-term
cycling stability of the Li−S cell.
DFT Calculations. To gain a deep insight into the
functionality of the AMTS in the Li−S system, theoretical
studies by DFT calculations were performed. The binding
energies between AMTS and S8/LiPSs/Li2S (Figure 3a) were
negative, implying that these systems are thermodynamically
favorable. Thus, AMTS is inclined to interact with these sulfur
species and is involved in their redox processes. The shorter
the sulfur chain, the more negative the binding energy.
Especially, the binding energy of AMTS to Li2S reaches
−4.077 eV, indicating the strongest affinity between them. This
fact can be confirmed by the FTIR spectrum in Figure S3,
where the characteristic peaks corresponding to Li2S (for
instance, 1003 cm−1 for the stretching vibration of the sulfur
atom) move toward lower wavenumbers with moderate
enhancement. The strong affinity between AMTS and Li2S
enhances the solubility of Li2S in the electrolyte. To identify

The interaction between AMTS and Li2S molecules was
further investigated by molecular electrostatic potential
(MESP).39 Figure 3b shows the calculated MESP map of the
independent AMTS and Li2S after relaxation. The negative
(blue) and positive (red) electrostatic potential regions
indicate strong tendencies toward electrophilic and nucleo-
philic reactions, respectively. The highly electronegative N
atom in the N−H group pulls electrons away from the H atom,
resulting in a concentrated positive charge on the H atom and
thus the extreme polar strength of the ammonium cation.40
After AMTS was added into the Li2S molecule (Figure 3c), a

promoting the dissociation/dissolution of solid Li2S into the
electrolyte and then decomposition of Li2S in the charging
process. Next, the interaction of AMTS with longer-chain
LiPSs becomes increasingly weaker (as mentioned in Figure
3a), and the additive is likely to be gradually rereleased during
charging41 due to the decreased binding energy between
AMTS and the sulfur species.
Meanwhile, the S−S bridged bond in LiPSs (S3−S4 in Li2S4
as an example) is also stretched after the addition of AMTS,
which is beneficial for the conversion of long-chain LiPSs to
short-chain sulfide in the discharge process. Figure 3e shows
the Gibbs free energy of each discharge step with/without the
AMTS. The first two steps of reducing S8 to Li2S8 and then to
Li2S6 are found to be spontaneous exothermic reactions, and
the following three steps from Li2S6 to Li2S are endothermic
reactions. The final reaction from Li2S2 to Li2S is the rate-
limiting step42 because it has the largest endothermic energy in
the baseline electrolyte. In contrast, the rate-limiting step is
changed, and the conventional solid−solid reduction from
Li2S2 to Li2S occurs much more easily in the AMTS-containing
electrolyte. Furthermore, the energy barriers for all of the
intermediate reactions are greatly reduced by the additive. The
theoretical calculation results suggest that AMTS helps
accelerate the entire discharge process, ensuring faster
conversion and shuttle suppression of soluble LiPSs.

Li2S6 symmetrical cells with/without AMTS were assembled
for CV and EIS measurements, and the results are compared in
Figure 4c. The symmetrical cell with AMTS delivers a higher
redox current than its counterpart without it, followed by a
lower charge transfer resistance across the interface. Evidently,
the additive is conducive to adequate redox conversion
between the LiPSs and sulfides. The CV test of a typical Li−
S cell was conducted to explore the contribution of AMTS to
the redox reaction kinetics. As shown in Figure 4d, the two
cathodic peaks correspond to the reduction of S8 into
intermediate product LiPSs (P1) and then to the final product
Li2S2/Li2S (P2), and two anodic peaks are related to the
conversion of sulfides into LiPSs (Q1) and then oxidized sulfur
(Q2). The smaller potential separation between the cathodic
and anodic peaks in the AMTS-containing electrolyte
compared to that in the baseline electrolyte reveals lower
polarization and better redox reversibility owing to the
additive.
Tafel plots corresponding to the redox peaks in Figure 4d
are shown in Figures 4e and S6, and the fitted slope values are
compared in Figure 4f. The Tafel slopes of the cells with
AMTS are all evidently lower than their counterpart for both
reduction and oxidation processes, suggesting the bidirectional
catalytic activity of AMTS in the redox conversion between
LiPSs and Li2S. The activation energies (Ea) of the discharge
and charge processes were further calculated based on the
Tafel plots.44 After the addition of AMTS, the Ea value was
dramatically reduced by 48, 50 and 28 kJ mol−1 for the
reactions from S8 to LiPSs and then to Li2S and the oxidation
from Li2S to LiPSs, respectively (Figure S7), confirming the
increased bidirectional conversion rate due to the reduced
energy barrier.
In addition, CV measurements of Li−S cells were further
conducted at different scan rates. It should be noted that the
two anodic peaks overlap at higher scan rates (Figure S8).
There is an approximately linear relationship between the
cathodic/anodic peak current (Ip) and the square root of the
scan rate (v) at each redox step (Figure 4g−i). According to
the classical Randles−Sevcik equation,45 the slope of the curve
(Ip/ν0.5) should theoretically be positively correlated to the
DLi

the AMTS-containing cell over its counterpart during the
whole redox process confirm that the introduction of AMTS
contributes to accelerated Li+ diffusion and hence increased
utilization of active species within the cell.

Corrosion Analyses. Considering the high reactivity of the
AMTS, we next focus on the investigation of Li corrosion issue
caused by the additive. First, the Li metal was immersed in
different electrolytes to examine the possible side reactions
with the additive. As shown in Figure S15, a small number of
white dots can be observed on the Li surface after immersion
in the electrolyte with 0.5% AMTS for 24 h, indicating slight Li
corrosion. The corrosion phenomenon was aggravated when
the AMTS amount was raised to 2 wt %. Fortunately, the
presence of LiNO3 in the electrolyte can effectively passivate
the Li surface and hence greatly restrains the chemical
corrosion cooperating with the moderate addition of AMTS
(2% LiNO3 + 0.5% AMTS). Second, the cycle test of the Li|Li
symmetrical cell was conducted at 0.4 mA cm−2, and the
results are provided in Figure S16. The decrease in the
hysteresis potential at the beginning of cycling can be
attributed to the breaking of the native surface film on the
Li metal and the rebuilding of the SEI film.47,48 Then, the
LiNO3/AMTS-coexisting electrolyte provides an almost
identical potential response over repeated cycles exceeding
800 h, reflecting that a favorable SEI for Li+ migration can be
formed and reversible Li plating/stripping cycling is sustained.
Therefore, the chemical/electrochemical stability of AMTS
toward Li metal can be controlled by seeking an effective Li
protection strategy, mean while decreasing the amount of
AMTS.
The Al current collector was also immersed in DME with/
without AMTS to examine the possible corrosion reaction
involving AMTS. The Al surface remained intact and had an
appearance identical to its original state after immersion in
AMTS-containing DME for 24 h, and the Al 2p XPS spectra
further indicated a negligible difference between the two cases
with and without AMTS (Figure S17). This result reflects the
excellent compatibility of AMTS with the Al current collector.
The corrosion behaviors of LiPSs toward Li anodes in
different electrolytes were further investigated by observing the
surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the harvested Li
anode from the Li−S cell after 200 cycles. Severe structural
damage is found in the cycled Li using the baseline electrolyte
(Figure 6a,b), with large amounts of massive deposits on the Li
surface as a result of the uncontrollable LiPS shuttle. The
dissolved LiPSs diffusing from the cathode side can even
penetrate into bulk Li through the holes/cracks between these
deposits; hence, a closer inspection of the cross-sectional image
(Figure 6c) indicates that Li metal was deeply corroded upon
repeated cycles, despite the LiNO3 additive being employed.
When a moderate amount of AMTS was introduced in the
baseline electrolyte, the cycled Li surface became quite uniform
and dense (Figure 6d,e), presenting a smooth passive film on
it. Meanwhile, the bulk Li still keeps an intact structure after
cycling, and a decreased thickness of the passivation layer can
be identified (Figure 6f), suggesting effective Li protection and
hence restricted LiPS corrosion to Li metal in the AMTS-
containing electrolyte.

■CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES
In summary, AMTS was used as a bifunctional electrolyte
additive for the first time to improve the electrochemical
properties of Li−S batteries. The experimental studies
combined with DFT calculations reveal that AMTS can not
only accelerate the reduction kinetics of sulfur species with a
decreased reaction energy barrier by spontaneously reacting
with LiPSs, but also promote the dissociation/dissolution of
discharge products Li2S2/Li2S in the electrolyte and their
reversible oxidation by a strong hydrogen-binding effect. Based
on the above factors, the Li−S cell with AMTS additive
exhibits a distinct enhancement of reversible capacity and cycle
stability in contrast to its counterpart without AMTS, with a
preserved capacity of 724.3 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 0.5C,
and 802.9 mAh g−1 after 90 cycles at 0.2C in lean electrolyte
conditions (5 μL mgs
−1).
This proof-of-concept work illustrates an effective redox
regulation strategy to improve Li−S cell performance by
adding AMTS and elucidating the catalytic mechanism of the
functional additive. Then, several crucial criteria for screening
suitable additives that enable bidirectional redox mediation
analogous to AMTS can be summarized as follows: (1)
acceptable solubility in the electrolyte; (2) spontaneous
chemical reaction with sulfur species; (3) production of active
intermediates capable of being further reduced and accelerat-
ing sulfur conversion; (4) the reaction is highly reversible for
minimizing the irreversible consumption of the additive and
sulfur species; (5) strong interaction with short-chain sulfides
to promote their dissociation and reoxidation; and (6) good
chemical/electrochemical compatibility with other battery
components.
Based on the above criteria, various ion-type additives
consisting of a cation containing S−S bonds and an anion with
high polar strength could be potential candidates to meet the
application demands. However, the undesirable Li corrosion
caused by these additives (including their intermediates) with
intrinsic reactivity would be a main challenge in the electrolyte
system; thus, it is necessary to rationally control the additive
amount and/or seek the assistance of persistent Li protection
strategies (e.g., artificial barrier or surface passivation).
Meanwhile, the self-discharge issue as a result of the
spontaneous reaction and its effect on the cell performance
requires more attention. Besides, the residual polythionate
complex after discharging triggers the question: How can one
rationally control their residual amount and reduce the
possible negative effects on interface chemistry and sulfur
utilization? Lastly, the altered reaction process is still doubtful;
e.g., what is the exact final product for the reduction of the
polythionate complex (besides sulfides)? Are other dispropor-
tionation reactions of the active intermediates involved?
Therefore, the latent reaction mechanism and a reasonable
approach to further improve the catalytic capability of additives
remain to be investigated.
