Role of Polysulﬁde Anions in Solid-Electrolyte Interphase Formation
at the Lithium Metal Surface in Li−S Batteries

ABSTRACT: Delineating intricate interactions between highly reactive Li-metal electrodes
and the diverse constituents of battery electrolytes has been a long-standing scientiﬁc
challenge in materials design for advanced energy storage devices. Here, we isolated lithium
polysulﬁde anions (LiS4
−) from an electrolyte solution based on their mass-to-charge ratio
and deposited them on Li-metal electrodes under clean vacuum conditions using ion soft
landing (ISL), a highly controlled interface preparation technique. The molecular level
precision in the construction of these model interfaces with ISL, coupled with in situ X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy and ab initio theoretical calculations, allowed us to obtain
unprecedented insight into the parasitic reactions of well-deﬁned polysulﬁdes on Li-metal
electrodes. Our study revealed that the oxide-rich surface layer, which is amenable to direct
electron exchange, drives multielectron sulfur oxidation (S0 →S6+) processes. Our results
have substantial implications for the rational design of future Li−S batteries with improved
eﬃciency and durability.
T
he ability to control the evolution and charge transfer
kinetics of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers will
revolutionize electrochemical energy storage technologies by
enabling bottom-up materials design. The structural and
chemical complexity of SEI layers arises from cascading
interfacial reactions between the collective constituents of
electrolytes and the heterogeneous surface chemistry of
electrodes. Despite eﬀorts to develop in situ spectroscopic
and microscopic characterization techniques, an understanding
of the molecule-speciﬁc reactivity of electrolyte components
with chemically deﬁned metal surfaces has remained
elusive.1−8 The challenge in gaining molecular-level under-
standing is the diﬃculty involved in deconvoluting intertwined
interactions.9,10 This complexity, in turn, impedes our ability to
experimentally detect, isolate, and ﬁngerprint each reaction
pathway and product of interfacial processes.11−13 In situ
characterization of the reaction pathways of isolated electrolyte
molecules with well-deﬁned electrode surfaces will enable
deconvolution of the complex processes involved in SEI
evolution.
Ion soft landing (ISL) is a versatile mass-spectrometry-based
approach that isolates ions from solution based on their mass-
to-charge ratios and selectively deposits them onto surfaces at
controlled conditions.11,12,14−21 ISL allows investigation of the
decomposition pathways of selected ionic constituents of
electrolytes (e.g., solvated cations and/or bare anions) under
vacuum conditions that preserve the reacted components on
the metal surface enabling a greater degree of certainty in the
results. We selected lithium polysulﬁde as a probe molecule to
investigate the surface reactivity of Li-metal owing to (i) the
direct relevance of SEI layer evolution in the performance of
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries and (ii) the fact that sulfur-
based clusters are amenable to multielectron transfer processes
resulting in diverse oxidation states with well-established
ﬁngerprints that may be measured using photoelectron
spectroscopy. Probing changes in the oxidation state of sulfur
upon surface interaction will reveal the underlying reactivity of
Li-metal surfaces and associated charge transfer processes.
In Li−S batteries, the dissolution of polysulﬁdes from
cathodes and their parasitic reaction with Li-metal anodes
dictate the evolution of the SEI layer and overall cycling
performance.22−28 The initial parasitic reaction pathways
between the Li-anode and dissolved polysulﬁde species drive
the chain of subsequent redox reactions leading to evolution of
the SEI layer. Understanding the “time-zero” events that lead
to formation of the SEI layer is challenging due to the
polymorphism of polysulﬁdes and chemical heterogeneity of
Li-metal surfaces that contain functionalities such as oxides,
hydroxides, carbonates, and halides. Polysulﬁdes, which are

Initially, the Li-metal substrate was cleaned by Ar+ sputtering
under ultrahigh vacuum (≤8 × 10−10 Torr) conditions.
Despite use of various sputtering conditions, the pristine
metallic Li 1s signature peak was not observed in the XPS
spectra. Instead, a substantial amount of an oxide phase
(∼72.8%) along with minimal carbonate (∼1%) and residual
sulﬁde (∼0.1%) phases were found to be persistently present
on the Li-metal surface (see SI Tables S1 and S2).
Nevertheless, it represents realistic Li metal anodes used in
batteries, where native oxide layers are ubiquitous. Based on
the diﬀerence in the kinetic energies of the O 1s and Li 1s
photoelectrons, we estimated the average thickness of the
surface oxide layer on Li to be ≥5 nm. It should be noted that
the Li surface layer is likely to have heterogeneous thickness
and composition due to the high energy Ar+ sputtering process
utilized here. The LiS4
−anion was identiﬁed (m/z = 134.9)
from a polysulﬁde solution using electrospray ionization mass
spectrometry (ESI-MS) and, subsequently, isolated and
deposited on a sputter cleaned Li-metal surface using ISL
(Figures 1a and S1).
The in situ XPS analysis was performed through in vacuo
transfer of the sample from the ISL deposition to the XPS
analysis chamber. It should be noted that ions soft landed on
the Li metal surface have a Gaussian-like spatial distribution in
coverage due to the nature of the mass-selected ion beam
generated by the soft landing instrument. In order to eliminate
the inﬂuence of layer thickness and ion density and
characterize the interface where soft landed ions meet the Li
surface, XPS analyses were performed on the edge of the
deposited spot where the distribution is equivalent to a
monolayer or less of soft landed ions.

Spontaneous reduction of sulfur into the sulﬁde phase is
expected at pristine Li-metal surfaces (eq 3). However,
considering the dominant oxide phase and scarcity of pristine
Li-metal on the surface, a surface oxide assisted reduction of
sulfur is also plausible. Our previous ab initio computational
study revealed that a nanometric Li2O layer on Li can
accommodate up to 8 electrons transferred from the
underlying Li-metal.1 This electron-rich Li2O surface layer
may act as an electron donor (up to 2e−per sulfur atom) for
sulﬁde reduction. Similarly, the presence of sulfate and sulﬁte
phases indicates multielectron sulfur oxidation processes (up
to 7e−per sulfur atom). For example, a terminal sulfur atom
(St
−) in the polysulﬁde species may transfer 5e−and 7e−to the
Li2O/Li metal surface as part of a multistep oxidative process
to yield the observed sulﬁte and sulfate species, respectively.
Sulfate has been identiﬁed as an integral part of SEI layer
evolution during the Li−S cycling process.34,35 However, no
explanation regarding the origin and reaction pathways for
sulfate phases were provided, probably due to complexities in
analyzing and delineating the multitude of reactants at the
interface. For example, the sulfate component of the SEI layer
in a Li−S cell may be linked to (a) oxidative reaction of the
sulfonyl component in bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(TFSI) anions, (b) polysulﬁde radicals reacting with oxygen-
containing solvent molecules (e.g., dioxolane), or (c)
polysulﬁde oxidation at surface lithium oxide. By using ISL,
we precisely controlled the interfacial constituents and gained
direct evidence of polysulﬁde oxidation at oxide rich Li-
surfaces. In addition, we inferred that the surface passivation
eﬃciency of the lithium oxide layer might depend on local
composition and topology, as evident in the presence of both
reduced sulﬁde (S2−) and oxidized sulfate at oxide rich Li-
metal.
To correlate spatial regions of the oxidative and reductive
evolution of decomposed sulfur-based products, we performed
two-dimensional chemical mapping (800 μm2) using imaging
XPS. The spatial distribution of reduction and oxidation
products of LiS4
−anions soft landed on the Li2O/Li metal
surface is presented in Figure 1f. These spatially resolved XPS
measurements (resolution of ∼5 μm) were performed at the
same spot on the sample where the S 2p core-level spectra
(Figure 1c) were obtained with a focus at 169 (SO4
2−), 167
(SO3
2−), and 160 eV (S2−) binding energies to analyze the
distribution of decomposed sulfur species. The imaging XPS
spectra revealed that reduced lithium sulﬁde species and
oxidized sulfate/sulﬁte species mostly nucleated in diﬀerent
regions of the surface oxide layer with notable overlap (∼20%).
Overall, both the core-level and imaging XPS analyses imply
that the surface oxide layer is amenable to direct electron
exchange with polysulﬁde species leading to both oxidative and
reductive interfacial reactions. Additionally, the presence of
sulfate as a dominant product along with a lower concentration
of sulﬁte species (Figure 1b) indicates that soft landed
polysulﬁde undergoes multistep oxidation processes with
sulﬁte to sulfate conversion as a possible rate limiting step.
Ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were
performed in order to obtain atomistic insight into the
multielectron reduction and/or oxidation processes of
polysulﬁdes on various Li-based surfaces that are observed
experimentally. Surface chemistries representing pristine Li-
metal (100 facet), a nanometric layer of Li2O over Li (Li2O/
Li), and bulk Li2O were analyzed. Less common surface
chemistries, such as Li-metal (111 facet) along with LiOH and
Li2CO3 layers were also simulated but did not yield
substantially diﬀerent reaction products with polysulﬁde
molecules within the time scale of the AIMD simulations
(see the SI). Each surface conﬁguration was run with a Li2S4
molecule until stable decomposition products or no further
changes were observed (with a time ceiling of 15 ps). We
presumed that the structure of the neutral Li2S4 molecule is
similar to the LiS4
−anions studied experimentally, as this
substitution enables us to track charge transfer processes
without the inﬂuence of polarization in the simulation box.36
The neutral Li2S4 molecules were used in AIMD simulations to
establish a charge-neutral interfacial system, as it simpliﬁes the
model and reduces computational errors. The Li−S bond
cleavage leading to the dissociated LiS4 anion (same as the
soft-landed species) is the primary step observed in the AIMD
simulations. In the presence of surface layers with negatively
charged terminal atoms (such as oxygen and ﬂuorine) the Li
dissociation from neutral Li2S4 is even more pronounced due
to electrostatically driven interactions of Li+ at the surface.
The Li2S4 species were initially oriented so that the Li were
closest to the surface. Because of this orientational constraint,
only one initial AIMD geometry for each surface/species
combination was explored. All calculations were repeated with
a (Li2S4)3 molecule and similar reaction products were
observed. These ﬁndings provide a self-consistent veriﬁcation
of the initial geometries and system conditions used in the
simulations. The results obtained from these calculations
correspond well with what was observed experimentally,
indicating that a neutral molecule in this orientation is an
accurate representation of the anion and experimental surface.
To fully examine the kinetically limited reactions and
account for an additional kinetic energy of ∼5 eV that was
imparted to the ions during the ISL experiments, simulations
were performed at temperatures of both 300 and 350 K for the
bulk Li2O surface. No substantial changes in the reaction
products were observed with increased temperature. There-
fore, all further simulations were run at 300 K. As expected, the
Li2S4 molecules were fully reduced to Li2S within 1 ps at the
pristine Li(100) surface.
We also repeated the simulation with a methodology similar
to that of Angarra et al. where the Li2S4 species were placed 11
Å above the Li2O surface and given an initial velocity toward
the surface corresponding to an impact energy of 5 eV.37,38 In
this calculation we observed the formation of the same reaction
products as in our other simulations and no noticeable eﬀect
on the surface morphology (see Figure S8 and the SI videos).

repeated ion deposition are beyond the scope of what is
observable on the size- and time-scale of AIMD. The relatively

The temporal evolution of the calculated Bader charges of
various interfacial constituents reveals electron transfer path-
ways, as shown in Figure 2a. Li2S4 consists of two terminal S
atoms (St
−) with monovalent negative charge and two bridging
S atoms (Sb
0) with neutral charge. The reduction process is
driven by both electron and Li+ transfer from the top of the
Li(100) metal surface to the terminal sulﬁde (St
−) atom,
followed by the bridging sulfur (Sb
0) atoms, leading to
formation of a lithium sulﬁde (S2−) phase. Conversely, for
both the nanometric and bulk Li2O substrates, an oxidization
rather than sulﬁde reduction process was observed (Figure 2b,
c). Speciﬁcally, the bulk Li2O acts as an electron acceptor and
oxidizes the terminal sulﬁde (St
−) of Li2S4 to form a sulfur-
oxide (−S−S2+=Ox) type phase. We observed the transfer of
up to 4e−from the terminal sulﬁde atom of Li2S4 to the Li2O
lattice, leading to increased electron density within the
subsurface Li−O lattice (Figure 2c). The sulfur-oxide phase
is likely to be a transient metastable intermediate that is part of
a multistep oxidation of Li2S4 to stable sulﬁte (SO3
2−) and
sulfate (SO4
2−) species, as observed in the experimental high-
resolution S 2p XPS spectra.
At the nanometric Li2O substrate [i.e., ∼1 nm Li2O surface
layer on Li-metal (Li−Li2O)] the oxidative process was
relatively slower (∼4 ps) than on bulk Li2O (∼1 ps). This
sluggish charge transfer to the nanometric Li2O layer is due to
the underlying Li-metal, which acts as a competing electron
source and impedes oxidation of Li2S4. The transfer of electron
density from the underlying Li-metal surface to the
neighboring oxygen atoms of Li2O makes the rest of the
oxygen sublattice less prone to receive electrons from the
polysulﬁde (Figure 2b). Interestingly, none of these charge
transfer processes were observed on nanometric LiOH on Li-
metal and bulk Li2CO3 substrates within the time scale of the
AIMD simulations (SI Figures S4 and S5), indicating a higher
energy barrier for electron transfer with polysulﬁde species on
these surfaces.
Although reduction of Li2S4 on Li2O−Li was not observed
within the time scale of the AIMD simulations, it is possible
that the oxygen sublattice may transfer excess electron density
to the sulfur atoms of Li2S4 and initiate the reduction process.
On the other hand, the reaction of Li2S4 with bulk Li2O
induces a substantial change in the charge distribution and
coordination length of the lattice compared to nanometric
Li2O (Figures 2d, e and S6). Radial distribution function
(RDF) plots presented in Figure S6 show pronounced changes
in the Li−O bond length within the bulk Li2O system
compared to the Li2O/Li(100) layer, which is in agreement
with the relatively higher e−charge density on the Li2O system
after Li2S4 reaction. Substantial changes in the oxygen
coordination arrangements of bulk Li2O are also evident in
Figure 3d,e and plots of the RDFs in Figure S7. The RDF plots
show signiﬁcant changes in Li−O and O−O bond lengths in
the bulk Li2O system compared to Li2O/Li(100) layers. This
indicates active participation of O and Li atoms in the redox
reaction of trimeric polysulﬁde, resulting in more pronounced
changes in their coordination environment than monomeric
reactions. It also raises a quite interesting question about the
role of intramolecular interactions between neighboring Li2S4
molecules in the redox process.
In addition to the Li surface chemistry, the inter- and
intramolecular interactions of polysulﬁde species may inﬂuence
the energetics of the redox process. For example, our previous
studies have revealed clustering of polysulﬁde species leading
to higher order multimeric forms (e.g., dimers and trimers) in
electrolyte solutions.1,2 Hence, it is likely that crowding of
anionic LiS4
−on the Li-metal substrate prepared by ISL
(surface coverage of ∼7 × 1011 ions/mm2 with a Gaussian
radial spatial distribution) may lead to clustering and
subsequent evolution into multimeric polysulﬁde structures.
To understand the eﬀect of multimeric structure on the
polysulﬁde redox process, we modeled the Li6S12 (trimer) with
diﬀerent Li surface chemistries (vide supra). Li6S12 consists of
six terminal S (St
−) and six bridging S (Sb
0) atoms. On a
pristine Li(001) surface, the trimer cluster undergoes stepwise
sulﬁde reduction within a picosecond (Figure 3a) starting from
the terminal and followed by the bridging sulfur atoms.
Interestingly, a similar reduction process was also observed at
the Li2O/Li(100) surface, where a bridging Sb
0 and terminal
St
−atom of Li6S12 gained an electron within a picosecond.
Unlike the monomeric species, the trimeric polysulﬁde cluster
readily accepts electrons from the oxygen sublattice of the
nanometric Li2O layer on Li-metal and undergoes a
spontaneous reduction process (Figure 3b). Conversely,
Li6S12 undergoes an oxidation process on the bulk Li2O
surface with relatively faster electron transfer than observed for
the monomeric species (Figure 3c). This enhanced reactivity
of trimeric Li6S12 can be attributed to a greater number of
conformal sites that can interact with the Li2O surface, whereas
monomeric Li2S4 is restricted by energetics of orientations and
inherent conformal inﬂexibilities. The enhanced reactivity of
Li6S12 with bulk Li2O results in splitting of the trimeric cluster
and subsequent oxidation of two bridging and terminal sulfur
atoms within the AIMD time scale (Figure 3f). Substantial
changes in the oxygen coordination are shown in Figure 3d, e
and plots of the RDF are shown in Figure S6. The RDF plots
show substantial changes in Li−O and O−O bond lengths for
the bulk Li2O system compared to the Li2O/Li(100) layer.
These changes indicate active participation of O and Li atoms
in the redox reaction of the trimeric polysulﬁde, resulting in
more pronounced changes in their coordination environment
than in monomer reactions. The active participation of
sublayers of Li2O suggests that crystal defects (a ubiquitous
component of oxide layers) may be an inﬂuential factor in the
polysulﬁde redox process.
We hypothesize that these transient defects in the surface
oxide layer may play any or all the role as an initiator, inhibitor,
and product in the observed redox process. For example,
consumption of lattice oxygen during oxidation of sulfur to a
sulﬁte phase may lead to formation of three oxygen vacancies
(3VO
••, in Kroger−Vink notation) within the Li2O surface.
Similarly, reduction of sulfur to sulﬁde may create a lithium
vacancy (VLi″) in the surface. These O and Li vacancies may
generate delocalized charges and serve as active redox centers
for the reactions of subsequent polysulﬁde molecules.39 The
possible evolution of these vacancy sites and electron transfer
at the surface oxide layer may initiate a cascading polysulﬁde
redox process, as shown in Figure 4. Experimental and
theoretical conﬁrmation of such a surface defect evolution
during the redox process of polysulﬁdes will require more in-
depth study, which will be the focus of future work.

−), isolated from electrolyte solution based on their mass/
charge ratio, were soft landed on oxide rich Li-metal surfaces
under high vacuum conditions. Our combined XPS and ab
initio MD studies revealed multielectron (up to 6e−per sulfur

formation of sulfate and sulﬁte phases on oxide rich Li-metal
surfaces. The reaction pathway depends on local surface
chemistry and multimeric structures of polysulﬁde molecules.
The polysulﬁde redox process will generate vacancies and
augment electron density across the oxide sublayer of the Li2O
phase, thereby catalyzing further interfacial reactions. Surface
heterogeneities of Li-metal currently impede further exper-
imental analysis of defect evolution, electron transfer, and
distribution mechanisms. Nevertheless, our novel study
elucidates the molecular details of previous reports regarding
the presence of sulfate and sulﬁte phases as part of the
evolution of SEI layers in Li−S batteries.35 Overall, the ISL
technique, combined with in situ spectroscopic character-
ization, provides an unprecedented opportunity to custom
assemble well-deﬁned interfaces and obtain predictive under-
standing of SEI layer evolution.
On a broader note, this work represents an important
conceptual leap forward in our ability to predict the speciﬁc
reactivity of polysulﬁde anions, an electroactive ion formed
during Li−S battery operation, at the reactive lithium metal
anode. Our multimodal platform provides direct measure of
the isolated reactivity of critical electroactive species within the
complex SEI layer evolution by selectively engaging electrolyte
