Thick lithium foil (500 μm) and thin lithium foil (75 μm) were purchased from China Energy

(DOL) were kindly provided by Tinci Materials Technology Co. Ltd. 
1.2 Synthesis of solvents 
2,5-Dimethyl-1,2,5-thiadiazolidine 1-oxide (CSA). Firstly, anhydrous THF (120 mL), TEA 
(12 mL), and DMEDA (5.4 mL) were added to the round bottom flask, and the mixture was 
cooled to −78°C using a liquid nitrogen/acetone cold bath under N2 atmosphere. Then, SOCl2 
(3.6 mL) was added dropwise, and the reaction was stirred for 1 h. Afterwards, the mixture was 
maintained at room temperature for 10 h. The crude mixture was washed with anhydrous THF. 
The residue was extracted with diethyl ether and filtered again. The obtained organic phases 
were concentrated under reduced pressure. The colorless product (CSA) was purified by three 
successive rounds of reduced pressure distillation with a yield of 80%. 1H NMR (600 MHz, 
CDCl3, δ/ppm): 3.55~3.26 (m, 4H), 2.78~2.75 (m, 6H). 13C-NMR (150 MHz, CDCl3, δ/ppm): 
52.67, and 34.43.

1.6 Preparation of sulfur cathodes 
Preparation of Li2S6 cathode: The Li2S6 solution was prepared by dissolving sulfur powder 
and Li2S (1:5 by molar) in DME. 40 μL Li2S6 solution was dropped onto carbon fiber cloth 
disks (12 mm diameter) and dried at 60°C for 12 h in an argon-filled glovebox. The active sulfur 
loading (calculated based on the mass of sulfur atoms) was controlled to approximately 1.4 mg 
cm−2. 
Preparation of KB/S cathode: sulfur and Ketjen Black (KB) were first mixed in a batch with 
a mass ratio of 7:3. This mixture was then combined with carbon nanotubes and LA133 binder 
at a mass ratio of 77.5:12.5:10 in water/isopropanol to form a slurry. The obtained slurry was 
then coated on carbon-coated Al foil and freeze-dried at −80°C for 15 h to fabricate porous 
structure sulfur cathode, achieving efficient electrolyte penetration. The areal sulfur loading 
was in the range of 1 to 9 mg cm−2. The sulfur cathode was cut into circular disks with a diameter 
of 12 mm.

1.8 Assembly and evaluation of Li−S pouch cell  
The Li−S pouch cells were constructed under dry room conditions (dew point < −40°C). The 
double-sided LMAs (75 μm thickness on a single side) roll-bonded onto copper foil (4.0 × 7.0 
cm−2). The single-side areal sulfur loading was 8.2 mg cm−2. The pouch cells were applied with 
a E/S ratio of 2.9 ge gS−1, and galvanostatically cycled within a voltage window of 1.70-2.55 V. 
The gravimetric energy density was calculated using the total mass of the pouch cells, 
accounting for all components: anodes, cathodes, electrolyte, separators, Al/Cu current 
collectors, tabs, and package.

2.1 Thermodynamic analysis of the dissolution process 
The dissolution of solutes (e.g., lithium salts) in solvents is governed by the second law of 
thermodynamics. The thermodynamic requirement for spontaneous dissolution is a negative 
change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG < 0), where ΔG = G2 – G1. Here, G1 represents the Gibbs free 
energy of the separated solute and solvent, and G2 represents that of the homogeneous solution. 
If ΔG > 0, dissolution is non-spontaneous, indicating poor solubility. If ΔG = 0, the system 
attains a dynamic dissolution–precipitation 
equilibrium. This thermodynamic 
criterion 
determines the spontaneity of dissolution. The dissolution process primarily involves two steps: 
(i) dissociation of the solute lattice and (ii) solvation of the dissociated ions. 
(i) Lattice dissociation. The Gibbs free energy change (∆Glat) for breaking the solute lattice 
is expressed as following equation (6): 
∆Glat = ∆Hlat − T∆Slat 
= ∆U + p∆V − T∆Slat, ΔV ≈ 0 
≈ ΔUlat − T∆Slat                                                                                    (6) 
where ∆Hlat is the lattice disruption enthalpy change; ∆Slat is the lattice disruption entropy 
change; T is the absolute temperature. For the whole solid-state systems, the volume change of 
the lattice disruption is negligible (ΔV ≈ 0), rendering the pΔV term negligible. 
(ii) Ionic solvation. The Gibbs free energy change (∆Gsol) is expressed as the equation (7): 
∆Gsol = ∆Hsol − T∆Ssol                                                                            (7)

2.2 Solubility and compatibility of Region I solvents for Li−S batteries 
Both LiTFSI and LiNO3 are critical solutes in electrolyte for Li−S batteries, yet LiNO3 
exhibits significantly lower solubility than LiTFSI in carbonate-based solvents (Region I). This 
discrepancy arises from that NO3− possesses a higher charge density than TFSI−, resulting in a 
substantially higher lattice energy for LiNO3 (Figure S2).28 Meanwhile, the higher DN of NO3− 
(22.2 kcal mol−1) compared to carbonate solvents (DN < 19 kcal mol−1) leads to an insufficient 
solvation enthalpy (∆Ηsol) to overcome the lattice energy (∆Ulat) of LiNO3. Consequently, 
LiNO3 displays ultralow solubility (< 0.1 M) in carbonate-based electrolyte.29 Moreover, for 
Li−S batteries, LiPSs act as strongly nucleophiles that attack carbonate solvents (e.g., EC or 
PC) via nucleophilic substitution, yielding thiocarbonates and lithium alkoxides (Figure S3). 
This side reaction not only hinders the conversion of long-chain LiPSs to short-chain LiPSs but 
also depletes carbonate solvents, ultimately terminating the discharging process (Figure S4). 
Owing to the poor solubility of LiNO3 and chemical incompatibility with LiPSs, Region I 
solvents (primarily carbonates) are unsuitable for practical Li−S batteries.
