A B S T R A C T
Ether based electrolyte exhibits extraordinarily high lithium-ion conductivity at low temperature due to inherent 
low melting point and strong coordination strength of ether solvents. However, such fascinating properties do not 
bring desired low-temperature electrochemical performances of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. One of the 
critical challenges is the tendency to generate massive Li polysulfide (LiPS) clusters at sub-zero temperature, 
which hinders mass transport and retards polysulfide lithiation during discharge. Declustering LiPSs at low 
temperature is a prerequisite endeavour to expedite the reaction kinetics. Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) has been 
ubiquitously employed to protect Li anode, while its strong coordination strength to break LiPS clusters and 
generate charged LiPS species remains elusive. This work demonstrates the importance of cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+
species that can facilitate the formation of charged LiPSs. Such charged LiPSs are, experimentally and theoret­
ically, proved to promote the LiPS conversion kinetics at low temperature. It is expected to enrich LiPS solvation 
fundamentals and shed light on unique neutral molecule to charge LiPSs for facile LiPS conversion.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are a superior candidate as a next- 
generation battery system due to the outstanding energy density 
(2600 Wh kg−1) [1–4]. Extending the operation temperature is of great 
importance to tap the potential of Li–S batteries in broader scenarios 
[5–7]. However, imminent issues related to sluggish reduction kinetics 
during liquid–solid conversion impede capacity delivery [8–12]. Espe­
cially under low temperature conditions, Li–S batteries are readily 
arrested without the second discharge plateau capacity [13]. This phe­
nomenon is intuitively contradictory to the properties of ether-based 
electrolyte widely used in Li–S batteries [14,15], as ether solvents 
exhibit low melting point and strong coordination strength to facilitate 
ion migration and favor the population of solvent separated ion pair 
(SSIP) [16]. Nonetheless, recent studies argue that the dominant Li 
polysulfide (LiPS) species in conventional DOL/DME (DOL and DME 
stand for 1,3-dioxolane and ethylene glycol dimethyl ether, 
respectively) media are presented in neutral aggregates (AGGs) or 
high-order clusters due to the strong bonding energy of the Li cation to 
the terminal S atoms of LiPSs [17]. The tendency to generate neutral 
AGGs or high-order clusters is exacerbated at low temperature, which 
causes severe capacity decay and inferior rate capability [18,19].
Clusters that integrate solvents, cations, and anions into one unit 
carry a mild charge density comparing with SSIPs that bear large charges 
to favor rapider mobility and greater activity for smooth redox reactions 
[20,21]. Therefore, a bottleneck to expedite LiPS redox kinetics lies in 
declustering LiPSs and increasing the charged LiPS species. This 
requirement motivates the exploration of high-donor-number or 
high-dielectric-constant solvents and anions, such as dimethyl sulfoxide 
(DMSO), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and lithium trifluoroacetate 
(LiTFA) [22]. The underlying mechanisms of applying these solvents 
and anions are to exert competitive coordination to Li+ over LiPSs an­
ions, creating charged sulfur species for subsequent reduction.
However, too strong coordination strength solvents, like DMSO and

in aprotic Li–S batteries, since the electrophilic moieties of these sol­
vents easily attack nucleophilic LiPSs anions, leading to irreversible 
capacity loss and early cell death [23]. Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) has been 
generally employed to protect Li anode, and recent studies revealed the 
possibility to improve Li–S battery performances at low temperature due 
to the greater anion donicity [24]. However, the in-depth insight in 
interrupting LiPS clusters by employing LiNO3 remains elusive.
One of the critical issues that needs to be answered is whether anion 
(NO3
−) or neutral (LiNO3) species performs well in altering the LiPS 
structure. Since the strong coordination strength nature of NO3
−favors 
the formation of contact ion pair (CIP) between Li+–NO3
−, the thermo­
dynamic principle does not allow the dissolved LiNO3 with CIP structure 
to be initially disassociated into Li+ and NO3
−
and then facilitate the 
uncoordinated NO3
−to capture the Li+ of LiPSs [25,26]. However, the 
addition of LiNO3, experimentally, does favor the redox kinetic of LiPSs 
and benefit the low-temperature performances of Li–S batteries [27]. 
These contractionary clues indicate that LiNO3 with CIP structure 
probably has major impact on LiPS structure.
In this work, two key issues are intended to be addressed: why do 
charged LiPS species exhibit greater redox kinetics, and what are the 
refining LiNO3 molecule structures that enable the greater population of 
charged LiPS species? Molecular hybrid orbitals inform that the unique 
structure of NO3
– with "π" bond enhances electron delocalization, 
endowing it with a possibility to seize the Li+ through forming cationic 
Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ species, which benefits dynamic equilibrium (LiPS +
LiNO3 ↔ Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ + [Li1-δPS]δ−) toward right. The [Li1-δPS]δ−ex­
hibits rapid reduction kinetics, giving a larger capacity from Li2S4 to 
Li2S2/Li2S conversion. This work sheds light on the neutral lithium salt 
in electrolyte to charge LiPSs and refines the insights into LiNO3 in the 
redox reactions of low-temperature Li–S batteries beyond the Li anode 
protection.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. Formation of polysulfide clusters at low temperatures
In an attempt to confirm the aggregation behavior of LiPSs at low 
temperature, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) is per­
formed to probe the chemical shift of 7Li in Li2S4 containing electrolyte 
(Fig. 1a). As the nominal Li2S4 has a very low solubility (<0.01 M) in 
DOL/DME mixture (Fig. S1), a stronger coordination strength G4 (tet­
raethylene glycol dimethyl ether) solvent is selected to dissolve 0.1 M 
Li2S4 [23]. Lowering the temperature leads to the 7Li chemical shift 
toward high field, corresponding to the enhanced electron cloud sur­
rounding 7Li [28]. The electron donating LiPSs anions shield Li by 
gathering them. The tendency of generating LiPS cluster is computa­
tionally verified by calculating the formation energy of representative 
(Li2S4)n with different orders from 1 to 4 (−1.35 to –1.58 eV), and the 
negative values indicates the energetic preferences in developing 
high-order (Li2S4)n clusters (Fig 1b).
The second discharge plateau is characterized by kinetically unfa­
vorable conversion from Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S, which is exacerbated by 
intensified Li2S4 cluster at low temperature, resulting in deteriorating 
the reduction kinetics and terminating the cell at the second discharge 
plateau (Fig. S2) [29]. These experimental results can be theoretically 
rationalized by the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy 
level (Fig. 1c), which approximates to the electronic affinity [30]. The 
lower LUMO energy represents easier tendency to get electrons for LiPSs 
species and manifests better LiPSs reductivity [31]. The LUMO energy of 
(Li2S4)n increases from −1.75 eV to 0.19 eV and further to 0.67 eV when 
the n ranges from 1 to 4, respectively, suggesting that the high-order 
(Li2S4)n clusters retard the LiPSs reductivity. However, the negatively 
charged LiPSs with lower LUMO energy levels exhibits high reduction 
tendency compared to neutral LiPS clusters. The LUMO energy level of 
LiPS species unquestionably hints that the charged LiPSs are easier to be 
reduced.

2.2. LiNO3 competes with polysulfide for Li+
The strong coordinating strength NO3
−favors the Li–NO3 CIP struc­
ture, which is hardly ionized in DOL/DME mixture [34]. This Li–NO3 
configuration in ether-based electrolyte raises the possibility that the 
neutral LiNO3 instead of charged NO3
−takes out the Li from LiPSs. In 
order to analyze the possible interaction between LiNO3 molecule and 
LiPS clusters, Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) of the competition reaction 
is proposed to quantify energetic tendency of various reactions 
(Table S1), and the negative ΔG implies greater tendency in forming the 
charged species (Fig. 2a). The computational results inform that LiNO3 is 
more prone to bind Li+, forming the cationic aggregate (Li+−LiNO3) 
(Fig. 2b). All the formation of Li2n-1S4n show a negative ΔG value, 
indicating that the LiNO3 is thermodynamically favorable to charge the 
LiPSs in electrolyte at 253.15 K. The charged Li2n-1S4n lead to more 
negative charge on the sulfur atoms, which probably benefits lithiation 
during reduction (Fig. S3). The charged LiPSs are also verified through 
the high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) (Fig. 2c). The LiPSs with 
LiNO3 produce a wide variety of charged species, unambiguously indi­
cating the de-lithiation of LiPS clusters. Hereby, two charged LiPSs 
(Li3S8
−and Li7S16
−) are exemplified in this work to confirm the validity of 
electronic affinity (Fig. 1c) and thermodynamical calculation (Fig. 2a), 
which are resulted from the thermodynamically favored reactions 
between LiNO3 and LiPSs clusters, contributing to the larger quantity of 
charged species in the E-[Li2S4-T-N] electrolyte than that of the 
E-[Li2S4-T] electrolyte (Fig. 2d).
The ultraviolet and visible spectrum (UV–vis) with E-[Li2S4-T-N] 
electrolyte shifts toward high wavelength, representing the generation 
of charged LiPSs, since the charged LiPSs weakening the intermolecular 
interactions and thus requiring less vibration energy to exhibit character 
peaks [35]. Besides, the characteristic peak located at about 280 nm in 
the Fig. S4 is attributed to the presence of S4
2−species [36]. The greater 
peak area of E-[Li2S4-T-N] electrolyte in S4

wavenumber suggests 
larger intendency of S4
2−generation.
The mystery of LiNO3 that can charge LiPSs originates from the 
unique LiNO3 structure. LiNO3 takes a special sp2-hybridized structure. 
The outermost orbit of N atom has 5 electrons, three of which participate 
in the formation of σ bonds (with three O atoms) and the remaining two 
are involved in the formation of π bonds. Each of O atoms carries six 
electrons in the outermost obit, with 4 electrons forming 2 paired 
electrons and another two electrons being unpaired. One of unpaired 
electrons contributes to σ bond (with N), while another one involves in 
the π bond. This atomic hybridized structure totally has five electrons 
available for the formation of π bonds, with two electrons from the N 
atom and three electrons from three O atoms (each one donating one 
electron). With the extra electron to enable negatively charged NO3
−, 
there are totally six electrons involved in the formation of the π bond. 
The LiNO3 hybridization collectively contribute to a six-electron large π 
bond on a four-atom center, called π6
4 (Fig. 2e). The molecular hy­
bridization structure with the electron participation can be schemati­
cally illustrated in Fig. 2f (with blue arrows representing the electron 
from O atoms, red arrows representing the electron from N atom, light 
blue arrow representing the extra electron). The unique π6
4 bond is 
exceptionally attractive to the positively charged cations around the 
entire LiNO3 structure, which endows more than one Li cations sur­
rounding the NO3
−anion. This assumption can be confirmed by the 
electrostatic potential (Fig. S5). The Table S6 shows the polar surface

2.3. Directing Li solvation and transportation by charged LiPSs at low 
temperature
The high Li affility of LiNO3 tailors the solvation structure of both Li+
and LiPSs, which remarkablely impacts the redox kinetics of LiPSs, 
especially at low temperature. Theoretical calculations and experi­
mental verifications are employed to unveil the consequence of 
Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ on LiPS solvation. The parameters adopted for the calcu­
lation are available in Table S2 and the results are demonstrated in 
Fig. 3a. The quantity of coordinated Li+ surrounding NO3
−
center is 
identified to be 2.01 at the distance of 3.89 Å, which evidently indicates 
the generation of Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ in the first coordination shell. The Li-rich 
Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ species are capable of causing the starved Li in LiPS clus­
ters due to the equilibrium, leading to the change of structure and charge 
of LiPSs. The tendency of Li+ to bind S4
2−was inhibited by the addition of 
LiNO3 (Fig. S6a), dropping from 1.68 to 1.03 at the distance of 3 Å 
(Fig. S6c). Besides, the dynamic simulation at room temperature also 
indicates the LiPS charging effect by LiNO3 (Fig. S6b and d). The charged 
tendency of LiPSs at low temperature shows more sensitive compared 
with the one at room temperature. The g(r) of S4
2−
to Li+ attenuates 
36.92 % at low temperature compared with the room temperature 
condition (34.34 %).
Clustering LiPSs intensifies cation–anion–solvent interaction within 
a local unit, enhancing the energy barrier for subsequent reductions, 
which causes the curtail of second discharge plateau of Li–S cells. In 
order to exam the reaction kinetics of charged LiPSs imparted by the 
cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+, the Li2S2/Li2S nucleation, which is considered as 
the sluggish conversion from easily tangled Li2S4, was tested at low 
temperatures through potential static measurement (Fig. S7) and the 
quantity of Li2S2/Li2S nucleation is charted in Fig. 3b [39]. Two elec­
trolyte formulations are named in E-[Li2S8-T], E-[Li2S8-T-N], in which T 
and N represents LiTFSI and LiNO3, respectively. The capacity of 
deposited Li2S2/Li2S with the additive of LiNO3 is about three times 
higher than that of the routine electrolyte, corresponding to the facile 
conversion from Li2S4 species to Li2S2/Li2S. The cell with E-[T-N] 
electrolyte delivers higher redox peaks and narrower voltage gap than 
that of E-[T] based cell (Fig. S8). Besides, The Li-deficient nature of the 
charged polysulfides, E-[Li2S4-T-N] lacking lithium exhibits lower acti­
vation energies (40.53 kJ mol−1) (Fig. S9). The shuttle current of the cell 
with E-[T-N] is much smaller than that of the cell with E-[T] at around 
2.3 V that are dominated by soluble LiPSs. It is presumed that the 
negatively charged LiPSs tend to move to the positive electrode, which is 
also an important factor to affect shuttle effect beside the Li anode 
protection by LiNO3 (Fig. S10).
The charged LiPSs facilitate their migration in electrolytes, which is 
confirmed by measurement of ionic conductivity based on electro­
chemical impedance spectra (Table S3), delivering 28.5, 104.3 and 
158.6 μS cm–1 for E-[Li2S4], E-[Li2S4-T] and E-[Li2S4-T-N] (Fig. 3c), 
respectively. The introduction of LiTFSI in E-[Li2S4] increases ionic 
conductivity due to the LiTFSI dissociation. Moreover, a noticeable 
enhancement of ionic conductivity (nearly 50 % increment) is observed

Furthermore, it is considered that LiNO3 alters the migration 
mechanism of Li+, since cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ leads to the presence of 
charged while aggregated LiPSs, the former of which commonly exerts 
vehicle ionic migration while the latter of which adopts hopping 
migration [40]. The internuclear distances between Li+ and represen­
tative O of DME are monitored by MD trajectory for supporting this 
speculation. An invariable distance of Li+ with its solvation sheath im­
plies the vehicle mechanism when Li+ moves across the electrolyte. On 
contrast, a variable distance between Li+ and solvent means that Li+ rids 
of initial environment and participates into a new solvation structure, 
signifying the hopping mechanism. Totally 10 Li+ dynamic trajectories 
are abstracted from E-[Li2S4-T-N] formulation and 3 of them are 
randomly highlighted to visualize the general Li+ migration pathways 
(Fig. 3d). Especially, it is noticed that a significant curve transition from 
4.31 Å to 9.25 Å (Pathway-2) appears at 7 ps, representing that Li+ hops 
from the designed DME and re-coordinates with another DME. This 
hopping mechanism could provide sufficient Li+ for accelerating the 
lithiation of charged LiPSs and promote battery performance.
2.4. Low temperature battery performances enabled by cationic 
Liδ[LiNO3]δ+
The important role of cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ at low temperatures is to 
charge LiPSs and propel their redox kinetics, which has the potential to 
improve rate and cyclic capability of Li–S batteries. The electrochemical 
performance of the cells with E-[T-N] and E-[T] electrolyte are depicted 
in Fig. 4. The cell with E-[T-N] demonstrates a capacity of 816 mAh g−1 
at 0.1 C and achieves excellent reversibility even after cycling at 0.5 C 
comparing with E-[T] based cell (Fig. 4a). This improvement sub­
stantiates the high reactivity of middle-order LiPSs, manifesting an 
enlarged capacity of second plateau (Fig. 4b). E-[T-N] based cell delivers 
a high capacity of 923 mA h g–1, superior to the E-[T] based cell (453 
mAh g–1). In addition, the cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ also helps to build 
robust solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) to mitigate the shuttle effect and 
improve cycle stability (Fig. 4c). The cell with E-[T-N] yield stable 
discharge capacity (923 mAh g−1 for the 1st cycle and maintaining 70 % 
after 200 cycles) and Coulombic efficiency (approximately 99 %).
Besides, the implication of different Li salts concentration has been 
compared in Fig. S12 and S13 (refers to the concentration of LiTFSI and 
LiNO3). The recent electrochemical performances of Li–S batteries are 
listed in Table S8. The low temperature Li–S batteries are quite unsat­
isfied comparing with room temperature, suggesting the severity of LiPS 
clustering at low temperature. It’s also interesting to study the charged 
reaction of LiPSs at high temperature. The cells could endure the 0.5 C to 
discharge at 55 ◦C stably (Fig. S14).

has focused on taming the solvent coordinating strength to mitigate 
shuttle effect and decrease the de-solvation energy [41–43]. Such an 
approach fascinates the formation of LiPS clusters, which impacts redox 
kinetics 
at 
sub-zero 
conditions. 
Another 
strategy 
takes 
high-coordinating strength anions, like NO3

LiNO3 remain unclear, and the neutral LiNO3 or anionic NO3

perform well in dictating LiPS structure is rather debated and ambig­
uous. This work deliberately probes the special hybridized type and π 
bond formation of LiNO3, and identify the cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ to 
charge LiPSs, resulting in high reduction reactivity. This distinctive role 
of LiNO3 enriches the Li–S chemistry beyond its protection of Li metal 
anode.

3. Conclusion
Based on the unique LiNO3 structure, this work identifies the 
distinctive role of cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ to facilitate charged LiPSs, 
which are experimentally and computationally verified to propel 
reduction kinetics comparing with neutral LiPSs. Especially, the LiPS 
species during reduction is corroborated by electron affinity. Incorpo­
rating LiNO3 into electrolyte is capable of charging LiPSs through seizing 
the Li+, which drive the equilibrium (LiPS ↔ δLi+ + [Li1-δPS]δ−) toward 
right, and thereby increasing ionic conductivity and reduction reactivity 
of charged LiPSs. The intrinsic strong affinity between neutral LiNO3 and 
Li+ originates from unique electrostatic potential surrounding the LiNO3 
molecule, which is resulted from unique π6
4 bond with extensively de- 
localized electron cloud. The cationic Liδ[LiNO3]δ+ enables charge car­
rier sulfur species and benefits Li transportation in both vehicle and 
hoping mechanisms. The enhanced LiPS reactivity and facilitated Li 
transportation collectively contribute to improved rate and cyclic per­
formances of Li−S batteries. This work highlights the critical role of 
neutral Li salts instead of strong-coordination-strength NO3
−anion in the 
mediation of LiPS structures, and refines the fundamental mechanism of 
LiNO3 in Li–S batteries.
