A B S T R A C T
In the context of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery technology, LiNO3 is known to improve performance by
protecting the lithium anode via the formation of an optimized solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) as well
as suppressing the associated lithium polysulﬁdes shuttle effect during cycling. Herein, the concentration
of added LiNO3 (0.05–0.4 mol kg1) in a C4mpyr-TFSI- organic mixed electrolyte has been varied, with any
changes in cell performance monitored against the physical (viscosity) and ion-transport (NMR-based
ion diffusion and conductivity) properties of each variant. We ﬁnd that an electrolyte with 0.1 mol kg1
LiNO3 shows the best performance and that this is because this electrolyte has the highest conductivity,
lowest viscosity and shows the lowest glass transition temperature (Tg), measured with differential
scanning calorimetry (DSC). While the long-term beneﬁts of adding lithium nitrate to the electrolyte of
Li-S cells are known to be related to effects centred on the lithium anode, the short-term inﬂuence of this
additive on capacity performance is clearly related to promoting better access to the porous cathode. The
range of concentration over which this effect is operative (0.05–0.20 mol kg1) overlaps with the range
recommended for optimum performance of the lithium anode.

1. Introduction
In spite of the many advantages of lithium-sulfur batteries, in
particular the high theoretical speciﬁc energy (2600 Wh kg1),
limitations due to the shuttle phenomenon associated with the
solubility of polysulﬁde intermediates and anode corrosion hinder
the application of Li-S as a replacement for conventional Li-ion
batteries. The solubility of the polysulﬁde intermediates leads to a
permanent loss of sulfur as the active redox species from the
cathode during cycling [1]. In addition, the formation of dendrites
during the deposition of lithium, which is dependent on electrolyte
composition, leads to safety issues in lithium batteries [2–4]. For
these reasons, surface modiﬁcation and creating a proper solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers are required on the lithium
anode [5]. One approach in this regard is to use a compatible
additive in the electrolyte to modify the electrode/electrolyte
interphase in this system [6–11]. Mikhaylik from Sion Power Corp.,
has patented a method that employs N-O bond containing
additives, such as LiNO3, in the electrolyte to suppress the PS
shuttle effect [12]. Subsequent research conﬁrmed this claim in
which the effects of this additive in Li-S battery with organic-based
electrolyte have been discussed [1,6,13–19]. Indeed, this additive
protects the lithium anode by promoting the formation of an
effective, homogeneous SEI layer and consequently preventing or
minimising chemical and electrochemical reduction of polysul-
ﬁdes on the anode [14,17].
Various LiNO3 concentrations between 0.1 and 0.5 M have been
tested by different research groups (see Table 1), but only a few
groups have studied the inﬂuence of varying the concentration of
LiNO3 on the performance of Li-S batteries [1,6]. Kim et al. [1]
showed that, although LiNO3 suppresses the polysulﬁde shuttle
effect, the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte decreases when
LiNO3 concentration is increased due to increase in viscosity. They
reported an optimum amount of 0.2 M LiNO3 in an electrolyte
solution

(TEGDME)/1,3-Dioxolane (DOL). These works focussed on Li-S

With the prospect of combining the beneﬁts of LiNO3,
suppressing the redox shuttle of lithium polysulﬁdes, with the
effectiveness of ionic liquids in stabilising lithium metal surfaces
against detrimental morphology changes during cycling, we have
recently studied in detail the SEI layer properties of the lithium
metal anode and the performance of respective Li-S batteries
containing electrolytes based on the ionic liquid 1-butyl-1-
methylpyrrolidinium 
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(C4mpyr-TFSI) [20]. Given that this previous study was restricted
to a single concentration of LiNO3, here we investigate the
performance of this mixed electrolyte for different concentrations
of LiNO3, with a view to optimising the use of this additive.
Accordingly, we systematically vary the concentration of LiNO3 in a
series of mixed organic–ionic-liquid electrolyte solutions while
examining any correlations between solution physical properties
and the electrical performance of Li-S cells constructed with each
electrolyte variant. Results suggest that linked variations of
conductivity and viscosity with nitrate concentration can be
directly related to cathode capacity, which in turn limits cell
performance.

3. Results
3.1. Inﬂuence of LiNO3 concentration on the performance of Li-S cells
As discussed previously [20], the presence of LiNO3 additive in
this electrolyte leads to the formation of a more stable SEI layer and
consequently better capacity retention of Li-S cell. Fig. 1 compares
the discharge capacity of Li-S cells with electrolytes containing
different amounts of LiNO3 (see Table 2). From these data a
dependence on LiNO3 concentration is apparent which is, however,
not linear. Firstly, the effect of this salt on maintaining capacity in
the early stages of cycling is obvious from the steep decay in
capacity in the absence of LiNO3, El0. Secondly, over approximately
40 cycles, cells containing electrolytes of a LiNO3 concentration of
up to 0.15 mol kg1 show a signiﬁcantly higher capacity than those
with [LiNO3]  0.25 and 0.4 mol kg1. Among these, cells with
[LiNO3]  0.05 and 0.15 mol kg1 maintain the highest initial
capacity (900 mAhg1) for nearly 8 cycles before gradually
decreasing, over the next 10 cycles, to a level approaching that of
cells with [LiNO3]  0.1 mol kg1. The reason for this behaviour is
not immediately apparent. Thirdly, between the three lower
concentrations, El2 with 0.1 mol kg1 [LiNO3], shows the best long
term capacity retention, albeit the performance of El3 is tracking
that of El2 for nearly 60 cycles before more erratic behaviour sets
in. In the case of the cell containing El1 (0.05 LiNO3 mol kg1), the
initially high capacity fades steadily after 40 cycles and decreases
within 40 cycles to the level observed for El0. This result is
entirely consistent with observations by others that there is a net
consumption of nitrate during the formation of the SEI on the
lithium electrode [18]. Hence, electrolyte El1 is effectively
converted to El0 within a few tens of charge–discharge cycles.
An obvious recommendation that arises is that this consumption
must be considered when setting the initial concentration of
LiNO3.
For further comparison, the performance of cells with electro-
lytes El2, El4, and El5 were also tested at 50 C. As shown in Fig. 2,
all cells show similar discharge capacity with high initial capacity
above 1000 mAh g1 due to increase in ionic conductivity (and
decrease in viscosity) at higher temperature. Notably, at the
elevated temperature there are no signiﬁcant differences in initial
capacity observed as is the case at ambient temperature. Over the
next 20 cycles capacities decline to around 500 mAh g1, which
could be due to higher polysulﬁde solubility at this temperature.
3.2. Transport properties and DSC measurements as a function of
LiNO3 concentration
For an assessment of the underlying reasons causing the LiNO3
concentration dependent cycling behaviour we have measured the
main properties that govern ion transport (h,s, D), along with
phase-temperature data (DSC), for each of the electrolyte solutions
under investigation. Viscosity and conductivity data are repre-
sented in Fig. 3 and summarised in Table 2. As expected, viscosities
increase with increasing LiNO3 concentration, however, the values
for the ﬁrst two concentrations are almost the same. A signiﬁcant
increase is observed only at [LiNO3] 	0.15 mol kg1. This behaviour
is also mirrored in the ionic conductivity data for the largest part of
the concentration spectrum, except for the ﬁrst two concentrations
where s(El1) < s(El2), after which s steadily decreases. The
occurrence of a point where ion conductivity goes through a
maximum over a range of lithium salt concentrations is commonly
observed in electrolytes [36,37]. The conductivity increases by
adding LiNO3 up to 0.1 mol kg1 due to more available charge
carriers, however, at higher concentration, with increasing the
number of ions, the ion association is also increased which leads to
the decrease in the number of charge carriers and their mobility
[36,37]. Therefore at [LiNO3] 	0.15 mol kg1, the conductivity
decreases due to more association of solvate species. It is worth
noting that a minimum of 0.1 mol kg1 of nitrate salt is needed to

having the smallest size among all other ions, it has the lowest

being present in solution, these must undergo fast ligand exchange
since only one single resonance is observed in the Li-NMR at
temperatures above 25 C. Furthermore, the TFSI anion has a
smaller diffusion coefﬁcient compared to the pyrrolidinium cation
which is not only common in ionic liquids but also correlates to the
fact that the former does not diffuse as a single ion. Indeed the
complexation of lithium to form [Li(TFSI)n](n1) species, leads to
slower diffusion of both Li+ and TFSI ions, compared to the ionic
liquid cation, which is in agreement with previous reports
[31,35,40,41]. The concentration dependent diffusion coefﬁcients
clearly show that the peaks for all species at [LiNO3]  0.1 mol kg1
for temperatures 25 and 40 C, which on one hand reﬂects the
complex behaviour of salt solutions and on the other hand is a
typical observation made for other lithium salt electrolyte
composition in which the mobility decreases for more concentrat-
ed electrolytes due to more ion association [36,37]. Overall, by
increasing the temperature to 40 C, this difference is more obvious
for proton mobility. Above 40 C, the dependency becomes more
linear because complex association processes with the medium are
resolved and facilitate ion mobility at higher temperature in all
electrolytes.
In spite of the lower conductivity of the LiNO3-free electrolyte,
El0, similar diffusion coefﬁcients are observed for El2 and El0.
Looking more closely at the Li diffusion coefﬁcient which has the

then slightly increases in the presence of 0.1 mol kg1 LiNO3. In line
with the trend in conductivity, by adding more nitrate salt after this
point, Li mobility decreases.

In differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), heat effects associ-
ated with phase transition or chemical reaction are monitored as a
function of temperature by increasing the temperature at a
constant rate. Here the DSC is used to compare the glass transition
temperature for different electrolytes. Passerini’s group have used

additive-containing electrolytes. All tested electrolytes show one
phase change correlated to the glass transition.
4. Discussion
Broadly speaking, the longer term cycling behaviour of cells
based on this series of electrolyte solutions follows a correlation
with the measured transport properties. Speciﬁcally, as conduc-
tivities and lithium-ion diffusion coefﬁcients decrease with
increasing 
concentration 
of 
LiNO3, 
the 
measured 
discharge
capacities also decrease. We interpret this in line with established
knowledge of the behaviour of porous electrodes, according to
which the utilization of the cathode material (sulfur) is strongly
dependent on access of electrolyte into all regions of the electrode.
This mixed organic – ionic-liquid electrolyte is rather viscous,
compared with standard organic-only formulations, with the
result that the utilization of cathode material is well below the
theoretical maximum value. 
The cell 
discharge capacity 
is
therefore limited by access of the electrolyte solution into the
cathode, and the capacity recorded is clearly very sensitive to
changes in electrolyte viscosity, as noted in another recent study of
similar electrolyte mixtures [45].

While the cell with electrolyte El2 (0.1 mol kg1 LiNO3)
displayed relatively low capacity in the very early part of cycle-
life, within around 10 cycles its capacity was similar to both El1 and
El3 (0.05 and 0.15 mol kg1 LiNO3 respectively), and these cells
were all performing better than the remainder (Fig. 1). This
situation continued until around cycle 40, after which the
discharge capacity of cell El1 began a steady decrease, ultimately
falling to the much lower levels recorded for cell El0 (zero LiNO3).
As noted earlier, this observation is consistent with the known
consumption of nitrate at the lithium electrode during the
formation of the SEI. As is well-known, the latter continues to
grow over many cycles, the duration of which is probably a
function of the actual medium. Importantly, our results indicate
that at a concentration of 0.05 mol kg1, the consumption of nitrate
lowers its concentration to values close to zero such that
conductivity and viscosity regress to less favourable values. Again,
it is the physical characteristics of the electrolyte which effectively
determine the output of the cathode, and hence the cell.
Apparently there is a band of LiNO3 concentration (0.05–
0.20 mol kg1) within which the physical properties of these
electrolyte solutions are optimum for the operation of the sulfur
cathode. 
Consumption 
of 
nitrate 
in 
the 
cells 
with 
higher
concentrations of this ion will also take place, but do not lower
the concentration below the lower limit of the desired range
(0.05 mol kg1). Interestingly, the DSC data shows a signiﬁcant
depression of the Tg for El2 relative to the other solutions. This
coincides with slightly higher conductivity and suggests that
0.10 mol kg1, lies close to the local maximum within the optimum
range. Variations in glass-like behaviour for these multi-compo-
nent systems are typically related to subtle changes in the balance
of interactions between the various ionic and non-ionic species
present. In these relatively complex electrolyte formulations, it is
likely that a number of lithium complex species are in equilibrium;
namely, [Li(NO3)n](n1), [Li(TFSI)n](n1), [Li(NO3)n(TFSI)m], Li
(TEGDME)(TFSI), Li(TEGDME)(NO3), etc. are present in solution,
varies. Changes in solution speciation are consistent with the
observed changes in Tg, and may also impact on the SEI formation
during initial cycling.
Looking overall at the lithium-sulfur system in the context of
these results, it is worth emphasising that our ﬁndings highlight an
effect which, although predictable, has until now not been
described in any real detail. While until recently, major studies
of the effects of lithium nitrate in Li-S cells had focused on the
‘chemical’ effects of nitrate in modifying the composition of the SEI
on the lithium anode, our data reveal a ‘physical’ effect in which
added LiNO3 creates a minimum in viscosity for a mixed organic 
ionic-liquid electrolyte. Given that target concentrations of lithium
nitrate for optimized anode performance lie in the range of 0.1–
0.4 mol kg1 (see Table 1), our results show a coincidence of the
optimum concentrations for both cathode and anode in the mixed
electrolyte. At concentrations of lithium nitrate around 0.1 mol
kg1, an effective SEI on lithium is formed and a typical carbon-
based sulfur cathode will yield its best performance.
5. Conclusions
In summary, the performance of lithium-sulfur cells utilizing a
blended C4mpyr IL-organic electrolyte was studied with varying
LiNO3 additive concentrations, from which 0.1 mol kg1 of the
additive was determined to be the optimum concentration. While
nitrate is known to beneﬁt long-term cell performance through the
creation of an SEI on lithium that moderates the loss of polysulﬁde
species, its effects in the early stages of cycle-life are clearly due to
the modiﬁcation of physical and transport properties of the
electrolyte medium. Within the concentration range of 0–0.40 mol
kg1 LiNO3, conductivity and lithium-ion diffusivity peak in value,
while viscosity is least, for values 0.05–0.15 mol kg1. This range
also includes a minimum in Tg. The combined effect is to alleviate
the restriction of ion movement within the porous carbon-sulfur
cathode, hence maximizing the discharge capacity observed.
Conﬁrming the correlation between the ionic conductivity/
viscosity and cathode-limited capacity, operating replicate cells
at 50 C revealed very little spread in performance across the range
of nitrate concentration. This is consistent with the higher
temperature lowering all viscosity values to a similar level, at
which access to all regions of the cathode is no longer a limiting
feature. Capacity loss at the higher temperature is most likely
dominated by the greater solubility, and subsequent loss, of
polysulﬁde species.
