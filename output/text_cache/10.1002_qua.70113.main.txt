ABSTRACT
The structure and UV/Vis-absorption spectra of Li2Sn (n = 2–8) in sulfolane solution (Sl), as well as the thermodynamic parameters
of their com- and disproportionation, have been studied using DFT to gain a deeper understanding of the nontrivial mechanistic
steps underlying the S-shaped discharge voltage curve of the Li-S electrochemical system. Comproportionation of Li2Sn was estab-
lished using PBE0/6–311 + G(d,p) approximation with PCM to be thermodynamically favorable: the greater the difference in the
oxidation states of the interacting forms, the more significant the Gibbs free energy drop. The transformations of Li2S3 and Li2S6
drop out of the general pattern: dis- and comproportionation for them are allowed equally, which can lead to a smooth change in
the concentrations of Li2S8, Li2S4, and Li2S2 and, thus, provide a relatively constant potential in the low-voltage part of the Li-S
battery discharge curve. UV/Vis-absorption spectra of Li2Sn obtained using TD-DFT [TPSS/6–311 + G(d,p)] with PCM + explicit
solvation were found to present allowed electronic transitions located from 30 000 to 50 000 cm−1. It has also been observed that
Li2Sn self-association in Sl is relevant mainly for short-chain structures (n = 2–4); there is a dynamic equilibrium between different
forms of Li2Sn, and the solution is usually presented by a mixture of these forms.

Introduction
In recent years, interest in the lithium–sulfur electrochemical
system (LSES), as one of the most prospective for creating batter-
ies with high-performance characteristics (high energy density,
a high number of battery discharge cycles, fire safety, and ecol-
ogy), has been steadily growing. The advantages of LSES include
a high theoretical specific energy (2600 W⋅h⋅kg−1) as well as the
widespread occurrence of lithium and sulfur in the Earth’s crust
[1–6] that determines the relatively low cost of a battery made on
their basis.

The electrochemical reduction of sulfur to lithium sulfide in
lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB) is carried out through the sequen-
tial formation of lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn, n = 2–8). Sulfur and
intermediate products of its reduction are soluble in electrolytes
based on aprotic dipolar solvents and are in equilibrium between
the liquid phase and the positive electrode of LSB, which makes
it designated as a liquid cathode battery [7–13].

great influence on the mechanism of electrochemical transfor-
mations of lithium polysulfides, since lithium polysulfides form

Our research team recently considered [20] and subsequently
modeled, using DFT methodology [21], one of the major elec-
trochemical trajectories, assuming the sequential generation of
lithium polysulfides in sulfolane solution (polarizable continuum
model + explicit solvation), with the number of sulfur atoms
described by the sequence 2(4-i), where i = 1, 2, 3, 4 is the step
number of sulfur reduction, as presented in Figure 1.
As can be seen from Figure 1, the quantum-chemical model of
sulfur reduction and lithium oxidation in the given interpreta-
tion during the discharge of LSB is characterized by a drop in
its potential, asymptotically tending to a certain limiting value
of E(Li2S2 →Li2S). The revealed feature is consistent with other
theoretical works [23] and correlates qualitatively well enough
with the experimental data; however, it does not reproduce the
sophisticated character of the change in the potential from the
discharge capacity in detail. One of the reasons for the observed
discrepancy between the experimental and theoretical data may
be the neglect of transformations of lithium polysulfides accom-
panied by a change in the formal charge located on sulfur atoms
without the participation of the reducing agent, that is, neg-
ative electrode material, as it is colored red in the following
scheme [24–27]:
These transformations do not lead to the generation of cur-
rent in LSB; however, they make it possible to maintain a
quasi-stationary concentration of the most thermodynamically
stable forms of lithium polysulfides for a while. According to
some researchers [28–31], a slowdown in the potential decrease
and the formation of pseudo linear parts of the LSB discharge
voltage profiles can serve as a marker for such reactions, as illus-
trated by Figure 1. These transformations are characterized by
considerable variability and can, in general, be expressed as fol-
lows:

where k/l and m/n are the numbers of sulfur atoms in
lithium polysulfides before and after the reactions of dis- and
comproportionation (DCRs), respectively. It should be noted that
octasulfur may also participate in the charge redistribution, in
addition to Equation (1), at the initial step of LSB discharge, as
long as its concentration in the electrolyte solution is still quite
high. The parallel proceeding of these reactions in LSB raises
an acute issue relative to their reliable experimental identifi-
cation using electronic/vibrational spectroscopy [28, 29, 32–36]
and, thus, the construction of a comprehensive scheme of sulfur
reduction in LSB.
In the present work, we structured DCRs of lithium polysulfides
in sulfolane solution and assessed their direction of spontaneous
progress by calculating their equilibrium thermodynamic param-
eters using DFT methodology. Having modeled the allowed elec-
tronic transitions in the approximation of time-dependent DFT
(TD-DFT), we also accomplished UV/Vis-spectral identification
of lithium polysulfides to account for the possibility of their exper-
imental quantification. The performed research allowed us to
pull a cluster of the most significant reactions and refine the
electrochemical trajectory of sulfur reduction, taking these into
account.

Calculation Procedures
All the calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09
Rev. C01 program package [37] for standard conditions (1 atm,
298.15 K). The primary processing of the quantum chemical com-
putation results was carried out using the Chemcraft 1.8 (build
574b) [38]. Geometry optimization, equilibrium vibration fre-
quency (harmonic) calculation, and natural bond orbital anal-
ysis (NBO, version 3) [39–43] were performed using PBE0, a
hybrid GGA (generalized gradient approximation) functional,
with a predefined amount of DFT (75%) and exact (25%) exchange
(PBE0, also called PBE1PBE in the Gaussian program) [44,
45]. Pople’s triple split-valence basis set augmented with d-
and p-type polarization functions and a set of diffuse functions
[6–311 + G(d,p) (5D,7F)] [46, 47] was applied. All the structures
correspond to minima on the PES, as confirmed by a set of pos-
itive vibrational frequencies found during the Hessian calcula-
tion. The dispersion correction was not added to the computation
scheme in this case because it leads to an increase in error along
with the chosen functional [48]. An integration grid having 175
radial shells and 974 angular points [49] was used to increase the
level of accuracy and improve the convergence of structures with
a flat potential energy surface (PES) in terms of geometry. Wave
function stability of all the considered structures was checked
according to technique [50, 51]. Earlier, it was demonstrated that
the chosen functional is an effective and relevant tool for the
investigation of lithium polysulfides [52–54].

The nonspecific solvation by sulfolane was considered within

integral equation formalism variant (IEF-PCM) [55] at every step

covered using the Pierotti model [56]. The cavity was extensively
modified using the UFF force field (the method is supposed to
model hydrogens by individual spheres [explicit hydrogens]). We
used the default values of 43.962 and 2.200179 (standard condi-
tions, 1 atm, 298.15 K) for static (ε0) and dynamic (ε∞) dielectric
constants, respectively. The solute–solvent boundary was speci-
fied as the Solvent Accessible Surface (SAS). The sulfolane sol-
vation radius used in the calculations was estimated based on
molecular volume calculations (Monte Carlo method) using the
Gaussian 09 Rev. C01 software package and it was 4.07 Å.
The specific solvation by sulfolane was covered on the base of
the solvation scheme of lithium polysulfides used in DFT mod-

sulfides and their associates via coordination of the oxygens

Disproportionation
and Comproportionation Reactions
and Thermodynamic Equilibrium of Lithium
Polysulfides in Sulfolane Solution
Transformations of lithium polysulfides that are not accompa-
nied by metallic lithium oxidation and thereby do not lead to the
generation of electricity are possible at any step of the LSB dis-
charge. One can generally find at least̃ 50 transformations for
LSB (see Equation (1)). Having evenly distributed sulfur atoms
in the resulting products, we tried to reasonably narrow the set of
the most probable DCRs of lithium polysulfides:
1. The interaction of lithium polysulfides with the same par-
ity and different chain lengths leads to the formation of a
pair of polysulfides with an arithmetic average number of
elements.
2. Interaction of lithium polysulfides with different parity and
chain lengths leads to the formation of a pair of polysulfides
differing by one sulfur atom;
3. The interaction of the same lithium polysulfides is accom-
panied by the formation of two polysulfides differing by two
sulfur atoms.
The selection rules described above are illustrated by the con-
tent of Table 2. They made it possible to narrow the range of
reactions under consideration from 50 to 28. The thermodynamic
parameters were calculated taking into account sulfolane as a
medium in two approximations for the remaining transforma-
tions: PCM only and PCM + explicit solvation models. The results
of DFT calculations obtained solely with dielectric continuum
theory have earlier been shown [21] to be less consistent with
experimental data in terms of thermodynamic parameters of LSB
discharge than those estimated on the basis of the hybrid solva-
tion model. However, PCM-based calculations are characterized
by lower computational time costs, and therefore make it possi-
ble to model electronic absorption spectra up to the border of deep
ultraviolet (̃ 50 000 cm−1) for lithium polysulfide associates, as we
will discuss below. The results of calculations carried out using
the hybrid solvation model are presented in Table 3 as more trust-
worthy. All the reactions, except the disproportionation of lithium
octasulfide, are given in the simplest stoichiometric ratios, which

It is natural to assume that the values of enthalpy and Gibbs free
energy presented in Table 3 are characteristic of dilute solutions
of standalone polysulfides stabilized by a bulk solvation shell.
However, in concentrated solutions, which are more typical of
feasible LSB, polysulfides can form self-associates. We studied the
structure, as well as energy characteristics, of Li2Sn-Li2Sm asso-
ciates in this regard. The formation and transformation thermo-
dynamics of associates consisting of lithium polysulfides with the
same and different chain lengths are presented below.

Self-Association of Lithium Polysulfides
We were guided by the geometry of standalone lithium polysul-
fides when modeling their self-associates as presented in Figure 2
(see also Table S11). Considered self-associates of lithium polysul-
fides have two general structural features. (1) The lithium cations
are grouped between two polysulfides in such a way that each
one is ionically bonded to at least three of the nearest sulfur
atoms (S*). The NBO point charges of lithium ions and the termi-
nal sulfur atoms are approximately equal to +0.8 and −0.7 a.u.,
respectively (see Table S11). At the same time, the IBSImean val-
ues of Li-S* and S*-S* bond paths are calculated to be very small
(0.03 and 0.02, respectively), far below the IBSI covalent domain
(0.15 < IBSI < 4.00) for Sl-Li2S7-Li2S7-Sl (Figure 3) as an example.

We tried to maintain a balance between the number of breaking
and forming contacts between reaction participants when deriv-
ing the chemical equation of self-association of lithium polysul-
fides in sulfolane solution [59]:
2Sl −Li2S𝑛−Sl + Sl −Li2S𝑚−Sl
→Sl2 + Sl −Li2S𝑛−Li2S𝑚−Sl
(2)
The proposed approach allows us to sufficiently level out the
basis set superposition error (BSSE). This is especially true in our
case because there is a significant collectivization of atoms and,
consequently, orbitals centered on them in the resulting struc-
ture, which sufficiently complicates the isolation of the original
fragments within the framework mainly used in BSSE estima-
tion by the counterpoise correction technique [68, 69], as can
be seen from Figure 3. Thus, we aimed to evaluate thermody-
namics through the competition between the two interactions
(Li2Sx →Sl and Li2Sx →Li2Sx). The abovementioned balance can-
not be maintained in any way when accounting for the solvent
exclusively at the continuum level of the theory in this connec-
tion. The latter can lead, on the one hand, to an underestimation
of the entropy of the complex relative to the initial reagents and,
on the other hand, to an overestimation of its stability. Therefore,
the interaction of two polysulfides was modeled to be accompa-
nied by the expulsion of two sulfolane molecules when solva-
tion is taken into account explicitly. Each of the remaining two
sulfolane molecules is coordinated by the oxygens of the sulfone
group to lithium cations, as shown in Figures 2 and 3.
Figure 4 shows the change in the Gibbs free energy upon the
association of lithium polysulfides with the same and different
numbers of sulfur atoms, as well as the symmetry of the result-
ing associate. As can be seen from Figure 4, there is a satisfactory
correlation at the trend level (for n equal to 1–5 for the same
and 1–3 for different lithium polysulfides, respectively) between
the thermodynamic characteristics calculated using PCM only
and PCM + explicit solvation models. However, the hybrid solva-
tion model is characterized by a much narrower range of Δ𝐺˚

The association may be favorable in terms of Gibbs free energy
change in the middle (for lithium octasulfide) and toward the

Disproportionation Reactions of Lithium
Polysulfides as a Continuation of Self-Association
Having combined the calculation results for associates formed
from the same and different lithium polysulfides, we estimated

UV/Vis Absorption Spectra of Lithium
Polysulfides and Their Self-Associates
The potential for self-association of lithium polysulfides and
changes in the oxidation state of polysulfide chains in their com-
position complicate the analysis of the electrochemical trajec-
tory somewhat and raise the urgent question of their identifi-
cation using spectral methods, especially. We have modeled the
electronic absorption spectra of Li2Sx in various forms using
the TD-DFT methodology (TPSS/6–311 + G(d,p)), which have
proved themselves well enough in calculations of allowed elec-
tronic transitions of elemental sulfur and its associates in sul-
folane solutions [59], to clarify this issue. Figure 6 shows the
calculated (b) and experimental (a) (see “Methods” section)
absorption spectra of lithium polysulfides. As can be seen from
Figure 6, there is moderate agreement between the experimental
and theoretical data at both the qualitative (correlation of spectral
ranges, general shape of the spectra) and the quantitative (com-
parable values of extinction coefficients (𝜀)) levels as a whole.
Some data conflict may be caused not only by the imperfection of
the quantum chemical approximation used in this study but also,
obviously, by the more complex composition of the real polysul-
fide solution.
It was found (see Figures S2, S4, S5, S7, S9, S11, and S13 for
details) that as the number of sulfur atoms increases, the differ-
ence between the absorption spectra obtained within the scope
of the two aforementioned solvation models decreases. Taking
this fact into account, as well as the high computational time
costs associated with calculating electronic transitions for explic-
itly solvated associates of lithium polysulfides, we limited our-
selves to covering the medium only at the continuum level of
theory when modeling the UV/Vis absorption spectra. As can
be seen from Figure 7, the process of association of polysulfides
has little effect on electronic transitions (see Figures S17–S24):
it does not influence the spectral range and slightly reduces the
extinction coefficients. There is no obvious specificity in chang-
ing the shape of the spectrum: smoothing (Li2Sn-Li2Sn, n = 1, 2,
3, 5, 6, and 7) or a blue shift of the absorption maximum (n = 1,
2, 6, and 8) is observed in some cases. It should also be noted
that calculated UV/Vis absorption spectra of associates of lithium
polysulfides (Figure 7a) and their products of disproportionation
(Figure 7b) with an odd number (n) of sulfur atoms (Li2Sn-Li2Sn
↔Li2Sn-1-Li2Sn + 1, n = 2–7) are characterized by nearly the same
shape (see Figures S25–S30 for detailed comparison).

Conclusion
One of the pressing issues in the functioning of LSBs is the non-
trivial shape of the discharge curve correlating weakly with the
results of calculations of thermodynamic parameters obtained
using different quantum chemical approximations. In this work,
various DCRs reactions were examined in detail to gain insight
into the situation. On the one hand, these reactions are not
accompanied by lithium oxidation; on the other hand, they sig-
nificantly affect the redistribution of lithium polysulfide forms
in solution and, thus, are capable of substantially influencing
the “momentary” potential of the system. A set of DCR (non-
electrochemical transformations) was localized using the DFT
method and a hybrid solvation model that takes into account
the solvent at both the continuum (PCM) and explicit levels
of theory. The most important of them are reactions involving
lithium hexa- and tri-sulfides. These compounds have a feature
to act as “buffers,” providing a relatively constant potential in the
range from 400 to 1000 mAh. The latter reasonably agrees with
the results of experimental studies [70, 71]. The calculated ther-
modynamic data, together with the experimental and electronic
UV/Vis absorption spectra modeled in approximation of TD-DFT
(TPSS/6–311 + G(d,p)) made it possible to form a complete pic-
ture under which (1) self-association of lithium polysulfides in
sulfolane solution is relevant mainly for short-chain ones; (2)
there is a dynamic equilibrium between different forms of lithium
polysulfides, and thus, the solution is usually multicomponent.
The latter is indicated by a smooth change in the shape of the
absorption spectrum when varying the ratio of soluble lithium
sulfide and elemental sulfur as well as a moderate correlation of
experimental and calculated electronic UV/Vis absorption spec-
tra. In our opinion, the results obtained in this work can signifi-
cantly simplify the analysis of the sulfur reduction process occur-
ring in LSB, clarifying the range of the most important electro-
chemical trajectories of sulfur reduction, as well as better under-
standing the limits of applicability and the possibilities of UV/Vis
spectral methodology concerning the studied system.
