A B S T R A C T  
In the lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries with high sulfur loading and low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio, the cathodic 
reaction is hindered due to the insulating properties of active S and Li2S, resulting in poor reaction kinetics. 
Herein, the whole S8↔Li2S reaction pathway can be regulated by fluorenone (FL) molecules through an in-situ 
free radical supplement strategy. Firstly, FL acts as a capturer and stabilizer of trisulfur radical (S3
•−) above 2.1 V. 
Secondly, after the reduction of S3
•−at 2.1 V, FL become an exogenous radical (FL•−) to promote the reaction 
kinetic through a new electron transfer pathway. The in-situ Raman spectra and theoretical calculation prove the 
strengthened reaction pathway. As a result, the capacities of Li-S batteries can be improved from 218.7 to 1099.7 
mAh g−1 in the presence of FL at an E/S ratio of 5 µL mgS
−1. Moreover, the cells with FL achieve an initial capacity 
of 978.2 mAh g−1 with a high S loading of 5.5 mg cm−2 at 0.2 C. This work provides the fundamental under­
standing of S reduction reaction, and also demonstrates a new idea and method to solve the problem of poor 
kinetics of Li-S battery with low E/S ratio.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium sulfur (Li-S) batteries have attracted extensive attention due 
to their low price and environmental friendliness, as well as high energy 
density (2600 Wh kg−1) [1–5]. The insulating properties of active S and 
Li2S result in the low electron transfer rate and reaction kinetics of 
S8↔Li2S interconversion through solid-solid reaction pathway. Soluble 
polysulfides (Li2Sn) as the intrinsic redox mediators (RMs) can promote 
the electron transfer via disproportionation and electrochemical 
reduction reactions at the interface. Therefore, the redox reactions of 
S8↔Li2S based on such a solid-liquid-solid path (S8↔Li2Sn↔Li2S) are 
accelerated [6–8]. Unfortunately, the contents of Li2Sn relative to total 
contents of S species are greatly reduced when the electrolyte/sulfur 
(E/S) ratio decreases, and the limited content of Li2Sn cannot meet the 
requirements of redox reaction for fast electron transfer rate [9–11]. As a 
result, the actual energy density of the Li-S batteries with low E/S ratio 
are far away from their theoretical values [12–14]. 
In order to improve the reaction kinetics between S8 and Li2S at low 
E/S ratio, researchers have devoted their efforts to fabricate cathode 
materials and adjust electrolyte compositions [15–19]. Specifically, 
modifying the electrolyte composition is considered as the effective way 
to improve the concentrations of Li2Sn. For example, Pang and 
co-workers referred when high polar solvents (e.g., DMSO, DMF) were 
used as the electrolyte, the dissolution of Li2Sn was significantly 
enhanced [16]. Moreover, the solvents with high donor number (DN) 
can strongly solvate Li+ as soft acids to stabilize S3
•−(soft base), which 
can accelerate S8 reduction [20–25]. This leads to an improvement of 
reaction kinetic at the first discharge platform (S8→Li2Sn). However, 
these solvents cannot effectively promote the nucleation and growth of 
Li2S. This is because most of the Li2Sn will be reduced, resulting in the 
insufficient electron transfer in the Li2S generation stage. In addition to 
the modulation of Li2Sn as intrinsic RMs, the introduction of extrinsic 
RMs can provide another solution mediated pathway for the conversion 
of S8/Li2S [26–30]. For example, Zhang et al. used cobaltocene as a 
soluble RM catalyst, which can promote electron transfer rate from the 
electrode to Li2Sn, thereby improving the performance of Li-S batteries

at low E/S ratios [27]. However, most of RMs can only work in the 
partial reaction pathway based on their energetics of RMs and sulfur 
species. In general, those above methods are difficult to maintain effi­
cient electron transfer throughout the reaction process, especially at low 
E/S ratios. This common challenge limits the improvement of the redox 
kinetics of Li-S batteries, which can be understood by the bucket effects. 
Thus, it is necessary to develop new strategies to accelerate the overall 
electron transfer and reaction kinetics of Li-S batteries at low E/S ratio. 
In this work, we propose an in-situ free radical supplement strategy 
based on the fluorenone (FL) additve to accelerate electron transfer and 
improve redox kinetics in the full sulfur reduction reaction and Li2S 
oxidation process of Li-S batteries. Specifically, for the reduction of S8 to 
Li2S4 and oxidation of Li2S to Li2Sn, FL captures and stabilizes S3
•−by 
utilizing the electron accepting ability of carbonyl groups to increase the 
concentrations of soluble Li2Sn, thereby accelerating the electron 
transfer and promoting reaction kinetic. Moreover, at the stage of Li2S4 
reduction to Li2S, FL is reduced to radical form (FL•−) at the similar 
potentials, which mediates the electron transfer between Li2S4, Li2S2 
and Li2S (Fig. 1). As a result, compared to those without FL, the Li-S 
batteries with FL show 5 times enhancement of discharge capacities 
(1099.7 mAh g−1) at an E/S ratio of 5 µL mgS
−1. 
2. Results and discussion 
2.1. The effect of FL on the electrochemical performance of Li−S cells 
As shown in Fig. S1, cyclic voltammogram (CV) curve of FL show a 
pair of reversible redox peaks above 1.8 V vs Li+/Li, corresponding to 
the reversible conversion of FL and FL•−. The initial reduction potential 
of FL is 2.1 V vs Li+/Li, and the oxidation potential of FL•−is 1.8 V vs 
Li+/Li. A small polarization potential can be achieved even at large scan 
rates due to its fast diffusion coefficient (4.65×10−6 cm2 s−1, Fig. S1). 
This character ensures the rapid conversion and diffusion of FL and FL•−, 
which are important for FL to play a regulatory role. Then the effect of 
FL on the redox behaviors of S8↔Li2S interconversion was explored. 
Two distinct reduction peaks at an E/S ratio of 15 µL mgS
−1 appear in the 
presence and absence of FL at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 (Fig. 2a), and the 
reduction peaks centered at 2.31 and 2.02 V are assigned to S8→Li2S4 
and Li2S4→Li2S, respectively. The redox behaviors at such low scan rates 
are quite similar in the presence and absence of FL. It is due to that 
polysulfides as intrinsic RMs can ensure the required electrons under 
low current, so that FL does not show its accelerating effect. 
As the scan rate increases, the transient generation of Li2Sn amount is 
not enough for effectively mediating the electron transfer between S8/ 
Li2S and electrode, the slow reaction kinetic may occur. At this time, the 
acceleration effect of FL on the electrochemical reaction should be re­
flected. To confirm the hypothesis, the scan rate of CV was increased to 
1 mV s−1 (Fig. 2b). In the presence of FL, the first reduction peak po­
tential is positively shifted, and the peak current density is also 
increased. The difference of the second reduction peak is clearer. As the 
Li2Sn concentration decreases, the reduction reaction of Li2S4 to Li2S is 
difficult to be realized, the peak current for the reference is only 5.18 
mA. In contrast, the peak current increases to 9.31 mA and the peak 
potential show the positive shift about 126 mV in the presence of FL. 
Tafel slope further confirms the enhanced redox activity (Figs. 2c, d, S2). 
Both the first and second reduction process have lower Tafel slopes in 
the presence of FL, demonstrating an acceleration effect on the full 
reduction of S8 to Li2S. The activation energy corresponding to the two 
stages can be further analyzed from calculating Tafel slopes (Fig. 2g). 
The activation energy for S8 to Li2Sn decreases by 64.08 kJ mol−1 in the 
presence of FL, and those for Li2Sn to Li2S decreases by 95.85 kJ mol−1. It 
means that FL has a strong promotion ability for sulfur reduction, 
especially in the Li2S generation stage with lack of soluble polysulfides. 
The above results prove that FL can significantly accelerate the re­
action of S8 to Li2S. To further verify its effect, CV under the condition of 
low E/S ratios was further tested with the same scan rate (Fig. 2e, f). At 
such condition, the decrease of electrolyte volume leads to a greatly 
reduced Li2Sn content, and the problem of limited electron and ion flow 
rates will become more seriously. At an E/S ratio of 10 µL mgS
−1, the 
reaction kinetics are significantly increased in the presence of FL 
(Fig. 2e). Moreover, in the case of an E/S ratio of 8 µL mgS
−1, the second 
reduction process (Li2S4→Li2S) is well established in the presence of FL 
in comparison to its disappearance in the absence of FL (Fig. 2f). The gap 
of peak potentials in different E/S ratios in Fig. 2h directly reveals the 
increase of reaction kinetics by FL. As the E/S ratios decrease, the effect 
of FL on the reaction kinetic becomes more prominent compared to 
those without FL. It is worth noting that the promoting effect of FL on the 
two-step reduction reaction process is different, which may be caused by 
the different roles of FL and FL•−in different reaction stages. 
2.2. S3
•−improves the redox kinetics of S8↔Li2S4 
To elucidate the promotion effects of FL at different reaction stages, 
we firstly analyzed the reduction process of S8 to Li2Sn in the presence of 
FL, in which Li2S6 solution was used as a representative long-chain 
polysulfide for spectroscopic characterization. 13C NMR spectra 
(Fig. S3) show that, after mixing with Li2S6 solution, the carbonyl carbon 
of FL at 193.1 ppm shifts to 192.7 ppm, while the other carbons shift 
relatively little, indicating the electron donation from polysulfide to the 
carbonyl group. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. S4) also reveals the interac­
tion between FL and Li2Sn. After mixing FL and Li2S6 solution, a new 
peak appears at 533 cm−1 that corresponds to S3
•−. Such results indicate 
that FL can stabilize S3
•−in the electrolyte based on its carbonyl group 
[31–35]. 
In-situ Raman spectroscopy coupling with electrochemical cells was 
further to confirm the above process. As shown in Fig. 3a, at a current of 
1 C, the voltage polarization of the Li-S batteries is very high and the S 
reduction cannot be proceeded, which is due to the limited electron 
transfer rate, well consistent with CV patterns. Raman spectra (Fig. 3e) 
show that the signal of S8 exists before 2.10 V in the absence of FL. The 
signal of Li2Sn (396 cm−1, 451 cm−1) appears from 2.00 V, and it can be 
detected up to 1.70 V. This indicates that most of Li2Sn are not utilized 
when the discharge cut-off voltage reaches, consistent to the low 
discharge capacity of the cells. In the presence of FL, the shape of 
discharge curve changes (Fig. 3b), and two standard plateaus (2.40 to 
2.04 V, 2.04 to 1.70 V) corresponding to the reduction of S8 to Li2S4 and 
Li2S4 to Li2S appear, respectively. Furthermore, Raman spectra (Fig. 3f) 
reveal the increase of S3
•−concentration in the electrolyte. The existence

2.3. FL•−accelerates reduction of Li2S4, Li2S2 to Li2S 
Next, we investigated the role of FL on the Li2S generation stage. As 
shown in Fig. 4a, b, the potentiostatic discharge profiles at 2.09 V were 
firstly collected to accurately explore the effect of FL on Li2S nucleation. 
The results show that the Li2S nucleation peak of the cell with FL is about 
2100s earlier than that of the cell without FL. More positive current 
response indicates the improved reaction kinetics. Calculating from the 
integral of the current, it is found that most of the cell capacity without 
FL is still contributed from the reduction of S8
2– and S6
2–. Moreover, Li2S 
precipitation without FL only contributes the capacity of 83.9 mAh g−1, 
much smaller than that with FL (136.9 mAh g−1). It is worth noted that, 
FL has been reduced to FL•−at the test potential of above experiments. 
The same result can also be seen in the constant current discharge test, 
which the cells with FL can maintain a high capacity at large rate. In 
contrast, the capacity of cell without FL has significantly reduced 
(Fig. S6). The enhanced capacities during Li2S precipitation is attributed 
to the reaction between FL•−and Li2S4, Li2S2. 
To confirm this prediction, the mixed FL•−and Li2S4 solution was 
probed by Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 4c). It is found that along with the 
disappearance of S6
2– (396 cm−1) and S4
2– (451 cm−1), the signal of 
carbonyl group appears at 1720 cm−1, proving the reaction between 
FL•−and Li2Sn. This phenomenon is also proved by the in-situ Raman 
spectra (Fig. 3f). In cells with FL, the Li2Sn signals disappear at 2.08 V, 
which can be attributed to the reaction between FL•−and Li2Sn. FL•−
donates its electrons to Li2S4 to promote the generation of Li2S, forming 
a new reaction pathway for Li2S formation. 13C NMR spectra (Fig. S7) 
further confirm the reaction. No peak assigning to carbonyl carbon is 
shown for the solution of FL•−, and it totally becomes free radicals after 
reduction. After adding it to Li2S4, the carbonyl signal appears and the 
solution color changes, indicating the reaction between FL•−and Li2S4. 
Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was used to further study 
the reaction (Fig. 4e). The Li2S4 and FL solutions show no ESR signal, but 
a strong and sharp signal can be found for the FL•−
solution. After 
mixing FL•−with Li2S4, its signal almost disappears and a new signal 
assigning to another free radical appears. The disappearance of FL•−
means that they have been oxidized by Li2S4 to FL, and the new free 
radicals should be S3
•−after FL stabilization [36]. Moreover, it is found 
that the color of Li2S2 containing solution can be also changed in the 
presence of FL•−, accompanying with an increased amount of precipitate 
in the bottom of bottle (Fig. S8). It indicates that Li2S2 can be reduced by 
FL•−. Raman spectrum was used to confirm this point (Fig. 4d). In 
addition to the appearance of the carbonyl signal, the intensity of peak at 
372 cm−1 assigning to Li2S is significantly enhanced. In order to exclude 
the interference of other Li2Sn on the Raman signal, the UV–vis spec­
troscopy was further tested through extracting the supernatant of Li2S2 
and mixing it with FL•−. As shown in Fig. 4f, the original Li2Sn signal 
completely disappears in the presence of FL•−, proving no Li2Sn in the 
solution. Besides, the theoretical calculation results also show that the

The ex-situ morphology analysis was also conducted at different 
discharge depths to detect the nucleation and growth of Li2S in the 
presence of FL. For the battery without FL, a thin layer of Li2S film de­
posits appears on electrode surface (Fig. 6b) at the initial stage of Li2S 
growth. With the increased discharge depth, the film-like Li2S gradually 
connects and completely covers the electrode surface (Fig. 6c, d). This 
confirms that Li2S tends to grow with two-dimensional mode in the 
commonly used low-donicity ether-based electrolytes. The insulating 
nature of Li2S makes it difficult for the three-dimensional growth. In 
contrast, the growth mode of Li2S is changed in the presence of FL. In the 
initial stage, Li2S deposition shows a three-dimensional growth of 
island-like Li2S, in which the length and width are about 5 and 3 µm, 
respectively (Fig. 6e). With the increased discharge depth, these deposits 
became the porous block-like structures with a thickness of approximate 
3 µm (Fig. 6f, g). In addition, we found that the sufficient naked cathode 
surface can effectively reduces the resistance of electron transfer at the 
cathode/electrolyte interface, so that the smaller impedance is obtained 
(Fig. S9). The change in deposition morphology can attribute to the new 
electron transfer pathway for the nucleation and growth of Li2S in the 
presence of FL, thereby changing the density and distribution of Li2S 
nuclei. 
2.4. Cells performance 
The aforementioned results proved the effect of FL on the two steps 
of sulfur reduction pathway and Li2S oxidation, and then we assembled 
Li-S batteries to check its promotion effect on cell performance. The cells 
with FL achieve the discharge capacities of 1418, 1080, 995, 916, and 
844 mAh g−1 at the rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 C, respectively, 
much higher than those without FL. The polarization voltages are also 
much lower than the cells without FL, indicating the reaction kinetics of 
S8↔Li2S conversion is greatly improved (Fig. S10). It is known that Li-S 
batteries experience a voltage drop before the second plateau of 
discharge process, which is caused by the poor redox kinetics from Li2S4 
to Li2S. As expected, the presence of FL makes the voltage drop three- 
times lower than the cells without FL at 2 C (Fig. S11) due to the pro­
moted reaction kinetic. Besides, the cells with FL exhibit much lower 
charging potential plateau, indicating the conversion of the Li2S to Li2Sn 
to S8 is also greatly promoted, consistent to the observation in in-situ 
Raman spectra. 
The cycling performances were further compared at 0.5 C, as shown 
in Fig. 7a. The initial capacities of cells with FL are 1020 mAh g−1, and a 
reversible capacity of 611 mAh g−1 can be maintained after 520 cycles, 
corresponding to a cyclic decay rate of 0.077% per cycle. In a sharp 
contrast, the cells without FL only show an initial capacity of 892 mAh 
g−1 and a cyclic decay rate of 0.2% per cycle. Moreover, the Coulombic 
efficiency of the cells with FL is much higher than that without FL during 
cycling. This is due to that the redox activity of the cells without FL is 
low. With the increase of cycling, the amounts of unused active mate­
rials gradually increase, and the voltage polarization gradually increases 
(Fig. 7b, c). The high cycling stability and small polarization voltage are 
attributed to the presence of FL to accelerate the electron transfer rate 
and enhance the sulfur redox kinetics. Even at higher rates, the rapid 
electron transfer pathway caused by FL can still make the cells exhibit 
high reactivity, increasing battery capacity and cycle life (Fig. S12). To 
determine the optimal concentration of FL in the cells, different con­
centrations of FL (20, 50, 100 mmol L−1) are added to the electrolyte for 
a cycling test at 0.5 C (Fig. S13). With the increase of the concentration, 
the improvement factor of cell performance is enlarged. When the 
concentration is increased to 100 mmol L−1, the strengthened shuttle 
effect leads to the decay of capacity. In order to rule out the contribution 
of FL to the capacity, a stationary lithium-FL flow battery is assembled

3. Conclusion 
In this work, the electron transfer rate and reaction kinetics at 
cathodic reactions can be modulated by FL additive in Li-S batteries with 
low E/S ratios. Different from the ever-reported additives, FL can 
maintain the concentration of electrons in the liquid phase during the 
whole redox process in the Li-S batteries through a free radical supple­
ment strategy. In the stage of S8↔Li2Sn, FL captures and stabilizes S3
•−to 
increases the intrinsic RM concentrations; in the stage of Li2S4→Li2S, FL 
accepts one electron to form a radical (FL•−), making up for the reduc­
tion of S3
•−. As a result, at low E/S ratios, the capacities of Li-S batteries 
are 5 times higher than those without FL. The present study provides the 
deep understanding of S redox chemistry, also demonstrates a new 
strategy to increase the electron transfer rate and reaction kinetics 
through solution-mediated pathway.
