ORIGINAL PAPER
New insights into the relationship between capacity fading and sulfur 
loading and electrolyte/sulfur ratio obtained from Li–S pouch cell
Jianqiang Guo1 · Jing Li2 · Hao Liu1 · Shaomin Li1 · Furong Qin3 · Bo Hong3
Received: 14 May 2021 / Revised: 26 May 2021 / Accepted: 1 June 2021 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2021
Abstract
High-energy density lithium-sulfur (Li–S) cells have been greatly studied due to the urgent requirements for advanced energy 
storage applications. During the past decades, many achievements have been obtained through great fundamental researches 
for inhibiting the shuttle effect, especially including designing various cathode materials and interlayers. As a result, the 
prepared coin cells in the references deliver a superior long-term cycling performance even for 1000 cycles. However, when 
facing the practical application of Li–S pouch cells, the poor cycle performance is still existed. This indicates that there is 
wide gap of scientific knowledge between the fundamental research and the application need. Herein, Li–S pouch cells are 
designed and used for exploring the key factors that influence the cycle life of the Li–S cell. We mainly discuss the influences 
of sulfur loading and electrolyte/sulfur ratio on the capacity contribution and cycle life. Combining electrochemical cycle 
performance and analysis for the Li–S pouch cells after dismantling, we propose two different capacity fading mechanisms 
for Li–S pouch cells with different E/S ratio (3, 10) and sulfur loading (5.8 mg ­cm−2, 10.8 mg ­cm−2). In all, this work pro-
vides new understandings into the relationship between capacity fading and sulfur loading and electrolyte/sulfur ratio. This 
is beneficial for developing practical Li–S cell with high-energy density far exceed the traditional Li-ion cell.
Keywords  Li–S pouch cell · Sulfur loading · Electrolyte/sulfur ratio · Cycle life
Introduction
The traditional lithium-ion cells accomplish energy stor-
age and release via shuttling and intercalating of lithium 
ions between the oxide cathode and the graphite anode [1, 
2]. However, the process of reversibly inserting/extracting 
lithium will lead to lithium loss, causing the capacity fad-
ing of lithium-ion cells [3]. Therefore, new energy storage 
systems that could achieve high capacity and energy density 
by other conversion reactions should be developed [4, 5]. 
In response, lithium-sulfur cells, which release and storage 
energy by two electrons redox reaction per sulfur atom, were 
developed [6–8]. Li–S cells have drawn much attention in 
academic and industrial area due to their high theoretical 
specific capacity (1675 mAh ­g−1 and energy density (2600 
Wh ­Kg−1)) [9, 10].
Although the two electrons transfer electrochemical 
reaction based on lithium anode and sulfur cathode could 
achieve high specific capacity and energy density, the mul-
tistep conversions, which are from solid-phase sulfur to liq-
uid-phase polysulfide, and finally solid-phase sulfide, lead 
to complex kinetics and reversibility of the cathode redox 
reaction [11–13]. Especially, the formation of liquid-phase 
soluble polysulfide causes severe “shuttle effect,” which is 
corresponding to the loss of active materials and capacity 
decay [14, 15]. Besides, the poor electronic conductivity 
and Li dendrite growth on the anode also bring about poor 
electrochemical performance of Li–S cells [16–18].
To deal with these issues, a great amount of fundamental 
researches have been made to the design of suitable sulfur 
host cathode materials, including various carbon materials 
[19–25], metal oxides [26–28], metal sulfides [29–32], metal

On the other hand, various functional separators and inter-
layers are developed to improve the electrochemical perfor-
mance of the Li–S coin cells [39–45]. So far, Li–S coin cells 
have reached excellent cycle stability (500–1000 cycles) [46, 
47], yet compared with the outstanding performance of Li–S 
coin cells, few scientific researches have been reported based 
on Li–S pouch cells.
Pouch cell is the most accurate way to evaluate the per-
formance of electrodes [48]. Moreover, it is more meaning-
ful to mention and calculate the energy density in pouch 
cells, where all the masses of the components are consid-
ered, including electrode films, electrode lugs, separators, 
electrolytes, and plastic shells [49]. However, when facing 
the practical application of Li–S pouch cells, the poor cycle 
performance still exists. This indicates that there is big dif-
ference between Li–S coin cells and Li–S pouch cells.
In this work, 1 Ah Li–S pouch cells with different sul-
fur loading and electrolyte/sulfur ratio were developed, to 
identify the reasons that restrain the cycle performance of 
Li–S pouch cells. It is found that Li–S pouch cells with low 
sulfur loading and high electrolyte/ratio show superior cycle 
stability. Therefore, it can be concluded that sulfur loading 
and electrolyte/sulfur ratio have direct influence on the cycle 
numbers of Li–S pouch cells. For Li–S pouch cells, they 
show complex failure mechanisms at different S loading and 
E/S ratio. The poor cycling stability was attributed to the 
incomplete electrochemical reaction caused by the precipi-
tation of polysulfide at low E/S ratio. However, the shuttle 
effect of the polysulfide and severe pulverization of lithium 
anode are the main issues at high E/S ratio.
Experimental section
Preparation of Li–S pouch cells
The C/S cathode materials were prepared based on our pre-
vious publications [50]. The sulfur content in the C/S com-
posites is about 72%. The electrode slurry was obtained by 
mixing C/S composites, conductive agent SP, binder poly-
vinylidene fluoride (PVDF) with ratio of 88:10:2. N-methyl-
2-pyrrolidone (NMP) was used as dispersant. The electrode 
slurry was coated on the surface of the Al film by coating 
machine. After that, the as-prepared electrode film was dried 
at a vacuum atmosphere for 12 h at 60 °C. By controlling 
the chink of the coating machine, two areal density electrode 
films were obtained (0.95 g ­dm−2, 1.75 g ­dm−2). The sulfur 
loadings were 5.8 mg ­cm−2 and 10.8 mg ­cm−2, respectively. 
Rectangles with a size of 57 mm × 58 mm were punched as 
cathodes for Li–S pouch cells.
The copper-lithium composite belt (Tianjin Zhong-
neng Lithium Co., Ltd.) was used as anode with a size of 
60 mm × 60 mm. A layer-by-layer process was used to alter-
nate the cathodes and anodes with Celgard 2400 polypropyl-
ene membranes as the separator in a Li–S pouch cell.
The cathode and anode were welding by mash welder 
with Al lug and Cu/Ni lug, respectively. 1.0 M lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with 2.0 wt% 
­LiNO3 additive dissolved in dimethyl ether (DME) and 
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) with a volume ratio of 1:1 was used as 
electrolyte. The amounts of the electrolyte were designed as 
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) mass ratio of 3 and 10, respectively. 
All the preparing process of Li–S pouch cells is accom-
plished in the drying room (dew point ≤ -45℃). The whole 
preparing process of Li–S pouch cell is displayed in Fig. 1.
Material characterization and electrochemical 
measurements
The morphology of the C/S cathode composites was 
observed by using scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
MIRA 3 LMU TESCAN, USA) and transmission electron 
microscope (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIX, FEI, USA). ­N2 
adsorption measurements were performed on ASAP 2460 
analyzer (Micromeritics, USA) at 77 K. The as-prepared 
Li–S pouch cells were tested in a galvanostatic mode within 
a voltage range of 1.7–2.8 V in Xinwei battery tester at the 
current density of 0.1 °C.
Results and discussion
Material characterization and design of Li–S pouch 
cell
The C/S cathode composites were prepared according to our 
previous publications. The morphology of the C/S cathode 
composites was observed by SEM and TEM, as shown 
in Fig. S1. To investigate the evolutions of the electrode, 
Li–S pouch cells were prepared by stacking method with 
four pieces of cathode and five pieces of anode (Fig. 2a 
and b). In detail, the area of the cathode is about 33 ­cm2 
(57 mm × 58 mm). The sulfur loadings are designed as 
5.8 mg/cm2 and 10.8 mg/cm2, respectively. The anode area 
is 36 ­cm2 (60 mm × 60 mm). The electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) 
mass ratios are 3:1 and 10:1, respectively. Two kinds of sul-
fur loading and E/S ratio were designed to study the influ-
ence of sulfur loading and E/S ratio on the electrochemical 
performance of Li–S pouch cell.
To clearly demonstrate the influence of S loading and 
E/S ratio on the electrochemical performance of Li–S pouch 
cell, two part discussions were made as follows: (1) low E/S 
ratio with different S loading and (2) high E/S ratio with 
different loading.

Low E/S ratio with different S loading
Figure 3a shows the discharge/charge curves of Li–S pouch 
cell with E/S = 3 and S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2. It only 
reaches an initial discharge capacity of 570 mAh (740 mAh 
­g−1, Fig. 3b) at 0.1 °C. This low initial capacity is mainly 
ascribed to the bad wettability of the cathode under low 
E/S ratio. Therefore, it suffers from poor lithium-ion trans-
port ability and low active material utilization [51]. This is 
because the low E/S ratio in the pouch cell is failed to ensure 
the full capacity release of sulfur active materials. Besides, 
from the ­10th discharge curve (Fig. 3a), it can be clearly 
observed that the voltage plateau at 2.1 V is beginning to 
decrease compared with the former discharge curves. Even 
for the ­20th discharge curve, the pouch cell demonstrates a 
large overpotential. With the continuous discharge/charge 
process, the voltage plateau originally at 2.1 V even disap-
peared with the plateau at 2.3 V left only. As it has been 
well known, the main capacity contribution of Li–S battery 
is obtained from the plateau at 2.1 V, which represents the 
transformation of soluble LiPSs to ­Li2S [52]. The disappear-
ance of plateau at 2.1 V means that there is only one step 
electrochemical reaction remained (transformation from ­S8 
to soluble LiPS). Therefore, the capacity of the pouch cell 
suffers from a rapid decrease.
In terms of the whole rapid capacity fading, two fading 
processes should be divided (Fig. 3c). (1) The rapid capacity 
fading at the initial 4 cycles is caused by the precipitation of 
solid products ­Li2S2/Li2S on the cathode framework [53]. (2) 
The sudden capacity decrease at ­30th cycle is related to the 
severe polarization increase upon cycling. As for the further 
reason, it can be concluded as follows. With the increase 
of discharge/charge cycles and LiPSs concentration in the 
electrolyte, the soluble LiPSs will be precipitated from the 
electrolyte solution, leading to increased electrolyte viscos-
ity and poor lithium ion conductivity. In fact, the results can

High E/S ratio with different S loading
Figure 5a shows the discharge/charge profiles of Li–S pouch 
cell with E/S = 10 and S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2. The initial 
capacity of the cell is 720 mAh (935 mAh ­g−1, Fig. 5b) at 
0.1 °C, which is much higher than the Li–S pouch cell with 
E/S = 3 and S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2 (570 mAh, 740 mAh 
­g−1). This higher initial capacity is attributed to the increased 
E/S ratio, which could ensure sufficient wettability of the 
electrode and higher active materials utilization. In addition, 
the discharge/charge profiles of Li–S pouch cell (E/S = 10, S 
loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2) exhibit typical two plateaus at 2.3 V 
and 2.1 V without any plateau disappearance from beginning 
to the end compared with the pouch cell with E/S = 3 and 
S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2. This indicates that full two-step 
electrochemical reactions (solid-phase ­S8 → liquid phase 
LiPSs → solid-phase ­Li2S) are existed in this Li–S pouch 
cell [57]. Therefore, it shows gradual capacity fading other 
than sudden capacity fading during the whole electrochemi-
cal cycles.
The capacity fading of Li–S pouch cell with E/S = 10 and 
S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2 is assigned to the shuttle effect of 
soluble LIPSs and pulverization of lithium anode, which 
is different from pouch cell with E/S = 3 and S loading of 
5.8 mg ­cm−2. Figure 5c shows the cycling performance of 
Li–S pouch cell with E/S = 10 and S loading of 5.8 mg ­cm−2. 
Three stages are divided for the whole 100 cycles. The initial 
capacity fading in stage 1 is also caused by the precipitation 
of solid products ­Li2S2/Li2S on the cathode framework. A 
relatively stable stage 2 can be observed, which means the 
stable electrochemical reaction. This is also consistent with 
the position of the discharge/charge profiles ­(5th to ­60th), 
which overlap well, indicating superior cycling stability. For 
the stage 3 in the cycling curve, the gradual capacity fading 
is ascribed to the active material loss and pulverization of 
lithium anode with the increase of cycle numbers [58].

Discussion of potential challenges for designing 
high‑energy Li–S pouch cells
Although the theoretical gravimetric energy density of Li–S 
cells is estimated to be 2600 W h ­kg−1, the practical energy 
density is limited by important parameters such as sulfur 
loading, the sulfur content, and the ratio of electrolyte/sul-
fur (E/S). Therefore, the high-energy density will only be 
achieved with high sulfur loadings and low E/S ratio. Bonick 
et al. modeled the gravimetric energy density as a function 
of sulfur loading and E/S ratio for a Li–S cell, which is con-
sisted of 75 wt% sulfur positive electrode and an average 
discharge potential of 2.15 V [60]. It was founded that E/S 
ratio < 4 will likely be necessary to reach 400 W h ­kg−1, 
and < 3 to reach 500 W h ­kg−1. Additionally, the sulfur 
loading should be above at least 3 mg ­cm−2. However, it is 
difficult to achieve high sulfur utilization and long cycling 
performance with low E/S ratio and high sulfur loading. This 
result has been demonstrated in our above analysis. There-
fore, the main challenges for designing high-energy density 
Li–S pouch cell are lowering E/S ratio and improving sulfur 
loading.
First, lowering the E/S ratio is a challenge for the liquid 
electrolytes due to the precipitation of polysulfide (con-
firmed in 3.2 low E/S ratio with different S loading). This 
is because the discharge mechanism of Li–S cells is from 
solid phase ­(S8) to liquid phase (polysulfide solution, ­Li2Sx, 
4 ≤  ×  ≤ 8) and finally to solid ­(Li2S2, ­Li2S). That is to say, it 
requires all active sulfur to dissolve into the electrolyte dur-
ing the first discharge platform. If lowering the E/S ratio, a 
series of effects will be occurred, including the increase of 
LiPSs concentration in the electrolyte, increased electrolyte 
viscosity, decreased ionic conductivity, and the precipita-
tion of LiPSs from the electrolyte. As a result, this impedes 
the formation of solid ­Li2S, leading to incomplete electro-
chemical reaction. Therefore, based on the dissolution dis-
charge mechanism, the most efficient method is to develop 
new type electrolyte, which could improve the solubility of 
LiPSs in electrolyte. However, this will be a contrary direc-
tion for the research community, which paid much effort to 
decrease LiPSs solubility to inhibit the shuttle effect. On the 
other hand, it could develop solid electrolytes to replace the

Second, improving the sulfur loading is also a main chal-
lenge for achieving high-energy density Li–S pouch cell. 
Because of the low electrical conductivity of sulfur and 
lithium sulfide, sulfur active materials are only effectively 
worked when they are composited with conductive hosts 
or near conductive networks. However, the employment of 
these conductive materials reduces the sulfur loading of the 
S-based electrode. Therefore, to accomplish the high sul-
fur loading electrode, the much thicker electrode should 
be coated. However, doing this will reduce the gravimetric 
energy density of the whole Li–S pouch cell. Meanwhile, the 
thick electrode will bring some problems, such as difficult 
li-ion transport, the expansion of electrode film during the 
electrochemical cycles, and this begs a question: How to 
balance the sulfur loading and E/S ratio? During the past 
decades, great achievements have been obtained for improv-
ing the electrochemical cycles in Li–S coin cell. However, 
when facing the practical application of Li–S pouch cells, 
the poor cycle performance is still existed. We think that 
without a suitable balance between sulfur loading and E/S 
ratio, achieving high-energy density with superior cycling 
performance will be great challenge for Li–S pouch cell.
Conclusions
The relationship between capacity fading and sulfur load-
ing and E/S ratio in Li–S pouch cell has been studied in 
our work. We proposed two different capacity fading 
mechanisms for Li–S pouch cells with different E/S ratio 
(3, 10) and sulfur loading (5.8 mg ­cm−2, 10.8 mg ­cm−2). 
For the pouch cell with low E/S ratio (3), with the increase 
of LiPSs concentration in the electrolyte, the soluble LiPSs 
will be precipitated from the electrolyte. Besides, due to the 
increased electrolyte viscosity, the transport for lithium-ions 
becomes difficult. As a result, the precipitated LiPSs are 
failed to react with lithium-ions, leading to incomplete elec-
trochemical reaction. However, for the pouch cell with high 
E/S ratio (10), the main failure mechanisms are the shuttle 
effect of soluble LIPSs and pulverization of lithium anode. 
Due to the presence of enough electrolyte, the LIPSs could 
migrate between the cathode and anode without precipita-
tion. On the other hand, the disabled Li anode contributed 
to a failed pouch cell. The presence of powdery Li indicated 
that the pulverization of lithium anode finally terminated the 
lifespan of Li–S pouch cells.
In conclusion, the prepared Li–S coin cells in the ref-
erences deliver a superior long-term cycling performance, 
which is ascribed to the enough electrolyte and lithium 
anode. Therefore, the main problem is the shuttle effect of 
the polysulfide during the electrochemical cycles in Li–S 
coin cells. However, for Li–S pouch cells, they show com-
plex failure mechanisms, including the incomplete elec-
trochemical reaction caused by the precipitation of poly-
sulfide (low E/S ratio), the shuttle effect of the polysulfide, 
and severe pulverization of lithium anode (high E/S ratio). 
Finally, based on the relationship between capacity fading 
and sulfur loading and E/S ratio, we discussed the two main 
challenges for designing high-energy density Li–S pouch 
cells. Only by finding the balance for E/S ratio and sulfur 
loading, high-energy density Li–S cells could be achieved.
