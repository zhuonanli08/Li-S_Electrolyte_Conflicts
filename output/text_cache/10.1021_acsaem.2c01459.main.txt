Homogeneous Organometallic Catalysts for Improved
Electrochemical Kinetics of Li−S Batteries

ABSTRACT: The dissolution of polysulfides into electrolytes and sluggish electro-
chemical conversion kinetics primarily impede the practical realization of Li−S batteries.
Homogeneous catalysis is an effective strategy to overcome the challenges involved
under lean electrolyte conditions. Metallocenes, a class of organometallic compounds,
hold promise to anchor and catalyze polysulfides. In this study, we used first-principles
density functional theory (DFT) simulations to understand the role of metallocenes
(using titanocene (TiCp2) as an archetypical example) as homogeneous catalysts in the
electrolyte medium to suppress the shuttle effect and promote the reaction kinetics. The
calculated electrochemical stability window of TiCp2 reveals that the composition is
electrochemically inactive in the operating potential range of Li−S batteries and can thus
be leveraged as an additive to expedite the reduction kinetics of soluble lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs). We studied the detailed characteristic behavior of LiPS interactions
with TiCp2 in both gas and solvent phases and the kinetics of elementary sulfur
reduction reactions (SRRs). We found that TiCp2 provides adequate binding toward various LiPSs to mitigate the shuttle effect, and
the structural integrity of LiPSs is well retained without any chemical decomposition. The catalyzing effect of TiCp2 is evident from
the observed significant reduction in the SRR barriers, particularly for the rate-determining step, which is expected to favorably
promote the deposition of Li2S on the cathode surface. We further propose a mechanistic scheme of TiCp2 homogeneous catalyst-
boosted sulfur redox cycles. Overall, our simulations predicted moderate binding and improved kinetics of polysulfide chemistry with
the metallocene-based homogeneous catalyst and are expected to lead to a paradigm shift in the design of organometallic additives
for achieving high-performance metal−sulfur batteries.
KEYWORDS: homogeneous catalyst, additive, titanocene, shuttle effect, DFT
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur batteries (LiSBs) are inevitably being consid-
ered as a game-changing technology to meet the demand for
safe and high-performance energy storage systems for the next
generation. Li−S batteries work on the principles of electro-
chemical redox reactions (S + 2Li →Li2S) and possess an
incredibly high energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), theoretical
capacity (1675 mAh g−1), lower cost, and environmental
benignity.1,2 However, the commercialization of Li−S batteries
is hindered by major scientific hurdles such as the dissolution
of higher-order polysulfides into the electrolyte, known as the
“shuttle effect,” and sluggish electrochemical reaction kinetics
of the battery chemistry.2−4 The dissolution of higher-order
polysulfides into solvents such as ether results in a significant
loss of active materials, a short cycle life, and irreversible
capacity fading. On the other hand, the sluggish conversion
kinetics detrimentally affects the rate performance and
reversible specific capacity, which renders rapid charging
functionality impractical.5 Moreover, the limited mass-loading
issues caused by the poor electron conductivity of sulfur and
the volume change in the cathode material because of sulfur
agglomeration during cycling preclude the utilization of the
complete theoretical energy density in practical applica-
tions.6−8
In the last decade, the improvement of the specific capacity
and cycling stability in LiSBs by constructing conductive
scaffolds has received significant attention.6,9 The physical
prevention of lithium polysulfide (LiPS) leakage using
nanocarbon materials with a variety of dimensional config-
urations has been shown to reduce LiPS crossover.10,11
Mesoporous carbon, graphene, hollow carbon spheres,
metal−organic framework particles, hollow carbon nanofibers,
and conductive polymers are used as preferred carbon-based
host materials.12−15 In recent years, to anchor LiPSs and boost
their conversion rates via catalytic processes, techniques

carbon surface have been proposed.16−18 In addition, the
longevity and electrode stability of LiSBs can be improved
using functional binders and separators in conjunction with
lithium metal protectors.19−22 Although sizeable progress has
been made in the improvement of the cycle life and the
understanding of polysulfide reaction mechanisms in the Li−S
system, for the vast majority of the studies, thin-film sulfur
electrodes (2 mg cm−2) and/or flooded electrolytes are used,
with an E/S ratio (mL/g) of at least 20.23 The high volume of
electrolyte results in a low energy density in Li−S batteries.
For a competitive energy density and low cost of LiSBs,
reduction of the electrolyte volume to at least E/S < 5 is
essential.24 However, lowering the E/S ratio inevitably results
in adverse conditions in Li−S batteries. First, they exhibit weak
reversibility and poor sulfur reaction kinetics because of the
low quantity of the polysulfide redox mediator for the
transition from higher-order LiPSs to lower-order ones and
significant polarization. Second, most carbon-based cathode
materials generally require a higher volume of electrolyte to
moisten the cathode surface. However, under lean electrolyte
conditions, the irregular and uncontrollable accumulation of
insulating S/Li2S passivates the electrolyte−electrode interface
regulating the sulfur redox reactions further, and dwindles the
cell capacity.25
In recent years, several strategies have been investigated to
improve sulfur reactions in a lean electrolyte environment.26
The approaches include the use of heterogeneous electro-
catalysts or homogeneous/heterogeneous mediators to accel-
erate the S8/Li2S conversion kinetics and modulate the
morphology of solid accumulation.27−29 Heterogeneous
electrocatalysis of polysulfides in cathodes such as metals,
organic composites, and heteroatom-doped carbon materials
demonstrates performance improvements in terms of de-
creased polarization, extended cycle lifetimes, and improved
rate capabilities.26 However, the catalytic reaction efficacy in
heterogeneous catalysis is considerably affected by the limited
accessibility of the catalyst surface and the poor diffusion of
LiPS from the bulk electrolyte to the catalytic reaction sites.26
The limitations of heterogeneous catalysis in addressing the
shuttling and slow kinetics of polysulfides can be overcome
with a homogeneous catalyst dissolved in the electrolyte
solution as an additive, which captures and accelerates the LiPS
conversion at the homogeneous interfaces.26,30 The use of
additives modifies the Li anode/electrolyte interface and alters
the polysulfide dissolution, thereby regulating the cathode−
electrolyte interfaces.31 For example, Wu et al. reported that
the use of biphenyl-4,4′-dithiol (BPD) as an additive for LiSB
considerably improved the capacity retention because of the
suppression of polysulfide dissolution and expedited charge−
discharge kinetics with BPD−short-chain polysulfide complex
formation.31 Liu et al. claimed that the electrolyte additive
dithiothreitol (DTT) can favorably mitigate polysulfide
shuttling and promote the reaction kinetics by selectively
cleaving the S−S bonds of polysulfides.32
Recently, organic additives with enhanced catalytic ability
and electrolyte stability have been used as homogeneous
catalysts in LiSBs because of their capability to capture LiPS,
boost the electrochemical reaction kinetics, and control Li2S
nucleation. For example, Luo et al. used density functional
theory (DFT) calculations and electrochemical character-
izations to study the effect of nickel chloride dimethoxy-
methane additives as homogeneous catalysts for LiSBs, and
they claimed that under lean electrolyte conditions, say E/S ≈
5, the battery exhibited a stable capacity of 784 mAh g−1 at 0.1
C for 500 cycles.33 Recently, metallocene-based organometallic
compounds with distinct electronic properties, along with a
valence electron count and modification of metal−carbon
(M−C) bonds, showed incredible catalytic properties in redox-
flow batteries (RFB),34−37 olefin polymerization,38,39 anti-
cancer reagents, etc.40 For example, Ding et al. found that the
design of Li−RFB with a cobaltocene redox mediator
improved the working potential to 1.7 V; in addition, the
working potential was increased to 2.1 V when methyl groups
were added to the Cp ring, illustrating the possibility of
tailoring the properties of metallocene through functionaliza-
tion.34 Another approach is to use metallocenes as extrinsic
redox mediators besides polysulfides to aid the polysulfide
conversion reactions and the growth of Li2S in LiSBs.41−43 A
number of imide-based redox mediators developed by Helms
and co-workers for Li−S chemistry have shown potential to
achieve high capacities.43 Aurbach and co-workers used redox
mediators to retrieve most of the theoretical capacity of Li2S
during the first activation and to lower the charging
overpotential to 2.9 V.44 In addition, a cobaltocene-based
redox mediator with its reduction potential within the
polysulfide reduction region was in turn found to act as an
electron-transfer agent between the polysulfides and the
conductive substrate.45 In contrast, metallocenes such as
nickelocene (NiCp2) and 1,1′-bis(diphenylphosphino)-
ferrocene (DPPF) are reported to be electrochemically inactive
within the LiSB operating potential window; however, they can
potentially act as chemical adsorbents for trapping poly-
sulfides.42 The striking disparity observed between NiCp2 and
DPPF compared with other metallocene-based redox media-
tors demonstrates that not all metallocenes can act as redox
mediators.42 In fact, the observation opens up a new avenue to
leverage metallocenes as electrolyte additives when their
electrochemical stability windows are outside of the operating
potential range of LiSBs. Although there are a significant
number of studies on metallocenes as redox mediators for
LiSBs, there exists a wide knowledge gap concerning the
development of homogeneous metallocene-based additives to
promote the sulfur conversion kinetics under lean electrolyte
conditions.
Herein, for the first time, we use metallocenes (titanocene
(TiCp2) as an archetypical example) as functional additives to
suppress the shuttle effect and enhance the electrochemical
performance of LiSBs. The unique structural feature of the
bent titanocene allows direct interactions of adsorbates with
the transition metal. TiCp2 has been previously found to
exhibit superior activity toward nitrogen reduction and oxygen
evolution reactions.40,46,47 To the best of our knowledge, no
studies have been conducted to investigate the LiPS interaction
with the bent titanocene. We employ first-principles DFT
calculations to understand the interactions of TiCp2 with
polysulfides. The TiCp2 homogeneous catalyst exhibits a
moderate binding strength toward various LiPS species and
acts as a catalyst to expedite the rate-determining steps in
sulfur reduction reactions (SRR) over the discharging process.
On the basis of insights obtained from the adsorption energy
calculations, we proposed a mechanistic scheme for the sulfur
redox cycle facilitated by the homogeneous TiCp2 catalyst.
During discharge, the higher chemical affinity of TiCp2 toward
LiPS compared with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) results in
the formation of the TiCp2@LiPS complex and promotes the

2. CALCULATION METHODOLOGY
All of the geometric optimization simulations were performed
using the DFT method. We used the B3LYP hybrid functional
along with the 6-31+G* basis set for the C/H/O and
LALNDZ effective core potentials (ECPs) for the Ti atom for
all of the calculations. To account for the solvent effect, the
COSMO-SMD implicit solvation model was used considering
diethyl ether as the medium with a dielectric constant of ε =
4.5, which is close to the dielectric constants of DME and 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL).48,49 We calculated Gibbs free energies at a
temperature of 298 K using the procedure outlined in our
previous study.50 The empirical dispersion correction utilizing
Grimme’s DFT-D2 method was employed to improve the
accuracy of the adsorption calculations. We used NWChem
software for all of the calculations.51
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Structure of TiCp2. We relaxed the titanocene
(TiCp2) at the B3LYP/(6-31+G* and LALNDZ) level of
theory, and the optimized configuration was found to result in
a bent geometry. The optimized structure (illustrated in Figure
1) has a Cp−M−Cp (θ) angle of 134.24°, and the calculated
bending angle (Y = (180 −θ)/2)) is 22.88°. The observed
value of Y is lower when compared with those of other bent
metallocenes such as VCp2, WCp2, and MoCp2 because there
are fewer nonbonding electrons to interact with the Cp ring of
the titanocene.40 Moreover, hydrogens in the Cp ring bent
outward because of the effective overlap of diffuse unoccupied
d orbitals with the ring.40 The calculated Cp−M−Cp (θ) angle
mainly depends on the overlap between the filled p orbitals of
C and d orbitals of Ti. The optimized geometry results in a
Cp−Ti bond length of 2.2 Å, comparable to those of other
works.40
3.2. Electrochemical Stability Window. It is generally
known that the interconversion of Li2S4 and Li2S (∼2.1 V vs
Li/Li+) and the conversion of S8 to Li2S4 (∼2.5 V vs Li/Li+)
are the two key electrochemical reaction steps in the redox
chemistry of S in LiSBs. Redox mediators are a class of
homogeneous catalysts whose electrochemical potentials are
carefully matched with the sulfur conversion reaction
potentials.42,43 In contrast to redox mediators, to work as
electrolyte additives, homogeneous catalysts for LiSBs must
have an inactive electrochemical redox behavior toward the
interconversion sulfur redox reactions within the operating
potential window of the batteries. In this regard, we calculated
the reduction and oxidation potentials of the studied TiCp2
based on the thermodynamic cycles shown in Figure 2. The
calculated reduction and oxidation potentials of TiCp2 with
respect to Li/Li+ are 0.35 and 1.98 V, respectively. It is evident
from the results that TiCp2 undergoes single-electron
reduction outside the operating window of the LiSB (1.8−
2.8 V vs Li/Li+), an important criterion for the design of
electrolyte additives. Again, the calculated oxidation potential
of 1.98 V is lower when compared with that of Li2S oxidation
(∼2.4 V vs Li/Li+). The oxidized TiCp2 in the working
potential range will get reduced back by receiving electrons
from the current collector. The observation illustrates that the
studied TiCp2 is not involved in the redox mediation process
for Li2S activation unlike redox mediators such as ferrocene
and decamethylferrocene.44 A similar kind of observation was
made in other metallocenes such as cobaltocene, nickelocene,
and dibenzenechromium; because of their lower oxidation
potentials compared with Li2S decomposition, they act as
chemical adsorbents.42,45 Overall, the comparison of the
electrochemical stability window of TiCp2 and the operating
potential range of LiSB highlights the relevance of TiCp2 as an
electrolyte additive, and TiCp2 is expected to chemically
adsorb and accelerate the reduction kinetics of LiPSs.

materials, are found to interact with the LiPSs in two binding
configurations.52 The Li binds with nonmetal atoms, whereas

the discharge performance of LiSBs with homogeneous

−0.494 eV). Next, three successive reduction processes from

the studied homogeneous catalyst accelerates the redox

3.5. Proposed Mechanisms of TiCp2 Interactions with
LiPSs. The proposed reaction mechanism of LiSB on the
TiCp2 homogeneous catalyst is illustrated in Figure 7. The
chosen catalyst, TiCp2, dissolves in the electrolyte as an
additive. Based on our simulation results, we found that the
affinity of TiCp2 toward LiPS (1.69 eV for Li2S8) is much
higher than that toward DME (0.70 eV). Hence, TiCp2 on the
electrolyte solution tends to bind with Li2S8 to form the
TiCp2−Li2S8 complex, liberating the DME. Our simulation
results show higher binding energies of LiPSs with the TiCp2
than that with DME, illustrating the effectiveness in
suppressing the shuttle effect. Moreover, the acceleration of
SRR during the discharge process is evident from the Gibbs
free energy calculations. This observation illustrates that the
studied TiCp2 catalyst could effectively accelerate the
reduction of soluble higher-order polysulfides to insoluble
Li2S. It is important to note that as the homogeneous catalyst
undergoes molecular interactions with polysulfides, it can
increase the utilization of the catalyst; that is, the addition of a
lower amount of TiCp2 into the electrolyte could accelerate a
larger amount of LiPS.33 Thus, the Li2S nucleation and growth
process is expected to improve on the cathode surface with the
aid of the TiCp2 catalyst, thereby improving the electro-
chemical performance. Previously, it has been experimentally
reported that the use of cobaltocene resulted in an increased
amount of Li2S precipitation on the cathode carbon cloth.45
Our observation of the reduced free energy barriers of SRR
indicates the facile transformation of higher-order LiPSs into
Li2S, which is well correlated with the experimental
observation.33,45 Next, during the charging process, because
of the much lower interaction between TiCp2 and S8 (0.30 eV)
compared with that of TiCp2 with DME (0.70 eV), TiCp2 is
expected to be liberated and subsequently dissolved and
coordinated with DME in the electrolyte solution, realizing the
sulfur redox reaction cycle (see Figure 7). Hence, the addition
of TiCp2 into the electrolyte reduces the LiPS shuttling due to
their stronger chemical interaction and accelerates the SRR
kinetics at homogeneous interfaces, which is essential for the
practical realization of LiSBs.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, for the first time, the homogeneous catalyst,
TiCp2, is introduced into the commonly used electrolyte as an
additive for Li−S batteries. We used DFT calculations to
understand the potential of TiCp2 as a homogeneous catalyst
by systematically exploring their interaction with LiPSs at
homogeneous interfaces. The electrochemical stability window
calculations reveal that TiCp2 can work as an additive to
accelerate the reduction kinetics of soluble LiPSs because of
the electrochemical inactivity within the operating potential
range of LiSBs. We studied the binding behavior of TiCp2 with
various LiPSs in both gas and solvent phases. The calculated
binding energies of LiPS with TiCp2 are in the range of 1.30−
2.52 eV. The chemical affinity of the TiCp2−LiPS complex
arises from the formation of a strong Ti−S bond. Further, the
comparison of calculated binding energies with that of the
commonly used DME electrolyte solvent demonstrates the
effectiveness of the studied homogeneous catalyst in
suppressing the shuttling of polysulfides. Our analysis of the
bonding characteristics illustrates that the physical vdW is
dominant for S8 interaction, whereas chemical interactions
