Electrocatalyst for High-Energy-Density Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have great potential as high-energy-
density energy storage devices. Electrocatalysts are widely adopted to accelerate the
cathodic sulfur redox kinetics. The interactions among the electrocatalysts, solvents, and
lithium salts significantly determine the actual performance of working Li−S batteries.
Herein, lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), a commonly used lithium
salt, is identified to aggravate surface gelation on the MoS2 electrocatalyst. In detail, the
trifluoromethanesulfonyl group in LiTFSI interacts with the Lewis acidic sites on the
MoS2 electrocatalyst to generate an electron-deficient center. The electron-deficient center
with high Lewis acidity triggers cationic polymerization of the 1,3-dioxolane solvent and
generates a surface gel layer that reduces the electrocatalytic activity. To address the above
issue, Lewis basic salt lithium iodide (LiI) is introduced to block the interaction between
LiTFSI and MoS2 and inhibit the surface gelation. Consequently, the Li−S batteries with
the MoS2 electrocatalyst and the LiI additive realize an ultrahigh actual energy density of
416 W h kg−1 at the pouch cell level. This work affords an effective lithium salt to boost the electrocatalytic activity in practical
working Li−S batteries and deepens the fundamental understanding of the interactions among electrocatalysts, solvents, and salts in
energy storage systems.
■INTRODUCTION
Developing next-generation energy storage devices with an
energy density far exceeding that of the currently commercial
lithium-ion batteries is essential to deal with the rapidly
growing demand for electric vehicles, portable electronics, and
smart grids.1−3 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are deemed as
promising candidates due to their attractive energy density of
2600 W h kg−1 in theory and the eco-friendly properties of
sulfur.4−7 A typical Li−S battery is composed of a sulfur/
carbon composite cathode, a lithium metal anode, and an
ether-based electrolyte.8−11 The sulfur/carbon composite
cathode undergoes complicated multielectron and multiphase
redox reactions between solid sulfur (S8), soluble lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs), and solid lithium sulfide (Li2S).12−15
Nevertheless, the above redox reactions are highly sluggish in
kinetics and have severely concealed the advantages of Li−S
batteries by triggering deteriorative reaction resistance,
degraded capacity along with cycling, and retarded rate
responses.16−19 Therefore, accelerating the sulfur redox
kinetics is necessary to realize high-performance Li−S
batteries.
Introducing electrocatalysts to the sulfur cathode constitutes
a straightforward approach to address the above kinetic issue.
Previous research studies have demonstrated that electro-
catalysts such as hetero-doped carbon,20−22 transition metal
compounds,23−25 and their composites26,27 can efficiently
reduce the reaction activation energy and promote the battery
performance. Generally, most electrocatalysts promote the
sulfur redox kinetics by affording strong chemical interactions
with polysulfides.28−30 In detail, the Lewis acidic sites of the
electrocatalysts interact with the electron-rich Lewis basic
sulfur atoms of polysulfides, lower the reaction barrier by
stabilizing the transient states, and promote the subsequent
redox kinetics.31−33 Various Lewis acidic electrocatalysts have
been proven to be effective for promoting the sulfur redox
kinetics.34,35 For instance, a great number of previous works
have demonstrated that metal disulfides [for instance,
molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)] with high-valence metal
elements exhibit strong Lewis acidity and thus endow favorable
sulfur redox kinetics in Li−S batteries.36,37
Although the sulfur redox kinetics can be accelerated by
introducing Lewis acidic electrocatalysts, it is worth noting that
lithium salts and solvents in the electrolyte with Lewis basic
sites inevitably interact with the Lewis acidic sites on the
electrocatalysts as well.38,39 For instance, 1,3-dioxolane (DOL),

In this contribution, LiTFSI is identified, for the first time, to
aggravate surface gelation on the MoS2 electrocatalyst in
working Li−S batteries. In detail, the CF3SO2−group in
LiTFSI interacts with the Lewis acidic sites on the MoS2
electrocatalyst and generates an electron-deficient center
following a nucleophilic substitution reaction. The electron-
deficient center with a stronger Lewis acidity triggers the
cationic ring-opening polymerization of the DOL solvent and
generates an odious surface gel layer that renders reduced
electrocatalytic activity of the MoS2 electrocatalyst. To address
the above issues at the source, a Lewis basic salt, lithium iodide
(LiI), is introduced to Li−S batteries to block the interaction
between LiTFSI and MoS2, avoid the generation of the
electron-deficient sites, and inhibit the subsequent surface
gelation. Consequently, Li−S batteries with the LiI additive
and the MoS2 electrocatalyst exhibit improved rate responses
up to 4.0 C, optimized discharge capacities (1380 mA h gS

for 7.1 mgS cm−2 cathode), and an ultrahigh actual energy
density of 416 W h kg−1 in practical 2.5 Ah-level pouch cells.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To systematically investigate the interactions between electro-
catalysts and lithium salts or solvents in the electrolyte, LiTFSI
and DOL were selected as the representative lithium salt and
solvent, respectively. In consideration of the electrocatalyst,
MoS2 was selected due to its high Lewis acidity and popularity
in Li−S batteries.42−44 When mixing MoS2, LiTFSI, and DOL
together in a reagent bottle, the quasi-solid gel was generated
rapidly, and the MoS2 particles were stuck to the bottle’s
bottom tightly (Figure 1a). A similar gelation phenomenon
also occurred in the system of DOL and MoS2 but was absent
in the system of DOL and LiTFSI (Figure S2). The above
results indicate that MoS2 plays an essential role in gelation.
Furthermore, taking the gelation time into account, the
gelation process was completed fast for the system of DOL,
LiTFSI, and MoS2 within only 5 min but took a rather long
time of about 10 days for the system of DOL and MoS2
(Figure 1b). Such a phenomenon indicates that the gelation
rate depends on the addition of LiTFSI. In brief, MoS2 triggers
the gelation, while LiTFSI aggravates the gelation, indicating
complicated interactions among the MoS2 electrocatalyst,
DOL solvent, and LiTFSI salt.
Detailed characterizations of the gel were carried out to
explore the aforementioned interactions. Fourier transform
infrared spectrometry was conducted to probe the composition
of the gel. The variations in the characterized signals of C−O
and C−H were explicitly observed (Figure S3). Furthermore,
gel permeation chromatography analysis was carried out to
determine the molecular weight of the gel to be around 104 g
mol−1 and manifest the polymeric nature of the gel (Figure
S4). Considering the unsaturated structure of DOL, it is
hypothesized that the gel was generated from the polymer-
ization of DOL. The 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C
NMR) spectrum proves the hypothesis that the signals
emerged at 67.1 ppm (A1) and 95.8 ppm (B1) represent the
carbon atoms in −O−CH2−CH2−O−and −O−CH2−O−,

The surface property of the MoS2 electrocatalyst after
gelation is the most concerning issue for electrocatalysis in Li−
S batteries since all the electrocatalytic processes take place on
the electrocatalyst surface. To detect the surface variation in
the MoS2 electrocatalyst before and after gelation, scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) were employed. Compared with the
MoS2 electrocatalyst before gelation, the MoS2 electrocatalyst
after gelation was interspersed with blurry spots which were
considered as the insulating gel (Figure S6). Furthermore, the
characterized layered MoS2 region and gel region were
explicitly distinguished in the TEM image (Figure 1d), and
the nanometer-sized gel layer wrapped the MoS2 nanoparticles
tightly (Figure S7). Observations using high-angle annular field
scanning TEM and corresponding energy-dispersive spectros-
copy further proved the existence of carbon and oxygen in the
surface gel layer (Figure S8). Meanwhile, the bulk properties of
the MoS2 electrocatalyst before and after the gelation were
characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).
It is seen that the characteristic Mo and S XPS peaks of the
MoS2 electrocatalyst before and after gelation had identical
binding energies (Figure 1e and Table S1). Raman spectros-
copy provided analogous results with no structural change
(Figure S9).45 Therefore, the gelation does not affect the bulk
properties but generates a gel layer on the surface of the MoS2
electrocatalyst that is expected to influence the electrocatalytic
performances in working Li−S batteries.
The effect of surface gelation on electrocatalytic perform-
ances was accordingly evaluated. Concretely, MoS2 nano-
particles were dispersed and loaded on separators (denoted as
MoS2 separators, Figure S10), and Li2S6 symmetric cells were
assembled with the MoS2 separators for evaluating the sulfur
redox kinetics. The MoS2 separators after gelation rendered the
cells with a retarded reaction charge transfer resistance (Rct,
3.67 Ω after gelation while 2.29 Ω before gelation) as seen in
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) spectra and
passivated peak current densities (8.4 mA cm−2 after gelation
while 12.0 mA cm−2 before gelation) as seen in cycling
voltammetry (CV) profiles (Figure S11). The decreased sulfur
redox kinetics illustrates that the surface gel layer reduces the
electrocatalytic activity of the MoS2 electrocatalyst. Worse still,
Li−S coin cells coupled with the MoS2 separators displayed ca.
70 mA h g−1 decrease in the discharge specific capacity after
gelation, further manifesting that surface gelation is poisonous
for electrocatalysts (Figure S12). Therefore, the surface gel
layer covering the MoS2 electrocatalyst impairs the electro-
catalytic performances and renders frustrated sulfur redox
kinetics.
To inhibit the surface gelation, it is necessary to elucidate
the underlying mechanism of surface gelation and clarify the
fundamental variables that regulate surface gelation therein.
Since LiTFSI aggravates surface gelation (Figure 1a), it is of
great necessity to investigate the specific role of LiTFSI in
gelation by revealing its concrete interactions with DOL and
the MoS2 electrocatalyst.
The functional group of LiTFSI that functions during the
surface gelation process was first located (Figures 2a and S13).

using corresponding NaTFSI or Pyr14TFSI (Figure 2a, left and

suggesting that the aggravated surface gelation was irrelevant

Accordingly, the O atom in the CF3SO2−group was
speculated as the actual site that interacted with MoS2 and
greatly activated the subsequent gelation. To validate the above
argument, methyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (TfOMe), a
nonionized molecule containing only CF3SO2−, was mixed
with DOL and MoS2. As expected, the fluid turned into a gel
phase rapidly, similar to that in LiTFSI (Figure S14). Based on
the above results, the functional group in LiTFSI for activating
the gelation process is the Lewis basic O atom in the CF3SO2−
group rather than the Li+ or N atom. Accordingly, diminishing
the Lewis basicity of LiTFSI by molecular design on the O
atom is expected to inhibit surface gelation and promote the
electrocatalytic performance in working Li−S batteries.
To chemically explain the specific role of the Lewis basic O
atom in the CF3SO2−group in aggravating surface gelation, a
hypothesized chain-initiation mechanism for DOL ring-open-
ing polymerization was proposed based on the Lewis acid−
base theory (Figure 2b). Concretely, the O atom in the
CF3SO2−group interacts with the Lewis acidic Mo atom in
the MoS2 electrocatalyst, and the lone-pair electron of O 2p is
shared with the Mo 3d empty orbital to form an oxonium ion.
Then, the electropositive center is transferred to the adjacent S
atom in the CF3SO2−group, and an electron-deficient S center
is generated with stronger Lewis acidity than the original Lewis
active site of the Mo atom in the MoS2 electrocatalyst.
Following that, the lone-pair electron of the O atom in the
DOL solvent attacks the Lewis acidic S atom and triggers the
loss of the CF3
−group. Afterward, an electropositive center is
generated on the O atom of the DOL molecule and initiates
the subsequent ring-opening polymerization of DOL. Consid-
ering the rate-determining step of the ring-opening polymer-
ization is the activation of DOL (i.e., the chain-initiation step
based on the polymer chemistry), higher Lewis acidity of the
active site affords a higher rate for surface gelation. The
interaction between LiTFSI and the MoS2 electrocatalyst
affords a highly active electron-deficient S center through Mo−
O−S triatomic interactions that is higher in Lewis acidity than
the Mo atom in the MoS2 electrocatalyst. Consequently, the
apparent gelation rate was determined with LiTFSI and other
molecules with the CF3SO2−group.
To verify the above hypothetical mechanism, density
functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. First,
the O atom in the CF3SO2−group binds to MoS2 with the
Mo−O1 bond with a bond length of 2.16 Å (Figure 2c) when
the LiTFSI molecule is absorbed on the surface of MoS2 (110).
Obvious interactions were observed according to the differ-
ential charge density (Figure S15). The increased charge
density between the Mo atom in MoS2 and the O atom in
LiTFSI illustrates a strong electronic interaction between the
Lewis acidic electrocatalyst and salt, suggesting that MoS2 and
LiTFSI are susceptible to reaction. Simultaneously, the charge
density within LiTFSI was altered, resulting in corresponding
bonds being weakened or strengthened. Bond length analysis
afforded further quantitative information on the changes. The
C1−S1 bond was lengthened by 0.008 Å, while other main
chemical bonds including C1−F1, O2−S1, and N1−S1 bonds
were shortened by 0.008, 0.012, and 0.020 Å, respectively
(Figure 2d). The above results illustrate that the C1−S1 bond
tends to break when LiTFSI interacts with MoS2, correspond-
ing to a CF3
−leaving group and higher Lewis acidity of the S
atom afterward. Evidently, the DFT results are in good
agreement with the hypothetical mechanism for the generation
of an electron-deficient S center in the CF3SO2−group.
To ascertain the interaction between the electron-deficient S
atom and the DOL solvent, the interaction between the DOL
molecule and the residual part of LiTFSI after removing LiCF3
(i.e., the CF3SO2N−S+O2 residue, Figure 2e) was calculated.
Strong interactions between the S atom in the LiTFSI residue
and the O atom in DOL were observed through the differential
charge density analysis results (Figure S16) and the
quantitative bond length analysis results. Concretely, the
C2−O3 and C3−O3 bonds were lengthened by 0.05 and 0.03
Å, respectively, while the bond length of C2−O4 was shortened
by 0.03 Å (Figure 2f). Consequently, the original symmetric
DOL molecule became distorted due to the asymmetric
interactions with the LiTFSI residue and a strong tendency for
the C2−O3 bond cleavage, and further DOL polymerization
initiation is induced. The above calculation results additionally
prove the validity of the hypothetical mechanism in Figure 2b
showing that DOL polymerization is triggered by the electron-
deficient S center.
Based on the above analysis, weakening the interactions
between MoS2 and LiTFSI to avoid the generation of the
electron-deficient Lewis acidic S center is theoretically effective
to inhibit the surface gelation on the MoS2 electrocatalyst and
promote the corresponding electrocatalytic performances.
Herein, LiI as an additive was introduced into the electrolyte
to block the interaction between MoS2 and LiTFSI and inhibit
the surface gelation considering the Lewis basic property of LiI.

Considering the above reaction, surface gelation on the
MoS2 electrocatalyst is effectively inhibited (Figure 3e).
Concretely, the reaction between LiI and MoS2 weakens the
interaction between LiTFSI and MoS2, prevents the generation
of the electron-deficient S center, and avoids the gelation
aggravation phenomenon. Additionally, the Lewis acidic MoS2
is surrounded by Lewis basic I−and is partly reduced to Mo3+
with lower Lewis acidity, thus decreasing the overall Lewis
acidity of the MoS2 electrocatalyst and eliminating the surface
gelation at the source.
To evaluate the gel inhibition effect of LiI in boosting the
sulfur redox kinetics on the MoS2 electrocatalyst, kinetic
evaluation on the liquid−liquid, liquid−solid, and solid−liquid
conversions was conducted. To probe the kinetics in liquid−
liquid conversions, Li2S6 symmetric cells were assembled with
MoS2-coated Al foils as electrodes (Figure S23). The Li2S6
symmetric cells with LiI exhibited a 2.3 times higher peak
current density and 2.2 times smaller Rct than those of Li2S6
symmetric cells without LiI in the CV profiles and EIS spectra,
respectively (Figures 4a and S24), indicating improved liquid−
liquid redox kinetics. Meanwhile, the potentiostatic inter-
mittent titration technique was used on Li2S8 cells to probe the
liquid−solid conversion kinetics (Figure S25). The Li2S8 cells
with LiI displayed a peak time around 2000 s earlier than that
of the Li2S8 cells without LiI and a peak current that was 1.15
times higher that of the Li2S8 cells without LiI. Evidently, the
liquid−solid conversion kinetics was also strengthened by the
addition of LiI. Furthermore, linear sweep voltammetry was
conducted to probe the solid−liquid conversion kinetics. In
detail, the Li2S8 cells with LiI afforded a 20 mV lower Li2S
oxidization voltage at 0.2 mA than that of the Li2S8 cells
without LiI, proving the promoted kinetics of the solid−liquid
conversion as well (Figure S26). According to the above
results, it is concluded that the gel inhibition attributed by LiI
effectively unlocks the electrocatalytic activity of the MoS2
electrocatalyst and accelerates full-range sulfur redox kinetics.
The gel inhibition effect of LiI was further evaluated in
working Li−S batteries. Li−S coin cells were first evaluated
with MoS2 separators and a blank electrolyte or LiI electrolyte.
Rate performances were evaluated on the regular-sulfur-loading
cathodes (1.2 mgS cm−2, Figure 4b). The cells with LiI
exhibited superior discharge specific capacities of 1208, 1077,
1006, 931, 795, and 655 mA h g−1 at 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and
4.0 C (1 C = 1672 mA gS
−1), respectively, 12.8−103% higher
than those of the cells without LiI (Figure S27). In addition,
the introduction of LiI also endowed the Li−S batteries with
higher long-term cycling stability. Concretely, the capacity
retention of the Li−S coin cells with LiI was 74.5% after 200
cycles under 0.5 C while that of the blank cells was only 56.7%
(Figure S28). Even under a high cycling rate of 2.0 C, the Li−S
coin cells with LiI delivered an initial discharge specific
capacity more than 200 mA h g−1 higher than the cells without
LiI and cycled stably for over 250 cycles (Figure 4c). It is
worth noting that LiI itself is electrochemically inert within the
working voltage range of Li−S batteries (Figure S29) and
cannot promote the performances of Li−S batteries without
the MoS2 electrocatalyst (Figure S30). Therefore, the above
superb battery performances under working conditions
evidently prove the recovered electrocatalytic activity of the
MoS2 electrocatalyst due to the gel inhibition effect of LiI.
Li−S coin cells with 4.4 mgS cm−2 high-sulfur-loading
cathodes were further assembled and evaluated under practical
conditions. Evidently, the introduction of LiI rendered high-
sulfur-loading Li−S coin cells with excellent rate performances
(Figure 4d). A superior discharge specific capacity of 742 mA h
g−1 was achieved under 0.4 C in the cells with LiI, while a
limited discharge specific capacity of only 393 mA h g−1 was
obtained in the cells without LiI. According to the charge−
discharge profiles, reduced polarization and extended discharge
plateaus in the cells with LiI were identified to manifest kinetic
promotion in full-range sulfur redox reactions (Figure S31).
Moreover, the cells with LiI exhibited superb long-term cycling
stability with a capacity retention rate of 81.0% for 75 cycles
under 0.1 C (Figure S32) while that for the cells without LiI
was reduced to 52.7%. Therefore, the Lewis basic LiI is proven
to be effective for gel inhibition and electrocatalyst activation
under practical high-sulfur-loading working conditions.

To fully demonstrate the feasibility of employing electro-
catalytic MoS2 and LiI for gel inhibition in practical high-
energy-density devices, 2.5 Ah-level Li−S pouch cells with the
MoS2 electrocatalyst and LiI electrolyte were assembled. In
detail, the areal sulfur loading was controlled to be 7.1 mgS
cm−2 on a single side, and the electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio
was set to 3.5 and 3.0 gelectrolyte gS
−1 for the 350 W h kg−1-level
and 400 W h kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells, respectively. The 350
W h kg−1-level Li−S pouch cells exhibited smooth discharge
profiles in the first cycle with a high discharge specific capacity
of 1228 mA h gS

kg−1 at a current density of 0.05 C (Figure S33). Moreover, the
pouch cells could operate stably for 24 cycles with superb
capacities and energy densities (Figure S34). When the E/S
ratio was reduced, the actual energy density of the Li−S pouch
cells was further promoted to 416 W h kg−1 with an initial

−1, and the 400 W h kg−1-

maintained Coulombic efficiencies (Figures 4e and S35).

of all the Li−S pouch cell components including the
electrodes, electrolyte, separators, current collectors, tabs,
and the package (Table S3). The Li−S pouch cell perform-
ances fully manifested the success of the LiI and MoS2 couple
in practical high-energy-density Li−S pouch cells and further
confirmed that the LiI additive can efficiently unlock the
electrocatalytic activity of the Lewis acidic MoS2 electrocatalyst
by inhibiting the surface gelation.
The mechanism of lithium salt regulation on the surface
gelation of DOL on MoS2 and corresponding effects are shown
in Figure 5. In the blank electrolyte, the CF3SO2−group in
LiTFSI interacts with the Lewis acidic sites on MoS2 to result
in the loss of CF3
−and generation of an electron-deficient
center. The electron-deficient center exhibits higher Lewis
acidity compared with that of the pristine MoS2 that interacts
strongly with the DOL solvent, aggravates the surface gelation
on the MoS2 electrocatalyst, and deteriorates the electro-
catalytic activity toward the sulfur redox reactions (Figure 5,
left). In the LiI electrolyte, however, Lewis basic I−
preferentially interacts with the Lewis acidic sites on MoS2
and blocks the interaction between MoS2 and LiTFSI.
Consequently, the polymerization of DOL is inhibited, and
the electrocatalytic activity of MoS2 is unlocked to afford
promoted battery performances (Figure 5, right).
■CONCLUSIONS
The interactions among the MoS2 electrocatalyst, DOL
solvent, and LiTFSI salt in Li−S batteries show that LiTFSI
aggravates the surface gelation of DOL on the MoS2
electrocatalyst. In detail, the CF3SO2−group in LiTFSI
interacts with the Lewis acidic sites on the MoS2 electrocatalyst
to generate an electron-deficient center that aggravates the
cationic polymerization of DOL. The gel layer covers the
surface of the MoS2 electrocatalyst and renders reduced
electrocatalytic activity. To address the above issues, a Lewis
basic additive LiI is introduced to Li−S batteries to block the
interactions between MoS2 and LiTFSI, avoid the generation
of the electron-deficient center, and inhibit surface gelation.
Consequently, Li−S batteries with the LiI additive and the
MoS2 electrocatalyst exhibit improved rate responses up to 4.0
C, optimized discharge capacities (1380 mA h gS
−1 for 7.1 mgS
cm−2 cathode), and an ultrahigh actual energy density of 416
W h kg−1 in practical 2.5 Ah-level pouch cells. This work not
only affords an effective lithium salt to boost the electro-
catalytic activity of Lewis acidic electrocatalysts in practical
high-energy-density Li−S batteries but also enlightens
fundamental understanding of the interactions among electro-
catalysts, solvents, and salts in energy storage systems.
