Experimental Section
Materials. 
Commercial Li-S electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME + 1wt% LiNO3) and LiNO3 was purchased from DodoChem. Production of LiFPB salt: 0.0326 g LiBH4 powder and 5 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) were added into a 25 mL round bottomed flask, and then the flask was sealed with reverse plug and filled with nitrogen gas. After that, 1 g of perfluorinated pinacol was added into the reaction flask with ice bath under stirring conditions. Then, removing the ice bath and the reaction was conduct at 60 °C for 10 hours. Finally, the solvent was removed under vacuum conditions to obtain the crude product of LiFPB. Dissolve 1 g of crude product in 1 mL of THF and precipitate with 10 mL n-hexane. The powder state product is obtained after drying it under vacuum at 100 °C and the yield is about 82.3%. Considering electrolyte conductivity, viscosity and the battery performance, LiFPB-containing electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.2M LiFPB salt, 0.8M LiTFSI salt and 1wt% LiNO3 into DME and stirred until completely dissolved, then added the DOL with the same volume as DME. For the sulfur electrode, The S, Superp, and PVDF were mixed at a weight ratio of 6:3:1 by milling, and Add N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and mix thoroughly. The corresponding slurry was cast onto a carbon-coated aluminum (Al) foil and dried at 60 °C for 4 hours and further dried at 80 °C for 12 hours under vacuum, the S load is approximately 1.2mg cm−2.
Material characterization.
The morphology of Li-metal was investigated by an SEM (Hitachi S-4800, operated at 10 kV) and AFM (Agilent 5400AFM). Li was deposited with different capacity at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out by the ESCALAB 250 XPS with monochromatic Al kα radiation, and all the binding energy values are fitted based on the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Li was deposited with 5 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2, and stripped/deposited Li with 1 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2 for 5 cycles. The Li-metal used for XPS, SEM and AFM tests were disassembled from coin cells and washed by DME and held in a vacuum until all the electrolyte residue was removed. The Raman spectra of the electrolyte was measured in a Thermo Scientific DXRXI system with excitation from an Ar laser at 532 nm between 100 and 1500 cm−1. The electrolyte density was measured by a 5 mL pipette, and the average of five tests was used as the final value. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to evaluate the interfacial impedance of lithium-lithium cells.[1] The data were recorded at room temperature over the frequency range from 7 MHz to 100 mHz by applying a sine wave with an amplitude of 10 mV. NMR of the electrolytes were performed on a NMR (Bruker AV400 spectrometer), respectively. For NMR tests, 0.1 M LiFPB in DMSO-d6 was used as an external reference. The NMR sample and the external reference were separated by a coaxial NMR tube. All the 2032 coin-type cells mentioned above were assembled in an argon filled glovebox with moisture and oxygen contents less than 0.1 ppm. Time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) analysis at negative modes was performed with a TOF-SIMS instrument (IONTOF GmbH, Germany).
Electrochemical measurements.
All 2032 coin-cells were fabricated in an argon-filled glovebox, and PP membrane was used as the separator in the coin cell. For lithium-copper CE tests, deposited 1 mAh cm−2 capacity with a current of 0.5 mA cm−2 onto the Cu followed by charging to 1 V. For the Aurbach CE test, Li deposition with 5 mAh cm−2 at a current of 0.5 mA cm−2 was carried out and then stripped to 1 V, and then deposited 5 mAh cm−2 on Cu under 0.5 mA cm−2, and stripped/deposited Li with 1 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2 for 10 cycles and stripped Li to 1 V. The Li-S batteries were cycled between 1.7 and 2.8 V. All the charge/discharge tests were carried out on battery testers (Wuhan Land), and all the coin cells were placed in the incubators. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was carried on an electrochemical workstation (VMP-300, BioLogic Science Instruments Co), the lithium-copper cells were scanned from the open circuit voltage (OCV) to 1.7 V, and then back to 2.8 V at a scanning rate of 0.2 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was tested by two blocking electrodes (Li) on the electrochemical workstation with a frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz with a perturbation amplitude of 10 mV. Tafel curves were tested by cyclic voltammetry (CV) using a lithium-lithium symmetric cell on an electrochemical workstation (VMP-300, Bio Logic Science Instruments Co), with a scanning start voltage of 0 V, a termination voltage of 0.1 V, and a scanning speed of 0.2 mV s−1. 
Calculation
For the HOMO/LUMO energy calculation, the Gaussian 09 suite of programs was used to optimize the configurations. Structural optimization and electronic properties were performed using the B3LYP functional with the 6–31G (d, p) basis set. As for the DFT calculation, All spin-polarized first-principles calculations were carried out using a DFT framework as implemented with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method by the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP).[2, 3] The electronic exchange-correlation interaction was described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA).[4] The kinetic cutoff energy for the plane-wave basis was set to 450 eV and the convergence thresholds for total energy and force during geometry optimizations were 10−4 eV and 0.02 eV·Å−1, respectively. Meanwhile, the DFT-D3 method was adopted to take the long-range van der Waals interaction into account.[5] The Brillouin zone was sampled using the Gamma-centered Monkhorst-Pack method with a 1×1×1 k-point mesh[6] for the 6×6×1 supercell of Li (001) slab with 5 layers of atoms. The lattice constant along the z direction was set to 37 Å for reducing the influences between mirror images.
The binding energy Eb is calculated according to the equation Eb = Etotal − Eslab − Especie, where Etotal is the total energy of adsorbed TFSI− or FPB−, Eslab is the energy of pristine Li, and Especie is the energy of isolated TFSI− or FPB−.

Figure S5:(a) The 19F NMR spectrum of the freshly mixed solution of LiTFSI and Li2S6 in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6), (b) The 19F NMR spectrum of the solution of LiTFSI and Li2S6 in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) after stirring for 60 hours.In the mixture of LiTFSI and Li2S6, the characteristic F peak of LiTFSI shifted from -79.8 ppm (Figure S5a) to -79.52 ppm, with an additional peak appearing at -75.39 ppm (Figure S5b). This indicates that LiTFSI is incompatible with polysulfides and is susceptible to attack by them. The S=O structure in LiTFSI acts as an electrophilic center, while the sulfur in polysulfides, with its high electronegativity and lone pairs of electrons, exhibits strong nucleophilicity. Consequently, polysulfides attack LiTFSI, leading to its decomposition and alteration of the solvation structure.



Figure S6: (a) The 19F NMR spectrum of the freshly mixed solution of LiFPB and Li2S6 in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6), (b) The 19F NMR spectrum of the solution of LiFPB and Li2S6 in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) after stirring for 60 hours.In contrast, the characteristic F peak of LiFPB remains invariant in the mixture with Li2S6 (Figure S6a). This underscores LiFPB's resilience against nucleophilic assault by polysulfides and its commendable compatibility with them (Figure S6b). The highly fluorinated and boronic acid groups within the LiFPB structure preclude the formation of high-valence electrophilic centers. Notably, the sulfur in LiTFSI exhibits a +6 oxidation state, whereas the highest oxidation state of boron in LiFPB is +3. This distinction endows LiFPB with exceptional chemical stability. Moreover, the substantial molecular volume of LiFPB enables it to seamlessly integrate into the solvation sheath of lithium ions, thereby forming a robust solvation structure. This structure minimizes direct contact between polysulfides and lithium ions, effectively mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect.

TOF-SIMS employs a high-energy ion beam to bombard the sample surface, generating secondary ions. Figure S14. Electrochemical behaviors of lithium-organic batteries in varied electrolytes. (a) Advantages of lithium-organic batteries with LiFPB-containing electrolyte. (b) Challenges of lithium-organic batteries with conventional electrolyte. In traditional lithium-sulfur batteries, significant challenges persist not only with the cathode, characterized by low ionic conductivity and limited tap density, but also with the instability of the lithium metal anode. On the anode side, severe parasitic reactions occur between the electrolyte and the lithium metal, resulting in the formation of an unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer. This instability leads to the generation of "dead lithium" and irreversible lithium loss. The introduction of LiFPB-containing electrolytes effectively addresses these issues by mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect and facilitating the formation of a robust and stable SEI layer, enriched with LiF and LiBxOy, on the surface of the lithium metal anode. Moreover, the result SEI layer is thinner, more uniform, and significantly more stable, thereby improving the overall performance and longevity of the battery system.



Figure S15. Cyclic voltammogram curves (CV) of Li||S cell in conventional electrolyte with different scanning speeds. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests demonstrated that, when conventional electrolytes are employed, the lithium-sulfur battery displays reduced redox peak currents across varying scan rates. This observation indicates that the transport of ions and electrons within the battery is hindered during the charge and discharge cycles, thereby impeding the overall electrochemical performance of the battery.


Figure S16: Cyclic voltammogram curves (CV) of Li||Cu cell in conventional electrolyte and LiFPB-containing electrolyte with a scan rate of 5 mV S−1. Upon analysis of the results, it was found that, compared with LiTFSI, the reduction potential of LiFPB salt occurs preferentially, and it exhibits a higher reduction peak intensity. This indicates that LiFPB salt is more prone to decomposition reactions and has better compatibility with metallic lithium. The SEI formed from the reduction of LiFPB salt is more effective during the battery cycling process. The reduction peak in the 1.0-1.5 V range suggests that it is easily reduced, especially when compared with the reduction potentials of common lithium battery solvents (carbonates at around 0.8 V (Acta Phys. Sin., 2020, 69(22): 228205) and ether solvents at around 0.5 V (ACS Energy Lett. 2023, 8, 1379−1389)). 
Figure S17: (a) The atomic configurations of DME adsorbed on the Li (001) surface, (b) The atomic configurations of DOL adsorbed on the Li (001) surface. By calculating and analyzing that the binding energies of DME and DOL with the lithium metal surface are lower than that of LiFPB, it can be concluded that LiFPB will preferentially adsorb onto the lithium metal and undergo a reaction.

Figure S18. (a) Raman spectra of DOL solvent, DME/DOL solvent, LiTFSI-DME/DOL, LiFPB-DME/DOL, LiTFSI + LiFPB-DME/DOL.(b) Raman spectra of LiTFSI-DME/DOL, LiTFSI +LiFPB-DME/DOL. Raman spectroscopy further elucidated the changes in the coordination environment of the electrolyte. Specifically, the vibration modes of DOL and LiTFSI showed no significant alteration upon the addition of LiFPB to the electrolyte system, suggesting that the modification of the SEI layer by LiFPB is not driven by changes in the solvation structure. Instead, this modification likely occurs through interactions with the electrode surface, leading to the formation of a more stable and ion-conductive SEI layer. By optimizing the SEI, the migration efficiency of lithium ions is enhanced, leading to a reduction in internal resistance and mitigating performance degradation in the battery.


Figure S19. The corresponding DRT γ(τ) of EIS at different temperatures using different electrolytes in Li||Li symmetric cells: (a) LiFPB-containing electrolyte. (b) Conventional electrolyte. The impedance measurements of Li||Li symmetric cells conducted at varying temperatures demonstrate that the LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits a significantly lower impedance compared to conventional electrolytes. This reduction in impedance indicates that the LiFPB-containing electrolyte effectively lowers the internal resistance of the battery, thereby minimizing energy losses during both the charging and discharging cycles.
