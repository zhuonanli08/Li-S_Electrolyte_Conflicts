ABSTRACT: The emerging lithium−sulfur batteries using sulfurized poly-
acrylonitrile cathodes feature exceptional stability and good compatibility with
various electrolytes. However, the study of correlating battery performance with
electrolytes based on ultrafine interphase structures is nearly blank. This work
compares the solid- and cathode-electrolyte interphases (SEI and CEI) formed
in five representative electrolytes, from the perspective of ultrafine structures
acquired via cryogenic transmission electron microscopy and electron energy
loss spectroscopy. The premature battery failure in the baseline ester- and
ether-based electrolytes is related to the thick and uneven interphase layers on
both the anodes and cathodes, which reflects continuous electrolyte
decomposition. The use of film-forming additives brings substantial improve-
ment in cycle stability, which is attributed to an inorganic-rich interphase that
blocks electrolyte permeation and further decomposition. However, it comes
along with aggregated byproducts on both SEI and CEI. Better stability is
delivered in a localized high-concentration electrolyte, which aligns with the compact SEI and CEI layers with condensed inorganic
species. Based on our observations and reasoning, the formation of inorganic-rich interphase involves anions or additives, which
could serve as a practical guideline for rational electrolyte designing.
KEYWORDS: sulfurized polyacrylonitrile, solid-electrolyte interphase, cathode-electrolyte interphase,
cryogenic transmission electron microscopy, localized high-concentration electrolyte
■INTRODUCTION
Developing batteries that offer high energy density at low cost is
an unwavering pursuit in both the academic and industrial
communities. Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs), thanks to their
high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and abundant
sulfur resources, represent the most promising candidate for
next-generation energy storage systems and capture wide
attention.1−3 Despite decades of research, key challenges hinder
the practical use of LSBs, including the polysulfide shuttle effect,
the insulating nature of sulfur and Li2S, lithium dendrite growth,
and severe self-discharge.4,5 Although confining sulfur in carbon
hosts extends cycle life and adding catalysts improves reaction
kinetics, these strategies remain inadequate under high sulfur
loading or lean electrolyte conditions.6,7 Organosulfur materials,
typified by sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), are emerging as
appealing alternatives to physically confined sulfur.8 The sulfur
atoms in SPAN are covalently bonded to the polymer backbone,
which allows SPAN to cycle only via a solid−solid pathway with
over 98% S utilization.9,10 In addition, the polymer backbone
helps stabilize sulfur species via chemical adsorption on its
pyridine nitrogen, and SPAN exhibits good compatibility toward
ester-based electrolytes extensively used in Li-ion batteries.11−13
Despite the multiple advantages of SPAN, building practical
Li-SPAN batteries is not an easy feat. Beyond the structural
integrity of SPAN cathodes, the chemical and mechanical
stability of the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) also plays a
critical role in achieving acceptable cycle lifespan. Not only
should CEI be able to protect the SPAN cathode from
electrolyte etching but also adapt to the volume variation during
lithiation and delithiation processes without destruction.14 The
most straightforward way to tailor CEI structures is electrolyte
engineering, normally by adjusting solvation structures or using
film-forming additives.15,16 For any attempts to optimize the
electrolyte formula for SPAN cathodes, its impact on the solid-

electrolyte interphase (SEI) of Li metal anodes should also be
considered. Currently, significant progresses have been made on
the SEI structures of Li metal anodes, especially after the use of
cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM) that
allows imaging SEI at atomic resolution with negligible beam
damage.17−19
The ultrafine CEI structures of SPAN cathodes, on the
contrary, have rarely been reported in the literature.
Spectroscopic characterizations, typically X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS),
are commonly done to acquire certain chemical information on
the CEI, based on which it would be hard or even misleading to
figure out the whole CEI picture, that is, partially because of the
limitations of spectroscopic techniques in resolution and
ambiguities in data interpretation. Another reason is attributed
to the porous nature of SPAN that might lead to interior CEI
formation.20 In terms of accuracy and reliability, it is better to
resort to Cryo-TEM for unveiling the CEI structures of SPAN.
Meanwhile, Cryo-TEM, integrated with an electron energy loss
spectroscopy (EELS) module, can allow users to map elemental
distribution of CEI and even hopefully probe chemical
compositions.16,21,22 The significance of structural information
for understanding CEI properties and formation mechanisms
has become a research consensus; however, formidable Cryo-
TEM has not been widely applied due to its high price and high
technical threshold.
Getting back to the development of Li-SPAN batteries, it is
paramount to find proper electrolytes that give rise to ideal CEI
and SEI. Both ester- and ether-based electrolytes are optional for
Li-SPAN batteries; however, the lack of information on ultrafine
structures hinders a profound understanding of how interphase
properties dictate performance metrics like Coulombic
efficiency (CE) and cycle lifespan. Empirical optimization
strategies have been developed, while formulating rational
designing principles is still like a castle in the air.23−25 With this
in mind, we carried out investigations on the interphase of Li||
SPAN batteries via Cryo-TEM and EELS, comparing the
ultrafine structures of both the SEI and CEI in commonly used
ester- and ether-based electrolytes. To be specific, we studied
two types of ester-based electrolytes and three ether-based ones,
the latter including a localized high-concentration electrolyte
(LHCE). The impacts of two typical additives, fluoroethylene
carbonate (FEC) and LiNO3, on interphase structures are
discussed with respect to ultrafine SEI and CEI structures.
Particularly, an in-depth exploration is carried out to shed light
on the correlation between the interphase structures and the
superior cycle performance delivered in the LHCE.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To study the performance and interphase of Li-SPAN batteries
in various electrolyte systems, we evaluated five types of
electrolytes. They are 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) and
diethylene carbonate (DEC) with a volume ratio of 1:1 denoted
as ESTER, ESTER with 10% FEC denoted as ESTER-FEC, 1 M
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL), and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with a
volume ratio of 1:1 denoted as ETHER, ETHER with 2% LiNO3
denoted as ETHER-LNO that is the most common electrolyte
used for LSBs, and the electrolyte of lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)-
imide (LiFSI) in DME and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-
tetrafluoropropylether (TTE), whose molar ratio of LiFSI:DM-
E:TTE is 1:1.2:3, namely, the LHCE.
The Li plating/stripping reversibility in the five types of
electrolytes is evaluated from the CE measurement of Li||Cu
cells by various methods. As shown in Figures 1a and S1, the CEs
measured by the Aurbach method26 in ESTER and ESTER-FEC
are 73.7% and 96.5%, while those in ETHER and ETHER-LNO
are 93.9% and 99.1%, and those in LHCE remarkably reach
99.5%. In the cycle tests at 1 mA cm−2 for 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure
1b), the CE values of Li||Cu cells using ESTER and ETHER are
only about 85%, and the latter cannot even be cycled 10 times
before CE plunges. FEC does improve the CE of Li||Cu cells
using ESTER to about 90%; however, it fails to extend cycle
lifespan notably. By contrast, the Li||Cu cells using ETHER-
LNO exhibit high CEs around 99% for over 100 cycles, while the
Li||Cu cells using the LHCE deliver excellent stability in the 300-
cycle tests, offering an average CE of 99.1%. To perform
corrosion tests, the Li||Cu cells are aged for 7 days between Li
plating for 2 mAh cm−2 and stripping to 1.0 V. As shown in
Figure 1c, the cells using the electrolyte that enables better cycle
stability exhibit higher capacity retention, namely, the CE after
aging (see the voltage profiles in Figure S2). It is worth
mentioning that the LCHE promises higher cycling stability and
anticorrosion capability than ETHER-LNO at the price of larger
voltage hysteresis between Li plating and stripping (Figure S3).

To decipher the differing performance in the five
representative electrolytes from the perspective of the
interphase, both Li metal anodes and SPAN cathodes are
subjected to Cryo-TEM characterization. As shown in Figure
2a−e, the morphology of Li deposits strongly depends on the
electrolyte. Mixed Li dendrites and chunks are observed in the
two ester-based electrolytes. In other words, FEC fails to alter
the Li plating morphology effectively. In comparison, the Li
deposits in ETHER turn out to be lumps rather than dendrites,
and the LiNO3 additive makes the Li deposits more spherical
and larger, which is similar to the case in LHCE. Observing the
Li deposits under larger magnification, we also find that the SEI
structures, as shown in the high-resolution Cryo-TEM (Cryo-
HRTEM) images, varied greatly from electrolyte to electrolyte
(Figure S7). The SEI formed in ESTER (Figure 2f) is a uniform
layer about 8 nm in thickness, and the corresponding EELS
mapping (Figure S8) indicates a uniform distribution of C, O, F,
P, and Li in the SEI. The addition of FEC, as seen in Figure 2g,h,
does not cause obvious changes in SEI thickness or composition;
however, it does lead to the generation of aggregated byproducts
attached to the SEI, which are usually termed as “indirect SEI.”27
Its LiF-rich characteristic is proved from the EELS mapping of F
K-edge and spectrum of Li K-edge (Figures 2i and S9); however,
as speculated in the literature, it is not supposed to make a
principal contribution to the Li-ion diffusion kinetics.27 The
improved stability could be more relevant to the localized LiF
species in the SEI.
The SEI layers formed in the ether-based electrolytes are
relatively thicker. Figure 2j shows the 40 nm SEI formed in
ETHER, which is an amorphous layer embedded with randomly
distributed nanocrystals (Figure S10). The fringe d-spacing
values of 0.28 and 0.42 nm, confirmed with fast Fourier
transform (FFT) patterns (Figures S11 and S12), could be
ascribed to the (002) and (110) planes of Li2CO3 or (101) and
(012) planes of Li2S2O7.28 Also, the lattice fringes with a spacing
of 0.20 nm, corresponding to the (200) plane of LiF, were found
on the inner region of the SEI. No obvious aggregates were
observed on the surface of Li dendrites generated in ETHER;
however, the thickness of the SEI varied greatly, and aggregated
byproducts were found on the current collector (Figure S13).
With the addition of LiNO3, a small amount of similar
aggregated byproducts was observed on the surface of lithium
dendrites, similar to the case of ESTER-FEC (Figure 2k). The
SEI formed in ETHER-LNO has a similar thickness but adopts a
bilayer structurean amorphous outer layer plus an inorganic-
rich inner layer (Figure 2l). As revealed in the corresponding
EELS mapping (Figures 2m and S14), the former exhibits
intensive C and F signals, while the latter has much higher N and
O signals. The fringe d-spacing of 0.27 nm can be indexed to the
(111) plane of Li2O (Figures S15 and S16).18 Based on the
above information, it is reasonable to speculate that preferential
LiNO3 decomposition contributes to the formation of the inner
Li2O- and Li3N-rich layer, and the sequential solvent/LiTFSI
decomposition results in an organic-rich outer layer with
aggregated C and F signals. Such a bilayer structure is supposed
to provide uniform Li-ion flux and protect Li metal from
electrolyte corrosion more effectively than a mosaic-type SEI. In
the LHCE, no obvious aggregate is observed; meanwhile, the
electrolyte also forms the bilayer SEI (Figure 2n,o), which is
much thinner (about 20 nm) and features different composi-
tions. The inner layer is enriched with N, F, and S (Figure S17)
originating from the decomposition of anions and diluents,
namely, FSI−and TTE, while the O/F-rich outer layer is
decorated with abundant nanocrystals, whose fringe d-spacing of
0.28 nm could be best ascribed to the (001) plane of LiOH
(Figures 2p−r, S18 and S19).29,30 Since the C signal is mainly
distributed in the outer layer, it is inferred that solvent
decomposition is well suppressed in the later stage of SEI
formation, or rather such a SEI structure inhibits further solvent
decomposition, which is in good agreement with the superior
stability of Li metal anode in the LHCE.

squares (MLLS) fitting of C K-edge spectra.
Looking deep into the ultrafine CEI structures, we find that
the CEI formed in various electrolytes varies greatly in
morphology and composition. The CEI in ESTER is totally
amorphous and thick (Figure 4a,b), and the EELS mapping
reveals the elemental distributions of Li, C, N, O, S, and F. From
the C K-edge spectra of selected regions, we do find that the C-
CEI contains characteristic CO and C−H signals, while the
C-SPAN does not (Figures 4k and S20). Such EELS analysis,
particularly the C species of CEI and the CEI coating on SPAN,
is consistent with the XPS depth profiles shown in Figure S21.
Besides, intense Li signals are also detected in the SPAN regions,
which indicates incomplete delithiation in the charging
processes. In the case of ESTER-FEC, aggregates are observed
on the SEI of Li metal anodes, which are supposed to be derived
from FEC because of the enrichment of Li and F (Figures 4c,d
and S22). The Li K-edge and F K-edge spectra collected from
the CEI resemble those from pure LiF, and the C K-edge
spectrum is basically identical to that from the CEI formed in
ESTER (Figures 4k and S23); both discoveries are verified by
XPS results (Figure S24). The EELS and XPS data can be
interpreted to indicate that the involvement of FEC in CEI
formation brings LiF ingredients, as well as LiF-rich aggregates
scattered on the CEI layers, which perhaps could be termed as
“indirect CEI.” To put it more plainly, the so-called “film-
forming” FEC additive could indeed dictate the interphase
formation, probably by decomposing preferentially and limiting
further electrolyte decomposition; however, its continuous
decomposition until depletion would produce LiF-rich
aggregates on both the SEI and CEI, bringing about Li loss
while adding nothing to ionic conductivity.
In the ETHER electrolyte, a CEI layer is also formed, attached
with LiF-rich aggregates originating from LiTFSI decomposi-
tion; however, their size is relatively smaller (Figures 4e,f,k and
S25). What is different is the infiltration of CEI into the SPAN
region instead of the coverage of CEI layer derived from the
ESTER electrolyte, as well as the appearance of S in the CEI
region that is confirmed with the XPS S 2p spectra and N 1s
spectra collected without sputtering (Figure S26). Additionally,
the signal of C−S in the C K-edge spectrum of the SPAN region
is substantially attenuated (Figures 4k and S27). The results
obtained in ETHER-LNO are quite different: the CEI is thinner,
the aggregates are smaller (Figures 4g,h, S28 and S29), the C−S
signal is retained, the LiF feature is not reflected on the Li K-edge
spectrum, and the F signal is close to invisible (Figures 4k and
S30). Based on these observations, it is inferred that the cleavage
of C−S bonds is inhibited in the ether-based electrolyte with
LiNO3 to a large degree and so is the decomposition of LiTFSI.

On the basis of the above discussions, the structural
characteristics of electrode materials and interphase layers
formed in the five representative electrolytes are schematically
illustrated in Figure 5. The Li plating morphology strongly
depends on the electrolytes; however, the mechanisms are rather
complex. According to established theories in the literature,
nucleation and growth kinetics are relevant to the desolvation
dynamics of the electrolytes, the compulsive Li plating driven by
the external electric field, and very likely the characteristics of
SEI layers.33 For the purpose of stable cycling in long-term
operations, compact Li plating with minimal volume changes
and less Li involved in SEI formation is preferred. In this regard,
dendritic Li growth in ester-based electrolytes is very disadvanta-
geous. The morphology changes of SPAN after cycling in
different electrolytes are not observable; however, the
morphology of the CEI layers does vary greatly. In the ester-
based electrolytes, thick and uneven CEI layers are formed, and
so do in the ether-based electrolyte without LiNO3. The SEI and
CEI formed in ETHER-LNO and LHCE are thin and dense,
which is supposed to be related to their inorganic-rich feature.
That is because the inorganic-rich interphase could block
electrolyte permeation or rather have a small swelling ratio in the
organic electrolytes; thus, continuous decomposition of electro-
lytes is inhibited effectively.34,35 The inorganic species, such as
LiF, Li2O, Li2CO3, LiOH, and other Li salts, originate mainly
from the decomposition of anions or film-forming additives.
Tailoring solvation structures or using additives rationally could
induce the formation of inorganic-rich and hopefully bilayer
interphase; however, it is worth emphasizing that the inorganic-
rich interphase does not grant fast L-ion migration kinetics.36,37
Both the SEI and CEI could be modulated by additives in
electrolytes; however, caution should be exercised when using
the additive strategy. Whether in the case of FEC in ESTER and
that of LiNO3 in ETHER, aggregated byproducts are observed
on both the SEI and the CEI layers. The content of additives
should be carefully optimized to avoid unnecessary electrolyte
decomposition and Li loss.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, to determine the correlation between the
interphase and electrochemical performance of Li-SPAN
batteries, the ultrafine SEI and CEI structures produced in five
representative electrolytes, including typical ester- and ether-
based ones, are unraveled via Cryo-TEM characterization. For
the ester-based electrolytes, rapid capacity fading of Li metal
anodes and SPAN cathodes is partially attributed to thick and
uneven interphases caused by uncontrolled electrolyte decom-
position. The use of conventional film-forming FEC additive
does augment cycle stability but also produces LiF-rich
aggregates randomly distributed on both the SEI and CEI,
resulting in excessive Li loss and electrolyte depletion. In the
ether-based electrolytes, the LiNO3 additive can bring
substantial improvement in cycle stability and anticorrosion
capability and, at the same time, strengthen the structural
robustness of SPAN during long-term cycling. It is reasonable to
make connections between these advantages and the interphase

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials
1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-
dioxolane/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME, 1:1 by volume) and the
electrolyte with 2 wt % lithium nitrate (LiNO3) were purchased from
DoDoChem. The chemicals for electrolyte preparation, including
DME, ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), fluoro-
ethylene carbonate (FEC), 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluor-
opropylether (TTE), and lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI),
were also purchased from DoDoChem. All solvents were dried with
molecular sieves in a glovebox for 24 h before use. For the preparation
of SPAN, sublimed sulfur (S, purity > 99.9%) was purchased from
Aladdin and polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 150,000) from Sigma-
Aldrich.
Preparation of SPAN and Cathode Films
S powders (4 g) and PAN (1 g) were ground and mixed in a mortar for 1
h and then heated at 450 °C for 6 h in a tube furnace with a temperature
ramp rate of 5 °C per min under Ar flow. After the mixture was cooled
to room temperature, the obtained SPAN powders were collected. For
cathode preparation, the SPAN powders were mixed with Super P and
carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) binder with a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in a
proper amount of water to form a slurry, which was cast on carbon-
coated aluminum foil with a doctor blade and dried under vacuum at 80
°C for 12 h. The as-prepared cathode films were punched into 12 mm
discs for the coin cell assembly. The active material loading for
conventional electrochemical testing is approximately 1.8 mg cm−2,
while for limited lithium cycling tests (50 μm), the loading is about 4.7
mg cm−2, resulting in an N/P ratio of around 3.6.
Coin Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Measurements
The cycling tests were conducted on a NEWARE battery testing
system. The SPAN cathodes and Li foils were assembled into coin cells
with a Celgard separator and various kinds of electrolytes. For cycling
tests of regular Li||SPAN cells, the first two charge−discharge cycles
were conducted at a current density of 0.05 A g−1 as an activation step,
followed by subsequent cycling at 0.5 A g−1. For Li||SPAN cells with
limited lithium anodes, a current density of 0.1 A g−1 was used for the
charge and discharge cycling. Li||Cu cells were assembled for the
evaluation of Li metal anodes in various electrolytes. Before CE
evaluation, Li||Cu cells were cycled five times in the range of 0 ∼1 V.
For conventional Li||Cu cell cycling tests, the charge/discharge current
density and areal capacity are set to 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2,
respectively. For Aurbach-type CE measurements, the following
standard protocol was adopted: (i) initially, plating Li onto a Cu
substrate at 0.5 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 5 mAh cm−2, followed
by stripping to 1 V; (ii) depositing a Li reservoir of 5 mAh cm−2 at 0.5
mA cm−2; (iii) conducting 10 successive stripping/deposition cycles at
0.5 mA cm−2 with 1 mAh cm−2 per cycle; and (iv) finally stripping all
remaining Li by charging to 1 V. The electrolyte volume in all tested
cells was limited to 50 μL, corresponding to approximately 44 μL cm−2.
All key performance metrics were tested using a minimum of three
individually assembled cells for data acquisition.
Material Characterizations
The cells of interest for microscopy and spectroscopy measurements
were disassembled inside an Ar-filled glovebox, and the SPAN cathodes
and Li metal anodes were gently washed with DMC or DME to remove
residual salts and then dried. All of the samples are transferred under Ar
protection to avoid air exposure. The morphologies of samples were
characterized using a Hitachi SU8230 cold field emission scanning
electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. X-ray
diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded in a diffractor (Rigaku, Smart
Lab 3 kW) equipped with Cu Kα radiation. XPS measurements were
carried out on a Thermo Scientific Escalab Xi+ spectrometer. For the
Cryo-TEM characterization, the SPAN specimens are prepared by
loading cycled SPAN on TEM grids, and the specimens of Li metal
anodes are prepared by placing a TEM grid on Cu foils of Li||Cu cells,
where Li was plated on the grid at 1 mA cm−2 for 1 mAh cm−2. The
Cryo-TEM specimens were instantly frozen in liquid nitrogen and
transferred to a Cryo-TEM (Titan Krios G3i) instrument using a Cryo-
transfer holder to avoid any possible air exposure. All the high-
resolution Cryo-TEM images were acquired with an electron dosage
rate of ∼4 e−· Å−2 s−1 for 5−10 s. EELS spectra were acquired using an
11 pA current with 0.1 s dwell time at each pixel for core edges. Cryo-
TEM was operated at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon 3 direct
electron detector camera and a Gatan Continuum K3 EELS
spectrometer (Model 1069). To ensure the representativeness of data
acquisition, Cryo-HRTEM images were collected from at least five
distinct interfacial regions, while Cryo-STEM-EELS analyses were
performed on areas corresponding to a minimum of three of the Cryo-
HRTEM sampling locations.
