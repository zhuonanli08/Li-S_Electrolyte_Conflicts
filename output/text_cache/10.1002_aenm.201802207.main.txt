Solvent has a strong impact on Li2S dep-

pioneering studies suggest that the solution mediation process 
plays a critical role in Li2S deposition and highlight the urgent 
need for developing quantitative and comprehensive correla-
tion between solvent property and Li2S nucleation and growth.
In this work, we establish structure–property relationship 
of solvent in controlling solid Li2S deposition and develop 
quantitative solvent-mediated Li2S growth models as guides to 
solvent selection. We investigate three solvent’s properties and 
their roles on Li2S deposition: (1) the donicity which governs 
the stability of the polysulfide anions (i.e., the precursor of Li2S 
deposition) through (Li+)sol–polysulfide interactions;[12,13] (2) the 
polarity (dielectric constant) which governs the solvation ability 
of the final product Li2S;[14] (3) the viscosity which strongly 
affects the diffusivity of polysulfide and dissolved Li2S.[15] We 
show that these solvent-controlled properties are essential fac-
tors pertaining to the sulfur utilization, electrode kinetics, 
and reversibility of electrochemical reduction of elemental 
sulfur. Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the solvent 
selection criteria developed in this study in identifying new and 
more effective electrolytes for Li–S batteries.

Controlling electrochemical deposition of lithium sulfide (Li2S) is a major 
challenge in lithium–sulfur batteries as premature Li2S passivation leads to 
low sulfur utilization and low rate capability. In this work, the solvent’s roles 
in controlling solid Li2S deposition are revealed, and quantitative solvent-

and reversibility of lithium–sulfur batteries. It is further demonstrated that the 
solvent selection criteria developed in this study are effective in guiding the

and design criteria for computational and experimental electrolyte develop-
ment for lithium–sulfur batteries.

Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
1. Introduction
Elemental sulfur offers one of the highest gravimetric capacity 
among all electrode materials reported to date with large earth-
abundance and relatively low cost.[1] Electrochemical reduction 
of solid sulfur (S8) by lithium ions to form lithium sulfide 
(Li2S) yields a gravimetric energy (2600 Wh kgS−1) higher than 
today’s Li-ion batteries.[1c,2] Controlling electrochemical deposi-
tion of Li2S is a major challenge in lithium–sulfur batteries as 
premature Li2S passivation[1d,3] leads to low sulfur utilization 
and low rate capability.[1d,3] The morphology and structure of the 
solid Li2S product are key factors determining the achievable 
discharge capacity,[4] rate capability,[5] active materials utilization 
and reversibility of sulfur-based energy storage.[6] The mecha-
nism of Li2S deposition/growth and its dependence on electrode 
substrate have been extensively investigated via theoretical[7] and 
experimental approaches (scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
imaging,[6a] atomic force microscopy (AFM)[8]). Electrode micro-
structure[4,9] and polarity of host materials[9a,10] were also reported 
to affect the growth mechanism of Li2S. Polar host materials

which can preferentially stabilize harder Lewis base over the 
softer Lewis base via Pearson’s hard-soft acid-base theory.[23] 
Consequently, the quasiequilibrium potential of the second 
reaction plateau increases with decreasing solvation energy of 
lithium cations.
2.2. Kinetics of Nucleation and Growth from Polysulfide to Li2S
We investigate the kinetics of nucleation and growth of Li2S 
from polysulfide using potentiostatic electrodeposition at given 
applied electrode potentials ranging from 2.05 to 1.95 V versus 
Li+/Li (VLi) in the two-compartment cell. Results obtained at 
2.00 VLi are shown in Figure 2a–e; other potentials are shown 
in Figure S2 (Supporting Information) and the replications 
are shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information). Catholyte 
consisted of 0.5 m Li2S8 in 1.0 m LiTFSI-0.4 m LiNO3 was used 
as the sulfur source in all the solvents in two-compartment 
cells. Qualitatively, the initial current drop of the current–time 
response is due to nonfaradaic double-layer plus solution phase 
reduction of long-chain polysulfides to mid-chain polysulfides. 
The succeeding part reflects a nucleation and growth process. 
At the beginning, the current rises which indicates the increase 
of electroactive area owing to the growth in size of each nucleus 
or the increase of the number of the nuclei of Li2S precipitation 
on the electrode surface. Subsequently, the current drops in the 
growth phase due to: (1) impingement between neighboring 
nuclei centers, of which the projection can be obtained by the 
Avrami theorem;[24](2) the overlap of the adjacent hemispherical 
diffusion zone developed by the sparsely distributed nuclei to 
an infinite linear concentration gradient.[25] The chronoampero-
grams of G1, DMSO, and Me-Im are shown in Figure S2 
(Supporting Information). They are not used to perform the 
nucleation and growth fitting model because no obvious peaks 
were observed. We believe that this can be attributed to the 
ultrafast nucleation/growth for G1 and ultraslow for DMSO 
and Me-Im, which are in line with Figure 5.
We perform quantitative peak fitting to the current–time 
transient profiles to reveal kinetic properties of Li2S deposition 
(Figure 2f–j). Four classical models of electrochemical deposi-
tion were considered to fit the current–time responses obtained 
in chronoamperic test (Figure  3). First, Bewick, Fleischman, 
and Thirsk (BFT) models: (i) 2D progressive nucleation (2DP) 
or (ii) 2D instantaneous (2DI) nucleation, both of which are 
followed by incorporation of adatoms into the lattice interface 
(see governing equations in Equation (S1)–(S10), Supporting 
Information).[26] Second, Scharifker–Hills (SH) models: (iii) 
3D progressive (3DP) nucleation or (iv) 3D instantaneous 
(3DI) nucleation, both of which are followed by a volume 
diffusional controlled growth (see governing equations in 
Equations (S11)–(S26), Supporting Information).[25,27] The 
density of nuclei on a certain substrate (N) at given time t is 
related to the density of available nucleation site (N0) and the

2.3. Morphology
Figure  2k–o shows the SEM images of the discharge elec-
trode after potentiostatic electrodeposition. In DMF and DMA, 
Li2S granules with uniform size are formed and randomly 
distributed over the substrate (DMA: 82.5  ±  7.5  nm, DMF: 
112.5  ±  7.5  nm, Figure S8, Supporting Information). The 
granular shape of Li2S particles observed in DMF/DMA is con-
sistent with the hemispherical nucleus 3D nucleation growth 
model where the Li2S nuclei undergo isotropic growth in all 
directions, leading to the formation of the thermodynamically 
favorable spherical morphology. In glyme-based solvents with 
2D lattice incorporation growth, preferred orientation is devel-
oped in the Li2S morphology forming plate/sheet-like deposits. 
The size and the thickness of the nanoplates decrease from 
G2 (1.5 ± 0.2 µm in size/85 ± 25 nm in thickness, Figure 2k), 
G3 (220  ±  40  nm in size/24  ±  6  nm in thickness, Figure  2l) 
to G4 (very fine feature forming film-like structure, Figure 2m). 
Among G2–G4, Li2S deposition in G4 has the highest lat-
eral growth rate with the least Li2S solubility and the highest 
viscosity (slow polysulfide diffusion), it is expected to form 
film-like Li2S deposits and passivate the electrode surface. G3 
has the slowest lateral growth rate with the highest Li2S solu-
bility, which allows Li2S deposition extending into electrolyte, 
resulting in porous structure of Li2S (Figure 2l).
We examine the change in Li2S morphology with different 
applied potentials in a given solvent (Figure S9, Supporting 
Information). First, the morphology of Li2S in G2 shifted 
from the plate/sheet shape at 2.00 VLi to the granular shape 
at 2.05 VLi, which agrees well with the transition from 2D to 
3D nucleation probed by the dimensionless current transient 
responses in G2 (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The 
morphology of Li2S deposits in G1 is similar to that in G2, with 
high 2D lateral growth, and form the self-assembled flower-
like structure (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Second, 
the granular Li2S observed in DMA at 2.00 VLi transformed to 
plate/sheet shape of Li2S at 1.95 VLi, which is in line with the 
transformation from 3D to 2D nucleation determined by the 
current transient responses in DMA (Figure S4, Supporting 
Information). The SEM images of Li2S deposits in DMSO and 
Me-Im are shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information). No 
well-defined shape can be identified in DMSO and Me-Im. 
These changes in morphology further support that the granular 
morphology is resulted from a slower nucleation rate (lower 
density of nuclei) and the plate/sheet shape is associated with a 
higher nucleation rate (higher density of nuclei).

2.5. Galvanostatic Voltage Profiles of Li–S Batteries
We connect three solvent-governed properties to the electro-
chemical behaviors of Li–S cells: (1) solvent donicity (related 
to Li2S nucleation kinetics); (2) dielectric constant (related to 
Li2S solubility[15] (Figure S12, Supporting Information)); and (3) 
viscosity (related to polysulfide/Li2S solute mobility). Figure 5 
shows the galvanostatic voltage profiles of Li-Li2S8 catholyte 
cells (0.5 m Li2S8 ≈ 2.56 mgS cm−2) at 0.05 C (≈0.21 mA cm−2). 
All the catholytes were separated from the lithium anode by 
LAGP. The first reaction stage (I) shown in Figure  5a–h is 
attributed to the reaction from Li2S8 to Li2S4 (≈209 mAh gs−1). 
Since Li2S8 was employed as the starting materials instead of 
S8, the typical discharge plateau associated with S8 electrode 
(≈209 mAh gS−1, based on S8 → Li2S8) is absent in Figure 5a–h. 
Interestingly, the capacity percentage of the slopping region I is 
similar in all solvents (≈200 mAh gs−1) and very close to the the-
oretical value, that is, 209 mAh gs−1 (based on Li2S8 → Li2S4). 
This indicates that the achievable capacity of the solution phase 
reactions is not sensitive to solvent.
The achieved discharge capacity of region II (Li2S deposition) 
varied significantly from solvent to solvent. For glymes, recall 
that Li2S deposition adopts 2D nucleation mechanism at around 
2.00 VLi and the deposition is terminated when insufficient elec-
trode surface is left for further reaction (discussed in Figure 2). 
Therefore, the glyme-based solvents with relatively higher 
donicity and higher Li2S solubility could slow down the lateral 
growth (i.e., delaying termination) and facilitate solution media-
tion process for 3D growth, respectively. This helps to explain 
differences seen among glymes. First, G1 has both ultralow 
donicity and Li2S solubility, leading to fast lateral growth and fast 
electrode passivation and consequently low capacity (Figure 5a). 
In addition, from G1 to G3, the discharge capacity of region II 
(Li2S formation) increased with increasing solvent donicity and 
Li2S solubility. G4 has a high donicity, however, it exhibits lower 
Li2S solubility and higher viscosity than G2/G3. These two prop-
erties could block the solution pathway for 3D growth leading 
to lower discharge capacity and poor rate capability (Figure 5d).
For the high-donicity group, we show that Li2S electrodepo-
sition exhibits sluggish nucleation kinetics followed by a 3D 
volume diffusion mass-transport controlled growth at around 
2.00 VLi. To improve this group, therefore, solvents with lower 
donicity (for higher nucleation rate), higher Li2S solubility, and 
lower viscosity (faster polysulfide diffusivity) are beneficial in 
order to achieve high discharge capacity. For example, DMA 
which has low viscosity and high Li2S solubility (Table S8, 
Supporting Information)[12], delivers the highest discharge 
capacity (≈1070.8 mAh gs−1 out of 1465.6 mAh gs−1 from 
Li2S8 to Li2S) in the group (Figure  5e). DMF has a similar 
donicity and viscosity with DMA but a lower Li2S solubility.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we reveal that Li2S deposition follows a 2D instan-
taneous nucleation with lateral lattice incorporation growth 
to form plate/sheet-like Li2S deposits in low-donicity glymes. 
The fast nucleation rate (related to low solvent donicity) and 
low Li2S solubility account for premature battery termination 
in glymes. In high-donicity solvents, the nucleation process is 
more sluggish owing to strong stabilization of the long-chain 
polysulfide. Consequently, lower density of nuclei is formed on 
the electrode surface with a hemispherical diffusion controlled 
growth zone established, leading to quasispherical granular 
shape of Li2S solid. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the sol-
vent selection criteria developed in this study in identifying new 
and more effective electrolytes. Our study reveals root causes 
responsible for the drastic different electrochemical behaviors 
of the model solvents and provides effective design criteria for 
rational solvent development for lithium–sulfur batteries.

Electronics Co., Ltd.) (Figures 5 and 6).

All samples were vacuum dried overnight before transferring to the 
microscope chamber. The microstructures were observed using Quanta
