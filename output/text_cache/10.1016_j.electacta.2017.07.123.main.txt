A B S T R A C T
Although the cathode of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries has a theoretical speciﬁc capacity of
1,672 mAh g1, its practical capacity is much smaller than this value and depends on the electrolyte/
sulfur ratio. The operation of Li-S batteries under lean electrolyte conditions can be challenging,
especially in the case when the solubility of lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) sets an upper bound for polysulﬁde
dissolution. In this work, specially designed cathode structures and electrolyte conﬁgurations were built
in order to analyze the effects of LiPS solubility on cell capacity. Two reaction pathways involving the
reduction of LiPS in liquid and solid phase are proposed and analyzed. We show that at discharge rates
above 0.4 mA cm2 the reaction in the liquid phase dominates the discharge process. Once the electrolyte
becomes saturated, the solid phase LiPS cannot be further reduced and does not contribute to the
capacity of the cells. This phenomenon prevents Li-S batteries from achieving their high theoretical
speciﬁc capacity. Finally, the speciﬁc energy of the Li-S cell is reevaluated and discussed considering the
limitation imposed by LiPS solubility.

1. Introduction
Li-S batteries are among the most promising candidates for the
next generation rechargeable batteries due to their high energy
density, low cost of sulfur, and environmental friendliness [1].
Despite this great potential, Li-S batteries present many challenges,
including low conductivity of elemental sulfur and lithium sulﬁde,
large volume expansion of 80% upon discharge, and unfavorable
redox shuttle effect due to the soluble nature of intermediate
lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) [2,3]. To increase the capacity and
cyclability of these batteries, the electrolyte should have large ionic
conductivity, as well as high chemical and electrochemical stability
[4]. In addition, the electrolyte should promote the dissolution of
the intermediate LiPS in order for the LiPS molecules to reach the
electron conductive material faster and increase the reaction rate,
while at the same time should reduce the shuttle effect. These
requirements are currently met by using polar-aprotic solvents
that yield high solubility for LiPS molecules and adding electrolyte
additives such as LiNO3 to help passivate the Li anode surface [5–7].
In order to increase the energy density of rechargeable Li-S
batteries the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio should be decreased as
much as possible [8–10]. However, despite recent improvements in
electrode and electrolyte design, most of the Li-S batteries
presented in the literature still use a large amount of electrolyte
and the reported energy densities are much below their maximum
theoretical limit. There are multiple reasons for using a large
amount of electrolyte in Li-S batteries. First, the Li-S batteries
should contain enough electrolyte to fully dissolve the intermedi-
ate LiPS products. For instance, in the widely used 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME): 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) binary electrolyte (solubility
for LiPS species is [S] = 6 M), the minimum E/S ratio that enables
the full dissolution of LiPS is 5.2 mL g1 [11]. Then, additional
electrolyte is needed to avoid 
problems related to severe
performance degradation at low E/S ratios and electrolyte
consumption from side 
reaction [12–15].

In this work, freestanding three-dimensional carbon nanotube
(CNT) foams were fabricated and used as carbon hosts for
synthesizing cathodes with different initial sulfur and LiPS
loadings. The cathodes were purposely designed to have different
and well-deﬁned initial states-of-discharge, which allows us to
separate and analyze individually the electrochemical processes
that take place during the discharge of Li-S batteries. Our work
focuses on the analysis of the effects of LiPS solubility on cell
capacity and shows that LiPS solubility plays a critical role in
achieving high speciﬁc capacities for Li-S cells.
2. Experimental
2.1. Material preparation
The CNT foams were fabricated based on the method reported
in [17,18]. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in
dimethylformanide (DMF, Sigma-Aldrich) to form a solution with 1
weight percent of PAN concentration. The PAN/DMF solution was
added to CNTs (average 2 mm in length and 10 nm in diameter,
General Nano) to achieve a weight ratio of PAN-to-CNT of 0.5, and
the appropriate amount of isopropyl alcohol (IPA, Sigma-Aldrich)
was added. The mixture was well dispersed by high power
sonication (Misonix Sonicator 3000) for 30 min. Then, poly(methyl
methacrylate) (PMMA) microspheres with an average diameter of
3.69 mm (Sunjin Chemical) and PMMA-to-CNT weight ratio of 2.9
were added to the suspension. After a 10-min bath sonication, the
mixture was put into a vacuum ﬁltration system to make a CNT/
PAN/PMMA solid composite that was then peeled off from the ﬁlter
membrane and dried overnight at room temperature to remove the
IPA and DMF. The CNT foams were obtained through a two-step
thermal treatment in a tube furnace (MTI, GSL-1700X), ﬁrst for 3 h
in air at 300 C and, then, for 1 h in nitrogen at 1200 C with
80 ml min1 ﬂow rate. The PMMA microspheres were removed and
PAN was stabilized and carbonized during the thermal treatment.
In this work, we prepared and tested three types of cathodes
that we refer to as C-only cathodes, C/LiPS cathodes, and C/S
cathodes. C-only cathodes contained only CNT foam. The C/LiPS
cathodes were made by loading CNT foam with solid-state LiPS.
The selected LiPS species Li2S8 were prepared by mixing sulfur
powder (Sigma-Aldrich) and Li2S powder (VWR) with a molar ratio
of 7:1 in a DME (Sigma-Aldrich) solution. The precursor solution
was heated at 45 C and stirred vigorously for 48 h in an argon-
ﬁlled glovebox (MBraun). After 48 h the sulfur powder reacted
completely with Li2S to produce LiPS. The C/LiPS cathodes were
obtained by soaking CNT foams in LiPS solution with controlled
concentration and then dried overnight to evaporate the solvent.
The C/S cathodes were made by inﬁltrating sulfur into the CNT
foams via the melting diffusion method. Sulfur powder was placed
onto the surface of the CNT foam electrode uniformly and then
heated and melted into the CNT foam with a hot plate set at 158 C.
The electrode was rotated regularly during sulfur inﬁltration until
designed loading was met. Three cathodes with different LiPS and S
loadings were obtained for each of the C/LiPS and C/S cathodes.
These cathodes contained approximately 4 mg, 2 mg, and 1 mg of
LiPS and S, respectively and are denoted by #1, #2, and #3. The
diameter of the cathodes was 12.7 mm. The average weight of the
C-only cathode was 1.57 mg.
2.2. Material characterization
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL-JSM7401F, 10 kV) was
used to characterize the morphology of the CNT foams. The
porosimetry results of the CNT foams were measured by a surface
analyzer (Autosorb iQ Micropore Instrument) using the nitrogen
gas adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K. The samples were
outgassed at 200 C for 2 h prior to taking the measurements.
2.3. Electrochemical characterization
The discharge performance of the cathodes described above
was analyzed using three different electrolytes. First, a regular
electrolyte was composed of 1 M bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfoni-
mide Li salt (LiTFSI) and 0.1 M LiNO3 in DME: DOL (Sigma-Aldrich,
1:1 v:v). The second electrolyte contained a high concentration of
5 M LiTFSI in DME: DOL (1:1 v:v). The third electrolyte was a Li2S8
catholyte, prepared by mixing stoichiometric amounts of sulfur
and Li2S powders in a 0.5 M LiTFSI and 0.1 M LiNO3 in DME: DOL
(1:1 v:v) solution, which was then heated at 45 C and stirred
vigorously for 48 h in an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. The prepared
cathode was assembled with a glass ﬁber separator (0.26 mm, GE
healthcare) and a Li foil anode (0.25 mm, MTI) into a CR2032-type
coin cell. 60 mL of desired electrolyte were used in all the cells. The
assembled Li-S cells were discharged in galvanostatic mode under
a current density of 0.4 mA cm2. The linear sweep voltammetry
(LSV) measurements were made using a Gamry Instruments-
Reference 3000 at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s1.
3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 presents the SEM images of the PMMA microspheres and
the CNT foam used in the fabrication of the cathodes. The thickness
of the foam is 100 mm and the mass density is 125 mg cm3.
Fig. 1a presents the SEM image of PMMA microspheres. The PMMA
microspheres have a moderately monodisperse size distribution
with an average diameter of 3.69 mm and are used as the template
for the synthesis of CNT foam. As shown in Fig. 1b, the cellular
microstructure of CNT foams consists of closely-packed micro-
scale pores, which are formed as a result the thermal degradation
of PMMA microspheres template as described in [18]. Fig. 1c shows
that the micro-scale pores do not match exactly the shape of the
PMMA microspheres because CNTs are much longer than the
diameter of the PMMA microspheres and because CNTs and PMMA
microspheres are deposited layer by layer during the vacuum
ﬁltration process. Fig. 1d presents a higher magniﬁcation image of
the wall of micro-scale pores formed by interconnected CNTs. The
walls of these pores contain numerous nano-scale pores, which
greatly improves the surface area of the material. The porosimetry
results measured by the nitrogen adsorption-desorption method
are summarized in Fig. S1 and in Table S1 in the Supplementary
Material section. The CNT foam plays the role of the current
collector. In addition, since it is ﬂexible and robust it can be easily
processed to fabricate freestanding structures with the desired
loadings of sulfur and LiPS. Uniform coating of sulfur and LiPS on
the surface of CNT are achieved by using the melting-diffusion and
the solution-based method, respectively.

the Li-S cells with regular electrolyte. The discharge proﬁles clearly
present two discharge plateaus, which correspond to the two
cathodic peaks of the LSV curves. The speciﬁc capacity of the cell
with the C/S cathode is higher than the speciﬁc capacity of the cell
with the C/LiPS cathode, due to the different starting points of the
reduction reaction. As seen in the discharge proﬁles, the upper

When the regular electrolyte is used, it becomes difﬁcult to
observe the LiPS saturation because of the possible excess of
electrolyte in the cell and because the reactions become too
sluggish to fully utilize the entire active material at low E/S ratios.
One approach to create a saturation condition is to decrease LiPS
solubility in electrolyte deliberately by modifying the components
of the electrolyte. It is reported that when a high Li salt electrolyte
is used, the solubility of LiPS would be reduced signiﬁcantly due to
the common ion effect [19]. As a result, LiPS species would become
mostly insoluble in electrolyte and the electrochemical reactions of
Li ion intercalation would occur in the solid phase. Fig. 3a shows
the performance of the cell with the electrolyte containing 5 M
LiTFSI. The voltage proﬁles still exhibit two-stage discharge
characteristics, however, the discharge voltage has decreased with
respect to the cells with regular electrolyte, which was also

chemical reactions, it is noticed that the sluggish solid-solid
process tends to result in reduced cell conductivity and increased
cell polarization, while the liquid phase reaction yields much faster
reaction kinetics.
If both reaction pathways coexisted in a liquid cell, LiPS
solubility would not limit the capacity of the cell. In this case, as
illustrated in Fig. 4, when the sulfur reaction occurs at the point
where saturation is reached in the electrolyte, LiPS would be
continuously formed and precipitate in the electrolyte. The LiPS in
the liquid electrolyte can be further reduced through a liquid phase
reaction, while the precipitated LiPS can be reduced through a
solid-solid reaction. In addition, when the concentration of the LiPS
in the liquid electrolyte decreases below saturation, the solid-state
LiPS is expected to be dissolved and reduced in liquid phase.
In order to verify the above assumptions, we prepared a
saturated electrolyte containing both the liquid phase and the solid
phase reaction pathways. The saturation condition was achieved
by using a high concentration LiPS catholyte ([S] = 5 M) together
with the sulfur-containing cathodes. During the preparation of the
LiPS catholyte, it was observed that further increasing the
concentration to [S] = 6 M results in solid precipitation in the
electrolyte, indicating that the maximum LiPS concentration in the
electrolyte is less than [S] = 6 M. The sulfur loading in the cathodes
corresponds to an extra sulfur concentration of [S] = 0.5–2 M
assuming full conversion. Therefore, the extremely high concen-
trations of LiPS and co-salts saturate the solution, prohibiting
further dissolution of LiPS.
As seen in Fig. 5a, the capacities of the upper plateaus increase
monotonically with the cathode sulfur loading. The upper plateaus
can be further divided into two parts as seen in the lower-left inset.
First, the relatively ﬂat region at the beginning of the discharge
(indicated by solid lines in Fig. 5a) is attributed to the reduction of
sulfur to Li2S8. Note that the overall increase of the upper plateau
capacity originates from this region. According to the capacities in
this region, if the sulfur species of sample #1 C/S was dissolved in
the electrolyte the total Li2S8 concentration would be [S] = 7 M,
which is above the solubility limit. Therefore, we conclude that
there exists a solid phase Li2S8. Second, the dashed oblique lines
are associated with the conversion from high-order LiPS to low-
order LiPS. Note that the slopes and associated capacities are
similar in this region.
These results imply that the reduction of sulfur to Li2S8 is not
inhibited by the high concentration of the Li2S8 catholyte.
However, the generated Li2S8 does not further convert to low-
order LiPS, indicating that the active material is not used
completely. This effect is most likely due to the fact that the
electrolyte is saturated during the conversion from high-order LiPS
to low-order LiPS and that LiPS has reached its solubility limit. We
also observe that the overall capacities of cells with C/S cathodes
decreased compared to the capacity of the cell with carbon-only
cathode. This decrease is due to the reduction of the lower plateau
capacities (2.9 mAh for C-only, 0.9 mAh for #1 C/S, 1.3 mAh for
#2 C/S, and 1.2 mAh for #3 C/S). The corresponding peak intensity
of the sample #2 C/S cathode is signiﬁcantly reduced compared to
the C-only cathode, as shown by the LSV measurements presented
in Fig. 5b.

Although not indicated in Fig. 6, our experiments show that one
does not obtain any capacity gain by discharging the cell down to
1.2 V. This phenomenon is due to the fact that the solid-state LiPS
cannot be further dissolved in the saturated electrolyte to react in
liquid phase and, at the same time, cannot be electrochemically
reduced through the solid reaction pathway. The different
discharge proﬁles under the two LiPS concentrations clearly
demonstrate the effects of LiPS solubility on cell capacity. Despite
the fact that there are two pathways to reduce Li2S8 (through either
the LiPS-soluble liquid phase or the LiPS-insoluble solid phase), the
LiPS-soluble liquid phase contributes the most to the discharge
process. This can be attributed to the difference in the reaction
rates of the two pathways. In general, at the discharge rates used in
this article, the reactions in the LiPS-soluble liquid phase pathway
dominate the discharge process. Therefore, the cell capacity is
given almost exclusively by the amount of soluble LiPS in the
electrolyte, which is determined by the solubility of LiPS. Once the
saturation condition is reached, the solid-state LiPS no longer
contributes to cell capacity, which explains why the C/LiPS
cathodes with high concentration catholyte have similar capaci-
ties.
When LiPS is generated as a result of sulfur reduction, the
battery is unlikely to deliver its entire theoretical capacity. The LiPS
must be readily reduced electrochemically, otherwise it eventually
becomes a “dead” sulfur material. The above results suggest that
the LiPS-soluble reaction pathway dominates in Li-S cells with
liquid electrolyte. When the LiPS solubility limit is reached, any
additional sulfur is reduced to solid-state LiPS and the subsequent
reduction reactions occur exclusively in the liquid phase. Since the
maximum number of LiPS species available for reaction in the
liquid phase is limited by LiPS solubility, the total speciﬁc capacity
of the cell also becomes limited.

taking into consideration the limitations imposed by the ﬁnite
solubility of LiPS in the electrolyte. In these calculations, only the

the LiPS solubility has been reached is redundant.
To boost the speciﬁc energy of Li-S cells, one should use
electrolytes with high LiPS solubility. Fig. 7d plots the speciﬁc
energy of the Li-S cell as a function of the E/S ratio for different LiPS
solubilities. By increasing the solubility of LiPS above 6.5 M, one
can decrease the E/S ratio of the cell to 4.8 ml g1, and reach the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) speciﬁc energy target of 500 Wh kg1.
A primary Li-S battery with an energy density of 504 Wh kg1 was
recently reported [25]. However, this high energy density was
achieved at an extremely low rate of 0.01C. At this very low
discharge rate, the two reaction pathways might coexist and the
simultaneous subsequent reactions could consume LiPS in solution
and enable dissolution of more solid-state LiPS as supplement,
thus increasing the “effective” LiPS solubility and speciﬁc energy.
Despite the high theoretical speciﬁc capacity of Li-S batteries,
the “effective” energy density is much lower when considering the
mass of the electrolyte. The common E/S ratio of Li-S cells in the
literature is well above 10 ml g1. A sulfur loading of 1.12 M was
optimized to suppress polysulﬁde shuttle in [26] and an E/S ratio of
20 ml g1 was determined for optimal cell performance in [27].
However, these values are not yet feasible to drive the speciﬁc
energy up to 500 Wh kg1. Recently, Li-S batteries with high
capacity and low E/S ratio of 4–5 ml g1 were reported [28,29]. For
the state-of-art practical Li-S batteries, a minimum E/S ratio of 3–
4 ml g1 is considered to be necessary [30]. The difference between
the E/S ratio of coin cell assemblies and practical pouch cells casts
addition doubt on the scalability of the laboratory-scale process. In
addition, increasing the capacity under saturation conditions
seems to be an equally difﬁcult task. As shown in this article,

4. Conclusion
The E/S ratio of Li-S cells has a signiﬁcant effect on both the
performance and the theoretical energy density of Li-S batteries.
Specially designed cathode structures and electrolyte conﬁgura-
tions were built to evaluate the effects of LiPS solubility on cell
capacity. Two pathways involving reduction reactions of the active
material in liquid and solid phase are analyzed. At normal
discharge rates the reaction in the liquid phase dominates the
discharge process. We conclude that, although sulfur is reduced to
solid-state LiPS in saturated electrolytes, its further electrochemi-
cal conversion is hindered by the ﬁnite LiPS solubility and,
therefore, the cell does not reach its maximum theoretical
capacity. Our analytical calculations show that the theoretical
energy density of Li-S batteries is signiﬁcantly reduced by taking
into consideration the ﬁnite solubility of the LiPS in the electrolyte.
More theoretical and experimental studies are needed to under-
stand the Li-S cell performance and evaluate its energy density
under lean electrolyte conditions.
