High-eﬃciency Lithium Metal Stabilization and Polysulﬁde
Suppression in Li-S Battery Enabled by Weakly Solvating
Solvent
Thuy Duong Pham, Abdullah Bin Faheem, Junam Kim, Seung-Hyeok Ma,
Kyungwon Kwak,* and Kyung-Koo Lee*
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered a highly promising
next-generation energy storage technology due to their exceptional energy
density and cost-eﬀectiveness. However, the practical use of current LSBs is
hindered primarily by issues related to the “shuttle eﬀect” of lithium
polysulﬁde (LiPS) intermediates and the growth of lithium dendrites. In
strongly solvating electrolytes, the solvent-derived solid electrolyte interphase
(SEI) lacks mechanical strength due to organic components, leading to
ineﬀective lithium dendrite suppression and severe LiPS dissolution and
shuttling. In contrast, the weakly solvating electrolyte (WSE) can create an
anion-derived SEI layer which can enhance compatibility with lithium metal
anode, and restricting LiPS solubility. Herein, a WSE consisting of 0.4 м LiTFSI
in the mixture of 1,4-dioxane (DX):dimethoxymethane (DMM) is designed to
overcome the issues associated with LSB. Surface analyses conﬁrmed the
formation of a beneﬁcial SEI layer rich in LiF, enabling homogeneous lithium
deposition with an average Coulombic eﬃciency CE exceeding 99% over 100
cycles. Implementing the low-concentration WSE in Li||SPAN cells yielded an
impressive initial speciﬁc capacity of 671 mAh g−1. This research highlights
the advantages of WSE and oﬀers the pathway for cost-eﬀective electrolyte
development, enabling the realization of high-performance LSBs.

toward enhancing the performance and safety of LMA-based

Over the past few years, there has been a growing interest in
weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) as prospective electrolytes
for both LIBs and LMBs.[21,24–26] WSEs utilize solvents possess-
ing low dielectric constants, which inherently result in weaker
solvating abilities. WSEs exhibit a distinctive solvation structure
that promotes the formation of contact ion pairs (CIPs) and ag-
gregates (AGGs), even when salt concentrations are at a low to
moderate level. This unique characteristic arises from the weak
interaction between the salt ions and the solvent, causing the
salt anions to preferentially interact with Li+ and form abundant
CIPs and AGGs. As a result, the interfacial chemistry shifts from
solvent-dominated to anion-dominated. Unlike conventional liq-
uid electrolytes where the cost of Li salt constitutes a signiﬁ-
cant portion of the total cost (≈70%), WSEs oﬀer a potential
solution to the cost issue by allowing regulation of the solva-
tion structure at low concentrations.[27] Yao et al. introduced an
electrolyte composed of 1.0 м lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide
(LiFSI) in 1,4-dioxane (DX) for use in a WSE. This electrolyte
exhibited a distinctive solvation structure characterized by the
prevalence of CIPs and AGGs.[28] It demonstrated exceptional in-
terfacial charge transport kinetics and remarkable stability, thus
enabling rapid charging and long-term cycling of LIBs employing
graphite electrodes. In our previous work, a WSE which consisted
0.3 м LiFSI and 0.2 м lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) in DX was proposed.[21] In the electrolyte system, the
predominant proportion of the salt anions, such as FSI– and
TFSI–, were present in the form of CIPs and AGGs. This conﬁg-
uration yielded an average CE of 99.2% for Li plating/stripping
and led to enhanced cycling stability in the Li||LiFePO4 (LFP) cell.
This cell exhibited a capacity retention of 78.5% after 400 cycles.
LSBs have gained signiﬁcant attention as a promising next-
generation energy storage technology, primarily attributed to
their elevated theoretical energy density of 1675 mAh g−1, cost-
eﬀectiveness, and environmental friendliness.[29–31] However, the
industrialization of LSBs faces several challenges,[29,30] includ-
ing the dissolution of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) generated
during the charge and discharge.[29,32] These intermediate reac-
tion species, particularly long-chain lithium LiPSs (Li2Sx,4<x<8)
can dissolve in the electrolyte and migrate to the anode side,
where they react with Li metal to be reduced to short-chain LiPSs
(Li2Sx,1<x<3). It leads to the consumption of the LMAs and the
formation of “dead” sulfur, ultimately reducing the CE and stabil-
ity of LSBs. This phenomenon, often referred to as the “shuttle
eﬀect” hampers the stability and performance of LSBs. It is well
known that a highly solvating electrolyte can promote the forma-
tion of trisulfur radicals and exhibit a strong coordination capabil-
ity to solvate LiPSs, thereby increasing their solubility.[33] Hence,
one approach to address the challenges of LSBs is decreasing
the solubility of LiPSs by using WSEs. Zheng et al. reported a
WSE consisting of 1.0 м LiFSI in dimethoxymethane (DMM)], in
which Li|| sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) exhibited an initial
discharge capacity (422.3 mAh g−1) and acceptable capacity reten-
tion (63.8%) after 120 cycles at −40 °C.[25] However, the existing
literature on the impact of WSEs on LSB is limited, indicating a
potential need for additional research in this area. In this study,
a WSE consisting of low concentration LiTFSI of 0.4 м and bi-
nary weakly solvating solvents (WSSs) such as DX and DMM,
was investigated for application in LSBs. The investigated WSE
not only eﬀectively inhibited the dissolution of LiPSs but also pro-
tected the LMAs from the formation of dendrites. Consequently,
the LSBs exhibited enhanced electrochemical performances and
high cycling eﬃciency. The investigated WSE and its application
in LSBs oﬀer a pathway to address the challenges associated with
LiPSs dissolution and improve the performance and stability of
LSBs.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Rational Design Strategy
Sulfur-based cathodes are gaining attention as a promising alter-
native to metal oxide intercalation cathodes due to their potential
to achieve high energy densities of up to 500 Wh kg−1 in full-cell
batteries.[30,33] The key advantages of sulfur-based cathodes are
low cost, abundant supply, and high storage capacity. However,
the development of electrolytes for LSBs is still an active area of
research. In this section, the rational design of investigated low-
concentration WSE for LSBs will be discussed, focusing on the
eﬀects of each component and the principles of component se-
lection and optimization.
In lithium battery electrolytes, lithium salts play a crucial role
in providing Li+ ions and inﬂuencing the properties of the elec-
trolyte. Therefore, the selection of the lithium salt is also an im-
portant factor to consider. Among the various lithium salts, LiFSI
and LiTFSI are commonly used as primary salts in electrolytes
for LMBs due to their favorable characteristics, including good
thermal stability, high dissociation capacity, high ionic conduc-
tivity, and compatibility with ether solvents.[34] However, when
it comes to LSBs, LiTFSI is preferred over LiFSI. This prefer-
ence stems from the relatively weak N−S bond in the FSI– an-
ion, which allows it to break and react with Li2Sx, resulting in the
formation of lithium sulfate (LiSOx).[35,36] In contrast, the use of
LiTFSI-based electrolytes leads to form Li2Sx on the LMAs, and
this Li2Sx is more reversible than the LiSOx formed with LiFSI-
based electrolytes.[36] Therefore, LiTFSI was chosen as the pri-
mary salt for the investigated electrolyte in this study. Figure 1a
illustrates the impact of FSI– and TFSI– anions on the stability of
LSBs.
Choosing solvents for use in LSBs is more challenging than
in LMBs or commercial LIBs because the solvent must be stable
against LMAs and LiPSs at a minimum.[37] Carbonate solvents
have rarely been used for LSBs electrolytes because they can re-
act with Li2Sx to form thioether and sulfonium functionalities.[34]
In contrast, ether solvents are more compatible with LSBs due
to their high chemical and electrochemical stability.[34] Among
them, the DOL:DME (1:1 by volume) mixture is a popular solvent
mixture for LSBs.[34,35,38] However, one of the challenges in the
electrolyte systems is the high solubility of LiPSs at low and mod-
erate concentrations, which leads to irreversible reactions and the
formation of Li2S.[39]
A WSE is a promising option for use in LSBs as it meets the
necessary conditions for an electrolyte. For the anode side, the
weak solvation power of the solvent in WSE enables a larger num-
ber of anions to coordinate with Li+ and form an anion-derived
SEI layer. The cyclic solvent DX exhibited diminished solvating
power due to steric eﬀects that lead to alterations in the solva-
tion structure of Li+; hence, DX was selected as the ﬁrst WSS.[28]
However, the solubility of LiTFSI in DX is less than 0.3 м,

On the sulfur cathode side, a key objective is the mitigation
of the dissolution of LiPSs, eﬀectively averting the troublesome
“shuttle eﬀect” induced by these LiPSs. The dissolution tendency
of LiPS can be estimated based on its solubility of LiPS in a
given solvent. To elaborate, in order to diminish the dissolution of
LiPS, a solvent with a low solvating capacity for LiPSs is the opti-
mal choice. As LiPS adopts a salt-like composition encompassing
both a cation and anion, its solubility characteristics may mirror
those of lithium salts. It is worth noting that WSS compounds
may also exhibit restricted solubility of both LiPS and lithium
salts. Considering LiPSs as a form of lithium salt, its solubility
within a speciﬁc solvent will inherently reach a saturation point.
This notion was veriﬁed through an assessment of the solubility
of 0.1 м Li2S8 in a strong solvating solvent (DOL:DME) and the
WSE context (DX:DMM), as depicted in Figure 1c. The solution
of 0.1 м Li2S8 in DOL:DME underwent a color transformation
to dark brown within 24 h, aligning with the intense signal ob-
served in the ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectra. In contrast, the
solution of 0.1 м Li2S8 in DX:DMM maintained light yellow, con-
sistent with a weak UV–vis light absorption proﬁle.
In general, polysulﬁdes (PSs) tend to form clusters with the
Li+ ions. If the cluster is big in size, solvent interactions with the
PS species are reduced and the LiPS exhibits poor solubility.[39,40]
As discussed previously, the solubility of LiPSs was greater in
DOL:DME (1:1 by volume) in comparison to DX:DMM (1:1 by
volume) (Figure 1c). In order to investigate the solvation struc-
ture, CMD simulations were carried out for systems consisting
of 0.1 м Li2S8 in DOL:DME (1:1, by vol) or DX:DMM (1:1, by
vol) (see Supporting Information). The CMD force ﬁelds for the
solvent molecules are evaluated in Section 2.1 and the Support-
ing Information, while the force ﬁeld for LiPS has been validated
elsewhere.[41]

2.2. Physical Characteristics and Solvation Structure of the
Investigated Electrolyte
The macroscopic characteristics, encompassing ionic conductiv-
ity, shear viscosity, and the Li-ion transference number (t+), were
evaluated for two investigated electrolytes: the reference ether-
based electrolyte (REE) composed of 1.0 м LiTFSI in DOL:DME
(1:1 by volume), and low-concentration weakly solvating elec-
trolyte (LWE) containing 0.4 м LiTFSI in DX:DMM (1:1 by vol-
ume). The results of these assessments are summarized in
Table S2 (Supporting Information). The ionic conductivity
of LWE (0.3 mS cm−1) is notably inferior to that of REE
(10.63 mS cm−1). This decrease in ionic conductivity in LWE
can be attributed to the restricted presence of eﬀective charge
carriers, such as free ions. This phenomenon has also been
documented in prior literature, such as 0.5 м LiFSI in DX
(0.54 mS cm−1) and 1.0 м LiFSI in DMM (≈0.2 mS cm−1).[21,25]
Nonetheless, within LWE, the establishment of a conductive
and enduring SEI is attainable through the electrochemical re-
action of associated species (to be discussed in Figure 5). The
viscosity of LWE (1.29 cP) was slightly lower than that of REE
(1.57 cP). The t+, which signiﬁes the proportionate involve-
ment of Li+ in the overall ionic transport the overall ionic
transport, was determined by employing the Bruce–Vincent
method (refer to the Experimental Section in Supporting Infor-
mation). The LWE exhibited t+ value of 0.56, while REE had
a t+ value of 0.36. In electrolytes where t+ exceeds 0.5, cation
movement makes a greater contribution to total ionic conduc-
tivity than anion movement.[42] A high t+ can also improve
electrode kinetics and help prevent the formation of lithium
dendrites.[43]

Within liquid electrolytes, the solvation structure of Li+ is de-
termined by the interplay between the coordination of Li+ with
both anions and solvents. These species compete for interac-
tions with Li+ through ion–ion or ion–dipole interactions.[44]
A straightforward approach to enable greater coordination be-
tween Li+ and salt anions is by reducing the binding strength
between Li+ and the solvent. This leads to the formation of anion-
derived SEI layer on LMA. To investigate the solvation structures
of REE and LWE, Raman spectroscopy was employed. This analy-
sis was conducted in the frequency range of 700–780 cm−1, which
corresponds to the symmetric stretching vibration mode of the
S─N─S bonds present in the TFSI– anion (Figure 3). The orig-
inal spectra were represented by dotted black lines, while the
ﬁtting results were depicted by solid beige lines. The spectra
were deconvoluted into multiple Voigt (Gaussian–Lorentz) func-
tions, and the proportion of ionic speciation was calculated by
dividing the area of each speciﬁc peak by the total area encom-
passing all peaks. The peaks observed at ≈738 cm−1 (blue line),
≈741 cm−1 (olive line), ≈745 cm−1 (light red line), and ≈748 cm−1
(green line) corresponded to free TFSI–, SSIP (TFSI– anions sep-
arated by more than one solvent), CIP (one TFSI– interacting
with one Li+), and AGG (TFSI– interacting with two or more
Li+), respectively.[45–47] The analysis of the solvation structure of
the REE reveals that free anions dominate, comprising 55.6% of
the electrolyte. Additionally, SSIP and CIP account for 25.2% and
19.2%, respectively. Notably, the absence of a vibration signal at
≈748 cm−1 indicates the scarcity of AGGs in the REE. This sug-
gests that TFSI−anions predominantly exist as free anions, and
correspondingly, the cations are also mainly found as free cations
coordinated with solvents in the electrolyte. The notable presence
of abundant free Li+ and TFSI−species can be attributed as the
primary factor contributing to the high ionic conductivity in the
REE. However, this high concentration of free ions also leads to a
low Li+ transference number. In contrast, the analysis of the sol-
vation structure of LWE reveals that a signiﬁcant portion of TFSI−
anions existed in associated species, speciﬁcally CIP (37.8%) and
AGG (24.4%). This suggests a strong coordination between Li+
and TFSI−within the binary WSSs. The WSE studied in this work
had a distinct solvation structure, with CIPs and AGGs being pre-
dominant even at low LiTFSI concentrations of 0.4 м. However, as
these complexes are eﬀectively neutral, they are unable to serve
as eﬃcient charge carriers, leading to a reduction in ionic con-
ductivity.
As seen from the Raman spectra, in comparison to REE, the
LWE can signiﬁcantly modify the solvation structures in the elec-
trolyte. To further investigate this, CMD simulations of REE
and LWE were carried out (see Supporting Information). From
the calculated physical properties of REE and LWE, it can be
seen that the CMD simulations reasonably reproduce experi-
mental trends. This signiﬁes the eﬃcacy of the methods used
herein.

2.3. Electrochemical Characteristics of the Investigated
Electrolytes
2.3.1. Inherent Electrochemical Stability of Investigated Electrolytes
The inherent electrochemical stability window of the REE and
LWE was evaluated through linear sweep voltammetry (LSV)
measurements. A platinum (Pt) disk was employed as the work-
ing electrode, with Li metal fulﬁlling the roles of the counter
and reference electrodes. For every LSV measurement, new elec-
trodes and fresh electrolytes were used. Figure S1a (Support-
ing Information) illustrates that LWE has a higher oxidation
potential than REE. However, in the case of both electrolytes,

2.3.2. Cycling Performance of Lithium Metal Anode and
Characterization of the SEI Layer
The compatibility between electrolyte and LMA was evaluated by
measuring the Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li||Cu cells, as de-
picted in Figure 5a. The CE was calculated as the ratio of the Li
deposited and subsequently stripped from the Cu substrate. This
test was performed at a current density of 0.25 mA cm−2 and a
deposition capacity of 0.25 mAh cm−2. The CE serves as an indi-
cator of the degree of side reactions between LMA and the elec-
trolytes during the strip and deposition of Li. A high CE value
indicates a low occurrence of side reactions and suggests good
compatibility between the LMA and the electrolytes. As shown in
Figure 5a, there is a clear and noticeable diﬀerence in the CEs of
Li||Cu cells using the two electrolytes. The CE of Li||Cu cell with
REE started at ≈90%, but it ﬂuctuated and decreased during cy-
cling. After 15 cycles, the CE had dropped to below 57%, indicat-
ing severe side reactions. Conversely, Li||Cu with LWE exhibited
a remarkably high CE of ≈99% for over 100 cycles. These high
CEs indicate low reactivity of WSE and minimal side reactions
with the electrodes, which can be attributed to the formation of
a stable SEI layer on the lithium anode surface (to be discussed
later). Despite LWE having lower ionic conductivity compared to
REE, its cycling performance improved owing to favorable inter-
face chemical properties.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to as-
sess the compatibility and stability of the REE and LWE with
LMAs. To determine the resistances of the Li||Li symmetric cells
with studied electrolytes at diﬀerent time intervals (0, 1, 5, 10,
20, and 40 h) after assembling the coin cells, electrochemical
impedance spectra were measured and Nyquist plots are plotted
in Figure S2a,b (Supporting Information). An equivalent circuit
(Figure S2c, Supporting Information) was employed to analyze
the bulk electrolyte resistance (Rb), SEI layer resistance (RSEI),
and charge transfer resistance (Rct). REE exhibited a smaller Rb
(≈2 Ω) compared to LWE (≈25 Ω) because of its higher ionic con-
ductivity. The evolution of EIS over time provides insights into
the chemical stability of the Li/electrolyte interface. For REE, the
RSEI rapidly increased after only 1 h, continued to rise until 5 h,
and then experienced a sharp decrease between 5 and 10 h. Sub-
sequently, it gradually increased again between 20 and 40 h. This
indicates that the REE was unable to establish a stable SEI layer,
resulting in ongoing side reactions between REE and LMA dur-
ing the standing time. In contrast, the RSEI of the cell with LWE
slightly increased between 0 and 5 h and then did not change
signiﬁcantly after 10 h. This indicates the elevated chemical sta-
bility of the Li/electrolyte interfaces formed with LWE. After 40 h,
the RSEI in the cell with LWE (≈155 Ω) was more than two times
lower than that of the cell with REE electrolyte (≈282 Ω). As a re-
sult, the total resistance of the cell employing LWE electrolyte was

To gain further insights into the composition of the SEI layer,
XPS measurements were conducted. Figure 5d and Figure S3
(Supporting Information) illustrate the spectra of C 1s and F 1s,
respectively, along with the deconvoluted peaks representing the
composition of the corresponding SEIs formed on the electrodes.
The binding energy in the XPS analysis was calibrated based on
the hydrocarbon C 1s peak (285 eV). As displayed in Figure S3
(Supporting Information), in both electrolytes, XPS spectra of
the SEI layers showed peaks corresponding to C─C, C─O, and
O─C═O bonds in the C 1s spectra, indicating the presence of
solvent decomposition. Remarkably, the peak intensity of the sp3
C─C atom increased in REE despite having fewer organic sol-
vents compared to LWE. Moreover, the atomic composition per-
centage of C within the SEI layer reveals the following sequence
for the C-containing species in the cell with investigated elec-
trolytes exhibits: LWE (47.49%) < REE (51.67%). These results
suggest that REE electrolyte has a stronger tendency to form an
SEI layer predominantly composed of solvent-derived organic
species. An SEI layer primarily constituted of organic compo-
nents lacks the ability to adequately curb ongoing side reactions
between Li metal and the electrolyte. This is substantiated by the
low CE and dendrite morphologies in Figure 5a,b, respectively.
Furthermore, organic species can be easily dissolved in the or-
ganic electrolyte, exposing the bare reactive surface of LMA. This
leads to the continuous undesired reaction of the electrolyte, low-
ering CEs. In the F 1s spectra, both electrolytes showed two peaks
at 685.2 and 689.1 eV, corresponding to the presence of LiF and
─CF3 (LiTFSI), respectively.[42] The LiF peak originated from the
decomposition of TFSI–, while the ─CF3 peak can be attributed
to the original LiTFSI salt.[49,50] Therefore, the relative area ratio
of LiF/─CF3 can serve as an indicator of the complete decomposi-
tion of TFSI– anions. The relative area ratio of LiF/─CF3 was 2.3
and 3.7 in REE and LWE, respectively. This result implies that
LiTFSI more completely decomposes to form LiF on the anode
surface in LWE relative to REE, due to its unique solvation struc-
ture (further discussed in the upcoming section). Consequently,
LiTFSI decomposition in LWE tends to form LiF species, which
gives the SEI layer improved mechanical strength and adhesive
strength. This enables the SEI layer to accommodate the defor-
mation occurring on the surface of Li metal anode, as displayed
in Figure 5c.[51,52] It is commonly accepted that a solvent with a
low reduction potential, like LWE electrolyte, facilitates the pref-
erential decomposition of ﬂuorinated salts.[53] Therefore, the for-
mation of LiF-rich layers in LWE plays a crucial role in achieving
high CE and inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites.
To provide additional evidence for the aforementioned hypoth-
esis, quantum calculations were conducted to determine the low-
est unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of the free mixed
solvents, free TFSI−anion, and associated complexes (Li+ ···
TFSI−) in diﬀerent solvents. Solvation eﬀects were incorporated
through an implicit solvent model using the polarized contin-
uum model. The results of the DFT calculations are outlined in
Figure S4 (Supporting information). In terms of thermodynamic,
the LUMO level signiﬁes the energy level at which an additional
electron can be accommodated This implies that molecules pos-
sessing lower LUMO levels are more likely to undergo reduc-
tion. As depicted in Figure S4 (Supporting information), the as-
sociated complexes [CIP (−0.77 eV) and AGG (−2.40 eV)] in
DX:DMM exhibited lower LUMO energy levels compared to CIP
(−0.62 eV)in DOL:DME, indicating their preferential reduction
in forming a SEI layer during the cycling process. Additionally,
the analysis of the solvation structure of LWE revealed a signif-
icant presence of TFSI−anions in associated state complexes,
speciﬁcally CIP (37.8%) and AGG (24.4%) (refer to Figure 3).
Consequently, the associated complexes in LWE preferentially
undergo the reduction reaction, leading to the formation of a ro-
bust SEI layer derived from salt anion, as evidenced by the XPS
results presented in Figure 5d. Notably, this SEI layer is enriched
with conductive inorganic compounds like LiF.
2.3.3. Li||SPAN Full Cell Performance
Sulfur-based cathodes have emerged as a promising alterna-
tive to metal oxide intercalation cathodes for achieving high
energy densities. The SPAN cathode is particularly noteworthy
due to its unique physical conﬁnement of small molecular sul-
fur within a conductive polymer network. This conﬁnement al-
lows for a speciﬁc capacity of over 550 mAh g−1 and an av-
erage discharge potential of ≈1.8 V, making the SPAN cath-
ode highly attractive for energy storage applications.[54] To eval-
uate our designed electrolyte systems in electrochemical cells,
Li||SPAN cells were cycled in two diﬀerent electrolytes such as
REE and LWE (Figure 6). The cells were cycled between 1.0 and

1.0 C = 1672 mAh g−1

high initial capacity (after two formation cycles) of 619 mAh g−1.
However, this capacity quickly decreased to 311 mAh g−1 after
100 cycles, resulting in a capacity retention of only ≈50%. On
the other hand, the Li||SPAN cell using LWE showed excellent
electrochemical performance in all three aspects: 1) it had a high
initial speciﬁc capacity of 671 mAh g−1, 2) it had a high capac-

solubility of LiPSs, which stands as a primary challenge in LSB.

3. Conclusion
In summary, our study focuses on the design of a low-
concentration weakly solvating electrolyte that exhibits improved
performances for both LMA and SPAN cathodes in LSB. Both the
experimental and theoretical results showcased the ability of the
investigated WSE in being able to eﬀectively prevent the dissolu-
tion of LiPSs. As a result, the shuttle eﬀect commonly observed in
LSBs during repeated charge/discharge cycles was successfully
suppressed. Raman spectroscopy and MD simulations provided
insights into the strong coordination between Li+ and TFSI−in
binary WSSs (DX:DMM). This coordination resulted in a distinct
solvation structure characterized by the prevalence of CIPs and
AGGs at a low salt concentration of 0.4 m. Surface analyses fur-
ther conﬁrmed the formation of a beneﬁcial SEI layer rich in LiF.
As a consequence, homogenous deposition of Li was achieved
with a high average CE of ≈99% over more than 100 cycles. LWE
was successfully employed in constructing Li||SPAN cells, re-
sulting in a remarkable initial speciﬁc capacity of 671 mAh g−1
and substantial capacity retention of 70% after 100 cycles. This
study presents an eﬀective strategy for stabilizing both LMAs and
SPAN cathodes in WSEs. This concept can pave the way for the
practical implementation of LSBs, addressing the challenges as-
sociated with electrode stability and enabling the realization of
high-performance and cost-eﬀective energy storage solutions.

Materials:

Chemicals.
1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME,
anhydrous,
99.5%),
1,4-

and bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI, 99.95% trace
metals basis), lithium sulﬁde (Li2S, 99.98% trace metals basis) were
ordered from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were stored in a glove box
ﬁlled with argon gas, ensuring H2O and O2 levels below 1.0 ppm.
Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 150,000), elemental sulfur (S, powder,
≥99.0%), poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF, Mw = 534,000), and N-Methyl-
2-pyrrolidone (NMP, anhydrous, 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich. Carbon black (super P, conductive, 99+% (metals basis)) was or-
dered from Alfa Aesar. Copper (Cu, thickness = 20 μm) foil, aluminum
(Al, thickness = 25 μm) foil, and polypropylene ﬁlm (PP, Celgard 2400)
were procured from Wellcos Corporation; while lithium metal chips (di-
ameter = 16.0 mm, thickness = 250 μm) were acquired from MTI Corpo-
ration.
Electrolyte Preparation:
The reference electrolyte was prepared by com-
bining 1.0 м LiTFSI with DOL:DME (1:1, by vol.). The investigated elec-
trolyte consists of 0.4 м LiTFSI mixed with a combination of DX and DMM
(1:1, by vol.). All handling and preparation of electrolytes were performed
in the glove box.
Electrode Preparation:
Cu substrates were obtained by punching Cu
foil to a diameter of 19 mm. Lithium metal chips were employed as the
anode without any modiﬁcations. To synthesize sulfurized polyacryloni-
trile (SPAN), PAN and S were milled together in a mortar with a mass
ratio of 1:4. The milling process continued until a homogenous mixture
was achieved. The blended powders underwent heating within an argon-
ﬁlled tube furnace at 450 °C for 6 h, employing a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1,
followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. The sulfur loading in
the SPAN was determined to be ≈39.0% by mass via elemental analysis
(refer to Table S1, Supporting Information). To prepare the SPAN cathode,
the synthesized SPAN powder was combined with super P and PVDF with
a mass ratio of 80:10:10 in NMP solvent to form a slurry. The slurry was
then cast on etching Al and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C overnight.
Polysulﬁde Dissolution Measurement:
The lithium polysulﬁde dissolu-
tion experiments were conducted as follows: Li2S and S were mixed in a
mole ratio of 1:7. This mixture was then dissolved in solvents (DOL:DME
and DX:DMM) and investigated electrolytes at 0.1 м concentration. To
assess the dissolution of LiPS, the UV–vis absorbance spectrum of the
prepared solutions was obtained using a Shimadzu UV-2550 Spectropho-
tometer. The spectrophotometer was conﬁgured for single-beam opera-
tion, scanning a wavelength range from 300 to 900 nm. Before the mea-
surements, baseline corrections were applied.
Physical Properties Measurement:
The ionic conductivity of electrolytes
was measured using a conductivity benchtop meter (S30, Mettler Toledo)
equipped with a Cond sensor Inlab710 (Mettler Toledo). Prior to measure-
ment, the instrument was calibrated with a standard aqueous solution
(0.1 m KCl). Shear viscosity measurements were conducted using Brook-
ﬁeld DV3T rheometer. The rheometer was calibrated using deionized water
as reference ﬂuid. Both the ionic conductivity and viscosity measurements
were performed at a temperature of 25 °C.
Characterization of Solvation Structure:
Raman spectroscopy was con-
ducted at 25 °C using a Raman microscope (RAMANtouch, Nanophoton)
equipped with a laser with a 532 nm laser as the light source.
Electrochemical Measurements:
The lithium transference number (t+)
was ascertained by combining direct-current (DC) polarization and alter-
nating current (AC) impedance measurements within a symmetrical Li||Li
cell. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed to determine the
electrochemical stability windows of the electrolytes. A three-electrode cell
system was employed, with a platinum (Pt) disk as the working electrode
and lithium metal serving as both the counter and reference electrodes.
LSV was carried out at a scan rate of 2 mV s−1. For the evaluation of elec-
trolytes and resistance of cell systems, electrochemical impedance spec-
troscopy (EIS) was utilized in the frequency range from 10 mHz to 1.0
MHz, employing amplitude of 10 mV. LSV, EIS, and tLi
+ measurements
were conducted at 25 °C with a Bio-logic SP-50 instrument.
Cycling tests were performed using coin cells (CR2032-type) on a
WBCS3000L battery cycler system (manufactured by Wonatech, Korea),
maintaining constant temperature conditions of 25 or 50 °C. The Columbic
eﬃciency of the lithium electrode was assessed through Li||Cu cells em-
ploying a PP separator, where Cu acted as the substrate for Li metal depo-
sition. In the case of Li||SPAN coin cells, galvanostatic charge/discharge
was executed within the voltage range of 1.0 to 3.0 V versus Li/Li+.
Characterization of Electrodes:
Surface morphologies and surface com-
position of Li deposition were analyzed using a scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). After opening
the cells in the glovebox, the Li deposited on Cu substrates were soaked
and rinsed with the corresponding fresh ether solvent. These samples were
then vacuum dried at 25 °C in the glovebox antechambers. To prevent ex-
posure to air, the samples were sealed under an argon atmosphere into a
glass container before transferring to the SEM or XPS instruments. SEM
images were obtained using a Hitachi S4800 instrument, applying an ac-
celerating voltage of 5 kV and an absorbed current of 10 μA. XPS measure-
ments were performed utilizing a K-alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrom-
eter (Thermo Fisher) with a monochromatic Al K𝛼source (1486.7 eV) for
excitation.
Computational Methods:
Quantum mechanical (QM) calculations
were performed in order to determine the binding energies between Li+
and TFSI– in the DOL:DME and DX:DMM solvents. Furthermore, the op-
timized structure and molecular orbital energies of the free solvents, free
TFSI−, and associated complexes (Li+ ··· TFSI−) were obtained.
In order to investigate the solvation structures present within the in-
vestigated electrolytes and the solubility of LiPS in the DOL:DME or
DX:DMM solvents, classical molecular dynamics (CMD) simulations were
performed. Further details regarding the QM calculations and the CMD
simulations can be found in the Supporting Information.
