Tandemly promoting the sulfur redox kinetics through low concentration
mixed organodiselenide and organoditelluride in Ah-level high-energy-
density Li-S batteries
Jiangqi Zhou1,4, Chengyong Shu1, Qianyu Zhang3*, Wei Tang1* and Yuping Wu2*
ABSTRACT
Homogeneous redox mediation is efficient in
alleviating the shuttling effect and slow redox kinetics of li-
thium polysulfides in lithium-sulfur batteries. However, their
perfect performance is not fulfilled owning to the fact that the
multi-step transformation of lithium polysulfides requests the
multifunctional active positions for the tandem catalysis.
Based on the redox comediation principles, a promoter of
mixing organodiselenide and organoditelluride (mixed-Se/Te)
was raised to induce tandem catalysis and boost the effective
electrochemical conversion of lithium polysulfides. More
specifically, diphenyl diselenide facilitated the liquid-liquid
and solid-liquid transformation between lithium polysulfides
and sulfur, while diphenyl ditelluride improved the solid-li-
quid transformation concerning lithium sulfide deposition.
Consequently, even under high sulfur loading of 6.5 mg cm−2
and low electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 5.88 μL mg−1, the 10 mM
low concentration mixed-Se/Te promoter offered a high dis-
charge capacity of 6.6 mAh cm−2 and high rate performance of
4.1 mAh cm−2 at 0.5 C. Moreover, the assembled 1.5 Ah-level
lithium-sulfur pouch cells provide an energy density of
332 Wh kg−1 at 0.05 C and good cycling stability. Our research
demonstrates the applicability of propelling continuous sulfur
conversion reactions with detached active positions and is
anticipated to stimulate deep molecular design of kinetic
promoter to targeted energy-associated redox reactions.
Keywords: lithium-sulfur batteries, redox kinetics, tandem cat-
alysis, organodiselenide, organoditelluride
INTRODUCTION
The rapid advancement in global energy and environmental
demands has spurred extensive research into next-generation
energy storage technologies [1–3]. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) bat-
teries have attracted significant attention due to their theoretical
ultrahigh energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 and the low cost of
sulfur raw materials. Typically, sulfur undergoes redox reactions
between solid lithium sulfide (Li2S), soluble lithium polysulfides
(LiPSs, Li2Sn, 3 ≤n ≤8), and solid elemental sulfur during the
charge and discharge processes [4–6]. These multi-phase and
multi-electron transformations result in a working battery with
an exceptionally high specific capacity of 1675 mAh g−1. How-
ever, the full utilization of the cathode’s advantages is severely
restricted by the sluggish conversion kinetics of LiPSs. This
challenge is further compounded by high sulfur loading and lean
electrolyte conditions, which are necessary to achieve practical
high energy densities [7–9]. Therefore, the development of
kinetic promoters is crucial to enhance sulfur redox reactions
and achieve high-energy-density Li-S batteries, particularly
under practical conditions.
To address this issue, a variety of heterogeneous promoters,
including heteroatom-doped nanocarbon, metal nanoparticles,
metal compounds, and single-atom electrocatalysts, have been
proposed to enhance surface adsorption of LiPS intermediates
through intermolecular interactions and reduce the energy
barriers for LiPS redox conversions [10–12]. However, these
surface adsorption processes are hindered by the limited number
of active sites at the interfaces between the heterogeneous pro-
moters and the substrates (Fig. 1a, b). Such active sites can be
easily covered by insulating Li2S deposits, ultimately becoming
inactive. As a result, the specific capacity of the battery decays
rapidly during cycling when using these heterogeneous pro-
moters [13–15].
Homogeneous promoters presented by minor molecular
redox mediators (RMs) provide a unique yet effective approach
to accelerating redox kinetics by modulating the reactions of
LiPSs species in tandem with electrochemical processes (Fig. 1c)
[16–19]. Among these, the redox comediation strategy is
recently proposed via enhancing the inherent redox mediation
properties of LiPSs by altering their molecular structure [20,21].
Redox comediators (coRMs) are employed to reversibly interact
with LiPSs, resulting in products with superior redox mediation
capacities, thereby accelerating sulfur redox kinetics [22]. For
instance, Di-t-butyl disulfide was initially used as a coRM to
react with LiPSs, producing lithium t-butyl polysulfide species
with higher reactivity and diffusivity, thus improving perfor-
mance [23]. Similarly, organodiselenide and organoditelluride,
containing reactive selenium and tellurium, act as coRMs to
modify LiPSs and enhance redox kinetics [24–26]. However, the
continuous transformation of LiPSs requires a catalyst design
with consistently structured and functionally unique active sites
for tandem catalysis. This design should effectively trap and

intermediates, while neglecting others. Thus, it is crucial to

In the past decade, Li-S pouch batteries have garnered sig-
nificant research attention, yet they remain in the early stages of
development. While these batteries exhibit a remarkable increase
in energy density, their cycle life has not seen substantial
advancements [30–32]. For instance, Li-S pouch batteries with a
capacity below 0.1 Ah typically struggle to exceed 300 cycles,
with a retention rate falling below 80%. Similarly, batteries with
capacities ranging from 0.1 to 1 Ah demonstrate comparable
cycle life challenges, while those exceeding 1 Ah often achieve
only a few dozen cycles. Consequently, the cycle life of Li-S
pouch batteries is significantly below commercial standards,
highlighting the urgent need for focused research on improving
cycle stability and overall longevity.
In this work, in accordance with the principles of the redox
comediation strategy, we present a comprehensive and com-
parative study on the partial substitution of sulfur in LiPSs with
its group 16 counterparts, such as selenium and tellurium.
According to previous literature, diphenyl diselenide (DPDSe)
and diphenyl ditelluride (DPDTe) decompose in the electrolyte,
reacting spontaneously with LiPSs to produce soluble lithium

promoter of mixing organodiselenide and organoditelluride

the effective electrochemical conversion of lithium polysulfides

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To elucidate the chemical interactions between LiPSs and
DPDSe/DPDTe, Li2S6 was selected as a representative redox

scopic analysis was conducted to verify these chemical reactions.

of the DPDSe + Li2S6 and DPDTe + Li2S6 samples at 280 nm
significantly exceeded those of the pure Li2S6 and DPDSe/
DPDTe mixtures (Fig. 2a), indicating a spontaneous exchange
between Se–Se/Te–Te and S–S bonds [33,34]. Raman spectro-
scopy further confirmed the occurrence of exchange reactions

Furthermore, the diffusion kinetics of the active materials
substantially influenced the overall electrochemical perfor-
mance. To assess the diffusivity of the active components, cyclic
voltammetry (CV) was employed on Li||Li2S6, Li||DPDSe + Li2S6
and Li||DPDTe + Li2S6 cells. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, at a scan
rate of 50 mV s−1, the initial two pairs of redox characteristic
peaks of the Li2S6 cell merged into a single pair in the DPDTe +
Li2S6 and DPDSe + Li2S6 cells. This merging occurred due to the
reaction between Li2S6 and DPDTe/DPDSe. Notably, the DPDTe
+ Li2S6 and DPDSe + Li2S6 cells exhibited higher redox currents
than the Li2S6 cell, indicating enhanced electrochemical reac-
tivity of the LiPhTePSs and LiPhSePSs products. By utilizing the
renowned Randles–Sevcik equation [39], the peaks of the oxi-
dation and reduction processes were selected to determine the
diffusion coefficients. The DPDSe+Li2S6 and DPDTe + Li2S6 cells
demonstrated higher diffusion coefficients during the redox
process than the pristine Li2S6 cell (Fig. 2e, f). This enhancement
in mass transfer suggests potential factors for improving both
homogeneous and heterogeneous electrochemical reactions of
the sulfur-containing species [40].
The efficiency of the DPDSe and DPDTe promoters was
evaluated in Li-S full cells through galvanostatic discharge-
charge experiments at various rates. The obtained CNT/S cath-
ode retains the original 1D composite structure morphology
with a homogeneous distribution of sulfur throughout the
composite (Figs S2 and S3). As shown in Fig. 3a, the typical
charge/discharge curves displayed two distinct reduction pla-
teaus and a long oblique charge plateau, indicating the multi-
electron redox reactions. The cells with DPDSe and DPDTe
promoters (denoted as w/DPDSe and w/DPDTe cells) exhibited
maximum discharge capacities of 1416 and 1460 mAh g−1 at
0.2 C, respectively, and lower polarization than cells without
promoters (denoted as blank cells). Furthermore, regarding rate
performance as illustrated in Fig. 3b, the w/DPDTe and w/
DPDSe cells achieved capacities of 1401/1485, 1064/1243, 964/
1143, 913/1088, 896/1007, and 828/890 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5,
1, 2, and 4 C, respectively, significantly outperforming the blank
cells. The galvanostatic charge-discharge curves at various rates
revealed the source of the enhanced rate capability (Fig. 3c). The
DPDTe and DPDSe co-mediators notably extended the second
discharge plateaus at 0.1 C and reduced polarization at higher
rates to afford two-plateau discharge curves, consistent with
previous kinetic test results. Additionally, the capacities of each
discharge plateau (Qhigh and Qlow) were used as indices to eval-
uate the kinetic enhancement effectiveness of different pro-
moters for the liquid-solid and liquid-liquid transformation
processes [41–43] (Fig. S4). The cells with the DPDSe co-med-
iator showed the highest Qhigh of 310 mAh g−1, surpassing those
of the DPDTe and blank cells. Simultaneously, due to the pro-
moting effect of DPDTe on the liquid-solid transformation, the
cell with the DPDTe promoter provided the highest Qlow of
980 mAh g−1.

To develop a high-energy-density Li-S battery, it was crucial to
increase the sulfur areal loading while maintaining a stable
amount of DPDTe/DPDSe to study their comediating effect.
Similar to the low-sulfur-loading scenarios, the high-sulfur-

6.27 mAh cm−2 at 0.1 C. After 60 cycles, they retained high
capacities of 4.4 and 4.3 mAh cm−2, respectively, demonstrating
a significant enhancement in LiPSs conversion (Fig. 3g).

reaction of the stepwise reduction of sulfur with DPDSe and
DPDTe RMs, focusing on their unique roles in sulfur transfor-
mation. It is important to note that while previous reports have
independently highlighted the facilitative role of DPDSe and
DPDTe in LiPSs conversion, these findings do not contradict
our research, which primarily focuses on elucidating the dif-
ferences between these two RMs under comparable conditions.

Moreover,

To further validate the impact of DPDTe and DPDSe additives
on Li2S deposition, we performed potentiostatic discharge tests
using glassy carbon as the working electrode, lithium foil as the
counter electrode, and Li2S8, DPDSe + Li2S8, and DPDTe + Li2S8
solution as electrolytes. As illustrated in Fig. S11, the deposition
of Li2S on the glassy carbon electrode in the pure Li2S8 solution
was minimal and sparsely distributed. Conversely, in DPDSe +
Li2S8 and DPDTe + Li2S8 solution, Li2S deposition on the glassy
carbon electrode was significantly enhanced. This demonstrates
that the introduction of DPDSe and DPDTe facilitates Li2S
deposition. Moreover, the Li2S deposition in the DPDTe + Li2S8
solution was denser and more uniformly distributed than that in
the DPDSe + Li2S8 solution, indicating that DPDTe is more
effective in promoting Li2S deposition. Moreover, the corre-
sponding Li2S dissolution curves also confirm that the DPDSe/
DPDTe-mediated Li2S dissolution shows a shorter time for the
occurrence of oxidation current peak and higher Li2S dissolution
capacity than the intrinsic LiPSs mediators, suggesting a pre-
ferential dissolution of Li2S (Fig. 4g).
Raman spectroscopy with a 532 nm laser was utilized to
monitor the discharge process of w/DPDTe and w/DPDSe cells.
As illustrated in Fig. 4h, i, during the first stage of discharge
process, peaks at 156, 223, and 476 cm−1 for elemental sulfur
were observed at the open-circuit voltage for all cells. For the
w/DPDTe cell, these sulfur peaks remained stable until 2.04 V,
indicating strong assimilation of sulfur on DPDTe promoters
[46]. At the second plateau of 2.08 V, a new peak at 398 cm−1
emerged, attributed to Li2Sn. As the voltage dropped to the end
of the second plateau (~1.95 V), peaks at 448, 203, and 514 cm−1
appeared, signifying the transition from LiPSs to Li2S [47,48].
Notably, the Li2S peak was not detected even at 1.8 V, likely due
to the sluggish solid diffusion of Li ions and the signal is difficult
to be captured during in-situ measurement [49]. In comparison,
for the w/DPDSe cell, Raman results indicated a much faster
conversion from sulfur to soluble LiPSs, with the three char-
acteristic sulfur peaks decreasing at 2.20 V and disappearing
completely at 2.08 V. However, the Li2S peak was also hard to be
detected at 1.8 V, while peaks for LiPSs still maintained, which
suggested high concentrations of LiPSs in the electrolyte and
slow reduction to Li2S [50,51]. The contrast between Li-S bat-
teries with DPDSe and DPDTe promoters aligns with the above
findings, indicating that DPDSe promoters effectively catalyze
the sulfur-to-soluble LiPSs conversion and liquid-liquid con-
version between soluble LiPSs, while DPDTe promoters facilitate
the soluble LiPSs to Li2S transformation with a reduced energy
barrier. For the charge process (Fig. S12), the Li-S batteries with
the DPDTe/DPDSe promoter facilitate the fast and full con-
version of Li2S to soluble LiPSs and eventually back to sulfur as
the battery is charged to 2.6 V, completing a reaction cycle for
sulfur redox reaction.
To gain an in-depth understanding of the kinetic processes of
LiPhSePSs and LiPhTePSs over LiPSs, first-principles compu-
tations were performed on typical molecules including Li2S4–8,
LiPhSePS4–8 and LiPhTePS4–8 (Fig. S13). A strong intramolecular
interaction between lithium and the benzene ring was identified
in LiPhSeS6/LiPhTeS6 and LiPhSeS4/LiPhTeS4, while C–S inter-
actions were dominated in LiPhSeS8/LiPhTeS8 due to the longer
sulfur chain. To elucidate the redox properties of these mole-
cules, the energy levels of the lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital
(HOMO) were analyzed (Fig. 4j). The results indicate that
LiPhSePSs and LiPhTePSs have distinct HOMO and LUMO
levels compared to LiPSs with the same number of sulfur atoms,
suggesting different redox characteristics and mediation abilities
during the discharge and charge. Additionally, LiPhSePS6 and
LiPhSePS8 exhibited narrower band gaps than LiPhTePS6 and
LiPhTePS8, indicating higher activity for higher-order LiPh-
SePSs. In the low-order LiPSs stage, LiPhTePSs possess narrower
band gaps, implying better reaction kinetics than LiPhSePSs at
this stage. Thus, DPDSe coRMs exhibit a more rapid catalytic
conversion of long-chain sulfur species (Li2S8, Li2S6), while
DPDTe coRMs are particularly effective in catalyzing Li2S4 and
short-chain sulfur species (Li2S2, Li2S). Although these simula-
tion results primarily provide insights into thermodynamic
behavior, they offer a reference for further elucidations of
DPDSe/DPDTe coRMs and the mechanism of action of DPDSe/
DPDTe coRMs in Li-S batteries [52,53].
To eliminate the contribution of promoters, the capacities of
DPDTe and DPDSe electrolytes were evaluated using carbon
paper as a cathodic current collector (Fig. S14). The observed
discharge capacities for DPDTe and DPDSe were merely 4 and
5 mAh g−1, respectively, which are negligible in comparison to
conventional lithium-sulfur batteries. This limited capacity
underscores that the enhancement in the performance of
lithium-sulfur batteries mediated by DPDTe and DPDSe pri-
marily arises from kinetic facilitation rather than the presence of
additional active materials.
Regarding the catalytic effect on LiPSs conversion process,
DPDSe/DPDTe coRMs can be evenly distributed within Li-S
battery systems, thereby restricting the diffusion of LiPSs to
some extent. However, a single type of catalytic site can only
efficiently interact with certain intermediates and enhance spe-
cific steps of the tandem sulfur transformation. Consequently,
the catalytic influence on accelerating sulfur redox kinetics and
inhibiting LiPSs shuttling was inevitably disrupted. In contrast,
the precise utilization of mixed-coRMs can promote robust
chemisorption and accelerate multiple tandem reactions of
LiPSs, effectively restraining the LiPSs shuttling effect.

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have demonstrated the efficacy of tandem
catalysis using DPDSe and DPDTe coRMs in Li-S batteries. A
mixed-Se/Te promoter is strategically proposed to enhance the
tandem reactions of LiPSs, resulting in a high capacitive reten-
tion of 78% after 500 cycles at 1 C and high capacity of
6.6 mAh cm−2 at 0.2 C. Experimental tests and density func-
tional theory (DFT) simulations have confirmed that DPDSe
and DPDTe RMs alternatively catalyzed the transformation of
long-chain LiPSs (S8 ↔Li2S4) and short-chain LiPSs (Li2S4 ↔
Li2S), respectively. To significantly accelerate the catalytic con-
version of LiPSs, the concentration of the mixed-Se/Te promoter
was optimized to ensure that the two sequential reactions during
the conversion occurred primarily at active sites with excellent
catalytic performance, thereby eliminating the shuttle effect
caused by utilizing high-concentration coRMs and achieving
efficient tandem catalysis. Our research validates the use of
mixed organoditelluride and organodiselenide as kinetic pro-
moters to develop high-energy-density Li-S batteries, and it
provides significant insights into molecular design for kinetic
promoters targeting energy-related redox processes.
