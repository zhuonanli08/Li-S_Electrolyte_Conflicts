Lithium-Sulfurized Polyacrylonitrile Batteries Hot Paper
Constructing Robust Electrode/Electrolyte Interphases for Highly
Stable Lithium–Sulfurized Polyacrylonitrile Batteries
Siyuan Shao+, Jinze Hou+, Youxuan Ni, Chunyan Zhu, Shuang Wu, Xinyi Liu, Can Wang,
Kai Zhang, Zhenhua Yan, Yong Lu,* and Jun Chen*
Abstract: Lithium–sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN)
batteries show great promise for energy storage, but are
plagued by poor cycling stability, which can be attributed
to unfavorable electrode/electrolyte interphases for both
anode and cathode. Here we design and achieve the
anion-moderate solvation structure based interconnected
clusters in electrolyte by regulating the interactions
of solvent with Li+ and diluent to construct robust
anode/electrolyte and cathode/electrolyte interphases
simultaneously for high-performance Li–SPAN batteries.
The optimal electrolyte endows Li plating/stripping with
a high Coulombic efﬁciency of 99.47% at 1 mA cm−2
in Li||Cu cells. The Li–SPAN batteries show excellent
cycling stability with a high capacity retention of
94.21% after 1215 cycles. Moreover, the assembled
pouch-type Li–SPAN battery under limited electrolyte
condition (2.40 g Ah−1) achieves a capacity of 3.75 Ah,
corresponding to a high energy density of 180 Wh kg−1
based on the total mass of the battery. This work provides
a good electrolyte design principle to construct robust
anode/electrolyte and cathode/electrolyte interphases for
batteries.
Introduction
Lithium metal batteries have garnered extensive attentions
in recent years owing to the high theoretical speciﬁc capacity
(3860 mAh g−1) and low redox potential (−3.04 V vs standard
hydrogen electrode) of Li metal anode.[1–5] For cathode
materials, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), which consists
of abundant elements of C, H, O, N, and S, is one of the most
promising cathode materials for Li metal batteries due to its
high theoretical speciﬁc capacity (>500 mAh g−1) and the

absence of a need for transition metal elements.[6–8] Although
SPAN is free of transition metal elements, it still exhibits
good intrinsic electronic conductivity thanks to its highly
conjugated structure. In addition, the preparation of SPAN
can be simply achieved by the solid-state reaction between
polyacrylonitrile and sulfur.[8–9] Thus, it is of great signiﬁcance
to develop high-performance Li–SPAN batteries.
Despite Li–SPAN batteries show various advantages, they
still suffer from two main obstacles. For the SPAN cathode,
the generated polysulﬁdes during cycles will dissolve in
electrolytes, leading to serious shuttle effects and followed
by capacity decay. As for the Li metal anode, the problems
are low Coulombic efﬁciency (CE) and dendrite growth.[10]
Electrolyte optimization is regarded as a promising solution to
solve these problems.[11–16] Traditional ether solvents, such as
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), show good compatibility with
Li metal anode, but cannot avoid the dissolution and shuttling
of polysulﬁdes.[17–19] As for ester solvents, such as ethylene
carbonate (EC), they show much better compatibility with
SPAN cathode owing to the favorable electrode/electrolyte
interphases.[12,20] However, the parasitic reactions between
ester electrolytes and Li metal anode inevitably lead to
unfavorable anode/electrolyte interphase (SEI) and poor
performance of Li–SPAN batteries.[21–24] In addition, molec-
ular engineering,[21,25] such as the introduction of ﬂuorinated
segments in carboxylic esters, can effectively enhance the
compatibility with Li metal anode. Unfortunately, it poses
difﬁculties in forming a stable cathode/electrolyte interphase
(CEI), and its low ionic conductivity is unfavorable for fast
charging. Another method is the introduction of electrolyte
additives, such as LiNO3, which can form favorable SEI
or CEI by sacriﬁcing themselves.[26] Nevertheless, the addi-
tives would be depleted during repeated cycles, leading to
unsatisfactory cycle life. Overall, although much progress
has been made, previous works still have difﬁculties in well
solving the anode and cathode problems simultaneously.
Constructing robust SEI and CEI simultaneously is expected
to be effective to solve the problems and achieve high-
performance Li–SPAN batteries, but it is challenging and still
not realized.
Herein, we report that regulating the Li+–solvent and
solvent–diluent interactions can realize the anion-moderate
solvation structure based interconnected clusters in elec-
trolyte, which are beneﬁcial to construct robust both SEI and
CEI simultaneously for high-performance Li–SPAN batteries.

bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), ﬂuoroethylene carbonate

Results and Discussion
The electrolyte design strategy for Li–SPAN batteries can be
seen in Figure 1, which illustrates three different electrolyte
structures by regulating the interactions of solvent with Li+
and diluent, along with the corresponding characteristics of
electrode/electrolyte interphases. As shown in Figure 1a, at
low TTE ratios, the interactions between Li+ and solvent
dominate, promoting the formation of anion-insufﬁcient
solvation structure based integrated salt-solvent clusters in
electrolyte. This structure shows poor compatibility with Li
metal anode, leading to the formation of undesirable SEI.
In contrast, at high TTE ratios, the interactions between
solvent and TTE diluent dominate, which leads to anion-
rich solvation structure. Meanwhile, from the view of larger
size, the salt-solvent clusters will be separated completely
by the large amounts of TTE diluent, resulting in anion-
rich solvation structure based isolated clusters in electrolyte.
Although this type of electrolyte exhibits better compatibility
toward Li metal anode, the unstable CEI still make it
difﬁcult to achieve high-performance Li–SPAN batteries
(Figure 1c). Thus, we aim to achieve a balance between
Li+–solvent and solvent–diluent interactions to form anion-
moderate solvation structure based interconnected clusters in
electrolyte, which are expected to construct uniform CEI and
robust SEI simultaneously for high-performance Li–SPAN
batteries (Figure 1b). To achieve this structure of electrolyte,
we conducted a series of optimization to regulate Li+–solvent
and solvent–diluent interactions in the following sections.
At ﬁrst, in addition to the common linear ester solvent
DMC, we chose FEC rather than EC as the cyclic ester
solvent in our system (Figures S1–S3). Then, we optimized
the LiFSI ratio by altering the ratio of solvent (FEC-DMC)
and meanwhile ﬁxing the ratio of TTE diluent. Speciﬁcally,
LiFSI, FEC-DMC (FD), and TTE were mixed in a molar
ratio of 1:9.14:3.5 (where the volume ratio of FEC to DMC
is 1:1) to produce the electrolyte, termed as Li-9.14FDT
(where the LiFSI concentration is 1.4 mol L−1). Similarly,
when the ratio of FD is changed to 4.92 and 3.38, LiFSI
concentrations in the resulting electrolytes are 2.6 and
3.8 mol L−1, respectively. We named them as Li-4.92FDT (a
molar ratio of 1:4.92:3.5) and Li-3.38FDT (a molar ratio of
1:3.38:3.5), respectively. Electrolytes without the addition of

Different electrolytes were characterized by Raman spec-
troscopy, and peak ﬁtting was conducted for the S─N─S
stretching vibration peak of FSI−(Figures 2b and S4). Based
on the extent of interactions between the cations and anions
of the salt, these structures are categorized into free FSI−
ions without Li+ coordination, contact ion pairs (CIPs)
with one FSI−coordinated to one Li+, aggregate ion pairs
(AGG-I) with one FSI−coordinated to two Li+, and more
strongly coordinated aggregate ion pairs (AGG-II) with one
FSI−coordinated to more than two Li+.[27–28] It is observed
that as the LiFSI ratio increases, the predominant struc-
ture transitions from CIP to AGG-II, indicating a stronger
interaction between Li+ and FSI−.

LiFSI ratio electrolytes. As displayed in Figure S6, the battery
with Li-3.38FDT shows the highest charge transfer resistance

electrode/electrolyte interface. In contrast, both Li-4.92FDT

After optimizing LiFSI ratios, we ﬁxed the LiFSI ratio
to explore the effects of TTE diluent ratio on interfacial
behavior and battery performance. Speciﬁcally, LiFSI, FEC-
DMC (FD), and TTE diluent were mixed in a molar ratio
of 1:4.92:x (with FEC and DMC in a volume ratio of 1:1) to
obtain the electrolytes, designated as FDT-xT. The ternary
phase diagram of LiFSI, FD, and TTE is shown in Figure 2d.
Numbers 2–9 represent eight samples with progressively
increasing diluent ratios in a large range.
We used infrared (IR) spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy,
and NMR to study the effects of varying diluent ratios on the
solvation structure. When the TTE ratio increases from 0.1
to 3.27, the C═O vibration peaks of FEC in IR spectra shift
from 1831 and 1804 cm−1 to 1834 and 1805 cm−1, respectively
(Figures 2e and S12). In Raman spectra, the vibration peak of
FEC at 870.2 cm−1 red-shifts to 868.8 cm−1 with increasing
TTE ratio. IR and Raman results both indicate that the
interactions between FD and TTE strengthen.[32–33] TTE pulls
free FD and some solvent molecules coordinated with Li+
ions from the inner solvation shell to the outer layer, allowing
more FSI−ions to enter the inner solvation shell of Li+
ions.[29,33] Li+–solvent interactions decreases in the process
and a balance between Li+–solvent and solvent–diluent
interactions is gradually built.
When the TTE ratio exceeds 3.27, the C═O vibration
peaks of DMC and FEC in IR spectra keep nearly unchanged,
and the vibration peak of FEC in Raman spectra stabilized
at 868.8 cm−1 (Figure 2e). The IR and Raman results
all suggest that the interactions between TTE and FD
approach saturation near FDT-3.27T, indicating that the
interactions between solvent (FD) and diluent (TTE) begin
to dominate. To eliminate the inﬂuence of lithium salt,
FD and TTE were mixed into a solution without LiFSI
under the same conditions for IR and Raman tests (Figure
S13). The results show a trend of shift initially followed
by stability in the corresponding vibration peaks of solvent
molecules as the TTE ratio increases, indicating the interac-
tions between solvent and diluent will approach saturation.
This result is similar to that of electrolytes containing LiFSI
salt.
Then, we further investigated the viscosity and ionic
conductivity (σ) of electrolytes with different TTE ratios. In
order to avoid the inﬂuence of Li+ ion concentration change
on the conductivity, we used molar ionic conductivity (m)
instead of σ. Note that we used the LiFSI concentration
(cLi+) to represent the corresponding different TTE ratios
in Figure 2f because cLi+ is based on the total volume of
diluent (TTE) and solvent. In the absence of TTE, cLi+ is
2.6 M (x = 0), and the electrolyte viscosity is 14.5 mPa·s. As
low-viscosity TTE is added, the viscosity of the electrolyte
rapidly decreases, which leads to an increase in m. When the
TTE ratio increases to cLi+ = 2 M (x = 0.76), the viscosity
curve exhibits a change in slope, and the increasing trend of
m gradually diminishes. With further addition of TTE, m
reaches a maximum value of 3.21 × 10−4 S m2 mol−1 before
slowly decreasing, and the viscosity of the solution uniformly
decreases. When cLi+<1.27 M (x = 2.74), there is a signiﬁcant
decline of the m curve.
The previous work manifests that the sudden changes
in ion conductivity reﬂect the structural transformation of
clusters in electrolyte.[34] In our system, a “critical point” in
m is identiﬁed at cLi+ = 1.27 M (Figure 2f), below which m
exhibits a signiﬁcant decrease. This is because as the TTE ratio
increases, most salt-solvent clusters are gradually separated
and ﬁnally surrounded by TTE to form isolated clusters.
Thus, Li+ ions need to transport between these clusters
and TTE, leading to a rapid decline in m. Interestingly,
as mentioned above, IR and Raman results show that the
FDT-3.27T electrolyte begins to show saturated interactions
between diluent and solvent (Figure 2e), where the TTE ratio
of FDT-3.27T electrolyte roughly corresponds to the “critical
point” in Figure 2f. This indicates that the variations of Li+–
solvent and solvent–diluent interactions in solvation structure
are roughly consistent with the change of cluster structure.
Thus, combining the m change trend of FDT-xT electrolytes
and the previous studies[34–35] about cluster structures, as well
as the aforementioned IR and Raman characterizations, we
can broadly infer that the cluster structure of electrolytes
could be roughly categorized into the following three types
with the increasing TTE ratios, including integrated clusters
(cLi+ = 2.6–2 M), interconnected clusters (cLi+ = 2–1.27 M),
and isolated clusters (cLi+< 1.27 M).
In order to achieve a further comprehension of the
characteristics of solvation structures in different clusters,
we selected one sample from each of the aforementioned
three regions: FDT-0.1T, FDT-2.3T, and FDT-5.22T. First,
they were characterized by Raman (Figure 2g), and peak
ﬁtting was conducted for the S─N─S stretching vibration
peak of FSI−(Figure 2h). With a low TTE ratio, dominated
Li+–solvent interactions promote a certain amount of free
FSI−ions formed in FDT-0.1T, resulting in anion-insufﬁcient
solvation structure. With the gradually increasing TTE ratio,
more CIP and AGG-I structures were found in FDT-2.3T,
indicating that more anions participate in inner solvation
shell because of the intensiﬁed solvent–diluent interactions.
This results in the formation of the anion-moderate solvation
structure. With further increasing TTE ratio, solvent–diluent
interactions begin to dominate. This promotes more FD
solvents moving away from inner solvation shell, leading to
the signiﬁcant increase of AGG-II structures in FDT-5.22T
(Figure 2h), and ﬁnally achieving the anion-rich solvation
structure. These results are roughly consistent with the 17O
NMR spectra (Figure 2i), where the chemical shift of O in
FEC moves towards high ﬁeld owing to the increasing TTE
ratio from FDT-2.3T to FDT-5.22T. To exclude the inﬂuence
of lithium salt, the solutions were prepared using only FD and
TTE, and the corresponding 17O NMR spectra yield similar
results (Figure S14).

After revealing the structure of different electrolytes,
we studied the corresponding interfacial chemistry because
the electrode/electrolyte interphases play a crucial role in
enhancing the cycling performance of batteries.[36–37] We ﬁrst
investigated the interphase formation behavior on the SPAN
cathode in electrolytes with different structures. The cycled
SPAN cathodes were observed by the transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) to analyze the morphology of the CEI.
As shown in Figure 4a, thick and inhomogeneous CEI layer
formed on cycled SPAN cathode in FDT-0.1T electrolyte,
which attributes to the decomposition of solvents in anion-
insufﬁcient solvation structure. In contrast, for the FDT-2.3T
electrolyte, a uniform and dense CEI layer (9.34–9.45 nm)
formed on the cycled SPAN. However, with the further
increase in TTE ratio, the thickness of the CEI layer gradually
decreases and becomes uneven and discontinuous, indicating
that it is difﬁcult for anions in isolated clusters which are
gradually surrounded by TTE to participate in interfacial
reactions.
Furthermore, the cycled SPAN cathodes in different
electrolytes were characterized by XPS. It is observed that the
cycled SPAN in FDT-2.3T electrolyte shows a higher content
of inorganic ﬂuorides and a lower atomic ratio of C in CEI
(Figure S18). Moreover, the atomic ratio of C in the CEI
decreases synchronously with etching time (Figure 4b), while
the atomic ratio of F increases correspondingly (Figure 4c).

the atomic ratio of C in the SPAN cathode cycled in FDT-
5.22T decreases slightly with etching time, it still remains
much higher than that in FDT-2.3T, indicating that in addition
to inorganic substances, a relatively large amount of organic
components exists in CEI (Figure 4b). The relatively strong
peaks of C─F (688.1 eV) in F 1s spectra of SPAN cathode
cycled in FDT-5.22T also prove this structure (Figure 4d).
Moreover, peaks of polythiosulfate in S 2p spectra indicate
severe parasitic reactions between FDT-5.22T electrolyte
and polysulﬁdes (Figure 4e), proving the formation of the
crackable and unstable CEI, characterized by a thin and
discontinuous layer with organic–organic mixed components.
Different CEI structures profoundly affect the dissolution
behavior of SPAN cathode during cycles. With the cycled
SPAN at the 500th cycle characterized by in situ ultraviolet–
visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy (Figure S19), the FDT-2.3T
sample has no obvious absorption peak, which further
supports that its dense gradient organic-inorganic CEI
effectively inhibits the dissolution of polysulﬁdes. In situ
Raman tests were performed on the SPAN at the 2nd and
501st cycles (Figure S20) in FDT-0.1T. After 500 cycles, SPAN
shows only a faint S─S bond signal, implying signiﬁcant loss
of SPAN active materials. Due to the superior interfacial
stability in FDT-2.3T, the cycled SPAN still exhibits a distinct
S─S stretching vibration peak after 500 cycles (Figure 4f),
indicating stable electrochemical reactions occurring on the
SPAN.[40–41] In contrast, the S─S bond strength of SPAN
cycled in FDT-5.22T after 500 cycles signiﬁcantly weakens
(Figure 4g). Additionally, EIS test indicates that the battery
with FDT-2.3T electrolyte shows the smallest Rct after 500
cycles (Figure S21), reﬂecting a fast interfacial kinetics.
In addition to the CEI on the SPAN cathode, we then
study the compatibility between Li metal anode and different
electrolytes. The Li||Li symmetric cell reﬂects the interfacial
behavior of Li metal anode with electrolytes featuring differ-
ent solvation structures (Figure 5a).[4] The Li||Li symmetric
cell in FDT-2.3T electrolyte demonstrates stable cycling for
over 2900 h (1455 h in FDT-0.1T and 1995 h in FDT-
5.22T), with the corresponding overpotential signiﬁcantly
lower than that of the other two systems. Subsequently,
Li||Cu cells were assembled and tested using the Aurbach
method.[42–43] The FDT-2.3T electrolyte with anion-moderate
solvation structure based interconnected clusters endows Li
plating/stripping with a high CE of 99.47% at 1 mA cm−2,
which is higher than that in FDT-0.1T (97.12%) and FDT-
5.22T (98.48%) electrolytes (Figure 5b). Compared with
the representative recent reports, the cycling performance
of the Li||Li cell and CE of the Li||Cu cell are superior
(Figure 5c). Moreover, the Li||Cu asymmetric cell with FDT-
2.3T electrolyte is able to stably run over 360 cycles without
obvious ﬂuctuations and the average CE is 96.89% during the
10th–364th cycles under the conditions of 1 mA cm−2 and
1 mAh cm−2 (Figure 5d,e).
Then, we characterized the cycled Li metal anode in dif-
ferent electrolytes. In FDT-0.1T and FDT-5.22T electrolyte,
the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images show that
the cycled Li metal anodes exhibit a distinct phenomenon of
dendritic growth and dead Li accumulation (Figure 5f). The
phenomenon in FDT-0.1T is similar to that in classical ester
electrolytes, which show poor compatibility with Li metal
anode. In contrast, the Li metal anode cycled in FDT-2.3T
electrolyte displays a more uniform and denser morphology,
which can be attributed to the anion-moderate solvation
structure based interconnected clusters in this electrolyte.
Additionally, the SEI layers formed on Li metal anodes
cycled in different electrolytes were characterized by XPS.
The detailed surface elemental contents and F 1s XPS spectra
for the cycled Li metal anode are shown in Figures S22 and
S23. It is observed that the SEI in FDT-2.3T electrolyte shows
the highest content of inorganic ﬂuorides among the three
samples. In the F 1s spectra, SEI with FDT-2.3T was found
to have the strongest LiF (684.8 eV) peak among the three
types of electrolytes.[44] The more abundant LiF contributes
to a stable and robust SEI on Li metal anode in FDT-2.3T
electrolyte with anion-moderate solvation structure based
interconnected clusters. This type of SEI can effectively
suppress dendrite growth and promote long-life Li metal
anode.
After revealing the CEI and SEI structures, we evaluated
the electrochemical performance of Li–SPAN batteries in
different electrolytes. According to the linear sweep voltam-
metry test (Figure S24), the three electrolytes meet the
operating requirement of Li–SPAN batteries. As shown in
Figures 6a and S25, the Li–SPAN battery with FDT-2.3T elec-
trolyte exhibits signiﬁcantly enhanced cycling performance
with a high capacity retention of 94.21% after 1215 cycles
and an average CE of 99.95% at a current density of 3 C
(1 C = 550 mA g−1). The superior cycling performance can
be attributed to the simultaneously stabilized SPAN cathode
and Li anode. In contrast, the Li–SPAN batteries with FDT-
0.1T and FDT-5.22T electrolytes exhibit much inferior cycling
performance. Moreover, the Li–SPAN battery with FDT-
2.3T electrolyte also shows good rate performance, delivering
92.09% of the capacity (1 C) at 5 C (Figure S26).
Moreover, we investigated the effects of electrolytes with
different ratios of LiFSI, FD, and TTE on the performance of
Li–SPAN batteries, which are 29 types of electrolytes in all.

of each sample point on the ternary phase diagram (molar
ratio are detailed in Table S1). The capacity retentions after

To demonstrate the practical application prospects of
the optimal electrolyte, we further assembled Li–SPAN full
batteries under practical conditions with a limited Li foil
and a lean electrolyte. As shown in Figure 6c, the batteries
with a mass loading of SPAN of 4.28 mg cm−2, a 50 µm
Li foil, and 20 µL FDT-0.1T (or FDT-5.22T) electrolytes,
begin to exhibit rapid capacity decay after 40 (or 97) cycles
at 0.2 C. The primary reasons are unstable CEI and low
reversibility of Li metal cycling.[14,45] In contrast, the full
battery using FDT-2.3T electrolyte under the same conditions
maintains a capacity retention of 96.13% after 185 cycles
(Figures 6c and S29). Moreover, the full battery with a mass
loading of SPAN of 2.45 mg cm−2 and 25 µL FDT-2.3T
electrolyte can operate stably over 280 cycles (Figure S30).
In addition, the full battery with a mass loading of SPAN of
4.10 mg cm−2 and 20 µL FDT-2.3T electrolyte also exhibits
good rate performance with 87.39% of the capacity (0.1 C)
at 1 C (Figures 6d and S31), which could be attributed to
the interconnected clusters based network for Li+ transport
in FDT-2.3T electrolyte. Meanwhile, the existence of TTE
reduces the viscosity of the electrolyte, which is also helpful
for rate performance. It is noteworthy that the Li–SPAN
full battery under more strict conditions, that is, a low
negative/positive (N/P) ratio of 1.81 and a electrolyte usage
of 5.54 g Ah−1, can retain approximately 100% capacity after
55 cycles at 0.2 C (Figure S32).

Conclusion
In summary, we have designed and realized the anion-
moderate solvation structure based interconnected clus-
ters in the electrolyte composed of salt (LiFSI), solvent
(FEC+DMC), and diluent (TTE) with a molar ratio of
1:4.92:2.3. Various characterizations indicate that robust SEI
and CEI were built simultaneously in Li–SPAN batteries.
Thus, the Li plating/stripping exhibits a high CE of 99.47%
at 1 mA cm−2 in Li||Cu cells. The Li–SPAN batteries show
excellent cycling performance with a high capacity retention
of 94.21% over 1215 cycles. Moreover, the assembled pouch-
type Li–SPAN battery under limited electrolyte condition
(2.40 g Ah−1) achieves a capacity of 3.75 Ah, corresponding
to a high energy density of 180 Wh kg−1 based on the
total mass of the battery at 0.1 C. Our work paves a
way to realize practical applications of Li–SPAN batteries
through electrolyte structure regulation from the point of
electrode/electrolyte interface.
