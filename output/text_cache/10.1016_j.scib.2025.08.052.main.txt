High energy density and long cycle life are considered to be incompatible in battery design. Lithium metal 
batteries often have high energy density but poor cycle stability, while graphite (Gr)-based batteries usu-
ally have long cycle life but are limited in energy density. Therefore, mixing lithium with Gr as the anode 
is expected to balance high energy density and long cycle life. Reducing the negative/positive areal capac-
ity (N/P) ratio to less than 1 is the simplest means to achieve a hybrid anode. The battery with sulfurized 
poly(acrylonitrile) (SPAN) as the cathode and an ultra-low N/P ratio (N/P = 0.6) is expected to leverage the
signiﬁcant advantage of its cathode’s energy density far exceeding that of traditional cathodes, while
maintaining stable cycling performance. This makes it a highly promising battery system. Through the
design of anion-mediated electrolyte engineering, the capacity retention rate of the SPAN||Gr pouch cell
at N/P = 0.6 after 300 cycles was 92%, and its energy density was increased by 24 % compared with that at
N/P = 1.1, achieving a balance between energy density and cycling stability. This strategy establishes N/P
engineering coupled with electrolyte design as a scalable paradigm for next-generation energy-dense
batteries.

1. Introductio n
The striking advancement of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has 
enabled their extensive adoption in a wide range of portable elec-
tronic devices. Nevertheless, for electric vehicles, the demands for
lower cost, more exceptional energy density, and longer cycling life
are becoming increasingly critical [1–3]. The graphite (Gr) anodes 
dominate commercial LIBs on account of exceptional cycling stabil-
ity, while their low speciﬁc capacity (372 mAh g−1) fundamentally
limits the energy density of LIBs (＜300 Wh kg−1) [4–9]. Lithium 
metal anodes, with a theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 3860 mAh g− 1,
represent a promising alternative for high energy density batteries
[10,11]. However, due to severe dendrite formation and interfacial 
instability, the cycle life of Ah-level pouch cells with lithium metal
anodes typically fails to exceed 100 cycles [12–15]. It is evident
that materializing a balance between high energy density and long 
cycle life continues to pose a noticeable challenge in current
research.

Employing a hybrid anode with Gr and lithium metal is antici-
pated to break this impasse. Latest breakthroughs in lithium metal 
stabilization, encompassing anode structuring, electrolyte engi-
neering, and artiﬁcial interphases, have laid critical foundations
for hybrid strategies that partially replace Gr with lithium [16– 
23]. Two hybrid strategies dominate: (i) by employing Gr-
encapsulated lithium metal hybrid anodes and (ii) reducing the 
negative to positive capacity (N/P) ratio (where ‘‘N” denotes the
areal capacity of the Gr anode) [24–29]. Among these hybrid 
strategies, reducing the N/P ratio is considered the simplest and 
most feasible approach. By intentionally decreasing the amount 
of Gr to N/P < 1, lithium deposition compensates for the Gr capacity 
deﬁcit while residual Gr preserves cycling stability. This dual
mechanism synergistically heightens energy density and retains
a long cycle life for LIBs. Nonetheless, the efﬁcacy of reducing the
N/P ratio signiﬁcantly hinges on the cathode’s intrinsic energy den-

sity. The energy density of traditional cathode materials such as 
LiFePO4 (LFP) and LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NCM811) is limited; thus, 
reducing the N/P ratio exhibits a constrained impact on elevating
the energy density of the LIBs.

Sulfur cathodes, which immensely exceed the energy density of 
traditional cathodes, present a transformative opportunity when
their cycling stability can be reconciled with high energy density
[30–33]. Sulfurized poly(acrylonitrile) (SPAN), a sulfur-based cath-
ode with promising prospects for long-term operation, has shown
remarkable cycling stability within the SPAN||Gr batteries [34]. The 
theoretical energy density of the SPAN cathode exceeds that of 
both the LFP cathode and the NCM811 cathode. Reducing the N/P
ratio in SPAN||Gr batteries may offer noticeable potential for ele-
vating the energy density of sulfur-based LIBs. Fig. 1a and Tables 
S1–S3 (online) present a comparison of the mass proportion of 
each component in the batteries of three battery systems: LFP|| 
Gr, NCM811||Gr, and SPAN||Gr. It should be noted that SPAN is lithi-
ated; on that account, its mass includes the mass of the required 
lithium. The SPAN||Gr pouch cells exhibit inverted mass propor-
tions versus traditional LIBs: the Gr anode dominates (40 %) over
the SPAN cathode and electrolyte. This inversion stems from
SPAN’s superior speciﬁc capacity over Gr, necessitating Gr mass
upscaling to match capacities. This phenomenon is likely to be
prevalent in the majority of high-capacity sulfur-based cathode
LIBs. As depicted in Fig. 1b and Table S4 (online), as the N/P ratio 
decreases from 1.1 to 0, the SPAN||Gr system demonstrat es the
most pronounced enhancement. Below an N/P ratio of 0.6, the
energy density of the SPAN||Gr battery system becomes the high-
est, and the mass of SPAN surpasses that of Gr (Fig. 1c and 
Table S5 online). For this reason, it is evident that when the N/P 
ratio is reduced, the SPAN||Gr battery system demonstrates a more 
remarkable propensity to fully leverage the high energy density
advantage of the cathode material. As such, this conﬁguration rep-
resents a battery system with enormous promise for future
applications.

Herein, a SPAN||Gr battery system with an N/P ratio of 0.6 was
meticulously designed and constructed (Fig. 1d). To address the 
issues of intensiﬁed lithium deposition non-uniformity and side 
reactions caused by the reduction of the N/P ratio, an anion-
mediated electrolyte system was adopted, which was inspired by 
the related research on lithium metal stabilization. By promoting 
preferential anion coordination in lithium ions (Li+ ) solvation 
structures, this electrolyte induces inorganic-rich interphases. 
The anion-enriched environment simultaneously strikingly height-
ens electrochemical dynamics and stabilizes the electrode inter-
face to suppress side reactions. Apart from that, it promoted the 
uniform deposition of lithium metal onto the Gr surface. The opti-
mized SPAN||Gr pouch cell (N/P = 0.6) achieves 92 % capacity reten-
tion over 300 cycles, which is consistent with conventional cells
(N/P = 1.1). Additionally, the pouch cell achieves a substantial
24 % increase in energy density compared to the conventional cells.
This work establishes N/P engineering as a scalable paradigm to
balance high energy density and long cycle life for sulfur-based
LIBs. The anion-mediated electrolyte engineering employed is

highly compatible with this battery system, ensuring optimal per-
formance and stability, which accelerates the deployment of
sulfur-based batteries for next-generation energy storage.

2. Experimen tal
2.1. Materials 
All reagents were commercial products and were used directly 
without further puriﬁcation. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) with a 
molecular weight (Mw) of 150,000 g mol−1 was procured from 
Sigma-Aldrich. Sulfur was obtained from Aladdin. The commercial 
carbonated ester-based electrolyte LB-015 (1 mol L−1 LiPF6 in EC: 
DEC = 1:1 vol% with 5% FEC), denoted as LB, the commercial 
ether-based electrolyte LS-009 (1 mol L−1 LiTFSI in DME: 
DOL = 1:1 vol% with 2 wt% LiNO3), denoted as LS, and the localized
high-concentration electrolyte (LiFSI:DME:HFE in a molar ratio of
5.0:4.9:5.8), denoted as LH. All electrolytes were purchased from
DodoChem. Thin lithium foil (20 lm) and the 50 lm lithium foil
were sourced from China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. The Gr anode
was supplied by Calb Technology Co., Ltd. The polypropylene (PP)
separator (25 lm) was acquired from Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.
2.2. Preparation of the SPAN materials
SPAN materials were synthesized through the thermal treat-
ment of a sulfur and PAN mixture at a mass ratio of 2:1, heated 
to 350 °C for 6 h under an argon atmosphere. Subsequently, to 
eliminate residual excess sulfur, the resultant sample was further
subjected to thermal treatment at 300 °C for an additional 6 h
under the same inert atmosphere. The sulfur content in the ﬁnal
product was determined to range between 50 and 55 wt%.
2.3. Characterizatio n
The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured by employ-
ing a PANalytical X’pert PRO-DY2198 diffractometer (Holland) 
with Cu Ka radiation (k = 0.15406 nm). X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) data were obtained by adopting an AXIS-ULTRA 
DLD-600W spectrometer with Al Ka radiation. The scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) analysis was performed on an FEI Nova 
NanoSEM450 microscope operated at 10 kV. The in-situ electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were recorded with 
a frequency ranging from 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz. The linear sweep 
voltammetry (LSV) data were collected on an electrochemical 
workstation (CHI660E). The in-situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) data 
were collected on an ultraviolet and visible spectrophotometer
(Lambda 35). Raman spectra were collected using a confocal
Raman microscope (LabRAM HR800) with a 532 nm excitation
from an argon-ion laser. The ultra-depth three-dimensional micro-
scope (UTM) test was conducted using the EasyZoom5 3D UTM
system, and the Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) test was carried
out with the JAMP-9510F AES system. The Differential capacity-
potential plots, Coulomb efﬁciency (CE) tests, and all cycling per-
formance tests of the batteries were conducted using the LAND
(CT3002A) system and the NEWARE Battery Test System (MIHW-
200-160CH, Shenzhen, China).
2.4. Cell assembly
All testing CR2032-type coin cells were assembled in an open-
air environment. The SPAN cathode slurry was prepared by mixing 
SPAN powder, LA133 binder, and Super P at a mass ratio of 8:1:1
(the content of SPAN denoted as RSPAN, is 0.8) or 9:0.5:0.5
(RSPAN = 0.9), followed by drying at 70 °C in a vacuum oven. The
diameter of the cathode was 10 mm, the diameter of the lithium 
foil was 12 mm, and that of the anode was 12 mm. For the 
single-layer pouch cell, the dimensions for the SPAN cathode were 
3.0 × 3.0 cm2 , the thin lithium foil was 3.5 × 3.5 cm2 , and the Gr 
anode was also 3.5 × 3.5 cm2 . The 20 lm ultra-thin lithium foil 
is attached to the surface of the SPAN cathode, then the separator 
is placed, followed by the Gr anode, the electrolyte is injected, and 
ﬁnally the battery is encapsulated. After the electrolyte is injected 
into the battery, due to the potential difference, the SPAN cathode 
will automatically undergo lithiumization. For the Ah-level SPAN|| 
Gr pouch cell, both the cathodes and anodes are coated on both 
sides. The SPAN cathode (8.0 × 6.0 cm2 ), PP separator, lithium foil 
(8.0 × 6.0 cm2 ), and Gr anode (8.2 × 6.2 cm2) were assembled
accordingly. Lithium foil is uniformly attached to the cathodes
for in-situ lithiation. In the case of the 1 Ah pouch cell, it consists
of 5 layers of the cathode and 6 layers of the anode. Regarding
the 2 Ah pouch cell, it is composed of 7 layers of the cathode and
8 layers of the anode. For the 1 Ah SPAN||Li pouch cell, the thick-
ness of the lithium foil is 50 lm. The mass loading of all SPAN cath-
odes was 4.3 mg cm−2. The pouch cell assemblies were performed
in an argon-ﬁlled glove box. The cut-off voltage window is set
between 1.0 and 3.0 V. The calculation of speciﬁc capacity is based
on the total mass of SPAN, with 1 C deﬁned as 1000 mA g−1.

2.5. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculation method
Quantum chemistry calculations were carried out to investigate 
the reduction and solvation capabilities of various molecular spe-
cies. DFT was employed, with the Becke, 3-parameter, Lee-Yang-
Parr hybrid functional (B3LYP) selected for the optimization of 
electrolyte components. Initially, the 6-31G (d,p) basis set was 
used to optimize the molecular geometries of PF6 
− , EC, FEC, DEC,
FSI−, HFE, and DME solvent molecules. Subsequently, the 6-311+
+G (d,p) basis set was applied for vibrational frequency calcula-
tions of these molecules. To enhance the accuracy of all computa-
tional steps, the DFT-D3 dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson
(BJ) damping was incorporated.
2.6. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
MD simulations were conducted to replicate the electrolyte sol-
vation environment as realistically as possible. Two types of elec-
trolyte models were constructed: one consisting of 100 LiFSI/98 
DME/116 HFE, and the other composed of 27 LiPF6/106 EC/200 
DEC/17 FEC. All atomic charges and force ﬁeld parameters were 
assigned based on the COMPASS III force ﬁeld. Each initial model 
underwent the following simulation procedures. First, energy min-
imization was carried out using the smart minimizer algorithm, 
with convergence criteria set to a maximum energy deviation of 
less than 0.0001 kcal/mol. Subsequently, a ﬁve-step annealing pro-
cess was performed under an NVT ensemble (T = 298–598 K) to
enhance conﬁgurational sampling, followed by an NPT ensemble
simulation at T = 298 K and P = 1.01 × 105 Pa for 50 ps to further
optimize the system’s energy and structural conﬁguration. Equilib-
rium was considered to be actualized when both energy and tem-
perature ﬂuctuations were within ±5 % over the ﬁnal simulation
period.

5.2 mg cm−2 . By employing a straightforward in-situ pre-lithiation 
approach, an ultrathin lithium ﬁlm is attached to the surface of the
SPAN cathode throughout battery assembly and becomes lithiated
upon the addition of the electrolyte [34]. To ensure that the battery 
has sufﬁcient lithium, the 20 lm ultra-thin lithium foil was used
for pre-lithiation of the electrodes. According to the calculation
in Table S6 (online), lithium foil can provide a capacity of approx-
imately 4.09 mAh cm−2 , while the battery requires a capacity of 
4.08 mAh cm−2 . This indicates that lithium is sufﬁcient. As 
N/P = 0.6, when the battery is charged to 60 % state of charge
(SOC) or more, the Gr anode becomes saturated and can no longer
intercalate Li+. The remaining capacity will be deposited as lithium
metal on the surface of the Gr anode [25]. On that account, in this 
ultra-low N/P ratio battery system, both lithium-intercalated Gr 
and lithium metal are present concurrently. To ensure the stability 
of this hybrid anode and the reversibility of the lithiation/delithia-
tion process, the choice of electrolyte is critically important. The
SPAN||Gr battery system exhibits superior cycling stability in car-
bonated ester-based electrolytes at N/P = 1.05 [34]. This is attribu-
ted to the fact that no polysulﬁdes are dissolved in carbonated 
ester-based electrolytes, and the Gr anode is also well-matched
with carbonated ester-based electrolytes [35,36]. However, the 
compatibility of lithium metal with carbonated ester-based elec-
trolytes is far from satisfactory, so the stability of the battery at
N/P < 1.0 still needs to be veriﬁed.

As shown in Fig. 2a, b, and Fig. S1 (online), in LB, when the N/P 
ratio is 1.1, the battery exhibits high stability. The capacity of the 
cathode material is calculated based on the overall mass of SPAN. 
Nevertheless, as the N/P ratio diminishes, the cycle stability of 
the battery concomitantly declines. Speciﬁcally, when N/P = 0.6, 
after 170 cycles, the battery’s capacity retention rate is merely 
50 %. Moreover, when N/P = 0 (anode-free), the battery scarcely 
retains any capacity after approximately 40 cycles. This indicates 
that the LB electrolyte, which is typically employed when the N/
P ratio exceeds 1 under normal conditions, is challenging to ensure
the cycling stability of the battery under ultra-low N/P ratios. It is
suggested that for the SPAN||Gr battery systems at N/P = 0.6, the
design of novel electrolytes is imperative to ensure the long-term
cyclic stability of the battery. It is worth noting that as the N/P ratio
decreases, the discharge voltage plateau of the battery increases,
which is beneﬁcial to the improvement of the battery’s energy den-
sity (Fig. S2 online).
Ether-based electrolytes are more compatible with lithium 
metal compared to carbonated ester-based electrolytes [37]. How-
ever, in LS electrolyte with a low salt concentration, polysulﬁdes 
dissolve in the cathode of SPAN. Moreover, there is an incompati-
bility issue between the electrolyte and the Gr anode [38]. As a 
result, the battery is difﬁcult to operate (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the 
localized high-concentration electrolytes can not only inhibit the 
dissolution of polysulﬁdes but also be compatible with both Gr
and lithium metal [39–41]. Afterwards, to compare the disparity 
in polysulﬁde dissolution between LH and LS, in-situ UV-vis mea-
surements were performed on the SPAN||Li optical cell. The results 
show that after the discharge process, there is scarcely any polysul-
ﬁde in LH, whereas multiple polysulﬁde signals are detected in LS
(Fig. S3 online). LH retains its transparency, while LS turns yellow
(Fig. S4 online). This evidence suggests that LH effectively sup-
presses the dissolution of polysulﬁdes and ensures the stability
of the SPAN cathode.
As depicted in Fig. 2a, LH can effectively maintain a stable 
charging and discharging process for the battery. The discharge 
voltage plateau of the battery in LH is 1.82 V, with a capacity of 
763 mAh g−1 . Moreover, LH exhibits superior cycle stability com-
pared to LB. After 250 cycles, the battery exhibits a capacity reten-
tion rate of 90 %, which is comparable to that of the battery
operating stably in LB with an N/P ratio of 1.1 (Fig. 2b). At higher
current densities (1.0 A g−1 ), although the deposition of lithium 
might become more difﬁcult to control, which could bring about 
a decline in cycle stability, the battery still demonstrated better
cycle stability in the LH electrolyte. After 50 cycles, the battery
capacity retention rate in LH was 92 %, while in LB it was only
71 % (Fig. S5 online). On the other hand, the battery with an N/P 
ratio of 1.1 also shows similar cycle stability in the LH electrolyte
compared to the LB electrolyte. After 100 cycles, the capacity
retention rate reached 96 % (Fig. S6 online), which was higher than 
that of the battery using LB electrolyte under the same conditions
(94 %, Fig. S1 online). This further veriﬁes the suitability of the LH 
electrolyte for this battery system. As is evident from the LSV
curves in Fig. S7 (online), LH exhibits relatively greater stability, 
which may be related to the enrichment of anions and the reduc-
tion of free solvents in the Li+ solvation structure of LH. In an effort 
to compare the friendliness of LH and LB towards lithium metal, CE
tests were conducted on Li||Cu batteries that were cycled in the
two electrolytes. The greater polarization in the LH electrolyte
might be related to its lower ionic conductivity (Fig. S8 online). 
The results in Table S7 (online) showed that the CE of the battery 
in LB was only 97.4 %, while that of the battery in LH exceeded 
99.0 %. This further indicates that for battery systems containing 
lithium metal, LH is more conducive to maintaining the cycle sta-
bility of the battery. From the ﬁrst 50 cycles of the differential 
capacity-potential plot, it is evident that for the battery cycled in
the LB electrolyte, the peak area decreased substantially. This
was accompanied by a shift of the charging peak to a higher poten-
tial and the discharging peak to a lower potential. In contrast, for
the battery cycled in the LH electrolyte, both the peak areas and
positions remained relatively stable, showing no obvious changes
(Fig. 2c and d). This indicates that in LB, the battery suffered severe 
loss of active lithium, accompani ed by an increase in resistance,
while in LH, both of these phenomena were well suppressed.

3.2. Interface and electroch emical dynamic characteristics
Electrolytes with a high concentration of anions are more favor-
able for the decomposition of anions to form the SEI/CEI. The
resulting SEI/CEI is typically dominated by inorganic components.
As depicted in Fig. 3a, the high-resolution F 1s XPS spectrum of 
the Gr anode cycled in the LB electrolyte reveals the presence of
by-product LixPOyFz [44]. In contrast, no such by-product is 
detected in LH. Conversely, a higher content of LiF is detected.
LiF is an inorganic component characterized by high stability and
favorable electrochemical dynamics [45]. Regarding the SPAN 
cathode, in LB, by-products were also detected in the cathode, 
and the content of LiF was relatively low. In LH, no by-products
were identiﬁed in the cathode, and LiF emerged as the main com-
ponent (Fig. 3b). The C 1s XPS spectrum of the Gr anode cycled in 
LB reveals the presence of the C@O bond, which results from the
decomposition of the solvent (Fig. 3c). Conversely, in LH, this par-
ticular signal is absent. This absence implies that the decomposi-
tion of the solvent has been mitigated in LH. Furthermore, the 
CAF bond can be detected in LH; this suggests that a more mechan-
ically stable SEI might be formed. Such an SEI is likely to play a cru-
cial role in inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites [46]. In the 
SPAN cathode, the CAO bond signal in LH is weaker than that in 
LB, which also indicates that LH can inhibit the decomposition of
the solvent and is conducive to the formation of a CEI with lower
interfacial impedance (Fig. 3d).

The electrochemical dynamic characteristics of batteries can be 
compared via in-situ EIS testing. The impedance spectra of the bat-
tery cycled in LH at different SOCs are depicted in Fig. 3e, f, and the 
impedance spectra of the battery cycled in LB at different SOCs are
presented in Fig. S11 (online). Through detailed distribution of 
relaxation times (DRT) analysis, the impedance changes of various 
electrochemical processes of the battery can be determined. The
DRT data of the batteries in LB and LH are presented in Figs. S12 
and S13 (online), respectively. It can be clearly noted that the 
DRT data of the batteries exhibit nine peaks. The positions of each
peak and the corresponding electrochemical processes are detailed
in Table S8 (online). Among them, s1 is assigned to contact resis-
tance, which is related to internal particles, that is, ohmic resis-
tance (RX). s2 and s3 are assigned to the impedance of the

<<PAGE 6>>
double-layer process (RDL). s4 and s5 are assigned to the impedance 
of SEI/CEI (RSEI). s6 and s7 are assigned to the charge transfer impe-
dance (Rct). s8 and s9 are assigned to the impedance of diffusion
(Rdiff). Through the transformation and integration of the DRT data,
the speciﬁc impedance values of each electrochemical process can
be derived (Figs. S14 and S15 online) [47]. It is evident that the RX 
of the battery cycled in LH is higher than that of the battery cycled
in LB (Fig. S16 online). The relatively high RX might be attributed to 
the fact that LH is essentially a localized high-concentration elec-
trolyte, with its ionic conductivity slightly lower than that of LB
(Fig. S17 online). The localized high-concentration electrolytes typ-
ically exhibit a relatively high viscosity, which further leads to a 
higher Rdiff in LH than in LB. Although RX and Rdiff in LH do not have 
advantages, the enrichment of anions in LH is conducive to the for-
mation of SEI/CEI rich in inorganic components. Therefore, the RSEI
of batteries cycled in LH is generally smaller than that of batteries
in LB (Fig. 3g). In addition, the solvation structure enriched with 
anions improves the wettability of the electrode interface and 
reduces the polarizati on capacitance. Therefore, the RDL of the bat-
tery in LH is also smaller than that in LB.

regardless of whether it is LB or LH, during the battery charging 
process from 0 % SOC to approximately 60 % SOC, the Rct exhibits 
a decreasing trend. This reduction in Rct can be attributed to two 
main factors. Firstly, on the Gr anode, the gradual intercalation of 
Li+ into Gr contributes to the decrease in Rct. Secondly, on the SPAN 
cathode, there is a gradual transformation from the less conductive 
Li2S to the more kinetically favorable Li2S4. The combined effect of
these two phenomena results in a decrease in Rct. Conversely,
when the battery is charged from 60 % SOC to approximately
100 % SOC, although the continuous deposition of lithium metal
on the Gr anode still tends to reduce Rct, the cathode undergoes a
transformation into the less conductive S8. This transformation

on the SPAN cathode becomes the dominant factor inﬂuencing the 
magnitude of Rct, leading to an increase in its value. This phe-

the SOC rises, is a distinctive characteristic of sulfur-based LIBs. 
Based on the Rct values of batteries in LH and LB, it can be observed 
that the majority of the R ct values for the battery in LH are lower
than those in LB. This phenomenon can potentially be attributed

result, it becomes easier for Li+ to undergo desolvation, thereby
leading to a decrease in Rct.

3.3. Deposition of lithium at the anode
During the charging process, the deposition behavior of lithium 
at the anode exhibits distinct characteristics in different elec-
trolytes. The schematic diagram of lithium deposition on the anode
is presented in Fig. 4a. In LB, the lithium deposition is loose and 
porous, uneven, and not dense, while in LH, the lithium deposition
is dense and smooth. As shown in Fig. 4b, in the XRD patterns, dur-
ing the battery charging process in LH, the Gr undergoes a sequen-
tial transformation. Initially, Gr is fully converted into LiC12, and
subsequently, it is entirely transformed into LiC6. When the SOC
reaches 60 % and above, the only detectable signal corresponds 
to LiC6. This phenomenon suggests that in LH, Li+ initially interca-
late into the Gr structure, and only after the Gr is fully intercalated, 
lithium metals start to deposit on the Gr surface. The observed 
reaction process is both uniform and complete. This can potentially 
be attributed to the fact that the LH environment is favorable for
the formation of a stable and kinetically efﬁcient interface. Such
an interface plays a crucial role in inhibiting the growth of lithium
dendrites and mitigating numerous side reactions. The structures
of SPAN at different SOCs in different electrolytes are similar,
which indicates that the structure of the cathode is relatively
stable (Fig. S18 online). As shown in Fig. 4c, neither the PP separa-
tors nor the Gr anodes have been rinsed. It can be observed that in 
LB, the lithium deposition on the Gr anode is not dense, while in
LH, the lithium deposition is more uniform and exhibits a metallic
luster.

3.4. Performance of different cycle conditions
Diverse cycling conditions can exert an impact on the perfor-
mance of batteries. As exhibited in Fig. 5a and Fig. S24 (online), 
the capacity retention rates of the SPAN||Gr batteries at N/P = 0.6 
before and after a one-month rest at 100 % SOC and 0 % SOC are 
98 % and 100 %, respectively. It can be seen that when lithium 
metal is precipitated (100 % SOC), there will be a certain amount
of self-discharge after a one-month rest. This may be due to the
existence of certain interfacial side reactions, but the self-
discharge rate is relatively low. After cycling, the capacity retention
rate can be restored to over 99 % (Fig. S25 online), indicating that 
the LH electrolyte is well adapted to this battery system and there 
will be no continuous and large-scale side reactions during the bat-
tery’s rest process. Since the diffusion impedance of LH is larger
than that of LB, but its interfacial kinetics are superior to those of
LB, the electrochemical dynamics of the battery may not deterio-
rate. As shown in Fig. 5b, the rate performance of the battery cycled 
in the LH electrolyte is better than that cycled in the LB. This indi-
cates that the anion-enriched LH, with its lower RSEI and Rct, pro-
motes the overall kinetics of the battery. It further veriﬁes that
LH not only enhances the cycling stability of the battery but also
improves its rate performance.
In light of the fact that the N/P ratio of this battery system is 0.6, 
regulating the depth of discharge (DOD) at 60 % to avoid the depo-
sition of lithium metal on the anode could be an effective approach 
to improving the cycle stability. This renders the battery system a
promising candidate for an energy storage battery with pre-
supplied additional lithium. In accordance with prior research, it
has been established that the capacity decay of SPAN||Gr batteries
primarily stems from the loss of active lithium [34]. Consequently, 
pre-supplementing additional lithium can contribute to the exten-
sion of the cycle life. Here, two methods are adopted to control the 
DOD at 60 %. One is to control the charging capacity at 60 % of the
initial capacity, and the other is to control the charging voltage at
the voltage corresponding to 60 % of the initial capacity. As shown
in Fig. 5c, it is the cycle performance with the controlled charging 
capacity at 60 % of the initial capacity. Under such conditions, as
the number of cycles increases, the charging voltage will rise due
to the increase in resistance (Fig. S26 online). The increase in resis-
tance is caused by the thickening of the SEI/CEI due to the volume 
changes of the anode and cathode. After 500 cycles, the battery was
charged and discharged at 100 % DOD, and its capacity retention
rate was 93 % of the initial capacity. As can be seen from Fig. 5d, 
the resistance of the battery increased, which was the cause of 
capacity decay. However, its stability was higher than that of the
battery cycled under the condition of always 100 % DOD. It can 
be seen that this method can reduce the deposition of lithium 
metal, thereby reducing side reactions and capacity loss. Another 
method is achieved by controlling the voltage. Due to the increase
in resistance, the capacity gradually decays, resulting in a lower
actual cycle capacity in the later stage. Therefore, after 500 cycles,
a 100 % DOD charge and discharge is performed, and the recovered
capacity is higher (Figs. S27 and S28 online). Nevertheless, this 
method suffers from a rather low cycle capacity. Thus, it is more 
practical to conduct the 60 % DOD charge and discharge by control-
ling capacity rather than voltage.

For such ultra-low N/P ratio batteries, lithium metal deposition 
occurs during the charging process. Therefore, charging with a 
smaller current and discharging with a larger current is an effective
strategy to suppress the growth of lithium dendrites [25]. As 
shown in Fig. 5e and Fig. S29 (online), this low-current charging 
strategy did not signiﬁcantly improve the stability of the cycle. 
The battery cycle in LH remained relatively stable, which might 
be due to the fact that the capacity decay caused by the thickening 
of SEI/CEI could not be avoided. Low-current charging might be 
more effective in preventing rapid capacity decay (lithium den-
drites). It is worth noting that when this strategy is used to conduct
tests on anode-free systems (N/P = 0), a capacity retention rate of
96 % can be achieved in the ﬁrst 100 cycles (Fig. S30 online). This 
is attributed to the effect of LH on the one hand, and on the other 
hand, the smaller current can prevent the rapid capacity decay 
caused by dendrite growth in the ﬁrst 100 cycles. However, the 
capacity of the battery rapidly decays during the 100–200th cycle, 
and its cycle life was shorter than that of the battery with N/P = 0.6.
This further indicates the signiﬁcance of the presence of Gr in this
battery system for maintaining a long cycle life. In the hybrid
anode composed of lithium and Gr, both lithium and Gr play indis-
pensable roles.

dominated by the SPAN cathode, while the anode with Gr compo-

signiﬁcance of Gr in reducing overall stress variations.

4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, the SPAN||Gr battery strikes a balance between 
energy density and cycling stability through anion-mediated elec-
trolyte engineering and ultra-low N/P ratio (N/P = 0.6) design. The 
hybrid anode harnesses the cycling stability of Gr and the high 
capacity of lithium metal, overcoming the energy-density limita-
tions of traditional LIBs. The custom anion-enriched LH electrolyte 
(LiFSI:DME:HFE) 
reshapes 
Li+ 
solvation 
structures, 
driving 
inorganic-rich (LiF-dominant) SEI/CEI formation, suppressing poly-
sulﬁde dissolution, and ensuring uniform lithium deposition on Gr. 
In addition, the inorganic interface constructed by LH achieves a 
lower interface impedance for the battery and enhances the elec-
trochemical dynamics of the battery. Ultimately, after 300 cycles, 
the SPAN||Gr pouch cell with an N/P ratio of 0.6 exhibited a capac-
ity retention rate of 92 %, ensuring its stability. Simultaneously,
compared to the cell with an N/P ratio of 1.1, the energy density
of this cell increased by 24 %. By precisely controlling the DOD of
this battery system, it is possible to switch the battery system
between Gr-based batteries with additional lithium inventory
and hybrid anode batteries to meet different energy storage
requirements. This strategy bridges the gap between energy den-
sity and cycle life, positioning sulfur-based batteries as viable can-
didates for electric vehicles and grid storage.
