ABSTRACT: Understanding electrolyte/electrode interface reac-
tions during battery operation is essential for the development and
optimization of next-generation secondary batteries. There have
been many reports on the discharge reaction mechanism in lithium
sulfur batteries, which are next-generation secondary batteries.
However, most of these reports are related to the sulfur reduction
reaction, which is strongly affected by the electrolyte system. Here,
we investigated not only the active material sulfur but also the
electrolyte near the interface during discharging using operando
Raman spectroscopic methods to reveal the discharge behavior in a Lithium sulfur battery with sulfolane (SL)-based concentrated
electrolyte and lithium-glyme solvate ionic liquid (Li-G SIL). We have succeeded in experimentally demonstrating that the
concentration polarization of Li+ occurs significantly in the Li-G SIL system with a low Li+ transference number. This is consistent
with the fact that the intermediate products differ: the intermediates produced in the SL-based electrolyte system contain Li+,
whereas in the G4-based electrolyte system, the chemical species containing Li+ is hardly confirmed. We believe that the difference in
the Li+ concentration at the interface affects the overvoltage of the battery.
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery is recognized as one of the
candidates to break through the specific energy limitations of
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) because of the high theoretical
specific energy.1,2 Sulfur, which is used as the active material in
Li−S batteries, receives 16 electrons during discharge and
ultimately becomes Li2S, exhibiting a high discharge capacity of
1672 mA g−1. However, this reaction involves the formation of
lithium polysulfide (LiPS) as an intermediate, and the shuttle
effect of the long-chain LiPS (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) produced at
high voltage is preventing the practical application of Li−S
batteries.3 The shuttle effect, in which the LiPS dissolves in the
electrolyte and diffuses to the lithium anode, contributes to the
deterioration of the lithium anode, resulting in poor long-cycle
stability.
In order to suppress the shuttle effect, various efforts have
been taken, including shielding with a separator, protecting Li
metals by forming a passivation layer called as solid electrolyte
interface, and the suppression of dissolution into the
electrolyte.4 Watanabe and Dokko et al. have proposed a
method for suppressing the dissolution of LiPS5−7 using the
Li-Glyme solvated ionic liquid (Li-G SIL) and the sulfolane
(SL) based concentrated electrolyte solution (CES). As
solvent-shared ion pair (SSIP) and contact ion pair (CIP)
form in these electrolytes,8−10 free solvents to solvate the
polysulfide are insufficient, suppressing the shuttle effect.
Furthermore, the battery performance is improved by using
localized high-concentration electrolytes6,11 with weakly
coordinating solvents added to reduce viscosity while
maintaining these characteristic structures.12,13
Two discharge voltage plateaus are observed in discharge
curves of Li−S batteries, and it is widely accepted that the
reaction at 2.3 V corresponds to a reduction from S8 to Li2S4,
and that at 2.0 V corresponds to the further reduction to Li2S.
These reactions are multistep electron transfer reactions, and
their reaction mechanisms have been elucidated by in situ
characterization techniques.14−23 From operando XAS, it was
confirmed that S4
2−, S6
2−, and S2
2−were formed as
intermediates during discharge in a Li−S battery using Li-G
SIL.24 However, the sulfur reduction reactions are quite
complicated, not only because of the multistep electron
transfer, but also due to the strong influence of the electrolyte
system, such as the donor number of solvents.25 Therefore, the
sulfur reduction reaction mechanism needs to be further
investigated.
On the other hand, the reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S is a
liquid−solid conversion from soluble PS to the insoluble

sulfur near the electrode interface during charge/discharge of
the Li−S battery with Li-G SIL and SL-based CES by means of
the operando Raman and ab initio calculation.
■EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
Chemical Material. A Ketjen Black (KB)-Sulfur (KB-S)
composite electrode was prepared by the following procedure.
First, the required amounts of elemental sulfur and KB (60:30
by mass) were thoroughly mixed by grinding. Next, the
mixture was complexed by heating at 155 °C for 6 h and then
cooled to room temperature. Finally, the mixture was kneaded
with an agate mortar to break up the agglomeration and obtain
a complex. The obtained KB−S composite was mixed with a 2
wt % CMC aqueous solution of a binder at a mass ratio of
90:10 and then applied to Al to prepare a sulfur positive
electrode.
The Li−S battery used for the measurement was equipped
with a sulfur positive electrode composed of this KB−S
composite and a Li metal negative electrode. As the electrolyte,
SL-based CES and Li-G SIL were used. SL-based CES was
prepared by mixing LiTFSA (Li(CF3SO2)2N), SL (Sulfolane
(Tetrahydrothiophene-1,1-dioxide)), and HFE (1,1,2,2-Tetra-
fluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-Tetrafluoropropyl ether) at a molar ratio of
1:2:2. Li-G SIL was prepared by mixing LiTFSA, G4
(Tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether), and HFE at a molar
ratio of 1:1:4.
Operando Microscopic Raman Spectroscopy. To
perform Raman microscopy measurements, Miclab’s caulking
cell was modified to create an operand cell holder. An
observation window with a diameter of 5 mm was opened in
the center of the operand cell holder, and a quartz window
with a thickness of 0.5 mm was installed. The coin cell was
CR2032, assembled from the bottom in the order of the
cathode, separator, and anode. A 3 mm diameter window was
opened in the upper lid, separator, and negative electrode of
the coin cell. A Li metal (Honjo Metal, 16 mm diameter) and a
glass separator were used as the anode and separator,
respectively.
The charge/discharge test was performed at a rate of 0.05C
(167 μA/cm2). The voltage range was from 3.3 to 1.0 V.
Compact charge−discharge equipment (EC-frontier, ECAD-
1000) was used for the measurements. Raman spectra were
measured using a JASCO NRS4500 Raman spectrometer
equipped with an aberration correction type Czerny Turner
monochromator and a CCD detector at an optical resolution
of 4.9 cm−1. For excitation, a laser wavelength at 532 nm was
used and was operated at 2.1 mW to avoid KB damage. Each
measurement was performed by integrating the exposure time
for 60 s and integrating four times every 50 min during
charging and discharging of the Li−S battery. In addition, the
ultralong operation 100× projector lens (Work Distance = 7.6
mm) was used for the measurements.
Quantum Chemical Calculations. The Gaussian 16
programs27 were used for DFT and MP3 calculations with
basis sets implemented in the same program. Some stable
geometries of PS and LiPS species were achieved by MP3/cc−
pVDZ level.28−30 Theoretical Raman bands were calculated at
the B3LYP/6−311+G(d,p) level.31
Single-Particle Electrochemical Measurements. For
single-particle measurements, an original microprobe was used
to contact a single particle. The microprobe was made by
inserting a 0.2 mm diameter Cu wire and a welded 10 μm
diameter Pt wire into a glass capillary and polishing the tip.
The electrolytes used for single-particle measurements were
the following: LiTFSA/SL = 1:2 solution, and LiTFSA/G4 =
1:1 solution. For the cathode, particles with a diameter of
about 20 μm were used. The cathode was placed on a porous
glass filter, and a microprobe was brought into contact with the
particles from directly above. The counter electrode was made
of Li metal. Charge−discharge tests were conducted using a
Biologic SP-200 in the voltage range of 1.0−3.0 V at a current
of 2.0 nA for SL-based electrolyte and 100 pA for G4-based
electrolyte. For comparison, charge−discharge tests were also
performed using coin cells (CR2032) at a rate of 0.42C. The
discharge capacities of the sulfur electrodes in the SL-based
electrolyte were 480 pAh (single particle) and 200 mAh/g
(coin cell), respectively. The discharge capacities of the sulfur
electrodes in the G4-based electrolyte were 48 pAh (single
particle) and 206 mAh/g (coin cell), respectively.
In single-particle measurements, there is an excess of
electrolyte in the cell compared with coin cells. However, the
discharge capacity of the Li−S battery using polysulfide
insoluble electrolytes is almost independent of the ratio of the
electrolyte volume to sulfur weight (E/S [μL_electrolyte/
mg_sulfur]).32 Thus, the amount of polysulfide dissolution is
considered to be almost the same between the coin cell
measurements and the single-particle measurements.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Ab Initio Calculations of Lithium Polysulfide. There are
many possible chemical species for LiPSs that are the reductive
intermediate. Ab initio calculations were carried out to narrow
down the possible species. Figure 1a shows the HOMO and
LUMO energy levels of the S8, S8 monoanions (S8
−), dianions
(S8
2−), and the chemical species with the addition of one or
two lithium for each. The LUMO energy of S8 is low enough
to accept an electron. The LUMO energy of S8
−is high,
making it difficult to accept a second electron; however, the
LUMO energy decreases when it forms a contact ion pair with
Li+. Figure 1b shows the HOMO and LUMO energy levels for
the other lithium polysulfides. All LiPSs have LUMO energy
levels similar to those of S8. When S8 is reduced to produce
S8
2−, it is not possible for S8
2−to accept electrons on its own.
However, the coexistence of lithium ions leads to a further
reduction reaction. These calculations suggest that the
discharge reaction proceeds as the polysulfides dispropor-
tionate by electron transfer to the LUMO energy level.

the Raman bands of Li2S3 and Li2S5. This suggests that the
Li2S4, Li2S3, and Li2S5 are produced in both discharge and

during charging, indicating that irreversible reactions occur in
the Li−S battery with SL-based electrolytes. Figure 2f−k shows

Raman bands at 240 and 435 cm−1 can be attributed to S8

produced during discharging and charging are completely
different in the SL-based and G4-based electrolyte systems.
The intermediates produced in the SL-based electrolyte system
contain Li+, whereas in the G4-based electrolyte system, the
chemical species containing Li+ is hardly confirmed.
Li+ ions in LiPSs, which are generated during discharge, are
supplied from electrolytes. We investigated the Li+ solvation
structures of the electrolytes during the charge/discharge.

To evaluate Li+ solvation structure more quantitatively, the
complementary least-squares analysis (CLSA)37 was per-
formed on the experimental Raman spectra. The CLSA is a
type of multivariate analysis that evaluates the Raman
scattering factors and their concentrations of each chemical
species. In our previous research,10,38 the CLSA was applied to
the Raman peaks of TFSA anions in SL-based electrolyte and
Li-G4 SIL to clarify the solution structures. In the SL-based
electrolyte, there are three chemical species: free, CIP and/or
aggregate (CIP/AGG), and AGG. CIP/AGG are anions
directly coordinated to Li+, and AGG is the bridging one in
the Li+-TFSA-solvent aggregates. In the Li-G4 SIL, TFSA
anions can take on three different chemical states: free, solvate
shared ion pair (SSIP), and CIP (or AGG). Figure 3 shows the
species distribution function of chemical species near the
cathode as a function of the second discharge time, obtained
by analyzing the ratio of the Raman bands of anions (TFSA) to
the solvents. The black, blue, and red straight lines describe the
distribution of each chemical species in the bulk. In the SL-
based electrolyte system, the fraction of AGG is almost
constant and consistent with that of the bulk until the middle
of the discharge. In the early part of the discharge, the fraction
of CIP/AGG is higher than that of bulk; it slightly decreases,
and the fraction of Free anion increases as the discharge
progresses. In the final stage of discharge, AGG changes to
CIP/AGG and Free anions. In the Li-G4 SIL system, the
fraction of the free anion increases as the discharge time
increases. The change in the chemical species of anions during
discharge reflects the Li+ ion concentration near the electro-
lyte/electrode interface. Even as the discharge progresses, the
SL-based electrolyte system has a sufficient amount of Li+ ions
near the interface. By contrast, the Li+ ion concentration at the
interface is depleted in the Li-G4 SIL system. S8 is reduced to
S6
2−and then S6
2−is gradually converted to S4
2−at first plateau
voltage; however, it has been proposed that a reaction
converting S8 to S4
2−occurs in the concentrated electrolytes
(1/2S8 + 2Li+ + 2e−⇆Li2S4).24 In the SL-based electrolyte
system, Li+ is sufficiently supplied at the interface, resulting in
the formation of intermediates containing Li+ such as Li2S4. In
contrast, in the Li-G4 SIL system, the Li+ concentration at the
interface becomes depleted, leading to the formation of
intermediates that do not contain Li+.
To obtain insight into the relationship between the
discharge behavior and Li+ ion concentration, galvanostatic
intermittent titration (GITT) measurements were carried out.
Figure S5 shows the discharge curves obtained during the
GITT measurements of the Li−S batteries with (a) SL-based
electrolyte and (b) Li-G4 SIL. By taking the difference
between the thermodynamic equilibrium voltage and the
discharge voltage, we can determine the overvoltage of the
entire Li−S battery. The thermodynamic equilibrium voltage
was almost constant regardless of the electrolyte; however, the
overvoltage increases as the discharge processes. The over-
voltage of the Li−S battery with Li-G4 SIL is higher than that
with SL-based electrolyte. Figure 4 shows the Raman intensity
ratio of anion to solvent and peak position of the TFSA Raman
band with maximum intensity as a function of the overvoltage
for the Li−S batteries. The yellow-green lines describe the
Raman intensity ratio and the peak position in the bulk
electrolytes. For both electrolytes, the changes in intensity ratio

In the Li−S batteries, a composite material electrode is used,
which is fabricated by kneading carbon material, a conductive
additive, and a binder, and then applying it to a current
collector. The electrolyte is trapped between or within the
particles of the composite material electrode near the
composite material electrode/electrolyte interface. At the
electrolyte/electrode interface, there is the electrical double
layer of just a few nm39−42 and a diffusion layer where mass
transfer occurs, which is approximately 10 μm. The spatial
resolution of the operando micro-Raman measurement in this
work was approximately 2 μm, suggesting that the concen-
tration changes from the vicinity of the electrode to the
diffusion layer were observed. Therefore, we believe that the
results of the measurement reflect the fact that the
concentration of ion pairs increases due to the formation of
the reaction interface during the initial discharging stage and
that the Li+ ion concentration decreases due to the influence of
the diffusion layer as the discharge progresses.
Single-Particle Electrochemical Measurements. The
Li−S battery cathode is a composite material electrode, which
makes the interpretation of overvoltage extremely complex.
Therefore, single-particle electrochemical measurements were
performed. Figure S6 shows the charge−discharge curves of
the single particle acquired by using the probe electrode. The
capacities at the discharge end voltage and charge end voltage
for each cycle were defined as State of Discharge (SOD) =
100% and State of Charge (SOC) = 100%, respectively. In
single-particle measurements, CES and SIL without the HFE
were used as electrolytes, resulting in a larger overvoltage than
that observed in operando Raman measurements. For the Li−S
battery using the SL-based electrolyte, the voltage was reduced
by approximately 0.2 V compared to the coin cell during
charging. In the discharge process, the only plateau appears at
1.75 V. For the Li−S battery using the Li-G4 SIL, the voltage
decreases by approximately 0.3 V compared to the coin cell
during charging, and the first plateau of the discharge curve is
longer than that of the coin cell and decreases monotonically.
Figure 5 shows the voltage difference (ΔE) between the coin
cell and single particles during the charge/discharge. In the
charging process, the ΔE exhibits a similar shape for both
systems; however, during the discharging process, the position
of the difference in voltage differs between the SL-based
electrolyte system and the Li-G4 SIL system. The difference in
voltage between the coin cell and the single-particle system
may be related to the resistance between particles and the
contact area of the solid−liquid interface between particles and
electrolytes. The contact area for the single particle is larger
than that of the coin cell, and the supply of Li+ from the
electrolyte to the electrode interface is sufficient, resulting in
the resistance is small. By contrast, the contact area of the coin
cell is small, and the supply of Li+ is insufficient, which may
cause a large overvoltage. In the SL-based electrolyte system,
the voltage difference at the beginning of the discharge is small
and increases from the middle to the end of the discharge,
whereas in the case of the Li-G4 SIL system, the voltage
difference is relatively large from the beginning of the discharge
and remains almost constant. This would be related to the
difference in the transference number (tLi+). The tLi+ for Li-G4
SIL, i.e., 0.028, is lower than that of SL-based electrolytes, i.e.,
0.68.43 In the Li-G4 SIL system, due to the low transference
number, Li+ ions are not sufficiently supplied to the cathode
electrode, resulting in the large overvoltage from the beginning
of the discharge. In the operando Raman measurements, the
electrolyte solution containing the diluent was used, differing
from the electrolytes employed in the single-particle measure-
ments. Therefore, note that the overvoltage during operando
Raman measurements cannot be directly compared with that
during single-particle measurements. However, even for the
electrolyte system with the added diluent, the tLi+ for Li-G4
SIL, i.e., 0.018, is lower than that of SL-based electrolytes, i.e.,
0.48,11 suggesting that the depletion of Li+ ion concentration at
the solid−liquid interface may influence the magnitude of
overvoltage.
■CONCLUSION
We investigated the charge−discharge reaction behavior of
cathode-insoluble Li−S batteries with SL- and G4-based
electrolytes by means of the Ab initio calculations, operando
micro-Raman spectroscopic measurements, and single-particle
electrochemical measurements. According to the ab initio
calculations, it was suggested that the discharge reaction
progresses as the polysulfide is disproportionated by the
electron transfer to the LUMO level. In the Li−S battery with
the SL-based electrolyte, Li2S4, Li2S3, and Li2S5 were produced
as intermediate products within the cathode. By contrast, S8,
S6, and LiS6
−were produced within the cathode in the Li−S
battery with Li-G SIL during charge/discharge. In the early
stage of discharge, the concentrations of AGG and CIP were
higher than the bulk concentration, indicating that the Li+ ion
concentration at the interface was high. Moreover, the Li+ ion
concentration decreased at the electrode/electrolyte interface
