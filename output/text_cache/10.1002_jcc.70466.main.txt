ABSTRACT
Lithium-­metal anodes present remarkable theoretical capacity for advanced batteries. However, their practical application is hin­
dered by interfacial instabilities and dendritic lithium morphologies, which undermine safety and longevity. This study utilizes 
machine-­learned interatomic potentials to conduct molecular dynamics simulations of the interface between a lithium-­metal 
anode and an electrolyte composed of elemental sulfur (S8), propylene carbonate (PC), and lithium Otf (LiOTf). Because the 
employed neutral MLIP does not explicitly describe electrode potential, electron transfer, or charge-­state evolution, the simu­
lations are interpreted as reactive structural models of lithium redistribution, aggregation, and protrusion growth rather than 
as a complete electrochemical plating/stripping description. Within this scope, the simulations provide atomistic insight into 
early dendrite-­like lithium protrusions across different electrolyte compositions. Species detection, connection-­matrix analysis, 
maximum protrusion height, and local-­order analysis are used as complementary structural descriptors of interfacial reorgani­
zation. Supporting benchmarks against DFT include RDF validation for the interface model and structural and reaction-­energy 
comparisons for adsorption and reaction in modeled lithium clusters. These benchmarks indicate that the r2SCAN readout gives 
more consistent structural and energetic agreement for the present lithium surface chemistry, whereas the 휔 B97X readout is 
retained as a sensitivity test for readout dependence. The findings provide an atomic-­level view of how S8, PC, and Otf− correlate 
with lithium aggregation and morphology changes in S8/PC/LiOtf electrolytes, while clarifying the present limits of pre-­trained 
MLIPs for mechanistic electrochemical interpretation.
1   |   Introduction
Lithium-­sulfur (Li-­S) batteries present an appealing potential to 
replace the currently prevalent lithium-­ion (Li-­ion) batteries in 
response to rapidly growing energy requirements [1]. Li-­S bat­
teries offer a compelling option for future energy storage tech­
nologies, as they provide significantly higher theoretical energy 
density and utilize more economic raw materials compared to 
conventional Li-­ion batteries [2, 3]. Nonetheless, effective imple­
mentation of Li-­S batteries faces significant challenges due to 
unwanted side reactions between the electrolyte and electrode. 
These unwanted side reactions lead to the formation of a solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI), which results in the consump­
tion of active lithium from the anode [4]. The SEI can result in

unregulated dendrite growth, [5] which presents considerable 
safety hazards and significantly diminishes the cycle life of the 
battery.
Numerous factors influence the formation of dendrites, and 
understanding this process remains a significant challenge in 
the field of battery research. Many studies demonstrate that 
the current density and operating temperature impact the for­
mation of lithium dendrites [6–9]. Another important aspect 
is the composition and concentration of the electrolyte. The 
specific ions present and their concentrations in the electro­
lyte have a considerable influence on both the rate and the 
morphology of dendrite formation. Unstable electrolytes or 
elevated ion concentrations can promote the growth of den­
drites [10–12].
Dendrite growth does not have a universally accepted expla­
nation; rather, it is typically recognized as an instability that 
emerges from the interplay of various physical and chemical 
processes occurring at the interface of a material. In Li-­S sys­
tems, confronted with these obstacles, researchers have pro­
posed three strategies for inhibiting the growth of lithium 
dendrites: focusing on the electrolyte, [13–18] anode, [12, 19–24] 
and separator [25–28]. Each stabilization strategy has its inher­
ent limitations, so combining their strengths, such as pairing an 
optimized anode with suitable additives or electrolyte modifica­
tions to stabilize the SEI and reduce side reactions, is essential. 
For industrial Li-­S batteries, major progress is needed in high-­
loading sulfur cathodes, stable anodes, advanced separators and 
electrolytes, and manufacturing, especially to improve perfor­
mance with lean electrolytes, balanced electrode capacities, and 
under extreme temperatures [29]. Given these constraints, it is 
essential to conduct computational studies to explore anode-­
electrolyte interactions to observe the formation of dendrites at 
a molecular timescale.
Prior investigations have yielded valuable insights into lithium 
metal anode dynamics, as demonstrated by Camacho-­Forero 
and Balbuena, who employed DFT-­based simulations to un­
cover the mechanisms by which charged interfaces enhance 
electrolyte decomposition and SEI formation. However, their 
methodology is constrained by substantial computational cost, 
which limits the timescales available for analysis [30]. Lee et al. 
utilized advanced imaging and computational modeling to dy­
namically observe dendrite growth and its relationship with 
interfacial reactions; however, the transferability of their mod­
els across various chemical conditions may be limited [31]. In 
comparison to these methods, Machine-­Learned Interatomic 
Potentials (MLIPs) provide the capability to effectively simulate 
larger and more intricate systems over extended timescales with 
accuracy close to ab initio levels, tackling issues of scalability 
and transferability. Nonetheless, MLIPs rely on high-­quality 
training data and may occasionally fall short in terms of physi­
cal interpretability.
This study focuses on early interfacial lithium restructur­
ing at the interface between lithium metal anodes and liquid 
electrolytes, addressing a critical process related to dendritic 
morphology development in lithium metal batteries. The 
investigation was conducted through molecular dynamics 
(MD) simulations, employing the Basis-­v0 MLIP with two 
task heads, namely r2SCAN and 휔 B97X. In particular, this 
study examines the impact of adding elemental sulfur (S8) to 
the electrolyte, systematically adjusting its concentration to 
assess its correlation with lithium aggregation, protrusion 
height, and interfacial structural order. We emphasize that 
the present simulations do not impose an electrode potential 
and do not explicitly describe electron transfer; therefore, the 
term dendrite is used operationally to denote anisotropic, 
dendrite-­like lithium protrusions and aggregates that emerge 
from the simulated lithium surface. The mechanistic dis­
cussion is consequently restricted to structural correlations, 
bond-­formation/bond-­breaking events available to the MLIP, 
and readout-­dependent solvent decomposition, rather than to 
a complete electrochemical deposition mechanism.

was trained on a broad corpus of atomistic data curated from 
open-­source DFT datasets [42–54] providing diverse coverage of 
chemical space, using a multi-­fidelity learning strategy [55] to 
enable training a single unified model across different levels of 
theory and chemical domains.
The architecture of Basis-­v0 combines the node-­wise body-­ordered 
tensor product introduced by the MACE architecture [56] with 
more expressive edge representations generated by a deep com­
position of equivariant edge-­wise update functions. In contrast to 
the standard equivariant convolution used by architectures such 
as MACE, NequIP [57], and SevenNet [58] to update edge features, 
Basis-­v0 uses a sequence of environment-­dependent equivariant 
functions to construct edge representations iteratively. We call the 
number of update functions in this sequence the recursion depth, 
a hyperparameter R. Conceptually, increasing R enables the model 
to learn more expressive descriptions of the local chemical envi­
ronment without extending its receptive field (similar to Allegro 
[59]). We find that models with higher R reliably provide improved 
accuracy in practice. Further details on the design of Basis-­v0 are 
provided in the Supporting Information.
In contrast to MLIPs developed for individual chemical systems, 
Basis-­v0 is a pre-­trained MLIP trained on a large, diverse data­
set and designed to perform reactive MD simulations of complex 
chemical systems without requiring upfront system-­specific 
training. To enable training on varied sources of atomistic data, 
Basis-­v0 is equipped with a separate readout layer for each DFT 
level of theory in the pre-­training corpus. At the same time, all 
other model parameters are shared across levels of theory. This 
enables simultaneous training on multiple DFT datasets across 
different domains of chemistry, improving the model's robust­
ness and chemical representation while providing higher accu­
racy in domains with higher-­fidelity training data [55].
As a result of the multi-­fidelity learning strategy, Basis-­v0 pro­
vides readouts for different levels of theory. By default, we perform 
simulations using energies and forces derived from the readout, 
matched to the r2SCAN [47, 60] level of theory, which is a high 
level of theory suitable for general chemistry. Other readouts are 
expected to perform better in specific domains of chemistry, given 
the available training data. Thus, we also performed simulations 
with the 휔 B97X [33, 46, 48, 53] readout, which was hypothesized 
to provide high accuracy for the small-­molecule reactive chemistry 
relevant to the electrolyte. In the present adsorption and reaction 
benchmarks for modeled lithium clusters, however, the r2SCAN 
readout gives substantially smaller reaction-­energy deviations 
than the 휔 B97X readout for the representative lithium surface 
motifs, so r2SCAN is treated as the primary readout and 휔 B97X 
as a readout-­sensitivity comparison.

4   |   Conclusions
We conducted MD simulations utilizing the Basis-­v0 multi-­
fidelity pre-­trained MLIP to investigate lithium metal/liquid 
electrolyte interfaces at various S8 concentrations. The simula­
tions employed two Basis-­v0 readout heads, trained on 휔 B97X 
and r2SCAN DFT data, respectively. Because the present neu­
tral MLIP simulations do not explicitly describe electrode po­
tential, electron transfer, or charge-­state evolution, the observed 
structures should be interpreted as dendrite-­like lithium protru­
sions and aggregates rather than as a complete electrochemical 
deposition process.
Within this structural interpretation, the simulations show lith­
ium redistribution away from the initial anode region, growth of 
anisotropic lithium protrusions, changes in local lithium coor­
dination order, and electrolyte-­dependent bond rearrangements. 
The lithium atom count in the initial anode region is therefore 
a redistribution descriptor, not a measure of lithium consump­
tion. The maximum height and tetrahedral order analyses pro­
vide complementary structural information, but the tetrahedral 
order parameter alone is not a unique morphology identifier.
The two MLIP readout heads behave differently for the ad­
opted interface system. The 휔 sB97X readout gives lith­
ium protrusion growth without strong solvent dissociation, 
whereas the r2SCAN readout describes PC dissociation into 
C3H6, consistent with previous ab initio simulation results re­
ported elsewhere [30]. The Supporting Information includes 
both RDF validation against a VASP r2SCAN trajectory and 
BASIS–DFT comparisons of optimized structures, bond dis­
tances, and electronic reaction energies for adsorption and re­
action in modeled lithium clusters. In these modeled-­cluster 
tests, BASIS-­r2SCAN gives lower reaction-­energy deviations 
than BASIS-­ 휔 B97X for the representative lithium surface 
reactions. For this reason, the r2SCAN readout is considered 
the more reliable description for interfacial metal/electrolyte 
chemistry in the present study, while the 휔 B97X readout is 
used to assess readout sensitivity.
The role of S8 is therefore best described as modulatory rather 
than essential. S8 participates in Li–S coordination and, in the 
r2SCAN redistribution metric, higher S8 concentration cor­
relates with faster lithium redistribution from the initial anode 
region. However, dendrite-­like lithium protrusions are also ob­
served in the absence of S8, and the tetrahedral order parameter 
does not show a monotonic S8-­concentration trend. These results 
indicate that PC and Otf− are also important drivers of interfa­
cial lithium restructuring. The added DFT benchmarks support 
the use of BASIS-­r2SCAN for structural and relative energetic 
trends in the selected lithium surface motifs, but a complete 
electrochemical dendrite-­growth mechanism would still require 
explicit treatment of electrode potential, charge-­state evolution, 
and electron-­transfer pathways.
