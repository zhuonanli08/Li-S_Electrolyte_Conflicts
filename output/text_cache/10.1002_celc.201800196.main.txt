Organic Polysulfides Based on SSS Structure as
Additives or Cosolvents for High Performance
Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Sui Gu,[a, b, c] Jun Jin,[a] Shangjun Zhuo,[b] Rong Qian,*[b] and Zhaoyin Wen*[a]
Lithium-sulfur (LiS) batteries are presently attracting immense
research interest on the global level due to their excellent
specific capacity and energy density. However, the practical
development of LiS batteries has so far been hindered by a
series of obstacles. To enhance the sulfur utilization rate and
facilitate capacity performance of LiS batteries, a new organo-
sulfide, dimethyl trisulfide (DMTS, CH3S3CH3), as electrolyte
additive or cosolvent was employed. Boosted capacities were
demonstrated with appropriate amount of DMTS. LiS batteries
with 5% and 10% DMTS delivered discharge capacities of 1207
(1st)/1009(100th) and 1648(1st)/1133(100th) mAh g1, respectively.
Such high capacities are particularly attractive for LiS batteries
towards high energy density devices. The methyl-terminated
polysulfides (DMTS and reaction products of DMTS/S8) were
supposed to contribute the extra capacity.
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (LiS) batteries have recently attracted great
attentions as promising energy storage devices due to their
high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAhg1) and energy
density (2600 Whkg1).[1] Also, elemental sulfur has advantages
of abundance, low cost and being environmentally benign.
Despite of these potentials, the practical application of LiS
batteries is challenging to be realized. The main obstacles are
the fast capacity fading and low utilization of sulfur, which are
attributed to the shuttle of intermediate polysulfides and
insulating nature of sulfur. Large volume changes during
cycling are known as another reason for poor long-term cycle
life of batteries. Great efforts have been made to improve
electrochemical performance of LiS batteries, including various
carbon additives[2] to enhance cathode conductivity; chemical
adsorbents[3] or physical barriers[4] to trap polysulfides within
cathode and accelerate the Li2S conversion; electrolyte addi-
tions[5] and modified anodes[6] to prevent lithium from corrosion
of dissolved polysulfides. Improved performance has been
demonstrated through these approaches. However, addressing
the key issues of LiS batteries still remains to be a big
challenge.[7]
To enhance the sulfur utilization rate and facilitate capacity
retention of LiS batteries, tremendous efforts have been made
by trapping polysulfides within cathodes, or, inversely, introduc-
ing functional additives in electrolyte, like polysulfides,[8] to
establish a dynamic equilibrium between dissolution and
precipitation of polysulfides. Through such an approach, the
sulfur loss can be effectively mitigated and the overall capacity
of the LiS cells also can be increased in polysulfides-rich
electrolyte. Recently, a new strategy, introduction of organo-
sulfide (CH3S2CH3, DMDS) into electrolyte for LiS cells, has
demonstrated extreme high capacity delivery (almost double
the value obtained in typical batteries) with a new electro-
chemical reduction pathway.[9] Sulfur was found to react with
CH3S2CH3 and the as-formed methyl-polysulfides boosted the
capacity performance. This strategy demonstrates great appli-
cation potential in high energy density storage. Our work aims
to select a new derivative with polysulfide bonds structure
(methylSSnSmethyl, n1) as electrolyte co-solvent or
additive. By adding them into ether-based electrolyte, the
improved capacity performance of the LiS cells will be
obtained.
Herein, we report a new organosulfide, dimethyl trisulfide
(DMTS, CH3S3CH3), as electrolyte additive or co-solvent for LiS
batteries. A small amount of DMTS added into electrolyte
brings boosted discharge capacity of LiS cells (sulfur cathode
with 5%, 10%, 25% and 50% DMTS delivered initial discharge
capacities of 1207, 1648, 2118 and 2234 mAhg1, respectively).
Such high capacities are particularly attractive for LiS batteries
towards high energy density devices. Moreover, a cross-linked
morphology with chemical compositions of CH3SLi and CH3SnLi
was identified on cathodes surface, which is found to be
evolved highly reversible. The methyl-terminated polysulfides
(DMTS and reaction products of DMTS/S8) were supposed to
contribute the extra capacity. Appropriate amount of DMTS is
favorable not only to the capacity improvement but also the
capacity stability

2. Results and Discussion
Figure 1 shows the electrochemical performances of LiS
batteries with different amounts of DMTS (1%, 3%, 5%, 10%,
25%, and 50% by volume). The initial discharge-charge voltage
profiles are illustrated in Figure 1a. With the increase of DMTS
amounts, boosted discharge capacities are observed. The
battery with 10% DMTS electrolyte delivered a discharge
capacity of 1648 mAhg1, close to the theoretical value of
1675 mAhg1. As the DMTS increased to 25% and 50%, the
discharge capacities have more doubled than the value without
DMTS (962 mAhg1). The voltage profiles with DMTS also
behaved differently. At low contents (0%, 1%) of DMTS, the
discharge voltage curves contain two plateaus, corresponding
to the reduction of elemental sulfur to long-chain polysulfides
and the further reduction to Li2S2/Li2S.[10] As the ratio increased
to 3%, the first discharge plateau became slope, while the
second one elongated gradually and contributed the major
capacity. After 100 cycles (Figure 1b), the discharge curves still
comprised of a slope in the first reduction step and a long
plateau in the second reduction step (DMTS: 3%, 5%, 10%,
25%), suggesting
a reversible
redox reaction
for
DMTS-
containing LiS batteries. The cell with 50% DMTS was
deteriorated. The cycling performance and coulombic efficiency
of LiS batteries with different electrolytes are shown in
Figure 1c and 1d. In the DMTS-free sample, the discharge
capacity stabilized around 700 mAhg1 after 100 cycles. In
contrast, the batteries with 5%, 10% and 25% DMTS delivered
1009, 1140, 900 mAhg1, respectively. It is worth noticing that
high content of DMTS causes serious degradation of battery
performance. Capacity retention rates were only 43% and 0.4%
for 25% and 50% DMTS-containing batteries. The decreased
coulombic efficiency further proved the degradation of bat-
teries. We supposed that some kinds of particular electro-
chemical reaction in the LiS batteries with DMTS would
account for the complex performance characteristics. Chen et.al
ever reported the reduction process of DMDS (dimethyl
disulfide, CH3S2CH3),[9] which comprised of two steps, i.e., a
chemical reaction of elemental sulfur with DMDS, and a
reduction of dimethyl polysulfide to Li2S. In the DMTS system,
we proposed that several reactions contributed the complex
interconnected chemistry (as illustrated in Figure 2a).
To support the proposed reduction mechanism, we inves-
tigate the composition changes of S/C cathodes during
discharge in 10% DMTS-containing electrolyte. The initial
discharge profiles of LiS batteries are shown in Figure 2b,
including DODs of 5%, 10%, 20%, 40%, 75%, 100% (based on
the practical capacity after discharged to 1.8 V). Figure 2c
exhibits the XPS results of S2p spectra on the S/C cathodes at
different DODs. At 5% DOD (black curve), the XPS spectrum
shows two S2p contributions between 159~163 eV, ascribed to
the sulfide Li2S.[11] This assignment is much different from the
reduction products of typical S/C cathodes, since 5% DOD
belongs
to
the
range
of
first

plateau,
where
elemental sulfur is reduced to polysulfides, and Li2S only
emerges at the second discharge plateau.[12] As discharge
processed to 10% DOD and 20% DOD, the strong contribution
of S2p spectra between 159~163 eV demonstrates the increase

To further investigate the electrochemical characteristics of
cathode, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed at a scan rate
of 0.2 mVs1 for LiS batteries (or LiC battery) with DMTS-
containing electrolyte (Figure 3). Figure 3a and 3b show the
initial and 5th scans of sulfur-free carbon paper cathode with
10% DMTS. Under cathodic current, no sharp reductive peaks
are observed. There are one redox couple situated at 2.4/1.9 V
(vs. Li/Li+), corresponding to the de-intercalation and intercala-
tion of Li+ ions from/into DMTS-containing electrolyte. The
large separation of 0.5 V between the anodic and cathodic
peaks exhibits the slow kinetics. Figure 3c and 3d show the CV
curves of basic and DMTS-containing LiS batteries. The DMTS-
free LiS battery displays typical characteristics with two redox
couples, located at 2.35/2.30 V and 2.30/2.01 V, corresponding
to the redox reactions of S8/Li2Sn/Li2S.[13] The LiS battery with
10% DMTS exhibits one redox couple situated at 2.35/1.92 V,
fitting with the elongated second discharge plateau. The
negative current before cathodic peak implies the occurrence
of redox reaction and corresponds to the slope of first
discharge step on voltage profiles, which also emerged for
DMTS battery with sulfur-free cathodes, implying that DMTS or
its analogs (methyl-Sn-) contributes the first discharge step.
When DMTS increased to 50%, the CV plots exhibit the same
characteristics as the sulfur-free battery with DMTS electrolyte.
After 5 scans, the cathodic peaks shift to higher potential and
anodic peaks to lower potential for basic and 10% DMTS LiS
batteries, indicating an improvement of kinetics and reversi-
bility. Batteries with a high content of 50% DMTS or a sulfur-
free cathode have no obvious change for redox peaks. It is
conjectured that the difference is ascribed to the passivated
surface between electrolyte and cathode by redox products of
DMTS in electrolyte, which will be discussed later. Conclusively,
with high content of DMTS (50%) for LiS battery, the redox
reaction of DMTS dominated the electrochemical chemistry.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is employed
to evaluate the interfacial resistance and reversibility of LiS
batteries with DMTS electrolyte. The EIS of the LiS batteries
before and after 50 cycles are shown in Figure 4. In general, the
EIS plots are composed of two semicircles, corresponding to
the interfacial impedance (Ri) in the high frequency and charge
transfer impedance (Rc) in the medium frequency, and a sloping
straight line in the low frequency related to the Warburg
impedance.[14] The intercept of the semicircle in the high-
frequency region on the Z’ axis corresponds to the resistance of
electrolyte Re. Rc is determined by the rate of electrochemical
reactions on the sulfur electrode.[14a] As shown in Figure 4a, the
EIS plots before cycle are composed of only one semicircle for
LiS batteries with different amounts of DMTS. Ri is almost
disappear and the semicircle corresponds to the charge transfer
resistance (Rc).[2b] Obviously, the charge transfer process and
semi-infinite Warburg diffusion were dominant for fully charged
state of DMTS-containing LiS batteries before cycle. It can be
clearly observed that Rc increases dramatically with the increase
of DMTS content, implying that the surface electrochemical

Figure 5a–d show the surface morphology of fresh and
cycled S/C cathodes in LiS cells with basic and 10% DMTS
electrolyte, respectively. The surface morphology of cathode
cycled in basic electrolyte has no obvious change (Figure 5a-b).
The appearance of cathode also shows no difference on digital
photograph. The reduction products (Li2S/Li2S2) composited
with carbon matrix constitute the cycled cathode and have
little contribution on surface morphology (cut-off voltage of
discharge: 1.8 V).[15] While, the S/C cathode cycled in 10% DMTS
electrolyte shows a cross-linked morphology, which is much
different from that of in basic electrolyte (Figure 5c–d). The
digital photo also exhibits the difference with white appearance
of DMTS-containing cathode after discharging. Based on the
XPS analysis, the chemical composition of cathode surface is
mainly composed of CH3SLi and CH3SnLi, which contributed to
the distinctive morphology of cathode.

the XPS analysis has proved the main formation of Lithium
sulfides in this discharge step, Figure 2b. At the second

The rate capability of LiS cells with DMTS electrolytes was

to 0.5 C and 0.2 C, the capacities come back to 950 and
1046 mAhg1, respectively. Both excellent rate capability and
stability were evident owing to the good reaction kinetics in
the S/C cathode with low content DMTS. With 10% DMTS
(Figure 7b), the poor discharge capacity at high current density
of
3–5 C

changed back to 0.5 C. The rate performance of LiS cell with
25% DMTS was also tested and shown in Figure S4. The
unfavorable rate capability further implied that the high
content of DMTS is adverse to the reaction kinetics in the S/C
cathode for LiS cells.

3. Conclusions
In
summary,
we
reported
the
employment
of
thioether
(CH3S3CH3, DMTS) in electrolyte to boost the capacity of sulfur
cathode. Such high capacities are particularly attractive for LiS
batteries towards high energy density devices. The resulting
improvements of capacity is linked to the synergistic effect of
DMTS and elemental sulfur. It is also found that an appropriate
amount of DMTS is imperative for LiS cells because of the
increased total resistance and severe corrosion reactions caused
by by-products. Further effort into anode protection and
system optimization to fully demonstrate the potential of DMTS
for LiS batteries with superior capacity stability and rate
performance is still needed.
Experimental Section
Preparation of Electrolytes
Lithium salts were a mixture of LiTFSI (Lithium bis (trifluorometha-
nesulfonyl) imide, Sigma Aldrich, 99.99%) and LiNO3 (Sigma Aldrich,
99%) with desired concentration in solvents. The pristine solvent
was a mixture of DME (1,2-dimethoxyethane, 99.99%, Sigma
Aldrich) and DOL (1,3-dioxolane, 99.99%, Sigma Aldrich) (volume
ratio 1:1). LiTFSI and LiNO3 were dried under vacuum at 1008C
prior to use. The basic electrolyte used was 0.6 M LiTFSI and 2 wt%
LiNO3 in solvents of DOL/DME. Different amounts (1%, 2%, 3%,
5%, 10%, 25%, and 50% by volume) of dimethyl trisulfide (Sigma
Aldrich, 98%) were added into the basic electrolyte.

The slurry of a S/C composite was prepared by mixing 80 wt% S/
Ketjen Black (weight ratio 6:4), 5 wt% styrene-butadiene rubber,
5 wt% carboxymethyl cellulose and 10 wt% acetylene black by ball
milling in deionized water. Then the slurry was cast onto aluminum
foils and dried at 608C. The coated electrodes were cut into disks
with a diameter of 12 mm and further dried at 608C under vacuum
for 24 h. Two-electrode coin cells (CR2025) were assembled with
Celgard 2320 as separator and lithium foil as both the counter and
reference electrodes in argon-filled glovebox (content of oxygen
and water less than 1 ppm). The cells were equilibrated for 12 h
before operation. The cells were discharged and charged between
cutoff potential of 1.8 and 2.6 V in galvanostatic mode on LAND
CT2001A battery test system (Wuhan, China). Specific capacities
were calculated based on the mass of sulfur. Cyclic voltammograms
(CV) were recorded on an Autolab Electrochemical Workstation
(Wuhan, China) in the range of 1.8 to 2.6 V at scan rate of
0.2 mVs1. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) were also
measured on the Autolab Electrochemical Workstation with a
frequency response analyzer (FRA) between 1 MHz and 100 mHz
with amplitude of 10 mV. EIS and CV were measured by a three-
electrode configuration coin cell with lithium as both the reference
and counter electrodes. The typical sulfur loading of the cells was
about 1 mgcm2. The amount of electrolyte employed in cells was
fixed at 40 ml. All measurements were carried out at 258C.
Characterization
Surface morphology of electrodes (fresh and cycled) was charac-
terized by Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM, HITACHI S-3400 N).
The cycled electrodes were extracted after disassembling cells and
washed with DME in glovebox. X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy
(XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to detect the chemical
composition of electrode surfaces at different depths of discharge
(DOD). The binding energy values of S2p spectra were calibrated
using the C 1s peak (285 eV). Samples for SEM and XPS tests were
sealed in a vial filled with argon and quickly transferred to chamber
of testing system.
