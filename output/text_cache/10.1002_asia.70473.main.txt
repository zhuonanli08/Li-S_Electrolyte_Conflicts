ABSTRACT
Lithium–sulfur batteries are promising for meeting growing global energy needs and supporting sustainable development.
However, the shuttle effect is a key barrier to their wide use. Reducing Li+ ion solvation is an effective solution. In this study,
adiponitrile (ADN), featuring two highly electronegative cyano groups, forms a stable [Li(ADN)]+ complex that contracts the
solvation shell of Li+. Its moderate molecular size also helps form a denser interfacial protective film on the sulfur cathode, boosting
surface stability. Density functional theory (DFT) simulations show ADN’s cyano groups bind strongly to Li+, forming stable local
structures that suppress polysulfide migration and improve cycle stability. Experimentally, batteries with ADN retain 75% of initial
capacity after 120 cycles at 0.2 C and have a 744 mAh g−1 discharge capacity at 2 C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms
ADN-Li+ interaction and reveals ADN’s role in regulating the electrolyte’s solvation environment. This work provides new insights
for electrolyte design in next-generation Li–S batteries.

Introduction
In the realm of emerging energy storage technologies, lithium–
sulfur (Li–S) batteries have garnered considerable attention due
to their distinctive performance attributes [1–3]. As demand
intensifies globally for advanced storage systems, especially in
electric vehicles, unmanned aerial systems, electrified aviation,
and grid-scale applications, Li–S batteries stand out with a
theoretical energy density as high as 2600 Wh kg−1. Coupled
with their cost-effective raw materials and environmentally
sustainable characteristics, they present promising prospects for
widespread deployment [4–6]. Despite substantial advances in
fundamental research, the translation of Li–S batteries into
practical systems remains fraught with technical obstacles. A
primary issue arises during cycling: lithium polysulfides are
formed as the intermediate soluble species during redox reac-
tions. They can traverse the porous separator and interact with the
lithium metal anode, where they initiate parasitic reactions and
contribute to lithium surface degradation. This process not only
accelerates lithium corrosion but also fosters irregular lithium
dendrite formation [7–9]. As these dendrites grow, they may
eventually pierce the separator, create pathways for internal short
circuits, and pose significant safety threats. In extreme cases,
such failures can endanger user safety [10–12]. Moreover, the
accumulation of polysulfides on the lithium surface contributes
to the development of a fragile and unstable solid–electrolyte
interphase (SEI). Thus, the lifespan of the lithium metal is
reduced. Additionally, the dissolution and diffusion of sulfur-
containing intermediates severely hamper the active material’s
retention by leading to sulfur utilization loss and shortened
battery cycling life [13–15]. To address these persistent challenges,
extensive research efforts have been devoted to enhancing the sta-

bility and overall performance of Li–S batteries. On the cathode
side, Li and collaborators [16] developed a novel catalyst design by
anchoring Fe/Co dual single atoms onto porous nitrogen-doped
carbon derived from waste biomass. This “neighboring dual-atom
relay catalysis” approach marks a significant advancement in
cathode engineering. By accelerating the redox kinetics of lithium
polysulfides, the strategy effectively suppresses the shuttle effect
and thereby enhances both the long-term cycling stability and
charge–discharge efficiency of the battery. In related work, Sun
et al. [17] introduced zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) and
their derivatives into sulfur cathodes. Materials such as ZIF-67
and ZIF-8, recognized for their high surface area and porous
architecture, serve as physical confinement hosts for sulfur
species. These frameworks enhance ionic transport, mitigate
cathode volume expansion, and significantly improve specific
capacity and capacity retention. For the lithium metal anode,
several approaches have been proposed to mitigate corrosion
and stabilize the interface. These include the construction of
three-dimensional porous architectures [18–20], the application
of lithium-rich alloys [8, 21–24], and the fabrication of artificial
solid electrolyte interphases [25–28]. While these techniques can
enhance the mechanical and electrochemical stability of lithium
metal, they often demand substantial amounts of electrolyte and
involve intricate manufacturing processes, which limit their fea-
sibility for large-scale or commercial deployment. Compared to
the previously mentioned approaches, incorporating functional
additives into the electrolyte presents a more cost-effective and
scalable strategy for enhancing Li–S battery performance. Even
in small concentrations, such additives can substantially modify
the physicochemical properties of the electrolyte, leading to
improvements in overall battery behavior [29–31].
Electrolyte additives address the polysulfide shuttle effect and
performance degradation through diverse mechanisms. For
instance, metal nitrate-based additives such as lithium nitrate
(LiNO3) are known to facilitate the in-situ formation of a robust
SEI on the lithium anode. This protective layer helps miti-
gate side reactions with polysulfides and stabilizes the lithium
surface [32]. Organic sulfur-containing compounds, such as 4-
mercaptopyridine (4-Mpy), can modulate the redox conversion
pathways of polysulfides, promoting the formation of Li2S in
a three-dimensional morphology. This process enhances the
reaction kinetics and cyclic durability of the system [33]. Mean-
while, iodide-based additives such as lithium iodide have been
explored as redox mediators to expedite the conversion of soluble
polysulfides and improve electrochemical reversibility [34, 35].
Moreover, recent studies have shown that amino acids, such as
L-leucine (Leu), can reduce the degree of Li+ ion solvation when
used as electrolyte additives. Even at a concentration as low
as 0.1 wt.%, L-leucine has been reported to enhance electrolyte
wettability on separators and facilitate Li+ ion transport. Simulta-
neously, it accelerates the solidification of polysulfides into Li2S,
effectively reduces their diffusion, and suppresses the shuttle
effect [36]. These findings underscore the potential of weakly
solvating electrolyte additives to manipulate electrode–electrolyte
interactions. By decreasing the solubility and mobility of inter-
mediate polysulfides, such additives offer a viable pathway to
mitigate shuttle-related losses and extend battery lifespan [37–40].
Those earlier findings have demonstrated that nitrile-based addi-
tives offer a promising route for mitigating lithium polysulfide
shuttling and thereby extend the operational life of Li–S batteries.
The strong coordination interactions between cyano functional
groups and cobalt sites within the sulfur cathode led to the
formation of a stable interfacial layer. This barrier significantly
restricts the diffusion of polysulfide intermediates into the elec-
trolyte and enhances structural stability at the electrode surface.
Specifically, adiponitrile (ADN), characterized by its dual cyano
groups with high polarity, can engage in coordination with
Li+ ions. This interaction modifies the solvation environment
of Li+, which produces a more stable solvation sheath that
hinders the co-migration of polysulfides and Li+ ions toward
the anode. As a result, the suppressed shuttle effect markedly
leads to improved coulombic efficiency and enhanced structural
integrity of the electrodes. Experimental results highlight that
Li–S cells containing ADN additives maintained 75% of their
initial capacity after 120 cycles at a current density of 0.2 C.
Moreover, these cells demonstrated outstanding rate capability by
delivering a reversible discharge capacity of 744 mAh g−1 even at
a higher rate of 2 C. These outcomes indicate that ADN facilitates
weak solvation of Li+ ions, reducing the mobility of dissolved
polysulfides and significantly improving the cycling durability of
the battery system.

Results and Discussion
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization (Figure 1a–
d) reveals significant differences in the surface morphology of
lithium metal electrodes after cycling in various electrolyte sys-
tems. The electrode cycled in the baseline electrolyte (Figure 1a)
exhibits a rough and irregular surface, indicative of uneven
lithium deposition. This morphology is primarily attributed to
the severe polysulfide shuttle effect, which induces localized
corrosion of the lithium metal and thereby disrupts uniform
plating. The corresponding cross-sectional view (Figure 1b) shows
a loose and disordered SEI. This results from the repeated break-
down and regeneration of the interfacial layer during lithium
plating and stripping. This instability facilitates the growth of
irregular lithium dendrites and causes uncontrollable volumetric
fluctuations, posing substantial safety and performance chal-
lenges. In contrast, the lithium surface in cells containing ADN
additives (Figure 1c) appears significantly smoother and more
compact with minimal dendritic features. This improvement
highlights ADN’s ability to effectively suppress lithium corrosion
and promote the formation of a more uniform SEI. Figure 1d
further illustrates the reduced depth of lithium corrosion in ADN-
containing systems compared to the baseline. This underscores
the additive’s role in limiting polysulfide solubility and transport.
Such suppression of parasitic reactions at the anode interface
contributes to prolonged electrode lifespan. Figure 1e presents
the high-resolution Li 1s XPS spectra of both the cathode and
anode after 50 cycles. In the control cell, the peak at 54.9 eV
corresponds to Li–S bonds, 55.4 eV indicates the presence of
Li–N bonds, and the 56.0 eV peak is attributed to Li–F bonds,
which are the primary decomposition products of lithium salts.
In the cell with the AND additive, all Li 1s binding energies
shift to higher values after cycling. This shift arises from the
weak solvation effect of AND on Li+, which reduces the electron
cloud density around Li+ and increases its attraction to sur-
rounding electrons, thereby raising the binding energy [41]. These
findings collectively confirm that ADN acts as a key functional

Excessive solubility of polysulfides in the electrolyte can sig-
nificantly shorten the life cycle of Li–S batteries [42]. Density
functional theory (DFT) was employed as a technique to calculate
binding energies and examine the coordination patterns between
Li+ ions and various solvent molecules. As illustrated in Figure 2a,
the solvation structures of Li+ with increasing numbers of
coordinating molecules are modeled. These results show that,
as more solvent molecules attempt to coordinate with a single
Li+ ion, steric hindrance becomes increasingly pronounced.
Geometric optimization of these ion-solvent complexes revealed
that due to the bulky nature of the TFSI−anion, a Li+ ion can at
most associate with three TFSI−molecules to form a Li+(TFSI)3
complex. Conversely, it can coordinate with up to four ADN
molecules to form a Li+(ADN)4 structure. The total energies
(E) of these configurations are shown in Figure 2b. Among
them, the Li+(TFSI)(ADN)2 complex exhibits the highest energy,
approximately 0.28 Hartree, which is notably higher than that of
Li+(TFSI)2. This indicates that the introduction of ADN modifies
the solvation environment through transitioning the coordina-
tion structure from Li+(TFSI)2 to Li+(TFSI)(ADN)2. A schematic
comparison of the solvation structures before and after ADN
addition is shown in Figure 2c. ADN is a polar organic molecule
characterized by the presence of nitrile groups at both ends,
which contribute to its significant dipole moment. Compared to
conventional ether-based solvents, commonly used for dissolving
lithium polysulfides, ADN displays a distinct solvation behavior
due to differences in polarity and molecular interactions. In gen-
eral, solvation involves interactions such as electrostatic forces or
hydrogen bonding between solvent molecules and solute species,
forming a solvation shell. Ether solvents, like dimethoxyethane
(DME), possess lone-pair electrons on their oxygen atoms, which
can strongly coordinate with Li+ ions in polysulfide species to
enhance their solubility. Although ADN is highly polar, its nitrile
groups interact with Li+ ions in a manner different from ether
molecules and offer relatively weaker coordination strength.
When ADN is introduced into a solvent system that already sup-
ports polysulfide dissolution, it may disrupt the existing solvation
shell by competing with the original solvent molecules. As a
result, ADN can partially displace ether molecules and destabilize
the solvation shell around Li+ ions. This disruption decreases the
stability of lithium-polysulfide complexes and ultimately reduces
the solubility of polysulfides in the electrolyte. The solubility of
lithium polysulfides depends heavily on the integrity and strength
of their surrounding solvation environment. Altering the solvent
composition by adding ADN impacts the molecular structure
and stability of this shell, thereby suppressing the dissolution of
polysulfides in the liquid phase.
As observed in Figure 3a, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles
obtained at a slow scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 demonstrate the
beneficial role of the CEI layer derived from ADN in promoting
the conversion of lithium polysulfides. Distinct cathodic peaks
are observed at 2.30 and 2.03 V. They correspond to the sequential
reduction of long-chain polysulfides (S8 →Li2S4) and short-
chain species (Li2S4 →Li2S), respectively. The anodic peaks
appearing at 2.34 and 2.40 V reflect the reversibility of oxidation
processes. When compared to the control cells without ADN,
those containing this additive exhibit both higher peak current
densities and a narrower voltage separation between redox peaks
(318 mV). This indicates improved reaction kinetics. Since the
deposition of Li2S is the rate-limiting step during discharge and
is often hindered by its uneven nucleation and growth [43–
44], the earlier onset of Li2S formation in the presence of ADN
suggests that the additive promotes more uniform deposition.
Additionally, the internal redox kinetics can be evaluated through
Li+ ion diffusion characteristics [45]. As shown in Figure 3b,c, CV
measurements were conducted at varying scan rates of 0.1–0.5 mV
s−1. Using the Randles–Sevcik equation, Figure 3d–f presents
the linear fitting results between peak current densities and the
square root of scan rates at different potentials.

Figure 4a shows the constant current charge-discharge curves of
the battery at the current density of 0.1 C. The battery with the
ADN additive exhibited a higher discharge capacity (1182 mAh
g−1), while the control battery had a discharge capacity of only
989 mAh g−1. During discharge in Li–S batteries, the high voltage
plateau capacity (QH) corresponds to the conversion process from
S8 to soluble long-chain lithium polysulfides Li2S8, Li2S6, and
Li2S4), while the low voltage plateau capacity (QL) corresponds
to the transformation of long-chain polysulfides into insoluble
discharge products like Li2S2/ Li2S through a multistep reaction.
The ratio of these two capacities (QL/QH) can intuitively reflect
the degree to which both stages of the reaction occur. As shown
in Figure 4b, the ADN-added battery exhibited a higher (QL/QH)
value. This is because ADN induces weak solvation of Li+ ions,
which thins the solvation shell around Li+ and facilitates des-
olvation, thereby promoting the complete conversion of lithium
polysulfides. Figure 4c presents a comparison of the performance
differences between the two battery systems through multi-rate
tests conducted from 0.1 to 2 C (with a theoretical sulfur-
based capacity of 1675 mAh g−1). The battery with ADN showed
discharge capacities of 1133, 919, 799, 746, and 588 mAh g−1 at
0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C, respectively. Upon reverting the current
density to 0.2 C, the cell maintained a discharge capacity of
870 mAh g−1, corresponding to a retention rate of 94.7%. In
contrast, the control cell retained 91.9% of its capacity when
the current density was restored to 0.2 C following a series of
discharge cycles at varying rates. This is attributed to the addition
of ADN, which reduces the solvation of Li+ ions, suppresses the
dissolution of polysulfides, and effectively mitigates their loss,
resulting in excellent electrochemical reversibility of the ADN-
containing battery. By comparing the charge-discharge curves in

two batteries at different current densities are evident. The battery

The Li||Li symmetric cells were assembled to further investigate
the impact of the additive on the performance of the lithium
anode. Figure 6a illustrates the voltage–time profiles of various
symmetric lithium cells. The battery using the baseline electrolyte
exhibited significant voltage fluctuations and a high overpotential
of 82 mV. This phenomenon can be attributed to the instability
of the SEI layer, which requires continuous formation of a
new SEI layer. This leads to uneven lithium deposition and
thus higher overpotential. In contrast, the battery with ADN
additive displayed a lower overpotential of 18 mV and operated
stably for over 450 h (Figure 6b). This improvement is due
to the low solvation effect of the added ADN on Li+, which
reduces the solubility of lithium polysulfides. Furthermore, the
protective layer formed at the cathode inhibits the shuttle effect
of lithium polysulfides. As a consequence, parasitic reactions
between polysulfides and lithium metal are significantly reduced,
which protects the lithium anode by decreasing corrosion of
the lithium metal, and ultimately extends the lifespan of the
lithium metal. Consequently, the battery with ADN maintains
a low overpotential even after long cycles. To investigate the
changes in lithium deposition/dissolution characteristics, the
current density was varied (1–4 mA cm−2) while maintaining a
deposition capacity of 1 mAh cm−2, as shown in Figure 6c. As
indicated, the battery with ADN exhibited a low overpotential,
and the overpotential increased slowly with the increase in
current density. Additionally, as seen in Figure 6d, once the
current density was reverted to 1 mAh cm−2, the overpotential
remained low. However, the battery with the baseline electrolyte
exhibited a sharp increase in overpotential when the current
density reached 4 mAh cm−2, and the overpotential fluctuated
in subsequent cycles. Apparently, the results suggest that the
battery with the ADN additive has significant advantages in
the Li||Li symmetric cell cycling. This is because ADN reduces
the solubility of lithium polysulfides, suppresses their shuttle
effect, significantly mitigates the corrosion of lithium metal by
polysulfides, and extends the lifespan of lithium metal during
the cycling process. The addition of ADN plays a crucial role in
improving the battery’s cycling stability.
As shown in Figure S3a, impedance tests were conducted on
the batteries with and without the ADN additive after differ-

electrode interface of the battery, referred to as the interface

transfer resistance (Rct). Compared to fresh batteries, both the
untreated and ADN-added batteries displayed two semicircles
after cycling, indicating the formation of a CEI layer on the
electrode surface during cycling. It can be seen that, whether
before cycling, after 50 cycles, or after 100 cycles, the impedance
of the battery with ADN is lower than that of the battery
without the additive. This indicates that the addition of ADN
effectively improves the charge transfer rate. Additionally, the
Rct values of the ADN-added batteries are smaller, suggesting
that the CEI layer formed with ADN has better conductivity,
which can effectively promote the conversion of polysulfides.

increase in interface resistance. Additionally, the unstable CEI
layer continuously forms to consume the electrolyte. In contrast,
the impedance of the ADN-added battery increases more slowly,
as ADN helps form a stable, high-quality electrode interface

To further reveal the antioxidant capabilities of the electrolyte
and ADN, frontier molecular orbital energy calculations were
performed for DOL, DME, and ADN. The highest occupied
molecular orbital (HOMO) represents the molecular orbital with
the highest electron occupancy, and electrons are easily lost from
the HOMO. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)
represents the molecular orbital with the lowest energy level that
can accept electrons. The lower the LUMO energy, the stronger
the reduction ability of the molecule, while the higher the HOMO
energy, the stronger the oxidation ability of the molecule. As
shown in Figure S4a, the HOMO energies of DOL, DME, and
ADN are −6.70, −6.69, and −8.89 eV, respectively. The LUMO
energies of DOL, DME, and ADN are 2.36, 2.49, and 0.53 eV,
respectively. ADN has the lowest LUMO and HOMO energies,
indicating that it has a higher oxidation potential. As shown in
Figure S4b, from the independent gradient model (IGMH), it
can be concluded that van der Waals forces exist between ADN
and DOL/DME. Under this synergistic effect, the addition of
ADN enhances the overall oxidation resistance of the electrolyte.
As a result, the electrolyte is less likely to decompose during
long-term cycling, thereby improving the battery’s lifespan while
significantly enhancing the safety of the battery.
3
Conclusion
In summary, the participation of AND substantially enhances
the overall performance of Li–S batteries. First, the strongly
polar cyano group in the ADN molecular structure interacts
with Li+ ions and participates in the solvation of Li+ ions.
The Li+ ion solvation structure involving ADN is more stable,
which helps reduce the energy barrier for Li+ ion desolvation
at the electrode surface, allowing Li+ ions to more easily trans-
fer and undergo intercalation/deintercalation reactions at the
electrode-electrolyte interface. This leads to improved charging
and discharging efficiency, as well as enhanced rate performance
of the battery. Second, the solvation of Li+ ions involving ADN
forms a relatively stable structure around the Li+ ions, which
makes it more difficult for polysulfides to penetrate the solvation
shell and migrate to the lithium anode with lithium ions. This
significantly alleviates the polysulfide shuttle effect and leads to
an enhancement in the battery’s long-term cycling performance
as well as rate capability. After 120 cycles at 0.2 C, the battery
with ADN maintains a capacity retention of up to 75%. Moreover,
the battery with ADN exhibits exceptional high-rate charge and
discharge performance, retaining a discharge capacity of 744 mAh
g−1 at 2 C. This work demonstrates the potential of ADN as an
additive and provides insights into the design of advanced Li–S
battery electrolytes.
