ABSTRACT: Engineered electrolytes are critical for high-performance lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs). Present electrolyte selection
for simultaneously forming a stable bilateral solid−electrolyte interface (SEI) on both electrodes is largely heuristic. Although the
dielectric constant, viscosity, dipole moment, donor number, and orbital energy levels have all been used for electrolyte screening,
their effectiveness has not been systematically studied. Here, the effectiveness of these parameters was investigated using a key metric
of battery performance. Based on 51 mixed electrolytes investigated in this study, the enhanced stability of LSBs is attributed to the
mixed electrolytes’ high dielectric constant (ε > 35), which ensures the separation of the LiTFSI salt ions and potentially reduces
dendrite growth. However, 3 other high dielectric (ε > 35) mixed electrolytes based on diglyme exhibited a % drop of > ± 1.4%,
which is ∼2 times larger than the % drop exhibited by batteries with high dielectric (ε > 35) compositions devoid of diglyme.
Classical molecular dynamics indicated the presence of large diglyme molecules in the solvation shell, causing a ∼30% reduction in
diffusivity and adversely affecting battery performance. This study indicates that a high dielectric constant (ε > 35) along with the
absence of large molecules in the solvation shell are good criteria for LSB mixed electrolyte selection.
KEYWORDS: mixed electrolytes, high dielectric constant, small solvation shell, high diffusivity, capacity retention
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium metal batteries are of significant research interest for
next-generation energy storage devices because lithium is the
lightest known metal and has a very high theoretical capacity of
3860 mA h/g because of the largest negative reduction
potential (−3.04 V) versus the standard hydrogen electrode
among alkali metals.1 Combining the lithium anode and sulfur-
based cathode has been explored extensively as a strategy for
next-generation batteries because sulfur is abundant, cheap,
and has a high theoretical capacity of 1675 mA h/g.2 However,
lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been limited by rapid
capacity fade and low cycle life because of dead Li formation3
associated with dendrite growth and the polysulfide shuttle.4
Reducing dendrite growth in lithium metal batteries has
been a topic of intense interest.5 Controlling mass trans-
port,6−16 stabilizing the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) with
electrolyte additives17−20 and protective layers,21,22 creating
artificial SEI layers,23,24 using 3D structured anodes and 3D
mixed ionic conductors to reduce local current density,2,25 and
homogenizing lithium nucleation and flux1,26 are some of the
methods that have been investigated to control dendrite
growth.
The polysulfide shuttle in LSBs leads to rapid capacity
deterioration because of repeated reactions between poly-
sulfides and lithium. Moreover, carbonate solvents tend to
react irreversibly with polysulfides23 in batteries with tradi-
tional sulfur electrodes. It has been shown that using

micropores, and the assimilation of short-chain sulfur in
micropores.27 Different strategies, such as microporous-
mesoporous carbon−sulfur composite microstructures28−31
and sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathode,4 have been
adopted to mitigate the polysulfide shuttle effect in LSBs when
carbonate electrolytes are used. Fan et al.2 reported that while
ethers generally form a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
on the lithium metal anode, carbonates generally form a stable
SEI on the SPAN cathode. Hence, electrolytes obtained by
mixing ethers and carbonates generally were found to promote
bilateral SEI formation, which mitigates polysulfide shuttle and
dendrite growth.2 Thus, using mixed electrolytes that promote
bilateral SEI formation is one strategy that can tackle both the
issues associated with LSBs: polysulfide shuttle and dendrite
growth. However, as reported in Ref.2 not all combinations of
ethers and carbonates were measured to have a high capacity
retention, indicating that specific ethers and carbonates must
be combined carefully.
Alternatively, the energy gap between the energies of the
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), also known as the
HOMO−LUMO gap, has been used to predict the electrolytes'
ability to generate an SEI layer on the electrodes.2 This makes
it clear that simply mixing ethers and carbonates may not
guarantee a stable bilateral SEI. While stable bilateral SEI
formation may enable higher capacity retention, the overall
rate performance of a battery is governed by the diffusivity of
Li+ ions. Assuming a constant temperature, diffusivity itself
depends on the salt’s solubility, solvation shell structure, and
viscosity.32 Various parameters such as dielectric constant,33
dipole moment,34,35 donor number,36,37 and carbon−oxygen
ratio38,39 have been used traditionally to select electrolyte
solvents with higher salt solubility. Since developing LSBs that
not only adopt bilateral SEI strategy but also exhibit high
capacity is a priority, electrolyte selection involves using all the
above-mentioned parameters.
The use of several parameters listed above has made it
difficult to develop a coherent strategy for electrolyte selection.
In this work, batteries were prepared and tested with 51
electrolytes containing both ethers and carbonates (Figure 1)
to examine the effectiveness of the several parameters listed
above in predicting the battery’s rate performance. The
solvation shell structure was examined using classical molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations, and the diffusivity was calculated
to rationalize the experimental results. Using this experiment-
MD nexus, it is clear that for LSBs based on sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) electrodes, a high dielectric constant
(ε > 35) is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for
electrolyte selection to ensure high capacity retention and
excellent rate performance. Additionally, the electrolyte
components should be selected wisely so that the solvation
shell structure radius is not too large, as it may lead to sluggish
Li+ ion diffusion kinetics, which further affects battery
performance.
2. METHODS
2.1. SPAN Cathode Preparation. Initially, elemental S and
polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 150,000) were mixed uniformly in a
mass ratio of 3:1. SPAN was synthesized at 450 °C in an N2
atmosphere by chemical vapor deposition. The temperature was
raised at 5 °C/min and then held constant at 450 °C for 6 h.
Synthesized SPAN (36.49% S, 15.74% N, 0.5% H, 41.48% C) was
then mixed with carboxymethyl cellulose and carbon black in a mass
ratio of 70:15:15. The resulting mixture was dissolved in water, and
the solution was stirred overnight. The resulting slurry was then
coated on carbon-coated aluminum foil to make the cathode, which
was first air-dried and then dried at ∼100 °C for 12 h in an oven.
2.2. Electrolyte selection. All the tested electrolytes have a
concentration of 1 M and use lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI) as the salt.
2.3. Battery testing. One of the major mechanical causes for
capacity degradation in LSBs is dendrite growth at higher C rates.40
However,40 to ensure that LSBs with mixed electrolytes perform well
even at both higher and lower C rates, rate performance was tested by
cycling the batteries (CR2032 type coin cells with SPAN cathode, a
Celgard polypropylene separator, and lithium metal anode) at several
C rates. Specifically, after preconditioning the batteries at 0.1C, they
were cycled for 5 cycles each at 0.25C, 1C, 7.5C, and 1C, and 10
cycles at 0.25C. The % drops in average capacity at 0.25C for the
initial 5 and final 10 cycles were used to evaluate the rate performance
of the battery across different C rates.
2.4. Molecular Dynamics. Classical MD simulations were run
using LAMMPS (Large Scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel
Simulator).41 A nonpolarizable force field comprising bonded and
nonbonded interactions was used for the simulations.42 Parameters

prepared by mixing ethers (1,2 dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3
dioxolane (DOL)), and carbonates (propylene carbonate
(PC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), ethylene carbonate (EC),

lithium metal anode and the SPAN cathode,2 DME50DOL50 is
mixed with PC/DMC/EMC such that the compositions have
the formula CarbonatexDME(100−x)/2DOL(100−x)/2 where 0 ≤x
≤100 in increments of 10. Additionally, batteries were

4.1. Dipole moment. Solvents with higher dipole
moments enable higher salt solubility,53,54 which is expected
to enhance ionic conductivity, rate performance, and capacity
retention. The poor correlation shown in Figures S6a, S7a and
S8a and Table 1 may result from the fact that even nonpolar
solvents with polar bonds can exhibit salt solubility.
4.2. Donor number. Donor number is defined as the
negative of the enthalpy of the 1:1 adduct formation reaction
between the chosen compound and a standard Lewis acid
SbCl5 in a solvent with zero donor number (1,2 dichloro-
ethane). Hence, a high donor number indicates a higher
propensity to donate electrons, implying that a solvent with a
high donor number could coordinate with lithium cations
easily. However, a high donor number can also promote
polysulfide dissolution, thus leading to poor capacity retention

4.3. C/O Ratio. A higher C/O ratio (i.e., carbon to oxygen
ratio) is expected to reduce the polysulfide shuttle problem in
LSBs38,39 because a higher number of O atoms increases
lithium polysulfide solubility, thus increasing the polysulfide
shuttle. However, Figures S6c, S7c, S8c, and Table 1 show a
poor correlation between capacity retention and % drop with
C/O ratio. This can be explained by the fact that in
multisolvent mixtures, solubility and solvation shell structures
result from a complex interplay between various solvents and
salt ions. While a high C/O ratio is expected to reduce
polysulfide shuttle, it may also reduce the solvating ability
because O is a highly electronegative element that can act as a
co-ordination site for the formation of the solvation shell
structure. Furthermore, in multisolvent mixtures, the C/O
ratio of a single solvent or the volume-averaged C/O ratio of
the solvent mixture may still not be a good criterion as the
proportion of solvents in the solvation shell structure may not
necessarily correspond to the proportion of solvents in the
mixture. Additionally, the C/O ratio fails to account for
solvent−solvent interactions and hence is a poor predictor.
4.4. HOMO, LUMO, and HOMO−LUMO Gap. The
HOMO energy, LUMO energy, and HOMO−LUMO energy
gap have all been used to screen electrolytes based on their
electrochemical stability window (ESW). However, these
metrics fail to account for solvation energy, solvent−solvent,
and solvent−salt interactions. While determining the ESW for
electrolytes, it is necessary to account for the oxidation and
reduction stability for all the electrolyte species, such as
solvated cations, free salt anions, and free solvent molecules.
HOMO energy, LUMO energy, and HOMO−LUMO energy
gap only account for the ESW of free solvent molecules, thus
leading to a poor correlation with both capacity retention and
percentage drop, as seen from Figures S6d−f, S7d−f, S8d−f,
and Table 1. Accurate calculation of HOMO, LUMO energies
and HOMO−LUMO gap for all the entities (e.g., solvation
shell structures with varying numbers of solvent molecules
and/or anions, free solvent molecules, free anions, etc.) present
within the electrolyte can sometimes involve a mixture of
computationally costly MD simulations and ab initio DFT
calculations. MD simulations would be required to identify the
‘entities’ for which HOMO−LUMO calculations need to be
performed. Furthermore, such MD simulations should be
performed near the electrode−electrolyte interface as
“solvation shell structures” deform in the vicinity of electrodes
due to the presence of polar solvents and/or cations/
anions.53,54 Once, the 'entities' are identified, ab initio
calculations using Molecular Orbital (MO) theory may be
performed to obtain the HOMO, LUMO energies and the
HOMO−LUMO gap. An alternative method to calculate the
HOMO, LUMO energies of the identified entities, especially
the solvation shell structures, could be utilizing the
“thermodynamic” cycle proposed by Kazemiabnavi et al.,55
which utilizes the solvation energies and the previously known
HOMO, LUMO energies of the solvents to calculate the
HOMO, LUMO energies and the HOMO−LUMO gap for the
“solvation shell structures”. However, this is not the norm and
has not yet been widely adopted by the literature because it is a
2 step process involving costly computations in either case.
Currently, to the best of our knowledge, there is no easy
solution to this problem other than performing cyclic
voltammetry or linear sweep voltammetry experiments to
obtain the electrochemical stability window.
Thus, from among the several metrics that have been used
for electrolyte selection, only the dielectric constant seems to
be a metric that could be used for electrolyte selection for Li-
SPAN batteries. A high dielectric constant, and the absence of
large molecules from the solvation shell structure are the
criteria that may be used for next-generation electrolyte
selection and design for Li-SPAN batteries.
In recent years, one of the major thrusts in electrolyte
engineering for the LSBs has been the development of
electrolytes that support fast diffusion and stable SEI formation
on both electrodes. A recent review article by Miao et al.56
highlights the trends in developing ether-based and carbonate-
based highly concentrated, localized highly concentrated
electrolytes. However, Miao et al.56 also pointed out the
need for better transport properties as concentrated electro-
lytes typically lag behind in this respect. Because our study is
originally based on the “bilateral SEI strategy”, which utilizes
both ethers and carbonates (two of the most commonly used
functional groups for LSB electrolytes), this study is
proceeding in the correct direction. Furthermore, in the
current manuscript, we have clearly identified electrolyte
design conditions to enable “faster diffusion” by focusing on
having a “smaller solvation shell”. Moreover, recently used
solvents for electrolytes such as triethyl phosphate,57 fluoro-
ethylene carbonate, and methyl propionate58 have relatively
high dielectric constants (13.2, 78.4, 6.3).58−60 Additionally,
intrinsically safe tetrapropoxysilane-based electrolytes with a
high dielectric constant have been used for LSBs at 80 °C.61
The fact that high dielectric constant organic electrolytes with
functional groups other than carbonates and ethers (the ones
that the current manuscript focuses on), such as esters and
silane-based ethers, have also been demonstrated to work well
in LSBs exemplifies the versatility of the proposed condition of
high dielectric constant.
Therefore, novel future solvents for next-generation electro-
lytes may be selected based on similarity from the Grover
feature data set as long as they follow the general rule of having
a high dielectric constant (ε > 35) and a low radius of gyration.
5. CONCLUSIONS
Various parameters such as dielectric constant, viscosity, dipole
moment, HOMO−LUMO energies, donor number, number of
oxygen atoms, c/o ratio, and relative solvating power have
been used in the literature for electrolyte screening.
Furthermore, the analysis shows that specifically for SPAN
cathode-based LSBs, the dielectric constant is directly
correlated with capacity retention at high C rates. Additionally,
it was found that a high dielectric constant also implied low %
drop. Hence, to overcome the challenge of long experimental
times, % drop was used as the metric for electrolyte selection.
Newly identified high dielectric (ε > 35) compositions such as

show that the presence of a bulky solvent in the solvation shell
significantly reduces the diffusivity, thus affecting the battery
performance. Thus, a high dielectric constant is a necessary but
not sufficient condition for LSB electrolyte selection. Along
