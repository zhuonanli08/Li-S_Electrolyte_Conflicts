ABSTRACT: Metal−sulfur (M−S) batteries, including Li−S and Na−S systems, are
promising next-generation energy storage technologies due to their high energy density
and cost-effectiveness. However, their practical application is limited by complex redox
reactions and the dissolution of intermediate polysulfides in ether-based electrolytes,
leading to capacity fading and poor cyclability. In this work, we employ a combined
approach using operando UV−vis spectroscopy and rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE)
voltammetry to investigate the sulfur redox mechanisms in Li−S and Na−S batteries
with 1 M LiTFSI and 1 M NaPF6 in DOL/DME (1:1) electrolytes, respectively. UV−vis
spectroscopy has been used to determine the lithium polysulfide intermediates formed
during the battery cycling in previously published reports. Our study extends its application to Na−S systems and, for the first time,
integrates it with RRDE to directly detect and quantify soluble intermediates by calculating the number of electrons involved in the
reduction processes in both systems (i.e., Li−S and Na−S).
KEYWORDS: Sulfur Redox Chemistry (Li/Na−S), Operando UV−Vis, Rotating Ring-Disk Electrode (RRDE),
Solvent-Dependent Redox Chemistry
■INTRODUCTION
Over the past two to three decades, lithium-ion (Li-ion)
batteries have experienced remarkable advancements, becom-
ing the dominant energy storage solution for a wide array of
portable electronic devices. This evolution has significantly
contributed to the enhancement of modern human lifestyle
and technological convenience.1−3 The viability of Li-ion
batteries as an alternative for large-scale energy storage
applications such as electrified transportation systems and
grid-scale energy storage infrastructure has garnered substantial
attention across both academic research and industrial
innovation sectors.4,5 Despite these successes, Li-ion batteries,
which predominantly operate through intercalation-based
electrochemical mechanisms, are inherently limited in terms
of achievable energy density.6−8 Furthermore, the elevated cost
and limited availability of critical raw materials, such as lithium
and transition metals (notably cobalt), pose serious economic
and sustainability challenges, thereby constraining their
scalability for extensive stationary and mobile storage
applications.9
In contrast, metal−sulfur (M−S) battery systems, including
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) and sodium−sulfur (Na−S) chem-
istries, have emerged as promising next-generation alternatives
due to their potential to deliver significantly higher theoretical
specific capacities, estimated to be approximately two to three
times greater than those of conventional Li-ion batteries.10−18
However, the practical implementation and commercial
development of M−S battery technologies are impeded by
several critical challenges. These include (a) the inherently low
electrical conductivity of elemental sulfur, which necessitates
the use of conductive additives, and (b) the dissolution and
uncontrolled shuttling of soluble intermediate polysulfide
species (Li2Sx or Na2Sx, where 4 ≤x ≤8) between the
cathode and anode, which leads to capacity fading and poor
cycle stability.19−25
In addition to the well-documented challenges associated
with lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, the electrochemical
behavior of metal−sulfur systems, particularly with regard to
their rate performance and cycle stability, is highly influenced
by the kinetics of intermediate polysulfide species. These
include their formation, dissolution, and transport within the
electrode and electrolyte during both charging and discharging
processes.26−29 Consequently, a comprehensive understanding
of the reaction kinetics of these intermediate polysulfides in
varying electrochemical environments is crucial for optimizing
the cell performance. In this context, Lu et al. employed
rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) voltammetry, a powerful
technique that enables the quantitative evaluation of electron
transfer numbers involved in redox processes and the intrinsic
catalytic activity associated with the sulfur reduction reaction.30
Similarly, Lacey et al. utilized a pouch cell configuration to

elemental sulfur (S8) to lithium tetrasulfide (Li2S4) proceeds
via rapid electrochemical kinetics, whereas the subsequent
formation of lithium sulfide (Li2S) likely occurs through a non-
electrochemical dissociation pathway rather than a direct
electrochemical reduction. Furthermore, the overall rate
capability of Li−S batteries appears to be governed by a
combination of mass transport constraints and limited electron
transfer kinetics. To probe the dynamic behavior of sulfur
species during cell operation, Wu et al. applied Raman
spectroscopy to monitor the kinetics of both the formation
and decomposition of these intermediates during charge and
discharge cycles.32 Their study revealed that the rate constants
governing the generation and disappearance of short-chain
polysulfides and elemental sulfur are nearly identical during
both charging and discharging, suggesting that these redox
processes are effectively quasi-reversible. While significant
insights into the Li−S system have been gained through these
investigations, a similarly detailed kinetic study for the
sodium−sulfur (Na−S) battery system remains largely absent
in the existing literature.
The sulfur redox process involves a sequential trans-
formation across various oxidation states, transitioning from
higher-order polysulfides to lower-order polysulfides. The
stability of these intermediate polysulfides is strongly
influenced by both the donor number and the dielectric
constant of the solvent medium. Zou et al. demonstrated that
solvents possessing moderate to high dielectric constants
combined with elevated donor numbers such as the DOL/
DME mixture can effectively stabilize the S4
2−intermediate
species.33 Additionally, the interaction between sulfur-based
intermediates and counter cations (e.g., Li+ and Na+) varies
due to the differences in their polarizability and coordination
behavior. This motivates our investigation into the fundamen-
tal nature and speciation of sulfur redox intermediates in the
presence of different alkali metal cations (Li+ and Na+). To
achieve this, we employ operando UV−vis spectroscopy
coupled with rotating disk electrode (RDE) measurements to
elucidate their behavior under electrochemical conditions.
In this study, we investigate the redox behavior of metal−
sulfur systems, specifically lithium−sulfur (Li−S) and sodium−
sulfur (Na−S) chemistries, using rotating ring-disk electrode
(RRDE) voltammetry and operando UV−vis spectroscopy in
electrolytes composed of 1 M LiTFSI and 1 M NaPF6 in a
DOL/DME (1:1) solvent mixture. RRDE voltammetry has
been extensively employed to probe the reaction kinetics and
mass transport characteristics of the oxygen reduction reaction
(ORR), particularly in the context of metal−air batteries.34,35
Similarly, UV−vis spectroscopy has been widely utilized in
earlier studies to identify and monitor lithium polysulfide
intermediates formed during the electrochemical cycling of
Li−S batteries.36−40 In this work, we extend the application of
operando UV−vis spectroscopy to elucidate the mechanistic
pathways of sulfur reduction and oxidation in both Li−S and
Na−S cells. By integrating RRDE measurements, we
quantitatively assess the number of electrons transferred
during the sulfur reduction process and determine the specific
electrochemical activity of sulfur in both systems. This
combined analytical approach offers a deeper understanding

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Chemicals. Anhydrous 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, >99%), 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME, >99%), lithium bis(trifluorometh-
anesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, purity ∼99%), sodium hexafluor-
ophosphate (NaPF6, purity > 99.99%), acetonitrile (99.8%
anhydrous, Sigma-Aldrich), AgNO3 (99.99% metal basis,
Sigma-Aldrich), and elemental sulfur powder (S8, purity
99.99%) are purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without
further purification.
Operando UV−Vis Spectroscopy. The schematic of the
operando UV−vis measurements cell and the detailed
experimental setup are discussed in our previously published
work.46 Platinum mesh is employed as a working electrode,
and platinum wire is used as a counter electrode. The
nonaqueous Ag/Ag+ reference electrode consists of a glass tube
filled with 0.01 M AgNO3 and 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium
perchlorate (TBAP) in dry acetonitrile. For Li−S studies, 4.0
mM S8 and 1.0 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) are dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1 volume ratio). For
Na−S studies, 4.0 mM S8 and 1.0 M sodium hexafluor-
ophosphate (NaPF6) are dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1 volume
ratio). Both solutions are stirred overnight to dissolve the
sulfur into the electrolyte. The cell is assembled in an argon
filled glove box Mbraun (H2O < 0.5 ppm, O2 < 0.5 ppm). An
AutoLab UB spectrophotometer (Metrohm) in DIO trigger
mode with a deuterium/halogen light source is used for all
operando measurements. The electrochemical cyclic voltam-
metry and UV−vis measurements are collected simultaneously.
The scan rate for cyclic voltammetry (CV) is kept at 1.0 mV/s,
and UV spectra are collected after every 30 s.
Rotating Ring-Disk Electrode (RRDE) Measurements.
The working electrode consists of a PTFE embedded Pt disk
that is 5.0 mm in diameter surrounded by a platinum ring with
an internal diameter of 6.5 mm and an external diameter of 7.5
mm. Before use, the ring-disk electrode is cleaned by an
alumina suspension and polished. The counter electrode
consists of a platinum wire. The reference electrode used is
nonaqueous Ag/Ag+. 4 mM sulfur and 0.1 M LiTFSI are
dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1 volume ratio) and stirred
overnight for the study of the Li−S system. 4 mM sulfur and
0.1 M NaPF6 are dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1 volume ratio)
and stirred overnight for the study of the Na−S system. The
RRDE working electrode, the Pt wire counter electrode, and
the reference electrode are assembled inside a glove box in an
argon atmosphere (H2O < 0.5 ppm, O2 < 0.5 ppm). Once the
assembly is completed, the electrochemical cell is taken out of
the glove box, and the working electrode rod is mounted onto
the rotator. All electrochemical measurements are performed
using an AutoLab (Metrohm) electrochemical workstation.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Redox Behavior of Sulfur in Na−S System. UV−vis
spectroscopy, along with cyclic voltammetry, is employed to
study the redox behavior of sulfur in the Na−S system, as
shown in Figure 1. This method offers simultaneous
correlation between the changes in the chemical states of
sulfur and the corresponding charge/discharge voltage. For this
measurement, platinum mesh is used as a working electrode,
and the Ag/Ag+ reference electrode consists of a glass tube
filled with 0.01 M AgNO3 and 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium
perchlorate (TBAP) in acetonitrile. Figure 1a shows the cyclic
voltammetry curve of the 4 mM S8 + 1 M NaPF6 in DOL/
DME electrolyte vs Ag/Ag+. Two reduction steps (R1 and R2)
with peaks at −1.19 and −1.37 V consequently lead to the
transformation of higher-order polysulfides to lower-order
analogues, and two corresponding oxidation steps (O1 and O2)
are observed at −1.15 and −0.66 V in the cyclic voltammo-
gram due to the reversible transformation to sulfur. The
corresponding operando UV−vis spectra are plotted for each
step (Figure 1b−e).
Figure 1b shows the UV−vis spectra of the first reduction
wave (R1) in the voltage range of −1.1 to −1.25 V, as shown in
Figure 1a. The UV−vis spectra shown here focus on the
voltage regions corresponding to the main oxidation (O1 and
O2) and reduction (R1 and R2) features. These regions capture
the key redox transitions of sulfur in the cell. While we do not
show the full spectral evolution, these selected features are
representative of the main mechanistic steps, allowing for clear
interpretation of the sulfur conversion process. At the
beginning of the discharge at −1.1 V, the UV−vis spectra
show a single broad band at ∼265 nm. This is attributed to
presence of sulfur in the electrolyte. As the reduction process
moves from −1.1 to −1.25 V, the UV spectra show a
continuous increase in the absorbance value, and after −1.15
V, the broad band splits into two new bands at 230 and 270
nm. These bands are attributed to the reduction of sulfur to
higher-order polysulfides (S6
2−and S8
2−). The intensity of the
two new bands at 230 and 270 nm increases from −1.15 to
−1.25 V, which confirms the complete conversion of sulfur to
higher-order polysulfides during the first discharge in the 4
mM S8 + 1 M NaPF6 in DOL/DME electrolyte, in line with
previously published literature.33 Figure 1c shows the UV−vis
spectra of the second reduction wave (R2) in the voltage range
of −1.3 to −1.75 V, as shown in Figure 1a. Compared to the
first reduction wave (R1), where the absorbance bands at ∼230
and ∼260 nm reach intensities of ∼4 (arbitrary units), these
same bands are markedly attenuated in the second reduction
wave (R2), decreasing to ∼1.5−1.7. This attenuation indicates
the further reduction of higher-order polysulfides formed
during R1 into lower-order species, primarily Na2S2 and Na2S.
Because Na2S2 and Na2S are insoluble discharge products, they
cannot be detected by UV−vis spectroscopy. The formation of
such solid species during sulfur reduction in Li−S systems
using DOL/DME electrolytes has also been confirmed in
previous studies by operando XAS.41
Two clear oxidation steps (O1 and O2) are observed by the
operando UV−vis spectra during the oxidation scan conducted
in the 4 mM S8 + 1 M NaPF6 in DOL/DME electrolyte, as
shown in Figure 1a. Figure 1d shows the operando UV−vis
spectra of the first oxidation wave (O1) between −1.35 and
−1.1 V. Similar to the R2 step, we do not observe any changes
in the O1 step. This is due to adsorption of the solid Na2S2/
Na2S on the electrode surface, which is also observed from the
cyclic voltammogram (Figure S1). The two UV−vis bands
appear with slightly less intensity, which shows the presence of
Na2S2 and Na2S in the electrolyte after the first oxidation step.
Figure 1e shows the operando UV−vis spectra of the second
oxidation wave (O2) between −0.85 and −0.25 V. At the
beginning of charge, two absorption bands appear at 230 and
260 nm. The intensity of these absorption bands is almost 2
times higher than that of the bands in the O1 step, which shows

band appearing at about 210−215 nm in both Figure 1b (later
in R1) and Figure 11e (earlier in O1). Assigning this band
uniquely from UV−vis data alone is difficult because both
elemental sulfur (S8) and some polysulfide electronic
transitions absorb in the near-UV, and peak positions are
sensitive to chain length, charge state, solvent, and
concentration. In many experimental UV−vis studies, S8 ring
(elemental sulfur) contribution appears in the ∼220−250 nm
region, and computational studies predict UV transitions for
various Sn species across the 200−400 nm window. By
contrast, several shorter-chain polysulfide signatures (e.g., S4
2−

wavelengths in the visible region. Accordingly, the feature at
∼210−215 nm in our spectra is most plausibly attributed
either to residual or formed elemental sulfur (S8) or to UV
transitions of short-chain polysulfide (S4
2−) species; we
therefore report it as an unresolved short-wavelength band
and note that definitive identification would require comple-
mentary characterization (e.g., operando Raman spectroscopy,
XAS, or ex situ LC-MS).42,43
These results confirm that the stepwise redox conversion of
sulfur in Na−S batteries involves intricate electrochemical
transformations among elemental sulfur (S8) and soluble
higher-order polysulfides (e.g., Na2S6 and Na2S8). Our
experimental observations provide valuable insight into the
sequential reduction and oxidation processes of soluble species,
highlighting the importance of understanding the full redox
pathway for optimizing reaction kinetics, mitigating polysulfide
shuttling, and enhancing the electrochemical reversibility and
overall performance of Na−S battery systems.

Redox Behavior of Sulfur in Li−S System. An
experiment similar to that performed for the Na−S system is
conducted for the Li−S system. Both operando UV−vis
spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry are used to find the
intermediate species in the reduction/oxidation reaction
during charge and discharge of the electrochemical cell. Figure
2a shows the cyclic voltammetry curve of the Li−S system
obtained in the 4 mM S8 + 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME
electrolyte. Two reduction steps (R1: −1 V; R2: −1.38 V (vs
Ag/Ag+)) and two corresponding oxidation steps (O1: −1.34
V; O2: −0.66 V) are observed in the cyclic voltammogram for
a redox process similar to that observed for the Na−S system.
However, the second reduction process and its corresponding
oxidation are separated by 34 mV, indicating a reversible
electrochemical process likely associated with the surface
adsorption and formation of solid-state Li2S and Li2S2. This
phenomenon is discussed in greater detail later. Interestingly,
the formation kinetics of polysulfide species such as S8
2−and
S6
2−are faster and more thermodynamically favorable in the
presence of Li+ compared to Na+,52,53 as clearly demonstrated
in Figure S1. The onset potential for the Li2S8/Li2S6 redox
couple is −0.87 V, which is −0.2 V more than that observed
for the sodium system. In the presence of sodium
hexafluorophosphate, the adsorption energy of Na2S2/Na2S
on the electrode surface is significantly higher.54 As a result,
during the reverse (oxidation) scan, the voltammetric curve
intersects the reduction curve, a feature also confirmed by
operando UV−vis spectroscopy, as shown in Figure 1. The
corresponding operando UV−vis spectra for each electro-
chemical step for the Li−S system are presented in Figures
2b−e.
Figure 2b shows the operando UV−vis spectra of the first
reduction wave (R1) in the voltage range of −0.8 to −1.25 V,
as shown in Figure 2a. The first reduction wave (R1) starts at
−0.8 V. The UV−vis spectra show the increase in the intensity
with increasing depth of discharge. At the end of the first
discharge wave (R1), two bands at 240 and 270 nm are
observed. These bands are attributed to the reduction of sulfur
(S8) to higher-order polysulfides (S6
2−and S8
2).
Figure 2c presents the operando UV−vis absorption spectra
corresponding to the second reduction process (R2), occurring
within the potential window of −1.25 to −1.75 V, as shown in
Figure 2a. The onset of the second reduction wave (R2) is
observed at −1.25 V. The spectral evolution during this stage,
shown in Figure 2c, reveals two characteristic absorption bands
in a spectral region similar to that seen during the first
reduction (R1). These bands remain constant as the potential
is swept more negative from −1.25 to −1.75 V. Notably, no
emergence of new absorption features is detected during this
interval.
This spectral behavior suggests that higher-order polysulfide
species (e.g., S6
2−and S8
2−) are further electrochemically
reduced to insoluble discharge products, primarily lithium
disulfide (Li2S2) and lithium sulfide (Li2S), which are optically
inactive in the UV−vis region. The formation of these solid-
state species, although not UV−vis active, has been previously
confirmed through X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) at
the final discharge state, as reported in the literature.41

Figure 2d displays the operando UV−vis spectra associated
with the first oxidation process (O1), as indicated in Figure 2a.
Two absorption bands reappear in the same spectral region
previously attributed to the reduction step R2, although with a
lower intensity. This indicates partial reoxidation of Li2S2 and
Li2S back to soluble polysulfide intermediates, which remain in
the electrolyte following the initial charging phase. Figure 2e
illustrates the operando UV−vis spectra during the second
oxidation wave (O2), occurring between −0.85 and −0.25 V.
At the onset of this stage, two pronounced absorption bands
emerge at approximately 230 and 260 nm. The intensity of
these bands is nearly double that observed during O1, implying
a more extensive oxidation of solid Li2S2 and Li2S back into
higher-order polysulfides (S8
2−and S6
2−). As the potential
increases toward −0.25 V, the intensities of these bands
decrease, and the two distinct peaks merge into a broader
absorption feature centered around 240−270 nm. This broad
band closely resembles the spectral signature observed at the
onset of the initial reduction wave (R1), signifying the
regeneration of long-chain polysulfides and, ultimately, their
conversion back to elemental sulfur (S8) during the charging
cycle of the Li−S system.
In the Li−S system, we also observe a distinct absorbance
feature around 210−215 nm (see Figures 2b,e). Similar to
Na−S, assigning this peak solely from UV−vis spectra is
challenging, as both elemental sulfur (S8) and certain
polysulfide species absorb in the near-UV, with positions
influenced by chain length, charge, solvent, and concentration.
Experimental studies commonly place S8 ring transitions near
220−250 nm, while shorter-chain lithium polysulfides (e.g.,
Li2S4) can exhibit weak UV absorption at slightly lower
wavelengths. Therefore, the 210−215 nm band in our Li−S
spectra likely arises either from residual or newly formed
elemental sulfur or from short-chain polysulfide species.
Definitive identification would require complementary techni-
ques such as operando Raman spectroscopy, XAS, or ex situ
LC-MS.42,43 These results indicate that the stepwise redox
conversion of sulfur in Li−S batteries primarily involves
elemental sulfur (S8) and soluble higher-order polysulfides
(e.g., Li2S6 and Li2S8). Our experimental observations offer
insights into the sequential reduction and oxidation of these
soluble species, underscoring the importance of understanding
the full redox pathway to improve reaction kinetics, limit
polysulfide migration, and enhance the electrochemical
reversibility and overall performance of Li−S systems.
To shed light on the redox process, the relative
concentration change during the redox process has been
calculated from the operando UV−vis absorbance spectrosco-
py, which is well known as an analytical tool for protein
chemistry and sulfur redox.44−46 In the presence of sodium
electrolyte, the formal potentials during reduction are −1.19
and −1.38 V. Interestingly, during the oxidation process, a
three-step reaction occurs, which is not well resolved from the
cyclic voltammogram (Figure S5). The E1/2 values for the
oxidation process are −1.17 , −0.78, and −0.67 V, which are in
good agreement with the redox peak in the cyclic voltammo-
gram. In contrast, lithium electrolyte also shows a two-step
reduction and three-step oxidation process, which is shown in
Figure S5. However, relative absorbance is not prominent
when solid product is formed on the electrode surface, which is
elaborate upon later.

Rotating Ring-Disk Electrode (RRDE) Analysis for
Elucidating the Na−S Reduction Mechanism. A measure-
ment similar to that used for Li−S was carried out to gain
insights into the fundamental reaction mechanisms and to
contextualize the spectral observations from operando UV−vis
spectroscopy in the Na−S system; the rotating ring-disk
electrode (RRDE) technique was utilized along with
Koutecký−Levich (KL) analysis. Figure S6 shows the cyclic
voltammogram of 4 mM S8 in 0.1 M LiTFSI and 0.1 M NaPF6
in DOL/DME electrolyte at a 10 mV/s scan rate and a 1600
rpm rotation speed to confirm the stabilization of the CV at a
single rotation speed.
Figure 5a displays the RRDE voltammograms collected at
varying rotation rates for a Pt disk electrode in the Na−S
electrolyte. From the reduction peak at −1.5 V, the number of
electrons transferred was extracted using the Koutecký−Levich
equation. The calculated value of ∼3.5 electrons indicates that
the initial solution-phase reduction of sulfur in Na−S proceeds
mainly through higher-order polysulfides (Na2S8 →Na2S6 →
Na2S4), similar to the early reduction stages identified in the
operando UV−vis spectra. The slightly lower electron count
relative to the Li−S system (∼5.3 e−) suggests that in Na−S,
further reduction to low-order polysulfides (Na2S2, Na2S) is
kinetically hindered during this stage, likely due to the weaker
solvation and lower diffusivity of Na+, as well as limited the
solubility of lower polysulfides in ether-based electrolytes.

two-step reduction mechanism is preserved, TEGDME slightly
enhances the kinetics of both solution-phase and solid-state
processes relative to DOL/DME.
■CONCLUSION
In this study, we employed operando UV−vis spectroscopy to
elucidate the redox mechanisms of Li−S and Na−S batteries,
providing detailed insights into the intermediates formed
during each stage of their redox reactions. Our results
demonstrate that in the Na−S system, the initial reduction
step converts sulfur into higher-order polysulfides, which are
subsequently reduced to solid Na2S2 and Na2S precipitates
during the second reduction step. These polysulfide
intermediates are reversibly oxidized back to sulfur upon
charge. A similar redox pathway was confirmed for the Li−S
system, highlighting the analogous electrochemical behavior in
both chemistries. Furthermore, rotating ring-disk electrode
(RRDE) electrochemistry allowed quantification of the
electron transfer, revealing an average of 5.35 electrons
involved in the total reduction process for Li−S and 3.48
electrons for Na−S batteries. In DOL/DME solvent, the
predominant intermediate is S4
2−, whereas in TEGDME
solvent, the trisulfide radical anion (S3
•−) emerges as the
dominant intermediate, independent of the counterion. These
findings advance the fundamental understanding of sulfur
redox chemistry in alkali metal−sulfur batteries, contributing
valuable knowledge toward optimizing their electrochemical
performance and cycling stability.
