Unveiling the Reaction Mystery Between Lithium
Polysulﬁdes and Lithium Metal Anode in Lithium–Sulfur
Batteries
Chen-Xi Bi, Nan Yao, Xi-Yao Li, Qian-Kui Zhang, Xiang Chen, Xue-Qiang Zhang,
Bo-Quan Li,* and Jia-Qi Huang*
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are widely regarded as one of the most
promising next-generation high-energy-density energy storage devices.
However, soluble lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) corrode Li metal and deteriorate
the cycling stability of Li–S batteries. Understanding the reaction mechanism
between LiPSs and Li metal anode is imperative. Herein, the reaction rate and
products of LiPSs with Li metal anode, the composition and structure of the
as-generated solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), and the mechanism of lithium
nitrate (LiNO3) additives for inhibiting the corrosion reactions are
systematically unveiled. Concretely, LiPSs react with Li metal anode more
rapidly than Li salt and generate a Li2S-rich SEI. The Li2S-rich SEI is highly
reactive with LiPSs, which exacerbates the formation of dendritic Li and the
continuous corrosion of active Li. LiNO3 functions dominantly by modulating
the solvation structure of LiPSs and inherently reducing the reactivity of LiPSs,
rather than the conventional understanding of LiNO3 participating in the
formation of SEI. This work reveals the reaction mechanism between LiPSs
and Li metal anode and inspires rational regulating of the solvation structure
of LiPSs for stabilizing Li metal anode in Li–S batteries.

Herein, we systemically unveil the reaction rate and products
of LiPSs and Li metal, clarify the composition, structure, and
properties of the as-generated SEI, and elucidate the mechanism
of the LiNO3 additive on side reaction inhibition. Compared with
Li salt, the LiPSs react more rapidly with Li metal and yield a
Li2S-rich SEI on the surface of Li metal anode. The as-generated
Li2S-rich SEI is thick, heterogeneous, and chemically unstable
against LiPSs, leading to continuous corrosion of LiPSs on active
Li metal. Consequently, the Li2S-rich SEI aggregates uneven de-
position of Li metal and exacerbates the growth of dendritic Li
and the generation of inactive Li. Furthermore, the LiNO3 addi-
tive is found to function dominantly by participating in the sol-
vation structure of LiPSs and thereby reducing the intrinsic reac-
tivity of LiPS on Li metal, rather than mainly participating in the
formation of SEI and substantially altering the SEI composition.
The as-proposed mechanism of LiNO3 on inhibiting the shuttle
eﬀect indicates solvation structure modulation are more eﬀective
strategies for Li metal anode protection in Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
The side reactions between LiPSs and Li metal characteristi-
cally generate S-containing solid products in SEI that aﬀect fur-
ther plating and stripping of the Li metal anode. Li2S8 was se-
lected as a representative LiPS to study the side reaction with
Li metal as Li2S8 is generally considered with higher reactivity
and contributes dominantly to the LiPS shuttle eﬀect.[15] Lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI) as a commonly used
Li salt was also evaluated as its reaction with Li metal also pro-
duces S-containing solid products in SEI. To decouple the origin
of the complex S-containing SEI, we sequentially investigated the
reaction products of Li metal with electrolytes containing Li2S8 or
LiTFSI.
The
commonly
used
1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)
and
1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) with a volume ratio of 1:1 were
used as the basic solvent. The Li2S8 electrolyte was prepared by
dissolving Li2S8 in DOL/DME with a total sulfur concentration
of 1.0 M, and the LiTFSI electrolyte was 1.0 M LiTFSI dissolved
in DOL/DME. The reaction of Li metal with each electrolyte
was studied by analyzing the cycled Li metal anodes in Li |
Li symmetric cells. The components of SEI generated by the
reaction were characterized by analyzing the SEI composition
using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).
The components of the SEI formed by the reaction between Li
metal and LiTFSI were ﬁrst clariﬁed. As shown in Figure 1a(1),
only high-valence sulfur species are detected in the S 2p XPS
spectrum of the SEI generated in LiTFSI electrolyte, which comes
from the decomposition of LiTFSI itself (Figures S1 and S2,
Supporting Information). The F 1s and N 1s XPS spectra are
also consistent with LiTFSI according to the binding energy of
F at 688.9 eV and N at 399.7 eV (Figures S3 and S4, Support-
ing Information).[16] Further analysis of the depth information
reveals that there is still a large amount of high-valence sulfur
species in the S 2p XPS spectra, and some low-valence sulfur
species such as Li2S and Li2S2 appear due to the decomposition
of LiTFSI. In addition, the atomic concentration of sulfur in each
sputtered layer is less than 1%, implying a low reaction degree
of LiTFSI and Li metal (Figure 1b; Figure S5, and Table S1, Sup-
porting Information). In contrast, only low-valence sulfur species
especially Li2S is detected in the S 2p XPS spectrum of the SEI
generated in Li2S8 electrolyte (Figure 1a(2)). As the XPS sputter-
ing time increases, the SEI composition is mainly composed of
Li2S (Figures S6 and S7, Supporting Information). Moreover, the
sulfur content is signiﬁcantly higher than the SEI generated in
LiTFSI electrolyte (10.0% at surface and 12.6% at 60 s sputtering
time, Figure S8 and Table S1, Supporting Information). There-
fore, Li2S8 is highly reactive with Li metal, and a Li2S-rich SEI is
generated as a result.
The competition between Li2S8 and LiTFSI in the reaction with
Li metal was further investigated by dissolving Li2S8 in LiTFSI
electrolyte with a total sulfur concentration of 1.0 M (donated
as Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte) considering both Li2S8 and LiTFSI
exist in working Li–S batteries. As shown in Figure 1a(3), the
SEI generated in Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte exhibits characteristics
dominantly aﬀorded by Li2S8 with only low-valence sulfur species
including Li2S, Li2S2, and incompletely reacted LiPSs in the S
2p XPS spectrum. The high-valence sulfur species aﬀorded by
LiTFSI is not observed. With the increased sputtering time, the
sulfur concentration increases without the occurrence of high-
valence sulfur species as well (Figures S9 and S10, Supporting
Information). Only a small amount of the reaction products from
LiTFSI can be observed in the high-resolution F 1s XPS spec-
trum regarding the partially decomposed LiTFSI and product LiF
(Figure S11, Supporting Information). However, the atomic con-
centration of F is signiﬁcantly lower than that of S (1.0% vs 5.9%
and 1.2% vs 9.0% at the surface and 60 s sputtering time, respec-
tively, as shown in Figure S12 and Table S1, Supporting Infor-
mation). The above results indicate that when Li2S8 and LiTFSI
coexist, the reaction between Li2S8 and Li metal anode dominates
to construct a Li2S-rich SEI, while the reaction degree of LiTFSI
and Li metal can be ignored.
It is worth mentioning that the above results diﬀer from the
observations regarding a large amount of high-valence sulfur
species on the surface of Li metal anode reported in many Li–
S battery studies.[17] Considering that there are no species capa-
ble of oxidizing LiPSs or Li2S in the Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte, the
residual LiTFSI on the surface of the Li metal is suggested to
cause the increase in the content of high-valence sulfur species.
The decomposition of LiTFSI under Ar+ sputtering was con-
ducted. The results are consistent with previous reports that

In brief, LiPSs have higher reaction activity with Li metal than
LiTFSI to generate a Li2S-rich SEI with thick and heterogeneous
morphology. The Li2S-rich SEI is unstable with LiPSs and unable
to inhibit the subsequent reactions with LiPSs, leading to severe
shuttle eﬀect and low Coulombic eﬃciency of Li–S batteries. The
continuous corrosion of LiPSs on Li metal anode is highly disad-
vantages to constructing long-cycling Li–S batteries. One of the
most widely used methods to suppress the LiPS shuttle eﬀect
is adopting the LiNO3 additive.[15] For understanding the work-
ing mechanism of this LiNO3 additive, previous works suggest
LiNO3 mainly functions by participating in the formation of SEI.
For instance, Zhang et al. pointed out that LiNO3 can oxide LiPSs
to high-valence sulfur species while LiNO3 is reduced to LiNO2
to construct a compact and stable SEI.[22] However, this proposed
mechanism is a bit rough, and increasing the N–O species in SEI
is not always eﬀective in prolonging the cycling lifespan of Li–S
batteries. Therefore, clarifying the working mechanism of LiNO3
on inhibiting the LiPS shuttle eﬀect is highly important for ratio-
nally stabilizing Li metal anodes in Li–S batteries.

To gain insights into the LiNO3 working mechanism, we fur-
ther investigated the eﬀect of LiNO3 on the reaction products
of the electrolyte containing LiPSs and Li metal. The Li metal
cycled in Li2S8-LiTFSI-LiNO3 electrolyte was analyzed by XPS
and ToF-SIMS. Contrary to the previous reports, only low-valence
sulfur species including Li2S, Li2S2, and LiPS exist in S 2p
XPS spectrum while no high-valence sulfur species are observed
(Figure 4b; Figure S38, Supporting Information). Moreover, the
atomic concentration of sulfur in the SEI is signiﬁcantly lower
than that in the SEI generated in the electrolyte without LiNO3
(Figures S39,S40, and Table S1, Supporting Information). As the
sputtering time increases, Li2S gradually occupies the main com-
ponent of the SEI. The above results indicate that the addition of
LiNO3 can hardly oxidize the reaction products of LiPSs and Li
metal, while the reaction degree between LiPSs and Li metal is
indeed reduced. The signal intensity of F is low and the atomic
concentration does not exceed 1% according to the F 1s XPS
spectrum (Figure S41, Supporting Information), which is con-
sistent with the results of the SEI generated in Li2S8-LiTFSI elec-
trolyte where LiTFSI is not massively involved in the reaction
with Li metal. Notably, the content of nitrates including NO3
−
and NO2
−derived from LiNO3 is rather low in N 1s XPS spec-
trum as well (Figure S42, Supporting Information), indicating
LiNO3 hardly reacts with Li metal to generate massive nitrogen-
containing components in SEI. Furthermore, the concentration
of LiNO3 was increased to 5 wt.% in the Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte
to verify the above conclusions (donated as Li2S8-LiTFSI-5LiNO3
electrolyte). Under the same Li metal cycling conditions, the S 2p
XPS spectrum of the Li plated in Li2S8-LiTFSI-5LiNO3 electrolyte
still shows only low-valence sulfur without high-valence sulfur
signals (Figure S43, Supporting Information). The atomic con-
centrations of F and N are also lower than that of S, indicating that
LiTFSI and LiNO3 contribute little to the reaction with Li metal
(Figures S44 and S45, Supporting Information). The above re-
sults imply that LiNO3 neither oxidizes the disadvantageous low-
valence sulfur species into high-valence states nor contributes ni-
trogen species to the SEI to a signiﬁcant extent. The components
of the SEI on Li metal cycled in Li–S full cells were further con-
ducted to verify the reaction of LiPSs and Li metal anode. The
electrolyte was 1.0 M LiTFSI and 2 wt.% LiNO3 in DOL/DME
(1:1, by vol.) (donated as LiTFSI-LiNO3 electrolyte) with an elec-
trolyte to sulfur ratio (E/S ratio) of 7.0 μL mgS
−1. After cycling,
the Li metal anode at the charged state was taken out and ana-
lyzed by XPS. In the S 2p XPS spectrum (Figure S46, Support-
ing Information), only low-valence sulfur species are observed,
and the atomic concentration of S is signiﬁcantly higher than
that of F and N (Figures S47,S48, and Table S1, Supporting In-
formation). The results indicate that the SEI composition on the
Li metal cycled in Li–S full cells is consistent with that cycled in
Li | Li symmetrical cells. In summary, LiNO3 neither changes the
sulfur species in SEI nor signiﬁcantly participates in the forma-
tion of SEI regarding the reaction between LiPS electrolyte and
Li metal.
The 3D reconstruction of the SEI by ToF-SIMS exhibits similar
results as the SEI derived from Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte (Figure
S49, Supporting Information). However, it takes a longer sputter-
ing time of 1155 s to reach the maximum intensity of the S−sig-
nal in the SEI derived from the Li2S8-LiTFSI-LiNO3 electrolyte,
again indicating the reaction between LiPSs and Li metal is inhib-
ited (Figure 4c; Figure S50, Supporting Information). To sum up,
the addition of LiNO3 reduces the sulfur content and suppresses
the reaction between LiPSs and Li metal, but the key SEI charac-
teristics regarding sulfur, ﬂuorine, and nitrogen species change
little.
The SEI structure was further probed by cryo-TEM. Bulk Li2S
still exists in the SEI generated in Li2S8-LiTFSI-LiNO3 electrolyte
in addition to Li2O, while LiF and Li3N can hardly be identiﬁed
possibly due to their low content in SEI (Figure 4d; Figure S51a,
Supporting Information). The SEI composition is consistent with
the results of the SEI derived in Li2S8-LiTFSI electrolyte without
LiNO3. The only diﬀerence in SEI morphology is that the addi-
tion of LiNO3 thickens the SEI to 80–100 nm (Figure 4e; Figure
S51b, Supporting Information). Also, the content of S and F ele-
ments decreases along the SEI outward in comparison with the
SEI generated without LiNO3 (Figure 4e1–e2).
Based on the above results, the addition of LiNO3 indeed in-
hibits the side reaction between LiPSs and Li metal but no sig-
niﬁcant change is observed in the as-generated SEI, especially
the sulfur and nitrogen species. How LiNO3 eﬀectively inhibits
the shuttle eﬀect requires further exploration. Concretely, does
the SEI really inhibit the reaction of LiPSs with Li metal de-
spite no obvious change in composition, or is the intrinsic re-
activity of LiPSs and Li metal eﬀectively suppressed by LiNO3
through LiPS solvation structure modulation without signiﬁcant
SEI change? To answer the above questions, we supposed 3 pos-
sible mechanisms of how LiNO3 inhibits the LiPS shuttle eﬀect
(Figure 5a). 1) The addition of LiNO3 alone generates a highly
stable SEI against LiPS corrosion; 2) The synergistic eﬀect of
LiNO3 and LiPSs forms a highly stable SEI against LiPS cor-
rosion; 3) LiNO3 participates in the solvation structure of LiPS
and thereby reduces the intrinsic reactivity of LiPSs with Li
metal.
The above hypotheses are quantitively veriﬁed by measuring
the reaction rate of LiPSs with Li metal through quantitative
UV–Vis spectroscopy. To verify the ﬁrst 2 hypotheses, the spe-
ciﬁc SEI was prepared by immersing a piece of bare Li in an
electrolyte containing LiNO3 (donated as LiNO3 treated Li) or
both LiPSs and LiNO3 (donated as Li2S8–LiNO3 treated Li). Af-
terward, the Li metal with the as-generated SEI was transferred
to electrolyte with Li2S8 and LiTFSI but without LiNO3 to quan-
titatively measure the consumption rate of Li2S8. Meantime,
bare Li without pre-generated SEI served as the control sample.

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, we systematically unveil the reaction mechanism
between LiPSs and Li metal anode in Li–S batteries. Concretely,
LiPSs and LiTFSI produce low-valence and high-valence sul-
fur species in SEI by reacting with Li metal, respectively. LiPSs
demonstrate higher reaction activity than LiTFSI, thereby the re-
action between LiPSs and Li metal dominates the product to gen-
erate a Li2S-rich SEI. The Li2S-rich SEI is thick, heterogeneous,
and highly reactive with LiPSs, leading to the formation of mas-
sive dendritic Li and continuous corrosion of active Li. The chem-
ical instability of the Li2S-rich SEI against LiPSs is therefore iden-
tiﬁed as the main cause for Li metal anode failure in Li–S batter-
ies. Contrary to the conventional understanding, the addition of
LiNO3 neither changes the sulfur species in SEI nor participates
in the formation of SEI itself to a signiﬁcant extent. Model ex-
periments and theoretical simulations indicate the mechanism
of LiNO3 in inhibiting the LiPS shuttle eﬀect is modulating the
solvation structure of LiPSs to reduce the inherent reactivity in-
stead of violently changing the SEI. Therefore, introducing addi-
tives that can participate in the solvation structure of LiPSs and
reduce the LiPS reactivity is expected to more eﬀectively protect
the Li metal anode in working Li–S batteries. Nitrates with other
cations or organic functional groups may aﬀord higher protection
capability than LiNO3. Additives with Lewis basic sites may also
aﬀord suitable interaction with LiPSs to better inhibit the corro-
sion reaction or improve the protection durability. The research
methodology proposed in this work may also be applicable in fu-
ture studies on SEI and Li anode protection in advanced battery
systems.
