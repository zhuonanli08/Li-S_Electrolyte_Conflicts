Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis
The validity of the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements was first evaluated by performing Kramers-Kronig (K-K) transforms using Relaxis software. For valid EIS data, the relative residuals of both the real and imaginary components of the impedance were confirmed to be almost entirely within the limit of ±3%.
Subsequently, the same Relaxis software was employed to calculate the DRT profiles and to mathematically reproduce the EIS spectra from the experimental data. Tikhonov regularization was used to fit the discrete experimental data in a non-linear least-squares manner, and a Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.5 was used for data discretization. The experimental data were fitted using both the real and imaginary components of the EIS. Prior to the DRT transformation, high-frequency inductive data points were discarded. At the low-frequency end, only the terminal approximately 45° Warburg segment, characterised by an approximately unity slope in the Nyquist representation and attributed to semi-infinite diffusion, was excluded to minimise boundary artefacts during the finite-frequency DRT inversion. All remaining low-frequency data containing resolved relaxation features were retained, and the same preprocessing criterion was applied consistently to all spectra. A second-order regularization derivative was applied, and the regularization parameter (λ) was set at 0.0001. Under these optimized parameters, the relative errors between the experimentally measured impedance and the reproduced impedance derived from the DRT results were strictly within 2%, indicating a highly reliable and appropriate fitting of the complex EIS data.
Finally, the derived DRT profiles were fitted with a Gaussian non-linear curve fit using the Levenberg-Marquardt iteration method in OriginPro to calculate the polarization resistances (represented by peak areas) and time constants. During the peak fitting process, the number of peaks was manually assigned based on the physical spectral features, and the baseline of the peaks was strictly fixed to zero. Other peak parameters, such as peak centre position, area, and FWHM, were set as free variables. The iterations were performed continuously until the fit fully converged (R2≈1).

Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique analysis
An apparent Li⁺ diffusion coefficient DLi+ was estimated using the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT). Under the assumptions of Fickian transport, semi-infinite diffusion during each current pulse, and an approximately linear relationship between the transient voltage response and the square root of pulse time, DLi+ was calculated using the simplified Weppner–Huggins relation:

where the variables are defined as follows:
τ: The duration of the applied constant current pulse (600s).
mB: The active mass of the sulfur cathode (0.001289g).

VM: The molar volume of the active material (15.5 cm3 mol-1).
MB: The molar mass of the active material (32.06 g mol-1).
S: The geometric surface area of the electrode in contact with the electrolyte (1.13cm2).
ΔES: The change in the steady-state open-circuit voltage (thermodynamic equilibrium potential) during a single GITT step, obtained by subtracting the initial rest voltage from the final rest voltage (V).
ΔEτ: The total change of the cell voltage during the constant current pulse, excluding the instantaneous internal resistance (IR) drop (V).
Because sulfur cathodes undergo coupled ion transport, dissolution, precipitation and multiphase conversion during cycling, the values obtained using this treatment should not be interpreted as intrinsic solid-state Li⁺ diffusion coefficients of a single homogeneous phase. Instead, DLi+ represents an apparent transport parameter that incorporates the overall kinetic response of the sulfur cathode under the adopted GITT conditions and is used here primarily for comparison among the electrolyte systems tested under identical conditions. The validity of the calculation was evaluated from the linearity of the transient voltage response against the square root of pulse time. Data points near the terminal discharge cut-off that did not satisfy this relationship or the semi-infinite diffusion assumption were excluded from the comparative analysis.

Visualized solvation behaviour of lithium polysulfides in different electrolytes
To intuitively evaluate the solvation capability of various electrolytes toward long-chain polysulfides, visualized dissolution experiments were systematically conducted. To ensure absolute thermodynamic equilibrium, all mixtures were continuously stirred for an extended period of one month and subsequently allowed to rest for 24 hours prior to capturing the images. As visually evident in Figure S21, when lithium polysulfides were introduced, the conventional DOL-DME-based electrolytes (the two vials on the right) exhibited severe dissolution. Both the pure DOL-DME and the LiNO3 containing DOL-DME rapidly formed homogeneous, dark reddish-brown solutions, signifying highly solvating environments that inherently facilitate the liquid-phase polysulfide shuttling.
In stark contrast, the tailored localized high-concentration ether electrolytes completely altered this behaviour. The G2-TTE electrolyte (leftmost vial) maintained a distinctly pale appearance with obvious undissolved precipitates (Figure S23). Similarly, the CME-TTE electrolyte (second vial from the left) manifested a highly viscous state with the active species remaining largely undissolved as a distinct phase (Figure S23). Identical long-term equilibrium tests performed across varying polysulfide concentrations and varying intermediate chain lengths (Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8) all yielded universally pale supernates and distinct phase-separation behaviours for the G2-TTE system (Figure S22). This universally low solubility qualitatively indicates the tailored electrolyte’s ability to avoid the formation of soluble lithium polysulfides. Consequently, this thermodynamic confinement physically blocks polysulfide dissolution into the bulk electrolyte, fundamentally eliminating the shuttle effect from the root and promoting a highly reversible quasi-solid-state conversion mechanism.

Figure S36. Operando UV–vis spectra of the DOL–DME electrolyte.
Operando UV–Vis measurements were performed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan) over a wavelength range of 200–800 nm. A custom airtight optical cell was used, in which the cathode and lithium-metal anode were positioned face-to-face on opposite lateral sides of the cell. Electrical tabs were welded to the two electrodes and connected to a Gamry potentiostat (Interface 1010E). After addition of a sufficient amount of electrolyte, the cell was sealed using a lid and Parafilm to maintain gas tightness. The incident light beam passed through the electrolyte at the midpoint between the two electrodes, thereby probing the bulk electrolyte in the inter-electrode region. UV–Vis spectra were collected operando during electrochemical potential scanning at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s⁻¹
Distinct visual appearances were observed for cells employing different electrolytes after cycling. The DOL–DME cell became uniformly yellow throughout the electrolyte volume, indicating extensive dissolution and redistribution of coloured polysulfide species. In contrast, the CME–TTE cell remained almost transparent, with no apparent yellow coloration. For the G2–TTE cell, only faint yellow coloration was observed within a small region adjacent to the cathode, while the electrolyte in the inter-electrode region remained essentially colourless (Figure S33).
These visual observations are consistent with the operando UV–vis spectra. The DOL–DME cell exhibited substantially higher absorbance than both TTE-based systems. In particular, a pronounced broad absorption feature developed between approximately 400 and 500 nm. Absorption in this region is commonly associated with dissolved medium- and long-chain polysulfide dianions, with representative bands reported for S₄²⁻ at approximately 420–425 nm, S₆²⁻ at approximately 450–475 nm, and S₈²⁻ near 490 nm, although the precise band positions and relative intensities depend on the electrolyte and solvation environment.18,19 The strong 400–500 nm response of DOL–DME therefore indicates the accumulation of appreciable amounts of dissolved medium- and long-chain polysulfides in the bulk electrolyte during discharge. In contrast, no comparable absorption feature was detected in either CME–TTE or G2–TTE, demonstrating that the concentrations of these dissolved polysulfide species in the probed bulk electrolyte were markedly reduced.
Although CME–TTE exhibited somewhat higher absorbance than G2–TTE, predominantly in the ultraviolet region below approximately 400 nm, it showed almost no characteristic absorption in the 400–500 nm region and remained visually transparent. Therefore, this difference should not be interpreted as evidence for substantial accumulation of medium- or long-chain polysulfides in CME–TTE. Nevertheless, the consistently lower absorbance of G2–TTE, together with the confinement of its faint yellow coloration to the immediate vicinity of the cathode, indicates that G2–TTE more effectively restricts the dissolution and outward transport of sulfur intermediates. The combined visual and spectroscopic evidence is consistent with the strongly suppressed shuttle behaviour observed electrochemically and supports a localized, quasi-solid-state sulfur-conversion pathway in G2–TTE rather than a conventional bulk dissolution–diffusion-mediated mechanism.

In situ Raman tests


Figure S39. Optical electrochemical cell used for the operando Raman measurements. 

Figure S40. Operando Raman spectra of G2–TTE during discharge. 

Figure S41. Operando Raman spectra of G2–TTE during charge. 
In situ Raman measurements were performed using a LabRAM Soleil Raman spectrometer (HORIBA) controlled by LabSpec 6 software. An EL-CELL optical electrochemical cell was used, in which the sulfur cathode and lithium-metal anode were arranged side-by-side. The cell was filled with 120 μL of G2–TTE electrolyte, corresponding to an excess electrolyte amount. Raman spectra were acquired using a 532 nm laser and a 600 gr mm⁻¹ grating (750 nm blaze) at 6.3% laser power (6.7 mW). A 50× objective lens and a confocal hole size of 500 μm were used. Each spectrum was collected with an acquisition time of 5 s and 10 accumulations, and measurements were repeated over 100 time points. All other acquisition parameters were maintained at automatic or default settings.
Electrochemical cycling was controlled using a Gamry Interface 1010E potentiostat. Before each measurement, the cell was held potentiostatically at 2.8 V. Because the electrochemically active electrode area and capacity could not be precisely determined for each individual optical cell, cyclic voltammetry was employed instead of galvanostatic cycling. Cells were scanned from 2.8 to 1.8 V for discharge and subsequently from 1.8 to 2.8 V for charge at 0.1 mV s⁻¹, corresponding approximately to a 0.36 C rate.
To minimise spectral interference associated with cathode volume variation and displacement during cycling, the laser was focused on the electrolyte region rather than directly on the electrodes. The Raman measurements therefore primarily probe dissolved electrolyte-phase sulfur species.
In situ Raman analysis of sulfur species in G2–TTE. During discharge, Raman bands at approximately 400, 450, and 535 cm⁻¹ emerged, which are assigned to S₆²⁻, S₄²⁻, and S₃•⁻, respectively (Figure S40). The sequential appearance and relative evolution of these signals indicate that S₆²⁻ and S₃•⁻ are transient intermediate species during sulfur reduction. 
To compare spectral evolution during cycling, the Raman spectra were normalized to the TFSI⁻ band at 745 cm⁻¹. The polysulfide-related signals became detectable at approximately 2.5 V, reached their maximum intensity near 2.3 V, and subsequently decreased to the pre-discharge baseline by the end of discharge. Consistently, the absolute intensity of the TFSI⁻ band decreased by approximately 45% during this interval and recovered upon completion of discharge, indicating transient optical attenuation by dissolved coloured sulfur species. 
Importantly, no persistent dissolved-polysulfide signal was detected at the end of discharge. This behaviour differs from that of conventional dissolution-mediated Li–S conversion, in which dissolved polysulfides remain present during the second discharge plateau and provide the reservoir responsible for shuttle reactions. The transient Raman response in G2–TTE is therefore consistent with strongly restricted polysulfide dissolution and a localized, quasi-solid-state sulfur-conversion process. 
Short-chain species, including Li₂S₂ and Li₂S, were not detected under the present measurement conditions. This absence should not be interpreted as direct evidence for their absence during electrochemical conversion, because these species are largely insoluble and predominantly exist in the solid phase in ether-based electrolytes. In addition, under 532 nm excitation, long-chain polysulfides and S₃•⁻ benefit from resonance or pre-resonance enhancement, whereas colourless short-chain species exhibit much weaker Raman scattering. Therefore, the Raman data are used to compare the relative temporal evolution of detectable dissolved species rather than to quantify the concentrations of individual sulfur species. 
During the subsequent charge process, a weak signal attributable to re-formed elemental sulfur was observed, consistent with reversible conversion towards S₈.

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) characterisation

Figure S42. Two-dimensional ToF-SIMS negative-ion maps of the Cu electrode recovered from a Li–Cu cell after repeated Li plating/stripping in the G2–TTE electrolyte

Figure S43. 3D ToF-SIMS reconstructions of selected negative secondary-ion fragments in the SEI formed on the Cu electrode after repeated Li plating/stripping in the G2–TTE electrolyte

Figure S44. ToF-SIMS sputter-depth profiles of selected negative secondary-ion fragments in the SEI formed on the Cu electrode after repeated Li plating/stripping in the G2–TTE electrolyte
ToF-SIMS measurements were performed using an IONTOF TOF.SIMS 5 instrument (IONTOF GmbH, Münster, Germany) at Imperial College London. The Cu electrode was recovered from a Li–Cu cell after repeated Li plating/stripping in the G2–TTE electrolyte and subsequently subjected to ToF-SIMS characterization. Selected negative secondary-ion fragments were analysed to investigate the chemical composition and spatial distribution of the resulting solid electrolyte interphase (SEI). An argon ion beam was used for sputtering during depth profiling. Because the sputter rate was not calibrated against a reference material, the depth-dependent evolution is reported as a function of sputter time rather than absolute depth.
The two-dimensional ion maps show spatially extensive F⁻, LiF⁻, LiF₂⁻ and SOₓ⁻ signals across the analysed area, confirming the widespread formation of fluorine- and sulfur-containing interphase components. The CF₃⁻ and CF₃SO₂⁻ fragments are attributed to TFSI-derived species, whereas CH₃O⁻ represents oxygen-containing fragments originating from the ether solvent. Because secondary-ion yields depend strongly on the chemical species and local matrix, the absolute intensities of different fragments are not directly compared.
The sputter-depth profiles reveal a clear compositional evolution across the interphase. The CF₃SO₂⁻ and CH₃O⁻ signals are more pronounced near the outer surface and progressively decrease with sputtering, consistent with the enrichment of TFSI- and ether-derived molecular fragments in the outer region. In contrast, the LiF⁻ and LiF₂⁻ signals increase with sputter time and remain relatively high toward the interior. The SO⁻, SO₂⁻ and SO₃⁻ signals reach their maximum intensities at intermediate sputter times, indicating the formation of sulfur–oxygen-containing products derived from anion decomposition. The emergence of the CuO⁻ signal after approximately 100 s indicates that the sputtering front begins to reach the Cu substrate or locally thinner regions of the interphase. The persistence of the SEI-related fragments after the onset of CuO⁻ is likely associated with lateral variations in interphase thickness, electrode roughness and sputter-induced mixing.
Together with the three-dimensional reconstructions, these results support the formation of a compositionally graded, LiF-containing interphase with TFSI-derived sulfur–oxygen and molecular fragments in G2–TTE. Because secondary-ion yields are species- and matrix-dependent, the signals are interpreted through their individual spatial and depth evolution rather than by comparing absolute intensities among fragments. Moreover, because ToF-SIMS was performed only for G2–TTE, the measurement establishes the interfacial composition of this electrolyte but does not demonstrate that its interphase is quantitatively more inorganic or superior to those formed in the other electrolytes.

(a), (b) Representative structures, (c) atomic CNs, (d) neighbouring-molecule counts, and (e) normalized molecular composition.
The bulk-electrolyte simulations reveal distinctly different Li⁺ coordination environments. In G2–TTE, O(G2) provides the dominant short-range coordination, with a coordination number of approximately 3.63 at 3.0 Å, while O(TFSI⁻) contributes approximately 1.78. In CME–TTE, the solvent contribution decreases to approximately 1.19 O(CME), whereas the O(TFSI⁻) coordination number increases to approximately 3.03. The negligible Li–O(TTE) coordination in both systems confirms that TTE primarily acts as a non-coordinating diluent. The representative clusters further illustrate the multidentate coordination of G2 and the more anion-rich local environment formed with monodentate CME.
For Li⁺ neighbouring Li₂S₆, the Li–S coordination number increases from approximately 1.99 in G2–TTE to 2.35 in CME–TTE. The corresponding O(TFSI⁻) coordination number also increases from approximately 1.21 to 1.55. In contrast, solvent-oxygen coordination decreases from approximately 2.02 for G2 to 0.89 for CME. These results indicate that Li⁺ remains strongly chelated by G2 near Li₂S₆, whereas CME–TTE forms a more strongly polysulfide- and anion-associated local environment. Li–O(TTE) coordination remains negligible in both electrolytes.
Atomic and molecule-based analyses provide complementary descriptions of the Li₂S₆-local solvation structures. In G2–TTE, approximately 2.02 G2-oxygen contacts are supplied by only 0.79 G2 molecules per selected Li⁺, corresponding to about 2.55 coordinating oxygen atoms per participating G2 molecule. In CME–TTE, approximately 0.89 oxygen contacts arise from 0.90 CME molecules, corresponding to nearly one coordinating oxygen per molecule. This quantitatively confirms the multidentate coordination of G2 and the predominantly monodentate coordination of CME.
The numbers of neighbouring Li₂S₆ and TFSI⁻ molecules are also lower in G2–TTE than in CME–TTE, at approximately 1.05 versus 1.15 for Li₂S₆ and 1.15 versus 1.49 for TFSI⁻. Nevertheless, the normalized molecular compositions are relatively similar between the two systems. This comparison demonstrates that their principal difference arises from coordination connectivity and solvent denticity, rather than simply from the total number of neighbouring molecules.
Simplified model of field-induced interfacial organization

Figure S48. Simplified atomistic model of field-induced interfacial organization in the G2–TTE electrolyte. 
(a) Overall simulation cell containing idealized planar carbon-cathode and Li-metal-anode surfaces. (b), (c) Corresponding snapshots highlighting the distributions of Li⁺/G2 and Li⁺/TFSI⁻, respectively. (d) Relative concentration profiles of Li⁺, TFSI⁻, G2 and TTE along the z direction. The model is intended to illustrate qualitative interfacial redistribution rather than quantitatively reproduce the electrical double layer of an operating cell.
For the electric double-layer (EDL) model, a simplified graphite structure was adopted to represent the conductive carbon black. The Li (001) surface was used as the anode, while the cathode and anode were placed on opposite sides along the z-axis. The simulation box dimensions were set to 62 × 62 × 90 Å³.
The initial configurations were equilibrated in the NPT ensemble at 600 K for 3 ns, followed by a cooling process from 600 K to 298 K over 3 ns. Subsequently, the systems were further equilibrated at 298 K for 2 ns. The equilibrated structures after annealing were used as the starting configurations for production simulations, which were conducted in the NVT ensemble for 15 ns. The integration time step was set to 1 fs. During the annealing process, the pressure was maintained at 1 bar, with temperature and pressure coupling constants of 0.1 ps and 10 ps, respectively.
To obtain a qualitative picture of field-induced electrolyte organization at the electrode interfaces, an idealized slab model was constructed with the G2–TTE electrolyte confined between planar carbon-cathode and Li-metal-anode surfaces. Fixed partial charges of +0.01 e per carbon atom at the cathode and −0.089286 e per lithium atom at the anode were assigned to generate an electric field directed from the cathode towards the anode. The spatial distributions of Li⁺, TFSI⁻, G2 and TTE were subsequently evaluated along the surface-normal z direction.
This calculation represents a deliberately simplified interfacial model. In particular, it employs idealized planar electrode surfaces and fixed atomic charges and does not explicitly account for constant-potential electrode polarization, electron-transfer reactions, sulfur-conversion products, the porous carbon/sulfur composite structure, binder, surface roughness, SEI formation or the time-dependent evolution of the interfaces during cycling. Accordingly, the model is used only to visualize qualitative field-induced redistribution of electrolyte species and should not be interpreted as a quantitative prediction of the electrical-double-layer thickness, potential profile, capacitance or exact interfacial composition in an operating Li–S cell.
The simplified interfacial model reveals clear field-induced redistribution of the G2–TTE electrolyte components. A pronounced TFSI⁻ maximum develops adjacent to the positively charged carbon surface, consistent with electrostatic enrichment of anions at the cathode-side interface. In contrast, Li⁺ becomes enriched near the negatively charged Li-metal surface. The anode-side Li⁺ enrichment is accompanied by an increase in the local G2 density, indicating that G2-containing Li⁺ coordination environments can be retained near the Li surface. TFSI⁻ also exhibits interfacial structuring on the anode side, consistent with the participation of anions in the locally concentrated coordination environment. By comparison, TTE shows a substantially weaker interfacial response, in agreement with its weakly coordinating role and limited participation in the primary Li⁺ solvation structure.
These results provide a qualitative molecular picture of interfacial heterogeneity in G2–TTE: Li⁺, coordinating G2 and TFSI⁻ undergo different degrees of redistribution under the imposed electric field, whereas TTE remains comparatively weakly structured. Such organization is consistent with the locally concentrated character identified from the bulk RDF and coordination-number analyses. Nevertheless, because the calculation uses planar electrodes with fixed atomic charges, the peak positions and relative intensities should not be interpreted as quantitative measures of the real double-layer thickness or species concentrations. In particular, this simplified model does not directly demonstrate SEI formation or the interfacial reaction mechanism.

The laboratory-scale pouch cell contained two single-sided sulfur cathodes with a total sulfur mass of 182.4 mg and one double-sided Li-coated Cu anode.
