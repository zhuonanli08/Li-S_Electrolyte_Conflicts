A B S T R A C T
Lithium sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered as promising candidates for high-energy-density battery systems
owing to the high theoretical capacity of sulfur (1675 mAh g1) and low cost of raw materials. However, their
practical application is hampered by low rate capability and rapid degradation of capacity, arising from the
passivation of the cathode by lithium sulﬁdes (Li2S2/Li2S) deposited during discharge and low interfacial stability
of the Li anode. Herein, we report on a comprehensive strategy to select co-solvent to the electrolyte to regulate
the deposition of lithium sulﬁdes during charge-discharge process. We show that addition of a co-solvent with
high solubility, and strong interaction with Li2S to a conventional electrolyte effectively mitigates the formation of
a passivating layer on the sulfur cathode and dramatically improves the interfacial stability of the Li anode. We
demonstrate that Sulfolane (SL) has these properties and that a Li–S cell with an electrolyte containing 6 vol% SL
exhibits outstanding cyclic performance (0.083% decay per cycle) and rate capability (capacity density of 765
mAh g1 at rate of 1.0C). Thus, we provide a facile strategy for the selection of co-solvent for improved per-
formance of Li–S batteries, realizing their practical application for high-energy-density battery systems.
1. Introduction
In past decades, Li-ion batteries (LIBs) have been developed to
power a variety of applications from portable digital devices to electrical
vehicles [1–3]. Unfortunately, the energy density of LIBs is rapidly
approaching their theoretical limit (around 400 Wh kg1), which is
insufﬁcient
to
meet
the
demands
from
next
generation
high-energy-density battery systems [4]. Rechargeable lithium sulfur
(Li–S) batteries are considered as promising alternatives because of the
high theoretical speciﬁc capacity, (1675 mAh g1) of the electrochemical
reaction between S and Li, environmental friendliness, and high natural
abundance of active materials [5–7]. However, the practical application
of Li–S batteries is severely hampered by low utilization of active ma-
terial (S) and an unstable interface on the Li anode, resulting in low ca-
pacity, fast fading and low rate capability, particularly critical in cells
with sulfur loading that is necessary for practical applications [7].
The discharge process of the most common Li–S cells, utilizing liquid
electrolytes, can be divided into two stages, reduction of element sulfur
to long-chain lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, 4  n  8) with high solubility
and further reduction of polysulﬁdes to insoluble lithium sulﬁdes (Li2S2/
Li2S) [8–12]. The insoluble Li2S generated in the S cathode will passivate
the surface of the carbon matrix, forming an insulating layer and hin-
dering the transport of both ions and electrons, and prevent full con-
version of active material [13–16]. Moreover, the long-chain lithium
polysulﬁdes dissolved in the typical electrolyte can migrate to the Li
anode side where they can be chemically reduced to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S.
This undesired side reaction will lead to fast self-discharging and low
Coulombic efﬁciency of the full cell and to a passivation also of the Li
anode by the formation of an insulating and unstable interface [5,17,18].
A host of strategies have been developed to trap the highly-soluble pol-
ysulﬁdes and control the precipitation of solid end products, e.g. by
nano-structure design of the carbon matrix in the cathode [19–22],
functionalizing the separator with active groups [23–25] and functional
binders [26,27]. However, even though each strategy adds some bene-
ﬁcial features, the problem with rate capability and unstable solid elec-
trolyte interphase (SEI) and severe dendritic growth at the Li anode [28].

To overcome the problems discussed above tailoring the electrolyte is
a key issue. The conventional liquid electrolytes (e.g. 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/
DME with 0.2 M LiNO3, called CLE) possess the advantages of low
permittivity and high solubility of long-chain polysulﬁde. While, this is
necessary in order to have good kinetics and high active material utili-
zation it also leads to shuttle effects and the formation of an unstable SEI
on the Li anode [29,30]. However, they also show a low solubility of the
shortest polysulﬁdes (Li2S2/Li2S) leading deposition of an insulating
layer on the cathode hindering the electrochemical reactions to proceed
and limiting the capacity. To target the issue of deposition of Li2S2/Li2S
and the shuttle effect, previous works have suggested that the solvent
used in the electrolyte should have the following properties, including
high permittivity, high viscosity as well as suitable donor number (DN)
and acceptor number (AN), etc. The promising solvents which embrace
above properties are expected to increase the solubility of Li2S2 or Li2S in
electrolyte as well as the interaction between solvent and lithium poly-
sulﬁdes, and to inhibit migration of long-chain polysulﬁdes in the elec-
trolyte [31]. On the other hand, tuning the electrolyte composition is also
one of the most effective methods to enhance the interfacial stability of Li
anode [32–36]. Therefore, selecting the co-solvents with different func-
tionality to complement the properties of the conventional electrolyte
would be a viable method to address the issues on both the S cathode and
Li anode to enable high-performance Li–S batteries also in practice.
Here, we report on a strategy to tune the electrolyte for Li–S batteries
with a co-solvent by considering the permittivity and the viscosity [37].
We show that sulfolane as a co-solvent has the ability to not only enhance
the utilization of insoluble Li2S2/Li2S and weaken the shuttle effect of
long-chain polysulﬁdes (S8
2, S6
2, S4
2), but also to stabilize the SEI on the
Li anode. Li–S batteries with a sulfolane containing electrolyte deliver
outstanding cycling and rate performances also at a relatively high
loading of S necessary for a high energy density in practical cells.
2. Results and discussion
The typical solvent used in Li–S battery electrolytes are weak polar
solvents, such as, dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME) with
relative permittivity of 7.21 and 7.98, respectively (Fig. S1). In contrast,
the permittivity of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and SL is considerably
higher, 46.68 and 43.3 respectively [7]. Based on the rule of “like dis-
solves like”, the higher permittivity of DMSO and SL is expected to have a
higher solubility of Li2S, and is also experimentally conﬁrmed by the data
presented in Fig. 1b which shows that the solubility of Li2S is more than
one order of magnitude higher for DMSO and SL compared to DOL and
DME. This high solubility for short polysulﬁdes makes DMSO and SL
promising as co-solvents for Li–S battery application. To further inves-
tigate the interaction between solvents and density functional theory
(DFT) calculations were performed and the results are displayed in
Fig. 1c and Fig. S2 [38]. Among the investigated solvents the adsorption
energy (ΔEa) is highest between Li2S and SL (2.58 eV for SL). We also
calculated ΔEa between long chain polysulﬁdes and solvents and with the
increasing length of polysulﬁde chain, the ΔEa for SL decreases, see
Fig. S2. Compared to the other solvents one can note that the adsorption
energy is similar for SL already from Li2S2. This behavior can be
explained by considering that SL is a weak Lewis base and strong Lewis
acid, from the high AN and low DN [37]. The Lewis basicity of poly-
sulﬁdes decreases with the increasing length, and thus the interaction
between SL and Li2S is the strongest [39–41]. In fact, for the longer
polysulﬁdes the major electrolyte solvents, DME and DOL, have a higher
adsorption energy than SL. In addition, the highest occupied molecule
orbital (LOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecule orbital (LUMO) were
calculated with DFT to reveal the redox behavior of the solvents (Fig. 1d).
SL has a lower LUMO energy (0.03 eV) than that of DOL (1.04 eV) and
DME (0.99 eV), and thus the decomposition of SL on the Li anode will
occur prior to that of DME and DOL. Thus, SL can have dual roles in the

To investigate the compatibility towards a metallic Li anode the
electrolytes with the DMSO and SL as co-solvents were tested in sym-
metric cells (Fig. 1e). The symmetric cell with the standard electrolyte
(1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME with 0.2 M LiNO3) shows a stable polarization
voltage during 24-h cycling. In contrast, when adding DMC and DMSO to
the electrolyte, the cells fail after few cycles, indicating the formation of
an unstable interface on Li. Interestingly, the symmetric cell with SL
displays similar cycling stability as the bare electrolyte, showing a good
compatibility toward to Li electrode.
The chemical stability between SL and CLE is evaluated by Fourier-
transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), as shown in Fig. S3. Intensity
of the peak located at 1310 cm1–1225 cm1 that is corresponding to
S––O group increases with addition of SL. No extra peak is observed after
the addition of SL, indicating that SL is stable towards the components of
CLE. The viscosity of solvents and electrolytes is summarized in Fig. S4
and Fig. S5. Due to the relatively high viscosity of SL (10.29 mPa s), the
SL-CLE electrolytes show increasing viscosity with the addition of SL,
which is beneﬁcial for slowing down the migration of long-chain poly-
sulﬁdes toward Li anode. However, the increased viscosity is accompa-
nied by a small decrease in ionic conductivity, as shown in Fig. S6. The
electrochemical stability window of SL-CLE electrolytes is up to 4.5 V,
which is sufﬁcient to operate Li–S batteries (Fig. S7).
On account of the obtained results SL is selected as a promising co-
solvent to tune the electrolyte for enhanced performance of Li–S batte-
ries. To investigate if SL can add the desired functionality in terms of
controlling Li2S deposition at discharge and the formation of a passiv-
ation layer, cycled S cathodes were investigated using scanning electron
microscope (SEM) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As
shown in Fig. 2a, the pristine S cathode shows porous structure that is
beneﬁcial for fast reactions. After 100 cycles, the surface of S cathode
(Fig. 2b) using CLE is covered with a thick layer of solid products. This
passivation of the carbon matrix will make it difﬁcult for the transport
and reduction of polysulﬁdes in the cathode. With the addition of SL to
CLE, the passivation by insoluble Li2S2/Li2S on surface of S cathode is
reduced, as shown in Fig. 2 d-e where a porous morphology is found,
indicating a less passivation of the carbon matrix. In fact, a decrease of
deposition products can be observed with as little as 2% SL, Fig. S8.
Furthermore, the EIS spectra of cells before and after 5 cycles (Fig. 2c and
f) were collected and ﬁtted by using the equivalent circuit shown inside
Fig. 2c and f. Rel represents resistance of electrolyte, Rsl represents the
total resistance of the surface layers on the S cathode and Li anode, and
Rer represents the resistance to charge transfer on the S cathode [42,43].
All the ﬁtting results are summarized in Table S1. The value of Rer for cell
with CLE increases rapidly from 20.41 to 31.84 Ohm after 5 cycles,
indicating a remarkable rising on the resistance of charge transfer. By
contrast, the value for the cell using SL as cosolvent is stable or even
lower after cycling. These results are consistent with SEM images dis-
cussed in the prior section.
To obtain a better understanding of the passivation, X-ray photo-
electron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to verify the chemical
composition on the surface of cycled S cathodes (Fig. 3a). With increasing
SL content in the electrolyte an obvious decrease of the peak corre-
sponding to Li2S is observed (161.8 eV). This conﬁrms that the accu-
mulation of insoluble sulfur species on the S cathode is lowered by the
addition of SL. The peak at 167.1 eV is attributed to of Li2SO3, which is
related to the decomposition of SL ([O––S––O]2-) and LiTFSI (Li2O) [44].
The strong peak at 169.1 eV can be traced back to Li2SO4 species and is a
result of the oxidation reaction of between sulfur species and LiNO3,
which leads to irreversible capacity decay. With the addition of SL, the
intensity of this peak decreases, illustrating that SL can also partly sup-
press this side reaction. As shown in Fig. 3b, the abundance of sulfur on
the surface of cycled cathode with CLE is 14%. This value is dramatically
lowered by using SL as a co-solvent further conﬁrming the decrease in the
passivation.
On account of the morphology, interfacial impedance and XPS results,
we suggest that the thick passivation layer is generated on the surface of S
cathodes. This passivation layer not only insulated active mass from
carbon matrix but also blocked the charge and mass transfer in the
cathode. The addition of SL enhanced the solubility of Li2S/Li2S2 in SL-
CLE during the electrochemical charge-discharge process, promising a
less accumulation of passivation layer.

symmetric cells were assembled with CLE and 6%SL-CLE. As shown in
Fig. 4a, a continuously increasing polarization voltage is observed for the
cell with CLE illustrating an unstable SEI layer during the electrochemical
stripping/plating process. In contrast, the cell with 6%SL-CLE shows
stable voltage proﬁle for 800 h. When the operating current density is
increased to 2.0 mA cm2, the control cell with CLE fails after 80 h while

Galvanostatic charge-discharge tests were carried out to verify the
effect of SL as co-solvent on the performance of full Li–S cell. Discharge
voltage proﬁles for the ﬁrst cycle are displayed in Fig. 5a and b, showing
the typical two-plateau discharge process for Li–S cells with liquid elec-
trolyte. The ﬁrst plateau, at 2.4 V–2.1 V, corresponds to the reduction of
element S to soluble long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, 4 < n < 8) and the
second plateau, at 2.1 V, relates to the conversion from soluble long-
chain Li2Sn to the insoluble end product, Li2S2 [47,48]. With a low S
loading (1.0 mg cm2), the cells with 6% and 10% SL-CLE deliver a
discharge capacity of 1130 mAh g1 and 1050 mAh g1, respectively,
which are higher than that with neat CLE (1020 mAh g1). At high
loading (3.0 mg cm2) the role of SL becomes even more evident, since
more material has to be converted in the reaction. The discharge voltage
proﬁle of the cell with CLE shows a shorter ﬁrst plateau and a sloping
voltage plateau at lower voltage, in comparison to the cells with SL-CLE,
demonstrating that the addition of SL effectively reduces the polarization
during the discharge process (DCLE ¼ 9.04*105 cm2 s1, D6%SL-CLE ¼
1.48*104 cm2 s1 and D10%SL-CLE ¼ 1.11*104 cm2 s1). This improve-
ment is further demonstrated by the evolution of rate capability, as

To verify the kinetics of the cell with various electrolytes, cyclic
voltammetry (CV) tests were performed at different scanning rates to
quantitatively estimate the diffusivity of Li-ions in second discharge
plateau. As shown in Fig. 5d, e and f, two cathodic peaks are observed at
2.2–2.5 V (C1) and 1.9–2.1 V (C2), which are corresponding to the
reduction of element S to soluble polysulﬁdes and further reduction to
insoluble lithium sulﬁdes (Li2S2, Li2S), respectively [49]. With the in-
crease of scanning rate, the peak current of C1 and C2 peaks shift to lower
potentials. To determine the diffusivity of Li-ions the classical Randles
Sevcik equation can be used: Ip¼ (2.65  105) n3/2 AD1/2 Cν1/2, where Ip
is the peak current, n is the number of electrons for the reaction, A is the
active area between electrode and electrolyte, D is the diffusion coefﬁ-
cient of lithium ions, ν is the scan rate, the n, A and C are constant
[50–52]. As shown in Fig. 5g, the peak current of C2 varies linearly with
ν1/2, and we can thus estimate the diffusivity of Li-ions form the slope.
We clearly observe a higher slope from the cells with the SL-CLE elec-
trolytes indicating that the diffusivity of Li ions is dramatically improved
in the reduction process of polysulﬁde to Li2S, which is consistent with
prior results and discussion.
The long-term cycling performance of a Li–S cell with 6%SL-CLE is
shown in Fig. 5h. The cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1090
mAh g1and 662 mAh g1 after 400 cycles, revealing a slow decay of
0.083% per cycle. In contrast, the capacity of the cell with CLE is only 295
mAh g1 after 400 cycles. This clearly demonstrates that the observed
effects of the addition of SL on both Li2S deposition and stabilization of
the Li anode are of vital importance for the long term cyclability of Li–S
cells. The comparison between previous literatures involving the elec-
trolytes for Li–S batteries and this report is summarized in Table S2. It
shows that the 6%SL-CLE embraces great advancement and boosts the
practical application of high-energy-density Li–S batteries.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we show a route to selecting co-solvents to optimize the
electrolyte for Li–S batteries by considering their permittivity, viscosity
and adsorption energy. We demonstrate this by showing how SL as a co-
solvent with high permittivity and adsorption energy for Li2S efﬁciently
can reduce the thick passivation layer formed on the S cathode by
insoluble Li2S. This signiﬁcantly improves the performance in terms of a
lower polarization and fast kinetics. Moreover, the electrolyte with SL is
also stable towards the Li anode and is able to suppress the formation of
inert “dead Li”. As a result, Li–S cell with the 6%SL-CLE electrolyte ex-
hibits excellent long-term cycling performance and rate capability. Thus,
our results provide a practical pathway to enhance the cycling perfor-
mance and rate capability of Li–S batteries for next generation high-
energy-density battery systems by rationally tuning the electrolyte
properties through the addition of a co-solvent.
