A B S T R A C T
Practical sulfur (S) cathode usage is still in a dilemma owing to their low-areal-capacity and polysulfide-shuttle 
challenges in ether electrolytes. Though using ester-compatible small-molecule Sx can bypass these kinetic issues, 
their low S content limits high-energy charge-storage utilizations. To overcome above constraints, we propose to 
make lean-ester-electrolyte-compatible S8-rich Zn0.06S@Sx (total usable S content >64 wt%) microsphere cath­
odes featured by high tap/compaction densities and gradient core-shell configurations. The outer nucleophilic 
shielding layers can protect S8 components from ester solvent penetrations, help to stabilize cathode interfaces 
and offer capacity contributions/prompt solid-state Li+ paths for inner thionic reactions. Systematic in-situ de­
tections affirm the dispersed polar ZnS nano domains (~4 nm) in cores can expedite adjacent S8 redox con­
versions by chemical coordination and electrovalent mediations (Zn+2→Zn+1.01). Such cathodes own a very 
impressive S utilization ratio of ~89.3%, with exceptional discharge capacity (893 mAh g-1; based on all usable 
Zn0.06S@Sx cathode mass) and rate/cyclic behaviors. Packed pouch cells with remarkable cathode areal loading 
(~14.4 mg cm-2) and lean electrolyte conditions (~2 μL mgs
-1) exhibit salient energy densities of ~883.3 W h L-1/ 
398.6 Wh kg-1, holding great promise as high-energy power sources for robotic industries.
1. Introduction
Pursuits for thriving artificial intelligence plus (AI+) robotic in­
dustries propel the development of high-energy and low-cost power 
technologies beyond current leading Li-ion batteries (LIBs) [1–4]. 
Among candidates, metal-sulfur (S) batteries, typically like Li-S cell 
systems, are conceived as strong contenders on account of great natural 
abundance/cost-effectiveness of S (nearby 250 $ ton-1), showing an 
impressive theoretical gravimetric energy (EG) of 2600 Wh kg-1 and 
good promise in reaching 500 Wh kg-1 in practical pouch cells [5–7]. 
According to their distinct working mechanisms, S cathodes can be 
classified into two general sub-categories, comprising the elemental S8 
(obeying “solid-liquid-solid” conversions in ether-based electrolytes) 
and immobilized small-molecule Sx (x<4) in polymeric frameworks 
(relying on “solid-solid” changes in ester ones) [8,9].
Elemental S8 cathodes often possess a relatively high S content over 
60% in weight ratio (wt%) and distinct redox chemistry; their packed 
cells hold potential in storing more charges than almost all LIBs [10,11]. 
Nevertheless, inevitable/formidable challenges still stand in their way 
for further usage. Primarily, making full use of S8 actives (especially at 
high-areal-loading conditions) remains rather difficult due to their 
insulating/sluggish reaction properties [12]. To offset these intrinsic 
drawbacks for S8 or end-discharged Li₂S, conducting host reservoirs 
coupled with redox-mediated catalysts (normally featured by high 
porosity/specific surface areas) require using in cathodes [13–15]. 
Incorporating these incompact (<0.5 g cm-3) hosts and electrochemi­
cally inert species would set barriers in making large areal S loading 
ratios (>5 mg cm-2) under standardized film-pasting techniques, leading 
to low realistic EG and volumetric energy density (EV) in cell units 
[16–18]. Despite thickening electrode films is a rational way to augment 
S8 loadings, a proportionate rise in areal capacity and full-cell EG is 
rarely achieved owing to serious concomitant cathode polarizations [19,

intermediates (LiPSs) induces parasitic reactions (e.g., LiPSs dis­

Unlike S8 cases, small-molecule Sx cathodes run in more lean, safe 
and economical ester electrolytes; the adverse LiPSs shuttling is avoided 
by their expedite solid-solid redox conversions, giving rise to high 
electrode CE (close to 100%) and prolonged cyclic lifespan over 103 
cycles [24,25]. Generally, these Sx species need chemically attaching to 
polymer mainstays like polyacrylonitrile (PAN), or physically confined 
in microporous carbons (MCs). However, there only exist limited 
anchoring sites in such supporting frameworks, enabling the S wt% 
rarely more than 45% (corresponding reversible capacities below 700 
mAh g-1) [26,27]. To remedy this limitation, tremendous efforts have 
been dedicated to raising the S content as much as possible [28–30]. 
Nonetheless, such “overloaded” S is mostly not present as a simple form 
of chain-like molecules, since most assembled cells are subjected to re­
action deteriorations or even total failures that usually appear in S8 
cases, driven by negative nucleophilic reactions between LiPSs and ester 
solvents [31]. Additionally, their discharge voltage plateau (mid-value: 
1.55~1.85 V) differs from that of S8 cathodes with upper values (>2.1 
V) in ether electrolytes, due to their distinct reaction paths/redox ki­
netics; the compromised operation voltage and capacities, together with 
S areal loading dilemmas similar to former S8 cases, endow Sx cathodes 
with less competitiveness in EG/EV parameters once packed in Ah-level 
cells [32,33].
Trying to maximize practical S content/areal loadings and meantime 
preserve the eminent cathode reactivity/operation stability in lean ester 
electrolytes, we herein propose to construct functionalized inner-Zn0.06S 
(defined by the Zn to S stoichiometric ratio)/outer-Sx cathodes (denoted 
as Zn0.06S@Sx). There involve several highlights below: 
I. The as-built Zn0.06S@Sx particles are in a micro-spherical shape, 
with less central diameter (~2.3 μm) and narrower size distri­
bution than Sx powders (taking one typical sulfurized PAN 
product for example, its size is wide-distributed and as large as 
~20.7 μm on average; See Fig. S1a-b). These features enable a 
notable rise by 26-31% in physical tap/compaction densities, 
benefiting later electrode film pasting (actual S loadings surpass 
~10.0 mg cm-2 under a unified 80 μm film thickness) and superb 
EV implementation (Fig. 1a);
II. Intact shielding of outer elastic Sx layers averts the direct expo­
sure of S8-rich cores to ester solvents, and also helps to accom­
modate Li+ insertion-induced volume expansions. While for 
cathode kinetics, this Sx shell makes notable capacity contribu­
tions, offers prompt Li+-transfer routes to guarantee solid-solid 
phase changes for inner thionic actives, and foremost enables

III. Lithiophilic polar ZnS nanoclusters (~4 nm) dispersed in cores

electrovalent variations. Upon discharge depth, Li+ tends to

solid-solid changes to Li2S, achieving an ultra-high S utilization 
ratio of ~89.3% (Fig. 1c).

2. Experimental section
2.1. Material synthesis
Synthesis of ZnS@PAN Microspheres: 10.0 g anhydrous zinc acetate 
(99.99%, Macklin), 10 g thioacetamide (99%, Macklin) and 4.0 g poly­
vinylpyrrolidone (PVP; Molecular weight: 45000-58000; Macklin) are 
dissolved into 500 mL deionized water and magnetically stirred at room 
temperature. The homogeneous solution is then heated by water bath at 
70 ◦C for 12 h. The yielded ZnS nanoparticles are separated by centri­
fugations and washed several times with deionized water. Next, 6 g ZnS 
nanoparticles are ultrasonically dispersed into 200 mL deionized water, 
and 1 mL acrylonitrile (AN; 99%, Macklin), 1 g Na2S2O3 (99%, Macklin) 
and 1.6 g (NH4)2S2O8 (99.99%, Macklin) are stepwise added. The 
mixture solution is heated at 65 ◦C and magnetically stirred for 2 h under 
N2 atmospheres. After filtering/washing, ZnS@PAN nanoparticles are 
collected and ultrasonically dispersed into 600 mL deionized water. 
Last, the solution is magnetically stirred, connected to a peristaltic pump 
and spray-dried at 180 ◦C for the production of ZnS@PAN microspheres.
Synthesis of Zn0.06S@Sx, S8@Sx and Sx: ZnS@PAN microspheres and 
S powders are mixed in a mass ratio of 3:1 and heated at 350 ◦C for 6 h in 
Ar atmospheres to form ZnS@Sx precursors. Afterwards, 1 g ZnS@Sx 
microspheres are added into 100 mL 0.3 M Fe(NO3)3 solutions with 
magnetic stirring at room temperature for 30 h to convert ZnS compo­
nent to S by following the reaction equation of [34]: 
ZnS(S) + 2Fe3+
(aq)→Zn2+
(aq) + S(s) + 2Fe2+
(aq)
(1) 
The final Zn0.06S@Sx microspheres are filtered/washed by deionized 
water, and dried at 60 ◦C for later use. Whereas, S8@Sx microspheres are 
made by the same procedures, except the time for ZnS@Sx soaking in Fe 
(NO3)3 solution is set to 100 h to remove all ZnS components. As for Sx 
synthesis, the commercial PAN (Molecular weight: 150000; Macklin) 
and S mixtures in the same mass ratios are directly vulcanized at 350 ◦C 
for 6 h in Ar flow (50 sccm).
2.2. Samples characterizations
Samples morphology/elemental distribution are probed by field- 
emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JEOL JSM-7800F) 
and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2010F) equipped 
with a high-precision energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). X-ray 
Photoelektron spectroscopy (XPS) data are gained from a Thermo Sci­
entific K-Alpha electron spectrometer. X-ray absorption near-edge 
structure (XANES) detections are made by an easyXAFS300+ equip­
ment, and corresponding results are analyzed by the Athena software 
package. The UV absorption spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific, GENESYS 
40 Vis) of electrolyte is performed by sealing the solutions in capillary 
tubes within a wavelength range of 250–900 nm. The powder X-Ray 
diffraction (XRD) patterns are obtained by a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray 
diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (40 kV/40 mA; 2θ range: 10-60◦). 
For in-situ Raman testing (HORIBA, LabRAM HR Evolution, Laser 
source: 532 nm), a CR2032 positive electrode shell with a 2 mm 
diameter punch is designedly utilized, and Raman signals are collected 
in a wavenumber of 100-1000 cm-1. The physical powder tap/compac­
tion densities are measured by a density tester (AccuPyc 1340). 
Elemental weight ratios in Zn0.06S@Sx are determined by the Agilent 
7800 elemental analyzer (ICP-MS) and thermogravimetric analysis 
(TGA; TA Instruments, SDT Q600) under N2 atmospheres (ramped rate: 
10 ◦C min-1). In details, the overall S (~64.7%) and Zn (~7.4%) contents 
are initially ascertained by ICP testing. The S in ZnS is then determined 
to be ~3.6% according to the Zn:S molar ratio, and the S8 component 
(~41.5%) is measured by TGA. The remained Sx is calculated at a level 
of ~19.6% (64.7%-3.6%-41.5%=19.6%).
2.3. Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemical testing is performed using CR2032 coin-type cells 
assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox (MIXROUNA; H2O<0.1 ppm, O2<0.1 
ppm), with microporous polypropylene (PP) membrane (Celgard 2400) 
and Li anode foils (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.; thickness: 200 μm) as 
the counter/reference electrodes. Cathodes are made by mixing 
Zn0.06S@Sx (or S8@Sx, Sx), carbon black and sodium alginate (SA; Mo­
lecular weight: 140000-150000; Macklin) with a weight ratio of 
9.0:0.5:0.5 in deionized water to form homogenized slurries. Then, the 
slurry is pasted onto an Al foil and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 ◦C for 
overnight. Later, electrode films (S areal loading: ~8.5-10.0 mg cm-2) 
are cut into 12 mm diameter disks. The used ester electrolyte is 1.0 M 
LiPF6 dissolved in ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC) 
and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC, 1:1:1 vol%). For pouch cells, 10 wt% 
Si-carbon/90 wt% graphite hybrids coated on Cu foils are prelithiated 
(by directly attaching to Li in electrolytes for 20 h) and serve as the 
matched anodes (N/P ratio: ~1.1:1). Subsequently, 3~9 cathode pieces 
and 4~10 anode pieces are cut into 4 × 5~5 × 8 cm2 sheets, and stacked 
by layer-by-layer PP membrane separations. The injected electrolyte is 
nearby an electrolyte/S ratio of ~2 μL mgs
-1. After cells formation, the 
nominal capacity of assembled pouch cells at room temperature is in a 
range of 0.5-2.5 Ah. Galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) tests are 
conducted in a voltage range of 1.0-3.0 V using professional battery 
testers (NEWARE). The cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) are performed on a Corrtest electro­
chemical station (CS3250M). The galvanostatic intermittent titration 
technique (GITT) is performed undergoing a 15 min pulse current (rate: 
0.05 C) followed by a 45 min current pulse relaxation. The EG/EV of cell 
units are calculated by following equations of: 
EG =
U × A × Ms × Qs
Mcathode + Manode + Mmembrene + Melectrolyte + MAl + MCu + Mpackage
(2) 
EV =
U × Ms × Qs
Dcathode + Danode + Dmembrene + DAl + DCu + Dpackage
(3) 
where U, Ms and Qs represent the nominal voltage in discharge process 
(V), actives mass loading per area (mg cm-2), and cathode specific ca­
pacity, respectively. The Mcathode, Manode, Mmembrene, Melectrolyte, MAl, MCu 
and Mpackage refer to the mass for cathode, anode, membrane, electrolyte, 
Al foil, Cu foil and package, while the Dcathode, Danode, Dmembrene, DAl, DCu 
and Dpackage represent the typical thickness value for the cathode, anode, 
membrane, Al foil, Cu foil and package, respectively. For our typical 
pouch cells, the mass data of each component are shown below: Mcathode 
(~2592 mg), Manode (~3056 mg), Mmembrene (~116 mg), Melectrolyte 
(~4620 mg), MAl (~160 mg), MCu (~300 mg) and Mpackage (~190 mg).

3. Results and discussion
The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) images disclose the evolved Zn0.06S@Sx particles 
have a spherical geometric profile (central diameter: ~2.3 μm; Fig. 2a- 
b). The high-resolution TEM observation (Fig. 2c) reveals numerous tiny 
nanocrystals (~4 nm) are distributed in microspheres; both the selected 
area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern and detected lattice fringes 
(0.31 and 0.29 nm) are well indexed to the (111) and (002) facets of 
sphalerite ZnS [38]. The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) 
witnesses the presence of S (~66.8%), Zn (~8.2%), C (~20.2%), N 
(~3.7%) and O (~1.1%) constituents (Fig. 2d). The elemental mapping 
(Fig. 2e) and linear scan (inset in Fig. 2b) uncover the delicate 
Zn0.06S@Sx microstructures, where Zn signals tend to lie in core regions, 
and C/N ones appear at exterior shells. According to the inductively 
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Fig. 2f and Fig. S2a) and 
thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA; Fig. S2b), the overall S content 
reaches ~64.71% (S8: ~41.5%; S in ZnS: ~3.6%; Sx: 19.6%; See 
calculation details in Experiment Section). The X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS; Fig. 2g) reveals four peaks arise at binding energies 
of ~161.0, 162.1, 163.4 and 164.5 eV in S 2p spectrum, successively 
corresponding to S-C and S-S bonds. As for C 1s XPS detections, two 
predominant peaks at 282.0 (C-S) and 284.8 eV (C-C) stem from the Sx 
part. While in Zn 2p spectrum, only weak signals are distinguished at 
~1044.2 (Zn 2p1/2) and ~1021.3 eV (Zn 2p3/2), highly consistent with 
surface-deficient Zn distribution features in sample units [39].
The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy is 
performed to examine the electronic structure/valence state of samples. 
The Zn K-edge energy of Zn0.06S@Sx declines a bit (ΔE: ~0.1 eV, for­
ward the metallic Zn baseline) when compared to that of pure ZnS 
(Fig. 2h). Such a redshift phenomenon suggests there occurs a slight 
decrease in Zn oxidation state. This is tightly associated with peculiar 
coordination environments in Zn0.06S@Sx cases, since polar ZnS electron 
clouds are readily disturbed by adjacent electronegative ⋅Sx⋅ chains (e.g., 
⋅S2⋅/⋅S6⋅ free segments yielded by
S8 ring-opening reactions among thermal vulcanization steps) via δS

δSx

tions would lessen the electrovalent interaction strength of Zn-S bonds 
[40]. Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) profiles (Fig. 2i) 
affirm the ionic polarization-induced contraction in Zn-S coordination 
distance (ΔR = 0.04 Å). The wavelet transform (WT) oscillation com­
parisons for all samples (Fig. 2j and S3a-b) reveal there exist great dif­

value for Zn, ZnS and Zn0.06S@Sx are 7.65, 5.97 and 5.71 Å-1, respec­
tively, once again notarizing the shortened Zn-S coordination distance in 
Zn0.06S@Sx cases [41]. The industrial-concerned density parameters for 
ester electrolyte-compatible Zn0.06S@Sx and Sx cathodes are measured 
as well (Fig. 2k and S4a-b). Zn0.06S@Sx powders own larger tap/com­

revealing their superiorities in achieving practical high-areal-capacity 
cathodes.

Galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) plots reveal Zn0.06S@Sx cathodes 
output a superior reversible capacity of ~893 mAh g-1 over Sx (~557 
mAh g-1) or S8@Sx (~434 mAh g-1). Also note they exhibit the highest 
potential plateau (mid-value: ~1.90 V) and least cell polarization 
(Fig. 3e), highly in line with former CV analysis; even under enlarged 
current rates (from 0.1C to 10C) by 100 times, there is no substantial 
decline in the potential plateau of Zn0.06S@Sx cathodes (Fig. 3f) in 
contrast with others (Fig. S8a-b). Such eminent rate capabilities are 
thanks to the conductive polymeric layers (after full lithiation reactions) 
for long-range electrons transport, and semi-conducting ZnS nano 
“islands” well-dispersed in each S sub-unit for short-range electrons 
transfer (See the schematic in Fig. S9). Differential capacity versus po­
tential plots (Fig. S10) for distinct cathode discharge processes reveal 
that unlike Sx with a single peak (range: ~1.53-2.05 V) centered at 1.74 
V, Zn0.06S@Sx cathodes exhibit several peaks ranging from ~1.53 to 
2.32 V. Such unusual peak signals beyond 1.8 V chiefly stem from 
Zn0.06S core sections; the sharp peak nearby 2.05 V is definitely asso­
ciated with S8 redox conversions. Programmed current-varied cyclic 
records (Fig. 3g) reveal Zn0.06S@Sx cathodes show a salient S utilization 
ratio of ~89.3% (calculated on basis of all usable S species), exceeding 
the level of S8@Sx (~41.8%) or even Sx (~88.1%). At the end cycling, a 
notable capacity of ~751 mAh g-1 is still retained, with a superb capacity 
retention of ~84.1% (whereas, relevant records for S8@Sx and Sx are 
only 188 mAh g-1/~43.3% and 418 mAh g-1/~75%, respectively).
To deep understand Zn0.06S@Sx phase changes in cycling, in-situ 
Raman spectroscopy testing is conducted purposely. Once cells are 
stepwise discharged to 1V, peaks for C-S (302 and 369 cm-1; deriving 
from Sx molecules) and S-S vibration modes (468 and 941 cm-1; in both 
Zn0.06S and Sx phases) turn vanished (Fig. 4a). The absence of C-S bonds 
is ascribed to their evolution into Raman-insensitive C-S- Li+ ones, while 
the S-S bond disappearance is related to chemical conversions into Li-S 
ionic bonds. After charged to 3V, though all C-S and S-S vibration peaks 
are recovered, their declined intensities are related to irreversible losses 
of partial S in initial discharging, as hinted by CV/GCD plots (Fig. S11a- 
b) [43,44]. In-situ XRD detections (Fig. 4b) testify the inner S8 compo­
nents indeed participate in redox reactions. Upon discharging, S8 char­
acteristic peaks (~21.9 and 51.3◦) gradually disappear; instead, the Li2S 
peak (~53.1◦) turns dominant at the discharge end; in subsequent 
charging, this Li2S peak diminishes and S8 ones re-appear accordingly 
[45]. In-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests and the 
distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis are also performed 
(Fig. S12a-b). Prior to the 1st discharge, there only exists one semicircle 
that corresponds to the charge-transfer resistance (Rct). Afterwards, a 
new semicircle present in later charge/discharge periods suggests the 
CEI layers formation [46]. SEM observations (Fig. S13a-d) confirm a 
gel-like layer covers on all surfaces of discharged Zn0.06S@Sx to 1.4 V, 
and this film would be more compact/thick upon the discharge depth. 
After hundreds of cycles, the CEI layers still remain intact and dense 
(Fig. S12 c and S13 e-f). The C 1s, O 1s and Li 1s XPS spectra 
(Fig. S14a-c) verify the generation of ROCOOLi, ROLi, Li2CO3 and spe­
cies after cells operation [47], while the S 2p XPS spectrum (Fig. S14d) 
shows the presence of sulfate and RSOx that evolved by electrochemical

tolerated cell volume expansions and negligible cyclic/voltage plateau

nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NCM)-based ones: ~700 Wh L-1/~300 
Wh kg-1) [52]. To proof their application potential in AI+ robotic fields, 
our cells are deliberately used to drive a writing robot (Fig. 5c and Video 
records). A fully charged cell pack (two pouch cells linked in series; 
average operating voltage: ~3.4 V) can persistently drive the AI+ robot 
to write “SWU” (Southwest University abbreviation) over several hours, 
evidencing its great potential in long-lasting power-supply applications. 
Also, the cyclic/expansion records of pouch cells under 0 ◦C and 45 ◦C 
are provided (Fig. S18 and S19). At 45 ◦C, cells exhibit a high capacity 
retention (~99.1%) and a thickness rise from 1.203 to 1.388 mm (device 
expansion ratio: ~15.4%) after 100 cycles. While at 0 ◦C, they show the 
reduced capacity reduction (down to ~0.48 Ah) and retention (~88.0%) 
at the cycling end, with altered thicknesses from 1.202 to 1.314 mm and 
thus a total expansion ratio of ~9.3%. Such cell behaviors are chiefly 
thanks to the sound actives utilization efficiency and high areal loading 
ratios of Zn0.06S@Sx, almost outperforming those of all reported S 
cathode systems (Fig. 5d) [53–59]. Considering major key parameters 
for practical cells (Fig. 5e and Table S2-3), our configured cells show 
great competitiveness when compared to other state-of-the-art para­
digms [53–59].

4. Conclusion
In summary, we have developed ester-compatible Zn0.06S@Sx 
microsphere cathodes featured by high tap/compaction densities 
(~0.81 g cm-3/~1.93 g cm-3) and functionalized core-shell architec­
tures. The in-situ Raman and XRD testing validate all thionic components 
take part in redox reactions and make capacity contributions. Addi­
tionally, the in-situ EIS tests coupled with DRT analysis unveil the gen­
eration of steady CEI layers on cathode surfaces. XANES detections and 
corresponding WT-EXAFS oscillation unveil ZnS nano domains suffer 
from chemical coordination/electrovalent changes upon cells operation, 
driven by spontaneous Li+ attractions and ionic electronegativity com­
petitions. The DFT analysis also accounts for the positive role in boosting 
the transformation of S8 into Sx as well as their phase conversions. The 
integration of active S8, Sx and ZnS nanoclusters enables a high usable S 
active content of ~64.7%, and the actual cathode areal loading even 
reaches ~14.4 mg cm-2 under a film thickness of 80 μm. Notably, such 
thick cathodes can deliver a reversible discharge capacity of ~893 mAh 
g-1 (0.1 C) and impressive capacity retention of 84.1% after all cycling. 
The assembled pouch cells exhibit ultrahigh EV/EG value of 883.3 W h L- 
1/398.6 W h kg-1 under ultra-high cathode areal loading, and lean ester 
electrolyte conditions, holding good promise in power supplies for AI+
robotic applications.
