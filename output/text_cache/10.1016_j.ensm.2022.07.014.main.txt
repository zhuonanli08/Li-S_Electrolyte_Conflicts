A B S T R A C T  
High energy density lithium (Li) metal batteries (LMBs) hold great promise to become next-generation energy 
storage devices. However, their commercialization process is severely hindered by low Coulombic efficiency (CE) 
and potential safety hazard caused by non-uniform Li deposition and flammable electrolytes. Herein, a brand- 
new ultralow concentration (0.3 M) mixed ether electrolyte is proposed to regulate the electrolyte structure, 
flammability and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) composition for LMBs. The high proportion of flame retarded 
inert solvent (94% by volume) remarkably improves the security of LMBs and promotes anions involving in Li+
solvent sheath structures. Therefore, differing from Li+−solvent dominant solvent sheaths in traditional low 
concentration electrolyte, the abundant Li+−anion aggregate cluster in this ULCE could lead to sufficient 
decomposition of anion and formation of inorganic-rich SEI. Based on this electrolyte design, the average Li 
deposition/stripping CE reaches > 99.3% under 2 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 among 250 cycles. Moreover, 
superior electrochemical performance of Li||Li4Ti5O12 and Li||sulfur full cells also confirm the practical appli­
cation value of this ULCE. This work proposes a fresh strategy to design low concentration electrolytes with 
unique solvated structures for high energy density metal batteries.   
1. Introduction 
High energy density and safe rechargeable batteries are utterly 
crucial for the nowadays society [1,2]. Traditional lithium (Li) ion 
batteries (LIBs) cannot satisfy the ever-increasing demand due to the 
limitation of their theoretical energy density and high flammability of 
electrolytes. Li metal batteries (LMBs) have been considered as quite 
promising candidates because of most negative electrode potential 
(-3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode) and high theoretical specific 
capacity (3860 mAh g−1) of Li metal anode (LMA) [3,4]. The 
lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) using LMA and high-capacity sulfur 
cathode (theoretical specific capacity: 1675 mAh g−1) have a high 
theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 [5]. However, the applica­
tion of LMB is still severely hindered by uncontrolled growth of Li 
dendrite and resultant low Coulombic efficiency (CE), short circuit, 
burning risk and short cycling lifespan of battery [6]. In a common 
perception, the dendritic Li deposition and growth are caused by het­
erogeneous and non-uniform solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) 
generated from the solvent-leading decomposition in routine concen­
tration (1-2 mol L−1) electrolytes [7,8]. For example, commercial car­
bonate electrolytes have successfully promoted the rapid development 
of graphite-based LIBs [9]. Nevertheless, this electrolyte is imperfect for 
LMB due to the high corrosivity of carbonate ester on LMA [10]. In this 
case, the ether-based electrolytes have been largely employed in LMBs, 
especially LSBs, because of their relatively high stability on anode side 
[11,12]. However, this ordinary ether-based electrolyte still cannot 
achieve high CEs and long-term cycling on account of the disordered 
electrolyte structures. In these electrolytes, the solvents and anions are 
competitive to be reduced on Li metal surface, thereby forming an 
inorganic/organic hybrid SEI. The inorganic and organic components in 
SEI possess different characteristics in rigidity, interfacial energy and Li 
affinity, which could cause non-uniform Li deposition and stripping 
[13]. Therefore, optimizing the compositions of SEI through regulating 
electrolyte components and solvent sheath structures is significant for 
driving the practical application of LMBs.

Evidently, the simultaneous realization of high CE (> 99%) and long 
cycling stability (> 200 cycles) under rigorous cycling conditions (≥1 
mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2) is quite difficult in traditional HCEs or 
LHCEs due to their insufficient Li+−anion interaction and unstable SEI 
films. It is worth noting that 0.1% improvement in CE is precious and 
difficult when > 99% because of the inherent irreversibility of LMA 
[21]. 
Actually, the AGGs in Li+ solvent sheath can be specifically divided 
into AGG-1 (FSI−
coordinating to two Li+) and AGG-2 (FSI−
coordi­
nating to three or more Li+). The AGG-2 possesses much better adjusting 
ability to SEI compared to AGG-1 and CIP. As proved, increasing the 
inert/active (I/A) solvent volume ratio is effective to promote the for­
mation of AGG-2 [22]. However, the most reported LHCEs only possess 
low I/A ratio of < 5, which is mainly restricted by intrinsic dissolving 
capability of active solvent and incompatibility between active and inert 
solvents at high I/A ratios [23–25]. In this case, picking appropriate 
active/inert solvent pairs and lowering the apparent concentration of 
electrolyte can further increase I/A ratio, thus facilitating the Li+−anion 
interaction and formation of inorganic-rich SEI. Additionally, the low 
concentration electrolyte possesses significant advantages for batteries, 
such as low viscosity and good wettability [26]. However, the realiza­
tion of low concentration electrolyte in LMB is an enormous challenge in 
traditional active solvent-rich electrolyte due to high reducibility of Li 
metal and resultant unstable and thick organic-rich SEI derived from the 
continuous decomposition of Li+−solvent coordinations during cycling, 
and the battery would fail if the dissociative Li+ is insufficient. There­
fore, novel strategies and Li salt/solvents combinations are highly ex­
pected to construct desired high-performance low concentration 
electrolytes and optimized SEI for LMBs. 
Here, we employed a non-flammable ultralow concentration (0.3 M) 
mixed ether electrolyte (ULCE) with a high I/A ratio of 15.7 to construct 
stable and uniform SEI on LMA. In this ULCE, 1, 2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) was employed as a model active solvent and flame retarded 
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) was an 
inert solvent. Benefiting from the high solvent power and great reduc­
tive stability of DME and presence of a high amount of inert HFE (94% 
by vol.), ULCE accelerates the interaction between Li+ and anions and 
the decomposition of FSI−, generates a stable anion-derived inorganic- 
rich SEI. Based on this electrolyte design, the LMA exhibits a high 
average Li deposition/stripping CE of > 99.3% with a low fluctuation of 
0.6% under the current density of 2 mA cm−2 and cycling capacity of 1 
mAh cm−2 among 250 cycles. Besides, both Li||Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) and Li|| 
sulfur (S) full cells employing the optimized ULCE display greatly 
increased electrochemical performance compared to traditional car­
bonate- and ether-based electrolytes. Moreover, other ULCEs based on 
different active ethers, including diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2), 
triethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G3), tetraethylene glycol dimethyl 
ether (G4) and tetrahydrofuran (THF), also demonstrated similar 
enhancement for LMBs. 
2. Results and discussions 
Here we utilized DME as a model active solvent to construct ULCE of 
0.3 M LiFSI dissolved in DME/HFE (1:15.7 by vol., 0.3-LDH) mixed 
ethers with localized concentration of 5 M, due to the strong solvent 
power and high donor number (DN, 20) of DME [27]. Traditional LHCE 
of 1 M LiFSI dissolved in DME/HFE (1:4 by vol., 1-LDH) and HCE of 5 M 
LiFSI in DME (5-LD) were prepared for comparison. In addition, other 
contrast electrolytes were also considered, including routine concen­
tration electrolyte of 1 M LiFSI in DME (1-LD), ordinary low concen­
tration electrolyte of 0.3 M LiFSI in DME (0.3-LD), commercial 
carbonate-based electrolyte of 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate 
(EC)/diethyl carbonate (DEC) (1:1 by vol.) and commercial ether-based 
electrolyte of 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl) imide 
(LiTFSI) in 1, 3-dioxolane (DOL)/DME (1:1 by vol.). Initially, the mul­
tiple physical properties of different electrolytes were investigated as 
shown in Figs. 1 and S1, S2. The ion conductivity test shows that 1-LD 
electrolyte owns the highest conductivity of 17.14 mS cm−1. The in­
crease or decrease of concentration would lower ion conductivity of 
electrolytes. Though ULCE of 0.3-LDH possesses a relatively low ion 
conductivity of 0.85 mS cm−1, it still exceeds most solid electrolytes and 
is sufficient for use in batteries [28–30]. Besides, the ultralow concen­
tration makes 0.3-LDH possess a lower viscosity of 1.68 mPa s compared 
with HCE (5-LD) and LHCE (1-LDH) electrolytes, which is beneficial for 
wettability and capacity delivery. The detailed ion conductivity and 
viscosity data are listed in Table S2. The contact angles of different 
electrolytes on separators were also measured as displayed in Figs. 1b–d 
and S1a–d. The 0.3-LD and 0.3-LDH exhibit the lowest contact angle of 
20.8◦, much less than other electrolytes especially high concentration 
5-LD electrolyte (68.6◦). The low concentration induced high wettability 
is favorable to the absorption of electrolyte in separator, accelerating Li+
transfer kinetics. Moreover, the flammability tests of electrolytes dis­
played in Movies S1-7 confirm the high flame retardant capability of 
0.3-LDH electrolyte, while other electrolytes all have different levels of 
flammability. This high flame retardancy is derived from the high con­
tent (94% by vol.) of non-flammable HFE in 0.3-LDH electrolyte. 
Though the flame of 1-LDH electrolyte is relatively small due to its 80% 
volume content of HFE, it is still burnable and could cause safety hazard 
in practical application. 
To investigate the solvent sheath structures of different electrolytes, 
Raman spectra were collected as shown in Fig. 1h. Firstly, the vibration 
bands of free DME (821 and 849 cm−1) and free HFE (835 and 855 cm−1) 
molecules can be clearly observed in their spectra profiles. As for routine 
concentration (1-LD) and ordinary low concentration electrolytes (0.3- 
LD), the coordinated DME emerges at 876 cm−1 and free DME is still 
present [31]. In HCE, LHCE and ULCE, the coordinated DME becomes 
entirely prominent and free DME disappears, indicating that the DME 
molecules completely participate in Li+ solvent sheaths in high con­
centration or localized high concentration systems. Besides, the bands 
corresponding to HFE molecules in 1-LDH and 0.3-LDH are invariable 
compared with pure HFE solvent, suggesting that the inert HFE cannot 
be engaged in Li+ solvent sheath structures. Furthermore, the detailed 
analyses of the coordination situations of Li+ and FSI−
in different 
electrolytes were conducted as shown in the enlarged Raman spectra

(Fig. 1i and j). The vibration bands can be deconvolved into free FSI−,

and CIP are dominant with few AGGs. By contrast, the peak area ratio of 
AGG-1 in 5-LD is 62.8%, which is caused by high concentration induced 
strong interaction between Li+ and FSI−. Further, the AGG-2 emerges

The reversibility of Li deposition/stripping was evaluated by CE tests 
of Li||Cu cells using smooth Cu foils as working electrodes (Fig. S3). 
Here two methods were employed to estimate the average CEs. The Li 
deposition/stripping CE in 0.3-LDH electrolyte can maintain 99.04% 
after 10 cycles using Method-1 (Fig. 2a) [34]. And the CEs of 1-LDH, 
5-LD, 1-LD and 0.3-LD electrolytes are 98.85%, 98.00%, 97.50% and 
96.47%, respectively. This results indicate that the anion-dominating 
solvent sheath is favorable to Li cycling reversibility. Besides, the Li 
CEs of commercial carbonate- and ether- based electrolytes are even 
lower to 95.57% and 91.44% due to their disordered solvent sheath 
structures. For long-term tests of 100 cycles using Method-1, the CE of 
0.3-LDH electrolyte can reach a high value of 99.51%, while the com­
mercial electrolytes even cannot support 100 cycles tests with huge 
voltage polarizations (Fig. 2b). When employing Method-2, the average 
CEs of repeated Li deposition and stripping in different electrolytes are 
still contrasting. Fig. 2c–h display the CEs and polarization curves of Li|| 
Cu cells in HCE, LHCE and ULCE. The average CE among 250 cycles after 
stabilization in ULCE can exceed 99.3% with a tiny fluctuation of 0.6% 
under the current density of 2 mA cm−2 and cycling capacity of 1 mAh 
cm−2. Meanwhile, voltage polarization during the whole cycling is also 
stable and less than 100 mV. By contrast, the average CEs are lower and 
fluctuations are more severe in LHCE and HCE, corresponding to 99.1%, 
1.4% and 98.8%, 1.2%, respectively. The polarizations of Li||Cu cells in 
LHCE and HCE gradually increase with cycling due to the unstable in­
terfaces between electrodes and electrolytes. Besides, to explore the 
possibility of ULCEs with lower concentrations and higher I/A ratios, we 
also prepared the electrolytes of 0.1 M (I/A = 49) and 0.2 M (I/A = 24) 
LiFSI dissolved in the same mixed ethers with a constant localized 
concentration of 5 M (Fig. S4). The voltage-time profiles show that the 
electrolytes with an overall concentration < 0.3 M cannot support 
long-term cycling in this designing scheme. In addition, the Li CEs and 
polarization profiles of routine concentration electrolyte (1-LD) and 
ordinary low concentration electrolyte (0.3-LD) are displayed in Fig. S5, 
which delivered low CEs and inferior cycling performance due to the 
free FSI−/CIP-dominated Li+ solvent sheaths and unstable interfaces. 
The CE of commercial ether electrolyte also falls off after 200 cycles and 
was even worse in commercial carbonate electrolyte with severe fading 
and irregular polarization (Fig. S6).

its high stability on LMA. Therefore, DME solvent could minimally affect 
the regulating function of anion in SEI structures. The low HOMO value 
of HFE suggests its adaptability of different battery systems. Considering

The Li deposition morphology was tested at the current density of 1 
mA cm−2 and deposition capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. As seen, the Li metal 
deposit in 0.3-LDH exhibits a uniform surface and dense structure with a 
thickness of only 7.5 μm (Fig. 4a, b). While in 1-LDH and 5-LD elec­
trolytes, the Li deposits present relatively loose morphology with a 
certain amount of Li dendrites. The thicknesses of Li deposits are 12 and 
14 μm in 1-LDH and 5-LD electrolytes, respectively. The apparent Li 
deposition morphology is strongly correlated with microcosmic SEI film 
morphology and structure. Therefore, cryogenic-transmission electron 
microscopy (cryo-TEM), exhibiting tremendous potential in monitoring 
beam and air-sensitive materials, was employed to further detect the SEI 
characteristics in different electrolytes [40]. Initially, the Cu grids with 
deposited Li were observed by SEM (Fig. S10). The Li deposits in 
0.3-LDH electrolyte are uniform with bulk morphology. However, 
non-uniform Li deposits with holes and dendrites are clearly detected in 
other electrolytes. Fig. 4c shows that the SEI thickness formed in 
0.3-LDH electrolyte is about 20 nm. By contrast, the SEI thicknesses in 
1-LDH and 5-LD electrolytes are > 100 nm. This result reveals that the 
primeval SEI in 0.3-LDH is stable and can prevent continuous decom­
position of solvent and Li salt to form thick SEI film. The compact and 
thin SEI film in 0.3-LDH is beneficial to Li+ transport and formation of 
uniform and dense Li blocks. Besides, the Li deposition morphology in 
contrast electrolytes of 1-LD and 0.3-LD was also investigated, which 
show even looser surface with larger Li deposition thickness (Fig. S11).

As a result, based on the Raman spectra, DFT calculations, MD 
simulations and XPS data analyses, we drew the schematic diagram of 
solvent sheath structures and the corresponding SEI in traditional HCE 
(5-LD) and new-type ULCE (0.3-LDH) in this work (Fig. 5i). The more 
AGGs, especially for AGG-2, in ULCE have strong tendency to be reduced 
on LMA and can introduce firm inorganic-rich SEI on surface, which 
effectively obstruct the contact between LMA and electrolyte and sub­
sequent decomposition of electrolyte. While in HCE, the insufficient 
decomposition of FSI−and competitive decomposition of solvents bring 
about heterogeneous inorganic/organic hybrid SEI. The results above 
confirm that regulation of electrolyte structures can effectively alter the 
components of SEI and thereby influence Li deposition behaviors.

3. Conclusions 
In summary, a non-flammable ultralow concentration mixed ether 
electrolyte was proposed to construct anion-derived inorganic-rich SEI 
by regulating the Li+−FSI−solvent sheath structures. The high content 
of HFE inert solvent could grant ULCE excellent fire resistance and 
effectively compress the space of active region and promote the inter­
action between Li+ and FSI−anions. Therefore, the new-type aggregate 
cluster of AGG-2, with a strong tendency to be reduced on LMA, was 
increased in electrolyte, resulting in sufficient decomposition of FSI−
and more inorganic components in SEI. This inorganic-rich SEI could 
remarkably promote uniform and dense Li deposits and highly reversible 
Li deposition and stripping. The average Li CE on Cu substrate was up to 
99.51% during 100 cycles at 1 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2 and reaches >
99.3% among 250 cycles at 2 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2 in repeated cycling. 
Besides, both Li||LTO and Li||S full cells using ULCE displayed 
outstanding cycling stability mainly because of the optimized interface 
structures. This work widens the available concentration range of elec­
trolytes and provides new guidelines to regulate the electrolyte and SEI 
structures, which can also be utilized in other high energy density metal 
batteries.
