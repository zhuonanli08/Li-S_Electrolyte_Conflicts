Effect of Electrolyte Composition on Performance and
Stability of Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Yuki Ishino, Keitaro Takahashi, Wataru Murata, Yasuhiro Umebayashi, Seiji Tsuzuki,
Masayoshi Watanabe, Minori Kamaya, and Shiro Seki*
The development of new electrolytes for lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries is
important. Electrolytes based on 1:1 glyme:Liþ complexes can be locally
destroyed to form free glyme during charge/discharge cycles. Free glyme can
cause Li2Sx dissolution, which is a major factor in Li–S battery degradation. To
decrease the local generation of free glyme, the glyme:Liþ electrolytes with
higher proportions of Li salts are developed to control the Li2Sx dissolution into
the electrolyte. The chemical solubility of Li2S8 and the charge/discharge
properties of Li–S cells are investigated by varying the glyme:Li salt molar ratio.
Nonequimolar (high Li salt concentration) glyme:Li salt mixture electrolytes
directly suppress the Li2S8 dissolution and improve the charge/discharge
properties (cycling and coulombic efﬁciency) of Li–S cells. The electrolyte
composition can control the chemical stability of reactive intermediates at the
Li2Sx electrode and provide high-performance Li–S cells for next-generation
batteries.
Li-ion batteries have high energy densities and have the potential
for scale-up from consumer electric devices to large-scale appli-
cations such as electric vehicles.[1,2] The demands for improved
performances (in particular, energy density) and resources
increase year-by-year, and the development of low-cost and
high-performance batteries is important. Li–S batteries have
attracted attention as candidates for next-generation secondary-
battery systems.[3] Elemental S, which is positive electrode
(PE) material, forms compounds with Li during multistep
charge/discharge operations. The overall
chemical reaction between S and Liþ is

However, during the multistep reaction
between Liþ and S shown in Equation (1),
Li2Sx is formed and dissolved into the
electrolyte solution.[4] The dissolved Li2Sx
diffuses to the Li metal negative electrode
(NE) and triggers a redox shuttle phenom-
enon, which causes rapid capacity fading of
cells.[5,6] The use of solvate ionic liquids
(SILs), which consist of a low-molecular
weight ether (glyme) and a Li salt at a high
concentration, has been reported to be a
good option for Li–S batteries.[7] Glyme
and a Li salt interact strongly, similar to the case in complexes,
and have high thermal and electrochemical stabilities.[8] SILs
have low Lewis acidities/basicities and weak coordinating abilities.
Those physical properties enable the suppression of the Li2Sx
dissolution into electrolytes and give stable charge/discharge for
over 800 cycles.[9] SILs are generally 1:1 molar complexes of a
multioxygen-containing glyme and a Li salt under static condi-
tions. In the charge/discharge process at electrode interfaces,
the complex with a 1:1 molar ratio is destroyed and free glyme
is temporally formed. Free glyme readily reacts with Li2Sx or
causes its dissolution into the electrolyte. Recently, we reported
that the use of nonequimolar (high Li salt concentration) SILs
can compensate for local composition changes and suppress
the formation of free glymes,[10] even though high-concentrated
electrolytes were reported as effective design for high-performance
Li–S batteries. In this study, we investigated the solubility of Li2Sx
(x ¼ 8) into SILs of various compositions and the correlation bet-
ween Li–S battery performances and SIL composition.[11,12]

Figure 2a shows photographs of [Li(G3)x]TFSA (x ¼ 0.8, 0.9,
1.0, 1.11, and 1.25) solutions of Li2S8; the solutions were diluted
four times with 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl
ether (HFE). Addition of HFE did not change the order of color
tones as shown in Figure 2. This shows that HFE does not
change the solvated structure of the electrolyte and only has a
simple dilution effect.[13] In practical applications in batteries,
a combination of high-mobility reactive ionic species (in this
study, Li) and an electrolyte with high electrochemical stability
is important, and the addition of HFE could be used to improve
the performances of Li–S cells. Figure 2b shows the UV–vis
spectra of [Li(G3)x]TFSA (x ¼ 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.11, and 1.25) solu-
tions of Li2S8, which were diluted four times with HFE. At
x ¼ 1.11 and 1.25, i.e., large amount of free glyme,[14] the UV
absorbance maximum values were above the measurement
limit, i.e., 400 nm. As the LiTFSA concentration increased, the
peak decreased to around 260 nm (attributed Li2S8).[15]
Figure 3a shows the charge/discharge curves of the [S-PE |
[Li(G3)0.8]TFSA | Li metal NE] cell. The temperature was set at
318 K to eliminate the viscosity effects and accelerate the cycling
life tests. In this study, the initial discharge and charge cycles
were deﬁned as the ﬁrst and second cycles, respectively. The ini-
tial discharge capacity of each Li–S cell (n ¼ 3) was in the range
1100–1300 mAh g1, which corresponds to 65%–80% of the the-
oretical capacity of elemental S. The discharge curve contained
two voltage plateaus, at around 2.2 and 2.0 V. Figure 4 also shows
the discharge capacities (b), calculated relative capacities (c),
and coulombic efﬁciencies (d) of the [S-PE | [Li(G3)x]TFSA | Li
metal NE] cells during cycling. The cycling performances of the
prepared cells clearly improved with increasing LiTFSA concentra-
tion; this result reﬂects the solubility of Li2Sx, which is one of the
main factors in the degradation of the Li–S batteries. The retained
discharge capacities after 50 cycles were 43.0% ([Li(G3)1.25]TFSA),
45.4% ([Li(G3)1]TFSA), and 64.7% ([Li(G3)0.8]TFSA), although
the measurement temperature was relatively high (this affects
the solubility of Li2Sx). A similar improvement in coulombic
efﬁciency was observed with increasing LiTFSA concentration.
The cell with [Li(G3)0.8]TFSA retained a coulombic efﬁciency
above 98% after more than 50 cycles; this is a high value for
a Li–S battery operated at high temperature. These results con-
ﬁrm that excess LiTFSA (carrier ionic species) in G3 (glyme-
based solvent) leads to the formation of rigid complex cations,
and these ionic species compensate for the charge/discharge
of S8 and Li2S. This improves the electrolyte/PE (S) interface
in the charge/discharge process and leads to longer cycling
lifetimes of Li–S batteries.

in a dry argon-ﬁlled glove box ([O2] < 1 ppm, [H2O] < 1 ppm, Miwa
MFG Co., Ltd.). LiTFSA and G3 were weighted in various compo-
sition ratios ([Li(G3)x]TFSA, x ¼ 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.11, and 1.25, respectively)
and stirred in a sample bottle to obtain a homogeneous liquid
electrolyte.
Dissolution Tests of Li2Sx: Speciﬁc lithium polysulﬁde was fabricated
for dissolution tests for electrolytes. Li2S8 was prepared by mixing S8
(purity >99.99%, Japan Pure Chemical Co., Ltd.) and Li2S (Sigma-Aldrich)
to 7:8 in molar ratio in the glove box. Each Li2S8 saturated [Li(G3)x]
TFSA solutions (0.3 wt.%) was diluted to 40 times (molar ratio) with
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (HFE, Daikin indus-
tries Co., Ltd., Figure 1), and photo-absorbance was measured using
UV–vis spectrometer (V630, Jasco) at room temperature.

electrically conductive additive, and polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP K30,
