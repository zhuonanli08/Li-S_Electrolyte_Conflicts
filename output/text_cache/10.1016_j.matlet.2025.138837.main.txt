A B S T R A C T
Current research on wide-temperature lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries mainly focuses on electrocatalyst design and 
synthesis. However, their performance across a wide temperature range still falls short of practical requirements. 
To address this, it is crucial to examine the factors affecting Li-S battery performance at both high and low 
temperatures. This study investigates electrolyte formulations suitable for extreme temperatures, beginning with 
simplified recipes. Our goal is to develop Li-S batteries with low self-discharge, high sulfur loading, and robust 
wide-temperature performance. Results show that cells with 0.2 M and 1.0 M electrolytes exhibited excellent 
cycling stability at −5 ◦C and 60 ◦C, with the 0.2 M electrolyte maintaining 89.1 % capacity after one week rest 
and 100 % capacity retention over 70 cycles at high sulfur loading (5.0 mg⋅cm−2). These findings offer valuable 
insights for developing practical Li-S batteries with wide-temperature operability.
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are a promising technology for next- 
generation energy storage, offering high theoretical energy density 
and cost-effectiveness [1]. However, their practical deployment is hin­
dered by significant challenges, including polysulfide shuttling, limited 
sulfur utilization, and poor cycling stability. These issues are further 
exacerbated under broad temperature conditions (e.g., −5 ◦C–60 ◦C), 
limiting the reliability and performance of Li-S batteries in diverse 
operational environments [2,3]. At elevated temperatures, polysulfide 
dissolution and shuttling destabilize the battery, while at sub-zero 
temperatures, sluggish polysulfide redox kinetics severely reduce 
discharge capacity [4].
To address these challenges, researchers have explored modifications 
to various battery components, such as the sulfur cathode, separator, 
lithium anode, and electrolyte. Advancements in cathode and separator 
designs have improved performance [5–13]. Despite these advance­
ments,

ionic transport kinetics across temperatures, tailoring electrolyte for­
mulations offers a promising avenue for improving wide-temperature 
battery performance [14].
Herein, we investigate ether-based electrolytes through concentra­
tion engineering, focusing on Li+ transport kinetics, cycling stability, 
self-discharge rates, and high-sulfur loading performance. Our findings 
reveal that low-concentration electrolytes (0.2 M) effectively reduce 
self-discharge at room temperatures, while moderate concentrations 
(1.0 M) optimize cycling performance at elevated temperatures. In high- 
sulfur-loading cathodes, low-concentration electrolytes perform better 
under low current densities, whereas higher current densities benefit 
from concentrated formulations. These insights provide a foundation for 
designing electrolytes tailored to wide-temperature Li-S battery appli­
cations, advancing their scalability and practicality.

3. Results and discussion
Raman analysis revealed concentration-dependent solvation dy­
namics (Fig. 1a). Peaks at ~740 (TFSI−), 870 (DME), and 940 cm−1 
(DOL) showed intensified TFSI−signals in high-concentration electro­
lytes (1.6–2.0 M), indicating Li+-TFSI−
ion pairing with reduced free 
solvents [15]. Low concentrations (0.2–0.5 M) exhibited weaker TFSI−
peaks, suggesting Li+ solvation dominance.
EIS measurements correlated solvation with interfacial resistance. 
The 0.2 M electrolyte displayed higher initial impedance (Fig. 1b, 
Fig. S2), reflecting Li+ deficiency induced resistance [16]. All concen­
trations showed impedance growth after 24 h standing (Fig. S3, Table. 
S1), attributable to the passivation layer formation between electrolytes 
and electrodes.
Discharge tests showed that the 0.2 M electrolyte outperformed 
higher concentrated systems in initial capacity and ambient cycling 
stability (Fig. 1c, Fig. S4). This may be because the low-concentration 
electrolyte reduced viscosity and optimized interfacial reactions [17], 
resulting in the best performance at room temperature. Moderate con­
centrations (0.8–1.0 M) optimized capacity retention by balancing ion 
mobility and viscosity, while higher concentrations (1.3–1.6 M) suffered 
from rapid decay due to polarization and limited migration (Fig. S5).
Polarization analysis corroborated these trends (Fig. 1d, Fig. S6). 
Cells with low concentrated electrolytes exhibited higher polarization 
voltages, whereas high concentrated electrolytes showed narrower po­
larization and higher discharge plateaus (Table S2). It indicates that 
electrolyte concentration plays a critical role in balancing conductivity, 
viscosity, and ion-pair dynamics.
Under the sub-zero temperatures (e.g., –5 ◦C), dilute electrolytes may 
enhance ion migration via lower viscosity [18]. The 0.2 M electrolyte 
showed peak conductivity (5.0 × 10−4 S⋅cm−1) due to optimal viscosity- 
mobility balance (Fig. 2a, Table S3). According to the differential form 
of the Arrhenius equation, rapid reaction kinetics corresponds to lower 
activation energy (see Supporting Information for details).
EIS analysis shows that the 0.2 M electrolyte exhibits the lowest 
charge transfer impedance (Fig. 2b, Fig. S7, Table S4). Low-temperature 
cycling indicates that, the capacity retention for the 0.2 M system ranges 
from 828 to 580 mAh⋅g−1 (Fig. 2c, Fig. S8), which is attributed to 
viscosity-driven high Li+ mobility and suppression of the polysulfide 
shuttle [19].
Polarization analysis at the 50th cycle revealed the lowest voltage 
(0.35 V) for the 1.0 M electrolyte, indicating rapid kinetics (Fig. 2d). The 
battery with the 0.2 M electrolyte maintained relatively stable capacity 
during low-temperature cycling, indicating its ability to support long 
cycle life even under low-concentration conditions (Fig. 2e). This 
finding underscores the critical role of electrolyte concentration in 
balancing ionic conductivity, viscosity, and migration kinetics to ach­
ieve optimized low-temperature performance.
Under the high temperatures (e.g., 60◦C), the 1.0 M system showed 
peak ionic conductivity (Fig. 3a, Table S3), reflecting optimal viscosity- 
mobility balance versus 1.3 M, 1.6 M and 2.0 M counterparts.
EIS analysis confirmed 1.0 M electrolyte’s lowest charge transfer 
impedance, indicating superior kinetics (Fig. 3b, Fig. S9, Table S4). 
Higher concentrations (1.3–2.0 M) showed impedance growth from ion-

Polarization analysis revealed the lowest voltage of 0.25 V for 1.0 M 
electrolyte system (Fig. 3d). Long-term cycling test confirmed its

demonstrate that the 1.0 M electrolyte is suitable for high-temperature

The polysulfide dissolution/shuttling generally caused self-discharge 
in Li-S batteries. We tested the self-discharge behaviors for the two 
preferable electrolytes of 0.2 and 1.0 M. The 0.2 M electrolyte exhibited 
a sharp capacity loss of 25.3 % and followed by a capacity recovery of 
14.4 % after resting (Fig. 4a, Fig. S11). This may be attributed to the 
higher solubility of polysulfides in dilute electrolytes (e.g., 0.2  M). 
During the self-discharge stage, some intermediate polysulfides dissolve 
into the electrolyte. After the rest period, these dissolved species can re- 
participate in electrochemical reactions [21]. In contrast, the 1.0 M 
electrolyte suffered from irreversible 37.7 % capacity loss due to mini­
mal polysulfide reutilization from higher viscosity (Fig. 4b, Fig. S11).
For practical high-sulfur-loading applications (e.g., 5.0 mg⋅cm−2), 
the rate capability comparison results (Fig. 4c, d) demonstrated that the 
0.2 M electrolyte showed better rate capability under room temperature, 
as well as maintaining decent polarization voltages (Fig. S12, S13). 
However, the 1.0 M electrolyte showed faster capacity decay when the 
current rate switching back to 0.2C.
Practical cycling stability with high-sulfur-loading cathodes was 
tested under different electrolytes. Compared to the 1.0 M system, the 
cell with 0.2 M system maintained 100 % capacity retention after 70 
cycles under the current of 0.2C (Fig. 4e). As for the high-rate cycling at 
1.0C, the 1.0 M electrolyte retained 76 % capacity despite lower initial 
capacity (Fig. 4f). In contrast, the 0.2 M electrolyte suffered rapid decay 
to 360 mAh⋅g−1 at 1.0C. This highlights an important trade-off: low- 
concentration electrolytes (0.2 M) perform better in low-current sce­
narios by minimizing polysulfide losses, while high-concentration

4. Conclusion
This study investigates the impact of electrolyte concentration on the 
wide-temperature performance of Li-S batteries. Key findings include: 
(1) Low-concentration electrolytes enhance low-temperature cyclability 
by reducing viscosity; (2) Moderate concentrations improve high- 
temperature stability by reducing charge transfer resistance; (3) Low 
concentrations help reducing self-discharge behavior, while moderate 
concentrations support high-current operations when with high-sulfur 
cathodes. These insights advance electrolyte design strategies for Li-S 
batteries.
