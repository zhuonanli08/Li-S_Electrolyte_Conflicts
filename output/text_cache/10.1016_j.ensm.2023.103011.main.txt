A B S T R A C T  
The commercialization of lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) faces challenges due to uncontrolled lithium deposition 
and lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) shuttling. In this study, a class of chloropyrazine-based electrolyte additives was 
introduced to address these issues. The results showed that the presence of chloropyrazine facilitated the for­
mation of a robust and smooth organic-inorganic hybrid solid-electrolyte interface (SEI) enriched with LiCl. This 
SEI layer enabled reversible lithium plating/stripping, suppressed dendrite growth, and protected the lithium 
anode from detrimental side reactions with LiPSs. Furthermore, Density functional theory (DFT) results 
demonstrate that chloropyrazine additives effectively modulated the molecular orbital energy levels of LiPSs and 
promoted the kinetics of their conversion reactions. LSBs incorporating chloropyrazine additives demonstrated 
outstanding high-rate performance and long-term cycling stability, with over 800 cycles at 1 C rate and a ca­
pacity retention of 81.6 %. Even under challenging conditions of high sulfur loading (5.8 mg cm−2) and low 
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (5 μL mg−1), the battery demonstrated capacity decay rate of only 0.066 % per cycle 
after 500 cycles at 0.2 C rate. With the highlights of dual-functional chloropyrazine electrolyte additives in 
improving interfacial chemistry and modulating the orbital energy levels of LiPSs, this work offers a promising 
direction for advanced electrolyte design in LSBs.   
1. Introduction 
The rapid growth in global energy demand has driven the develop­
ment of next-generation high-energy-density secondary batteries [1]. 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), with sulfur as the positive electrode 
active material, have garnered significant attention. Their high theo­
retical capacity of up to 1675 mAh g−1 makes them promising candi­
dates for high-energy-density secondary batteries [2]. Additionally, 
sulfur is abundant in nature, cost-effective, and environmentally 
friendly [3–5]. However, during the charge and discharge process, solid 
sulfur is converted into soluble long-chain lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in 
the electrolyte, which can migrate through the separator to the lithium 
anode side, and lead to irreversible loss of active sulfur and rapid ca­
pacity decay [6–10]. Conversely, at the lithium negative electrode, re­
actions between metallic lithium and the electrolyte during discharge, as 
well as the uneven deposition of lithium during charging, result in the 
formation of an unstable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) and uncon­
trolled growth of lithium dendrites [11–13]. This can cause the sepa­
rator to rupture, which leads to direct contact between the cathode and 
anode, and ultimately results in a short circuit [14–16]. To address these 
challenges, researchers have developed various strategies. In the posi­
tive electrode, Nazar et al. [17] pioneered the use of a highly ordered 
mesoporous carbon material (CMK-3) to achieve high reversible ca­
pacity by encapsulating of S, LiPSs, and the final discharge product 
Li2S2/Li2S. Subsequently, using carbonaceous materials as carriers for 
sulfur has become an effective approach to suppress the shuttle effect 
caused by soluble LiPSs [18]. For the lithium negative electrode, serious 
issues arising from lithium dendrite growth and the shuttle effect with 
soluble LiPSs have been mitigated by combining other novel methods, 
such as using three-dimensional current collectors [19–21], creating 
artificial solid electrolyte interfaces, and introducing functional barrier 
interlayers such as protective films and coatings for artificial SEI layers

Compared with the above strategies, introducing additives into the 
electrolyte is a more economical, and convenient way to enhance bat­
tery performance. Even small amounts of electrolyte additives can 
significantly alter the properties of electrolyte [27,28]. For example, 
additives like LiNO3 [29], Ce(NO3)3 [30], LiN3 [31], and nitrogen-rich 
imidazoles [32] have been found to be effective in improving the 
physical and chemical characteristics of the electrode-electrolyte inter­
face in LSBs. They can mitigate the shuttle effect of LiPSs, reduce the 
activation energy for Li2S oxidation, accelerate the kinetics of poly­
sulfide conversion reactions, alleviate interface reactions between LiPSs 
and Li, and promote uniform Li deposition to protect the lithium metal 
anode. Recently, researchers have discovered that thionyl chloride 
(SOCl2) can react with lithium metal to build an SEI layer rich in LiCl 
[33].This layer prevents the growth of lithium dendrites and inhibits 
direct contact between the electrolyte and lithium metal .LSBs with 
SOCl2 additives have exhibited exceptionally high initial discharge ca­
pacities and high reversible capacities of 1021 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles. 
Halides can also help reinforce the in-situ formation of SEI. Species like 
LiF [34], LiCl [35], and LiBr [36] are considered stable SEI components. 
These results demonstrate that halogen-containing organic electrolyte 
additives play a beneficial role in regulating the electrode-electrolyte 
interface and interface reactions in LSBs. 
In this study, chloropyrazine derivatives were proposed as additives 
in LSB electrolytes based on several considerations. Firstly, chloropyr­
azine molecules can undergo nucleophilic substitution reactions with 
LiPSs [37], leading to the formation of organic lithium salts C4H3N2SxLi 
(Li-Sx-2,5-DCP) and LiCl(x = 1,4,6,8). These compounds, along with 
other lithium species, deposit on the lithium anode and form a stable 
organic-inorganic hybrid SEI enriched with LiCl. The dense SEI interface 
effectively passivates the lithium anode and prevents continuous lithium 
consumption by solvents and LiPSs. As a result, a smooth and dense 
surface morphology is formed on the lithium anode. This surface 
morphology plays a crucial role in providing protection to the lithium 
metal and contributes to maintaining stable cycling performance during 
battery operation [6]. Secondly, the nitrogen atoms in chloropyrazine 
molecules can interact with lithium atoms in LiPSs. This interaction 
would influence the molecular orbital energy levels of LiPSs and accel­
erate their mutual conversion kinetics [38]. The dual-functional chlor­
opyrazine molecules were expected to enhance the polysulfide 
conversion kinetics in LSBs and improve the interfacial chemistry of the

2.2. Effect of additives on the lithium anode 
To investigate the protective effect of the pyrazine additives on the 
lithium anode, symmetric Li||Li cells were assembled to explore the 
modulation of the additives on lithium deposition and dissolution 
behavior. Fig. 2a shows the voltage-time curves of different symmetric 
lithium cells at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 
mAh cm−2. As seen, the absence of pyrazine additives in the cell results 
in significant voltage fluctuations, high overpotential (100 mV), and a 
short circuit after 300 h of cycling. This is caused by the rupture of the 
SEI layer, which exposes the lithium metal and leads to rapid and un­
even growth of lithium dendrites and disordered deposition. In contrast, 
Fig. 2b demonstrates that the cells with pyrazine additives exhibit 
excellent stability within 800 h low overpotentials of 70 mV (PZ), 25 mV 
(2-CP) and 15 mV (2,5-DCP), and as well as their excellent battery ki­
netics. This suggests that the addition of pyrazine additives can provide 
a stable SEI layer, inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites, and ensure 
stable cycling performance of the battery. The plating/stripping tests 
were further conducted in Fig. 2c at different current densities of 1‒4 
mA cm−2 under a capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. As shown, the cell with 2,5- 
DCP additive exhibits the smallest overpotential. With increasing cur­
rent density from 1 mA cm−2 to 4 mA cm−2, the overpotential gradually 
increases to 39 mV, 31 mV and 15 mV, respectively. However, the 
control cell shows a sharp increase in voltage after approximately 40 h, 
which is followed by oscillatory fluctuations in subsequent cycles, and 
ultimately leads to a short circuit. Based on those behaviors, it can be 
concluded that the symmetric Li||Li cells assembled with pyrazine ad­
ditives outperform the control group in terms of overpotential and cycle 
life, with the cell containing 2,5-DCP exhibiting the best battery capacity 
and cycling stability, followed by the cells with 2-CP and PZ additives. 
The presence of these additives results in lower overpotentials during 
the charge-discharge process. This suggests their contribution to 
improved kinetics of the electrode reactions and energy conversion ef­
ficiency. They facilitate the uniform deposition of lithium and regulate 
the interfacial chemical processes. 
Fig. S3 and Fig. 2d present the electrochemical impedance spec­
troscopy (EIS) curves of symmetric Li||Li cells before and after 50 cycles 
at an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 and a current density of 1 mA cm−2. 
Typically, the intersection of the curve in the high-frequency region with 
the real axis corresponds to the ohmic resistance (Rb), while the semi­
circle appearing in the high-to-medium-frequency region represents the 
interfacial contact resistance (RSEI) and charge transfer resistance (Rct). 
Additionally, the slope in the low-frequency region corresponds to Li+
diffusion resistance [39]. In the cycling process of control cell, the SEI 
layer on the lithium surface continuously collapses and regenerates, 
which results in the accumulation of dead lithium and an increase in the 
charge transfer resistance during the Li+ extraction process. Further­
more, the RSEI in the control cell increases after cycling, originating from 
side reactions occurring between lithium and LiPSs at the lithium sur­
face. However, the cells with pyrazine additives exhibit lower charge 
transfer resistance and interfacial resistance (Fig. 2e), indicating that the 
addition of pyrazine additives help to improve the interface properties of 
the battery, reduce charge transfer resistance and interfacial resistance, 
and promote faster kinetic behavior. Fig. 2h-k display SEM images of the 
lithium anode surface after 100 cycles at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 
in a Li||Li cell. Fig. 2f-g present schematic illustrations of the lithium 
anode from both a top view and side view. The lithium metal in the 
control cells displays a rough surface with some larger cracks in Fig. 2h. 
Additionally, the side view (Fig. 2i) reveals a loose and thick SEI layer, 
which is attributed to the instability of the SEI during lithium plati­
ng/stripping processes. The continuous breakdown and regeneration of 
the interface result in uncontrolled volume expansion and irregular 
dendritic growth on the lithium metal surface. In contrast, as shown in 
Fig. 2j, the lithium metal surface in the cells with 2,5-DCP additive ex­
hibits a flat morphology without significant dendrites and has a dense 
SEI layer. From Fig. 2k, it can be observed that the corrosion depth is 
reduced by approximately 69 μm when 2,5-DCP is added compared with 
the control cell. These results confirm the critical role of pyrazine-based 
additives in enhancing the performance of lithium metal anodes. 
To investigate the influence of additives on the structure of the 
lithium anode interface, the SEM images of the lithium anode in the LSB 
after 50 cycles at 0.2 C were compared. Without pyrazine additives, the 
lithium anode surface in Fig. 3a appears stacked and rough with moss- 
like lithium deposition. The instability of the SEI layer formed on the 
lithium anode surface leads to its collapse during the plating/stripping 
process and further uneven lithium deposition. In contrast, the surface 
with pyrazine additives exhibits a relatively uniform and dense 
morphology (Fig. 3b). The surfaces with 2-CP and 2,5-DCP additives 
(Fig. 3c‒d) display even smoother and more uniform surface 
morphology. This implies the positive effect of additives in forming a 
stable SEI on the lithium surface through mitigating the corrosion of the 
lithium anode. Besides, the surface element mapping results in Fig. 3e 
confirm the involvement of C, N, Cl, S, and F elements in forming the 
SEI. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed in Fig. 3f‒h 
to determine the composition of the SEI. In the Li 1 s XPS spectrum of PZ 
(Fig. 3f), the peaks at 54.8 eV, 55.5 eV, and 56.2 eV can be fitted to Li-S, 
Li-N, and Li-F peaks, respectively. This confirms the presence of Li-S, Li- 
N, and Li-F compounds [37,40,41]. Compared with PZ, the samples with 
2-CP and 2,5-DCP additives exhibit an additional LiCl shoulder peak at 
56.2 eV, which confirms the presence of LiCl [33,37]. This conclusion is 
further supported by the Cl 2p XPS spectrum in Fig. 3g, where the signal 
of chlorine atoms is significantly enhanced in the presence of 2,5-DCP. 
Additionally, it’s noteworthy that the Li-F peak shifts to lower binding 
energies, indicating a change in the chemical environment on the sam­
ple’s surface. This shift is attributed to the interaction between the 
chlorine atoms in Pyrazines and lithium and fluorine, which leads to a 
reduction in the binding energy of Li-F compounds. Similarly, the 
introduction of 2,5-DCP induces a rearrangement of the electron cloud 
around sulfur atoms. The presence of 2,5-DCP initiates a charge transfer 
effect and influences the electron distribution around sulfur atoms. This 
effect can induce alterations in the electron cloud of sulfur atoms, 
resulting in a shift in the binding energy of the S 2p peak and a change in 
its position. As illustrated in Fig. S4, the -C-N- and -C=N- peaks origi­
nated from the pyrazine ring indicate the presence of organic compo­
nents in the SEI. Meanwhile, the Li-N bond is formed between the N 
atom of pyrazine and the Li atom of Li2Sx. The formation of Li-N bonds 
suggests the involvement of pyrazine compounds in the formation of the 
interface layer, which allows for the adsorption of polysulfides, allevi­
ates the shuttle effect, and thus improves the utilization of active ma­
terials. In the S 2p XPS spectrum (Fig. 3h), the S-O bond (-NCF3SO2) 
arises from the decomposition of lithium salt, and the sulfite species are

2.3. Regulation of multisulfide molecular orbital levels 
Density functional theory (DFT) was employed to analyze the com­
plex structures of pyrazine compounds and LiPSs to understand their 
interactions. The ESP maps of PZ, 2-CP, 2,5-DCP and Li2Sx (x = 1,4,6,8) 
were mapped onto the isodensity surface (0.0004) in Fig. 4a. The ESP 
provides an intuitive depiction of the predicted positions for the for­
mation of new chemical bonds. The lowest ESP in the red regions in­
dicates the highest electronegativity and nucleophilic character. The 
highest ESP in blue regions represents the lowest electronegativity and 
electrophilic character. From the ESP map of LiPSs, completely blue of 
lithium atoms indicates their strong positive potential. In contrast, the 
minimum value of the ESP for PZ (−20.97 kcal mol−1) is located near the 
N atom, and the strongly negative potential corresponds to the red re­
gion (Fig. 1a). These findings suggest that the nitrogen atoms in the 
pyrazine compounds are electron-rich and engage in strong nucleophilic 
interactions with lithium in LiPSs, leading to the formation of stable Li-N 
bonds. Additionally, the red regions of 2-CP and 2,5-DCP exhibit greater 
depth in comparison to PZ, with an ESP minimum of −23.37 kcal mol−1 
for 2-CP and −26.78 kcal mol−1 for 2,5-DCP. This is because the intro­
duction of chlorine atoms alters the electron density around the nitrogen 
atoms in the pyrazine ring. The chlorine atoms attract electrons from the 
conjugated system, which reduces the electron density around the ni­
trogen atoms and makes them stronger nucleophilic centers. In partic­
ular, the 2,5-DCP molecule contains two chlorine atoms, and the 
electron density around the nitrogen atoms is further reduced due to the 
electron-withdrawing nature of chlorine. This is an enhancement effect 
resulting from an increased number of chlorine atoms. As revealed by 
DFT analysis, there are strong interactions between pyrazine compounds 
and LiPSs, and the interaction strength is effectively enhanced with an 
increasing number of chlorine atoms involved in the electron effect. 
These results are crucial for understanding the interaction mechanisms 
between pyrazine compounds and LiPSs. Fig. S8 (a–d) displays the most 
stable geometrical shapes formed by the interaction between Li2Sx (x =
1,4,6,8) and pyrazine-like substances. Their relative binding energies 
(△E) were calculated to measure the strength of the interaction be­
tween pyrazine-like substances and Li2Sx: △E = EAB –EA–EB+EBSSE. 
According to this definition, the larger the negative value, the more 
stable the structure. As shown, the isomers of Pyrazines-Li2Sx are formed 
by the interaction between the nitrogen atoms in the pyrazine ring and 
Li+ in Li2Sx. The calculations suggest that Li2Sx and Pyrazines can 
combine into stable configurations. By comparing the △E of various 
compounds (Pyrazines-Li2Sx), it was found that 2,5-DCP is more likely to 
form stable configurations with Li2Sx. By comparing the △E of PZ-Li2Sx, 
2-CP-Li2Sx, and 2,5-DCP-Li2Sx, the stability sequence is 2,5-DCP-Li2Sx >
2-CP-Li2Sx > PZ-Li2Sx. Furthermore, the relative binding energy strength 
is comparable to hydrogen bonds [47]. This suggests that the formation 
and dissociation of Li-N bonds between Pyrazines and Li2Sx are revers­
ible. Therefore, the interaction is not sufficient to hinder the

To unravel the origin of the faster redox kinetics of Pyrazines-SxLi 
compared with LiPSs, first-principles calculations were performed on 
several species including Li2Sx, PZ-SxLi, 2-CP-SxLi, and 2,5-DCP-SxLi (x 
= 1,4,6,8) to investigate their molecular orbital energy levels. Fig. 4b 
and Fig. S5‒S6 show the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) 
and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of these in­
termediates. It is evident from Fig. 4c that each Pyrazines-SxLi (x =
1,4,6,8) has a narrower bandgap compared with Li2Sx. The lower LUMO 
energy indicates higher electron affinity, while the higher HOMO energy 
reflects the ability to release electrons [48]. Therefore, this confirms that 
Pyrazine-SxLi (x = 1,4,6,8) intermediates exhibit higher conductivity 
and faster charge transfer compared with LiPSs. This leads to faster 
electron acceptance processes and faster kinetics, which can explain 
their superior electrochemical performance [49,50].

for the Li2S6 to Li2S4 transformation, i.e., the pyrazine additives catalyze 
the liquid-solid reduction step. From Fig. 5i, it is evident that the voltage 
difference between the oxidation and reduction peaks of the control cell 
is 197 mV, while the cells with pyrazine additives exhibit reduced 
voltage differences of 160, 151, and 150 mV, respectively. This reduc­
tion in voltage difference indicates that the cells with pyrazine additives 
experience lower electrochemical polarization. Overall, the addition of 
chloropyrazine additives exhibits better electrocatalytic effects on the 
redox reactions involving LiPSs, and promots the nucleation process of 
Li2S and facilitating faster electron acceptance.

3. Conclusion 
In summary, the exceptional electrochemical performance observed 
with pyrazine additives in lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) can be attrib­
uted to several key factors. Firstly, the formation of stable organic- 
inorganic hybrid solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) containing LiCl and 
organic lithium salts contributes to long-term cycling stability by pro­
tecting the lithium anode from continuous corrosion by dissolved LiPSs 
and regulating lithium deposition behavior to suppress dendrite growth. 
Secondly, the pyrazine additives exhibit superior electrocatalytic effects 
on the oxidation–reduction reactions involving LiPSs, which promote 
the nucleation process of Li2S, facilitate faster electron acceptance, and 
improve reaction kinetics. Among the four types of LSBs investigated, 
the order of their performance in terms of capacity, cycling stability, and 
kinetics is as follows: 2,5-DCP > 2-CP > PZ > without additive. The 
optimal additive 2,5-DCP in the LSB demonstrates an initial discharge 
capacity of 898 mAh g−1 at 1C current density and maintains a capacity 
of 734 mAh g−1 after 800 cycles. Even under the challenging conditions 
of high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte, the LSB with pyrazine ad­
ditives exhibits high discharge capacity and excellent cycling stability, 
with a capacity retention of 66.6 % after 500 cycles. This work un­
derscores the significance of pyrazine additives in enhancing the per­
formance and stability of LSB and provides guidance and insights for the 
development of efficient LSBs and other energy storage systems.
