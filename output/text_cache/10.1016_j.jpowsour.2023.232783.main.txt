A B S T R A C T  
Lithium–sulfur batteries have sparked a lot of interests because of their high specific capacity and low cost. 
However, due to the ‘shuttle effect’, unstable Li metal and side reactions, the performance of LSBs is less than 
expected. Herein, we design a localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE), using carbonate as the base 
solvent, hexafluoroisopropyl methyl ether (HFME) as the diluent, and vinylene carbonate (VC) as the filmogen to 
realize a stable solid-solid cyclic process in LSBs. For the cathode side, carbonate solvent molecules with Li+- 
coordination can reduce the nucleophilic side reactivity with lithium sulfide. The initial surface reaction between 
VC and lithium sulfide forms an effective cathode electrolyte interface (CEI), which can continuously protect the 
cathode active substances and realize a stable solid-solid conversion process. For the anode side of lithium metal, 
in combination with Li+-coordinated TFSI−in LHCE, a stable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) can be formed by 
VC on Li anode which improves the stability of Li anode. As a result, the cyclic performance of LSBs is signifi­
cantly improved, demonstrating a high specific capacity and stability. Furthermore, the self-discharge is nearly 
stopped. This work provides a universal strategy to design new electrolytes to improve Li–S battery performance.

1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) with a high specific capacity (1672 
mAh g−1) and specific energy (2600 Wh g−1) are considered to have the 
potentiΩal to meet the urgent market demand [1]. In a typical Li–S 
electrochemical process, solid phase sulfur first transforms into liquid 
phase lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) intermediatΩe which dissolves into 
the commonly used ether electrolytes [2]. Then it’s finally converted 
into solid phase Li2S. However, the LiPSs are subsequently dissolved in 
this ’solid-liquid-solid’ electrochemical process, resulting in the 
well-known shuttle effect [3,4]. It continuously consumes active mate­
rial and leads to irreversible capacity loss, low coulombic efficiency, and 
self-discharge [4,5]. Many attempts have been made in recent years to 
reduce the ’shuttle effect,’ including using conductive matrix [6–8], 
polarity host [9–11], 3D fast mass transfer structure [12], catalytic 
agents [13–16] and covalently immobilized sulfur [17]. These strategies 
mainly focus on anchoring the LiPSs, accelerating the kinetics of LiPSs 
conversion to suppress the ‘shuttle effect’ which effectively improves 
LSBs performance [18]. Suppressing the diffusion of LiPS by modifying 
the electrolyte is also an effective strategy to solve the ‘shuttle effect’, 
including electrolyte solvation structure design and using additives to 
tune the solubility of LiPSs [19,20]. Furthermore, changing the sulfur 
redox mechanism is another way to avoid the shuttle effect. According to 
theoretical calculations [21], solvated LiPSs are thermodynamically 
stable. In a soluble environment, sulfur will first change into LiPSs, then 
into Li2S. Unsolved LiPSs, on the other hand, are not thermodynamically 
stable, causing sulfur to convert directly to Li2S. This indicates that if the 
sulfur cathode is in an insoluble solvent environment, LiPSs cannot be 
formed, implying that sulfur is converted directly to Li2S. Theoretically, 
sulfur can realize a solid-phase conversion mechanism by simply using 
the common carbonate electrolytes because of its insolubility of LiPSs 
[22]. However, the carbonate solvents will undergo severe nucleophilic

attack reaction [23] with the discharge product Li2S which consumes 
significant amounts of active substances and results in huge capacity 
loss. To avoid this side reaction, increasing the concentration of Li+ in 
carbonate electrolyte is an effective strategy [24]. With the increasing 
concentration of Li+, the average coordination number (CN) of car­
bonate solvent molecules also rises which turns most of the carbonate 
molecules from ‘free’ molecules to coordinated ones. The Li+-coordi­
nated carbonate molecules’ side-reactivity is greatly reduced, which 
improves capacity and cyclic stability. However, such a high concen­
tration will inevitably increase the viscosity of the electrolytes which 
hinders Li+ transportation [25]. Furthermore, the side reaction has just 
been suppressed but not fundamentally eliminated. 
Herein, we design a localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) 
to synthesize cathode electrolyte interface (CEI) in situ on the cathode 
surface which protects the cathode from contacting the carbonate sol­
vent and allows Li+ transport. The active materials are lithiated/deli­
thiated in the CEI layer to achieve a stable solid-solid conversion 
process. The CEI film is in situ synthesized by the prepared LHCE which 
is composed of carbonate solvent, diluent Hexafluoroisopropyl methyl 
ether (HFME) and co-solvent vinylene carbonate (VC). LHCE has a 
unique solvation structure: the localized high concentration of ion- 
solvent clusters are dispersed in the diluent, and most of the solvent 
molecules are coordinated with Li+. By raising the coordination of Li+- 
carbonate solvent, at the end of the first discharge, the side reaction 
between Li2S and carbonate solvent is initially inhibited. At the same 
time, the co-solvent VC and a small amount of surface Li2S dominate the 
reaction on the cathode surface [26], forming an in situ CEI film rapidly 
[27]. In the subsequent cycles, the CEI film continues to protect the 
active material inside, improving the capacity and cycle stability. The 
schematic illustration of in situ CEI synthesis by LHCE is shown in Fig. 1.

[28], including the dendritic lithium growth and the unstable solid

MD simulation, and DFT calculations are conducted to analyze the 
localized high-concentration solvent structure and the CEI formation 
mechanism. Combined with the electrochemical performance test, we

realized inside the in situ formed CEI film by simply using LHCE. As a 
result, the KB/S cathode demonstrates a much higher reversible capacity 
of 1102.7 mAh g−1 at 0.1C, better cyclic stability of an average 0.12% 
capacity decay at 0.5C, nearly 100% Coulombic efficiency and self- 
discharge is also suppressed. Moreover, characterized by High-

resulting in 600 h stable cyclic performance of the Li–Li symmetrical

2. Experimental 
2.1. Material preparation 
The S/C composite was prepared by the melting method. Firstly, 
commercially available Ketjenblack (EC600, Cabot Corporation) and 
sublimed sulfur in a weight ratio of 1:2 was ball-milled in a high-energy 
planetary, ball miller at a rotation speed of 600 rpm for 8 h. Then the 
mixture was sealed in a vacuum glass tube and heated at 155 ◦C for 12 h 
in a muffle furnace. Finally, extra S remaining outside the carbon pores 
was re-infiltrated or removed via heat treatment at 250 ◦C for 2 h. 
The sulfur cathode was fabricated by mixing the KB/S composite, 
conductive carbon (Super P), and Sodium Alginate binder in a weight 
ratio of 8:1:1. Firstly, a uniform slurry was prepared by stirring three 
materials in deionized water. Then, the slurry was coated onto an 
attended aluminum foil and dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h. The cathode elec­
trode was cut into discs (φ = 8 mm). The loading of sulfur is around 1.0 
mg cm−2. 
2.2. Preparation of electrolytes 
All electrolytes were prepared and used in a glove box with argon 
(Ar) (O2/H2O < 1 ppm). The LHCE were consisted of lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), solvent (ethylene carbonate 
(EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC)), diluent (hexafluoroisopropyl methyl 
ether (HFME)) and additive (vinylene carbonate (VC)). The solution was 
first mixed with EC, DMC, HFME and VC at the corresponding volume 
ratio. Then mix with LiTFSI and make up to 1 mol/L electrolyte. 
(denoted as EDHV-xyz, where E stands for EC, D stands for DMC, H 
stands for HFME, V stands for VC, xyz stands for the volume ratio of EC/ 
DMC/HFME = x:y:z. VC is added at a fixed volume ratio of 10% in the 
EC/DMC/HFME solution according to previous research [27]. The 
commercial carbonate (ED) electrolyte is consists of 1 mol/L LiTFSI in 
EC:DMC = 1:1. The commercial ether (CE) electrolyte is consists of 1 
mol/L LiTFSI in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL):1,2-dimethoxy ethane (DME) =
1:1. 
2.3. Electrochemical measurements 
2016 coin-type cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box with 
lithium foil as the counter electrode and celgard 2500 (Celgard, USA) as 
the separator. 
Galvanostatic discharge–charge tests were performed in a voltage 
interval of 3.0–1.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) using a Land Battery Measurement 
System (Land, China) in an open environment. Cyclic voltammetry was 
carried out with Autolab Electrochemical Instrumentation (Metrohm) 
workstation in the potential range of 3.0–1.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a scan rate 
of 0.1 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectra were obtained from 
an Autolab Electrochemical Instrumentation with a frequency range 
from 100 KHz to 1 Hz. 
2.4. Characterization 
Raman spectra were collected by a Horiba Evolution Raman spec­
trometer operated with an incident laser beam at 532 nm. The mor­
phologies and structural characteristics of the test electrodes were 
observed by using a field-emission scanning electron microscope 
(Merlin) and transmission electron microscopy (Talos F200X). X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on the 
Thermo Scientific K-Alpha with an Al Ka X-ray source. Fourier- 
Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy of samples was collected on 
the Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 instrument. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization and mechanism of LHCE 
Fig. 3a and b shows the Raman Spectra of different LHCE with 
different ratios of diluent. ‘Free’ EC and DMC molecules exhibit 
stretching vibration bands at 717.03 and 893.71 cm−1, respectively. 
These bands systematically shift up to 730.06 and 904.96 cm−1, 
respectively, with the introduction of diluent HFME. The solvent 
molecule coordinates with the Li+ to form a ‘solvation sheath’ thereby 
exhibiting a higher wave-number [30,31]. The peak strength of ‘free’ EC 
and DMC weakens as the proportion of diluent ratio increases, showing 
that the carbonate molecules’ coordination is strengthened. As shown in 
Fig. 3d–f, simulated by Molecular Dynamics, radial distribution function 
(RDF) of Li+ with three kinds of particles in LHCE with different pro­
portions of diluent are calculated. The RDFs of Li+-EC and Li+-DMC are 
increased with the proportion of diluent increases, indicating that co­
ordination of Li+-EC and Li+-DMC are strengthened. Besides, the RDF of 
Li+ and HFME is much smaller than the carbonate solvent, showing that 
diluent HFME is not involved in the solvated structure. From the Raman 
Spectra and MD simulation we can infer that the solvent structure of 
LHCE: ion-solvent clusters are dispersed in the diluent, turning the 
components of the cluster in a high local concentration. 
Fig. 3g shows the 0.1C cyclic performance of KB/S cathode in 
different LHCEs. As the ratio of diluent/solvent increased, the specific 
capacity of KB/S cathode also increased. However, when the diluent/ 
solvent ratio was over 2:1 (EDHV-114), the specific capacity of KB/S 
cathode didn’t increase but remained at the level of around 1100 mAh 
g−1. To further confirm the role of the co-solvent VC, cyclic performance 
of KB/S cathode in different LHCEs without were also conducted. As 
shown in Fig. 3f, with the ratio of diluent/solvent increased, the initial 
specific capacity of KB/S cathode also increased. However, in following 
cycles, all of them experienced severe capacity loss, showing that 
although increasing the coordination of Li+ and carbonate solvents can 
decrease the side-reactivity at the beginning. The side effect still occurs 
in subsequent cycles, which reduces the cathode capacity. These test 
above shows that co-solvent VC is significant for stable cycling due to the 
CEI formation property. However, there is a competitive reaction be­
tween EC and VC, which prevents the formation of CEI to a certain 
extent. So it is necessary to introduce a certain proportion of diluent to 
raise the localized concentration of carbonate molecules to hinder the 
carbonate solvent side reactions in the initial stage. 
To investigate the side reaction inhibition and CEI formation 
mechanisms of LHCE, we calculated the Localized Orbital Locator (LOL) 
[32] of the ‘free’ and coordinated solvent molecules which can well 
describe the properties of chemical bonds. Figs. S1a–f display the 
two-dimensional LOL profile of the corresponding ‘free’ and coordinated 
solvent molecules. Figs. S1g–j show the one-dimensional LOL profile 
between two atoms at the reaction site. According to the nucleophilic 
attack reaction mechanism between Li2S and carbonate solvent moluc­
ular, the C–O bond in the carbonate group will be broken under the 
nucleophilic attack of Li2S. As shown in Figs. S1g–j, for coordinated EC, 
DMC and VC molecules, the energies of their C–O bonds are strength­
ened, which means Li + coordination can hinder the nucleophilic attack 
by Li2S. But the energy of the VC’s C––C bond is almost unchanged 
before and after Li+ coordination, which means the polymerization re­
action of VC is not affected. Therefore, in the LHCE system, VC can 
dominant the cathode surface reaction to successfully generate a pro­
tective CEI film. The diagram of VC polymerization by Li2S is shown in 
Fig. S1k. 
From the experiments and simulations above, the mechanism of 
LHCE can be concluded as follow: by adding diluent, the solvent struc­
tures of carbonate molecules will turn into the localized high concen­
tration structures, which raises the Li + coordination of the carbonate 
molecules. As a result, the coordination structure hinders the nucleo­
philic attack side reaction on the carbonate group’s C–O bond, and the

3.3. SEI formation by LHCE 
The LHCE is not only beneficial to the cathode side, but also the 
stable cycling of the Li anode. Fig. 6a and Fig. S7 shows the of cycling 
performance of Li–Li symmetrical battery and coulombic efficiency of 
Li–Cu asymmetrical. This result shows that LHCE can effectively stabi­
lize Li metal anode. To investigate the morphology of Li metal after 
cycling in different electrolytes, SEM morphology characterization was 
conducted, as shown in Fig. 6b and c. The Li foil was taken out after 24 h 
cycling to take the SEM images. The Li foil cycling in ED-11 exhibit 
rough morphologies and some cracks, which is hard to stabilize a SEI 
film and increases the risk of short circuit. In LHCE, large nodule-like Li 
particles are observed, which reduced surface area and decreases side 
reactions between the electrolyte and the Li metal. Therefore, the cell 
using LHCE can cycle steadily for a long time. 
To investigate the SEI film component of the Li anode cycled in 
EDHV-114, EDS mapping and XPS were conducted. As shown in Fig. S8, 
the result of EDS mapping shows that there are mainly C and F elements 
distributed on the lithium surface. C 1s XPS profile shows the compo­
nents of polycarbonate, Li2CO3 and R–Li (Fig. 6d). And F 1s XPS profile 
shows the existence of LiF (Fig. 6e). So it can be inferred that the SEI film 
formatted in EDHV-114 is mainly at attribute to the decomposition of VC 
and LiTFSI on the Li anode in situ. In addition to forming CEI which is 
proved above, VC is also a widely used SEI additive [34]. The decom­
position of LiTFSI is mainly related to the LHCE. As shown in Fig. 3b, in 
the LHCE, the peak of LiTFSI is shifted to the peak of the coordinated 
state, which is also confirmed by MD simulation in Fig. S9. According to 
the DFT calculation in Fig. S10, the Lowest Unoccupied Molecular 
Orbital (LUMO) of coordinated LiTFSI is lower than the ‘free’ TFSI-, 
which makes LiTFSI predominantly reduced on the Li anode surface and 
decomposed 
of 
inorganic 
components 
LiF. 
This 
kind 
of 
polymer-inorganic composite SEI layer provides good mechanical 
strength with fast ion transport [35,36], resulting in stable Li 
plating/stripping property. 
3.4. Electrochemical performance 
With the protection of the in situ CEI film, the side reactions caused 
by carbonate solvents can be blocked efficiently. So a stable solid-solid 
conversion of sulfur can be realized in LHCE. Therefore, the cyclic sta­
bility and capacity are greatly improved. Because the first discharge 
included the CEI formation process, we choose the stable second cycle to 
start the cyclic performance comparison. Fig. 7a shows the KB/S 0.5C 
cyclic performance in LHCE and carbonate electrolyte. The cell using 
LHCE electrolyte exhibits high capacity (of 786.4 mAh g−1 at second 
cycle), and nearly 100% 
Coulombic Efficiency due to the in situ CEI protection. After 100 
cycles, the cell still maintains a specific capacity of 691.4 mAh g−1. 
Oppositely, KB/S cycling in ED-11 displays an extremely low specific 
capacity of 79.2 mAh g−1 at the second cycle due to the severe side 
reactions between carbonate solvents and Li2S. The Coulombic Effi­
ciency, which is significantly higher than 100%, also validates the severe 
side reaction. The rate performance of KB/S using LHCE is evaluated at 
various current densities from 0.1 to 1C, and then recovered back to 
0.1C. As is shown in Fig. 7b, the cell demonstrates good capacities of 
1015, 936.4, 779.5, 700.1, 689.2 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.75 and 1C, 
respectively. After switching the current density back to 0.1C, the cell 
recovers a stable capacity of 1005.3 mAh g−1. The long term cyclic 
performance of KB/S in LHCE and ED-11 electrolyte at 0.1C is shown in 
Fig. 7c. Usually, the capacity loss caused by side reaction and ‘shuttle 
effect’ will be more severe at a low current density [31]. The cell using 
LHCE electrolyte exhibits high capacity (of 1102.7 mAh g−1 at second 
cycle), and nearly 100% Coulombic Efficiency. After 200 cycles, the cell 
still maintains a specific capacity of 939.2 mAh g−1. This is due to the in 
situ formatted CEI film having long shielded the KB/S cathode. Simi­
larly, the cell using ED-11 also displays an extremely low specific ca­
pacity of 79.2 mAh g−1 due to the severe side reactions. And its 
Coulombic Efficiency is significantly higher than 100%. For the cell 
using CE-11 electrolyte, although no side reaction happened between 
solvent and active materials, the cell still suffers from rapid capacity loss

4. Conclusions 
In summary, by using a carbonate based localized high- 
concentration electrolyte, the VC dominates the initial reaction to 
form a dense CEI film on the KB/S cathode and realize a stable ‘solid- 
solid’ conversion mechanism, which continuously protects the active 
substance inside from the side effect caused by carbonate solvents. 
Moreover, the localized high-concentration electrolyte is also proved to 
stabilize the Li anode by its polymer-inorganic composite SEI formation. 
Their mechanism is confirmed by various spectroscopic studies and 
theoretical simulations. Benefiting from these advantages, the capacity 
and stability of the LSBs are greatly improved compared with com­
mercial carbonate and ether electrolytes. We believe our work can offer 
valuable guidance for the electrolyte design to develop a stable LSB for 
both cathode and anode sides.
