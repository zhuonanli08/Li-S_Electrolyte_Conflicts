a b s t r a c t 
Metal-organic small molecules have recently emerged as homogeneous catalysts, presenting a new para-
digm for catalysis in lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries. Precise tailoring of molecular conﬁgurations is crucial 
for elevating their catalytic activity in facilitating the redox reactions in Li–S chemistry. In this study, 
paired oxygen-bridge and chlorine-bridge bonds are precisely incorporated into a binuclear copper com-
plex (Cu-2-B) to generate a homogeneous catalyst in situ in the electrolyte. This versatile molecular engi-
neering modulation effectively reduces the proximal distance between adjacent copper atoms in the 
complex molecule from 4.7 to 3.2 Å, leading to an optimized catalyst conﬁguration. Using synchrotron 
radiation X-ray three-dimensional nano-computed tomography, the Li2S nucleation and growth at a 
high-resolution and spatial-distribution level were visualized. It is found that the introduction of an oxy-
gen bridge into Cu-2-B enables the Li2S deposition that is high in mass, small in size, and uniform in dis-
tribution. By combining neutron imaging and small-angle neutron scattering techniques, surface and
internal deposited lithium are qualitatively and quantitatively deciphered. It is conﬁrmed that Cu-2-B
can guide the lithium stripping/plating behaviors, resulting in a robust lithium working surface and inner
architecture. Beneﬁting from the enhanced homogeneous catalytic effect of the saturated Cu-2-B, the Li–S
cell achieves stable cycling over a wide temperature range of 273–328 K. Impressively, the Cu-2-B
endows a 1.1-g-sulfur pouch cell with a gravimetric energy density of 345.3 Wh kg−1, indicating its great
potential for future power supply implementations.

1. Introductio n
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are regarded as one of the most 
promising next-generation secondary battery systems due to their 
high theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 1672 mA h g−1 and energy 
density of 2600 Wh kg−1. Moreover, sulfur, as the active material
for Li–S batteries, offers advantages in terms of its abundance,
low cost, and environmental friendliness [1–3]. However, their

In recent years, electrocatalytic strategies targeting the cath-
odes of Li–S batteries have emerged as a central research focus in
the ﬁeld of high-energy batteries, providing a crucial pathway
toward their commercial viability [13,14]. Catalysts, such as transi-
tion metal oxides [15,16], sulﬁdes [17–19], carbides [20,21], and 
nitrides [22,23] have been extensively utilized to generate active 
sites for modulating PS evolutions. Nevertheless, these catalysts 
are primarily heterogeneous in nature, implying their catalytic efﬁ-
ciency largely depends on the accessibility of active sites for 
adsorption, diffusion, and subsequent conversion processes. Dur-
ing the catalytic cycle, they often face difﬁculties in achieving suf-
ﬁcient interaction with solid-phase reactants, exhibit limited
structural variability, demonstrate inferior selectivity, and possess
poorly deﬁned catalytic mechanisms [24,25]. To address these lim-
itations, soluble metal-based organic small molecules have been 
recently introduced into the electrolyte as redox mediators to form
homogeneous catalysts [26,27]. These catalysts offer uniformly 
distributed and mobile active sites within the electrolyte system, 
more effectively accelerating sulfur redox kinetics, compared with
heterogeneous catalysts [28–30]. For instance, Lu and co-workers
[31] successfully synthesized a macrocyclic copper complex 
through self-assembly, which effectively regulates sulfur conver-
sion and lithium deposition behaviors. This process reduces the 
energy barrier for the liquid-to-solid transformation, ultimately 
improving sulfur utilization under conditions of high sulfur load-
ings and a low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio. In our previous explo-
ration of copper-based phenanthroline complexes, we found that
the proximity of adjacent copper atoms in a chlorine bridge-
enabled binuclear copper complex signiﬁcantly inﬂuences catalytic
efﬁciency [27,32,33]. Building on these insights, it is highly desir-
able to propose a strategy for developing more efﬁcient homoge-
neous catalysts by further modulating the proximal distance of 
adjacent copper atoms. Of speciﬁc note, modulating the coordina-
tion of metal centers, elucidating the working mechanism, and
optimizing design principles are of utmost importance to ulti-
mately enabling stable, long-cycle Li–S batteries capable of effec-
tively
performing
under
diverse
conditions,
including
wide
temperature ranges, high current rates, and elevated sulfur
loadings.
In this work, we ingeniously introduced paired oxygen and 
chlorine-bridge bonds into a binuclear copper-based complex (de-
noted as Cu-2-B) to precisely regulate the spatial proximity of adja-
cent copper atoms within the catalyst molecule. The oxygen-
bridging bond can shorten the proximal distance between adjacent 
copper atoms from 4.7 to 3.2 Å, constructing a homogeneous cata-
lyst with signiﬁcantly enhanced activity for Li–S reactions. Notably, 
the catalytic capability of the Cu-2-B can be guaranteed by opti-
mizing its concentration in the electrolyte with a 0.3 wt% satura-
tion level. Electrochemical kinetic analyses combined with 
synchrotron radiation X-ray three-dimensional nano-computed 
tomography (SR X-ray 3D nano-CT) demonstrate that Cu-2-B 
enhances the Li2S nucleation and growth kinetics, propelling the 
large-mass and uniform spatial deposition of Li2S. Moreover, neu-
tron imaging and small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) results
reveal the effectively modulated lithium stripping/plating behavior
by the Cu-2-B, stabilizing both the surface interface and internal
structure of the lithium metal anode. The strategic incorporation
of bridge bonds into the catalyst molecular framework, with con-
centration optimization, warrants efﬁcient and homogeneous cat-
alytic
function
of
Cu-2-B.

such as high sulfur loadings, lean electrolyte dosage, and wide tem-
perature range. Particularly, a 1.26 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell is fab-
ricated, delivering an actual energy density of 345.3 Wh kg−1.

2. Experim ental
2.1. Synthesis of Cu-2-B
The binuclear copper-based precursor (Cu-2) was synthesized 
via heating in an oil bath. This copper complex was prepared using 
1,10-phenanthroline, copper (II) chloride dihydrate, and sodium 
phenolate as reactants. Deionized water and ethanol with a volu-
metric ratio of 1:2 were used as the solvent system, with a trace 
amount of formic acid added to the system. The mixture was 
sealed in a ﬂask and maintained at 50 ℃for 2 h. Upon completion,
the mixture was allowed to cool at room temperature. A blue pre-
cipitate, identiﬁed as Cu-2 powder, was collected through ﬁltration
and subsequently washed. Cu-2 powder was dissolved in 1 mL of
an electrolyte composed of DME/DOL solvent, 1.0 mol L–1 LiTFSI,
and 2.0 wt% LiNO3, forming the ﬁnal product of Cu-2-B.
2.2. Material characterizations
The blue single crystal of Cu-2-B was obtained by electrolyte 
evaporation. The structure was analyzed using an X-ray single 
crystal diffractometer with a Bruker SMART APEX-II CCD detector 
at 150 K with Mo Ka radiation (k = 0.71073 Å). The structure 
was veriﬁed by SHELXT and reﬁned by full-matrix least-squares 
on F2 using SHELXL. The chemical compositions of the Cu-2-B were 
analyzed via XPS on a Thermo Scientiﬁc K-Alpha system. The FTIR
spectra of samples were collected on a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrom-
eter. The UV-vis spectra of the samples were recorded on a Shi-
madzu UV-vis spectrometer. The morphologies, and elemental
distributions of samples were determined with the ZEISS Sigma
360 instrument. The surface roughness of lithium electrodes after
cycling was examined using a Bruker Dimension Icon AFM.
2.3. Li2S nucleation and decomposition tests
A 0.2 mol L–1 Li2S8 solution was prepared by mixing Li2S and 
sulfur at a mole ratio of 1:7 using DME as the solvent. A commer-
cial carbon paper was cut into small disks with a diameter of 
13 mm and then loaded with 0.2 mg of Super P (carbon), leading 
to the cathode. Lithium metal was employed as the anode. To 
assemble the cells, 20 lL of the Li2S8 solution containing Cu-2-B 
was added to the cathode side, and 20 lL of the blank electrolyte
was added to the anode side. The batteries underwent a galvanos-
tatic discharge process at 0.112 mA until the voltage dropped to
2.06 V. Following this, a potentiostatic discharge was carried out
at 2.05 V until the current was below 0.01 mA. For the Li2S decom-
position tests, the battery was ﬁrst discharged at 0.112 mA until
the voltage decreased to 1.7 V, followed by a charge at 2.35 V till
the current reached 0.01 mA.
2.4. Li stripping/ plating tests
Cu||Li cells were fabricated using copper foil as the cathode and 
lithium metal as the anode. The electrolyte consisted of DME/DOL 
solvent with 1.0 mol L–1 LiTFSI, 2.0 wt% LiNO3, and 0.3 wt% Cu-2-B. 
Lithium stripping/plating tests were conducted at a current density 
of 0.5 mA cm–2, with a stripping/plating capacity of 0.5 mA h cm–2.
The Li||Li cells were constructed using two pieces of lithium metal
as the working electrodes, with a control group consisting of a
blank electrolyte.

3. Results and discussion
We reported the synthesis of a binuclear copper complex (de-
noted as Cu-2-T) in previous investigation [27]. In this study, trace 
formic acid is introduced into the afore-mentioned preparation 
procedure. This forms a new oxygen-bridge bond and an additional
chlorine-bridge bond between adjacent copper atoms within the
catalyst molecule [34,35]. The resulting complex dissolves in a 
commercial electrolyte consisting of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), 
1,3-dioxolane (DOL), 1 mol L–1 LiTFSI, and 2.0 wt% lithium nitrate 
(LiNO3), to yield the ﬁnal homogeneous catalyst of Cu-2-B. Note 
that the dissolved content of the molecular complex in the elec-
trolyte is critical for the Cu-2-B catalytic function. Accordingly,
the saturation concentration of Cu-2-B was determined to be in
the range of 0.1 wt% to 0.5 wt%. The overall process, based on a sys-
tematic research methodology, is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The proxim-
ity of adjacent copper atoms within the catalyst molecule is tuned 
by manipulating their bonding interactions. The newly formed 
oxygen-bridge and chlorine-bridge bonds effectively decrease the 
proximal distance from 4.7 to 3.2 Å, leading to a highly active 
molecular catalyst. Consequently, the Cu-2-B molecular catalyst 
provides active, abundant homogeneous, and mobile active sites
within the dynamic electrolyte. This optimized catalyst-electrode
interface helps to favorably guide the sulfur and lithium species
reactions. Li2S nucleation and lithium deposition behaviors criti-
cally inﬂuence the discharge capacity, lifespan, and safety of the
Li–S batteries.
A suite of electrochemical measurements on both cathode and 
anode is conducted to elucidate these reaction behaviors. Further-
more, a Li2S nucleation model based on the Cu-2-B catalyst is iden-
tiﬁed to disclose the underlying kinetics. Due to the metastable 
nature of electrochemical intermediates and the difﬁculty in 
resolving their spatial distribution, high-ﬂux X-ray and neutron 
detection techniques are utilized to further decipher the working 
mechanism of Cu-2-B. SR X-ray 3D nano-CT exhibits the merits 
of high-resolution and in-depth detection, and can be used to visu-
alize spatially distributed Li2S in the substrate. Neutron imaging
and SANS, as non-destructive and highly sensitive techniques, col-
lectively offer qualitative and quantitative information on lithium
deposit morphology and distribution within long-term accumu-
lated
layers.
The
combination
of
advanced
characterization
approaches precisely identiﬁes the states and evolution behaviors
of sulfur and lithium species facilitated by Cu-2-B. Fig. 1b and 
Fig. S1 (online) illustrate the underlying working mechanism of 
the Cu-2-B molecular catalyst for both the sulfur cathode and 
lithium anode. A carbon layer is used as an efﬁcient barrier that
prevents the migration of Cu-2-B molecules and PSs to the anode
side. The SEM images of the carbon-layer with a mass loading of 
0.42 mg cm–2 reveal a homogeneous surface morphology and a
coating thickness of around 10.9 lm (Fig. S2 online). The functions 
of Cu-2-B can also be concluded as follows: First, the dual copper 
centers with optimized proximity exhibit intrinsically improved 
catalytic activity. Second, under a saturated concentration condi-
tion, the Cu-2-B molecular catalyst exposes completely accessible 
active sites, enabling homogeneous management of sulfur and 
lithium species evolutions. Third, the Cu-2-B molecular catalyst 
in the electrolyte possesses favorable mobility, which further 
enhances the efﬁciency of the optimization process. Therefore,
Li2S can nucleate and grow efﬁciently, while lithium-ions can dif-
fuse in a high-ﬂux as well as uniform manner and thus form stable
lithium deposition layers. The molecular design engineering strat-
egy provides a remarkably active homogeneous promoter that
enables synchronized regulation of the kinetic evolution of sulfur
and lithium species, thus facilitating the development of state-of-
the-art Li–S batteries.

The Cu-2-B was precipitated as crystals from the electrolyte 
through solvent evaporation, and its structure was characterized
by single-crystal X-ray diffraction, as shown in Fig. 2a and Fig. S3 
(online). More structural details of Cu-2-B can be obtained by 
referring to the crystal structure data in the Cambridge Crystallo-
graphic Data Centre (CCDC number: 2471530). The Cu-2-T is 
bridged by a single chlorine atom (CCDC number: 2243502), 
whereas Cu-2-B features both double chlorine bridges and a car-
boxyl oxygen bridge. These bridging bonds reduce the distance
between adjacent copper atoms from 4.7 to 3.2 Å, thereby inﬂuenc-
ing their catalytic activity. The key information regarding the bond
lengths and angles of Cu-2-B is provided in Tables S1, S2 (online). 
The elemental compositions of the binuclear copper-based precur-
sor (denoted as Cu-2) were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). The full XPS spectrum, shown in Fig. S4 (on-
line), reveals a characteristic O 1s peak at 530.7 eV, which conﬁrms 
the successful incorporation of oxygen-b ridge bonds in Cu-2. The
high-resolution O 1s spectrum for Cu-2 is presented in Fig. 2b, 
where the peaks at 532.3 and 530.7 eV correspond to C–O and
Cu–O bonds, respectively [36,37]. Scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) was utilized for the semi-quantitative elemental analysis of
Cu-2, as shown in Fig. S5 (online). The results indicate that oxygen 
is uniformly distributed throughout the material. All detected ele-
ments in the EDS spectrum align with the obtained results from
the full XPS spectrum. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spec-
tra of Cu-2 and Cu-2-B are presented in Fig. 2c. In the Cu-2 struc-
ture, the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration peaks of 
the O–C–O bond are identiﬁed at around 1521 and 1435 cm–1 , 
respectively, while the stretching vibration peak of the Cu–O bond
is observed at 480 cm–1. In the FTIR spectrum of Cu-2-B, the char-
acteristic peaks of the O–C–O and Cu–O bonds displayed slight red-
shifts [38,39]. Additionally, an asymmetric stretching vibration 
peak of the S–N bond appeared at around 1044 cm–1 , with the cor-
responding symmetric stretching vibration peak located at 
735 cm–1, which can be attributed to the TFSI⁻ group. This observa-
tion conﬁrms the coordination of LiTFSI within the Cu-2-B
structure.

4. Conclusi on
In summary, bridge bonding engineering is exerted to precisely 
tailor the proximity of adjacent copper atoms within the complex 
molecule by synchronously introducing oxygen and chlorine bridg-
ing bonds. This strategy enables the rational design of binuclear-
copper complex molecular catalysts at the molecular and atomic 
levels. Notably, the proximal distance between neighboring copper 
atoms is effectively reduced from 4.7 to 3.2 Å, thereby generating 
an optimized molecular catalyst conﬁguration. This molecular 
complex, when incorporated into the electrolyte at a saturated 
concentration (0.3 wt%), provides abundant and highly mobile 
active sites, facilitating the formation of an efﬁcient triple-phase 
boundary involving the electrolyte, sulfur, and substrate. Thanks 
to the effective homogeneous optimization function of Cu-2-B,
the sulfur cathode achieves in-depth discharge even under a high
mass loading of 7.2 mg cm–2, while the lithium anode maintains
a stable layered structure and robust working surface throughout
prolonged cycling operation. Moreover, the introduced bridging
bonds enhance the batteries’ cycling capability under harsh tem-
perature working conditions (273 to 328 K). Of speciﬁc note, under
the homogeneous catalytic effect of Cu-2-B, an Ah-level Li–S pouch
cell holds a practical speciﬁc energy density exceeding 345 Wh kg–
1 along with favorable cycling stability.
