On the Factors Affecting Aging and Self-Discharge of
Lithium–Sulfur Cells. Effect of Positive Electrode Composition
Elena Kuzmina,* Elena Karaseva, Alexey Ivanov, and Vladimir Kolosnitsyn
Fast self-discharge (reversible capacity loss during storage) and aging (irreversible
capacity loss during cycling and storage) are important and difﬁcult problems in
the development of lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB). Both problems are caused by
the solubility of the active materials of the positive electrode in the electrolyte.
Processes at the negative and positive electrodes result in self-discharge and
aging of LSB. Sulfur (S) and lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn), dissolved in the elec-
trolyte, interact with the lithium metal electrode, causing the formation of
insoluble lithium sulﬁde (Li2S). The main reasons for irreversible capacity loss are
the accumulation of Li2S on the negative electrode and the binding of S and Li2S
in the micropores of carbon materials, included in positive electrodes, during
prolonged cycling and storage of LSB. Li2S does not accumulate on the lithium
electrode during storage of LSB for 120 days. The equilibrium content of Li2S on
the lithium electrode is 0.06 mg cm2. The main reason for the irreversible
capacity loss is the binding of sulfur in the pore spaces of the carbon particles.
The interaction of sulfur and Li2Sn (n > 4) with metallic lithium, causing the
formation of soluble Li2Sn (n ≤4), is the reason for a reversible capacity loss.
1. Introduction
Electrochemical energy storage devices are widely used in vari-
ous areas of modern technology. Their production is steadily
being increased, and the requirements for their characteristics
are becoming more rigid.[1,2] The most important characteristics
of electrochemical batteries are speciﬁc energy, energy density,
rate capability, cycling stability, safety, and lifetime.[3]
Lithium-ion batteries have the best energy characteristics.
Their speciﬁc energy reaches 250–260 Wh kg1.[4] However,
market demands require the development of batteries with
greater speciﬁc energy.[5]
Thepracticalspeciﬁcenergyofbatteriesisdeterminedbythespe-
ciﬁc energy of the electrochemical system used and their design.
Lithium-ion batteries were released into the market in 1991 and,
over the years, have reached the limits of their development.[1,6–8]
To increase the speciﬁc energy of batteries, it is necessary to use
new electrochemical systems with greater
theoretical values for speciﬁc energy com-
pared with lithium-ion systems.
One of the most promising electrochem-
ical systems is lithium–sulfur because it has
a
high
theoretical
speciﬁc
energy
of
2600 Wh kg1, which is in three to ﬁve
times greater than the theoretical speciﬁc
energy of electrochemical systems used in
the current state-of-the-art lithium-ion bat-
teries.[2,9] In addition, sulfur is an abundant,
low cost, and environmentally sustainable
material.[3,10,11] Studies on the development
of lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been
intensively
conducted
over
the
last
decade.[2,9,12]
However, despite the signiﬁcant efforts of
researchers, LSBs that meet all market
requirementshavenotyetbeendeveloped.[13]
The main problems that prevent the
release of LSBs to the market are the
following: low compared with the expected
value of speciﬁc energy (the speciﬁc energy
of prototypes of LSBs is 300–400 Wh kg1 instead of the expected
500–600 Wh kg1),[2,9,14,15] fast capacity depletion during cycling
(usually, the average rate of capacity depletion is 0.05–0.1% per
cycle instead of the required value, which is lower than
0.01–0.04% per cycle),[1,2] high self-discharge rate (5–15% per
month instead of the desired 1–3% per month), and rapid aging
during cycling and storage. The aging rate (the rate of irreversible
capacity loss of LSBs during cycling and storage) can reach
several percentage values per month.
Problems in the development of commercially acceptable
LSBs are caused by the fact that LSBs are batteries with liquid
cathodes because sulfur and lithium polysulﬁdes, the active com-
ponents of the positive electrode, are soluble in the electrolyte.[9]
The low speciﬁc energy value of LSBs, compared with the
expected, is due to the necessity of loading a large amount of
electrolyte into the batteries to solvate all lithium polysulﬁdes
generated during discharge and charge.[16]
The direct chemical interaction of sulfur and lithium poly-
sulﬁdes, dissolved in the electrolyte system, with a metallic lith-
ium electrode, causes the self-discharge of LSBs (Equation (1)
and (2)).[17–19]

There are several ways to reduce the self-discharge of lithium–
sulfur cells as follows. First, coating the barrier layers on
the separator, preventing the diffusion of lithium polysulﬁdes
to the lithium electrode. The barrier layers can consist of
carbon
materials
with
different
types[22–26],
ion-selective
membranes,[27,28] and transition metal compounds.[29] Second,
the introduction of the additional membrane, preventing the
transfer of sulfur and lithium polysulﬁdes to the negative elec-
trode between the positive electrode and the separator.[30]
Third, the optimization of the composition of electrolyte systems
to reduce the mobility of sulfur and lithium polysulﬁdes[31–34]
and/or their solubility.[35,36] To reduce the solubility of lithium
polysulﬁdes, the composition of electrolyte solutions can be opti-
mized using different solvents[35] or increasing support salt con-
centrations.[37,38] Four, the introduction of additives into the
electrolyte systems can be performed. The additives form the pro-
tective layer on the lithium electrode, which will prevent direct
chemical interaction of metallic lithium with sulfur and lithium
polysulﬁdes dissolved in the electrolyte systems.[24,27,32–34,39,40]
Five, the introduction of adsorbents into the composition of
the positive electrode to retain lithium polysulﬁdes in the volume
of the electrode.[41–43] Finally, optimization of the structure and
composition of the current collector of the sulfur electrode can
also be done.[20,21,44]
Thus, it can be concluded that most of the methods in
decreasing the self-discharge of LSBs prevent the access of sulfur
and lithium polysulﬁdes, dissolved in the electrolyte systems, to
the lithium metallic electrode. These methods made the
reduction of the self-discharge of LSBs to 0.1–1.0% per day
for short-term storage (less than a week) possible.
In the process of charge–discharge cycling, ﬁne, dispersed
lithium precipitates (dendrites, moss, and so on) with higher
chemical activities compared with compact metallic lithium (lith-
ium foil) can form and accumulate on the surface of the metallic
lithium electrode. The formation and accumulation of ﬁne,
dispersed lithium can signiﬁcantly increase the self-discharge
rate of LSBs.
One should also consider the fact that the reactivity of lithium
polysulﬁdes decreases with a decrease in their order.[45] As the
composition of lithium polysulﬁdes is determined by the state
of charge of LSBs,[46–49] the rate of self-discharge of the batteries
depends on the state of their charge.[17,50]
An analysis of publications devoted to solving problems in the
development of LSBs concludes the following: the mechanisms
of self-discharge and aging of LSBs attract relatively little atten-
tion. Mostly, short-term studies on the self-discharge of LSBs are
reported in the literature. Systematic studies of the effect of vari-
ous factors (for example, the composition of the positive elec-
trode, the composition and physicochemical properties of
electrolytes, the properties of carbon materials, and so on) on
self-discharge and aging of LSBs are single.[17,20,51] It should also
be noted that in most works, the factors causing the irreversible
and reversible loss of capacity of LSBs are not analyzed.[50] Self-
discharge (reversible loss of capacity) of LSBs during storage is
due to the direct chemical interaction of sulfur and lithium poly-
sulﬁdes with the metallic lithium electrode (Equation (1) and (2)).
Aging (irreversible loss of capacity) is due to various processes
occurring on both positive and negative electrodes.[17,50]
The aim of the present work was to study the effect of carbon
content in the positive electrode of lithium–sulfur cells on their
self-discharge rate and aging.
2. Results and Discussion
The self-discharge rates and aging of lithium–sulfur cells are
appreciably determined by their prehistory and composition.
Therefore, in this study, we studied the self-discharge and aging
of lithium–sulfur cells as a function of the composition of posi-
tive electrodes and the number of cycles.
2.1. Self-Discharge of the Non-Precycled Lithium–Sulfur Cells
The open-circuit voltage (OCV) of lithium–sulfur cells, measured
immediately after assembling, is usually slightly greater than the
thermodynamic value and is usually in the range of 3.1–3.2 V.
The elevated value of the OCV of freshly assembled lithium–
sulfur cells is due to the presence of oxygen dissolved in the
electrolyte and sorbed by the carbon component of the positive
electrodes.
During long-term storage of lithium–sulfur cells that were not
precycled, in the open-circuit state, their OCV initially, within a
few days, decreased sharply from 3.1–3.2 V to 2.5–2.55 V, and
then, the rate of change of the OCV signiﬁcantly decreased
and the OCV stabilized. After several days of storage in the
open-circuit state, the OCV of lithium–sulfur cells reached a
value close to the thermodynamic value for the electrochemical
system of lithium–sulfur (Figure 1). The sharp decrease in the
OCV of lithium–sulfur cells in the ﬁrst days of storage is
explained by the consumption of oxygen in the cells for the oxi-
dation of sulfur and metallic lithium. It should be noted that the
carbon content in the positive electrode has a small effect on the
change in the OCV of lithium–sulfur cells during their storage.
To estimate the effect of storage time in the open-circuit state
on the mechanism and self-discharge rate of lithium–sulfur cells,
the charge–discharge cycling of the following two groups of cells
was studied: cells stored for 1 h after assembling and cells stored
for 2 months after assembling.
On the discharge voltage proﬁle of lithium–sulfur cells, stored
for 1 h before cycling (Figure 2), there are ﬁve sections: in the
ﬁrst section (high voltage section) of the discharge voltage proﬁle,
there is a sharp drop in voltage from 2.8 to 2.5 V. In the second
section (high voltage plateau) of the discharge voltage proﬁle, the
voltage decreases slowly from 2.5 to 2.3 V. In the third section,
the discharge voltage decreases rapidly from 2.3 to 2.0 V. In the
fourth section (low voltage plateau) of the discharge voltage pro-
ﬁle, the voltage decreases slowly from 2.0 to 1.9 V. In the ﬁfth
section, the discharge voltage drops from 1.9 to 1.5 V.
It should be noted that in the hour after assembly, the OCV of
the lithium–sulfur cells was 3.0–3.1 V, and the initial discharge
voltage was 2.75–2.80 V.
Most likely, the initial part of the discharge voltage proﬁle
corresponds to the electrochemical reduction of oxygen impuri-
ties contained in the cells. The lower discharge voltage value
(Figure 2) compared with the OCV (Figure 1) may be caused

The composition of positive electrodes and the storage time of
lithium–sulfur cells before the start of cycling affect the
discharge capacity depletion during cycling (Figure 4).
At the initial stages of the cycling, the capacity of the lithium–
sulfur cells, stored for 1 h after assembling, decreases rapidly
regardless of the carbon content in the positive electrodes.
Then, the rate of the discharge capacity depletion decreases.
There are plateaus on the capacity depletion proﬁles. After
60–80 cycles, the discharge capacities began to fade again during
cycling. It should be noted that for lithium–sulfur cells with
positive electrode SE-5, the discharge capacity is greater, and
the rate of capacity depletion during cycling is lower than cells
with positive electrode SE-15.
The proﬁles of the capacity depletion of the cells, which were
stored for 2 months before cycling, differ from similar proﬁles
for the cells stored for 1 h. There are no plateaus in the proﬁles
of the capacity depletion of the cells that were stored for 2 months
before cycling. Moreover, for the initial cycles, the discharge
capacity of cells that were stored for 2 months after assembling
decreases more slowly than for cells that were stored for 1 h.
The observed regularities in changing the capacity of lithium–
sulfur cells with positive electrodes containing different amounts
of carbon and stored for different times after assembling can be
explained as follows.
We
have
previously
shown
that
during
galvanostatic
charge–discharge cycling of lithium–sulfur cells in pores of
carbon particles (Ketjenblack EC-600JD), “electrochemically
inactive” lithium sulﬁde gradually accumulates.[56]
The higher rate of capacity depletion for lithium–sulfur cells
with positive electrode SE-15 at the initial stages of cycling com-
pared with the rate of capacity depletion for cells with electrode
SE-5 can be caused by the decrease in the amount of electro-
chemically active sulfur due to encapsulation of lithium sulﬁde
into the pores of carbon particles (Ketjenblack EC-600JD). Simple
calculations of the sorption capacity of carbon (Ketjenblack
EC-600JD) in positive electrode by Li2S (the amount of lithium
sulﬁde that can be placed into the pores of carbon particles)
show that the positive electrode SE-5 can accumulate up to
0.33 mg cm2 of Li2S, and the electrode SE-15 can accumulate
up to 1.13 mg cm2 (Table 2).
The sulfur content in the electrodes is 1.5 mg cm2, which is
equivalent to a lithium sulﬁde content of 2.15 mg cm2 if the
sulfur is completely reduced to lithium sulﬁde. Thus, the initial
decrease in the capacity of lithium–sulfur cells due to encapsu-
lation and blocking of lithium sulﬁde into the pores of carbon
particles can reach 15% in the case of electrode SE-5 and 53%
with electrode SE-15.
The capacity depletion of the cells (Figure 4) in the initial
stages of cycling adequately correlates with the calculated values
of the maximum possible amount of “electrochemically dead”
lithium sulﬁde blocked in the pores of carbon particles
(Table 2). This may indicate that one of the main reasons for
the initial capacity depletion of lithium–sulfur cells with
Ketjenblack EC-600JD is the encapsulation and blocking of sulfur
in the form of lithium sulﬁde in the pores of carbon particles.
To test the hypothesis expressed, we studied the effect of the
properties of carbon, which is part of the positive electrode, on
the capacity depletion of lithium–sulfur cells in the ﬁrst 50
charge–discharge cycles. Multiwall carbon nanotubes (MCNTs)
with a signiﬁcantly smaller pore volume and speciﬁc surface area
were selected as the object of study (Table 3). The study was
performed under identical conditions. The composition of the
positive electrode was 15 wt% carbon, 65 wt% sulfur, and
20 wt% binder (PEO).
Studies have shown that the properties of the carbon compo-
nent (speciﬁc surface area and pore volume) do not affect the
discharge capacity at the ﬁrst cycle (Figure 5). The shapes of
the discharge voltage proﬁles of lithium–sulfur cells with carbon
black Ketjenblack EC-600JD and MCNTs are similar. However,
the properties of the carbon component signiﬁcant affect initial
capacity depletion. In the case of MCNTs, the initial capacity

Sulfur can also accumulate on the negative lithium electrode

We studied the accumulation of lithium sulﬁde on the surface
of the lithium electrode during long-term storage of lithium–

and redox titration. It is shown that the accumulation of lithium

sulfur cells in the open-circuit state for 2 months, regardless
of the sulfur/carbon ratio, the cells gradually self-discharge
because of the interaction of sulfur dissolved in the electrolyte
with the metallic lithium electrode. The self-discharge rate
weakly depends on the carbon content in the positive electrode.

trode SE-5 is 3.2% per month, and the self-discharge rate of cells
with positive electrode SE-15 is 2.7% per month. The difference
in the rates of self-discharge is probably caused by differences in
the diffusion mobility of sulfur in electrodes containing different
amounts of carbon.
It has been established that during long-term storage of
lithium–sulfur cells under the open-circuit state, lithium sulﬁde
does not accumulate on the lithium electrode.

Lithium–sulfur cells have lower discharge capacities after
2 months of storage. The cells with positive electrode SE-15 have
the biggest capacity loss. For example, the discharge capacity of
the cell with the positive electrode SE-15 is 890 mAh g1(S) at the
10th cycle and 300 mAh g1(S) at the 11th cycle after storage
(Figure 4 and 6). The capacity of the lithium–sulfur cell with
the positive electrode SE-5 is 950 and 435 mAh g1(S) at the
10th and 11th cycles.
On subsequent charge–discharge cycles, the capacity of
lithium–sulfur cells only partially restores without reaching
the values of similar discharge capacities for cells that are cycling
continuously. During cycling after storage, the lithium–sulfur
cells with positive electrode SE-5 restore capacity better than
the cells with electrode SE-15. However, it should be noted that
in all cases, the discharge capacity does not recover to the values
of the cells that were cycled without pauses (Figure 6). This
indicates that during storage, the lithium–sulfur cells have a
reversible capacity loss (self-discharge) and an irreversible
capacity loss (cell aging).
During the initial cycles, the cells with positive electrode SE-15
aged stronger than the cells with electrode SE-5. During cycling,
the aging process slows down. The decrease in carbon content in
the positive electrodes of lithium–sulfur cells also slows the aging
process.
As the cells are cycled after storage, the irreversible capacity
loss of the cells gets smaller (Table 1).
Irreversible capacity loss (cell aging) can be explained as fol-
lows. Lithium sulﬁde can be formed both in electrochemical and
disproportionation reactions.[57–59] When lithium–sulfur cells
are discharged, these processes proceed in parallel, and during
storage, lithium sulﬁde can be formed only in disproportionation
reactions. Lithium sulﬁde formed during cycling of the cells, as
well as during their storage, can be deposited both in the pores of
carbon particles and in the structural pores of the electrode.
When deposited in the structural pores of the positive electrode,
lithium sulﬁde partially blocks the surface of the carbon network
(conducting phase) and prevents the electrochemical processes.
During precipitation in the pores of carbon particles, lithium
sulﬁde can completely block the pores, thereby eliminating
the possibility of electrochemical reactions inside the pores.[56]
The lithium sulﬁde, blocked in the pores of carbon particles,
cannot participate in electrochemical reactions due to the difﬁ-
culty of the electrolyte accessing the pores. Therefore, lithium
sulﬁde, formed in the pores of carbon particles, can be only par-
tially oxidized when the lithium–sulfur cells are charged. In the
work[60] using the method of the low-temperature sorption of
nitrogen, it was shown that due to the formation of lithium sul-
ﬁde, when the lithium–sulfur cell is fully discharged, the micro-
pores of the Ketjenblack EC-600JD are blocked, and the size of
the mesopores decreases. Due to transport difﬁculties, the disso-
lution of lithium sulﬁde as a result of electrochemical oxidation
(Equation (3)) or chemical interaction with lithium polysulﬁdes
(Equation (4)) is difﬁcult, maybe even impossible.

Due to their small size, depassivation of the pores in carbon
particles occurs either slower than structural pores of the elec-
trode or does not occur at all. Therefore, the large irreversible
capacity loss of the cells with positive electrode SE-15 compared
with electrode SE-5 is probably caused by a large total pore
volume of carbon particles.
Cycling of lithium–sulfur cells after long-term storage pre-
dominantly causes depassivation of the structural pores of the
positive electrode, which leads to the gradual increase in the
capacity of the cells. As the depassivation of the pores of the car-
bon particles does not occur or does only partially, the full recov-
ery of the capacity of lithium–sulfur cells during cycling after
storage is not observed.
During cycling of the lithium–sulfur cells, lithium sulﬁde
gradually ﬁlls the pores of the carbon particles. Therefore, the
capacity of lithium–sulfur cells decreases with cycling, and the
irreversible loss capacity during storage decreases.
This hypothesis is conﬁrmed by the fact that the irreversible
capacity loss of lithium–sulfur cells (Table 1) is comparable with
the amount of lithium sulﬁde that can be placed in the pores of
carbon particles of Ketjenblack EC-600JD (Table 2). This may
indicate that the irreversible capacity loss of the cells is due to
the accumulation of lithium sulﬁde in the pores of carbon
particles and their complete blocking.
Also, it should be noted that the irreversible capacity loss of the
lithium–sulfur cells previously cycled for ten cycles and stored
for 2 months (Table 1) is comparable with the initial capacity loss
of the cells cycled without pauses (Figure 6). The aging processes,
associated with the encapsulation of lithium sulﬁde in the pores
of carbon particles, occur both during cycling and storage of
lithium–sulfur cells.
The irreversible capacity loss after ten charge–discharge cycles
and storage for 2 months is 15–20% for the lithium–sulfur cells
with electrode SE-5 and 35–40% with electrode SE-15.
Thus, the irreversible and reversible capacity loss of the
lithium–sulfur cells with sulfur electrode SE-5 is lower than
for similar cells with electrode SE-15.
The irreversible capacity loss does not depend on the carbon
content in the positive electrode for the lithium–sulfur cells that
were cycled for the additional 50 cycles (60 and 120 cycles) and
stored for 2 months (Table 1, Figure 6). The other aging pro-
cesses can occur in the cells in addition to the encapsulation
of lithium sulﬁde in the pores of the carbon particles. Most likely,
they are the decomposition of the components of the electrolyte
system at the lithium electrode and the accumulation of lithium
sulﬁde on the lithium electrode (the product of the direct chemi-
cal interaction of sulfur and lithium polysulﬁdes with lithium).
3. Conclusion
Thus, the following can be concluded. When non-precycled
lithium–sulfur cells are stored in the open-circuit state for
2 months, regardless of the sulfur/carbon ratio, the average
self-discharge rate is 0.1% per day. Irreversible capacity loss is
not observed.
It has been established that lithium sulﬁde does not accumu-
late on the lithium electrode during the storage of non-precycled
lithium–sulfur cells in the open-circuit state for 120 days. The
equilibrium content of lithium sulﬁde on the lithium electrode
is 0.06 mg cm2.
The encapsulation of sulfur or lithium sulﬁde in the pores of
carbon black (Ketjenblack EC-600JD) causes the irreversible
capacity loss and the initial capacity fade. Increasing the carbon
(Ketjenblack EC-600JD) loading in the electrode increases the
irreversible capacity loss of lithium–sulfur cells during storage
and the initial rate of capacity fade during cycling. To reduce
capacity loss, it is necessary to prevent the precipitation and
blocking of lithium sulﬁde into the carbon particles.
Sulfur and lithium polysulﬁdes dissolve in the electrolyte and
interact with the lithium metal electrode, causing the formation
of lithium sulﬁde. The accumulation of lithium sulﬁde on the
negative electrode is another reason for the irreversible capacity
loss during prolonged cycling and storage of the lithium–sulfur
cells.
Sulfur and highly ordered lithium polysulﬁdes interact with
the metallic lithium electrode, causing the formation of soluble
lithium polysulﬁdes with the lower order. This is the reason for
the reversible capacity loss.
