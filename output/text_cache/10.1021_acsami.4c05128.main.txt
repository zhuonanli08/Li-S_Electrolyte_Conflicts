Synergistic Effect of Lactam and Pyridine Nitrogen on Polysulfide
Chemisorption and Electrocatalysis in Lithium Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: Sulfur undergoes various changes, including the formation of negative charge-bearing lithium polysulfides during the
operation of Li−S batteries. Dissolution of some of the polysulfides in battery electrolytes is one of the reasons for the poor
performance of Li−S batteries. The charge injection into the sulfur and polysulfides from the electrode is also a problem. To address
these issues, a small-molecule additive, 3,6-di(pyridin-4-yl)-2,5-dihydropyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-dione, was designed and
synthesized with carbonyl oxygen atoms and two types of nitrogen. The pyridinic nitrogen increases the electronegativity of the
carbonyl oxygen atoms. The pyridinic nitrogen, carbonyl oxygen, and lactam nitrogen provide multiple binding sites concurrently to
the polysulfides, which increases the binding efficiency between the additive and polysulfides. A control molecule without the
pyridine moiety displayed decreased binding to lithium polysulfides. Furthermore, the band edges of lithium polysulfide and 3,6-
di(pyridin-4-yl)-2,5-dihydropyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-dione are commensurate for efficient charge transfer between them, leading to
the efficient electrocatalysis of lithium polysulfides. The cyclic voltammogram of the Li−S battery fabricated with 3,6-di(pyridin-4-
yl)-2,5-dihydropyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-dione exhibited sharp and well-defined peaks, confirming the formation of Li2Sy (where y
varies between one and eight) from S8. These Li−S batteries showed a specific capacity of 950 mA h/g at 0.5 C, with a capacity
retention of 70% at the 300th cycle. The pyridine-free control molecule, 3,6-diphenyl-2,5-dihydropyrrolo[3,4-c]pyrrole-1,4-dione,
showed relatively poor performance in a Li−S battery.
KEYWORDS: Li−S battery, phenyl-diketopyrrolopyrrole, pyridine-diketopyrrolopyrrole, polysulfides, noncovalent interaction
■INTRODUCTION
Due to their high theoretical capacity (1675 mA h/g) and
energy density (2600 W h/kg), Li−S batteries are considered a
potential alternative to commercial Li-ion batteries. The low
cost and abundance of sulfur on the earth’s crust make Li−S
batteries more attractive.1 However, Li−S battery chemistry
involves many fundamental challenges that are yet to be solved.
During the battery operation, the cyclic S8 opens and forms a
series of lithium polysulfides Li2Sy, wherein y varies between
one and eight. Some of the Li2Sy species are soluble in a battery
electrolyte. The polysulfide dissolution and subsequent
transport of these soluble species to the anode are known as
the polysulfide shuttle effect. Furthermore, these dissolved
polysulfides passivate the Li metal anode surface with an
insulating Li2S2/Li2S layer, resulting in high polarization and
dendrite formation. This affects important battery metrics such
as Coulombic efficiency and cycling life. In the Li−S batteries,
half the capacity originates from the transformation of Li2S2 to
Li2S, and this transformation is considered a rate-limiting step
during sulfur reduction. Pronounced Li2S deposition during
discharge and a high energy barrier for its decomposition

during charging cause passivation of the electrode/electrolyte
interface, thus resulting in quick capacity fade and poor cycling
life.2−6 Therefore, it is important to rationally design and
synthesize sulfur hosts with strong lithium polysulfide
adsorption capability and moieties to electrocatalyze Li2Sy
conversions.
Toward this objective, many electronically conducting
carbon-based sulfur hosts have been developed. Carbon
allotropes with various pore architectures are used to confine
the polysulfides. However, the polysulfides detach from the
surface of the carbon since it possesses a weak binding
interaction between the nonpolar carbon surface and polar
polysulfides.7−13 To address this issue, heteroatom doping or
installation of polar functionality on the carbon surface was
introduced since they possess a strong polysulfide binding
affinity. The polar functional groups also induce the electron
transfer to lithium polysulfides during the discharge, thus
improving the catalytic conversion of Li2Sy.14−17 For instance,
N-doped carbon materials and polymers with rich nitrogen
content have been studied extensively, and they have shown
excellent performance as cathodes in Li−S batteries.16,18−21 It
is well established that the pyridinic nitrogen and pyrrolic
nitrogen exhibit very different electrocatalytic activities.22−24
However, most of the nitrogen-doped carbon systems are
prepared via high-temperature carbonization, and control over
the nature of the N species is very challenging.25−27 On the
other hand, control over the nature of N species in organic
small molecules is relatively easy. Previous studies have shown
that pyridinic N plays a significant role in suppressing the
polysulfide shuttle effect. Furthermore, the pyridine motif
could facilitate faster Li+ ion diffusion, which in turn enhances
the charge transfer kinetics during battery operation.28−31
From our earlier work involving nickel phthalocyanine, we
know that the Li+ ions interact with pyrrolic nitrogen. The
interaction energy decreased as a function of the increase in
the number of sulfur atoms in the polysulfide. From literature
reports, we understand that the interaction can be stronger
with pyridinic N.25−27 With this background, we asked the
following questions. If a molecule comprises both pyrrolic and
pyridinic N, would Li2Sy interact with higher efficiency? Will
the interaction energy vary despite having two types of
nitrogen as a function of variation in the number of sulfur
atoms in Li2Sy? Which nitrogen is efficient in transferring
charge to the lithium polysulfide? Does the presence of oxygen,
which is a close neighbor of nitrogen in the electronegativity
scale, improve the interaction between a host and Li2Sy? To
address these questions, we synthesized a molecule labeled
PyDPP with a carbonyl oxygen, a pyrrole type nitrogen (lactam
N), and a pyridine nitrogen (pyridine N). A control molecule
with only the lactam N and carbonyl oxygen was also
synthesized, labeled PhDPP. High specific capacities of 903
and 950 mA h/g were obtained for PhDPP/C/S and PyDPP/
C/S, respectively at 0.5 C. High sulfur loading did not impact
the battery performance. Furthermore, the specific capacity
loss for the cell comprising PyDPP at 1.0 C is very low as a
function of 400 charge−discharge cycles.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. Anhydrous iron(III) chloride (≥99%), t-amyl alcohol,
Na metal, concentrated HCl (37%), methanol, diethyl succinate,
benzonitrile, 2-pyridinecarbonitrile, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP),
sulfur, lithium sulfide (Li2S), lithium bis(trifluoromethane) sulfoni-
mide (LiTFSI), lithium nitrate (LiNO3), 1,2-dimethoxyethane
anhydrous (99.5%, inhibitor free, DME), and 1,3-dioxolane (99.5%,
DOL) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich chemicals. Poly(vinylidene
fluoride) PVDF, Super P carbon, the carbon-coated aluminum current
collector, Li foil (300 μm), CR2032 coin cell components, and the
CR2300 polymer separator were procured from Global Nano-
technology Pvt. Ltd. All reagents were used as received without any
further purification.
Synthesis of PhDPP and PyDPP. PhDPP was synthesized by
following the reported procedure with a slight modification.32 In a
typical synthesis, Na metal (700 mg) and FeCl3 (10 mg) were added
to t-amyl alcohol in a 250 mL two-neck round-bottom flask equipped
with a magnetic stirrer. The mixture was heated at 90 °C under a N2
atmosphere until all the sodium pieces dissolved completely. Then,
benzonitrile (2.0 g) was added dropwise, and subsequently, diethyl
succinate (1.4 g) was added after 15−20 min. The resulting mixture
was heated at 90 °C for 24 h. The mixture was then allowed to cool
and added to 100 mL of precooled methanol, followed by dropwise
addition of concentrated HCl. Finally, the obtained precipitate was
filtered to get the PhDPP (yield 40%). Similarly, PyDPP was
synthesized by following the above procedure and replacing
benzonitrile with 2-pyridinecarbonitrile (2.0 g). The yield was 35%.
Collectively, the molecules are referred to as DPP.
Preparation of the Carbon/Sulfur (C/S) Composite. The C/S
composite was prepared using a conventional melt diffusion method
by mixing Super P carbon and sulfur at a 3:7 weight ratio. The
mixture was ground for an hour, followed by heating at 155 °C for 12
h under an Ar atmosphere.
Structural Characterization. 1H and 13C NMR spectra of the
DPP were recorded on a BRUKER arx 400 and 500 MHz AVANS
spectrometer. The structure of the molecules was confirmed by 1H,
13C NMR spectroscopy and mass spectroscopy (BRUKER MALDI
TOF MS). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to
examine the chemical states of the elements (Thermo Scientific K-
Alpha+, a fully integrated, monochromatic small-spot XPS system).
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to examine the thermal
stability and sulfur loading of the materials (SDT Q600 DSC-TDA
thermogravimetric instrument; temperature range of 10−900 °C;
ramp of 10 °C/min; nitrogen atmosphere). A field emission scanning
electron microscope was used to analyze the microstructure and
morphology of the electrodes [FEI Nova Nano SEM 450 field-
emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) instrument]. UV−
visible (UV−vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a
SPECORD 210/PLUS spectrophotometer.
Polysulfide Adsorption Test. For the polysulfide adsorption test,
a 0.2 M Li2S6 solution was prepared by mixing sulfur and Li2S with a
molar ratio of 5:1 and was added into the mixture of 1,3-dioxolane
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME, 1:1, v/v). This solution was
stirred at 80 °C for 24 h. The resulting solution was further diluted to
0.005 M Li2S6. Either PhDPP (10 mg) or PyDPP (10 mg) was added
to 5.0 mL of 0.005 M Li2S6 solution and kept quiescent overnight.
The solution was further analyzed using UV−vis absorption
spectroscopy.
To study the dissolution of polysulfide and DPP, after cycling the
cell through 20 charge−discharge cycles, the cell was dismantled in a
glovebox. The separator was soaked in 3 mL of a DOL/DME (1:1. v/
v) solution for 30 min before obtaining the UV−vis absorbance of the
solution, assuming that a similar volume of electrolyte was present in
each separator.
Symmetric Cell Fabrication and Analysis of Polysulfide
Conversion Kinetics. The electrodes for symmetric cells were
prepared by casting a homogeneous slurry of Super P carbon, either
PyDPP or PhDPP, and PVDF with a mass ratio of 8:1:1 using NMP
solvent onto an Al current collector and drying overnight at 120 °C.
The electrodes were cut into circular discs and assembled into
standard CR-2032-coin cells using two identical electrodes. The
electrolyte, 40 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6 dissolved in a DOL/DME (1:1 v/v)
mixture with 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3, was prepared. The
symmetric cells were cycled between −1.0 and +1.0 V at a scan rate of
10 mV/s.

Electrochemical Characterization. The electrodes were pre-
pared by mixing the C/S composite, super P carbon, either PyDPP or
PhDPP, and PVDF in a 7:1:1:1 mass ratio in NMP, and the slurry was
cast onto the carbon-coated Al foil and dried under vacuum at 80 °C.
The electrodes were cut into circular discs, and the half cells were
assembled using a CR2300 polymer separator and 50 μL of 1.0 M
LiTFSI in DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) with a 0.2 M LiNO3 electrolyte. The
sulfur loading was maintained at 1.0−2.0 mg/cm2. The cells were
assembled inside an argon filled glovebox with moisture, and oxygen
levels were maintained at <0.1 ppm. Galvanostatic charge−discharge
experiments were conducted using NEWARE battery testers with a
potential window of 1.5 to 3.0 V against Li/Li+. Cyclic voltammetry
and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were carried
out using a BioLogic VSP-300 potentiostat. For the EIS analysis, a
frequency range of 10 mHz−1 MHz with an amplitude of 10 mV was
used.

To further understand the interaction of DPP with
polysulfides, density functional theory (DFT) calculations
were performed. The optimized geometry of various Li2Sy
interactions with DPP are shown in Figures S9−S10, and the
adsorption energy values are listed in Table S1. Li2Sy (1 ≤y ≤
8) exhibits constant interaction energy with the pyridinic N of
PyDPP (Figure 2c), but the interaction energy with the lactam
N decreased to a small extent as a function of the increase in
the number of sulfur atoms in Li2Sy. The interaction of Li2Sy
with carbonyl oxygen remained unchanged as a function of the
number of sulfur atoms (Figure 2). This could be due to the
fact that the pyridinic N increases the electronegativity of the
carbonyl oxygen, which leads to the possibility of better
interaction with polysulfides. Thus, the presence of pyridinic N
shows an additive effect in the interaction energy with Li2Sy.
The higher interaction energy with the soluble Li2Sy is
expected to circumvent the polysulfide shuttle effect. In the
absence of pyridinic N (PhDPP), the interaction energy
between polysulfides and lactam N decreases as the number of
sulfur atoms in Li2Sy increases. The same trend is observed for
the interaction energy between carbonyl oxygen and Li2Sy
(Figure 2d). This may be due to the absence of pyridine in
PhDPP; hence, there is no change to the electronegative
character of the carbonyl oxygen.
The other important aspect is the charge transfer between
Li2S and DPP. For this process, the energy levels of DPP and
Li2Sy must be commensurate. The energy of the highest
occupied molecular orbital, EHOMO, of Li2S is −0.1604 eV and
those of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, ELUMO, of
PhDPP and PyDPP are situated at −0.1062 and −0.1768 eV,
respectively. The ELUMO of PhDPP located at −0.1062 eV is
not commensurate with charge transfer between Li2S and DPP.
Thus, the batteries based on this molecule exhibit poor
performance. On the other hand, ELUMO of PyDPP located at
−0.1768 eV is commensurate with charge transfer for Li2S. In
the battery operation, the DPP was reduced to its radical
anion; hence, the ELUMO values of these species were
calculated. The ELUMO values of radical anion of PyDPP and
PhDPP are found to be −0.1742 and −0.1762 eV, respectively
(Table S2), which are aligned with the Li2S energy level for
charge transfer (Figure 2e). Therefore, both PyDPP and
PhDPP work as a good host for sulfur and polysulfides.
However, due to increased interaction of Li2Sy with PyDPP,
the shuttle effect is decreased significantly, leading to overall
increased performance compared to that of PhDPP-based
batteries.
The orbital overlapping of Li of Li2S with 2p orbitals of
carbonyl oxygens (PhDPP) and pyridinic N and carbonyl
oxygens of PyDPP is shown in Figure S11a−d. The stronger
interaction of Li from Li2S with the pyridinic N of PyDPP
stems from the in-phase overlap of the 1s orbital of Li with the
2p orbital of pyridinic N (Figure S11e), leading to the small
change in the energy gap of 0.0322 eV from 0.0281 eV. Thus,
the pyridinic N can greatly improve the conversion kinetics
and the overall performance of the batteries, as evident from
the frontier molecular orbitals and adsorption energy values.
To investigate the electrocatalysis of Li2Sy, symmetric cells
were fabricated using PyDPP/C/PVDF and PhDPP/C/PVDF
electrodes using Li2S6 and a 1.0 M LiTFSI electrolyte. These
cells were cycled between −1.0 and +1.0 V at a scan rate of 10
mV/s. The PyDPP cell displayed two reversible redox peaks at
±0.15 and ±0.65 V corresponding to the reversible conversion
of Li2S6 to Li2S and subsequent conversion to sulfur (Figure
3a), with a higher current than that of PhDPP. This suggests
that the catalytic activity of PyDPP is better than that of
PhDPP, and PyDPP promotes Li2Sy conversion at a faster rate
than that of PhDPP.38

isorption of Li2Sy and electrocatalysis. Therefore, the voltage
dip is low in the case of PyDPP. This corroborates the fact that
PyDPP can electrocatalyze the Li2Sy conversion better than
PhDPP. It is noted that while the area under the CV curve for
PhDPP/C/S is higher, the capacity of the battery comprising a
PhDPP/C/S cathode is less than that of the PyDPP/C/S one
because of the minimum overpotential and faster redox
kinetics in the case of PyDPP.

were observed for PyDPP/C/S and PhDPP/C/S, respectively
(Figure 4a). The low overpotential for this process stems from
the strong chemical affinity between Li2Sy via the pyridinic N−
Li interactions, which promotes the kinetics of Li2S nucleation,
precipitation, and conversion catalysis.41,42

To understand the kinetics of Li+ ion diffusion and
polysulfide conversion reactions at the electrode−electrolyte
interface, electrochemical impedance tests were performed.
The Nyquist plots for both PyDPP/C/S- and PhDPP/C/S-
based batteries after the first discharge experiment are shown
in Figure 5a. The Nyquist plot shows a semicircle in the high−
medium frequency regime with an inclined line in the low-
frequency regime. After the 50th cycle, two semicircles are
observed at the midhigh-frequency regime with an inclined line
in the low-frequency regime in the Nyquist plot (Figure 5b).
The plots were fitted with an equivalent circuit where the
component Ro represents the solution resistance, Rct represents
the charge transfer resistance, and the component Rs represents
the resistance contribution from Li2S2/Li2S deposition (see
insets, Figure 5a,b).43−45 The linear line corresponds to Li+ ion
diffusion. PhDPP/C/S exhibits a higher Rct value at the first
cycle than the PyDPP/C/S cathode, indicating better charge
transport in PyDPP due to the strong pyridinic N−Li
interaction. With charge−discharge cycling, the Rct values
increased in both cathodes, which could be attributed to the
activation step before attaining the electrochemical stability.
The Ro values were low in both the cathodes comprising
PyDPP and PhDPP, which may be due to the relocation of
sulfur that reduces the tendency to form a passivation layer on
the electrode surface.22,26 However, the Ro values of the
PhDPP-comprising cathode are higher than those of PyDPP-
comprising cathodes, which is attributed to the high
concentration of polysulfides in the electrolyte due to the
poor interactions of polysulfides with PhDPP. In addition,
PyDPP/C/S also possesses a small Rs value, indicating the
accelerated conversion of lithium polysulfides. The charge
transfer processes at various potentials were investigated, with
the impedance curve reported at the 50th cycle relating to the
cell under charging at 2.3 V. It is noteworthy that at 2.3 V, two
semicircles were observed in high- to mid-frequency regions,
corresponding to charge transfer resistance and resistance by
residual polysulfides. Figure S13 depicts the impedance
measurements at different charge and discharge states, where
at 2.1 V D (D = discharge), a small second semicircle starts
appearing for PyDPP/C/S, which is attributed to the
conversion of soluble lithium polysulfides to the insoluble
Li2S2/Li2S.22,46,47 When the cell is discharged further, the
diameter of the semicircle is increased. Upon charging of the
cell containing PyDPP/C/S, the diameter of this semicircle
gradually decreased after 2.3 V C (C = charge), reached a
minimum at 2.4 V C, and disappeared at 2.7 V C completely,
indicating the complete conversion of Li2S to higher-order
soluble polysulfides and finally to S8. A similar trend was
observed in PhDPP/C/S, but with higher values of charge
transfer resistance, bulk resistance, and resistance from lithium
polysulfides. This could be due to the much lower adsorption
energy of polysulfides on PhDPP, which leads to an increased
concentration of polysulfides in the electrolyte, and hence, the
resistance values are high compared to those of PyDPP-
comprising cathodes.
The cycling stability of these cells was investigated at
different C rates. PyDPP/C/S delivered a high specific capacity
of 1220 mA h/g and retained ∼70% of its initial capacity after
300 cycles (Figure 6a) at 0.2 C with a Coulombic efficiency of
∼99%. PhDPP/C/S showed an initial capacity of 1043 mA h/g
at 0.2 C, where the capacity was reduced significantly to 500
mA h/g after 300 cycles (Figure 6a). The corresponding
charge−discharge profile is shown in Figure S12. The
improved cyclic stability of the PyDPP/C/S cathode is
attributed to the strong polysulfide-immobilizing capabilities
of PyDPP through interactions between Li2Sy and pyridine N,
lactam N, and carbonyl oxygen. As mentioned earlier, the
efficient electron transfer between PyDPP and Li2Sy is due to
the energy level offset. The batteries were also tested at 0.5 C,
where the PyDPP/C/S-containing cells displayed an initial
capacity of 950 mA h/g, and after 300 cycles, the cell retained
70% of its initial capacity. On the contrary, PhDPP/C/S
delivered a capacity of 903 mA h/g with almost 50% loss after
300 cycles (Figure 6b).
Furthermore, the PyDPP/C/S-containing cell was tested at
1.0 C, showing an improved performance with a maximum

reduced significantly in the case of a cell containing PyDPP
compared to those of the cell containing PhDPP. This shows
that PyDPP is more effective in binding lithium polysulfides
than PhDPP.
■CONCLUSIONS
Two organic conjugated small molecules with pyridine- and
phenyl-substituted DPP have been rationally designed and
synthesized. These molecules were utilized as additives in
sulfur cathodes, and their electrochemical performance was
evaluated. The pyridinic N provides strong affinity toward
polysulfides and facilitates their quick conversion via electron
transfer compared to lactam N. Ex-situ XPS and DFT
calculations reveal the strong interaction energies for
polysulfides via Li···N interactions, thus suppressing the
polysulfide shuttle effect and improving the cycling life of
these batteries. The electrochemical performance of a Li−S cell
comprising PyDPP outperforms that of a PhDPP-containing
cell in terms of specific capacity, cycling stability, and rate
performance. This is attributed to the strong adsorption energy
of pyridinic N for lithium polysulfides. Overall, this small-
molecule additive played a significant role in improving the
overall performance of the Li−S batteries. This study paves the
way to design many such small-molecule additives to achieve
high-performance and cost-effective Li−S batteries.
