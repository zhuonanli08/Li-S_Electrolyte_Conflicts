A B S T R A C T
N-methoxyethyl-N-methylpyrrolidinium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Pyr1,2O1TFSI)-based elec-
trolyte is used in Li/S cells, and a series of electrolyte with binary Pyr1,2O1TFSI-1,3-dioxolane (DOL) or
Pyr1,2O1TFSI-1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent is investigated, and bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide lithium
(LiFSI) is further used as the additive. The effect of Pyr1,2O1TFSI is studied in terms of scanning electron
microscopy (SEM), UV-visible absorption spectroscopy (UV) as well as electrochemical measurement.
The results show that using 1 mol kg1 bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide lithium (LiTFSI)/Pyr1,2O1TFSI-
DOL (65:35 w/w) with 1 wt % LiFSI electrolyte leads to the most stable cycling and decent capacity
performance, along with high coulombic efﬁciency.

1. Introduction
Lithium/sulfur (Li/S) battery is an important candidate for next
generation energy storage due to its high theoretical capacity
(1675 mAh g1) and low cost of sulfur (S) [1–3]. However, many
issues need to be tackled before its practical application, such as
the insulating nature of S and dissolution of the reaction
intermediates, mainly lithium polysulﬁde (Li2Sx), into the electro-
lyte [4] which causes the capacity decay and low coulombic
efﬁciency. Aiming to solve the problems, well-designed S/carbon
(C), S/polymer or other multi-layer composites have been proposed
to replace pure S as cathode. They greatly enhance the sulfur
utilization and cycling stability because carbon or polymer
network can increase the electrical conductivity and conﬁne the
polysulﬁde within 
the 
cathode 
rather 
than 
diffuse 
to the
electrolyte even Li anode [5–7].
Besides the intensive works on S cathode, electrolyte optimi-
zation attracted researchers’ attention in the recent years. An ideal
electrolyte for Li/S battery should have low solubility of the
polysulﬁde, high stability as contacted with metal lithium, and
being capable of forming suitable solid electrolyte interphase with
Li anode.
Ether-based electrolytes, such as DOL and DME, are the most
commonly used solvents in Li/S battery [8]. DME, a polar solvent,
though with high solubility to polysulﬁde, can facilitate the redox
reaction of S cathode [2,9]. On the other hand, DOL can improve the
interfacial property between the electrodes and electrolyte.
However, too high content of DME or DOL both cause the degraded
cycling stability [10]. Hence, a binary mixture of DOL + DME instead
of pure DOL or DME solvent is widely used in Li/S cell. Second to
this electrolyte is other liquid electrolytes based on high molecular
weight analogues of DME or CH3[OCH2CH2]nOCH3 (2  n  5), and
all compounds are also known as glymes (Gn) [11,12].
Other than ether solvent, researchers attempted to use ionic
liquids (ILs) as the solvents in Li/S cell. IL has many well-known
merits, including low volatility, low ﬂammability, high thermal
stability, and wide electrochemical potential window [13–16].
More importantly, the solubility of Li2Sx in ILs is expected to be
lower than that in organic electrolytes because of typical IL wholly
consists of charge-delocalized bulky ions [17]. Yuan et al. [18]
demonstrated the improved cycling stability of S cathode in N-
methyl-N-butyl-piperidinium 
bis(triﬂuromethanesulfonyl)imide
(PP14TFSI) 
electrolyte, 
and 
explained 
it 
by 
the 
suppressed
polysulﬁde dissolution. Park et al. quantitatively veriﬁed the very
low solubility of Li2Sx in 0.64 mol L1 LiTFSI in N,N-diethyl-N-
methyl-N-(2-methoxyethyl) ammonium bis(triﬂuoromethanesul-
fonyl)imide ([DEME][TFSI]) [16]. Wang et al. proposed a novel ionic
liquid electrolyte consisting of 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis

much more stable cycling of S/C composite in IL-based electrolyte
than in standard electrolyte [19]. Despite many successful
examples, ILs still incurred some concerns on the rate and capacity
performance due to the higher viscosity and relatively lower
conductivity [13,15], especially when pure IL was used as the
electrolyte solvent. Using hybrid solvents was proposed accord-
ingly. Shin et al. [20] used a hybrid solvents of N-Methyl-(n-butyl)
pyrrolidinium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Pyr14TFSI) and
poly(ethylene glycol) dimethyl ether (PEGDME) in Li/S cells, and
Wu et al. [21] reported another hybrid solvents with tetra(ethylene
glycol) dimethyl ether (TEGDME) and Pyr1,2O1TFSI. They both
showed increased ion conductivity and lower viscosity [21–24].
In this work, binary solvent of Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL or Pyr1,2O1TFSI/
DME was studied in detail. The inﬂuence of composition on
capacity and cycling stability was investigated. In addition, LiFSI
addition and its effect were evaluated. Based on the results, the
optimum electrolyte was proposed, in which stable cycling and
good capacity performance was achieved.
2. Experimental
2.1. Synthesis and characterization of the IL
Pyr1,2O1TFSI was synthesized following the route in reference
[25]. The structure was determined by NMR measurement (Varian
Mercury 300M). 1H NMR (CDCl3): d = 2.32 (br s, 2  2 H), 3.30 (br s,
3 H), 3.40 (s, 3 H), 3.79 (br s, 3  2 H), 3.95 ppm (s, 2 H).
According to previous study, using pure ILs as solvent usually
brought low capacity due to its high viscosity and low conductivity,
and sophisticated design of the electrolyte was necessary. 14
different electrolytes recipe were used in the study, and the
information was detailed in Table 1. Typically, the electrolyte was
1 mol kg1 LiTFSI dissolving in different solvents. For convenience,
binary solvent of Pyr1,2O1TFSI and DME (mass ratio of 9:1) was
shortened in P9-DME1, while binary solvent of Pyr1,2O1TFSI and
DOL (mass ratio of 8:2) was shortened in P8-DOL2, and so on. In
addition, the effect of LiFSI was also evaluated, and the content of
LiFSI varied from 1 wt. % to 4 wt. %. For example, P6.5-DOL3.5-
0.01LiFSI represented the electrolyte in which the mass ratio of
Pyr1,2O1TFSI: DOL was 6.5:3.5, and the content of LiFSI salt was 1 wt.
%. It has to be noted that considering the inﬂammability of the
electrolyte, the lowest IL content in the binary solvent was 50%, or
in another word, the lowest mass ratio of Pyr1,2O1TFSI: DOL or
Pyr1,2O1TFSI: DME was 5:5 [26]. The electrolyte preparation was
conducted in the Ar-ﬁlled glove box (MB10, MBRAUN, H2O < 1
ppm, O2 < 1 ppm).
2.2. Preparation of S/C composite
S/C 
composite 
was 
obtained 
through 
a 
melt-inﬁltration
method. S was mixed with conductive carbon (ECP 600, Ketjen
Black) at the weight ratio of 7:3, and then the mixture was heated
at 155 C for 10 h to ensure S inﬁltration into the pores of
conductive carbon. After being cooled to room temperature, the
powder was collected and stored in the glove box. The S content in
S/C composite was determined as 70% by TG analysis.
2.3. Cell assembly and electrochemical characterization
Sulfur electrodes were fabricated by mixing the S/C composite
with polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVdF) binder at a weight ratio of
85:15 in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) dispersant to form
homogeneous slurry. The slurry was coated on Al foil, and vacuum
dried at 60 C for 3 h. Then the electrode was punched and vacuum
dried for another 5 h at 60 C before the cell assembly. Typical
sulfur loading was 1.2 mg cm2.
CR2016-type coin cells were composed with S/C cathode, Li
anode separating by a piece of Celgard 2400 separator. Galvano-
static charge and discharge was performed on LAND cycler
(CT2001A, Land, China) in the voltage range of 1.5–3.0 V, and
the current rate was 0.1C in the ﬁrst 3 cycles and then increased to
0.2C 
in 
the 
subsequent 
cycles 
(1C = 1675 mAh g1). 
All 
the
electrochemical tests were carried out at room temperature.
2.4. SEM and UV-visible absorption spectroscopy analysis
The morphology of Li anodes after cycling test was observed on
SEM (SEM 6095, Carl Zeiss).
UV-visible absorption spectroscopy (UV-2550, Shimadzu) was
employed to study the content of species dissolved in the
electrolytes. The cells were discharged to 2.14 V at 0.01C, and
then dissembled in the glove box. The separator soaked with
electrolyte was taken out, well weighed and immersed into DOL or
DME solvent (if the binary solvent contained DOL, immersing the
separator in DOL and so on) for at least 2 h, then thus-obtained DOL
or DME solvent was subjected to UV measurement.

The electrochemical stability of Pyr1,2O1TFSI has been conﬁrmed
in previous study, and the anodic reaction cannot be initiated
below 4.5 V [13,28]. Thus, its application in Li/S cell can be
guaranteed. The cycling property of Li/S cells in Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME
solvents with different ratio are shown in Fig. 1a. Apparently,
higher DME content led to higher capacity but poorer cycling
stability. The tendency was well corresponded to previous study, in
which DME was found to offer faster reaction kinetics of
polysulﬁde but higher solubility of polysulﬁde [10]. The voltage
proﬁle in the 1st cycle was exhibited in Fig. 1b. Typical discharge
curve composing of two plateaus was observed in all the solvent.
The voltage plateau at around 2.3 V represented the transition from
S8 to long chain polysulﬁde molecules (Li2S8, Li2S6 or Li2S4), while
the one at about 2.1 V reﬂected the further reduction of Li2S4 to
Li2S2 and Li2S [29–31]. However, in pure DME solvent, very serious
shuttle appeared in the 1st charge/discharge and it led to a low
efﬁciency of 80%. Actually, from the 3rd cycle, Li/S cell almost could
not complete a whole charge/discharge cycle in DME solvent
because the shuttle was too severe and the upper voltage limit of
3 V was hardly reached. When the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio was
increased to 5/5, the efﬁciency issue was greatly alleviated, and
further increasing Pyr1,2O1TFSI content brought higher efﬁciency
until nearly 100% efﬁciency was achieved when Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME
ratio was 7/3. Along with the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio increase, the
capacity decreased gradually and the voltage gap between
charging and discharging was enlarged. When the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/
DME ratio was increased to 9/1, less than half of the theoretical
capacity could be released.
There should be some relation between Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio
and polysulﬁde dissolution. To verify this point, UV spectrum was
collected to quantitatively compare the polysulﬁde dissolution in
different solvents. In Fig. 1c, in all the electrolytes, three
absorbance peaks at around 262 nm, 270 nm and 280 nm were
found which should be referred to the long-chain polysulﬁde
species [32,33]. However, the peak intensity signiﬁcantly varied
with the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio. Highest peak intensity was
observed in pure DME solvent. Increasing the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME

the

and the polysulﬁde dissolution in pure DME was set as the
reference for comparison. When the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio
increased to 5/5, the dissolution of polysulﬁde was reduced by

Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DME ratio led to slight but continuous decline in

was obtained. Thus, the results in Fig. 1a and b can be well
explained. Incorporating Pyr1,2O1TFSI in the solvent can suppress
the dissolution of polysulﬁde, reduce the shuttle and enhance the
cycling stability. On the other hand, high Pyr1,2O1TFSI content in the

3.2. Performance of LiTFSI/Pyr1,2O1TFSI-DOL electrolyte
As being widely proved in previous study, DOL and DME plays
different role in standard DOL + DME binary solvent. The former
beneﬁts the interfacial property and the latter beneﬁts the reaction
kinetics. In the above discussion, it was demonstrated that
Pyr1,2O1TFSI-DME combination could greatly alleviate the dissolu-
tion of polysulﬁde and improve the cycling stability, however, SEM
observation indicated that Pyr1,2O1TFSI-DME binary solvent could
not sufﬁciently stop Li corrosion. So, we further investigated
Pyr1,2O1TFSI-DOL binary solvent.
The cycling property of Li/S cells in Pyr1,2O1TFSI-DOL binary
solvent with different ratio is shown in Fig. 3a. Generally,
comparing with the results in Fig. 1a, DOL-containing solvent
brings much better cycling stability of Li/S cell. For instance, in P5-
DOL5 solvent, the 100th capacity was more than 750 mAh g1,
while in P5-DME5 solvent, the 100th capacity was less than
600 mAh g1. The difference conﬁrmed the previous conclusion
that DOL could induce better interfacial property in Li/S cell and it
should be the reason for the difference in Figs.1 a and 3 a. In Fig. 3a,
we also noticed that the more IL was added, the better capacity
retention could be obtained. However, there was a tradeoff
between capacity and cycling stability. For example, in P8-DOL2
solvent, the capacity vs. cycle curve was totally ﬂat, while the poor
capacity of 450 mAh g1 was unacceptable. Fig. 3b presented the
voltage proﬁle of Li/S cell in different solvent. In pure DOL, the
highest capacity of 1207 mAh g1 could be obtained, accompany
with the lowest efﬁciency of 87%. Herein, it had to be noted that the
efﬁciency issue that we encountered in DME solvent was greatly
alleviated in DOL, and the efﬁciency of Li/S cell in pure DOL solvent
could be maintained at about 85% in the cycling test. The result
well agreed with the previous conclusion that DME could better
dissolve polysulﬁde. When half DOL was replaced by Pyr1,2O1TFSI,
the efﬁciency jumped to 97%, and the capacity slightly decreased to
1164 mAh g1. Further increasing the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL ratio led to
efﬁciency increase but accompany with capacity reduction and
enlarged polarization. Increased viscosity with Pyr1,2O1TFSI addi-
tion was the reason as being proved in the literatures [22,34].
The relationship between Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL ratio and polysul-
ﬁde dissolution was built according to the result of UV measure-
ment. In Fig. 3c, the intensity of the absorbance band representing
the long-chain polysulﬁde species kept decreasing with the
increased Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL ratio. From Fig. 3d, we could clearly
see that when the Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL ratio increased to 5/5, the
dissolution of polysulﬁde was reduced by 40% comparing with
pure DOL solvent, and higher Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL ratio led to slight
and continuous reduction in polysulﬁde dissolution.
Fig. 4 shows the SEM images of the Li anodes that have been
cycled 20 times in pure DOL and P5-DOL5 solvent. Different from in
pure DME, in DOL solvent, the cycled Li anode better reserved the
original metal morphology, and we still could ﬁnd smooth metal
surface, embedded with much moss-like accumulation. The
observation should be associated with the conclusion of DOL
favoring interfacial property. In P5-DOL5 solvent, the surface
morphology of Li anode totally changed, a compact layer covered
on Li, and no obvious accumulation could be found, indicating that
very dense SEI was formed on Li anode in P5-DOL5 solvent. The
SEM images also well evidenced the reduced polysulﬁde dissolu-
tion in Pyr1,2O1TFSI/DOL binary solvent and thus-resulted much
less Li corrosion.
Therefore, combining the results in Figs. 3 a, d and 4, we chose
P6.5-DOL3.5 solvent for further investigation as it could greatly
reduce the polysulﬁde dissolution while Li/S cell could keep a
decent capacity in it.

performance due to the viscosity increase brought by IL. Hence,

4. Conclusion
The IL-based electrolytes with DOL or DME as co-solvent were
designed, and the electrochemical performance of the Li/S cells
with these electrolytes was investigated. The Li/S cells with
electrolyte containing hybrid solvent of Pyr1,2O1TFSI and DOL
exhibited excellent cycling stability and acceptable capacity.
Further capacity improvement of Li/S cells was achieved by using
LiFSI additive in the electrolyte. The dependence of polysulﬁde
dissolution on the solvent was revealed by UV measurement.
Higher DME or DOL content and more LiFSI addition both were
proved to promote polysulﬁde dissolution. The optimum electro-
lyte composition was proposed after well balancing the viscosity,
capacity and cycling stability. The Li/S cell with LiTFSI/P6.5-
DOL3.5-0.01LiFSI electrolyte has excellent cycling stability, good
capacity performance as well as coulombic efﬁciency higher than
99%.
