A B S T R A C T
Traditional electrolytes impose tremendous limitation on the effective operation of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batte­
ries at elevated temperatures due to insufficient thermal stability and aggravated side reactions, wherein battery 
failure mechanism and electrolyte design principle remain elusive. Herein, we developed a varied-temperature 
multimodal nuclear magnetic resonance (VT-mNMR) methodology to elucidate temperature-dependent elec­
trolyte solvation structure and ion dynamics, whereby a thermotolerant bistratal solvation structure electrolyte 
(BSSE) was formulated to concurrently maintain compact inner solvation sheath and restrict polysulfide shuttling 
at high temperatures. Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy combined with X-ray photoelectron spec­
troscopy depth profiling discloses rich inorganic components in the inner layer of solid electrolyte interface. 
Consequently, Li–S batteries with BSSE can sustain stable operation with a high capacity retention of 90 % at 
60 ◦C, which also harvest a stable cycling performance under a wide temperature range within 20–80 ◦C. Our 
study provides a reliable toolbox for studying liquid electrolyte chemistry in Li–S batteries and beyond, which 
opens a new avenue for advancing extreme-temperature electrolyte design.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have stimulated a burgeoning 
research attention to serve as a promising candidate for next-generation 
energy storage system.[1–9] Although conspicuous progress has been 
achieved in improving the reversible capacity and cycling lifetime to 
date, their high-temperature (HT) performance is still far from satis­
fying.[10,11] However, in specialized application scenarios like mili­
tary, aerospace exploration, and mining, a high working temperature 
above 60 ℃ is highly requested. In this respect, interfacial degradation 
and parasitic reactions are deemed as the key concerns, which are highly 
associated with the electrolyte.[12–17] The prevailing electrolyte sys­
tem for Li–S batteries includes 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfo­
nyl) imide (LiTFSI) dissolved into a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2- 
dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent with 2 wt% LiNO3 additive.[18] 
Despite apparent advantages at room temperature, it will incur a 
multitude of challenges when operating at elevated temperatures (>
60 ◦C). To begin with, both solvents possess a low boiling point and 
hence suffer from thermal decomposition while the LiNO3 additive 
would trigger safety hazards. Besides, the construction of a desired 
anode/electrolyte interface is tough due to the fragile electrolyte 
microstructure and severe side reaction. Moreover, the dissolution and 
shuttling of polysulfide (PS) will be aggravated at elevated tempera­
tures, thereby causing rapid and irreversible capacity decay. Taken 
together, achieving steady operation of Li–S batteries above 60 ◦C re­
mains a tremendous challenge.[19].
These detrimental issues urgently necessitate the judicious adjust­
ment of traditional electrolyte recipe for achieving reliable HT Li–S

Results and Discussion
Principles and functions of VT-mNMR methodology.
To begin with, VT-mNMR methodology is subject to a comprehen­
sive clarification, which combines chemical shift, diffusion coefficient, 
and relaxation time information for shedding light upon the coordina­
tion, solvation, and motion information of ions and PSs in Li–S batteries 
(Fig. 1a). It is acceptable that 7Li and 19F are two NMR-sensitive nuclei, 
hence it would be facile to reveal the microstructure and dynamics of 
Li+/PSs and TFSI–, respectively. Furthermore, we aim to introduce the 
basic principle and key functions of NMR techniques used in this 
methodology. Chemical shift of the measured nucleus can be obtained 
from 1D NMR spectroscopy, which helps to probe the change of electron 
cloud density, further elucidating the ion coordination information in 
electrolytes (Fig. 1b, c).[25,26] Specifically, the higher the electron 
density, the more the given nucleus is shielded accompanied by an up­
field shift. Diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) follows a pulsed field 
gradient sequence, whereby the whole self-diffusivity of ions or solvents 
can be obtained (Fig. 1d, e).[27] In general, larger size and higher vis­
cosity will decrease the diffusion coefficient (D), which can investigate 
the dynamics of ion transport. T1 (spin–lattice) relaxation time repre­
senting longitudinal relaxation reflects the energy exchange rate be­
tween a specific atom and surrounding atoms, which offers a dynamic 
insight into the surrounding coordination environment (Fig. 1f, g).[28] 
In parallel, T2 (spin–spin) relaxation time is related to the transverse 
relaxation. It is well established that a smaller value of T2 stands for a 
stronger ion interaction, which is beneficial to studying the ion dy­
namics in liquid solutions. It is worth noting that the varied-temperature 
NMR tests can be synchronously carried out to study the temperature- 
dependent electrolyte solvation structure and ion dynamics for Li–S 
batteries. In conclusion, such a versatile VT-mNMR methodology can 
provide fundamental insight and invaluable guidance for rational liquid 
electrolyte design en-route toward HT Li–S batteries.
VT-mNMR analysis on Li–S batteries
With respect to liquid electrolytes, it is imperative to unravel the 
crucial roles of various components in determining the ion microstruc­
ture and dynamics. Moreover, elucidating the temperature-dependent 
evolution is of paramount significance to rationalize the electrolyte 
design for HT Li −
S batteries. Along this line, we first carried out VT- 
mNMR analysis at room temperature (298 K). Note that 1 M LiTFSI in 
DME, DOL/DME, and DOL/DME with 2 wt% LiNO3 were prepared as 
three model electrolytes (denoted as E1, E2, and E3, respectively) while 
their corresponding Li2S6 solutions with a concentration of 50 mM are 
denoted as PS/E1, PS/E2, and PS/E3, respectively. These can help us to 
investigate the dynamic information of various electrolyte species 
accompanied by their microstructure adjustment. 1D NMR spectra of 
7Li, 19F, 17O, and 1H were collected to systematically study the solvation 
structure of electrolytes prior to and after the dissolution of Li2S6.
As shown in 7Li NMR spectra of three electrolytes, an apparent peak 
can be observed at the negative ppm (Fig. 2a and Fig. S1), suggestive of 
the electron shielding effect around Li nuclei.[29] This is caused by the 
electron transfer from ethereal oxygen atoms to Li ions due to the strong 
ion–dipole interaction in electrolytes. The introduction of LiNO3 addi­
tive will enable a stronger Li+–NO3
– coordination in the inner solvation 
sheath due to higher donor number of NO3
– than that of DME, thereby 
leading to a significant downfield shift. After the dissolution of Li2S6, for 
one thing, there is still only one 7Li peak, manifesting that Li ions 
experience a similar coordination environment that are surrounded by 
DME molecules. For another, 7Li resonance exhibits a downfield shift, 
which indicates the reduced electron density on Li atoms. This is 
attributed to the high electronegativity of neighbored S atoms with an 
electron-withdrawing effect. In the case of 19F NMR spectra of TFSI–, 
there is no apparent difference of chemical shift between pure electro­
lyte and Li2S6 solution (Fig. 2b and Fig. S2). It is understandable that 
TFSI– with a relatively low donicity are hard to enter the Li-ion inner 
solvation sheath. Note that as compared to E1, the introduction of DOL 
into electrolyte leads to a shielding effect on F nuclei. In this respect, 
DOL with a relatively weak solvation effect will dominate into the Li-ion 
outer shell almost without direct coordination with Li ions, hence there 
exists a possible chemical interaction between DOL and TFSI– outside 
the central solvation structure. With respect to the 1H NMR spectra, the 
peaks assigned to DME and DOL share identical chemical shift in E2 and 
E3, which also remain unchangeable after the dissolution of Li2S6. It is 
understandable that DOL will not participate in the solvation structure 
of Li+ and PS. Although DME coordinates with Li2S6 to enable the facile 
dissolution, it will not change the electron density of H atoms on DME 
due to the low PS concentration as compared to that of Li salt. Therefore, 
most DME molecules in electrolyte coordinate with Li+ instead of Li2S6. 
However, E1 and PS/E1 exhibit an evident downfield shift as compared 
to the above counterparts, confirming that the introduction of DOL as a 
co-solvent will weaken the interaction between Li+ and DME, thus 
impeding the Li+-O electron transfer. 17O NMR of TFSI– will exhibit an 
upfield shift in the DOL-containing electrolytes as compared to that of 
E1. This further corroborates the moderate interaction between DOL and 
TFSI–, aligning with the previous 19F NMR results. Besides, such an 
upfield shift of 17O NMR can also be observed in the case of DME, which 
again confirms that the introduction of DOL can weaken the interaction 
between Li+ and DME. Moreover, 1H chemical shift of E1 becomes more 
negative after the dissolution of Li2S6, suggesting that although Li+ is 
always surrounded by the ethereal oxygen atoms to form a stable sol­
vation structure, the ion–dipole interaction between Li2S6 and DME 
molecules will become stronger without the shielding of DOL in the 
outer solvation shell.
7Li and 19F DOSY NMR tests were carried out to investigate the 
diffusion dynamics of cations and anions, respectively, which is corre­
lated with the viscosity and hydrodynamic radius (Table S1). First, it is 
intriguing that in three electrolytes, the D values of 7Li are several times 
lower than that of 19F since the radius of single Li+ is small (Fig. 2c, 
d and Fig. S3). This suggests that Li ions are always solvated by solvent 
molecules to form an ion–solvent complex while TFSI anions almost 
exist as free states. The introduction of LiNO3 greatly lowers both the 
DLi+ and DTFSI–, which is attributed to the increased viscosity and strong 
Li+–NO3
– interaction.

temperature, indicative of the inferior thermal stability of electrolyte 
solvation structure. Encouragingly, the introduction of DOL and LiNO3

weakly solvating effect and ion pair configuration are beneficial to 
achieving thermally stable electrolyte solvation structure.

Based on the above discussions, solvation structures of Li+ and PS 
along with their evolution at elevated temperatures are proposed 
(Fig. 2i, j). In general, it is well established that TFSI– will not participate 
in the Li-ion solvation structure in low-concentration DME-based elec­
trolytes. In parallel, Li+ preferentially coordinates with dual-denticity 
DME, leading to the concurrent existence of DME and NO3
−
in the 
inner solvation sheath. The strong solvation ability and chelating effect 
of DME also enable its presence in the first solvation sheath of PS 
through the binding between ethereal oxygen atoms and terminal Li 
atoms of Li2S6. DOL with a much weaker solvating ability will locate the 
outer shell of the Li2S6-DME complex and encapsulate the inner-layer 
PS. There is almost no interaction between the DOL and Li2S6 while a 
cluster is formed instead. In the meanwhile, there is a non-negligible 
interaction between DOL and TFSI–. Upon the increase of temperature, 
the solvation structure of Li ions and PS will become looser accompanied 
by the dissociation of solvent molecules. As shown in 1H–19F hetero- 
nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (HOESY) (Fig. S18), the 
normalized ratio of I(TFSI–/DOL): I(TFSI–/DME) is increased from 3.2 
(25 ◦C) to 8.8 (60 ◦C) by integrating the peak areas. Hence, the inter­
action between DOL and TFSI– will be enhanced at high temperatures, 
which further weakens the encapsulation effect of DOL toward soluble 
PS intermediates and deteriorates the “shuttling effect”.
Design and characterization of BSSE
Based on the foregoing temperature-dependent electrolyte ion dy­
namics analysis, we herein propose the electrolyte design principle for 
thermotolerant Li–S batteries. i) Substituting DOL and DME with high- 
boiling-point solvents to ensure the intrinsic thermal stability of elec­
trolyte and avoid side reactions. ii) A bistratal solvation structure is 
desired, wherein strong ion-diploe interaction is obtained in the inner 
layer to stabilize the Li-ion solvation configuration at increased tem­
peratures. In the meanwhile, outer-layer solvent with a weak solvation 
effect is requested to effectively encapsulate soluble PSs and shield their 
diffusion. iii) Rich cation–anion pairs are desired to sustain the stability 
of solvation structure and form inorganic-rich SEI at high temperatures,

properties of various solvent candidates containing boiling point, 
flashing point, dielectric constant, and viscosity were summarized in

developed to fabricate thermotolerant and longevous Li–S batteries 
(Fig. 3a), wherein DEGDEE and OTE were selected as the inner and outer 
solvent amongst multiple solvents, respectively. For one thing, tri- 
denticity DEGDEE is conducive to forming a stable Li-ion inner solva­
tion structure. Besides, it shows a similar donor number in comparison 
with that of DME, thereby enabling a strong ion–dipole interaction be­
tween Li ions and DEGDEE. Moreover, it can ensure sufficient dissoci­
ation of Li salts and hence favorable ion transport at an increased 
electrolyte concentration. The extended alkyl chain also weakens its 
affinity with polysulfides, which aids to suppress their dissolution and 
shuttling at elevated temperatures. For another, as compred to common 
TTE, OTE with more F-containing groups exhibits a stronger electron- 
withdrawing effect, which will stay away from the first solvation 
sheath of Li ions and PSs as a diluent to reduce the whole electrolyte 
viscosity. In addition, the boiling/flashing point of OTE is evidently 
higher than that of TTE, thereby enhancing the thermal stability and 
safety of the as-derived electrolyte. It can be regarded as an encapsu­
lated network to constrain the motion of PSs. At the end, localized high 
salt concentration gives rise to enhanced ion pairs, which further alle­
viates PS shuttling and affording inorganic-rich SEI.
A series of NMR tests were employed to confirm the vital merits of 
BSSE. First, as compared to ethylene glycol diethyl ether (EGDEE)-based 
BSSE with dual-denticity ethereal oxygen sites, the 7Li chemical shift of 
DEGDEE-BSSE with a unique tri-denticity coordination exhibits much 
smaller change when increasing the temperatures (Fig. 3b), suggestive 
of more stable Li-ion solvation structure.[32,33] Besides, the values of 
full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 7Li peaks are probed (Fig. 3c), 
which are related to the electrostatic attraction and paramagnetic pro­
portion of mobile Li ions. Of note, DEGDEE-BSSE shows an apparently 
lower FWHM value than that of EGDEE-BSSE at each temperature, 
accompanied by an enhanced homogeneity of NMR signal with negli­
gible value change. This manifests that our multi-denticity chelation 
strategy can improve the thermal stability of inner solvation strategy, 
which is anticipated to maintain interfacial stability and favorable ion 
transportation for HT Li–S batteries. To evidence the bistratal solvation 
structure of BSSE, 1H diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) test was 
carried out prior and after the addition of LiTFSI into the DEGDEE/OTE 
mixture.[34] With respect to the pure binary solvent, DEGDEE and OTE 
share a similar D value (3.35 × 10–10 and 3.22 × 10–10 m2 s−1, respec­
tively) while there is a great difference between them in BSSE (Fig. 3d 
and Fig. S19). Specifically, DOTE is almost double as much as DDEGDEE, 
manifesting that Li ions are prone to coordinate with DEGDEE rather 
than OTE to form the inner solvation sheath while OTE will serve as the 
outer-layer solvent without direct coordination with Li ions. Moreover, 
as compared to that of MSSE, BSSE exhibits a lower slope by fitting the 
logarithm of the reciprocal of the D values and temperature’s inverse 
(Fig. 3e and Fig. S20,21), indicative of a lower ED and hence reduced 
energy for ion transportation. Besides, the as-derived Li+ transfer 
number (t+) from the D of Li+ and TFSI– exhibit a negligible difference at 
different temperatures, verifying the high reliability of NMR method. It 
is also well acceptable that BSSE and MSSE share a similar t+, mani­
festing that OTE can reduce electrolyte viscosity and facilitate ion 
transportation whilst guaranteeing favorable transfer of Li+. Fig. 3f 
demonstrates the 7Li T1 of BSSE at varied temperatures (Fig. S22; 
Table S6), whose time scale is always lower than 1 s even at 343 K.[35] 
This suggests that there exist a multitude of ion pairs in the solvation 
structure of BSSE with the participation of anions, which would aid to 
enhance the ionic conduction and mechanical strength of as-formed SEI. 
2D 1H−1H NMR correlation spectroscopy (COSY) of BSSE was collected 
(Fig. 3g and Fig. S23), wherein there are no crossover peaks can be 
observed between DEGDEE and OTE at either 25 or 60 ◦C, further 
confirming the bistratal solvation structure of BSSE. In this respect, the 
interaction between inner-layer and out-layer solvent is negligible even 
at a fast solvent exchange rate under high temperatures, which helps to 
effectively encapsulate PSs and restrict their diffusion.
Analysis on Li metal anode at high temperatures
Li plating/stripping stability is crucial to sustaining smooth opera­
tion of HT Li–S batteries. At 1 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2, BSSE enables a 
steady cycling of Li|Li symmetric cell over 500 h with a low over­
potential of around 20 mV (Fig. 4a). In stark contrast, overpotential of 
TE is continuously increased upon the initial cycling stage, making it 
hard for the cell to maintain long-term operation. Besides, Coulombic 
efficiency (CE) is a vital descriptor to evaluate the stability of Li metal 
anode. In this respect, Li|Cu cell with BSSE at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh 
cm−2 exhibits a much higher average CE and prolonged cycling lifetime 
than that of TE (Fig. 4b), which is anticipated to sustain the long-term 
operation of HT Li–S batteries. Besides, scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) images of deposited Li metal at 60 ◦C were recorded. As shown in 
Fig. 4c and Fig. S24, there are a myriad of cracks and pores can be 
observed for TE. On the contrary, dense and flat Li metal morphology 
can be observed for BSSE, in line with the high Li plating/stripping 
stability from electrochemical tests.
To understand the interfacial chemistry of Li metal anode at high 
temperatures, cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) 
was implemented to analyze the spatial distribution and chemical 
components of SEI layer formed at 60 ◦C.[36–38] With respect to the Li 
metal plated in TE, it exhibits a dendritic morphology with messy dis­
tribution from low-magnification cryo-TEM images. Moreover, the 
thickness of SEI reaches up to 100 nm (Fig. S25), which is also loose and 
inhomogeneous. Such severe dendrite formation and low-quality SEI 
might be attributed to the severe solvent decomposition and side reac­
tion of TE at high temperatures, thereby inevitably degrading the sta­
bility of Li metal anode. In stark contrast, the SEI layer formed in the Li/ 
electrolyte interface of BSSE is tightly adhered to the surface of Li metal, 
which displays a compact and homogenous morphology (Fig. 4d and 
Fig. S26). Note that the average thickness is only ca. 10 nm. Such a 
desired SEI layer would be beneficial to elevating the reversibility of Li 
metal anode, further sustaining the smooth operation of Li–S batteries at 
high temperatures. Furthermore, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images 
along with corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis were 
collected to elucidate the chemical composition of SEI at an atomic 
scale. For one thing, in the region labelled with a pink dashed box, two 
crystal plane spacings of 2.81 Å and 2.66 Å can be observed, which 
match well with the crystalline Li2CO3 (002) and Li2O (111) planes, 
respectively, again confirming the rich inorganic components. For 
another, it is revealed that the SEI formed in BSSE displays a unique 
double-layer structure, wherein the outer layer is rich in Li2CO3 and 
Li2O is dominant in the inner layer. This is expected to simultaneously 
achieve enhanced mechanical strength and facilitated ion transport, 
thereby stabilizing the electrode/electrolyte interface at high tempera­
tures. To confirm the speculation on this SEI structure, cryo-electron 
energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was carried out from outer SEI 
throughout inner SEI to Li bulk (Fig. 4e). Notably, with respect to the Li 
K-edge, Li bulk in BSSE exhibits more metallic fine structure without the 
occurrence of the peak at around 62 eV. Meanwhile, inner SEI has more 
peaks at 70–78 eV as compared to outer SEI, indicative of rich inorganic 
components. Besides, the intensity of the peaks in the C K-edge is 
continuously decreased from the surface to the bulk, which manifests 
that only the outer SEI contains many organic components with car­
bonate groups. In parallel, inner SEI exhibits a highest peak at 545.5 eV 
ascribed to Li2O, further suggesting that it is rich in inorganic compo­
nents. Besides, the cryo-EELS elemental mapping showcases the 
concentrated distribution of Li, C, and O signals (Fig. 4f and Fig. S27), 
which can be mainly detected in the Li bulk, outer SEI, and inner SEI,

In-depth XPS profiling was performed to analyze the content and

First, it is disclosed from quantified element ratio analysis that BSSE 
possesses an evidently higher content of F, S, N in the whole SEI layer

Furthermore, as shown in high resolution XPS Li and O 1 s spectra

decomposition and low-quality SEI layer. It is therefore concluded that 
TE can hardly construct a desired Li/electrolyte interface at high tem­

Encouragingly, SEI formed in BSSE is rich in inorganic components, 
thereby sustaining superior interfacial stability during cycling process.
Analysis on electrolyte and S cathode at high temperatures
The electrolyte consumption rate at high temperatures were exam­
ined by quantitative NMR (qNMR) measurement, wherein 2,4-dichloro­
benzotrifluoride (DCBF) was employed as an internal reference for both 
1H and 19F (Fig. 5a).[42] For one thing, with respect to the remaining 
amounts of solvents and anions after 50 cycles, BSSE shows an appar­
ently higher retention for both of them (Fig. 5b). In stark contrast, the 
remaining amounts are quite low in TE especially for DOL (1.6 %) and 
TFSI– (2.4 %), which is attributed to the instable solvation structure and 
increased side reactions at an elevated temperature. For another, the 
molar ratio analysis of various components based on the integral of the 
corresponding NMR peaks also manifests that TE suffers from a faster 
consumption rate for both outer-layer solvent and anions (Fig. S31), 
which leads to severe polysulfide shuttling and hence significant 
degradation of battery cycling performance.

Electrochemical performance of HT Li–S batteries
Coin-type Li–S batteries were assembled with various electrolytes to 
probe the electrochemical performance difference at high temperatures. 
[49,50] To begin with, a series of BSSEs based on the proposed elec­
trolyte design principle were prepared via adjusting various components 
and the ratio of solvent-to-diluent (Table S8), which aims to achieve the 
optimal electrolyte recipe for longevous HT Li–S batteries. With respect 
to the cycling performance at 0.2C initiated by 3 pre-cycles at 0.1C 
(Fig. 6a and Fig. S34), it is revealed that our BSSE based on DEGDEE and 
OTE not only delivers an outstanding initial capacity but also harvests 
the highest capacity retention at 60 ◦C, which is mainly attributed to the 
superior balance between restricted polysulfide dissolution and favor­
able reaction kinetics (Fig. 6b). In this respect, inner-layer solvent 
should possess a moderate reactivity of polysulfide to enable kinetically 
favorable sulfur redox as well as an appropriate dielectric constant to 
render feasible dissociation of Li salt. In the meanwhile, low polysulfide 
reactivity and donor number are highly desired for outer-layer solvent, 
thus essentially suppressing polysulfide shuttling and irreversible ca­
pacity loss. To showcase the advantages of BSSE over MSSE and TE on

discharge/charge behavior and smooth cycling operation owing to 
kinetically favorable sulfur redox reaction. Li–S batteries were further

TE are unable to operate normally. Encouragingly, BSSE can still

stable cycling performance over 50 cycles can also be achieved at room

commercialization. We also make a systematic comparison between two 
battery systems from five crucial aspects (Fig. S39), indicating that Li–S 
batteries are a promising candidate to achieve specialized applications 
that acquire high working temperatures.
Conclusion
In summary, a VT-mNMR methodology is developed to study the 
temperature-dependent electrolyte solvation structure and ion dy­
namics, which helps to rationalize electrolyte design for HT Li–S bat­
teries. Accordingly, BSSE with a bistratal and stable solvation structure 
has been developed, which provides vital merits in maintaining inter­
facial stability and restricting polysulfide shuttling at elevated temper­
atures. Benefiting from stabilized Li metal anode and favorable sulfur 
redox reaction, Li–S batteries equipped with BSSE achieve a stable 
cycling performance at 60 ◦C. Under harsher conditions of high sulfur 
loading (4 mg cm−2) and working temperature (80 ◦C), Li–S batteries 
can still sustain a steady operation, holding tremendous potential for 
high-temperature applications. Our work opens a new avenue for 
advancing electrolyte design for HT Li–S batteries at extreme tempera­
tures, which further provides a reliable approach for studying liquid 
electrolyte chemistry.
