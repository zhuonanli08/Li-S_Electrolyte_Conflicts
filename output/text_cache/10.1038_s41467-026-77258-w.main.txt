Unlocking High-Valent Chalcogen Redox
with Halide-Rich Electrolytes for High-Energy
Lithium Batteries

Elemental chalcogens (Ch) are promising positive materials for sustainable,
high-energy lithium batteries, yet their chemistry is generally limited by the
two-electron Ch0/Ch2−conversion below 2.5 V. This leaves the high-valent
redox regime largely unexplored due to the instability of oxidized chalcogen
species. Here, we report a halide-rich electrolyte design that enables reversible
high-valent chalcogen redox in lithium batteries. By using soluble organic
halide salts with asymmetric cation, the electrolyte provides active chloride or
bromide anions to promote high-valent redox and stabilize oxidized inter-
mediates, thereby enabling a redox-amphoteric selenium (Se) conversion
pathway. This transition from reduction-only chemistry (Se2−/Se0) to three-
electron conversion (Se2−/Se0/Se+) is evidenced by a distinct plateau at ~2.6 V,
corresponding to the Se0/Se+ process. Consequently, Li | |Se cell achieves a
reversible discharge capacity of 980 mAh g−1 and a speciﬁc energy of 2003 Wh
kgSe
−1 with stable cycling performance over 200 cycles at 400 mA g−1. This
strategy is further extended to sulfur and selenium sulﬁde materials, activating
high-valent conversion. These results establish a potentially general route to
access high-valent, multi-electron chalcogen chemistry, broadening the
energy density limits for next-generation batteries.
Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries remain the cornerstone of elec-
triﬁed transportation and large-scale energy storage, yet the energy
density of state-of-the-art intercalation-based positive electrodes is
increasingly constrained by the single-electron redox limits of transi-
tion metals and the rigid structural requirements of host lattices1–3.
Lithium-sulfur (Li | |S) and lithium-selenium (Li | |Se) conversion che-
mistries offer a compelling alternative, boasting high theoretical
capacities (1675 mAh g−1 for S and 679 mAh g−1 for Se) by utilizing earth-
abundant active materials3–5. Compared with S, Se possesses orders-of-
magnitude higher electronic conductivity (Se: 1 × 10−5 S cm−1; S: 5 × 10−30
S cm−1), which intrinsically facilitates high-rate operation and active-
material utilization. Despite these advantages, practical Li | |Se chem-
istry has remained largely conﬁned to the classical two-electron Se0/
Se2−conversion. Challenges such as polyselenide dissolution and
shuttle, slow heterogeneous phase evolution, and unstable electrode/
electrolyte interphases collectively prevent even this two-electron
limiting from being fully realized6,7. A transformative strategy to break
this ceiling is to activate additional Faradaic processes of Se at higher
potentials, thereby accessing previously untapped high-valent redox
states and enabling genuinely multi-electron storage within the same
elemental positive electrode.
Realizing such high-valent redox chemistry is fundamentally a
challenge of simultaneous activation and stabilization8. The oxidation
of Se0 to Sen+ (n = 1, 2, or 4) typically yields electrophilic selenium
halides (e.g., Se2Cl2, SeCl2, SeCl4) or complexed cationic species that
are prone to hydrolysis and irreversible side reactions in conventional

electrolyte, have demonstrated the potential of high-valent S0/S4+ (S/
SCl4) chemistry19. However, the reliance on aluminum chloride (AlCl3)/
SOCl2 raises practical concerns regarding corrosivity and handling.
Another approach involves the direct introduction of lithium bromide
(LiBr) powder into the sulfur positive electrode13. Yet, embedding
electronically insulating LiBr signiﬁcantly reduces overall electronic
conductivity and limits the high-rate performance of the Li | |S/LiBr
cell, as the S/LiBr reaction proceeds via a solid-solid pathway with a
large kinetic barrier. These limitations highlight an urgent need for a
rational electrolyte design that provides high, mobile halide activity to
activate and stabilize the high-valent Se0/Se+ redox couple.
Here, we address this challenge by designing a nonaqueous
lithium electrolyte incorporating organic halide salts with asymmetric
cation
architectures
(Tributylammonium
chloride,
TBMACl;
Tributylmethylammonium bromide, TBMABr). This electrolyte design
delivers high halide solubility and effective halide activity, unlocking a
reversible high-valent Se0/Se+ conversion in a nonaqueous lithium
system, featuring a distinct ~2.6 V plateau. A redox-amphoteric Se
conversion is thus achieved, transitioning from a conventional
reduction-only mechanism to a three-electron Se2−/Se0/Se+ pathway in
Li | |Se cells. Comprehensive analysis, combined with ex situ and in situ
spectroscopic characterizations, reveals the formation of Se2Cl2 or
Se2Br2 at high voltages and their reversible conversion. As a result, the
optimized Li | |Se cells deliver a reversible discharge capacity of
980 mAh g−1, retaining a reversible capacity of 633 mAh g−1 after 200
cycles. Importantly, this electrolyte concept generalizes to activate
high-valent reactions in Li | |S and Li | |SeS2, delivering pronounced
gains in discharge capacity.
Results
Reversible redox-amphoteric Se conversion chemistry
Se is uniquely positioned to support ambipolar redox chemistry
(Fig. 1a). On the one hand, its strong electron afﬁnity (ﬁrst electron
afﬁnity, −195 kJ mol−1) enables efﬁcient uptake of electrons and storage
of metal cations (Li, Na, K, Mg) through conversion to metal
selenides20–22. On the other hand, Se also exhibits a comparatively low
ionization energy (ﬁrst ionization energy, 940.9 kJ mol−1), implying
that oxidation to high-valent cationic selenium species (Se+, Se2+, Se4+)
is accessible if the electrolyte can effectively activate and stabilize the
oxidized states. When an organic electrolyte simultaneously supplies
electrochemically active Li+ and coordinating halide anions (Cl−/Br−),
Se can therefore be driven into a redox-amphoteric conversion mani-
fold: during discharge, Se is reduced by two electrons and couples with
Li+ to form Li2Se; during charge, Se is oxidized to high-valent states
that are stabilized by halides, resulting in the formation of cationic
selenium halides (e.g., Se2Cl2/Se2Br2) within a deﬁned potential win-
dow. The optimized molecular structures and electrostatic potential
(ESP) mapping of Se8, Se2Cl2, and Se2Br2 (Supplementary Fig. 1) indi-
cate that halogenation disrupts the Se8 ring framework. In the resulting

more electronegative halogen atoms exhibit localized electron den-
sity; notably, the greater polarization observed in Se2Cl2 suggests that
Cl−is a more effective charge-compensating/coordination anion for
activating the Se0/Se+ process. Importantly, combining the conven-
tional reductive Se0/Se2−conversion with an oxidative Se0/Se+ reaction
is expected to increase the theoretical speciﬁc capacity by ~50% rela-
tive to the two-electron Se0/Se2−chemistry (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the
elevated potential ( ~ 2.6 V) of the Se0/Se+ reaction signiﬁcantly
enhances the theoretical energy density by >50%.
To evaluate this three-electron Se2−/Se0/Se+ conversion chemistry,
we assembled Li | |Se cells using a lithium metal negative electrode and
a Se/Ketjen black composite positive electrode (denoted as Se/KJ). The
cells were operated in a conventional electrolyte (1 M lithium bis(tri-
ﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a Dimethoxyethane/Dioxo-
lane (DME/DOL) mixture (1:1, v/v), with 5 wt.% lithium nitrate (LiNO3)
added as an additive, denoted as Baseline electrolyte) and in a
designed chloride-containing electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI + 1 M lithium
bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) + 0.5 M LiNO3 + 1 M TBMACl in 1,3-
Dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI), denoted as Designed-Cl electro-
lyte). The Designed-Cl electrolyte exhibits good compatibility with
Se2Cl2, the charged product formed during high-valent Se redox
(Supplementary Fig. 2). The highly porous, conductive KJ framework is
expected to enhance electronic percolation within the Se/KJ electrode
and physically conﬁne both charged products (Se2Cl2) and discharge
intermediates (lithium polyselenides). This conﬁnement strategy
effectively mitigates active-material migration to the Li electrode,
thereby suppressing the shuttle effect and minimizing capacity loss.
Structural characterization conﬁrms effective Se conﬁnement: after
mechanical ball milling, the characteristic Se diffraction peaks remain
visible but undergo obvious broadening (Supplementary Fig. 3a),
indicative of a reduction in Se domain size. After thermal treatment at
260 °C, Se reﬂections are barely detectable in the Se/KJ composite,
indicating that Se successfully inﬁltrated into the KJ pore network.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further corroborates this infer-
ence: pristine Se consists of aggregated ~1 μm particles (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 3b), whereas no exposed Se particles are observed in Se/KJ
(Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). These observations are consistent with
efﬁcient encapsulation of Se within the porous carbon matrix. Addi-
tional Se2Cl2 adsorption tests (Supplementary Fig. 4), post-cycling
SEM/energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of the Se/KJ
electrode (Supplementary Fig. 5), and SEM/EDS/X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) characterization of the Li electrode (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 6, 7) further demonstrate that the synergistic combination of
the KJ host and tailored electrolyte formulation effectively suppresses
active-species diffusion and shuttle effect, thereby mitigating potential
corrosion or passivation of the Li negative electrode.
The electrochemical activation of high-valent states was investi-
gated via a charge-ﬁrst protocol, which provides direct evidence for
electrolyte-dependent accessibility of the oxidative Se0/Se+ reaction.
When charged from the initial state (Supplementary Fig. 8), the Li | |Se
cell in Baseline electrolyte exhibits no capacity. In stark contrast, the
cell in Designed-Cl electrolyte delivers a clear charge capacity of
180 mAh g−1, demonstrating that the Se0/Se+ oxidation pathway is
uniquely activated by the high chloride activity of the designed elec-
trolyte. The corresponding differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis
(Supplementary Fig. 8d) assigns this oxidation to a distinct process at
2.61 V. Upon the subsequent ﬁrst discharge, the Li | |Se cells in the
Baseline and Designed-Cl electrolytes delivered speciﬁc capacities of
591 and 1050 mAh g−1, respectively, corresponding to gravimetric
speciﬁc energies of 1218 and 2003 Wh kgSe
−1 based on Se mass, or 584.6
and 961.4 Wh kgelectrode
−1 based on the total positive electrode mass,
respectively; thus, Li | |Se cell in Designed-Cl electrolyte increases dis-
charge capacity and speciﬁc energy by 77% and 64%. The carbon cloth
(CC) current collector contributes negligible additional capacity
(Supplementary Fig. 9). The advantage persists upon cycling: in the
2nd/3rd cycles, Li | |Se cell in Baseline electrolyte delivers discharge
capacities of 503/482 mAh g−1, whereas Li | |Se cell in Designed-Cl
electrolyte maintains capacities of 980/958 mAh g−1, indicating mark-
edly improved reversibility and stability in the halide-containing elec-
trolyte. The 2nd-cycle voltage proﬁle in Designed-Cl electrolyte
(Fig. 1c) exhibits high Coulombic efﬁciency, consistent with a highly
reversible three-electron conversion process. Moreover, the charging
dQ/dV curve (Fig. 1d) displays well-resolved oxidation features at
2.10 V, 2.24 V, and 2.63 V: the lower-potential peaks (2.10 and 2.24 V)
are attributed to the Se2−/Se0 conversion, while the high-potential peak
at 2.63 V corresponds to the Se0/Se+ process. These distinct electro-
chemical signatures corroborate the successful activation of a three-
electron Se2−/Se0/Se+ reaction manifold, fundamentally enabled by the
halide-anion chemistry of the designed electrolyte. The three-
electrode Li | |Se measurements (Supplementary Fig. 10) further con-
ﬁrm the reversible three-electron Se2−/Se0/Se+ conversion.
Mechanistic origin of multivalent Se conversion
The conversion mechanism of the Se electrode was elucidated using
various characterization techniques. To investigate the structural
evolution during electrochemical cycling, in situ Raman spectroscopy
was performed utilizing a customized cell conﬁguration as shown in
Supplementary Fig. 11. Figure 2a–c present the galvanostatic charge-
discharge proﬁle of the Li | |Se cell at 400 mA g−1 and the correspond-
ing Raman spectra, respectively. Upon discharge, the Raman band at
233 cm−1, characteristic of Se−Se vibrations in elemental Se, progres-
sively attenuates and becomes undetectable by 2.0 V, indicating
depletion of the Se electrode. Further discharge to 1.7 V gives rise to an
additional feature at 250 cm−1, which we assign to Li−Se interactions on
the basis of reference spectra of Se and Li2Se powders (Supplementary
Fig. 12). Notably, the apparent “silent” region between 2.0 and 1.7 V is
consistent with the formation of soluble polyselenide intermediates
during discharge. Dissolution of these transient species into the elec-
trolyte reduces their local concentration at the interrogated electrode
surface and consequently suppresses their Raman detectability. At full
discharge to 1.5 V, the associated band of Li−Se reaches maximal
intensity, consistent with accumulation of Li2Se as the dominant dis-
charged phase.
During the subsequent charge, the Raman evolution reverses,
reﬂecting re-oxidation of reduced selenium species. The Li−Se feature
gradually diminishes, while the Se−Se vibration recovers. At 2.4 V
(Fig. 2c), the spectrum is dominated by the Se−Se signature attribu-
table to Se8 structure. Upon further charging, the Se−Se band under-
goes a discernible red shift from 233 to 231 cm−1, and an additional
band emerges at 326 cm−1, which is characteristic of Se−Cl bonding and
consistent with the reference Raman spectrum of Se2Cl2 dissolved in
the Designed-Cl electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 13). The red shift of
the Se−Se vibration is consistent with a shortened Se−Se bond in Se2Cl2
relative to Se8 (Se−Se in Se8: 2.36 Å; Se−Se in Se2Cl2: 2.23 Å; Supple-
mentary Fig. 1), supporting assignment of the charged-phase species
to selenium chlorides. The concomitant appearance of the Se−Cl mode
and the Se−Se red shift thus provides direct spectroscopic evidence
for electrolyte-enabled formation of Se2Cl2 at the positive electrode
upon charging. Importantly, the presence of these bands indicates that
Se2Cl2 remains stabilized within the electrode. The ultraviolet-visible
(UV-vis) spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 14) of the solution obtained by
immersing the fully charged electrode in acetonitrile further shows a
characteristic absorption peak of Se2Cl2 at 301 nm23, conﬁrming the
formation of Se2Cl2 in the charged state. On the subsequent discharge,
the Raman signatures evolve in a stepwise manner: the Se−Cl band
disappears concomitantly with a blue shift of the Se−Se vibration
(consistent with reduction of Se2Cl2), followed by re-emergence of the
Se−Se band associated with Se8, and ultimately replacement by the Li
−Se feature as discharge proceeds. This stepwise spectral progression

To further examine the chemical states of the selenium species,
XPS was performed on Se electrodes collected at representative states
of charge (Fig. 2d). To minimize artefacts arising from air/moisture
sensitivity of the electrochemically generated selenium species, all
electrodes were sealed in the glovebox and transferred to the XPS
chamber using an inert-atmosphere transfer holder. The pristine Se
electrode exhibits a characteristic Se 3 d spin-orbit doublet corre-
sponding to elemental Se (Se0), with the Se 3d5/2 and Se 3d3/2 com-
ponents located at 55.7 eV and 56.6 eV, respectively. After full
discharge to 1.5 V, the Se⁰ doublet vanishes and is replaced by an
additional Se 3 d doublet at 55.3 eV (3d5/2) and 56.1 eV (3d3/2), con-
sistent with the −2 valence position of Se and therefore with the for-
mation of Li2Se as the discharged product. Upon charging to 3.3 V, the
Se 3 d features shift to 55.1 eV and 55.9 eV (aligned with the Se+ state)24.
To support this assignment, a reference spectrum was obtained from a
synthesized Se2Cl2 drop-cast onto carbon cloth. The Se 3 d doublet of
the fully charged electrode coincides quantitatively with that of the
Se2Cl2 reference, providing strong evidence forthe formation of Se2Cl2
during the charging process. Notably, no residual Se0 contribution is
detectable in the charged electrode, indicating that selenium under-
goes essentially complete oxidation under the applied charging con-
ditions. Furthermore, XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 15) of the Se
electrodes collected at the charged states after the 5th and 10th cycles
consistently shows clear Se+ signals, further conﬁrming the highly
reversible and sustained formation of high-valent Se species over
extended cycling.
Synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was
employed to track the evolution of the local chemical environment of
Se in the Se/KJ electrode during electrochemical cycling. For com-
parison and spectral assignment, reference spectra of elemental Se,
Na2Se, and Se2Cl2 were collected under identical conditions. In the Se
K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) proﬁles recor-
ded during the ﬁrst discharge (Fig. 3a), the absorption edge exhibits a
progressive blue shift as the cell voltage decreases, reaching its
maximum shift at 1.5 V. At full discharge, the edge position nearly
coincides with that of the Na2Se reference, indicating the formation of
Se2−species and the generation of Li2Se as the discharge product.
During the subsequent charge (Fig. 3b), the Se K-edge exhibits a non-
monotonic evolution: it ﬁrst red-shifts from 1.5 to 2.4 V and then blue-
shifts from 2.4 to 3.3 V. At 2.4 V, the edge position overlaps with that of
the Se reference, consistent with regeneration of Se0. Upon further
charging to 3.3 V, the edge position becomes indistinguishable from
that of the Se2Cl2 reference, identifying Se2Cl2 as the charged product
and thereby supporting a stepwise Se2−/Se0/Se+ reaction sequence
during charge. The apparent blue-shift accompanying Se0/Se+ oxida-
tion is associated with the distinct bonding and electronic structure of
Se2Cl2, and is consistent with the XPS observation that the Se0/Se+
process is accompanied by a shift of Se 3 d binding energies to lower
values (Fig. 2d).
To gain deeper insights into the local structural evolution,
extended X-ray absorption ﬁne structure (EXAFS) was analyzed for the
pristine state (Fig. 3d), fully discharged (1.5 V; Fig. 3e) and fully charged
(3.3 V; Fig. 3f) states. To disentangle backscattering contributions, we
performed wavelet transform (WT) analysis on the k3-weighted EXAFS
data, visualized as two-dimensional intensity maps in R and k space.
The pristine Se electrode shows a dominant WT maximum at
R = 2.10 Å, corresponding to Se−Se scattering. At 1.5 V, an additional
WT feature emerges at R = 2.16 Å, consistent with Li−Se scattering and
supporting the formation of Li2Se. At 3.3 V, the WT maximum shifts to
R = 1.97 Å, which we assign to Se−Cl scattering in Se2Cl2. This is con-
sistent with the reﬁned structural parameters (Supplementary Fig. 1)
showing that the Se−Cl bonds in Se2Cl2 is shorter than the Se−Se bonds
in Se8.

Design of non-aqueous electrolyte
The oxidation of elemental Se is kinetically demanding. Consequently,
accessing the Se0/Se+ reaction requires an electrolyte that provides
high-activity, coordinatively competent charge-compensating anions
within a sufﬁciently wide stability window. Anion screening is primarily
governed by nucleophilicity and ionic size (Fig. 4a): more nucleophilic,
smaller anions are capable of more effectively stabilizing the highly
electrophilic Se⁺-based intermediates through stronger ion pairing/
coordination25,26.
Consequently,
conventional
anions
(bis(tri-
ﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(TFSI−),
triﬂuoromethanesulfonate
(OTf−), hexaﬂuorophosphate (PF6
−)) are generally unsuitable because
they are bulky and weakly nucleophilic. In contrast, halides (Cl−, Br−)
possess the requisite combination of compact size and higher
nucleophilicity for effective coordination. Activating the high-valent I0/
I+ redox chemistry of I2 by introducing Cl⁻ or Br−into the electrolyte
has been widely reported in aqueous Zn batteries27–31, translating this
strategy to non-aqueous media remains a challenge. This is primarily
due to the very limited solubility of conventional inorganic halides
(LiCl, LiBr) in organic carbonates or ethers, which prevents maintain-
ing high concentrations and high activity of free Cl−/Br−in the elec-
trolyte. We address this by using organic halide salts, whose cation
structures tune ion pairing and dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 16). In
particular, quaternary ammonium halides with asymmetric cations

TBMA+;

raethylammonium,
TEA+;
Tetrapropylammonium,

butylammonium, TBA+) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 17). This lower

Solvent selection is equally critical for achieving both high salt
solubility and controlled intermediate transport32–34. Compared with
ether solvents (DME, Diglyme (DEGDME), DOL), DMI has a high
dielectric constant (ε = 37.6), which promotes dissolution of organic
halide salts and ion separation, while its moderate viscosity can sup-
press dissolution and long-range diffusion of oxidized products
(Se2Cl2) and lithium polyselenide intermediates, mitigating shuttle
effect and capacity loss. The molecular structure and electrostatic
potential distribution of DMI are shown in Fig. 4d. Solubility mea-
surements of a series of organic chloride and bromide salts in DME and
DMI (Supplementary Fig. 18) conﬁrm this design rationale: owing to
their low dissociation energies combined with the strong solvating
capability of DMI, TBMACl and TBMABr reach solubilities exceeding
3 M in DMI, providing a practical route to halide-rich organic electro-
lytes suitable for unlocking high-valent Se+/Se0 chemistry.
The solvation structures of the designed halide-containing elec-
trolytes were elucidated through an integrated approach combining
Raman spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectro-
scopy and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Raman spectroscopy
(Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Figs. 19–21) provides a direct probe into
the local coordination environment of DMI solvent. In neat DMI, the
symmetric C = O stretching band is centered at 1690 cm−1. Upon
addition of 1 M LiTFSI or 1 M LiFSI, this vibration upshifts to 1696 cm−1,
indicative of a strong Li+−O(C = O) coordination35. By contrast, adding
1 M TBMACl to DMI produces only a modest shift of the C = O band to
1692 cm−1, indicative of comparatively weak TBMA+−solvent interac-
tions. Notably, the Raman spectrum of TBMACl/DMI exhibits no
additional discernible bands beyond those of DMI in the probed
region, suggesting that the organic halide salt does not introduce
prominent additional vibrational features and that its primary role is to
modulate ionic speciation rather than solvent structure. In compar-
ison, LiTFSI/DMI and LiFSI/DMI solutions display characteristic anion
bands associated with Li+ coordination: a feature at 740 cm−1 attribu-
table to Li+-coordinated TFSI−and a feature at 720 cm−1 attributable to
Li⁺-coordinated FSI−, respectively35,36. In both solutions, a signal near
778 cm−1 is observed, which can be assigned to the S −N −S vibration
common to TFSI⁻ and FSI⁻. In the fully formulated Designed-Cl elec-
trolyte, the DMI carbonyl band shifts further to a higher wavenumber,
indicating a stronger perturbation of the C = O group.
1H NMR (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 22, 23) provides com-
plementary evidence for Li+−solvent coordination. Upon adding LiTFSI
or LiFSI to DMI, the resonances of the −CH2−and −CH3 protons in DMI
shift upﬁeld, indicating increased local electronic shielding. This trend
is consistent with the coordination of the carbonyl (C = O) group in
DMI to Li+, which redistributes electron density within the DMI fra-
mework. The upﬁeld shifts become more pronounced in the higher-
salt-concentration Designed-Cl and Designed-Br electrolytes, con-
sistent with strengthened Li+−DMI interactions. In addition, clear
halogen signals are detected in the 35Cl NMR spectrum (Fig. 4h) of the
Designed-Cl electrolyte and in the 81Br NMR spectrum (Supplementary
Fig. 23) of the Designed-Br electrolyte, supporting the presence of
highly available halide species and thus high halide activity in these
electrolytes.

Electrochemical performance of the Li | |Se cells
The electrochemical performance of Li | |Se cells was evaluated using
the Designed-Cl electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves recorded
at 0.1 mV s−1 (Fig. 5a) exhibit two well-deﬁned oxidation peaks centered
at 2.20 V and 2.63 V, which are assigned to the Se2⁻/Se0 and Se0/Se+
processes, respectively. Notably, the peak positions remain essentially
unchanged over the ﬁrst three cycles, indicating good electrochemical
reversibility in this electrolyte environment. As the scan rate is
increased from 0.2 to 1.0 mV s−1 (Fig. 5b), the current associated with
the Se0/Se+ couple increases markedly, suggesting that the high-
potential Se0/Se+ conversion becomes increasingly dominant under
rapid polarization. Moreover, the log(i)-log(v) analysis (Supplementary
Fig. 27) yields a slope of 0.57 for the Se0/Se+ peak, implying that the
process is primarily diffusion-controlled rather than capacitive. Gal-
vanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) measurement further resolves
the potential response of the Se electrode (Fig. 5c and Supplementary
Fig. 28). The linear relationship between potential and concentration
during the GITT measurements supports the validity of the calculated
diffusion coefﬁcients. Compared with the low ion diffusivity ( ~ 10−12
cm2 s−1) in the Se0/Se2−plateau region, a much higher diffusivity ( ~ 10−9
cm2 s−1) is achieved in the Se+/Se0 plateau region. Such a high diffusion
coefﬁcient suggests that selenium oxidation in our Li | |Se system
proceeds with markedly faster kinetic characteristics than the sele-
nium reduction process in conventional Li | |Se system.
Galvanostatic charge-discharge proﬁles (Fig. 5d) acquired at
400 mA g−1 overlap well from the 2nd to the 5th cycle, conﬁrming
stable cycling behavior under practical speciﬁc currents. The corre-
sponding dQ/dV curves (Supplementary Fig. 29) display peak pairs at
2.66/2.43 V and 2.16/1.82 V, assigned to the Se0/Se+ and Se2⁻/Se0 redox
couples, respectively. The smaller hysteresis for Se0/Se+ couple relative
to Se2⁻/Se0 further supports faster kinetics in the halide-mediated high-
voltage conversion, consistent with the high diffusivity of Cl⁻ in the
electrolyte. Cycling performance (Fig. 5e) at 400 mA g−1 shows that the
cell retains a reversible capacity of 633 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles, cor-
responding to a capacity retention of 62.2%, which is comparable to
most reported conversion-type positive electrodes (Supplementary
Fig. 30). The initial capacity decrease followed by recovery is likely
associated with the gradual electrochemical activation of the Se
cathode during cycling, which improves the utilization of active Se
material. In self-discharge tests (Supplementary Fig. 31), the Li | |Se cell
using the Designed-Cl electrolyte retained 92.66% of its initial

Discussion
In summary, we report an electrolyte-mediated route to unlock high-
valent chalcogen redox chemistry in nonaqueous lithium batteries. By
rationally designing halide-rich (Cl−or Br−) non-aqueous electrolytes,
we enable a three-electron redox-amphoteric Se chemistry in Li | |Se
cells, accessing a reversible Se2−/Se0/Se+ conversion that transcends
the conventional two-electron Se0/Se2−limit. As a result, the optimized
Li | |Se cells deliver a reversible discharge capacity of 980 mAh g−1 and a
gravimetric speciﬁc energy of 2003 Wh kgSe
−1. These metrics represent
a 77% increase in capacity and a 64% increase in speciﬁc energy relative
to the baseline electrolyte. The Se2−/Se0/Se+ reaction mechanism is
supported by in situ Raman spectroscopy in conjunction with ex situ
XPS and XAS, which collectively conﬁrm the formation and reversible
interconversion of high-valent selenium species (Se2Cl2 or Se2Br2). The
selection of halide salts with an asymmetric organic cation simulta-
neously ensures high solubility and a high activity of halide anions.
These halide anions are essential not only for activating the Se0/Se+
oxidation process but also for stabilizing oxidized Se+ products,
enabling reversible cycling. Furthermore, this electrolyte-enabled
strategy can be extended to other chalcogen positive electrodes.
When applied to Li | |S and Li | |SeS2 cells, the designed electrolyte
activates analogous high-valent reactions of S and SeS2, leading to
increased discharge capacity. Overall, this electrolyte strategy pro-
vides a potential approach for accessing high-valent chalcogen redox
chemistry and improving the energy density of lithium batteries.

Preparation of Se/KJ, Se/CMK3, S/KJ, and SeS2/KJ materials
Se/KJ and Se/CMK3 materials: Elemental Se and carbon materials (KJ or
CMK3) were mixed at a mass ratio of 3:2 by mechanical ball milling at
500 rpm for 5 hours using a planetary ball mill (MITR, YXQM-2L). The
milling was conducted in a 50 mL stainless-steel jar with stainless-steel
balls of 5 mm diameter. The total mass of the starting materials was 2 g,
and the ball-to-powder mass ratio was 40:1. The resulting Se+KJ or Se
+CMK3 composite was then transferred to an autoclave and heat-
treated at 260 °C for 15 h. Unless otherwise speciﬁed, all Li | |Se cells in
this work employed Se/KJ as the positive material.
S/KJ material: Elemental S and KJ were mixed at a mass ratio of 3:2
by mechanical ball milling at 500 rpm for 5 h under the same milling
conditions described above. The resulting S + KJ composite was then
transferred to an autoclave and heat-treated at 155 °C for 15 h.
SeS2/KJ material: Elemental SeS2 and KJ were mixed at a mass ratio
of 3:2 by mechanical ball milling at 500 rpm for 5 hours under the same
milling conditions described above. The resulting SeS2 + KJ composite
was then transferred to an autoclave and heat-treated at 250 °C
for 12 h.
Preparation of Se2Cl2
Typically, 0.1 mol of SeO2 (99%, Aladdin) was placed in a ﬂask and
dissolved in 50 mL of concentrated HCl (37%, Anaqua). Subsequently,
0.3 mol of Se was added, followed by the dropwise addition of con-
centrated H2SO4 (98%, Anaqua) until the evolution of HCl gas ceased.
After cooling, the red product Se2Cl2 was separated using a separatory
funnel and washed several times with concentrated H2SO4. Residual
H2SO4 in the product was removed by treatment with anhydrous BaCl2
(99.9%, Aladdin), after which the solid was ﬁltered.
Preparation of electrolytes
All the electrolytes were prepared in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox ( < 1 ppm of
water and O2). The baseline electrolyte consisted of 1 M LiTFSI dis-
solved in a DME/DOL mixture (1:1, v/v), with 5 wt.% LiNO3 added as an
additive. The designed halogen-rich electrolyte comprised 1 M LiTFSI,
1 M LiFSI, and 0.5 M LiNO3 dissolved in DMI, with the addition of either
1 M TBMACl or 1 M TBMABr.

Electrochemical measurements
A slurry composed of active materials, Super P, and PVDF binder in a
mass ratio of 8:1:1, mixed in NMP, was used to prepare the positive
electrodes. The solvent-to-solid mass ratio in the slurry was 3:1. The
slurry was homogenized using a centrifugal stirring defoaming
machine (TMAX-XXH-1500S) at 600 rpm for 4 h and then coated on
carbon cloth (W0S1011, CeTech, thickness: 0.35 mm) using a doctor
blade (MTI Corporation), followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 80 °C
for 12 hours. Prior to electrode fabrication, the carbon cloth was ren-
dered hydrophilic by treatment with concentrated nitric acid (Oriental
Chemicals & Lab Supplies Ltd.) at 80 °C for 12 h. The obtained single-
sided-coated positive electrodes (thickness: ~0.35 mm) were punched
into round pellets with a diameter of 9 mm using a manual hole punch
(MTI Corporation). The mass loading of Se, S, and SeS2 materials on
carbon cloth was around ~2.0 mg cm–2. The chalcogenide constituted
48 wt.% of the electrode material.
Coin-2032 cells were assembled for electrochemical measure-
ments. The coin-cell cases, spacers, and springs were made of 316 L
stainless steel. A single-sided, coated positive electrode with a dia-
meter of 9 mm was used. A Li foil (100 μm in thickness, 14 mm in
diameter) was used as the negative electrode, and a glass ﬁber ﬁlm
(Whatman GF/A, 1.6 μm pore size, 260 μm in thickness, 16 mm in dia-
meter) was used as the separator. The Li foil was used directly without
any surface cleaning, polishing, or other pretreatment before cell
assembly. The amount of electrolyte for each cell is 80 μL. For the
three-electrode measurements, two Li foils were used as the reference
electrode and counter electrode, respectively. CV curves (0.2 mV s−1,
1.5–3.3 V) and LSV (1.0 mV s−1, from open circuit potential to 5.0 V) were

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed with the

using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional under the gen-
eralized gradient approximation (GGA) framework42–44. Ultrasoft
pseudopotentials were adopted, and the kinetic energy cutoff for the
plane-wave basis set was set to 380 eV. Structural relaxation was con-
ducted with the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) optimi-
zation

5 × 10−5 eV per atom. The solvation effect has not been considered for
DFT calculations since the adsorption behaviors of different ions in the
solution have not been investigated in this work. The geometry opti-

the ESP and the dissociation energy.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation
For the MD simulations, we have constructed the electrolyte models in
a 43 × 43 × 43 Å box, which consists of 32 TBMACl, 16 LiNO3, 32 LiTFSI,
and 32 LiFSI in the ionic condition with 296 DMI atoms. All ions and
solvent molecules are randomly distributed in the simulated box. The
MD simulations are performed by the Forcite package with the COM-
PASSII force ﬁeld applied for all elements. The cut-off distance of
nonbonded interactions was 12.5 Å. The long-range electrostatic and
van der Waals interactions are calculated by the Ewald method and the
atom-based method, respectively. Before MD simulations, the initial
electrolyte structures were pre-equilibrated via geometry optimiza-
tions and annealing to release excessive strain. For the annealing
process, the system was equilibrated under the NVT ensemble by
heating from 300 K to 500 K and maintaining 500 K for 500 ps, fol-
lowed by gradual cooling back to 300 K. There will be a total of 10
heating ramps per cycle with 200 dynamic steps for each cycle. After
each cycle, we will perform a geometry optimization. For the MD
simulations, we apply the NPT ensemble under 298 K and 1 atm for 2 ns
with each step of 1 fs. The Nosé-Hoover thermostat has been applied
for the temperature control during MD simulations. We adopted the
NPT ensemble instead of NVT because the density of this highly con-
centrated multi-salt electrolyte cannot be reliably predeﬁned. The NPT
ensemble allows the system volume to self-adjust to an equilibrium
density under realistic temperature and pressure conditions, ensuring
more reliable radial distribution functions, coordination numbers, and
diffusion coefﬁcients.

density. The coordination number (CN) is directly derived from the
RDF and represents the cumulative number of neighboring particles
within a given cutoff distance. Mathematically, the coordination
number is obtained by integrating the RDF according to:
