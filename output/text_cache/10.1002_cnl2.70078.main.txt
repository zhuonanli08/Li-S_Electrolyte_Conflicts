ABSTRACT
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries face significant commercialization hurdles, predominantly due to challenges in promoting
sulfur conversion reactions and simultaneously stabilizing cathode/electrolyte/anode interfaces. To tackle these issues,
Fluoroisatin (FRN), as an electrolyte additive, was introduced in Li–S batteries, which dissolves in the electrolyte, undergoes
partial deprotonation, and reacts with LiTFSI/lithium polysulfides. Consequently, it can regulate sulfur conversion pathways,
accelerate reaction kinetics, and construct a LiF‐rich solid‐state electrolyte interface. The Li–S battery, with merely 0.5 wt% FRN
additive and operating at −20°C, shows a high initial discharge capacity of 912 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, and it is maintained at
830 mAh g−1 after 120 cycles. The potential of FRN, as a versatile electrolyte modifier, has potential for using in high‐
performance Li–S batteries.

Introduction
Among the emerging energy technologies, lithium–sulfur (Li–S)
batteries stand out due to their exceptional theoretical specific
energy capacity, environmentally friendly nature, and economic
viability [1–5]. Despite decades of research, their commercial
applications remain a formidable challenge, with limitations
such as lithium polysulfide (LiPSs) intermediate shuttle effects,
parasitic chemical/electrochemical reactions, poor sulfur con-
ductivity, and safety hazards associated with Li dendrites
[6–10]. Although considerable progress has been achieved in
regulating conductive sulfur hosts, efficient LiPSs catalysts, and
electrode protective layers, improvements to individual aspects
may not address all of the above issues [11–18].
Strategic design of electrolyte additives has emerged as a pivotal
approach to mitigate the intrinsic challenges of Li–S batteries
[19, 20]. It shows superior process compatibility with existing
electrode architectures without compromising structural integ-
rity, and functional additives simultaneously suppress the
polysulfide shuttle and Li dendrite growth, tailoring electrolyte
solvation structures, synergistically enhancing energy density

and safety [21–23]. For instance, lithium nitrate (LiNO3) ex-
emplifies a dual‐function additive that passivates the Li anode
while oxidizing soluble LiPSs, extending the cycle life by > 200%
in ether‐based electrolytes [24, 25]. Similarly, organosulfur
compounds like dimethyl disulfide dynamically reconstruct the
cathode–electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer, suppressing poly-
sulfide diffusion without sacrificing ionic conductivity [26, 27].
Despite these merits, additive‐based strategies face inherent
trade‐offs in terms of dosage, scalability, and shuttle suppres-
sion capability. There remains a compelling need for cost‐
effective, efficient, and multifunctional additives to enhance Li–
S battery performance.
Commonly, the standard electrolyte formulation for Li–S bat-
teries has an equimolar mixture of 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2‐
dimethoxyethane (DME). This binary solvent system achieves
optimal performance by combining low viscosity with high
LiPSs solubility, thereby maximizing sulfur utilization effi-
ciency [28, 29]. To further improve Li+ transport kinetics
while mitigating LiPSs' shuttle effects, electrolyte formulations
typically
incorporate
lithium
salts,
including
lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and lithium bis
(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), as functional additives [30, 31].
However, the limited solubility of these lithium salts presents
technical challenges affecting both electrolyte performance and
electrode stability [32]. Commercial battery systems address this
limitation through the strategic incorporation of specialized
functional additives.
Metal porphyrin/phthalocyanine biomimetic catalysts, demon-
strated in prior studies to show exceptional electron/hole
transport capabilities due to their pyrrolic rings, provide a
foundational framework [33–36]. Indole, a benzopyrrole deriv-
ative, combines advantageous attributes, including a pyrrolic
scaffold, cost‐effectiveness, high electron density, and multi‐site
functionalizability. Its ‐NH‐ moiety undergoes single‐electron
oxidation to facilitate Li+ coordination at the N center, gener-
ating an organometallic complex that modulates Li+ conduction
[37–41]. Crucially, Qie et al. revealed that amide groups in
2‐bromoacetamide form hydrogen bonds with polysulfides,
enhancing Li2S solubility and conversion kinetics while miti-
gating cathode passivation [42]. Concurrently, polyhalogenated
benzene rings in ether‐based electrolytes readily undergo li-
thiation, thereby optimizing solvation structures and enabling
uniform Li+ deposition [43]. Complementary work by Zou et al.
established that carbonyl groups anchor polysulfides via Lewis
acid–base interactions while accelerating their conversion
kinetics, with reversible coordination between lone–pair elec-
trons and Li+ enhancing ion transport [44]. These synergistic
mechanisms position indole derivatives as multifunctional
modulators capable of concurrently enhancing bulk electrolyte
properties and electrode interfaces, further advancing the
development of Li–S batteries.
In this study, 6‐fluoroisatin (FRN), a benzopyrrole‐derived com-
pound with fluorine and carbonyl modifications, is used as an
electrolyte additive to boost Li–S battery performance. Extensive
electrochemical testing and in situ spectroscopic analysis revealed
that FRN facilitates the formation of solvent cluster structures
within the electrolyte. These structures markedly enhance Li+
transport and sulfur conversions, thus mitigating the shuttle effect.
More importantly, the presence of FRN led to the formation of a
lithium fluoride (LiF)–rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer
on the Li anode, which not only regulates Li+ transport but also
promotes uniform Li deposition. As a result, a battery containing
0.5 wt% FRN showed exceptional performance at both ambient
temperature and −20°C. After 1200 cycles at a 1 C rate, the
capacity decay rate was only 0.057% per cycle. Moreover, the
battery retained a capacity of 830 mAh g−1 at a 0.2 C rate even
after 120 cycles under −20°C, with an average decay rate of 0.078%
per cycle. These results underscore the potential use of indole
derivatives in enhancing interfacial stability and enabling Li–S
battery development.

Results and Discussion
In an effort to elucidate the mechanisms behind the perform-
ance enhancement of Li–S batteries through the use of FRN
electrolyte additives, this study delves into the role of FRN in
mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect and in stabilizing the
SEI layer via interactions with polysulfides. Typically, in Li–S
batteries, soluble polysulfides produced during the charge–
discharge cycles can migrate through the separator, leading to
continuous corrosion of the Li anode and the formation of an
unstable SEI layer, which markedly diminishes the cycling
stability, as depicted in Figure 1a. However, when FRN is
incorporated into Li–S batteries, its nitrogen‐active sites engage
with LiPSs and lithium salts, facilitating the conversion of
polysulfides and modulating the solvation structure around Li+
ions. This dual action substantially curbs the polysulfide shuttle
effect, stabilizes the electrode interface, and consequently bol-
sters the overall cell performance. Drawing on this coordination
effect, on the cathode side, FRN interacts with LiPSs to govern
their conversion pathway and promote polysulfide conversion,
thereby mitigating the negative impacts of the shuttle effect.
Concurrently, on the anode side, FRN infiltrates the solvated
structure of Li+ and modulates the decomposition of lithium
salts, as shown in Figure 1b. In 2018, Yang et al. reported that
indole compounds undergo directed electron transfer under an
electric field, enhancing single‐electron removal and the effi-
cient formation of electron‐deficient intermediates [45]. Single‐
electron oxidation is an effective means of transforming indole
compounds. In organic electrolytes with lithium salts, the cyclic
amide group, which has a p–π conjugated system, can interact
with Li⁺ through cation–π interactions. As reported in our
previous work and related literature, its electron‐rich conju-
gated structure regulates lithium‐ion transport [46–48]. More-
over, halide ions on the compound act as sacrificial agents.
They can easily detach from the compound and react with
lithium metal to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
film on the negative electrode [49]. The single‐electron oxida-
tion lithiation of FRN and its interaction with polysulfides and
lithium salts are graphically represented in Figure 1c. The
resultant decomposition products, in conjunction with other
lithium species, are deposited on the Li metal anode, forming
an inorganic LiF‐rich SEI layer. This SEI layer establishes a
uniform anode interface, curbs dendrite growth, and shields the
lithium metal anode from ongoing corrosion by polysulfides.

To substantiate the efficacy of FRN in facilitating sulfur
conversion within Li–S batteries, both the Galvanostatic Inter-
mittent Titration Technique (GITT) and lithium sulfide ex-
periments were meticulously carried out. Throughout the
constant current charging and discharging phases of GITT
curves, LiPSs are transformed into Li2S2 and Li2S during dis-
charge, with Li2S reverting to S8 during the charging phase. The
observed voltage fluctuations in these tests are partly attrib-
uted to alterations in the internal resistance. Following the
cessation of the constant current phase, a relaxation period
ensues, during which the voltage progressively decreases as
the system trends toward equilibrium due to the Li+ diffu-
sion. As illustrated in Figure 2a,b, the voltage polarization
during the nucleation and activation of Li2S in the 0.5 wt%
FRN cell is recorded at 37 and 134 mV, respectively, which is
notably lower than that observed in the cell without FRN,
where polarization values were 39 mV for nucleation and
142 mV for activation. This reduction in voltage polarization
indicates that the inclusion of FRN significantly alleviates
cell polarization, diminishes internal resistance, and en-
hances the kinetics of the reactions involved. In order to gain
insights into the transformation behavior of polysulfides
under practical operating conditions, we have calculated the
lithium‐ion diffusion (DLi+) coefficient throughout the entire
charge–discharge process of the battery. As shown in Sup-
porting Information S1: Figure S5, the 0.5 wt% FRN battery
shows a higher DLi+ during both the charging and discharging
stages compared to the reference battery. This observation
indicates that the incorporation of FRN significantly en-
hances the kinetics of sulfur conversion.

The influence of FRN on the solid–liquid sulfur conversion can
be directly evaluated by examining the constant voltage dis-
charge curve at 2.10 V. The initial dip in the current within this
curve is due to the reduction of long‐ to medium‐chain LiPSs.
Following this, as the nucleation of Li2S begins, the current
gradually ascends, with the significant subsequent current
reflecting the growth of existing nuclei. The gradual decline in
current over time is a result of the overlap of adjacent atomic
nuclei or within the diffusion zone [50, 51]. The peak current
(Im) occurring at a specific time (tm) signifies the convergence of
adjacent Li2S nuclei or the diffusion hemispheres around them.
A shorter tm value indicates a quicker surface coverage, which
facilitates the assessment of the relative rate of electrode pas-
sivation and the rate of solid–liquid sulfur conversion. In the
cell without FRN (as shown in Figure 2d), tm is recorded at
13,810 s, with a deposition capacity of 344 mAh g−1. Conversely,
the cell containing 0.5 wt% FRN (as depicted in Figure 2c)
demonstrates a tm of 3600 s and a deposition capacity of
630.741 mAh g−1, highlighting the capability of FRN to expedite
the conversion of LiPSs into Li2S.

To assess the compatibility of FRN with Li‐negative electrodes,
Li–Cu coin cells were constructed and were subjected to Li
plating cycle testing under specific conditions: a current density
of 0.5 mA cm−2, a capacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2, and a cut‐off
voltage of 0.5 V. The charging and discharging profiles across
successive cycles were scrutinized to gauge the cell stability. It
was found that the cell containing 0.5 wt% FRN showed con-
sistent charging and discharging curves over 100 cycles, without
any signs of overcharging (Figure 3a). Conversely, the cell
without FRN maintained stability only for the initial 40 cycles,
after which there was a marked decline and a tendency toward
overcharging from 40 to 80 cycles, culminating in peak over-
charge after 100 cycles (Figure 3b). A meticulous comparison of
the overpotential distribution during the 20th cycle (Figure 3c)
revealed that the 0.5 wt% FRN cell showed reduced mass
transfer control overpotential (28.5 mV) and nucleation over-
potential (5.3 mV). Moreover, the Coulombic efficiency of the
0.5 wt% FRN cell consistently remained above 97% throughout
100 cycles, whereas the cell without FRN maintained stability
for the first 60 cycles but then experienced fluctuations, leading
to a Coulombic efficiency of approximately 90% thereafter
(Figure 3d). These results collectively suggest that 0.5 wt% FRN
is highly compatible with Li‐negative electrodes, enhancing
interface stability. This improved stability is probably attributed
to the fluorine in FRN, which forms a LiF‐rich SEI layer on the
surface. The LiF‐rich SEI layer facilitates Li+ transport and
significantly boosts the electrochemical performance [53].
Li–Li symmetric cells were constructed and tested under vari-

Furthermore, Li–Li symmetric cells were also cycled extensively

The cell containing 0.5 wt% FRN showed stable cycling per-

countering a precipitous decline in voltage polarization, plum-

3 mA cm⁻² and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm⁻², the FRN‐
modified electrolyte demonstrates significantly reduced voltage
polarization (ΔV = 50 mV vs. 178 mV in baseline, Supporting
Information S1: Figure S8). Moreover, the chronopotentiometry
measurements show > 200 h of stable cycling under this severe
condition. These experimentally validated results quantitatively
confirm FRN's dual role in suppressing uneven Li⁺ flux through
interfacial charge redistribution and maintaining robust SEI
integrity under a high‐current operation. This means that FRN
plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the negative electrode and
enhancing the overall performance of the cell.
To investigate the effects of varying proportions of FRN on Li–S
batteries, cyclic performance testing was conducted by incorpo-
rating different amounts of FRN into the battery. The results,
illustrated in Figure 4a, show significant variations in discharge
capacities at different rates (0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 C) for batteries
containing 0.5 wt%, 0.1 wt%, and 1 wt% FRN. The 0.5 wt% FRN
battery displayed higher discharge capacities of 1376, 998, 900,
826, and 771 mAh g−1, outperforming both the 0.1 wt% FRN (1219,
933, 857, 783, and 695 mAh g−1) and 1 wt% FRN batteries (1360,
971, 871, 793, and 720 mAh g−1). Although the 0.1 wt% and 1 wt%
FRN batteries showed lower discharge capacities than the 0.5 wt%
FRN batteries, they still outperformed the batteries without FRN
(1201, 920, 733, 666, and 619 mAh g−1). Notably, even during a
transition from 3 to 0.2 C, the 0.5 wt% FRN battery maintained a
highly reversible discharge capacity of 910 mAh g−1, exceeding the
reversible capacities observed for other proportions (without FRN:
765 mAh g−1, 0.1 wt% FRN: 844 mAh g−1, 1 wt% FRN: 839 mAh
g−1). The galvanostatic charging and discharging curves at 0.2 C,
as shown in Figure 4b, showed a prolonged and stable discharge
platform for the 0.5 wt% FRN cell. Consequently, the integration of
0.5 wt% FRN is deemed optimal for significantly improving battery
performance.

The practical application of Li–S batteries hinges significantly
on sulfur content, the electrolyte‐to‐sulfur ratio (E/S), sulfur

Conclusion
In summary, we introduced FRN as an electrolyte additive, a
compound derived from benzopyrrole with modifications
involving fluorine and carbonyl groups. The incorporation of
fluorine facilitates the formation of a stable SEI layer en-
riched with LiF, which effectively suppresses Li dendrite
formation and promotes uniform Li+ deposition, thus en-
hancing the battery performance. Additionally, FRN en-
hances the redox kinetics of polysulfides, mitigating shuttle
effects, and plays a crucial role in establishing a solvation
layer that stabilizes the interface between the electrolyte and
electrodes. As a result, a battery containing 0.5 wt% FRN
demonstrated
exceptional
performance,
sustaining
1100
cycles at 25°C and showing stable cycling at −20°C, with
minimal single‐cycle decay rate. These successful outcomes
underscore the versatility of FRN as an electrolyte additive,
offering valuable insights into additive structure design and
