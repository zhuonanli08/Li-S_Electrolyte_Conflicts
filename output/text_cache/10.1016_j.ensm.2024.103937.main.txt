A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur batteries face a critical challenge: LiNO3 limits the electrochemical stability window of electrolyte
and poses safety risks. This study presents FePC@NH2−MIL-68, a biomimetic enzyme catalyst stable in ether-
based electrolytes, to address these issues, which can replace LiNO3 and facilitate Li-anion decomposition.
Theoretical and spectroscopic investigations show it promotes LiTFSI decomposition, forms robust CEI and SEI
interfaces, and enhances sulfur conversions. Consequently, the FePC@NH2−MIL-68 cell, without LiNO3 additive,
delivers a high discharge capacity of 1549 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and a low capacity decay rate of 0.067% over 1000
cycles at 1.5 C. Even under high sulfur loading of 7.9 mg cm−2 and low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 5.5 μL mg−1,
the cells maintain 720 mAh g−1. This work emphasizes that in developing sulfur conversion catalysts, special
attention must be paid to their effects on other lithium salt components, which provides a guidance for new
catalysts design for metal-sulfur batteries.
1. Introduction
The widespread adoption of portable electronics, consumer devices,
and large-scale grid energy storage systems has driven the demand for
high-performance and long-cycle-life batteries [1–3]. Lithium sulfur
(Li-S) batteries, which could theoretically achieve a high energy density
of 2600 Wh kg−1 based on the stepwise conversion reaction of S8 with
Li+, are considered as a next-generation energy storage candidate [4–6].
However, most these batteries reported to date exhibit unsatisfactory
Coulombic efficiency during cycling, typically ranging from 95% to 98%
[7–9]. This falls short of commercial Li-ion batteries (>99.9%), leading
to rapid capacity decay and severely limiting cycling lifespan [10,11].
The primary factors contributing to the Coulombic efficiency limitations
in Li-S batteries are the lithium polysulfide (LiPS) shuttle effect and
lithium anode erosion [12–14]. The LiPS shuttle effect triggers irre-
versible sulfur conversion reactions, resulting in the loss of active sulfur
over time [15–17]. Meanwhile, lithium anode erosion causes uneven Li
plating/stripping and accelerates the depletion of active Li [18,19].
Consequently, urgent measures that aimed at suppressing the LiPS
shuttle effect and regulating lithium deposition are required to mitigate
these issues.
To address these challenges, Mikhaylik and Akridge first proposed
lithium nitrate (LiNO3) as an electrolyte additive in Li-S batteries in
2008 [20]. This innovation significantly boosted Coulombic efficiency
from below 80% to above 95%, and in some instances, nearly 100%. The
primary mechanism behind this improvement involves LiNO3 promot-
ing the formation of a stable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer be-
tween the lithium anode and the electrolyte [21,22]. This SEI layer
enhances the stability of the lithium anode, prevents further electrolyte
decomposition, reduces self-discharge, inhibits the lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) shuttle effect, and thereby improves Coulombic efficiency [23,
24]. This groundbreaking work has spurred further research into
enhancing the performance and stability of Li-S batteries [25]. Recent
studies have proposed that NO3

promoting the cleavage of C-F bonds in TFSI−, and further forming LiF

prevent the reduction of LiPS on the cathode [28]. However, LiNO3
limits the electrochemical stability window of the electrolyte [29], with
deeper discharges causing more irreversible nitrate reduction [30] and
adversely affecting the redox reversibility of the sulfur cathode [31].
Most importantly, in the future commercialization process of Li-S bat-
teries, large pouch cells or cylindrical cells require a significant amount
of electrolyte, leading to a substantial amount of LiNO3 being added
[32]. If the internal temperature of the battery runaway, the carbon/-

with LiNO3, potentially forming hazardous gunpowder that significantly
increases the risk of explosions [33]. Even if an explosion does not occur
initially, a battery fire could pose a subsequent explosion risk (Scheme
1). Therefore, there is an urgent need to find a replacement material that
can simultaneously form an in-situ protective layer on the Li anode and
promote the conversion of LiPSs at the cathode, thereby enhancing the
overall performance of Li-S batteries [34].
Unfortunately, few studies have directly replaced LiNO3 while
forming stable CEI/SEI [35]. Archer et al. demonstrated using multi-
dentate molecule (SiCl4) as a replacement to form a stable SEI [36].
Catalysts, such as black phosphorus quantum dots, show promise as
alternatives, which were found to catalyze LiPS conversion and main-
tained CE >98% when LiNO3 was omitted from the battery electrolyte
[37]. However, most reports on “LiNO3 mimic” are merely focused on
forming a stable CEI/SEI or accelerating the conversion of LiPSs, but
neglect the interaction between LiNO3 and LiTFSI. Interestingly, LiNO3,
LiTFSI and LiPS are all Li salts in the Li-S system and the design of LiPS
conversion catalysts offers a new approach to replace LiNO3 for LiTFSI
decomposition. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) represent a new
class of organic–inorganic hybrid materials [38], which have attractive
characteristics, such as chemical tunability, high surface area and per-
manent porosity [39]. Recent research has shown that loading enzymes
or organometallic small molecules into MOFs can mimic enzyme active
centers to achieve biomimetic catalysis [40]. For example, Zhou et al.
successfully loaded lipase into ZIF-8 MOF, observing significant im-
provements in enzyme activity and stability [41]. Farha et al. con-
structed iron porphyrin active centers mimicking peroxidase in NU-1000
MOF, demonstrating excellent catalytic activity for hydrogen peroxide
decomposition [42]. Combining these findings with our previous works,
we believe that MOF-based biomimetic enzyme catalysts have excellent
potential for promoting the decomposition of both LiTFSI and LiPSs.
Herein, we have successfully encapsulated iron(II) phthalocyanine
(FePC) within amino-functionalized MIL-68(In) MOF to construct an
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst for advanced Li–S batteries. Through a
comprehensive series of in-situ and ex-situ spectroscopic analyses,
coupled with ab initio molecular dynamics simulations, we discovered
that FePC@NH2−MIL-68 can effectively capture LiTFSI via In-N bonds,
and replace LiNO3 to promote LiTFSI decomposition, forming stable
SEI/CEI layers. Besides, this novel catalyst can also accelerate the con-
versions of lithium polysulfides and significantly reduce the reaction
energy barrier (Fig. 1a). As a result, the cathode incorporating
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 exhibits exceptional battery performance, deliv-
ering a high initial discharge capacity of 1549 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C (where 1
C = 1675 mA g−1). Notably, even under challenging conditions with a
high sulfur areal loading of 7.9 mg cm−2 and a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/
S) ratio of 5.5 mL mg−1, the cell maintains 698 mAh g−1 after 65 cycles
at 0.1 C.
2. Results and discussion

FePC into NH2−MIL-68 molecular frameworks through a series of hy-
drothermal and centrifugal treatments. Specifically, the stick-shaped
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 can be conveniently obtained by self-assembly of
indium salt, 2-aminoterephthalic acid (NH2-H2BDC) and FePC (more
detailed synthetic procedure please see the experimental section in
Supporting Information). The microstructure and composition of this
catalyst were analyzed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform
infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), Raman spectroscopy, scanning electron

2.2. FePC@NH2−MIL-68 adaptability in Li-S battery chemistry
Considering the catalytic applications of MOFs, stability, including
chemical, thermal, and electrolyte stability, is a major concern [44].
FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. S5a) indicates that after heat treatment and
electrolyte immersion, there is no significant change in the crystal
structure. This is further supported by SEM results (Fig. S6), which
reveal the retained nanorod-like morphology of FePC@NH2−MIL-68
after heat treatment and electrolyte immersion, suggesting unchanged
coordination between ligands and In sites. Subsequently, we conducted
the TEM and XRD tests of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 after heat treatment and
electrolyte immersion (Fig. S7). The results indicated that there were no
significant changes in the bonding mode or structural composition after
immersion in electrolyte and subsequent heat treatment, thereby
confirming the excellent electrolyte stability and thermal stability of the
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst. Furthermore, the electrochemical stability
of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 was also confirmed through symmetric cell tests.
CV tests at a sweep rate of 0.5 mV s−1 and 10 mV s−1 were conducted at
room temperature within a potential window of -1.5–1.5 V which
revealed a pair of rectangular-like redox peaks, indicating the pseudo-
capacitance characteristics of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 (Fig. S5b-c). The
overlapping CV curves in subsequent cycles suggest the good structural
integrity of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 in the electrochemical environment.

2.3.3. LiTFSI decomposition reforming SEI layer

increasing impedance and electrolyte consumption. Conversely, the

2.4. FePC@NH2−MIL-68 mediating sulfur conversion reactions
Similar to traditional catalysis, the adsorption of LiPSs, as an
important prerequisite, affects subsequent sulfur catalytic reactions
greatly [57–59]. Thus, the static Li2S6 adsorption tests were carried out
to visualize the adsorption of LiPSs on FePC@NH2−MIL-68. As exhibited
in Fig. 5a, in contrast to NH2−MIL-68 and FePC samples, the yellow color
of pristine Li2S6 solution fades and becomes transparent after the addi-
tion of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 after 8 h, suggesting the FePC@NH2−MIL-68
catalyst can strongly adsorb LiPSs. Ex-situ UV−vis spectroscopy was
used to quantitative evaluate the LiPS adsorption capacity (Fig. 5b) [60].
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 displays significantly weaker adsorption of LiPS
compared to FePC, NH2−MIL-68, and pristine Li2S6 solutions. Notably,
when FePC is mixed with the Li2S6 solution, the solution turns green,
suggesting that FePC has dissolved and is lost into the electrolyte. This
observation is further supported by UV−vis spectroscopy. The charac-
teristic Q-band of FePC, located at 690 nm and attributed to the
π-electron transition from the 2a1u to 6eg, orbitals of the phthalocyanine
ligand, shows a pronounced signal in the case of FePC. In stark contrast,
the FePC signals are almost undetectable in FePC@NH2−MIL-68, indi-
cating that NH2−MIL-68 framework effectively prevents the dissolution
of FePC. These findings are consistent with previous results (Fig. 1d).

2.5. Cell performance
The electrochemical tests were carried out on 2025-coin cells
without LiNO3 additives by controlling the sulfur mass on the cathode as
~1.2 mg cm−2. A series of CV profiles were recorded to evaluate the
effects of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 on the redox kinetic promotion (Fig. 6a
and Fig. S11). In a voltage range of 1.6 to 2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+), the first (IH,
2.31 V) and second (IL, 2.03 V) reduction peaks corresponded to the
conversion of S8 to Li2Sn (4 ≤n ≤8) and then to Li2S2/Li2S, respectively;
while the oxidation peak (2.32 V) corresponded to the reversible for-
mation of Li2Sn and S8. It is noteworthy that the potential difference
between the anodic peak and cathodic peak at around 2.0 V of
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 electrode is 0.33 V, which is much smaller than
NH2−MIL-68, FePC, and CNT electrodes, suggesting the enhanced redox
reaction kinetics by the FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst. Meanwhile, the
higher peak current of the electrode with FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst
suggests the enhanced sulfur utilization due to the effective polysulfides
trapping and reusing by the FePC@NH2−MIL-68 interfaces. It is worth
noting that CV curves of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 in the following cycles
overlap well, demonstrating the good reversibility (Fig. S12). These
findings provide strong evidence for the efficient immobilization and
rapid catalysis of LiPSs by the FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst.
The role of FePC@NH2−MIL-68 interfaces can be more clearly
elucidated through rate capability tests under various current densities,
which involve faster charge transfer and higher conversion reaction
kinetics during the charge/discharge process. Fig. 6b illustrates the rate
performances
of

with
CNT,
FePC,
NH2−MIL-68,
and
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 electrodes. As anticipated, the cathode with
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 demonstrates the highest discharge capacity across
all current densities. It achieves superior discharge capacities of 1549,
1239, 1149, and 1090 mAh g−1 at cycling rates of 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0
C, respectively (based on the mass of sulfur, 1 C = 1675 mAh g−1).
Notably, it still delivers a capacity of 1012 mAh g−1 even at a high
current density of 1.5 C. This performance surpasses that of cathodes
with CNT (1082, 755, 718, 691, and 627 mAh g−1), FePC (1257, 900,
835, 798, and 734 mAh g−1), and NH2−MIL-68 (1390, 1029, 955, 911,
and 867 mAh g−1) at corresponding rates. When the rate is reverted to
0.2 C, the cathode with FePC@NH2−MIL-68 maintains a stable
Coulombic efficiency of >98%, compared to 72%, 76%, and 82% for
CNT, FePC, and NH2−MIL-68 cells, respectively, indicating superior
electrochemical reversibility, which outperforms most previously re-
ported Li–S batteries with LiNO3 mimic (Table S1). By further adjusting
the proportion of FePC@NH2−MIL-68, a mass ratio of 1% in the elec-
trode was determined to be the optimal dosage of FePC@NH2−MIL-68
catalyst (Fig. S13). Furthermore, the discharge/charge profiles with the
FePC@NH2−MIL-68 catalyst exhibit longer and flatter plateaus with
reduced polarization compared to NH2−MIL-68, FePC, and CNT, sug-
gesting improved sulfur redox kinetics (Fig. S14). As the current density
increases, the polarization of cells using FePC@NH2−MIL-68 remains
low (Fig. 6c), indicating rapid reactions attributable to the multifunc-
tional catalytic properties of FePC@NH2−MIL-68. To further investigate

the adhesive effect of PVDF, and the effective contact of electrode
components ensure that CEI maintains its integrity under the rigorous
conditions of high sulfur load batteries.
