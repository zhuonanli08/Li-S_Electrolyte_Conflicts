Experimental section
Materials: Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), dimethoxy ethane (DME) and ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN) were purchased from Dodochem. 1,2-dimethoxypropane (DMP), polyacrylonitrile (PAN) (Mw=150000) and sulfur were purchased from Aladdin. DMP was dried with 4 Å molecular sieves before use. The ‘M’ in this work means molar salt in liter solvent.
Preparation of electrolyte: LiFSI was added into DME solvent to prepare 2 M electrolyte (named DME electrolyte), and DMP solvents to prepare 2 M electrolyte (named DMP electrolyte). For DMP-PFPN electrolyte, 2	M LiFSI was dissolved into the mixed solvent of DMP-PFPN (v:v=3:7). All the electrolytes were prepared inside an argon-filled glove box.
Preparation of electrodes: S and PAN powders were ball milled with a mass ratio of 3:1. The homogeneous mixture was heated at 350℃ in a tube furnace for 6 h with a ramp rate of 3℃ min-1. The prepared sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), Super P and Polyacrylic acid (PAA) were mixed at a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in deionized water. The homogeneous slurry was uniformly coated on a carbon-coated aluminum foil and dried at 80℃ under vacuum overnight. By controlling the coating thickness, the SPAN electrode with different loading can be obtained. The SPAN loadings were controlled to be ≈1 and 4.8 mg cm−2 for, respectively, low and high loading assessments.
Electrochemical measurement: All the coin cells were assembled and disassembled in an argon-filled glove box with H2O and O2 less than 0.01ppm. For Li||SPAN cells, the pre-weighted SPAN electrode, 16 mm-diameter Celgard 2325 separator and 50 μm lithium foil ware sequentially placed into a 2032-type coin cell, followed by the addition of 30 or 40 μL electrolytes for low and high loading assessments. The Li||SPAN cells were rested for 8 h before electrochemical test. The cut-off voltage was from 1.0 V to 3.0 V. Capacity values were calculated based on the sulfur content in the SPAN powder.
Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted with Li||stainless steel cells with the CHI660E electrochemical workstation. The sweep voltage range was between 2.5-6.0 V, with a ramp rate of 5 mV s-1. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was also with the CHI660E electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of 0.1-0.5 mV s-1. Electrochemical impendence spectroscopy (EIS) was tested on CHI660E electrochemical workstation in a frequency range from 0.1 HZ to 1 MHZ.
Material characterization: The self-extinguishing time (SET) is determined as follows: Take the pre-weighed electrolyte sample, ignite it, and record the duration from the ignition to the self-extinguishment. Each experiment is repeated three times, and the final result is the average of the three measurements. Raman spectra (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Bruker 400MHZ) spectroscopy were carried out to characterize the solvation structure of various electrolytes. The electrode-electrolyte interphases were analyzed by cold field emission transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM F200). The micromorphology of cycled lithium metal anodes was characterized by using field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7800F). The surface compositions of cycled electrodes were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha). The sulfur content of SPAN was detected using organic element analyzer (EA, Elementar Unicube). 
Computational methods: The theoretical calculations were performed via the Gaussian 16 suite of programs [1]. The structures of the studied molecules and their complexes with Li+ were fully optimized at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311+G(d) level of theory. The vibrational frequencies of the optimized structures were carried out at the same level. The structures were characterized as a local energy minimum on the potential energy surface by verifying that all the vibrational frequencies were real. The interaction energy (ΔE) of the optimized complexes was calculated. ΔE is defined as the difference between the complex and the sum of energies of monomers, which can be obtained by the following formulas ΔE =E(AB)-E(A)-E(B). The molecular orbital levels of studied compounds were investigated via theoretical calculations, including the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). The Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) program [2] was used to plot the color-filled iso-surface graphs to visualize the molecular orbitals and the molecular electrostatic potential (MESP). To avoid basis set superposition error (BSSE), counterpoise correction [3] was applied to obtain the complexation energies at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311+G(d) level of theory.
In this system, the x, y and Z dimensions were periodic boundary conditions using Classical MD simulations by a modied MD code based on the GROMACS package. The 2 M LiFSI in DME, 2 M LiFSI in DMP and 2 M LiFSI in DMP/PFPN structures had been establsihed using the LiFSI, DME, DMP, PFPN molecules. The size of the simulation box is 3.9×3.9×3.9 nm3. The OPLS force field, which is suitable for electrolyte solution, was used to optimize sample structures for preliminary structural optimization. Atomic charges of ions were multiplied by scale factor 0.73 to correct the polarization effect of anion and cation. First, the conjugate gradient algorithm and energy minimization were performed to obtain a stable structure before using dynamic simulations. Each sample was then equilibrated under the NPT ensemble at a constant temperature of 400 K to achieve an equilibrium state with zero pressure for 5 ns. Subsequently, the system temperature was reduced from 400 K to 298 K for annealing 5 ns under the NPT ensemble (under 1 atmosphere). The Andersen feedback thermostat and Berendsen barostat algorithm were applied in the system with temperature and pressure conversion. Next, MD simulations were carried out under the NVT ensemble at 298 K for 10 ns, with an integration time step of 1 fs. The v-rescale thermostat was adopted to maintain the temperature stability of the electrolyte system during the NVT simulation. Non-electrostatic interactions were computed with a cutoﬀ distance of 1.2 nm. The electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) grid spacing set at 0.1 nm. For the computation of electrostatic interactions in real space, a cutoﬀ distance of 1.2 nm was applied.
