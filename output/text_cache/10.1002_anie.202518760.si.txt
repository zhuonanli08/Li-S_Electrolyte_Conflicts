Experimental Procedures
Preparation of electrolytes. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)-imide (LiTFSI, 99.9%) were obtained from MREDA. The LiTFSI were dried at 120 °C for 24 hours under vacuum conditions. The fluorinated nitrile solvents were synthesized according to the previous report.[1] The 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (TTE, >98%) and fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC, >98%) were purchased from Aladdin. Prior to use, all solvents were treated with activated molecular sieves (4 Å pore size) for three days. The electrolytes were prepared inside an argon-filled glovebox. The electrolyte was formulated by dissolving the LiTFSI in pure or mixed solvent.
Preparation of electrodes. The Ketjen black/sulfur (KB/S) composite with 70 wt% sulfur was synthesized via melt-diffusion by heating the mixture at 155 °C for 12 h. The sulfur electrodes were prepared by casting the homogeneous slurry consist of KB/S, Super P, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) in an 8:1:1 weight ratio in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) onto carbon-coated Al foil, followed by drying under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h. The dried electrodes were punched into 10 mm diameter discs. The typical sulfur loading for electrochemical testing was 1-1.2 mg cm−2. The Si/C electrodes were prepared by casting the homogeneous slurry consist of Si/C, Super P, sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), and styrene butadiene rubber (SBR) in an 8:1:0.5:0.5 weight ratio in DI-water onto Cu foil, followed by drying under vacuum at 90 °C for 12 h. The dried electrodes were punched into 12 mm diameter discs. The typical Si/C loading for electrochemical testing approach 1.8 mg cm−2. The high-loading electrode were prepared by casting the homogeneous slurry consist of KB/S (or Si/C material), Super P, CMC, and SBR were mixed in a mass ratio of 8:1:0.5:0.5 weight ratio in DI-water onto carbon-coated Al (or Cu) foil. After drying under vacuum at 60 °C (or 90 °C) for 12 h, the KB/S (or Si/C) electrode was cut into circular discs with a diameter of 10 mm (or 12 mm).
Electrochemical measurements. The electrochemical performance of Li–S and LiSi–S cells with various electrolytes was evaluated using CR2032 coin cells. An electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 20 μL mg−1 was used for electrochemical cycling of all Li–S and LiSi–S cells unless otherwise stated. All experimental procedures were carried out in an argon-filled glovebox. Galvanostatic cycling of Li–S cell was conducted using a Neware system (CT-4008Tn) between 1.2 and 3.0 V at 35 °C at 0.2C after one cycle activization at 0.1C (1C=1675 mA h g−1). GITT measurements were conducted at 35 °C on the Neware system, using a current pulse of 0.025 C and a relaxation time of 2 h. Prior to assembling the LiSi–S cell, the Si anode was prelithiated in a Li–Si half-cell using 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC/FEC for three cycles. The lithiated Si (LiSi) electrode was then transferred to the LiSi–S cell. The N/P ratio is designed to be approximately 2. Galvanostatic cycling of LiSi–S cells were conducted using the Neware CT-4008Tn system between 1.0 and 3.0 V at 35 °C. Si–LiSi symmetric cells were assembled with a pristine Si electrode and a lithiated Si (LiSi) electrode, separated by a Celgard 2500 membrane. Charge/discharge tests were performed at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 2 mA h cm−2. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement of the LiSi–S cells was performed on a Garmy electrochemical station (IFC1010) with the frequency between 0.1-100 kHz and 10 mV perturbation amplitude. Li|stainless steel cells containing 40 μL electrolyte were assembled and performed linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) from the open-circuit potential to 6.5 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1 under room temperature.
Physical Characterizations. Raman spectra were recorded on a Micro Raman Imaging Spectrometer (LabRAM Odyssey, Horiba France) using a 532 nm laser. The 7Li NMR spectra were measured on a 400MHz instrument (AV Z HD 400, Bruker) in a 3 mm NMR tubes at Beijing NMR Center, Peking University, and the resonances were referenced to an external standard (1 M LiCl in D2O) in a coaxial 5 mm NMR tube. Ex-situ synchrotron-based X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra was carried out at the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF). Sulfur K-edge was collected on the Soft X-ray Microcharacterization Beamline (SXRMB) by total electron yield (TEY) mode. The electrode was disassembled and sealed in glovebox under Argon to avoid oxidation. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to beamline for test. The in-situ XRD measurements were performed on a Rigaku diffractometer (SmartLab 9 kW, Rigaku) using a Cu Ka radiation from 20° to 60° at a step size of 0.01° and a speed of 2.5° min−1. The electrochemical XRD cell with a beryllium window was purchased from Tianjin Aida Hengsheng Technology Development. Freestanding cathodes with S loading of ~3 mg cm−2 were used here and the discharge/charge rate was 0.05C. The viscosity of the electrolytes was measured using a viscometer (Anton Paar, SVM 3001). The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on EON, F3EON, F5EON, and F8EON solvents using a TA Instruments TGA 55 analyzer. Approximately 9.1 mg of each sample was placed in alumina crucibles with the temperature increasing to 500°C at a constant rate of 10 °C min−1 under an inert atmosphere. The electrode microstructure was characterized by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, SU8220). Field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FETEM, FEI Tecnai F20) was employed to investigate the CEI and SEI structures. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on cycled LiSi anodes and S cathodes using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha system. The electrodes were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox to avoid air or moisture exposure and then transferred to the XPS system. The ionic conductivities of the electrolytes were measured via EIS using a Gamry IFC1010 workstation. Specifically, swagelok cells were assembled using two molybdenum electrodes and a PTFE ring spacer with inner diameter of 6 mm and thickness of 0.2 mm. A volume of 400 μL electrolyte was added in each cell. The ionic resistance (Rb) was obtained from the x-axis intercept of the Nyquist plot at high frequency. Computational simulations. The DFT calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 software package[2]. The optimum structures were optimized by B3LYP method in combination with the 6-31G (d, p) basis set and the more precise energy was calculated by same method in combination with the 6-311 G++ (d, p) basis set. Electrostatic potential was subsequently calculated using Multiwfn[3] and visualized with VMD. 
The MD simulations were performed with GROMACS 2023 program by adopting the optimized potentials for a liquid simulations all-atom (OPLS-AA) force field.[4] The force field parameters for the solvent molecules were generated by the LigParGen web expect for using CM5*1.2 atomic charges.[4b] According to the molar ratio in experiments, 50 LiTFSI molecules, 225 EON molecules (184 F5EON molecules in the second system), 165 TTE molecules, and 7 FEC molecules were randomly put into the cubic simulation box by using Packmol software.[5] The steepest-descent method was applied to perform energy minimization of the system. Subsequently, 10 cycles of quench-annealing dynamics between 298 K and 698 K were conducted to eliminate the persistence of metastable states. After that, 50 ns MD simulation was carried out in an isothermal-isobaric ensemble at a temperature of 298 K and a pressure of 1 bar, and the last 20 ns of the MD trajectory was used for analysis. During the simulation, the temperature was controlled by the Nosé-Hoover thermostat algorithm with a coupling constant of 0.2 ps, and the pressure was controlled by the Parrinello-Rahman algorithm. The LINCS algorithm was employed for bond constraints. The long-range electrostatic interactions were handled by the particle - mesh Ewald (PME) method. The non-bonded potential truncation was performed with the cutoff radius of 12 Å was adopted for the Lennard - Jones potential. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three directions. The time step was set to 1 fs. The visualization and analysis of the simulation results were performed using VMD and internal codes.


Results and Discussion

Figure S1 The physical properties of varied electrolytes: (a) Ionic conductivity and (b) viscosity. The EON/TTE electrolyte exhibits the highest ionic conductivity, attributed to the high dissociation degree of LiTFSI. In contrast, the F3EON/TTE electrolyte shows reduced ionic conductivity due to lower LiTFSI dissociation and increased viscosity. For F5EON/TTE, the ionic conductivity further decreases due to the formation of more ionic aggregates. Nevertheless, it maintains a conductivity of 1.8 mS cm−1 at 30 °C, which is sufficient for normal ion transport. However, the ionic conductivity of F8EON/TTE significantly declines due to a dramatic increase in viscosity. Noteworthy, increasing the volume fraction of TTE does not improve its ionic conductance. Because the Li+ conductivity is also influenced by the total ion concentration in the electrolyte. In the low concentration regime, ionic conductivity generally increases with higher salt concentration. In this work, the actual dissolved LiTFSI concentration falls below 1 M (0.75M) when the F8EON/TTE ratio is reduced to 1:2 due to the reduced solvation capability of F8EON solvent resulting from its fluoroalkyl chain length. Furthermore, the formation of abundant AGG (aggregate) coordination structures may additionally impede ion transport. Therefore, the simultaneous reduction in both salt concentration and viscosity collectively contributes to the observed decrease in ionic conductivity.



Figure S2 The TG curves of different solvent. The results indicate that the volatilization temperature of the solvents gradually increases with the degree of fluorination. F5EON starts vaporization at 112.7°C, significantly higher than the 80°C boiling point of conventional DME/DOL solvents, demonstrating its broader working temperature range.





Figure S3 The ignition test of electrolytes with 1M LiTFSI FnEON/TTE (1:1 vol%). The EON/TTE electrolyte sustained burning after ignition. In contrast, the fluorinated FnEON/TTE electrolytes did not continue burning after the ignition source was removed. This flame-retardant effect is attributed to the release of hydrogen fluoride, which scavenges radicals in the flame through hydrogen atom transfer, thereby disrupting the chain reactions required for combustion.




Figure S4 The LSV curves of varied electrolytes tested using Li|stainless steel cell at 1 mV s−1 from open-circuit potential to 6.5 V. The 1M EON/TTE electrolyte begins to decompose at 3.9 V vs Li/Li+, while the anodic stability of the 1M F3EON/TTE electrolyte is improved, with an onset potential of 4.6 V vs Li/Li+. The decomposition potentials of the 1M F5EON/TTE and 1M F8EON/TTE electrolytes further increase to 4.9 V and 5.0 V vs Li/Li+, respectively. This enhancement in anodic stability can be attributed to the improved molecular stability of the fluorinated structures, as well as the formation of aggregate coordination structures that effectively suppress the decomposition of electrolyte components.





Figure S5 The Raman spectra of various electrolyte. (a) The S–N–S band for electrolytes with 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in various solvents. (b-c) The C−O−C (b) and −C≡N (c) vibrational bands in different electrolytes and the corresponding pure solvents. The peaks at 741, 746, and 751 cm−1 in the S–N–S region correspond to solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs), contact ion pairs (CIPs), and aggregates (AGGs), respectively. Compared with the DOL/DME solvent, the proportion of TFSI anions in SSIP form gradually decreases in fluorinated nitrile solvents as the number of fluorine substitutions increases. Concurrently, the AGG proportion increases, indicating strengthened anion coordination. The C–O–C band exhibits negligible Raman shifts in both F3EON and F8EON electrolytes, suggesting that the C–O–C group is likely not participate in Li+ solvation. For the −C≡N group, the free solvent peak shifts to the coordinate peak in both F3EON and F8EON electrolyte, suggesting the obvious coordination with Li+.



Figure S6 (a) The typical i-t curves and (b) EIS plot of the cells using different electrolytes. (c) The Li+ transfer number of different electrolytes. The calculated tLi+ increase from 0.51 to 0.59 with the degree of fluorine substitution. Combined with the coordination structure analysis from Raman spectroscopy, this trend indicates that the increased proportion of AGG complexes in the electrolyte significantly enhances the Li⁺ transference number. This can be attributed to the fact that in both CIP and AGG coordination structure, anions are "anchored" to Li⁺ via coordination bonds. Under an external electric field, CIP and AGG species migrate as entities, which markedly reduces the independent mobility of free anions, thereby leading to an overall increase in the lithium-ion transference number.








Figure S7 The optical photograph showing the solubility of 0.2 M Li2S8 in 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte using various FnEON/TTE co-solvent with volume ratio of 1:1. The influence of fluorinated nitrile solvents on Li2S8 solubility was examined, as Li2S8 is among the most soluble sulfur intermediates. The EON/TTE electrolyte appears yellow, indicating great Li2S8 dissolution. In contrast, F5EON/TTE and F8EON/TTE electrolytes remain colorless, substantially lower LiPSs solubility. The variations in LiPSs solubility could influence the sulfur reaction pathways and kinetics, thereby affecting the voltage profiles and cycling performance.




Figure S8 The voltage profiles of the Li−S cells with different electrolytes during the 1st cycle at 0.1C and 35 °C: (a) 1M LiTFSI EON, (b) 1M LiTFSI F3EON, and (c) 1M LiTFSI F5EON. The voltage profile of the Li–S cell with the EON electrolyte exhibits two distinct plateaus, indicating a typical solid-liquid-solid multistep reaction pathway. In contrast, cells with F3EON and F5EON electrolytes display a ‘quasi-bi-plateau’ discharge profile with voltages at ~2.15 V, implying a QSSSR mechanism. The reduced solubility of LiPSs evidently drives the shift in the sulfur redox pathway.



Figure S9 The voltage profiles of the Li−S cells using 1M LiTFSI F3EON/TTE electrolyte during the 1st cycle at 0.025C and 35 °C. The S in F3EON/TTE-based electrolyte undergoes a quasi-solid-state reaction pathway; however, the cell still experiences a pronounced shuttle effect, as indicated by a lower discharge capacity compared to the charge capacity.




Figure S10 (a) The ionic conductivity of various electrolytes at 25 °C. (b) The initial voltage profiles of Li–S cells using different electrolytes at a charge/discharge rate of 0.02C. (c) The cycling performance of the Li–S cells using different electrolytes at 0.2C after 2 cycles activation at 0.02C. A comparable level of ionic conductivity is observed at 0.5 M and 1.0 M salt concentrations. However, a marked decline to 0.65 mS cm−1 occurs at 1.5 M, resulting from heightened viscosity and an increased proportion of CIP and AGG species that adversely affect ion transport. In the 0.5 M electrolyte, the voltage profile exhibits two adjacent voltage plateaus connected by a "dip", indicating a quasi-solid-state sulfur reaction (QSSSR). When the concentration increases to 1.0 M, the voltage profile shows nearly equal plateaus; however, it displays a more pronounced polarization "dip", suggesting that the phase transition process encounters greater kinetic barriers due to reduced LiPS solubility. Upon further increasing the LiTFSI concentration to 1.5 M, the voltage profile presents an earlier and widened "dip", accompanied by a significantly lowered discharge potential in the later stage. This behavior is attributed to the slower ion transport and more severe kinetic limitations. Additionally, although the QSSSR process can be achieved under the low concentration of 0.5 M, the corresponding cells demonstrate a low Coulombic efficiency (97.7%) in the initial few cycles, indicating non-negligible shuttle effects that compromise cycling stability.






Figure S11 The GITT voltage profiles of Li−S cells in different electrolytes: (a) 1M EON/TTE (b) 1M F3EON/TTE (c) 1M F5EON/TTE (d) 1M F8EON/TTE. The GITT voltage profiles were measured with a 20-minute current pulse of C/40 followed by 2-hour relaxation. Inset: The detailed processing procedures of ηcon and ηact + ηohm. The concentration polarization (ηcon) was quantified as the voltage difference between the OCV after the relaxation period and the OCV at the very beginning of the relaxation. It results from the sluggish diffusion of active species from the electrolyte to the electrode surface, as well as limited Li+ diffusion within the active material. The ηact + ηohm was calculated as the voltage difference between the OCV at the very beginning of the relaxation and the voltage after current pulse. The ηohm arises from electronic and ionic resistance within the bulk electrode and electrolyte. The ηact stems from slow charge transfer kinetics at the electrode-electrolyte interface.




Figure S12 Analysis of the S cathode polarization and the ion diffusion at different stages in various electrolytes by the GITT test. (a) The sum of activation polarization (ηact) and ohmic polarization (ηohm), ηohm+ηact, (b) the concentration polarization (ηcon), and (c) the diffusion coefficient. The ηohm arises from electronic and ionic resistance within the bulk electrode and electrolyte. The ηcon results from sluggish diffusion of active species from the electrolyte to the electrode surface, as well as limited Li+ diffusion within the active material. The ηact stems from slow charge transfer kinetics at the electrode-electrolyte interface. At the initial stage of discharge, EON/TTE and F5EON/TTE cells exhibit similar ηohm+ηact and ηcon values. At the beginning of the second discharge plateau, ηcon increases sharply, indicating highly sluggish ion diffusion. Due to the extremely low solubility of LiPSs in F5EON/TTE and F8EON/TTE electrolytes, the sluggish ion transport is likely dominated by Li+ diffusion between two distinct solid-phase products. During charging, the F5EON/TTE cell shows the lowest polarization, attributed to stable interfacial ion diffusion. The diffusion coefficients suggest enhanced Li+ mobility under the solid-liquid-solid transformation mechanism.



Figure S13 (a) The contour plot of the XRD patterns for a Li–S cell sing F5EON/TTE electrolyte and its corresponding voltage profiles. (b) The quantitative evaluation of solid-state sulfur species (S8 and Li2S) as a function of DOD and SOC. The sulfur content is determined by the peak area of the main diffraction peak ((222) plane for S8 in F5EON/TTE electrolyte, (222) plane for S8 during discharge and (311) plane for S8 during charge in F5EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte, and the (111) plane for Li2S). The evolution of S and Li2S as a function of depth of discharge (DOD) and state of charge (SOC) was quantified by integrating the main diffraction peaks from XRD patterns. During discharge, the F5EON/TTE electrolyte promotes more rapid consumption and earlier depletion (~41% DOD) of crystalline α-S8 (hollow markers in Figure S8b). Simultaneously, it triggers earlier nucleation of Li2S at ~56% DOD. In contrast, the presence of FEC retards both the consumption of S8 and the nucleation of Li2S (solid markers in Figure S8b). Additionally, Li2S formed in FEC-based electrolytes exhibits a more amorphous structure. This phenomenon is primarily ascribed to the CEI formed via the reaction between FEC and LiPSs, which impedes electron and ion transport, thereby retarding sulfur conversion and Li2S nucleation. Similarly, the CEI-coated electrode exhibits delayed Li2S depletion and affects sulfur reformation during the charging process. Although S8 (α-S8) re-emerges in both electrolytes, the kinetics of its reformation differ significantly. The CEI-induced ion transport barrier delays the nucleation, forming the α-S8 with poor crystallinity. Furthermore, the crystalline orientation of regenerated α-S8 is significantly altered, as evidenced by the changed (222)/(311) ratio, which is absent in F5EON/TTE cells. These near-amorphous species may exhibit improved reactivity and reversibility, alleviating the kinetic constraints imposed by lattice disruption.


Figure S14 The voltage profiles of the Li|Si half-cell with conventional carbonate electrolyte containing 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC/FEC. The Si electrode delivers an initial specific capacity of 2070 mA h g−1 with a Coulombic efficiency of 78.74%. In subsequent cycles, the capacity stabilizes at approximately 1765 mA h g−1, indicating the formation of stable SEI.




Figure S15 (a) The voltage profiles of the Si−LiSi cells using various electrolytes with a 0.5 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h cm−2 at 35 °C. (b-c) The enlarged voltage profiles of the Si−LiSi cells at different cycling: (b) 54-77 h, (c) 221-241 h. The Si electrode was prelithiated to 3.0 Ah in a conventional carbonate-based electrolyte to prepare LiSi electrode. The LiSi electrode was then paired with a pristine Si electrode to assemble the Si−LiSi cell. The cells were initially cycled for two times at 0.25 mA cm−2, followed by long-term cycling at 0.5 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 2 mA h cm−2. The cell with EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte exhibits a rapid voltage rise to 3 V within 92 hours. After 230 hours, the voltage abruptly drops and fluctuates due to micro-short circuits induced by active material detachment caused by the repeated expansion/contraction of the Si electrode. In contrast, the cell with F5EON/TTE-FEC exhibits a gradual increase in overpotential to 4 V over 250 hours, indicating enhanced cycling stability due to the formation of a stable SEI.




Figure S16 Electrochemical performance of LiSi–S cells with different FEC-free electrolytes: (a) Initial voltage profiles of LiSi–S cells at a charge/discharge rate of 0.1C. (b-d) The voltage profiles at the 1st, 5th, and 20th cycles for cells at 0.2C using EON/TTE-FEC (b), F3EON/TTE-FEC (c), and F5EON/TTE-FEC electrolytes (d). (e) The capacity retention of cells with different FEC-free electrolytes at 0.2C and 35 °C. In the absence of FEC, the EON/TTE-based cell shows rapid voltage hysteresis and severe capacity fade due to shuttle effects and interfacial instability. The F3EON/TTE electrolyte mitigates these issues to some extent. In contrast, F5EON/TTE and F8EON/TTE cells exhibit more stable voltage plateaus, suggesting enhanced electrode interfacial stability. However, the capacity remains limited, retaining only 33.83% after 200 cycles. These results highlight the critical role of FEC in stabilizing the electrode interface and improving the cycling lifespan.




Figure S17 (a) The voltage profiles of the LiSi−S cells at initial cycle with discharge/charge rate of 0.1C. (b-c) The voltage profiles of the cell at the 1st, 5th, and the 20th cycle in EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte (b) at 0.2 C. As the number of fluorine substituents increases, the discharge voltage decreases due to the sluggish kinetics caused by the reduced LiPSs solubility. In the EON/TTE-FEC and F3EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte, cells still exhibit a multi-step transformation mechanism, following a solid-liquid-solid reaction pathway. In contrast, cells with F5EON/TTE-FEC electrolytes demonstrate a quasi-solid-state sulfur redox mechanism. The voltage profiles of the cell in EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte reveal rapid capacity fading and increased polarization over 20 cycles, indicating an unstable interphase associated with the EON solvent.




Figure S18 (a) The voltage profiles and (b) the cycling performance of LiSi−S cells at 0.1C with S loading of 2.3 mg cm−2 and 11 µL mg−1. The cell delivers a specific capacity of 892.2 mA h g−1 under a sulfur loading of 2.3 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 11 µL mg−1. After 50 cycles, a capacity of 807.4 mA h g−1 is retained, with an average Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.8%.





Figure S19 The F1s XPS spectra of the LiSi anode after 20 cycles in different electrolytes. The F1s spectra reveal that fluorine species mainly comprise inorganic LiF and organic C–F compounds. The elements quantified analysis further confirms that the SEI formed in the F5EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte contains higher amounts of LiF inorganic components and organic components originating from the decomposition of TFSI⁻ and FEC. The mechanical flexibility of the organic components and the grain boundary conductivity of the inorganic LiF accommodate the volume changes of the LiSi electrode, thereby enhancing long-term cycling stability.
