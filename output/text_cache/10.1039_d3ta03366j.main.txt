Mechanistic insights into trisulfur radical
generation in lithium–sulfur batteries†
Xu Han
and Xuefei Xu
*
Trisulfur radicals (LiS3
$ and S3
$) are crucial reaction intermediates that can activate new reaction pathways
to facilitate sulfur species conversion in liquid electrolytes of lithium–sulfur batteries. Understanding the
complicated solution chemistry of trisulfur radical generation can help to discover new strategies for
improving sulfur conversion kinetics. In this work, we employ density functional theory (DFT) to
investigate the generation mechanism of trisulfur radicals in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL) solvents. DMSO solvent with a higher dielectric constant and a larger donor number has
a stronger lithiophilicity than DOL and can weaken the Li–S bonds of Li-containing polysulﬁdes more
eﬃciently, resulting in the discrepancy that Li2S6 can dissociate into LiS6
 and S6
2 in DMSO, while
keeping a neutral state in DOL. The computed reaction energetics suggest that hexasulﬁdes (Li2S6, LiS6
,
and S6
2) are thermodynamically feasible to generate trisulfur radicals in DMSO, and the generation
reaction would be more exergonic when hexasulﬁdes are more fully solvated. However, the formation of
trisulfur radicals in DOL is always unfavorable. Possible reaction mechanisms to produce trisulfur radicals
are further proposed, and the computed maximum free energy barriers along the proposed pathways
are 9.62 and 19.08 kcal mol−1 in DMSO and DOL, respectively, that explains the solvent-dependence of
trisulfur radical generation from the kinetic aspect. This work provides a deeper understanding of
solution-based sulfur chemistry and contributes to electrolyte optimization for high-rate polysulﬁde
conversion.
1.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, with an ultrahigh theoretical
energy density as high as 2600 W h kg−1, have proven to be
a promising alternative to traditional lithium-ion batteries.1,2 As
opposed to the lithium-ion (de)intercalation mechanism in
lithium-ion batteries, the cathode reaction of Li–S batteries
involves the redox conversion of sulfur and lithium polysuldes
(LiPSs). Some LiPSs (Li2Sx, 2 < x # 8) can dissolve into liquid
electrolytes. Such inevitable dissolution would lead to the shut-
tling of LiPSs into the anode side and the subsequent undesirable
parasitic reactions with the lithium metal, which eventually
damages the capacity and cycling stability of batteries.3–6
To suppress this shuttling phenomenon, tremendous eﬀorts
have been dedicated to reducing LiPS dissolution by strength-
ening
their
anchoring
and
conversion
on
the
cathode
surface.7–12 However, through this surface-based strategy, it is
hard to fully escape the kinetic constraints of cathode materials
due to the insulation nature of sulfur bulk deposits.13,14 The
polysulde-assisted
solution-based
pathway
provides
an
alternative solution to inhibit the shuttle eﬀect, as it allows for
the dissolution of LiPSs but aﬀords an electrolyte environment
for enhancing interactions of sulfur species.15–20 This strategy
guarantees a greater improvement of LiPS conversion kinetics
than the surface-based one as theoretically claimed: (i) the
soluble sulfur species in electrolytes drastically alleviate the
dependence of triple-phase interfaces for redox reactions, and
solution surroundings can enable more smooth mass/charge
transport;
(ii)
solvents
serve
as
mediators
participating
directly in the conversion of sulfur species and activate addi-
tional reaction paths involving S3$ radicals to facilitate the
eﬀective utilization of sulfur.15,18,21,22
Since S3$ radicals serve as a key redox intermediate to
enhance sulfur interconversion reactions, probing into their
origins can contribute to the understanding of the sulfur redox
mechanism and to the electrolyte optimization of Li–S batteries.
It is generally accepted that S3$ is produced by the S–S bond
cleavage of S62.23,24 Concerning that in situ experiments are
diﬃcult to distinguish the lithiation states of dissolved poly-
suldes (e.g., neutral molecules Li2Sx, monoanions LiSx, or
dianions Sx2),25–27 to be exact, experimentally detected radicals
are possibly in the form of either LiS3
$ or S3$, whose parent
species can be Li2S6, LiS6
, or S62. For convenience, we refer to
Li2S6, LiS6
, and S62 as hexasuldes and to LiS3
$ and S3$ as
trisulfur radicals. Interestingly, trisulfur radicals are not

dependent on the solvent types. For example, trisulfur radicals
have been proven to exist in solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO) and N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMA), while they are
rarely measurable in ether-based solvents such as 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxy ethane (DME).18,28–31 This solvent-

ancy of solvent properties including the dielectric constant (3)
and donor number (DN).16,18,30 The DN of a solvent, proposed by
Gutmann,32 is dened as the negative reaction enthalpy for the
1 : 1 adduct formation between the solvent (sol) and the stan-

sol + SbCl5 / sol −SbCl5, DN = −DH (kcal mol−1)
(1)
where the solvent sol and SbCl5 are both diluted in the reference
solvent 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) at 25 °C. A higher DN value
reects stronger binding strength of the solvent sol as the Lewis
base.
Theoretical tools play a vital role in explaining this experi-
mental observation. Based on DFT methods, Du et al. revealed
the tendency that the higher the 3 and DN of solvents, the more
favorable the formation of trisulfur radicals.33 Zhang et al.
calculated the dissociation constants from Li2S6 to LiS3
$ and
displayed the negligible concentration of LiS3
$ in low 3/DN DME
solvent.34 Nevertheless, how 3 and DN of solvents inuence the
formation of trisulfur radicals remains vague, and deeper
theoretical insights are required.
Moreover, the generation of free radicals in solution is
a complex process rather than just breaking S–S bonds because
the solvation environment intricately aﬀects the structures and
energetics of dissolved sulfur species. Specically, solvent
molecules explicitly bond with solutes, and the (de)solvation
energy would be distinct when solutes are coordinated by
diﬀerent numbers of solvents, namely in diﬀerent solvation
statuses
(e.g.,
non-solvated,
partially
solvated,
and
fully
solvated).35–37 Lin et al. found that varying the concentration of
electrolytes results in diﬀerent solvation statuses of poly-
suldes, thus signicantly aﬀecting the open circuit voltage of
batteries.38 Whether the solvation statuses of sulfur species
aﬀect the generation of trisulfur radicals is worthy of in-depth
exploration. Furthermore, in addition to extensive thermody-
namic studies on the conversion reactions of sulfur species in
solution,33,34,39–42 kinetic analysis is also necessary to elucidate
the mechanism. Therefore, a combined kinetics and thermo-
dynamics investigation with adequate solvation considerations
would be of crucial signicance for understanding the genera-
tion of trisulfur radicals in liquid electrolytes.
In this work, the generation reactions of trisulfur radicals in
DMSO and DOL are studied by DFT calculations. Here, DMSO
and DOL are chosen as they are respectively representative high
3/DN and low 3/DN solvents commonly used in Li–S batteries.43
We rst study the solvation behavior of reaction-relevant
species including Li2S6, LiS6
, S62, LiS3
$, S3$, and Li+. Pref-
erential solvation statuses (non-solvated, partially solvated, and
fully solvated) are identied. Based on the reaction free energies
of inter-transformation among Li2S6, LiS6
, and S62 in various
solvation statuses, the favorable lithiation forms of hexasuldes
are found to be distinct in DMSO and DOL. Additionally, the
underlying reason for this observation is discussed. Then the
decomposition reactions of hexasuldes to form trisulfur radi-
cals are evaluated from the thermodynamic aspect. In the end,
combined with a kinetic analysis, possible reaction pathways to
produce trisulfur radicals are proposed. Our study analyzes the
critical roles of solvents in the interactions with sulfur species
and demonstrates the detailed mechanisms for trisulfur radical
generation in liquid electrolytes.
2.
Computational details
All the density functional calculations were performed using
a locally modied Gaussian 0944 program with the Minnesota
Gaussian Functional Module (MN-GFM).45 The Kohn–Sham
(KS) exchange correlation functional has been carefully selected
to achieve satisfactory precision and eﬃciency with Wuhan–
Minnesota
scaling
(WMS)46
calculations
for
the
S62/S3$ þ S3$ gas-phase reaction as the benchmark. The
details are given in the ESI.† A global-hybrid meta-GGA func-
tional namely revM06 47 with ultrane integration grids was
nally utilized for all calculations. Referring to the MG3S basis
set48 and the Complete Basis Set (CBS) method,49 we utilized
a combination of Pople-type basis sets50 to obtain high accuracy
energy. Specically, we adopted 6-311+G(d,p) for all atoms
except S and 6-311+G(2d) with one extra polarization function
for S atoms in all geometry optimizations. For simplicity, we
label this group of basis sets as BASES. Aer the optimization
step, the potential energy was recalculated with a larger basis
set, that is, 6-311+G(2df,2p) for non-sulfur atoms and 6-
311+G(3d2f) for S atoms. This group of basis sets is denoted as
BASEL. The reasonability of this combination of model chem-
istry (revM06/BASEL//revM06/BASES) was evaluated and results
are shown in the ESI.† Unless otherwise stated, all potential
energies (symbol E) are calculated by using revM06/BASEL and
all free energies (symbol G) are calculated as the sum of
potential energies by using revM06/BASEL and free energy
corrections by using revM06/BASES at room temperature
(298.15 K). Basis set superposition error (BSSE)51 corrections
have been considered and found negligibly small (see Table
S4†). To simulate the solvent environment, we employed a SMD
continuous
solvation
model.52
Calculation
setups
in
the
Gaussian 09 program for diﬀerent solvent systems are provided
in the ESI.† Besides, the solvation shell of solute species was
treated with explicit solvent molecules.
In order to examine the stability of solute species solvated by
diﬀerent numbers of solvent molecules, we calculate the total
binding free energies (Gb) and the successive binding free
energies (DGb) using the following equations.

depicted in Scheme 1, three kinds of possible solvation statuses

increasing number of solvent molecules available for coordi-
nation, the solute could be successively in partially solvated and
fully solvated statuses. Note that a partially solvated solute
[Gb(n) < 0 and DGb(n + 1) < 0] only exists in the system without
uncoordinated free solvent molecules because free solvents, if
any, would spontaneously bind with this solute. Only in fully
solvated systems [Gb(n) < 0 and DGb(n + 1) > 0] can free solvent
molecules stably coexist with solutes.
3.
Results and discussion
3.1
Reactions involved in the generation processes of
trisulfur radicals
The trisulfur radicals can be formed directly via the dissociation
reaction of S62/2S3$. Based on the present calculations, in
the gas phase, this reaction is exergonic with a free energy
barrier and reaction free energy of 10.65 and −58.83 kcal mol−1,
respectively. However, in a practical electrolyte environment,
the generation of trisulfur radicals is more complicated. Firstly,
as mentioned before, S62 and S3$ may stay at diﬀerent lith-
iation states, so in addition to S62 and S3$, we also need to
consider the roles of Li2S6, LiS6
, and LiS3
$ species when
examining the presence of trisulfur radicals. Secondly, solute–
solvent interactions may play a vital role in the structures and
energetics of dissolved species, and thus the solvation eﬀect
also has to be explicitly considered. Generally, as depicted in
Scheme 2, two types of reactions are involved in the generation
processes of trisulfur radicals (LiS3
$ and S3$) from hexasuldes
(Li2S6, LiS6
, and S62). The rst type determines the prefer-
ential lithiation states of hexasuldes (see reactions R1–R3) in
diﬀerent solvents, and the second one, including R4–R9,
corresponds to the decomposition of hexasuldes to form tri-
sulfur radicals.
3.2
Solvation of relevant species
Reactions R1–R9 in Scheme 2 involve six likely species, namely
Li2S6, LiS6
, S62, LiS3
$, S3$, and Li+. The solvation structures
of these species are fully explored. For each possible solvation
status, lots of initial congurations with the coordinated solvent
molecules in diﬀerent binding sites and orientations are
designed and fully optimized, and then the most stable struc-
tures are selected for further analysis. Note that in this work we
only consider the coordinated solvents in the rst solvation
shell as the peripheral solvent shells have negligible interac-
tions with solutes.

As shown in Fig. S1–S6†, in the solvation structures of
lithium polysuldes (Li2S6, LiS6
, and LiS3
$), lithium always
acts as the bridging atom between the sulfur chain and solvent,
directly binding with the oxygen atoms in DMSO or DOL
molecules. For S62 and S3$ without lithium, their solvation is
found to be energetically unfavorable because of positive
binding free energies with DMSO or DOL (see Table 1). Namely,
S62 and S3$ are always in the non-solvated status regardless of
the content of solvent molecules in electrolytes. Such intuitive
inspection reveals that Li–solvent chemistry plays a crucial role
in the dissolution and solvation of sulfur species.
Table 1 shows that Gb(n) values in DMSO solvent are usually
more negative than those in DOL for each Li-containing species
(Li2S6, LiS6
, LiS3
$, and Li+) with the same number of coordi-
nated solvents (NCS, n), indicating a stronger solute–solvent
interaction in DMSO than in DOL. The comparison of Gb(n) in
DMSO and DOL agrees with the cognition that the DN of DMSO
(29.8 kcal mol−1)32 is larger than that of DOL (18.0 kcal mol−1).30
With the increase of NCS (n), Gb(n) rst decreases and then
increases until the saturation of solvent coordination (see
Fig. 1a and Table 1). The n value with the most negative Gb(n) is
denoted as the best NCS. Regardless of the solvent type (DMSO
or DOL), the best NCS of Li2S6, LiS6
, LiS3
$, and Li+ is deter-
mined to be 4, 2, 2, and 4, respectively. Combined with the
results of the successive binding free energies [DGb(n + 1)], we
nd that when n is smaller than the best NCS, the solute stays in
the partially solvated status because DGb(n + 1) <0. Once n
equals or exceeds the best NCS, the solute can be seen as fully
solvated. Note that, in DOL the DGb(n + 1 = 3) of Li2S6 is already
positive although the NCS of two is less than the corresponding
best NCS of four. We still classify Li2S6(DOL)2 as partially
solvated rather than fully solvated because Li2S6(DOL)2 is hard
to stably coexist with more than one free solvent molecule
[DGb(4) < 0]. As for LiS6
 (DOL)2, we consider it to be fully
solvated because three DOL molecules fail to coexist in the rst
solvation shell of LiS6
 based on our geometry optimizations.
Corresponding optimized structures with the best NCS are
shown in Fig. 2 and S11†. All of these structures exhibit
a tetrahedral coordination mode of Li, in which central lithium
atoms are directly bound to nearby oxygen or sulfur atoms.
Previous studies also demonstrated this extremely robust four-
coordinated
feature
in
the
solvation
of
Li+
or
lithium
polysuldes.34,53–55 Note that, as shown in Fig. 1b, the DGb(n) of
Li+ starts to become positive when n exceeds four, indicating

Li+(sol)4. However, Li+ is still likely to aggregate more than four
solvent molecules together owing to the negative Gb values of
Li+(sol)5 and Li+(sol)6, whose remarkable stability is mainly
attributed to the intermolecular electrostatics and dispersion
eﬀect (see Table S6†).

a measure of the donor properties of solvents, approximately
represents the lithiophilicity of solvents in the solvation systems
with Li–solvent interactions dominant. The solvent molecule
and sulfur chain are competitive in binding with Li+ so that
increasing the DN of solvents would cause Li+ to transit from
a sulfur-preferred to a solvent-preferred coordination state. The
donor properties of a molecule are also directly related to its
electrostatic potential values.58 We thus further map the iso-
surface of electrostatic potential to intuitively compare the DN
of DMSO and DOL. As apparent in Fig. 3, the oxygen atom
region of DMSO exhibits more negative electrostatic potential
than that of DOL, further inducing more powerful binding of Li+
to DMSO, which is reected in the more negative Gb(n = 1) of Li+
with one DMSO molecule (−14.54 kcal mol−1) compared to that
with one DOL molecule (−10.02 kcal mol−1).
Overall, DMSO solvent with a higher 3 and DN and accord-
ingly with stronger lithiophilicity contributes to larger polarity
and weakness of Li–S bonds, so that Li+ separates more easily
from the sulfur chain, being benecial to the dissociation of
Li2S6 to LiS6
 and S62. In contrast, DOL with a low 3 and DN
makes Li–S bond breaking rather improbable, and Li2S6 thereby
remains neutral in DOL.
3.4
Generation of trisulfur radicals
3.4.1
Thermodynamic
feasibility.
Aer identifying the

unfavorable lithiation state Li2S6 as the reactant because the
dissociation of Li2S6 into LiS6

3.3. The results of R4–R8 are shown in Tables S11–S15†. Reac-

mol−1. Exergonic paths of R4–R6 and R9 reactions in DMSO

neous through the fragmentation of solvated Li2S6 or of S62.
However, in DOL, it is thermodynamically unfavorable to
generate trisulfur radicals, as the DGr values of R4–R6 are all
positive. As shown in Table 3, which lists the lowest DGr value
for each kind of trisulfur radical generation reaction with the
reactant in a specic solvation status, the more complete the
solvation of hexasuldes, the more thermodynamically feasible
the generation of trisulfur radicals. The trend is similar to that
of the reactions R1–R3 observed in Table 2. Although it is not
favorable to form trisulfur radicals in DOL, we note that in Table
3, some DGr values of R4 in DOL are relatively low (even as low
as 3.70 kcal mol−1), implying that LiS3

LiS3

sulfur species is also benecial to produce trisulfur radicals.
Specically, the solvation statuses (non-solvated, partially
solvated, and fully solvated) of solute species aﬀect the reac-
tiveness. The higher the degree of solvation of hexasuldes, the
more thermodynamically favorable the generation of trisulfur
radicals.

Kinetic feasibility and mechanisms. Based on the
computed reaction energetics, we have identied some reac-
tions with negative DGr in DMSO (see Tables 3 and S11–S13†)
that are energetically favorable to generate trisulfur radicals
from a thermodynamic standpoint. To gain a more thorough
understanding of these reactions, a kinetic analysis is con-
ducted, and probable reaction mechanisms from hexasuldes
to trisulfur radicals are therefore proposed.
To produce trisulfur radicals from hexasuldes, reaction
barriers have to be overcome for breaking Li–S and S–S bonds.
As presented in Table S7,† the dissociation of the S–S bond
always consumes more energy than that of the Li–S bond.
Moreover, solvent molecules can directly bind with lithium to
weaken the Li–S bonds but have little eﬀect on S–S bonds.
Therefore, we expect that it is kinetically easier to break Li–S
bonds than S–S bonds. In this work, we focus on determining
the rate-determining step to assess the kinetic feasibility of the
whole reaction pathway, so we limit our kinetic analysis to the
S–S bond breaking steps.

4.
Conclusions
We explore the generation of trisulfur radicals in DMSO and
DOL solvents by DFT calculations. Two types of reactions
involved in the radical generation processes are summarized.
The rst type determines the preferred lithiation state of hex-
asuldes in solution, and the second one refers to the decom-
position of hexasuldes to form trisulfur radicals. The solvation
behavior of relevant species in these reactions, including Li2S6,
LiS6
, S62, LiS3
$, S3$, and Li+, are adequately explored. We
nd that the Li-containing species all prefer the solvated status,
in which the Li–solvent chemistry plays a critical role, and the
tetrahedral coordination conguration of lithium is revealed to
be the most stable. For S62 and S3$ without lithium, they are
always in non-solvated status, and their solvation is energeti-
cally unfeasible.
The preferential lithiation states of hexasuldes (Li2S6,
LiS6
, and S62) are found to be distinct in DMSO and DOL. Due
to the larger 3 and DN and the consequent stronger lith-
iophilicity of DMSO than DOL, Li–S bonds are more weakened
and Li+ are more strongly bound by solvent molecules in DMSO,
resulting in the spontaneous dissociation of Li2S6 into LiS6

and S62. In contrast, hexasuldes keep mainly in a neutral
state (Li2S6) in DOL. The computed reaction energetics from
hexasuldes to trisulfur radicals also heavily depend on the
solvent type. In particular, trisulfur radicals are very likely to be
produced in DMSO, and the generation would be thermody-
namically easier when hexasuldes are more fully solvated.
However, the formation of trisulfur radicals is always unfavor-
able in DOL.
Possible mechanisms for generating trisulfur radicals in
DMSO and DOL are proposed, along with a kinetic analysis. The
generation process is highly complex and involves steps such as
(de)solvation, delithiation, Li–S bond cleavage, and S–S bond
cleavage. The maximum free energy barriers along the proposed
pathways are computed to be 9.62 kcal mol−1 in DMSO and
19.08 kcal mol−1 in DOL, respectively. The results indicate that
the generation of trisulfur radicals in DMSO is feasible from
both thermodynamic and kinetic perspectives.
In summary, compared to DOL, DMSO with a higher 3 and
DN facilitates the dissociation of Li2S6 into LiS6
 and S62 and
subsequent formation of LiS3
$ and S3$. Moreover, the full
solvation of sulfur species is also benecial to produce trisulfur
radicals. Our mechanistic investigation highlights the advan-
tages of electrolytes with high 3/DN solvents and fully solvated
polysuldes in facilitating trisulfur radical generation. Consid-
ering the crucial role of trisulfur radicals in sulfur conversion
reactions, this work is expected to enlighten the electrolyte
design toward high kinetic Li–S batteries.
