Molecular Design of Nitrile Electrolytes Enabling Lithiated
Silicon–Sulfur Batteries with Quasi-Solid-State Sulfur Reaction
Mengxue He, Yunpeng Fu, Lujun Zhu, Yue Ma, Chenxi Zheng, Guo Ye, Zhitong Xiao,
Yongfeng Jia, Xin Gao, Mingchuan Luo, Kenneth Ozoemena, Mohammadhosein Safari,
Shaojun Qiu, Jinglun Wang,* and Quanquan Pang*
Abstract: The development of lithium–sulfur (Li−S) batteries is hindered by the polysulﬁde dissolving, cross-over and the
inherent lithium metal anode instability. We herein instead describe a lithiated silicon−sulfur (LiSi−S) battery enabled
by molecular engineering of highly solvating nitrile electrolytes toward weakly solvating to fundamentally decouple
the reactions of the two electrodes and eliminate their cross-talk. Speciﬁcally, by controlled ﬂuorination of the ethoxy-
nitrile base solvent, the charge distribution on the solvent is manipulated which suppresses the solvation for polysulﬁdes
promoting a quasi-solid-state sulfur reaction (QSSSR) mechanism. The promoted anion participation in Li+ solvation,
along with the ﬂuoroethylene carbonate additive, further stabilizes the interphases at both sulfur cathode and LiSi anode
mitigating the mechanical degradations. The QSSSR-based LiSi−S cell shows a high capacity of 1499.0 mA h gsulfur−1 at
0.1C, and achieves a high capacity retention of 90.2% over 100 cycles at 0.2C with an average Coulombic efﬁciency of
99.9%. This work highlights the essence of molecular engineering for manipulating the primary reactions and interphasial
behaviors at both electrodes toward high performance sulfur batteries.

LiSi–S batteries. Conventional ether electrolytes based on
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) have
been predominantly used for Li–S batteries,[16] but they lead
to SEI with poor mechanical robustness in light of the large

enable a relatively more stable SEI for silicon anodes, these
electrolytes undergo extensive nucleophilic reactions with

their high oxidation stability, high thermal stability, and low
ﬂammability, are attractive as electrolyte solvents, promot-
ing safety under extreme conditions such as over-charging
and high temperature operations.[20,21] However, primitive
nitrile electrolytes are susceptible to reductive decomposi-
tions at the anodes (graphite, silicon, and LMA). Recent
studies have demonstrated that high-concentration nitrile-
based electrolytes (HCEs) show improved compatibility with
both lithium metal and graphite anodes.[22,23] Moreover,
tuning the Li salt/acetonitrile molar ratio was reported to
suppress LiPSs dissolution and promote QSSSR,[24] but
the high viscosity, poor ion transport, and high cost of
HCEs hinder their commercial viability. Molecular design
of functionalized nitrile-based solvents and electrolytes
represents a unique opportunity, while being challenging,
for developing high-performance and yet high-safety sulfur
batteries.[25]
In this study, we describe a molecular engineering
approach for designing a series of ﬂuorinated nitrile sol-
vents integrating the beneﬁcial properties of acetonitrile
and hydroﬂuoroethers. With precise molecular modiﬁcation
with electron-withdrawing ﬂuorine, the charge density of
the nitrile group is lowered, which weakens coordination
with Li+ while strengthening Li+–anion interactions. The
optimized electrolyte with 3-(2,2,3,3,3-pentaﬂuoropropoxy)
propionitrile effectively suppresses LiPSs dissolution enabling
QSSSR pathway. Furthermore, ﬂuoroethylene carbonate is
introduced to further encapsulate LiPSs via self-limited
nucleophilic reactions, while simultaneously stabilizing the
SEI of LiSi anode, mitigating the electrode mechanical
degradations. With the optimized electrolyte, the LiSi−S
cell exhibits an initial discharge capacity of over 1499 mA
h gsulfur−1 at 0.1C and maintains 90.2% capacity after
100 cycles at 0.2C with an average Coulombic efﬁciency
of 99.9%.
Results and Discussion
The Molecular Engineering of Nitrile Solvents
The short-chain nitrile molecule, acetonitrile, offers high
ionic conductivity,[26] high solvation power, and resistance
to oxidation. However, its poor reduction stability results
in undesirable formation and degradation of SEI.[27,28] In
contrast, the highly ﬂuorinated hydroﬂuoroethers, such
as
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl
ether
(TTE), exhibit complementary properties, including excellent
reduction
stability
and
inherent
nonﬂammability.[29–31]
However, TTE shows marginal solubility for lithium salt
owing to its low polarity. For LiSi−S batteries, our hypothesis
for achieving stable performance is to fully decouple the
reactions on the two electrodes and eliminate the undesired
cross-talk between sulfur and silicon. One way to achieve
this is to design weakly solvating electrolytes which restricts
solvation for LiPSs while showing good ion transport
properties and stabilizing the interphases at both electrodes.
As such, herein we develop a novel ﬂuorinated nitrile
molecule as the solvent by grafting a hydroﬂuoroether moiety
onto a propionitrile backbone, thereby combining the merits
of both nitrile and hydroﬂuoroether, which features regulated
charge distribution and solvation power. To systematically
demonstrate
the
molecule
engineering
approach,
we
investigated the inﬂuence of differently ﬂuorinated units
on the solvents’ properties and solvation structure. As such,
we synthesized a series of ﬂuorinated nitrile derivatives
(denoted as FnEON; n is the mole of ﬂuorine) with
varied
ﬂuorination:
3-(2,2,2-triﬂuoroethoxy)
propionitrile
(F3EON),
3-(2,2,3,3,3-pentaﬂuoropropoxy)
propionitrile
(F5EON),
and
3-((2,2,3,3,4,4,5,5-octaﬂuoropentyl)oxy)
propionitrile (F8EON). The nonﬂuorinated analogue, 3-
ethoxypropionitrile (EON), is also included for comparison
(Figure 1a). Their basic physio-chemical properties are listed
in Table S1. FnEON shows similar dielectric constants to that
of EON. However, F8EON displays a higher viscosity than the
other solvents. The electrolytes were prepared by dissolving
1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in
FnEON/TTE (1:1 vol%), wherein the TTE is introduced to
reduce the viscosity of electrolytes. The ionic conductivity
and viscosity of these electrolytes are presented in Figure S1.
The F5EON/TTE electrolyte exhibits the lowest viscosity
and a moderate ionic conductivity of 1.8 mS cm−1 at 30 °C,
sufﬁcient to support effective ion transport. In additional, the
electrolyte also exhibits excellent antioxidation stability and
ﬂame-retardant capability (Figures S2–S4).
The ﬂuorination of the ethoxy-nitrile base molecule can
impact on the distribution of charges and hence the Li+
solvation.[32] As such, we ﬁrst examine the electrostatic
potential (ESP) mapping of the various ﬂuorinated nitriles,
along with the classical molecules of AN, DME, and
TTE, using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As
illustrated in Figure 1b, the negative charges in AN and
DME molecules are primarily localized on the nitrile and
ether oxygens, respectively, which exhibit a very negative
ESP value of −1.69 and −1.30 eV. For EON, the ESP
value of ether-oxygen is −0.89 eV, much higher than that
in DME (−1.30 eV), owed to the electron-withdrawing
effect of the nitrile group. The nitrile group bears a more
negative charge (−1.69 eV) than the ether-oxygen, indicating
preferential coordination of nitrile with Li+. For the F3EON
molecule, the charges on both nitrile and ether-oxygen
are much reduced compared to those in EON, resulting
from the strongly electron-withdrawing ─CF3 group, which
indicates a weakly solvating nature for F3EON. The ether-
oxygen in F3EON exhibits an ESP value of −0.62 eV,
similar to that of the oxygen in the nonsolvating TTE,
suggesting that the ether-oxygen is unlikely to participate
in solvating Li+. For F5EON and F8EON molecules with
longer alkyl chain and higher ﬂuorination, the charge distri-

The Quasi-Solid-State Sulfur Reaction Mechanism
The regulated solvation structure of the nitrile electrolytes
effectively restricts solvation for LiPSs, thereby resulting in
different sulfur reaction mechanisms. As evidenced by the
optical images of electrolytes with 0.2 M Li2S8, the EON/TTE
and F3EON/TTE electrolytes appear yellow indicating disso-
lution of Li2S8 (insets of Figure 3a, Figure S7). In contrast, the
F5EON/TTE and F8EON/TTE electrolytes remain colorless
after long standing at room temperature, indicating restricted
solvation for LiPSs (insets of Figure 3b,c). The reduced
solubility of LiPSs can alter the sulfur reactions pathway and
suppress undesired cross-talk of sulfur species with the LiSi
anode, as we will discuss below.
To investigate the effect of distinct LiPS solvation envi-
ronments on the sulfur reaction mechanism, Li–S cells were
ﬁrst assembled with plain KB/S cathodes and lithium metal
anodes. The cell with EON/TTE electrolyte shows a two-
plateau discharge proﬁle with signiﬁcant overcharge and a low
Coulombic efﬁciency (CE) of 71.6%, indicating severe LiPS
shuttle effect (Figures 3a and S8a). In contrast, the cells with
F3EON/TTE and F5EON/TTE electrolytes exhibit a “quasi-
bi-plateau” discharge proﬁle at ∼2.15 V indicating a QSSSR
mechanism (Figures S8,S9 and 3b).[6] The voltage dip in the
middle of the discharge curve originates from the kinetic
barriers associated with the conversion processes between
different quasi-solid-phase products. Furthermore, the cell
with F8EON/TTE electrolyte shows a notably increased volt-

LiPSs solubility and lower electrolyte viscosity. These ﬁndings
indicate that regulated LiPSs solubility – and not necessarily
very low or none – within the frame of QSSSR is important
for facilitating both mass transport and reaction kinetics at the
sulfur cathode.
The chemical evolution of S, particularly the speciation
of LiPSs, in the ﬂuorinated nitrile electrolyte was further
examined using synchrotron X-ray absorption ﬁne structure
spectroscopy (XAFS). At the initial stage of discharge,
a characteristic peak at 2471.0 eV attributed to LiPSs
emerges and its intensity increases as the discharge pro-
ceeds (Figure 3d).[38,39] Meanwhile, the peak at 2472.5 eV
(attributed to S0) gradually diminishes and the peak at
2476.0 eV (attributed to Li2S) emerges.[40] These results
conﬁrm the formation of LiPSs as the intermediate during
discharge instead of a direct bi-phase reaction between S0 to
Li2S. Similarly, the XAFS spectra on charging clearly show the
formation of LiPSs intermediate as Li2S is gradually oxidized
back to elemental S0 (Figure 3d). Therefore, LiPSs are indeed
formed during cycling, which justiﬁes the manipulation of
LiPS solvation by molecular engineering of the nitrile solvents
as discussed above.
Electrochemical Performances and Interphase of LiSi−S Cells
The other challenge for LiSi–S battery is the notable vol-
ume breathing effect of both silicon and sulfur electrodes.
Interphase engineering is hence essential for alleviating the
mechanical degradation of the cell, and one effective way to
achieve this is by electrolyte additive design.[41,42] Here we
demonstrate the strategy of electrolyte additive design for
realizing formation of robust interphases for both electrodes.
Fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) is known to favor formation
of an robust SEI for silicon anodes, and also of sulfur cathode–
electrolyte interphase (CEI) by self-contained reaction with
LiPSs during cycling.[18,19] Such interphases further restrict
cross-talk of LiPSs with the lithiated silicon anodes, fully
decoupling the reactions and interphase formation of the two
electrodes.
The LiPSs, being nucleophilic in nature, can react
with FEC, forming an interphase rich with LiF and Li-
polycarbonate.[18,43] This ion-conductive CEI would serve as a
protecting layer and create nano-conﬁned reactors regulating
the undesired spatial re-distribution of sulfur species.[44]
Furthermore, we found that the FEC-induced CEI inﬂuences
both the redox pathways and the discharge/charge products.
Our operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) studies reveals that
the FEC-derived CEI facilitates a more smooth consumption
of S8 and Li2S nucleation (Figures 3e, S13), indicating
prolonged presence of LiPSs intermediates to mediate the
QSSSR. Additionally, the FEC facilitates formation of a more
disordered Li2S and α-S8 with altered crystal orientation
(Figure 3f, detailed in Figure S13). These highly disor-
dered species exhibit enhanced reactivity and reversibility as
discussed below.
The LiSi anodes were prepared via electrochemical pre-
lithiation of Si electrodes (Figure S14). First, we examined the
electrochemical behavior of symmetric Si–LiSi half cells. The
cells with F5EON/TTE-FEC exhibit rather stable evolution
of overpotential over 250 h, indicating the formation of a
stable SEI that mitigates electrode degradation (Figure S15),
whereas the cell with EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte shows
rapidly increasing overpotential to 3 V within 92 h of cycling
(Figure S15).
For the LiSi–S cells, 1% of FEC is added into the
FnEON/TTE electrolytes and a negative-to-positive capacity

Conclusion
In this study, we present a molecular engineering approach to
develop high-performance LiSi–S batteries through rational
design of ﬂuorinated nitrile electrolytes. By precisely tuning
molecular structures, polar nitrile groups are integrated with
nonpolar hydroﬂuoroether segments. The incorporation of
electron-withdrawing ﬂuorine lowers the electron density of
the nitrile group, thereby enhancing Li+–anion coordination.
The F5EON/TTE electrolyte facilitates a QSSSR mechanism
by suppressing the LiPS dissolution. The FEC further encap-
sulates the LiPSs by forming a CEI via nucleophilic reaction
with LiPSs and stabilizing the SEI at the LiSi anode, thereby
mitigating shuttle effects and silicon anode volume expansion.
The optimized F5EON/TTE-FEC electrolyte delivers stable
electrochemical performance, remaining 90.2% of its initial
capacity after 100 cycles at 0.2C with high average CE of
99.9%. These ﬁndings provide fundamental insights into elec-
trolyte design toward the practical deployment of high-energy
LiSi–S batteries.
