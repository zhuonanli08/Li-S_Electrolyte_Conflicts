A B S T R A C T
With rapid advances in portable electronics and electric vehicles, demand for electrochemical energy storage 
systems with higher capacity and longer lifespan has grown considerably. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have 
attracted substantial research interest as a promising post-lithium-ion energy storage technology, primarily 
because of their remarkably high theoretical specific capacity. Currently, a key challenge for Li-S batteries lies in 
the polysulfide shuttle phenomenon, where soluble lithium polysulfides migrate between electrodes during 
charge-discharge cycles. This migration compromises cycling performance and poses a major barrier to com­
mercial viability. Building a protective layer on the sulfur cathode has emerged as a facile and effective approach 
to mitigate this problem. In this study, 1,3,6-hexanetrinitrile (HTN) was employed as an electrolyte additive in 
Li-S batteries. Through the coordination between the cyano groups in HTN and the cobalt atoms in the cathode 
material, HTN adsorption onto the sulfur cathode surface was promoted, forming a stable protective cathode- 
electrolyte interphase that effectively suppressed the diffusion of polysulfides into the electrolyte. Benefiting 
from this protective structure, the HTN-modified Li-S cell maintained a capacity retention of 73.7% after 120 
cycles at 0.2 C. Furthermore, no polysulfide dissolution from the cathode was detected after one week of im­
mersion in dimethyl ether solution. These findings offer a novel electrolyte engineering approach for improving 
cycling stability of Li-S batteries.
1. Introduction
Li-S batteries represent one of the most intensively studied candi­
dates to succeed conventional lithium-ion systems, offering a theoretical 
gravimetric energy of 2600 Wh kg–1 and theoretical specific capacity of 
1675 mAh g–1. Beyond their superior energy metrics, these batteries 
benefit from sulfur's abundance, cost-effectiveness, and minimal envi­
ronmental impact, making them particularly appealing for sustainable 
energy applications [1–3]. Nevertheless, simultaneously achieving high 
energy density and reliable long-term cycling stability in Li-S batteries 
remains a formidable challenge. Beyond the inherent instability of the 
lithium metal anode, poor sulfur utilization and insufficient cathode 
reversibility place substantial limitations on the practical deployment of 
Li-S systems [4,5]. Furthermore, in conventional ether-based electro­
lytes, the sulfur cathode proceeds through a "dissolution-deposition" 
electrochemical pathway in which the intermediates—lithium poly­
sulfides—are highly soluble. This solubility causes active material loss, 
lithium anode corrosion, and the shuttle effect, eventually causing 
accelerated capacity fading and degraded Coulombic efficiency [6–9]. 
The shuttle effect degrades anode surface morphology, and the ongoing 
growth of lithium dendrites reduces battery performance [10–14]. 
Among these challenges, the polysulfide shuttle phenomenon consti­
tutes the foremost obstacle impeding wider commercial adoption of Li-S 
technology.

intermediates, numerous approaches have been explored, including

For example, succinonitrile (SN) as a co-solvent can expand the elec­
trochemical window to 4.7 V, while 1,3,6-hexanetrinitrile (HTCN) co­
ordinates with metal ions on the cathode surface through a "lattice 
coupling mechanism," lowering the O 2p band center energy and 
inhibiting the generation of highly oxidizing substances. This allows 
LCO cells to maintain 80% capacity retention after 770 cycles at 4.55 V 
[24,25]. Multicyano compounds (such as 1,3,5-pentanetrinitrile PTN) 
can also form a uniform CEI film on the cathode, reducing transition 
metal dissolution and enhancing high-temperature cycling stability. 
Mononitrile compounds like acetonitrile and butyronitrile as co-solvents 
can lower the electrolyte operating temperature limit to −20oC while 
maintaining high ionic conductivity at low temperatures (e.g., acetoni­
trile maintains 62% capacity at 1C rate)[26]. In lithium metal cell sys­
tems, cyano additives can work synergistically with salts like LiNO3 to 
form an SEI film rich in Li3N and LiF, promoting spherical lithium 
deposition. For example, after adding 1% HTCN to FGN-182 electrolyte 
(containing LiFSI/LiNO3), lithium metal symmetric cells can cycle for 
1000 h at 5 mA/cm2 without significant dendrite formation. Nitrile 
additives can also optimize lithium deposition kinetics by reducing 
interface impedance (e.g., ethylene glycol dipropylnitrile ether enables 
98.1% capacity retention after 60 cycles). In other cell systems such as 
aqueous zinc-sulfur batteries, sulfocyanated polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) 
works synergistically with iodine additives through tandem catalysis to 
reduce polysulfide dissolution, achieving 81.8% capacity retention after 
800 cycles. In sodium-ion cells, cyano-modified electrolytes can improve 
sodium dendrite growth [27–30]. To overcome these limitations, Liu 
et al. [31] proposed the formation of an in-situ CEI with carbon mate­
rials on the cathode during discharge, which resulted in improved 
electrochemical performance. However, the CEI layer suffered from 
limited conductivity and insufficient mechanical strength to withstand 
long-term cycling. Hence, developing more robust and scalable in-situ 
cathode coating strategies remains an urgent research priority. Xin 
et al. [32] designed an electrolyte capable of simultaneously forming an 
in-situ LiF-Li3N composite interface on both the lithium anode and SPAN 
cathode surfaces. The CEI formed on the cathode side effectively sup­
pressed Li2S formation and polysulfide shuttling, enabling stable Li-S 
battery operation with no degradation over 800 cycles. S-KB-LiTFSI 
composite materials were developed by introducing LiTFSI into S-KB to 
form an in-situ LiF interface containing Li3N components during cycling, 
thereby enhancing the performance of all-solid-state Li-S batteries[33]. 
Wang and colleagues [34] designed an in-situ electrochemical poly­
merization electrolyte, that formed a fast ion-conducting poly­
ether-based CEI on the S@pPAN cathode. Through CEI component 
optimization, a 99.5% capacity retention after 400 cycles was achieved, 
along with ultra-high Coulombic efficiency and strong fast-charging 
capability. Although substantial progress has been made in addressing 
lithium polysulfide dissolution, challenges such as limited conductivity, 
low mechanical strength, complex preparation processes, and high costs 
still remain. Therefore, finding more efficient and robust solutions 
continues to be a critical objective in this field.
In this work, 1,3,6-hexanetrinitrile (HTN) was incorporated as a 
multifunctional electrolyte modifier for Li-S battery systems. The 
strongly polar cyano moieties of HTN interact with cobalt (Co) centers in 
the cathode, promoting selective adsorption of HTN onto the sulfur 
cathode surface and forming a robust CEI. This interfacial protective 
layer markedly restricts polysulfide dissolution and diffusion into the 
electrolyte, suppresses parasitic reactions at the lithium anode, and 
thereby extends lithium metal anode service life. Moreover, the lone 
electron pairs on the cyano nitrogen atoms complex with lithium ions 
(Li+), facilitating Li+ migration, promoting uniform lithium deposition, 
and mitigating lithium dendrite formation. As a result, the HTN- 
containing battery cell achieved a capacity retention rate of 73.7% 
after 120 cycles at 0.2 C, which is 15.5% higher than that of the refer­
ence cell prepared without HTN addition. This work demonstrates an in- 
situ cathode passivation strategy based on HTN addition, offering new 
insight into electrolyte engineering for long-cycle-life Li-S batteries.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of electrolyte additives
The blank electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1.0 M lithium bis 
(trifluorosulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.1 M LiNO3 in a 1:1 vol ratio 
mixture of 1,3-dioxane (DOL) and dimethyl ether (DME). Subsequently, 
1,3,6-hexanetrinitrile (HTN) was dissolved into the prepared electrolyte 
at target concentrations of 40,60 and 80 mM, respectively.
2.2. Dissolution test
To investigate the dissolution of species, positive electrodes were 
collected after 100 cycles (0.2 C) and soaked in 5 mL of DME for one 
week. The obtained solutions were then characterized by UV–visible 
spectroscopy to compare the reference cell with the HTN-modified cell.
2.3. Theoretical arithmetic
All quantum chemical computations employed the B3LYP density 
functional as implemented in Gaussian 09, using the 6–31G(D) basis set 
supplemented with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction (em=gd3bj). 
Geometry optimizations were verified to be true energy minima by 
confirming the absence of imaginary frequencies. Molecular orbital 
isosurfaces for the HOMO and LUMO were rendered using Multiwfn in 
conjunction with Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD).
3. Results and discussion
To probe the surface composition and chemical bonding state of the 
HTN-modified cathode, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was 
applied to cycled electrode surfaces following 50 cycles at room tem­
perature (Fig. 1). Through peak fitting analysis of the C 1 s spectrum, 
compared to the reference material (Fig. 1a), the XPS spectrum of the 
HTN-modified electrode (Fig. 1b) exhibited two new characteristic 
signal peaks at binding energies of 286.3 eV and 291.3 eV. The peak 
observed at 286.3 eV corresponds to C–––N triple bond vibrations, 
providing direct evidence that cyano functional groups from 1,3,6-hex­
anetricarbonitrile were incorporated into the electrode interface layer 
structure [35]. The peak at 291.3 eV corresponds to the Co-C bond, 
indicating coordination between the cyano groups in HTN and cobalt in 
the cathode material, resulting in the formation of Co-C bonds [36]. 
Through Co-C coordination bonding, HTN constructs a durable protec­
tive layer on the sulfur cathode that effectively blocks polysulfide 
dissolution and transport into the electrolyte. Simultaneously, the ni­
trogen atoms in Co-NC form Li-N bonds with lithium polysulfides, 
chemically anchoring these species and inhibiting their outward diffu­
sion [37]. The N 1 s spectra (Fig. 1c-d) display a peak at 398.3 eV 
consistent with Li-N bonding, corroborated by the Li 1 s spectrum 
(Fig. 1e) [38]. A new component at 401.4 eV appears in the 
HTN-modified cycled electrode, attributable to C–––N groups [39] 
(Fig. 1d). The simultaneous observation of C–––N-related signals in both 
C 1 s and N 1 s spectra strongly supports incorporation of HTN into the 
cathode interfacial layer. Co 2p XPS analysis (Fig. S1) identifies three 
resolved components at 780.0, 781.2, and 782.1 eV, assigned to Co0, 
Co3+, and Co2+, respectively. The Co0 component indicates formation 
of metallic cobalt nanoclusters, while the Co2+ signal is consistent with 
cobalt-nitrogen coordination compounds. Low valence states (Co0, 
Co2+) typically exhibit high catalytic activity, which kinetically favors 
the progression of redox reactions [40]. Comparing Fig. 1e and f shows 
that the Li 1 s peak shifts significantly to a higher binding energy. This 
shift occurs because the nitrogen atoms in cyano groups undergo sp 
hybridization, resulting in regions of high electron density [41]. When 
cyano groups coordinate with lithium atoms, the attraction of electrons

The morphological evolution of sulfur cathodes upon cycling under 
different electrolyte conditions was examined by scanning electron mi­
croscopy (SEM). Fig. 2 presents SEM images of pristine electrodes 
alongside those cycled 50 times in the baseline electrolyte and in the 
HTN-containing electrolyte. SEM analysis reveals pronounced morpho­
logical changes on the cycled electrode surfaces relative to their pristine 
state. As shown in Fig. 2a and d, the fresh electrodes exhibited a rela­
tively loose morphology. After 50 cycles, the disassembled cells con­
taining the baseline electrolyte displayed significant cracks in the sulfur 
cathode, as shown in Fig. 2b and e. This phenomenon is attributed to the 
large density difference between elemental sulfur and lithium sulfide, 
leading to structural degradation and mechanical failure of the sulfur 
cathode material during repeated charge-discharge cycles, causing rapid 
capacity decline and potential safety risks. In contrast, a protective film 
was observed on the cathode surface after the HTN-containing cell was 
disassembled after 50 cycles (Fig. 2c and f). The electrode surface in the 
reference cell was eroded by lithium sulfide, forming a disordered 
interfacial layer; while the electrode surface with HTN showed a rela­
tively dense CEI layer. This observation indicates that reaction products 
formed between HTN and polysulfides adhere firmly to the cathode 
surface. This CEI preserves internal electron transfer pathways while 
reducing insulating Li2S accumulation and improving active sulfur uti­
lization; simultaneously, it confines polysulfide redox reactions within 
the cathode region, suppressing the deleterious shuttle effect. This CEI 
layer covering the sulfur cathode surface was formed as a result of co­
ordination between HTN and Co in the carbon material. The CEI formed 
in the presence of HTN effectively suppressed the shuttle effect of pol­
ysulfides, significantly improving the cycling stability of the cell. 
Moreover, this layer effectively mitigated structural degradation during 
the charge-discharge process, slowed capacity decay, and enhanced 
battery safety. Further elemental analysis of the sulfur cathode after 
adding HTN is shown in Fig. 2(g). Elements including carbon, nitrogen, 
oxygen, fluorine, sulfur and cobalt were detected by EDS. This suggests 
that these components contributed to the CEI layer's development, with 
uniform distribution across the lithium anode surface, demonstrating 
excellent dispersion characteristics and homogeneity (Fig. 2(h-l)).

At 0.1 C (Fig. 4a), the HTN-modified cell delivers a discharge ca­
pacity of 1207.8 mAh g-1, surpassing the reference cell (1057.7 mAh g- 
1) by 14.2%. Both the high-voltage plateau capacity (QH) and low- 
voltage plateau capacity (QL) are markedly improved, reflecting HTN- 
accelerated polysulfide conversion across the entire discharge voltage 
range. Rate capability testing (Fig. 4b; 1 C = 1675 mAh g-1) shows a 
gradual capacity decrease from 1194.8 to 616.0 mAh g-1 as current 
density increases from 0.1 C to 2 C. Upon recovery to 0.2 C, the HTN cell 
regains 937.0 mAh g-1 (96.3% retention), compared with 92.1% for the 
reference. This superior rate performance is attributed to the stable 
HTN-derived interfacial coating, which reduces polysulfide loss and 
enhances electrochemical reversibility.
As illustrated in Fig. 4c and d, the HTN-modified cell demonstrates 
notably elevated voltage plateaus across various current densities when 
compared to the reference cell during charge-discharge cycling. Even at 
a high current density of 2 C, well-defined plateau characteristics were 
maintained, demonstrating the performance enhancement effect of the 
HTN additive. At 0.2 C, the HTN-containing cell capacity decreased from 
1098.8 mAh g–1 to 809.4 mAh g–1 after 120 cycles, indicating a capacity 
retention rate of 73.7%, whereas the reference cell exhibited notably 
lower stability under the identical conditions, with 58.2% retention rate 
and a remaining capacity of 520.9 mAh g–1 (Fig. 4e). Long-term 500- 
cycle testing at 0.5 C (Fig. 4f) shows that the HTN cell starts with 
730.7 mAh g-1 and maintains an average CE of 95.67%. The reference 
cell, by contrast, delivers only 628.3 mAh g-1 initially and undergoes 
rapid capacity decay with a pronounced CE drop beyond 150 cycles, 
averaging only 92.69%. This superior long-term performance stems 
from the robust HTN-derived protective layer, which restricts poly­
sulfide dissolution and migration, preserves active sulfur, and reduces 
polysulfide-lithium side reactions, collectively enhancing cycling dura­
bility. Post-cycling SEM (Fig. S4, 500 cycles) shows pronounced grooves 
and pores on the reference cathode, whereas the HTN-modified cathode 
remains smooth and compact. This morphological advantage is a direct 
result of the HTN-derived CEI suppressing lithium polysulfide shuttle, 
markedly improving cycling stability. The robust HTN-derived protec­
tive layer curtails polysulfide dissolution at the cathode interface, pre­
serves active sulfur, and reduces polysulfide-lithium corrosion, 
collectively improving cell cycling performance [45,46].

To further assess the antioxidant properties of the electrolyte and 
HTN, frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energy level calculations were 
performed for 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), ethylene glycol dimethyl ether 
(DME), and HTN. A lower LUMO energy level implies stronger reduction 
capability, while a higher HOMO energy indicates stronger oxidation 
properties. As shown in Fig. S6a, the HOMO energies of DOL, DME, and 
HTN are −6.70 eV, −6.69 eV, and −8.91 eV respectively and their 
corresponding LUMO energies are 2.36 eV, 2.49 eV, and 0.05 eV. The 
FMO calculation results indicate that both the LUMO and HOMO energy 
levels of HTN are positioned at relatively low levels, reflecting its high 
electrochemical oxidation stability. According to the Independent 
Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partitioning (IGMH) illustrated in 
Fig. S6b, van der Waals forces are present at the HTN-DOL/DME inter­
face. This synergistic interaction enhances the electrolyte's resistance to 
oxidative degradation, reducing susceptibility to decomposition 
throughout prolonged charge-discharge cycles. Consequently, the 
incorporation of HTN extends battery cycle life while substantially 
elevating operational safety performance.
Open-circuit voltage (OCV) retention tests, also known as self- 
discharge tests, were carried out to evaluate the energy storage stabil­
ity of the assembled Li-S cells (Fig. 6). Initially, all cells were charged to 
full capacity, then discharged to 2.1 V, and stored at room temperature 
for 20 days (480 h) before continuing discharge to 1.7 V. As illustrated in 
Fig. 6a, the reference cell exhibits substantial capacity loss (32.8%) 
following extended storage periods. This degradation stems from the 
ongoing dissolution of polysulfides, which leads to the continuous 
depletion of electrochemically active sulfur species and consequently 
causes self-discharge phenomena. According to Fig. 6c, the open-circuit 
voltage of the reference cell steadily declined during storage, confirming 
pronounced self-discharge behavior. By contrast, the cell containing the 
HTN additive exhibited only an 18.1% reduction in capacity following
