Magnetic Control of Electrolyte Trapping Polysulﬁde for
Enhanced Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Yongan Cao,1 Qiao Wu,1 Yuchao Chen,1 Dong Chen,1 Chenlong Liu,1 Xiaoqian Hao,2
Tianjiao Zhu,1 Bo Zhang,1 Jiaxuan Zou,1 and Wenju Wang1,z
1School of Energy and Power Engineering, Nanjing University of Science and Technology, Nanjing 210094, People’s
Republic of China
2School of Science, Nanjing University of Science and Technology, Nanjing 210094, People’s Republic of China
Lithium sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered one of the most promising energy storage devices due to their high speciﬁc capacity,
pollution-free reactant, and low cost. However, the “shuttle effect” of lithium polysulﬁde (Li2Sx) leads to a fast capacity decay and
poor cycle life. Here, the magnetorheological effect (MRE) is ﬁrst applied in Li-S batteries and a magnetic control electrolyte is
designed by introducing carbonyl iron powders (CIPs) to improve the performance of Li-S batteries. According to adsorption
bonding theory, the binding energy of Fe site with Li2S4 is up to 2.68 eV via discrete Fourier transform (DFT) calculation. After
coupling an external magnetic ﬁeld, the uniform distribution of CIPs avoids the accumulation on the cathode surface. The induced
magnetic ﬁeld of spherical particles captures dissolved Sx
2−effectively by Lorenz force, which is conﬁrmed by adsorption
experiments. These magnetized particles form a magnetic shield layer in the electrolyte and alleviate the “shuttle effect.” At 0.2 C,
the initial speciﬁc capacity reaches 1296 mAh g−1. Magnetic control electrolyte provides not only a novel insight but also creates a
new possibility for mitigating the “shuttle effect” thereby promoting performance of Li-S batteries.

With the rapid development of portable consumer electronics and
electric vehicles, energy storage devices with high energy density,
good cycle stability and low cost have attracted extensive interest.1–3
Since 1990s, lithium-ion (Li-ion) battery has been commercialized
and occupied the market of energy storage devices. However, Li-ion
battery development is plagued by its theoretical limitation and can’t
meet current need.4 The replaced cathode material sulfur provides
not only ultrahigh theoretical capacity [Eq. 1, 1675 mAh g−1],
almost as ﬁve times as that of Li-ion battery, but also advantages of
high working voltage, no memory effect and pollution-free, making
lithium sulfur (Li-S) battery become one of the most prospective
energy storage devices.5,6 Nevertheless, there are still a variety of
obstacles to plague Li-S batteries to be commercialized, such as
insulation of sulfur (5 × 10−30 S cm−1 at 25 °C),7 a large volume
ﬂuctuation (80%)8 and the “shuttle effect” of long chain lithium
polysulﬁde (Li2Sx, 4 ⩽× ⩽8).9 Therein, Li2Sx is soluble in the ether-
based electrolyte around the cathode, and it can pass through the
separator and arrive at the anode surface because of the concentra-
tion difference. The dissolved Li2Sx is chemically reduced [Eq. 2] on
the surface of anode, permanently consuming both lithium metal and
active material sulfur.10–12 This problem leads to declining capacity,
short lifespan and low coulombic efﬁciency (CE), which has
seriously hampered the development of Li-S battery.13

Current strategies to alleviate the “shuttle effect”could be sorted
into two main categories. The ﬁrst one is physical conﬁnement.
Many porous materials with a high surface area were applied as the
host of sulfur, such as materials with micro-mesoporous network,14
yolk-shell structure15 and diamond-cage porous framework.16 Some
different synthetic methods of porous carbon carriers were reviewed
and summarized.17,18 Recently, chemisorption has gradually become
a research hot-spot because of its high adsorption capacity with
Li2Sx.19 Functional non-metal heteroatoms added in carbon based
substrates were conﬁrmed to help mitigate the “shuttle effect,” such
as O,20 N21 and B.22 Moreover, though discrete Fourier transform
(DFT) calculation, metal atom tended to adsorb Li2Sx more strongly,
such as W2C,23 Mo2C24 and Fe3O4.25 It’s proved that transition
metal, like iron, had a great adsorption performance with Li2Sx, and
was promising in anchoring Li2Sx and improving cyclic stability of
Li-S batteries.26 Early on, there were some applications of magnetic
nanoparticles reported to improve the performance of capacitors.27,28
For Li-S batteries, Fe/Fe3C/graphene material was used as the host
of sulfur.29 A built-in magnetic ﬁeld was formed in cathode during
cycling and altered the path of dissolved Sx
2−by Lorenz force and
had a great improvement on mitigating the “shuttle effect.” In
addition, a magnetic and mesoporous Fe3O4-modiﬁed glass ﬁber
separator was designed to block dissolved Li2Sx passing through the
separator.30
In this work, carbonyl iron powders [CIPs, Fe(CO)5], a kind of
soft magnetic particles, were used as electrolyte additive to realize
multi-functional Li2Sx trapping strategies, containing dual chemical
adsorption and physical magnetic shielding effect. CIPs, with good
magnetic saturation, have been widely applied in magnetorheolo-
gical ﬂuid and are stable in ﬂuid.31,32 According to the magnetor-
heological effect (MRE), magnetic particles can be distributed along
magnetic induction lines with the external magnetic ﬁeld, and the
distribution will be restored to its original state in a short time when
the external magnetic ﬁeld is off. As shown in Fig. 1, this experimental
is mainly divided into three parts. The adsorption effect after adding
CIPs and magnetic ﬁeld is demonstrated respectively. Firstly, due to
the high binding energy of Fe with Sx
2−and C=O with Li+,33 metallic
and nonmetallic dual chemical adsorption of CIPs is realized and Li2Sx
is anchored around the surface of cathode. The above adsorption
performance can be conﬁrmed by DFT calculation. External magnetic
ﬁeld in this experiment was provided via a permanent magnet. And
because of the MRE, the distribution becomes more regular and
uniform, and avoid the accumulation of CIPs on the surface of
cathode, which establishes a more favorable diffusion gradient.29 The
induced magnetic ﬁeld of magnetized CIPs alters the diffusion path of
the Sx
2−by adsorbing Li2Sx through Lorenz force. There is a magnetic
shielding layer formed on the surface of cathode, which prevents Li2Sx
shuttling. The conjecture is conﬁrmed intuitively by the adsorption
experiment. This work indicates that magnetic control electrolyte can
provide not only a novel insight but also develop a new possibility for
mitigating the “shuttle effect” thereby promoting the performance of
Li-S batteries.

Experimental
Preparation of materials.—As for cathode, carbon nanotube
(CNT) was used as the host of active material sulfur (S8). The mass
ratio of CNT and S8 was set to 1:3 and the mixture was grinded
evenly in a mortar. After being dried, it was put into an ampoule,
sealed and further transferred in a tubular furnace. The ampoule was
heated at 155 °C for 12 h with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1 under a
N2 atmosphere.34 After being cooled, CNT/S compound was
obtained. As for cathode slurry, CNT/S compound, carbon black
(super P and Ketjen black ECP600JD, 1:1 wt %) and water-soluble
binder (LA133) were mixed with a mass ratio of 8.0:1.5:0.5. The
above compound was grinded evenly in a mortar by using deionized
water as solvent. Controlling the viscosity of the slurry, it was coated
on an aluminum foil with a two-sided carbon layer. Then the foil was
dried at 50 °C in a vacuum drying oven overnight.
Assembly of Li-S batteries.—CR2032 cells were assembled for
following electrochemical measurements. The dried pole pieces
were used as cathode, porous polypropylene (PP) membrane as the
separator and lithium metal as the anode. In addition, 1.0 M lithium
bis (tri-ﬂuoromethanesulfone) imide (LiTFSI) into the mixture of
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxymethane (DME) with a volume
ratio of 1:1 including 1% LiNO3 was used as the electrolyte on the
anode side. The above electrolyte with 0%, 5%, 15% and 35% CIPs
was used as the electrolyte on the cathode side. All cells were
assembled in the glove box with high purity argon in which O2 and
H2O contents were below 0.01 ppm.
Material characterization.—The mass content of sulfur in CNT/S
compound was tested under argon ﬂow with a temperature ramp of
5 °C min−1 from 30 °C to 600 °C. The microstructure of CIPs and
cathode pole pieces with dropping electrolyte with CIPs were
characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, COXEM,
Korea) at l5 kV. In addition, after cycling with different contents of
CIPs, the color of the electrolyte on the side of anode was also
observed by taking some cells apart. The sulfur and carbon distribu-
tion of pole pieces was measured by energy dispersive spectroscopy
(EDS, Oxford, UK), as well the Fe distribution of the pole pieces after
dropping CIPs.
Electrochemical test of Li-S cells.—Cells with different contents
of CIPs were tested via using a LAND CT2001A cell test instrument
(LANHE, China) within the cutoff voltage of 1.7 V−2.8 V. Parts of
cells were tested upon a permanent magnet. Magnetic induction
intensity was measured by a hand-held Gauss meter. Electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) with different contents of CIPs was
measured in the frequency range from 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz at 5 mV s−1
on a CHI-604E electrochemical workstation (Chenhua, China).
Li2Sx adsorption test.—To evaluate the adsorption capacity of
CIPs to Li2Sx, three bottles containing 400 ul of 5 mM Li2S6 solution
were prepared in the glove box. The speciﬁc preparation process of
Li2S6 solution was shown in the supporting information. Therein,
one bottle was as a control group. Others contained same mass of
CIPs, but one of them was above a permanent magnet. Bottles were
sealed and dwelled in the glove box for adsorption test.
DFT calculation.—In order to theoretically prove the adsorption
of CIPs with Li2Sx, the models of Li2S4, Li2S6, Li2S8 and CIPs were
constructed,35,36 as shown in Fig. S1 (available online at stacks.iop.
org/JES/168/070510/mmedia). The binding energy of Li2Sx with
CIPs was calculated respectively by Eq. S2, containing Fe site with
Sx
2−and C=O site with Li+. In addition, CNT model was
constructed and its binding energy with Li2Sx was also calculated
as a control group. DFT calculation details were shown in the
supporting information.
Results and Discussion
Characterization of materials.—A cathode composite material
was prepared by using CNT as the host of sulfur. Due to the great
conductivity, CNT was gradually becoming a great choice of sulfur
carrier.37 In order to obtain the composite with a great proportion of
sulfur, only a small amount of conductor is needed, reaching 74.5%
in this study. It’s meaningful for increasing the speciﬁc capacity of
Li-S batteries. The cathode pole pieces were observed by SEM and
EDS, as shown in Figs. 2a–2c. The distribution of carbon and sulfur
elements was uniform apparently. It’s proved that sulfur was evenly
dispersed among CNT by a hot-melt method.
CIPs were bought from BASF, Germany, which were produced
via high pressure gas phase synthesis method. The speciﬁc introduc-
tion of technology was listed in the support information. From the
SEM image (Fig. 2d), CIPs are spherical and the diameter is around
1–4 um. The microstructure of sphere is uniform. Raman spectra of
three CIPs samples were measured, as shown in Fig. 2e. In all three

samples, one characteristic Raman peak is observed at around
1250 cm−1.38 It’s proved that the CIPs are highly homogeneous.
Electrolyte with 35 mg ml−1 CIPs was dropped onto the cathode
pole piece and diffused freely, which made CIPs distribute on the

Electrochemical performance.—To evaluate the electrochemical
performance of Li-S cells with different contents of CIPs, a series of
electrochemical tests were performed with assembled CR2032 cells,
and test results were shown in the Fig. 3. Firstly, cells with 0, 5, 15 and
35 mg ml−1 CIPs in electrolyte were tested in a multi-rate cycle. With
the content increasing, the speciﬁc capacity increases gradually. The
initial speciﬁc capacity is 987, 1028, 1104 and 1177 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C
respectively. And with the rate increasing, the difference becomes
bigger. At 2 C, the difference between 0 and 35 mg ml−1 is almost
180 mAh g−1. At 1 C and 2 C, compared with lower content of CIPs,
the slope of the curve with 35 mg ml−1 is small and tends to be ﬂat.
It’s obvious that the descent rate of capacity with a high content of
CIPs is low, which leads to a greater effect at a big rate. Two pairs of
theoretical reduction peaks, 2.35 V and 2.10 V vs Li+/Li, is shown in
the galvanostatic charge-discharge curves of 35 mg ml−1 showed.9
Two main steps of electrochemical reduction reaction are not affected
by CIPs. In addition, there is an reduction of over-potential (η), which
representing the fast reaction kinetic contributed by the electro-
catalysis of CIPs.39
As shown in Fig. 3c, EIS was carried out to evaluate the
electrochemical kinetic properties. The semicircle and a straight
line are associated with charge transfer and the solid state diffusion
process of Li+ in the electrode.30 Rs is almost the same, which is the
value of the high-frequency intercept on the real axis and represents
the all resistance depending on the electrolyte solution, active
material and current collector. So the CR2032 cells assembled in
this work are repeatable. Rct, which is the charge transfer resistance
at the electrode-electrolyte interface, decreases when the content of
CIPs increases. The existent of CIPs is beneﬁcial for the electro-
chemical reduction and accelerates the transform speed between S8
and Li2Sx. It’s consistent with the change of the over-potential η. It
can be attributed to the catalysis of transition metals.40,41
In order to conﬁrm the continual effect of CIPs at a big rate, a 300-
cycle long life test at 0.5 C was carried out, as shown in Fig. 3d. With
35 mg ml−1 CIPs, the initial speciﬁc capacity is 906 mAh g−1, bigger
than 819 mAh g−1 without CIPs. And around the 75th cycle, the slope
of the curve starts to become ﬂat. The capacity attenuation rate
is 0.114%, only two third of that without CIPs (0.149%). Compared
with the capacity attenuation rate of other references’ results
(0.5 C-300%-0.15%,20 0.5 C-100–0.2930), adding CIPs has a better
improvement. Fe atom and Li+ have vacant d-orbital. Sx
2−and C=O
have lone pair electrons. According to the adsorption bonding
theory,42 there are dual chemical adsorptions between CIPs and
Li2Sx. There is a tremendous ability to anchor Li2Sx, which makes
chemical reduction reaction [Eq. 2] difﬁcult to occur on the anode. The
“shuttle effect” is mitigated and cycle performance can be improved.

In order to verify the inhibition of the “shuttle effect”, some cells
that completed the multi-rate cycle with different contents of CIPs
were taken part. Through the Fig. 4a, cathode pole pieces are all
well-preserved after cycling. With different contents of CIPs, the
color of electrolyte, close to anode side, is different on the surface of
separator. Li2Sx is soluble in ether-based electrolyte, and the
difference in concentration provides the impetus for diffusion.
From the Fig. 4a, in the absence of CIPs, yellow electrolyte is
obvious. It means that a lot of Li2Sx shuttles from cathode side to
anode side. Li2Sx will have chemical reduction with lithium metal,
which lead to the loss of Li2Sx, and ﬁnally a rapid decrease of
capacity. With CIPs, there is high binding energy between CIPs and
Li2Sx. On Li2Sx dissolving near the surface of cathode, the Fe atom
and C=O groups of the CIPs layer upon the cathode surface can
capture the Sx
2−and Li+ respectively. The color of the electrolyte on
the anode side is getting lighter. Higher the content of CIPs is, more
obvious the inhibition effect of “shuttle effect” is.
To theoretically prove the adsorption effect of CIPs on Li2Sx, the
binding energy of CIPs was calculated through DFT calculation.
Firstly, models of dissolved lithium polysulﬁde (Li2S4, Li2S6, and
Li2S8), Fe(CO)5 and CNT were constructed (Fig. S1). The adsorp-
tion models of CIPs and CNT with Li2S4 were shown in Figs. 4b and
4c and the binding energy between Fe site and C=O site with Li2S4,
Li2S6, and Li2S8 was calculated respectively, as well the control
sample CNT, as shown in Fig. 4d. It’s obvious the binding energy of
CIPs with Li2Sx is higher than that of CNT, especially the Fe site
with Li2S4 (2.66 eV). The adsorption of CNT is low so that it can
hardly absorb Li2Sx and there is a bad “shuttle effect.” Transition
metal, like Fe atom with some vacant electron d-orbital, is prone to
form strong bond with Sx
2−, which has some long pair electrons.
Consequently, the “shuttle effect” is alleviated greatly and similar
conclusions were reached in similar investigations.23–25
Magnetorheological ﬂuid (MRF) is a kind of intelligent material
which can control the arrangement of magnetic particles in the liquid
with the external magnetic ﬁeld. Therein, CIPs, due to the excellent
soft magnetic properties and low cost, is widely used in MRF.43,44
After being dropped on the cathode pole piece, the distribution of
CIPs was observed as Figs. 2f–2g. When adding a permanent magnet
under the pole piece, according to the MRE, the distribution of CIPs
becomes more regular and uniform. This phenomenon has been
conﬁrmed in the application of MRF, like the similar change in the
Fig. S2.45,46 Figure 5a shows the top and front view of the pole piece
with an external magnetic ﬁeld (a large content of CIPs is used here
to make this change obvious). From the picture, CIPs are distributed
in the direction of the magnetic induction line, making the distribu-
tion of CIPs ﬂuffy. The contact between CIPs and Li2Sx becomes
more sufﬁcient. This makes more Li2Sx be anchored around CIPs,
which can mitigate the “shuttle effect”.
The adsorption experiment was performed to compare the effect
with CIPs and external magnetic ﬁeld, as shown in Fig. 5b. The color
of blank sample is not changed during the test period. With CIPs, the
color begins to fade in 3 h, which directly conﬁrms the above
discussion of the adsorption of CIPs. CIPs are soft magnetic particles
and will be magnetized in a magnetic ﬁeld. CIPs are raised into the
electrolyte with external magnetic ﬁeld because of the Lorentz force.
There is an induced magnetic ﬁeld around these spherical particles.
Through the Lorentz force, the induced magnetic ﬁeld changes the
distribution of the Sx
2−by attracting them around, leading a limited
solution of Li2Sx in electrolyte, as shown in Fig. 5c. So the color is
changed from brown to almost transparent quickly. The adsorption is

In addition, the electrochemical performance of cells is improved
with external magnetic ﬁeld. Firstly, from the Fig. 6a, the initial
speciﬁc capacity reaches 1286 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C (1427 mAh g−1 at
0.1 C), better than the test data at 0.2 C (Fig. 3a), and its CE is
almost over 99.6%. In Fig. 6b, during the process of charging and
discharging, the cells maintain the stability of redox voltage
platforms, as shown with the dash line (2.1 V). The polarization is
low and not changed obviously, which is attributed to the high
conductivity of CNT. The effect of magnetic ﬁeld on CE is
demonstrated more visually by Fig. 6c. At 0.5 C, the CE with
magnetic ﬁeld is generally higher than that without magnetic ﬁeld.
According to the previous research of our group,47 magnetic ﬁeld
can effectively improve the deposition process of lithium ions in
anode and inhibit the generation of lithium dendrites. Thus, coupling
a magnetic ﬁeld can improve the coulombic efﬁciency. In order to
test the cycling performance at a high rate, a long-life cycle
experiment at 0.5 C and 1 C was carried out. The initial speciﬁc
capacities of 0.5 C and 1 C are 865 and 782 mAh g−1 respectively.
The capacity attenuation rate at 0.5 C is smaller than that at 0.5 C
just coupling the magnetic ﬁeld. This great cycle performance is
attributed to physical magnetic ﬁeld effect, and additional chemical
adsorption of magnetized CIPs.
Conclusions
In this work, different contents of CIPs were added into the
electrolyte to form the chemical and physical synergistic inhibition
of the “shuttle effect”. According to adsorption bonding theory, Fe
site and C=O groups of CIPs have high binding energy with Li2Sx,
especially Fe site with Li2S4 (2.68 eV) through DFT calculation. A
high content of CIPs (35 mg ml−1) is favorable for improving
electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries, and the difference
is more obvious at a big rate. According to the MRE, the distribution
of CIPs is controlled by adding an external magnetic ﬁeld and
becomes uniform, which establishes a more favorable diffusion
gradient. In the adsorption experiment, because of the magnetic
shield layer of Li2Sx constructed between cathode and separator,
there is a strong adsorption for Li2Sx with the induced magnetic ﬁeld
of CIPs spherical particles by Lorenz force. At 0.2 C, the initial
speciﬁc capacity and coulombic efﬁciency reaches 1296 mAh g−1
and 99.6% respectively. Such a unique Li2Sx trapping mechanism
with controlled particles has the potential to mitigate the “shuttle
