Experimental Procedures 
1. Experimental Details 
1.1. Raw materials 
Elemental sulfur (S8), lithium sulfide (Li2S), lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide 
(LiTFSI), lithium bis(pentafluoroethanesulfonyl)imide (LiBETI), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), deuterated chloroform (CDCl3), and lithium 
nitrate (LiNO3) were purchased from Alfa Aesar Chemical Co., Ltd. Multi-walled carbon nanotube (CNT), Celgard 2400 polypropylene 
(PP) membranes, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, HSV-300) were purchased from Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd. 
Lithium (Li) metal foils were purchased from China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. 
1.2. Preparation of LiPS solutions 
DME solvent was adopted for preparing all the solutions in this work. The mixture of Li 2S and S8 with a molar ratio of 8:5 and 8:3 
was added into DME solvent to prepare Li2S6 and Li2S4 solution, respectively. The Li2S6 concentration was 0.10 M, and the nominal 
concentration of Li2S4 was 0.25 M (M refers to mol L−1). The solutions were stirred and left standing for 7 days to ensure thorough 
reaction between Li2S and S8. For conductivity measurements, the Li2S6 solutions were diluted with DME to reach a specific Li2S6 
concentration. In addition, LiFSI, LiTFSI, and LiBETI were added to the above Li 2S6 or Li2S4 solutions according to the concentrations 
mentioned in the main text or the experimental section below. All the above procedures were conducted in an Ar-filled glove box with 
O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm. 
1.3. Conductivity measurements 
The ionic conductivity of the LiPS solutions was measured using a DDS-308F Conductivity Meter (INESA Scientific Instrument Co. 
Ltd.). The instrument was calibrated with standard KCl aqueous solution before measurements. The total ionic conductivity (σ) of the 
solution was directly recorded from the instrument, and the molar conductivity (Λm) was calculated by dividing σ by the molar 
concentration of the solution.  
1.4. Spectroscopy characterization 
The t1 relaxation of 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra was recorded on a JNM-ECA600 NMR spectrometer. 0.10 M 
LiFSI, LiTFSI, or LiBETI, with or without 0.10 M Li2S6 solutions in DME solvent were sealed in NMR tubes to perform the NMR tests at 
−40oC, −20oC, 0oC, 20oC, and 40oC, respectively. A built-in coaxial NMR tube filled with CDCl3 was used to assist with shimming during 
the NMR measurements. Raman spectra were measured by a Horiba Jobin Yvon LabRAM HR800 Raman spectrometer under a 
Raman laser wavenumber of 532 nm. 0.10 M LiFSI, LiTFSI, or LiBETI, with or without 0.10 M Li 2S6 solutions in DME solvent were 
sealed in quartz cuvette to perform the Raman tests. 
1.5. Electrochemical evaluation 
For the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests, Li2S6 symmetric cells were assembled into a standard 2025 coin cell 
configuration with both of the electrodes (13.0 mm in diameter) adopting CNT-coated Al foils.[1] The separators were Celgard 2400 PP 
membranes with a diameter of 19.0 mm and the amount of electrolyte was 40 μL for each cell. 100 mM Li2S6 with 1.5 M LiFSI, 1.0 M 
LiTFSI, or 0.84 M LiBETI was employed as the electrolyte to afford the same equilibrium Li+ concentration. The EIS measurements 
were conducted on a Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation using the potentiostat mode at the open-circuit voltage. A sinusoidal 
voltage with an amplitude of 10 mV and a frequency range from 0.1 to 10000 Hz were applied. A high- and low-temperature alternating 
test chamber (WGD6005) was used in conjunction with the Solartron electrochemical workstation to measure the EIS spectra at −10°C, 
0°C,10°C, and 20°C. The interval time between each temperature was 10 min. The EIS responses were fitted using the ZView software 
with a previously reported equivalent circuit model to determine the charge transfer resistance (Rct).[1]  
For the nucleation measurements, CNT | Li cells were assembled into a standard 2025 coin cell configuration. 0.25 M nominal Li2S4 
with 0.74 M LiFSI, 0.50 M LiTFSI, or 0.42 M LiBETI was employed as the electrolyte to afford the same equilibrium Li+ concentration. 
The CNT-coated Al foils (13.0 mm in diameter) were employed as the cathode and Li metal foils (16.0 mm in diameter, 600 μm in 
thickness) were employed as the anode. The separators were Celgard 2400 PP membranes with a diameter of 19.0 mm. The amount 
of electrolyte was 40 μL for each cell. During the nucleation measurements, the cells were discharged galvanostatically at 0.10 mA 
using the Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation until the voltage reached 2.12 V. Subsequently, the cells were kept at 2.12 V 
for 2.0 h and then discharged potentiostatically at 2.05 V, and the current–time profiles were recorded. 
1.6. Fabrication of sulfur cathodes 
CNT and S8 were blended with a mass ratio of 3:7 and ball-milled thoroughly. The mixture was then melted under vacuum at 155°C 
for 2.0 h to obtain a composite. The composite and PVDF with a mass ratio of 9:1 were dispersed in NMP solution and stirred completely 
to form a homogeneous slurry.

For the Li–S coin cell evaluation, the slurry was coated on Al foils, dried at 60°C for 12.0 h, and punched into small disks with a 
diameter of 13.0 mm as sulfur cathodes. The areal sulfur loading was controlled as 1.1 or 4.0 mg S cm−2. For the Li–S pouch cell 
evaluation, the slurry was double-side coated on Al foils and dried at 60°C for 12.0 h. Then, 4 × 7 cm2 electrodes were cut. The areal 
sulfur loading was 7.6 mgS cm−2 on a single-side. 
1.7. Li–S cell assembly and electrochemical tests 
Li–S coin cells for rate performance evaluation and cycle performance evaluation were assembled using standard 2025 coin cell 
configuration. A 600 μm Li metal foil with a diameter of 16.0 mm was used as the anode and PP membrane with a diameter of 19.0 mm 
served as the separator. DME solvent dissolving 1.0 M LiFSI, LiTFSI or LiTFSI with 2% LiNO3 was adopted as the electrolyte. The 
electrolyte amount of the coin cells was 20 or 27 μL for the cells with sulfur loading of 1.1 or 4.0 mg S cm−2, respectively. The coin cells 
were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–2.6 V on a LAND multichannel battery cycler under different C-rates (1 C = 1672 mA g−1).  
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted between 1.7 and 2.6 V with a scanning rate of 0.1 mV s−1 on the Solartron 1470E 
electrochemical workstation. The activation overpotential during discharge was determined by galvanostatic intermittent titra tion 
technique (GITT) in Li–S coin cells. The cells were activated for 2 cycles at 0.05 C firstly. Then the cells were charged at 0.1 C, and 
discharged at 0.1 C for 5 min followed by relaxation at the open circuit for 60 min in each step. To avoid missing any fluctu ations of the 
voltage and capacity, the data were recorded every 0.2 s. The value of activation overpotential was then extracted following the 
previously reported data processing procedure.[2] Ohmic overpotential was neglected due to its limited proportion in the total polarization. 
To assemble Li–S pouch cells, Li metal anode sheets were cut with the same size as the sulfur cathodes. The thickness of the Li 
metal anode was 75 μm (single side) on Cu current collectors. The sulfur cathodes and the Li metal anodes were stacked layer by layer 
with PP separators. All the electrodes were controlled in parallel connection through an electrode lug terminal. 0.30 M LiFSI with 5.0% 
LiNO3 dissolved in DME solvent was adopted as the electrolyte, and the electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio was controlled as 2.0 g gS−1. 
The cells were galvanostatically cycled between 1.7–2.55 V on the LAND multichannel battery cycler under 0.05 C (1 C = 1000 mA g−1 
for pouch cell). 
2. Computational Details 
2.1. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation 
The DFT calculations of electrolyte structures and interaction were conducted in Gaussian 16 program with Becke’s three-
parameter hybrid method using the Lee–Yang–Parr correlation functional (B3LYP) at 6-311++G(d, p) level.[3] The solvation effect was 
considered with the universal solvation model of SMD using parameters of DME (dielectric constant = 7.2). [4] Frequency analysis was 
performed to ensure the ground state of optimized complexes.  
Calculations on the sulfur reduction reaction model were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). [5] The 
Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)[6] exchange–correlation functional within a generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [7] was 
implemented. The plane-wave cut-off energy was set at 520 eV, and all structures were optimized to a force tolerance less than 2×10−2 
eV Å−1 and the total energy convergence lower than 10−5 eV. The k-point was set to 8 × 8 × 1, which was verified by our previous 
work.[8] DFT-D3 method was used to include the van der Waals force.[9]  
2.2. Gibbs free energy change of ion binding 
The following reaction was considered to determine the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) of an anion (denoted as An–, An– = FSI–, 
TFSI–, or BETI–) entering the first solvation sheath of cationic polysulfides (Li3Sn+, n = 4, 6, or 8): 
 
Li3Sn+−DME + An− → Li3Sn+−An− + DME 
(1) 
Therefore, ΔG was defined as follows: 
 
ΔG = G(DME) + G(Li3Sn+−An−) – G(Li3Sn+−DME) – G(An−) 
(2) 
The Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) charge analysis was also performed to consider the charge transfer during the interaction. [10] 
2.3. Gibbs free energy diagram of sulfur reduction reaction 
The polysulfides (Li2S4) and radicals (LiSn·, n = 1, 2, or 3) were regarded as solvated molecules adsorbed on the graphene surface, 
while insoluble Li2S and Li2S2 were treated as crystals during the DFT calculations. The structure of Li2S2 was taken from a theoretical 
hypothesized structure.[11] The explicit solvation model was considered to describe the impact of solvation by solvent and anions. The 
solvation model allowed one Li+ to bind with the polysulfides or radicals, forming polysulfide cations. Furthermore, the cations were 
solvated by one anion and two DME molecules (Figure S11–S13). A vacuum layer of 20 Å was adopted in the z-axis to minimize the 
interaction between the adjacent layers.  
Compared with the symmetric mechanism, the overall reaction follows the asymmetric mechanism according to the energy profiles. 
The hypothesized elementary reactions of the six-electron-transfer process from Li2S4 to Li2S are as follows: 
 
Li2S4 + (Li+ + e−) + * → LiS3·* + Li2S  (step 1) 
(3)

2.3.1. Calculations of Gibbs free energy changes in elementary reactions 
The formation Gibbs free energies of radicals on the active sites were defined as: 
 
ΔG(LiS3∙*) = G(LiS3∙*) − G(*) + [G(Li2S) − G(Li) − G(Li2S4)] 
(9) 
 
ΔG(LiS2∙*) = G(LiS2∙*) – G(*) + [G(Li) − G(Li2S2)] 
(10) 
 
 
ΔG(LiS∙*) = G(LiS∙*) − G(*) + [G(Li2S) − G(Li) − G(Li2S2)] 
(11) 
where G(LiS3∙*), G(LiS2∙*), and G(LiS∙*) are the free energies of adsorbed LiS3∙, LiS2∙, and LiS∙ complexes. G(Li), G(Li2S2) and 
G(Li2S) were considered in solid states. The Gibbs free energy of Li+ and electron can be obtain through: 
 
G(Li+) + G(e−) = G(Li) – eU 
(12) 
where e represents the elementary charge, U is the applied potential vs. Li+/Li. Following the above rules, the Gibbs free energy 
changes for step 1−6 can be expressed as follows: 
 
ΔG1 = ΔG(LiS3∙*) + eU 
(13) 
 
ΔG2 = ΔG(LiS2∙*) + ΔG(LiS∙*) − ΔG(LiS3∙*) + 2G(Li2S2) − 2G(Li) − G(Li2S4) + eU 
(14) 
 
ΔG3 = − ΔG(LiS∙*) + 2G(Li2S) − 2G(Li) − G(Li2S2) + eU 
(15) 
 
ΔG4 = − ΔG(LiS2∙*) + 2ΔG(LiS∙*) + G(Li2S2) + 2G(Li) − 2G(Li2S) + eU 
(16) 
 
ΔG5 = − ΔG(LiS∙*) + 2G(Li2S) − 2G(Li) − G(Li2S2) + eU 
(17) 
 
ΔG6 = − ΔG(LiS∙*) + 2G(Li2S) − 2G(Li) − G(Li2S2) + eU 
(18) 
To avoid the heavy calculation of vibrations, the Gibbs free energies of all species in the equations (9–18) are approximated with 
total energies due to the small contribution of zero-point energy and the entropy change in solid-state conditions.[12] 
2.3.2. Overpotential calculations 
To give a quantitative comparison, the calculated overpotential (η) is introduced to evaluate the intrinsic reactivity of polysulfides 
solvated by different Li salts. The η is determined by the potential-determining step, which is the least exothermic step. Taking these 
considerations into account, η can be expressed as 
 
η = E + max(ΔGi0) / e 
(20) 
 
where E represents the equilibrium potential, ΔGi0 is the Gibbs free energy change of an elementary reaction at an applied potential 
U = 0 V vs. Li+/Li. In order to accurately describe the kinetics order of polysulfides solvated different anions at the same working potential, 
the E of the three Li salt systems was assumed to be the same (see the calculation results in Figure 5d) and a definite U = 2.20 V was 
adopted to substitute E based on the method reported in our previous research.[13] Therefore, the η can be directly reflected by ΔG(LiS∙*) 
and ΔG(LiS2∙*), which are greatly affected by the solvation structure.
