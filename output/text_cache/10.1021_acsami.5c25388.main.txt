Suppressing Shuttle Effect through Solvent Coordination and
Nitrate Synergy Enables Stable Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: The shuttling of soluble polysulfide (PS) intermediates between the cathode and anode leads to rapid capacity fade
and remains one of the foremost barriers to the commercialization of lucrative lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries. Previous electrolyte
strategies have been constrained by a critical trade-off: weakly coordinating electrolytes and electrode architectures effectively
suppressing the shuttle effect at the expense of reduced capacity. Typically, strongly coordinating electrolytes deliver higher sulfur
utilization but intensify polysulfide migration and mostly rely on LiNO3 additives for anode stability. In this study, a balanced
electrolyte design is introduced using a binary solvent system comprising strongly coordinating tetrahydrofuran (THF) and weakly
coordinating cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME). A direct correlation is established between solvent coordination strength,
polysulfide solubility, and LiNO3 concentration along with their combined influence on the shuttle effect. By tuning solvent polarity
(dielectric constant) within the THF:CPME binary system, sufficient polysulfide dissolution is achieved to sustain robust redox
kinetics while avoiding excessive dissolution that accelerates capacity decay. Using 0.7 M LiNO3 in 4:1 THF:CPME electrolyte, the
system delivers 92% capacity retention over 100 cycles, a ∼20% improvement over the conventional electrolyte, along with high
Coulombic efficiency (99−100%). Further analysis confirms the formation of a LiNO3-derived protective interphase and regulated
polysulfide dissolution on both the anode and cathode surfaces.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, shuttle effect suppression, solvent coordination, lithium nitrate synergy, polysulfide dissolution
1. INTRODUCTION
The integration of electronic devices into our society and the
gradual spread of electric vehicles renew the need for advanced
energy storage technology. Currently, lithium-ion batteries
(LIBs) are the most mature energy storage device owing to its
long cycle life, high power density, and low maintenance
cost.1,2 However, the development of traditional LIBs based on
graphite anode, lithium oxide cathode, and carbonate electro-
lyte has reached a bottleneck in terms of energy density; in
addition, the materials used to fabricate the cathodes often
include rare metals such as cobalt with high cost and
detrimental environmental impact.3,4 Both factors limit the
use of current LIBs for future applications with increasing
demand and call for an alternative solution.
One of the promising candidates for the next-generation
energy storage device is the Li−S battery, a combination of two
high-capacity electrode materials, sulfur and lithium metal. The
sulfur cathode has a high theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh/g
as opposed to the 250 mAh/g for the lithium oxide cathode,
while lithium metal anode has a theoretical capacity of 3860
mAh/g, higher than the 372 mAh/g of graphite anode.5
Elemental sulfur is produced in excess of 7 million tons
annually as a byproduct of hydrodesulfurization processes, far
exceeding its industrial demand and available at a cost of
approximately 0.13 USD kg−1.6 In contrast, commonly used
cathode materials for lithium-ion batteries, such as lithium

cobalt oxide, rely on scarce and expensive metals like cobalt,
which can cost upward of 30 USD kg−1 and pose greater
environmental and ethical concerns. Compared to these
alternatives, sulfur’s abundance, low cost, and relatively low
toxicity make it a sustainable and economically attractive
choice for next-generation battery cathodes.7,8
Unlike the ion-insertion mechanism between the graphite
anode and the layered oxide cathode in the LIB, Li−S battery
stores energy through a redox conversion mechanism between
sulfur cathode and lithium metal anode.8,9 In liquid electro-
lytes, the sulfur redox reaction continues through a four-step
process. First, solid S8 is reduced to form soluble Li2S8 species.
These then undergo further reduction to generate soluble
polysulfides, such as Li2S2 and Li2S4. As the process continues,
the soluble Li2S4 converts into insoluble products, namely,
solid Li2S2 and Li2S. Finally, Li2S2 is further reduced to yield
solid Li2S as the end discharge product.10,11 However, the
soluble PS intermediates (Li2S6 and Li2S4) tend to diffuse
between the cathode and anode and deposit on anode as Li2S/
Li2S2 during battery operation, a notorious phenomenon often
referred to as “shuttle effect”, which causes loss of active
material and degradation of lithium anode.9,12−14 Hence,
designing a shuttle-free electrolyte is a key challenge for Li−S
batteries, and there are two schools of thought to achieve that
goal: (1) prevent excess polysulfide formation by reducing the
polysulfide solubility in the electrolyte. This method typically
favors weakly coordinating solvents with medium to low
dielectric constant to make the polar PS less likely to dissolve;
one example is fluorinated ethers, which also adds beneficial
LiF content to the SEI layer.15−17 High-solvation electrolytes,
such as 1 M LiTFSI in 1,2-dimethoxyethane and 1,3-dioxolane
(DME/DOL; 1:1 by vol.) with LiNO3, enhance polysulfide
solubility and enable fast Li−S reaction kinetics with low
electrolyte use, but they also promote polysulfide shuttling,
leading to poor Li metal stability and reduced cycle life.18
Furthermore, moderately solvating electrolytes were developed
such as DME, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran (2-MeTHF), and
tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl)orthoformate (TFEO) to enhance Li
metal stability, suppressing the polysulfide shuttle and
improving long-term cycling.19 However, the cell capacity
inevitably suffers as the liquid-phase sulfur reduction kinetics is
hindered by the low solubility. (2) Passivate the lithium metal
anode so that the Li2S cannot deposit on it. The most common
approach to do so is to use LiNO3 as an electrolyte additive,
which is believed to be able to form a robust SEI layer with
lithium anode as well as protecting sulfur cathode.20,21
Nevertheless, LiNO3 is continuously consumed during the
battery cycling, which requires a solvent with a high dielectric
constant and high coordinating strength to dissolve sufficient
LiNO3 and could potentially accelerate initial capacity
fade.22,23 In addition, excess LiNO3 was previously reported
to be unhelpful for Li−S battery operation as it lowers sulfur
utilization.24
In this work, a balance between the two prevailing
electrolyte design strategies is achieved by formulating a
binary ether electrolyte with a moderate solvating power.
Combining a strongly coordinating solvent (THF) with a
weakly coordinating one (CPME) enables dissolution of
sufficient LiNO3 for effective SEI formation while inherently
limiting polysulfide solubility. These solvents were selected
based on the large contrast in their dielectric constants (7.58
for THF and 4.76 for CPME),25−27 allowing the coordination
strength of the electrolyte to be tuned by adjusting their ratio.
Previous studies have consistently shown that electrolyte
design critically influences the Li−S battery performance. For
example, partially fluorinated ethers enhance Coulombic
efficiency by suppressing polysulfide dissolution,28 and high-
LiNO3 liquid electrolytes have enabled >1 Ah pouch cells29
with energy densities exceeding 150 Wh kg−1. Recent work
further shows that LiNO3 simultaneously modulates solvation
chemistry and interfacial reactions.30 Collectively, these studies
establish that solvent coordination, salt chemistry, and phase
state are effective levers for controlling polysulfide behavior,
thereby supporting the present strategy of tuning the
coordination strength in binary ether electrolytes.
The electrolyte design was systematically optimized through
a stepwise strategy to decouple and then integrate the effects of
the solvent composition and LiNO3 concentration. Initially,
CPME-rich electrolytes without LiNO3 were investigated to
isolate the role of the THF/CPME ratio on the Li+ solvation
structure, polysulfide solubility, and early interfacial behavior,
as elucidated by Raman spectroscopy and Li∥Cu cell
measurements. Subsequently, the LiNO3 concentration was
varied in THF-rich electrolytes to determine the minimum
nitrate content required to establish a stable and effective
protective interphase while avoiding excessive interfacial
passivation. Guided by these single-variable insights, we jointly
tuned both parameters to probe their synergistic effects. This
optimization reveals that neither solvent ratio nor LiNO3 alone
dictates electrochemical performance; rather, a sufficiently
THF-rich environment to dissolve LiNO3, combined with
CPME-induced moderation, is essential. The optimized
THF:CPME (4:1) electrolyte containing 0.7 M LiNO3 delivers
high sulfur utilization and 92% capacity retention over 100
cycles, significantly outperforming the conventional
DOL:DME system. Surface analyses confirm the formation
of LiNO3-derived interphase species (Li3N, Li2O, and LiF),
which correlate directly with suppressed polysulfide shuttle and
stabilized long-term cycling.
2. RESULT AND DISCUSSION
The solvent coordination strength can be most effectively
analyzed in the context of the Lewis basicity/donor number
(DN) and the resulting solvation structure surrounding Li+.
Donors with higher DN solvents show a preference for solvent-
separated ion pairs (SSIP), while donors with lower DN
solvents exhibit a tendency to favor contact ion pairs (CIP)
and aggregates (AGG) in which anions enter the first shell.
SSIP, CIP, and AGG represent distinct modes of cation−anion
association in solution: SSIP denotes a cation and anion
separated by at least one solvent molecule, CIP is where the
ions are in direct contact, and AGG refers to larger ion
aggregates involving multiple cations and anions. Raman
spectroscopy was performed in order to study the coordination
strength of the respective electrolyte solvent through
examination of the coordination environment of FSI−anion
in the electrolyte, represented by the peaks between 700 and
780 cm−1.31,32 For that purpose, five electrolytes of 1 M LiFSI
in pure THF(1F-THF), in pure CPME(1F-CPME), and in
composition of THF:CPME with volume ratios of 1:1(1F-
11TC), 1:2(1F-12TC), and 1:3(1F-13TC), respectively, were
prepared. Figure 1a shows that the deconvoluted FSI−peak has
three components: SSIP, CIP, and AGG.33 Evidently from
Figure 1b, SSIP is dominant in 1F-THF than AGG in CPME,
suggesting that THF has higher coordination strength than
that of CPME, consistent with the previous finding that a high

Polysulfide Solubility and Shuttle Suppression
1 M LiFSI in THF (1F-THF) and in CPME (1F-CPME) were
assessed, along with that of the 1F02N-11DD (1 M LiTFSI +
0.2 M LiNO3 in 1:1 DOL:DME) electrolyte as a reference, to
assess their distinct properties as electrolyte solvent (Figure
2a). A clear contrast in polysulfide (Li-Sx) dissolution is
observed between strongly and weakly coordinating solvents.
In THF (high donor strength), strong Li+ solvation promotes
extensive Li-polysulfide complexation, leading to high
solubility.37 During the discharge process, sulfur predom-
inantly remains in solution until low potentials are reached,
resulting in the production of a voltage ″bump″ at
approximately 2.05 V (additional overpotential for Li2S
nucleation on the cathode surface).11 Upon being subjected
to a charge, THF cells show a protracted plateau at
approximately 2.3−2.4 V. This phenomenon is attributed to
the uninterrupted diffusion and reoxidation of soluble
polysulfides, such as Li2S8.
This observation aligns with the established characteristics of
a shuttle, as previously defined in the literature.38 This severe
shuttle effect yields a poor Coulombic efficiency (CE) and
rapid sulfur loss. Conversely, CPME (weak Lewis base, low
dielectric constant) exerts a pronounced effect on polysulfide
solubility, exhibiting a marked suppression of the latter. The
precipitation of Li-Sx intermediates is rapid, leading to the
elimination of the initial voltage bump and the anomalously
long charge plateau. The immobilization of polysulfides as a
solid Li2S/Li2S2 phase within the shuttle layer effectively
suppresses the reaction, thereby achieving near-100% con-
version efficiency in the initial cycles. Therefore, a reduction in
solvent coordination strength, as observed with the transition
from THF to CPME, has the capacity to minimize polysulfide
solubility and shuttle effects.37 However, this reduction also
shifts sulfur redox toward solid-phase conversion. The 1F02N-
11DD electrolyte containing LiNO3 delivers the characteristic
discharge plateaus at ∼2.3 and ∼2.1 V, corresponding to the
sequential formation of long- and short-chain polysulfides.
During charging, the reappearance of the 2.3 V plateau,
followed by a rapid voltage rise to the cutoff, reflects the
reversible polysulfide conversion process. Consistent with
established mechanisms, LiNO3 oxidizes polysulfides and is
reduced to form a protective LixSOy/LixNOy interphase on the
lithium anode, which suppresses shuttle and mitigates
electrolyte decomposition.30
Cycling Performance and Coulombic Efficiency
To evaluate the stability of the lithium anode in THF:CPME
solvent, the Li∥Cu plating/stripping experiment was per-
formed with 1F-11TC, 1F-12TC, and 1F-13TC (Figure S4).
All THF:CPME blend electrolytes in this study exhibit good
initial compatibility with the lithium metal anode, rapidly
forming passivating interphases that enable high plating/
stripping efficiencies. Notably, the higher-CPME content
electrolyte achieves ∼98% Li∥Cu plating CE in the fewest
cycles (within ∼20 cycles, Figure 2b), with smooth voltage
profiles indicating stable Li plating/stripping kinetics,
attributed to the formation of a dense, inorganic-rich SEI
layer. This can be rationalized by the solvation structure:
Raman spectroscopy confirms that in CPME-rich electrolytes,
the FSI−anion is more prevalent in the Li+ solvation sheath
(due to the weaker Lewis basicity of CPME), which promotes
anion-induced SEI formation.27 Prior studies have shown that
such an anion-derived SEI (rich in LiF and inorganic species)
is highly effective at passivating lithium surfaces. In our cells,
this manifests as the faster attainment of ∼99% Coulombic
efficiency with an increased CPME fraction, indicating that a
robust LiF-rich interphase is quickly established on the lithium
anode in the weakly solvating media. The long-term cycling
results underscore that solvent coordination strength also
governs capacity retention and lithium anode stability in Li−S
cells (Figure 2c,d). Over extended cycling, the benefits of
reduced polysulfide solubility become even more evident. For
instance, by the 50th cycle, the 1:1 THF:CPME cell begins to
show a rising charge plateau (∼2.6 V) and a gradual decline in
CE, signaling the onset of polysulfide shuttle as soluble species
accumulate over time (analogous to DOL:DME behavior).39
In contrast, the high-CPME 1:3 electrolyte maintains a flat
charge−discharge profile with no anomalous high-voltage
plateau through 50+ cycles. Concomitantly, the CPME-rich
cell exhibits a slightly lower initial discharge capacity (owing to
lower polysulfide availability), but it suffers the smallest overall
capacity fade among the tested formulations. After 100 cycles,
the 1F-13TC electrolyte retains ∼44% of its initial capacity,
with a Coulombic efficiency of ∼68%. Moreover, all three
electrolytes show a drastic capacity decline beyond the 100th
cycle (Table S2); this behavior can be attributed to the gradual
impact of Li2S plating on the anode, which persists despite
reduced PS migration from lower solubility. Coupled with the
observation that the Coulombic efficiency for 1F-13TC
decreases more slowly than that for the other electrolytes,
these results indicate that minimizing PS solubility effectively
mitigates the shuttle effect, even if long-term capacity fade is
eventually influenced by Li2S deposition.
The choice of solvent coordination strength dictates shuttle
suppression and sulfur redox kinetics in Li−S cells. In weakly
solvating CPME, discharge plateaus appear at ∼2.1 V (S8 →
Sn
2−) and ∼1.8 V (Sn
2−→Li2S). These plateaus are
significantly lower and more sloped than those in conventional
ethers (e.g., DOL:DME at ∼2.3 and 2.1 V). This compression
of plateaus reflects increased polarization and sluggish kinetics
because only a small fraction of polysulfides dissolve and
conversion to Li2S proceeds via surface-bound intermediates.
In contrast, the strong coordination of THF stabilizes soluble
polysulfides, producing higher discharge voltages and an
extended second plateau. This is indicative of faster liquid-
phase reduction. However, the same solvation that accelerates
kinetics also aggravates polysulfide shuttling. Mixed solvents
provide a rational compromise. A 1:1 THF:CPME blend
produces higher discharge plateaus and capacities than pure
CPME because of moderately increased polysulfide solubility.
However, it avoids the severe shuttle signature of THF. By the
50th cycle, high-THF blends exhibit a new, high-voltage charge
plateau (∼2.6−2.8 V) due to polysulfide reoxidation. In
contrast, CPME-rich blends (1:3) maintain flat charge−
discharge profiles without late-cycle polarization. Thus, the
strength of solvent coordination directly tunes the balance
between the kinetics and stability. Strong solvation enhances
sulfur utilization at the expense of shuttling, while weak
solvation suppresses shuttling but increases overpotentials.
Mixed coordination environments achieve both acceptable
kinetics and minimized parasitic reactions.

LiNO3 Concentration Optimization for Stable Cycling
While weakly coordinating solvents effectively suppress
polysulfide shuttling, their kinetic limitations necessitate
complementary strategies for anode protection. A prevalent
methodology entails the integration of LiNO3, a compound
that fosters the establishment of a robust passivation layer on
lithium. To identify the optimal concentration, we systemati-
cally varied the LiNO3 content in a THF-based electrolyte (1
M LiFSI in THF with 0.1−1.0 M LiNO3, denoted 1FxN-
THF). A distinct threshold behavior was identified: cells with
0.1−0.2 M LiNO3 exhibited rapid capacity decay, losing much
of their capacity within approximately 50 cycles. This was
evident in Figure 3b, as 1FxN-THF voltage profiles show a
clear concentration-dependent stabilization effect.
In contrast, 0.5 M LiNO3 significantly enhanced stability,
retaining over 80% of the initial capacity after more than 100
cycles (Figure 3a), and maintained far more stable charge−
discharge curves with minimal voltage hysteresis growth. This
requirement for a minimum nitrate concentration is indicative
of its rapid consumption during electrode−electrolyte
interphase formation.38 When the initial level is insufficient
(i.e., less than 0.3 M), LiNO3 is depleted within the first dozen
cycles, leaving the anode unprotected and accelerating the
shuttle processes. Beyond its classical function as an
interphase-forming additive, LiNO3 also modulates the Li+
solvation structure. The nitrate anion (NO3
−) competes with
ether molecules due to its polar coordinating ability, thereby
influencing the balance between solvent-separated and contact

At a concentration of 0.5 M, a robust and persistent LiF-rich

The observed behavior from Figure 4a−d aligns with
previous reports showing that LiNO3 converts soluble
polysulfides into insoluble surface species,42 forming an
inorganic-rich SEI that suppresses shuttle but can hinder ion
transport when excessively dense.43 This effect is reflected in
the impedance evolution: The low-nitrate 0.1 M electrolyte
(1F01N-THF) shows a continual rise in both Rint and Rct,
indicating the generation and accumulation of insulating Li2S
and Li2S2 and fewer soluble polysulfides. At the other extreme,
the highly concentrated 1.0 M LiNO3 system (1F1N-THF)
develops a noticeably thicker passivation layer and displays
inconsistent charge-transfer behavior, suggesting that excessive
nitrate content drives overpassivation and introduces kinetic
penalties. In contrast, the 0.5 M LiNO3 electrolyte (1F05N-
THF) exhibits the most stable and favorable impedance
response across all of the cycles. Its Rint remains relatively
constant, and the pronounced reduction in Rct from cycle 20 to
cycle 50 is attributed to enhanced electrochemical accessibility
following the consumption of insulating sulfur. This balanced
behavior demonstrates that 0.5 M LiNO3 provides sufficient
passivation to suppress the shuttle while avoiding the mass-
transport limitations observed at both lower and higher
concentrations.
The results indicate that 0.5 M LiNO3 in THF is optimal to
sustain interfacial integrity and suppress shuttle activity
without imposing transport limitations. The concentrations
that exceed the optimal range (∼1.0 M) have been observed to
yield diminishing returns and to result in a marginal reduction
in sulfur utilization. Following this analysis, the LiNO3 content
was established at approximately 0.7 M in the subsequent
mixed-solvent electrolytes with the objective of maximizing
solubility and ensuring robust interfacial protection.

properties. A 1 M LiFSI salt concentration was maintained

the capacity and CE of three battery samples under different C-
rates. Among them, 1F07N-41TC demonstrates the best
electrochemical performance, showing higher discharge
capacity and better capacity retention across all cycling rates,
while maintaining nearly 100% CE, indicating superior stability
and reversibility compared to 1F03N-11TC and 1F05N-32TC.
The exceptional durability of 1F07N-41TC can be attributed
to the synergy of moderate solvent coordination and optimized
nitrate content. The presence of 80 vol % THF in the
electrolyte has been demonstrated to facilitate enhanced
polysulfide solubility and efficient redox kinetics, as indicated
by the observations of reduced polarization and augmented
rate capability. This solvating strength is further moderated by
the introduction of 20 vol % CPME, thereby preventing the
uncontrolled diffusion behavior often associated with pure
THF. In essence, CPME calibrates the coordination environ-
ment to an intermediate level, thereby facilitating the
dissolution of polysulfides for expeditious kinetics while
concurrently preventing extensive crossover. Upon introducing
0.7 M LiNO3 to the 1F-41TC system (1F07N-4TTC), the
coordination equilibria shift toward a slightly more CIP
(Figure S2), reflecting competitive coordination of NO3
−and
FSI−to Li+ and promoting clustered anion environments that
can enhance robust, anion-derived interphase. This equilibrium
is reflected in the 99−100% Coulombic efficiencies and the
absence of late-cycle voltage anomalies.
The 0.7 M LiNO3 formulation further improves interfacial
stability by continuously regenerating a protective SEI on the
lithium surface, thereby suppressing lithium reactivity and
shielding the metal from direct corrosion. This reinforced
passivation is evidenced by the marked enhancement of
Coulombic efficiency. As previously discussed, concentrations
below ∼0.3 M are inadequate for providing sustained
protection. Conversely, concentrations near 1.0 M offer
negligible additional benefit and can impede kinetics by
forming excessively thick interphases. At a concentration of 0.7
M, LiNO3 reaches an optimal level, where it supports the long-
lasting SEI components, such as Li3N, Li2O, and Li2SO4, at
both electrodes. It does this without hindering transport
processes, thereby suppressing parasitic reactions.42 Addition-
ally, the high donor strength of nitrate enables it to participate
directly in the Li+ solvation shell, complementing the
coordination environment defined by the THF:CPME solvent
blend. This interaction not only enhances LiNO3 solubility in
THF-rich electrolytes but also lowers the chemical activity of
dissolved polysulfides by modifying their solvation equilibrium.
Thus, LiNO3 serves a dual role of regulating both solvation
dynamics and interfacial chemistry to realize synergistic
suppression of shuttle behavior.
Conversely, less optimal solvent ratios underscore the
significance of both solvent moderation and nitrate content.
Within the 1:1 THF:CPME system, wherein LiNO3 solubility
was constrained to approximately 0.3 M, the electrolyte
exhibited an inadequate capacity to provide anode protection,
resulting in accelerated capacity deterioration and diminished
discharge voltage. The 3:2 mixture (0.5 M LiNO3) exhibited
enhanced retention, yet it did not reach the level observed in
the 4:1 case. This finding reinforces the importance of an
elevated THF content, which leads to a concomitant increase
in both LiNO3 solubility and polysulfide mobility. This
increase is crucial for optimizing performance. Therefore,
intermediate ratios of solvents resulted in intermediate
outcomes; the 4:1 THF:CPME with 0.7 M LiNO3 exhibited
the most optimal equilibrium. Quantitative comparisons are
summarized in Table S4 with recent literature, showing the
highest retention and CE for 1F07N-41TC relative to
conventional DOL:DME baseline electrolyte.

3. CONCLUSIONS
This study establishes that synergistic solvent−salt design
provides an effective route to suppress polysulfide shuttling
while preserving high sulfur utilization in Li−S batteries. A
binary ether electrolyte, THF:CPME = 4:1 with 0.7 M LiNO3
(1F07N-41TC), achieves 92.6% capacity retention over 100
cycles and near-unity Coulombic efficiency, far outperforming
conventional DOL:DME baseline. Mechanistically, Raman
spectroscopy reveals that THF promotes solvent-separated
ion pairs and high polysulfide solubility, whereas CPME favors
contact ion pairing and aggregation with reduced solubility.
Blending the two establishes an intermediate solvation regime,
which balances liquid-phase redox kinetics with suppressed
crossover. Electrochemical analyses confirm that CPME-rich
media minimize shuttle but suffer from higher overpotentials,
while THF accelerates kinetics but exacerbates shuttling; the
mixed solvent reconciles these trade-offs. The effects of LiNO3
are of particular interest, as they exhibit critical thresholds. At
concentrations below 0.3 M, LiNO3 is rapidly consumed
during the formation of the interphase, which hinders the
protection of the anode. Conversely, concentrations greater
than 0.5−1.0 M led to excessive impedance and reduced sulfur
utilization. At approximately 0.7 M, lithium nitrate (LiNO3)
exhibits sustained interphase longevity without incurring
transport penalties. Surface and XPS analyses verify the
formation of robust inorganic-rich SEI/CEI composed of
LiF, Li2O, Li2CO3, and Li3N. This process largely eliminates
anomalous carbides and suppresses polysulfide-derived sulfur
deposition by 1 order of magnitude. The detection of Li3N and
residual nitrate species further underscores the dual role of
LiNO3, namely, its function as both anode passivation via Li3N
formation and cathode stabilization via polysulfide oxidation.
The findings of this collective study establish a generalizable
design principle, which asserts that high-performance Li−S
electrolytes necessitate concurrent optimization of solvent
coordination and nitrate availability. The optimized solvent-
salt environment produces inorganic-rich, ionically conductive
interphases and confines sulfur to the cathode, delivering both
stability and utilization. This collaborative design approach
provides a comprehensive strategy for the advancement of
lean-electrolyte, high-loading Li−S cells toward practical
deployment.
4. MATERIAL AND METHODS
Materials
Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich. The 20 μm lithium copper foil and carbon-coated aluminum
foil were purchased from MSE Supplies LLC.
Electrode Preparation
The sulfur/carbon (S/C) composite was prepared by ball-milling
nanosulfur (Sky Spring Nanomaterials, Inc.) and graphene powder
(MSE supplies) in a 2:1 ratio for 30 min, followed by sieving through
a 75 μm mesh to obtain the composite. This S/C material was then
combined with Super P conductive carbon (Timcal), carbon
nanotubes (CNT; Sigma-Aldrich), and PVDF binder (Sigma-Aldrich)
in a weight ratio of (S/C):PVDF:Super P:CNT = (50/25):15:5:5,
using an appropriate amount of N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) as the
solvent. The mixture was homogenized in a Thinky planetary mixer
for 30 min to form a uniform slurry, which was subsequently coated
onto carbon-coated aluminum foil and vacuum-dried, yielding
electrodes containing 50 wt % sulfur in the total composite.
Electrolyte Preparations
All electrolyte preparation was completed in a glovebox filled with
argon at <2 ppm oxygen and <1 ppm water content. The 1F02N-
11DD was prepared by adding 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 to a 1:1
vol ratio DOL:DME solution. 1 M LiFSI was added to THF and
CPME in 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 volume ratios to prepare the corresponding
electrolyte. In addition, electrolytes with 1 M LiFSI and saturated
LiNO3 in THF and CPME in 1:1, 3:2, and 4:1 ratios were also
prepared.
Polysulfide Solubility Test
Stock solution of 0.2 M Li2S6 in THF solution was first prepared by
adding a 1:5 molar ratio of sulfur nanoparticle to lithium sulfide (Li2S,
Sigma-Aldrich) to THF in an argon-filled glovebox and subsequently
stirred for 3 days under room temperature forming a homogeneous
solution. Then, the stock THF solution was diluted and
corresponding CPME was added to make 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3
THF:CPME solution with an equal concentration of Li2S6.
Electrochemical Measurements
For each electrolyte, CR2032 stainless-steel coin cells were assembled
using prepared sulfur cathode with sulfur loading of ∼1.77 mg/cm2
(12 mm disk), lithium (20 μm thick)-copper laminated foil (14 mm
disk) as the anode with mass loading of ∼1.07 mg/cm2, and a Celgard
polypropylene separator (18 mm disk). 25 μL amount of electrolyte
was used for each cell to obtain an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of
around 12.5 μL/mg. The lithium copper (Li∥Cu) cells were prepared
with rolled lithium metal (14 mm disk) as the anode, copper foil (19
mm disk) as the cathode, and Celgard polypropylene separator (20
mm disk). The electrochemical cycling of the cells was conducted in a
Neware BTS-4000 cycler and a Landt CT3002A cycler. LiS cells were
all cycled at a rate of C/10 for the first five cycles and at C/5 for all
subsequent cycles. Li∥Cu cells were first charged at 765 mA for 1 h
and then discharged at the same current until the potential reached 1
V.
Material Characterization
Raman spectroscopy was performed on a Thermo Scientific DXR
Raman Microscope with a 633 nm laser. A total of 50 scans were
conducted with an exposure time of 7.5 s for each scan. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Kratos X-ray
Photoelectron Spectrometer; the samples were washed in their
respective electrolyte three times for 10 min total before experiment.
To perform the depth profiling, argon sputtering of 5 kV energy was
conducted on sample; data collection was done after 0, 20, 60, and
180 s sputtering for cathode and 0, 20, 60, and 90 s sputtering for
anode. The resulting data was deconvoluted with CasaXPS. Scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on an FEI NOVA
nanoSEM Field Emission SEM.
