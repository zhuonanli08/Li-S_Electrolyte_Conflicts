Abstract
This work introduces nickel (II) tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP) as a multifunctional
electrolyte additive to simultaneously address polysulfide shuttle and uncontrolled
lithium dendrite growth in lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. Mobile NiTPP species act
as homogeneous catalysts with Ni–N4 centers for rapid polysulfide redox mediation
to suppress migration, while planar molecules featuring lithium-affinity capability
migrate to the lithium metal surfaces, enabling uniform lithium flux and anode protec-
tion. NiTPP-modified electrolytes significantly enhance Li–S battery performance,
demonstrated by 634.9 mAh g1 at 4C, 758.3 mAh g1 after 100 cycles at 0.2 C
(83.4% retention), and 757.5 mAh g1 under high sulfur loading (2.9 mg cm2) with
lean electrolyte (E/S = 5 μL mg1), all with high Coulombic efficiency. Crucially,
NiTPP, acting as a soluble dual-functional mediator enabling homogeneous operation
in electrolytes, simplifies Li–S battery fabrication while demonstrating the commer-
cial viability of molecularly engineered mobile active sites for stabilizing both elec-
trodes in high-energy-density Li–S batteries.

INTRODUCTION
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries represent one of the most promising
next-generation energy storage technologies, primarily due to their
exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g1) and
energy density (2600 Wh kg1), significantly surpassing conven-
tional lithium-ion batteries.1–3 Furthermore, the use of sulfur as the
cathode active material offers compelling advantages of natural abun-
dance, low cost, and environmental benignity.4,5 These attributes
make Li–S batteries highly attractive for applications demanding
extended range, such as electric vehicles and portable electronics.
However, the practical realization of Li–S batteries faces formidable
challenges stemming from both the sulfur cathode and the lithium
metal anode. The cathode suffers from the notorious polysulfide shut-
tle effect,6,7 where soluble intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPSs)
diffuse between electrodes, leading to active material loss, rapid
capacity fading, and poor Coulombic efficiency.8–10 Concurrently, the
lithium anode is plagued by uncontrolled dendrite growth during
cycling,2,11,12 posing serious risks of internal short circuits and battery
failure, alongside continuous electrolyte consumption due to reactions
with LiPSs and the unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).13–15
To combat the polysulfide shuttle, extensive research has focused
on cathode modification strategies.16–18 These typically involve
designing host materials (e.g., porous carbons,19 conductive poly-
mers20) or introducing functional coatings/separators embedded with
catalytic or adsorbent sites (e.g., polar metal compounds,21 single
metal sites22). While effective in enhancing LiPSs confinement and
conversion kinetics locally, these approaches often rely on fixed, het-
erogeneous catalytic/adsorbent sites primarily concentrated at the
electrode/electrolyte
interface.

restricts interaction with polysulfides diffusing freely in the bulk elec-

centric strategies predominantly address the sulfur cathode issues but
offer little or no direct benefit to the lithium anode,23 leaving the criti-
cal problems of dendrite growth and unstable SEI unresolved. The
complexity and potential cost of sophisticated cathode/separator
engineering can also hinder scalability.
In contrast, electrolyte modification strategies present a distinct
and potentially more versatile pathway.24–26 By introducing functional
additives directly into the electrolyte bulk, they offer the unique
advantage of creating homogeneously distributed, homogenous active
sites throughout the entire cell volume. This enables dynamic and
continuous interaction with polysulfides wherever they diffuse,
potentially offering more comprehensive shuttle suppression than
static interfacial modifications. More importantly, a well-designed
electrolyte additive can potentially exert beneficial effects simulta-
neously at both electrodes.25,27 For instance, addition species could
accelerate LiPSs conversion kinetics in the electrolyte phase, while
also participating in stabilizing the Li anode interface or regulating Li+
flux to promote uniform deposition and suppress dendrites.28 This
dual-functional capability, coupled with the relative simplicity of inte-
grating an additive into the existing electrolyte system, offers a highly
attractive route toward holistic performance enhancement and
improved commercial viability for Li–S batteries. The exploration of
novel electrolyte additives that can accelerate the conversion of lith-
ium polysulfides and promote uniform lithium deposition represents a
compelling frontier in Li–S battery research, offering solutions that go
beyond conventional approaches confined to electrode interfaces.
Here, considering the highly efficient catalytic active sites and the
coordination structure capable of regulating lithium nucleation, nickel
(II) tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP) is incorporated into the electrolyte
system of Li–S batteries. Porphyrin features a large conjugated struc-
ture containing nitrogen atoms, exhibiting lithiophilicity and featuring
a planar macromolecular structure, dynamically migrate to the lithium
metal anode surface to form a dense molecular layer, thereby guiding
uniform Li deposition and mitigating parasitic reactions induced by
direct contact between the lithium metal surface and the electrolyte.
Additionally, Ni metal, as a highly efficient catalytic active metal, is
widely employed as a host material for sulfur cathodes,29–31 particu-
larly the NiN4 sites in NiTPP, which consistently demonstrate excep-
tional

to

rapid

introduction of NiTPP into the electrolyte system enables mobile Ni–

cient homogeneous catalysis of polysulfides. By conducting compre-
hensive testing and validation across diverse systems, the crucial roles
that NiTPP plays at both the anode and cathode can be clarified. Spe-

remarkable specific discharge capacity of 634.9 mAh g1 at a high rate
of 4 C. Moreover, after 100 cycles at a lower rate of 0.2 C, these bat-
teries retain an impressive 758.3 mAh g1, corresponding to a capac-
ity retention rate of 83.4%. Modifying the electrolyte with additives is
a scalable process that can simultaneously address issues at both the
anode and cathode, thereby enhancing the feasibility of industrial
applications for Li–S batteries.

EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1
|
Preparation of electrolyte
The electrolyte formulations were prepared by incorporating NiTPP
(Macklin, 97%) at concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 mM into a conven-
tional ether—based electrolyte system. The base electrolyte was com-
posed of a binary solvent mixture. First, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME,
J&K scientific, 99.9%) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, J&K scientific, 99.9%)
were mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio. To this solvent mixture, 1 M
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide lithium salt (LiTFSI, J&K scientific,
99.9%) and 0.2 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3, Aladdin, 99.9%) were added
as the electrolyte salts. For the preparation of the NiTPP-containing
electrolytes, the appropriate amount of NiTPP was weighed and
added to the pre-prepared conventional ether electrolyte. The result-
ing mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 8 h inside an
argon-filled glovebox (with O2 and H2O levels both below 0.1 ppm) to
ensure complete dissolution of NiTPP and to obtain the electrolytes
required for the subsequent experimental procedures.

nanotubes, and binder (PVDF) were mixed in an agate ball—mill tank

Li2S6 symmetrical batteries
Graphite felt served as the positive and negative electrodes, and
0.2 M Li2S6-DOL/DME solution was used as the electrolyte. Each
graphite felt received 20 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6 solution and 10 μL of
ether-based electrolyte, added to the separator to prepare the
batteries.
3.4
|
Deposition and dissolution of Li2S
Carbon paper acted as the positive electrode, and lithium foil as the
negative electrode, with 0.2 M Li2S8-tetraethylene glycol dimethyl
ether solution as the electrolyte. Each carbon paper received 20 μL of
0.2 M Li2S8 solution, and 20 μL of the prepared ether electrolyte was
added to the separator, forming a battery for testing Li2S deposition
and dissolution.
The electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT 2273) conducted
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry
(CV) tests. EIS measured the frequencies from 100 kHz to 10 mHz,
with an AC voltage amplitude of 5 mV. The voltage range for Li2S6
symmetrical batteries was 0.8 to 0.8 V, with CV curves tested at a
scan rate of 5 mV s1. The deposition process discharged batteries at
0.112 mA to 2.06 V, then held at 2.05 V until the current fell below
105 A. The dissolution process reduced the voltage to 1.8 V at
0.10 mA and 1.7 V at 0.01 mA, ensuring complete conversion from
LiPSs to solid Li2S. The batteries were then charged at a constant
potential of 2.35 V until the current was below 105 A, ensuring com-
plete dissolution of Li2S.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The introduction of electrolyte additives, nickel (II) tetraphenylpor-
phyrin
(NiTPP),
into
ether-based
electrolytes
(1 M
LiTFSI
in
DOL/DME 1:1 (v/v) with 2 wt% LiNO3) offers a significantly simpli-
fied and direct strategy for establishing catalytic sites relative to com-
plex host material synthesis for sulfur cathodes (Figure 1A). This
approach involves simply dissolving the additive into the electrolyte
for immediate use in Li–S cell assembly. Its streamlined process, fea-
turing minimal steps, a shortened preparation cycle, and reduced
material/energy consumption, effectively tackles key Li–S battery
challenges. This additive strategy thus offers a highly cost-effective,
easily integrable solution, better suited for large-scale manufacturing
than complex cathode modifications (Figure 1B). NiTPP, a soluble
organic molecular additive, leverages its highly catalytically active Ni
centers to enable dynamic polysulfide conversion across the electro-
lyte, effectively suppressing the shuttle effect while its mobility
ensures widespread accessibility of active sites at electrode interfaces
and within the bulk, promoting uniform Li2S deposition. Mobile Ni–N4
sites act as homogeneous catalysts in the electrolyte through a
dynamic cycle: they capture soluble polysulfides in the bulk electro-
lyte, transport the complexes to electrode interfaces for rapid catalytic
conversion via proximity to conductive substrates, and then separate
from solid Li2S products due to their solubility, enabling immediate
catalyst regeneration and re-entry into the electrolyte. This spatial
decoupling of capture (bulk) and conversion (interface) processes,
coupled with continuous catalyst recycling, significantly enhances cat-
alytic efficiency and reaction kinetics. Concurrently, at the lithium
anode, its nitrogen-rich conjugated π-system enhances lithiophilicity
to guide homogeneous Li nucleation/deposition, accelerate Li+ trans-
port kinetics, and stabilize the SEI layer, collectively improving battery
cycling stability (Figure 1C). Unlike static catalytic sites restricted to
local cathode-electrolyte interfaces—where Li2S passivation rapidly
degrades
activity
and
anode
processes
remain
unregulated

To gain deeper insight into the impact of NiTPP on lithium metal
anode stability, a series of tests were performed on Li-Li symmetric
cells. As shown in Figure 3A, rate properties at various current densi-
ties revealed significantly reduced overpotential in cells containing
NiTPP compared to the blank electrolyte. Critically, this lower polari-
zation was maintained even at a low current density of 0.2 mA cm2,
demonstrating enhanced interfacial kinetics. Long-term cycling stabil-
ity at a current density of 1 mA cm2 and a capacity of 1 mAh cm2
was remarkably improved by NiTPP addition, extending the cell life-
span from 600 to over 1500 h (Figure 3B). Furthermore, stable opera-
tion exceeding 2000 hours was achieved at 0.5 mA cm2 (Figure S6).
It is noteworthy that during the cycling tests, a certain degree of over-
potential fluctuation was observed in the initial stages, which later
stabilized. This behavior can be attributed to the progressive forma-
tion of a dense and stable NiTPP molecular layer on the Li metal sur-
face. Once fully established, this layer provides effective protection to
the Li anode, thereby contributing to the long-term cycling stability
observed in subsequent cycles. The consistently low overpotential
and smooth voltage profiles throughout cycling indicate significantly
faster ion transport kinetics facilitated by NiTPP and the absence of
failure mechanisms associated with exceeding the Sand's time. The
low electrochemical impedance data also indicate that the NiTPP
additive can facilitate the smooth progress of the lithium plating and
stripping process (Figure 3C).

The electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries with varying
NiTPP concentrations was systematically investigated. Rate capability
tests revealed that NiTPP-containing electrolytes exhibited signifi-
cantly enhanced discharge capacities compared to the blank electro-
lyte across all current densities. Notably, the 2 mM NiTPP system
delivered exceptional high-rate performance with specific capacities
of 1070.3, 903.3, 819.1, 761.0, 709.4, 668.7, and 634.9 mAh g1 at
0.1–4 C rates respectively, demonstrating a remarkable capacity
enhancement at 4 C (Figure 4A). Long-term cycling stability at 0.5 C
further confirmed the beneficial effects of NiTPP addition (Figure 4B).
All NiTPP-containing cells maintained stable CE and significantly
higher capacity than the blank electrolyte throughout 200 cycles. The
2 mM NiTPP achieved the optimal performance with 605.9 mAh g1
capacity remaining after 200 cycles (76% retention), corresponding to
a low-capacity decay rate of 0.12% per cycle. However, the 3 mM
system exhibited accelerated degradation after 140 cycles, indicating
a concentration-dependent threshold beyond which excess NiTPP
induces adverse effects. These results collectively demonstrate that
moderate NiTPP concentration (2 mM) effectively enhances both rate
capability and cycling stability in Li–S batteries, while excessive addi-
tive may compromise electrochemical performance through unfavor-
able side reactions or kinetic limitations.
The concentration-dependent performance of NiTPP electrolytes
in Li–S batteries can be rationalized through electrochemical analysis of
active site utilization and polysulfide regulation mechanisms (Figure 4C).
At low concentration, insufficient active sites limit effective polysulfide
immobilization and rapid catalytic conversion, resulting in inadequate
suppression of shuttle effects and incomplete electrode surface protec-
tion. Conversely, excessive NiTPP concentrations exceed the solubility
limit in the electrolyte, inducing molecular aggregation and precipitation
during the cycling process. As shown in Figure S12, the 3 mM NiTPP
electrolyte exhibited significant sedimentation after standing, whereas
no such phenomenon was observed at concentrations of 2 mM or
lower. These insoluble aggregates not only reduce bioavailable active
sites for polysulfide adsorption but also physically block electrochemical
interfaces through heterogeneous deposition on electrode surfaces.
Such surface coverage impedes ion/electron transport pathways, traps
active sulfur species (dead sulfur formation), and covers lithium metal
(dead lithium accumulation), collectively degrading cycle life and
CE. The optimal 2 mM concentration balances sufficient catalytic activ-
ity with homogeneous dispersion, ensuring maximal active site accessi-
bility while avoiding detrimental interfacial effects. In addition, the
NiTPP additive employed in this work also exhibits certain performance
advantages when compared with other previously reported electrolyte
additives for Li–S batteries (Table S1).

Galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles at various current densities
indicated that cells with 2 mM NiTPP consistently maintained two dis-
tinct discharge plateaus (Figure 5A,B), corresponding to the sequential
reduction of S₈ to long-chain polysulfides (Li₂Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) and further
reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S. Additionally, the NiTPP electrolyte consis-
tently delivered higher discharge capacities alongside significantly
reduced reaction polarization across all rates (Figure 5C). This combi-
nation directly indicates substantially enhanced sulfur redox kinetics,
attributable to the NiTPP additive's efficacy in lowering the conver-
sion energy barriers between S₈ and Li2S. Further quantitative analysis
defined the capacities contributed by the two characteristic discharge
plateaus: ΔQ1 (solid–liquid transition) and ΔQ2 (liquid–solid transi-
tion).37,38 The calculated ΔQ2/ΔQ1 ratio was significantly higher for
the 2 mM NiTPP cell compared to the blank electrolyte (Figure 5D).
This elevated ratio provides strong evidence that the mobile Ni sites
within the NiTPP additive effectively capture soluble polysulfides and
catalyze their efficient conversion, particularly enhancing the crucial
liquid–solid transformation step.
The addition of 2 mM NiTPP to the electrolyte significantly
enhances the electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries, even
under low current density. 2 mM NiTPP cells retained a high specific
capacity of 758.3 mAh g1 after 100 cycles (Figure 5E), corresponding
to a capacity retention of 83.4%. In stark contrast, control cells with
blank electrolyte suffered rapid capacity decay, delivering only 342.0
mAh g1 after the same number of cycles. This improvement corre-
lates with stabilized CE in NiTPP-containing cells, which maintained
consistent CE values throughout cycling, in contrast to pronounced
fluctuations observed in the blank system. Such CE stability also impli-
cates NiTPP's role in suppressing parasitic shuttle reactions and
electrolyte decomposition. The additive's efficacy persisted under
practical stress conditions. At elevated sulfur loadings (2.9 and
3.9 mg cm2) with lean electrolyte (E/S = 5 μL mg1), the 2 mM
NiTPP-containing cells delivered 757.5 mAh g1 and 511.6 mAh
g1 respectively after 100 cycles (Figure 5F). Mechanistically, the
consistent capacity delivery and CE stabilization imply NiTPP mod-
ulates redox reaction pathways through catalytic mediation of
polysulfide conversion via mobile Ni sites, while its chelating effect
of N-containing porphyrin structure likely suppresses electrolyte
decomposition. These synergistic effects collectively demonstrate
NiTPP as a viable electrolyte engineering strategy for achieving
high-energy-density, long-cycling Li–S batteries under industrially
relevant conditions.

CONCLUSION
In summary, this work demonstrates that soluble nickel tetrapyridyl-
porphyrin (NiTPP) serves as an effective bifunctional electrolyte addi-
tive for high-performance Li–S batteries. The mobile Ni–N4 catalytic
sites enable high-efficiency capture and catalytic conversion of poly-
sulfides within the electrolyte bulk, significantly enhancing sulfur
redox kinetics. Simultaneously, the nitrogen-rich planar structure facil-
itates the formation of a lithiophilic molecular layer on the lithium
metal surface, which promotes uniform lithium deposition while sup-
pressing interfacial side reactions, thereby constructing a stable anode
interface. This synergistic dual functionality results in exceptional
electrochemical performance: high-rate capabilities of 761.0, 709.4,
and 634.9 mAh g1 at 1, 2, and 4 C, respectively, and stable cycling
with 83.4% capacity retention (758.3 mAh g1) after 100 cycles at
0.1 C. Critically, the homogeneous catalytic nature of soluble metal
porphyrins offers distinct advantages over immobilized active sites:
the mobile catalytic centers simultaneously optimize both sulfur cath-
ode and lithium anode processes. Furthermore, the straightforward
electrolyte additive approach presents excellent compatibility with
existing industrial manufacturing processes, facilitating scalable imple-
mentation for advanced Li–S batteries.
