ABSTRACT
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are among the most promising next-generation battery technologies due to their high theoretical
specific capacity. Li-S battery performance is intricately linked to materials and cell design, with electrolyte design playing
a particularly crucial role owing to the complex mechanisms within the cell. Thus, the primary challenge in realizing high-
performance Li-S batteries lies in achieving reasonable capacities and cycling performance at low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratios,
which might be attainable through unconventional electrolyte systems. This study aims to explore how varying the amount and
composition of electrolytes affects the performance of Li-S batteries across different electrolyte systems. Sulfolane (SL)-Lithium
bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) and triglyme (G3)-LiTFSI electrolytes demonstrate superior cycling performance
across a range of E/S ratios. Notably, the SL-LiTFSI electrolyte system shows promise under lean electrolyte conditions, demon-
strating superior performance at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1, likely due to sulfolane’s electrochemical stability, high dielectric
constant, and donor number.
1 | Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries are among the most widely used electro-
chemical storage technologies in various sectors, especially trans-
portation. Even though Li-ion batteries have high energy and
power densities and long life, they have high costs and limited
capacity for newly emerging technologies such as electric
vehicles [1–3]. Consequently, researchers seek new battery sys-
tems with higher capacity and lower cost. Lithium-sulfur
(Li-S) batteries have recently been a top research topic on
next-generation energy storage technologies due to their high
theoretical specific energy that is three to fivefold (nearly
2600 Wh/kg) of Li-ion batteries and the high theoretical specific
capacity, 1675 mAh g−1, of their cathode active material, sulfur
[4–9]. In addition to this, sulfur is low-cost, naturally abundant,
and nontoxic [10–13]. However, due to the complicated mecha-
nisms within Li-S cells during cycling, several challenges must be
handled before commercialization. One of the biggest obstacles is
the shuttling of the lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) produced by the
complex reactions in the cell [12, 14–16]. This shuttle mechanism
leads to vital problems, such as the deterioration of the anode and
loss of active cathode material [17, 18]. In addition to the poly-
sulfide shuttle mechanism, the instability of the Li metal and the
insulating nature of S and the final discharge product Li2S, hin-
dering the reaction kinetics, are major concerns. All these
obstacles give rise to capacity fading, deficient coulombic effi-
ciency, and short cycle life [1, 19–22]. There are different
approaches in the literature to overcome these challenges,
including engineering the cathode [4, 23–26], using a protective
layer on the anode surface [27–29], and modifying the separator
[30–34].

trolyte design, as the electrolyte type, properties, and amount

and polysulfide shuttle mechanisms. Consequently, the ideal
electrolyte should be chemically and electrochemically stable
across a wide range of voltage and temperature, have high ionic
conductivity (10−3 to 10−2 S cm−1 at room temperature), compat-
ibility with the Li metal, and low viscosity [35]. Therefore, the
selection of the electrolyte components is highly determinative
of battery performance. Conventionally, ether-based electrolytes
are used in the cell due to their compatibility with the cathode,
low viscosity, and stability toward the polysulfides. However, in
ether-based electrolytes, the shuttle effect is severe, and the rapid
evaporation of solvent causes safety concerns [36]. The most
commonly used electrolyte system in Li-S batteries is 1 M lithium
bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonomide
(LiTFSI)
in
1,3-dioxolane
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxy ethane (DME) mixture (1:1 vol%).
DOL:DME exhibits excellent polysulfide solubility and strong
chemical stability against polysulfides [36]. DME, a polar solvent
with higher polysulfide solubility, provides high polysulfide
kinetics but is more likely to react with lithium. DOL reduces
polysulfide solubility but aids in creating a more stable solid elec-
trolyte interface (SEI) on the lithium anode surface [36]. A mix-
ture of DOL:DME offers a compromise between electrolyte
characteristics such as ionic conductivity, polysulfide solubility,
mobility, and the generation of an SEI on the lithium anode.
However, the ether solvents’ low flash point creates a variety
of safety concerns, mainly when operating at higher tempera-
tures [36, 37].
The salt used in the electrolyte should have high ionic conduc-
tivity and not lead to unwanted side reactions with the LiPSs.
LiTFSI, the most widely used salt in Li-S battery electrolytes,
enhances the electrolyte’s electrochemical stability and ionic
conductivity. Lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiTF) and lith-
ium perchlorate (LiClO4) are alternative salts that can be used in
electrolyte design. The former helps the solubility of shorter-
length LiPSs and has high thermal stability, while the latter acts
as a prohibitor of the polysulfide shuttle mechanism [38–40]. As
for the solvents, they should have high solubility for short-chain
polysulfides and poor solubility for long-chain polysulfides.
Triglyme (G3) is a promising candidate as a solvent since it
has high polysulfide solubility and is chemically stable against
polysulfides [41]. Also, it has lower flammability, flash point,
and toxicity than the conventional electrolyte [42]. Another
promising electrolyte solvent is sulfolane (SL), which can solve
the shorter-length polysulfides formed during cycling owing to its
high dielectric constant. Sulfolane is also thermally stable and
has a high boiling point and chemical stability, allowing the
use of reactive salts [38, 43].
In addition to electrolyte components, the electrolyte-to-sulfur
(E/S) ratio is also a crucial parameter for electrolyte design
because it considerably affects the electrochemical performance
and system- and cell-level-specific energy and energy density of
the Li-S batteries. Also, the E/S ratio plays a significant role in the
reactions and the polysulfide shuttle mechanism because of its
effect on the polysulfide concentration on the cathode surface
[44–46]. At very low E/S ratios, the discharge capacity and
cycling performance are poor; an increase in the electrolyte vis-
cosity due to the rising polysulfide concentration results in lower
ionic conductivity and poor reaction kinetics. Moreover, low E/S
ratios may lead to a deficiency of the electrolyte in the cell due to
Li metal consuming the electrolyte via unwanted reactions [47].
An increase in the electrolyte amount impacts the voltage, cycle
life, and capacity of the cell favorably by improving sulfur utiliza-
tion and reaction kinetics. However, a further increase in the E/S
ratio adversely affects capacity retention since it may accelerate the
polysulfide shuttle mechanism. High electrolyte amounts also
diminish the energy density [8, 35, 45, 46, 48–55]. Recently, we
developed electrochemical models to reveal the impact of E/S ratio
and electrolyte type on battery performance [56].
To achieve high energy density at the pack level, which is critical
for commercializing these batteries, the E/S ratio should be min-
imized within the cell. However, as discussed, battery perfor-
mance decreases severely with decreasing electrolyte amount
for the conventional electrolyte system [47, 55, 57–62]. There-
fore, the bottleneck of achieving high-performance Li-S batteries
is to attain reasonable capacities and cycling performance at low
E/S ratios, which may be possible using electrolyte systems dif-
fering from the conventional one [61, 62]. In this regard, this
study aims to mechanistically investigate the effect of the electro-
lyte amount and components on the performance of Li-S batter-
ies with different electrolyte systems.
2 | Results and Discussion
Figure 1 shows the cycling performance of Li-S batteries with
various electrolyte systems, SL-LiTFSI, SL-LiTF, SL-LiClO4,
G3-LiTFSI, and G3-LiTF, at four different E/S ratios. At higher
E/S ratios and flooded conditions, cells exhibit initial discharge
capacities above 800 mAh g−1. It is observed that Li-S batteries
containing SL-LiTFSI and G3-LiTFSI electrolytes demonstrate
superior cycling performance compared to other electrolytes at
almost all E/S ratios. This superiority may be attributed to the
perfect cycling stability and rate capability of the LiTFSI [63].
Despite these benefits, LiTFSI may lead to higher viscosity,
decreasing ionic conductivity [38, 63–65], especially at low E/S
ratios, exacerbating the polysulfide shuttle mechanism [50, 51].
This is apparent in Figure 1d, particularly in the G3-LiTFSI elec-
trolyte; at an E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1, the cell with G3-LiTFSI shows
inferior performance. The cycling performance of the Li-S cell
with SL-LiTFSI is better than that of G3-LiTFSI, most probably
due to the electrochemical stability, high dielectric constant, and
donor number of sulfolane [38, 63, 66]. Sulfolane-based electro-
lytes have higher coulombic efficiencies (CEs) at all E/S ratios
around 90%, whereas glyme-based electrolytes have lower and
noisier CEs, demonstrating the high amount of shuttling in
Figure S1. Even though LiClO4 has been reported to result in
high coulombic efficiency and suppress the shuttle effect
[40, 46, 65], cells with the SL-LiClO4 electrolyte exhibit the lowest
discharge capacity for almost all E/S ratios (except for 16 mL g−1).
While the cycling performance of Li-S cells with SL-LiTFSI is not
affected by the alteration of the E/S ratio, a change in the E/S
ratio affects the performance of the other electrolytes. In partic-
ular, G3-LiTF shows the best discharge capacity and capacity
retention at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1 (Figure 1d) despite
having low coulombic efficiencies (Figure S1). The alteration of
the voltage profiles of all electrolytes at various cycles is pre-
sented in Figures S2-S6. These results show that electrolytes with
different properties are not affected by the variation of the elec-
trolyte amount in the same way.

Sulfur utilization is the measure of how close the high
voltage capacity is to the theoretical high voltage capacity
(418 mAh g−1), indicating the ability of active solid sulfur material
present in the cathode to be turned into polysulfides dissolved in
electrolyte. On the other hand, the capacity ratio is the ratio of the
low-voltage capacity to the high-voltage capacity, which has a the-
oretical value of 3, highlighting the reduction of longer-chain poly-
sulfides to the final discharge product Li2S [79]. Figure S8 depicts
the sulfur utilization and capacity ratio for different electrolyte sys-
tems for an E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1. With the increase in the cycle
number, sulfur utilization decreases, likely due to the loss of active
material with polysulfide shuttling, electrode surface passivation,
or side reactions. Generally, sulfolane-based electrolytes have
higher sulfur utilization than glyme-based electrolytes. It can fur-
ther be deduced that the higher sulfur utilization can also be bene-
ficial for having higher system-level energy densities. The
SL-LiTFSI system outperforms other electrolyte systems, consis-
tently demonstrating higher sulfur utilization across cycling. As
in the case of the capacity ratio, it increases slightly with an
increase in the cycle number. For glyme-based electrolyte systems,
the capacity ratio is higher than that of sulfolane-based electrolytes,
showing a higher reduction possibility of longer chain polysulfides
to lower chain polysulfides.

3 | Experimental
The carbon-sulfur composite cathode was prepared first, adjust-
ing the weight percentages of sulfur, carbon (Super C65), and
binder (PVDF) as 60, 30, and 10, respectively, corresponding
to a carbon-to-sulfur ratio of 0.5. The obtained sulfur loading
was 1.1–1.3 mg cm−2, and 2.01 cm2 cathode disks were used.
Before the preparation of the electrolytes, sulfolane and triglyme
solvents were dried by molecular sieves (0.4 nm, Sigma Aldrich),
which were freshly calcined. Then, dried solvents were mixed
with the required amounts of salts with a magnetic stirrer over-
night in a glovebox (MBraun) in which the humidity and oxygen
contents were less than 0.5 ppm. Five different electrolyte sys-
tems were prepared: 1 M LiTFSI, 1 M LiTF, or 1 M LiClO4 in sul-
folane and 1 M LiTFSI or 1 M LiTF in triglyme. To assemble
CR2032-type coin cells, metallic lithium anode (2.01 cm2,
170 μm) and polymeric Celgard (3.01 cm2, 25 μm) separators
were used under an inert atmosphere. Cells with different E/S
ratios of 6, 9, 16, and 23 mL g−1 were prepared for different types
of electrolytes, keeping other parameters such as the sulfur load-
ing and carbon-to-sulfur ratio constant. The prepared cells were
kept at the open circuit voltage (OCV) for 16 h for stabilization.
Galvanostatic cycling was carried out at room temperature by a
Neware battery cycler. After resting, each cell was cycled at 0.1 C-
rate between cut-off voltages of 1.5–2.8 V. For the EIS measure-
ments, cells again rested at the OCV for 16 h. Then, at the end of
the rest period and the end of the first, third, fifth, eighth, and
tenth discharge, the EIS measurements were conducted within
10 mHz to 1 MHz, with a 10 mV amplitude using Biologic SP
300/300e. Before each EIS measurement, cells rested for
30 min for equilibration. In the EIS characterization, the lowest
E/S ratio, 6 mL g−1, was chosen since we are particularly inter-
ested in low E/S ratios for practical Li-S batteries. Experiments
were conducted with at least three replicates, and the results
were reported as averages of replicates. CV analysis was per-
formed between 1.5 and 3.0 V with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1
by using Biologic SP 300/300e. The lithium electrodes for mor-
phological characterization were obtained by disassembling the
cells in a glovebox and rinsing them with DME to remove loosely
bound compounds and lithium salts; they were then dried over-
night under an inert atmosphere. They were transferred to the
SEM in vacuum containers. The electrode morphologies were
characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM;
Thermo Scientific, Quattro S), and energy-dispersive spectros-
copy (EDS) analysis was conducted with the SEM system.
4 | Conclusion
The impact of the E/S ratio on the electrochemical performance
of Li-S batteries with various electrolyte systems was analyzed.
1 M LiTFSI and 1 M LiTF salts in sulfolane and triglyme solvents,
and 1 M LiClO4 salt in sulfolane electrolyte systems were tested
for E/S ratios of 6, 9, 16, and 23 mL g−1. SL-LiTFSI and G3-LiTFSI
electrolytes exhibit superior cycling performance compared to
others across various E/S ratios, owing to the exceptional cycling
stability and rate capability of LiTFSI. However, at the lowest E/S
ratio of 6 mL g−1, the Li-S cell with SL-LiTFSI demonstrates bet-
ter cycling performance than G3-LiTFSI, likely due to the elec-
trochemical stability, high dielectric constant, and donor number
of sulfolane. Yet, G3-LiTF exhibits the highest discharge capacity
and capacity retention at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1 across
all electrolytes despite suffering from low CE. EIS measurements
reveal two semicircles and a linear line, indicative of Warburg
diffusion resistance, across all electrolytes. The total cell resis-
tance peaks in the initial cycle and slightly decreases afterward.
CV analysis of Li-S cells and morphological investigations of Li
anodes cycled with different electrolyte systems also enriched our
mechanistic analysis. Overall, the impact of the E/S ratio on
discharge capacity and cycling performance varies among differ-
ent electrolyte systems, highlighting the significant role of
the solvent and its combination with lithium salts in battery
performance. Designing electrolytes for optimal performance
under lean electrolyte conditions is crucial for achieving high-
performance Li-S batteries. Notably, the 1 M LiTFSI in sulfolane
