ABSTRACT: Li−S batteries have been regarded as one of the most promising
alternatives of the next-generation Li batteries. However, the dissolution and shuttling
of lithium polysulﬁdes lead to low cycle stability and low Coulombic eﬃciency, which
intensively hinder the practical application of Li−S batteries. Herein, we propose a
strategy to simultaneously promote the redox kinetics and inhibit the shuttle of
lithium polysulﬁdes, through in situ synthesis of insoluble organopolysulﬁdes by
adding a special additive. Attractively, the thus-formed insoluble organopolysulﬁdes
in the form of nanoparticle aggregates are also capable of adsorbing unconverted
lithium polysulﬁdes and hence eﬀectively spatially suppress the shuttle eﬀect.
Furthermore, the organopolysulﬁdes served as active redox mediators, showing faster
redox kinetics of S chemistry than that of lithium polysulﬁdes. As a result, the Li−S
batteries showed impressive capacity, improved rate performance, and long cycling
stability even under lean-electrolyte and high sulfur loading conditions.
KEYWORDS: organopolysulﬁdes, Li−S batteries, additives, redox kinetics, shuttle eﬀect
T
he rapid development of portable electronics, electric
vehicles, and smart grids creates large demand for
energy storage devices with not only high energy
density but also low cost.1,2 Therefore, various alternatives of
traditional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been devel-
oped.3−9 Among these, Li−S batteries are one of the most
promising candidates due to their ultrahigh theoretical energy
density (2600 Wh kg−1) and the advantages of environmental
benignity and the low cost of sulfur.10−12 However, the back
and forth diﬀusion of polysulﬁdes during the cycling leads to
rapid capacity loss,13−15 which strongly precludes the develop-
ment and practical application of Li−S batteries.
Much eﬀort has been devoted to addressing the dissolution
and shuttle issue of polysulﬁdes in Li−S batteries, such as (1)
adding a functional separator16−22 to conﬁne the shuttle of
polysulﬁdes, (2) using a micro-/nanohost to encapsulate or
adsorb the polysulﬁdes,23−25 and (3) modifying the electrolyte
or electrodes by adding additives to protect either the cathode
or anode.26,27 Compared with these methods of spatial
restriction of the soluble polysulﬁdes, the strategies through
the mediation of redox kinetics of S chemistry to directly
reduce or even eliminate the presence of soluble polysulﬁdes
are more attractive,28−31 which could be achieved by adding
heterogeneous mediators (such as metal oxides,22,32−35 metal
sulﬁdes36−38 and MXene,39−42 etc.). It can be understood that
the mediation process is highly dependent on the exposed
surface of the mediators and hence could not eﬀectively
modulate the redox kinetics of S chemistry. In order to form
homogeneous interactions with the polysulﬁdes, soluble
organic redox mediators such as conjugated quinones43,44 or
lithium t-butyl disulﬁdes45 are introduced into the electrolyte
or electrodes recently, which could eﬀectively modulate the
redox kinetics and has the advantages of easy processing and
good compatibility with current commercial technology of
LIBs. However, the dissolution of such organic redox
mediators would lead to even severe shuttle eﬀect and anode
corrosion.
Herein, we proposed a strategy to simultaneously promote
the redox kinetics and inhibit the shuttle of lithium
polysulﬁdes, through in situ synthesis of insoluble organo-
polysulﬁde redox mediators by adding certain amount of
additives. Encouraged by our previous works46 demonstrating

and the Li-salt anions or the solvent molecules could regulate
the Li solvation sheath, 1,3,5-triformylphloroglucinol (TFP)
was selected here to interact with polysulﬁde anions.
Attractively, the addition of TFP resulted in the formation of
insoluble organopolysulﬁdes, which were in the form of
nanoparticle aggregates and also capable of adsorbing
unconverted lithium polysulﬁdes; hence, these could eﬀectively
spatially suppress the shuttle eﬀect of lithium polysulﬁdes

active redox mediators, showing faster redox kinetics of S
chemistry than that of lithium polysulﬁdes. As a result, with
eﬀective spatial suppression of shuttle eﬀect and suﬃcient
mediation of the redox kinetics of S chemistry, the Li−S
batteries with TFP showed impressive capacity, improved rate
performance, and long cycling stability even under lean-
electrolyte and high sulfur loading conditions. These results
demonstrate a method to achieve eﬃcient spatial and kinetic
co-mediation by insoluble organopolysulﬁde redox mediators
and shed light on practical applications of Li−S batteries.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In Situ Formation of Organopolysulﬁdes. TFP was
synthesized according to our previous works.46 When TFP was
added into the solution of lithium polysulﬁdes (e.g., Li2S6), the
TFP could spontaneously react with Li2S6 and generate
insoluble organopolysulﬁdes (white ﬂocks), as shown in Figure
1b. Figure S1 demonstrates the rough calibration of the
amount of TFP that was required for the conversion of soluble
Li2S6 into insoluble organopolysulﬁdes. It showed that after
addition of 0.75 equiv of TFP relative to the amount of Li2S6,
the solution became almost colorless with pale yellow ﬂocks;
when the amount of TFP was increased to 1.0 equiv, the color
of ﬂocks completely changed to white. Because of the hydroxyl
groups in TFP, it could form strong intermolecular interactions
between TFP molecules and may react with lithium
polysulﬁdes (Figure S2). The formation of H2S in the system,
as shown in Figure 1b, veriﬁed the possible reaction of TFP
with lithium polysulﬁdes, resulting in the organopolysulﬁdes.
Because the strong intermolecular interactions including the
hydrogen bonds and the ion−ion (or ion−dipole between Li+
with anions, O, or S atoms) interactions, the formed
organopolysulﬁdes became insoluble. Considering the com-
plexity of lithium polysulﬁdes and the corresponding organo-
polysulﬁdes, the ﬂocks and the ﬁltrate were investigated. The
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the ﬂocks
(Figure 1c) showed aggregates of nanoparticles, which could
be ascribed to the strong intermolecular interactions as
mentioned above. The colorless ﬁltrate indicated the complete
conversion of lithium polysulﬁdes into organopolysulﬁdes.

Considering the strong intermolecular interactions, the ﬂocks
could probably further adsorb lithium polysulﬁdes. As shown
in the inset of Figure 1c, when the white ﬂocks were added into
the Li2S6−DME solution, the yellow solution also gradually
became colorless. The rough calibration of the amount of
lithium polysulﬁdes that could be adsorbed by the insoluble
organopolysulﬁdes was also conducted, as shown in Figure S3.
It showed that after addition of 0.4 equiv more lithium
polysulﬁdes, the solution could remain colorless. However, the
addition of more Li2S6 solution would result in a colored
solution, showing that the minimum amount of TFP was about
78.1 wt % relative to sulfur (S8) for inhibiting the dissolution
and shuttle of polysulﬁdes. The combination of conversion of
lithium polysulﬁdes into insoluble organopolysulﬁdes with the
further adsorption of unconverted lithium polysulﬁdes is
expected to eﬀectively suppress the shuttle of polysulﬁdes,
improve the reaction kinetics of S chemistry, and hence
enhance the cycling stability of Li−S batteries.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out to
investigate the chemical composition of the white ﬂocks.
According to the S 2p spectra (Figure 1d), the peak at around
168 eV can be assigned to the O−S bonds, indicating that the
removal of H+ of −OH bonds in TFP and the formation of O−
S−or O−Sn−Li (n ≥1). Besides, in the O 1s spectra, the peak
at around 529.5 eV could be identiﬁed to the presence of Li−
O bonds, indicating the formation of O−Li bonds. Meanwhile,
the signal of the CO bond was still present, indicating that
the −CHO groups remained unchanged. Moreover, in the S 2p
spectra, the peaks at around 162.9 and 161.8 eV could be
identiﬁed to the bridge (SB
0) and terminal (ST
−1) S atoms,
respectively, indicating that the resulted solids still maintained
the typical linear structure of polysulﬁdes and hence the
electrochemical activity depending on the multielectron
transfer process of S chemistry could be retained. Besides, in
the Li 1s spectra, the peak at around 53.7 eV could be assigned
to the Li−S bonds, which conﬁrmed the presence of either
lithium polysulﬁdes or organopolysulﬁdes in the solids.
Furthermore, the FTIR spectroscopy (Figure S4) of the
white ﬂocks showed the −CO bond and −CH bond signals
of the −CHO groups, which could rule out the possible
thionation of the aldehyde. In contrast, the −OH peak of RMs
at around 1434 cm−1 disappeared, which could further verify
the lithiation of the hydroxyl group and/or the formation of
organopolysulﬁdes. These results agreed well with the XPS
characterization.
In order to understand more deeply the chemical structures
of the organopolysulﬁdes, MALDI-TOF mass spectra (MS)
was utilized. As shown in Figures 2 and S5 (negative and
positive mode, respectively), the fragments of partially lithiated
TFP, lithiated TFP, organopolysuﬁldes (lithiated or unlithi-
ated) with linear sulfur chains of diﬀerent lengths, dimers
interacted by hydrogen bonds (e.g., O−H···O, O−H···S.
Similar ionic interactions, such as O−Li···O, O−Li···S, or S−
Li···O bonds are also possible) were all observed. These results
coincide well with the XPS analysis, showing the presence of
O−S, S−S, O−Li, and S−Li bonds. The formation of these

Kinetic Evaluation of S Chemistry. Subsequently, cells
were utilized to evaluate the redox kinetics of Li−S batteries by
using carbon black (super P) as symmetric electrodes and Li2S6
solution as electrolyte. As shown in Figure 3a, the CV proﬁles
of super P/Li2S6/super P symmetric cells showed negligible
redox peaks, because the polysulﬁdes detached from the
current collector were not electrochemical reactive and would
only result in linear I−V curves. On the contrary, with the
addition of TFP into the electrodes, the direct reaction of TFP
and polysulﬁdes resulted into insoluble organopolysulﬁdes,
which were spatially conﬁned in the electrodes and
consequently the symmetric cells showed obvious redox
behavior (Figure 3b). Furthermore, the capacitive characters
of I−V curves (originated from the fast charge transfer and
ionic diﬀusion by using super P as electrodes) also indicated
the promoted redox kinetics of the liquid−solid conversion
(the conversion between soluble polysulﬁdes (e.g., Li2S6 and
Li2S8) and insoluble polysulﬁdes (e.g., Li2S, Li2S2 and
organopolysulﬁdes)). However, we noted that the current
was lower than that without addition of TFP, which can be
ascribed to the consumption of Li2S6 originating from the
reaction between TFP and Li2S6 (as shown in Figure S2) and
the probably low conductivity of the formed organo-
polysulﬁdes.
Typical Li−S cells were further assembled by using sulfur
(with or without TFP) as cathodes and Li as anodes. The two
cells with or without TFP in the cathodes showed similar initial
reduction peaks, which could be ascribed to the similar
mechanism. As mentioned above, the organopolysulﬁdes are
organic materials with sulfur chains of diﬀerent lengths. Hence,
the intrinsic electrochemical behavior of the organopolysulﬁdes
should be similar to sulfur in typical Li−S batteries: both of
them are involving in the cleavage and reformation of S−S
bonds. However, the TFP-based Li−S cells exhibited higher
current densities of the oxidation peaks, indicating that the in
situ formation of organopolysulﬁdes during the redox reactions
facilitated the reaction kinetics and inhibited the dissolution of
lithium polysulﬁdes into the electrolytes (Figure S6). Besides,
the promoted redox kinetics of liquid−solid/solid−liquid
conversion process can be clearly observed in the charge/
discharge curves of asymmetric Li−S batteries at 0.2C.47 As
shown in Figure 3c, the negative potential peak was
corresponding to the overpotential for initial nucleation
process of insoluble polysulﬁdes. Obviously, the Li−S cells
with addition of TFP into the S electrodes showed lower
nucleation overpotential of insoluble polysulﬁdes (17 vs 37 mV
for the cells without TFP). However, in the typical charge
curves (Figure 3d), the positive potential peak referred to the
overpotential for the conversion from insoluble to soluble
polysulﬁdes, which was conventionally regarded as one of the
slowest process in S chemistry.14 In the cells without the
presence of TFP, the overpotential was as high as 90 mV. In
sharp contrast, the overpotential reduced to only 7 mV after
the addition of TFP into the cathodes, indicating the
accelerated redox kinetics. To further verify the promoting
eﬀect of organopolysulﬁdes on the redox kinetics of the
liquid−solid conversion, chronoamperometry tests were
carried out on batteries without and with addition of TFP in
the electrodes, demonstrated by the deposition of Li2S.48 The
cells were ﬁrst discharged to 2.1 V at 0.1 mA, and then
potentiostatically discharged at 2 V for electrodepositing Li2S.
As shown in Figure S7, the nucleation peak of the cells with
TFP appeared slightly later than that without TFP, which can
be ascribed to the poor conductivity of the formed organo-
polysulﬁdes. However, the current of the peak was much
stronger, indicating that the electrochemical reaction kinetics
were improved after the addition of TFP. The nucleation test
was consistent with the previous analysis. These results
indicated that the organopolysulﬁdes eﬀectively promoted
the redox kinetics of both liquid−solid and the solid−liquid
conversion in Li−S batteries.
First-principle calculations were adopted to reveal the
possible reason why the insoluble organopolysulﬁdes showed
faster redox kinetics than polysulﬁdes. Figure 3e showed the
theoretically calculated lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)
energy levels of diﬀerent S species. Obviously, the speciﬁc
organopolysulﬁdes possessed lower LUMO and HOMO
energy levels and narrower band gaps than those of the
conventional lithium polysulﬁdes. Typically, narrower band
gaps mean higher conductivity and faster charge transfer
(which could be further proved by the lower resistance and
lower voltage hysteresis below), indicating the faster redox
kinetics after formation of organopolysulﬁde species.

Electrochemical Performance of Li−S Batteries. To
further investigate the spatial and kinetic comediation on the
Li−S batteries, the cycling performance under diﬀerent
conditions was tested. However, it is interesting that when
the normal PP separator was assembled in the Li−S cells with
addition of TFP (50 wt % TFP, which means that the mass of
TFP was 50 wt % of sulfur (S8), i.e., the 23 wt % relative to the
total amount of electrode materials, similarly hereinafter), the
capacity showed rapid loss within initial 100 cycles at 0.2C (1C
= 1673 mA g−1), and a capacity as low as 550 mAh g−1 can be
obtained, although the capacity afterward became stable
(Figure S8). The cells were disassembled after 400 cycles,
showing lots of insoluble organopolysulﬁdes on the PP
separator. In this case, due to the limited conductivity of the
organopolysulﬁdes, the insoluble organopolysulﬁdes on the
separator became “dead sulfur”, which should be the reason
that the capacity is very low. However, the stable capacity
afterward also indicated that the addition of TFP resulted in
the formation of insoluble organopolysulﬁdes, partially
conﬁned in the electrodes and hence beneﬁted for the long-
term cycling.
On the basis of these ﬁndings, a functional separa-
tor9,18,49−51 was adopted to further conﬁne the organo-
polysulﬁdes and fully utilize the sulfur. All the cells below
contained functionalized PP separators that were modiﬁed
with thin layer of super P, if without special note. In this case,
once the soluble lithium polysulﬁdes were generated, it could
react with TFP and form insoluble organopolysulﬁdes around
the cathode. As a result, the conductive super P layer could
serve as “vice electrode” and activate the “dead sulfur”. As
shown in Figure 4a, after functionalization of the separator, the
S cathode without TFP showed initial capacity of 1134 mAh
g−1 with obvious degradation in the subsequent cycles, leading
to a capacity of 762.1 mAh g−1 and Coulombic eﬃciency (CE)
about 93.68% after 150 cycles. Even worse, both the capacity
and CE showed sudden drops after 180 cycles, indicating the
shuttle eﬀect of polysulﬁdes and the failure of battery. When 50
wt % of TFP was added into the S cathodes, the initial capacity
was still higher than the cathode without TFP (1219 vs 1134
mAh g−1), although “sulfur loss” may exist according to the
above analysis (Figures S9 and S10). These results indicated
the optimized redox kinetics in the discharge process reduced
the soluble polysuﬁdes species and the formation of organo-
polysulﬁdes spatially inhibited the shuttle eﬀect. The results
also conﬁrmed that the formed organopolysulﬁdes was still
electrochemically active through the breaking and reformation
of S−S bonds. The TFP based cathodes showed long-term
stability more than 300 cycles with CE of 99.59% and capacity
of 847.6 mAh g−1. The components of the cathodes after
cycling were hence further conducted by MALDI-TOF mass
spectra (negative mode), as shown in Figure S11. The MS
spectra proved the repeated formation of organopolysulﬁdes
after cycling, which are similar to typical Li−S batteries and
enabled the reversibility, although the resulted organo-
polysulﬁdes may be more and more complex.52
Due to the complexity of the reaction between TFP and
lithium polysulﬁdes, it is diﬃcult to determine how much TFP
should be added in the electrodes to optimize the electro-
chemical performance of Li−S batteries. If all of the soluble
polysulﬁdes (e.g., Li2S8) were converted into insoluble
polysulﬁdes (e.g., Li2S2) by TFP, then considering the three
OH groups in TFP, the minimal weight ratio of TFP to S8
should be 1.09:1. However, the synergistic conversion of
soluble lithium polysulﬁdes into insoluble organopolysulﬁdes
and the full adsorption of unconverted lithium polysulﬁdes as
shown above indicated that the weight ratio of TFP could be as
low as 78.1 wt % (means that the mass of TFP was 0.781 times
of sulfur). Furthermore, the strong intermolecular interactions
between polysulﬁdes with TFP may also inhibit the dissolution
of lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, n ≥4); hence, only half of the

overall performance of Li−S batteries, a much lower amount of

TFP (1373 vs1219 mAh g−1). However, less TFP also led to

cause more sulfur loss due to the formation of H2S, which
together with the poor conductivity of TFP resulted in more
capacity decay and even worse cycling stability (Figure S12).
Figure 4b showed the rate performance of Li−S batteries
with or without TFP. It is clear that without the addition of
TFP, the cells showed poor capacity and rate capability,
indicating that the addition of TFP indeed suppressed the
shuttle eﬀect and improved the redox kinetics. Regarding to
the amount of the TFP, the 10 wt % TFP based cathodes
showed a higher capacity in the initial cycles, due to the lower
sulfur loss, which became comparable with that of the 50 wt %
TFP based cathodes (656.4 vs 640 mAh g−1) at a current
density of 3C, indicating that the 10 wt % TFP was insuﬃcient
for realizing the fast kinetic redox of S chemistry. In addition,
when the current density returned to 0.1C, the 10 wt % TFP
based cathodes showed a capacity almost similar to that of the
50 wt % TFP based cathodes, and the capacity degradation was
even faster with lower CE, indicating that the addition of 50 wt
% TFP could more eﬀectively inhibit the shuttle eﬀect of
polysulﬁdes (Figure S13). The long-term cycling of the cells
showed a similar phenomenon. At a current density of 3C
(Figure 4d), the cells without TFP showed a reversible
capacity of only 469 mAh g−1, which was slightly lower than
that of the 50 wt % TFP based cathodes (488 mAh g−1).
However, the capacity of the cells without TFP showed a
sudden plummet after 380 cycles, indicating battery failure
induced by the severe shuttle eﬀect and anode corrosion. On
the contrary, the cathode with 50 wt % of TFP showed
considerable cycling stability for more than 1000 cycles. Again,
the cells with 10 wt % TFP showed an initial capacity higher
than that with 50 wt % of TFP, but with faster degradation.
According to the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
(EIS) (Figure S14), the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of Li−S
batteries with 50 wt % TFP after 10 cycles at 0.2C was much
lower than that of the others, even though the pristine Rct was
the highest due to the insulating TFP. It should be noted that
the capacity contribution from the TFP itself was negligible
(Figure S15), though the dissolution of a small amount of TFP
could also functionalize as bifunctional additive to stabilize the
Li anodes as mentioned in one of our previous works (Figure
S16).46 All of these results indicated that the addition of 50 wt
% TFP was capable of inhibiting the shuttle eﬀect, accelerating
the redox kinetics and hence enhancing the capacity, rate
capability and cyclability of Li−S batteries.
It has been widely accepted that for the practical applications
of Li−S batteries, the electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio should be
as low as possible.53 Therefore, the E/S ratio was reduced to 5
μL mg−1 and the sulfur loading was increased to 3 mg cm−2 to
explore the eﬀect of TFP on the cycling performance of Li−S
batteries under rigid conditions. At a current density of 0.2C,
the TFP-based Li−S batteries showed a high capacity of 877.6
mAh g−1 after 40 cycles and considerable stability over more
than 150 cycles with capacity retention of 87.3% (Figure 4c).
To evaluate the potential application of TFP-based Li−S
batteries, the cells were assembled in harsher conditions
according to the reference.54 When the sulfur loading of Li−S
batteries was increased to 5 mg cm−2, the TFP-based lean-
electrolyte Li−S batteries could still show a capacity higher
than that of Li−S batteries without TFP during 30 cycles
(Figure S17). These electrochemical results indicated that the
addition of TFP could suppress the shuttle eﬀect eﬀectively
through turning the soluble polysulﬁde into insoluble organo-
polysulﬁdes and facilitating the redox kinetics of S chemistry,
therefore leading to stable cycling performance and consid-
erable capacity.

limiting step,31 although the TFP also facilitated the reaction
kinetics of this step (see the smaller polarization after addition
of TFP). However, the capacity from the second plateau,
which referred to the conversion from liquid Li2S4 to solid Li2S
(12e transfer),15 was enhanced signiﬁcantly after addition of
TFP with much smaller polarization, indicating that the
addition of TFP eﬀectively accelerated the reaction kinetics of
the liquid−solid conversion (Figure 5a,b). Although the
addition of TFP resulted in the capacity retention enhance-
ment for both plateaus (the ﬁrst plateau and the following
sloping region: from 74.3% to 92.3 and 88.1% for the cells
without TFP, with 10 and 50 wt % TFP, respectively, after 50
cycles; for the second plateau and the following sloping region:
from 77.1% to 76.0 and 82.1% for the cells without TFP, with
10 and 50 wt % TFP, respectively, after 50 cycles, Figure 5b−
d), the portion of the liquid region (ﬁrst plateau and the
sloping area) in the total capacity (i.e., the value of (Q1 + Q2)/
Q becomes smaller after addition of TFP than that without
TFP, both in the ﬁrst or after 50th cycles; Figure S18),
reconﬁrming that the addition of TFP eﬀectively accelerated
the conversion of liquid lithium polysulﬁdes to solid Li2S.55
The eﬀect of TFP on the inhibition of dissolution and
shuttle of polysulﬁdes was further illustrated visually by
discharge−charge of Li−S batteries in a transparent bottle.
As shown in Figure S19, in the absence of TFP, the electrolyte
was initially colorless. However, the electrolyte gradually
turned yellow when the cell was discharged to 2.2 V,
corresponding to the generation of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes.
After the addition of TFP in the S cathode, due to the
dissolution of TFP, the electrolyte was initially light yellow.
However, when the cell was discharged to 2.2 V, the color of
the electrolyte showed a negligible change, and obvious ﬂocks
can be observed around the cathode. These results visually
showed the evidence that the addition of TFP could form
insoluble organopolysulﬁdes and inhibit the dissolution and
shuttle of polysulﬁdes. The conversion of soluble lithium
polysulﬁdes into the insoluble organopolysulﬁdes, the further
adsorption of unconverted lithium polysulﬁdes by the ﬂocks of
organopolysulﬁdes, the facilitated redox kinetics of S
chemistry, and the functional separator together eﬀectively
enabled the capacity, rate capability and stability of Li−S
batteries.
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we proposed a strategy to promote the redox
kinetics and suppress the shuttle of lithium polysulﬁdes
simultaneously, through in situ synthesis of insoluble organo-
polysulﬁdes redox mediators by adding certain amount of TFP
additives. The addition of TFP could convert the soluble
lithium polysulﬁdes into insoluble organopolysulﬁdes and
facilitate the redox kinetics of S chemistry. Moreover, the
organopolysulﬁdes in the form of nanoparticle aggregates were
able to further adsorb the unconverted lithium polysulﬁdes and
hence spatially inhibit the shuttle eﬀect eﬃciently. As a result,
the TFP-assisted Li−S batteries showed considerable cycling
stability even under low E/S ratio and high sulfur loading
conditions. These results demonstrate a method to achieve
eﬃcient spatial and kinetic co-mediation by insoluble organo-
polysulﬁde redox mediators and shed light on practical
applications of Li−S batteries.
METHODS AND EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. All the materials and solvents were purchased from
commercial sources (Aldrich, Energy, etc.) without further
puriﬁcation. TFP was synthesized according to the literature.56 XPS
measurements were carried out on a ThermoFisher EscaLab 250Xi at
a 58° angle of emission using a monochromatic Al Kα source
(Ephoton = 1486.6 eV) with a 10 mA ﬁlament current and a 14.7 keV
ﬁlament voltage source energy. FE-SEM images were acquired from a
Gemini 300 (Zeiss) scanning electron microscope (SEM).
Electrochemical Characterization. The electrochemical per-
formance was evaluated at room temperature with 2032 coin-type
cells, which were assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox with O2 and H2O
content below 1 ppm. As electrolyte, we employed 1 M LiTFSI with 2
wt % LiNO3 in 1:1 (v/v) 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-
dioxacyclopentane (DOL). The bare cathode was prepared by mixing
sulfur, super P, and PVDF at ratio of 6:3:1 in the presence of NMP.
The TFP-based cathode was prepared by adding a ﬁxed amount of
TFP (based on the mass of sulfur) to the cathode slurry (“10 wt %
TFP” means that the mass of TFP was 10 wt % that of sulfur). Then
the slurry was coated on the Al foil and dried at 55 °C under vacuum
overnight. The resulted cathode possesses a mass loading of 1.2−1.4
mg cm−2. The high-loading cathode (3 mg cm−2) was prepared by
mixing sulfur, super P, and PVDF at ratio of 7:2:1 in the presence of
NMP and adding 50 wt % TFP. Then the slurry was coated on the Al
foil and dried at 55 °C under vacuum overnight. The coin cells were
formed using a LANHE-CT2001A test system (Wuhan, China), and
the CV curves were recorded on a BioLogic VMP3 potentiostat at
room temperature.
Details of DFT Calculations for Monomers and Complexes.
All structures were fully optimized at the level of M06-2X/6-311+
+G(d,p) using the Gaussian 16 program. The frequency calculations
were carried out to conﬁrm the optimized structures being the local
minimum on the potential surfaces. The energy levels of their lowest
unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) and highest occupied
molecular orbitals (HOMO) were calculated at the same level with
the optimization.
