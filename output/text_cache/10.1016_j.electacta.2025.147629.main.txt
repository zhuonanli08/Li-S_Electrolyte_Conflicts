A B S T R A C T
Replacing lithium metal anodes with pre-lithiated graphite (Gr) offers a promising solution to several critical 
challenges in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, including substantial volume expansion, lithium dendrite growth, 
and polysulfide shuttling. However, conventional ether electrolytes suffer from irreversible solvent co- 
intercalation and specifically, conventional dimethoxyethane (DME) molecules infiltrate graphite interlayers, 
causing structural collapse and severely limiting graphite anode application. To overcome this limitation, we 
introduce a weakly solvating electrolyte system employing 2-methyl-1,3-dioxolane (MDOL), and its low Li+
binding energy promotes anion-rich solvation structures, thereby significantly accelerating Li+ desolvation ki­
netics at the electrode-electrolyte interface. This unique solvation chemistry enables exceptional graphite 
compatibility by complete suppression of solvent co-intercalation. The S@C||Li-Gr full cells demonstrate 
remarkable electrochemical performance in MDOL-based electrolyte, achieving a high Coulombic efficiency of 
98.5% and sustaining over 500 cycles at 0.1 C rate with a limited negative/positive (N/P) capacity ratio of ~1.1. 
This work provides a new advanced electrolyte design strategy for the next generation of Li-S batteries, paving 
the way for the development of high-safety, long-cycle Li-S batteries.
1. Introduction
The advancing trend towards electrification and smart technologies 
has intensified the demand for high-energy-density energy storage sys­
tems. While lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) approach their theoretical en­
ergy density limits, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries emerge as a promising 
next-generation alternative, offering exceptional theoretical energy 
density (2600 Wh kg-1), cost-effectiveness, and environmental sustain­
ability [1,2]. However, the practical cycle life of the Li-S batteries is not 
satisfied owing to many critical challenges from Li metal: the shuttle 
effect of polysulfide species in electrolyte reacting with Li anode to 
severely electrolyte/anode interface, as well as significant volume 
expansion of Li anode and uncontrolled dendrites growth during cycle 
[3–7].
To address these challenges, researchers have pursued multiple 
strategies, including: (1) developing polar materials to catalyze and 
adsorb polysulfide [8–10]; (2) engineering lithium metal structure or 
surfaces to enable uniform deposition [11–13]; (3) modulating electro­
lyte systems to construct stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) 
[14–16] and (4) replacing Li metal with Li-hosting materials to stabilize 
interface and suppress volume expansion and dendrites growth [17–19]. 
Among these strategies, using stable graphite as the anode is believed to 
be a very effective approach to obtain long-life Li-S batteries [20,21]. 
Graphite as a commercial LIBs anode, exhibits excellent cycling stability 
larger than 10,000 cycles. However, electrolytes employed in LIBs and 
Li-S batteries are fundamentally incompatible. LIBs typically utilize 
carbonate-based electrolytes (e.g., 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DMC), while Li-S 
systems rely on ether-based formulations (e.g., 1 M LiTFSI in 
DOL/DME). The carbonate electrolytes used in LIBs cannot be used in 
Li-S battery systems because of the polysulfide-induced decomposition

of carbonate solvents [22]. Conversely, the ether-based electrolytes 
commonly used in Li-S batteries are incompatible with graphite. The 
strong binding energy between DME molecules and Li⁺ promotes solvent 
co-intercalation into graphite, causing structural degradation [23,24]. 
Hence, designing an electrolyte simultaneously suitable for graphite 
anode and S cathode is important for developing long-life Li-S batteries.
Weakly solvating electrolytes are characterized by diminished Li+- 
solvent interactions, enabling increased anion participation in solvation 
structures at standard concentrations, to achieve rapid desolvation ki­
netics and avoid solvent co-intercalation into graphite [25,26], which 
have gained increasing recognition for their high ionic conductivity and 
economic viability. In prior work, our group engineered a weakly sol­
vated 1 M LiTFSI-DOL/1,2-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)ethane (TFEE) 
electrolyte via molecular modification of linear ether DME, achieving 
exceptional compatibility with both sulfur cathodes and graphite anodes 
[27]. Herein, through molecular engineering of cyclic ether DOL, we 
engineered a weakly solvated MDOL-based electrolyte by formulating 1 
M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a 3:7 v/v 
blend of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 2-methyl-1,3-dioxolane (MDOL), 
denoted as 1 M DOL+MDOL, with conventional 1 M LiTFSI in 1, 3-diox­
olane (DOL)/1, 2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1 by volume, named 1 M 
DOL+DME) serving as the control electrolyte. As shown in Fig. 1a, in 
conventional ether electrolyte (1 M DOL+DME), the DME will be tightly 
bound to Li⁺ in the solvation structure with its strong solvating capa­
bility, which hinders the desolvation of Li⁺ and results in serious solvent 
co-intercalation in graphite interlayer. Conversely, in 1 M DOL+MDOL 
electrolyte, the weekly solvating capability of MDOL promotes more 
anions to participate in the Li+ solvation structure, which is conductive 
to speeding up the Li+ desolvation process. Hence the Li+ desolvation 
energy in 1 M DOL+MDOL electrolyte is greatly reduced compared with 
the 1 M DOL+DME electrolyte. Due to the fast desolvation kinetics, the 
weakly solvating electrolyte is highly compatible with graphite without 
co-intercalation. Furthermore, the weakly solvating electrolyte can 
inhibit the dissolution of polysulfide in the electrolyte and thus inhibit 
the shuttle effect, which is inevitable in conventional ether electrolytes. 
This point is proved that the S@C||Li battery shows a reversible specific 
capacity up to 1113.2 mAh g-1 at 0.05 C and better capacity retention 
rate of 58.0 % after 100 cycles at 0.1 C in 1 M DOL+MDOL electrolyte 
than those in 1 M DOL+MDOL electrolyte. Especially, when using 
pre-lithiated graphite (Li-Gr) as anode to avoid the volume expansion 
and dendrite growth, and alleviate the side reactions between poly­
sulfide species and lithium metal, the S@C||Li-Gr battery (N/P ≈1.1) 
shows a reversible specific capacity of 773.4 mAh g-1 and can be charged 
and discharged normally within 100 cycles in 1 M DOL+MDOL elec­
trolyte, while there is almost no reversible capacity in 1 M DOL+DME 
electrolyte. When using 2 M DOL+MDOL electrolyte, the S@C||Li-Gr 
battery (N/P ≈1.1) can achieve a specific capacity of 940.1 mAh g−1 
at 0.05 C and a reversible cycle life of more than 500 cycles at 0.1 C, with 
an average Coulombic efficiency of 98.5 %.

S@C electrodes were prepared as followed. (1) Dimethylacetamide 
(DMAC) was dissolved in deionized water to prepare a 15 wt. % aqueous

electrodes were stamped into 12 mm discs, named S@C electrodes, with 
a sulfur content of 58.5 % and a sulfur loading of about 1 mg cm-2.
The preparation process of graphite electrodes is similar to that of 
S@C electrodes. Battery-grade graphite (90 wt. %), conductive acety­

homogenized in N-methylpyrrolidone solvent (NMP). The slurry was 
coated onto copper current collectors and dried at 80 ◦C for 6 h, 
stamping into 15 mm discs and achieving an active material loading of 
≈3.5 mg cm-2. Li-Gr electrodes were prepared by making prepared 
graphite electrodes contact directly with Li foils with the presence of 1 M 
DOL+MDOL electrolyte under pressure for 12 h.
2.3. Preparation of electrolytes
Homogeneous, transparent electrolytes were formulated by dissolv­
ing 1 M or 2 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 
either: (1) 1:1 v/v DOL/DME blends, or (2) 3:7 v/v DOL/MDOL mix­
tures. To probe polysulfide dissolution behavior, sublimed sulfur was 
immersed in these electrolytes. All preparations occurred in an argon- 
filled glovebox (<1 ppm H2O) at 25 ◦C.

2.5. Characterization
Raman spectroscopy (LABRAM, HR) with a laser of 532 nm and 
Fourier Transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Thermo Scientific 
Nicolet 6700 spectrometer) were used to investigate the coordination 
information of ions and molecules in different electrolytes. The lattice 
structures of graphite were examined by X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) 
(Bruker D8 diffractometer) with a 2θ scale of 10–80◦and at a scanning 
rate of 2◦min−1.The morphologies of the graphite electrode surface 
were measured using Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (S4800, 
Hitachi, Japan) with a working voltage of 5 KV. The samples for SEM 
analysis were first rinsed in DOL and dried in the glovebox.
The interfacial chemistry of graphite and sulfur-carbon electrodes 
surface was studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo 
Scientific K-Alpha+), which was conducted on a spectrometer (72 W and 
12 kV) with an Al Kα source. The interfacial chemistry of lithium metal 
electrodes surface was further characterized by Time-of-flight secondary 
ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS, IONTOF TOF.SIMS 5–100), which 
was equipped with a 1 keV Cs+ sputter gun and an electron flood gun for 
charge neutralization.
2.6. Theoretical calculations
The Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed 
by DMol3 module in Materials Studio software [27,29]. The generalized 
gradient approximation (GGA) of the Becke-Lee-Yang-Parr (BLYP) 
functional and a double numerical plus polarization (DNP) basis set was 
employed to describe electronic exchange and correlation effects. The 
convergence tolerances for energy, maximum force, and maximum 
displacement were set to be 2.0 × 10−5 Ha, 4.0 × 10−3 Ha Å−1, and 5.0 ×
10−3 Å. The binding energy (Eb) between solvent and Li+ was calculated 
as following formula: Eb = E(Li+ - Solvent) −E(Li+) – E(Solvent), where 
E(Li+ - Solvent) is the total energy of the Li+ - solvent complex, E(Li+) is 
the energy of Li+, and E(Solvent) is the energy of the solvent molecule.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were also performed by using 
the Forcite module in Materials Studio software [30,31]. Simulations 
were conducted using amorphous cells with dimensions of 42.4 × 42.4 
× 42.4 Å3 and 41.9 × 41.9 × 41.9 Å3, designed to represent the two 
electrolyte systems with molar compositions of 50:212:390 for 1 M 
DOL+MDOL and 50:353:241 for 1 M DOL+DME, respectively. 
Following structural optimization with the COMPASS III force field, the 
systems were equilibrated under NPT conditions for 50 ps. Temperature 
and pressure were maintained at 298.15 K and 1 bar using a Nos´e 
thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Production simulations 
were then carried out in the NVT ensemble, with temperature regulated 
at 298 K via the Nos´e method. An integration time step of 1.0 fs was 
employed throughout the simulations. The total simulation time reached 
300 ps (equivalent to 300,000 steps), with trajectory frames recorded at 
intervals of 1000 steps. To characterize the Li⁺ solvation environment, 
radial distribution functions (RDF) and coordination numbers (CN) were 
computed from the final 150 frames of the molecular dynamics trajec­
tory [32,33].

3. Results and discussion
Electrolyte solvation chemistry involves three fundamental in­
teractions: cation-solvent, cation-anion, and anion-solvent, among 
which the cation-solvent interaction critically regulates solvent stability, 
cation desolvation and transport behavior [34,35]. Density functional 
theory (DFT) calculations revealed that 2-methyl-1,3-dioxolane (MDOL) 
exhibits a higher electrostatic potential (ESP) minimum (-0.057 Ha) and 
weaker Li+ binding energy (-184.71 kJ mol-1) compared to dime­
thoxyethane (DME, -0.07 Ha, -282.70 kJ mol-1), positioning MDOL as an 
ideal solvent for weakly solvating electrolytes as shown in Fig. 1c and d. 
DOL exhibits the highest ESP minimum (-0.055 Ha) and weakest Li+
binding (-171.66 kJ mol-1), aligning with prior reports [36]. To probe 
weak solvation effects, 1 M DOL+MDOL was formulated as the target 
electrolyte, with 1 M DOL+DME serving as the conventional control. 
The basic physical properties of the three solvents are shown in Table S2, 
and the physicochemical properties of different electrolytes are first 
measured. As shown in Fig. S1a, the polysulfide solubility in 1 M 
DOL+DME is much higher than that in 1 M DOL+MDOL. The oxidation 
potential of 1 M DOL+MDOL is about 4.0 V, which is sufficient to meet 
the operating voltage range of Li-S battery (Fig. S1b). The ionic con­
ductivity analysis demonstrates that incorporating DOL into 1 M 
DOL+MDOL enhances charge transport characteristics, with the 
formulated 1 M DOL+MDOL system achieving 1.679 mS cm-1 at 25 ◦C. 
However, this value remains lower than that of 1 M DOL+DME (9.033 
mS cm-1), which is attributed to MDOL’s intrinsically low dielectric 
constant (ε = 7.1) and its inherently weak lithium salt solvation capacity 
(Fig. S1c).
The solvation structure critically governs electrolyte’s ionic trans­
port kinetics through cation-anion-solvent coordination dynamics [28]. 
Raman spectra revealed intensified Li+-TFSI- coordination in the 1 M 
DOL+MDOL, evidenced by the S-N stretching vibration peak 
approaching 747 cm-1 (characteristic of LiTFSI) in Fig. 2a, with Fourier 
transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectra (Fig. 2h) corroborating 
anion-rich solvation structures. Comparative solvent coordination 
analysis demonstrated MDOL’s inherently weak solvation capacity: the 
C-O stretching vibration of DME shifted from 847 cm-1 to 870 cm-1 in 1 
M DOL+DME (Fig. 2b), whereas MDOL exhibited a minimal shift 944 
cm-1 to 950 cm-1 in 1 M DOL+MDOL (Fig. 2c), indicating weaker 
Li+-solvent interaction of MDOL. This attenuated solvent coordination 
inversely strengthened Li+-TFSI- binding, fostering anion-rich solvation 
clusters that accelerate desolvation kinetics. Molecular dynamics (MD) 
simulations further validated these findings: radial distribution func­
tions (RDF) and coordination number (CN) analyses (Fig. 2d-g) showed 
the increase in Li+-TFSI- coordination and a substantial reduction in 
Li+-solvent interactions for the 1 M DOL+MDOL system compared to 
conventional 1 M DOL+DME, conclusively establishing its optimized 
solvation architecture.
The solvation structure of electrolytes fundamentally determines Li+
desolvation behavior, with emerging evidence suggesting that solvent 
coordination dynamics rather than traditional SEI considerations pri­
marily govern graphite compatibility [23]. While SEI’s role warrants 
further investigation, rapid desolvation kinetics demonstrably enhance 
graphite stability. This advantage is absent in Li||Gr cells employing 
conventional 1 M DOL+DME, where serious incomplete desolvation 
persists. To quantify these effects, we conducted temperature-dependent 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on Li symmetric cells 
(Fig. S2a and b), deriving Li+ desolvation energies from charge transfer 
resistance (Rct) Arrhenius plots. The 1 M DOL+MDOL exhibited a lower 
desolvation activation energy (24.11 kJ mol-1 vs. 25.42 kJ mol-1 in 1 M 
DOL+DME systems, Fig. 2i), attributable to weakened Li+-solvent in­
teractions and anion-enriched solvation clusters (Fig. S2c). We further 
quantified Li+ deposition kinetics through exchange current density (i0) 
measurements, where elevated i0 values directly correlate with accel­
erated Li+ desolvation kinetics [37]. Tafel analysis of exchange current 
density in cycled Li/Li cells (0.1 mV s-1, -0.1–0.1 V window) revealed 
faster Li deposition kinetics in 1 M DOL+MDOL (1.318 mA cm-2 vs. 
0.437 mA cm-2, Fig. S2d), directly correlating with enhanced interfacial 
desolvation 
efficiency.

To comprehensively assess cycled graphite structural integrity, we 
conducted multi-modal characterization of graphite electrodes from 
cells cycled in different electrolytes. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis 
(Fig. 3e) reveals that electrodes from 1 M DOL+MDOL cells maintain 
pristine graphite crystallinity, with characteristic (002) and (004) peaks 
at 26.4◦and 55.9◦respectively, alongside unaltered Cu current collector 
signatures (43.2◦, 50.4◦, 76.1◦). Conversely, 1 M DOL+DME cycled 
electrodes exhibit complete (002)/ (004) peak disappearance, indicating 
graphite lattice collapse. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. S4) corroborates 1 M 
DOL+MDOL systems preserve the graphite G-band at 1580 cm-1, while 1 
M DOL+DME induce a 12 cm-1 upshift to 1592 cm-1, characteristics of 
disordered graphene-like structures. The SEM morphology after cycling 
of both electrolytes further confirms MDOL’s superiority. Graphite 
particles cycled in 1 M DOL+MDOL retain structural integrity (Fig. 3f 
and h), whereas electrodes cycled in 1 M DOL+DME display extensive 
fracturing and surface exfoliation (Fig. 3g and i). To elucidate the 
interfacial chemical evolution, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
depth profiling of cycled graphite electrodes revealed similar surface 
chemical composition (Fig. 3j, k and S5). However, MDOL-derived SEI 
contains higher LiF (684.5 eV) content, attributable to preferential TFSI- 
decomposition in anion-rich solvation environments. The LiF-enriched 
SEI establishes a dense barrier that effectively passivates graphite an­

To systematically evaluate the compatibility with sulfur-carbon 
(S@C) cathode in weakly solvated electrolytes, S@C||Li half-cells 
were assembled with varying electrolyte formulations. Initial 
screening of DOL/MDOL volume ratios (Fig. S6) identified the 3:7 v/v (1 
M DOL+MDOL) formulation as optimal, balancing discharge capacity 
and Coulombic efficiency. As shown in Fig. 4a, b and S7, this electrolyte 
delivered 1113.2 mAh g-1 initial capacity at 0.05 C, retaining 58.0 % 
after 100 cycles at 0.1 C with 74.8 % average Coulombic efficiency (CE). 
In contrast, conventional 1 M DOL+DME exhibited higher initial ca­
pacity (1260.3 mAh g-1 at 0.05 C) but suffered rapid degradation (49.3 
% retention, 71.2 % CE), highlighting the trade-off between polysulfide 
utilization and cycling stability. The 1 M DOL+MDOL system’s superior 
stability arises from dual mechanisms: (1) reduced polysulfide solubility 
(Fig. S1a) suppresses active material loss; (2) accelerated Li+ des­
olvation kinetics promotes uniform lithium deposition, evidenced by 
stable CE in Li ||Cu half-cells (Fig. S8) and dendrite-free Li+ deposition 
morphology (Fig. S9). These advantages are more strengthened at -20 
◦C: cells cycled in 1 M DOL+MDOL retained 86.4 % capacity after 50 
cycles and 56.3 % (463.0 mAh g-1) after 100 cycles at 0.1 C (Fig. 4c and 
S10), while 1 M DOL+DME counterparts failed abruptly at 44 cycles 
(33.3 % retention, 346.1 mAh g-1).

To validate the practical viability of 1 M DOL+MDOL in Li-S batte­
ries with lithium-carbon anodes, we constructed S@C||Li-Gr full cells 
using pre-lithiated graphite electrodes (N/P = 1.1; Fig. S11). The full cell 
with 1 M DOL+MDOL demonstrated an initial Coulombic efficiency 
(CE) of 85.9 % and sustained 95.9 % average CE over 100 cycles at 0.1 C 
(Fig. 5a and b), outperforming S@C||Li half-cells by 21.1 % in CE, which 
confirms effective shuttle effect suppression by the graphite anode. 
Notably, although the use of graphite anodes significantly improves the 
CE of the battery, the limited lithium inventory (only 10 % excess) in 
graphite unlike the virtually unlimited lithium supply in lithium metal 
anodes, leads to rapid capacity fading, as the consumed lithium cannot 
be replenished during cycling. Therefore, when a graphite anode is 
employed, the cell becomes more sensitive to parasitic reactions 
involving polysulfides, making it essential to further suppress the attack 
of polysulfides on lithium to reduce irreversible lithium loss. Raising 
electrolyte concentration can reduce the number of free solvent mole­
cules, thereby lowering polysulfide dissolution, thus mitigating parasitic 
reactions between lithium and polysulfides [40]. Elevating electrolyte 
concentration to 2 M DOL+MDOL further enhanced cell performance 
(Fig. S12 and S13), the S@C||Li-Gr full cell achieved 98.5 % average CE 
and 940.1 mAh g-1 initial capacity at 0.05 C, retaining 603.4 mAh g-1 
(74.3 %) after 100 cycles and 417.7 mAh g-1 (44.4 %) after 300 cycles at 
0.1 C, ultimately achieving a long cycle life exceeding 500 cycles 
(Fig. 5c).

4. Conclusion
By using the weak Li⁺ coordination capability of MDOL, we have 
developed a novel weakly solvated electrolyte that promotes anion-rich 
solvation clusters, thereby accelerating desolvation kinetics compared 
to conventional ether electrolytes. This optimized desolvation capability 
enables exceptional graphite compatibility by eliminating solvent co- 
intercalation phenomena prevalent in DME-based systems. Compara­
tive analysis of half-cell (74.8 % average Coulombic efficiency) and full- 
cell (95.9 % average Coulombic efficiency) configurations reveal that Li- 
Gr anode integration critically reduces the shuttle effect of Li-S battery. 
The 2 M DOL+MDOL full-cell system demonstrates 940.1 mAh g-1 initial 
capacity at 0.05 C, retaining 74.3 % capacity (603.4 mAh g-1) with 98.5 
% average CE after 100 cycles at 0.1 C. Crucially, this work demonstrates 
a long-cycle-life Li-S battery sustaining over 500 cycles with merely 10 
% lithium excess, providing an innovative electrolyte design strategy for 
the next generation of Li-S batteries.
