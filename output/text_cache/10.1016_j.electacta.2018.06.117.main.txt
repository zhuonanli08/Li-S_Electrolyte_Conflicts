a b s t r a c t
High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) can be used to systematically and rapidly screen the
potential additives for the inhibition of the polysulﬁde shuttle effect in a Li-S battery. The method is
proven effective by investigating the 18 compounds which were reported in the literature as redox
shuttle inhibitors for Li-S batteries. The change of the polysulﬁde ions from being exposed to 18 different
redox shuttle inhibition additives was qualitatively and quantitatively determined. The changes of pol-
ysulﬁde species and elemental sulfur were successfully used to study the inhibition effect of the additives
in Li-S batteries. It was conﬁrmed that nitrate salts under the same concentration showed the best in-
hibition effect for the polysulﬁde shuttle reaction. However even in the electrolyte with nitrate additives,
the shuttle reactions between Li metal and polysulﬁdes (and elemental sulfur) are still notable, and thus
the search for a better polysulﬁde shuttle inhibitor is ongoing and critical for improving the electro-
chemical performance of Li-S batteries.

1. Introduction
Since the commercialization of state-of-art Li-ion batteries
(LIBs) in the 80's, over the past three decades LIBs have been
extensively studied and developed. Currently the development of
LIBs has almost reached a ceiling, although the applications of LIBs
in the large scale such as in electric vehicles and micro-grid are still
under development. Recently rechargeable lithium sulfur (Li-S)
batteries have been attracted much attention as a potential candi-
date to replace state-of-art LIBs, owning to its high theoretical
energy density (1672 mAh*g1), good safety and low cost. Lots of
research has been done to maximize the utilization of the high
energy density of Li-S batteries, for example, under optimum
conditions Li-S batteries can be cycled over thousand times and at
least over half of the theoretical capacity can be retained [1e21].
In spite of its promising future, the current development of Li-S
batteries is still hindered by some challenging obstacles, such as
high self-discharge rate, high capacity decade, low coulombic
efﬁciency and lithium dendrite formation. Among those, the ﬁrst
three obstacles are associated with the chemical reactions between
sulfur contents, including elemental sulfur and polysulﬁdes and the
lithium anode. The well-known phenomenon due to the chemical
reaction is called “polysulﬁde shuttle” which describes the chem-
ical reductions of the electrochemically generated long chain pol-
ysulﬁdes with a lithium anode and a sulfur cathode. Numerous
efforts have been taken to minimize the “polysulﬁde shuttle”
[1e21]. Three approaches were taken: restricting the polysulﬁdes
within the cathode by physical and chemical absorption, blocking
the diffusion of polysulﬁdes from catholyte to anolyte by polymeric
or ceramic separators and inhibiting the chemical reaction of pol-
ysulﬁdes and lithium metal by electrolyte additives.
The most widely used inhibition additive in Li-S batteries is
LiNO3. During their pioneering work [22], Mikhaylik investigated
the effects of different inorganic and organic additives containing a
nitrogen-oxygen bond, including LiNO3, KNO3, CsNO3, and nitro-
methane etc. It was found that LiNO3 is the best additive due to the
noticeable inhibition effect and the wide solubility range in com-
mon electrolytes for Li-S batteries [22]. Recently, Choi et al. [23] and
Li et al. [24] reported that CsNO3 [23] and KNO3 [24] additives had
bifunctional effects on the inhibition of the polysulﬁde shuttle and

2.2. Sample preparation and methods
A stock solution of a polysulﬁde mixture was made by adding
0.147 g Li2S and 0.512 g S8 into 200 mL 1 M LiTFSi in DME/DOL
electrolyte (DME:DOL ¼ 1:1 in vol. ratio) in an Ar-ﬁlled glove-box.
The ﬁnal concentration is 16 mM, this is based on the formation of
Li2S6 stoichiometrically (although it's a mixture of different poly-
sulﬁdes). After stirring for 24 h under 60 C, the homogenous pol-
ysulﬁde mixture (no solid observed) was stored as the stock
solution. Then the electrolytes with different additives were pre-
pared by dissolving the corresponding amount of additive in 5 mL
of stock solution of polysulﬁdes mentioned above and the ﬁnal
concentration of the additive in the electrolyte with 16 mM poly-
sulﬁdes is 0.1 M. To monitor the inhibition effect for each additive,
the stock polysulﬁde electrolyte (as the baseline electrolyte) and
the polysulﬁde electrolytes with different additives (1.5 mL for each
electrolyte) were mixed with Li metal (thin ﬁlm, total weight is
around 0.15 g). At different lengths of contact times (6 h, 24 h, 48 h,
96 h), each electrolyte was taken out and derivatized in an HPLC
vial by the following procedure: 525 mL DME with 0.22 mmol
methyl triﬂate (25 mL) was added into 1.5 mL HPLC vial and vor-
texed for 1 min, 75 mL of electrolyte out of each electrolyte was then
added into the DME solution with methyl triﬂate and immediately
the HPLC vial with the mixture was capped by an air-tight cap and
vortexed for 5 min. The derivatized electrolyte samples in the air-
tight HPLC vials were taken out of the glove-box for the HPLC
analysis. All of the above preparations were under an Ar-ﬁlled
glove-box, with the oxygen and moisture levels in the glove-box
being less than 0.1 ppm.
0.3134 g S8 was dissolved in 340 mL 1.0M LiTFSi/DME/DOL
(1:1 vol ratio) solution to prepare the catholyte with elemental
sulfur (concentration about 3.6 mM). To conﬁrm the inhibition ef-
fect, several additives were added separately into the catholyte at a
concentration of 0.1 M. The Li-S cells were prepared in a 25 mL 4-
neck round bottom ﬂask: the cathode was a carbon felt electrode
(30  10  4.6 mm, SIGRACELL KDF 4.6, from SGL group in Ger-
many), the anode was made of lithium metal, lithium metal was
also used as a reference electrode and 10 ml of the catholyte was
used as both the supporting electrolyte and a sulfur source. To
minimize the concentration and diffusion polarizations (due to the
low surface area of the carbon felt cathode and the high volume of
electrolyte), the cells with and without additives were stirred and
cycled at 0.2 mA (the C rate is about C/77, for just one discharge/
charge cycle). After one discharge (to 1.75 V)/charge (to 3.0 V) cycle,
the catholyte was immediately derivatized and analyzed by the
HPLC method mentioned above. If there is no shuttle effect, after
being fully charged, the elemental sulfur concentration in the
catholyte should be the same as before discharge in theory. Thus by
comparing the concentration change of elemental sulfur before
discharge and after charge, the inhibition effects of different addi-
tives can be investigated.

3. Results and discussion
The reactions among Li metal, elemental sulfur and polysulﬁdes
were reported. For example, Rauh et al. [36] prepared Li2Sn (lithium
polysulﬁde) by directly mixing elemental sulfur with Li metal
powder in aprotic solvents, in which Li metal ﬁrst reacted with
elemental sulfur and then the formed polysulﬁdes being further
reduced by the Li metal. Fig. 1 shows the continuous reactions
among the polysulﬁde ions, dissolved sulfur and metallic Li. The
chromatographs on the left show the distributions of the poly-
sulﬁde ions in the baseline electrolyte and the electrolytes con-
taining the nitride and LiClO4 additives, while the chromatographs
on the right show the change of corresponding polysulﬁde ions
distributions after being in contact with metallic Li for 96 h. Clearly,
except LiClO4 which indeed reacted with long chain polysulﬁde
ions, the nitride additives did not react with the dissolved poly-
sulﬁde ions since the distribution of the polysulﬁde ions remained
unchanged. However, after being in contact with Li for 96 h, the S8,
S62, S72, S82 species almost disappeared, S42, S52 species were
abundant while S42 was the dominant ion. This observation clearly
demonstrated the parasitic shuttle reactions between Li and the
long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, n  5) as well as elemental sulfur.
The products from those parasitic shuttle reactions were mainly the
medium-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2S4, Li2S3) and the short-chain pol-
ysulﬁde (Li2S2), and Li2S.
The chromatographic results for electrolytes with different ad-
ditives before and after being in contact with Li metal for 4 days
(the results for different time of contact are summarized in the
supporting information) are shown in Figs. 1e3. Clearly, not all the
additives are chemically stable with polysulﬁde ions, severe re-
actions with the dissolved polysulﬁdes in electrolyte and some
additives were evident, such as the strong oxidants NaIO4 and
KBrO3 (shown in Fig. 2), and P2S5 (shown in Fig. 3). Obviously, the
oxidant additives can oxidize the dissolved polysulﬁde ion to
elemental sulfur since sulfur was the most abundant (only dissolve
sulfur compound in the case of NaIO4 and KBrO3) compound in the
chromatograph as soon as the additives were added before being
exposed to Li metal. At least one of oxidant additives, P2S5, was used
both as the promoter for the dissolution of Li2S and as the inhibitor
for shuttle phenomenon [28]. Evidentially, the oxidant additives
were sacriﬁcial additives which oxidized the dissolved polysulﬁdes
in the electrolyte. The impacts of this kind of additives e.g. P2S5 in a
Li-S battery need to be further investigated in future studies.

additives were clearly demonstrated in Fig. 1. The chromatographic
distributions of dissolved polysulﬁde ions after being in contact
with Li metal for 4 days were slightly changed from those of the
original electrolytes without exposure to Li metal. The chromato-
graphic peaks belonging to the long-chain polysulﬁde species (S62,
S72) just slightly decreased together with a slight increase of the
medium-chain polysulﬁde species (S42). The only notable change in
the chromatograms with nitrate additives was the decrease of the
chromatographic peak of elemental sulfur after being in contact
with Li metal for 4 days. The decrease of elemental sulfur after
being in contact with Li metal could be attributed to the lack of
passivation of the Li metal surface during the reaction with
elemental sulfur since the reaction products (probably the long-
chain polysulﬁdes) between Li metal and elemental sulfur were
highly soluble. For some additives, such as CS2 and BTFE, although
those two were reported as shuttle inhibitors [30,31], the inhibition
effect was hardly observed in the chromatograms in Fig. 3. After
being in contact with Li metal for 4 days, a dramatic increase of the
medium-chain polysulﬁde species (S42) in the electrolyte with
BTFE additive and the total disappearance of all polysulﬁdes and
elemental sulfur in the electrolyte with CS2 additive were observed
unambiguously. The situations were similar to the chromato-
graphic result for the baseline electrolyte without any additives.
Traditionally, electrochemical cycling tests are used to study the
inhibition effect for different additives in the electrolyte in most of
the published works [23e31]. Based on the different cycling per-
formance the inhibition effect for different additives were quanti-
tatively compared as shown in Mikhaylik's work [22]. However it's
well-known that the cycling performance of Li-S batteries are
highly dependent on the experimental conditions such as the
loading of sulfur, the amount of electrolyte and the rate of charge/
discharge [37,38]. Consequently a direct comparison of the elec-
trochemical results for different additives was not only time-
consuming and tedious but also associated with big experimental
uncertainties, especially when the comparisons were done with
data from different research groups. In this work, based on the
kinetics of the reactions among the polysulﬁde ions, elemental
sulfur and metallic lithium were monitored for the change of the
chromatographic peak intensities, hence a simple and reliable
quantitative analysis for the inhibition effects of different additives
was developed. Since the shuttle reactions were mainly the re-
actions among Li metal, long-chain polysulﬁdes (Sn2, with n  5)
and elemental sulfur, and the medium-chain polysulﬁdes (Sn2, with
n ¼ 3, 4) would be the products of the shuttle reactions. Therefore,
the relative ratio of the sum of the chromatographic peak areas for
RS5R, RS6R, RS7R, RS8R and S8 over the sum of the chromatographic
peak areas for RS3R, RS4R can be used to indicate either a decrease
of long-chain polysulﬁdes or an increase of medium-chain poly-
sulﬁdes (or both). The relative ratios for the electrolytes with 15
different additives (not including NaIO4, KBrO3, and P2S5) and
baseline electrolyte without any additive before and after being in
contact with Li metal at different periods of time are presented in
Fig. 4 (the values can be found in the supporting information).

To eventually demonstrate the practical simplicity, clearness,
and signiﬁcance of our HPLC method for studying the inhibition
effect of different additives. 7 Li-S cells with and without 6 different
additives (including LiNO3, LiBr, LiClO4, BMPTFSi, BTFE, and CS2)
were discharged and charged in ﬂooded three-electrode cells. The
elemental sulfur was added into the electrolyte to form the cath-
olyte as the sulfur source. By doing this, the quantity of the
elemental sulfur can be easily analyzed by our HPLC method before
discharge and after charge. In theory, the recovery rate (ratio of the
sulfur concentration in catholyte before discharge and after charge)
should be 1 when there is no shuttle effect. Since the shuttle effect
will inevitably consume some of the sulfur source on the Li elec-
trode, in reality, this recovery rate should be smaller than 1. By
using the recovery rate of catholyte without any additives as a
standard, one can easily compare the inhibition effects for different
additives. Good additives have a recovery rate close to 1 and larger
than the recovery rate of the catholyte without any additives,
borderline additives have a recovery rate similar to that of the
catholyte without any additives and bad additives have a recovery
rate much less than that of the catholyte without any additives. The
recovery rates of sulfur and the coulombic efﬁciencies of the
charge/discharge for the 7 investigated Li-S cells are summarized in
Table 2 (more detailed information, such as discharge/charge
curves, chromatographic results, were included in Tables S-21 and
S-22, Fig. S-4 to S-10 in supporting information). Clearly the Li-S
cell with 0.1 M LiNO3 in the catholyte has an over 95% recovery
rate of elemental sulfur which is close to our previous report [35].
This observation obvious indicates the superior shuttle-inhibition
effect for LiNO3 (and other nitrate salts) and supports the HPLC
results mentioned above. For others, some additives (such as
LiClO4, BMPTFSi) have the recovery rate of elemental sulfur similar
to that of no additive, which may indicate the little or no shuttle-
inhibition effect of these two additives. Some additives (such as
LiBr, BTFE, and CS2) surprisingly have a recovery rate of elemental

4. Conclusion
In summary, a reliable and quantitative HPLC method for
analyzing the changes of polysulﬁde species and sulfur was suc-
cessfully used to investigate the inhibition effect of different addi-
tives in the electrolyte of Li-S batteries. Based on the clear, reliable
and quantitative HPLC results, the inhibition effect for 18 different
additives was compared. It was found that P2S5, NaIO4, KBrO3 were
not compatible with polysulﬁdes (react severely); ﬁve nitrate ad-
ditives demonstrated good inhibition effects while the other ten
additives showed limited or no inhibition effect against the shuttle
reactions. The inﬂuence of the additive concentration [22] and
electrolyte concentration [39] on the inhibition effect for shuttle
reactions will be studied in future work.
It is also worth to emphasizing that as well as the chemical
interaction of polysulﬁde ions, electrochemical reactions, both
homogeneous and heterogeneous also play important roles in the
“shuttle effect”. The electrochemical behaviors of polysulﬁde ions
and the subsequent chemical interactions were reported in ref. 32
and 33. Taking into consideration of both electrochemical and
chemical behaviors of the polysulﬁde ions in a Li-S battery, a quick
and reliable screening method is proposed in the paper for the fast
screening of the “shuttle effect” inhibitors.
