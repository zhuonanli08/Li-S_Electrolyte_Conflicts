ABSTRACT 
Stabilizing electrode/electrolyte interphases is crucial for high-performance lithium–sulfur batteries; however, simultaneously 
fabricating robust interphases on both the lithium anode and sulfur cathode is challenging. Here, we report an anion-transferring 
functional electrolyte containing non-expendable tetramethylammonium (TMA) bromide that enables the in situ formation of 
LiF-rich interphases on both electrodes. The TMA+ cations act as ion carriers that electrostatically shuttle PF6 − anions from 
the bulk electrolyte to the electrode/electrolyte interfaces in the form of ion pairs. This localized enrichment of PF6 
− facilitates 
its electrochemical decomposition, leading to the formation of a uniform LiF-rich solid electrolyte interphase on the anode and a 
cathode-electrolyte interphase on the sulfur cathode. The LiF-rich interphases show a high Young’s modulus, which help suppress 
interfacial parasitic reactions and Li dendrite formation. The adsorbed TMA+ cations form an electrostatic shielding layer that 
mitigates local Li+ flux heterogeneity, further suppressing dendrite growth. When applied to Li-SPAN batteries, they maintain a 
92% capacity retention after 350 cycles at 1 C and deliver a high reversible capacity of 1039 mAh g− 1 after 300 cycles at 0.5 C. This 
work provides an innovative alternative to conventional interphase engineering for stabilizing lithium battery interphases.

Introduction 
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are environmentally-friendly, 
next-generation energy storage systems because of their high 
theoretical energy density ( ∼ 2600 Wh kg− 1 ) and the natural abun- 
dance of sulfur [ 1, 2 ]. However, they show persistent interfacial 
instability on both electrodes. In conventional elemental-sulfur 
cathodes, the dissolution and migration of soluble lithium poly- 
sulfides (LiPSs) can induce shuttle-related active-sulfur loss and 
rapid capacity fading. In contrast, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile 
(SPAN) confines sulfur within a PAN-derived carbon framework 
and follows a solid-state conversion pathway in carbonate- 
based electrolytes, which largely suppresses the formation and 
migration of long-chain polysulfide intermediates and miti- 
gates the conventional shuttle effect [ 3, 4 ]. Nevertheless, SPAN 
cathodes still suffer from sluggish sulfur-conversion kinetics, 
interfacial polarization, volume changes during cycling, and 
progressive deterioration of the electrode/electrolyte interface.

These processes can increase charge-transfer resistance, promote 
electrolyte consumption, and gradually reduce sulfur utilization 
and cycling stability. On the Li anode, highly-reductive metallic Li 
facilitates continuous, parasitic electrolyte decomposition, which 
forms a heterogeneous and mechanically fragile solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) [ 5 ]. During repeated Li plating/stripping, this 
unstable SEI undergoes cyclic fracture and regeneration, which 
forms local current hotspots, Li dendrites, and inactive dead 
Li [ 6 ]. These interfacial failures together accelerate electrolyte 
depletion, increase cell impedance, and lead to rapid capacity 
fading, low Coulombic efficiency, and significant safety risks [ 7, 
8 ]. Therefore, simultaneously stabilizing the Li-metal anode and 
the SPAN cathode interfaces is essential for achieving durable 
Li|SPAN cells. 
Electrolyte engineering provides an effective route to regulate 
interphase chemistry on Li metal [ 9 ]. For example, constructing 
a LiF-rich SEI on the Li-metal anode can improve interfacial 
stability because LiF possesses a relatively high Young’s modulus, 
high interfacial energy, and favorable physicochemical stability 
[ 10–13 ]. When uniformly distributed within the SEI, LiF can 
help regulate Li+ transport, homogenize local current density, 
promote uniform Li nucleation, and suppress dendritic growth 
[ 14 ]. Despite progress on the Li anode, constructing a stable 
cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) on the S cathode is an unre- 
solved challenge [ 15 ]. Because SPAN generally operates within a 
relatively low voltage window of 1.0–3.0 V versus Li+ /Li, its CEI is 
supposed to stabilize the electrode/electrolyte interface, suppress 
continuous interfacial side reactions, limit the accumulation of 
resistive decomposition products, maintain intimate contact dur- 
ing sulfur conversion, and facilitate interfacial Li+ transport [ 16 ]. 
A fundamental obstacle is the reduction potential of most elec- 
trolyte components, which typically lies below 1.0 V versus Li+ /Li, 
which is significantly lower than the operating voltage window of 
SPAN cathodes [ 16 ]. Consequently, conventional electrolytes lack 
sufficient thermodynamic driving force to undergo controlled 
reductive decomposition directly on the cathode surface and 
form a uniform protective interphase. The absence of a CEI 
allows direct contact between the S species and the electrolyte, 
which causes continuous parasitic reactions, accumulation of 
resistive interphase products, increased impedance, and deterio- 
rated electrochemical reversibility [ 17 ]. Conventional approaches, 
such as the use of highly concentrated electrolytes or sacrificial 
additives such as fluoroethylene carbonate, can partially alleviate 
these issues. However, highly concentrated electrolytes often 
suffer from high viscosity, poor wettability, and increased cost, 
whereas sacrificial additives may be continuously consumed 
during cycling, thereby limiting long-term interfacial stability 
[ 18–20 ]. Therefore, there is a need to develop a novel electrolyte 
to simultaneously construct stable, LiF ‑rich interphases on both 
electrodes without continuously depleting its components. 
In this work, we introduce an anion-transfer functional elec- 
trolyte that incorporates non-consumable tetramethylammo- 
nium (TMA) bromide as an additive in a conventional carbonate 
electrolyte to regulate the local coordination environment and 
interfacial chemistry of Li|SPAN batteries. TMA+ dynamically 
interacts with PF6 
− and modifies the local coordination state 
and interfacial availability of PF6 
− -containing species through 
reversible association, dissociation, and preferential interfacial 
adsorption. This regulation promotes favorable PF6 
− -derived 
interfacial reactions and facilitates the formation of thinner, 
more uniform, LiF-rich interphases on both electrodes. Rather 
than serving as a stoichiometric film-forming precursor, TMA+ 
remains largely non-consumable and continuously regulates the 
electrolyte coordination environment and interfacial reaction 
pathways. The resulting dual LiF-rich protective layers exhibit a 
high Young’s modulus and high surface energy, allowing them to 
suppress polysulfide shuttling, parasitic reactions, and Li dendrite 
growth. Adsorbed TMA+ cations also act as an electrostatic 
shielding layer that homogenizes Li+ flux and inhibits dendrite 
nucleation. As a result, Li|Li symmetric cells with this electrolyte 
demonstrate cycling stability from − 25◦C to 50◦C. When applied 
to Li|sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) full cells, the system 
achieves 92% capacity retention after 350 cycles at 1 C and 
delivers a reversible capacity of 1039 mAh g− 1 after 300 cycles 
at 0.5 C. This work establishes a new method for anion-transfer- 
mediated interphase engineering. This approach offers a scalable 
route to stabilize electrode/electrolyte interphases and provides 
a new perspective for the development of high ‑energy ‑density 
lithium–sulfur batteries.

Results and Discussion 
Figure 1 illustrates the proposed mechanism for the in situ for- 
mation of a LiF-rich SEI and CEI in TMA-containing electrolyte 
during the discharge and charge processes of a Li–S battery. In the 
bulk electrolyte, TMA+ cations were electrostatically associated 
with PF6 
− anions and formed dynamic ion pairs that acted as 
reversible anion carriers. This ion-pairing was critical for the 
spatially targeted delivery of fluorine sources to both electrode 
interfaces. During discharge, TMA+ preferentially adsorbed onto 
the electrified Li surface of the anode. This adsorption con- 
structed an electrostatic shielding layer that homogenized the 
local Li+ flux, mitigating the tip-effect that typically induces 
dendrite growth (Figure 1a ). The accumulated TMA+ cations 
also electrostatically attracted and enriched PF6 
− anions at the 
anode/electrolyte interface. This localized enrichment of PF6 
− 
lowered its decomposition energy barrier, promoting its preferen- 
tial reductive decomposition at a potential higher than that of the 
carbonate electrolyte. This reaction produced a uniform, LiF-rich 
SEI. 
Conversely, during charging, the redistribution and interfacial 
availability of PF6 
− toward the positive SPAN electrode were facil- 
itated by reversible TMA+ -PF6 
− association and dissociation. This 
transport mechanism created an anion-rich microenvironment at 
the cathode interface that was thermodynamically favorable for 
the electrochemical decomposition of PF6 
− within the cathode 
potential window. This reaction generated a conformal and 
robust LiF-dominated CEI (Figure 1b ). The establishment of this 
dual LiF-rich interphase architecture engineered via the anion- 
transfer of TMA+ suppressed parasitic electrolyte decomposition, 
mitigated polysulfide shuttling, and inhibited lithium dendrite 
proliferation. The TMA+ cation itself did not undergo perma- 
nent redox reactions and functioned as a non-expendable ion 
shuttle, ensuring sustained interfacial regulation during long- 
term battery cycling. Through reversible TMA+ -PF6 
− association 
and dissociation, TMA+ regulates the spatial distribution and 
interfacial availability of PF6 
− -containing species. At the Li 
anode, preferential TMA+ adsorption homogenizes the local Li+

To further evaluate the impact of TMA+ on the electrolyte 
microenvironment, the solvated structures of the TMA-free and 
TMA-containing electrolytes were examined using a combination 
of density functional theory (DFT) calculations, Raman spec- 
troscopy, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figure 2 ). 
MD trajectories were analyzed to explore the coordination states 
of cations with solvent molecules and anions in both electrolytes. 
Simulation snapshots revealed a fundamental restructuring of 
the local solvation environment after incorporating TMA+ . In 
the TMA-free electrolyte, the solvation structure was mainly 
governed by the central coordination of Li+ , with PF6 
− anions 
and carbonate solvent molecules distributed uniformly with- 
out forming ionic aggregates (Figure 2a ). In contrast, TMA+ 
induced the spatial redistribution of PF6 
− , which formed localized 
ion pairs while maintaining a continuous solvent framework 
(Figure 2b ). A magnified cross-sectional view (Figure 2 ; Figure 
S1 ) showed an enhanced local distribution of PF6 
− around TMA+ , 
indicating dynamic TMA+ -PF6 
− association in the simulated 
multicomponent electrolyte. 
The thermodynamic driving force for this association was deter- 
mined by electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution diagrams for 
PF6 
− , TMA+ , and the TMA+ -PF6 
− ion pair (Figure S2 ). PF6 
− 
showed a uniform negative potential distribution, while TMA+ 
displayed a positive potential distribution. When paired, the 
calculated binding energy reached − 3.06 eV, providing thermody- 
namic evidence for the spontaneous formation of a stable TMA+ - 
PF6 
− ion pair (Figure S2 ). To corroborate these observations, we 
used radial distribution functions (RDFs) to analyze the coordi- 
nation distances between electrolyte components (Figure 2d,e ). 
In both electrolytes, sharp primary solvation peaks for Li+ paired 
with O(EC), O(DEC), and F(PF6 
− ) consistently appeared near 
2.0 Å, indicating that the primary Li+ solvation shell remained 
intact. In the TMA-modified electrolyte, the N(TMA+ )-F(PF6 
− ) 
RDF exhibited a broad peak at approximately 4.5 Å with a 
coordination number of 0.20, indicating a non-random but 
limited local association between TMA+ and PF6 
− . Combined 
with the changes in Li+ -solvent and Li+ -anion coordination, these 
results suggest that TMA addition alters the local electrolyte 
coordination environment. The decreased Li+ -solvent and Li+ - 
anion coordination numbers further suggest a redistribution of 
the Li+ solvation structure in the presence of TMA. 
Figure 2f,g shows the CNs derived from the RDF. In the TMA-free 
electrolyte, the CNs of Li+ -O(EC), Li+ -O(DEC), and Li+ -F(PF6 
− ) 
were 2.86, 1.85, and 0.47, respectively, which decreased to 2.18, 1.14, 
and 0.42 after adding TMA+ . The significant decrease in both sol- 
vent and anion coordination indicates the formation of a thinner 
and more loosely bound Li+ solvation shell. This configuration is 
energetically favorable, as it reduces the desolvation penalty dur- 
ing Li+ deposition at the electrode interface, thereby enhancing 
the reaction kinetics. Additionally, Figure 2h shows that TMA+ 
exhibited a much higher lowest unoccupied molecular orbital 
(LUMO) than LiPF6 , indicating greater resistance to reduction. 
This electronic structure ensured that TMA+ functioned as a non- 
expendable interfacial regulator that preferentially adsorbed at 
the interface without being prematurely consumed to form SEI 
components [ 21 ].

ther examined using DFT calculations. As illustrated in Figure 2i , 
TMA+ preferentially adsorbed parallel to the Li surface, yielding 
a highly negative adsorption energy of − 1.9 eV, indicating that 
this process is thermodynamically favorable [ 20 ]. This binding

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was 
used to examine the interfacial structures formed in different 
electrolytes. In the TMA-containing electrolyte, a compact and 
uniform SEI layer with a thickness of 16 nm formed on the 
Li surface (Figure 3d ). High-magnification imaging (Figure 3e ) 
revealed lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.23 nm, 
corresponding to the (111) crystal plane of LiF, indicating the 
formation of a LiF-rich interphase [ 32 ]. In contrast, the TMA-free 
electrolyte formed a much thicker (57 nm) and morphologically 
heterogeneous SEI (Figure 3f ). The corresponding lattice fringes 
(Figure 3g ) exhibited a spacing of 0.28 nm, which was indexed to 
the (002) crystal plane of Li2 CO3 , suggesting that the interphase 
was dominated by carbonate-derived decomposition products 
[ 33 ]. The thinner LiF-dominated interphase formed in the TMA- 
containing electrolyte suggests a mechanically robust and elec- 
trochemically stable passivation layer [ 12 ]. The formation of this 
LiF-dominated interphase was associated with the TMA-induced 
ion-pair, which promoted preferential PF6 
− decomposition at the 
interface and facilitated LiF generation. 
To further investigate how TMA+ affected the chemical com- 
position of the SEI, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
was used to analyze the lithium metal anodes cycled in both 
electrolytes. The C 1s spectra contained peaks at 284.8, 286.5, 
288.7, and 289.9 eV, corresponding to C ─C/C ─H, C ─O, C ═ O, 
and Li2 CO3 species, respectively. These species originated from 
the decomposition of the carbonate electrolyte (Figure 3h ) [ 34 ]. 
Li2 CO3 was the main component of the SEI produced in the TMA- 
free electrolyte, and it has been shown to increase the electrode 
impedance and overpotential, both of which can destabilize the 
SEI and shorten the cell lifespan [ 35, 36 ]. This confirms the earlier 
conclusion that the unmodified electrolyte formed an anion-lean, 
solvent-rich interface, which promoted solvent decomposition. 
In the O 1s XPS spectra (Figure S6 ), the intensity of the signal 
corresponding to ROCO2 Li species was much stronger in the 
TMA-derived SEI compared to the TMA-free electrolyte. Since 
organic ROCO2 Li components enhance the mechanical flexibility 
of the SEI [ 37 ], the SEI formed in the TMA-containing electrolyte 
possessed superior elasticity, allowing it to accommodate elec- 
trode volume changes and mitigate mechanical stresses during 
cycling. The F 1s XPS spectra (Figure 3i ) provide support for the 
anion-transfer mechanism. The addition of TMA+ substantially 
enhanced the LiF signal at 684.4 eV [ 38 ], confirming its ability to 
facilitate the decomposition of PF6 
− . The high Young’s modulus 
of LiF suppressed lithium dendrite growth and enhanced the 
interfacial stability [ 12 ]. The N 1s XPS spectrum (Figure 3j ) 
contained no nitrogen signal in either the TMA-free or TMA- 
containing electrolytes after cycling, indicating that TMA+ did 
not decompose during electrochemical operation. The Br 3d XPS 
spectra (Figure S7 ) and N 1s XPS spectra (Figure 3k ) confirmed 
the presence of TMA+ on the Li surface after plating, reinforcing 
that it was non-expendable [ 39, 40 ]. 
The ionic conductivity of the SEI layers formed in each electrolyte 
was quantitatively evaluated by electrochemical impedance spec- 
troscopy (EIS) in Li|Li symmetric cells, following a previously 
reported method [ 41 ]. The SEI derived from the TMA-free 
electrolyte exhibited a conductivity of 2.4 ×10− 4 mS cm− 1 , whereas 
the SEI formed in the TMA-containing electrolyte achieved a 
higher value of 8.7 × 10− 4 mS cm− 1 (Figure S8 ). This enhanced 
conductivity was attributed to the abundant LiF content in 
the TMA-derived interphase, which facilitated Li transport and 
improved the interfacial stability and overall electrochemical 
performance. The TMA electrolyte exhibited a much lower 
contact angle (10.7◦) on lithium metal compared to the TMA-free 
electrolyte (28.1◦), indicating substantially improved interfacial 
wettability (Figure S9 ). This was attributed to TMA+ adsorption 
on the lithium surface and the enhanced interfacial compatibility 
provided by the formation of TMA+ -PF6 
− ion pairs (Figure S9 ). 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the cycled Li|Li 
symmetric cells (Figure S10 ) indicated the smooth and uniform 
Li deposition in the TMA-containing electrolyte. The TMA- 
free electrolyte exhibited rough Li dendrites, confirming the 
enhanced plating stability with TMA. 
In situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate changes 
in the electrode-electrolyte interphase during Li plating. For the 
TMA-free electrolyte, the Raman signal intensities attributable to 
free EC (715, 890 cm− 1 ), EC-Li+ (725 cm− 1 ), and DEC (901 cm− 1 ) 
continuously decreased as plating proceeded (Figure 3l ) [ 24 ]. 
This indicated progressive solvent decomposition during cycling, 
which produced a concentration gradient near the Li metal inter- 
face [ 42 ]. In the TMA-containing electrolyte, the intensities of 
these Raman peaks did not greatly change throughout the plating 
process (Figure 3m ). This difference was attributed to the ability 
of TMA+ to form stable TMA+ -PF6 
− ion pairs, which weakened 
coordination between Li+ and solvent molecules (EC, DEC). 
This restructuring reduced the proportion of solvent molecules 
in the Li+ solvation shell, diminishing the possibility of solvent 
molecules directly participating in reductive decomposition at 
the electrode interface. Solvent decomposition was suppressed, in 
agreement with the proposed anion-transfer mechanism wherein 
TMA+ orchestrated a PF6 
− -dominated interfacial environment. 
The optimal concentration of the TMA was determined to 
be 1 wt.% through long-term cycling tests (Figure S11 ). This 
concentration was used to assess the influence of TMA on 
the reversibility of lithium plating/stripping at different current 
densities and areal capacities. During cycling at 0.5 mA cm− 2 
and 1 mAh cm− 2 (Figure S12 ), a Li|Cu cell with the TMA-free 
electrolyte experienced rapid Coulombic efficiency (CE) decay 
and failed within 120 cycles. In contrast, cells employing the 
TMA-containing electrolyte maintained an average CE of 95% for 
more than 360 cycles, demonstrating enhanced electrochemical 
reversibility. Increasing the plating capacity to 3 mAh cm− 2 at 
the same current density (Figure S13 ) further amplified these 
performance differences. The TMA-free electrolyte failed before 
40 cycles, whereas the TMA electrolyte maintained a CE of 
97% over 110 cycles. Similar trends were observed when the 
current density was increased to 1 mA cm− 2 . At a capacity 
of 1 and 3 mAh cm− 2 (Figures S14 and S15 ), the CE of the 
TMA-free electrolyte decayed after 100 and 50 cycles, while the 
cells with TMA retained a CE of 95% for 120 cycles. The cor- 
responding plating/stripping voltage profiles corroborate these 
observations (Figure S16 ). Cells with TMA exhibited reduced 
polarization and highly overlapping curves from the first to 
the 100th/150th cycles, indicating stable and kinetically facile 
interfacial behavior. In contrast, the TMA-free electrolyte exhib-

To further evaluate how TMA+ affected the interfacial stability, 
Li|Li symmetric cells were subjected to long-term cycling at 
different current densities and plating capacities. At 0.5 mA cm− 2 
and 4 mAh cm− 2 (Figure S17 ), cells with the TMA-free electrolyte 
exhibited increasing polarization and short-circuited after 800 h. 
In contrast, the TMA-containing electrolyte stably operated for 
over 1250 h, with a gradual increase in voltage hysteresis. The 
overpotential was 43 mV at a current density of 1 mA cm− 2 
(Figure S18 ). When the cycling conditions were increased to 
3 mA cm− 2 and 3 mAh cm− 2 (Figure S19 ) or 5 mA cm− 2 and 
5 mAh cm− 2 (Figure S20 ), the performance difference was even 
more pronounced. Cells with the TMA-free electrolyte showed 
substantial overpotential fluctuations and short-circuited within 
160 and 75 h, respectively, whereas the TMA-containing cells 
remained stable for 350 and 200 h, respectively. TMA substan- 
tially enhanced the rate capability of the lithium metal anodes 
(Figures S21 and S22 ). Cells with TMA exhibited controlled 
polarization potentials at different current densities and stably 
cycled for more than 3000 and 850 h, respectively. In contrast, the 
TMA-free cells failed due to internal short circuits after only 1400 
and 400 h of cycling. 
The compatibility of these electrolyte systems with lithium metal 
anodes was evaluated from − 25◦C to 50◦C. Figure S23 shows that 
after static storage at − 25◦C for 8 h, both the TMA and TMA- 
free electrolytes remained unfrozen, confirming their operational 
viability at sub-ambient temperatures. When Li|Li symmetric 
cells were used to assess cycle life and lithium deposition 
morphology from − 25◦C to 50◦C, the cell with the TMA-free 
electrolyte exhibited limited cycling life and large voltage hys- 
teresis (Figure 4a,c,e ). The corresponding SEM images revealed 
mossy Li deposits indicative of uncontrolled dendrite growth 
and poor interfacial instability (Figure 4b,d,f ). In contrast, the 
TMA-containing electrolyte enabled prolonged cycling stability 
with reduced polarization (Figure 4a,c,e ) and uniformly dense 
Li deposition morphologies (Figure 4b,d,f ). This uniform and 
compact plating inhibited the accumulation of inactive dead Li 
and mitigated parasitic side reactions between the electrolyte 
and lithium metal, which enhanced the overall electrochemical 
efficiency and cycling performance. 
The morphological characteristics of lithium deposits at different 
current densities and areal capacities at 25◦C are presented in 
Figure S24 . Even under 5 mA cm− 2 and 5 mAh cm− 2 , the TMA- 
containing electrolyte consistently produced lithium deposits 
with uniform morphologies, demonstrating that it suppressed 
dendrite formation and promoted the formation of a smooth, 
homogeneous coating. These observations support the mecha- 
nism in which adsorbed TMA+ cations created an electrostatic 
shielding layer at the lithium surface. This mitigated the local 
electric field intensity at the Li-electrolyte interphase, promoted a 
more homogeneous Li+ flux, and suppressed dendrite formation 
and growth. As illustrated in Figure 4g and Table S1 [ 14, 24, 
43–51 ], the TMA electrolyte exhibited superior long-term cycling 
performance compared to previously reported electrolyte sys- 
tems, demonstrating its ability to stabilize Li anodes. Li|Cu cells 
were also assembled for quantitative CE evaluation using the 
Aurbach protocol [ 52 ]. Figure 4h shows that the Li+ nucleation 
overpotential on copper foil with TMA (33 mV) was lower 
than that of the TMA-free electrolyte (159 mV), indicating more 
energetically favorable nucleation kinetics. As shown in Figure 4i , 
the Li|Cu cell using the TMA-containing electrolyte achieved an 
average CE of 97.09%, which surpasses that of the cell using the 
TMA-free electrolyte (94.92%). This enhanced CE confirmed the 
superior stability and reversibility of lithium plating/stripping 
enabled by the TMA-induced, LiF-rich interface. 
To quantitatively evaluate the impact of the TMA-induced inter- 
face on Li+ transport kinetics, EIS was performed on Li|Li 
symmetric cells with both electrolyte systems. Cells with the 
TMA-containing electrolyte exhibited substantially lower charge- 
transfer resistance ( Rct ) and solid electrolyte interphase resistance 
( RSEI ) compared with the TMA-free electrolyte, both before 
cycling and after 20 cycles (Figure 5a ). The lower interfacial 
resistance, particularly the RSEI , indicated the formation of a more 
conductive and mechanically robust SEI layer in the presence 
of TMA+ . The decrease upon cycling confirmed the enhanced 
stability and self-passivating nature of the LiF-rich interphase 
derived from TMA-mediated anion transfer. 
To deconvolute the individual electrochemical processes con- 
tributing to the overall impedance, distribution of relaxation time 
(DRT) analysis was conducted using the EIS spectra (Figure 5b ). 
In the DRT spectra, three relaxation processes appeared in the 
timescale of 10− 4 –100 s, corresponding to Li+ transport across 
the SEI layer (10− 4 –10− 3 s), charge-transfer (10− 2 –10− 1 s), and 
ion diffusion (100 s) [ 53, 54 ]. All three peaks were significantly 
suppressed when using the TMA electrolyte, indicating reduced 
SEI resistance, faster desolvation kinetics, enhanced charge- 
transfer rates, and minimized concentration polarization. All of 
the integrated peak areas in Figure 5c were lower for the TMA 
electrolyte compared with the TMA-free electrolyte, demon- 
strating the superior electrochemical kinetics enabled by TMA- 
induced interfacial modification. The exchange current density 
( I0 ) is a fundamental kinetic parameter reflecting the intrinsic 
rate of charge transfer and was derived from the Tafel plots 
(Figure 5d ). The TMA electrolyte achieved an exchange current 
density of 0.50 mA cm− 2 , which was higher than that of the 
TMA-free electrolyte (0.22 mA cm− 2 ). This twofold enhancement 
confirmed that the LiF-rich, TMA-modified interphase facilitated 
significantly faster charge-transfer kinetics. The Li+ transference 
number ( tLi 
+ ) was measured to evaluate the ionic transport 
efficiency (Figure 5e and Figure S25 ). The TMA electrolyte 
delivered a higher tLi 
+ of 0.74 compared to only 0.27 for the TMA- 
free electrolyte. This improvement was likely because TMA+ 
restructured the Li+ solvation shell, wherein the formation of 
TMA+ -PF6 
− ion pairs weakened Li+− anion interactions, which 
facilitated more efficient Li+ migration. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) 
performed on Li|Cu half-cells revealed that the TMA electrolyte 
exhibited a higher current density than the TMA-free electrolyte 
(Figure 5f ), indicating that the modified interfacial environment 
accelerated the lithium plating/stripping kinetics.

electrolyte exhibited a lower Ea (54.8 kJ mol− 1 ) than the TMA-free 
electrolyte (62.8 kJ mol− 1 ), indicating that the LiF-rich, TMA-

The long-term cycling performance of Li|SPAN full cells is shown 
in Figure S32a . In the TMA-containing electrolyte, the SPAN 
cathode delivered a reversible capacity of 1039 mAh g− 1 at 0.5 
C, with a capacity retention rate of 88% after 300 cycles. In 
contrast, the cell with the TMA-free electrolyte underwent rapid 
capacity decay after 50 cycles. The corresponding voltage-capacity 
profiles indicated a lower voltage gap ( ΔE ) between the charging 
and discharging processes for the TMA-containing cell, reflecting 
the enhanced redox kinetics and reduced polarization (Figure 
S32b ). These performance differences were amplified when the 
current density was increased to 1 C. The Li|SPAN full cell with 
TMA-containing electrolyte delivered a reversible capacity of 
914 mAh g− 1 , which was higher than the value obtained with 
the TMA-free electrolyte (781 mAh g− 1 ; Figure S33 ). The cell 
with the TMA-containing electrolyte cycled stably, showing 92% 
capacity retention after 350 cycles, while the TMA-free Li|SPAN 
full cell showed severe capacity fading within approximately 40 
cycles (Figure 6i ). As shown in Figure 6j and Table S2 , this 
cycling stability positions the TMA-containing electrolyte among 
the best-performing systems reported for Li-SPAN batteries, as 
benchmarked against advanced electrolyte formulations in the 
literature [ 43, 47, 48, 62–68 ]. 
Rate capability tests (Figure 6k ) demonstrated that the TMA- 
containing electrolyte enabled superior reversible capacities 
across a range of C-rates from 0.1 C to 2 C, indicating enhanced 
interfacial charge transfer. The voltage profiles at different rates 
(Figure 6l ) showed reduced polarization under high current 
for the cells with TMA. EIS revealed that the TMA electrolyte 
greatly lowered the interfacial resistance both before and after 20 
cycles, indicating the formation of a stable and highly conductive 
electrode-electrolyte interphase (Figure S34 and Table S3 ). 
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Figure S35 ) revealed that the TMA- 
containing cell exhibited a higher peak current density of 
− 0.32 mA mg− 1 than the TMA-free cell ( − 0.24 mA mg− 1 ), reflect- 
ing enhanced redox reversibility and faster reaction kinetics. 
CV at scan rates from 0.6 to 1.0 mV s− 1 confirmed that TMA 
enhanced the Li+ diffusion kinetics (Figure S36a,b ). The Li+ 
diffusion coefficient ( DLi + ) derived from the linear relationship 
between peak current ( Ip ) and the square root of scan rate 
( v1/2 ) demonstrated that the TMA electrolyte exhibited a 1.4- 
fold higher DLi + during charging compared with the TMA-free 
electrolyte (Figure S36c ). These enhanced kinetics were directly 
attributable to TMA-mediated anion transfer, which facilitated 
the in-situ formation of an ion-conductive, LiF-rich CEI that 
enabled efficient Li+ transport. 
Due to the merits of the TMA, a Li|SPAN pouch cell was 
assembled to evaluate its performance under practical conditions. 
The TMA-containing pouch cell achieved a reversible specific 
capacity of 1016 mAh g− 1 for 95 cycles at 0.1 C, corresponding 
to a capacity retention of 91% (Figure 6m,n ). Galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique (GITT) analysis revealed that 
the cell with TMA-containing electrolyte exhibited a higher 
discharge plateau and a more stable voltage profile compared 
with the TMA-free electrolyte (Figure S37 ). This highlights 
the improved Li+ transport kinetics and reduced interfacial 
resistance [ 69 ].

Conclusion 
We have developed an anion-transfer functional electrolyte by 
incorporating non-expendable tetramethylammonium (TMA) 
bromide as an additive to regulate the interfacial chemistry 
of Li–S batteries. The TMA+ cations electrostatically associ- 
ated with PF6 
− anions and formed dynamic ion pairs that 
served as reversible anion carriers that shuttled fluorine-rich 
species between both electrode interfaces. This targeted anion 
enrichment has induced the preferential reductive decom- 
position of PF6 
− within the respective operating potential 
windows. This has enabled the in situ formation of uni- 
form, LiF-rich interphases on both the lithium anode and 
sulfur cathode. These LiF-dominated protective layers have 
shown a high Young’s modulus, allowing them to suppress 
parasitic interfacial side reactions, mitigate polysulfide shut- 
tling, and inhibit lithium dendrite growth. The adsorption 
of TMA+ cations on the Li surface has formed an elec- 
trostatic shielding layer that has homogenized the local Li+ 
flux and has further stabilized Li deposition. The TMA+ 
cation itself has not decomposed throughout electrochemi- 
cal operation and has functioned as a persistent interfacial 
regulator without being continuously depleted like conven- 
tional sacrificial additives. As a result, Li|Li symmetric cells 
with the TMA-containing electrolyte have demonstrated sig- 
nificantly better cycling stability from − 25◦C to 50◦C. When 
applied in practical Li|SPAN full cells, 92% capacity reten- 
tion has been retained after 350 cycles at 1 C. This anion- 
transfer-mediated interphase engineering mechanism has pro- 
vided a feasible strategy for stabilizing high-energy-density Li–S 
batteries.
