A New Hybrid Solid/Solvating Sulfur Conversion for
Energy-Dense Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Xiaoyu Jin, Xiaoqun Qi, Fengyi Yang, Han Zhou, Ruining Jiang, Dan Yang, Zhou Fang,

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery based on the conversion chemistry of
2Li + S →Li2S
delivers
an
ultrahigh
theoretical
energy
density
of 2600 Wh kg1.[1–7] However, in practical terms, its available energy
density is low because the operation of Li-S batteries in the mainstream
dioxolane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME) electrolyte requires a high
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio (usually >5 μL mg1).[8–12] DOL and
DME with high donor numbers show high dissolution of the Li poly-
sulﬁdes (LPSs); therefore, the 1M ether electrolyte participates in the
“dissolution-precipitation” conversion process at the cathode side and
converts to catholyte after dissolving LPSs.[13,14] Under lean-ether-
electrolyte
conditions,
our
previous
work
revealed that S cathodes suffer from sluggish
conversion
kinetics
caused
by
signiﬁcantly
decreased Li+ conductivity and deteriorated
wettability of the high-LPSs-ratio catholyte.[15]
In addition to low gravimetric energy density,
the volumetric energy density of Li-S batteries is
largely limited by the demand for high-porosity
(>70%) S cathodes.[16] A dense S cathode fails
to work in conventional ether electrolytes
because it undergoes a locally low E/S ratio
with a decreased electrolyte uptake in the cath-
ode structure. Catalytic cathodes and mediation
additives have been extensively exploited to
improve the conversion kinetics under low E/S
ratios, but their effects are limited to a certain
extent.[17–24]
Sparingly solvating electrolytes are promising
candidates to decrease the E/S ratio by funda-
mentally altering the conversion pathway. Spar-
ingly solvating electrolytes were developed by
increasing Li salt to an extent where the electro-
lyte mainly consists of Li salt-solvent coordina-
tion with little free solvent.[25] This unique
electrolyte structure can effectively suppress the
dissolution of LPSs and consequently drives a solid conversion process,
which is expected to be less dependent on electrolyte amount in com-
parison
to
the
“dissolution-precipitation”
process
in
solvating
electrolytes.[26] A series of acetonitrile (AN)-,[27–29] ether-,[30–32]
carbonate-,[33,34] and sulfolane-based sparingly solvating electrolytes
have been developed.[35] Among them, AN-based ones successfully
enable Li-S batteries to operate under the lowest E/S ratio of 1 μL mg1
as previously being demonstrated.[15,29] However, the use of sparingly
solvating electrolytes is critically challenged by sluggish S redox chemis-
try due to the solid conversion pathway and the very low Li+ conduc-
tivity of the electrolyte.
Herein, a new hybrid solid/solvating conversion pathway, that is, S
converting to both solid and solvated LPSs, is achieved by a moderately
solvating electrolyte, which combines lean-electrolyte operation as in
sparingly solvating electrolytes and fast conversion kinetics as in solvating
electrolytes. The moderately solvating electrolyte is formulated by meth-
oxyacetonitrile (MAN) since MAN possesses a high relative permittivity
(21) that contributes to a high Li+ conductivity of 11.7 mS cm1 for 1M
lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in MAN, along
with a low donor number (14.6 kcal mol1) that brings about a
decreased solubility to LPSs (1/6 of that in DOL/DME). Via in-situ
Raman spectra, we demonstrate that the 1M MAN electrolyte offers a

To boost the practical energy density of lithium-sulfur batteries, replacing
conventional solvating electrolytes with sparingly solvating ones has shown
promise by enabling solid-state sulfur conversion and reducing electrolyte
consumption. However, this approach often compromises sulfur redox
kinetics. This study reports a new sulfur conversion pathway distinct from
both traditional solvated and sparingly solvated mechanisms. Speciﬁcally,
sulfur is converted into a mixture of solid and solvated lithium polysulﬁdes
(LPSs). Such a hybrid solid/solvating conversion pathway is achieved using a
newly formulated moderately solvating electrolyte, accomplishing both
lean-electrolyte operation and fast conversion kinetics for lithium-sulfur
batteries. Methoxyacetonitrile (MAN) is selected as the solvent to formulate
the moderately solvating electrolyte due to its high relative permittivity (21)
that contributes to a high Li+ conductivity (11.7 mS cm1 for 1M lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl)imide in MAN) and low donor number
(14.6 kcal mol1) that reduces the solubility to LPSs to 1/6 of that in
mainstream solvating electrolytes. The as-formulated MAN electrolyte
enables sulfur cathodes to operate at a low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of
2 μL mg1 and a low cathode porosity of 52%, displaying excellent prospects
for boosting both gravimetric and volumetric energy density.

2. Results and Discussion
S cathodes undergo different conversion pathways in different
electrolytes.[36–38] In highly solvating electrolytes, for example, 1M
LiTFSI
in
DOL/DME,
S
cathodes
perform
the
well-known
“dissolution-precipitation” conversion pathway,[39] of which the
“dissolution” step is solid S converting to solvated LPSs as shown in
Figure 1. This conversion mechanism offers fast kinetics because the
solvated LPSs function as mediators to promote redox reactions.
However, the “dissolution” process fundamentally requires a high
E/S ratio (usually >5 μL mg1) to fully dissolve LPSs intermediates,
leading to a signiﬁcant reduction in the overall energy density of
Li-S batteries as shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information. With
sparingly solvating electrolytes, S cathodes almost perform a solid
conversion process. Leveraging this mechanism, the E/S ratio can be
reduced to extremely low levels, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing the
energy density of the system. The changes in mass distribution and
the improvement in energy density resulting from the reduced E/S
ratio are illustrated in Figure S2, Supporting Information. The spe-
ciﬁc mass parameters of each component and the corresponding
energy densities are presented in Tables S1 and S2, Supporting
Information, respectively. But Li-S batteries suffer from sluggish con-
version kinetics in sparingly solvating electrolytes, which can be
attributed to two aspects. One is that the conversion process from
solid S to solid LPSs without the mediation of solvated LPSs is kinet-
ically slow. The other is that sparingly solvating electrolytes com-
monly have low Li+ conductivity,[30,40–41] for example, 0.27 mS
cm1 for a diglyme (G2)-based electrolyte (G20.8LiTFSI, the molar
ratio of G2 to LiTFSI is 0.8:1, diluted by 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl
2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropylether), in comparison with a high Li+ con-
ductivity of 12.6 mS cm1 for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte as
shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information. The sluggish kinetics
in sparingly solvating electrolytes leads to unsatisfying cyclability and
poor rate performance.[42] Besides, sparingly solvating electrolytes
are costly due to their high content Li salts,
which makes them difﬁcult to be used in
large scale.
Disappointingly, the electrolytes designation
for Li-S batteries always follows the above two
principles: highly solvating electrolytes and
sparingly solvating electrolytes. Although Li-S
batteries based on these electrolytes have made
great progress, the development is limited to
the laboratory level and is far from being prac-
tical. Under this circumstance, a moderately
solvating electrolyte that has a moderate solvat-
ing ability, lower than that in highly solvating
electrolytes but higher than that in sparingly
solvating electrolytes, toward LiPSs is immediately needed to induce
new hybrid solid/solvating sulfur conversion, and thus guarantee the
operation of Li-S batteries under lean-electrolyte condition with fast
conversion kinetics.
Here, MAN is selected to formulate the moderately solvating electro-
lyte. Among common aprotic solvents, nitriles have distinguished char-
acters, with high relative permittivity that offers high Li+ conductivity,
and with low donor number that brings about low dissolution to LPSs.
A typical example is acetonitrile (AN); however, it has a poor reductive
stability and severely reacts with Li metal (Figure S4, Supporting
Information).[43] In comparison with AN, the introduction of methoxy
for MAN reduces the polarization degree (Figure 2a), which lowers
the ability of MAN to acquire electrons and thus contributes to
enhanced compatibility with Li metal, as demonstrated by the immer-
sion tests (Figure S4, Supporting Information).
The basic properties of MAN are further compared with DOL and
DME. MAN has a decent relative permittivity of 21 that is beneﬁcial to
Li+ conductivity,[44] in comparison to DOL (6.8) and DME (7.2)
(Figure 2b). Figure 2c exhibits MAN-based electrolytes perform high
Li+ conductivity comparable to the value of DOL/DME-based electro-
lytes at different concentrations. In this work, we applied the 1M MAN
electrolyte with a high Li+ conductivity of 11.7 mS cm1 into Li-S bat-
teries, with the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte as the baseline. Besides, MAN
has a lower donor number of 14.6 than DOL (21.2) and DME (21.0),
which contributes to lower dissolution to LPSs (Figure 2b). To demon-
strate the moderately solvating character of MAN, we prepared
Li2S6-saturated DOL/DME and MAN solutions by stoichiometrically
reacting Li2S and S (detailed preparation in Experimental Section). As
shown in Figure 2d, The Li2S6-saturated MAN shows a light brown
color in contrast to a dark black color for the Li2S6-saturated DOL/DME,
indicating a lower LPSs solubility in MAN solution. The Li2S6 solubility
was measured via a precipitation method previously reported by Zhang
et al.[45] Beneﬁted from the low DN, MAN shows a solubility to LPSs of
3 mol L1 based on S1/3 (Figure 2d), which is only 1/6 of that in
DOL/DME.
Subsequently, we prepared Li2S6-saturated electrolytes to simulate
the property evolution of catholyte under lean electrolyte conditions.
The 1M DOL/DME electrolyte shows a signiﬁcant decrease of Li+ con-
ductivity from 12.7 to 5.7 mS cm1 with saturated Li2S6 (Figure 2e).
In contrast, the 1M MAN electrolyte maintains a high conductivity of
11.7 mS cm1 with saturated Li2S6. The contact angle of electrolytes
and Li2S6-saturated electrolytes was tested to evaluate the wettability, as
shown in Figure 2f. The pristine 1M DOL/DME electrolyte displays a
low contact angle of 28° on S cathodes, but it shows a signiﬁcant
increase to 85° with saturated Li2S6, indicating wettability deterioration

The MAN electrolyte brings about new S conversion behaviors. The
electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries in the moderately solvat-
ing MAN electrolyte is compared with that in the mainstream 1M
DOL/DME electrolyte (an example of highly solvating electrolytes) and
the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte (an example of sparingly solvating electro-
lytes). The Li-S battery shows a typical two-plateau discharge proﬁle in
the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte corresponding to the “dissolution-
precipitation” process, and shows a one-plateau discharge proﬁle in the
G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte corresponding to the solid conversion process.
Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test was performed to conﬁrm the
anodic oxidation stability of the solution (Figure S6, Supporting Infor-
mation) and the onset potential of oxidative decomposition close to
4.0 V (vs Li/Li+) far exceeding the charging cut-off voltage (2.8 V) of
Li-S batteries. When applying the 1M MAN electrolyte, the Li-S battery
also displays a two-plateau discharge proﬁle (Figure 3a), but the ﬁrst
plateau shows a lower voltage of 2.23 V than that in the 1M DOL/DME
electrolyte of 2.28 V, and the second plateau shows a higher voltage of
2.14 V in comparison to 2.09 V for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte. CV
curves (Figure 3b) show similar results, with two reduction peaks at
2.19 and 2.06 V for the 1M MAN electrolyte, in comparison to two
reduction peaks at 2.28 and 1.99 V for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte,
and one reduction peak at 1.52 V for the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte.

thermodynamically

different
from

in

Besides, GITT proﬁles demonstrate the 1M MAN electrolyte provides
the fastest conversion kinetics among the three electrolytes. The Li-S
battery with the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte unsurprisingly shows high
overpotentials during the whole discharge process, owing to the slow
solid conversion pathway and low Li+ conductivity. As for the 1M
DOL/DME electrolyte, it is widely accepted to afford fast redox Li-S
chemistry with the mediation effect of solvated LPSs, and the corre-
sponding GITT proﬁle shows low overpotential. However, when
applying the 1M MAN electrolyte, the overpotential is even lower
than the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte at the ﬁrst discharge plateau, indi-

more

conversion
dynamics.
Rate

G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte offers the poorest rate performance with only
85 mAh g1 at 1C. The Li-S battery with the 1M DOL/DME electro-

demonstrate the 1M MAN electrolyte alters the conversion mechanism
of S cathodes and enables fast conversion kinetics.

The working mechanism of S cathodes in
the 1M MAN electrolyte under conditions of
low E/S ratio and low porosity are illustrated,
respectively, in Figure 5e,f. During sulfur conversion in ether electro-
lytes, the high-LPSs-content catholyte signiﬁcantly decreases Li+ con-
ductivity and deteriorates wettability. In contrast, 1M MAN electrolyte
—with its “high dielectric constant (ε) and low donor number (DN)”
design—provides sufﬁcient free Li+ while moderately inhibiting LPSs
dissolution to avoid electrolyte deterioration. Therefore, S cathodes in
1M MAN electrolyte perform a hybrid conversion process (Figure 5e)
with solid S to solvated LPSs, as well as solid S to solid LPSs, substan-
tially reducing electrolyte involvement during redox reactions, ensur-
ing a complete conversion under lean-electrolyte conditions. Similarly,
dense S cathodes are also capable to work at locally lean electrolyte
conditions beneﬁted from the unique S conversion mechanism in the
1M MAN electrolyte as shown in Figure 5f.

delivers a high initial discharge capacity of 907 mAh g1 and exhibits
stable cycling for 50 cycles with 72% capacity retention (Figure 5g).

3. Conclusion
In this work, we developed a moderately solvating MAN electrolyte to
address the key issues (high electrolyte dosage and high cathode poros-
ity) limiting the overall energy density of Li-S batteries. MAN possesses
a high relative permittivity but a low donor number, combining fast
Li+ conductivity and suppressed dissolution to LPSs. The 1M MAN elec-
trolyte provides a unique conversion mechanism distinguished from
that in highly solvating and sparingly solvating electrolytes, as revealed
by in-situ Raman characterizations, with S converting to both solvated
and solid LPSs. The hybrid solid/solvating mechanism brings about fast
conversion kinetics and avoids high-LPSs-content catholyte, as formed
in the traditional DOL/DME-based electrolytes, which kinetically
impedes the conversion process under lean and locally lean electrolyte
conditions. The 1M MAN electrolyte successfully enables S cathodes to
run at an ultralow E/S ratio of 2 μL mg1 and a low cathode porosity
of 52%, signiﬁcantly boosting both gravimetric and volumetric energy
density of Li-S batteries. This work provides a methodology for devel-
oping electrolytes for high-energy-density Li-S batteries.
4. Experimental Section
Preparation of electrodes and electrolyte: S and Ketjen black (KB, ECP-600JD,
Lion) in the mass ratio of 4:1 were ﬁrst ground for 1 h to obtain a well-mixed
S/KB mixture, which was then heated at 155 °C in an Argon atmosphere. Sulfur
cathode slurry was prepared by mixing S@KB composite, Super P (DodoChem),
and LA133 binder (Chengdu Indigo Power Sources) at mass ratios of 80:12:8 (for
the cathodes with 64 wt% S content) or 85:7:8 (for the cathodes with 68 wt% S
content) in deionized water using a planetary centrifugal mixer (AR-100, THINKY)
at 2000 rpm. The cumulative mixing duration exceeded 30 min. The resulting
slurry was coated onto an aluminum foil current collector at a wet thickness of
400 μm to achieve an active material loading of 4 mg cm2. For low-porosity
cathodes (52% porosity), a freeze-drying process was employed: the as-prepared
electrodes were frozen at 40 °C for 2 h followed by vacuum treatment at 0.1 Pa
for 2 h to facilitate ice crystal sublimation. All electrodes were vacuum dried at
60 °C for 10 h prior to use. Dense sulfur cathodes were fabricated through calen-
dering, where freeze-dried electrodes were repeatedly compressed at 60 °C using
a hot roller system, with iterative reduction of the roller gap until the desired
compacted thickness was achieved. The 1M ether electrolyte was prepared by
adding lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, DoDoChem) into 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL, 99%, DoDoChem) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.5%,
DoDoChem). The 1M methoxyacetonitrile (MAN) electrolyte was prepared by
ﬁrst adding LiTFSI into MAN (Adamas) and then magnetically stirring for 1 h to
obtain a clear solution. The Diglyme (G2, 99.9%, Adamas)-based sparingly solvat-
ing electrolyte was prepared by ﬁrst mixing LiTFSI and Diglyme in a 0.8:1 molar
ratio and magnetically stirring at 60 °C for 12 h, then mixing the obtained
LiTFSI/solvent solution with TTE in a 1:2 volume ratio.
Solubility test of LPSs: Excessive Li2S and S powder with a molar ratio of 1:5
were added into the DOL/DME mixture and MAN solvent. The solutions were
stirred at 600 r/min and at 60 °C for 48 h to obtain supersaturated Li2S6/solvent
solutions, which were then centrifuged to obtain the supernatants. Hydrogen per-
oxide (H2O2, sourced from China National Pharmaceutical Group, Ltd.) and lith-
ium hydroxide (LiOH, obtained from Adamas Reagent, Ltd.) were added into the
supernatants to oxidize S2
x
to SO2
4 . Subsequently, barium chloride (BaCl2, Ada-
mas Reagent, Ltd.) was introduced to precipitate barium sulfate (BaSO4). The pre-
cipitate was collected by ﬁltration, washed, dried, and then weighed. The sulfur
content was calculated based on the measured mass.
