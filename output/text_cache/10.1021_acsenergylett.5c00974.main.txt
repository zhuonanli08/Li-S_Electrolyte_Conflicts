a Six-Membered Cyclic Urea Solvent

ABSTRACT: Highly solvating electrolytes hold great prospects for achieving
the goal of practically high-energy-density lithium−sulfur batteries, yet they
suffer from short cycle life due to their poor compatibility with Li-metal anode
(LMA). Herein, a new six-membered cyclic urea, N, N′-dimethylpropyleneurea
(DMPU), was rationally proposed as an electrolyte solvent to conquer this
longstanding contradiction. The DMPU with high electron donicity
preferentially coordinates with Li+ to dominate the inner-solvation shell,
which not only initiates and stabilizes the S3
•−radical to facilitate S redox
kinetics and 3D Li2S deposition but also suppresses its contact reaction with
LMA kinetically. More encouragingly, the DMPU molecule with a six-
membered ring bears small ring strain, thus weakening their reactivity with
LMA thermodynamically. Consequently, the DMPU-based electrolyte bestows
an extremely high area capacity of 8.4 mAh cm−2 for a practical lithium−sulfur
cell under a high S mass loading of 8.2 mg cm−2 and ultralow E/S ratio of 3.8 μL mgsulfur
−1.
T
he lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries represent one of the
most attractive rechargeable energy storage systems
because of their high theoretical energy density (2600
Wh kg−1), low cost, and environmental benignity.1,2 In the past
two decades, tremendous efforts have been devoted to
propelling the developments of Li−S batteries, including
mechanism investigation,3−6 cathode construction,7−10 separa-
tor functionalization,11,12 and electrolyte engineering.13−15
Although the reversible capacity and stable cycling performance
have been enhanced impressively, there is still a huge gap
between the laboratory prototype and the industrial application
of Li−S batteries.16−18 To promote the practical application of
Li−S batteries, the following key issues should be resolved: (1) S
cathode undergoes a unique multistep, multielectron, multi-
species, and multiphase (abbreviated as “4M”) conversion
reaction, in which solid S is first reduced to soluble lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs) and finally transformed to solid and
insulative Li2S, resulting in sluggish redox reaction kinetics
and low utilization of S.19 (2) Soluble LiPSs intermediates can
shuttle across the separator and induce parasitical reactions with
Li-metal anode (LMA), leading to rapid depletion of limited
LMA.20,21 (3) The flooding electrolytes with high electrolyte-to-
sulfur ratios (E/S ratios) were normally adopted to achieve high
capacities with high utilization of S, while it would dramatically
impair the energy density of the whole battery device.22,23
The electrolyte of Li−S batteries determines the solvation
structure of LiPSs, which plays a vital role in promoting the
reversible conversion of LiPSs. Usually, the solvation structure
of LiPSs are composed of polysulfide anions and Li+ through the
ion−ion interaction,24 which are further surrounded by the
solvents via ion−dipole interactions that results in a solvent
shell.25 For instance, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-
dioxolane (DOL), which are the representative ether-based
solvents with moderately solvating ability for LiPSs, exhibit low
solubility for LiPSs. This low solubility of LiPSs would intensify
the precipitation of short-chain LiPSs, especially at low E/S
ratios (also known as lean electrolyte conditions), resulting in
the premature formation of Li2S passivation layer upon
discharge, which inevitably results in low practical capacity
with low S utilization.26 Furthermore, the parasitic reactions
between the shuttled LiPSs and LMA would shorten the cycle
life of Li−S batteries. The employment of sparingly solvating
electrolytes can shift the S conversion pathway from a
dissolution−precipitation reaction to a quasi-solid−solid
reaction, which suppresses the solvation and shuttle of LiPSs,
effectively extending the cycle life of Li−S batteries even under
lean electrolyte conditions.27 Nevertheless, it inevitably

To promote the S cathode redox kinetics under lean
electrolyte conditions, recently, highly solvating electrolytes
based on solvents with high Gutmann donor number (DN)
were proposed, such as N, N, N′, N′-tetramethylurea (TMU),30
N, N′-dimethylethyleneurea (DMEU),31 dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO),32 and 3-fluoropyridine (3-FPN),22 which possesses
strong electron-donating ability to regulate the solvation
structure of LiPSs as well as promotes the solubility of LiPSs
(Table S1). Furthermore, high DN solvents can provide an
additional disproportionation pathway of LiPSs by triggering the
formation of trisulfur radical anion (S3
•−) intermediate, leading
to the radical-assisted conversion pathway for LiPSs with rapid
conversion kinetics.33 Moreover, in contrast to the two-
dimensional (2D) deposition morphology of Li2S in moder-
ately/sparingly solvating electrolytes, the highly solvating
electrolyte bestows the Li2S with a three-dimensional (3D)
deposition morphology, which offers more assessable surface
area for the reversible conversion between LiPSs and Li2S,
leading to high capacity with high S utilization. Unfortunately,
high DN solvents with high electron donicity significantly
increases the reactivity toward LMAs, which quickly consumes
the limited Li source and hence shortens the cycle life.34
Therefore, promoting the S cathode redox kinetics while
improving LMA compatibility simultaneously is essential to
achieving high-capacity and long-cycling Li−S batteries, which is
unattainable for current electrolyte design, especially under lean
electrolyte conditions.
Herein, a six-membered cyclic urea, N, N′-dimethylpropyle-
neurea (DMPU), was first incorporated as a cosolvent for Li−S
battery electrolyte to address the longstanding contradiction
between S cathode redox kinetics and LMA compatibility. As
illustrated in Figure 1 and Table S2, the DMPU has a high DN of
34.0 kcal mol−1 and a dipole moment (μ) of 4.23 D, which is
much higher than that of conventional ether solvents (DME and
DOL) and the five-membered cyclic urea (N, N′-dimethylethy-
leneurea, DMEU). Therefore, the DMPU is expected to strongly
coordinate with Li+ to form the inner-solvation shell, which not
only promises high dissolution of LiPSs but also initiates S3
•−−
based conversion pathway with 3D Li2S deposition morphology,
realizing fast conversion kinetics with high S utilization.
Furthermore, the strong confinement of DMPU in the inner-
solvation shell minimizes contact of DMPU with LMA and thus
suppresses their parasitic reactions at the interface. More
encouragingly, the DMPU is featured with a six-membered ring,
which exhibits much less ring strain compared to the five-
membered counterpart (i.e., DMEU), showcasing much
improved thermodynamic stability toward LMAs. As a result,
using a DMPU-based electrolyte (denoted as E-PDD), a Li−S
coin cell achieves an extremely high capacity of 1403 mAh g−1
(corresponding to a high S utilization of 84%) and is able to
maintain 77% capacity after 135 cycles at 0.1 C. Moreover, the
superiority of E-PDD is also demonstrated in Li−S pouch cells.
The conventional ether-based electrolytes, represented by 1.0
M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in
DOL/DME (denoted as DD), must fulfill the flooding
electrolyte condition (E/S > 20 μL mg−1) to realize high
capacity (>1200 mAh g−1), which would severely diminish the
practical energy density of Li−S batteries. This was ascribed to
the weak electron donicity and low dipole moment (μ) of DOL
(DN: 17.5 kcal mol−1, μ: 1.19 D) and DME (DN: 20.0 kcal
mol−1, μ: 1.72 D), which exhibits moderately solvating ability
with LiPSs, resulting in low solubility of LiPSs and sluggish S
redox kinetics with unfavorable 2D Li2S deposition (Figures 1a
and S1). Recently, a series of highly solvating electrolytes based

can significantly enhance the solvation of LiPSs and trigger the

To address this dilemma, we revisit the solvation structure of
highly solvating electrolyte and molecule structure of DMEU.
The desirable high DN solvents should fulfill the following two
requirements simultaneously: 1) the solvent should strongly
coordinate with Li+ and be confined within the inner-solvation
shell, thereby minimizing its contact with LMAs; and 2) the
solvent itself should possess better intrinsically thermodynamic
stability toward LMAs. Toward this end, the DMPU with a six-
membered cyclic ring was proposed. Compared with the
previously reported five-membered cyclic DMEU, the DMPU
exhibits higher dipole moment (μ: 4.23 D) and higher DN (34.0
kcal mol−1) (Figure S2 and Table S2), which can further
enhance the interaction of Li+−solvent to achieve DMPU-
confined inner solvation shell, holding great prospects to favor
the conversion kinetics and minimize free DMPU in the
electrolyte to alleviate parasitic reactions with LMA. Further-
more, based on the bond angles of DMEU and DMPU in Figure
S2 and Table S3, it can be found that the DMPU molecule
exhibits smaller ring strain than the DMEU molecule, which
elevates the intrinsically thermodynamic stability of the DMPU
molecule,35 weakening the reactivity of free DMPU solvent with
LMA.
The electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution of solvents36
was calculated in Figure 2a, which shows that O atom of DMEU
and DMPU displays stronger lowest negative ESP (i.e., ESPmin)
than those of DOL and DME, indicating their strong tendency
to coordinate with Li+. It is worth noting that the highest positive
ESP (i.e., ESPmax) of DMPU is higher than that of DMEU,
indicating a greater degree of charge separation efficiency, which
leads to higher polarity and dipole moment (Figure S2 and
Table S2). Then, the binding energies (ΔE) of Li+−solvent were
calculated in Figure 2b. Due to the bidentate coordination of
DME, ΔEDME shows the most negative value of −2.74 eV. For
those monodentate coordination solvent, ΔEDMPU, ΔEDMEU,
and ΔEDOL are of −2.65, −2.54, and −1.74 eV, respectively,
indicating that the coordination of Li+−DMPU is the strongest.
In addition, the Li−O bond length of Li+−DMPU is the smallest
(i.e., 1.69 Å), further validating their strongest interaction.

light-blue and navy-blue color, respectively, indicating the
existence of S3

reaction pathway of LiPSs, which is consistent with other
reported high DN solvents.31 In sharp contrast, the 20 mM
LiPSs solution in DD appears to be turbid with dark-yellow
color, indicating poor solubility of LiPSs with nondissociated
Sx

EDD and PDD solutions, attributing to the formation of S3
•−,
which could potentially serve as an internal redox mediator to
promote the conversion kinetics between LiPSs and Li2S.38

The solvation structure of the electrolyte was first investigated
by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 2e, the vibration
peak at 1630 cm−1 is assigned to the CO group of pure
DMPU solvent, which was up-shifted to 1651 cm−1 for 1 M
LiTFSI in DMPU/DOL/DME(denoted as LiTFSI-PDD).40 In
addition, the N−C−N stretching vibrations peaks also display
an upshift from 707.1 cm−1 in pure DMPU to 716.0 cm−1 in
LiTFSI-PDD (Figure S5), indicating the strong interaction of
Li+−DMPU. Moreover, the C−O stretching peak of DME at
848.4 cm−1 barely shifts, manifesting that the majority of DME
solvent exists in the free state. This demonstrates that the
DMPU is mainly coordinated with Li+ and preferentially
occupied the inner-solvation shell of Li+, leading to a DMPU-
dominated inner-solvation shell of LiPSs. As shown in Figures
S6a-b, both CO vibration peak of DMEU and C−O
stretching peak of DME is up-shifted in 1 M LiTFSI in
DMEU/DOL/DME (denoted as LiTFSI-EDD), suggesting
that both DMEU and DME are coordinated with Li+ in the
inner-solvation shell, denoted as the DMEU/DME-dominated
inner-solvation shell of LiPSs. In comparison, the vibration
peaks of both DOL and DME upshift obviously in 1 M LiTFSI in
DOL/DME, indicating strong coordination of DOL and DME
with Li+ (Figure S7), which leads to the DME/DOL-dominated
inner-solvation shell of LiPSs.
Furthermore, the solvation structure of LiPSs (taking Li2S6 as
a sample) was analyzed by
7Li and 17O nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectra. Compared with the 0.1 M Li2S6 in
EDD (denoted as Li2S6-EDD), the upfield shift of 0.1 M Li2S6 in
PDD (denoted as Li2S6−PDD) is remarkable (Figure 2f) with
0.2 M LiCl in DMSO-d6. This result confirms the stronger
coordination of Li+ with DMPU than that with DMEU, meaning
that DMPU is preferentially occupied in the inner-solvation shell
of Li2S6. As shown in the 17O spectra in Figure 2g, compared to
that with DMEU, a larger upfield shift is found for DMPU after
adding Li2S6, also proving the stronger interaction of Li+−
DMPU than that of Li+−DMEU. While the chemical shift of
DOL and DME in both Li2S6-EDD and Li2S6−PDD solution
shows the opposite trend of downfield shift, demonstrating the
deshielding effect of DOL and DME (Figures S8−S9).
Specifically, a larger downfield shift of DOL/DME is found for
Li2S6−PDD, indicating that less DOL/DME solvent is
participated in the inner-solvation shell of Li2S6−PDD
compared to that of Li2S6-EDD.
More importantly, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was
also performed to unveil the solvation structure of LiPSs from
the molecular level (Figures 3). To simulate the actual solution
environment in the Li−S batteries, 0.1 M Li2S6 was added to the
electrolyte solutions during MD study, namely 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0
M LiTFSI in PDD, EDD, and DD, respectively. Taking S6
2−as
the center model, the radial distribution function (RDF) of Li+
and solvents was calculated to quantify the local environment
around S6
2−in the electrolytes. For 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0 M LiTFSI
in PDD solution, the S6
2−strongly interacts with Li+ and DMPU
within 4.2 Å, validating the DMPU-dominated inner-solvation
shell of Li2S6 (Figures 3a and 3d), where the DMPU solvent with
high electron donicity is expected to promote the conversion
kinetics of LiPSs.41 In sharp contrast, for 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0 M
LiTFSI in EDD solution, the S6
2−tends to interact with both
DMEU and DME, leading to a DMEU/DME-dominated inner-
solvation shell with limited conversion kinetics improvement
(Figures 3b and 3e). Since only part of the DMEU solvent is
involved in the solvation shell, there are some parts of DMEU
molecules that inevitably exist in a free state in the solution,
which leads to the parasitic reactions with Li-metal. Yet for 0.1 M
Li2S6 + 1.0 M LiTFSI in conventional DD, both DME and DOL
occupy the solvation shell of S6
2−, leading to a DME/DOL-
dominated inner-solvation shells (Figures 3c and 3f), which is in
good agreement with the Raman and NMR results as well as the
previous work.42 It is worth mentioning that the inner solvation

The solvents with high DN could regulate the solvation
structure of LiPSs and the deposition morphology of Li2S to
promote the S redox kinetics with high S utilization.
Unfortunately, they generally react severely with LMAs and
thus shorten the cycling life of Li−S batteries. Therefore, the
electrochemical compatibility of Li-metal was investigated in the
Li||Li symmetric cells. As shown in Figures 4a and S10, at a
current density of 1 mA cm−1 with a capacity of 1 mAh cm−1,
using different electrolytes of 1 M LiTFSI + 0.4 M LiNO3 in
PDD, EDD, and DD (denoted as E-PDD, E-EDD, and E-DD,
respectively), Li||Li cells exhibit a smooth and low overpotential
during the initial 800 h. Thereafter, the overpotential sharply
increases for the cell using E-EDD, which was ascribed to the
accumulation of thick byproducts and dead Li resulting from the
continuous parasitic reaction between the DMEU solvent and
LMA. While the cycling life was extended to about 1000 h for the
cell using E-DD, owing to the absence of corrosive DMEU
solvent (Figure S10a). Remarkably, the cell using E-PDD
exhibits extremely stable cycling for over 1400 h with negligible
voltage polarization increase. Furthermore, the morphology of
the Li-metal electrode recovered from the Li||Li cell after 400 h
was observed by SEM. The Li-metal cycled in E-EDD and E-DD
shows obvious cracks with porous mossy-like morphology
(Figures 4b and S10b), whereas that in E-PDD delivers smooth
and uniform morphology (Figure 4c), indicating superior Li-
metal compatibility possessed by the DMPU solvent. Moreover,
the Tafel plot shows that the exchange current density of E-PDD
(0.52 mA cm−2) is much higher than that of E-EDD (0.42 mA
cm−2) and E-DD (0.12 mA cm−2) electrolyte (Figure S11a),
benefiting from rapid Li+ transport kinetics in E-PDD (Figure
S11b), which can eliminate the local aggregation of Li+ and
suppress the growth of Li dendrites.45,46
To understand the origin of better Li-metal compatibility in
the DMPU-based highly solvating electrolyte, the Gibbs free
energy changes (ΔG) of DMPU and DMEU reacting with Li-
metal were calculated through density functional theory (DFT).
The underlying decomposition pathways of DMPU or DMEU
with Li-metal are proposed in Figure S12, where the N center of
the cyclic urea is more likely to undergo attack by Li, leading to
the formation of oxynitride lithium and alkyl lithium. As shown
in Figures 4d and 4e, the ΔGDMPU (−3.1 eV) is lower than that of
ΔGDMEU (−3.7 eV), indicating that the DMPU solvent exhibits
lower reactivity toward Li-metal compared to that of DMEU.
This validates that the six-membered cyclic DMPU molecule
exhibits smaller ring strain than DMEU molecule, which
improves its intrinsic stability thermodynamically, thereby
weakening the reactivity of DMPU solvent with LMA. In
addition, as has been demonstrated in the Raman, MD, and
NMR studies, most of the DMPU solvent was strongly
coordinated with Li+ to form DMPU-dominated inner-solvation
shell, which minimizes the free DMPU in the electrolyte to
alleviate parasitic reactions with LMA kinetically.
First, the systematical evaluation of Li2S deposition using
various electrolytes was shown in Figures S13−22 and Table S4,
which demonstrates that the DMPU-dominated inner solvation
shell from E-PDD electrolyte can remarkably induce 3D Li2S
deposition. Whereafter, Li||Li2S6 coin cells were tested to verify
the electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries using E-PDD
electrolyte. As shown in Figures 5a-b, the cell using E-PDD
exhibits an extremely high initial discharge capacity of 1403 mAh
g−1 at 0.1 C, corresponding to a high S utilization of 83.8%,
which is much higher than that using E-EDD (1164 mAh g−1)
and E-DD (964 mAh g−1). Furthermore, the cell with E-PDD is
still able to retain 1079 mAh g−1 (77% of the capacity retention)
after 135 cycles with low voltage polarization (Figure S23),
whereas both cells using E-EDD and E-DD fail within 130 cycles
due to a serious parasitic reaction between the solvent/LiPSs
and LMAs. Specifically, the capacity at the low plateau
contributes three-quarters of the theoretical capacity. As
shown in the voltage profiles in Figure 5b, the discharge
capacity difference among these three electrolytes was mainly
attributed to their distinct capacity difference at the low plateau,
which are 937 mAh g−1 (E-PDD), 785 mAh g−1 (E-EDD), and
577 mAh g−1 (E-DD), respectively. This could be attributed to
the high solubility of LiPSs and formation of an S3
•−
intermediate in the highly solvating electrolyte, which induces
the 3D electrodeposition of Li2S to access more capacity.
Moreover, as shown in Figures S24−25, both the shuttle
constant (ks) and shuttle current measurements further
confirmed that the DMPU-dominated inner-solvation shell of
LiPSs in the E-PDD electrolyte availably restricts the casual
diffusion of LiPSs to LMA and enables effective S-based redox
conversion.

batteries (Table S5).

In summary, a new high donor six-membered cyclic urea, N,
N′-dimethylpropyleneurea (DMPU), was proposed as an
electrolyte solvent to overcome the long-standing contradiction
between S redox kinetics and Li-metal compatibility in Li−S
batteries. The theoretical and experimental studies have
demonstrated that the DMPU with high electron donicity
strongly coordinates with Li+ to form the DMPU-dominated
inner-solvation shell, which effectively regulates the solvation of
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) to initiate a S3
•−radical-assisted
conversion pathway and 3D deposition behavior of Li2S,
facilitating the S redox kinetics and utilization under lean-
electrolyte conditions. Furthermore, in addition to the strong
confinement of DMPU in the inner-solvation shell that
minimizes their contact with LMA to suppress their parasitic
reactions kinetically, the DMPU solvent featured with six-
membered ring bears small ring strain to weaken their reactivity
with LMA thermodynamically, thus enabling highly reversible Li
plating/stripping over 1400 h. Consequently, this DMPU-based
electrolyte enables the Li−S battery to achieve an extremely high
capacity of 1403 mAh g−1 (corresponding to a high S utilization
of 84%) and maintain a 77% capacity after 135 cycles. More
significantly, under practical conditions of high S mass loading of
8.2 mg cm−2 and extremely lean-electrolyte (E/S = 3.8 μL
mgsulfur
−1), the cell with DMPU-based electrolyte achieves a
remarkably high area capacity of 8.4 mAh cm−2. In addition, a
single layer Li−S pouch cell with an energy density of 238 Wh
kg−1 was also demonstrated under lean-electrolyte conditions.
Therefore, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of tuning
the ring structure of the solvent in improving the S redox kinetics
and Li-metal compatibility simultaneously, which represents a
significant advancement in developing functional electrolytes for
practical Li−S batteries with a high capacity and long cycle life.
