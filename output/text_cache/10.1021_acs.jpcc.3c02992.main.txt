ABSTRACT: The capacity of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries
fades continuously with the increasing number of charge−
discharge cycles as a result of the dissolution of lithium polysulfides
(Li2Sx, x = 4−8) generated during charge and discharge into the
electrolyte. To mitigate this, a fluorinated solvent-based electrolyte
was applied to a mesoporous carbon cathode containing confined
sulfur for use in a Li−S battery. During operation, a solid
electrolyte interface (SEI) was formed at the surface of the positive
electrode because of electrolyte decomposition, and the SEI
significantly suppressed the dissolution of the lithium polysulfides.
The chemical composition and formation mechanism of the SEI
were determined through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and operando soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy analyses. Crucially,
the Li−S battery assembled with the fluorinated electrolyte exhibited favorable discharge rate performance (620 mAh/g-of-sulfur at a
5C rate) and good cycling stability.
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used in small
electronic devices such as mobile phones, laptop computers,
and digital cameras, as well as in large devices, such as hybrid
electric vehicles (HEV) and battery electric vehicles (BEV),
because of their long operating lifetimes and high energy
densities.1 In recent years, the cruising distances of BEVs have
increased significantly. Therefore, the development of electric
storage devices having energy densities that exceed those of the
current LIBs is desired. Because the capacity of the positive
electrode is lower than that of the negative electrode in
conventional LIBs, approaches for improving the energy
density of LIBs have focused on the development of enhanced
positive electrode materials. Typically, the positive electrode
materials used in LIBs are transition metal oxides because they
exhibit excellent cycle stability.2,3 However, because the metals
in the metal oxide are heavy, there is a limit to the
improvement in terms of the theoretical capacity of positive
electrodes. In addition, rare metals, such as Co, are too
expensive for widespread use.
To achieve a high energy density, sulfur has been proposed
as a positive electrode material.4 Because sulfur positive
electrodes have a high theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g−1)
when compared to the equivalent materials in LIBs, lithium−
sulfur (Li−S) batteries using sulfur as the positive electrode
material and lithium as the negative electrode material could
be high-energy-density electric storage devices.4,5
However, sulfur-positive electrodes have several disadvan-
tages:6−10 (1) a large amount of conductive carbon additive is
required because sulfur has a low electrical conductivity, (2) a
volume change of 180% occurs during charge and discharge,
and (3) long-chain lithium polysulfide intermediates (Li2Sx, x
= 4−8) are generated during charge and discharge and dissolve
in the electrolyte. The dissolution of the lithium polysulfides is
a crucial problem because it causes rapid capacity fading. In
addition, the dissolution of lithium polysulfides induces the so-
called redox shuttle effect, and the resulting leakage current
reduces the Coulombic efficiency of the charge−discharge
cycle. This is because the lithium polysulfides that dissolve
from the positive electrode diffuse to the negative electrode,

resulting in self-discharge.7,8 Furthermore, the carbonate-
solvent-based electrolytes used in LIBs cannot be applied to
Li−S batteries because they cause side reactions with these
lithium polysulfides.9,10 Hence, many studies have been
conducted to prevent the dissolution of lithium polysulfides.
The use of “solvate ionic liquid electrolytes” or solid
electrolytes has been proposed to limit the dissolution of
lithium polysulfide. In addition, the use of composite materials
such as microporous carbon confining sulfur has also been
tested.10−19 In a solvate ionic liquid, all solvents coordinate
with lithium to form a complex cation. Therefore, a free
solvent for dissolving the lithium polysulfides is not present.
Consequently, the dissolution of lithium polysulfides is
prevented.11,12 Similarly, there is no solvent for dissolving
lithium polysulfides in solid electrolytes.13 A composite of
microporous carbon and sulfur as the positive electrode
material has been found to be effective.14−19 In particular,
when all of the sulfur is supported within the pores of the
microporous carbon having a pore diameter of less than 2 nm,
the solvent cannot physically enter the pores. Hence, as there is
no direct contact between the active material and the solvent,
the dissolution of lithium polysulfides cannot occur.10,14,15
However, neither the electrolytes, which prevent polysulfide
dissolution, nor the effect of microporous carbon is perfect;
therefore, lithium−sulfur batteries have not yet reached the
commercialization stage.
In this Article, we report an alternative method to prevent
the dissolution of lithium polysulfides using an electrolyte
based on a fluorinated solvent. When this electrolyte was
applied in a Li−S battery, reductive decomposition within the
operating potential of the Li−S cell occurred, resulting in the
formation of a solid electrolyte interface (SEI). Hence, direct
contact between the active material in the pores and the
solvent was prevented, which suppressed the dissolution of
lithium polysulfides at the surface of the positive electrode. As
mentioned above, microporous carbon is highly effective in
confining sulfur, but its sulfur loading is limited because of its
moderate/low pore volume.14,15,18,19 In this study, therefore,
mesoporous carbon with a high pore volume was selected to
improve sulfur loading. We found that the Li−S battery using
the fluorinated electrolyte not only achieved stable cycling by
suppressing the dissolution of lithium polysulfides but also
exhibited favorable rate performance. The chemical features of
the SEI film and the related reaction mechanisms were
elucidated by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
and operando soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS)
measurements.
2. METHODS
2.1. Preparation of Sulfur−Mesoporous Carbon
Composite. CNovel (Toyo Tanso Co., Ltd.) was used as
the host mesoporous carbon for sulfur. The sulfur−
mesoporous carbon composite was prepared by mixing
CNovel with sulfur (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd.,
purity > 98%) in a CNovel/sulfur weight ratio of 35:65, sealing
the mixture in a stainless-steel container, and then heating to
155 °C for 5 h to allow the diffusion of the melted sulfur into
the pores of the carbon.
2.2. Assembly of the Li−S Cell. The sulfur−mesoporous
carbon composite positive electrode was prepared by mixing
the sulfur−carbon composite, acetylene black, carboxymethyl
cellulose, and styrene−butadiene rubber in a weight ratio of
90:5:3:2 and coating the slurry on an Al foil current collector.
Cells having a sulfur−carbon positive electrode and lithium−
metal-foil negative electrode were assembled in a glovebox
under an argon atmosphere. The positive electrode was 12 mm
in diameter and approximately 51 μm thick (including 30 μm
of aluminum foil); the sulfur loading was 1.1−1.2 mg cm−2.
The negative electrode was metallic Li foil (Honjo Metal Co.,
Ltd.) with a diameter of 13 mm and a thickness of 200 μm.
The Li salt used was lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI; ED, Solvay Japan, Ltd.). Ethylene carbonate
(EC, LBG, Kishida chemical Co.), dimethyl carbonate (DMC,
LBG, Kishida chemical Co.), fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC,
Daikin Industries, Ltd.), tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-
propyl ether (D2, daikin industries, Ltd.), tetraglyme (G4,
Nippon Nyukazai Co., Ltd.), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME,
LBG, Kishida chemical Co.), and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, LBG,
Kishida chemical Co.) were used as solvents. Using these
lithium salts and solvents, the electrolytes were prepared in a
glovebox under an argon atmosphere. The prepared electro-
lytes are listed in Table 1.
2.3. Electrochemical Measurements. Constant-current
charge−discharge tests were carried out at a current density of
167.2 mA g−1 (0.1C) with charge and discharge cutoff voltages
of 3.0 and 1.0 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were
carried out in the potential range of 1.0−3.0 V (vs Li/Li+) at a
scan rate of 0.01 mV s−1. Rate tests were conducted under the
following conditions: After three cycles of precycling at 0.1C,
charging was carried out at a constant C-rate of 0.1, whereas
discharging was carried out at C-rates of 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.2,
and 0.1. All electrochemical measurements were performed at
25 °C.
2.4. Characterization. Nitrogen adsorption−desorption
isotherms were measured at −196 °C using an Autosorb-iQ
(Quantachrome Ltd.). X-ray diffractometry (XRD, JEOL Ltd.)
measurements were carried out using Cu Kα radiation. The
samples were observed by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM; SU-1500, Hitachi Ltd.). Elemental analyses were
performed by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS,
JPS-9010, JEOL Ltd.) with Mg Kα radiation. Ar-ion etching
was carried out at 400 V. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)
was performed using a DTG-60AH instrument (Shimadzu
Co.).
Operando XAS spectra of the S K-edge for sulfur in the
mesoporous carbon composite electrode were measured in
partial fluorescence yield (PFY) mode at beamline BL27SU of
the SPring-8 synchrotron radiation facility in Hyogo, Japan. A
laboratory-made cell, which has been reported previously, was
used for the operando XAS measurements.20 The mesoporous

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Figure 1a shows the nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherm
of CNovel. The isotherm shows a large hysteresis and is
IUPAC type-IV, which is characteristic of mesoporous
materials.21 Figure 1b shows the pore size distribution of
CNovel calculated using the Barrett−Joyner−Halenda (BJH)
method. Based on these measurements, we confirmed that the
micropore region was approximately 2.7% and the mesopore
region was approximately 97.3%, indicating a very mesopore-
rich carbon. The total pore volume was 2.861 cm3 g−1, and the
specific surface area calculated using the Brunauer−Emmett−
Teller (BET) method was 1717 m2 g−1.
Figure S1(a) shows an SEM image of CNovel. The particles
were determined to measure ca. 10−50 μm. Figure S1(b)
shows the SEM and backscattered electron (BSE) images of
the sulfur−carbon composite. In the BSE image, the contrast
depends on the atomic number of elements on the sample
surface. However, there is no noticeable difference in the
contrast in the image of the sulfur−carbon composite. Thus,
sulfur was not present on the CNovel surface and was
incorporated into the pores.
Figure 2 shows the XRD patterns of sulfur, CNovel, a
physical mixture of sulfur and carbon, and the sulfur−carbon
composite. The XRD patterns of sulfur and CNovel show
characteristic peaks of crystalline sulfur and a broad peak,
respectively. For the sulfur−carbon composite, no peak
characteristic of crystalline sulfur was observed, but a broad
reflection similar to that of CNovel was observed. Therefore,
from these results, we inferred that crystalline sulfur did not
remain on the surface of CNovel, and all sulfur had filled the
carbon pores.14,16
Figure S2 shows the TGA results for elemental sulfur and
the sulfur−carbon composites. Elemental sulfur showed a
weight loss at 250−300 °C, which is indicative of sulfur
sublimation. Conversely, the sulfur−carbon composite showed
a weight loss at 250−350 °C. This suggests that the sulfur
filling the carbon pores sublimated at a higher temperature
than free sulfur, suggesting that the thermal stability of sulfur
was improved by confinement.10,14,15 Moreover, the weight
loss ratio of sulfur in the sulfur−carbon composite indicates
that the amount of confined sulfur was approximately 65%.
Figure 3a shows the result of the constant-current charge−
discharge tests of the Li−S cell using 1 M LiTFSI/DME:DOL,
which is a common electrolyte for Li−S batteries. We
confirmed that this Li−S cell has two discharging plateaus,
which is the general discharge behavior in Li−S batteries
containing ethereal solvents.22,23 However, as cycling was
repeated, the charge and discharge capacities decreased
remarkably. Thus, not only does the present mesoporous
carbon have a poor ability to suppress sulfur dissolution, but
lithium polysulfides (Li2Sx, x = 4−8) can also easily dissolve in
ether-based electrolytes.6 In addition, the charge capacity was
greater than the discharge capacity. This indicates that self-
discharge occurred, and the lithium polysulfides dissolved in
the electrolyte during charging were reduced at the negative
electrode.7,8 Figure 3b shows the results of the constant-
current charge−discharge tests of the Li−S cell using 1 M
LiTFSI/EC:DMC as the electrolyte. Note that this is the main
carbonate solvent used for LIBs. The discharge capacity after
the first cycle was only 3.5 mAh g−1, which indicates that
repeated cycling is impossible. This can be explained as
follows: The carbonate solvents (EC and DMC) in the
electrolyte react with the dissolved polysulfide anions (Sx
2−) to
produce highly resistant organic sulfur compounds irrever-
sibly.9,10 Figure 3c shows the result of constant-current
charge−discharge tests of the Li−S cell using 1 M LiTFSI/
FEC:D2 = 1:1. We considered that FEC was unusable for Li−S
batteries, because it is a carbonate solvent. However, we found

These results suggest that the fluorinated solvent-based
electrolyte forms an SEI by the reductive decomposition of the
electrolyte on the positive electrode surface during the first
discharge cycle. Hence, we consider that the charge−discharge
mechanism has changed because the reaction pathway has
changed from a liquid-phase reaction involving the electrolyte
to a solid-phase reaction without participation of the
electrolyte. In addition, sulfur does not come into direct
contact with the solvent in the electrolyte because of the SEI
covering the electrode surface. This suggests that the
dissolution of lithium polysulfides could be suppressed.
Therefore, we next investigated the effect of the initial
reductive decomposition step on the chemical composition of
the positive electrode surface in the fluorinated-solvent-based
electrolyte. This experiment was carried out in potential ranges
between 3.0 and 1.0 V as well as between 3.0 and 2.0 V when
charging and discharging a Li−S cell containing the
fluorinated-solvent-based electrolyte. Crucially, the former
potential range should induce effective SEI formation, whereas
the latter should avoid it. Notably, when discharging was
limited to 2.0 V, effective SEI formation in the electrolyte did
not occur, and further cycling beyond the second cycle was not
possible (Figure 5). These results indicate that the success of
fluorinated solvent-based electrolytes arises from their ability
to form an effective SEI layer covering the carbon pores via
reductive decomposition, even on mesopore-rich carbon
surfaces.
The XPS spectra (see Figures 6 and 7) contain strong peaks
attributable to LiF for the sample cathode discharged to 1.0 V,
which resulted in subsequent stable cycling,24−26 unlike that
discharged to 2.0 V. LiF is a reductive decomposition
component of fluorinated electrolytes. Therefore, the XPS
measurements provide further evidence of the formation of an
SEI when discharged to 1.0 V. Figure S3 shows that the initial
irreversible reduction current has already started at approx-
imately 2.7 V and that the current gradually increases at 2.0 V.
Therefore, LiF and other decomposition products are probably
formed to some extent, even at discharges as high as 2.0 V.
Thus, SEI formation is considered to progress gradually during
the discharge process. In particular, because Cnovel contains
both micro- and mesopores, an SEI covering the micropores is
likely formed first, and then more electricity is required to
completely cover the mesopores. This would be completed
when the voltage decreased to 1 V.
Figure 8 shows the rate performance of the Li−S cells using
ethereal, fluorinated-solvent-based, and solvated ionic liquid
electrolytes. Table 2 lists the viscosities and ionic conductiv-
ities of the electrolytes. The viscosity and ionic conductivity of
the fluorinated-solvent-based electrolyte were higher and lower
than those of the ethereal electrolyte, respectively. However,
the fluorinated-solvent-based electrolyte exhibited excellent
rate performance comparable to that of the ethereal electrolyte
even at a C-rate of 5 in terms of the discharge capacity,
although the polarization in the fluorinated electrolyte was
larger than that in the ethereal electrolyte owing to the higher
viscosity and lower conductivity of the fluorinated electrolyte
(Figure S4). Thus, the fluorinated electrolyte not only enables
stable cycling by suppressing the dissolution of lithium
polysulfides but also shows favorable rate performance.
Despite the viscosity and conductivity of the G4/D2
electrolyte being close to those of the FEC:D2 electrolyte,
the rate characteristics of the G4/D2 electrolyte are
significantly inferior. As shown in Figure S4, the discharge
curves of the G4/D2 electrolyte are always two-step, indicating
that the intermediates react via liquid phase reactions.
Therefore, the rate characteristics of the G4/D2 electrolyte
are likely to be sensitive to its high viscosity and low
conductivity. Conversely, in the case of the FEC:D2
electrolyte, after SEI formation, the reaction proceeds as a
solid phase reaction, so the reaction intermediates are not
affected by the physical properties of the electrolyte, and this
may be the reason for the high-rate characteristics.
In addition, operando XAS measurements were performed
to investigate the compositional changes in the mesoporous
carbon-supported sulfur cathode during the initial discharge.
Figure 3S(a) shows the charge−discharge performance of a cell
used for operando XAS measurements during the first three
cycles. In the first discharge process, the mesoporous carbon−
sulfur composite cathode material yielded a discharge capacity
of 1102 mAh g−1, and the charge−discharge cycle contained
two plateaus at 2.1 and 1.7 V. Figure S5(b) shows the
cyclability and Coulombic efficiency of the operando XAS cell,
revealing a reversible capacity of 300 mAh g−1 after the initial
discharge process and a reversible capacity retention of over
80%. The charge−discharge performance of the operando cells
obtained here is clearly lower than those of the cells prepared
for charge−discharge testing (see Figure 3). This is because of
the structure of the cell required for XAS measurements, which
makes a lower charge−discharge performance to some extent
inevitable. We have observed this lower performance in
operando cells in the past; however, it does not affect our
analysis of these cells.19,27

Figure 9a shows the operando XAS results, which reveal the
compositional changes in the mesoporous carbon-supported
sulfur cathode during the initial discharge. Before discharge,
absorption peaks were observed at 2472 and 2480 eV. During
the discharge process, there was almost no absorption peak
corresponding to linear polysulfides at 2470 eV (see reference
spectra of Li2S6, Li2S4, and Li2S2 in Figure 9a), but two new
peaks appeared at 2472.8 and 2475.2 eV, which can be
attributed to Li2S.28 However, this is a rough analysis based
only on the shape of the spectra; therefore, a fuller analysis was
carried out using linear coupling fitting (LCF).
In more detail, LCF was performed to estimate the ratio of
the sulfur components during the discharge process (Figure
9b).29 The LCF analysis used pristine sulfur (S8), long-chain
polysulfide (S6
2−), medium-chain lithium polysulfide (S4
2−),
short-chain polysulfide (S2
2−), and Li2S spectra as references.
Interestingly, during the initial potential plateau phase, that is,
the first three points, the sulfur component changed minimally.
This is probably because, in the initial plateau, the reductive
reactions involve the decomposition of the electrolyte and
generation of a few sulfur intermediates. Moreover, the
generated sulfur intermediates do not remain in the electrode
and dissolve; therefore, they are unlikely to be detected. The
reason for the loss of these species is that the effective SEI,
which inhibits the dissolution of reaction intermediates, is not
formed in this voltage region; the initial SEI formed above 2.0
V is not effective for inhibiting the intermediate dissolution as
described above (Figures 5−7). As shown by the experiments
carried out at different discharge termination voltages (Figure
5), on discharging to approximately 2 V, the amount of sulfur
reversibly available for charging was negligible, which is
consistent with the XAS results. Thereafter, the amount of
Li2S increased with a decrease in the amount of S8. In addition,
a few other intermediate components were observed. Further,
unreacted S8 and a few S2
2−species were still present in the
discharged state at 1.0 V.
The lithium content calculated by LCF analysis (Figure 9c)
increased for x = 0−1.7, but the contents were lower than
those estimated by electrochemical measurements and were
still lower than 0.8 in the final discharge state (x = 1.7 based on
capacity). These results indicate that charge is consumed by
the decomposition of the electrolyte as well as the elution of
intermediates, as mentioned earlier, and that the final and
unreacted sulfur species are present. As shown in Figures 3, S5,
and 9, the reversible capacity of this system, that is, that with
mesoporous carbon, is less than 1000 mAh g−1, which is lower
than the values obtained with the microporous carbon system
(in which the electrolyte does not penetrate the pores) and the
solid electrolyte system (where there is no electrolyte).14,19,27
This can be explained by the impairment of the reversible
utilization of sulfur, as indicated by XAS analysis.
Nevertheless, sulfur, which would normally be easily and
significantly leached from mesoporous carbon, could be
reversibly charged and discharged as a result of the SEI
formed by the reduction of the electrolyte. Based on the results
of this study, we are now attempting to develop an electrolyte
that forms a better SEI, and we anticipate that this will increase
the rate of the reversible charge−discharge reactions of sulfur.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we have demonstrated that a fluorinated solvent-
based electrolyte enhances the cycling stability of a Li−S
battery. We showed that the fluorinated electrolyte reductively
decomposed on the cathode surface during the first cycle and
formed an SEI layer. This layer prevented the excessive
dissolution of lithium polysulfides by limiting their contact
with the electrolyte. The operando XAS measurements
revealed that S8 was almost directly reduced to Li2S without
