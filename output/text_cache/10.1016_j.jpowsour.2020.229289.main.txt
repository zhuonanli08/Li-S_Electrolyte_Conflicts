A B S T R A C T  
Lithium sulfur battery is an attractive next generation technology that can meet many demands of modern so­
ciety. Unfortunately, the lithium sulfur battery faces unique issues related to the polysulfide shuttle effect, that is 
due to reduction products dissolving in the electrolyte and their subsequent reduction on the lithium metal 
electrode. This adds further problems to the already challenging needs of understanding and engineering a solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer with desired properties. One of the most important SEI properties is its 
passivation of lithium metal which is critically important to the overall battery performance. Passivation is 
difficult to measure experimentally without the influence of many factors. This study reports an investigation of 
the reduction of the intermediate Li2S8 over lithium already passivated with Li2O, Li2CO3, LiOH, LiF and Li2S 
along with exploration of Li2S8 reduction over pristine lithium nanoclusters using first principles computational 
models. Significant formation of Li2S phase nucleation is found to stabilize the reduction products of the Li2S8. 
The formation of Li2S is explored in-depth with lithium nanocluster-based models determining a 2 V potential 
increase for the reduction of polysulfides due to the formation of Li2S. This investigation demonstrates passiv­
ation effects of important SEI components including Li2S.

integral to many parts of modern life. Whether the application is 
portable electronics such as laptops, phones or other devices, or long- 
range electric vehicles, there is a consumer demand for longer lasting

and greater charge life battery technology. Advanced energy storage is 
also desired for other applications like storing green energy off grid and 
increasing resilience of current energy grid [1–3]. Depending on the 
application, there are many different battery technologies that may 
fulfill the desired requirements. For many applications, the battery 
chemistry of focus has been lithium-based technologies [4]. Though it is 
important to note that there are many different possibilities in the field 
of advanced energy storage especially regarding different battery sys­
tems [5–9]. 
Lithium batteries are desirable for their high energy density, good 
cycling life, and body of knowledge and development. Most of currently 
implemented lithium battery technology utilizes lithium ion technology 
which some commercial systems have a theoretical energy density of 
~400 Wh kg−1 [10]. Though these values are good for many technol­
ogies there is a demand to increase energy density while maintaining 
similar cycle life. There are several next generation lithium technologies 
that could meet this goal while not having to reinvent the entire battery 
chemistry. One such option is the lithium-sulfur battery (LiSB). The LiSB 
offers a theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 [11–13]. This would 
be a definite and major improvement over the conventional lithium ion 
battery technology. However, the use of sulfur as an electrode in­
troduces problems that must be solved. The two largest challenges fac­
ing the LiSB is the use of a lithium metal anode and the polysulfide 
shuttle effect [14–16]. 
Since the sulfur cathode offers higher capacity, the corresponding 
anode must have a higher capacity to match. Most of the focus has been 
on using a lithium metal anode to satisfy this requirement [10]. How­
ever, lithium metal is highly reactive which reacts with the electrolyte to 
form the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer on the electrode. This 
changes many of the properties which is very challenging from a battery 
design perspective [17]. Much is unknown about the properties of the 
SEI especially regarding their passivation due to challenging experi­
mental environments and the highly reactive systems. The SEI is also 
closely related to the formation of lithium dendrites which can ruin 
battery life and create significant hazards [18,19]. How does one engi­
neer an electrode to have the desired properties when much is unknown 
about the SEI, the reactions are difficult to control and experiments are 
challenging to execute? 
The polysulfide shuttle effect is a problem specific to the lithium 
sulfur battery [20,21]. This effect arises from the dissolution of sulfur 
reduction products on the cathode. This causes active material loss from 
the cathode that enters the electrolyte and shuttles or moves through the 
electrolyte. The dissolved polysulfides will inevitably shuttle to the 
lithium electrode where they will complete their reduction forming Li2S 
and Li2S2 [22]. This contributes to loss of active cathode material as well 
as adds to the SEI introducing yet more chemical species that will change 
the properties of the electrode. 
There have been many investigations of the SEI both computational 
and experimental focusing on different aspects. Some experiments have 
focused on characterizing the SEI in situ determining how and what 
species are present. XPS experiments have provided fine chemical 
insight into the surface of the SEI [23–25]. New techniques have been 
developed to allow for molecular level precision such as soft landing 
ionization [26–28]. Other investigations have explored characterizing 
the properties of the SEI to determine how to engineer the SEI to our 
advantage [29–31]. Due to the challenging environment, computational 
work has been utilized well to assist in investigations of SEI chemistry 
and properties. Computational chemistry and materials science simu­
lations and calculations have long been used in the field of electro­
chemistry to probe fundamental processes [32–34].A wide variety of 
computational studies have explored properties of materials, reaction 
energetics and pathways, and other detailed chemical information [16, 
31,35,36]. There still is a lack of knowledge of SEI passivation properties 
with different electrolyte compositions as well as the reduction reactions 
of polysulfide species. A better fundamental understanding of these 
processes is necessary for the intelligent design and engineering of the 
LiSB to bring the battery from the laboratory to practical 
implementation. 
In this work, we used two different types of simulation to explore 
reduction of polysulfides in the presence of an SEI and without. For the 
investigation of electrochemical systems with SEI, interfaces are criti­
cally important. The chemistry that governs the reactions in a system 
such as the lithium-sulfur battery has many components which are 
inherently heterogeneous. By using large models that contain not only 
the lithium-SEI interface but also the SEI-electrolyte interface, a simu­
lation can be built and explored that contains many of the important 
interfacial dynamics. These models can provide critical information 
such as trends in passivation with different electrolyte compositions, the 
reduction reactions occurring in the system at different points in time, 
and interfacial structures and dynamics of the different materials. These 
models provide significant improvement to previous models which 
looked at thinner films or were not able to incorporate SEI, lithium metal 
and electrolyte into one simulation. The more that can be simulated in 
one box, the less assumptions that must be made. 
For the systems without SEI, lithium nanoclusters provide a 
computationally cheaper and higher level of theory pathway to inves­
tigate important battery chemistry. With computational chemistry 
simulations, there is a tradeoff between the size of the simulation and 
the level of theory used. Though small systems like nanoclusters are 
missing many aspects that one would look for in a practical battery, 
these models contain some of the most fundamentally important aspects 
of battery chemical and electronic properties. In this case, enough 
lithium atoms to react like a metal and some molecules of an electrolyte 
may constitute a model system. Though, more assumptions must be 
made, the nanoclusters of lithium can give an alternative perspective 
and tool to explore polysulfide speciation, reduction potentials, and SEI 
phase formation. 
Here we report on the reduction of Li2S8 and relative passivation of 
systems with lithium and Li2O, Li2CO3, LiF, LiOH and Li2S in the pres­
ence of a DME electrolyte with Li2S8. We found that Li2O and Li2S 
experienced the most polysulfide reduction followed by Li2CO3, LiF and 
LiOH respectively. Unlike in previous simulations with similar models, 
there was significant formation of Li2S phase nucleation adding to the 
existing SEI. Due to limitations of the periodic boundary conditions 
methods, obtaining a closer look at the formation of Li2S from an en­
ergetics and finer structure perspective requires the use of additional 
methods. In order to investigate these ideas further, lithium nanocluster 
models were used to determine reduction potentials and explore the 
formation of Li2S with a novel analysis method based on ideas from 
graph theory. This combined approach provides multiple perspectives 
and a more comprehensive view of the reduction of polysulfides over a 
lithium metal anode that is critical to understanding lithium sulfur 
battery performance. 
2. Methodology 
2.1. Multiple interface lithium/SEI/Li2S8 with DME models 
These electrode/electrolyte interfacial models were simulated using 
Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [37–39] using the slab 
model approximation to represent the electrode surface in contact with 
the electrolyte. VASP was chosen due to its high performance, available 
simulation methods and wide usage. Electron-nuclei interactions are 
implemented via projector augmented wave pseudopotentials and the

to represent solid structures [40,41]. For this study, density functional 
theory (DFT) calculations were used to determine the electronic struc­
ture and then the forces for ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) sim­
ulations. A NVT ensemble was implemented at 330 K with a Nose 
thermostat with 0.5 as the damping parameter. 400 eV was used as a

cutoff energy with 1 × 10−6 eV as the electronic convergence parameter. 
Gaussian smearing was used with a smearing parameter of 0.5. A 2 × 2 
×1 Monkhorst-Pack K point grid was used for surface Brillouin zone 
integration [42]. The structures used are the same for Li2O, LiOH, 
Li2CO3 and LiF as in previous publications [43,44]. The Li2S layer was 
built for this work following previously established procedure [43]. The 
AIMD simulations were run for ~15 ps per simulation in order to pro­
vide enough time to obtain a sampling of processes in the system and 
observe polysulfide reduction. All charge analysis for these systems was 
obtained with the Bader partial charge scheme [45–47]. 
All the systems for the AIMD simulations were created by separately 
optimizing bulk lithium and SEI structures. Li (100) facet was chosen for 
the lithium surface based on previous calculations where it was found to 
have the lowest surface energy [48,49]. The SEI facets were chosen 
based on what facets would minimize the lattice mismatch between the 
lithium and SEI. If the lattice mismatch is too high, the simulation is 
unable to converge or provide reasonable results due to the energy 
introduced from the strain of the mismatch. The initial interfacial dis­
tance between the SEI and lithium was calculated by finding the distance 
that provided the minimum energy in a potential energy scan. In this 
work the surfaces used for Li2O, LiOH, Li2CO3, LiF and Li2S respectively 
are 100, 100, 101,100 and 100. For the case of Li2O and Li2S, the 
resulting slabs created by cutting at the 100 planes are asymmetric. One 
side of the slab has Li on the surface whereas the other surface is an 
exposed O/S layer. For both the Li2O and Li2S, the orientation where the 
oxygen/sulfur layer was exposed to the lithium slab had a significantly 
lower interfacial energy (Table S-2/S-3). We selected this orientation 
because a lower interfacial energy is critical to the convergence and 
physicality of the results of the model. For all of the surfaces used, these 
planes do not necessarily provide the lowest surface energy out of all 
possible facets when the surfaces are exposed to vacuum. However, 
there are multiple interfaces in our system and we must balance the 
interfacial energy, lattice mismatch and surface energy along with 
computational limitations. The results reported here should be consid­
ered unique to the particular planes that are being used. The structures 
were then optimized before adding an electrolyte layer on top of the SEI. 
The electrolyte layer was incorporated by amorphously packing a vac­
uum slab with solvent molecules (1,3 – dimethoxyethane) and one Li2S8 
molecule. This particular choice of solvent was made because DME is a 
commonly used solvent in lithium sulfur battery technology and Li2S8 is 
the most likely polysulfide to shuttle from the sulfur electrode to the 
lithium electrode. The longer the chain length of the polysulfide (# of 
sulfur atoms) the more soluble the species is and therefore more likely to 
shuttle. The polysulfide was placed near the surface in a configuration 
such that the sulfur atoms were all close (<3 Å) from the surface. Finally, 
a single helium layer was added on top of the electrolyte to prevent 
interactions across the Z-axis of the system. This stops the electrolyte 
from reacting with the bottom of the lithium slab due to periodic 
boundary conditions. 
2.2. Nanocluster models 
These models were simulated with Gaussian09 [50] and visualized 
with GaussView 6 [51]. The hybrid exchange-correlation functional 
used to solve the first principles equation was B3PW91 with a 
6–311++G (d,p) basis set for all the lithium clusters. All of the models 
utilize implicit solvent of DME by using the IEFPCM solvent model in 
Gaussian09 [23,52]. The model solvent used was THF with a dielectric 
constant of 7.2. These methods were chosen based on keeping methods 
similar to previous publications for these models for comparison [44]. 
All simulations were submitted for geometry optimizations and fre­
quency calculations. This allows for the calculation of the free energy 
and determine that a structure is not in a transition state. The initial 
structures for the models were created by separately optimizing the 
structures of each polysulfide and each lithium cluster. Then the opti­
mized models were placed near each other such that the polysulfide 
species was centered on the cluster and ~1.5 Å distance from the 
lithium. The coordination representation based on graph theory was 
made using the NetworkX package in python [53]. The Kamada-Kawai 
force directed drawing algorithm was used to create the map layout 
[54]. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Li/SEI/Li2S8 with DME (periodic interface models) 
First, we characterize the passivation effects and structural changes 
in the passivation layer when the Li metal is covered by a thin layer of 
common SEI components: Li2CO3, LiF, Li2O, LiOH and Li2S. The 
passivation effect is tested by following the dynamic evolution of the 
system when exposed to an electrolyte composed of Li2S8 dissolved in 
DME solvent. The initial and final configurations of all of the multi 
interface systems are shown in Figures S-1 to S-5 and the energy over 
time is displayed in Figure S-6. 
Fig. 1 shows a summary of the simulation where Li2CO3 is used as the 
passivation layer. Over the 15 ps of simulation time, the Li2S8 underwent 
four reduction reactions. The first three occurred in the first 4 ps 
reducing the S8 into S3, 2 S2 and one S species. At about 12 ps, a final 
reaction occurs where the S3 is reduced into a S2 and S. Based on the 
Bader charge curves, the polysulfide curve tracks with the lithium curve 
thus implying that the electrons for the reduction are coming from the 
lithium slab. It is important to note that the lithium curve does not 
match the polysulfide curve exactly due to the transfer of charge be­
tween the lithium and SEI layers. The Li2CO3 SEI curve has some elec­
tron build up at the beginning of the simulation (~2 e) which is similar 
to our previous studies. This initial charge comes from the lithium slab 
which likely occurs because of the favorability of the interfacial struc­
ture between the lithium and the Li2CO3 but there is some instability in 
the curve which we was reported before with Li/Li2CO3 [43,55]. 
As seen from the system snapshots, the products of the polysulfide 
reduction are closely coordinating with the Li2CO3 surface indicating 
initial phase formation of LixS2 or LixS. Compared to our previous work 
with LiTFSI and LiFSI, the Li2S8 experienced an additional ~4 e−
of 
reduction. 
The polysulfide in the LiF system (Figure S-7) experienced less 
reduction extent than that on Li2CO3. There were two reduction re­
actions in this system. First the S8 was divided into a S5 and a S3 around 1 
ps into the simulation. Then the S5 experienced further reduction by 2 
ps, breaking into an S3 and an S2. These products underwent no more 
reactions for the time of the simulation. From the snapshots, it is clear 
that the reduction products did not significantly modify the surface of 
the LiF. This is a different outcome than in the Li2CO3 simulation, where 
the polysulfide reduction products heavily coordinated and modified the 
interface. This is due to the difference in surface structure. Li2CO3 has 
lithium atoms that are less coordinated and have more degrees of 
freedom in the surface layer than LiF. The Bader charge curves 
demonstrate that the electrons which reduced the Li2S8 came solely from 
the lithium layer which agrees with previous work. As with the Li2CO3, 
the Li2S8 had a greater extent of reduction than both LiTFSI and LiFSI.

The Li2O system, Figure S-9, exhibits quite different behavior than 
the previous simulations. The largest difference is in the speed that the 
system reacts with the Li2S8. It can be observed from the first frame that 
the polysulfide has already been reduced by ~8 e. This can be confirmed 
from the snap shots as well. At frame A, many changes have already 
occurred in the system as seen by the broken PS bonds and movements of 
Li atoms. No additional reactions occur in the system for the rest of the 
simulation. Another interesting change in behavior from previous work 
done with Li2O, is that the SEI has a nearly constant net Bader charge of 
~0 e. In previous simulations, we found that in the first frame the 
lithium transferred a large number of electrons to the Li2O. In this 
simulation, we see the lithium passing a large number of electrons to the 
polysulfide directly. We have hypothesized that the reason for the quick 
electron transfer was due to the favorable interface between lithium and 
Li2O. This quick transfer of electrons was recently reported on by an 
experimental study [56]. That effect could still be important here but 
with the added question of how it transfers to the Li2S8 as quickly. 
Perhaps this is an extension of the nucleophilic reactions that Li2O can 
have or due to the reduction products closely interacting with the sur­
face lithium atoms. When comparing the Li2S8 to LiFSI and LiTFSI 
simulations, the Li2S8 was reduced further than the lithium salts on this 
surface. 
Li2S is a possible SEI component because the SEI in this case is 
formed by reactions between lithium and the electrolyte. Due to the 
polysulfide shuttle effect, polysulfides will be present in the electrolyte 
which can then react with lithium to form Li2S if they are fully reduced. 
The Li2S simulation presented here was created according to previously 
established procedure. 
Fig. 2 summarizes the results for the Li2S system. The Li2S8 un­
dergoes a series of quick reactions between 0 and 1 ps. Then the 
remaining polysulfide products remain unreduced for the rest of the 
simulation time. From the Bader charge curve, the Li2S layer started 
with ~5 e from the lithium layer. This is similar to observations from the 
Li2O which have similar interfaces. In the case of the Li2O, an oxygen 
layer was exposed to the lithium metal layer where in Li2S, there is a S 
layer exposed to the lithium. During the first picosecond when the 
polysulfide is being reduced, the electrons for the reduction come from a 
combination of the lithium and Li2S layers. As with the Li2CO3 and Li2O, 
the small chained polysulfide by products of the reduction are closely 
interacting with the lithium atoms on the surface of the Li2S. The charges 
of the Li2S layer by layer are included in Figure S-28. As found in our 
previous work, the surfaces which are exposed to the interface have a 
different net Bader charge per atom than those in the bulk of the Li2S. 
The bulk net Bader charges for both the lithium and the sulfur layers are 
quite similar which implies the presence of a bulk charge structure. 
Taking a closer look at the geometry of the system in Figure S-10b, 
the formation of new Li2S is present. On the left there are only 4 layers of 
sulfur and on the right, there are 5 layers of sulfur. There is an additional 
layer of sulfur that is not present in the initial simulation configuration. 
The additional sulfur from this layer comes from the byproducts of the 
Li2S8 reduction which is 2 S2 and 4 S species. It is worth noting that the 
new Li2S layer does not have the same number of atoms as the already 
existing layers. However, there were only two lithium ions from the 
Li2S8 but there are several lithium atoms in the new 5th layer. The video 
of the system shows that lithium atoms diffuse from lower layers in the 
Li2S and from the lithium metal itself to make up these atoms in the new 
layer (Figure S-11) The lithium atoms in the new layer originate in the 
first layer of lithium in the Li2S layer, but other lithium atoms diffuse 
upward to replace the lithium atoms that moved from the top layer. 
The Li2S system was not the only simulation to nucleate a new Li2S 
phase. Li2O and Li2CO3 showed close coordination with the Li2S8 
reduction products and lithium atoms which were on the surface. This 
behavior was not observed for LiOH and LiF. The reason behind this 
difference is due to the structure of the SEI layers. In the structures 
where more lithium atoms are exposed to the top layer, such as Li2O and 
Li2S, many surface Li atoms are pulled from the surface and are incor­
porated into a new Li–S layer. Li2CO3, LiF and LiOH have less lithium 
atoms on the surface but in the case of Li2CO3, the lithium atoms have 
more degrees of freedom. This allows the facile coordination of lithium 
atoms compared to LiF and LiOH. 
Figure S-12 (Tables S–1) compares the different SEIs by extent of 
reduction and time it took the simulation to reach that extent. The 
reactivity trend of the systems was Li2O––Li2S > Li2CO3> LiF > LiOH. 
Compared to our LiFSI and LiTFSI work the trends are partially different. 
In the case of LiTFSI, there were no differences in the extent of the 
reduction but some systems were faster than others (Li2O >
LiOH––Li2CO3). In the case of LiFSI, there were significant differences in 
the observed extent of reductions leading to more refined trends in 
reactivity (Li2O > LiOH > LiF––Li2CO3). The biggest difference is in the 
position of LiOH and Li2CO3. With LiTFSI and LiTFSI, the Li2CO3 was 
one of the most passivating materials but with Li2S8 it’s the 3rd least 
passivating SEI. LiOH which passivated LiTFSI and LiFSI less than 
Li2CO3 and LiF now passivates Li2S8 the most. This is particularly 
interesting because the species being reduced changes the trend of 
passivation. This would mean that there is no general passivation trend 
but it depends on the particular chemical species in question other than 
for Li2O’s lack of passivation. There were also differences in the 
reduction times compared to the LiFSI and LiTFSI systems as well. In the 
cases with noticeable Li2S formation, the kinetics were much faster. 
We hypothesize the reason behind this change in trend is due to the 
formation of Li2S phases. As we identified, Li2O, Li2S and Li2CO3 seem to 
nucleate a new Li2S phase. Given the favorability of Li2S formation, the 
ability to form Li2S would cause the reduction to be driven to a higher 
extent since the resulting Li2S would be stabilized. The initial formation 
of Li2S has been observed before in another computational study that 
looked at similar systems. This study used different structures that 
involved a much higher degree of surface strain which causes the sys­
tems to be generally more reactive. 
In these simulations, the Li2S8 was reduced into small fragments such 
as S3, S2 or S. In Li2S, Li2O, LiF and Li2CO3, there were no species larger 
than S3. In LiOH, there were two long lived S4 fragments. When inves­
tigating the polysulfide speciation during the reactions there are some 
observations such as that longer fragments are preferentially reduced 
into smaller fragments. For instance, this happens in the LiF system, 
where at the B- > C reaction, the S5 is reduced instead of the S3. This was 
investigated in previous work with a different level of theory [57]. The 
conclusion from this previous work is that longer chained polysulfides 
will be preferentially reduced due to their more favorable electron af­
finity. This observation is confirmed using a different level of theory and 
in the presence of the five different SEI surfaces. 
3.2. Li nanocluster with LiSx and Li2Sx 
From the multiple interface models, several observations are clear. 
One, the polysulfide will undergo reduction reactions to some extent 
creating a wide variety of polysulfide chain length speciation. Two, the 
polysulfide’s reduction products can form Li2S quite easily and that 
modifies the passivation trends of the SEI. These two aspects are 
particularly interesting for further investigation given the importance of 
polysulfide’s reduction reactions with the lithium metal anode. These 
model systems can be used to probe the formation of a Li2S phase at a 
more precise level. Figure S-13 shows an overview of the systems used 
to explore these aspects. The polysulfide species were placed near the 
lithium nanocluster in the presence of an implicit solvent. Then each 
system was optimized to find the ground state minimum energy 
configuration which provides the thermodynamically stable structure, 
properties and energetics which can be used for further analysis. These 
simulations allow for a closer look at the chemistry identified in the 
previous AIMD simulations. 
The first systems explored were ones with no additional electrons 
added to the system. This is equivalent to a completely unpolarized

Due to the large number of simulations run with the lithium nano­
cluster, the speciation of the polysulfide species is investigated. For the 
smaller lithium clusters, the extent of polysulfide reduction was less than 
in larger clusters which is expected due to the number of lithium atoms. 
Unlike in the AIMD simulations which are time based, the lithium 
nanocluster models are based on thermodynamics so one cannot follow 
a particular species being reduced into progressively smaller fragments. 
However, it is clear from the reduction products of the nanoclusters that 
longer chained species are not favorable. The largest species observed is 
S5 which can only be found in the Li1 simulations. The dissociation of 
long chained polysulfides has been investigated by our group and found 
similar results that in the presence of many lithium atoms, the poly­
sulfide is almost completely reduced and dissociated [49]. 
In our previous study with lithium nanoclusters, DME, LiTFSI and 
LiFSI were probed using the same methodology [44]. In these simula­
tions, the extent of reduction was not at the same magnitude as with the 
polysulfides. This also matches with comparisons in reactivity with the 
PBC multiple interface simulations. The resulting reduction products of 
the LiTFSI and LiFSI would separate from the salt molecule and attach to 
the surface which was considered to be the beginning of possible phase 
formation of some types of SEI such as LiF or Li2O. In the case of the 
polysulfides, the reduction products cause a significant amount of 
change to the overall geometry of the cluster. Instead of sulfur just 
coordinating on the surface, in many cases the sulfur is deeply incor­
porated into the structure. This is a large difference in behavior 
compared to the previously studied systems and must be unique to the 
polysulfides and formation of Li2S. 
Investigating the phase formation of Li2S is challenging from exam­
ining the structures visually. This is because of the amount of defor­
mation of the clusters that the sulfur reduction products caused via 
incorporation into the nanoclusters. This led us to develop an alternative 
characterization technique based on analyzing the systems with a 
coordination-based technique. Instead of using the normal XYZ co­
ordinates, we reduced the dimensionality of the geometry by using only 
the distances between atoms (From XYZ to R coordinates). This data was 
used to create a new representation of the system utilizing graph theory. 
In this representation, each atom is a node and if the atoms are within 
3.5 Å of each other, then an edge is drawn between them which is 
weighted according to the calculated distance. An example of the co­
ordination representation is demonstrated in Fig. 3. 
The advantage of this representation is that it allows an easier 
analysis of what atoms are connected to which other atoms without 
ambiguity which becomes even more useful as cluster sizes increase. The 
reduction of dimensionality with this representation does change the 
structure of the cluster which should be considered. From Fig. 3, we can 
identify that the polysulfide was reduced once, resulting in a S2 and S3 
fragment. The S3 fragment is closely coordinating with the Li atom while 
the S2 fragment is closely coordinating with the original Li+ ions. The 
coordination representation can also be used to show other types of data 
as well such as the atomic partial charges of the system (Figures S-15 and 
S-16). Figure S-9 shows the same system, but with the inclusion of 
atomic polar tensor (APT) [58] partial charges as a secondary color 
scheme. The results here show what is expected for the sulfur species. 
That is the terminal sulfur atoms such as 3 and 5 are more negatively 
charged than a bridge sulfur atom such as 4. All of the lithium atoms in 
the system are similarly charged regardless if they were initially a 
lithium ion or a lithium atom. They all have high positive values due to 
oxidation for the reduction of the sulfur as well as polarization effects 
from the terminal sulfur atoms. Centrality measures were also explored 
and discussed in the SI (Figures S-17/S-18). 
Though the geometries of the nanoclusters seem to be widely vari­
able from a first glance there are many general trends that the coordi­
nation representations follow. Based on the characteristics of the 
coordination representation, several families of similar structures were 
identified. In the small lithium nanoclusters (Li1 and Li5), three families 
were more common than others. These were named PS (polysulfide), 
Chain, and Structured. Examples are shown in Fig. 4. The PS family has 
the characteristic of a mostly intact polysulfide which is seen in the 
representation as sulfur atoms in close proximity with many shared 
edges. This type of structure is common to any simulation where longer 
chained polysulfides remain intact. The Chain family is identified by the 
coordination representation having pinch point atoms that are shared 
with other distinct parts of the cluster. This pattern is representative of 
clusters in which the atoms are loosely coordinating and connecting to 
each other. The Structured family is characterized by the distinctive 
patterned look that arises from the coordination of a sulfur to ~4 lithium 
atoms that are shared amongst multiple sulfur atoms. These three fam­
ilies all arise due to different charge states, polysulfide sizes, and 
nanocluster sizes. The PS family is common for low charge state and low 
number of lithium atoms since the polysulfides are extensively reduced 
otherwise. The chain structure is common for systems with a low 
number of lithium atoms where the polysulfide has experienced more 
reduction. Finally, the structured family arises when the polysulfide is 
completely reduced and the system is at a higher charge state. 
In the larger systems, there are two families of coordination repre­
sentation that are more common: Cluster and Central families (Fig. 5). 
The Cluster family is characterized by the lack of incorporation of sulfur 
into the lithium structure. This corresponds to when the polysulfide 
reduction products stay on the surface of the nanocluster. The Central 
family corresponds to when the polysulfide is incorporated into the

3.2.1. Applying coordination representation to Li/SEI/Li2S8 models 
The coordination representation established in the previous section 
is not specific to just a molecular system. This type of analysis can be 
used for any system such as the larger systems with multiple interfaces. 
Since the main objective of this representation is to explore Li2S phase 
formation, only a small part of those systems is relevant. The interface 
between the SEI and the electrolyte was trimmed so that the only 
remaining species were the lithium atoms in direct contact with the 
polysulfide and the polysulfide itself. The decision not to include DME or 
the non-lithium parts of the SEI was made to allow for easy comparison 
to the nanocluster models. An example of the coordination representa­
tion applied to the Li/LiF/Li2S8 system excluding non-Li and S atoms is 
shown in Figure S-19. The number of atoms is not constant for each 
frame because some atoms will move further away or closer to the 
interface during the dynamics. The other atom types were removed for 
better clarity of figures and to compare to the nanocluster systems. 
Figure S-20 contains examples for systems where the atom types were 
not removed. 
The Lithium atoms in LiF (Fig. 5) are connected in very orderly way 
that looks squarish. This is not surprising given the structure of the LiF 
and that the lithium atoms remain closely bound to the fluorine for the 
duration of the simulation. The polysulfide is reduced twice but still 
remains closely interacting with the lithium ions. The sulfur remains 
coordinated to mainly the same lithium atoms without being incorpo­
rated into the structure. The structure of Li2CO3 is much less organized 
looking than LiF due to the increased movement of the Li2CO3. The 
largest behavior difference here is the incorporation of the sulfur atoms 
into the lithium structure. The remaining polysulfide fragments become 
separated as they coordinate more closely with the lithium atoms but 
there are some lithium atoms shared amongst the sulfur atoms. 
Like LiF, LiOH experienced less reduction than some of the other 
simulations which is noticeable by the clearly intact polysulfide. The 
lithium atom structure remains mostly unperturbed as the sulfur is never 
incorporated. This is similar to the PS family of coordination 
representation. Another interesting observation is that the lithium atom 
coordination is very stable. From previous analysis of the structure with 
atom density profiles, there was a significant amount of rearrangement 
present in the LiOH. Though this rearrangement occurs, the lithium- 
lithium coordination remains constant. 
As discussed earlier, the Li2O system (Fig. 6) exhibits the most 
reduction for the Li2S8. Even though the S8 is reduced by ~8 e, there is a 
long lived S3 species that lasts throughout the entire simulation (sulfur 
atoms 28, 26, and 25). The coordination representations here are almost 
indistinguishable from the nanocluster simulations in the cases of the 
Central family because the sulfur atoms are well incorporated into the 
lithium structure and the ratio of lithium atoms to sulfur atoms is close 
to the Li15 nanocluster models. This demonstrates that the Li nanocluster 
models can provide similar behaviors to the much large simulations with 
both a higher level of theory and reduced computational cost. 
For the Li2S, we observed the clearest example of Li2S phase for­
mation out of all of the investigated systems. The coordination repre­
sentation shows the progression of the polysulfide from being on the 
edge of the representation to being mostly incorporated into the struc­
ture by structures D and E. There are few differences between the green 
nodes and yellow nodes regarding their coordination which further 
demonstrates how a new layer of Li2S was formed in the simulation. 
Figure S-21 and Figure S-22 show the average coordination numbers 
for different species in each of these systems over time. For the inves­

3.2.2. Cluster formation and reduction energies 
One advantage of using a different type of simulation with higher 
level of theory is the relative ease of determining reduction energies. The 
lithium nanoclusters are used to determine the cluster formation energy 
(from infinite separation to close contact with no added electrons) as 
well as the first and second reduction potentials. First, we examined the 
cluster formation energy which is represented by Gcluster. This is defined 
as Gcluster = GLi+PS – GLi - GPS. From first observation, the numbers for the 
cluster formation energies are quite chaotic in general (Tables S–4). 
Some values for cluster formation are within reasonability (i.e. ~-2 eV), 
while others are high in magnitude (>|5| eV). There is no obvious trend 
from just looking at the cluster formation energies but there are some 
large differences in the extent of polysulfide reduction from the simu­
lations. Fig. 7 shows the scatter plot of cluster formation energies as a 
function of the number of sulfur bonds broken. What is clear from these 
scatter plots is that the cluster formation energy is highly linear based on 
how many sulfur bonds were broken during the optimization. This ex­
plains the variation in Tables S–4. There is a ~ - 4 eV change in the 
cluster formation energy per S bond that is broken. This implies that 
there is a −4 eV per reduction of the polysulfide. Even though no elec­
trons are being added to the system, there is a transfer of electrons from 
the lithium atoms to the sulfur atoms. This number is higher than ex­
pected from our previous electron affinity calculations by around −2 eV 
[57]. The only difference in these simulations is the presence of the 
lithium nanocluster. This additional stabilization in the energy is pro­
vided by the interactions between the polysulfide and lithium cluster 
which is essentially the formation of Li2S. The -4eV corresponds to ~ 3 V 
vs Li/Li+. This compares well with the experimentally determined 
voltage plateau associated with the reduction of S8. 
This linear trend was accounted for by subtracting the slope times the 
number of sulfur bonds broken from the cluster formation energies. This 
was done to allow for comparisons in the energies that were not related 
to the extent of reduction of the polysulfide species. Figure S-23 is the 
same data with the correction of the linear trend. These scatter plots now 
show no trend as expected, thus confirming the physical meaning of the 
slope in Fig. 7. 
In order to recreate what processes would occur in battery operation, 
electrons were added to these systems. In the cluster formation energies, 
the simulations had no excess electrons. However, in battery operation 
there will be polarization that can be accounted for in these simulations 
by adding 1 e−and then 2 e−to determine the first and second reduction 
potentials. Figures S-24 and S-25 have the non-modified cluster forma­
tion energies along with the 1st and 2nd reduction potential energies in 
the terms of eV for the Li2PS and LiPS systems respectively. 
From the reduction potentials alone, there are few conclusions to be 
drawn. For most of the systems, there is no connection between the 
length of the polysulfides and the reduction energies. There doesn’t 
seem to be any connection between the cluster size and the reduction 
trends either. This implies that the simulations must be examined on a 
case by case basis to characterize the reduction energies. Other than the 
final geometry of the structure, two other types of data were used to look 
at the reductions. One is the HOMO of the system and the other is the 
spin density if defined. The HOMO shows where the highest energy 
electron is located which is an approximation for where the most 
recently added electron should be located to determine what was 
reduced. A better measure for this is the spin density which is the dif­
ference in spin up and spin down electrons which shows where an

One interesting observation from the deconvoluted cluster formation 
energies is that the Li2PS systems have generally more positive cluster 
formation energies than the neutral LiPS systems. For the Li1 nanocluster 
simulations, the cluster formation energies of the Li2PS are ~2 eV 
(Figure S-26) which is associated with polysulfide reduction whereas 
the LiPS systems are ~ −3 eV *(Figure S-27) with the same type of 
reduction. Though the values are not as constant, this same trend is 
found in all of the other nanocluster systems. This means that LiPS 
species have a higher affinity for lithium and would be expected to form 
these clusters spontaneously unlike the Li2PS systems. This is likely due 
to a difference in stability between the two types of polysulfides. 
When looking beyond the Li1 clusters, there is more variation in the 
cluster formation energies. For the Li2PS curve with Li5, there are values 
close to 0, 0.5 and values around 3 eV (Figure S-24). From closer ex­
amination, the values that are closer to 0 and negative are all associated 
with the lithium being reduced. The values that are around 2 eV or 
higher were the simulations where the polysulfide was reduced. This 
result shows that as the lithium clusters become larger, the size starts to 
influence the behavior. Now that there are 5 lithium atoms in the cluster, 
there is observation of localization of the spin density in the lithium 
cluster. For the LiPS models with this cluster, the cases associated with 
lithium reduction are more positive than the sulfur cases. In the other 
larger clusters (Li10, Li15 and Li20), the generalizations of the corrected 
cluster formation become less clear. For instance, in the Li2PS with Li15 
systems, all of the systems are associated with the lithium cluster being 
reduced but the values range from the ~0.5 eV–~2.0 eV. A possible 
reason for the increased spread of cluster formation energies is due to 
changes in the geometry and in the products of the simulation. Once the 
nanocluster sizes are in the 10-lithium atom or greater range, the ratio of 
sulfur to lithium becomes such that the metallic nature of the lithium 
remains intact even with extensive reduction. 
4. Conclusions 
The reduction of polysulfide over lithium metal in the presence of SEI 
and without SEI is a critically important process in the operation of the 
LiSB due to the polysulfide shuttle effect and reactivity of lithium metal. 
From our PBC simulations with multiple interfaces we observed that 
none of the test SEI layers: Li2O, Li2CO3, LiOH, Li2S, LiF were able to 
completely passivate the reduction compared to bare Li metal. Out of 
these systems, Li2O and Li2S were the least passivating while LiOH was 
the most passivating to Li2S8. The trends for passivation did not match 
the previously determined trends for passivation that were determined 
with LiFSI and LiTFSI which demonstrates that the passivation trends 
need to be analyzed considering the particular species being reduced. 
When the reduced molecule is Li2S8, these results suggest that this is due 
to the formation of Li2S. The simulations where Li2S was able to form 
experienced more reduction and clear formation of Li2S on top of the 
other SEI components. The extent of this phase formation was far more 
than observed for LiF or Li2O in previous systems. 
The formation of Li2S was then explored with lithium nanoclusters 
representing nuclei of the Li deposition process, and the marked increase 
in phase formation was observed again. Compared to previous publi­
cations with these systems, small amounts of LiF or Li2O were formed. 
With polysulfide reduction, much of the sulfur was completely incor­
porated into the structure of the lithium which demonstrates the ther­
modynamic favorability of Li2S phase formation. We also reported the 
cluster formation and reduction energies for these systems which pro­
vides expected reduction energies after accounting for additional poly­
sulfide reduction. There is an associated ~ −4 eV for each sulfur bond 
broken in the polysulfide which is due the expected 2 eV reduction of a 
polysulfide and an additional 2 eV due to stabilization by the lithium 
including Li2S formation. 
These results demonstrate the inevitability of polysulfide reduction 
and subsequent formation of Li2S on the Li metal surface, even if covered 
by passivation layers. Li2S phases on the lithium metal provide little 
passivation to polysulfides partially due to the ability to form more Li2S. 
The inclusion of Li2S as a SEI component is necessary in discussions of 
the SEI for lithium sulfur battery technology.
