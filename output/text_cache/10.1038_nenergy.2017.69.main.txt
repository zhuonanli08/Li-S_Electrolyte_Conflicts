Direct observation of lithium polysulﬁdes in
lithium–sulfur batteries using operando
X-ray diﬀraction
Joanna Conder1, Renaud Bouchet2, Sigita Trabesinger1, Cyril Marino1, Lorenz Gubler1
and Claire Villevieille1*
In the on going quest towards lithium-battery chemistries beyond the lithium-ion technology, the lithium–sulfur system is
emerging as one of the most promising candidates. The major outstanding challenge on the route to commercialization is
controlling the so-called polysulﬁde shuttle, which is responsible for the poor cycling eﬃciency of the current generation of
lithium–sulfur batteries. However, the mechanistic understanding of the reactions underlying the polysulﬁde shuttle is still
incomplete. Here we report the direct observation of lithium polysulﬁdes in a lithium–sulfur cell during operation by means
of operando X-ray diﬀraction. We identify signatures of polysulﬁdes adsorbed on the surface of a glass-ﬁbre separator and
monitor their evolution during cycling. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the adsorption of the polysulﬁdes onto SiO2 can be
harnessed for buﬀering the polysulﬁde redox shuttle. The use of fumed silica as an electrolyte additive therefore signiﬁcantly
improves the speciﬁc charge and Coulombic eﬃciency of lithium–sulfur batteries.
T
wenty-five years after its commercialization, the lithium-ion
battery1 remains the most widely used device for electrical-
energy storage in areas relying on high energy densities, such
as mobile electronics or automotive applications. However, despite
the substantial progress that has been made over the years, there is
an emerging consensus that lithium-ion batteries will not be able
to satisfy the requirements of future technologies2,3. In response,
batteries based on other chemistries are being developed. One of
the most promising candidate systems among them is the lithium–
sulfur (Li–S) battery4,5, whose theoretical energy density is three
times higher than that of the best Li-ion batteries. Furthermore,
with sulfur being both abundant and environmentally friendly, Li–S
batteries are advantageous systems with a view to sustainability and
commercial viability6,7.
Important advances have been made in recent years towards
practical Li–S batteries. In particular, issues related to the insulating
nature of sulfur have, to a large extent, been overcome8–15.
Nonetheless, progress is impeded by the presence of the so-called
polysulfide (PS) shuttle, which causes poor cycling eﬃciencies
and the loss of active material. The PS shuttle is caused by
thermodynamic equilibria that exist within the Li–S battery. During
cycling, the PSs undergo primarily solid–liquid and liquid–liquid
reactions, which are more diﬃcult to understand, analyse, and
control than solid–solid reactions. During lithiation (discharge),
sulfur is reduced to diﬀerent soluble PS species, ranging from long-
chain Li2S8 and Li2S6 to short-chain Li2S3 and Li2S2, and, finally, to
solid Li2S that precipitates onto the surface of the positive electrode.
As PSs are soluble in the electrolyte, they can diﬀuse within it and
be reduced at the negative electrode (metallic lithium), resulting in a
loss of active material and the formation of an insulating layer, and
ultimately in an increase of the cell resistance. During delithiation
(charge), the soluble PSs generated on the positive electrode can
shuttle between the negative electrode (reduction) and the posi-
tive electrode (oxidation) through a process known as the redox
shuttle16. As a result, the Coulombic eﬃciency of Li–S batteries
rarely exceeds 80%, and only with the help of electrolyte additives,
such as LiNO3, can it be increased to a level close to 100% (refs 17,
18). The soluble nature of the PSs also means that these species are
diﬃcult to observe in the operating device, thus severely limiting the
insight that can be gained into the relevant reaction mechanisms.
Notwithstanding, operando techniques have contributed sub-
stantially to our mechanistic understanding of Li–S batteries19–25.
For example, operando ultraviolet/visible studies26,27 and operando
X-ray absorption spectroscopy studies (XAS)28 have provided a
better understanding of the stability and reactivity of PSs. Moreover,
operando X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) has revealed the unexpected
transition of α-S8 into β-S8 at the end of the first cycle29–31. However,
in these XRD studies, the presence of PSs has been inferred
indirectly, whereas the direct operando observation of PSs by XRD
remained elusive. This is primarily due to the solubility of PSs in the
electrolyte, leading to rapid molecular reorientation. This in turn
prevents long-range ordering and therefore makes solutes typically
undetectable in XRD experiments.
Here, we report a clear XRD signature of PSs detected
by operando X-ray diﬀraction. In our approach, the PSs are
made visible through the organized adsorption of the long-chain
polysulfides at the silica surface of a glass-fibre separator. We
have further exploited the adsorption of PSs on fumed SiO2 by
using it as an electrolyte additive. We showed that through its PS-
scavenging action, this simple additive can significantly improve the
performance of the Li–S cell.

However, due to the insulating nature of sulfur and the redox shuttle
developing during cycling, this theoretical specific charge is not
reached. Most of the results in the literature using commonly engi-
neered electrodes report specific charge values of 800–900 mAh g−1

Investigation of the reaction mechanism by operando XRD
In theory, the only species that can be identified in the Li–S
cell by XRD are S8 (α- and β-polymorphs) and Li2S. Other
reduction and oxidation products generated during cycling are in
the liquid state and, therefore, in principle not detectable by XRD.
However, here we demonstrate an approach making these ‘invisible’
compounds visible.
As shown in Fig. 2a,b, once the lithiation process has started
(open circuit voltage, OCV, interval is represented in red), the
sharp crystalline α-S8 peaks (JCPDS 008-0247) rapidly disappear
and two broad peaks form at 25.56◦and 28.32◦. The latter two
peaks have never been described before in the literature. Also,
the signatures of possible candidates, such as Li2O2, Li2O, Li2CO3,
silicates, Li–Si–S–O complexes, carbon, or lithium do not match that
of the newly observed broad peaks (Supplementary Fig. 1). These
peaks appear immediately after the disappearance of the α-S8 peaks
(at ∼220 mAh g−1; pink pattern in Fig. 2a)—that is, after sulfur is
reduced to diﬀerent PS species soluble in the electrolyte (Fig. 1 and
equations (1) and (2)). At this early stage of lithiation, the peaks
may be attributed only to the long-chain PSs, in particular to Li2S8,
which tends to be unstable32, or to Li2S6. Apart from the intensity and
broadness of the peaks, almost no diﬀerence is observed between the
patterns recorded at the voltage dip between two voltage plateaux
(at ∼220 mAh g−1 and ∼375 mAh g−1, respectively).
Both the intensity and full-width at half-maximum (FWHM)
of these peaks continuously increase until the beginning of the
lower-voltage plateau. This stage of lithiation still corresponds to a
liquid–liquid equilibrium, wherein Li2S8 is reduced first to Li2S6 and
later to Li2S4. The pattern recorded close to the end of the lower-
voltage plateau (at ∼1,100 mAh g−1; orange pattern in Fig. 2a,b),
reveals the appearance of a new peak at ∼27◦. This peak can be
ascribed unequivocally to the cubic Li2S phase (JCPDS 023-0369).

The intensities of the two broad peaks at 25.56◦and 28.32◦(hereafter
referred to as PS1 and PS2, respectively) decrease over time towards
the end of the lithiation process. There (at 1,250 mAh g−1; dark-
green pattern), the broad peaks do not disappear completely from
the diﬀractogram but are still visible in the background, indicating
that a fraction of the PS species adsorbed on the surface of
glass fibres remains there permanently and does not take part in
further reduction and/or re-oxidation processes. Meanwhile, the
peak attributed to Li2S reaches its maximum intensity at the end of
the lithiation process. This observation is in good agreement with
the expected behaviour, according to which the system returns to
the solid state, and Li2S2 and Li2S are formed at the end of lithiation.
During the delithiation process, the intensity of the Li2S peak
gradually decreases and the two broad peaks reappear, reaching
their maximum intensity at around 160 mAh g−1 (starting from
0 mAh g−1 at the end of lithiation). These broad peaks almost vanish
at the end of the delithiation process (at ∼1,070 mAh g−1; dark blue
pattern), when a new set of peaks, attributed to the β-S8 phase,
appears in the diﬀractogram (JCPDS 071-0137)14,29,30.
To better illustrate the evolution of peak intensities during
cycling, we display the XRD patterns in a contour plot (Fig. 2c).
The presence of diﬀraction peaks, albeit broad, at the state of charge
(SOC) where only soluble PS species are expected, is rather surpris-
ing. However, our observation can be explained by the interactions
of PSs with other species present in the cell, in particular the glass-
fibre separator, giving rise to XRD-detectable features. The interac-
tions of the PSs with the surface groups of the glass fibres could lead
to specific adsorption with the formation of an organized layer33,34
that in turn gives rise to the broad peaks in the diﬀractograms.
Investigation of the role of SiO2 in Li–S cell
To simplify the system, we used SiO2 (fumed silica) directly as an
electrolyte additive only (Fig. 3a) and not as an additional separator
(glass-fibre separator). Interestingly, with fumed-SiO2-containing
electrolyte, we can readily observe the formation, and later the
evolution, of PS1 and PS2 peaks. These peaks appear and are
most intense at the end of the upper-voltage plateau (where long-
chain PSs are generated); thereafter, during further lithiation, their
intensity continuously decreases (Fig. 3a,b). The interactions of the
PSs with the fumed SiO2 lead to specific adsorption of only long-
chain polysulfides with the formation of an organized layer33,34 that
in turn gives rise to the broad peaks in the diﬀractograms. We noted
that no peaks are detected in the absence of fumed silica and when
a conventional polyolefin separator35 is used.

The evolution of the PS1 peak area during the first cycle is shown
in Fig. 3c. To minimize the error in the peak-area determination, the
values for the FWHM and the 2θ angle were assumed to be constant.
The PS1 peak first appears at the end of the upper-voltage plateau
(corresponding to the solid–liquid equilibrium with the formation
of the first long-chain PSs, Li2S8) and has a relatively large area.
Its area decreases linearly along the lithiation process, indicating a
decrease in the amount of long-chain PSs adsorbed at the surface
of fumed SiO2. The latter is most probably a consequence of the
disproportionation of long-chain PSs in the electrolyte and their
further reduction to Li2S. The absolute minimum is reached at the
end of the lithiation process. The peak, however, never completely
disappears, indicating that some PS species, mainly long-chain PSs,
remain adsorbed on the glass-fibre separator and that, for this
reason, a fraction of the charge is always lost here. However, contrary
to standard Li–S batteries described in the literature, these ‘lost’
long-chain PSs are not diﬀusing to the Li counter electrode and are
not further reacting therein, explaining the enhanced Coulombic
eﬃciency and higher specific charge (discussed later and shown
in Fig. 7).
Morphological analyses of the polysulﬁde adsorption
In an attempt to deepen the analyses of the origin of the newly
identified peaks, PS1 and PS2, we performed experiments using
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 4), transmission electron
microscopy (TEM, Fig. 5), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS,
Supplementary Fig. 2), and XRD (Supplementary Fig. 3). Morpho-
logical analyses (SEM and TEM) were performed on the glass fibres
extracted from the Li–S cells at the end of lithiation and delithiation
processes. The morphology was compared to that of pristine glass
fibres soaked with the electrolyte and with Li2S6 solution.
As seen in Fig. 4, in all cases the surface of the glass fibres
is covered by a polymer-like layer, yet the thickness and the
homogeneity of this layer seems to vary depending on the sample.
For the pristine separator soaked only with the electrolyte (Fig. 4a),
the covering layer is relatively thin and not continuous. We can
still distinguish the original fibrous morphology of the separator,
unlike in the case of the glass fibres soaked with Li2S6 solution
(Fig. 4b). Here, the layer is thicker and less homogeneous, with
randomly distributed agglomerates/precipitates. After cycling, the
morphology changes significantly. At the end of lithiation (Fig. 4c),
the polymer-like layer covering the glass fibres is smoothened out
and almost continuous, with some cluster-like features in between
the fibres. From the EDX analyses we know that these clusters
are mainly composed of sulfur and, thus, might be related to the
adsorbed sulfur species. Interestingly, these cluster-like features are
no longer observed at the end of delithiation (Fig. 4d), although the
surface of the glass fibres is still covered by a polymer-like layer.
This layer seems, however, to be much thinner. Most probably it is
composed of the PS species that, once adsorbed on the SiO2 fibres,
remain there permanently and do not take part in further reduction
and/or re-oxidation processes.

The same series of samples was characterized by TEM. As can
be seen in Fig. 5, the glass fibres soaked only with the electrolyte
exhibit no specific features (Fig. 5a). This is not, however, the
case for the glass fibres soaked with Li2S6 solution (Fig. 5b), in
which a bright layer (compared to the darker core made of SiO2)
appears at the surface of the glass fibre, further confirming the
adsorption of the polysulfides on the glass fibres. The thickness of
this layer is in the range of few nanometres, in the present case
not more than about 4–5 nm (in agreement with the post-mortem
XPS data (Supplementary Fig. 2) and the crystallite size, calculated
from the peak widths of operando XRD patterns, using the Scherrer
equation). It is also apparent, in the case of the separators extracted
after cycling, that the thickness of this layer with the brighter
contrast varies depending on the SOC. At the end of lithiation
(Fig. 5c), the layer covering the glass fibre is approximately two times
thicker compared to that seen previously on the glass fibres soaked
with the Li2S6 solution (about 10–12 nm versus about 4–5 nm). In
addition, on top of it there can be seen some ‘crystal-like’ features,
most probably arising from the adsorbed sulfur species and solid
end products, as discussed earlier in the text (SEM/EDX analysis). At
the end of delithiation (Fig. 5d), the thickness of the adsorption layer
decreases to about 7–8 nm and there are significantly less ‘crystal-
like’ features that can be distinguished at the surface of the glass
fibres. Most probably, at this SOC, it is a result of the oxidation of
PS species back to the elemental sulfur. The SEM and TEM analyses
were further complemented by XPS measurements (Supplementary
Fig. 2 and Supplementary Note 1).

Based on the results obtained from these diﬀerent characteriza-
tion techniques, we can conclude that the strong interaction between
the PSs and the SiO2 particles originates from the adsorption of the
sulfur-based species on the glass fibres. The estimated thickness of
the adsorption layer is in the range of a few nanometres, yet the
absolute value changes as a function of the SOC (thicker in lithiated
than in delithiated state). It, therefore, explains the relatively poor
quality of the newly identified XRD peaks, PS1 and PS2 (due to the
short-range ordering of this specific dynamic interaction). These
observations are also in agreement with the ex situ XRD measure-
ments, performed on the same samples (Supplementary Fig. 3).
Despite the rinsing of the glass fibres prior to their ex situ XRD
analysis, the adsorbed layer is still visible on the samples extracted
at end of lithiation and at end of delithiation processes, respectively.
Most probably, once the adsorbed layer is organized (giving rise
to PS1 and PS2), it is strongly attached to the surface of the glass
fibres, at least within the first 4–5 nm. The latter thickness estimated
from the TEM analysis is well in agreement with the crystallite sizes
calculated based on the Scherrer equation, and coincides with the
evolution of the area under the peak (PS1 and PS2).

The electrochemical and structural results are similar to
those for the first cycle (Fig. 2). However, the Li2S peak is
continuously broadened as a result of extended cycling. This
indicates a pronounced reduction of the Li2S-crystallite size, caused
by reoccurring volume changes and by repetitive dissolution
and precipitation of the solid end-products during prolonged
cycling. These observations suggest that the Li2S structure tends to
become increasingly amorphous. Using the Scherrer equation36, the
crystallite size was estimated to be 4.5 nm for the 1st cycle, 3.3 nm
for the 8th cycle, and 1.3 nm for the 33rd cycle. Furthermore, we
had also observed that on further cycling the peaks belonging to the
β-S8 phase are getting broader, relative to the first and second cycles,
indicating a loss of crystallinity. The same observations were made
regarding the galvanostatic curves becoming smoother after each
cycle. This progressive amorphization of Li2S during cycling may be
one of the reasons for the poor cyclability of the Li–S batteries, as
the specific charge tends to decrease simultaneously with growing
amorphization. We presume that with the ageing of the Li–S battery
it becomes more diﬃcult to form the end-of-lithiation product—
that is, Li2S with long-range order.

Fumed SiO2 as an electrolyte additive
To further explore the practical significance of the adsorption of
long-chain polysulfides on the SiO2, we tested fumed SiO2 as an
electrolyte additive in the standard coin-type cells. We found that
the presence of SiO2 in the electrolyte increases the specific charge
by at least 25% relative to that obtained using the reference cell
with standard electrolyte. Starting from the second cycle (after
the irreversible transition from α-S8 to β-S8), the specific charge
is ∼1,080 mAh g−1 for the cell with fumed SiO2 as an electrolyte
additive, and only ∼870 mAh g−1 for the cell cycled without it
(Fig. 7). After 30 cycles, the diﬀerence in specific charge between the
two cells is more than 38% (830 and 600 mAh g−1, respectively). This
indicates that the adsorption of PSs at the surface of SiO2 not only
improves the cycling performance, but also slows down the ageing
of the Li–S cell by scavenging the PS species as well as indirectly pro-
tecting the Li counter electrode. The same trend is observed for the
Coulombic eﬃciency. The Coulombic eﬃciency of almost 90% is
achieved when SiO2 is used as an additive, whereas in the case of the
reference cell with the standard electrolyte, the Coulombic eﬃciency
barely reaches 78%. This diﬀerence further confirms the findings
of our XRD studies, and highlights the potential significance of the
discovered interactions between the PS species and the glass-fibre
separator for improving Li–S-cell performance.
Conclusions
Using operando XRD, we identified the structural signatures of the
Li2Sn species adsorbed at the surface of glass fibres. We demon-
strated that only the long-chain polysulfides are adsorbed at the
surface of the glass fibres, which remain therein during the repetitive
lithiation/delithiation processes. We have utilized these findings to
not only demonstrate that the liquid–liquid interactions in Li–S
cells can be studied by means of XRD, but also to show that the
fumed silica is a promising electrolyte additive, which significantly
enhances the performance of the Li–S cell. Based on the acquired
knowledge, the role of the polysulfide scavenger(s) in the overall
reaction mechanism can be better understood. Also, the already
existing strategies to eﬀectively control and/or suppress the poly-
sulfide redox shuttle can be optimized. Certainly, our study opens
up many perspectives as several polysulfide adsorbents/scavengers,
other than fumed SiO2, have been recently identified and tested
in Li–S cells. The operando XRD can serve well in probing the
eﬀectiveness of these adsorbents/scavengers and discerning their
role in the overall reaction mechanism, depending on the surface
chemistry, particle size, concentration of the adsorbent/scavenger,
and other parameters. Also, the polysulfide adsorbents/scavengers
can be used in cells, designed specifically to probe, among others,
the reaction kinetics and the diﬀusion of the PSs.
Methods
Electrodes. A self-standing electrode film composed of 50-wt% sulfur
(Sigma-Aldrich), 25-wt% PVDF binder (BASF), and 25-wt% Super-C carbon
(Imerys) was used in the operando XRD analyses. The film was cast from an
acetone/ethanol suspension onto glass and then dried in air for 24 h prior to cell
assembly. After drying, the electrodes (11 mm in diameter) were punched out,
and XRD electrochemical cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box. The
sulfur loading was around 2.5 mg cm−2. Glass fibre (1 mm, EUJ116,
Hollingsworth & Vose) and Celgard 2400 (Celgard), both considered as
state-of-art separators for Li–S cells (Celgard37,38 and glass fibres27,39,40), were used
as separators, and soaked with 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide
(LiTFSI) dissolved in dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (Diox) with a
weight ratio of 2:1 (henceforth referred to as standard electrolyte; BASF). In our
experiments, we used ∼25 ml of electrolyte per gram of sulfur.
Polysulfides. The PS solutions in tetrahydrofuran (THF) were provided by Bosch
(Germany). The denotation of PSs indicates the nominal composition, calculated
based on the stoichiometric ratios of sulfur and lithium.
Electrochemical tests. The electrochemical cycling tests were carried out
galvanostatically between 2.7 V and 1.5 V versus Li+/Li with an applied current of
80 µAh if not indicated otherwise (equivalent to a C-rate of C/50).
XRD operando measurements. All measurements were performed at room
temperature with a PANalytical Empyrean diﬀractometer using Cu Kα radiation,
employing a home-made in situ XRD electrochemical cell developed specifically
for operando measurements41. Schematics of this cell and a characterization of its
performance are given in the Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4.
The cell was assembled with a sulfur/carbon self-standing film as a positive
electrode and metallic lithium as a negative electrode, separated by Celgard
(facing the positive electrode) and glass fibre (facing the negative electrode).
The operando measurements were performed using diﬀerent XRD-apparatus
configurations. In a preliminary attempt, a full 2θ range screening from 15◦to
55◦was performed (not shown). The patterns were collected for 1 h with a step
size of 0.033◦. Subsequently, two regions of interest were chosen—one between
22◦and 30◦, and the other between 25◦and 28◦. The XRD patterns were
collected every 30 min with a step size of 0.01667◦.
Electrolyte with SiO2 additive. Experiments in which SiO2 was used as an
electrolyte additive were performed using coin-type cells. Fumed silica
(Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI in DME:Diox (2:1), to
obtain a 2-wt% solution (a sort of colloidal solution) under ambient conditions.
The resulting solution was stirred overnight. 50 µl of the electrolyte with SiO2
additive was introduced into cells assembled only with a Celgard separator.
Identical Li–S cells, only with standard electrolyte (without SiO2), were also
assembled and used as a reference.
Morphological analyses. The glass-fibre separators soaked with the electrolyte
and Li2S6 solution as well as those extracted from the Li–S cells after cycling (a
side of the separator facing the positive electrode) were examined using SEM
(Carl Zeiss UltraTM 55), operating at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV in
secondary-electron imaging mode. The samples were transported to the
microscope without exposure to air and moisture, using an in-house developed
transfer chamber. In addition, the samples were characterized using TEM at an
accelerating voltage of 200 kV using a JEOL-2010 microscope. For this purpose
the samples were soaked with dimethyl carbonate (DMC, Sigma-Aldrich) until
some fibres detached from the separators. Subsequently, the resultant emulsions
were deposited onto carbon-coated Cu grids. Thereafter, the samples were dried
in Ar flow and transferred to the microscope using an Ar bag. The XPS
measurements were carried out with a VG ESCALAB 220iXL spectrometer
(Thermo Fisher Scientific) using focused monochromatized Al Kα radiation
