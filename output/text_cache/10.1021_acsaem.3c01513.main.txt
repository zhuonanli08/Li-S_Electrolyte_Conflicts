ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are considered ideal candidates for next-
generation batteries with high energy densities. However, the practical use of sulfur cathodes
is hindered by the dissolution of Li polysulfides in the electrolyte during the charging and
discharging processes, resulting in reduced sulfur utilization and enhanced shuttle reactions.
The use of a highly concentrated sulfolane-based electrolyte is a solution to this problem in
Li−S batteries; however, this electrolyte results in low reversibility of the Li-metal anode.
The use of a fluorinated additive in nonaqueous electrolytes is a simple and effective solution
for improving the reversibility of Li anodes. Therefore, we attempted to add
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI) anions, but the additional LiFSI was hardly soluble in the
electrolyte. Here, we found that potassium FSI (KFSI) is highly soluble as an electrolyte
additive, forming a stable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) at the Li anode. Consequently, the
Coulombic efficiency of Li was significantly improved from 87.5 to 98.8% by preparing a
sulfolane-based electrolyte containing an optimal amount of 7 wt % KFSI. The improved Li
deposition morphology, lower interfacial impedance, and large amounts of Li fluoride in the SEI were identified as factors
contributing to the improved reversibility of the Li anode. The electrolyte containing KFSI also plays an important role in improving
the cycle stability of Li−S cells. These results indicate that KFSI is an effective additive for the protection of Li-metal anodes and the
cycling stability of Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, mesoporous carbon, KFSI, sulfolane, lithium metal anode, sulfur cathode
■INTRODUCTION
Since Li-ion batteries (LIBs) were first commercialized by the
Sony Corporation in 1991, they have been used in a wide
range of applications, including smartphones and notebook
computers.1 Currently, LIBs are expanding the market for
electric vehicles and energy storage systems. However, the
energy density of commercial LIBs using graphite as the anode
material has already reached the theoretical limit, requiring the
development of new rechargeable batteries.2,3 Because Li−S
batteries have a theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1,
which is higher than that of LIBs, they are expected to become
the next-generation batteries. However, the dissolution of Li
polysulfide into the electrolyte, poor electronic conductivity of
sulfur, and volume change of up to 80% have hindered the
practical application of sulfur cathodes.4−6
The dissolution of Li polysulfide in the electrolyte is
particularly problematic, and various solutions have been
attempted. For example, composites of sulfur and carbon
materials with nanometer-scale pores and the use of electro-
lytes inhibit the dissolution of Li polysulfide.7−9 Highly
concentrated electrolytes are generally used to suppress the
dissolution of Li polysulfide.10,11 Among these, the sulfolane-
based electrolyte developed by Watanabe et al. is the most
widely used.12,13 This electrolyte not only suppresses the
dissolution of Li polysulfide but is also reported to have good
Li-ion transport properties, which are generally considered
challenging for highly concentrated electrolytes. These
favorable properties are attributed to a special Li-ion
conduction mechanism that occurs when the molar ratio of
Li salt to sulfolane is 1:2.
However, sulfolane-based electrolytes have low Coulombic
efficiency owing to the continuous reductive decomposition of
sulfolane at the Li-metal anode.14 Therefore, it is necessary to
modify the Li-metal interface. Measures to modify the Li metal
interface can be classified into two types: (1) surface treatment
before cell assembly and (2) in situ reaction of the Li metal
anode with electrolyte additives in the cell.15,16 The first
method of artificially forming a solid electrolyte interface (SEI)
requires an additional manufacturing process and is unsuitable
for industrial applications. In contrast, the second method of

using a self-constructed SEI is simpler and does not require

requirements of industrial production, it is considered
preferable to use an additive to modify the Li metal interface.17
Li fluoride (LiF) is considered a favorable component of an
SEI formed by additives,18 and numerous studies have shown
that a LiF-rich SEI can enhance the stable operation of Li-
metal anodes. Examples of additives include fluoroethylene
carbonate,19,20 HF,21,22 and trace amounts of water.23 Among
these fluoride products, our group focused on lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI). This salt is one of the most
influential electrolyte salts for lithium secondary batteries
today, and a significant amount of research has been carried
out on using it.24 Qian et al.25 reported that electrolytes
containing high concentrations of LiFSI can achieve very high
Coulombic efficiency of 99.1% at 0.2 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh
cm−2. Their report encouraged us to use LiFSI as an electrolyte
additive to the solfolane-based electrolyte reported by
Watanabe et al.12,13 However, LiFSI cannot dissolve in highly
concentrated sulfolane-based electrolytes.
In this study, to avoid the processing problems encountered
when using LiFSI and still achieve a LiF-rich SEI on the Li
metal, we applied potassium FSI (KFSI) as an additive as a
substitute for LiFSI because KFSI has a much higher solubility
in the concentrated electrolyte than LiFSI. The suitability of
FSI for Li−S battery systems should be noted as follows. Li
polysulfide dissolved in electrolytes may irreversibly react with
various electrolyte components.26 Although Li polysulfide can
be compatible with FSI anions in certain electrolytes,27,28 the
two can react irreversibly, rendering Li−S batteries inoper-
able.29 In the present study, sulfur was loaded on activated
carbon containing abundant pores with diameters less than 2
nm, through which the electrolyte cannot penetrate,30−38 so
that the Li polysulfide and the electrolyte containing FSI do
not come into contact.39−41 In this context, we report that
KFSI is an excellent additive for improving the reversibility of
metal Li anodes in Li−S batteries with a microporous carbon−
S cathode and sulfolane-based electrolyte.

electrolyte. Each electrolyte was prepared in a glovebox under an Ar
atmosphere with a dew point below −80 °C.
Preparation of Sulfur Cathode. Sulfur microporous activated
carbon (AC; MSC-30SS, Kansai Coke and Chemicals Co., Ltd.)
composites were prepared by mixing AC and sulfur at a weight ratio
of AC/S = 30:70, followed by annealing at 155 °C for 5 h and 300 °C
for 2 h. The obtained AC−S composite contained 69 wt % of S
according to TG analysis. The AC−S cathode was prepared by mixing
AC−S, carbon nanotubes, carboxymethyl cellulose, and styrene
butadiene rubber in a weight ratio of 93:0.5:3:3.5, followed by
coating on aluminum foil. Here, the sulfur loading of the AC−S
cathode was 3 mg cm−2.
Determination of Electrolyte Viscosity and Ionic Con-
ductivity. The ionic conductivities of the electrolytes with and
without KFSI were measured by using an electrical conductivity meter
(CM-60G, DKK-TOA Corp.). The electrolyte viscosities were
measured by using a viscometer (VM-10A, Sekonic Corporation).
The ionic conductivity and viscosity were measured in a thermostatic
bath at 30 °C in a dry room with a dew point of −50 °C.
Cell Configuration. Li cyclability was evaluated using a flat two-
electrode cell (Hohsen Corp.) containing electrolytes with or without
1, 5, 7, and 10 wt % KFSI, copper foil (active area: 13 mm diameter,
Furukawa Electric Corp.) as the working electrode, Li foil (200 μm
thickness and 13 mm diameter; Honjo Metal Corp.) as the counter
electrode, and a polyolefin microporous membrane (20 mm diameter;
Asahi Kasei Corp.) as the separator. Additionally, a CR2032-type two-
electrode coin cell (Hohsen Corp.) was constructed using sulfolane-
based electrolytes with various additives (7 wt % KFSI, KTFSI, or 1
wt % LiFSI), copper foil as the working electrode, Li foil as the
counter electrode, and a polyolefin-based microporous membrane as
the separator. Furthermore, a Li symmetric cell was constructed using
a coin cell containing a sulfolane-based electrolyte (with or without 7
wt % KFSI) and a polyolefin-based microporous membrane as the
separator.
For the Li−S full cell, the above-mentioned sulfur cathode,
polyolefin microporous membrane the separator, Li metal foil anode,
sulfolane electrolyte, and 7 wt % KFSI-doped sulfolane electrolyte
were used to construct the two-electrode flat cell. All cells were
constructed in a glovebox under an Ar atmosphere, with a dew point
below −80 °C.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The dissolution characteristics of LiFSI and KFSI in the
electrolyte consisting of LiTFSI, sulfolane, and D2 used in this
study were first examined. The results indicated that LiFSI
dissolved slightly more than 1 wt %, whereas KFSI dissolved
above 10 wt %. In addition, the results of the dissolution
properties of these two salts in solution systems related to the
electrolyte used in this study are illustrated in Figures S1
(dissolution in D2), S2 (dissolution in sulfolane), and S3
(dissolution in LiTFSI sulfolane solution). These dissolution
tests revealed the following: For a single solvent without
LiTFSI, no difference was observed in the solubilities of LiFSI
and KFSI, i.e., neither of the two salts dissolved very well in D2
(Figure S1), and both dissolved well in sulfolane (Figure S2).
In contrast, LiFSI was insignificantly soluble in the sulfolane
that consisted of a large amount of LiTFSI beforehand,
whereas KFSI was significantly soluble (Figure S3). This
tendency was the exact same as that of the two salts in the
electrolyte system used in this study, i.e., the sulfolane + D2
system containing a large amount of LiTFSI.
These results suggest that when a certain type of Li salt
(here LiTFSI) is present in large amounts beforehand, adding
another type of Li salt (here LiFSI) is difficult. In addition,
given the fact K salts can be significantly soluble in solutions
containing large amounts of Li salts, the “common ion effect”
may occur, which is a limit to the amount of dissociated Li ions
that can be present.43 Therefore, KFSI was selected primarily
for the addition of FSI anions in this study.
Figure 1 shows the results of a galvanostatic charge−
discharge test using Li−Cu two-electrode cells with various
contents of KFSI additive. The cycle life increased with
increasing KFSI content, reaching a maximum for the
electrolyte containing 7 wt % KFSI. This was expected because
the reductive decomposition products of KFSI act as an SEI to
protect the Li metal. To further verify the good performance of
KFSI, constant-current charge−discharge tests were conducted
using sulfolane-based electrolytes without additives, with 7 wt
% KFSI, and with the maximum soluble concentration of LiFSI
(1 wt %); the results are shown in Figure 2a. The addition of
both types of FSI salt significantly improved the cycle life. The
CE values of the electrolytes containing LiFSI and KFSI were
95.8% and 98.8%, respectively. This is presumably because the
electrolyte containing KFSI contains more FSI anions than
LiFSI, which has a poor solubility in the electrolyte. However,
to verify this hypothesis, it was necessary to investigate the
effect of adding potassium ions to the electrolyte. Therefore,
we conducted constant-current charge−discharge tests with an
electrolyte containing KTFSI with a concentration equivalent
to 7 wt % KFSI addition, and the results are shown in Figure
2b. KTFSI was selected as the salt for validating the potassium-
ion addition effect because TFSI is already present in large

Figure 4 shows the charge−discharge curves of a sym-
metrical cell using Li foil for both electrodes. Similar to the
charge−discharge results in Figure 2, the use of the electrolyte
with added KFSI enabled a much longer charge−discharge
cycle. In contrast, the electrolyte without KFSI quickly
increased in polarization and reached 0.2 V as the cycle
progressed.
Figure 5 shows cross-sectional SEM images of the Li
electrode after 5 cycles in sulfolane electrolyte with and
without 7 wt % KFSI. The Li electrode cycled in the pure
sulfolane electrolyte had a porous structure with deposits of
about 160 μm in size. In contrast, for the electrode cycled in
the electrolyte containing 7 wt % KFSI, a dense structure was
observed with deposits of about 40 μm in diameter. Porous
structures such as those observed in the sulfolane electrolyte
may exhibit high resistivity since the loss of electronic
pathways and further electrolyte degradation due to the high
specific surface area are likely to occur.
The surface and cross-sectional SEM observations above
suggest that the degree of decomposition of the electrolyte
may differ owing to differences in the deposition morphology.
Therefore, the SEI resistance was evaluated by the AC
impedance analysis. The results are shown in Figure 6. Here,
the semicircles in the high-to-midfrequency region represent
the interfacial resistance (Ri), such as the grain boundary
resistance and SEI resistance, whereas the semicircles in the
low-frequency region are attributed to the charge-transfer
resistance (Rct). Figure 6a shows that Ri increased significantly
over five cycles in the electrolyte without KFSI (from 4 to 40
Ω). This is attributed to excessive electrolyte decomposition,
which is consistent with the SEM results. In contrast, for the
electrolyte containing KFSI, Ri decreases drastically in five
cycles rather than in one cycle (from 21 to 5 Ω), as shown in
Figure 6b. This suggests that the addition of KFSI leads to the
formation of a low-resistance interface.

EDS was performed to investigate the SEI components
leading to the formation of the low-resistivity interface. The
results are shown in Figure 7 and Table 1. Figure 7a,b shows
the surface SEM images of the areas of Li formed in the
sulfolane electrolyte without and with KFSI, respectively. EDS
measurements were performed at multiple locations at 100×
magnification, and no differences were observed in the analysis
results obtained. Therefore, the obtained results represent the
overall composition of the entire Li metal surface. Table 1
shows the results of the EDS evaluation of the Li metal surface
when the electrolyte with or without KFSI was used. The
addition of KFSI increased the atomic fraction of nitrogen
atoms. This suggests that the decomposition rate of salts, such
as LiTFSI and KFSI, in the electrolyte increased. However,
despite the increase in the salt decomposition rate, the atomic
fraction of oxygen atoms (which should increase owing to salt
decomposition) was the same with and without the addition of
KFSI. This suggests that among the electrolyte components
other than the salt, the decomposition of sulfolane, which has a
large number of oxygen atoms in its molecules, may have been
suppressed with the addition of KFSI.
XPS depth profiles were obtained to further describe the SEI
of the Li metal. The results and surface models of the Li metal
with each electrolyte are shown in Figure 8. As shown in
Figure 8a, the intensity of the Li 1s spectra stopped increasing
after a certain etching time, and no difference in the intensities
was observed for the samples prepared with and without the
addition of KFSI. Thus, the SEI thickness was probably similar
to and without the addition of KFSI. As shown by the O 1s
spectra in Figure 8b, the addition of KFSI shifts the peak
attributed to SO2 to that attributed to ether. This is attributed
to the suppression of sulfolane degradation by the addition of
KFSI, which is consistent with the EDS results. Furthermore,
as shown by the F 1s spectra in Figure 8c, the intensity of the
peak attributed to LiF (685 eV) increased near the surface of
the SEI owing to the addition of KFSI. The presence of the
chemically stable LiF on the SEI surface may effectively protect
the Li metal surface.
Based on these results and discussion, we propose a surface
model of the Li metal (Figure 8d), in which the electrolyte
without KFSI decomposes the solvent, especially sulfolane, to
form an SEI rich in organic components. This probably results
in repeated disintegration and reforming of the SEI, resulting
in the deposition of fine Li particles. In contrast, in the
electrolyte containing KFSI, anion decomposition occurs, and
an SEI rich in inorganic components was formed. In particular,
the distribution of LiF on the SEI formed in electrolytes with
and without KFSI differed. LiF was present on the surface of
the SEI formed in the electrolyte containing KFSI.
Consequently, relatively large grains of Li were deposited
because the dissolution and reformation of the SEI were
suppressed.
As the KFSI electrolyte additive was shown to improve the
electrochemical properties of the Li metal anode, it was applied
to Li−S batteries. Figure S4a shows the charge−discharge
curves of these batteries in the electrolytes with and without

electrolyte without KFSI in any cycle. As shown in Figure S4,

sulfolane-based electrolyte is a practical measure for improving
the charge−discharge characteristics of lithium-sulfur batteries.
■CONCLUSIONS
We sought to improve the reversibility of Li metal anodes by
adding FSI to a typical sulfolane electrolyte used for Li−S
battery applications and found that LiFSI could hardly be
dissolved, whereas KFSI could be dissolved in large amounts.
The addition of an appropriate amount of KFSI allowed us to
take full advantage of the favorable effect of LiF in improving
the electrochemical properties of the Li-metal anode.
Specifically, LiF, which constitutes the SEI on the Li metal,
was distributed even up to the surface of the SEI. This change
led to improved structural and chemical stabilities of the SEI,
resulting in uniform and compact Li deposition. As a result, the
charge−discharge reversibility of Li is greatly enhanced. In Li−
S full cells using S-confined microporous carbon as the
cathode, which was expected to be unaffected by side reactions
between FSI anions and sulfur reaction intermediates, the
addition of KFSI to the sulfolane electrolyte significantly
improved the Li−S battery performance.
