Addressing Passivation in Lithium–Sulfur Battery  
Under Lean Electrolyte Condition
Huilin Pan, Kee Sung Han, Mark H. Engelhard, Ruiguo Cao, Junzheng Chen,  
Ji-Guang Zhang, Karl T. Mueller, Yuyan Shao,* and Jun Liu*
Reducing the electrolyte amount is critical for the high specific energy of 
lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries in practice. The reduced electrolyte condition 
(a so-called “lean electrolyte”) raises a complex situation for sulfur redox 
reactions since the reactions rely on the electrolyte mediation. The insulating 
nature of discharge product Li2S and its uncontrollable accumulation 
(passivation) at the cathode interface is one of the major challenges for stable 
cycling of a Li–S battery under lean electrolyte condition. In this study, it is 
presented that the NH4TFSI additive in electrolyte solution greatly alleviates 
the passivation issue in Li–S batteries under lean electrolyte conditions. The 
ammonium additive enhances the dissociation of Li2S and largely reduces the 
insoluble and large Li2S particles in the sulfur cathodes, which facilitate the 
reversible and sustainable redox reactions of sulfur. Therefore, the cycle life 
of Li–S batteries under lean electrolyte conditions is significantly improved. 
In addition, it is found that the morphology of Li anodes is dependent on the 
cathode structures. An ammonium additive enables homogeneous surface of 
cathode and Li anode and extended cycle life.

1. Introduction

Li2S, has attracted tremendous attention due to its high theoret-
ical energy and the use of the earth-abundant, low-cost sulfur, 
and lithium metal anode.[1] Different from Li-ion intercalation

2. Results and Discussion
The E/S ratio is a critical factor influencing the sulfur redox 
reactions by adjusting the concentration of polysulfides and vis-
cosity of electrolyte.[14] In the lean electrolyte regime, the Li–S 
electrochemistry could be significantly different from that in 
flooded electrolytes. We found that E/S ratio is closely related 
to the cyclic stability of Li–S batteries using regular sulfur/
carbon nanotubes (S/CNT) composite cathodes as shown in 
Figure 1. A similar phenomenon has been reported using the 
mesoporous ketjen black/sulfur (KB/S) composite cathode.[14] 
The CNT carbon host used here has much reduced surface area 
and lower porosity compared with mesoporous KB material, 
which is expected to avoid excessive electrolyte and to be ben-
eficial for the low E/S ratio in Li–S cells. Lowering the E/S ratio 
from 20 to 5, though the capacity was increased due to the less 
loss of polysulfides from sulfur cathode with reducing electro-
lyte amount, the cycle life was significantly decreased from 400 
to 50 cycles (Figure 1). This could be mainly due to the inability 
to maintain active interface of electrodes under lean electrolyte 
condition. Our previous work has shown that the irreversible 
insulating Li2S at the reaction interface of sulfur cathode would 
passivate the sulfur electrode and deteriorate the reactivity 
of sulfur species during cycling.[13a] The insulating products 
cannot reversibly react, which accumulate cycle by cycle and 
finally passivate the sulfur electrodes, causing the fast capacity 
decay after limited cycles.
We have previously demonstrated that ammonium salts, 
such as NH4NO3 and NH4TFSI, are effective to increase the 
solubility of Li2S and other short-chain species, by tailoring the 
ionic strength of solution, forming hydrogen binding between 
sulfide anion and ammonium cation, and promoting the disso-
ciation of Li2S in the solution.[15] This is expected to mitigate the 
uncontrollable accumulation of irreversible Li2S and improve 
the sustainability of sulfur electrodes through removing 
insulating Li2S from electrolyte/electrode interface, which 
otherwise blocks charge transfer under the lean electrolyte 
condition. NH4TFSI is soluble in the baseline electrolyte (1 m  
LiTFSI, 2  wt% LiNO3 in 1,3-dioxolane:1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DOL:DME)) and was used as an additive for sulfur elec-
trodes (S/CNT) under the lean electrolyte condition (E/S = 5) 
in Figure 2. With no additive, the sulfur electrode shows a 
capacity increase for initial cycles and reaches a maximum 
capacity of ≈860 mAh g−1 in about ten cycles, probably due to 
the increased wetting of the sulfur cathode by electrolyte during 
the initial cycles. The capacity fades rapidly after ≈40 cycles and 
the coulombic efficiency decreases quickly from initial 99% to 
less than 94% within 100 cycles. On the contrary, the Li–S cell 
with 10 wt% NH4TFSI additive in electrolyte shows significant 
improvement on the cyclic stability and sulfur utilization with 
coulombic efficiency of ≈98% over 100 cycles and ≈97% over 
200 cycle cycles (Figure 2a and Figure S1, Supporting Informa-
tion). In addition, the NH4TFSI additive improves the electro-
lyte wetting with no capacity activation process.
Scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to investi-
gate the morphologies of two sulfur electrodes in the base-
line electrolyte and NH4TFSI additive electrolyte after cycling. 
Uncontrollable accumulation of insulating sulfur-containing 
particles (≈200 µm) and a continuous surface layer on the sulfur 
cathode were observed in the baseline electrolyte (Figure 2b,c). 
This would passivate the sulfur cathode and increase the charge 
transfer resistance at the interface between sulfur electrode 
and electrolyte (Figure S2, Supporting Information), leading 
to large charge/discharge overpotential (Figure S3, Supporting 
Information). Such passivation accumulates cycle by cycle 
and causes poor rechargeability and reversibility of the sulfur 
cathode and thereby limits cycle life (yellow curve in Figure 2a 
and Figure S4, Supporting Information). In addition, the elec-
trolyte consumption and decomposition during cycling may fur-
ther reduce the available electrolyte amount and aggravate the 
passivation issue by its decomposition compound.[16] Flooded 
electrolyte (E/S ≥ 10) would alleviate the passivation phenom-
enon on the cathode as shown in the Figure S5 (Supporting 
Information); however, it is not a realistic 
solution since a large amount of electrolyte 
sacrifices practical energy density (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information).[17] Instead, the 
NH4TFSI additive significantly mitigates the 
particle aggregation, and enables uniform 
and smaller particles, and lowers discharge 
overpotential in the sulfur cathode under a 
low E/S ratio of 5 (Figure 2d,e and Figure S3, 
Supporting Information). This benefits from 
the high solubility of Li2S due to the stronger 
solvation promoted by the NH4TFSI additive 
in the electrolyte and therefore inhibiting the 
formation of large particle accumulation.[15]

The S 2p peaks were fitted with spin–orbit coupled 2p3/2 
and 2p1/2 doublets with 1.18 eV separation and a characteristic 
2:1 area ratio (Figure 3b). The S 2p peaks in sulfur cathode 
harvested from the baseline electrolyte are mainly attributed to 
Li2S, which indicates the large irreversible Li2S covering sulfur 
cathode at charged state (Figure 3b).[13a] The Li2S (S 2p3/2 at 
160.5 eV), polysulfide Li2Sx (S 2p3/2 at 162.2 eV) and elemental 
S (S 2p3/2 at 163.4 eV) are observed together for the sulfur cath-
odes from the electrolyte with NH4TFSI additive,[18,20] where the 
amount ratio of irreversible Li2S is greatly reduced. The exist-
ence of Li2Sx in the sulfur cathode, instead of just Li2S in base-
line electrolyte, should be caused by the favorable oxidation of 
discharge product of Li2S with a higher solubility promoted by 
the NH4TFSI additive in the electrolyte during the charge pro-
cess.[15] The Li2Sx has a higher solubility than Li2S; therefore, 
it can function as an effective redox mediator to promote 
the sulfur reactions.[21] A new peak S 2p peak at 159.3  eV is 
observed in the electrolyte with NH4TFSI additive, which is 
ascribed to the extremely electron-rich S2− species sharing neg-
ative electrons with carbon host.[22] The new S2− species is prob-
ably favorably formed through the competing solvation effect 
between ammonium additive and S2− anion rather than solva-
tion between Li+ and S2− anion.[15] The binding energy of sol-
uble S2− species is not electrostatically stabilized by Li+ ions as 
that in the bulk Li2S, which is considered to facilitate the elec-
trochemical sulfur redox reactions during cycling. This is con-
sistent with the improved performance and less aggregation in 
sulfur electrodes after cycling (Figure 2). The bumps of S 2p3/2 
peaks located at 166.2 and 168.5 eV are ascribed to the LiSOx 
and Li2SO4, respectively,[18] which might be from the decompo-
sition of TFSI− anion.[19]

In addition, the morphologies of Li metal anodes were char-
acterized to understand the effect of ammonium additive on 
extending cycle life of Li–S batteries (Figure 5). It can be found 
that the Li metal surface becomes porous and the corrosion is 
highly heterogeneous for the baseline electrolyte as indicated 
by the arrows in Figure 5a. Point-type corrosion occurs, which 
could be induced by the formation of large agglomeration of 
particles in the sulfur cathode side as shown in Figure 2b,c. 
This phenomenon causes severe corrosion through the bulk 
lithium at certain corrosion points as shown in the cross-
section image of lithium metal in Figure 5b. Such corrosion 
easily builds dead lithium and causes cell failure even though 
a large area of clean lithium still exists in the Li metal anode. 
In contrast, with the ammonium additive, the Li metal sur-
face is much smoother, and a completely different corrosion 
mode takes place which produces a much thinner and homo-
geneous surface layer on the Li anode (Figure 5c,d). The result 
suggests that the fading of Li metal anode and sulfur cathode 
are highly coupled (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The

lombic efficiency and extended cycle life.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we report NH4TFSI additive to address the pas-
sivation issue in Li–S batteries under lean electrolyte condition. 
The ammonium additive enhances the dissociation of Li2S and 
greatly reduces the accumulation of insoluble and insulting 
Li2S large particles in the sulfur cathodes, which facilitate the 
reversible and sustainable redox reactions of sulfur. The cycle 
life of Li–S battery under lean electrolyte condition was signifi-
cantly improved with ammonium additive. The morphologies 
of Li anode is dependent on the cathode structure. Ammo-
nium additive enables homogeneous surface of cathode and 
Li anode and extended cycle life. Nevertheless, the long cycle 
life of Li–S battery under lean electrolyte is still challenging. 
We believe that investigations by coupling advanced electrolyte 
with advanced cathode and anode architectures would further 
improve the cyclic performance of Li–S battery under lean elec-
trolyte condition.
4. Experimental Section
Material and Electrode Preparation: Elemental sulfur (Alfa Aesar) 
and CNTs (Cheap Tube Inc.) were mixed at a weight ratio of 4:1 by ball 
milling for 2 h, and then the mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined 
stainless steel autoclave and treated at 155 °C in a furnace for 24 h, 
resulting in S/CNTs composite. The S/CNT electrodes were prepared 
by mixing 80  wt% S/CNT composite powder, 10  wt% super P carbon 
additive, 5  wt% LA133 binder (Chengdu Indigo Co.), and 5  wt% SBR 
binder (MTI Corp.) and forming a slurry with water. The slurry was 
cast onto a carbon coated Al foil (MTI Corp.) and dried at 60 °C under 
vacuum overnight. The dried sulfur electrodes were punched into ½’’ 
disk for use. The sulfur loading was 3–4 mg cm−2.
Preparation of Electrolytes: The electrolytes were prepared by dissolving 
1 m LiTFSI (BASF), 2  wt% LiNO3 (Alfa Aesar), and 10  wt% NH4TFSI 
in DOL (BASF) and DME (BASF) (1:1 by volume). The NH4TFSI salt 
was homemade through the reaction of trifluoromethanesulfonimide 
(HTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich) and ammonium hydroxide solution (Sigma-
Aldrich). First, the HTFSI reacted with an excessive amount of ammonia 
water to form NH4TFSI solution, then the solution was vacuum dried 
using rotary evaporator at 90 °C to get the dried NH4TFSI powder. The 
baseline electrolyte was prepared according to the same way without 
NH4TFSI additive.
Electrochemical 
Measurements 
and 
Characterizations: 
The 
electrochemical properties Li–S batteries were evaluated in 2032-type 
coin cells (MTI Corp.). The Li–S coin cell was assembled with the 
S/CNT as the working electrode, Celgard 3501 as the separator, and 
450 µm Li (MTI Corp.) as the anode respectively in Ar-filled glove box. 
The electrolyte to sulfur ratio was 5, 10, and 20 mlE g−1S (E/S = 5, 10, 
and 20). The cells were tested using Lanhe battery tester (Wuhan Land 
Electronics Co., Ltd.) at a current rate of 0.1 C and a voltage range of 1.7–
2.7 V. The capacity was based on the mass of sulfur. The electrochemical 
impedance was measured using 2-electrode coin cell on the Solartron 
1287 electrochemical workstation.
The sulfur and lithium metal electrodes were harvested from 
cycled Li–S coin cell and washed with DME solvent and vacuum dried 
overnight for post-tests. The morphologies of sulfur and lithium metal 
anode were measured using SEM (FEI Helios). The XPS measurements 
were performed on a Physical Electronics Quantera Scanning X-ray 
Microprobe using a focused monochromatic Al Kα X-ray (1486.7  eV) 
source for excitation and a spherical section analyzer. The ex situ SEM 
and XPS were taken for the parallel cells of Figure 2a at 180 cycles to 
understand the function of NH4TFSI additive.
7Li and 19F MAS NMR were performed for sulfur electrodes at 
charged state using a 2.5  mm HXY probe on a 600  MHz solid-state 
NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at the spinning speed of 20 kHz 
and ambient temperature (≈25 °C). The sulfur electrode materials 
without rising were scraped from current collector and dried in glove 
box before NMR test. The 7Li and 19F NMR spectra were obtained using 
single pulse excitation with 90 degree pulse lengths of 5 and 3.2 µs, 
respectively, followed by Fourier transformation. The 7Li and 19F 
chemical shifts were calibrated with 0  ppm of 1 m LiCl solution and 
−70.4 ppm of CF3CH2OH, respectively, as external references. The peak 
intensity was normalized using the loading amount of the sample in a 
rotor. The samples were transferred to rotors in a glove box and carried 
to a magnet within a sealed bottles to avoid air contact. Dry nitrogen gas 
was used to spin the rotors.
