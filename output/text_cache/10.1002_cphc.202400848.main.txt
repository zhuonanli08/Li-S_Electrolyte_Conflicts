The Interaction of Ether-Based Functionalized Ionic Liquids
in Lithium–Sulfur Batteries: A First-Principles Study
Chengren Li, Nan Zhou, Jiaxin Tang, Chen Wang, Rongde Sun, Baifeng Yang, Zhigao Chen,
Xiaohan Lu, Zhen Chang, Shaoze Zhang,* Changjun Peng, and Honglai Liu
Ionic liquids (ILs) as electrolytes in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries
effectively mitigate the shuttle effect. Solvated cationic ether-
based ILs, comprising [Li(G1)2]þ, [Li(G2)2]þ, [LiG3]þ, [LiG4]þ, or
[LiG6]þ paired with bis(trifluoromethyllsulfonyl)imide ([TFSA]–)
anions, are evaluated for their ability to suppress short-chain
lithium polysulfide (LiPS: Li2S1, Li2S2, Li2S4) adsorption on lith-
ium metal. The chelating capacity of solvated cations governs
interactions with LiPSs and anions. Solvation via Liþ chelation
prevents free Liþ fusion with LiPSs, reducing shuttle effects.
Remarkably, the cyclic [LiG6]þ cation exhibits superior Liþ che-
lation, stability, and minimized LiPS adsorption compared to lin-
ear cations. Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations confirm
ether-based ILs stabilize anions and lower LiPS-lithium surface
reactivity. These findings highlight solvated cation ILs as tailored
electrolytes to control interfacial LiPS behavior, advancing high-
performance Li─S battery design.
1. Introduction
Ether-based solvents, renowned for their strong solvation capa-
bility, are widely employed in metal salt electrolytes due to their
ability to form solvated cations through coordination between
metal ions and ether oxygen lone pairs.[1] Glymes, polyether
molecules with multiple ether oxygen groups, have garnered
significant attention as high-concentration lithium salt solvents
for advanced battery systems, including lithium–sulfur (Li─S)
batteries.[2,3] Their low volatility, chemical stability, and structural
similarity to poly(ethylene oxide)-based electrolytes make them
ideal candidates for suppressing lithium polysulfides (LiPS)
dissolution.[4–6] Notably, crystalline solvates formed by glymes
and lithium salts exhibit tunable Liþ coordination numbers (4–6),
as demonstrated in systematic studies by Henderson et al.[7–11]
Li─S batteries stand out for their exceptional theoretical
energy density (2600 Wh kg1) and cost-effectiveness, driven by
sulfur’s high capacity (1675 mAh g1) and natural abundance.[12–18]
However, the persistent “shuttle effect"—where soluble LiPS inter-
mediates migrate and react with the lithium anode—severely
degrades cycling stability and hinders commercialization.[19–24]
Recent advances highlight glyme-based solvated ionic liquids
(SILs) as promising solutions. For instance, [Li(G4)][BETI] (bis(penta-
fluoroethenesulfonyl)amide, [BETI]) SILs achieve 98.5% Coulombic
efficiency over 100 cycles by stabilizing LiPS intermediates, while
their cyclic solvated cation structures effectively mitigate free Liþ
reactivity.[25–29]
Despite these advances, the atomistic mechanisms governing
LiPS adsorption on lithium metal surfaces in SIL electrolytes
remain poorly understood. Conventional ionic liquids (ILs), with
their high polarity and tunable ion interactions offer unique
opportunities to modulate LiPS behavior. Yet, the subtle interplay
between solvated cations, anions, and LiPS at electrode interfaces
is challenging to probe experimentally. In this work, we address
this gap through a first-principles study of ether-functionalized
SILs
composed

bis(trifluoromethyllsulfonyl)imide

anions and glyme-Liþ cations ([Li(G1)2]þ, [Li(G2)2]þ, [LiG3]þ,
[LiG4]þ, and a novel cyclic [LiG6]þ). Using surface electrostatic
potential (ESP),[30] independent gradient model (IGM),[31] atoms-
in-molecules (AIM),[32] and electron density difference (EDD) anal-

adsorption dynamics. Crucially, our designed cyclic [LiG6]þ dem-

suppressing LiPS–lithium surface interactions while maintaining
structural integrity—a finding corroborated by ab initio molec-
ular dynamics (AIMD)[33–35] simulations. This work establishes
a molecular-level rationale for SIL design to combat the shuttle
effect, advancing high-performance Li─S battery development.

Given that the structure of glymes tends to exhibit cyclic char-

group of glymes, namely G1, G2, G3, and G4. G1 (monoglyme)
and G2 (diglyme) are short-chain oligoethers with two and three
oxygen atoms per molecule, respectively. A single G1/G2 mole-

G6) exhibit a strong chelate effect due to their multidentate coor-
dination, which stabilizes Liþ through entropic and enthalpic
gains from forming cyclic complexes. Shorter glymes (G1, G2) lack
sufficient chain length to form stable cyclic complexes, using two
molecules compensates for this deficiency, enabling effective Liþ
coordination (Two G1 molecules ([Li(G1)2]þ) provide four oxygen
atoms, satisfying Liþ coordination and two G2 molecules
([Li(G2)2]þ) provide six oxygen atoms, forming a stable octahedral
coordination.).[46] We proceeded to construct a series of solvated
cations, including [Li(G1)2]þ, [Li(G2)2]þ, [LiG3]þ, [LiG4]þ, and
[LiG6]þ.
Through this investigation, our aim was to explore the distinct
interactions between ether-based functionalized ILs and the
surface of lithium metal, with a specific focus on their effects
in lithium–sulfur batteries. It should be noted that due to the chal-
lenging nature of long-chain lithium polysulfides reaching the
lithium metal surface in lithium–sulfur batteries, this study con-
centrated on the theoretical exploration of short-chain lithium
polysulfides, namely Li2S1 and Li2S2, along with the transition
state Li2S4, in combination with ILs on the lithium metal surface.
3. Results and Discussion
3.1. The Geometric and the Energetic Information
The sign of ESP (Vr), whether positive or negative, indicates
whether the dominant contribution to the ESP at a given position
(r) arises from the nuclear charge or electronic contributions.[30] In
Figure 1, we provide ESP maps for anions and cations, while also
indicating their respective maximum (Vs,max) and minimum (Vs,min)
ESP values. These values allow for a comparison of the attractive
or repulsive abilities of these species.
The negative ESP for anion arises due to the strong electron-
absorbing capacity of the oxygen atoms, resulting in the cluster-
ing of the most negative ESPs around these atoms (5.01 eV). The
cations display scattered positive ESPs that are concentrated
around Liþ ions. These potentials exhibit a fluctuating pattern,
alternating between decreasing and elevating as the number
of glymes increases. In particular, [LiG6]þ showcases the highest
positive ESP of 9.23 eV. Figure 1 illustrates that the number of
covalent bonds formed between individual glyme molecules
and lithium (Li) atoms increases incrementally from 2 to 5 as
the cation progresses from [Li(G1)2]þ to [LiG4]þ, indicating a cor-
responding enhancement in chelating ability. Conversely, despite
[LiG6]þ exhibiting the highest positive ESP value, it forms only
three Li─O covalent bonds due to its complete ring structure,
which constrains the ability of Li atoms to establish covalent
interactions with each oxygen atom. The cyclic conformation
of [LiG6]þ allows oxygen atoms to coordinate with Liþ in a near-
planar arrangement (Figure 1), forming a rigid solvation shell that
minimizes Liþ exposure. This contrasts with linear glymes (e.g.,
[LiG3]þ), where partial coordination leaves “exposed” Liþ sites vul-
nerable to polysulfide interaction. The high-surface ESP of [LiG6]þ
(9.23 eV, Figure 1) generates a strong repulsive field against neg-
atively charged sulfur atoms in polysulfides (e.g., Sx

adsorption affinity. The specific bonding capability and stability of
this configuration require further investigation through subse-
quent analyses. Through this chelating capacity, Liþ ions that
are bound to the anions are effectively SEQUESTered, making

The issue of the shuttle effect in lithium–sulfur batteries
presents a significant challenge in the field, as the adsorption
and deposition of insoluble short-chain lithium polysulfides, such
as Li2S1 and Li2S2, onto the surface of lithium metal leads to cor-
rosion and depletion of active substances. This poses a formida-
ble obstacle for scientists to overcome.[47–50] As a result, we
directed our focus not only to the anions and cations themselves
but also to their interaction with lithium polysulfides on the lith-
ium metal surface. To better approximate the real-world scenario,
we included the discussion of the adsorption of lithium polysul-
fides, even though only a relatively small fraction of Li2S4 in the
transition state approaches the surface of the lithium metal. This
approach allows us to explore the transitional process from Li2S4
to Li2S1 and Li2S2, as well as the specific adsorption properties of
short-chain lithium polysulfides. The adsorption energy quantifies
the thermodynamic favorability of interactions between the IL

In contrast, the adsorption energy does not consistently rise
with an increase in the number of glymes. Surprisingly, it follows a
distinct pattern depending on the specific binding of different
lithium polysulfides. When binding with Li2S1, various cations
exhibit similar and relatively lower adsorption energies, which
hover around 8.0 eV. This behavior suggests that the capacity
of Li2S1 to absorb Li atoms approaches saturation when con-
fronted with solvated cations. Therefore, none of these cations
display a propensity to approach the lithium metal surface further
under the influence of different cations.
However, in the case of binding with Li2S2, both [Li(G1)2]þ and
[LiG4]þ cations demonstrate a decrease in adsorption energy
compared to the binding with Li2S1. This decrease can be attrib-
uted to the structural characteristics of these cations. The ring-like
but smaller notched rings of [Li(G1)2]þ and [LiG4]þ possess a
higher positive ESP in comparison to the [Li(G2)2]þ cation, result-
ing in a greater affinity for Li2S2 binding. Thus, a stronger IL bind-
ing occurs, leading to an overall reduction in the adsorption
energy.
During the binding process of Li2S4, the adsorption energy
continues to increase with the incorporation of ethylene glycol
dimethyl ether-like chains up to [LiG3]þ. However, it undergoes
a sudden decrease at [LiG4]þ and continues to decrease until
[LiG6]þ. This nonmonotonic trend is rooted in the structural
and electronic evolution of the solvated cations: shorter glymes
(G1–G3) adopt linear configurations, allowing Liþ to coordinate
with multiple oxygen atoms while leaving residual sites for
Li2S4 adsorption. As the chain elongates (G4–G6), the glymes fold
into cyclic conformations (e.g., crown ether-like for [LiG6]þ), fully
saturating Liþ coordination and sterically blocking Li2S4 access.
Cyclic cations [LiG6]þ exhibit higher surface ESPs (e.g., 9.23 eV
for [LiG6]þ vs. 6.76 eV for [LiG3]þ, Figure 1), creating stronger
repulsion against the electron-rich sulfur atoms in Li2S4. G4 marks
the critical chain length where glymes transition from linear to
cyclic coordination. While [LiG4]þ retains partial flexibility, its
incipient cyclic structure begins to shield Liþ and introduce steric
hindrance, causing the abrupt adsorption energy decrease. The
trend intensifies in [LiG6]þ due to full ring closure. While linear
[LiG3]þ partially stabilizes Liþ, its incomplete coordination leaves
“exposed” Liþ sites that bind Li2S4. In contrast, cyclic cations
achieve full Liþ encapsulation, eliminating these reactive sites.
3.2. Interaction Analysis
In 1985, Bader introduced a topological analysis known as
(“AIM”),[32] which utilizes wave function analysis to examine elec-
tron density. This theory identifies critical points (CP) as the loca-
tions in which the electron density gradient reaches zero. Using
this method, a comprehensive and intuitive understanding of
chemical bonding and interatomic interactions in molecules can
be attained through the analysis of electron density gradients.
During the AIM analysis, the systems under investigation were
identified based on the identification of CP between their respec-
tive atoms in the molecular structure. These interactions were
visualized in the molecular image through the use of red dots,
representing the bonds between the atoms, which were con-
nected by red bond paths. Moreover, the bond energy (EB),
obtained from the potential energy density (V) at the CP, serves
as a valuable descriptor for characterizing the strength of each
interaction.[51,52]

This analysis aimed to investigate the diverse array of inter-

respective bonding energies.
In light of the innovative and outstanding performance dem-
onstrated by the [LiG6]þ system in the preceding section, we
have chosen to feature [LiG6]þ as the representative system in
the main text to avoid redundancy in visual representations.
The remaining systems, [Li(G1)2]þ to [LiG4]þ, are presented in the
supplementary materials. Figure 3 illustrates the AIM diagrams of
the anions, cations, and lithium polysulfide within the [LiG6]þ
system, along with the bond energies associated with various
types of bonds.
In Figure 3 and Figure S1 to S4, Supporting Information, the
Li2S2 system exhibits a distinctive characteristic wherein it does
not require a singular strong bond to establish a robust overall
interaction. This can be attributed to the fact that, unlike Li2S1,
which boasts a simplistic structure with individual strong Li···O
bonds, and Li2S4, which incorporates a greater number of sulfur
atoms that provide ample bonding sites, the Li2S2 system man-
ifests a homogeneous interaction region paired with relatively
moderate bond strength. Consequently, this system ensures an
overall favorable interaction due to the presence of a uniform
interaction region and a reasonably strong bond.
Considering the influence of different cations, it is noteworthy
that the [LiG6]þ system exhibits the highest EB, measuring at
606.58 meV. This observation can be attributed to the unique
annular structure of [LiG6]þ, which offers an increased number of
binding sites for O atoms. As a result, the formation of Li····O
bonds with lithium polysulfides becomes more facile, resulting
in higher bond energies. Additionally, the presence of a more sta-
ble cyclic structure within [LiG6]þ augments its propensity for
establishing interactions. While [LiG3]þ forms more Li─O bonds,
their longer lengths and distributed electron density result in
weaker individual bonds. The cyclic [LiG6]þ compensates for
fewer bonds with shorter, denser, and electrostatically reinforced
interactions, achieving superior per-EB.
While [LiG6]þ exhibits the highest individual EB, the system
that demonstrates the greatest overall bond strength is the
[LiG3]þ system, as illustrated in Figure S3, Supporting Information.

robust Li···O covalent bonds present within this system compared

The IGM analysis predominantly relies on δg and its various forms
of isosurfaces to effectively illustrate interactions within chemical
systems. Notably, it is important to highlight that the magnitude

correlation with the strength of the interaction.[53] Consequently,

In Figure 5, we depict these interactions by colorizing strong

present a 2D scatterplot, which illustrates the relationship

the region with negative values of the horizontal coordinate.
These spikes signify the presence of attractive forces within
the system. Conversely, spikes in the region with positive values
of the horizontal coordinate indicate the presence of repulsive
forces due to the site-damping effect in the ring structure.
The weak interactions of Liþ within the cation exhibit a fluc-
tuation pattern consistent with the trend observed in the maxi-
mum positive ESP analysis. Figure 5 and S5 to S8, Supporting
Information, reveal that, with the increasing number of glymes,
the fluctuations exhibit a continuous pattern of alternating
decreases and increases. It is clear that the spatial arrangement
and magnitude of the positive ESP within the cation play a crucial
role in its ability to chelate Liþ.
Interestingly, the interactions between anions and lithium
polysulfides, as well as those between lithium polysulfides and
the lithium metal surface, show minimal variation with the
increasing presence of glymes. This observation underscores
the significant influence of cations in the adsorption process,
highlighting that their role is paramount. In contrast, the interac-
tions between anions and the metal surface generally surpass
those between lithium polysulfides and the surface. These find-
ings emphasize the complex interplay of forces in these systems
and underscore the importance of cation design for optimizing
the performance of lithium–sulfur batteries.
This discrepancy can be attributed to the inherent disparity
in electron-attracting capabilities between the O and S atoms
in lithium polysulfide. Specifically, the O atom exhibits a stronger
tendency to attract electrons than its sulfur counterpart.
Furthermore, the relatively low level of anion–cation interactions
can be explained by the formation of a relatively stable structure
through the chelation of Liþ by the cation itself. This stability
reduces the interaction between the cation and anion. This is sup-
ported by the observation that fewer, lower-energy bonds are
formed between the anion and cation in AIM analyses.
Similarly, we illustrated the weak interactions within each
cation in Figure 7. The analysis indicates that [LiG6]þ continues
to exhibit the strongest weak interactions. By integrating the
number of covalent bonds formed, the respective bond ener-
gies, and the weak interactions, we can categorize the chelating
ability in the following order: [LiG6]þ > [Li(G1)2]þ > [LiG3]þ >
[LiG4]þ > [Li(G2)2]þ.
3.3. Electron Transfer Analysis
When two fragments interact and form a complex, it often leads
to a transfer of charge between the fragments. The investigation
of charge transfer has been a prominent focus in the field of
chemistry. EDD (Δϱ) analysis is a commonly employed qualitative
technique for studying charge transfer. In the case of a complex
composed of fragments A and B, the EDD can be defined in the
following manner
Δϱ ¼ ϱAB  ϱA  ϱB
(4)
where ϱAB, ϱA, and ϱB denote the electron densities of the overall
system subsequent to optimization and the electron densities of
fragments A and B, respectively (where A and B retain the same
coordinates as those in the complex). By graphing the isosurfaces
of the Δϱ function within 3D space and coloring the isosurfaces
differently for positive or negative values, we can observe the
amalgamation or dispersal of electron density amid the frag-
ments during the formation of the complex. This visualization
technique permits us to obtain a clearer depiction of the procliv-
ity of electrons to migrate between complexes. EDD analyses
were carried out for various systems, and the findings are pre-
sented in the figure, with red indicating the region of escalating
electron density and blue indicating the region of diminishing
density.
The solvated cation formed by the coordination of Liþ
with the cation demonstrates remarkable stability, exhibiting
minimal electron transfer interactions with the anion, lithium
polysulfide, and the surface, as depicted in Figure 6 and
Figure S9 to S12, Supporting Information. While this character-
istic is also observed in the system with [LiG4]þ, it is further
enhanced by the cyclic structure of [LiG6]þ. This finding is cor-
roborated by subsequent AIMD analysis, which further validates
the superior performance of [LiG6]þ in mitigating lithium poly-

from the lithium metal surface to the anion undergoes a decline.
This implies that the enhanced chelating capacity of the cation
inhibits the binding of anions to the Li atoms on the lithium
metal surface, consequently reducing the availability of free
Liþ ions for neutralizing the shuttling effect of lithium polysul-
fide intermediates.

substantiated by the IGM analysis performed in this investigation.
The role of solid–electrolyte interface (SEI) films in batteries, uti-
lizing ILs ([N2,2,1(2O1)][TFSI] and [Li(G4)][TFSI]), was investigated by
Nakatani and co-workers.[55] It was observed that [Li(G4)][TFSI]
demonstrates the ability to generate a resilient SEI film on the car-
bon electrode, thereby impeding further degradation of the electro-
lyte. This finding is consistent with the observation that [Li(G4)][TFSI]
maintains a robust independence from the lithium metal surface
and exhibits minimal interaction with the surface, as determined
through IGM and EDD analyses conducted in this study.
4. Conclusion
The present study focuses on examining the adsorption behavior
of ether-containing functionalized ILs, composed of a combination
of bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide [TFSI] anion and cations
[Li(G1)2]þ, [Li(G2)2]þ, [LiG3]þ, [LiG4]þ, and [LiG6]þ, on the lithium
metal surface within the context of lithium–sulfur batteries.
Specifically, the investigation explores the interaction between
these ILs and Li2S1, Li2S2, and Li2S4.
The number of covalent bonds formed between individual
glyme molecules and lithium (Li) atoms increases from two to five
as the cation changes from [Li(G1)2]þ to [LiG4]þ, indicating a sig-
nificant enhancement in chelating capacity. However, despite
[LiG6]þ exhibiting the highest positive ESP, it forms only three
Li─O bonds due to its intact ring structure. This configuration
may also facilitate the formation of high-bond-energy Li─O inter-
actions. Furthermore, [LiG6]þ is characterized by strong internal
weak interactions, which contribute to its stability and overall
bonding characteristics.
The number of sulfur (S) atoms in lithium polysulfide signifi-
cantly affects adsorption energy, with a higher count of S atoms
resulting in increased adsorption energy due to their preference
to bind with lithium (Li) atoms on the lithium metal surface.
However, the relationship between adsorption energy and the
number of glymes is not strictly linear, as the chelating ability
of the cations plays a crucial role in mediating interactions
between lithium polysulfide and the lithium surface. Among var-
ious lithium polysulfide species, Li2S4 stands out for its abundance
of binding sites, facilitating electron transfer within the system.
The Li2S2 species is also critical due to its homogeneous structure
and moderate bond strength, with bonding energy increasing
alongside the number of glymes. Furthermore, electron transfer
dynamics reveal that the chelating ability of cations impacts the
interaction between lithium polysulfides and the lithium metal
surface; specifically, an increase in glyme quantity correlates with
a decrease in electron transfer, highlighting the cation’s role in
modulating these interactions.
In conclusion, both cations and anions are pivotal in the lith-
ium polysulfide system. Their ESPs, chelating abilities, and inter-
actions with lithium polysulfide and lithium metal surfaces
