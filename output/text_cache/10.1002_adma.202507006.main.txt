Synergistic Compound Additives for High-Performance
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Chuang Sun, Tingxuan Tang, Mengting Zheng, Chao Lai,* and Jun Lu*
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, with theoretical energy densities exceeding
2600 Wh kg−1, are poised to revolutionize energy storage. However, their
practical viability hinges on resolving two critical challenges: uncontrolled
lithium dendrite growth at the anode and polysulﬁde shuttling at the sulfur
cathode. Here, a compound additive integrating lithium nitrate (LiNO3),
sodium saccharin (SAC), and octaphenyl polyoxyethylene (OP-10) is proposed
to construct an electrolyte for Li–S battery. With the compound additive
added, the as-prepared electrolyte is capable of evolving a robust solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) with reﬁned morphology while suppressing
polysulﬁde reactivity. The synergistic eﬀects of this additive enable Li|Li
symmetric cells to achieve unprecedented cycling stability (>1400 h at
1 mA cm−2 and 3 mAh cm−2) and Li–S full cells with high sulfur loading
(4.12 mg cm−2) to retain 2.72 mAh cm−2 after 150 cycles. This work
underscores the importance of dual-electrode stabilization in electrolyte
design, oﬀering a scalable strategy for high-energy-density Li–S batteries and
related systems plagued by dendrites and shuttle eﬀects. This study
highlights the eﬀectiveness of synergistic electrolyte engineering in
suppressing lithium dendrites and polysulﬁde shuttling, providing new
insights for the development of high-performance Li–S batteries and other
energy storage systems facing similar challenges.

teries, leveraging sulfur’s high capacity (1672 mAh g−1) and
lithium’s unmatched energy density (3861 mAh g−1), emerge as
a transformative alternative, oﬀering theoretical energy densities

2. Results and Discussion
We ﬁrst applied molecular orbital theory to help understand the
role of each component additive. The Highest Occupied Molec-
ular Orbital (HOMO) and the Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Or-
bital (LUMO) energy levels of various molecules are depicted in
Figure 2a. LiNO3 (−1.575 eV) and SAC (−1.757 eV) exhibit signif-
icantly lower LUMO energies compared to conventional solvent
molecules (DOL: 0.223 eV; DME: 0.368 eV), positioning these ad-
ditives as preferential reductants for SEI ﬁlm formation. Addi-
tionally, SAC displays a higher HOMO energy (−6.711 eV) than
DOL (−7.186 eV) and DME (−7.144 eV), indicating a greater ox-
idation propensity at the cathode interface.[16] This electrochem-
ical behavior allows SAC to contribute to CEI ﬁlm formation,
which could help suppress electrolyte decomposition while si-
multaneously stabilizing the cathode structure through an in-
terfacial passivation mechanism. Notably, OP-10 additive was
found to stay electrochemically stable as it does not have de-
ﬁned HOMO and LUMO energy levels. This non-redox mech-
anism ensures long-term stability and contributes to the overall
enhancement of interfacial properties in the electrolyte system.
As shown in Figure 2b, the incorporation of individual addi-
tives (LiNO3, SAC, or OP-10) eﬀectively enhances the conductiv-
ity of the blank electrolyte. Notably, systems containing compos-
ite additives demonstrate substantial conductivity improvements
compared to their single-additive counterparts, with synergistic
combinations of two or more additives achieving particularly sig-
niﬁcant enhancements in ionic transport. This trend suggests a
cooperative interaction between additives that optimizes charge
carrier mobility within the electrolyte matrix. Figure 2c quan-
titatively evaluates the throwing power of additive molecules,
a key parameter for regulating spatially uniform lithium metal
deposition.[17] LiNO3 (0.270) and SAC (0.333) show moderate
throwing power values, while the addition of OP-10, with a high
throwing power of 0.488, results in the LiNO3/SAC/OP-10 com-
posite system achieving a markedly enhanced value of 0.464. This
indicates that the eﬀects of the three additives are not mutually
exclusive. The observed complementarity suggests distinct yet co-
ordinated mechanisms: LiNO3 and SAC likely modify interfacial
energetics, while the superior throwing power of OP-10 governs
morphological control. Crucially, this multi-additive strategy al-
lows for the simultaneous optimization of SEI robustness and
lithium deposition uniformity, addressing two critical challenges
in lithium metal battery development through targeted interfacial
engineering.

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) is a non-destructive method
for characterizing nanostructures in liquids, and a fundamental
understanding of the nanostructures of liquid electrolytes is
expected to provide evidence for their role in enhancing elec-
trochemical stability.[18] We examined the microscopic structure
of blank, LiNO3/SAC-additive, and LiNO3/SAC/OP-10-additive
electrolyte systems using SAXS. As shown in Figure 2f, the SAXS
curves of the LiNO3/SAC and LiNO3/SAC/OP-10 groups exhibit
a noticeable intensity increase in the q range from 0.6 to 1.3
Å−1. This result demonstrates that the addition of a composite
additive eﬀectively increases the ion cluster, which contributes
to an expanded electrochemical stability window, distinguishing
it from the superconcentrated LiTFSI electrolyte.[19]
In addition, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were
performed to compare the binding energies between lithium ions
and both solvent and additive molecules (Figure S5, Supporting
Information). The results show that the additive molecules ex-
hibit signiﬁcantly stronger binding aﬃnities with lithium ions
than the solvents, suggesting that they preferentially occupy
lithium-ion solvation shells. This behavior reduces parasitic re-
actions induced by solvent molecules during lithium deposition.
The binding energies between diﬀerent components within the
mixed-additive system were also calculated to investigate their
mutual interactions (Figure S6, Supporting Information). No-
tably, combinations involving lithium nitrate exhibit the highest
binding energies, primarily due to lithium-ion sharing. These
ﬁndings highlight that lithium nitrate not only contributes to
the formation of a stable SEI but also serves as a stabilizing
agent for other additive components, further enhancing the over-
all electrolyte stability. Moreover, Raman spectroscopy was con-
ducted to investigate the solvation structure of Li+ ions in elec-
trolyte systems containing diﬀerent additives (Figure S7, Sup-
porting Information). The results indicate that the strong in-
teractions between the additives and Li+ signiﬁcantly modiﬁed
the Li+ solvation environment. Notably, a discernible Raman
shift toward higher wavenumbers was observed for the S–N–
S bending vibration mode of the TFSI−anion, suggesting en-
hanced Li+–TFSI−coordination. This implies that a substantial
portion of solvent molecules were displaced from the primary sol-
vation shell of Li+. Furthermore, the increased intensity of the
free DOL peak in the Raman spectra corroborates this observa-
tion, indicating a reduced participation of DOL in the solvation
sheath.

In situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to thoroughly inves-
tigate the polysulﬁde transformation process during the battery
discharge (Figure 5d–f). A 532 nm laser was directed through the
perforated anode shell to reach the separator. At the beginning of
discharge (E = 2.471 V), the cell with the blank electrolyte shows
characteristic peaks at 200, 409, and 469 cm−1, corresponding to
the formation of Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8, respectively. As discharge
progresses (E = 2.14 V), the signals at 200 and 469 cm−1 persist,
with increasing peak intensities, while the characteristic peak at
409 cm−1 gradually strengthens.[24] These results indicate a sig-
niﬁcant shuttle eﬀect and irreversible loss of sulfur species dur-
ing discharge in the blank electrolyte system. In stark contrast,
within the mixed formulation electrolyte system, only a mini-
mal presence of polysulﬁde-related signals is observed during the
latter half of the discharge process, indicating that the compos-
ite additive electrolyte eﬀectively facilitates the rapid conversion
of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) while suppressing polysulﬁde
shuttling.[25] In the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of the Li–S
cells (Figure 5g), the cathodic peaks at 2.28 and 2.04 V correspond
to the reduction of solid S8 to soluble long-chain LiPSs and fur-
ther reduction to solid Li2S2/Li2S, respectively. The anodic peaks
at 2.34 and 2.40 V correspond to the oxidation of solid Li2S2/Li2S
to LiPSs, which are further oxidized to solid S8. The cell with com-
pound additives exhibits the lowest peak polarization, indicating
accelerated polysulﬁde conversion kinetics and improved sulfur
utilization.
In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was also
conducted to monitor impedance changes in Li–S batteries dur-
ing cycling (Figure 5h). During cycling, LiNO3/SAC/OP-10 ex-
hibits a lower electrolyte internal resistance compared to the
LiNO3/SAC and blank electrolyte groups, likely due to better wet-
tability of the electrode with the LiNO3/SAC/OP-10 electrolyte.
Additionally, LiNO3/SAC/OP-10 and LiNO3/SAC show lower
electrolyte/electrode interface resistance, indicating that the cell
with blank electrolyte experiences a thicker CEI and more severe
destruction/reformation. Due to the solid-liquid-solid transfor-
mation of sulfur species during charging and discharging, the
Rct of LiNO3/SAC and the blank electrolyte groups show a com-
plex variation pattern in subsequent charge and discharge cycles.
Meanwhile, the charge transfer resistance of the LiNO3/SAC/OP-
10 group remains relatively stable and consistently lower
than that of the LiNO3/SAC and blank groups, suggesting
a faster ionic migration rate in the LiNO3/SAC/OP-10-based
cell.
3. Conclusion
In summary, a composite electrolyte formulation containing
LiNO3, SAC, and OP-10 additives has been developed to ad-
dress both polysulﬁde shuttling and dendritic lithium growth in
lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. These three additives play dis-
tinct roles on the lithium metal side: facilitating initial nucle-
ation, forming a protective ﬁlm during the growth stage, and
ensuring long-term stability of the solid electrolyte interphase
(SEI). Additionally, both LiNO3 and OP-10 contribute to sup-
pressing the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect. LiNO3 optimizes the sol-
vation structure of polysulﬁdes, thereby reducing their reactiv-
ity with lithium metal, while OP-10, through its polar functional
groups, slows the dissolution rate of polysulﬁdes by acting as a
shielding barrier. Ultimately, the composite electrolyte eﬀectively
protects the lithium anode from dendrite growth, ensuring that
Li|Li symmetric half-cells operate stably for over 1400 h at a cur-
rent density of 1 mA cm−2 and a ﬁxed areal capacity of 3 mAh
cm−2. Under quasi-practical conditions, the Li–S cell exhibits a
high areal capacity of 2.72 mAh cm−2 after 150 cycles. Given the
beneﬁts of the compound additives method in preventing lithium
dendrite growth and inhibiting the shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs, this
work provides a new direction in electrolyte engineering for high-
performance Li–S batteries.
