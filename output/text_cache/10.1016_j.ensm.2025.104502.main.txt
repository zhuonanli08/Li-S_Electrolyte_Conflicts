A B S T R A C T
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising in realizing high energy density but hindered by limited cycling 
lifespan due to the corrosion of lithium metal anode by soluble lithium polysulfides. Employing weakly-solvating 
electrolyte effectively prolongs the cycling lifespan via suppressing the anode corrosion but inevitably results in 
severe cathode capacity degradation under lean-electrolyte conditions. Herein, the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox 
kinetics is systematically evaluated in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries to guide target kinetic promotion strategy 
design. High activation polarization caused by sluggish interfacial charge transfer kinetics is identified as the key 
limiting factor by polarization decoupling. Smaller Li2S nuclei and thinner Li2S deposition are formed to render 
reduced deposition capacity. To overcome the above challenge, a bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide lithium electrolyte is 
designed to realize a discharge capacity of 984 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C under a lean electrolyte volume of 5 μL mg−1 in 
weakly-solvating Li–S batteries. This work affords rational guidance for accelerating the sulfur redox kinetics 
toward lean-electrolyte and weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries.
1. Introduction
The low-altitude economy constitutes a crucial driving force for 
future economy growth, where unmanned aerial vehicles serve as key 
devices for transportation and logistics [1,2]. To meet the long-range 
operation requirements, the energy density of rechargeable batteries is 
expected to exceed 600 Wh kg−1 [3]. The current commercial 
lithium-ion batteries can hardly achieve an actual energy density 
beyond 400 Wh kg−1 due to the limitations of the lithium ion interca­
lation reaction mechanism [4–7]. To this end, developing advanced 
rechargeable batteries with practical high energy density is highly 
required to meet these requirements [8,9].
Lithium‒sulfur (Li‒S) batteries afford an ultra-high theoretical en­
ergy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, making them one of the most promising 
candidates as next-generation high-energy-density recharge battery 
systems [10–13]. Typical Li‒S batteries rely on solid‒liquid‒solid 
conversion between elemental sulfur, dissolved lithium polysulfides 
(LiPSs), and solid lithium sulfide (Li2S) in ether-based electrolyte 
[14–16]. The soluble LiPSs avoid direct conversion between the elec­
tronic and ionic insulated sulfur and Li2S and facilitate their deposition 
and dissolution by chemical disproportionation and comproportionation 
reactions, thereby contributing to high utilization of the cathode active 
materials and high energy density of Li‒S batteries [17–19]. However, 
the dissolved LiPSs chemically react with lithium metal and inevitably 
corrode the lithium anode continuously, which is widely acknowledged 
to cause the shuttle effect [20–22]. The corrosion of lithium anode by 
the dissolved LiPSs irreversibly consumes the limited active lithium 
reservoir [23], and more importantly, generates low-valence sulfur 
species on surface of lithium anode to induce uneven lithium deposition 
and massive generation of inactive lithium [24–26]. The severe corro­
sion of lithium anode by the dissolved LiPSs results in rapid failure of Li‒ 
S batteries and restricts their practical application.
The dissolved LiPSs are solvated in electrolyte. The solvation struc­
ture of LiPSs greatly influences the reaction kinetics of LiPSs and the Li–S 
battery performances [27–30]. Employing weakly-solvating electrolyte 
is an effective strategy to suppress the shuttle effect and improve the

Li–S battery cycling stability [31–33]. Typically, weakly-solvating 
electrolyte is constructed by introducing low donor number (DN) 
co-solvents. For instance, Kaskel and co-workers constructed a 
weakly-solvating electrolyte by using a 1,1,2,2-tetrafluorethyl-2,2,3, 
3-tetrafluorpropyl ether co-solvent [34]. The cycling lifespan of Li‒S 
pouch cells was prolonged by three times accordingly. Hou et al. further 
studied the solvation structure of LiPSs in weakly-solvating electrolyte 
by partially replacing conventional high-DN 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) solvents with a low-DN di-isopropyl 
sulfide (DIPS) co-solvent [35]. A LiPS encapsulating solvation structure 
was identified via molecular dynamics simulation, mainly constructed 
by a DME-dominated inner solvation shell and a DIPS-dominated outer 
solvation shell. A longer cycling lifespan of 100 cycles was achieved in 
Li‒S pouch cells with the help of this weakly-solvating electrolyte. Up to 
the present, weakly-solvating Li–S batteries have demonstrated promi­
nent advantages in realizing long cycling lifespan under practical 
working conditions.
Generally, the weakly-solvating electrolytes protect the lithium 
anode without harming the sulfur redox kinetics under flooded elec­
trolyte conditions as the LiPSs are fully solvated to guarantee smooth 
sulfur redox kinetics [36,37]. However, massive electrolyte usage 
inevitably reduces the actual energy density of Li‒S batteries [38]. The 
electrolyte usage is usually described by the ratio of electrolyte volume 
to sulfur mass (E/S ratio) [39]. Li‒S batteries can hardly achieve an 
energy density over 400 Wh kg−1 when the E/S ratio is higher than 5 μL 
mg−1 [40]. Obviously, lean-electrolyte conditions are the prerequisite to 
achieve practical high-energy-density Li‒S batteries. Nevertheless, 
lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries suffer from highly sluggish sulfur redox 
kinetics due to high LiPS concentration or even LiPS saturation, and the 
actual performances are greatly degraded regarding the discharge ca­
pacity, cell polarization, and rate responses [41–44]. The above chal­
lenges are even more severe in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries due to 
the lower solvating power of the electrolyte with low-DN co-solvents 
[45], leading to unexpected rapid cell failure due to the dramatic 
degradation of the sulfur redox kinetics [46–48]. To develop Li‒S bat­
teries with both high energy density and long cycling lifespan, in-depth 
kinetics evaluation and targeted kinetic promotion strategies are 
essential for the weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte 
conditions.
Herein, the sulfur redox kinetics under lean-electrolyte conditions is 
systematically evaluated in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries to guide 
targeted kinetic promotion strategy design. Typical weakly-solvating 
electrolyte is constructed by using DIPS as the low-DN co-solvent. Tak­
ing kinetic polarization as the key indicator, a galvanostatic intermittent 
titration technique combined electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(GITT‒EIS) method is employed to decouple the total polarization into 
activation, concentration, and ohmic polarizations under different E/S 
ratios. By excluding the minimal anode polarization, the cathode acti­
vation polarization at the Li2S nucleation stage increases by 640 % as the 
electrolyte usage decreases and occupies 61 % of the total polarization 
under lean-electrolyte conditions. Furthermore, the activation energy of 
the interfacial charge transfer process of Li2S nucleation increases by 29 
% at higher LiPS concentration, together identifying the cathode inter­
facial charge transfer kinetics at the Li2S nucleation stage as the main 
limiting factor. Due to the reduced surface reaction rate at higher sulfur 
concentration, the Li2S nucleation changes from a three-dimensional 
diffusion-controlled hemisphere morphology to a two-dimensional sur­
face reaction-controlled needle morphology. Accordingly, the size of the 
Li2S nuclei decreases from 184 to 63 nm and the thickness of the Li2S 
deposition layer decreases from 262 to 187 nm as the sulfur concen­
tration increases, in correspondence to the degraded deposition capacity 
by 41 %. To accelerate the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox kinetics in 
weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries, a lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide- 
based electrolyte (LiFSI electrolyte) is designed to achieve a high 
discharge capacity of 984 mAh g−1 and 50 stable cycles at a high rate of 
0.2 C under a low E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1. Furthermore, an actual energy 
density of 410 Wh kg−1 is achieved in 5 Ah Li‒S pouch cells with the 
LiFSI electrolyte. This work identifies the surface reaction-controlled 
Li2S deposition kinetics as the main kinetic challenge and highlights 
the LiPS solvation structure regulation strategy to accelerate the sulfur 
redox kinetics in lean-electrolyte weakly-solvating Li–S batteries.
2. Results and discussion
The weakly-solvating electrolyte was constructed by using DIPS as 
the low-DN co-solvent, and the thermodynamics and kinetics of Li‒S 
batteries using the weakly-solvating electrolyte were systematically 
analyzed under lean-electrolyte conditions. Li‒S coin cells with the 
cathode sulfur loading of 4.0 mgS cm−2 and gradually decreased E/S 
ratios of 12, 8, 6, 5, or 4.5 μL mg−1 were assembled. GITT at a current 
density of 0.1 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) was conducted on the coin cells to 
decouple the thermodynamics and kinetics (Figure S1‒S5). Concretely, 
the thermodynamic voltage curves were obtained via connecting the 
final voltage points of each relaxation stage along the degree of 
discharge or charge and the connection of the final voltage points of 
each galvanostatic discharge or charge stage constituted the kinetic 
voltage curves (Fig. 1a). For the discharge process, the thermodynamic 
voltage of the first discharge plateau slightly decreases as the electrolyte 
volume decreases, which is resulted from increased LiPS concentration 
in electrolyte [49]. Besides, the thermodynamic voltage of the second 
discharge plateau is basically overlapped at 2.173 V under different E/S 
ratio conditions. Meantime, the variation of the thermodynamic voltage 
during the charge processes is basically the reverse of the discharge 
process under different E/S ratios. Therefore, the lean-electrolyte con­
ditions have little effect on the thermodynamics of weakly-solvating Li‒ 
S batteries.
The kinetic polarization under different E/S ratios was further 
analyzed by calculating the difference between the thermodynamic 
voltage and the kinetic voltage. When the E/S ratio decreases from 12 to 
6 μL mg−1, the discharge polarizations are basically not changed at 
around 100 mV at the Li2S nucleation stage (Figure S6), suggesting the 
electrolyte amount is enough for smooth cathode reactions within this 
flooded E/S ratio range. However, when the E/S ratio decreases from 6 
to 4.5 μL mg−1, the discharge kinetic polarization rapidly increases from 
96 to 300 mV at the Li2S nucleation stage and the kinetic voltage reaches 
as low as 1.868 V. Comparingly, the kinetic voltage curves during the 
charge process are basically overlapped under different E/S ratios, 
indicating the kinetics of the charge process is barely affected by the 
electrolyte amount variation (Figure S7). Therefore, the sluggish ki­
netics of the discharge process, especially at the Li2S nucleation stage, is 
the main problem of weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean- 
electrolyte conditions.
The kinetics of the sulfur cathode and the lithium anode together 
contribute to the total kinetic polarization of Li‒S batteries. The kinetic 
polarization of lithium anode under lean-electrolyte conditions was 
further estimated to quantify the portion of anode polarization within 
the total kinetic polarization. Li | Li symmetric cells with 0, 1, or 4 M[S] 
LiPS electrolyte were assembled and cycled at a current density of 0.72 
mA cm−2 equaling to the current density of the Li‒S coin cells at 0.1 C 
(Fig. 1b). The bulk resistances of the Li | Li symmetric cells were 
determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) mea­
surements and the anode kinetic polarization under different sulfur 
concentrations was compared after eliminating the ohmic polarization 
(Figure S8‒S10, see more details in Supplementary Information). The 
anode kinetic polarizations under sulfur concentrations of 0, 1, and 4 
M[S] are all smaller than 16 mV, which only occupies 5 % of the total 
kinetic polarization maximally (Fig. 1c). Therefore, the high kinetic 
polarization of weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte 
conditions is resulted from the sluggish cathode kinetics.

electrolyte weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries, the cathode kinetic polari­
zation was further decoupled. A GITT‒EIS method is used to

quantitatively decouple the total polarization into activation (ηac), 
concentration (ηcon), and ohmic polarization (ηohm) according to their 
dependence on the relaxation time (Figure S11‒S15, see more details in 
Supplementary Information) [50]. The ηac rapidly increases to the peak 
value at the Li2S nucleation stage during discharge, and then decreases 
by the end of discharge (Figure S16). The ηcon gradually increases at the 
beginning of discharge and becomes stable after Li2S nucleation 
(Figure S17). When the discharge process reaches 90 % DOD, the ηcon 
rapidly increases to above 300 mV and causes the termination of 
discharge. Besides, the ηohm also reaches its peak value at the Li2S 
nucleation stage and then decreases eventually (Figure S18). Obviously, 
the Li2S nucleation stage is the most kinetically sluggish stage as all the 
three kinetic polarizations reach a high value at this stage (Fig. 2a). Once 
the Li2S nucleation stage is passed, the polarizations of Li‒S batteries 
gradually decrease.
The three polarizations at the Li2S nucleation stage are quantitatively 
compared under different E/S ratios to locate the key kinetic limiting 
factor. The ηac is 25 mV at the Li2S nucleation stage at the E/S ratio of 12 
μL mg−1, and the ηcon and ηohm are 60 and 7 mV under this electrolyte 
condition, respectively (Fig. 2b). The ηac reaches 44 mV when the E/S 
ratio decreases to 6 μL mg−1. The low ηac indicates the cathode con­
version kinetics is smooth under flooded electrolyte conditions. How­
ever, the ηac rapidly increases to 185 mV when the E/S ratio further 
decreases to 4.5 μL mg−1, while the ηcon and ηohm are only 78 and 38 mV, 
respectively. Furthermore, the polarization ratios of ηac to ηcon (ηac/ηcon) 
and ηac to ηohm (ηac/ηohm) at different E/S ratios are calculated to 
demonstrate the rapid increase of ηac over ηcon and ηohm. The ηac/ηcon is 
0.7 at the E/S ratio of 6 μL mg−1 and reaches 2.4 when the E/S ratio 
further decreases to 4.5 μL mg−1. Similarly, the ηac/ηohm is 2.2 at the E/S 
ratio of 6 μL mg−1 and reaches 4.9 at the E/S ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1. 
Obviously, the ηac is the key kinetic limiting factor of weakly-solvating 
Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte working conditions.

The kinetic polarization at the sulfur dissolution (10 % DOD) and the 
Li2S deposition stages (70 % DOD) were also analyzed. At the sulfur 
dissolution stage, the ηac increases from 17 to 53 mV when the E/S ratio 
decreases from 6 to 4.5 μL mg−1 and is 13 and 33 mV higher than the ηcon 
and ηohm at the E/S ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1, respectively (Figure S19). The 
ηac is also the dominant kinetic polarization component. At the Li2S 
growth stage, the three polarizations are generally stable at different E/S 
ratios (Figure S20), indicating the overall kinetics at the Li2S growth 
stage is not violently affected by the electrolyte amount.
To further confirm the sluggish kinetics of the cathode interfacial 
charge transfer process under lean-electrolyte working conditions, the 
activation energy at the Li2S nucleation stage under different sulfur 
concentrations was measured according to the Arrhenius relationship. 
Three-electrode Li | Li2S8 cells were assembled with 2, 4, or 6 M[S] Li2S8 
electrolyte, where a lithium coated copper wire served as the reference 
electrode. The sulfur mass in the Li | Li2S8 cells with different sulfur 
concentration electrolytes was the same by controlling the electrolyte 
addition amount. The Li | Li2S8 cells were discharged to the Li2S 
nucleation stage and the EIS spectra of the cells in a wide temperature 
range were recorded. Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was 
then conducted to decouple the interfacial charge transfer process from 
the overall cathodic electrochemical processes (Figure S21‒S23) 
[51–53]. The cathode interfacial charge transfer activation energy was 
obtained through the Arrhenius plots (Fig. 2c). The activation energy 
reaches 193 kJ mol−1 in 6 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte, which is 43 kJ mol−1 
higher than that in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte. The increased activation 
energy as the sulfur concentration increases demonstrates an exponen­
tial decline of the reaction rate coefficient under lean-electrolyte con­
ditions according to the Arrhenius equation. In summary, ηac is the 
dominant kinetic polarization, and the interfacial charge transfer ki­
netics is confirmed as the critical limiting factor of weakly-solvating Li‒ 
S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
To further correlate the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox kinetics with 
the cathode active material utilization, the Li2S nucleation and deposi­
tion behaviors at different sulfur concentrations were observed. Li | 
Li2S8 cells with the sulfur concentration of 2, 4, or 6 M[S] Li2S8 were 
disassembled at the Li2S nucleation stage. The Li2S nucleation mor­
phologies were observed via scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
Fig. 3a). The deposited Li2S is hemispherical at the sulfur concentration 
of 2 M[S], while the morphology changes to needle-like when the sulfur 
concentration increases to 4 and 6 M[S]. The nucleus size of Li2S was 
further quantitatively analyzed (Figure S24‒S26). The average Li2S 
nucleus size in 6 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte is 63 nm and is 66 % smaller than 
that in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Fig. 3b). The lower Li2S nucleation re­
action rate results in the Li2S nucleus changes from a diffusion- 
controlled three-dimensional hemisphere-like morphology to a surface 
reaction-controlled 
two-dimensional 
needle-like 
morphology. 
Controlled by the surface reaction, only a little amount of LiPS is able to 
be electrochemically reduced and form Li2S nuclei with smaller size on 
the conductive surface, which results in larger kinetic polarization to 
stabilize the small Li2S nuclei.
The Li2S deposition morphologies at the end of discharge were 
further studied. The Li2S deposition keeps the hemisphere-like 
morphology in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Figure S27). Surprisingly, a 
hemisphere-like morphology composited by the needle-like Li2S is 
eventually formed in 4 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Figure S28). When the 
sulfur concentration increases to 6 M[S], the needle-like Li2S constitutes 
the bulk with irregular shape (Figure S29). The deposition amount of 
Li2S is evaluated by comparing the Li2S deposition thickness 
(Figure S30). The deposition thickness of Li2S at the sulfur concentration 
of 2 M[S] is 262 nm and decreases to 226 and 187 nm at the sulfur 
concentration of 4 and 6 M[S], respectively (Fig. 3c and Figure S31‒ 
S32). The thinner Li2S deposition layer at higher sulfur concentration is 
resulted from higher degree of surface reaction-controlled Li2S deposi­
tion and corresponding two-dimensional Li2S deposition. Thinner 
deposition thickness brings more rapid conductive surface coverage by 
the insulating Li2S deposits, thereby blocking the subsequent reactions. 
To validate the above argument, Li | Li2S8 cells with the same sulfur 
mass but different sulfur concentration of 2, 4, or 6 M[S] were poten­
tiostatically discharged at 2.10 V (Figure S33‒S35). The deposition 
capacity is 124 mAh g−1 when the sulfur concentration is 2 M[S], and 
decreases to 97 and 73 mAh g−1 at the sulfur concentration of 4 and 6

Li salt anions are able to coordinate with LiPSs and are expected to 
accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics in weakly-solvating electrolyte 
under lean-electrolyte conditions [54–56]. A LiFSI electrolyte was pre­
pared and applied in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under 
lean-electrolyte working conditions. The rate performances of Li‒S 
batteries with the LiFSI electrolyte or the conventional lithium bis 
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide-based electrolyte (LiTFSI electrolyte) 
were firstly evaluated in Li‒S coin cells with the cathode sulfur loading 
of 4.0 mgS cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1. The Li‒S cells with the 
LiFSI electrolyte cycle stably at 0.3 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) and deliver a 
high discharge capacity of 798 mAh g−1 (Fig. 4a, S36, and S37). Com­
paringly, the Li‒S cells with the LiTFSI electrolyte can only work at 0.1 C 
so that the maximum cyclable current density is improved by twice. 
Besides, the Li‒S cells deliver a high discharge capacity of 980 mAh g−1 
at the current density of 0.1 C in the LiFSI electrolyte, which is 180 mAh 
g−1 higher than that in the LiTFSI electrolyte. The medium voltage of the 
Li‒S cells was further analyzed to demonstrate the kinetic advantages of 
the LiFSI electrolyte (Figure S38). At high current densities of 0.2 and 
0.3 C, the medium voltages in the LiFSI electrolyte are 1.90 and 1.82 V, 
while the Li‒S cells fail to overcome the huge polarization at the Li2S 
nucleation stage in the LiTFSI electrolyte. Therefore, the LiFSI electro­
lyte successfully accelerates the interfacial charge transfer kinetics of 
weakly-solvating Li–S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
Furthermore, the long-cycling capability of the LiFSI electrolyte was 
evaluated. Li‒S coin cells with the LiFSI electrolyte at the E/S ratio of 
5.0 μL mg−1 were assembled and cycled at the current density of 0.2 C 
(Figure S39). The LiTFSI electrolyte can hardly guarantee smooth sulfur 
redox kinetics at high current density, and the Li‒S cells with the LiTFSI 
electrolyte fail immediately at 0.2 C. Comparingly, the Li‒S cells with 
the LiFSI electrolyte achieve a high discharge capacity of 984 mAh g−1 at 
0.2 C and cycle stably for 50 times before the capacity reaches 60 % of 
the initial capacity. The battery performances were further conducted 
using high sulfur loading cathodes of 7.0 mgS cm−2. A maximum cycling 
rate of 0.25 C is achieved in the LiFSI electrolyte while the LiTFSI 
electrolyte only supports a maximum cycling rate of 0.15 C. When the 
cycling rate changes back to 0.1 C, 15 stable cycles are achieved with the 
help of the LiFSI electrolyte, while the weakly-solvating Li‒S cells with 
the LiTFSI electrolyte fail rapidly after only one cycle at 0.1 C (Fig. 4b).
To demonstrate the kinetic promotion advantage of LiFSI, perfor­
mances of Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte were further eval­
uated. Rate performances were first evaluated in 3 Ah Li‒S pouch cells 
(Figure S40). The Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte delivers high 
discharge capacities of 1219, 1139, 1099, 1080, and 939 mAh g−1 at 
0.025, 0.05, 0.08, 0.1, and 0.15 C, respectively (Fig. 4c). In comparison, 
the Li‒S pouch cell with the LiTFSI electrolyte fails rapidly at 0.05 C and 
the discharge capacity is less than 200 mAh g−1 at higher rates due to the 
sluggish kinetics (Figure S41). 5 Ah Li‒S pouch cells with the LiFSI 
electrolyte were assembled and a high discharge capacity of 1150 mAh 
g−1 is delivered, which is 855 mAh g−1 higher than that in the LiTFSI 
electrolyte (Figure S42). The actual energy density of the Li‒S pouch cell 
is 410 Wh kg−1 based on the total mass (Table S1 and S2). Furthermore, 
an 8 Ah Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte was assembled and a 
high discharge capacity of 1260 mAh g−1 is achieved at the 1st cycle, 
contributing to an actual energy density of 474 Wh kg−1 (Fig. 4d). The 
high-energy-density pouch cell maintains 20 cycles and delivers an 
actual energy density of 412 Wh kg−1, during which the Coulombic ef­
ficiency is stable around 99 % (Fig. 4e and S43). The performance stands 
out as one of the best pouch cells among the recent reports (Fig. 4f and 
Table S3). Therefore, Li‒S batteries with both high energy density and 
long cycling lifespan can be achieved by the LiFSI electrolyte that 
effectively accelerates the sulfur redox kinetics in weakly-solvating Li–S 
batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
3. Conclusion
In conclusion, systematic cathode kinetic analysis is conducted on 
weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions. The 
sluggish interfacial charge transfer kinetics is identified as the key ki­
netic limiting factor by quantitative polarization decoupling and acti­
vation energy measurements. The Li2S deposition process changes from 
diffusion-controlled to surface reaction-controlled as the sulfur con­
centration increases, leading to smaller Li2S nucleation size and thinner 
Li2S deposition thickness corresponding to rapid coverage of the 
conductive surface and consequent low deposition capacity. A LiFSI 
electrolyte is proposed to accelerate the interfacial charge transfer ki­
netics, and weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries achieve a high discharge
