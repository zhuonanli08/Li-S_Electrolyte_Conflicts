Lithium sulfur (Li–S) battery is an appealing energy storage system due to its high theoretical speciﬁc capacity and speciﬁc energy.
However, two main challenges, lithium polysulﬁdes (LPS) dissolution from the sulfur cathode and the unstable Li anode, impede
the practical application of Li–S battery. Developing advanced electrolyte is an effective strategy to tune the stability of both sulfur
cathode and Li anode. Here, a concentrated imidazolium-based ionic liquids (IL) electrolyte was explored for Li–S battery with the
synergistic advantages of both IL and high concentrated electrolyte. A systematic study was conducted to reveal the effect of salt
concentration on the properties of the IL electrolyte and the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery. It is found that an
effective suppression of LPS dissolution and improved stability of Li anode can be obtained with increase of salt concentration. As
a result, a good cycling stability of the Li–S battery is achieved in the concentrated IL electrolyte, with high capacity retention of
92% after 100 cycles at current density of 0.1 C. The concentrated electrolyte based on IL solvent exhibits good compatibility with
both cathode and anode, offering a new opportunity for designing electrolyte to achieve stable electrochemical system.

Lithium sulfur (Li–S) battery, using sulfur as the cathode and Li
metal as the anode, is regarded as one of the most promising
candidates for next-generation energy storage system due to its high
theoretical speciﬁc capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and high speciﬁc
energy (2600 Wh kg−1).1,2 In addition, the elemental sulfur is
naturally
abundant,
environmentally
friendly
and
low
cost.
Nevertheless, Li–S battery suffers from practical challenges of low
active material utilization, poor cycle stability, low Coulombic
efﬁciency and safety hazards. These issues are mainly resulted
from the incompatibility of electrolyte with both sulfur cathode and
Li anode.3,4
On the side of sulfur cathode, the serious dissolution and shuttle
of lithium polysulﬁdes (LPS, Li2Sx, 2 < x ⩽8) is considered as the
primary problem. The LPS are generated as the reductive inter-
mediates from the elemental sulfur that can be easily dissolved into
organic electrolyte before forming into the ﬁnal reductive products
of Li2S/Li2S2, leading to the massive loss of active material and fast
capacity fading.5 Besides, such dissolution phenomenon can even
spontaneously occur during the static storage period, causing the
open circuit voltage (OCV) fall and energy loss, which is so-called
self-discharging behavior.6,7 In addition, the dissolved LPS can cross
over the separator and migrate to the Li anode, where it reacts with
Li to trigger shuttle effect, leading to poor Coulombic efﬁciency,
irreversible capacity loss and Li anode corrosion.8 On the side of Li
anode, the main problem is the instability of solid electrolyte
interface (SEI), which is generated by the spontaneous reaction of
Li anode with electrolyte. Randomly formed SEI is usually not
strong enough to bear the huge volume expansion during Li plating/
stripping processes, leading to repetitive fracture of SEI and
continuous consumption of electrolyte, and even Li dendrite
formation.9 Alongside, aforementioned LPS dissolution aggravates
the corrosion of Li surface.
Various strategies have been worked out to improve the
performances of Li–S cells, such as optimizing the electrolyte,10
fabricating sulfur cathodes11 and protecting Li anode.12 Among
them, increasing the salt concentration to fabricate the concentrated
electrolyte has been receiving intense attention due to its unique
solvation structure to realize attractive functions, especially for
suppressing LPS dissolution and stabilizing Li anode.13 For ex-
ample, Chen’s group10 found that an improved capacity retention
and Coulombic efﬁciency nearing 100% were achieved by simply
increasing the salt concentration in typical electrolyte system
(LiTFSI
in
1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)
and
1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME)), which was disclosed by the effective suppression of both
LPS dissolution and Li dendrite growth. The function of protecting
Li has been largely demonstrated, which is revealed by the uniform
Li deposition and dissolution in the concentrated electrolyte.14,15
Despite the attractive advantages of the concentrated electrolyte,
several issues are still needed to be improved with regard to the
solvents. Traditional organic solvents, such as ethers and sulfones,
are popularly adopted to design the electrolyte, posing the threat of
LPS dissolution due to their intrinsic high afﬁnity towards LPS.16
Besides, the hidden safety hazards still exist as most of these organic
solvents are volatile and ﬂammable. Therefore, there is strong need
to explore alternatives to replace the traditional organic solvents.
In this regard, ionic liquids (IL) might be promising alternatives
due to their unique physicochemical properties. Generally, IL show
week interaction to LPS due to their weak Lewis acidic cations and
Lewis basic anions, reducing the risk of LPS dissolution.17 Besides,
IL are featured with the properties of low volatility, non-ﬂamm-
ability, and large electrochemical stability, providing improved
safety.18 Nevertheless, it is doubtful to combine the advantages of
concentrated electrolyte and IL for Li–S battery. The main concern
is the high viscosity of general IL, resulting in slow ionic
conductivity. Increasing salt concentration usually results in higher
viscosity,19 which might make this problem much worse. Hence, low
concentration (typically 0.5 ∼1 M) or mixture with other solvents
are commonly employed in IL-based electrolyte. Though there are
several works employing IL electrolyte with relatively high salt
concentration,20,21 their research for Li–S battery is very limited. To
explore the feasibility of using concentrated IL electrolyte for Li–S
battery, a relatively high salt concentration is urgently needed.
Further considering their feature of high viscosity, a study of the
physicochemical properties of the concentrated IL electrolyte may
provide better understanding for its inﬂuence on the electrochemical
performances of Li–S battery.

Bearing these in mind, we try to prepare concentrated electrolyte
by using IL as the solvent and explore its inﬂuence on the
electrochemical performances of Li–S battery. By using 1-Allyl-3-
methylimidazolium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(AMImTFSI) as the solvent and lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesul-
fonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the salt, a concentrated IL electrolyte with
relatively high salt concentration of 3 M was achieved. The proper-
ties of the electrolyte containing different salt concentration were
measured for better understanding its inﬂuence on the electroche-
mical performances of Li–S battery. To evaluate its compatibility
with both electrodes, the effects of the IL electrolyte on both sulfur
cathode and Li anode were systematically studied in terms of the
electrode morphologies, chemical components, and interfacial prop-
erties. Our study offers a new alternative way to design electrolyte
for Li–S batteries with stable cathode and anode.

Cell
fabrication and electrochemical measurements.—The
slurry for sulfur electrode was prepared by mixing S/KB, AB and
CMC/SBR (1:1 wt/wt) with a mass ratio of 70:20:10 in deionized
water as solvent using planetary centrifugal mixer (Kakuhunter SK-
350TⅡ). The slurry was coated on aluminum foil (15 μm thick) using
doctor blade. After drying at 60 °C in vacuum oven overnight, the
electrodes were punched into circular disks with 15 mm diameter.
The obtained sulfur mass loading in this work was ∼1 mg cm−2. The
fabricated S/KB electrode was used as cathode. Circular disk of Li
foil with 15 mm diameter was used as both reference and counter
electrode. Using the as prepared electrolyte, the electrochemical
performance was evaluated in CR2025-type coin cell conﬁguration.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed with electrochemical
workstation (Bio-Logic VSP) with a scanning rate of 0.05 mV s−1
in the voltage range of 1.6∼2.8 V vs Li/Li+. The cycling stability
and discharge/charge plots were evaluated using galvanostatic
charge discharge technique at the current density of 0.05 C (1 C =
1.672 mA g−1) within the potential range of 1.6∼2.8 V vs Li/Li+.

The electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.—To remove
the ambiguity regarding performance contribution from other
components of the battery, conventional cathode material, sulfur/
Ketjen Black (S/KB) composite containing sulfur weight amount of
60% without any modiﬁcation (Fig. S1 (available online at stacks.
iop.org/JES/169/050515/mmedia)), and the electrolyte with LiTFSI
as the salt and AMImTFSI as the solvent were used without any
additives. In such simple system, the sole effect of the electrolyte on
the electrochemical performances can directly be observed.
Considering the problem of self-discharge induced by LPS
dissolution, the changes in the OCV of Li–S battery were monitored
after the cell fabrication without applying current. As shown in
Fig. 2a, the OCV for Li–S cell of 1 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI
gradually decreases from initial 2.88 V to 2.67 V, with potential
retention of 92.7% after 30 d. In contrast, 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI
as electrolyte holds higher potential retention of 96.8% after 30 d.
From the comparison of speciﬁc values in Table SII, the change of
OCV clearly decreased with increased LiTFSI concentration, in-
dicating that increasing salt concentration in IL electrolyte can help
suppress self-discharge effect, which is consistent with the results of
viscosity and LPS dissolution measurements. Figure 2b compares
the cycling performance of Li–S battery in the fabricated IL
electrolyte at the current density of 0.05 C (1 C = 1.672 mA g−1)
with potential window of 1.6 ∼2.8 V. For 1 M LiTFSI in
AMImTFSI, the capacity shows the lowest value with a fast fading
and ﬂuctuant Coulombic efﬁciency. Improved capacity and cycling
stability can be seen when increasing salt concentration. The
electrolyte of 2 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI exhibits the highest initial
capacity, while a fast capacity fading is still observed. Though a
moderate initial capacity of 1071 mAh g−1 is obtained in 3 M
LiTFSI in AMImTFSI as electrolyte, a good cyclability with stable
capacity of ∼950 mAh g−1 can be achieved during the 50 cycles.
The cycling performance of 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI as electro-
lyte was further assessed at higher current density of 0.1 C in Fig. 2c.
Still, a very good cycling stability can be obtained, with capacity
retention of 92% after 100 cycles. From the results of OCV,
viscosity and LPS dissolution experiment, the enhanced cycling
stability in 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI can be attributed to the
effective suppression of LPS. The moderate value of initial capacity
might be caused by the increased viscosity and decreased ionic
conductivity, reducing the reactive kinetics. This speculation can be
further supported from the corresponding discharge/charge plots in
Figs. 2d–2f. For 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, the largest voltage
hysteresis of 0.51 V is found, indicating the large polarization and
reduced redox kinetics. While, a highly overlapping discharge/
charge curves is displayed, reﬂective of the stable electrochemical
reaction.
From the above results, it can be concluded that increasing salt
concentration likes a double-edged sword for the electrochemical
performances of Li–S battery. One the one hand, decreased self-
discharge, improved cycling stability and Coulombic efﬁciency can
be achieved, attributing to the effective suppression of LPS dissolu-
tion in the concentrated IL electrolyte. One the other hand, decreased
initial capacity is obtained as the increased polarization. It should
make a balance between the cyclability and capacity when
increasing the salt concentration.

the cathode, XPS measurements were conducted for the electrodes

corresponding atomic concentration ratio of C–F and Li–F. Careful

deconvolution of C 1s XPS spectra in Fig. 4b. In the lower binding
energy religion of C 1s, four peaks of 284.5 eV (Caliphalic, C–C/
C–H), 285.5 eV (Chetero, C6/C7), 287.0 eV (Cinter,C4/C5), and

observed,35 implying that AMIm cation also participates in the
surface ﬁlm formation. With increasing the LiTFSI concentration,
two peaks located at 290.5 eV and 293.4 eV become stronger, which
can be ascribed to C–SO2 and C–F3, respectively.36 This indicates
that the TFSI anion decomposition becomes easier with increasing
salt concentration, which is well agreed with the DFT calculations
suggesting presence of higher EHOMO in the higher concentration
electrolyte. The TFSI anion decomposition can also be supported by
the F 1s and Li 1s XPS spectra. From the Li 1s XPS spectra in
Fig. 4d, the peaks associated the inorganic components of LiF,
LixSOy, Li3N and Li2O can be detected, which can be ascribed to the
decomposed products from TFSI anion.37 From the F 1s XPS spectra
in Fig. 4c, two clear peaks associated with C–F (C–Fx) and Li–F
(LiF) bond are exhibited. In addition, the percentage of Li–F shows
an increasing tendency with increased LiTFSI concentration in
Fig. 4f, which is believed to be good for building a stable SEI due
to its high electrochemical stability to suppress the side reaction at
the electrode/electrolyte interface.38 The aforementioned reactions

spectra, in which the peak at 400.0 eV corresponding to TFSI anion

cation (denoted as Ncation) can be clearly detected.39 On the basis of
literatures8 and results of XPS spectra, the cathode surface ﬁlm
formation mechanism in the concentrated IL electrolyte can be
proposed in the following manner. Firstly, the TFSI anion is
decomposed by breaking of C–S or S–N bond to produce molecular
groups like R–SO2–R, –CFx, and further decomposed to small
fragments, like LiF, LixSOy, and Li–N. Furthermore, the decom-
position of TFSI anion would induce the AMIm cation polymeriza-
tion on the cathode based on the following reaction.40
From the above results, it can be concluded that the AMImTFSI-
based electrolyte with concentrated LiTFSI is helpful to decrease the
polysulﬁde dissolution through two effect. One is the formation of
surface ﬁlm on the sulfur cathode, which is generated by the TFSI
anion decomposition and AMIm cation polymerization in the
concentrated AMImTFSI-based electrolyte. The formed surface
ﬁlm can act as a protective ﬁlm to suppress the LPS diffusion into
the electrolyte. Another is the increased viscosity and ionic effect
make it difﬁcult for LPS to dissolve and diffuse in the concentrated
IL electrolyte. As a result, improved cycling stability can be
achieved in the concentrated AMImTFSI-based IL electrolyte.

visibly mossy Li is found, which could be due to the inhomogeneous
Li plating/striping processes. With the increase of LiTFSI concen-
tration, the surface morphologies of Li anode become smoother and
