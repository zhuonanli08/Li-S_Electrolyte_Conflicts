ABSTRACT: Reversible lithium-ion (de)intercalation in the carbon-based anodes
using ethylene carbonate (EC) based electrolytes has enabled the commercialization of
lithium-ion batteries, allowing them to dominate the energy storage markets for hand-
held electronic devices and electric vehicles. However, this issue always fails in
propylene carbonate (PC) based electrolytes due to the cointercalation of Li+-PC.
Herein, we report that a reversible Li+ (de)intercalation could be achieved by tuning the
solvent−solvent interaction in a PC-based electrolyte containing a fluoroether. We
study the existence of such previously unknown interactions mainly by nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, while the analysis reveals positive effects on the
solvation structure and desolvation process. We have found that the fluoroether
solvents interact with PC via their δ−F and δ+H atoms, respectively, leading to a
reduced Li+-PC solvent interaction and effective Li+ desolvation followed by a successful
Li+ intercalation at the graphite anodes. We also propose an interfacial model to
interpret the varied electrolyte stability by the differences in the kinetic and
thermodynamic properties of the Li+-solvent and Li+-solvent-anion complexes. Compared to the conventional strategies of
tuning electrolyte concentration and/or adding additives, our discovery provides an opportunity to enhance the compatibility
of PC-based electrolytes with the graphite anodes, which will enable the design of high-energy density batteries (e.g., Li-S
battery) with better environmental adaptabilities.
KEYWORDS: electrolyte, solvation structure, solvent−solvent interaction, ion intercalation chemistry, lithium-ion batteries,
lithium−sulfur batteries, graphite anode

on intercalation chemistry is an indispensable subject in
electrochemical energy storage systems, as the trans-
portation and storage of cations or anions in the host
materials take place via an intercalation process.1−3 For
example, the reversible Li+ (de)intercalation processes in the
cathodes (e.g., LiCoO2,4 LiFePO4,5 LiNiMnO4,6 etc.) and
carbon-based anodes (e.g., graphite7) in lithium-ion batteries
(LIBs) is the critical prerequisite that accounts for the
technology readiness for practical applications.8,9 In particular,
the discovery of reversible Li+ (de)intercalation within the
graphite anode upon the use of ethylene carbonate (EC) based
electrolytes is one of the greatest breakthroughs in LIB
development, which has enabled the commercialization of LIBs
and then stimulated the revolution of portable electronics and
electric vehicles.10 The advantages of EC-based electrolytes are
mainly ascribed to the formation of a robust solid electrolyte
interphase layer (SEI) through the reduction of EC solvent on
the graphite anode.11 The SEI is commonly believed to
effectively prevent the Li+-solvent cointercalation within the
graphite anode and mitigate further electrolyte decomposition.
On the other hand, the SEI protects the graphite anode and
enables reversible Li+ migration and storage in the layered
graphite structure.12,13 In contrast, Li+-solvent cointercalation,
graphite exfoliation, and/or severe electrolyte decomposition
occur upon the use of PC-based electrolytes due to the failure
to form a robust SEI.14−16 This issue inhibits the utilization of
PC-based electrolytes in LIBs, although the electrolytes offer a
wide liquid range (−48−242 °C vs 36−248 °C for EC) and an

Many strategies have been extensively exploited to enhance
the SEI formed by PC-based electrolytes, including engineer-
ing the graphite anode (e.g., coating with polymers,20,21
hydrothermal modification,22 chemical surface treatments,23
doping24), introducing a cosolvent (e.g., ethylene carbonate
(EC),16 diethyl carbonate (DEC),25,26 cyclic carbonate
derivatives,27 etc.), adding film-forming additives28−33 (e.g.,
vinylene carbonate (VC),29 vinyl ethylene carbonate (VEC),31
vinyl ethylene sulfite (VES),32 prop-1-ene-1,3-sultone
(PES)33), and increasing the electrolyte concentration34−38
(e.g., 3.27 mol kg−1 LiN(SO2C2F5)2/PC,34 3.5 M LPF6/PC36).
However, the irreversible Li+ (de)intercalation issue remains
problematic, leading to a short lifetime of the batteries. To
achieve a reasonable battery performance, the use of PC in the
electrolytes should be strictly controlled to ensure the
reversible Li+ (de)intercalation, rendering relatively narrow
temperature and potential windows of the electrolytes
compared to that of PC as the primary electrolyte solvent.
Recently, an emerging paradigm has been discovered to
effectively design battery electrolytes by engineering the
electrolyte solvation structure. In particular, instead of the
SEI, the Li+-solvent interaction is found to determine the
performance of graphite anodes.39−41 By performing electro-
lyte-exchange experiments, it was discovered that the good
SEI-coated graphite (i.e., graphite@SEI) formed in the EC-
based electrolyte could not inhibit the Li+-PC cointercalation
once the electrolyte was replaced with the PC-based
electrolyte.39 Further, additives are found to change the Li+
solvation structure and weaken the Li+-solvent interaction,
resulting in a reversible Li+ (de)intercalation in the graph-
ite.42,43 This viewpoint, which shifts the longstanding emphasis
from the SEI to solvation structures, has enhanced our
understanding of how the electrolyte stabilizes the electrodes
and then led to further exploitation of electrolyte materi-
als.44−47 However, to date, the solvent−solvent interactions in
the solvation structure remain unexplored, leading to an
incomplete understanding of the electrolyte microstructure
and its relationships with electrode performances.
Herein, we discover that a reversible Li+ (de)intercalation
could be achieved by introducing a fluorinated ether diluent of
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE)
into a PC-based electrolyte. In particular, we detected the
existence of a solvent−solvent interaction between the
fluoroether and PC by employing
1H−19F heteronuclear
Overhauser effect spectroscopy (HOESY). The fluoroether
interacts with the PC solvent via electronegative fluorine (δ−F)
and electropositive hydrogen (δ+H) interactions in the
electrolyte, which significantly weakens the Li+-PC solvent
interaction that can enable an easier Li+ desolvation and
reversible (de)intercalation. This study presents an insight into
improving the compatibility of PC-based electrolytes with the
intercalation electrodes, which creates an opportunity to design
many more advanced electrolytes for metal-ion batteries.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Features of the Newly Designed Electrolytes. Progress
in the development of PC-based electrolytes and proposed
strategies to address the compatibility issue of the electrolytes
with graphite anodes are summarized in Figure 1. Prior to
1993, the poor SEI microstructure formed on the anode was
commonly believed to be the main reason for the failure of Li+
(de)intercalation. Then, the strategies of adding film-forming
additives (e.g., vinylene carbonate (VC),29 vinyl ethylene
sulfite (VES),32 prop-1-ene-1,3-sultone (PES)33) and/or
increasing the electrolyte concentration (e.g., 3.27 mol kg−1
LiN(SO2C2F5)2/PC,34 3.5 M LiPF6/PC36) to form a better
SEI were developed in 1993 and 2008, respectively, by which
the reversibility of Li+ (de)intercalation could be enhanced.
Later in 2018, the Li+ solvation structure, particularly the Li+-
solvent interaction, was demonstrated to be the dominant
factor in determining the graphite performance,39 which has
been an interesting viewpoint besides the commonly known
SEI theory.39,42 Herein, we find that the solvent−solvent
interaction in the Li+ solvation structure also significantly
influences the graphite performance; particularly, we can detect
its existence by 2D-NMR analysis and show its positive effects
on the graphite performance.
We discovered the solvent−solvent interaction when
designing a nonflammable electrolyte of 1.0 M LiFSI in PC/
HFE (1/2, vol) in which HFE was added to the PC-based
electrolyte as the flammable retardant and diluent. First, the
self-extinguishing time (SET) of the newly designed electrolyte
is 0 s g−1 (Figure 2a), which is much shorter than 112.1 s g−1
of the 3.0 M LiFSI in PC electrolyte (Figure 2b),
demonstrating the nonflammable feature of the electrolyte.
More importantly, we find that the PC-based electrolyte
becomes compatible with the graphite anode when HFE is
added, particularly when the volumetric ratio of HFE/PC
reaches 2/1. Then, a typical charge−discharge curve that
corresponds to the Li+ (de)intercalation is observed, where the
initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) is as high as 93.4%. Herein,
the concentration of 1.0 M LiFSI is sufficient to make the PC/
HFE-based electrolyte compatible with the graphite electrode
(i.e., to achieve a reversible Li+ (de)intercalation). This
concentration is always used and cost-effective in commercial
LIBs,48 whereas the cycling stability of the graphite electrode
could not be well maintained at lower concentrations (e.g.,
0.75 M) (Figure S1). In contrast, in the absence of HFE
solvent, even by increasing the concentration of LiFSI to 3.0 M
in the PC-based electrolyte, Li+-PC cointercalation, graphite
exfoliation, and electrolyte decomposition remain obvious (i.e.,

and shows a reversible oxidation peak at 0.3 V in cyclic

polypropylene (PP) separator than that without HFE (inset

The reversible Li+ (de)intercalation upon the (dis)charge
processes was further monitored by in situ X-ray diffraction
(XRD). In the 1.0 M LiFSI in the PC/HFE electrolyte, the
characteristic (002) peak of graphite located at 26.5° (002)
splits into peaks corresponding to LiC18, LiC12, and LiC6
phases during the discharge process. The LiC18, LiC12, and
LiC6 phases disappear sequentially to form the initial C6 phase
during the charging process, demonstrating the reversible Li+
(de)intercalation within the graphite anode (Figure 2e). Upon
the use of 3.0 M LiFSI in the PC electrolyte, there is no
variation in the strong characteristic peak (002) of the
graphitic layers at 26.5° during the discharge process; however,
the intensity of the peak gradually decreases, indicating the
destruction of the van der Waals force and π−π interaction
between graphite layers (Figure 2f). This process is also
accompanied by the decrease in the graphite crystallization
degree and the change in the interlayer distance, further
showing the failure of Li+ intercalation as a result of
cointercalation and decomposition of Li+-PC in the PC-
based electrolytes without HFE.
The electrolyte stability was further verified by the cycling
performance of graphite anodes, showing a high discharge
capacity of 328 mAh g−1 over 50 cycles (Figure 2g). Besides
HFE, we find that the electrolyte compatibility could be
improved by adding TFTFE (1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2-
trifluoroethyl ether) diluent, which has a property similar to
that of HFE (Figure S2a). Moreover, the electrolyte
compatibility could be obtained by employing LiTFSI instead
of LiFSI while HFE is present in the electrolyte (Figure S2b).
The results demonstrate that adding fluorinated ether solvents
could be a general method to enhance the compatibility of PC-
based electrolytes with graphite anodes. However, it is
important to understand whether the improved electrolyte
compatibility is mainly caused by the SEI formed on the
graphite or rather by the Li+ solvation structure (e.g., solvent−
solvent interaction). Thus, we investigated the influence of the
SEI through an “electrolyte-exchange experiment”. The SEI-
coated graphite (i.e., formed in the PC/HFE electrolyte) could
not further sustain the cycle performance once the electrolyte
was replaced with the PC-based electrolyte, demonstrating the
insufficient effect of the SEI on the graphite performance
(Figure S3). The main reason for the improved electrolyte
compatibility should be due to the electrolyte microstructure;
thus, the effects of HFE on the Li+ solvation merit further
investigation at the molecular scale (inset of Figure 2g).
Solvent−Solvent Interactions in the Electrolytes. The
change in the electrolyte microstructure could be judged by the
variation in the environment of the PC solvents. First, we find
that all hydrogens in the PC molecules shift upfield toward
lower values in the
1H NMR spectra when HFE was
introduced to the electrolyte (Figure 3a), indicating that the
electron cloud density of H atoms in the PC was changed due
to HFE (inset of Figure 3a). Note that the phenomenon of the
upfield shift in the 1H NMR spectra is interesting, since there is
still a lack of understanding of the Li+ solvation structure.
Generally, the presence of the F atom with a strong electron-
withdrawing capability leads to the deshielding effect of the H
atom; thus, the addition of HFE should result in the downfield
displacement of the 1H chemical shift toward higher values in
the 1H NMR spectra. Therefore, the observed upfield shift is
ascribed to the polarization of electrons in the PC molecules
toward the H atoms due to interactions with the strong
electron-withdrawing F atoms in the HFE, resulting in the shift
of the 1H NMR spectra of PC toward lower values. The
increased electron cloud density surrounding the H atoms in
the PC is further demonstrated by the electrostatic potential
distribution of molecules before and after the addition of HFE
(Figure S4a). The results further demonstrate the existence of
the intermolecular interaction between PC and HFE that may
induce the polarization of electrons in the PC molecule (Figure
S4b). In addition, FTIR and Raman spectroscopy further
corroborate the rationale of our analysis. We find that the
vibration frequency of the CO bonds in the pure PC that
appears at ∼1781.9 cm−1 has a blue shift to ∼1783.8 and
∼1791.5 cm−1 after the addition of HFE (Figure 3b). This
phenomenon demonstrates that the energy required by the
spectrometer to excite the electron in the CO bonds
becomes higher after the addition of HFE, which corresponds
to the increased charge distribution in the CO where the
electrons occupy the higher energy level. Thus, the result
reconfirms the existence of intermolecular interactions
between HFE and PC molecules, involving δ−F atoms in the
HFE with δ+H in PC and particularly δ+H atoms in HFE with
δ−O in the PC. The interactions result in an increased electron
cloud density and dipole moment of CO bonds in the PC,49
leading to a blue shift in the FTIR and Raman spectra (Figure
3b,c). We also find almost no peak in the ranges of 1850−1750
and 700−720 cm−1 for HFE in the FTIR and Raman spectra
(red curve in Figure 3b,c), reconfirming the accuracy of our
analysis.
The change in the carbonyl oxygen of PC after adding HFE
diluent is also detected by 17O NMR. The observed shielding
effect in the spectra demonstrates the increased electron cloud
density on carbonyl oxygen after adding HFE, giving evidence
of interactions between PC and HFE (Figure 3d). Moreover,
the interactions are confirmed by
1H−19F heteronuclear
Overhauser effect spectroscopy (HOESY) (Figure 3e). The
dipolar contacts between H and F indicate a solvent−solvent
interaction between PC and HFE formed by electronegative
fluorine (δ−F) and electropositive hydrogen (δ+H). Note that
the two-dimensional NMR HOESY spectra show intra-
molecular interactions and intermolecular interactions, respec-
tively. Among them, the circled rectangle indicates the
interaction between HFE and PC, where the strongest
interaction occurs between the H atoms in the PC at different
positions (A−C) and the F atoms at position 1 in the HFE, as
shown in Figure 3e. The strength of the interaction decreases
in the order of F1 > F2 > F3 for all three positions (A, B, C) of
the hydrogen atom in PC (i.e., δ+HA −δ−F1 > δ+HA −δ−F2 >
δ+HA −δ−F3, δ+HB −δ−F1 > δ+HB −δ−F2 > δ+HB −δ−F3,
δ+HC −δ−F1 > δ+HC −δ−F2 > δ+HC −δ−F3). The remaining
ones that are not circled represent the intramolecular
interaction in the HFE, which is stronger than the

The interactions between PC and HFE are summarized in
Figure 3f. The overall charge distribution of the PC molecules
could be polarized toward CO (δ−O) due to the electron-
donating effect of −CH3 and the electron-withdrawing effect of
CO, while the electron cloud density on H is relatively low.
In the HFE molecules, the charge distribution on HFE is
mainly polarized on F (δ−F) due to the strong electron-
withdrawing effect of F, while the charge density on H is
relatively low (δ+H). Besides, the H atoms in the HFE could
also interact with O in the PC to increase the electron cloud
density in the PC. In other words, HFE causes the electrons in
the PC to be pulled toward both sides and then increases the
electron cloud density of O and H in the PC, which is
consistent with the results in Figure 3a−e. Thus, the
interactions between F in the HFE and H in the PC are
clearly shown in the 2D NMR HOESY spectra that are mainly
achieved by the interactions between the stronger δ−F and δ+H
(Figure 3e). The interactions between PC and HFE molecules
that occur in different atoms are listed in Figure 3f. In addition,
the electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution also confirms our
conclusion (Figure S4a). Besides, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG)
of the single solvent to form cosolvent complexes (e.g., PC +

interactions between PC and HFE. Specifically, the Gibbs free
energy ΔG1 of the PC-HFE mixture is −9.44 kcal mol−1, which
is much lower than that of PC-PC solvents (i.e., ΔG2 = −5.07

mol−1), respectively, demonstrating the preferentially hetero-
molecular PC-HFE interactions compared to the homomo-
lecular interactions (Figure 3g−i). The existence of PC-HFE
interactions in the electrolyte solvation structure is an
important discovery in the Li+ solvation structure, which
completes the common understanding of the known Li+ and
solvent interactions.

phenomenon is further verified by comparing the characteristic

electrolyte. There is no change in the characteristic peaks of

The role of HFE in tuning the solvent−solvent interactions
and the Li+ solvation structure is further detailed by NMR
characterizations. The 1H NMR spectra of the electrolytes with
and without HFE diluent demonstrate that all H atoms in the
PC shift upfield toward lower values after adding HFE (Figure

distribution due to the presence of HFE. The result is
consistent with the PC-HFE solvent mixture without lithium
salt in Figure 3a. In detail, in the 1.0 M LiFSI in PC/HFE
electrolyte, HFE gives rise to a dipole−dipole interaction with
H in the PC (δ+H-δ−F), resulting in a decrease in the electron
density of CO in the PC, thereby weakening Li+ solvation by
PC, i.e., Li+-PC. The argument is supported by 7Li NMR
analysis, where the 7Li signal shifts toward the lower values
after adding HFE to the PC-based electrolyte. The downfield

shielding effect on Li+ (Figure 4d), indicating the decreased
Li+-OPC interaction due to the HFE-PC interaction. Note that
the weakened coordination of PC with Li+ may allow the
anions (i.e., FSI−) to stay closer to Li+ (Figure 4e), making the
SO bond of FSI−shift upfield after adding HFE.
The intermolecular interaction between PC and HFE in the

the interaction between FSI−and Li+. Moreover, the binding

resultant weakened Li+-PC interaction, which in turn facilitates
the Li+ desolvation process, enables the reversible Li+
deintercalation within graphite anodes, and mitigates the
electrolyte decomposition as discussed later.
Solvation Structure and Interfacial Model. Based on
the above analysis, we construct a Li+ solvation structure by a
formula of Li+[solvent]x[anion] (where x is the molar ratio of
the solvent to Li+) and then present an interfacial model of the
Li+ desolvation process at the electrolyte−electrode interfaces
to understand the influences of the solvent−solvent
interactions on the graphite performance. We use d and d′
to represent the relative distance between Li+ and the solvent
in the bulk electrolyte and that at the graphite interface,
respectively. In addition, we use f and f′ to represent the
relative distance between Li+ and FSI−. The strength of the
interaction is inversely proportional to the distance, where a
long distance means a weaker interaction. In the 3.0 M LiFSI
in PC electrolyte (i.e., Li+ [PC]3.93[FSI−]), the amount of PC
in the solvation structure is insufficient due to the low molar
ratio of PC/Li+ (i.e., 3.93 molar ratio, while the Li+
coordination number is about 4−6), so all the PC molecules
coordinate with Li+ in the first layer of the solvation structure.
Thus, the interaction between PC and Li+ (i.e., Li+-OPC, d1) is
strong, while the FSI−anion stays far from Li+ (i.e., f1) due to
the high dielectric constant of PC (ε = 64.92) leading to the
dissolution of the Li+/FSI−interaction. In this way, Li+ has
difficulty in desolvating from the Li+-PC complexes at the
interface due to the strong Li+-PC interaction (i.e., d1′). On the
other hand, there is no neutralization effect of FSI−on the Li+-
PC complexes due to the far distance of FSI−(i.e., f1′), which
could not weaken the Li+-PC interaction effectively. As a result,
the Li+-PC cointercalates at graphite, leading to graphite
exfoliation and electrolyte decomposition even if the
concentration of LiFSI is as high as 3.0 M (Figure 5a).
In contrast, in the 1.0 M LiFSI in PC/HFE electrolyte (1:2
volume; i.e., Li+[PC]3.93[HFE]4.4[FSI−]), PC molecules
coordinate with Li+ to form the first layer of the solvation
structure due to the high coordination capability of PC, where
HFE molecules remain in the second solvation shell making
molecular interactions with PC (i.e., PC-HFE interactions).
Thus, the interaction between PC and Li+ (i.e., Li+-OPC‑HFE, d2,
d2 > d1) effectively weakens due to the PC-HFE interaction,
and then FSI−can keep close to Li+ (f 2, f 2 < f1) due to the
decrease in the solvation capability of PC. In the interfacial
model, Li+ could be desolvated readily from the PC-HFE
solvent pair at the interface, as the Li+-PC interaction is
weakened by PC-HFE interactions (i.e., d2′, d2′ > d1′). In
particular, FSI−that is close to Li+ (i.e., f 2′, f 2′ < f1′) may
neutralize Li+, leading to the weakened Li+-PC interaction. As a
result, Li+ could be reversibly (de)intercalated within the
graphite anode.
We further validated the rationale of our proposed solvation
structure and interfacial model by analyzing the electrolyte
properties, particularly the Li+ transference number and ionic
conductivity. First, we found that 1.0 M LiFSI in PC/HFE
electrolyte exhibits a higher Li+ transference number (t+ =
0.4602) than that of 3.0 M LiFSI in PC electrolyte (t+ =
0.2882) (Figure S7a,b). The result is reasonable, as the
intermolecular interaction between HFE and PC weakens the
Li+-PC interaction, resulting in an enhanced Li+-FSI−
interaction and a decreased population of free FSI−anions in
the electrolyte solvation structure (i.e., t+ + t−= 1). In addition,
the 1.0 M LiFSI in the PC/HFE electrolyte demonstrates a
slightly lower ionic conductivity of 2.722 mS cm−1 than the 3.0
M LiFSI in the PC electrolyte (i.e., 3.122 mS cm−1) due to the
increased interaction between Li+ and FSI−in addition to the
lower electrolyte concentration (Figure S7c). The results
establish a correlation between the solvation structure and
electrolyte properties (i.e., Li+ transport). Thus, the proposed
electrolyte solvation structure and interfacial model could
interpret the electrolyte properties (Figure 5).
In addition to the thermodynamics of Li+ desolvation and
the kinetics of Li+ transport in the bulk electrolyte, we evaluate
the electrochemical stability of desolvated clusters at the
graphite anode interface (i.e., electrolyte stability) by the
energy difference between the orbitals of the solvation
structure complex (i.e., ΔE = HOMO′ −LUMO). The
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) represents the
critical orbitals for the Li+-solvent-(anion) complex, where the
corresponding orbitals become HOMO′ when one extra
electron is received from the electrode during the discharge
process (i.e., Li0-solvent-(anion) complex). The influence of
the anion is considered to depend on the distance between the
anion and Li+. The smaller energy difference (ΔE) indicates
similarity with the orbitals, resulting in a smooth electron
transfer at the interface and poor stability of solvent molecules.
Herein we take the Li+-PC, Li+-PC/HFE, Li+-PC-FSI−, and
Li+-PC/HFE-FSI−complexes to study the electrolyte stability.
In the 1.0 M LiFSI in PC/HFE electrolyte, ΔE of Li+-PC/HFE
(i.e., 0.0975 hartree) and Li+-PC/HFE-FSI−(i.e., 0.1229
hartree) is much higher than those of Li+-PC (i.e., 0.0883
hartree) and Li+-PC-FSI−(i.e., 0.1066 hartree) in the 3.0 M
LiFSI in PC electrolyte, demonstrating the enhanced stability
of the newly designed electrolyte (i.e., higher stability of Li+-
PC/HFE and Li+-PC/HFE-FSI−) on the surface of graphite
anodes. The simulated results are consistent with the
experimental results, which further support our interfacial
model.

graphite anodes is further confirmed by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) analyses (Figure S8). We find that there is no obvious

Application in Li−S Batteries. We demonstrate the
advantage of our nonflammable PC/HFE-based electrolyte
and the reversible Li+ (de)intercalation in the newly designed
lithium-ion−sulfur (i.e., Li−S) system, composed of a
sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathode and lithiated
graphite anode (i.e., LiC6) (Figure 6a). The PC/HFE-based
electrolyte offers nonflammability and a higher boiling point
than the traditional ether-based electrolytes (e.g., 1,3-
dioxolane/dimethyl ether, DOL/DME),53 which will signifi-
cantly improve the battery safety as well as create an easier
production process. Further, our electrolyte ensures a good
cycle performance of the SPAN cathode and the reversible Li+
deintercalation at the graphite electrode, which are hard to
achieve by the conventional ether-based electrolytes since the
Li+-solvent cointercalation and/or graphite exfoliation remain
as the problematic issues.54,55 The graphite electrode was
precycled in the half-cell (i.e., graphite|Li) to compensate for
the Li+ loss during the formation cycles, and then Li+ was
intercalated into the graphite to form LiC6. The working
voltage of the full battery was set at 0.8−3.0 V. Note that such
a moderate cutoff voltage is beneficial for the stabilization of
PC/HFE-based electrolytes in the Li−S battery56 to overcome
the intrinsic low antioxidation of HFE at the high cutoff
voltage for other cathodes (e.g., LiCoO2, LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4, etc.)
in LIBs, finally achieving a comparable high energy density
without using expensive transition-metal-based cathodes (e.g.,
Ni, Co, Mn, etc.). Furthermore, our strategy will enable a
nonflammable, sustainable, environmentally adaptable, and
high-energy-density Li−S battery.
The Li−S full battery achieves an extremely high average
capacity of up to 583 mAh g−1 at a current density of 200 mA
g−1 during the initial 100 cycles (Figure 6b). The discharge
capacity could be maintained at 446 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles
with a high-capacity retention of 76.5% (Figure 6b). We find

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have achieved a highly reversible Li+
(de)intercalation at graphite anodes using PC/HFE-based
electrolytes by tuning the Li+ solvation structure. This strategy
is more effective in developing nonflammable and functioning
electrolytes compared to adding film-forming additives or
increasing the salt concentration. In particular, we have
detected the existence of an intermolecular interaction
between PC and HFE, which could be the reason for the
improved Li+ (de)intercalation. The fluoroether solvents (e.g.,
HFE) strongly interact with PC molecules via their δ−F and
δ+H atoms, respectively, which leads to the change in the
charge distribution on the surface of solvation clusters and
then weakens the Li+-PC interaction. As a result, the Li+ can
more easily desolvate on the surface of graphite anodes for the
reversible Li+ (de)intercalation, enabling a newly designed Li−
S battery to achieve a high cycle performance and its potential
for applications in a wide range of temperatures. This study
sheds light on the influences of solvent−solvent interactions on
interfacial chemistry, which is an in-depth view angle to design
nonflammable electrolytes for high-performance batteries.
EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials and Preparation. The chemicals, including propylene
carbonate (PC), lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl) imide (LiFSI, 99.5%),
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI), and 1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) were purchased
from Dodo Chemical (Suzhou, Jiangsu Province, China). The
graphite was purchased from Huzhou Kunlun Power Battery
Materials Co., Ltd. All reagents were used without further purification.
The stoichiometric ratio of lithium salt and solvent based on the
electrolyte concentration (mol L−1) was used to prepare the target
electrolytes. Typically, the solvent was placed in a brown bottle, and
then the lithium salt was added with stirring to form a clear electrolyte
solution. The entire process was carefully handled in an argon-filled
glovebox, where the moisture and oxygen levels were controlled at
about 0.5 ppm. The graphite electrode was prepared by mixing
graphite powder, Super C45, carboxymethyl cellulose sodium
(CMC), and styrenebutadiene rubber (SBR) binder with a mass
ratio of 90:5:2:3 in water. Then, the mixture was milled for 5 min by
using a Hasai planetary mixer to form a uniform slurry, which was
coated on copper foil and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven for 12 h.
The sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) electrode was prepared by
mixing SPAN powder, acetylene black, and carboxymethyl cellulose
sodium (CMC) with a mass ratio of 70:20:10 in water. Then, the
mixture was milled for 5 min by using a Hasai planetary mixer to form
a uniform slurry, which was coated on aluminum foil and dried at 60
°C in a vacuum oven for 12 h. The mass loadings of graphite and
SPAN in the electrode were about 5.2 and 1.5 mg cm−2, respectively.
The graphite electrode was precycled in a half-cell (i.e., graphite|Li) to
compensate for the irreversibility in the initial cycles, and then Li+ was
intercalated into the graphite to form LiC6. N/P of the SPAN|LiC6
full cell was controlled at about 1.6 by adjusting the active mass of the
negative and positive electrodes.
Characterizations. The graphite|lithium half-cells and SPAN |
LiC6 full cells were assembled using 2032-type coin cells with a glass
fiber separator (Whatman GF-A) in an argon-filled glovebox, with the
content of O2 and H2O maintained below 0.5 ppm. The cutoff voltage
was set at 0.01−1.5 and 0.8−3.0 V in the half-cells and full cells,
respectively. In the long-term cycling test, the SPAN|LiC6 full cell was
first cycled three cycles at current densities of 50 and 100 mA g−1,
respectively, then the full cell was cycled 100 cycles at 200 mA g−1. In
the rate test, the SPAN|LiC6 full cell was tested at the different current
densities of 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mA g−1, respectively,
where the full cell was cycled at each rate for three cycles. Then, we
select the (dis)charge curves in the second cycle at each current
density to compare the performance. In the low-temperature test, the
SPAN|LiC6 full cell was cycled at a current density of 50 mA g−1 at 25
°C first, and then the cell was cycled at a current density of 100 mA
g−1 at −10 °C. The galvanostatic charge and discharge curves were
recorded by a Neware instrument.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and the Li+ transference number were
performed by using a Biologic VMP3 electrochemical workstation. In
the CV test for the graphite electrode, the cutoff voltage of the
graphite|Li half-cells was controlled at 0.01−3.0 V, while the scan rate
was set at 0.1 mV s−1. To measure the lithium-ion (Li+) transference
number, the Li||Li symmetrical cells were assembled and precycled at

pulse was applied to the Li||Li symmetrical cells for 10 min to obtain
the initial currents of I0 and Iss when the current reached a steady
state. The EIS test was acquired before (R0) and after (Rss) the
polarization between a frequency range from 300 kHz to 100 mHz by
a sinusoidal amplitude of 10 mV. Then, the transference number was

Theoretical Simulation. Quantum chemistry calculations were
performed first to optimize the molecular geometries of PC and HFE
solvent molecules using the Gaussian 16 package at the B3LYP/6-
311+G(d) level of theory. The atomic partial charges on these solvent
molecules were computed by fitting to the molecular electrostatic
potential at atomic centers with the Møller−Plesset second-order
perturbation method and the correlation-consistent polarized valence
cc-pVTZ(-f) basis set. The atomistic force field parameters for all ions
and molecules are described in the AMBER format and are taken
from previous work.65 The cross-interaction parameters between
different atom types are obtained from the Lorentz−Berthelot
combination rule. Two modeling systems were constructed, and the
detailed simulation system compositions are given in Table S2.
Atomistic simulations of this modeling system were performed using
the GROMACS package with cubic periodic boundary conditions.66
The equations for the motion of all atoms were integrated by using a
classic Verlet leapfrog integration algorithm with a time step of 1.0 fs.
A cutoff radius of 1.6 nm was set for short-range van der Waals
interactions and real-space electrostatic interactions. The particle-
mesh Ewald (PME) summation method with an interpolation order
of 5 and a Fourier grid spacing of 0.15 nm was employed to handle
long-range electrostatic interactions in reciprocal space. All simulation
systems were first energetically minimized using the steepest descent
algorithm and thereafter annealed gradually from 700 K to room
temperature (300 K) within 10 ns. All annealed simulation systems
were equilibrated in an isothermal−isobaric (NPT) ensemble for 20
ns of physical time maintained using a Nosé−Hoover thermostat and
a Parrinello−Rahman barostat with time coupling constants of 0.4
and 0.2 ps, respectively, to control the temperature at 300 K and the
pressure at 1 atm. Atomistic simulations were further performed in a
canonical ensemble (NVT) for 50 ns, and simulation trajectories were
recorded at an interval of 100 fs for further structural and dynamic
analysis. Representative solvation structures were extracted from
extensive atomistic simulations, and these solvation structures were
adopted as starting configurations for additional DFT calculations.
DFT calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software67 at
the same level of theory (B3LYP/6-311+G(d)) and with Grimme’s-
D3 (gd3bj) dispersion correction to obtain the optimized
coordination structures, and thereafter to calculate binding energies
of relevant structures.
