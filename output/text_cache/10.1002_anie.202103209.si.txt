EXPERIMENTS  
Material preparation  
 
Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl) and Li3PS4 powders were fabricated according to previous reports [1-2]. In 
detail, Li2S (Alfa Aesar, 99.9 %), P2S5 (MACKLIN, ≥ 99 %), and (or) LiCl (Alfa Aesar, ≥ 99 %) 
were mixed according to their corresponding molar ratio. The mixed powders were then sealed into a 
stainless-steel pot and ball milled for 4 hrs using a milling instrument (FRITSCH, pulverizette 7), 
after which the ball milled powders were sintered at 300 °C for 8 hrs to obtain Li3PS4 and 550 °C for 
8 hrs to obtain LPSCl. All these processes were conducted under argon.  
 
To prepare the leakage-proof electrolyte, LPSCl powders were firstly added in a mixture of 
ECA : (DOL/DME, volume ratio, 1 : 1) with the volume ratio of ECA : DME/DOL = 1 : 1. After 
being heated at 60 °C, a yellow transparent solution was obtained, which was defined as 
LPSCl-grafted Poly(ECA) (LgP) solution. Then, a concentration of 1 M LiTFSI was added into the 
LgP solution. At last, different amount of LgP solution (with LiTFSI) was added into the commercial 
electrolyte, i.e., 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (volume ratio 1 : 1) with the addition of 1 wt.% LiNO3 to 
accomplish the preparation of leakage-proof electrolyte.  
 
To prepare bare S cathode, bare S cathode slurry was firstly prepared by grinding 60 wt % 
commercial sulfur powders (Sigma-Aldrich), 30 wt % Super P, 5 wt % styrene-butadiene rubber, and 
5 wt % carboxymethyl cellulose in deionized water. Then the slurry was casted on an Al foil and 
dried in a vacuum oven for 24 hrs at 60 °C to obtain bare sulfur cathode. The bare S cathodes had 
sulfur loading of 1.01 ± 0.04 or 4.9 ± 0.2 mg cm–2. To prepare S@CNT (carbon nanotube) cathode, 
the commercial sulfur powders were firstly ground with the CNT with a mass ratio of 70 : 30, sealed 
into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 155 °C for 24 hrs to obtain S@CNT composites. The 
S@CNT cathode slurry was obtained by grinding 80 wt% S@CNT composites, 10 wt% Super P, and 
10 wt% poly(vinylidene fluoride) binder in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%). Then 
the slurry was cast on Al foil and dried in a vacuum oven for 24 hrs at 60 °C to obtain S@CNT 
cathode. The S@CNT cathodes had different S loading for different purposes, which are mentioned 
in the respective cells.  
Battery assembly  
 
Symmetrical steel|LPSCl|steel battery (for conductivity test) was fabricated by pressing 0.13 g 
powders in one 12 mm diameter mold. Symmetrical steel|liquid electrolyte|steel batteries (CR2032, 
for conductivity test) were fabricated by injecting 100 μL of the leakage-proof electrolyte, LgP 
solution (without LiTFSI), or commercial electrolyte into glass microfibers (Whatman) with 
thickness of 40 μm and diameter of 16.5 mm sandwiched between two steel discs with 16.5 mm in 
diameter. Li−S coin cells (CR2032) to demonstrate the electrochemical performance used bare S 
cathodes with 12 mm in diameter, Li anodes with 16 mm in diameter, and glass microfiber separators 
with 16.5 mm in diameter. 30 μL of the leakage-proof electrolyte or commercial electrolyte were 
injected in the coin cells using bare S cathodes with S loading of ~ 1.0 mg cm–2 and 100 uL were 
injected in the coin cells using bare S cathodes with S loading of ~ 4.9 mg cm–2. The coin cells 
(CR2032) used to test the self-discharge behavior were fabricated using S@CNT cathodes (S loading,

The pouch cells for leakage-proof demonstration were fabricated using Li anodes, S@CNT 
cathodes (S loading, 0.72 mg cm–2; single-sided) and polythene separators (Shenzhen LiDe). The 
dimensions of cathode and anode were 30 × 40 mm2 and 32 × 42 mm2, respectively. The electrode 
sheets were stacked and packed into a laminate bag made with aluminum-plastic film. After 600 μL 
leakage-proof electrolyte or commercial electrolyte being injected, the laminate bag was sealed. Al 
and Ni terminals were employed for S cathode and Li anode, respectively.  
 
The pouch cells for energy density estimation were fabricated using double-sided S@CNT 
cathode with S loading of 4.0 mg cm–2. The size of a single slice cathode was 58 × 82 mm2. A single 
slice of cathode was stacked with a polythene separator, and a Li foil (62 × 86 mm2 in area and 100 
μm in thickness) anode alternately to compose a single cell with 2 slices of cathodes and 3 slices of 
anodes. The total mass of the current collector, cathode, anode and separator was 2.78 g. After 2 ml 
leakage-proof electrolyte being injected, the laminate bag was sealed. Al and Ni terminals were 
employed for S cathode and Li anode, respectively.  
 
The pouch cells to test nail penetration were fabricated using double-sided S@CNT cathode 
with S loading of 13.6 mg cm–2. The size of a single slice cathode was 143 × 82 mm2. A single slice 
of cathode was stacked with a polythene separator, and a Li foil (147 × 86 mm2 in area and 100 μm 
in thickness) anode alternately to compose a single cell with 11 slices of cathodes and 12 slices of 
anodes. After 40 ml leakage-proof electrolyte being injected, the laminate bag was sealed. Al and Ni 
terminals were employed for S cathode and Li anode, respectively.  
Characterization  
 
To identify the elementary composition and thus the reaction mechanism of LPSCl with ECA, 1 
mL LgP solution was dropped into 10 mL ethyl acetate to obtain precipitates, which were then 
washed four times using ethyl acetate. After dried at room temperature, the precipitates were 
observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) with element mapping analysis 
(Figure S5).  
 
X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE) analysis was carried out to confirm the crystal 
phase of LPSCl and Li3PS4. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz) and 
fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Bruker VERTEX 70) were conducted for related 
experiments. Gel-permeation chromatograph was conducted to estimate the molecular weight of LgP 
using N,N-Dimethylformamide as solvent.  
 
Ionic conductivity was estimated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using an 
electrochemical working station, Biologic VMP-300, in a frequency range of from 6 MHz to 1 Hz at 
room temperature. Cyclic voltammetry measurement of Li−S coin cell was conducted on Biologic 
VMP-300 electrochemical workstation with a scan rate of 0.2 mV s−1 in the potential range of 1.8 – 
2.8 V. The galvanostatic charge−discharge tests of Li−S batteries were carried out via LAND testing 
system.

Theoretical calculation 
 
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to study the behavior of LPSCl in DME. 
The simulation calculation was conducted with an integration time-step of 1 fs. Periodic boundary 
conditions were applied in the x- and y-dimensions. The box size of the samples was 2 ×  2× 2 nm3. 
First, the conjugate gradient algorithm and energy minimization were performed to obtain a stable 
structure. Condensed-phased Optimized Molecular Potential for Atomistic Simulation Studies force 
field was also used to optimize these structures in the Materials studio with forcite Module. Each 
sample was then equilibrated under the NPT ensemble at a constant temperature of 300 K to achieve 
an equilibrium state with zero pressure for 30 ns. Furthermore, a potential cutoff radius of 2.25 nm 
was applied in the calculation of the non-bonded interaction. And the PPPM was used to describe the 
electrostatic. The Andersen feedback thermostat and Berendsen barostat algorithm were applied in 
the system with temperature and pressure conversion. Finally, the properties of the structures were 
obtained in the last 3000 ps.

Figure S2. (a) 1H and (b) 13C NMR spectra of DOL, DME, ECA, and LgP. The structural formula of ECA and 
Poly(ECA) with hydrogen and carbon atoms in different positions are shown. (c) 31P and (d) 7Li NMR spectra of 
LgP. The NMR tests are conducted using solvents of deuterated chloroform. (e) IR spectrum of LgP.  
 
To elucidate the reaction mechanism between LPSCl and ECA, NMR is firstly conducted. 1H 
NMR spectrum of ECA (Figure S2a) shows four peaks at 6.98, 6.58, 4.26, and 1.29 ppm, 
corresponding to the hydrogen atoms located in different environments from № 1 to 4 as marked in 
ECA structural formula. After mixed with DOL and DME, all the hydrogen atom positions remain 

<<PAGE 7>>
6 
 
unchanged, which means no reactions happen among ECA, DOL, and DME. After LPSCl added into 
the mixture, i.e., for LgP, the peaks related to № 1 and 2 hydrogen atoms disappear completely while 
another peak in the range of 2.77 − 2.66 ppm emerges. In this case, the C=C bond changes to C−C 
bond thus № 1 and 2 hydrogen atoms change to № 1' and 2' hydrogen atoms while hydrogen atoms 
of № 3 and 4 remain unchanged as illustrated in the structural formula, implying that ECA can be 
polymerized into Poly(ECA) with LPSCl grafted. The 13C NMR spectra also suggest C=C bond in 
ECA absolutely changes to C−C bond in LgP, agreeing well with the 1H NMR results.  
 
It is commonly acknowledged that the polymerization of ECA is initiated by anions on the basis 
of anionic polymerization mechanism. Since LPSCl is dissociated into Cl−, S2−, and PS4
3− in 
DME/DOL/ECA mixture (Figure S3), to clarify which anions initiate ECA polymerization, LiCl, 
Li2S, and Li3PS4 are separately added into DME/DOL/ECA mixture as they will be dissociated into 
the solo anion of Cl−, S2−, and PS4
3−. Both 1H and 13C NMR spectra from Li2S−DME/DOL/ECA, 
LiCl−DME/DOL/ECA, and Li3PS4−DME/DOL/ECA in Figure S4 are the same as those of LgP, 
indicating that all the anions, i.e., Cl−, S2−, and PS4
3−, can initiate the polymerization of ECA. Though 
no S or Cl signals are detected owing to the low resolution of 33S (33 MHz) and 35Cl (39 MHz) NMR, 
31P NMR spectrum (Figure S2c) is powerful evidence to verify the speculation. In addition, element 
mapping analysis displays clearly that S, P, Cl, C are contained in LgP (Figure S5). Because element 
P and Cl come from the anions of PS4
3− and Cl− while C from Poly(ECA), the above speculation is 
confirmed. To neutralize the negative charge, Li+ must be contained and thereby makes LgP 
Li+-conductive. The existence of element Li is demonstrated by 7Li NMR spectrum in Figure S2d, 
which shows one peak at ca. −0.99 ppm.  
 
The IR spectrum of LgP is shown in Figure S2e. The characteristic peak at 1744 cm−1 
corresponds to the carbonyl (C=O) of the Poly(ECA) segment while the characteristic peak at 1600 
cm−1 can be attributed to the ethenolate motif on LgP, which is resulted from the reaction between 
LPSCl and ECA. Details can be found in Scheme S1.

Figure S3. Schematic view of the theoretical MD simulation of LPSCl in DME from (a) pristine to (b) stable state. 
1 M LPSCl is dissociated into 1 M Cl−, 1 M S2−, and 1 M PS4
3− in DME. (c) Radial distribution functions (RDFs) 
of Li-DME, Li-PS4, Cl-DME, Cl-PS4 pairs in DME and Li-PS4 pair in DME/ECA mixture.  
 
According to the MD simulation, 1 M LPSCl is dissociated into 1 M Cl−, 1 M S2−, and 1 M 
PS4
3− in DME. To describe the interaction between LPSCl and DME, radial distribution functions 
(RDFs) are performed. As shown in Figure S3c, in DME, the Li-DME and Cl-DME coordination are 
identified at 3.31 and 3.50 Å, respectively, which are much smaller than those of Li-PS4 (4.94 Å) and 
Cl-PS4 (5.22 Å), indicating that both Li+ and Cl− are solvated by DME molecules. In other words, 
LPSCl is dissociated in DME. To identify the influence of ECA on the dissolution of LPSCl, the 
coordination of Li-PS4 in DME and Li-PS4 in DME/ECA is compared. Figure S3c shows that in 
DME/ECA mixture, the coordination of Li-PS4 is 5.56 Å, larger than that of Li-PS4 in DME, which 
means that ECA facilitates the dissolution of LPSCl in DME.

Scheme S1. The proposed reactions among ECA and Cl−, S2−, PS4
3− to generate LgP.  
 
According to the anionic polymerization mechanism and NMR results, the reaction mechanism 
between ECA and LPSCl to generate LgP is proposed in Scheme S1. The polymerization of ECA 
initiated directly by Cl−, S2−, and PS4
3− are depicted based on R1 − R3. Taking the Cl− (R1) for an 
example, the electron transfers from Cl− to the C=C bonds thus subsequently resulting in anionic 
active species, which initializes 
the rapid chain propagation. Moreover, the strong 
electron-withdrawing side group, i.e., the C≡N, stabilizes the anionic active species to feature high 
reaction activity. The reaction product of R1 is defined as (Cl-PECA)−. The reaction processes of S2− 
(R2) and PS4
3− (R3) are the same as that of Cl− (R1) except that two or three PECA chains are 
appended to S or PS4 with the reaction products defined as (S-2PECA)2− and (PS4-3PECA)3−, 
respectively. According to the previous NMR studies on the thiophosphates [3-7] and Figure S2c, the 
peak at 84.6 ppm represents PS4 related components while the peak at −2.4 ppm can be attributed to 
PS5. The generation of P with five coordinations can be depicted in R4: the PS4
3− is firstly attacked 
by S2− to result in PS5
5− and further initiating the polymerization of ECA to produce (PS5-5PECA)5− 
with the same procedure as R1 − R3. Accordingly, LgP is composed of the segments of (Cl-PECA)−, 
(S-2PECA)2−, (PS4-3PECA)3−, and (PS5-5PECA)5− with Li+ attracted around.

Figure S6. (a) Conductivity dependence of commercial electrolyte on ECA or LgP content. Inset shows the EIS 
plot of the LgP solution (without LiTFSI). (b) Electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries based on different 
electrolytes at a rate of 0.5 C with bare sulfur loading of 1 mg cm−2.  
 
σ of LgP solution (without LiTFSI) is estimated to be 8.09 × 10–5 S cm–1 according to the 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) plot in Figure S6a. The performance of the 
leakage-proof electrolyte with different LgP content is tested in Li–S coin cells utilizing bare S 
cathodes with 60 wt.% loading (1 mg cm–2). At a charge/discharge rate of 0.5 C, the discharge 
specific capacity of the battery using the leakage-proof electrolyte can reach 700 mA h g–1 (10 and 
30 wt.% LgP), 1.7 times higher than that of the battery using commercial electrolyte, 412 mA h g–1. 
However, at a higher LgP loading, the discharge capacity is as low as 295 mA h g–1 for the battery 
with 50 wt.% LgP, probably due to the low conductivity (0.54 mS cm–1) and the strong affinity of 
polysulfides with LgP. Among these, the battery with 20 wt.% LgP is the most stable one with a 
satisfactory initial discharge capacity of 607 mA h g–1 and a capacity retention of 85.0 % after 100 
cycles.

Figure S8. Self-discharge behavior of Li–S coin cells using commercial and the leakage-proof electrolyte. After 
charge/discharge at 0.5 C for 6 cycles, the cells based on (a) commercial electrolyte and (c) leakage-proof 
electrolyte rest for 72 hours to observe the voltage evolution. After the rest, the cells based on (b) commercial 
electrolyte and (d) leakage-proof electrolyte are then sequentially discharged and charged at 0.5 C to see the 
capacity decay.  
 
To test the self-discharge behavior, coin cells are fabricated with S@CNT cathode (10 mm in 
diameter; sulfur loading, 1.12 mg cm–2), glass fiber separator (16.5 mm in diameter), and Li anode 
(16 mm in diameter). 30 μl of the relevant electrolytes are injected into single cell. After working for 
6 cycles at 0.5 C (charged to 2.8 V), the cells rest for 72 hours to observe the voltage evolution. After 
rest, the cells are then sequentially discharged and charged. As shown in Figure S8, for the 
commercial electrolyte based cell, during rest, the voltage drops from 2.54 to 2.31 V vs Li/Li+ (9.1 % 
decay). As a consequence, the following discharge capacity is only 586 mAh g–1, 17.8 % decay 
compared with the capacity before rest, 713 mAh g–1. By a huge contrast, for the leakage-proof 
electrolyte based cell, the voltage decay is only 2% during 72 hours rest, which leads to a very low 
capacity decrease from 899 to 857 mAh g–1 (4.7 %). The comparison of self-discharge behavior also 
verifies the suppressed polysulfide shuttle in the cells using leakage-proof electrolyte.
