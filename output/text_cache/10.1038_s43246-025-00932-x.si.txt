Reviewers' comments: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This paper presents electrolyte additive Li(CB11H12). By altering the solvation structure, it reduces the solvation free energy
and the electrolyte's reducing ability. This suppresses LiPS dissolution at the S cathode and enables a stable lithium anode
reaction. However, some issues need to be addressed to make this manuscript suitable for publication: 
1. The authors claim that the reduction in the highly decomposed polysulfide species (LixSOy) in Figure 4c proves a
decrease in polysulfide dissolution. However, significantly more Li₂Sₓ signals are detected on the surface of the
experimental sample compared to the control sample. This indicates that the additive only suppresses the decomposition of
lithium polysulfides on the anode surface but does not inhibit their dissolution in the electrolyte. Please provide the solubility
of LPSs in the electrolyte with and without additive. 
2. The results in Figures 4d and 4e show that the formation of Li₃N and LiF (derived from NO₃⁻ and TFSI⁻) on the anode
surface is reduced after the addition of the additive. The reduction in inorganic components is not beneficial for the cycling
stability of the lithium metal. The authors should reconsider how the additive improves the cycling stability of the anode. 
3. The paper claims that the introduction of Li(CB11H12) promotes Li+-solvent coordination and expels anions. Is there more
direct evidence for the change in solvation structure to support this? 
4. More solvent molecules are incorporated into the electrolyte's internal structure. Why does this suppress the dissolution of
polysulfides? Please provide a clearer explanation and evidence for this suppression, such as UV-Vis analysis. 
5. In Figure 3(a), why does the second plateau rise during discharge? How is this related to the dissolution and
transformation behavior of polysulfides? 
6. If possible, please try to confirm the effect of Li(CB11H12) using electrolyte without LiNO3. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this manuscript, the authors employ a complex hydride as an electrolyte additive to modify the solvation structure of the
electrolyte, suppress sulfur dissolution, and stabilize the lithium metal anode. However, the electrochemical performance
reported in the article still falls significantly short when compared to other works in the same field. Therefore, I recommend
considering publication only after major revision. 
1. Why was Li(CB₁₁H₁₂) chosen as the electrolyte additive? Does it offer any cost advantage compared to other additives? 
2. The authors mention that the addition of Li(CB₁₁H₁₂) increases the number of solvent molecules around Li⁺ while pushing
anions further away. Please explain the underlying reason for this phenomenon. 
3. In Figure 2c, the Raman spectrum shows overlapped peaks (peak convolution) between the free TFSI⁻ and CIP peaks,
while the ACG peak appears near the trough between them. This deconvolution is not scientifically justified and should be
corrected. Additionally, please explain why a new distinct peak appears in the range of 750–770 cm⁻¹ after the addition of
Li(CB₁₁H₁₂). 
4. In Figure 2e, it is observed that under 0.5C long-term cycling, the Coulombic efficiency with Li(CB₁₁H₁₂) is worse
compared to other electrolyte systems. Please clarify the reason for this. Moreover, in many other studies, lithium-sulfur
batteries are typically cycled over 1000 cycles. Why does this work only show 100 cycles? ( Multi-Effect Ionic Liquid
Additives Achieve High Cycle Stability Lithium-Sulfur Batteries by Constructing an Electrostatic Shielding Layer and
Eliminating “Dead Sulfur”. (10.1002/adfm.202500077)) 
5. In Figure 3c, the polarization during the first 400 hours of symmetric cell cycling appears to be higher with the Li(CB₁₁H₁₂)-
containing electrolyte than the baseline electrolyte. Please explain this observation. Furthermore, there is a noticeable
fluctuation in the curve around 400 hours—could this indicate the onset of a short circuit? 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 

<<PAGE 3>>
In this article, the authors introduced Li(CB11H12) additive into DME/DOL electrolyte, which mitigates the shuttle effect.
Additionally, the Li(CB11H12) additive forms a highly reducing complex hydride−based interfacial layer on the lithium
anode, which effectively stabilizes the lithium metal interface. This novel additive demonstrates commendable
improvements in cycling performance compared to the commercial DME/DOL electrolyte. However, the tested S cathode
mass loading has very low mass loading (1.3 mg cm-2). It is highly recommended to evaluate the efficiency of the
Li(CB11H12) additive in higher mass loading S cathode. Furthermore, the following issues need to be addressed. I
recommend reconsidering this manuscript after these concerns are resolved. 
1. In Fig. 1c, why does the free solvent peak at ~ 1,022 cm-1 also increase in the Li(CB11H12) electrolyte? 
2. In 7Li NMR (Figure 1d), the upfield shift of Li+ peak could be from the enhanced Li-solvent interactions. The downfield
shift of the anions seems to indicate stronger Li-anion interactions. It is recommended that the authors should also perform
NMR measurements for the solvents. 
3. Explanation and discussion of the solvation structure is not robust enough. Why does this additive repel anions to the
outer solvation shell instead of itself being in the outer shell? 
4. Solvation free energy should be higher (less negative) for the Li(CB11H12) electrolyte. In other words, the decreased V1st
and the increased V2nd should result from the increased ΔG (Figure 3a). 
5. In the CV curves, why does the Li(CB11H12) electrolyte show a higher voltage for peak 3 while it has a decreased V1st in
the voltage profile? Also, the peak current strength of Figure 3b should be linked to the mass of active material. Please
convert to peak current per unit mass for comparison. 
6. In the XPS analysis, the authors claim that LiPS shuttling and electrolyte decomposition is less in the Li(CB11H12)
sample. I suggest that a quantification of each element based on the XPS survey scan should be performed to better
understand the SEI composition and support that claim. 
7. CE measurements with Li || Cu configuration should be provided to clarify the efficiency of Li(CB11H12) additive on
protecting the Li metal anode.

Responses to Reviewer’s Comments 
 
Reviewer #1 
 
This paper presents electrolyte additive Li(CB11H12). By altering the solvation structure, it 
reduces the solvation free energy and the electrolyte's reducing ability. This suppresses LiPS 
dissolution at the S cathode and enables a stable lithium anode reaction. However, some issues 
need to be addressed to make this manuscript suitable for publication: 
 
Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for the thorough evaluation of our manuscript and 
the valuable comments. In response, we have carefully addressed all the raised concerns and 
revised the manuscript accordingly. A detailed point−by−point response is provided below. 
 
1. The authors claim that the reduction in the highly decomposed polysulfide species (LixSOy) 
in Figure 4c proves a decrease in polysulfide dissolution. However, significantly more Li₂Sₓ 
signals are detected on the surface of the experimental sample compared to the control sample. 
This indicates that the additive only suppresses the decomposition of lithium polysulfides on 
the anode surface but does not inhibit their dissolution in the electrolyte. Please provide the 
solubility of LPSs in the electrolyte with and without additive. 
 
Response: We found this reviewer’s point extremely helpful in improving the quality of the 
manuscript. This is much appreciated. According to the reviewer’s comment, we conducted 
UV−vis absorption spectroscopy measurements to compare the solubility of the pristine and 
0.05M Li(CB11H12) electrolytes, as shown below (Supplementary Fig. 15). A polysulfide 
solution (0.02 M) of Li2S6 was prepared in a mixed solvent of 1,3−dioxolane (DOL) and 
1,2−dimethoxyethane (DME) with a 1:1 volume ratio. Equal volumes of this Li2S6 solution 
were then added to each electrolyte system to examine the LiPS solubility. A blank sample, 
consisting of Li2S6 solution without any added electrolyte, was also analyzed to serve as a 
baseline for comparison. The experimental results indicate that the 0.05M Li(CB11H12) 
electrolyte exhibits significantly lower absorbance peaks associated with dissolved lithium 
polysulfides (LiPS) species compared to the pristine electrolyte, supporting that the Li(CB11H12) 
additive effectively reduces the solubility of LiPS. To reflect this point, the given UV−vis 
absorption spectroscopy results were added to Supplementary Fig. 15 and the relevant 
sentences were added to the revised manuscript as follows:

2. The results in Figures 4d and 4e show that the formation of Li₃N and LiF (derived from NO₃⁻ 
and TFSI⁻) on the anode surface is reduced after the addition of the additive. The reduction in 
inorganic components is not beneficial for the cycling stability of the lithium metal. The authors 
should reconsider how the additive improves the cycling stability of the anode. 
 
Response: We sincerely appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comment. The beneficial role of 
inorganic components such as Li3N and LiF−formed via the reductive decomposition of NO3− 
and TFSI− in passivating and stabilizing the lithium metal surface has well been studied. As 
highlighted in several recent studies (Nat. Energy 2021, 6, 487–494; Nature 2019, 572, 511–
515), not only the presence of specific inorganic components but also the formation of a 
uniform and compact solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) is considered critical for the long−term 
stability of lithium metal anodes. Although Li3N and LiF possess high mechanical and chemical 
stability, they can hinder Li+ transport and are prone to cracking or fragmentation during 
repeated charge/discharge cycles. In other words, if the SEI is not sufficiently compact and 
stable, electrolyte can continuously penetrate into the Li−metal, accelerating its corrosion and 
the consumption of electrolyte, which ultimately deteriorates long−term cycling performance. 
These effects are further exacerbated in Li−S batteries due to the presence of LiPS.  
To investigate this issue in detail, we conducted cross−sectional SEM analysis of Li||Li 
symmetric cells (Supplementary Fig. 19), which supports that the 0.05M Li(CB11H12) 
electrolyte forms a thinner and more compact interfacial layer after long−term cycling. These 

<<PAGE 11>>
results imply that the Li(CB11H12) additive developed here facilitates the formation of a 
chemically stable and spatially uniform SEI, which effectively suppresses electrolyte 
infiltration and mitigates parasitic reactions. It is thus believed that despite the potentially 
detrimental effects of Li₃N and LiF, the Li(CB11H12) additive contributes to maintaining the 
integrity of the initial SEI over prolonged cycling, owing to its high reductive stability. To 
reflect these points, the SEM results after long−term cycling were added to Supplementary Fig. 
18 and the relevant sentences were modified in the revised manuscript as follows: 
 
[Supplementary Fig. 19]

a) 0.05M Li(CB11H12) electrolyte and b) pristine electrolyte after 200 and 400 cycles at a 
current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2.
