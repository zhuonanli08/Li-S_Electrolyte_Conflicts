electrolytes needs to be carefully tuned and well understood. In 
most studies, a low sulfur loading (less than 1 mg cm –2 ) is used, 
but a high S loading is desired for high-capacity full cells. [ 25,26 ] 
 In this paper, lithium triﬂ uoromethyl-4,5-dicyanoimidazole 
(LiTDI) is studied for Li-S cells. We study the effect of LiTDI on 
the solubility of polysulﬁ des. The performance of this electro-
lyte was investigated with a high sulfur (i.e., practically usable) 
loading at 3 mg cm –2 under a high current density. 
 A comparison of the physical properties of electrolytes with 
either 1  M LiTFSI or LiTDI in DOL/DME (1/1, v/v) is shown 
in  Table  1 . Both electrolytes have a comparable conductivity 
and viscosity, suitable for the electrochemical needs, but an 
83% decrease in the Li 2 S 8 solubility is observed in the LiTDI 
electrolyte. Restricting the polysulﬁ de solubility to a low level is 
expected to improve the Li-S cell cycle life because of the sup-
pression of the polysulﬁ de shuttle mechanism, reduction of 
anode contamination, and the minimization of the loss of active 
material from the cathode. [ 22 ]

bility and electrolyte supporting salt,  7 Li and  17 O nuclear mag-
netic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic measurements were used 
to aid in understanding the solvation interactions. The  δ ( 7 Li) for 
the LiTDI and LiTFSI-based electrolytes without polysulﬁ des 
was –0.8 and –1.2 ppm (relative to Li + cations fully solvated by 
H 2 O molecules) ( Figure  1 a–c), respectively. The  δ ( 17 O) for the 
DME molecules shifts to ≈ –27.7 and –26.9 ppm in the LiTDI 
and LiTFSI-based electrolytes, respectively, from –24.3 ppm 
in a neat DOL/DME solvent mixture. The  δ ( 17 O) for the DOL 
molecules near 35 ppm shifts by 1–2 ppm. The smaller shift 
of  δ ( 17 O) for the DOL molecules relative to the DME molecules 
and signiﬁ cant broadening of the peaks for the latter sug-
gest that the Li + cations may be preferentially solvated by the 
DME. [ 27 ] It has been reported that the solvation of Li + cations

 Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have attracted wide interest due 
to their potential high capacity (1672 mAh g −1 ), low environ-
mental impact, and low cost. The commercialization of Li-S 
batteries is hindered, however, by capacity fading upon exten-
sive cycling. This is principally caused by the dissolution of the 
lithium polysulﬁ des generated during the discharge/charge 
reactions. [ 1,2 ] The soluble polysulﬁ des easily migrate out of 
the cathode and diffuse into the electrolyte. The “corrosive” 
electrolyte containing polysulﬁ des continuously reacts with 
the Li metal anode (the so-called shuttle mechanism), thereby 
increasing the cell impedance by forming an insulating layer 
on the anode. All these factors contribute to the poor cycling 
stability and low round-trip efﬁ ciency of Li-S batteries. [ 3 ] 
 A general approach to address the capacity fading issue is 
to conﬁ ne the soluble polysulﬁ de species in carbon scaffolds 
(the sulfur active material is an insulator so the carbon also 
serves as to the electron conductor for the sulfur) with various 
architectures including porous carbons, carbon nanotubes, 
and graphene. [ 4–9 ] The functionalization of carbon substrate 
or the direct incorporation of oxides such as metal-organic 
frameworks further immobilizes the polysulﬁ de anions at the 
atomic level. [ 10–13 ] Different polymers have also been studied 
to stabilize the polysulﬁ des through the formation of chemical 
bonds with the sulfur. [ 14–16 ] In contrast, reducing the poly-
sulﬁ de solubility by modifying the electrolyte formulation has 
been less explored, possibly due to the complicated interactions 
between the ions, solvent molecules, and polysulﬁ des during 
the electrochemical processes. [ 17,18 ] 
 The state-of-the-art electrolyte formulation used for Li-ion 
batteries (e.g., LiPF 6 in an ethylene carbonate/dimethyl car-
bonate (or other acyclic carbonate) solvent mixture) is not 
suitable for Li-S cells due to the incompatibility between the 
carbonate solvents and the polysulﬁ des and S 3 · radicals. [ 19,20 ] 
The most widely used electrolyte for Li-S cells therefore is

 To explain the Li + ion solvation with polysulﬁ des, ab initio

 One of the most critical issues for the practical utilization of 
Li-S batteries is to increase the cell performance with a high 
sulfur loading. Cathode wettability becomes a critical challenge 
as the sulfur loading increases. Another challenge associated 
with such cathodes is that rapid Li anode degradation occurs 
for cycled cells with a high areal capacity (AC). [ 33,34 ] Figure  3 
compares the charge–discharge curves and cycling performance 
of a cell with 3 mg S cm −2 loading. The cell is ﬁ rst discharged/
charged at a C/10 (i.e., 10 h) rate (0.5 mA cm −2 ) for 5 cycles, 
followed by a C/2 (i.e., 2 h) rate (2.4 mA cm −2 ) up to 300 cycles. 
Both of the cells with the 1  M LiTFSI or LiTDI in DOL/DME 
electrolytes have only a 300 mAh g −1 discharge capacity due to 
the large polarization for the thick sulfur cathode. The poly-
sulﬁ de additives will promote the polysulﬁ de dissolution to 
an equilibrium state and the EIS spectrum of the cell would 
be greatly reduced so that it would not only promote the elec-

 In conclusion, the use of LiTDI as a supporting electrolyte 
salt for Li-S batteries effectively decreases the polysulﬁ de solu-
bility thereby minimizing the polysulﬁ de shuttle mechanism 
(i.e., which causes a loss of active sulfur material from the 
cathode and the degradation of the Li metal anode). NMR and 
DFT calculation indicated a higher Li + solvation mainly with 
the DME in the DOL/DME mixture, which is different in the 
dilute solution from that of the concentrated pure DME solu-
tion. AIMD simulation reveals that the TDI anion coordination 
affects the polysulﬁ de disproportion to form Li 4 S 8 dimer and 
reduced the solubility. Furthermore, with the incorporation of 
0.1% Li 2 S 8 and 0.2  M LiNO 3 additives, excellent Li–S cell perfor-
mance with cathodes having a high sulfur (3 mg S cm −2 ) loading 
was obtained using the LiTDI-based electrolyte. 
 Experimental Section 
 The procedure to prepare S-coated multi-walled carbon nanotubes 
(S-CNT) was previously reported. [ 37 ] Elemental sulfur (S 8 , Alfa Aesar) 
and the MWCNTs (Cheap Tubes Inc.) (80:20 w/w) were mixed together 
by mechanical ball milling for 5 h. The mixture was then sealed in a 
polytetraﬂ uoroethylene (PTFE) container and heated to 155 °C in an oven 
for 24 h under the protection of inert Ar gas. Morphology observations 
were performed with a dual-focus ion beam (FIB) SEM (FEI Helios). 
The  7 Li and  17 O NMR spectra for these electrolytes were obtained from 
Fourier transformed free induction decays after a single pulse excitation 
at Larmor frequencies of 233.2 and 81.4 MHz, respectively, at ambient 
temperature (≈25 °C) using a 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Agilent, 
USA) equipped with a 5 mm liquid NMR probe (Doty Scientiﬁ c, USA). 
The  7 Li and  17 O chemical shifts for a 1  M LiCl in H 2 O solution and dilute 
water were used as external references (i.e., 0 ppm), respectively. The 
ionic conductivity of the electrolytes was measured by a conductivity 
meter (Orion 3 Star, Thermo Scientiﬁ c) at 30 °C. The shear viscosity ( η ) 
of the electrolytes was measured using a viscometer (Brookﬁ eld, DV2T) 
at 30 °C. All of the measurements were performed more than twice to 
ensure their reliability. 
 Calculation of the Variation of Hydrodynamic Radius, r s , Li /r Li : The 
temperature-dependent diffusion coefﬁ cients measured for LiTFSI and 
LITDI-based electrolytes plotted in Figure S1 (Supporting Information) 
were in well agreement with the Stokes–Einstein theory of diffusion, 
 D =  k B T /6π ηr s , where  k B ,  T ,  η , and  r s are the Boltzmann constant, 
absolute temperature, viscosity, and hydrodynamic radius of diffusing 
molecules, respectively. Based on the variations of diffusion coefﬁ cient 
and  δ ( 17 O) of solvent molecules, the interaction of DOL molecules 
to other electrolyte components was assumed negligible than the 
hydrodynamic radius of DOL molecules; ( r s,DOL ) will be the same to 
its actual size for all samples,  r s,DOL =  r DOL . And if both Li + and DOL 
were completely isolated from other components of the electrolyte, the 
diffusion ratio  D 0,Li / D 0,DOL =  r DOL / r Li , where  D 0,Li and  D 0,DOL were the 
diffusion coefﬁ cients of Li + and DOL without any interaction to other 
electrolyte components and  r DOL and  r Li were the sizes of DOL and Li + 
cation. Therefore, the variations of hydrodynamic radius of Li + cation 
were as follow: ( D 0,Li / D 0,DOL )/( D Li / D DOL ) = ( r DOL / r Li )/( r s , DOL / r s , Li ) =  r s , Li / r Li . 
This value was proportional to the degree of ion association between 
Li + cations and anions. Using the diffusion coefﬁ cients the ratio  r s,Li / r Li 
for LiTFSI and LiTDI-based electrolytes was calculated with and without 
0.05  M Li 2 S 8 . The larger value of  r s,Li / r Li reﬂ ected the larger size of Li 
complexes. 
 Electrochemical Measurements : The thin cathode with <1 mg S cm −2 
loading was prepared by mixing 80 wt% S-CNT composite powder, 
10 wt% Super P (Timcal Graphite & Carbon), and 10 wt% polyvinylidene 
ﬂ uoride (PVDF) binder together and adding  N -methyl-2-pyrrolidinone to 
form a slurry. The slurry was then coated onto an Al foil current collector 
and dried at 60 °C under vacuum overnight. A thick cathode with 
≈3 mg S cm −2 loading was prepared similarly by mixing 80 wt% S-CNT 
composite powder, 10 wt% Super P, 5 wt% LA133 binder (Chengdu 
indigo Co.), and 5 wt% styrene-butadiene-rubber(SBR) binder (MTI 
Corp.) and forming a slurry with water. This was coated onto a C-coated 
Al foil current collector (MTI Corp.). Electrodes were punched to a 
0.9 cm 2 diameter size. The electrolytes used were either 1  M LiTFSI or 
1  M LiTDI dissolved in a mixture of DOL and DME (1/1 v/v), respectively. 
Nominal Li 2 S x solutions were obtained by mixing stoichiometric Li 2 S and 
S 8 and stirring at 60 °C in oil bath for 1–10 h inside the glove box. The 
solubility was obtained by ﬁ rst adding extra amount of stoichiometric 
Li 2 S 8 followed by diluting the saturated solution gradually for every 
10 h until all species were dissolved. Half cells with Li metal foil (400 μm 
thick from MTI Corp.) as the anode and Celgard 3501 as the separator 
were assembled using CR2032 coin cells in an argon-ﬁ lled glove box. The 
active S/E was carefully controlled, [ 29 ] ranging from 15 g L −1 (15 g sulfur 
per liter of electrolyte) to 100 g L −1 . The discharge/charge cycles were 
tested galvanostatically using a Lanhe battery tester at room temperature 
in a voltage cutoff protocol between 2.8 and 1.5 V. The speciﬁ c capacity 
(SC) was based on the sulfur mass in the C/S composites, and the AC 
was calculated by the equation AC = SC × sulfur loading ratio. 
 Computational Details : For AIM-D simulation, the calculations were 
carried out with plane wave basis sets as implemented in the VASP 
code. [ 38,39 ] All the calculations were spin-polarized and carried out using 
the gradient corrected exchange-correlation functional of PBE (Perdew, 
Burke, and Ernzerhof) under the projector augmented wave method, 
with plane wave basis set up to a kinetic energy cutoff of 300 eV. [ 40,41 ] The 
van der Waals method of Grimme (i.e., DFT-D2) was used throughout 
AI-MD calculations with the convergence criterion of the total energy 
set to be within 1 × 10 −4 eV. [ 42 ] For the simulation of the electrolyte, the 
simulation box (18 × 18 × 18 Å 3 ) was consisted of randomly populated 
20 solvent molecules (DOL:DME = 1:1) and 5 salt molecules (i.e., LiTDI 
or LiTFSI) with a solvated Li 2 S 8 molecule with presumed liquid density 
of ≈0.96 g cm –3 . To investigate the thermodynamic stability of the 
system at room temperature, all the system were thermally equilibrated 
at  T = 300 K based on an Nose–Hoover paticles, volume, temperature 
(NVT) system ensemble with a time step of 1 fs, and the production run 
was obtained after thermal equilibration of ≈2.5 ps.
