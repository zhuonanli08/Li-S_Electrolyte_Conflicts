A B S T R A C T
The discharge and charge mechanisms of the Li-S battery involve the formation of soluble lithium polysulﬁde
species that can diﬀuse through the battery and cause issues related to capacity fade and poor Coulombic
eﬃciency. In order to control the behavior of the lithium polysulﬁdes, thiol-based electrolyte additives such as
biphenyl-4,4′-dithiol (BPD) were used to enhance capacity retention in lithium-sulfur batteries by controlling
polysulﬁde dissolution. In situ Raman spectroscopy, in situ UV–vis spectroscopy, and electrospray ionization
mass spectrometry show that an additional sulfur reduction process observed at ~2.1 V vs. Li/Li+ as a result of
BPD addition is associated with the formation of BPD-short chain polysulﬁde complexes such as BPD-Sn anion
(1≤n≤4). The interaction between BPD and short chain polysulﬁde postpones formation of soluble short chain
polysulﬁdes and alters the kinetics of the dissolution process. A smooth SEI/Li entangled phase is found on the
Li anode with BPD addition. The BPD additive increases the capacity retention of lithium-sulfur batteries,
mainly due to the formation of various BPD-polysulﬁde complexes which prevents polysulﬁde dissolution.
Comparison with other thiol-based additives shows that the optimal additive balances solubility and polysulﬁde-
additive stabilization.
1. Introduction
The ever-increasing demand for portable energy has prompted a
renewed interest in batteries and other energy storage devices [1].
While Li-ion batteries still dominate portable applications, other
chemistries have attracted recent attention because they promise
increased gravimetric or volumetric capacities over those available
from Li-ion chemistries. One of these emerging chemistries is the
lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries [2–8]. The theoretical capacity of the S
cathode is 1672 mAh g−1 which is over 5 times higher than the
theoretical capacity of the LiCoO2 cathode (~272 mAh g−1) in conven-
tional Li ion batteries [3,5,6]. In addition to the increased energy
density, elemental sulfur is readily available, environmentally friendly,
naturally abundant, and cheap.
Many problems associated with battery performance still attend the
practical application of Li-S batteries. These problems include the
stability of the cathode material [9,10], the dissolution of intermediate
species from the cathode [6,7], dendrite formation at the Li anode, and
the high reactivity of the Li metal anode which degrades the electrolyte
[4,11].
Many of these issues are caused by the formation of soluble
polysulﬁde intermediates during the reduction process. The electro-
chemical reduction of sulfur leads to the formation of various poly-
sulﬁde intermediates such as soluble long chain polysulﬁdes and less-
soluble short chain polysulﬁdes through a multistep process [12–15].
While the ﬁnal product at the end of discharge is the insoluble lithium
sulﬁde, the intermediate polysulﬁdes are soluble in the solvents
typically utilized for the Li-S battery [16,17]. The soluble polysulﬁde
intermediates dissolve into electrolyte upon cycling, leading to capacity
fade. Additionally, the polysulﬁde anions can freely migrate between
the cathode and anode, causing a so-called shuttle eﬀect that results in
low Coulombic eﬃciency and rapid capacity fade [18].
To address issues associated with polysulﬁde dissolution, both cell-
level and electrode level modiﬁcations to the Li-S battery have been
pursued [4–7,19]. At the cell level, interlayers and porous current
collectors are used to enhance the capacity retention [6]. In another
implementation, highly cross-linked polymers with various functional
groups prepared via thiol–ene chemistry were coated on the cathode
surface to increase the battery performance [20]. At the electrode level,
diﬀerent types of S-composites have been examined including various
carbon materials [5,6,19,21–24], cathode additives,[25–28] and func-
tional polymers based on inverse vulcanization and other schemes
[6,29–31]. However, these methodologies require very specialized
composites, the scaling of which for practical batteries is complicated.

solvents such as ionic liquids and ﬂuorinated ethers were used to
improve the Coulombic eﬃciency and prevent self-discharge in Li-S
batteries [34,35]. The use of a polysulﬁde additive as the functional
molecules creates a concentration gradient of polysulﬁde species at the
cathode/electrolyte interface and alleviates some polysulﬁde dissolu-
tion [36,37]. LiNO3 is another well-known electrolyte additive that
limits the shuttle eﬀect by forming a passivation layer on the Li anode
[38,39]. Beneﬁts of a protective layer on the Li anode have been
demonstrated using other electrolyte additives such as P2S5 and LiI
[40,41]. However, in most cases the interaction between electrolyte,
additive, and electrodes is uncertain.
In this work, we examine thiol-based electrolyte additives such as
biphenyl-4,4′-dithiol (BPD) to improve the performance of Li-S
batteries. In addition to reporting enhanced capacity retention as a
result of BPD addition to the electrolyte solution, we also provide a
comprehensive understanding of the origin of capacity retention
observed with these additives at both the sulfur-carbon cathode and
the Li-metal anode.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Electrolyte preparation
The basic electrolyte was 1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfo-
nyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 1:1 (v/v) solution of tetraethylene glycol
dimethyl ether (TEGDME, ≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL, anhydrous, Sigma-Aldrich). The thiophenol (≥99%), benzene-
1,4-dithiol (99%, GC), biphenyl-4,4′-dithiol (95%), 1,4-bis(4-mercap-
tophenyl)benzene (96%) and pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopro-
pionate) (≥95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without
further puriﬁcation. The additives were dissolved in the basic electro-
lyte solution described above at various concentrations.
2.2. Sulfur-carbon slurry and two-electrode Swagelok-cell
preparation:
The cathodes were cast from a slurry consisting of 60 wt% sulfur
(99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich), 30 wt% carbon black (Super-P Li, Timcal
Inc.), and 10 wt% polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF, Kynar 2801) binder
mixed with anhydrous N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma-Aldrich)
and stirred overnight. Preparation of the sulfur-carbon cathode was
described previously.[12] The sulfur loading was 0.7–1.2 mg-sulfur/
cm2.
2.3. Electrochemical measurement:
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) of the sulfur-carbon cathode were ob-
tained by using a 760C or 760D CH Instruments potentiostat at scan
rates of 0.1 mV/s in the two-electrode Swagelok cell and the 0.5 mV/s
in the in situ Raman cell between 3.2 V and 1.5 V (vs. Li/Li+),
respectively. The current density was estimated by using the geometric
area of the Al disk.
Charge and discharge processes in the Swagelok cell (nylon, 1″
inner diameter, Chicago Fluid System Technologies) were carried out at
the rate of 0.1 C (calculated using the weight of sulfur-carbon cathode)
between 3.2 V and 1.8 V by using an Arbin Battery Tester (Model BT
2043, Arbin Instruments Corp., USA). The counter/reference in the
two-electrode Swagelok cell was Li metal, and potentials are given with
respect to Li/Li+.
2.4. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy measurements:
The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were performed
in the lithium metal symmetric Swagelok cell using a Bio–Logic
Variable Multichannel Potentiostat (SP-150, Science Instruments,
France) The frequency range is in a 1000 kHz to 10 mHz range with
an amplitude of 100 mV.
2.5. In situ Raman Spectroscopy measurements:
In situ Raman spectroscopy experiments were performed using a
home-built glass cell described previously [12]. Fig. S1 shows the cell
design for the in situ Raman spectroscopy experiments. The working
electrode was a sulfur-carbon cathode (~0.01 g) supported on an Al
disk (3/8 in. in diameter, 99.99%, Alfa Aesar). Li metal was used as the
reference and counter electrode (99.9%, Alfa Aesar). The typical
acquisition time was 120 s per spectrum. In order to reach electro-
chemical equilibrium, the potential was held at each 100 mV step at
least 10 min before collecting the spectrum. The band intensities used
for the data analysis in this study were baseline corrected [12].
2.6. In-situ UV–vis absorption spectroscopy measurements:
The in-situ UV–vis measurements (Varian model Cary 500 Scan
UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer) were carried out in an air-tight
10 mm path length quartz cell (Starna, USA) with a cycling region
between 3.2 V and 1.5 V and a scan rate of 0.5 mV/s. The electrolyte
volume is 3.5 mL. The working electrode is the sulfur-carbon cathode
(~0.6 mg sulfur) on an Al plate. The reference and counter electrodes
were Li metal. In order to reach electrochemical equilibrium and avoid
a concentration gradient of dissolved polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte, the
potential was held at each 200 mV step at least 30 min before collecting
the spectrum.
2.7. Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS)
measurements:
The electrolyte was a mixture of 5 mM polysulﬁdes such as Li2S4
and 5 mM thiol-based molecule in a 1:1 (v/v) solution of TEGDME and
DOL. The polysulﬁde solutions were prepared from stoichiometric
mixtures of S8 and Li2S. Prior to measurement, the electrolyte was
diluted 1000-fold with ethanol. The microdroplets entering the mass
spectrometer undergo desolvation at a capillary temperature of 275 °C
in an LTQ-Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer. (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc,
San Jose, CA, USA) The Orbitrap MS was typically operated using the
following parameters: single-stage m/z range = m/z 60–500, m/z
resolution setting =100,000 at m/z 400, mass accuracy =2–5 ppm,
microscans =1, ion injection time =500 ms, tube lens voltage =110 V,
spray voltage =5 kV.
2.8. SEM measurements:
The surface morphology of the Li anode before and after battery
cycling was analyzed using SEM (JEOL, JSM-7000F). The Li-S
batteries were cycled in the cycle region between 3.2 V and 1.5 V and
emerged at 3.2 V. These Li anodes were then dried in an Ar-ﬁlled glove
box overnight and put into a well-sealed container to transfer to the
SEM instrument. However, brief ( < 10 s) exposure to air was unavoid-
able during loading of the sample into the SEM.

reduction peak marked with the red arrow in Fig. 1A. Thus, compared
to the basic electrolyte, addition of 25 mM BPD results in a lower
current density of the reduction peak at 1.95 V (black arrow) and
higher current density of the peak at 2.05 V is likely due to a reaction
involving both S and BPD.

3.6. The eﬀect of diﬀerent thiol-based additives on capacity fade
We next investigated other thiol-based molecules as additives to
evaluate the eﬀect of diﬀerent functionalities on Li-S battery perfor-
mance. Fig. 8 shows the battery performance with diﬀerent thiol-based
additives such as thiophenol (TP), benzene-1,4-dithiol (BD), biphenyl-
4,4′-dithiol (BPD), 1,4-bis(4-mercaptophenyl)benzene (MPB) and pen-
taerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP) charged and
discharged at a C rate of 0.1C between 3.2 V and 1.8 V. The capacity
of the Li-S battery with the 25 mM BPD addition is ~650 mAh/g-sulfur
after 100 cycles and falls only to ~575 mAh/g-sulfur after 300 cycles.
With the addition of 25 mM TP, 25 mM BD, or 1 mM MPB, the Li-S
capacity reaches ~500 mAh/g-sulfur after 100 cycles. The capacity of
Li-S battery with 25 mM PETMP addition results in even worse
capacity fade during cycling and reaches ~100 mAh/g-sulfur after
100 cycles.
We next discuss possible reasons for the diﬀerent behavior of these
thiol-based additives. PETMP is known to undergo a polymerization
process which apparently leads to irreversibly capacity fade [64]. MPB
exhibits low solubility in the TEGDME/DOL electrolyte and its eﬀect is
correspondingly small. While TP, BP, and BPD are all soluble in the
electrolyte, (-)ESI-MS results summarized in Table 3 show that BPD
forms more complexes with short chain polysulﬁdes relative to the
other two additives. There are many possible origins of the increased

4. Conclusions
The use of thiol-based electrolyte additives like BPD enhances the
capacity of Li-S batteries. The formation of BPD-short chain polysulﬁde
(Sn
2-, 1≤n≤4) complexes inhibits the formation of isolated short chain
polysulﬁdes such as S3
•- and changes the kinetics of formation of short
chain polysulﬁdes. The BPD-polysulﬁde complexes formed during
discharge decreases polysulﬁde dissolution which in turn yields better
capacity retention. Additionally, a smooth SEI/Li entangled phase on
the Li anode is found in the presence of BPD after cycling. Other thiols,
however, exhibit varying degrees of capacity retention, most likely due
to their diﬀerent solubilities. BPD is found to be the best performing
electrolyte additive and the reactivity of the BPD toward soluble
polysulﬁde intermediates and the resulting change in S reduction
kinetics shows that the S reduction process can be tailored to yield
beneﬁcial cycling behavior by tailoring reactive electrolyte additives.
