ORIGINAL PAPER
The enhanced performance of lithium sulfur battery with ionic
liquid-based electrolyte mixed with fluorinated ether
Hai Lu1 & Zhen Chen1 & Huiling Du1 & Kai Zhang2 & Jinlei Wang1 & Zhenzhong Hou1 & Jing Fang2
Received: 15 October 2018 /Revised: 20 November 2018 /Accepted: 22 November 2018 /Published online: 11 December 2018
# Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2018
Abstract
1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) was selected as a co-solvent of ionic liquid for fabricating the
functional electrolyte of Li-S batteries. The basic properties of the electrolyte and electrochemical performances of the cell with
alterative TTE contents were intensively investigated. It is found that the fluorinated ether helps to promote ion conduction in the
electrolyte, modify and stabilize SEI on Li metal, reduce charge transfer impedance, as well as restrict dissolution and shuttle of
polysulfides. Consequently, high reversible capacity, good cycle, and rate capability are achieved at moderate TTE addition. The
novel electrolyte guides a promising direction to construct Li-S batteries with high-energy density, long life, and high safety.
Keywords Lithium-sulfur battery . Fluorinated ether . Ionic liquid . Electrolyte . Polysulfide
Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery [1] has been one of most prom-
ising energy storage choice because of its higher energy den-
sity compared to other rechargeable batteries, enabling the
development of smaller and lighter device storing more ener-
gy. Moreover, sulfur is environment-friendly and abundant in
resources. These advantages make it highly suitable for the
application in an electric vehicle. However, the battery still
suffers from several drawbacks. The major one is that the
polysulfides (Li2Sn n > 2) generated during the charge/
discharge process can be dissolved in the liquid electrolyte
easily, which will migrate in the electrolyte and react with Li
anode, then shuttle between the cathode and anode [2]. It may
cause a series of problems such as (i) consuming active spe-
cies, (ii) corroding Li anode, and (iii) polarizing Li anode once
insoluble byproducts are deposited on the surface [3]. This is
the main reason for bad cycle stability, low columbic efficien-
cy, and low active material utilization of the Li-S batteries.
As the dissolution and shuttle of polysulfides all happen in
the electrolyte, optimization of the electrolyte composition is
considered to be an effective approach to solve the above
problems. Various ethers (e.g., DME, DOL, TEGDME) have
been widely used in Li-S batteries [4, 5], which can always
bring high discharge capacity but low cycle efficiency because
of polysulfide dissolution. Room temperature ionic liquid (IL)
is an advantageous solvent choice by the merit of high chem-
ical and electrochemical stability, wide temperature range, and
good dissociation ability of Li salts [6]. Moreover, there are
two distinctive functions of introducing ILs into the Li-S cell
system [7–9]: (i) reducing polysulfide dissolution owing to a
weak donor ability, which is strongly dependent on the anionic
structure; (ii) delaying polysulfide migration due to high vis-
cosity. These are together helpful for restraining the shuttle
phenomenon. But high viscosity is a double-edged sword,
which can also hinder the transport of Li+ and sacrifice ionic
conductivity of the electrolyte, resulting in a limited current
density and poor rate performance [10, 11]. To offset the de-
fect, researchers have generally chosen low-viscous DME
and/or DOL as co-solvent cooperating with ILs [12–14]. For
example, the battery performance was improved when ether-
functionalized IL P1,2O1TFSI and TEGDME was combined in
the optimum mass ratio of 7:3 [15]. Unfortunately, these
ethers have a relatively high donor number (DN) and prefer-
ably coordinate with Li+, so polysulfide dissolution is

and the ordinary ethers.
Recently, various fluorinated ethers have been reported to
restrict the shuttle and capacity fading of the Li-S batteries
[16–18], which draws on the experience of fluorinated carbon-
ate as a film-improving agent in Li-ion batteries. What is more
is that the multi-fluorine substituted chain structure is accom-
panied by low Li+ solvation ability, so fluorinated ether can
serve to minimize polysulfide solubility [17, 18]. Certainly,
they also have relatively lower viscosity compared to most
of ILs and the abundant flame-retardant element of F.
Therefore, it looks like that the fluorinated ether is more com-
plementary with IL than ordinary ethers in properties and can
be regarded as the suitable choice of co-solvent for ILs.
However, employing fluorinated ether in the ionic liquid-
based electrolyte for Li-S batteries is rarely reported.
Herein, a fluorinated ether named 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) was proposed to coordinate
with 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)
imide (EMITFSI) for fabricating a polysulfide-inhibiting electro-
lyte. By adding moderate content of TTE into the IL, the remark-
ably increased capacity and improved rate capability are present-
ed in the study.
Experimental
Sulfur/mesoporous carbon (CMK-3, XF Nano) composite
(S/C, with 68.3% of sulfur content) was prepared by co-
heating the sulfur and CMK-3 (7:3, wt/wt) at 155 °C for
12 h. The S/CMK-3 composite, carbon black (Super P
Timcal), and poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, 6020 Solef)
(8:1:1, wt) were mixed in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) to ob-
tain a homogeneous slurry. The electrode was prepared by
spreading the slurry onto Al foil with a doctor blade and dried
at 60 °C under a vacuum overnight, then cut into disks with a
diameter of ~ 14 mm. The typical sulfur loading on each elec-
trode is 1.5~1.8 mg cm−2.
Li-S cell was assembled by using as-prepared electrode
as a cathode, Li foil as an anode, and glass fiber GF/A as
the separator. The basic electrolyte is 1 M lithium
bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) amide (LiTFSI) dissolved in
EMITFSI (denoted as PE). The modified electrolytes are
prepared by adding TTE accounting for 25%, 50%, and
75% (mass ratio for PE) into the above basic electrolyte
(denoted as ET25, ET50, and ET75, respectively), as
shown in Table 1. In each cell, the ratio of electrolyte
amount to sulfur loading on the cathode is 15~18 μL mg−1.
To realize the solubility of polysulfides in the electrolytes,
S and Li2S with a molar ratio of 5:1 were mixed and added in
each electrolyte with magnetic stirring. The pictures of various
solutions after different rest times are recorded. The ionic con-
ductivity of the electrolyte was measured by electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of stainless steel (SS) symmet-
rical cell (Swagelok type) on a CHI660E electrochemical
workstation, and calculated using relation d/(Rb × A), where
Rb is the bulk resistance, d is the membrane thickness, and A is
the SS area [19]. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of SS/
Li cell was used for measuring the potential window of the
electrolyte with a scanning rate of 5 mV s−1. The Nyquist plots
of Li/Li symmetrical cells under different storage times were
recorded at the frequency range of 105~0.1 Hz.
For the Li-S cell, the discharge/charge tests were performed
in the potential range of 1.5∼3.0 V under a LAND CT2001A
system. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and EIS measurements
were conducted using the electrochemical workstation. The
scanning rate and voltage range of CV tests were 0.2 mV s−1
and 1.5∼3.0 V, respectively. The frequency range of EIS was
105∼0.1 Hz, with the perturbation amplitude of 5 mV. After
50 cycles, the Li anode was carefully extracted from the Li-S
cell and washed with glycol dimethyl ether, then further dried
in a glove-box for 4 h to remove residues. The morphology
characterization and element constitution of the electrode sur-
face were investigated by a scanning electron microscopy
(SEM, S-4800) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS), respectively.
Results and discussion
The solubility of polysulfides in various electrolytes was in-
vestigated firstly. Generally, the normal electrolyte without
any polysulfides presents colorless and transparent.
However, the color of PE once added with Li2S6 becomes
yellow and deepens gradually with time (Fig. 1a). It suggests
that the solubility of polysulfides still exists to some extent in
the IL-based electrolyte. When using TTE as co-solvent, the
solution seems lighter than PE at the same standing steps,
revealing further decreased polysulfide dissolution.
Moreover, more TTE content leads to the smaller amount of
polysulfides in the electrolyte. The influence of TTE on the
ionic conductivity and viscosity of the electrolyte was exam-
ined in Fig. 1b. Obviously, the ionic conductivity reduces and
the viscosity increases continually with the increase of the
TTE content. This means that the addition of TTE has a

viscosity. The potential windows of different electrolytes were
determined by LSV test (Fig. 1c). All the electrolytes were
kept stable within 5.0 V vs. Li/Li+ regardless of electrolyte

Besides, the flammability of electrolyte was also measured
by a self-extinguishing experiment by igniting a glass fiber
ball adsorbing the electrolyte and then removing the fire
source. It is found that all the glass fiber balls are difficult to
burn constantly and the flames extinguish very quickly, which
is profited from the excellent flame retardant ability of the IL
and fluorinated ether.
A solid electrolyte interface (SEI) will immediately form
when metallic Li contacts with organic electrolyte because of
high Li reactivity [20]. So the EIS measurement of Li/Li sym-
metrical cell was employed for investigating the state of SEI
on Li. As shown in Fig. 2a, the first semicircle at a high
frequency representing interfacial resistance (RSEI) is mainly
related to the transfer of Li-ion across the Li/electrolyte inter-
face [21]. RSEI formed in PE increases gradually with
prolonged time, demonstrating continuous reaction occurring
between the Li metal and the electrolyte. On the contrary, the
interfacial resistances for TTE-added electrolytes decrease at
the initial stage, probably attributed to the breaking of the
native surface film on the Li electrode and SEI rebuilding
[22]. Then these resistances increases slowly and tend to sta-
bilize. It illustrates that the achievement of a favorable inter-
face state needs a procedure. As an exception, ET25 provides
an unstable interface, because of the fluctuant impedance re-
sponse during the early storage and rapid growth at a later
period. Besides, the RSEI for ET25 and ET50 are much smaller
than PE and ET75, especially ET75 leads to highest value. It is
likely that the fluorinated ether in the electrolyte participates in
film formation on Li [23–25], and thus more amounts result in
thicker and denser SEI with higher resistance for ion transport.
The Li-S cells with various electrolytes were checked by
CV and galvanostatic charge/discharge test. As shown in
Fig. 3a, two reductive peaks located in 2.1 and 1.95 V are
observed in the first cathodic scan, standing for the reduction
of elemental sulfur to soluble polysulfides and then to the
insoluble Li2S2/Li2S, respectively. One oxidative peak during
the subsequent anodic process represents the formation of
Li2Sn (n > 2) and oxidized active sulfur [26–29]. The positions
and areas of these redox peaks show little change for TTE-
added electrolyte (ET50 as an example) during multiple scans
(Fig. 3b), reflecting good electrochemical stability. The shape
of CV curves is similar with some previous reports where
various ILs were employed in the electrolyte as well [15, 30,
31]. The potential plateaus in the charge/discharge profiles
(Fig. 3c) correspond to those redox peaks of CVs. It is note-
worthy that the TTE-added electrolytes own steady low-
potential plateau, while the plateau nearly disappears in the
PE, instead of a fast and continuous slope. Obviously, fluori-
nated ether in the electrolyte contributes to more specific ca-
pacities. These plateaus in the case of ET50 maintains stable
during subsequent cycles (Fig. 3d), well consistent with the
previous CV results.

various electrolytes. The quite low initial capacity
(413 mAh g−1) and big irreversible loss (capacity retention
of only 10.1% after cycling) occur in the pure IL-based elec-
trolyte. In comparison, the discharge capacity is sharply in-
creased if using TTE-added electrolytes. For example, a high

As a whole, the cycle and rate capability of the cell are
improved when selecting TTE as a support solvent for the
EMITFSI. It is mainly profited from the synergistic functions
of IL and fluorinated ether. On the one hand, the IL has low
solubility to polysulfides, while fluorinated ether serves to
further limit the dissolution of polysulfides because it is too
fluorinated to participate in Li+ solvation [32]. The dissolved
loss of active material and shuttle self-discharge is reduced
and consequently, more reversible capacities are reserved.
On the other hand, the lower viscosity of fluorinated ether
compared to IL increases ionic conductivity of the electrolyte,
thus the polarization will decline when the cell is operated at
high-rate conditions. In addition, it should be noted that fluo-
rinated ether is also a weak interaction with lithium salts and
usually thermodynamically unstable (low boiling point),
while IL is just the opposite. Therefore, the two solvent com-
ponents have perfect complementarity on basic properties and
favor the overall improvement of cell performance, including
capacity, cycle, rate, and safety.

To further understand the cycle difference for the cells in
different electrolytes, the surface morphologies of Li anodes
extracted from the cycled Li-S cells were examined. Fresh Li
anode (Fig. 6a) owns a smooth surface, while the morphology
becomes rough accompanied with many holes/cracks after
cycling in PE (Fig. 6b). The dissolved polysulfides can pene-
trate into bulk Li through these holes/cracks; hence, a stable
surface state is hard to be formed in PE due to the continuous
attack of polysulfides species towards Li [35]. The side reac-
tions between polysulfides and Li metal generate undesired
short chain sulfides deposited on the electrode surface, in-
creasing cell impedance (as shown in Fig. 5). However, after
adding TTE in ET50 (Fig. 6c), the Li metal becomes compact
and dense, forming an intact passivation film on it. The EDS
mapping (Fig. 6d) reveals the existence of fluorine-containing
species, with a small quantity of sulfur signals. It has been

polysulfides and Li metal. Therefore, Li corrosion is limited
and better Li morphology is preserved in the TTE-contained
electrolyte. However, excess fluorinated ether is instead harm-

stronger dissolution restraint of polysulfides are obtained.
While deficient fluorinated ether (ET25) leads to various in-
adequate improvements, including dissolution restriction,
shuttle suppression, film formation quality, and ion conduc-
tion. Here, it is suggested that the four roles of fluorinated
ether mentioned above are all beneficial to the cell perfor-
mance, which is a bit different from previously reported con-
centrated electrolyte consisting of solvated ionic liquid and
fluorinated ether [33].
Conclusions
Significant effects of fluorinated ether on the basic properties
of IL electrolyte and electrochemical performances of Li-S
batteries were studied in this paper. TTE has lower viscosity,
weaker dissolution of polysulfides compared to EMITFSI,
and good flame-retardant ability, thus suitable for cooperating
with the IL as a co-solvent. In addition, the fluorinated ether
helps to form a favorable protective film on Li, preventing the
Li corrosion by polysulfides. The cell in TTE-contained elec-
trolyte owns a higher reversible capacity, better cycle, and rate
capability than the one without it. A high capacity of
505 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles is obtained for the cell in
ET50. It is believed that the cell performance can be further
promoted if the type and ratio of fluorinated ether and IL were
optimized next.
