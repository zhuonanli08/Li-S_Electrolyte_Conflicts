ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have ultrahigh theoretical specific capacity, but the practical application is hindered
by the severe shuttle effect and the sluggish redox kinetics of the intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPSs). Effectively enhancing the
conversion kinetics of LiPSs is essential for addressing these issues. Herein, the redox kinetics of LiPSs are effectively improved by
introducing 6-azauracil (6-AU) molecules to the organic electrolyte to modulate the molecular orbital energy level of LiPSs. The 6-
AU as a soluble catalyst can form complexes with LiPSs via Li−O bonds. These complexes are liable to transform because of the
elevated HOMO and the reduced LUMO energy levels as compared to the dissociative LiPSs, resulting in small energy gaps (Egap)
and exhibiting stronger redox activity. Benefiting from the rapid conversion kinetics, the shuttling effect of LiPSs is alleviated to a
great extent, so that sulfur utilization is improved and the lithium electrode is protected. In addition, the introduction of 6-AU
modulates the deposition behavior of Li2S and eases the coverage of the cathode surface by the insulating Li2S layer. The Li−S
battery containing 6-AU provides superior capacity retention of 853 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at 0.2 C and shows remarkable high-
rate performance and retains a specific discharge capacity of 855 mAh g−1 at 5 C. This study accelerates the kinetics of Li−S batteries
by tuning the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of LiPSs, which opens an avenue for designing functional electrolyte additives.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, electrolyte additive, electrocatalysis, molecular orbital energy level, redox kinetics
1. INTRODUCTION
With the increasing demand for energy storage systems in
modern society, the development of high-energy-density
secondary batteries with long-term cycle stability has become
the focus.1−3 Li−S batteries are considered to be the next-
generation batteries due to their high theoretical energy
density of 2600 Wh kg−1.4−6 Despite the extensive research on
Li−S batteries over the years, they still face several persistent
challenges that hinder their commercial viability.7−10 First, due
to the electronic insulating properties of the two insulating
solid end-members of sulfur and Li2S and the intermediate
products of soluble LiPSs, the utilization of the active material
is poor and the sulfur cathode may lose electrical contact with
the conductive matrix during cycling, resulting in serious
attenuation of battery capacity.11,12 Because LiPSs can be
dissolved in ether-based electrolytes, the continuous dissolu-
tion of LiPSs could offer interspace for the reduction of
internal sulfur to make up for the low utilization of sulfur.13−15
However, due to the concentration gradient and the voltage
difference, LiPSs tend to migrate to the Li anode side, where
they undergo side reactions. This phenomenon can adversely
affect the Coulombic efficiency and cycling performance of the
battery.16−20 Furthermore, the electrochemical reaction rate
based on multistep solid−liquid−solid phase conversion is

large accumulation in the electrolyte, which would not only
increase the viscosity of the electrolyte but also sacrifice the
rate performance of the battery.21 Considering the above
factors, the conversion rate and the electrochemical activity of
LiPSs play key roles in the electrochemical performance.
Numerous efforts have been made to introduce catalytic
materials as additives or hosts into sulfur cathodes. Today, the
selected catalytic materials are focused on transition-metal-
based oxides,22,23 nitrides,24,25 phosphides,26 sulfides,27,28 and
borides,29 which could greatly accelerate the conversion
kinetics of LiPSs. However, transition-metal-based compounds
with the natural insulation or semiconductivity properties
hinder the charge transfer ability, which causes irreversible
deposition of Li2S2/Li2S on the cathode and the passivation of
the electrode. When the electrode is covered by inert sulfur
species, the adsorption and catalysis of LiPSs are gradually
deactivated, leading to battery gradation. Therefore, it is
feasible to develop host materials with high electrical
conductivity and catalytic activity for high reversible capacity
and superior cycle stability. For example, materials with high
electronic conductivity and semimetallic behavior, such as
ruthenium dioxide (RuO2),30 cobalt hydroxide (CoOOH),31
and cobalt and platinum metal,32,33 could serve as host
materials for Li−S batteries. Moreover, heterogeneous
structures have been developed, such as alloy catalysts, to
make up for the shortcomings of a single metal by adjusting the
structure of the electron cloud. By screening suitable alloy
catalytic and exploiting the synergy among components, the
cathode with high electrochemical activity for driving the redox
reactions of LiPSs can be achieved.34−36 These materials can
affect the redox process at the electrolyte/electrode interface.
However, most LiPSs are dissolved in the electrolyte, and the
heterogeneous reaction limits the catalytic effect. Therefore,
soluble catalysts in the electrolyte are expected to accelerate
the kinetics of LiPSs.
As a key component that affects Li+ transport and
conductivity, the electrolyte plays a crucial role in the
construction of high-performance Li−S batteries. Introducing
suitable additives into the electrolyte can not only stabilize the
cathode−electrolyte interface but also protect the Li anode.
Several noteworthy studies have explored the enhancement of
conversion kinetics in LiPSs through the use of soluble
catalysts. For instance, 1,5-bis(2-(2-(2-methoxyethoxy)-
ethoxy)ethoxy)anthra-9,10-quinone37 and 2,5-di-tert-butyl-
1,4-benzoquinone38 have demonstrated their effectiveness as
catalysts for LiPSs, leading to improved battery redox kinetics.
Importantly, catalytic additives can promote the conversion of
LiPSs by improving the electron transfer process or adjusting
the frontier molecular orbital (FMOs) energy level of LiPSs.
This catalysis could not only alleviate the shuttle effect caused
by the accumulation of a large amount of LiPSs in the
electrolyte but also effectively accelerate the electrochemical
kinetics of the battery and enhance the rate property. However,
the catalytic mechanism of electrolyte additives needs to be
furtherly investigated.
Herein, we introduce 6-azauracil (6-AU) as the soluble
catalyst to enhance the kinetics of LiPSs, which could adjust
the FMOs energy level of LiPSs. Theoretical calculations and
experimental results demonstrate that 6-AU can form
complexes with Li2Sx (x = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8) through Li−O
bonds. The complexes have higher HOMO energy level, lower
LUMO energy level, and the resulting smaller energy gap
(Egap) than dissociative LiPSs, which makes the complexes
facile to be oxidized and reduced. Because of the modulating of
the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of LiPSs via 6-AU, the
redox kinetics of LiPSs is effectively accelerated (Scheme 1).
The battery with the 6-AU electrolyte additive provided an
excellent capacity retention of 853 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at
0.2 C, which is superior to that in the blank electrolyte (530
mAh g−1). Moreover, the battery with 6-AU electrolyte
additive exhibits excellent rate properties and retains a specific
discharge capacity of 855 mAh g−1 at 5 C. This study provides
a new strategy for developing functional catalyst additives.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

prepared by dissolving 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI) and 0.2 M LiNO3 in dimethyl ether/1,3-dioxolane
(DME/DOL, 1:1 ratio by volume). 6-AU electrolyte was formulated
by adding 6-AU molecules to the blank electrolyte (Figure S1). The
optimal content of 6-AU was investigated by adding different amounts
(10, 30, and 50 mM) into the blank electrolyte.

S/G composite was synthesized through a traditional melt-diffusion
method. Specifically, 300 mg of sulfur powder and 100 mg of
commercial graphene (The Sixth Element, Inc., SE1233) were
physically mixed and then calcined at 155 °C for 15 h to produce
the S/G composite. The S/G electrode was fabricated by dispersing

to the cathode sides. The assembled batteries were first discharged
galvanostatically to 2.09 V at a constant current of 0.257 mA, followed
by discharging at 2.05 V for 20000 s. The sulfur cathode was obtained
by disassembling the recycled batteries to observe the surface
morphology of the cathode by SEM.
2.5. Material Characterization. The crystal structures of
materials were measured by an X-ray powder diffractometer (Rigaku
mini Flex II). The morphologies of the composite were investigated
via a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7800F) coupled with
an elemental mapping apparatus (EDS). Nitrogen adsorption−
desorption isotherms (BET, ASAP 2460) were performed to
determine the surface areas of the materials. Thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA, Mettler-Toledo) was used to determine the sulfur
content in the composite, which was heated from room temperature
to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under an argon atmosphere. To
define the surface chemical states of the complex, X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi) was used. The
functional groups of the complex were confirmed by Fourier
transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet IS10). The nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of 7Li in Li2S6 and Li2S6 with 6-
AU were measured by a Bruker AVANCE III 600 M NMR
spectrometer.

To further verify the binding form of 6-AU to LiPSs, MEP
mapping of the 6-AU molecule was first drawn to provide a
visual representation of the active sites (Figure 3a). In MEP
mapping, the red regions represent the electron-rich area, the
blue regions represent the electron-deficient area, and green
indicates electrostatic potentials with zero electrostatic
potential. The electron-rich regions of the MEP mapping are
preferred for the electrophilic attack, while electron-deficient
regions of the MEP mapping are preferred for the nucleophilic
attack. The MEP mapping of 6-AU shows that the region
around the hydrogen atom is a blue electron-deficient region,
while the region around the oxygen atom shows a strong red
electron-rich region, which is caused by the lone pair of
electrons of the oxygen atom. The MEP mapping indicates that
the position containing oxygen atoms in the 6-AU molecule
has the strongest activity, which is consistent with the binding
mode of 6-AU molecules and LiPSs calculated by the DFT

6-AU would form Li−O bonds and generate stable complexes.

comparable to that of hydrogen bonds,40 suggesting that the

would not impede the conversion of LiPSs. The above
calculation results are consistent with the experimental results
of the NMR and FTIR spectra.
In order to understand the specific mechanism of how 6-AU
reacts with LiPSs, first-principles calculations on LiPSs and
LiPSs−6-AU systems were performed. As is well-known, the
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) exhibits the
ability to donate an electron, while the lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) exhibits the ability to accept an
electron. These orbitals are commonly termed FMOs.11,46 The
HOMO and LUMO energy levels are closely related to the
redox ability of the complex. The higher HOMO energy
represents that the material loses more electrons, and the lower
LUMO energy represents that the material is easier to accept
electrons. Taking the combination process of Li2S4 as an
example, the distribution of HOMO and LUMO orbits of
Li2S4 and Li2S4 with 6-AU calculated by the M062X/
def2TZVP level of theory are shown in Figure 4a. The
HOMO of Li2S4 is −7.26 eV, and the LUMO is −0.68 eV.
Compared with dissociative Li2S4, the HOMO of the Li2S4−6-
AU system is increased by 0.25 eV and the LUMO is decreased
by 0.96 eV. The delocalization of the HOMO energy level of
the Li2S4−6-AU system is mainly located on Li2S4, and the
increase of the HOMO suggests that the Li2S4−6-AU system is
more easily to lose electrons than the dissociative Li2S4
molecule, making the charging reaction easier to occur. The
delocalization of the LUMO of the Li2S4−6-AU system is
mainly located on 6-AU, and the reduction of the LUMO
energy level shows that the system can accept electrons more
easily than the dissociative Li2S4 molecule, making the
discharge reaction easier to occur. Simultaneously enhancing

provide a detail of how 6-AU effectively enhances the redox
kinetics of LiPSs. In other words, 6-AU can combine with
LiPSs that exist as free radicals or neutral molecules in the
electrolyte via Li−O bonds to form complexes.47 Compared
with dissociative LiPSs, these complexes can significantly raise
the HOMO and lower the LOMO energy level, thereby
reducing the Egap and accelerating the electrode reactions.

were studied by using CR2032 coin batteries. The concen-
tration of the additive has a slight effect on the electrochemical
performance (Figure S2), and the electrolyte containing 30
mM 6-AU was used. The electrochemical properties of the
batteries are significantly influenced by the electrolyte additive
of 6-AU, and the initial discharge/charge curves of the
batteries at 0.2 C are shown in Figure 5a. The capacity of the
cell with the 6-AU electrolyte is 1327 mAh g−1, which is
superior to that in the blank electrolyte (1250 mAh g−1). In
addition, the battery utilizing the 6-AU electrolyte exhibits
lower polarization (163 mV) than the blank electrolyte. The
polarization is defined as the voltage difference between the
respective potentials at the midpoint of the discharge and
charge capacities. The increase in capacity and decrease in
polarizability are mainly contributed by the enhanced kinetics
of the redox process. The rate property of the cells with the 6-

electrolyte (2.28 and 2.00 V), and the oxidation peak of the
battery using 6-AU appears at lower potential (2.29 V) than
the battery using a blank electrolyte (2.28 V). The
introduction of 6-AU causes the reduction peak to shift in a
positive direction and the oxidation peak to shift in a negative
direction and results in a higher peak current compared to that
in the blank electrolyte. These observations further confirm the
enhanced redox kinetics of LiPSs through complexation with
6-AU. In addition, the CV curves in the 6-AU electrolyte
achieve 3 cycles of stable cycling at 0.1 mV s−1 and exhibit little
change in the peak current density, indicating high reversibility
of the battery (Figure S3). To further study the kinetics of the
cathode, the CV curves of the batteries with blank electrolyte
and the 6-AU electrolyte at various scan rates (0.1−0.5 mV
s−1) are exhibited in Figure 6b,c. As the scan rate increases,
charges accumulate at the cathode, leading to a shift of the
reduction peak to a lower potential and the oxidation peak to a
higher potential.
Furthermore, the cathodic and anodic peaks exhibit a linear
relationship with the square root of the scan rates, indicating
that the rate of the redox reaction is controlled by the diffusion
process (Figure 6d−g). On the basis of the Randles−Sevcik
equation, the electrolyte with 6-AU possesses the fastest Li+
transfer than the blank electrolyte.48,49 The Nyquist plots of
the batteries with the 6-AU electrolyte and the blank
electrolyte after 100 cycles are shown in Figure 6h, which
could provide further insight into the kinetics of the battery
process. The fitting curves were obtained based on the
equivalent-circuit diagram. The fitted parameters, including the
solution resistance (Rs), the charge-transfer resistance (Rct),
the resistance of the Li2S/Li2S2 layer (RL), and the constant
phase element (CPE) are listed in Table S2.50 The Nyquist
plots show that the Rs in the 6-AU electrolyte is larger than
that in the blank electrolyte because the addition of the
additive increases the viscosity of the electrolyte. However, the
introduction of 6-AU decreased the Rct, which indicates the
improved electrochemical activity of the Li−S batteries.
Besides, the electrolyte with 6-AU has a small RL because of
the fast transformation of LiPSs. The above results
demonstrate that the addition of 6-AU enhances the
electrochemical activity of the batteries and facilitates the fast
transformation of LiPSs (Figure 6i).

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we developed a catalytic electrolyte additive, 6-
AU for Li−S batteries. By adjusting molecular orbital energy
levels of LiPSs vis the formation of Li−O bond, the redox
kinetics of intermediate LiPSs is effectively accelerated.
Moreover, the coverage of the cathode surface by inert sulfur
species is improved, and the porous electrode structure can
maintain unimpeded Li+ diffusion channels, which sustains
high utilization and high conductivity of the cathode. The Li−
S battery containing the soluble catalyst 6-AU showed high-
capacity retention (853 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at 0.2 C) and
maintained a reversible capacity of 798 mAh g−1 after 180
cycles at 0.5 C. This work provides a strategy for designing
functional catalysts for Li−S batteries.
