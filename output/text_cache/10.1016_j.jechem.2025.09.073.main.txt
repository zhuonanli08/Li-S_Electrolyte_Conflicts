a b s t r a c t 
Weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) promote the formation of anion-driven solid electrolyte interphases 
(SEI), enabling stable lithium metal batteries. However, current strategies involving alkylated modiﬁca-
tion, steric hindrance incorporation, coordinated oxygen (O) regulation, and selective ﬂuorination face 
poor-diversity interfacial chemistry, high cost, or environmental concerns. Here, we propose a 
heteroatom-substitution strategy to design a WSE composed of lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) 
and 1,4-oxathiane (OTA) as a single solvent. Substituting oxygen with sulfur in conventional 1,4-
dioxane (1,4-DX) generates OTA with a modulated dipole and charge distribution, weakening Li+-OTA 
coordination while promoting anion-involved solvation sheath. This unique solvation structure triggers 
the formation of an inorganic-rich SEI with sulfur-containing species, enabling high Li plating/stripping
coulombic efﬁciency and stable Li||Li symmetric cells cycling for 1000 h. Beneﬁting from the superior
interfacial chemistry and wettability of the electrolyte to the LiFePO4 cathode, full cells exhibit excep-
tional cycling stability even at low negative-to-positive (N/P) ratios. A pouch cell coupled with
3.58 mAh cm−2 LiFePO4 and 20 lm Li (N/P ∼1.15) maintains 88.77 % capacity after 150 cycles. This work
shows a ﬂuorine-free solvent design paradigm for advanced WSEs, providing novel insights toward stable
LMBs.

1. Introductio n
Lithium (Li) metal batteries (LMBs) are regarded as a pivotal 
solution to overcome the energy density limits of current energy 
storage technologies, owing to the ultrahigh theoretical speciﬁc
capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and the lowest electrochemical potential
(−3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode) of Li metal anode [1–3]. 
However, the practical implementation of LMBs is hindered by 
interfacial instability between the reactive Li metal and traditional
electrolytes [4]. This instability originates from a strong correlation
between electrolyte solvation structures and interfacial chemistry
[5]. The solvation determines the conﬁgurations of Li+ solvation 
sheath, which in turn governs the chemical compositions and
mechanical properties of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the
Li metal anode [6].

In recent years, efforts to regulate solvation structures have 
focused on establishing anion-involved solvation conﬁgurations, 
especially in the widely recognized high-concentration electrolytes 
(HCEs), localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs), and 
weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) [7]. In these systems, anions 
in the primary Li+ solvation sheath can decompose into stable inor-
ganic species that form protective layers on the Li metal anode, 
mitigating electrolyte degradation and active Li loss [8]. This mech-
anism differs from the conventional carbonate/ether-based

<<PAGE 2>>
electrolytes, where strong Li+-solvent interactions dominate. These 
systems typically form a solvent-dominated solvation sheath and 
organic-rich SEI, which exhibit limited Li+-conducti vity and poor
mechanical strength, failing to suppress dendritic Li penetration
and triggering short circuits and rapid capacity decay [9]. Com-
pared with high-cost HCEs and LHCEs, WSEs composed of weakly 
coordinating solvents and weakly dissociating Li salt demonstrate 
the competitive Li+-anion coordination even in diluted electrolytes.
These unique properties not only ensure fast Li+ transport but also
improve cost-effectiveness, making WSEs a promising candidate
for advanced LMBs [10].

The prevailing WSEs reconstruct the Li+ solvation structure 
depending on the molecular engineering of the solvent [11]. The 
common molecular engineering strategies include the alkyl carbon 
chain modiﬁcation at terminal (e.g., extending methyl to ethyl in 
triethyl orthoform ate (TEOF) to enhance steric hindrance, reducing
Li+-solvent interaction and increasing aggregates (AGGs) to 78 %
[12]), the incorporation of high-steric groups into the skeleton such
as methyl, methoxyl [13], and cyclopentyl (e.g., cyclopentyl in 
cyclopentylmethyl (CPME) weakening solvation power via steric
effects, achieving 99.4 % Coulombic efﬁciency (CE) [14]), the con-
tent regulation of oxygen (O) atoms to chelate Li+ (e.g., by reducing 
the number of oxygen atoms to one and extending the alkyl chain 
to a propyl group, diglyme (DIG) is reconﬁgure d into dipropyl ether
(DPE), thereby changing the solventization mode from being dom-
inated by solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) to being dominated
by AGGs, achieving 99.42 % Li plating/stripping CE [15]), and the 
selective ﬂuorination at the methyl or methylene sites (e.g., ﬂuo-
roethylene carbonate/2,2,2-triﬂuoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEC/ 
FEMC) reduces the electron density of oxygen atoms and incorpo-
rates the F group [16]). The ﬁrst three approaches regulate the 
molecular dipole via altering the functional groups with the assem-
bly of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and O elements to weaken the
interaction of Li+ and O and promote the increase of contact ion
pairs (CIPs) and AGGs proportion [17]. Although the anion-
derived inorganic components can effectively stabilize the Li 
anode, solvent decomposition products also play a critical yet
underexplored role in interphase chemistry [12]. Owing to elemen-
tal composition, conventional C-/H-/O-containing solvents tend to
yield organic species in the SEI [18]. As to the ﬂuorination, it lowers 
the electron density on the O atom via the strong electron-
withdrawing effect of ﬂuorine (F), weakening Li+-solvent interac-
tion and promoting anion-dominated solvation structures [16]. 
This facilitates the formation of an inorganic-rich interface, espe-
cially lithium ﬂuoride (LiF). However, ﬂuorinated solvents often
suffer from high synthetic costs, insufﬁcient lithium salt solubility,
and environmental concerns [19–21]. Consequently, within the 
framework of the anion-dominated solvation, it is highly desirable 
to rationally formulate ﬂuorine-free solvent with novel insights for
developing WSEs and concurrently achieving SEI formation with
these featured species to stabilize LMBs [22]. 
Here, we propose a heteroatom-substitution strategy to tailor a 
unique WSE that consists of lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) 
and 1,4-oxathiane (OTA) as a single solvent system for stable LMBs. 
The OTA molecule with good wettability is derived from the conven-
tional 1,4-dioxane (1,4-DX) by substituting one oxygen (O) atom with 
a sulfur (S) atom. Experimental characterizations and theoretical sim-
ulations reveal the appropriate modulation of O atom charge and 
molecular dipole moment for OTA, which sustains the weak Li+-OTA 
coordination and promotes more Li+-anion interactions, as well as 
the preferential reduction capacity. These specialties induce the forma-
tion of inorganic-enriched SEI containing S species, leading to an
improved Li plating/stripping coulombic efﬁciency (CE) of 98.9 %,
stable overpotentials, and compact Li deposition morphology. When
paired with LiFePO4 cathodes, Li||LiFePO4 cells with OTA-dedicative
electrolyte demonstrate excellent battery capacity retentions under
different negative-to-positive (N/P) ratios. Even in pouch cells with 
high-loading LiFePO4 cathodes (∼3.58 mAh cm−2 ) and a thin Li metal 
anode (20 lm), a capacity retention of 88.77 % i s maintained over 150
cycles. The comparison data with the electrolytes designed by other
molecules are shown in Table S1. This work offers a new molecular 
design concept for ﬂuorine-free WSEs and guides future exploration
for advancing LMBs.

2. Results and discussion
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) has been one of the most commonly used 
electrolyte solvents in Li-based battery chemistries , with a high
dielectric constant, low viscosity, and good compatibility with Li
metal [23]. However, DOL is prone to ring-opening polymerization 
in the presence of Lewis acids, such as PF6
−, DFOB−, and FSI−anions
[24–26]. As shown in Fig. S1, when 1.0 M (which signiﬁes ‘‘mol 
L−1 ’’) LiFSI was dissolved in DOL, the electrolyte spontaneously
forms a gel-like polymer with no ﬂuidity [27]. This instability ren-
ders DOL unsuitable as a single solvent for electrolyte preparation,
despite its weak Li+ solvation ability [28]. To optimize the solvent 
structure, the regulation of the member of the carbon ring was car-
ried out, yielding the 1,4-DX molecule (Fig. 1a), which had been 
reported as a representative weakly coordinating solvent in Li-
ion batteries [29]. Due to its dominance of weak Li+ solvation, 
1,4-DX exhibits a relatively limited LiFSI solubility (<2.0 M,
Fig. S2). Its poor compatibility with Li metal is also revealed by 
the Li plating/stripping tests of Li||Li symmetric cell and Li||Cu cell
with 1.5 M LiFSI-1,4DX (Figs. S3 and S4), which display unstable 
voltage proﬁles and low and ﬂuctuating CE [30].
Replacing one O atom in 1,4-DX with an S atom obtains OTA. 
Such substitution disrupts the balanced dipole distribution of 
1,4-DX and reconﬁgures the charge distribution due to the differ-
ence in electronegativity between the O and S atom (3.44 vs.
2.58) [31–33]. The rationally-designed OTA shows the multifunc-
tional advantages: (1) it retains a weak solvating feature that guar-
antees the formation of anion-dominated solvation structure; (2) it 
improves compatibility with Li metal, attributed to a stable SEI
formed via the synergistic reduction of the FSI−anion and the sol-
vent; (3) it enhances LiFSI solubility to 2.0 M due to the changed
dipole moment (Fig. S5); (4) it offers good wettability toward 
high-loading electrodes, as demonstrated in later section.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal the charge 
distribution of various solvent molecules through electrostatic 
potential (ESP) analysis (Fig. 1 b) [34–36]. The three solvents exhi-
bit different ESPmin and ESPmax values: −1.42 and 1.01 eV for DOL, 
−1.48 and −0.80 eV for 1,4-DX, and −1.43 and 0.89 eV for OTA, 
respectively. The calculated ESPmin −ESPmax value of OTA is 
−2.32 eV, which is located between those of DOL (−2.43 eV) and 
1,4-DX (−2.28 eV). A larger | ESPmin −ESPmax| value indicates higher
solvation capability [37]. Therefore, OTA retains a moderate Li+ sol-
vation ability, which is stronger than 1,4-DX but weaker than DOL. 
To further clarify Li+-solvent coordination strength, we examine 
several parameters, including charge density on the O atom, 
molecular dipole moment, and both binding energy and bond
length of Li+-solvent complexes. As shown in Fig. 1(c ), DOL exhibits 
an electron density of −0.393 e Å−3 on each oxygen atom. In con-
trast, 1,4-DX shows a lower density of −0.341 e Å−3 due to an addi-
tional carbon atom in the ring. OTA exhibits an intermediate 
charge (−0.351 e Å−3 ), consistent with the ESP results. The molec-
ular dipole moment that determines the strength of ion-dip ole
interaction (namely, solvation) also reﬂects this situation [38]. 
DOL shows a strong dipole of 1.23 D, while 1,4-DX is nearly nonpo-
lar (0.0004 D). OTA displays a moderate dipole of 0.23 D, indicating 
the enhanced polarity upon sulfur substitution, which accounts for 
its improved LiFSI solubility. The binding energy of the solvent

To reveal the solvation chemistry of the electrolytes, molecular 
dynamics (MD) simulations using a periodic box model (Fig. S7) 
were performed to count the complexes in the Li+ solvation struc-
tures, which are dependent on the radial distribution functions
(RDFs) and coordination number (CN) [41]. As shown in Fig. 2(a), 
in the 2.0 M LiFSI-EC-EMC electrolyte, due to the strong solvating 
power and Li+ coordination ability of EC (Fig. S8), a sharp Li+ –OEC 
peak appears at 2.37 Å, with the highest g(r) intensity and a CN 
value of 0.42, far exceeding those of Li+ –OEMC and Li+ –OFSI 
− . This 
suggests that EC dominates the initial Li+ solvation shell with
minor contributions of EMC and FSI anions. These results are fur-
ther conﬁrmed by the various Li+ solvation patterns in Fig. 2(b) 
and Fig. S9 [42], where solvent-dominated conﬁgurations includ-
ing Li+-3EC, Li+-3EC-EMC, Li+-4EC, Li+-2EC, and Li+-2EC-EMC collec-
tively account for 89.41 %. Only a small proportion of Li +-FSI−
interaction is observed, demonstrating a solvent-dominated solva-
tion structure in the reference electrolyte (Fig. 2c). By contrast, the 
2.0 M LiFSI-OTA electrolyte shows distinct coordination behaviors.
As shown in Fig. 2(d), the Li+ –OOTA RDF shows a modest peak at 
2.37 Å, while a Li+ –OFSI 
− peak is located at 2.43 Å. The corresponding 
average CNs are 0.67 for OTA and 1.50 for FSI− , suggesting that FSI− 
anions dominate the primary solvation shell of Li+ with the limited
participation of weakly coordinating OTA. Statistical analysis fur-
ther conﬁrms that almost all FSI−anions interact with Li+, along
with variable numbers of OTA molecules (Fig. 2e and Fig. S10). 
These results demonstrate that the solvation sheath in 2.0 M 
LiFSI-OTA electrolyte is enriched in CIPs and AGGs, namely anion
richness (Fig. 2f) [43].

Raman spectroscopy was experimentally employed to probe the 
solvent/anion-derived solvation structures of the electrolytes. As
shown in Fig. S11, EC solvent exhibits two characteristic bands at 
713 and 893 cm−1 , corresponding to the symmetrica l stretching
vibration of the ring skeleton and the stretching vibration of C–C
bond [44]. Upon dissolving LiFSI, a broad band of 700–750 cm−1 
appears, overlapping the vibration of EC-involved and anion-
involved bands (Fig. 2g). Owing to their similar positions, it is dif-
ﬁcult to assign these features. Nevertheless, in both 1.0 M LiFSI-EC-
EMC and 2.0 M LiFSI-EC-EMC electrolytes, coordinated EC species 
and free anions dominate the spectra, verifying that the carbonate
solvents govern Li+ solvation shell. For OTA solvent, the dominant
band at 663 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the
C–S bond (Fig. S12), which can be preserved in the OTA-based elec-
trolytes. Upon LiFSI dissolution, the featured bands of FSI− anion 
emerge at 710–760 cm−1, assigned to the symmetric and antisym-
metric S–N–S stretching vibrations [45]. In Fig. 2(h), the ﬁrst band 
at 718 cm−1 belongs to free FSI−anions, namely SSIP [46], while the 
other two bands originate from coordinated anions including CIP
and ionic AGG modes [47]. In the 1.0 M LiFSI-OTA electrolyte, the
ratios of SSIP, CIP, and AGG are 0.51 %, 42.41 %, and 57.08 %, respec-
tively. Such a high percentage of coordinated anions at the com-
mon LiFSI concentration conﬁrms the weak-solvation effect of 
OTA and the facile formation of anion-rich solvation. Increasing
the LiFSI concentration to 2.0 M slightly raises the SSIP fraction,
while a portion of CIP converts into AGG, which sharply rises to
92.74 % (Table S2). This conversion reveals the participation of 
abundant FSI− anions into the primary Li+ solvation shell. Further 
evidence for the weak OTA-Li+ coordination is provided by 7Li
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. As shown in
Fig. 2(i), compared to the carbonate-based electrolytes, a signiﬁ-
cant downﬁeld chemical shift is detected in the 2.0 M LiFSI-OTA 
electrolyte. This indicates the reduced electron shielding and the
decreased local electron density around the Li+ nucleus due to
the coordination with electronegative anions [48]. Such strong 
Li+-anion interactions facilitate a lower desolvation barrier and 
the formation of an SEI with featured inorganic components.

Electrolyte wettability was also evaluated via contact angle 
measurement to examine its physical effects on internal resistance
and battery cycle life (Fig. 3g and Fig. S18) [52]. On the separator 
surface, only a small difference is observed between the carbonate 
electrolyte (40.3°) and the OTA-based electrolyte (48.9°) after 300 s 
of wetting. In contrast, for ultrahigh-loading LiFePO4 electrodes, 
the 2.0 M LiFSI-OTA electrolyte exhibits superior wettability with 
an extremely low contact angle of 9.6°, far below 27.6° for the car-
bonate electrolyte. The exceptional wettability of the OTA solvent
in LiFePO4 cathodes stems from the unique physicochemical prop-
erties of sulfur atoms. Compared to oxygen, the higher polarizabil-
ity of sulfur enhances dipole-p interactions and van der Waals
forces at carbon interfaces, collectively overcoming the intrinsic
hydrophobicity
of
carbon
materials
[53–55]. This physical 
adsorption-based mechanism contrasts sharply with the chemical 
coordination mechanism observed in high-nickel cathodes, high-
lighting the necessity for precise optimization of electrolyte design 
based on cathode interface chemistry. This improvement, attribu-
ted to favorable interfacial surface energy, facilitates electrolyte
inﬁltration and full-scale activation of active materials [56]. As 
shown in Fig. S19, Li||LiFePO4 full cells with the two electrolytes 
display a clear difference in capacity after various resting periods. 
The 2.0 M LiFSI-OTA electrolyte achieves a higher capacity until 8 
standing resting, as well as cycling lifespan and CE. These results
demonstrate that electrolyte wettability toward high-loading elec-
trodes is a critical limiting factor for long-term stability, and OTA
can overcome the inﬁltration limitations.
Systematic investigations were conducted to assess the electro-
chemical reversibility of Li plating/stripping using Li||Li and Li||Cu
cells (Fig. 4a–d). At a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and a capacity 
of 1.0 mAh cm−2 , the Li||Li symmetric cell with the 2.0 M LiFSI-OTA
electrolyte exhibits stable cycling for up to 1000 h (Fig. 4a). 
Throughout cycling, the polarization voltage increases only 
slightly. In contrast, the carbonate-based electrolyte sustains 
stable operation for only 320 h, after which the voltage exhibits
large ﬂuctuations due to a ‘‘soft” short circuit (Fig. S20). A similar 
trend is observed in pre-lithiated Li||Cu cells (Fig. 4b). The pre-
deposited 5.0 mAh cm−2 on the Cu foil only maintains 75 cycles 
of Li plating/stripping for 2.0 M LiFSI-EC-EMC electrolyte. Eventu-
ally, the voltage exceeds the measurement range, resulting in cell 
failure. In comparison, the OTA-based electrolyte enables the cell
to cycle stably for 750 h without failure. Li||Cu cells were further
assembled to evaluate Li plating/stripping reversibility. As shown
in Fig. 4(c), the cells using carbonate-based electrolyte deliver a 
low CE value of 95.72 % before 79 cycles at 0.5 mA cm−2 and an

3. Conclusion s
This study demonstrates that the OTA-based weakly solvating elec-
trolyte differs from conventional carbonate systems by forming an 
anion-dominated solvation structure. The total ratio of CIP and AGG
in the OTA-based electrolyte reaches 99.23 %. This solvation structure 
promotes preferential FSI− reduction to generate an inorganic-rich SEI. 
The mechanism preserves weak Li+-OTA coordination while maintain-
ing signiﬁcant Li+-anion interactions and preferential solvent reduc-
tion. These features induce the formation of an inorganic-enriched 
SEI layer containing sulfur species, leading to improved Li plating/ 
stripping. As a result, the electrolyte enables a Coulombic efﬁciency 
of 98.9 % for Li plating/stripping, stable overpotential proﬁles, and uni-
form Li deposition morphology. Meanwhile, the excellent electrolyte 
wettability to electrodes synergistically contributes to high-loading 
cathodes for long-term cycling stability. Li||LiFePO4 cells with OTA-
based electrolyte demonstrate excellent capacity retention under
stringent negative-to-positive (N/P) ratios. Even assembled pouch cells
with high-loading LiFePO4 cathodes (∼3.58 mAh cm−2) and thin Li
anodes (20 lm) maintain 88.77 % capacity retention after 150 cycles.
This work proposes a novel solvent design strategy and provides prin-
