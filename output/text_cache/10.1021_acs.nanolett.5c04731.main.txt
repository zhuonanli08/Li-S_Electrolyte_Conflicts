Iron Porphyrin-Mediated Electrolyte Chemistry for Advanced
Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: The real implementation of lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries suffers from severe polysulfide shuttling, sluggish sulfur
redox reaction kinetics, and inhomogeneous lithium deposition, all
of which lead to diminished rate capability, and cycling
performance and safety concerns. In this study, iron(III) meso-
tetraphenylporphyrin chloride (FeTPP) is introduced into the
electrolyte as an effective homogeneous catalyst to address the
interconnected challenges in Li−S batteries. The FeTPP molecules
present high metal utilization efficiency, an optimized coordination
environment, and remarkable mobility within the electrolyte,
thereby demonstrating outstanding working activity in homoge-
neously mediating the kinetic evolution of sulfur and lithium
species. Moreover, a saturated concentration of 0.1 wt % further
pledges a sufficient number of FeTPP molecules to act as homogeneous promoters in the electrolyte. Therefore, both a highly
efficient sulfur cathode and a stable lithium anode are achieved simultaneously, significantly contributing to the enhanced capacity,
prolonged cycling life span, and improved operational safety of Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, redox reaction, iron porphyrin, molecular catalyst, homogeneous catalysis
T
he demand for high-energy density storage systems has
been steadily increasing.1,2 Currently, the energy density
of conventional Li+ batteries is approaching its theoretical
limit.3 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, which offer an
exceptional theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), are
deemed to be among the most promising candidates for next-
generation energy storage technologies.4−6 However, the
commercialization of Li−S batteries is severely impeded by
lithium polysulfide (LiPS) shuttle, sluggish reaction kinetics,
and nonuniform lithium deposition.7,8
To date, various strategies have been developed to address
the above issues. The introduction of host materials with
anchored ability for sulfur species has been implemented.9
Recently, advancements in catalyst engineering through defect
regulation,10 size control,11 phase modulation,12 heterojunc-
tion design,13,14 and confinement manipulation15 have further
rationalized the Li−S redox chemistry. However, these
catalysts employed in the cathode and separator are
heterogeneous and often show limited catalytic activity.16,17
In contrast, the more recently emerging homogeneous
catalysts, which are soluble in the electrolyte and can permeate
into the cathode, enable more extensive interaction with sulfur
and lithium species, leading to enhanced catalytic effi-
ciency.18−20 For instance, Wang and co-workers also confirmed
that the vanadyl acetylacetonate in the electrolyte could be
used to effectively influence the sulfur interconversion
reaction.21 Yang and co-workers designed a binuclear Cu-
based homogeneous catalyst, and the proximal distance-
enabled coordination configuration could significantly facilitate
the Li2S nucleation/decomposition reactions. The conjugated
porphyrin macrocycle in iron(III) meso-tetraphenylporphyrin
chloride (FeTPP) enables chemical adsorption via π−π
interactions. Its planar structure exposes coordination sites,
allowing FeTPP molecules to anchor species during the
homogeneous dispersion. The central Fe3+ also forms
coordination bonds with nonmetal atoms in nearby molecules.
These structural and electronic features make FeTPP a
versatile ligand for building metal−organic frameworks and
two-dimensional materials used in gas storage and electro-
chemical applications.22,23
In this Letter, FeTPP was utilized as a homogeneous catalyst
for Li−S batteries. The porphyrin structure exhibits polarity
and a π-electron-conjugated system,24 enabling effective

FeTPP shows controllable solubility in the Li−S electrolyte
with a saturated concentration of 0.1 wt %, which facilitates
electron transfer through the vacant d orbitals of the central
metal atom, thus exerting a pronounced homogeneous working
effect on the evolution of sulfur and lithium species.22 Li−S
batteries incorporating FeTPP as a homogeneous catalyst
display a capacity retention of 87.2% after 100 cycles at 0.2 C
and maintain stable cycling capability at 1.0 C.
The working mechanism of FeTPP as a homogeneous
catalyst for Li−S redox chemistry is illustrated in Figure 1a.
Featuring a coordinate porphyrin ligand and central Fe3+,
FeTPP not only displays high polarity, enabling strong
electrochemical species adsorption, but also facilitates electron
transfer through the vacant d orbitals of the central metal atom.
It is noteworthy that the small molecular size of FeTPP allows
it to diffuse freely within the electrolyte, thereby ensuring
efficient contact with the sulfur species and promoting their
conversion at the electron-conductive interface. Meanwhile,
the Li+ flux can be effectively guided, which kinetically
optimizes the sulfur cathode and stabilizes the lithium anode
working surface.25 The carbon layer performs a dual function:
preventing the migration of FeTPP to the anode and
propelling the conversion of sulfur species on its conductive
surface. The homogeneous modulation of FeTPP on both the
cathode and anode contributes to improved discharge capacity
and extended operational life span. The spectral character-
izations of FeTPP were conducted by Fourier transform
infrared (FTIR) and ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectrosco-
py. The characteristic peaks at around 997 and 749 cm−1 are
attributed to the Fe−N and C−H stretching vibrations,
respectively (Figure S1). The UV−vis absorption spectrum
of FeTPP shows a Soret band at about 420 nm and a Q-band
at around 550 nm, which align with the spectral features typical
of metal porphyrin complexes (Figure 1b).26 The results are

The long-term stability of FeTPP in the electrolyte was also
detected to assess its practical applicability. As shown in Figure
S2, the UV−vis absorption peaks of FeTPP remain nearly
unchanged in intensity after 30 days. Shuttle current profiles of
Li−S batteries using FeTPP-containing and blank electrolytes
were recorded. Compared to the control sample, the FeTPP-
based battery exhibits a significantly lower shuttle current
(Figure 1c), demonstrating that FeTPP effectively captures
LiPSs in Li−S batteries. The 0.1 wt % FeTPP in the electrolyte
endows the battery with more favorable battery capacity and
cycling stability than other dilution concentrations, confirming
a sufficient concentration of the soluble catalyst to more
uniformly optimize battery reactions (Figure S3).
As exhibited in Figure 1d, the calculated binding energies of
FeTPP with Li2S, Li2S2, and Li2S4 are 1.84, 2.33, and 0.60 eV,
respectively, which are significantly higher than those of
graphene (0.80, 0.80, and 0.60 eV for Li2S, Li2S2, and Li2S4,
respectively). The Gibbs free energy changes for the stepwise
conversion of sulfur species were also calculated. As illustrated
in Figure 1e, the conversion of solid S8 to liquid Li2S8 belongs
to a thermodynamically spontaneous exothermic process, as
indicated by a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG). The
conversion from Li2S6 to Li2S4 exhibits the largest ΔG value,
representing the rate-limiting step of the sulfur reaction on the
FeTPP surface. The very small ΔG for the relevant rate-
determining step implies that the conversion of sulfur species
on FeTPP involves minimal energy requirements. To
determine the optimal concentration for achieving the most
effective homogeneous catalytic performance, a saturated
electrolyte containing 0.1 wt % FeTPP was initially prepared,
and the supernatant was subsequently diluted 0.5 and 1.0 times
for the corresponding cycling performance tests (Figure S3).

The coulombic efficiencies (CEs) of Cu||Li cells were tested
at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mA h cm−2 (Figure 3a). The Cu||Li
cell with the FeTPP catalyst maintains a stable CE of 94.3%
after 100 cycles, whereas the CE of the Cu||Li cell with the
blank electrolyte rapidly decreases after 40 cycles. The lower
CE may be due to the formation of a nonuniform and loose
solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) film, which is more
susceptible to dendritic penetration. The voltage profiles of
lithium plating and stripping for the Cu||Li cells during the first
cycle show that the nucleation overpotentials, which are
reflected by the gap between the voltage drop and the voltage
plateau, differ significantly. Notably, FeTPP contributes to a
nucleation overpotential of 49.8 mV at 1 mA cm−2, which is
lower than that of the blank electrolyte (102.0 mV) (Figure
3b,c). The substantial reduction in the lithium nucleation
overpotential indicates that the incorporation of the FeTPP
catalyst facilitates a more uniform lithium deposition
procedure. As shown in Figure 3d and Figure S13, under
conditions of high current density (1.0 mA cm−2) and high
capacity (1.0 mA h cm−2), the Li||Li symmetric cell with the
blank electrolyte exhibits significant voltage fluctuations due to
uncontrollable lithium deposition behavior. In contrast, the cell
with the FeTPP catalyst displays a stable cycling process
exceeding 500 h, which is significantly longer than that of the
control cell.
Diffusion barriers along the relevant diffusion pathways were
calculated based on density functional theory (DFT) (Figure
3e and Figure S14). The calculated diffusion barrier of the Li
atom on FeTPP is as low as 0.19 eV, which is beneficial for
enhancing the electrochemical reaction kinetics. The EIS
curves of Li||Li symmetric batteries at different temperatures
were further implemented (Figure 3f,g). One can observe from
the EIS curves of both Li||Li symmetric cells that the
resistances decrease with an increase in temperature, but the
cell with the FeTPP electrolyte consistently exhibits lower
resistance than that using the blank electrolyte. The activation
energy (Ea) for Li+ migration through the SEI film was
calculated based on the Arrhenius equation. The SEI film
formed in the FeTPP electrolyte-based cell displays a smaller
Ea value (67.3 kJ mol−1) compared to that in the blank
electrolyte-based cell (70.4 kJ mol−1), as shown in Figure 3h.
The three-dimensional (3D) contour images of the cycled
lithium anodes with two types of electrolytes were detected by
atomic force microscopy (AFM). As expected, after cycling at
1.0 mA cm−2, the lithium anode associated with the FeTPP
electrolyte exhibits a relatively flat surface, whereas the anode
optimized by the blank electrolyte shows multiple protrusions
(Figure 3i). To facilitate a more detailed comparison of the
surfaces of the two lithium anodes, a representative section of
the 3D contour images was selected for height analysis. The
lithium anode in the FeTPP-containing cell presents
significantly reduced surface undulation compared to the
blank control group (Figure 3j), suggesting that FeTPP
enables uniform lithium deposition. This consistent lack of
Fe signals in XPS and elemental mapping images demonstrates
that FeTPP is not involved in the construction of the SEI film
(Figures S10 and S11). The calculated Li+ migration number
(TLi
+) values for the cells with the blank electrolyte and FeTPP
are 0.44 and 0.56, respectively, confirming the FeTPP
enhances Li nucleation kinetics by regulating Li+ flux (Figure
S12).
An electrolyte containing 0.1 wt % FeTPP was used to
fabricate the Li−S batteries. In Figure 4a, at rates of 0.2, 0.5,
1.0, and 2.0 C, the reversible capacities of the FeTPP-
containing battery are 1052.5, 896.2, 725.6, and 543.6 mA h
g−1, respectively. In contrast, the reversible capacities of the
blank electrolyte-based battery are 923.9, 814.9, 594.6, and
428.2 mA h g−1, respectively. Compared to the blank
electrolyte, the FeTPP electrolyte demonstrates superior
activity in the electrochemical reactions along with the change
in current density. Additionally, reaction polarization was
evaluated by the overpotentials derived from the voltage gap in
the charge/discharge curves (Figure 4b and Figure S15). The
battery with FeTPP exhibits a lower overpotential value,
indicating the significant catalytic efficiency of FeTPP.
Through the rate performance test, 0.2 C was first selected
to test the cycling performance of the batteries. In Figure 4c,
the initial specific capacities of the batteries with FeTPP and
the blank electrolyte are 1067.3 and 959.2 mA h g−1,
respectively, and the capacity retention rates after 100 cycles
are 87.2% and 82.5%, respectively. The discharge curves of the
battery with FeTPP at 0.2 C show a narrower distribution
within 100 cycles than the control sample (Figure 4d,e). In
addition, in comparison with the control sample, the battery
with FeTPP reaches prolonged low-voltage (∼2.1 V) and high-
voltage (∼2.3 V) plateaus (Figure S16), which can be
attributed to the homogeneous catalytic effect of FeTPP. EIS
curves of the batteries with different electrolytes in Figure S17
display a semicircular arc in the high-frequency region and a
linear slope in the low-frequency region. The former
corresponds to the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the
battery, while the latter reflects the Li+ diffusion kinetics. The
Rct value of the FeTPP-containing battery is reduced by 30.0 Ω
compared with that of the blank electrolyte-containing battery
(94.1 Ω), indicating that FeTPP facilitates the formation of a
more electron-conductive environment within the battery
system.
In Figure 4f and Figure S18, FeTPP enables the battery at
1.0 C to deliver an initial discharge capacity of 767.3 mA h g−1,
with a capacity retention rate of 85.5% after 200 cycles and a
beneficial capacity retention rate of 74.5% after 400 cycles,
confirming the capability of FeTPP to sustain long-term
cycling stability. Cycling performance tests were conducted on
an FeTPP-containing battery with a sulfur mass loading of 6.6
mg cm−2 and a correponding cathode thickness of 81.6 μm
(Figure 4g and Figure S19). The battery maintains an areal
capacity of 4.6 mA h cm−2 after 90 cycles at 0.1 C, with a
capacity retention of 85.8%. Notably, compared with other
reported electrolyte-engineered Li−S batteries, the high-load
battery with FeTPP achieves superior cycling performance
under high sulfur loads (Figure 4h).30−33 SEM images in
Figure S20 reveal that the cathode shows fewer holes and
cracks with the assistance of FeTPP after 100 cycles at 0.2 C.
The pouch cells measuring 3 cm × 3 cm were fabricated, and
cycling tests were performed at 0.5 C. The initial capacity of
the FeTPP-containing pouch cell reaches 922.6 mA h g−1, and
after 100 cycles, the reversible capacity remains at 794.6 mA h
g−1, achieving a capacity retention of 86.1% (Figure 4i and
Figure S21). The favorable electrochemical performance under
realistic operating conditions highlights the potential of FeTPP
as a homogeneous catalyst for enabling high-performance Li−S
pouch cells. As demonstrated, Li−S pouch cells incorporating
FeTPP can stably power a windmill device for more than 40

FeTPP has been demonstrated to homogeneously regulate
the evolution of sulfur and lithium species. Specifically, the
rational coordination structure, saturated concentration, and
favorable mobility collectively enhance the homogeneous
modulation capability. As a result, FeTPP in the electrolyte
promote the sulfur cathode activity by faciliatating the Li2S
nucleation and decomposition reactions, while stabilizing the
lithium anode through optimized lithium planting/stripping
behaviors, as supported by experimental and theoretical
findings. Therefore, the FeTPP-containing Li−S battery with
a sulfur loading of 6.6 mg cm−2 maintains a retention rate of
85.8% after 90 cycles at 0.1 C. The as-assembled pouch cell can
work for more than 100 cycles. This work paves the way for
the practical deployment of Li−S batteries.
