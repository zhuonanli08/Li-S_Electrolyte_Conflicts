a b s t r a c t
In practical lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), the shuttle effect and Li cycling coulombic efﬁciency (CE) are
strongly affected by the physicochemical properties of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI). LiNO3 is widely
used as an additive in electrolytes to build a high-quality SEI, but its self-sacriﬁcial nature limits the abil-
ity to mitigate the shuttle effect and stabilize Li anode during long-term cycling. To counteract LiNO3 con-
sumption during long-term cycling without using a high initial concentration, inspired by sustained-
release drugs, we encapsulated LiNO3 in lithiated Naﬁon polymer and added an electrolyte co-solvent
(1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethylene 2,2,2-triﬂuoromethyl ether) with poor LiNO3 solubility to construct high-
quality and durable F- and N-rich SEI. Theoretical calculations, experiments, multiphysics simulations,
and in-situ observations conﬁrmed that the F- and N-rich SEI can modulate lithium deposition behavior
and allow persistent repair of SEI during prolonged cycling. Hence, the F- and N-rich SEI improves the Li
anode cycling CE to 99.63% and alleviates the shuttle effect during long-term cycling. The lithium anode
with sustainable F- and N-rich SEI shows a stable Li plating/stripping over 2000 h at 1 mA cm2. As
expected, Li||S full cells with this SEI achieved a long lifespan of 250 cycles, far exceeding cells with a rou-
tine SEI. The Li||S pouch cell based on F- and N-rich SEI also can achieve a high energy density of about
300 Wh kg1 at initial cycles. This strategy provides a novel design for high-quality and durable SEIs in
LSBs and may also be extendable to other alkali metal batteries.

1. Introduction
The growing demand for electric vehicles has driven the devel-
opment of high energy-density secondary battery systems. The
lithium-sulfur battery (LSB) is a promising rechargeable battery
owing to its high theoretical speciﬁc energy of 2570 Wh kg1
[1,2]. Nonetheless, the low Li cycling efﬁciency and shuttle effect
of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) lead to quick failure of LSBs and pre-
clude their commercialization [3,4]. These problems are closely
linked to an unstable routine solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
[5,6]. Firstly, the shuttle effect originates from long-chain LiPSs dis-
solved in the electrolyte, which will pass through the routine SEI
and react with the metallic Li anode [7,8]. Secondly, the routine
SEI with low Li+ conductivity and poor electrical insulation leads
to formation of Li dendrite, and thus lowers Li cycling coulombic
efﬁciency (CE) [9,10]. Therefore, regulating the properties of SEI
is essential for inhibiting the shuttle effect and improving Li
cycling CE.
The properties of an SEI are determined by its components and
structure [11,12]. Recently, extensive efforts have been devoted to
constructing high-quality SEIs, mainly by tailoring the electrolyte
composition or preparing artiﬁcial SEIs [13,14]. Among these, addi-
tives in the electrolyte such as benzoselenol [15], cerium nitrate
[16], 3,5-bis(triﬂuoromethyl)thiophenol [17], and LiNO3 [18] have
been proven to be a facile and effective strategy for optimizing
the components and structure of SEI. Today, LiNO3 is regarded as
a superstar additive in the ﬁeld of LSBs, because a small amount
of NO3
 can generate a dense SEI enriched with high Li+ conductiv-
ity LiNxOy and Li3N components [19–21]. Such a compact SEI
strongly mitigates the shuttle effect by preventing the LiPSs from
contacting with the Li anode, while a high Li+ conductivity N-rich

SEI can inhibit the formation of Li dendrites, enhancing Li cycling

A certain amount of LiNO3 can be dissolved in the standard
ether-based electrolytes used in LSBs [24], but this additive is grad-
ually depleted during the constant rupture and reconstruction of
the SEI. Once NO3
 is exhausted, the electrochemical performance
of LSBs deteriorates rapidly in terms of CE and discharge capacity.
The rate of LiNO3 depletion is related to its original amount in the
electrolyte and the battery cycling capacity. Under mild conditions
(e.g., at a low S loading and a high electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio),
LiNO3 can signiﬁcantly enhance the lifespan and stability of
LSBs [25]. However, a practical LSBs requires the use of a high S
loading cathode (>6 mg cm2), lean electrolyte (<3 lL mgS
1),
and a thin Li anode (<50 lm) [26]. Under such harsh conditions,
even a high concentration of electrolyte failed to achieve satisfac-
tory LSB cycling performance due to premature failure of Li
anode [7].
Although a higher initial LiNO3 concentration in the electrolyte
directly ensures the sustainability and compactness of SEI, a LiNO3
concentration above 0.5 M not only converts the ‘‘active S” in cath-
ode into inactive sulphate but also increases the cost and viscosity
of the electrolyte [27]. So far, there has been no report of a sustain-
able SEI that continuously mitigates the shuttle effect and
enhances the Li cycling CE for practical LSBs. Researchers recently
identiﬁed various slow-release strategies for LiNO3 to achieve sus-
tainable SEI in ester-based electrolytes (low LiNO3 solubility),
including pre-impregnation of the separator, encapsulation in a
polymer gel [28], intercalation between bilayers of the separator
[29], and encapsulation in nano capsules [30]. However, these
strategies are not applied to LSB electrolytes because of the high
solubility of LiNO3 in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxy ethane
(DME) [31]. In the drug ﬁeld, reducing the drug release rate is a
common means of improving the effectiveness of the drug
[32,33]. Compared to conventional methods, drugs in sustained
release systems can maintain effective concentrations for longer
periods of time, prolong the duration of action, increase the effec-
tiveness, and improve the utilisation of the drug [34,35].
Herein, inspired by sustained-release drugs strategies, we
encapsulated LiNO3 in polymers and added ether-based co-
solvent for solubility-mediated sustained release during LSB
cycling. The goal of this strategy is to construct a high-quality
and durable SEI to mitigate the shuttle effect and improve the CE
of Li plating/stripping. Speciﬁcally, the LiNO3 nanoparticles were
dispersed in lithiated Naﬁon (Li-Naﬁon) polymer and coated on a
polypropylene melt-blown cloth. This LiNO3 sustained-release ﬁlm
(LNO-SRF) swells in the electrolyte to achieve sustained release of
LiNO3. At the same time, a ﬂuorinated ether with ultralow Li salts
solubility (1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethylene 2,2,2-triﬂuoromethyl ether,
TFTFE) was added to the electrolyte as a co-solvent to further slow
down the release of LiNO3 and generate insulating LiF component
in the SEI, inhibiting electron leakage. During battery operation, a
high-quality and durable SEI enriched with F and N was formed,
and it increased the Li plating/stripping CE to 99.63% at a current
of 1.0 mA cm2, a capacity of 1.0 mA h cm2. In a Li||S full cell with
a high S loading cathode, a low E/S ratio, and a limited excess Li
anode, the shuttle effect of LiPSs was effectively suppressed during
cycling and an outstanding cycling lifespan of 250 cycles was
achieved.
2. Experimental
2.1. Fabrication of LNO-SRF
Li-Naﬁon powder was prepared according to previous reports
[36]. LiNO3 (10 wt%) and Li-Naﬁon (1 wt%) powder were fully dis-
solved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) under magnetic stirring.
The mixed solution was blade-coated onto a polypropylene melt-
blown cloth and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 C. The obtained
LNO-SRF was punched into 15-mm round disks and transferred
to a glovebox. The mass of LNO-SRF is about 3 mg cm2, and the
LiNO3 loading is about 1.2 mg cm2. The LNO-SRF was placed
between separator and Li electrode or Cu electrode.
2.2. Electrochemical measurements
All electrochemical tests were performed in CR2032-type coin
cells with Celgard 2400 separators. Three types of cells (symmetric
Li||Li, Li||Cu, and Li||S) were assembled in a glovebox. Electrochem-
ical tests were conducted using an electrochemical workstation
(BioLogic, France) and a LAND battery test system. Details of the
electrochemical tests are described in the Supporting Information.
2.3. Calculations and multiphysics simulation
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out
using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) to obtain
the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and electrostatic
potential (ESP) charge distributions of various solvents and anions.
Finite element modelling (FEM) was conducted using COMSOL
multiphysics software. Details of the computation and simulation
are provided in Supporting Information.

Generally, the chemical composition of SEI signiﬁcantly inﬂu-
ences the nucleation and growth of Li. Therefore, the morphology
and surface ﬂatness of Li deposited below various SEIs were ana-
lyzed. For R-SEI, the Li electrode surface displayed a loose, uneven,
and dendritic surface, and greyish-black dead Li was observed after
only ﬁve cycles in Li||Li cell (Fig. 3a). A porous and loose structure
of deposited Li will exacerbate the side reactions on the surface of
Li electrode, resulting in massive Li dendrites and shuttle effect.
When the content of LiF (F-SEI) or Li3N/LiNxOy (N-SEI) components
in the SEI increases, Li deposition became more uniform, with
somewhat inhibited growth of Li dendrites and dead Li formation
(Fig. 3b and c). In contrast, the surface of Li electrode with FN-SEI
was quite dense and smooth without obvious dendrites or dead
Li (Fig. 3d), indicating that the F- and N-rich SEI could effectively
regulate Li+ deposition and suppress dendritic growth. In addition,
FN-SEI had a high Young’s modulus of 4.95 GPa (Fig. S6), which
inhibited the growth of Li dendrites. This suppression of Li den-
drites signiﬁcantly reduced the reactive surface area, thus lowering
the possibility side reactions.
The average CE is a key index for the cyclability of Li electrodes.
According to the method proposed by Zhang et al. [41], we deter-
mined the average CE of Li stripping/plating in Li||Cu cells with dif-
ferent SEIs. The time-voltage curves of Li||Cu cells with R-SEI, F-SEI,
N-SEI, and FN-SEI are shown in Fig. 3(e–h), respectively. Among
them, the cell with FN-SEI displayed the lowest nucleation overpo-
tential of 46 mV and deposit overpotential of 24 mV (Fig. S7), sug-
gesting that FN-SEI has the highest Li+ conductivity, which is in line
with the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measure-
ments (Fig. S8). In addition, the cell with FN-SEI had the highest
CE of 99.63%, exceeding those cells with R-SEI (98.46%), F-SEI
(99.02%), and N-SEI (99.17%).

we employed a commercial-level carbon nanotubes/sulfur cathode

electrolyte (5 lL mgS

in cells with R-SEI and FN-SEI at initial cycles and 25 cycles to
demonstrate that FN-SEI could effectively suppress the shuttle
effect during long-term cycling. As shown in Fig. 5(a and b), the
shuttle currents of these two cells were not signiﬁcantly different
in the initial cycle, and both were close to 0. However, after 25
cycles the shuttle current of the FN-SEI cell was approximately ﬁve
times lower than that of the R-SEI cell, indicating that FN-SEI main-
tained a dense state during cycling. In Fig. S14, Li||S cells with R-SEI
and FN-SEI exhibited nearly identical initial discharge capacities of
about 1250 mA h g1. After approximately 25 cycles, the discharge
capacity for R-SEI decreased rapidly with concurrent dramatic ﬂuc-
tuations in CE because of severe dendrite growth and exhaustion of
LiNO3. In contrast, Li||S cell with FN-SEI exhibited stable cycling
over 250 cycles (Fig. 5c). The degradation discrepancy between
cells can be explained by the charge-discharge proﬁles. The polar-
ization voltage of the Li||S cell with R-SEI rapidly increased to
360 mV, whereas that of the Li||S cell with FN-SEI was only
220 mV (Fig. S15). This is ascribed to severe passivation of R-SEI,
which causes increase of polarization. In addition, the Li-Naﬁon
membrane also can improve the cycling life to a certain extent
because the Li-Naﬁon can shield LiPSs and polypropylene melt-
blown cloth skeleton can regulate Li+ ﬂux (Fig. S16). The structure
and chemical components of the cycled Li anode surface were fur-
ther analyzed by XPS. Based on the S 2p spectra (Fig. 5d and e),
strong peaks of Li2S and Li2SOx were observed on the Li anode with
R-SEI, whereas Li anode with FN-SEI exhibited much weaker Li2S
and Li2SOx peaks. The smaller amounts of S-containing species
can be attributed to fewer side reactions occurring between Li
anode and LiPSs. The assembled Li||S cell with FN-SEI was further
used to power electronic devices to validate their practical applica-
tions. Fig. 5(f) displays a Radar chart comparing the cycling life of
Li||S batteries using the LiNO3 sustained release strategy and other
Li anode protection methods. Li||S cell with FN-SEI is superior to

4. Conclusions
In summary, to enhance the cycling life of Li anodes and pro-
mote further practical applications of LSBs, we designed and con-
structed a high-quality and durable F- and N-rich SEI on the Li
anode by utilizing LNO-SRF and TFTFE as co-solvents in the elec-
trolyte. LNO-SRF provided a continuous release of LiNO3 into the
electrolyte, and the TFTFE co-solvent slowed the release speed of
LiNO3, resulting in a dense and durable F- and N-rich SEI. The for-
mation mechanism of such a superior SEI was determined using
DFT calculations. Such an SEI effectively suppresses Li dendrite for-
mation and regulates Li deposition, as demonstrated by the exper-
imental results. When used in a cell, symmetrical cells with FN-SEI
achieve dendrite-free Li plating/stripping with reduced overpoten-
