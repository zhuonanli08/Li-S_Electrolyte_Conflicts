Replenish the Electron-Deﬁcient State of Carbonyl Carbon
Atoms to Achieve Stable Li–S Battery
Sheng Gu, Yu Zhang,* Meng Li, Qianru Lin, Chenyang Zhao, Guoqi Xu,*
and Naiqing Zhang*
Carbonate-based electrolytes are widely used in Li-ion batteries (LIBs) due to
their safe, stable properties and wide operating temperature range. However,
the nucleophilic attack at carbonyl carbon atoms by polysulﬁde anions and
the low solubility of lithium nitrate (LiNO3<0.1 m) in carbonate-based
electrolytes seriously hinder the application of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
in carbonate-based electrolytes. Herein, the electrophilicity of carbonyl carbon
atoms is gradually reduced through solvent molecules redesign, eliminated
the attack of polysulﬁde anions on carbonyl carbon atoms, and also changed
the intermolecular interactions in the electrolyte, improving the solubility of
LiNO3 (>1.0 m) and forming stable LiNxOy-containing SEI on the surface of
the lithium metal. The assembled Li–S battery with these designed solvent
molecules delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1251.7 mAh g−1 at 0.1C. In
addition, the lithium–sulfur battery assembled with the designed solvent
molecule has a discharge capacity of 631 mAh g−1 after 420 cycles at 0.2 C,
with a capacity retention of ≈60%. Moreover, the Li||Li symmetrical cell shows
stable cycle performance over 1200 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2.

2. Results and Discussion
Ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) solvents,
which are the most used in Li-ion batteries research, play an im-
portant role in commercial electrolyte applications. Their mod-
iﬁcation is beneﬁcial to maintain their low viscosity, wide op-
erating temperature range, and high chemical stability. Tables
S1 and S2 (Supporting Information) present the basic physic-
ochemical properties of the modiﬁed solvents. Similar physical
and chemical properties ensure the inherent advantages of the
designed solvent molecules. To describe gradient regulation in-
tuitively, we ﬁrst use EC as a reference object. The two oxygen
atoms that are adjacent to the carbonyl carbon atoms in EC are
replaced by the ─NR2 electron-donating amide groups in turn
to obtain 3-methyl-2-oxazolidinone (MOX) and 1,3- dimethyl-2-
imidazolidinone (DMI) solvents, as depicted in Figure 2a. The
electron-donating nature of the ─NR2 amide groups was con-
ﬁrmed by electrostatic potential (ESP) calculations. Apparently,
MOX and DMI have signiﬁcantly increased negative charges
around the carbonyl carbon atoms compared with EC, as shown
in Figure 2b. The electron-deﬁcient state of the carbonyl carbon
atoms is gradually supplemented, which conﬁrms their lower
electrophilic properties toward high-order polysulﬁde anions.
Therefore, we believe that replenishing the charge to carbonyl
carbon atoms can solve the problem of polysulﬁde anions attack-
ing carbonyl carbon atoms at the molecular level. Based on the
theoretical results, a representative high-order polysulﬁde (Li2S8)
was selected to study the chemical stability of the designed sol-
vent molecules. The brown-colored Li2S8 immediately becomes a
white powder when mixed with the carbonate solvent, indicating
the violent attack of the polysulﬁde anion on the carbonyl carbon
atoms. After introducing the ─NR2 amide group with electron-
donating ability, there was no signiﬁcant color change in the mix-
ture of MOX and Li2S8 during the initial stage of the reaction.
When the standing time was extended to 10 days, a trace of white
ﬂocculent precipitate appears at the bottom of the glass bottle.
When the standing time was extended to 50 days, the brown color
disappears and a small amount of white precipitate formed. The
above experimental phenomena indicate that the introduction of
─NR2 electron donors greatly weakens the attack of polysulﬁde
anions on carbonyl carbon atoms in MOX solvents, but cannot
eliminate this slow attack trend completely. Subsequently, the
DMI solvent was obtained by introducing ─NR2 amide groups
to replace the oxygen atom adjacent to the carbonyl carbon atom
in the MOX solvent to further supplement the charge of the car-
bonyl carbon atom. In the mixture of DMI and Li2S8, there was
no deposition at the bottom of the glass bottles even after 50 days,
indicating that the introduction of ─NR2 amide groups in the
MOX solvent can completely supplement the electron-deﬁcient
state of carbonyl carbon atoms and eliminate the attack of poly-
sulﬁde anions on carbonyl carbon atoms completely. As a result,
DMI solvent and Li2S8 can coexist stably, as shown in Figure 2c
and Movie S1 (Supporting Information). These experimental re-
sults were further conﬁrmed by NMR spectroscopy characteriza-
tion, as shown in Figure 2d and Figure S1 (Supporting Informa-
tion). Next, UV–vis spectroscopy was used to monitor the content
changes of 0.5 mm Li2S6 exposed to MOX and DMI solvents at
diﬀerent times, as shown in Figure 2e. Interestingly, compared to
DOL/DME, the introduction of ─NR2 amide groups signiﬁcantly
altered the presence state of polysulﬁdes in the designed solvent
molecules (Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information).[22] No-
tably, MOX exhibited a strong peak at 618 nm indicative of the
sulfur radical (S3
•−). The other two peaks of low intensity 475
and 420 nm were attributed to S6
2−and S4
2−, respectively. As the
standing time prolonged, although the peak intensity of various
polysulﬁde species gradually decreased, the peak of S3
•−was still
detected on the 50th day, indicating that the chemical reaction be-
tween polysulﬁde anions and MOX solvent was very slow, indicat-
ing that the attack of polysulﬁde anions on carbonyl oxygen atoms
was eﬀectively suppressed. The DMI solvent obtained by further
introducing electron donating ─NR2 amide groups into MOX sol-
vent to supplement carbonyl carbon atoms, even after standing
for more than 50 days, the peak intensities of various polysul-
ﬁdes remain unchanged, indicating that the attack of polysul-
ﬁde anions on carbonyl carbon atoms has been completely sup-
pressed. The above results are consistent with the results of NMR
spectroscopy and optical photographs. Therefore, we believe that
the use of electron donor groups to supplement the electron-
deﬁcient states of carbonyl carbon atoms can gradually eliminate
the nucleophilic attack of high-order LiPSs on carbonyl carbon

In the molecular structure of ester solvents, the electronegativ-
ity of the two oxygen atoms adjacent to the carbonyl carbon atom
is stronger than that of the carbon atom, which not only leads
to the carbonyl carbon atom to be in a highly electron-deﬁcient
state but also leads to the decrease of the electron cloud den-
sity of the oxygen atom interconnected with the carbonyl carbon
atom. According to previous reports, the dissociation of lithium
salt is dominated by carbonyl oxygen atoms in the ester molecu-
lar structure.[23] Therefore, it is expected that the ─NR2 electron-
donating amide group cannot only supplement the charge of car-
bonyl carbon atoms but also have a positive eﬀect on carbonyl
oxygen atoms, thereby promoting the dissolution of LiNO3. For
ease of description, we tested the solubility of LiNO3 in annular
EC, MOX, and DMI solvents to evaluate the eﬀect of the intro-
duction of ─NR2 electron donating amide group on the solubil-
ity of LiNO3. In the EC-based mixture, the solubility of LiNO3 is
even < 0.05 m. After the ─NR2 amide group is introduced suc-
cessively to replace the two oxygen atoms adjacent to the car-
bonyl carbon atom, the solubility of LiNO3 in MOX and DMI
solvents exceeds 0.5 and 1.5 m, respectively (Figure 3a). This re-
sult is conﬁrmed by the electrostatic potential (ESP) (Figure 2a).
The introduction of ─NR2 amide groups sequentially leads to a
more negative equipotential surface of carbonyl oxygen atoms,
resulting in a stronger aﬃnity for Li+ and promoting the dis-
sociation of LiNO3. This theoretical result is also applicable in
linear DMC, MDI, and TMU solvents. Subsequently, the solva-
tion structure of DMI-TMU electrolyte was studied by Raman
spectroscopy (Figure 3b; Figure S10, Supporting Information).

discharge capacities of DMI-TMU-0.1 m LiNO3, DMI-TMU-0.3 m
LiNO3, DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3, and DMI-TMU-0.7 m LiNO3 Li–
S cells are 530, 634, 790, and 678 mAh g−1 after 115 cycles at 0.1 C,
respectively (Figure 5a). Obviously, the DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3
cell exhibited better cycling performance and capacity retention.
Although DMI-TMU-0.7 m LiNO3 Li–S cells also exhibited a high
speciﬁc capacity, excessive LiNO3 resulting in a large viscosity
of the electrolyte itself is not desirable for practical application.
Subsequently, DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 electrolyte was selected to
further study the cyclic stability. As shown in Figure 5b, the dis-
charge capacity of the DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 battery after 420
cycles at 0.2C is 631 mAh g−1, with a capacity retention of ≈60%.
The rates test show that the DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 cell had bet-
ter performance at low rates, and slightly worse performance at
high rates (Figure 5c). To evaluate the feasibility of the practical
application of DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 battery, the performance
of the battery with high sulfur loads was tested,[32,33] as shown in
Figure 5d. The sulfur loading was increased to 6.1 mg cm−2 and
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 7 μL mg−1, the Li–S battery still
had a discharge capacity of 622 mAh g−1 after 70 cycles. These
results indicate that DMI-TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 electrolyte has prac-
tical potential.
In the previous characterization of basic physicochemical
properties, we noted that the gradient regulation of the electron-
deﬁcient state of carbonyl carbon atoms does not change the ba-
sic physical properties of the solvent molecules, and the obtained
solvent molecules have good thermal stability and high ﬂash
points. This special advantage inspires us to delve deeper into the
electrochemical properties of the obtained solvent molecules at
high temperatures. Therefore, we selected commercially mature
ether-based electrolytes as controls to evaluate the cycling perfor-
mance of DOL/MDE-0.5 m LiNO3 (named DOL/DME) and DMI-
TMU-0.5 m LiNO3 batteries at 30, 60, and 80 °C, respectively. The
tested current density is 0.2 C. The DOL/DME cells delivered ini-
tial discharge capacities of 1037.4,1167.3, and 1001.4 mAh g−1
at temperatures of 30, 60, and 80 °C, respectively (Figure 6a–c).
However, as the ambient temperature gradually increases, the
polarization voltage of DOL/DME battery signiﬁcantly increases,
and the coulomb eﬃciency gradually decreases, which may be
a side reaction caused by the volatilization of solvent molecules

carbon atoms, not only can gradually eliminates the nucleophilic
attack of polysulﬁde anions on carbonyl carbon atoms but also
can change the intermolecular interaction in the electrolyte, thus
