Accelerated Li2S conversion in sparingly-solvating electrolytes enabled 
with dipole-dipole interaction for wide-temperature Li-S batteries

A B S T R A C T  
Utilizing sparingly-solvating electrolytes (SSEs), which suppress the dissolution of lithium polysulfides (Li2S4−8, 
LPSs), could reduce electrolyte consumption and thus retrieve the energy density competitiveness of lithium- 
sulfur batteries (LSBs). However, SSEs-based LSBs suffer from sluggish kinetics and rapid capacity degradation 
due to the accumulation of Li2S in the cathode. The catalytic approach, which is widely used to promote the 
sulfur conversion in ether-based LSBs, is rarely explored in SSEs-based LSBs due to the lack of a catalyst workable 
in the absence of dissolved LPSs. Here, by embedding CuS nanoparticles, which have high dipole-dipole inter­
action with Li2S, as polar sites into the carbon matrix of the sulfur cathode, we homogenize the Li2S deposition 
and accelerate the conversion kinetics. As a result, the cell with S/KB@CuS cathode realized a remarkable initial 
discharge capacity of 1469.5 mAh g−1 (87.7% of the theoretical value) with a reduced polarization potential 
(0.526V). Even at a low temperature of −15◦C, the cell with S/KB@CuS cathode still delivered a capacity- 
retention of 799.0 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. More importantly, the universality of the as-proposed dipole- 
dipole-interaction strategy is demonstrated with many other SSEs and polar additives.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), known for their high theoretical 
energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), hold great promise as next-generation 
secondary batteries [1]. However, in commonly employed ether-based 
electrolytes, LSBs could hardly work under lean-electrolyte conditions 
(e.g., electrolyte/sulfur ratio < 5 μL mg–1) due to the deteriorative ion 
conductivity and electrolyte wettability caused by the dissolution of 
lithium polysulfides (LPSs) [2], which compromise the energy density 
and cycle life of LSBs [3]. As an alternative, sparingly-solvating elec­
trolytes (SSEs), are regarded as a promising solution to improve the 
performance of LSBs under lean-electrolyte conditions [4]. SSEs can 
decouple the reaction mechanisms from electrolyte usage by altering the 
reaction 
pathway 
between 
sulfur 
and 
lithium 
sulfide 
from 
solid-liquid-solid to quasi-solid by suppressing the dissolution of LPSs in 
electrolytes. Thus, SSEs-based LSBs have the potential to realize prac­
tical applications. However, the use of SSEs for LSBs always leads to poor 
dynamic and low sulfur utilization, especially at low temperatures [5]. 
For LSBs with solvating electrolytes (e.g., lithium bis(trifluoro­
methane sulfonimide) in the mixture of 1,3-dioxolane and 1,2-dime­
thoxyethane), various transition metal compounds [6], single-atom 
electrocatalysts [7], doped carbon [8], and metal-organic frameworks 
[9], etc. have been proposed as catalysts to promote the dynamic of LSBs. 
The catalytic process involves the adsorption of the dissolved long-chain 
LPSs and then the promotion of their conversion into sulfur (S) or 
lithium sulfide (Li2S), which ensures a low reaction barrier and high 
sulfur utilization [10]. However, in SSEs, where the dissolution of LPSs 
is suppressed, the catalytic reaction through LPSs fails. Theoretically, 
the poor dynamic and low sulfur utilization of SSEs-based LSBs are 
mainly due to the aggregation of large-sized and electronic insulative 
Li2S particles [11], which can be largely attributed to the weak inter­
action between polar Li2S and the nonpolar carbon hosts. Therefore, to 
promote the practical applications of SSEs-based LSBs, the main chal­
lenge is to uniform the Li2S deposition [12]. 
Aiming to improve the interaction between Li2S and carbon hosts, we 
introduced copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles into the carbon matrix of

As polar sites for the nucleation of Li2S, the as-embedded CuS nano­

2. Result and discussion 
In our previous research, it is found that, in SSEs, the discharge 
product Li2S is difficult to completely delithiated during the charging 
process with a charge-cut-off voltage of 2.8V due to the formation of 
large-sized Li2S particles [13]. The accumulation of the residual Li2S 
with cycling leads to the rapid failure of LSBs [14]. Fundamentally, the 
unevenly distributed Li2S particles could be ascribed to the poor inter­
action between the polar Li2S with the nonpolar carbon matrix. To 
obtain smaller and uniformly distributed Li2S particles, one solution is to 
introduce polar sites, which can serve as nucleation centers to induce 
more nucleations through the generation of dipole-dipole interaction 
between Li2S and the carbon matrix (Fig. 1a), into sulfur electrodes. 
Here, we selected CuS as the polar site because of its unique sulfiphilic 
and electrical conductivity [15]. The molecule dynamic simulations 
show that the interaction energies between the Li2S molecule and both 
the (001) and (010) planes of CuS are nearly 5 times greater than that 
between Li2S and the surface of the carbon (Fig. 1b-e). Similar results are

(Fig. S1). Furthermore, to confirm the interaction between CuS and Li2S, 
we soaked KB and CuS powders in solutions of Li2S in AN and monitored 
their color change. As shown in Figs. 1f and g, and S2, the blue color of 
the CuS-soaked solution disappeared after 24h, in contrast, the 
KB-soaked one remains blue, indicating the strong interaction between 
CuS and Li2S. Moreover, the redshift of the Raman spectra belonging to 
Li2S and CuS for the CuS/Li2S mixture (Fig. 1h) provides additional 
evidence of the interaction between CuS and Li2S. The strong interaction 
between Li2S and CuS may promote the nucleation of Li2S, and thus 
solve the problems of the aggregation and consequent poor dynamic of 
the delithiation process of Li2S. 
To validate the effectiveness of CuS polar sites, we prepared sulfur 
cathodes with embedded CuS nanoparticles. Fig. S3 shows the X-Ray 
Diffraction (XRD) pattern of the S/KB@CuS cathode, in which peaks 
representing planes of CuS can be observed. Furthermore, images of the 
Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS) of element mapping (Fig. S4) 
exhibit the uniform distribution of Cu and S elements. Results of XRD 
and EDS indicate the uniform distribution of CuS particles. Next, we 
compared the morphology and elemental composition of the S/KB@CuS 
cathode after 25 cycles with the control sample (the corresponding cy­
clic performance of used cells is shown in Fig. S5). As the scanning 
electronic microscopy (SEM) images shown in Fig. 2a, the cycled S/KB 
cathode exhibits a massive aggregate surface, revealing the formation of 
large-sized Li2S particles. In contrast, the S/KB@CuS cathode exhibits a 
surface morphology characterized by loose dispersion and small particle 
size (Fig. 2b). The differences in SEM images demonstrate that the 
morphology (caused by the conversion from S to Li2S) of the sulfur 
cathode is uniform with the introduction of CuS polar sites. Further­
more, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is used to confirm the 
effectiveness of CuS polar sites. Before cycles, peaks belonging to Cu 
2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 in the XPS spectrum of Cu 2p (Fig. S6c) and corre­
sponding peak in the XPS spectrum of S 2p (Fig. S6d) obtained from S/ 
KB@CuS cathode indicate the existence of CuS in the surface of S/ 
KB@CuS cathode. After 25 cycles, for the charged S/KB cathode, peaks 
belonging to Li2S (161.4 and 159.9 eV) and polysulfide intermediates 
(163.3 and 161.9 eV) are detected (Fig. 2c), suggesting the incomplete 
conversion from Li2S to S during the charging process [16]. However, no 
peaks belonging to Li2S or polysulfide intermediate are found for the 
charged S/KB@CuS cathode, which demonstrates that Li2S is fully 
converted to S in the S/KB@CuS cathode during the charging process. 
The sharp contrast in XPS spectra demonstrates that the uniformly 
deposited Li2S derived by the CuS polar sites effectively boosts the 
conversion of Li2S into S. In addition, peaks belonging to CuS can still be 
observed in the XPS spectrum of Cu 2p after cycles (Fig. S7), indicating 
the stability of CuS polar sites during cycles.

To facilitate the understanding of the effect of CuS polar sites, we 
drew a diagram of the conversion of sulfur species during the dis­
charging/charging process on S/KB and S/KB@CuS cathodes. Compared 
with the cell with the S/KB@CuS cathode, the cell with the S/KB cath­
ode shows a loss of capacity during the charging process (Fig. 2e), 
despite the almost same capacities for both cells in the discharging 
process. The higher reversible capacity of the cell with the S/KB@CuS 
cathode demonstrates the incomplete conversion from Li2S to S caused 
by the large particle size in the control cell is avoided, which indicates 
the effectiveness of the introduction of CuS polar sites. Diagrams in 
Fig. 2e illustrate the possible evolution process of sulfur species during 
the discharging/charging process. During the discharging process, un­
like the S/KB cathode, where the Li2S tends to aggregate and form large- 
sized particles, the polar CuS nanoparticles in S/KB@CuS cathode pro­
vide sufficient nucleation centers for Li2S, leading to the formation of 
small-sized and thus uniformly-deposited Li2S particles, which shorten 
the path for both electron and Li+ transportation, and thus promote the 
conversion from Li2S to S, avoiding the residue of the Li2S during the 
charging process. 
Next, we compared the electrochemical performance of cells with the 
S/KB and S/KB@CuS cathodes. Previously, we evaluated the capacity of 
CuS in sulfur cathode. As a result, with a mass ratio of 4% in the sulfur 
cathode, the capacity of CuS (theoretical specific capacity is 560 mAh 
g–1) only equivalent to 1.1% of the capacity contributed by S (Fig. S8 and 
Table S2), demonstrating that the capacity of CuS does not influence the 
experimental results. The charging/discharging curve (Fig. 3a) of the 
cell with the S/KB@CuS cathode exhibits a reduced polarization (0.526 
V) compared with the cell with the S/KB cathode (0.737 V), confirming 
the embedded CuS nanoparticles successfully reduce the reaction bar­
rier. The reduced polarization could also be observed in the cyclic vol­
tammetry (CV) curves. As the results shown in Fig. 3b, with the 
introduction of CuS polar sites, the oxidation peak that could be 
attributed to the conversion from Li2S to the sulfur of the cell is reduced 
from 2.72 to 2.56 V, demonstrating the improved dynamic for the cell 
with S/KB@CuS cathode [17]. The current peak located at 1.98 V may 
be attributed to the discharge of CuS, as the CV curve of Li||CuS half cell 
shown in Fig. S9. Fig. 3c and d display the Tafel slopes calculated based 
on the Tafel plots in Fig. S10 for the cells with S/KB and S/KB@CuS 
cathodes, it is observed that for both the discharging (~1.90 V) and 
charging (~2.40 V) processes, the Tafel slopes of the cell with 
S/KB@CuS cathode are smaller than the control cell, reflecting the 
resistance of the electrode process is weakened with the introduction of 
CuS [18]. To further verify the improvement of dynamics of the cell with 
the S/KB@CuS cathode, we measured the electrochemical impedance

the long-term cycling stability and energy efficiency of cells with S/KB 
and S/KB@CuS cathodes. The control cell exhibits a rapid capacity 
decay and a low energy efficiency, which could be attributed to the 
accumulation of Li2S. In contrast, the cell with S/KB@CuS cathode de­
livers a higher reversible capacity of 805.9 mAh g−1 after ninty cycles, 
and improved energy efficiency of ~8.8% (the capacity increasing of the 
cell with the S/KB@CuS cathode at initial cycles may attribute to the 
gradually-improved wettability of G2-based SSEs to cathode caused by 
the volume expansion of sulfur species during discharging process). In 
addition, we assembled Li||Li2S half cells to confirm the efficiency of the 
catalytic effect of CuS. Li2S particles are used as active materials to 
ensure that the particle size of Li2S is consistent with or without CuS in 
the initial charging process. As shown in Fig. S16, with the same particle 
size of Li2S, CuS cannot reduce the polarization of the Li2S decomposi­
tion in the initial charging process, indicating the induced formation of 
small-sized particles that play a major role in the acceleration of dy­
namics for the Li2S decomposition. In conclusion, the introduction of 
polar CuS nanoparticles successfully suppressed the aggregation of Li2S 
in the sulfur cathode, thus promoting the dynamics and cycling per­
formance of LSBs. To further extend the cyclability for the potential 
practical applications of SSEs-based LSBs, the dead Li issue and the 
growth of Li dendrite should be alleviated by optimizing the electrolyte 
composition and Li anode protection. 
The performance of LSBs under high and low temperatures is criti­
cally significant for their practical applications [20]. To explore the 
potential of the strategy inducing the uniform deposition of Li2S through 
the dipole-dipole interaction in SSEs-based LSBs, we compared the 
electrochemical performance of cells with the S/KB and the S/KB@CuS 
cathodes at demanding working environments such as 60 and −15 ◦C. 
Fig. 4a shows the second discharging/charging curves of cells with the 
S/KB and S/KB@CuS cathodes at 60 ◦C. The cell with the S/KB@CuS 
cathode shows low polarization of 0.250 V and a discharge capacity of 
1327.3 mAh g−1, much better than those of the control cell (0.684 V and 
662.1 mAh g−1). It is noticed that the cell with the S/KB cathode ex­
periences a rapid capacity attenuation, which is even faster than that 
observed at 25 ◦C (Fig. 4b). The early failure of the control cell could be 
ascribed to the more serious aggregation of undelithiated Li2S particles 
at a higher temperature, as the cell returned to a normal working state 
after being recharged to 4.2 V to promote the full delithiation of Li2S 
(Fig. S17). In contrast, the cell with S/KB@CuS cathode shows improved 
cycling stability with enhanced energy efficiency. The same results were 
obtained by electrochemical performance tests at 40 ◦C (Fig. S18). At 
low temperatures, the SSEs-based LSBs suffer from poor dynamics [21]. 
As the discharging/charging curves of cells with the S/KB and the 
S/KB@CuS cathodes at −15 ◦C shown in Fig. 4c, both cells show 
increased polarization as compared with those at 25 ◦C. The discharge 
capacity of the cell with the S/KB cathode is less than 400.0 mAh g−1 and 
only remains 234.1 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the 
cell with the S/KB@CuS cathode achieves a specific capacity of ~ 900.0 
mAh g−1 after the activation process of ~25 cycles and retains a capacity 
of 799.0 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. The improved electrochemical per­
formance demonstrates the uniform deposition of Li2S derived by the 
dipole-dipole interaction can also improve the dynamic of SSEs-based 
LSBs at low temperatures. 
To further confirm the practicability of the dipole-dipole-interaction 
strategy, we assembled pouch cells with a double-side sulfur cathode 
(40mm × 45mm). With a total sulfur loading of 135 mg (3.5 to 4.0 
mgsulfur cm–2 for each side) and a lean-electrolyte condition (electrolyte/ 
sulfur (E/S) ratio of 3.8 μL mg–1), the pouch cell could still deliver a 
capacity of 155.6 mA, and survived nine cycles (Fig. 4e). With two as- 
assembled pouch cells, we were able to light up an LED lamp plate 
(working voltage: 4 V) as shown in Fig. 4f. 
The as-proposed dipole-dipole-interaction strategy shows high uni­
versality, which was first confirmed by comparing the polarizations of 
the cells obtained with acetonitrile (AN)-based, trimethylene glycol 
monomethyl ether (G3)-based, tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G4)- 
based and G2-based SSEs. As the results are shown in Fig. 5a, the cells 
with S/KB@CuS cathodes exhibit lower polarization than those of the 
cells with the S/KB cathode in all of the above SSEs. In addition, as 
shown in Fig. 5b, by replacing CuS with other polar materials such as 
LiCoO2, Co3O4, and MoS2, reduced polarizations could also be obtained 
(corresponding discharging/charging curves are shown in Fig. S19). 
However, due to the weaker electronic conductivity and weaker inter­
action with Li2S of these other polar inorganic materials compared with 
CuS (Fig. S20 and Table S4), they do not improve the polarization of 
SSEs-based LSBs as much as CuS. Anyway, the universality is beneficial 
for finding other materials to improve the electrochemical properties of 
the cathode in SSEs-based LSBs. 
3. Conclusion 
In summary, by introducing CuS nanoparticles, which have high 
dipole-dipole interaction with Li2S, as the polar sites into the carbon 
matrix of the sulfur electrode, the uniform deposition of Li2S is realized. 
As a result, the conversion from Li2S to S is accelerated, which promotes 
the cycling stability and sulfur utilization of SSEs-based LSBs at a wide 
temperature range. Furthermore, the universality of polar materials in 
improving the dynamic of LSBs in various types of SSEs through dipole- 
dipole interaction is confirmed. Overall, we provide a simple and 
effective strategy for the cathode fabrication of SSEs-based LSBs, which 
may activate the practical application for SSEs-based LSBs.
