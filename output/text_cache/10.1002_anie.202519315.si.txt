Experimental Section 

Materials
Li metal foils (400 and 50 μm) were purchased from Tianjin Zhongneng Lithium Industry Co., Ltd. Cyclopentyl Methyl Ether (CPME, 98%, Energy Chemical), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, battery grade, LionGo New Energy), 1,3-dixolane (DOL, battery grade, LionGo New Energy), lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, analytically pure, LionGo New Energy), and LiNO3 (99.99% metals basis, Aladdin) were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of electrolytes and electrodes
The conventional DOL/DME electrolyte was prepared with 1.0 M LiTFSI and 2.0 wt.% LiNO3 dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1, by vol.). CPME/DME electrolyte was composed of 0.4 M LiTFSI and 0.4 M LiNO3 dissolved in CPME/DME (4:6, by vol.). Both electrolytes were prepared in an argon-filled glove box.
Sulfur/carbon nanotube (S/CNT) composites were prepared by infiltrating sublimed sulfur (battery-grade, Aladdin) into CNTs (battery-grade, Shandong Dazhan Nano Materials Co.) via a melt-diffusion method. CNTs and pristine sulfur were ball-milled at a 1:4 mass ratio for 4 h. The mixture was then transferred to a sealed vessel and heated at 155 °C for 10 h. After cooling to room temperature, the S/CNT composite was obtained. 
A 20 wt.% aqueous ethanol (EtOH, 99.7%, Shanghai Rongrun Chemical Reagent Co.) solution was prepared by mixing EtOH with deionized water. S@C cathodes were fabricated as follows: The S/CNT active material, conductive additives (Super P (battery-grade, Termeco GMBH): CNT: graphene (battery-grade, Knano Graphene Technology Co.) at 6:2:2 mass ratio), and LA133 binder (battery-grade, Sichuan Indigo Technology Co.) were mixed in the EtOH solution at 8:1:1 mass ratio to form a slurry with 25 wt.% solids content. The slurry was coated onto aluminum foil and dried at 80 °C for 6 h. The sulfur content in the final electrode was 64%, with sulfur loadings of 1.1, 4.1, and 6.2 mgₛ cm⁻² for electrochemical testing. 

Materials Characterizations
Solutions containing Li₂S₈, Li₂S₆, and Li₂S₄ were prepared by dissolving S/Li₂S mixtures (molar ratios: 7:1, 5:1, and 3:1) in DOL/DME or CPME/DME solvents. Phase separation behavior during aging was documented using a high-resolution digital camera.
To determine the composition of co-existing phases, saturated Li₂S₈ solutions were prepared in various solvents. After sampling, volumetric dilution, and quantitative analysis, sulfur content was measured by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, ICPOES7200, Thermo Scientific, USA). 
Ionic conductivity of electrolytes was characterized using a conductivity meter (DDS-307A, INESA Scientific Instrument Co., China). Dynamic viscosity of prepared electrolytes was evaluated at 25 °C with a digital rotational viscometer (NDJ-9S, Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., China).
All physicochemical properties in this work, including density, sulfur content, ionic conductivity, and viscosity, represent mean values derived from three independent measurements.
Ionic and molecular coordination information in different electrolytes was characterized by Raman spectroscopy using a 532 nm laser (Renishaw InVia reflex, Renishaw plc, UK) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (VERTEX 80v, Bruker Corporation, UK).
To observe the morphology and composition of the SEI on lithium anodes, Li-S batteries were disassembled after 50 cycles. Lithium anodes were rinsed three times with pure DME to remove residual electrolytes and thoroughly dried within the glovebox. Prior to SEM, XPS, and TOF-SIMS testing, electrodes were preserved in custom-made argon-filled containers to prevent air exposure during transfer. Morphological characterization was performed using scanning electron microscopy (Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan) at 15.0 kV accelerating voltage. Chemical composition of the SEI was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (PHI 5000 VersaProbe III, ULVAC-PHI, Japan) with Al Kα radiation. TOF-SIMS depth profiling (PHI nanoTOF II, ULVAC-PHI, Japan) was conducted in negative ion mode, where secondary ions were generated using a 2 keV Cs⁺ ion beam for surface sputtering. Compositional depth profiling employed a pulsed 30 keV Bi₃⁺ primary ion beam in high-current mode, with sputtering and analysis areas of 400 × 400 μm² and 100 × 100 μm², respectively.
All ¹⁷O NMR spectra were acquired on a JNM-ECA600 spectrometer using deuterated chloroform (CDCl₃) as solvent. The resonance frequency for ¹⁷O was 81.34 MHz with 2000 scans and a relaxation time of 1 s. ⁷Li NMR measurements were performed at 233.18 MHz with 64 scans and a relaxation time of 5 s. All experiments were conducted at ambient temperature.

Electrochemical measurements
Li-S coin cells were assembled in a standard 2025 coin cell battery. To evaluate cycling performance and shuttle current, the following configurations were tested under mild conditions: low-loading sulfur cathode (1.1 mgS cm⁻², 12 mm diameter), thick Li foil anode (400 μm, 16 mm diameter), a high electrolyte/sulfur loading ratio (E/S = 60 μL mgS⁻¹), and polypropylene (PP) separator (Celgard 2500, 19 mm diameter). Charge/discharge tests were performed using a Neware battery test system (MIHW-200-160CH-B, Shenzhen, China) in constant current mode at 0.2C and 0.5C rates (1C = 1600 mA g⁻¹) over a voltage range of 1.7–2.6 V. Under practical conditions, the performance of the battery was tested at a current density of 0.1C and a constant temperature of 25 °C using a high-capacity sulfur cathode (4.1 mgS cm⁻²), a thin Li foil anode (50 μm) and a low electrolyte/sulfur loading ratio (E/S = 6 μL mgS⁻¹). In order to analyze the morphology and composition of the SEI on the surface of the Li anode, the battery was disassembled after 50 cycles. Li||Cu cells were assembled in a standard 2032 coin cell battery and tested with a current density of 0.5 mA cm⁻² and a surface capacity of 1.0 mA h cm⁻².
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted using an electrochemical workstation (Zennium XC, Zahner Elektrik, Germany) with a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz and a perturbation amplitude of 5 mV. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of pure solvents were measured using an electrochemical workstation (Zennium XC, Zahner Elektrik, Germany), with carbon-coated current collector as working electrode and lithium as counter electrode and reference electrode, at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s⁻¹.
To measure shuttle currents, lithium-sulfur batteries were first charged to full capacity. Using an electrochemical workstation (Zennium XC, Zahner Elektrik, Germany) in chronoamperometry (CA) mode, constant voltages were applied at 2.90, 2.60, 2.45, 2.43, 2.40, 2.39, 2.38, 2.37, 2.36, 2.35, 2.34, 2.25, and 2.20 V. Due to polysulfide shuttle effects, the cell potential gradually decreases. The steady-state current required to counteract this potential decay is defined as the shuttle current.
The in-situ Raman spectroscopy (LABRAM, HR) was tested with 785 nm laser excitation. A three-dimensional current collector was employed for the cathode, and an additional carbon cloth was used as the electrolyte retention layer. The Raman beams were focused on this electrolyte layer. The Li-S visible cell was assembled in a H-type cell by using S/C (coated on the carbon cloth, area capacity 7 mA h cm-2) as cathode and Li metal (400 μm) as anode. The current density of H-type cell is 0.7 mA cm-2, and the images were recorded based on the different points of the discharge curves.

Li-S pouch cells
Li-S pouch cells were assembled in a dry room with a dew point below -40 °C. The single-layer pouch cells were assembled using a high sulfur-loading cathode (4.3 mgs cm⁻², size 6.5 × 4.5 cm²), a Li-plated Cu foil anode (50 μm Li, size 6.7 × 4.7 cm²), a PP separator (Celgard 2500), and a low electrolyte/sulfur loading ratio (E/S = 7.5 μL mgs⁻¹). The battery is tested at a constant current charge and discharge rate of 0.05C in the voltage range of 1.7–2.6 V. 
Multi-layer pouch cells (1.8 Ah) were assembled using four-layer stacks of high-loading sulfur electrodes (the loading of each side is 6.2 mgs cm⁻², size 6.5 × 4.5 cm²), five-layer stacks of thin Li foil anode (the loading of each side is 50 μm Li, size 6.7 × 4.7 cm²), PP separator (Celgard 2500), and low electrolyte/sulfur loading ratio (E/S = 3 μL mgs⁻¹). The battery is tested at a constant current charge and discharge rate of 0.05C in the voltage range of 1.7–2.6 V. All pouch cell tests are performed in a 25 °C incubator. 

Theoretical calculations
Materials Studio software was used for theoretical simulation calculations in this work. DMol3 module was used to perform DFT calculations to optimize the structure of ions and solvent molecules in different electrolytes, and calculate the electrostatic potentials and binding energy () of solvents and LiPSs.[1] The  was calculated by the following formula: . ,  and  were the total energy of the Li2S8-solvent, single Li2S8 and single solvent, respectively.[2] Geometry optimizations were performed using the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA)/Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional and a double numeric polarization (DNP) basis set.[3] The convergence criteria was set to be 1.0 × 10-5 Ha for energy, 2.0 × 10-3 Ha Å-1 for maximum force, and 5.0 × 10-3 Å for maximum displacement. Classical MD simulations were carried out using the Forcite package in Materials Studio.[4] Two amorphous cells were constructed for the Li2S8/DOL/DME with molar ratio of 10:144:100 and the Li2S8/CPME/DME with molar ratio of 10:90:100. The COMPASSIII force field was applied, with atom types and partial charges optimized based on previously validated studies.[5,6] The system was first equilibrated in the NPT ensemble for 50 ps (picoseconds) with a time step of 1.0 fs (femtoseconds) under controlled thermodynamic conditions. A Nosé-Hoover thermostat and a Parrinello-Rahman barostat were ultilized[7,8], with the following setting: Cell time constant: 1.0 ps; Compressibility: 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ GPa⁻¹; Coupling mode: Isotropic. The simulations were conducted at 298.15 K and 1 bar. Following equilibration, production runs were carried out in the NVT ensemble, maintaining the temperature at 298.15 K using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat. A 1.0 fs time step and a total simulation duration of 2 ns were employed.
