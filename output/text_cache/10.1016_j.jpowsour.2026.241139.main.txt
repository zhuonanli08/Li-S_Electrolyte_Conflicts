A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur batteries are promising next-generation alternatives to lithium-ion batteries owing to their high 
energy density. However, practical application is hindered by Li dendrite growth, parasitic anode reactions, and 
undecomposed Li2S accumulation at high sulfur loading, which accelerate capacity fading and impair cycling 
stability. In this work, we investigate the role of lithium polysulfide additives in stabilizing the Li-metal anode 
and modulating Li2S deposition morphology at the cathode. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further 
elucidate the interaction affinity and polarity of polysulfide additives, providing critical insights into their 
reactivity with Li metal. Additionally, the polysulfide additives enabled a high initial areal capacity of 5.6 mAh 
cm−2 (870 mAh g−1) and stable cycling over 200 cycles with a capacity retention of 55% at 0.3C with a high 
sulfur loading cathode (6.5 mg cm−2), attributed to the induced 3D growth of Li2S. This study proposes a dual 
interface modulation strategy for Li–S batteries using polysulfide additives, which simultaneously facilitates the

1. Introduction
With the growing demand for high energy density rechargeable 
batteries, particularly for unmanned aerial vehicles, lithium-sulfur 
(Li–S) batteries have emerged as one of the most promising next- 
generation energy storage technologies [1]. Owing to the high theo­
retical specific capacity of lithium (3860 mAh g−1) and sulfur (1675 
mAh g−1), Li–S batteries offer a high energy density up to 2600 Wh kg−1, 
far exceeding that of conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) [2]. In 
addition to their energy advantage, sulfur is abundant, inexpensive, and 
environmentally benign, making Li–S batteries an attractive energy 
storage system [3–8]. Despite these advantages, the practical imple­
mentation of Li–S batteries is hindered by severe interfacial instabilities 
at both electrodes. The Li-metal anode is plagued by dendritic growth 
and continuous parasitic reactions with the electrolyte, leading to the 
formation of a chemically unstable and inhomogeneous solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) [9]. These issues result in rapid electrolyte consumption 
and poor cycle life. On the cathode side, the soluble lithium polysulfide 
intermediates (Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) generated during redox conversion 
readily migrate to the anode, triggering parasitic shuttle reactions 
[10–12]. Moreover, under practical high sulfur loading conditions 
(>5 mg cm−2), the sluggish conversion kinetics promote the formation 
of insulating, two-dimensional (2D) Li2S deposits that passivate the 
cathode surface, hindering subsequent electrochemical reactions 
[13–15]. Electrolyte additives have been widely explored to mitigate 
these issues, including LiNO3 [16,17], tetraethylammonium nitrate 
[18], LiPO2F2 [19], LiDFOB [20], or specific polysulfides [21–23]. 
Although lithium polysulfides such as Li2S8 have previously been 
employed as electrolyte additives in Li–S batteries, their functions have 
generally been discussed mainly in terms of anode passivation [24–26].

chain-length-controlled polysulfide species and their coupled effects on 
both the Li-metal anode and sulfur cathode interfaces have not been

different chain lengths. In addition, concentration-dependent perfor­

length and concentration govern interfacial chemistry is essential for 
establishing rational polysulfide-mediated electrolyte design principles.
Here, we introduce low-concentration, chain-length-controlled pol­
ysulfide additives (Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8) as a dual-function electrolyte 
engineering strategy to stabilize both the anode and cathode. Specif­
ically, the polysulfide additives are employed as a pre-aging agent that 
promotes the rapid formation of a uniform sulfur-derived interphase at 
the onset of cycling. By providing a controlled and homogeneous sulfur- 
containing chemical interphase, this approach suppresses localized Li 
corrosion and mitigates early-stage heterogeneity. We further establish a 
chain-length-dependent additive design by correlating polysulfide sol­
vation stability and molecular polarity with the evolution of lithium 
reversibility. Concurrently, this controlled polysulfide mediation is 
leveraged to modulate the sulfur cathode chemistry by facilitating three- 
dimensional (3D) Li2S precipitation over traditional 2D film-like growth. 
This morphological shift effectively alleviates cathode passivation, 
ensuring that active surface sites remain accessible and preserving rapid 
redox conversion kinetics even under practical high-loading conditions. 
This dual-electrode regulation is supported by density functional theory 
and ab initio molecular dynamics calculations, which elucidate how 
polysulfide solvation stability and polarity govern interphase evolution, 
interfacial reaction pathways, and the balance between beneficial 
passivation and parasitic consumption. Therefore, Li–S pouch cells 
(5 × 5 cm2) demonstrate enhanced cycling stability and capacity 
retention, delivering a high reversible initial capacity of 887 mAh g−1 at 
0.3C over 159 cycles with a sulfur loading of 2.5 mg cm−2. These results 
show the efficacy of controlled polysulfide additives as a dual-function 
electrolyte design strategy, which simultaneously suppresses anode 
degradation and cathode passivation in high-performance Li–S batteries.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
Lithium (Li) metal foils (Honjo Metal Co., Ltd.), 1, 3-Dioxolane (DOL, 
Sigma-Aldrich), 1, 2-Dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich), Lithium 
bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich), Lithium 
nitrate (LiNO3, Sigma-Aldrich), elemental sulfur (S, Sigma-Aldrich), 
Lithium sulfide (Li2S, Sigma-Aldrich), Ceramic coated separators 
(Hebei Gellec New Energy, China; thickness: 16 μm, porosity: ~42%), 
Carbon nanotube (CNT, Sigma-Aldrich), A commercial carbon paper 
(H23C5, Freudenberg, Germany; thickness: 220 μm, bulk density: 
~0.50 g/cm3, porosity: ~76%), Polyacrylic acid (PAA), Isopropanol 
(IPA), deionized (DI) water were used as received without further 
purification.
2.2. Preparation of electrolyte
The blank electrolyte was composed of 1.0 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 
in DOL/DME (1:1, by volume). Prior to electrolyte preparation, all 
powder materials were dried in a vacuum oven at 90 ◦C for 24h. The 
polysulfide containing electrolyte was prepared by dissolving Li2S and 
elemental sulfur into the blank electrolyte. A 0.2 M concentrated poly­
sulfide solution was first synthesized and then diluted with the blank 
electrolyte to obtain lower concentration polysulfide solutions. The final 
mixtures were stirred at 30-40 ◦C for 24h until homogeneous solutions 
were obtained. All electrolyte preparation procedures were conducted 
inside an Ar-filled glovebox.
2.3. Preparation of S/C composite cathodes
We fabricated an S/C composite by infiltrating sulfur into CNTs via a 
melting diffusion process conducted at 155 ◦C, resulting in a sulfur 
content of 80%. The cathode electrode was prepared by slurry casting a 
mixture composed of the active S/C composite (90 wt%), a carbon based 
conductive agent (Super-P, 5 wt%), and a binder solution consisting of 
high molecular-weight PAA (450,000 g/mol) dissolved in deionized 
water (10 wt%), which accounted for 5 wt% of the overall formulation. 
In addition, 4g of a solvent mixture of isopropanol and deionized water 
(IPA: DI = 5: 95, w/w) was added to adjust the slurry viscosity. The 
slurry was subsequently mixed using a Resodyn acoustic mixer for 3 min 
and then cast onto carbon paper. The coated electrodes were dried in an 
oven at 80 ◦C for 2h.
2.4. Battery performance evaluation
The Li | Li symmetric coin cells were assembled in standard CR2032 
coin cells using ultrathin Li foils (20 μm, 16.0 mm diameter) and 
ceramic-coated separators (19.0 mm diameter). The cells were tested at 
a current density of 1.0 mA cm−2 with an areal capacity of 1.0 mAh 
cm−2, as well as at 5.0 mA cm−2 with areal capacities of 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 
mAh cm−2. For interfacial resistance analysis, electrochemical imped­
ance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted on Li| Li symmetric cells

2.5. Materials characterizations
The surface morphologies of the sulfur cathodes and Li-metal anodes 
were examined using Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE- 
SEM, Hitachi S-4800, 5 kV). For analysis, electrodes were rinsed with 
DME to remove residual electrolyte and subsequently dried. Surface 
chemical analysis was carried out using an X-ray Photoelectron Spec­
troscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific NEXSA G2). For Atomic Force Micro­
scope (AFM) analysis, Li metal was immersed in the blank electrolyte 
and an electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S6, and aged for 2, 12, and 24 h. 
After the aging process, the electrodes were rinsed with DME prior to 
AFM measurements.
2.6. Li2S deposition testing
For the Li2S deposition experiments, Li2S6 cells were assembled using 
carbon paper as the cathode and Li metal as the anode. The electrolyte 
consisted of 20 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6 dissolved in DME and an additional 
100 μL of a modified electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S6. For compari­
son, 100 μL of the blank electrolyte was used under otherwise identical 
conditions. The cells were first discharged to 2.11 V at a constant current 
of 0.2 mA, followed by a constant-voltage step at 2.02 V for 50 min. The 
Li2S deposition behavior was analyzed using chronoamperometry-based 
nucleation models [27,28]. The Bewick–Fleischmann–Thirsk (BFT) 
models describing 2D progressive (2DP) and instantaneous (2DI) 
nucleation and the Scharifker–Hills (SH) models describing 3D pro­
gressive (3DP) and instantaneous (3DI) nucleation were employed [29].
2.7. Computational details
For the structural optimization of polysulfide additives (Li2S4, Li2S6, 
and Li2S8) and calculation of the adsorptions at anode and cathode in­
terfaces, DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio 
Simulation Package (VASP) [30]. A generalized gradient approximation 
(GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [31] form was used for the 
exchange and correlation functionals, and the Grimme-D3 method [32] 
was applied to consider van der Waals forces. The k-point sampling and 
cutoff energy of plane-wave basis set were determined to satisfy 
convergence criteria of total energy changes below 10 meV per atom and 
pressures below 10 kbar. All structures were fully optimized until the 
forces on each atom were less than 0.01 eV Å−1. The initial molecular 
structures of polysulfides and electrolyte (DOL and DME) species were 
considered from former literatures [33–36]. To accurately mimic the 
realistic solvation environment, we explicitly accounted for the solvent 
coordination effect by exploring configurations coordinated with two 
solvent molecules, as detailed in Fig. S1. For modeling the interfaces of 
Li-metal anode and sulfur cathode, bcc-Li (1 1 0) surface [37] and S8 (1 
0 0) surface structures were used, respectively (Fig. S2). For the surface 
calculations, the bottom layer is fixed during structural relaxation. 
AIMD simulations were also performed to investigate the solvation 
structures of polysulfides in the DOL/DME environment. Initial config­
urations were generated using the PACKMOL package [38], in which 
atoms were randomly placed in a cubic simulation box with a volume of 
1 nm3. Each system consisted of one polysulfide together with four DOL 
and four DME molecules, resulting in a concentration of approximately 
1 M and a total of ~120 atoms. In this work, LiTFSI is not included in the 
simulations to avoid computational complexity. All AIMD simulations 
were performed in the canonical (NVT) ensemble at 300 K using a 
Nos´e–Hoover thermostat [39,40]. To obtain diverse solvation configu­
rations between polysulfides and DOL/DME molecules, the simulations 
were conducted for a total duration of 100 ps with a time step of 2 fs. The 
solvation energy (Esolvation) of polysulfides was calculated according to

3.1. Characterization of polysulfide additive-induced stabilization of Li 
metal
Li metal possesses the lowest reduction potential (−3.04 V vs. SHE), 
causing spontaneous reduction of electrolyte species upon contact, even 
during simple surface contact [49]. The resulting decomposition prod­
ucts accumulate on the Li surface, while concurrent oxidation of Li

occurs spontaneously, leading to undesirable parasitic reactions. To 
evaluate the corrosion behavior of the Li-metal anode in a conventional 
ether-based electrolyte, soaking tests were conducted using fresh 
Li-metal electrodes in the control electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME, 
50/50 v/v, with 0.2 M LiNO3) and in the control electrolyte containing 
0.02 M Li2S6 additive, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Severe corrosion spots 
appeared on Li metal in the control electrolyte after only 4 days, and the 
size of these corrosive regions progressively increased during the 60 
days soaking test. In contrast, the electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S6 
showed noticeable uniform corrosion, with the Li surface retaining its 
initial yellowish appearance from 4 days through 60 days of aging. As 
shown in the SEM results (Fig. 1(b)), Li metal soaked in the base elec­
trolyte for 13 days exhibited numerous cracks and a highly uneven 
surface due to aggressive corrosion. In contrast, the electrolyte with 
0.02 M Li2S6 additive produced a more uniform surface with minimal 
localized damage and smoother morphology. Cross-sectional analysis 
further revealed that the control electrolyte led to the formation of a 
thick, porous corrosion layer (~52 μm), whereas the Li2S6 containing 
electrolyte resulted in a significantly thinner layer (~33 μm). 
Furthermore, AFM analysis verified the evolution of surface roughness 
during the soaking test, showing that the control electrolyte exhibited 
increasing Rq values of 182, 219, and 245 nm after 2, 12, and 24 h of 
soaking, respectively (Fig. 1(c)). In contrast, the electrolyte containing 
0.02 M Li2S6 maintained nearly constant Rq values of 156, 150, and 
161 nm over the same aging periods, indicating suppressed surface 
degradation and a stable surface morphology during prolonged expo­
sure. To characterize the surface chemical composition of Li-metal 
anode, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was per­
formed. A noticeably diminished C-C bonding peak at 284.8 eV was 
observed for the electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S6, signifying sup­
pressed electrolyte decomposition at the interface [50,51]. This result 
indicates that, as evidenced by the reduced thickness of the porous layer 
on the Li metal, the Li2S6 additive promotes the formation of a more 
compact and uniform thin corrosion layer compared to the control 
electrolyte (Fig. 1(d)). As expected, the Li2S6 containing electrolyte 
exhibited a stronger Li2S signal, attributed to the co-oxidation of poly­
sulfides and electrolyte components. The soluble Li2S6 species act as 
mild oxidants that are spontaneously reduced to insoluble Li2S on the Li

within the first 12 h, after which the impedance remained relatively

twice that of the control electrolyte. The 0.02 M Li2S6 and Li2S8 additives 
exhibited significantly lower saturated impedance during the aging 
period compared to the control electrolyte, suggesting the formation of a 
more stable interfacial layer. The effect of this stabilized interface on 
electrochemical performance will be further discussed.

superior cycling performance in symmetric cells. This result suggests 
that the medium chain length of Li2S6 provides an optimal balance for 
stabilizing the Li surface, effectively suppressing excessive porosity 
while maintaining structural integrity during repeated plating/strip­
ping. To investigate the evolution of interfacial resistance during 
cycling, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted 
on Li symmetric cells (Fig. 2(c)). The control electrolyte initially 
exhibited a low impedance of <2 Ω, which included both solid elec­
trolyte interphase resistance and charge transfer resistance, and this 
value remained relatively stable up to the 50th cycle. However, after 
100 cycles, the impedance increased nearly threefold, indicating sig­
nificant growth of interfacial resistance during repeated Li plating/ 
stripping. This increase is attributed to the formation of a resistive 
porous layer on the Li surface, which hindered efficient charge transfer. 
In contrast, the electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S6 exhibited a lower 
initial impedance of less than <1.5 Ω, which remained stable up to 150 
cycles. These results indicate that even a small amount of Li2S6 additive 
effectively reduces interfacial resistance and sustains a stable interface 
between the electrolyte and the Li-metal anode. To further verify the 
influence of polysulfide chain length on Li reversibility, Li/Cu half-cell 
tests were conducted to evaluate the stripping/plating efficiency on a 
Cu current collector (Fig. 2(d)). In the control electrolyte without ad­
ditives, the cells displayed unstable Coulombic efficiency from the initial 
cycles, dropping below 90% by the 10th cycle. Progressive efficiency 
loss ultimately led to cell failure by the 60th cycle, at which point the 
Coulombic efficiency had decreased to ~77%. These results indicated 
the severe instability of Li deposition/stripping in the absence of poly­
sulfide additives. For the electrolyte containing 0.02 M Li2S4, the Li/Cu 
half-cell sustained a Coulombic efficiency of ~98% up to the 30th cycle, 
followed by a progressive decline that culminated in complete cell 
failure by the 45th cycle. In contrast, the 0.02 M Li2S6 additive delivered 
markedly improved stability, maintaining ~99% Coulombic efficiency 
for up to 180 cycles. The longest-chain polysulfide, Li2S8, also achieved 
high Coulombic efficiency (~99%), but its cycle retention was limited to 
fewer than 86 cycles, indicating higher long-term stability compared to 
Li2S4. Fig. 2(e) shows the voltage profiles of the Li/Cu half cells, where 
the control sample exhibited a gradual decline in the plating to stripping 
capacity ratio, indicating a decrease in Coulombic efficiency. In 
contrast, the cell with 0.02 M Li2S6 additive maintained nearly identical 
overpotentials during plating and stripping, achieving a Coulombic ef­

3.2. Theoretical investigation of chain length dependent solvation 
behavior of polysulfide
The observations so far, showing pronounced differences in the 
passivation layer thickness and porosity that are closely correlated with 
the interfacial resistance and Coulombic efficiency, suggest that poly­
sulfide additives fundamentally modulate interfacial reactions and the 
resulting surface-layer properties on the Li-metal anode. Notably, the 
Li2S6 additive produced the thinnest and most compact porous layer and 
maintained low interfacial resistance and high reversibility, indicating 
that Li2S6 additive may play a distinct role from other polysulfide ad­
ditives. To elucidate the atomic-scale origin of these experimental re­
sults, we conducted theoretical modeling on the solvation structures and 
polarity of polysulfide additives as a function of chain length. In Fig. 3
(a), the calculated solvation energies, which account for the energy 
difference of each polysulfide structure with or without surrounding 
electrolytes (1:1 mixture of DOL and DME), are plotted from ab initio 
molecular dynamics (AIMD) trajectories. (Details of the AIMD simula­
tions are provided in Experimental Section and Fig. S7–S9) The solva­
tion energy plot was constructed by averaging every 10 single-point 
energies calculated at 0.1 ps intervals over the final 50 ps of the AIMD 
trajectory at 300 K. Though the solvation energies are considerably 
fluctuating by the finite temperature effect, the obvious trend can be 
derived that the longer chain length results stronger interaction with 
electrolytes. As the lower (i.e., stronger) solvation energy indicates 
greater thermodynamical stability and better dispersibility, Li2S4, Li2S6, 
and Li2S8 are expected to be increasingly stable and more uniformly 
distributed in the electrolyte, in that order. Notably, Li2S6 and Li2S8 
often exhibit similar solvation energies, which is consistent with the 
experimental observation that these two additives yield significantly 
lower saturated impedance in the soaking tests (Fig. 1(e)). In addition, 
we also quantitatively compare the dipole moments of the polysulfide 
additives as shown in Fig. 3(b), calculated from snapshots sampled every 
2.5 ps in the final 50 ps of AIMD trajectory. The corresponding time 
evolution of the dipole moments is provided in Fig. S10. The represen­
tative solvent-coordinated structures of polysulfides are provided in 
Fig. 3(c–e), where distinct configurations were selected from structures 
located near the most probable dipole moment values. Specially, LiS6
−
structures were observed in the 50–70 ps region of the AIMD trajectories 
(Fig. 3(a)). This result is consistent with previous reports indicating that 
Li2S6 can exist in the electrolyte in the form of LiS6
−through the disso­
ciation reaction (Li2S6 → Li+ + LiS6
−) [53,54]. Consequently, Li2S6 ex­
hibits the lowest dipole moment values, followed by Li2S4 and Li2S8, 
respectively. According to recent reports, low-polarity electrolytes 
suppress side reactions between Li-metal anode and lithium poly­
sulfides, enhance SEI stability, and protect the electrode interface [55]. 
In this context, the relatively low dipole moment of Li2S6 additive in­
dicates that they establish a comparatively low-polar environment at the 
Li-metal anode interface, which can effectively mitigate excessive 
parasitic reactions. Overall, the low polarity of Li2S6 supports the for­
mation of a thin and stable passivation layers in electrolytes containing 
Li2S6 additive, thereby stabilizing the interface, while its strong solva­
tion energy enhances the electrochemical performance of the Li-metal 
anode. In contrast, the longest-chain Li2S8 adopts a ring-opened struc­
ture (Fig. 3(e)) and exhibits relatively low solvation energy (at 57-85 ps 
region in Fig. 3(a)). As consequence, ring-opened Li2S8 are more likely to 
exist in the electrolyte, which leads to lower structural stability 
compared to the Li2S6. Additional molecular dynamics (MD) simulation 
performed in a larger simulation cell assisted by machine-learned 
interatomic potential (MLIP) also support that Li2S6 distributes uni­
formly in the electrolyte, whereas Li2S8 predominantly exists in 
ring-opened, dissolved configurations (Fig. S11).

Chronoamperometry at 2.02 V was employed to study the precipi­
tation of Li2S on carbon paper substrates during the electrochemical 
reduction of liquid phase lithium polysulfides. As shown in Fig. 6(a and 
b) the potentiostatic discharge current–time (i–t) curves reveal the Li2S 
precipitation process, as characterized by the profile of each curve. To 
further analyze the Li2S deposition mechanism, both current and time 
were normalized by the maximum current (im) and its corresponding 
time (tm) to construct dimensionless i–t plots [59]. These normalized 
profiles enable the identification of Li2S nucleation and growth behavior

The presence of a small amount of 0.02 M Li2S6 additive effectively 
influences the initial Li2S precipitation and subsequent Li2S reduction, 
leading to 3D growth behavior. Ex-situ SEM images of the Li2S deposits

growth mechanism; the control sample without the additive exhibited a

over lateral dispersion corroborates the observed macroscopic transition 
from a 2D film to 3D agglomerate growth. To further elucidate the 
atomic-scale mechanism of the 3D growth, we also investigated the 
sequential adsorption behavior of Li2S6 (Fig. S20). Notably, a second 
Li2S6 molecule exhibits a significantly stronger adsorption energy 
(−1.49 eV) when binding adjacent to a pre-adsorbed Li2S6, which serves 
as a highly active and vertically oriented nucleation site. Consequently, 
the transition from a 2D film-like morphology to 3D spherical growth 
upon the addition of 0.02 M Li2S6 is attributed not only to the altered 
nucleation energy barrier but also to the mediation of solution-phase 
kinetics. In the control electrolyte, high interfacial energy drives Li2S 
to spread laterally (2D) to minimize surface energy, leading to rapid 
surface passivation. Conversely, the Li2S6 additive promotes a 
solution-mediated growth pathway. By enriching the interfacial region 
with long-chain polysulfides, it lowers the desolvation energy barrier for 
Li+ ions and facilitates the sustained transport of sulfur species toward 
pre-existing nuclei [62,63]. This shifts the growth from surface-limited 
(2D) to diffusion-controlled (3D), allowing Li2S to grow vertically into 
the electrolyte bulk as porous agglomerates rather than a dense, insu­
lating film.
4. Conclusion
A dual function electrolyte strategy using polysulfide additives was 
developed to simultaneously stabilize the Li-metal anode and regulate 
sulfur redox reactions. The sulfur-derived stable solid electrolyte inter­
phase (SEI), formed during the initial aging period with 0.02 M Li2S6 
additive, enabled stable cycling of a 20 μm Li-symmetric cell for 590 
cycles at 1 mA cm−2 with areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. At the cathode 
side, Li2S6 additive promotes uniform 3D Li2S growth, preventing sur­
face passivation and improves sulfur redox reaction reversibility. 
Consequently, the 0.02 M Li2S6 additive significantly improved the 
cycling stability of high-sulfur-loading coin type Li–S cells, which 
delivered an initial areal capacity of 5.6 mAh cm−2 and retained 55% of 
the capacity after 200 cycles at 0.3C with a sulfur loading of 
6.5 mg cm−2. Furthermore, improved interfacial kinetics were 
confirmed by enhanced rate performance, reduced self-discharge, and 
lower voltage hysteresis. This study demonstrates that controlled poly­
sulfide mediation provides balanced anode protection and cathode 
redox enhancement at both electrodes, offering an effective and scalable 
route toward long life Li–S batteries.
