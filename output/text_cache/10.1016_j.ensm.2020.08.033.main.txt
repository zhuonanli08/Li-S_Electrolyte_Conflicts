The shuttling of soluble lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) intermediates bottlenecks practical uses of lithium-sulfur (Li- 
S) batteries. A commercial organometallic salt, nickel chloride dimethoxyethane adduct (NiDME), is introduced 
as the homogenous catalyst dissolved in the electrolyte to suppress the shuttle eﬀect. A very small amount of 
catalyst (only 0.5 wt% additive relative to the electrolyte) brings the battery capacity a big rise from 408 to 784 
mAh g − 1 over 500 cycles at 1 C. More promisingly, the required amount of electrolyte is greatly reduced, and 
the battery works well with a lean electrolyte (5 𝜇L (electrolyte) /mg (sulfur) ). The improved performance is attributed 
to the catalyst-accelerated LiPS conversion which is characterized by largely decreased activation energy (E a ) of 
the sulfur redox reaction, especially for Li 2 S deposition, and much reduced accumulation of LiPSs intermediates. 
Specially, the NiDME additive captures LiPSs in the electrolyte, accelerates the redox reactions homogeneously 
and regulates a uniform and fast deposition of Li 2 S, all these contributing to a perfect catalytic cycle with less 
LiPS shuttling. This work deepens our understanding on catalysis in accelerating sulfur redox conversion and 
indicates homogeneous catalysis as a simple yet fundamental solution to shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs intermediates, 
which boosts practical Li-S batteries with less electrolyte needs and long working life. 
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have been widely investigated as 
promising next-generation high-energy batteries [1–4] . However, the 
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) (Li 2 S n , 4 ≤ n ≤ 8) intermediates formed dur- 
ing the charging-discharging process are soluble in the electrolyte, re- 
sulting in a shuttling from cathode to anode and the fast capacity degra- 
dation. The shuttle eﬀect severely hinders their practical use. Porous car- 
bons with a high electrical conductivity and polar inorganic compounds 
with a strong chemical adsorption ability have been widely investigated 
as the sulfur hosts to block and suppress this shuttling [5–10] . However, 
the slow conversion of soluble LiPSs to insoluble Li 2 S or sulfur, results 
in the accumulation of LiPSs in the electrolyte, especially in long-term 
cycling or high sulfur loading conditions, which is the basic cause for 
the shuttle eﬀect and hard to be fundamentally suppressed by the pas-

Catalysis has recently been introduced into Li-S batteries, having 
aroused great interests due to the accelerated conversion of soluble 
LiPSs, indicating a proactive solution to the shuttle eﬀect [11,12] . Het- 
erogeneous catalysts like metals and metal oxides/sulﬁdes/nitrides and 
their heterostructures have been designed to achieve the strong adsorp- 
tion and fast catalytic conversion of LiPSs [13–15] . However, the cat- 
alytic conversion of LiPSs only occurs on their surface. The slow diﬀu- 
sion of LiPSs from the bulk electrolyte to the catalyst surface and the lim- 
ited accessible surface of the catalyst severely limit the catalytic reaction 
eﬃciency. Homogeneous catalysts that are dissolved in the electrolyte 
as an additive, which capture the LiPSs in the electrolyte and acceler- 
ate their conversion at the homogenous interfaces, should be better for 
solving the shuttling problem [16] . Some electrolyte additives, namely

lithium azide/iodide or organic solvents, have been used to suppress the 
shuttling of LiPSs, but do not normally show any catalytic activity for 
LiPS conversion and only physically block the shuttling [17,18] . Most of 
heterogeneous catalysts are inorganic which are insoluble in the organic 
electrolyte and cannot homogeneously interact with the dissolved LiPSs 
[19] . Single-atom catalysts with higher catalyst activity and utilization 
eﬃciency are also promising materials to improve the electrocatalysis 
in Li-S batteries [20] . However, to further improve the loading of single- 
atom is still a challenge. Alternatively, electrolyte-soluble organic addi- 
tives with catalytic activity could be used as homogeneous catalysts in 
Li-S batteries. The dissolved catalyst is directly introduced in the elec- 
trolyte to achieve homogeneous interactions with LiPSs. The captured 
LiPSs are deposited on the electrode without shuttling which produces 
fast conversion and regulates Li 2 S nucleation. 
Based on the above analysis, soluble organometallic compounds are 
potential candidates for the homogeneous catalysts, which enable inor- 
ganic salts to be dissolved in the electrolyte through complexing with 
the organic ligands. Here, a nickel-based organometallic additive (nickel 
chloride dimethoxyethane adduct, NiCl 2 . DME, denote NiDME), a cata- 
lyst usually for cross-coupling reactions, was used as a representative 
catalytic additive in the electrolyte [21] . Dimethoxyethane ( DME) was 
specially selected as the ligand because it is a normally used electrolyte 
solvent for Li-S batteries, which guarantees good solubility of NiDME 
in the electrolyte. Promisingly, according to the theoretical calculation, 
NiCl 2 shows a higher aﬃnity towards LiPSs yet lower aﬃnity towards el- 
emental sulfur than that towards DME, and therefore it can capture LiPSs 
in the electrolyte while releases DME in the discharging and rebind with 
DME after charging to the elemental sulfur, realizing recycling of the 
NiDME additive. Electrochemical measurements support the speculation 
by demonstrating the high catalytic activity of NiDME additive in the 
LiPS conversion with a much reduced activation energy (E a ) specially 
for the deposition of Li 2 S and the resulting fast redox rate. The NiDME 
additive also regulates the uniform deposition of Li 2 S on the carbon sub- 
strate, facilitating full utilization of the sulfur. As a result, with only 0.5 
wt% electrolyte additive, an improved cycling performance (784 mAh 
g − 1 at 1.0 C after 500 cycles) and high rate performance (635 mAh 
g − 1 at 3.0 C) are obtained for the battery. The accelerated LiPS conver- 
sion decreases the LiPS accumulation and reduces the required amount 
of electrolyte to 5 𝜇L (electrolyte) /mg (sulfur) . This study presents a simple 
yet fundamental approach to suppress the shuttle eﬀect using a small 
amount of soluble electrolyte additive, not making any changes to the 
battery conﬁguration, and shows great potential in promoting the prac- 
tical applications of Li-S batteries through homogenous catalysis. 
2. Material and methods 
2.1. Preparation of the electrolyte with NiDME additive 
The NiCl 2 . DME catalyst was purchased from Macklin Inc and directly 
used as the additive in commercial lithium-sulfur battery electrolyte 
(1 M bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with 1 wt % LiNO 3 
in DME and dioxolane (DOL) mixture (1:1 vol.)). Diﬀerent weight ratios 
of NiDME (0.8, 0.5 and 0.2 wt%) were added into the electrolyte and 
ultrasonicated 30 minutes to form stable solutions. 
2.2. Electrochemical measurements of the batteries 
The carbon/sulfur hybrid was synthesized by the heat-melting 
method at 155 °C for 10 h using the mesoporous carbon (CMK-3) as 
the host and sulfur nanoparticles as the active material (the weight ra- 
tio of sulfur was 70 wt%). The cathodes of the battery were prepared 
with the carbon/sulfur hybrid, conductive carbon (Super P) and binder 
(polyvinylidene ﬂuoride, PVDF) in a weight ratio of 8:1:1, and the areal 
sulfur loading was about 1 mg cm − 2 . The CR2032 coin cells were as- 
sembled in the Ar-ﬁlled glove box using Li foils as the anodes and above 
electrodes as the cathodes, and galvanotactic discharge-charge results of 
these cells were obtained using the LAND system. The cyclic voltamme- 
try (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were measured 
in the Princeton PARSTAT electrochemical work station. The frequency 
range of the EIS test is between 100 kHz and 0.01 Hz with 5 mV ampli- 
tude. 
2.3. The Li 2 S deposition measurements 
The Li 2 S deposition experiment was measured with assembled cells 
using carbon paper as the cathode and lithium foil as the anode. In 
the cell assembly process, 20 𝜇L electrolyte containing 0.2 M Li 2 S 8 
was added on the carbon paper side and another 20 𝜇L electrolyte 
with/without NiDME was dropped on the Li foil side. The cell was ﬁrst 
discharged at constant current to the 2.06 V and then discharged with 
the constant voltage at 2.05 V until the current was below 10 − 5 A for 
the nucleation and deposition of Li 2 S. 
2.4. The Li 2 S 6 symmetric cells measurements 
The symmetric cells were assembled using carbon paper as electrodes 
and added 40 𝜇L 0.5 M Li 2 S 6 solution as the electrolyte. Another 10 𝜇L 
electrolytes with/without NiDME was dropped on the separator. The 
CV proﬁles of symmetric cells were tested at the scan rate of 50 mV s − 1 
between -1.0 and 1.0 V. 
2.5. Characterization 
SEM images were obtained on a Hitachi SU-8010. The UV–vis spec- 
tra were measured through Agilent Cary 5000. The XPS spectra were 
collected by the PHI-5000 Versaprobe-II system.

2.8. In-situ EIS test 
The in-situ EIS cell was assembled with an EL-Cell three-electrode 
cell (ECC-Ref) with lithium metal as the counter and reference electrodes 
and carbon/sulfur electrode as the working electrode. The tests were 
carried at constant current (0.1 C) for discharging process between 2.8 V 
and 1.7 V. The cell was ﬁrst discharged for 1 h, then settled for 30 
minutes before measuring the impedance. The procedure was repeated 
several times until the cell was discharged to 1.7 V. The frequency range 
of the EIS test is between 100 kHz and 0.01 Hz with 5 mV amplitude. 
2.9. In-situ UV-Vis spectra tests 
The UV Lambda 750 UV/Vis/NIR spectrometer was used to obtain 
the in-situ UV/Vis spectra of the assembled cell. The carbon/sulfur elec- 
trode was used as the cathode with a sulfur loading of 1 mg cm − 2 . The 
lithium foil was used as the counter electrode, in the middle of which 
was a hole (one third the size of the cathode) to expose the UV light. 
The 100 μL electrolyte with/without NiDME was used and added on the 
separator. The punched cathode was sealed by an optical glass window. 
The UV–Vis spectra were recorded at diﬀerent voltages of the cell at 0.5 
C. 
3. Results and discussion 
The NiDME ( Fig. 1 a) directly dissolved as an additive in the elec- 
trolyte (1 M bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with 1 wt % 
LiNO 3 in DME and dioxolane (DOL) mixture (1:1 vol.)) to form a trans- 
parent and stable dispersion. According to DFT calculation in Fig. 1 b, 
NiCl 2 has a much stronger aﬃnity towards LiPS (-2.05 eV) than to- 
wards DME (-0.84 eV). Thus, as expected, when the NiDME disper- 
sion was added into the Li 2 S 6 solution, the NiDME preferred to interact 
with Li 2 S 6 and released DME to form a Li 2 S 6 -NiCl 2 complex ( Fig. 1 c), 
which showed potentials in suppressing the dissolution of LiPSs in the 
electrolyte. After settling, the upper solution was collected and charac- 
terized by UV–Vis spectroscopy (Figure S1). The intensity of S 6 
2 − was 
signiﬁcantly decreased, suggesting that LiPSs were mostly anchored on 
the additive and precipitated [27] . The strong interactions of NiCl 2 with 
Li 2 S and LiPSs are consistent with the higher binding energies which are 
reduced when the LiPSs are transformed into elemental sulfur ( Fig. 1 d). 
These results demonstrate the NiCl 2 has consistent strong interaction 
with LiPSs in the electrolyte and precipitate phases. Due to the much 
lower binding energy between NiCl 2 and S 8 (0.27 eV), the NiCl 2 was 
likely to have been released and then dissolved and rebinded with DME 
dissolved in the electrolyte after the redox reactions (after charging, 
forming S 8 ), realizing the overall electrochemical catalytic cycle illus- 
trated in the schematic ( Fig. 1 e). The deﬁnition of homogeneous catal- 
ysis refers to the reactions where the catalyst is in the same phase as 
the reactants, while the heterogeneous catalysis describes the processes 
where the catalysts and reactants are in distinct phases. As the redox 
reaction in Li-S battery undergoes a reversible phase change between 
liquid and solid, it is signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from the traditional homo- 
geneous or heterogeneous catalysis. As Fig. 1 e demonstrated, the NiCl 2 
is in the same phase as the reactants (the LiPSs in the electrolyte and the

The cells designed for the Li 2 S deposition measurements were assem- 
bled with carbon ﬁber papers as the cathode, lithium foil as the anode 
and Li 2 S 8 solution with/without the NiDME additive as the electrolyte. 
Figs. 1 f and 1 g show the measurements of the Li 2 S deposition rate and 
capacity based on Faraday’s law [28] . The cell with NiDME additive had 
a faster deposition of Li 2 S (average precipitation rate of 1.43 ×10 − 5 mA 
s − 1 ) and a higher deposition capacity (178 mAh g sulfur − 1 ) compared to 
the additive-free case (average precipitation rate of 7.38 ×10 − 6 mA s − 1 
and deposition capacity of 143 mAh g sulfur 
− 1 ), demonstrating the ac- 
celerated conversion from LiPSs to Li 2 S. The deposited Li 2 S uniformly 
covered the carbon ﬁber surface without aggregation in the presence of 
the NiDME additive (inset of Fig. 1 g), in contrast to the severe aggrega- 
tion of Li 2 S free of the additive (inset of Fig. 1 f). These results indicate 
the NiDME additive regulates Li 2 S nucleation and deposition due to its 
strong interaction with LiPS as shown earlier. The uniform deposition 
of Li 2 S lowers the overpotential of Li 2 S conversion and improves its uti- 
lization in the battery [29] . 
The electrochemical performance of the Li-S battery cells with 
porous carbon (CMK-3)/sulfur as the cathode and NiDME as the catalytic 
additive was measured and is shown in Fig. 2 . The amount of additive in 
the electrolyte was optimized according to the electrochemical perfor- 
mance as shown in Figures S2–3 and only 0.5 wt% NiDME is enough to 
obviously improve the performance. A higher concentration of NiDME 
may induce the inhomogeneous dispersion, decreasing the stability and 
utilization eﬃciency of the additives in the electrochemical process. The 
charge-discharge proﬁles with diﬀerent scan rates are shown in Figures 
S4–5. The plateau at 2.3 V and 2.0 V correspond to the transformation 
from S 8 to long-chain Li 2 S n (6 ≤ n ≤ 8) and from short-chain Li 2 S n (2 ≤ 
n ≤ 4) to Li 2 S, respectively. The second plateau of the cell with NiDME 
( Fig. 2 a) is longer and has lower polarization than that without NiDME, 
demonstrating the accelerated conversion from Li 2 S n to Li 2 S. The higher 
utilization and enhanced conversion of LiPSs can also be reﬂected by 
the capacity ratio between second discharge plateau and ﬁrst discharge 
plateau, where the cell with NiDME has a much higher ratio of 2.43 af- 
ter 100 cycles (Figure S6) than the additive-free cell (1.57). Capacities 
of 979 mAh g − 1 and 635 mAh g − 1 were obtained respectively at 0.3 C 
and 3 C for the cells with NiDME, much higher than the additive-free 
cases ( Fig. 2 b), showing that the additive enhances the utilization of ac- 
tive materials. The slightly lower capacity than that showing in Fig. 2 a 
ascribes to the unavoidable sulfur loss during the activation cycles with 
low current density before the rate test. The cycling stability of the elec- 
trolyte with the NiDME additive was also measured through a long cy- 
cling test at 1.0 C rate ( Fig. 2 c). The cell with the NiDME additive had a 
higher initial capacity of 1031 mAh g − 1 , and was maintained at 784 mAh 
g − 1 after 500 cycles with only 0.05% decay per cycle, as compared to the 
additive-free case (408 mAh g − 1 after 500 cycles). The improved per- 
formance could also be achieved in high sulfur loading electrodes (5 mg 
cm − 2 ) as shown in Figure S7. These results show that the cells with the

The catalytic activity of the NiDME additive was characterized by 
cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV proﬁles of the Li 2 S 6 symmetric cells 
containing carbon papers as electrodes and Li 2 S 6 solutions as elec- 
trolytes are shown in Fig. 3 a. The much higher redox current in the 
electrolyte with the NiDME additive proves the accelerated conver- 
sion of LiPSs [30,31] . The Nyquist plots obtained from electrochemi- 
cal impedance spectroscopy (EIS) show that although the cell with the 
NiDME additive has a higher internal resistance, the charge-transfer re- 
sistance is much smaller than the additive-free cell (inset of Fig. 3 a), 
suggesting a smoother LiPS conversion process in the NiDME case. The 
CV proﬁles of asymmetric cells with CMK-3/sulfur as the cathode and 
lithium foil as the anode are shown in Fig. 3 b, where the two cathodic 
peaks (C1 and C2) correspond to the transformation from S 8 to Li 2 S n (6 
≤ n ≤ 8) and from Li 2 S n (2 ≤ n ≤ 4) to Li 2 S, respectively. The C1 and C2 
peaks for the cell with the additive-based electrolyte shift to higher volt- 
ages with higher intensities and the two anodic peaks (corresponding to 
the transformation from Li 2 S to Li 2 S n and ﬁnal S 8 ) shift to lower poten- 
tials, compared with those free of the NiDME additive. The peak shifts 
indicate the lower polarization and enhanced redox reaction of sulfur- 
based species in the additive-based electrolyte and the higher intensity 
shows that the LiPS conversion eﬃciency is also improved. CV proﬁles 
at diﬀerent scan rates are shown in Figures S8–9, where the peaks with 
the additive-based electrolyte are well maintained with only a slight 
voltage shift at higher scan rates. However, the C2 peaks at 2.0 V with 
the additive-free electrolyte were broadened with the increased scan 
rates corresponding to the slower conversion from LiPS to Li 2 S, larger 
charge-transfer resistances and polarization.

shown in the experiment section and the results are shown in Fig. 3 d. 
The E a values of both steps are dramatically reduced when NiDME is 
added to the electrolyte, where the E a diﬀerences between the additive- 
based and additive-free cases are 12 kJ mol − 1 and 119 kJ mol − 1 respec- 
tively for the reactions from S 8 to Li 2 S n (E a1 ) and from Li 2 S n to Li 2 S

conversion rate by reducing the Ea. Note much larger Ea drop is found 
for the Li 2 S n -to-Li 2 S step, indicating greatly enhanced Li 2 S deposition 
due to the homogenous catalysis. 
In-situ EIS measurement was used to further elucidate the catalytic 
conversion process. EIS was recorded for diﬀerent depths of discharge 
(DOD) in the 1st discharge process ( Figs. 3 e-g and S11). The charge 
transfer resistance was obtained by ﬁtting with the equivalent circuit 
model shown in Figure S12, which is ascribed to the charge transfer of 
LiPS conversion [32,33] . The increased resistance from 16.7% to 33.3% 
DOD for the additive-free case indicates that the conversion reaction be- 
came slower due to the more dissolved LiPSs in electrolytes, where the 
long-chain Li 2 S n transformed into short-chain Li 2 S n . However, slight re- 
sistance changes for the cell with the NiDME additive indicate that the 
LiPSs in the electrolyte were trapped on the electrode without severe 
shuttling. Moreover, the overall charge transfer resistance of cells with 
the NiDME additive is much smaller than for those without NiDME ad- 
ditive, showing the faster conversion rate due to the lower activation 
energy. These in-situ EIS results strongly prove that the NiDME additive 
suppresses the shuttling of LiPSs and accelerates the redox reaction. 
To investigate the catalytic mechanism, further characterization was 
conducted and the results are shown in Fig. 4 . The NiDME is chemical 
stable against lithium metal, which can be proved by the stable lithium 
stripping/plating cycles in Figure S13 and also demonstrate the addi- 
tive is not ionized in the electrolyte. The deposited complex in Fig. 1 c 
was collected and characterized X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
to analyze the interaction between NiDME and LiPSs. The Ni 2p XPS 
proﬁles show that additional peaks appear after adding Li 2 S 6 ( Fig. 4 a), 
ascribed to the strong interaction of NiDME with LiPS, and the Ni 2 + par- 
tially transforms into Ni [34,35] . As a result, some S n 2 − was oxidized 
and turned into sulfate or sulﬁte as shown in the S 2p proﬁle ( Fig. 4 b). 
Besides, the appearance of a Ni-S peak with high intensity conﬁrms the 
strong interaction between LiPSs and NiDME [36,37] . The in-situ UV–
Vis spectra were also collected from the battery cell with carbon/sulfur 
cathodes discussed earlier to investigate the concentration changes of 
LiPSs during the 1st cycle of discharge and the speciﬁc concentration 
of diﬀerent LiPS was obtained [38,39] . As shown in Fig. 4 c the reﬂec- 
tion gradually shift to higher wavelengths during the conversion from 
Li 2 S 8 to Li 2 S 4 in the additive-free electrolyte. In sharp contrast, the cells 
with the NiDME additive show no obvious shifts, indicating a smaller 
amount of dissolved LiPSs and a fast conversion rate. The concentra- 
tions of diﬀerent LiPSs (e.g. Li 2 S 8 ) were determined by the diﬀerence

4. Conclusions 
In summary, homogeneous catalysis in Li-S batteries is achieved 
by directly adding a small amount (0.5 wt%) of organometallic salt, 
NiDME, as an electrolyte additive, which is characterized by largely re- 
duced activation energy (E a ) of the battery redox reaction, especially 
for the Li 2 S deposition, and proposed as a fundamental solution to the 
shuttle eﬀect of LiPS intermediates. The NiDME additive captures LiPSs 
in the electrolyte, accelerates the conversion of these intermediates at 
homogeneous interfaces and regulates the uniform deposition of Li 2 S, 
all these contributing to a catalytic cycle with less LiPSs shuttling, as 
revealed by DFT calculations and experimental studies including in-situ 
UV–Vis and EIS measurements. With the accelerated redox conversion 
and reduced accumulation of LiPSs, the battery with NiDME additive 
shows stable cycling with 784 mAh g − 1 after 500 cycles at 1.0 C, and 
decreased needs of the amount of electrolyte, working well with a lean 
electrolyte of 5 𝜇L (electrolyte) /mg (sulfur) , as compared with the additive- 
free battery. This work deepens our understanding on catalysis in Li-S 
batteries and indicates a proactive and practical strategy by using a small 
amount of electrolyte additive, not making any changes to the battery 
conﬁguration, to solve the bottleneck of Li-S batteries approaching lean 
electrolyte and long working period for practical use.
