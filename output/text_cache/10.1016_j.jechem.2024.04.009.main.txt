a b s t r a c t
Li-S batteries are regarded as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation battery systems
with high energy density and low cost. However, the dissolution-precipitation reaction mechanism of the
sulfur (S) cathode enhances the kinetics of the redox processes of the insulating sulfur, which also arouses
the notorious shuttle effect, leading to serious loss of S species and corrosion of Li anode. To get a balance
between the shuttle restraining and the kinetic property, a combined strategy of electrolyte regulation
and cathode modiﬁcation is proposed via introducing 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoroprpyl
ether (HFE) instead of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and SeS7 instead of S8. The introduction of HFE tunes
the solvation structure of the LiTFSI and the dissolution of intermediate polysulﬁdes with Se doping
(LiPSSes), and optimize the interface stability of the Li anode simultaneously. The minor Se substitution
compensates the decrease in kinetic due to the decreased solubility of LiPSs. In this way, the Li-SeS7 bat-
teries deliver a reversible capacity of 1062 and 1037 mAh g1 with 2.0 and 5.5 mg SeS7 cm2 loading con-
dition, respectively. Besides, an electrolyte–electrode loading model is established to explain the
relationship between the optimal electrolyte and cathode loading. It makes more sense to guide the elec-
trolyte design for practical Li-S batteries.

1. Introduction
Li-ion batteries as the portable and mobile energy storage sys-
tem have made great achievements in the energy density [1–3].
However, restricted by the intercalation chemistry of the electrode
materials, Li-ion batteries have almost approached the upper limit
of their energy density (350 Wh kg1). Besides, lithium-sulfur (Li-
S) batteries, with S8 as cathode and Li metal as anode, offer high the-
oretical energy densities up to 2600 Wh kg1 or 2800 Wh L1 [4–6],
have been regarded as one of the most promising candidates for
next-generation battery systems with high energy density [7–9].
However, the rapid capacity fading and low coulombic efﬁ-
ciency (CE) of Li-S batteries hamper their commercialization. It is
extensively believed that the root cause is the poor conductivity
of sulfur and the parasitic reactions between the soluble cathodic
intermediates and Li anode during the electrochemical reactions
[10].
The
ether-based
electrolytes
are
widely
used
as
the
electrolyte for Li-S system in which various forms of intermediate
polysulﬁdes (LiPSs, Li2Sn, 8  n  3) are generated during the lithi-
ation/delithiation process. The LiPSs would dissolve in the elec-
trolyte. And the solubility decreases as the chain of LiPSs
shortened. The insoluble Li2S/Li2S2 is formed at the ﬁnal stage of
discharge. In the charging process, the highly soluble long-chain
LiPSs diffuse to anode and react with Li metal without contributing
capacity. The phenomenon is the notorious shuttle effect [11].
What is worse, the solid products of the diffused long-chain LiPSs
deposit on the surface of Li metal, resulting in sluggish kinetic of
the further electrochemical reactions and the loss of active species.
Researchers tackle the issue by conﬁning the LiPSs within a con-
ductive cathode matrix. Various carbon materials [12–14], metal-
organics [15], covalent-organics [16] and conductive polymers
[17,18] are used as the frameworks. Some of the matrixes can also
act as catalysts to accelerate the conversion process of LiPSs to
Li2S2/Li2S [19–21]. However, additional weight has been unavoid-
ably introduced to the cathode via these strategies, which would
weak the advantage of the high energy density of the Li-S battery
devices. Besides, when the reactive sites of the catalysts are
occupied by the solid products completely, the catalytic effect will
lose.

Electrolyte designs can be another effective solution to improve
the rechargeability of the Li-S batteries [22–24]. The recently
developed high concentration electrolytes or localized high con-
centration electrolytes, have shown the effectiveness in prolonging
the cycle life of the Li-S batteries. However, these electrolytes
weaken the good mobility and strong electrochemical activity of
the LiPSs which enhance the reaction kinetic in traditional ether
electrolytes [7,13]. Besides, the largely enhanced concentration of
the expensive Li salts implies elevated costs, posing a challenge
to the commercialization of Li-S batteries. To deal with the
dilemma, a shuttle-weaken and no-high concentration electrolyte
system is urgently needed.
In this manuscript, 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuor
oprpyl ether (HFE), which possesses weak solvating power, is
introduced into the electrolytes to partially replace DME. HFE can
play the role in encapsulating LiPSs to hinder the parasitic reac-
tions between them and Li metal [23,25,26]. What is more, to com-
pensate the potential decrease in kinetic properties due to the
decreased solubility of LiPSs, SeS7 is used as the cathode material
instead of S8. As congeners of S, Se undergoes the dissolution-
precipitation electrode similar with S but it has a much better
kinetics because the conductivity of Se (1  103 S m1) is much
higher than that of S (5  1028 S m1) [27]. Combined the modi-
ﬁcation of the electrolyte and the cathode, a kinetic balance can be
obtained between the dissolution and precipitation of intermedi-
ate polysulﬁdes with Se doping (LiPSSes). In addition to the
decreased dissolution of intermediate LiPSSes, more TFSI emerge
in the solvate shell of Li+ as DME is replaced, resulting in increased
participation of TFSI in the formation of the solid electrolyte inter-
phase (SEI) on the surface of Li anode. As a result, the Li-SeS7 cell
delivers a reversible capacity of 1062 and 1037 mAh g1 with 2.0
and 5.5 mg cm2 loading condition, respectively.

Here, three kinds of electrolytes are designed to explore the Li+
solvation and the corresponding battery performances of the
LiTFSI-DME/DOL/HFE system. They are 1 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL
(1:1,v/v) (named as DME/DOL hereafter), 1 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL:
HFE (1:2:1,v/v/v) (DME/DOL/HFE) and 1 M LiTFSI in the DOL/HFE
(1:1,v/v) (DOL/HFE). It is worth noting that all tests are done with-
out adding LiNO3 which can effectively suppress the shuttle effect,
to objectively evaluate the advantage of the electrochemical per-
formances of the solvent system. As shown in Fig. 1, the LiPSSes-
solvent binding energy of the three electrolytes are 2.57, 2.15,
1.79 eV in turn. Obviously, the solvating capability of HFE is
much weaker than that of DME. It can be expected that the solubil-
ity of LiPSSes will decrease as the ratio of HFE to DME increases,
and the solvation structure of Li+ would change accordingly.
Besides, the parasitic reactions between LiPSSes and Li metal will
be reduced, too (Fig. 1).
Fig. 2(a–c) show the theoretical calculation results of the molec-
ular dynamics (MD) simulation for the LiTFSI-DME/DOL/HFE sys-
tem. The sharp peak at 2.06 Å corresponds to the Li–O (DME)
pairs which indicates the strong coordination between Li+ and
DME molecules in DME/DOL electrolyte. The 2.10 Å peak relates
to the Li–O (TFSI) pair. Obviously, in DME/DOL electrolyte, the
participation of TFSI in the solvation layer of Li+ is very low.
Besides, the peak corresponds to Li–O (DOL) is not obvious which
indicates a weaker solvation ability of DOL compared to DME. In
the DME/DOL/HFE electrolyte, the sharp peaks at 2.08 and 2.12 Å
align to Li–O (DME) and Li–O (TFSI) pairs. The peaks are further
away from Li+ compared to the DME/DOL electrolyte. And the coor-
dination number of Li–O (TFSI) is higher than that in DME/DOL. In
DOL/HFE electrolyte, the Li–O (TFSI) pair shifts to 2.14 Å, indicat-
ing the TFSI anions locate the farthest from Li+ in the three elec-
trolytes. But the coordination number of Li–O (TFSI) in the DOL/
HFE system is the highest. The change trend of the coordination
number of Li–O (TFSI) and Li–O (DOL) indicates more DOL mole-
cules and TFSI anions enter into the Li+ solvation structure and the
DME molecules in the inner layer of the Li+ solvation structure
gradually decreases accordingly.

A smooth curve was used to connect these scattered points to
get the insights into the continuous variation of the electrolyte
composition. It can be seen the LiPSSes relative dissolution degree
and DV both show downward convex functions rather than a
straight line. It indicates that the LiPSSes solubility and the kinetic
properties decrease signiﬁcantly in the early stage and level off in
the later stage, featured as convex curves. The reasons can be as
follows: due to the weak solvation capability, HFE tends to be dis-
tributed in the outlayer of LiPSSes rather than in the bulk phase,
which causes the migration of LiPSSes in the electrolyte to be hin-
dered and the electrochemical accessibility of LiPSSes is weakened
accordingly. Fig. 4(d) shows the cycle performance of the batteries
in different electrolytes. It is observed that the initial discharge
capacities of the cathodes in the three electrolytes are relatively
close. The discharge capacity of the DME/DOL, DME/DOL/HFE and
DOL/HFE batteries are 1050, 1218 and 1062 mAh g1 with the
retention ratios 53.9%, 59.7% and 72% after 50 cycles, and 49.3%,
52% and 64% after 100 cycles at 0.2 C, respectively. The DOL/HFE
battery demonstrates the most stable cycle, corresponding to the
lowest concentration of LiPSSes and the side reaction with Li metal.
The interface of the Li anode also relates closely with the disso-
lution of LiPSSes. The assembly process of Li||cycled-Li batteries
involves the disassembly of cycled batteries that are assembled
with different electrolytes. Li metal with Li2S/Li2S2 deposited in
cycled batteries are used together with fresh Li metal to assemble
Li||cyeled-Li batteries. The cycling performances are shown in
Fig. 4(e). It can be seen the battery cycled with DOL/HFE electrolyte
shows the lowest polarization and the highest stability during long
cycles. The polarization reﬂects the deposition of the solid Li2S2
and Li2S on the surface of Li metal. Obviously, the DOL/HFE elec-
trolyte has the lowest LiPSSes shuttle, corresponding to the lowest
passivation and the lowest polarization during the cycles.

Fig. S13 shows the result of the battery resting test to further

Taking the active species loading and the electrolyte kinetic
properties into account, the scheme of the cathode loading-
electrolyte model is presented in Fig. 5(d). The plane represents
the changing trend of the kinetic properties of the electrolyte
required as the cathode loading varies. More reactants require bet-
ter kinetic properties for the battery to release the same speciﬁc
capacity. The curved surface represents the kinetic properties of
electrolyte which is inﬂuenced by the composition, and the color
of the curved surface indicates the dissolution of LiPSSes. The
model is divided into different regions. The portion of the plane
that is higher than the curved surface represents the performance
deterioration due to the inability of the electrolyte to meet the
kinetic requirement of the electrode. And the part of the curved
surface that is higher than the plane means that the electrolyte
dominates the battery performance. The intersecting line repre-
sents the optimized electrolyte composition in different loading
situation. The model can well explain the performance differences
of the electrolytes in Fig. 4(d) and Fig. 5(a): in low loading situa-
tion, DOL/HFE is closer to the optimal ratio electrolyte composi-
tion; in high loading situation, DME/DOL/HFE is closer to optimal
ratio electrolyte composition while the DOL/HFE system leads to
the sluggish kinetics and the DME/DOL system deserves severe
shuttle effect.
3. Conclusions
In summary, HFE has been used as a co-solvent to regulate the
solvation of the LiTFSI salts and the dissolution of LiPSSes simulta-
neously. Via tuning the composition and ratio of the electrolyte,
the shuttle effect of the Li-SeS7 battery is alleviated, and the capac-
ity retention of the battery is signiﬁcantly improved. Furthermore,
the relation of the electrolyte and the cathode loading is modeled.
This discovery would guide the rational design of electrolytes for
practical Li-S batteries.
