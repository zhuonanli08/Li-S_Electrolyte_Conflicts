ABSTRACT
The electronic market starves for efficient batteries and high energy density at low temperatures. The electrolyte serves as the sole
medium for ion transport within a battery and directly determines its fundamental functionality. Its role is irreplaceable, as it
governs critical performance parameters such as ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability, and compatibility with electrodes.
In this study, we designed a lithiated carboxylated fullerene that was incorporated as an electrolyte additive for low-temperature
lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, which not only facilitates a high loading of active materials but also accelerates the sluggish
electrochemical kinetics observed at reduced temperatures. Additionally, the lithiated carboxylated fullerene additive helps miti-
gate dendrite formation and reduces the clustering of lithium polysulfides. Consequently, the capacity of Li2S6@CNT cathode
with a loading mass of 10 mg cm−2 could reach the capacity of 546.8 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C under −40°C. This work provides some
initial insight that could be useful for the eventual development of high-sulfur-loading Li–S batteries at low temperatures, with
possible relevance to extreme-environment energy storage in the future.
1 | Introduction
The development of low-temperature batteries has garnered sig-
nificant attention due to their increasing demand and promising
applications. However, their practical implementation is hin-
dered by critical challenges, particularly the diminished energy
output and reduced efficiency caused by sluggish ion transport
kinetics under subambient conditions. Among various energy
storage systems, lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are poised to
revolutionize energy storage due to their exceptional theoretical
specific energy of 2600 Wh kg−1 [1, 2], which is highly appeal-
ing for applications requiring minimization of mass, such as
unmanned aerial vehicles, electric vehicles, and space missions
[3, 4]. However, these applications often face exposure to low-
temperature or even extremely low-temperature environments.
Notably, Li–S batteries use solvents like 1,3-dioxolane and
1,2-dimethoxyethane with very low freezing points (−95°C
and −58°C) [5], which theoretically enables superior perfor-
mance in cold conditions. Yet, practical challenges remain as
Li–S batteries also exhibit curtailed performance at low temper-
atures. One major bottleneck is the clustering and aggregation
behavior of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in solution, particularly
pronounced at low temperatures. This phenomenon constrains
the kinetics of electrochemical conversion, impacting battery per-
formance negatively [6–9]. The energetics and coordination
implications of this behavior necessitate a deeper understanding
of dissolved lithium species dynamics.

teries with high performance. Advanced material design and

advanced energy storage technology.
In this work, lithiated carboxylated fullerene derivatives (1,4,
11,15,30-penta(lithium
γ-aminobutyrate-N-yl)-2-Cl-1,2,4,11,15,
30-hexahydro[60]fullerene), denoted as PCH-C60-Li, were syn-
thesized and introduced as an electrolyte additive, which can
alleviate LiPS clustering, facilitate charge transfer, and contribute
to the suppression of lithium dendrites, a key factor mitigating
safety risks [17–20]. Li–S battery was assembled using Li2S6@
CNT cathode and incorporated with electrolyte containing PCH-
C60-Li. The performance was systematically evaluated at −40°C.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations proved the accelerated
kinetics and the suppression of LiPS clustering. This study dem-
onstrates the viability of Li–S batteries featuring high sulfur load-
ing and stable operation at low temperatures. The design and
promotion strategy developed herein holds broad applicability
and can be readily adapted to diverse energy storage systems tar-
geting cryogenic operational environments. This work reveals
the great potential of high-loading and low-temperature Li–S bat-
teries and inspires the development of advanced materials effec-
tive in battery systems under extreme conditions.
2 | Results and Discussion
PCH-C60-Li was designed as an electrolyte additive to enhance low-
temperature performance. It was prepared by first reacting C60Cl6
with tert-butyl 4-aminobutyrate to form PCH-C60-But, after which
the tert-butyl protecting groups were removed using TFA to yield
PCH-C60. Finally, PCH-C60 was neutralized with LiOH to form the
lithium salt (PCH-C60-Li), and excess LiOH was removed by dialy-
sis (Figure 1a). Figure 1b shows a photograph of the electrolyte con-
taining the PCH-C60-Li additive, demonstrating excellent stability
and dispersibility in the electrolyte. Figure 1c presents the model
diagram of the PCH-C60-Li modified electrolyte system, including
PCH-C60-Li, LiTFSI (lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide),
DOL (1,3-dioxolane), and DME (1,2-dimethoxyethane).
The structure design of PCH-C60-Li is the key to realizing its
function. The molecule carries multiple lithiated carboxylate side
chains, which can act as competitive coordination sites to disrupt
the aggregation of LiPSs by weakening the Li+–Sx
2−interaction
between LiPSs species. This molecular-level intervention can
promote faster ion transport and reduce mass transfer resistance;
the direct evidence is that the diffusion coefficient measured in a
wide temperature range is significantly improved. In order to
reveal the change of the solvation structure of the electrolyte after
the introduction of PCH-C60-Li additive, we further carried out
MD simulation. The analysis of the simulated data (Figure 2a)

3 | Conclusion
In summary, we have designed and demonstrated a lithiated car-
boxylated fullerene PCH-C60-Li as a bifunctional kinetic electrolyte
additive for Li–S batteries, leveraging the synergistic effect of the
fullerene skeleton and lithium-rich functional groups to achieve
dual suppression of lithium dendrite growth and low-temperature
lithium polysulfide aggregation. Upon the addition of PCH-C60-Li,
the dendrite growth can be suppressed effectively, and the LiPS
clustering can be alleviated at low temperatures, showing improved
cycling stability and enhanced rate performance. This work high-
lights the significant promise of high-loading, low-temperature Li–
S batteries and provides valuable insights for designing advanced
materials capable of enabling robust battery performance under
extreme low temperature conditions.
4 | Experimental Section
4.1 | Synthesis and Purification of PCH-C60-Li
1,4,11,15,30-Penta(tert-butylγ-aminobutyrate-N-yl)-2-chloro-
1,2,4,11,15,30-hexahydro[60]fullerene (PCH-C60-But) was prepa-
red following a reported method [23]. Briefly, PCH-C60-But was
synthesized by reacting chlorofullerene (C60Cl6) with tert-butyl 4-
aminobutyrate, and the tert-butyl (But) protecting groups were
subsequently removed with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). PCH-
C60-Li was obtained via neutralization of PCH-C60 with lithium
hydroxide. C60Cl6 was synthesized with a purity exceeding 95%
according to a previous report [24]. The compound was then
reacted with 7.5 equivalents of tert-butyl 4-aminobutyrate hydro-
chloride in a mixture of toluene and aqueous NaOH solution. The
crude product was purified by silica gel column chromatography
using a toluene–ethyl acetate mixture to afford red powders of
PCH-C60-But. The purity of PCH-C60-But was above 95%, and
high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS, ESI) showed a peak
at m/z 1548 for [C100H80N5O10Cl +H]+. Removal of the tert-butyl
groups was achieved using 50% TFA in CH2Cl2 to yield PCH-C60,
which was obtained with a purity greater than 95%. HRMS (ESI)
revealed a peak at m/z 1265 for [C80H40N5O10Cl -H]−. The solid
PCH-C60 was dissolved in an aqueous LiOH solution to form
PCH-C60-Li, which was further purified by dialysis against ultra-
pure water and subsequently freeze-dried.
4.2 | Preparation of Li2S6 Solution and Adsorption
Test
Crystalline sulfur and Li2S (molar ratio = 5:1) were dissolved in a
mixed solution containing dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME, volume ratio = 1:1), and the mixture was stirred at
60°C in an Ar-filled environment for 48 h. In the adsorption experi-
ments, 2 mL of 5 mM Li2S6 was used under −40°C, and 10 mg of
electrode materials were used.

4.4 | Electrochemical Measurements
CR2032 coin cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box with
CNT as a cathode, Li2S6 as the sulfur source, lithium metal as
an anode, glass fiber as the separator, and 50 μL of electrolyte was
injected into the cell. The electrolyte was composed of 1.0 M lith-
ium bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) in a mixture of
DOL and DME (volume ratio = 1:1) containing 2 wt% LiNO3 and
PCH-C60-Li as the additive. In this work, sulfur loadings of 1, 2, 5,
10 mg cm−2 were employed, corresponding to 26 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6,
26 μL of 0.4 M Li2S6, 26 μL of 1 M Li2S6, and 52 μL of 1 M Li2S6
per cm2, respectively. Rate and cycling performance were tested
on Neware (Neware Electronic Co., Ltd., China) and LAND
(Wuhan Land Electronic Co., Ltd., China) battery test systems with
a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V (vs. Li+/Li). All cells were placed in
the thermostatic cabinet for 10 h and then activated at 0.05 C for
the first cycle. CV and EIS were tested on a Bio-Logic VMP3
electrochemistry workstation.
