a b s t r a c t
Sulfur utilization improvement and control of dissolved lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS; Li2Sx, 2 < x  8) are cru-
cial aspects of the development of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, especially in high-loading sulfur elec-
trodes and low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratios. The sluggish reaction in the low E/S ratio induces poor
LiPS solubility and unstable Li2S electrodeposition, resulting in limited sulfur utilization, especially under
high-loading sulfur electrode. In this study, we report on salt concentration effects that improve sulfur
utilization with a high-loading cathode (6 mgsulfur cm2), a high sulfur content (80 wt%) and a low E/S
ratio (5 mL gsulfur
1
). On the basis of the rapid LiPS dissolving in a low concentration electrolyte, we estab-
lished that the quantity of Li2S electrodeposition from a high Li+ diffusion coefﬁcient, referring to the
reduction of LiPS precipitation, was signiﬁcantly enhanced by a faster kinetic. These results demonstrate
the importance of kinetic factors for the rate capability and cycle life stability of Li-S battery electrolytes
through high Li2S deposition under high-loading sulfur electrode.

1. Introduction
It has been a continuing struggle since Sony’s commercializa-
tion of rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) in the early
1990s to develop high-energy rechargeable batteries [1–3]. LIBs,
as a representative of rechargeable batteries, have continuously
evolved to enhance the lifespan, energy density, and safety of the
applications for energy storage devices [4,5]. Nonetheless, the
demand for post-LIBs is growing since current LIBs have unstable
supply chains of raw materials associated with expanded market
volume, and their energy density has been saturated due to a lack
of revolutionary capacity enhancement of active materials [2,6]. As
an alternative to these issues, sulfur is an ideal choice for supply
chain security and has a remarkable reversible capacity as a cath-
ode active material owing to its high theoretical capacity and
energy density of 1675 mA h g1 and 2600 W h kg1 in Li-S batter-
ies [7–9]. Sulfur is a naturally abundant element and a byproduct
of the petroleum industry, making it a cost-effective option and a
sustainable supply chain [10]. Although sulfur has a theoretical
capacity that is more than 7 times that of cathode materials in LIBs,
Li-S batteries have obvious drawbacks that preclude them from
being employed in practical applications: (i) both S8 and Li2S2/
Li2S have very low electrical conductivity, preventing electrons
from passing through [11]; (ii) the reaction kinetics of solid–
liquid-solid phase transition of sulfur are sluggish [12,13]; (iii)
lithium undergoes dendritic growth and the deposition of unex-
pected compounds on its surface [14]; and (iv) the dissolution
and migration of intermediate LiPS species into the ether-based
solvent, known as the ‘shuttle effect’ [15].
The Li-S battery system, which differs from the redox mecha-
nism of the LIBs, is subject to the multi-phase conversion reaction,
in which a solid S8 is dissolved in an electrolyte, reduced to LiPS on
the carbon surface, and then undergoes the Li2S nucleation process
in the surface-pathway [16,17]. The growth of Li2S occurs as the
surface-pathway forms an insulator passivation layer in parallel
with a solution-mediated pathway until the cluster reaches satura-
tion [18]. Notably, the kinetics and morphology of Li2S electrode-
position, which accounts for three-quarters of sulfur utilization
(1256 mA h g1), is signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by solvent properties
such as a donicity, polarity, and viscosity [19]. In Li2S electrodepo-
sition, the shortcomings of Li-S cause the electrode to exhibit a rel-
atively high resistance and sluggish kinetics, resulting in limited
sulfur utilization, an unstable lifespan, and low Coulombic efﬁ-
ciency (CE), especially for high-loading sulfur cathodes and low
E/S ratios [20]. Therefore, optimizing the composition and amount
of the electrolyte is critical to achieving the desired high energy
density of the Li-S battery, which increases sulfur utilization and
decreases the LiPS shuttle effect.

The composition of a frequently used electrolyte in Li-S batteries
is a 1 M lithium bis-(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) salt in
a 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1, v/v) sol-
vent with a 2 wt% LiNO3 additive [19,21,22]. This electrolyte, how-
ever,
has substantial disadvantages,
including limited sulfur
utilization due to the LiPS solubility limit at low E/S ratios and the
shuttle effect generated by dissolved LiPS migration to the anode
surface [16]. Previous studies proposed the common ion effect to
suppress LiPS dissolution by sparingly solvated LiPS with a high con-
centration electrolyte [23,24]. But, the sparingly solvated method of
LiPS was evaluated under very low sulfur loadings (1–2 mgsulfur
cm2) and sulfur contents (<60 wt%) and it effectively inhibited
the shuttle mechanism and improved lithium metal stability [25].
With very low sulfur loadings, it is difﬁcult to understand the effects
of sluggish kinetics caused by high viscosity and low solubility of
LiPS because excess electrolytes can digest LiPS from sulfur elec-
trode [26]. To avoid the limitation of solubility of LiPS in DOL/DME
solvent, highly solvating electrolytes were proposed to improve sul-
fur utilization. A previous study introduced the dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO) solvent as a superior high donor number solvent with a
Li2S8 solubility exceeding 14 M [27], compared to the ether-based
solvent with a Li2S8 solubility of 6.6 M (E/S = 4.74 mL gsulfur
1
) [28].
Similarly, 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI) was investigated
as a high sulfur utilization solvent, yielding results that were close
to the theoretical capacity of sulfur at E/S = 5 mL gsulfur
1
[29]. The high
donor solvent can dissolve a large amount of LiPS and facilitate 3-
dimensional deposition of Li2S through the S3 radical pathway,
resulting in reduced passivation layers and formation of conductive
areas. However, the high donor electrolytes are incompatible with
lithium metal and have slow kinetics of Li2S electrodeposition
[18,20]. These phenomena drastically decrease the activation of sul-
fur when evaluating a high-loading cathode with a high donor elec-
trolyte. Therefore, the high-loading cathode activation at a low E/S
ratio, which is a practical condition for Li-S batteries, should develop
fast kinetic reactions by promoting LiPS dissolution and Li2S
deposition.
The low concentration electrolyte delayed the formation of LiPS
passivation layers because the electrolyte contained a small amount
of lithium ions, resulting in better rate performance of Li-S batteries
[26]. In addition, the low concentration of LiTFSI facilitated the
aggregation of short-chain LiPS better than 1 M LiTFSI in DME/
DOL, thus revealing a reduced shuttle effect and stable cycle life
[30]. Herein, we investigated the utilization of a low concentration
electrolyte (0.25 M LiTFSI in DME/DOL) to improve rate perfor-
mance and cycling stability with increased initial sulfur dissolution
and Li2S deposition using a high-loading cathode and low E/S ratio.
In this study, an alternate perspective on low concentration elec-
trolytes is proposed by presenting the correlation between sulfur
utilization and electrodeposition kinetics. We used 0.25 M LiTFSI
instead of 1 M LiTFSI to induce more LiPS dissolution and faster
Li2S electrodeposition because Li+ mobility increased with decreas-
ing salt concentration. The enhancement of electrodeposition kinet-
ics
by
lowering
the
salt
concentration
could
improve
LiPS
dissolution and Li2S deposition. The 0.25 M LiTFSI exhibited high
sulfur utilization and stable lifespan with a high-loading cathode
(6 mgsulfur cm2), high sulfur content (80 wt%), and low E/S ratio
(5 mL gsulfur
1
) comparison with 1 M LiTFSI.
2. Experimental
2.1. Electrolyte preparation and LiPS solubility test
The tested electrolytes were prepared by adding LiTFSI salt into
DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) solvent (Inchems Co., Ltd.). And 0.6 M LiNO3
additive was added to sustain a long cycle life for the high-
loading sulfur electrode [31,32]. All electrolytes were prepared in
an argon-ﬁlled glove box (O2, H2O < 0.1 ppm). The LiPS solubility
was evaluated by adding stoichiometric quantities of elemental
sulfur and Li2S into each concentration of electrolytes to target
the 0.2 M Li2S8 solution. After stirring for 180 min, the solution
was heated to 60 C and continuously stirred until the sulfur pow-
der dissolved.
2.2. S/MWCNT composite preparation and cell assembly
The cathode material was prepared by compounding sulfur and
multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT). Elemental sulfur pow-
der (Miwon Co., Ltd.) 16 g and MWCNT (Hanwha Chemical Co.,
Ltd.) 1 g were ground in an agate mortar for 30 min. Thereafter,
the mixture was heated in a box furnace at 155 C for 12 h under
an argon atmosphere to form S/MWCNT composites. The compos-
ites were pulverized in a zirconia jar with 50 g of zirconia balls and
20 g of distilled water for 36 h using a planetary ball-mill machine
(XQM-2A, Changsha Tianchuang Powder Technology) at 500 r
min1. After the wet-milling, the composite was vacuum-ﬁltered
with a polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) ﬁlter and dried in a vacuum
oven at 60 C for 12 h. The sulfur content in the S/MWCNT compos-
ite was measured using thermogravimetric analysis (TG/DTA7300,
Hitachi Co., Ltd.) at a heating rate of 5 C min1 from 30 to 600 C
under atmospheric conditions. The electrode slurry was prepared
with 85 wt% S/MWCNT composite, 10 wt% Super-P, and 5 wt%
polyacrylamide binder in de-ionized water, where the sulfur con-
tent of solid materials was 80 wt%. The as-prepared slurry was cast
on 12-lm-thick aluminum foil using a doctor blade and dried in a
vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h. The electrodes were punched into
discs that were 12 mm in diameter. The areal loading mass of sul-
fur was set to 6 mgsulfur cm2. The lithium metal foil (200 lm thick,
Honjo Metal) was used as an anode. The separator was prepared by
carbon coating on a polyethylene (PE) separator (W-scope Korea
Co., Ltd.). The CR2032 coin-type cells were assembled for electro-
chemical testing in an Ar-ﬁlled glove box.
2.3. Electrochemical evaluation
The
electrochemical
properties
were
measured
using
a
WBCS3000L battery cycler (WonATech Co., Ltd) at 25 C. The for-
mation process was carried out at a current rate of 0.1 C during
the ﬁrst 2cycles. The rate capability upon discharging was mea-
sured at current rates of 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.3 C, 0.5 C, and 1 C (1 C =
1000 mA h gsulfur
1
) after 0.1 C charging. The cycle life was measured
at a constant current charge/discharge under 0.3 C. The cyclic
voltammetry (CV) curves were obtained using an electrochemical
test unit (VMP3, Biologic) at a scan rate of 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3,
and 0.4 mV s1 for each cycle. The voltage range of all electrochem-
ical tests was 1.7–2.7 V vs. Li/Li+. The electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out using an electrochemical test
unit in the frequency range from 1 to 10 MHz, with a voltage per-
turbation of 10 mV. The galvanostatic intermittent titration tech-
nique (GITT) was conducted from OCV to 1.7 V at 0.03, 0.05, and
0.1 C with a 20 min discharge interval and 1 h rest protocol.
2.4. Li2S deposition
For Li2S deposition experiments, 0.2 M of Li2S8 was dissolved in
each electrolyte having different salt concentrations between 0.25
and 2 M. Carbon paper, which served as the working electrode in a
coin-type cell with a PE separator and Li metal anode, was injected
with Li2S8 dissolved electrolytes. The open-circuit voltage of
assembled cells was 2.27 V and cells were galvanostatically
discharged from OCV to 2.15 V with the current of 0.112 mA to
consume the high-ordered LiPS in electrolyte and then potentio-
statically discharged at 2.09 V constant voltage to induce Li2S

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Effect of low concentration electrolytes on a high-loading sulfur
cathode
The following method was implemented to determine the inﬂu-
ence of salt concentration effects on the practical application of a
Li-S battery. Concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 M LiTFSI in
DME/DOL (1:1, v/v) with 0.6 M LiNO3 (expressed as ‘‘0.25, 0.5, 1,
and 2 M LiTFSI”) were evaluated with a high-loading cathode, the
sulfur content of 80 wt% ﬁxed at 6 mgsulfur cm2, and E/S ratios
of 5, 8, and 11 mL gsulfur
1
. When clamping the coin cell, the elec-
trolyte overﬂowed if the E/S ratio exceeded 11; hence, coin cells
with E/S ratios of 18 and 35 were assembled with low sulfur load-
ing electrodes containing 2 and 4 mgsulfur cm2.
As shown in Fig. 1(a), the speciﬁc discharge capacity with E/S
ratio 35 exceeded 1100 mA h gsulfur
1
regardless of LiTFSI concentra-
tion, and the speciﬁc discharge capacity with 0.25 M and 0.5 M of
LiTFSI delivered more than 1000 mA h gsulfur
1
regardless of E/S
ratios. Particularly, if the ES ratio, i.e., the amount of electrolyte,
declines, the speciﬁc capacity tends to decrease, with the effect
becoming more prominent as the salt concentration increases.
Even at a salt concentration of 1 M LiTFSI, which is widely used,
the capacity decreases dramatically to 590 mA h gsulfur
1
at an E/S
ratio of 8. With regard to Li-S batteries, which have a beneﬁt in
terms of gravimetric energy density, the higher the energy density
can be guaranteed, the lower the E/S ratio. The effect of the E/S
ratio on reversible capacity will vary depending on the battery’s
size or structure, but considering the reaction characteristics of
the sulfur electrode, the fact that a low salt concentration is advan-
tageous for achieving a high reversible capacity is highly indicative.
Even in the lean electrolyte environment of this study, with an E/S
ratio of 5, 0.5 M LiTFSI shows an unstable voltage proﬁle at the end
of the discharge, indicating that the deposition reaction of Li2S is
being hampered (Fig. 1c). Either at 0.25 M of LiTFSI, the delivered
capacity of the ﬁrst voltage plateau during the discharge reaction
between 2.4 and 2.1 V is less than that of E/S 35. This stage con-
verts S8 to Li2S8, which is soluble in electrolytes. The reduced
capacity implies that the amount of pre-dissolved salt is interfering
with the conversion reaction.
Interestingly, the lower concentration electrolytes of 0.25 and
0.5 M LiTFSI revealed moderate sulfur utilization, even at E/S 5.
Fig. 1(b and c) illustrates the voltage proﬁles of each electrolyte
concentration at E/S 35 and 5. At 2.2–2.4 V, the upper plateau
showed reduction reactions from the solid S8 to the liquid Li2S8,
whereas the lower plateau at 2.0–2.1 V showed Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S
reduction [33]. The proﬁle graph of 0.25–1 M LiTFSI showed a sim-
ilar trend where the lower the concentration, the higher the capac-
ity, whereas 2 M LiTFSI showed a lower potential of the upper
plateau and an unstable lower plateau with suppressed sulfur uti-
lization (Fig. 1b). Under the severely sluggish conditions at E/S 5
[17,32], 2 M LiTFSI showed upper plateau shrinkage and a sudden
voltage drop in the initial stage of the lower plateau, and 1 M LiTFSI
showed a remarkably depressed lower plateau (Fig. 1c). The solid-
state conversion of S8 to LiPS in 0.5 M LiTFSI showed a similar
trend, whereas the Li2S conversion section at the endpoint of the
lower plateau showed a signiﬁcant depression compared to that
of the 0.25 M LiTFSI. These results indicate that low concentration
electrolytes provide a greater extent of LiPS and Li2S formation that
improves sulfur utilization even under sluggish conditions.
3.2. The solid-state S8 dissolution to LiPS
Fig. 2(a) shows schematic illustrations of how much sulfur is
converted to Li2S according to LiTFSI concentrations. Each concen-
tration affects the high-loading sulfur cathode to elucidate the dif-
ference between wettability and sulfur utilization. The higher
electrolyte concentrations could impede the wettability and sulfur
utilization because the hydrophobicity of carbon materials affects
the ﬂow of an ether-based solvent [21]. In addition, the thickness
of the Li-S cathode is nearly 140 lm for a sulfur loading of
5 mg cm2, which is thicker than the commercial LiBs because of
the highly porous electrode for sulfur utilization [34], and the con-
siderable amount of carbon materials with a large speciﬁc surface
area [35]. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the electrolyte wettability was
measured using the contact angle measurement. Contact angles
of 14.92, 30.66, 34.59, and 43.18 were obtained from 0.25,
0.5, 1, and 2 M LiTFSI, respectively. A bare PE separator was used
for the accuracy of the measurement, and the angle was measured
after 1 s. The contact angle measurement suggests that a lower
concentration electrolyte could provide a kinetically favorable con-
dition and a uniform reaction in a high-loading cathode.

conventional electrolyte 1 M LiTFSI dissolved only after heating
at 60 C, and a color change in 2 M LiTFSI was only observed after

3.4. Electrochemical analysis

increases, another semi-circle corresponding to the charge transfer

trolyte concentration in order to investigate how the generation
of LiPS and Li2S inﬂuences electrode resistance. EIS was measured
at DOD of 0%, 20%, 40%, 80%, and 100%, respectively, after the cell
was galvanostatically discharged at 0.1C. The 0.1C discharge volt-

4. Conclusions
We
demonstrated
that
the
low
concentration
electrolyte
(0.25 M LiTFSI) exhibited high performance in Li-S batteries with
a high-loading cathode (6 mgsulfur cm2) and low E/S ratio
(5 mL gsulfur
1
) compared to the high concentration electrolyte
(1 M LiTFSI) that represented a conventional salt concentration in
many studies. Under low-rate discharge conditions with a substan-
tial quantity of electrolyte, the discharge capacity of the low-salt
concentration electrolyte was close to the theoretical capacity of
sulfur. The discharge capability of a highly loaded sulfur electrode
tends to drop dramatically as the salt concentration rises. Speciﬁ-
cally, in the case of an electrolyte with a high salt concentration
and a small capacity in the ﬁrst discharge reaction in which the
high order polysulﬁde is dissolved, it is assumed that the elec-
trolyte has insufﬁcient space to accept the high order PS due to
the concentration of the already sufﬁciently dissolved salt. Further-
more, we focused on the resistance owing to the Li2S deposition at
the end of discharge, as well as the difference in voltage proﬁles.
The potentiostatic discharge test conﬁrmed that the amount of
Li2S deposition on carbon paper decreased with less salt concentra-
tion. The XPS analysis corroborated that LiPS peaks developed with
increasing salt concentration, suggesting that the higher the salt
concentration, the lesser the LiPS conversion to Li2S. At the end
of discharge, DOD 80%, the EIS and GITT measurements indicated
that the high salt concentration showed higher reaction resistance;
this was because the kinetic reaction was greatly inﬂuenced by Li2S
deposition. The rate-dependent CV measurement illustrated the
differences in Li2S deposition by kinetic effect; the 0.25 M LiTFSI
had a relatively high Li+ diffusion coefﬁcient value. Both LiPS solu-
bility and reaction kinetics that effectively increased Li2S deposi-
tion were investigated in terms of salt concentration. Based on
this study, we propose that the signiﬁcant factor in electrolyte
design of Li-S batteries is the reaction kinetics while ensuring sol-
ubility of LiPS, especially in the practical application of a high-
loading cathode with a low E/S ratio. We believe that these ﬁndings
contribute to optimizing the electrolyte for advanced Li-S battery
technology that satisﬁes the higher energy density with maxi-
mized sulfur utilization and a longer cycle life.
