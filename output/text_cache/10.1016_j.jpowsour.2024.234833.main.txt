1. Introduction 
The growing demand for energy storage devices in mobile applica­
tions has highlighted the limitations of commercial lithium-ion batteries 
(LIBs) owing to their relatively low energy density [1–5]. Lithium-sulfur 
batteries (LSBs) have emerged as promising contenders for the next 
generation of energy storage systems due to their high energy density 
(2600 W h kg−1), substantial specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1), abun­
dance in nature, environmental friendliness and cost-effectiveness 
[6–8]. Traditional LSBs involve two fundamental processes: i) the 
solid-liquid-solid conversion of sulfur at the cathode interface, pro­
gressing from sulfur to intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), and 
eventually to lithium sulfide (Li2S); ii) the dissolution and deposition of 
lithium on the anode side [9]. However, despite the considerable energy 
density potential exhibited by LSBs, they grapple with several notable 
challenges [10–13]. On the sulfur cathode side, challenges include the 
insulation characteristics of S/Li2S, the shuttle effect driven by soluble 
LiPSs intermediates, and the substantial volume change of the cathode 
(roughly 80 %). These challenges contribute to sluggish reaction ki­
netics, low sulfur utilization, and rapid capacity decay [14–16]. Mean­
while, on the lithium anode side, the high chemical reactivity of metallic 
lithium presents two major issues: i) Uneven deposition of lithium can 
result in the formation of dendrites, which may penetrate separators and 
cause short circuits to compromise battery safety. ii) Transference of 
LiPSs to the lithium anode through the shuttle effect, where they react 
directly with metallic lithium. This forms an unstable solid-electrolyte 
interface (SEI) layer, and results in active material loss, electrolyte 
decomposition and rapid anode degradation [17,18]. These critical 
challenges obstruct the practical deployment of LSBs. 
To tackle these challenges, researchers have proposed several stra­
tegies to enhance LSB performance. These approaches encompass the 
rational design of sulfur cathodes, including various carbon/sulfur 
composite structures [19–21], engineering advancements and protec­
tive measures for the lithium anode, such as artificial SEI [22,23], 
three-dimensional current collectors [24,25], and modifications to the 
battery’s membrane [26,27]. Nevertheless, many of these technologies 
necessitate intricate synthesis processes. In contrast to the aforemen­
tioned strategies, enhancing LSB performance through electrolyte opti­
mization offers a more straightforward approach. The electrolyte in LSBs 
plays a crucial role by serving as a conductor for Li+, providing a media 
with high solubility for polysulfides, and thereby promoting the elec­
trochemical reaction process of the sulfur cathode [28]. Therefore, it is 
possible to effectively enhance battery performance by refining elec­
trolyte design. Typically, the electrolyte comprises high-purity organic 
solvents, lithium salt electrolytes, and necessary additives. Introducing 
electrolyte additives proves to be an economical, convenient, and effi­
cient method compared to adjusting solvent and salt compositions or 
developing new electrolyte formulations. Usually, a small concentration 
of electrolyte additives (often less than 10 %) is sufficient to induce 
significant changes in the electrolyte’s properties [29]. 
LiNO3 is a frequently employed additive in ether-based electrolytes 
for LSBs. Its primary function is to passivate the lithium surface through 
the conversion of precipitated reduced sulfur species into oxidized sulfur 
species (Li2SxOy). This conversion leads to the formation of a relatively 
stable SEI layer, which contributes to improved battery performance and 
longevity [30]. The SEI layer, however, demonstrates constrained Li ion 
conductivity, and its resistance gradually escalates with the progression 
of battery cycling due to nitrate consumption [9]. In this aspect, Li et al. 
[31] successfully developed stable inorganic SEI layer enriched with 
LiCl on lithium metal by introducing thionyl chloride (SOCl2) into the 
system. This innovation effectively prevented the growth of dendrite. 
Additionally, in the quest to optimize SEI composition, researchers have 
identified substances such as LiF [32], LiCl [33], and LiI [34] as stable 
components of the SEI. Furthermore, the introduction of organic small 
molecules presents a viable approach. Organic additives are favored 
because of their diverse constituent elements, structural versatility, and 
the high tunability of functional groups within their molecules. Those 
features surpass the capabilities of inorganic salt additives. In this 
context, Fu’s group [35] explored various isomers of benzene dithiols 
(BDTs). Their investigation was coupled with in-situ Raman character­
ization and it unveiled that 1,4-BDT effectively modified the sulfur redox 
pathway, and thus restrained the shuttle effect of LiPSs. Moreover, 1, 
4-BDT exhibited resistance to steric hindrance due to its 180◦bond 
angle. In contrast, the other two isomers of 1,2-BDT and 1,3-BDT were 
found to be less effective due to spatial hindrance effects. However, 
current research primarily focuses on addressing the challenges of in­
dividual electrodes, either the lithium anode or sulfur cathode. Research 
aimed at simultaneously optimizing the performance of both the anode 
and cathode in LSBs to attain synergistic enhancements is relatively 
scarce [36,37]. To elevate the electrochemical performance of LSBs, 
there is an urgent requirement to identify appropriate electrolyte addi­
tives that are capable of concurrently bolstering the interface stability of 
both the lithium anode and sulfur cathode. 
In this study, the dichloropyrazines (DCPs) were utilized as dual- 
functional additives in LSBs. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, DCPs are organic 
pyrazine compounds with the chemical formula C4H2Cl2N2 and they are 
isomers of one another. Through a combined approach involving ex­
periments and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, two key 
roles played by DCPs were uncovered in enhancing LSB performance. 
For example. when 2,5-DCP is used in the lithium anode, it reacts with 
Li2Sx to form LiCl and an organic lithium salt C4H2N2ClSxLi (Li-Sx-2,5- 
DCP) (x = 4, 6, 8) [38]. These compounds deposit on the lithium anode 
along with others, which in-situ creates a stable, LiCl-rich, uniform, 
dense, and reliable organic-inorganic hybrid SEI. The formation of such 
a SEI diminishes the overpotential for lithium nucleation, facilitates 
uniform deposition of lithium and effectively averts dendrite growth 
[39]. Regarding the sulfur cathode, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS) and DFT calculations have validated that Li in LiPSs forms com­
plexes with the N atoms in the hexagonal framework of 2,5-DCP via Li–N 
bonds. This interaction fine-tunes the energy levels of LiPSs’ molecular 
orbitals and accelerates the kinetic processes between LiPS species [40]. 
Batteries with DCPs exhibit outstanding rate performance of 604.8 mAh 
g−1 at a 5C rate, while also displaying impressive cycling stability with 
86.6 % capacity retention after 800 cycles at a 1C rate. This study in­
troduces a dual-functional electrolyte additive based on DCPs, which 
effectively enhance the kinetic processes of sulfur and optimize the 
interfacial chemical properties of advanced LSBs.

As illustrated in Fig. 2a‒c, the isomer of DCPs-Li2SX arises from the 
nitrogen-lithium interaction between DCPs and LiPSs. Specifically, 2, 
3-DCP and 2,5-DCP can each form a single type of complex with Li2Sx, 
referred to as 2,3-DCP-Li2Sx and 2,5-DCP-Li2Sx, respectively. However, 
when Li2Sx interacts with 2,6-DCP, it can form different structures by 
connecting through N atoms at position 1 and position 4, resulting in 
complexes known as ortho-2,6-DCP-Li2Sx and para-2,6-DCP-Li2Sx. 
Computational calculations have demonstrated that stable configura­
tions can be formed when LiPSs interact with DCPs. In particular, the 
analysis of the △E among various compounds involving DCPs-Li2Sx 
reveals that 2,5-DCP exhibits a strong propensity to establish a stable 
configuration with LiPSs. This suggests that 2,5-DCP exhibits a strong 
affinity for LiPSs, which makes it an effective candidate for enhancing 
the performance of LSBs. Taking Li2S6 as an example, the stability order 
of the various isomers of DCPs-Li2S6 compounds is following: 2, 
5-DCP-Li2S6 (−20.98 kcal mol−1) < ortho-2,6-DCP-Li2S6 (−20.37 kcal 
mol−1) < para-2,6-DCP-Li2S6 (−20.05 kcal mol−1) < 2,3-DCP-Li2S6 
(−19.75 kcal mol−1). Comparing the △E of 2,3-DCP-Li2Sx, 2, 
5-DCP-Li2Sx, ortho-2,6-DCP-Li2Sx, and para-2,6-DCP-Li2Sx, the stability 
order of the three isomers is 2,5-DCP-Li2Sx > 2,6-DCP-Li2Sx > 2, 
3-DCP-Li2Sx. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the relative strength of 
binding energy bears resemblance to hydrogen bonds, which suggests 
the reversible nature of formation and dissociation of Li–N bonds be­
tween DCPs and Li2Sx [40]. This reversibility is crucial because it en­
sures that the interactions between DCPs and LiPSs do not impede the 
electrochemical reactions of LiPSs in improving battery performance. 
To assess the impact of DCPs on the kinetics of electrochemical 
reactions involving LiPSs and reveal the origin of faster redox reaction 
kinetics between DCPs and LiPSs compared to the reactions among 
LiPSs, the DFT calculations were performed to determine the energy 
levels and distributions of the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs). 
Fig. 3a‒c provides visual representations of the highest occupied mo­
lecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital 
(LUMO) for the intermediates involving DCPs and Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, 8). 
Understanding the energy levels of these orbitals is essential for com­
prehending their electrochemical properties. For instance, a lower 
LUMO energy level indicates that the compound possesses a greater 
ability to accept electrons, which in turn signifies a higher electron af­
finity and a more potent oxidative capability as an oxidant. Conversely, 
a higher HOMO energy level of a compound suggests a more effortless 
release of electrons, which speaks to a stronger reduction ability [40, 
45]. In this context, the DCPs-Li2Sx intermediates show a narrower 
bandgap compared to Li2Sx, as illustrated in Fig. 3d. This narrower 
bandgap is indicative of better conductivity in the DCPs-Li2Sx in­
termediates compared to pure Li2Sx. The lower bandgap and improved 
conductivity of DCPs-Li2Sx intermediates play pivotal roles in facili­
tating faster charge transfer and electron kinetics, as indicated by pre­
vious research [46,47].

CV as a crucial technique for assessing the kinetics of electrochemical 
reactions can directly reflect the reversibility and stability of these re­
actions. It provides additional validation of the enhanced electro­
chemical performance and mechanism resulting from the inclusion of 
DCPs. The CV curve in Fig. 5a illustrates that batteries with DCPs exhibit 
higher peak current intensities and narrower voltage gaps (2,3-DCP: 
344 mV; 2,5-DCP: 279 mV; 2,6-DCP: 315 mV; control cell: 404 mV). The 
CV peak observed at 2.7 V represents the disproportionation reaction 
between Li2S6 and DCPs, which contributes to the sulfur capacity [38]. 
Additionally, the peak intensities of these three compounds at 2.7 V 
differ and 2,3-DCP battery exhibits a lower peak compared to 2,5-DCP 
and 2,6-DCP batteries. This variance can be elucidated by the fact that 
2,3-DCP has a lower HOMO energy level compared to the other addi­
tives from Li2S6 to Li2S8, while exhibiting a higher LUMO energy level 
during the process from Li2S6 to Li2S4. A higher HOMO energy level 
indicates easier oxidation reactions, while a lower LUMO energy level 
facilitates easier reduction reactions [40,45]. Therefore, the results 
indicate that 2,3-DCP is relatively less effective in promoting the 
disproportionation reaction of Li2S6, and this leads to the lower peak 
intensity observed in the CV analysis. In contrast, 2,5-DCP and 2,6-DCP 
have lower LUMO energy levels and higher HOMO energy levels. This 
makes them more conducive in promoting the disproportionation re­
action of Li2S6 and resulting in higher peak intensities and different CV

Fig. S13 and Fig. 6b present the EIS curves of symmetrical Li||Li cells

contact (RSEI) and during charge transfer (Rct). The resistance to Li+
diffusion is indicated by the slope observed in the low-frequency region.

and regeneration of the solid electrolyte interface layer on the lithium 
surface resulted in a constant accumulation of dead lithium. This leads 
to the increase of charge transfer resistance during the process of lithium 
plating/stripping, which consequently hinders the deintercalation of 
lithium ions. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that after cycling, the control 
battery exhibited an increase in RSEI, and this increase can be attributed

contrary, the cells with DCP additives display reduced Rct and RSEI, as 
illustrated in Fig. 6d. This observation implies that the addition of DCP 
additives effectively enhanced the interfacial characteristics of the bat­

formation of RSEI. All those factors collectively contribute to the accel­

enhancement in lithium-ion transference is attributed to the coordina­

which improved the interface between the electrolyte and the electrode 
in LSBs and reduced interface polarization. Moreover, the addition of 
2,5-DCP results in a higher lithium-ion transference number with 
accelerating reaction kinetics and improving ion transport rates. Addi­
tionally, the addition of DCP has a significant impact on the electrolyte’s 
conductivity. The addition of DCP can promote the decomposition of 
lithium salts, thereby increase the number of charge carriers, and 
improve the conductivity [43]. The increase in conductivity mainly 
comes from the enhancement in electronic and ionic conductivity, 
which helps to improve sulfur redox kinetics.

3. Conclusions 
In summary, this study introduces a novel category of dichloropyr­
azine (DCP) electrolyte additives with dual functions in lithium-sulfur 
batteries (LSBs). These DCP additives enhance sulfur redox kinetics 
and protect the lithium anode. On the anode side, DCPs improve inter­
face stability and result in the formation of a uniform and stable organic- 
inorganic hybrid solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer. This SEI layer 
effectively suppresses side reactions and promotes uniform lithium 
deposition. On the cathode side, DCP additives optimize the energy 
levels of molecular orbitals in lithium polysulfides (LiPSs). This modi­
fication facilitates the transfer of charges, reduces energy barriers and 
activation energies during reactions, and enhances charge transfer rates 
on the electrode surface, which ultimately accelerates reaction kinetics. 
A performance ranking is evident with addition of the different DCP 
isomers: 2,5-DCP >2,6-DCP >2,3-DCP. These findings are supported by 
relative binding energies and frontier orbital energy level calculations. 
As a result of these enhancements, LSBs incorporating DCPs exhibit 
exceptional rate capability and cycling stability. The battery incorpo­
rating the optimal 2,5-DCP additive exhibits an initial discharge ca­
pacity of 894 mAh g−1 at a current density of 1C, while maintaining a 
capacity retention of 773 mAh g−1 after 800 cycles. This approach sulfur 
improves redox kinetics, safeguards the lithium anode, and ultimately 
enhances battery performance. Furthermore, density functional theory 
(DFT) calculations can be employed to facilitate the design and 
screening of analogous compounds with comparable chemical struc­
tures. This approach helps address the inherent obstacles encountered in 
lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) by predicting the electrochemical prop­
erties and performance of potential additives.
