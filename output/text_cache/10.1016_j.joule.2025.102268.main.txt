SUMMARY
Conventional lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, which rely on alkali metal ions (Li⁺/Na⁺/K⁺) as charge carriers, suf-
fer from polysulfide dissolution, poor Li 2 S conductivity, and low redox potential (− 0.5 V vs. the standard 
hydrogen electrode [SHE]). Here, we report a copper-ion-mediated sulfur battery chemistry that fundamen-
tally circumvents these limitations by leveraging the intrinsically low solubility of Cu x S in non-aqueous elec-
trolytes. By replacing alkali metal ions with copper ions as the charge carrier in a 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-dime-
thoxyethane (DOL/DME)-based electrolyte, we achieve an insoluble Cu x S discharge intermediate, which 
elevates the sulfur redox potential to 0.5 V vs. SHE, more than a 1.2 V increase over conventional Li-S sys-
tems, enabling a discharge voltage of 3.3 V in a hybrid Li-S full cell. Simultaneously, the formation of conduc-
tive CuₓS species ensures efficient reaction kinetics while eliminating polysulfide shuttling. The copper-ion-
mediated sulfur electrochemistry unlocks high energy density and fast kinetics, paving the way for practical 
sulfur-based batteries.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have emerged as a leading next-
generation energy storage technology due to the exceptional 
theoretical specific capacity (1,675 mAh g − 1 ), environmental sus-
tainability, and low cost of sulfur cathodes. 1–3 However, conven-
tional Li-S batteries, which rely on alkali metal ions (Li + , Na + ,
and K + ) as charge carriers, face critical challenges that hinder their 
practical deployment: (1) the dissolution of polysulfide intermedi-
ates (e.g., Li 2 S x ) into the electrolyte, leading to active material loss 
and rapid cycling degradation 4–6 ; (2) the poor electronic conduc-
tivity of the discharge end product (Li 2 S), which impedes reaction 
kinetics 1,7 ; and (3) the relatively low redox potential of sulfur 
(− 0.5 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode [SHE]), resulting in 
a cell voltage of only ∼2.1 V, significantly lower than that of tradi-
tional lithium-ion batteries. 8,9
Current research efforts primarily focus on mitigating 
polysulfide dissolution through advanced sulfur host mate-
rials, 10–12 catalytic strategies, 13,14 or functional interlayer de-
signs 15 to improve polysulfide conversion kinetics. While these ap-
proaches alleviate some issues, they fail to address the root cause 
of these challenges. A more fundamental solution lies in

lution problem that has long plagued traditional Li-S batteries.
RESULTS
Design and synthesis of the S@CNT composite
Pristine commercial sulfur particles exhibit a broad size distribution 
ranging from several to tens of micrometers (Figure S1). This large 
size, combined with the intrinsically poor electronic conductivity 
(5 × 10 − 30 S cm − 1 ) and significant volume expansion during the 
discharging process, severely limits its practical application. 19,23 
To mitigate these issues, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were em-
ployed as the conductive host to encapsulate sulfur (Figure S2). 
The sulfur@carbon nanotube (S@CNT) composite was synthe-
sized via the melt-diffusion method, with thermogravimetric anal-
ysis (TGA) confirming a high sulfur content of ∼73 wt %. The 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the S@CNT composite aligns 
well with the crystalline sulfur (Figure S3), indicating successful sul-
fur infiltration without phase alteration. Scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) images and elemental mapping further demonstrate 
uniform sulfur distribution within the CNT matrix, while preserving 
the structural integrity of the CNT network (Figure S4). This inter-
connected architecture ensures continuous electron transport 
pathways, critical for efficient charge transfer. X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) corroborates the coexistence of carbon and 
sulfur in the composite (Figure S5), confirming effective sulfur 
confinement within the conductive framework.
Engineering copper-ion electrolyte for non-aqueous 
sulfur redox
To fundamentally overcome the limitations of conventional sulfur 
batteries, we propose a carrier-ion engineering strategy that re-
places alkali metal ions with copper ions. The successful imple-
mentation of this approach requires the development of a stable, 
non-aqueous copper-ion electrolyte system compatible with sul-
fur redox chemistry. We selected DOL/DME (1:1 v/v), a widely 
used ether solvent in traditional lithium-sulfur batteries, as the 
organic medium for the copper-ion electrolyte and evaluated the 
compatibility of three copper salts. Among them, CuSO 4 proved 
insoluble, CuCl 2 exhibited limited solubility, while Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 
demonstrated complete solubility, enabling the preparation of a 
clear, homogeneous 1 M electrolyte solution (Figure 1A). The 
high solubility of Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 can be attributed to the weakly coordi-
nating ClO 4 
− anions, which reduce Coulombic interactions be-
tween Cu 2+ and anions. 24,25 Consequently, the Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 electro-
lyte (1 M) achieves the highest ionic conductivity (3.9 mS cm − 1 ), 
significantly exceeding the saturated solutions of CuCl 2 (1.4 mS 
cm − 1 ) and CuSO 4 (0.04 mS cm − 1 ) (Figures 1B and S6). Further 
investigation of copper-ion plating/stripping behavior in symmet-
ric Cu-Cu cell corroborated the advantages of the Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 elec-
trolyte, revealing a low overpotential of 0.02 V at 0.1 mA cm − 2 . In 
contrast, CuCl 2 failed to support reversible plating/stripping, while 
the CuSO 4 electrolyte exhibited an excessively high overpotential 
of 1 V (Figure 1C). These findings establish Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 as an appro-
priate Cu 2+ source for non-aqueous copper-ion electrolyte.
The electrolyte concentration critically modulates ion transport 
dynamics and solvation structure. 26 As concentration increases 
from 1 to 4 M, all electrolytes maintain homogeneous, precipita-
tion-free solutions (Figure S7). The ionic conductivity initially rises, 
peaking at 6.75 mS cm − 1 for 3 M. Beyond 3 M, conductivity de-
clines due to dominant viscosity effects and ion aggregation 
(Figures 1D and S8). 27 Complementary restricted diffusion mea-
surements reveal the effective diffusivity of the Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 in the 
DOL/DME at various concentrations (Figure S9). 28,29 Concurrently, 
the Cu 2+ transference number reaches its maximum value of 0.31 
at 3 M (Figures 1E and S10). The activation energy reaches to a min-
imum of 44.12 kJ/mol at 3 M concentration (Figures 1F and S11). 
To analyze the evolution of solvation structure with the concen-
tration increase, Raman and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) 
spectroscopy were employed. The decreasing intensity of DME-
specific peaks at 822 cm − 1 (CH 2 rocking) and 850 cm − 1 (C–O 
stretching) indicates reduced free solvent molecules at higher con-
centrations (Figure 1G). 30 Meanwhile, the symmetric stretching vi-

indicating the formation of ionic aggregation at high concentrations 
(Figures 1H and S12). 31,32 FTIR spectra further elucidate these

Sulfur redox chemistry in non-aqueous copper-ion 
electrolyte
The reaction mechanism of sulfur cathode in non-aqueous cop-
per-ion electrolyte remains unexplored. To elucidate the reaction 
pathway, we conducted a comprehensive suite of ex situ and in 
situ characterizations, including XRD, high-resolution transmis-
sion electron microscopy (HRTEM), XPS, and in situ XRD anal-
ysis during cycling.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis reveals a dynamic redox 
evolution: the first cycle exhibits a single reduction peak at 
0.11 V, while subsequent cycles show two well-defined reduc-
tion peaks at 0.18 and 0.08 V (Figures 3A and 3B). Ex situ XRD 
analysis confirms the formation of crystalline Cu 2 S (JCPDS 09-
0328) as the final discharge product in the initial cycle 
(Figure 3C). This is further corroborated by XPS analysis of the 
Cu 2p spectrum, where the characteristic binding energies align 
with the Cu + oxidation state in Cu 2 S (Figure S26). In the second 
cycle, the CV curve shows two distinct reduction peaks: an inter-
mediate Cu 9 S 8 phase (JCPDS 36-0379) forms after the first

Upon charging, the final charge product consists of a compos-

crystalline CuS (Figure S27), while HRTEM identifies the (103) lat-

of amorphous sulfur (Figures 3E–3G, S28, and S29). The 
absence of distinct sulfur diffraction peaks is attributed to its 
amorphous nature. Additionally, the S 2p XPS spectrum of the 
charged electrode shows dual contributions from S 0 and S 2− 
species (Figure S30), confirming incomplete re-oxidation to 
elemental sulfur. This also explains the observed discrepancy 
between the first charge and discharge capacities.
In order to dynamically track the transition reaction path, in situ 
XRD was performed throughout the 1 st , 2 nd , and 3 rd cycles. As 
the discharge proceeded, the intermediate copper sulfide 
phases sequentially form: Cu 9 S 8 (47.9 ◦ ), Cu 7 S 4 (46.8 ◦ ), and 
Cu 31 S 16 (46.3 ◦ ). At the fully discharged stage, new peaks emerge 
at 46.1 ◦ and 48.7 ◦ , corresponding to the (600) and (346) planes of 
Cu 2 S, respectively. In the following charge process, the Cu 2 S 
progressively disappears, with the reappearance of Cu 31 S 16 , 
Cu 7 S 4 , and Cu 9 S 8 in reverse order (Figures 3H and S31), demon-
strating a multi-step conversion pathway: S ↔ Cu 9 S 8 ↔ Cu 7 S 4 ↔ 
Cu 31 S 16 ↔ Cu 2 S. CV analysis with varying scan rates demon-
strates three oxidation peaks and two distinct reduction peaks, 
as well indicating multi-step redox reactions (Figure S32).
The comprehensive analysis reveals a multi-stage reaction 
pathway for sulfur in non-aqueous copper-ion electrolytes. During 
the first discharge, sulfur undergoes complete reduction to form 
Cu 2 S. Upon charging, partial re-oxidation occurs, yielding a com-
posite of CuS and amorphous sulfur rather than fully regenerating 
elemental sulfur. In subsequent cycles, the system exhibits revers-
ible conversion between this CuS/amorphous S composite and 
Cu 2 S, progressing through intermediate copper sulfide phases 
(Cu 9 S 8 , Cu 7 S 4 , and Cu 31 S 16 ) in a stepwise manner (Figure 3I). 
The polysulfide shuttle is effectively suppressed by the 
extremely low solubility of the Cu x S intermediates, as confirmed 
by the absence of any Raman peaks for soluble sulfur species 
throughout multi-cycle processes (Figure S33). The theoretical 
calculation results further demonstrate the discharge/charge 
product exhibits extremely low thermodynamic solubility in the 
solvent, which further confirms the high potential in copper-
ion-mediated sulfur battery system (Figure S34).
Accelerated reaction kinetics due to conductive Cu x S 
intermediates
The reaction mechanism indicates that the sulfur cathode un-
dergoes a transformation into a complex of S/CuS in subsequent 
cycles. Hence, the component evolution of the electrode prod-
ucts during the cycling process was analyzed through electron 
probe micro-analysis (EPMA) element mapping of fully charged 
cathodes after the 1 st and 30 th cycles (Figures 4A and 4B). The 
results reveal an enhancement in the characteristic X-ray signal
intensity of copper element, while the signal of sulfur element 
relatively weakens, indicating that the proportion of CuS in the 
charging products gradually increases.
The effect of this incomplete transition on the copper-ion-
mediated sulfur battery system was further analyzed by GCD 
tests. As illustrated in Figure 4C, the initial discharge potential 
of the second cycle exhibits a marked increase compared to 
the first cycle. This observation is further supported by CV re-
sults, where the onset reduction potential in the second cycle 
is higher than that observed in the first cycle (Figures 4D and 
4E). Moreover, two distinct reduction peaks emerge in the sec-
ond cycle, in contrast to the single peak observed initially. This 
transition is attributed to the dynamic phase evolution during 
cycling. The initial cathode consists of sulfur with poor conduc-
tivity, leading to sluggish kinetics that merge the distinct reduc-
tion steps of the intermediate phases into a single, broad peak. In 
the subsequent cycle, the charged product is a conductive com-
posite of CuS and amorphous sulfur. The formed conductive 
CuS network significantly accelerates the reaction kinetics, 
enabling the individual reduction steps (S/CuS → Cu 9 S 8 and 
Cu 9 S 8 → Cu 2 S) to be resolved as two distinct peaks in the CV 
curve. Quantitative analysis of the change in capacity of two 
discharge plateaus also confirms the effect of CuS (Figure S35). 
To further elucidate the kinetic properties during the 
discharge/charge process, we performed distribution of relaxa-
tion times (DRT) analysis on the electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) data (Figures 4F, 4G, and S36). According 
to relaxation time, these peaks can be divided into the contact 
resistance (R c ) and charge transfer resistance (R ct ). 45–47 During 
a single discharge/charge process (Figure 4F), the results indi-
cate that the cathode maintains a consistently low R c throughout 
the whole process, indicating optimal contact between the cath-
ode and the collector, while the R ct decreases during discharge 
due to the formation of conductive intermediate Cu x S phases. In 
the subsequent charging process, the impedance increases 
gradually resulting from the decline of Cu x S phases and the 
accumulation of S. Notably, the observed leftward shift of R ct 
peaks in the DRT spectra indicates reduced interfacial capaci-
tance, consistent with the lower dielectric constant of Cu x S. 
Moreover, the impedance variation over the initial 10 cycles of 
the cell was tested (Figure 4G). The results demonstrate that 
the R c of the cell remains constant with an increase in the number 
of cycles, substantiating the stability of the copper-ion-mediated 
non-aqueous sulfur reaction system. Meanwhile, R ct exhibits a 
continuous leftward shift and a substantial decrease with 
cycling. This further suggests that the accumulation of CuS dur-
ing cycling significantly enhances the conductivity of the S/CuS 
composite, which in turn accelerates the electrode reaction 
kinetics.

This overall performance underscores the promising potential 
in high-energy-density applications.
Extended cycling tests reveal gradual capacity decay 
(Figure S42), which is attributed to partial Cu 2+ crossover 
through the membrane. Post-cycling XPS analysis of the lithium 
anode confirms the presence of copper and the complete 
absence of sulfur signals, indicating Cu plating without a polysul-
fide shuttle (Figure S43). Quantitative analysis by inductively 
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) further reveals a 
Cu content of 0.36 wt % on the lithium anode and a concentra-
tion of 4.73 g L − 1 in the anolyte (Table S5), corresponding to a 
crossover flux of 1.69 × 10 − 11 mol cm − 2 s − 1 (Table S6). Address-
ing this crossover represents a key area for future optimization to 
enhance long-term cyclability.
DISCUSSION
In summary, this work reimagines sulfur electrochemistry 
through copper-ion mediation. We demonstrate a non-aqueous 
sulfur battery that simultaneously overcomes the voltage, stabil-
ity, and kinetic limitations of conventional Li-S systems. The 
intrinsic insolubility of Cu x S intermediates elevates the sulfur 
redox potential to 0.5 V vs. SHE, enabling a hybrid Li-S full cell 
with a 3.3 V discharge voltage—the highest reported for sulfur-
based batteries. This design eliminates polysulfide shuttling 
while ensuring efficient kinetics through conductive Cu x S 
phases, delivering an energy density of 980 Wh kg − 1 . Our work 
establishes carrier-ion engineering as a general strategy to 
decouple sulfur electrodes from alkali metal constraints, opening 
pathways for practical high-energy storage systems.
Despite the promising electrochemical performance, several 
challenges remain to be addressed in the future research. First, 
the incomplete oxidation of Cu 2 S to S during the initial charge cy-
cle results in a reduced first-cycle Coulombic efficiency, high-
lighting the need for improved oxidation kinetics. Second, the 
Cu 2+ crossover through the membrane in the hybrid full cell leads 
to parasitic reactions at the lithium anode and gradual capacity 
fading during extended cycling. This issue highlights the neces-
sity for the development of more selective and stable mem-
branes with improved Cu 2+ blocking capability. Furthermore, 
the currently employed H-type full-cell configuration requires a 
large amount of electrolyte, and its substantial inter-electrode

METHODS
Synthesis of S@CNT composite and preparation of 
electrodes
S@CNT composites were synthesized by the melt-diffusion 
method. S (Aladdin, 99.95%) and CNT powders (Aladdin, 98%) 
were homogeneously mixed in a mass ratio of 3:1. Subse-
quently, the mixture was heated up to 155 ◦ C for 12 h under 
an argon atmosphere. The S@CNT electrodes (thickness:
∼350 μm) were prepared by mixing S@CNT composite, carbon 
black (Alfa Aesar, 99%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) 
binder (Alfa Aesar) with a mass ratio of 8:1:1. This mixture was 
then dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Aladdin, 99%) 
to form a slurry. The corresponding slurry was coated onto a 
stainless-steel mesh (12 mm in diameter) and dried under vac-
uum at 60 ◦ C for 12 h to obtain the electrodes.
Preparation of electrolytes
Copper perchlorate hexahydrate (Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 ⸱6H 2 O; Meryer, re-
agent grade) was dried at 80 ◦ C under vacuum for 12 h before us-
ing. ‘‘M’’ refers to mol-salt in L-solvent, with 1, 2, 3, and 4 mol of 
salt per 1 L of solvent referring to 1, 2, 3, and 4 M, respectively. 
Furthermore, 1, 2, 3, and 4 M Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 electrolytes were pre-
pared by completely dissolving 0.37, 0.74, 1.11, and 1.48 g 
Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 ⸱6H 2 O in the 1 mL mixture solvent of DOL/DME with 
a volume ratio of 1:1. Furthermore, to investigate the electro-
chemical properties of Cu-S cell with different electrolytes, the 
electrolytes were also prepared by dissolving Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 ⸱6H 2 O 
in EC/DMC and EC/EMC with a volume ratio of 1:1. The anolyte 
electrolyte with 1.5 M Li(ClO 4 ) 2 in DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) of the full 
cell was purchased from DoDo Chem.
Material characterization
The morphology and microstructure analysis of the samples were 
investigated by SEM (JSM-7900F, Japan) and TEM (JEOL JEM-
2100F, Japan). The XRD (DX-200, Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 A

was investigated using TGA (NETZSCH STA 449 F3) in a N 2 atmo-
sphere at a heating rate of 10 ◦ C min − 1 . The XPS was collected

Electrochemical measurements
Cu-Cu symmetric cell and Cu-S cell were both assembled in the 
CR2032-type coin cells (calendaring pressures, 10 MPa). For the 
Cu-Cu symmetric cell, Cu foil was directly used as electrodes. 
For the Cu-S cell, the S@CNT electrode was used as the cathode
and the Cu foil (16 mm in diameter) as the anode. The glass-fiber 
(Whatman GF/D, 16 mm in diameter) membrane was employed 
as the separator. The total mass of a coin cell is about 3.85 g. All 
the specific capacities were calculated based on the mass of sul-
fur. The areal loading of S is about 2 mg cm − 2 . The Li-S hybrid full 
cell was assembled with the H-cell configuration, the catholyte 
and anolyte was 1 M Cu(ClO 4 ) 2 and 1.5 M LiClO 4 in DOL/DME 
(1:1, v/v), the amount of electrolytes were all 5 mL. S@CNT 
electrode acted as the cathode, Li foil served as the anode (the 
thickness of Li foil is 2 mm), and the AEM (fumasep FAPQ-330,
∼30-μm thick) was used to separate the catholyte and the ano-
lyte. All electrochemical tests were performed at 25 ◦ C.
The galvanostatic charge-discharge measurements of the 
Cu-S cell and Li-S full cell were carried out on the Neware battery 
testing system (5 V, 10 mA; Shenzhen, China). The GITT curves 
were obtained by a series of galvanostatic discharge processes 
of 15 min at 0.1 A g − 1 followed by 60 min rest at each step. The 
EIS was tested on the CHI760E electrochemical workstation with 
the frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz with a disturbance 
amplitude of 5 mV. The chronoamperometry (CA) and the CV 
measurements were performed on the BioLogic VSP-300 elec-
trochemical workstation.
