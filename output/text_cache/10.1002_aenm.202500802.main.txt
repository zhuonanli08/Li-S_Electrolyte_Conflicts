Tuning Polysulﬁdes into Clustered-States via
Non-coordinating Molecular Encapsulation to Achieve an
Alternative Kinetics in Li─S Batteries
Qingyi Zheng, Leyuan Cui, Xiaojiao Zhao, Ruming Yuan, Chutao Wang, Kun Wang,
Jingmin Fan, Mingsen Zheng,* and Quanfeng Dong*
The practical application of lithium–sulfur batteries is hindered by the
polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect and sluggish kinetics inherent in solid–liquid–solid
conversion mechanism, particularly under lean electrolyte conditions
(<5 µL mg−1). Weakly solvating electrolytes and localized high-concentration
electrolytes can suppress polysulﬁde dissolution and enable a quasi-solid-
-phase mechanism but suﬀer from severely limited reaction kinetics. Herein, a
clustered-polysulﬁde-mediated sulfur conversion mechanism enabled by a
novel electrolyte composed of 1,2-dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimeth-
oxyethane (DME) is proposed. The encapsulation eﬀect of DTL and the
coordination of TFSI−with polysulﬁdes drives the aggregation of polysulﬁdes
so that the clustered polysulﬁdes with virtual shell boundaries can be the new
basic reactive that bridges the gap between the traditional dissolution-domi-
nated mechanism and quasi-solid-state mechanism. The clustered polysulﬁde
electrolyte (CPE) not only suppresses the shuttle eﬀect and stabilizes the
lithium anode by mitigating parasitic reactions but also enables alternative
reaction kinetics and promotes 3D Li2S deposition, minimizing electrode
passivation. Ultimately, lithium–sulfur batteries can achieve excellent
electrochemical performance and can stably operate under lean electrolyte-
(<4.0 µL mg−1) with an area capacity of >4 mAh cm−2. This work elucidates
the relationship between polysulﬁde dissolution behavior and redox kinetics,
providing a new insight into the understanding of complex sulfur conversion
mechanisms.

Herein, guided by the idea of regulating the state of reactive
species, we developed a novel electrolyte with low salt concentra-
tion (1 mol L−1) and low density (1.1 g cm−3) by employing 1,2-
dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as sol-
vents and achieved a clustered polysulﬁdes-mediated sulfur con-
version mechanism. The encapsulation eﬀect of DTL with the
ligand-mediated action of bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(TFSI−) drives the formation of polysulﬁde clusters in the
electrolyte, thus realizing a clustered polysulﬁde encapsula-
tion mechanism intermediate between the conventional solid-
liquid dissolution mechanism and the quasi-solid-solid conver-
sion mechanism. Clustered polysulﬁde electrolyte (CPE) not only
signiﬁcantly suppresses polysulﬁde solubility but also optimizes
Li2S deposition behavior, guiding the formation of 3D Li2S parti-
cles that minimize conductive electrode coverage. Furthermore,
the CPE eﬀectively mitigates side reactions between polysulﬁdes
and lithium metal, thereby stabilizing the Li anode interface. As
a result, the lithium–sulfur batteries exhibit exceptional reaction
kinetics and electrochemical reversibility, achieving a remarkable
coulombic eﬃciency of 99.6% and delivering a high capacity of
718 mAh g−1 at 5 C. Moreover, the batteries can stably operate
under lean electrolyte conditions with electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio
(E/S) < 4.0 μL mg−1, highlighting its potential for practical high-
energy-density applications.

60DTL electrolyte, respectively. The numbers represent the vol-

The dissolution of polysulﬁdes is closely related to the shuttle
eﬀect in lithium–sulfur batteries. The solubility of sulfur atoms
in diﬀerent electrolytes was determined by the Li2S6 dissolution
experiment. As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information),
lithium polysulﬁdes are completely insoluble in pure DTL sol-
vent and the solution still appears clear and transparent. In con-
trast, lithium polysulﬁdes dissolved heavily in 0DTL and the solu-
tion appeared dark black. With DTL content increasing, the sol-
ubility of lithium polysulﬁde decreases and the color gradually
changes from dark black to red. UV–vis spectroscopy was fur-
ther performed to obtain the solubility of polysulﬁdes in diﬀerent
electrolytes, as shown in Figure 1c. As shown in Table S3 (Sup-
porting Information), the solubility of sulfur atoms in 50DTL,
60DTL, and 70DTL was 305, 247, and 101 mmol L−1, respec-
tively. Among them, the sulfur solubility of 60DTL is only ≈5% of
that in conventional ether electrolytes (4000 mmol L−1).[2d] Con-
sequently, the dissolution of polysulﬁdes is signiﬁcantly reduced
due to the reduced electrolyte solvating capacity after introducing
DTL.
To reveal the driving eﬀect of the DTL, the 7Li nuclear mag-
netic resonance (NMR) and Raman spectroscopy were performed
to examine the diﬀerent electrolytes with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI as
lithium salt. Raman spectra show that the C─O─C vibration peak
of free DME molecules at 847 cm−1 diminishes with the increas-
ing volume fraction of DTL from 0% to 80% (Figure S2, Sup-
porting Information), indicating a decrease in the free DME sol-
vents and an increase in the liganded DME solvents. As shown
in Figure 1d, the coordination of lithium ions with TFSI−anions
is enhanced with increasing DTL volume fraction. It leads to an
increase in the electron cloud density of lithium atoms and an
increase in the shielding eﬀect, which in turn leads to a decrease
in the chemical shift of 7Li NMR. The 7Li NMR peak exhibits the
lowest chemical shift when the DTL content is 50–60%. In this
case, the strongest encapsulation eﬀect of DTL can drive aggrega-
tion of TFSI−and Li+. However, excessive DTL molecules (>60%,
v/v) make insuﬃcient solvents for coordination with lithium
ions, instead destroying the coordination environment of lithium
ions, which in turn results in an increase in the chemical shift of
7Li peak. Similarly, the electrolyte with Li2S6 as the only lithium
salt also shows a decreasing chemical shift of 7Li peak with in-
creasing DTL content, further demonstrating the driving eﬀect
of DTL (Figure S3, Supporting Information).
2.2. Clustered-Polysulﬁdes Species in CPE
In lithium–sulfur batteries, lithium polysulﬁde intermediates
possess important roles in the sulfur reduction reaction. As
shown in Figure 2a, lithium polysulﬁdes tend to exist as a
single neutral molecule in the 0DTL electrolyte (conventional
ether electrolyte, noted as CE). In this work, by the driving
eﬀect of DTL, the polysulﬁdes were allowed to aggregate in
CPE and form cluster species with the mediation of TFSI−. As
a result, polysulﬁdes no longer exist as a free molecule, but
form a cluster state molecule aggregated in CPE, as shown in
Figure 2b,c.
Machine learning potentials for molecular dynamics (MLMD)
simulation were performed to reveal the CPE mechanism. Re-
cently, Liu et al. revealed that Li2S4 is the key species that deter-
mines the kinetics of sulfur reduction reaction.[19] So the short-
chain Li2S4 was chosen as the typical polysulﬁde species because
it is the only liquid phase species for the liquid-solid conversion
process (Li2S4→Li2S) and largely determines the kinetics of sul-
fur reduction reaction. Snapshots of the two electrolytes with
Li2S4 molecules are shown in Figure 2d,e. In CE, the Li2S4 ex-
ists as typical neutral molecules. In contrast, the Li2S4 molecules
in CPE are aggregated to form cluster-state species. Figures S4
and S5, (Supporting Information) show the localized simulated
snapshots of CE and CPE, demonstrating the conversion of active
species from single-molecule Li2S4 to clustered Li2S4 species in
60DTL electrolyte. In addition, the stretching central S─S bond
of Li2S4 in CPE species was observed (from 1.95–2.45 to 2.45–
2.85 Å), suggesting that the clustered polysulﬁde species have a
stronger reactivity than polysulﬁdes (Figure S5, Supporting In-
formation).
A sharp peak of Li (Li2S4)─O (TFSI) appeared within the sol-
vent sheath layer at 2.5Å in CPE (Figure 2f). The coordination
number of Li (Li2S4)─O (TFSI) in the CPE electrolyte was in-
creased from 0 to 0.72, indicating that polysulﬁdes are agglomer-
ated under the mediation of TFSI−. In contrast, the radial distri-
bution plots around Li atom of Li2S4 molecules indicate that only
DME or THF molecules are present within the solvated sheath of
Li2S4 at 2.5 Å in CE (Figure 2g). Also, as shown in Figure S6 (Sup-
porting Information), the radial distribution plots around Li+ of
LiTFSI molecules reveal a signiﬁcantly enhanced ship peak of
Li(TFSI)-S(Li2S4) in CPE.
The 19F and 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spec-
tra were performed to verify the formation of cluster species.
20 mmol L−1 Li2S4 or Li2S6 was added to the CE and CPE elec-
trolyte with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI. As shown in Figure 2h,i, the 19F
peak of CE electrolyte was hardly shifted after adding lithium
polysulﬁdes, which suggests that polysulﬁde species are present
as free molecules and have not entered the solvated sheath of
TFSI−. In contrast, the 19F peak of CPE exhibits a pronounced
increased chemical shift (≈0.2 ppm), which directly evidences
the formation of a cluster state between polysulﬁdes and TFSI−.

and polysulﬁdes during the formation of cluster state molecules:
Li+ forms a 3D structure by bridging the oxygen atoms of TFSI−
and the sulfur atoms of the polysulﬁdes, and this rigid coordi-

Potentiostatic-intermittent titration technique (PITT) was fur-
ther performed to reveal the Li2S deposition behavior.[21] As
shown in Figure 3b,c, the PITT curves of the Li─Li2S8 cells ap-
pear an obvious broad peak with a gradual decrease of voltage,
which corresponds to the deposition process of Li2S4 to Li2S. The
Li2S nucleation potential was elevated from 2.04 to 2.08 V in the
CPE electrolyte. The deposition kinetic behavior was revealed by
quantitative peak ﬁtting to the current-time transient curves. The
deposition process of Li2S can be categorized into four classical
models: 2D progressive nucleation (2DP), 2D instantaneous nu-
cleation (2DI), 3D progressive nucleation (3DP), and 3D instanta-
neous nucleation (3DI). The deposition of polysulﬁdes to lithium
sulﬁde is divided into nucleation and growth. The nucleation pro-
cess follows below:
N = N0 (1 −exp (−At))
(1)
where N represents the nuclei density on a certain substrate, t is
the given time, N0 represents the density of available nucleation
sites, and A is the nucleation rate. When the nucleation rate A is
large, Li2S nuclei are formed rapidly in a short period, which is
called instantaneous nucleation. When A is small, the nucleation
density increases linearly with time, which is called progressive
nucleation. The growth process of Li2S is controlled by the diﬀu-
sion of active species, which can be divided into 2D deposition
and 3D deposition according to the diﬀerent control steps.[22] As
shown in Figure 3d,e, the Li2S deposition is well matched with
the 3DP model in the CPE electrolyte, while it is situated between
the 2DP and 2DI models in the CE electrolyte. The 3D deposition
model allows cells to release higher capacity and avoids prema-
ture end of discharge process caused by insulating Li2S covering
the electrode surface.[23] Meanwhile, the Li2S deposition process
in diﬀerent electrolytes was compared by chronoamperometry
experiment at 2.08 V. As shown in Figure 3f, the deposition pro-
cess in 0DTL electrolyte could not proceed due to the large over-
potential. With the increasing DTL content, the deposition peak
appeared and gradually advanced, indicating that the Li2S depo-
sition kinetics gradually accelerated. The highest deposition ca-
pacity of Li2S (434 mAh g−1) was observed in 60DTL electrolytes,
which is consistent with the results of CV experiments.
The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of Li2S de-
posited on carbon paper are shown in Figure 3g–j. In 0DTL elec-
trolytes, small lithium sulﬁde particles can be observed in the
middle of discharge, and moss-like lithium sulﬁde can also be
observed in some areas, which corresponds to the 2D deposition
mode of lithium sulﬁde (Figure 3i). In 60DTL electrolytes, due to
the change of deposition mode (2D to 3D), the lithium sulﬁde can
grow along the 3D direction, forming large-sized lithium sulﬁde
in the form of liquid droplets (Figure 3g). Ultimately, a 3D lithium
sulﬁde deposition morphology can be realized in a 60DTL elec-
trolyte (Figure 3h), while the mossy lithium sulﬁde in a 0DTL
electrolyte grows into a ﬁlm-like lithium sulﬁde and completely
covers the conductive electrode (Figure 3j). Figure S12 (Support-
ing Information) also shows the Li2S deposition morphology for
electrolytes with diﬀerent DTL contents. Consequently, the de-
position morphology of lithium sulﬁde can be modulated by the
clustered polysulﬁdes.
2.4. Sulfur Redox Reaction Mediated by CPE
The charge/discharge curves of Li─S cells in 0DTL and 60DTL
electrolytes are shown in Figure 4a. The behavior of a charge-
discharge process is diﬀerent, especially in the initial stage. In
Li─S batteries, the ﬁrst discharge plateau corresponds to the con-
version of sulfur to long-chain polysulﬁdes, while the second dis-
charge plateau corresponds to the conversion of polysulﬁdes to
lithium sulﬁde. The tilted region between the ﬁrst and second
plateaus corresponds to the conversion of dissolved long-chain
polysulﬁdes to short-chain polysulﬁdes. The decrease in poly-
sulﬁde solubility and the change in the state of reactive species
caused the sulfur reaction to intermediate between the liquid
and “quasi-solid” reactions. Therefore, the voltage of the ﬁrst dis-
charge plateau decreases from 2.35 to 2.2 V in 60DTL electrolyte,
accompanied by a signiﬁcant decrease in the capacity of the tilted
region. In addition, signiﬁcantly higher capacity and increased
voltage of the second platform in 60DTL electrolyte indicate that
the reduction process from Li2S4 to Li2S is eﬀectively promoted.
During the charge process, two voltage platforms exist in 0DTL
electrolyte, corresponding to the conversion of lithium sulﬁde
to polysulﬁdes and polysulﬁdes to sulfur. In contrast, only one

species also change the reaction mechanism of the charging pro-

trolyte was increased from 1002 to 1371 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, and
the overpotential was reduced from 173 to 116 mV. It is notewor-
thy that the coulombic eﬃciency of cells with 60DTL electrolyte
is increased from 76.3% to 99.6% due to the eﬀective inhibition
of the shuttle eﬀect.

2.5. Electrochemical Performance of Li─S Batteries
The altered electrode process regulated by the CPE mechanism
results in a remarkable improvement in the electrochemical
performance of lithium–sulfur batteries. The rate performance
of Li─S cells with diﬀerent electrolytes is shown in Figure 5a,
and the corresponding coulombic eﬃciency of cells is shown in
Figure S18, Supporting Information. The batteries with 60DTL
electrolytes have the best rate performance. The speciﬁc capac-
ities of cells with 60DTL were 1371, 1164, 1078, 998, 914, 841,
and 702 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 5 C, respectively.
In comparison, cells with 0DTL can only realize capacities of
1116, 787, 707, 624, 569, 510, and 302 mAh g−1, respectively.
When the current density was changed from a high rate (5 C)
back to a low rate (0.1 C), cells with 0DTL failed to operate due
to the severe shuttle eﬀect caused by accumulated lithium poly-
sulﬁdes. Charge–discharge curves of Li─S cells at diﬀerent rates
are shown in Figure 5b,c. The cells with 60DTL can maintain two
discharge plateaus of charge-discharge curves well even at high
current densities of 5 C. On the other hand, the polarization of
cells with 0DTL rose rapidly with the increase of current density.
The rate performance of DOL/DME electrolyte (DOL/DME
= 1/1, v/v) was also compared with our electrolyte. Neverthe-
less, cells with 60DTL electrolytes still have higher speciﬁc ca-
pacities (Figure S19a,b, Supporting Information). The polar-
izations of cells with two electrolytes at diﬀerent rates are
shown in Figure S19c (Supporting Information), the smaller
slope of ﬁtted lines indicates that cells with CPE electrolytes
have faster reaction kinetics than cells with DOL/DME elec-
trolytes. The cycling performance of the DOL/DME electrolyte
and 60DTL electrolyte is shown in Figure S20 (Supporting
Information). In electrolyte without LiNO3, DOL/DME elec-
trolyte only has a Coulombic eﬃciency of 76% and a spe-
ciﬁc capacity of 230 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles. In contrast,
60DTL electrolytes exhibit signiﬁcantly increased discharge ca-
pacity with superior cycling stability. The cycling performance
of DOL/DME electrolyte improved after the addition of LiNO3,
and the 60DTL electrolyte still showed better electrochemical
performance.
The cycling performance of Li─S cells at 1C is shown in
Figure 5d. Cells using 0DTL electrolytes exhibit rapid capacity de-
cay due to the severe shuttle eﬀect. In contrast, cells with 60DTL
electrolytes show signiﬁcantly improved cycling stability, with a
capacity of 640 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles. The capacity reten-
tion of cells with 60DTL and 0DTL after 200 cycles at 1 C was
90.6% and 12.2%, respectively. Charge–discharge curves of cells
with 60DTL overlapped well for 200 cycles at 1 C (Figure 5e),
while the second discharge plateau of cells with 0DTL shortens
rapidly and speciﬁc capacity decreases rapidly as cycling proceeds
(Figure 5f). Electrochemical impedances of cells at 0.5 C for dif-
ferent cycles are shown in Figure 5g,h. The impedance of cells
with 0DTL electrolytes increased signiﬁcantly after 200 cycles,
showing that lithium polysulﬁde in the electrolyte was accumu-
lating due to severe shuttle eﬀect and sluggish reaction kinetics.
In contrast, cells with 60DTL electrolytes have lower impedance
and remain stable. Nyquist plots and the corresponding equiva-
lent circuits of Li─S cells at diﬀerent depths of discharge (DOD)
are shown in Figures S21 and S22 (Supporting Information).
Cells with 60 DTL electrolytes exhibit consistently smaller charge
transfer impedance (Rct) than cells with 0DTL electrolytes during
discharge, suggesting faster reaction kinetics of sulfur reduction.
Meanwhile, the interfacial impedance (RSEI) of cells with 0DTL
increases rapidly in the late stage of discharge (>65% DOD),
which is caused by the insulating Li2S covering the conductive
cathode. 60DTL electrolyte can regulate the 3D deposition of Li2S
to prevent the coverage of discharge product, and thus Li─S cells
always keep a small RSEI throughout the discharge process. We
further compared the cycling performance of Li─S batteries with
LiNO3 at 1 C. As shown in Figure S23 (Supporting Information),
the electrolyte with DTL is well-compatible with LiNO3 additives,
and the 60DTL electrolyte with LiNO3 has a higher capacity as
well as better cycle stability compared to 0DTL electrolyte with
LiNO3. The cell with 60DTL electrolyte still has a capacity of
639 mAh g−1 after 600 cycles at 1 C, and the capacity degrada-
tion is reduced from 50.65% to 29.86%. Compared to the other
reported electrolytes for lithium–sulfur batteries, our CPE elec-
trolyte achieves favorable reaction kinetics with substantially re-
duced polysulﬁde solubility (Figure 5i). Therefore, even in the ab-
sence of LiNO3, the Li─S batteries can achieve good Coulombic
eﬃciency, higher discharge capacity, and longer cycle life (Table
S4,5, Supporting Information).

2.6. Stabilization of Lithium Anode on CPE Electrolyte
DTL, as a non-coordinated solvent with a low DN value and
dielectric constant, has excellent chemical stability for lithium
metal. As shown in Figure S26 (Supporting Information), the sur-
face of lithium metal remains bright and consistent with fresh
lithium ﬂakes after immersing in DTL pure solvent for 1 month,
indicating that lithium metal does not have side reactions with
DTL.

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, this work demonstrated a clustered polysulﬁde
encapsulation mechanism through a novel electrolyte composed
of 1,2-dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME).
Both computational and experimental results elucidate the ag-
gregation behavior of polysulﬁdes in 60DTL electrolytes under
the regulation of TFSI−. The clustered polysulﬁdes, as new active
species, modulate the reaction mechanism of lithium–sulfur bat-
teries, enhancing the lithium sulﬁde deposition process and en-
abling 3D lithium sulﬁde deposition. Furthermore, the solubility
of polysulﬁdes is ﬁnely regulated, leading to signiﬁcant suppres-
sion of the shuttle eﬀect and eﬀective stabilization of the lithium
anode. As a result, the CPE electrolyte exhibits improved electro-
chemical performance than conventional DOL/DME-based elec-
trolytes, and the rate performance and cycling stability of the
battery are signiﬁcantly improved, with smooth operation under
lean electrolyte conditions. This work not only presents an alter-
native polysulﬁde-conversion mechanism via a new electrolyte
system with excellent reaction kinetics and anode stability for
Li─S batteries but also elucidates the critical role of polysulﬁde
dissolution behavior in governing sulfur redox reactions. These
ﬁndings provide valuable insights into the complex sulfur con-
version process and oﬀer a signiﬁcant advancement in the un-
derstanding of Li─S battery chemistry.
