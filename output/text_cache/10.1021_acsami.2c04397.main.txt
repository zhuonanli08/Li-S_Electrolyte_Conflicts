ABSTRACT: The passivation caused by the deposition of the
insulating discharge final product, lithium sulfide (Li2S), leads to
the instability of the cycle and the rapid capacity fading of lithium−
sulfur batteries (LSBs), which restricts the development of LSBs.
This paper proposes the employment of trifluoroacetamide (TFA)
as an electrolyte additive to alleviate the passivation by increasing
the solubility of Li2S. The solubilization effect of TFA on Li2S is
attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonds and O−Li bonds.
Li2S in the TFA-based electrolyte exhibits a flower-like 3D
deposition behavior, which further alleviates the surface passivation of the electrode and impels conversion kinetics. In addition, the
LiF-rich solid electrolyte interface layer can effectively defend the Li metal anode and suppress the growth of Li dendrites.
Accordingly, the discharge capacity of the TFA-based battery remains at an excellent 681.2 mA h g−1 after 400 cycles with a
Coulombic efficiency of 99% at 0.5 C. After the battery stabilizes, the capacity decay is only 0.036% per cycle. Under harsh
conditions, such as high rates (2 C) and high sulfur loadings (5.2 mg cm−2) with lean electrolytes and elevated temperatures (60
°C), TFA-containing batteries exhibited more durable and stable cycling. This paper provides new insights into solving practical
problems and gives an impetus in cycle stability for LSBs.
KEYWORDS: trifluoroacetamide, lithium−sulfur battery, solubility, deposition behavior, SEI
1. INTRODUCTION
Benefiting from a two-electron reaction pathway of elemental
sulfur, lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) achieve ultrahigh
theoretical specific capacity (1672 mA h g−1) and energy
density (2600 W h kg−1).1−3 Combined with resourcefully
abundant and environmentally friendly raw materials, LSBs are
considered to be one of the most promising candidates for
next-generation secondary batteries.4
However, there are still many factors hindering the progress
of LSBs toward commercialization. One of the culprits is the
notorious shuttle effect of polysulfides. The constant shuttling
back and forth of polysulfides provokes the continuous
consumption of active materials and metallic lithium, which
leads to the reduction of Coulombic efficiency and shortening
of lifetimes.5 The corrosion of the lithium metal anode and the
growth of lithium dendrites are similarly responsible for the
poor cycling stability.6 Apart from these, the usually less-
concerning electrode passivation is also one pivotal trouble
that cannot be ignored. Due to the negligible solubility in
conventional ether-based electrolytes, lithium sulfides (Li2S)
are immediately deposited on the conductive substrate surface
once formed. The insulating deposited layer wraps the
conductive substrate tightly, triggering the passivation
phenomenon on the surface. The passivation layer slows
down the conversion to Li2S by occluding the active sites and
reducing the conductivity of the electrode. The capacity
contributed by the deposition process of the sulfur species
accounts for 75% of the total capacity wherein its significance
in improving the performance of LSBs is self-evident.7
Electrochemical deposition involves the solubility of Li2S
and its deposition behavior, and among which, the deposition
behavior is affected by solubility. The use of high Gutmann
donor number (DN)8 or dielectric constant9 electrolytes is an
effective strategy to enhance solubility, creating an environ-
ment that promotes interactions with electrophilic cations and
implying enhanced solubility of polysulfides. Furthermore,
variation in the Li2S deposition morphology was reported in
the dissolution of DNs solvents.10 In this regard, Pan et al.11
revealed that Li2S grows in the manner of a thin film in low
DN solvents, while flower-like patterns are observed in
medium- and high-DN solvents. Unfortunately, their compat-
ibility with Li anodes is a severe drawback due to the inherent
high activity of high-DN solvents.12 Progressively, Chu et al.13
replaced the solvent with high-DN lithium salts to reduce the
side reactions of lithium anodes. Compared to solvents, lithium

are still not fully comparable to conventional electrolytes
(LiTFSI in DOL/DME). On the other hand, the interaction
with Li2S can be exploited to improve the solubility of the
electrolyte. A eutectic electrolyte based on ε-caprolactam and
acetamide solvents was developed to dissolve all polysulfides
and Li2S.14 The hydrogen in the amide group acts as a
hydrogen-bond donor, generating an intermolecular hydrogen

tion of the electrodes. The hydrogen-bonding effect of the N−
H structure in the ammonium salt greatly improves the
solubility of Li2S. The pioneering investigations suggest that
hydrogen bonding plays an important role in promoting the
ability of the electrolyte to dissolve Li2S. The limitations of the
methods reported above should also be recognized. The
viscosity of the organic amide solvent is too high, and the
inorganic NH4NO3 additive has been studied in the DMSO
system. Therefore, the topic of interest is optimizing the
electrolyte formulation to improve the solubility of Li2S in the
electrolyte without greatly affecting the properties of the
electrolyte.
In this contribution, considering that the solvation of Li+ in
the electrolyte is mainly dominated by the oxygen atoms of the
solvent, the bonds between lithium and oxygen and the
hydrogen bonds cooperate to participate in the interaction
with Li2S. At the same time, the increase in solubility may
bring about side reactions to the lithium anode. This occurs to
introduce fluorine atoms to stabilize the lithium anode. The
fluoroalkyl moieties such as trifluoromethyl are important
sources in deriving fluoride.6,17 Consequently, the authors
proposed trifluoroacetamide (TFA) as an electrolyte additive
to relieve the electrode passivation and guard the lithium
anode. The hydrogen atoms in the TFA molecule interact with
the sulfur atoms of Li2S to form intermolecular hydrogen
bonds (S−H), and the oxygen atoms interact with the lithium
atoms of Li2S to form Li−O bonds, which promotes the
dissolution of Li2S in the TFA electrolyte. In addition, the TFA
electrolyte induces the 3D deposition behavior of Li2S. The
LiF-abundant solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer derived
from TFA suppresses the growth of Li dendrites while
reducing the corrosion of Li anodes by polysulfides. Based
on the above merits, the battery with the TFA additive behaves
with a steady cycling performance, satisfactory Coulombic
efficiency, and boosted kinetics in comparison to the blank
electrolyte. Even so, regardless of the high rate (2 C), high
sulfur loading (5.2 mg cm−2), or elevated temperature (60 °C),
the TFA-based cells show more stable cycling, a higher
Coulombic efficiency, and extended cycle life.

sulfur−carbon composite materials. The composite cathode was

in the dispersant N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) with a homoge-
nizer for vigorous stirring and rapid dispersion and then coated on the
current collector with a blade and transferred to a vacuum oven to dry

The formulation of the blank electrolyte is a binary solvent system

2.2. Electrochemical Measurements. Lithium−sulfur coin
batteries (CR2025) were assembled by combining the composite
cathode, metal lithium anode, and polypropylene separator filled with
the electrolyte with an electrolyte−sulfur ratio of 20 μL mg−1 (E/S =
20). Under constant-temperature (25 °C) and high-temperature (60
°C) conditions, the assembled lithium−sulfur batteries were tested in
the voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V (vs Li/Li+) through a LAND
CT2001A test system for cycle performance with approximately 1.1
and 5.2 mg cm−2 sulfur loading. The rate performance of the battery
was recorded in that the lithium−sulfur batteries were cycled in 5
cycles at rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C, and then the rate is restored
to 0.2 C for cycling. Lithium−polysulfide cells were assembled by
coupling carbon paper with the lithium anode where 40 μL of the
Li2S8 catholyte was injected for the control. The above cells were
galvanostatically discharged to 2.12 V and then potentiostatically
discharged at 2.11 V until the current was less than 10−2 mA. The
cyclic voltammetry curve was collected by cyclic voltammetry (CV)
with a scanning speed of 0.05 mV s−1 and a voltage range of 1.5−3 V.
The Li+ diffusion was compared by CV curves at different scanning
rates based on the Randles−Sevcik equation. Electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was done by applying an interference
voltage with an amplitude of 5 mV in the frequency range of 105 to
10−2 Hz under an open-circuit potential. The above CV and EIS data
were measured by an electrochemical workstation (Solartron 1470E)
at room temperature.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Dissolution and Deposition Behavior of Li2S. In
order to research the influence of the introduction of the TFA
additive for the solubility of Li2S in the ether-based electrolyte,
equal amounts of Li2S powder were added to the same volume
of the blank electrolyte and TFA-containing electrolyte that are
colorless and transparent solutions (Figure S1). With Li2S
introduced in these electrolytes, it can be observed from Figure
1a that the blank electrolyte presented a dark yellow and turbid
suspension indicating its almost negligible solubility, while the
TFA electrolyte became yellow and was still clearly transparent
revealing that TFA can boost the solubility of Li2S in the ether
electrolyte. Aiming at the purpose of studying the interaction
between TFA and Li2S and analyzing the mechanism of TFA
solubilization, NMR spectroscopy was further conducted
through 1H and 7Li spectra as exhibited in Figure 1b,c. For
the 1H NMR spectrum, the spectrum of pure TFA displays a
residual solvent peak at a chemical shift of 2.5 ppm, water peak
with a chemical shift of 3.3 ppm, and split doublet located at a
chemical shift of 8−9 ppm assigned to the N−H bond in the
primary amine group of TFA. With the addition of Li2S, the
spectrum of the mixture shows the weakness of the N−H bond
in which, at the same time, one new resonance peak appears at
a chemical shift of 2.2 ppm derived from the formation of the
S−H bond.14 These results present that the hydrogen atoms in
the amide group (−NH2) are involved in forming
intermolecular hydrogen bonds with S2−. With regard to the
7Li NMR spectrum, the spectrum of Li2S shows only one peak
that belongs to the Li−S bond at a chemical shift of 2 ppm.
When TFA was mixed with Li2S, this peak migrates in the
negative direction15 and a new NMR resonance peak emerges
at 1.5 ppm, which is attributed to the formation of the O−Li
bond between the oxygen atoms in the amide group and the
lithium atoms in Li2S. What is more, compared with DME and
TFA in Raman spectroscopy shown in Figure 1d, the peaks
marked by dashed lines appear, corresponding to the O−Li
bond19 and S−H bond.20 Similarly, this reveals the interaction
between TFA and Li2S to promote the solubility of the
electrolyte. Also, the absorption peak of the S2−species can be
observed in Figure S2 after dissolving Li2S in the TFA
electrolyte, while no peak appears in the one without Li2S.
According to the above, it can therefore be concluded on the
mechanism of the interaction between TFA and Li2S that one
is the intermolecular hydrogen bond formed between the
sulfur atom in Li2S and the primary amine group in TFA. On
the other hand, the O−Li bond is formed between the lithium
atom in Li2S and the oxygen atom in TFA, which promotes the
dissolution of Li2S in the TFA electrolyte from two aspects.
The solubilization of the additive leads to increased
solubility that affects the deposition behavior of Li2S reduced
by short-chain polysulfides. Hence, a chronoamperometry
technique was performed to study the electrochemical kinetics
and morphology of lithium sulfide deposition with the blank
electrolyte and TFA electrolyte. From the response current−
time curves in Figure 2a,d, the response current decreased in
the initial stage, which was attributed to the Faraday reduction
reaction of the remaining long-chain polysulfide. Next, as the
short-chain polysulfide is reduced, lithium sulfide begins to
nucleate and grow on the surface of carbon paper, causing the
response current increase. The area of electrochemical activity
is enhanced along with the increase in the number of nuclei
and nuclei growth, resulting in the rise of the response current
until the maximum current value (Im) is reached. Sub-
sequently, the current begins to drop because of the overlap
of adjacent nuclei or hemispherical diffusion regions after the
peak.21 The specific time (tm) corresponding to the maximum
response current can help us evaluate the surface passivation
speed, namely, the lower the value, the faster the passivation
rate. The time of the TFA electrolyte is higher than that of the
blank electrolyte, indicating that this additive can effectively
alleviate the passivation of the electrode surface.13,22 The area
of curves is divided into three regions that are the reduction of
Li2S8, precipitation of Li2S, and reduction of Li2S6.23 The
precipitation capacity of the electrolyte with TFA is heightened
as calculated by integrating the curve (Figure S3), which
reveals the promotion of the conversion of short-chain
polysulfides to lithium sulfide. The dimensionless current (I/
Im)−time (t/tm) profiles fitted in the corresponding
chronoamperometry curves are drafted as Figure 2b,e,
comparing four classical models of electrochemical deposition
that consist of 2D instantaneous (2DI) and 2D progressive
(2DP) nucleation of Bewick, Fleischmann, and Thirsk (BFT)
models24 and 3D instantaneous (3DI) and 3D progressive
(3DP) nucleation of the Scharifker and Hills (SH)
model21,25,26 (Tables S1 and S2). In the process of progressive
nucleation, the growth of nuclei is controlled by migration and
transmission in the electrolyte. The curve is approximately
close to the 2DP model in the blank electrolyte owing to the
faint solubility of Li2S in the ether-based electrolyte that
hinders the diffusion and movement of S2−relying heavily on
the conductive substrate by a surface-mediated routine. In
sharp contrast, with the employment of the TFA additive, the
fitting curve shifts to the 3DP model. The higher solubility is
conducive to the diffusion of dissolved lithium sulfide in the
bulk electrolyte rather than being deposited immediately once
nucleated. At the same time, lithium sulfide is more inclined to
deposit in 3D dimensions on the surface of other lithium
sulfide aggregates,27 thereby inducing 3D growth of lithium
sulfide by a diffusion-controlled pathway. The SEM images of
carbon paper after galvanostatical discharge are provided in
Figure 2c,f. When lithium sulfide is deposited from the blank
electrolyte, the Li2S film is formed on the surface compared to
the pristine carbon paper (Figure S4). The insulation product
causes severe electrode passivation, blocking the contact of
polysulfide with the reaction site and inhibiting the conversion
of polysulfide. However, flower-like depositions were observed
under the effect of the TFA additive. This flower-like 3D
deposition behavior can extremely restrain the passivation of
the electrode surface, promote the conversion of polysulfides
to lithium sulfide, and improve the utilization of active
materials (Figure 2g).

characteristic cathodic peaks during the negative scanning

electrolyte through the formation of hydrogen bonds and O−
Li bonds with TFA so that part of the liquid−solid reaction is
transformed into a liquid−liquid conversion, thereby accel-

rate. According to the Randles−Sevcik equation that is Ip =
(2.65 × 105)n3/2AD1/2Cv1/2, the diffusion coefficient of Li+ is
improved in Li2S generation by estimating the curve slope with
two kinds of electrolytes.32
Long-term examination was carried out at various rates of
0.5 and 2 C (activation at 0.1 C) for verifying the impact of
TFA as an electrolyte additive on the cycle performance of
LSBs whose results are shown in Figure 3d,e. When cycling at a
rate of 0.5 C, the battery with 1.0 wt % TFA conveys an initial
discharge capacity of 1101.2 mA h g−1 and the capacity
remains 681.2 mA h g−1 after 400 cycles. Especially, from the
50th to the end, the capacity decays 0.036% per cycle
indicating superior cycle stability. As for the blank cell, the
initial specific capacity is 1050 mA h g−1, and the battery
capacity then drops rapidly to 494.2 mA h g−1 at the 400th
cycle. As the cycle rate is raised to 2 C (Figure 3f), the battery
without TFA decays to below 50% in only cycling 300 times.
In strong contrast, the TFA cell retains stability during 400
cycles of which the discharge capacity is 436.3 mA h g−1. This
additive is beneficial in improving cycle stability and extending
battery lifespan. In addition, the Coulombic efficiency of the
battery using the blank electrolyte is 98% for 400 cycles at 0.5
C, while that containing TFA increases to 99% after the same
number of cycles. Similarly, the batteries at 2 C behave with a
Coulombic efficiency of 84% at 300 cycles in the blank
electrolyte and 94% at 400 cycles with the TFA electrolyte.
The above conclude that the introduction of the TFA additive
can significantly improve the Coulombic efficiency of LSBs.

Figure S8 shows the charge−discharge profiles of lithium−
sulfur cells at different cycles with two electrolytes. During the
discharge process, two obvious discharge platforms appear at
voltages of 2.3 and 2.0 V, which correspond to the reduction of
elemental sulfur to polysulfides and the reduction of
polysulfides to Li2S2/Li2S, respectively. For the charging
process, the curves express a slope corresponding to the
oxidation process of Li2S and the formation process of
elemental sulfur.33 The platform, especially at the lower
potential, of the control group is shortened, obviously
indicating the depletion of active materials and hindrance of
polysulfide conversion during the discharge process compared
with the TFA group. At the same time, during the charging
process, the battery containing TFA showed a lower Li2S
oxidation overpotential, illustrating that the liquid Li2S
dissolved in the electrolyte is easier to be oxidized than the
solid state since the liquid Li2S has a smaller activation energy
and reduced the energy barrier of the Li2S oxidation reaction.34
To further explore the effect of the TFA additive on the
conversion of polysulfides to Li2S, the ratio of the platform at
the lower potential to the higher potential is calculated to
evaluate the ability of the conversion of polysulfides to Li2S in
that of theoretical value is 3.0 approximately.35 The values of
the initial cycle are 2.23 and 2.09 for TFA and the blank cells
and then these decrease to 2.06 and 1.94 at the 400th cycle.
However, the value of the TFA group is higher than that of the
blank group, which means that the solubilization effect of TFA
on Li2S contributes to the promotion of polysulfide reduction
resulting from reducing the deposition of non-conductive
products so that the polysulfides can undergo a reduction
reaction at the exposed carbon sites. In addition, to meet the
requirement of actual application, taking into account the high
sulfur loading (>5 mg cm−2) and low electrolyte−sulfur ratio
(E/S = 10), the electrochemical performance of cells with two
electrolytes is tested at 0.2 C as given in Figure 3g. The battery
with the blank electrolyte delivers a tendency for rapid decay
after 100 cycles with a specific capacity of 609 mA h g−1. More
notably, the battery containing TFA maintains a relatively
stable cycle for 150 times and the specific capacity of the 150th
cycle is 668.7 mA h g−1. From the perspective of the charge−
discharge profiles (Figure S9), the TFA battery presents a
smaller polarization voltage and lower oxidation potential.
Even when the ambient temperature is increased to 60 °C, the
TFA group has a more sluggish attenuation, more stable cycle
performance, and longer times of cycles (Figure S10). Under
the above harsh conditions, the TFA additive still plays a
positive role in improving the electrochemical performance of
the battery and prolonging the battery life.
LSBs assembled from the TFA electrolyte and blank
electrolyte were operated at different rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1,
and 2 C to study the effect of TFA as an electrolyte additive on
the rate performance. In Figure 4a, the battery containing TFA
has capacities of 1190, 933, 848.7, 836.1, and 797.5 mA h g−1,
exceeding those of the blank electrolyte. With the rate restored
to 0.2 C, the battery with TFA can restore the same capacity
level as before and can continue to stabilize for 60 cycles with
almost no capacity degradation. However, the battery without
the additive did not reach the previous level and there is
significant capacity decay during the cycle. The above suggests
that TFA expedites the rate performance of batteries ascribed
to improved Li+ diffusion and reaction kinetics. Figure 4b
shows the electrochemical impedance spectra of batteries. Due
to the employment of TFA in the electrolyte, some physical
properties such as viscosity and ionic conductivity will be
altered resulting in a slight increase in the bulk impedance.
After 400 cycles, one more semicircle appears on the spectrum,
which corresponds to the impedance of the SEI film. To
further quantitatively analyze the variation of charge transfer
impedance and SEI film impedance, the map is fitted into an
equivalent circuit and the corresponding impedance values are
calculated and exhibited in Figure S11. From a numerical point
of view, the charge transfer resistance of TFA is lower than that
of the blank electrolyte for the reason that TFA reduces the
accumulation of insulating products on the cathode by
enhancing the solubility of Li2S, thereby promoting the

3.3. Rate Performance and the Effect of Additives on
the Sulfur Cathode. The solubilization effect of TFA on Li2S
in the ether-based electrolyte affects the surface morphology
and sulfur-containing species of the cathode. SEM and XPS are
used to study these. Figure 4c,d shows the SEM morphology of

cathode circulating in the blank electrolyte, causing severe
electrode passivation. As for the TFA electrolyte, the non-

indicating that TFA can alleviate the degree of passivation by
dissolving a certain part of Li2S. Figure 4e,f shows the XPS S
2p diagram of the cycled cathodes at the charged state with

cycling in the blank electrolyte mainly consist of Li2S at 160.3
and 161.5 eV,37 Li2S2 at 162.1 and 163.3 eV,36 elemental sulfur
at 164.138 and 165.3 eV, thiosulfate at 167.2 and 168.4 eV,39
and decomposition products of lithium salt at 169.2 and 170.4
eV.40 The characteristic peak of Li2S disappears, the peak of
Li2S2 weakens, and the peak of elemental sulfur increases
significantly after the circulation in the TFA electrolyte. This
may be because TFA improves the reaction kinetics by
dissolving Li2S to a certain degree, which makes Li2S more
susceptible to oxidation to form polysulfides conducive to the
generation of elemental sulfur.
3.4. Impact of the Additive on the Lithium−Metal
Anode. The lithium symmetrical battery whose results are
shown in Figure 5a is assembled using two electrolytes and
conducted at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and capacity
density of 0.5 mA h cm−2. The battery with the blank
electrolyte exhibits an overpotential of 20.7 mV during the
electrochemical plating and striping process with cycling for
900 h. Strongly in contrast, the battery with added TFA can
cycle stably for up to 1600 h with less overpotential. The
reversibility of the plating and striping procedure is contributed
by the SEI layer derived from TFA with a robust property.
After circulating of LSBs, the lithium anode was taken out to
explore the influence of TFA additives on the morphology by
SEM in Figure 5b,c. In the blank electrolyte, it can be observed
that the surface of the lithium anode is highly rough and the
mossy lithium is stacked together. A large number of gullies are
seen on the surface, accompanied by the growth of a large
number of needle-like deposits indicating severe corrosion and
a side reaction of the lithium anode. By contrast, as for the
TFA electrolyte, the surface of the lithium anode is extremely
smooth and dense and needle-like precipitation almost
disappears after cycling. Figure S12 shows an EDS diagram
of the cycled lithium anode of the two electrolytes. Compared
with the blank electrolyte, the sulfur content of the TFA
electrolyte is apparently reduced and the fluorine content is
slightly mounted. The drop in the sulfur content declares that
the side reaction between polysulfide and metallic lithium is
suppressed and the LiF-rich SEI film derived from TFA can
effectively block the spread of polysulfide from the lithium
anode to improve the Coulombic efficiency of batteries and
lengthen the service life of the lithium anode. In addition, XPS

with the atomic content of etching levels (Figure S13), the
above results all reflect the positive effect of the SEI layer on
suppressing side reactions, which is a key factor for the TFA
additive to stabilize the lithium anode, improve the Coulombic
efficiency, and prolong the lifetime.
Figure 6 shows the schematic illustration of the lithium−
sulfur battery with the TFA electrolyte. With the reduction of
polysulfides, the generated Li2S interacts with TFA to increase
its solubility in the electrolyte. The functional group that works
is the amide group in which H atoms tend to form
intermolecular hydrogen bonds with S atoms in Li2S and
O−Li bonds are formed by coupling O atoms and Li atoms.
Li2S dispersed in the electrolyte is prone to deposition on the
bulk phase, inducing a 3D deposition behavior. The increased
solubility collaborated with the altered deposition behavior
effectively alleviates the phenomenon of surface passivation.
The participation of exposed active sites significantly improves
the availability of active species as well as the reaction kinetics.
In addition, TFA is beneficial for the formation of the LiF-rich
SEI layer, blocking polysulfide attack and side reactions. The
high surface of LiF enables a uniform distribution of Li+ and
inhibits the growth of Li dendrites.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, this paper provides an effective strategy to
alleviate passivation accompanying stabilization of the metallic
lithium anode through trifluoroacetamide (TFA) as an
electrolyte additive. The solubilization effect of TFA on Li2S
is due to the amide functional group consisting of
intermolecular hydrogen bonds and O−Li bonds. The TFA
electrolyte has a certain regulatory effect on the behavior of
lithium sulfide, which is reflected in the 3D deposition
behavior. In addition, the LiF-rich SEI layer derived from
TFA can effectively reduce the side reactions of the Li anode,
induce uniform deposition of Li+, and suppress the growth of
Li dendrites. Based on the above merits, at 0.5 C, the battery
with 1.0 wt % TFA exhibited a 400-cycle capacity of 681.2 mA
h g−1 and Coulombic efficiency of 99%. The electrochemical
performances were investigated under 2 C high rate, high
sulfur loading, and high temperature conditions to verify the
above concepts. The introduction of a TFA electrolyte additive
is beneficial to improving the cycle stability of LSBs. This
article proposes a new strategy for those seeking to prolong the
cycle life of LSBs.
