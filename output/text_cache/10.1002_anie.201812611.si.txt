Experimental Details 
 
Preparation of the CPL/acetamide eutectic solvent. All reagents used in this experiment 
were analytic grade purity and were used as received. In an argon filled glove box, 1 mole of 
ε-caprolactam (99% purity, Sigma Aldrich) and 1 mole of acetamide (99% purity, Sigma Aldrich) 
were dried under argon in the glovebox overnight at 50°C before use. They were then mixed 
and stirred at 50°C for 30 minutes. After a clear mixture was obtained, it was rested in vacuum 
at 60 oC for 12 hours to remove any absorbed gas.  
 
Preparation of the Catholyteconv. 1.2 M of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide, (LiTFSI, 
99.95%, Sigma Aldrich) and 0.1 M of LiNO3 (reagent plus, Sigma Aldrich) were dissolved in 
1,3-dioxolane (1,3 DOL, ≧99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) and 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (1,2 DME, anhy-
drous, 99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) with 1:1 volume ratio overnight at 40°C. 
 
Preparation of the Catholyteeut. In an argon filled glove box, 0.4 M of Li2S (99.98%, Sigma 
Aldrich) was mixed with the CPL/acetamide eutectic solvent and the mixture was stirred at 
90°C for 24 hours until fully dissolved. 2.4 M of LiTFSI and 0.2 M of LiNO3 were dissolved in 
DOL and DME with 1:1 volume ratio overnight at 40°C. 0.4 M Li2S in eutectic solvent was 
mixed with the DOL/DME electrolyte in a volume ratio of 1:1 at 40°C for 2 hours. 
 
Preparation of the TiO2 coated carbon electrode. Carbon paper (thickness 0.19 mm, bulk 
density 0.44 g/cm3, Fuel Cell Earth) was first soaked in 28 wt% nitric acid (Fisher Scientific) at 
40°C overnight to increase the hydrophile of carbon paper. 0.075 M of titanium(Ⅳ) butoxide 
(97%, Sigma Aldrich) was diluted by isopropyl alcohol (99%, Pharmco-Aaper), and was gently 
stirred for 5 minutes. The carbon fiber paper was dipped into this solution and was dried at 
70°C for 2 hours. The dipping process was repeated four times in order to obtain a uniform 
layer. At last, the coated carbon fiber paper was heated at 600°C for 2 hours in a tube furnace 

<<PAGE 3>>
2 
 
(Lindberg Blue M) under the flow of nitrogen gas to increase the crystallinity of TiO2. 
 
Electrochemical Measurements. CR2032-type coin cells (MTI Corp.) were used during coin 
cell assembly. Lithium chip (99.9% purity, 0.6 mm thick, MTI Corporation) with a diameter of 
12.7 mm were used as the anode. It was first soaked in 0.5 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (v/v=1:1) 
containing 2 wt% LiNO3 for 1.5 hours to obtain a passivation layer on the lithium surface. Car-
bon paper with a diameter of 9 mm was used as the current collector. 20 µL of the Catholyteeut 
was added into the carbon paper and the weight was recorded. One porous polypropylene/pol-
yethylene separator (25 µm thick, Celgard) was sandwiched between the carbon paper and 
the lithium chip to avoid direct contact. Galvanostatic cycling electrochemistry measurement 
was performed on Landt battery tester (Lanhe, Wuhan) and electrochemical impedance spec-
troscopy test (EIS) was performed on Bio-logic VMP3. For all cells, the first cycle is charged 
and discharged at 0.1 C to allow the full activation of Li2S. The voltage range for cycling is 1.8-
2.7 V, and 1 C rate is 1650mA g-1. 
 
SEM and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy characterization for precipitation ex-
amination. The carbon current collector after cycling was washed with DOL / DME with 1:1 
volume ratio three times to dissolve the polysulfide as well as the eutectic solvent. Then the 
carbon fiber paper was dried in vacuum for 5 hours. SEM (Light Zeiss Microscope) was used 
to examine the surface of carbon paper and EDX (Bruker) was used for elemental analysis. 
The accelerated voltage of the electron gun for EDX is 15 kV. 
 
Raman Spectroscopy. The spontaneous Raman spectra were acquired by a laser confo-
cal Raman microscope (Xplora, Horiba Jobin Yvon). A 532-nm diode laser (12 mW, after 
the microscope objective) was used to excite the sample through a 50× air objective (MPlan 
N, 0.75 N.A., Olympus). The acquisition time was 5 s for each spectrum with the LabSpec 
6 software. 
 
Nuclear Magnetic Resonance. All samples were dissolved by dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 first. 1H 
NMR measurement on liquid samples was operated on a Bruker 300WB spectrometer. The 
identification of the peaks is realized by MestReNova. 
 
X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy. Surface analyses of the anodes at different depths were 
performed by a PHI 5500 XPS with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray excitation source (1,486.6 
eV) and a Spectrum 400 FT-IR spectrometer. All the sample transfer processes were pro-
tected by argon gas. 
 
Simulation details. We conduct ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations to investi-
gate the dynamics of Li2S solvated in CPL/acetamide and DOL/DME electrolytes. The inter-
actions between the valence electrons and the ionic cores are calculated by the projector 
augmented wave pseudopotentials (PAW) method.[1] The electron-electron exchange correla-
tions are described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof generalized-gradient-approximation 
(PBE-GGA)[2] with a plane wave energy cutoff of 400 eV and a Gaussian smearing width of 
0.05 eV. The Brillouin zone is sampled using the Γ-point only.[3] The convergence criterion for 

<<PAGE 4>>
3 
 
the electronic self-consistent loop in each dynamic step is set to 1×10-4 eV. All the simulations 
are performed under constant atom number, volume, and temperature (NVT) ensemble at 300 
K using a Nose-Hoover thermostat. Hydrogen masses are changed to tritium to allow the use 
of larger time steps (1 fs in this study). The Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) soft-
ware[4] was used to perform the AIMD simulations. 
 
Although the Li-S battery electrolyte usually contains a mixture of solvents along with Li-salts 
and other additives as in our experiments, we simplify the simulation by removing the presence 
of Li-salts and consider pure CPL/acetamide and DOL/DME mixtures. In the simulations, ion 
pairs of two Li+ ions and one S2- dianion are positioned in the middle of cubic simulation cells. 
Solvent molecules at a molar ratio of 1:1 are randomly packed around the ion pairs based on 
the solvent densities using PACKMOL software.[5] To match 0.4 M solubility in the experiments, 
the CPL/acetamide system has 30 solvent molecules in a 15.95×15.95×15.95 Å3 simulation 
box, and the DOL/DME system has 28 solvent molecules in a 15.93×15.93×15.93 Å3 cell. 
Before running the AIMD simulations, the initial configurations are relaxed by performing en-
ergy minimization in classical molecular dynamics using GROMACS.[6] An AIMD simulation 
includes 5 ps of equilibration and 10 ps of production run. Radial distribution functions (RDF) 
and simulation snapshots for solvation structure characterization are obtained using Visual 
Molecular Dynamics (VMD).[7] Bader charge analysis is applied to estimate the partial charges 
of different atoms in the solvent molecules. [8] 
 
Viscosity Measurement. The viscosity measurements were performed using a Modular Ad-
vanced Rheometer System (HAAKE MARS, Thermo Scientific USA) equipped with a 
cone/plate rotor C60/1Ti geometry (A-factor, 17,583 Pa/N m; M-factor, 57.39 rad/s) at 298 K. 
The ratio of dispersed phase viscosity over matrix phase viscosity was about unity at this 
temperature. The cone diameter was 63.564 mm. The gap size h is 0.5 mm. Under rotational 
tests with controlled shear rates from 1 to 1000 s−1 for 300 s, a constant value of the shear 
viscosity is observed for all the electrolyte samples, indicating Newtonian behavior. It has been 
verified here that the morphology does not change during the dynamic measurements. HAAKE 
Universal Temperature Controller (UTC) with a Peltier system was used for temperature sta-
bilization. The temperature uncertainty was ±0.1 K, and the uncertainty of the viscosity de-
termination was 0.3 %. Automated data analysis was performed using the HAAKE RheoWin 
software, with the assumption that testing fluid is purely viscous with no elasticity involved.  
 
Thermo-Gravimetric Analysis. All the samples were tested in O2 and heated at a rate of 
10 °C min-1 by TA Instruments Q500.

Supporting Note 1. AIMD and NMR analyses on Li2S dissolved in eutectic solvent 
 
Besides strong interactions between S2- and the amide hydrogen (N-H), and between 
Li+ and carbonyl oxygen in the eutectic solvent discussed in the main text, a stronger peak of 
S-O peak at 3.65 Å is also observed in the 0.4 M Li2S/eutectic solvent. This can be attributed 
to two factors: 1) Intimate interaction between carbonyl oxygen and Li in the eutectic solvent, 
which results in a strong Li-O peak. 2) In the short period of simulation, Li-S is not fully disso-
ciated, and thus the Li-S bond is approximately the same in both solvents. Therefore, a strong 
Li-O peak is translated into a strong and shorter S-O peak.1 However, the distance of S-O 
bond (3.65 Å) is much longer than S-H (2.15 Å), meaning that the S-H interaction is signifi-
cantly stronger and most likely the reason for the S dissociation. In summary, the stronger 
interaction between Li2S and the CPL/acetamide eutectic solvent results in better dissolving 
capability than DOL/DME, as evidenced by stronger Li-O (Figure S8a/c), S-H and S-O peaks 
(Figure S8b/d).   
 
We performed 1H NMR spectra on all samples to confirm that amide hydrogen is respon-
sible for the dissolution of Li2S. In CPL (Figure S7a), Acetamide (Figure S7b) and the eutectic 
solvent (Figure S7c), all 1H peaks in the eutectic solvent are from either CPL or acetamide 
without any chemical shift. When 0.2 M Li2S is dissolved in the eutectic solvent, all the C-H 
peaks did not shift while the N-H peaks initially residing at three different locations (6.67 ppm, 
7.27 ppm, and 7.37 ppm) migrate and merge to a new peak (7.0 ppm). This peak represents 
the H atoms in the N-H groups that do not directly form bonds with S2- but they are strongly 
affected by the electronegativity of sulfur. Meanwhile, a new peak representing the H-S bond 
appears at 2.0 ppm (Figure S7d).[9] When the Li2S concentration increase to 0.4 M, the N-H 
bonds at 7.0 ppm is further weakened because more H atoms are involved in forming the 
bonds with S2-, while the intensity of H-S bonds almost doubled (Figure S7e). Other C-H peaks 
remain the same (Figure S7f).

Supporting Note 3. TGA analysis 
 
TGA measurements (Figure S12) are performed on all samples in pure O2, including 
DOL/DME, the eutectic solvent, 0.4 M Li2S / the eutectic solvent, and the Catholyteeut. The mass 
of each sample is around 20 mg. The temperature ramps at a speed of 10 °C /minute in all 
tests. For DOL/DME, the evaporation is fast. The sample disappears entirely before 45 °C, 
which is lower than their boiling points (75 °C for DOL and 85 °C for DME), proving its high 
evaporation rate. For CPL/acetamide, the mass loss begins at 150 °C, and the solvent entirely 
evaporates at 208 °C. Such a result is similar to their boiling point (221 °C for acetamide and 
270 °C for CPL). It is worth noting that the evaporation process experiences a wide tempera-
ture and time window (~58 °C, ~6 minutes), showing its relatively low vapor pressure and non-
flammability in this temperature region. This can be attributed to intermolecular hydrogen 
bonds among components in the electrolyte. When Li2S is dissolved into the eutectic solvent, 
the TGA curve of the solution barely changes, and the evaporation window appears slightly 
wider.  
The TGA of the Catholyteeut is further carried out. The Catholyteeut has a composition of 
0.2 M Li2S /0.1 M LiNO3/1.2 M LiTFSI in CPL/acetamide/DOL/DME (The weight ratio of 
DOL/DME and CPL/acetamide are 27% and 41.8% respectively). The result shows weight 
loss over an even wider temperature range (Green color curve in Figure S12) from 25 to 
400 °C, exhibiting low vapor pressure and evaporation rate below 400 °C. The low evaporation 
rate is likely the reason behind why the Catholyteeut is difficult to ignite.

Supporting Note 4. Characterization of the SEI formed on the lithium metal surface by 
XPS 
 
We further performed XPS depth profiling on the lithium metal anodes after they were 
cycled 20 times in the Catholyteeut and in the Catholyteconv to understand the composition of 
their SEI. Before the XPS testing, all the cells were fully discharged to 1.8 V. As shown in 
Figure S15, the thickness of the SEI formed in the Catholyteeut (SEIeut) is approximately 45 nm, 
as we detected very strong Li metal signals at this depth. The thickness is similar to the SEI 
formed in the Catholyteconv (SEIconv). At this depth of 45 nm, trivial amounts of Li2CO3, Li-R, 
Li2S and LiF were detected for both samples. This proves that a thin and stable SEI can form 
in the presence of eutectic solvent.  
Interestingly, SEI of the two electrolytes exhibited different compositions at different depths. 
At the depths of 15/30 nm, both SEI films contain LiF, Li2CO3, Li-R and Li3N. The difference 
was sulfur’s valence: SEIconv contains Li2S/Li2S2/Li2S4/Li2S6/Li2S8 while SEIeut only contains 
Li2S/Li2S2. This is probably because only high-order polysulfides (Li2S8–Li2S4) can be dis-
solved in DOL/DME, while Li2S/Li2S2, which dominate the majority of the time in the cycling 
process, participate more in the SEIeut formation in Catholyteeut and passivate Lithium surface. 
Therefore, the valence of S in SEIeut appears lower and only Li2S/Li2S2 show up. This result 
shows that sulfur is involved in the formation of the SEIeut and still exists as a component of 
the passivation film in the SEIeut.  
At the lithium surface (0 nm), only elements of C/F/N/Li are detected in the SEIeut and there 
is no trace of S. In the control sample (SEIconv), all kinds of sulfur are detected (Li2S-Li2S8). 
This result shows that the SEIeut has formed a unique double-layer structure. The inner SEIeut 
(15 nm ~ 45 nm) is composed of LiF, Li2S/Li2S2, Li2CO3, Li-R, Li3N, which is similar to the 
SEIconv (except with more Li2S4 in SEIconv); while the outer SEIeut, mainly composed of LiF, 
Li2CO3, Li3N and Li2N2O2 without S, is a passivation layer that can protect the inner SEI from 
the Catholyteeut. Such a double-layer structure can protect the lithium metal and prevent the 
shuttle effect simultaneously.

Supporting Note 5. Discharging Voltage and efficiency 
 
    We have tested a control sample of Catholyteconv as shown in Figure S18. Two character-
istics are observed: First, the two discharging plateaus in the eutectic solvent are higher than 
those in the conventional catholyte. The average discharge voltage of the Catholyteeut is 2.21 
V, higher than the average discharging voltage of 2.12 V in the Catholyteconv (Table S3). This 
means that the energy density of the battery with Catholyteeut could potentially be 4.2% higher. 
The second point is that the energy efficiency of the battery with eutectic solvent is 93.2%, 
which is similar to that of the DOL/DME based standard catholyte (93%). 
It is interesting to understand why the efficiency does not change. As shown in Figure 
S18C, the Li-S battery with Catholyteeut shows a much smaller voltage hysteresis than Li-S 
batteries with Catholyteconv in the region for generating Li2S2 and Li2S, which are insoluble in 
DOL/DME. Considering the relatively higher viscosity and lower ionic conductivity of the Cath-
olyteeut (1.4*10-2 Pa s and 2.5*10-3 S cm-1) than Catholyteconv (2.5*10-4 Pa s and 2.7*10-2 S cm-
1), the smaller overpotential of the Catholyteeut should be the result of enhanced reaction ki-
netics brought about by the full dissolution of the sulfide/polysulfide species between Li2S and 
Li2S4. It should be noted that this region is typically considered as a rate-limiting region for Li-
S batteries. 
On the other side, the overpotential is only ~ 5 mV for the Catholyteconv in the range of 
Li2S4 to Li2S8. Such a small overpotential is a result of high solubility, low viscosity and fast 
reaction kinetics of these polysulfides in DOL/DME. These species are limited by the higher 
viscosity, lower solubility and ionic conductivity of the Catholyteeut in this region, resulting in 
the overpotential increasing to over 50 mV in the Catholyteeut. Therefore, although the over-
potential is low in the region of Li2S to Li2S4 for the Catholyteeut, the overall efficiencies of these 
two catholytes are very close (~ 93%).  
We also find that the voltage plateau transition is different in the eutectic solvent, and the 
first plateau has a capacity ~400 mAh g-1. The reason of the longer first plateau is that the first 
plateau ends at Li2S3, as Li2S6/Li2S4/Li2S3 has a higher electrode potential in eutectic solvent 
than in the DOL/DME electrolyte (Table S7 and Figure S17), which is supported by GITT ex-
periment (Figure S17). First, in the eutectic solvent-based catholyte (Catholyteeut, Figure 
S17a), both discharge voltages and GITT rest voltage curves are ~0.1 V higher than that with 
conventional DOL/DME electrolyte (Catholyteconv, Figure S17b), indicating different reaction 
chemistry. Second, in the equilibrium potential in GITT (red dash lines) and OCV of various 
Li/polysulfides (Li2Sx, x=1,2,3,4,6, green dots) in Figure S17, clearly the first plateau ends at 
Li2S4 in Catholyteconv (Figure S17b); on the contrary, it ends at Li2S3 in Catholyteeut (Figure 
S17a/c).

Figure S22. Coulombic efficiencies of the cells in Figure 4b at 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 C. The average 
coulombic efficiencies (3rd cycle to 40th cycle) for all three cells are above 99%. The CEs of 
the first two cycles are low, but these phenomena only exist in the first two cycles. After the 
first two cycles, a robust SEI is nearly formed and the shuttle effect is nearly eliminated. For 
the 2nd cycle, the low Coulombic efficiency is caused by the changing current rate, since the 
charge is at 0.1 C but the discharge is at higher rate (e.g. 0.3/0.5 C). Therefore, the CE is 
lower, which is common in all types of battery cycling. For the 3rd cycles and cycles afterward, 
the Coulombic efficiency becomes nearly 100%, as shown here. The average CE (without 1st 
and 2nd cycle) for 0.1, 0.3, 0.5 C and with TiO2 additives are 99.5%, 99.0%, 99.2%, 99.5% 
respectively. This means there should be negligible shuttling and self-discharge in the follow-
ing cycles.
