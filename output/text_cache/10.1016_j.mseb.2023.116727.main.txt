Multi-functions of amino thiophenol additives for shielding lithium metal 
anode in advanced Li-S battery 
Zhao Wang a, Basem Al Alwan b, K.Y. Simon Ng a,* 
a Department of Chemical Engineering and Materials Science, Wayne State University, Detroit, MI 48202, United States 
b Chemical Engineering Department, College of Engineering, King Khalid University, Abha 61411, Saudi Arabia  
A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have been considered as a promising candidate for the next generation of battery systems due to their high specific capacity and energy 
density. However, some barriers impede the application of Li-S batteries, such as the “shuttle effects” of lithium polysulfides (LPS), the poor electrical conductivity of 
sulfur, and lithium dendrite formation on lithium metal electrodes. Herein, we investigated a series of amino thiophenol as electrolyte additives: 2-amino thiophenol 
(2-ATP), 3-amino thiophenol (3-ATP), and 4-amino thiophenol (4-ATP); as well as p-phenylenediamine (PPD). These additives react with Li to form a stable and 
uniform solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) to protect the Li anode and minimize the interaction of Li with soluble LPS. Initial specific capacity at 0.1C and reversible 
specific capacity after 100 cycles are 1420 and 1043 mAh/g, 1429 and 982 mAh/g, 1148 and 871 mAh/g, 1317 and 791 mAh/g for 4-ATP, 3-ATP, 2-ATP, and PPD, 
respectively. Long-term cycling testing of Li-S cells was conducted at 0.2C and the cell with 4-ATP additives deliver an initial specific capacity of 1159 mAh/g and 
capacity retention of 77% after 300 cycles. The morphology and composition of the after-cycled SEI layers were examined by SEM and XPS. The findings suggest 
amino thiophenols (ATPs) form stable and smooth closed-packed SEI layers, that ameliorate lithium dendrite problems and LPS reactions with Li metal. 4-ATP is 
found to exhibit the best electrochemical performance followed by 3-ATP, 2-ATP, and PPD, which are in good agreement with the surface morphology. In addition, 
the amine group can assist the reaction between the thiol group and S8 on the cathode and modify the redox pathway of polysulfide.   
1. Introduction 
With the rapid development of electric vehicles (EVs) and portable 
electronics, Lithium-ion batteries reach the limit to meet the ever- 
increasing demand for higher energy density systems [1–4]. Lithium- 
sulfur (Li-S) batteries, which couple a Li metal anode with a sulfur 
cathode, have attracted increasing attention and are considered as one 
of the most promising candidates for next-generation energy storage 
systems due to their higher energy density (2600 Wh/kg), the abun­
dance of elemental sulfur (S8), and nontoxicity [5–7]. Among Li-S bat­
teries, lithium metal is regarded as the “holy grail” of anodes due to its 
highest theoretical specific capacity of 3860 mAh/g and low electrode 
potential of −3.04 V (vs standard hydrogen electrode). Moreover, 
elemental S also possesses a quite high theoretical capacity of 1675 
mAh/g in the cathode. However, there are still several shortcomings in 
Li-S batteries that hinder their practical application: (1) low conduc­
tivity of S and Li2S, (2) shuttling effects of lithium polysulfide (LPS), and 
(3) large volume change in the sulfur cathode [8,9]. Consequently, 
intensive efforts were devoted to modifying sulfur composite cathodes to 
mitigate the intrinsic drawbacks of sulfur by using nanostructured car­
bon host materials, conductive polymers, and transition metal oxide/ 
nitride/chalcogenide materials [10–15]. 
Although significant progress has been made to enhance sulfur 
cathode in Li-S batteries, challenges associated with Li metal anode still 
need to be addressed including lithium dendrite growth, [16] the 
accumulation of dead lithium in the anode [17], among others. The 
stabilization of the Li metal anode is crucial to enhancing the electro­
chemical performance and cycling stability of Li-S batteries [18–21]. It 
is well-known that Li metal is a highly reducing agent that readily reacts 
with the electrolyte components to form a solid electrolyte interphase 
(SEI) layer [16,17,22,23]. During the charge and discharge process, the 
sulfur in the cathode goes through a solid–liquid-solid conversion (the 
“shuttle effect”) [8,24]. Initially, the elemental sulfur is reduced to in­
termediates which are soluble lithium polysulfides (LPS) such as Li2S8, 
Li2S6, and Li2S4, then the soluble LPS are further reduced to insoluble 
Li2S2 and Li2S. During the process, soluble LPS migrates to the anode 
side to react with Li metal to cause a continuous consumption of Li 
metal, which contribute to the capacity fading of Li-S batteries. More­
over, the insoluble LPS (Li2S2 and Li2S) precipitate on the surface of Li 
metal to form an insulating layer to minimize the reaction between the 
LPS/electrolyte and Li metal anode [5,9,24–26]. LiNO3 has been widely 
used as an additive in the Li-S system, with limited success, to form a

stable SEI layer to protect Li anode [27]. The compositions of the SEI 
layer are LiNO2, LiSO3, LiSO4, and Li2S which are the reaction products 
of LiNO3 and LPS with Li metal [28]. Nevertheless, the resulting SEI 
layer is not uniform and dense enough to prevent the growth of Li 
dendrite and uneven reaction products deposition and formation of 
cracks in the SEI during the charge and discharge process. Therefore, 
LiNO3 additive alone is not quite sufficient for enhancing the electro­
chemical and cycling performance of Li-S batteries. 
Sulfur-based organic materials have been investigated as additives 
for Li-S batteries due to their diverse functional groups and stable 
structures [29–37]. The functional groups may promote LPS reactions in 
the sulfur cathode, while the unique structure of organic materials 
provides a denser and stable SEI structure on the surface of Li metal 
anode [38–41]. Lian et al. [29] first introduced the 1,4-benzenedithiols 
(1,4-BDT) as novel electrolyte additives in Li-S batteries. Their additives 
(1,4-BDT) are not only react with Li metal to form a smooth and stable 
SEI layer that consists of uniform benzene rings, but also interact with 
sulfur in cathode by their functional group -SH. More importantly, the 
Li-S batteries with 1,4-BDT additives exhibit excellent capacity and 
cycling stability as initial specific capacity of 1347 mAh/g and 909 
mAh/g after 500 cycles under 0.2C rate. Guo et al. [31] reported a 
bifunctional electrolyte additive 1,3,5-benzenetrithiol (BTT) which can 
both react with Li metal to form a stable SEI layer on Li metal surface and 
react with sulfur to generate an oligomer/polymer SEI layer to protect 
the sulfur cathode. Wei [30] and his group published their electrolyte 
additives 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)thiophenol (BTB) as sulfur-containing 
polymers to enhance the SEI layer’s capability to protect their Li metal 
anode. These studies show that organosulfur additives are promising in 
improving the electrochemical performance and cycling stability of Li-S 
batteries. However, these additives are not very effective in minimizing 
shuttling effects and enhancing conversion kinetics in cathode. Mean­
while, their specific capacity and cycling stability still needs to be 
improved [30,42–45]. 
In this work, a series of amino thiophenols (ATPs) were investigated 
for the first time as electrolyte additives in the Li-S batteries. 2-ATP, 3- 
ATP, and 4-ATP represent the isomeric structure of ATP that the amino 
group and thiol group are located in different positions of benzene ring 
such as ortho-, meta-, para-isomer. For comparison, p-phenylenediamine 
(PPD) was also utilized as an additive in Li-S batteries. ATP is composed 
of a phenyl ring, a thiol group, and one amino group (Fig. 1a). Thiol 
groups are known to react with sulfur to change the redox pathway of 
polysulfides in Li-S system [29–31,42,46]. Specifically, the S8 ring 
undergoes a nucleophilic attack to form polysulfides (RSSxH) and this 
step can be further assisted by an amino group that can deprotonate the 
thiol to form the more nucleophilic thiolate [47]. Moreover, the amino 
group may facilitate the conversion of sulfur to polysulfides [47]. It is 
postulated these ATP additives can readily react with elemental S8 to 
form organo-sulfur polysulfides (RSSx-1SR) on the surface of sulfur 
cathode (Fig. 1b) [29,48,49]. In addition, the presence of ATP additives 
alters the redox pathway of S8 within the cathode. This occurs through 
the copolymerization of the thiol group in ATP with elemental sulfur, as 
depicted in Fig. 1b. Notably, this particular step can be catalyzed by the 
amino group in ATP [50]. It should be noted that the 4-ATP is widely 
utilized as self-assembled monolayer (SAM) in various applications 
[48,49,51]. More importantly, the phenyl ring containing SEI may 
enhance the stability of the layer and shield Li metal from lithium pol­
ysulfides (LPS) [29,30]. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Materials 
Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), Lithium ni­
trate (LiNO3), Dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL)s, 
elemental sulfur, and Amino-thiophenols (2-ATP, 3-ATP, 4-ATP) were 
purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes were 
purchased from Cheaptubes, Vermont, USA and used as received. 
Lithium metal foils were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. 
2.2. Preparation of electrode and electrolytes 
The cathode electrode slurry was prepared by mixing sulfur with 
CoS@MWCNT-20% by a weight ratio of 70 to 30 wt% by melt-diffusion 
method. Preparation and characterization of CoS@MWCNT-20% was 
described in detail elsewhere [52]. The mixture was heated at 115 ℃ for 
1 h and then 10 wt% Polyvinylidene (PVDF) was added as binder in N- 
Methyl-2-Pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent to form a slurry. After grinding for 
30 min, the slurry was coated on Al foil by a doctor blade and dried at 
60℃ under vacuum for overnight to prepare sulfur cathode. The sulfur 
cathode, with sulfur loading of around 3 mg/cm2, was cut into disks 
with a diameter of 14 mm. 
The basic electrolyte for Li-S batteries is prepared by mixing 
appropriate amount of 1 M LiTFSI, 2 wt% LiNO3 in 1,2-dimethoxy­
ethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v:v = 1:1). The 0.05 M ATP

2.3. Materials characterization 
The Li anodes were obtained by disassembling the cycled Li-S cells 
and washed by DME solvent several times and dried in the glovebox. To 
avoid contact with air and moisture, Kapton tape was used to protect the 
Li metal. The morphology of the cycled Li metal and sulfur cathode were 
characterized by a field-emission scanning electron microscopy (JSM- 
7600 FE SEM). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization 
was carried out using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nexsa surface analysis 
system, and XPS spectra were analyzed by Avantage software. Elec­
trochemical measurements. 
Galvanostatic discharge–charge tests were conducted by a Maccor 
Model 4200 Automated Test System between a voltage range of 1.5–3.0 
V (vs. Li/Li+) at room temperature. The electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments were 
tested by using a CH Instruments CHI660D Electrochemical Worksta­
tion. Specifically, the scan rate of CV is 0.1 mV/s and the frequency of 
EIS is from 0.1 to 100,000 Hz. 
3. Results and discussion 
The effects of different electrolyte additives on the electrochemical 
performance of Li-S batteries, conducted at 0.1C for 100 cycles, are 
shown in Fig. 2a. Cells are fabricated with a high sulfur loading (3 mg/ 
cm2) and a low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio (around 8 μL/mg). The 
specific capacities are calculated based on weight of elemental sulfur. 
The capacity and stability of Li-S cells with different ATP additives (2- 
ATP, 3-ATP, and 4-ATP) are significantly enhanced compared to the 
cells without additives. The cell with 4-ATP shows good cycling stability 
and the highest specific capacity. The initial specific capacities were 
1258, 1317, 1291, 1501, and 1488 mAh/g, and capacity retention after 
100 cycles were 59.8%, 62.3%, 67.5%, 68.7%, and 84.1% for cells with 
no additive, PPD, 2-ATP, 3-ATP, and 4-ATP, respectively. In addition, 
the coulombic efficiency (CE) was improved with the additives, espe­
cially the cell with 4-ATP additive was stable around 99% for 100 cycles. 
It should be noted that 4-ATP performs better than 2-ATP and 3-ATP 
which can be attributed to the steric stabilization effect for SEI layer 
formation on lithium anode;[29,40,53] and to the reactivity of thiol as 
related to the position of amine on the benzene ring. The Li-S cells with 
ATP additives exhibit higher discharge specific capacity because the 
thiol group contributes to the capacity [54]. Moreover, 2-ATP and 3-ATP 
tend to be self-polymerized to form stable oligomers due to steric sta­
bilization since the angle between the amino group and thiol group is 
smaller than 180◦. These self-polymerized oligomers limit the interac­
tion with elemental sulfur thus resulting in lower capacity as compared 
to 4-ATP. On the other hand, there is limited steric hindrance effect on 4- 
ATP (due to 1,4 configuration) resulting in increased freedom and

Fig. 2b shows the impedance of Li-S cells with various additives 
before cycling, which correlate well with the cycling performance 
(Fig. 2a). The impedances are 27, 35, 39, 49, and 52 Ω for Li-S cells with 
4-ATP, 3-ATP, 2-ATP, PPD, and no additive, respectively. The EIS data 
suggested that 4-ATP plays an important role in forming least resistance 
layers on both lithium metal anode and sulfur cathode. Additionally, 
rate capability of the cells with different additives were conducted under 
different C rates (Fig. 2c). The cells with 4-ATP exhibit a stable average 
capacity of 1411, 1143, 1050, 817, and 583 mAh/g for 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 
and 2C, respectively; and show a good recovery capability (90 % 
recovered) when the C rate returns to 0.1C. The cells with 3-ATP, 2-ATP, 
and PPD show good rate capability and follow the same trend with 
cycling performance (Fig. 2a). On the other hand, the rate capability of 
the cell without any additive shows a greater fluctuation under different 
C rates. The enhanced performance of Li-S cells suggests better electrode 
kinetics, which can be attributed to the high Li+ diffusion in the 
electrode. 
The morphology of Li metal anodes after 100 cycles was character­
ized by SEM (Fig. 3). The sample without additives (Fig. 3a) shows a 
higher degree of surface deformation, while samples with additives 
(Fig. 3b, 3c, 3d, and 3e) show a smoother surface. It is noted that in 
Fig. 3d there are some holes which can be attributed to the nonuniform 
SEI layer and the lithium dendrite growth. This is particularly noticeable 
in the 4-ATP sample which shows an intact surface. This suggests that 4- 
ATP provides better protection to the lithium and ebbs side reactions 
between the lithium metal and the electrolyte. Fig. 3f shows the EDS of 
Li metal with 4-ATP additive. Although the mass ratio of EDS is semi- 
quantitative, the carbon atomic % is relatively high which can be 
attributed to the deposition of phenyl ring of 4-ATP. The strong signal of 
sulfur in Fig. 3f further indicates the reactions between 4-ATP and Li 
metal, and the Ph-S-Li bonds were formed on the surface of Li metal in 
the SEI layer. Moreover, additives also lead to smoother surfaces in 
sulfur cathodes (Fig. 4b-f), as compared to the sulfur cathode without 
additive (Fig. 4a). Figure S1 shows the higher magnification images of 
the sulfur cathode with and without additives. These findings suggest 
the thiol in additives react with sulfur on cathode and form a denser and 
smoother layer with phenyl ring. 
The effect of the organosulfur-containing SEI on the behaviors of Li 
plating/stripping in the electrolyte solution with/without 4-ATP addi­
tives was investigated. (Fig. 5a) A current density of 1 mA/cm2 (ca­
pacity density 1 mAh/cm2) was applied for the galvanostatic cycling 
testing of the Li symmetric cells. The cell with 4-ATP maintains an 
overpotential around 0.02 V after 70 h, which indicates the excellent 
reversibility of the Lithium deposition/stripping. The low overpotential 
also indicates that the SEI layer facilitates reversible cycling and reduces 
interfacial resistance [55]. In contrast, the cell without 4-ATP has an 
overpotential as 0.15 V. These findings suggest that 4-ATP is able to 
improve the efficiency of lithium deposition/stripping and the cycling 
stability of Li metal. In addition, as shown in the Fig. 5b, the EIS results 
of the Li symmetric cells after 70 h cycling are consistent with the 
overpotential data. The Li symmetric cell with 4-ATP shows a smaller 
impedance of 40 Ω, while the cell without 4-ATP exhibits an impedance 
of 50 Ω. 
Fig. 6a shows the long cycle life testing of Li-S cells with 4-ATP and 
without additives at 0.2C. The initial specific capacity of Li-S cell with 4- 
ATP is 1195 mAh/g with a 75% capacity retention after 300 cycles. 
However, the cell without any additives delivers initial discharge ca­
pacity of 1024 mAh/g with a 60% capacity retention after 300 cycles. 
The long cycling testing indicates that the addition of 4-ATP can stabi­
lize the SEI layer on the surface of lithium metal. More cycling perfor­
mance of Li-S cells with other additives are presented in Figure S2. The 
EIS spectra of Li-S cells with 4-ATP and without additive before and after 
300 cycles are shown in Fig. 6b. The EIS spectra of the sample with 4- 
ATP after 300 cycles exhibits 2 semi-circles. In general, the semi-circle 
in high frequency is related to the Li+ ion migration in the SEI layer 
(RSEI), while the semi-circle in low frequency is related to charge transfer 
in the SEI layer (RCT) [30,31]. After 300 cycles, RSEI of Li-S cells with 4- 
ATP additive is very stable around 15 Ω, and the RCT is around 8 Ω. In 
contrast, the cell without additive not only exhibits a large impedance as 
40 Ω before cycling, but also shows a much larger impedance (80 Ω) 
after 300 cycles. The lower impedance of cells with 4-ATP can be 
attributed to the fast migration of Li+ ion in the SEI layer. Fig. 6c shows 
the cyclic voltammetry (CV) of cells with and without 4-ATP additive. It 
is worth noting that the gap between oxidization peak and reduction 
peaks in 4-ATP is smaller than the cell without additive. This implies the 
better reversibility of the electrochemical reactions between anode and 
cathode in the cell with 4-ATP. The reduction peak shows a larger cur­
rent response than the cell without additive, thus a larger discharge

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to characterize the 
SEI layer on Li foil after 10 cycles at 0.1C. To minimize the effects of 
impurity on the surface of lithium foil, DME solvents are utilized to wash 
lithium metal several times before XPS characterizations. In the S 2p 
spectra (Fig. 7a), deconvoluted peaks at 167.4, 169.1, and 170.5 eV 
correspond to Li2SO4, Li2SO3, and Li2S2O3, respectively [59]. These 
oxidized sulfur species are the byproducts of lithium polysulfide, lithium 
oxide and LiNO3 [28]. The strong peak at 163.4 eV indicates the thiol- 
lithium bond which can be attributed to the reaction of -SH group in 
4-ATP additive with Li metal (Li-ATP). The deposition of phenyl en­
hances the strength of SEI layer to protect Li metal. Moreover, the 
strongest deconvoluted peak at 399.5 eV of N 1s spectra (Fig. 7b), 
correspond to C-NH2, suggesting that the –NH2 moiety is relatively inert. 
However, amine may accelerate the kinetic conversion of S8 to poly­
sulfides on the cathode, and enhance the reactivity of thiol on Li metal 
anode to form the SEI layer [47,53]. This is consistent with our proposed 
reactions of 4-ATP with sulfur and Lithium (Fig. 1b). Based on XPS, the 
composition of SEI layer includes organic (ATP), and inorganic (Li2SO4, 
Li2SO3, and Li2S2O3, LiNO2 and Li2S) components. 
XPS, SEM, and electrochemical performance results suggested that 
the ATP additives enhance the growth of stronger and intact SEI layers, 
and also the amine in ATP may accelerate the kinetic conversion be­
tween S8 and thiol groups. With ATP additives, the SEI layer is composed 
of phenyl (organic), and LiSO4, LiSO3, and LiNO2 (inorganic). The ex­
istence of organic materials (phenyl ring) in SEI layer led to a smoother 
surface of Li metal anode as indicated by SEM images. In addition, amine 
in ATP may deprotonate the thiol to enhance the reaction of S8 and thiol 
in ATP. As a result, the thiol group in ATP readily reacts with sulfur to 
form organo-polysulfides which may modify the redox pathway of S to 
lithium polysulfides and minimize the shuttle effect to some degree. The 
reaction between thiol group in 4-ATP and S8 is irreversible. Further­
more, the thiol group (RSH) can react with S8 to form RSSxH and this 
process can be catalyzed by amino group in the electrolyte [47,50]. The

4. Conclusion 
This is the first study of ATPs as electrolyte additives in Li-S batteries. 
The electrochemical performance follows the trend of 4-ATP > 3-ATP >
2-ATP attributed to the steric effect of amino and thiol positions. The Li- 
S cell with 4-ATP deliver high initial specific discharge capacity of 1195 
mAh/g and capacity retention of 75% after 300 cycles at 0.2C. The 
enhanced electrochemical performance of Li-S cells with ATP additives 
can be attributed to the dense and smooth SEI layers grown on Li metal 
minimize the reaction between soluble LPS and Li metal anode. More­
over, the existence of amino groups may enhance the reactivity of thiol 
group on both sulfur cathode and Li metal anode, the conversion of S8 to 
polysulfides, and the migration of Li+ diffusion on the cathode. ATP 
additives, with amino moiety and thiol group, shows great potential as 
organo-sulfur based electrolyte additive for Li-S batteries.
