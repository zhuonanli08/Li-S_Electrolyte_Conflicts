ABSTRACT: The conventional lithium−sulfur battery (LSB) under-
goes a “solid−liquid−solid” cathode process during which the
intermediate polysulﬁdes dissolve into the electrolyte, leading to a
serious “shuttle” reaction and signiﬁcantly shortened lifespan. Here, we
realize a novel “solid →solid” cathode mode for LSBs via a
transplantable solid electrolyte interface (SEI). The SEI is in situ
formed in a carbonate-based electrolyte with high-concentration dual-
salt during the initial discharge process. The solid →solid cathode
process does not involve any dissolution of the intermediates; hence, the
“shuttle eﬀect” can be totally eliminated. Furthermore, the SEI shows a
high electrolyte compatibility and can be transplanted to the conven-
tional carbonate-based/ether-based electrolytes. The sulfur/carbon composite with 65% sulfur delivers a reversible speciﬁc
capacity of 1009 mA h g−1 and negligible self-discharge. The SEI strategy can successfully break the limitation from the
traditional “catholyte” electrode mechanism. Meanwhile, it provides large ﬂexibility for designing high-loading carbon hosts and
selecting an electrolyte for high-performance LSBs.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, high-concentration electrolyte, transplantable SEI, “solid →solid” conversion,
negligible self-discharge
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have great potential to meet
the urgent market demand owing to their low cost,
environmental benignity, and overwhelming theoretical energy
density (2600 W h kg−1).1−3 However, sulfur (S) and its
reduzates (Li2Sx, 1 ≤x ≤8) are inherently insulative,4 leading
to poor electrochemical accessibility and low S utilization.5,6
To address these issues, various nanostructured and highly
conductive matrices7,8 are developed as matrices for the
insulative S to improve the conductivity of the cathode, and
more importantly, a “catholyte” electrode mode is widely
applied for the LSB system. Cyclic or linear ethers, which have
high polysulﬁde (PS; Li2Sx, 2 < x ≤8) solubility are chosen as
electrolyte solvents of the LSB. Such electrolytes create a “solid
→liquid →solid” electrode process for the S cathode. The
dissolution of the insulating intermediates exposes the fresh
surface of the insulative S, so that a sustainable redox reaction
is enabled. Under the synergistic eﬀect of the conductive
matrix and the solid →liquid →solid cathode mode, the
electrochemical accessibility of S is enhanced drastically. Some
well-designed S composite cathodes even demonstrate initial
speciﬁc capacities that approach the theoretical value.9
Unfortunately, the dissolution of the intermediates causes the
noted “internal shuttle” phenomenon that leads to a poor
battery cycle life. Numerous architectures based on car-
bons,10,11 polymers,12 metal oxides,13,14 metal sulﬁdes,15,16 or
metal carbides17,18 have been employed to immobilize the
dissolved PS through physical or chemical absorption.
However, as long as the “solid →liquid →solid” phase
conversion dominates the cathode process, the PS loss is
unavoidable. The S conversion during the electrode process
alternates between the “solid →liquid” and “liquid →solid”
reactions. During the solid →liquid reaction, the liquid
electrolyte ﬁlls the spaces within the pores of the host, which
are originally occupied by the solid phase, S8/Li2S/Li2S2. In the
liquid →solid reaction, the new-formed solid phase, Li2S2/
Li2S/S8 squeezes out the related electrolyte. Thus, although the
physical or chemical forces from the host can immobilize some
of the Sx
2−ions, many of them inevitably escape out from the
cage along with the electrolyte current. What is more, in regard
to the practical cell device, the dissolution of the intermediates
demands large amount of the electrolytes, and the high

electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio greatly decreases the practical
energy density of the battery. Therefore, although the solid →
liquid →solid cathode mode greatly contributes to the high
electrochemical accessibility of S, it also brings a big challenge
for the enhancement of the cycle stability and the practical
energy density of the full battery.19
Recently, a solid →solid S conversion has shown great
advantages to overcome the dilemma of the solid →liquid →
solid cathode mode. The S2−4/microporous carbon composites
are the pioneer materials that undergo solid →solid S
conversion, which shows unusual compatibility with the
carbonate electrolyte and unique single voltage platform
during discharge.20 In the S2−4/microporous carbon system,
a solid →solid S conversion is realized via space conﬁnement:
the micropores are small enough to block the inﬁltrating
solvent molecules.21 As a result, the dissolution of PS is
avoided and the lithiation/delithiation of S is operated via a
solid →solid process. The S2−4/microporous carbon
composite demonstrates a great advantage in the cycle stability.
However, to restrain the solvent molecules, the size of matrix
pores should be <1 nm, leading to the low S accommodation
capacity (<40%) and serious limitation of the practical energy
density of the full cell.22 More recently, Ai’s group has reported
a solid−solid sulfur conversion via the SEI strategy.23 They
introduce carbonate into the conventional ether-based electro-
lyte as a cosolvent to produce insoluble reaction products
through a prompt nucleophilic reaction between the PS and
the carbonate molecules which in situ creates the SEI between
the S/electrolyte interface. Liang et al. also realized the solid−
solid cathode process via a similar SEI strategy, in which the
concentrated electrolyte with a PS nonsolvating characteristic
was used.24 In these two works, the solid−solid conversion of
sulfur is realized in the “S8” system and the sulfur content in
the cathode is largely improved compared with the previous
S2−4/microporous carbon system, which makes the solid−solid
sulfur conversion more attractive to practically fabricate viable
Li−S batteries.
Compared with the S/microporous carbon system which
depends on the size eﬀect, the SEI strategy provides large
ﬂexibility for the choice of carbon host. It is beneﬁcial for the
enhancement of the S loading, which is vital for the practical
application of the Li−S battery. To improve the sulfur-loading
capability, the pores of the hosts should be as large as possible.
In the meantime, because of the poor electronic conductivity
of S/Li2S, there should be a tradeoﬀon the pore size choice. So
far, the roles that the pores with diﬀerent sizes play have not
yet been fully understood. The above-mentioned two works
adopted BP2000 and Ketjen black as the hosts for sulfur,
respectively. Both the carbons demonstrate broad pore-size
distribution from micropores to macropores so that the
relation between the electrochemical performances and the
pore size is still ambiguous. Herein, we choose the mesoporous
carbon (CMK-3) as the host for sulfur to realize the solid−
solid conversion. The CMK-3 matrix has a single pore-size
distribution centered at 2.5−4.3 nm, which can help to clearly
evaluate the feasibility of the solid−solid cathode mode within
this pore range. In addition, a carbonate-based electrolyte with
high-concentration dual-salt is adopted. It is found that the
resulting SEI in this system demonstrates a high electrolyte
compatibility, which can be transplanted to the conventional
carbonate-based/ether-based electrolytes with 1.0 M Li salt. In
the meantime, quantum mechanical DFT-MD simulation has
been used to obtain the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(LUMO) of the electrolyte to explain the source of the SEI. It
is also found that the low voltage discharge in the initial cycle
contributes to the optimization of the components of the SEI.
In this work, the S content reaches up to 65% in the C/S
composite and a reversible speciﬁc capacity of >1000 mA h g−1
is obtained. Both the “shuttle phenomenon” and the self-
discharge are completely depressed. The solid →solid
electrode mode via the SEI strategy can break the limitation
of the catholyte electrode mechanism for LSBs. We believe
that the strategy proposed in this work is applicable to the
design of S cathodes, the selection of the carbon host, as well
as the electrolyte for practical LSBs.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of CMK-3/S Cathode. The CMK-3 (Nanjing
JC-nano, China) and sulfur (S, Sigma) powder were mixed via ball
milling at a certain ratio. The mixture was put into a glass tube for
heat treatment at 155 °C for 12 h under vacuum. The resulting CMK-
3/S was mixed with super-P, sodium carboxyl methyl cellulose, and
styrene butadiene rubber (8:1:0.5:0.5, by weight) to form a uniform
slurry. The slurry was casted on carbon paper (Shanghai Hesen,
China) and heated at 70 °C for 12 h in an oven to obtain the CMK-
3/S cathode. The loading of S is ∼1.5−2.0 mg cm−2.
2.2. Preparation of Electrolytes. All electrolytes were prepared
and used in a glove box ﬁlled with argon (Ar) (O2/H2O < 1 ppm).
The 1.0 M (M is molality) carbonate electrolyte (to be named LB315,
Guotai Huarong, China) comprised 1.0 M lithium hexaﬂuorophos-
phate (LiPF6) in mingled carbonate solvents ethylene carbonate
(EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and ethyl methyl carbonate
(EMC) (EC + DMC + EMC (1/1/1, v/v/v)). The 1.0 M ether
electrolyte is made of 1.0 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI, Sigma) dissolved in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) (DOL + DME (1/1, v/v)). The diﬀerent
concentrated dual-salt electrolytes were prepared using LiTFSI and
lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, Suzhou Fluolyte Co, Ltd,
China) with desired concentration in a mixed ester solvent. The
carbonate solvents were made up by EC, diethyl carbonate (DEC),
and ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC) (EC + DEC + FEC (1/1/1, v/
v/v)). The three diﬀerent concentrated dual-salt electrolytes are 0.5
M LiTFSI−0.5 M LiFSI−EC/DEC/FEC (T0.5F0.5C), 2.0 M LiTFSI−
1.0 M LiFSI−EC/DEC/FEC (T2.0F1.0C), and 4.0 M LiTFSI−2.5 M
LiFSI−EC/DEC/FEC (T4.0F2.5C).
2.3. Characterizations. X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) patterns were
collected by X-ray powder diﬀractometry (PANalytical X’Pert PRO-
DY2198, Holland), operating at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu Kα
radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). Nitrogen sorption isotherms were
measured at −196 °C using a Micromeritics ASAP 2010 analyzer
(US). Before testing, the sample was dried at 200 °C for 12 h under
vacuum. The Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) surface area was
calculated according to the adsorption data in the relative pressure
range of 0.06−0.2. The content of S was calculated by
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) in N2 atmosphere. The cycled
cells were disassembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glove box, and the S cathodes
were washed three times with DEC and placed in the glove box for 3
days to fully evaporate the solvent before being used for character-
ization. The morphologies of the fresh electrode and cycled S
electrode were observed by SEM (FEI, Sirion 200) and TEM (JEM-
2100). The electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) elemental
mappings of the cycled S cathode were measured on Nova Nano SEM
450 (FEI, Holland). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; AXIS
ULTRA DLD-600W) and attenuated total reﬂection−Fourier trans-
form infrared (ATR−FTIR) (VERTEX 70) were used to analyze the
composition information of the SEI.
2.4. Coin-Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Measure-
ments. The CR 2032 coin cells were assembled in a glove box
ﬁlled with Ar. The Li-metal foil was used as the counter electrode and
Celgard 2300 membrane as the separator. The CMK-3/S@SEI
cathode was obtained by disassembling a cell which had been

2.5. Quantum Mechanical DFT-MD Simulation. The Quan-
tum chemical calculations were carried out to theoretically elucidate
the origin of the unique electrolyte solvation structure to form the SEI
with low discharge cut-oﬀvoltage. The quantum chemical
calculations, especially the density functional theory (DFT) and
molecular dynamics (MD) have been widely adopted as important
tools to investigate the electronic structure of chemical reactions and
physical movement of molecules in a complex system, respectively.
After amorphous cell packing to place EC/DEC/FEC solvent
molecules into a TFSI−/FSI−anion system, DFT-MD was performed
to sample the dynamic trajectories of electrolyte molecules in three-
dimensional space. DFT-MD simulations were carried out with time
steps of 1.0 fs in the NVT microcanonical ensemble dynamics at a
ﬁxed volume and a constant energy at 298 K for 50 ps, and diﬀerent
starting conﬁgurations were considered, although no statistic
approach was performed on the starting geometries.
The DFT calculations were carried out using DMol3 code. The
Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof exchange−correlation functional and
generalized gradient approximation were used to describe the
exchange−correlation eﬀects. The all-electron method was used to
describe the contribution of all electrons from the system atoms. A
double numerical basis set with polarization functions (DNP) and a
real space cutoﬀof 4.0 Å were used. The k points of (1 × 1 × 2)
Monkhorst−Pack grid were used for bulk lattice optimizations with
optimized unit cell parameters (a = b = 1 Å; c = 2 Å). The
contribution of the solvent on the system was considered using the
COSMO solvation model, and the dielectric constant of the solvent
was set as 56.94.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
For a demonstration of the solid →solid S conversion via the
in situ formed SEI for the S cathode, mesoporous CMK-3
carbon was chosen as the model host for the C/S composite
materials. CMK-3 has uniform mesopores with the size of 2.5−
4.3 nm (Figure S1a) and large pore volume of 1.36 cm3 g−1
(Figure S1b), ensuring a high S loading capability. In the
previous reports, the CMK-3/S cathode can only work in the
ether-based electrolyte because of the side reactions between
the carbonate solvent and the nucleophilic PS.25−27 In our
case, CMK-3 can unify the particle size of the insulative S to
aﬀord a decent electrochemical accessibility. Meanwhile, a
high-concentration dual-salt electrolyte with carbonate solvent
is chosen to form the SEI on the cathode. Figure 1 shows the
schematic illustration of the two fundamentally diﬀerent
electrode processes. During the traditional solid →liquid →
solid conversion process (Figure 1a), the intermediate PS
dissolves into the electrolyte and the incident “internal shuttle”
process causes a quick S loss and low Coulombic eﬃciency
(CE). For the solid →solid electrode mode in Figure 1b,
during the initial discharge process, both the chemical
reactions between PS and the carbonate molecules23,28 and
the electrochemical reduction of the electrolyte at low
voltage29−31 jointly create an integrated high-quality SEI,
which constructs a perfect wrapping layer for the S conversion
reaction. As long as the SEI forms, further inﬁltration of the
solvents is blocked and the side reactions are hampered. Li+
transport is allowed via the SEI, and a solid →solid S
conversion is successfully realized.
The high-concentration dual-salt electrolyte is composed of
4.0 M LiTFSI−2.5 M LiFSI in EC, DEC, and FEC (EC + DEC
+ FEC (1/1/1, v/v/v)) (T4.0F2.5C). The high salt concen-
tration lowers not only the solubility of PS,24 but also the
activity of the carbonate solvents32−34 which suppresses the
side reactions between PS and carbonate molecules. The
addition of the LiFSI salt promotes the formation of a LiF-rich
ﬁlm, which contributes to the stability and ionic conductivity
of the SEI during long cycling.29 As shown in Figure 2a, during
the initial discharge, the CMK-3/S cathode is deeply
discharged to 0.1 V to fully form the SEI. The cathode/
electrolyte interface change is monitored by EIS. As shown in
Figure 2b1−b3, the depressed semicircle existing from the
beginning corresponds to the electric double-layer. During
discharge, a new semicircle appears, which implies the presence
of the passivation layer at the cathode surface. When
discharged to 0.1 V, the two semicircles separate completely,
indicating that the electrode/electrolyte interface evolves
continuously. The insets show the equivalent circuit with
two RC parallel circuits. The morphology evolution also
conﬁrms the formation of the SEI. In Figure 2c1−c3, the fresh S
cathode shows a smooth surface. When discharged to 1.0 V, a
ﬂocculent layer is generated on the surface. When further
discharged to 0.1 V, the coated ﬁlm becomes denser and
thicker. The ﬁlm is identiﬁed as the SEI layer formed from the
products of the anionic polymerization reaction triggered by
the nucleophilic sulﬁde anions,23,28 and the electrochemical
reduction products of the solvents and Li salt anions at low
voltage.31 The corresponding EELS elemental mappings in
Figure 2d2−e2 (indicated in Figure 2d1−e1) show that the SEI
layer contains C, F, S, and O elements and the contents of F
and O increase with further discharging. The existence of F is
originated from LiF, which is one of the electrochemical
reduction products of the Li salts and FEC. On the other hand,
O comes from Li2CO3, ROCO2Li, Li2SO3, the polythionate
complex, and so forth.31,35,36 Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX)
demonstrates similar results (Figure S2). As discharge
progresses, the content of F increases from 0 to 5.5 and
10.9% at 1.0 and 0.1 V, respectively. The increase of F content
implies the increase of LiF content, which beneﬁts the
electrochemical stability and Li+ conductivity of the SEI. The
increase of O content should be assigned to the formation of Li
alkylate and Li carbonate, which are the main organic
ingredients of the SEI.37

(Figure S8). Figure 5d shows the cycling performance of the
CMK-3/S@SEI cathode (50% S) in the conventional
carbonate electrolyte (1 M) and ether electrolyte (1 M).
The CMK-3/S@SEI cathode (50% S) demonstrates a good
electrolyte compatibility in both electrolytes. Figure S9 shows
the EIS spectra of CMK-3/S@SEI at 1.0 V in the 1st and 50th
cycle in the T4.0F2.5C electrolyte (Figure S9b,c) and the 1.0 M
carbonate electrolyte (Figure S9d,e). In either electrolyte, the
spectra do not change signiﬁcantly after cycling, indicating that
the SEI formed on the cathode maintains well during the
cycling in both electrolytes. The reversible capacities of 1341
and 1360 mA h g−1 are achieved during the initial cycle and the
capacities maintain at 1071 and 1064 mA h g−1 after 80 cycles
in the carbonate and ether electrolytes, respectively. It should
be noted that the CMK-3/S@SEI cathode in the two 1.0 M
electrolytes displays a single discharge platform with the
average voltage of 1.95 V (Figure S10a,b), which is higher than
that in the T4.0F2.5C electrolyte (∼1.75 V, Figure S6c). This
may be because the electrolyte with 1.0 M salt has a lower
viscosity and higher ionic conductivity. As expected, the CMK-
3/S cathode cannot work in the carbonate electrolyte at
all (Figure S10c). Even in the ether electrolyte (Figure S10d),
although it gives a comparable initial discharge capacity of
1362 mA h g−1, the cycle stability is poor. The capacity
maintains at only 643 mA h g−1 after 80 cycles, and the CE is
about 90% due to the serious internal shuttle eﬀect. In sharp
contrast, the solid →solid cathode demonstrates ultralow self-
discharge rate: in the 1.0 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (1/1, v/v)
electrolyte without LiNO3, after 3 days rest, the CMK-3/S@
SEI cathode shows negligible self-discharge. After 7 and 15
days, the self-discharge rates are only 0.5 and 0.9%, respectively
(Figure 5e). As a comparison, for CMK-3/S, the self-discharge
rates after 3, 7, and 15 days are 7.8, 10.8, and 18.8%,
respectively (Figure 5f).
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, a novel solid →solid S conversion is successfully
realized via a simple transplantable SEI strategy in the S/CMK-
3 composite material system. The SEI layer allows Li+ to pass,
but completely blocks the emigration of PS and the
immigration of the solvent molecules. Compared with the
previous solid →solid S conversion via the microporous
carbon, the SEI strategy in this work provides large ﬂexibility
for the host choice, and, in the meantime, it oﬀers a great
possibility to increase the S content. We believe that this work
can oﬀer some valuable guidance for the cathode design and
electrolyte selection for developing high-performance LSBs.
