Carbon Composites for a High-Energy Lithium–Sulfur
Battey with a Glyme-Based Electrolyte
Lorenzo Carbone,[a] Jing Peng,[b, c] Marco Agostini,[d] Mallory Gobet,[b] Matthew Devany,[e]
Bruno Scrosati,[f, g] Steve Greenbaum,*[b] and Jusef Hassoun*[h]
1. Introduction
Increasing demand for energy storage in a wide range of appli-
cations, such as electronic devices, electric vehicles, and smart
energy grids, is triggering the need for new batteries charac-
terized by superior efficiency and capacity.[1] A commercial lithi-
um-ion battery, that is, the most appealing available system
with a specific capacity of 150 mAhg@1 delivered at 3.6 V, re-
quires breakthroughs in terms of energy density to satisfy the
severe targets of modern markets.[2] In this respect, the lithi-
um–sulfur battery is considered to be one of the most promis-
ing candidates, due to a high theoretical specific capacity and
energy, namely, 1675 mAhg@1 and 2600 Whkg@1, respectively.[3]
Additional bonuses of sulfur, such as wide availability and low
environmental impact, are expected to produce minimal nega-
tive economic impact on the manufacture of lithium–sulfur
batteries.[4] Despite these attractive characteristics, sulfur suf-
fers from several issues in a lithium cell, including low electron-
ic and ionic conductivity[5] and soluble polysulfide formation
during the electrochemical processes.[6] Polysulfides of general
formula Li2Sx are soluble in common lithium battery organic
electrolytes, and may migrate to the anode side and react at
the lithium surface, leading to salt deposition, shuttle process-
es, and finally to cell failure.[7] Furthermore, the low conductivi-
ty of the sulfur cathode significantly depresses the delivered
capacity and the rate capability in the lithium cell due to in-
creased polarization during the lithium–sulfur electrochemical
process.[8] Infiltrating sulfur into several kinds of carbonaceous
materials, such as graphite layers,[9] amorphous carbon,[10]
carbon nanotubes (CNTs),[11] nanosheets,[12] and hierarchical
structured carbons,[13] has actually led to great improvement of
the cycling stability; electron and ion conductivity; and, conse-
quently, the rate capability and cycle life of sulfur cathodes in
lithium cells.[14–16] However, most studies reporting sulfur com-
posites,[17,18] mainly based on carbons,[10,19,20] involved complex
procedures for the preparation of active material that might
actually lead to a rapid increase in the economic impact of the
cell. Instead, our study proposes a simple syntactical pathway,
A comparative study of sulfur composites using carbon of vari-
ous natures, namely, graphite, mesocarbon microbeads, and
multi-walled carbon nanotubes, is performed in lithium battery
design and evaluation. Morphological and structural analyses,
by means of SEM and XRD, cyclic voltammetry and galvano-
static cycling in lithium cells are employed for characterization
of the materials. Tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether containing
lithium trifluoromethansulfonate is considered the preferred
electrolyte for performing the electrochemical tests. Prior to
use in cells, the electrolyte characteristics in terms of 1H, 7Li,
and 19F nuclei self-diffusion coefficients, ionic conductivity, and
ionic association degree are studied by combining NMR and
impedance spectroscopy. The best lithium–sulfur composite re-
ported herein achieves a capacity higher than 500 mAhg@1
over 140 cycles with no sign of dendrite formation or failure.
This performance is considered sufficiently suitable for the de-
velopment of high-energy lithium batteries, in particular, con-
sidering the expected safety of the cells by employing a non-
flammable glyme electrolyte instead of a conventional carbon-
ate-based one.

Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation
The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiCF3SO3 (1 mol; Sigma
Aldrich) in TEGDME (1 kg, Sigma Aldrich), which was designated as
TEGDME-LiCF3SO3 (1 molkg@1). This was performed in an argon-
filled glove box. Before mixing, TEGDME was dried over molecular
sieves until the water content was lower than 10 ppm (determined
by Karl Fischer titration instrument Metrohm) and LiCF3SO3 was
dried under vacuum at 808C for 24 h.
Electrode Preparation
The active materials were prepared by mixing elemental sulfur and
graphite, MCMB, or MWCNTs provided by Sigma Aldrich. Elemental
sulfur was melted at 1358C, added to the selected carbon powder
in a 2:3 weight ratio, and subsequently stirred for 3 h until a homo-
genous mixture was obtained. The mixture was subsequently
cooled to room temperature then refined by mortar and high-
energy ball milling 4 times for 15 min at a frequency of 15 Hz with
30 min of rest between steps by using a Retsch Mill MM400 instru-
ment. For electrodes preparation, the active material powders (i.e.
S-graphite, S-MCMB, or S-MWCNTs) were mixed together with poly-
vinylidene difluoride (PVdF; Solvay Solef) binder in an 85:15
weight ratio, and dispersed in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP). The re-
sulting slurries were stirred for 1 h, cast on aluminum foils by the
Doctor Blade technique with a wet thickness of 300 mm, and dried
for 3 h at 608C under air. Before cell assembly in the glove box,
the electrodes were disk punched and vacuum dried overnight at
608C.

2. Results and Discussion
Figure 1 reports the TEGDME-LiCF3SO3 characteristics, as deter-
mined by combining NMR spectroscopy and EIS. The 1H, 7Li,
and
19F nuclei self-diffusion coefficients determined by NMR
spectroscopy increase with increasing temperature over the in-
vestigated temperature range (Figure 1A), as indeed was ex-
pected for glyme-based electrolytes for application in lithium
batteries.[22] Lithium cations and CF3SO3
@ anions, in other
words, the charge carriers in the solution, exhibit similar self-
diffusion coefficients (DLi and DF, respectively) with lower
values than that corresponding to TEGDME (DH) due to their
substantial solvation shells. Electrolyte conductivity may be cal-
culated by using Equation (1) with the self-diffusion coeffi-
cients (dNMR) or directly measured by EIS (dEIS). Figure 1B, which
provides a comparison between dNMR and dEIS values, reveals
the same increasing trend with raising temperature. However,
the two values of the conductivity differ by one order of mag-
nitude, that is, in the order of 10@3 Scm@1
for dEIS and
10@2 Scm@1 for dNMR, due to the different nature of the two
techniques. Indeed, EIS focuses only on Li+ and CF3SO3
@ trans-
port, whereas PFG-NMR spectroscopy reveals the movement of
all charge carriers, including neutral ionic pairs and multiple as-
sociated ions.[29,30] Accordingly, the combination of dNMR and
dEIS in Equation (2) leads to the ionic association degree (a),
which assumes a value of about 0.87 over the explored tem-
perature range (Figure 1C), which is in line with values ob-
served for similar glyme-based electrolytes.[31,32] It is notewor-
thy that carbonate-based electrolytes, such as ethylene carbon-
ate (EC)/dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and LiPF6 (namely, commer-
cial LP30), have a room-temperature conductivity of about 5V
10@3 Scm@1,[33,34] whereas our solution has a conductivity of 2V
10@3 at the same temperature. The slightly lower conductivity
value may be ascribed to ion association due to the character-
istic interaction between glyme solvent and salt. Nevertheless,
the abovementioned ion association still allows our electrolyte
to have an ion conductivity value that is suitable for lithium
battery applications. Figure 1D, which presents the lithium
transference number calculated by using DLi and DF deter-
mined by NMR spectroscopy and Equation (3), reveals a stable
value of about 0.5 over the whole temperature range; this
value is considered to be value for electrolyte applications in
lithium batteries.[35] This method does not take into account
the effects of ion association and is therefore used only as
a guide to compare behavior in electrolytes.[22,31]
The structure of the electrode materials S-graphite, S-MCMB
and S-MWCNTs is detected by means of XRD and reported in
Figure 2A, B, and C, respectively. The diffraction patterns reveal
the characteristic peaks ascribed to orthorhombic sulfur S8
(JCPDS 85-0799) and broad reflections of the hexagonal
carbon lattice (JCPDS-751621); thus suggesting that the high-
energy ball milling treatment is not sufficient for complete
sample amorphization. In addition, the results in Figure 2 sug-
gest lower crystallinity for the S-MWCNTs sample, whereas the
highest crystallinity is obtained for the S-graphite sample. The
presence of an amorphous phase is expected to improve the
composite electrode conductivity and enhance sulfur reten-
tion; thus resulting in increased rate capability and stability
during cycling in a lithium cell.[36]

Figure 5 displays the galvanostatic behavior of the various
electrodes in lithium–sulfur cells by using TEGDME-LiCF3SO3 as
the electrolyte. Interestingly, all cells reveal a stable trend upon
prolonged cycling; thus accounting for practical interest of the
synthetic pathway used for electrode preparation. Further-
more, the cycling tests provide evidence of differences in the
voltage shape and magnitude between the electrodes and
reveal an activation trend at the beginning of the test. For all
cells, the first cycle shows a limited discharge, which is fol-
lowed by a higher charge capacity that may be due to partial
self-discharge of soluble sulfur.[43] The activation trend during
the subsequent 20 cycles could be associated with kinetic ef-
fects at the electrode–electrolyte interface and progressive
electrode wetting with the electrolyte.[44] Accordingly, several
reports in the literature have attributed effects such as a low
initial capacity, anomalous discharge–charge profile during the
first cycle, and the increase of the capacity by cycling to pro-
gressive electrode surface wetting.[45,46] This phenomenon
allows electrolyte flooding into the internal part of porous
electrodes, such as those studied in our work, which progres-
sively become electrochemically active through contact with
the electrolyte. The relatively low electrolyte amount used
herein (i.e. 30 mL) may actually lead to activation cycles as-
cribed to the abovementioned wetting issue. However, the low
electrolyte amount positively affects the cell stability by limit-
ing polysulfide production.[45] Furthermore, the results in
Figure 5 suggest that the extent of the activation process is di-
rectly affected by the electrode structure, crystallinity, and mor-
phology (see XRD and SEM results in Figures 2 and 3, respec-
tively). The S-graphite electrode (Figure 5A) shows a steady-
state specific capacity of about 400 mAhg@1 (0.52 mAhcm@2),
a coulombic efficiency exceeding 95%, and a voltage profile
(Figure 5B) that reflects the lithium–sulfur electrochemical pro-
cess. However, the electrode exhibits substantial charge/dis-
charge polarization; thus accounting for the low capacity ach-
ieved in the lithium cell. The S-MCMB electrode reveals en-
hanced performance (Figure 5C), with a steady-state specific
capacity approaching 500 mAhg@1 (0.65 mAhcm@2) and a volt-
age profile (Figure 5D) characterized by lower polarization
with respect to S-graphite. However, the cell shows a rapid de-
crease of the efficiency from about 95 to 80% by the final
stage of the cycling test; thus suggesting partial dissolution of
the S-MCMB electrode by prolonged cycling, leading to a shut-
tle effect and consequently decreased efficiency.[47] The best
performance is achieved for the S-MWCNTs electrode, with
a very stable cycling trend and a steady-state capacity ap-
proaching 600 mAhg@1 (0.8 mAhcm@2) (Figure 5E). Further-
more, the S-MWCNTs electrode shows the lowest charge–dis-
charge polarization (Figure 5F), a working voltage centered at
about 2.1 V and a stable efficiency of about 95%; thus suggest-
ing this material as the most promising candidate for applica-
tions in advanced, high-energy lithium–sulfur cells. Therefore,
polarization observed for the cells studied herein is likely to be
ascribed to the nature of the materials employed for cathode
preparation and to the electrode/electrolyte interface, rather
than electrolyte conductivity and transference number, which
may allow limited polarization. Nevertheless, the very limited
material cost and simple synthetic pathway of the electrodes
may account for a slightly lower energy density due to cell
polarization.
3. Conclusions
Various sulfur electrodes, with natural graphite, MCMB, and
MWCNTs as the carbon hosts, were studied and compared for
application in lithium–sulfur cells of practical interest. The cells
employed TEGDME-LiCF3SO3 electrolyte and were investigated
by EIS and NMR spectroscopy methods. The TEGDME-based
electrolyte revealed suitable characteristics for battery applica-
tions, such as a high self-diffusion coefficient, excellent value
of the ionic conductivity over the whole investigated tempera-
ture range, and good mobility of the lithium ions, as reflected
by a lithium transference number of about 0.5. These charac-
teristics proved the high performance of the electrolyte for
lithium batteries. The study demonstrated differences in the
electrochemical behavior of the sulfur–carbon composites due
to differing reaction kinetics directly affected by the electrode
structure and morphology. Among the various materials, the S-
MWCNTs electrode showed the most promising performance
by
revealing
a
very
stable
capacity
approaching
600 mAhg@1(0.8 mAhcm@2), which was delivered at an average
working voltage of 2.1 V and led to an energy density as high
as 1260 Whkg@1 based on the mass of sulfur in the cathode.
Certainly, an increase of the practical capacity might be ob-
tained by increasing the mass loading and the sulfur amount
of the cathode. However, we are confident that the compara-
tive study herein reported, as well as the limited expected cost
and environmental compatibility of the employed materials,
suggests that the Li/TEGDME-LiCF3SO3/S-MWCNTs cell is a suita-
ble candidate for applications in a new generation of energy-
storage systems.
