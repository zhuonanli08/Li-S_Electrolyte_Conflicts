Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered promising candi-
dates for next-generation high-energy-density storage devices;
however, their commercialization remains hindered by several
challenges. Supramolecular macrocyclic compounds, owing to
their unique structures and properties, hold significant potential
for improving Li-S battery performance. In this study, the inclu-
sion mechanism of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) by cucurbiturils
(CB[n], n = 6,7) was systematically investigated. Density func-
tional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to analyze the
recognition capabilities of CB[6] and CB[7] for polysulfides. The
results indicate that both CB[6] and CB[7] effectively encapsu-
late polysulfides through non-covalent interactions. Specifically,
CB[6] exhibits a stronger affinity for medium-chain sulfur species,
with the most pronounced encapsulation observed for Li2S4. In
contrast, CB[7] demonstrates superior encapsulation efficiency
for longer-chain sulfur species, with the most stable inclusion
occurring for Li2S6. Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simu-
lations based on DFT calculations revealed that Li2S4@CB[6] and
Li2S6@CB[7] exhibit high stability in battery electrolytes. These
findings suggest that CB[6] and CB[7], as supramolecular mate-
rials, can effectively inhibit the dissolution and migration of
polysulfides in Li-S batteries, mitigating the “shuttle effect” and
significantly enhancing battery cycle life and charge-discharge
efficiency. This research provides valuable insights into the
optimization of materials for Li-S battery applications.
1. Introduction
The increasing energy consumption and environmental pollu-
tion associated with conventional fuels have driven the ongoing
search for alternative energy sources. Among them, rechargeable
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have garnered significant research
attention due to their high energy density (2600 Wh/kg), excel-
lent theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh/g), low cost, non-
toxicity, environmental friendliness, and abundant raw material
reserves[1–3] However, despite these advantages, Li-S batteries
still face several challenges in practical applications, including
active sulfur loss, low Coulombic efficiency, limited cycle life,
and self-discharge, which hinder their widespread adoption.[3,4]
During the charging and discharging process of Li-S batter-
ies, soluble long-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn, n = 4–8) are
generated. Due to concentration gradients, these polysulfides
migrate across the battery separator and reach the lithium
anode surface, where they are reduced to short-chain polysul-
fides (Li2Sn, n = 2–4). Driven by both the electric field and
concentration differences, the short-chain polysulfides then dif-
fuse back to the cathode via the electrolyte, where they are

can lead to uncontrolled volume expansion and lithium den-

Li-S batteries.[5–8] To address these challenges, researchers have
explored various strategies, including designing efficient and
stable sulfur host materials,[9,10] modifying battery separators,[11]
incorporating supramolecular frameworks,[12] and developing
atom-doped materials.[13] Regardless of the approach, the pri-
mary objective remains to facilitate rapid molecular transfor-
mation of LiPSs,[8] suppress the shuttle effect, and enhance
the exposure of adsorption sites for LiPSs.[14,15] In recent years,
there has been increasing interest in the study of macrocyclic
supramolecular compounds due to their unique structures, host-
guest chemical properties,[16–22] and multifunctional applications
across various fields, including materials science and energy
storage.[23–25] Several studies have explored the potential use
of macrocyclic supramolecules in Li-S batteries. For example,
Sun et al.[26] developed ion-imprinted macroporous membranes
(IIMMs) using crown ethers, which showed high selectivity for
lithium ions. In 2018, Yang et al.[27] used β-cyclodextrin poly-
mers hybridized with graphene as efficient sulfur host materials
to prevent LiPSs from shuttling, leveraging host-guest interac-
tions within the supramolecular system. More recently, crown
ether-based supramolecular covalent organic frameworks,[28]
polyoxometalate-cyclodextrin cluster organic supramolecular
frameworks,[29] and Keggin-type polyoxometalate ester-crown
ether complexes[30] have been investigated for their applications
in Li-S batteries.

Cucurbituril (CB[n]) is a class of supramolecular macrocyclic
compounds that feature a central cavity and two polar car-
bonyl ports at the top and bottom. Its structure, resembling a
pumpkin, is a hollow, gourd-shaped macromolecule formed by
n glycoluril units, where the number of glycoluril units deter-
mines the size of the cavity in CB[n].[31,32] The unique cavity
structure, coupled with its highly symmetrical and rigid archi-
tecture, offers substantial advantages for the construction of
novel supramolecular self-assembled complexes based on CB[n].
These features also enhance the encapsulation and recogni-
tion of guest molecules, which may include biomolecules, small
organic compounds, or LiPSs.[33–35] Notably, in 2017, Zhang et al.
demonstrated the use of supramolecular cucurbituril CB[6] as
a separator coating to facilitate the storage and transport of
polysulfides in Li-S batteries.[36] Despite extensive research on
supramolecular macrocyclic compounds in Li-S batteries, stud-
ies on cucurbiturils in this context remain limited. We believe
that exploring the application of cucurbiturils in Li-S batteries
through both experimental and theoretical research holds signif-
icant potential and value. Additionally, the precise mechanism by
which cucurbiturils inhibit the polysulfide shuttle effect remains
unclear. Therefore, it is crucial to deepen our understanding of
these mechanisms to provide insights for developing materials
that can effectively mitigate the polysulfide shuttle effect. Com-
putational simulations,[37–39] as powerful tools, are commonly
employed to address these mechanistic challenges.
In this study, we propose a computational method to inves-
tigate the encapsulation mechanism of LiPSs by cucurbiturils
(CB[n]). Given that the size of the CB[n] cavity and the diameter
of its doorway may selectively influence the specific recognition
of guest molecules,[40] we selected the experimentally validated
cucurbituril CB[6] and its homolog CB[7], for comparison. Density
functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to elucidate
the molecular recognition mechanism of CB[n] for polysulfides.
Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were con-
ducted to examine the storage and transport of polysulfides by
CB[n] within the battery’s operating environment. The aim of
this study is to demonstrate how these macrocyclic structures
can enhance the performance of Li-S batteries through theo-
retical calculations and simulations, to explore their potential
application areas, and to provide theoretical guidance for future
experimental research.
2. Computational Detail
2.1. DFT Calculations
In the investigation of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and cucurbi-
turils (CB[n]), comprehensive structural optimizations were car-
ried out using DFT calculations at the M06-2X/def2-SVP level of
theory. The M06-2X functional is a meta-GGA hybrid functional
that incorporates a high proportion of Hartree–Fock exchange
(approximately 54%). It is particularly effective in describing
non-covalent interactions, transition state energies, and main-
group chemistry, making it well-suited for studies involving
hydrogen bonding, π-π interactions, and molecular reaction
mechanisms.[41,42] The def2-SVP basis set was chosen for its
optimal balance between computational accuracy and efficiency.
Following structural optimization, frequency analysis was
performed at the same theoretical level to confirm the absence
of imaginary frequencies, ensuring the stability of the optimized
structures. Additionally, the frequency analysis provided thermo-
dynamic properties at 298.15 K and 1 atm. All DFT calculations
were conducted using the Gaussian 16 package.[43]

2.2. Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations
Suppressing the polysulfide shuttle effect is a key factor
in enhancing the performance of Li-S batteries. To investi-
gate the host-guest interactions between CB[n] and LiPSs in
commonly used electrolyte solvents 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and
dimethoxyethane (DME)[50] molecular dynamics (MD) simula-
tions were conducted using the Materials Studio (MS) software
platform.[51] All MD simulations were performed with the uni-
versal force field (UFF).[52–54] Previous experimental studies have
demonstrated that graphene serves as a highly conductive sub-
strate and a soft template for directing the growth of CB
nanoparticles.[36] Given that cucurbituril particles loaded onto
graphene are at the nanoscale, modeling tens of thousands
of nanocomposites is computationally impractical. Therefore,
our simulations primarily focus on elucidating the host-guest
complex behavior between CB[n] and LiPSs in electrolyte sol-
vents, whereas graphene is considered a structural support and
conductive substrate, as observed in previous studies. Since
graphene does not directly influence the host-guest interactions
under investigation, it is not explicitly included in the model.
In MS, an amorphous cell (AC) module was used to construct
a cubic simulation box containing a single host-guest complex
and 500 solvent molecules (DOL:DME = 1:1). The system first
underwent a relaxation process, followed by multiple geometric
optimizations and simulated annealing steps using the Forcite
module to establish a well-equilibrated simulation setup and
identify the most stable conformation. Finally, the system was
simulated in an NVT ensemble[55,56] with a time step of 1 fs,
employing the Nosé method[57] to maintain a constant volume
and a temperature of 298 K. Trajectory data was recorded at stan-
dard intervals of 5 ps, generating a total of 4000 frames over a
simulation period of 20 ns.

3. Results and Discussion
CB[n] represents a broad class of supramolecular macrocyclic
compounds, where n denotes the number of glycoluril units,
thereby determining the cavity size of each cucurbituril.[40]
Among these, CB[6] was first reported by Xie et al. in 2017 as a
supramolecular capsule capable of reversibly storing and trans-
porting LiPSs in Li-S batteries. The research team experimentally
demonstrated that a composite film[36] was successfully fabri-
cated using conductive graphene-guided CB[6] growth, signifi-

we selected cucurbit[6]uril (CB[6]) and cucurbit[7]uril (CB[7]),
hereinafter referred to as CB[6] and CB[7], as host molecules to
investigate their mechanisms of LiPSs storage and transport.

3.1. Frontier Molecular Orbitals and ESP
The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are fundamental concepts
in molecular orbital (MO) theory and frontier molecular orbital
(FMO) theory.[58,59] These orbitals serve as essential tools for
understanding chemical reaction mechanisms and predicting
molecular properties. As shown in Figure 3, the frontier molec-
ular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) of the host molecules CB[6]
and CB[7] are depicted. Both host molecules exhibit similar
orbital distributions. Specifically, the HOMO is primarily local-
ized on the urea units of the cucurbiturils, whereas the LUMO
is primarily localized around the hydrogen and carbon atoms
of the cucurbituril structure. Furthermore, the overlap between
the HOMO and LUMO orbitals facilitates charge transfer within
the molecule, enhancing its potential for charge delocalization
and reactivity. As shown in Figure 3, The ESP mapping is dis-
played alongside the extrema of CB[6] and CB[7], illustrating
the electrostatic surface potential characteristics of their molecu-
lar structures. CB[6] exhibits a significantly negative electrostatic
potential (−60.69 kcal/mol) at the upper and lower portals,
whereas a positively charged region (31.16 kcal/mol) is observed
near the periphery of its glycoluril units. The negative regions
correspond to areas of high electron density, primarily associ-
ated with carbonyl groups, whereas the positive regions may be
attributed to the presence of positively charged hydrogen atoms.
Similarly, CB[7] exhibits highly negative portals (−65.99 kcal/mol)
and a positively charged periphery near its glycoluril units
(30.81 kcal/mol). The green coloration within the central cav-
ity indicates near electrostatic neutrality, suggesting that both
CB[6] and CB[7] can effectively bind neutral or charged guest
molecules.
The more negative potential of CB[7] indicates a slightly
stronger affinity for such guests compared to CB[6]. However,
the suitability of a particular cucurbituril also depends on the
size of the guest molecule, which plays a crucial role in deter-
mining the preferred host-guest interaction. Further analysis of
the HOMO-LUMO energy gaps of CB[6] and CB[7] reveals val-
ues of approximately 9.63 and 9.62 eV, respectively. This indicates
that CB[6] and CB[7] exhibit nearly identical electronic properties
in terms of the HOMO-LUMO gap, which is a key parameter in
understanding their reactivity and stability. The comparable gap
values suggest that both CB[6] and CB[7] can participate in simi-
lar electronic interactions with guest molecules. Additionally, the
relatively large HOMO-LUMO gap implies high overall stability
and low kinetic reactivity, making these cucurbituril molecules
well-suited for encapsulating polysulfides in lithium-sulfur (Li-S)
batteries.[60]
To further investigate their electronic structures, the partial
density of states (PDOS) calculation was performed for cucurbi-
turils. The PDOS plots of CB[6] (Figure S3a) and CB[7] (Figure S3b)
illustrate the electronic state distributions of carbon (C), nitrogen
(N), oxygen (O), and hydrogen (H). While the overall PDOS trends
for both compounds are similar, subtle differences are observed.
The electronic density of states for C is comparable in CB[6]
and CB[7], with the peak in CB[7] slightly shifted toward higher
energy. In contrast, the contribution of N is notably enhanced
in CB[7], suggesting a more significant role of nitrogen in its
electronic structure. The contributions of O and H remain rel-
atively unchanged, with O exhibiting a similar distribution in
both compounds and H showing minimal contribution. These
subtle differences may influence their interactions with guest
molecules in distinct ways. By analyzing these structural and
electronic properties, we gain deeper insights into the encapsu-
lation and storage capabilities of CB[6] and CB[7] for polysulfides,
underscoring their potential applications in Li-S batteries.

the interacting gradient model for Hirschfeld partitioning (IGMH)

3.4. Electron Transfer Analysis in Complexes
Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis is a powerful method for
investigating charge transfer and conjugation interactions within

4. Conclusion
In this study, the structure, properties, and host-guest chem-
istry of macrocyclic CB[n] were investigated using computational
simulations. DFT calculations confirmed that CB[n] maintains a
highly symmetrical structure at the M06-2X/def2-SVP compu-
tational level. Its rigid, electron-rich cavity effectively coordi-
nates with LiPSs, the selected guest molecules. The interaction
between CB[n] and LiPSs is primarily governed by van der Waals
forces and electrostatic interactions. Additionally, energy analy-
sis highlights the strong potential of CB[n] as a host material for
LiPSs, a conclusion further supported by MD simulations con-
ducted in a solvent mixture of DME and DOL. Specifically, the
encapsulation of Li2S4 in CB[6] and Li2S6 in CB[7] demonstrates
great potential for optimizing Li-S battery materials. The mod-
erate interaction strength and stable encapsulation state effec-
tively inhibit polysulfide dissolution and migration, mitigating
the shuttle effect and thereby enhancing both the cycle life and
charge-discharge efficiency of the battery. This study provides a
theoretical foundation for the future design of lithium-sulfur bat-
tery materials and offers insights into improving LiPSs stability
through the rational selection of macrocyclic molecules.
Furthermore, future experimental studies could explore the
fabrication of novel battery separators by optimizing the mixing
ratio of CB[6] and CB[7], paving the way for enhanced Li-S battery
performance.
