ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) are regarded as significantly potential future-oriented power sources. Nevertheless,
one of the significant challenges that needs to be addressed in LSBs is lithium dendrite overgrowth. In this study, we have introduced
1% LiNO3 and 2% Y(NO3)3 (1% Li & 2% Y) additives to form an in situ solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer to overcome the
challenges of lithium dendrite overgrowth and enhance the electrochemical performance in LSBs. Including Y(NO3)3 (2% Y) as an
additive builds the emergence of the composite passivation layer containing Y2S3/Li2S/Li2S2 covering the Li anode. Y2S3 not only
effectively enhances the mechanical stability of the SEI layer on the Li anode, but also catalyzes the decomposition of Li2S at the
cathode. In addition, the incorporation of LiNO3 also contributes to an improved coulomb efficiency of the LSB. The electrolyte
formulated with 1% Li & 2% Y additives demonstrates a capacity of 711.9 mA h g−1 after 250 cycles at 0.5C, showcasing excellent
cycle stability. Furthermore, the capacity decay rate for each cycle is only 0.08%. These results validate the effectiveness of the 1% Li
& 2% Y additives in protecting the lithium anode effectively, thereby establishing the potential for developing environmentally
friendly and long-lasting batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, solid−electrolyte interface, electrolyte additives, lithium dendrites, yttrium nitrate
■INTRODUCTION
To effectively mitigate environmental pollution and preserve
biodiversity, it is crucial to pursue the development of green
and eco-friendly energy systems energetically.1 This entails
focusing on the vigorous development of electrochemical
energy storage technologies and exploiting renewable energy
sources.2 The pursuit of green development in both the
economy and society has resulted in a significant request for
energy storage systems with high energy density.3 In this
regard, lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have emerged as a
promising solution, boasting an impressive high discharge
capacity of 1675 mA h g−1. Notably, the energy density of
LSBs is markedly superior to that of conventional lithium-ion
batteries.4 By continuously advancing the research and
development of LSB technology, we can accelerate its
commercialization and widespread deployment. This promotes
the transition toward a greener and contributes to reducing
greenhouse gas emissions and preserving our natural environ-
ment.5
Nevertheless, the functional deployment of LSBs encounters
issues with the challenge of a Li anode. The overgrowth of
lithium dendrites covering the anode during repetitive plating
and stripping of lithium ions leads to issues such as low
Coulombic efficiency, short cycle life, and potential safety
risks.6 To address this, researchers are working on modifying
the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer through adjustments
in electrolyte composition or adding functional additives. The

of the anode.7 First, the surface protection provided by SEI
layers impacts nonfaradaic processes between anode−electro-
lyte and interface. A well-formed SEI layer reduces charge
conversion, minimizing the consumption of fresh lithium
metal.8 Second, SEI layers alter interface properties of Li
anode, preventing the overgrowth of dendrites as well as
inhibiting undesired reactions that could damage the anode.9
Third, inorganic materials are considered more suitable for
improving the stability of anode−electrolyte interface due to
their mechanical strength and lithium-ion transport proper-
ties.10 The desirable SEI layer should exhibit uniformity and
flexibility to withstand mechanical stress and repeated cycling.
In addition, research suggests that the type of ionic compounds
which present in the SEI layer, such as inorganic lithium
compounds like Li2O, influences its formation and character-
istics.11 Efforts are underway to develop SEI layers with
improved uniformity, mechanical strength, and flexibility. By
enhancing the stability and integrity of the SEI layer,
researchers aim to overcome the challenges associated with
Li dendrite formation and improve the overall performance
and longevity of LSBs.12,13
On the other hand, to improve the SEI in LSBs, researchers
have developed various active materials. One approach is to
incorporate functional additives into the electrolytes, which
provides a simplified method for modifying the SEI and
stabilizing the Li anode.14 Specifically, NO3
−was demonstrated
to inhibit the polysulfide shuttle in LSBs effectively.15
However, when used in ether electrolytes, the nitrate anion
may not adequately stabilize the lithium surface morphology.
Additionally, the nitrate anion can be consumed during cycles,
as the SEI layer undergoes degradation and regeneration.
Metal ions are another type of electrolyte additive that can
enhance the stability of the lithium anode.13,16 These cations
could be reduced by metallic lithium or react with intermediate
polysulfides during charge−discharge processes, forming metal
sulfide products. However, further exploration is still needed to
fully understand the effects of different electrolyte additives,
particularly metal cations, in the LSBs.17,18 Researchers
achieved substantial advancement in addressing the challenges
of LSBs by developing metal-based materials with unique
catalytic activity. For example, introducing La(NO3)3 into the
electrolyte can form a composite passivation film of
lanthanum/lithium sulfide on Li anode, which stabilizes its

This SEI film protects the lithium negative electrode from
polysulfide corrosion and improves the capacity, cycle

reducibility and undergoes fast electrochemical dissolution/
deposition reactions, resulting in significant changes in the
appearance of Li anode surface.20,21 Metal sulfides, especially

reducing the reaction energy of intermediates and improving

SEI film containing metal or metal sulfide on the surface of the

effectiveness in promoting the kinetic transformation of
intermediates in LSB reactions.
Herein, an in situ artificial SEI layer with Y/Li sulfides was
prepared using 1% Li & 2% Y additives in the electrolyte. This
kind of functional electrolyte could accelerate the reaction and
enhance the generation of the SEI layer. The nitrate anion was
demonstrated to effectively inhibit the polysulfide shuttle in
LSBs. However, when used in ether electrolytes, it could not
be sufficient to maintain the surface structure of the lithium
electrode. In this context, we primarily discuss the impact of
rare earth metal yttrium ions on the SEI film and electro-
chemical performance. With this improved electrolyte, the Li
anodes delivered discharge capacities of 711.9 mA h g−1 after
250 cycles at current densities of 0.5C. The incorporation of
yttrium ions into the SEI layer helps to improve mechanical
strength and inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites. This
results in enhanced cycling stability and Coulombic efficiency
of the battery.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To provide an evaluation of the influences of various additives
on the modification of the Li anode, we presented SEM images
in Figure 1a−d. These images depict the Li anode before and
after 200 cycles with the addition of 2% Y, 1% Li (1% LiNO3),
and 1% Li & 2%Y in the electrolyte. As shown in Figure 1b, it
was evident that the Li anode tested with the 2% Y additive
exhibits a split surface with several cracks. Conversely, the
SEM image with the 1% Li additive (Figure 1c) revealed a
nonuniform surface with light volume expansion. However,
when both the 1% Li and 2% Y additives were combined
(Figure 1d), the SEM image demonstrated a denser and
smoother surface of the Li anode with minimal crack
formation. This notable improvement effectively inhibited
the powdering of the Li anode. Furthermore, as depicted in
Figure 1e,f, the thickness of the SEI film measured 12 μm after
200 cycles with the additive. The energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) results of the SEI surface confirmed the
presence of sulfur, oxide, and yttrium. Importantly, these three
elements were uniformly distributed throughout the SEI layer.
In summary, the SEM images illustrated that the 1% Li & 2% Y
additives led to a denser and smoother Li surface with reduced
crack formation, inhibiting the powdering of the Li anode.
Additionally, the composition of SEI layers illuminated the
availability of sulfur, oxide, and yttrium, further supporting the
effectiveness of the additives.
To further investigate the impact of different additives on
electrochemical performance, we conducted tests using Li∥Li
cells for a duration of 1000 h at a constant current density of 1
mA cm−2. As shown in Figure 1g,h, the Li∥Li cells with
electrolytes containing Y(NO3)3 displayed lower overpoten-
tials and a flatter voltage curve at 1 mA cm−2, with notable
advantages observed when utilizing electrolytes with 1% Li &
2% Y additives. After 120 cycles, a polarization voltage
difference of 0.145 V was observed with electrolytes containing
1% Li and 1% Li & 2% Y additives. In comparison, the
difference was only 0.029 V with electrolytes containing 1% Li
& 2% Y and 2% Y additives. Subsequently, electrolytes
containing 2% Y and 1% Li showed a significant increase of
overpotential due to the consumption of electrolytes, resulting
from continuous accumulation of interfacial impedance caused
by ongoing side reactions. In contrast, electrolytes containing
1% Li & 2% Y additives demonstrated stable cycling
performance for the entire 1000 h duration without a
noticeable increase of overpotential. Based on these compelling
results, it could be inferred that the inclusion of Y-containing
compounds in the SEI layer on the Li anode not only reduced
overpotentials but also accelerated reactions. The experimental
data aligned with the proposed reaction mechanism, and the
gradual stabilization of the overpotential corresponded to
different stages of the experimental process.
According to the results of cyclic voltammogram tests
performed at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, all three different
electrolyte additives (1% Li & 2% Y, 2% Y, and 1% Li) in the
LSBs displayed similar peak patterns.9,25 Compared to those
with 2% Y (0.28 V), it could be observed that the potential
hysteresis (0.25 V) between the reduction and oxidation peaks
is smaller with 1% Li & 2% Y (Figure 2a−c). This indicated
that the presence of 1% Li in the electrolyte effectively
promoted the electrochemical reaction. Additionally, the
polarization potential with the additive of 1% Li & 2% Y was
only 0.06 V, which was smaller than that of the cell with 1% Li
in the electrolyte (0.08 V). This suggested lower polarization
and faster redox kinetics after adding 2% Y into the electrolyte.
Moreover, the oxidation peaks for the cell with 1% Li & 2% Y
in the electrolyte exhibited higher intensities compared to the
other cells. Compared to the blank electrode, the potential
hysteresis (0.282 V) is higher than others, and the polarization
voltage was 0.08 V, which was higher than that of the cell with
1% Li & 2% Y in the electrolyte. These findings indicated that
the 1% Li & 2% Y additives in the electrolyte enhanced the
electrochemical reactions, reduced polarization, and improved
the redox kinetics of the LSBs.26

The experimental setup involved an electrolytic cell, with two
pieces of lithium foil serving as electrodes and aluminum foil
acting as the current collector. Notably, no partition was placed
in the middle. The system underwent multiple charge−
discharge cycles, and each cycle was conducted at three
multiple current levels: 10, 20, and 50 mA cm−2, lasting for 30
s per step. According to Figure 3d, the cycling time reached
120 s (10 mA cm−2), and a minimal amount of lithium
dendrites was observed on the lithium cells when an
electrolyte-containing additive of 1% Li was used. Subse-
quently, as the cycling time increased to 220 s (20 mA cm−2),
dendrites in the LSBs showed significant increases. When the
current density reached 50 mA cm−2 (500 s), after two cycles,
it was observed that the dendrites grew more vigorously and
approached each other on both sides. This not only reduced
the number of active substances but also posed a risk of short
circuits. These findings highlight the potential challenges
associated with dendrite formation in LSBs, particularly at
higher current densities and longer cycle times. In contrast, as
illustrated in Figure 3d, the electrolyte containing the additive
1% Li & 2% Y demonstrated the organization of dense and
consistent SEI films during the cycling process. The surface of
the SEI film remained stable throughout continuous
deposition; even at a current density of 50 mA cm−2, the
SEI layer remained intact. Furthermore, as depicted in Figure
S5, it was evident that the voltage profile of 1% Li & 2% Y
exhibited smoother behavior, particularly at high current
density (50 mA cm−2). These findings highlight the promising
potential of incorporating 1% Li & 2% Y into the electrolyte to
promote the formation of a robust SEI film, improve cycle
stability, and inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites. As shown
in Figure S6, it was speculated that the gas evolution with the
two additives was not much different.
Based on the in situ observation of SEI film, it could be
inferred that Y3+ potentially played a role in the formation of
functional coating by adsorbing onto the exterior of the Li
anode. This interaction was believed to regulate the deposition
behavior of Li+ and suppress the generation of lithium
dendrites. To expand the investigation and validate reaction
mechanism of the electrolytic system containing Y(NO3)3, the
composition of the SEI layer after cycling was studied through
density functional theory (DFT) calculations.29,30 The DFT
calculation performed using VASP revealed the highest/lowest
unoccupied occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO/LUMO)
energy levels for Li+ NO3, Y3+ NO3, DOL, DME, and TFSI−.
Figure 4a displays the HOMO/LUMO energy levels of these
species, and the LUMO level of Y3+ NO3 was the lowest
among the studied species, indicating that Y3+ was easily
reduced in electrochemical reactions. In the base electrolyte
system (1% LiNO3), the Li anode suffered damage due to side
reactions occurring with solvents, TFSI−, and soluble lithium
polysulfides.31 These side reactions could lead to the
overgrowth of dendrites. In contrast, when the electrolyte
system contained the additive 1% Li & 2% Y, Y3+ was readily
reduced on the surface of the Li anode and actively contributed
to the formation of a stable SEI layer. This SEI layer effectively
protected the Li anode against solvent and TFSI−, inducing
side reactions, thereby inhibiting dendrite formation.32

Therefore, as shown in Figure 4h, it was inferred that there
was a competitive effect of the reaction between Y(NO3)3 and
LiNO3 with lithium metal. It suggested that Y(NO3)3 might
react with lithium metal because of the lower LUMO, leading
to the formation of Y2S3 and a small amount of yttrium
metal.49 This reaction was considered irreversible. Y2S3 had
acted as a catalyst in the oxidation reaction of LSBs, improving
the reaction rate, and increasing the concentration of
polysulfides indirectly. Meanwhile, there was a competitive
effect between polysulfides and LiNO3 in the reactions with
the Li anode. LiNO3 preferentially reacts with the Li anode,
forming Li2S/Li2S2.50 Li2S−Li2S2−Y2S3 was identified as the
main component of the SEI layer, which helps to passivate the
surface of the Li anode and reduce the reaction between
polysulfide and Li anode. Y(NO3)3 and LiNO3 had synergistic
effects in making lithium deposition uniformly.51 The presence
of Y2S3 in the SEI layer played a crucial role in stabilizing the
lithium surface by forming a uniform and compact SEI layer.
This protective layer acted as a barrier with mechanical
properties, which inhibited side reactions and prevented
corrosion of the Li anode.43,52,53
The evaluation of different electrolytes with functional
additives was conducted in LSBs. As illustrated in Figure 5a,
when the electrolyte containing 1% Li & 2% Y additives, the

■CONCLUSIONS
To sum up, we have recommended a facile approach of
elevating the electrochemical properties of LSBs by protecting
the lithium anode using functional additives of 1% Li & 2% Y
in the electrolyte. (1) Due to the competitive effect of the
reaction between Y(NO3)3 and LiNO3 with lithium metal, Y3+
to yttrium metal at the Li anode and organization of Y2S3
through interaction of Y3+ with polysulfides. Y2S3 actively
participates in the formation of a stable SEI, contributing to the
protection of the Li anode. (2) Y2S3 acted as a catalyst in the
oxidation reaction of LSB, thereby improving the reaction rate
at the cathode and increasing the concentration of polysulfides
indirectly. This catalytic effect enhanced the overall electro-
chemical performance of the battery system. (3) LiNO3
preferentially reacts with the Li anode compared to
polysulfides, forming Li2S/Li2S2. Adding LiNO3 not only
enhanced the electrochemical performance of LSBs but also
improved the coulomb efficiency of LSBs. (4) Y(NO3)3 and
LiNO3 had synergistic effects in making lithium deposition
uniformly. As a result of adding 1% Li & 2% Y into the
electrolyte, our approach has demonstrated a specific capacity
of 515.8 mA h g−1 at 2C, with a specific capacity retention of
62.14% after 500 cycles. This study offers a valuable approach
for enhancing the surface stability of the lithium anode and
elevating the electrochemical properties of lithium−metal
batteries.
