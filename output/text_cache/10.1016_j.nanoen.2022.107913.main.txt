An overlooked parameter in Li-S batteries: The impact of 
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio on capacity fading

A B S T R A C T  
The Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) system has gained a lot of interest as a promising next-generation rechargeable battery 
due to the high theoretical energy density, high theoretical capacity and abundance of sulfur. Many parameters 
of Li-S batteries such as sulfur loading, electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, type of the conductive network, electrode 
design, etc. have a profound impact on the capacity, energy density and cycling performance. But the effect of E/ 
S ratio on the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries is often neglected, although it is one of the most 
important parameters. A high electrolyte amount in the cells could decrease the energy density and increase the 
cost, therefore it could limit the practical use of Li-S batteries. In this work, we first presented a statistical study 
on the sulfur loading and electrolyte quantity in Li-S cells by reviewing 240 selected papers from the state-of-the- 
art Li-S research. This analysis revealed that the electrolyte quantity was not reported as often as the sulfur 
loading in the literature, and the reported E/S values differ by a fair amount from each other. Second, in order to 
explore the effect of different E/S ratios on the performance of Li-S cells, batteries with 5:1, 10:1, 20:1 and 30:1 
E/S ratios were prepared. Galvanostatic cycling with potential limitation (GCPL), electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) and galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) were used to analyze the effect of E/ 
S ratio on the capacity fade, impedance, internal resistance and ion diffusivity in Li-S batteries. The effects of 
different electrolyte penetration conditions on the electrochemical performance of cells with the same E/S ratios 
were also studied by preparing cells with different resting times. It was shown that E/S ratio has a strong in­
fluence on the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries, and an optimal E/S ratio should be achieved, which 
is low enough to minimize the free migration of active materials between the electrodes, and at the same time 
high enough in order to have a sufficient electrolyte wetting of the active material. It is suggested that capacity 
decay in batteries with low E/S ratios could be originating from electrolyte depletion, whereas the capacity decay 
in batteries with high E/S ratios could be due to the dissolved lithium polysulfide species in the liquid electrolyte 
and their diffusion to the lithium anode surface.   
1. Introduction 
Electric vehicles have rapidly occupied the market in recent years 
and begun to gradually replace traditional fuel vehicles in line with the 
goals to achieve a more environmentally friendly and sustainable society 
[1–6]. However, the growing demand in high capacities and energy 
densities requires new advancements in lithium-ion batteries as the 
capacity of electrode materials with traditional electrode chemistries 
has reached its limit [7–10]. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have gained 
a lot of interest owing to their low cost, high theoretical capacity (1675 
mAh/g) and high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh/kg) [11–15]. 
Despite these advantages, it is difficult to reach the theoretical capacity 
and energy density of the Li-S system, and to achieve a stable long-term 
charging and discharging process without significant capacity fading 
because of the electrical insulation property of sulfur, large volumetric 
expansion of the cathode, and free movement of dissolved discharge

eter to achieve high capacities and energy densities is the sulfur loading 
in the electrode since sulfur loading determines the amount of active 
material per unit mass and volume [22–24]. The quantity of the elec­
trolyte in Li-S cells represents another important criterion that con­
tributes to competitive energy densities as well as stable cycling 
performances [25–27]. Since the electrical conductivity of sulfur is low, 
it is usually embedded in a conducting network such as carbon, and in 
order to activate the sulfur in the composite electrode, a considerable 
amount of electrolyte is generally needed [28–32]. However, the extra 
weight from high electrolyte quantities could decrease the energy den­
sity of the batteries, and increase the cost [33–36]. Therefore, investi­
gating the effect of electrolyte quantity on Li-S battery performance is 
important in order to realize Li-S batteries in practical applications. The 
sulfur loading is usually reported in the literature; however, the elec­
trolyte quantity is the most overlooked or ignored parameter. In this 
paper, the sulfur loading and electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio data from 
the state-of-the-art Li-S battery research were summarized by reviewing 
240 papers published from 2018 to 2021. All of these papers were 
identified by the Web of Science search engine and listed in Table S1. As 
shown in Fig. 1a, the E/S ratio was reported in 106 of 240 papers, which 
account for only 44% of the total number of papers reviewed. For some 
of these papers, E/S ratio was calculated by the authors using the re­
ported values of electrolyte quantities and the sulfur loadings. Fig. 1b 
shows the values of E/S ratios used in these publications. As seen from 
the figure, there is a big variation in the E/S ratios reported or calculated 
from the reviewed papers, most of which are in the 5–20 µL mg-1 range. 
As shown in Fig. 1c, 199 out of 240 papers provided information about 
the sulfur loading, which accounts for 83% of the total articles reviewed. 
This result shows that most of the reviewed work attaches great 
importance to the sulfur loading but not as much to the electrolyte 
quantity. As recorded from the statistical data, a total of 281 different 
sulfur loading values have been used (some studies used various sulfur 
loadings). Among all of the different sulfur loadings used, around 47% of 
them are less than 2 mg/cm-2, around 63% of them are less than 3 
mg/cm-2, and only about 14% of them are greater than 7 mg/cm-2. This 
could be an indication that achieving a good cycling performance in Li-S 
batteries is more difficult with higher sulfur loadings. In addition, the 
number of papers that provide E/S ratio (or the electrolyte quantity and 
sulfur loading) is only around half of those that provide the sulfur 
loading. This shows that the effect of the electrolyte quantity on the 
performance was not given enough attention or was not the focus in 
most of the research reported. From the statistical data, a total of 129 
E/S ratios are recorded (some works used various E/S ratios). Around 
40% of the reported E/S ratios are less than 10 µL mg-1, whereas the rest 
60% of the reported E/S ratios are higher than 10 µL mg-1, increasing to 
as much as 100 µL mg-1 in some of the papers. More importantly, 
possible effects of the selected E/S ratios on the electrochemical per­
formance were not mentioned in most of the studies, and it is not 
mentioned why those specific amounts of electrolyte were used. 
In the literature, when the E/S ratio of the cells is not reported at all, 
researchers usually saturate Li-S cells with excessive electrolytes. One 
advantage of using excessive electrolytes is the increased number of 
cycles since more material could be activated by a higher amount of 
electrolyte penetrating into the cathode. In addition, if the aim is solely 
testing the performance of electrode structure, it might be reasonable to 
isolate the electrode from other parameters such as electrolyte quantity 
[37], therefore saturating the cathode with excessive electrolyte could 
be an option. However, in the Li-S system, electrolyte has a profound 
effect on the performance since the activation of the sulfur is maintained 
by the dissolved lithium polysulfides in the liquid electrolyte, which can 
also lead to free migration of these discharge products between the two 
electrodes and result in various problems [38]. Furthermore, the dead­
weight from excessive electrolyte could result in low energy densities, 
which could create problems in practical applications of Li-S cells. In 
addition, capacity fading, low Coulombic efficiency and high

to be considered to move the Li-S battery out of the lab for real field 
applications [39]. Some work has been done to study the relationship 
between the self-discharge behavior and E/S ratio of Li-S batteries. Chao 
Shen et. al. found that the self-discharge is closely related to the type and 
concentration of the polysulfides, and choosing the appropriate E/S

both C-rate and E/S ratio on the performance of Li-S batteries, and found 
that high C-rate with high E/S ratio would reduce the capacity degra­

with different E/S ratios was implemented by taking one significant step 
into consideration, which is the conditioning cycles or formation cycles, 
which are the first few cycles at lower rates to activate the materials. 
Batteries with varying E/S ratios were assembled and their perfor­
mances during both conditioning cycles and regular cycles were 
analyzed. In order to observe the effect of electrolyte quantity on the 
electrochemical performance of the cells more significantly, two 
different groups of batteries were assembled. In the first group, a resting 
time of 12 h was used. In the second group, a resting time of 6 h was 
used, and an extra separator was placed inside the batteries. For both 
groups, E/S ratio is varied from 5 µL mg−1 to 30 µL mg−1. The specific 
capacities, differential capacities, internal resistance and total imped­
ance values were obtained and they were compared to see the impact of 
E/S ratio. Analytical electrochemical characterization techniques such 
as galvanostatic cycling with potential limitation (GCPL), electro­
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic intermittent 
titration technique (GITT) were utilized to investigate the impact of 
electrolyte quantity on the performance and capacity evolution of Li-S 
batteries. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Electrode/battery preparation 
Acetylene black (Alfa Aesar, 99%) was used as the carbon source, 
and it was combined with sulfur (Sigma Aldrich, 99.998%) to achieve a 
1:1.5 wt ratio. The mixture was poured in dimethyl sulfoxide (Fisher 
Chemical) and they were heated for 3 h at 90 ◦C. After heating, the 
solution was rested until it reaches room temperature. Dimethyl sulf­
oxide was washed away from the consequent carbon/sulfur composite 
by using ethanol followed by drying the composite at 60 ◦C for 12 h. A 
slurry was prepared by mixing the carbon/sulfur composite with a 
binder (PAA, poly(acrylic acid), Sigma Aldrich) using a weight ratio of 
4:1, and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone was used as the solvent. After coating 
the slurry on Al sheets (Alfa Aesar, 99.45%) with a doctor blade, they 
were rested in a convection oven at 60 ◦C for 12 h. Circular electrodes 
with 16 mm diameters were cut from the sheet, and they had an active 
material loading of ~2.0 mg/cm2. The electrolyte was 1 M bis(trifluoro- 
methane)-sulfonimide (LiTSFI, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) 
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, 1:1 in volume) solvents with the 
addition of 1 wt% LiNO3. CR2032 type coin cells were prepared in two 
different groups, each group having cells with E/S ratios of 5, 10, 20 and 
30 µL mg−
1. The cells in the 1st group had a resting time of 12 h, while 
the cells in the 2nd group had a resting time of 6 h. Also, an additional 
separator was used inside the cells in the 2nd group. Lithium chips were 
used against the sulfur electrodes inside the cells. All Li-S cells were

2.2. Electrochemical measurements 
Batteries were cycled galvanostatically between 1.7 and 2.8 V vs. Li/

chemical impedance spectroscopy was performed by a tester (Bio-Logic, 
BCS 810) with an amplitude of 10 mV, and a frequency range of 10 kHz 
to 20 mHz. All EIS results were collected at fully charged state after 
30 min rest for equilibrium. Using the same Bio-Logic battery tester, 
galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was performed to 
calculate the internal resistance of the cells.

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 2a and b show the specific capacities at the first 30 cycles of 
Group 1 and Group 2 batteries, respectively. Capacity retentions for the 
1st and 2nd groups are presented in Fig. 2c and d, respectively. During 
this early cycling regime, the capacity of the batteries in the 1st group 
decay mostly in the conditioning cycles. The cell with 30:1 E/S ratio 
exhibits the greatest capacity in the first cycle (~1110 mAh/g). Both in 
1st and 2nd groups, batteries with the lowest E/S ratio of 5:1 exhibit 
lowest capacities in the first cycle. For these batteries, low electrolyte 
quantity may have resulted in insufficient number of carriers of electric 
charge. In addition, an inadequate amount of electrolyte can cause a 
nonuniform wetting of the cathode, which could decrease the amount of 
material that can be activated by lithium ions, leading to a low utiliza­
tion of sulfur in the cell. Thus, higher E/S ratio could be more beneficial 
in terms of sulfur utilization. However, as seen from Fig. 2a and c, the 
capacity retention rate was only 35% for the battery with the greatest E/ 
S ratio (30:1) as the capacity decreased from 1110 mAh/g to 390 mAh/g 
at the end of 30th cycle. The decrease in specific capacity during this 
cycling regime could be due to the constant source of lithium poly­
sulfides in the cells originating from the high quantity of electrolyte, 
because the decay in capacity is bigger than those seen in batteries with 
lower electrolyte quantities. For the 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios, there is a 
significant difference between the evolution of specific capacities among 
the two groups: while the capacities of the cells in the 1st group seem to 
decrease in the conditioning cycles (Fig. 2a), the capacities of the cells in 
the 2nd group seem to increase (Fig. 2b) when the E/S ratio is 5:1 or 
10:1. As the batteries in the 2nd group were rested for a shorter amount 
of time and they contained an extra separator, the wetting of the cathode 
by the electrolyte must have been impaired, resulting in a lesser sulfur 
utilization. Since the penetration of the electrolyte into the cathode 
would increase progressively from the first conditioning cycle to the last 
one, sulfur utilization should also increase gradually, causing an in­
crease in the specific capacities of the cells with 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios 
(Fig. 2b). 
The voltage profiles during the conditioning cycles of 1st and 2nd 
group batteries are given in Figs. S1 and S2, respectively. Discharge 
curves of the batteries in Group 1 and Group 2 are compared in Fig. S3 
for the last conditioning cycle. It can be seen from Fig. S3 that the upper 
discharge plateau at ~2.3 V becomes more apparent for the 2nd group, 
whereas it is relatively contracted in the 1st group. Since the upper 
plateau is related with the conversion of elemental sulfur into soluble 
long-chain lithium polysulfides, the capacity contribution from the long- 
chain polysulfides is smaller in the 1st group compared to the 2nd group. 
For both groups, plateau at 2.1 V widens and the voltage profile be­
comes more stable with the increase of the electrolyte quantity as seen 
from Figs. S1 and S2. The widening of the profile at the lower voltage 
plateau could imply that the transformation from long-chain to short- 
chain polysulfides is improved, because the lower discharge plateau is 
related with the conversion of long-chain soluble lithium polysulfides 
into short-chain insoluble lithium polysulfides [42]. As the lower 
discharge plateau profiles appear shorter for the cells prepared with 5:1 
and 10:1 E/S ratios, these batteries may have less Li2S generation 
compared to the ones prepared at higher E/S ratios. It was also noted in 
the literature that little or no Li2S production was detected on the 
electrodes when the material utilization was low [43]. With little or no 
production of insoluble lithium polysulfide species at the surface of the 
electrodes, and having less soluble lithium polysulfides in the

This is concluded from the big difference between charge capacity and 
discharge capacity originating from long charging times which might be 
due to active material migration between the electrodes. For instance, 
3rd cycle for the cell prepared with 10:1 E/S ratio exhibits a much higher 
charge capacity compared to its discharge capacity. This led to a lower 
Coulombic efficiency for this cell, and it might be because of the poly­

covered with short-chain lithium polysulfides could lead to an extremely

the cathode. The same phenomena are seen for 20:1 (cycle 2) and 30:1

10:1 in the 2nd group, specific capacities increase from the first cycle to 
the third one, which indicates that wetting of the cathode was impaired 
due to the shorter rest time and the additional separator inside the cells. 
From the first cycle to the third one, material utilization gradually 
increased. In contrast, for the cells prepared with E/S ratios of 20:1 and 
30:1, specific capacities decrease from the first cycle to the third one, 
likely because of their greater electrolyte quantity resulting in an 
enhanced effect of polysulfide shuttling as observed from the higher 
specific charge capacity values of the cells at the 2nd cycles. 
Cycling performance of the cells at a rate of C/10 for 300 cycles are 
presented in Fig. 3. Cycling performance data from all of the prepared 
batteries for each group can be seen in Fig. S4. Since the capacity decay 
during conditioning was largest for the cells prepared with 30:1 E/S 
ratio for both 1st and 2nd groups, they started their C/10 cycling with 
low specific capacity values of 480 mAh/g and 540 mAh/g, respectively. 
Therefore, the cells with the highest amount of electrolyte delivered the 
lowest specific capacities of 260 and 270 mAh/g after 300 cycles for the 
1st and 2nd groups, respectively. On the other hand, the cells with 5:1 
and 10:1 E/S ratios delivered specific capacities higher than 400 mAh/g 
at the end of 300 cycles. In the 1st group, the cell with 30:1 E/S ratio 
delivers 45% lower specific capacity compared to the cell with 10:1 E/S 
ratio after 300 cycles. Similarly, in the 2nd group, the cell with 30:1 E/S 
ratio delivers 40% lower specific capacity compared to the cell with 10:1 
E/S ratio after 300 cycles. Therefore, 30:1 E/S ratio led to the largest 
capacity decay in both groups. When the E/S ratio was smaller than 
30:1, it was seen that the performances of the cells were comparable, 
however, cells prepared with 10:1 E/S ratio demonstrated a better 
performance during the first 100 cycles of deep cycling in both 1st and 
2nd groups. The dissolution of the polysulfide species in the liquid 
electrolyte influences the electrochemical performance of the cells by 
producing insoluble short-chain lithium polysulfides such as Li2S and 
Li2S2, and these products can accumulate on the electrode surface and 
lead to poor active material utilization [44,45]. Thus, our findings

In both of the groups, the cells prepared with the highest E/S ratio of 
30:1 exhibit the highest Coulombic efficiencies, although their specific 
capacity values were lower as shown earlier. Columbic efficiency of the 
cells with 30:1 E/S ratio was 94% in the 1st group, and it was 91% in the 
2nd group at the end of 300 cycles. When the E/S ratio was 20:1, 
Coulombic efficiency was 90% in the 1st group, and 85% in the 2nd 
group at the end of 300 cycles. The cells that were rested for 12 h 
demonstrate slightly better Coulombic efficiencies compared to the ones 
that were rested for 6 h with an extra separator as seen from Fig. 3 by 
comparing the performance of 1st and 2nd groups. But the difference is 
not very large and it depends on the E/S ratio. For example, for 20:1 and 
30:1 E/S ratios, the difference in Coulombic efficiencies is small between 
the two groups. But for 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios, batteries in the first 
group have higher Coulombic efficiencies compared to the second 
group. Although the difference is not very much, there is a possibility of 
insufficient electrolyte wetting in the initial cycles that might be 
resulting in a higher concentration of polysulfides in the electrolyte, 
therefore increasing the electrolyte viscosity and affecting the Columbic 
efficiency of the batteries. For the same E/S ratios, Coulombic effi­
ciencies of the cells in the 1st group are higher compared to those in the 
2nd group. In addition, Coulombic efficiencies seem to increase with 
increasing electrolyte quantity within each group, and these values do 
not seem to vary too much throughout cycling for the cells prepared with 
20:1 and 30:1 E/S ratios. This is not the case for the cells prepared with 
5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios, as their Coulombic efficiencies decrease 
throughout cycling by a considerable amount in both groups. Particu­
larly for 5:1 E/S ratio, it can be seen that Coulombic efficiency loss was 
the biggest in both the 1st and 2nd groups. At the end of first 50 cycles, 
Coulombic efficiency falls from 85% to 75% in the 1st group, whereas it 
falls from 86% to 62% in the 2nd group. Moreover, the cells prepared 
with 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios exhibit the lowest Coulombic efficiencies 
(ranging between 55% and 70%) at the end of 300 cycles in both 1st and 
2nd groups. From these results, it can be concluded that the cells pre­
pared with 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios suffer from greater losses in 
Coulombic efficiencies after 300 cycles compared to the cells prepared 
with higher E/S ratios. Presumably, electrolyte depletion could be the 
underlying cause for the failure of these cells. Their electrolyte quantity 
is lower than those prepared with 20:1 and 30:1 E/S ratios, therefore the 
impact of electrolyte depletion should be more significant for these cells. 
Coulombic efficiencies of the cells prepared with 30:1 E/S ratio 
decrease in the first few cycles, probably because of the dissolution of 
lithium polysulfide species in the electrolyte causing a strong polysulfide 
shuttle mechanism. Therefore, these cells lose a lot of capacity in the 
conditioning cycles. Next, their capacities become more stable and the 
Coulombic efficiencies increase for both 1st and 2nd groups (Fig. 3a and 
b). However, because of this large initial capacity loss, the cells prepared 
with 30:1 E/S ratio have the lowest specific capacities after long cycles.

Although the variations are more apparent for E/S ratios of 5:1 and 10:1 
between 50th and 100th cycles, they can be observed for 20:1 (between 
50 and 75 cycles) and 30:1 (around 100th cycle) as well. Another sign 
that indicates those fluctuations happened at the same time is that they

after a certain time when the temperature variations happened, the cells 
with lower capacities completed more cycles compared to those with 
higher capacities. That is why the cycle number of the cells when the 
fluctuations happened shifts to the right as the E/S ratio increases. It can 
be concluded from these results that the electrolyte quantity of the cells 
prepared with 20:1 E/S ratio was optimum for the electrode structure 
and sulfur loading used in this work since they deliver highest capacities 
with minimal loss in Coulombic efficiency compared to the cells pre­
pared with other E/S ratios. Coulombic efficiency of the batteries in Li-S 
cells is adversely affected by the amount of dissolved lithium poly­
sulfides in the liquid electrolyte, because these species can freely 
migrate from cathode to anode, and once their concentration increases 
on the anode surface, they can diffuse back to the cathode and re-oxidize 
[22]. This phenomenon can be observed in all Li-S cells, but depending 
on some of the cell parameters, severity of the shuttle effect can vary. For 
example, if the sulfur loading in the electrode is low, the shuttle effect 
may not be very significant since the polysulfide concentration in the 
liquid electrolyte would be low, which would result in a higher 
Coulombic efficiency. Other parameters affecting the shuttle effect are 
the E/S ratio, polysulfide solubility of the electrolyte, and the type of the 
conductive network that hosts elemental sulfur. In the literature, there 
are various works focusing on trapping the polysulfides species in the 
cathode region and alleviating the shuttle effect [22], which would 
improve the Coulombic efficiency. However, in this work no further 
modification to the cathode structure after producing the acetylene 
black/sulfur composite electrode was applied to isolate the effect of E/S 
ratio on the polysulfide formation in order to understand the capacity 
fading mechanism better. 
Porosity is another important parameter that affects the performance 
of sulfur cathodes. Generally, active material utilization increases when 
the porosity is higher [46]. This might happen because of possibly larger 
transport path for the dissolved products which would then provide a 
better access to the carbon surface. However, dissolution/precipitation 
processes in the sulfur cathode also cause volumetric changes in the 
structure, and it was reported that pores of smaller sizes experience less 
volumetric changes [46]. In this work, we used commercially available 
carbon sources and solvents with minimal modifications to the cathode 
structure and used a straightforward method to dissolve sulfur in the 
carbon network. Therefore, we kept the porosity as a fixed parameter 
among the prepared electrodes. 
For selected cycle numbers, the voltage curves of the cells prepared 
with different E/S ratios in the 1st and 2nd groups are given in Figs. 4 
and 5, respectively. Both in 1st and 2nd groups, the difference between 
the charge and discharge curves (ΔV) were the largest for 5:1 E/S ratio, 
suggesting a greater polarization. For both groups, the upper discharge 
plateau is less noticeable when the E/S ratio is 5:1, and it is more distinct 
when the E/S ratio increases (10:1, 20:1 and 30:1). Also, width of the

Using the voltage profiles, differential capacity curves are generated 
for the conditioning cycles at C/50 (Figs. S5 and S6) and cycling at C/10

the voltage curves, and they are presented in Fig. 6. It can be seen that

increase of E/S ratio from 20:1–30:1 seems to increase ΔV back. For the 
same E/S ratios, ΔV is larger in the 2nd group which shows a greater 
polarization for the batteries with less resting time. It can be concluded 
from these results that inadequate electrolyte quantities could result in 
slower kinetics inside the batteries causing a greater polarization. 
Especially, the cells with the least amount of electrolyte (E/S 5:1) have 
the greatest polarization, and this could be due to sluggish lithium ion 
and polysulfide transport into the sulfur host structure, along with un­

electrolyte wetting. 
To investigate the influence of varying electrolyte quantity on the 
performance of Li-S cells, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
is performed. For 5:1, 10:1 and 20:1 E/S ratios, the EIS measurements 
during the conditioning cycles are analyzed and the results are presented 
in Fig. 7. All EIS plots consist from two semicircles and a tail. One of the 
semicircles is observed in middle frequencies and it is related to the 
charge-transfer resistance, shown as R3. The other semicircle is 
observed in higher frequencies and it is related to the resistance of solid- 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer, shown as R2. The tail of the plot is 
observed at low frequencies and it is associated with the diffusion of 
lithium ions in the solid-state [47]. R1 is the equivalent series resistance, 
which is the intersection point between the plot and x-axis at high fre­
quencies and it is related with multiple resistances such as (1) the ionic 
resistance of electrolyte, (2) contact resistance between the current 
collector and active material, and (3) intrinsic resistance of active ma­
terial [48–51]. The equivalent circuit model that was used for the fitting 
of the EIS data is given in Fig. S9. Nyquist plots that were taken after 
conditioning cycles for the cells with varying E/S ratios are shown in 
Fig. 7a. R1, R2 and R3 at the conditioning cycles are obtained from the 
fitting procedure and they are presented in Fig. 7b, c and d, respectively. 
For the batteries cycled at C/10, resistance values obtained from the 
fitting procedure are shown in Fig. 8. After 3 conditioning cycles, R1 is 
highest for E/S 10:1 as seen from Fig. 7b. This is not expected since E/S 
20:1 has a higher electrolyte quantity, and that would increase the ionic 
resistance of electrolyte due to a higher amount of polysulfide dissolu­
tion. This means that the high R1 value may be related with the contact 
resistance for the battery prepared with 10:1 E/S ratio. R2 and R3 values 
decrease during conditioning, and they further decrease during the 
cycling at C/10 rate for all batteries. Decline in R2 values is associated 
with the production of smaller amounts of insoluble short chain lithium 
polysulfides as the cycle number increases. However, after around 50 
cycles, it increases back again for all E/S ratios as shown in the inlet of 
Fig. 8b. This increase in solid-electrolyte interphase resistance (R2) 
beginning from around 50 cycles may be originating from degradation 
of the electrode due to the formation of a film from solid discharge 
products at the electrode surface.

Current interruption and charge/discharge steps are utilized from 
the raw GITT data and the IR drops are calculated for the determination 
of total internal resistances. Fig. 12 demonstrates the calculated values 
for the internal resistances during the conditioning cycles. All plots 
contain a peak resistance around 20–30% state of discharge (SOD). This 
corresponds to the part in the voltage curves where the upper discharge 
plateau at ~2.3 V terminates and the voltage begins to decrease. At that 
peak point, the concentration of dissolved lithium polysulfides becomes 
a maximum in the liquid electrolyte. As the battery keeps discharging, 
resistance begins to drop and it becomes a minimum. But then it builds 
up later again since the reaction kinetics slows down significantly as the 
solid lithium polysulfide production starts. When the battery is fully 
discharged, electrodes should be coated with solid lithium polysulfide 
species which blocks the transfer pathways and increases the resistance 
to another peak. As the battery starts charging, resistance decreases 
since solid lithium polysulfide species oxidize into dissolved lithium 
polysulfides. Next, the amount of soluble lithium polysulfides in the 
liquid electrolyte increases, and this causes a slight increase in resis­
tance. As the battery keeps charging, soluble long chain lithium poly­
sulfide species start to oxidize into elemental sulfur and the resistance 
decreases. As the sulfur turns back to solid, resistance increases to reach 
a peak at the end of charging process. Although all batteries have similar 
changes in internal resistance during charge and discharge due to the 
same type of electrochemical reactions, the magnitude of the resistance 
and the position of observed resistance peaks vary depending on the E/S 
ratio. The magnitude of the internal resistance decreases with increasing 
E/S ratio during both discharge and charge. This indicates a low ionic 
resistance of the electrolyte when the E/S ratio is high. In addition to the 
smaller magnitudes in internal resistance, the position of the resistance 
peak also shifts to lower state of discharge values. This shows that the 
capacity contribution from conversion of long-chain to short-chain 
lithium polysulfides constitutes a larger portion of the overall capac­
ity, since the conversion starts right after this peak where the dissolved 
lithium polysulfide concentration reaches a maximum.

that the batteries with less E/S ratio had lower material utilization due 
to the insufficient electrolyte penetration into the cathode structure. 
This was observed as smaller capacities and greater losses in Coulombic 
efficiencies compared to the batteries prepared with higher electrolyte 
quantities. When the E/S ratio is high, the amount of dissolved lithium 
polysulfides in the electrolyte could be higher and this may have caused 
active material loss throughout cycling since a higher amount of lithium 
polysulfides could diffuse from one electrode to the other. This may be 
the reason for large differences between charge and discharge capacity

The practicality of the batteries is contingent on several parameters 
such as the required energy density for the application, material cost and 
safety. There are multiple factors affecting the energy density of Li-S 
batteries. Some of the most important parameters are sulfur loading, 
material utilization and E/S ratio. As we prepared half cells in this work, 
we did not want to speculate on the energy density of a potential full 
cell. However, at this sulfur loading and utilization, the E/S ratio should 
be less than 10:1 in order to have a high energy density surpassing that 
of current technologies [52]. Nevertheless, since the cost of sulfur is 
much lower compared to those used in current lithium metal oxide 
based cathodes, E/S ratios higher than 10:1 with relatively less energy 
densities might still be useful for some applications if safety issues of the 
lithium anode can be overcome. 
In order to make real applications possible, the performance of Li-S 
full cells should be tested on pouch cells. However, research with coin 
cells is also important as it provides foundational understanding of the 
limitations of the Li-S chemistry and informs design strategies [52]. As 
shown in the present study, analysis of 240 papers in the field revealed 
that E/S ratios and sulfur loadings used in the literature vary signifi­
cantly. Even for coin-cell level evaluations, the reported results would be 
more valuable and informative if the E/S ratios adopted in these works 
were reported given their impact on performance. More than half of the 
papers that were analyzed did not report the E/S ratios, and for the ones 
that were reported, the E/S ratios varied greatly and thus were not 
comparable most of the time. In this work, we demonstrated that even 
for coin-cell level evaluation, the performance is greatly affected by the 
E/S ratio. Our work is important for understanding the mechanisms 
causing the capacity fading as we quantify the performance of the cells 
by reporting specific capacities, capacity retentions, Coulombic effi­
ciencies, various key parameters (from EIS results: Equivalent series 
resistance, SEI resistance, charge transfer resistance, from GITT tech­
nique: internal resistance, polarization levels, ion diffusivity levels) at 
different E/S ratios. 
4. Conclusions 
Electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, a parameter usually ignored in Li-S 
research, is shown to have a great impact on the capacity evolution, as 
well as overall performance of Li-S cells. Our investigations demon­
strated that capacity decay in batteries with low E/S ratios could be 
originating from electrolyte depletion, whereas the capacity decay in 
batteries with high E/S ratios could be due to the dissolved lithium 
polysulfide species in the liquid electrolyte and their diffusion to the 
lithium anode surface. The cells prepared with 5:1 and 10:1 E/S ratios 
suffered from greater losses in Coulombic efficiencies compared to the 
cells prepared with higher electrolyte quantities. Performances of the 
cells prepared with 20:1 E/S ratio were optimum for the electrode 
structure and sulfur loading used in this work as they delivered highest 
capacities with minimal loss in Coulombic efficiency compared to the 
cells prepared with other E/S ratios. This could be because the electro­
lyte quantity that was used to achieve 20:1 E/S ratio is smaller than that 
of 30:1 E/S ratio, which would act as a smaller polysulfide reservoir 
tank, relieving the shuttle effect; also, the electrolyte quantity that was 
used to achieve 20:1 E/S ratio is bigger than that of 5:1 and 10:1 E/S 
ratios, which could improve the penetration of the electrolyte into the

polysulfide migration to the anode, resulting in a decrease in active

supported by the voltage curves and differential capacity plots that 
increased E/S ratios could lead to a more significant polysulfide shuttle 
effect. Our results from conditioning cycles with the batteries including 
high electrolyte quantities suggest that more soluble and insoluble 
polysulfides could be created initially, and that might lead to a greater 
loss in active material during the following cycles, resulting in an overall 
decreased active material utilization. In addition, inadequate electrolyte 
quantities could result in slower kinetics inside the batteries causing a 
greater polarization. Especially, the cells with the least amount of 
electrolyte (E/S 5:1) had the greatest polarization, and this could be due 
to sluggish lithium ion and polysulfide transport into the sulfur host 
structure, along with unsteady reactions at the surface of the cathode 
originating from the lack of electrolyte wetting. 
The present study conducted a literature review and statistical 
analysis of 240 papers in the field and summarized their sulfur loading 
and E/S ratio data. The results suggest that the electrolyte depletion 
could be the cause of capacity fading when the electrolyte quantity is 
low, and dissolved lithium polysulfides and their free migration in the 
electrolyte could be the cause when the electrolyte quantity is high. The 
electrochemical polarization levels for different E/S ratios were quan­
tified, which helped to explain the ion kinetics and resulting capacity 
fading mechanisms for both early and long cycling regimes. The varia­
tions in electrolyte resistance, SEI layer resistance, and charge-transfer 
resistance for different E/S ratios for both early and long cycling re­
gimes provided us information about the cause of performance degra­
dation related to solid-electrolyte interphase layer. Finally, we reported 
internal resistance and ion diffusivity levels for different E/S ratios and 
explained their relationship with the dissolution-precipitation mecha­
nisms. The results of this work could be beneficial as it highlights the 
importance of E/S ratio, and they could be utilized for Li-S systems with 
various electrode structures for the optimization of the cell performance 
to realize practical applications of high capacity and high energy density 
lithium-ion batteries.
