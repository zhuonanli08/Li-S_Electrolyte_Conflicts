A B S T R A C T
The lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery is a very promising candidate for the next generation of energy storage systems
required for electrical vehicles and grid energy storage applications due to its very high theoretical speciﬁc
energy (2500 W h kg−1). However, low Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) during repeated Li metal plating/stripping
has severely limited the practical application of rechargeable Li-S batteries. In this work, a new electrolyte
system based on a high concentration of LiNO3 in diglyme (G2) solvent is developed which enables an ex-
ceptionally high CE for Li metal plating/stripping and thus high stability of the Li anode in the sulfur-containing
electrolyte. The tailoring of electrolyte properties for the Li anode has proven to be a highly successful strategy
for improving the capacity retention and cycle life of Li-S batteries. This electrolyte provides a CE of greater than
99% for over 200 cycles of Li plating/stripping. In contrast, the Li anode cycles for less than 35 cycles (with a
high CE) in the state-of-the-art 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3 in 1,3-dioxolane:1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL:DME)
electrolyte under the same conditions. The stable Li anode enabled by the new electrolyte may accelerate the
applications of high energy density Li-S batteries in both electrical vehicles and large-scale grid energy storage
markets.
1. Introduction
Li-S batteries have been studied extensively during the last few
decades, yet progress has been slow on critical issues such as high self-
discharge rate, the polysulﬁde shuttle mechanism leading to a low
Coulombic eﬃciency (CE), and low cycle life [1–3]. More than 80% of
recent
studies have focused on one electrolyte:
lithium bis(tri-
ﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a binary mixture of 1,3-diox-
olane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with LiNO3 as an additive
[1–8]. The binary mixture of DOL:DME was originally developed by
Mikhaylik and Akridge (Sion Power Corporation) for its excellent rate
capability at low temperature, while LiTFSI is used because it is one of
the most stable salts in the presence of polysulﬁdes and their corre-
sponding radical reactions [9,10]. Since the breakthrough discovery by
Mikhaylik in 2008 of the reduced impact of the polysulﬁde shuttle
achieved by the addition of LiNO3 [11], this salt has become a universal
additive in nearly every reported Li-S battery study [7,12]. The eﬀect of
LiNO3 on the passivation of Li metal surfaces has been thoroughly
studied [5,13–16], with the surface layer often described as one or more
LixNOy species, as ﬁrst suggested by Aurbach et al.[17] This surface
(passivation) layer is commonly referred to as a solid electrolyte in-
terphase (SEI) [18]. This layer is formed by reactions between the Li
metal and electrolyte and its composition is highly dependent on the
electrolyte formulation. An ideal SEI should limit or prevent further
reactions of the electrolyte, as well as Li metal dendrite growth at the Li
surface (during Li metal plating). Another desired property is that the
SEI only conducts Li+ ions, while preventing electrons from reducing
the electrolyte (i.e., it serves as an electronic insulator). This requires
that it have a uniform morphology and composition to ensure a
homogeneous current distribution and it should be elastic and ﬂexible
to maintain its shape during volume changes which occur upon plating
and stripping of the Li metal [18,19]. However, SEIs formed in most

of more SEI by decomposition of the electrolyte, as well as promote

during the last decade in attempts to achieve the formation of high
quality SEIs [20–23].
Although it is well established that LiNO3 is critical in preventing
the polysulﬁde shuttle, to the best of our knowledge, a careful study on
the eﬀect of this salt's concentration on the performance of Li metal
anodes and Li-S batteries and its fate after long-term cycling has yet to
be reported. In this work, we show that LiNO3 is very eﬀective in
forming an SEI layer on Li metal which is distinguished by the inclusion
of Li2O. Electrolytes without LiNO3 do not contain Li2O as an SEI
component. The greatly enhanced CE and prolonged cycle life of elec-
trolytes with a high concentration of LiNO3 strongly suggest that Li2O is
a beneﬁcial SEI component, acting to prevent excessive decomposition
of the electrolyte. During repeated stripping and plating of Li metal,
LiNO3 is consumed in a self-limiting manner as it passivates freshly
exposed Li surfaces. The morphology of deposited Li metal in the high
concentration LiNO3 electrolyte is found to be “nodule-like” with par-
ticle sizes in the range of ~5–20 µm. The SEI replicates the underlying
“nodule” particle (3D) surface structure and the SEI growth during each
cycle results in a layer of porous residual SEI which continuously
builds-up. The use of electrolytes containing high concentrations of
LiNO3 therefore is a promising means to signiﬁcantly extend the cycle
life of Li-S batteries.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Electrochemical measurements
CR2032 coin-type cells were used for all tests, with the exception of
the in-situ FTIR experiments and NMR experiments. The electrolytes
were prepared by dissolving the desired amount of salt(s) into the
solvent(s) in an MBraun LABmaster glove box with an Ar atmosphere
(< 1 ppm O2 and < 1 ppm H2O). Battery-grade DME and DOL were
obtained from BASF Corporation. The diethylene glycol dimethyl ether
(diglyme, G2) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (> 99.5%) and was
dried over 4 Å molecular sieves prior to use. LiTFSI was obtained from
BASF and LiNO3 was obtained from Alfa-Aesar (98%). These salts were
dried for four days under vacuum at 120 °C before use. The Cu||Li coin
cells were assembled using Li foil (250 µm thick, MTI Corporation) as
the counter/reference electrode, two layers of Celgard 2045 separator
with 75 µL of electrolyte added, and Cu foil (All Foils) as the substrate
for Li metal plating. The active surface area of the Cu foil disks was
2.11 cm2. For galvanostatic cycling, a capacity cut-oﬀwas used for
plating and a voltage cut-oﬀof 1.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) was applied for
stripping. The Li-S cell was assembled using a cathode prepared from
multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, Cheap Tubes Inc.) and PTFE
(60 wt% aqueous dispersion, Sigma-Aldrich) in a weight ratio of 80:20.
The cathode containing the MWCNTs and PTFE was rolled into a free-
standing ﬁlm and then punched into a disk (1.6 cm2). The total mass of
the MWCNT/PTFE electrode was 5.8 mg/cm2. The electrolyte used
contained the sulfur source in the form of dissolved polysulﬁdes: 50 µL
of [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2 + Li2S8 (2 M based on S) which is
equivalent to 3.2 mg of elemental sulfur. This mass of sulfur is what the
reported values of speciﬁc capacity are based oﬀof. The theoretical
nominal capacity of the cell is 5.36 mAh (1675 mAh/gsulfur). The anode
was a Li chip (400 µm thick, MTI Corporation) and two layers of
Celgard 2045 separator were used. The Li-S cell was cycled at 0.4 mA/
cm2 (200 mA/g) between 1.9 and 2.7 V which is approximately C/7
rate. A Lanhe battery testing station and a temperature control chamber
set to 30 °C were used to control all galvanostatic cycling protocols. The
cyclic voltammetry and linear sweep voltammetry studies were con-
trolled with a Model 600E potentiostat from CH Instruments.
2.2. Spectroelectrochemistry
A Thermo Nicolet iS50R FTIR spectrometer with a liquid Ar cooled
MCT high D* detector and a spectral resolution of 0.5 cm−1 was used
for the FTIR spectroelectrochemical measurements. The spectrometer
was housed in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox to ensure minimal interference
from oxygen and moisture. The measurements were conducted using a
home-built ATR electrochemical window cell (Fig. S13) similar to those
used by others [24], and a VeeMAX III ATR accessory with a 45° silicon
prism (Pike Technologies, Madison, WI). The cell was sealed onto the
ATR accessory and prism using a Kalrez® O-Ring. The angle of incidence
was set to 50°. The working electrode was prepared directly on the
prism surface as follows: First, the native oxide was removed using an
HF etch. The prism was then immediately transferred into vacuum to
minimize exposure to air and oxide formation, and 100 nm of Cu was
sputtered onto the prism surface. The Cu-coated prism was transferred
from the vacuum chamber to the glovebox for FTIR measurements
without exposing it to air. Ohmic contact to the working electrode was
made using a Pt pin to contact the Cu surface via a separate borehole
external to the solution compartment to ensure that electrochemical
reactions occur on the Cu surface and not the Pt pin. An Autolab po-
tentiostat (Metrohm) was used to make the electrochemical measure-
ments. The potential of the cell was controlled using a leakless Ag/AgCl
reference electrode (Warner Instruments). A Pt coil housed in a fritted
glass chamber (BASi, Inc) served as the counter electrode. 200 inter-
ferrograms were acquired and averaged to form one absorbance spec-
trum every 2.25 min. The spectra were then subtractively normalized to
the ﬁrst spectrum in the series to create a series of diﬀerence spectra.
2.3. Characterization
The morphologies of the Li plated on the Cu and Li substrates and
the residues on the Cu substrates after stripping were analyzed by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on an FEI Quanta 650 ESEM at
5.0 kV and 0.17 nA.
XPS measurements were performed using a Specs PHOIBOS 150
hemispherical energy analyzer using a monochromated Al Kα X-ray
source. The load-lock of the analytical UHV system is connected di-
rectly to a glovebox, enabling opening of coin cells and loading of
samples without any exposure to ambient atmosphere. Samples were
rinsed overnight in puriﬁed G2 (H2O content < 1 ppm) after removal
from the coin cell in order to remove residual salt, followed by an ad-
ditional ~ 1 h rinse in fresh G2 before loading into the UHV system.
Although it is possible that the rinsing process does modify the surface
speciation, the relative diﬀerences observed between samples are still
meaningful as all samples were prepared using the same protocol.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Design principles for LiNO3-containing electrolytes
After testing the CE for Li metal plating/stripping with the state-of-
the-art electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME) with and without LiNO3
(Fig. 1a), we found that a high CE (> 99%) is only achieved when the
LiNO3 additive is used. In the work of Li et al. more cycles were ob-
tained with 5 wt% LiNO3 than 1 wt%., suggesting that the LiNO3 ad-
ditive is consumed during repeated Li plating/stripping cycling [25]. In
this study, these authors reported that the maximum concentration of
LiNO3 in 1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME is approximately 0.75 M [25]. As
shown by Henderson et al. [26,27] at room temperature, LiNO3 forms a
solid crystalline complex when added to pure DME, even for dilute
concentrations, and LiNO3 is insoluble in pure DOL. The binary
DOL:DME solvent system is thus required to achieve even the relatively
low salt dissolution limit of 0.75 M. In contrast, LiNO3 is highly soluble
in the higher order glyme solvents with the crystalline solvates formed
having a low melting point, thus enabling the preparation of solutions
with high concentrations of LiNO3 [27].
Although various concentrations of LiNO3 have been examined up
to 5 wt% in DOL:DME, the concentration eﬀect of LiNO3 has typically
been masked or down-played with respect to the role of dissolved
polysulﬁdes on the plating/stripping behavior of Li metal [25,28]. The
main issue which has hampered studying the LiNO3 concentration ef-
fect in DOL:DME is its limited solubility in this solvent system. By
switching to a diﬀerent solvent (diethylene glycol dimethyl ether or
diglyme (G2)), we were able to solubilize up to 4 M LiNO3, thus al-
lowing an examination of an extended LiNO3 concentration range re-
lative to DOL:DME (< 0.75 M). The Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li
metal plating and stripping in a series of LiNO3/G2 electrolytes was
tested using Cu||Li half cells with Cu serving as the working electrode
(Fig. 1b). A roughly linear increase in the number of cycles attained
before a decrease in the CE is observed with increasing concentration of
LiNO3 from 1 M to 4 M. It should be noted, however, that the maximum
CE achieved with LiNO3/G2 mixtures is only ~ 96%. This is notably
lower than what is observed in Fig. 1a for the 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M
LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolyte and what has been reported for 1 M
LiTFSI + x wt% LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolytes [25,28]. We found that
mixtures of LiTFSI and LiNO3 are necessary to increase the CE of the Li

3.2. Characterization of [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2
To fully realize the beneﬁts of sulfur cathodes over intercalation
cathodes currently used in Li-ion cells, high loading (high capacity)
cathodes need to be used. This means that relatively large amounts of Li
metal will be stripped and plated, in excess of 15 µm. In Fig. 2, a Cu||Li
cell is cycled with the new electrolyte to a capacity of 6 mAh/cm2 (~
30 µm Li) at a current density of 0.6 mA/cm2 (C/10). Even at this high
capacity, Li cycles with > 99% CE (Fig. 2a). The CE only begins to
decrease slightly after 50 cycles (Fig. 2b).
The morphology of Li deposited onto and subsequently stripped
from the Cu surface is shown in Fig. 3 for the ﬁrst deposition/stripping
cycle. Here a new electrolyte was prepared as a reference without any
LiNO3 (1 0.4 M LiTFSI in G2) to study the eﬀect of LiNO3 on the CE and
morphology. Although the total salt concentration (LiTFSI + LiNO3)
plays a role in both the CE and morphology of deposited Li [29], this
composition was selected as a reference, since opposite of LiNO3, pure
LiTFSI has a limited solubility (< 2 M) in the G2 solvent. The physical
properties of these two electrolytes are shown in Table S1. Li deposited
from the electrolyte which does not contain LiNO3 possess a random
particulate and rod-like morphology (Fig. 3) commonly recognized and
categorized as dendritic Li. In contrast, the G2-based electrolyte with a
high concentration of LiNO3 shows a smooth, uniform nodule-like
morphology (Fig. 3b). This morphology is almost identical to that of the
Li deposited from a 4 M LiFSI/DME electrolyte which resulted in ultra-
high CE values of up to 99.8% [29,30]. Of equal importance is the vi-
sualization of the stripped surfaces. The insets of Fig. 3c, d show optical
images of the Cu surfaces after stripping the deposited Li in the two
electrolytes to a voltage cut-oﬀof 1 V vs. Li/Li+. A thick silver/grey
layer remains on the Cu surface after stripping in the 1 M LiTFSI/G2
electrolyte (Fig. 3c). This is “dead Li” which cannot be removed due to
electrical isolation. Dead Li is the direct result of a thick, insulating SEI
which forms on the deposited Li surface leading to loss of contact with
the current collector [31]. In contrast, a very thin, black ﬁlm remains
on the Cu foil after stripping in the [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2
electrolyte (Fig. 3d). The SEM image of this residue has the appearance
of a sheet which was presumably the thin and dense SEI layer coating
the nodular particles of Li metal. The corresponding CE measurements
for the 1 M LiTFSI/G2 and [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2 electrolytes
are displayed in Fig. S1. Only about 20% of the deposited Li is suc-
cessfully stripped in the 1 M LiTFSI/G2 during each cycle and the re-
mainder is built up as dead Li. In contrast, with the nitrate-containing
electrolyte, the CE is > 99% after the ﬁrst few cycles and the small
parasitic loss is related to the formation of additional thin, dense SEI
layer content during each cycle. This will be discussed in more detail
below.

In practical batteries with an excess of Li metal (i.e., excess anode
capacity to extend cycle life), Li metal will be stripped from and re-
deposited onto the excess Li substrate rather than a Cu substrate, as has
been used for the CE measurements. To more accurately mimic prac-
tical cycling conditions and estimate the long-term stability of the
electrolytes, Li symmetrical cells were prepared. In Fig. S4, Li//Li cells
were cycled at 0.5 mA/cm2 to a capacity of 5 mAh/cm2 (C/10 rate). In
this case, the increase in polarization was used as a criterion to de-
termine the practical cycle life of Li metal in the diﬀerent electrolytes.
Electrolytes based on the DOL:DME solvent system with and without
LiNO3 (1 M LiTFSI and 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3) were compared to
the optimized G2 electrolyte with a high concentration of LiNO3
([0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2). With no LiNO3, the cell with 1 M
LiTFSI/DOL:DME electrolyte had a very limited cycling life. The po-
larization began to increase after ~ 200 h and complete cell death oc-
curred (cell voltage > 1 V) after ~ 500 h. The increase in polarization is
attributed to the build-up of an insulating layer on the two Li metal
electrode surfaces [35]. When LiNO3 is used as an additive in small
quantities (0.3 M), the cycle life is extended substantially from ~ 500 to
~ 1400 h. Astonishingly, however, the G2-based electrolyte containing
a high concentration of LiNO3 (3 M) cycled for over 3000 h and only
began to show a very slight increase in the polarization after 2800 h.
This behavior is a substantial improvement relative to the cycle life of
the Cu||Li cell at high capacity (Fig. 2) which began to increase in
polarization after 50 cycles (i.e., after 1000 h). This is the diﬀerence
between exhaustively stripping all of the Li to 1 V and redepositing Li
onto a Cu surface for each cycle with the Cu||Li cells, while only a part
of Li is stripped and redeposited onto a Li surface in the case of the
Li||Li cells. Although the morphology of Li deposited onto Li (Fig. S5)
looks very similar to that of Li deposited onto Cu (Fig. 3), the Li surface
is quickly passivated by an SEI layer which prevents excessive reductive
decomposition of the electrolyte. In contrast, prior to initially depos-
iting Li, the voltage of the Cu electrode decreases from +1 V to a ne-
gative voltage when a fresh layer of Li is formed. During this time and
voltage range (+1 to 0 V vs. Li/Li+), the electrolyte can be electro-
chemically reduced as shown in Figs. S6 and S7.
To further demonstrate the reversibility of Li plating/stripping in
the new electrolyte, cyclic voltammetry was performed on a Cu working
electrode in a LiNO3 containing electrolyte (0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3
in G2) at 5 mV/s (Fig. S6). Irreversible peaks are observed during the
ﬁrst reduction scan and are attributed to the decomposition of LiNO3.
All subsequent cycles show very reversible plating and stripping peaks.

The voltage window for electrolytes containing LiNO3 was de-

the discharge cut-oﬀvoltage must be set equal to or higher than 1.9 V

of Jozwiuk et al. shows that LiNO3 in G2 acts as a stabilizing additive to
drastically diminish gas evolution in Li-S cells [13].
A combination of in-situ FTIR and ex-situ XPS measurements were
performed, providing complementary information about the organic
and inorganic components of the SEI layer, to better understand the
surface speciation that develops in this electrolyte system. FTIR is
widely used for characterizing the organic and inorganic components of
the SEI formed on Li anodes [38], but a mechanistic interpretation from
FTIR data is diﬃcult due to the presence of many overlapping peaks. In
contrast, the use of the spectroelectrochemical technique subtractively-
normalized in-situ FTIR (SNIFTIRS) greatly simpliﬁes the spectral sig-
nature and facilitates a mechanistic interpretation by displaying only
the vibrational modes which change during the course of the experi-
ment, thus enabling the correlation of these changes to both time and
applied potential.
The SNIFTIRS spectra corresponding to the in-situ electrochemical
reduction and oxidation of LiTFSI/G2 with LiNO3 are shown in Fig. 4.
The cell potential was swept linearly from 1.2 to −0.2 V (reduction
sweep) and then back to 1.2 V (oxidation sweep) at a sweep rate of
0.2 mV/s (Fig. 4a) while recording the spectroelectrochemical response
(Fig. 4b). Although the reduction sweep appears to be free of electro-
chemical processes in the potential range of 1.2–0.2 V, signiﬁcant
changes are observed in the spectra between 800 and 1200 cm−1
during this portion of the voltammetric scan. These vibrational bands
relate to the LiNO3 and LiTFSI salts [17], and most likely result from
changes in adsorption of salt species in the electrochemical double-
layer. Reductive breakdown of the electrolyte is shown commencing at
around 0.2 V, as indicated by a small negative current in the CV.
Concurrently, the intensity of the spectral features between 800 and
1200 cm−1 diminishes slightly (Fig. 4c) due to consumption of the
electrolyte near the electrode surface as it decomposes. As the potential
is swept negative of 0.0 V, a large reduction current can be seen, sig-
nifying Li electrodeposition. The corresponding spectra exhibit more
pronounced changes relating to reactions of the electrolyte and newly
formed Li metal at the anode. Speciﬁcally, broad absorbance bands
centered at 898 and 960 cm−1 exhibited a sizeable decrease in intensity
(Fig. S8). This is attributed to the degradation of the TFSI- anion via
chemical and electrochemical processes, such as electrochemical re-
ductive breakdown of the electrolyte, electrochemical reduction of Li
(which displaces some of the anion from the interface), and chemical
reactions of LiTFSI with freshly-formed Li metal resulting in the for-
mation of additional SEI layer content [17]. Several broad absorbance
bands centered around 1442, 1300, and 1124 cm−1 appear to increase
in intensity, commensurate with the decrease in intensity of the bands
at 898 and 960 cm−1 from the degradation of TFSI- (Fig. S8). These new
signals above 1100 cm−1 can be attributed to Li carbonates, N-Ox
species, and LixSOy species, resulting from the electrochemical re-
ductive breakdown of the electrolyte components, and subsequent re-
action of the breakdown products with metallic Li and LiNO3 [17]. The
spectra did not change signiﬁcantly during the oxidation sweep
(Fig. 4d), indicating that the observed spectral changes are due to the
irreversible deposition of the organic and inorganic SEI components
mentioned above. The speciﬁc assignments of the vibrational modes to
particular molecular species and functional groups was not attempted
due to the many overlapping spectral signatures of the species present
in solution.
The SNIFTIRS spectra for LiTFSI in G2 without LiNO3 displayed only
minor diﬀerences which can be attributed to reactions of LiNO3 (Figs.
S9 and S10). Most notably, the SNIFTIRS spectra recorded in the ab-
sence of LiNO3 lacked the appearance of the new vibrational bands at
1442, 1300, and 1124 cm−1. Broad absorbances between 700 and
1500 cm−1 are more intense for the electrolyte containing LiNO3 [17].
Accordingly, changes to the spectra in this region appear more intense
during the course of electrolyte reduction. The relative intensities of the
spectral changes observed in the diﬀerent electrolytes, combined with
the results from cyclic voltammetry, suggest that the presence of LiNO3
is directly related to the enhanced Li electrodeposition and SEI forma-
tion, as well as the increased CE. It also aﬀects the morphology of the Li
deposition (nodular vs. dendritic) formed in these two electrolytes.
To investigate how speciation develops after more extensive cycling,
ex-situ XPS measurements were performed on Cu||Li coin cells that had
been cycled 100 times (0.4 mA/cm2 current density and 1 mAh/cm2
deposition capacity) using both the 1 M LiTFSI/G2 and 0.5 M LiTFSI +
3 M LiNO3/G2 electrolytes (Fig. 5). XPS data acquired are from the Cu
electrodes for consistency with the FTIR results; however, we note that
Li electrodes exhibit essentially identical speciation to that of the Cu
electrodes (Fig. S11), which is consistent with SEM observations of si-
milar Li morphology regardless of the plating surface (Fig. 3 and Fig.
S5). The most notable diﬀerence between the two electrolytes is the
clear presence of Li2O on the surfaces cycled in the LiNO3-containing
electrolytes, as shown by the O1s core level spectra (Fig. 5c), which is
consistent with the highly oxidizing nature of LiNO3 and previous lit-
erature reports [17]. Quantiﬁcation of the various surface species
(Fig. 5b) also shows lower concentrations of both F and S on the elec-
trodes cycled in the LiNO3-containing electrolyte. Comparisons of the
S2p and N1s core level spectra (Fig. S12) indicate that this is likely the
result of less TFSI- decomposition in the presence of LiNO3. Also ob-
served in the N1s core level spectra is the presence of NO3/NO2, con-
sistent with earlier observations of LixNOy species on Li surfaces ex-
posed to LiNO3-containing electrolytes [15]. While these anionic
species are detected by XPS, we believe they play a minor role in the
stabilization of Li metal relative to Li2O since these former species are
very soluble in the electrolyte [37]. For the same reason(s) that LiTFSI
is detected with both electrolytes, it is likely that some LiNO3/LiNO2
(LixNOy) is trapped amongst the various SEI components or that
washing the electrodes after cycling did not fully remove all of the salts.
As shown in Fig. S7b, the soluble LiNO2, produced by the reaction
between LiNO3 and Li metal, can be oxidized at the positive electrode
[37]. This clearly indicates that LiNO2 lacks the insolubility require-
ment of a robust SEI component. For the complex chemistry of Li-O2
batteries, the function of LiNO3/LiNO2/Li2O has been thoroughly stu-
died by others. It is generally agreed that LiNO2 is a soluble electrolyte
component, whereas the insoluble Li2O beneﬁcially protects the Li
metal [37,39,40]. Based on the combination of NMR, FTIR, and XPS
results here, we propose that Li2O is the key SEI component responsible
for the improved performance of LiNO3-containing electrolytes in Li-S
batteries.

3.3. Performance of a Li-S battery catholyte
Similar to the increased solubility of LiNO3 in G2 over DOL:DME,
we found that the G2 solvent also highly favors the solvation of Li2Sn
polysulﬁdes (where 4 ≤n ≤8). The presence of a high concentration
of NO3
- anions increases the solubility of polysulﬁdes relative to com-
parable solutions with only TFSI- anions. The enhanced solubility of
polysulﬁdes in [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2 relative to 1 M LiTFSI/
G2 is thought to be due to the higher ionic association strength of LiNO3
(than for LiTFSI) [27], similar to LiCF3SO3 in the work of Pan et al. [41]
In fact, we were able to dissolve up to ~ 4 M Li2S8 (based on based on S)
in the [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2 electrolyte. The combination of
high CE Li plating/stripping and a high concentration of dissolved
polysulﬁdes allows us to cycle Li-S batteries with the active sulfur
originating in solution. This concept has been tested in the past
[41–44], but was found to have limitations of low cycle life and low
energy densities, owing to the limited CE of the Li plating/stripping and
limited concentration of soluble active S. With the electrolyte for-
mulation reported here, however, we are able to cycle Li-S cells with
carbon electrodes (containing no sulfur initially) with a low capacity
fade rate and ~ 100% CE (Fig. 6). The ﬁrst cycle is excluded in Fig. 6a
because the Li2S8 in the electrolyte was oxidized ﬁrst to elemental
sulfur (S8
2-→S8 + 2 e-) before full capacity (based on the initial S
content in the electrolyte) and stable cycling could occur. The initial
capacity (second cycle) of this cell is 1349 mAh/g (2.69 mAh/cm2) and
after 100 cycles it is 1215 mAh/g (2.43 mAh/cm2), corresponding to a
capacity retention of 90%. Typically, a drastic fade in capacity is
observed over the ﬁrst few cycles for Li-S batteries using conventional
electrolytes. This is thought to be due to the dissolution of a certain
portion of sulfur, in the form of polysulﬁdes, from the cathode into the
electrolyte during discharging/charging which is not able to deposit
back onto/into the cathode. The redistribution and loss of sulfur in the
cathode can be completely avoided by starting with sulfur in the elec-
trolyte. During the ﬁrst discharge/charge cycle of the cell in Fig. 6, the
active sulfur is deposited from the electrolyte in the form of solid Li2S/
S. Using this method, the limiting factor is the capacity of the sulfur (or
Li2S) with which the carbon electrodes can maintain a suﬃcient elec-
trically conductive network. This is in contrast to traditional methods,
starting with a sulfur (or Li2S) loaded cathode, which are typically ﬁlled
with excess S (or Li2S) which is electrochemically inactive due to a lack
of electrical and/or ionic contact (with the current collector and elec-
trolyte, respectively). This under-utilized sulfur in electrodes prepared
by traditional methods is reﬂected in a lower capacity (than the theo-
retical capacity (1675 mAh/g S)) on the ﬁrst cycle and extensive ca-
pacity fade during subsequent cycles. This outstanding performance
using a catholyte is only possible with an electrolyte that allows Li
metal plating/stripping to occur with exceptionally high CE.
4. Conclusions
The prevalent electrolytes used for Li-S batteries are based on the
DOL:DME binary solvent system with LiNO3 as an additive to prevent
the polysulﬁde shuttle mechanism. The SEI layer formed on the Li
anode surface in the presence of LiNO3 is very stable and the main

electrolyte co-salt, LiTFSI, and the solvent (G2 in the present study), it
also clearly inhibits the reduction of soluble polysulﬁde species by the
Li metal anode. This, in turn, prevents the polysulﬁde shuttle me-
chanism and results in nearly a 100% CE for Li-S cells cycled with a
catholyte (i.e., S active material dissolved in the electrolyte) and re-
markable capacity retention.
Here, we have shown that LiNO3 is consumed during cycling by
chemical reactivity with Li metal. When used as an additive (i.e., in
small amounts), as is the case for the state-of-the-art DOL:DME elec-
trolyte, the beneﬁcial eﬀect of LiNO3 in stabilizing the SEI is limited.
The SEI forms (and reforms) during each cycle, which relatively rapidly
consumes all of the additive. Li-S batteries with a high CE after
extensive cycling are enabled, however, by an electrolyte which con-
tains a high concentration of LiNO3 in addition to a second supporting
salt to increase the ionic conductivity (LiTFSI). The high concentration
of LiNO3 serves two purposes: 1) to passivate the Li metal negative
electrode, thus enabling > 99% CE for Li plating/stripping for an ex-
tended number of cycles, and 2) to increase the solubility of Li poly-
sulﬁdes (Li2Sn where 4 ≤n ≤8), thereby allowing the discharge/
charge process to occur via the liquid phase (electrolyte) rather than the
reversible solid-state conversion of sulfur (S) to Li sulﬁde (Li2S).
One of the critical challenges with Li metal anodes is the continuous
consumption of the electrolyte during cycling. Ideally, as with graphite
anodes, a stable SEI would form during the ﬁrst few cycles which would
inhibit any further breakdown of salt/solvent components. In this case, the

continuously builds up during cycling. Strategies to enable practical, long-

also on controlling the morphology of Li metal deposits such that the SEI
does not need to grow each cycle and thus the exposure of fresh Li surfaces
to the electrolyte can be avoided.
