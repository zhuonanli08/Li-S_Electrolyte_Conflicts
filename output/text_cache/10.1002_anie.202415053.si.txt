Experimental Procedures  
Preparation of electrolytes.  
The 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, purity > 99%) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, purity > 99.8%) were 
purchased from Macklin and MREDA, respectively. The hexamethylphosphoramide (HMPA, 99%),

Lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)-imide (LiTFSI, 99.5%) and lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99%) were obtained 
from Aladdin. All solvents were dried with activated molecular sieves (4 Å) for three days before using. 
The LiTFSI and LiNO3 were dried for 48 hours under vacuum at 130 °C. The electrolytes were prepared 
inside an argon-filled glovebox. The base electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.5 M LiTFSI and 0.8 
M LiNO3 in the DOL/DME mixed solvent (1:1, v/v, denoted as DD). To prepare the DD_HMPA, 
DD_TPPO, DD_DMSO, and DD_DM electrolytes, the HMPA, TPPO, DMSO, and DMF were mixed 
with the base DD electrolyte with a volume fraction of 8% (unless stated otherwise).  
Preparation of sulfur cathodes.  
The graphene/sulfur composite with sulfur loading of 80 wt% (G/S-80%) was prepared by melt diffusion 
method by mixing the graphene and sulfur powder at 155 °C for 12 hours. The graphene used here was 
synthesized in a way according to our previous report.[1] The sulfur cathodes were prepared by casting 
the homogeneous slurry prepared from G/S, Super P, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) in an 8:1:1 
weight ratio in N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP) onto carbon-coated aluminum foils, followed by vacuum 
heating of the electrode at 60 °C for 12 hours. The dried electrodes were cut into discs with a diameter of 
10 mm for cell assembly. 
Preparation of polysulfide solutions.  
Sublimed sulfur powder (S, 99.95%) and lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin and 
Macklin, respectively. The lithium polysulfide solutions were prepared in an argon-filled glovebox by 
stirring stoichiometric amounts of Li2S and S powders at 60 °C for 24 hours to achieve the nominal 
stoichiometry of Li2S6. The solvent of DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) or DOL/DME/X (X=HMPA, TPPO, DMSO, 
DMF) was used for the preparation of Li2S6 solution for UV-vis measurements. The Li2S6 catholyte 
solutions for the chronoamperogram experiments were prepared by adding 0.5 M LiTFSI and 0.8 M 
LiNO3 to the aforementioned lithium polysulfide solutions. 
Electrochemical measurements.  
The electrochemical characteristics of Li-S cells employing different electrolytes were evaluated in 
CR2032 coin cells. The electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratios of 10 μL mgsulfur−1, 5.0 μL mgsulfur−1 or 4.0 μL

mgsulfur−1 were used for electrochemical cycling tests as specified. A Celgard 2500 separator and a lithium 
metal foil anode were used for each cell. All cell assembly procedures were conducted inside an argon-
filled glovebox. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were carried out on the Gamry electrochemical station 
(IFC1010) within a voltage window of 1.6-2.7 V with the scan rates ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1. 
Galvanostatic cycling tests were performed using the Neware system (CT-4008Tn) with a voltage 
window of 1.7-2.6 V. The Li|Cu cells were assembled using a Li metal negative electrode and a Cu foil 
positive electrode. The charge/discharge tests were carried out with a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and 
an areal capacity of 1.0 mA h cm−2 with a charging voltage cutoff of 1 V. The electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) measurement of the Li-S cells was performed on a Garmy electrochemical station 
(IFC1010) using a 10 mV amplitude with the frequency between 1 Hz and 100 kHz. The Li2S cathodes 
were prepared by dropping 15 μL of Li2S suspension (in DOL, 50 mg mL−1) onto the carbon paper (10 
mm diameter). 20 μL of electrolyte was added in each cell. The cells were initially charged to 4.0 V at 
0.05 C, followed by charge/discharge within a voltage range of 1.7-2.6 V. 
Physiochemical characterizations.  
The field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, SU8220) was used to analyse the 
microstructure of the Li metal anodes and the Li2S deposition. The XPS characterization was carried out 
on a high-resolution Kratos AXIS 165 X-ray photoelectron spectrometer using monochromic Al Kα 
radiation. Prior to analysis, the electrodes were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox and then transferred 
to the XPS using an air-tight transfer system to prevent exposure to air or moisture. Raman spectroscopy 
was carried out on a Micro Raman imaging spectrometer (DXRxi) employing a 532 nm laser for 
excitation. The size of the laser spot was 10 μm2, and the spatial resolution was 3 μm. The NMR 
measurements of the electrolytes was performed on a Bruker AV-400 NMR spectrometer using coaxial 
NMR tube with the 1 M LiCl-D2O adopted as the internal standard. The 7Li chemical shift was referenced 
to 1 M LiCl solution at 0 ppm, and the 1H NMR spectra were recorded in CDCl3 as the internal standard. 
The UV-vis spectra of the Li2S6 solutions were collected on the Shimadzu UV3600Plus UV-VIS-NIR 
spectrophotometer. 
Nucleation of lithium sulfide.  
Li2S nucleation on carbon paper (CP, HCP020N) current collectors (as the working electrode) were 
investigated at 25 °C. The cells were assembled using the Li2S6 catholyte solution and a piece of lithium 
foil serving as the counter electrode. The CP electrode and the Li counter electrode were separated by a 
Celgard 2500 separator. A volume of 20 μL of Li2S6 catholyte was applied onto the CP, while 20 μL of 
the corresponding Li2S6-free electrolyte was added to the anode compartment. At 25 °C, the cells were 
pre-discharge to 2.07 V at 0.05 C, followed by a potentiostatic hold at 2.02 V to allow for the nucleation 
and growth of Li2S. The potentiostatic discharge was terminated when the current dropped below 10−5 A.

Computational simulations.  
The ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio 
Simulation Package (VASP5.4.4),[3] employing the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. The 
Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was employed to describe the exchange-correlation energy. 
[4] The long-range van der Waals (vdW) dispersion interactions were accounted for using the DFT-D3 
method developed by Grimme and co-workers. [5] The plane-wave energy cutoff was set as 400 eV, and 
the Brillouin zone was sampled at the Γ point only. Two systems, DD_HMPA electrolyte and DD 
electrolyte, containing 696 and 692 atoms, respectively, were simulated. To ensure the densities in the 
simulation are close to the experimentally measurements, the simulation boxes were set to cuboid with 
side lengths of 30.14, 15.07, 15.07 Å for the DD_HMPA system and 30.17, 15.08, 15.08 Å for the DD 
system. The temperature was set at 298 K, consistent with the experimental conditions. The systems were 
equilibrated at 298 K in the canonical ensemble (NVT) for 100 ps using a time step of 1 fs.[6] The Nose–
Hoover thermostat was employed to keep a constant temperature.7 The DFT calculations were conducted 
using the Gaussian 16 software package. The optimum structures were optimized by B3LYP method in 
combination with the 6-31G (d, p) basis set and the more precise energy was calculated by same method 
in combination with the 6-311 G++ (d, p) basis set. For Li2S6 coordination complexes, the binding energy 
between the components was calculated as: Eb=EA+EB-Etotal, where Etotal, EA, and EB are the total energy 
of A-B configuration, A component, and B component, respectively, where A and B can refer to Li2S6, 
Li[S3]• and solvent molecules. 
Preparation of the pouch cells.  
The cathode slurry was prepared by mixing the G/S-80%, carbon nanotube, Super P, and LA133 with a 
weight ratio of 85:4:6:5 in water. The double-sided sulfur cathodes were prepared by casting the 
homogeneous slurry onto carbon-coated Al foil, followed by 50 °C heating for 12 hours. The sulfur

The in-situ UV−vis spectroscopy measurements 
A 10 mm cuvette was used as body for the in-situ UV-vis cell. The working electrode was prepared by 
coating the cathode slurry composed of graphene/S, super P, and PVDF with mass ratio of 8:1:1 on the 
Mo foil current collector. The S loading is about 1 mg. The counter electrode was prepared by rolling the 
lithium metal foil on a Mo foil current collector. The light source focuses on the electrolyte part between 
the two electrodes. The cell was discharged at the rate of 0.15 C. UV-vis spectra are collected every 20 
minutes in the range of 800-200 mm with a scan rate of 600 nm min−1.

Figure S6. DFT calculations showing the complexes of solvent molecule with Li[S3]•/Li2S6 and their 
binding energy. (a) solvent-Li[S3]• and (b)solvent-Li2S6. The solvents interact with the Li2S6 primarily 
through solvent-Li+ bonding (Li+ is a Lewis acid). The high-DN solvents generally show higher binding 
energies with Li+ than the DME or DOL does, indicating stronger solvent-Li+ interactions, which again 
leads to softer Lewis acidity for solvent-Li+. Given that S62- is a harder Lewis base (than [S3]•−), the DME 
or DOL derived solvent-Li+ complex would form a stronger complex with S62- than the high-DN solvents 
do. In contrast, the high-DN solvents show stronger solvent-Li+ binding than DME, and therefore the 
formed solvent-Li+ complex shows weaker Lewis acidity (softer Lewis acid), which in turn shows 
stronger binding with the soft Lewis base [S3]•- (S6
2- → 2[S3]•−).[7] This has been widely adopted to explain 
the stabilization of lithium superoxide LiO2 (Li+-O2-) over lithium peroxide Li2O2 (Li+-O22-) by using 
high-DN electrolyte solvents in Li-O2 batteries.[8] Note that O2- is a softer base than O22-. Specifically, 
HMPA shows the stronger coordination with Li+ than DME or DOL does, resulting in a softer Lewis acid, 
which stabilizes the [S3]• radicals by forming stable HMPA-Li[S3]• complex. The stabilized HMPA-
Li[S3]• complex can prevent extensive colliding between [S3]• free radicals, therefore prolonging their life 
time.

Figure S8a shows the evolution of UV-vis spectra of the catholyte in the cell in the DD_HMPA 
electrolyte at different stages (which are noted as stage I, II, and III). We can observe that [S3]•− is detected 
along with the long- and medium-chain polysulfides (S62-, S42-) at the initial stage, and they all increase 
prominently as it continues (stage I). Subsequently, the concentration of medium-chain S42- decrease 
slightly, indicating its further reduction to short-chain polysulfides. Impressively, the concentration of 
[S3]•− free radicals remains relatively constant for a rather long period of time during stage II, indicating 
the possible presence of complex disproportionation reactions (stage II). On stage III, the concentration 
of [S3]•− free radicals drops rapidly, along with decrease in S42- and S62. In contrast, in the cell using the 
DD electrolyte (Figure S8b), there is no visible presence of [S3]•− radicals, and only S62- and S42- are 
observed during the stage I. On stage II, the concentration of medium-chain S42- increases rapidly, 
followed by a gradual rise in concentration of S62- (stage II). On stage III, the concentration of S42- 
decreases, indicating its continuous conversion to short-chain sulfides.

mechanisms as impacted by the HMPA. In the DD_HMPA electrolyte, species such as S62-, S42- and [S3]•−

occur. As discharge progresses (stage I), the concentrations of S62-, S42- and [S3]•− radicals gradually 
increase. The reactions can be facilitated by the [S3]•− radicals (e.g. Equation 5). During stage II, the 
concentration of S42- decreases slightly, which may have been further reduced to short-chain S2- (Equation 
6). Meanwhile, the radicals are further reduced and promote the conversion from long-chain polysulfides 
to short-chain polysulfides (Equations 7 and 8). During stage III, the concentration of [S3]•− radicals

In the DD electrolyte, the primary products in stage I are S62- and S42- without formation of [S3]•− 
radicals (following equations 1, 2, and 4). During stage II, the concentration of S62- remains relatively 
constant, while S42- increases gradually, primarily due to the reduction of S82-. Subsequently , medium-
long chain S42- further reduces to short-chain S2-, while the short-chain species will react with S8 or S82- 
to form medium-long chains species like S62- and S42- (equations ). This promotes further reduction of S8 
or S82-, increasing the concentration of S62- and S42-. During the discharge stage Ⅲ, the concentration of 
S42- decreases, indicating its further reduction. 
In conclusion, the in-situ UV-vis measurement reveals that the DD_HMPA electrolyte promotes 
[S3]•− radicals generation. These free radicals are present for a rather long period of time, which could 
facilitate the rapid reaction of sulfur at the initial stage. Additionally, during the later stages, the [S3]•− 
radicals can readily accept electrons, and the products promote the further reduction of long-chain 
polysulfides to short-chain polysulfides. Therefore, [S3]•− radicals provide a new pathway for converting 
long-chain polysulfides to short-chain sulfides, enhancing the sulfur utilization, as depicted in Figure S8b. 
In contrast, the DD electrolyte does not significantly generate free radicals and the sulfur reaction follows 
a stepwise mechanism, as depicted in Figure S8d.

Figure S16. The Nyquist plots of the Li-S cells using varied electrolytes at different states: (a) before 
cycling, (b) after the first discharge and (c) after the first charge. The Nyquist plots of the cells exhibit 
very close impedance (particularly the charge transfer impedance at medium frequencies) before cycling 
(Figure S14a). After discharge, the cells show two separate semi-circles representing interface resistance 
associated with the cathode surface and charge transfer resistance. The cell using DD_HMPA shows a 
lower resistance than others, indicating a higher-surface-area deposition of Li2S enabled by the prolonged 
presence of [S3]•− radicals owed to HMPA (Figure S14b), consistent with the 3D island-like Li2S 
deposition morphology as shown by the potentiostatic electrodeposition test. After charge, the cell using 
DD_HMPA still exhibits a lower interface resistance than other cells, indicating fast charge transfer with 
the deposited sulfur (Figure S14c). While we have not studied extensively the deposition of sulfur upon 
charge, these findings provide further evidence of promoted deposition of Li2S/S as regulated by the [S3]•− 
radicals in the cell with DD_HMPA electrolyte.
