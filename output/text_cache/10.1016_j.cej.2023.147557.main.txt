A B S T R A C T  
As the next candidate of secondary batteries, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are hampered by short cycle life and 
safety issues caused by combustion of electrolytes and unstable cycling performance generated by Li dendrites 
growth, which become more serious when Li-S batteries are operated at high temperatures. To solve these 
problems, an intrinsic flame-retardant siloxane ether-based electrolyte, 2.5 M LiFSI/tetrapropoxysilane (TPOS), 
is innovatively investigated in Li-S batteries with sulfurized pyrolyzed poly(acrylonitrile) (SPAN) cathode at 80 
℃. This electrolyte exhibits desirable flame retardancy, unique solvation structure and excellent interfacial 
stability. Particularly, this electrolyte performs outstandingly in cathode protection derived from the robust and 
flexible electrolyte/electrode interphases simultaneously for Li metal anode and SPAN cathode. Li-S batteries 
delivered a reversible capacity of 760 mAh gSPAN
−1 
(1535 mAh gsulfur
−1
) and a capacity retention of 98.4 % after 85 
cycles at 1C and 80 ℃. This work provides the concept and paves the way to develop high temperature Li-S 
batteries.   
1. Introduction 
The first generation of secondary batteries, Li-ion batteries, have 
been commercialized since 1990s and achieved great advances in elec­
tric vehicles, notebook computers, aerospace, and other equipment [1]. 
However, Li-ion battery has gradually been approaching the ceiling on 
account of limited resources and energy capacity [2]. Therefore, 
developing safe, long cycle life, and high energy density batteries has 
been a longstanding goal for electrochemical power sources filed. To 
date, ascribed to the low cost of elemental sulfur, environment- 
friendliness and ultra-high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), 
Li-S batteries have been intensely investigated and improved by exten­
sively comprehensive strategies [3]. Nonetheless, safety concern and 
high temperature operation are still two crucial factors to hinder the 
large-scale practical application of Li-S batteries [4]. These problems 
principally originate from the combustion of organic electrolyte and 
inhomogeneously fragile organic–inorganic solid electrolyte interphase 
(SEI)/ cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer formed on the surface 
of electrodes by electrolyte decomposition [5]. As a result, enhancing 
the intrinsic safety and simultaneously promoting their electrochemical 
performances play a vital role in the development of Li-S batteries. 
Electrolyte basically provides ionic conductive channels between 
cathode and anode, additionally forms a robust SEI/CEI layer and im­
proves electrochemical performances of Li-S battery [6]. Nevertheless, 
inherent flammability, low melting points and low flash points of con­
ventional electrolytes such as 1 M LiTFSI/DME-DOL (1:1, v:v) (DME: 
1,2-Dimethoxyethane, DOL: 1,3-Dioxolane) lead to potential safety risk, 
which extremely limits the Li-S battery operation at high temperatures 
[7]. In addition, mechanically fragile and chemically heterogeneous SEI 
layer derived from conventional electrolyte is incompetent in protecting 
Li anode from attack, which triggers the growth of Li dendrites and 
germinates dead Li. It is distinct to pose a severe threat to the safe and 
stable long-lifespan of Li-S batteries [8]. Numerous efforts have been 
made to address safety concerns and regulate SEI/CEI in terms of com­
ponents regulation, electrolyte type innovation [9] and additives addi­
tion [10]. It is well known that LiF has an outstanding impact on 
constructing robust SEI/CEI layer, which is beneficial to accommodate 
large volume changes during the process of Li+ stripping/plating, 
accelerate ion diffusion rate, and protect electrodes from side reactions 
[11]. Furthermore, F element is capable of scavenging H, O and OH free 
radicals of organic electrolyte during combustion and then inhibiting 
combustion [12]. Hence, fluorinated electrolytes have attracted a lot of

environmentally friendly, low-cost, safe and high electrode compati­

In previous reports, it has been elucidated that siloxane electrolytes 
based on silyl ether protecting groups endow electrolytes with 
competitive advantages on environmental friendliness, dielectric con­
stant [16], high voltage resistance [17] and thermal stability [18] in Li- 
ion batteries, which is similar to the effects of fluorinated reagents [19]. 
Furthermore, compared to C atom, Si atom in the same group has an 
empty 3d orbital to accommodate lone pairs of adjacent atoms through 
conjugation, which enables the bond energy of Si–O bond to be much 
stronger than C–O bond (452 kJ mol−1 vs. 352 kJmol−1) [20]. In 
addition, replacing C–O bond with Si–O bond has been proved to be 
effective to improve the chemical stability in organic synthesis [21]. 
More importantly, Si–O bond replacement strategy marvelously hand 
out the challenges of oxidation and reduction derived from C–O bond 
and C––O bond respectively at the same time [20]. All above results 
implicate that as the environmentally friendly F-free electrolytes, 
siloxane electrolytes can achieve the same effect as fluorinated elec­
trolytes, which is in favor of the safe and stable cycle of Li-S batteries. 
Herein, saturated concentration of tetrapropoxysilane (TPOS) 
(Fig. 1a) electrolyte composed of 2.5 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide 
(LiFSI) (Fig. 1a) in the TPOS solvent, was adopted as electrolyte to probe 
the effect of siloxane ether electrolyte in Li-S batteries at high temper­
atures. 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte enables Li-S batteries to exhibit a 
capacity of 760 mAh gSPAN
−1 
(1535 mAh gsulfur
−1
), a capacity retention of 
98.4 % after 85 cycles and a high sulfur utilization of 91.83 % at 1C and 
80 ℃. Especially Li-S system with dual flame-retardant cathode and 
electrolyte effectively address the safety concerns at high temperatures, 
which might profoundly promote the process of high temperature Li-S 
batteries. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Electrolyte preparation and characterization 
TPOS, EC and DMC solvents (EC: ethylene carbonate, DMC: dimethyl 
carbonate) are treated with anactivated lithiated 4 Å molecular sieve to 
remove trace water. After that, mix EC and DMC in equal volume and 
then added lithium salt (LiPF6) into the mixed solvent to construct a 
standard electrolyte (note as STD electrolyte). To provide comprehen­
sive electrolyte matching concentration between electrodes and elec­
trolytes, different concentrations (1 M, 1.5 M, 2.0 M and 2.5 M) of TPOS 
electrolyte were screening firstly and then prepared by stirring TPOS 
with lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) at room temperature. 
Among them, 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte was saturated electrolyte. 
TPOS (purity > 99 %) solvent was purchased from Beijing Inokai tech­
nology company. LiPF6, EC, DMC and LiFSI were purchased directly 
from company Xiaoyuan. The entire preparation of electrolytes was 
carried out in an argon-filled glove box (O2 and H2O content is less than 
0.1 ppm). 
2.2. Sulfurized pyrolyzed Poly(acrylonitrile) (SPAN) synthesis 
8.000 g elemental sulfur and 1.000 g polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw =
1.5 × 105, Aldrich) were accurately weighed on a precision electronic 
balance. The mass ratio of elemental sulfur and PAN was 8:1. After 
weighing, put sulfur and PAN into the agate ball mill tank and then 
added dispersant (anhydrous ethanol). After that, put the ball mill with 
the speed of 300 r/min. Light yellow paste was obtained after 12 h of 
uniform ball milling. Put the paste in the oven and kept it at 80 ℃ for 12 
h to remove the anhydrous ethanol from the paste. Then weighed evenly 
mixed powder and evenly spread in the quartz boat. Heated the powder 
in the quartz boat at the heating rate of 5 ℃/min to 300 ℃ in the slowly 
flow nitrogen gas, and preserved 300 ℃ for 6 h. After natural cooling, 
we could get a loose black lump solid. An appropriate amount of solid 
was removed and ground in agate mortar to obtain a black sulfide 
powder, SPAN [22]. The sulfur content of the composite was about 49.5

respectively. Li-S cells were assembled by the Li foil as anode and 49.5

cycle and 1C later with 1.77 mg cm−2 sulfur loading mass. Sulfur sym­

same electrolyte to test electrochemical impedance on the surface of 
cathode. The electrolyte/sulfur ratio was controlled at 20 mL gsulfur

separator used at room temperature is polyethylene (PE) separator, and 
the polyimide (PI) separator is used at high temperatures. 
2.4. Electrode characterizations 
The morphology of CEI film was observed by transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM). The morphology of SPAN cathode, Li deposition on 
Li foil and sulfur surface were observed by scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) (FEI Nova nano-scanning electron microscope). We disassembled 
the Li symmetric cells in an Ar-filled glove box and immersed the ob­
tained Li foil in dimethoxyethane (DME) completely to remove the 
remaining salt. Then sealed dried Li deposition on the Li foil in an Ar- 
filled container and quickly transferred to the SEM device to avoid 
oxidation. The surface composition of the electrodes was analyzed by X- 
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using the Kueto axis ultra-DLD 
spectrometer (Cueto Analytica Shimadzu Group Inc.) and mono­
chromatic aluminum source (1486.6 eV) under ultra-high vacuum after 
cycling with 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte and STD electrolyte. 
2.5. Theoretical calculations 
All the molecules of LiFSI and TPOS were optimized at the PBE0/ 
def2-SVP theoretical level with use of Gaussian 16 program [23], 
Then the molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed by the 
GROMACS software [24]. The forced field parameters originate from 
Amber and GAFF force fields. The size of box is 6 × 6 × 6 nm3, with 
periodic boundary conditions in all three directions. The simulation 
systems contained 125 LiFSI and 175 TPOS molecules. The Particle Mesh 
Ewald (PME) method were utilized to compute the long-range electro­
static interactions. A cutoff length of 1.0 nm was used in the calculation 
of electrostatic interactions and non-electrostatic interactions in real 
space and the integration time step was 1 fs. The system was annealed 
from 0 to 298 K in 0.5 ns, and retain a constant temperature for another 
2.0 ns to reach equilibrium. The temperature and pressure coupling 
were performed in V-rescale and Berendsen method, respectively. 
Finally, a 10-ns production simulation was conducted for post- 
processing analysis. Constant temperature was maintained at 353 K 
using V-rescale temperature coupling method and pressure was 
maintained at 1 atm with Parrinello-Rahman coupling method [25]. The 
periodic boundary box was built by Packmol program [26], and the 
electron localization function (ELF) and non-covalent interaction anal­
ysis were visualized by Multiwfn and VMD programs [27]. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Physicochemical and electrochemical properties 
Safety issues of batteries originated from the flammability and leak 
of electrolytes have been hindering the application of Li-S batteries at 
high temperatures. To propagate safety, a series of ignition tests were 
carried out to establish the flame-retardancy of TPOS electrolytes (2.5 M 
LiFSI/TPOS: Fig. 1b, 1.0 M/1.5 M/ 2.0 M LiFSI/TPOS: Fig. S1a) contrast 
to the violently burning conventional electrolyte (1 M LiPF6/ EC-DMC 
(1:1, v:v), noted as STD electrolyte, Fig. 1b). This is mainly attributed 
to that on the one hand, siloxane generates a silicon carbide (SiC) coking 
isolation layer between the condensed and gas phases through a physical 
interdiction process [28]. On the other hand, TPOS has higher boiling 
point (225 ◦C) and flash point (95 ◦C) than those of STD electrolyte 
solvents (Table S1), which was devoted to solving the threat of flatu­
lence during battery operation [29]. The contact angle tests were con­
ducted to demonstrate the excellent wettability and compatibility of 
TPOS electrolytes with separator (Fig. S1b). And the ionic conductivity 
of aimed electrolyte was tested under different temperatures (Fig. S1c), 
which showed that ionic conductivity of electrolyte increased when 
rising temperature. And the ionic conductivity reaches its maximum 
value of 0.375 mS cm−1 at 80 ℃. Moreover, 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS elec­
trolyte had a wider electrochemical window (4.94 V) than STD elec­
trolyte (4.09 V) measured by linear scanning voltammetry (LSV), which 
implied that aimed electrolyte had a better oxidation resistance to 
substantially prevent electrolyte from decomposition at high 
temperatures. 
Poor electrochemical performance is another obstacle to the appli­
cation of Li-S batteries at high temperatures, which is closely related to 
the stability of CEI/SEI layer. Over the past decades, the perspective of 
regulating solvation structures to form a robust CEI/SEI layer has 
contributed remarkably to many progresses of Li-S batteries [9]. Among 
regulation strategies, solvation structure of high-concentration electro­
lytes stands out, which is related to the following aspects: (1) In such 
structure, almost every solvent molecule is attracted by Li+ and more 
anions are motivated into the first shell of Li+ compared to dilute elec­
trolyte [30]. (2) The concentration polarization of Li+ decreases with 
increasing concentration. Migration of anions in the first shell of sol­
vation structure is obstructed and then indirectly increase the migration 
of Li+ [31]. (3) The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of 
solvent-Li+ complex is higher than that of free solvents in dilute elec­
trolyte, which prevents solvents from decomposition and enables anions 
of Li salts to preferentially decompose on the surface of electrodes to 
passivate Li anode [32]. (4) Unique solvation structure changes the 
structure of electric double layer. When applying electric field, solvation 
structure of high-concentration is contributed to allowing more anions 
closer to the inner Helmholtz layer, which further certifies the prefer­
ential decomposition of anions [33]. And when electrode polarization 
occurs, all solvent molecules can be removed from the electrode surface 
and not oxidized [34]. Prior anion decomposition prompts a robust and 
dense CEI/SEI layer to be formed, which is beneficial for interfacial 
stability [35]. And compared to other concentrations of electrolytes, 
better electrochemical performance of saturated electrolyte (Figure S2), 
2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS, further verified the superiority of high-concentration 
electrolyte, which was consistent with previous reports. More impor­
tantly, we discovered that 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte was a kind of 
weakly solvated electrolyte through 7Li chemical shift of nuclear mag­
netic resonance (NMR), Raman spectra and theoretical calculations 
[36]. 7Li NMR can detect the coordination components in the solvated 
shell sensitively. An increase in electron density around Li+ triggers a

occurred

the interaction between Li+ and solvent. As a result, the solvation ability 
of the solvent is impaired and FSI−anions become more competitive in 
the coordination with Li+. To unveil the anion–solvent interaction, the 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on 2.5 M LiFSI/ 
TPOS. As show in Figure S4, the radial distribution function indicates a 
much stronger association between O from LiFSI with Li+ than O from 
TPOS with Li+, indicating preferential interaction with LiFSI rather than 
TPOS. Moreover, the electron localization function (ELF, Figure S5) and 
non-covalent interaction analysis between LiFSI and TPOS presents the 
similar results with the oxygen atoms from TPOS have smaller attraction 
for Li+ due to the steric hindrance of alkyl groups nearby [42]. Indeed, 
the fact that FSI−vibration moved to high-field with the concentration 
increasing (Fig. 1e) implied that concentration of CIP and AGG structure 
and interaction between Li+ and FSI−were increasing. Consequently, we 
supposed that weakly solvated saturated electrolyte, 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS 
was conducive to the construction of anion-derived interface layer, 
which enables Li-S batteries with benign electrochemical performance 
at high temperatures.

3.3. Interphase chemistry 
To further confirm the effect of 2.5 M LiFSI electrolyte in terms of 
protecting cathode and accelerating reaction kinetics, we made great 
efforts on analyzing interphase chemistry. In addition to that elevating 
temperature is conductive to increasing Li+ diffusion rate on the cath­
ode, CEI film also has an extraordinary impact on Li+ diffusion rate. It 
has been demonstrated that SPAN cathode bases on solid–solid 
conversion mechanism and avoids shuttle effect at the root. That de­
pends on the protection effect of CEI film to prevent sulfur from disso­
lution, which further reflects the importance of CEI film. An ideal CEI 
film should be equipped with thin thickness, sufficiently mechanical 
strength, and chemical homogeneousness, which is closely related to the 
regulation of interphase chemistry. Therefore, investigating interphase 
chemistry of cathode is distinctly crucial to improve electrochemical 
performance of batteries. To explore the effect on the thickness of CEI 
film, we soaked the SPAN cathode after cycling in 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) solvent and made full use of ultrasound to enable cathode and 
CEI film coating on the surface of cathode to be uniformly dispersed in 
the DME solution together. After dropping solution on the copper, 
morphology of CEI film was observed by advanced transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM). It has been admitted that electrolyte solvents react 
with Li+ to produce by-products that coat on the surface of cathode to 
form a passivating film with solid electrolyte characteristics during first 
cycle, which is defined as CEI film. Therefore, thinner thickness of CEI 
film represents less amount of Li+ and electrolytes consumption. As 
shown in Fig. 3a, CEI film of 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte was a much 
thinner, denser, and more homogeneous amorphous outer layer than 
that of STD electrolyte, which indicated the stability of CEI film without 
constant rupture and regeneration. It was beneficial for the stably long- 
lasting cycling of batteries. Moreover, we observed the morphology on 
the surface of SPAN cathode through scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) to further certificate the importance of stable CEI film. There was 
almost no change of morphologies between SPAN cathode after cycling 
in the 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte (Fig. 3b) and fresh SPAN cathode 
(Figure S8), which presented as homogeneously spherical granules. 
However, morphology of SPAN cathode cycling in the STD electrolyte 
(Fig. 3b) displayed irregular shape, structural collapse, and agglomer­
ation of plentiful polycarbonate components, which enabled SPAN 
cathode to fail in terms of stably long cycling. And the same phenome­
non was also vividly observed on the Li anode, which further confirmed 
the effective protection effect of aimed electrolyte. Morphologies of Li 
anode surface after cycling in different electrolytes were recorded by 
SEM technology. After 40 h of cycling, the deposition in the 2.5 M LiFSI/ 
TPOS displayed larger nucleation particle size with a granular, flat, 
smooth, and dendrite-free morphology (Fig. 3c). Whereas the deposition 
in STD electrolyte presented a loose moss-like morphological structure 
was observed on the surface (Fig. 3c).

increased power of diffusion, adsorption, electrochemical reaction ki­
netics, the impedance spectra of aimed electrolyte impressed lower 
interfacial resistance than that of STD electrolyte. It mainly derived from 
the decrease of side reaction on the cathode interphase under the pro­
tection of thin and robust CEI film, which corresponded to the phe­
nomenon observed through TEM image (Fig. 3a, b). Later, we detected 
the interfacial resistance through Li symmetrical batteries after 25 cycles 
at the current density of 1 mA cm−2 (Figure S10), which demonstrated 
the same law as the SPAN interphase. All above revealed that 2.5 M 
LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte was indeed equal to form a robust, dense, and 
stable CEI film. It was dominantly contributed to protecting SPAN 
cathode, accelerating reaction kinetics, and enhancing cycling stability. 
Since components determine the performance of CEI film, exploring 
components of CEI film plays a significant role in regulating CEI film. 
Notably, the formation of CEI film is accompanied by two processes: 
growth and dissolution. However, the dissolution rate of CEI film at high 
temperatures is much greater than that of formation, which easily 
generated fragile CEI film unable to form effective protection for sulfur 
cathode. This is the decisive factor of poor performance for sulfur 
cathodes at high temperatures. As the research deepens, it is found that 
LiF-rich CEI film performs well on account of its remarkable mechanical 
strength, ion conductivity and chemical homogeneousness even at high 
temperatures. This point is also the original intention of choosing a 
weakly solvated high-concentration electrolyte, which is to construct a 
LiF-rich CEI film at a relatively low Li salt concentration compared with 
strongly solvated high-concentration electrolyte. And this intention was 
proved to be realized by X-Ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) anal­
ysis. As shown in Fig. 4, 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte displayed unique 
characteristic peaks of Li2O (~528.5 eV, Fig. 4a), LiF (~55.6 eV, Fig. 4b/ 
~ 684.8 eV, Fig. 4c) [45], which endowed CEI film with extraordinary 
mechanical strength, ion conductivity and chemical homogeneousness 
(Figure S11b). This is in stark contrast to the components detected in the 
STD electrolyte. Regrettably, STD electrolyte exhibited a series of 
characteristic peaks derived from the products of carbonate and LiPF6 
decomposition, such as LiOH (~54.7 eV, Fig. 4b), Li2CO3 (~56.2 eV, 
Fig. 4b), ROCO2Li (~58.1 eV, Fig. 4b), and LixPOyFz (~685.9 eV, 
Fig. 4c). These components suggested that a fragile and chemically 
heterogeneous (Figure S11e) CEI film was formed in STD electrolyte. 
And this type of CEI film was extremely weak in protecting cathode at 
high temperatures so that continuous side reactions between electrolyte 
and cathode were triggered. This is the fundamental reason why Li-S 
batteries cannot cycle stably in the STD electrolyte. Besides, the 
unique characteristic peaks of O-Si-O [46] (~103.0 eV, Fig. 4d) in 2.5 M 
LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte was detected, which indicated that TPOS 
participated in the formation of CEI film. This conclusion was drawn on 
the comprehensive consideration of Si-O characteristic peak (~531.5 
eV, Fig. 4a), O-Si-O characteristic peak (~103.0 eV, Fig. 4d) and elec­
tron energy loss spectroscopy mapping of Si element (Figure S9c). In-situ 
synthesized O-Si-O bonds of CEI film effectively promoted the toughness 
of CEI film [47]. Therefore, under the synergistic effects of LiF and O-Si- 
O bonds, the strength and flexibility of CEI film were effectively 
improved, which offered a long-lasting protection on cathode. In sum­
mary, 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte formed a robust and flexible CEI 
film to effectively protect the SPAN cathode, which equipped the 
assembled Li-S cells with exceptional cycling performance. Excellent 
protection depended on the advantages as following (1) Unique weakly 
solvated structure of 2.5 M LiFSI/TPOS electrolyte facilitates the gen­
eration of anion-derived CEI film, which improves the mechanical 
strength of CEI film, diminishes the consumption of solvents and ac­
celerates the diffusion of Li+. (2) O-Si-O bonds endow CEI film with 
outstanding toughness, which effectively relieves the pressure of volume 
expansion. More importantly, rich-LiF CEI film enhances the mechanical 
strength and ionic conductivity of CEI film. It is sufficiently beneficial for 
protecting cathode and accelerating reaction kinetics of CEI film, which 
guarantees the cyclic stability of batteries. (3) Benign interfacial ion 
conductivity and high temperature endow Li+ with larger nucleation 
particle size, which avoids the growth of Li dendrites at the root and 
guarantees the stably long cycling of Li anode. (4) After the CEI film 
protects sulfur cathode from attack, thermal stability and structural 
stability of the sulfur cathode is terribly important for maintaining stable 
cycling of Li-S batteries.

4. Conclusions 
In this paper, we report an intrinsic safe siloxane ether-based elec­
trolyte which exhibited good compatibility simultaneously for Li metal 
anode and SPAN cathode. And regulating solvation structure enabled it 
to be competitive in constructing LiF-rich CEI film even without fluo­
rination. Simultaneously, O-Si-O bonds generated by siloxane partici­
pating in the formation of CEI film greatly enhances the toughness of the 
film. Therefore, a robust and flexible CEI film was formed in 2.5 M LiFSI/ 
TPOS electrolyte, which was in favor of the stable cycling of Li-S bat­
teries. This work provides an innovation option for building highly safe, 
high-energy density and high-temperature operation Li-S system. 
Particularly, it fully demonstrates the superiority of SPAN cathode at 
high temperatures. Li-S batteries delivered a high reversible capacity of

Li-S batteries under high temperature conditions. We believe that this 
work provides the concept and paves the way to develop high temper­
ature Li-S batteries with SPAN cathode at solid–solid conversion 
mechanism.
