Summary
To realize the full potential of the lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, the shuttling of
the polysulfide (PS) intermediates between the electrodes should be prevented.
Moreover, inactive material mass in the battery pack should be minimized to
increase the system-level energy density. In this respect, ionic liquids (IL) are get-
ting increasing attention as they can reduce the PS shuttle mechanism with their
limited PS solubility and functionality in lean electrolyte conditions. In this work,
a dataset is constructed from the experimental literature data, which uses ILs as
their electrolytes, to analyze the important cell variables and promising ILs for
high cell- and system-level performances using association rule mining. The study
reveals that with the help of ILs, high-performance cells with high sulfur loadings
and low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratios can be attained. It is found that 1-ethyl-
3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (EMI_TFSI) or ionic
liquids with the n-methyl-n-propylpyrrolidinium (P13) cation lead to higher cell
capacities, whereas Li(tetraglyme) bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li[G3]
_TFSI) is found promising for high system-level energy densities.
Highlights
• Machine learning method of association rule mining is used to investigate
the effect of critical materials and cell design factors on the performance of
Li-S batteries using ionic liquid electrolytes.
• ARM was employed first for the experimental peak discharge capacity data
and then for energy densities and specific energies predicted by a proposed
system-level performance model.
• IL electrolytes show promising results in peak discharge capacities at high
sulfur loadings and low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratios; however, their cycling
performance at high C-rates needs to be improved.

INTRODUCTION
With the increasing demand for high-range electric vehi-
cles, post Li-ion batteries are drawing more attention as
they theoretically provide higher specific capacities.
Owing to a theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh/g
and its abundance in nature, the sulfur cathode can pro-
vide higher capacities at lower prices.1,2 However, sulfur
is an insulating material; thus, additional conductive
materials are needed to enable electrochemical reac-
tions.3 In addition, the lithiation process of sulfur
involves multi-step reactions where soluble polysulfide
(PS) intermediates are formed. Shuttling of these inter-
mediates between the negative and the positive elec-
trodes
rather
than
staying
at
the
cathode
surface
considerably decreases the Coulombic efficiency, result-
ing in active material loss and hence fast capacity decay.4
To realize the real potential of the Li-S batteries, the
described PS shuttle mechanism (PSM) should be pre-
vented.5 Meanwhile, in order to increase the system-level
performance of the battery, the dead mass (ie, electrolyte,
carbon, etc.) in the cells should be minimized.6
Various strategies have been adopted to solve the
problems of the Li-S batteries and hence increase the
practical capacities. Many studies focus on the positive
electrode design for both restraining the PSs inside the
cathode matrix and increasing the conductivity of the
electrode.7-13 In addition, interlayers between the posi-
tive and the negative electrodes are added to keep the
PSs in the cathode side.14-18 However, these modifica-
tions are not sufficient by themselves; the electrolyte
system of the Li-S batteries should also be optimized for
better performance. Ideal electrolytes need to be chemi-
cally and electrochemically stable at a wide voltage
range. In addition, they should have high Li-ion conduc-
tivity and limited PS solubility to suppress the PSM.19,20
1,3-dioxolane:1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL:DME) mixture
with LiNO3 and LiTFSI salts is the most commonly used
electrolyte system in Li-S batteries. Although these
organic electrolytes are good in terms of Li+ diffusion,
their high PS solubilities hinder their effectiveness.7,21 In
this respect, room temperature ionic liquids (RTIL) are
getting significant attention as electrolyte solvents with
limited PS solubility and mobility.22 Moreover, IL electro-
lytes can also be used in high sulfur-loaded cells at lean
amounts, which is a must for the Li-S batteries to have
high energy densities.23 A techno-economical model per-
formed by Eroglu et al. presented that electrolyte-starved
cathodes with sulfur loadings higher than 7 mg/cm2 are
required to achieve high-performance Li-S batteries.24
Dörfler et al. also stated that to improve the energy den-
sity of the cells, the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio
should be below 4 mL/g; above this point, the electrolyte
takes the largest volume/weight fraction in the cell.25
Even though these limits differ to some extent in the lit-
erature, the main conclusion is the same; the literature
must focus on maximizing the battery performance at
low E/S ratios and high sulfur loadings in the develop-
ment of materials for the Li-S batteries.26
An early study utilizing an RTIL showed that n-
methyl-n-butyl-piperidinium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (PP14-RTIL) has a
working potential range of 5.2 V  0.15 V (vs Li/Li+),
which doubles the initial discharge capacity as compared
to carbonate electrolytes.27 Hwa et al. fabricated Li-S cells
with a sulfur loading of 6.3 mg/cm2 and showed that the
presence of PP14-RTIL in the conventional electrolyte
with an E/S ratio of 10 mL/g improved the Coulombic
efficiency and limited the PS mobility and thus, the
PSM.28 Zhang et al. found that solvate ionic liquids (SIL)
mixed with ether electrolytes increased the capacity
retention while suppressing the PSM and increasing the
ionic mobility.29 Matsumae et al. also investigated the
effect of the sulfur loading and the E/S ratio on the Li-S
battery performance with cathodes having a sulfur load-
ing of 5.7 mg/cm2 and an E/S ratio of 10 mL/g.30 In
another
study,
the
utilization
of
1-ethyl-
3-methylimidazolium
bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide
(EMI-TFSI) with a fluorinated ether solvent was studied
with different vol.% of ILs in the Li-S batteries.31 7:3 wt.%
of Pyr1,2O1-TFSI (n-methoxyethyl-n-methylpyrrolidinium
bis[trifluoromethane- sulfonyl]imide) and tri(ethylene
glycol)dimethyl ether (TEGDME) was found to be the
best ratio for both high capacity retention and Coulombic
efficiency.32 Similar works were conducted by utilizing
various ILs, electrode materials, and cell design variables
in the Li-S batteries.33-37 However, the optimum cell
design variables and promising ILs in terms of both cell-
and system-level performances have not been identified
yet. Compared to molecular solvents, the synthesis of ILs
is more costly and hard38; hence, rather than a trial-and-
error procedure, machine learning methods can be uti-
lized to determine the promising cell design parameters
and ILs without wasting time and material. Even though
machine learning is highly valuable for such complex
systems as batteries with a large number of reported data,
studies investigating the effect of cell and materials
design on the battery performance, specifically on the
energy density, using ML are very limited. Jeschke and
Johansson classified the Li-S electrolytes, including SILs,
based on their Li2S8 solubilities using supervised machine
learning.39 Our group performed some works for the Li-S
and Li-oxygen batteries to understand the effects of mate-
rials and design parameters on the performance and to
identify the important patterns in the results reported in
published literature. However, as far as we know, a

In this work, we collected 207 data points having

MATERIAL AND METHODS
2.1
|
Data collection and preprocessing
Web of Science database was used to collect the experi-
mental articles reporting the use of ionic liquids as their
liquid electrolytes in Li-S batteries; the application of ILs
in solid electrolytes or cathode matrices was excluded.
After the data collection step, the dataset was prepro-
cessed to reduce inconsistencies and to select suitable
variables (ie, those widely reported in the literature and
influential on the performance), while the rarely reported
variables such as electrode thicknesses and material
properties were not included to have a statistically reli-
able result. Similarly, the cells tested at temperatures
higher than 30C were not considered here because those
data were significantly different from the ones obtained
at room temperature, yet, not in sufficient numbers to
show the temperature effects. In the end, a dataset with
244 data points from 42 articles, including important cell
design variables and materials, was built. The data points
in the dataset using only molecular solvents (37 data
points), reported for comparison reasons, are not used in
the analysis either. Consequently, our final dataset was
composed of 207 data points; the final dataset, together
with references, is given in Supporting Information (SI),
while the variables and their alternatives (levels) are
shown in Table 1.
As seen in Table 1, there are 15 important variables
in the dataset. Most of these variables are already categor-
ical by their nature; the originally continuous variables
were also categorized into intervals to be able to be used
in the ARM analysis, which is more suitable for the cate-
gorical data. Although an E/S ratio of 15 mL/g is high
and a sulfur loading of 4 mg/cm2 is low to be the upper
and lower limits of the low E/S ratio and high S loading
classes,
respectively,
the
range
of
the
intervals
is
determined according to the distribution of the data
points to have a statistically significant number of data in
each class with the best representation of the patterns in

2.2
|
Calculation of system-level energy
density and specific energy
Here, we modified the BatPaC model, which was devel-
oped by Argonne National Laboratory for the calculation
of the system-level performances of Li-ion batteries,40 for
the Li-S battery systems to estimate the system-level
energy density and specific energy.41 In short, a one-
dimensional electrochemical model calculates the area-
specific impedance and overpotential of the cell compo-
nents, specifically, negative and positive electrodes and
their current collectors, together with the separator using
the
experimental
inputs
including
PDCs,
cathode
wt. percentages, E/S ratios, and sulfur loadings. The cell
voltage was determined by subtracting the total overpo-
tential from the open circuit cell potential of 2.2 V. After-
ward, the battery pack was designed according to the
voltage, energy, and power requirements. The typical
values of 80% and 50% of the degree of discharges were
determined as the rated power and energy requirements,
respectively. With the calculation of the required cell area
and other cell variables, suitable packaging and thermal
management
components
were
determined.
Conse-
quently, the system-level energy densities and specific
energies were calculated by taking all the system vari-
ables into account. Although material properties such as
carbon/electrolyte densities and conductivities are impor-
tant input variables for the electrochemical model, as
shown in Figure S1 and S2, the system-level perfor-
mances were found to be rather insensitive to these fac-
tors. In fact, the effect of these material properties on the
energy density and specific energy is implicitly included
in the model since these properties are highly effective on
the PDCs and the PDC is the most determinative variable
on the system-level performance. Hence, their effects on
the system-level performance are taken into account with
the variance in the PDC value. The default parameters,
together with the governing equations used in the model,
are given in SI.

Modeling details
ARM model was used to identify the single factor associa-
tions between each variable and the performance metrics.
First of all, the high class was defined by setting a mini-
mum performance limit, either PDC, specific energy, or
energy density, and the levels of each factor, as shown in
Table 1, that can lead to that high class were analyzed
using the ARM results. ARM model was developed using
R Studio software and its apriori algorithm of arules pack-
age.42,43 This algorithm provides support, confidence, and
lift values calculated with the following equations, where
A shows the count of a specific level of a factor, and B
and N show the number of instances in the high class
and the total dataset, respectively.

In association rule mining, the strengths of the associa-
tions are expressed in support, confidence, and lift rather
than reporting an error value. Support and confidence
show the most direct trends in the dataset where the frac-
tion of a level with high performance in the entire and
the high-performance datasets, respectively, are calcu-
lated. On the other hand, more complex patterns can be
found using lift values, which are calculated by the ratio
of the confidence to the fraction of that level in the total
dataset7,44; the lift values above one signify positive asso-
ciations between that level and high performance since it
is more frequently found in the high-class subset.45 Fur-
thermore, higher lift values imply a higher probability of
having better performances; this will be more evident in
the next section when it is discussed on specific exam-
ples. Typically, higher lift and support values indicate
stronger associations.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1
|
ARM results for peak discharge
capacity
The lowest capacity limit for the high-capacity subset was
chosen as 800 mAh/g, and its ARM results are given in
Table 2. This table was ranked with respect to the lift
values as higher lift values present stronger associations
between that factor and the high-class cells, while lift
values below one imply negative correlations. Confidence
and support are also given together with the count value,
which is the number of cases satisfying the rule, as a
more practical measure to see the significance of the
results. Indeed, some of the entries with the highest lift
values have only a few cases; hence, they cannot be gen-
eralized with high confidence as a rule to follow. The
calculation of support, confidence, and lift can be shown
for EMI_TFSI as an example to better understand the
results. The total number of data points in the dataset is
207, while only 125 of them have PDCs ≥800 mAh/g,
the high-class subset. On the other hand, 7 data points
contain EMI_TFSI in their electrolytes, and 6 give high
performances (count). Hence, support, confidence and
lift values are found as 6/207 = 0.029, 6/125 = 0.0480
and 0.0480/(7/207) = 1.42, respectively. As seen in the
table, the electrolyte parameters, specifically the ionic liq-
uid type, are at the top, indicating that they are very
effective for providing high-capacity Li-S batteries. The
rules or conclusions deduced for the factor effects can be
refined further with slightly different use of ARM as fol-
lows; the lower limit of the high-performance class is set
to a different level (such as 800 to 1000 mAh/g, and so
on) and ARM analysis is performed for each case. Since
the number of data decreases with increasing lower limit
(tighter performance requirement), the lift of a level pro-
moting high performance should increase because the
number of data satisfying this condition should remain
constant or decrease less than the others. Hence, the
increasing trend in the lift can be used as a further indi-
cator of the positive effect of that level. Therefore, the

there are 19 different ionic liquids, which are combina-
tions of 14 cation and 2 anion groups in the dataset; 92%
of ionic liquids use TFSI as their anion group, and there
are only a few studies investigating the effect of the anion
group on the performance.46,47 The main reason for this
preference is probably the known low PS solubility of the
TFSI anionic group compared to other organic solvents.47
However, further analysis of the ARM results shows that
the use of different anion groups (BETA, FSI) for P13
cation may be beneficial considering that the lift is
almost tripled with the increasing performance measure
(higher limits for the high-performance class); in other
words, the use of the different anionic groups (BETA,
FSI) resulted in high PDCs if P13 was used as the cation.
This may suggest that the other anion groups should also
be investigated for the other cations in addition to TFSI,
which seems to be the default anion.
Rather than using pure IL electrolytes, most of the
data points in the dataset use additional molecular sol-
vents (MS), which are generally organic solvents with
certain salts.48,49 In fact, 64% of the data uses these sol-
vents to decrease the viscosity of ILs to facilitate Li+ ion
transfer in general. However, Figures 2A,B do not seem
to support this practice; the use of suitable pure IL elec-
trolytes works better as the “None” level has the highest
lift at the highest capacity limit. On the other hand, if an
MS is needed, DOL:DME solvent mixture should be used
with the condition that IL/MS vol.% should not exceed
50 vol.%. It was also found that the addition of various
salts other than LiTFSI favors high PDCs, whereas LiNO3
decreases the probability of having high PDCs in ionic
liquid electrolytes (Figure S3).

fur loading on the PDC of Li-S batteries with ionic liquid
electrolytes are compared with the Li-S cells with molec-
ular solvent electrolytes using the dataset we created in
our previous work.7 In Figure 3A, it is shown that low
E/S ratios are more favorable for reaching higher PDCs
in ionic liquid electrolytes (Figure 3A); on the other

CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we analyzed 207 data collected from
42 experimental articles which use ILs as their liquid
electrolytes to identify the patterns and hidden relations
between the cell variables and the cell- and system-level
performances of Li-S batteries using ARM. It was found
that the type of the ionic liquid material is significant in
achieving high PDCs, energy densities, and specific ener-
gies. For instance, P13_TFSI and EMI_TFSI were shown
to be promising for high PDCs, while Li(G3)_TFSI and
P14_TFSI were found to be beneficial for high specific
energy. The use of P13 cation with other anions also led
to high performance. Furthermore, the addition of a sec-
ondary solvent was not found to be favorable. On the
