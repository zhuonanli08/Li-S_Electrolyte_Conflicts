ABSTRACT 
The performance of lithium-sulfur (Li ─S) batteries is severely constrained by fatal polysulfide shuttling, sluggish sulfur redox 
kinetics, and uncontrollable lithium deposition. Organic metal-based molecules have recently emerged as a novel type of 
promoters capable of modulating sulfur and lithium species evolution through either heterogeneous or homogeneous mechanisms 
to respond these issues. Herein, homogeneous metal-based phenanthroline molecular catalysts are developed by tailoring 
coordination micro-environments within electrolyte. By altering metal center type, the Co-based complex in the electrolyte (Co- 
ETL) shows an identical coordination geometry of Co–N4 , whereas the Fe-based complex in the electrolyte (Fe-ETL) exhibits 
dual Fe ─N2 /Fe ─N4 coordination structures. Specifically, the Fe ─N2 coordination enhances adsorption of sulfur and lithium 
species, whereas the Fe ─N4 coordination promotes lithium atom diffusion more efficiently. Such a rational functionality division 
remarkably enhance the homogeneous optimization activity of the Fe-ETL toward the kinetically favorable sulfur cathode 
reactions and improved lithium anode stability. Therefore, the battery demonstrates stable cycling at 5.0 C over 500 cycles with 
a low degradation of 0.03% per cycle. Even under a sulfur loading of 7.1 mg cm− 2 , the battery delivers a remarkable initial areal 
capacity of 6.4 mA h cm− 2 and maintains a favorable cycling stability.

Introduction 
The merits of sulfur, mainly pertaining to its natural abun- 
dance, environmental benignity, and an ultrahigh theoretical 
specific energy density of 2600 Wh kg− 1 , position lithium-sulfur 
(Li ─S) batteries as one of the most promising alternatives for 
next-generation renewable energy storage technologies [ 1–4 ]. 
However, the commercialization of Li ─S batteries is significantly 
impeded by several critical technical bottlenecks, including 
unregulated sulfur evolution [ 5, 6 ] and uncontrollable lithium 
deposition [ 7, 8 ], which give rise to the reduced discharge 
capacity, limited working lifespan, and potential safety concerns. 
First, both sulfur and the discharge end-product of lithium sulfide 
(Li2 S) are electronically non-conductive [ 9, 10 ], resulting in large 
electron diffusion resistance and thus low sulfur utilization 
efficiency. Second, the sluggish redox kinetics of sulfur species, 
particularly during the conversion of Li2 S2 to Li2 S, lead to 
incomplete discharge reaction, hence reducing the utilization 
efficiency of sulfur. Third, the dissolution and migration of 
intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) in organic electrolyte

constitute a notorious shuttle effect [ 11–13 ]. This phenomenon 
contributes significantly to irreversible sulfur loss, and the 
accumulation of “dead sulfur” on the lithium anode. Finally, 
the uncontrollable lithium plating/stripping behavior leads to 
inferior anode working interface and the formation of dendrites, 
which deteriorates cycling capability and poses serious safety 
risks [ 14–16 ]. Overall, the intrinsic physicochemical properties of 
sulfur and lithium species, as well as their complex evolution 
behaviors, collectively limit the achievable capacity and working 
lifespan of Li ─S batteries. 
Thus far, extensive research efforts have been devoted to devel- 
oping various strategies aimed at comprehensively addressing 
the afore-mentioned issues in Li ─S battery technology and its 
commercialization [ 17–19 ]. In particular, a wide range of elec- 
trocatalysts, varying in size from nanoparticles [ 20–22 ], clusters 
[ 23–25 ] to single atoms [ 26–28 ], and in types from carbon- 
based materials [ 29, 30 ], metals to metal compounds [ 31, 32 ], 
have been explored as feasible solutions to deal with the fatal 
problems in Li ─S reactions. Among these, single-atom cata- 
lysts (SACs), which feature atomically dispersed metal centers 
anchored onto suitable substrates, are particularly notable for 
maximum atomic utilization and tunable coordination envi- 
ronments [ 33, 34 ], demonstrating high activity for regulating 
sulfur and lithium species reactions. Despite the remarkable 
progress achieved in Li ─S batteries, the development of SACs 
with satisfactory catalytic activity for Li ─S redox chemistry 
remains an important challenge. For instance, these catalysts are 
immobilized, and cannot adequately contact with the diffusive 
electrochemical species in electrolyte, particularly for the thick 
sulfur cathodes. Therefore, some innovative electrocatalysts with 
high catalytic activity have emerged to tackle these problems, 
including soluble molecular catalysts in electrolytes [ 34, 35 ]. 
Thereinto, an electrolyte-engineered binuclear copper catalyst for 
rationalizing the Li ─S redox reactions was successfully developed 
by our group [ 36 ]. As a homogeneous catalyst, the copper- 
based complex provided completely active sites to boost sulfur 
redox kinetics and stabilize the lithium anode, enabling Li ─S 
battery with favorable durability. Along this line, we proposed a 
promoter system composed of soluble and insoluble copper-based 
complexes for homogeneously or heterogeneously catalyzing the 
Li ─S redox reactions [ 37, 38 ]. Note that the combination of 
two catalysts efficiently overcomes the limitation of individual 
enabler, which pledges the promotion efficiency for the entire 
electrode. Nonetheless, a systematical design methodology for the 
metal-based molecular promotors based on tunable coordination 
micro-environments remains in its infancy. Moreover, a compre- 
hensive understanding of the underlying working mechanisms is 
urgently desired. 
Herein, the coordination micro-environments of metal-based 
organic complexes are precisely manipulated in the electrolyte 
by substituting the metal centers from cobalt (Co) to iron (Fe) 
within the molecular structures. By altering metal center type, the 
Co-based complex in the electrolyte (Co-ETL) shows an identical 
coordination geometry of Co–N4 , whereas the Fe-based complex 
in the electrolyte (Fe-ETL) exhibits dual Fe–N2 /Fe–N4 coordi- 
nation structures. Specifically, the Fe–N2 coordination enhances 
adsorption of sulfur and lithium species, whereas the Fe–N4 
coordination promotes lithium atom diffusion more efficiently. 
Such complementary functionality derived from the bifunc- 
tional molecular structures in contrast to Co-ETL establishes a 
unique synergistic and divisional mechanism, which remarkably 
enhance the homogeneous optimization capability of the Fe- 
ETL toward the kinetically favorable sulfur cathode reactions and 
improved lithium anode stability. Furthermore, by combining a 
heterogeneous copper-based catalyst positioning onto the surface 
of separator, the overall optimization efficiency for the battery 
system is significantly improved, thereby substantially boosting 
the battery performance. We systematically tune the coordi- 
nation structures of homogeneous metal-based phenanthroline 
molecular catalysts within the electrolyte by altering the metal 
center. Such a design strategy for molecular catalysts and their 
homogeneous application remains scarcely reported, establishing 
a new paradigm in catalysis for Li ─S batteries. Of specific 
note, the incorporation of different metals generates distinct 
molecular coordination environments and catalytic mechanisms. 
The unique design and deciphered mechanistic insights differ 
markedly from our recent work [ 37, 38 ] and hold implications 
for both the theoretical understanding and practical operation of 
catalytic processes in Li ─S batteries.

Results and Discussion 
To tune the coordination micro-environments of the molecu- 
lar catalysts, Fe and Co elements are deliberately selected to 
construct soluble organometallic complexes in electrolyte. The 
used electrolyte pertains to a mixture of 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v/v = 1:1) containing 1 mol L− 1 
LiTFSI. According to the single crystal X-ray diffraction results 
in Figure 1a1 , when Fe(III) acts as the coordinating metal, two 
different structures exist simultaneously at a ratio of 1:1 in the 
electrolyte (CCDC: 2424826). One structure features two nitrogen 
and four chlorine atoms coordinated to the Fe atom, which is 
designated as Fe–N2 . The other structure contains four nitrogen 
and two chlorine atoms coordinated to the Fe atom, denoted as 
Fe–N4 . The information of bond lengths and angles regarding 
Fe-based complex is listed in Tables S1 and S2 . As displayed in 
Figure 1a2 , Fe-ETL can be readily dissolved in the electrolyte, 
yielding a red solution with a concentration of 0.25 wt.%. The 
ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectrum of Fe-ETL shows a strong 
absorption peak at a wavelength of around 510 nm, which is 
attributed to ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT), confirming 
the interaction between Fe atom and the phen ligand. In contrast, 
when Co(II) is applied as the coordinating metal, only a single 
coordination structure, pertaining to one Co(II) center coordi- 
nating with four nitrogen and one chlorine atom (designated 
as Co–N4 , CCDC: 2424825), is formed (Figure 1b1 ). The detailed 
values of bond length and angle are presented in Tables S3 and 
S4 . According to the visualized results, the Co-ETL solution 
with a concentration of 0.25 wt.% exhibits a greige color, and its 
LMCT-derived absorption peak appears at a wavelength of about 
480 nm (Figure 1b2 ). Additionally, the peaks at around 640 cm− 1 in 
the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum further confirm 
the coordination of the metal and non-metal atoms in both Fe 
and Co-based complexes (Figure S1 ). The distinct coordination 
behaviors of Fe-ETL and Co-ETL stem from differences in charge 
density and electronic configuration. Fe(III) presents high charge 
density and a d5 configuration, enabling flexible coordination. Its 
small spin-state energy gap allows both five- and six-coordinate 
structures to remain stable. Co(II) displays lower charge density

Visualized penetration tests by a bottle-in-bottle glass setup 
confirm that the Cu-NCS layer acts as an effective barrier, 
reducing Fe-ETL penetration and mitigating the LiPS shuttling 
(Figure S7 ). Control batteries were assembled using Fe-ETL as

performance was evaluated. As presented in Figure S8a,b , the Fe- 
ETL-based battery with the Cu-NCS layer consistently delivers 
higher discharge capacities than the counterpart without the Cu- 
NCS layer during cycling at both 0.5 and 5.0 C, substantiating 
the essential role of the Cu-NCS layer in assisting the Fe-ETL to 
optimize the performance of Li ─S battery systems. The overpo- 
tentials were analyzed from the galvanostatic charge/discharge 
(GCD) curves as shown in Figure S8c,d . The Li ─S batteries 
assembled with Cu-NCS exhibit smaller overpotentials than those 
without Cu-NCS at 0.5 and 5.0 C, indicating that the Cu-NCS 
provides immobilized catalytic sites to compensate for the limited 
activity of Fe-ETL under saturation condition. We conducted XPS 
characterization of the SEI film and elemental mapping analysis 
of the Li anode surface from a cycled battery at 0.2 C, with 
results presented in Figures S9 and S10 , respectively. No Fe 2p 
signal is detected in the XPS full survey spectrum (Figure S9 ), 
and no Fe element is observed on the Li anode surface (Figure 
S10 ), substantiating that Fe–N4 and Fe–N2 species cannot reach 
the anode side to participate in SEI film generation or react 
with the Li metal surface, due to the presence of Cu-NCS layer 
on the separator. Consequently, the homogeneous Fe-ETL and 
heterogeneous Cu-NCS operate synergistically in a cooperative 
manner to manage the evolution of sulfur and lithium species, 
encompassing LiPS shuttling, sulfur redox conversions, and 
lithium plating/stripping procedures. In contrast, Co-ETL, which 
contains a single coordination structure, shows a less pronounced 
homogeneous regulation effect compared to Fe-ETL, implying its 
lower activity for the entire battery system. 
To validate the detailed catalytic effect on the Li ─S redox chem- 
istry, a series of electrochemical tests were conducted on cells 
using Fe-ETL and Co-ETL as homogeneous catalysts. Figure 2a 
shows the linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) curves of the 
batteries measured within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V for the 
two homogeneous catalysts. In Figure 2a , two typical reduction 
peaks are observed in the LSV profiles. Peak 1, located in the 
high voltage range, corresponds to the conversion of solid sulfur 
(S8 ) to soluble LiPSs. Peak 2, appearing at a lower voltage of 
1.9–2.0 V, is attributed to the subsequent formation of insoluble 
LiPSs (Li2 S2 /Li2 S). Notably, both reduction peaks in the LSV 
curve with regards to battery with Fe-ETL show a positive 
shift compared to those of battery with Co-ETL (Figure S11a ). 
Moreover, the battery with Fe-ETL exhibits enhanced current 
responses during the reduction process (Figure S11b ). These 
results confirm the more optimized sulfur reduction conversion 
kinetics by the dual Fe–N2 /Fe–N4 coordination structures than 
the control. To further analyze the sulfur reduction reaction 
kinetics, Tafel plots corresponding to the two reduction peaks are 
presented in Figure 2b,c . The Tafel slopes at Peak 1 are 50.3 mV 
dec− 1 for Fe-ETL-enabled battery and 68.6 mV dec− 1 for Co-ETL- 
optimized battery. Similarly, for Peak 2, the Tafel slope for the 
battery with Fe-ETL is 60.2 mV dec− 1 , the value of which is 
remarkedly lower than that of battery with Co-ETL (132.0 mV 
dec− 1 ), corroborating that Fe-ETL significantly enhances the 
reaction kinetics for both S8 →Li2 S4 and Li2 S4 →Li2 S conversion 
steps. It is well recognized that these reactions correspond to 
the discharge plateau at approximately 2.35 and 2.15 V, which 
collectively contribute to the discharge capacity of battery. To 
probe the active energy ( Ea ) associated with these key conversion 
steps, charge transfer resistances ( Rct ) were evaluated at the 
respective voltages under varying temperatures (Figure 2d,e ). The 
Ea values were determined by fitting Rct data to the Arrhenius 
equation. As can be seen from Figure 2f , the logarithm of the 
reciprocal of the Rct plotted against the inverse of temperature 
yields a linear relationship. Based on the linear fitting results, the 
Ea values for the Li2 S8 →Li2 S4 and Li2 S4 →Li2 S conversions are 
0.46 and 0.66 eV, respectively, at the discharge plateaus of 2.35 
and 2.15 V under the catalysis of Fe-ETL, those values of which 
are significantly lower than those obtained with Co-ETL (0.79 
and 0.78 eV), as shown in Figure 2g . These findings indicate that 
Fe-ETL demonstrates superior catalytic activity in propelling the 
sulfur reduction reactions compared to Co-ETL. 
First-principles simulations based on density functional theory 
(DFT) were further employed to thermodynamically depict the 
sulfur species conversions. Figure 2h shows the Gibbs free energy 
changes ( ∆G ) for the sulfur reduction reactions in the presence of 
various coordination structures. Initially, the conversion of solid 
S8 to liquid Li2 S8 exhibits a negative ∆G , indicating that this step 
is thermodynamically spontaneous and exothermic. Generally, a 
step with a positive ∆G represents an energy barrier that must 
be overcome during the reaction. The transformation associated 
with the highest positive ∆G value is defined as the rate-limiting 
step in the whole sulfur evolution. Based on this principle, the 
Fe–N4 , Fe–N2 , and Co–N4 structures show the ∆G values of 1.55, 
1.64, and 1.87 eV during the rate-limiting step, respectively. These 
results suggest that both the Fe–N4 and Fe–N2 structures in Fe- 
ETL facilitate the sulfur species reduction reaction occurrence 
than the Co–N4 structure in Co-ETL. Specifically, the conversion 
reaction on Fe–N4 structure is much easier than that on Fe–N2 
structure. Since appropriate adsorption capability toward LiPSs 
is crucial for mitigating the shuttle effect, theoretical simulations 
were conducted to evaluate the related binding energies. As 
depicted in Figure S12 , the calculated binding energies of Fe–
N2 structure with S8 , Li2 S8 , Li2 S6 , Li2 S4 , Li2 S2 , and Li2 S are 0.84, 
0.67, 0.17, 0.72, 1.58, and 2.98 eV, respectively, which are highest 
in contrast to that of Fe–N4 structure (0.13, 0.61, 0.22, 0.40, 1.52, 
and 1.80 eV) and Co–N4 structure (0.75, 0.68, 0.13, 0.48, 1.10, and 
1.87 eV). These computational results suggest that Fe-ETL can 
provide a stronger sulfur species capture ability in a working Li ─S 
battery than Co-ETL. Of specific significance, the Fe–N2 structure 
presents more higher binding energies than the Fe–N4 structure, 
substantiating its dominated effect on anchoring sulfur species.

maintaining favorable reversibility of the lithium metal anode 
during charge and discharge processes is essential. To evaluate 
the stability of lithium planting and stripping, Cu//Li asymmetric 
cells incorporating Fe-ETL and Co-ETL catalysts were fabricated, 
and their Coulombic efficiency ( CE ) was recorded at a current 
density of 1 mA cm− 2 and a deposition capacity of 1 mA h 
cm− 2 (Figure 4a ). The Fe-ETL-based cell maintains a stable CE 
of approximately 98.3% over 100 cycles, whereas the Co-ETL- 
based cell exhibits a sudden CE drop around the 70th cycle. The 
lithium plating and stripping cycling performance was further 
assessed using Li//Li symmetric cells under the same conditions 
(Figure 4b ). With Fe-ETL regulation, the Li//Li symmetric cell 
displays a relatively stable voltage-time curve over 800 h, indi- 
cating stable lithium plating and stripping behaviors. In contrast, 
the Co-ETL-based Li//Li symmetric cell presents significant 
voltage fluctuations after around 330 h, due to severe polarization 
and uneven lithium deposition (Figure S18 ). To investigate the 
differences in lithium behavior regulated by Fe-ETL and Co-ETL, 
we measured the activation energy ( Ea ) for Li+ transport through 
the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) film, a key parameter for 
evaluating lithium evolution behavior. Temperature-dependent 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements 
were conducted on Li//Li symmetric cells after cycling at temper- 
atures ranging from 293 to 308 K (Figure 4c,d ). The Ea values were 
calculated using the Arrhenius equation by plotting the logarithm 
of the reciprocal of Rct against the inverse of temperature. The 
fitting results reveal that the Fe-ETL-based cell exhibits a lower Ea 
value of 45.2 kJ mol− 1 compared to the Co-ETL-based cell (49.3 kJ 
mol− 1 ) (Figure 4e ). 
To further examine the surface morphology of the lithium anodes 
after cycling, the cycled Cu//Li cells were disassembled and 
analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown 
in Figure S19 , the lithium anode surface in the Co-ETL-based 
cell appears rougher and more uneven than that in the Fe- 
ETL-based cell. The 3D morphologies of the lithium anodes 
were further characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM). 
As observed in Figure 4f,g , the cycled lithium anode with Fe- 
ETL facilitates homogeneous lithium deposition and suppresses 
dendrite generation. The binding energies and diffusion energy 
barriers of lithium atom on different catalytic structures were 
investigated using DFT method. The related theoretical models 
are illustrated in Figure S20 . As shown in Figure 4h , the Li 
binding energies on Fe–N4 and Co–N4 are similar, both at about 
2.0 eV. However, the Fe–N2 structure with fewer coordinated 
nitrogen atoms presents a much higher binding energy of 3.7 eV, 
indicative of its superior lithiophilicity compared to both Fe–
N4 and Co–N4 . The migration energy barriers of Li atom on 
different structures were calculated. The exchange-correlation 
energy was approximately described by the Perdew–Burke–
Ernzerhof (PBE) functional based on the generalized gradient 
approximation (GGA). In all calculations, the root mean square 
(RMS) convergence was set to 0.002 Ha Å− 1 . In Figures 4i–k and 
S21 , the calculated diffusion barriers for lithium atoms on Fe–
N2 , Fe–N4 , and Co–N4 are 0.22, 0.21, and 0.28 eV, respectively. 
Therefore, the Fe-ETL demonstrates lower lithium atom diffusion 
barriers than Co-ETL owing to the existence of Fe–N2 and Fe–N4 . 
Of particular note, the Fe–N2 structure in Fe-ETL accelerates the 
lithium atom adsorption, while the Fe–N4 structure propels the 
lithium atom diffusion, thereby enabling a targeted optimization 
of lithium species evolution, which effectively guides the lithium 
plating/stripping behavior.

Conclusion 
In summary, the coordination micro-environments of metal- 
based organic molecular catalysts are precisely manipulated by 
altering the metal centers. The Fe–N2 and Fe–N4 configurations 
in Fe-ETL present distinct functionalities in modulating the 
relevant electrochemical species. Specifically, Fe–N4 structure 
enables the Li-ion diffusion, and the Fe–N2 structure is more 
effective in capturing sulfur and lithium species. Such coopera- 
tive molecular structures represent complementary functionality, 
enabling higher efficiency in the comprehensive catalysis of the 
sulfur reactions and the management of Li-ion evolution behavior 
compared to the Co–N4 in Co-ETL. Notably, the homogeneous 
Fe-ETL and the heterogeneous Cu-NCS together form a dual 
synergistic catalytic system for more effectively boosting the 
battery performance. Impressively, the battery incorporating the 
homogeneous/heterogeneous catalytic system achieves an out- 
standing capacity retention of 83.1% at 5 C after 500 cycles, with 
a minimal capacity decay rate of 0.03% per cycle. The proposed 
design strategy is universally applicable to other metal-based 
complexes. For instance, the analogous electronic structure of 
Ni to that of Fe and Co suggests the potential for implementing 
Ni-ETL as a homogeneous catalyst in the Li ─S battery system.
