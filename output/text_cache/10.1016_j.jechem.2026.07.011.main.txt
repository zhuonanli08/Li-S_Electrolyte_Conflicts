Lithium-sulfur batteries offer a compelling route toward high-energy and resource-abundant recharge-
able storage, yet their practical development remains limited by the coupled instability of sulfur conver-
sion, electrolyte solvation, lithium-metal interfaces, and cell-scale transport. This review examines 
practical Li-S batteries through a validation-linked electrolyte-coupled interfacial and catalytic design 
framework, in which solvation structure, sulfur conversion, Li2S deposition, solid electrolyte interphase 
(SEI)/cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) formation, lithium inventory, and pouch-cell ageing are treated 
as coupled elements of one full-cell reaction microenvironment rather than as separate cathode, anode, 
electrolyte, or separator problems. We ﬁrst deﬁne practical operating conditions in terms of sulfur load-
ing, areal capacity, electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, negative-to-positive electrode capacity ratio (N/P 
ratio), cathode structure, pouch-cell relevance, ageing behavior, and failure transparenc y. We then sum-
marize the fundamental sulfur redox pathway, lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) shuttle, Li2S precipitation, self-
discharge, and diagnostic methods that reveal how local chemistry evolves under lean and heterogeneous
conditions. Particular emphasis is placed on electrolyte and solvation chemistry as the upstream regula-
tor of LiPS speciation, sulfur utilization, interphase formation, and lithium compatibility. Recent progress
in cathode hosts, vacancy-rich catalysts, heterostructures, single-atom motifs, high-entropy catalysts,
homogeneous and molecular catalysts, lithium-metal protection, separator engineering, and pouch-cell
translation is critically assessed against realistic constraints. Finally, we propose descriptor-based design
rules and validation criteria for coordinating electrolyte modulation, catalytic conversion, lithium-
inventory preservation, and pouch-cell diagnostics toward practical Li-S batteries.

1. Introductio n
1.1. Energy promise and the practical gap
Lithium-sulfur batteries remain among the most compelling 
beyond-lithium-ion candidates because sulfur conversion offers a 
theoretical speciﬁc capacity that is far higher than that of conven-
tional intercalation cathodes, while lithium metal supplies both 
low mass and the most negative practical anode potential. This
combination creates a theoretical cell-level energy ceiling that sub-
stantially exceeds that of established lithium-ion systems and
explains the long-standing interest in Li-S chemistry for applica-
tions where gravimetric energy density matters as much as cost
and resource availability [1–3]. Sulfur also beneﬁts from natural 
abundance, low price, and the absence of the supply concentration 
concerns associated with Ni- and Co-rich cathodes, which further
strengthens the case for Li-S in large-scale electriﬁcation [1–3]. 
Early cell-level analyses therefore positioned Li-S as one of the 
few battery chemistries with a credible path beyond incremental 
lithium-ion improvement, provided that sulfur loading, sulfur uti-
lization, electrolyte amount, and lithium excess could be optimized
at the same time [2,4]. More recent studies under partially practi-
cal conditions have reinforced that promise by showing that high 
areal sulfur loading and lean-electrolyte operation can deliver 
remarkable projected or measured pouch-cell metrics, including
very high areal capacities and energy densities in the 400 to
500 Wh kg−1 class under selected designs [3,5,6].

The difﬁculty is that impressive laboratory metrics do not auto-
matically translate into durable practical batteries. The literature 
has repeatedly shown that design choices that look favorable in
small cells can lose much of their value once inactive mass, elec-
trolyte inventory, lithium excess, and realistic cell architecture
are counted rigorously [1,24]. Among these parameters, the 
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio exerts especially strong control over true 
cell-level speciﬁc energy, so many apparent ly excellent cycling
results become much less persuasive when ﬂooded-electrolyte
conditions are included in the mass balance [2]. The same concern 
applies to materials strategies that perform well in small-format 
cells but remain insufﬁciently validated under high sulfur loading,
low electrolyte inventory, limited lithium excess, and pouch-cell
assembly [3,5,6]. Even early work on LiNO3-mediated stabilization 
already suggested that durable Li-S operation depends on a deli-
cate balance of surface chemistry at the lithium interface rather
than on sulfur cathode design alone [7]. For this reason, the central 
task is no longer to expand the catalogue of hosts, adsorbents, and 
catalysts, but to reorganize the ﬁeld around the conditions that 
actually determine whether high energy density can survive at
the cell level. This review therefore treats practical Li-S batteries
as coupled electrochemical systems whose promise is meaningful
only when it is tested against realistic architectural and materials
constraints.
1.2. Why isolated optimization has not solved practical Li-S batteries
The persistent underperformance of practical Li-S batteries can-
not be traced to a single dominant failure mode because the major 
degradation pathways are chemically and structurally entangled 
from the beginning of operation. Sulfur redox proceeds through 
soluble lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) intermediates, which means that
cathode conversion chemistry is inherently linked to LiPS solvation
and transport in the electrolyte rather than remaining conﬁned to
the cathode surface [8,9]. Once these dissolved species form, they 
continuously interact with the lithium-metal anode and trigger 
shuttle, self-discharge, parasitic reduction, and interphase instabil-
ity. Direct shuttle-current measurements make clear that these
processes are not merely qualitative concerns but measurable elec-
trochemical leakage pathways that translate directly into Coulom-
bic inefﬁciency and progressive capacity decay [10]. Mechanistic 
and modeling studies further show that self-discharge, overcharge 
behavior, active-material migration, and low efﬁciency are not sep-
arate defects that can be solved independently, but different man-
ifestations of the same coupled shuttle network [8,9,11]. 
This coupling becomes even more severe under practical condi-
tions. At the cathode, Li2S precipitation is not simply a terminal 
discharge product, but a kinetic bottleneck whose nucleation and
growth depend strongly on local concentration, transport, and
interfacial environment [12]. Under lean-electrolyte conditions, 
the higher local concentration of dissolved sulfur species intensi-
ﬁes cathode passivation and transport limitation at the same time,
which means that sulfur utilization and reaction uniformity deteri-
orate together rather than separately [12]. At the anode, electrolyte 
components, salts, and dissolved LiPS species all participate in 
competing interfacial reactions, so lithium corrosion cannot be
treated as a secondary issue to be addressed only after cathode
optimization [13]. Long-term self-discharge studies also show that 
even in the presence of LiNO3, dissolved sulfur species and cell 
components continue to drive parasitic chemistry during rest, con-
ﬁrming that additive-based mitigation suppresses but does not
eliminate the thermodynamic driving force for crossover and
chemical reduction [11,14,15]. As a result, stronger polysulﬁde 
adsorption at the cathode can reduce crossover yet still create slug-
gish conversion and higher polarization, while improved lithium
protection can slow corrosion but still fail to deliver practical gains
if electrolyte depletion, cathode passivation, and spatially nonuni-
form sulfur conversion continue elsewhere in the cell.

This is why isolated optimization has produced many impres-
sive local improvements without yet yielding robust practical cells. 
A sulfur host that immobilizes LiPS species but requires excessive 
carbon loading or electrolyte does not solve the system-level prob-
lem. A lithium-protection strategy that performs well in symmetric 
cells but cannot withstand realistic LiPS exposure is equally incom-
plete. Practical Li-S batteries therefore require a design framework
that treats sulfur redox chemistry, LiPS solvation, lithium inter-
phase evolution, electrolyte consumption, cathode passivation,
and scale-up heterogeneity as one coupled failure network rather
than as separable targets for incremental optimization.
1.3. Scope of this review and deﬁnition of practical conditions
In this review, practical conditions refer to Li-S operation under 
constraints that make projected energy density, cycle life, and fail-
ure analysis meaningful at the device level rather than only at the 
materials-screening level. The ﬁrst requirement is sufﬁciently high 
sulfur loading and areal capacity. Multiple engineering analyses
indicate that sulfur loadings near or above about 5–7 mgS cm−2,
together with areal capacities above roughly 4–6 mAh cm−2, are
needed before Li-S data can be discussed in relation to realistic
cell-level energy density targets [5,16]. Several representative 
studies go further by demonstrating 7.4 mgS cm−2 in 6 Ah pouch 
cells, 13 mgS cm−2 in lean-electrolyte cathodes, and even higher 
areal-capacity architectures, yet these same studies also show that
high loading alone is insufﬁcient when electrolyte inventory or
lithium excess remains too large, or when failure emerges after
only limited cycling [17–19]. 
The second requirement is a constrained electrolyte budget. 
Practical Li-S validation must report the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/ 
S) ratio, and ideally the electrolyte-to-capacity (E/C) ratio, because
electrolyte mass is one of the strongest determinants of true cell-
level speciﬁc energy [5,20]. Studies discussed in this review there-
fore carry the greatest practical weight when they operate near 
lean-electrolyte conditions, typically around an E/S ratio ≤4 lL mgS 
−1 
and preferably closer to 3 lL mgS
−1 or below, while still sustaining
meaningful sulfur utilization and areal capacity [5,19,20]. The third 
requirement is limited lithium excess, quantiﬁed by the negative-
to-positive electrode capacity ratio (N/P ratio) or, more directly, by 
anode thickness. Practical cells cannot rely on an effectively unlim-
ited lithium reservoir, so thin-lithium or otherwise constrained-
anode conﬁgurations are essential if reported energy density is to
remain credible [16,17]. The fourth requirement is pouch-cell rel-
evance. Coin-cell data remain valuable for mechanistic interpreta-
tion, but this review places greater emphasis on studies that 
directly evaluate pouch-cell energy density, ageing, and failure sig-
natures because the literature has already established a substantial
gap between coin-cell durability and pouch-cell survival [21,22]. 
Finally, practical validation in this review is deﬁned not by a 
single headline metric, but by the combined satisfaction of energy, 
durability, and failure transparency. A high speciﬁc-energy claim is 
not treated as convincing when it depends on short-lived opera-
tion, hidden electrolyte excess, or poorly deﬁned failure criteria. 
In this respect, both 400 Wh kg −1 class pouch cells and near-
700 Wh kg−1 pouch cells are highly informative, not only because
of the energy densities they reach, but also because their ageing

2. Fundamental sulfur redox chemistry, failure routes, and
diagnostic frameworks
2.1. Sulfur redox pathway and intermediate evolution
The electrochemistry of Li-S batteries is often compressed into a 
nominal two-step conversion from elemental sulfur to Li2S, yet 
experimental evidence shows that the real pathway is a distributed 
reaction network involving dissolution, comproportionation, dis-
proportionation, nucleation, and precipitation rather than a single 
cathode-conﬁned transformation. During the early stage of dis-
charge, cyclo-S8 is reduced into soluble long-chain LiPS species, 
co mmonly represented as Li2S8 and Li2S6, which dominate the
upper voltage plateau. As discharge proceeds, these dissolved
intermediates evolve toward shorter-chain polysulﬁdes and ﬁnally
toward insoluble short-chain products, conventionally assigned to
Li2S2 and Li2S and associated with the lower plateau [24–26]. In 
situ and operando spectroscopy has been especially important in 
reﬁning this picture because it shows that the pathway is not 
strictly serial. Operando sulfur K-edge X-ray absorption near edge 
structure (XANES) spectra revealed that soluble intermediates 
and Li2S-related species can coexist over a substantial fraction of
discharge, and that the dielectric environment of the electrolyte
strongly inﬂuences how rapidly the late-stage conversion from
polysulﬁdes to Li2S proceeds [27]. In parallel, in situ 7 Li nuclear 
magnetic resonance (NMR) combined with ab initio phase analysis 
showed that Li2S formation can begin much earlier than the classi-
cal plateau-based description implies, already within the ﬁrst dis-
charge plateau, so the distinction between a polysulﬁde plateau
and a later Li2S plateau should be understood as a shift in dominant
speciation rather than a clean handoff between fully separated
chemistries [26]. 
Evidence for the ﬁnal discharge products has also become more 
nuanced than the traditional Li2S end-point picture suggests. Solid-
state and solution NMR studies identiﬁed short-chain insoluble 
species at deep discharge and provided direct support for the pro-
gressive conversion of soluble long-chain polysulﬁdes into Li2S2
and Li2S-type products [25]. At the same time, operando X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) and transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) 
showed that clearly crystalline Li2S is not always straightforward 
to detect in working cathodes, which implies that at least part of
the discharged material may be poorly crystalline, highly dis-
persed, or structurally heterogeneous within the porous electrode
framework [28]. More recent operando 7 Li/6 Li and 33 S NMR studies 
extend this understanding further by locating the onset of Li2S for-
mation near the beginning of the low-voltage region, around 30
percent depth of discharge in the examined system, while simulta-
neously following the soluble polysulﬁde pool and the evolution of
the lithium-metal side [29]. This point is mechanistically impor-
tant because it shows that discharge products cannot be inferred 
from the voltage proﬁle alone. A single apparent plateau can
accommodate soluble long-chain species, short-chain radical-like
intermediates, and precipitating sulﬁdes at the same time.
Reversibility is therefore only partial and strongly condition-
dependent. In situ XRD and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) 
indicated that charging can broadly retrace discharge chemistry
in reverse, but only when the relevant intermediate equilibria
remain chemically and spatially accessible [24]. Operando NMR 
further shows that incomplete re-oxidation of soluble LiPS species 
back to S8 can leave a persistent dissolved sulfur inventory after
charge, creating a chemical memory that carries into later cycles
and complicates the assumption of full state reset between cycles
[29]. Taken together, these studies establish the mechanistic base-
line for the rest of this review. Practical Li-S behavior emerges from
a redox network in which soluble and solid sulfur species overlap

2.2. Shuttle, self-discharge, and parasitic redox as system-level failure
origins
The shuttle effect is often described simply as the diffusion of 
dissolved LiPS species from the cathode to the lithium anode, but 
for practical Li-S batteries, it is more useful to treat the shuttle as 
a system-level failure framework that uniﬁes several apparently 
separate symptoms. Foundational work already argued that self-
discharge, depressed Coulombi c efﬁciency, distorted charging
behavior, and apparent overcharge phenomena can all arise from
the same parasitic loop, in which soluble LiPS species are reduced
at the lithium surface, and the resulting sulfur species are trans-
ported back toward the cathode, allowing charge to be consumed
without proportional recovery of stored energy [8]. Shuttle is 
therefore not merely a crossover. It is a circulating redox process
that transforms sulfur mobility into irreversible energy loss.

Later studies converted this qualitative description into a mea-
surable and modelable degradation route. Direct shuttle-current 
measurements demonstrated that the parasitic current can be 
quantiﬁed as a function of state of charge and electrolyte chem-
istry, and that the decay behavior of this current provides a mean-
ingful predictor for shuttle-related capacity loss [10]. Building on 
that framework, a self-discharge model parameterized by directly 
measured shuttle current showed that short-time resting losses 
depend strongly on both temperature and depth of discharge, par-
ticularly at high states of charge where the soluble sulfur inventory
is largest [30]. These advances matter because they turn shuttle 
from a descriptive label into an engineering variable, allowing 
chemical crossover to be connected directly to efﬁciency loss,
rest-state decay, and broader system instability.
Self-discharge studies across different periods and cell conﬁgu-
rations reinforce the same system logic. Cells using stainless-steel 
current collectors exhibited pronounced voltage and capacity loss 
during storage, indicating that dissolved sulfur migration can cou-
ple with current-collector-dependent side chemistry rather than
acting as an isolated sulfur-only problem [15]. TEGDME-based cells 
showed similarly severe storage losses, with self-discharge rates 
that depended strongly on collector choice and storage duration,
demonstrating that resting failure is a major design constraint
rather than a marginal laboratory artifact [14]. Even in LiNO3-
containing electrolytes, redox shuttle and self-discharge remain 
practically relevant, which means that additive-enabled passiva-
tion suppresses but does not eliminate the thermodynamic driving
force for LiPS crossover and chemical reduction [11]. 
More recent work clariﬁes why shuttle must be interpreted as a
coupled transport-reaction problem rather than a single interface

2.3. Li2S precipitation, re-dissolution, passivation, and reaction
heterogeneity
If shuttle deﬁnes one major failure axis of Li-S batteries, Li2S 
precipitation deﬁnes another. The key issue is not simply that 
Li2S forms near the end of discharge, but that its nucleation, 
growth, and later decomposition are kinetically difﬁcult and spa-
tially nonuniform. Careful electrodeposition studies established 
that Li2S does not generally appear as a benign bulk solid. Instead,
it nucleates only after a substantial overpotential and then grows
preferentially along the three-phase boundary in a largely two-
dimensional manner, so the density of nuclei and the morphology
of the deposit depend strongly on local interfacial conditions
[12,34]. This makes the ﬁnal stage of discharge intrinsically vulner-
able to passivation. If nuclei are sparse or surface coverage 
becomes electronically blocking, further sulfur conversion is throt-
tled not by the nominal supply of sulfur but by the progressive loss
of accessible reaction sites.
Electrolyte environment strongly modulates this behavior. 
Solvent-mediated deposition studies showed that donor number, 
dielectric character, and viscosity collectively inﬂuence whether 
Li 2S forms in a way that supports continued conversion or in a
way that rapidly blocks the surface [34]. Low-electrolyte and 
high-polysulﬁde-concentration conditions aggravate the problem 
further. Direct kinetic measurements demonstrated that increasing 
polysulﬁde concentration, as expected under lean-electrolyte oper-
ation, slows both Li2S nucleation and growth and therefore raises
polarization [35,36]. This point is especially important for practical 
cells because reducing the E/S ratio improves projected energy 
density while simultaneously concentrating dissolved sulfur spe-
cies and pushing the cathode toward a transport-coupled kinetic
bottleneck rather than a purely thermodynamic limit.
Recent work suggests that the precipitated products formed 
under lean conditions are not always best described as fully ter-
minal and irreversible Li2S deposits. Under sufﬁciently concen-
trated conditions, reversibly precipitated sulfur
species
can
form in a concerted manner across the cathode and later re-
dissolve when near-surface concentration falls, effectively acting
as a transient sulfur reservoir [37]. This ﬁnding complicates the 
conventional passivation narrative. Not all precipitation is harm-
ful. Certain reversible precipitates may buffer dissolved polysul-
ﬁde concentration and help sustain discharge energy under lean 
electrolyte conditions. The practical challenge is that beneﬁcial
reversible precipitation and harmful blocking precipitation are
separated only by subtle differences in local supersaturation, sol-
vation environment, and interfacial accessibility rather than by a
clear materials boundary.

Operando imaging reinforces how spatially uneven these pro-
cesses become in realistic electrodes. Transmission X-ray micro-
scopy showed that dissolution and re-deposition vary strongly 
with the state of lithiation and that re-deposition is nucleation-
limited, promoting sulfur agglomeration and large particle-size
changes during cycling [38]. In practical high-energy pouch cells, 
reaction heterogeneity emerges over even larger length scales 
because electrolyte scarcity and in-plane transport gradients cause
the center and edge of the cathode to experience different sulfur-
utilization histories [39]. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS) analysis then provides a system-level signature of this evolu-
tion by separating fast and slow fading processes and linking impe-
dance growth to changes in sulfur species and electrode state [40]. 
Taken together, these studies show that Li2S precipitation is not 
simply an end-of-discharge product step. It is a morphology-
sensitive, electrolyte-sensitive, and scale-sensitive kinetic bottle-
neck that couples directly to passivation, incomplete re-
dissolution, and heterogeneous sulfur utilization in practical cells. 
Recent in situ and operando studies provide more direct evidence 
that Li2S formation and electrolyte redistribution are dynamic, spa-
tially heterogeneous processes rather than simple end-point phe-
nomena. In situ atomic force microscopy (AFM) revealed that Li 2S
deposited on catalytic Pt surfaces forms three-dimensional spher-
ical nanoclusters rather than the lamellar deposits observed on
non-catalytic electrodes, and that the deposition pathway can shift
from progressive to instantaneous nucleation as overpotential
increases. Importantly, high polysulﬁde concentrations promote
Li2S aggregation and deviation from ideal reaction kinetics, indicat-
ing that catalyst deactivation and interfacial passivation become
more severe under practical lean-electrolyte conditions [41]. Com-
plementarily, operando neutron imaging of lean-electrolyte Li-S 
pouch cells directly visualized nonuniform electrolyte wetting 
and redistributi on, including periodic ‘‘breath-in” and ‘‘breath-
out” wetting behavior correlated with sulfur dissolution and pre-
cipitation [42]. 
2.4. Operando and in situ diagnostic methods for practical Li-S
batteries
Because Li-S chemistry involves dissolved intermediates, pre-
cipitating solids, evolving lithium morphology, and spatially 
nonuniform electrolyte distribution, no single characterization 
method can explain practical-cell behavior on its own. The ﬁeld
has therefore moved toward a toolbox logic in which each oper-
ando or in situ technique answers a different mechanistic question
[43]. The logic is summarized in Table 1, which compares represen-
tative diagnostic categories not only by their mechanistic readouts, 
but also by their major artifacts and relevance to lean-electrolyte 
or pouch-relevant Li-S cells. This comparison shows that diagnostic

For sulfur speciation and pathway tracking, X-ray and NMR 
methods remain the most informative. Operando spectromi-
croscopy and XANES mapping directly visualize where sulfur spe-
cies reside and how their spatial distribution shifts during cycling,
which makes them especially valuable for testing whether polysul-
ﬁdes remain localized near the cathode or progressively establish
gradients toward the anode [44]. Quantitative operando sulfur K-
edge absorption on both electrode sides extends this capability 
by measuring dissolved polysulﬁde accumulation separately at
the cathode and anode, thereby revealing how charge-state-
dependent gradients drive sulfur migration and fade [45]. In situ 
6 Li, 7 Li, and 33 S NMR add complementary chemical resolution by 
following the formation of soluble LiPS species, the onset of Li2S 
formation, and changes at the lithium side in real time, which is
particularly powerful when distinguishing whether fading is dom-
inated by incomplete cathode-side re-oxidation or by genuine
shuttle-driven sulfur loss in a given chemistry [29]. 
When the target is dissolved-species detection rather than full 
pathway assignment, more specialized tools become valuable. 
Electrochemical in situ polysulﬁde detection methods can use 
voltammetric signatures as a direct readout of soluble LiPS concen-
tration and are therefore useful for screening separator coatings,
catalytic additives, or concentrated electrolytes that suppress
dissolved-species buildup [46]. Operando optical ﬂuorescence 
microscopy provides an even more intuitive window by imaging 
the spatiotemporal distribution of ﬂuorescently labeled polysul-
ﬁdes and their migration through the separator, making the shuttle
directly visible rather than indirectly inferred [47]. 
Practical-cell diagnostics also require tools that capture trans-
port and morphology beyond sulfur speciation alone. Operando 
neutron imaging can map electrolyte wetting and redistribution 
inside lean-electrolyte pouch cells, thereby answering where elec-
trolyte actually resides during rest and cycling and whether local
dry-out is emerging as a precursor to reaction heterogeneity [42]. 
Online pressure tracking provides a different but complementary 
system signal because it can distinguish whether apparent cell 
swelling is dominated by gas generation or by sulfur-species depo-
sition and stripping together with free-volume change, which is
especially useful when diagnosing solvent-dependent failure in
dense electrodes [48]. In the same spirit, the diagnostics frame-
work emphasizes impedance-based methods as interpreters of 
coupled kinetic and transport losses rather than as generic resis-
tance numbers, helping relate spectral evolution to charge transfer,
contact loss, and passivation growth under realistic test conditions.
In aggregate, these methods allow the ﬁeld to ask not only what
sulfur species are present, but also where they appear, when they 
evolve, which gradients they generate, and how those gradients
propagate into practical performance limitations.

2.5. Section-end critical assessment
Taken together, the mechanistic literature now provides a cred-
ible foundation for organizing Li-S degradation into four linked but 
distinguishable failure modes. The ﬁrst is solution-mediated loss,
including LiPS shuttle, self-discharge, active-sulfur redistribution,
and parasitic redox at the lithium side [8,10,29]. The second is 
solid-product passivation, dominated by Li2S/Li2S 2 nucleation,
growth, pore blockage, and incomplete re-oxidation [12,29,40]. 
The third is interfacial crosstalk, where dissolved sulfur species, 
electrolyte decomposition products, and heterogeneous cathode
reactions destabilize both CEI and SEI chemistry [45,49,50]. The 
fourth is cell-format ampliﬁcation, where a limited E/S ratio, thick 
electrodes, restricted lithium inventory, and pouch-scale wetting
heterogeneity magnify local chemical failures into macroscopic
capacity decay [39,42,49]. 
An important gap, nevertheless, remains between mechanistic 
identiﬁcation and quantitative predictive control. Current diagnos-
tics can reveal LiPS accumulation, Li2S deposition, interphase 
degradation, and electrolyte redistribution, but the thresholds at
which these processes become terminal failure modes remain
dependent on electrolyte formulation, sulfur loading, electrode
architecture, and cell format [29,45,49]. Therefore, the following 
section treats electrolyte chemistry not as an independent solution 
category, but as the upstream set of design variables that shifts the
balance among these failure modes.
3. Electrolyte and solvation chemistry as the upstream 
regulator of practical Li-S behavior
Building on the failure-mode framework deﬁned in Section 2, 
electrolyte chemistry is discussed here as a set of controllable 
design variables rather than as a separate solution category. The 
most important variables include solvent donor/acceptor ability, 
Li+ coordination environment, salt concentration, anion participa-
tion, additive reactivity, electrolyte density, viscosity, ionic con-
ductivity, LiPS solubility, and electrode wetting behavior. Each
variable changes the balance among sulfur accessibility, shuttle
suppression, Li2S deposition, SEI/CEI formation, lithium corrosion,
and pouch-cell electrolyte distribution. The key question is there-
fore not whether an electrolyte is simply ‘‘strongly” or ‘‘weakly”

3.1. Classical LiNO3 chemistry and its evolving interpretation
LiNO3 occupies a singular place in the history of Li-S batteries 
because it was the ﬁrst electrolyte additive that made the classical 
ether-based system appear electrochemically manageable. Early 
studies established the now familiar picture in which nitrate is
reduced on lithium metal, forms a passivating interphase, sup-
presses direct parasitic reaction between dissolved LiPS species
and lithium, and therefore improves Coulombic efﬁciency and
cycling stability [51,52]. In that early framing, LiNO3 looked almost 
like a corrective patch for the intrinsic incompatibility between 
soluble sulfur chemistry and a highly reactive lithium-metal 
anode. Yet even those foundational reports already suggested a 
more complicated role. Zhang showed that LiNO3 is not a purely
protective spectator but a sacriﬁcial reagent consumed on both
electrodes. While it passivates lithium effectively, it also undergoes
substantial irreversible reduction on the sulfur side once the cell
reaches a sufﬁciently deep discharge, thereby lowering cathode
reversibility [51]. Rosenman and co-workers sharpened that view 
by showing that the nitrate reduction window overlaps with the 
low-potential region relevant to deep sulfur discharge, so nitrate
can generate parasitic cathode-side products instead of remaining
exclusively beneﬁcial [53]. 
This duality became clearer once the ﬁeld began to examine 
how LiNO3 behaves in realistic composite electrodes rather than 
in simpliﬁed half-cell logic. Surface-analysis studies showed that 
nitrate-derived ﬁlms on lithium are chemically heterogeneous
but functionally valuable, containing LiNxOy-rich inorganic species
together with organic reduction products that collectively produce
a denser and more uniform interphase than nitrate-free elec-
trolytes [52]. Operando X-ray absorption evidence later extended 
that interpretation by showing that the most effective LiNO3-
enabled SEI is not produced by nitrate alone, but by a synergetic 
interaction between nitrate reduction and incoming polysulﬁdes,
which together form a sulﬁte and sulfate-rich protective layer that
resists continued corrosion more effectively than a purely sulﬁde-
derived surface [54]. In other words, the same dissolved LiPS spe-
cies that create the shuttle problem also participate in the chem-
istry that allows LiNO3 to build a useful anode-side interphase.
However, once the community moved beyond the simpliﬁed 
statement that LiNO3 is simply good for lithium metal, its limita-
tions became increasingly visible. Cathode formulation changes 
how aggressively nitrate is consumed. Oxygenated binders such 
as alginate or carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) accelerate LiNO3
decomposition below about 1.8 V, which makes additive durability
more sensitive to cathode composition and voltage-window con-
trol than early formulations implied [55]. Concentration depen-

The conceptual interpretation also evolved on the cathode side. 
Zhang later argued that nitrate should not be understood only as a 
shuttle suppressant because nitrate species can actively redirect
sulfur chemistry by promoting conversion from highly soluble LiPS

quasi-catalytic role within the cathode reaction environment

line rather than a settled mechanism. It began as an anode protec-
tor, then became understood as a consumable bifunctional reagent, 
and ﬁnally emerged as a system-level variable whose value 
depends on depletion rate, sulfur loading, transport penalty, cutoff 
voltage, and the evolving composition of both electrodes. From a 
practical perspective, LiNO3 should therefore be treated as an
inventory-limited additive rather than as a universal remedy for
Li-S instability. Its effectiveness depends ﬁrst on concentration:
insufﬁcient LiNO3 cannot maintain an anode-protective interphase
over extended cycling, whereas excessive LiNO3 can increase vis-
cosity, introduce cathode-side parasitic reduction, and penalize
transport under lean-electrolyte operation [51,56,57]. Its durability 
also depends on continuous consumption at both electrodes. 
Nitrate-derived species are consumed during SEI formation on 
lithium, can be further reduced at deep cathode potentials, and
may need to be replenished or be present at higher initial concen-
tration when sulfur loading and dissolved LiPS inventory increase
[51,57,58]. In addition, LiNO3 compatibility is formulation-
dependent. Its beneﬁt can change with solvent donor ability, salt 
identity, ionic-liquid or concentrated-electrolyte environments,
cutoff voltage, cathode binder chemistry, and the extent of LiPS
crossover [53,55,56]. Under lean-electrolyte and long-term condi-
tions, LiNO3 is therefore most useful when its sacriﬁcial protection 
is matched to electrolyte formulation, sulfur loading, voltage win-
dow, and lithium inventory; otherwise, nitrate depletion or trans-
port penalty can convert an initially effective additive into a ﬁnite-
life constraint.
3.2. Salt-solvent coordination and LiPS solvation structure
If LiNO3 revealed that additives can reshape Li-S behavior 
through interfacial chemistry, later work on salt-solvent coordina-
tion made clear that the deeper upstream control variable is the 
solvation environment itself. In Li-S electrolytes, the solvent does 
not merely dissolve salts and transport Li+ . It determines which 
polysulﬁde species are stabilized, how fast they diffuse, what 
degree of ion pairing they adopt, which reduction pathway is 
kinetically favored, and how aggressively the resulting sulfur spe-
cies attack lithium metal. Classical electrochemical studies already
showed that sulfur redox is highly sensitive to solvent descriptors.
He et al. found that the reduction of elemental sulfur correlates
mainly with solvent acceptor number, whereas the redox behavior
of dissolved LiPS species is governed more strongly by donor num-
ber, indicating that different stages of sulfur conversion are stabi-
lized by different aspects of the solvent environment [60]. Earlier 
ether-electrolyte comparisons pointed in the same direction, 
showing that stronger solvating ethers increase discharge capacity 
largely because they delay cathode passivation and keep sulfur
species in mobile, electrochemically accessible form for longer
[61]. 
Transport studies then reﬁned that picture by showing that sol-
ubility alone is too blunt a descriptor. Safari, Kwok, and Nazar 
demonstrated that polysulﬁde motion in DOL and DME-based elec-
trolytes is governed by coupled multicomponent transport rather
than by a simple self-diffusion picture, with cross-diffusion terms
and

3.3. From highly solvating to moderately or weakly solvating
electrolytes
The modern design landscape for Li-S electrolytes can be under-
stood as a shift from maximizing LiPS dissolution toward tuning 
the solvation environment that controls both sulfur accessibility 
and parasitic crossover. Highly solvating electrolytes generally pre-
serve liquid-phase sulfur chemistry and improve sulfur utilization,
especially when thick electrodes require facile ionic access, but
they can also enlarge the dissolved LiPS inventory and intensify
lithium-side parasitic reactions [68,69]. Thus, the key design ques-
tion is no longer whether LiPS dissolution should simply be maxi-
mized or minimized, but which solvation regime best balances
sulfur accessibility, shuttle suppression, Li2S conversion, and
lithium compatibility under practical E/S constraints. Fig. 2 sum-

Weakly or sparingly solvating electrolytes emerged as one

triﬂamide solvent can deliver very high Coulombic efﬁciency even 
without LiNO3, reinforcing the idea that solvation engineering
itself can take over part of the anti-shuttle role classically assigned

because they suppress LiPS crossover thermodynamically and can
enable higher-energy operation with lower electrolyte inventory.

the electrolyte becomes too weakly solvating, sulfur utilization is 
no longer limited mainly by shuttle but by cathode-side reaction 
kinetics and the morphology of Li 2S formation. Correlative kinetic

if it beneﬁts the lithium side, because reduced LiPS availability and
constrained interfacial exchange increase activation polarization
[68]. Chen et al. and Li et al. made this point explicitly by showing 
that in weakly solvating lean-electrolyte systems, the dominant 
penalty is not concentration polarization but sluggish charge-
transfer kinetics associated with Li2S nucleation and growth, which
can cap capacity despite good shuttle suppression [73]. In practice, 
weak solvation can shrink the soluble reaction space so effectively

3.5. Practical electrolyte architectures beyond conventional liquid
formulations
As the ﬁeld moved closer to practical conditions, it became 
increasingly clear that merely changing solvent ratios within an 
otherwise conventional liquid electrolyte was often insufﬁcient. 
Several inﬂuential approaches therefore began to treat electrolyte 
design as an architectural problem, meaning not only what mole-
cules are present, but also how the sulfur reaction environment 
is spatially organized, replenished, or compartmentalized. One of
the clearest examples is the recognition that electrolyte inventory
itself must be engineered. Qin et al. showed that extremely high
areal-capacity sulfur cathodes become much more viable when
paired with an electrolyte reservoir concept because thick elec-
trodes otherwise suffer from local depletion, poor wetting continu-
ity, and progressive loss of access to active sulfur during cycling
[18]. Zerrin et al. reached a related conclusion from a different 
angle by showing that capacity fading is minimized not at either 
E/S extreme, but in a balanced regime where the electrolyte is nei-
ther too scarce to sustain interfacial transport nor so excessive that
it ampliﬁes free polysulﬁde migration [85]. 
This architectural view opened the door to more radical elec-
trolyte formats. Hou et al. introduced an encapsulating lithium-
polysulﬁde electrolyte in which LiPS species are surrounded by a
nanostructured solvent shell designed to suppress parasitic reac-
tion with lithium metal without eliminating the dissolved reaction
route entirely [86]. This is not a conventional additive strategy. It is 
a direct attempt to wrap reactive sulfur intermediates in a more 
selective local environment. Anti-solvent-tamed quasi-solid-state 
chemistry follows a similar architectural logic. Liu et al. used aro-
matic anti-solvents to break the continuity of a sparingly solvating
medium into chemically distinct subdomains, creating one envi-
ronment that can dissolve elemental sulfur and another that keeps
LiPS solubility low, thereby reconstructing the reaction landscape
instead of simply making the whole liquid more or less solvating
[87]. 
Other approaches went further by introducing functional spe-
cies that actively redeﬁne the reaction pathway under lean condi-

3.6. Electrolyte-regulated interface formation on both the cathode and
lithium sides
One of the most consequential conceptual advances in Li-S 
research is the recognition that electrolyte design governs not only 
polysulﬁde solubility but also the simultaneous formation of inter-
faces on both electrodes. On the lithium side, this principle is now 
well established. Classical interphase studies on lithium metal
showed that SEI growth, corrosion, and dendrite ampliﬁcation
are strongly electrolyte-dependent and mutually reinforcing. Once
deposition becomes heterogeneous, the resulting morphology
feeds back into local current concentration, continued electrolyte
decomposition, and further interphase nonuniformity [94]. In Li-S 
cells, this feedback is even harsher because the SEI is exposed 
not only to solvent and salt decomposition products, but also to
sulfur and polysulﬁde species that can destabilize or chemically
bypass it [95]. Thus, whether an electrolyte produces a dense, 
inorganic-rich, ion-conductive interphase or a fragile, continuously
reforming one directly shapes lithium cycling efﬁciency and den-
drite tendency.

deliberate electrolyte-regulated interface formation. Surface-ﬁlm

mixed inorganic and organic layers on lithium that are much

The literature reviewed above shows that electrolyte chem-

4. Cathode hosts, polysulﬁde anchoring, and electrocatalytic

4.1. From physical conﬁnement to chemical anchoring and catalytic
conversion
The design logic of Li-S cathodes has undergone a clear concep-
tual transition. Early host design was dominated by physical con-
ﬁnement, in which porous carbons, hollow structures, and 
geometric reservoirs were expected to contain sulfur species 
within the cathode and buffer volume expansion. That logic 
remains useful, but it is now widely recognized as insufﬁcient 
because soluble LiPS species are not passive guests that can simply
be caged. Once formed, they redistribute through the electrolyte,
interact with the host surface, and either continue toward the
anode or re-enter the sulfur redox sequence depending on the local
chemical environment. Cathode engineering, therefore, moved
progressively from nonpolar conﬁnement toward polar anchoring,
where host surfaces are deliberately designed to bind LiPS species
through stronger chemical interactions [98,99]. TiN is a represen-
tative early case in this transition because it already departed from 
pure carbon conﬁnement by combining conductivity with strong 
chemisorption
toward
polysulﬁdes,
showing
that
adsorption
strength itself can be a primary design variable rather than an acci-
dental byproduct [98]. In parallel, electrolyte-side regulators such 
as ZrO(NO3)2 illustrated that selective interactions with dissolved 
sulfur species could suppress diffusion and redirect conversion
chemistry, further emphasizing that effective cathode control
requires chemical rather than merely geometric intervention [99]. 
The next step was the recognition that even strong anchoring is 
not automatically beneﬁcial if it only immobilizes intermediates 
without helping them continue along the redox pathway. This is 
why the ﬁeld moved from a trapping mindset to a trapping-
conversion mindset. ZnSe and N-doped carbon nanoreactors are
emblematic because they combine sulﬁphilic ZnSe sites, lithio-
philic N-doped carbon sites, and a hollow internal architecture,
thereby integrating conﬁnement, adsorption, ion transport, and
accelerated Li2S nucleation and decomposition into one host con-
cept [100]. The key insight is that the best hosts do not merely 
lower polysulﬁde mobility. They provide multiple complementary 
binding environments that keep intermediates electrochemically 
available. A similar lesson emerges from theoretical analysis of 
metal phthalocyanine covalent organic frameworks (COFs). Song 
et al. showed that very strong adsorption alone does not guarantee
good cathode behavior. Ti-centered or V-centered systems can
bind sulfur species effectively, yet overly strong or geometrically
unfavorable interactions can impede later sulfur conversion,
whereas Mn-centered COFs better balance conductivity, anchoring,
and Li2S oxidation kinetics [101]. This balance between retention 
and reversibility has now become central to catalyst-oriented cath-
ode design.
More recently, the cathode host has been redeﬁned again as an 
electrocatalytic reaction environment rather than as a sulfur con-
tainer. Functional barriers such as polymeric zwitterions make this 
shift especially clear. Their value lies not only in restricting LiPS
crossover, but also in selectively regulating sulfur-species trans-
port while preserving rapid lithium-ion motion, thereby stabilizing
the chemical space in which sulfur conversion occurs [102]. In this 
more mature view, conﬁnement, anchoring, and catalysis are not 
competing paradigms but an evolutionary sequence. Physical 
architecture still matters, yet it now serves as the scaffold for polar 
adsorption and catalytic conversion. Strong adsorption still mat-
ters, yet only when it is paired with low conversion barriers and
rapid bidirectional kinetics. The best cathode hosts, therefore, no
longer behave as inert reservoirs. They function as chemically
programmed and kinetically active microreactors that control 
where polysulﬁdes reside, how long they remain soluble, and
how efﬁciently they are converted during cycling [100–102]. This 
conceptual evolution is schematically summarized in Fig. 3. The 
cathode host ﬁrst acts as a physical reservoir for soluble sulfur spe-
cies, but its function progressively expands toward polar anchoring 
and catalytic mediation. In this framework, sulfur-chain intermedi-
ates are retained within a polar or catalytic porous host and are
then converted into lithiated sulfur deposits during discharge,
while the reverse process is promoted during charge. The key
design target is therefore not maximum conﬁnement alone, but
reversible adsorption-conversion coupling that remains effective
under practical operating conditions

cathode-side material platform. The reason is that vacancies do

nonequivalent active sites that can stabilize different sulfur species

4.3. Heterostructures and interfacial catalysts for accelerating
bidirectional sulfur redox
If vacancy engineering tunes local active sites within one phase, 
heterostructure design introduces a second and often more power-
ful lever, namely interface creation. In Li-S cathodes, interfaces can 
redistribute charge, create built-in ﬁelds, expose chemically com-
plementary domains, and separate functional roles between 
adsorption and catalysis. This matters because sulfur redox is
intrinsically bidirectional. Efﬁcient discharge requires rapid poly-
sulﬁde reduction and Li2S nucleation, whereas efﬁcient charge
requires facile Li2S decomposition and sulfur reformation. A single
chemically uniform surface often struggles to optimize both direc-
tions simultaneously. Interfacial catalysts address this challenge by 
allowing different domains to specialize while remaining electron-
ically coupled. CeO2−x and Fe interfaces are an early and persuasive 
example. Oxygen-defective CeO 2 contributes strong sulﬁphilic
adsorption, whereas Fe improves conductivity and interfacial elec-
tron transfer, together enabling faster polysulﬁde conversion than
either component alone [114]. Even simpler CeO2−x and carbon 
nanotube (CNT) sulfur composites point in the same direction 
because oxide-carbon integration already shows that catalytic oxi-
des become far more useful when coupled to electronically efﬁ-
cient conductive frameworks rather than deployed in isolation
[115]. 
704
Recent heterostructures make the interfacial mechanism even 
more explicit. CoS2 at Co3O4 nanosheets use sulﬁde-oxide coupling 
to adjust cobalt electronic structure, shift the d-band center, and
combine strong adsorption with rapid lithium-ion and electron
transport [116]. Oxygen-defect-rich CeO2−x at ZrO2 Mott-Schottky 
catalysts go further by exploiting tunable interfacial charge redis-
tribution. The built-in electric ﬁeld across the heterojunction accel-
erates electron ﬂow and lowers the barriers for both LiPS reduction
and Li2S oxidation, making the interface itself a catalytic entity
[117]. A bidirectional Ti3C2 and (NiCo)0.85Se catalyst illustrates 
the same logic in a functionally partitioned form, where the MXene 
domain favors LiPS reduction while the selenide domain facilitates
Li2S decomposition, so the composite improves both discharge and
charge rather than only one side of the cycle [118]. CoS2 at carbon, 
though structurally simpler, also conﬁrms that intimate conductive 
coupling around catalytically active sulﬁde domains is crucial for
turning adsorption into actual rate enhancement [119]. 
Interfacial catalysis is not limited to inorganic heterojunctions. 
COF-based systems demonstrate that molecularly designed inter-

4.4. Strain, facet, phase, and electronic-structure regulation
Strain, facet, phase, and electronic-structure regulation are best 
understood as different ways of manipulating the same underlying 
variable, namely the energetic alignment between catalyst surface 
states and sulfur redox intermediates. The point is not merely to 
expose a new morphology or stabilize a different crystal phase 
for its own sake, but to tune adsorption strength, charge transfer, 
lithium-ion diffusion, and Li2S nucleation and decomposition bar-
riers toward a more balanced catalytic landscape. Density func-
tional theory (DFT) studies on PtS2, TiC, and TiS2 all support this
framework. In these systems, applied strain modiﬁes band struc-
ture and surface reactivity enough to change LiPS adsorption and
downstream conversion barriers without necessarily sacriﬁcing
conductivity [123–125]. TiC is especially illustrative because the 
carbon p-band center was proposed as a useful descriptor for judg-
ing how strain simultaneously inﬂuences anchoring and catalysis,
while compressive strain in TiS2 lowers key sulfur-reduction barri-
ers and improves lithium-ion migration [124,125]. 
Experimental work reaches similar conclusions while ground-
ing them in real catalyst architectures. Weak-crystallinity-
induced lattice strain in SnO2 improves LiPS adsorption and accel-
erates conversion under both high-rate and lean-electrolyte condi-
tions, suggesting that local strain can be deliberately introduced as
a practical catalytic handle rather than treated as an accidental
defect [126]. Strain-induced MXenes provide another example, 
where octahedral distortion and a downward shift of the d-band
center
enhance
charge
transfer
and
LiPS
conversion
[127]. 
Surface-strained MoS2 makes this relationship particularly explicit 
because strain moves the d-band center closer to the Fermi level 
and creates more highly active surface sites, which in turn improve
polysulﬁde adsorption, lithium-ion transport, and long-cycle cat-
alytic stability [128]. Compressive-strain-regulated FeOOH adds 
an important nuance by showing that more interaction is not 
always better. An optimal strain level can lower the d-p energy
mismatch enough to balance adsorption and desorption, thereby
avoiding the penalty of overbinding reaction intermediates [129]. 
Facet engineering and phase control should be interpreted 
within the same descriptor-based framework. Facet-engineered 
WS2 with dominant 100 exposure exhibits more metallic elec-
tronic character and earlier onset for LiPS redox, illustrating that
surface orientation changes both the density and the type of acces-
sible active states [130]. Likewise, crystal facet engineering in ZnTe
and ZnO-derived interfacial structures generates polarization elec-
tric ﬁelds that inﬂuence sulfur conversion and ion redistribution,
effectively translating crystallographic control into a reaction-
ﬁeld effect [131]. Phase engineering of MoS2 adds a closely related 
dimension because switching between phases changes conductiv-
ity, orbital structure, and sulfur-species interaction, making phase
selection a route to controlling the same catalytic descriptors that
strain or facet exposure modulate [132].

Taken together, these studies argue against writing strain, facet, 
and phase regulation as isolated subtopics. They belong to one 
comparative dimension centered on electronic-structure tuning. 
Whether the perturbation comes from elastic distortion, crystallo-
graphic orientation, polymorph selection, or induced electric polar-
ization, the target remains the same, which is to place sulfur
intermediates in a binding regime that is strong enough for reten-
tion but weak enough for continued turnover while lowering the
kinetic barriers of both reduction and oxidation steps.
4.5. Single-atom and high-entropy catalytic motifs as frontier
directions
Among recent catalyst-design directions, single-atom and high-
entropy motifs stand out because they move the ﬁeld from simply 
increasing active-site density toward deliberately engineering local 
coordination environments. Single-atom catalysts are especially 
attractive in Li-S systems because isolated metal centers can pro-
vide well-deﬁned adsorption and catalytic sites without introduc-
ing large inactive domains or blocking conductive pathways. More 
importantly, recent work shows that isolated atoms alone are not
the endpoint. Their catalytic performance depends critically on
how their coordination shell is constructed. Co-N4 single-atom
sites have already demonstrated that atomically dispersed centers
can simultaneously regulate polysulﬁdes and lithium-ion ﬂux,
functioning as multifunctional sulfur and lithium regulators rather
than as narrow cathode catalysts [133]. The next conceptual step 
was to weaken or asymmetrize that coordination shell. Deﬁciently 
coordinated In-N3 single-atom catalysts outperform more conven-
tional In-N4 analogues because the unsaturated environment
increases indium and sulfur orbital hybridization, raising both LiPS
afﬁnity and bidirectional catalytic activity under practical sulfur
loading and lean-electrolyte conditions [134]. 
Vacancy-assisted single-atom systems push this idea further by 
coupling isolated atoms to a second local asymmetry. In single-
atom Mo anchored on sulfur-vacancy-rich Co9S8, the catalytic 
behavior does not arise from Mo alone, nor from sulfur vacancies
alone, but from their coupled inﬂuence on adsorption energy and
conversion kinetics [135]. Here, the vacancy acts as part of the 
active motif, showing that the relevant design unit is not the atom 
by itself, but the atom-plus-neighborhood ensemble. This distinc-
tion matters because it reframes single-atom catalysis from a dis-
persion problem into an environment-engineering problem.
High-entropy materials extend the same philosophy from iso-
lated sites to compositionally complex surfaces. High-entropy 
single-atom catalysts suggest that multiple atomically dispersed 
metals can create a broader ensemble of adsorption and catalytic 
states than a monometallic single-atom catalyst, allowing different
metals to contribute differently to LiPS capture and conversion. The
reported ﬁve-metal system is a direct example of such cooperative
site diversity [136]. High-entropy sulﬁdes, high-entropy oxides, 
and high-entropy perovskites generalize the strategy at the nanos-
cale. Their value lies in lattice distortion, broadened local coordina-
tion statistics, and multi-metal synergy, which together create
surfaces with distributed yet chemically rich active motifs for
adsorption and liquid-solid conversion [137–139]. Grapevine-like 
high-entropy oxide interlayers show that this concept also remains
effective when implemented as an interfacial network, where por-

ous transport architecture and entropy-stabilized catalytic 
nanoparticles work together [140].

What uniﬁes these frontier directions is a shift in design lan-

lysts, that means tuning coordination number, heteroatom iden-
tity, and nearby vacancies. For high-entropy catalysts, it means 
exploiting local compositional disorder to generate a spectrum of
active motifs rather than one canonical site. In both cases, the fron-
tier is not smaller catalysts or more complex compositions for their
own sake, but ﬁner control over the local environment that deter-
mines how sulfur intermediates adsorb, transform, and desorb
during cycling.

4.7. Section-end critical assessment
Cathode-side catalysis has clearly matured beyond the early 
stage of improving only rate capability in dilute laboratory cells. 
Recent work shows convincing progress toward high sulfur load-
ing, lean-electrolyte compatibility, and even pouch-cell relevance,
especially when active-site design is coupled with interfacial con-
trol and local-environment engineering [134,146,147]. This is a 
meaningful shift. The ﬁeld no longer treats cathode hosts as pas-
sive sulfur containers, but as catalytic reaction environments that
determine whether polysulﬁdes are trapped, converted, or allowed
to accumulate into kinetic and transport penalties.
At the same time, the strongest recent studies also expose the 
limits of current evidence. Even highly active catalysts can look 
more effective than they truly are when tested at low loading, gen-
erous electrolyte content, or short cycle windows. Practical rele-
vance requires more than high initial capacity or excellent rate
retention. A reliable catalytic design must continue to function
when sulfur loading is high, electrolyte is limited, and Li2S deposi-
tion becomes spatially constrained. Otherwise, the evidence
remains only semi-practical [146,147]. The importance of deﬁ-
ciently coordinated single-atom indium catalysts is especially 
instructive here because their signiﬁcance lies not only in 
improved kinetics, but in demonstrating that catalytic beneﬁt can
survive under pouch-cell-level energy targets and constrained E/S
conditions [134]. 
The main challenge for the next stage is therefore validation 
under genuinely coupled practical constraints. Cathode catalysts 
should increasingly be judged by whether they preserve 
adsorption-conversion synergy, low activation polarization, and
reversible deposition pathways in cells that simultaneously impose
high loading, lean electrolyte, and long cycling. Only under those
conditions can cathode-side catalysis be regarded as a robust route 
toward practical Li-S batteries rather than as an elegant but still
partial materials solution [134,146,147].

5. Lithium metal anode protection and interfacial stabilization
in polysulﬁde-rich systems
5.1. Intrinsic reactivity between lithium metal and polysulﬁde species
The lithium metal anode in a Li-S battery is not merely a reser-
voir of lithium. It is a chemically active reductant placed in direct 
thermodynamic conﬂict with soluble sulfur intermediates. That 
incompatibility begins before any sophisticated interphase engi-
neering is applied. Early atomistic work showed that the lithium
surface is highly reactive not only toward electrolyte salts, but also
toward polysulﬁdes, with LiTFSI and Li2S8 both undergoing reduc-
tive decomposition on metallic lithium while common ether sol-
vents remain comparatively less reactive on the simulated time
scale [13]. This means that the anode-side problem is not simply 
dendrite growth in the abstract. Lithium continuously encounters 
dissolved sulfur species that can accept electrons chemically, con-
sume active lithium outside the intended electrochemical path-
way, and simultaneously rewrite the interphase into new forms
that are not necessarily protective. In practical terms, every shuttle
event is also an interfacial reaction event, and every interfacial
reaction event can alter the future pathway of lithium deposition.
Experimental evidence conﬁrms that this intrinsic reactivity is 
strongly species-dependent but never benign. HPLC-based studies 
found that longer-chain polysulﬁdes, especially those containing 
six or more sulfur atoms, react readily with lithium metal, whereas
LiNO3 can only suppress rather than eliminate this reaction ten-
dency [151]. More controlled contamination experiments reached 
the same conclusion from the reversibility side. Introducing 
deﬁned Li2Sn species to predeposited lithium consistently lowered
Coulombic efﬁciency and cycle life, with longer-chain polysulﬁdes
causing more severe reversibility loss than shorter ones [152]. 
These results are important because they separate shuttle damage 
from vague full-cell complexity and show directly that polysulﬁde 
identity matters. The most mobile and soluble sulfur species are 
also the ones most capable of chemically attacking the anode, so
lithium loss is not a uniform background process but a chain-
length-sensitive parasitic reduction network.
Recent mechanistic work sharpens this picture further by show-
ing that the ﬁrst product of lithium-polysulﬁde contact is often not

<<PAGE 17>>
a passivating barrier, but a reactive Li2S-rich interphase. Bi et al. 
argued that LiPS reacts with lithium faster than the salt-derived 
interface can stabilize, producing a surface enriched in Li2S that
remains highly active toward continued polysulﬁde attack [153]. 
That conclusion aligns closely with DFT studies showing that even 
when a model SEI is present, polysulﬁde reduction still proceeds
readily and tends toward Li2S formation at or within the interphase
[154]. Kamphaus and Balbuena further showed that Li2S nucleation 
itself stabilizes reduced polysulﬁde products and substantially 
reshapes the reaction energetics, implying that once Li2S begins
to form at the lithium side, the system can become self-
reinforcing rather than self-limiting [155]. This is a crucial point 
for the logic of this review. Li2S at the anode is not automatically 
evidence of successful sulfur utilization. It can also be the ﬁnger-
print of irreversible parasitic reduction.

5.2. SEI chemistry engineered by sulfur species, salts, and molecular
additives
If intrinsic lithium-polysulﬁde reactivity deﬁnes the problem, 
then SEI engineering deﬁnes the ﬁrst major class of solutions. In 
Li-S batteries, however, the SEI cannot be understood through 
the same logic used for conventional carbonate-based lithium-
metal systems. The interface is continuously exposed to sulfur spe-
cies, so the most effective SEI is rarely a purely salt-derived ﬁlm. 
Instead, the best-performing interphases are hybrid structures 
whose composition emerges from interactions among sulfur spe-
cies, ﬂuorinated fragments, nitrate-derived oxides, and deliber-
ately introduced molecular additives. One important branch of
this literature focuses on LiF-rich protection. Fluorinated ether
electrolytes have already shown that lithium can be stabilized
when the surface becomes enriched with LiF together with sulfate,
sulﬁte, and sulﬁde species, producing a layered sulfur-ﬂuorine
interphase that is less reactive than the conventional ether-
derived surface [78]. Later work made this concept more explicit 
by constructing LiF-rich regulation layers or inducing on-site ﬂuo-
rination through BiF3-derived chemistry, both of which promoted
more uniform plating while reducing polysulﬁde corrosion and
improving
lithium
utilization
under
constrained
conditions
[156,157].

Yet LiF alone is not the whole story. Sulfur-containing species 
inside the SEI can be either beneﬁcial or harmful depending on oxi-

valence sulfur species such as sulﬁte and sulfate stabilize lithium
deposition much more effectively than low-valence Li2S and
Li2S2-dominated surfaces, which instead correlate with dendritic
growth and dead-lithium accumulation [158]. Earlier interphase-
characterization work had already hinted at this layered logic by 
proposing that effective shuttle-suppressing SEI consists of a
sulfate-rich outer layer combined with lower-valence sulfur and
LiNxOy components closer to the lithium surface [159]. The nitrate 
literature ﬁts naturally into this picture. LiNO3 was ﬁrst shown to 
produce denser mixed inorganic and organic ﬁlms containing LiNx-
Oy species [52], while lanthanum nitrate extended the nitrate-
additive concept by promoting composite passivation layers that
further reduce surface reactivity and improve morphology [96]. 
Taken together, these studies suggest that the relevant question 
is not whether sulfur appears in the SEI, but what sulfur valence
states and structural hierarchy are created there.
This helps explain why organosulfur and additive-mediated 
interphases have become so inﬂuential. Organosulfur-containing 
SEI generated by thiophenol-type additives can shield lithium from
incoming polysulﬁde intermediates more effectively than conven-
tional ﬁlms, even under high sulfur loading, lean electrolyte, and
thin-lithium conditions [160]. Related amino-thiophenol additives 
form smoother and denser protection layers, again underscoring 
that molecular functionality can be designed to tune both SEI
chemistry and interfacial morphology at once [161]. SOCl2-based 
additives similarly create protective surface chemistry in situ while
improving electrochemical stability [162]. The same design princi-
ple extends to artiﬁcial or semi-artiﬁcial barriers with ion-selective 
character. Naﬁon-d erived layers reduce parasitic LiPS contact and
extend cycling life in working cells [163], while lithiated Naﬁon 
can go further by simultaneously shielding polysulﬁdes and regu-
lating lithium deposition [164]. Carbon-nitride-based bifunctional 
protection adds another version of this idea, using nitrogen-rich 
sites to homogenize lithium-ion ﬂux while also reducing direct
electrolyte and polysulﬁde attack [165]. 
The most important conceptual shift from this body of work is 
that SEI formation in Li-S batteries is not a passive side effect of 
electrolyte decomposition, but an actively engineerable reaction 
zone. Nanda and Manthiram showed that lithium deposition
dynamics depend jointly on salt chemistry, LiNO3 action, and poly-
sulﬁde participation, so the lithium-electrolyte interface must be
treated as a multi-component kinetic system rather than a ﬁxed
ﬁlm
[166]. Mixed-ether electrolytes and other interfacial-
regulation strategies reinforce that idea by changing both polysul-
ﬁde solubility and lithium compatibility simultaneously [167], 
while more direct protective-layer studies with Pt nanolayers or 
related barriers show that even thin interfacial modiﬁers can
noticeably reshape shuttle-induced degradation pathways [168]. 
Collectively, these studies argue for a broader deﬁnition of SEI 
engineering in Li-S batteries. The target is not merely a mechani-
cally strong layer, but a chemical ly selective interphase that resists
continued sulfur-side attack, maintains ion transport, guides uni-
form deposition, and remains effective in the presence of dissolved
redox-active sulfur species [169]. 
5.3. Lithiophilic hosts and deposition regulation strategies
SEI chemistry alone cannot fully solve the lithium problem if 
host geometry still concentrates current and ampliﬁes local depo-
sition instability. This is why a second major strategy in Li-S anode 
protection focuses on lithiophilic hosts and deposition regulation.
The core idea is not simply to provide more space for lithium,
but to control where nucleation begins, how ions are distributed
across electrode thickness, and whether deposition can remain 
three-dimensionally uniform as capacity increases. Double-
gradient host design represents one of the clearest expressions of 
this logic. Yang et al. used a ZnO and Co3O4 carbon-nanoﬁber archi-
tecture to create simultaneous lithiophilicity and nucleation-
overpotential gradients, so incoming lithium ions are guided into
a more favorable deposition pathway rather than being left to
accumulate at isolated hot spots [170]. In this framework, the host 
is not a passive scaffold. It acts as a ﬁeld-shaping structure that
redistributes electrochemical preference across space.
709
Interlayer engineering can achieve a related outcome from a 
different position in the cell. Bifunctional carbon nanoﬁber inter-
layers reduce the tip effect at the lithium side, improve local elec-
tronic and ionic uniformity, and at the same time suppress part of
the LiPS crossover from the cathode [171]. This dual action matters 
because uniform deposition is easier to maintain when the anode 
sees both a smoother current distribution and a lower local con-
centration of incoming reactive sulfur species. The interlayer there-
fore works as a mesoscale regulator. It does not replace the lithium 
surface, but it changes the environment in which the surface oper-
ates. In practical terms, strategies like this are valuable because
they address the morphological expression of lithium instability
without relying only on chemistry. Even a chemically improved
SEI can fail if current constriction remains severe, whereas a con-
ductive and porous interlayer helps alleviate that structural driver.
Solid protective coatings push this concept toward stronger 
physical and chemical decoupling. LiPON is a useful example 
because it offers ion conductivity together with chemical stability
and mechanical strength, allowing it to suppress direct corrosion
while also enforcing more uniform plating and stripping at the bur-
ied lithium interface [172]. However, broader evaluation of con-
ducting ceramic layers shows that not every nominally lithium-
ion-conductive solid is suitable in a polysulﬁde-rich environment. 
Castro et al. found that several ceramic protectors retain bulk con-
ductivity yet still suffer meaningful surface reactions when
exposed to sulfur-containing electrolytes, and even the more
promising ﬁlms such as Li3PO4 and LiPON provide only limited pro-
tection in high-loading full cells [173]. This is a useful corrective to 
simplistic narratives about solid coatings. Chemical compatibilit y
with polysulﬁde-bearing liquid electrolytes is just as critical as
ionic transport.
Taken together, host and coating strategies reveal that deposi-
tion regulation is a multi-scale design problem. Lithiophilic gradi-
ents determine where lithium prefers to nucleate. Nanoﬁber 
interlayers smooth ﬂux and lessen crossover-driven perturbation.
Solid coatings impose a chemically more selective boundary but
must survive the sulfur environment to matter [170,171,173]. 
The best-performing designs therefore do not merely increase sur-
face area. They align host architecture, current distribution, and 
chemical protection so that lithium sees a less heterogeneous elec-
trochemical landscape from the beginning of plating onward. This 
is especially relevant for Li-S batteries because deposition nonuni-
formity is magniﬁed by the simultaneous presence of crossover 
species. A host that would appear sufﬁcient in a polysulﬁde-free
lithium-metal test may behave very differently once sulfur inter-
mediates perturb the local interface. Accordingly, lithiophilic
should be interpreted functionally here, meaning not merely
strong lithium afﬁnity, but the ability to convert that afﬁnity into
spatially
uniform
and
chemically
durable
deposition
under
sulfur-rich full-cell conditions.

engineering
Not all effective lithium-protection strategies act directly on the 
metal surface. In Li-S batteries, separator and interlayer engineer-

protection as a transport-management problem rather than only 
an interphase problem. A separator modiﬁed with catalytically 
active or chemically adsorptive components can reduce the ﬂux 
of soluble LiPS species reaching the anode, accelerate their recon-
version on the cathode-facing side, and in some cases homogenize
lithium-ion transport through the separator thickness. CoTe2 and
carbon-nanoﬁber-modiﬁed separators illustrate this well. The
coating not only blocks and catalytically converts polysulﬁdes
but also smooths local ionic ﬂux and helps suppress dendrite-

This indirect route becomes especially powerful in high-loading

Although such studies are often categorized as cathode-side or 
separator-side engineering, their relevance to the lithium anode 
is direct because every polysulﬁde molecule retained or rapidly 
reconverted upstream is one less species available to parasitically 
react with metallic lithium. The same logic applies to catalytic
interlayers based on oxygen-vacancy-rich FeOOH. These interlay-

surface alone, but the entire concentration gradient that drives sul-

The bifunctional carbon-nanoﬁber interlayer literature makes 
this coupling even clearer because the interlayer was explicitly 
designed to address both deposition uniformity and shuttle sup-

between Sections 5.3 and 5.4. Interlayers can act as deposition reg-
ulators when placed near lithium, but they can also serve as cross-

transit. The distinction between direct and indirect protection is 
therefore practical rather than absolute. What matters is whether
the separator or interlayer architecture reduces crossover ﬂux suf-

mation, and local plating instability in the full cell.
The broader implication of this review is that anode stabiliza-
tion can be achieved upstream. A separator or interlayer that low-
ers polysulﬁde residence time in the electrolyte, blocks long-range
diffusion, or catalytically shortens the dissolved-state lifetime of
sulfur intermediates effectively changes the chemical exposure
history of the lithium surface [110,113,174]. This is especially valu-
able because direct lithium coatings often fail when crossover 
remains too high. In that case, the anode spends every cycle 
rebuilding protection against a reactant stream that never truly 
disappears. Separator and interlayer engineering offer a comple-
mentary route by reducing the incoming attack itself. These 
approaches should therefore not be treated as merely auxiliary
cathode-side optimizations. In polysulﬁde-rich systems, they are
integral lithium-protection strategies because the state of the
anode is controlled as much by crossover ﬂux as by the intrinsic
quality of the surface ﬁlm already present on lithium [175]. 
5.5. Lithium inventory depletion, dead lithium, and anode-free
extensions
The practical meaning of lithium protection becomes clearest 
when lithium inventory is no longer effectively inﬁnite. Under this
constraint, the key question shifts from whether lithium can be
made somewhat more stable to how rapidly the active lithium 
reservoir is being depleted, immobilized, or rendered electrochem-
ically inaccessible. Anode-free and Li2S-based conﬁgurations are 
especially informative because they expose this issue without the 
masking effect of thick excess lithium. Nanda and Manthiram 
showed that in anode-free Li2S and nickel full cells, cycle life is fun-
damentally limited by cumulative lithium inventory loss, while the
failure mode is better described as interphase growth and dead-
lithium accumulation than as total disappearance of metallic
lithium [176]. This point is conceptually important because a cell 
can fail even while some metallic lithium still exists if that lithium
is buried beneath resistive decomposition products or electrically
isolated from the current collector.
710
Anode-free Li-S studies extend this practical diagnosis into 
design space. Gold-modiﬁed copper current collectors improve 
lithium nucleation and produce denser depositio n, enabling Li2S-
based anode-free full batteries with more credible energy-density
logic than lithium-excess cells [177]. Tellurium-stabilized lithium 
deposition pushes the concept further by using tellurium-derived 
interfacial chemistry to improve deposition reversibility in both
anode-free and lean-electrolyte conﬁgurations [178,179]. These 
studies matter not only because they improve cycling, but because 
they redeﬁne the anode challenge. Once no sacriﬁcial lithium 
reservoir is available, every parasitic reaction becomes a direct 
subtraction from usable cell life, and every deposition defect mat-
ters immediately rather than eventually. In such systems, lithium
protection is no longer a longevity enhancer layered on top of an
otherwise functional design. It is the enabling condition for the
design to exist at all.
Dead lithium has recently emerged as a particularly revealing 
metric in this context. Rather than treating inactive lithium as an 
inevitable by-product of limited reversibility, newer strategies 
attempt to remove or even recycle it. The regenerative redox medi-
ator reported for Li-S pouch cells is an important example because
it can chemically consume dead lithium at the anode side and then
be regenerated electrochemically at the cathode [175]. That 
approach does not merely protect lithium in the classical sense. 
It actively reclaims inventory that would otherwise be lost. Con-
ceptually, this shifts the ﬁeld from static protection toward 
dynamic lithium management. It also suggests that future practical
Li-S systems may need to combine deposition regulation with
inventory-recovery mechanisms, especially under fast-charge or
pouch-cell conditions where dead-lithium buildup can be severe.
At the same time, inventory-preserving designs still rely heavily 
on interfacial chemistry. On-site ﬂuorination strategies that form 
LiF and Li3Bi composite protectio n layers show that improving
lithium utilization is inseparable from suppressing dendrites and
polysulﬁde corrosion at the deposition interface [157]. The impli-
cation is that inventory retention is not achieved by one metric 
alone. It depends on how much lithium is chemically consumed, 
how much becomes electrically disconnected, and how much 
remains kinetically accessible through the evolving interphase. 
From this viewpoint, dead lithium, SEI thickening, deposition mor-
phology, and lithium utilization are all different observables of the
same underlying inventory problem. Practical Li-S research there-

5.6. Section-end critical assessment
The literature on lithium protection in Li-S batteries strongly 
supports one conclusion. The anode should not be treated as an 
isolated subsystem whose fate is determined only by local coatings 
or SEI composition. Practical failure emerges from a coupled set of 
variables that includes electrolyte formulation, crossover intensity, 
sulfur-species residence time, deposition heterogeneity, and the
operating behavior of the cathode itself. Shi et al. made this explicit
under practical conditions by showing that early failure can origi-
nate from cathode-anode crosstalk, where nonuniform sulfur-side
reactions map into uneven lithium plating and stripping and even-
tually trigger internal shorting [50]. In this sense, lithium degrada-
tion is a systems-level output rather than a purely local defect. The
anode records what the rest of the cell has allowed to reach it.
Inventory-focused work reinforces the same point from another 
angle. Nanda and Manthiram showed that lithium loss is governed 
not simply by direct consumption, but by the co-evolution of inter-
phase growth and electrochemical inaccessibility, so apparent
anode failure is inseparable from the cumulative reaction history
of the whole cell [176]. The long-recognized gap between coin 
and pouch cells offers a practical demonstration of why this mat-
ters. Cheng et al. showed that pouch-cell failure is dominated by
lithium pulverization, dead-lithium buildup, and rising polariza-
tion even when coin-cell analogues appear durable [16]. Once sul-
fur loading rises and electrolyte inventory is reduced to practical 
levels, the anode no longer beneﬁts from the forgiving conditions 
that often obscure degradation in small-format tests. Instead, every
crossover event and every deposition nonuniformity becomes
more consequential because there is less excess lithium and less
electrolyte to buffer mistakes.
Accordingly, the most credible lithium-protection strategies are 
the ones that remain effective under high-loading, lean-electrolyte,
and pouch-relevant conditions while preserving the active lithium
budget over time [16,50,176]. This is a stricter requirement than 
showing improved lithium-lithium cycling or smoother post-
mortem morphology. It demands that the strategy survive the full 
coupled environment of a working Li-S cell, where the cathode
operating window inﬂuences polysulﬁde generation, the elec-
trolyte determines both solvation and interphase chemistry, and
deposition heterogeneity can feed back into further crossover-
driven damage. The critical assessment of Section 5 is, therefore, 
that lithium protection should be evaluated as part of a closed-
loop system. Real progress will come from strategies that jointly
reduce crossover ﬂux, control interphase chemistry, maintain uni-
form deposition, and conserve lithium inventory at the full-cell
level rather than from approaches that optimize only one of these
dimensions in isolation [16,50,176].

6.1. What counts as practical Li-S batteries
The phrase practical Li-S battery should not be used loosely 
because the literature has repeatedly shown that apparently strong 
electrochemical results can look very different once the cell is eval-
uated at the device level rather than at the material-screening 
level. A practical claim begins with sulfur loading and areal capac-
ity. Cell-level analyses consistently argue that sulfur loading must
move well beyond the 1–2 mg cm−2 regime typical of exploratory
coin-cell studies and approach at least about 5–7 mg cm−2 if the
chemistry is to carry enough active material to matter in a real
stack [2–4]. In the same spirit, areal capacity should not be treated 
as a secondary output. It is the operative bridge between sulfur 
chemistry and pack-level relevance because a high gravimetric sul-
fur capacity obtained at low loading can still correspond to a weak 
electrode in practical terms. Chung and Manthiram therefore
framed practically necessary parameters as a coupled target set
rather than as isolated numbers, emphasizing high sulfur loading,
high sulfur fraction, low electrolyte inventory, and stable cycling
in the same cell [5,6]. 
The second indispensable metric is electrolyte budget. Hagen’s 
early cell-level treatment made it clear that the E/S ratio is not a
cosmetic reporting detail, but one of the dominant variables set-
ting speciﬁc energy [2]. Yang et al. later reinforced that conclusion 
from a broader engineering and cost perspective, showing that 
electrolyte mass can erase the apparent advantage of sulfur unless
E/S and lithium excess are both pushed down together [1]. McClos-
key reached a similar conclusion from a gravimetric and volumet-
ric modeling standpoint. If Li-S is expected to compete with 
advanced lithium-ion systems, then low E/S, high sulfur loading,
and thin protection layers or other low-inactive-mass interfaces
are mandatory rather than optional [4]. However, the E/S ratio 
should not be interpreted as an isolated indicator of practical rele-
vance. The same nominal E/S ratio can impose very different con-
straints depending on sulfur loading, cathode sulfur fraction, 
porosity, tortuosity, electrode thickness, separator uptake, and cell 
format. For example, an E/S ratio that appears lean at low sulfur 
loading may still correspond to a relatively forgiving liquid envi-
ronment, whereas the same E/S ratio in a thick, high-sulfur-
fraction cathode can generate severe underwetting, local LiPS con-
centration gradients, ionic-transport limitation, and heterogeneous
Li2S deposition. Similarly, a low E/S ratio improves projected
energy density only when it is paired with sufﬁcient sulfur fraction
and controlled inactive mass; otherwise, the beneﬁt can be offset

<<PAGE 21>>
by excessive carbon, binder, separator, or protective-layer content. 
Therefore, the E/S ratio should be reported and evaluated together 
with sulfur loading, areal capacity, sulfur fraction, cathode porosity
or density, separator electrolyte uptake, N/P ratio, and cell conﬁg-
uration, rather than being used as a standalone practicality metric.

The third criterion, closely related to manufacturability, is cell

whether the test is a coin cell or a pouch cell, how much lithium 
excess is present, and whether the cycling window and current 
density resemble conditions that a high-energy cell could actually 
sustain. Coin-cell studies remain useful for mechanistic interpreta-

cussing real viability. The recent ﬁeld standard has therefore
shifted toward reporting pouch-cell energy density, E/C or E/S,
anode thickness or N/P ratio, and failure transparency instead of
only sulfur-side capacity [1,20]. Even when electrolyte chemistry 
is improved, its density and mass fraction still matter. Liu et al. 
showed this elegantly by lowering electrolyte density and thereby 
improving pouch-cell speciﬁc energy at the same nominal E/S,
which means that practice must include not only how much elec-
trolyte is used but also how much it weighs [20]. Taken together, a 
practical Li-S battery is best deﬁned as one that simultaneously 
demonstrates meaningful sulfur loading, meaningful areal capac-
ity, lean and clearly reported electrolyte inventory, constrained
lithium excess, credible cell-level energy density, and cycling
behavior interpreted against realistic failure criteria rather than
idealized laboratory conditions.
Recent pouch-cell studies further reinforce that practical Li-S 
performance must be evaluated through a combined form-factor 
and failure-mode perspective rather than by sulfur-side capacity 
alone. Optimization of advanced Li-S pouch-cell form factors 
showed that E/S ratio, N/P ratio, sulfur loading, and pouch-cell 
capacity strongly reshape the dominant ageing pathway, with 
lithium metal powdering and polarization sometimes becoming 
more decisive than LiPS shuttling. More recent Ah-level pouch-
cell demonstrations have also shown that high speciﬁc energy
can be approached only when electrocatalyst stability, lean elec-
trolyte inventory, and limited lithium excess are considered
together. For example, protective catalytic-layer design enabled
an Ah-level Li-S pouch cell exceeding 500 Wh kg−1, while a Lewis
acidic Ca2+ electrolyte additive allowed 493 Wh kg−1 operation at
an E/S ratio of 2.4 lL mg−1 by mitigating polysulﬁde saturation,
electrolyte jamming, shuttle, and lithium corrosion [77,150,180].

6.2. Thick cathodes, low tortuosity, and electrolyte distribution
management
Once Li-S cells are pushed toward high sulfur loading, the 
meaning of a given E/S ratio becomes inseparable from cathode 
porosity, tortuosity, sulfur fraction, and electrode thickness, 
because the cathode stops behaving like a thin reactive ﬁlm and 
becomes a transport-limited porous body whose internal geometry 
can determine whether the electrolyte remains useful at all. That is 
why thick-cathode design in practical Li-S batteries is less about 
maximizing surface area and more about controlling pore accessi-
bility, tortuosity, density, and local electrolyte pathways. Qin et al.
provided one of the clearest early demonstrations of this shift by
showing that a 13 mg cm−2 sulfur cathode could deliver double-

enough to develop internal dry zones and concentration gradients.
Later work reﬁned that picture by showing that high loading 
does not require a highly porous and fragile architecture if trans-
port pathways are engineered more intelligently. Chung and Man-
thiram designed high-loading cathodes for lean-electrolyte
operation by combining low surface area with controlled nanopore
structure, thereby reducing the amount of electrolyte immobilized
on the nonproductive carbon surface while still preserving sulfur
accessibility [19]. Fu et al. then clariﬁed through multiscale model-
ing that low-porosity sulfur electrodes do not inevitably fail. They 
fail when low porosity is combined with high tortuosity or poorly
connected channels that prevent wetting and slow species redistri-

ger through-pores can maintain usable sulfur conversion even in 
dense electrodes, especially when characteristic pore geometry
allows electrolyte to invade the full thickness rather than remain
concentrated near the separator-facing region.
This design logic was made even more explicit by low-
tortuosity electrode studies. Feng and co-workers built a dense 
single-particle-layer sulfur electrode in which large particles
deﬁne relatively straight ionic pathways, reducing the penalty that
dense packing usually imposes on wetting and species transport
[182]. The practical importance of that result is conceptual as 
much as electrochemical because it reframes electrode density 
and ionic accessibility as co-design variables rather than as ﬁxed 
trade-offs of default slurry casting. Kim et al. extended the same
thinking to binder-mediated structure control, showing that
lithium-containing additives can modulate poly(vinylidene ﬂuo-

improved wetting and lower effective tortuosity under lean-
electrolyte conditions [183]. In that case, the additive is not simply 
a chemical promoter. It is a microstructural actuator that changes
how liquid actually penetrates and redistributes in the electrode.
Binder design has accordingly become part of electrolyte man-
agement itself. Chen et al. introduced an interweaving three-
dimensional ion-conductive binder network that improved 
lithium-ion transport, maintained structural integrity at high load-
ing, and reduced the dependence of sulfur utilization on a locally
ﬂooded liquid phase [184]. Read together, these studies show that 
practical thick cathodes are governed by a three-part logic. Dense 
architectures are desirable because they reduce inactive volume. 
Low tortuosity is essential because dense packing otherwise suffo-
cates ionic access. Electrolyte-distribution management is the real
systems variable linking the two [181,182,184]. The practical cath-

6.3. Lean-electrolyte chemistry and kinetic regulation under practical
constraints
Lean-electrolyte Li-S operation is often described as an inven-
tory problem, but the deeper challenge is that electrolyte scarcity 
changes the reaction environment itself. Under practical con-
straints, the cell is no longer buffered by excess liquid, so wetting 
continuity, sulfur-species distribution, ionic conductivity, and 
interfacial kinetics all become tightly coupled. Zerrin et al. showed
that the E/S ratio sits at the center of this coupling. Too little elec-
trolyte accelerates transport failure and rapid fading, whereas too
much electrolyte increases the dissolved polysulﬁde inventory
and ampliﬁes lithium-side attack [85]. Sun et al. arrived at a com-
patible conclusion from high-loading cells, ﬁnding that E/S strongly 
controls power capability and, together with sulfur loading, also
shapes cycle life by altering the severity of lithium corrosion and
Coulombic inefﬁciency [23]. These studies establish that the 
lean-electrolyte regime is not just less solvent. It is a different elec-
trochemical operating state.
The chemistry becomes more subtle once weakly solvating or 
sparingly solvating electrolytes are introduced to suppress the 
shuttle. Jin et al. showed that in lean ether electrolytes, dissolved 
long-chain polysulﬁdes can worsen wetting and conductivity
enough to trigger internal contact loss and charge-transfer growth,
helping explain why poorly managed lean cells fail abruptly [49]. 
One response has been to engineer the salt-solvent system so that 
lithium-side protection and sulfur-side accessibility are balanced 
more deliberately. Kirchhoff et al. demonstrated that even within 
the same DME and HFE solvent family, salt choice can strongly
change conductivity, polysulﬁde solubility, and high-rate behavior,
with LiFSI offering the best combination in their lean-electrolyte
window [84]. Lu et al. then visualized the consequence of these 
choices directly in pouch cells, showing that lean electrolyte does 
not remain static after ﬁlling. It redistributes during rest and
cycling, and local dry or underwetted regions can shrink, merge,
or migrate in ways that strongly inﬂuence reaction uniformity [42]. 
At the cathode level, the dominant bottleneck under lean condi-
tions increasingly appears to be activation-controlled conversion 
rather than pure ohmic starvation. Operando and decoupled-
polarization studies by Zhao et al. and Chen et al. showed that
the high-order-to-low-order conversion region develops large

6.4. Pouch-cell demonstrations and ageing pathways
Pouch-cell demonstrations are indispensable because they con-
vert abstract practical metrics into measurable whole-cell behav-
ior, including the spatial and temporal failure modes that coin 
cells often hide. The 400 Wh kg−1 class pouch cells reported by
Ye et al. and Leonet et al. are especially informative because they
move beyond headline energy density and examine what begins
to fail ﬁrst during operation [21,186]. Ye et al. found that even after 
initial cycling, sulfur redox on the cathode side remained surpris-
ingly reversible, while the lithium metal anode already showed
signiﬁcant morphological degradation [21]. Leonet et al., using a 
1.45 Ah pouch cell, tracked ageing electrochemically and showed 
a transition from an early activation period to accelerated decay
around the ﬁrst few dozen cycles, with impedance evolution point-
ing to increasing lithium-ion transport limitations linked to
electrolyte-anode side reactions [186]. Together, these studies 
make an important point. Pouch-cell ageing cannot be inferred 
from sulfur capacity retention alone because the earliest practical
penalty
may
emerge
through
electrolyte
consumption
and

Failure-analysis work deepens that interpretation. Chen et al. 
showed that a 400 Wh kg−1 pouch cell can lose performance 
through growing polarization on the second discharge plateau,
and that reinjecting electrolyte partly restores capacity, directly
implicating electrolyte depletion as a principal ageing pathway
[22]. This observation matters because it separates two phenom-
ena that are often conﬂated, namely sulfur loss and reaction-
environment collapse. In these pouch cells, failure is not simply
that sulfur becomes inactive. Rather, the electrolyte inventory
and local interfacial conditions become too poor to sustain the
intended conversion pathway [22]. Shi et al. added a spatial dimen-
sion by showing that practical high-energy pouch cells develop 
strong reaction heterogeneity as electrolyte is consumed and redis-
tributed, with underfed regions propagating from the interior
toward the rest of the cell [39]. That spatial nonuniformity is not 
a cosmetic feature. Once parts of a pouch cell run drier or more
polarized than others, local aging accelerates unevenly and feeds
back into global failure.
The most ambitious demonstration in this literature is the near-
700 Wh kg−1 pouch cell reported by Cheng et al. [17]. Its impor-
tance lies not in proving that ultra-high-energy Li-S is already 
solved, but in showing where the practical boundary now sits. By 
combining high sulfur loading, ultra-thin lithium, very low elec-
trolyte inventory, and a redox mediator, the study demonstrated
that almost 700 Wh kg−1 is physically reachable at the pouch-
cell level for a few cycles [17]. Yet that very success also exposed 
the fragility of the regime because rapid failure followed, again
traced mainly to electrolyte exhaustion and severe polarization
[17]. In other words, the demonstration validated the energy pro-
mise while simultaneously highlighting how narrow the opera-
tional margin remains.

6.5. Early failure, cathode-anode crosstalk, and scale-up penalties
The failure of practical Li-S batteries is often misdescribed as a 
simple matter of coin cells not scaling. In reality, scale-up exposes 
couplings that small ﬂooded cells can partially mask, especially 
when electrolyte is abundant and current distribution is relatively 
forgiving. Cheng et al. articulated this gap clearly by comparing
long-lived coin-cell behavior with much shorter-lived pouch-cell
operation and showing that replacing the aged lithium anode in
a failed pouch cell can restore most of the lost sulfur-side capacity
[16]. That result sharply reoriented the interpretation of scale-up 
penalties. The main problem is not that sulfur chemistry suddenly 
changes in a larger format, but that the lithium anode becomes
unable to survive the harsher spatial and chemical environment
of a practical cell [16]. 
Lean-electrolyte failure studies explain why this happens. Jin 
et al. showed that when E/S is pushed down, dissolved long-
chain polysulﬁdes degrade electrolyte wetting and conductivity,
causing contact loss, higher internal resistance, and ultimately a
self-reinforcing collapse in local reaction conditions [49]. In a small 
coin cell with generous wetting and short transport distances, that
collapse can be delayed or at least spatially averaged. In a pouch 
cell, the same instability can become regionally concentrated and 
therefore far more destructive. Shi et al. demonstrated this directly
by identifying early failure under practical conditions as a crosstalk
problem between the sulfur cathode and the lithium anode [50]. 
Nonuniform sulfur reduction produces nonuniform ionic ﬂux and 
local electrolyte states, which are then mirrored at the lithium sur-
face as uneven plating and stripping. The result is an internally
coupled failure pathway in which cathode heterogeneity ampliﬁes
anode roughening, and anode roughening in turn worsens the cell’s
ability to sustain stable cathode conversion [50].

6.6. Practical relevance of electrocatalysts and coordinated materials

6.7. Section-end critical assessment
The practical-cell literature leaves little doubt that the center 
of gravity in Li-S research has shifted. The decisive problem is no 
longer how to maximize local sulfur activity under permissive 
laboratory conditions, but how to maintain a viable reaction 
environment once electrolyte and lithium inventory are tightly 
constrained. Pouch-cell studies around 400 Wh kg−1 and even
near 700 Wh kg−1 show that the chemistry can indeed approach
compelling energy-density territory, but they also show that the
cell becomes fragile as soon as electrolyte redistribution, polar-
ization growth, and lithium-side degradation outrun the ability
of
the

to
keep

synchronized
[17,21,22]. This is why practical success should not be judged 
by high sulfur utilization alone. A cell may still fail early if the
usable reaction environment collapses faster than the sulfur
chemistry itself.
The imaging and failure-analysis literature clariﬁes what that 
collapse looks like. Lu et al. showed that lean electrolyte in pouch 
cells is dynamically redistributed rather than permanently ﬁxed at
ﬁlling, meaning that local underwetting and local overfeeding can
both emerge during operation [42]. Shi et al. further showed that 
these spatial differences translate directly into reaction hetero-
geneity and then into crosstalk -driven early failure, where nonuni-
form cathode behavior maps onto nonuniform lithium deposition
and internal shorting risk [39,50]. The signiﬁcance of these ﬁndings 
is methodological as well as mechanistic. They reveal why appar-
ently successful coin-cell materials can become unconvincing at 
scale. If a strategy does not control where the electrolyte goes,
where the current concentrates, and how the lithium interface
responds, then its beneﬁt may disappear once the cell enters a
pouch format [39,42,50]. 
Accordingly, the strongest practical strategies in this section are 
the ones that integrate multiple controls at once. The 400 and 
700 Wh kg−1 demonst rations, despite their limitations, show that
high energy density becomes plausible only when electrolyte,
lithium excess, and electrode loading are engineered together
[17,22]. At the same time, the ageing and heterogeneity studies 
show that such integration must extend beyond initial assembly 
to the full cycling environment. Electrolyte must remain well dis-

ble,

must

uniformly even as the local liquid inventory changes [21,39,42]. 
This is the key practical lesson of the section. The route from mate-
rials advances to a usable Li-S cell is not a linear scaling of sulfur 
hosts or catalysts. It is the coordinated management of electrolyte,
interface, and electrode architecture under constrained inventory,
with pouch-cell evidence serving as the most honest test of
whether that coordination truly holds.

7. Integrated design rules and outlook
Before deriving the integrated design rules, it is useful to place 
the development of practical Li-S batteries in a historical context.
Fig. 5 summarizes the major conceptual transitions of the ﬁeld, 
from early recognition of LiPS shuttle and LiNO3-mediated interfa-

regulation, catalytic sulfur conversion, lithium-inventory manage-

under lean-electrolyte conditions.
7.1. Electrolyte-ﬁrst but not electrolyte-alone
Building on the validation-linked framework deﬁned in Sec-
tion 1.3, the following design rules organize practical Li-S strate-
gies by the microenvironmental variables they regulate, the 
failure-mode shifts they suppress, and the practical validation cri-
teria required to prove their relevance. The ﬁrst integrated design 
rule is that the electrolyte sets the reaction landscape, but its ben-
eﬁt becomes durable only when it is matched with cathode cat-
alytic sites and lithium-stable interfaces. This rule follows from 
the fact that the electrolyte does far more than provide ionic trans-
port. In Li-S batteries, the solvation environment determines which 
sulfur species are stabilized in solution, how rapidly they can inter-
convert, and whether the dominant bottleneck appears ﬁrst at the
cathode or at the lithium anode. Li et al. showed this clearly by
comparing strong, moderate, and weak LiPS solvation environ-
ments.
Strong
solvation
accelerates
sulfur
conversion
and
improves access to dissolved intermediates, but it also intensiﬁes
lithium-side parasitic reactions and destabilizes long-term cycling.
Weak solvation suppresses lithium corrosion more effectively, yet
it can slow sulfur conversion enough that cathode kinetics become
the main limitation. The practical lesson is therefore not that one
should always move toward weaker or stronger solvation, but that
electrolyte design deﬁnes the reaction landscape within which
both electrodes must operate [68].

is no longer a background medium. It becomes an active architect
of the redox pathway itself [66]. 
Yet electrolyte control alone does not guarantee durable perfor-

that catalyst-solvent interaction becomes decisive because a cata-

electrolyte-imposed local structure, not outside it. At the same

grated hierarchy. Electrolyte ﬁrst, because it deﬁnes the accessible 
chemistry. But not electrolyte alone, because cathode catalysis and
lithium interphase stability determine whether that chemistry
remains reversible over time.

and electrolyte inventory

electrolyte inventory. In Li-S research, catalysts are often described

7.3. Lithium protection must be evaluated as a full-cell inventory
problem
The third design rule is that lithium protection should be eval-
uated as a full-cell inventory problem rather than merely as an 
anode surface problem. Many lithium-protection strategies are still 
discussed in half-cell terms, where the anode is treated as an iso-
lated interface that can be stabilized independently by coatings, 
additives, or artiﬁcial SEIs. In practical Li-S batteries, this framing 
is too narrow. The lithium anode is part of a dynamic inventory 
budget in which dissolved LiPS species, interfacial side reactions, 
local plating heterogeneity, and inactive lithium formation all con-
sume or deactivate the same ﬁnite lithium reservoir. Nanda and
Manthiram made this explicit in anode-free Li-S cells by quantify-
ing lithium degradation over cycling. Their results showed that
failure is not governed simply by the disappearance of lithium
mass, but by the progressive conversion of lithium inventory into
interphase-covered or dead lithium that remains physically pre-
sent yet electrochemically inaccessible. This reframes anode failure
from lithium corrosion to inventory deactivation, which is much
more useful for full-cell design [176]. 
This inventory-based view becomes even more compelling once 
electrode crosstalk is considered. Shi et al. showed that early fail-
ure under practical conditions originates from crosstalk between 
the sulfur cathode and the lithium anode. Nonuniform sulfur-
side reaction topography leads to nonuniform local resistance 
and electrolyte distribution, which is then mirrored by localized 
lithium plating and stripping. The result is ampliﬁed current-
density heterogeneity, ﬁlament growth, and eventual internal
short circuit. In this framework, lithium instability is not solely a
material problem at the anode. It is a full-cell symptom generated
by heterogeneity elsewhere in the system, especially at the sulfur
electrode under lean-electrolyte conditions [50].

Yang et al. offered a complementary strategy by introducing a 
regenerative redox mediator that can consume dead lithium at 
the anode and then be electrochemically regenerated at the cath-
ode. The conceptual signiﬁcance of this work is that it treats inac-
tive lithium not as a purely terminal failure product, but as a
partially recoverable inventory-loss channel within a coupled
full-cell mechanism [175]. Cheng et al. had already shown, in their 
classic coin-versus-pouch comparison, that the durability gap 
between those formats is strongly tied to lithium-metal degrada-
tion, and that replacing the aged lithium anode can restore much
of the lost capacity [16]. Together, these studies show that lithium 
protection should be reported in terms of lithium-inventory reten-
tion, crossover tolerance, dead-lithium evolution, and pouch-cell 
ageing behavior, not just symmetric-cell lifetime or smoother
post-mortem morphology. Only then can anode stabilization be
judged as a practical full-cell solution rather than an isolated inter-
face improvement.
7.4. Diagnostics should become part of materials validation rather
than a post hoc explanation
The fourth design rule is that operando and in situ diagnostics 
should be embedded into materials validation from the start rather 
than being used only after failure to justify a preferred interpreta-
tion. Li-S batteries are especially vulnerable to hidden changes in 
polysulﬁde distribution, electrolyte wetting, interfacial blockage, 
and lithium deposition morphology. Because of this, conventional 
electrochemical metrics often underdetermine the mechanism. 
Two cells can show similar cycling curves while degrading through 
very different pathways, and an apparently successful material can 
owe its performance to an unmeasured side effect rather than to
the mechanism claimed by the authors. From a methodological
perspective, diagnostics are inseparable from experimental design
and data interpretation. Cell assembly choices, measurement
boundaries, and the interpretation limits of each diagnostic
method all shape what conclusions can honestly be drawn from
Li-S data. Their practical guide makes the important point that cell

Recent operando work shows how much stronger materials 
claims become when this philosophy is followed. Fritzke et al. used 
in situ 6 Li, 7 Li, and 33 S NMR to track sulfur redox pathways directly 
and found that early capacity decay can arise from incomplete re-
oxidation of polysulﬁdes back to S8 rather than from shuttle alone.
That result matters because it overturns a common default expla-
nation and shows that direct pathway tracing can reveal hidden
inefﬁciencies in the redox sequence itself [29]. Zech et al. used 
operando sulfur K-edge X-ray absorption at both electrode sides 
to quantify dissolved polysulﬁde accumulation over time. Instead 
of treating the shuttle as a generic narrative, they converted it into
a measured cross-electrode transport and imbalance process
linked directly to capacity loss [45]. Coke et al. pushed this further 
with operando optical ﬂuorescence microscopy, which enabled 
real-time visualization of polysulﬁde distribution and shuttle
toward the anode, as well as the associated emergence of dendritic

monitor how sulfur crossover, local accumulation, and lithium

7.5. Future directions for full-cell reaction microenvironment co-
design
Several future directions stand out, but the common thread 
among them is a shift from isolated component optimization 
toward deliberate control of local reaction environments under 
practical cell constraints. In practical Li-S batteries, full-cell reac-
tion microenvironment co-design should be treated as a technical 
roadmap rather than a broad conceptual slogan. The reaction 
microenvironment includes the Li+ /LiPS solvation shell, LiPS con-
centration and lifetime, catalytic-site coordination and stability, 
cathode pore wetting, Li2S nucleation/deposition morphology, 
cathode/electrolyte and anode/electrolyte interphases, lithium-
inventory accessibility, and pouch-scale electrolyte/current distri-
bution. A useful future strategy should therefore specify which 
microenvironmental variable is being tuned, which coupled failure 
mode is being suppressed, and which practical validation metric is
used to prove that the beneﬁt survives under high sulfur loading,
lean electrolyte, limited lithium excess, and pouch-relevant ageing.
First, moderate-solvation design remains one of the most promis-
ing directions because it offers a rational way to balance sulfur
accessibility, shuttle suppression, and lithium compatibility. In
the present literature pool, the clearest evidence for this philoso-
phy comes from Kautz et al., who designed a ternary moderately
solvating electrolyte that signiﬁcantly improved both cycling and
calendar stability by placing polysulﬁde solubility in a more pro-
ductive intermediate regime while preserving robust passivation
at both electrodes [75]. The corresponding technical pathway is 
to deﬁne a practical solvation window by jointly reporting LiPS sol-
ubility, donor/acceptor descriptors, electrolyte density, viscosity, 
ionic conductivity, Li2S nucleation behavior, and lithium Coulom-
bic efﬁciency under the same E/S and sulfur-loading conditions.
This suggests that future electrolyte design should target a stable
solvating window rather than either extreme of full dissolution
or near-complete immobilization.
Second, catalyst-solvent co-engineering deserves much more 
deliberate treatment. The underlying principle is that catalytic per-
formance is not an intrinsic property of an active site alone. It 
depends on the chemical environment that supplies sulfur species 
to that site. Guo et al. already imply this through their practical
single-atom indium catalyst, whose value was demonstrated only
because it remained effective under high sulfur loading and lean
electrolyte in a pouch-cell regime [134]. Du et al. add another layer 
by showing that high-entropy single-atom motifs can create coop-
erative adsorptio n and conversion behavior across multiple metal
centers, opening a path beyond one-site-one-function catalyst
thinking [136]. A natural next step is therefore to design catalysts 
and electrolytes as matched systems rather than screening them 
independently. The corresponding pathway is to pair each catalyst
motif with a deﬁned solvation environment and then test whether
the same catalyst still lowers activation polarization, prevents Li2S
passivation, and maintains active-site stability when LiPS concen-
tration increases under lean-electrolyte operation.

7.6. Technical limitations and engineering challenges of electrolyte-
catalysis synergy
Despite the strong conceptual appeal of electrolyte catalysis 
synergy, its translation into manufacturable Li-S cells remains lim-

design and electrode formulation. Many recently reported cata-

heterostructures, rely on multistep synthesis, high-temperature
processing, precise coordination regulation, or low-yield precursor
chemistry. These requirements may compromise batch repro-
ducibility, impurity control, and uniform dispersion across practi-
cal electrode areas. Therefore, the value of a catalyst should not
be judged only by its activity in dilute or idealized test conditions.

Electrolyte design presents a second and equally important con-
straint. Fluorinated solvents, localized high-concentration elec-
trolytes, specialized additives, and deliberately engineered 
solvation structures can suppress polysulﬁde migration, regulate 
interphase formation, and promote Li2S conversion. However, 
these advantages are often accompanied by high salt content, 
increased viscosity and density, reduced wettability, solvent 
volatility, ﬂammability, toxicity, or difﬁculty in solvent recovery. 
From a cell-engineering perspective, the electrolyte is not only a
reaction medium but also an inactive mass, a wetting agent, and
a key manufacturing variable. The practical relevance of electrolyte
catalysis synergy should therefore be evaluated together with elec-
trolyte density, viscosity, separator uptake, electrode wetting
kinetics, dry-room compatibility, and ﬁlling uniformity in large-
format cells.
Long-term interfacial durability is another unresolved issue. A 
catalyst electrolyte pair that accelerates polysulﬁde conversion 
during early cycling may still lose effectiveness if the catalyst sur-
face reconstructs, becomes passivated by Li2S or Li2S2 deposits, 
reacts irreversibly with sulfur species, or loses electronic and ionic 
contact within a thick cathode. Similarly, electrolyte additives that 
initially suppress shuttle effects or improve interphase chemistry
may be continuously consumed under lean-electrolyte and high-
sulfur-loading conditions. Recent pouch-cell-oriented studies indi-
cate that catalytic activity must be sustained together with surface
stability, electrolyte distribution, and lithium inventory retention,
rather than being inferred only from initial adsorption strength
or symmetric-cell kinetics.
Scale-up compatibility further determines whether a locally 
effective electrolyte catalyst design can survive under realistic cell 
conditions. Laboratory coin cells often employ excess electrolyte, 
thick lithium foils, small electrode areas, and carefully controlled
wetting conditions, all of which can mask transport and degrada-
tion modes that emerge in multilayer pouch cells. At larger for-
mats, electrode coating uniformity, calendering density, sulfur 
and catalyst distribution, electrolyte ﬁlling, gas evolution, pressure 
development, current-collector corrosion, tab conﬁguration, and 
stack-level thermal management become strongly coupled. If elec-
trolyte redistribution, local current concentration, and lithium
deposition are not controlled simultaneously, an apparently efﬁ-
cient catalytic interface can evolve into spatially heterogeneous
full-cell failure.
719
Future electrolyte catalysis strategies should therefore be 
assessed within a manufacturability-aware validation framework. 
Beyond sulfur loading, areal capacity, E/S ratio, and negative-to-
positive electrode capacity ratio, future studies should report cata-
lyst loading, synthetic scalability, electrolyte density and viscosity, 
additive concentration and depletion behavior, electrode porosity 
and tortuosity, pouch-cell ﬁlling protocol, cell-to-cell reproducibil-
ity, post-cycling catalyst and interphase stability, and failure anal-
ysis after extended cycling. Only strategies that maintain coupled
sulfur conversion, electrolyte distribution, lithium stability, and
process compatibility under constrained cell-level conditions can
be considered credible candidates for practical Li-S batteries.
7.7. Concluding p erspective
Overall, the practical gap that motivated Li-S research has nar-
rowed but has not closed. Substantial progress has been made in 
identifying the coupled origins of shuttle, self-discharge, Li2S pas-
sivation, electrolyte redistribution, and lithium-metal degradation, 
and in developing electrolytes, catalysts, interlayers, and inter-
phases that can improve one or more of these processes under 
increasingly realistic conditions. Several problems have therefore 
been substantially improved, including mechanistic diagnosis,
polysulﬁde management, sulfur redox mediation, and the con-
struction of more protective lithium interfaces, while others have
only been partially mitigated, including lean-electrolyte sulfur uti-
lization, cathode-anode crosstalk, lithium-inventory retention, and
pouch-cell ageing. The unresolved challenge is to convert these
local advances into a quantitatively predictable and manufac-

<<PAGE 29>>
turable full-cell architecture that maintains high areal capacity, 
low electrolyte inventory, limited lithium excess, stable interphase 
chemistry, and spatially uniform conversion over long cycling. 
Future practical Li-S batteries will therefore depend less on iso-
lated breakthroughs in one material class than on the coordinated
control of solvation, catalysis, interfaces, transport, and diagnostics
across the entire cell.
