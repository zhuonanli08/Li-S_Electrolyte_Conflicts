ABSTRACT
Novel electrolyte consisting of ethyl 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether (ETFE), con-
ventional ether 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and ﬁlm-forming additive LiFSI was
investigated for rechargeable lithium sulfur batteries. It is found that the addi-
tion of LiFSI promotes ionic conduction, reduces polysulﬁde solubility in the
electrolyte, and enhances the compatibility of the electrolyte with metallic Li.
Consequently, increased reversible capacity, improved cycle stability and rate
capability are exhibited in an additive-contained ﬂuorinated electrolyte with
moderate amount of DOL. It further reveals that the synergistic function of the
ﬂuorinated ether and LiFSI greatly modify and stabilize Li surface. However,
rich DOL employed in the ﬂuorinated electrolyte results in excessive polysulﬁde
dissolution and inferior interface quality despite the raised ionic conductivity,
which is unfavorable for the overall cell performance instead even though the
additive was employed.
1
Introduction
Green and highly-efﬁcient energy storage devices
have
spurred
intensively
attentions
due
to
the
worldwide energy shortage and environmental pol-
lution issues [1–6]. Li–S batteries have held great
potential to serve as power supply of electric vehicle
due to high theoretical energy density (* 2600 Wh
kg-1), abundant material resource and good envi-
ronmental friendliness [7–9]. However, the redox
process of the Li–S battery involves a series of the
polysulﬁdes
(Li2Sn,
2 B n B 8)
as
intermediate
products, which are easily dissolved in the conven-
tional liquid electrolyte and then shuttle between two
electrodes [10, 11], causing a series of problems such
as rapid capacity decay and serious self-discharge.
Since the dissolution and shuttle of polysulﬁdes all
happen in the electrolyte, the design of a suit-
able electrolyte environment seems to be quite
crucial.
So far, electrolytes based on various ethers such as
1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)
and
1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) are widely used in the Li–S batteries. These
solvents have the advantages of low viscosity, high

solubility of Li salts, and acceptable chemical/elec-
trochemical stability. Nevertheless, their solvation
ability offers excessive dissolution of polysulﬁdes
[12]. Organic ﬂuorinated ethers derived from con-
ventional ethers have been extensively studied for
preventing shuttle effect and improving battery per-
formance in recent years, by the merit of weak sol-
vation ability for minimizing polysulﬁde solubility
[13, 14]. Moreover, most of them are capable of pas-
sivating Li surface and impeding the corrosion of
soluble polysulﬁdes to metallic Li. However, these
ﬂuorinated solvents also bring about weak dissocia-
tion of Li salts and inferior thermal stability. It
reminds us that the amount of ﬂuorinated ether
needs to be limited when designing a suitable elec-
trolyte for Li–S batteries. This may result in poor
surface ﬁlm quality and limited Li passivation effect.
Our previous research indicated [15] that a binary
solvent consisting of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and ethyl
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl
ether
(ETFE)
brought
improved electrochemical performance but rapid
capacity decay still existed to some extent.
Further introducing some auxiliary ﬁlm-forming
ingredients into the ﬂuorinated electrolyte is consid-
ered to be a reasonable approach to tackle the above
problem. Various functional Li salts (LiNO3, LiBOB,
LiODFB
and
LiFSI)
have
been
attempted
for
enhanced stability of Li anode as ﬁlm-forming addi-
tives [16–19]. Despite LiNO3 is conducive for the
construction of protective ﬁlm on the Li surface, the
gradual consumption of this additive on both the
sulfur cathode and Li anode during cycling will lead
to the decrease even vanishment of protection func-
tion [20]. In addition, the strong oxidation of NO3
-
also has latent safety hazard. Another Li salt LiFSI,
possessing a similar structure but smaller size than
LiTFSI, was reported to realize high capacity and
stable
cycle
performance
of
the
sulfur
cathode
accompanied by the homogeneous Li deposition on
the anode when combining with LiTFSI to construct a
binary-salt electrolyte [21]. Kim et al. [22] demon-
strated that electrochemical reduction of LiFSI elec-
trolytes at a potential close to the sulfur cathode
operation forms in situ protective coating on both
cathode and anode, which is initiated by the FSI(-F)
anion radicals generated during electrolyte reduc-
tion. Yang et al. [23] reported that further capacity
improvement of Li–S cells was achieved by employ-
ing LiFSI additive in an ionic liquid-based electrolyte.
This work aims to design a novel multi-component
electrolyte containing ﬂuorinated ether as a func-
tional solvent and LiFSI as a functional additive for
rechargeable Li–S batteries. By the introduction of
LiFSI and regulation of solvent composition, the
optimized overall properties of the electrolyte and
excellent cell performance were demonstrated in this
study. Afterwards, the synergistic effect of ﬂuori-
nated ether and LiFSI on the Li surface chemistry is
intensively investigated and discussed.
2
Experimental
2.1
Material and cell preparation
Sulfur/carbon
(S/C)
composite
was
prepared
through a melt-inﬁltration strategy. The elemental
sulfur and mesoporous carbon (CMK-3, XF Nano)
with a mass ratio of 7:3 were mixed uniformly, and
then the resulting mixture was heated at 155 C for
12 h. After being cooled to room temperature, the
powder was collected and stored. The S content in the
composite was determined to be 69.6% by TG
analysis.
The electrolyte was prepared in an argon-ﬁlled
glove box with oxygen and water contents below
0.1 ppm.
Generally,
the
electrolyte
consisted
of
1 mol kg-1
(corresponding
to * 1.13 mol
L-1)
lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonel)imide (LiTFSI,
99.95%, Aldrich) in binary solvent mixture of DOL
(99.5%, Acros) and ETFE (98%, TCI). Then LiFSI
(99.8%, Canrd) was further added into the above
electrolyte as a functional additive. Detailed compo-
sitions of several electrolytes are listed in Table 1. The
1/1 weight ratio of DOL/ETFE corresponds to a
volume ratio of * 1.14/1.
Sulfur electrodes were fabricated by mixing the
S/C composite with the poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride)
(PVDF, 6020 Solef) and conductive carbon (Super P,
Timcal)
at
a
weight
ratio
of
8:1:1
in
N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) to produce homoge-
neous
slurry.
The
electrode
was
prepared
by
spreading the slurry onto a piece of Al foil by a
doctor blade, and dried at 60 C under vacuum
overnight, then cut into pellets with diameter of
1.4 cm.
The
typical
sulfur
loading
was * 2.5 mg cm-2. CR2025-type coin cells con-
sisted of the sulfur cathode, Li anode separated by a
piece of polypropylene membranes (Celgard 2500).

2.2
Material characterization
The ionic conductivities of various electrolytes were
recorded via a conductivity meter (Seven2Go S3,
Mettler Toledo). UV–visible absorption spectroscopy
(TU-1810PC, Persee) was employed to investigate the
solubility of polysulﬁde species in the different
solutions. The Li anodes and S cathodes of the Li–S
cells after cycling in various electrolytes were care-
fully extracted in the glove-box. The surface mor-
phologies
and
element
composition
of
these
electrodes were investigated by ﬁeld emission scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM, S4800) and energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), respectively.
2.3
Electrochemical measurement
The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of stainless
steel/Li cell was used for measuring the stable po-
tential window of the electrolyte at a scanning rate of
5 mV s-1. Li/Li symmetrical cell was fabricated and
galvanostaticly cycled for investigating the Li com-
patibility of various electrolytes at the current density
of 1 mA cm-2. Cycle and rate performances of the
Li–S cells were recorded between 1.5 and 3 V under a
professional test system (LAND CT2014A). Cyclic
voltammetry (CV) was conducted using an electro-
chemical workstation (CS350, Corr Test), with the
scanning rate and voltage range of 0.2 mV s-1 and
1.5 * 3.0 V, respectively.
3
Results and discussion
3.1
Physicochemical properties
of the electrolytes
The ionic conductivities of the as-prepared elec-
trolytes are compared in Fig. 1a. The increase in DOL
content is favorable for the ionic conductivity of the
electrolyte owing to its relatively low viscosity and
high salt solubility. The introduction of LiFSI further
improves the ionic transport in the electrolyte, prob-
ably attributed to the contribution of more mobile Li
ions provided by the additive. The LSV measurement
of Fig. 1b shows that LiFSI has little inﬂuence on the
cathodic/anodic stability of the electrolyte as a
functional component, meeting the operation voltage
need of Li–S batteries.
UV–Vis spectroscopy was employed for evaluating
the polysulﬁde solubility in various electrolytes. As
shown in Fig. 2, the wide-range absorption band is
attributed to disproportionation reaction and com-
plex equilibria of various polysulﬁde species in the
solutions [24]. It is found according to absorption
intensity that DE-2 has much high polysulﬁde solu-
bility due to the employment of excess DOL with
strong solvation ability (high DN value). Adding
LiFSI in the electrolyte slightly decreases the poly-
sulﬁde dissolution. This may be because that a part of
DOL contributes to the solvation of Li ions in the
additive, hence reducing the amount of the solvent
for interacting with Li polysulﬁdes.
To investigate the compatibility of various elec-
trolytes towards metallic Li, a long-term Li plating/
stripping experiment was conducted in the Li sym-
metric cell conﬁguration. Generally, solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) will immediately form when the Li
metal contacts with liquid electrolyte due to the high
reactivity of Li. As shown in Fig. 3, the electrolytes
with the additive always own lower overpotential
compared to their counterparts during long-term
cycling,
revealing
decreased
polarization
at
the
interface and lower resistance for Li ions through the
SEI ﬁlm in the presence of LiFSI. Especially, the
addition of LiFSI in the DE-1 (DE-1 ? 1%LiFSI) pro-
vides a relatively identical and small hysteresis
potential
over
the
repeated
cycles,
reﬂecting
a
stable and low-resistant interface layer for fast Li?
migration. When the SEI is well established and the
Li/electrolyte interfaces stabilized, the system does
not evolve signiﬁcantly and hence results in a

3.3
Electrode morphologies and elemental
analyses
The surface morphologies of both sulfur cathode and
Li anode cycled in different electrolytes were exam-
ined by SEM. The pristine cathode (Fig. 7a) shows a
highly porous and homogenous morphology before
cycle. After 50 cycles, rough surface covered by some
irregular precipitates are observed in the cathode
cycled in DE-1 (Fig. 7b). It is originated from the
random re-deposition of insoluble reaction byprod-
ucts in the charge–discharge process [28]. Excess
DOL in DE-2 (Fig. 7c) results in more massive
deposits due to more sulfur species dissolution and
migration in the electrolyte and then reaction with
metallic Li. These insulated precipitates will produce
electrochemical reaction barrier. The deteriorated
surface construction is still maintained in the DE-
2 ? 1% LiFSI to a certain extent (Fig. 7e), but is
obviously improved in DE-1 ? 1%LiFSI as a clearly
smooth and dense surface is built (Fig. 7d). The
reduced polysulﬁde dissolution is considered to be
an important reason for the surface amelioration of
the latter.
The SEM images of Fig. 8 further demonstrate that
the smooth surface (Fig. 8a) is converted into rough
one when the Li metal was cycled in DE-1 (Fig. 8b)
and DE-2 (Fig. 8c), illustrating irregular dendritic Li
and uncoordinated plating-stripping behavior. The
EDS spectra (Fig. 9a and b) conﬁrms that the pres-
ence of high S-containing species (sulfur content of
10.32 and 12.34 wt % for DE-1 and DE-2, respec-
tively). It should be noted that other additional peaks
at * 1.0 and 2.1 keV may come from the impurities

4
Conclusions
LiFSI was introduced into the ﬂuorinated electrolyte
with the solvent composition of ETFE and DOL as a
functional additive. The cell in the DE-1 ? 1%LiFSI
electrolyte delivers high initial speciﬁc capacity of
1254 mA h g-1 and capacity retention up to 78% after
50 cycles accompanied with stable CE over 95%. The
additive component not only improves ion transport
and slightly restrains polysulﬁde solubility of the
electrolyte, but also contributes to stable and low-
resistant passivation on the Li anode together with
the ﬂuorinated ether and the suitable amount of
DOL. This study primarily stresses on the applica-
bility of synergistic effect of the F-containing solvent
and additive in the regulation of polysulﬁde disso-
lution and shuttle.
