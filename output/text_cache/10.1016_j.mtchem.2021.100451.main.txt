a b s t r a c t
The solid electrolyte interface (SEI) plays an important role in the lithiumesulfur battery system. It not
only protects the stability of the lithium metal anode interface but also inhibits the growth of lithium
dendrites during charge and discharge. The relationship between the shape of the SEI and the transport
behavior of lithium ions affects the homogeneity of lithium dendrites. In this work, ﬁrst-principles
calculations are used to determine the stable structure and transport properties of the La-doped LiF
solid electrolyte interface (LaeLiF SEI) on the Li substrate. For the vertical transport of Li ions within the
LaeLiF SEI, the transport of Li ions in the grain boundary and that in the crystal grain was calculated
separately. Regarding the plane diffusion behavior of Li ions between the LaeLiF SEI and the lithium
anode, the diffusion of Li ions on the surface and interface of the lithium anode were calculated. The
effect of critical tensile strain on the diffusion of Li ions on the surface and interface was investigated. The
results show that doping with La solves the problem of excessive periodic grain boundary gaps caused by
the difference between LiF and Li lattices during the deposition process. The periodic gap is reduced from
0.478 nm to 0.306 nm after La doping. By comparing the migration energy barriers of each path, it is
found that lithium ions are more likely to be inserted and extracted at the LaeLiF SEI grain boundary. The
reason is that the existence of the rare earth element La causes the grain boundary to have a more stable
vacancy structure and a smaller transport energy barrier (0.789 eV). The critical tensile strain reduces the
diffusion energy barrier (0.813 eV) of Li ions on the surface of the lithium metal anode, which promotes
the fast diffusion and uniform deposition of Li ions between the interfaces. The establishment of SEI
transport characteristics under the coupling conditions of mechanical stretching and ion transport is
expected to improve the Li deposition behavior.

1. Introduction
The lithium metal batteries (LMBs) have the advantages of ul-
trahigh theoretical speciﬁc capacity, lowest redox potential, and
low volume density. The practical applications of LMBs have faced
many critical issues, mainly concerning lithium metal anode [1e3].
Due to the high reaction activity of metal lithium itself, it is easy to
deposit a layer of solid electrolyte interface (SEI) between the
surface of the anode and the electrolyte [4]. The uneven deposition/
peeling phenomenon on the anode surface during the electro-
chemical cycle effect the stability of the SEI. Therefore, the practical
application of LMBs is also restricted by dendrite growth and poor
SEI stability [5e9]. Li ions are mainly deposited on the uneven
protrusions of the Li anode during the deposition process [10]. As a
result, Li dendrites are gradually generated, which will pierce the
diaphragm and contact the battery's cathode, causing a short circuit
inside the battery and increasing the safety hazard during use. In
addition, with the growth of Li dendrites, they will fall off at the
electrode terminal. The electrolyte isolates the connection between
the detached Li dendrites and the electrode to form “dead lithium”
The existence of “dead lithium” hinders the transport behavior of
electrons and ions as well as the consumption of active materials,
causing serious capacity degradation and other issues.
To prevent the growth of lithium dendrites and to protect the
lithium anode, researchers have adopted three different strategies.
The ﬁrst approach is the design of the metal anode structure, such as
lithium foam, lithium microspheres, surface-modiﬁed lithium foil,
and a lithium-philic frame design [11e13]. The second approach is to

change the SEI composition by adding electrolyte additives and
lithium salt additives and by solvent optimization and other mea-
sures such as the addition of CO2, HF, SO2, and ﬂuoroether solvents
[14e18] to improve the properties of the SEI generated in situ. The
third approach is to modify the SEI artiﬁcially, optimizing the SEI

At present, artiﬁcial modiﬁcation of the SEI is a relatively
promising choice to solve the problem of lithium metal dendrites
and to protect the lithium metal anodes. The artiﬁcial modiﬁcation
of the SEI with LiF has always been the objective of researchers in
this ﬁeld. Cui et al. [19] selectively deposited LiF on defective h-BN
(hexagonal boron nitride). The mixed LiF/h-BN ﬁlm has good
chemical and mechanical properties and inhibits the growth of
lithium dendrites. Wang et al. [20] formed a layer of LiF-rich SEI in
situ between the lithium metal and the solid-state electrolyte (SSE),
and it successfully inhibited the penetration of Li dendrites into the
SSE. The low electronic conductivity and the inherent electro-
chemical stability of LiF prevent the side reaction between the SSE
and Li, prevent the further occurrence of interface reactions, and
achieve the inhibition of lithium dendrites. Cui et al. [21] placed Li
metal in gaseous Freon R134a to form a dense LiF coating on the
surface of the Li metal. By adjusting the temperature and pressure
to control the thickness of the LiF coating, the prepared artiﬁcial SEI
with chemical stability and high mechanical strength relieved the
volume change of the lithium metal electrode, interface reactions,
and other issues. Zhang et al. [22] rationally designed a bi-layer
artiﬁcial solid electrolyte interface (BL-SEI) composed of covalent
graphene/h-BN and inorganic SEI components (including LiF, Li2O,
Li3N, and Li2CO3) through comprehensive ﬁrst-principles ap-
proaches. In particular, the graphitic layer (GL), with its higher
mechanical strength and rigidity, can effectively bear the stress
caused by uneven growth and can inhibit the growth of dendrites,
and the inorganic layer (IL) can prevent the GL from being corroded
by the electrolyte due to chemical reactions at the interface. In
addition, the key interface characteristics of BL-SEI, such as chem-
ical stability, ionic conductivity, and mechanical properties, were
calculated. The calculation results show that the graphene/LiF
combination BL-SEI has the best interface stability and electro-
chemical performance. The Archer team [23] used radio-frequency
magnetron sputtering onto exposed Li foil to prepare the LiF
coating and act on the Li metal anode. Through the constant current
cycle test, it was found that the cycle characteristics and coulombic
efﬁciency of the Li anode protected by the LiF coating are signiﬁ-
cantly better than that of the exposed lithium anode. Lang et al. [24]
used the in situ reaction between a polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF)
and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) mixed solution and Li metal to
synthesize an Li anode stabilized by an excellent LiF layer on
lithium at different current densities. In the symmetrical battery
cycle test, the results show that the Li anode protected by LiF has
superior electrochemical performance compared with the exposed
lithium anode.
Studies have shown that lithium ﬂuoride has a simple and stable
structure and is a good ionic conductor and electronic insulator.
Whether it is used as a solid electrolyte or the main component of a
single-component SEI or a composite SEI, lithium ﬂuoride can be
effectively used for the protection of lithium anode. The rare earth
element La is widely used in photoelectric materials, catalyst ma-
terials, and lithium ion cathode material modiﬁcation design. Mo
et al. [25] calculated the electrochemical window of SSEs (Li3YCl6,
Li3YBr6) by ﬁrst-principles computations (DFT) to reach 4 V, which
is much wider than that of the current oxide and sulﬁde SSE
(1.75e3.71 V for Li0.33La0.56TiO3, and 1.72e2.29 V for Li10GeP2S12),
they found that the isoform Li3MX6 (M ¼ Dy, Gd, Ho, La, Nd, Sc, Sm,
Tb, Tm; X ¼ Cl, Br) has good phase stability and is expected to be
widely used in solid-state lithium batteries. Theoretical algorithms,
modeling and simulation, and advances in computers have sup-
plemented experimental work and can greatly help bridge the gap
between experiment and technology [26,27]. Research on solid
electrolytes has shown that mixing them with a variety of different
ion conductors can achieve good overall performance [28]. For this
reason, the interface model of a La-doped LiF SEI deposited on the
surface of a lithium metal anode is constructed. Lattice mismatch
causes the halide LiHa SEI groups to aggregate on the surface of the
lithium metal to form islands [29]. Using ﬁrst-principles calcula-
tions to determine the structural stability of LaeLiF placed on the Li
substrate, the theoretical analysis of the vertical transport behavior
of lithium ions in the LaeLiF SEI grain boundary and the crystal
grain, and the fusion of mechanical strain on the plane diffusion the
impact of lithium dendrites.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. The vertical transport behavior of Li ions in the LaeLiF solid
electrolyte interface
Vacancies are the most common point defects in solid materials
and have a signiﬁcant impact on the physical, mechanical, and
thermodynamic properties of the material. The parameter that
characterizes the vacancy is mainly the formation energy of va-
cancy defects, which is used to describe the stability of the defect
structure. The greater the formation energy of vacancy defects is,
the more stable the defect structure is. This article constructs va-
cancies to provide paths for ion transport and diffusion. Vacancies
at the grain boundary GB and grain G are formed by removing La or
Li atoms from each layer from layer one to layer four (Fig. 2b and f
GBLix/GLix). The calculation results show that the defect formation
energy at the grain boundary GB is much greater than the vacancy
defect formation energy at the grain G and interface I (Table 1),
indicating that the defect structure at the grain boundary GB is
more stable. This means that the stable defect structure at the grain
boundary provides a stable transport path for Li ion insertion/
extraction.
To study the insertion/extraction behavior of Li ions in the
LaeLiF SEI, the NEB method was used to calculate the transport
behavior of Li ions in an inorganic LaeLiF SEI. The initial state is a
stable structure with vacancies, and the terminal state is a new
vacancy structure formed after the vacancies exchange coordinate
positions with adjacent Li/La atoms. Vertical transport is divided
into two types: The ﬁrst type is the transport of Li ions inside the
crystal grains with a transport path of GLi1/GLi2/GLi3/GLi4. The
internal transport process of Li-ions in the LiF grain is from Li base
layer one to layer four through the LiF SEI to the surface, layer by
layer (Fig. 2b). The second type is the transport of Li ions in the
grain boundary, in which Li ions transport between LaeLa grain
boundaries. Similar to the ﬁrst type of intragrain transport, the
transport path is GBLi1/GBLi2/GBLi3/GBLi4. The Li ions transition
from the Li base to the La vacancies of adjacent layers until escaping
to the surface (Fig. 2c).
During the vertical detachment and transport of Li ions at the
LaeLiF SEI grain boundary GB, Li ions ﬁrst need 0.144 eV transport
activation energy to break the LieLi bond from the GBLi1 position
and leave the Li substrate (Fig. 3a). Then, they spontaneously
diffuse to the GBLi2 site with a high defect formation energy and
relatively stable structure. In the process of migration to GBLi3, to
overcome the inﬂuence of the interface, Li ions require a large
transport activation energy (0.789 eV) to cross the saddle point
(Fig. 3a and Supply information Table 1). Then, they diffuse to GBLi4
sites with higher defect formation energy. Since Li ions and F ions in
the transport process at the boundary have fewer LieF bonds, the
required transport activation energy is lower. During the vertical
transport of Li ions at the LaeLiF SEI grain G, the deformation of the
interface structure makes the vertical transport of Li ions from GLi1
to GLi2 more difﬁcult, and a large transport activation energy
(1.048 eV) is required to cross the energy barrier. Slightly less
favorable than the diffusion of Li through LiF (0.75 eV) and (0.81 eV)
as reported by Greeley [41] and Balbuena [42], respectively. During
the layer-by-layer migration of GLi2/GLi3/GLi4, Li ions need to
break the original LieF bond to form a new LieF bond. In the LiF
crystal grain, Li ions and F ions exist in a six-coordinate state.
Therefore, during the transport process, the bond energy of 6 LieF
bonds must be overcome, requiring a larger migration activation
energy. The energy required for transport is mainly determined by
the difference between the energy required to break the LieF bonds
and LieLi bonds and the energy released on forming new LieF
bonds. The transport path and relatively small energy change
together determine that the transport energy barrier of Li ions at
the grain boundary GB is much smaller than the transport energy
barrier at the grain G (Fig. 3a). It can be seen that the doping by La
increases the length of the transport path of Li ions but reduces the
transport energy barrier of Li ions at the GB. The stable defect
structure and low transport energy barrier promote the rapid
transport of Li ions at the grain boundary GB, which in turn pro-
motes the uniform deposition of Li ions at the SEI and stabilizes the
electrolyte interface to protect the Li anode.
3.2. The effect of mechanical strength on the plane diffusion of Li
ions between interfaces
Mechanical stability and strength are very important to the
stable cycle of Li metal anode. During charge and discharge, lithium
metal has a relatively inﬁnite volume change, and its surface
coating material undergoes repeated mechanical morphology
changes, which leads to a decrease in coating stability and produces
cracks [22]. Therefore, mechanical strength is essential for main-
taining the stability of the structure, especially in the interaction
between interfaces. The tensile properties of the bulk structure of
LiF-SEI and LaeLiF in the <001> direction are simulated, and the
critical tensile strain can be regarded as the upper limit for the
actual material. With the increase in the amount of stretching in the
<001> direction of the unit cell, the basis vector in the <100> and
<010> directions is gradually shortened under the Poisson effect. At
the same time, the atomic coordinates move slightly with the
change in the basis vector, gradually evolving from the original
non-lattice interface to a semilattice interface or even a eutectic
lattice interface. When the critical strain is reached, the interatomic

The diffusion of Li ions on the surface of the lithium anode

IBLix is expressed as the diffusion activation energy curve when no

symmetrical about the diffusion energy barrier. When no strain is
applied, the Bader value is the same as the activation energy curve,

with increasing activation energy and decreases with decreasing
activation energy. IALix is expressed as the activation energy curve

energy curve and the uniformity of the energy barrier are changed.
In this process, the Bader charge transfer value tends to be stable,
and the difference in Li ion diffusion position has no obvious in-
ﬂuence on the Bader charge transfer value. It can be seen that the
Bader charge transfer value amount for Li ions diffusing in this

adjacent LiF layer. The tensile strain increases the distance between
the interfaces, which reduces the amount of charge obtained by F
ions at any position, which is also the reason why the Bader charge
transfer value of the applied strain tends to be ﬂat. At the same
time, the Poisson effect caused by the tensile strain reduces the
length of the surface diffusion path.
During the migration process of Li ions between the interfaces,
the migration activation energy curves are basically the same
(Fig. 3c), and the strain increases the migration energy barrier be-
tween the interfaces from 1.597 eV to 2.07 eV (Fig. 3c and Supply
information Table 1). The migration process of Li ions from ISLi1
to ISLi4 is a process in which Li ions leave the Li substrate and jump
over the interface to embed in the LiF SEI layer. Strain has little
effect on the migration activation energy, and the Bader charge
transfer curves before and after strain are relatively ﬂat and coin-
cident. The migration process of Li ions from ISLi4 to ISLi3 is a process
in which Li ions depart from the LiF SEI and return to the Li sub-
strate; in this process, a larger migration energy barrier is required.
Except for individual intermediate states, the Bader charge transfer
of the unapplied strain is proportional to the migration activation
energy, and the applied strain causes the Bader charge transfer
value to be inversely proportional to the migration activation en-
ergy. The mechanical strength ﬁne-tunes the atomic structure of
the interface, changes the amount of charge transfer, and then
changes the magnitude of the ion transport energy barrier.
4. Conclusions
LiF is an inorganic component of the SEI, and whether used as an
electrolyte additive material or as artiﬁcial SEI, it has always been a
focus of research. In this work, ﬁrst-principles calculations are used
to determine the transport characteristics of Li ions in the LaeLiF
SEI interface system, including the vertical transport behavior in
the grain boundary GB and grain G and the plane diffusion behavior
in the interface I. In addition, strain engineering is added in a dis-
cussion of the inﬂuence of strain on the plane diffusion behavior in
the interface I.
The results show that the incorporation of La reduces the tensile
strength and strain of the system (the tensile strength is reduced
from 5.85 GPa to 2.28 GPa, and the strain is reduced from 13.81% to
8.029%). However, the formation energy of vacancy defects at the
grain boundary GB is improved, the structure of the vacancy defect
is stabilized, and a stable transport path is provided for the inser-
tion/extraction of Li ions. At the same time, the grain boundary GB
has a smaller migration activation energy barrier than the crystal
grain (0.813 eV), and the stable defect structure and low transport
energy barrier promote the rapid migration of Li ions at the grain
boundary GB. It can be seen that during the charging and dis-
charging of the battery, the insertion/extraction of Li ions tends
toward vertical transport at the grain boundaries. The critical ten-
sile strain reduces the formation energy of void defects between
the interface I, promotes the formation of vacancies and provides a
path for plane diffusion of ions. Simultaneously, this strain reduces
the energy barrier of Li ion diffusion on the lithium metal surface
(from 1.274 eV to 0.813 eV) and promotes the full diffusion and
uniform deposition of Li ions between the interfaces. Strain has
little effect on the migration behavior of Li ions leaving the Li
substrate and inserting into the LaeLiF SEI interface. Instead, strain
increases the energy required for lithium ions to escape from the
SEI. Therefore, the strain causes an increase in the migration barrier
of lithium ions back and forth between the LaeLiF SEI and the
lithium anode.
