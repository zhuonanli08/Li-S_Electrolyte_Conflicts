Leakage-Proof Electrolyte Chemistry for a High-Performance
Lithium–Sulfur Battery
Jiangwei Ju+, Shanmu Dong+, Yanyan Cui+, Yanfen Zhang, Ben Tang, Feng Jiang, Zili Cui,
Huanrui Zhang, Xiaofan Du, Tao Lu, Lang Huang, Guanglei Cui,* and Liquan Chen
Abstract: Electrolyte leakage is a severe safety concern in
lithium batteries. With highly volatile 1,2-dimethoxyethane as
solvent, the leakage related hazards are more pronounced in
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries. To address this concern,
a leakage-proof electrolyte is delicately designed through
functionalizing the commercial electrolyte by Li6PS5Cl-grafted
poly(ethyl cyanoacrylate), which can interact readily with the
aluminum-plastic packing through hydrogen bond to immo-
bilize the electrolyte. The moisture from ambient can also
catalyze a further polymerization of the macromolecules to
seal the leaking points and thereby to solve the leakage issue,
endowing Li-S batteries superior safety even in an artificial cut
pouch cell. With a bare S loading of 4.9 mgcm@2, the battery
can deliver good endurance owing to the suppressed poly-
sulfide shuttle by its polar groups. This work enlightens the
design of leakage-proof electrolyte and makes a milestone for
high performance Li-S batteries.
Introduction
Since its commercialization in 1991, rechargeable lithium
batteries have received great attention owing to its indispen-
sable role in todayQs consumer electronics, such as mobile
phones, laptops, and in electric vehicles. However, most of the
commercial high energy density lithium batteries employ
volatile, toxic, flammable organic solvents as electrolyte
components, and aluminum-plastic film as package. The
leakage of these liquids in the form of exudation from
unsuccessful sealing points is unavoidable in the course of
battery packing and using, resulting in serious safety hazards
like toxication, combustion, or even explosion.[1] More
importantly, the volatilization of the solvent within electro-
lytes will greatly worsen these safety concerns as well as
battery performance. It is noted that, 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), which is the most promising electrolyte solvent used
in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery, is extremely volatile and
flammable.[2] Therefore, the leakage related concerns in Li-S
batteries are much more severe than those of commercial
lithium-ion batteries, which will retard the commercialization
processes of this promising next-generation battery technol-
ogy.
Replacing the conventional liquid electrolyte by solid
electrolyte has been a promising strategy to avoid the liquid
leakage.[3] Yet, in solid-state Li-S battery, without the
dissolution of polysulfides, the electrochemical reduction of
nonconductive sulfur only occurs on the sulfur/carbon inter-
face and thus the bulk of active sulfur cannot be fully utilized,
resulting in low specific capacity and significantly reduced
energy density.[4] Accordingly, liquid electrolyte is indispen-
sable
in
Li-S
battery
to
gain
high
energy
exceeding
500 Whkg@1 at present. To this end, the addition of soluble
polymers to modify the electrolyte properties, for example,
reducing the liquidity or enhancing the adhesive force,
suggests to be the best option to solve the leakage issue.[5]
However, promising candidates for such kinds of functional
polymers are scarcely reported, as the solvation capacity of
DME for common polymers is very low. Moreover, the
addition of insulating polymers will decrease the ionic
conductivity of the electrolyte and thereby degrade the
battery performance substantially. Till now, an DME-soluble
polymer with satisfactory ionic conductivity to reduce the
liquidity or to enhance the adhesive force with the soft-
package film has never been reported as the best of our
knowledge.
Herein, the delicately designed DME-soluble and lithium
conducting macromolecules are presented from the reaction
between ethyl cyanoacrylate (ECA) and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl) as
schematically shown in Figure 1. Both experimentally and
theoretically, the macromolecules with functional groups of
> C=O and -C/N demonstrate strong hydrogen bond inter-
action with the aluminum-plastic packing film of the pouch
cell, which will immobilize the electrolyte firmly within the
pouch cell. It is also noted that the macromolecules can be
further polymerized by the moisture from ambient environ-
ment to seal the leaking point if the battery is damaged by
accidents. Moreover, the as-designed electrolyte with such
strong leakage-proof capability presents superior ionic con-
ductivity of 1.11 mScm@1. The Li-S battery based on the
leakage-proof electrolyte can not only demonstrate stable
charge/discharge behavior in one damaged cell, but also

Results and Discussion
ECA is a well-known main ingredient of super glue, which
has been also proved as promising polymer electrolyte for
lithium batteries owing to enhanced electro-oxidation resist-
ance and considerable ionic conductivity.[6] Polymerization of
ECA is initiated by anions in the absence of water and
ambient air, what is called anionic polymerization.[7] On the
basis of this mechanism, LPSCl with strong nucleophilicity as
well as high Li+ fraction neutralized by anions of PS4
3@, S2@,
and Cl@ is a very promising choice to initiate ECA polymer-
ization,[8] in which the anions are bonded to poly(ECA)
segments while Li+ is attracted around by coulombic force to
make ECA conductive. The detailed preparation process and
the reaction mechanism between LPSCl and ECA can be
found in the Supporting Information. The reaction products
are defined as LPSCl-grafted poly(ECA), i.e., LgP, for
convenience.
The leakage-proof electrolyte is then obtained via func-
tionalizing the commercial electrolyte, i.e., 1 M lithium
bis(tri-fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)
: DME (volume ratio, 1:1) containing 1 wt.% LiNO3, with
LgP. Compared with the ECA (without LPSCl grafting)
modified commercial electrolyte, LgP significantly improves
the ionic conductivity of the leakage-proof electrolyte. The
ionic conductivity dependence on ECA and LgP content is
shown in the Supporting Information, Figure S6a. Ionic
conductivity of the commercial electrolyte with glass fiber
separator is 2.57 mScm@1, decreases sharply to 0.87, 0.51, 0.42,
0.31, and 0.17 mScm@1 after 10–50 wt% ECA addition, which
are several times smaller than those of LgP modified
commercial electrolyte. At the content of 20 wt%, the ionic
conductivity of leakage-proof electrolyte is 1.11 mScm@1, 2.6
times larger than that of the ECA modified one, indicating the
huge priority of the Li+ conductive additives. Figure S6b
displays that in Li-S coin cells, the leakage-proof electrolyte
with 20 wt% LgP delivers the best cycling performance
among these different components.
Consequently, unless stated otherwise, the leakage-proof
electrolyte discussed below is the one with 20 wt% LgP
presence.
To date, aluminum-plastic film is the most frequently used
material to pack lithium batteries. Hence, the slide behavior
of liquids on aluminum-plastic film is studied as a criterion to
check the leakage proof function of the leakage-proof
electrolyte. As shown in Figure 2a, when the slant angle
increases from 0 to 208, both electrolytes are immobile. This
situation for the commercial electrolyte remains until the
slant angle increases to 358, at which the drop moves 1.05 mm
within 20 seconds. By a sharp contrast, the leakage-proof
electrolyte drop holds steady even when the slant angle
continuously increases to 908. Detailed processes can be seen
in Video S1 and Video S2. In this case, it can be speculated
that the leakage-proof electrolyte will not flow or exude from
the cut of a battery and thus achieving leakage proof
kinetically as illustrated by Figure 1.
Immobility of the leakage-proof electrolyte is verified to
result from the intermolecular interaction between LgP and
the aluminum-plastic film. Infrared spectroscopy (IR, Fig-
ure S7a) demonstrates that the internal surface of the
aluminum-plastic film is composed of polythene. In addition,
no peaks shift when LgP is added into the commercial
electrolyte (Figure S7b), which means no interaction happens
among LgP, DOL, or DME molecules, agreeing well with the
NMR results (Figure S2). When contacting with the alumi-
num-plastic film, the CH2 peak of DOL in commercial
electrolyte shifts from 1357 cm@1 to 1352 cm@1,[9] implying the

Apart from the immobility of the leakage-proof electro-
lyte on the aluminum-plastic film, it is found that the LgP
macromolecules can be further polymerized to suppress
DME volatilization when exposed to ambient air, which also
contributes to the leakage-proof function. The average
molecular weight of the as-prepared leakage-proof electro-
lyte
is
2787 gmol@1,
which
increases
significantly
to
13430 gmol@1 only after a transient exposure to ambient air.
It is speculated that the sharp weight increase origins from the
fast polymerization of the residual ECA monomers catalyzed
by the moisture from ambient air.[6,7,10] To verify this, IR
spectra of the leakage-proof electrolyte before and after
exposed to ambient air are compared. As shown in Fig-
ure S7e, after exposed to ambient air, the intensity of the peak
at 1600 cm@1 decreases while the intensity of the peak at
1744 cm@1 increases substantially, which means that the
ethenolate motif decreases while the carbonyl (C=O) of the
Poly(ECA) segment increases, confirming the speculation of
further polymerization. The possible reaction mechanism is
proposed in Scheme S2 in the Supporting Information. To
show the suppressed volatilization by the further polymerized
LgP macromolecules, Figure S7d compares the volume loss of
commercial electrolyte and leakage-proof electrolyte. After
exposed to ambient air for 24 h, the commercial electrolyte
loses 42 vol% due to the violent volatilization of DME while
the leakage-proof electrolyte only has a loss of 12 vol%.
Consequently, it is conceivable that when the leakage-proof
electrolyte is exposed to ambient air by accidents, the
resultant large molecular polymers will seal the leaking point
to inhibit the DME volatilization as well as to stop electrolyte
leaking effectively. With the two functions, much safe Li-S
pouch cells with stable charge/discharge behavior are dem-
onstrated below.
The leakage-proof function of leakage-proof electrolyte is
demonstrated in a pouch cell packed using aluminum-plastic
film. As shown in Video S3, after cutting the bottom right
corner, the commercial electrolyte comes from the cut drop
by drop. By a huge contrast, no one drop is seen after cutting
the leakage-proof electrolyte-based cell (Video S4) due to the
immobility of leakage-proof electrolyte on aluminum-plastic
film, agreeing well with the slide behavior as shown in
Figure 2a and 2b. To vividly demonstrate the leakage proof
function and the resultant safety, flame test is conducted on
cut cells. As shown in Figure 3a and Video S5, reddish flame
arises as soon as the lighter is close to the cut. Due to the
electrolyte leakage and extreme volatilization of DME, the
fierce flame nearly covers the whole cell within only
5 seconds. After 10 seconds, the reddish flame turns into
orange, which means that the exhaustion of the electrolyte
and combustion of the aluminum-plastic film or separator.
The burning process lasts for 35 seconds, after which the
pouch cell is badly burned. By a huge contrast, due to the
suppressed volatilization by the resultant polymers catalyzed
by moisture, only a small fire is seen from the cut of the
leakage-proof electrolyte-based pouch cell, which may origins
from the exposed a small quantity of electrolyte. More
importantly, this burning process lasts for no more than 10
seconds, after which, the cell still has a healthy appearance
(Figure 3a and Video S6). It should be noted that for safety
reasons, the anode used in the pouch cells for flame test is
indium rather than lithium.
With the leakage-proof electrolyte, not only the safety but
also the electrochemical stability of Li-S battery are ensured.
It is acknowledged that during the discharge process, S is
firstly lithiated to form a series of long-chain polysulfides, i.e.,
S8!Li2S8!Li2S6!Li2S4, which are dissolved readily into
DME based electrolyte.[11] Upon further lithiation, these
dissolved long-chain polysulfides form short-chain sulfide
species, i.e., Li2S2 and Li2S, and precipitate onto the electrode.
Based on the working mechanism, the pouch cells are cut
after discharging for 2.5 h at which the solid S is all converted
to long-chain polysulfides and dissolved into the electrolyte.
For the cell using commercial electrolyte, brown liquids are
trickling from the cell and a vigorous voltage disturbance is
shown in the discharge profile (Figure 3b). The dissolved
active material, for example, Li2S4, losses with the electrolyte
leakage. As a result, the discharge capacity of only 1.46 mAh
is demonstrated after cut to result in a total value of
3.71 mAh. What is more, the sequential charge capacity is
merely 2.12 mAh, suggesting the great active material loss
due to the electrolyte leakage. For the leakage-proof electro-
lyte-based cell, only a slight voltage disturbance is observed
after being cut. More importantly, the cell demonstrates
a discharge capacity of 7.70 mAh and a sequential charge

Besides the leakage-proof function, the leakage-proof
electrolyte also has significantly improved effect on suppress-
ing polysulfide shuttle. To show this vividly, optically trans-
parent Li-S batteries based on the leakage-proof electrolyte
and commercial electrolyte are assembled.
The visual changes of the electrolyte with battery
discharge are shown in Figure 4a. For the battery with the
commercial electrolyte, light yellow liquids diffuse visually
from the cathode into the transparent electrolyte after 3 h of
discharge while the whole electrolyte appears brown after 6 h
of discharge, owing to the high concentration of soluble long-
chain polysulfides. However, for the battery with leakage-
proof electrolyte, no apparent color change is observed within
6 h of discharge, indicating the suppressed polysulfide diffu-
sion, which can also be verified by the obvious color change
after directly adding the leakage-proof electrolyte into Li2S8
solution (Video S7), and the comparison of the self-discharge
behavior of Li-S coin cells using both commercial and the
leakage-proof electrolyte (Figure S8).
Given the component difference, the suppressed diffusion
may origins from the interaction (not reaction) between LgP
and polysulfides because there are no changes of the
oxidation/reduction peaks in cyclic voltammetry and of the
plateau in charge/discharge profiles (Figure S9), which means
no charge and discharge principles are changed. As shown in
Figure S10, the IR peak of S8
2@ shifts readily from 1075 to
1084 cm@1, resulting from the strong interaction between S8
2@
and > C=O (@1.45 eV), which is twice of the DOL-poly-
sulfide (@0.74 eV) and 15 times larger than that of the DME-
polysulfide (@0.10 eV). Just because of the strong affinity of
LgP for polysulfides, when LgP content is high, for example,
50 wt%, the polysulfides are strongly anchored by LgP thus
cannot easily diffuse back to fulfill the full electrochemical
conversion, resulting in inferior battery performance (Fig-
ure S6b). An optimized balance between the adsorption and
diffusion is necessary to keep polysulfides active during
cycling and to ensure good cycle stability.[12] Therefore, in this
work, the leakage-proof electrolyte with 20 wt% LgP is the
optimal electrolyte for high performance Li-S battery.
Utilizing bare S cathodes with 60 wt.% (1 mgcm@2)
loading, the leakage-proof electrolyte battery demonstrates
outstanding rate performance at 0.1–2C (Figure 4b). For
example, at a low rate of 0.1C, the average discharge capacity
is about 920 mAhg@1 while it is only 690 mAhg@1 for the
battery using commercial electrolyte. When the rate increases
to 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0C, the discharge capacity decreases to 722,
608, and 534 mAhg@1, respectively, which are all higher than
that of the commercial electrolyte-based battery. Even at
a high rate of 2C, the leakage-proof electrolyte-based battery
still possesses a satisfactory value of 455 mAhg@1. The long-
term cycling performances of the batteries are tested at 0.5C.
The battery with the leakage-proof electrolyte delivers an
initial discharge capacity of 644 mAhg@1. After 300 cycles,
a capacity of 401 mAhg@1 is still retained. By a huge contrast,
the retained capacity is only 189 mAhg@1 for the commercial
electrolyte-based battery (Figure 4c). Moreover, the leakage-
proof electrolyte-based battery with 4.9 mgcm@2 sulfur load-
ing can also deliver a high areal capacity of 405 mAhg@1 after
200 cycles, over 9 times larger than that of the commercial
electrolyte-based battery, 44 mAhg@1 (Figure S11).
Conclusion
The DME soluble and Li+-conductive macromolecules
are dedicatedly designed via the reaction between ECA and
LPSCl. A leakage-proof electrolyte for Li-S battery is
prepared by adding LgP into the commercial Li-S electrolyte.
The optimized leakage-proof electrolyte possesses a room
temperature ionic conductivity of 1.11 mScm@1. With this
electrolyte, the Li-S battery using bare S (60 wt.%) cathode
can deliver a satisfactory discharge capacity of 455 mAhg@1
even at a high rate of 2C and stable cycling with a sulfur
loading of 4.9 mgcm@2 owing to the strong affinity of LgP for
polysulfide. With this electrolyte, the electrolyte leakage is
prevented effectively by the potent intermolecular forces
between LgP and the inner aluminum-plastic package. Even
in an injured Li-S battery, the exposed LgP macromolecules
can be catalyzed by moisture to be further polymerized to seal
the battery openings, ensuring good battery safety and charge/
discharge stability. This contribution highlights the impor-
tance and guides the design of leakage-proof electrolyte for
highly safe and electrochemically stable battery.
