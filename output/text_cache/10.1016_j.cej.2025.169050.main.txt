A B S T R A C T
Attaining both high energy density and prolonged cycle stability in lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries remains a 
persistent challenge, particularly for sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathodes, owing to interfacial instability 
and restricted sulfur incorporation. Furthermore, elevated sulfur content in the electrode exacerbates capacity 
degradation in Li-SPAN cells. Herein, we report a pre-potentiostatic strategy to induce in situ reductive 
decomposition of LiPF6 based carbonate electrolytes, with the formation of a compact and LiF-rich cathode 
electrolyte interphase (CEI) on a S8@S7Se/PAN composite cathode with a high sulfur loading content. The robust 
LiF-rich CEI effectively suppresses polysulfide dissolution, mitigates parasitic side reactions, and facilitates solid- 
phase sulfur redox reactions by providing a mechanically stable and ionically conductive interface. Unlike 
conventional organic-rich transient CEIs, the LiF-rich CEIs exhibit superior uniformity and long-term stability 
under lean electrolyte (E/S ≤4.5 μL mg−1) and high mass loading (>4 mg cm−2) conditions. Electrochemical 
tests demonstrate a high initial capacity of 1622.68 mAh g−1 at a current density of 0.2C and stable cycling 
performance with a reversible capacity of 714.7 mAh g−1 over 5000 cycles under the lean electrolyte conditions 
(E/S = 3.5 μL mg−1) in Li(~ 45 μm)||S8@S7Se/PAN(5.51 mg cm−2) cells. This study presents a universal and 
scalable CEI engineering strategy that effectively resolves the conventional trade-off between energy density and 
cycling stability in Li-SPAN systems. The progressive degradation of the CEI is a critical determinant of electrode 
performance decay at high active material loading, thereby providing a viable pathway toward the practical 
implementation of high-performance Li–S batteries.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) possess remarkably high theoretical 
gravimetric (EG = 2600 Wh kg−1) and volumetric (EV = 2800 Wh L−1) 
energy densities, positioning them as promising candidates to satisfy the 
growing demands of next-generation electric vehicles and advanced 
portable electronics [1]. Nonetheless, concurrently realizing both high 
practical energy density and extended cycle life remains a formidable 
challenge, primarily due to the lagging phase-transition redox of sulfur 
cathode. More concretely, the solid–liquid–solid conversion pathway 
has been employed to facilitate the transformation from S8 to Li2S. While 
this approach circumvents the low electronic conductivity of sulfur (5 ×
10−30 S cm−1) and its solid-state discharge products Li2S and Li2S2 
(10−13 S cm−1) [2], which otherwise hinder electrochemical activity and 
sulfur utilization, it introduces a host of new complications. Notably, the 
formation of soluble lithium polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates in ether- 
based electrolytes leads to their diffusion driven redistribution, trig­
gering the well-known “shuttle effect” [3]. This phenomenon results in 
electrolyte contamination, corrosion of the lithium anode, and rapid 
capacity and energy density degradation-effects that are particularly 
detrimental when the total cell mass is considered. Moreover, the 
multiphase nature of this redox pathway necessitates a large number of 
electrode–electrolyte interfacial contact sites, requiring high electrode 
porosity (50–60 %) to enable efficient reaction kinetics [4]. This 
requirement is inherently at odds with the need for densely packed 
electrodes featuring high sulfur loading (>5 mg cm−2) [5], thereby 
exacerbating electrolyte consumption and diminishing practical energy 
density. Additionally, the non-uniform deposition of sulfur in­
termediates and significant volumetric expansion during cycling further 
accelerate kinetic degradation and compromise electrode stability. To

To address these issues and achieve practical LSBs (> 400 Wh kg−1) 
with enhanced cycling stability, it is imperative to upgrade the 
expendable solid–liquid–solid redox pathway to an idealized solid phase 
conversion mechanism. Employing carbonate-based electrolytes paired 
with compatible sulfur cathodes can effectively prevent the formation of 
soluble LiPS intermediates, significantly improving battery stability [6]. 
Among various cathode materials, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile(SPAN) 
stands out due to its superior theoretical energy density (1184 Wh kg−1), 
surpassing commercially available cathodes like LiFePO4(LFP) and 
LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NCM811) [7]. SPAN holds an enhanced conduc­
tivity (10−7 - 10−5 S cm−1) compared with conventional C/S composites 
due to thermally induced conjugated polymeric networks within poly­
acrylonitrile(PAN) [8], providing efficient electronic conduction chan­
nels for solid redox. Atomically dispersed short-chain sulfur (-Sx-) 
species on PAN achieve over 90 % utilization and nearly 100 % 
coulombic efficiency(CE) benefiting from its solid-solid redox [9]. 
Additionally, SPAN's synthesis is straightforward, requiring only a 
simple one-step high-temperature pyrolysis reaction between sulfur 
powder and PAN, without the complexity typical of universal micro­
porous carbon synthesis. Meanwhile, SPAN cathodes demonstrate 
excellent compatibility with high flash point (60 ◦C) carbonate-based 
electrolytes such as ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate 
(DMC), in contrast to their degradation in ether-based systems [10]. This 
compatibility not only supports high-temperature stability but also en­
hances operational safety, marking a critical advancement for the 
practical deployment of LSBs in demanding environments.

the cycle life declines as sulfur content increases. These limitations are

Efforts have been made to achieve suitable CEI for high sulfur con­
tent cathodes. Electrolyte additives assisted in-situ growth of CEI and 
molecular layer deposition (MLD) encapsulation of sulfur have been 
proven to be effective [17,18]. However, these strategies inevitably 
complicate the fabrication process and raise concerns regarding long- 
term durability. Increasing the proportion of inactive components in 
the Li–SPAN system also undermine energy density. The inorganic-rich 
modulated CEI embodies an optimal interfacial architecture. Lithium 
fluoride (LiF) distinguishes itself through its wide bandgap (8.9 eV), 
high diffusion barrier (0.729 eV), superior mechanical modulus (70 
GPa), exceptional Interfacial energy(73.28 meV Ǻ) and thermodynam­
ically stable [19,20]. LiF's weak interfacial adhesion to the SPAN cath­
ode enables adequate mechanical resilience to mitigate volumetric 
expansion. Its inherently low electronic conductivity demands an ul­
trathin CEI layer, effectively reducing the energy barrier for interfacial 
lithium-ion transport [7]. Moreover, it enhances the electrode's struc­
tural integrity under elevated-temperature conditions. Furthermore, the 
efficacy of LiF-rich CEI in preserving electrode integrity and long-term 
cycling stability has been experimentally demonstrated in various 
cathode materials, including LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2, LiCoO2 and LiMn2O4 
etc. [12]
To extend the lifespan of Li-S batteries and enhance overall energy 
density, we propose a collaborative strategy for constructing high‑sulfur 
cathode materials coupled with the facile in-situ growth of a robust CEI 
as shown in Fig. 1(b-c). Given the synthetic complexity of LiF-rich CEI, 
redox approaches can facilitate the in-situ decomposition of fluorine- 
containing lithium salts in the electrolyte, thereby enabling the depo­
sition of LiF on the electrode surface [21,22]. Selective decomposition of 
key electrolyte components during CEI formation in Fig. 1(b) can create 
a LiF-rich robust CEI, improving electrochemical stability and cycling 
performance non-destructively [23]. S8@S7Se/PAN composites for Li-S 
batteries are synthesized via a two-step spray-phase inversion method 
followed by heat treatment in Fig. 1(d) [21], where a S8/PAN/DMF 
solution is sprayed into water to rapidly form a solidified PAN shell 
around sulfur particles due to solubility differences firstly. The sulfuri­
zation step involves annealing with S7Se at 350 ◦C, where S7Se vapor 
dehydrogenates and cyclizes PAN into a conductive pPAN network 
while substituting sulfur at reactive sites, stabilizing S8 sublimation 
through capillary confinement in micropores to minimize sulfur loss. 
The agglomerated sulfur nanoparticles encapsulated within a micropo­
rous S7Se/PAN layer, forming core–shell microspheres (diameter: 0.5–2 
μm) to ulfill the fabrication for compact cathodes.
This study presents several key advancements: (1) The nanoscale 
(10–30 nm) aggregation structure of the S7Se/PAN layer exhibits high 
electron and ion transport capabilities, (5.12 × 10−9 S cm−1 and 9.66 ×
10−8 cm2 s−1, respectively), thereby activating the encapsulated S8 
particles electrochemically. The synergistic contribution of active ma­
terials within the layer elevates the effective active material content to 
approximately 67.4 wt%. (2) A 1.0 M LiPF6 EC: DMC electrolyte pro­
motes the decomposition of LiPF6 under 1.75 V constant potential 
reduction, forming a thin and compact LiF-rich CEI on the surface of the 
S8@S7Se/PAN cathode, wherein the threefold LiF atomic ratio 
(comparing with conventional CEI), substantially mitigates the struc­
tural degradation induced by sulfur volumetric stress. (3) The robust 
LiF-rich CEI completely isolating the S8 from direct contact with car­
bonate solvents, thereby eliminating electron leakage, suppressing the 
oxidative decomposition of the solvent, and effectively preventing side 
reactions at the cathode interface. Given the observed dependence of 
high sulfur loading S8@S7Se/PAN electrodes on the CEI, we hypothesize 
that CEI degradation constitutes a critical determinant in the capacity 
fading of high sulfur loading electrodes. The regulation of the CEI fa­
cilitates the stablility of the high sulfur content SPAN cathode in 
carbonate-based electrolytes via a solid-solid redox mechanism, thereby 
reducing the Electrolyte/Sulfur (E/S) to even 3.5 μL mg−1 while 
achieving high capacity of 714.7 mAh g−1 over 5000 cycles, excellent 
rate performance, and prolonged cycling stability. Moreover, this 
straightforward strategy of selectively inducing the growth of a LiF-rich 
CEI via a pre-potentiostatic method, is also applicable to other cathodes 
with high active material content to realize extended cycle life.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. Materials characterization of high sulfur loading S8@S7Se/PAN
To fulfill the optimal design criteria for cathode materials, a tap 
density exceeding 0.7 g cm−3 and a sulfur mass fraction surpassing 60 wt 
% are required. The S8@PAN precursor, synthesized via spray phase 
inversion, exhibits an average diameter of 5 μm with a shell thickness of 
180–320 nm, as confirmed by SEM imaged in Fig. S1. Elemental map­
ping via energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) demonstrates 
distinct spatial segregation between carbon and sulfur signals, un­
equivocally validating the uniform core-shell morphology (see Fig. S1). 
These structural characteristics establish a critical foundation for sub­
sequent thermal processing [21,24].
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron mi­
croscopy (TEM) analyses verify the complete encapsulation of S8 within 
the S7Se/PAN shell, confirming a well-defined core-shell architecture. 
This engineered structure effectively suppresses detrimental in­
teractions between electrolyte and sulfur, thereby inhibiting irreversible 
polysulfide dissolution and parasitic side reactions at the CEI. The uni­
form S7Se/PAN coating ensures spatial confinement of sulfur, enhancing 
electrochemical stability while preserving active material integrity 
during extended cycling, rendering it a promising candidate for high 
performance lithium‑sulfur cathodes. Following annealing with S7Se at 
350 ◦C, the resulting S8@S7Se/PAN microspheres exhibit a reduced 
particle size (1–2 μm, Fig. 2a), with a compact morphology akin to 
commercial cathode materials such as LiFePO4 and LiMn2O4, thereby 
minimizing electrolyte consumption and reducing charge transfer 
resistance [19,25,26]. The S7Se/PAN coating also imparts electro­
chemical activity to the encapsulated S₈ core. Elemental composition 
data in Table S1 reveals a combined selenium and sulfur content of 67.4 
wt%, underscoring its high active species loading a pivotal attribute for 
enhancing practical energy density in Li-S cells. TEM analysis in Fig. 2b 
confirms the amorphous nature of the S7Se/PAN microporous shell 
(Thickness ≈92–126 nm). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption measure­
ments in Fig. S3(a–b) corroborate the presence of a microporous struc­
ture (pore size <2 nm), effectively isolating the sulfur core from 
electrolyte contact and suppressing parasitic reactions [27,28]. Ther­
mogravimetric(TGA) analysis in Fig. 2c shows S8@S7Se/PAN exhibits 
intermediate thermal stability (~300 ◦C) between S8 (<200 ◦C) and 
SPAN (>330 ◦C), initially decomposing like S8 before undergoing SPAN- 
like fragmentation. The robust thermal stability observed in the TGA 
profile in Fig. 2c further substantiates the superior sulfur encapsulation 
capability of this configuration. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of 
S8@S7Se/PAN reveals a broad peak centered near 2θ ≈25◦without 
crystalline PAN reflections, confirming complete vulcanization. These

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman spectroscopy

within S7Se/PAN. XPS analysis in Fig. 2d reveals S2p contributions from 
homopolar S–Se and S–S bonds, alongside polar C–S configurations, 
confirming sulfur's integration into the conductive carbon framework 
which is a prerequisite for electrochemical engagement. The reduced 
electron density of sulfur induces a slight negative shift in S–Se (163.18 
eV) and C–S (164.48 eV) binding energies, while selenium‑sulfur in­
teractions similarly shift the Se2p peak, validating S–Se covalent bond 
formation [10]. Raman spectra in Fig. S4(c–d) further identify C–S 
(307/367 cm−1), S–S (470/926 cm−1), and S–Se (387 cm−1) bonds. 
The D (1325 cm−1) and G (1532 cm−1) bands correspond to disordered 
and graphitic carbon, respectively [11,29,30]. The ID/IG ratio of 0.98 for 
phase inverted S8@S7Se/PAN, versus 0.89 for conventional S7Se/PAN, 
suggests enhanced graphitization, potentially improving charge trans­
port [31,32]. The incorporation of selenium into SPAN improves elec­
trochemical kinetics and sulfur accessibility through synergistic S–Se 
covalent interactions and optimized charge transport pathways, capi­
talizing on selenium's enhanced electrochemical activity and homoge­
neous atomic-scale distribution. Thus, the multifunctional S7Se/PAN 
shell comprises conductive polymeric chains functionalized with elec­
troactive species, optimizing both reactivity and energy density.
To assess the suitability of S8@S7Se/PAN for high-density cathodes, a 
systematic comparison of sulfur content and tap density is conducted 
across S7Se/PAN materials with different morphologies in Fig. 2e. The 
S8@S7Se/PAN microspheres show clear advantages, achieving a tap 
density of 2.23 g cm−3, more than double that of conventional S7Se/ 
PAN, and a sulfur content over 20 % higher than polymer-embedded 
sulfur composites [33]. As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the S8@S7Se/PAN is 
particularly advantageous for fabricating thick electrodes with high 
areal capacity. Thus, engineering the morphology of the SPAN matrix to 
accommodate additional sulfur represented a promising strategy to 
overcome the restricted dispersion of active elements in conventional 
SPAN cathodes [34,35]. Simultaneously, the electrode composed of 
densely compacted S8@S7Se/PAN microspheres achieves a high sulfur 
loading exceeding 5 mg cm−2 after roll-pressing, in contrast to con­
ventional loose SPAN composites, thereby meeting the fundamental 
criteria for practical cathode implementation [36,37].

By leveraging pre-potentiostatic modulation of LiPF6 decomposition 
kinetics, we achieve precise control over cathode interfacial engineer­
ing, facilitating the preferential formation of LiF as the dominant inor­
ganic constituent. This methodology produces a nanostructured, 
conformal LiF-rich CEI film exhibiting precisely tailored morphological 
homogeneity and interfacial density.
2.3. The critical role of LiF-rich CEI in solid redox conversion for cathodes
Electrochemical characterization was conducted on both pre- 
pulsation-treated (PS) and baseline (Base) cells to elucidate the mech­
anistic role of the LiF-rich CEI in regulating the redox kinetics of the 
S8@S7Se/PAN cathode, specifically its solid-solid phase transformation 
behavior. The presence of a singular redox peak in the galvanostatic 
charge-discharge profile signifies the highly reversible interconversion 
between S8 and Li2S. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is conducted at 
various potentials during the second cycling in Fig. 4(a). The charac­

and reached maximum intensity at the terminal voltage of 1.0 V. During 
the subsequent charging process, these Li2S peaks gradually diminished 
and became nearly undetectable by 1.9 V [14,44]. This reversible evo­

the S7Se/PAN layer, participate effectively in the solid-phase conversion 
to Li2S. The dense LiF-rich CEI synergistically combines with S7Se/PAN 
encapsulation to prevents carbonate solvent penetration while acceler­
ating both electron and Li+ transport, thereby enabling efficient elec­
trochemical solid-solid conversion of sulfur. To unravel the interfacial 
dynamics governing the LiF-rich CEI, DRT analysis is applied to solid 
redox systems in Fig. 4(b). The DRT profiles for S8@S7Se/PAN cathode

The investigation reveals that a LiF-rich CEI markedly enhances 
solid-state sulfur redox kinetics in S8@S7Se/PAN cathodes by effectively 
inhibiting carbonate solvent permeation while facilitating electron 
transfer and lithium-ion conduction. It also possesses a low interfacial 
impedance, efficient charge transfer kinetics, and exceptional electro­
chemical stability exhibited by the architecture. Conversely, organic- 
heavy CEIs demonstrate detrimental electrolyte penetration, poly­
sulfide shuttling phenomena, and compromised redox activity. LiF-rich 
CEI exceptional capacity to mitigate polysulfide dissolution and para­
sitic self-discharge processes, thereby confirming its superlative inter­
facial properties that ensure outstanding cycle life.
The interfacial reaction kinetics of LiF-rich CEI and organic-rich CEI 
in S8@S7Se/PAN cells were quantified to assess their influence on 
lithium-ion diffusivity. Cyclic voltammetry(CV) and galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique measurements(GITT) are performed at 
incremental scanning rates for electrochemical characterization. This 
observation is supported by galvanostatic intermittent titration tech­
nique (GITT) analysis in Fig. S11(c), which reveales that the lithium-ion 
diffusion coefficients for S8@S7Se/PAN with LiF-rich CEI cathodes 
ranged from 10−16 to 10−13 cm2 s−1 [50]. The characteristic amplitude 
of these values reflect a solid-phase diffusion mechanism, confirming 
that lithium-ion transport occurred exclusively through the CEI during 
redox processes. To further evaluate the ionic conductivity of the CEI, 
cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements are performed on cells featuring 
either S8@S7Se/PAN cells with organic-rich or LiF-rich CEI across scan 
rates ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1 in Fig. S11(d-e). As shown in Fig. 4
(g), the reduction and oxidation peak currents (Ip) are plotted against 
the square root of the scan rates (v0.5). According to the Randles-Sevcik 
equation, the slope of this linear relationship is directly proportional to 
the lithium-ion diffusion coefficient (DLi+). Cells incorporating the LiF- 
rich CEI demonstrate steeper slopes, corresponding to elevated DLi+
values of 9.66 × 10−8 cm2 s−1 and 9.24 × 10−8 cm2 s−1, respectively 
[51]. These diffusion coefficients are more than twice those of the 
organic-rich CEI counterparts, indicating accelerated ion transport and 
boosted redox kinetics. LiF-rich CEI simultaneously enhance bulk charge 
transfer kinetics and lithium-ion transport efficiency across the CEI, 
further enabling synergistic elevation in both energy density and cycling 
life of the sulfur cathode [52].
Given that LiF-rich CEI exhibits superior mechanical robustness, 
which typically broadens the thermal operational window of electrode, 
the electrochemical behavior of S8@S7Se/PAN cells is systematically 
evaluated at an elevated temperature of 60 ◦C. Owing to the robust 
mechanical integrity of the LiF-rich CEI, the high-temperature perfor­
mance (60 ◦C) of S8@S7Se/PAN cells is markedly strengthened in Fig. 4
(h) [34,53]. After 2600 cycles at a current density of 0.05C, the cells 
retained a discharge capacity of 796 mAh g−1 with a nearly 100 % 
coulombic efficiency. The 24 hourw self-discharge at high temperatures 
exhibits negligible impact. This superior thermal stability outperformed 
that of cells equipped with organic-rich CEIs and is attributed to the 
structural stability and high mechanical strength imparted by the LiF 
component.
2.4. Electrochemical performance in high sulfur loading S8@S7Se/PAN 
cathode enhanced by LiF-rich CEI
To rigorously examine the influence of S8@S7Se/PAN composites 
integrated with a LiF-rich CEI on the ultralong cycling stability and 
electrochemical behavior of lithium‑sulfur batteries, we performed a 
systematic and comprehensive assessment. Electrochemical evaluation 
under lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ≤4.5 μL mg−1) and high S8 
loading (> 4 mg cm−2) demonstrates that the S8@S7Se/PAN electrode 
with a LiF-rich CEI exhibited superior electrochemical performance. In 
the initial cycle, the S8@S7Se/PAN cells deliver a irreversible capacity of 
1458.5 mAh g−1 and a coulombic efficiency of 75.87 % (based on total 
S/Se mass) in Fig. 5(a) comparing with previous work. The relatively 
low initial efficiency is primarily attributed to the formation of the CEI 
layer on the electrode surface. With continued cycling, the coulombic 
efficiency rapidly increase to 100 % by the 6th cycle, and the cell 
demonstrates excellent cycling durability, maintaining a reversible ca­
pacity of 714 mAh g−1 after 5000 cycles. Notably, upon replacement of

The specific capacity and degradation mechanisms of S8@S7Se/PAN 
cells featuring a LiF-rich CEI were systematically analyzed under prac­

characterization to further elucidate the functional relevance of LiF-rich 
CEI in energy storage applications. S8@S7Se/PAN cathode with a LiF- 
rich CEI achieves a maximum areal capacity of approximately 3.87 
mAh cm−2 at an optimized film thickness of 75 μm (see Fig. 5(e)), sur­
passing performance metrics reported in previous studies. To further 
investigate capacity fading under practical conditions, differential 
capacity-voltage (dQ/dV) profiles of S8@S7Se/PAN cells with LiF-rich 
and Organic-rich CEIs from the 2nd to 50th cycles are compared 
(Fig. 5(f-g)) [55,64]. For the LiF-rich CEI cell, the redox peak positions 
remaines nearly unchanged, whereas the Organic-rich CEI cell exhibited 
a gradual shift of discharge peaks toward lower voltages and charge 
peaks toward higher voltages, indicative of continuous active lithium 
consumption and increasing polarization. EIS analyses conducted on 
S8@SPAN, S8@S7Se/PAN and S8@S7Se/PAN-PS cells at intervals cycles 
further revealed that the primary mechanism underlying the perfor­
mance deterioration of cathodes was predominantly associated with the 
degradation of CEI in Fig. S12(a-c).
As cycling progressed, the appearance of liquid-phase redox peaks 
during the charging process in the Organic-rich CEI cell indicate 
recurring CEI breakdown and reformation. The decline directly contacts 
on cathode interface and accelerate LiPS dissolution. In contrast, the 
redox peak positions and areas in the LiF-rich CEI cell remain virtually 
unchanged between the 500th and 550th cycles, confirming that the 
incorporation of LiF significantly enhances CEI stability (see Fig. S13 
(a),14). EIS analysis of S8@S7Se/PAN with LiF-rich CEI under varying E/ 
S ratios reveal that as electrolyte volume decreased in Fig. 5(h), the high- 
frequency intercept (associated with ohmic resistance Rb) and imped­
ance changes in the high to mid frequency range (related to CEI elec­
tron/ion transport) remained relatively minor [56]. In contrast, the 
impedance in the low-frequency region, governed by lithium-ion diffu­
sion, increases more substantially. These findings indicate that under 
lean electrolyte conditions (E/S < 4.5 μL mg−1) [55,57,63], lithium-ion 
diffusion becomes the primary limiting factor, while the electrode's 
charge transfer capabilities and CEI formation remain largely unaf­
fected. The presence of LiF effectively mitigates interfacial impedance 
growth, enabling the cathode/electrolyte interface to retain excellent 
electrochemical performance even under electrolyte-limited conditions 
[58,59–61]. These findings demonstrate that the elevated active mate­
rial concentration, combined with the structural robustness of the LiF- 
rich CEI in the S8@S7Se/PAN cathode, substantially enhances the 
composite's specific capacity and cycling durability. Furthermore, this 
configuration facilitates accelerated redox reaction kinetics, thereby 
optimizing the battery's rate performance.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we developed a high performance lithium‑sulfur bat­
tery system based on a novel S8@S7Se/PAN core-shell cathode archi­
tecture coupled with an in situ formed LiF-rich CEI. The S8 core ensures 
high sulfur content (67.4 wt%) and high energy density, while the S7Se/ 
PAN shell enhances electronic conductivity and suppresses volume 
expansion. Through a pre-potentiostatic protocol at 1.75 V, the 
decomposition of LiPF6 electrolyte induced the controlled formation of a 
compact and uniform LiF-rich CEI with superior mechanical robustness, 
thermal stability, and ionic conductivity. This interfacial layer effec­
tively prevented polysulfide dissolution, inhibited parasitic reactions at 
the cathode-electrolyte interface, and ensured stable lithium-ion trans­
port during extended cycling. Electrochemical tests revealed that this 
architecture delivers a high areal capacity of 3.87 mAh cm−2, a 
reversible specific capacity of 1229.4 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles(Ca­
pacity retention rate = 83 %) under lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ≤
4.5 μL mg−1), and exceptional rate performance (586.4 mAh g−1 at 3C).
