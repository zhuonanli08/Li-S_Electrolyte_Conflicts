TOPICAL COLLECTION: ADVANCED METAL ION BATTERIES
Protecting Li Metal Anode While Suppressing “Shuttle Effect” of Li‑S 
Battery Through Localized High‑Concentration Electrolyte
Zhenpeng Hou1 · Peng‑Fei Wang2,3   · Xinyi Sun1 · Wei Li1 · Chuanchao Sheng1 · Ping He1
Received: 20 April 2022 / Accepted: 2 June 2022 / Published online: 1 July 2022 
© The Minerals, Metals & Materials Society 2022
Abstract
Li-S batteries are widely studied due to their superior theoretical energy density. However, the “shuttle effect” on the cath-
ode and the unstable Li metal anode hinder their practical application. During cycling, the “shuttle effect” leads to severe 
self-discharge and accelerates the capacity decay. Moreover, the shuttled polysulfides aggravate the growth of dendrites and 
the loss of Li, causing a low Coulombic efficiency. Considering electrolyte plays a crucial role in the polysulfides solvation 
behavior and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)-formation process, a well-designed electrolyte is required. Here, we report a 
local high-concentration electrolyte for Li-S batteries. This electrolyte is prepared by adding “diluent” 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 
methyl ether (TME) to the conventional electrolyte. The reduction of free solvent in the Li-ion solvation sheath suppresses the 
dissolution of the polysulfides, and more anion-derived SEI can be formed. Our work simultaneously restrains the “shuttle 
effect” of the cathode and builds a stable SEI on the anode to protect the Li metal. Without any ­LiNO3 additive, the average 
Coulombic efficiency of the Li anode can reach 98.87% after 120 cycles, and Li-S batteries with superior capacity retention 
of 60% after 400 cycles at 0.5 C can be achieved.
Keywords  Local high-concentration electrolyte · Li metal anode · Coulombic efficiency · Shuttle effect · Li-S battery.
Introduction
As technology develops, energy storage apparatus has 
become increasingly important.1 Among them, Li-S bat-
teries are considered as favorable energy storage apparatus 
because of their superior theoretical specific energy and spe-
cific capacity.2 Also, S serves as the cathode material in Li-S 
batteries, which is abundant and environmentally friendly.3 
Unfortunately, there are still some difficulties hindering the 
feasible utilization of Li-S batteries.4,5 In the cathode, the 
Li polysulfide (LiPS) will be formed during the cycles, and 
it will further migrate to the Li metal anode. The LiPSs will 
be reduced on the Li metal anode, and subsequently migrate 
back to the cathode to be oxidized, leading to an overcharge 
phenomenon (also known as the “shuttle effect”).6 This phe-
nomenon leads to low Coulombic efficiency (CE), severe 
self-discharge, inferior utilization of sulfur, and rapid capac-
ity decay during the cycles. Unstable Li metal anodes in Li-S 
batteries is another problem, although Li metal has been 
considered to be the ultimate alternate for the Li-battery 
anode due to the uppermost theoretical specific capacity and 
the lowermost electrode potential.7 The rapid formation of 
Li-dendrite and inevitable parasitic reactions with the elec-
trolyte accelerate the failure of the battery.8
To overcome these problems, researchers have developed 
various methods. For the cathode, many scientists have dem-
onstrated that the chemisorption of polysulfides by hollow 
porous carbon,9 metal oxides,10,11 phosphides,12 carbides,13 
or nitrides,14 or ­electrocatalysis15 of the reaction process is a 
valid strategy to suppress the “shuttle effect”.16 Nevertheless, 
from a thermodynamic point of view, these strategies can only

mitigate but not exterminate the dissolution of soluble LiPSs 
in ether solvents.17 For the anode, researchers have developed 
current collector modification, 18–21 Li metal interface protec-
tion, 22–25 and electrolyte design,26–29 which have significantly 
improved the reversibility of Li metal anodes.
Recently, electrolyte design is considered an effective strat-
egy to deal with the “shuttle effect” and also achieve stable Li 
metal anodes.30 One promising approach is increasing the elec-
trolyte concentration. Under high concentration, the solubility 
of Li polysulfides can be evidently reduced, and the shuttle 
of polysulfides can be suppressed. This is because, in high-
concentration electrolytes, almost all the solvent molecules 
are coordinated with salt cations, and there is almost no free 
solvent in the solution, which reduces the dissolution of the 
polysulfides. In addition, in high-concentration electrolyte, Li 
salts are more likely to react with Li metal, forming a tremen-
dously dense and robust solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer 
rich in inorganic components, which successfully prevent the 
corrosion of the Li metal.
Nevertheless, high-concentration electrolytes suffer 
from the hindrances of high viscidity, relatively low ionic 
conductivity, and expensive Li salts, which limit their com-
mercial applications. To overcome these problems, a local 
high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) has been developed. 
By adding a “diluent” which has little or even no solubil-
ity for Li salts to the high-concentration electrolyte, 31  the 
original solvated structure can be retained. At the same time, 
it can effectively reduce the total salt concentration of the 
entire electrolyte system. Under this structure, low viscosity, 
high ionic conductivity, and economical electrolytes can be 
achieved.
In this study, we employ a novel “diluent”, 1, 1, 2, 2-tetra-
fluoroethyl methyl ether (TME), to design a LHCE system 
(LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME). In this system, the reduction of 
free solvent in Li-ion solvation sheath effectively inhibits 
the dissolution of the polysulfides. Meanwhile, more TFSI-
derived inorganic layers along with the organic outer layer 
arising from dimethoxyethane (DME) and dioxolane (DOL) 
solvent will build a robust SEI layer, which effectively 
improves the cyclic stability of the Li metal anode. Our work 
simultaneously restrains the “shuttle effect” of the sulfur 
cathode and builds a robust SEI upon the anode to protect 
the Li metal. Without using ­LiNO3, a superior average CE of 
Li-Cu batteries can be achieved, and the capacity retention 
of Li-S batteries were also evidently improved.
Results and Discussion
The CE of Li‑Cu Batteries with Different Electrolytes
The Coulombic efficiency of Li-Cu batteries with the LHCE 
(LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME) system was evaluated, and the 
molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL:TME was 1:1.5:1.5:6 for 
a total salt concentration equivalent to 1.8 M LiTFSI-DME/
DOL/TME. For comparison, two other electrolytes were 
also tested: high-concentration electrolyte (LiTFSI-DME/
DOL, the molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL is 1:1.5:1.5 for 
a total salt concentration equivalent to 5.5 M LiTFSI-DME/
DOL) denoted as HCE, and a low-concentration electro-
lyte (1M LiTFSI-DME/DOL v: v = 1:1) denoted as LCE. 
Li-Cu batteries were used to calculate the CE in the three 
systems (Fig. 1). The battery using LCE showed a poor ini-
tial CE of only 76% (Fig. 1a), and the CE quickly dropped 
to less than 80% after 40 cycles. The initial CE of Li was 
slightly improved to 82% when HCE was used (Fig. 1b). 
The CE then further increased to 98.5% (Fig. S1), while it 
began to decline after 60 cycles. Consequently, increasing 
the concentration of the electrolyte can evidently enhance 
the reversibility of the Li metal, but still cannot build a 
stable Li anode for long cycling stability. Remarkably, in 
the TME-based LHCE, the Li metal achieved a high initial 
CE of 93.16% (Fig. 1c), and the CE reached 99.37% after 
100 cycles (Fig. 1d). The corresponding average CE of 120 
cycles was 98.87%, even higher than those of the common 
electrolytes for Li-S batteries with the ­LiNO3 additive. After 
adding TME, the LHCE shows an improved CE compared 
with HCE. The CE of the battery with LHCE remarkably 
increased compared with that of LCE. On the one hand, 
the total salt concentration of LHCE (1.8 M) is higher than 
that of LCE (1 M), which could facilitate the formation of 
a more robust and stable SEI layer. On the other hand, the 
local concentration of LHCE is 5.5 M, which is also much 
higher than that in LCE. In addition, the CE of LHCE under 
limited Li was also evaluated. It can be seen that the CE of 
Li in LHCE improved to 99.13% with only 4 times excess 
Li. When the excess Li was further reduced to 0.5 times, the 
CE was still 98.35% (Fig. S2), indicating that a robust SEI 
is formed on the Li metal anode.
The aggregate capacity loss of Li-Cu batteries was also 
calculated (Fig. 1e). An amount of 4 mAh ­cm−2 of Li was 
deposited on copper foil as the anode and another copper 
foil as the cathode to assemble a Li-Cu battery. Under treble 
of excess Li, the battery with LCE and HCE malfunctioned 
after 65 and 78 cycles, respectively. Significantly, after opti-
mizing by diluent TME, the Li-metal battery in the LHCE 
still worked for over 157 cycles. Also, the overpotential of 
Li-Cu with LHCE was much lower thant in other electrolytes 
and kept stable upon the cycling process (Fig. 1f).
Solvation Structure Analysis of Electrolyte
In conventional standard concentration electrolytes, free 
solvent molecules are sufficient, thus ­Li+ coordinates with 
the solvent to construct solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs).

aggregates (AGGs). The lowest unoccupied molecular

Since the SEI is generated by competitive breakdown 
of the solvent and the anions, the LUMO energy level of 
­Li+-ether complexes is superior to that of ­Li+-carbonate 
complexes.34 Accordingly, the anion decomposition in ether-
based electrolytes is more pronounced.35
Subsequently, Raman characterization was carried out 
to investigate the ionic solvation behavior between the 
three systems (Fig. 2b). The addition of TME significantly 
changed the coordination of the Li ions based on the DOL 
and DME electrolyte systems. Raman spectra of different 
electrolytes were obtained. From LCE to HCE, the charac-
teristic peak of the S-N-S bond gradually shifted from 742 
to 746 ­cm−1 (Fig. 2c), corresponding to the transformation 
of free ­TFSI- in the electrolyte to ­TFSI- coordinated with 
­Li+.36 Remarkably, in the TME-based LHCE, the character-
istic peak of the S-N-S bond shifted to 749 ­cm–1 (Fig. 2c). 
This indicates that ­TFSI- increased in the ­Li+ solvation 
sheath of LHCE. Usually, SSIPs in dilute solution occupy 
the dominant position of a ­Li+ solvation structure. In high-
concentration electrolytes, the structure of CIPs and AGGs 
gradually increases. The addition of TME further increases 
the proportion of the AGGs structure.

Composition of SEI on Li surface
As is mentioned above, the Li metal anode in LHCE shows 
an improved CE compared with LCE and HCE. Consid-
ering that the ionic solvation behavior is closely related 
to the SEI layer construction, the SEI layer in different 
electrolytes was studied in detail. The SEI generated in 
HCE and LHCE was tested by the X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 3). In the HCE electrolyte, an 
obvious C-F peak can be seen at the binding energy of 
689.1 eV,36 and a relatively weak LiF peak can be seen at 
685 eV (Fig. 3a).36 Moreover, there is an obvious ­Li2CO3 
peak at 292.6 eV (Fig. 3c). The main composition of the 
SEI layer in HCE is a small amount of LiF, ­Li2CO3,  and 
organic oligomer components originated from the sustain-
ing reduction of DOL, such as C-O-C and ­CO2R (Fig. 3c). 
As for LHCE, the content of LiF in the SEI is signifi-
cantly higher than HCE (Fig. 3b), while ­Li2CO3 disappears 
(Fig. 3d). The content of ­Li2CO3 is much smaller than that 
of LiF in LiTFSI-DOL/DME, and the ­Li2CO3 is mostly 
distributed in the outermost SEI. Therefore, the content 
of LiF rises significantly and ­Li2CO3 disappears since the 
SEI in LHCE is dominated by ­TFSI- decomposition. This 
indicates that the addition of TME increased the content 
of LiF and reduced the proportion of loose ­Li2CO3. In this 
case, the anion ­(TFSI-) in LHCE first decomposes to form 
an SEI dominated by Li salt derivatives. Outer oligomers 
(around 286.8 eV) coming from the reduction of DME and 
DOL were measured (Fig. 3d). The outer oligomers lead to 
a more pliable SEI.36,38,39 The dense SEI layer composed 
of the LiF and the oligomer can impede the formation of 
Li dendrites, which has good adaptability to the volume 
expansion during ­Li+ 	
deposition and stripping, thus 
elevating the cycling performance of Li-S batteries.
Next, we analyzed the effect of TME on the “shuttle 
effect”. The fitted peak at the binding energy of 160.2 eV 
corresponds to the peak of ­Li2S, 40 while the two peaks at 
the binding energy of 161.6 eV and 162.8 eV correspond to 
the peak of ­Li2S2.40  For HCE, the content of ­Li2S and ­Li2S2 
on the Li surface is very large (Fig. 3e). After the addition 
of TME, the content of ­Li2S and ­Li2S2 on the Li surface for 
LHCE was significantly reduced (Fig. 3f). Since the reduc-
tion of free solvent in the solvated structure inhibited the 
dissolution of the polysulfides, the “shuttle effect” was sup-
pressed. In addition, the peak near the binding energy of 
166.6 eV corresponds to the substance of ­Li2SO3,40 and the 
peak at 169.5 eV corresponds to the -SO2-,40 which could

The Morphologies of Li Deposition
The morphologies were tested by using Li-Cu batteries 
(Fig. 4 a-f). The Li-deposition layer in LCE is relatively 
loose and unevenly distributed, and there are many small 
debris-like deposits among the Li deposition particles 
(Fig. 4a, b), which would destroy the cycling reversibility 
of the Li. In HCE, the Li deposition morphology is relatively 
dense, but there are many pores and strip-shaped dendrite 
protrusions (Fig. 4c, d). In contrast, the addition TME addi-
tives significantly changed the deposition morphology of 
the Li. The Li depositions in LHCE were closely packed and 
uniformly distributed, and Li dendrites can not be observed 
(Fig. 4e, f).
Combined with the SEI layer composition analysis, the 
weak Li-ion coordination (SSIPs) in the LCE caused a loose 
SEI which should be responsible for the unsatisfactory mor-
phology. In contrast, in HCE, increasing concentration could 
lead to a more tightly coordinated structure, forming denser 
SEI, which makes the deposition of Li more uniform and 
denser than that of LCE. After the addition of TME, a Li-ion 
coordination structure dominated by AGGs was formed in 
the LHCE, forming a more robust SEI than HCE and then 
facilitating the uniform deposition of Li ions through the 
most closely coordinated structure. In addition, the formed 
LHCE still effectively alleviates the uneven local current 
density caused by the concentration gradient, and restricts 
the preferential nucleation and growth of the Li in some

The Cycling Performance of Li‑S Batteries
First, 50 μL of electrolyte was dropped onto the surface of 
the separator to observe the wettability of the electrolyte 
(Fig. S5). HCE has a high viscosity, cannot spread, and has 
a large contact angle with the separator. In comparison, 
the LHCE obtained by adding the diluent TME has a very 
low viscosity, spreads out on the separator quickly, and has 
a smaller contact angle. At the same time, the electrolyte 
quickly penetrates the separator and infiltrates the cathode 
material. This shows that the addition of the diluent TME 
can greatly reduce the viscosity without reducing the effec-
tive Li salt concentration and increase the wettability. This 
promotes ion conduction in Li-S batteries.
The anode stability of LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME was then 
tested. The LHCE can keep stable under 4.5 V (Fig. S6). A 
Li-S battery was then assembled (Fig. 5a). The discharge 
capacity of the battery with HCE continued to decay 
(Fig. 5b). The capacity was only left with 463.69 mAh ­g–1 
after 400 cycles (Fig. S8). The capacity retention is just 
48.62% (Fig. S9) and the average CE is only 96.52%. This 
is because the higher viscosity and lower ionic conductance 
affect the utilization of active materials when the concen-
tration is higher. In contrast, as for the battery with LHCE, 
the capacity retention is significantly improved (Fig. 5c). 
After 400 cycles, the capacity still reaches 554.68 mAh ­g–1 
(Fig. S8), the capacity retention is as high as 60% after 400 
cycles (Fig. S9), and the average CE is 97.73%. This indi-
cates that with the addition of the diluent TME, the elec-
trolyte has a higher ionic conductivity and lower solubility 
of polysulfides, which makes the battery with LHCE have 
a higher active material utilization and capacity retention. 
Moreover, the Li metal anode avoids continuous erosion by 
polysulfides in the presence of a dense SEI layer. It means 
that the LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME system was able to protect 
Li metal anode and stabilize S cathode.
Conclusions
We have successfully prepared a LHCE LiTFSI-DME/DOL-
TME which can inhibit the “shuttle effect” of the cathode and 
build a robust SEI on the anode side to protect the Li metal. 
The reduction of free solvent in the Li-ion solvation sheath 
suppresses the dissolution of polysulfides. In addition, with 
increased content of anionic ­TFSI-, Li salt can be preferentially 
reduced and a LiF-rich SEI layer was achieved. This dense SEI
