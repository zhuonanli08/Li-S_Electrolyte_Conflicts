Quantum-mechanics-based molecular dynamics
simulations of the structure and performance
of sulfur-enriched Li3PS4 cathodes
Sergey I. Morozov,1 Boris V. Merinov,2,3,* Tridip Das,2 Sergey V. Zybin,2 Moon Young Yang,2
and William A. Goddard III2,*
SUMMARY
Sulfur-enriched Li3PS4 is a promising cathode for lithium-sulfur bat-
teries, but there is not yet clear structural information about this ma-
terial. Here, we use quantum-mechanics-based molecular dynamics
to predict the structures of the fully charged Li3PS4+5 and various
stages of discharge to nearly discharged Li3+9PS4+5 at 298 K. We
predict that the fully charged cathode has a distorted orthorhombic
structure similar to Li3PS4, but with additional S4 and S8 chains each
linked to an S atom of a PS4 anion. This leads to a density of 2.19
g/cm3 for this material. For the nearly discharged cathode, we ﬁnd
that the lithium atoms insert into all SS bonds, with none of the
aforementioned chains remaining, leading to a density of 1.71
g/cm3. We examined the discharge process as lithium ions from
the electrolyte diffuse into the cathode and react with the SS
chains to form Li2S. The predicted discharge curve agrees well
with the experiment.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are one of the most promising candidates for next-gen-
eration high-performance Li-batteries. However, several challenges must be
resolved for large-scale commercial applications of Li-S batteries. The most critical
are (1) large volume expansion as the sulfur-based cathode discharges; (2) the ‘‘shut-
tle effect’’ of lithium polysulﬁdes upon cycling, in which polysulﬁdes formed at the sul-
fur cathode dissolve in the liquid electrolyte and migrate into electrochemical cells,
leading to parasitic side reactions and degradation of the cell performance and a
short cycle-life; (3) reduced charge/discharge rates due to the low Li-ion conductivity
of the current cathode materials; and (4) applications using metallic lithium anodes to
improve electrochemical cell energy density lead to dendritic growth through the
liquid electrolyte during continuous cycling, which causes serious safety issues.
Incorporating solid-state materials into Li-S batteries has the potential to solve these
problems, which would constitute a breakthrough for energy storage devices. In this
regard, Li-containing sulﬁdes are of particular interest because they are attractive for
applications both as an electrolyte and as a cathode material.1–10
In a conventional Li-S battery, elemental sulfur (S8) in the sulfur-based cathode is
electrochemically reduced to long-chain soluble polysulﬁdes, Li2Sn (3 % n % 6),
and short-chain insoluble Li2S or Li2S2.11,12 The dissolution and migration of polysul-
ﬁde species (the shuttle effect) cause sulfur loss and cathode structure degradation,
leading to a short cycle lifetime.

Instead of S8, here we focus on modiﬁed sulfur-enriched compounds based on PS4
anions. In particular, Li3PS4+n has been shown to be a good candidate as the cathode
for all solid-state Li-S batteries (1) because the high Li-ion conductivities (104 to
105 S/cm) are comparable to those of lithium metal oxides and phosphates
(conventional cathode materials for Li-ion batteries), and (2) because of excellent
electrochemical reversibility during charge-discharge battery cycling.10
S8 reacts with lithium thiophosphate (Li3PS4) in tetrahydrofuran (THF) to form new
SS single bonds (Figure 110). The newly formed SS bonds can form chains that
link to the S atoms in PS4 groups giving rise to polysulﬁdophosphate materials
with the general formula Li3PS4+n, where n is the number of additional S. During
charge-discharge cycling, reversible electrochemical reactions involving adding or
removing Li to break or form SS bonded chains lead from the fully charged
Li3PS4+n to the fully discharged Li3+2nPS4+n cathode material and vice versa
(Figure 210).
Raman spectra conﬁrmed that the electrochemical insertion and removal of Li ions in
the S-enriched cathode is a reversible reaction of Li3PS4+5 to a mixture of Li2S and
Li3PS4 with the electrochemical scission and formation of SS bonds. It was reported
that Li3PS4+n remained stable over at least 300 cycles with an energy efﬁciency of
60% at room temperature and 83% at 60C.10 Important is that the voltage pro-
ﬁle does not have the 2 plateaus observed in conventional Li-S batteries. This indi-
cates that the electrochemical reactions in Li3PS4+n proceed through pathways
differently from the conventional sulfur-based cathode materials.
To provide a basis for advancing this PS4-based cathode technology, we used quan-
tum mechanics-based molecular dynamics (QM-MD) to predict the atomic level
structures and energetics for the fully charged Li3PS4+5 structure and the various
stages of discharge to the fully discharged Li3+10PS4+5 system. We report the evolu-
tion of the structure and potential during the charge and discharge processes.

The fully discharged (Li3+10PS4+5)8 system
To model the discharge process on the sulfur-enriched cathode, we started with a
slab based on the predicted (Li3PS4+5)8 bulk structure but added a vacuum that
could accommodate the addition of Li atoms. We then went through cycles of grad-
ually adding 8 Li on the top and bottom of this slab (Figure S2), equilibrating, and
then adding 8 more. We expected that the additional Li would react with additional
S, breaking the SS chains to form Li2S.
First, we placed 8 Li atoms on the top of the slab and perform energy minimization
of the system at 0 K, then added 8 more Li atoms on the bottom of the slab and
again optimized the system at 0 K, and so on up to 80 additional Li atoms. How-
ever, when the number of the additional Li atoms in the slab was 80, one of the
PS4 tetrahedra decomposed into PS3 and Li2S. After a series of unsuccessful at-
tempts to prevent decomposition of the PS4 tetrahedron, we decided to make
the process of the addition of Li at the last step smoother and more gradual.

Li-ion diffusivity and conductivity of the different cathode states
We performed 30 ns NVT MD simulations at 300 K to estimate Li-ion diffusivity and
conductivity in our predicted cathode structures of 3 different cathode states with
different Li concentrations: (1) fully charged with no excess Li in the formula unit,
Li3PS4+5; (2) partially discharged with 6 excess Li in the formula unit, Li3+6PS4+5;
and (3) nearly discharged with 9 excess Li in the formula unit, Li3+9PS4+5 (Table 2
and Figure S4). Experimental data on Li-ion conductivity are available only for the
fully charged case, Li3PS4+5.10 The corresponding value is 3.3 3 105 S/cm at 300
K, while the computationally predicted value is 13.4 3 105 S/cm at 300 K. We as-
sume that our predicted Li-ion conductivity is higher than the experimental because
the single crystal models were used in our simulations, whereas slurry, prepared by
dispersing Li3PS4+5, WVA-1500 carbon, and PVC binder in THF and then coated on a
b-Li3PS4 solid electrolyte pellet and dried under vacuum at 80C, was used in the
experiment.10 We also predict that the Li-ion conductivity in the S-enriched
Li3PS4+5 cathode linearly decreases with discharging and related Li-ion concentra-
tion increasing in the system (Figure S4).

The increase of the cathode ionic conduction by 2 orders of magnitude using these
PS4-based cathodes would signiﬁcantly improve the cathode performance and efﬁ-
ciency of the Li-S battery. Possibly, the ionic conductivity could be increased further
by doping these PS4-based sulﬁdes, with such cations as V and Mo to create more
vacancies for fast Li-ion diffusion.
Another possibility is to start with sulﬁdes that have higher Li-ion conductivity. For
instance, we could start with superionic conductors such as Li6(PS4)SCl, Li5(PS4)Cl2,
and Li7P3S11, which have conductivities of 7.1 3 103,15 6.5 3 102 (computationally
predicted16), and 1.7 3 102 S/cm,17 respectively.

ReaxFF reactive force ﬁeld to enable MD simulations at more realistic time scales of
20 ns and longer with spatial scales from 10 nm (100,000 atoms) to 30 nm (million
atoms). This would provide more realistic simulations of charge-discharge pro-
cesses in sulfur-enriched cathode materials and for predicting the structures and
properties of the electrode/electrolyte interface with sulﬁde-based electrodes
and electrolytes.

Computational methods: QM-MD
The QM-MD simulations (also called AIMD) were performed with the Vienna Ab Ini-
tio Simulation Package (VASP),18–20 which implements the projector augmented
wave (PAW) method for DFT. We applied the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) gener-
alized gradient approximation (GGA-PBE)21 with the D3 (Becke-Johnson) empirical
corrections22,23 for long-range pairwise van der Waals attractive forces (approxi-
mating the London dispersion).
The energy cutoff for the plane-wave basis expansion was 500 eV to achieve good
accuracy. We used the conjugate gradient algorithm to relax the atomic positions
of the optimized structure. The electronic convergence and geometry optimization
force criteria were set to 105 eV and 103 eV/A˚ , respectively.
For the bulk Brillouin zone integration, we applied a G-centered k-point mesh in all
QM-MD simulations within the supercell. Methfessel-Paxton order 1 smearing of
electronic occupations was used with a width of 0.2 eV in our simulations.
All simulations were spin unrestricted to permit the spin changes during the simulations
needed to ﬁnd the lowest barrier pathway in bond rearrangements of S atoms and to
ﬁnd the minimum energy structures during cell optimization and slab annealing.
Computational methods: Classical MD
To calculate diffusivity and conductivity of different cathode states, Li3PS4+5 (the fully
charged system), Li3+6PS4+5 (the partially discharged system), and Li3+9PS4+5 (the
nearly discharged system), we performed MD simulations with the Universal Force
Field (UFF)24 on the corresponding bulk structures, using LAMMPS.25 The UFF pa-
rameters for the van der Waals interaction and PS bond distance were used from
our previous work.22 We adjusted the SS bond distance parameter based on our
DFT calculated SS distance in Li3PS4+5. The UFF parameters were used for bond
angle and dihedral coefﬁcients (Table S11). The DDEC6 method26 was applied to
assign atomic charges calculated for the DFT minimized bulk structures. Then, we
built superstructures by tripling the respective cells along the z-direction (1 3 1 3
3) relative to the DFT minimized unit cells (Figure S3). These structures obtained
were minimized using the UFF. After velocity initializations at 10 K, the structures
were heated to 300 K and the 1 ns NPT MD simulations were carried out to remove
residual stress. An additional 20 ns NVT dynamics was required for Li3+9PS4+5 to
address pressure ﬂuctuations. Finally, a 30-ns NVT MD simulation was performed
on each structure at 300 K to predict the Li-ion diffusivity.
