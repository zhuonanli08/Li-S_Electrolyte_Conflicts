ABSTRACT: An electrochemical couple of lithium and sulfur
possesses the highest theoretical energy density (>2600 Wh/kg) at
the material level. However, disappointingly, it is out of place in
primary batteries due to its low accessible energy density at the cell
level (≤500 Wh/kg) and poor storage performance. Herein, a low-
density methyl tert-butyl ether was tailored for an ultralight electrolyte
(0.837 g/mL) with a protective encapsulation solvation structure
which reduced electrolyte weight (23.1%), increased the utilization of
capacity (38.1%), and simultaneously forfended self-discharge.
Furthermore, active fluorinated graphite partially replaced inactive
carbon to construct a hybrid sulfur-based cathode to bring the
potential energy density into full play. Our demonstrated pouch cell
achieved an incredible energy density of 661 Wh/kg with a negligible
self-discharge rate based on the above innovations. Our work is anticipated to provide a new direction to realize the practicality of
lithium−sulfur primary batteries.
■INTRODUCTION
As scientific explorations gradually move toward uncharted
territories, such as deep in the Earth’s core and outer space,
primary batteries with high energy density are urgently needed
to provide the energy supply in the unchargeable application
scenario.1 Among all the available electrochemical couples,
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries possess an extremely high
theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh/kg.2,3 However,
frustratingly, it failed for the primary battery due to the two
critical barriers below.4
The one hurdle is the low accessible energy density at the
cell level (≤500 Wh/kg5) because a high nonactive mass ratio
(>80%), including massive nonactive conductive carbon
additives and excessive electrolytes, is required to facilitate S
conversion and the sluggish solid−liquid−solid conversion
lowers the capacity utilization rate (<71%, S: <1200 mAh/g).6
The other hurdle is the poor energy storage performance
because the intermediates of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) are
soluble and quickly diffuse to corrode the Li anode, speeding
up the self-discharge. In detail, the self-discharge begins with
the slight dissolution of elemental S (9.957 mM in DME,7,8 for
example) from the cathode (S8s →S8sol). After that, the
solvated S will be transported to the anode by a concentration
gradient, reacting with Li to produce Li2Sx (S8sol + Li →
Li2Sx).9 The capacity of cells thus continuously fades during
resting.
Since these essentially interrelated issues occur in separate
locations (cathode surface, electrolyte bulk, and anode
surface), people used to treat them one-sidedly, such as by
adding catalytic materials,10−13 constructing a porous14−16 or
functional skeleton17−19 in the cathode, and fluorinating the
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)20 on the anode. However,
these chaotic methods are like robbing Peter to pay Paul. For
example, the functional skeleton may trap S species and thus
benefit the storage performance, but it becomes an extra
weight burden on the practical energy density. Therefore,
traditional methods cannot fully meet the requirements for the
practicality of Li−S primary batteries.
Given the bridging role of electrolytes in the entire battery
system, scientists recently chose to start with electrolytes to
solve the practical energy density problem and the self-
discharge problem step by step. Considering that the excessive
mass weight of Li−S batteries is mainly caused by the
electrolyte part (58.5 wt % vs 22.2 wt % in traditional lithium-
ion batteries),21,22 cutting down electrolyte weight has become
the most direct way to increase energy density. For this

purpose, reducing the electrolyte volume is the most frequently
used way. However, the porous cathode and the solid−liquid−
solid conversion mechanism of S have their intrinsic
requirement for the amount of electrolyte, indicating that
casually reducing volume will make the cathode insufficiently
infiltrated and deteriorate the capacity release, which becomes
counterproductive at last.23,24 Instead, the mass formula, m = ρ
× V, indicates that designing new electrolytes with lower
density can reduce the electrolyte weight more effectively when
reducing volume approaches marginal effectiveness.22,25
In this work, a single-oxygen chain-like ether, methyl tert-
butyl ether (MTBE), was proposed to construct the ultralight
electrolyte (UE, 0.4 m LiTFSI + 0.2 m LiNO3 in DME/MTBE
= 1/1 vol %, where m refers to mol/L) to realize high energy
density and good storage performance simultaneously.
Compared with the conventional electrolyte (CE), UE reduced
the electrolyte weight by 23.1% and boosted the capacity
output by 38.1% through its triple functions. Besides,
fluorinated graphite (CFx) was introduced to construct a
hybrid sulfur-based cathode (HC) to increase energy density
further. A 31-day resting experiment proved the superior
storage performance of our system. At last, a 300 mAh-level
pouch cell of our demonstrated system achieved an incredible
energy density of 661 Wh/kg with negligible self-discharge rate
after 1 week of resting, which strongly confirms the significance
of our work to the practicality of Li−S primary batteries.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Designing Ultralight Electrolyte by Introducing
MTBE. The conventional electrolyte for Li−S batteries
comprises 1 m lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) + 0.2 m LiNO3 in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)/
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) = 1/1 vol %. Considering that its good
compatibility with sulfur cathode has been demonstrated, we
prefer to design our ultralight electrolyte by the targeted
substitution of CE to lower the electrolyte density. The
sequence of major mass contribution in certain-volume CE is
DOL (41.9%) > DME (34.3%) > LiTFSI (22.7%), resulting
from the high density of the DOL (1.06 g/mL) and large
molecular weight of LiTFSI (287 g/mol) (Figure S1). As a
sacrificial protective agent for cycling,26 DOL is not necessary
in primary batteries. Directly removing DOL from CE can
reduce electrolyte density by 9.7% (0.983 vs 1.089 g/mL), but
that is insufficient. Considering the critical role of DME in S
conversion,27 we proposed introducing a superior solvent and
combining it with DME to achieve a fully ultralight electrolyte.
The design criteria for this solvent are possessing a lower
density than DME and good compatibility with LiTFSI. For
the convenience of the design process and cutting down the
electrolyte weight, the amount of LiTFSI has been reasonably
reduced to 0.4 m (Figure S2).28,29 The core of the entire
design process is reducing the solvent density by adjusting
functional groups (FGs) to reduce the molecular polarity. In
detail, the density of a solvent is determined by the
intermolecular force, which is closely related to the molecular
polarity (reflected by the dipole moment), while the molecular
polarity mainly originates from the functional groups. Herein,
we proposed to reduce the solvent density by sequentially
reducing the strength, content, and adjusting the structure of
FGs (Figure 1a, detailed information on the corresponding
solvents is listed in Table S1). Step 1 is to determine the type
of solvent with the lowest FG’s strength, and consequently, the
target area was narrowed down to ethers. For comparative
analysis, we chose typical solvents composed of a single
functional group and methyl groups for each solvent type,
including dimethyl sulfone (DMSO2) and dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO) for sulfones and sulfoxides, respectively; methyl
acetate (MA) for esters; dimethyl ether (DE) for ethers; and

these typical solvents, the sequence of FGs’ strength can be
concluded as sulfones or sulfoxides > esters > ethers > alkanes
(Figure 1b). Alkanes seem to be the most ideal. However, they
cannot dissolve lithium salts due to the excessively low
polarity,30 which violates the requirement that the targeted
solvent must be compatible with LiTFSI. Therefore, ethers
possess the acceptable lowest FG’s strength. Since higher
molecular weight will naturally lead to higher density, the
number of atoms in the backbone (all atoms except H) is
limited to <7 in the next design process. Step 2 determines the
FG’s content with the lowest polarity, and single-oxygen ethers
(C/O > 2) stand out.31 Two typical solvents with identical
structures except for oxygen content were selected for
comparison, including dimethyl acetal (DMA) with multiple
oxygens (C/O ≤2) and methyl sec-butyl ether (MSBE) with a
single oxygen (C/O > 2). Consequently, MSBE possesses a
lower dipole moment, indicating that less oxygen content leads
to a lower molecular polarity (Figure 1b). Step 3 is to
determine the FG’s structure (chain-like or ring-like) with the
lowest polarity, and consequently, single-oxygen chain-like
ethers win out. The ring-like structure can provide a better-
aligned dipole moment and thus get higher polarity,32 which
was verified by the higher dipole moment of tetrahydrofuran
(THF) than that of methyl n-propyl ether (MPE), where these
two solvents are identical except for a structure difference
(Figure 1b). The above theoretical analysis shows that single-
oxygen chain-like ethers are the best candidates for a suitable
low-density solvent. For further practical verification of the
above theory, we classified and statistically analyzed the
common solvent densities, and the results agreed well with our
theory (Figure 1c,d). After that, we selected 4 single-oxygen
chain-like ethers based on appropriate boiling point and
solubility criteria, including MPE, methyl n-butyl ether (MBE),
MSBE, and MTBE. Since these solvents have a similar density
(0.73−0.74 g/mL), the electrochemical test made the final
choice. It was found that the half cells with MTBE exhibited
the best capacity release (Figure 1e), and thus, MTBE (0.74 g/
mL) became our final choice. At last, the composition of UE
was established as 0.4 m LiTFSI + 0.2 m LiNO3 in DME/
MTBE = 1/1 vol %.
Compared to CE, UE dramatically reduces the density of
electrolyte by 23.1% (0.837 vs 1.089 g/mL) (Figure 2a).
Moreover, with UE, the conventional cathode (CC) can
provide a capacity of 1113 mAh/g, increased by 38.1%
compared to that of 806 mAh/g with CE (Figure 2b). This
indicates that our weight reduction process did not sacrifice
electrochemical performance, and instead dramatically im-
proved it, which can lead to double enhancement to energy
density. The mechanism of such a capacity increase has been
thoroughly studied. In Li−S batteries, Q1 refers to the 2.3 V
platform, at which the production of soluble LiPSs mainly
occurs. Q2 refers to the 2.1 V platform, which mainly reflects
the transition from soluble LiPSs to solid Li2S2 and Li2S. Since
the liquid−solid conversion is much more sluggish than the
solid−liquid conversion,33 higher Q2/Q1 indicates better
electrochemical performance. Compared to the CE system,
the UE system exhibits a lower Q1 (232 vs 304 mAh/g) and a
higher Q2 (881 vs 502 mAh/g) (Figure 2b). The much higher
Q2/Q1 (3.8 vs 1.65) and overall discharge capacity have fully
revealed the advantages of UE, which can be summarized into
three aspects including kinetics enhancement, diffusion
inhibition, and protective encapsulation solvation structure.
In the cathode region, the higher peak current at Q2 in linear
sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves confirms that UE can
provide enhanced reaction kinetics for the liquid−solid
conversion (Figure S3), derived from the low viscosity and
good conductivity. With almost half the viscosity of CE (1.28
mPa·s at 25 °C), UE (0.64 mPa·s) can sufficiently wet the
porous cathode (Figure 2c). Besides, UE possesses a lower
activation energy of 5.48 kJ/mol (vs 9.35 kJ/mol of CE),
which indicates lower energy barriers for ion transfer (Figure
2d). The above advantages can become more significant when
the discharge begins (Figure 2e). Although the ionic
conductivity of pure CE (11.8 mS/cm at 25 °C) is higher
than that of pure UE (3.34 mS/cm), in the actual discharge
process with dissolved LiPSs, such a difference becomes very
slight (∼5.5 vs ∼2.5 mS/cm). Moreover, after dissolving Li2S8,

of CE was further increased to 28.3 mPa·s. This reflects that, at
the beginning of Q2, CE has already been in a highly viscous

proves UE can inhibit the diffusion of LiPSs from cathode to
anode. With Li2S8 saturated, DME/MTBE (1/1 vol %) shows
lighter colors than DME/DOL (1/1 vol %), indicating less
dissolved Li2S8 (Figure 2f). For a single solvent, both the
saturated DME and DOL exhibit dark brown. In contrast,
MTBE exhibits light yellow, indicating the intrinsic low LiPSs
solubility of MTBE (Figure 2f). Therefore, it is exactly the
introduction of MTBE that makes UE capable of inhibiting the
diffusion of LiPSs.
On the anode region, UE can prevent the corrosion of Li by
a protective encapsulation solvation structure for LiPSs. In the
cyclic voltammetry (CV) test, the significant current of DME/
DOL near 0 V indicates its instability toward Li, while DME/
MTBE shows much lower current (Figure 3a).35,36 Further
research has found that regardless of whether DME or DOL is
unstable to Li. At the same time, MTBE exhibits excellent
stability (Figure 3b). Therefore, in UE, MTBE can somehow
shield DME to elevate the overall stability toward Li. Nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) further explored the solvation
structure (Figure 3c). With saturated LiPSs, the 17O spectral
shift of DME is up to −0.2 ppm, while those of DOL and
MTBE are as small as −0.03 and −0.01 ppm, respectively.
Therefore, DME is strongly bonded to Li+ and thus constitutes
the inner solvation shell. The bonding of DOL and MTBE to
Li+ is weaker, indicating that they mainly accumulate in the
outer layer. Consistently, in the 7Li test, the chemical shifts of
DME, DOL, and MTBE are 0.47, 1.24, and 1.32 ppm,
respectively (Figures S4 and S5). This result further confirms
the strongest interaction between Li+ and DME, and thus
DME occupies the inner solvation shell, while DOL or MTBE
occupies the outer solvation shell. In conclusion, the solvation
structure in UE can be depicted as LiPSs-reactive DME-
nonreactive MTBE, while that in CE can be depicted as LiPSs-
reactive DME-reactive DOL. The above analysis was
confirmed through radial distribution functions (RDFs)
around S8
2−(Figure 3d,e). For both CE and UE, incompletely
dissociated Li2S8 occupies the central position, and the first
solvation shell, mainly composed of DME, occurs at 0.41 nm.
For CE, the second solvation shell, mainly composed of DOL,
occurs at 0.67 nm, while that in UE, mainly composed of
MTBE, occurs at 0.71 nm. Although CE and UE have similar
double-shell solvation structures, both layers of CE are
vulnerable to Li, and the internal LiPSs can be easily exposed.
In contrast, the stable MTBE at the outer layer of UE
eliminates the possibility of contact between the internal LiPSs
and Li, thus capable of cutting off the corrosion reaction. Such
a protective effect by the encapsulation solvation structure in

In conclusion, the enhanced reaction kinetics brings more
harvested capacity. At the same time, the low solubility and
encapsulation solvation structure jointly result in less lost
capacity (Figure 3f). Thus, UE is naturally capable of enabling
a higher energy density. Besides, the storage performance has
also been improved by UE. In detail, S species can hardly get
dissolved and move to the anode, and even if it does happen,
these S species can hardly react with Li with the protection of
the encapsulation solvation structure. As a result, UE can help
achieve high energy density and good storage performance
simultaneously and thus is customized for Li−S primary
batteries.
Designing a Hybrid Sulfur-Based Cathode by In-
troducing CFx. Besides the electrolyte, the exorbitant carbon
content in the S cathode (27 wt % vs 4 wt % in traditional
lithium-ion batteries) is another reason for the unsatisfactory
practical energy density (Figure S10). Reducing the carbon
content without affecting electronic conductivity is thus
crucial. Herein, we propose to use active CFx to partially
replace the commonly used unactive Ketjen Black (KB) while
the electronic conductivity is guaranteed by the preferentially
formed graphite (CFx + Li →C + xLiF at 2.4 V > Q1 at 2.3 V)
(Figure S11).37 In addition, small amounts of graphene (G)
and carbon nanotubes (CNT) were used to realize a more
efficient multidimensional conductive network (Figure
S12).38,39 To explore the optimal amount of CFx addition,
we compared the estimated energy density at different CFx
contents, which was obtained by using the specific capacity
data collected under E/S = 6 μL/mg in coin cells to deduce the
situation of E/S = 3 μL/mg in pouch cells (Figure S13). When
introducing 25 wt % CFx, the cell could reach the maximum
estimated energy density of 608 Wh/kg (Figure 4a). Thus, the
optimum CFx proportion was settled as 25 wt %, and HC was
determined as (S/KB/CFx = 60/15/25 wt %)/(G/CNT = 1/1
wt %)/(CMC/SBR = 1/1 wt %) = 90/5/5 wt %.

The discharge process of HC starts with CFx, which can be
easily distinguished by the newly emerged LiF at 2.4 V (Figure
4b, Figures S11 and S12). When coming to the Q1 platform at
2.3 V, 84% capacity of CFx has already been released,
indicating the reaction of CFx and S can be regarded as
separate in terms of time (Figure S11 and S12), which
confirms the functional feasibility of using CFx to replace KB.
By reducing porous KB with super high specific surface area,33
not only the demand for electrolyte is reduced, but also the
loading of S can easily break through 6 mg/cm2 (vs ≤3 mg/
cm2 in traditional S-KB cathode40) (Figure S14). Meanwhile,
after deducting the contribution from CFx (308 mAh/g), the
specific capacity provided by S in HC is 1263 mAh/g, which is
even higher than that of 1113 mAh/g in CC (Figure 4c),
indicating an elevated discharge performance of S (Figures
S15−S17). As a result, compared to CC (3.34 mAh/cm2), HC
(9.43 mAh/cm2) enables an 182% increase in the areal
capacity of the cathode without addition of extra electrolyte
burden (Figure 4d), which naturally leads to higher energy
density.
Evaluating Storage Performance. For primary batteries,
storage performance is another critical factor, besides energy
density. Herein, we used three indexes, including open circuit
voltage (OCV) change rate, capacity decay rate, and solid
electrolyte interface resistance (RSEI), to demonstrate the
excellent storage performance of our system (HC + UE,
denoted as OS). Figure 5a depicts the change in the OCV with
time. For OS, the OCV of the fresh cell was 3.05 V, which
reached the inflection point of 2.87 V after resting for 17 h.
This value is close to but still higher than the standard Li−S
batteries’ OCV of 2.8 V, so this decrease is mainly due to the
further infiltration and distribution of electrolyte in the
cathode. After that, the OCV fluctuated to 2.9 V on the 31st
day. The rate of change of the OCV in the whole process is
only 4.68%. For the conventional system (CC + CE, denoted
as CS), the OCV of the fresh cell was 2.97 V and then reached
an inflection point of 2.38 V after 55 h. This value is
significantly lower than 2.8 V and close to the Q1 platform of
2.3 V, indicating a self-discharge reaction.41 After that, the
OCV was stable until 2.37 V on the 31st day, indicating that
the self-discharge reaction was continuous inside the battery.
After 31 days of rest, the OCV of CS decreased by 20.23%,
which is far greater than 4.68% of OS. Figure 5b exhibits the
capacity change with time. For the accuracy of experiments, we
used three identical cells as one group to explore the change in
both the range and average capacity (Figures S18 and S19).
For CS, huge performance gaps exist between cells, even in the
same group, due to the slight dissolution of S from the
cathode. Moreover, the average capacity release rapidly faded
during resting. The total capacity decay rate is about 23%. By
contrast, for OS, the self-discharge seems to be very slight. The
data of each group exhibit excellent repeatability, and the total
capacity decay rate is close to 0%. Figure 5c shows the
impedance change with time, where the equivalent circuit
diagram for the simulation is shown in Figure S20.42,43 For OS,
the impedance kept increasing throughout the 31 days. It is
due to the thickening of SEI derived from the continuous
decomposition of lithium salts and solvents.44 The increasing
resultants, such as LiF (3 × 10−9 S/cm) and lithium carbonate
(≈10−8 S/cm, including both Li2CO3 and RCO2Li),45,46
possess poor ionic conductivity and thus lead to the increase
of RSEI. For CS, the impedance exhibits a continuously
decreasing trend, which indicates the existence of self-
discharge. The process can be described as follows: When
immersed in the electrolyte, the pristine film on the Li surface
(usually comprised of Li2O, LiOH, and Li2CO3) will be
partially dissolved.47 The highly active LiPSs produced by the
dissolution of cathode contact with Li metal and process the
corrosion reaction, which is faster than the reduction of any
salt or solvent.48 As a result, compact SEI can never be built
under the continuous attack of LiPSs, and thus, RSEI keeps
decreasing. In conclusion, the SEI in OS gets strengthened,
while that in CS gets destroyed. All three experiments indicate
that the self-discharge in CS starts at the early period within
31-day resting. In contrast, OS exhibits stability throughout the

Assessing Performance under Pouch Cell Conditions.
Pouch cells were assembled to simulate further practical
conditions (Figure S21 and Table S2). To evaluate the storage
performance, two 150 mAh-level pouch cells with similar active
material (AM) loading (9.25 vs 9.42 mg/cm2) were tested on
the day of assembly and after 1 week of rest, respectively
(Figure 6a, Tables S3 and S4). The specific capacity loss after
resting is almost negligible. A 300 mAh-level pouch cell with a
double-side coated cathode was assembled to unleash energy
density potential fully. With narrowed dead space, E/S was
successfully compressed to 2.5 μL/mg (Figure S22). At last,
this pouch cell exhibits an incredible energy density of 661
Wh/kg (Figure 6b,c and Table S5). Both active S and CFx
have approached their theoretical specific capacities. It is
reasonable to predict that OS will exhibit much higher energy
density when assembled as Ah-level pouch cells.
■CONCLUSION
An ultralight electrolyte was designed by logical analysis
accompanied by experimental verifications to enhance reaction
kinetics, inhibit LiPSs diffusion, and prevent lithium corrosion
via a protective encapsulation solvation structure. Subse-
quently, by the introduction of CFx, the capacity output of
the cathode was further elevated. With the above innovations,
superior storage performance was identified through a 31-day
resting experiment. Finally, the demonstrated pouch cell
exhibits an incredible energy density of 661 Wh/kg and a
negligible self-discharge rate. Our work is anticipated to
provide a new direction to realize the practicality of Li−S
primary batteries.
