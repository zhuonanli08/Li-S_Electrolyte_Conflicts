A B S T R A C T
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN), a promising organic cathode with high specific capacity and cycling 
reversibility, is yet constrained by polysulfide dissolution and shuttling in conventional ether-based electrolytes. 
Herein, we adopt the asymmetric ether 1,2-dimethoxypropane (DMP) as solvating solvent. The extra methyl 
group induces steric hindrance, endowing weaker solvating ability than symmetric ether. Furthermore, the 
flame-retardant ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN) is for the first time incorporated into the SPAN 
battery system as cosolvent. PFPN exhibits ultra-weak coordination with Li+, which facilitates the formation of 
an anion-rich solvation structure and the construction of robust bidirectional electrode-electrolyte interphases 
with inorganic-rich species. Consequently, polysulfides dissolution is effectively suppressed. Leveraging the 
tailored electrolyte, the Li||SPAN cell exhibited exceptional capacity retention of 93.5% after 1200 cycles at 1C. 
Notably, despite the high SPAN loading (~5 mg cm−2), low N/P ratio (~2.8), and lean electrolyte usage (~8 μL 
mgSPAN
−1
), the cells demonstrated stable cycling at both room temperature and 60 ◦C. This work provides a feasible 
approach for designing high-performance and safe electrolytes for practical Li-SPAN batteries.
1. Introduction
The breakthrough in next-generation energy storage technologies is 
of decisive significance for mitigating the global climate crisis. Although 
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have achieved high commercialization and 
established a mature industrial system, their low energy density make it 
difficult to satisfy the requirement for high specific energy in next- 
generation new energy vehicles, power grid energy storage, and other 
fields [1–3]. Lithium‑sulfur batteries (LSBs) have been regarded as 
promising candidates for next-generation energy storage systems, owing 
to the high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh⋅kg−1, a merit derived 
from the sulfur cathode's exceptional theoretical specific capacity of 
1675 mAh⋅g−1. The natural abundance, low cost, and eco-friendliness of 
sulfur elements give it significant advantages in sustainable develop­
ment compared to traditional metal oxide cathodes. However, the 
durability of LSBs is severely limited by the “shuttle effect” of lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs), which can corrode lithium anode and cause loss of 
active sulfur species [4,5].
The sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathode material has been 
proposed as an alternative to traditional sulfur cathode, aiming to 
address the critical issue of polysulfide shuttling [6]. With the 
advancement of research, the chemical structure of SPAN has been 
gradually clarified. Yu's group confirmed the existence of covalent C–S 
and S–S bonds through investigations [7]. Subsequently, Zhang's group 
further proposed that sulfur is covalently anchored to the conjugate 
carbon skeleton composed of pyridine rings in the form of short-chain 
sulfur, a structure that can effectively suppress the dissolution of poly­
sulfides [8]. Beyond optimizing synthesis parameters [7,9], subsequent 
research has explored various directions including combining with 
conductive carbon materials [10,11], doping strategies [12,13], and 
electrolyte engineering [14,15]. Among these, electrolyte engineering is 
an effective strategy that achieves two goals at once, as it can regulate 
the performance of both the cathode and anode interfaces simulta­
neously. Carbonate electrolytes can form a favorable cathode-electrolyte 
interface (CEI) on the SPAN surface, effectively avoiding the generation 
of LiPSs and achieving reversible solid-solid conversion. However, the 
high reactivity with lithium metal anode severely limits the long-term 
cycling stability, and there may be safety hazards associated with

To compensate the shortcomings of ether electrolytes, researchers 
have investigated a series of high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) and 
localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) [19–23]. Compared 
to traditional ether electrolytes, these electrolytes have a unique sol­
vation structure. The participation of more anions forms a solvation 
structure dominated by contact ion pairs (CIPs) and ion aggregates 
(AGGs), which helps to form a robust electrode-electrolyte interphase 
layer to suppress the dissolution of polysulfides. In addition, the solva­
tion ability of the electrolyte acts as a critical driver in the solubility of 
polysulfides. Additionally, a series of molecular design methods based 
on DME molecular structure to construct weakly solvated electrolytes 
have been proposed, including reducing ether oxygen bonds, extending 
carbon chains, fluorination, or increasing steric hindrance through 
methylation/cyclization [15,24–27]. This approach can directly reduce 
the polysulfides dissolution in electrolyte, suppress the shuttle effect, 
and effectively alleviate the irreversible loss of active materials and the 
corrosion of metal anodes. Meanwhile, this weakly solvated electrolyte 
can also form an electrode-electrolyte interphase layer rich in anions, 
similar to HCEs and LHCEs [25,28].

lyte. Using 1, 2-dimethoxypropane (DMP) as a weak-coordinating sol­
vent, its extra methyl group (compared to conventional DME solvent) 
reduces α-H content and enhances steric hindrance, thus improving the 
electrolyte's oxidation resistance and weakening solvent-Li+ coordina­
tion.

2. Results and discussion
Ether-based electrolytes are widely used in Li-SPAN batteries owing 
to their favorable interfacial passivation efficacy on lithium metal an­
odes, with DME serving as a typical solvent. However, the high molec­
ular polarity of DME leads to severe polysulfides dissolution, causing 
rapid capacity decay [16]. Given that the configuration of solvent 
molecules influences their coordination ability, we introduce methyl 
group into DME molecules to synthesize the weakened solvating solvent 
(DMP). The weakly electron-donating methyl group increases the 
overall electron cloud density of DMP molecule, while its steric hin­
drance attenuates solvation ability and reduces polysulfide solubility 
[29]. As a well-recognized flame retardant, PFPN exhibits excellent 
flame retardancy (Fig. S1). The fluorinated cyclotriphosphazene ring in 
PFPN not only participates in constructing the electrode-electrolyte 
interface but also exerts ultra-weak solvation ability due to its signifi­
cant steric hindrance. Consequently, the electrolyte system (denoted as 
DMP-PFPN electrolyte) composed of DMP and PFPN—two solvents with 
distinct solvation capacities—not only ensures appropriate lithium salt 
solubility but also significantly inhibits polysulfide dissolution. More­
over, this designed electrolyte system addresses the flammability issue 
inherent in ether-based electrolytes (Fig. 1a, b).
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were applied to inves­
tigate three solvent molecules for the surface electrostatic potential 
(ESP) distribution and binding energy with Li+ (Fig. 1c). DME and DMP 
molecules exhibited more negative minimum ESP values and the 
stronger binding energy with Li+, verifying their effective solvation 
ability for electrolyte salts. In contrast, PFPN displayed a significantly 
higher minimum ESP and reduced binding energy with Li+, confirming 
its weak interaction with Li+. Solubility experiments further validated 
this conclusion (Fig. S2): LiFSI has limited solubility in PFPN, further 
confirming the extremely weak solvation ability of PFPN. Additionally, 
the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied 
molecular orbital (HOMO) of various solvent molecules were calculated 
to evaluate their reduction and oxidation potentials (Fig. 1d) [30]. The 
LUMO and HOMO energy levels of DMP molecules were comparable to 
those of DME, indicating that DMP maintains similar stability toward 
lithium metal anode. Notably, PFPN exhibited a substantially lower 
LUMO energy level, suggesting preferential reduction at the anode to 
construct an inorganic-rich SEI layer. Concurrently, the low HOMO 
energy level of PFPN enhanced the oxidation resistance of the electro­
lyte system, as corroborated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) results 
(Fig. S3).
Specifically, we dissolved 2 M LiFSI in DME as the base electrolyte 
(denoted as DME). And to validate the effectiveness of the molecular 
chain design, 2 M LiFSI was dissolved in DMP (denoted as DMP). To 
further optimize the Li+ solvation structure, we introduced PFPN with 
high steric hindrance and ultra-weak coordination ability as a cosolvent 
into the DMP electrolyte to prepared 2 M LiFSI in DMP-PFPN with 
different volume ratios. With the increase of PFPN, a solvent with ultra- 
weak solvating ability, the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte gradually 
decreases (Fig. S4). However, accompanied by the transformation of the 
solvation structure, the system forms a more favorable microenviron­
ment for Li+ migration, exhibiting a higher Li+ transference number 
(0.72), and thereby improving the interfacial reaction kinetics (Fig. S5). 
By combining with the cycling performance of Li||SPAN cells (Fig. S6), 
we finally identified the optimal volume ratio of DMP:PFPN as 3:7, and 
this system is named the DMP-PFPN electrolyte. The solvation behavior 
of various electrolytes was elucidated by Raman spectra. As presented in 
Fig. 2a, the S-N-S stretching vibrations of FSI−anions shifted from 721 
cm−1 in DME electrolyte to 726 cm−1 in DMP electrolyte, owning to the 
enhanced Li+-FSI−interactions within the solvation structures. In DMP- 
PFPN electrolyte, the peak of P=N-P in PFPN overlaps with that of S-N-S 
in FSI−, thus the ratio of various solvation structures cannot be quanti­
tatively analyzed [31]. Drawing from the precedent set by Lv and col­
leagues [32], the 1190–1250 cm−1 region, corresponding to the FSI−
anion's O=S=O stretch, was selected for quantitative solvation structure 
analysis (Fig. 2b, c). Concretely, the DME electrolyte exhibits a solvation 
structure primarily composed of solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs, 
57.9%), attributed to the strong coordinating propensity of DME solvent 
molecule. In contrast, the DMP electrolyte demonstrates diminished 
SSIPs proportion (46.4%) with concomitant increased contact ion pairs 
(CIPs, 35.5%), suggesting that the incorporation of methyl groups en­
hances steric hindrance of solvent, thus reducing the coordination 
ability between the solvent and Li+, and attracting more anions entering 
the primary solvation sheath. Notably, the introduction of PFPN induces 
a further blue shift, accompanied by the significant increased aggregates 
(AGGs, 59.4%). This indicates the existence of PFPN strengthens the 
coordination between Li+ and FSI−. The DMP-PFPN electrolyte with 
anion-reinforced solvation structure has improved thermodynamic 
stability.

surrounding FSI−is reduced, corresponding to a more positive chemical

surroundings of solvents (Fig. 2f). Compared to pure DMP, the intro­
duction of LiFSI leads to a downfield chemical shift of DMP molecule, 
which results from the interaction between Li+ and the electronegative 
oxygen atoms in DMP molecules and the electron-withdrawing nature of 
Li+, resulting in the reduced shielding effect on proton of DMP molecule 
[35]. With the introduction of PFPN, the free DMP molecules decrease, 
while the coordination with Li+ is strengthened. This amplifies 
deshielding effect on proton of DMP molecule, leading to a further 
downfield shift of the chemical shift [36]. To verify whether PFPN is 
involved in the coordination of Li+, we conducted NMR tests on the 
solutions before and after the addition of lithium salts. As shown in 
Fig. S7, the 17O NMR spectrum and 1H NMR spectrum of PFPN are 
presented respectively. Compared with the pure DMP + PFPN solvent, 
the chemical shifts of the relevant resonance peaks of PFPN in the DMP- 
PFPN electrolyte shift downfield slightly, which is attributed to the weak 
coordination between PFPN and Li+. This interaction causes a decrease 
in the electron cloud density around the PFPN molecule, resulting in a 
deshielding effect.
To further reveal the coordination behavior of Li+, molecular dy­
namics (MD) simulations were performed on three electrolyte systems. 
The radial distribution function (RDF) and coordination number 
(Fig. 2g-i) show the distribution of solvent molecules and anions around 
Li+ in the electrolyte systems. In DME electrolyte, the coordination 
number of DME in the first solvation sheath is higher than that of FSI−, 
stemming from the strong chelation between DME and Li+. While, the 
steric hindrance of DMP weakens its coordination with Li+, resulting in 
more FSI−entering the first solvation sheath. When PFPN with large 
steric hindrance and ultra-weak solvation ability is introduced as a co 
solvent, the Li+- FSI−interaction is significantly enhanced, resulting in 
an AGG-dominated solvation structure. This solvation structure is 
conducive to the generation of an inorganic-rich interfacial layer on the 
electrode surfaces, which has been evidenced to improve the cycling 
durability of batteries. The snapshots of the corresponding three elec­
trolyte systems are shown in Fig. 2j-i.

To further assess the practical performance of the electrolytes, the 
cycling performance of Li||SPAN full cells coupled with different elec­
trolytes was tested under practical operating conditions. Specifically, a 
high-loading (4.8 mg cm−2) SPAN cathode was matched with a 50-μm 
lithium foil (Fig. 3f). The cells were first activated with one cycle at 0.1C, 
followed by cycling tests at 0.2C. The full cell with DME electrolyte 
exhibits an initial discharge capacity of 1470.4 mAh g−1, higher than 
that of cells using DMP electrolyte (1428.5 mAh g−1) and DMP-PFPN 
electrolyte (1375.6 mAh g−1). However, its capacity decays rapidly 
during subsequent cycling, with only 24.4% capacity retention after 50 
cycles. This poor cycling performance is ascribed to severe polysulfide 
dissolution and poor lithium cycling reversibility. In contrast, DMP 
electrolyte showed improved cycling stability with a capacity retention 
rate of 58.7% after 50 cycles. While DMP-PFPN electrolyte maintains 
high stability during cycling, achieving an 83.5% capacity retention rate 
and an average coulombic efficiency of 99.9% after 150 cycles. This 
outstanding performance is attributed to the stabilization of SPAN 
cathode as well as lithium metal anode in DMP-PFPN electrolyte, 
reducing interfacial side reactions and enhancing active material utili­
zation. Additionally, the charge-discharge curves at the 30th cycle 
reveal that the cell using DMP-PFPN electrolyte exhibits significantly 
lower polarization (Fig. 3g). Additionally, the high boiling point of PFPN 
contributes to the high-temperature operation of DMP-PFPN electrolyte. 
As depicted in Fig. 3h, the Li||SPAN full cell still maintains 89% reten­
tion after 100 cycles at 60 ◦C under high loading and lean electrolyte 
condition.
Furthermore, cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were 
employed to obtain more information about electrochemical conversion 
behaviors of SPAN cathode materials in various electrolytes. The Li|| 
SPAN half cells were tested for five cycles with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 
(Fig. S12). In the DME electrolyte, except for typical solid-solid con­
version reactions, all cycles exhibit significant solid-liquid conversion 
peaks. In detail, two reduction peaks at ~2.37 V and ~ 2.02 V, and one 
oxidation peak at ~2.48 V, correspond to the transformation of sulfur to 
soluble polysulfides and the opposite conversion, respectively. In 
contrast, DMP solvent has a weaker solvation ability, resulting in less 
dissolution of polysulfides, and the solid-liquid conversion peaks man­
ifest after the third cycle. Furthermore, in the DMP-PFPN electrolyte, 
only the redox peaks of solid-solid conversion can be observed. This 
further proves that the electrolyte with an anion-dominated solvation 
structure can effectively suppress the shuttle effect of polysulfides. It is 
worth noting that the lithiation reaction of SPAN involves a cascade 
process of multi-electron transfer from the initial state to the fully 
lithiated state. In the solid-solid phase transition mechanism, each 
electron transfer step corresponds to a different voltage. In the DME 
electrolyte, the kinetic rate of the intermediate electron transfer step is 
insufficient, resulting in the overlap of multiple voltage platforms 
(convolution effect), which is manifested as the appearance of only a 
single merged solid-solid redox peak with increasing cycle numbers. In 
contrast, two discrete cathodic peaks appear in the DMP and DMP-PFPN 
electrolytes, which respectively correspond to the stepwise lithiation 
process of SPAN. This proves that the unique solvation structure of the 
DMP and DMP-PFPN electrolytes accelerates the conversion kinetics of 
SPAN. The reaction kinetics was further probed in detail through CV 
tests within the scan rate range of 0.1–0.5 mV s−1 (Fig. 4a-c and

In order to investigate the interfacial electrochemical evolution 
process of Li||SPAN batteries during discharge and charge in different 
electrolytes, we selected twelve states of charge (SOC) points from the 
initial discharge-charge curve for electrochemical impedance spectros­
copy (EIS) testing, and quantitatively deconvolved into relaxation time 
distribution (DRT) plots (Fig. 4f and Fig. S14). As shown in the Fig. 4g-i, 
the resistance within the distribution range of 10−1-100 s belongs to the 
polysulfides diffusion [38]. DME electrolyte exhibits the highest in­
tensity of relaxation time, which is associated with the dissolution and 
diffusion of polysulfides in DME electrolyte. The resistance within the 
distribution range of 10−5–10−3 s is related to the electrode/electrolyte 
interphase [39]. The interphase in DME electrolyte is unstable owing to 
the high solubility and shuttle of polysulfides. Therefore, the intensity of 
relaxation time within this range in DME electrolyte is greater than that 
of DMP and DMP-PFPN electrolytes, and fluctuates greatly with the 
evolution of SOC. The resistance within the distribution range of 
10−3–10−1 s reveals the positive electrode charge transfer kinetics [40]. 
The superior kinetics was obtained in DMP-PFPN electrolyte. This can be 
ascribed to the thin, uniform, and inorganic-rich CEI layer [41]. The 
resistance within the distribution range of 100–101 s is related to ion 
diffusion in the electrolyte [38]. In the early stages of the discharge 
process, the impedance of DMP-PFPN electrolyte is relatively higher 
compared to DME and DMP electrolytes, which is attributed to the weak 
solvation ability and slow ion diffusion. However, the viscosity of the 
electrolytes increases alongside the generation and subsequent dissolu­
tion of polysulfides in DME and DMP electrolytes, resulting in a more 
significant increase in impedance during the later charging process. The 
evolution of electrochemical impedance during the long-term cycling 
was also monitored. As shown in Fig. S15 and 16, the impedance of Li|| 
SPAN cells with the DMP-PFPN electrolyte gradually decreases with 
increasing cycles. Compared with the DME electrolyte and DMP elec­
trolyte systems, Li||SPAN cells with the DMP-PFPN electrolyte main­
tains significantly smaller electrode-electrolyte interface impedance 
(RSEI) and charge transfer resistance (Rct) throughout the entire cycling 
process. In contrast, Li||SPAN cells using the DME electrolyte and DMP 
electrolyte exhibit significant impedance fluctuations during cycling. 
This result indicates that Li||SPAN cell in the DMP-PFPN electrolyte has 
a more stable electrode-electrolyte interface and faster reaction kinetics.

self-discharge with CEs of 90.14% and 97.99%, corresponding to 2.0 and 
1.5 V, respectively. This demonstrates the relatively severe dissolution 
of polysulfides in the DME electrolyte. In comparison, in the weakly 
solvating DMP electrolyte, the self-discharge behavior is significantly 
impeded, resulting in higher CEs (97.73% and 98.15%). Obviously, the 
DMP-PFPN electrolyte exhibits higher CEs, even slightly higher than 
100%. This not only indicates that no self-discharge behavior occurred 
in the electrolyte, but also means that when the battery is idle in the 
electrolyte, the residual dead SPAN will be reactivated, leading to 
abnormal performance of CE slightly higher than 100% [24,26,42]. This 
phenomenon is similar to the capacity recovery mechanism during the 
discharged state calendar aging process, which promotes the recon­
nection of isolated lithium by dissolving residual SEI shells, thereby 
activating dead lithium and achieving capacity recovery [43].

demonstrated in Fig. 5a, transmission electron microscope (TEM) was 
employed to characterize the CEI formed on the SPAN cathodes after 
cycling in various electrolytes for 50 cycles. The CEI layers formed in 
DME and DMP electrolytes are thick and uneven. Whereas DMP-PFPN

To further elucidate the interfacial mechanism of the CEI layer, a 
compositional analysis of the CEI layer was performed. X-ray photo­
electron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the SPAN cathode was applied to 
anatomize the composition of CEI layer. In the C 1 s spectrum (Fig. S18), 
the significant enhancement of organic C–O (~286.5 eV), C––O 
(~288.6 eV) and organometallic Li–C (~283.8 eV) species in DMP- 
PFPN electrolyte indicates that the interfacial layer induced by this 
electrolyte has both electronic insulation and ionic conductivity char­
acteristics [26,44]. In the F 1 s spectrum (Fig. 5b), the inorganic 
component LiF (~685.0 eV) was found in all groups, which has been 
proven to be beneficial in hindering the diffusion of polysulfides [23]. In 
particular, in the S 2p spectrum (Fig. 5c), the reduced signal intensities 
of S-S/C-S (S 2p3/2 ≈163.4 eV) and Li2S2 (S 2p3/2 ≈161.7 eV) in the 
DMP-PFPN system compared to those in DME and DMP electrolytes 
confirm that a more condensed CEI layer was formed, and the occur­
rence of parasitic reactions between SPAN and electrolyte was signifi­
cantly decreased [21,23]. In addition, PFPN itself also participates the 
formation of CEI layer. In the N 1 s and P 2p spectrums (Fig. 5d, e), the 
inorganic products (N=P-N, LixPOy and LixPFy) from the decomposition 
of PFPN further enhance the ionic conductivity of the cathode interface 
layer [45,46]. These findings strongly corroborate that the synergistic 
interplay of DMP, acting as a weak solvent, and PFPN, functioning as a 
bulky steric diluent, effectively mitigates the side reactions at the 
cathode-electrolyte interface. This synergistic action facilitates the for­
mation of a robust cathode-electrolyte interface (CEI) layer, which is 
instrumental in retaining active sulfur species and enhancing the overall 
sulfur utilization efficiency [47]. By reducing interfacial reactivity, the 
carefully designed electrolyte system ensures better preservation of the 
electroactive components, ultimately contributing to improved elec­
trochemical performance and prolonged battery lifespan.
The stability of electrolytes toward lithium metal anodes signifi­
cantly influences the durability of Li||SPAN batteries. We assembled Li|| 
Cu cells to assess the reversibility of lithium deposition/stripping. 
Following the Aurbach protocol (Fig. 6a), the DMP-PFPN electrolyte 
exhibited a coulombic efficiency (CE) of 98.99%, outperforming the 
DMP (98.67%) and DME (97.71%) electrolytes. Cyclic performance of 
Li||Cu half cells was further investigated. As depicted in the Fig. 6b, at 1 
mA cm−2 with 1 mAh cm−2, the CE of the DME electrolyte-based cell 
began to fluctuate markedly after 100 cycles, whereas the DMP elec­
trolyte maintained stable CE up to 180 cycles. Notably, the DMP-PFPN 
electrolyte sustained stable cycling for 200 cycles, corroborating its 
superior compatibility with lithium metal anodes. To further evaluate 
the electrochemical stability of the lithium metal anode in different 
electrolytes, we assembled Li||Li symmetric cells. As shown in Fig. S19, 
under the harsh conditions of 3 mA cm−2 current density and 3 mAh 
cm−2 areal capacity, the Li||Li symmetric cell with the DMP-PFPN 
electrolyte maintained stable cycling for 200 h with an overpotential 
of approximately 63 mV, which was significantly superior to the sym­
metric cell using DME and DMP electrolytes.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to characterize 
the surface morphology of lithium metal anodes cycled in various 
electrolytes. As displayed in Fig. 6c, following 50 cycles of galvanostatic 
cycling for the Li||SPAN cells, the Li anode in DME electrolyte displays 
highly uneven deposition features with pronounced cracking, a direct 
manifestation of interfacial instability at the electrolyte‑lithium metal 
interface. This structural degradation is attributed to severe parasitic 
reactions between dissolved polysulfides and the lithium metal anode 
surface, which are exacerbated by inadequate electrolyte-anode 
compatibility and unoptimized solvation dynamics. In contrast, the 
lithium metal anode cycled in DMP electrolyte exhibits a relatively 
smoother morphology with diminished crack formation. Remarkably, 
DMP-PFPN electrolyte enables the most homogeneous and compact 
lithium deposition. This result's findings indicate that by modulating the 
solvent composition and optimizing the solvation structure, the DMP- 
PFPN electrolyte system can effectively mitigate polysulfide solubility. 
This strategy suppresses interfacial parasitic reactions, and ultimately 
yields a substantial enhancement in cell performance.
To comprehensively elucidate the underlying mechanisms respon­
sible for the surface morphological differences of lithium metal anodes, 
a detailed characterization of the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer 
components formed on the surface of cycled lithium metal anodes was 
analyzed. As shown in the Fig. S20, in the C 1 s spectrum, C–O, C––O, 
and CO3
2−were detected, which can be ascribed to the decomposition of 
the organic solvent [48]. Compared with the DME electrolyte, the pro­
portion of organic species in the electrolyte systems containing DMP 
solvent is reduced, especially in the DMP-PFPN electrolyte where the 
proportion of organic species is the lowest. This means that there is less 
decomposition of organic solvents in the DMP-PFPN electrolyte system, 
which is consistent with the previously mentioned less solvent-Li+ sol­
vation structure. An SEI layer abundant in inorganic components is 
regarded as highly beneficial for lithium metal anodes, contributing to 
improved anode stability and electrochemical performance [49]. In the 
F 1 s and N 1 s spectra (Fig. 6d, e), inorganic components such as LiF, 
S–F, Li3N, and –N–, which originated from the decomposition of the 
electrolyte salt, were detected in all electrolytes. In addition, the species 
of N=P-N, LixPOy and LixPFy (Fig. 6f), which are inorganic components 
derived from the decomposition of PFPN, further increase the propor­
tion of inorganic components in the SEI layer. This inorganic-rich SEI 
layer can effectively stabilize the lithium metal anode. In addition, the 
shuttling of polysulfides will affect the robustness of the SEI. In the S 2p 
spectrum (Fig. 6g), apart from SO2F (S 2p3/2 ≈161.7 eV), SO4
2−(S 2p3/2 
≈161.7 eV) and SO3
2−(S 2p3/2 ≈161.7 eV) species formed from the 
decomposition of the electrolyte salt, the shuttling of polysulfides leads 
to the presence of components like S-S/C-S, Li2S2 and Li2S (S 2p3/2 ≈
161.7 eV) in the SEI layer. Evidently, the DMP-PFPN electrolyte exhibits 
the lowest intensity of detected sulfur species. This observation, 
conversely, strongly suggests that the DMP-PFPN electrolyte is highly 
efficient in mitigating the shuttling of polysulfides. By suppressing this 
detrimental phenomenon, the electrolyte significantly enhances the 
utilization of active sulfur species, a finding that is in excellent agree­
ment with the previous test results. This congruence not only validates 
the effectiveness of the DMP-PFPN electrolyte in alleviating polysulfide- 
related issues but also underscores its pivotal role in optimizing the 
electrochemical performance of the system.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we designed a nonflammable quasi-localized high- 
concentration electrolyte with asymmetric DMP as solvating solvent and 
PFPN as a multifunctional cosolvent. PFPN's ultra-weak solvation ability 
enables it act as a diluent, while its excellent flame-retardant effect 
addresses the safety risks of flammable ether-based electrolytes. Under 
their synergistic effect, an anion-rich solvation structure is formed, 
which facilitates the construction of robust bidirectional electrode- 
electrolyte interphases, effectively suppressing the polysulfides disso­
lution into bulk electrolyte and reducing the corrosion on lithium anode. 
Leveraging the tailored electrolyte, Li||SPAN cell achieves a high ca­
pacity retention rate of 93.5% after 1200 cycles at 1C. Even under 
demanding conditions (high SPAN loading≈5 mg cm−2, lean electro­
lyte≈8 μL mgSPAN
−1
, and low N/P ratio≈2.8), the Li||SPAN full cells retain 
stable cyclability from room temperature to 60 ◦C. This work presents a 
multifunctional cosolvent, offering a novel electrolyte design approach 
for high-safety and long-lifespan Li-SPAN batteries.
