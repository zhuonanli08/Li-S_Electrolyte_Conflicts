ABSTRACT: Understanding polysulﬁde electrochemistry is critical for mitigation of the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect in Li-S batteries.
However, in situ imaging polysulﬁdes evolution in Li-S batteries has not been possible. Herein, we constructed a hollow carbon
nanotubule (CNT) wet electrochemical cell that permits real-time imaging of polysulﬁde evolutions in Li-S batteries in a Cs-
corrected environmental transmission electron microscope. Upon discharge, sulfur was electrochemically reduced to long-chain
polysulﬁdes, which dissolved into the electrolyte instantly and were stabilized by Py14
+ cations solvation. Metastable polysulﬁdes
prove to be problematic for Li-S batteries, therefore, destabilizing the Py14
+-solvated polysulﬁdes by adding low polarized solvents
into the electrolyte to weaken the interaction between Py14
+ cation and long-chain polysulﬁdes renders a rapid polysulﬁdes-to-Li2S
transition, thus eﬃciently mitigating polysulﬁde formation and improving the performance of Li-S batteries dramatically. Moreover,
the CNT wet electrochemical cell proves to be a universal platform for in situ probing electrochemistry of various batteries.
KEYWORDS: in situ TEM, carbon nanotubule wet electrochemical cell, polysulﬁdes, Li-S batteries, ionic liquid
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium-ion battery (LIB) has been broadly used in portable
electronics such as laptops, cell phones, etc.; however, for more
demanding applications, such as vehicle electriﬁcation and
renewable and grid energy storage, much higher energy density
and power density than the current LIB technology are
required. The so-called “post lithium” technology such as Li-S
battery is among the most promising candidates to meet the
aforementioned demands.1−5 The sulfur cathode has a
theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 1675 mAh g−1, which is much
higher than that of metal oxide cathode used in commercial
LIBs. Moreover, sulfur is abundant, rendering the fabrication of
Li-S battery more cost-eﬀective than the traditional metal-
oxide-based LIBs. Nevertheless, there are two challenging
issues in the development of Li-S technology, i.e., the poor
electric conductivity of sulfur and the so-called polysulﬁde
shuttle eﬀect.6 Signiﬁcant progress has been made toward
circumventing the ﬁrst obstacle by loading sulfur in carbon
backbone or other conducting medium, which forms an
electrical conducting network.3,7,8 Using ionic liquid (IL) as
the electrolyte is one of the most promising strategies to
mitigate the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect,9−13 as IL has a high
viscosity, which slows down the diﬀusion of polysulﬁde and
thus extends the lifetime of the battery.
To fully resolve the issues in Li-S battery, a fundamental
understanding of the polysulﬁde electrochemistry is a
prerequisite. In this context, in situ transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) has made a great stride in revealing the
electrochemistry in LIBs with the highest spatial and temporal
resolution.14−17 The Li-S electrochemistry has been inves-
tigated by several groups by in situ TEM in a solid-state

was a direct S-to-Li2S transformation without polysulﬁde

nanobattery and also revealed a direct S-to-Li2S trans-

ﬂat, suggesting that the S/Li2S interface was both electric and
ionic conducting. Xu et al. demonstrated that the performance
of a Li-S battery was signiﬁcantly improved by controlling the
chemical equilibria of the lithium polysulﬁde species in the
sulfur cells.18 When leveling the concentration gradient of the
polysulﬁde species at the cathode/electrolyte interface using a
polysulﬁde-based electrolyte, the electrode of a simple mixture
of sulfur and acetylene black powders demonstrated a very
stable cyclic capacity. In situ TEM analysis during the
discharge process of a Li-S solid cell demonstrates that lithium
diﬀusion preferentially occurred at the surface of the sulfur
particle and formed a solid Li2S crust on the bulk. The
insulating character of the formed Li2S layer makes the radial
diﬀusion of Li+ ions through the interface into the bulk more
diﬃcult compared to that along the contour of the particle. It
should be mentioned that only the ﬁrst half cycle (the initial
discharge reaction) was shown in all of these in situ solid Li-S
cells and cycling of Li-S nanobattery has not been achieved,
which is vital as the Li-S battery performance is critically
dependent on the cycling stability. In this context, Wang et al.
recently achieved cycling performance of Li-S nanobatteries at
high temperatures for the ﬁrst time and demonstrate that it is
the ionic conductivity rather than the electric conductivity that
dictates the reversibility of solid-state Li-S batteries.21
Despite the remarkable progress made in the in situ studies
of Li-S nanobatteries, in situ imaging the polysulﬁde formation
and the sulfur−electrolyte interface evolution have not been
possible. Such observations may provide important insight into
resolving the challenging issues in Li-S battery. Herein, we
report the construction of a novel carbon nanotubule-based
electrochemical cell consisting of a partial sulfur-ﬁlled carbon
nanotubule (S-CNT) cathode, an IL electrolyte, and a Li metal
anode, permitting real-time imaging of the polysulﬁde
formation and the sulfur−electrolyte interface evolution at
nanometer-scale resolution. Based on the in situ CNT wet
electrochemical cell, we visualized that the sulfur was
electrochemically reduced to soluble polysulﬁde in the IL
electrolyte both with and without Li salt, and the in situ
formed long-chain polysulﬁdes dissolved into the IL electrolyte
instantly and were stabilized by the Py14
+ cation solvation. The
Py14

talline sulfur on the separator surface, causing the formation of

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Preparation of S-Filled CNT Cathode and Lithium-Sulfur
Nanobattery. First, anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) membranes with
a pore size of about 200 nm were placed in a tube furnace, heated in
an argon (Ar) atmosphere to 700 °C, and coated with a carbon layer
of 15 nm via C3H6 decomposition at atmospheric pressure. Then, the
carbon-coated AAO was cooled to room temperature. Afterward, an
appropriate amount of sulfur was placed in a Petri dish sitting on a
heating table at 160 °C, and the sulfur was melted in the Petri dish.
Then, the carbon-coated AAO was dipped into the molten sulfur and
kept for 30 min. The excess of sulfur covering the surface of the
carbon-coated AAO was removed by heating the sample at 250 °C for
10 min. Then, AAO was etched away with 4 M sodium hydroxide
(NaOH) overnight. The NaOH was slowly rinsed out with deionized
water for at least 10 times. Finally, the S-ﬁlled CNT (S-CNT) bundles
were dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 10 h.15
To study the role of ILs in the lithium-sulfur battery, we
constructed an innovative lithium-sulfur nanobattery consisting of a
single S-CNT nanowire as a cathode, an IL electrolyte (ILE), and a
lithium metal anode (Figure 1a). In detail, the S-CNT nanowires were
dispersed in ethanol using ultrasonication and then the solution was
dropped onto a ﬁlter paper. The S-CNTs were attached to a tip-
ﬂattened aluminum (Al) rod with conductive silver epoxy after drying
in air. Metal Li was scratched onto an aluminum rod, and then a drop
of ILE was placed on the surface of the Li anode. Then, the ILE and
S-CNTs were inserted into a TEM scanning tunneling microscopy
(STM) holder. Using the TEM-STM holder, the ILE was driven to
contact with one of the selected S-CNTs. In this system, the S-CNT
was used as a working electrode and Li was used as a counter
electrode in the lithium-sulfur nanobattery.
Assembly of Lithium-Sulfur Coin-Cell Battery. CR2032 coin-
cell batteries were fabricated in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox (with H2O and
O2 concentrations below 0.01ppm), using a sulfur composite cathode,
Whatman glass ﬁber separator (GF/F, 16 mm diameter), a Li metal
(99.9%, Alfa Aesar) anode, and 60 μL of Py14TFSI ionic liquid
electrolyte (98%, TCI Chemicals). In detail, the sulfur composite
cathode consisted of sublimed sulfur powder (S8, 99.999%, Acros
Organics), super P, and poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF). Before
preparing the cathode, sulfur and carbon were mixed evenly in a
mortar at a ratio of 7:3 and then heated to 150 °C for 2 h in a sealed
glass tube to form an S8/super P composite. The composite,
poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF), and N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP)
were mixed to form a slurry at a ratio of 8:1:1, which was then spread
on Al foil using a bar-coater, followed by treating in a drying box at 60
°C. The discharge performance of the battery was carried out in a
potential window from 2.7 to 1.6 V (vs Li/Li+) at a rate of 0.05C. The
quantity of the active material was about 2.0 mg.
In Situ Raman Spectroscopy Experiments. In situ Raman
spectroscopy experiments were carried out using an ionic liquid
electrolytic cell. The cell composed of a sulfur/super P composite
working electrode, an ionic liquid electrolyte of Py14TFSI, and a Li
metal as the counter electrode. Raman spectra were acquired from a
customized LabRAM HR800 confocal Raman microscope (Horiba
Jobin Yvon) with a 50× objective and a 632.8 nm He−Ne laser. To
achieve the detected concentration during the discharge process, we
used the constant-current discharge mode with a current of 0.7 mA,
and the discharge time was continued for at least 30 min before
collecting the spectrum. The acquisition time of each spectrum was
150 s, and the total stack was four times.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In Situ TEM Studies of Li-S Nanobatteries. Our
experiments were conducted in a Cs-corrected environmental
TEM (ETEM, FEI, Titan G2, 300 kV), and the nanobattery
setup is shown in Figure 1a. Hollow CNTs with a diameter of
approximately 200 nm were synthesized via chemical vapor
deposition with anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) template.
Sulfur was inﬁltrated into the hollow of the CNT via molten
sulfur-impregnated method. The partially sulfur-ﬁlled S-CNTs
were extracted from the AAO template by chemically etching
away the AAO template. Individual S-CNTs were attached to
an aluminum (Al) rod and used as the cathode. A drop of 1-
butyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)imide
(Py14TFSI) IL with lithium bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI) salt (1.0 M) as the electrolyte was placed on the
surface of the metallic lithium, which was scratched to an Al
metal rod and used as the counter electrode. By touching the
half-empty S-CNT with IL, the IL electrolyte ﬂowed into the
hollow CNT and contacted the sulfur intimately in the CNT,
thus forming a Li|Py14TFSI + 1 M LiTFSI|S-CNT (Li-S)
nanobattery (Figure 1a and Movie S1). The IL was
encapsulated in the CNT, rendering the IL/S interface and
the sulfur cathode evolution clearly visible during the entire
electrochemical reaction process and thus enabling real-time
studies of the reaction mechanisms of Li-S batteries at
nanometer resolution, which hitherto has not been realized.
As shown in Figure 1b−e and Movie S2, the IL/S interface
(red arrowheads in Figure 1b−d) migrated from right to left
when a potential was applied to the S-CNT cathode, indicating
that a discharge reaction occurred in the S-CNT cathode.
Apparently, the discharge product dissolved into the IL
instantaneously once it was formed, because when the IL/S
interface advanced from right to left, the consumed S exhibited
almost the same contrast as the pristine IL. Interestingly, it
appeared that the IL has leapfrogged from a solid sulfur
segment in the middle of the CNT (Figure 1d), which is not
true, as a magniﬁed image of the solid sulfur segment actually
showed that there was a tiny gap between the solid sulfur and
the CNT (Figure S1b), permitting ﬂowing of the IL via a
capillary force from the left to the right of the solid sulfur
segment. In addition, the IL/S interface is a solid/liquid
interface rather than a solid/solid interface similar to a solid Li-
S battery reported by Xu et al. (detailed discussion in
Supporting Information Figures S2−S4).18 To the best of our
knowledge, this is the ﬁrst real-time visualization of the
polysulﬁde (evidence for the formation of polysulﬁde will be
provided later in the paper) dissolution into an electrolyte.
When the discharge was stopped at open-circuit voltage
(OCV), some semispherical particles attached to the CNT wall
emerged (Figure 1e).
Concurrent with the in situ TEM observations of the
microstructure evolution, we were able to record the
corresponding cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve of the nano-
battery, thus permitting real-time microstructure evolution and
the electrochemistry correlation down to a single nanowire/
nanotube level, rendering in situ nanoscale electrochemistry in
Li-S nanobatteries a reality. Although the recorded potential in
the in situ CV curve was an approximate value due to the drift
or polarization of the Li quasi-reference electrode, the potential
used in the in situ ETEM can be corrected semiquantitatively
based on the emergence of lithium dendrite in the nanobattery
(Figure S5 and detailed discussion in the Supporting

indicating the conversion from S8 to Li2S. The low-loss spectra
in the electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) of the pristine
and the discharge product conﬁrm the electrochemical
reduction of sulfur to Li2S (Figure 1h). As shown in Figure
1h, the low-loss plasmon peaks at 20 and 39 eV in the black
line proﬁle are attributed to collective valence electron
oscillation from S8; the peaks at 20, 39, and 61 eV in the
red line proﬁle are plasmonic oscillation due to Li2Sx; and the
peaks at 14.5, 19.5, and 61 eV in the blue line proﬁle are
plasmonic oscillations that arise from Li2S. These data are
consistent with those reported in the literature,19 and also
agree well with the EELS obtained from commercial S8 and
Li2S samples (Figure S6). The semispherical particles
precipitated out to the CNT wall are identiﬁed to be S8
from both EDP (Figure 1f-iv) and EELS (Figure 1h, purple
line proﬁle). The electrochemical dissolution of S8 and the
formation of Li2S were also observed in other S-CNT cathodes
when similar experimental conditions to Figure 1 were used
(Figure S7 and Movie S3), indicating good repeatability of the
experiments.
To summarize, upon discharge of the Li-S nanobattery, the
sulfur cathode was reduced to long-chain polysulﬁdes, which
dissolved into the IL electrolyte instantly, and then further
lithiated to Li2S nanoparticles (more detailed discussion in
Supporting Information Figures S8 and S9). The reformation
of crystalline sulﬁde (S8) at OCV is suggested to be a result of
disproportionation reaction of the long-chain polysulﬁde,
which will be discussed in detail later, rather than super-
saturated precipitation (detailed discussion in Supporting
Information Figures S2, S10, and S11).
Surprisingly, we discovered that the electrochemical
reduction of sulfur to polysulﬁde ions and the disproportio-
nation of long-chain polysulﬁde ion also occurred in the Li-S
nanobattery using Py14TFSI-only electrolyte without lithium
salt (marked as Li|Py14TFSI|S-CNT; Figure 2 and Movie S4).
When a negative potential was applied to the sulfur cathode,
the dissolution of sulfur was also observed (Figure 2b,c),
suggesting the electrochemical reduction of sulfur in the
Py14TFSI-only electrolyte (Figure 2a−c). It should be noted

The electrochemical reduction of S8 in Py14TFSI only
electrolyte is evident from in situ CV curve (Figure 2h) as well.
The reduction peak at 2.5 V (vs Li) (Figure 2h) indicates the
electrochemical reduction of S8. The reduction peak at 2.5 V
(vs Li) in the Py14TFSI-only electrolyte (Figure 2h) is slightly
higher than 2.25 V (vs Li) in the Py14TFSI with lithium salt
(Figure 1g), which may be attributed to the fact that sulfur was
mainly reduced to long-chain polysulﬁde ions in the former, as
the potential for the transformation from S8 to long-chain
polysulﬁde ion is higher than that from S8 to short-chain
polysulﬁde or Li2S. More precipitated S8 observed when the
voltage scans from 0.5 V to OCV (vs Li) in the former than the
latter conﬁrms the dominant long-chain polysulﬁdes formation
in the Py14TFSI-only electrolyte, as long-chain polysulﬁde ion
is easier to transform to S8 than the short-chain polysulﬁde ion.

The dissolution and precipitation of the S8 can be repeated
many times (Figure S12 and Movie S5). The gradient
distribution of sulfur particle sizes from the sulfur side (Figure
S12, left) to the IL side (Figure S12, right) as well as the
continuously decreased number of precipitated S8 particles
with increasing cycle numbers indicate that the sulfur was
consumed and dissolved into the electrolyte as the soluble
short-chain polysulﬁde ion products via disproportionation of
long-chain polysulﬁde ions. The reaction length (L) and time
(t) displayed a linear relationship (Figure 2j), indicating that
the electrochemical reaction kinetics was interface migration-
controlled rather than diﬀusion-controlled.28,29
The electrochemical reaction in Li-S nanobattery using
Py14TFSI-only electrolyte without lithium salt is not unique to
the Py14TFSI solvent. We have tested 1-butyl-3-methylimida-
zolium bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)imide (BmimTFSI) IL in
both in situ TEM and coin-cell batteries (Figure S13), and
similar results to that in the pure Py14TFSI solvent were
observed, substantiating the universality of operation of Li-S
battery in solvent-only IL.

indicating the successful operation of the Li|Py14TFSI|S + C
battery using an electrolyte without Li salts. From the
discharge curve (Figure 3a), only one voltage plateau was
observed in the Py14TFSI-only electrolyte while two were
observed in the lithium salt-containing electrolyte, implying
that the long-chain polysulﬁde ions were not further trans-
formed to S2−or S2
2−during the whole discharge process in
the former. This conclusion is conﬁrmed by the CV scans of
the two Li-S batteries (Figure 3b,c). Only one reduction peak
(2.0 V vs Li+/Li) was observed in the Py14TFSI-only
electrolyte in both the ﬁrst and second scans (Figure 3b),
while two reduction peaks were observed in the lithium salt-
containing electrolyte (Figure 3c). The second voltage plateau
in the discharge curves (Figure 3a) and the second reduction
peak in the CV curves (Figure 3c) are attributed to the
formation of the short-chain lithium polysulﬁdes including Li2S
or Li2S2.30−32 Interestingly, after discharge/charge cycles of the
Li|Py14TFSI|S + C battery, the second voltage plateau gradually
appeared, which may be attributed to the cracking of long-
chain polysulﬁde ions by Li+ generated from the discharge
reaction of the anode (Li →Li+ + e−, Figure 3d). These results
further indicate that the long-chain polysulﬁde ions (S8
2−or/
and S6
2−) can be stabilized by the soft acid Py14
+ ions, and it
can be cleaved to stable shorter-chain polysulﬁdes by the hard
acid Li+ ions. These coin-cell battery test results agree
excellently with the in situ TEM results, testifying the validity
and powerfulness of in situ TEM to reveal nanoscale
electrochemistry.
To further verify the eﬀectiveness of Py14
+ ions in stabilizing
the long-chain polysulﬁde ions, various Li-S batteries using the
mixture of DOL and DME (DOL/DME, volume ratio = 1:1)
as well as the mixture of DOL, DME, and Py14TFSI with
diﬀerent ratios (VDOL/VDME = 1:1, VDOL + DME/VPy14TFSI = 3:7,
5:5, and 7:3, respectively) and none containing lithium salt, as
the electrolytes were electrochemically cycled. No discharge
capacity was detected in the Li|DOL + DME|S + C cells with
the DOL/DME (V/V = 1:1) electrolyte, while high discharge
capacities of 1050, 1042, and 1013 mAh g−1 were observed in
the Li|DOL + DME + Py14TFSI|S + C cells using the DOL +
DME + Py14TFSI electrolyte with the DOL + DME/Py14TFSI
ratios of 3:7, 5:5, 7:3, and pure Py14TFSI, respectively (Figure
3e). As sulfur is a conversion-type cathode, the discharge
(reduction of sulfur) can occur in the Li+ ion-free electrolyte
when the supporting cations are present.32 In the Py14TFSI-
only electrolyte, the Py14
+ cations act as the supporting cation
to assist the electrochemical reduction of S8. More speciﬁcally,
the high viscosity of Py14TFSI and the strong interaction
between Py14
+ cation and the long-chain polysulﬁde ions
(based on the hard−soft acid−base theory) suppress the
further reduction of the long-chain polysulﬁde ion (S8
2−or
S6
2−) to short-chain polysulﬁde ion (such as S2
2−and S2−).
The stabilization of long-chain polysulﬁde ion by Py14
+ cation
caused the low capacity of Li-S battery with Py14TFSI-only
electrolyte (293 mAh g−1, Figure 3e), which is consistent with
that reported in the literature.33−36 The reduced viscosity and
the diluted Py14
+ cations by the low-polarization ether-based
solvent (DME, DOL, etc.) eﬃciently weaken the interaction
between the Py14

thus boosting the electrochemical reduction of sulfur to short-
chain polysulﬁdes in the DOL/DME-containing electrolyte, in
turn leading to signiﬁcantly higher capacity (>1000 mAh g−1,
Figure 3e) than the Py14TFSI-only electrolyte (293 mAh g−1,
Figure 3e).

It is worth noting that precipitation of S8 was detected on the
CNT inner wall in both the in situ TEM (Figure 1) and the
ﬁber separator in the coin-cell battery (Figure 3) rather than in
the electrolyte, suggesting a surface-mediated disproportiona-
tion of long-chain polysulﬁdes. The surfaces in both the ﬁber
glass separator in the Li|Py14TFSI|S+C coin-cell battery and in
the CNTs in the Li|Py14TFSI|S-CNT nanobattery provided the
eﬀective nucleation sites for the precipitation of S8. In Li|
Py14TFSI|S+C coin-cell batteries, the long-chain polysulﬁde
ions (S8
2−or S6
2−) shuttled into the electrolyte which were
electrically isolated; however, once they encounter a nucleation
medium such as a surface, they disproportionated to crystalline
sulfur on the surface and became electrochemically inactive
(Figure S17). The sulfur deposited on the separator became
“dead” sulfur (akin to “dead” lithium in the anode of a lithium-
ion battery) due to the loss of electric contact with the
electrode. “Dead” sulfur on the separator not only becomes
electrochemically inactive but also increases the barrier for ion
diﬀusion, which degrades the performance of Li-S batteries.
Figure S18 shows the Raman spectra on both sides of the
separator as marked by “a” and “b” after 400 cycles. The peaks
denoted by numbers “1”, “2”, and “3” are assigned to the S−S
bending mode of the “dead” sulfur. The “dead” sulfur on both
sides of the separator revealed that the dissolved polysulﬁde
ions shuttled back and forth between the cathode and anode.
The above results suggest that quick reduction of sulfur to
short-chain polysulﬁde ion via solvent tuning and high-eﬃcient
catalysis, and lowering the life span of the long-chain
polysulﬁdes are important strategies to improve the cycling
performance of the Li-S batteries. Moreover, utilizing the
surface-mediated disproportionation reaction to revive “dead”
sulfur is another way to enhance the cycling life of the Li-S
batteries (Figures 2 and S17a). For instance, graphitic mesh
insertion between cathode and separator, which acts as a
conductive layer and an intermediary for precipitation of
“dead” sulfur and blocks polysulﬁde migrations to the
anode.49−51 In addition, enhancing the adsorption of
polysulﬁde ions on the electrode materials of cathode is
another key strategy to enhance the cycling life of the Li-S
batteries.

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, a novel in situ CNT wet electrochemical cell in
aberration-corrected environmental TEM was developed to
investigate the polysulﬁde electrochemistry of Li-S batteries
with an IL-based electrolyte. The electrochemical reduction of
S8 to polysulﬁde ions and the disproportionation of long-chain
polysulﬁde ions to S8 and the short-chain polysulﬁde ions were
revealed for the ﬁrst time by in situ TEM. We provide direct
evidence that organic ammonium cations stabilize long-chain
polysulﬁde ions by solvation, and such stabilized polysulﬁde
ions prove to be detrimental to the performance of Li-S
batteries. Consequently, adding low polarized solvents such as
DOL and DME into IL can eﬀectively destabilize the solvated
polysulﬁdes, thus boosting the capacity of Li-S batteries
dramatically. These results oﬀer signiﬁcant new understanding
to the polysulﬁde electrochemistry in Li-S batteries, which may
provide scientiﬁc guidance toward the design of high-
performance Li-S batteries. Finally, the CNT-based in situ
wet electrochemical cell provides a unique technology to study
the fundamental electrochemistry of broad battery systems,
which may advance the science of batteries signiﬁcantly.
