A B S T R A C T  
The currently used lithium-ion batteries are facing two challenges of insufficient energy density for recharge 
mileage requirement of electric vehicles and low performance at sub-zero temperatures. Lithium-sulfur batteries 
(LSBs) with high theoretical energy density may be the next generation of lithium-based batteries. However, LSBs 
still have their challenges at low temperatures, including accumulation of lithium polysulfide, nucleation of 
lithium sulfide, lithium deposition, and formation of solid electrolyte interphase film, solvation degree and so on. 
Electrolytes of LSBs are partially responsible for these issues. To facilitate further research and development of 
LSBs particularly operated at sub-zero temperatures, this paper reviews the electrolytes of LSBs in terms of 
electrolyte materials, characterization, functional mechanisms, and performance optimization. The recent ad­
vances in electrolyte design strategies, including solvent optimization, lithium salt modification, additive 
introduction, and solid-state electrolytes of LSBs are emphasized. The challenges of both liquid and solid elec­
trolytes at low temperatures are analyzed and the research directions for overcoming the challenges are also 
proposed in this paper for further research and development toward practical application.   
1. Introduction 
With increasing energy consumption and the associated environment 
pollution, exploring new energy sources such as solar, wind, waterfall, 
etc. and developing the corresponding energy storage technologies for 
the intermittent electricity have become one of the frontier economic 
and technological efforts in today’s world [1]. Regarding energy storage 
and conversion, electrochemical energy technologies such as batteries 
[2–4], fuel cells [5,6], supercapacitors [7], and water-electrolysis for 
hydrogen generation [8] have been recognized as the efficient, reliable 
and practical options in recent decades. In particular, lithium-ion bat­
teries (LIBs) have been widely used in electronic portable devices and 
electrical vehicles successfully [9]. However, the energy densities of 
LIBs are insufficient in meeting the long recharge mileage of electric 
vehicles, suggesting that it is necessary to develop next-generation 
rechargeable batteries with high energy density [10]. In several types 
of possible high energy density lithium-based batteries, lithium-sulfur 
battery (LSB) is the one expected to meet the requirements of recharge 
mileage owing to its high theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh g−1 
and high energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 [11,12]. Furthermore, cathode 
sulfur material in LSB is abundant, non-toxic and cost-effective when 
compared to those cathode materials of LIBs [13]. Therefore, LSB is an 
attractive candidate for the future generation of high-energy lithium 
battery with the potential to change the energy storage landscape 
[14–23]. 
Many of the applications such as electric vehicles, unmanned aerial, 
subsea vehicles, grid energy storage, and space missions are inevitably 
required to operate in low-temperature environments [24]. To meet the 
power supply of these applications at low temperatures, thermal man­
agement systems are often required to ensure the operating temperature 
of the battery. However, thermal management system strategies are 
usually not feasible (internal thermal systems) or reduce the energy ef­
ficiency due to weight increase (external thermal systems) [25]. 
Therefore, the next generation rechargeable batteries should also focus 
on and improve their low-temperature performance. As identified, LSBs 
are particularly attractive for these applications. However, one common 
issue of poor performance at sub-zero temperature (lower than –20 ◦C) 
operation of lithium-based batteries is still true for LSBs, which has been

identified as a limitation [26–27]. For example, even the most advanced 
LIBs cannot provide a satisfied energy density at sub-zero temperatures 
[28,29]. Comparatively, LSBs with high energy density may be more 
suitable for operation at low-temperature conditions. 
Regarding a lithium-based battery, anode, cathode, and electrolyte 
are all make their partial responsibility for the performance at sub-zero 
temperature operation. It seems that the electrolyte is the major causer 
to the low performance. For LSBs, the commonly used electrolyte is an 
ether-based one consisted of 1,3-dioxy-pentane and 1,2-dimethoxy 
ethane (DOL/DME, volume ratio is 1:1), of which the former has a 
low-freezing-point of –97 ◦C and the latter has a value of –58 ◦C, 
respectively [30,31]. Therefore, LSBs have obvious advantages in elec­
trolytes at low temperatures. This low-freezing-point solvent can sup­
port a good ionic conductivity of 4 mS cm−1 even at a temperature as 
low as –40 ◦C to meet the minimum requirements for low-temperature 
operation of LSBs [32]. However, LSBs also show inevitable challenges 
at low-temperature conditions, including slow ion transport in the 
electrolyte [33], low Li+ de-solvation process [34–36], and high inter­
face charge transfer resistance of electrolyte-electrolysis [37,38], which 
lead to poor performance at low temperatures [39,40]. In addition, LSBs 
also have their own unique challenges of aggregation of lithium poly­
sulfides (LiPSs) at low temperatures due to the shuttle effect of LiPSs 
[32,41,42]. 
Up to date, more than 14,000 research articles have been reported on 
LSBs, but there has been particularly a few of researches on low- 
temperature electrolytes for LSBs. To overcome the low-temperature 
challenges of LSBs, researchers have explored many ways of opti­
mizing the electrolyte components for low-temperature LSBs. For 
example, Mikhaylik et al. [39] pioneeringly investigated the low- 
temperature performance of LSBs, and reported that the performance 
of LSBs at lower temperatures could be improved by making changes in 
the type of lithium salts and the proportion of solvents. Shin et al. [31] 
reported that the addition of DOL and methyl acetate (MA) to tetra 
(ethylene glycol) dimethyl ether (TEGDME) electrolyte could make LSB 
having a better discharge behavior at both low and room temperatures. 
The LSB had the first discharge capacities of 994 and 1342 mAh g−1 at 
–10 and 20 ◦C, respectively when an optimum electrolyte was used. The 
good discharge behavior at low temperatures was attributed to the 
addition of solvent with low freezing point and low viscosity. Gupta 
et al. [32] found that the modification of lithium trifluoroacetate 
(LiTFA) could adjust the interdependent electrostatic interaction 
occurring in the electrolyte to prevent polysulfide aggregation, enabling 
significantly enhanced electrochemical performance and providing a 
more complete insight of polysulfide coordination behavior at low 
temperatures. Therefore, as a general conclusion, in improving low- 
temperature performance of LSB, it is necessary to optimize each 
component of the electrolyte and explore electrolyte with high ionic 
conductivity, electrochemical stability, low freezing point, high safety, 
low-cost, and high affinity with the battery components at low 
temperatures. 
The related electrolyte reviews regarding the challenges of low- 
temperature electrolytes and the principle of designing low- 
temperature electrolytes for LSBs at low temperatures are very few 
[24,43,44]. This review focuses on the challenges of low-temperature 
electrolytes and the improvement of electrolyte components to 
improve the low-temperature performance of LSBs. The main contents 
include: i) describing the behavior of electrolytes at low temperatures in 
LSBs; ii) explaining the challenges of low-temperature electrolytes from 
following aspects: polysulfide accumulation, lithium sulfide (Li2S) 
nucleation, lithium dendrite growth, solid electrolyte interface (SEI) 
growth, and Li+ de-solvation for LSBs; and iii) summarizing the strate­
gies from the aspects of solvent optimization, lithium salt modification, 
introduction of additives, and solid electrolyte design to improve the 
low-temperature performance of LSBs. As summarized in Fig. 1a, the 
challenges, strategies, and applications of low-temperature electrolytes 
for LSBs are shown. Fig. 1b illustrates the role of electrolytes in LSBs 
under low-temperature conditions. Finally, a perspective for the future 
improvement of low-temperature electrolytes with the proposed 
possible research directions for the design of low-temperature electro­
lytes in LSBs is given for facilitating the further research and develop­
ment toward practical applications.

2.2. Challenges of low-temperature electrolytes of LSBs 
At low temperatures, the freezing of the electrolyte and the rapid 
increase in viscosity can lead to slow ion migration and material 
transfer, which limits the low-temperature performance of LSBs [50]. 
Moreover, the main challenges hinder high performance of LSBs at low 
temperatures are the dissolution and shuttle of LiPSs [51,52], the 
nucleation of Li2S [53], the growth of SEI films [54], the growth of 
lithium dendrites [55], and the Li+ de-solvation [36]. The insufficient 
cycling performance and rate capacity performance of LSBs at low 
temperatures are closely related to various components of the battery, 
especially the effect of electrolyte on the kinetic processes that require a 
basic understanding of the electrolyte chemistry at low temperatures. In 
this section, we summarize the fundamental challenges from the 
following four aspects of LSBs at low temperatures, which may provide 
scientific insight into the fundamental processes of the electrolytes in 
LSBs at low temperatures. 
2.2.1. Polysulfides accumulate result in a low contribution capacity in the 
second discharge platform at low temperatures 
The electrochemical performance of LSBs depends heavily on the 
structure and kinetics of LiPSs, which can dissolve and shuttle in the 
liquid electrolyte to adjust the electrochemical conversion processes 
during charging and discharging processes. The solubilities of poly­
sulfides in the electrolyte has both advantages and disadvantages. 
During the charging and discharging processes of LSBs, polysulfides can 
act as the solution mediators and facilitate charge transfer in the solu­
tion [56]. However, this also leads to a significant loss of active mate­
rials. Soluble LiPSs are present in the electrolyte not only in the form of 
individual Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8), but they are driven by intramolecular in­
teractions to aggregate and form LiPSs clusters [57,58]. In particular, 
the presence of LiPSs clusters limits the kinetics of electrochemical 
conversion at low temperatures [42]. 
Polysulfides tend to accumulate at low temperatures, resulting in a 
low contribution capacity in the second discharge platform [32,42,59]. 
Gupta et al. [32] studied and identified the phenomenon of polysulfide 
aggregation in a LSB under low-temperature conditions. Their first-

2.2.2. Li2S nucleation of two-dimensional growth trend is easier to 
passivate the electrode surface at low temperatures 
Besides the aggregation of LiPSs, the low-temperature conditions 
also have a significant effect on the nucleation and growth of Li2S which 
is the final discharge product of S8. Li2S with electronic insulating 
property can deposit on the electrode during the discharge process, 
which can passivate the electrode surface and prevent further electrode 
reactions [60,61]. At relatively low temperature, Li2S nanoparticles can 
resemble a thin film-like pattern that completely covers the electrode 
surface, blocking the electron pathway, prematurely passivating the 
electrode, and limiting subsequent electron transfer [24]. Yu et al. [40] 
used an in-situ X-ray operational method to directly observe the detailed 
evolution of the sulfur cathode and Li2S at different temperatures. By 
using this operational X-ray diffraction, they showed that the particle 
size of Li2S on the cathode tended to be smaller with decreasing tem­
perature. Since a larger overpotential was generated at lower tempera­
ture a higher nucleation number and smaller Li2S particles could be 
induced. Li2S with smaller size tended to be thin film-like rather than 
cluster nucleation [62]. To promote the three-dimensional (3D) growth 
of Li2S on the cathode, the growth model of Li2S can be regulated by the 
mediated solvent of electrolyte. Different permeability, polarity and 
viscosity of solvents could determine different deposition states of Li2S 
[63]. Chu et al. [64] used salt anions with high donor numbers (DNs) to 
achieve growth of particulate Li2S. They concluded that high DN salt 
anion could improve the solubility of Li2S in the electrolyte. When the 
solubility of Li2S is above a certain level, the growth of Li2S is proceeded 
by building Li2S agglomerates, leading to a three-dimensional growth of 
Li2S. Gerber et al. [62] applied benzo[ghi]-peryleneimide (BPI) as a 
redox mediator to promote the dissolved LiPSs being reduced to Li2S. 
The designed redox mediator turns to the potential of Li2S electrode­
position for changing Li2S from thin 2D layer to 3D deposit. Therefore, 
the 3D growth pattern of Li2S can be promoted by a component in the 
electrolyte to avoid early passivation, which can be used for electrolyte 
design at low temperatures.

necessary to control the morphology and structure of the deposited 
lithium to escape the generation of lithium dendrites on the lithium 
anode surface. SEI film is the reaction product of lithium metal and 
electrolyte, and the formation of dense and stable SEI film is essential for 
high-performance LSBs [70]. However, the problems of uneven lithium

deposition at low temperatures tends to form dead lithium, resulting in 
low CE. 
2.2.4. Li+ de-solvation dominates charge-transfer impedance at low 
temperatures 
As identified, the other two important factors responsible for the 
degradation of battery performance at lower temperatures are the slow 
transportation and migrations of Li+ via the SEI film in the electrolyte. 
Predominantly, in Li+ de-solvation, the strength of the interaction be­
tween Li+ and solvent molecules is considered to be the main impedance 
contributor [35,76]. Hatzell [36] believed that the interaction between 
ion and solvent molecule needed to be weaker so that the uniform 
lithium deposition could be better achieved in low temperature envi­
ronments (Fig. 5a, b). Holoubek et al. [35] compared two electrolyte 
systems (diethyl ether (DEE) and DOL/DME) to further demonstrate the 
effect of Li+ de-solvation. Using SEM images, they found that the 
negative lithium deposition of both systems showed similarity at 23 ◦C. 
However, at different low temperatures, local porosity and dendrites 
were observed in the lithium deposition of DOL/DME, while the lithium 
deposition in DEE had the same dense structure without lithium den­
drites. After an experimental and theoretical study of lithium plating 
behavior at low temperatures, the studied electrolyte was applied to a 
battery. The battery with DEE electrolyte could retain 76% of its ca­
pacity when operated at –60 ◦C, while the DOL/DME system retained 
only 2.8% of its capacity. Specifically, the ion–solvent interaction force 
in DEE was weak while in DOL/DME it was relatively strong. The 
stronger ion–solvent interaction force could make the de-solvation 
slower and prone to lithium dendrite formation. Wang et al. [76] 
developed an ionic dipole strategy to reduce the strength of the ionic 
dipole interaction between Li+ and polar solvent dipoles by increasing 
the fluorination of the solvent. The results showed that the weak solvent 
difluoro ethylene carbonate (DFEC) exhibited a faster ionic de-solvation 
behavior, the battery with such a DFEC electrolyte could provide 51% of 
room temperature capacity at –30 ◦C. These studies above reveal that 
the improved Li+ dissolution structure and ion–solvent interaction can 
facilitate the application of battery at low temperatures.

3.1. Liquid electrolyte design 
3.1.1. Solvent selection 
The solvents for LSBs should have basic properties such as wide 
electrochemical voltage window, high ionic conductivity, appropriate 
dielectric constant, low viscosity, and electrochemical inertness to other 
components of the battery [30]. The main two types of solvents used for 
LSBs are the carbonate-based solvents and ether-based solvents. 
Carbonate-based solvents are commonly used in LSBs with polymeric 
sulfur cathodes such as sulfurized-polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) [81,82], 
while ether-based solvents are more commonly used with monomeric 
sulfur cathodes. The mixture of DOL and DME has been widely used as 
an ether-based solvent for LSBs. Among them, DOL with lower solubility 
for LiPSs can promote the formation of more stable SEI films on Li anode. 
DME has high solubility of LiPSs and Li+ transfer kinetics, which is 
favorable for promoting the diffusion of Li+ in the electrolyte [83,84]. 
There are also many common solvents including ether solvents and 
carbonate solvents such as TEGDME, EC, and DEC [78,85]. Table 1 lists 
the properties of melting point, boiling point, viscosity, dielectric con­
stant, flash point, and molecular structure of commonly used solvents for 
LSBs. 
The choice of solvents for the liquid electrolytes of LSBs is more 
complicated than that of commercial LIBs, especially at low tempera­
tures. Carbonate-based electrolytes are good choice for LIBs, such as 
LiPF6 in EC/DEC, which have been extensively used in LIBs. It can 
provide high ionic conductivity, high dielectric constant, and stable SEI 
film formation on graphite anode. However, these carbonate-based 
electrolytes may not be suitable for LSBs because they are easy to 
react with the dissolved LiPSs, leading to irreversible loss of active 
substances and severe attenuation of battery capacity [86,87]. More­
over, the reaction between carbonate-based solvent and Li metal is 
active, resulting in lower CE for Li deposition [88]. The ion conductivity 
of carbonate-based electrolytes can decrease rapidly at low tempera­
tures, resulting in slow kinetics of Li+ extraction-insertion [89,90], 
which is considered a challenge. Cai et al. [91] developed a carboxylate 
electrolyte system of LiFSI-MP/FEC for ultra-low-temperature electro­
lyte. The system was based on SPAN for LSB, which could effectively 
overcome the problem related to the monomeric sulfur electrode 
[92,93]. The main solvent MP is a common carboxylate ester with a low 
melting point of –87.5 ◦C and a moderate dielectric constant of 6.23. FEC 
(10% by volume). In their study, 1 M LiFSI was used as a co-solvent 
additive and lithium salt, respectively, to stabilize SEI films and 
improve the compatibility between MP and Li metal. The Li||SPAN 
battery with LiFSI-MP/FEC electrolyte showed a higher metal compat­
ibility and stability, resulting in better charge/discharge performance at 
–40 ◦C when compared to that with LiPF6-EC/DEC electrolyte. At a 
current density of 0.1 mA g−1, it could retain more than 91% and 78% of 
room-temperature capacity at –20 ◦C and –40 ◦C, respectively. After 100 
cycles at –40 ◦C, this battery retained 86% of its initial capacity at a 
current density of 0.2 mA g−1 [91]. 
Compared with carbonate-based solvents, ether-based solvents are 
more commonly used in LSBs, especially at low temperatures. James 
et al. [49] improved the charge–discharge performance by adjusting the 
electrolyte formulation to allow operation at a temperature as low as 
–60 ◦C. It is important to note that both the charging and discharging 
processes could be carried out at low temperatures, unlike other systems 
that was charged at room temperature before discharging at low tem­
peratures. Mikhaylik et al. [39] studied the low-temperature perfor­

that the low-temperature performance at –40 ◦C could be improved by 
using the solvent with 86% of DOL and 14% of DME in the electrolyte, 
rather than the traditional volume ratio of 1:1. Ryu et al. [31] investi­

3.1.2. Lithium salts modification 
The Lithium salt is another key component in the electrolyte besides 
the solvent and is also a key factor to regulate the low-temperature 
performance of a LSB [96]. LiTFSI has been extensively used as the 
standard lithium salt for the basic ether-based electrolyte of LSBs, which 
has the properties of good thermal stability, good compatibility and 
strong dissociation ability [97–99]. There are also many other common 
lithium salts including lithiumbis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), lithium 
trifluoroacetate 
(LiTFA), 
lithium 
triflate 
(LiTF), 
lithium 
tri­
fluoromethanesulfonate (LiCF3SO3), and so on. LiFSI has a smaller anion 
relative to LiTFSI and therefore has a higher ionic conductivity and 
lower viscosity. LiCF3SO3 as a replacement salt has a higher ionic 
strength of association, which can helps improve the solubility of LiPSs 
intermediates [24,39,91,100]. 
Anionic modification of lithium salts with high dissociation capacity 
can provide more Li+ and increase the ion mobility in the electrolyte, 
thus enhancing the kinetics of redox reactions. Besides, lithium salts and 
LiPSs can coexist in the electrolyte of LSBs, and the coordination states 
can be affected and interdependent in a dynamic competitive balance 
[101]. Thus, Li+ in the electrolyte will be subjected to multiple charge 
attraction from polysulfide and salt anions. Their interactions will have 
a significant impact on the kinetic behavior of each species [58]. Kim 
et al. [102] studied the efficacy of adding imidazole salts to electrolytes 
to improve the low-temperature performance of LSBs. By introducing 
10% EMIBeti salt, the battery performance was improved at –10 ◦C and 
–20 ◦C, and the specific discharge capacity was increased from 58% to 
70% at –20 ◦C. The improved charge/discharge cycling characteristics 
might be related to the enhanced electrochemical reaction of the poly­
sulfide with imidazole ions and the improved stability of the surface 
morphology of the lithium anode. Yu et al. [103] proposed a high sol­
uble lithium salt electrolyte (6.5 M LiTFSI/FEC) with a stable lithium 
deposition/stripping behavior (Fig. 6a). It can be seen that the SEI in 6.5 
M LiTFSI/FEC is mainly composed of LiF, and the LiF-rich SEI can 
effectively inhibit the growth of Li dendrites (Fig. 6b), thus improving 
the electrochemical performance of LSB. The specific capacity of LSB 
constructed with this concentrated electrolyte is above 400 mAh g−1 
after 50 cycles at –10 ◦C (Fig. 6c). Therefore, the introduction of 
appropriate lithium salts at low temperatures can likewise adjust the 
dissolution structure of Li+, improve the aggregation state of LiPSs form 
a more stable SEI, and greatly enhance the low-temperature perfor­
mance of LSB. 
3.1.3. Introduction of electrolyte additives 
The introduction of electrolyte additives can change the components 
and structure of SEI on the lithium anode, thus promoting the formation 
of stable SEI, regulating the uniform growth of Li deposition, and 
inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites. In this regard, the LiNO3 
additive has received extensive attention and research due to its ability 
for promoting the formation of stable SEI layer and inhibiting the shuttle 
effect of polysulfides [104–106]. There are also many other additives 
including LiPS, BNNSs (boron nitride nanosheets), and so on, which can 
effectively overcome issues such as lithium deposition and lithium 
dendrite growth by improving the formation of SEI [107,108]. 
Yao et al. [109] introduced the charge transfer intermediate of 
pyrene in the reaction processes of LSB. As an electron transfer agent, 
the intermediate can significantly reduce losses of elemental sulfur or

studies of additives at low temperatures and those at room temperatures, 
the functions of additives can be summarized as follows: 1) promoting 
the formation of a stable SEI layer on the electrode surface to stabilize 
the lithium anode and prevent side reactions; 2) facilitating the oxida­
tion of Li2S, thus improving the utilization of the active material; and 3) 
interacting with polysulfides to promote the conversion process to 
improve the low-temperature performance of LSB. 
3.2. Solid-state electrolyte designs 
Solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) replacing organic liquid electrolytes is 
expected to be able to prevent shuttle effect of LiPSs, lithium dendrite 
growth, and other issues [110–112]. However, SSEs has lower conduc­
tivities of Li+ and higher interface resistance than liquid electrolytes, 
which limit the cycle performance of LSBs at low temperatures. To 
improve the charge transfer kinetics of Li+ in SSEs at low temperatures, 
polymer-based and ceramic-based SSEs have been designed to facilitate 
charge transportation [77,113–115]. Gu et al. [116] reported the low- 
temperature performance of semi SSE-based LSBs with FDE/1,3- 
(1,1,2,2-tetra-fluoroethoxy)propane (FDE-LAGP) hybrid electrolyte, 
and the results showed that such a SSE had an excellent low-temperature 
tolerance in LSB. This study showed that inorganic ceramic electrolytes 
had an advantage over those with liquid electrolytes because the solid- 
state ceramics with high Li+ transfer number could completely suppress 
the lithium anode with intermediate polysulfides (Sn
2–), and LSB with 
80% FDE-LAGP electrolyte had an impressive cycling stability at 1C. 
Their battery showed an initial discharge capacity of 915.0 mAh g−1 and 
remained at 668 mAh g−1 after 1200 cycles with an average capacity 
drop of 0.2 mAh g−1 per cycle. Even at 5 ◦C and –5 ◦C, the battery has 30 
turns of stable cycling performance and provides high reversible ca­
pacity at 0.1C. Li et al. [117] designed a conductive covalent organic 
frameworks (COFs)-based SSE, which achieved an ion conductivity of 
10−5 S cm−1 at –40 ◦C due to the formed COFs-based SSE with fast Li+
transport channels. Zhu et al. [118] developed a method to anchor a 
large anionic group on the skeleton of metal–organic frameworks 
(MOFs) and obtained superior single-ion conducting electrolytes (UiO­
LiTFSI). The single ion-conducting SSE has a high ionic conductivity at 
room temperature, and can effectively inhibit the formation of lithium 
dendrites. A battery with SSE and LiFePO4 cathode exhibits both 
excellent rate performance and cycling stability, especially at low- 
temperature lithium metal battery at 0 ◦C. Additionally, the ionic con­
ductivity of polyethylene oxide (PEO) electrolyte was also studied at low 
temperatures by Shin et al. [119], and the results indicated that the ionic 
conductivity of PEO polymer electrolyte prepared by ball milling could 
be increased by about one order of magnitude compared to that without 
ball milling and also increased with increasing ball milling time. By ball 
milling, the structure and morphology of the polymer electrolyte was 
changed to an amorphous and smaller spherical crystal structure, which 
had a high ionic conductivity at low temperatures.

4. Conclusion and perspectives 
In conclusion, this paper first describes the charge/discharge 
behavior of LSBs at low temperatures. Two discharge plateaus at room 
temperature and four or five discharge plateaus at low temperatures 
down to –40 ◦C are described and explained. The multi-platform 
behavior may be caused by changes in the solvation stability of poly­
sulfide clusters at low temperatures. Then, the challenges of low- 
temperature electrolytes are described in terms of polysulfide accumu­
lation, Li2S nucleation, lithium dendrite growth, SEI growth, Li+ disso­
lution, and so on. Specifically, at low-temperature conditions, 
polysulfides accumulation can result in a low contribution capacity in 
the second discharge platform; two-dimensional growth of Li2S nucle­
ation is easier to passivate the electrode surface; smaller particle size of 
Li deposition and dead lithium are easily to be formed; and Li+ de- 
solvation can dominate charge-transfer impedance. In fact, these chal­
lenges also exist at room temperature, but they are more complicated at

Through understanding the specific kinetic processes and mechanisms 
involved will be helpful for solving low-temperature problems and 
further promoting the development of LSBs at low temperatures in

The strategies to improve the low-temperature performance of LSBs 
are summarized in terms of solvent optimization, lithium salt modifi­
cation, additive introduction, and solid-state electrolyte design. Solvent 
with high ion mobility is necessary, which require the solvent with the 
characteristics of low freezing point and low viscosity at low tempera­
tures. For lithium salts modification, the interaction of cations and an­
ions of lithium salts with polysulfides in the electrolyte and the effect on 
the formation of SEI films at low temperatures should be considered. For 
additives introduction, adding additives into the electrolyte can effec­
tively enhance the low-temperature performance of LSBs. For SSEs 
design, improving ionic conductivity as well as reducing interfacial 
resistance is two important approaches for low temperature LSBs. In 
summary, the best electrolytes for low temperature LSBs should have the 
properties of low freezing point, high ionic conductivity, high safety, 
low-cost, good electrochemical stability, ability to form stable SEI films 
and high affinity with other battery components. 
There are several directions for the future development of advanced 
electrolytes for LSBs at low temperatures under practical conditions: 
1) The understanding of charge transfer mechanism, mass transfer 
kinetics, and thermodynamic stability in the electrolyte system can 
further guide the design of the electrolyte system and achieve excellent 
performance. In recent years, a large number of in-situ/operando 
characterization techniques have made great contributions to the 
mechanistic study of LSBs at room temperature [40,122–124]. Oper­
ando X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray microscopy (XRM) along with X- 
ray tomography (XRT) can observe the evolution of the morphology and 
crystal structure of the cathode and anode materials during the charging 
and discharging processes of the whole battery at low temperatures. In- 
situ UV–Vis, NMR, and infrared spectroscopy (IRS) can reveal the redox 
reactions in electrolytes of LSBs at low temperatures to further under­
stand the aggregation behavior of polysulfides and design a suitable 
electrolyte formulation. Therefore, by combining different in-situ/ 
operando spectroscopy techniques, we can gain insight into the reac­
tion mechanism of polysulfides, the mechanism of Li+ de-solvation in 
electrolyte during charging and discharging processes, and the kinetic 
mechanism in electrolytes at low temperatures. On this basis, further 
rational electrolyte optimization design in terms of composition and 
structure can significantly contribute to the practical application of LSBs 
at low temperatures. 
2) Exploring additives suitable for low-temperature electrolytes is 
essential to improve the performance of LSBs. Additives can promote the 
formation of stable SEI, regulate the uniform growth of lithium depo­
sition, and inhibit the growth of lithium dendrites. Machine learning has 
a key role in facilitating the discovery and design of novel energy storage 
materials. This includes performance prediction of electrode materials 
as well as liquid and solid electrolytes in batteries, and discovery of 
novel battery materials through composition and structure optimization 
[125,126]. Low-temperature electrolyte design has a key impact on the 
performance of low temperature LSBs, and machine learning can be used 
to predict the performance with different electrolyte designs to discover 
novel electrolytes.
