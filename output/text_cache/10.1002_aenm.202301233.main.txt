Tailoring Interfacial Derivative for Lithium–Sulfur Pouch
Cells with Ultra-Long Cycling Performance
Yu Jiao, Shuying Wang, Yuhong Ma, Mingjie Zhou, Li Zhang, Hao Zhang, Tianyu Lei,
Yuli Di, Chaoyi Yan,* Wei Chen,* Dongjiang Chen, Jianwen Huang, Yin Hu,*
and Yichao Yan
The lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery has been regarded as a promising alternative
to lithium-ion battery due to its high theoretical speciﬁc capacity. While some
eﬀorts have been made in developing high-performance laboratory-scale
coin cells, the relevant strategies cannot be readily transferred to a practical
pouch cell format because of its poor cycles. Herein a derivative controlled
liquid (DCL) electrolyte is demonstrated that contains 1,1,2,2,3,3-
hexaﬂuoropropane-1,3-disulfonimide potassium salt and bistriﬂuoro-
methanesulfonimide lithium salt to allow the preferential salt decomposition
on the surface of Li metal prior to the solvents. Such an electrolyte not only
enables the enrichment of inorganic potassium ﬂuoride and lithium ﬂuoride
in the solid electrolyte interface (SEI), but also suppresses the solvent
consumption via preventing the electron-gaining of solvent molecules from
the Li metal. As a result, the Li‒S pouch cellsenables a breakthrough from
several tens of cycles (<40 cycles) to more than 200 stable cycles at a current
of 300 mA per cell, demonstrating great potential for commercialization. This
study indicates that inducing preferential decomposition of salts rather than
solvents during the formation of the SEI could be a promising method to
stabilize the lithium metal anode and enable the production of practical Li‒S
pouch cells.

rechargeable batteries for electric vehicles and other portable de-
vices due to merits such as high speciﬁc capacity of 1675 mAh g–1,
natural abundance and low cost of sulfur.[1–4] However, the rapid

In this work, we adopt a derivative controlled liquid

the preferential passivation of the lithium anode by the KHFDF
salt to achieve a hierarchic SEI structure. The inner side of SEI
near the lithium metal is dominated by inorganic matter (KF and
LiF) to induce the dendrite-free lithium-metal deposition in the
DCL electrolyte.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Design Concept and Demonstration of Derivative Controlled
Liquid (DCL) Electrolyte
Considerable eﬀorts, such as preparing alloyed Li metal,[11] con-
structing appropriate Li architectures,[35] and using solid/quasi-
solid electrolyte,[36,37] have been established to relieve the para-
sitic reaction and eliminate the Li dendrites. Prolonged cycling
life at coin format has been reported for the Li anode coupled with
various cathodes. Nonetheless, these results are based on a steady
ﬂow of lithium supply and ﬂooded electrolyte (Figure 1a, Figure
S1, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, it is unclear how these
technologies will be compatible with existing pouch manufactur-
ing equipment or can achieve the same results at pouch cell for-
mat (Figure 1b). Herein, we adopted an electronic capture mech-
anism during the formation of SEI by using DCL electrolyte.
The consideration of electronic capture mechanism is based
on the frontier molecular orbital theory used for describing the
decomposition of complexes such as solvents and salts in the
liquid electrolyte. As shown in Figure 1c, in general, the de-
composition of solvents and salts highly depends on the lowest

lithium metal, thus reducing the solvent decomposition via mak-
ing it harder to gain electrons from Li metal. Moreover, the de-
composed product of KF arising from KHFDF is more conducive
to allow the even growth of lithium deposits according to the theo-
retical calculation shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information).
The strong adsorption characteristics of KF toward lithium ions
with higher lithium ion transfer number will make up for the
deﬁciency of lithium ﬂuoride (LiF).

The analysis of the solvation environment of KHFDF salt within
the DCL electrolyte is of particular importance to elucidate
the reaction mechanism. Therefore, we utilized micro-Raman
spectra to study the solvation structures of salts dissolved in
DOL. The results are shown in Figure 1d, where the dissolved
salt is compared to the individual LiTFSI, KHFDF, DOL solvent,
LiTFSI in DOL, and KHFDF in DOL. Upon dissolution, as
seen in the 1 m LiTFSI spectra, the characteristic peak arising

K+-coordinated HFDF−salt redshifts from 824.6 to 804.2 cm−1,
indicating that there is a stronger ionic coordination between K+
and solvent molecules, which will facilitate the HFDF−anions to
enter the double electric layer more easily, generating more KF.
Figure 1e lists the molecular structure of KHFDH and LiTFSI
and shows the uniform DCL electrolyte for compatible battery
assembly.
2.2. Characterization of SEI Derived from DCL Electrolyte
To identify the SEI components derived by DCL electrolyte,
we performed high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) assembled with an Ar+ sputtering depth proﬁling on the
cycled Li anode (Figure 2a). Possible decomposition paths of elec-
trolyte to form the SEI are summarized in Figure S4 (Support-
ing Information). The top of SEI layer on Li anode is derived
by the ether-based electrolyte, as shown in Figure S5 (Support-
ing Information), which consists plentiful organic COOR, COR
and inorganics such as LiF (Figure S6, Supporting Information).
Speciﬁcally, the intensities of COOR, which is usually selected as
the representative of organic species, present negligible changes
with increasing sputtering time. These results indicate that the
composition of SEI on the pristine Li mainly consists mixed or-
ganic and inorganic hybrids (Figure S7, Supporting Informa-
tion). According to the quantiﬁed atomic ratios of selected ele-
ments collected at diﬀerent depths (Figure S8, Supporting In-
formation), C atoms gradually decrease with the content from
30.9% (0 s) to 2.31% (600 s) while the O atoms present neg-
ligible change from 0 to 600 s. Therefore, it can be concluded
that oxygen-rich organics/inorganics (e.g., COOR) preferentially
dominate the SEI, which is consistent with the previous results

The surface topographies of cycled Li after diﬀerent cycles is
then evaluated. As shown in Figure 3c,e, after 20 cycles, the sur-
face morphologies of lithium metal with the blank and DCL elec-
trolyte are similar, and the Li deposition is smooth without Li
dendrites. However, with the increase of cycles, the surface mor-
phology of Li platting changes signiﬁcantly. For the blank elec-
trolyte, the lithium metal surface gradually changes from the ﬂat
structure (Figure 3c) into a porous structure (Figure 3d) due to the
lithium dendrite growth. The porous structure will increase the
contact area of lithium metal to liquid electrolyte, leading to the
fast exhaustion of electrolyte as shown in Figure S14 (Supporting
Information). By comparison, for the DCL electrolyte, the plat-
ting morphology of Li exhibits negligible change even after 100
cycles (Figure 3f). The above results demonstrate the advantage
of DCL electrolyte derived SEI in suppressing lithium dendrite
growth that allows for less contact area between lithium deposi-
tion and the electrolyte and reduces the possibility of electrolyte
decomposition.
To simulate the environment of lithium deposition in pouch
cell format with low external pressure applied, as shown in
Figure 3g, we directly observed the free growth of lithium depo-
sition in diﬀerent electrolytes by in situ optical microscope. Obvi-
ously, in the commercially available blank electrolyte with no ex-
ternal pressure to ensure stable Li platting, the growth of lithium
dendrite is more serious than that in coin cells. After 50 min, the
morphology of lithium deposition is almost chaotic. This further
shows that there is a huge diﬀerence between lab-scale coin cells
and the commercial pouch cells in lithium deposition, leading to
the fact that the current cycle of Li‒S pouch cells usually does not
exceed 60 cycles. For DCL electrolyte, we can observe that the de-
posited lithium metal is quite dense, and no dendrite growth is
observed. The above results further verify that DCL electrolyte is
more suitable for the application in Li‒S pouch cell environment
and could promote its long-term stability.
2.4. Performance Demonstration of Commercial Li‒S Pouch Cells
Electrochemical properties of coin Li‒S battery were ﬁrstly eval-
uated to demonstrate the performance gap between laboratory
coin cells and commercial pouch cells. As presented in Figure
S15 (Supporting Information), it can be observed that coin-scale
Li‒S cells can maintain normal redox reaction (Figure S16, Sup-
porting Information) using either the DCL electrolyte or blank
electrolyte at low current density of 0.2 C. However, the capacity
retention shows rapid decrease no mater using blank electrolyte
(≈70% retention) or DCL electrolyte (≈80% retention) because
the excessive E/S ratio (usually more than >30) causes the shuttle
eﬀect of LiPS that is more apparent in coin formats. As shown in
Figure S17 (Supporting Information), we further compared the
stability of cycle performance of pouch cell and coin cell at initial
40 cycles to assess the inﬂuence of shuttle eﬀect on stability. It
can be observed that the capacity retention only maintains ≈80%
for coin cell while the pouch cell can retain 90% capacity release
compared with the initial capacity, indicating that the shuttle ef-
fect of LiPS will be suppressed in the pouch cell formats due to
the low E/S ratio that can avoid massive dissolution of LiPS.
Pouch cell format is the ﬁnal architecture of commercial Li‒S
cells.[39] Therefore, the performances tested in pouch cell can bet-
ter reﬂect the eﬀectiveness of the proposed strategies. As shown
in Figure 4a, we assembled the cell using the same C/S electrode
which can steadily cycle hundreds of times in coin cell. However,
in pouch cell format, the stable cycling usually does not exceed 10
cycles. The cell performance can be re-activated if we inject fresh
electrolyte into the pouch cell after failure (Figure 4b), demon-
strating the importance in reducing the electrolyte decomposi-
tion for prolonging the cycle number instead of suppressing the
shuttle eﬀect at the pouch cell format, especially for the initial cy-
cles. Figure 4c shows the schematic illustration of proposed DSE
electrolyte that allows the bottom of SEI with KF/LiF-rich archi-
tecture to decrease the contact area between the Li deposition and
solvents. As shown in Figure 4d, the rate performance compari-
son can better explain the inﬂuence of Li dendrite growth to the
cell performance as higher current density will lead to more se-
rious dendrites. At the low current density of 100 mA per cell,
the Li‒S pouch cells with blank and DCL electrolyte exhibit anal-
ogously capacity and stability. This indicates that the consump-
tion of electrolyte is not serious enough to deteriorate the redox
of Li‒S pouch cells. However, with the stepwise increase of the
current density up to 500 mA per cell, the stability of the battery
with blank electrolyte decreases rapidly. By contrast, the DCL elec-
trolyte ensures excellent stability and ﬂat charge–discharge volt-
age plateaus (Figure 4e). The long-term stability of Li‒S pouch
cell is shown in Figure 4f. As the blank electrolyte cannot en-
sure the formation of stable SEI for inhibiting the growth of
lithium dendrite, the rapid capacity decay after ≈60 cycles is ob-
served (Figure S18, Supporting Information). Even if the content
of lithium nitrate is increased to 0.5 m, the cycle performance
is still poor (Figure S19, Supporting Information). By compar-
ison, the Li‒S pouch cell with DCL electrolyte achieves an un-
precedented report >200 cycles as well as maintaining the nor-
mal redox reaction (Figure 4g). The discharging capacity is pre-
sented in Figure S20 (Supporting Information). Moreover, we ac-
curately compared our Li‒S pouch cell with literature reports to

3. Conclusions
Expanding the lithium-sulfur cells from the laboratory coin-scale
to pouch-format is a necessary step for the commercialization. In
this work, we demonstrate that promoting the stability of lithium
metal anode is most urgent rather than inhibiting the polysul-
ﬁde shuttle eﬀect for improving the performances of Li‒S pouch
cell. Speciﬁcally, KHFDF salt is used to regulate the preferred
components of SEI, in which KHFDF salt decomposes prior to
solvent. The inner layer of SEI is successfully modulated to KF
and LiF enriched structure, which can eﬀectively alleviate the de-
composition of the electrolyte and improve the cycling stability of
Li‒S pouch cells. This strategy enables the realization of unprece-
dented long-term cycles (>200 cycles) and high rate (500 mA per
cell) characteristics at the format of Li‒S pouch cell with sulfur
