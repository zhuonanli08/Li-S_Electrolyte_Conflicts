polysulﬁde and low dendrite of lithium

A B S T R A C T
Although LiNO3 could eﬀectively solve the polysulﬁde (PS) shuttle problem in Li-S battery, however, it causes
explosive hazards with the cathode composition like gun powder (KNO3, Sulfur, and Carbon). Herein, a LiNO3-
free electrolyte was designed to simultaneously inhibit the PS shuttle and lithium dendrite problem, with pure
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) as the solvent, due to the low Ksp of polysulﬁde as well as the stable SEI on lithium. As a
synergistic eﬀect, it enabled the Li-S batteries to achieve 95% coulombic eﬃciency, 3 times longer cycle life, and
a slower self-discharging rate at 55 °C compared to the DOL/DME blend solvent. Four kinds of batteries, from
coins to soft packages, were carried out to conﬁrm the superiority of this electrolyte. Li-S soft package batteries
with 4000 mAh capacity were assembled, achieving the speciﬁc energy of 350 Wh kg−1 and speciﬁc power of
60 W kg−1, which could cycle over 30 times at 0.2 C with high capacity retention of 80%. It is among the best
comprehensive Li-S batteries performance reported using LiNO3-free electrolyte.
1. Introduction
The renewed interest in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries in recent
years is a response to the search for battery systems with higher energy
densities than the conventional Li-ion ones. The Li-S battery could
deliver an energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 theoretically and achieved
over 500 Wh kg−1 in reality with extremely low material cost [1].
However, several remaining technical challenges should be overcome
before its commercialization.
A major drawback, and perhaps the one with most focus on, is the
solubility of the long chain polysulﬁde (Sn
2-, n≥4) formed at the
cathode by the reduction of the pure sulfur or oxidation of short chain
polysulphides (Sn
2-, n < 4). The dissolved polysulﬁde further causes
unfavourable side reactions with Li anodes and shuttle between the
anode and cathode, which leads to low current eﬃciency and battery
failure. Besides that, due to the relatively slow diﬀusivity of PS at the
electrolyte/electrode interface, the C-rates capability of the Li-S battery
is much lower than that of the Li-ion battery.
To date, various routes have been explored to suppress the shuttle
of polysulﬁde, such as conﬁning sulfur within porous carbon [2–9],
using new binders [10–14], adding a Li+ conducting layer on the
separators [15,16] or cathodes [17,18], and coating the lithium anode
with protective layer [19–27]. Such approaches generally increase the
complexity and cost of cell fabrication, in the most cases, however, did
not yet achieve satisfactory performance improvements due to numer-
ous issues such as, the presence of high Li+ transport resistance or high
cost of the elaborated materials.
Given that the PS “shuttle” eﬀect is closely related to the composi-
tion of electrolyte in use, great pioneering eﬀorts have been engaged in
this area during the past several years [21,28–31]. The ideal Li-S
electrolyte is supposed to have a good Li+ conductivity, low PS
permeability, and high chemical stability with the lithium anode. In
order to meet all these above criteria, researchers have engaged in
developing novel electrolyte with diﬀerent solvents (DOL [32], DME,
THF [33], PC [34], TMS, EMS, etc. [35].), salts (LiTFSI, LiClO4, LiPF6,
etc.), additives (LiNO3 [32,36–42], LiBOB [43], P2S5 [44,45], PEO
[13], etc.) and ionic liquids [39]. Among them, the DOL/DME, LiTFSI,
and LiNO3 are the most preferred composition, exhibiting well-
approved battery performance.
The LiNO3 could help to form a stable SEI on the lithium surface
and achieve over 99% coulombic eﬃciency, which seems as an
optimized solution to the PS shuttle problem. However, the NO3

Recently, an important progress was achieved by Hu et al. [46], who
eﬀectively controlled the PS dissolving thermodynamics by simply
increasing the Li+ concentration in the electrolyte. Based on the theory
of solubility (Eq. (1)), the Ksp (or solubility) of polysulﬁde is also a key
parameter that aﬀects the PS concentration, determined by the inter-
molecular force between the PS and the solvents. So, the PS dissolving
could be controlled by choosing solvents with low Ksp, instead of high
concentration of Li-ion salts. By doing so, the viscosity and cost of the
electrolyte could be kept at a low value, which is feasible for practical
application.

In order to achieve this goal, non-aqueous liquids with low Ksp
should be considered as the solvent for the electrolyte. Currently, the
Ksp of polysulﬁde in non-aqueous solvent is still lack of research, except
that Sion Power Co. (USA) [47] preliminarily studied the Ksp of
polysulﬁde in several kinds of ether solvents (Table 1), which is
DME > THF > G2 > G3 > G4 > DOL > 2-2-2 > 2-1-2 > 4-4.
As shown in Table 1, the Ksp of PS decreases with the increasing of
molecular weight and C:O ratio of solvents, due to the increased
viscosity and decreased molecular polarity. However, the Li+ ions
conductivity would decrease in accordance, which is a paradox that
hinders the development of Li-S electrolyte. Among the most known
solvents, DOL is a unique exception, which shows a good balance
between Ksp and Li+ conductivity. Although DOL has been commonly
used in the lithium-ion battery as discussed in the literature, however,
it is seldom reported as a pure solvent of electrolytes for Li-S batteries,
because pure DOL was reported to decrease the sulfur utilization by
almost a half compared with the DME/DOL binary system [48,49]. It
was deduced that the discharge product of Li-S battery is mainly Li2S2,
instead of Li2S, in pure DOL solvent [48,49]. However, this report
might be misleading, because it's found that the utilization of sulfur is
also heavily depended on its dispersion on the electrode materials [50–
53]. In other words, the larger surface area of the electrode, higher
would be the sulfur utilization. So, the sulfur utilization in unary DOL
solvent could also be comparable with the binary DOL/DME solvent, if
only the surface area of electrode material is large enough. Based on
our previous research, carbon materials with the surface area of over
1500 m2 g−1 and pore volume of over 4.0 cm3 g−1 could well meet this
requirement [53]. Herein, an interesting idea comes out: “Is it possible
to use pure DOL as the solvent of Li-S electrolyte?”
The idea is simple but meaningful, surprisingly bringing with
multiple beneﬁts such as the lower electrolyte viscosity, higher Li+
conductivity, better Li anode protection as well as much lower
electrolyte cost compared with the ones with super high salt concen-
tration. In addition, the pure DOL solvent is also well known for its
compatibility with lithium, which can inhibit the lithium dendrite
formation and protect lithium with a thin layer of the membrane
formed by ring opening polymerization [54,55]. The brief functional
illustration of the pure DOL electrolyte is shown in Scheme S1. For
comparison, three kinds of electrolyte (noted as AE, BE, and CE in
Fig. 1a) with diﬀerent compositions were designed and tested with four
kinds of Li-S batteries from coin to soft package ones. The cathode S
content is 3.0 ± 0.1 mg cm−2.
2. Experimental section
2.1. C/S and electrolyte materials preparation
The carbon materials as sulfur hosts were prepared according to
Wang et. al [53], by etching Ketjent black EC-600JD (Lion Co., Japan),
KB600 with water steam at 900 °C for 90 min. The carbon/sulfur (C/S)
composite was prepared by heating the carbon materials and sulfur at
155 °C for 15 h in the argon atmosphere. The sulfur content in C/S
composite is 65%. The electrolytes were prepared by mixing solvent
(DOL or DME (all from Shanghai Demand Chemical Co. AR)) and
LiTFSI (Solvay, AR) in the argon-ﬁlled glove box. The PS solution
(Li2Sn) was synthesized by heating the sulfur (Shanghai Demand
Chemical Co. AR) and lithium (Zhongneng Lithium Co., AR) blended
in DME (n is designed to be 6, however, it was the mixture of 4, 6, and
8 due to the PS disproportionation). All the chemicals were directly
used without further puriﬁcation.
2.2. Batteries assembly
The cathodes were prepared by mixing the C/S composites with
sodium carboxymethyl cellulose at a weight ratio of 9:1 in water, and
coated onto carbon coated Al foil in room temperature. The electrodes
were further dried at 70 °C under vacuum overnight. The sulfur loading
on the aluminum is about 3.0 mg cm−2. The 2016 type coin cells were
assembled according to Wang et. al [53]. The Li/Cu cell was assembled
with pure lithium metal and pure copper foil as the anode and cathode
current collector. The single soft package batteries with the size of 2 cm
× 2 cm, 5 cm × 8 cm, and 4000 mAh soft package stacks were
assembled with a method similar to previous literature [56], as
illustrated in the Supplementary information (Fig. S1, Fig. S2).
2.3. Physical and chemical characterization
The PS solubility was determined via a complex process, by
weighing the mass of BaSO4 after oxidizing and depositing the PS with
hydrogen peroxide and barium chloride separately (details in Fig. S3).
The charge-discharge test was carried out using LAND CT-2001A
(Wuhan Landian Co.), with the voltage range between 1.5 V and 2.5 V,
respectively. The lithium depositing/stripping test on copper foil was
carried out with LAND CT-2001A, with deposition capacity of

3. Results and discussion
3.1. The solubility of PS in diﬀerent electrolytes
As shown in Fig. S4, the solubility of LiTFSI in diﬀerent solvents is
like: DME > DME/DOL (1/1 by volume) > DOL, under room tem-
perature without stirring. Considering that the LiTFSI salt solution in
pure DOL with concentration over 3 M is too thick to be impregnated
into the electrode quickly, the concentration of LiTFSI was controlled
below 2 M, for sake of battery assembly convenience. Three electrolytes
(AE, BE and CE) were prepared to study the diﬀerence caused by salt
concentration and solvent composition. Compared between AE and BE,
the concentration of LiTFSI increased from 1 M to 2 M. Compared
between BE and CE, the solvent was changed from DME/DOL (1/1 by
volume) to pure DOL (Fig. 1a). After simply adding equal amounts of
PS solutions into these electrolytes, as shown in Fig. 1b, the AE showed
dark reddish-brown solution with seldom sediments, while the BE and
CE showed obvious sediments. These sediments are deduced to be the
complex of amorphous Li2Sn (4 ≤n ≤8). Meanwhile, the PS in CE
solution showed diﬀerent colour compared to the AE and BE, perhaps
due to the diﬀerent solvation status of Sn
2-, which still need further
research. Fig. 1c illustrates that the PS solubility in diﬀerent electrolyte
decreases from 33 g to 5.5 g, agreeing well with Fig. 1b, due to the
increase of Li+ concentration (from AE to BE) and the decrease of Ksp
(from BE to CE). This phenomenon conﬁrmed the hypothesis of Eq. (1)
that the PS solubility could be well conﬁned via choosing solvents with
low Ksp of PS.

3.3. The diﬀusivity of PS in the electrolytes
Besides the Li+ conductivity, the PS diﬀusivity in the electrolyte is
another key issue for Li-S batteries. Although great eﬀorts have been
engaged in conﬁning the PS in conductive porous materials [34,57,58],
such as porous carbon, the diﬀusion of PS is still essential at the
carbon/electrolyte interface, for the thorough reaction of sulfur. It
seems like a paradox, however, a compromise must be made by
choosing proper electrolyte, to make the PS diﬀusion exactly around
the carbon/electrolyte interface. Therefore, the diﬀusion speed of PS in
the three kinds of electrolytes was directly observed, after separately
adding 1.0 M and 0.1 M PS solution (mainly Li2S6 dissolved in DME)
into the electrolytes (Figs. 3a and 3b). Due to the density diﬀerence, the
1.0 M PS solution sank to the bottom of the AE, and ﬂoat on the top of
BE and CE. During the 8 days’ testing period, the solvent colour
gradually became brown with the speed of AE > BE > CE, due to the
inevitable diﬀusion of PS, as illustrated in Fig. 3. In addition, the PS
diﬀusion distance in CE is the shortest during the same testing period,
which shows the lowest PS diﬀusivity. As an interesting phenomenon,
the PS solution keep integrated during this experiment, and the whole
PS solution even sank to the bottom of AE (Fig. 3a). Therefore, it was
considered that the chemical interaction among PS, Li ions, and ether
solvent is rather strong, which forms a kind of super molecules. This
phenomenon could be explained by the strong interaction among
lithium ions, polysulﬁdes, and ether solvents, which is also deemed
as the main reason that the polysulﬁdes could be absorbed by materials
with strong polarities, such as oxygen or nitrogen doped graphene
materials [12,59].
As it is well known, the PS cannot be 100% conﬁned in the cathode
zone, so it is meaningful to prevent the diﬀusion of PS with the
membranes between anode and cathode. According to Fick's diﬀusion
law (Fig. S5a and S5b), the PS ﬂux across the separator is proportional
to the diﬀusion coeﬃcient, which is aﬀected by the mutual interaction
between electrolyte and separators. Even with the same initial con-
centration gradient, the PS diﬀusivity across the separators decreases
from AE to CE, as depicted in Fig. S5c. Moreover, comparing the colour
of PS in Fig. 3 and Fig. S5c, the PS diﬀusion was obviously retarded by
the separators. In other words, the PS diﬀusion coeﬃcient across the
separators, AE > BE > CE, is aﬀected by electrolytes composition.
Seeking into the separation principle of membranes, this phenomenon
could be ascribed to the diﬀerent mutual interaction between diﬀerence
solutions and membranes. It is found that the Celgard 2325® could be
wetted more easily by DME than DOL, rendering the PS permeating
more easily across the Celgard 2325® membranes in DME solvent. This
deduction was conﬁrmed by testing the contact angle between diﬀerent
solutions and Celgard 2325® porous separators. The contact angle
between several solutions and electrolytes is like: DME < DME/DOL
(1/1) < DOL < DME (1 M LiTFSI) < DME/DOL (1 M LiTFSI) <
DOL (1 M LiTFSI) < DME (2 M LiTFSI) < DME/DOL (2 M LiTFSI)
< DOL (2 M LiTFSI), as demonstrated in Fig. S4d. It shows that the
separator is diﬃcult to be wetted by DOL and is most diﬃcult to be
wetted by the CE. Thus, the PS diﬀusion coeﬃcient across the
separators in CE is the lowest.
Given that CE exhibited the minimum PS solubility and slowest PS
diﬀusion speed (both Figs. 1 and 3) in bulk electrolyte, as well as
slowest diﬀusion speed across the separator, the PS concentration near
the anode should be like: AE > > BE > CE. Thus, the issue of PS
irreversible diﬀusion was well solved with CE.
3.4. The cycling reversibility of lithium anodes in the electrolytes
The cycling performance of lithium in three electrolytes was tested
by repeatedly depositing and dissolving the lithium on copper foil for
100 times under current density of 1.0 mA cm−2, with the Li/Cu type
cells. It is found that the Columbic eﬃciency in those electrolytes
increases from AE to CE (Fig. 4a), which is about 20%, 70%, and 95%
separately. Fig. S6 illustrates the typical voltage-capacity curves of Li/
Cu cells of the 100th cycle, which also clearly shows the Columbic
eﬃciency of diﬀerent electrolytes. Besides that, the lithium deposition
morphology also diﬀers a lot. As shown in Fig. 4(b, c, d), the lithium
deposited on the Cu foil in AE and BE is thick and loose, while it is thin
and dense in CE. The deposited lithium in AE and BE is also found very
brittle, which is abnormal compared with the commercial lithium foils.
By checking the top surface and cross-section morphology of deposited
lithium (Figs. 4c and 4d), it appears that the lithium is stacked
randomly and became denser from AE to CE. This phenomenon
conﬁrms that the CE is eﬀective to smooth the deposition morphology
of lithium.
The lower columbic eﬃciency in AE should be ascribed to its higher
inner resistance. The cell resistance after cycling for certain times was
tested with EIS method. As commonly known, the value of the joint
point between X axis and EIS curve represents the ohmic resistance
(Rb), the diameter of the semicircles illustrates the charge transfer
resistance (Rct) across the SEI layer on Cu foil, and the slope at low-

stabilizer.
3.5. The performance of Li-S batteries with diﬀerent electrolytes
Due to the combined eﬀect of inhibiting PS solubility and improving
lithium reversibility of CE, as discussed above, the CE can realize more
excellent Li-S battery performance than AE and BE. However, because
the Li-S battery performance is also heavily aﬀected by other two
factors: the electrolyte amount per sulfur [30,60,61] and the battery
assembling pressure, these factors must be excluded ﬁrst before
making a credible conclusion. Herein, four kinds of batteries were
carried out: 2016 single coin type, 2 cm × 2 cm single soft packages
type, 5 cm × 8 cm single soft packages type and 4000 mAh soft package
stacks (Table 2).

in Fig. 5c, due to the rapidly formed shorter chain PS that aﬀect the
composite potential of both electrodes. The short chain PS is ﬁrstly
formed at anode side via chemical reactions between the lithium and
sulfur that is slightly dissolved in the electrolyte. And then the short
chain PS would react with sulfur to form long chain PS. Meanwhile, the
long chain PS would be reduced by lithium to reproduce short chain PS.
These repetitive reactions would continuously consume the battery
capacity. Thus, the speciﬁc capacity delivered in AE and BE was only
1000 mAh g−1 (decreased by almost 400 mAh g−1), while that in the CE
was over 1200 mAh g−1 (decreased by less than 200 mAh g−1), as
shown in Fig. 5d. Furthermore, if the cell discharge were interrupted at
the end of the ﬁrst discharge stage (discharging 300 mAh g−1 (S)) and
kept rest for 10 h at 55 °C, the OCV of the CE was obviously higher than
that of the AE and BE (Fig. 5e). In addition, the remaining discharge
capacity could still reach 1000 mAh g−1 for CE, much better than AE
and BE, with almost no capacity loss during the rest period (Fig. 5f).
This phenomenon further conﬁrmed that the CE owns better ability to
control the self-discharge of Li-S batteries, due to the lower PS
diﬀusivity in CE.

3.5.3. The performance of single layer Li-S battery in 5 cm × 8 cm
soft packages
In addition, the performance of the soft package battery with the
size of 5 cm × 8 cm and capacity of about 300 mAh was carried out.
Due to the enlarged active materials area and limited slit volume, the
electrolyte mass is decreased to 4.0 folds of the sulfur mass, which
decreased by 60% from the 2 cm × 2 cm soft packages. Using this
electrolyte/sulfur ratio, the Li-S battery could achieve 300 Wh kg−1,
based on our practical experience [1]. The cycling stability of these soft
packages was tested at 0.2 C under room temperature. As shown in
Figs. 7a, 7b and 7c, the speciﬁc capacity, coulombic eﬃciency and
medium discharge voltage of Li-S batteries with AE and CE decreased
normally. However, that of the BE was rather abnormal, because of the
voltage polarization at the end of the ﬁrst discharge stage was very
serious when the BE were almost saturated with PS. As shown in
Fig. 7d, the discharge curve of BE was about 200～500 mV lower than
AE and CE. Especially in Fig. 7e, the second discharge stage in BE was
even unable to appear, when the cut-oﬀvoltage was set at 1.85 V. In
comparison, the AE shows lower voltage polarization, because the PS is
not saturated yet based on the high PS solubility in Fig. 1c.

3.5.4. The performance of Li-S battery stacks in 5 cm × 8 cm soft
packages
In addition, the 4000 mAh soft package battery with electrolyte/
sulfur ratio of 3.5:1 was fabricated, which could cycle at 0.2 C
(60 W kg−1) for over 30 times, with an initial speciﬁc capacity of
1400 mAh g−1 and capacity retention of about 80%. (Fig. 8). Its energy
density at 0.05 C is 350 Wh kg−1, which decreases to 200 Wh kg−1 after
laying at 55 °C for 18 h and could recover back to over 300 Wh kg−1 in
the subsequent cycles at 0.2 C. The battery has already approached the
performance of Li-S batteries used in the Zephyr series unmanned
solar energy aircraft, reported and manufactured by Sion Power and
Airbus Defence and Space Co. [63]. In comparison, the 4000 mAh soft
package batteries could only cycle less than 15 times with AE and BE.
Based on this electrolyte designation, Li-S batteries and unmanned
solar energy aircraft with better performance could be anticipated in
the future.
4. Conclusions
Based on our pragmatic consideration, pure DOL low Ksp of PS was
chosen as the solvent of electrolyte for Li-S batteries, simultaneously
achieving low PS solubility, slow PS permeability, high Li+ conductivity
and excellent reversibility of lithium anode. This innovation seems
simple but really meaningful, rendering it applicable to achieve the
state-of-art Li-S battery performance without hazardous LiNO3 addi-
tion. The electrolytes performance was comprehensively studied in
detail with coin cells, single pouches, and 4000 mAh stacks pouches,
showing excellent speciﬁc discharge capacity and capacity retention.
It's conﬁrmed that the cycle life of Li-S batteries is heavily aﬀected by
the electrolyte/sulfur ratio in the battery, as well as the assemble
pressure between anode and cathode. As a result, 350 Wh kg−1
secondary Li-S soft package batteries were reported for the ﬁrst time
in literature. And the 60 W kg−1 soft package Li-S batteries were also
realized, with initial discharge capacity near 1400 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C.
This progress is achieved by the unique designation of electrolyte,
which might set a bench mark and shed light on the further develop-
ment of Li-S batteries.
