EXPERIMENTAL
Material
1,2-Dimethoxy ethane (DME, 99.5%), tetrahydrofuran (THF, ≥99.9%), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 99.8%), methoxy cyclopentane (MCP, ≥99.9%), lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, >99.9%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, ≥99.9%), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, >99.5%), ketjen black carbon (300J), sulfur (S, 99.98%), and lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Super P was provided by Alfa Aesar, while poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) was procured from Kureha Chemical, and polypropylene (PP) separators were obtained from Celgard.
Electrolyte preparation
All electrolyte samples were prepared inside an argon glove box with O2 and H2O levels below 0.1 ppm. The THF-based electrolyte (denoted as THF-E) consisted of 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in the mixture of DME and THF (1:1, v/v) with 0.1 M LiNO3. The DOL-based electrolyte (denoted as DOL-E) was formulated by dissolving 1 M LiTFSI in the mixture of DME and DOL (1:1, v/v) with 0.1 M LiNO3. The MCP-based electrolyte (denoted as MCP-E) was prepared by dissolving 1 M LiTFSI in the mixture of DME and MCP (1:1, v/v) with 0.1 M LiNO3. The Li2S6 solution was prepared by dissolving Li2S and S in a 1:5 molar ratio in the electrolyte and allowing the mixture to react for 24 h at room temperature.
Electrode preparation
Sulfur cathodes were prepared by mixing S/ketjen black (KB) composite (3:1, w/w), PVDF binder, and Super P in a 7:1:2 weight ratio. The S/KB composite was obtained via a melt-diffusion process by heating sulfur at 155 °C for 12 h. For electrode fabrication, PVDF was dissolved in NMP, after which the pre-mixed S/KB composite and Super P were added to form a homogeneous slurry. This slurry was cast onto an aluminum (Al) foil and dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C overnight. Circular cathodes with a diameter of 12 mm were punched from the coated foil, with a sulfur loading of approximately 1.5 mg cm−2. Increased areal loading cathodes (≈2.6, and 3.2 mg cm−2) were produced using S/KB composite (7:1, w/w), LA133 binder, and Super P at a weight ratio of 7:1:2.
Characterization
The two-dimensional 1H–7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis was performed in the heteronuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (HOESY) mode on a 600 MHz FT-NMR spectrometer (Varian VNMRS600). Measurements were conducted at ambient temperature. A coaxial inner tube containing 1 M LiCl dissolved in D2O served as the field-frequency lock, while the electrolyte sample was placed in the outer tube.
Raman spectra were measured using a Raman Touch system equipped with a 532 nm laser source. 19F NMR spectra were acquired on an AVANCE NEO 500 MHz SPECTROMETER (Bruker) at room temperature. The samples were prepared using CDCl3 as an internal standard. 7Li NMR measurements were obtained on an AVANCE III 600 MHz (Bruker) under ambient conditions. For chemical-shift calibration, an internal coaxial insert tube containing 1 M LiCl dissolved in D2O functioned as the external standard.
Electrolyte viscosity was measured using a rotational viscometer (Brookfield). The wettability of the electrolytes toward the PP separator was evaluated via contact angle measurements performed with a drop shape analyzer (DSA100S, Kyoto Electronics Manufacturing).
In-situ Raman measurements were performed using a Raman spectrometer (Raman Touch system). For the in-situ Raman analysis, a customized cell was assembled by drilling a small hole in the Li-metal anode, which was aligned with the quartz window. A 532 nm laser was directed through the quartz window and the drilled hole to focus on the separator, thereby enabling monitoring of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) during cycling.
For the mass loss analysis of Li-metal immersed in LiPSs solution, Li-metal with a diameter of 14 mm were immersed in 10 mL of 3 M Li2S6 solution. After 7 days of immersion, the Li-metals were taken out, washed with DME, and dried for 2 h. The surface morphology was photographed, and the mass was subsequently measured. All procedures were performed in an argon glove box with O2 and H2O levels below 0.1 ppm.
Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS, ION TOF, Germany) was employed to acquire detailed information on the secondary ion distribution of Li-metal anodes (LMAs). Depth profiling was conducted by sputter etching with a Cs+ ion beam operated at 0.5 keV, and the analyzed area was set to 50 × 50 μm2.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS SUPRA, Kratos) equipped with Al-Kα radiation and depth-profiling capability was employed to characterize the chemical composition.
To quantify electrolyte consumption, 1H and 19F NMR analyses were conducted using an AVANCE NEO 500 MHz spectrometer (Bruker). After cycling, the coin cells were disassembled, and the cell components were rinsed with 1.5 mL of CDCl3 containing 0.1 M fluorobenzene to recover the residual electrolyte. The resulting solution was subjected to NMR analysis, and the remaining amounts of individual electrolyte components were determined relative to the peak intensity of fluorobenzene used as an internal standard.
Electrochemical measurement
CR2032 type coin cell equipped with Celgard 2400 PP separators were employed for all electrochemical evaluations. Cell fabrication was performed in an argon-filled glove box to avoid exposure to reactive species. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were collected on a Bio-Logic SP-150 system, using a 5 mV AC amplitude over the frequency range of 1.0 MHz to 0.1 Hz. The desolvation energies (Ea,ct) were calculated using EIS spectra acquired from Li||Li symmetric cells operated at 293.15, 303.15, 313.15, 323.15, and 333.15 K. The impedance data obtained at each temperature were subsequently fitted with an equivalent circuit to determine the charge-transfer resistance (Rct).
Exchange current density (i0) was evaluated through galvanostatic cycling of Li||Li symmetric cells at 30 °C, with applied currents incrementally varied from 20 to 100 µA. Li||Cu cells were first activated by depositing 2.0 mAh cm−2 of Li at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 and stripping to 1.0 V. After activation, three sequential cycles were performed by plating 2.0 mAh cm−2 of Li at 0.5 mA cm−2, resting for 24, 48, and 72 h, and then stripping to 1.0 V. The Coulombic efficiency of the Li||Cu cell was evaluated using a protocol adapted from the Aurbach method. A 12 mm Cu foil first underwent an activation step involving 1 cycle of Li deposition and removal, after which a 5.0 mA h cm−2 Li reservoir was plated onto the foil. From this reservoir, 1.0 mA h cm−2 of Li was alternately stripped and plated for 10 cycles. After the cycling sequence, the remaining Li was stripped until the voltage reached 1.0 V. All tests were performed at 30 °C at a constant current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 [1].
The Li||Li symmetric cells were examined for long-term cycling stability at a current density of 1.0 mA cm−2 with an areal capacity of 1.0 mA h cm−2 at 30 °C.
For the Li-metal corrosion test, Li||Cu half-cells were assembled using electrolytes with and without 1 M Li2S6. The cells were performed for five plating/stripping cycles at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 with a fixed Li capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2. Subsequently, Li was plated again and the cells were rested for 24 h. after the rest period, Li stripping was conducted at a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2 and continued until the voltage reached 1.0 V.
The cycling performance and rate capability of the lithium–sulfur (Li–S) cells were measured with a battery cycler (WonATech, WBCS 3000) under a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V at 30 °C. Following 2 activation cycles at 0.1 C, the cells were operated at distinct C-rates, specifically 0.3 C for 300 cycles and 0.5 C for 150 cycles. The rate capability was examined by cycling 10 cycles each at 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.3 C, 0.5 C, and 1.0 C, and the cells were then cycled by 0.1 C for an additional 50 cycles. The cycling performance of Li–S cells with increased areal loading cathodes (2.6 and 3.2 mg cm−2) was also evaluated at room temperature under the same voltage window. The cells were cycled for 2 formation cycles at 0.05 C and were then operated at 0.1 C for 100 cycles. Li–S pouch cells were assembled using sulfur cathodes coated on aluminum foil with a sulfur loading of 3.3 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 6 μL mg−1. Electrochemical cycling was performed at room temperature within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V, including two initial formation cycles at 0.05 C followed by cycling at 0.1 C.
For shuttle current measurements, Li–S cells were initially activated for two cycles, then discharged to 2.38 V, and subsequently maintained under potentiostatic conditions for 10 h. The shuttle current was quantified from the steady-state current at 2.38 V, and the Li–S cells were cycled at 0.1 C within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V.
For the self-discharge test, the Li–S cells were operated for 5 cycles at 0.1 C within a voltage window of 1.7–2.8 V and then held at rest for 100 h. Afterward, the cells were evaluated again at 0.1 C to compare the specific capacity and voltage drop before and after the rest period.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to evaluate the electrostatic potential (ESP) and molecular charge distribution, using the DMol3 code within the Materials Studio software environment (BIOVIA, San Diego, USA) [2]. The calculations utilized a double numerical basis set including polarization functions (DNP), together with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) formulated by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [3]. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criterion was defined as 1×10-6 Ha, while the total energy convergence threshold was set to 1×10-5 Ha.
Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations
Atomic charge distributions used in the subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were obtained from Mulliken population analysis [4], while the initial models were generated using the Amorphous Cell module, and solvation free energies were calculated within the Materials Studio software environment (BIOVIA, San Diego, USA) [2]. Molecular models of THF-E, DOL-E, and MCP-E were constructed based on experimental data, all containing 40 LiTFSI, 193 DME, 4 LiNO3, and 20 Li2S6 molecules, with the solvent components consisting of 247 THF, 286 DOL, and 172 MCP molecules, respectively.
To properly capture both intra- and intermolecular interactions, a modified DREIDING force field was employed [5]. 	(2)
Long-range Coulombic interactions were evaluated using the particle–particle particle–mesh (PPPM) method [6]. Molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS), developed by Plimpton [7,8]. The equations of motion were numerically integrated using the velocity–Verlet algorithm with a time increment of 1.0 fs [9]. 
To obtain well-equilibrated structures, a multi-step annealing procedure was applied. First, the system was gradually heated from 0 K to 298.15 K over 300 ps under NVT conditions. Each annealing cycle then comprised five sequential stages: (i) heating from 298.15 K to 600 K while isotropically expanding the simulation cell to twice its original volume over 100 ps, (ii) maintaining the system at 600 K for 100 ps, (iii) cooling back to 298.15 K while compressing the cell to its initial volume over 100 ps, (iv) repeating steps (i)–(iii) three times to promote configurational relaxation, and (v) further equilibration for 100 ps in the NVT ensemble followed by 500 ps in the NPT ensemble at 298.15 K. This complete annealing sequence was performed three times to confirm structural convergence. After annealing, an additional 20 ns NPT simulation at 298.15 K was conducted to stabilize the equilibrated configurations, followed by a 5 ns NPT production run for data collection. All reported results were averaged over at least three independently generated configurations to ensure statistical reliability.
