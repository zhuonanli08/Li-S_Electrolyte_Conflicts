heterogeneous conversion reactions are the fundamental causes of the poor 
reversibility of high-energy-density lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. The most 
direct manifestation is the unsatisfactory low Coulombic efficiency (CE). 
Herein the importance of CE in evaluating Li–S batteries is highlighted and 
a remedy is presented for such low efficiencies by in situ coating lithium 
sulfide (Li2S), as the cathode, with polymerizable electrolyte additives, where 
trithiocyanuric acid trilithium salt (TTCA-Li) is employed for a typical demon-

through a pouch cell demonstration with a practically high Li2S loading and 
the extension to elemental sulfur-based batteries by injecting the TTCA-Li 
additives into cycling cells.

1. Introduction

indicator for the reversibility of rechargeable batteries and their 
practicability by predicting the lifespan.[1] Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) 
batteries have been widely investigated as promising next-
generation high-energy-density batteries. However, most of the 
reported batteries show unsatisfied CE in cycling (normally

the CE of the sulfur-based batteries may be raised to some 
extent due to the use of Li-free anode, but it is still far from 
meeting the practical requirements and awaits the root solution 
for the Li2S side.[5]
The issues for Li2S conversion could be partially worked 
out by decreasing the size of Li2S particles and optimizing 
the carbon host structure. In addition, introducing catalysts to 
promote the reversible conversion of Li2S is also an important 
method,[6] and the rational design of catalysts has been widely 
investigated recently.[7] However, the sulfur conversion involves 
dissolution and re-deposition during cycling, causing the 
uncontrolled redistribution of Li2S and inferior contact with the 
carbon hosts and catalysts, which further hinders the complete 
conversion of Li2S. Although additives dissolved in the electro-
lyte can instantly contact the sulfur species, they are gradually 
consumed due to side reactions with the LiPSs or the Li metal 
anode.[8] Solving the problems of the high active potential of 
Li2S and its uncontrolled redistribution in the cathode upon 
cycling are therefore key to manufacturing Li–S batteries with a 
high CE and long cycling stability.
Herein, we develop a solution of in situ coating Li2S cathode 
with poly-organosulfide (POS), achieving a superhigh CE of 
above 99.5% (≈99.2% even in a long cycling) for the assem-
bled battery, which is substantially higher than the cases for 
the ever-reported Li–S batteries. The POS is produced by in situ 
electrochemical polymerization of soluble electrolyte additives, 
typically, trithiocyanuric acid trilithium salt (TTCA-Li), which 
is triggered by reactions between LiS* and TTCA-S* radicals 
during the initial charging (Figure 1a,b) and thus forms a dense 
coating around Li2S. The coating prevents the outward diffu-
sion of LiPSs, suppressing the uncontrolled redistribution of 
sulfur and Li2S in cycling, while the leftover TTCA-Li functions 
as a catalyst, accelerating the battery redox reaction. Thus, a 
pouch cell with a high Li2S loading > 7 mg cm−2 is stably cycled 
for over 100 cycles with a capacity retention of up to 70%. Even 
with a low N/P ratio of ≈1.2 by coupling the pre-lithiated silicon 
anode, the assembled battery can stably work over 200 cycles. 
Such in situ electrochemical polymerization strategy also shows 
its practicality and generality by improving the efficiency of 
elemental sulfur-based batteries through injecting the electro-
lyte-containing TTCA-Li additives into cycling cells.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. In Situ Formation of a POS Coating on Li2S
In this work, the organosulfide TTCA-Li with a carbon-nitrogen 
heterocycle was dissolved into the electrolyte (1 m bis (trifluo-
romethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with 1  wt. % LiNO3 in a 
DME and DOL mixture (1:1 vol.)), being as the monomer for the 
electro-polymerization to produce the POS coating (Figure S1, 
Supporting Information). The TTCA-Li was prepared by the 
reaction of trithiocyanuric acid (TTCA) with Li metal, and 7Li 
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Figure S2, Sup-
porting Information) confirmed the exchange of the H in the 
-SH groups in TTCA with Li, as revealed in Equation (1).

Based on the above results, we speculate that the mechanism 
for in situ formation of POS coating on the Li2S (Figure  2f). 
As a reference, the charging mechanism of TTCA-Li free of 
Li2S is shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information, where 
the TTCA-Li molecule loses three electrons and three lithium 
ions to form a TTCA-LiS* radical and the unstable radicals 
combine with each other to form the polymer linked by SS

CV curve. XPS and Raman spectroscopy confirmed with this 
(Figures S8 and S9, Supporting Information). In the additive-

form LiS* radicals, which then combine to form Li2S2. Thus, as 
shown in Figure 2f, when LiS* and TTCA-S* exist simultane-

product 1 which then loses Li ions to form a polymer linked by 
SS bonds. This process promotes the consumption of LiS*, 
destroying the structure of the Li2S cathode and accelerating its 
initial oxidation by lowering the over-potential.
The high-resolution transmission electron microscope 
(HRTEM) observation gives direct proof for the POS coating. 
Compared with the clean surface of the Li2S cathode before 
charging (Figure S10, Supporting Information), the HRTEM 
image shows a dense and homogeneous coating on the Li2S 
surface (Figure 3a) upon charging to 2.6 V (before the complete 
oxidation of Li2S to LiPSs), probing the formation of the POS 
coating, which has been further confirmed by the uniform dis-
tribution of N on the Li2S surface (Figure S11, Supporting Infor-
mation). Selected-area electron diffraction patterns show that 
the crystallinity of the Li2S cathode is decreased significantly 
after charging, and some Li2S nanoparticles are detected in the 
POS coating, suggesting the participation of Li2S, which causes 
the amorphous structure and the lower oxidation potential 
(Figure S12, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3b, 
during the initial charge, the voltage plateau at 2.2 V for the cell 
with TTCA-Li additive is attributed to the formation of the POS 
coating, and the activation voltage of Li2S was then reduced to 
2.44 V, which is far lower than that of uncoated Li2S (3.14 V).

2.3. Suppressed Sulfur Redistribution by POS Coating
The formation of POS coating helped stabilize the electrode 
structure during cycling by suppressing i) the dissolution of 
LiPSs in the electrolyte and ii) the uncontrolled redistribu-
tion of sulfur. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
at various states of charge (SOCs) during initial charging was 
performed in three-electrode Swagelok cells (Figure S16, Sup-
porting Information). The Li2S electrodes had the same elec-
trochemical behavior as in two-electrode cells in the oxidation 
process (Figure S17, Supporting Information). Figure 4a shows 
the simplified-contact Randles-equivalent circuit fit EIS of the 
working electrode, where the first semicircle is attributed to 
Li+ migration in the interfaces between the electrodes and elec-
trolyte (Rinterf), and the second semicircle is attributed to the 
charge transfer process (Rct).[14] As shown in Figure 4b, for the 
cell free of TTCA-Li additive, the increased values of Rct and 
Rinterf during charging indicate an unstable reaction due to the 
dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs in the electrolyte. However, 
the cell with TTCA-Li additive possesses smaller and unchanged 
Rct and Rinterf values, indicating that the stable POS coating sup-
presses the dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs in the electro-
lyte. UV–vis spectra further confirm this. The cathodes cycled 
with or without TTCA-Li in the electrolytes at 0.1 C for 5 cycles 
were taken out and soaked in a DOL/DME (1:1 vol.) solution for 
the test (Figure 4c). As shown in Figure 4d, the solution con-
taining the cathode free of TTCA-Li additive has three broad 
peaks at about 300, 400, and 600  nm, corresponding to the 
S62−/ S82− species. However, the solution containing the cathode 
with TTCA-Li additive only has a pair of peaks around 280 and 
320 nm belonging to the residual TTCA-Li (inset of Figure 4d), 
suggesting that the POS coating effectively limits the diffusion 
of LiPSs in the bulk electrolyte, which suppresses the redistri-
bution of Li2S. Morphology characterization by scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) supports this conclusion. Figure  4e 
shows that the Li2S cathode with TTCA-Li has a smooth sur-
face after cycling, indicating a stable structure with the help of 
the formed POS coating. In sharp contrast, the uncoated Li2S 
cathode becomes much rougher and severe structural fracture 
occurs due to the sulfur redistribution caused by the solid-
liquid conversion reaction (Figure 4f).
The cycled Li metal anode in the Li2S-Li battery was also 
characterized to support the above discussion. A smooth, 
uniform, and non-dendritic lithium anode after cycling with 
TTCA-Li additive were observed as shown in Figure 4g. In con-
trast, apparent cracks and large granular features appeared on 
the Li metal anode surface due to uneven deposition and LiPS 
corrosion for the battery free of TTCA-Li additive (Figure 4h). 
XPS profiles show composition differences of the solid elec-
trolyte interface (SEI) on these two anodes (Tables S1 and 
S2, Supporting Information). For the SEI formed in the elec-
trolyte with TTCA-Li, the C-N (398.6  eV in the N 1s spec-
trum, 292.7 eV in the C 1s spectrum) and C-S (289.0 eV in C 
1s spectrum, 161.6  eV/162.6  eV in the S 2p spectrum) peaks 
were seen throughout the SEI (Figure  4i). In addition, SS 
(163.5  eV/164.5  eV in the S 2p spectrum) was also detected, 
proving that the TTCA-Li participated in the formation of the 
SEI. In sharp contrast, these features were not detected on 
the Li anode surface free of TTCA-Li additive (Figure 4j), and 
Li2S, represented by S 2p signals (160.0 /161.0  eV), is always 
observed even after 20 min sputtering, indicating a severe reac-
tion between LiPSs and the Li metal anode. Therefore, TTCA-
Li not only suppresses LiPS shuttling and sulfur redistribution 
through the assistance of POS coating on the cathode side, 
but also participates in the formation of an SEI on the anode, 
thereby minimizing the loss of active sulfur.

We also examined the performance of the battery with a 
Li metal-free anode, which shows promises for practical Li–S 
batteries. We matched Li2S cathodes with pre-lithiated silicon-
based anode to prepare a full battery with a low N/P ratio of 1.2. 
The produced Li2S-Si battery shows similar charge-discharge 
behavior to the above Li2S-Li battery, proving the electrode 
reaction proceeds smoothly in the Li2S-Si battery (Figure S22, 
Supporting Information). The battery with TTCA-Li had a high 
reversible capacity of over 990 mAh g−1 with a retention of 71% 
for 200 cycles at 0.2 C, while the battery free of TTCA-Li only 
had a retention of 32% (Figure 5f). It is worth noting that the 
full cell with TTCA-Li additive still maintained an average CE 
as high as 98.9%, much higher than the additive-free case, sug-
gesting it has great potential to promote the practical uses of 
Li2S-based batteries.
High areal loading up to 7 mg cm−2 was used to show the 
practicality of the POS-coated Li2S cathode. The produced 
battery with TTCA-Li additive had a high initial area capacity 
of over 6 mAh cm−2 at a low electrolyte/Li2S ratio of 10 and a 
reversible capacity was also maintained at 3.32 mAh cm−2 with 
a capacity retention of 79% over 100 cycles at 0.5 C (Figure S23, 
Supporting Information). To be as close as possible to the prac-
tical working conditions, a pouch cell demonstration with the 
same high Li2S loading was used for the evaluation, and as 
shown in Figure 5g, the cell gave an initial capacity as high as 
1196 mAh g−1, and could be stably cycled at 0.2 C for more than 
100 cycles with a capacity retention of 70%.
The aforementioned finding gives a practical solution for 
improving the CE of Li–S batteries with interfacially coated 
Li2S cathodes. A question arises. Does this solution apply for 
normally called Li–S batteries with elemental sulfur cathode? 
Using small amount of TTCA-Li dissolved electrolyte as the 
additive for the cycling pouch cell, we can get the high CE ele-
mental-sulfur-based battery. Detailedly, as shown in Figure 6a, 
we injected the TTCA-Li additive (0.05  m, 100  µL electrolyte) 
into the pouch cell at the fully discharged state (cycling for 
2 cycles), and the cell was then charged at 3.2 V for 1 min to 
form POS coating onto the discharge product Li2S. As shown in 
Figure 6b, the CE of the battery with the injected additive rap-
idly increased to 99.3%, which is much higher than the original 
battery and remained stable in subsequent cycles.
3. Conclusion
We have demonstrated a promising practical solution towards 
a superhigh CE Li–S battery, with the Li2S cathode having a 
stable interfacial coating. An organosulfide of TTCA-Li can 
be used as an electrolyte additive to in situ produce such a 
coating during the initial charging of the Li2S, which decreases 
the oxidation overpotential of Li2S and effectively prevents the 
uncontrolled redistribution of sulfur/Li2S during cycling. The

4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation: Conventional Li–S electrolyte composed of 
1 m LiTFSI and 1 wt.% LiNO3 in DOL and DME (1:1 v/v) was purchased 
from DoDoChem as the blank electrolyte. TTCA was purchased from 
Meryer (Shanghai) Co., Ltd and added to the electrolyte to prepare 
homogeneous solutions with concentrations of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1  m, 
respectively. Then Li foils were added to the solutions to replace the 
protons. After standing for more than 24 h, the light-yellow clear solution 
was TTCA-Li-added electrolyte. For the electrolyte without LiNO3, the 
preparation method was the same.
Electrode Preparation: Commercial Li2S powder (Shanghai Aladdin 
Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) was first mixed with multi-walled 
carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology 
Co., Ltd.) at the weight ratio of 7:3 in a stainless-steel jar sealed in a 
glove box filled with Ar gas, using a high-energy ball mill machine at 
500 rpm. The MWCNTs powder was dried in a vacuum furnace at 70 °C 
for more than 48 h before mixing with the Li2S powder. Zirconia balls 
were used as the milling medium without solvents to reduce the size of 
the Li2S particles and obtain uniform mixing. The weight ratio between 
all milling balls and the Li2S-MWCNTs mixture was set to 15:1. To 
prevent overheating of the system, a rest time of 5 min was added after 
each 5-min ball milling, and the total milling time was 6 h (Figure S24, 
Supporting Information).[15] The Li2S cathodes were prepared by mixing 
Li2S-MWCNTs powder (80 wt.%), MWCNTs powder (10 wt.%) as a 
conductive additive, and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (10 wt.%) as a binder 
dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry, which was 
then coated onto an Al foil and dried at room temperature for 48 h. The 
dried foil was then cut into a circle of diameter 10 mm for use as the 
cathode. The Si anodes were prepared using the method of Chen et 
al.[16] and activated in LiSi half-cells for 5 cycles at a current density of 
200 mA g−1, followed by electrochemical pre-lithiation according to the 
cathode capacity to ensure an N/P ratio of 1.2.
Li2S6 Symmetric Cell: Li2S and S in amounts corresponding to the 
nominal stoichiometry of Li2S6 were added to the electrolyte (1 m LiTFSI 
in DOL and DME (1:1 v/v)) with/without TTCCA-Li and stirred overnight 
at 50 °C. The concentration of the Li2S6 solutions prepared was 0.02 and 
0.2 mol L−1. The CP was punched into small disks with a diameter of 
10.0 mm. Two identical CP electrodes were assembled into a standard 
2032-coin cell, and 40.0 µL Li2S6 electrolyte was added. The cells were 
tested by Autolab workstation, CV curves were obtained at a scan rate of 
10 mV s−1 with a voltage range of −0.8–8 V.
Li-Li Symmetrical Cell: Two identical Li foils were assembled into 
a standard 2032-coin cell, and 100.0  µL Li–S electrolyte with/without 
TTCA-Li was added. Then the cells were cycled on the Neware battery 
test system with different current densities and capacity densities.
Full Cell: CR2032 coin cells were assembled with Li2S cathode, lithium 
metal or Si as anode, Celgard 2500 separator, and Li–S electrolyte 
with/without TTCA-Li. The E/S ratios were about 20 and 10  µL mg−1  
(Li2S-based) for the electrodes with Li2S loadings of 0.5, 1.5, and 7.0 mg 
cm−2, respectively. The thickness of lithium metal anode was 200 µm. 
The N/P ratio of the Li2S-Si battery was controlled at 1.2. The pouch cells 
were assembled with Li2S cathode with a high loading of 7  mg cm−2, 
lithium metal sheets with a thickness of 100 µm as the anode, Celgard 
2500 separator, and the electrolyte containing TTCA-Li. The size of 
the cathode was 1.5×3.5 cm2, which was formed by coating Li2S slurry 
onto carbon felts. The size of the anode was 2.0×4.0 cm2. The current 
collectors were carbon-coated aluminum foil and copper foil. The whole 
device was sealed by aluminum-plastic film. The above operations were 
all done in a glove box filled with Ar gas.
The galvanostatic discharge/charge measurements were conducted 
using a Neware battery test system. The cathode-specific capacities were 
normalized only by the mass of sulfur, as per common practice. For 
Li2S-Li cells, the charge-discharge voltage range was 1.7–2.7 V (first cycle: 
1.7–3.8 V). For Li2S-Si cells, the voltage range was adjusted to 1.0–2.7 V 
(first cycle: 1.0–3.8 V). CV curves of Li2S-Li cells were obtained using an 
Autolab workstation at a scan rate of 0.05 mV s−1 with a potential range 
of 1.7–3.8 V (vs Li+/Li).
Electrochemical Characterization: LSV was tested by using an Autolab 
workstation coupled with the RDE technique. 10  µL of 2  mg mL−1 
MWCNTs ink (made by sonicating 2  mg MWCNTs in 1  mL ethanol 
and 20 µL 5 wt.% Nafion solution) was drop-cast onto a glassy carbon 
electrode (0.196 cm2) to form a flat film electrode with a loading of 
0.1 mg cm−2. The electrochemical test was performed in a three-electrode 
open-cell with the sweep rate of 10 mV s−1 in the voltage range of −0.8 to 
0.8 V (vs Ag/AgCl), using the MWCNT film, 0.02 m Li2S6/electrolyte (1 m 
LiTFSI in DOL and DME (1:1 v/v)) with/without TTCCA-Li, a platinum 
sheet, and Ag/AgCl electrodes as the working electrode, electrolytes, 
counter, and reference electrodes, respectively.
The RDE in the above system was replaced with a rotating ring-
disk electrode (RRDE) and CHI760e workstation was used to test. The 
MWCNT film of the same composition as above was used as disk 
electrode, and platinum ring as ring electrode. The potential on the disk 
electrode was held at −0.8 V (vs Ag/AgCl), and LSV test was performed 
on ring electrode with the sweep rate of 10 mV s−1 in the voltage range of 
−0.8 to 0.8 V (vs Ag/AgCl).
EIS was measured on the Autolab workstation between 100 kHz and 
0.01 Hz with three-electrode stainless steel Swagelok cells. Li2S cathode 
was used as the working electrode and Li foils were used as counter 
electrode and reference electrode.[17]
In Situ Raman Spectroscopy: Li2S-Li coin cells with a quartz window 
and a hole in the stainless steel were used for the in situ Raman 
spectroscopy analysis at 532  nm Laser. Each spectrum was acquired 
for 20 s with a frequency of 5%. The Li2S cathodes were prepared by 
dropping a slurry, which contained Li2S-MWCNTs powder (80 wt.%), 
MWCNTs (10 wt.%), and PVDF (10 wt.%) dissolved in NMP, onto 
carbon cloth and allowed to be dried at room temperature for 48 h. The 
cells (Li2S loading: 1.5  mg cm−2) were tested at a rate of 0.05 C with 
Ivium workstation.
Materials Characterization: SEM was performed on a Regulus 8100 
(Hitachi Japan) instrument with an accelerating voltage of 5.0  kV. 
HRTEM measurements were conducted on a JEM 2100F coupled with 
an energy dispersive spectrometer. The Raman spectra were recorded 
on a Raman system (Horiba Lab RAM HR800) with an argon ion laser 
(532 nm). XRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D-8 diffractometer 
(Cu Kα radiation, λ = 0.154 nm) at room temperature. XPS data were 
recorded by an ESCALAB 250Xi (Thermo Fisher) with a monochromatic 
Al Ka source to analyze the surface species and their chemical states. 
UV–vis adsorption tests were carried out by a Thermo UV-visible 
spectrophotometer. To characterize the concentration of TTCA-Li in Li–S 
cells after cycling, the cell with 20 µL electrolyte was disassembled and 
soaked in 3980 µL solvent, and 200 µL of the above solution was diluted 
10 times after 2 h for testing.

using density functional theory method as implemented in the VASP 
code. The electronic exchange-correlation energy was modeled using
