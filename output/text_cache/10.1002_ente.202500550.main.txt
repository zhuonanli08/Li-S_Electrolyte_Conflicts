Enhancing Safety in Li–S Batteries: Mitigating Thermal
Instabilities via Electrolyte and Separator Pair Optimization
Divyansh Pandey, Kritika Parihar, and Deepak Punetha*
1. Introduction
The future of electric vehicles (EVs) in India is poised for signif-
icant expansion, driven by a convergence of government policies,
evolving market dynamics and rising consumer environmental
awareness. As one of the fastest-growing economies with a
rapidly expanding middle class, India represents a promising
market for large-scale EV adoption.[1–3] The Indian government
has implemented ambitious policies under schemes such as the
Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Hybrid and Electric
Vehicles (FAME) and the National Electric Mobility Mission
Plan (NEMMP) 2020, aiming to decarbonize the transportation
sector. These initiatives, supported by incentives like subsidies,
tax relief, and reduced GST rates, have enhanced the ﬁnancial
viability of EVs. Concurrently, considerable
efforts are underway to develop essential
infrastructure such as public charging
stations and battery-swapping networks to
support
their
widespread
integration.
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been piv-
otal in enabling the commercial success of
EVs due to their high energy density, spe-
ciﬁc capacity, low self-discharge rate, and
long cycle life. Commercialized in 1991,
LIBs outperformed earlier chemistries like
lead–acid and nickel-based batteries. Lead–
acid batteries, though used for auxiliary
functions, lacked sufﬁcient speciﬁc energy
and energy density (up to 40 Wh kg1 and
90 Wh L1) to power EVs unlike modern
LIBs
(260 Wh kg1
and
700 Wh L1).[4]
Ni–Zn batteries offered high discharge
rates and thermal stability[5] but were lim-
ited by low energy density and lifespan.[6]
Ni-MH batteries improved upon this with
a speciﬁc energy of 60–120 Wh kg1,[7]
yet still trailed LIBs. Metal–air batteries
(e.g., Al–air, Zn–air) promise 3–30 times higher energy densities
than LIBs, though their practical use in EVs is still constrained
by recharging and stability challenges. Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batter-
ies have emerged as a highly promising next-generation energy
storage technology, primarily due to their theoretical speciﬁc energy
(≈2600 Wh kg1), cost-effectiveness, and environmental sustain-
ability. Despite their potential, practical implementation is currently
constrained by challenges such as polysulﬁde shuttling, poor con-
ductivity, and electrode degradation.[8] Sulfur, being abundant and
low-cost, contributes to reduced battery production costs and is
environmentally benign relative to materials used in conventional
LIBs. These characteristics make Li–S batteries attractive for high-
energy applications in EVs and aerospace. The separator plays
a critical role in Li–S battery performance and safety, acting
both as a physical barrier between the electrodes and a control
medium for polysulﬁde migration. It must exhibit excellent
mechanical strength, porosity, ﬂexibility, and ionic conductivity.
Conventional polyoleﬁn separators (e.g., polyethylene and polypro-
pylene) offer low cost and chemical stability but perform poorly
under elevated temperatures and in mitigating polysulﬁde diffu-
sion.[9] This can lead to severe capacity fading and increased safety
risks.[3] Advanced separators incorporating nonpolyoleﬁn polymers,
ceramics, nanoﬁbers, or modiﬁed conventional membranes have
been proposed to overcome these limitations.[10] Materials such
as naﬁon, polyacrylonitrile, poly(acrylic acid), polyvinylidene ﬂuo-
ride, polyethylene oxide (PEO), poly(methyl methacrylate), and pol-
yetherimide have shown potential in improving mechanical and

Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising candidates for next-generation
energy storage, offering high theoretical energy density (2567 Wh kg1) and
speciﬁc capacity (1675 mAh g1), with environmentally friendly sulfur cathodes.
However, their practical deployment faces challenges such as polysulﬁde
shuttling, short cycle life, and high self-discharge—issues exacerbated by thermal
instability. Excess heat can degrade performance and trigger thermal runaway,
compromising safety. This study investigates the impact of electrolyte and
separator selection on thermal behavior, using dimethyl ether, dimethoxy ethane,
dimethoxy sulﬁde, and acetonitrile as electrolytes, paired with boron nitride,
titanium dioxide, and polyethylene glycol as separators. Thermal metrics such as
peak temperatures (319.025 K–328.437 K), internal resistance, and rate of heat
change are evaluated, along with electrochemical performance (energy stored:
22.027 kJ; voltage range: 2.38 V to 1.7 V; electrolyte potential: 93.85 mV to
11.01 mV). The results highlight that strategic electrolyte–separator pairing can
effectively mitigate heat generation, advancing the safety and reliability of Li–S
batteries in demanding energy applications.

electrochemical properties. Polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based coat-
ings enhance lithium-ion transport and electrolyte uptake by
increasing hydrophilicity,[11] while carbon-based additives like
Super P, graphene, and carbon nanotubes improve electronic con-
ductivity and suppress polysulﬁde migration.[12] Reduced graphene
oxide (rGO) enhances surface area and offers chemical adsorption

and boron nitride (BN) contributes to thermal management and
lithium metal stabilization by blocking polysulﬁde diffusion.[15]
Electrolyte selection is equally crucial in Li–S battery design.
Carbonate-based solvents, such as ethylene carbonate (EC), diethyl
carbonate, propylene carbonate, and dimethyl carbonate, com-
monly used in LIBs, are generally incompatible with sulfur cath-
odes due to parasitic reactions with polysulﬁdes. Nonetheless, they
may be employed when sulfur is embedded in nanoporous struc-
tures or polymer matrices. Ether-based solvents such as dime-
thoxyethane (DME) and dioxolane are more suitable for Li–S
systems owing to their chemical compatibility with polysulﬁdes
and ability to enable complete redox reactions.[16] Among ethers,
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether offers superior solvating
power.[17] Alternatives such as ionic liquids (ILs) and sulfones have
also been explored, providing beneﬁts such as thermal stability,
low volatility, and suppression of polysulﬁde solubility. ILs like
[TFSI]-based compounds improve battery cyclability and capacity
retention by limiting polysulﬁde dissolution.[18] Less volatile sol-
vents such as NMP and dimethoxy sulﬁde (DMSO) are being
evaluated for their favorable dielectric properties, low toxicity,
and cost-effectiveness in Li–S conﬁgurations.
Thermal management in Li–S batteries is as critical as opti-
mizing capacity and energy density. Heat generation correlates
directly with energy loss and reduced material efﬁciency. To
improve the electronic conductivity of sulfur cathodes, carbon
additives are used; however, the sulfur–carbon composite poses
safety concerns due to its ﬂammability and reactivity at elevated
temperatures.[19] Excessive internal temperatures can lead to sep-
arator failure (e.g., melting of polyethylene or polypropylene) and
trigger thermal runaway.[20] Heat generation in Li–S batteries
arises from three primary sources: 1) ohmic losses in the elec-
trolyte, governed by the Nernst–Planck equation; 2) electrochem-
ical reaction enthalpy at the electrode–electrolyte interface; and
3) overpotentials, including activation and concentration polari-
zation. While polysulﬁde shuttling is a known challenge in Li–S
systems, its effects were intentionally excluded in the present
model to simplify the computational framework and isolate ther-
mal behavior. This modeling decision enables focused analysis of
heat generation and dissipation under controlled discharge con-
ditions, thereby supporting the evaluation of material-dependent
thermal responses. The study aims to investigate how thermal
parameters inﬂuence overall cell performance, particularly with
respect to power density, energy density, and energy storage.
2. Theoretical Approach
2.1. Model Development
The study has been performed over COMSOL Multiphysics
Simulation software by designing a 1D hypothetical Li–S
model using the inbuilt electrochemistry -> tertiary current
distribution, Nernst–Planck interface module, and heat trans-
fer in ﬂuids module in combination to design and simulate the
thermal behavior of the cell, involving two types of studies
namely, current distribution initialization and time-dependent
study.

3. Results and Discussions
3.1. 1-D Li–S Electrochemical Cell Model
The design of the model involves 1D geometry, as shown in
Figure 1, developed using the electrochemical heating multiphy-
sics interface in COMSOL Multiphysics Version 6.2. The param-
eters used in the study have been listed in Table 1. The separator
and the positive (cathode during discharge) porous electrode are
deﬁned as domains in the geometry. The leftmost boundary rep-
resents the negative lithium metal electrode, and the rightmost
boundary represents the positive metal current collector. The
electrolyte mass and charge transport are modeled using the
Nernst–Planck equations with electroneutrality. The charge
transfer reactions are deﬁned using the Nernst equation in com-
bination with the Butler–Volmer equation, assuming the law of
mass action. In addition to the earlier six polysulﬁde species, the
species Liþ and A are included in the electrolyte, where A is
the counter anion of the lithium salt. The same species are also
present in the separator domain. Two precipitation–dissolution
(non-Faradaic) reactions involving the solid species S8 and
Li2S are included in the model (in both domains). The model
is based on the Nernst–Planck model, which assumes electro-
neutrality for which it is assumed that one of the ions is inde-
pendent of its concentration, which is reasonably the counter
anion [A1] because it has a relatively high concentration and
behaves as a spectator ion, while the electrode reactions take
place. The tertiary current distribution, Nernst–Planck inter-
face, has been used wherein the model includes the separator
and the porous electrode, and it has been deﬁned that the reac-
tion takes place in their domain. In the separator as well as the
electrolyte, it has been only the precipitation equation that
leads to the precipitation of Li2S and S8; however, it is a point
to note that ﬁve sequential reactions have occurred correspond-
ingly in the cathode. The following ﬁve charge transfer reac-
tions and six participating polysulﬁde species are considered

The model has been designed with certain limitations that

of the model, being 1D is deﬁned over a length, i.e., from 0(ori-

2) The operating voltage of the model is 1.7 V, if during model
simulation, the voltage dips below 1.7 V, the processing termi-
nates. 3) The current density drawn from the cell for a ﬁxed dis-
charge rate is given constant which is at 1C discharge rate, and
the current density is 6.13 A m2. 4) To focus on the primary
thermal behavior of different separator–electrolyte combinations,
the model omits cross-electrode polysulﬁde transport (shuttling
effect). While this exclusion limits direct applicability to long-cycle
degradation behavior, it enables isolated thermal benchmarking
and reveals intrinsic material-dependent thermal proﬁles.

Separators in Li–S batteries play a critical role in preventing
short circuits while allowing lithium-ion ﬂow. Their sustainabil-
ity can be assessed based on production impact, recyclability, and
stability.
3.7. Polyethylene Glycol
PEG is derived from ethylene glycol, a petroleum-based chemi-
cal. However, it is biodegradable and used in pharmaceuticals,
showing promise as an eco-friendly material. Studies show that
PEG-based separators can enhance battery life while maintaining
environmental compatibility. PEG can be synthesized in a green
manner, and it has relatively low toxicity. Its polymeric nature
can also be adapted to recycling processes.[21]
3.8. Boron Nitride
BN is a highly stable ceramic material. While not biodegradable,
it offers excellent thermal and chemical stability, enhancing the
longevity of the battery and reducing the need for frequent
replacement. BN requires signiﬁcant energy to produce but
offers minimal toxicity and long-term environmental damage.
It is recyclable but requires specialized processes, reducing its
overall environmental friendliness.[22]
3.9. Titanium Dioxide (TiO2)
Extraction and reﬁnement of TiO2 have signiﬁcant environmen-
tal costs. TiO2 has moderate toxicity concerns related to the pro-
duction process, though it is considered a relatively safe material.
It is not easily biodegradable, and recycling TiO2 from battery
separators remains complex.

battery safety, performance, and environmental footprint. Their

3.10. Dimethyl Ether
DME is considered a clean fuel and can be produced from renew-

It exhibits high ionic conductivity in Li–S batteries and reduces

DMOE is a solvent with moderate environmental impact. It per-

from petroleum can contribute to carbon emissions.[24]
3.12. DMSO
DMSO is a sulfur-containing compound, meaning its production
can release harmful sulfur oxides (SOx). It has been found to pro-
vide high performance in Li–S batteries, but its environmental sus-
tainability is limited. DMSO is less eco-friendly due to its sulfur
content and the potential formation of hazardous byproducts.[25]
3.13. Acetonitrile
Acetonitrile is a solvent that can be used in many electrochemical
systems, including Li–S batteries. However, it is highly toxic and
poses environmental hazards during its production and disposal.
Acetonitrile is considered harmful to both health and the envi-
ronment. While effective in terms of battery performance, its tox-
icity makes it the least environmentally friendly option.[26,27]

4. Conclusion
This study investigated the thermal and electrochemical perfor-
mance of twelve separator–electrolyte combinations in Li–S batter-
ies using a 1D COMSOL Multiphysics model. Among these, the
BN–DME conﬁguration emerged as the most effective, exhibiting
a low peak temperature of 319.52 K, high thermal stability, and
minimal internal resistance (0.0326 Ω). It also achieved a maxi-
mum voltage of 2.38 V and a stable plateau at 2.025 V—among
the highest recorded. While the TiO2–DMSO pair showed the
slowest temperature rise rate, it underperformed in voltage and
resistance metrics. BN–DME also offers a favorable environmental
proﬁle compared to acetonitrile- or TiO2-based systems, support-
ing its sustainability. Though mechanical durability and long-term
cycling were not simulated, prior studies indicate BN–DME pro-
vides high ionic conductivity, better heat dissipation, and partial
suppression of polysulﬁde shuttling—factors beneﬁcial for cycle
life and structural reliability. The model also delivered energy stor-
age and power density results, with energy peaking at 22.157 kJ
and stepwise power behavior indicating consistent electrochemical
activity. A Ragone plot comparison with experimental data con-
ﬁrmed the model’s predictive reliability. Overall, the BN–DME
conﬁguration offers an optimal balance of safety, thermal perfor-
mance, and efﬁciency for advanced Li–S battery applications.
