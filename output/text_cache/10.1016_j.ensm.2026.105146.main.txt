A B S T R A C T
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries suffer from complex degradation pathways arising from polysulfide dissolution, 
Li-metal corrosion, and electrolyte depletion, which severely limit long-term durability. While extensive efforts 
have focused on suppressing shuttle effects or stabilizing electrode interfaces, the fundamental role of electrolyte 
solvation ability in governing these coupled degradation processes remains insufficiently understood. Herein, we 
recognize that these degradation phenomena are closely associated with electrolyte solvation ability, thereby 
motivating the design of electrolytes using solvents with distinct molecular structures to systematically modulate 
the solvation environment in Li–S batteries. Spectroscopic analyses combined with theoretical calculations 
demonstrate that electrolyte solvation ability is an effective tuning parameter for Li+–solvent interaction and 
polysulfide–solvent affinity. Notably, methoxy cyclopentane (MCP) attenuates solvent interactions with Li+ and 
polysulfide through steric hindrance from its bulky cyclopentyl substituent, thereby establishing a solvation 
environment that suppresses polysulfide-driven degradation. This solvation behavior reduces polysulfide 
dissolution and transport, mitigates polysulfide-induced Li-metal corrosion, and minimizes continuous electro­
lyte consumption. Concurrently, reduced solvent involvement in the Li+ solvation environment facilitates rapid 
Li+ desolvation and enhances interfacial charge-transfer kinetics, which in turn promotes the formation of a 
dense, inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the Li-metal surface, ultimately leading to improved 
electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries. This work provides a solvation-centered mechanistic under­
standing of coupled degradation pathways in Li–S batteries and establishes electrolyte solvation ability as a key 
design parameter for achieving durable and reliable Li–S battery systems.
1. Introduction
The transition to a sustainable, low-carbon society demands 
advanced energy storage technologies, with rechargeable batteries 
playing a central role. Among various next-generation candidates, lith­
ium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have gained considerable attention owing to 
their exceptionally high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, 
combined with the natural abundance, low cost, and environmental 
benignity of sulfur [1–4]. In contrast to conventional lithium–ion bat­
teries (LIBs) based on intercalation chemistry, Li–S batteries operate 
through a dissolution-precipitation mechanism, where elemental sulfur 
is progressively reduced to soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and ul­
timately converted into insoluble Li2S during discharge [5–7]. This 
multistep reaction provides a high theoretical capacity of 1675 mAh g−1. 
Furthermore, the Li-metal anodes (LMAs) offer an ultrahigh specific 
capacity of 3860 mAh g−1 and the lowest electrochemical potential 
(−3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode) [8–10]. The combination of 
sulfur cathodes and LMAs therefore presents intrinsic advantages for 
achieving energy densities that surpass those of state-of-the-art LIBs.
Despite these advantages, the implementation of Li–S batteries re­
mains hindered by several critical challenges. The intrinsic insulating 
nature of sulfur and its final discharge product Li2S restricts electron

tion, the soluble LiPSs intermediates generated during cycling readily 
diffuse through the electrolyte and migrate between electrodes, giving 
rise to the so-called shuttle effect, which induces parasitic reactions, loss 
of active sulfur, and low Coulombic efficiency (CE) [13–16]. At the same 
time, LMAs suffer from uneven Li deposition, dendritic growth, and 
formation of an unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer, all of 
which compromise both safety and cycle stability [17–19]. Collectively, 
these issues present substantial barriers to translating the high theoret­
ical promise of Li–S batteries into high-performance energy storage 
devices.
Electrolytes play a decisive role in governing the electrochemical 
behavior of Li–S batteries, as they regulate ion transport, mediate sulfur 
redox reactions, and determine interfacial stability at both electrodes. In 
particular, the solubility of LiPSs and the solvation environment of Li+, 
strongly influenced by the solvation ability of electrolytes, are critical 
factors that dictate the reversibility of sulfur conversion reactions. 
Owing to this importance, various electrolyte design strategies have 
been investigated, including fluorinated ether-based electrolytes, high 
concentration electrolytes (HCEs) [20,21], localized high concentration 
electrolytes (LHCEs) [22–24], and ionic liquid electrolytes [25,26]. 
More recently, significant progress has been achieved by tailoring 
solvent solvating power to regulate Li+ solvation structures and inter­
facial chemistry for stabilizing LMAs [27–29]. These approaches have 
demonstrated significant progress in mitigating polysulfide shuttling 
and stabilizing the SEI layers, but they are often limited by drawbacks 
such as reduced overall capacity and cyclability caused by high viscos­
ity, low ionic conductivity, and incomplete polysulfide conversion. 
Nevertheless, most of these studies have primarily focused on sup­
pressing shuttle effects and enhancing interfacial stability, while the 
fundamental impact of electrolyte solvation ability on evolution of sol­
vation environments, Li-metal corrosion, and long-term electrolyte 
consumption has remained largely underexplored.
In this study, we systematically investigated the influence of solvent 
molecular structures on solvation ability and the resulting changes in the 
solvation environment. We further examined how such modifications 
affect the shuttle effect, Li-metal corrosion, interfacial chemistry, and 
electrolyte consumption (Fig. 1). Through this approach, we aimed to 
establish a more comprehensive understanding of rational electrolyte 
design for Li–S batteries. To validate these strategies, we adopted a 
widely used ether-based electrolyte composed of 1 M lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethane sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) and 0.1 M lithium nitrate 
(LiNO3) dissolved in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dime­
thoxy ethane (DME) (1:1, vol%) as the baseline. To tailor its solvation

For clarity, the baseline electrolyte is denoted as DOL-E, and the 
designed electrolytes are denoted as THF-E and MCP-E throughout this 
work. To examine this concept, we combined spectroscopic character­
ization with theoretical calculations. THF-E exhibits the highest solva­
tion ability, consistent with previous reports and attributable to its 
strong Lewis basicity [30]. Consequently, the elevated solubility of LiPSs 
in THF-E increases the concentration of dissolved species, thereby 
leading to severe shuttle effect that promotes parasitic reactions with 
LMAs and ultimately causes persistent Li-metal corrosion. Moreover, 
THF strongly coordinates with Li+, resulting in a solvent-dominated 
solvation environment that induces excessive solvent decomposition 
during initial SEI formation. This process generates an unstable 
solvent-dominant SEI layer, while further accelerating electrolyte con­
sumption arising from continuous decomposition during prolonged 
cycling. In contrast, MCP-E shows the lowest solvation ability, which can 
be ascribed to the steric hindrance caused by the bulky cyclopentyl 
substituent. Accordingly, the reduced solubility of LiPSs alleviates the 
shuttle effect and mitigates Li-metal corrosion. Furthermore, the 
enhanced anion participation in the Li+ solvation environment in MCP-E 
promotes the formation of a dense and uniform inorganic-dominant SEI 
layer, which minimizes continuous electrolyte consumption during 
long-term operation. These distinct solvation environments lead to 
marked differences in electrochemical performance. The cell with THF-E 
suffers rapid capacity fade due to severe shuttle effect and electrolyte 
consumption, whereas the cell with MCP-E maintains stable cycling with 
improved Li reversibility and durable interfacial stability over pro­
longed cycling. In summary, this work demonstrates that the solvation 
ability of electrolytes is closely associated with various degradation 
pathways in Li–S batteries. This perspective suggests that electrolyte 
design strategies are expected to extend beyond suppressing the shuttle 
effect and ensuring interfacial stability to further encompass long-term 
durability and reliable operation.

through combined computational and spectroscopic analyses. As shown 
in Fig. 2a, the electrostatic potential (ESP) maps obtained from density 
functional theory (DFT) calculations visualize the intramolecular elec­

reflecting high electron density and a strong tendency to coordinate with 
Li+. Although molecular structural variations were expected to induce 
discernible differences in electron density, the ESP analysis reveals 
comparable negative potentials around the oxygen atoms across the 
solvents, demonstrating that ESP analysis alone is insufficient to fully 
capture subtle differences in Li+–solvent interactions. To more accu­
rately evaluate Li+–solvent affinity under realistic electrolyte conditions 
involving multiple solvent coordination, solvation free energies were 
calculated using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 2b). The 
results show that THF exhibits the most negative solvation free energy 
(−105.6 kcal mol−1), indicating a stronger affinity for Li+ compared to 
DOL (−99.1 kcal mol−1) across the three solvents. This behavior can be 
attributed to structural differences between the two solvents. Although 
both THF and DOL adopt five-membered ring structures, the single ox­
ygen atom in THF is less constrained by ring stabilization than the two 
oxygen atoms in DOL, resulting in higher Lewis basicity and stronger Li+
coordination [30]. In contrast, MCP shows the least negative solvation 
free energy (−88.6 kcal mol−1), which can be rationalized by steric 
hinderance arising from its bulky cyclopentyl group, effectively limiting 
Li+ approach and coordination.
To investigate how differences in solvation ability of solvent influ­
ence the solvation environment of electrolytes, 1H–7Li heteronuclear 
overhauser enhancement spectroscopy (HOESY) was analyzed to 
compare the intermolecular correlations between Li+ and the solvents 
(Fig. 2c–e and S1). Distinct cross-peaks between the protons of DME (Ha, 
Hb) and 7Li were consistently detected across all electrolytes, demon­
strating that DME forms the primary solvation environment of Li+. In 
contrast, we observe that the interaction strength between Li+ and the 
solvents (THF, DOL, and MCP) differs markedly according to their sol­
vation abilities. THF-E exhibits the most prominent cross-peak origi­
nating from the H1 and H2 protons of THF, whereas the corresponding 
signals associated with DOL in DOL-E are less pronounced. Notably, in 
MCP-E, cross-peaks to correlations between the MCP protons (H1, H2, 
H3) and 7Li are nearly undetectable. These observations reveal a pro­
gressive attenuation of Li+–solvent interaction in the order THF-E >
DOL-E > MCP-E. This feature is attributed to the substantial steric 
hindrance of MCP, which limits its ability to coordinate Li+, and is 
consistent with the calculated solvation free energy results.
Raman spectroscopy was conducted to elucidate the solvation envi­
ronments of the electrolytes with solvents of different solvation abilities 
(Fig. 2f). Due to the influence of solvent solvation ability on the char­
acteristics and distribution of ion associations within the electrolytes, 
the coordination environment was examined using the characteristic 
S–N–S stretching vibration of TFSI-. In this spectral region, the peaks can 
be assigned to solvent-separated ion pair (SSIP, ≈738.5 cm−1), contact 
ion pair (CIP, ≈743.0 cm−1), and aggregate (AGG, ≈750.0 cm−1) [31]. 
SSIP represents that the TFSI- is dissociated by one or more solvents, 
whereas CIP and AGG correspond to the coordination of one or multiple 
Li+ with the anion, respectively. As the solvent solvation ability in­
creases, the S–N–S band exhibits a gradual red shift, reflecting changes 
in the Li+–anion coordination environment. THF-E is predominantly 
composed of the SSIP fraction (71.3%). The absence of any detectable 
band near ≈750.0 cm−1, which corresponds to AGG clusters, indicates 
that these fractions are essentially negligible, suggesting that a large 
proportion of the Li+ solvation shell in THF-E is occupied by solvents. In 
comparison, DOL-E exhibits a notable decrease in SSIP (28.9%) along 
with an increase in CIP (57.6%) and the appearance of AGG (13.5%). 
Notably, MCP-E exhibits a dominant presence of AGG fractions, as 
evidenced by SSIP, CIP, and AGG ratios of 4.5%, 46.5%, and 49.0%. 
(Fig. S2). These results suggest that most anions in MCP-E participate in 
the Li+ solvation shell, which can be attributed to the weak coordination 
between Li+ and MCP solvent. The 7Li and 19F NMR spectroscopy were 
performed to more clearly investigate the solvation environments of the 
electrolytes. The peak in the 7Li spectra progressively shifts upfield from 
THF-E to MCP-E, reflecting an increase in the shielding effect around Li+
[32–34]. Such behavior can be interpreted as resulting from the 
enhanced Li+–TFSI- interaction induced by the lower solvation ability of 
MCP (Fig. 2g). Consistently, the 19F NMR spectra exhibit a downfield 
shift, suggesting enhanced electron density surrounding TFSI-, in 
agreement with the 7Li NMR results [35]. The combined evolution of the 
7Li and 19F chemical shifts reveals that the low solvation ability of the 
electrolyte promotes Li+–TFSI- coordination primarily governed by 
ion-pair associations, which is consistent with the Raman spectra 
(Fig. 2h). Accordingly, these results suggest that the weakened 
Li+–solvent interaction arising from the low solvation ability of MCP 
promotes the formation of an anion-rich solvation environment.
To further examine how such differences in solvation environment 
influence the electrolyte properties, the ionic conductivity was 
measured (Fig. S3). MCP-E exhibits relatively lower ionic conductivity 
compared to THF-E, which is consistent with its weak solvating nature. 
In contrast, the viscosity of the electrolytes (Fig. S4) and the contact 
angle on the polypropylene (PP) separator (Fig. S5) show no significant 
differences among the systems, owing to the comparable intrinsic vis­
cosities of the solvents. The Li+ transference number (tLi
+) was also 
evaluated to further probe the influence of the solvation environment on 
Li+ transport (Fig. S6). Notably, MCP-E exhibits a higher tLi
+ (0.67) 
compared to DOL-E (0.53) and THF-E (0.44), indicating a progressively 
increased contribution of Li+ to the overall ion conduction as the sol­
vation ability decreases. This trend can be attributed to the weakened 
Li+–solvent interaction in MCP-E, where the low solvating ability re­
duces the coordination strength between Li+ and solvent molecules, 
facilitating Li+ transport.
2.2. Interfacial kinetics and Li plating/stripping reversibility of LMAs
Li deposition behavior at the LMAs critically influences the perfor­
mance and safety of Li–S batteries. Unstable Li plating/stripping 
behavior directly undermines interfacial stability and limits the revers­
ibility of Li deposition. Such non-uniform deposition accelerates den­
dritic growth while simultaneously decreasing CE [36,37]. To quantify 
the activation energy associated with Li plating/stripping behavior, 
temperature-dependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
was carried out using Li||Li symmetric cells (Fig. S7). The activation 
energy was extracted by applying Arrhenius plots to the temperature 
dependence of the charge-transfer resistance (Rct), which reflects 
interfacial processes related to Li+ desolvation from its solvation sheath 
(Table S1) [38]. As shown in Fig. 3a, activation energy analysis reveals 
that MCP-E exhibits the lowest value of 42.8 kJ mol−1, which is mark­
edly lower than those obtained for THF-E (50.2 kJ mol−1) and DOL-E 
(47.6 kJ mol−1). This pronounced reduction is closely related to the 
low solvation ability of the MCP solvent, which reduces the energy 
requirement for Li+ desolvation and thereby facilitates more efficient 
interfacial charge-transfer processes. To further elucidate this behavior, 
a schematic illustration of the interfacial desolvation process is pre­
sented in Fig. S8, constructed based on previously reported interfacial 
desolvation mechanisms [39–42]. In MCP-E, the weak solvating nature 
of the solvent results in reduced Li+–solvent coordination, enabling Li+
to be more easily released from its solvation sheath at the electrode 
interface. The desolvation process becomes thus energetically more 
favorable, consistent with the experimentally observed lower activation 
energy. Interfacial kinetics in Li||Li symmetric cells can be quantita­
tively evaluated using the exchange current density (i0). Current–volt­
age responses were analyzed based on a simplified Butler–Volmer 
relationship [43,44], where the applied current was systematically

2.3. Polysulfide solvation environment in electrolytes
Polysulfides dissolution is widely recognized as a major factor 
responsible for the degradation of sulfur-based materials [49]. To 
evaluate how the solvation ability of the electrolytes governs the 
dissolution behavior of LiPSs, stoichiometric amounts of S8 and Li2S 
were added to each electrolyte to prepare 1 M Li2S6 solutions. As shown 
in Fig. 4a, the Li2S6 solution in THF-E exhibits the darkest color, whereas 
the solutions become progressively lighter as the solvation ability de­
creases from DOL-E to MCP-E. The darker coloration of the Li2S6 solu­
tion in THF-E indicates a higher concentration of dissolved LiPSs, 
demonstrating that higher solvation ability leads to increased Li2S6 
dissolution. In particular, the pale hue observed for the Li2S6 solution in 
MCP-E reflects the lowest solubility of LiPSs compared to the other so­
lutions, suggesting a limited affinity between MCP solvent and LiPSs.
To gain molecular-level insight into the interactions between LiPSs 
and electrolyte solvents, MD simulations were carried out (Fig. 4). In 
these simulations, representative LiPSs species (Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8) 
were examined in different electrolyte environments, and their solvation 
free energies were calculated to quantitatively assess solvent–LiPSs 
interaction strengths (Fig. 4b). Regardless of polysulfide chain length, 
THF-E consistently exhibits the most negative solvation free energies, 
indicating strong affinity between THF solvents and LiPSs species. In 
contrast, the solvation free energies in DOL-E are generally less negative, 
reflecting comparatively weaker interactions between LiPSs and the 
DOL solvents. A markedly different trend is observed for MCP-E. For this 
electrolyte, Li2S4 (−36.1 kcal mol−1), Li2S6 (−46.4 kcal mol−1), and 
Li2S8 (−54.1 kcal mol−1) exhibit the least negative solvation free en­
ergies relative to the other electrolytes. These results can be attributed to 
steric hindrance arising from the bulky cyclopentyl substituent in MCP, 
which restricts close approach of negatively charged LiPSs species. The 
calculated solvation energetics are in good agreement with experimental 
LiPSs solubility measurements, collectively indicating a reduced affinity 
of MCP toward polysulfides.
To more faithfully reproduce the solution conditions relevant to Li–S 
batteries, 1 M Li2S6 was explicitly introduced into the electrolyte models 
during the MD simulations. This approach enables a more realistic 
representation of the LiPSs solvation environment and provides detailed 
molecular insight into LiPSs-related electrochemical behavior under 
battery-relevant conditions. On this basis, radial distribution functions 
(RDFs) between Li+, TFSI-, solvent molecules, and the polysulfide spe­
cies were calculated to quantify the local coordination environment. 
Specifically, taking S6
2- as the reference species, the RDFs of ions and 
solvent molecules around S6
2- were analyzed to characterize its solvation 
environment in different electrolytes (Fig. 4c–e). For 1 M Li2S6 in THF-E, 
the S6

strong interactions between THF solvents and polysulfide species. The 
intensity of this peak is noticeably higher than those obtained for the 
S6

2–OMCP RDFs in MCP-E under the same 
Li2S6 concentration. In contrast, the S6

solvation environment emerges in the MCP-E. The S6

significantly attenuated, while the S6

the Li+ solvation environment changes markedly in the MCP-E. The 
Li+–OMCP RDFs peak intensity is significantly reduced, while the 
Li+–OTFSI- RDFs become notably enhanced. This shift in coordination 
indicates a diminished involvement of solvent molecules and a greater 
participation of anions in shaping the Li+ solvation shell, leading to an 
anion-involved solvation structure in MCP-E.
The simulation snapshots in Fig. 4f–h highlight clear variations in 
the spatial distribution of Li+–TFSI- ion pairs and Li2S6 species across 1 
M Li2S6 in electrolytes. In the THF-E and DOL-E, both ion pairs and 
polysulfide species are broadly and uniformly dispersed throughout the 
simulation box. By contrast, the MCP-E shows distinct spatial aggrega­
tion, with Li+–TFSI- ion pairs and Li2S6 species preferentially concen­
trated in localized regions. This distribution behavior is consistent with 
the experimental trends observed in Raman and NMR analyses. To 
obtain deeper insight into the solvation environment, representative 
microscopic configurations of LiPSs species are shown in Fig. 4i–k. In the 
1 M Li2S6 in THF-E, multiple small ion clusters are distributed 
throughout the system. In contrast, the 1 M Li2S6 in MCP-E features a 
large, aggregated cluster comprising most of the TFSI⁻ and Li2S6 species. 
This marked difference in microscopic organization reflects the reduced 
solubility of Li2S6 and LiTFSI in the MCP-E. Overall, the aggregation 
behavior revealed by the simulations closely mirrors the experimentally 
measured LiPSs solubility trends, underscoring strong consistency be­
tween theoretical predictions and experimental observations. Collec­
tively, these findings suggest that MCP-E maintains low polysulfide 
solubility while providing a solvation environment conducive to favor­
able interfacial processes, supporting its potential as an effective elec­
trolyte design strategy for Li–S batteries.

To clarify how electrolyte solvation ability governs the suppression 
of polysulfide shuttling and influences the overall electrochemical sta­
bility of Li–S cells, cycling performance was evaluated at 0.3 C within 
1.7–2.8 V (Fig. 5h). The cell with MCP-E delivers a high specific capacity 
of 709.4 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles with a capacity retention of 69.5%, 
while maintaining an average CE of 99.3%. In contrast, the cell with 
DOL-E exhibits rapid capacity decay, retaining a low specific capacity of 
295.0 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles (33.1% retention). Even more severe 
degradation occurs in the cell with THF-E, which fails after approxi­
mately 200 cycles. These pronounced differences in long-term cycling 
stability can be more clearly elucidated by analyzing the galvanostatic 
charge–discharge voltage profiles (Fig. S15). While the characteristic 
two-step discharge behavior of sulfur cathodes is observed for all elec­
trolyte systems, the cell with MCP-E exhibits a distinct voltage evolution 
compared with the other electrolytes. Specifically, the first discharge 
plateau appears at a relatively lower potential, whereas the second 
plateau is maintained at a higher voltage. This behavior suggests that, 
during the first plateau, the reduced solvation ability of MCP-E sup­
presses the dissolution of high-order LiPSs, thereby promoting a quasi- 
solid state conversion process. In contrast, during the second plateau, 
the effective participation of DME in the local solvation environment 
enables a more efficient conversion of LiPSs to Li2S [52]. Such a stabi­
lized and distinctive conversion pathway effectively mitigates polari­
zation buildup, thereby underpinning the sustained electrochemical 
performance of the cell with MCP-E during prolonged cycling. The 
Nyquist plots derived from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS) were interpreted using an equivalent circuit fitting model 
(Fig. S16). The results reveal that the evolution of both bulk and 
interfacial resistances during cycling is related to the solvation ability of 
the electrolytes. Before cycling, the bulk electrolyte resistance (Rb) is 
similar for all cells (Fig. S17). In contrast, after the 150th cycle and at 
the end of cycling, the cell with MCP-E maintains a distinctly lower Rb 
than the cells using the other electrolytes (Fig. 5i). This behavior dem­
onstrates that effective suppression of LiPSs shuttling in MCP-E limits 
the excessive accumulation of dissolved LiPSs in the electrolyte and the 
associated increase in viscosity. Conversely, cells using THF-E and 
DOL-E show a marked increase in Rb upon cycling, likely due to accel­
erated electrolyte consumption arising from repeated shuttle effects and 
parasitic side reactions. The interfacial resistance (RSEI) and Rct are also 
lowest for the cell with MCP-E. These results suggest that the anion-rich 
solvation environment established in MCP-E promotes the formation of 
a relatively stable and ionically conductive SEI layers at the LMAs. 
Notably, the persistence of a low Rct value in the cell with MCP-E even 
with increasing cycle number supports the effective suppression of 
interfacial accumulation of electrically resistive Li2S2/Li2S species, 
which are typically induced by LiPSs shuttling [53].
To further investigate differences in shuttle behavior across the 
electrolytes, the separator surface adjacent to the LMAs was examined 
by SEM after 50 cycles (Fig. 5j–l). The pristine polypropylene (PP) 
separator exhibits a smooth surface with uniformly distributed pores. In 
the case of MCP-E, the separator retains predominantly open pores and a 
morphology similar to that of pristine PP, confirming that polysulfide 
transport across the separator is effectively suppressed. In sharp 
contrast, the separators retrieved from THF-E and DOL-E show pro­
nounced pore blockage attributable to polysulfide deposition. These 
obstructed pores correspond to solid Li2S formed by the reduction of 
dissolved polysulfides on the Li-metal surface. This suppressed poly­
sulfide transport is consistent with the greatly improved long-term 
cycling stability observed for MCP-E. The superior capability of MCP-E 
to suppress LiPSs dissolution was further verified through a self- 
discharge test (Fig. S18). Li–S cells were first cycled for 5 cycles at 0.1 
C and then held at open-circuit conditions for 100 h while monitoring 
the open-circuit voltage (OCV). The magnitude of OCV drop follows the 
order of THF-E (0.23 V) > DOL-E (0.10 V) > MCP-E (0.05 V), indicating 
distinct differences in self-discharge behavior among the electrolytes. 
After the rest period, the cells were reactivated at the same C-rate. The 
cells with THF-E and DOL-E suffers pronounced capacity losses of 35.1% 
and 29.3%, respectively, which can be attributed to excessive LiPSs 
dissolution and the consequent loss of electrochemically active sulfur. In 
contrast, the cell with MCP-E exhibits negligible capacity decay after the 
rest period. These results consistently demonstrate that MCP-E effec­
tively mitigates self-discharge and suppresses LiPSs shuttling in Li–S 
cells. Even at an elevated rate of 0.5 C, the cell with MCP-E outperforms 
those using the other electrolytes, delivering both higher specific ca­
pacity and improved capacity retention (Fig. S19). Furthermore, the 
superior electrochemical performance of MCP-E was consistently pre­
served under increased sulfur-loading conditions in Li–S batteries 
(Fig. 5m). Specifically, under a sulfur loading of 2.6 mg cm−2 and an E/S 
ratio of 10 μL mg−1, the cell with MCP-E maintains stable cycling for 200 
cycles at 0.1 C, delivering a high specific capacity of 728.2 mAh g−1. In 
contrast, the cells with THF-E and DOL-E exhibit pronounced cell failure 
before reaching 200 cycles, terminating after 60 and 163 cycles, 
respectively. To clarify the origin of the premature failure, the cells were 
disassembled for examination of the LMAs. Notably, the Li-metal sur­
faces from the cells with THF-E and DOL-E show pronounced black 
discoloration (Fig. S20), indicative of severe Li-metal corrosion caused 
by cumulative side reactions involving dissolved LiPSs and electrolyte 
decomposition products during cycling.
To quantitatively evaluate polysulfide shuttling and parasitic re­
actions at the LMAs, shuttle current measurements were conducted. A 
monotonic decrease in shuttle current is observed with decreasing sol­
vation ability of the electrolyte, indicating that MCP-E effectively sup­
presses parasitic reactions at the LMAs. This suppression enables 
prolonged stable operation even under increased sulfur-loading condi­
tions and mitigating chemical and morphological degradation of Li after 
cycling (Fig. S21). Under more stringent conditions with an increased 
sulfur loading of 3.2 mg cm−2 and a reduced E/S ratio of 8 μL mg−1, the 
cell with MCP-E maintains stable cycling performance for 200 cycles. 
Although an inevitable decrease in specific capacity was observed with 
increasing areal capacity, the sustained cycling stability demonstrates 
that MCP-E can efficiently utilize active sulfur even under increased 
sulfur content and lean-electrolyte conditions (Fig. S22). Li–S pouch 
cells were assembled using sulfur cathodes with increased areal loading 
(3.3 mg cm−2) under a lean-electrolyte condition with an E/S ratio of 6 
μL mg−1 (Fig. S23). After two formation cycles at 0.05 C, the pouch cell 
was operated at 0.1 C for 100 cycles. Notably, even under these stringent 
conditions of increased sulfur areal loading and lean-electrolyte, the cell 
with MCP-E exhibits stable cycling performance with a capacity reten­
tion of 76% after 100 cycles, highlighting the strong potential of MCP-E 
for Li–S battery applications.

that the solvation ability of electrolyte plays a critical role in governing 
cell stability under practical conditions. Notably, reduced Li-metal 
corrosion was visually observed in MCP-E with lower LiPSs solubility.

polysulfide exposure to the LMAs, which can intensify interfacial side

under different solvation environments is further examined in the 
following section.
2.6. Interfacial chemistry and electrolyte consumption
To clarify how the distinct solvation environments influence inter­
facial reactions at the LMAs, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
was conducted to examine the chemical composition of the SEI layer 
formed on surfaces of LMAs after cycling. As shown in Fig. S29, the C 1 s 
spectra exhibit peaks associated with electrolyte decomposition prod­
ucts, including C–C/C–H (284.8 eV), C–O (286.2 eV), C = O (288.3 eV), 
and C–F (289.3 eV) [55]. In the F 1 s spectra, two distinct peaks corre­
sponding to C–F (688.4 eV) and Li–F (684.7 eV) are observed [56], with 
the Li–F peak arising from the reduction of the TFSI- [57,58] (Fig. S30). 
Quantitative analysis reveals that the SEI layer formed in MCP-E exhibits 
the lowest C atomic fraction and the highest F/C atomic ratio compared 
to those formed in THF-E and DOL-E (Fig. S31). This demonstrates that 
the solvent-derived organic species observed in the C 1 s spectra are 
effectively suppressed in MCP-E, accompanied by the formation of a 
LiF-rich SEI layer. Due to its low electronic conductivity and high me­
chanical strength, a LiF-rich SEI layer not only suppresses Li dendrite 
growth but also minimizes parasitic reactions between the electrolyte 
and LMAs [59], thereby inhibiting electrolyte decomposition. Therefore, 
the anion-rich solvation structure established in MCP-E not only sup­
presses solvent decomposition but also promotes the formation of an 
inorganic-rich SEI layer, thereby enabling more uniform Li deposition 
and improved interfacial stability.
To evaluate the extent of the shuttle effect arising from LiPSs 
dissolution, the S 2p spectra were analyzed to determine the content of 
sulfur-containing species present in the SEI layer (Fig. 7a). Across all 
electrolytes, both high-valence species (SO4
2-, 169.3 eV; SO3
2-, 167.1 eV) 
and low-valence species (SB, 163.4 eV; ST, 161.6 eV; Li2S, 160.0 eV) 
were detected [60]. For the SEI layer formed with THF-E and DOL-E, the 
spectra were dominated by low-valence sulfur species such as SB, ST, and 
Li2S, which are well-known products of reactions between dissolved 
polysulfides and LMAs. These spectral features suggest that the high 
solvation abilities of THF and DOL promote excessive LiPSs dissolution, 
thereby exacerbating the shuttle effect. In contrast, the SEI layer formed 
in MCP-E exhibits a higher proportion of high-valence sulfur species 
originating from the reductive decomposition of TFSI- [61], whereas the 
low-valence sulfur species generated through LiPSs shuttle effect were 
noticeably decreased. These observations suggest that the reduced sol­
vation ability of MCP-E suppresses LiPSs solubility, thereby contributing 
to a quasi-solid state conversion behavior, while simultaneously 
enhancing anion coordination in the Li+ solvation environment. The 
results for the high-valence sulfur species identified in the S 2p spectra 
are consistent with the F 1 s spectra, further confirming that MCP-E 
promotes the formation of a stable, anion-derived SEI layer that effec­
tively passivates the surfaces of LMAs during cycling. Overall, MCP-E 
not only suppresses LiPSs dissolution but also facilitates the formation 
of a LiF-rich inorganic SEI layer, thereby stabilizing the surfaces of LMAs 
during cycling and ultimately enhancing the long-term cycling dura­
bility and reversibility of the Li–S battery.
As observed in Fig. 5m, the pronounced capacity decay of the Li–S 
cells cannot be explained solely by active lithium loss associated with Li- 
metal corrosion, suggesting that cumulative electrolyte consumption 
accompanying this process should also be considered. To examine this 
interpretation, Li–S cells cycled for 50 cycles at a sulfur loading of 2.6 
mg cm−2 were disassembled, followed by 1H and 19F quantitative NMR 
(QNMR) analyses to quantify the residual amounts of individual elec­
trolyte components based on peak integration (Fig. 7b and 7c) [62,63]. 
The cell with MCP-E exhibits the highest retention ratios for DME, MCP, 
and the anion, corresponding to 70.4%, 53.9%, and 72.7%, respectively, 
compared with the other electrolytes (Fig. 7d). This trend is consistent 
with the suppressed Li-metal corrosion observed in MCP-E with lower 
LiPSs solubility (Fig. 6), suggesting that reduced exposure of Li-metal 
surfaces to dissolved LiPSs, together with the formation of stable 
inorganic-rich SEI layers, contributes to limiting electrolyte consump­
tion during cycling. To further assess the residual electrolyte amounts, 
the total remaining solvent volumes were calculated by multiplying the 
initially injected electrolyte volume by the retention ratios of the indi­
vidual components (Fig. S32). The total residual solvent amount follows 
the order MCP-E > DOL-E > THF-E, indicating accelerated electrolyte 
consumption during cycling in THF-E and DOL-E, likely associated with 
increased LiPSs dissolution and Li-metal corrosion.
3. Conclusion
In summary, this study demonstrated that electrolyte solvation 
ability plays a decisive role in dictating both LiPSs behavior and LMAs 
stability in Li–S batteries. By employing a low solvation MCP-E, poly­
sulfide dissolution and transport were effectively suppressed, thereby 
reducing the concentration of mobile polysulfides in the electrolyte and 
alleviating shuttle effect. This reduced LiPSs solubility directly mitigated 
polysulfide-induced corrosion and parasitic side reactions at the LMAs,

while simultaneously minimizing continuous electrolyte consumption 
during cycling. Importantly, a solvation environment arising from 
weakened Li+–solvent interactions promoted enhanced interfacial 
charge-transfer kinetics and rapid Li+ desolvation process. This facili­
tated the formation of a dense, inorganic-rich SEI layer that effectively 
passivated the Li-metal surface against corrosion. As a result, even Li 
deposition and highly reversible Li plating/stripping were achieved, 
enabling efficient Li utilization and sustained interfacial stability during 
prolonged operation. Consequently, the cell with MCP-E exhibited 
improved cycling stability and rate capability with reduced polysulfide 
shuttling and improved LMAs stability. Overall, this work elucidates 
how electrolyte solvation environment fundamentally shapes poly­
sulfide behavior and LMAs degradation in Li–S batteries, thereby 
establishing a mechanistic foundation for rational electrolyte design.
