A B S T R A C T
The practical application of lithium‑sulfur batteries is impeded by the polysulfide shuttle effect and slow redox 
kinetics. This study addresses these challenges through a synergistic strategy employing a 3R-2H (R3m-P63) 
phase-junction MoSe2 electrocatalyst combined with a dimethyl diselenide (DMDSe)-modified electrolyte. The 
rationally designed 3R-2H MoSe2 nanosheets, integrated with an rGO conductive network, provide abundant 
active sites and facilitate electron/Li+ transport, thereby enhancing polysulfide adsorption and conversion. 
Concurrently, the DMDSe additive functions as an effective electrolyte mediator, leveraging its tailored orbital 
energetics to construct stable electrode-electrolyte interphases and to implement in situ selenization of poly­
sulfides via Lewis acid-base interactions, which collectively suppress the shuttle effect. The optimized battery 
delivers a high specific capacity of 1456.6 mAh g−1 at 0.1C, remarkable rate capability (757.7 mAh g−1 at 2C), 
and ultralow cyclic decay of 0.041 % per cycle over 199 cycles at 0.5C. Furthermore, Ah-level pouch cells power 
a 49 g drone for 8.3 min of stable flight, underscoring the potential of this approach for high-energy and high- 
power applications.
1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries are at the forefront of next- 
generation energy systems due to their impressive theoretical specific 
capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) [1–4]. 
However, their practical application is hindered by three major issues: 
the severe shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides (Li2Sₙ, 4 ≤n ≤8), the loss 
of active materials, and the sluggish kinetics of the Li2S2/Li2S solid-state 
conversion [5–8]. Designing efficient and cost-effective catalysts for the 
catalytic conversion of lithium polysulfides is therefore crucial [9–11]. 
While traditional single-component sulfide/nitride catalysts can 
partially mitigate these issues, their static adsorption sites limit their 
widespread application as they cannot dynamically reconstruct poly­
sulfides during cycling [12–17].
Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), as emerging two- 
dimensional materials, have been widely investigated as electro­
catalysts [1,18]. Among them, molybdenum diselenide (MoSe2) stands 
out as a promising catalyst candidate for Li–S batteries due to its 
excellent catalytic activity, moderate polysulfide adsorption capability, 
ease of preparation, and abundant raw materials.[19–21] Research 
indicates that MoSe2 possesses good interfacial conductivity, which 
helps improve sulfur utilization. Furthermore, the selenium edges of 
MoSe2 enable the selective adsorption of lithium polysulfides, thereby 
alleviating the shuttle effect [22–24].
Electrolyte additive engineering represents a crucial approach for 
enhancing performance of Li–S batteries [25–27]. Recent years have 
witnessed extensive investigation into innovative strategies involving 
electrolyte additives [28–30]. For instance, chloropyrazine additives 
effectively modulated molecular orbital energy levels of polysulfides 
and promoted conversion kinetics, the battery demonstrated capacity 
decay of only 0.066 % per cycle after 500 cycles at 0.2C [31]. However, 
existing additives face challenges such as insufficient reduction stability 
and ambiguous multifunctional synergy mechanisms, while the inter­
play between electrocatalysts and electrolytes remains rarely explored 
[32–35]. This necessitates the development of cooperative optimization 
strategies for electrocatalysts and electrolyte additives to systematically 
improve overall electrochemical performance [36–38].

2. Results and discussion
Fig. 1a and b schematically illustrate the hydrothermal and two-step 
calcination synthesis of sulfur-loaded phase-junction MoSe2/reduced 
graphene oxide composite cathodes (MoSe2@rGO/S). The MoO2@rGO 
precursor was prepared by reacting Na2MoO4, CTAB, and GO in a 
stainless-steel autoclave at 200 ◦C for 10 h (Fig. 1a). Negatively charged 
MoO4
2−underwent electrostatic adsorption with the positively charged 
hydrophilic headgroups [-N+(CH3)3] of CTAB. Additionally, deproto­
nation of carboxyl groups on GO confers an overall negative charge, 
enabling electrostatic binding with the cationic headgroups of CTAB to 
enhance interfacial stability within the composite matrix [39]. As shown 
in Fig. 1b, the precursor was subsequently selenized by calcination with 
Se powder at 550 ◦C for 4 h, followed by sulfur infusion through sec­
ondary calcination with S powder at 155 ◦C for 12 h. The resulting 
MoSe2 nanosheets exhibit phase-junction characteristics with a hexag­
onal P63 (2H) surface phase and rhombohedral R3m (3R) bulk phase. 
While sharing an atomic layer configuration, these phases display 
distinct interlayer stacking (Fig. 1c). During selenization, MoO2 prefer­
entially reacts with gaseous Se to form metastable R3m-MoSe2 due to

Fig. 1d displays transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of 
phase-junction MoSe2@rGO. MoSe2 nanosheets are embedded within 
the rGO matrix, integrating abundant active sites with high electronic 
conductivity. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern 
(Fig. 1e) shows distinct diffraction spots indexed to the (100), (103), and 
(110) planes of the P63 surface phase (Hexagonal), confirming poly­
crystallinity. The exclusive manifestation of surface-phase features 
likely stems from electron beam penetration primarily probing surface/ 
subsurface atomic layers. High-resolution transmission electron micro­
scopy (HRTEM) imaging (Fig. 1f) reveals dual lattice spacings in MoSe2. 
Enlarged views (Fig. 1g) resolve spacings of 0.190 nm and 0.646 nm, 
assigned to (105) plane of P63 phase and (003) plane of R3m phase 
(Rhombohedral), respectively. High-angle annular dark-field scanning 
transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging (Fig. 1h) and 
corresponding elemental mapping (Fig. 1i-l) demonstrate homogeneous 
distribution of C, O, Se, Mo throughout the composite (EDS quantifica­

To validate the superior structure and performance of phase-junction 
MoSe2@rGO (C-MoSe2@rGO, where C denotes calcined sample ob­
tained after the first calcination in Fig. 1b), comparative analyses were 
conducted with MoO2@rGO precursor and hydrothermally synthesized 
H-MoSe2@rGO (H denotes hydrothermal, methods in Supporting In­
formation). Fig. 2a,d show scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images 
of MoO2@rGO at different magnifications. The surface is uniformly 
covered by rGO, while the interior contains bulky MoO2 particles. Only 
partial rGO layers serve as active sites for catalytic conversion. Fig. 2b,e 
display SEM images of H-MoSe2@rGO with abundant MoSe2 nano­
particles (~50 nm radius) distributed on rGO. These moderately 
monodisperse particles exhibit smooth surfaces with partial agglomer­
ation, enhancing specific surface area and active sites. In contrast, C- 
MoSe2@rGO (Fig. 2c,f) features well-aligned MoSe2 nanosheets (~100 
nm radius, several-nm thickness) with smooth basal planes and serrated 
edges, suggesting abundant edge defects. This ordered nanostructure 
confirms precise dimensional control and validates the reliability of the 
electrostatic adsorption self-assembly process [40].
Pore structure and specific surface area critically govern polysulfide 
confinement capability. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms 
(Fig. 2g-i) reveal all samples exhibit Type IV curves, confirming meso­
porous features. MoO2@rGO displays a narrow hysteresis loop with 
limited mesopores and low Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area 
(8.1248 m2 g−1), likely due to pore blockage by bulky MoO2 particles. H- 
MoSe2@rGO and C-MoSe2@rGO show expanded hysteresis loops with 
enhanced adsorption capacities (26.9832 and 146.5498 m2 g−1 respec­
tively). C-MoSe2@rGO isotherm exhibits a high adsorption capacity in 
the monolayer adsorption region, indicating abundant micropores. The 
steepest part of the curve, located in the capillary condensation region, 
signifies a well-developed mesoporous structure. H3-type hysteresis 
loop suggests slit-shaped pores formed by nanosheet stacking. Conse­
quently, C-MoSe2@rGO possesses the highest surface area and optimal 
pore architecture. Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) pore size distributions 
(Fig. S2-S4, Supporting Information) further show MoO2@rGO exhibits 
unimodal distribution peaking at 30 nm. H-MoSe2@rGO has primary 
pores at 4 nm, confirming mesopore generation during selenization. 
While C-MoSe2@rGO features the smallest pore size (3.8 nm) and largest 
pore volume (0.4 cm3 g−1), effectively mitigating electrode expansion 
and facilitating electrolyte diffusion.

Electrolyte engineering critically determines lithium‑sulfur battery 
performance. In liquid electrolytes, appropriate additives facilitate sta­
ble solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and cathode electrolyte interphase 
(CEI) formation, enhancing electrochemical behavior. Fig. 4a depicts 
the electrolyte energy diagram, introducing highest occupied molecular 
orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) 
concepts to define thermodynamic stability windows (Eg) and electrode 
compatibility. Frontier orbital theory posits that HOMO/LUMO orbitals 
host reactive frontier electrons (analogous to atomic valence electrons), 
governing initial reaction steps. Lower LUMO energy facilitates electron 
acceptance, promoting stable SEI formation at the anode. Higher HOMO 
energy favors electron donation, enabling robust CEI generation at the 
cathode. A narrow HOMO-LUMO gap enhances electron excitation and 
charge transfer kinetics.
Through molecular orbital theory and Gaussian software-assisted 
calculations, dimethyl diselenide (DMDSe) was identified as an 
optimal additive. DMDSe exhibits LUMO of −1.50 eV and HOMO of 
−6.34 eV (Fig. 4b). Contrasting with LiNO3 (LUMO: −1.04 eV, HOMO: 
−7.13 eV), lower LUMO of DMDSe thermodynamically favors decom­
position at lithium metal, participating in SEI formation. Simulta­
neously, its higher HOMO promotes preferential oxidation, creating Se- 
rich CEI layers. The electrostatic potential map (Fig. 4b inset) reveals 
pronounced negative potential at Se atoms (electron-rich regions), 
functioning as Lewis base sites for LiPSs anchoring. Fig. 4c illustrates 
LiPSs anchoring mechanism with DMDSe. Dynamic bond cleavage 
generates electrophilic CH3Se+ species, which selectively bind terminal 
sulfur atoms in Li2S4/Li2S6/Li2S8 to form Se-radical modified poly­
sulfides. While reductive Se converts soluble LiPSs into insoluble sul­
fides, collectively suppressing dissolution and shuttle effects. The 
essential role of both the DMDSe electrolyte additive and the phase- 
junction MoSe2@rGO catalyst is to promote the catalytic conversion of 
polysulfides and accelerate the redox kinetics. The selenium element 
present in the electrolyte and the selenium within the catalyst work 
synergistically—yet through distinct mechanisms—to collectively 
enhance the electrochemical performance of the lithium‑sulfur battery.
Li//Li symmetric cell was assembled to study the stability of the 
electrolyte (Fig. 4d). At a current density of 1 mA cm−2, cells with 
DMDSe exhibit minimal voltage fluctuation (ΔV: 46 → 52 mV over 1200 
h), while controls show severe polarization and failure after 30 h due to 
irreversible side reactions. Post-cycling SEM (Fig. 4d inset) confirms 
dendrite-free Li deposition with DMDSe versus irregular lithium 
morphology in controls, demonstrating DMDSe-facilitated stable SEI 
formation for homogeneous Li plating. Rate-dependent stability tests 
(0.5–10 mA cm−2, Fig. 4e) show DMDSe mitigates polarization growth 
under increasing current densities, indicating improved interfacial ki­
netics at high current densities.
Li2S deposition experiments (Fig. 4f) assess sulfur reduction and 
discharge product nucleation kinetics. The deposition profile comprises 
three regions: Li2S8 reduction (near y-axis), Li2S6 reduction (near x- 
axis), and Li2S precipitation (peak) [42,43]. DMDSe cell achieves com­
plete Li2S deposition within merely 2936 s, significantly shorter than 
8760 s for the blank control, demonstrating accelerated Li2S nucleation 
kinetics. Furthermore, DMDSe cell delivers a deposition capacity of 
100.23 mAh g−1, approximately double that of the blank group, con­
firming pronounced catalytic effect on Li2S conversion. Post-deposition 
SEM (Fig. 4f inset) reveals that DMDSe promotes uniform Li2S deposi­
tion morphology, confirming its role in regulating sulfide precipitation 
and enhancing active material utilization.
To evaluate the impact of phase-junction MoSe2 and DMDSe additive 
on the electrochemical performance of lithium‑sulfur batteries, a series 
of electrochemical tests were conducted on C-MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe, C- 
MoSe2@rGO, H-MoSe2@rGO, and MoO2@rGO. Fig. 5a presents the rate 
capability tests of four coin cell configurations (sulfur loadings of 
5.5–6.5 mg cm−2, E/S = 5 μL mg−1). The C-MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe bat­
tery delivers specific capacities of 1456.6, 1093.5, 986.1, 864.8, 806.8, 
and 757.7 mAh g−1 at rates of 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.3C, 0.5C, 1C, and 2C, 
respectively. When the current rate was restored to 0.1C, the cell 
maintained a high specific capacity of 1062 mAh g−1, significantly 
outperforming the other three samples at equivalent rates. The phase- 
junction MoSe2 primarily enhances electrochemical performance 
through improved specific capacity, while the incorporation of DMDSe 
demonstrates a remarkable increase in capacity at 2C, confirming its 
significant role in boosting reaction kinetics.
Cycling performance at 0.1C and 0.5C are presented in Fig. 5b and d, 
respectively. The C-MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe cell retains a capacity of 
975.1 mAh g−1 after 139 cycles at 0.1C, with an average capacity decay 
of 0.180 %/cycle. At 0.5C, it achieves 912.5 mAh g−1 after 199 cycles 
with an ultralow decay of 0.041 %/cycle. Both phase-junction MoSe2 
and DMDSe contribute to enhanced cycle life, with DMDSe particularly 
effective in maintaining capacity retention at high current densities. 
This improvement can be attributed to the Se radical modified poly­
sulfide intermediates and the formation of a stable SEI layer.
Galvanostatic charge/discharge curves (Fig. 5c) reveal that C- 
MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe exhibits a low polarization voltage (ΔE = 0.1438 
V) at 0.1C, with the second-to-first discharge plateau capacity ratio (Q2: 
Q1) approaching the theoretical 3:1 value. Increasing rates lead to 
reduced total capacity, decreased discharge plateaus, and elevated 
charge plateaus, indicating intensified electrochemical polarization. The 
first three cyclic voltammetry (CV) cycles of C-MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe at 
0.05 mV s−1 (Fig. 5e) show two characteristic reduction peaks at ~2.3 V 
(S8 → LiPSs) and ~ 1.9 V (LiPSs→Li2S). After the initial cycle, both peak 
potentials and intensities stabilize, confirming the formation of robust 
SEI/CEI under DMDSe regulation. The sharp redox peaks further indi­
cate high catalytic activity of the electrode [44].

Density functional theory (DFT) simulations (Fig. S8, Supporting 
Information) reveal adsorption configurations of S8/LiPSs on P63-MoSe2 
surfaces, where undercoordinated Se atoms provide strong chemisorp­
tion sites suppressing shuttling. Given that the P63 phase is the pre­
dominant surface component (52.78 %) of the heterostructure and its 
exposed active sites within the nanoflower architecture are crucial for 
electrolyte interaction and polysulfide conversion, it served as the 
representative model for our DFT calculations. Fig. 7a presents Gibbs 
free energy diagram for the multi-step sulfur reduction on P63-MoSe2 
surfaces. It confirms spontaneous reduction from S8 to Li2S8 and Li2S6 to 
Li2S2 (ΔG < 0), with minimal activation energies for Li2S8 → Li2S6 
(0.085 eV) and Li2S2 → Li2S (0.115 eV) steps, demonstrating capability 
of P63-MoSe2 to reduce reaction barriers and accelerate polysulfide 
conversion kinetics.
In situ XRD (Fig. 7b) elucidates dynamic sulfur evolution in C- 
MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe cell. At OCV, distinct α-S8 diffraction peaks are 
observed, with the strongest peak at 2θ = 23.3◦. As discharge proceeds, 
α-S8 peak intensity progressively decreases, vanishing completely near 
2.0 V. Concurrently, Li2S diffraction peaks (26.8◦) emerge and intensify, 
reaching maximum intensity at 1.7 V. During charging, Li2S peaks 
diminish gradually, disappearing completely at 2.4 V. Subsequently, 
polysulfides reconvert to the β-S8, evidenced by peaks at 23.1◦, 24.1◦, 
and 26.2◦. This phase shift reveals different crystalline S8 configurations 
before and after cycling. These results confirm complete sulfur utiliza­
tion: α-S8 fully dissolves and converts to Li2S during discharge, while 
deposited Li2S completely reconverts to S8 during charging [50]. This 
demonstrates that the phase-junction MoSe2 and DMDSe additive syn­
ergistically accelerate solid-state S8/Li2S redox kinetics, ensuring effi­
cient active material conversion while minimizing dead sulfur 
accumulation.

To evaluate the drone application potential of the C-MoSe2@rGO// 
DMDSe, we assembled and tested practical pouch cells, subsequently 
deploying them as power sources for a drone. As shown in Fig. 8a, the 
discharge profile of the C-MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe pouch cell at 0.05C 
displays characteristic two-plateau behavior similar to coin cells, with 
Q2:Q1 approaching theoretical values. The single pouch cell delivers a 
capacity of 1.3232 Ah (assembly schematic shown in Fig. 8a inset). Since 
drone takeoff and ascent require substantial instantaneous power (rates 
far exceeding 0.05C), rigorous rate capability tests were conducted 
(Fig. 8b). A pouch cell (6 mg cm−2, E/S = 5 μL mg−1) demonstrated 
specific capacities of 1374.4, 1124.5, 796.1, 517.0, and 211.2 mAh g−1 
at 0.1C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, and 3C, respectively. Crucially, when integrated 
into a drone (31.1 × 29.3 × 5.36 cm, 48.9 g, Fig. 8b inset), the system 
achieved >500 s (8.3 min) of stable flight (Fig. S9, Video, Supporting 
Information), maintaining voltage stability during takeoff, hover, and 
rapid ascent/descent. This validates rapid response to power surges and 
reliability under high-rate discharge. Collectively, the synergistic phase- 
junction catalysts and DMDSe additive strategy resolves core challenges 
of sluggish kinetics and poor rate capability in Li–S batteries, demon­
strating transformative potential for long-endurance aircraft with 
unparalleled power density and flight duration.
3. Conclusion
This work establishes a synergistic paradigm using 3R-2H (R3m-P63) 
phase-junction MoSe2 catalysts and DMDSe electrolyte additives to 
fundamentally enhance lithium‑sulfur electrochemistry. The C- 
MoSe2@rGO (C denotes calcination) composite cathode, synthesized via 
hydrothermal-calcination, features a dual-phase structure with hexag­
onal P63 surface and rhombohedral R3m bulk phases (≈1:1 ratio 
confirmed by XRD refinement) resulting from insurmountable phase- 
transition barriers. This unique architecture yields nanosheets with 
high electrochemically active surface area, while lattice distortions 
accelerate Li+ diffusion. The P63-R3m phase-junction MoSe2 confers 
exceptional thermal/chemical stability and strong polysulfide chemi­
sorption, with abundant edge defects enabling low charge transfer 
resistance and rapid conversion kinetics. For DMDSe, optimal HOMO/ 
LUMO levels (−6.34/−1.50 eV) facilitate robust Se-rich CEI/SEI for­
mation, while its Lewis base functionality mediates polysulfide bonding 
to suppress shuttling and optimize reaction pathways. The C- 
MoSe2@rGO//DMDSe coin cell delivers exceptional 2C rate capability 
(757.7 mAh g−1) and ultralow 0.041 % per-cycle decay over 199 cycles 
at 0.5C. In situ analyses confirming synchronized RSEI/Rct reduction and 
highly reversible S8/Li2S conversion. Practical validation via 8.3 min 
drone flight with pouch cells demonstrates transformative potential for 
high-power, long-endurance energy systems.
