ABSTRACT
Low  electrolyte  usage  is  a  key  to  attaining  high-energy-density  lithium–sulfur  (Li–S)  batteries.  However,  this  is  still  a
tremendous  challenge  for  traditional  ether-based  electrolytes  that  follow  a  dissolution–precipitation  mechanism.  Highly
solvating electrolytes, which can facilitate polysulfide dissolution and alter reaction pathway, are considered a promising
strategy.  Nonetheless,  mechanistic  understanding  and  kinetic  evaluation  remain  insufficient  while  the  principle  of  Li2S
nucleation and dissociation has not been elucidated. Herein, we unveil the Li-ion solvation and polysulfide speciation in
the  solvents  with  different  denticity  and  donicity.  The  origin  of  S3
•–  radical-directed  path  and  three-dimensional  Li2S
precipitation  in  high-donicity  electrolytes  has  been  uncovered.  It  is  revealed  that  ammonium  ions  enable  the  facile
dissolution and dissociation of Li2S via Lewis acid-base interaction and H···S2– binding. Consequently, Li–S batteries with
a low electrolyte and sulfur (E/S) ratio of 5 μL·mgs
–1 achieve a high capacity of 1092 mAh·g–1. Even at a harsh E/S ratio of
3 μL·mgs
–1 and a high sulfur loading of 4 mg·cm–2, they still sustain a stable operation over 30 cycles. Our work sheds light
on the underlying reaction mechanism and rationalizes the design of highly solvating electrolytes, which in turn opens a
new avenue for achieving pragmatic lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.

1    Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are recognized as one of the most
promising next-generation energy storage systems due to the
ultrahigh theoretical capacity (1672 mAh·g−1) and energy density
(2600 Wh·kg−1). Nevertheless, their pragmatic deployment is
plagued by the sluggish redox kinetics and high electrolyte usage
in conventional ether-based electrolytes [1–6]. For one thing,
sulfur redox undergoes sluggish multi-phase and multi-electron
conversion, thus inevitably deteriorating the reaction kinetics and
sulfur utilization [7, 8]. For another, typical dissolution–deposition
behavior of soluble polysulfides is highly dependent on the
electrolyte while common ethers can only render a moderate
polysulfide solubility [9, 10]. In this respect, sufficient electrolyte
usage is always requested to sustain the polysulfide reaction during
the long-term cycling process. Therefore, the rational design of
kinetically favored lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries, although vitally
appealing, remains a daunting challenge.
It is well accepted that sulfur redox kinetics is essentially
associated with the actual performance of Li–S batteries [11]. In
conventional ether-based electrolytes, the premature precipitation
of low-order polysulfides to Li2S triggers electrode passivation and
curtails sulfur utilization [12–14]. Besides, the dissociation of
insulating Li2S suffers from a high reaction barrier [15]. To
circumvent these detrimental hurdles, a multitude of polysulfide
electrocatalysts have been developed to accelerate bidirectional
sulfur redox [16–22]. Nonetheless, most of these strategies have
not fundamentally altered the dissolution property and reaction
mechanism of polysulfides. In this respect, high electrolyte usage
still handicaps their pragmatic application.
In a broader context, it was proposed by Bhargav et al. that only
when the electrolyte and sulfur (E/S) ratio is lowered to 5 μL·mgs
–1
can Li–S batteries showcase an apparent advantage over
commercial Li-ion batteries in terms of the energy density [23]. To
this end, sparingly solvating electrolyte has been developed to
reduce electrolyte usage via mitigating polysulfide dissolution and
achieving a quasi-solid-state reaction, yet sluggish redox kinetics
and low sulfur utilization are still the major concerns [24, 25].

Encouragingly, highly solvating electrolyte has also emerged as
another promising alternative to lower electrolyte usage whilst
harvesting favorable redox kinetics and high sulfur utilization,
wherein high-donicity solvents or salts are introduced to boost the
polysulfide solubility [26]. In this sense, Cuisinier et al. made
pioneering
 design
 and
 unraveled
 S3
•– 
radical-dominated
polysulfide speciation [27]. Along this line, Baek et al. developed a
novel high-donicity solvent (i.e., 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone),
which helps to harvest a significant elevation of sulfur utilization
[28]. To deepen the mechanistic understanding, Li et al. shed light
upon the nucleation and growth behavior of Li2S in solvents with
different donicity [29]. Despite conspicuous progress in this
research realm thus far, the speciation state and dissociation
process of Li2S have received insufficient attention. Besides,
fundamental insights on the electrolyte solvation structure as well
as chemical interaction and reaction mechanism of sulfur species
are still elusive, hence the deployment of advanced electrokinetic
tests, spectroscopic characterizations, and theoretical calculations
is urgent. Finally, it is imperative and meaningful to systematically
evaluate the electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries under
lean-electrolyte working conditions [30–33].
In this study, we design a highly solvating electrolyte with
1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and NH4I serving as the
customized solvent and additive, respectively. Electrokinetic tests
combined with spectroscopic characterizations unveil the origin of
S3
•– 
radical-dominant
 polysulfide
 speciation
 and
 three-
dimensional (3D) Li2S precipitation under the management of
high-donicity NMP and I–. Additionally, it is uncovered that NH4
+
is in favor of facilitating the facile dissociation of insulating Li2S via
H···S2– binding. Exhaustive theoretical calculations further reveal
the polysulfide-electrolyte nexus and sulfur redox reaction
mechanism. Consequently, an admirable capacity of 1092 mAh·g–1
can be obtained under a low E/S ratio of 5 μL·mgs
–1. More
impressively, even under a more stringent E/S ratio of 3 μL·mgs
–1
and a high sulfur loading of 4 mg·cm–2, as-assembled Li–S batteries
can still sustain a stable operation over 30 cycles. Our work
provides a meaningful guidance to design highly solvating
electrolyte in the pursuit of pragmatic lean-electrolyte Li–S
batteries.

3.1    Electrolyte  design  principle  and  solvation  structure
characterization
Conventional ether-based electrolyte using 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as the binary solvents (denoted
as DOL/DME) has been deemed as a prevailing recipe for Li–S
batteries,
 wherein
 DME
 ensures
 sufficient
 polysulfide
dissolution/conversion and DOL aids to stabilize Li metal anode
[34]. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a), several detrimental issues
impede its pragmatic application. To begin with, the typical
liquid–solid conversion reaction from Li2S4 to Li2S is kinetically
sluggish due to the two-electron transfer pathway, which also
results in the unfavorable two-dimensional (2D) precipitation of
insulating
 Li2S
 product
 with
 a
 film-like
 morphology.
Consequently,
 this
 aggravates
 electrode
 passivation
 and
jeopardizes sulfur utilization. Meanwhile, Li2S dissociation suffers
from high voltage barrier and hence slow reaction kinetics.
Besides,
 considering
 the
 typical
 dissolution–precipitation
characteristic of soluble polysulfide intermediates, high electrolyte
usage is always required due to the mediocre polysulfide solubility
of DME with a double-denticity and low-donicity feature. To this
end, with the introduction of single-denticity and high-donicity
NMP to replace DME as the co-solvent (denoted as DOL/NMP),
it is anticipated that S3
•– radical with a fairly negative charge
density and soft base property would be formed and stabilized as
the dominant polysulfide speciation [35]. In this respect, a fast
single-electron sulfur reduction pathway can be obtained, which
accordingly enhances reaction kinetics and alleviates cathode
polarization (Fig. 1(b)). In addition, it induces 3D Li2S deposition
and fast conversion efficiency owing to the strong solvation
capability of NMP. More importantly, NMP that shows strong
molecular polarity will have a high polysulfide solubility, hence
electrolyte usage can be markedly lowered while enabling
sufficient polysulfide dissolution and reaction. Meanwhile, it helps
to dig deep into the underlying mechanism of Li-ion solvation and
polysulfide speciation in different solvents, which is instrumental
to understanding the complicated Li–S chemistry [36]. Despite
these conspicuous advantages, the dissociation of insulating Li2S
solid still remains a huge challenge, which will accumulate onto
the limited conductive surface and hinder charge transport. To
this end, NH4I has been employed as an additive into the
DOL/NMP electrolyte (denoted as DOL/NMP-NH4I), which is
expected to facilitate the dissolution and dissociation of Li2S
(Fig. 1(c)) [37]. Specifically, there might be a strong chemical
interaction between NH4
+ and Li2S crystal due to the unique
dipole configuration and polar strength of N–H bond, which leads
to high electronegativity of terminal H atoms. Besides, high-
donicity I– with considerable redox mediation capability can aid to
stabilize S3
•– radical species and expedite polysulfide conversion
while elevating the dissolving capability of electrolyte for
polysulfides. As a consequence, favored redox kinetics and low
electrolyte usage can be simultaneously harvested with the aid of
such a synchronous solvent and additive engineering. Nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) test was carried out the investigate the
electrolyte solvation structure and chemical interaction. The
chemical shift of 7Li resonance can help to disclose the Li-ion
coordination environment, in which the electrolytes with and
without the addition of LiNO3 (denoted as w and wo LiNO3) were
taken into consideration (Fig. 1(d); Fig. S1 in the ESM). First, as
compared to the counterpart of wo LiNO3, the 7Li resonance of
DOL/DME w LiNO3 significantly shifts downfield. The more
positive chemical shift manifests that high-donicity NO3
– is prone
to enter into the Li-ion inner solvation sheath and attract the
electron cloud, thus causing an electron deshielding effect on Li+
[38]. While replacing DME with NMP, NO3
– can barely
coordinate with Li+ due to the lower donor number (22.2
kcal·mol–1) than that of NMP (27.3 kcal·mol–1) as demonstrated in
Table S1 in the ESM. Therefore, the 7Li resonance of DOL/NMP w
LiNO3 shows a slight downfield shift as compared to DOL/NMP
wo LiNO3, indicating that NMP solvents are dominant in the
inner solvation sheath of Li ions. Second, NMP-based electrolytes
display an obvious downfield shift of 7Li resonance in comparison
with that of the DME-based counterparts, demonstrative of lower
electron cloud density around Li+. It seems abnormal in the case of
the stronger solvation and electron-donating ability of NMP.
Hence we calculate the electron density of various elements in
DME and NMP (Fig. S2 in the ESM), which unfolds that single-
denticity and strong-polarity NMP solvent has a more negative
electron density of O atoms (–0.529) than that (–0.483) of DME
[39]. In this respect, donor number determines the coordination

the 2D nucleation model in the DOL/DME. However, 3D

microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2(c); Fig. S5 in the ESM),
precipitated Li2S exhibits distinctly different morphologies in three
electrolytes, in good agreement with the results obtained in the
foregoing dimensionless analysis. As expected, film-like Li2S
deposits can be observed in DOL/DME, which cover conductive
surface and curtail triple-phase interface, thus causing early
termination of the discharge process along with severe electrode
passivation. In contrast, precipitated Li2S in DOL/NMP becomes
thicker and looser with random distribution of rich pores. It is
desired that the introduction of NH4I additive results in the
formation of randomly distributed Li2S granules with a spherical
morphology, which originates from the isotropic growth toward
all directions. In conclusion, high-donicity components aid to
generate small-size and large-density Li2S nucleus, thus extending
the discharge process and elevating the sulfur utilization. With
respect to the reversible Li2S oxidation reaction, much larger
current response is obtained in DOL/NMP-NH4I as compared to
DOL/NMP from the PITT charging curves (Fig. 2(d)),
manifesting that NH4I plays a key role in promoting Li2S
dissociation. In stark contrast, due to the kinetically unfavorable
Li2S deposition, the current response for Li2S decomposition in
DOL/DME is also fairly low. Figure 2(e) displays the total
discharge and charge capacity calculated from the PITT profiles.
First, both discharge and charge capacity in DOL/DME are greatly
lower in comparison with that in NMP-containing electrolytes,
further suggesting that high-donicity components are in favor of
promoting sulfur utilization and achieving high capacity. Besides,
NH4I additive leads to a marked increase of charge capacity,
indicative of its vital merit on facilitating Li2S dissolution and
dissociation. The solid–liquid Li2S oxidation process was also
probed by the anodic scan of cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles of
as-assembled Li–S batteries with three different electrolytes
(Fig. 2(f)). In terms of the electrochemical kinetics, this reaction is
more sluggish in DOL/DME with much later peak appearance as
compared to the NMP-containing scenarios. Notably, as shown in
Fig. 2(g), the NH4I additive enables a lower Tafel slope (139.2
mV·dec–1) than that for pure DOL/NMP (157.8 mV·dec–1), which
matches well with the PITT results, further demonstrative of faster
Li2S dissociation kinetics in DOL/NMP-NH4I.
To investigate the redox reaction of polysulfides in three
different electrolytes, symmetrical cells employing carbon paper
(CP) as the identical electrode were assembled with Li2S6-
containing electrolyte [44]. The electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) test was operated prior to the cycling process,
where it is apparent that DOL/DME shows a much bigger
semicircle (i.e., higher charge transfer resistance (Rct)), which
presumably stems from the sluggish polysulfide conversion
reaction at the electrode/electrolyte interface (Fig. 2(h)). Benefiting
from the fast S3
•– radical-directed single-electron reaction, the
value of Rct is markedly reduced in the NMP-containing
electrolytes. In parallel, the corresponding CV curves at 50 mV·s−1
were recorded (Fig. 2(i)), in which the values of redox current
response follow the order of DOL/DME < DOL/NMP <
DOL/NMP-NH4I. It is also worth noting that the CV curve of
DOL/NMP-NH4I at 3 mV·s−1 clearly exhibits a pair of redox peaks
(Fig. S6 in the ESM), yet which are totally invisible for the
counterparts. These results signify that NH4I additive harnesses a
considerable redox mediation capability to accelerate Li–S
chemistry. To probe the real-time evolution of interfacial
resistance, in-situ EIS measurement was carried out via collecting
spectra at multiple voltage stages (Fig. 2(j)) [50]. Notably, as for
DOL/DME, the values of Rct maintain higher level as compared to
that for NMP-based electrolytes, which are adversely augmented
upon the charging process (Fig. S7 in the ESM). Taking previous
electrokinetic test results into consideration, it is reasonable to
infer that film-like Li2S deposits cover the conductive electrode
surface, which would retard mass transport and increase
interfacial resistance. When using NMP as the solvent, Rct is
apparently lowered upon the charging process due to the uniform
and loose Li2S deposition. More encouragingly, benefiting from
the more efficient dissociation of insulating Li2S, lower Rct can be
obtained with the aid of NH4I additive as compared to that of
DOL/NMP. In the meanwhile, the corresponding Bode plots are
displayed in Fig. 2(k), which are in consistent with the Nyquist
plots. Note that the middle-frequency region (5–1000 Hz)
represents the Rct. In view of the DOL/DME system, it shows the
extension of high phase angle region, which originates from the
kinetically unfavored Li2S precipitation and decomposition. As
such, DOL/NMP-NH4I shows the apparently lower phase angle
especially for the charging process, indicative of the expedited Li2S
oxidation reaction. It is well accepted that favorable bidirectional
conversion reaction between sulfur and Li2S gives rise to the
effective mitigation of “shuttle effect”. In this regard, the shuttling
current for DOL/NMP-NH4I electrolyte is the smallest amongst
three electrolytes (Fig. S8 in the ESM), further suggesting the
promoted sulfur redox chemistry under the regulation of such a
synchronous solvent and additive engineering.

Based on the above discussion, Li2S nucleation behavior
considerably varies in different electrolytes. Therefore, we aim to
demystify the underlying essence from a thermodynamic
perspective of homogeneous growth. It is well established that film
growth model is always employed to explain the crystal growth in
an organic solution, which has also been extended to the battery
system for grasping the dendrite growth [57]. It has been proposed
that film growth relies highly on surface energy difference between
the film (γF) and substrate (γS) if neglecting the lattice misfit [58].
Considering that γS is particularly low and almost equal in
different electrolytes, γF plays a pivotal role in determining the
surface energy difference and hence the Li2S nucleation behavior.
Concretely, when the value of γF‒γS is low, Li2S film growth tends
to expand across the substrate following the Frank–van der
Merwe (F–M) mode (Fig. S20 in the ESM). On the contrary, Li2S
film is prone to grow with an island-like surface morphology if
γF‒γS is high. This process occurs to decrease the surface energy
difference, which is called Volmer–Weber (V–W) mode. Notably,
the Stranski–Krastanov (S–K) mode combines both the
characteristics and exhibits a layer plus island growth. In this
respect, γF in the low-donicity electrolyte will be lower than that in
the high-donicity electrolyte due to the sluggish Li2S nucleation
process and tough initial deposition on the substrate. Taken
together, reaction mechanism regarding Li2S nucleation and
growth has been accordingly proposed. Concretely, as for the low-
donicity electrolyte (e.g., DOL/DME), initial formation of Li2S
nuclei from S4
2– is guided by surface diffusion of adatoms due to
the low γF‒γS (Fig. 4(h)). As a consequence, the precipitated Li2S
undergoes 2D planar/layered growth across the substrate, which is
highly dependent on the limited conductive area. At the end, the
growth process would be terminated by the fully coverage of
electrode surface. In other words, tri-phase interface (i.e.,
electrode, electrolyte, and Li2S) has been completely blocked. In
stark contrast, in high-donicity electrolytes, deposited Li2S roots in
the S3
•– radical and is guided by the volume diffusion of precursors
due to a high surface energy of the initial Li2S nuclei, which
exhibits 3D particulate/island growth toward different directions
(Fig. 4(i)). Such an electrochemical process relies highly on the
polysulfide conversion efficiency, hence it will be terminated by
insufficient mass transport after possible transport pathways are
thoroughly curtailed.

energy density. Note that other battery parameters are fixed as
shown in Table S3 in the ESM. The projected energy density is
plotted as a function of the E/S ratio in Fig. 6(h) while LS and CS
are fixed with varied US. It is evident that the low E/S ratio is
crucial to increasing the energy density. In specific, when the E/S
ratio is reduced to 3 μL·mgS
–1, the energy density of Li–S batteries
can readily reach up to 300 Wh·kg–1 and beyond. Moreover, highly
solvating electrolytes can elevate redox kinetics and hence the US,
hence which has emerged as a promising candidate to boost the
electrochemical performance under lean-electrolyte working
condition toward pragmatic application. In this sense, we made a
performance comparison of Li–S batteries with highly solvating
electrolyte design, wherein our upgraded DOL/NMP-NH4I
electrolyte exerts conspicuous advantages on the specific capacity
under no matter typical or starved electrolyte usage (Fig. 6(i)) [28,
52, 62–64]. As for future endeavors to advance the development of
highly solvating electrolyte, related strategies on the protection of
Li metal anode are highly required for stabilizing Li metal anode
and elevating cyclic stability toward pragmatic Li–S batteries.
