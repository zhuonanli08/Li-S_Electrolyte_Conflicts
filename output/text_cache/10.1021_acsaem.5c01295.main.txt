ABSTRACT: The cycle life of high-energy Li−S cells is largely constrained by
the quick electrolyte depletion. LiNO3 has been a well-established additive
known for protecting the Li metal anode and stabilizing the battery from
polysulfide “shuttling”. However, it can be depleted prematurely and can pose
safety risks when exposed to carbon, sulfur, or Li metal under harsh conditions.
In this study, LiPO2F2 was explored as a safe and durable alternative additive in
ether-based electrolytes. LiPO2F2 demonstrates superior performance in Li/S
batteries, especially under high sulfur loading (∼4 mg/cm2) and lean electrolyte
conditions (E/S = 4), achieving a long-term cycling stability of 40%, compared
to 14.7% with LiNO3. This additive facilitates the disproportionation of
polysulfides, reducing their dissolution and mitigating the shuttle effect.
Additionally, LiPO2F2 promotes the formation of a stable solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI) composed of inorganic anion-derived species, improving the
battery’s overall stability and functionality. These findings blaze a trail in the design of safer and more durable electrolytes for Li−S
batteries.
KEYWORDS: Li−S batteries, catalytic electrolyte additive, LiPO2F2, lean electrolyte, high-loading cathode
■INTRODUCTION
The continuous innovation in energy storage technologies
including lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has been driven by the
fast-growing demand for electric vehicles and grid energy.1−3
While LIBs with high specific energies have dominated the
energy storage market, their high material costs limit their
potential for large-scale applications. Lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries have emerged as a promising alternative for next-
generation energy storage systems due to their low cost and
environment friendliness. Moreover, Li−S batteries can
theoretically deliver a specific energy of 2600 Wh kg−1, nearly
10 times that of commercial LIBs.4,5 However, achieving such
high-energy and long-lifetime batteries for practical applica-
tions remains a significant challenge. Low ionic and electronic
conductivities of sulfur species, sluggish kinetics of the sulfur
reaction, the “shuttling effect” of Li polysulfides, and lithium
anode corrosion are key issues yet to be solved.6
Various strategies have been proposed and successfully
demonstrated to improve the Li−S battery performance,
including cathode host design,7,8 protective anode coating,9−13
reaction kinetics promoters,14−19 and electrolyte engineer-
ing.18,20−22 Among them, electrolyte design is considered an
effective approach, as it was proven to not only facilitate the
kinetics of sulfur reaction but also protect the Li metal anode.
LiNO3 was first introduced as an electrolyte additive in 2008
and has become one of the most well-known additives for Li−S
batteries.23 Even in small amounts, LiNO3 mitigates polysulfide
shuttling by forming a protective layer on the Li anode, leading
to improved Coulombic efficiency.24 It also contributes to the
formation of the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) by oxidizing
polysulfides into LixSOy species while reducing to LixNOy
itself.25 Additionally, LiNO3 was found to enhance sulfur
utilization and stability in sulfur cathodes,26,27 as well as to
modulate the solvation structure of LiPSs, thereby inherently
reducing their reactivity with the lithium metal.28 Despite
significant research, the exact mechanism behind the protective
function of LiNO3 in Li−S batteries is still debated and
requires further investigation.
It is worth noticing that the benefits of LiNO3 always come
with a trade-off. Zhang et al. described in the study that LiNO3
undergoes an electrochemical reaction on the sulfur cathode
when the discharge voltage drops below 1.7 V,29,30 which has a

Jozwiuk et al. further studied gas evolution in Li−S batteries
and linked it to the presence of LiNO3.32 These chemical and
electrochemical side reactions cause a quick depletion of
LiNO3, diminishing its stabilizing effect on the Li metal anode.
Increasing the LiNO3 content in the electrolyte can expedite
gas evolution. Moreover, when an excess of LiNO3 in the
presence of sulfur and carbon black is packed in a tight space
within a cell, it poses safety concerns, such as fire or explosion
under harsh conditions, owing to the similarity of the cathode
components to those found in gun powders (containing C, S,
and KNO3).26,27
To improve the long-term cyclability of Li−S cells, new
alternative additives that can effectively protect the Li anode
while mitigating electrolyte consumption are required. This
work introduced lithium difluorophosphate (LiPO2F2) as an
additive to replace LiNO3 in Li−S batteries. Our results
showed that LiPO2F2 can significantly improve the cycling
performance of a high-loading sulfur cathode (∼4 mg/cm2)
from 14.7 to 40% over 100 cycles. We investigated the
functions of LiPO2F2 in Li−S cells and highlighted its dual
effect to improve the Li−S cell performance under lean
electrolyte conditions. The study revealed that the LiPO2F2
additive can (1) accelerate the conversion of soluble LiPSs into
less soluble species, thereby limiting the crosstalk of LiPSs
between anode and cathode, and (2) protect the Li metal from
undesired reactions with lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) by
forming a robust, inorganic-rich SEI layer.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Properties of Electrolytes with Different Additives.
The ionic conductivities of the baseline, 0.5 M LiNO3, and 0.5
M LiPO2F2 electrolytes are presented in Figure 1a. At room
temperature, ionic conductivities of 0.5 M LiNO3 and 0.5 M
LiPO2F2 are comparable (11.46 mS cm−1), slightly lower than
the baseline without an additive (11.78 mS cm−1). This
decrease can be attributed to the increased salt concentration
in both 0.5 M LiNO3 and LiPO2F2, which increases the
viscosity and consequently reduces conductivity, particularly at
a low temperature. This observation is further supported by

The electrochemical stability of LiPO2F2 was evaluated and
compared with other electrolytes using cyclic voltammetry
(CV), with carbon paper as the working electrode to amplify
any potential side reactions. Without additives, the onset
potentials triggered by side reactions appear when the voltage
drops below 1.0 V vs Li/Li+ (Figure 1b). This phenomenon is
believed to be induced by the polymerization/decomposition
of DOL and the reduction of the LiTFSI salt. For the
electrolyte containing 0.5 M LiNO3, a sharp reduction peak
was observed at around 1.3 V, corresponding to the
electrochemical decomposition of LiNO3 (Figure 1c). It is
noted that the observed LiNO3 reduction potential is lower
than the typical 1.7 V reported for Li−S cells.33 This
discrepancy arises because carbon paper, rather than a sulfur-
containing cathode, was used as the working electrode in this
study. In addition, the reduction peak diminishes or shifts to
even lower potentials in subsequent CV cycles, underpinning
the depletion of LiNO3 or passivation of the electrode surface.
Interestingly, the 0.5 M LiPO2F2-based electrolyte witnessed a
minimal reduction above 1.0 V, with reductions beginning only
below 1.0 V (Figure 1d), similar to that of the baseline
electrolyte. This result suggests that LiPO2F2 does not undergo

Electrochemical Cycling Performance. The role of
LiPO2F2 alongside other additives in Li−S cells with high-
loading cathodes was studied (Figure 2). It is noteworthy that
LiPO2F2 has a similar ability to suppress LiPSs shuttling as
LiNO3. Without any additive, the cell showed a very low CE
starting from the first cycle and witnessed an implausibly large
charging capacity resulting from severe LiPSs shuttling (Figure
2a,e). In contrast, Li−S cells regardless of the additives used
achieved much higher CE, highlighting the effective
suppression of LiPSs shuttling. During the formation cycle at
C/20, the 0.5 M LiPO2F2 electrolyte (Figure 2c) delivered a
higher efficiency than the LiNO3-based one (Figure 2b), 94 vs
63%. In terms of cycling stability, the LiPO2F2 additive also
showed a higher capacity retention after 100 cycles, i.e., 40%
compared to 14.7% from LiNO3 (Figure 2d). These results
indicate that LiPO2F2 has a better functionality than LiNO3 in
suppressing LiPSs shuttling, which will be discussed in the
following sections.
The crucial role of LiPO2F2 in enhancing the durability of
Li−S batteries is further demonstrated by the performance of a
dual-additive electrolyte, containing 0.2 M LiNO3 and 0.3 M
LiPO2F2. While the dual-additive electrolyte showed slightly
lower initial charge/discharge capacities (1471 and 1027 mAh
g−1, respectively) compared to 0.5 M LiNO3, it exhibited
significantly higher CE and capacity retention, with a CE of
70% and a retention of 22%, compared to the LiNO3-based
electrolyte (CE = 60%, retention = 14.7%) (Figure S2). This
result highlights the effectiveness of LiPO2F2 even at a low
concentration (0.3 M) and in the presence of LiNO3. Further
optimization in terms of their composition might help combine
the benefits of both additives, which will be the focus of future
studies.
Fang et al. reported that the formation of inactive (“dead”)
lithiumwhich consists of both electrochemically formed Li+
compounds in the SEI and electrically isolated unreacted
metallic Li0causes capacity loss and safety hazards in Li
metal anode-based cells.34 The distinct quantification of
unreacted metallic Li0 and Li in components of the SEI in Li
metal batteries has been successfully performed using the
titration gas chromatography (TGC) method. However, in
Li−S systems, this quantification is more complicated, as
inactive Li metal (both SEI components + inactive metallic
Li0) can be revived through chemical reactions with
polysulfides.35 Herein, with the TGC method, we simply
quantified the total Li metal (active + inactive) after cycling at
10th and 50th cycles and compared these values to the pristine
Li metal (Figure 3). In the first 10 cycles, capacity loss in cells

To gain a deeper understanding of the effect of the LiPO2F2
additive on the LiPS conversion process, Li||Li symmetric cells
were employed to observe the variation in the overpotential on
the Li metal (Figure 4a). In the presence of LiPSs, the baseline
electrolyte exhibited the highest overpotential of 140 mV at the
beginning compared to 100 mV for both the 0.5 M LiNO3 and
0.5 M LiPO2F2 electrolytes. After 200 h of cycling, the
overpotential increased to 200 mV for the baseline electrolyte,
indicating the insulating behavior of the SEI layer caused by
reactions between the Li metal and high-order LiPSs, which
resulted in the formation of poorly conductive Li2S and Li2S2
on the Li surface. This was further confirmed by the EIS
spectra, where the baseline electrolyte displayed the largest
semicircle compared to the other two electrolytes (Figure 4b).
Meanwhile, the overpotential for both the 0.5 M LiNO3 and
0.5 M LiPO2F2 electrolytes remained almost the same over 200
h of cycling, benefiting from the interaction among their
additives, lithium metal, and LiPSs via different pathways. This
suggests that the SEI layers in these two electrolytes stabilize
after a few cycles and remain stable throughout 200 h of
cycling. For LiNO3, previous studies have shown that it acts as

The formation of less soluble species is evident from the
color tracking test (Figure 4c), where the color of the 0.5 M
LiPO2F2 electrolyte changes from brown to light yellow, with
the appearance of tiny yellow solids after 24 h. This implies
that the concentration of LiPSs was partially reduced by

CEI Analysis on S Cathode after 10 Cycles. The
composition of the CEI on the S cathode after 10 cycles was
analyzed by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).
The F 1s spectra reveal a prominent peak at 689.0 eV,
corresponding to the CF3 fragment from the TFSI−anion
(Figure 5b,f,j). Additionally, no significant P−F or Li−F peaks
were observed in the F 1s spectra, suggesting that the fluorine-
containing CEI on the cycled S cathode primarily originates
from the decomposition of the LiTFSI salt. Among the
electrolytes tested, the 0.5 M LiPO2F2 electrolyte exhibited the
highest fluorine content (32.7%) (Figure S4), which can be
attributed to the higher abundance of fluorine compounds in
this electrolyte compared to others. The presence of fluorine-
rich species in the CEI has been shown to provide enhanced
protection to the Li metal, reducing parasitic reactions with the
electrolyte, which aligns with the improved electrochemical
performance observed with the LiPO2F2 additive in this
study.38
Interestingly, P 2p spectra were observed exclusively in 0.5
M LiPO2O2 electrolytes (Figure 5m), with no corresponding
signal detected in the dual-additive electrolyte (Figure S3).
This observation confirms that the CEI derived from the
LiPO2F2 electrolyte contains inorganic compounds, such as
LixPFyOz and LixPFyOzSt. The formation of LixPFyOzSt occurs
through a nucleophilic reaction between highly soluble lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs) and LiPO2F2, resulting in the creation of
less soluble LixPFyOzSt species. This finding is consistent with
the color tracking test, where the addition of LiPSs to the 0.5
M LiPO2F2 electrolyte caused a color change from brown to
light yellow, accompanied by the appearance of a yellow
precipitate. These results suggest that the “shuttling” effect can
be mitigated by the addition of LiPO2F2, which redirects the
LiPSs conversion pathway more favorably.
Additionally, the N 1s spectra offer further insights into the
decomposition behavior of the TFSI−anion and NO3
−, as well
as the stability of the electrolytes. Since neither the baseline
nor the 0.5 M LiPO2F2 electrolytes contain LiNO3, the N-
containing decomposition products on the cycled S electrodes
are similar and consist of Li3N, −TFSI, N anion, and N cation.
In contrast, the two electrolytes containing LiNO3 show two
distinct peaks at high binding energy, corresponding to nitrite
and nitrate compounds. Nitrite is a product of the reduction
process, while nitrate originates from the residual salt absorbed
into the porous structure of the S cathode. It was also
demonstrated that the passive layer on the S cathode is not the
primary factor responsible for the mitigation of the “shuttling”
effect by the LiNO3 additive.

on the cycled Li anode was conducted to investigate the anodic
interfacial properties and their impact on the electrochemical
performance (Figure 6). Key SEI components were identified
in the N 1s and P 2p spectra, which highlight the role of the
investigated electrolyte additives. In both baseline and 0.5 M
LiNO3 electrolytes, the TFSI−anion decomposes into TFSI-
derived compounds and Li3N, indicating vigorous decom-
position of the TFSI−anion, especially in the baseline
electrolyte. However, when LiPO2F2 was introduced, only
TFSI-derived species were detected, suggesting a more stable
chemical environment that inhibits the decomposition of the
TFSI−anion. In the case of the LiNO3 additive, there is
evidence that NO3

the lithium metal, forming nitrite compounds. This finding
supports the spontaneous reactions between LiNO3 and Li

The decomposition of LiNO3 in Li−S batteries involves a
significant electron-transfer process, wherein the NO3
−ion
accepts eight electrons from lithium metal to undergo
complete decomposition. This process primarily results in
the formation of Li2O, along with a small amount of Li3N,
which are key components of the SEI. However, Li2O is not
detected in the XPS spectra, possibly due to its reaction with
polysulfides in the lean condition, leading to the formation of
oxidized sulfur products.43 Nevertheless, this ongoing side
reaction is depleting LiNO3 over time, and the oxidized sulfur
products passivating the interphase are uncontrolled, leading to
a thick SEI layer and capacity degradation.
On the other hand, as observed on the cycled S cathode,
only the P 2p signal can be seen on the cycled Li anode with
0.5 M LiPO2F2. It is suggested that LixNOy clusters, which
have a higher affinity for LiPSs than LiPO2F2, capture LiPSs
through a dipole−dipole interaction known as the “lithium
bond”.36 This interaction prevents the detection of P 2p signals
in the dual-additive electrolytes (Figure S3). Therefore, in the
presence of LiPO2F2 as the sole additive, highly soluble LiPSs
are effectively converted into less soluble species, which
mitigates the “shuttling effect” and reduces the corrosive
impact on the lithium metal anode. This observation is also
supported by the TGC results, where the rapid disproportio-
nation of LiPSs helps prevent Li loss through reactions
between the Li metal and polysulfides (Figure 3). Our XPS
analyses and electrochemical results, which show high
Coulombic efficiency (CE) and extended cyclability when
using LiPO2F2 as an additive, further confirm the additive’s
influence on redox kinetics and the polysulfide shuttle effect.
■CONCLUSIONS
The role of LiPO2F2 as an alternative additive to LiNO3 in Li−
S batteries was investigated. Results from Li||Li symmetric and
Li−S cells indicated that LiPO2F2 effectively protects the Li
anode, as evidenced by a reduced Li inventory loss and
enhanced cycling performance. Unlike LiNO3, which depletes
quickly due to its involvement in both chemical and
electrochemical pathways during cell operation, LiPO2F2
primarily operates through a chemical reaction pathway. This
process alters highly soluble LiPSs into less soluble species,
mitigating the “shuttling” effect caused by high-order LiPSs
and thereby extending the cell’s lifespan. Furthermore, detailed
XPS analysis revealed the formation of a fluorine-rich SEI layer
when LiPO2F2 is used, providing improved protection for the
anode. This dual functionality of LiPO2F2 facilitates highly
reversible reactions and significantly enhances cycling stability,
which is essential for the development of high-energy-density
Li−S batteries.
