A B S T R A C T
Lithium‑sulfur (Li−S) batteries are among the most promising candidates for advanced energy storage tech­
nology; however, their commercial viability is bottlenecked by the sluggish redox kinetics of sulfur and the 
notorious polysulfide shuttle effect. Here we apply a single amino acid, L-leucine (Leu), as a liquid electrolyte 
additive to curtail these critical issues and enhance the performance of the battery. With an ultralow dosage of 
0.1 wt%, the additive greatly increases wettability of the electrolyte on the separator and facilitates Li+ ion 
transport. Leu also exhibits a catalytic effect on the conversion of soluble Li2Sx to solid Li2S, effectively 
combating the shuttle effect. Consequently, Li-S cells with the Leu additive display improved rate performance 
and higher discharge capacities during cycling. This study showcases the novel and facile application of an 
abundant amino acid, opening avenues for further investigation into bio-derived electrolyte additives for Li-S 
batteries.
1. Introduction
Lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries are top contenders for advanced 
energy storage systems due to their exceptional theoretical capacity 
(1675 mAh/g), high gravimetric energy density (2600 Wh/kg), and 
their nontoxic and abundant cathode material [1–4]. However, Li–S 
batteries suffer from the notorious shuttle effect, in which polysulfide 
intermediates (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) generated during the sulfur reduction 
reaction dissolve into the liquid electrolyte and undergo parasitic re­
actions with the Li anode [5,6]. Even worse, the sulfur redox reactions 
are complex and sluggish due to the solid-liquid-solid phase change, 
diminishing rate capability and allowing continued polysulfide disso­
lution [7,8]. Ultimately, these key obstacles limit the commercial 
viability of Li–S batteries through severe performance deficits such as 
low Coulombic efficiency, persistent capacity decay, and shortened 
lifecycles.
Extensive efforts have been dedicated to solving the shuttle effect 
and improving the redox kinetics of sulfur. The most common approach 
involves the spatial confinement and electrocatalytic conversion of 
shuttling polysulfides, through modifications to the cathode (e.g. surface 
coatings [9–12], carbon host materials [13,14], or multifunctional 
binders [15–17]) or the separator (e.g. functionalization, [18–20] 
polysulfide-blocking interlayers, [21–24] or novel freestanding 
separators [25–28]). In addition, electrolyte additives have also proven 
to be an effective strategy. Since the electrolyte directly mediates in­
teractions with the separator and both electrodes, electrolyte additives 
have fostered performance improvements in the cells through a broad 
range of mechanisms. Electrolyte additives serving as soluble catalysts 
[29–32], SEI-forming compounds [33–36], redox mediators [37–39], 
and polysulfide-slowing agents [40,41] have all been reported in the 
literature, inhibiting the shuttle effect and improving cycling stability. 
However, these strategies often rely upon expensive, inaccessible, and/ 
or environmentally harmful materials. Hence, pursuing renewable bio- 
derived alternatives for these components is a promising prospect.
Proteins are natural polymers with structures that are more diverse 
and complex than conventional polymers. In biological settings, proteins 
carry out critical roles in mechanical reinforcement and ion transport 
[42–45]. These properties directly apply to battery components. 
Furthermore, the abundant polar groups contained within proteins have 
repeatedly demonstrated the ability to trap polysulfides. These favorable 
characteristics have been exploited through a variety of protein-based 
separators [46–48] interlayers [46,47], cathode binders [48–51], 
doped carbon precursors [52,53], conductive carbon nanocoatings 
[54,55], and gel-polymer electrolytes [56,57]. These treatments 
constrain soluble polysulfides to the cathode side, alleviating the shuttle 
effect and thus improving battery performance. While protein-based

modifications to the cathode and separator have been explored in Li–S 
batteries, the application of proteins to the liquid electrolyte is rarely 
reported. Different battery systems have employed proteins [58–61] or 
amino acids [62–66] as effective electrolyte additives, but only one 
instance has been reported for Li–S batteries [67]. Considering that 
proteins structures are highly variable and contain diverse sequences of 
amino acids, investigating an individual amino acid provides a simpli­
fied and tailored approach to biomolecular electrolyte additives.
In this study, we apply a free amino acid which fully dissolves into 
the liquid electrolyte. Out of three amino acids studied, L-leucine (Leu) 
displays the most promising performance. With an ultralow additive 
dosage of 0.1 wt%, the electrolyte gains affinity towards the separator, 
which facilitates wetting and Li+ transport. Moreover, Leu exhibits a 
catalytic effect on the redox reactions of soluble polysulfides, enhancing 
the electrochemical kinetics of the cell. Li2S nucleation tests demon­
strate that the electrolyte containing Leu accelerates the conversion of 
liquid-phase Li2Sx to solid Li2S2/Li2S, inhibiting the polysulfide shuttle. 
Consequently, Li–S cells with the Leu additive display improved rate 
performance, retain higher capacities for over 500 cycles, and achieve 
initial discharge capacities as high as 1044.4 mAh/g. These results 
illustrate the effectiveness of a simple bio-derived electrolyte additive in 
counteracting the critical issues of Li–S batteries.
2. Experimental
2.1. Raw materials
L-Leucine (Leu, >98 %), L-arginine (Arg, >98 %), L-glutamic acid 
(Glu, >99 %), sulfur (S, reagent grade), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 
mw = 534,000), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, reagent grade), 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL, anhydrous >99.8 %), dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous 
>99.5 %), and 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, anhydrous >99.5 %) were 
purchased from sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. Lithium sulfide (Li2S, >99.9 %) 
and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, >99 %) were 
purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Lithium metal chips with a 
diameter of 15.6 mm were purchased from BatterySource Co., LLC. 
Celgard 2400 (25 μm thickness), carbon-coated Al foil, and carbon black 
(CB, Super C45) were purchased from MTI Co., Ltd.
2.2. Preparation of electrolytes
The pristine electrolyte was comprised of 1 M LiTFSI in a mixture of 
DOL/DME (1:1, v/v), with 2 wt% LiNO3 added. L-leucine (0.5 wt%) was 
added to the pristine electrolyte and stirred at 70 ◦C for 24 h. The so­
lution was diluted to various concentrations (0.2, 0.1, and 0.05 wt%). 
Li2S6 electrolytes with a concentration of 0.2 M were prepared by dis­
solving a 1:5 molar ratio of Li2S and S powders into electrolytes with and 
without the Leu additive. The mixtures were stirred vigorously at 60 ◦C 
until no solids remained, yielding a red-brown stock solution. The stock 
Li2S6 electrolyte was diluted accordingly for the Li2S nucleation and 
symmetrical cell CV tests. All electrolytes were drawn through a 0.22 μm 
syringe filter to eliminate any impurities introduced during handling.
2.3. Preparation of sulfur cathode and dummy (sulfur-free) cathode
S and carbon black (CB) powders were milled together to obtain a 
homogeneous mixture, which was added into a PVDF/NMP solution to 
form a slurry. The mass ratio of PVDF/S/CB solids in the slurry was 
10:67.5:22.5. The slurry was cast onto carbon-coated Al foil with a film 
applicator (50 μm), then dried in a vacuum oven at 50 ◦C for 12 h. The 
cathode sheet was punched into 12.7 mm diameter discs with sulfur 
loadings in the range of 1.0–1.2 mg/cm2. Dummy cathodes were pre­
pared using the same method as the sulfur cathode, with mass loadings 
of ~0.6 mg/cm2 on Al foil. The mass ratio of PVDF/CB solids in the 
dummy cathode was 4:3.
2.4. Materials characterization
The wetting behaviors of electrolytes with and without Leu were 
characterized using an optical contact angle analyzer (OCA 15 plus, 
Dataphysics Instruments, SCA20). A constant droplet volume of 3 μL and 
drop speed of 15 μL/s were used for all measurements. Electrolyte up­
take was measured by comparing the weight of Celgard 2400 samples 
before and after immersion. For consistency, measurements were aver­
aged over six trials. The morphology of the cycled anode was charac­
terized by a scanning electron microscope (Apreo VolumeScope) with 
integrated energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The chemical 
composition of each electrolyte was analyzed via ATR-FTIR (Nicolet 
iS50, OMNIC).
2.5. Cell assembly
CR2025 coin cells and Celgard 2400 separators were used for all 
electrochemical characterizations. All coin cells were crimped to 50 MPa 
to ensure consistency. Unless otherwise noted, the electrolyte volume 
for all cells was fixed at 60 μL.
2.6. Ionic conductivity, Li+ transference number, and LSV tests
Ionic conductivity was measured via electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy of symmetrical stainless-steel cells, using an electro­
chemical workstation (BioLogic VSP, EC-Lab). Ionic conductivity is 
defined by Eq. (1): 
σ = t/ARb
(1) 
where t is the sample thickness (25 μm, Celgard 2400, manufacturer- 
provided), A is the electrode area, and Rb is the bulk resistance, which 
is determined from the x-intercept of the resulting Nyquist plot. Li+
transference numbers were measured through chronoamperometry of 
symmetrical Li||Li cells, applying a polarization of 10 mV until the 
response current reached a steady state. The Li+ transference number 
was calculated from Eq. (2): 
tLi+ = Iss(ΔV −I0R0)
I0(ΔV −IssRSS)
(2) 
where ΔV is the applied polarization; I0 and ISS are the initial and steady- 
state response currents, respectively; and R0 and RSS are the interfacial 
resistances before and after polarization, respectively. Both measure­
ments were averaged from three trials for consistency. LSV tests were 
conducted on Li||stainless-steel cells over the potential range of 1.0–5.0 
V at a scan rate of 1 mV/s.
2.7. Symmetrical cell CV and Li2S nucleation tests
For the symmetrical cell CV test, the cell consisted of two (sulfur- 
free) dummy cathodes and 40 μL of Li2S6 electrolyte (50 mM) with or 
without Leu (0.1 wt%). The cells were cycled over the potential range of 
−1.0–1.0 V at a scan rate of 5 mV/s. For the polysulfide nucleation test, 
Li||Li2S6 cells were assembled with Li metal anode and a dummy cath­
ode. The anolyte consisted of 20 μL pristine electrolyte, and the cath­
olyte consisted of 20 μL Li2S6 electrolyte (80 mM) with or without Leu 
additive (0.1 wt%). The cells were first galvanostatically discharged to 
2.08 V at a current of 0.1 mA, then potentiostatically discharged at 2.07 
V for 3000 s. The Li2S deposition capacity was determined by integrating 
the response current with respect to time. The highlighted regions cor­
responding to Li2S6 and Li2S8 reduction were generated from exponen­
tial curve fits. Both tests were conducted on an electrochemical 
workstation (BioLogic VSP, EC-Lab).

2.8. Electrochemical characterization of Li–S cells
Galvanostatic charge/discharge was conducted on a LANHE Battery

2.9. Computational detail
First-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations were 
conducted using the Quantum Espresso simulation package (QE 6.7.0) 
[68]. The exchange correlation functional was described by the Perdew- 
Burke-Ernzerhoff (PBE) method [69] under generalized correlation 
approximation (GGA). Element pseudopotentials were described by the 
efficient Standard Solid-State Pseudopotential (SSSP) library [70]. 
Binding geometry and structure of Arg, Leu, Glu, and Li2S6 were all 
optimized by self-consistent calculations until the total energy change 
was lower than 1.0 × 10−4 eV. Through the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb- 
Shanno (BFGS) [71] quasi-Newton algorithm, all components of all 
forces were <0.01 eV/˚A. The kinetic energy cutoff for charge density 
and potential was 480 Rydberg (Ry), and the cutoff for the wave func­
tion was 60 Ry (816 eV). Semiempirical Grimme's DFT-D3 scheme was 
incorporated into the conventional DFT calculation [72] to account for 
the Van der Waals interactions.
3. Results and discussion
For this study, three amino acids with drastically different side 
groups were selected for investigation. The structures of the amino acids 
L-arginine (Arg), L-leucine (Leu), and L-glutamic acid (Glu) are dis­
played in Fig. S1. First, the solubility of each amino acid in the pristine 
electrolyte was tested. Glu was immediately ruled out due to its inability 
to dissolve near the boiling point of the electrolyte (90 ◦C), while Leu 
and Arg both fully dissolved at 70 ◦C and 25 ◦C, respectively (Fig. S2). 
Next the efficacy of the Leu and Arg additives were compared through 
simple rate tests of Li–S cells. As observed in Fig. S3, Leu improves the 
capacity of the Li–S cell to a greater degree than Arg. As a result, Leu 
was selected for further investigation.
The concentration of Leu was optimized through additional rate 
performance tests (Fig. S4). With higher concentrations of Leu, the ca­
pacity decay decreased, likely due to stronger shuttle-suppressing in­
teractions between Li2Sx and Leu. However, the initial capacities began 
to decline at concentrations above 0.1 wt%, which is attributable to 
viscosity increases in the electrolyte that hinder Li+ transport and in­
crease interfacial resistances at the electrodes. Ultimately, a concen­
tration of 0.1 wt% Leu was selected for all subsequent characterizations 
in order to balance these competing effects. Due to the ultralow dosage 
of Leu, the FTIR spectra of the electrolytes with and without the additive 
were nearly identical, and no new peaks were noticeably introduced 
(Fig. S5).
The performance differences between Leu and Arg can be explained 
by the Sabatier principle for electrocatalysts, which states that the 
adsorption strength between a catalyst and reactant must not be too 
weak nor too strong. With weak adsorption, catalytic interactions will 
rarely occur, while excessive adsorption strength makes desorption 
unfavorable and passivates the catalyst surface. Therefore, optimal 
catalyst performance occurs at a medium adsorption strength [73]. The 
core structures of both amino acids contain carbonyl oxygens, whose 
partial negative charge provides a strong binding site for polysulfides. 
However, DFT simulations in Fig. S6 revealed that Arg possesses an 
additional binding site at its guanidino side group (~0.67 eV). 
Meanwhile, the nonpolar aliphatic side group of Leu interacts very 
weakly with polysulfides (~0.06 eV), contributing negligibly to poly­
sulfide binding. The excessive binding behavior of Arg is qualitatively 
demonstrated in Fig. S7. Upon mixing electrolytes containing Arg and 
Li2S6, a flocculent yellow precipitate forms due to the strong interaction 
between both components. On the other hand, Leu and Li2S6 both 
remain uniform and well-dissolved in the electrolyte after mixing, 
reflecting an appropriate interaction strength.
In understanding the catalytic mechanism of Leu, the carbonyl ox­
ygen in the core structure is of particular interest. Previous studies have 
reported that certain organic molecules with carbonyl oxygen groups 
can behave as soluble catalysts for Li–S batteries. By forming strong 
Li–O bonds between the carbonyl oxygen and the Li+ cation of Li2Sx, 
these compounds reduce the HOMO-LUMO gap of Li2Sx, effectively 
lowering the energy barrier for redox conversion of these species 
[31,32]. Since carbonyl groups possess a partial negative charge, this 
region of the Leu molecule has the highest proclivity to bind Li2Sx and 
form a Li–O bond. Hence, it is speculated that Leu catalyzes the con­
version of polysulfide species by forming Li–O bonds which modulate 
the HOMO and LUMO levels of Li2Sx.
To quantify changes in wetting behavior, optical contact angle 
measurements were taken for electrolytes with and without Leu, using 
the S cathode, Li anode, and Celgard separator as substrates. For both 
electrodes, Leu had a minimal effect on the electrolyte wetting (Fig. S8). 
On the S cathode, both electrolytes reach stable contact angles of ~2.75◦
and ~2.17◦after 1.5 s (for pristine and Leu, respectively). On the Li 
anode the electrolytes reach stable contact angles of ~33.3◦and ~34.0◦
after 1.5 s (for pristine and Leu, respectively). For the separator how­
ever, the electrolyte with Leu demonstrated superior wettability, with a 
stable contact angle of ~37.9◦at 1.5 s, significantly lower than ~44.4◦
for the pristine electrolyte (Fig. 1a). To further confirm the enhanced 
wettability towards the separator, average electrolyte uptake measure­
ments on Celgard were compared for both electrolytes. As seen in 
Fig. 1b, the Leu additive increases the electrolyte uptake of the separator 
from 264 wt% (for pristine) to 387 wt%.
The improved wettability can be rationalized by the amphiphilic 
structure of Leu, which possesses polar and nonpolar moieties on 
opposite sides. Numerous physical and computational studies have 
repeatedly demonstrated that hydrophobic amino acid side groups are 
prone to adsorb onto nonpolar surfaces in an aqueous medium [74–78]. 
For Leu specifically, the nonpolar aliphatic side group has been shown to 
reorient towards hydrophobic surfaces, with its polar carboxyl and 
amine groups facing outwards [79]. Given that the polypropylene 
separator is extremely hydrophobic, and that the organic electrolyte is 
highly polar due to high concentrations of Li salts, it stands to reason 
that the side group of Leu would adsorb onto the separator surface, 
driven by hydrophobic interactions. This phenomenon is illustrated in 
Fig. 1c, where the nonpolar side group of Leu reorients and adsorbs onto 
the hydrophobic separator surface. Subsequently, the polar carboxyl 
(−COOH) and amine (−NH2) groups of Leu face towards the electrolyte, 
endowing the separator with higher affinity for ions (Li+, TFSI−, NO3
−) 
and solvent molecules (DOL, DME). Owing to its amphiphilic structure, 
Leu effectively mediates the separator-electrolyte interface, guiding the 
electrolyte wetting of the separator.
The superior wettability imparted by the Leu additive also impacts 
the ion transport properties of the electrolyte. These changes were 
investigated through measurements of ionic conductivity and the Li+
transference number. As seen in Fig. 2a, the ionic conductivity of both 
electrolytes remains on the same order of magnitude (0.724 mS/cm for 
Leu, vs 0.645 mS/cm for pristine), indicating a trivial impact on overall 
ion transport. Since liquid electrolytes already possess relatively high 
ionic conductivities, further improvements are challenging to effectuate, 
especially considering the ultralow dosage of Leu. Thus, the minimal 
increase in ionic conductivity is reasonable, and the additive has no 
adverse effect on ion transport. Interestingly, a significant increase in 
the Li+ transference number was observed (0.655 for Leu vs 0.610 for

To assess the compatibility of the additive with the highly reactive Li 
anode, symmetrical Li||Li cells with and without Leu were subjected to 
galvanostatic cycling. Fig. S11 depicts the voltage profiles of each Li||Li 
cell. Throughout 150 h of cycling, both cells display similar over­
potentials, indicating that the addition of Leu does not destabilize the Li 
anode. This was further confirmed by soaking pieces of Li metal in each 
electrolyte (Fig. S12). Even after soaking for one week, no visible 
changes were observed on the Li metal or in the electrolytes, indicating 
the Leu additive is chemically compatible with the anode. Finally, linear 
sweep voltammetry (LSV) tests were performed on Li||stainless-steel 
cells to assess the electrochemical stability of the additive. As seen in 
Fig. 2c, the LSV response currents in the range of 1.0–5.0 V exhibit very 
similar profiles. No additional peaks are introduced, and neither elec­
trolyte decomposes until the threshold of ~4.0 V. Therefore, Leu is 
stable within the operating voltage range of Li–S cells.
To confirm the catalytic effect of Leu, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests 
were conducted on symmetrical cells with Li2S6 electrolytes and dummy 
(sulfur-free) cathodes. The resulting CV profiles are depicted in Fig. 3a. 
At a moderate scan rate of 5 mV/s, the cell with the electrolyte con­
taining Leu exhibits higher peak currents than the pristine cell, indi­
cating that the additive promotes the conversion of soluble Li2Sx. To 
understand the effect of Leu on Li2S deposition, polysulfide nucleation 
tests were performed via chronoamperometry. Fig. 3b–c depict the 
potentiostatic discharge curves of the Li||Li2S6 without and with Leu in 
the catholyte, respectively. The potentiostatic discharge curves can be 
divided into three regions, corresponding to (1) the rapid reduction of 
Li2S8, (2) deposition of Li2S, and (3) reduction of residual Li2S6. Since a 
small amount of Li2S8 is formed in the polysulfide catholyte due to 
disproportionation and comproportionation reactions, a rapid initial 
drop in current is observed as Li2S8 is rapidly reduced, denoted by the 
orange region. In the blue or gray regions, the response current reaches a 
peak where solid Li2S nucleates and deposits. Finally, after the electrode 
surface is passivated by insulating Li2S deposits which block electrically 
active sites, the residual Li2S6 reduces at a much slower rate, which is 
denoted by the yellow region. As observed from the polysulfide nucle­
ation test, the cell with Leu displays a higher Li2S deposition capacity 
(155.6 mAh/g, vs 139.6 mAh/g for pristine), suggesting that Leu ac­
celerates the conversion of Li2Sx to Li2S2/Li2S. Additionally, it is 
noticeable that the response current peak for the cell with Leu occurs at 
an earlier time (~650 s vs ~746 s for pristine), indicating faster 
nucleation time. This is consistent with higher average precipitation rate 
observed in the cell with Leu (2.39 × 10−4 mA/s, vs 2.14 × 10−4 mA/s 
for pristine). Due to the shorter nucleation times contributed by Leu, the 
opportunity for polysulfide accumulation in the electrolyte is reduced.
PITT (potentiostatic intermittent titration technique) tests of Li|| 
Li2S6 cells with and without Leu further confirm that the additive ac­
celerates Li2S nucleation (Fig. S13). Titration voltages above 2.05 V 
correspond to the reduction of Li2S6 to shorter chain lengths. The cell 
with Leu exhibits higher initial response currents at this stage, indicating 
faster liquid-liquid conversion of polysulfides. At 2.05 V, a broad peak 
appears, corresponding to the nucleation of Li2S on the dummy cathode. 
The peak for the cell with Leu has a higher intensity (~0.50 mA) and 
occurs earlier (~1328 s) than the pristine cell (0.43 mA, and 1472 s 
respectively) similarly to the nucleation tests (Fig. 3b–c). At 2.00 V, the 
pristine cell continues to display a sloping response current due to lag­
ging Li2S nucleation. In contrast, the response current of the cell with 
Leu resolves more quickly. This indicates that Leu reduces the over­
potential required to nucleate Li2S.

Fig. 3d compares the CV profiles of Li–S cells with and without Leu at a 
scan rate of 0.1 mV/s. Both cells exhibit two cathodic peaks and one 
anodic peak that are characteristic of Li–S electrochemistry. The two 
cathodic peaks at ~2.3 and ~2.0 V correspond to the conversion of 
cyclo-S8 to soluble Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, 8), and then further reduction to solid 
Li2S2/Li2S, while the anodic peak at ~2.55 V is ascribed to the oxidation 
of Li2S back to cyclo-S8. For the cell with Leu, the oxidation peak shifts 
towards lower potentials (2.55 V for Leu vs 2.56 V for pristine), while 
the reduction peaks shift towards higher potentials (1.99 and 2.29 V for 
Leu, vs 1.94 and 2.27 V for pristine). This suggests that Leu lowers the 
activation energy for the conversion of sulfur species, improving the 
redox kinetics. The cell with Leu also exhibits higher peak current 
densities, which further supports the notion of improved kinetics and Li+
transport.
Li–S CV tests were also performed at different scan rates to gain 
insight into Li+ ion diffusion behavior (Figs. 3e, S14). At higher scan 
rates, higher peak current densities are observed. Peak B is of particular 
interest, since it involves the conversion of soluble Li2Sx to solid Li2S2/ 
Li2S, known as the potential-limiting step of the sulfur reduction reac­
tion [80,81]. Upon increasing the scan rate from 0.1 to 0.5 mV/s, peak B 
for the cell with Leu shifts by 180 mV in the negative direction, while the 
pristine cell shifts by a greater magnitude (188 mV in the negative di­
rection). The smaller polarization at peak B is consistent with the Li2S 
nucleation tests, further indicating that the additive improves the 
deposition kinetics of Li2S. In Fig. S15, the Randles-Sevcik plots for 
peaks A, B, and C are generated by plotting the peak current densities 
against the square root of the scan rate. For all three peaks, both elec­
trolytes exhibit linear relationships, indicating that the rate of the 
electrochemical reaction is diffusion-governed [82]. According to the 
Randles-Sevcik equation, the lithium diffusion constant (DLi+) is directly 
proportional to the slope of the Randles-Sevcik plots. From Fig. 3f, 
higher slope magnitudes are observed for the cell with Leu at all three 
peaks. Therefore, it is concluded that the Leu additive increases the 
lithium diffusion constant for each peak. This corroborates the beneficial 
roles of Leu in separator wetting and Li+ ion transport.
To investigate the effects of Leu on the deposition morphology of Li2S 
at the cathode, SEM images were collected after 25 cycles. In the pristine 
cell (Fig. 4a), several large agglomerates of Li2S particles are visible on 
the cathode surface, resulting from the sluggish nucleation process. 
These particles block the porous cathode structure, cutting off active 
sites deeper within the cathode and preventing the conversion of soluble 
polysulfides which remain accumulated in the electrolyte. However, for 
the cell with Leu (Fig. 4b), no large deposits of Li2S are observed, and the 
cathode retains its interconnected porous structure. The porous and 
homogeneous morphology of the cathode cycled in Leu is attributed to 
the improved redox kinetics imparted by the additive, aligning with the 
characterizations conducted in Fig. 3.
SEM images were also collected for the cycled anodes. As seen in 
Fig. 5a, the Li anode cycled in the pristine electrolyte displays a rough 
surface morphology after 10 cycles. The stark contrasts between bright 
and dark regions indicate an uneven topography. Sluggish redox kinetics 
at the cathode allow soluble polysulfides to escape, which react para­
sitically with the anode and create pits as indicated by the darker regions 
of the image. The lighter regions are topographically higher, repre­
senting cluster-like Li2S deposits and mossy dendrite growths. The 
dendrites result from incomplete electrolyte wetting of the separator and 
limited Li+ transport channels, which cause localized Li+ deposition. 
Conversely, the anode cycled in the presence of Leu (Fig. 5b) shows 
milder contrasts between bright and dark regions, indicating a leveler 
topography and smoother surface after 10 cycles. Pits and Li2S deposits 
on the anode surface are smaller in size due to the inhibition of the 
polysulfide shuttle. After 25 cycles, (Fig. 5c–d) the morphological dif­
ferences are more pronounced. The anode cycled in pristine electrolyte 
has developed a thick and loose solid-electrolyte interface (SEI), with 
cracks and large granular Li2S deposits on the surface resulting from 
repeated polysulfide shuttling. Conversely, the cell with Leu develops a 
more compact SEI, and large Li2S deposits do not appear on the surface. 
Since Leu does not modify the separator morphology, dendrites inevi­
tably grow due to inconsistencies in the pore size/distribution of Celgard 
which promote nonhomogeneous Li+ flux and deposition. EDS maps of 
sulfur on the cycled anode surface after 25 cycles are shown in Fig. 5e–f. 
The anode cycled in the pristine electrolyte shows a significantly higher 
prevalence of sulfur compared to the anode in Leu, attributable to the 
pervasive Li2S deposition from shuttling polysulfides. These ex-situ 
characterizations support the previous findings and proposed mecha­
nisms of Leu.
Fig. 6 summarizes the mechanisms of Leu for improving Li–S battery 
performance. In Fig. 6a, the pristine electrolyte fails to properly wet the 
separator, resulting in fewer Li+ channels and poorer Li+ transport and 
deposition. Meanwhile, the electrolyte with Leu has superior wettability 
on the separator and more Li+ channels due to the amphiphilic structure 
of Leu, which mediates the electrolyte-separator interface. In Fig. 6b, the 
cathode in the pristine electrolyte suffers from sluggish redox kinetics, 
resulting in the accumulation and shuttling of soluble Li2Sx in the 
electrolyte. With Leu present on the other hand, the cathode converts 
Li2Sx more rapidly, which inhibits the polysulfide shuttle. Consequently, 
the Li–S cell with the Leu additive has faster Li+ transport and reduced 
polysulfide shuttling (Fig. 6c), contributing to improved battery per­
formance as shown in Fig. 7.
Coin cells were assembled to compare the performance of Li–S 
batteries with and without the Leu additive. The electrochemical 
impedance spectra (EIS) of fresh cells with both electrolytes were 
collected three times for repeatability (overlayed in Fig. S16). Each EIS 
plot consists of three main resistances which occur in the low-, mid-, and 
high-frequency regions: (1) The bulk resistance (Rb) occurs in the high- 
frequency region and corresponds to the x-intercept of each EIS plot. It is 
primarily governed by ohmic resistances from the separator and elec­
trolyte, as well as external contributions such as the battery casing or 
electrical connectors. Rb is generally associated with the movement of 
ions through the electrolyte, i.e. the ionic conductivity. (2) The inter­
facial resistance (RSEI) occurs in the mid-high frequency region, corre­
sponding to the first semicircle in the EIS plot. RSEI is governed by the 
diffusion of ions through the SEI on each electrode and is therefore 
associated with SEI growth and stability. (3) The charge transfer resis­
tance (RCT) occurs in the mid-low frequency range and corresponds to 
the second semicircle. RCT is governed by the charge transfer kinetics at 
the surface of the electrodes, and thus is associated with the surface 
properties of the cathode and the Li2Sx conversion kinetics between the 
solid and solution phases [83–86]. A fourth element, the Warburg 
impedance, is governed by mass transfer in the low frequency region but 
was excluded from analysis due to insufficient data points.

average resistance of each component are displayed in Fig. 7a. The cell 
with Leu displayed a marginally higher Rb than the pristine cell (3.6 Ω 
and 3.5 Ω respectively). This is consistent with the similar ionic con­
ductivities of both electrolytes, and the increase could be attributed to

4. Conclusions
In summary, the amino acid L-leucine (Leu) is employed as a liquid 
electrolyte additive for Li–S batteries. With the addition of only 0.1 wt 
%, electrolyte wetting and Li+ transport of the separator improved 
notably, evidenced through a lower contact angle, higher Li+ trans­
ference number, and a 1.5-fold increase in electrolyte uptake. This was 
attributed to the amphiphilic structure of Leu, which bridged the 
interaction between the nonpolar separator and polar electrolyte. 
Moreover, the additive counteracted the shuttle effect by catalyzing the 
conversion of soluble Li2Sx to solid Li2S. Due to these notable im­
provements, the Li–S cell with Leu achieved a high initial discharge 
capacity of 1044.4 mAh/g at 0.1 A/g, and retained higher capacities 
than the pristine cell for 500 cycles at a demanding current density of 0.5 
A/g. With a straightforward approach, this study illustrates the role of a 
single amino acid as a multifunctional electrolyte additive for effectively 
enhancing the performance of Li–S batteries.
