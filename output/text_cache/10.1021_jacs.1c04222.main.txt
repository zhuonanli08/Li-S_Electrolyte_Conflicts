ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have a high
theoretical speciﬁc energy; however, their performance is plagued
by the shuttle eﬀect of lithium polysulﬁdes and the instability of
the lithium anode interface. Great eﬀorts have been made using
electrolyte additives to address the issues. Herein, we report a class
of electrolyte additives, i.e., benzenedithiols (BDTs). Among the
three isomers of BDTs, 1,4-BDT shows the best eﬀect on the
performance improvement of a Li−S battery because it can bond
more sulfur atoms than the other two. The functionality of 1,4-
BDT on the cathode and anode involves the chemical reactions of
thiol groups. The S−S bonds were generated from 1,4-BDT and
sulfur through oligomerization, which change the original redox
path of sulfur and inhibit the shuttle eﬀect of lithium polysulﬁdes.
In addition, 1,4-BDT can form a smooth and stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI), which can enable the Li/Li symmetric cell
with an ultralow overpotential of 0.08 V at a high current density of 5 mA cm−2 for over 300 h. The Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT displays
the highest cycling stability at a C/5 rate, with an initial capacity of 1548.5 mAh g−1 and a reversible capacity of 1306.9 mAh g−1 after
200 cycles. The Li−S pouch cell with 1,4-BDT and 2.8 g of sulfur exhibits an initial capacity of 2640 mAh and a capacity retention
rate of 84.2% after 26 cycles at a C/10 rate. This work demonstrates that organodithiol compounds can be used as functional
electrolyte additives and provides a new direction to design materials for advanced Li−S batteries.
■INTRODUCTION
With the widespread deployment of electric vehicles and
intelligent electronic devices, high-energy-density and low-cost
rechargeable batteries are strongly demanded.1,2 Lithium−
sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been extensively studied as high-
energy-density alternatives to Li-ion batteries due to their high
speciﬁc capacity of electrodes.3 However, during charge or
discharge, the complicated Li−S chemistry involves the
formation of lithium polysulﬁde intermediates (LiPSs),
resulting in the shuttle eﬀect in the charge and discharge
processes.4,5 The high-order LiPSs (Li2Sx, x = 4−8), which are
highly soluble in the ether electrolyte, detach from the cathode
and reach the lithium metal anode to be chemically reduced
into low-order LiPSs (Li2Sx, x = 3). They subsequently shuttle
back to the cathode and get oxidized into high-order LiPSs.
Such LiPSs, shuttled between two electrodes due to the
chemical potential and concentration diﬀerences, result in
several drawbacks:6 low sulfur utilization, severe self-
discharge,7 electron/ion transfer obstruction, low Coulombic
eﬃciency, and Li anode corrosion.8,9
Numerous eﬀorts have been reported to address the shuttle
eﬀect for the sulfur cathode10−12 from physical conﬁnement or
chemical adsorption/catalysis.13−16 In addition, these mod-
iﬁcations cannot be applied to the Li metal anode
simultaneously. These modiﬁcations toward the cathode have
been implemented on the premise that LiPSs have been
produced.17−23 While these methods are built on the external
regulation, it is needed to provide alternative chemical
pathways for the sulfur redox reactions and transform the
LiPSs from the origin.24 Recently, with the progress of
electrode materials, great breakthroughs have been made using
electrolyte additives to address the Li−S battery problems.
Electrolyte additives owning to their functional groups could
supply the functional additive moieties, build the stable
interfacial layers, and suppress the shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs.
The introduction of organic functional groups is a feasible
scheme for the direct chemical modiﬁcation of LiPSs dissolved
in electrolyte through binding sulfur covalently.25−27 They
oﬀer tunable groups, which can be deeply involved in the
electrochemical conversion of sulfur and its properties
regulation, also be beneﬁcial for the Li anode.

Herein, the functional benzenedithiols (BDTs) as electrolyte
additives are proposed for the direct denaturation. BDT is an
organosulfur compound with the formula C6H4(SH)2. It
consists of a phenyl ring with a pair of thiol groups, having
three isomeric structures (i.e., 1,2-BDT, 1,3-BDT, and 1,4-
BDT), as shown in Figure 1a. The conjugate bases of these
diprotic compounds serve as building blocks for the synthesis
of other organosulfur compounds. For sulfur cathode, the
introduction of dithiol groups in BDTs directly changes the
reaction pathways of high-order polysulﬁdes through binding
sulfur radicals to form sulfur−sulfur (S−S) bonds during
charge process, as predicted in Figure 1b (1). Consequently,
the formation of S−S bonds between thiol groups and sulfur
displays a variety of advantages for Li−S batteries, including
stabilizing sulfur sites, activating dead sulfur atoms, enhancing
electrochemical kinetics, and supplying homogeneous chemical
reactions. For lithium metal anode, as predicted in Figure 1b
(2), 1,4-BDT (as a representative) can react with lithium
metal. The resultant organic lithium salt 1,4-PhS2Li2 (Li2−1,4-
BDT) in the electrolyte is deposited together with other
lithium compounds on the lithium anode to form solid-
electrolyte interphase (SEI). The SEI can promote ion transfer
but block electrons, thus protecting the lithium anode and
sustaining stable cyclability.28,29 Hence, Li−S cells with
covalently stabilized sulfur achieve a signiﬁcant capacity
retention of 67.5% for 500 cycles with a reversible capacity
of 909.3 mAh g−1. This strategy provides a new direction to
design functional SEI and interfacial chemistry for advanced
Li−S batteries.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To distinguish the eﬀect of three isomers on battery
performances, 1,2-BDT, 1,3-BDT, and 1,4-BDT were intro-
duced in a blank electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI and 1 wt % LiNO3 in
DME/DOL (1:1 v/v)) for a cycling test, which is called “BDT
electrolyte”. The eﬀects of three isomers additives with
diﬀerent concentrations on the performance of Li−S batteries
were compared. As shown in Figure S1a−c, with the increase
of the concentration from 0.15 to 0.30 and 0.45 M, the
capacities of the cells decay seriously. With the decrease of the
concentration from 0.15 to 0.10 and 0.05 M, the achievable
discharge capacities decrease (results not shown). The results
show that 0.15 M BDTs enable the cells to keep stable cycles.
The performances of cells with diﬀerent BDTs were also
compared. The results show that 0.15 M 1,4-BDT electrolyte
additive has the most excellent performance (Figure S 1d).
The further comparison is shown in Figure 1c; the Li−S cell
with the 0.15 M 1,4-BDT electrolyte displays the highest
cycling stability at a C/5 rate, with an initial capacity of 1548.5
mAh g−1 and a reversible capacity of 1306.9 mAh g−1 after 200
cycles. In comparison, the Li−S cells with 0.15 M 1,2-BDT or
1,3-BDT and the control cell (Li−S cell without electrolyte
additive) rapidly fade in capacity after 200 cycles (Figure S2a).
The speciﬁc capacity data of the ﬁrst and 200th cycles extracted
from Figure 1c were made into a tendency chart (Figure S2b).
The capacity retention rates of the 1,2-BDT, 1,3-BDT, 1,4-
BDT, and control cells are 81.4%, 77.8%, 84.4%, and 69.4%,
respectively, after 200 cycles. 1,4-BDT improves not only the
achievable capacity but also the long cycle performance of the
battery. Analyzing the data in more detail, the capacity
contributed by 1,4-BDT is about 100 mAh g−1 (Figure 1c and
Figure S3a). But, after using 1,4-BDT, the capacity and
plateaus of the cell were changed. The increase of cell capacity
brought by 1,4-BDT is greater than the contribution of 1,4-
BDT itself. As shown in Figure S3b, there is a clear diﬀerence
in the voltage proﬁles, especially in the ﬁrst discharge plateau
(2.40−2.06 V). The Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT has a smaller
slope angle and a larger discharge capacity, exceeding the
theoretical capacity in the ﬁrst discharge plateau of Li−S
battery.
To study the relationship between BDT isomers and battery
performance, the recharged products of the Li−S cells with
BDTs were analyzed by ultrahigh performance liquid
chromatography quadrupole time-of-ﬂight mass spectrometry
(UPLC-QTOF-MS). It shows that 1,2-BDT (Figure S4a) and
1,3-BDT (Figure S4b) in the Li−S cells are prone to be self-
polymerized because the bond angle of the thiol groups is less
than 180°. According to the previous work reported, 1,2-BDT
and 1,3-BDT are inclined to form the dimer and trimer,
respectively, due to the steric stabilization eﬀect.30 The S−S
bonds are stable and invulnerable to being attacked because of
steric stabilization eﬀect. Therefore, the capacity of self-
polymerized oligomers containing additional sulfur atoms
decreases and the elemental sulfur does not have suﬃcient
protection. However, 1,4-BDT is completely undisturbed by
the steric hindrance eﬀect because of the 180° bond angle.
Thus, the sulfur radicals have more freedom and sites to form
S−S bonds in the structure of the tetrasulﬁde oligomer. In
terms of the BDTs’ intrinsic eﬀect on the battery, 1,4-BDT is
the best choice of the three isomers as an additive in the
electrolyte. Hence, both BDT and lithium nitrate (LiNO3)
were used as additives in the ether-based electrolyte, which
would enable a synergetic eﬀect. It could reduce the sulfur loss
by forming oligomers and form a stable and uniform SEI layer
on the lithium anode.

the discharge capacity of the cell with 1,4-BDT increases about

yellow and pink regions) compared to that of the control cell.
In addition to the conversion of S to Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8),
oligomers formed from 1,4-BDT and sulfur also bond with Li+
and e−to generate Li2−1,4-BDT and Li2S. Hence, the
discharge capacity of the ﬁrst plateau is larger than that of
Li−S battery. In the pink region (2.35−2.06 V), the Li−S cell
with 1,4-BDT shows a high reversible capacity after 200 cycles.
This strongly indicates that the formation of oligomers inhibits
the loss and shuttle eﬀect of polysulﬁdes. For other cells, the
speciﬁc capacity loss in the pink region is much higher than
that in the cell with 1,4-BDT, leading to more attenuation in
the blue region (2.06−1.80 V). Especially, the control cell
exhibits serious capacity fading in the voltage range 2.30−1.80
V. In this region, the dissolved Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8) is gradually
transformed into Li2Sx (2 < x < 4) and insoluble Li2S2/Li2S,
which means heterogeneous reactions resulting in the loss of
active materials.31 When the charging process starts, insoluble
Li2S is converted to Li2Sx (2 ≤x < 4). With the increase of
sulfur atoms, Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8) begin to dissolve into the
electrolyte, encountering the benzenedithiolate radicals that
are extracted of Li+ and e−. Two benzenedithiolate radicals are
combined to from one tetrasulﬁde dimer, which could
accommodate 2∼4 sulfur atoms to form oligomers through
covalent S−S bonds. In the discharge process, they are
lithiated. With the formation and cleavage of S−S bonds, the
reversible storage and release of sulfur occur, which are
expected to directly constraint the sulfur atoms and fragments.
Consequently, the routine sulfur conversion is altered by
oligomerization on the basis of S−S bonds, leading to the
regulation of the sulfur species.
The electrochemical reaction of the traditional Li−S battery
involves solid−liquid−solid phase conversions of sulfur species
on the cathode.32 Multiple phase transitions result in mass
transfer processes on the interfaces, incomplete conversions of
Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8) in the reactions, and slow reaction
dynamics.32 The data of the Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT prove that
the introduction of 1,4-BDT has changed the electrochemical
redox process of Li−S battery, supplying homogeneous
chemical reactions and enhancing electrochemical kinetics.
The charge and discharge curves of the Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT
have the best overlap (Figure S6), indicating that the cell is
highly reversible. It suggests that 1,4-BDT can suﬃciently
promote the reduction from the S8 to Li2S2/Li2S and improve
the reversibility of the Li−S battery.
The electrochemical performance of the Li−S cell with 1,4-
BDT was evaluated using CR2032 coin cells. As shown in
Figure 2a, the initial discharge capacities of the Li−S cells with
and without 1,4-BDT are 1347.1 and 1121.9 mAh g−1,
respectively, at a C/2 rate. The capacities after 500 cycles are
909.3 and 413.6 mAh g−1 corresponding to the capacity
retention rates of 67.5% and 36.9%, respectively. The capacity
decay rate of the cell with 1,4-BDT is barely 0.065%, which is
in sharp contrast to 0.13% per cycle of the control cell. The cell
with 1,4-BDT also has a higher speciﬁc capacity and better
stability and Coulombic eﬃciency than the control cell at a 1 C
rate, as shown in Figure S7. The electrochemical process of the
Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT was studied with cyclic voltammetry
(CV). As shown in Figure 2b, when the voltage is swept from
3.0 to 1.8 V, two obvious cathodic peaks appear at 2.35 and
2.06 V. During the following anodic scan, the peaks at 2.25 and
2.40 V are attributed to the oxidation of lithium sulﬁdes to
sulfur/oligomers, which are consistent with the charging and
discharging plateaus. Moreover, the redox peaks are overlapped
completely, indicating that the cell is highly reversible. For
comparison, the CV curves of the ﬁrst cycle of the control cell,
Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT, and Li/1,4-BDT cell without sulfur
are provided in Figure S8. In the cathodic scan process, the
ﬁrst reduction peak (I) of the control cell corresponds to the
reduction of S8 to Li2Sx (x = 4−8), and the peak (I) of the cell
with 1,4-BDT is almost same as that of the control cell. The
diﬀerence between them appears in the voltage range 2.30∼

In addition, the 1,4-BDT electrolyte additive shows
conspicuous stability toward both the sulfur cathode and Li-
metal anode. The excellent rate performances of the 1,4-BDT
cell are shown in Figure 2c. The discharge capacities at C/10,
C/5, C/2, and 1 C rates are 1424.2, 1336.6, 1296.5, and
1199.6 mAh g−1, respectively. When the rate returned to C/2
and C/5, the discharge speciﬁc capacities were recovered to
1274 and 1365.6 mAh g−1, respectively (Figure S9), which are
all higher than those of the control cell. The corresponding
voltage proﬁles of diﬀerent C rates also show small voltage
hysteresis for the discharge plateaus.
Figure 2d shows the impedance evolution of the Li−S cell
with 1,4-BDT during cycling. The impedance in the high-to-
medium frequency region is assigned to the electron/ion
transfer resistance caused by passivating surface layer formed
on interfaces. The values of the cell with 1,4-BDT are lower
than those of the control cell (Figure S10a,b) in the fresh state
and 100th cycle. The addition of 1,4-BDT decreases the side
reactions on lithium metal anode and greatly reduces
accumulation of dead lithium. In addition, the decreasing
electron/ion transfer resistance in the cell with 1,4-BDT
conﬁrms the kinetic promotion. Recycling dead Li through
chemical reactions reduces the formation of mossy lithium on
the anode and creates a clean interface. The addition of 1,4-
BDT injects higher electrochemical activity to sulfur atoms,
regulates polysulﬁdes, and changes the electrochemical path-
ways of sulfur in the cell. To understand the beneﬁts of 1,4-
BDT in Li−S cells and facilitate the possibility for further
practical applications,33 the electrochemical performances with
high sulfur mass loadings were evaluated. Figure 2e displays
the cycling stability of electrodes with a sulfur mass loading of
3.5 mg cm−2 and E/S = 10 μL mg−1 at a C/10 rate. The Li−S
cell with 1,4-BDT achieves an initial discharge capacity of
1124.1 mAh g−1 and a reversible capacity of 1049.5 mAh g−1
after 50 cycles. The speciﬁc capacity and Coulombic eﬃciency
of the control cell decay rapidly after 20 cycles, which is related
to the shuttle eﬀect and the serious corrosion of the Li anode.
In addition, the cell with 1,4-BDT can work stably for 50 cycles
with a sulfur mass loading of 2.2 mg cm−2 and E/S = 6 μL
mg−1 at a C/10 rate (Figure S11). To show the potential
application of 1,4-BDT, it was applied in a Li−S pouch cell
with a sulfur mass loading of 6.5 mg cm−2 and a total sulfur
mass of 2.8 g (Figure 2f). The E/S ratio in the pouch cell can
be further reduced to 3.5 μL mg−1. As shown in Figure 2g, the
pouch cell shows a high initial capacity of 2640 mAh at a
discharge current of 300 mA, corresponding to the speciﬁc
capacity of 943 mAh g−1, which is based on the mass of sulfur.
The pouch cell delivers an initial speciﬁc energy of 349 Wh
kg−1 on the basis of the mass of the cathode, anode, and
electrolyte. The pouch cell remains at a decent cycling
performance, which maintains a high capacity of 2223.9 mAh
corresponding to the speciﬁc capacity of 794 mAh g−1, and
shows a speciﬁc energy of 298 Wh kg−1 after 26 cycles. The
voltage proﬁles (inset of Figure 2g) are the same as those of
the coin cells. The pouch cell data prove that the 1,4-BDT
electrolyte can be applied well in a large-scale cell and can
further reduce electrolyte dependence. However, it is worth
noting that the parameters of the pouch cell are derived from
the molar-ratio ampliﬁcation according to the coin cell, so the
performance of the pouch cell still has room to be further
optimized.

From the characterizations of the recharged and discharged
products and the exploration of the redox process, the charge−
discharge mechanism diagram of the Li−S cell with 1,4-BDT
was formulated, as elaborated in Scheme 1. H abstracted from
the S−H group is irreversible; then, Li2-1,4-BDT and Li2S are
produced during the initial discharge process. In the following
recharge, Li2S and Li2-1,4-BDT are delithiated and the formed
radicals PhS2·and S· are combined and oligomerized; then, the
oligomers C12H8S6 (I) and C12H8S8 (II) are formed. For the
oligomer C12H8S6 (I), the Li+ and e−attract the middle sulfur
atom, cause the bridge S−S bonds to be broken, and then yield
the Li2−PhS2 (1) and Li2−PhS4 (2), respectively. The lithiated
intermediate product 2 is involved in the following discharge,
causing the breakage of the S−S bonds in 2. The cleaved
radicals S· and PhS2· combine with 4 Li+ and 4 e−, forming the
ﬁnal products Li2S and Li2−PhS2 1. For the oligomer C12H8S8
(II), the bridge S−S bonds in the middle are broken ﬁrst,
forming Li2−PhS4 2. The second step is the same as above.
Therefore, the entry of S into the BDT oligomers initiates
structural recombination, which alters the redox pathways of S,
reduces the formation of lithium polysulﬁdes, and ultimately
reduces the shuttle eﬀect.

■CONCLUSION
In this work, a multifunctional electrolyte additive 1,4-BDT has
been identiﬁed for Li−S batteries. It can simultaneously aim to
the pressing challenges faced by Li−S batteries: (1) shuttle
eﬀect and (2) unstable interfaces. The Li−S battery protected
by the 1,4-BDT additive exhibits an enhancement in the
capacity and stability by 909.3 mAh g−1 after 500 cycles at a C/
2 rate, with an extremely low capacity fading rate of 0.065% per
cycle. A high C rate capability was achieved, i.e., 90% capacity
retention at a 1 C rate compared to that at a C/5 rate. The Li−
S pouch cell with 1,4-BDT exhibits stable performance, with a
capacity of 2223.9 mAh after 26 cycles. This research opens a
simple way originated from interfacial chemistry for the
exploration of a wide range of electrolyte formulations from a
more chemical perspective. Furthermore, the demonstration of
the ability of the proposed electrolyte additive to participate in
redox pathways opens the door for using organodithiols in
alternative cells such as semiliquid batteries and Na−S
batteries.
