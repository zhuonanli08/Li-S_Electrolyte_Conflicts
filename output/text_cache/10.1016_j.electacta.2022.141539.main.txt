Cluster-type lithium polysulfides regulator for high performance 
lithium-sulfur batteries

A B S T R A C T  
Lithium polysulfide intermediates suffer from serious "shuttle effect" and slow redox kinetics, resulting in limited 
rate performance, low discharge capacity and rapid capacity decay of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries. Here, we 
propose a concept of cluster-type lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) regulator to address the above-mentioned prob­
lems. Dipyridyl disulfide (DpyDS) is introduced to be an electrolyte additive to form low solubility macromo­
lecular lithium pyridine-2-thiolate (LiPyS) clusters in electrolyte by in-situ lithiation and Li bond network. The 
LiPyS monomer in the clusters form complexes (LiPyS-LiPSs) with LiPSs through bi-directional Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds, so as 
to restrict LiPSs to the clusters on the cathode side, preventing the parasitic reaction between LiPSs and Li metal. 
At the same time, LiPyS-LiPSs have higher HOMO energy levels and lower LUMO energy levels than that of single 
LiPSs. As a result, LiPSs can achieve an enhanced redox activity, and the Li-S battery regulated with DpyDS 
provides a capacity as high as 900 mA h g −
1 at 2 C rate.   
1. Introduction 
To achieve a cleaner and more sustainable society, the development 
of high-energy-density rechargeable batteries is fundamental [1]. Sulfur 
is one of the most abundant elements on the earth, and the theoretical 
energy density of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries is as high as 2600 Wh 
kg−1 [2]. It has been well known that the charge and discharge products 
of sulfur cathodes are insulating solid sulfur (S8) and lithium sulfide 
(Li2S), respectively, and the intermediate products are soluble lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs), such as Li2S4, Li2S6 and Li2S8 [3]. With the intro­
duction of carbon-based materials [4–6], the electrical conductivity of 
the charge and discharge products is improved, resulting in enhanced 
rate capabilities. However, the interaction between carbon and LiPSs is 
not sufficient to inhibit the shuttling of soluble intermediates [7]. At the 
same time, the transformation between solid and dissolved products 
inevitably leads to a loss of active species [8–10]. Therefore, confining 
LiPSs to the cathode side and improving the redox kinetics of sulfur 
species becomes a key for realizing high-performance Li-S batteries [11, 
12]. 
In these studies, the design of "thiophilic" surfaces is becoming a 
successful strategy that strongly interacted with LiPSs to suppress their 
diffusion in the electrolytes [13]. For example, surface modification of 
carbon materials with oxygen/nitrogen/sulfur functional groups can 
effectively reduce the shuttling effect [14–16]. On the other hand, a 
large number of catalysts [17,18] have been introduced into Li-S bat­
teries, such as Nb2O5-x [19], LiVPO4F [20], Co9S8 [21] and Li1+xMn2O4 
[22], to improve the ability of converting sulfur species by reducing the 
activation energy and elevating the redox kinetics. These works 
demonstrate that heterogeneous catalysts can enhance the bidirectional 
conversion of soluble long-chain LiPSs to insoluble short-chain 
Li2S2/Li2S and S8 [23]. 
Soluble catalysts can easily achieve fast conversion kinetics for LiPSs. 
Various electrolyte additives, such as dichlorobenzoquinone [24], thi­
oacetamide [25], and propionitrile [26], have been shown to be efficient 
catalysts for LiPSs and Li2S, which are useful to improve the 
charge-discharge capacity at high current densities. Unfortunately, 
liquid-phase catalysts cannot inhibit the "shuttle effect" of LiPSs due to 
their high solubility. In order to achieve intrinsically stable conversions 
of LiPSs, the introduction of a low solubility additive becomes a feasible 
strategy to improve the redox ability. 
Herein, we propose a cluster-type additive lithium pyridine-2- 
thiolate (LiPyS) group used as lithium polysulfides regulators to ach­
ieve stable transformation of LiPSs. Reference has confirmed that 
dipyridyl disulfide (DpyDS) is an electrode material with stable cycling 
performance [27]. Therefore, we dissolved DpyDS in the electrolyte as 
an additive for Li-S batteries, and then triggered a lithiating reaction in

2. Experimental section 
2.1. Material preparation 
2.1.1. Raw materials 
All raw materials, including elemental sulfur (S), lithium sulfide 
(Li2S), disulfide dipyridine (DpyDS), poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), 
N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP), polypropylene (PP) Separator, aluminum 
(Al) foil, lithium (Li) foil, 1,3-dioxane (DOL), 1,2-diamethoxyethane 
(DME), lithium nitrate (LiNO3), lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony) 
imide (LiTFSI), carbon paper (CP), and carbon nanofibers (CNF) were all 
purchased from commercial sources. The lithium-sulfur electrolyte 
consists of 1 M LiTFSI and 1% LiNO3 dissolved in DOL/DME (v: v = 1:1) 
solvent. 
2.1.2. Material preparation for characterization 
To analyze the products of the coordination reaction, three base 
solutions were prepared. The Li2S6 solution was prepared by mixing S 
and Li2S with a molar ratio of 5:1 in DOL/DME (v: v = 1:1) solvent, 
followed by vigorous magnetic stirring at 60 ◦C for 24 h under an argon 
atmosphere. As a result, a brownish-red Li2S6 solution (1 mol L −
1) was 
obtained. Additionally, a quantitative amount of DpyDS powder was 
dissolved in DOL/DME solution to obtain a DpyDS solution (1.0 mol L −
1). Subsequently, the two solutions were mixed in equal volumes to 
obtain a mixed solution (LiPyS-Li2S6) for material testing. 
2.1.3. Cathode preparation 
CNF and S were mixed with a weight ratio of 3:7. The mixture was 
uniformly ground in a mortar for about one hour, and then transferred to 
a glass tube for sealing. Afterwards, the mixture was heated in a tube 
furnace (nitrogen atmosphere) for 8 h to obtain CNF@S composite 
material. CNF@S (90 wt.%) and PVDF (10 wt.%) were thoroughly mixed 
in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry. The obtained slurry was 
evenly coated on a piece of carbon- covered aluminum foil, and vacuum 
dried at 60 ◦C for 8 h. Electrodes with a diameter of 12 mm were cut out 
by the MSK-T10 manual slicer. The areal sulfur loading was controlled at 
1.5 mg cm−2. Following the same method, a batch of 3 mg cm−2 elec­
trodes were prepared for testing at low electrolyte content.

2.3. Electrochemical characterization 
2.3.1. Equipment 
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectros­
copy (EIS) tests were conducted by a CHI660D electrochemical work­
station of Shanghai Chenhua Company. The charge/discharge curves of 
batteries were performed using a charge/discharge test cabinet of 
Shenzhen NEWARE Company. 
2.3.2. Symmetric cell measurements 
We chose a commercial CP as the current collector, cut into a 
semicircle with a diameter of 12 mm. Take 40 mL Li2S6 and the mixed 
solution and add quantitative LiTFSI respectively. Two current collec­
tors were assembled into a standard 2032-coin cell with a 20 mm 
diameter separator, and 20 μL of electrolyte was dropped on each side. 
Electrolyte types include blank, Li2S6 and mixed electrolytes. CV mea­
surements were performed at a scan rate of 3 mV s −
1 between −1.5 and 
1.5 V. 
2.3.3. Deposition test of charge and discharge products 
We used Li foil as the anode, porous PP as the separator, CP as the 
positive electrode current collector and different types of electrolytes 
(the positive and negative electrodes are Li2S8/LiPyS-Li2S8 electrolyte 
and ordinary lithium-sulfur electrolyte respectively) to assemble the 
cells. First, the cells were galvanostatically discharged to 2.08 V at 
0.112 mA, and then galvanostatically discharged at 2.07 V to promote 
Li2S nucleation and growth until the current was below 10−5 A. It was 
then charged to 2.39 V at a constant current of 0.112 mA, and finally 
charged to a current of 10−5 A at a constant voltage of 2.40 V to obtain S8 
deposition.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Configuration of LiPyS cluster 
DpyDS is an excellent cathode material for Li-ion batteries with a 
solubility higher than 0.20 M in Li-S electrolyte (Fig. S1). By analyzing 
the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of 13C, it was determined 
that the discharge product was LiPyS (Fig. S2). At a current density of 50 
mA g–1, the working potential window was determined to be larger than 
2.4 V, which is higher than the redox potential of sulfur, indicating that 
LiPyS can continue to play a role in the redox process of sulfur species 
(Fig. S3a). In the charge-discharge curves of 2–5 cycles, the battery ca­
pacity is hardly changed, indicating that DpyDS has been stabilized on 
the positive side during cycling (Fig. S3b). In the CV spectrum, more 
coincident cyclic curves also certify the stability of DpyDS in the 
working process (Fig. S3c). Therefore, DpyDS can be introduced into the 
Li-S battery system as an electrolyte additive (Fig. 1). During discharge, 
the DpyDS additive generates LiPyS through in-situ lithiation, and LiPyS 
forms clusters under the action of Li bonds. 
The optimally structured LiPyS monomer obtained by density func­
tional theory (DFT) computational analysis is presented in Fig. 1. The 
structure is the lowest-energy configuration in which the Li⋅⋅⋅N bond 
length is 1.97 Å, which is smaller than the calculated Li-N bond length in 
Li3N crystals, indicating a stronger bond between Li and N. The charge 
distributions in LiPyS with two different configurations are shown in 
Fig. S4. There is no significant difference in the charges of Li atoms, 
indicating negligible charge transfer after interaction of Li and N. 
Furthermore, the dipole is quite large compared to the charge transfer, 
up to 6.47 Debye. The above results are consistent with the study of Li 
bond chemistry [30], so we believe that Li in LiPyS builds clusters by 
combining pyridine N with Li bond. 
We further investigated the LiPyS cluster. The weight-average mo­
lecular weight (Mw) of the clusters is determined to be 4313 g mol−1 by 
gel permeation chromatography (GPC) (Fig. S5). Since the molar mass of 
the LiPyS monomer is 110 g mol−1, it can be seen that the cluster is 
composed of about 40 monomers. Using the SMD implicit solvent model, 
the calculated absolute value of the solvation free energy of octameric 
LiPyS (GSol = –11.7 kcal mol–1) is much lower than that of monomeric 
LiPyS (GSol= – 21.8 kcal mol–1). This result indicates that the con­
struction of macromolecular clusters reduces the solubility and mobility 
of LiPyS in electrolytes. Therefore, the clusters can be well stabilized on 
the cathode side as LiPSs regulators. The construction of LiPyS clusters 
changes the chemical environment in the electrolyte and lays the 
foundation for the regulation of LiPSs.

mixed to obtain a pale-yellow solution. Comparison with Li2S6 solution 
(reddish-brown) and LiPyS solution (colorless) of the same concentra­
tion indicates that some kinds of reactions take place between the two 
materials. Spectral evidences support the visualization. According to the 
ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectra, LiPyS had double absorption peaks 
and Li2S6 exhibited a significant absorption peak at 260 nm (Fig. S6).

DFT calculations were performed to verify the binding of clusters and 
LiPSs. The calculated structures of lithium polysulfides (Li2Sx, x = 4, 6, 
and 8) and LiPyS monomer were shown in Fig. S7. Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, and 8) 
and LiPyS can be adsorbed by each other through double Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds 
and combined to form stable complexes. The binding energies in LiPyS- 
Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, and 8) were not significantly different, and the inter­
action energies were moderate. This means that the formation and 
dissociation of double Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds between LiPyS and LiPSs are 
reversible and will not hinder the electrochemical reaction of LiPSs [31]. 
In order to further prove the occurrence of the binding reaction, 
Li2S6, LiPyS and the LiPyS-Li2S6 solutions were dried, and the obtained 
solid products were subjected to X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
tests. The S 2p spectra were shown in Fig. 2b, and the peaks at 168.3 eV 
were the thiosulfate generated during the tests [32]. The S 2p spectrum 
of Li2S6 exhibited two S 2p3/2 peaks at 162.3 eV and 163.4 eV, corre­
sponding to Li-S and S-S, respectively [33]. The S 2p spectrum of LiPyS 
also exhibited the same Li-S bond [34]. In contrast, the Li-S peak from 
LiPyS-Li2S6 exhibited a significant shift to the lower energy region. The 
XPS spectra of Li 1 s exhibited in Fig. 2c. The Li 1 s spectrum of Li2S6 
consists of a peak centered at around 54.4 eV, which is assigned to Li-S 
bond [30]. Li 1 s spectrum of LiPyS exhibited a peak at 55.5 eV due to the 
presence of Li bond, which can also be verified by N 1 s spectra of DpyDS 
and LiPyS (Fig. S8) [35]. After the combination of Li2S6 and LiPyS, the 
peaks of Li-S bonds in the new product moved to 55.8 eV and 56.1 eV. 
This means that there are multiple dipole-dipole interactions between 
the molecules [14,30], suggesting the formation of double Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds in 
the complexes. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of 7Li 
also effectively supported the XPS results [16]. Li2S6 spectrum exhibited 
an obvious resonance at 2.8 ppm and the chemical signal moved to 3.5 
ppm after the introduction of LiPyS (Fig. 2d). 
3.3. Influence of cluster on physical properties of LiPSs 
To further understand the effect of cluster-type regulator on LiPSs, 
first-principles calculations were performed on Li2Sx (x = 4, 6, and 8) 
and the corresponding complexes [36] (Fig. 3a). Taking the coordina­
tion process and results of Li2S6 as an example (Fig. 3b), the complex 
(LiPyS-Li2S6) exhibited a higher HOMO energy level and a lower LUMO 
energy level than Li2S6. Because of the lower LUMO energy of the 
complex, it is easier to accept electrons, and the higher energy of the 
HOMO makes it easier to lose electrons. As a result, the complexes can 
not only accept electrons more easily as an oxidizing agent, but also 
donate electrons as a reducing agent. The simultaneous enhancement of 
the oxidative and reductive abilities of the reactant can accelerate the 
reaction kinetics of Li2S6. The calculated results of Li2S4 and Li2S8 also 
exhibited the corresponding energy level changes before and after the 
combination with LiPyS, respectively, indicating that LiPyS can achieve 
excellent mediation ability throughout the discharge and charge pro­
cesses. These results provide a theoretical basis for studying the 
enhancement of the redox kinetics of sulfur species by LiPyS clusters. 
3.4. Evaluation of LiPSs transformation capability 
The actual effect of LiPyS clusters for improving the conversion ki­
netics of LiPSs was evaluated. First, symmetric cells with the equivalent 
amount of Li2S6 electrolyte were assembled, and the effect of the clusters 
on the liquid-liquid transition of LiPSs was investigated by cyclic vol­
tammetry (CV) tests [37]. For reference, a symmetric cell with the blank 
electrolyte containing zero Li2S6 was also tested. The result demon­
strates that current collector and electrolyte contributes negligible cur­
rent [38]. At a scan rate of 3 mV s −
1, the CV curves of the cells with 
Li2S6 and with Li2S6+DpyDS were respectively measured (Fig. 4a). 
Taking the right side of the curve as an example, the battery with 
Li2S6+DpyDS has three peaks, and the battery with Li2S6 has one peak. 
The first peak in the cell with Li2S6+DpyDS and the peak in the cell with 
Li2S6 both represent the transformation of Li2S6. From the comparison of 
the two peaks, we can see that the cells with DpyDS exhibited narrower 
peaks, larger response currents, and higher redox charge storage ca­
pacity, indicating that the modified LiPSs can be rapidly transformed. 
The other two peaks appearing in the cell with Li2S6+DpyDS are related 
to the redox of DpyDS, of which the upper side is the oxidation of LiPyS 
to DpyDS, and the lower side is the reduction of DpyDS to LiPyS. 
The changes of soluble Li2Sx to Li2S deposition and S8 deposition are 
liquid-solid phase transformation processes [39]. To demonstrate the 
transformation between Li2S and LiPSs, we investigated the dynamics of 
the Li2S nucleation process (Fig. 4b) [40]. In the fitted curves, no signal 
that belongs to the transformation of Li2S8 is observed in the cell with 
DpyDS. And in the cell without DpyDS, residual signal of Li2S8 can still 
be recognized when the scan potential is lower than 2.08 V which is a 
sign of polarization inhibition. The cell with DpyDS reached the highest

Li2S and S8, increase the specific surface area of the charge and 
discharge products, accelerate the liquid-solid conversions of sulfur

Finally, to further elucidate the effect of DpyDS on the LiPSs con­
version processes, 0.1–0.5 mV s −
1 CV [41] and electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests [42] were performed for the batte­
ries with and without DpyDS. Although a low solubility macromolecular 
lithium pyridine-2-thiolate (LiPyS) clusters formed, the resistance of the 
cell with DpyDS became smaller. In the EIS test as shown in Fig. S11a, 
we can see that the ohmic resistance (RΩ) increases and the charge 
transfer impedance (Rct) decreases from 165 Ω for the cell without 
DpyDS to 26 Ω for the cell with DpyDS. This is because the introduction 
of low solubility clusters will increase the viscosity of the electrolyte and 
lead to the increase of ohmic impedance. However, the introduction of 
clusters improves the electrochemical activity of Li-S cells and reduces 
the Rct which is the decisive factor and is much larger than the ohmic 
resistance. This result indicates that the clusters improve the electro­
chemical activity of Li-S cells and promote the rapid conversion of sulfur 
cathodes. The CV curves exhibited typical redox characteristics of Li-S 
batteries (Fig. 4c) [43]. The two reduction peaks of the battery with 
DpyDS were shifted to the right by nearly 0.03 V relative to the blank 
battery, and the redox peaks changed to sharper, which is also strong 
evidence that LiPyS clusters improve the redox kinetics of sulfur species. 
In addition, the cell with DpyDS achieved 7 cycles of stable cycling in a 
CV test of 0.2 mV s −
1 scan rate, illustrating the reversibility of the sulfur 
cathode and DpyDS (Fig. S11b). At different scan rates (Fig. S12), the 
well-known Randles-Sevcik equation is employed to describe the diffu­
sion of lithium ion (Li+) during the reaction, and the Li+ diffusion co­
efficient (D) is found to be positively correlated with the slopes of Ip and 
v1/2 [44]. The battery with DpyDS exhibited the higher Li+ diffusion 
coefficients among all of the different peaks (Fig. 4d). This indicates that 
the Li+ diffusion capacity as a decisive step has been improved in Li-S 
battery system.

LiPSs, LiPyS clusters can trap LiPSs through double Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds, effec­
tively suppressing the "shuttle effect", and thereby reducing the loss of 
sulfur. At the same time, the dispersion of LiPSs in the electrolyte is also 
reduced, becoming a low-concentration cluster-LiPSs solution. Accord­
ing to Le Chatelier’s principle, this process will accelerate the generation 
of LiPSs, which is beneficial to the full utilization of S8, and solves the 
problem of large overpotential in this process. Furthermore, the physical 
properties of LiPSs are changed due to the introduction of LiPyS, and the 
Li+ diffusion ability as a rate-limiting step is optimized. Therefore, under 
the action of the clusters, the nucleation process of Li2S is faster and the 
conversion rate is higher, which helps to improve the battery capacity. 
During the charging process, the clusters can also facilitate the decom­
position of Li2S by regulating the concentration of LiPSs, while sup­
pressing the dissolution/migration behavior of LiPSs and improving the 
conversion ability to S8. As the voltage increased, the clusters are 
reverted to DpyDS after completing the conversion of sulfur species. 
Therefore, the introduction of DpyDS additive enables the rapid and 
stable conversions of sulfur species in Li-S batteries. 
3.5. Electrochemical performance of DpyDS-containing Li-S batteries 
LiPyS cluster-modified LiPSs endow Li-S batteries with superior rate 
performance compared to blank battery [45]. Li-S battery with DpyDS 
achieved capacity of 1431, 1311, 1166, 1076, and 900 mA h g −

0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C (1 C = 1675 mA g −
1) rate (Fig. 6a), respectively, 
which is 9–55% higher than that of blank battery (Fig. S13). Although 
DpyDS provides some capacity, its catalytic activity and the restriction 
of clusters on LiPSs is the key factor to improve the performance. The 
galvanostatic discharge-charge profiles at different rates revealed the 
cause of the high current density capability (Fig. 6b). During discharge, 
the low plateau of the batteries with DpyDS exhibited good extension 
compared to the blank batteries. Especially at 2C rate, the low plateau 
exhibits an increase of over 200 mA h g −
1. In addition, the ratio of low 
plateau capacity/high plateau capacity (Qlow/Qhigh) is often used to 
evaluate the conversion efficiency of Li2Sx to Li2S. At 0.1 C rate, the Li-S 
cell with DpyDS provides a Qlow/Qhigh of 2.95, which is 40% higher than 
that without DpyDS, indicating that the introduction of DpyDS can 
enhance the nucleation/conversion of Li2S at higher current densities, 
which can be attributed to the larger area of surface discharge products 
and the optimized energy levels of LiPSs. In addition, the median voltage 
of the charge-discharge curves at 0.1 C decreased from 0.22 eV to 0.19 
eV, and the polarization potential was significantly reduced, which is 
consistent with the exploration results of LiPSs conversion. At the same 
time, a high electrochemical activity of the intermediate will facilitate 
an obvious platform, while a low activity cannot bear a platform due to 
polarization. In Fig. 6b, a new platform appears at 1.85 V for the battery 
containing additives, which may come from the solid-solid conversion 
process between a part of Li2S2/Li2S, indicating that the electrochemical 
activity of the discharge products has been improved. This result is also 
in agreement with the dynamics enhancement in the battery containing 
DpyDS.

batteries, the batteries were assembled under harsher conditions ac­

LiPyS clusters under the condition that the positive surface capacity is 3

demonstrate that under unfavorable conditions, the cluster-type regu­
lator can still be stabilized on the cathode side to continuously capture 
LiPSs and enhance their redox capacity, thereby achieving higher ca­
pacity. In addition, the optimal concentration was determined by 
comparing the effect of the long cycles of 1 C in electrolyte with different 
concentrations of DpyDS (Fig. S19). With the increasing of DpyDS 
concentration, the regulation effect on LiPSs gradually becomes better. 
However, when the concentration of DpyDS reaches 0.20 M, the battery 
capacity decreases sharply. This phenomenon is due to the excess of 
LiPyS relative to bulk converted LiPSs, which leads to deterioration of 
the "shuttle effect" and lithium anode corrosion. Therefore, the optimum 
concentration of DpyDS is 0.15 M. 
4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, we propose a stable cluster model that suppresses the 
"shuttle effect" via double Li⋅⋅⋅S bonds and rationally tunes the physical 
properties of LiPSs. LiPSs bound to LiPyS cluster have higher HOMO 
energy level and lower LUMO energy level, so LiPSs can achieve an 
elevated redox activity. The conventional sulfur cathodes with DpyDS 
exhibit a low-capacity decay rate (an average decay rate of only 0.051% 
after 500 cycles at 1 C) and an excellent rate performance (a capacity of 
900 mA h g −
1 at 2 C rate). The introduction of this cluster-type regu­
lator successfully solves the existing problems of LiPSs, and provides a 
new idea for realizing enhanced Li-S batteries.
