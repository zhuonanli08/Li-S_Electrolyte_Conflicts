lithium-sulfur battery

A B S T R A C T
Lithium metal is among the most promising anode materials for high-energy batteries due to its high theoretical
capacity and lowest electrochemical potential. However, uncontrolled lithium decomposition and dendrite
formation have hindered its practical applications. Here, we propose a facile and eﬀective strategy using thionyl
chloride (SOCl2) as electrolyte additive to in-situ build a stable interfacial protective layer on lithium anode to
prevent Li dendrite growth. Furthermore, the decomposition of SOCl2 could produce active sulfur to oﬀer extra
capacity for cathode in full battery. As a result, the as-assembled lithium-sulfur battery delivered an ultra-high
discharge capacity (2202.3 mA h g−1 at 400 mA g−1) and excellent rate performance (1348.6 mA h g−1 at
3000 mA g−1), as well as remarkable cycling performance. This study reveals an eﬀective avenue to suppress
the Li-dendrites growth and provide a signiﬁcant step towards safe and high-energy Li-S batteries.
1. Introduction
Due to the theoretical capacity and energy limits for traditional
lithium ion batteries (LIBs), the study and commercialization of new
generation batteries have been becoming urgent issues [1,2]. Among
those candidates, Li metal batteries (LMBs) are reviving and receiving
more and more attention in recent years, as Li metal present a high
theoretical capacity (3860 mA h/g) and low electrochemical potential
(−3.04 V vs the standard hydrogen electrode) to meet the requirements
of the “post Li-ion era” [3–6]. However, it need to be noted that several
undesired and fatal weaknesses are seriously hindering the practical
applications of LMBs, such as dendrites, corrosion, volume changes
and pulverizations of lithium anode, inﬁnite formation of solid
electrolyte interfaces (SEI), and other kinds of irreversible reactions
[5,6]. This phenomenon would lead to batteries failures such as low
rate capability, severe capacity loss, or even terrible safety issues.
Various strategies, such as employing solid-state electrolyte, optimizing
electrolyte contents, constructing artiﬁcial SEI layer and Li-hosting
materials, have been developed to suppress the Li dendrites growth [7–
17]. Modifying electrolyte with additives is of particular interest in
term of energy density, cost and easy operation for practical applica-
tions [5,18–20]. The general role of most additives is to assist the
constructions of stable SEI ﬁlms, suppress the formation of Li
dendrites, and enhance cycling performance. Although promising
results have been presented, developing more eﬀective electrolyte
additives are still highly desirable, as suﬃcient passivation between
Li anode and electrolyte is hardly achieved during prolong cycling.
Lithium sulfur (Li-S) batteries, as one of the most promising
energy storage devices in LMBs family, also suﬀer from these
agonizing drawbacks. Besides, there are several other unique bottle-
necks in Li-S batteries system, such as the insulation nature of
element sulfur, volume change, and especially well-known “shuttle
eﬀects” of polysulﬁdes [21–23]. For the improvement of Li-S
batteries, most of previous researches focus on the modiﬁcation of
sulfur cathode and the most successful and widely investigated
strategy is the fabrications of sulfur-based composite cathodes [24–
33]. For example, nanoporous carbon has been used a conductive
host for element sulfur to increase conductivity, provide void space to
buﬀer against volume ﬂuctuations, and physically conﬁne the dis-
solution of the polysulﬁde [34–36]. However, there are still cost and
consistency concerns for large-scale commercial production. In
addition, the host materials are always electro-inactive, which will
also seriously lower the total energy density of Li-S batteries [37–39].
Furthermore, such modiﬁcations can’t resolve the problem of den-
drites growth of Li anode, and resultantly it is hardly to achieve long-
term cycle life and high safety. Thus, it is still a serious challenge to

Herein we present a convenient and practical strategy for both Li
anode protection and capacity enhancement in Li-S system. By only
using small amount of thionyl chloride (SOCl2) as additive into
electrolyte, an inorganic SEI ﬁlm on Li metal could be built and the
total capacity of the battery would be increased simultaneously. These
dual-functions could be realized by a spontaneous redox reaction
between the SOCl2 additive and Li metal anode, which can be described
in Eq. (1) [40–42]:
8Li + 3SOCl2 →6LiCl + Li2SO3 + 2S
(1)
More details of the formation of the SEI ﬁlm and the related
functions could be found in Scheme 1. SOCl2 could be easily
dissolved in non-polarity solvents. After the redox reaction with Li
metal, a solid LiCl-rich ﬁlm could be formed on the metal surface
[41,42]. The reaction can take place before the reactions between
Li metal and electrolyte and thus the inorganic SEI ﬁlm could
signiﬁcantly stop the decomposition of electrolyte, cast oﬀthe
formation of dendrites and dead lithium, prevent the pulverization
of the lithium, and eventually improve the battery performance. In
addition, the generated S from SOCl2 can act as “redox-additives” to
oﬀer extra capacity to the Li-S battery when the Li anode is isolated
from electrolyte via the surface LiCl-rich ﬁlm. Moreover, such dense
SEI layer on Li anode can well prohibit the shuttle process of
polysulﬁde during cycling. Therefore, by adding SOCl2 in the
electrolyte, taking both advantages of anode protection and capacity
enhancements, the performance of Li-S battery could be compre-
hensively improved.

addition, some Li2CO3 are observed in C 1s high resolution patterns of

The in-situ forming robust SEI layer not only can realize anode
protection, but also can prohibit the conventional shuttle eﬀect, thus
leading to high cycle stability Li-S battery. Galvanostatic charge/
discharge measurements are ﬁrstly used to investigate the discharge-
charge performance. Charge/discharge proﬁles at diﬀerent cycles at the
current density of 400 mA g-1 are given in Fig. 3a. The capacity
calculation in this work are all based on the mass of S in pPAN@S
composites, and the sulfur content is about 43.0%. Similar charge/
discharge plateaus can be observed in E-0 and E-1, suggesting that the
adding of SOCl2 will not change the electrochemical mechanisms of the
batteries. The charge/discharge proﬁle are in line with the results of
CVs as presented in Fig. S7, and the CVs well overlap during the
following scan, indicating the excellent reversibility of the assembled
Li-S battery in E-1. It should be noted the initial discharge capacity can
reach up to 2202.3 mAh g-1
, much higher than the theoretical capacity
of element sulfur. The extra capacity is mainly generated from
electroactives additives of SOCl2, and these results conﬁrm that our
initial design is realized. Fig. 3b is cycle curves at the current density of
400 mA g-1 in E-0 and E-1, respectively. It can be seen that the battery
in E-1 show a much better cycle performance, and after 200 cycles, the
discharge capacity can be stably retained at 1531.2 mA h g-1. Fig. 3c
demonstrates the speciﬁc capacities of the Li-S batteries at diﬀerent
current densities (500, 1000, 2000 and 3000 mA g-1 respectively). After
adding SOCl2, the speciﬁc capacity of the E-1 cathode could reach
1602.5 mA h g-1 at current density of 500 mA g-1 after initial activation
process, and the total capacity of the Li-S battery increases signiﬁcantly
of about 300 mA h g-1, comparing with cathode in E-0. Although from
the EIS data in Fig. S8, there are additional RSEI in E-1 anode, the
capacity increasements at 3000 mA g-1 can still deliver a remarkable
achievement (1348.6 mA h g-1 in E-1 after 40 cycles) in comparison of
E-0 battery, proving that adding SOCl2 in electrolyte can be an eﬀective
strategy for enhancing the discharge capacity of pPAN@S battery, as
well as kinetic processes of the electrodes. As presented in Fig. 3d and
S9, the potential plateau in E-1 is well retained as the increasing of
current density, and show a much lower overpotential as compated to
that in E-0, indicating the better kinetic behavior of pPAN@S
composite in E-1. The long-term cycling performance are also given
in Fig. 3e. Under the current density of 1600 mA g-1, the reversible
speciﬁc capacity of battery for E-1 is 1670 mA h g-1, which is 330 mA h
g-1 higher than that in E-0. It should be pointed out that even after 500
cycles, the capacity still retains at 1021 mA h g-1 in E-1 (30% capacity
decay), while the E-0 sample drops to 267 mA h g-1 (80% capacity
decay). This performance can be result in the protection of Li metal,
blocking up the direct contact between the electrolyte and Li metal, and
retarding the formation of the passivation layer and capacity decay.
Now, there is another issue that should be clearly clariﬁed: the
capacity contribution of SOCl2 additives. And thus blank electrodes,
only comprising conductive black and binder, are tested in E-0 and E-1
electrolytes, respectively. The total weight of the blank electrode is used
for capacity calculation. As presented in Fig. S10, the blank electrode in
E-1 can oﬀer a high initial discharge capacity of 365 mA h g-1 and it can
be stably retained at 100 mA h g-1 after 200 cycles; while without SOCl2
additives, the reversible capacity is below 10 mA h g-1. Such a result
could well support our initial design of dual functional additive, which
can both oﬀer extra capacity and protect the lithium anode to ensure
excellent cycle performance. However, higher content of SOCl2 addi-
tives in electrolyte can’t oﬀer a better electrochemical performance. As
shown in Fig. S11, as the SOCl2 additives increase to 5% and 8%, the
initial discharge capacities of the cathode can reach up to 2582 and
3354 mA h g-1, respectively, which also further conﬁrms that the extra

should be noticed that at the ﬁrst 5 cycles in the symmetric cells, Li
metal with additive shows higher potential than the one without SOCl2,

After 5 cycles, the values of two overpotential curves reach to a same

In conclusion, we utilized a simple chemical reaction between
SOCl2 and metal Li to construct a stable SEI layer and simultaneously
generate extra active sulfur to improve the stability and speciﬁc
energy capacity of Li-S battery, respectively. The dissolving sulfur
can oﬀer additional capacity, and thus excellent cycle performance
with ultra-high capacity is obtained. The cathodes presents a ultra-
high initial capacity of 2202.3 mA h g-1 at the current density of
400 mA g-1, and with current density increasing to 3000 mA g-1, the
capacity still can be retained at 1348.6 mA h g-1. In particular,
excellent rate and cycling performance up to 100 h at 3.8 mA cm-2
are obtained in symmetric Li∣Li cells with SOCl2 additives, while poor
cycling performance, even short circuit, is observed in blank electro-
lytes. This work is simple and eﬀective, and more importantly, it will
provide novel thoughts and inspirations for targeting various battery
dramas through basic properties of diﬀerent simple chemical princi-
ples and agents.

3. Experimental procedures
3.1. Materials preparation and characterization
The cathode pPAN@S composite are prepared following the same
procedure we did in our previous work [47,48].
The morphology and structure of pPAN@S composite was exam-
ined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-8010), powder X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD, D8 ADVANCE) and Fourier-transform infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor 27). The components of SEI ﬁlm on the
lithium anode were detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS, Kratos Axis ULTRA incorporating a 165 mm hemispherical
electron energy analyzer) test, all binding energies were referenced to
the C 1s peak (284.8 eV).
3.2. Electrochemical measurements
The working electrodes were prepared by compressing a mixture of
the active materials, acetylene black, and binder (polytetraﬂuoroethy-
lene, PTFE), in a weight ratio of 70:20:10. The composite cathodes
were cut into wafers with a diameter of 10 mm, of which the active
material loading was approximately 1.0 mg cm-2. Li metal was used as
both the counter and reference electrodes and a Celgard membrane was
used as the separator.
CR2032 coin-type cells were assembled in an argon-ﬁlled M-Braun
glove box. A 1 M LiPF6 in propylene carbonate (PC): ethylene
carbonate (EC): diethyl carbonate (DEC) =1:4:5 vol% mixture was
used as the baseline electrolyte, and diﬀerent amount of thionyl
chloride (SOCl2, > 99.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.,Ltd) were
added into the electrolyte for comparison. If there is no special
instruction, electrolytes (50 μL) with 2% SOCl2 (volume) additive are
generally used in this work for discussion.
To measure the electrochemical capacity and cycle life of the
working electrodes, the cells are galvanostatically charged and dis-
charged using LANDCT 2001A battery tester (Wuhan, PRC) in a
voltage range from 1 to 2.8 V vs Li/Li+. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs)
were performed using a CHI 660D electrochemical workstation (CH
Instrument, Shanghai, PRC). CVs were recorded between 1.0 V and 2.8
V with a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s, using the composite as the working
electrode and a lithium sheet as both counter electrode and reference
electrode. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried
out with amplitude of 10 mV over the frequency range from 100 kHz to
0.01 Hz.
