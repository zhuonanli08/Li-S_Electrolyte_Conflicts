1. Supporting Text for Experimental procedures 
Materials and electrolyte preparation: Sulfolane (SL), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), dioxolane (DOL), 
sulfur (≥ 99.0%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99.99%) and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) were 
purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.9 wt.%) was 
purchased from Solvionic. 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE, > 95%) was 
obtained from  Fluorochem. Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, battery grade) was kindly provided 
by Nippon Shokubai. Lithium metal (Li0) disks (14 mm diameter) and foils were purchased from China 
Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. All solvents were treated with a 4Å molecular sieve to remove traces of water 
before use. 2D graphene-based activated carbon  (ResFArGO) composite carbon material was 
synthesized following a previously described method.[1] The conventional electrolyte, referred to as 
DME/DOL, was prepared by mixing 0.5 M of LiTFSI and 0.5 M LiNO3 into DME and DOL (1:1 in vol.) until 
the complete dissolution of salts. Furthermore, the reference sparingly solvating electrolyte, denoted 
as SSE_REF, was acquired by dissolving 2.5 M of LiTFSI in SL, and then adding TTE solvent (1:1.5 in vol.) 
after the complete dissolution of LiTFSI. Finally, the sparingly solvating dual-salt electrolyte, named 
SSE_LiFSI, was prepared similarly to the SSE_REF electrolyte but using 2 M LiTFSI and 0.5 M LIFSI 
followed by the addition of the co-solvent TTE (1:1.5 in vol.). 
Sulfur electrode preparation: The sulfur cathodes were prepared according to the procedure 
described in our recent publication.[2] Briefly, the cathode formulation consisted of 64 wt.% of sulfur, 
26 wt.% of porous and conductive carbon materials, and 10 wt.% of polymeric binder based on 5 wt.%

of CMC and 5 wt.% of SBR using water as solvent. The carbon material was a mixture of the 
commercially available KetjenBlack (KJ600) and a home-made ResFArGO, in a weight ratio of 62:38. 
The sulfur/carbon composite was prepared using a melt diffusion process at 155 ºC for 12 h. The 
positive electrode preparation was carried out following a procedure outlined in a previous work by 
the authors.[1,2] A reference cathode, denoted as KJ600 + Add, was also prepared following the process 
carried out in previous work and using a mixture of commercial conductive additives based on carbon 
nanotubes (CNT), graphene nanoplatelets (GNP), and C-65. 
Theoretical Methods and Computational Details: Classical MD simulations were conducted on the 
three electrolytes using Gromacs.[3–5] The simulation boxes contained the appropriate number of 
molecules to match the experimental concentrations: DME/DOL electrolyte (60 LiTFSI + 60 LiNO3 + 564 
DME + 828 DOL); SSE_REF electrolyte (120 LiTFSI + 510 SL + 480 TTE); SSE_LiFSI electrolyte (96 LiTFSI + 
24 LiFSI + 510 SL + 480 TTE). The initial boxes were generated directly with Gromacs, placing each 
molecule randomly in a larger simulation box with a low density, for all cases. Then, the structures 
were compressed at 10 K with a pressure of 10 bar to reach densities closer to the experiments using 
the Berendsen thermostat and the Parrinello−Rahman barostat (relaxation time of 1 ps in all cases). 
Following this, the systems were gradually heated to 600 K at 1 atm and equilibrated at this 
temperature in a NVT ensemble to break possible metastable configurations. The systems were then 
cooled to the simulation temperature of 298 K at 1 atm. The final configuration was further 
equilibrated for 1 ns at the same temperature and pressure. The production simulation was performed 
for 50 ns in the NVT ensemble to ensure that the system reached a diffusive regime. The cutoff for van 
der Waals force and the real space of Ewald summation was 10 Å. The fast smooth particle mesh Ewald 
(PME) electrostatics was used to treat Coulomb interactions in a periodic system. 
The force field parameters (bond, angle, torsion angle, and Lennard-Jones potential) for all the 
molecules were taken from the OPLS-AA force field and their structures were optimized at the density 
functional theory (DFT) level with the Gaussian 16 software.[6–8] The Becke’s three parameters (B3) 
exchange functional together with the Lee−Yang−Parr (LYP) nonlocal correlation functional (B3LYP) 
was adopted with the 6-31G basis set and single first polarization functions.[9–20] DFT was used to 
calculate the partial atomic charges required for the MD simulations using the restrain electrostatic 
potential (RESP) method. The partial charges for all the molecules were multiplied by a scaling factor 
of 0.8 to approximate polarizability effects.

Physicochemical characterizations: To evaluate the interaction between molecules and solvents in the 
studied electrolytes, Raman microscopy (Renishaw Via confocal Raman spectrometer) with a 532 nm 
wavelength excited laser ranging from 300-1200nm was employed.  
On the other hand, the surface and cross-section morphologies of the deposited Li on the copper (Cu) 
substrate were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta250 microscope). 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Phoibos 150 XPS spectrometer and a non-
monochromatic Mg Kα source (hν = 1253.6 eV) was performed, aiming to study the surface 
composition of the deposited Li. 
Flammability test: The Celgard® 2500 separator was soaked in the electrolyte solution, and the 
combustion behavior was tested by direct ignition using a lighter. The self-extinguishing time (SET) was 
measured by recording the time it took for the flame to naturally extinguish after ignition of a fixed 
amount of electrolyte (approximately 500 µL) dropped over onto a watch glass. The test was repeated 
a minimum of three times for each electrolyte, and the SET values were calculated as an average. 
Electrochemical characterization: The ionic conductivity of the electrolytes was measured at room 
temperature (23 ± 2 ºC) by analyzing the internal resistance of a 2-pole conductivity cell across a 
frequency range of 0.1-106 Hz.  
To evaluate the compatibility of the electrolytes developed in this work with lithium, electrochemical 
measurements of Li||Cu cells were conducted, in order to determine the Coulombic efficiency (CE). 
Different methods were carried out thus gaining a better understanding of the lithium 
anode/electrolyte interaction. In the first procedure, a given capacity of Li metal (QP = 0.5 mAh cm-2) 
was plated onto the Cu substrate, and the stripping/plating of Li metal with a cut-off voltage of 1 V (to 
remove the available lithium from Cu, QS) was performed for n cycles. For this method, the CE was 
calculated using the following equation: CE = (QP/QS)×100. In the second test, the modified Aurbach 
method was employed, which involved substrate preconditioning or stabilization step.[21] For this step, 
a high capacity of Li metal is plated on Cu (5 mAh cm-2) and then stripped the Li up to 1 V.  Afterward, 
the reservoir capacity of Li (QT = 5 mAh cm-2) was deposited on Cu at 0.5 mA cm-2 and cycled with a 
capacity of QP = 0.5 mAh cm-2 for a specific number of cycles at a current density of 0.5 mAh cm-2. 
Finally, the remaining Li in the last cycle was stripped at 0.5 mA cm-2 to 1 V, and the obtained capacity 
(QS) was measured. In this case, the CE is obtained by the following equation: CE= (nQP + QS) / (nQP + 
QT). Li||Li symmetric cells were employed to perform the galvanostatic cycling at different current 
densities ranging from 0.1 mA cm-2 to 2 mA cm-2.

The critical current density (CCD) test was carried out to further analyze the electrolytes' compatibility 
with lithium.  CCD test is generally defined as the maximum endurable current density of lithium 
battery cycling without cell failure. Li symmetrical coin cells (Li0| |Li0) were assembled in an argon-
filled glove box for this test. The Li0 symmetric cells were kept at 25 °C for 6 h and then galvanostatically 
cycled at different current densities ranging from 0.1 to 5.0 mA cm−2. 
Li-S cell performance: The Li-S coin cells were assembled inside an argon-filled glovebox, using the 
aforementioned S@C electrodes, with a Celgard 2500 separator of 16 μm diameter, and Li0 disk (China 
Energy Lithium, 500 μm) serving as the anode. For the pouch cell tests, a 39 × 55 mm Li0 anode was 
used for the prototype cell assembly. In both coin and pouch cells, a fixed electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio 
(E/S) of 7 μL mg−1 was employed for the battery cycling. In the case of the SSE_LiFSI prototype cell, an 
external stacking pressure of 66 kPa was applied during the cell cycling through a two-plate design 
tool. Two different tests were carried out to determine the performance of the different electrolytes 
developed and their suitability with the high performing cathodes used.  
In the C-rate test, 3 mgS cm−2 loading electrodes were used, being cycled at C/5, C/4, C/3, C/2, and C/5, 
with the first three conditioning cycles at C/20 and keeping the charge constant at C/5 during the whole 
test. This test aims to determine the maximum power obtained with the electrolyte/cathode 
combination. 
 For high sulfur loading cathodes (4 mgS cm-2), the cells underwent a long-cycling test at C/10 and C/5, 
preceded by 5 and 3 preconditioning cycles at C/20, respectively. In the cut-off voltage effect test, a 
first cycle of preconditioning was carried out at C/20, followed by a long cycling test at C/10 with the 
respective voltage ranges: 3.0-1.2 V, 2.7-1.4, and 2.5-1.7 V. All the battery cycling tests were conducted 
with a Maccor battery tester. 
The affinity between the SSE_LiFSI electrolyte and LiPS was determined by trying to generate LiPS in 
situ in the electrolyte. For that, stoichiometric amounts of S8 and Li2S in the SSE_LiFSI to obtain a 
maximum concentration of 0.5M Li2S6 were added to SSE_LiFSI. The mixtures were allowed to react 
for 3 days at 25 ºC and 60 ºC and then filtered.
