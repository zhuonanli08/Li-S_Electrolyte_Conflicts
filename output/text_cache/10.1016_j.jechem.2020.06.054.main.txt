Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries possess overwhelming energy density of 2654 Wh kg1, and are consid-
ered as the next-generation battery technology for energy demanding applications. Flooded electrolytes
are ubiquitously employed in cells to ensure sufﬁcient redox kinetics and preclude the interference of the
electrolyte depletion due to side reactions with the lithium metal anode. This strategy is capable of
enabling long-lasting, high-capacity and excellent-rate battery performances, but it mask the require-
ments of practical Li–S batteries, where high-sulfur-loading/content and lean electrolyte are prerequisite
to realize the energy-dense Li–S batteries. Sparingly and highly solvating electrolytes have emerged as
effective yet simple approaches to decrease the electrolyte/sulfur ratio through altering sulfur species
and exerting new reaction pathways. Sparingly solvating electrolytes are characterized by few free sol-
vents to solvate lithium polysulﬁdes, rendering a quasi-solid sulfur conversion and decoupling the reac-
tion mechanisms from electrolyte quantity used in cells; while highly solvating electrolytes adopt high-
donicity or high-permittivity solvents and take their advantages of strong solvation ability toward poly-
sulﬁde intermediates, thereby favoring the polysulﬁde formation and stabilizing unique radicals, which
subsequently accelerate redox kinetics. Both solvation chemistry approaches have their respective fea-
tures to allow the operation of cells under electrolyte-starved conditions. This Review discusses their
unique features and basic physicochemical properties in the working Li–S batteries, presents remaining
technical and scientiﬁc issues and provides future directions for the electrolyte chemistry to attain high-
energy Li–S batteries.

1. Introduction
Developing higher energy density battery technologies over
conventional lithium-ion batteries is a paramount task to fulﬁll
ever-growing demand of emerging applications, such as advanced
electronics and long-range electrical vehicles [1–5]. Lithium–sulfur
(Li–S) batteries can deliver an overwhelming theoretical energy
density of 2654 Wh kg1, assuming full conversion from sulfur to
lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) coupling with the use of lithium metal anode
[6–10]. This battery chemistry employs high capacity (1675 mAh
g1), low cost (100 US$ ton1) and environmentally friendly sulfur
as a cathode and metallic lithium as an anode, exerting a distinct
dissolution/precipitation mechanism to store and release electrical
energy [11–13]. The stepwise reduction of sulfur to soluble lithium
polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) is kinetically favorable, and then the insoluble
Li2S2/Li2S precipitate on conductive scaffolds through electrochem-
ical reduction or disproportionation [14–16]. This reaction route is
fundamentally different from conventional lithium-ion batteries,
which adopt intercalation electrodes to host lithium ions (Li+)
and exhibit acceptable chemical/physical satiability in working
conditions.
The unique conversion reactions of sulfur electrochemistry pre-
sent several impediments to achieve energy density as theoreti-
cally claimed: (i) The insulating nature of redox end members (S
and Li2S) requires addition of adequate conductive carbon materi-
als to obtain rapid reaction kinetics; (ii) the mobile LiPS species
cause the inevitable internal shuttle driven by concentration gradi-
ent and electrical ﬁeld, resulting in low Coulombic efﬁciency and
parasitic reactions with the lithium metal anode; (iii) large volume
changes of sulfur cathode (~80%) and lithium metal anode (theo-
retically inﬁnite) signiﬁcantly shufﬂe electrode hosts and lead to
surface instability [17–21].
Over the past ten years, great effects have been devoted to con-
structing conductive scaffolds to enhance speciﬁc capacity, cycling
stability and rate delivery of Li–S batteries [22–27]. Nanocarbon
materials with various dimensional structures have been demon-
strated to physically block the LiPS leakage and thus suppress LiPS
crossover [21,23,27–31]. The recent approaches involving carbon
surface modiﬁcation by heteroatoms or external mediators have
been proposed to anchor LiPSs and promote their conversion kinet-
ics through catalytic effects [32–42]. Functional binders and sepa-
rators, along with lithium metal protections afford additional
beneﬁts to prolong cell life and enhance electrode stability [43–
49]. These concerted efforts have progressively improved the sul-
fur capacity under decent sulfur content (wt%) and sulfur loading
(mg cm2) [50–55]. Such battery performance improvement is crit-
ically assisted by rational design of electrolyte recipes, enabling
efﬁcient mass/charge transport and lithium anode protection
[56–61].
The composition of a benchmark electrolyte in Li–S batteries, in
most cases to evaluate merits imparted by proposed nanostructure
hosts or interfaces, is 1.0 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane)sulfoni
mide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) mixture solvents (v/v = 1/1) with 1 wt%~5 wt% lithium
nitrate (LiNO3) [12,62]. The ether-based solvent is relatively stable
toward highly nucleophilic LiPS intermediates. The linear DME sol-
vent can dissolve high concentration of long-/medium-chain LiPSs,
which greatly facilitates reduction of insulating sulfur and acts as
solution-mediated pathway to drive solid product precipitation
[63]. The cyclic DOL solvent exhibits low viscosity and tends to
cleavage and polymerization, forming a robust solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) with assistance of LiNO3 decomposition [64,65].
The choice of LiTFSI with large complex anions allows sufﬁcient
solubility in ether solvents due to its high disassociation ability
[66]. This solution promises a balance of Li+ mobility, lithium metal
stability, and LiPS conversion kinetics, rendering high capacity,
long-term cycling and reasonable rate capability, assuming ﬂooded
electrolytes are applied in the cell.
A critical leap from laboratory-scale research to industrial real-
ity has to consider key parameters to jointly realize high-energy
and long-lasting Li–S batteries [67–72]. The modeling of Li–S bat-
teries on pouch-cell level indicates that a projected energy density
of 500 Wh kg1 need to push key parameters to stringent condi-
tions, especially the low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio [71,73]. Most
of studies over the past decade employ high E/S ratio, normally > 10,
in a coin-type cell to exclude the interference of electrolyte con-
sumption and afford sufﬁcient mass/charge transport properties
[24,25,74–76]. However, this is not the case in a real cell scenario,
where E/S < 4.5 is highly advocated to achieve the high-energy–
density goal [11,71,77]. Several approaches have been considered
to realize efﬁcient operation of Li–S batteries with a low E/S ratio:
(i) Spatially conﬁning sulfur into microstructured carbon materials
to avoid the triphasic interface limitation, and electrode optimiza-
tion to reduce porosity [78–84]; (ii) heterogeneous or homoge-
neous mediator to promote liquid–solid conversion and control
morphology of solid deposits [85–90]; (iii) design of electrolyte
with desired solvation properties to ﬁnd new reaction pathways
to break electrolyte-quantity-dependent limitations of sulfur redox
reactions [91,92]. Especially, a breakthrough of electrolyte that can
be applied under starved conditions would provide a driving force
to bring Li–S batteries into reality.
Electrolyte solvation chemistry of Li–S batteries dictates the sol-
ubility and speciation of LiPSs, and determines electrochemical
properties of the cell, such as voltage proﬁle, Li2S precipitation
and redox kinetics [92–96]. The LiPS stability is critically linked
with applied solvents, which consequently inﬂuences of chemical
equilibrium among diverse chain-length polysulﬁde dianions and
radicals [97]. In the benchmark electrolyte of Li–S batteries, sol-
vated Li+ are surrounded by large portion of free ethers [98], which
readily solvate dissolved LiPSs to form a LiPS-containing catholyte.

electrolytes.
3. Sparingly solvating electrolyte
Sparingly solvating electrolytes for Li–S batteries are an emerg-
ing research area to decouple the electrolyte volume from the reac-
tion mechanism. The concept of solvated electrolytes beneﬁts from
the ‘‘ionic liquid”-like features, which consist entirely of ions and
can be further deﬁned as molten salts having melting points lower
than

[114].

Coulombic forces, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonds dic-

solubility and stability of sulfur species in the working Li–S batter-
ies [115]. The weak Lewis acidity and basicity of cations and anions
leads to a low coordination ability to LiPSs and has the potential to
suppress LiPS shuttle. Watanabe and co-workers suggest that the
Li2S8 concentration in ionic liquid has three orders of magnitude
lower than that in an organic electrolyte [116]. The LiPS solubility
is majorly affected by donicity of the ionic solvents and low DN
ionic liquids mitigate the Li+ coordination. The lithium salts (e.g.
LiTFSI) in the ionic liquid further reduce LiPS solubility through
‘‘comment ion effects”.

vents, the LiTFSI is dissociated into Li+ and TFSI. The Li+ is coordi-

which is surrounded by uncoordinated solvents (Fig. 3a). A consid-
erable amount of free solvent molecules in such dilute electrolytes
readily solvate LiPSs, allowing super high LiPSs solubility with
respective to total atomic sulfur concentration (~5.7 M [S]) [117].

Increasing Li salt concentration renders the less free solvent mole-
cules and boosts electrolyte viscosity, which thus result in a
decreased solubility of LiPSs. At extremely high LiTFSI concentra-
tion, all solvents coordinate to Li+ and forms unique contact ion
pairs or aggregates (Fig. 3b) [98]. The substantial reduction or even
elimination of free solvent molecules in the electrolyte system is a
key feature to distinguish high-concentration electrolyte from the
dilute electrolyte. The concentrated electrolyte of 7.0 M LiTFSI in
DOL/DME with the nominal (DOL/DME)1.7  LiTFSI solvate has
been demonstrated to exhibit an extremely low solubility for LiPSs
(Fig. 3c) and protects metallic lithium anodes [118]. This bifunc-
tional ‘‘solvent-in-salt” electrolyte beneﬁts the high Columbic efﬁ-
ciency and long-term cyclability. The term of solvent-in-salt refers
to either the weight or volume ratio of salt-to-solvent exceeds 1.0
[118], while solvent-in-salt electrolyte structure does not mean all
solvents have bonded to lithium salts. A similar ‘solvent-in-salt’
electrolyte with non-free solvent allows the application of carbon-
ate solvents in Li–S batteries, while carbonate solvents are previ-
ously considered to be easily attacked by nucleophilic LiPSs
[106,119]. The ‘‘solvent-in-salt” electrolyte with non-free solvent
beneﬁts both lithium metal anode and LiPS inhibition, greatly
expanding the choice of solvents for Li–S batteries.
Another explanation for the suppression of LiPSs by the high
concentration electrolyte is so-called ‘‘common ion effects”, by
which the LiPS solubility in electrolyte is inﬂuenced by the existing
Li+ in the electrolyte. The ionic equilibrium in an electrolyte can be
expressed as Eq. (10) [120]:

where n and n0 are the LiPS solubility with and without lithium
salts in the solvent, respectively; C is the concentration of the

example, giving a saturated solubility of 0.713 M Li2S8 in the sol-

experimental data [99,121,122]. This discrepancy is probably
ascribed to the fact that the ‘‘common ion effect” theory is lack
of considering the change of Li+ coordination number when the
electrolyte falls into the solvated regime.
The solubility of a lithium salt in a given solvent is dependent
on two factors: (i) Interactions between Li+ and anions; and (ii)
donor ability of solvents, reﬂecting interactions between solvents
and Li+. The LiTFSI molecule consists of a strong Lewis acid (Li+)
and weak Lewis base (TFSI), leading to a low electrostatic interac-
tion [66]. Besides, the ether solvents with low steric hindrance
have the relatively high DN, rendering lone pairs of the ether oxy-
gen to donate to the Li+. These two features result in a high solubil-
ity of LiTFSI in an aprotic DOL/DME mixture [118]. LiPSs are also
kind of lithium salts and follow the basic role of dissolution. In
the solvated electrolyte, all ether solvents coordinate to the strong
Lewis acid of Li+, signiﬁcantly reducing the donating ability of sol-
vents. The occupied lone pairs of the ether oxygen by coordinated
Li+ weakens the donor ability of solvents. The reduced donor ability
of these solvents even has the less coordinating ability than basic
polysulﬁde anions, thus precluding further LiPSs dissolution. The
solvated ionic liquid consists of a complex cation (solvents chelat-
ing the Li+ cation) and an anion [99], and none of free solvents exist
in the electrolyte. The solvated ionic liquid electrolyte has been
demonstrated to effectively suppress LiPS leakage from the cath-
ode and prolong the cycling life to 400 cycles at 1/18C [99].
The unique solvent–salt interactions in solvated electrolyte is
proved to increase the reductive stability of solvents, bringing
about reduction-vulnerable acetonitrile (ACN) to be more tolerable
[123]. Nazar et al. reported a (ACN)2–LiTFSI complex to tailor sulfur
speciation and equilibria, leading to an uncharacteristic sloping
voltage proﬁle in the cell discharge [124]. The origin of successful
operation of Li–S batteries in the (ACN)2–LiTFSI electrolyte is due
to the formation of the 2 : 1 complex through binding all ACN
molecules to LiTFSI, leaving very few free ACN to be decomposed
on lithium anode and to solvate LiPSs (Fig. 4a). However, solely
deployment of (ACN)2–LiTFSI induces large overpotential and lim-
ited cycle life probably due to the high viscosity and poor lithium
mobility.

Hydroﬂuoroether (HFE) solvents exhibit low polarity, excellent
miscibility with the solvate complexes and superb SEI-forming
ability on lithium metal anodes [99]. The chelating oxygen of
HFE is sterically hindered by ﬂuorine, considerably reducing its
donor ability. The ether electrolyte with a co-solvent of HFE has
been shown to improve the cycling stability and Coulombic efﬁ-
ciency [125]. The low viscosity and donicity together permit the
HFE as an ideal diluent to achieve reasonable ionic conductivity
and LiPS suppression. The (ACN)2–LiTFSI complex with HFE does
not directly promote the biphasic reduction from S8 to Li2S.
Instead, this electrolyte composition allows the formation of LiPSs,
which has been corroborated by spectroscopic and electrochemical
evidences [124]. The formation of LiPSs in the solvated complexes
is ascribed to the addition of HFE. The minor interaction between
HFE and Li+ can release a relative quantity of free ACN molecules
to solvate LiPSs (Fig. 4b) [126,127]. However, the limited LiPSs
shuttle as reﬂected by the Coulombic efﬁciency indicates the poor
LiPS mobility. The localized LiPSs propel the sulfur interconversion
while curtail LiPS crossover, making Li–S batteries with acceptable
cyclability and rate capability [124]. The solvated (ACN)2–LiTFSI
electrolyte has also applied in the lithium–selenium (Li–Se) batter-
ies to mitigate polyselenides migration [128]. It is of note that the
polarization of Li–Se cells (0.22 V) is much lower than that of Li–S
cells (0.69 V) in the same electrolyte structure and that Li–Se cells
can be cycled even at 2C, indicating much facile redox kinetics of
selenium conversion. The detailed reaction mechanism deserves
future efforts and probably inspires effective approaches to pro-
mote rate capability of Li–S batteries with sparingly solvating
electrolytes.
The electrochemical reaction mechanism of sulfur species in the
solvated electrolyte with HFE diluents is still an open question. The
presence of LiPSs in solvated electrolyte has been validated by
spectroscopy [91,124]. However, the produced LiPSs are strin-
gently constrained in the cathode side, forming a localized redox
mediator to promote redox kinetics. The discharge proﬁle in this
system exhibits a distinguished long plateau, interrupted by a volt-
age dip at around 400 mAh g1 (Fig. 5a). An analogous supersatu-
ration point, also located in 400 mAh g1, in the two-plateau
discharge proﬁle is rationalized by a signature of the conversion
from soluble LiPSs to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S in the conventional
DOL/DME electrolyte. The discharge capacity with 400 mAh g1
corresponds to ~ 1/4 electron per S atom, suggesting a formation
of nominal Li2S4 according to Faraday’s law. These observations
likely imply that the sparingly solvating electrolyte in Li–S batter-
ies drives the sulfur species to medium- or short-chain polysulﬁdes
(e.g. Li2S4). The voltage dip is a consequence of semi-solid Li2S4
reduction to solid Li2S2/Li2S, a necessary overpotential for the solid
product nucleation. A voltage rise after the voltage dip is caused by
an unusual equilibrium that drives the cleavage of a divalent poly-
sulﬁde into a monovalent radical (Fig. 5b, c) [91]. The quasi-solid
reaction from S to Li2S2/Li2S bypassing the highly soluble long-
chain polysulﬁde sheds some lights to develop electrolytes that
break the couple between electrolyte volume and reaction mecha-
nisms, which has opportunities to reduce the electrolyte dosage in
cells [129].
The sparingly solvating electrolyte usually comprises lithium
salts that exist in a contacted ion pair or aggregates, which have
difﬁculties to disassociate into charged cations or anions. Such
chemical speciation of lithium salts leads to poor ionic conductiv-
ity and thus high overpotential. An ideal aprotic solvent should
have high capability to dissolve and disassociate lithium salts
(avoiding the formation of ion pairs) while suppress LiPS dissolu-
tion. The electron-donating alkyl groups in DOL and DME increase
their Lewis basicity of oxygen atoms, which boost the LiPS dissolu-
tion. The triﬂamide solvents embracing electron-withdrawing
groups, such as N, Ndimethyl triﬂamide (DMT) and N, N-dipropyl
triﬂamide (DPT), exhibits inferior basicity to disassociate LiPSs,
whereas

capability

conductivity, rendering high rate performance of Li–S cells.
Understanding mechanism of Li+ migration in sparingly solvat-
ing electrolyte is beneﬁcial for enhancing ionic conductivity, which
determines rate performance in such viscous electrolyte. Although
addition of low-polarity HFE has been found to markedly reduce
electrolyte viscosity and enhance diffusion coefﬁcient, the Li+
transport in interfacial region and bulk electrolyte are not as high
as expected. The Li+ transport in conventional electrolytes (~1.0 M)
predominately relies on a vehicular mechanism that depends on a
physical diffusion of the solvated Li+ [131]. When the electrolyte
structure falls into concentrated or solvated region, the diffusion
of the solvated Li+ is limited and Li+ transport shifts from vehicular
mechanism to Li+ hopping mechanism that involves Li+ exchange
reactions from one coordinating site to another. Li+ transport in
concentrated sulfolane (SL) electrolyte has been demonstrated to
follow the hopping mechanism [121,132]. The electrolyte structure
of SL–LiTFSI complexes is schematically presented in Fig. 6(a)
[132]. A SL molecule links two different Li+, while two oxygen
atoms of the SO2 groups on TFSI coordinate to two different Li+,
forming SL- and TFSI-bridged and chainlike Li+ coordination
structures.
Noncoordinating HFE has been ubiquitously employed as a
dilute to reduce viscosity of solvated electrolytes. However,
enhanced diffusion coefﬁcient imparted by relatively low elec-
trolyte viscosity does not deﬁnitely promise high Li+ conduction.
Watanabe group investigated Li+ conductivity and viscosity as a
function of HFE amount in SL–LiTFSI complexes [132] (Fig. 6b).
The electrolyte viscosity is reduced by almost two orders of magni-
tude with gradually increase of HFE/TFSI ratio. The drastically
decreased viscosity does not confer a desired ionic conductivity,

the harsh redox reaction conditions.
A critical shortcoming of sparingly solvating electrolyte is the
high mass density, which would impact speciﬁc energy of Li–S
pouch cells. The high lithium salt concentration coupling with high
mass density of HFE (1.53 g mL1 for TTE at 20 C) inevitably
increases the inert component in cells, while the mass density of
DME/DOL is 1.065 g mL1 at 20 C [122]. According to this line,
the low-mass-density solvents with limited donor ability would
serve as a bifunctional medium to reduce overall cell weight and
curtail LiPS formation. Althues and co-workers reported a hexyl
methyl ether (HME) with highly sterical hinderance and low mass
density (0.77 g mL1) to achieve expected LiPS solubility and
energy density [122]. The HME solvent molecule possesses only
one oxygen donor site, which is shielded by large hexyl groups,
presenting low solvation ability to LiPSs. This approach is classiﬁed
as an adoption of low dielectric constant electrolytes with high car-
bon to oxygen (C/O) ratio in their molecules. The reduction of the
solvating oxygen atoms in the solvent molecule can effectively
impede LiPS dissolution and possibly maintain relatively high ionic
conductivity [117]. Methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) has a poor sol-
vation to LiPSs (20 mM in MTBE vs. 0.5 M in DOL/DME) while can
dissolve 2.7 M LiTFSI [117]. It is worthy of note that high solubility
of LiTFSI does not contribute to much better ion conduction. This
disaccord is probably ascribed to the high disassociation energy
of LiTFSI in MTBE, forming rigid ion pairs rather than solvent-
separated charges (Li+ and TFSI).
4. Highly solvating electrolytes
Sparingly solvating electrolytes alleviate LiPS crossover and
mitigate lithium metal corrosion. However, the quasi-solid inter-
conversion between sulfur and Li2S2/Li2S imposes grand kinetic
challenges to achieve a reasonable rate performance, as well as
demands of elevated temperature to provide sufﬁcient mass/ion
transports. Another approach to decrease E/S ratio in Li–S batteries
while maintain relatively express ionic conduction is to deploy
high DN or dielectric constant solvents to dissolve greater LiPSs
than DOL/DME solvents, promoting solution-mediated reactions
and thereby the total sulfur utilization. The solution mediated
reactions circumvent the limitations of surface-based reductions
that undergo kinetically unfavorable propagation of highly insulat-
ing nature of Li2S.

high-donor-ability solvents are susceptible to the strongly reduc-

The dilemma of high LiPS solubility and severe lithium instabil-
ity in high-ND solvents is partially mitigated by exploring lithium-
compatible and high-dielectric electrolyte. Zhang and co-workers
proposed a tetramethylurea (TMU) solvent to exert radical-
directed and lithium-compatible merits [85]. The surface of
lithium anode is smooth, without visible cracks or pits after ten
cycles in Li–Li symmetric cells, in sharp contrast to ones in DMA
and DMSO cells (Fig. 7d). The TMU solvent kinetically facilitates
a faster radical-to-Li2S conversion and yields thicker, denser, yet
porous precipitates. These contributing factors result in an ultra-
high initial capacity of 1524 mAh g1 at 0.05C under an
electrolyte-starved condition (E/S = 3) in a pouch cell. However,
the long-term and high-sulfur-loading Li–S batteries with such
electrolytes still encounter the lithium anode instability.
A distinct feature of cell voltage in high-DN solvents is the rel-
atively high value in the ﬁrst plateau (solution region) and rela-
tively low value in the second plateau (solid region) [95,101].
The solvation of sulfur species directs sulfur reduction mecha-
nisms, which are reﬂected by the cell voltage proﬁles. High-DN sol-
vents exhibit strong solvation power to high-order LiPSs and
promote radical formation. Accordingly, chemical state and envi-
ronment of anionic and cationic species are quite different from
the case in the conventional DOL/DME solvents with relatively
low donicity. According to the Gibbs and Nernst equations, the
equilibrium constant shifts toward the formation of soluble LiPSs
and thus increases the cell potential due to favorable LiPS forma-
tion in such solvents [95]. As Li2S2/Li2S precipitate on substrates,
the solvated lithium cations and polysulﬁde anions must be de-
solvated, requiring a certain overpotential to overcome de-
solvation energy, which accounts for the lower cell voltage in com-
parison with the case of DOL/DME.
The achievable sulfur capacity is dominantly dependent on the
solid Li2S2/Li2S precipitation from soluble LiPSs. Low-donicity DOL/
DME solvents facilitate two-dimensional instantaneous nucleation
with lateral growth to form plate/sheet-like deposits [136,137].
The high capacity based on such deposition mechanism has been
realized by conventional strategies includes: (i) Increase of triple-
phase boundary by augmenting scaffold surface area; and (ii)
reduction of mass/charge transfer barrier by engineering scaffold
pores and channels. This approach usually requires large amount
of conductive scaffold and electrolyte, going against to the high-
energy–density target. The high-donicity solvent has strong ability
to stabilize radicals and high-order LiPSs, which signiﬁcantly
reduce nucleation density and are beneﬁcial for a hemispherical
diffusion-controlled growth [137]. Therefore, quasi-spherical gran-
ular shape of precipitates can be formed on scaffold, allowing a
non-encapsulation approach for high-performance Li–S batteries

Aside from solvent-regulated donicity of electrolytes, the anion
of lithium salts has also tremendous effects on Li2S electrodeposi-

lithium salt with high-DN anion enhances electron-donating prop-

ever, high-DN lithium salts of LiTf and LiBr also encounter the cor-

lithium metal anodes.

on the hard and soft acids and bases theory. If the LiNO3 serves as a

It should be noted that a low E/S ratio induces a saturated LiPS
solution in highly solvating electrolytes and the sulfur speciation
would be different from the one in the ﬂooded electrolytes. The
low E/S ratio leads to reduced free solvents in electrolytes and
decreases LiPSs dissociation, which may contribute the formation
of LiPSs clusters. Additional solution properties such as viscosity,
Li+ conductivity and wettability will also change upon increasing
the E/S ratio in cells. Such changes markedly inﬂuence overall bat-
tery performances and working mechanisms.
5. Summary and outlook
Electrolyte solvation chemistry determines chemical/electro-
chemical environment of cations, anions and molecules, and trans-
port properties (e.g. diffusion coefﬁcient, Li+ transference number
and viscosity), which comprehensively dictate redox mechanisms
and battery performances. Cutting down E/S ratio is considered as
one of the most effective ways to achieve energy-dense and cost-
effective Li–S batteries. Sparingly and highly solvating electrolytes
have their respective features to decrease electrolyte quantity. Spar-
ingly solvating electrolytes exhaust almost all free solvents and form
a unique lithium–solvent–anion complex to regulate redox reac-
tions and transport of active species. In this case, local yet minor
LiPSs promote sulfur redox interconversion rather than large
amount of LiPSs serve as self-mediators in the entire electrolyte
region. Sparingly solvating electrolyte system renders a quasi-
solid sulfur conversion and thus the electrolyte just functions as a

an opposite approach, in which high-donicity or high-permittivity

and facilitates a hemispherical diffusion-controlled growth, yielding
thick yet porous precipitates, which can alleviate the need of triple-
phase boundary for LiPS reduction.
Sparingly and highly solvating electrolytes for the electrolyte-
lean Li–S batteries are emerging approaches to achieve high energy
density. The successful application of such electrolytes has to con-
sider following challenges.
(i) Protection of lithium metal anodes. Localized soluble LiPSs in
sparingly solvating electrolyte greatly reduce the lithium metal
corrosion and curtail the LiPS shuttle. However, the parasitic reac-
tions between solvated electrolytes and metallic lithium persis-
tently take place and thus deplete electrolyte until cell death.
The high-donicity solvents in highly solvating electrolytes are
much more vulnerable to the lithium metal anode. The reactive
solvents coupled with mobile LiPSs present mountain challenges
for the stable lithium metal anode. Considering high areal capacity
of 5.0 mAh g1 (corresponding to the ﬂuctuation of the ~ 25 lm
thickness) required for practical applications, stable lithium metal
interface is indispensable for the long-lasting Li–S batteries with
both sparingly and highly solvating electrolytes. The artiﬁcial SEI
that can effectively tolerant lithium surface variation and hermet-
ically block solvents crossover might afford possible solutions to
enable long-term operation of lithium metal anodes.
(ii) Promotion of redox kinetics. Most of reported battery perfor-
mances based on sparingly solvating electrolytes are realized
through elevated temperature, despite of diluting the solvated
electrolyte by the non-solvation HFE diluent. The low viscosity of
electrolyte does not deﬁnitely promise high ionic conductivity
due to the formation of rigid ion pairs in such electrolyte system.
Exploring new sparingly solvating electrolyte with high lithium
transference number seems to be a plausible way to increase bulk
ion transport and facilitate local lithium ion transfer with assis-
tance of localized LiPS mediators. The kinetic feature of highly sol-

electrolyte

much
better
than

direction of the skeleton, retarding active components transport
in the long range. Improving transport properties of lithium and
sulfur species in the whole electrode region is still challenging in
the high-sulfur-loading with limited highly solvating electrolytes.
Heterogeneous and/or homogeneous mediators are suggested to
facilitate electron and ion conduction in the electrolyte. Finely tai-
loring electrolyte structures and scaffold surfaces that decrease the
LiPS concentration gradient will impart extra beneﬁts for the
kinetic features in the highly solvating electrolytes with large sul-
fur content/loading.
(iii) Understanding of working mechanisms. Spectroscopic and
macroscopic evidences have allowed us to predict reasonable reac-
tion mechanisms in both sparingly and highly solvating elec-
trolytes. Minor yet important LiPSs have been identiﬁed in the
sparingly solvating electrolyte, whereas the detailed LiPSs evalua-
tion and their conversion mechanisms have not yet been toughed.
