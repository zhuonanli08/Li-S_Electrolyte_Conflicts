The lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery is a promising technology for large-scale 
energy storage and vehicle electrification due to its high theoretical energy 
density and low cost. Reducing the sulfur cathode porosity has been iden-
tified recently as a viable strategy for improving the cell practical energy 
density and minimizing pore-filling electrolytes to extend cell life at lean elec-
trolyte conditions. Direct use of a low-porosity cathode for Li–S battery results 
in poor electrode wetting, nonuniform electrode reactions, and thus early cell 
failure. To understand and mitigate the barriers associated with the use of 
low-porosity electrodes, multiscale modeling is performed to predict electrode 
wetting, electrolyte diffusion, and their impacts on sulfur reactions in Li–S 
cells by explicitly considering the electrode wettability impacts and electrode 
morphologies. The study elucidates the critical impact of low tortuosity and 
large channel pore design for promoting electrode wetting and species diffu-
sion. It is suggested that the secondary particle size should be comparable 
with the electrode thickness to effectively promote electrolyte wettability and 
sulfur reactivity. This study provides new insights into the low-porosity elec-
trode material and designs and is expected to accelerate the development of 
practical high-energy Li–S batteries.

the sulfur reductions into a two-step chain, as suggested by 
Mikhaylik and Akridge,[4] to predict the discharge-charge curves. 
Along with 0-D models, the equivalent circuit model[22,23] and

Despite the recent development of numerical models, the 
understanding and prediction of Li–S cell performance under 
low-porosity cathode conditions remain a challenge. For prac-
tical high-mass-loading electrodes, the nonuniform distribution 
of electrolytes is one of the most challenging issues, particu-
larly for low-porosity electrodes.[25,26] The absence of electrolytes 
in local regions (i.e., electrode wetting issue) can potentially 
reduce sulfur reaction kinetics and utilization rate. The afore-
mentioned numerical models focus on the ideal condition of 
high porosity (>70%) or do not consider electrolyte wetting of 
a thick electrode. Under those conditions, the electrode is “ide-
ally” assumed to be fully wetted with constant sulfur loading 
and mass transport properties along the thickness direction 
before cell discharge. However, this condition is not true in the 
realistic conditions of high sulfur loading and low-porosity elec-
trodes. For a low-porosity electrode (≈45%), electrolyte wetting 
becomes a limiting factor; thus, those existing models may not 
explicitly provide an accurate prediction of the cell performance.
To bridge the gap between practical needs and models, a new 
multiscale modeling approach (Figure 1) has been proposed 
for a Li–S battery with low-porosity electrodes. Multiscale mod-
eling has been proved to be an important tool for analyzing 
Li–S batteries, which also involve multiple scales and complex 
physics.[27–29] In our proposed framework, three-dimensional 
(3-D) pore-scale model has been integrated with the 1-D device-
scale electrochemical model to investigate the effects of cathode 
structure on the battery performance. The 3-D pore-scale model 
simulates the electrolyte infiltration process with multiphase 
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)[30] to inform the 1-D 
electrochemical model of the electrode wetting and electrolyte-
distribution conditions. With that information, the effective 
species-diffusion coefficient, electrode specific area, and other 
related parameters become a function of the electrolyte wet-
ting conditions along the cell thickness direction. Informed by 
the 3-D electrode model, the 1D device-scale model then calcu-
lates the electrochemical reactions of sulfur species inside the 
partially wetted electrode at different locations. The multiscale 
model explicitly considers the electrode wettability impact on 
electrochemical reactions and electrode morphology change to 
predict the Li–S battery performance. With this integration, the 
developed multiscale Li–S model also can help us understand 
the advantages and trade-offs of low-porosity electrodes and 
help optimize the electrode design of the Li–S cell. A summary 
of the calculated low-porosity electrode (45%) performance is 
provided in the Table S1 (Supporting Information). As shown 
in the table, the low-porosity electrode can reduce the cathode 
thickness by half and double the volumetric capacity when 
using the 90 µm secondary particles for electrode at the same 
sulfur loading. However, the wetting of the low porosity elec-
trode becomes a more limiting factor, which can reduce the 
battery specific capacity and affect the battery long-term cycling 
performance. The details of low-porosity electrode performance 
and optimization strategies will be discussed.

2.2. Discharging Profile of the Low-Porosity Electrode
A comparison of the experimentally measured discharge curves 
with multiscale model predictions is shown in Figure 3a. As 
shown by experimental curves, cells with LPC and SPC achieve 
a specific capacity of 1001 and 451  mAh  g−1, respectively, at a 
discharge rate of 0.1 C. The model-predicted discharge curves 
match the experimental curves with a discharge capacity differ-
ence under 9%. The two typical discharge plateaus are observed 
in both experimental and simulation curves in the LPC. With a 
low-porosity electrode, the lower plateau in the discharge curve 
has an inclined slope, and the voltage drops from 2.1  V at a 
capacity of 250 mAh g−1 to 1.93 V at a capacity of 900 mAh g−1 
before a sharply decreasing tail appears at the end of discharge.

for the low-porosity electrode in the Li–S battery design.
The sulfur mass loading is another factor that can potentially 
affect the low-porosity electrode wetting pattern and thus the 
battery performance. At given porosity (45%), with the increase 
of mass loading, the cathode thickness would increase propor-
tionally. This increases the difficulty of the electrolyte infiltra-
tion with extended travel distance inside the pores and the air 
can be trapped more easily with a thicker cathode and nar-
rower pore channels. The proposed multiscale model quanti-
fies the average wetting percentage of the electrode at three dif-
ferent sulfur mass loading conditions as shown in Figure S5a  
(Supporting Information). Increasing the sulfur mass loading 
from 3 to 6  mg  cm−2, the electrode thickness increase from 
45 to 90  µm. All the low-porosity electrodes (45%) show a 
consistently decreased wetting percentage trend regardless of 
the secondary particle size. The wetting percentage change is 
reflected in the calculated battery specific capacity (Figure S5b, 
Supporting Information). Therefore, the sulfur mass loading 
should be carefully selected depending on the low-porosity elec-
trode particle size to achieve optimal battery performance.
With the gained understanding of the low-porosity electrode, 
different strategies can be proposed to promote low-porosity 
electrode wetting and its performance. One possible direction 
is to develop new electrolytes with better wettability cathode 
materials. With the reduced surface tension, the electrolyte can 
enter the narrow pore channels more easily inside the 
carbon matrix. On the other hand, the low-porosity electrode 
morphology can be modified accordingly to the multiscale 
model observations. A gradient-porosity electrode with linearly 
decreased porosity toward the current collector can be a poten-
tial candidate for low-porosity electrodes. With larger porosity at 
the separator/cathode interface, the open pore channels at the 
interface will be less likely to be blocked, thus enhancing sulfur 
utilization and electrolyte backflow. Generating straight pores 
or laser-perforated holes[36] along the thickness direction also 
can be a potential improvement to increase the mass transfer 
rate, which is especially important in diffusion-limited low-
porosity electrodes. Our proposed multiscale Li–S model can 
accommodate those potential modifications to the low-porosity 
electrode for further performance optimization. Several new 
electrode designs realized by the multiscale model are shown 
in Figure S6 (Supporting Information) as examples. Addition-
ally, the prediction of the charge curves and cycling stability are 
also important topics for assessing the low-porosity electrode 
performance. This requires two-way coupling of the 3-D pore-
scale model with the 1-D electrochemical model to account for 
the electrode morphology changes upon cycling, although it is 
still cost-prohibitive with current computational resources. The 
development of surrogate models can be one possible solution 
to address this challenge.
3. Conclusion
This work developed a multiscale model for predicting the 
Li–S battery discharge profiles with practical low-porosity 
electrodes. The 3-D pore-scale model applied the discrete ele-
ment method to reconstruct the low-porosity cathodes with 
different secondary particle sizes. Combined with multiphase 
flow simulations, the electrolyte infiltration and electrode wet-
ting information can be acquired for different low-porosity 
cathode designs. Simulations suggest that the LPC holds 74% 
more electrolyte inside its pores compared to the SPC, which 
provides a larger surface area and more accessible sulfur. The 
cathode wetting information was dynamically incorporated 
in the 1-D device-scale model for analyzing electrochemical 
reactions and predicting discharge curves. The multiscale 
model was able to predict the discharge profile and capacity 
difference using different low-porosity cathode designs. The 
predicted Li–S battery capacity using LPCs and SPCs match 
well with the experimentally measured capacity with an error 
smaller than 9%. Compared to the traditional 1-D model, the 
new proposed multiscale model considered both cathode mor-
phology and initial electrolyte infiltration for battery perfor-
mance predictions, which is not directly viable with traditional 
models.
The multiscale model suggests that for the low-porosity elec-
trode, increasing secondary particle size in the electrode would 
be critical to promote the electrode wettability and thus battery 
performance. The nonuniform electrolyte distribution can also 
result in an uneven Li2S precipitation. This leads to increased 
pore channel blockage and potentially decreased discharge 
capacity. Under the same averaged wetting percentage con-
ditions, the electrolyte distribution profile also can affect bat-
tery performance. The sulfur would be more efficiently used if 
most of the electrolytes were located near the separator/cathode
