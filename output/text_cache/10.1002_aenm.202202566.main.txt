The target of electrolyte engineering is 
to make Li-metal anode compatible with 
lean electrolyte conditions while ensuring 
fast cathode kinetics. One intensively 
explored direction of electrolyte design is 
to prevent LiPS dissolution, which extends 
the cycle life of the Li-metal anode. This 
goal can be attained by employing encap-
sulated sulfur cathodes and sparingly 
solvating electrolytes.[10,11] Unfortunately, 
such an approach sacrifices the kinetics 
of sulfur redox reactions and usually 
requires a relatively high operating tem-
perature (e.g., 60 °C) to propel the cathode 
reactions. In another direction, the solva-
tion structure of dissolved LiPSs is being 
tailored to meet the requirements of 
anode stability.[12,13] For example, the sub-
stitution of chalcogen elements (e.g., tel-
lurium) into polysulfide molecules results 
in a significant improvement in anode stability.[14] However, 
at this stage, rather few existing electrolyte-tailoring strategies 
meet the requirements of anode stability and cathode kinetics 
synergistically.
Considering the conflicts in electrolyte requirement 
for anode and cathode, decoupling the anode and cathode 
electrolytes provides the third pathway toward high-perfor-
mance lithium–sulfur batteries. The use of a dual-phase solid 
(inorganic or polymer) || liquid electrolyte system potentially 
mitigates LiPS crossover and enables fast cathode kinetics. 
However, with the inorganic solid electrolyte at the anode side, 
the anode interfacial stability becomes a concern. The solid 
polymer electrolyte at the anode side suffers from swelling and 
LiPS crossover during long-term cycling.[15,16] Beyond these 
two, a less-explored option is a thermodynamically immiscible 
liquid || liquid electrolyte system. Although the concept of dual-
phase liquid-based electrolyte has been reported,[17,18] there is no 
successful demonstration with Li–S batteries. Cho et al. reported 
the combination of 1 lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)
imide (LiTFSI) in ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS) and 2.3 m LiTFSI 
in tetraglyme to form a thermally immiscible dual-phase elec-
trolyte for Li–S batteries.[19] The central issue of this design is 
that i) tetraglyme as the anode electrolyte does not fully prevent 
the dissolution of LiPSs and ii) with the solvation of LiPSs, the 
density of EMS-based cathode electrolyte increases and even-
tually becomes the high-density phase that will mix with the 
tetraglyme anode electrolyte.
Dissolution of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) is essential for fast cathode 
kinetics, but detrimental for anode stability, especially under lean electro-
lyte conditions. In this work, the phase separation phenomenon between 
solvents with different polarities (tetramethyl sulfone [TMS] and dibutyl 
ether [DBE]) is utilized to enable the design of a dual-phase electrolyte. 
High-polarity, high-density TMS–lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)
imide–ammonium trifluoroacetate as the cathode electrolyte strongly 
solvates LiPSs, which propel the sulfur redox reaction. Moreover, the 
composite of DBE and a polymeric ion conductor serves as the anode 
electrolyte. The addition of DBE in the anode side effectively prevents 
the crossover of corrosive species (LiPSs and ammonia trifluoroacetate), 
enabling a significant improvement in Li-metal anode stability. The 
electrode-specific dual-phase electrolyte design provides electrochemical 
performance superior to conventional electrolytes. Without additional elec-
trode engineering, pouch cells assemble with the dual-phase electrolyte 
cycle under a lean electrolyte (4 µL mg−1) and low-Li-excess condition  
(N/P = 3) for 120 cycles.

1. Introduction
The lithiation of sulfur in the liquid electrolyte system under-
goes a solid–liquid–solid reaction pathway, with the formation 
of Li2S as the final product and lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) as 
intermediates.[1–3] While being an enabler for fast sulfur con-
version kinetics, the dissolution of LiPS intermediates into the 
liquid electrolyte is detrimental to the stability of the Li-metal 
anode.[4] When the electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio is maintained 
at a practical level (e.g., 5 µL mg−1) to reach a reasonable energy 
density, most cells suffer from fast decay within 100 cycles. This 
is because in the conventional liquid electrolyte system with a 
low E/S ratio, i) the electrolyte containing a high concentration 
of LiPSs is highly corrosive for Li-metal anode,[5,6] and ii) the 
kinetics of sulfur redox reaction is sluggish under lean elec-
trolyte conditions, leading to significant non-uniformity in the 
cell. Due to the excessive addition of liquid electrolytes, there is

Motivated by these challenges, we propose a new dual-
phase electrolyte design strategy, which is enabled by the 
complete phase separation between low-density dibutyl ether 
(DBE) and high-density tetramethyl sulfone (TMS). There are 
two ion-conducting immiscible liquid-based electrolyte layers 
in the cell. The top phase is a composite of solid polymer ion 
conductors and DBE-LiTFSI, and the bottom phase is the 
TMS-LiTFSI. The two electrolyte layers are seamlessly connected 
in series, showing a low interfacial resistance (1.7 Ohm cm2). 
This approach results in immiscible electrolyte layers with 
no concerns of electrolyte interdiffusion and LiPS crossover. 
Moreover, the highly solvating TMS-based cathode electro-
lyte enables a facile dissolution of LiPSs. By decoupling the 
solvents of anode and cathode electrolytes, the low-Li-excess 
(N/P = 3) and lean electrolyte (4 µL mg−1) pouch cell can main-
tain above 72% of its initial capacity after 120 cycles.
2. Result and Discussion

solvating DBE is lighter (0.77 g cm−3). This benefits the phase

The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra in 
Figure  1d display the characteristic peaks of DBE for the top 
phase, whereas those peaks of TMS appear only for the bottom 
phase. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted 
to quantify the interfacial tension between these two liquid 
phases. Consistent with the experimental observations, the 
pre-mixed two phases separated extremely fast according to 
the simulation (20 ps). The interfacial tension as a descriptor 
reached 0.86 mN m−1, exceeding the reported value for EMS/
TEGDME (0.58 mN m−1) electrolyte system (Figure S2, Sup-
porting Information).[19] The immiscibility of these two phases 
minimized the contact surface area between the anode and 
cathode electrolytes.
In a real electrochemical cell, liquid DBE-LiTFSI is combined 
with an in situ derived polymer ion conductor and a polymer 
matrix to derive a GPE. This process is required because first, 
during the cell assembly process, the porous structure of the 
separator and electrode can induce a capillary flow of liq-
uids.[20,19] Once DBE-LiTFSI is confined in the polymer matrix, 
it is unlikely going to migrate into the cathode. Second, in the 
DBE/TMS system, due to the difference in solvating power, 
DBE cannot solvate enough Li salt to ensure fast ion transport.
To immobilize the liquid DBE-LiTFSI, the GPE was derived 
from the thermally-induced polymerization of the liquid pre-
cursor (see details in the Experimental Section). The GPE 
is composed of three compositions: poly(DOL)-LiTFSI and 
DBE-LiTFSI as the ion conductors and poly (pentaerythritol 
tetraacrylate [PETEA]) as the polymer matrix. The in situ 
derived poly (PETEA) matrix prevents the phase separation of 
poly(DOL)-LiTFSI and liquid DBE-LiTFSI during the thermal-
curing process.[21] Although DBE-LiTFSI is still in its liquid 
phase, the binding between DBE molecules and polymer 
matrix (polyDOL and PETEA) in GPE effectively prevents DBE 
from migrating into the cathode.
We further considered the capability of GPE to transport Li+ 
ions and prevent LiPS crossover. Considering the difference 
in the solvating power, the Li salt is inclined to distribute non-
uniformly across the two phases. Although DBE solvates LiTFSI 
(up to 3  m solubility), the Li salt concentration in the DBE 
phase decreases to a non-detectable level (according to 19F NMR 
spectra) when an equivalent volume of TMS is added, which is 
because of the significant difference of solvating power between 
TMS and DBE. The polymer ion conductor (polyDOL-LiTFSI) 
in GPE is essential for retaining the Li salt since polyDOL has a 
moderate polarity (dielectric constant) and reasonable solvating 
power (Figure  2a).[22] After putting the same volume of GPE 
and TMS-LiTFSI with the same lithium salt concentration (1 m) 
together for over 1 week, 19F NMR shows that the GPE still con-
tains 0.83 m of LiTFSI, which ensures reasonably fast Li+ ion 
transport.

Like almost all polymer ion conductors, polyDOL displays a 
high affinity with the highly polar TMS, leading to significant 
swelling of GPE. Moreover, polyDOL solvates LiPSs very easily 
(Figure  2c,d). For the GPE filled with DBE-LiTFSI, the LiPS 
crossover problem is resolved because DBE repels TMS out of 
the GPE (see Figure  2d and Figure S1c, Supporting Informa-
tion), even when the GPE is in the bottom of the vial.
Sulfur cathode requires high ion mobility and low viscosity 
for faster kinetics.[23,24] Due to strong electrostatic interactions 
between the positively charged Li+ and negatively charged ter-
minal sulfur of polysulfide molecules, a cluster consisting of 
multiple LiPS molecules is found to be a more stable configura-
tion than discrete isolated polysulfide chains. The formation of 
the LiPS cluster results in lower solubility and inferior cathode 
kinetics. Therefore, one direction to boost kinetics is to employ 
additives that can weaken the Li+–S− binding and promotes 
LiPS dissolution.
Inspired by the previous work in the literature, we reveal 
that ammonium trifluoroacetate (CF3COONH4, NH4TFA) is 
a feasible low-cost additive for propelling sulfur redox reac-
tions.[25–27] NH4TFA dissociates to 
+
NH4 cation and TFA− anion 
in a liquid electrolyte, which spontaneously reacts with LiPSs to 
form diammonium polysulfides and LiTFA. This reaction pre-
vents the clustering induced by the coordination between Li+ 
and terminal sulfur. However, NH4TFA additive is detrimental 
to the cyclability of Li-metal anode, since 
+
NH4 cation can be 
reduced by Li-metal to form insulating LiH or LiNH2 species.[28] 
Ideally, the NH4TFA additive needs to be confined within the 
cathode region to prevent anode corrosion.
As shown in Figure  3a, the addition of NH4TFA in TMS 
(>0.2 m solubility) changes the color of the LiPS solution from 
dark grey to orange-red. Further addition of DBE does not alter 
the solution color, which is due to the insolubility of NH4TFA 
in DBE. This was further confirmed by 1H NMR spectra, where 
no peak can be assigned to 
+
NH4 cation (Figure S3, Supporting 
Information). Because of the large size of 
+
NH4 cation (147 vs 
76 pm for Li+), DBE solvent molecules cannot overcome the lat-
tice energy to dissociate the NH4TFA salt.
Under the lean electrolyte condition, various analytical 
techniques further validate the enhancement of sulfur utili-
zation induced by NH4TFA. The 7Li NMR characterization 
further validates that the addition of NH4TFA suppresses the

The higher Li+ mobility induced by additives benefits the 
kinetics of sulfur conversion. According to the nucleation 
and growth model of Li2S deposition, the fast kinetics leads 
to smaller surface overpotential, and thus, lower nucleation 
rate of Li2S, promoting the growth of Li2S particles (see sum-
mary schematic in Figure  3b). In contrast, sluggish cathode 
kinetics results in Li2S film deposition, leading to electrode 
surface passivation at a low capacity (Figure  3b). The cyclic 
voltammetry profile obtained at different scan rates serves as 
a direct confirmation of the enhancement in charge transfer 
and redox kinetics under the presence of an NH4TFA addi-
tive (Figure  3d,e). Chronoamperometry experiments were 
conducted on Li–S cells and the morphology of the discharge 
product was compared (Figure 3f–h). NH4TFA additive enables 
a much higher peak current compared with that without addi-
tive. Moreover, within the void of the carbon paper, the precipi-
tates grew into large-sized particles (Figure 3g,h).
The kinetics of sulfur redox reactions was further investi-
gated with operando XRD (Figure  4).[29] Precipitation of LiPSs 
out of the liquid electrolyte was a non-negligible issue affecting 
sulfur utilization under lean electrolyte conditions. Operando 
XRD clearly shows the difference in sulfur utilization since it 
is a highly sensitive tool for LiPS detection. The peak located at 
26.5° is attributed to the LiPS cluster.[29] Conversion from sulfur 
to LiPS was clearly captured by the operando XRD, suggesting 
that the resolution of the measurement is high enough. With 
standard electrolyte, there was a significant amount of LiPS res-
idue after complete discharge (Figure 4a–c). In contrast, the cell 
with NH4TFA shows a much-enhanced conversion from LiPS to 
Li2S. The peak from LiPS vanished when the cell was fully dis-
charged to 1.7 V (Figure 4d–f). Interestingly, there was a relatively 
weak XRD signal for Li2S at 27° for both samples, which is cor-
related with the previous finding that the high-polarity electrolyte 
facilitates the formation of amorphous discharge product.[30,31]
The stability of the Li-metal anode against liquid electrolytes 
is crucial for the long-term cycling of pouch cells. The Li || Ni 
half-cell was fabricated to compare the Coulombic efficiency 
of Li stripping/plating with different electrolytes including 
DBE-LiTFSI, GPE (poly(DOL)/DBE-LiTFSI), TMS-LiTFSI, and 
TMS-LiTFSI/NH4TFA. The DBE-LiTFSI or TMS-LiTFSI elec-
trolyte results in an average (from 20 to 100th cycle) Coulombic 
efficiency of 90.7% and 62.2%, respectively (Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information). The GPE-protected Ni foil achieves the 
highest average Coulombic efficiency of 98.8% (from 20 to 100th 
cycle) cycles. Moreover, a significant morphology difference was 
observed for the Li deposit derived from GPE and TMS. GPE 
results in highly conformal and uniform surface morphology, 
while the Li deposit derived from TMS displays dendritic fea-
tures, even at a rather small capacity (0.5 mAh cm−2). Clearly, 
ether performs better to stabilize the Li metal anode, since ether 
is more stable against reduction and facilitates the formation 
of inorganic SEI.[32] The polymer matrix in GPE also improves 
the mechanical stability of SEI, contributing to improved 
Coulombic efficiency.

Although the Coulombic efficiency achieved by the GPE does 
not represent the highest among all liquid-based electrolyte 
systems, the Coulombic efficiency can be further improved by 
modifying the current collector, electrolyte additives, etc.[33–36] 
The value of Coulombic efficiencies became highly scattering 
when there was the addition of 0.2 m NH4TFA in TMS-based 
electrolytes, suggesting the anode corrosion induced by 
NH4TFA is a non-negligible issue.
The long-term stability of the anode and cathode were fur-
ther studied in coin and pouch cells under low-Li-excess condi-
tions (100 µm-thick Li foil as the anode, N/P = 3) (Figure 5). At 
a sulfur loading of 4 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7.5 µL mg−1 
(electrolyte volume based on the cathode electrolyte), the coin 
full cell was assembled to understand the cyclability. With the 
GPE/TMS dual-phase electrolyte, after 250 cycles, the coin cell 
achieved a specific capacity of 715 mAh g−1 (2.86 mAh cm−2) 
and high retention of 84.3% (0.4C discharge/0.2C charge). The 
Coulombic efficiency of the full cell was constantly maintained 
above 98.5%. Rate performance of the coin cells based on dual-
phase electrolyte was further evaluated (Figure S6, Supporting 
Information); the cell retained an areal capacity of 2.43 mAh cm−2 
(601 mAh g−1) at a 1 C rate, which is around 65% of its capacity 
at 0.4 C rate.
For the cell with single-phase electrolyte (1  m LiTFSI, 
0.2  m NH4TFA in TMS), the cell decayed to 35% of its ini-
tial capacity after 100 cycles. The cycled Li (at the charge 
state, after 250 cycles) displays a highly dense and uniform 
morphology for cells with GPE (Figure S5, Supporting 
Information).
Without DBE in the GPE, the dual-phase electrolyte coin 
cell (poly(DOL)-based GPE/|TMS) decayed to 64% of its initial 
capacity after 80 cycles (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The 
result clearly confirms that the GPE layer does not prevent LiPS 
crossover without the effect of phase separation.[37] Another 
benchmark, the single-phase electrolyte cell with the classical 
DOL/DME-based electrolyte, also decayed rapidly, retaining 
only 32% of its initial capacity after 80 cycles (Figure S7, 
Supporting Information). Furthermore, the cycling perfor-
mance of a control cell with the configuration of Li | TMS | 
GPE(DBE) | TMS | sulfur cathode was measured (Figure S8, 
Supporting Information); the cell could maintain stable cycling 
for 30 cycles and experienced a fast capacity drop afterward. 
This is consistent with our half-cell results that the Li metal dis-
plays poor compatibility with the sulfolane-based electrolytes.
The lean-electrolyte Li–S pouch full cell enables a critical 
assessment of the sulfur cathode kinetics under practical con-
ditions.[38] The cathode electrolyte to sulfur ratio was further 
reduced to 4 µL mg−1 (Figure 5d,e). The overall weight ratio of 
GPE + TMS-LiTFSI versus sulfur is 7.7. Under such a low E/S 
ratio, the NH4TFA additive performs a critical role in enhancing 
the cathode kinetics. With the protection of GPE, after 
120 cycles, the cell achieved a specific capacity of 770 mAh g−1 
(3.08 mAh cm−2), retaining 73% of its initial capacity. At 0.2 C 
rate, with NH4TFA additive, the cell displays an initial capacity 
2.3 times higher than the cell without additive. Moreover, after 
discharge, at the cathode side, microsized Li2S particles are 
observed in the cell, confirming the effects of the additive on 
promoting Li2S growth (Figure 5f).

3. Conclusion
In summary, we demonstrated a liquid-based dual-phase elec-
trolyte design for lithium–sulfur batteries, which is highly 
compatible with the lean electrolyte cycling condition. The 
dual-phase electrolyte was designed by considering the mutual 
immiscibility of the two liquid phases and the mediator-
solvating property. The selected dual-phase electrolyte system, 
polyDOL/DBE-LiTFSI || TMS-LiTFSI, enabled the confine-
ment of corrosive electrolyte additive (NH4TFA) and dissolved 
LiPS in the cathode region. The controlled diffusion resulted 
in improved Li-metal stability. Moreover, the use of highly 
solvating catholyte promises high sulfur utilization. Conse-
quently, a lithium–sulfur pouch cell with the dual-phase elec-
trolyte exhibited a much more sustainable cycling performance. 
Without any electrode modifications, the cells were capable of 
cycling under lean electrolyte (E/S = 4 µL mg−1), low Li excess 
(N/P = 3) conditions. The concept of liquid-based dual-phase 
electrolytes can spur renewed efforts to meet the electro-
lyte requirements for various types of Li-metal batteries with 
aggressive battery chemistry.
4. Experimental Section
Materials: All chemicals were used as received. LiTFSI, lithium nitrate, 
lithium hexafluorophosphate, DBE, TMS, DME, DOL, NH4TFA, Ketjen 
black (KB), multiwalled carbon nanotube (MWCNT), conductive carbon 
(Super P), polyethylene oxide (MW = 40 000), polyvinylpyrrolidone 
(MW = 55 000), PETEA, and azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) were received 
from Sigma-Aldrich.
Gel Polymer Electrolyte and Anode Preparation: The liquid precursor was 
prepared in a two-step process. First, 1 m LiTFSI in DBE (33.3 vol%) was 
mixed with 1 m LiTFSI in DOL (66.7 vol%). Further, LiPF6 (2 wt%), LiNO3 
(2 wt%), PETEA (2 wt%), and AIBN (0.5 wt%) additives were added to 
the liquid precursor (93.5 wt%). To prepare the GPE-protected anode, 
two phosphate-coated Celgard 2500 separators were first placed on the 
surface of Li foil, and then the liquid precursor was added (5 µL cm−2). 
The Li metal and separator were sealed in a coin cell or a pouch cell to 
complete the thermal curing process (60 °C for 4 h). Owing to the high 
boiling point of DBE, negligible swelling was observed for the pouch cell 
after heating. The as-derived GPE-protected Li anode was taken out and 
used for cell assembly.
Cathode Preparation: The sulfur cathodes were prepared by mixing 
0.7 g of sulfur/KB, 0.1 g of Super P, and 0.1 g of MWCNT with 8 wt% 
polyethylene oxide and 2 wt% polyvinylpyrrolidone in 10  mL DI water. 
The resultant slurry was blade-casted onto a carbon-coated Al foil and 
dried to yield cathodes with a loading of 4 ± 0.5 mg cm−2. The electrolyte 
(based on TMS) to sulfur ratio was controlled to be 7.5 µL mg−1 for coin 
cell and 4 µL mg−1 for pouch cell, respectively. To understand the kinetics 
of sulfur conversion, one layer of carbon paper (Avcarb P-50, 1.1 cm2) 
with a low surface area was in use. 50 µL liquid electrolyte containing 
0.4  m Li2S6 (3.8  mg sulfur) and 1  m LiTFSI in TMS was added to the 
carbon paper. The conditions with and without 0.2 m NH4TFA additives 
were compared. The anode was all protected by the GPE during the 
kinetic measurement.
Cell Assembly and Test: CR2032 coin cells were assembled inside an 
argon-filled glovebox with O2/H2O level below 0.1 ppm. All full or half 
cells used one layer of Celgard 2325 separator filled with liquid electrolyte 
or GPE without specially specified. The TMS-based or DOL/DME-based 
liquid electrolytes were further added onto the 4 mg cm−2-loading sulfur 
cathode when assembling the cell. This is followed by the addition of a 
separator and a 100 µm-thick (20 mAh cm−2) Li metal foil. To evaluate 
the Coulombic efficiency of Li stripping/plating, Li || Ni half cells were 
assembled by sandwiching the 600 µm-thick Li foil, a separator, and Ni 
foil.
Single-layered soft-packaging pouch half and full cells (cathode size: 
5.3 cm × 4.8 cm) were fabricated like the coin cells. The tabs were welded 
onto the aluminum and nickel foil backings, respectively. The pouch cells 
were injected with a controlled amount of liquid electrolyte (360  µL) 
before being sealed.
The pressure control setup was built in-house. The pressure sensor 
was received from SIMBATOUCH Co. Ltd. In all coin and pouch cell 
tests, the anode was put on top of the cathode for two purposes: 
i) gravity can prevent the inter-diffusion of electrolytes, and ii) TMS-based 
catholyte does not corrode the unprotected edge of Li-metal anode.
Characterization: 1H and 19F-NMR spectroscopies were performed 
via a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. 128 scans of four 
each sample were performed, and the chemical shifts (δ) were calibrated 
using the residual solvent peak as 10 vol% d6-acetone was used as 
the internal standard. 19F NMR was used to determine the LiTFSI salt 
concentration in TMS; 0.1 m CF3COOLi (LiTFA) was fully dissolved in the 
TMS electrolyte as the benchmark.
To understand the Li+ mobility, each 7Li NMR scan was performed 
with a Varian VNMRS 600  MHz spectrometer at 23 °C. Sixteen scans 
were run at each temperature, with a relaxation delay of 60 s, a pulse 
width of 14.5 s, and an acquisition time of 2 s. In the case of overlapping 
peaks, the spectra were deconvoluted to process the raw data and 
determine peak locations. Samples were prepared in an argon-filled 
glovebox consisting of either 0.2 m NH4TFA or 0.2 m NH4TFA and 0.1 m 
Li2S6 in TMS. 10 vol% d6-acetone was used as the internal standard.
Morphological characterization of sulfur cathode and Li-metal anode 
was carried out with a field-emission scanning electron microscopy 
(Vega), employing a source with a beam voltage of 10 kV.
Operando XRD analysis was performed with an Ultima IV X-ray 
diffractometer. The slurry containing sulfur/ketjen black and PEO/PVP 
binder in a weight ratio of 4:1 was coated onto a beryllium disk to reach 
an areal sulfur loading close to 8 mg cm−2. A GPE-protected Li foil was in 
use. 32 µL cm−2 liquid electrolyte containing 0.2 m NH4TFA additive was 
added to reach an E/S ratio of 4 µL mg−1. The scan rate was 1° min−1, 
and the operando XRD cell was cycled between 1.7 (or 1.5) and 3 V at a 
0.05 C rate with a Land CT2001A battery test system. Cyclic voltammetry 
and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) (1  MHz ∼ 0.1  Hz, 
alternating current amplitude 50 mV) were performed on a potentiostat 
(Bio-logic, VMP3) via the two-electrode setup.
Molecular Dynamics Simulations: The calculation was conducted 
with GROMACS 2020.4. DBE molecules displayed red color, while 
TMS molecules displayed yellow color (Figure  1e). First, a random 
amorphous model was constructed with packmol, containing 632 TMS 
molecules and 356 DBE molecules, and the initial model size was

was completed. The energy-minimized system was pre-equilibrated with 
NPT for 1 ns, the temperature was controlled by the V-rescale method to 
298.15 K, and the Berendsen method was used to control the pressure 
for 1 atm. When the formal NPT molecular dynamics calculation
