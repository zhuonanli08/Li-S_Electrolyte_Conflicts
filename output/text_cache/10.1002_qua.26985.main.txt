Abstract
The structure of lithium polysulfides has been studied using density functional the-
ory. The thermodynamic characteristics of their most probable transformations in
sulfolane (Sl) have been evaluated. Various models of lithium oxidation and reduction
of sulfur are studied by the PBE1PBE/6–311 + G(d,p) approximation, considering the
influence of the solvation environment by the IEF-PCM. The most probable mecha-
nism of sulfur reduction in the Li–S system is considered (Voltage vs. Li/Li+):
(I) S8 + 2Li ! Li2S8 (2.6 V), (II) Li2S8 + 2Li ! 2Li2S4 (2.3 V), (III) Li2S4 + 2Li ! 2Li2S2
(1.6 V), (IV) Li2S2 + 2Li ! 2Li2S (1.3 V). It was shown that the potential of the reduc-
tion of sulfur compounds is substantially determined by the composition of the sol-
vate complexes of lithium polysulfides. The solvate complex Li2S82Sl is the most
stable in the form of a «broken crown». The preference of the fragmentation of Li2S8
into LiS4
 and LiS4 upon electron transfer is shown using FMO theory.

INTRODUCTION
The lithium–sulfur electrochemical system (LSES) is considered as one of the most promising systems for developing batteries with outstanding
specific energy characteristics since it has a high theoretical specific energy (2600 Wh kg1). An important advantage of the Li–S system is the
low toxicity of sulfur, its widespread occurrence in nature, and, as a result, availability and low cost.
Sulfur and intermediate products of its reduction (lithium polysulfides) are soluble in electrolyte solutions and are equilibrium distributed
between the electrolyte solutions [1–3] and the positive electrode of lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB), which are batteries with a liquid cath-
ode [4–10].
During the LSB discharge, sulfur is reduced to lithium polysulfides, which form solvate complexes upon dissolution [11, 12]. The stability and
composition of complexes of lithium polysulfides with solvent molecules of the electrolyte system affect the characteristics of the LSB: discharge
voltage profiles, depth of sulfur reduction during discharge, and specific energy [11, 13–15]. Electrochemical reduction of sulfur, which occurs
during discharge, is a complex process and is conducted in several stages (Figure 1) [16].

Initially, sulfur is reduced to lithium octasulfide (section I on the discharge voltage profile), then the generated lithium octasulfide is reduced
to lithium tetrasulfide (section II on the discharge voltage profile). Lithium tetrasulfide is reduced through a series of intermediate transformations
to lithium disulfide (section III on the discharge voltage profile). At the final stage of the LSB discharge, lithium disulfide is reduced to lithium sul-
fide (section IV on the discharge voltage profile). The presented description of the process of electrochemical reduction of sulfur is highly simplis-
tic and does not fully reflect the whole variety of chemical, physicochemical, and electrochemical processes occurring in lithium–sulfur batteries
during their discharge. Note that as the length of the polysulfide chain decreases, the rate of electrochemical transformations of lithium poly-
sulfides decreases.
The composition and properties of electrolyte systems have a great influence on the kinetics of electrochemical transformations of lithium
polysulfides, since lithium polysulfides form solvate complexes with electrolyte solvents. The composition and properties of lithium polysulfide
solvates, formed during the LSB discharge, are determined by the properties of electrolyte solvents. Many compounds have been proposed as
electrolyte solvents for LSB electrolytes [17, 18]. Sulfones are of great interest as promising electrolyte solvents, and, in particular, sulfolane (Sl),
since it is capable of solvating lithium cations by oxygen atoms of the sulfonic group [19], dissolving, and ionizing electrolyte salts well, including
lithium polysulfides. Sulfolane is highly resistant to strong acids, bases, and oxidants, as well as it is of high thermal stability [4, 20]. Sulfolane is of
moderate toxicity [21–23].
Despite the variety of experimental and theoretical studies, the composition and structure of solvate complexes of lithium polysulfides with
different coordination and electrolyte solvents are not completely clear; there is no understanding of the most probable trajectories of their elec-
trochemical transformations.
A number of theoretical works have been published on the study of the structure of lithium polysulfides and the reactions of their reduction
to lithium sulfide [24–27]. In particular, the effect of organic solvents on the processes of clustering of polysulfide structures in the Li–S electro-
chemical system was modeled [25]. It is important to note that the effect of the solvent on the structure and mechanism of the reduction of lith-
ium polysulfides in published works is usually made, at the continuum level (IEF-PCM) [28]. The main reasons for the rare use of explicit solvation
models [29] in modeling electrochemical systems are:
1. the ambiguity of the construction of the first solvation sphere of the solute;
2. the increase in the error of the theoretical estimate due to the flat shape of the potential energy surface (PES) of their structure;
3. limitation of the use of high-level ab initio methods for calculating the electronic structure due to the potentially high computational cost;

Despite the above limitations, the complication of the model of the solvation sphere of lithium polysulfides by additional solvent molecules is

Thus, the aim of this work was to assess the effect of sulfolane solvation of lithium polysulfides with different order on the thermodynamics
of the processes of their electrochemical transformations, and to develop an adequate model of redox processes in the Li–S electrochemical sys-
tem by DFT methods.

METHODS
All the calculations were carried out using Gaussian 09, Revision C.01 [30] program package for standard conditions (1 atm, 298.15 K). The pri-
mary processing of quantum chemical computation results including structure comparing was performed using Chemcraft 1.8 (build 574b) [31].
Geometry optimization, equilibrium vibrational frequency calculation, and natural bond orbital analysis (NBO, version3) [32–36] were performed
at the level of density functional theory. PBE generalized gradient functional with a predefined amount of exact exchange (PBE1PBE also known
as PBE0) [37, 38] was used. Earlier, it was demonstrated that the chosen functional is an effective and relevant tool for the investigation of lithium
polysulfides [39–41]. Pople's triple split-valence basis set augmented with d- and p-type polarization functions and a set of diffuse functions
[6–311 + G(d,p) (5D,7F)] [42, 43] was applied. The dispersion correction was not added to the computation scheme in this case because it leads
to an increase of error along with chosen functional [44]. Integration grid having 175 radial shells and 974 angular points [45] was used to increase
the level of accuracy and improve the convergence of structures with flat potential energy surface (PES) in terms of geometry. Open shell
unrestricted wave function (unrestricted Kohn–Sham equations in case of DFT computations) was used for structures with unpaired electrons
and spin polarization. Wave function stability was checked according to technique [46, 47] for all chemical species considered in this work.
Enthalpy and Gibbs free energy of some model transformations of lithium polysulfides calculated using PBE1PBE functional and G4 composite
technique (G4, geometry optimization was performed using B3LYP/GTBas3) (6D, 7F) [48].
As may be seen from Table 1, chosen functional, PBE1PBE, allows us to get the results reasonably consistent with those of high-level sophis-
ticated calculations. Note that the energy is overestimated by 40 kJ mol1 (it is equivalent to voltage difference 0.5 V) compared with the G4
method for structures S3 and S5. The observed picture may be caused by an insufficient covering of static electron correlation at the level of the
density functional theory in view of the quasi-degeneracy of the singlet and triplet PES of the pointed sulfur compounds and as a consequence,
the multiconfigurational character of their wave function.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The development of an adequate model of the electrochemical system is a non-trivial task because of the variety of physicochemical processes
occurring during its functioning. The Li–S electrochemical model system, studied in this work, consists of solid negative electrode (metallic lithium)
and lithium polysulfides of various structures Li2Sn (n = 1…8), which are sequentially formed during the reduction of elemental sulfur S8 and stabi-
lized by a solvent, sulfolane. In the framework of the model used, we did not consider the composition of the positive electrodes and the pro-
cesses on their surface: adsorption and reduction of participants in electrochemical processes on the electrode surface, the passivation of the
electrode surface with lithium sulfide and etc. Another model limitation is ignoring the participation of the support salt in electrochemical
processes.
The specificity of reactions in electrochemical cells makes it possible to separate the description of oxidation and reduction processes. The
proposed model includes several components:
• oxidation of a lithium atom at the negative electrode, solvation of the generated lithium cation and its release into solution;
• reduction of sulfur to lithium sulfide with the formation of intermediate compounds - solvated lithium polysulfides.
In both cases, solvation effects play a key role, which affects the thermodynamics of the electrochemical reduction of sulfur and lithium poly-
sulfides of various dimensions. In work [27], the effect of the solvent was described using the polarized continuum model, but the sufficiency of
this approach for obtaining reliable estimates is not obvious. Thus, Table 2 shows the thermodynamic characteristics (ΔH, ΔG) of four reactions
for the reduction of sulfur and lithium polysulfides to lithium sulfide, calculated considering only the IEF-PCM solvation model.
The values of E, estimated using only the continuum model, demonstrate the experimentally observed trend of the change in E(OCV), how-
ever, with a significant overestimation of the potential at the initial stages of sulfur reduction (Table 2). Obviously, it is necessary to correct E
using the combined model of solvation of active species by sulfolane, which is a solvation cluster in a continuous medium (EXPL + PCM). This
approach has been implemented for both lithium oxidation and sulfur reduction.
The factors complicating the description of electrochemical processes, but apparently necessary for constructing of the adequate model of
Li–S electrochemical system, are the processes occurring at the interface between the solid and liquid phases: the oxidation of lithium on the
metal surface, as well as the transition of lithium sulfide from solution to the solid state. These factors were taken into account within the

Second model. Taking into account the high dipole moment (μ = 4.7 D), polarity (ε0 = 43.962), and structural features of the sulfolane mole-
cule, we described the interaction of the lithium cation with the solvent at an explicit level to eliminate the above-described drawback of the con-
tinuum model. For this purpose, a lithium cation complex with two sulfolane molecules was modeled (Figure 2 and Table S1).
When sulfolane is coordinated to Li+, the positive charge on the lithium cation decreases compared with the results of the continuum model
(Δq[Li+] = 0.116 a.u.). In the optimized structure of the Sl–Li+–Sl complex, bidentate binding of the cation by the sulfolane molecule takes place,
which makes it possible to compensate for the imperfection of the continuum model. This model has only the stabilization of the Li+ cation by
sulfolane molecules and leads to an overestimated value of the absolute potential of lithium oxidation 0.8 V (Etheor(SHE) = +4.0 V) compared
to the value of the first model.
It is well known that oxygen has a high affinity to lithium; therefore, the sulfolane molecule can also coordinate to the neutral lithium atom. In
the third model, the physical meaning of the described interaction is the presence of the solvation layer at the interface between the negative
electrode and the electrolyte solution. The model of solvation of the Li atom by one sulfolane molecule led to a reasonable decrease in the abso-
lute potential of lithium oxidation 1.9 V (Etheor(SHE) = +2.9 V).
The disadvantage of the above models is that they ignore the state of aggregation of metallic lithium. The Li/Li+ potential is formed not by
one Li atom but by the material of the negative electrode, that is, some energy must be spent to break the bonds in the crystal lattice of the metal.
To cover this energy component in the fourth and fifth models, we considered the Li dimer and a cluster of six Li atoms (Figure 3), solvated by
one sulfolane molecule as an electron donor. These models provide additional stabilization of the Li atom on the surface of the negative electrode
by 0.4 V (Etheor(SHE) = +2.6 V) and 0.7 V (Etheor(SHE) = +2.3 V) compared with the third model. Note that the model of a cluster of lithium
atoms Lin (n ≤9) was used in calculating the change in the Gibbs energy of an LSES in [25]. However, the authors of the work limited themselves
to an explicit description of only the solid phase of the electrode, sequentially increasing the cluster due to alkali metal atoms. It was found that
for nine lithium atoms, the change in the relative total energy within the chosen approximation becomes negligible. The solvation of the oxidized
form of lithium in the form of polysulfides (Li2Sn)8/n (n = 1, 2, 4) was taken into account by the authors of [25] on the continuum level of theory.
In the model, we used a 3D cluster with a smaller size Li6 (Figure 3C), which provides the 3D structure of the metal both in the initial state and
after the release of the lithium-ion into the volume of the electrolyte solution (Figure 3D). The size of the lithium cluster was chosen based on the
results of calculations of the redox potentials Li/Li+ and 0.5H2/H+ for the aqueous medium. It is shown (Equations (S1–S4)) that the cluster model
(Li6-water) of a lithium electrode (Etheor(SHE) = 3.022–3.264 V) makes it possible to reproduce the reference data (Eexp[Li/Li+] = +3.040 V
[54]). Simpler forms of describing the anode tend to overestimate the potential, as demonstrated by the Equations S2–S4 of the Data S1
(Table S2).
Thus, as the scheme for modeling the processes occurring on the negative electrode becomes more sophisticated, the calculated value of the
potential Etheor(SHE) changes significantly and became 2.3–2.6 V for the most complicated models (Table 3). Our estimations are in good agree-
ment with the data of [25], in which Etheor(SHE) values were calculated in the range 2.1–2.8 V. for oxygen-containing solvents (THF, acetone,
dimethyl sulfoxide).

Electron density
The aforementioned trend in the changing in the calculated bond lengths Li–S and S–S at the switch from PCM to EXPL + PCM model illustrates
the importance of explicitly taking into account the solvent for a correct description of the properties of lithium polysulfides. The calculated effect
can be explained by the partial transfer of electron density from sulfolane to the lithium cation and, as a consequence, a decrease in the degree of
ionicity of the Li–S bond, as indicated by the lower values of NBO charges on Li cations obtained using the EXPL + PCM approach. Solvation also
affects the properties of S–S bonds. As it can be seen from Table S4, within the EXPL + PCM approach, for all compounds of the general formula
Li2Sn (n = 2, 4, 8), an increase in the electron density ρ is observed, with the exception of S3–S4 (S5–S6) bonds of lithium octasulfide.

characterized by a 70% contribution to the total energetics of the process.

mini-cluster (Li2S)2 is significant but does not sufficiently improve the results of calculating E.

Thermodynamics of the discharge of Li–S electrochemical system
To calculate the thermodynamic parameters of the LSES discharge process, we combined the results of modeling the half-reactions occurring on
the negative (lithium oxidation) and positive (sulfur reduction) electrodes. It should be noted that for the oxidation of lithium, the most detailed
models for considering solvation effects were chosen, which most reliably describe the oxidation potential of an alkali metal. The data are summa-
rized in Table 5, where the key structural differences of the previously presented lithium oxidation models are highlighted.
As it can be seen from the results of calculating the thermodynamic characteristics of reactions (1–4), the complex approach (EXPL + PCM)
to considering the effect of the condensed medium (sulfolane and the crystalline phase of the electrode) on the energetics of electron transfer
processes provides a satisfactory description of the change in the potential of the LSES during its discharge. Indeed, the considered model of sul-
fur reduction to lithium sulfide in combination with various models of lithium oxidation (Table 5) reproduces not only the general trend of change
in E at different stages of the discharge of the LSES, which is achievable already at the continuum level of solvation (Table 2), but also the abso-
lute values of the potential. It is important to note that (III - V) models of lithium oxidation take into account the explicit factors of interaction
between an atom (crystal lattice) and a cation (solvation by sulfolane) of the alkali metal on the negative electrode, which is necessary for an ade-
quate description of the LSES potential. The most reliable results were obtained in the framework of the V model, in which special attention is
paid to the process of solvation of the lithium cation at the interface between the solid phase and the electrolyte solution, as well as to changes
on the electrode surface. The observed deviations from the experimental dependence of the LSES potential on the specific discharge capacity (up
to 30 kJ mol1 at the 3rd stage of the discharge) may be due to the model constraints described above. The developed LSES model can be easily
modified. In our opinion, the most natural candidates for improving the model are possible processes of disproportionation/crosslinking of

CONCLUSIONS
Quantum-chemical calculations of the reduction of sulfur to lithium sulfide, as well as the oxidation of lithium on the negative electrode in the
sulfolane medium, were performed using the DFT approximation [PBE1PBE/6–311 + G(d,p)]. A mixed model of solvation of lithium polysulfides
is proposed, within which the solvent is taken into account both at the continuum level and explicitly through coordination with respect to the
alkali metal cation in the complex of the general formula Li2Sn2Sl (n = 2, 4, 8). Possible directions of fragmentation of polysulfide chains were
selected using boundary MOs. The energetic preference of symmetric fragmentation of S8
2 and S4
2 has been established, which is in agreement
with the results of calculations by the authors of [25]. Models of lithium oxidation on the negative electrode, considering the stabilization of the
alkali metal atom and cation before and after oxidation to varying degrees, are proposed. It was shown that the most reliable is the model
according to which lithium in the composition of a metal electrode is described by a six-atom cluster, and in solution—in the form of the symmetric
solvate complex Sl–Li+–Sl. Combining the models of the processes occurring on the positive and negative electrodes made it possible to obtain
the value of the lithium–sulfur electrochemical system's potential at various stages of its discharge, which is reasonably consistent with the exper-
iment. Thus, an adequate model of the Li–S electrochemical system has been developed with an explicit description of the solvation of lithium
polysulfides by sulfolane. It can be used as a basic model for a detailed description of the processes taking place in the LSES. A number of factors
affecting the electrochemical properties have not been adequately studied. In particular, this is the interaction of polysulfides with support salts
and with each other. The response of the proposed model to taking these interactions into account will make it possible to determine the role and
scale of the effect of these factors, and the need for further detailing of the model. Work in this direction seems to be of top priority in the devel-
opment of the topics of our research.
