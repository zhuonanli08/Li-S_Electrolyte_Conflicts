A B S T R A C T
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have garnered significant attention due to high energy density and low cost. 
However, their practical development is impeded by sluggish reaction kinetics and the growth of lithium (Li) 
dendrites, primarily caused by the dissolution of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) intermediates in the electrolyte. In 
this work, we propose a novel strategy involving rare-earth-based electrolyte additives. Specifically, holmium 
chloride (HoCl3) is introduced as an additive in the electrolyte. The Ho³ ⁺ serve as homogeneous catalysts, 
significantly accelerating the LiPSs conversion and reducing the decomposition energy barrier of lithium sulfide 
(Li2S). Furthermore, these rare-earth ions participate in the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on 
the Li anode, promoting the generation of an SEI layer enriched with rare-earth inorganic compounds and 
chlorides. Ho ions construct a cationic electrostatic shielding layer on the Li anode, thereby effectively sup­
pressing the growth of Li dendrites. As a result, Li–S batteries incorporating HoCl3 exhibit markedly improved 
conversion kinetics and excellent electrochemical performance. The battery maintains a high specific capacity of 
852.87 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles at 0.2 C. Even at a high rate of 5 C, it delivers 657.62 mAh g−1, and under a high 
sulfur loading of 11.43 mg cm−2, it achieves an areal capacity of 17 mAh cm−2. This study not only offers a new 
approach for designing electrolyte additives in Li–S batteries but also provides valuable insight into the appli­
cation of rare-earth materials in advanced energy storage systems.
1. Introduction
Li–S batteries have attracted widespread attention due to the high 
theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh g−1 and theoretical energy 
density of 2600 Wh kg−1 [1,2]. Additionally, sulfur, the cathode active 
material, is abundant, inexpensive, and often derived from industrial 
waste, making it a highly promising candidate for next-generation 
rechargeable batteries. However, sulfur’s inherently low electronic 
conductivity significantly hinders its electrochemical performance in 
batteries. This limitation has been addressed by incorporating conduc­
tive materials such as graphene and carbon nanotubes to enhance 
overall conductivity [3–5]. Another critical challenge of Li–S batteries 
lies in their complex multi-electron redox reactions, involving the 
stepwise conversion of sulfur to LiPSs and eventually to Li2S [6]. The 
intermediate LiPSs are highly soluble in ether-based electrolytes, lead­
ing to severe shuttle effects [7]. Moreover, the sluggish reaction kinetics 
of LiPSs, the high decomposition energy barrier of Li2S, and detrimental 
side reactions with the Li anode further hinder the realization of long 
cycle life in Li–S batteries.
Several strategies have been developed to address the above chal­
lenges. High catalytic activity heterogeneous and homogeneous cata­
lysts have been developed to accelerate the transformation of LiPSs, 
targeting slow reaction kinetics [8]. Heterogeneous catalysts primarily 
consist of metal compounds, such as metal sulfides [9], heterostructures 
[10], defect catalysts [11], and single-atom catalysts [12], while ho­
mogeneous catalysts are characterized by high catalytic activity and 
enable bidirectional transformation of LiPSs. For instance, Kwun Nam 
Hui et al. [13] discovered that introducing vacancies into MoS2 could 
effectively enhance its catalytic activity in Li–S batteries. However, the 
high surface energy of MoS2 limits the stability of the catalyst. To 
address this, an In-doped strategy was proposed, introducing Indium 
(In) into the vacancy-deficient MoS2 lattice (In-Vs-MoS2). This design 
not only improves the stability of sulfur vacancies but also modulates the 
spin state of the catalyst. Doping interacts with surface vacancies, 
optimizing charge distribution, facilitating the redox kinetics of sulfur, 
and reducing the formation energy barrier for Li2S. This provides

valuable insights for the design of transition metal dichalcogenide 
(TMDs) electrocatalysts. Compared to transition metal catalysts, 
single-atom catalysts (SACs) offer a theoretical 100% catalytic activity 
and hold promise for accelerating LiPSs catalytic reactions. For example, 
Geping Yin et al. [14] proposed an Nb single-atom catalyst for theo­
retical batteries, finding that the distorted Nb–N bond exhibited a 
stronger interaction compared to traditional fourth-period single-atom 
catalysts. This enhanced the d-p orbital hybridization between SAC and 
LiPSs, benefiting from abundant active sites and accelerated LiPSs re­
action kinetics provided by Nb-SAC. This enabled the realization of a 
“capture-coupling-conversion” mechanism for LiPSs in theoretical bat­
teries, improving the cycling stability with a high specific capacity of 
740 mAh g−1 at 7 C, offering a new approach for the development of Li–S 
catalysts. However, catalytic sites of heterogeneous catalysts are not 
fully utilized, and while single-atom catalysts can theoretically achieve 
100% catalytic activity, their complex preparation process and low yield 
limit their large-scale application. Homogeneous catalysts, which 
contain metal ions and dissolve in the electrolyte, can fully interact with 
LiPSs due to their solubility, thereby promoting LiPSs conversion and 
enabling high-performance Li–S batteries. For example, Cu-containing 
homogeneous catalysts are considered suitable additives in Li–S bat­
tery electrolytes. The self-assembled macrocyclic Cu (II) complex (CuL) 
acts as an effective catalyst to homogenize and maximize liquid-phase 
reactions. Such structures not only reduce the energy barrier for the 
liquid-solid conversion (from Li2S4 to Li2S2) but also guide the 
three-dimensional (3D) deposition of Li2S2/Li2S [15]. For instance, 
Zhouguangmin et al. [16] used a bimetallic copper complex bridged by 
dichlorides, which underwent two anion substitutions to eventually 
form a bimetallic copper complex with a single-chloride bridge in the 
electrolyte, leading to the formation of a final homogeneous catalyst, 
Cu-2-T. They proposed that an in-situ generated bimetallic copper 
complex in the electrolyte could serve as a homogeneous catalyst to 
regulate both sulfur conversion and lithium species evolution.
Electrolyte engineering offers a promising approach to simulta­
neously address two critical challenges in Li–S batteries: the high 
decomposition energy barrier of Li2S caused by sluggish LiPSs kinetics, 
and the formation of Li dendrites at the anode [17,18]. Among various 
strategies, the use of electrolyte additives stands out due to its simplicity, 
scalability, and practicality. Rare-earth materials, owing to unique 
electronic structures and effective catalytic activity toward LiPSs, have 
been widely explored in Li–S batteries and other battery systems. For 
instance, in zinc metal batteries, introducing trivalent yttrium ions (Y³⁺) 
enables the formation of a dynamic cationic shielding layer on the zinc 
anode surface, which effectively suppresses corrosive reactions between 
the electrolyte and the zinc electrode [19]. Similarly, cerium 
chloride—a rare-earth additive—has demonstrated comparable protec­
tive effects [20]. In Li metal batteries, multivalent cations have also been 
employed as electrolyte additives to protect the Li metal anode. For 
example, Tetsu Ichitsubo et al. [21] reported that Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, La³ ⁺, and 
Ce³ ⁺ additives can regulate the solvation structure of the electrolyte, 
significantly reducing Li+ consumption at the electrode surface. Among 
these, rare-earth additives such as La³ ⁺ contribute to the formation of 
La–O and La–F bonds, which enhance the uniformity and stability of the 
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), effectively suppressing dendrite 
growth. In the context of Li–S batteries, rare-earth materials have also 
been employed as electrolyte additives. For example, Gao et al. [22] 
proposed the use of lanthanum nitrate as an additive, which facilitates 
the formation of a La/Li2S composite passivation layer on the Li metal 
anode. This layer reduces the reactivity of Li and enhances anode sta­
bility. Moreover, Professor Arumugam Manthiram [23] introduced a 
bifunctional additive, Nd(OTf)3, for anode-free Li–S batteries. Nd(OTf)3 
not only catalyzes the conversion of LiPSs and accelerates reaction ki­
netics, but also regulates Li plating and stripping processes, providing 
effective protection for the Li anode. These studies highlight the great 
potential of rare-earth elements in electrolyte engineering. However, 
existing research has primarily focused on their catalytic functions and 
protective roles for Li anodes, while largely overlooking their influence 
on the decomposition energy barrier of Li2S during the LiPSs conversion 
process. Therefore, the application of rare-earth materials as electrolyte 
additives in Li–S batteries remains a fertile area for further investigation. 
This study reveals that the catalytic properties of rare-earth materials 
hold promise for facilitating LiPSs catalysis and protecting the Li anode.
In this work, we propose a strategy for designing rare-earth-based 
electrolyte additives. By incorporating HoCl3 as an electrolyte additive 
in Li–S batteries, the unique homogeneous catalytic conversion of Ho³ ⁺ 
ions significantly promote the transformation of LiPSs and reduces the 
decomposition energy barrier of Li2S. Furthermore, rare earth ions 
participate in the formation of the SEI layer on the anode, inducing the 
formation of an SEI layer enriched with rare earth inorganic compounds 
and chlorides. Ho ions establish a cationic electrostatic shielding layer 
on the Li anode, effectively suppressing the growth of Li dendrites. As a 
result, Li–S batteries with this HoCl3 electrolyte additive exhibit signif­
icantly enhanced conversion kinetics and excellent electrochemical 
performance. After 100 cycles at 0.2 C, they retain a high specific ca­
pacity of 852.87 mAh g−1. At a high rate of 5 C, the battery still main­
tains a specific capacity of 657.62 mAh g−1, and at a high areal loading 
of 11.43 mg cm−2, it demonstrates an impressive areal capacity of 17 
mAh cm−2. This work not only provides new insights into electrolyte 
additives for Li–S batteries but also offers a direction for the application 
of rare earth materials in batteries.
2. Results and discussion
As shown in Fig. 1a, the performance of Li–S batteries is severely 
hindered by the sluggish reaction kinetics of LiPSs and their diffusion to 
the Li anode, where they corrode the Li metal. Additionally, the con­
version between LiPSs and Li2S involves significant energy barriers, 
further impeding the redox reaction kinetics. To tackle these challenges, 
we employed the rare-earth salt HoCl3 as an electrolyte additive. The 
introduced Ho³ ⁺ species not only facilitate the homogeneous catalytic 
conversion of LiPSs and promote the generation of favorable SEI com­
ponents, but also establish a cationic electrostatic shielding layer that 
effectively suppresses Li dendrite growth. More importantly, HoCl3 
substantially lowers the decomposition energy barrier of Li2S, thereby 
accelerating the overall LiPSs conversion kinetics (Fig. 1b).

lower plateau at 2.1 V is associated with the reduction of lower-order 
LiPSs into Li2S. The voltage gap (ΔV) between charge and discharge 
reflects the polarization of the cells with different electrolytes [26]. In

the electrochemical process, with a larger ratio representing more effi­

sulfur utilization is shown in Fig. 2d-e. The HoCl3 electrolyte exhibited a 
smaller polarization (196 mV) and higher sulfur utilization (2.4)

Li2S during charging was only 2.18 V for the HoCl3 electrolyte, in

electrolyte. Notably, this low decomposition voltage was well main­
tained over 100 charge–discharge cycles at 0.2 C (Fig. 2f). By compar­
ison, the blank electrolyte exhibited a consistently higher decomposition 
voltage, suggesting sluggish reaction kinetics and excessive energy 
barriers during LiPSs conversion (Fig. S3). Rate performance tests were 
also carried out for both electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 2g, the HoCl3 cell 
consistently delivered higher specific capacities across a wide range of 
rates, maintaining 831.1 mAh g⁻¹ even at 2 C, and recovering to 1079.1 
mAh g⁻¹ upon returning to 0.1 C, indicating excellent rate capability. 
The charge–discharge curves at various rates are displayed in Fig. 2h 
and Fig. S4. In all cases, the HoCl3 electrolyte exhibited smaller voltage 
polarization. A direct comparison of the two electrolytes under different 
rates (Fig. 2i and Fig.S5) further confirms that the HoCl3 cell consis­
tently maintained a lower Li2S decomposition voltage throughout the 
rate tests, highlighting the unique ability of HoCl3 to facilitate Li2S 
conversion.

Considering that the HoCl3 additive electrolyte effectively reduces 
the decomposition voltage of Li2S, promotes the kinetics of LiPSs re­
actions, and suppresses dendrite growth on the Li anode to form a stable 
SEI, we conducted electrochemical performance tests of Li–S batteries 
under higher rates and elevated cathode loadings. As shown in Fig. 7a, 
the long-term cycling performance at 1 C for both the blank and HoCl3 
additive electrolyte was evaluated over 300 cycles. The initial discharge 
capacities for the HoCl3 electrolytes was 958.4 mAh g−1, respectively, 
with the HoCl3 additive electrolyte maintaining a high specific capacity 
of 739.7 mAh g−1 after 250 cycles. Additionally, during the charging and 
discharging processes, it exhibits lower voltage polarization (Fig. 7b). 
Further enhancing the discharge rate to 5 C (9.48 mA cm−2), the HoCl3 
additive electrolyte achieved an initial discharge capacity of 657.62 
mAh g−1 and retained a high specific capacity of about 400 mAh g−1 
after 200 cycles, demonstrating its excellent high-rate performance 
(Fig. 7c). Moreover, the HoCl3 electrolyte also demonstrates outstanding 
high-temperature performance, maintaining a high specific capacity of 
669.1 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles at an elevated temperature of 60 ℃ 
(Fig. 7d). Achieving high-load cathodes is crucial for realizing high 
energy density in Li-S batteries. As illustrated in Fig. 7e and S10, we 
increased the sulfur loading in the cathode to 11.43 mg cm−2 while 
maintaining the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio around 6 μL mg−1. The 
HoCl3 additive electrolyte showed a remarkable areal capacity of 17 
mAh cm−2 during the first discharge. After undergoing 15 charge- 
discharge cycles at 0.05 C, it still maintained a high areal capacity of 
7.5 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 7f). Compared to recently reported high-loading 
performance in Li–S batteries, the HoCl3 additive electrolyte exhibited 
outstanding characteristics, further validating the potential of HoCl3 
additives in Li–S battery applications (Fig. 7g and Table S1) [44–48].

cilitates homogeneous catalysis for the LiPSs conversion, thereby 
enhancing the kinetics of the redox reactions involving LiPSs. Further­
more, HoCl3 regulates the Li⁺ plating/stripping behavior on the Li 
anode, facilitating the formation of an inorganic-rich SEI layer. Mean­
while, the constructed cationic electrostatic shielding layer effectively 
homogenizes Li⁺ flux and suppresses Li dendrite growth. Ultimately, Li–S 
batteries with HoCl3 added to the electrolyte achieve a high specific 
capacity of 958.4 mAh g−1 at 1 C, and maintain a commendable capacity 
of 657.62 mAh g−1 even at a high rate of 5 C. Moreover, when the sulfur 
loading on the cathode is increased to 11.43 mg cm−2, a high areal ca­
pacity of 17.5 mAh cm−2 is still realized. This indicates the significant 
application potential of this rare earth additive in Li–S batteries, which 
is expected to further promote the utilization of rare earth materials in 
battery technologies.
