Dynamically Regulating Polysulﬁde Degradation via Organic
Sulfur Electrolyte Additives in Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Teng Deng, Juan Wang, Hongyang Zhao, Zhengqian Jin, Li Jin, Xinliang Men,
Jianan Wang, Yatao Liu, Wei Tang, Amr M. Abdelkader, R. Vasant Kumar, Shujiang Ding,
Yongzhu Fu, and Kai Xi*
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries oﬀer promising prospects due to their high
energy density and cost-eﬀectiveness. However, the sluggish kinetics of
lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) conversion, particularly the crucial stage from
LiPSs to lithium sulﬁde (Li2S), hampers their development. Herein, a novel
strategy for dynamically regulating LiPSs conversion by incorporating
4-mercaptopyridine (4Mpy), as a LiPSs Redox Regulator (RR) in the electrolyte
is introduced. This organic sulfur additive actively interacts with LiPSs during
discharge, facilitating rapid conversion and promoting the formation of a
three-dimensional (3D) Li2S structure, thereby enhancing reaction kinetics.
Both theoretical and experimental results reveal that the redox conversion
mechanism with the 4Mpy additive diﬀers from traditional electrolytes. Upon
lithiation, 4Mpy forms lithium-pyridinethiolate (Li-pyS), which reversibly
engages in the LiPSs conversion during the charging/discharging cycles,
signiﬁcantly improving the redox process. As a result, the Li-S battery with
4Mpy additive demonstrates superior performances, achieving 10.05 mAh
cm−2 under a high sulfur loading of 10.88 mg cm−2, surpassing industrial
benchmarks. This study not only presents an approach to mitigating the
shuttle eﬀect in Li-S batteries but also oﬀers valuable insights into electrolyte
design for other metal batteries.

attention due to the high speciﬁc ca-

LiPSs materials,[5] catalyst materials,[6] or by changing the elec-
trolyte through adding functional additives.[7] However, more ef-
fort is required to achieve the practical requirements without sac-
riﬁcing the energy density or the capacity. It is commonly agreed
that developing high-energy-density Li-S batteries is closely re-
lated to modifying state-of-the-art electrolytes.[8] As an environ-
ment for the dissolution of LiPSs, electrolytes play a fundamen-
tal role in the formation and shuttle of LiPSs.[9] In the battery
system, electrolytes account for more than half of the total mass
and cost.[10] Therefore, it is crucial to develop electrolytes that can
solve the problems of the shuttle eﬀect and slow kinetics of LiPSs
conversion with minimum impact on the capacity and energy
density.
One way to modify the electrolyte properties without com-
pletely changing their chemistry is by using electrolyte additives,
such as the classic LiNO3 additive, which forms a stable solid elec-
trolyte interface (SEI) on the surface of the Li anode, suppressing
the shuttle eﬀect.[11] For Li-S batteries, in addition to stabilizing
the Li anode, more electrolyte additives are mainly used to pro-
mote the conversion process of LiPSs. Classically, the electrolyte
additives can play the following roles: homogeneous catalysis,[12]
redox mediators (RM)[13] that regulate reaction kinetics, and elec-
trode modiﬁers that help construct inorganic/organic SEI.[14] Ho-
mogeneous catalysts have shown higher eﬃciency in the LiPSs
conversion than heterogeneous catalysts. For example, the self-
assembled macrocyclic Cu (II) complex (CuL) achieved good ho-
mogeneous catalytic conversion of LiPSs by reducing the energy
barrier of liquid–solid conversion and guiding the Li2S2/Li2S 3D
deposition.[15] However, homogeneous catalytic additives solve
the problem of LiPSs slow conversion kinetics by reducing reac-
tion energy barriers and lacking adjustable ﬂexibility for LiPSs
shuttle. RM additives regulate the reaction of LiPSs and fur-
ther enhance the kinetics of sulfur redox reactions. When ZnI2
was used as an electrolyte additive, the redox couple of I−and
I3−can promote the conversion of Li2S2 and Li2S, alleviate the
LiPSs shuttle, and eliminate dead S and dead Li.[16] Some or-
ganic sulfur and organic selenium compounds have been used
as additives in Li-S batteries with promising success. The func-
tional groups of the organic additives facilitate their binding
with LiPSs, minimizing the shuttle eﬀects.[17] For example, 1,4-
benzenedithiols (1,4BDT) undergoes an oligomerization reac-
tion with LiPSs, changing the LiPSs’ conversion pathway to sup-
press shuttle eﬀects while forming a strong and smooth SEI layer
to suppress the side reactions of Li anode.[18] Similarly, trithio-
cyanuric acid (TMT)[19] and 1,3,5-phenyl trisulﬁde (BTT),[20] bind
with LiPSs to generate a series of intermediate products that reg-
ulate the conversion process of LiPSs. However, the stability of
additives in the long-term charge/discharge cycle is ignored in
most of the current electrolyte additives for Li-S batteries. The

In this work, we propose an organic sulfur, 4Mpy, a LiPSs
RR as an electrolyte additive that can regulate the LiPSs con-
version. The new electrolyte additive simultaneously suppresses
the LiPSs shuttle and the shuttle problem of the additive it-
self, facilitating LiPSs conversion and promoting Li2S deposition
(Figure 1c). When comparing the coulombic eﬃciency with the
traditional redox mediators additive LiI, 4Mpy stimulates much
higher coulombic eﬃciency, ensuring the promotion of sulfur
conversion kinetics. During the ﬁrst discharge, 4Mpy is trans-
formed to Li-pyS, which decreases the concentration of LiPSs in
the electrolyte. In the subsequent charging, Li-pyS is converted
into 4,4-pyS2 in a process that proved to be highly reversible.
Beneﬁting the dynamic regulation of the LiPSs conversion, the
electrochemical performance of the electrolyte is signiﬁcantly im-
proved, with the battery able to maintain a capacity of 10.05 mAh
cm−2 under high loading 10.88 mg cm−2. In addition, the ki-
netics of the LiPSs conversion reaction is proven to be signif-
icantly faster with 4Mpy added via a molecular orbital frontier
analysis.
2. Results and Discussion
The 4Mpy molecule has the -SH functional group and pyridine
nitrogen. With its strong electronegativity, the pyridine nitrogen
can adsorb LiPSs onto the 4Mpy molecules, facilitating the link
between the polysulﬁdes and the -SH functional groups in the
subsequent step. Figure 1d shows the structural conﬁgurations
of 4Mpy, Li2S6, and the adsorbed Li2S6 molecules. The S─S bond
in the adsorbed Li2S6 molecule increases in length from 2.120 to
2.130 Å, making it easier for the adsorbed S─S bonds to break.
Furthermore, the adsorption energy for Li2S6 is 0.93 eV, indicat-
ing that 4Mpy has a suitable adsorption eﬀect on LiPSs. The ad-
sorption energies for other LiPSs on 4Mpy are surmised in Figure
S1 (Supporting Information). Then, we determine the cyclic sta-
bility and the columbic eﬃciency of the 4Mpy electrolyte additive
and compare it to a traditional redox electrolyte additive, LiI. The
testing battery is constructed from a CP cathode and Li anode.
In Figures 1e and S2a (Supporting Information), the coulombic
eﬃciency of the RM electrolyte additive LiI is much lower than
the 4Mpy additive at 0.2 mA and 1.7-2.8 V. The 4Mpy additive
enables an average coulombic eﬃciency as high as 99.14% in
500 cycles, indicating its high degree of reversibility and stabil-
ity. The 4Mpy additive also maintains high coulombic eﬃciency
over 500 cycles at 1 mA higher current density (Figure S2b, Sup-
porting Information), suggesting stable rate capability and facile
reaction kinetics. The poor coulombic eﬃciency of the electrolyte
with LiI is related to the shuttle loss of polyiodide ions in the elec-
trolyte. In addition, the charging and discharging curves of the
electrolytes with 4Mpy additive show an oxidation-reduction po-
tential plateau at ≈2.45 V versus Li/Li+ (between 1.7 and 2.8 V),
which ensures the 4Mpy can participate in the reaction during
the charging/discharging process of Li-S batteries (Figure S2c,d,
Supporting Information). The Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) results
agree with the charge–discharge analysis. As shown in Figure 1f,

This accelerated rapid ion transport signiﬁcantly improves LiPS
conversion kinetics. The ﬁrst and second charge/discharge cy-
cles of Li-S batteries with 4Mpy additive at 0.1 C are shown in
Figure 2b. A short shoulder ≈2.45 V emerged during the sec-
ond discharge cycle, a phenomenon attributed to the 4Mpy addi-
tive. Under an external electric ﬁeld, ≈SH groups are more prone
to undergo dehydrogenation reactions, leading to the formation
of sulfur-free radicals and subsequent thiol salts. In the second
and subsequent charge–discharge cycles, the organic sulfur com-
pounds with –S–S– bonds form and decompose, corresponding
to the voltage plateau observed at 2.45 V. Based on previous litera-
ture reports, thiol-based groups are prone to form organic sulfur
products in Li metal batteries.[19–20] The second charge/discharge
curve has a smaller voltage gap, indicating further polarization
reduction during this reaction process. The redox reaction pro-
cess in the Li-S cell with 4Mpy additive is further studied with CV
(Figures 2c and S5, Supporting Information). The battery without
4Mpy additive exhibits typical reduction peaks at 2.3 and 2.1 V, as
well as oxidation peaks at 2.5 V. In addition to the typical redox
peak, a pair of extra reversible redox peaks appear at ≈2.45 V in
4Mpy additive, consistent with the charging/discharging results.
The battery with 4Mpy additive exhibits higher current density
and a smaller voltage gap (Figure 2d). The results obtained above
indicate that the 4Mpy additive improves the kinetics of LiPSs
conversion. To further investigate the inﬂuence of 4Mpy on alert-
ing the LiPSs conversion, we employed several ex situ spectro-
scopic techniques for the batteries after 5 cycles at 0.1 C. First,
Raman spectrum was conducted on sulfur-free cathodes with
charge cut-oﬀafter cycling (Figures 2e and S6, Supporting Infor-
mation). The Raman spectrum shows peaks of the –S–S– bond
at position 452, 219, and 471 cm−1, as well as the signal of disul-
ﬁde bond –S–S–, proving the presence of organic sulfur and or-
ganic polysulﬁde lithium in Li-S batteries with 4Mpy additive.[23]
Then, high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrom-
etry (HPLC-MS) was performed to analyze the composition of the
intermediate organic compound products after charge/discharge
cycles in Li-S batteries (Figure S7, Supporting Information).[24]
Figure 2f,g show the mass spectra results of discharged prod-
ucts. The peak of mass to charge ratio (m/z) located at 112.0221
(found at 112.0228) is attributed to the generation of protonation
in C5H4NSLi, which is easily replaced by hydrogen for lithium
ions (Li-4pyS).[25] The m/z peak located at 143.9942 (found at
143.9951) can also be attributed to the discharged product of Li-
4pyS2 (C5H4NS2Li). The m/z peak at 221.0207 (found 221.0211)
shown in Figure 2h,i, is for the recharged product C10H8N2S2
(4,4-pyS2), and the peak at 284.9649 (found 284.9652) is for
C10H8N2S4 (4,4-pyS4), indicating the generation of organic sul-
fur recharged products. The LC-MS results indicate that the al-
tered redox process in Li-S batteries with 4Mpy additive is related
to the formation and breakage of the S─S bond of the organic
sulfur. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of
the cathode with 4Mpy additive after cycling show the CNT host
has no obvious large sulfur particles. The corresponding elemen-
tal mapping also shows no sign of large sulfur particles, indi-
cating the uniform distribution of the products after charging
(Figure S8, Supporting Information). The above results suggest
that 4Mpy promotes the formation of organic lithium polysul-
ﬁde products and organic sulfur-recharged products, which can
reduce the production of long-chain LiPSs and the dissolution of
LiPSs in the electrolyte. Furthermore, the scanning electron mi-
croscope (SEM) images of the cycled Li anode show many Li den-
drites on the surface of the w/o 4Mpy additive. The surface of the
anode taken after cycling the battery with 4Mpy electrolyte is ﬂat
and smooth, indicating that Li dendrites are suppressed (Figure
S9, Supporting Information).[26] The Li-Li symmetric cell assem-
bled with 4Mpy additive has stayed stable for 700 h at 1 mA cm−2
with 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure S10, Supporting Information) During
the initial cycling of Li-Li symmetric cells, a signiﬁcant overpo-
tential is observed, likely due to the initial activation of the cells.
The battery w/o 4Mpy additive experienced a micro short circuit
after operating for ≈300 h. The Li-Li symmetric cell with 4Mpy
additive had an overpotential of only ≈10 mV after operating for
600 h, indicating that the 4Mpy simultaneously regulates the con-
version of LiPSs and suppresses Li dendrites.
In a typical Li-S battery, the reaction process usually follows:
the S8 ring undergoes an opening ring reaction, gradually re-
ducing to long and short-chain LiPSs in discharge and ﬁnally
generating the discharged product of Li2S.[27] During the charg-
ing process, Li2S is decomposed, transformed into LiPSs, and ﬁ-
nally oxidized back to S8.[28] Based on the above analysis, we be-
lieve a diﬀerent redox reaction process with 4Mpy additive may
exist. Figure 3a shows the possible reaction mechanism when
4Mpy is added to the electrolyte. In the ﬁrst step, 4Mpy is lithi-
ated or reacts with LiPSs to form Li-pyS, corresponding to the
ﬁrst discharge process. The Li-pyS promotes the Li2S decompo-
sition in the following charging process, corresponding to the
electrochemical reaction at 2.3 V. Then, Li-pyS is oxidized to 4,4-
pyS2 at ≈2.45 V, forming the corresponding organic sulfur.[29,30]
In the subsequent discharge process, the disulﬁde bond of 4,4-
pyS2 breaks to form Li-pyS, which reacts with LiPSs (represented
by Li2S6) and may generate two routes: In route 1), the product
Li2S3 continues to react with Li-pyS, generating product 1 and
Li2S. Product 1 continues to form product 2 and Li2S by lithi-
ation process, while product 2 generates the ﬁnal products Li-
pyS and Li2S. In route 2), product 3 will undergo lithiation to
generate Li2S and product 1, followed by gradual lithiation to
generate Li-pyS and Li2S. Product 1 continues to lithify during
the discharge process to form product 2 and Li2S, while prod-
uct 2 generates the ﬁnal products Li-pyS and Li2S. This process
corresponds to the second and subsequent discharge processes
in the CV and charge/discharge curve. Relevant products Li2S
and Li-pyS were detected by LC-MS, and corresponding organic
sulfur products 4,4-pyS2 and 4,4-pyS4 were also detected in the
recharged products. In the subsequent discharge and charging
reactions, Li-4pyS and 4,4-pyS2 are accompanied by the reaction
of LiPSs, reducing the long-chain LiPSs in the electrolyte and pro-
moting the conversion kinetics of LiPSs.
Molecular orbital frontier analysis was used to investigate the
kinetic process of the above reaction mechanism.[31] In Figure 3b,
during the conversion from Li-pyS4 to Li-pyS2 and Li-pyS, the
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level in-
creased, indicating that Li-pyS4 is ﬁrst reduced to Li-pyS2 and
Li-pyS during the discharge process. In the oxidation process
from Li-pyS and Li-pyS2 to Li-pyS4, the highest occupied molec-
ular orbital (HOMO) energy level is higher, indicating that Li-
pyS2 and Li-pyS are preferentially oxidized during the charging
oxidation process. The narrower bandgap represents the prior-
ity occurrence of the redox reactions. Statistics of bandgaps are

For Li-S batteries, the decomposition and deposition of Li2S is
a critical step in the charging and discharging process.[33] To shed
light on the Li2S deposition process, constant current discharge
tests were conducted using Li2S8 as the active material. The re-
sults show that the battery with 4Mpy additive (Figure 4d) has
higher Li2S deposition capacity and earlier corresponding time
than the battery w/o 4Mpy additive (Figure 4a), suggesting faster
and suﬃcient deposition. Based on the Bewick, Fleischman, and
Thirsk (BFT) models, the deposition curve of Li2S is analyzed
(Figure 4b,e).[34] The deposition mode of Li2S in batteries with
4Mpy additive tends to follow the 3D progressive (3DP) model,
but it tends to the 2D instantaneous (2DI) model in battery w/o
4Mpy additive. The morphology of deposited Li2S is diﬀerent
when 4Mpy is added to the electrolyte, as shown in Figure 4c,f on
CP electrodes. When 4Mpy is added to the electrolyte, large par-
ticles were observed on the CP and conﬁrmed by the EDS to be
Li2S (Figure S13a–d, Supporting Information), further conﬁrm-
ing the deposition of 3D deposited Li2S particles. The decompo-
sition of the deposited Li2S was also investigated using diﬀerent
electrolytes. The constant current test in Figure 4g shows more
Li2S decomposition with a faster rate when 4Mpy is added to the
electrolyte. The SEM images of the CP electrode taken from the
battery with 4Mpy (Figure S13e,f, Supporting Information) show
no sign of Li2S after decomposition, suggesting a complete reduc-
tion. To evaluate the eﬀect of LiPSs catalysis, CV was performed.
As shown in Figures 4h,i, and S14a (Supporting Information),
the CV curves of both electrolytes exhibit redox peaks, which cor-
respond to the conversion processes of Li2S6, speciﬁcally S6
2−+

2−↔8e−+ 3S8. The CV curve for the
4Mpy electrolyte shows two distinct sets of oxidation-reduction
peaks with a higher current density and larger area, indicating
enhanced catalytic kinetics of LiPSs. Additionally, the peak po-

compared to the w/o 4Mpy electrolyte (−0.25 vs −0.36 V), sug-

4Mpy additive can promote the reaction kinetics in Li-S batteries,
catalyzing the conversion process of LiPSs and regulating the 3D

We conducted a further evaluation of the Li-S battery’s elec-
trochemical performance of the electrolyte with 4Mpy additive.

adding 4Mpy as a LiPSs redox regulator not only accelerates the
