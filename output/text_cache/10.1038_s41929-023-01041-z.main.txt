ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li/S) batteries constitute a prom-
ising, next-generation energy storage technology due to their high
theoretical energy density and low cost. To increase sustainability,
processability, and battery performance, conducting organic
polymers have become a focus of research for the development
of better cathode materials. Here, we investigate the solvation
structure of a single conjugated poly(4-(thiophen-3-yl)-
benzenethiol) (PTBT) polymer as a high-potential macro-
molecular candidate for synthesizing optimized cathodes in Li/S
batteries. Using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation with newly
optimized force-field parameters, we examine the effects of
polymer length and various molar fractions of the popular
dimethoxyethane (DME) and dioxolane (DOL) solvents on the structure of the PTBT polymer at a temperature of 300 K. We
characterize basic polymeric properties as well as the composition-dependent solvent adsorption structure and thermodynamics and
uncover significant tunability by the solvent. Importantly, we find an interesting cosolvency effect, namely that a solvent mixture
composed of 25% DME and 75% DOL leads to maximum swelling behavior, which should be important for optimizing cathode
fractality and permeability in applications. Our study thus reveals intriguing polymer−solvent correlations and serves as an important
prerequisite for future MD studies of realistic polymeric cathode structures and processes, e.g., toward charge transport in vulcanized
(S-linked) network topologies.
KEYWORDS: Solvent quality, cosolvency effect, conjugated polymer, polymer cathode, molecular dynamics simulations,
force-field development, lithium-sulfur batteries, solvation structure
1. INTRODUCTION
The lithium−sulfur (Li/S) battery1−6 is a promising electro-
chemical system with a high theoretical specific capacity of
1672 mA hg−1 and a favorable specific energy density of 2567
Wh kg−1, making it a promising alternative for a traditional Li-
ion battery. Furthermore, sulfur is a naturally abundant, easily
available, and relatively inexpensive material that is also
environmentally friendly, all of which is favorable for battery
applications. However, despite the above-mentioned theoreti-
cal advantages, a few drawbacks need to be overcome in order
to be commercially viable. These include (i) the insulating
nature of elemental sulfur and sulfur-containing organic
compounds,7 (ii) the formation and diffusion of low- and
high-order lithium polysulfide, which lead to capacity
degradation over time due to the polysulfide shuttle effect,8,9
and (iii) the volumetric expansion of the Li/S cell that can
cause degradation of the electrodes.10
Up to now, two major strategies have been proposed for
designing the cathode to limit the shuttle effect. Based on the
first strategy, although the production and dissolution of
polysulfides still occur with a solid cathode, the structure of the
cathode prevents the shuttling of polysulfides from the cathode
to the anode.11−14 However, the nonpolarity of the carbon
materials results in insufficient physical confinement of lithium
polysulfides leading to low coulomb efficiency, rapid capacity
decay, and self-discharge.15 The second strategy involves the
use of conjugated organosulfur-based polymer systems16−19
that chemically confine the polysulfides, thereby minimizing
their transport from the cathode to the anode.
Organosulfur-based polymers20,21 have attracted much
attention due to their abundant resources, environmental
friendliness, and adaptable structure. In organosulfur-based
polymers, rather than being free, sulfur chains are introduced
between organic functional groups (R) by means of covalent
C−S bonds. Such an organosulfur-based polymer offers a S−S
bond, which splits during reduction (RSm
−) and reforms during
oxidation (RSmR), where m is the rank of the sulfur chain. This

group suppresses the shuttle effect and thus prevents capacity
fading. Recently, Zou et al.22 have presented an overview of the
benefits of sulfur-containing polymers, the latest advancement,
and an in-depth analysis of the mechanism in Li/S batteries.
However, nonconjugated organosulfur compounds show very
poor conductivity.20 To improve conductivity, conjugated
organic compounds such as polypyrrole, polyaniline, and
polythiophene can be added to the backbone of the
organosulfur-based polymer.23 Like organosulfur-based poly-
mers, conjugated organic compounds can also chemically
confine the polysulfide and thus prevent the shuttle effect.16 In
summary, a conjugated organosulfur-based polymer can reduce
the polysulfide shuttle effect by confinement of sulfur atoms,
increase the conductivity of the polymer, and effectively reduce
the volume change of the sulfur cathode during the charge/
discharge process.
Therefore, in the present work, we consider poly(4-
(thiophene-3-yl)benzenethiol) (PTBT) (Figure 1) as a
conjugated organosulfur-based polymer in which 4-(thio-
phene-3-yl)benzenethiol (TBT) (Figure 1) acts as a monomer.
The PTBT polymer is a flexible, conjugated, carbon-additive-
free cathode for Li/S batteries.24 The thiophene ring in TBT
acts as the conjugated backbone, while benzenethiol is the
organosulfur-based side chain. In recent work, some of us have
shown that out of different regioregularities of thiophene
groups, a head-to-tail/head-to-tail regularity leads to a
crystalline phase, while a head-to-head/tail-to-tail regularity
shows no long-range order.25
The TBT monomer has an active sulfur binding site in the
benzenethiol group, which helps to confine the polysulfide
during the discharge process. The structure of PTBT and
vulcanized PTBT, known as S/PTBT24 (see Figure 1), has
been investigated both theoretically26 and experimentally.24 It
has been found that the S/PTBT cathode exhibits a reversible
capacity of around 870 mAhg−1 at 0.1C (C-rate: the speed at
which a battery is fully charged or discharged), and cycling
performance is better than the physical mix of sulfur & PTBT
cathode.24
Among the other components in lithium−sulfur batteries,
the solvent, the electrolyte types, and the electrolyte
composition27 play significant roles in enhancing the initial
capacity and cyclic stability. The choice of solvent must be
carefully matched to the specific cathode material in order to
achieve optimal performance. The basic requirements of a
solvent for lithium−sulfur batteries are (i) a high ionic
conductivity, (ii) a moderate polysulfide solubility, (iii) a low
viscosity, and (iv) it should be electrochemically stable against
lithium.28 For polymer-based cathodes, since the solvent
quality controls the polymer conformation, the solvent quality
should also be viewed as a basic requirement.
Wang et al.28 reported that ether-type solvents have good
solubility of elemental sulfur and good stability against the
polysulfides in an electrolyte solution. One solvent that has
been found to have good solubility of elemental sulfur and
good stability against polysulfides in an electrolyte solution is
1,3-dioxolane (DOL)29 (Figure 2c). This cyclic ether solvent
has a low polysulfide solubility and kinetics, but it has a more
stable solid electrolyte interface on the anode.30 On the other
hand, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (Figure 2d) is a polar
ether-type solvent with a relatively low viscosity that is reactive
with lithium30 but provides high polysulfide solubility and
kinetics.
Recent developments have demonstrated that binary
mixtures of DME and DOL are found to be suitable solvents
for Li/S batteries.28,30−32 These solvents are able to balance
the trade-off between polysulfide solubility and viscosity,
resulting in improved performance and stability for lithium−
sulfur batteries. Luque Di Salvo et al.33 recently conducted a
study using the ether-based solvents DME and DOL to study
the conformational space of lithium polysulfide (PS). The
results showed a clear dominance of the closed conformer of
PS over the open conformer. Apart from that, several
theoretical and experimental studies34−36 have been performed

As a first step toward a polymer-based cathode, the aim of
this study is to reveal the structural and dynamical properties
of a single PTBT polymer interacting with a mixture of DME
and DOL solvents. Mixed solvents with varying compositions
have been demonstrated by simulations to have intricate effects
on polymer conformations because of the complex interplay
between side-chain chemistry and hydrogen bonding effects.37
Our results on the solvent-mediated conformational behavior
of a single polymer will then help to rationalize the complex
mechanical behavior of the whole cathode material in future
studies. The objectives of this paper are, in particular, to (i)
construct and optimize the force field for PTBT and S/PTBT
polymers, (ii) characterize the basic polymeric properties such
as bond length, persistence length, contour length, and the
characteristic ratio of PTBT, and (iii) reveal how a certain
composition of DME and DOL solvent can be used as a design
parameter to optimize the solvation and structural properties
of a conjugated organosulfur-based polymer. Our study thus
lays the ground for further MD studies of realistic polymeric
cathode structures and processes, for example, to construct S-
linked cathode network topologies and study electrolyte
transport.38
The paper is structured as follows. First, we introduce the
polymer and provide a brief overview of the PTBT and S/
PTBT polymers. We then proceed to discuss the force fields
used for both the polymer and solvents, providing details on
the simulation methods and parameters used. In the following
section, we present and analyze the simulation results, focusing
on the basic properties of the polymer, its conformation, and
the adsorption properties of the solvent. Finally, we summarize
our findings and draw conclusions in the last section.
2. COMPUTER SIMULATIONS AND ANALYSIS
METHODS
2.1. PTBT and S/PTBT Polymer. The preparation of a S/PTBT
polymer is illustrated in Figure 1. In the first step, TBT monomers are
electropolymerized to form a PTBT polymer. In the next step, the
sulfur is embedded into the PTBT framework during vulcanization to
form a free-standing S/PTBT polymer. Schütze et al.26 suggested two
different ways in which polysulfide can bind to the TBT units: (i) the
sulfur chain can connect two adjacent TBT units of the same polymer
chain to form an intrachain (Figure 1(a)) and (ii) a sulfur chain can
form interchain cross-linking TBT monomers that belong to different
polymer chains (Figure 1(b)).
TBT is the basic polymeric unit, both for PTBT and S/PTBT
polymers. A TBT unit consists of 20 atoms, which can be grouped
into 7 different atom types, CA, CW, CS, HA, HS, SH, and S, based
on the OPLS-AA force field39 as described in Figure 2. In the case of
S/PTBT, two TBT units are connected to each other via a sulfur
chain of rank m, or in other words, by a sulfur chain containing m
sulfur atoms. While in principle, the vulcanization process can lead to
both intra and interchains, Schütze et al.26 demonstrated using density
functional theory (DFT) data combined with a statistical binding
model that S/PTBT with interchains and a chain length of m = 5 has
the highest probability of being formed during the vulcanization
process. Since no empirical potential to model such a system is
available in the open literature, a force field was developed for both
PTBT and S/PTBT polymers.

The nonbonded interactions are treated by a combination of
Lennard-Jones (LJ) and electrostatics potentials, while the electro-
statics interactions are decomposed in short- and long-range
contributions. The LJ and the short-range Coulomb potentials are
truncated at 1 and 0.8 nm, respectively. The long-range electrostatic
interactions are evaluated with the particle−particle−particle−mesh47
(PPPM) method with a desired relative error in forces of 10−4 kcal/
(mol Å). The classical geometrical combination rules, σij = (σiiσjj)1/2
and ϵij = (ϵiiϵjj)1/2, are considered for the LJ size and energy
parameters, respectively. Finally, all pairs of connected atoms
separated by up to three bonds (1−2, 1−3, and 1−4 pairs) are
excluded from nonbonded interactions.
Unlike the “hard parameters” of the bond length and angle,
dihedrals are soft and highly coupled to the electrostatics. Therefore,
partial charges for both PTBT and S/PTBT polymers are para-
metrized. We note that we do not expect that slightly different partial
charges in the vulcanized PTBT (S/PTBT) compared to the model
compounds (PHS−, PHS2
−, and PHS3
−) to significantly affect the force-
field parameters (CA−CA−S−S, CA−S−S−S, and S−S−S−S−S), as
contribution of the relative energy difference from the MD simulation
is smaller than that from the DFT calculation (see Figures S3 and
3(a,b)). We obtain the partial charges by utilizing the restrained
electrostatic potential48 (RESP) scheme. We chose this scheme
because it is robust and allows us to accurately detect the variations in
the molecular environment we are studying. The molecular
electrostatic potential surface is obtained from the single-point
calculation CAM-B3LYP/cc-pVTZ using the molecular package
GAMESS. The RESP charges are calculated using the MOLBY49
software.
In Tables S1 and S5, we have listed the charges for DiTBT and S/
PTBT monomers, respectively. To ensure the charge neutrality of the
molecule, we have confirmed that the net charge for each individual
residue is zero, allowing for the construction of long polymer chains
without losing charge neutrality.
The force field was validated by recovering structural properties of
DiTBT-Sm-DiTBT for m = 3−18 in the gas phase predicted by DFT
calculations (see Figures S4 and S5).

2.3. Simulation Details. All-atom MD simulations are carried out
with the LAMMPS simulation package.40 The simulations are
performed using the following protocol. For the different PTBT
chains considered in this work, a short MD simulation (∼20 ps) in
vacuum is performed to coil up the chain. Then, the chain is solvated
with DME, DOL, or a combination of both in different ratios. After
minimization, the temperature of the system is equilibrated at 400 K
for 2 ns in the NVT ensemble, and once equilibrated, a barostat is
turned on to stabilize the pressure at 1 bar while maintaining the
temperature at 400 K within a time interval of 2 ns. Finally, the
temperature is decreased from 400 to 300 K within a time interval of
2 ns while maintaining the pressure at 1 bar. The production
simulations are carried out in the NPT ensemble at constant pressure
and temperature of 1 bar and 300 K, respectively, with a time step of 1
fs. The pressure is controlled by a Nose-Hoover barostat50 with a time
constant of 1 ps, and the temperature is controlled by a Nose-Hoover
thermostat51 with a time constant of 0.1 ps. For analysis, snapshots
are saved every 20 ps.
Nine different PTBT chain lengths are studied in this work. The
chain length, n, is varied by changing the number of monomers
between 3 and 40. The interactions between these polymers and a
solvent (DME, DOL, and the mixture of both) are simulated for 600
ns for n = 5−40 and 200 ns for the case of n = 3. To minimize
interactions between the polymer and its own periodic images, the
box size was scaled with the chain length. Table S10 summarizes the
number of solvent molecules for each solvent composition and each
chain length.

Kirkwood Buff (KB) integral is a measure of the accumulation/

3.2. Polymer Conformation and Solvent Quality.
Snapshots extracted from the simulations for n = 40 are
shown in Figure 4 to illustrate the changes in the polymer’s
state from a collapsed to a moderately expanded state as the
solvent transitions from a pure solvent to a mixed solvent. To
gain further insight, the radius of gyration and structure factor
of the polymer were calculated at different values of x.
The average radius of gyration, as calculated and reported in
Table S10, generally increases as the chain length increases. In
Figure 5(a), the Rg’s are plotted against n for all the
configurations. By fitting Rg values to Flory’s scaling law (eq
7), the scaling constant and the Flory exponent can be
determined as a function of the molar fraction. The results of
this fitting are shown in Figure 5(b) and reported in Table S13.
We found the smallest Flory exponents ν = 0.3 and ν = 0.34
in the cases of pure DOL (x = 0) and pure DME (x = 1),
respectively. However, for mixtures of DME and DOL, the
Flory exponent increased up to ν = 0.41 for a molar fraction of
x = 0.25. This is consistent with the observed changes in
persistence length and the characteristic ratio of the polymer
obtained as a function of x. The lower value of the Flory
exponent indicates a more compact chain, particularly in the
case of pure DOL and pure DME. However, when we mix
these two poor solvents, the chains slightly expand, that is,
resulting in a so-called “cosolvency”65 effect in our polymer−
solvent system. Nonetheless, “cosolvency” should be important
for applications where the cathode material can thus be tuned
in fractality and permeability by scanning through solvent
compositions in this regime.

the qualitative behavior of the solvent quality. The S(q)
obtained for PTBT polymers with different chain lengths in a
solvent mixture of molar fraction x = 0.25 are reported in

of the ideal chain, Sideal(q), and the one of the solid sphere,
Ssolid(q), are plotted as well. Sideal(q) and Ssolid(q) are calculated

wave vectors, q > 4.2 nm−1. Between these two extreme
regions, there is an “intermediate scale regime” (II) bounded
by the structure factor of the ideal chain model and the one of
the solid sphere model. Since the first region provides
information about the length scale of the polymer chain, we
can see in Figure 6 that the magnitude of the structure factor

and gPOL‑DME(r) (see Figure 8(a)). For clarity, we have
reported the RDFs obtained for different solvent molar
fractions only in the case of n = 35, since the data obtained
for the other chain lengths show a similar trend (see Figure
S8). The analysis of the RDFs revealed that the accumulation
of solvent molecules near the polymer region is always higher
for DOL than DME. It is worth noting that the COM of a
polymer may not necessarily be located within the polymer
itself. As a result, a significant peak at r = 0 in the RDF would
indicate the presence of a few solvent molecules located near
the COM. However, they have nonsignificant contributions to
the coordination number.
To reveal the accumulation (or depletion) properties of
solvent molecules around PTBT polymers, we calculated the
KB integral between the polymer and solvents for chain length
n = 35 using eq 12. The results are plotted in Figure 8(b). As it
can be seen, the corresponding KB integral follows the same
trend as the one obtained for a trimer (see Figure 7(b)). Thus,
with the addition of DME to the pure DOL solution, ΓPOL−DOL
around the polymer is less depleted than ΓPOL−DOL obtained
for x = 0. Inversely, with the addition of DOL to the pure
DME solution, DME is more depleted around the polymer
than in the pure DME solvent. Finally, for any fixed molar
fraction in [0.25, 0.75], ΓPOL‑DOL > ΓPOL‑DME. So, DOL is less
depleted around the polymer than DME.

As discussed in Figure 5(b), the Flory exponent is
nonmonotonic with molar fraction, and such a nonmonotonic
behavior is known as “cosolvency”. Mechanisms such as
preferential solvation, competitive hydrogen bonding, and
geometric frustration have been proposed to explain
phenomena like “cosolvency” and “co-non-solvency”.67,68 In
this work, the recurring collapse of the polymer with increasing
DOL concentration (toward x = 0) may be due to a decrease
in the effective solvation of the polymer by the DOL solvent.
This reduction of polymer solvation may be caused by the
increased preferential mutual coordination of DOL molecules
by each other in the bulk region in the absence of DME.
According to preferential solvation theory,63 a combination of
interactions involving the polymer−solvent and solvent−
solvent (here given by DOL−DOL, DME−DME, and
DOL−DME) drives the final adsorption and the polymer
state. We have seen before that DOL has a general preference
to the polymer than DME, probably because of hydrogen
bonding and less entropic penalties for the stiff DOL to bind
(Figure S10). Furthermore, since DOL−DME coordination is
less preferred than DOL−DOL or DME−DME coordination
(as reported in Figure S12, ΓDOL−DOL > 0 > ΓDME−DME >
ΓDOL−DME), then DOL moves in excess to the polymer. This
effect must be maximized for a certain composition (which we
find to be about x = 0.25) because for x = 0 and 1 only DOL or
DME is present in the bulk, respectively. Hence, the change of
solvent compositions, for which DOL−DOL, DME−DME,
and DOL−DME coordination are all different, could lead to a
decrease in the effective solvation of the polymer by the solvent
for x = 0, causing collapse again.

4. CONCLUSION
In this study, we used MD simulations to investigate the
structural properties of PTBT oligomers and polymers in DME
and DOL mixtures of various compositions for application in
lithium−sulfur batteries. We considered nine different chain
lengths and five different molar fractions, varying from pure
DOL (x = 0) to pure DME (x = 1). To accomplish this task,
we have developed the force field for the missing dihedrals of
conjugated organosulfur polymer, S/PTBT, using a combina-
tion of density functional theory and MD simulation for three
dihedrals. We utilized the RESP method to calculate the
charges for the PTBT and S/PTBT monomers. Finally, using
the combination of OPLS methods and newly developed
parameters, we were able to construct the full force field for
PTBT and S/PTBT polymers. We further validated the force
field of PTBT and S/PTBT systems by recovering structural
properties of DiTBT−Sm−DiTBT for m = 1−18.
We reported the bond length, persistence length, and
contour length of PTBT. Assuming PTBT to be a semiflexible
polymer, we reported the Kuhn length and characteristic ratio
of the PTBT polymer. To estimate the solvent quality, we
calculated the Flory exponent by scaling the radius of gyration
with polymer size. Our result shows a compact chain condition
in the cases of x = 0 and x = 1, whereas, in the case of x = 0.25,
the polymer is in a moderately expanded state. Intriguingly,

Our study of the monomer−solvent system in the mixture of
DOL and DME has revealed that DOL is less depleted than
DME. This was done by analyzing the RDFs and calculating
the KB integral between the monomer and solvent molecules.
We also found that the coordination number of DOL around a
single monomer is higher than that of DME, regardless of the
solvent molar fraction. Furthermore, to further understand the
polymer−solvent behavior, we calculated solvent distributions
between solvents and the whole polymer. We observed
consistently higher DOL accumulation as in the monomer−
solvent case. Finally, we calculated the averaged partial molar
volume of a TBT monomer over different chain lengths, which
we find tunable (while not a large effect) by solvent
composition. These findings provide important insights into
the adsorption thermodynamics of DOL and DME with the
polymer.
Since the polysulfide solubility is not relevant for the
optimization of the solvent-electrolyte when considering an
organosulfur-based cathode, one needs to consider the effect of
the solvent on the structural property of the polymer primarily.
In conclusion, our study of the monomer−solvent and
polymer−solvent systems in the mixture of DME and DOL
revealed important insight into how the solvent composition
can tune polymer structural properties. Hence, our work
provides important insights into the adsorption and
interactions of DME and DOL with the conjugated organo-
sulfur polymer. With the constructed force fields, it also serves
as a first methodical step for the development and study of
further MD simulations of a more realistic polymeric network,
structures, and processes, such as vulcanized (S-linked)
polymers and whole network topologies. In future work, we
plan to investigate the impact of adding salts to the solvated
polymeric systems to gain a deeper understanding of their
behavior as electrolytes for Li−S batteries and design more
efficient electrolytes. In terms of structural flexibility and
polymer solvation structure, the presence of Li ions and
counteranions might induce important changes, such as the
occurrence of cation-pi structures,70 “sandwich” structures,70
and related conformational constraints.
