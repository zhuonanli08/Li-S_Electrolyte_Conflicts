Abstract
To meet the development demands of high-energy-density, long-cycle-life lithium-ion batteries, this study focuses on the 
development of high-performance electrolyte additives, and has successfully synthesized and systematically characterized 
a novel lithium salt additive lithium tetrafluorophosphate (LiPOF4). By optimizing the reaction process between silicon 
tetrafluoride (SiF4) and phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5), the critical intermediate phosphorus trifluoride (POF3) was suc­
cessfully prepared using the gas-phase contact method, which was then reacted with lithium fluoride (LiF) to synthesize 
LiPOF4. SEM and TEM results showed that the obtained LiPOF4 was predominantly amorphous in nature. Thermogravi­
metric analysis confirmed that the material exhibits excellent thermal stability, with a significant decomposition tempera­
ture of 310 °C. Electrochemical testing revealed that adding 10 wt% LiPOF4 to the electrolyte significantly enhances the 
performance of lithium-sulfur batteries: after 100 cycles at a 0.5 C rate, the coulombic efficiency was 83.7% (compared 
to 77.1% for the control group), the charge specific capacity was 594.7 mAh/g (control group: 534.7 mAh/g), and the dis­
charge specific capacity was 755.1 mAh/g (control group: 697.4 mAh/g). Additionally, the addition of LiPOF4 effectively 
extended the charge-discharge plateau of the lithium-sulfur battery. This work provides new materials and new ideas for 
improving electrolyte performance.

electrolytes, its thermal stability remains inadequate, caus­
ing it to decompose into lithium fluoride (LiF) and phospho­

through theoretical research that lithium tetrafluorophos­
phate (LiPOF4) not only has high electrochemical stabil­
ity and good thermal stability, but also exhibits excellent 
compatibility with electrode materials. It can significantly 
improve the cycle life, rate performance, and safety charac­

enhancing the stability of the electrode/electrolyte interface, 
and promoting the uniform deposition of lithium ions. It is 
considered to be a highly promising alternative electrolyte 
salt to LiPF6 at present. However, despite the enormous 
potential of LiPOF4 as a new type of lithium salt additive 
in optimizing lithium-ion battery performance, there is cur­
rently a lack of systematic experimental research.
Currently, there are extensive reports on the synthesis of 
LiPF6. The conventional synthesis method is to react phospho­
rus pentachloride (PCl5) and hydrofluoric acid (HF) to produce 
PF5, which is then reacted with LiF to produce LiPF6 [34, 35]. 
To address the safety concerns of traditional processes, Yu et 
al. [36, 37] improved the method by replacing the highly toxic 
HF with calcium fluoride (CaF2) as the fluorine source, which 
reacts with PCl5 to form the PF5 intermediate. They further 
replaced PCl5 with phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) to construct 
a P2O5-CaF2 solid-phase reaction system (as shown in reac­
tion Eq. 1). However, Tarbutton et al. [38] pointed out in their 
previous study that the reaction between CaF2 and P2O5 at tem­
peratures ranging from 500 to 1000℃ produced phosphorus 
trifluoride (POF3) (as shown in reaction Eq. 2), and they did not 
detect PF5 in the products. The direct synthesis of LiPOF4 can 
be achieved by reacting POF3 with LiF (as shown in Reaction 
Eq. 3), but possibly due to the toxicity and hazardous nature of 
POF3, experimental reports on the direct synthesis of LiPOF4 
are currently notably scarce. Due to the uncertainty in product 
selectivity and discrepancies in literature results from the afore­
mentioned methods, and to obtain reliable POF3 raw material 
for LiPOF4 synthesis, this paper draws on the synthesis route 
for LiPF6, utilizing the reaction between SiF4 and P2O5 as the 
source of POF3 (as shown in Reaction Eq. 4). Subsequently, the 
POF3 prepared by this method is reacted with LiF to ultimately 
synthesize the target product LiPOF4.

Silicon tetrafluoride (SiF4) is a by-product of the wet phos­
phoric acid process [39, 40] and has the advantages of low 
cost and high accessibility. At the same time, it has a stable 
structure, and under normal conditions, SiF4 is unlikely 
to undergo reactions in which fluorine atoms elements are 
replaced by other elements. However, previous literature 
has reported that SiF4 can react with magnesium chloride 
and calcium chloride [41], phosphorus halides [42], alkali 
metal fluorides [43], and silicon halides [44] under elevated-
temperature conditions. Interestingly, although SiF4 is stable 
overall, its Si-F bond readily hydrolyzes with water at room 
temperature [45]. Therefore, when conducting research 
using SiF4, it is essential to strictly isolate it from moisture 
in the air. As early as 1983, Suresh et al. [46] explored the 
properties of Silicon tetrahalides, SiX4 (X = F, Cl, Br). Their 
research indicated that SiX4 (X = F, Cl, Br) and the fluoro­
silicates of sodium and potassium react with phosphorus 
pentoxide above 300 ℃. The tetrahalides give rise to the 
corresponding phosphoryl halides and silica, while the fluo­
rosilicates form the corresponding metal fluorophosphates 
and SiF4. In the reactions between SiF4, silicon tetrachloride 
(SiCl4), and silicon tetrabromide (SiBr4) with P2O5, SiBr4 
undergoes excessive decomposition, causing bromine to 
escape from the reaction system along with phosphorous 
bromide and silicon dioxide (SiO2); The reactions of SiF4 
and SiCl4 are relatively controllable, requiring only the 
introduction of fresh P2O5 samples and reheating the mix­
ture to the desired temperature to complete the reaction; 
It is worth noting that the reaction between SiF4 and P2O5 
requires a lower temperature and is more efficient, mak­
ing it more suitable for this reaction system. Additionally, 
the main products of the reaction between SiF4 and P2O5 
are POF3 and SiO2, without the formation of other volatile 
byproducts such as PF5.
This paper aims to explore the process and mechanism 
for experimental synthesis of LiPOF4. Specifically, SiF4 is 
used as a fluorinating agent to react with P2O5, generating 
POF3. The resulting POF3 is absorbed in an organic sol­
vent and subsequently reacts with LiF at low temperature 
to synthesize LiPOF4. By optimizing the reactor design, 
the problem of reduced reaction efficiency caused by SiO2 
encapsulating P2O5, as discovered in the research by Suresh 
et al. [46] can be alleviated, thereby providing new ideas for 
the efficient preparation of LiPOF4. Furthermore, this paper 
conducted a preliminary assessment of the electrochemical 
performance of the successfully synthesized LiPOF4. Fur­
thermore, this paper conducted a preliminary evaluation of 
the electrochemical performance of the successfully syn­
thesized LiPOF4. The results demonstrated that, compared 
to the blank group, LiPOF4 as an electrolyte additive can 
improve the cycle stability and capacity retention rate of 
the battery, comprehensively enhancing its electrochemical

Experimental details
Materials
All reagents used in this experiment were purchased directly 
from commercial suppliers. Specifically, these include: 
SiF4 (99.99% purity, stored in high-pressure cylinders), 
P2O5 (98.5% purity), diethyl ether (C2H5OC2H5, 99.5% 
purity), LiF (99.9% purity, vacuum-dried at 110 ℃), tolu­
ene (C6H5CH3, 99.5% purity), and high-purity nitrogen gas 
(N2, 99.999% purity, used to establish an inert reaction sys­
tem and as a carrier gas). Deionized water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 
MΩ·cm) was used throughout the experiment.
Synthesis of POF3
The experiment first involved the synthesis of POF3, using 
the experimental apparatus shown in Fig. 1. The specific 
experimental steps are as follows:
First, weigh out an appropriate amount of P2O5 and set up 
the reaction furnace heating program (heating from 25 ℃ to 
300–550 ℃ at a rate of 9 ℃/min, maintaining the reaction 
temperature for 2 h, then cooling at a rate of 5 ℃/min to 50 
℃ before stopping). Start the reaction furnace while intro­
ducing high-purity N2 to purge air and moisture from the 
reaction system. This step is critical to prevent silicon tet­
rafluoride (SiF4) from reacting with moisture in the system 
and to avoid degradation of P2O5 due to moisture absorp­
tion. Once the reactor reaches the predetermined reaction 
temperature, adjust the N2 flow rate to an appropriate level 
(15–30 ml/min) and open the SiF4 inlet valve.
Secondly, under the transportation of the N2 carrier gas, 
the sublimated P2O5 is transported to the reaction zone to 
undergo a gas-phase reaction with SiF4 (see Eq. 4), which 
lasts for 2 h. The gas-phase products (POF3) formed during 
the reaction are conveyed out of the reaction zone by the 
carrier gas, while the solid-phase products are deposited on 
the inner wall of the reaction tube.
Finally, the gaseous products and unreacted SiF4 are car­
ried by N2 through a bubbling process into an ether absorp­
tion system for absorption, where N2 and SiF4 are almost 
insoluble in ether. Unabsorbed SiF4 enters the tail gas treat­
ment system, where it is treated with an alkaline solution 
before being discharged. The entire ether absorption process 
is maintained at 0 ℃ to ensure absorption efficiency, and the 
temperature is strictly controlled using a low-temperature 
constant temperature bath.
LiPOF4 synthesis, battery assembly, and 
electrochemical performance testing
The synthesis steps for LiPOF4 are shown in Fig. 2. First, 
precisely weigh the LiF dried at 110 ℃ and slowly intro­
duce it to the ether solution containing POF3. Then, place 
the test tube in a low-temperature constant-temperature 
bath and lower the temperature from 0 ℃ to −6 ℃ at a rate 
of 1 ℃/min to allow POF3 to slowly crystallize out from 
the ether. Maintain magnetic stirring throughout the reac­
tion process, and continue the reaction for 30 min after the 
temperature reaches − 6 ℃. After the reaction is complete, 
vacuum filtration is performed using a Büchner funnel to 
collect the unreacted LiF filter residue for recycling. Next, 
toluene is slowly added to the filtrate at a volume ratio of 
1:4, and the mixture is allowed to stand for 48 h to allow 
the crystals to precipitate completely. Under argon protec­
tion, filtration is performed using a sand core funnel, and

performed. Performance was tested through 100 cycles at

As shown in Fig. 12, within the discharge plateau 
range of 300–640 mAh/g specific capacity, batteries with 
added LiPOF4 exhibit a lower discharge plateau than those 
without. This phenomenon appears to contradict the con­
ventional understanding that “a lower discharge plateau 
typically indicates reduced capacity.” In reality, batteries 
with added LiPOF4 actually demonstrate higher capacity. 
The core reason lies in LiPOF4’s tendency to decompose 
preferentially on both electrode surfaces. This decomposi­
tion forms dense SEI (Solid Electrolyte Interphase) and CEI 
(Cathode Electrolyte Interface) films rich in LiF and Li3PO4. 
These films regulate Li+ transfer through two pathways: 
“inhibiting electrolyte-electrode side reactions” and “con­
structing inorganic Li+ transport channels.” Although these 
interface films increase charge transfer resistance, leading 
to enhanced polarization effects and reduced plateau volt­
age during discharge, they effectively suppress continuous 
side reactions between the electrolyte and electrodes. This 
reduces irreversible loss of active lithium and powdering/
shedding of electrode materials, thereby improving active 
material utilization and battery cycling stability [6, 58, 59]. 
This “trade-off effect of sacrificing a small voltage drop for 
capacity retention” ultimately enables batteries with added 
LiPOF4 to exhibit higher actual discharge capacity than 
systems without LiPOF4. This phenomenon fundamentally 
stems from the interface protection mechanism achieving 
decoupled regulation of capacity and voltage, rather than 
the conventional understanding that “a lowered plateau 
voltage equates to capacity decay” [60, 61]. If LiPOF4 can 
form an interfacial film with a “dense structure + low dif­
fusion energy barrier inorganic component,” it can fully 
meet the performance requirement of “thickening without 
blocking lithium.” Therefore, the experimental results indi­
cate that using LiPOF4 as an electrolyte additive in lithium-
sulfur batteries can effectively extend the charge-discharge 
plateau, enhance the stability and specific capacity of elec­
trochemical reactions, and thereby improve the battery’s 
cycle performance.

Conclusions
This paper reports the optimized synthesis of a novel 
LiPOF4 electrolyte additive and its performance in lithium-
sulfur batteries. By implementing a gas-gas phase reaction 
between sublimated P2O5 and SiF4 during the key step of 
POF3 generation, the paper effectively mitigates the criti­
cal problem of reduced reaction efficiency arising from the 
formation of SiO2 encapsulating P2O5 in traditional gas-
solid reactions. Subsequently, organic solvents were used 
to absorb gaseous POF3 and react with LiF, and LiPOF4 
was successfully synthesized through toluene precipita­
tion. SEM and TEM structural characterization showed 
that the obtained LiPOF4 was mainly amorphous and pre­
sented a unique irregular porous hierarchical morphology.
