ABSTRACT: Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to investigate the
structure
and
dynamics
of
[LiTFSA]:[FEC]:[HFE]
(TFSA,
bis-
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)amide; FEC, fluoroethylene carbonate; HFE, 1,1,2,2-

electrolyte for lithium−sulfur batteries; however, it exhibits high performance
(ratio of 1:6:3) or low performance (ratio of 1:4:5) depending on the ratio of the
constituents. Simulations revealed that both electrolyte systems spontaneously
liquid−liquid phase-separate into two distinct domains, namely, a large LiTFSA-
rich microphase and an HFE-rich phase; however, the microphase properties
significantly vary with the constituent composition. For the high-performance
electrolyte, the flexible microphase facilitates the formation of small Li-ion-containing clusters in the HFE-rich phase, promoting
enhanced Li-ion diffusion. Furthermore, the Li ions diffuse via different mechanisms on the 0.001, 0.1, 10, and 100 ns time scales. In
contrast, the low-performance electrolyte exhibited a more rigid microphase with fewer small clusters, restricting Li-ion transport
mainly to the microphase. These variations alter the pathways for ionic conduction, implying that even when identical chemical
species are used, changes in the constituent composition can result in minor modifications in the physical properties of the
microphase, significantly altering the performance. This insight underscores a key consideration for electrolyte design, not only for
lithium−sulfur batteries but also for other battery systems.
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are lightweight, next-gener-
ation batteries with a high theoretical capacity, leading to their
rapid development. Similar to other batteries, the electrolyte
plays a key role in the overall performance by facilitating ion
transfer between the electrodes during charging and discharg-
ing, and by enabling long-term operation through the
formation of a solid electrolyte interphase.1−3 It is generally
beneficial to lower the overall viscosity of the electrolyte by
incorporating diluents, such as 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-
tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) or water,4,5 but performance
diminishes if the diluent concentration deviates from an
optimal range. These observations imply that the ion
conduction mechanism varies with concentration, although
the precise details remain to be elucidated.
Insights into the dynamic properties of Li ions and the
solvation structure surrounding them provide deep knowledge
for the development of novel electrolytes.6−24 Therefore,
numerous studies have employed molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations to investigate the behavior of Li ions at the
microscale.6−12,15,18−20,24 Li-ion transport in electrolyte
solutions has traditionally been explained by two primary
mechanisms: the vehicular mechanism, in which Li ions
transport with their solvation shells, and the solvent exchange
mechanism, in which Li ions are transferred via the exchange
of solvent molecules between the solvation shell and the bulk
solvent. Moreover, recent investigations have shown that the
addition of organic solvents, such as ethylene carbonate and
acetonitrile, shortens the lifetime of the Li-ion solvation shell,
thereby increasing the Li-ion transfer frequency and enhancing
Li-ion transport efficiency.18,19 However, although these
mechanisms capture certain aspects of Li-ion diffusion, they
are limited to short time scales, and the relationship between
these short-term dynamics and macroscopic Li-ion conduction
remains unclear.
Studies have recently started focusing on the long-term
dynamics to capture larger-scale heterogeneities. In 2017,
Borodin et al.9 were the first to differentiate the dynamics
within one phase from those in other regions by analyzing the
coordination states of the Li ions. They quantitatively
demonstrated that in concentrated electrolytes, Li ions exhibit
a fast diffusion mode in the water-rich phase and slow diffusion
in the TFSA phase. Nonetheless, details of the phase-
separation structure’s dynamic properties, such as how the
structure fluctuates, were not addressed. Furthermore, because
Li ions shuttle between the water-rich and TFSA phases,

Numerous low-concentration electrolytes (CLi ≈1 M) have
been developed,25 employed in various battery systems, and
commercialized. In recent years, high-concentration electro-
lytes (CLi > 3 M) have also been developed and
reported.10,26,27 Although such electrolytes exhibit increased
viscosity owing to their high salt contentresulting in slower
lithium-ion diffusion and lower ionic conductivityselecting
appropriate solvents, such as sulfolane, can enhance the
lithium-ion transference number. This allows high-concen-
tration electrolytes to deliver current densities comparable to
those of low-concentration systems. Nonetheless, the high
viscosity poses challenges for battery manufacturing. To
overcome this, localized high-concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs) have been considered,28−31 in which a high-
concentration electrolyte is diluted with an inert diluent.
Torii et al. developed a high-performance LHCE for Li−S
batteries by using FEC as the solvent and HFE as the diluent at
an appropriate mixing ratio.2 Despite these advances, the
molecular-level structure and the associated ion dynamics in
LHCEs remain poorly understood.
In this study, long-term all-atomistic MD simulations (∼500
ns) were performed for [LiTFSA]:[FEC]:[HFE] = 1:6:3
(TFSA, bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)amide; FEC, fluoroethy-
lene carbonate; HFE, 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluor-
opropyl ether), which has been identified as a high-perform-
ance LHCE for Li−S batteries.2 We also carried out
comparative simulations on the 1:4:5 formulation, which
exhibits poorer performance in Li−S cells, despite its lower
viscosity relative to the optimal 1:6:3 mixture. Voronoi
polyhedral analysis was conducted to investigate the electrolyte
structure, enabling a clear determination of whether the Li ions
were incorporated. Consequently, the flexibility of the
microphase was found to vary significantly depending on the
composition of the electrolyte constituents. Differences in
composition were found to affect the Li-ion diffusion pathways,
which greatly influence the overall diffusion rate. Meanwhile,
Li ions diffuse via different mechanisms on various time scales,
on the order of 0.001, 0.1, 10, and over 100 ns.
■METHODS
MD Calculations
In this study, long-term all-atomistic MD simulations were performed
for both the 1:6:3 and 1:4:5 [LiTFSA]:[FEC]:[HFE] systems.
Hereafter, the 1:6:3 and 1:4:5 systems are referred to as Ele163 and
Ele145, respectively. For the Ele163 system (best performance for Li−
S batteries2), the MD cell comprised 28,800 FEC molecules, 14,400
HFE molecules, 4800 TFSA molecules, and 4800 Li ions. For the
Ele145 system (poor performance for Li−S batteries), the MD cell
contained 24,000 FEC molecules, 19,200 HFE molecules, 4800 TFSA
molecules, and 4800 Li ions. These molecules (see Figure 1) were
randomly packed into an MD simulation box using PACKMOL.32 All
MD calculations were performed using GROMACS 2021.33−39 The
time step was set to 1 fs, and the OPLS-AA force field,40,41 which is
well-established for organic molecules, was used for FEC, HFE, and
Li, while the OPLS-2009IL42,43 force field was employed for TFSA
(see the Supporting Information for details). However, because the
charges of the FEC and HFE molecules were not tuned, we
recalculated them to achieve better agreement with the dynamical
properties. The atomic charges for the FEC and HFE molecules were
obtained from electrostatic potential calculations performed using
Gaussian 16 at the MP2/cc-pVTZ level of theory, employing the
Merz−Kollman scheme (pop = MK). The long-range Coulomb
interactions were calculated using the smooth particle mesh Ewald
(SPME) method,44 with a grid spacing of 0.1 nm and an Ewald
tolerance set to 10−5. The cutoff distances for both the Lennard−
Jones (LJ) and short-range SPME interactions were set to 1.2 nm, and
long-range corrections for the LJ potential were applied to both the
energy and pressure. The temperature was maintained at 298.15 K
using the velocity rescaling method45 with a relaxation time of 1 ps,
and the pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Parrinello−Rahman
method46 with a relaxation time of 2 ps. The chemical bonds
involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS
algorithm.47 All MD simulations were performed for 500 ns, and
the trajectories after 300 ns were used for analysis.
Definition of the Li−TFSA Clusters Using Voronoi
Polyhedral Analysis
Li and TFSA are known to aggregate and form clusters.9,13,48,49 In the
present study, such clusters were identified using Voronoi polyhedral
analysis.50 A Voronoi region around a given atom is defined as the set
of all points in space that are closer to that atom than to any other. In
practice, the perpendicular bisector plane is constructed for each pair
of atoms. For a target atom, each bisector divides space into two half-
spaces: one containing the atom itself and the other containing its
neighbor (green line in Figure 2a). The Voronoi region is then
obtained by intersecting all the half-spaces that include the target
atom. The resulting cell is a convex polyhedron whose faces lie on the
bisector planes (yellow area in Figure 2a). This method provides
geometric information such as the volume of the Voronoi region, the
number of contacting atoms, and the total contact area. It therefore
enables direct identification of which molecules are in contact, as well
as the number and species of contacting atoms.
In this study, we first calculated the occupied regions of Li and
TFSA molecules using Voronoi polyhedral analysis. Li−Li, Li−TFSA,
and TFSA−TFSA contacts were considered part of the same cluster.
Furthermore, because FEC molecules can coordinate with Li ions,
they may be incorporated into Li−TFSA clusters or reside
predominantly on their surfaces. If an atom of an FEC molecule is
located at the surface of a Li−TFSA cluster, it is considered part of
the cluster if the surface area in contact with the cluster exceeds half of
its total Voronoi surface area (Figure 2b). The volume of a cluster is
then defined as the sum of the Voronoi volumes of the Li, TFSA, and
the included FEC atoms. Voronoi polyhedral analysis was performed
using the Qhull program.51

The time evolution of the microphase volume is also shown
in Figure 4a,b. In the initial structures, Li ions, TFSA, FEC,
and HFE molecules were randomly placed in the MD cell
boxes. However, the volume of Ele163 gradually increased and
significantly fluctuated until 300 ns, after which it stabilized,
indicating that the system had reached equilibrium. In contrast,
the volume of Ele145 stabilized after 100 ns, suggesting that it
reached equilibrium much earlier than Ele163. Therefore, all
subsequent analyses were performed using data from 300 to
500 ns. For the Ele163 and Ele145 systems, the microphase
volumes averaged 889 nm3 (∼11% of the MD cell volume) and
1163 nm3 (∼12% of the MD cell volume), respectively.
Approximately 90% of the remaining region is occupied by
FEC and HFE, and hence we refer to this phase as the HFE-
rich phase to distinguish it from the microphase, where FEC is
also present. Within this phase, small clusters composed of Li
ions, TFSA, and FEC are observed in both the Ele163 and
Ele145 systems (Figure 4a,b and Supporting Movies). Figure
4c shows the size distributions of the small clusters with
volumes below 1 nm3 for the two systems and the log−log plot
over the entire range, revealing the existence of clusters
spanning a wide size range, 0.02−100 nm3. A greater number
of these clusters was observed in Ele163 than in Ele145. Their
higher abundance may contribute to the superior performance
of Ele163, by enhancing Li+ solvation in the HFE-rich phase.
We quantified the volume fluctuations for both the MD cells
and the microphase using the following equation: (⟨V2⟩−
⟨V⟩2)/⟨V⟩2, where V is the volume of the microphase or the
MD cell. For the MD cell, the volume fluctuations are
approximately 3.4 × 10−5 for Ele163 and 4.5 × 10−5 for
Ele145, and for the microphase, the fluctuations are 14 × 10−5
for Ele163 and 2.0 × 10−5 for Ele145. Notably, the volume
fluctuation of the Ele163 microphase is approximately 4 times
larger than that of the MD cell. These observations suggest
that small clusters dynamically detach from and reattach to the
microphase at its junctions. Conversely, the microphase
volume fluctuation in the Ele145 is smaller than the MD cell
volume fluctuation, indicating that the detachment of cluster
members has little effect in this case, owing to fewer FEC
molecules. Thus, although a Li−TFSA microphase forms in
both Ele163 and Ele145, their dynamic characteristics
markedly differ. The enhanced detachment/reattachment of
clusters in the Ele163 microphase likely contributes to its
superior performance over Ele145. Therefore, electrolyte
design must account not only for the static, phase-separated
morphology but also for the dynamic fluctuations of these
microphases.

into Li-Ion Solvation and Phase Separation

coordination numbers, N(r), between Li ions and the atoms in
the TFSA, FEC, and HFE molecules for Ele163 and Ele145.

preferentially surround the Li ions, which is attributed to their

difference arises from the concentration of the electrolyte

number of FEC molecules is 6 times greater than the number
of TFSA molecules. Conversely, N(r) is smaller for FEC than

However, experimental results show that the diffusion
coefficients of Ele145 are nearly identical to those of Ele163,
suggesting that factors other than viscosity are suppressing
diffusion in Ele145. In our MD simulations, which employ a
point-charge model for Coulomb interactions, ion−ion
interactions tend to be overestimated. This leads to artificially
reduced diffusion coefficients and increases the discrepancy
with experimental values. Nonetheless, both the experimental
and simulation results consistently indicate that diffusion is
more hindered in Ele145 than in Ele163. These findings
suggest that, despite its limitations, the present simulation
model is suitable for capturing the qualitative trends in ion
dynamics across these systems. Although polarizable models
generally yield diffusion coefficients in better agreement with
experimental data, their significantly higher computational cost
limits their applicability. Currently, they can only handle
approximately one-thirtieth of the system size and much
shorter time scales than typical point-charge MD simulations.
Both spatially resolved and long-time scale analyses, such as
those conducted in this study, are impractical with polarizable
models. Therefore, although the point-charge model may lack
quantitative accuracy, it remains advantageous for exploring
spatially and temporally extended ion dynamics in complex
systems.
In contrast to the long-time diffusive behavior discussed
above, Figure 7b reveals that the MSD exhibits a different
trend at shorter time scales (t < 100 ns), suggesting the
presence of a subdiffusive mode at t < 100 ns.

exponential decay; thus, we hypothesized the presence of two

1.8 and 287 ps for the Ele163 system, and 1.8 and 602 ps for
the Ele145 system. The short relaxation time corresponds to a
displacement of ∼0.1 nm, whereas the longer relaxation time
corresponds to a displacement of ∼0.3 nm. For each system,
the shorter time constants correspond to an oscillatory mode
in which the Li ions transiently attach to the neighboring
oxygen atoms of TFSA molecules, whereas the longer

mode). Notably, the hopping time is longer in the Ele145
system than in the Ele163 system, which is consistent with the
log plot of the MSD shown in Figure 7b, showing a longer
duration for the initial slope in the Ele145 system.

Differences in the Li-Ion Conduction Pathways due to
Differences in the Microphase Properties
Because Li ions repeatedly attach to and detach from the
microphasesthereby mixing the attach−detach contributions
in the overall diffusion coefficientwe separated these events
and calculated the MSDs of the Li ions in the microphase and
HFE-rich phase, as shown in Figure 10 (see the Supporting
Movie 3, which shows an MD simulation of the Li-ion
diffusion behavior). For the Ele163 system, there is a
significant difference between the MSDs in the microphase
and HFE-rich phase. In the HFE-rich phase of the Ele163
system, as much as 26% of the Li ions are found in a spatially
dispersed state. Consequently, the presence of these Li ions
increases the overall MSD. As shown in Figure 6, in the HFE-
rich phase, the Li ions diffuse in an FEC-coordinated state (i.e.,
with a high positive charge density). These results indicate that
the electrolyte achieves highly efficient charge transfer by using
the HFE-rich phase as an electrical conduction pathway.
In contrast, the region-specific MSD in the Ele145 system is
similar to that in the Ele163 system, indicating that the
diffusion mechanisms and time constants within each phase
remain largely unchanged regardless of composition. However,
for the Ele145 system, the overall MSD is consistent with the
MSD measured within the microphase. This suggests that ion
transport in the Ele145 system is predominantly due to
diffusion within the microphase, which is reasonable because
the microphase is more rigid and small clusters are less
prevalent in the HFE-rich phase.
Our results reveal that although a microphase is invariably
formed regardless of composition, its intrinsic properties
depend on composition. This shift in properties reshapes the
ionic conduction pathways, suggesting that even when using
the same chemical species, subtle changes in physical
properties of the microphasefrom the viscosity to the
connectivity of the conduction pathwayslead to marked
differences in the performance of the electrolyte. Overall, our
findings show that the superior performance of an electrolyte
arises from a subtle balance between its microstructural
characteristics and the ion mobility, which together enable
efficient charge transport.
■CONCLUSIONS
MD simulations were performed for Ele163 and Ele145 to
investigate the structure and dynamics of the electrolyte system
in Li−S batteries. Our simulations revealed that the electrolyte
spontaneously phase-separates into two distinct domains: a
large LiTFSA-rich microphase and an HFE-rich phase, where
the properties of the microphase vary significantly with
composition. In Ele163, the flexible microphase facilitates the
formation of Li-ion-containing small clusters in the HFE-rich
phase, thereby promoting enhanced Li-ion diffusion. In
contrast, Ele145 exhibits a more rigid microphase with fewer
small clusters, forcing Li-ion transport to primarily occur
within the microphase. Radial distribution function analysis
further revealed an average phase heterogeneity of approx-
imately 7 nm.
Our investigations of the MSD, the Li−TFSA binding time
correlation function, and the Li-ion residence time correlation
function in the HFE-rich phase revealed that the Li ions diffuse
via four distinct mechanisms. First, Li ions oscillate by
transiently attaching to and detaching from neighboring
TFSA molecules, with a time constant of roughly 2 ps.
Second, Li ions hop to different TFSA molecules, with a time
constant of approximately 300 ps for Ele163 and 600 ps for
Ele145. Third, Li ions remain in the microphase or HFE-rich
phase for approximately 20 ns. Finally, beyond 100 ns, the
slope of the MSD reaches 1, indicating that the Li ions transit

LiTFSA:FEC:HFE-based electrolytes for Li−S batteries, we

comparative analysis. These results demonstrate that different
ionic conduction pathways can emerge within the same
electrolyte depending on its composition, highlighting an
important factor to consider in the optimization of electrolyte
composition, not only for Li−S batteries but for various battery
systems.
