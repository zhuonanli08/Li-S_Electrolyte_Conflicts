Abstract
Progress in sulfur cathodes and separator interlayers has substantially curtailed polysulfide shut-
tling and sluggish kinetics in Li–S batteries. However, the instability of lithium metal anodes
remains a major barrier to commercialization. Replacing lithium with mature lithiated graphite is
attractive, but ether-based electrolytes used in Li–S cells induce Li+/solvent co-intercalation and
rapid failure of graphite electrodes. Building optimized interlayers and sulfur cathodes, this study
focuses on making graphite fully compatible with ether electrolytes (DOL/DME). By systemati-
cally adjusting the lithium-salt concentration and LiNO3 additive, we found a finely tuned com-
positional regime in which the graphite electrode is free from solvent co-intercalation without
markedly impairing cathode kinetics. This enabled the integration of an H–TiO2/S cathode, a thin
freestanding PVDF/MS interlayer, and a lithiated graphite anode into a full cell, which delivered
1066 mAh·gsulfur−1 at 0.1 C (N/P = 0.87). The resulting prototype demonstrates that Li–S chem-
istry can operate without a lithium-metal anode, offering a practical route toward safer and more
commercially viable Li–S batteries.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have attracted intense attention because they use earth-abundant sul-
fur and lithium metal, which enables exceptionally high gravimetric energy density [1–3]. Coupled
with the growing demand for lightweight, long-range power sources in sectors such as electric vehi-
cles, drones, and portable electronics, Li–S batteries are often regarded as one of the most promising
candidates for next-generation rechargeable systems. In addition, the low cost, ease of material recy-
cling, and environmental benignity enhance the appeal of Li–S chemistry. However, the Li-metal anode
remains a persistent major obstacle. Its high reactivity, dendritic growth, and large volume change
severely compromise safety and long-term stability, making it one of the primary barriers to practical
commercialization [4, 5].
Introducing graphite into the Li–S system to replace the Li-metal anode offers a practical route to
eliminate the intrinsic safety risks associated with lithium metal, potentially serving as a transitional
architecture for Li–S battery commercialization. The lithium source can be positioned on either elec-
trode, but a lithiated graphite anode is generally preferred due to its substantially lower cost com-
pared with Li2S and its compatibility with well-established manufacturing processes [6–8]. Although

remains competitive with conventional Li-ion cells (∼300–500 Wh·kg−1), providing a commercially
viable pathway toward safer Li–S systems while preserving a significant portion of their theoretical
advantage [9].
However, integrating graphite anodes into Li–S chemistry faces intrinsic compatibility challenges
with conventional electrolytes. The carbonate-free ether systems typically employed in Li–S batter-
ies, most commonly mixtures of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), tend to
co-intercalate solvent molecules along with Li+ into graphitic layers, leading to the formation of
ternary graphite-intercalation compounds (t-GICs) and subsequent electrode exfoliation and failure
[10, 11]. Efforts to suppress co-intercalation have primarily focused on replacing the DOL/DME sys-
tem with carbonate-based solvents [12] or other substitutes, including hexyl methyl ether (HME) [13],
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) [14], 1,2-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)ethane
(TFEE) [15] based electrolytes, solvated ionic liquid electrolyte [16], and quasi-solid-state electrolyte
[17], or by adopting high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) [18, 19]. However, these approaches
either result in sluggish sulfur conversion kinetics or suffer from high cost and limited scalabil-
ity for practical application. Developing an approach that retains the DOL/DME electrolyte while
ensuring stable operation of both sulfur and graphite electrodes is therefore of considerable practical
significance.
Moreover, recent progress in host design and interlayer engineering has significantly mitigated shuttle
effects [20, 21]. While early studies mainly employed carbon hosts, subsequent developments introduced
polar metal compounds capable of adsorbing polysulfide intermediates and catalyzing their conversion,
thereby accelerating reaction kinetics [22]. Among these materials, metal oxides have drawn particular
interest for their chemical stability, low cost, and inherently polar surfaces [23]. While pristine metal
oxides often exhibit low electrical conductivity and modest catalytic activity, their reduced counterparts
can deliver markedly enhanced performance by providing more active sites and improved charge transfer
capability [23, 24]. In parallel, ultrathin self-supporting interlayers placed between the cathode and sep-
arator have proven effective in regulating ion flux, suppressing polysulfide diffusion and providing addi-
tional catalytic sites [25, 26]. The development of thin, mechanically robust interlayers maximizes these
benefits while minimizing additional mass and transport resistance, making them increasingly attractive
for high-performance Li–S batteries.
In this work, we integrate these advances by employing a reduced hollow titanium oxide/sulfur
composite cathode (H–TiO2/S) with strong polysulfide adsorption capability and high catalytic activ-
ity, together with an electrospun, self-supporting thin interlayer composed of SnO2-loaded MXene
and PVDF (MS-PVDF) to simultaneously trap polysulfides, accelerating their conversion and smooth
Li+ flux. On the anode side, we reveal that the concentration of lithium salt and LiNO3 additive in
the electrolyte critically affects the stability of the graphite electrode. By tuning the contents of lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and LiNO3 while retaining the DOL/DME solvent system,
solvent co-intercalation is completely suppressed without significantly impairing cathode reaction kinet-
ics. With these optimized components, we assemble a full lithiated graphite–sulfur (LiGr-S) cell, demon-
strating that Li–S chemistry can be realized without a lithium-metal anode, thereby providing a practical
pathway toward safer and more commercially viable Li–S batteries.
2. Experimental section
2.1. C/S cathode fabrication
The C/S composite (60 wt% sulfur) was obtained by mixing sulfur with Super P followed by heat treat-
ment at 155 ◦C for 12 h. To prepare the electrodes, the composite was mixed with an aqueous LA132
binder (acrylonitrile copolymer latex) at a weight ratio of 9:1 to produce a slurry, which was coated
onto Al foil, dried in air, and subsequently vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h. Circular electrodes with
the diameter of 13 mm were punched out and preserved for cell fabrication. The sulfur loading for C/S
cathode is 0.9–1.4 mg cm−2.
2.2. CM-PVDF interlayer fabrication
SnO2/MXene was synthesized as reported [27]. To prepare the electrospinning ink, SnO2/MXene parti-
cles were ultrasonically dispersed in DMF/acetone (7:3 v/v) for 30 min, then mixed with a PVDF solu-
tion in DMAC/DMF (7:3 w/w) to obtain a precursor with a polymer-to-SnO2/MXene ratio of 80:20.
Electrospinning was conducted at 13 kV, 1.2 ml h−1, 15 cm, and 40% RH. The interlayers (∼20 µm
thick, ∼0.3 mg cm−2) were dried at 60 ◦C for 3 h.

2.3. H–TiO2/S cathode fabrication
Hollow-structured TiO2 (H–TiO2/S) was synthesized by a hard-templating method, involving SiO2
sphere preparation, TiO2 shell coating, template removal, and subsequent hydrogen reduction. Briefly,
SiO2 spheres were prepared via a modified Stöber method, coated with a mesoporous TiO2 shell, and
etched with NaOH solution to yield hollow TiO2. The obtained sample was further reduced under 5%
H2/Ar at 500 ◦C for 3 h to obtain reduced H–TiO2.
In H–TiO2/S composite preparation, Elemental sulfur was introduced into the reduced H–TiO2 by
in-situ precipitation from sodium thiosulfate under acidic conditions, followed by filtration, washing,
and drying to obtain the H–TiO2/S composite with a sulfur loading of ∼47%. For electrode fabrication,
the composite was blended with 10% conductive carbon and 10% binder. The sulfur loading of the H–
TiO2/S cathode is comparable to C/S cathode.
2.4. Graphite and lithiated graphite anode fabrication
Artificial graphite powder (MTI), Super P, and an LA132 aqueous binder were mixed at a weight ratio
of 90:5:5 and coated onto Cu foil. The resulting electrodes were punched into 13 mm discs to serve as
graphite anodes. To prepare lithiated graphite anodes, these electrodes were assembled into half-cells
with Li metal and discharged at 0.05 C to a cut-off voltage of 0.02 V. The cells were subsequently dis-
assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox, and the retrieved electrodes were collected as lithiated graphite.
2.5. High LiTFSI concentration electrolyte preparation
For low-salt electrolytes, the volume expansion of the solution can be neglected. However, at higher salt
concentrations, the volume change upon salt dissolution must be considered. To quantify this, several
identical transparent vials were used for electrolyte preparation. After adding LiTFSI and stirring until
complete dissolution, the liquid level was recorded, and the electrolyte volume was determined from the
liquid height. The electrolyte concentration was then defined as the molar amount of LiTFSI relative to
the final electrolyte volume.
2.6. Characterizations
Sample morphologies were examined using a FEI Quanta 650 field-emission SEM (Nano SEM 230).
Secondary electron imaging (ETD) was employed to reveal surface topography, while compositional con-
trast was obtained in concentric backscatter mode. The elemental mapping of the samples was examined
using an EDS detector on a Zeiss LEO Supra 35VP SEM-FEG field-emission electron microscope. Phase
composition and crystal structure were identified by x-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8). Surface chem-
ical states were further analyzed by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI Quantum 2000), with
Ar+ sputtering applied for surface cleaning (60 s on the H–TiO2/S cathode and 120 s on the graphite
anode).
2.7. Cell assembly and electrochemical tests
For the assembly of Li–S and Li–graphite half cells, electrodes were paired with Li chips separated by a
PP membrane, with 70 µl of electrolyte added in each CR2032 coin cell. When an interlayer was used,
it had the same diameter as the cathode and was placed directly on its surface. For the LiGr-S full cell,
the lithiated graphite electrode was employed as the anode, paired with the sulfur cathode and interlayer,
while maintaining the same electrolyte amount (70 µl). The N/P ratio of the full cell is controlled within
0.8–1.0, with theoretical capacities of 372 mAh g−1 for graphite and 1675 mAh g−1 for sulfur. The spe-
cific capacity of the full cell is calculated based on the mass of active sulfur.
Galvanostatic charge–discharge cycling and rate capability tests were performed on a LAND battery
testing system (CT-2001 A, Wuhan Rambo Testing Equipment Co., Ltd.). The voltage ranges were set
to 0.02–1.5 V for graphite electrodes, 1.7–2.8 V or 1.8–2.6 V for Li–S cells, and 1.5–2.6 V for full cells.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were conducted on
a VSP potentiostat (Bio-Logic, France). For electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), a 10 mV per-
turbation was applied, and spectra were recorded from 100 kHz to 100 mHz at open-circuit potential.
3. Results and discussion

were incorporated to tailor the interfacial chemistry. Two MXene configurations were explored: calcined
MXene (CM) and SnO2-decorated MXene (MS). The SnO2 decoration on MXene serves dual functions:

chemical kinetics and interfacial impedance (figure S3). In the CV profiles, the forward shift of both
oxidation and reduction peaks is indicative of accelerated redox kinetics and reduced polarization. As
shown in Figure S3(a), the two reduction peaks of the MS-PVDF cell appear at noticeably higher poten-

cess, both MS-PVDF and CM-PVDF exhibit a pronounced anodic shift relative to the bare C/S cathode.
These results suggest that the introduction of an interlayer facilitates the polysulfide conversion reaction,
thereby mitigating electrochemical polarization. In addition, the bare C/S and CM-PVDF cells exhib-
ited uncontrolled current amplification during the anodic sweep, typically indicative of internal short-
circuiting from Li dendrite penetration, whereas this behavior was absent in the MS-PVDF cells. In EIS
(figure S3(b)), the MS-PVDF cell exhibits the smallest semicircle diameter, corresponding to the lowest
charge-transfer resistance, followed by CM-PVDF. This trend is consistent with the polarization behav-
ior observed in CV. Overall, the incorporation of either CM-PVDF or MS-PVDF interlayers improves
the reaction kinetics of Li–S cells, with the MS-PVDF interlayer demonstrating the most pronounced
enhancement.

in which more active material becomes electrochemically accessible over the first cycles. A few mecha-
nisms usually contribute at the same time: (1) Gradual electrolyte wetting and sulfur utilization, early
on, the electrolyte does not fully penetrate the porous electrode structures, cycling helps the electrolyte
infiltrate deeper, increasing the fraction of sulfur that participates in the reaction; (2) Activation of
redox pathways, Li–S batteries rely on complex conversion reactions involving dissolved lithium poly-
sulfides. In early cycles, these reactions can be kinetically sluggish. With cycling, the system stabilizes—
interfaces improve and reaction pathways become more efficient—so more sulfur is converted per cycle;
(3) Formation and stabilization of interphases (SEIs/CEI), the solid electrolyte interphase on lithium
and surface films on the cathode evolve during the first cycles. Once these layers stabilize, side reactions
decrease and Coulombic efficiency improves, which can show up as a capacity increase. At high rates,
the system is initially far from optimal (poor transport, incomplete utilization). Any improvement in
kinetics or accessibility has a larger relative effect, so the capacity rise is more noticeable than at low cur-
rents where utilization is already closer to maximum. The same tendency was observed in the CV results
discussed above. These findings indicate that the MS-PVDF interlayer is more effective in regulating a
uniform Li+ flux and suppressing dendrite-induced failure.
Based on the comparative electrochemical performance, the MS-PVDF interlayer exhibited the most
favorable reaction kinetics and cycling stability. Therefore, the MS-PVDF configuration was selected for
subsequent experiments to further evaluate its performance with different cathodes.
3.2. Evaluation of H–TiO2/S cathode
Owing to the intrinsically polar surface, TiO2 offers markedly stronger interaction with lithium poly-
sulfides (LiPSs) than bare carbonaceous materials, which is expected to improve shuttle suppression.
Thus, a hollow TiO2 framework (H–TiO2) was designed, in which hydrogen reduction was employed
to enhance electrical conductivity and catalytic activity. When integrated with sulfur, the resulting
H–TiO2/S cathode is schematically illustrated in figure 2(a). Morphological analysis of the pristine
H–TiO2/S cathode by SEM (figures 2(b) and S5), using both secondary electron and compositional
backscattered modes, confirmed its spherical morphology and hollow architecture of the sulfur host.
XPS measurements revealed the coexistence of Ti in mixed valence states (Ti4+, Ti3+, Ti2+) in the H–
TiO2/S composite (figure 2(c)), attributable to the hydrogen reduction treatment. In the S 2p spectrum,
terminated sulfur was detected (figure 2(d)), which is likely formed through the reduction of sulfur by
Ti3+/Ti2+ or via chemisorption on H–TiO2 surfaces through S–Ti bonding. In contrast, the pristine C/S
electrodes contained only bridged sulfur (S8), as shown in figure S6. These results provide direct evi-
dence of the strong interaction between H–TiO2 toward sulfur. The XRD pattern of H–TiO2/S confirms
the rutile phase of TiO2 and reveals an additional reflection attributable to a reduced titanium oxide,
predominantly along a single crystallographic plane, indicating the presence of surface-confined reduced
phases on H–TiO2 (figure S7).
In electrochemical tests, C/S and H–TiO2/S cathodes were compared, both incorporating the
MS-PVDF interlayer. To enhance electrode stability, the voltage window was narrowed to 1.8–2.6 V.
Figure 2(e) displays the cycling performance at 0.1 C. The C/S cathode experienced a precipitous capac-
ity drop between the first and second cycles, whereas the H–TiO2/S cathode delivered a markedly
higher capacity in the initial cycles. However, the capacity of H–TiO2/S declined more noticeably over
extended cycling, and by the 50th cycle its advantage over C/S had largely diminished, with both capac-
ities falling below 800 mAh g−1. This indicates that, while H–TiO2/S provides a considerable initial
capacity at 0.1 C, its capacity fade is relatively rapid. In contrast, the rate capability tests revealed a
pronounced advantage for H–TiO2/S at high current densities (figure 2(f)). Notably, at 2 C it achieved
>700 mAh g−1, far surpassing the ∼400 mAh g−1 delivered by C/S.

by the dependence of the rate-limiting processes on current density. At low rates (0.1 C), the overall

sion), not surface catalytic conversion. As a result, even the weakly adsorbing C/S cathode can achieve
comparable sulfur utilization, because the extended reaction time compensates for its limited surface
interaction. At high rates (e.g. 1 C or higher), however, the rate-determining step shifts from diffusion
to surface reaction kinetics. Each LiPS molecule has only a very short residence time at the electrode
surface, and must rapidly complete the full sequence of adsorption, electron transfer, and desorption.
Under these fast-discharge conditions, the strong chemisorption and the Ti3+/oxygen vacancy catalytic
sites on H–TiO2 become the decisive factor. This is consistent with the Sabatier principle: the value of
lowering the activation barrier is only fully realized when the surface reaction itself becomes the kinetic
bottleneck. The catalytic activity of TiO2 is primarily governed by its surface adsorption capability, and
optimal catalysis is achieved under appropriate adsorption strength [23].
Secondly, the shuttle effect is inherently more pronounced at low rates, which partially offsets the
capacity retention advantage of H–TiO2/S at low current densities. At low rates, the prolonged discharge
time provides a larger window for soluble LiPSs to diffuse across the separator, exacerbating the polysul-
fide shuttle. This well-known phenomenon in Li-S batteries means that capacity retention at low rates is
inherently suppressed for both cathodes. At high rates, the shortened reaction time limits LiPS diffusion
and mitigates the shuttle effect, allowing better capacity retention. Taken together, the similar perfor-
mance at low rates reflects thermodynamic equivalence and aggravated shuttle effects, while the superior
performance of H–TiO2/S at high rates reflects its kinetic advantage, providing also better stability and
higher capacity at higher rate compared to lower rate.
CV and EIS measurements were also conducted to evaluate the C/S and H–TiO2/S electrodes (figure
S8). In the CV curves, the first reduction peak of H–TiO2/S appeared markedly earlier than that of C/S,
whereas the second reduction peak and the oxidation peak showed no obvious shift. Nevertheless, both
peaks exhibited substantially higher current densities for H–TiO2/S. In the EIS spectra, the semicircle
diameter for H–TiO2/S was significantly smaller than that for C/S, indicating a lower charge-transfer
resistance. These results suggest that the strong surface adsorption capability of H–TiO2 promotes faster
redox kinetics, which accounts for its superior rate capability and overall performance compared with
the conventional C/S cathode.

Increasing the concentration of LiTFSI fundamentally alters the solvation environment of Li+,
thereby weakening Li+–solvent interactions and facilitating desolvation. Figure 3(a) shows that the ini-
tial co-intercalation plateau gradually disappears with increasing LiTFSI concentration. At 3 M LiTFSI,
the graphite electrode undergoes normal lithiation/delithiation, delivering 380 mAh g−1 in the first cycle
with a Coulombic efficiency of 90%, and thereafter maintaining a reversible capacity of ∼360 mAh g−1
(figure S10). At lower concentrations, co-intercalation persists. Nevertheless, higher salt concentrations
consistently improve the charge–discharge behavior. However, higher salt concentrations inevitably com-
promise the reaction kinetics of the sulfur cathode. As illustrated in figure S11, when the LiTFSI con-
centration exceeds 2.3 M, the discharge profile of a typical C/S electrode displays only a single plateau.
This is because high-salt electrolytes hinder the dissolution of lithium polysulfides, thereby limiting their
conversion and deposition. At lower current rates, the second plateau reappears (figure S12). Thus, while
a higher LiTFSI concentration effectively suppresses solvent co-intercalation at the graphite anode, an
excessively high concentration also reduces polysulfide solubility and slows the kinetics of the sulfur
cathode.
LiNO3 has long been valued in Li–S cells for its ability to stabilize the Li-metal SEI and enhance
polysulfide redox [28, 29]. Beyond these established roles, LiNO3 is also found to significantly sup-
presses solvent co-intercalation at graphite anodes in ether electrolytes. As shown in figure S13, in 1 M
LiTFSI electrolytes with varying LiNO3 contents, although a first-cycle co-intercalation plateau is still
observed, the graphite delithiation plateau becomes progressively more distinct and the reversible capac-
ity increases systematically with LiNO3 concentration. At 1.6 M LiTFSI (figure S14), the addition of
2 wt.% LiNO3 still produces a first-cycle co-intercalation plateau, yet subsequent cycles clearly reveal a
graphite delithiation plateau with a reversible capacity of ∼240 mAh g−1. When LiNO3 concentration

On the sulfur cathode side, the addition of LiNO3 is generally recognized to effectively suppress the
polysulfide shuttle effect and improve the redox kinetics of polysulfides. Figures S16 and S17 investigate
the influence of LiNO3 on sulfur electrodes with 1.6 M and 2.3 M LiTFSI, respectively. In the 1.6 M
system (figure S16), cells without LiNO3 exhibit severe shuttle effects in the third cycle. Incorporation
of LiNO3 markedly alleviates the shuttle, although it causes a slight decrease in the initial discharge
capacity. Nevertheless, the capacities in the second and third cycles improve progressively with increas-
ing LiNO3 content. In the 2.3 M system (figure S17), cells without LiNO3 suffer from an unstable or
markedly suppressed second discharge plateau. In contrast, LiNO3 addition not only stabilizes and
enhances the second plateau but also increases the overall discharge capacity. Notably, an addition of
1 wt.% LiNO3 yields superior performance compared to 2 wt.%. Therefore, beyond mitigating co-
intercalation issues at graphite anodes, LiNO3 also plays a critical role at the cathode by suppressing the
shuttle effect and enhancing the reaction kinetics of polysulfides.
Therefore, based on the above investigations, two representative electrolyte systems were identified:
1.6 M LiTFSI with 3 wt.% LiNO3, and 2.3 M LiTFSI with 2 wt.% LiNO3 (figure S18). Both formula-
tions exhibit favorable compatibility between the sulfur cathode and the graphite anode. In the 2.3 M
LiTFSI with 2 wt.% LiNO3 system, the graphite electrode exhibits excellent cycling stability, maintain-
ing a capacity >350 mAh g−1 over 50 cycles at 0.1 C (figure 3(b)), although its rate performance dete-
riorates above 1 C (figure S19). The CV curves of the sulfur and graphite electrodes in this electrolyte
system is also presented in figure 3(c).
XPS reveals compositional contrasts in the SEI formed under different electrolyte conditions. As
shown in figures 3(d) and (e) and S20, after identical sputtering, the 1 M electrolyte sample shows a
dominant C–C peak in the C 1 s region and relatively stronger signals from the graphite substrate, while
the 2.3 M sample exhibits enhanced contributions from C=O/COO/CFx and Li–F species, together with
the appearance of NOx in the N 1 s region. These observations indicate that the 2.3 M electrolyte leads
to a thicker interphase comprising SEI and salt-derived species, whereas the 1 M sample is covered by
a comparatively thinner layer. Although LiNO3 is often expected to modify SEI chemistry, its addition
does not change the XPS-detectable composition, suggesting that the suppression of co-intercalation may
not be achieved through direct alteration of SEI constituents. Furthermore, electrodes stabilized in 2.3 M
with 2 wt% LiNO3 revert to co-intercalation once transferred into 1 M electrolyte, demonstrating that
the phenomenon is governed by the electrolyte environment rather than by the SEI acting as a physical
barrier to solvent molecules (figure S21).
Figure 3(f) illustrates the mechanism of the suppression of co-intercalation. Highly concentrated elec-
trolyte reduces coordination of Li+ with solvent molecules, so that Li+-solvent interactions are weak-
ened, which is beneficial for the reversible solvation and desolvation of Li+. For the function of LiNO3,
a reliable explanation is that NO3- participates in the primary solvation sheath of Li+, altering the sol-
vation environment and further weakening Li+–solvent interactions, which was also discussed in other
works [30, 31]. All in all, the combined regulation of salt concentration and additive chemistry is essen-
tial to suppress solvent co-intercalation at graphite anodes in ether-based electrolytes.
3.4. Sulfur-lithiation graphite full cell evaluation
The graphite electrode was electrochemically prelithiated to obtain lithiated graphite (LiGr). Compared
with the pristine electrode, LiGr shows a conformal SEI overlayer that smooths the particle surfaces and
partially blocks the pore entrances (figure S22). LiGr, together with the corresponding electrolyte, the
previously optimized MS-PVDF interlayer, and the H–TiO2/S cathode, was assembled into a full cell,
as illustrated in figure 4(a). Figures 4(b) and (c) present the CV and EIS profiles of the full cell using
2.3 M LiTFSI with 2 wt.% LiNO3. The CV curves exhibit the typical two reduction peaks located at
2.15 V and 1.8 V, along with a single oxidation peak at 2.3 V. In the EIS spectra, the Nyquist plot shows
the characteristic two-semicircle feature. Post-cycling SEM characterization of the H–TiO2/S cathode,
MS-PVDF interlayer, and LiGr anode reveals well-preserved morphologies with no significant structural
degradation (figure S23), providing direct evidence for the structural integrity of the full-cell system
under prolonged cycling conditions.

4. Conclusion
In summary, a full cell comprising an H–TiO2/S cathode with an MS-PVDF interlayer and a lithiated
graphite anode was successfully constructed, representing a prototype of a Li-anode-free Li–S battery.
In this design, the ratio of LiTFSI and LiNO3 salts in a conventional DOL/DME (1:1 v/v) electrolyte
was carefully tuned, enabling compatibility with both the sulfur cathode and the graphite anode, which
is an essential prerequisite for the feasibility of the full cell. Moreover, comparison between the H–
TiO2/S cathode and a conventional C/S cathode demonstrated the advantage of reduced metal oxides
with active sites over bare carbonaceous hosts. A freestanding MS-PVDF interlayer was also fabricated

an advanced sulfur cathode, thereby circumventing the safety concerns associated with Li-metal anodes.
