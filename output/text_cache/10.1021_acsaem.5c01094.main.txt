ABSTRACT: The uncontrolled deposition of insulating lithium sulfide (Li2S)
induces inactive Li2S deposits, severely passivating the sulfur cathode and
fundamentally limiting the sulfur utilization in lithium−sulfur batteries. While
electrolytes employing high-donor-number solvents can mitigate this issue, their
propensity to exacerbate lithium metal corrosion remains a critical challenge.
Herein, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the high-donor-number solid
electrolyte interface (SEI) in the Li2S deposition/dissolution reactions. The SEI
in the sulfur cathode was constructed by using LiNO3 or LiI with a high-donor-
number anion as the electrolyte additive. The interactions between the donor-
enriched SEI and the LiTFSI increased the accumulation of LiTFSI on the
electrode interface, conductive to the high concentration of Li+ for the polysulfide
reduction reaction to deposit Li2S, while the interactions between the deposited
Li2S and the donor-enriched SEI developed the electron coupling contributing
positively to the Li2S-oxidation reaction. Consequently, electrochemical and spectroscopic analyses revealed that the donor-enriched
SEI synergistically optimized the Li2S deposition/dissolution kinetics and mitigated the polysulfide shuttling, bringing about
improved cell capacity and Coulombic efficiency. The results of this study provide direct evidence of the beneficial effects of a high-
donor-number SEI on the Li2S deposition/dissolution reaction kinetics.
KEYWORDS: lithium sulfide, SEI, high donor number, deposition and dissolution, lithium−sulfur battery
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur batteries have emerged as one of the most
promising candidates for next-generation energy storage
systems, owing to their high theoretical gravimetric capacity
of elemental sulfur (1675 mA h g−1) and high energy density
(2600 W h kg−1) of the sulfur cathode when it is coupled to a
lithium metal anode.1−3 The low material cost and relatively
low environmental impact of sulfur are added advantages.
However, the practical applications of lithium−sulfur batteries
have encountered several difficult technical challenges.4−6 One
of the major issues is the sluggish deposition/dissolution
behaviors of the end discharge product Li2S. The electrically
insulating nature of intermediate reaction products (lithium
polysulfides) and Li2S increases the reaction barrier and
decreases the reaction kinetics of lithium polysulfide trans-
formation. The sluggish reaction kinetics increases the
accumulation of soluble lithium polysulfides in the cathode,
leading to uneven Li2S deposition and continual loss of lithium
polysulfides to the electrolyte by diffusion. Together they lead
to the low utilization of sulfur and the rapid capacity fading
upon cycling.
The high theoretical capacity of sulfur cathode is based on
the multielectron reactions, wherein during the discharge
process, the active sulfur is reduced to form insoluble Li2S
through the soluble lithium polysulfide intermediates.7−9 The
deposition reaction of Li2S from the soluble Li2S4 polysulfide
contributes a theoretical capacity of 1256 mA h g−1 (almost
75% of the sulfur cathode capacity). One approach to increase
the capacity of the sulfur cathode and to reduce the polysulfide
dissolution is improving the conversion efficiency of end
discharge product Li2S, including the Li2S deposition and
dissolution processes.10−12 Significant research efforts have
been devoted to investigate the Li2S precipitation, especially on
the geometric structure of the deposited Li2S.13 The good
dispersion and good electrical contact of the deposited Li2S
can reduce the formation of the “dead” Li2S to improve the

growth orientation of the deposited Li2S according to the

accelerating the conversion kinetics of Li2S on the catalytic

of Li2S, particularly concerning the conversion of Li2S to
polysulfides, required further elucidation. Specifically, the
reaction mechanism of the Li2S deposition/dissolution
involved in the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) formation in
the sulfur cathode has not yet been clearly identified.
The most common method to accelerate the Li2S
deposition/dissolution kinetics is using the electrochemical
catalysts to improve the polysulfide conversion.17−19 However,
most of the catalyst materials still face challenges such as
limited catalytic sites, electron and ion transfer limitations, and
low electrochemical stability. The use of polysulfide catalysts
often involves the design of an elaborate sulfur-loading host
structure with a complex sulfur encapsulation process and
introduces an inevitable “dead weight” effect.20 As an
alternative to the use of a catalyst, developing new electrolyte
formulations has been proven to be another strategy to
improve the Li2S conversion kinetics, especially for the Li2S
dissolution.21 As the pivotal component in lithium−sulfur
batteries closely related to the polysulfide dissolution, an
electrolyte plays crucial roles in providing the ion transport and
constructing a stable SEI on the sulfur cathode. Fast ion
diffusion and the high affinity of electrolyte components to the
deposited Li2S are usually supposed to favor the Li2S
conversion reactions. Optimizing the electrolyte formulation
to use the solvents with a high Gutmann donor number has
been reported as a solution for modulating the intrinsic
chemistry of Li2S deposition reaction due to the altered
chemical transformation between Li2S and soluble polysul-
fides.22,23 Additionally, electrolytes with high electron-donating

extreme reactivity with a lithium metal anode and increase the
dissolution of polysulfides in the electrolyte to deteriorate the
notorious shuttle effect.25 Moreover, how the SEI in the sulfur
cathode affects the Li2S deposition/dissolution is also revealed.
In this work, we investigate the effect of the SEI on the Li2S
deposition/dissolution behaviors. The SEI in the sulfur
cathode was constructed by using LiNO3 or LiI with a high-
donor-number anion as the electrolyte additive.15,23 LiNO3 has
been generally employed in lithium−sulfur batteries to
suppress the polysulfide shuttle.26,27 LiNO3 and LiI have
been studied in the lithium−metal battery to form a SEI on the
lithium metal anode to improve the lithium metal deposition/
dissolution.28−30 We are interested in the effect of the high-
donor-number SEI on the Li2S deposition/dissolution
behaviors; this information is currently unavailable in the
literature. The experimental results confirmed that the SEI
formed on the sulfur cathode was constituted with the high-
donor-number components from the LiNO3 and LiI
decomposition, and that the Li2S deposition/dissolution
reactions were improved on both the LiNO3 and LiI-derived
SEI. Specifically, the Li2S deposition/dissolution conversion
kinetics on the LiNO3-based SEI were higher than those on the
LiI-based SEI. This work not only sheds light on the
mechanism of the Li2S deposition/dissolution behaviors on
the SEI, but also presents some new insights into the effect of
high-donor-number SEI on the Li2S deposition/dissolution
reaction kinetics.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The morphological evolution of the deposited Li2S upon
discharge in the Li-CP cell with and without lithium salt
additive (LiNO3 or LiI) was first examined by field emission
scanning electron microcopy (FESEM) measurement.31 The
Li-CP cell consisted of carbon paper as the working electrode
and lithium metal as the counter electrode. The electrolyte was
0.2 M Li2S6 and 1 M LiTFSI in a 1:1 (v/v) 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL)/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) mixture without (de-
noted as baseline electrolyte) or with lithium salt additive
(LiNO3 or LiI) (Figure S1). The cells were discharged to 1.5 V
using a constant current of 0.1 mA cm−2 to obtain the stable
Li2S deposit on the working electrode. The sulfur source for
the Li2S deposition came from Li2S6 in the electrolyte. The
pristine working electrode exhibited the morphology of
interlaced carbon fibers with a smooth surface (Figure 1a).
Comparison of the working electrode in the baseline
electrolyte after discharge to 1.5 V (Figure 1b) and the
pristine electrode confirmed that the scattered Li2S granules
were deposited on the carbon fibers. The carbon fibers in the
electrolyte with LiI were mostly covered by the Li2S with a
mossy morphology, indicating more Li2S were precipitated
(Figure 1c). When LiNO3 was used in the electrolyte, the
carbon fibers were acutely passivated by the agglomerated Li2S,
demonstrating the considerable promotion of Li2S deposition
by LiNO3.
The working electrodes after discharging to 1.5 V in
different polysulfide electrolytes were examined by XRD, as
shown in Figure 1e. The diffraction peaks of the pristine
working electrode are in good agreement with the graphite
phase (PDF#41-1487), and the broad diffraction peak at
around 2θ = 20° can be attributed to the Capton foil (Figure
S2). The diffraction peaks at 2θ = 26.9° and 55.6° are
indexable to the Li2S antifluorite phase (PDF#23-0369).13 The
working electrode in the baseline electrolyte showed the
weakest intensity of the Li2S diffraction peaks, suggesting the
least deposited Li2S. The Li2S diffraction peak intensity
increased when using LiI in the electrolyte, and the working
electrode in the electrolyte with LiNO3 showed the strongest
intensity, an indication of the most facile Li2S deposition with
LiNO3, which was consistent with the FESEM results (Figure
1d). The facile Li2S deposition on the working electrode in the
electrolyte with LiNO3 was further corroborated by the SANS.
SANS provides the possibility of morphological character-
ization of the Li2S precipitate on the electrode with the carbon
fiber matrix.32,33 The scattering data for the working electrodes
in different electrolytes after Li2S deposition are shown in
Figures 1f and S3. The intensity is plotted as a function of the
scattering vector Q, which demonstrates the directional change
and momentum transfer of a neutron during scattering.34
Compared to the pristine working electrode, the electrodes
after Li2S deposition in different electrolytes showed an
increase in the scattering intensity, which were attributed to
the deposition of Li2S nanoparticles. The Li2S deposition in
the electrolyte with LiNO3 showed the highest scattering
intensity, indicating the most significant Li2S precipitate on the
electrode.35
Using LiI and LiNO3 in the electrolyte showed their
promotion on the Li2S deposition kinetics, especially the more
facile deposition by the LiNO3. The effectiveness of LiI and

measured by the potentiostatic method using the following
step: from 2.2 to 1.5 V (discharging) to deposit Li2S on the
working electrode. The cells were recharged to 2.6 V using the
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) to evaluate the kinetics of the
Li2S dissolution. Figure 2a shows the current−time response
from the potentiostatic discharge of Li-CP cells with different
electrolytes. The discharge currents resulted from the Li2S6
reduction to Li2S on the working electrodes, which was
assigned to Li2S deposition and could identify the difference in
the deposition reaction kinetics among the different polysulfide
electrolytes.36,37 Compared to the baseline electrolyte, the cells
with LiI and LiNO3 both showed the increased discharge
current, while the largest Li2S deposition peak occurred in the
cell with LiNO3, indicating the most facile Li2S deposition with
LiNO3. Figure 2b shows the LSV curves of the cells after the
Li2S deposition. The deposited Li2S on the working electrode
was oxidized to form the sulfur through the lithium polysulfide
intermediates during the cathodic scan from 1.5 to 2.6 V. The
two cathodic peaks from the cell with LiNO3 at about 2.5 V
could be associated with the oxidation of the deposited Li2S on
the working electrode to polysulfides (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) and
the following conversion of the latter to sulfur, respec-
tively.38,39 The sharpness of the peaks indicates facile Li2S
dissolution and good electrochemical reversibility between
polysulfides and deposited Li2S. When LiI is used in the
electrolyte, the broadened peaks and the decreased peak
potential are indications of slower Li2S dissolution and reduced
electrochemical reversibility. Li2S dissolution and electro-
chemical reversibility were the lowest with the baseline
electrolyte, resulting in the lowest reaction current and the
largest potential polarization.
The effectiveness of LiNO3 and LiI as electrolyte additives
was further evaluated by the galvanostatic discharge−charge of
the Li-CP cells, which provided a holistic understanding of
how the LiNO3 and LiI affected the Li2S deposition/
dissolution behaviors and hence the operational cycling
performance of the lithium−sulfur batteries. The galvanostatic
discharge−charge curves of the cyclability tests are shown in
Figures 3a and S4. In the first discharge curves from the open
circuit voltage to 1.5 V, only plateaus at about 1.9 V appeared.
Since the Li2S6 was the only electrochemically active species in
the electrolyte, it is reasonable to assume that Li2S6 was
reduced to Li2S on the working electrode.40 Compared to the
baseline electrolyte and the electrolyte with LiI, the cell with
LiNO3 in the electrolyte delivered the highest discharge
capacity, indicating facile Li2S deposition. In the third
discharge curves, there were two discharge plateaus at about
2.3 and 1.9 V, which resulted from the typical sulfur reduction
to soluble long-chain polysulfides (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) and the
redeposition of Li2S on the working electrode, respectively.41
The two plateaus in the charge curve at about 2.3 and 2.4 V
were attributed to the reverse of these reactions, which
converted Li2S to sulfur.42 The reversibility of the Li2S
conversion including the Li2S deposition and dissolution
processes can be evaluated by the Coulombic efficiency of the
Li-CP cell. The Coulombic efficiencies of the second cycle
were 99.93% for the cell with LiNO3 and 90.05% for the cell
with LiI (Figure 3b). The improved Li2S deposition/
dissolution kinetics by using LiNO3 are expected to be the
contributive factors in the higher Coulombic efficiency and
charge/discharge capacities. The Li-CP cell with the baseline
electrolyte showed the least satisfactory performance with the
Coulombic efficiency of only 83.18%. In the galvanostatic
charge curve of the cell with the baseline electrolyte, there was
a noticeably large charge capacity for the 2.4 V plateaus, which
led to the low Coulombic efficiency (Figure 3a). This unusual

reactions on the lithium metal anode can be suppressed.27,44
Since the polysulfide shuttle effect was associated with the
interconversion reactions among different soluble polysulfides,
promoting the conversion of soluble polysulfides to solid sulfur
by using LiNO3 or LiI could limit the polysulfide shuttle effect.
For additional insights into the Li2S deposition behavior on
the SEI in the cells with different polysulfide electrolytes, the
cells after discharging to 1.5 V were measured by electro-
chemical impedance spectra (EIS) and the working electrodes
with the Li2S deposit were examined by X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). The comparison of the first discharge
curves among the Li-CP cells with different polysulfide
electrolytes is shown in Figure 4a. The electrode reactions
that contributed to the 2.05 V plateau were the reduction of
Li2S6 to deposit Li2S on the working electrode. The plateau
was extended when LiI was used in the electrolyte and was
further extended when using LiNO3, demonstrating the most
facile Li2S deposition kinetics by LiNO3. The use of LiNO3
increased the voltage plateau during the cell discharging,
providing further evidence for the more facile electrode
reaction. EIS measurements (Figure 4b) also registered the
smallest resistance of 75.9 Ω for the cell with LiNO3 compared
to 107.8 Ω for the cell with LiI and 132.6 Ω for the cell with
baseline electrolyte, as shown by the smallest size of the high-
frequency semicircle in the Nyquist plot.45
Since the interface resistance was closely associated with the
Li2S deposition reaction kinetics, the surface compositions of
the working electrodes after discharging to 1.5 V were
characterized by XPS. The binding energies were calibrated
with respect to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. As shown in Figure
4c, the 160.5 and 161.8 eV peaks can be attributed to the Li2S
and Li2S2 deposited on the working electrodes, while the peak
at 168.1 eV can be attributed to polythionate and the peak at
169.3 eV to sulfate.15 The high-valence sulfur species of
polythionate and sulfate could come from the decomposition
of LiTFSI in the electrolyte. The peak intensities of the high-
valence sulfur species augmented when LiI was used in the
electrolyte, while the intensities were significantly increased
when using LiNO3. The XPS results indicated more LiTFSI on
the interface of working electrodes in the electrolyte with LiI
and LiNO3, suggesting the favor of LiTFSI accumulation. It
has been reported that the high donor anions from the LiI and
LiNO3 can form ion−ion interactions with LiTFSI and form
the bonding with the Li+ ion through the electron pairs.46,47
The interactions between the high donor species from LiI and
LiNO3 in the SEI and the LiTFSI increased the level of
accumulation of LiTFSI on the SEI, which led to the high XPS
peak intensity of the high-valence sulfur species. The large
amount of LiTFSI could increase the Li+ concentration on the
electrode interface, which could favor the polysulfide reduction
reaction to deposit Li2S.48 There was also evidence in the S 2p
XPS spectra for the interactions between Li2S and LiNO3 and
the LiI-derived SEI to promote the Li2S deposition. The peaks
of low-valence sulfur species (Li2S2 and Li2S deposits) slightly
shifted to a lower binding energy, suggesting the electron
transfer from the high donor species in the SEI.18 The N 1s

In order to understand the Li2S dissolution behavior on the
SEI, the cells after the Li2S deposition were recharged to 2.6 V
and investigated by EIS measurement. The cells were then
disassembled to investigate the surface composition of the
working electrodes by XPS. Figure 5a shows the comparison of
the first galvanostatic charge curves among the Li-CP cells with
different polysulfide electrolytes after discharging to 1.5 V. The
two distinct charge voltage plateaus (at ∼2.23 and 2.35 V)
could be attributed to the conversion of Li2S to lithium
polysulfides and the formation of sulfur from the latter. It
should be noted that the 2.35 V plateaus of the cell with the
baseline electrolyte was higher than the cell with LiNO3 or LiI,
which resulted from the notorious polysulfide shuttling in
lithium−sulfur batteries and hence led to the lowest
Coulombic efficiency (Figure 3b). The cell with LiNO3 in
the electrolyte performed the lowest voltage plateaus and
highest charge capacity for the 2.23 V plateaus reaction, an
indication of the largest amount of Li2S deposits and the most
facile Li2S dissolution reaction kinetics. EIS also measured the
smallest interface resistance for the cell with LiNO3. The first
high-frequency semicircle in the Nyquist plot was attributed to
the resistance from the SEI, while the second semicircle
resulted from the charge transfer in the electrode. The smallest
semicircles for the cell with LiNO3 (46.1 and 95.9 Ω,
compared to 62.2 Ω and 115.8 Ω for the cell with LiI; and
89.1 and 141.3 Ω for the cell with baseline electrolyte)
demonstrated that the kinetics of the Li2S dissolution reaction
was the most facile on the LiNO3-derived SEI, thereby
resulting in the lowest impedance.

It has been reported that the high-donor-number anions
would enhance the partial solubility of Li2S through the
interaction between the high-donor-number anions and the
Li2S, which could increase the binding distance of Li+−S2−and
decrease the closely existing S2−population.15 The donor-
enriched SEI developed the electron coupling with Li2S and
may also increase the Li+−S2−binding distance of the
deposited Li2S conducive to their partial solubility during the
charging process (Figure 4c). The increasing Li+−S2−binding
distance and partial solubility of Li2S would facilitate the Li2S-
oxidation reactions, probably through the involvement of some
metastable S3
•−species.22 The LiNO3 decomposition species
in the SEI enhanced the Li2S-oxidation reaction kinetics may
also through the formation of NOx radical, which could
participate in the oxidation reaction of polysulfides to promote
the conversion of Li2S.26
The above results indicated that both the LiNO3 and LiI-
derived SEI could promote the Li2S deposition/dissolution
reaction kinetics, leading to increasing charge/discharge
capacities and battery Coulombic efficiencies (Figure 3b).
Considering the soluble lithium polysulfides produced from
the sulfur cathode can diffuse to the anode side through the
electrolyte under the concentration gradient and are reactive
on the lithium metal anode to cause the polysulfide shuttling

indicating that the stable SEI formed to inhibit the corrosion
from polysulfides and the formation of inactive lithium metal
and lithium dendrite.
■CONCLUSION
In summary, we have investigated the effectiveness of the SEI
constructed by the components from the decomposition of
LiNO3 or LiI with high-donor-number anions on the Li2S
deposition/dissolution behaviors. The experimental results
confirmed that the Li2S deposition/dissolution reactions were
improved on both the LiNO3 and LiI-derived SEI, bringing
about improved cell capacity and Coulombic efficiency.
Specifically, the Li2S deposition/dissolution conversion
kinetics on the LiNO3-based SEI were higher than those on
the LiI-based SEI. During the discharge of the sulfur cathode
to form the Li2S deposition, the interactions between the high
donor species from LiI and LiNO3 in the SEI and the LiTFSI
increased the accumulation of LiTFSI on the SEI. The large
amount of LiTFSI could increase the Li+ concentration on the
electrode interface, which favored the polysulfide reduction
reaction to deposit Li2S. The high electron donating species
from the SEI could also facilitate the polysulfide reduction to
form Li2S through weakening the S−S bonds in polysulfides.
During the charge of the sulfur cathode to reduce the
deposited Li2S, the LiNO3 or LiI-derived SEI would enhance
the partial solubility of Li2S through the interaction between
the high-donor-number species and Li2S, which could increase
the binding distance of Li+−S2−and hence the Li2S reduction
reaction kinetics. This study here shows, more importantly,
how SEI on the sulfur cathode may be used to enhance the
sulfur reaction kinetics and some insights about the inner
working of the high-donor-number SEI.
