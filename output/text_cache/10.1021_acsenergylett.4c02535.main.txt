Practical Lithium−Sulfur Batteries: Beyond the
Conventional Electrolyte Concentration

Cite This: ACS Energy Lett. 2024, 9, 5576−5586

ABSTRACT: Advances in electrolyte chemistry and the development of electrolyte
systems have revealed that electrolyte concentration significantly affects battery
performance. However, the relationship between electrolyte concentration, poly-
sulfide formation, and lithium−sulfur (Li−S) battery performance remains unclear,
which hinders the developmental progress of practical Li−S batteries. In this study,
we compared the electrolyte structures and performance of Li−S batteries with
various electrolyte concentrations and developed a method that links microscopic
interactions, apparent electrolyte parameters, and battery performance. The
relationship between polysulfides and the electrolyte system was analyzed at various
concentrations, which revealed that systems with a low lithium-salt concentration,
especially below 1 M, are better suited to practical Li−S batteries and that polysulfides play crucial roles in ion-transport
processes under practical conditions. This study supports the development of electrolytes for practical Li−S batteries and
provides guidance for controlling ion-transport processes in liquid electrolytes for other secondary batteries.

As key components of electrolytes, lithium salts play crucial
roles in promoting ion transport and constructing stable
electrode interfaces. Fluoride salts have been selected as the
main lithium salts for Li−S batteries, such as lithium
difluoromethanesulfonamide (LiTFSI) and lithium difluorosul-
fonamide (LiFSI),5 because they do not react chemically with
polysulfides (LiPSs). Thus, traditional electrolytes for Li−S
batteries are constructed based on 1 M LiTFSI or LiFSI.
However, under practical conditions (low electrolyte volume,
high S load), a significant increase in LiPS content can influence
the traditional electrolyte significantly, such as increasing
electrolyte viscosity or exacerbating interfacial side reactions;
hence, traditional electrolytes that contain 1 M of the lithium salt
are practically limiting. Adjusting (increasing or decreasing) the
lithium-salt concentration has proven to be an effective way of
addressing this limitation. For example, increasing the lithium-
salt concentration can suppress the diffusion of LiPSs and build
an anion-induced solid−electrolyte interphase (SEI) or
cathode−electrolyte interphase (CEI), thereby improving the
cycling stability of the battery.6,7 On the other hand, reducing
the lithium salt concentration can reduce viscosity and optimize
interfacial reactions, thereby enhancing the utilization rate and
cycle life of the battery, especially under high-load and low-
temperature conditions.8−10 However, the mutual influence
mechanism between LiPS content and lithium-salt concen-
tration in these systems still requires clarification. Therefore,
exploring how the lithium-salt concentration impacts the LiPSs
and battery performance, especially under practical conditions,
is crucial.
This paper analyzes the performance of Li−S batteries that
use electrolyte systems with different lithium-salt concentra-
tions, reveals the key impact of electrolyte concentration, and
provides direction for the development of liquid electrolytes for
practical Li−S batteries. We first analyzed the performance of
Li−S batteries with various electrolyte concentrations, after
which the impact of concentration on the apparent parameters
of the electrolyte and the interactions between various
components in the electrolyte were investigated. Furthermore,
we analyzed the significant roles played by LiPSs in determining
the lithium-salt concentration and, consequently, the charge−
discharge processes in practical Li−S batteries. Through these
discussions, this paper aims to provide guidance for optimizing

The electrolyte, which functions as the ionic conductor that
transports lithium ions between the electrodes, is one of the four
key components of a lithium-ion battery. While the pursuit of
high energy densities has driven extensive research into active
electrode materials,11,12 electrolyte chemistry has been
developed somewhat more slowly, which has resulted in the
continued use of formulations developed during the early
commercialization of lithium-ion batteries; consequently, 1 M
has become the “standard” electrolyte salt-solution concen-
tration.13 This standard was primarily established because a 1 M
salt concentration was observed to typically result in the highest
bulk conductivity for most nonaqueous electrolyte systems. Bulk
conductivity is traditionally understood to depend on the
product of the number of lithium ions and their mobility. The
number of lithium ions dominates at low electrolyte lithium-salt
concentrations; consequently, increasing the concentration
increases electrolyte conductivity. However, ion aggregation
and the higher viscosity associated with a higher concentration
result in a larger solvated lithium-ion radius, which reduces ion
mobility and consequently lowers conductivity. Therefore,
electrolyte conductivity is determined by balancing the number
of lithium ions and their mobility, with an optimal relationship
existing between lithium-ion concentration and conductivity.
This optimal relationship led to a lithium-salt concentration of 1
M as the habitual choice for electrolytes.14 Consequently, early
mainstream electrolytes for Li−S batteries also adopted 1 M
LiTFSI. However, a variety of new electrolyte systems have been
developed in recent years to meet the practical requirements of
Li−S batteries, including systems based on high-concentration
electrolytes (HCEs), low-concentration electrolytes (LCEs),
and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs). The
impact that the electrolyte concentration on battery perform-
ance has garnered increasing attention.
The numbers of free solvent and solvent-separated ion pairs
(SSIPs) decrease as the lithium-salt concentration in the
electrolyte exceeds a certain critical range, whereas the numbers
of contact ion pairs (CIPs), aggregates (AGGs), and nano-
aggregates (n-AGGs) increase;15 this condition typically
corresponds to an HCE (Figure 1b). Research into Li−S
battery HCEs began in 2013 when Oh et al. discovered that 5 M
LiTFSI in tetraglyme (G4) as the electrolyte limited the
overcharge capacity to less than 10 mAh/g and delivered a
Coulombic efficiency (CE) that exceeded 99%.16 HCEs that
contain 7 M LiTFSI in 1:1 glyme (DME)/dioxolane (DOL)7
and 12 M LiFSI in DME17 also exhibited superior CEs; this
improved performance is attributable to the higher lithium-salt
concentration that leads to less LiPS dissolution and higher
electrolyte viscosity, thereby minimizing LiPS diffusion to the
anode and suppressing shuttling.
Apart from the combinations of fluorinated lithium salts and
ether solvents mentioned above, researchers have also studied
how altering the lithium salt or solvent in the HCE affects Li−S
battery performance. For instance, Zhang et al. used 3 M LiNO3
and 0.5 M LiTFSI as the lithium salts in diglyme (G2) as the
solvent to construct an HCE for Li−S batteries;18 this
electrolyte exhibited good lithium-anode deposition behavior
because the high concentration of LiNO3 supplements the SEI
during its breakdown. Additionally, compared with HCEs
containing pure LiTFSI, the high concentration of LiNO3
facilitated more LiPS dissolution, which ensured good reaction
kinetics at the cathode. In addition to changing the lithium salt,
studies have shown that HCEs that use nonether solvents, such
as esters19 and sulfones,20 can also reduce the number of solvent-
based side reactions and suppress LiPS diffusion. Due to the
different dielectric constants (ε) and donor number (DN)
values of these solvents, their solvation structures in HCE may
vary. A higher ε or DN value leads to stronger binding energies
between the solvent and lithium ions, typically requiring a higher
lithium salt concentration to form CIPs or AGGs, which in turn
reduces polysulfide dissolution.

batteries evidently offer several characteristics and advantages
compared with traditional electrolytes that are ascribable to

changes in solvation structure, namely: (1) less free solvent
leading to lower electrolyte volatility; (2) fewer free anions
leading to a higher lithium-ion transference number; (3) more
anion−Li+ associations resulting in a lower anionic lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy leading to the
formation of a salt-derived SEI; (4) greater solvent−Li+
coordination, which reduces the solvating abilities of the solvent
molecules; and (5) CIP and AGG, which promote the formation
of a 3D network structure that inhibits LiPS dissolution and
shuttling. However, HCEs also have some drawbacks compared
with traditional electrolytes. For example, HCEs have poorer
wettability, which weakens Li+ transfer at phase interfaces, while
increasing HCE viscosity reduces the ability to transport ions,
which is detrimental to reaction kinetics at the electrode; the
excessive use of lithium salts also incurs higher costs.
Although LiPSs directly cause shuttling in a Li−S battery,
their roles as electronic mediators in interfacial reactions at the
cathode cannot be overlooked.21,22 Researchers have proposed
an effective solution to develop LCE (Figure 1c), which
harnesses the benefits of LiPSs for promoting cathodic
interfacial reactions while avoiding the slow lithium-ion
migration associated with HCEs due to their high viscosities.
Recent studies have highlighted the advantages of LCEs in other
batteries.23 Hu et al. discovered that an “ultralow electrolyte
concentration” (0.3 M) not only offers cost advantages but also
expands the operational temperature range of sodium-ion
batteries (−30 to 55 °C).9 In contrast, Li−S batteries are
always accompanied by LiPSs during cycling. On the one hand,
LiPSs supplement the lithium-salt concentration in the LCE; on
the other hand, LCEs ensure that sufficient LiPSs that promote
interfacial reactions at the cathode are present. Therefore,
theoretically, LCEs have certain applications prospects for use in
Li−S batteries.
LCE research for Li−S batteries began in 2018. Sun’s group
reported reducing the concentration of the LiTFSI salt (to 0.5
M) in order to lower the viscosity of the electrolyte solution
(from 1.54 to 0.72 mPa·s), thereby enhancing the wettability of
self-supporting cathodes in pouch cells.24 Yushin et al.
subsequently reported that reducing the lithium-salt concen-
tration to 0.2 M resulted in comparable or superior rate
performance than that obtained using a standard 1 M electrolyte
owing to its lower viscosity (0.613 mPa·s) and demonstrated
comparable or better cycling performance.25 Additionally, Wang
et al. found that highly loaded Li−S batteries deliver better rate
and cycling performance at low concentrations (0.5 M) and
attributed the performance improvement to a superior SEI rich
in organic components.26
Wu et al. identified three key reasons for why LCEs improve
Li−S battery performance at low temperatures: (1) LCEs
regulate Li2S nucleation and growth, thereby promoting 3D Li2S
deposition; (2) LCEs inhibit LiPS cluster formation, which
accelerates reaction kinetics at the cathode; and (3) the LiPSs in
LCEs are able to better collaborate with LiNO3 to construct
dense SEIs that inhibit shuttling.8 Furthermore, Yan et al.
reported that the energy barrier for lithium-ion desolvation is
positively correlated with the lithium salt concentration at
concentrations below 1 M.27
The examples mentioned above challenge the traditional
perception that LCEs are poorly stable and promote the
formation of lithium dendrites. The advantages associated with
the viscosity-lowering behavior of an LCE can be fully utilized,
while its negative impact on electrolyte conductivity can be
overlooked as long as a good passivation film is formed on the
electrode surface.28 However, this notion is controversial and
requires further research and validation. Additionally, Li−S
batteries with LCEs will encounter the appearance and
disappearance of more LiPSs under practical conditions.
Although some studies have attempted to reveal the relationship
between the LCE and the amount of LiPSs present, the
discussions remain unclear or even contradictory. By adjusting
parameters such as the ε and DN value of the solvent, the
solvation structure of polysulfides can be regulated. This
approach holds promise for achieving stable interface con-
struction in LCEs containing polysulfides, thereby improving
the cycling stability of the battery. Nonetheless, LCEs offer an
attractive developmental direction for practical Li−S batteries
owing to their excellent wettabilities and low costs, which are
crucial for the manufacturing and large-scale promotion of Li−S
batteries.
The development of LHCEs represents another significant
research direction, which involves introducing weakly solvating
diluents. LHCEs retain the microstructures of the correspond-
ing HCEs while overcoming issues associated with their high
viscosities (Figure 1d). Although the LHCE concept has not
been established for long,29 the unique electrolyte structure of
an LHCE offers significant design flexibility that potentially
satisfies multiple electrolyte performance requirements.
The introduction of diluents into Li−S batteries began in
2015 when Watanabe et al. added 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE), a hydrofluoroether
(HFE), to a G4-based ionic-liquid electrolyte and examined
its effect on Li−S battery performance.30 This study inspired the
development of a variety of LHCEs with new diluents, such as
1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether
(OFE),31 2,2-dimethyl-3,6,9-trioxa-2-siladecane,32 hexafluoroi-
sopropyl methyl ether (HFME),33 fluorobenzene (FB),34 and
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl methyl ether (TME).35 These electro-
lytes were found to reduce LiPS dissolution and suppress the
growth of lithium dendrites to deliver high CEs and stable
cycling performance.
In a similar manner to an LCE in which LiPSs partially
replenish lithium ions in the electrolyte during cycling, LHCEs
with total salt concentrations of 1 M or even lower have also
attracted significant research attention. Zhang et al. developed a
series of encapsulating LiPS electrolytes (EPSEs) with total salt
concentrations of 1 M based on new diluents that differ from
traditional HFE but are also weakly solvating, including
isopropyl ether (DIPE),36 diisopropyl sulfide (DIPS),37 and
hexyl methyl ether (HME).38,39 These electrolytes can impede
parasitic reactions between lithium anodes and LiPSs without
affecting the cathodic conversion kinetics. In addition, electro-
lytes with a total salt concentration below 1 M can also be
achieved by increasing the diluent content; such electrolytes are
potentially compatible with interfacial reactions at cathodes and
anodes. For instance, Wang et al. developed a low-concentration
LHCE (0.3 M LiFSI in 0.06:0.94 DME:TTE) that inhibited
LiPS shuttling and provided better rate performance.40 Addi-
tionally, the introduction of diluents into conventional or LCEs
has attracted research attention. For example, Yushin et al.
studied the impact of adding HFE (as the diluent) to a 0.1 M
lithium-salt electrolyte on battery performance and observed
that the addition of HFE increased both the capacity and CE of
the battery.41 Lu et al. introduced n-hexane (NH), as a nonpolar
diluent, into a conventional electrolyte to create a low-
concentration diluted electrolyte with fast lithium-ion diffusion
that stabilized the lithium anode and reduced LiPS dissolution.42

In addition to developing and optimizing new LHCEs,
incorporating other functional components can further enhance
performance. For instance, Xin et al. added trimethylsilyl azide
(TMS-N3), as a film-forming additive, to a DME/TTE-based
LHCE, which led to improved temperature adaptabilities and
cycling stabilities of Li-SPAN batteries via the formation of
stable interfaces on both the cathode and anode, thereby
delivering high capacities and long cycling lives.43
Based on the discussion precented above, LHCEs evidently
exhibit many advantages similar to those of HCEs, such as fewer
side reactions and stabilized electrode interfaces. However,
LHCEs need to be further developed by addressing potential
issues before they can be used in Li−S batteries. First, safety
concerns need to be addressed because diluents typically have
low boiling points, which may lead to gas formation and related
problems. Second, density and cost issues associated with
fluorination must be addressed, with the development of new
defluorinated diluents potentially providing an effective
solution. Researchers have recently proposed criteria that are
expected to aid in the development of new diluents. For
example, Huang et al. suggested that diluents should be selected
based on the relative binding energy between the diluent and the
main solvent as well as the ε value.44 It is emphasized that the
low dielectric environment afforded by diluents enhances the
interaction between anions and cations, promoting greater
anion recruitment into the Li solvation sheath to form AGGs
and therefore facilitates anion-derived inorganic-rich SEI,
ultimately improving the cycling stability. Wang et al. suggested
that the DN value of the diluent should be less than 10 to keep
LHCE structures and endow peculiar anion-derived SEI.45
Based on a summary of a large number of samples, it was found
that compared to dilute solutions, diluents in concentrated
electrolytes should be designed based on DN values rather than
ε, as the electrolyte can no longer be considered as a dielectric
continuum, and the local interactions between salt ions, main
solvents, and diluents determine the local solvation structure.
Finally, side reactions are also concerning, with some studies
revealing that strong coordination between an ether solvent and
an alkali metal leads to solvation that promotes electrophilic
reactions involving the fluorinated ether diluents in an LHCE.46
Moreover, the microstructures and lithium-transport proper-
ties of LHCEs warrant further investigation. Recent studies have
shown that LHCEs have micelle-like structures, which
broadened our understanding of LHCE microstructures.47
Additionally, the optimal ratio between the diluent and the
main solvent must be considered to ensure proper electrolyte
viscosity, ion transport, and reaction kinetics at the cathode.
McCloskey et al. reported that, while diluents reduce viscosity,
they also lower the lithium-ion transference number while not
significantly increasing conductivity.48 In addition, Zhang et al.
optimized the HFE content and showed that LHCEs with
moderate solvation degrees balance the kinetics at the cathode
and anode, with improvements in the cycling performance of
working Li−S batteries also observed.49 These new findings are
expected to promote the development of LHCEs and call for
further mechanistic research and discoveries. In particular, the
microinteraction mechanisms involving LHCEs and LiPSs need
to be elucidated for Li−S batteries.

Based on the Stokes−Einstein equation,51 Kohlrausch’s
Law,52 and the Nernst−Planck equation,53 factors that influence
lithium-ion conductivity (σLi+) in an electrolyte include
temperature (T), concentration (c), potential gradient (E/l),
concentration gradient (c/x), viscosity (η), and lithium-ion
transference number (tLi+). With a deeper understanding of
electrolyte chemistry by researchers in recent years,54 the degree
of lithium-salt dissociation (k), interion electrostatic interaction
potential (V), and desolvation energy barrier (Esol) will also have
a significant impact on lithium transport processes. However, it
is currently challenging to incorporate these factors into precise
mathematical formulas. To better understand the impact of
concentration and other apparent parameters on ion transport,
we have integrated these parametersbased on their facilitative
or inhibitory effects on lithium transportinto the numerator
or denominator, resulting in the following empirical relation-
ships.

The electrolyte solvation structure transitions from SSIP to
CIP and then to AGG and n-AGG with increasing
concentration. The lithium-ion transport mechanism depends
on the solvation structure. For example, lithium ions are
predominantly transported via vehicular mechanisms at low
concentrations, and via structural mechanisms at high
concentrations.55 The influencing factors play different roles
in the various transport mechanisms; therefore, determining the
critical position for transition of the solvation structure is crucial
for analyzing the impacts of these factors. Researches have
shown that 3−5 M is the critical concentration range for
solvation-structure transition in various electrolyte systems,57
which depends on the molar volume of the solvent; this
transition point typically corresponds to a lithium -to-solvent
molar ratio of 1:4,58,59 beyond which CIP structures inevitably
form (Figure 2a).
The impact of c/x on bulk σLi+ in equilibrium is relatively
minor at a given temperature for a low-concentration system
with a fully dissociated lithium salt. Moreover, E/l is constant for
a specific electrode composition and test conditions. Therefore,
these two factors can be ignored when discussing bulk σLi+.

increasing concentration before reaching the critical concen-

understanding and reflects the competition between the number
of lithium ions and their transference number. During this phase,
lithium-ion transport follows the vehicular mechanism, and the
desolvation energy barrier has little impact on bulk σLi+.
Comparatively, increases in c and tLi+ significantly enhance
bulk σLi+, whereas increases in η and V reduce it. Therefore, the
primary factors that influence electrolyte conductivity can be

While the two above-mentioned relationships are quite
similar, whether or not interactions between the solvent and
lithium ions60 are considered is the key difference. It is worth
noting that V (in the denominator) is significantly larger beyond
the critical range, while η is generally higher and continues to
increase with concentration, leading to an overall decline in σLi+.
Ion transport at the interface can also be understood using the
above relationships. While bulk conductivity is commonly used
to judge rate performance, lithium-ion transport at the
electrode/electrolyte interface is the true determinant of
charging capability. Studies have shown that the activation
energy for interfacial lithium-ion transfer (50 kJ/mol) is
significantly higher than that for lithium-ion migration in the
bulk phase (<30 kJ/mol).61 Unlike bulk ion migration, the
interfacial c/x value, related to lithium-ion consumption and
generation at the interface, cannot be ignored. Additionally, the
E/l parameter at the interface, influenced by the electric double
layer at the electrode/electrolyte boundary, is also critical.62
Additionally, desolvation occurs at the interface irrespective of
concentration, which necessitates further consideration. Factors
such as wettability, chemical reactions involving anions and
cations, and their effects on this process must also be discussed.
For instance, during charging at room temperature, lithium ions
deintercalate from the cathode into the electrolyte, increasing c
at the cathode/electrolyte interface and causing concentration
polarization (Δc) (Figure 2b). Simultaneously, lithium ions
intercalate into the anode, reducing c at the anode/electrolyte
interface and further intensifying Δc. At the cathode, the
interfacial potential (φ) of the electric double layer and E/l are
aligned, promoting ion transport. In contrast, at the anode, they
oppose each other, hindering ion transport. Furthermore, η
determines wettability and, consequently, uniformity of the local
electric field distribution at the interface. A higher η value
generally results in poorer wettability and more-difficult

The electric double layer plays a key role in determining
interfacial ion transport. Chemical reactions or specific
adsorptions on the electrode surface, which significantly affect
the double layer, therefore critically affect interfacial σLi+. Strong
interactions between lithium ions and the electrode increase c/l
and decrease V and Esol. For example, alloying reactions can
significantly reduce interfacial impedance, thereby bypassing
desolvation limitations. The value of φ can also be adjusted by
adding high-valence metal cations62 and utilizing their
adsorption on the electrode surface to influence interfacial
lithium-ion transport. Moreover, the formation of electronically
insulating SEI and CEI layers affects the double-layer structure,
with each case requiring specific analysis.
The above relationships show how various parameters
influence ion transport in the bulk and at interfaces under
different conditions. However, determining the relative
importance of each parameter in an actual system is difficult.
For example, while an increase in c can enhance ion transport at
the interface by increasing c/l, it can also decrease ion transport
by increasing η; consequently, how conditional changes impact
the overall outcome is challenging to predict. In practice, the ion
transport process in the electrolyte is primarily governed by the
change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of the system before and after
lithium-ion transport.63 A ΔG < 0 indicates that the specific
condition favors ion transport. Since ΔG is additive, researchers
can combine the ΔG values associated with each parameter
when external conditions change to assess their collective impact
on the overall outcome.
According to the relationship: ΔG = ΔH −TΔS, any change
involves a competition between changes in entropy and enthalpy
(ΔS and ΔH, respectively). Increasing or decreasing the
concentration is a mixing or demixing process that corresponds
to an increase or decrease in entropy. However, ΔH impacts
local ion transport more than ΔS for an electrolyte already in
equilibrium. For example, the ΔH of solvation is usually 5−5.6
eV for an organic electrolyte, while ΔS usually lies between only
−0.5 and −1 meV/K.64 Therefore, ΔH is usually only
considered to simplify analysis.
Factors that affect ΔH in an electrolyte include cation−
solvent interactions (Ucation−sol), cation−anion interactions
(Ucation−anion), anion−solvent interactions (Uanion−sol), and
solvent−solvent interaction (Usol−sol). The strengths of these
interactions can be judged from ion−ion (Uion−ion), ion−dipole
(Uion−dipole), and dipole−dipole (Udipole−dipole) interaction
paradigms according to classical physical models, with
interaction strength decreasing in the order given above at the
same distance.65 Analyzing these microscopic interactions
enables changes in ΔG to be more effectively quantified.
Existing studies have shown that these interactions and
nonlinear regression methods can be used to predict σLi+ and
η relatively well,66 which demonstrates that apparent parameters
can be analyzed and regulated through these simple
intermolecular interactions.
The anions of lithium salts usually have large structures with
relatively delocalized charges; consequently, they usually exhibit
small ion−dipole interactions with solvents. Consequently,
previous studies have focused more on stronger interactions,
such as Ucation−sol in dilute solutions and Ucation−anion in
concentrated solutions. However, as electrolyte chemistry
develops, an increasing number of researchers are paying
attention to how weak interactions in electrolytes impact battery
performance, including Uanion−sol, Usol−sol, and other long-range
interactions. Notably, Ming et al. systematically studied
electrolyte solvation chemistry and interfacial models and
revealed that weak interactions in electrolytes can significantly
impact ion transport at interfaces under specific condi-
tions.56,67,68 Although Ucation−anion increases most significantly
as concentration increases after the critical point, the absolute
values of the other interactions (Uanion−sol, Usol−sol) also increase,
which implies that what were previously considered to be weak
interactions become increasingly important. Therefore, how

The characteristics of the LiPS-dissolved electrolyte deter-
mine how LiPSs change dynamically during charging and
discharging in a Li−S battery. Traditional studies have focused
on scenarios in which the amount of cathode active material is
relatively low and the electrolyte content is high, where the
LiPSs impact in a manner that primarily accelerates reactions at
the cathodic interface69 and corrodes the lithium anode, with ion
transport affected in a relatively minor way. However, as Li−S
battery technology progresses toward practical applications,70
the demands for higher sulfur loadings and reduced electrolyte
volumes significantly amplify the impact of LiPS dissolution on
the electrolyte’s chemical properties. For example, with an E/S
ratio of 3 mL/mg (assuming all LiPSs are present as Li2S6), the
sulfur cathode is fully converted by dissolving 1.74 M of Li2S6 in
the electrolyte, leading to a high lithium-ion concentration of up
to 3.48 M. When considering the original lithium salt in the
electrolyte, the total ion concentration clearly surpasses the
critical range of the solvation structure. For instance, the critical
point (1:4) corresponds to a lithium-ion concentration of
approximately 2.41 M in pure DME, while in pure DOL it is
around 3.58 M. Such phenomena, occurring beyond the critical
solvation range, significantly influence various apparent
parameters and interactions among components. Therefore,
understanding and controlling the LiPS dissolution process,
particularly under conditions of high sulfur loadings and low
electrolyte volumes, is crucial for optimizing battery perform-
ance and stability.
LiPSs dissociate to a lower degree than traditional lithium
salts; hence, the LiPS-dissolution process is akin to increasing c
and augmenting Ucation−anion. Based on the previous analysis and
the relationship σLi+ ∝A″•[k•c•tLi+•(E/l)•(c/x)]/(η•V•Esol),
LiPS dissolution in the electrolyte will increase c, c/x, η, and V
while decreasing k and Esol, which impacts the component
interaction parameters in two stages: before the critical range,
where Ucation‑sol and Uanion‑sol increase (as evidenced by an
increase in quantity), Ucation−anion strengthens (as evidenced by a
smaller distance), and Usol‑sol decreases, and beyond the critical
range, where Ucation−anion increases, while Uanion‑sol, Ucation‑sol, and
Usol‑sol decrease. The “decrease” mentioned for the solvent-
related terms refers to the relative reduction in the contribution
of each parameter to ΔH in the microscopic solvation structure;
however, the sum of the solvent interaction terms increases
overall owing to the concentration effect. Continuous increases
in η and V further decrease bulk σLi+ as more LiPSs dissolve and c
further increases as a consequence. The Esol term in the
structural transport mechanism becomes important, and the V
term becomes particularly crucial, especially after surpassing the
critical range, both of which further lower bulk σLi+.
Interfacial lithium-ion transport still follows the relationship
σLi+ ∝A‴•[k•c•tLi+•((E ± φ)/l)•((c+Δc)/x)]/(η•V•Esol);
however, LiPS dissolution is akin to storing lithium ions in the
electrolyte during discharging. At this time, disulfide bond
interactions between sulfur atoms in solid sulfur determine the
value of ΔH for LiPS formation in the solution. Given the
relatively low energy of the disulfide bond (251 kJ/mol),71 the
LiPSs usually dissolve easily for very electronically conductive
electrodes. LiPS dissolution favors lithium-ion transport at the
interface under these circumstances. LiPSs also dissolve at the
cathode during charging, which involves interfacial lithium-ion
transport from solid Li2S to solution LiPSs, which is essentially
the reverse of the lithium-ion migration process from the
electrode to the electrolyte; therefore, a higher Esol facilitates this
process in this phase, which may provide a reason for why high-
polarity solvents that stabilize LiPSs accelerate the conversion of
Li2S into LiPSs. Additionally, LiPS dissolution itself does not
particularly affect interfacial ion transport during charging on the
lithium anode side, which is similar to the effect observed when c
increases. However, chemical reactions of the LiPSs with the
lithium anode during charging leads to the continuous reverse
migration of lithium ions from the anode to the electrolyte
owing to shuttling, which often becomes the main contributor to
the ΔH for interfacial lithium-ion transport at this time. It should
be noted that while only ΔH associated with local interactions is
considered here, diffusion driven by ΔS following LiPS
dissolution also benefits interfacial ion transport, although this
effect is similarly overshadowed by chemical reactions between
the LiPSs and the lithium anode. Furthermore, the solvation
structure and transport mechanism change under practical
conditions in which the LiPS concentration is very high, which
increases the significance of other factors related to the higher c
in the above relationships, such as η, V, and φ. Therefore,
although the positive impact of LiPSs on electronic transport is
more notable than its effect on lithium-ion transport under mild
conditions, and their dissolution is generally favorable for
lithium-ion transport at the cathodic interface, the continuous
dissolution of LiPSs under practical conditions not only reduces
their capabilities as liquid-phase electronic mediators but also

Conversely, reducing the amount of LiPSs is similar to
lowering c, which has the opposite impact on bulk σLi+ to that
observed when more LiPSs are present. Specifically, this
reduction during discharging implies that LiPSs are converted
into Li2S, which is an intercalation process. Here, LiPS reduction
leads to decreases in (E ± φ)/l, (c+Δc)/x, V, tLi+, and η;
therefore, the conversion of LiPSs into Li2S affects interfacial
transport from the electrolyte to the electrode, which depends
on the comprehensive ΔH value for these parameters. Note that
solid Li2S is nonconductive, and its solid deposition may result
in passivation that significantly affects interfacial lithium-ion
transport. The reduction in the amount of LiPSs corresponds to
their conversion into sulfur during charging, which involves
simultaneously desulfurizing and de-electronizing the LiPSs
dissolved in the electrolyte. However, in a similar manner to
Li2S, sulfur is also an ionic and electronic insulator, which
implies that sulfur deposition during charging significantly
affects interfacial mass transport.
In general, because LiPSs primarily exist in the electrolyte
phase and are transported therein, their impact on bulk ion
transport can be analyzed based on the relationship for bulk
lithium-ion transport or corresponding changes in ΔG.
Interfacial mass transport is a typical interfacial mass-transfer
chemical-reaction process because LiPSs are reduced to Li2S/
Li2S2 or oxidized to S at the interface. Therefore, the effect of
electrolyte concentration on interfacial LiPS transfer and
reaction should be considered from the perspectives of mass-
transfer processes, potential distributions, and interfacial
chemical-reaction components. Theoretically, these relation-
ships can be analyzed according to the aforementioned
interfacial Li-ion transport relationships. However, factors
such as the SEI film on the anode and the network of conductive
additives/sulfur in the cathode in a practical battery system will
significantly affect the local distributions of E and φ at the
interface, which complicates analysis of the contributions of the
interactions among components. Nevertheless, the effect of
LiPS dissolution and disappearance on the lithium-transport
process can still be explored by qualitatively analyzing the impact
of increasing and decreasing the amount of LiPSs on various
interactions (such as Ucation−anion, Uanion‑sol, Ucation‑sol, Usol‑sol) and
related apparent parameters (such as η, V, etc.), thereby
providing a valuable perspective for understanding the complex
ion-transport processes in Li−S batteries.
In summary, we systematically analyzed how electrolyte
concentration affects the performance of Li−S batteries. This
article first compared the characteristics of systems with different
electrolyte concentrations, including HCEs, LCEs, and LHCEs.
Relationships between concentration and lithium transport in
the bulk and at the interface, as well as associated analytical
strategies were discussed. In addition, the importance of LiPS
dissolution under practical conditions for the aforementioned
transport processes and battery performance was also discussed.
Regulating the electrolyte concentration was found to be crucial
for practical Li−S batteries, and developing LCE and LHCE
systems should be a future research focus. The following points
require further research attention to provide further guidance
when designing electrolyte systems that promote the develop-
ment of practical Li−S batteries.
1. LCE and LHCE systems need further systematic study:
The mechanisms used by LCEs to enhance the rate and
low-temperature performance of practical Li−S batteries
should be investigated. Costs and other issues need to be
considered when determining the optimal electrolyte-
concentration range under practical conditions. Further-
more, how diluents impact electrolyte chemistry and
battery performance in the LHCE system need to be
investigated, new low-cost diluents should be developed,
and LHCEs with total salt concentrations below 1 M
should be designed. The diluent/main-solvent combina-
tion and proportions are required to maintain solvated
lithium-unit continuity, balance viscosity and lithium-ion
transfer number, and provide optimal conditions for
lithium-ion transport. Relationships for LiPSs, LCEs, and
LHCEs need to balance LiPS shuttling and its accelerating
effect on positive-electrode reactions.
2. Quantitative models need to be developed: Quantitative
relationships and models that involve solvent-structure-
related, apparent, and battery-performance parameters
should be establish. Cui et al. recently reported that the
solvation free energy and natural logarithm of the LiPS
dissociation constant are negatively correlated,72 which
further affects the CE and initial capacity of the battery.
This example highlights the need to systematically explore
quantitative relationships between interaction-related
parameters and apparent parameters for dissolution/
solvation processes and the performance parameters of
Li−S batteries, which is expected to ultimately promote
their practical development.
3. Li2S/S passivation of the cathode needs to be reduced:
Reducing cathode passivation by Li2S and S is crucial
because these species are major obstacles to interfacial
mass transfer. The deposition of Li2S and other
influencing factors during desulfurization of the electro-
lyte and sulfurization of the cathode during charging are
significant because they accelerate the conversion of
LiPSs into Li2S/S and weaken LiPS shuttling. For
example, strategies that construct liquid-phase S or Li2S
to mitigate passivation or regulate the 3D deposition
behavior of solid products, use additives to weaken the
binding of solvated LiPSs to sulfur atoms, or regulate
cathode components leading to new chemical interactions
that improve the dependence of the cathode on S−S
interactions, are worth researching in the future.
