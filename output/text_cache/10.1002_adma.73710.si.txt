Experimental procedures
1.1 Materials
All raw materials were commercially sourced and used without further purification or modification. Specifically, the materials included: lithium (Li, China Energy Lithium Industry Co., Ltd.), carbon paper (CP, Toray Industries, Inc., Japan), sulfur powder (Shanghai Guangnuo Chemical Co., Ltd.), lithium sulfide (Li2S, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.), N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, HSV-900, Shenzhen Kejing Electronics Co., Ltd.), Super P (Kelude New Material Technology Co., Ltd.), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, Kelude New Material Technology Co., Ltd.), and diisopropyl dithiocarbonate (DIP, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.)
1.2 Preparation of the Li2S cathode 
Commercial lithium sulfide powder and MWCNTs were weighed and mixed at a mass ratio of 7:3. Prior to mixing, the MWCNTs powder was dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C for over 48 hours, and then transferred together with the Li2S powder into a stainless-steel grinding jar inside an argon-filled glovebox. The mixed powders were subjected to high-energy ball milling at a rotational speed of 550 revolutions per minute (rpm), with zirconia balls used as solvent-free milling media to reduce the particle size of Li2S and achieve homogeneous mixing of the materials. To prevent overheating of the system during milling, the ball-milling process was conducted in a cyclic mode of "5 minutes of milling followed by 5 minutes of rest", with a total milling duration of 6 hours.
For the subsequent preparation of the Li2S cathode slurry: the aforementioned Li2S-MWCNTs mixed powder (80 wt.%), additional MWCNTs as a conductive additive (10 wt.%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) (10 wt.%) were thoroughly mixed. After stirring to form a homogeneous slurry, it was coated onto an aluminum foil substrate and dried at room temperature for 48 hours. Finally, the dried aluminum foil was cut into circular pieces with a diameter of 10 mm to serve as the Li2S cathode.
1.3 Preparation of the electrolytes.
The conventional electrolyte was composed of 1 mol L-1 lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 2 wt.% lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME). To this base electrolyte, DIP was added at different concentration gradients of 0.01 mol L-1, 0.05 mol L-1, and 0.1 mol L-1, respectively. After preparation, all modified electrolytes with varying DIP concentrations were sealed and vigorously stirred for 24 hours to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneous dispersion of DIP in the base electrolyte system. The electrolytes were only used for subsequent battery assembly and testing after achieving a uniform and stable state.
1.3.1Preparation of 0.2 M Li2S6 for the LiPSs catalytic experiment. 
The 0.2 mol L-1 Li2S6 electrolyte was prepared by reacting S8 and Li2S at a molar ratio of 5:1 in 1 mol L-1 LiTFSI in TEGDME, with or without DIP incorporated. The mixed system was then placed in a constant-temperature environment at 60 °C and continuously stirred for 48 hours until the solid-phase reaction proceeded to completion and the solution exhibited a homogeneous reddish-brown color.
1.3.2Preparation of 0.2 M Li2S8 for the Li2S nucleation and decomposition.
The 0.2 mol L-1 Li2S8 electrolyte was prepared by reacting S8 and Li2S at a molar ratio of 7:1 in 1 mol L-1 LiTFSI in TEGDME, with or without DIP incorporated. The mixed system was then placed in a constant-temperature environment at 60 °C and continuously stirred for 48 hours until the solid-phase reaction proceeded to completion and the solution exhibited a homogeneous reddish-brown color.
1.4 Electrochemical measurements.
The assembly of CR2032 coin cells was conducted entirely in an argon-filled glovebox. The cathode was the aforementioned Li2S electrode, while the anode was selected based on the battery configuration: lithium metal foil for lithium-metal battery systems or copper foil for full-cell systems. Celgard 2400 was used as the separator, and the electrolyte was Li-S electrolyte with or without DIP (1 mol L-1 LiTFSI in TEGDME, with 2 wt% LiNO3). Specifically, for coin cells, the E/S ratio was 15 μL mg⁻1 and the Li2S loading was ~1 mg cm⁻2; for pouch cells, the E/S ratio was 4.9 μL mg⁻1 and the sulfur loading was 8 mg cm⁻2.Galvanostatic charge-discharge tests were performed using a Neware BTS battery testing system. Prior to formal testing, the Li2S cathode underwent an activation cycle: charged to 3.4 V and discharged to 1.8 V at a rate of C/20 (1 C = 1166 mA g-1). After activation, subsequent cycling tests were carried out within the voltage range of 1.8–2.8 V at various C-rates.
Electrochemical characterizations were performed using a CHI 660e electrochemical workstation. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were recorded in the potential range of 1.8–2.8 V (vs Li⁺/Li) with scan rates of 0.1–0.5 mV s-1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted over the frequency range of 0.01–105 Hz. For Li∥Li symmetric cells, long-term cycling tests were performed continuously for 700 hours under the conditions of 1 mA cm-2 current density and 1 mAh cm-2 capacity.
1.5 Li2S6 symmetrical battery testing 
The assembly of Li2S6 symmetric cells was carried out in an argon-filled glovebox, with carbon paper serving as the counter electrode. During assembly, 10 μL of Li2S6 solution and 10 μL of modified electrolyte were added to the two sides of the separator, respectively, to ensure sufficient wetting of the separator and electrodes by the electrolytes. Cyclic voltammetry measurements were performed using an electrochemical workstation. The potential window was set to -1~ 1 V, and the scan rate was controlled at 5 mV s-1.
1.6 Li2S deposition decomposition testing
Li2S8 batteries were assembled for Li2S deposition experiments, where carbon paper was used as the cathode and metallic lithium foil as the anode. The electrolyte system consisted of a mixture of 20 μL 0.2 mol L-1 Li2S8 solution and 20 μL modified electrolyte.
The Li2S deposition process was conducted as follows: the battery was first galvanostatically discharged to 2.05 V at a constant current of 0.112 mA, and then switched to the potentiostatic discharge mode at 2.02 V for another 2500 s to complete the deposition of Li2S on the carbon paper electrode.
Batteries with the same configuration as those used in the Li2S deposition experiment were employed to evaluate the Li2S decomposition performance. Specifically, the battery was initially galvanostatically discharged to 1.7 V at 0.112 mA, followed by further discharge at a small current of 0.011 mA until the voltage dropped below 1.7 V to ensure complete deposition of Li2S on the electrode surface. Subsequently, the battery was galvanostatically charged at a constant voltage of 2.4 V for 4000 s, and the voltage-time curves were recorded.
1.7 Characterizations
The structure and phase composition of samples were investigated by powder X-ray diffraction (MiniFlex 600, Japan) with Cu K-α radiation (λ=0.154 nm). The valence states and chemical composition and were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) using Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi. The nitrogen adsorption desorption isotherms and pore size distributions of as-prepared samples were investigated by Micromeritics ASAP2460 (USA) instrument measured at 77.3 k. UV-vis spectra were performed on Shimadzu UV-2600 (SHIMADZU, Japan) spectrophotometer to investigate the adsorption tests.
1.8 Density functional theory (DFT) calculations
Theoretical calculations of the geometric and electronic structures of molecular entities involved in this study were all performed using the Gaussian 16 package. Initial geometry optimization was carried out at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level of theory. Harmonic vibrational frequency analyses were performed at the same level to confirm that each optimized geometry corresponds to a true local minimum (ground state) on the potential energy surface, as evidenced by the absence of imaginary frequencies. The most stable configurations were selected for subsequent interaction energy and electronic structure analyses. To further verify the accuracy and reliability of our calculated results, higher‑level single‑point energy corrections were performed using the M06‑2X/def2‑TZVP theoretical level. DFT‑D3 dispersion correction was applied to account for long‑range van der Waals interactions. Solvation effects were considered using the SMD implicit solvation model to simulate the TEGDME electrolyte environment, ensuring reliable interaction energies and molecular geometries under realistic liquid electrolyte conditions.
For all optimized structures, we performed vibrational frequency analyses at the same theoretical level. Zero‑point energy (ZPE) and entropy (S) at 298.15 K were obtained using the Shermo program.The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for each elementary step of the sulfur reduction reaction was calculated according to the standard thermodynamic formula:ΔG = ΔE + ΔZPE – TΔS, where ΔE is the DFT total energy difference, ΔZPE is the zero‑point energy correction, and ΔS is the entropy change derived from vibrational frequencies. The final Gibbs free energy was obtained by adding the single‑point energy to the thermal correction term. This procedure explicitly accounts for entropic contributions (–TΔS) and thus ensures a rigorous treatment of the reaction thermodynamics.
The calculations of relative structural energies and adsorption energies of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) were based on the electronic energies of relevant species corrected by ZPE. The adsorption energy of LiPSs is defined by the formula: Eb=EAB−(EA+EB), where Eb is the binding energy, EAB is the total energy of the combined system of A and B, and EA and EB are the energies of A and B in their isolated states, respectively.
The ground states of different Li2S and DIP-SxLi (x = 1,2,4,6,8) were optimized at the CAM-B3LYP level using the Gaussian 16 program. Harmonic frequency calculations were also performed at the same theoretical level to confirm that the optimized geometries correspond to local minima. Three-dimensional (3D) plots of molecular orbitals were generated and visualized using the Multiwfn and VMD programs.

Figure S1. This figure demonstrates that the dosage of DIP additive must match the electrolyte system. Specifically, the 0.02 M DIP group exhibits limited adsorption and catalytic effects on LiPSs due to insufficient additive content, failing to suppress LiPSs shuttling and active material loss, thus leading to fast capacity decay. The 0.08 M DIP group shows a slight capacity increase benefiting from its sulfur-containing property, yet excessive DIP triggers side reactions and increases electrolyte viscosity, which impairs the cycling stability. In contrast, the 0.05 M DIP group achieves optimal compatibility with the electrolyte and LiPSs: it not only effectively regulates LiPSs to inhibit the shuttling effect but also avoids side effects caused by excessive additives, resulting in the highest cyclic capacity retention rate. Therefore, unless otherwise specified, 0.05 M is selected as the optimal DIP concentration for subsequent experiments.
