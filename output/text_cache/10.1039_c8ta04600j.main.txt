rich polyethylene oxide-like species in a solid-
electrolyte interphase in a polysulﬁde/carbonate
electrolyte†

The formation of Li dendrites is eﬀectively inhibited by utilizing a small
amount of polysulﬁde (1 to 2 mM Li2Sx, 2 # x # 8) in a conventional
carbonate electrolyte to introduce a signiﬁcant amount of poly-
ethylene oxide (PEO)-like polymers into solid-electrolyte interphase
(SEI) ﬁlms. The polysulﬁde added likely acts as a catalyst rather than an
additive,
which
is
not
consumed
during
repeated
charging/
discharging cycles. Thus the stability of Li-metal batteries during
prolonged cycling is remarkably improved.
Introduction
To meet the rapid growth in demand for energy density, the
replacement of current commercial graphite anodes with
lithium (Li) metal anodes has become an important research
direction in the eld of Li-ion batteries.1–4 However, Li dendrites
unavoidably form on the surface of the Li metal anode during
repeated charging/discharging cycles in liquid electrolytes,
causing undesired accumulation of “dead Li”, short circuiting,
and, consequently, loss of anode capacity.5,6 In recent years,
many research groups have made great eﬀorts towards inhib-
iting the formation of Li dendrites.1,7–12 Some novel approaches
have been proposed, e.g., adding an articial solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI) (boron nitride, graphene)13–16 or packaging Li
metal into a 3D structured substrate (nickel foam, carbon cloth
or
hollow
carbon).3,7,17
However,
these
methods
cannot
completely prevent the side reactions between the electrolyte
and the Li anode, and an undesirable SEI forms, particularly
aer prolonged cycles or storage of Li-metal batteries.18–20
Therefore, to obtain good cycling performance in Li metal
batteries, the SEI composition should be optimized, and the
stability of the SEI components should be enhanced, while the
side reactions related to forming unstable species in SEI lms
should be minimized.
Carbonate-based electrolytes are commonly used in state-of-
the-art 4 V Li-ion batteries;21 unfortunately, the highly chemical
reactivity of Li metal towards carbonate-based electrolytes leads
to forming non-uniform and unstable SEI lms, together with
the growth of Li dendrites.22 It is demonstrated that the
intrinsic SEI lms formed in carbonate-based electrolytes
consist of various organic compounds (oligomers and poly-
mers) and inorganic species, such as ROCO2Li, PEO, LiF,
Li2CO3, LiOH and Li2O.23 Among these species, the relatively
stable polymers (e.g., PEO) and LiF are believed to not only
increase the mechanical exibility and chemical stability of the
SEI lm but also play an important role in the inhibition of Li
dendrite growth.21,24 However, the polymer content in the SEI
lms formed in carbonate-based electrolytes is limited.21,24
The formation of polymers in the SEI lms, e.g., PEO, is
attributed to the ring opening reactions of ethylene carbonate
(EC) and the subsequent polymerization process.23 The kinetics
of the EC ring opening is usually slow in carbonate electrolytes;
therefore, the amount of PEO formed on the surface of a Li anode
is usually limited.23 However, if electrolyte additives such as u-
oroethylene carbonate or vinylidene carbonate are added into
carbonate electrolytes, the additives can preferentially undergo
the ring-opening process instead of EC, resulting in an increased
concentration of PEO-like polymers.25 Unfortunately, these elec-
trolyte additives are usually consumed during subsequent cycles,
making them unsuitable for prolonged cycling of Li anodes.25
In this paper, we investigated the cycling performance and
morphology of Li metal electrodes in Li-metal batteries by
adding a small amount of polysulde (Li2Sx, 2 # x # 8) into
carbonate-based electrolytes. Gao et al. reported that when EC
was used as an electrolyte component in Li–S batteries, poly-
sulde reacted with the EC at room temperature, which

Results and discussion
The battery in this work consisted of LiFePO4 (LFP) as the
cathode electrode, a Li plate electrode as the anode and
a commercial membrane, producing an LFPrLi conguration.
The carbonate-based electrolytes containing polysulde were
prepared by the mixing method shown in Fig. 1a. Li2S and S
powders (mole ratio ¼ 1 : 7) were initially added into dimethyl
ether (DME) to obtain a polysulde solution, followed by mixing
the polysulde solution with a carbonate electrolyte (1.1 M
LiPF6, EC : DMC ¼ 1 : 1 vol) with a 1 : 10 volume ratio. Four
diﬀerent concentrations of polysulde in the polysulde/
carbonate electrolyte solutions were prepared (0 mM, 0.5 mM,
1 mM and 2 mM). The LFPrLi batteries with these polysulde/
carbonate electrolytes were initially tested by galvanostatic
charge/discharge cycling. The discharge capacity retentions for
these batteries are similar, as indicted in Fig. S1.†
An important note is that the morphology of the cycled Li
electrodes was found to be strongly dependent on the
concentration
of
polysulde
in
the
electrolyte
solution.
Fig. 1b1–e2 compare the scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
photographs of the Li electrodes aer the 50th cycle. In the
absence of polysulde, a typical dendritic microstructure was
evident on the surface of the Li electrodes (Fig. 1b1-2), which is
in agreement with those observed in previous reports.27,28 With
the addition of polysulde (0.5 to 2 mM), the morphology of the
deposited Li metal on the surface of the Li electrodes was
signicantly diﬀerent, Fig. 1(c1–e2). When the polysulde
concentration was lower than 1 mM, the Li deposits were porous,
Fig. 1(c1-2), which might be attributed to the as-formed SEI lms
being thin and not strong enough to withstand volume defor-
mation during the repeated deposition/dissolution processes.
However, when the polysulde concentration was increased to
1 mM, the formation of Li dendrites was eﬀectively inhibited,
and dense and compact microstructures were observed for the Li
deposits, Fig. 1(d1-2). When the polysulde concentration was
further increased to 2 mM, the surface morphology of the Li
deposits did not change signicantly, as shown in Fig. 1(e1-2).
This suggests that the possible desired concentration of poly-
sulde in the carbonate electrolyte is 1 mM.
Note that the morphology of Li metal electrodes cycled in
a mixed carbonate–DME (10% vol) electrolyte (without poly-
sulde) is similar to that cycled in a carbonate-based electrolyte
without polysulde, Fig. S2†. This is in accordance with

The above XPS results show that the compositions of the SEI
lms formed on the surface of cycled Li electrodes in various
electrolytes is strongly dependent on the concentration of
polysulde and is not dependent on DME. Compared with that
obtained in the carbonate electrolyte, the Li anode cycled in the
carbonate–DME mixed electrolyte (50th cycle) only shows
a slight decrease in the amount of unstable ROCO2Li, Li2O,
LiOH and Li2CO3, along with a slight increase in the amount of
relatively stable PEO-like polymers and uoride salt in the SEI,
Fig. 2(b1–b3).29 However, in the polysulde/carbonate electrolyte
(aer 50 cycles), the SEI lm formed on the surface of the cycled
Li anode was mainly composed of PEO-like polymers, Fig. 2c2,
and the uoride salt, Fig. 2c3. Furthermore, the relative
percentage of the PEO-like polymers in the SEI was the highest
among all the values reported.29,31 Moreover, the chemically
unstable components, such as ROCO2Li, LiOH and Li2CO3,
signicantly decreased, Fig. 2c2. The Li2O component even
disappeared in the SEI, Fig. 2c2. In addition, the IR results also
indicated that the presence of PEO-like polymers on the surface
of the cycled Li electrodes, showing typical IR peaks similar to
that of commercial PEO, Fig. S3.†
It seems that the inhibition of Li dendrite growth in the
polysulde/carbonate electrolyte might be attributed to the
high concentration of the PEO-like polymers on the surface of
the cycled Li anode. The PEO-like polymers were reported to
exhibit a high chemical stability against HF, resulting in the
stabilization of the SEI and suppression of the formation of Li
dendrites.29 Furthermore, the presence of the PEO-like poly-
mers (high exibility) and the uoride salt (high thermody-
namic and kinetic stability) in the SEI can eﬀectively protect
the Li electrodes from HF attack, leading to a better stability

These results suggest that Li deposits gradually formed with

suppressed. It is noteworthy that the element S could not be
detected on the surface of the cycled Li anode by XPS,
Fig. 3(e1–4). This may suggest that the polysulde in the elec-
trolyte has not irreversibly transformed into chemical species in
the SEI lms. The presence of the polysulde in the electrolyte
aer cycling was conrmed by Raman results which show
similar peaks associated with the polysulde before and aer

carbonate electrolyte was consumed during cycling, the reac-
tion would be terminated in a few cycles due to the small
amount of the polysulde added to the electrolyte, which is in
agreement with the gradual increase of the PEO-like polymers
with the cycle number. Thus, it is likely that the polysulde may
act as a catalyst in the electrolyte, resulting in the observed
gradual change of the SEI composition and morphology of the
Li deposits during cycling.
The exact mechanism behind the formation of the PEO-rich
polymers with the polysulde is unclear. However, based on the
above results, one possible mechanism associated with the
catalytic reactions of the polysulde during cycling is shown in
Fig. 4. In stage I, the polysulde undergoes a nucleophilic
reaction with EC molecules, resulting in an accelerated EC ring
opening and the formation of a polysulde functional group, as
reported by Gao et al.26 In stage II, the polysulde may execute
a nucleophilic attack on the EC molecules to generate alkylene
carbon,26 inducing the release of CO2 and forming basic
monomers of PEO from the unstable ring-opened EC mole-
cules.32 In stage III, the basic monomers of PEO trigger the
polymerization of PEO-like polymers, accompanied by the
repeated reaction of ring opening of EC and the release of CO2.32
The polysulde functional group in the PEO-like polymers is
likely released back to the electrolyte to keep the chain reactions
as shown in stages I, II, and III.
Another possible explanation is that the small amount of the
polysulde (1 mM) may be reversibly oxidized and reduced in the
electrolyte during the charging/discharging process, and there-
fore has not been consumed with prolonged cycling.33 That is,
the polysulde may act as an electron donor and be oxidized by
EC, which thus initiates EC ring opening and decarboxylation
reactions to form the PEO-like polymers on the cycled Li metal
electrode. This is supported by the result that the content of the
PEO-like polymers increased gradually with prolonged cycling.
Conclusions
We reported that a signicantly increased amount of PEO-like
polymers were obtained in the SEI lms of cycled Li electrodes
by introducing polysulde into a carbonate electrolyte, which
resulted in the inhibition of the formation of Li dendrites. The
polysulde likely acts as a catalyst and is not consumed during
repeated charging/discharging cycles, and thus, the long-cycle
stability
of
lithium-metal
batteries
can
be
remarkably
improved. An extremely small amount of the polysulde added
into the carbonate electrolyte can trigger a series of chain reac-
tions to generate PEO-like polymers and LiF during cycling,
which are relatively stable species. These compounds are rapidly
accumulated to form a uniform and robust SEI lm aer only
a few cycles, resulting in the subsequent inhibition of the growth
of Li dendrites and the improvement of the long-term cycling
performance of the Li-metal anode. The Li deposits on the
surface of Li electrodes cycled in the polysulde/carbonate
electrolyte exhibited a dense structure and a smooth surface
even aer 300 cycles under a current density of 1 mA cm2. The
fundamental ndings revealed in this work may open a new
avenue for designing Li-metal battery electrolytes and possible
applications of polysulde in polymer electrolytes.
