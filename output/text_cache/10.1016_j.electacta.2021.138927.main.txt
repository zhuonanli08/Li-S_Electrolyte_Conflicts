a b s t r a c t 
Lithium −sulfur (Li −S) batteries are being evaluated as low-cost and high-capacity next-generation sec- 
ondary batteries to improve the mileage in electric vehicles. Accordingly, cathodes and electrolytes for 
Li −S batteries have been under active development in recent years. However, for the commercial use of 
Li −S batteries, it is ﬁrst necessary to address the low electrical conductivity of sulfur particles as well as 
the low capacity retention caused by lithium polysulﬁde. We therefore investigated the effects of various 
metal iodides as additives in the electrolyte used in Li −S batteries. Metal iodides ( i.e. , LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , 
and SnI 4 ) added to ether-based electrolytes impacted the charge–discharge performance as well as physi- 
cal properties such as Li ion conductivity and viscosity. The extent of their impact depended on their con- 
centration. Electrolytes that included LiI or MgI 2 additives in particular formed a stable solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) layer on the Li metal and suppressed the polysulﬁde shuttle reaction. This is because the 
viscosity of the electrolyte is appropriately increased by polymerization, and Mg ions along with Li ions 
are deposited on the Li metal surface to form a stable SEI. Consequently, our approach demonstrates a 
strategy for developing new electrolytes for Li −S batteries that takes advantage of the chemical reactivity 
of the additives used in the electrolyte.

1. Introduction 
Lithium −sulfur (Li −S) batteries are a promising technology for 
next-generation energy storage systems to meet the energy de- 
mands of electric vehicles and smart energy grids. Li −S batteries 
are composed of elemental sulfur as the cathode active material 
and Li metal as the anode, have a theoretical energy density of 
2500 Wh kg −1 , and a theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 1672 mAh 
g −1 , which is 10 times higher than commercialized Li-ion batter- 
ies. In addition, elemental sulfur as an active material is a com- 
mon by-product of the petroleum reﬁning process, which makes it 
cost competitive and environmentally friendly to obtain. Elemental 
sulfur is also non-toxic [ 1 , 2 ]. However, there are still issues that 
hinder the commercialization of Li −S batteries such as low capac- 
ity utilization and an increase in cell internal resistance caused by 
critical issues such as the low intrinsic electrical conductivity of 
sulfur (5 × 10 −30 S cm −1 at 25 °C) and dissolution of Li polysulﬁde 
intermediates (Li 2 S x , 4 < x < 8) [ 3–6 ]. Furthermore, in ether-based 
electrolytes, although the soluble Li polysulﬁde reacts with Li den-

or decomposition of active Li metal by the continuous crushing of 
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) leads to failure of the Li −S 
batteries [ 7–9 ]. The use of additives in the electrolyte plays a very 
important role in controlling the soluble Li polysulﬁdes and stabi- 
lizing the Li electrode/electrolyte interface to alleviate these prob- 
lems. Li salt additives in particular have demonstrated interesting 
results in providing additional stable SEI components to limit Li 
polysulﬁde reactivity with fresh Li metal in Li −S batteries. 
Among the Li salts, LiNO 3 has been the most comprehensively 
investigated for Li −S batteries and is the most commonly stud- 
ied as an additive. The passivation layer formed on the Li metal 
surface using LiNO 3 can limit contact between the fresh Li and 
Li polysulﬁdes, providing a simple remedy for suppressing the re- 
dox shuttle effect and continuous corrosion of the Li metal. How- 
ever, there are signiﬁcant drawbacks: irreversible consumption of 
LiNO 3 during SEI formation increases SEI resistance, continuous 
sulfur species loss causes capacity fading, and the strong oxida- 
tion of NO 3 −raises serious safety problems [10] . Currently, LiBOB 
(lithium bis(oxalato)borate) produces fewer corrosive by-products 
than LiNO 3 and has more thermal stability. Surface ﬁlms formed 
using LiBOB incorporate the advantages of two types of passiva- 
tion ﬁlms, one of which depends on the salt concentration in the

investigated the effect of metal iodides (i.e., LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , and 
SnI 4 ) as electrolyte additives on the electrochemical properties of 
Li −S batteries, with the aim of suppressing the Li polysulﬁde redox 
shuttle effect and modifying the interface between the electrolyte 
and the Li anode. Here, the Li and metal (i.e., Li, Mg, Al, Ti, and Sn) 
iodides simultaneously co-deposited on the Li metal, and iodides 
also polymerized the DME in the electrolyte. 
2. Experimental 
Lithium iodide ( > 99.99%), magnesium iodide ( > 99.99%), alu- 
minum iodide ( > 99.99%), titanium iodide ( > 99.99%), and tin io- 
dide ( > 99.99%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar and dried un- 
der vacuum at 80 °C for 12 h prior to use. The electrolytes in- 
cluding metal iodides as additive were produced by dissolving 
in 1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a 
mixed solvent of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL) (DME–DOL, 1:1 v/v, battery grade, Enchem Co., Ltd., Republic 
of Korea). All electrolyte-related procedures were performed in an 
Ar ﬁlling glove box, and the moisture content in the electrolytes 
was < 20 ppm. The ionic conductivities for electrolyte were mea- 
sured using a data recording device consisting of two platinum 
electrodes (Cond 3110, WTW). The viscosities were recorded using 
a AND SV-A series viscometer. The solubility of Li polysulﬁdes in 
the electrolyte was performed via ultraviolet visible (UV-Vis) spec- 
troscopy (Shimadzu SolidSpec-3700) after direct contact of the sul- 
fur cathode with Li foil and immersion in the electrolyte for 3 days. 
The cathode electrode for Li −S battery evaluation was pre- 
pared by mixing 60% weight active material powder (sulfur pow- 
der, 45 μm, 99.5%, Alfa Aesar), 20 wt% carbon black (Super 
P ), and 20 wt% polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF, dissolved in N - 
methylpyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry. This slurry was coated 
on aluminum foil, pressed, and dried at 80 °C for 2 h. The load- 
ing of the active material was 2.5–2.8 mg cm −2 . CR2032 coin 
cells were assembled using Celgard 3501 as a separator, Li foil as 
the counter electrode, and 0.03 mL of the above-described elec- 
trolytes. Charge −discharge experiments were performed at a con- 
stant current density of 100 mA g −1 within a potential range of 
1.5 −2.7 V vs. Li + /Li using a battery tester (Maccor 40 0 0). Linear 
sweep voltammetry was measured for the cells using a PARSTAT 
40 0 0 potentiostat/galvanostat. Stainless steel was employed for the 
working electrode and Li metal was used for the counter and ref- 
erence electrodes. The scan rate was 5 mV s −1 . Electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out on a PARSTAT 40 0 0 
by applying a 10 mV amplitude signal in the 10 to 0.01 Hz fre- 
quency range. For these EIS experiments, an active material con- 
tent of ~2.5 −2.8 mg cm −2 served as the working electrode and Li 
foil was employed for the counter and reference electrodes. The 
impedance response was recorded after the ﬁfth cycle and ﬁtted 
using ZSimpWin software. 
The morphology and microstructure of the Li electrodes af- 
ter cycling were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; 
TESCAN Mira LM). The surface chemical states on the Li metal

3. Results and discussion 
The electrochemical performance of Li −S batteries depends on 
the type of electrolyte used. It is also essential to use stable sol- 
vents for the electrolyte, especially against nucleophilic attack by 
polysulﬁdation. Therefore, ether-based solvents are the most com- 
monly used because they are chemically stable with regards to the 
polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte. However, Li polysulﬁde intermedi- 
ates (Li 2 S m ) that are dissolved in ether-based solvents can still be 
reduced at the Li metal anode. In addition, low-order intermedi- 
ates can oxidize at the cathode during the charge process, thereby 
reducing capacity. To prevent this, additives can be used in ether- 
based solvents as supplemental agents for Li −S cell electrolytes. 
DME and DOL are commonly used solvents in Li −S battery sys- 
tems because of their high donor number ( DN ; 20 and 18, respec- 
tively) and low viscosity. It is expected that DME and DOL could 
behave like non-viscous solvents, although the dielectric constant 
of DME and DOL ( εr ; 7.3 and 7.1, respectively) is low. In general, 
a high dielectric constant implies the solvent can dissolve a large 
amount of salt, whereas a high viscosity means that the move- 
ment of ions dissolved in the solvent is slow. In this study, the 
electrolyte (1M LiTFSI in DME/DOL) with ionic conductivity and 
viscosity of 12.3 mS cm −1 and 1.74 mPa •s, respectively, was used 
as base electrolytes. Fig. 1 exhibits the ionic conductivities of the 
electrolytes with LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 additives. The con- 
centration of the additive in the electrolyte was 30 0 0 ppm. The 
electrolytes having LiI or MgI 2 additives showed high ionic con- 
ductivity, with that of the electrolyte having LiI being 13.3 mS 
cm −1 . The electrolytes containing AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 presented lower 
ionic conductivity (10.9 −11.1 mS cm −1 ) than the electrolytes con- 
taining LiI or MgI 2 . Fig. S1 shows the changes in ionic conductiv- 
ity of the electrolytes containing LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 as a 
function of the additive concentration. The highest ionic conduc- 
tivity (13.6 mS cm −1 ) was observed for the LiI additive. For all 
additives, the ionic conductivity slightly decreased with increasing 
additive concentration, although different additives showed differ- 
ent degrees of reduction. For the electrolyte containing MgI 2 , ionic 
conductivity did not signiﬁcantly decrease as MgI 2 concentration 
increased but was maintained at a value between 13.6 and 12.6 mS 
cm −1 even when the concentration of the additive increased from 
50 0 to 50 0 0 ppm. Electrolytes containing AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 at a 
concentration of 500 ppm showed lower ionic conductivity (be- 
tween 11.2 and 11.9 mS cm −1 ) than electrolytes containing LiI or 
MgI 2 at the same concentration, and ionic conductivity was main-

The viscosity of the electrolyte typically increases in proportion 
to the concentration of dissolved salts. In addition, since the mi- 
gration of ions is slow in solvents having a high viscosity, the con- 
ductivity of the ions is reduced in solutions with a high concen- 
tration of dissolved salts [ 19–21 ], as was seen in the results above. 
The viscosities of the electrolytes containing 30 0 0 ppm of additive 
are compared in Fig. 2 . The electrolytes with LiI and MgI 2 addi- 
tives showed relatively low viscosity. In contrast, the electrolytes 
containing AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 additives had higher viscosities than 
electrolytes containing LiI or MgI 2 . This result is in agreement with 
the ionic conductivity results. The viscosity of the electrolyte as a 
function of the additive concentration is shown in Fig. S2. All vis- 
cosities increased with an increase in the weight fraction of the ad- 
ditives. The electrolytes containing LiI or MgI 2 showed only a slight 
increase in viscosity with increasing concentration, despite the ad- 
ditive concentration increasing from 500 to 50 0 0 ppm. In contrast, 
electrolytes containing AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or SnI 4 exhibited a rapid increase 
in viscosity as the concentration of the additive increased. SnI 4 had 
the most dramatic change in viscosity, increasing by 0.7 mPa •s at 
each concentration step between 500 and 5000 ppm.

chemical stability of the electrolytes containing 30 0 0 ppm addi- 
tives was determined using linear sweep voltammetry ( Fig. 3 ). All 
electrolytes with additives showed stability between 1.0 and 3. V 
vs. Li + /Li, which is the voltage range where Li −S batteries are typ- 
ically used. Reduction peaks were observed for all the electrolytes 
at ~1.3 V vs. Li + /Li, which is related to the reaction of the sac- 
riﬁcial additives [22] . The linear sweep voltammetry of the elec- 
trolytes with different additive concentrations are shown in Fig. 
S3. The electrolytes containing additives decomposed at ≥5 V vs.

tion did not signiﬁcantly depend on the additive concentration. An 
error in the experimental process caused the oxidation potential of 
the electrolyte containing 10 0 0 ppm of MgI 2 to appear at ~4.3 V

Further evaluation of the physical and electrochemical proper- 
ties of the electrolytes was done using 30 0 0 ppm of the additives 
as the representative samples. Fig. 4 shows photographs of the 
electrolytes containing the additives after being stored in an Ar- 
ﬁlled glove box at room temperature for 3 days. The colors are dis- 
played from transparent to yellowish brown in the order LiI, MgI 2 , 
AlI 3 , SnI 4 , and TiI 4 . Regardless of the original color of the addi- 
tives, the color of the electrolyte solution depends on the extent 
of dissociation of the additive in the solvent. After stirring con- 
tinuously for 3 days, the electrolytes with LiI and MgI 2 were in 
a liquid state and a change in viscosity could not be observed with 
the naked eye. For AlI 3 , TiI 4 , and SnI 4 , the viscosity of the solu- 
tion gradually increased from the surface of the electrolyte, and 
gelation proceeded until the electrolyte had changed to a semi- 
solid state. Electrolytes containing iodide anions can participate in 
DME/I −oxidation reactions with I • neutral radicals, resulting in hy- 
drogen abstraction by I −that then forms a DME(-H) • radical and 
H + I −. The DME(-H) • radicals can easily cause condensation reac- 
tions, which increases the viscosity of the electrolyte by forming 
linear or comb-branched oligoethers [17] . These polymerization re- 
actions can lead to the formation of comb-branched polyether pro- 
tective ﬁlms on the anode and cathode, which can induce an ad- 
ditional improvement in cell performance through the prevention 
of Li polysulﬁde dissolution [17] . However, this reaction also forms 
too high a viscosity, which interferes with Li ion diffusion in the 
cell, thereby can require appropriate control. 
Because the composition of the electrolyte affects the types 
of Li polysulﬁdes formed, as well as the ionic conductivity, vis- 
cosity, stability, and SEI properties of the electrolyte, we com- 
pared the performance of Li −S battery cells with different elec- 
trolytes. Fig. 5 (a) shows the charge −discharge proﬁles of the Li −S 
cells based on electrolytes consisting of 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in 
DME/DOL with an additive of 30 0 0 ppm LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , or 
SnI 4 . The theoretical capacity of elemental sulfur is ~1672 mAh g −1 
when the reduction reaction is complete (Li 2 S). However, the ca- 
pacity of the ﬁrst discharge (Li insertion) of all the Li −S cells was 
1056 −1162 mAh g −1 because the elemental sulfur utilization in 
the electrode was low. The voltage proﬁles exhibited two plateaus. 
The ﬁrst voltage plateau, which was observed between 2.4 and 
2.0 V, was ascribed to the reduction of S 8 to Li 2 S 4 by the for- 
mation of Li 2 S 8 . The second voltage plateau, which occurred at 
~2.0 V, was attributed to the reduction of Li 2 S 4 to Li 2 S. For elec- 
trolytes with metal iodide additives, the increase in discharge ca- 
pacity and occurrence of overpotentials were relatively similar to 
or lower than that of the electrolyte without additives (Fig. S4). 
This is because the metal iodides have relatively good ionic con- 
ductivity and control properties for the formation of Li polysulﬁdes 
[ 17 , 23 ]. The charge (Li extraction) curve shows voltage plateaus at 
~2.2 and 2.4 V that are related to the oxidation reaction. How- 
ever, the charge capacity of an electrolyte containing TiI 4 or SnI 4 
reduced the initial Coulombic eﬃciency because of the relatively 
large increase in charge capacity. This is probably due to the less 
stabilization of the Li metal surface. The cyclabilities of the Li −S 
cells based on the electrolyte consisting of 1 M LiTFSI dissolved 
in DME/DOL with an additive are compared in Fig. 5 (b). The cells 
with LiI, MgI 2 , AlI 3 , TiI 4 , and SnI 4 electrolyte additives showed ca- 
pacities of 690, 680, 573, 556, and 501 mAh g −1 , respectively, af- 
ter 50 cycles. Although the capaciies of all samples decrease grad- 
ually as charge and discharge proceed, their decreasing tendency 
indicates a relatively more stable characteristic than the cell using 
an electrolyte without additives (Fig. S5). The cells in which the 
electrolyte contained LiI or MgI 2 had the cyclability after 50 cy- 
cles, retaining 61 or 64% of their initial capacity, respectively. This 
is a slight improvement compared to the cell with 30 0 0 ppm of

To gain further insight into the key differences in the SEI com- 
position on the Li metal anode as well as the reaction between 
the Li metal and Li polysulﬁdes delivered to the Li metal anode, 
ex situ XPS analysis was performed after ﬁve cycles (after delithia- 
tion) and is shown in Fig. 6 . All spectra were measured after treat- 
ing the sample for 10 min by Ar sputtering to remove impuri- 
ties formed from the XPS instrument chamber as well as the ox- 
ide ﬁlm formed during the sample transport. The 284.5 eV peak 
assigned to the hydrocarbon in the C 1 s spectrum was used to 
calibrate the binding energy. The high-resolution spectra for the 
Li 1 s region ( Fig. 6 (a)) shows the contribution of Li compounds 
such as LiF, Li 2 CO 3 (or LiOH), and Li 2 O at 55.7, 54.7, and 53.7 eV, 
respectively. The Li metal electrodes using an electrolyte contain- 
ing a LiI additive show slightly more relative LiF formation than Li 
metal electrodes using electrolytes with other additives. In addi- 
tion, in the I 3d spectra, peaks corresponding to the iodine phase 
generated by the additive were observed at 630.5 (I 3d 3/2 ) and 
619 (I 3d 5/2 ) eV. The peak ratio of I 3d 3/2 and I 3d 5/2 was ~2:3, 
which is in good agreement with the LiI species present as de- 
scribed in a previous report [24] . Notably, the integral intensity of 
the peak for the LiI additive was larger than for the other additives, 
which implies that the Li metal contains more LiI on the surface. 
In the XPS spectrum for each metal, only magnesium showed a 
clear peak at 1303 eV, which is consistent with the binding energy 
of 49.8 eV observed for Mg 2 p. This indicates that metal species 
ionized from the AlI 3 , TiI 4 , and SnI 4 additives were not deposited 
on the Li metal surface during the discharge process; only metal 
ions generated from the MgI 2 additive were deposited on the Li 
metal surface. These XPS results suggest that a LiF component can 
help to form a stable SEI and that not all metal complexes that in- 
clude an iodine anion form a stable SEI. These results also suggest 
that an alloy phase with Li is possible, or that the deposition of 
metallic elements onto the Li metal surface can help form a stable 
SEI layer. 
Fig. 7 shows the SEM images of the Li metal anode surfaces af- 
ter ﬁve cycles (after delithiation). They have a non-smooth, uneven 
shape owing to non-uniform dendrite formation. Nevertheless, it 
appears that the surface of the Li metal from cells using an elec- 
trolyte with an additive are relatively smoother than that of the 
electrolyte without an additive (Fig. S8). The Li metal surfaces of 
cells using an electrolyte containing LiI or MgI 2 had some agglom- 
eration or separation, but their relatively smoother surfaces may 
be because LiI, LiF, and Li −Mg alloy species contributed to the for- 
mation of a stable SEI.

4. Conclusions 
In this work, we aimed to understand the effect of the metal io- 
dide additive in the electrolyte on the electrochemical performance 
of Li −S batteries. The addition of metal iodides to the electrolyte 
exhibited a few interesting features. First, not all metal complexes 
with iodine anions formed a stable SEI layer on the Li metal. Sec- 
ond, iodine ions suppressed the polysulﬁde shuttle reaction by in- 
creasing the viscosity of the electrolyte through polymerization of 
the ether solvent, but also could deteriorate the electrochemical 
performance of the cell by interfering with Li ion conductivity and 
diffusion. Third, alloy phases with Li were possible, and depositing 
a metal element helped form a stable SEI on the Li metal. Finally, 
there was a difference in electrochemical performance depending 
on the type and amount of additive. The electrolytes containing 
LiI or MgI 2 additives in particular showed high ionic conductivities 
and usable low viscosities. The chemical reactivity of the additives 
can affect the electrochemical performance due to the proper poly- 
sulﬁde diffusion control and stable Li metal surface. We believe 
that elucidating the chemical interaction control associated with 
protection of the Li metal with an additive that includes an iodine 
anion in the electrolyte will lead to the development of advanced 
electrolytes for Li −S batteries.
