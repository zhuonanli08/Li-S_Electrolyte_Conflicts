ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been explored
extensively for high-capacity electric-power storage, but their
practical application has been prevented by severe issues stemming
from the use of a lithium anode and an organic-liquid electrolyte in
which Li2Sx intermediates of the cell discharge reaction are soluble
and shuttle to the anode. Both problems are addressed using bis(4-
nitrophenyl) carbonate as an additive in the organic-liquid
electrolyte. The soluble Li2Sx polysulﬁdes react with the additive
to create insoluble polysulﬁdes with a lithium byproduct; this
byproduct reacts with the Li-metal anode to create an anode passivation layer that is a good Li+ conductor, which allows
for safe and rapid plating/stripping of lithium metal with a low impedance.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, lithium polysulﬁde, solid−electrolyte interphase (SEI), electrolyte system, battery cycle life
T
he lithium−sulfur battery is under global investigation
as a candidate for next-generation rechargeable
batteries because it promises a large capacity on the
reduction of S8 molecules to Li2S.1−5 However, this battery
requires a lithium metal anode to give a competitive voltage.
Conventional liquid Li+ electrolytes with suﬃcient Li+
conductivity form lithium dendrites on the anode during
charge, which causes a safety issue. These liquid electrolytes
also allow for the solvation of intermediate Li2Sx species,
reducing the charge/discharge cycle life of the cell. Therefore,
a liquid electrolyte from which a dendrite-free lithium anode
can be plated and in which Li2Sx intermediate species are not
soluble is a high-priority technical target.
Previous work has explored the use of ether-based liquid
electrolytes such as 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), tetra-
ethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME), and 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL).5−8 However, the dissolution of Li2Sx intermediates is
not suppressed in these electrolytes,9−13 limiting their practical
application.14−19 Various attempts to eliminate this problem
with solvents and electrolyte additives such as LiNO3 or LiF
have been explored without success.20−28 Carbonates in liquid-
carbonate-based electrolytes react with the soluble Li2Sx
intermediates because they are highly polar.8,22,29,30 For this
reason, we chose to use the most common commercialized
electrolyte for Li−S batteries (LiNO3/LiTFSI in DME/DOL)
as a baseline electrolyte and introduced bis(4-nitrophenyl)
carbonate (BNC) as an additive (Figure S1).6 The BNC reacts
with any soluble Li2Sx intermediates, causing them to
precipitate as insoluble species. This process not only
eliminates the problem of Li2Sx diﬀusion to the anode but
also provides a Li+-conductive passivation layer on the lithium
metal to suppress the growth of dendrites from the anode. Our
Li+ liquid electrolyte shows signiﬁcantly improved capacity
retention over 300 charge/discharge cycles.

elucidated by the fact that the chemical nucleophilicity reaction
between the polysulﬁdes and bis(4-nitrophenyl) carbonate
leads to the generation of insoluble lithium polysulﬁde
derivatives, ridding the cell of the traditional dissolution
problem of lithium polysulﬁde intermediates in the electrolyte
during the battery cycling process.
In situ SEI depth proﬁling was performed by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with a MAGCIS ion gun
to examine the components of the SEI layer in the BNC-added
electrolyte system (Figure 3a). The composition of the surface
SEI layer consisted mainly of Li2SO3, Li2SO4, LiF, Li2CO3, and
LiNPH; no signals from Li2S/Li2S2 were found (Figure 3b,c).
Such ﬁndings are signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from those with the
conventional baseline electrolyte where Li2S/Li2S2 was usually
observed on the lithium anode surface arising from the soluble
lithium polysulﬁde ions shuttling from the cathode. Thus, the
absence of Li2S/Li2S2 on the lithium anode surface in the
BNC-added electrolyte reveals a greatly suppressed polysulﬁde
shuttling eﬀect owing to the formation of insoluble
intermediate compounds (Figure S10). The dynamic for-
mation of the SEI layer is shown in detail through high-
resolution XPS spectra in Figure S11. The intensity of
elemental carbon (Figure 3d), sulfur (Figure 3e), nitrogen
(Figure S12a), and ﬂuorine (Figure 3f) all decrease with
increasing etching time, revealing a reduced thickness of the
SEI layer. The signal of elemental lithium element continu-
ously increased, showing metallic Li is underneath the SEI
(Figure S12b).
The SEI passivation layer is formed in situ on the surface of
the Li-metal anode. If the SEI has a high Li+ conductivity and
is wet by the metallic lithium, lithium may be plated/stripped
reversibly at high rates without the fear of dendrite growth.36
The structure and composition of the SEI layer were explored
with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the cycled Li-
metal surface after exposure to diﬀerent electrolytes as well as a
fresh lithium wafer, for comparison (Figure S13a). The surface
morphology of lithium cycled in the baseline electrolyte is
caused by short-chain Li2Sx deposition as well as dendrite
formation after Li plating/stripping (Figure S13b); the
evolution of moss-like and porous lithium is also observed.
The cracks or pores were caused by a continuous attacking and
corroding of the lithium surface by the soluble polysulﬁde
species, showing that the SEI layer is unstable. Figure S13c
depicts the surface of lithium cycled in the NBC-added
electrolyte, which shows a much smoother surface when
compared to the lithium cycled in the baseline electrolyte,
indicating that a dense SEI layer is formed with the BNC
additive. Notably, the SEM analysis of the Li surface after 50
cycles has also been performed to further demonstrate the

Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping of the elements on
the anode SEI surface layer was conducted on lithium anodes
cycled in both the baseline electrolyte and the BNC-added
electrolyte. Without the additive, the average atomic
percentages in the SEI are F (29 at. %), O (30 at. %), C
(19 at. %), S (12 at. %), and N (10 at. %); see Figure S16a.
The denser and thicker SEI shows an improvement in its
chemical stability, Figure S16b. With the BNC additive, the
average atomic composition of the SEI layer changed from that
without the additive to a lower sulfur composition (7 at. %)
and the introduction of a N element (16 at. %), consistent with
a signiﬁcant decrease in polysulﬁde crossover. The nitrogen-
containing component also introduces a fast Li-ion transfer
across the SEI, Figure S17, with low impedance for Li-metal
plating/stripping, Figure S18. The uniform and dense SEI layer
contributes to the homogeneous diﬀusion of Li ions, leading to
a uniform and smooth Li-metal surface, which is further
demonstrated by the atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of
BNC-added electrolyte. However, the surface of the baseline
electrolyte exhibits a rough texture pitted with holes and large
granular features, leading to side reactions and Li dendrite
growth. As shown in Figure S19, the enhanced rigidity and
ﬂexibility of the SEI for the BNC-added electrolyte were
estimated with corresponding indentation testing. The
increased Young’s modulus (1354 MPa) after adding the
BNC-added electrolyte corresponds to the excellent rigidity of
the SEI layers, suggesting that it is not as easily broken as the
SEI layers that develop by baseline electrolyte during the
cycles. The dense SEI layer with greatly improved Li+ transfer
rate contributes to the homogeneous diﬀusion of Li ions,
further promoting the uniform lithium deposition. During the
discharge process, various polysulﬁdes (Li2Sx, 2 ≤x ≤6),
generated at the cathode, dissolve in the electrolyte and react
with BNC to create an insoluble sulﬁde complex, which blocks
the polysulﬁdes at the sulfur cathode and prevents its further
dissolution in the electrolyte (Figure S20). Moreover, SEM
images of the sulfur cathode and corresponding EDX mapping
of N element after 10 cycles were conducted to further
demonstrate that the insoluble sulﬁde complex could uniformly
disperse on the surface of the sulfur cathode. As shown in
Figure S21, EDX mapping suggests atomic N distributes evenly
on the surface of the sulfur electrode. No apparent porous
structure and granular polysulﬁde aggregates are observed on
the surface of the sulfur cathode, indicating the uniform
distribution of the precipitates.
A cyclic voltammetry (CV) scan of a Li−S battery with a
BNC-added electrolyte (Figure S22) is consistent with
galvanostatic charge/discharge proﬁles shown in Figure S23.
The two cathodic peaks correspond to the conversion reaction
to Li2S2/Li2S. During the anodic scan, a broad peak reﬂects the
reverse conversion from polysulﬁdes to elemental sulfur.

Further advantages of the BNC-added electrolyte system are
shown by the rate capability of the Li−S battery demonstrated
in Figure S27. While cycled at continuously varying current
densities of 0.1−5.0 C, the Li−S battery with the BNC-added
electrolyte system retained a high Coulombic eﬃciency. It is
worth noting that the Li−S battery using the BNC-added
electrolyte system can deliver a high reversible discharge
capacity of approximately 1167.8 mAh g−1 at 0.3 C with
superior capacity retention after 200 cycles (1.52 mg cm−2,
Figures 4c and S28). Figure 4d shows the cycling performance
with the BNC-added electrolyte over 300 cycles (1.49 mg
cm−2); the capacity of the Li−S battery maintained 778.7 mAh
g−1 (a decay rate of 0.025% per cycle) with a stable Coulombic
eﬃciency of almost 100%, indicating the reversibility of the cell
with insoluble polysulﬁdes. In contrast, the Li−S battery with
baseline electrolyte shows poor cycling performance with a
36.5% capacity fade after 300 cycles. This result shows that
BNC-added electrolyte allowed for the formation of insoluble
polysulﬁde intermediates that were not transported to the Li
metal, suppressing harmful parasitic reactions. The BNC-
added electrolyte also allowed for a signiﬁcant improvement in
Coulombic eﬃciency over the baseline electrolyte, which
remains near 100% at high current densities (Figure S29).
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, the use of bis(4-nitrophenyl) carbonate as an
additive to a liquid electrolyte is shown theoretically to react
with soluble Li2Sx polysulﬁdes to form an insoluble sulﬁde
complex and lithium 4-nitrophenolate. Experimentally, the
insoluble sulﬁde complexes prevent shuttling of the soluble
polysulﬁdes away from the conductive cathode to the anode
surface, and the LNPH byproduct reacts at the anode to form a
dense passivating SEI layer with a high Li+ conductivity below
which metallic lithium can plate/strip with a low impedance.
Our liquid electrolyte provides a low-cost, safe Li−S battery
with a Li-metal anode that has a long cycle life for storage of
electric power from an alternative power source.
METHODS
Preparation of Baseline Electrolyte and BNC-Added
Electrolyte. The baseline electrolyte was composed of 1 M LiTFSI
in a solution of DME and DOL (1:1 by volume) with 0.46 M lithium
nitrate (LiNO3). For comparison, 0.11 M bis(4-nitrophenyl)
carbonate was added to 10 mL of the baseline electrolyte under
adequate stirring, hereon noted as BNC-added electrolyte. All organic
solvents were dried with molecular sieves to remove residual water
prior to use.
Electrochemical Measurements and Material Character-
ization. C/S composites (the sulfur content: ∼76.3%) were obtained
by ball-milling a mixture of commercial sulfur and carbon nanotubes
and heating the mixture at 155 °C for 12 h. The C/S composites (80
wt %) were then mixed with carbon black (10 wt %) and
polyvinylidene diﬂuoride (10 wt %) in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone. For
the polar binder, the sodium carboxyl methyl cellulose (10 wt %) was
used in the cathode fabrication. The sulfur loading density was
evaluated to be 1.44 to 6.79 mg cm−2. The mixture was cast onto an
Al-foil current collector by a doctor-blade method. The cells were
cycled within a voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V on a CT2001A battery
cycler (Wuhan LAND Electronic Co., Ltd.). The morphology,
microstructure, and composition of Li metals were examined using
ﬁeld emission SEM and energy dispersive spectroscopy mapping
analysis with a SU8010, Hitachi, Ltd. For SEM observations of cycled
Li metal, the Li metal was removed from a disassembled Li−S cell and
washed with DME or DOL to remove any residual electrolyte. Li2S2,
Li2S3, Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8 were prepared by direct in situ reaction of
S8 with Li2S in corresponding electrolytes. All cell assembly was
performed in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox with a water/oxygen content lower
than 1 ppm. AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker) was used to analyze the
mechanical properties of the SEI layer and investigate the surface
morphology. Raman spectra were recorded on an HR Evolution
(Horiba Jobin Yvon) Raman spectrometer.
In Situ UV/Vis Analysis. Li−S batteries were packaged in a
fenestrated 2025-type coin cell with a sealed glass cover (0.13 mm)
for in situ UV/vis experiments. A lithium wafer, with a hole in the
middle, was used as a counter electrode. A Whatman glass microﬁber
ﬁlter was used as a separator. A 100 μL amount of electrolyte was
added in in situ UV/vis spectroscopy measurements following the
electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 15:1 μL mg−1, which is in accordance with
electrochemical measurements. UV−vis absorption spectra were
recorded on a UV Lambda 750 UV/vis/NIR spectrometer equipped
with an integrating sphere between 200 and 800 nm. The total
number of time steps was about 1200. Model calculations of
polysulﬁde concentrations were based on the theory of polysulﬁde
concentration above 1 mM, which is a particularly low point in Li−S
battery cycling. Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were carried out
at a current density of 0.2 C.
X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy Analysis of the SEI
Layer. XPS was used for analyzing the surface chemistry of the SEI
layer formed on the lithium anode. The XPS analysis was performed
with a Thermo Scientiﬁc ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray photoelectron
spectrometer microprobe, ﬁtted with a MAGCIS ion gun for rapid,
high-resolution depth proﬁling. The MAGCIS ion gun is fully
automated and operates at an energy range from 100 eV to 4 keV and
has a maximum beam current of >6 μA at 3 keV and a minimum spot
size of 200 μm at 2.5 μA and 4 keV. After washing the cycled Li-metal
anodes with DME or DOL and allowing them to dry in an Ar-ﬁlled
glovebox, the samples were sealed in a standard Preploc chamber of
an ESCALAB Xi+ base system. This chamber allows for the samples to
remain under vacuum while they are being transferred into the
instrument.
Computational Method. The Gaussian 093 program suite using
DFT was used to simulate covalent interactions. All atoms were
simulated by using the 6-31G(d+p) basis set, and the gas-phase Gibbs
free energy was used during all present discussions. The stationary
points as local minima or transition states were characterized by
analyzing vibrational frequency within the same theoretical level.
