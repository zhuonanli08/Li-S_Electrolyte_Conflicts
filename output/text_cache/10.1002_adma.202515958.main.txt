Cosolvent Electrolyte Design for Li–S Batteries: Suppressing
the Shuttle Eﬀect via Phase Separation
Changyu Yeo, Seungyeop Kang, Yun-Jeong Lee, Seungwoo Choo, Juyoung Kim,
Seung-Ho Yu, Jun-Woo Park,* Dong-Joo Yoo,* and Minkyung Kim*
Lithium–sulfur batteries are promising candidates for next-generation energy
storage due to their high energy density and low cost. However, their
commercialization is hindered by poor cycling performance caused by the
polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect. While strategies such as physical barriers or
chemical adsorption have been proposed, they inevitably introduce inactive
components, reducing energy density. These limitations underscore the need
for a more fundamental approach that avoids the use of inactive materials. In
this study, a cosolvent-based electrolyte design as a fundamental strategy is
presented to suppress the shuttle eﬀect without relying on inactive additives.
A high donor number solvent is used as the base, and four cosolvents with
distinct physicochemical properties are individually introduced. By varying the
cosolvent, the lithium polysulﬁdes solubility is systematically tuned, directly
inﬂuencing electrochemical kinetics. Notably, the combination of two
low-miscibility solvents induced local phase separation, which hindered the
diﬀusion of lithium polysulﬁdes and eﬀectively mitigated the shuttle eﬀect. As
a result, signiﬁcantly improved cycling stability is achieved. These ﬁndings
provide a new direction for Li–S battery electrolyte development, emphasizing
the importance of solvent miscibility in governing polysulﬁde transport.

While these strategies are eﬀective in suppressing the shut-

materials, ultimately compromising the practical energy den-
sity of the cell. Thus, a more fundamental solution is re-
quired. In this context, solid-state electrolytes have been ex-
plored because shuttle eﬀect does not occur since the LiPSs
are not dissolved in the solid electrolytes.[18,19] However, solid-
state Li-S batteries suﬀer from poor kinetics and challenges in
processing and interface engineering, limiting their practical
viability.[20,21]
In this study, we propose a new liquid electrolyte that eﬀec-
tively suppresses the shuttle eﬀect without relying on excessive
inactive components. Developing electrolytes oﬀers a more fun-
damental solution than increasing the proportion of inactive ma-
terials. To achieve this, a cosolvent strategy was employed. N,N′-
dimethylimidazolidinone (DMI), a high donor number (DN) sol-
vent was selected as the primary solvent due to its strong solvat-
ing ability for LiPSs.[22] This allows for reduced electrolyte vol-
ume in the cell. However, DMI is highly reactive toward lithium
metal, leading to undesirable side reactions and poor cycling
stability.
To tailor the electrolyte properties and overcome this limi-
tation, we systematically explored four organic cosolvents: 1)
tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G4) with a moderate DN of
16.6 kCal mol−1,[23] 2) diethyl ether (DEE), a short-chain ether
with a moderate DN of 19.2 kCal mol−1, 3) 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl
2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (TTE) medium-chain ether con-
taining ﬂuorinated groups and having a low DN of 1.9 kCal
mol−1, and 4) 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), cyclic ether with a highest DN
of 21.2 kCal mol−1 among the cosolvents.[23,24] The physicochem-
ical properties of each solvent, including dielectric constants are
summarized in Figure 1a.[22–25]
By varying the cosolvent, the solvation power of the electrolyte
was tuned, which signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced both redox voltages
and kinetic behavior of Li-S cells. In contrast, cycling stability
appeared relatively independent of solvation strength. Interest-
ingly, however, the DEE/DMI electrolyte exhibited remarkable
improved cycling performance with suppression of the shuttle
eﬀect. This behavior is attributed to the low miscibility between
DMI and DEE, which induces local phase separation and the for-
mation of DEE-rich clusters. These clusters act as diﬀusion bar-
riers for LiPSs, thereby mitigating the shuttle eﬀect.
This ﬁnding highlights the critical role of solvent miscibil-
ity, independent of solvation strength, in controlling polysul-
ﬁde transport. Although the DEE/DMI electrolyte shows limited

2. Results
2.1. Impact of Cosolvent Regulation on Solvation Power and
Redox Voltages
To investigate the eﬀects of cosolvent regulation on LiPSs sol-
vation behavior and electrochemical properties, the base solvent
DMI was mixed with each of the four selected cosolvents at a 1:1
volume ratio. The resulting electrolytes contained 1 M LiTFSI
and 0.5 M LiNO3. The galvanostatic charge/discharge voltage
proﬁles of Li-S cells with each electrolyte are shown in Figure 1c.
All samples exhibited two characteristic discharge plateaus: the
1st plateau, associated with the conversion of sulfur to long-chain
LiPSs, and the 2nd plateau, corresponding to the further conver-
sion of LiPSs to Li2S. The 1st plateau voltage was highest for the
DMI-only electrolyte (DMI100), and gradually decreased in the
order of G4/DMI, DOL/DMI, DEE/DMI, and TTE/DMI. In con-
trast, the 2nd plateau voltage followed the reverse trend. Since
the solvation strength of LiPSs directly inﬂuences the redox volt-
age, electrolytes with stronger solvation power exhibit a higher 1st
plateau and a lower 2nd plateau voltage.[26,27] These results sug-
gest that the four cosolvent-containing electrolytes possess dis-
tinct LiPSs solubilities.
To evaluate the solubility of LiPSs in each electrolyte, 1 M Li2S6
(containing 6 mol of S) was added to each electrolyte, and the
visual appearance was compared in Figure 1b. DMI100 showed
complete dissolution without any visible precipitation. G4/DMI
enabled nearly full dissolution, with only minor residue. In con-
trast, DOL/DMI formed a thin sediment layer at the bottom while
both DEE/DMI and TTE/DMI exhibited signiﬁcant precipitation,
with the TTE-containing electrolyte showing the thickest layer. To
quantitatively assess LiPSs solubility, each electrolyte was grad-
ually added to the 1 M Li2S6 until its complete dissolution oc-
curred, without stirring at room temperature. The approximate
solubility values are summarized in Figure S1 and Table S1 (Sup-
porting Information). G4/DMI electrolyte exhibited the highest
solubility of 290 mM, indicating the strongest solvation power
for Li2S6. The solubility decreased in the order of DOL/DMI (230
mM), DEE/DMI (170 mM), and TTE/DMI (130 mM), which cor-
responds with the visual images. The solvation power of LiPSs de-
creases in the order of DMI100, G4/DMI, DOL/DMI, DEE/DMI,
and TTE/DMI, which corresponds well to the redox voltage.
To understand the solvation power of each electrolyte, solva-
tion structures of Li2S8 in each electrolyte were investigated by
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, as shown in Figure 1d–g,
where the radial distributions were plotted by the S atom at the
end of the S8
2−ion. In the G4/DMI (Figure 1d), the ﬁrst g(r) peaks
for both DMI and G4 molecules appeared at approximately 3.5 Å
indicating that both solvents actively participate in LiPSs solva-
tion. Similarly, in the DOL/DMI (Figure 1e), the peak for DOL
indicates the participation in solvation, but the contribution of
DOL was lower than that of G4, with a corresponding increase in
DMI coordination. Following this trend, the g(r) peaks of the co-
solvents of DEE (Figure 1f) and TTE (Figure 1g) were further re-
duced, and DMI became the dominant coordinating species. For
TTE/DMI, TTE molecules were absent from the inner solvation
shell and appeared only in the outer region, conﬁrming its lowest
solvation power among the cosolvents. The decrease in the sol-
vation capability of the cosolvent from G4 to TTE also inﬂuenced
the number of Li+ ions coordinating with S8
2−. Based on the val-
ues obtained from the ﬁrst g(r) peak, the coordination number
of DMI increased from 0.52 in G4/DMI to 1.17 in TTE/DMI,
accompanied by a corresponding increase in the number of co-
ordinated Li+ ions from 0.56 to 0.80. This change implies that
weaker Li+-cosolvent interactions promote stronger Li-S coordi-
nation, resulting in the lower solubility of LiPSs. Not only Li2S8
but also various liquid LiPSs such as Li2S4 and Li2S6 will exist in
the real electrolyte. Their solvation structures are similar to that
of Li2S8 (Figure S2, Supporting Information).
These simulation results are in good agreement with the ex-
perimentally observed trends in LiPSs solubility. Notably, despite
having a moderate DN, G4 exhibited the strongest solvation abil-
ity among the cosolvents. As shown in the calculated results in
Figure S3 (Supporting Information), G4 exhibited the highest
binding energy with lithium cations. This behavior is attributed
to the chelation eﬀect provided by G4’s long-chain molecular
structure, indicating that solvation power is inﬂuenced not only
by DN but also by molecular geometry.[28,29] These ﬁndings high-
light that, beyond donor number, the structural characteristics of
the cosolvent play a crucial role in determining solvation behav-
ior.
2.2. Impact of Cosolvent Regulation on Cycle Stability of Li-S
batteries
In addition to solvation behavior, cycling stability of Li-S cells was
investigated using diﬀerent electrolytes. As shown in Figure S4
(Supporting Information), the baseline electrolyte without cosol-
vent (DMI100) retained 62% of its initial capacity after 50 cycles.
However, as shown in Figure 2a, the G4/DMI and TTE/DMI elec-
trolytes showed lower retention of 52% and 45%, respectively,
while DOL/DMI exhibited a moderate improvement at 62%. Re-
markably, the DEE/DMI electrolyte demonstrated exceptional cy-
cling stability, retaining 97% of its initial capacity after 50 cycles.
It was also conﬁrmed that the DEE/DMI exhibits highly stable
cycling performance compared with the conventional DOL/DME
electrolyte in Figure S5 (Supporting Information). Representative
voltage proﬁles during cycling are shown in Figure 2c–f.
In the case of G4/DMI, the initial discharge capacity was ap-
proximately 1100 mAh g−1; however, it gradually decreased to
≈580 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles (Figure 2c). Both the 1st and 2nd
plateaus showed a signiﬁcant decrease within the initial 10 cycles,
indicating poor electrochemical stability during the early cycles.
DOL/DMI exhibited a higher initial capacity (≈1200 mAh g−1),
which decreased to ≈750 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles (Figure 2d).
Unlike G4/DMI, initial capacity was well maintained during the
initial 10 cycles, conﬁrming that the initial cycling stability was
superior compared to G4/DMI. DEE/DMI electrolyte showed
outstanding cycle performance, maintaining a high capacity of
1270 mAh g−1 even after 50 cycles from an initial capacity of
around 1300 mAh g−1 (Figure 2e). The capacity increased to ap-
proximately 1380 mAh g−1 within the ﬁrst ﬁve cycles. After 5th

The high-donor-number electrolyte DMI is known to be cor-
rosive to lithium metal, leading to poor cycling stability. To in-
vestigate the impact on lithium metal, the composition of the
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed in diﬀerent electrolytes
was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). In
the F 1s spectra in Figure 2g, signiﬁcant peaks of S–F (687.8
eV) and LiF (684.7 eV) were observed except for the DEE/DMI
sample.[30] Signals of both peaks decrease in the DEE/DMI, in-
dicating relatively F-deﬁcient SEI formation. In N 1s spectra in
Figure 2h, LiNxOy (399.5 eV) and Li3N (397.4 eV) are observed.[31]
Electrolytes with high solvation power, such as G4/DMI and
DOL/DMI, showed relatively weak intensities of the N com-
pounds, and intensities of both LiNxOy and Li3N are similar. In
contrast, stronger N-related signals, particularly LiNxOy appear
in the DEE/DMI and TTE/DMI electrolytes, suggesting the for-
mation of a more N-rich SEI layer. In particular, the DEE/DMI
electrolyte yielded an F-deﬁcient, N-rich SEI on lithium metal.
These results are consistent with the MD simulation ﬁndings
in Figure 1f, which revealed that DEE/DMI contains a high con-
centration of NO3
−anions within the LiPSs solvation shell. This
indicates an increased involvement of NO3−anions in the solva-
tion structure of Li+. In low solvating power electrolytes, cosol-
vents such as DEE and TTE are less involved in the coordination
with Li+, thereby allowing NO3
−anions to be more readily incor-
porated into its solvation shell. As shown in the radial distribu-
tion function (RDF) results for Li+ (Figure S6, Supporting Infor-
mation), both the g(r) peak and coordination number of NO3−in-
crease progressively as the solvation power decreases from G4 to
TTE. Because solvated anions exhibit a lower lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level, they are more suscepti-
ble to reduction. As a result, NO3
−is more readily reduced in
low-solvation-power electrolytes, promoting the formation of an

2.3. Impact of Cosolvent Regulation on Kinetic Characteristic of
Li-S Batteries
The kinetic performance of each electrolyte was evaluated by rate
capability tests, as shown in Figure 3a. Electrolytes with high sol-
vation power, such as G4/DMI and DOL/DMI, demonstrated su-
perior rate capability. Speciﬁcally, The G4/DMI electrolyte exhib-
ited a discharge capacity of 1064 mAh g−1 at 0.1C, and 870, 748,
675, and 556 mAh g−1 at 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2C, respectively, retain-
ing 87%, 71%, 61%, 55%, and 45% of the capacity compared to
that at 0.05C. Similarly, the DOL/DMI electrolyte delivered ca-
pacities of 1120, 960, 860, 784, and 694 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5,
1, and 2C, respectively, corresponding to 82%, 71%, 63%, 58%,
and 51% of the capacity at 0.05C, showing a comparable rate per-
formance to that of G4/DMI. However, the capacities of G4/DMI
and DOL/DMI dropped sharply at 5C, reaching 190 and 215 mAh
g−1, respectively. The corresponding capacities and voltage pro-
ﬁles are shown in Figure 3a and Figure S7 (Supporting Informa-
tion).
In contrast, electrolytes with lower solvation power, such as
DEE/DMI and TTE/DMI, exhibited inferior rate capability. Al-
though DEE/DMI electrolyte showed comparable rate capability
up to 0.5C (1148, 1035, 884 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5C), capac-
ity decreased dramatically to 237 mAh g−1 at 1C (only 18% of its
initial capacity at 0.05C) and further decreased to 49 mAh g−1 at
5C, indicating poor kinetics under fast reaction conditions. The
TTE/DMI, with the weakest solvation strength, retained 513 mAh
g−1 at 1C (38% of the 0.05C capacity), and the capacity dropped
to below 155 mAh g−1 from 2C onward. Interestingly, despite
the poor rate performance, DEE/DMI demonstrated the best ca-
pacity recovery at 0.1C after the rate test. The rate performance
were compared to conventional electrolyte, DOL/DME in Figure
S5 (Supporting Information).
To understand electrolyte-dependent kinetics, Potentiostatic
intermittent titration technique (PITT) measurements were con-
ducted in Figure 3c–f. When phase transition that requires signif-
icant activation energy occurs, leading to a characteristic current
hump. The voltage at which this transition occurs varies depend-
ing on the cosolvent, increasing in the order of G4, DOL, DEE,
and TTE (2.30, 2.05, 2.051, and 2.07 V, respectively), which cor-
relates with their decreasing solvation powers. The current be-
haviors at each voltage are compared in Figure 3b. With the max-
imum current as a reference point, the initial rise corresponds
to nucleation-dominated processes, while the subsequent decay
reﬂects growth-dominated behavior that is attributed to nucleus
impingement,[34] which reduces the available three-phase bound-
ary (TPB) between the electrolyte, Li2S, and conductive substrate
(illustrated in Figure 3b)
The reaction termination order of the Li2S formation process is
DOL/DMI > G4/DMI > DEE/DMI > TTE/DMI, with DOL pro-
moting the fastest kinetics, as shown in Figure S8 (Supporting
Information). For relatively high-solvation-power electrolytes like
G4/DMI and DOL/DMI, a hill-like current shape was observed
in Figure 3b, which indicates high current and short reaction
time. We deﬁned onset current as the value of 0.04 mA mgsulfur
−1,
where the current slope in each of the two graphs began to in-
crease sharply. DOL/DMI showed fast nucleation at 3,275 and
also reached its maximum peak current more quickly at 8,225 s.
On the other hand, G4/DMI exhibited the delayed nucleation on-
set at ≈11,307 s. However, growth reaction (current decay) termi-
nates very fast. For relatively lower-solvation-power electrolytes
such as DEE/DMI and TTE/DMI, the current proﬁle was more
ﬂattened (less current) and less deﬁned. While nucleation begins
earlier than high solvating power electrolytes, the growth reaction
is slower. Total reaction termination takes much longer than G4
and DOL.

electrolyte has high LiPS solubility, nucleation of Li2S from dis-
solved LiPS takes longer due to the high solubility. In con-
trast, limited solubility of LiPS can promote rapid precipitation
of solid Li2S. Indeed, DMI100 exhibits delayed nucleation like
G4/DMI in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). Thus, high LiPS

with the higher discharge capacities observed at 1C and 2C for
DOL/DMI.

mogeneous. However, at 60 vol%, a thin upper phase began to

3. Discussion: Feasibility of Li–S Cell Operation
Under Practical Conditions
High-donor-number electrolytes are known to exhibit high LiPS
solubility, which potentially reduces the E/S ratio, and they can
accelerate sulfur reduction reactions by facilitating 3D Li2S depo-
sition. However, it should be noted that not all high-donor elec-
trolytes enable stable operation under practical conditions. De-
spite their favorable donor characteristics, achieving reliable per-
formance in Li–S cells with high sulfur loading and lean elec-
trolyte conditions remains highly challenging.
Electrolyte impact on cell operation under practical conditions
was investigated. When Li-S cells operated under higher loading
density (>3.5 mgsulfur cm−2) and E/S ratio of 10 μl mgsulfur
−1, high
solvating electrolytes, DOL/DMI and G4/DMI exhibit compara-
ble capacities of ≈380 mAh g−1 and ≈450 mAh g−1, respectively.
On the other hand, relatively low-solvating electrolytes such as
DEE/DMI and TTE/DMI obtained very limited capacities (less
than 150 mAh g−1) in Figure S17 (Supporting Information).
These results are consistent with the rate capability, which indi-
cates fast Li2S transformation enables the cells to operate under
more practical conditions. Rationally, working with a high load-
ing density electrode corresponds to a large charge (Q) being pro-
cessed simultaneously, which corresponds to high current den-
sity (C-rate) operating conditions. Thus, ensuring suﬃciently fast
Li–S reaction kinetics is indispensable for practical application.
To demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed phase separa-
tion behavior under practical conditions, two diﬀerent electrolyte
modiﬁcation was conducted. First, DMI vol% increase, in or-
der to make Li2S phase transition fast; DMI:DEE = 8:2 vol%.
When Li2S are dissolved on discharging, local phase separation
can still occur under the volume ratio (Figure S19 and Table S5,
Supporting Information). With high loading density, 2.8 mgsulfur
cm−2 and 25 μl mgsulfur
−1, ≈550 mAh g−1 of discharge capac-
ity was achieved. Second strategy was introducing DOL into the
DEE/DMI electrolyte to facilitate the Li2S phase transition. The
DEE content was ﬁxed to remain the local phase separation, while
part of the DMI was replaced by DOL (DOL:DMI:DEE = 2.5:2.5:5
vol%). Under this formulation, higher capacities, ≈380 mAh g−1
were achieved with at 2.7 mgsulfur cm−2 and 30 μl mgsulfur
−1.
When even further decreasing E/S ratio to 10 μl mgsulfur
−1 and
3.5 mgsulfur cm−2, ≈300 mAh g−1 were achieved with stable cy-
cling for 60 cycles (Figure S18, Supporting Information).
These results demonstrate that local phase-separation elec-
trolytes can operate under practical conditions by tailoring sol-
vent composition. Further investigations should be conducted
to develop high-performance electrolytes for Li–S batteries. Be-
yond the electrolytes proposed in this study, new solvent systems
that balance moderate LiPS solvation with phase-separation be-
havior may represent a promising strategy for realizing stable,
high-performance Li–S batteries under practical conditions.
4. Conclusion
In this study, we investigated the eﬀect of co-solvents in a high-
donor-solvent DMI-based electrolyte on the electrochemical per-
formance of Li–S batteries. The solvation power for LiPSs de-
creased in the order of G4 > DOL > DEE > TTE, inﬂuenced
not only by the donor number (DN) but also by the molecu-
lar structure of each solvent. The solvation strength strongly af-
fected the redox behavior, where stronger solvation resulted in
a higher ﬁrst-discharge plateau and a lower second-plateau volt-
age. In terms of kinetics, the DOL/DMI electrolyte with moderate
solvating power exhibited the highest rate capability. High LiPS
solubility was found to delay Li2S nucleation, while the reduced
concentration of S3
•−radicals in low-solvation electrolytes may
also contribute to slower growth.
Interestingly, the DEE/DMI electrolyte demonstrated the high-
est cycling stability, retaining 91.5% of its initial capacity after
100 cycles. This superior performance is attributed to phase-
separation behavior, which induces local DEE-rich clusters that
hinder LiPS diﬀusion toward the lithium anode. As a result, the
shuttle eﬀect was eﬀectively suppressed, enabling long-term ca-
pacity retention. To enable operation under practical conditions
while maintaining phase-separation behavior, the solvent com-
position should be carefully tailored. Overall, this study high-
lights that cosolvent engineering not only tunes solvation char-
acteristics but also introduces phase behavior that regulates LiPS

5. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation:
All electrolytes were prepared in an Ar-ﬁlled
glove box with an O2 concentration below 0.1 ppm. Prior to electrolyte
preparation, all solvents, including DMI (99.5+ %, Sigma-Aldrich), G4
(99+ %, Sigma-Aldrich), DOL (99.8+ %, Sigma-Aldrich), DEE (99.9%,
Sigma-Aldrich), and TTE (95.0+ %, SejinCI), were treated with molecular
sieves to remove H2O. For the preparation of 50% co-solvent electrolytes,
LiTFSI (98.0+ %, SejinCI) and LiNO3 (99+ %, anhydrous, thermo scien-
tiﬁc) were ﬁrst dissolved in DMI and left until the solution became com-
pletely transparent. Subsequently, G4, DOL, DEE, and TTE were added in
equal volume to DMI and further left until a transparent solution was ob-
tained. The resulting co-solvent DMI (1:1 v%) electrolytes were prepared
to have concentrations of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.5 M LiNO3. The reference
electrolyte, DOL/DME, was prepared by mixing DOL and DME (99.0%,
SejinCI) in a 1:1 volume ratio and adding equimolar amounts of LiNO3
and LiTFSI. For shuttle current measurements, electrolytes were prepared
using the same procedure but without the addition of LiNO3. For Raman
spectroscopy measurements, a co-solvent DMI (3:1 v%) electrolyte was
prepared by dissolving 2 M LiTFSI and 1 M LiNO3 in DMI, followed by the
addition of a co-solvent in a volume three times that of DMI.
Electrochemical Performance Test:
The cathode was fabricated by a
slurry coating method. The slurry was prepared by mixing C(KB)/S com-
posite, carbon (carbon black, acetylene, 50% compressed, 99.9+ %), and
polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) at a mass ratio of 75:20:5. The electrode
contained 52.5 wt% sulfur. Then 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99+ %,
Sigma-Aldrich) was added to adjust the viscosity. The mixture was homog-
enized using a Thinky mixer for 20 minutes to obtain the ﬁnal slurry. The
slurry was then cast onto carbon paper and dried at 55 °C for 24 hours.
The loading of the prepared electrodes ranged from 1.0 – 1.9 mg cm−2
(0.5 – 1 mgsulfur cm−2) with 13 mm diameter. The diameters of the sepa-
rator and Li metal were 16 mm and 15 mm, respectively. All electrochem-
ical measurements were conducted using CR2032-type half-cell. The cells
were assembled in an argon-ﬁlled glove box using a lithium metal anode,
a monolayer polypropylene separator (Celgard 2400), and a C(KB)/sulfur
composite cathode. For the cycling test shown in Figure 5e, carbon-coated
separators and carbon-coated Al foil were used. The sulfur content in the
C/S composite was 75 wt%, and the active material–conductive additive–
binder was mixed in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 in the slurry, resulting in a sulfur
content of 60 wt% in the entire electrode. To validate the electrolyte per-
formance, reference cell data were presented in Figure S16 (Supporting
Information). The electrolyte was injected at a ratio of 25 μl mgsulfur−1.
Galvanostatic tests for the assembled coin cells were conducted using
a NEWARE BTS cycler at a constant temperature of 30°C. All electrolytes
were tested three times to ensure reproducibility. The tests were performed
at C-rates ranging from 0.01 C to 5 C (1 C = 1200 mA g−1) within a volt-
age range of 1.8 – 2.8 V. The C-rate of the cycle test was calculated using
1200 mAh g−1, which was a practical speciﬁc capacity, not a theoretical
capacity.[37]
The potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT) and shut-
tle current measurements were performed using WMPG 1000S cycler
(WonATech Co., Ltd.). For PITT, after the ﬁrst cycle at 0.1 C, the voltage
was decreased stepwise from 2.8 V in increments of 0.01 V. The current
response was recorded, and the measurement proceeded to the next step
once the current dropped below 0.01 C. Shuttle current measurements
were conducted following a similar protocol.[33] After the ﬁrst cycle at 0.1
C, the cell was charged to 2.8 V, and measurements began from 2.7 V.
Upon reaching the target voltage, the cell was allowed to rest for 1 hour
to stabilize the open-circuit voltage (OCV). The ﬁnal recorded OCV was
then applied for 2 hours, during which the resulting current response was
recorded as the shuttle current. This process was repeated stepwise by
decreasing 0.1 V for each step.
Electrode Characterization:
All electrodes for analysis were obtained by
disassembling the cells inside a glove box immediately after reaching the
target cycle and voltage. The extracted electrodes were washed with DOL
solvent and dried overnight. To minimize the inﬂuence of H2O and O2,
sample handling was conducted inside the glove box, and all measure-
ments were performed with minimal air exposure. The X-ray photoelec-
tron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the lithium metal after 21th discharge
was conducted using a Nexsa instrument with an Al-K𝛼(1486.6 eV) X-ray
source under vacuum conditions of 5.0 × 10−9 mbar. The obtained spec-
tra were calibrated by adjusting the hydrocarbon peak to 284.7 eV. The
deposition morphology of lithium metal after 21th charge was examined
using scanning electron microscope (SEM) with an SNE-4500M Plus in-
strument, operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.
Electrolyte Characterization:
For Raman spectroscopy analysis of the
liquid electrolytes, all liquid samples were sealed in vials inside the glove
box to prevent air exposure during measurement. Similarly, LiTFSI and
LiNO3 powders were sampled using a dedicated sealing holder within the
glove box to minimize moisture exposure. All Raman measurements were
conducted using an In Via Raman Microscope (RENISHAW) with a 785 nm
laser source, with the output power ﬁxed at 1%. Each sample was analyzed
over a spectral range of 100–4000 cm−1, and measurements were repeated
three times to ensure reproducibility.
The solvation power test for high concentration LiPSs was conﬁrmed by
dissolving 1M Li2S6 by adding Li2S and S powder in a stoichiometric ratio
to the prepared electrolyte and leaving it at 25 °C for one week without
stirring. The solubility of Li2S6 was determined by gradually adding 0.05
mL of electrolyte to a solution containing 0.1 M Li2S6, followed by stirring
for 2 hours and resting at room temperature for 2 hours without stirring.
The point at which no visible precipitate remained was recorded and used
for the solubility calculation.
Density Functional Theory (DFT) and Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simu-
lation:
All DFT calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 soft-
ware. The geometries of the molecules and Li+ ion-solvent complexes were
optimized in the gas phase at the B3LYP/6-311G+(d,p) level. Using the
optimized structures, the HOMO/LUMO energies and binding energies
were calculated. The binding energies were determined by subtracting the
total energy of the individual solvent and ion from those of the correspond-
ing Li+ ion-solvent complexes.
Molecular dynamics calculations were performed using LAMMPS
software.[38] The OPLS-AA force ﬁeld[39] was applied to the molecules.
The force ﬁelds for Li+, TFSI−, and NO3−ions were obtained from Dang
et al,[40] Gouveia et al.,[41] and Mendez-Morales, Trinidad, et al.,[42] re-
spectively. For the bonded parameters of S82−ions, the force ﬁeld from
Rajput et al.[43] was applied, while the Lennard-Jones (LJ) parameters for
S atoms were taken from the DREIDING force ﬁeld.[44] Restrained electro-
static potential (RESP)[45,46] charges were assigned to each atom in all
molecules. For Coulomb interactions in ionic molecules, the electronic
continuum correction (ECC) method[47,48] was applied, which implicitly
includes electronic polarizability eﬀects. According to Equation (1), par-
tial ionic charges (qi) were converted into scaled charges (qeﬀ
