ABSTRACT: The electroactivity of the sulfur composite cathode generally studied requires
high electrode porosity, which brings many constraints to the design of lithium−sulfur (Li−
S) batteries (e.g., electrolyte quantity and energy density). Here, we focus on electrolyte
engineering for highly stable covalent-type sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) to realize
practical Li−S full batteries with jointly improved volumetric energy density (Ev) and
cyclability. The conformal polycarbonate cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) derived by
cyclic carbonate is determined to play a fundamental role in eliminating the fatal shuttle
eﬀect, thereby safeguarding the “solid-phase” mechanism of SPAN. The tailored electrolyte
also induces a bilayered solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) with enhanced Li+ transport and
mechanical strength, which unlocks the compatibility of an ultrathin Li anode. Practical Li-SPAN pouch cells, composed of
high-capacity SPAN cathodes (4.08 mAh cm−2) and 1.2× excess Li anodes, can achieve an Ev of 615 Wh L−1 and show a cycle
life at least 7 times that of the conventional carbonate-based electrolyte.
L
ithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are recognized as
promising next-generation energy systems in pursuit
of high energy density, combining the large speciﬁc
capacities of Li (3862 mAh g−1) and S (1672 mAh g−1).1,2
Numerous researchers have made great contributions to
promoting the batteries’ cyclability during the past decade.3,4
However, a surplus of Li metal (>500 μm) and liquid
electrolyte (>20 μL mgS
−1) is generally necessary for the
classical “liquid” Li−S battery conﬁguration because of its
intrinsic redox reaction characteristics, which suppresses the
energy density (gravimetric energy density (Eg) < 200 Wh kg−1
and volumetric energy density (Ev) < 400 Wh L−1) and leads
to a long cycle illusion.5,6 The evaluation results based on “half-
cells” push the practical applications of Li−S batteries to the
limit.7 Designing full cells with stringent parameters (i.e., S
loading, negative to positive electrode (N/P) ratio, electrolyte-
to-S (E/S) ratio, and cathode density) is extremely crucial to
realizing the commercial value of Li−S batteries.8
In the construction of practical Li−S full batteries, the
challenges of Li−S electrochemistry become simultaneously
prominent:9 (1) the shuttle eﬀect and the high reactivity of Li
with electrolyte consume both S and Li inventory;10 (2) the
“solid−liquid” mechanism requires properly dissolved poly-
sulﬁdes to improve redox kinetics;11 (3) the porous sulfur/
carbon composite cathodes demand abundant electrolyte to
achieve good wetting;12 (4) enhancing cathode density may
block the Li+ diﬀusion pathway and have a negative eﬀect on
the S electroacitivity.13 These vital points limit the minimum
amount of Li and electrolyte and critical electrode porosity,
presenting challenges to the development of high-energy Li−S
batteries with superior performance. Recently, academia’s
awareness of devising Li−S full cells has been gradually raised.
Research interests are mainly focused on the design of
electrode frameworks (e.g., catalyst-modiﬁed carbon fab-
rics14−17 and lithiophilic 3D metal skeletons18−20), but the
introduction of porous hosts will intensify the compromise of
Ev.21 More importantly, the N/P ratio is rarely explicitly
controlled, which does not satisfy the eligibility criteria of Li−S
full cells. In this stalemate, turning to S cathodes with a “solid-
phase” mechanism, such as sulfurized polyacrylonitrile
(SPAN), ﬁnds a new path for Li−S full cells to coordinate
the contradiction between energy density and cyclability:22 (1)
the strong interaction between S fragments and polymer hosts
eliminates shuttle eﬀect, avoiding the internal coupling of S
and Li, increasing S utilization (nearly 100% Coulombic
eﬃciency (CE)), and inhibiting Li corrosion;23,24 (2) the
independence of redox kinetics on polysulﬁdes enables stable S
cycle with high compaction density and under a lean
electrolyte,25,26 opening up opportunities to meet the practical

Unfortunately, it has been found that the above properties of
SPAN cathodes are supported only in carbonate-based
electrolyte (stable cycle of more than 500 cycles),28 whereas
the dissolution of polysulﬁdes still exists in ether electrolyte,
which shows rapid capacity decay (70% capacity loss in 100
cycles).29 It is reasonable to speculate that the cathode−
electrolyte interphase (CEI) will play a pivotal part in
alleviating interface-related problems such as polysulﬁde
release,30−32 but the interaction mechanism between electro-
lytes and SPAN cathodes has received little attention. Besides,
the conventional electrolytes for SPAN cathodes cannot
eﬀectively passivate Li because of the formation of undesirable
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).33,34 Therefore, the further
development of Li-SPAN full cells will overwhelmingly depend
on the tailored electrode−electrolyte interphases of S and Li,
and designing adapted electrolytes undoubtedly represents a
shortcut favoring stable Li-SPAN full cells.
In this work, we demonstrate the role of cyclic carbonate in
converting SPAN from “solid−liquid” mechanism to “solid-
phase” mechanism in ether electrolytes through interface
analysis. Conformal polycarbonate-CEI tends to be formed in
EC-containing ether electrolyte, which is hypothesized to have
a stronger ability to hinder the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in
comparison to inorganic-rich CEI formed in common ether
electrolyte. Lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)/EC (2
M) is designed and identiﬁed as an electrolyte highly
compatible with both SPAN and Li metal. The “solid-phase”
mechanism and high Li CE jointly promise Li-SPAN full cells,
endowing them with a long cycle life close to 7 times that of
commercial carbonate-based electrolyte, with an N/P of 1.22
(the reversible areal capacity of 3.6 mAh cm−2). Notably, the
outstanding cycling performance can be reproduced in
practical Li-SPAN pouch cells delivering an Ev of 615 Wh
L−1, which outperforms most reported Li−S pouch cells. Such
advanced electrolyte exploitation supplies a potential strategy
toward electrode−electrolyte interphase control, pointing to
the possibility of practical Li−S full batteries with superior Ev
and cyclability.
Transition of Electrochemical Behavior. SPAN is
synthesized by solid-phase reaction of S and polyacrylonitrile
(PAN) under Ar atmosphere at 350 °C, with a uniform
morphology and 40.1% S content (Figure S1 and Table S1).
The 1 M LiFSI/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) is chosen as the
baseline ether electrolyte, where the Li-SPAN half cells exhibit
extremely poor capacity retention and anomalous CE
(>120%), as shown in Figure 1a. Such poor electrochemical
performance can be considered to result from the dissolution
of polysulﬁdes and the shuttle eﬀect, which is further veriﬁed
by UV−vis spectroscopy (Figure 1b). The color of the leaching
solution gradually darkens with time, and S4

that part of the active S follows the dissolution−precipitation
pathway. Notably, the above phenomena can be overturned
when replacing a small amount of ether solvent with EC (i.e., 1
M LiFSI/DME-EC, v/v 4:1). The introduction of EC allows
SPAN to restore the properties of the solid−solid conversion
mechanism, including ultrahigh reversible cycles, undetectable
polysulﬁde intermediates, and a single electrochemical plateau
(Figure 1a−c), which results in a long-term stable cycle.
However, the improvement of cyclability fails when switching
to linear carbonate (e.g., dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and
diethyl carbonate (DEC)), as shown in Figure S2, highlighting
that cyclic carbonate is the part that stabilizes SPAN cathodes
in the carbonate-based electrolyte.
The diﬀerence and change of electrochemical behavior are
interrogated through cyclic voltammetry (CV). In the baseline
electrolyte, there are three reduction peaks and two oxidation
peaks that can be divided into two couples after the ﬁrst cycle
of activation (Figure 1d), accordant with the discharge and
charge behavior indicated by the voltage proﬁle. The two peaks
at 2.3 and 2.0 V correspond to the reduction of pristine S to
long-chain polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) and then to Li2S2/
Li2S, while the 1.6 V peak is related to the conversion from
−Sx−(2 ≤x ≤4) to Li2S2/Li2S.35 It can be found that the
peaks of the “solid−liquid” couple are strengthened as the
cycle progresses, which suggests that the continuous
production and accumulation of polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte
increases the ratio of “solid−liquid” mechanism for the S cycle.
The inability to control the existence state of polysulﬁdes leads
to severe shuttle eﬀect, and speciﬁcally, for Li metal anode
(LMA), polysulﬁdes participate in the construction of SEI and
cause grievous Li corrosion (Figure S3a,b). In contrast, as
shown in Figure 1e, the EC-containing electrolyte just displays
the “solid-phase” couple, that is, the 1.7 V average reduction
peak and the 2.4 V average oxidation peak. Besides, the CV
curves that basically coincide from the second cycle reveal the
high stability of the electrode composition and structure.
Conﬁning the active S in the cathode molecular skeleton
simultaneously avoids its interference on LMA (Figure S3c,d).
The transition of electrolyte behavior analyzed above is
generally applicable to ether electrolyte systems with diﬀerent
Li salts or solvents (Figures S4 and S5).
The volume fraction of EC is further decreased in order to
investigate the working mechanism of the EC addition. Cycling
performance and voltage proﬁles similar to 20% EC (EC20)
can be observed in 15% EC (EC15) and 10% EC (EC10)
(Figure S6). Even 5% EC (EC05) can bias the S cycle behavior
toward the solid−solid conversion, despite the large polar-
ization and obvious capacity degradation within the ﬁrst 100
cycles. Such a small proportion of EC may well indicate that it
depends on the formation of an eﬀective CEI to complete the
shift of the redox mechanism (Figure 1f,g). It is worth noting
that the cycled SPAN cathodes in the EC-containing
electrolyte can maintain the “solid-phase” mechanism for
several cycles after changing into the EC-free ether electrolyte
(Figure S7), which further illustrates that EC’s stabilizing eﬀect
on SPAN can be attributed to the interface issue. However, the
subsequent CE exceeds 100% and continues to rise, which
suggests that the expected components of CEI are easily
destroyed and rely on the electrolyte coordination.36 There-

Chemistry at Cathode Interphases. High-resolution
transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) characterization
is carried out to understand the reasons for the excellent
cathode protection of the EC-containing electrolyte. As shown
in Figure S8, the spherical SPAN particles with an average
diameter of 150 nm agglomerate into large clusters, which is
consistent with the scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
images. Combined with the results of electron diﬀraction and
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) (Figure S9), it can be deduced that
SPAN is in an amorphous structure. The signal of S basically
overlaps the signals of C and N for energy dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Figure S10),
verifying the homogeneous distribution in SPAN. However,
additional surface layers, CEI layers, can be clearly observed
after 50 cycles in the two electrolytes. In 1 M LiFSI/DME, the
thickness of the CEI is quiet uneven, ranging from 5 to 14 nm,
and even some areas are not covered, which conduces to the
dissolution of polysulﬁdes (Figures 2a, S11, and S12a).
Besides, the CEI shows a heterogeneous structure containing
a large domain of crystalline inorganic nanoparticles embedded
in the amorphous components (Figure S12b−d). The lattice
fringes and the fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns reveal
the coexistence of LiF and Li2CO3. LiF may dominate the
inorganic components, as shown by the identiﬁed LiF (111)
electron diﬀraction pattern inserted in Figure 2a. By
comparison, the CEI formed in 1 M LiFSI/DME-EC
conformally encloses the SPAN particle even in a large
observed area, with an average thickness between 2.5 and 3.5
nm (Figures 2b and S13). Meanwhile, the CEI consists of
primarily amorphous materials, which is probably derived from
EC decomposition/polymerization. One possible explanation
for this is that almost all the EC cosolvent molecules in 1 M
LiFSI/DME-EC participate in the primary solvation sheath of
Li+ owing to the strong Li+−EC interactions,37 dominating the
formation reaction of the CEI.38 The change in solvation
structure is further conﬁrmed by
17O nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) (Figure S14), where the chemical shift of
carbonyl 17O (214.2 ppm) experiences an upﬁeld displacement
in EC-containing electrolytes due to the shielding eﬀect of Li+
on the lone pair electron density.
In parallel, EDS expresses the chemical information on CEIs.
The signal of F obviously appears, and the signal of O is
enhanced to a certain extent after cycling. Furthermore, the
signals of F and O have a larger region along the contour of the
signal of S according to elemental mapping (Figures S15 and
S16), determining the existence of F and O in the CEIs, which
is further conﬁrmed by line scan where the signals of F and O
have concentrated distribution areas at the two edges of
particles (Figure 2c−e). It should be noted that the higher
intensity of the F K-edge map and two more prominent peaks
of the F K-edge line scan with 1 M LiFSI/DME prove that the
corresponding CEI is richer in F-containing compounds
derived by LiFSI than that with 1 M LiFSI/DME-EC. Instead,
the signal intensity increment of both F and O is relatively
small, and the bimodal distribution form of O is much more
obvious than that of F, within the CEI induced by the EC-
containing electrolyte, indicating that this thin and conformal
CEI is mainly composed of organic species.

The models of CEIs in the two electrolytes can be described
by the diagrams in Figure 3b,c. Mixed CEI with high inorganic
content is inclined to be formed in routine ether electrolytes
but presents defective accumulation. The incomplete con-
formation provides the access for direct contact between
electrolyte and SPAN, which engenders the constant release of
polysulﬁdes, promotes the transition to a “solid−liquid”
mechanism, and results in capacity degradation. As for the
EC-containing electrolyte, the enrichment in organic phase
endows the CEI with an intimate composition, which helps
eliminate the polysulﬁde leakage from the SPAN framework.
Meanwhile, the compact CEI prevents further invasion of the
electrolyte, alleviates the continuous decomposition of LiFSI
and solvents, and thus possesses a self-controlled thickness,
similar to the properties of the SEI. Importantly, the conformal
structure makes the tailored CEI cocoon the SPAN particles,
ensuring the ability to control the electrochemical behavior of
active S.
Design of Electrolyte for Li-SPAN Batteries. Although
it has been determined that the EC-containing ether
electrolytes can keep the good cycle stability of SPAN
cathodes, they are generally not compatible with LMAs
(Figure S19), which manifests as poor reversibility, and are
prone to a drastic capacity drop when limiting the N/P ratio.
To realize Li−S full batteries, it is important to eﬀectively
stabilize the Li deposition. We design the LiFSI/EC electrolyte
with 2 M salt concentration, of which the ionic conductivity is
about 5.6 × 10−3 S cm−1 and the melting point is around 3.6
°C (Figure S20). Li/Cu cells are assembled to gauge the Li CE
at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure 4a). Markedly
improved Li CE is observed when 2 M LiFSI/EC is employed,

tested to quantitatively evaluate the long-term stability of

cells after 30 cycles is estimated by the Tafel slopes. The i0 with

M LiFSI/EC-DEC (0.22 mA cm−2) (Figure S21a), from which

in the reference electrolyte hinders the charge-transfer kinetics.
The above testing results indicate that linear carbonate is
detrimental to Li metal, possibly because of severe
decomposition, and that cyclic carbonate has the function of
stabilizing Li deposition, which is in line with the views of
recent research.41

The morphology of LMAs from disassembled cells conﬁrms
our speculation about the Li electrochemical behavior. As for
the Li+ plating on the Cu current collector at 1 mA cm−2 and 3
mAh cm−2, in 1 M LiFSI/EC-DEC, the deposited Li particles
with random diameters pile up in a disordered manner,
shaping a porous and loose microstructure with a thickness of
35 μm (Figure 4d), because the heterogeneous mosaic SEI is
unable to ﬁne-tune the Li deposition behavior. The huge
volume expansion exacerbates the electrolyte corrosion and the
risk of dead Li formation. With the protection of the modiﬁed
SEI in 2 M LiFSI/EC, the Li deposits own a relatively regular
morphology with a widened diameter, and the thickness of the
Li deposition layer is greatly reduced to 20 μm (Figure 4e),
which can be ascribed to the fact that the well-controlled Li+
ﬂux contributes to the dense construction. In the meantime,
the smooth surface shown in Figure S23 lays a foundation for
high-safety LMAs. More obvious divergences in the degree of
Li pulverization can be heeded for the cycled LMAs (Figure
S24). The Li foil cycled in 1 M LiFSI/EC-DEC (mainly SEI
and dead Li) is full of cracks, and its thickness increased from
45 to 144 μm, while 36 μm of fresh Li inventory still remains
in 2 M LiFSI/EC. The Li layer involved in the cycle holds the
compact and ﬂat morphology; in other words, there are
unimpeded electronic channels, which reinforces the con-
clusions from the electrochemical test results.
Electrochemical Performance of Li-SPAN Full Cells.
The electrode/electrolyte interphases (i.e., CEI and SEI)
tailored by 2 M LiFSI/EC create the possibility for the Li−S
full batteries to be able to cycle well under practical testing
conditions (45 μm ultrathin LMAs are used here). The cycling
performance of working batteries matching SPAN cathodes
with medium areal capacity (i.e., 1.86 mAh cm−2, the
corresponding N/P ratio of 3.59) is ﬁrst appraised (Figure
5a). Beneﬁtting from the conformal CEIs formed by the
ingredient EC, the stable SPAN cathode cycle is supported
before the battery failure in all investigated electrolytes.
However, the full cells with 1 M LiPF6/EC-DEC can barely
exhibit 36 cycles at 0.5 C, and only a triﬂing improvement (45
cycles) is realized when the Li salt is replaced with LiFSI. With
the better Li stability in 2 M LiFSI/EC, the lifespan of the full
cells is lengthened signiﬁcantly, retaining 80% of reversible
capacity after 120 cycles. Postcycling characterization of LMAs
provides direct understanding of the battery deterioration
mechanism (Figure S25). In 1 M LiFSI/EC-DEC, powdered
Li piles up chaotically and even detaches from the Cu current
collector, which sets oﬀthe loss of electronic connection and
complete consumption of active Li. In contrast, sharply shaped
Li chunks can still be discerned, and the Li erosion is assuaged
greatly, accompanied by a limited volume expansion, under the
passivation of cyclic carbonate. Furthermore, through electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) (Figures 5d and S26
and Table S2), it is surprisingly found that cyclic carbonate is
likely to favor SPAN with faster reaction kinetics, according to
the charge-transfer resistance (Rct) of SPAN cathodes, which
accounts for the main decrease in the two electrolytes.
The salient merits brought by the tailored CEI and SEI can
be better realized when engaging high-loading SPAN cathodes
(3.64 mAh cm−2), because the excessively low N/P ratio
(1.22) and ghastly large Li volume ﬂuctuation (18.2 μm per
half cycle theoretically) will accelerate battery death. As
expected, 2 M LiFSI/EC enables Li-SPAN full cells with
superior cycling performance (69 cycles; only 10 cycles in 1
M/LiFSI/EC-DEC) at 0.3 C (Figure S27). More impressively,
the single-layer pouch cell (100 mAh) assembled by a 50 μm
LMA and a thick SPAN cathode with 4.08 mAh cm−2 S can
still show remarkable cyclability, as shown in Figure 5b,c. A
high capacity retention of 75% is attained after 85 cycles under
demanding conditions, and the calculated Ev is up to 615 Wh
L−1 (Figure S28 and Table S3), which corroborates the
potency of the tailored electrolyte for scaling eﬀect and the
feasibility of practical application. The Ev can be further
boosted to 659 Wh L−1 for punch cells at 1 Ah level (5-layer
cathodes). Compared to the reported Li−S full cell
conﬁgurations in the literature in Figure 5e and Table S4,
the electrochemical performance of Li-SPAN full batteries
using 2 M LiFSI/EC in our work has highly competitive
advantages. Moreover, we further control the E/S ratio to 5 μL
mgS
−1, which allows batteries higher energy density, but it is
challenging for coin cells owing to their vast dead volume.
Comfortingly, the Li-SPAN full cells have the ability to
maintain a long stable cycle under lean electrolyte (Figure
S29). However, the occurrence of overcharge makes the
batteries terminate prematurely, which can be explained as the
micro-short circuit caused by uneven electrolyte distribution or
partial exhaustion. Further optimization of the conﬁguration,
such as using separators with better mechanical strength,
developing compatible electrolytes with higher Li CE, is
underway to promote the practicality of Li−S full cells.
In summary, we reported that cyclic carbonate helps to
sustain the “solid-phase” mechanism for SPAN redox by
forming a conformal polycarbonate-CEI (i.e., poly(CO3) and
−(CH2CH2O)n−) that can eﬀectively prevent the dissolution
of polysulﬁdes, which provides design guidance for the
electrolyte adapting to SPAN cathodes. Further, 2 M LiFSI/
EC is developed as a tailored electrolyte for Li-SPAN full
batteries. It is discovered that this cyclic carbonate-based
electrolyte not only has an excellent compatibility with SPAN
cathodes but more importantly achieves stable Li deposition
because of the formation of a bilayered SEI. The dense Li2O
outer layer and the nanocrystallite-rich (i.e., Li2O and Li2CO3)
inner layer ensure uniform and fast Li+ transport in the SEI,
leading to high CE of LMAs with greatly alleviated
pulverization. The optimization of interface engineering
enables working Li-SPAN pouch cells with a low N/P ratio
of 1.20 and a high reversible areal capacity of 4.08 mAh cm−2
to stably cycle over 85 cycles as well as deliver a high Ev of 615
Wh L−1, providing a speciﬁc example for breaking the mutual
restriction between Ev and cycling performance in Li−S
batteries. The electrolyte design principles described in this
work demonstrate the practical value and application potential
of Li−S full batteries.
