Investigation and Determination of Electrochemical Reaction
Kinetics in Lithium-Sulfur Batteries with Electrolyte LiTFSI in
DOL/DME
Sean Grabe,1 Matthew Dent,1 Shumaila Babar,1,2 Teng Zhang,1,2 Stephen Tennison,1,2 John
F. Watts,1,2 and Constantina Lekakou1,2,z
1Centre of Engineering Materials, School of Mechanical Engineering Sciences, University of Surrey, Guildford GU2 7XH,
United Kingdom
2The Faraday Institution, Quad One, Harwell Campus, Didcot OX11 0RA, United Kingdom
A major challenge in the simulation of Li-S batteries is that the electrochemical reaction parameters supplied from the ﬁtting of 0-d
or 1-d models depend on the cathode and separator microstructure, so these parameters cannot be used in the design and
optimization of material microstructure. The present investigation ﬁts the electrochemical reaction kinetics employing a continuum
model taking into account the pore size distribution and tortuosity of cathode and separator in the transport of sulfur molecules and
ion species (Li+ ion, electrolyte and sulﬁde anions) in solvated or desolvated form depending on pore size. Hence, the speciﬁed
reaction kinetics parameters are independent from the material microstructure. The Li-S redox reaction model includes six redox
reactions in the cathode and the lithium redox reaction at the anode. Reactions are assumed to take place in the electrolyte solution
rather than in the solid phase of sulfur or lithium sulﬁde precipitates, where dissolution/precipitation kinetics is modeled especially
for the low solubility compounds: sulfur, Li2S and Li2S2. The ﬁtting exercise is conducted based on experimental data of a cyclic
voltammetry cycle accompanied by in operando UV–vis spectroscopy. The investigated battery has electrolyte LiTFSI in DOL/
DME and no electrocatalysts in any part of the cell.

Intensive research in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries worldwide
has been offering optimized electrolytes,1 novel cathode materials
that might trap soluble polysulﬁdes via physical microstructural
restriction,2–4
adsorption
effects5
or
high
tortuosity
of
2D
materials,6–8 and selective separators.9,10 The complex and com-
peting processes occurring in Li-S batteries and affecting their
performance create the need for physicochemical models at con-
tinuum level for the design of materials and optimization of the
operation conditions for the formation cycles, battery cycling and
possibly recovery.
Zero-dimensional (0-d) models constitute the starting step,
focusing on the modeling of the electrochemical reactions where
for each reaction the equilibrium potential is given by the Nernst
equation (as a function of the reactants and products concentration),
the current associated with each electrochemical reaction is related
to the potential by the Butler-Volmer equation, and the two are
combined in the equation of reaction kinetics governing the rate of
evolution of each sulﬁde. Two-step reaction is considered in many 0-
d models in the literature covering the high and low voltage plateau
in the charge-discharge curves of Li-S batteries.11,12 Sometimes such
models are enhanced with an empirical term representing polysulﬁde
shuttling11,12 but such terms and any associated empirical coefﬁ-
cients are not related to the microstructure and functional groups of
cathode and separator. One-dimensional (1-d) models for Li-S
batteries13–15 have included ion transport equations with terms for
ion migration under the drift current, ion diffusion, dissolution and
precipitation of species and rate of reaction but are still limited to a
single pore size and porosity that might change with time or position.
Many of these 1-d models also consider multistage reactions.
However, the issue is that ﬁtting the reaction kinetics for such
models is dependent on a particular microstructure and functional
groups of the cathode and separator of the battery from which the
experimental data used for the ﬁtting has been derived. The so
determined reaction constants and parameters cannot be used
universally for the design of materials of Li-S batteries or for the
optimization of the operation of any other Li-S battery with different
cathode and separator materials.
For this reason, we have developed a continuum model of ion
transport taking into account the pore size distribution of the cathode
and separator, based on previous ion transport model without any
redox reactions which was validated for electrochemical double
layer capacitors (EDLCs).16,17 This has been extended in the present
study for Li-S batteries including a multistep reaction mechanism
depending on the electrolyte system and possibly electrocatalyst
cathode host or additives. More speciﬁcally, a popular electrolyte for
Li-S batteries, 1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME 1:1 v/v (DOL: 1,3
dioxolane; DME: 1,2-dimethoxyethane), is considered in which the
chain of redox reactions in cathode follows the following order
during discharge18: from S8, to Li2S8, to Li2S6, to Li2S4, to Li2S2 and
to Li2S, with the reverse reactions and reaction chain during charge,
where a typical carbonaceous cathode host is assumed without any
electrocatalyst effects in cathode, separator or any additives. A redox
reaction from Li to Li+ ions takes place at the anode.
This model is then employed in simulations of the cyclic
voltammetry (CV) in charge and discharge of a Li-S battery cell
to ﬁt the kinetics of the above electrochemical reactions as well as an
additional redox reaction in cathode for the conversion of S4
allotrope in ultramicropores to Li2S4. The model ﬁtting and
determination of the reaction kinetics parameters is conducted on
the basis of CV data, accompanied by in operando UV–vis data from
the experimental part of this study.

The system of Eqs. 1–7 and (SI.1)–(SI.16) is solved by applying

Experimental part
The cathode host was activated carbon fabric (ACF) ACF Kynol®
ACC-507–15 (Kynol Europe GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) of
0.6 mm thickness, areal density of 12 mg cm−2 and speciﬁc surface
area SSABET = 1461 m2 g−1.24–26 After drying ACF samples
overnight, an equal mass of sulfur (12 mg cm−2) was sprinkled on
the top surface of each sample. The sulfur-ACF samples were each
placed in aluminium foil “parcels” which were fold-closed manually
leaving an open volume enclosed above the sample. The parcels
were placed in a stainless-steel sealed pressure vessel. The vessel
was placed on a hot plate at 175 °C for 2 h.24 The vessel was allowed
to cool to room temperature before the samples were removed. The
samples were weighed before and after the procedure yielding 35 wt
% sulfur content in the composite S-ACF cathode. Figure 2 shows
the discretized pore size distributions (PSDs) of the ACF (from
experimental data of N2 adsorption17) and 35 wt% S-ACF cathode as
fabricated (from simulations of the sulfur inﬁltration of ACF under
heat using the model and algorithm described in24,25). Given the
dimensions of the pores and sulfur allotrope molecules, 0.7 nm and
0.4 nm for S8 and S4 respectively,19,24,25 sulfur in pores of width
greater than or equal to 0.7 nm is assumed to be in the form of S8
allotrope, sulfur in pores of width between 0.7 and 0.4 nm is
assumed to be in the form of S4 allotrope and pores of width less
than 0.4 nm cannot accommodate any sulfur. These discretized PSDs
of Fig. 2 have been inputted as initial condition of the cathode
microstructure in the simulations of this study using the multipore
model described in the previous modeling section.
This S-ACF cathode was cut to a 5 × 5 mm specimen and
combined with an aluminium current collector (cathode side) with a
small hole, a Celgard separator, lithium foil anode cut to 5 × 5 mm
and electrolyte 1 M LiTFSI and 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME 1:1 v/v.
The assembly was housed in a cell holder with a quartz glass
window on the side of the cathode, by the hole of the aluminium
current collector on the cathode (Fig. 3).
This Li-S battery cell was tested using a Gamry Interface 1010E
potentiostat where it was subjected to EIS (electrochemical im-
pedance spectroscopy) testing in potentiostatic mode in the fre-
quency range of 10 mHz—1 MHz and a CV test of one cycle of
discharge/charge at a scan rate of 4 × 10−2 mV s−1. At the same
time in operando UV–vis spectroscopy was conducted on the
cathode window every 10 min using a StellarNet system consisting
of a portable BLK CXR UV–vis spectrometer with 273–900 nm
range, a SL1-SL3 Combo deuterium and halogen light source for
200–2300 nm range, and a R600–8-UVVIS-SR reﬂectance probe on
a stand (see diagram in Fig. 3). The reﬂectance derivatives of the
obtained UV–vis spectroscopy data was ﬁtted against peak wave-
length values expected for sulfur: 345 nm27 and each sulﬁde in DOL:
DME from the literature28,29 as follows: Li2S8: secondary peak
560 nm (primary peak of 515 nm is close with the peaks of other
sulﬁdes), Li2S6: 530 nm (primary peak), Li2S4: 470 nm, Li2S2:
430 nm (primary peak), and Li2S: 450 nm (primary peak). The
derivatives of the spectra were used to increase clarity on peak
location and peak area.30
Results
Figure 4 displays the spectra of the in operando UV–vis
spectroscopy monitoring the cathode during the ﬁrst CV cycle of
the Li-S cell at 0.04 mV s−1. Due to practicalities in this experiment,
this scan rate was selected so that it was sufﬁciently slow to offer the
best possibility for the dissolution of the impregnated sulfur in
discharge while the test duration of each CV stage (discharge or
charge) was limited to about 7 h so that all UV–vis spectroscopy
took place during one working day with the researcher in attendance.

Discussion
The ﬁtting of the electrochemical reaction parameters in this
study was essentially conducted against the experimental CV data.
The in operando UV–vis spectra are compared a posteriori against
the predicted concentration proﬁles of sulfur and sulﬁdes. There are
two reasons why the UV–vis data was not used to ﬁt the reaction
kinetics parameters: (i) The reﬂectance derivative peaks of some
sulﬁdes are relatively close as can be seen in Fig. 4, resulting in peak
overlap for Li2S, Li2S2 and Li2S4 and loss of precision. Furthermore,
the very close proximity of the primary peak for Li2S8 to that of
Li2S6 led to selecting to follow the secondary peak of Li2S8, which is
much weaker in standard solutions.28 (ii) It is uncertain whether the
UV–vis spectroscopy data represented only the cathode surface by
the quartz glass of the battery cell (x = xL) or also some depth into
the cathode (given the large pores between ﬁbers and ﬁber tows in
the ACF of the order of 10–100 μm). It is certainly expected that
only the micro-, meso- and macropores of the surface of ﬁbers were
represented rather than the bulk of the ﬁbers. Hence, it is unclear
whether Fig. 6(b) corresponds to x = xL (cathode/current collector)
or to x = xS (cathode/separator) in Fig. 7 or an intermediate location
through the cathode thickness.
The present study and determination of the parameters of the
proposed seven electrochemical reactions in the DOL/DME electro-
lyte solution is a signiﬁcant advancement compared to the 1-d model
of Kumaresan at al.13 because (a) it takes into account the pore size
distribution of the cathode and any resulting microstructure re-
stricting effects or allowances of sulﬁde and sulfur migration in
interpore transport and transport to and from the separator and
anode; (b) it considers dissolution and precipitation kinetics as a
function of the dynamically changing surface area of the solid-
electrolyte interface for the low solubility sulﬁdes, Li2S and Li2S2,
and sulfur, with the dissolution/precipitation kinetics parameters
experimentally determined in the studies of our group,20,21 whereas
no interfacial area dependence is considered in13 and their dissolu-
tion/precipitation parameter values were assumed.
Model B is clearly better than Model A according to the ﬁt of
predictions against experimental data in Fig. 5. This is justiﬁed by
the use of realistic dissolution/precipitation model for the low
solubility sulﬁdes and sulfur, especially where Model B considers
the low solubility of Li2S2 whereas Model A considers that Li2S2 has
similarly high solubility as the higher order polysulﬁdes. The
reaction kinetics parameter ﬁtting trial-and-error procedure started,
in fact, with Kumaresan’s reaction kinetics parameters as a ﬁrst
guess which was improved in an iterative trial-and-error process
with the new inputted reaction kinetics parameters at each new
iteration run speciﬁed by the user. Both Tables I and II compare the
reaction kinetics parameters of the best ﬁts of Model A and B
respectively to experimental CV data with the corresponding
parameters of Kumaresan et al.’s13 study. The value for io,j,ref is
the same in all cases for j = 1, the lithium redox reaction at the
anode. The values for io,j,ref for the rest of reactions at the cathode (j
= 2 to 7) vary from those of Kumaresan’s especially for Model B for
j = 2 (S8 to Li2S8 reaction is much slower in our Model B) and for j
= 6 and 7 (Li2S4 to Li2S2 and then to Li2S reactions are much faster
in our Model B). For j =2, this can be attributed to the low value
Csat,Li2S8 = 38 mol m−3 assumed in13 against the experimental data
value of Csat,Li2S8 = 750 mol m−3,20,21 used in the present study with
dissolution kinetics depending on the large interfacial area of
cathode in this study, which in this case makes the value determined
in this study for Model B more accurate. However, the present study
assumes electrochemical reactions occurring only in the solution
phase and not in the precipitates. This is of particular signiﬁcance for
Li2S2 and its conversion to Li2S, especially in the microporous
cathode host of this study. It is possible that some degree of reaction
may take place in solid or semisolid state,4 in which case some io,j,ref
values may change, especially for reactions j = 2, 6 and 7 that
involve low solubility compounds.
Conclusion
A continuum model based on the ion transport and electroche-
mical reactions taking into account the pore size distribution of the
cathode and separator has been applied to a Li-S battery to simulate
the ﬁrst CV discharge-charge cycle. The CV cycle simulations have
been used to determine the electrochemical reaction parameters for
seven Li-S redox reactions, six at the cathode and one at the lithium
metal anode by ﬁtting the predictions to experimental data from the
CV test. The so determined electrochemical reaction parameters are
for electrolyte LiTFSI in DOL/DME and a cathode and separator of
any microstructure but without any functional or electrocatalyst
groups. The model that best ﬁtted the CV data included dissolution/
precipitation kinetics for S8, Li2S2 and Li2S depending on the solid/
liquid interfacial area, with values for saturation concentrations and
mass transfer coefﬁcients from previous studies by our group.20,21
The beneﬁt of this model is that the electrochemical reaction
constants and parameters determined in this study for Li-S batteries
can be used universally for the design of cathode and separator
materials of Li-S batteries including their microstructure and layer
thickness or for the optimization of the operation of this or any other
Li-S battery with different cathode and separator materials of
different dimensions and microstructure.
The set of electrochemical reaction parameters determined in this
study can also be used for any other non-polar electrolytes, such as
LiTFSI in TEGDME, provided they do not contain any reaction
affecting agents such as mediators or electrocatalysts. Of course,
some new physical data would be needed, such as the saturation
concentrations and dissolution/precipitation kinetics of sulfur and
lithium sulﬁdes in the new solvent system,20,21 coordination number
and dimensions of all solvated and desolvated ions (electrolyte
cation and anion, and sulﬁde ions) and sulfur molecule.19,31 On the
other hand, the ﬁtting exercise for the determination of reaction
constants would need to be repeated for polar electrolyte solvents
such as EC or DMSO in which additional cyclic (non-electroche-
mical) polysulﬁde reactions have been proposed.18 A new ﬁtting
exercise is also needed for Li-S batteries with electrocatalyst groups
or compounds in cathode, separator or electrolyte which are clearly
going to modify the reaction kinetics.
