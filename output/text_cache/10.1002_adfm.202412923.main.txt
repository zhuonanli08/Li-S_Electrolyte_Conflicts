Potassium 3-Thiophenetriﬂuoroborate Based Preferential
Redox toward Highly Eﬃcient Bilateral Protection for Full
Li–S Batteries
Fuyong Liu, Dexiang Xu, Zifeng Liu, Lu Wang,* Miao Chen, Dan Zhou, and Zhubing Xiao*
Electrolyte additives have been promising strategies aimed at lithium metal
dendrite growth and active materials loss of sulfur cathode those have
troubled the development of Li-S batteries (LSBs), while current interface
modulations of electrodes are still challenged by low protection eﬃciency and
high N/P ratio. Herein, potassium 3-thiophenetriﬂuoroborate (KPTB) is
adopted as an additive to construct versatile interface for both lithium metal
anode (LMA) and sulfur cathode. The preferentially electrochemical reduction
and oxidation of PTB (3-thiophenetriﬂuoroborate) moiety, as well as
electrostatic shielding exerted by K+ ion, enable F-rich solid electrolyte
interphase for LMA and construct polythiophene based protection layers on
the two electrodes, facilitating fast and uniform ion/electron transfer across
the interface, suppressing polysulﬁde outﬂow and maintaining integrity of
electrodes. Electrochemical measurements indicate that optimized dosage of
KPTB in electrolyte remarkably enhances reversibility and cyclic stability of the
two electrodes, and endows LSBs with excellent performances over wide
temperature range (−25–50 °C). Remarkably, the Li-S full cells using a 75 μm
of LMA can deliver a capacity of 6.0 mAh cm−2 and excellent cyclic
performances for 350 cycles, at a N/P ratio as low as 1.4. This work can
provide new reliable solution for electrolyte additive based interface
protections in LSBs.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are consid-
ered as one of the most promising can-
didates for next-generation secondary bat-
teries, due to the high theoretical capacity
(1675 mAh g−1 for sulfur and 3800 mAh g−1
for Li) and high theoretical energy density
(2600 Wh kg−1), along with resourcefully
abundant and environmentally friendly sul-
fur active materials.[1–3] It is also noted
that, there are still many factors hindering
their development to commercialization.
For the sulfur cathode, the “shuttling eﬀect”
of dissolved intermediate lithium polysul-
ﬁdes (LiPSs) results in fast capacity decay,
low Coulombic eﬃciency, or even batter-
ies failure.[4–6] Regarding the lithium anode,
the tameless growth of lithium dendrites
could not only induce an unfavorable solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer and forma-
tion of “dead” lithium, which hinders Li-ion
diﬀusion and increase voltage hysteresis,
but also leads to depletion of the electrolyte
to exacerbate the situation or even cause ex-
plosion hazards due to Li metal penetrating
across the separator.[7–9] Moreover, the dis-
solution of Li2Sx might corrode the lithium
metal anode (LMA) via a widely known
self-discharge process, and aggravate the degradation of LMA.
The eﬀorts regarding sulfur host modiﬁcations have remarkably
reinforced the restraint toward shuttle eﬀect of LiPSs and im-
proved the conversion kinetics of sulfur-contained species. And
various reservoir structures have been reported to regulate the
platting behaviors of Li+ ion and the derived morphologies of the
electrochemically deposited Li metal, to construct dendrite-free
LMA. Compared with the unilateral structural modiﬁcations of
sulfur host or LMA, the electrolyte-mediated electrochemistry of
Li-S systems dictates the key role of electrolyte or the solvation
structure of electrolyte, and electrolyte modiﬁcation has been one
of the hot areas in Li-S community.[10–12]
One of the mainstream strategies for electrolyte modiﬁcation
in Li-S systems is regulating the solvation structure of Li+ cation
as well as polysulﬁdes anion via tailoring the electrolyte concen-
tration or screening the solvating power of concerned solvents,
which eﬀectively inhibited the dissolution of polysulﬁdes, and in-
creased the redox reversibility of liquid-solid conversion of sulfur

species. Additionally, various additives have been also introduced
to construct electrochemically active mediators thus to boost the
redox conversion kinetics among sulfur-contained species, as
well as lowering the activation barrier of Li2S.[13–15] The solva-
tion structure regulations and additive adoptions have remark-
ably improved the performances of LSBs in terms of sulfur cath-
ode or LMA. However, some pending issues still need to be re-
solved, such as that the solvents with strong solvating those are
aimed to increase sulfur utilization and mitigate voltage polariza-
tion have been challenged by poor compatibility with the highly
active LMA, and the detachment of polysulﬁdes anion from sul-
fur cathode toward LMA needs further circumvention. Moreover,
the SEI of LMA formed in conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) electrolytes containing LiNO3 addi-
tive is porous and brittle, making it highly susceptible to fracture
during cycling-induced volume changes.[16,17] Bilateral interfacial
protections are strongly anticipated for both the sulfur cathode
and LMA. The sulfur-contained additives including polymers and
monomer molecules have been veriﬁed to construct SEI layers,
regulating the interfacial structures of both sulfur cathode and
LMA and improving the performances of LSBs.[18–21] However,
the generally involved reactions between additives and the active
materials of sulfur and lithium metal might compromise the ca-
pacity delivery, and cause unconformal protection of the individ-
ual electrode, thus excessive Li metal (thickness of ≈500 μm or
N/P>50) has been usually needed for the reported LSBs. Addi-
tives that could form stable and highly eﬀective protection layers
by themselves for both sulfur cathode and LMA are therefore de-
sired in electrolyte modiﬁcation.
Here, a potassium 3-thiophenetriﬂuoroborate (KPTB) has
been presented as an additive for the lithium bis(triﬂuorome-
thanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI)/DOL/DME-based electrolyte in Li-
S system. The dissociated K+ ions with delayed deposition could
attach on the LMA to exhibit electrostatic shielding toward Li+
ion, promoting uniform stripping/platting of Li and subduing
lithium dendrites formation. PTB with favorable HOMO (high-
est occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital) is prone to oxidation and reduction, in com-
parison with other ingredients in DOL/DME based electrolyte.
And it has been also elaborated experimentally that, F-rich in-
organic component in the SEI of LMA could be obtained from
reductive decomposition of BF3 moieties, thus the inorganic LiF
stuﬃng with low activation energy for Li+ diﬀusion could facil-
itate uniform Li+ ions ﬂux and compact deposition as lithium
metal, and oﬀer high surface energy and satisfying mechanical
strength, eﬀectively suppressing the growth of lithium dendrites.
Moreover, the thiophene group (deﬁned as TH) itself experiences
an electrochemical oxidation polymerization or reduction poly-
merization to form a PTH (polythiophene) polymer protection
layer on sulfur cathode and LMA, respectively. The PTH polymer
with acceptable conductivity is aimed to refrain LiPSs dissolu-
tion out of the sulfur cathode and complement electron transfer
for sulfur cathode; The PTH polymer ﬁlm on LMA could pro-
tect it against electrolyte exposure and suppress dendrite bulge
of deposited Li metal. Electrochemical measurements further evi-
dence that the KPTB-modiﬁed electrolytes enable remarkably im-
proved reversibility and cyclic stability of LMA and sulfur elec-
trodes, thus excellent performances of LSBs could be harvested
over wide temperature range (−25–50 °C), even upon high areal
loading of 5.1 mgs cm−2 and lean electrolyte of 4.5 μL mgs
−1. No-
ticeably, the full LSBs equipped with a 70 μm of LMA (N/P: ≈6.6)
could deliver a high capacity of 1200 mAh g−1 and operate for 350
cycles. Even at a low N/P ratio of 1.4, a high reversible areal ca-
pacity of 6.0 mAh cm−2 was harvested at 0.1 C, and a capacity of
4.62 mAh cm−2 was retained after 80 cycles. The KPTB additive
shows satisfactory application prospect for electrolyte regulation
in Li-S systems.
2. Results and Discussion
The molecular structure of KPTB composes a thiophene ring
with BF3 moiety and K+ ion, as shown in Figure 1a. Density func-
tional theory (DFT) calculations regarding HOMO and LUMO
of PTB illustrates that PTB exhibits lower LUMO and higher
HOMO, in comparison with the cases of typical ingredients in-
cluding DOL, DME, LiNO3, and LiTFSI in Li-S electrolyte. Theo-
retical calculations indicate that KPTB is prone to oxidation and
reduction among the typical electrolyte recipe (Figure 1b). And
the K+ ion dissociated from KPTB could exert electrostatic shield-
ing and repel Li+ ions toward uniform deposition at the LMA
interface, as demonstrated schematically in Figure 1c, which
has been widely acknowledged.[22] The dosage of KPTB addi-
tive was optimized via preliminarily electrochemical measure-
ment of Li||Cu asymmetric cells. As shown in Figure 2a and
Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the Li||Cu asymmetric cells
using 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte could stably deliver a high Coulom-
bic eﬃciency of ≈98% for 400 cycles at a current density of 0.5 mA
cm−2 with a capacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2, much better than the cases
in 0.01 m KPTB, 0.03 m KPTB and KPTB-free electrolyte. When
focused on the incipient platting behavior of Li+ ion, a nucleation
overpotential of 30.6 mV was detected for lithium metal deposi-
tion on Cu substrate in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte, smaller than
the cased of other three counterpart electrolytes, and the voltage
hysteresis during Li platting in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte was also
superior to the other three electrolytes (Figure S2, Supporting In-
formation), indicating that a favorable interface on Cu electrode
was pre-constructed in 0.02 M KPTB electrolyte, thus facilitat-
ing Li+ ion diﬀusion and nucleation of Li metal, which was con-
ducive to the inhibition of “dead lithium” and lithium dendrites
formation.[23] The cross-sectional SEM images of the deposited Li
metal on Cu electrode after 50 cycles are shown in Figure 2b and
Figure S3 (Supporting Information), and it can be found that the
strucure of deposited lithium metal in 0.02 KPTB electrolyte was
compact and uniform, dixtinct to loose and uneven structures us-
ing the other three electrolytes upon the same cut-oﬀcapacity of
0.5 mAh cm−2. When the current density was increased to 1.0 mA
cm−2 with a cut-oﬀcapacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2 (Figure 2c), the
Li||Cu cell using 0.02 M KPTB-modiﬁed electrolyte exhibits more
stable Coulombic eﬃciency and lowered voltage polarization of
53.2 mV, compared with the cases of 57.2, 56.0, and 123.1 mV in
0.01 m KPTB, 0.03 M KPTB and KPTB-free electrolyte (Figure 2d;
Figure S4, Supporting Information), respectively, supporting the
superiority of 0.02 M KPTB electrolyte to the other three recipes.
The corresponding top view SEM images of deposited Li metal
on Cu electrodes witness a smooth and tight morphology in 0.02
KPTB electrolyte, while cratered and ﬂuﬀy structure of deposited
Li metal has been derived in KPTB-free electrolyte (Figure S5,
Supporting Information), which suggests continuous side

The mechanism behind KPTB toward the sulfur cathode was
preliminarily investigated via electrochemical measurements.
The CV (cyclic voltammetry) curves in Figure 4a illustrate that,
compared with the tardy response of sulfur cathode in KPTB-free
electrolyte, sharp redox peaks were observed for sulfur electrode
in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte. The Tafel plots were further derived
from CV curves. As shown in Figure 4b, a Tafel slope of 35.8 mV
dec−1 was obtained for C2 peak corresponding to conversion of
S8 into long-chain LiPSs species for sulfur electrode measured
in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte, lower than the 54.8 mV dec−1 col-
lected in KPTB-free electrolyte, and the Tafel slope of the sulfur
electrode using KPTB additive was determined as 60.4 mV dec−1
in the subsequent liquid-solid conversion (C1 peak), also lower
than 70.4 mV dec−1 of KPTB-free sulfur cathode (Figure S15,
Supporting Information), which indicates fast kinetics of sul-
fur conversion reactions in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte.[37] The CV
curves measured at diﬀerent scan rates also show that the de-
formation of the redox peak in cell with 0.02 m KPTB is weak-
ened compared with those of 0.03 M KPTB, 0.01 m KPTB incor-
porated electrolytes and KPTB-free, and the gap between reduc-
tion and oxidation peaks was narrowed for 0.02 m KPTB incor-
porated electrolyte (Figures S16,S17, and S18a,e, Supporting In-
formation), which reaﬃrms the enhanced redox reaction kinet-
ics, mitigated overpotential and rapid reaction kinetics in 0.02 m
KPTB-incorporated electrolyte, explaining the excellent rate per-
formances above.[38] The corresponding activation energy and
the energy barrier for redox reduction process of sulfur were cal-
culated based on the Tafel slope, and it could be found that ad-
dition of 0.02 KPTB signiﬁcantly reduced the activation energy
for the whole solid-liquid-solid reaction, especially the liquid-
solid reaction, in comparison with KPTB-free case (Figure 4c;
Figure S18d,h, Supporting Information). The voltage proﬁles
derived from GITT (Ggalvanostatic intermittent titration tech-
nique) proﬁles in Figures S19 and S20 (Supporting Information)
show that the capacity of sulfur cathode is higher and voltage hys-
teresis is smaller in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte, than the cases in
0.03 m KPTB, 0.01 m KPTB incorporated electrolytes and KPTB-
free electrolytes. The internal resistance during charge/discharge
was also processed, and the case in 0.02 m KPTB electrolyte ex-
hibites lower resistance, especially for the Li2S nucleation and
activation (Figure 4d,e; Figure S21, Supporting Information). A
chronoamperometry was further employed to study the electro-
chemical kinetics and morphology of Li2S deposition in elec-
trolytes with and without KPTB. To gain detailed insights into the
inﬂuence of KPTB on Li2S nucleation, a dimensionless analysis
of the current-time curves based on the Scharifker–Hills model
were conducted, and the dimensionless current (I/Im)-time (t/tm)
proﬁles derived from chronoamperometry curves were drafted.
As shown in Figure 4f–i, and Figure S21 (Supporting Informa-
tion), the response current initially decreased due to the Fara-
day reduction of remaining long-chain polysulﬁdes, and Li2S
nucleation subsequently occurred due to decrement of short-
chain polysulﬁdes, resulting in an increased response current.
The electrochemically active region was enhanced upon nuclei
number increment of Li2S, and further growth of Li2S nuclei
caused the response current to rise until the maximum current
value (Im) reached. A precipitation capacity of 247.52, 264.76 and
287.09 mAh g−1 was obtained using KPTB-free, 0.01 m KPTB
and 0.03 M KPTB incorporated electrolytes, respectivley. And a

3. Conclusion
In summary, a bilateral interface protection strategy based on a
KPTB additive modiﬁed electrolyte has been prognosticated and
presented for both LMA and sulfur cathode in LSBs. A protection
interfacial structure has been constructed for dense and compact
LMA, based on electrostatic screening of K+ ion, BF3 moiety de-
rived F-rich SEI and organic layer resulted from electrochemi-
cally reductive polymerization of thiophene ring, and the oxida-
tion polymerization of thiophene ring has been designed to form
a protective layer to maintain the integrity of sulfur cathode and to
inhibit dissolution of LiPSs toward the LMA. The KPTB-derived
favorable bilateral protection enables LMA in Li||Li or Li||Cu cells
to stably cycle for 1200 h, and sulfur cathode to achieve excellent
cyclic performances with high capacities over wide temperature
range of −25–50 °C; even upon an areal loading of ≈5.0 mgs cm−2
and an electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 4.5 μL mgs
−1, a high areal capac-
ity of 5.0 mAh could be harvested in both coin and pouch cells.
Remarkably, the full LSBs equipped with a 70 μm of LMA could
deliver a high capacity of 1200 mAh g−1 and stably operate for
350 cycles, (N/P: ≈6.6). Even at a N/P ratio as low as 1.4, a high
reversible areal capacity of 6.0 mAh cm−2 was harvested. This
work has extended the methodology and could provide new in-
sight into electrode interface modiﬁcation for advanced LSBs.
