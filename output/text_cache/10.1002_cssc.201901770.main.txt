Designing a Safe Electrolyte Enabling Long-Life Li/S
Batteries
Marco Agostini,*[a] Matthew Sadd,[a] Shizhao Xiong,[a] Carmen Cavallo,[a] Jungwon Heo,[b]
Jou Hyeon Ahn,[b] and Aleksandar Matic*[a]
Introduction
Since their commercialization, Li-ion batteries (LiBs) have been
the leading energy-storage system for powering electronic de-
vices.[1] LIBs provide a long cycle life and a higher energy den-
sity than any other commercial secondary battery system.[2,3]
However, new technologies such as smartphones, high-per-
formance laptops, and electric vehicles (EV) demand an in-
crease in energy density, reduction in costs, and improvement
in safety and sustainability, making current commercial LiBs in-
adequate.[4]
LiB technology exploits the use of electrode materials that
can accommodate Li+ ions in their chemical structure through
intercalation/deintercalation, achieving energy densities be-
tween 150 and 200 Whkg@1.[5] Looking inside the chemistry of
the electrodes, the anode side exploits a Cu-supported graph-
ite material, with a Li+ intercalation potential close to 0 V and
a specific capacity of 372 mAhg@1, whereas the cathode is a
LiCoO2-based composite (LCO) with a working potential of
3.8 V and a specific capacity of 150 mAhg@1. Considering the
gap in specific capacity between the anode and cathode, it is
clear that the limit in energy density of commercial LiBs is re-
lated to the LCO cathode.[6] The specific capacity is not the
only limitation of the LCO cathode; there are also concerns
about the low abundance of Co, its high cost, and toxicity.[7]
The energy density of LiBs can be increased by developing
new cathodes materials, for example, those based on Ni-rich-
Li[NixCoyMz]O2 layered oxides.[8] Indeed, if the Ni content is in-
creased, the cut-off voltage during charge can be increased by
0.2–0.3 V and more Li+ can be extracted from the cathode, in-
creasing the specific capacity and thus the energy density to
250–300 Whkg@1.[9] Despite these improvements, there are still
concerns because the theoretical limit for insertion chemistry
has been approached and the technology still relies on the use
of scarce metals.[10] Thus, the development of alternative chem-
istries using materials that combine high energy density, low
toxicity, abundance, and low cost is a mandatory step.[11]
Lithium/sulfur (Li/S) chemistry meets all the above require-
ments. It is based on a conversion reaction from S to Li2S and
results
in
a
superior
theoretical

(1672 mAhg@1) and energy density (2600 Whkg@1).[12,13] A Li/S
battery consists of a sulfur-containing cathode and a Li metal
anode, and the reaction mechanism involves the formation of
a series of Li polysulfides (Li2Sx, 3,x,8). The polysulfides, in
particular those with longer chains, are soluble in the common
electrolytes, and there is a risk that they migrate to and from
the Li electrode through a “shuttle” process, by which they are
continuously reduced and oxidized at the electrodes without
contributing to the capacity.[14] This mechanism induces a
series of issues including the loss of active material, parasitic
reactions, and formation of an insulating interphase on the Li
metal anode. Strategies adopted to overcome these problems
have been based on the modification of the electrolyte by
using additives that are able to prevent the shuttle process by
Lithium–sulfur (Li/S) batteries suffer from “shuttle” reactions in
which soluble polysulfide species continuously migrate to and
from the Li metal anode. As a consequence, the loss of active
material and reactions at the surface of Li limit the practical ap-
plications of Li/S batteries. LiNO3 has been proposed as an
electrolyte additive to reduce the shuttle reactions by aiding
the formation of a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) at
the Li metal, limiting polysulfide shuttling. However, LiNO3 is
continuously consumed during cycling, especially at low cur-
rent rates. Therefore, the Li/S battery cycle life is limited by the
LiNO3 concentration in the electrolyte. In this work, an ionic
liquid (IL) [N-methyl-(n-butyl)pyrrolidinium bis(trifluoromethyl-
sulfonyl)imide] was used as an additive to enable longer cycle
life of Li/S batteries. By tuning the IL concentration, an en-
hanced stability of the SEI and lower flammability of the solu-
tions were demonstrated, that is, higher safety of the battery.
The Li/S cell built with a high sulfur mass loading (4 mgcm@2)
and containing the IL-based electrolyte demonstrated a stable
capacity of 600 mAhg@1 for more than double the number of
cycles of a cell containing LiNO3 additive.

the formation of a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), the
most common being LiNO3.[15,16] However, upon cycling, LiNO3
is continuously consumed and thus a sacrificial salt in the elec-
trolyte.[17] The consumption depends on its concentration in
the electrolyte as well as on the current applied, but cells with
LiNO3 as an additive will inevitably fail when all additive has
been consumed.[17,18] Additionally, the most common liquid
electrolytes for Li/S batteries containing LiNO3 as an additive
are based on highly flammable organic solvents, which, to-
gether with the Li metal anode, bring challenges of high reac-
tivity and safety.[19,20]
New electrolytes without LiNO3 as an additive have been
proposed to improve the stability and reduce the flammability
of Li/S cells.[1–3] Among others, glass electrolytes,[21] polymer
membranes,[22] and gels[23] have features that help to decrease
or prevent shuttle reactions. However, a major common draw-
back of these systems that limits their application in practice is
a low ionic conductivity and/or high interfacial resistance, at
least below 708C. To tackle this problem, electrolytes that pos-
sess faster kinetics at room temperature and the ability to pre-
vent shuttling of polysulfide species, with additional features
such as low flammability and lower reactivity with the Li metal
anode, have to be developed.[24,25]
Ionic liquids (ILs) have been highlighted as potential electro-
lytes for next-generation batteries owing to their intrinsic
properties such as low flammability and high ionic conductivi-
ty.[26] Their use in Li/S batteries has been demonstrated with
LiNO3 as an additive for reducing self-discharge,[27] polysulfide
dissolution,[28,29] the formation of a stable interphase on the Li
metal anode,[30] and improving the performance with tailored
S/C electrodes.[31,32] However, so far, the use of IL-based elec-
trolytes for improving the cycle life of Li/S batteries that use
high-loading sulfur electrodes has not been reported. Here,
with the intention of designing a stable and safe electrolyte
for long-life Li/S batteries, we report on the use of N-methyl-
(n-butyl)pyrrolidinium
bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide
(Py1,4TFSI) as an additive in 1,3-dioxolane/1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DOL/DME) lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI)-
based liquid electrolytes. We demonstrate that by tuning the
IL concentration the shuttle reactions can be dramatically re-
duced, as evidenced by an increase in the coulombic efficiency.
Improvements in the thermal stability and flammability of the
electrolyte were also obtained. With the tailored IL-added elec-
trolyte, we demonstrate an improvement in Li/S battery cycle
life compared with state-of-the-art electrolytes containing
LiNO3 as an additive owing to the formation of a stable SEI.
Results and Discussion
Properties of electrolytes with IL additive
To determine the influence of the addition of an IL to the
ether-based electrolyte, we characterized the solutions with re-
spect to safety, ionic transport, and compatibility with Li metal.
The safety is mainly related to the thermal stability and the
flammability of the electrolyte. The thermal stability is assessed
by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; Figure 1a). The neat IL
electrolyte, that is, Py1,4TFSI 1m LiTFSI (IL100, blue line), was
stable up to more than 4008C; in contrast, the ether-based
electrolyte, DOL/DME 1m LiTFSI (IL0, black line), was very vola-
tile, and the weight loss started immediately upon heating
from room temperature. The thermal stability was improved
after the IL was added to the ether-based electrolyte. There
was still a weight loss below 1008C, but the loss rate was con-
siderably lower. The flash point (Tflash), which describes the
lowest temperature at which vapor of a solvent can be ignited,
is commonly used to characterize the flammability of an elec-
trolyte. The neat IL electrolyte had a flash point of approxi-
mately 1958C, which is more than 1808C higher than the
ether-based electrolyte, (Tflash=158C; Figure 2b). The flash
point continuously increased after the addition of the IL.
Therefore, the addition of IL led to an improved safety, which
was also confirmed by the significantly suppressed flammabili-
ty under ignition conditions (see Figure S1 in the Supporting
Information).
The ionic conductivity of the investigated electrolytes is
shown in Figure 1c. The ether-based electrolyte had a high
conductivity, 10@1–10@2 Scm@1, whereas the neat IL had a con-
ductivity that was approximately two orders of magnitude
lower. The conductivity of the electrolyte decreased after the
addition of IL to the ether-based electrolyte, but not as dra-
matically as for the neat IL. Previous work suggests that the re-
duction of conductivity can be attributed to an increase in vis-
cosity.[30] This is confirmed by the dynamic viscosities reported
in Figure 1d. The concentration behavior of the viscosity fol-
lows the same trend as the conductivity, with a slight increase
with the addition of the IL.
The ionic conductivity of an electrolyte is a result of the con-
tribution of all ionic species. To investigate the contribution
from Li ions, we determined the Li+ transference number (tLiþ)
as a function of the electrolyte composition (Figure 2a). The
ether-based electrolyte had a tLiþ of approximately 0.45, in
agreement with previously reported results.[16] With the addi-
tion of IL, the transference number decreased, and the neat IL
electrolyte had the lowest tLiþ (0.15). On a microscopic scale,
Li+ transport is directly related to the local environment
around the Li+ ions. In a Py1,4TFSI/LiTFSI-based electrolyte the
Li+ is coordinated to two TFSI anions in a bidental configura-
tion, thus the diffusion of the Li ion takes place through the
diffusion of a Li[TFSI]2
@ triplet.[33]
To determine the local coordination of Li ions, Raman spec-
troscopy was used, and spectra in the region between 730 and
760 cm@1 for the different electrolytes are reported in Fig-
ure 2b. The vibrational band at 739–742 cm@1 corresponded to
the contraction/expansion of the TFSI anion. This band is par-
ticularly sensitive to interactions of TFSI@ and can be used to
determine the coordination with Li+.[34] For anions that weakly
interact with cations, the band is found at 738–742 cm@1. For
anions interacting with Li ions, the band shifts to higher fre-
quencies, 744–748 cm@1. In the ether-based electrolyte, only a
weak band at higher frequencies was observed, indicating that
most of the Li ions were solvated by the organic solvent,
whereas in the neat IL electrolyte the high-frequency band do-
minated as a result of the presence of Li[TFSI]2
@ triplets.[33,35]

Cycling performance in Li/S cells
Galvanostatic cycling tests were performed to evaluate the
effect of Py1,4TFSI as an additive on the Li/S cell cycling stabili-
ty. The cells were built by using an optimized and high-loading
C/S composite cathode, in which the amount of S was maxi-
mized to a value up to 80% and a loading of 4 mgcm@2.[36] In
this way, the influence of the IL additive on the cycling per-
formance of practical high-sulfur-loading electrodes can be
demonstrated. The performance of the Li/S cell containing the
pristine ether-based electrolyte solution (IL0) is reported in Fig-
ure 3a. During the first cycle, the cell delivered a specific dis-
charge capacity of 1300 mAhg@1. However, the cell showed an
irreversible capacity during the following charge and poor cou-
lombic efficiency, decreasing after few cycles to values as low
as 60% of the capacity (Figure 3b). The voltage profiles of Li/S
cells containing electrolytes with IL as an additive during the
first cycle are shown in Figure 3c. The cell containing the neat
IL electrolyte showed a low specific capacity, only 20% of the
theoretical value (1675 mAhg@1).
This poor performance was owing to the low ionic conduc-
tivity and tLiþ, as well as the high viscosity, which strongly re-
duces the reaction rate and formation of Li-polysulfide species.
If IL was used as an additive in the ether-based electrolytes,
the capacities of the Li/S cells improved. The IL50 electrolyte
displayed the highest specific discharge capacity during the
first cycle, 1200 mAhg@1, approximately 70% of the theoretical
specific capacity, and during the following charge it showed

Py1,4TFSI versus LiNO3 in Li/S cells
To further confirm the effect of Py1,4TFSI as an additive to the
ether-based electrolyte solution, the IL50 mixture, that is, the
best-performing in terms of cycling stability and coulombic ef-
ficiency, was compared with a standard DOL/DME 1m LiTFSI
electrolyte using LiNO3 (0.4m) as an additive in Li/S cells.[16]
The voltage profiles of the Li/S cells with the two electrolytes
for different cycles are reported in Figure 5a. The cell contain-
ing the IL additive (left panel) had the highest discharge ca-
pacity during the first cycle, 20% higher than the cell contain-
ing the LiNO3 additive. This was related to the fact that the Li/
S cell containing the IL additive had a higher capacity during
the first discharge plateau, that is, from 2.4 to 2.0 V, at which
the solid S8 is converted to soluble long-chain polysulfides
(e.g.,
Li2S8,
Li2S6,
and
Li2S4),
delivering
a
capacity
of
450 mAhg@1, whereas the cell containing LiNO3 additive had a
limited capacity in this region (250 mAhg@1). The subsequent
cycles showed that the dissolution of the sulfur electrode into
the electrolyte was faster in the cell with LiNO3 as an additive,
as indicated by the faster capacity fade in Figure 5b. The spe-
cific capacity fell to a stable level of 600 mAhg@1 after only
20 cycles, whereas the Li/S cell containing the IL additive had a
more sluggish dissolution of sulfur and reached the same
stable specific capacity after 80 cycles. This indicated that the
higher viscosity of the IL50 electrolyte slowed down the disso-
lution of lithium polysulfides. After prolonged cycling tests, the
two Li/S cells showed an important difference. The cell con-
taining the IL additive displayed no sign of capacity decay, and
the coulombic efficiency was maintained at approximately
100% (Figure 5c). The Li/S cell containing the LiNO3 additive
showed signs of capacity decay after 100 cycles, and, further-
more, a decay in coulombic efficiency that accelerated after
the 150th cycle. This is a result of the continuous consumption
of LiNO3 upon cycling, especially at lower current rates, for ex-
ample, C/30 (56 mAg@1) used in this experiment.
This consumption causes a reduction of the LiNO3 concen-
tration in the electrolyte, and when a low electrolyte volume is
used in a cell, which is needed to have a practical high energy
density, this consumption is faster, and the capacity decay be-
comes evident. In addition to the continuous degradation of
the SEI layer, the use of LiNO3 as an additive also causes the
evolution of gases at the Li metal anode and swelling of the
cell.[17,18]

stable cycling and high coulombic efficiency because a more

Conclusions
N-methyl-(n-butyl)pyrrolidinium
bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)i-
mide (Py1,4TFSI) was used as an additive to stabilize the solid
electrolyte interphase, reduce the flammability of the electro-
lyte, and extend the cycle life of Li/S cells when using high-
loading sulfur/carbon composites. The ionic liquid (IL) additive
suppresses the electrolyte reactivity towards Li metal and re-
duces the solvent flammability, ensuring higher safety charac-
teristics. Additionally, mitigation of sulfur deposition through
the Li-polysulfide migration to the anode side was demonstrat-
ed. The IL concentration was investigated and tuned to ach-
ieve the highest effect on the Li/S cell cycling performance.
The optimized IL-containing electrolyte was compared with a
standard electrolyte containing LiNO3 as an additive. Excellent
cycling performance, with coulombic efficiencies of more than
99%, was demonstrated over extended cycling (300 cycles) of

Characterization
According to the measurement protocol D6450 by Grabner, the
flash point (Tflash) was determined for 1 mL of sample heated in a
closed cup from Tinitial to Tfinal. To follow the protocol exactly, Tinitial
should be equal to Tflash minus 188C and Tfinal should be high
enough to detect Tfash. Tflash is defined as the temperature at which
the ignition of the fumes produces an increase in pressure that
surpasses a predefined criterion (20 kPa) under a certain airflow,
heating rate (5.5 Cmin@1), and ignition interval (1 test per 8C). TGA
(209 TG F1, Netzsch) was performed in the temperature range of
25–6008C at a heating rate of 108Cmin@1 under a flow of N2.
The viscosity of the mixed electrolytes was determined with a
Lovis 2000M/ME (Anton Paar) viscosimeter in the temperature
range of 10–408C. The temperature was increased in steps of 58C.
Dielectric measurements were performed with a Novocontrol
GmbH broadband dielectric spectrometer equipped with a Quatro
Cryosystem temperature control unit. The mixed electrolytes were
placed in a coin cell, and dielectric measurements were performed
in the frequency and temperature ranges of 10@1–107 Hz and 0–
508C, respectively. The temperature was increased in steps of
108C, and at each temperature an equilibration time of 30 min was
used.
Raman spectra were collected by using a Bruker MulitiRAM FT
Raman spectrometer with a 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser fitted with a
liquid-N2-cooled Ge-diode detector. Spectra were collected over
2000 scans with a 2 cm@1 spectral resolution (full width half maxi-
mum).
XPS
measurements
were
performed
on
lithium
foils
after
50 charge/discharge cycles. Prior to the experiments, the foils were
washed with dimethyl carbonate to remove residual electrolyte on
the surface and subsequently dried for 30 min under vacuum to
remove residual dimethyl carbonate solvent. The process was per-
formed in an argon glovebox (MBraunLabstar, H2O<1 ppm, O2<
1 ppm). An argon-atmosphere-controlled glove-bag (Aldrich) was
employed to transfer the samples from the glovebox to the cham-
ber of the XPS systems (PHI 5800, Physical Electronics). The meas-
urements were performed under an argon atmosphere using AlKa
radiation (200W, 13 kV), at a pressure of 1.33@7 Pa, and the diame-
ter of the analyzed surface was 800 mm. The spectra were calibrat-
ed to the binding energy of the C1s peak (BE=284.5 eV). All spec-
tra were fitted by the deconvolution software (Casa XPS, Casa Soft-
ware). Gaussian–Lorentzian (30% Gaussian) functions and a Shirley-
type background were employed in all fitting spectra.
Sulfur/CMK3 electrode synthesis.
The ordered mesoporous silica, SBA-15, was prepared by dissolving
the triblock copolymer poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene
glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) (4 g, EO20PO70EO30, P123, Al-
drich) in 1m HCl solution (105 mL) as the surfactant and tetraethyl
orthosilicate (8.5 g, TEOS, Aldrich) as the silica source and stirring
for 4 h at 408C and for 74 h at 1208C. The resulting mixture was fil-
tered and dried at 808C for 24 h and then washed with deionized
water until a pH of approximately 6 was achieved. The obtained
material was calcined in air at 5508C for 6 h. The product was fil-
tered, washed, and dried in air at 908C.
The ordered mesoporous carbon CMK3 was synthesized through a
two-step impregnation process of SBA-15. An aqueous solution
was prepared by dissolving sucrose (3.14 g) and H2SO4 (0.35 g) in
deionized H2O (12.55 mL); SBA-15 (2.51 g) was impregnated with
this solution. The mixture was sonicated for 1 h and then heated
at 1008C for 12 h and at 1608C for 12 h. The silica sample contain-
ing partially polymerized and carbonized sucrose was treated
again with aqueous solution (12.55 mL) containing sucrose (2 g)
and H2SO4 (0.23 g) at 100 and 1608C, as previously reported.[36] The
composite was then heated at 9008C for 5 h with a heating rate of
58Cmin@1 under nitrogen flow (20 mLmin@1). The SBA-15 template
was finally removed with 5% HF solution at room temperature,
and the product was washed with deionized water and dried at
room temperature to obtain the mesoporous CMK3 carbon.
The as-prepared mesoporous CMK3 carbon was mixed with sulfur
(15:85, w/w) by high-energy ball milling at 25 Hz frequency, sealed
in a 50 mL vessel under N2 flow (20 mLmin@1), and heated at
1558C for 20 h and at 3008C for 20 h. The final sulfur content in
the CMK3 composite was 80%.[36]
