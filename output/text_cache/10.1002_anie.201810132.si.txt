1. Experimental Section 
1.1. Preparation of electrolyte 
Three sorts of electrolyte were prepared for each solvent: blank electrolyte, dilute and 
concentrated Li2S6 electrolytes. First, blank electrolyte was prepared with 1.0 mol L−1 (M) lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.30 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) as the salts and 
mixed solvents that referred to as dioxolane (DOL)/X. X included dimethoxyethane (DME), 
dimethyl acetamide (DMA), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and tetramethylurea (TMU). Solvents 
were mixed in a volume ratio of 1:1 before salts were added. Then, 2.5 M Li2S6 (in sulfur) 
electrolyte was prepared in another container using the blank electrolyte and sulfur and lithium 
sulfide (Li2S) powder in a molar ratio of 5:1. After all solid was dissolved under stirring, 2.5 M [S] 
Li2S6 electrolyte was obtained and could be diluted with the blank electrolyte to any 
concentration for use. The entire process was accomplished in argon (Ar)-filled glove box with 
oxygen and water contents less than 0.1 ppm. 
1.2. Solubility measurement 
Both sulfur and Li2S powder were weighed in stoichiometric molar ratio (3:1 for Li2S4, 7:1 
for Li2S8), respectively. Their mass was controlled to obtain 4.0 mmol Li2S4 and 20.0 mmol Li2S8. 
0.20 mL of mixed solvent (DOL/DME or DOL/TMU) was added into the bottle every time, and 
then stirred rigorously for 10 hours. The procedure was repeated until no solid residue was 
observed. The solubility of polysulfides was calculated based on the recorded solvent volume. 
1.3. Ultraviolet-visible light (UV-vis) and Raman spectroscopy 
0.20 M [S] Li2S6 solutions was prepared for spectroscopic tests. The solution was diluted 
100 times for UV-vis measurement. Diluted polysulfide solution was then sealed in a quartz 
glass cuvette under Ar atmosphere and measured in UV-3600 spectrometer (Shimadzu).  
Original Li2S6 solution was sealed in a capillary tube and the Raman spectra were recorded 
with 633 nm He-Ne laser in Horiba Jobin Yvon LabRAM HR800 Raman spectrometer. 
1.4. Li | Li symmetric cell assembly and measurements

Li | Li symmetric cell composed of two identical lithium metal electrodes separated by a 
Celgard 2400 polypropylene (PP) membrane. Lithium foils were all 16.0 mm in diameter. On 
each side of the membrane, 15 μL of 0.50 M [S] Li2S6 electrolyte was added. As-fabricated cells 
were galvanostatically cycled on LANHE CT2001A battery cycler. Discharge and charge current 
density was 0.25 mA cm−2, and areal capacity was 0.25 mAh cm−2. Cells were set to 
automatically shut down when potential reached ±5.0 V. 
1.5. Li | carbon paper (CP)/polysulfide cell assembly and measurements 
Li | CP/polysulfide cells were fabricated using CP as the cathode current collector, lithium 
foil as the anode, and PP separator. CP was cut to disks of 13.0 mm in diameter, then washed 
by ethanol and dried completely before cell assembly. Catholyte consisted of 20 μL 2.5 M [S] 
Li2S6 electrolyte, while 10 μL blank electrolyte was dropped on anode to ameliorate shuttle effect. 
Active material in each cell was equivalent to 1.6 mg sulfur. Galvanostatic cycle was carried out 
on LANHE battery cycler at 0.1 C (1 = 1675 mA gsulfur−1) between 1.8 and 2.7 V. In galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique (GITT) test, the cell was held at open circuit for 10 hours every 
100 mAh gsulfur−1 of discharge. 
Chronoamperometry test was conducted on Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation. 
The cell was firstly discharged to 2.12 V at 0.1 C, and then kept at constant potential (2.09 V for 
DOL/DME, 2.07 V for DOL/TMU) for 60000 s. The cell was then dissembled in glove box and 
the cathode was taken out for further characterization. 
1.6. Li | carbon nanotube (CNT)/sulfur cell assembly and measurements 
CNT/sulfur composite slurry that contained 90% CNT/sulfur composite (prepared by ball-
milling) and 10% poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) binder was casted onto aluminum (Al) foil, 
dried under 60oC for 12 hours, and cut into 13.0 mm disks. Sulfur content in cathode composite 
was about 63%. The sulfur loading was 1.02±0.07 mg cm−2. Electrolyte for each cell was 17 μL 
blank electrolyte. Cells were cycled on LANHE battery cycler under 0.1 C and 0.05 C between 
1.8 and 2.7 V. 
1.7. Pouch cell assembly and measurements

Pouch cell was fabricated using double-sided cathode and anode. Cathode was produced 
by large-scaled blade casting of the same slurry as described in section 1.6. After been punched 
into 30 mm × 50 mm, a single slice of cathode was stacked with a PP separator, and a lithium 
foil anode alternately. The structure and more details about the fabrication of pouch cell could 
be found in previous work.[1] The areal density of different components in the cell could be found 
in section 2. The areal sulfur loading was 2.5 mg cm−2 and electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio was 
3:1. After resting for 12 hours, the fabricated pouch cell was cycled at a current rate of 0.02 C 
between 1.7 and 2.5 V. 
1.8. Ionic conductivity and Li+ diffusivity measurement 
Ionic conductivities of four types of electrolytes with different solvents were measured using 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on Solartron 1470E electrochemical workstation. 
Symmetric cells with two stainless steel (SS) electrodes and a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) 
ring spacer (inner diameter: 10 mm, thickness: 0.5 mm) were assembled, with 30 μL of 0.50 M 
[S] Li2S6 electrolyte. The ionic resistance could be represented by the x-axis intercept (Rb) of 
the impedance curve on a Nyquist plot, which is the impedance of the cell at high input signal 
frequency (105 Hz). The ionic conductivity σ was calculated based on the following equation: 
σ = l/(Rb•S) 
where I and S are the thickness and cross-section area of inner circle of PTFE ring spacer, 
respectively. 
1.9. Materials characterization 
The CP cathodes in Li | CP/polysulfide cells were taken out after 60000 s of potentiostatic 
discharge, washed with pure DME or TMU solvent for DME and TMU cells, respectively, and 
dried in glove box. The cycled lithium anodes were taken out from Li | Li symmetric cells after 
ten cycles at 0.25 mA cm−2 and treated in the same way using corresponding solvent for washing. 
Then, their morphology was characterized by a JSM 7401F (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) operated at 3.0 kV. The solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) of lithium 
anodes after cycling in Li | CP/polysulfide cells was investigated using X-ray photoelectron

2. Energy Density Estimation for Pouch Cells 
In the following calculation, the areal density of current collectors and separator was set to 
be the same as that used in our pouch cells: 3.2 mg cm−2 for Al foil, 8 mg cm−2 for copper (Cu) 
foil, and 2 mg cm−2 for the PP separator. Considering that all electrodes were double-sided, the 
total density of current collector and separator was 7.6 mg cm−2. The mass of electrode lugs 
and wires was omitted; the mass of package was measured before cell fabrication and 
subtracted from the total weight of cell. Lithium was set to be 1.5 times excessive relative to the 
sulfur in cathode, therefore, the mass of lithium would vary with the sulfur loading. The average 
voltage was assumed to be 2.05 V. 
Under these assumptions, there would be four factors that influence the energy density: 
E/S ratio, sulfur loading, sulfur utilization, and sulfur content. The range of these parameters 
covered the commonly reported value in literatures.

4. SEI component analysis through XPS 
XPS was employed to characterize the components of SEIs induced by different 
electrolytes. Figure S8 shows the atomic contents of Li, C, N, O, F, and S in SEIs induced by 
different electrolytes. SEI induced by TMU exhibits the highest amount of Li but the lowest 
amount of C, implying its inorganic-compound-rich nature. DMA and TMU renders SEIs with 
more N than DME and DMSO because the former two are N-containing solvents. Since there 
are few differences in Li1s, N1s, and F1s spectra between SEIs induced by different electrolytes, 
we then focus on analyzing the refined spectra of C1s, O1s, and S2p, which exactly indicate 
some significant differences. 
Figure S9a–c show the refined C1s, O1s, and S2p spectra, respectively. C1s spectra were 
fitted using a set of characteristic peaks: 284.8 eV for C–C/C–H (essential signals for organic 
compounds), 282.6 eV for C–Li (deeply reduced products by lithium), 286.3–286. 7 eV for C–
O/C–N/C–S (depending on the heteroatoms in solvent molecules and the difference in 
electronegativity of these atoms), and 289.9 eV for C–SOx (derived from TFSI− anions).[3] It can 
be clearly seen that SEI induced by TMU contains the least organic C–C/C–H components and 
the least decomposition products from TFSI− anions. In contrast, other three SEIs contains 
either more organic compounds (DME), or TFSI− decomposition products (DMA), or both 
(DMSO). 
O1s spectra were fitted using a set of characteristic peaks: 531.4 eV for SOx/NOx (derived 
from TFSI− and NO32− anions, as well as oxidized polysulfides anions), 528.4 eV for Li2O (most 
likely the deeply reduced products from ether solvents such as DOL/DME), and 530.1 eV for 
ROLi (derived from C–O scissoring of ether and/or C=O reducing of amide).[4] It can be clearly 
seen that SEI induced by TMU contains the least anion decomposition products of SOx/NOx but 
the most Li2O. Note that there is no ROLi signal for TMU spectra fitting, which is in contrast to 
other three spectra. That could be ascribed to deeper reduction of DOL on lithium with the

S2p spectra were fitted using a set of characteristic 2p3/2/2p1/2 peak doublets (strictly 
following the criteria that the binding energy of 2p1/2 is 1.2 eV higher than 2p3/2, the peak area 
of 2p1/2 is half of 2p3/2, and the peak shape is the same; finally represented by the peak position 
of 2p3/2): 160.2 eV for Li2S and 161.8–162.0 eV for Li2S2/Li2Sx (derived from reactions between 
lithium and polysulfides), and 167.0 eV for SOx and 168.8–169.3 eV for –SO2CF3 (derived from 
TFSI− anions). It can be clearly seen that SEI induced by TMU contains the least S-containing 
anion decomposition products. Besides, the ratios of Li2S/Li2S2/Li2Sx to SOx/–SO2CF3 for DME 
and TMU-induced SEIs are significantly higher than those with DMA and DMSO. The inert 
Li2S/Li2S2/Li2Sx components are believed to suppress the further reaction between electrolytes 
and lithium metal.[4]  
Therefore, we could conclude that (1) DME, DMA, and DMSO-based electrolytes result in 
SEIs that contain more organic compounds (indicated by C content in Figure S8 and ROLi 
specie content in Figure S9b) than SEI in TMU; (2) TMU-induced SEI contains much less 
decomposition products (C-F in Figure S9a, SOx/NOx in Figure S9b, and –SO2CF3/SOx in Figure 
S9c) from salt anions (TFSI−, NO3−, and polysulfide di-anions/radicals) than SEIs in other three 
electrolytes; (3) DME and TMU-based electrolytes stabilize lithium metal/electrolyte interfaces 
through enriched inorganic compounds such as Li2S and Li2O, of which the mechanism is 
somehow elucidated in other reports.[4, 5] 
The suppression of anion decomposition in TMU-based electrolytes could be ascribed to 
the low Gutmann acceptor number (AN) of TMU (9.2 vs. 10.2 for DME, 13.6 for DMA, and 19.3 
for DMSO, and see Table S1). High AN means that the solvent has stronger solvating ability 
toward anions. Since lithium metal anode is negatively charged,[6] it prefers to adsorb solvated 
Li+ ions rather than neutral solvated Li+-naked anion complexes and naked anions. But once an 
anion is solvated by a sheath of solvent molecules, the electrostatic repulsion between lithium 
metal and solvated anion will be reduced, thus enabling anion to participate in the formation of 
SEI. The above mechanism well explains the trend of how likely anions decompose on lithium 
in a sequence of DMSO > DMA > DME > TMU, which is exactly inverse to their AN sequence.
