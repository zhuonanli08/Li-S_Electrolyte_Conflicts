Part 1: Auxiliary Plots of Spatial Variation 
Spatial plots of several computed variables are presented in this section to support or enhance elements 
discussed in the main body of the manuscript.  The whole-cell sandwich is shown schematically in Figure 
1, with the porous separator regions having a thickness l2 = 20 µm, and the porous composite cathode has 
a thickness l1 = 100 µm.  Some variables are computed across the whole 120 µm thick cell sandwich, 
whereas others are computed across the 100 µm cathode region, alone.  All plots in Figure S1 are for the 
reaction parameters kf = 1x10-3 s-1 and KS3 = 1x105 mol/m3 and a discharge rate of 0.5C.  
Figure S1a is a series of short discharge-time concentration profiles (<50 seconds, representing <1% 
discharged) showing how the profiles presented in Figure 5c get established. In Figure S1a, the discharge 
times are comparable to the diffusion timescale, O(l22/Di). Because the radical anion is generated within 
the cathode, the species grows relatively uniformly within the cathode at short times (see 0.3% discharge 
curve). Also evident at this short time is the beginning of transport out of the cathode and into separator 
region by both diffusion and migration at the separator/cathode boundary. At 0.5% of discharge, the 
influence of the no-flux condition at the protected anode (with counteracting migration and diffusion 
gradients) drives a minimum concentration within the separator, while the whole inventory of radical 
anion increases owing to the continuous generation of polysulfide species as the discharge advances. This 
balance of diffusion, migration, and polysulfide generation establishes the basic inventory and profile 
shapes seen in Figure 5 as discharge progresses.

Figure S1c shows the spatial variation of volume fraction of the Li2S(s) precipitate product in the cathode 
as discharge progresses, with precipitation preferentially occurring at the separator/cathode boundary. 
Li2S(s) generation is not appreciable until late in the discharge when fully reduced S2– is generated as a 
reactant for the precipitation.  Because the sulfur cathode involves a conversion reaction from solid 
elemental sulfur to reduced Li2S(s) solid, overall porosity can evolve spatially, depending upon where in 
the cathode the reactant sulfur dissolves and the product Li2S(s) precipitates. Figure S1d shows the spatial 
evolution of cathode porosity during a deep discharge.  As elemental sulfur dissolves out of the composite 
cathode and forms soluble species, the porosity increases, first near the current collector within the 
cathode. As discharge continues to 50%, the porosity reaches a maximum and levels off because the 
elemental sulfur has been depleted. Then, the Li2S(s) solid precipitate begins to refill the composite porous 
structure, as noted in Figure S1c, and the porosity declines accordingly.
