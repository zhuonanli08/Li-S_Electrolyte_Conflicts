A B S T R A C T
Li2S is highly desired for high energy-density lithium sulfur batteries since it allows to be matched with Li metal-
free anodes and alleviates the volume expansion of S cathode. However, Li+ extraction from Li2S usually suﬀers
from a huge voltage barrier due to its poor electron conductivity and sluggish Li+ ion diﬀusion during activa-
tion. In this work, trace sulfone-assisted-NH4I is adopted as an electrolyte additive with synergistic dissolution
and catalysis eﬀects to alleviate the voltage barrier of Li2S. On one hand, the intercalation between sulfone-
NH4
+ and Li2S turns solid Li2S into liquid phase, which signiﬁcantly speeding up the transport of electrons and
Li+ ions during activation. On the other hand, I−works as a redox mediator, which further facilitates the
oxidation of Li2S to polysulﬁdes. By taking the advantages of synergistic dissolution and catalysis eﬀects from
sulfone-NH4
+ and I−, the Li2S cathode can be activated at ~3.0 V with a very ﬂat plateau. Finally, the Li2S
cathode cycled in the electrolyte with optimized 0.5% sulfone-assisted-NH4I additive shows a high initial dis-
charge capacity of 1160 mAh g−1 at 0.05C, along with enhanced cycling performances and rate capabilities.
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur batteries (Li-S, 2600 Wh kg−1 or 2800 Wh L−1) with
high energy–density are regarded as a promising alternative to the
state-of-art Li ion batteries [1–3]. However, the notorious Li dendrite
growth and the “shuttle eﬀect” of long chain polysulﬁdes signiﬁcantly
deteriorate the safety and stabilities of Li-S batteries [4,5]. In addition,
the parasitic side reactions between Li and electrolytes and the large

volume expansion of S (over 80%) also hinder the applications of Li-S
batteries [6–8]. Recently, Li2S based Li-S batteries have attracted much
more attentions since they allow to use Li metal-free anodes (such as
graphite; silicon and tin), and remain the advantage of high energy
density of Li-S batteries as well [9,10]. Furthermore, employing Li2S as
active material could eﬀectively avoid the volume expansion and sig-
niﬁcantly alleviate the structural damage of electrode during cycling
[11]. Unfortunately, due to the intrinsic electronic insulating nature
and large charge transfer resistance of Li2S, the initial activation of Li2S
suﬀers from a huge voltage barrier, which needs a high charging vol-
tage up to 4 V [12]. At such high activation voltage, the ether-based
electrolytes are known to be unstable, which deteriorates the electro-
chemical performances [13,14].
In general, the voltage barrier is tightly associated with the initial
phase nucleation of polysulﬁdes while the amplitude of the voltage
barrier is due to the huge charge transfer between Li2S and electrolyte
and slow Li+ diﬀusion in Li2S [12,15]. According to previous in-
vestigations, during initial activation, the Li2S particle ﬁrstly generates
Li+ and electrons to form solid Li2-xS species, which with a core/shell
like structure and a highly Li+ deﬁcient Li2-xS surface layer [15,16].
The charge transfer of this process is slow and the charge transfer re-
sistance is large since the involved species are solid phase and the
diﬀusion of Li+ in Li2S is sluggish. Upon further charging, the Li2−xS
starts to oxidize into soluble polysulﬁdes, which subsequently can
participate in the redox reactions. Since the polysulﬁdes can act as the
nucleation center, the kinetics between Li2S and polysulﬁdes is proved
to be fast, indicating a faster charge transfer. At the end of charging,
only soluble polysulﬁde phase existed and thus the charge transfer
process is much easier in the electrolyte [12,15,17,18].
To overcome the huge voltage barrier, several strategies have been
proposed. One is alleviating the voltage barrier by reducing the particle
size of Li2S to nanoscale and coupling with conductive matrix, which
can both enhance the transfer of Li+ in the interior Li2S particles and
the electron transport kinetics [17,19,20]. For instance, Hu et al. en-
capsulated 5 nm Li2S particles in N-rich carbon and achieved a high
initial discharge capacity of 1046 mAh g−1 [19]. Another approach is
employing redox mediator additives to facilitate the oxidation of large
particles with a fast kinetics [21,22]. Many redox mediators have been
adopted, such as lithium iodine [23,24], ferrocene [25] and quinone
[26]. Although these approaches are eﬀective, the initial phase nu-
cleation of polysulﬁdes still suﬀers from a large charge transfer re-
sistance and sluggish Li+ diﬀusion because of the solid Li2S phase,
which inevitably make the oxidation of Li2S to S diﬃcult [27,28]. In
this state, the activation of Li2S is uncompleted and the ﬁnal electro-
chemical performance is still unsatisﬁed.
Recently, Zhang et al. reported dissolving solid Li2S species with
high-dielectric solvents is beneﬁcial for improving the reaction kinetics
of Li2S, which enables smaller nucleus size and larger nucleation den-
sity [29–31]. Moreover, our previous work also proved that turning
solid Li2S into liquid phase by adopting trace ethanol or acetonitrile as
an electrolyte additive can signiﬁcantly reduce the voltage barrier of
Li2S [32]. Inspired by these works, herein we further develop sulfone
(SL)-assisted-NH4I as an electrolyte additive with synergistic dissolution
and catalysis eﬀects to reduce the voltage barrier and facilitate the
transformation of Li2S into polysulﬁdes. The SL can assist NH4I to dis-
solve into the electrolyte. On one hand, the dissolution eﬀect beneﬁt
from the interaction, via N-H…S2−bond, between NH4
+ and Li2S [33],
and the high-dielectric constant of SL can turn solid Li2S into liquid
phase, which signiﬁcantly speeds up the transport of electrons and Li+
ions for Li2S activation. On the other hand, the catalysis eﬀect beneﬁt
from I−can further facilitate the transformation of Li2S into poly-
sulﬁdes. By taking the advantages of the synergistic dissolution and
catalysis eﬀects, the activation of Li2S becomes much easier and the
voltage barrier is eﬀectively reduced, along with improved electro-
chemical performances.
2. Experimental
For the preparation of a Li2S/graphene composite, commercial Li2S
(Aalfa Aesar Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd) was directly mixed with high-
quality boron-doped graphene with a mass ratio of 6:4 by ball milling at
400 rpm for 4 h under argon atmosphere. Typically, the high-quality
boron-doped graphene was obtained from an electrochemical exfolia-
tion method as we reported previously [5]. The Li2S cathode was ob-
tained by mixing 70% Li2S/graphene composite, 20% ketjen black (KB)
and 10% poly (vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF, Solvey HSV900) in N-me-
thyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co.
Ltd), and being casted on Al foil subsequently. After vacuum drying for
12 h, the electrode was cut into disks with a diameter of 10 mm. To
standard the testing, the mass loading of Li2S on one piece of electrode
is controlled at about 1.0 mg cm−2. The S/C composite was prepared by
mixing S powder (Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co. Ltd) and
carbon nanotube (CNT, Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech. Co. Ltd.) with
a mass ratio of 7:3 by ball milling, followed by heat treatment at 155 °C
for 12 h. The S cathode was obtained by mixing 80% S/CNT composite,
10% KB and 10%PVDF in NMP, and then casted on Al foil. The mass
loading of S on one piece of electrode (Φ10 mm) is controlled at about
2.5–2.7 mg cm−2.
The standard (STD) electrolyte is a mixture of 1.0 M lithium bis
(triﬂuoromethane sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1 by volume ratio) with 2 wt% LiNO3. Since
NH4I is insoluble in the STD electrolyte, the NH4I (Shanghai Aladdin
Bio-Chem Technology Co. Ltd) additive is ﬁrstly dissolved in SL
(Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co. Ltd) with a mass ratio of
1:10 (donated as SL-NH4I); then the obtained solution was added into
the STD electrolyte with diﬀerent amounts (0.5%, 1.0% and 2.0% by
weight ratio). In addition, 2.0% SL is also added into the STD electro-
lyte to investigate the eﬀect of SL on the activation of Li2S cathode. The
solubility of Li2S in SL without and with NH4I is tested according to
previous investigation [30].
Coin 2032-type cells were assembled with Celgard 2400 separator.
For better evaluating the “shuttle eﬀect” in the electrolyte without and
with SL-NH4I, the amount of electrolyte related to the active mass is
controlled slightly higher (30 µL per mg Li2S or 18 µL per mg S).
Galvanostatically charge–discharge curves of the cells were tested on
the battery test system of Neware, with an electrochemical window of
1.8–2.8 V after initial activation at 0.05C. Electrochemical impedance
spectra (EIS) of the cells were tested on the CHI604D electrochemical
workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instruments Co. Ltd.) in the frequency
range from 100 kHz to 10 mHz. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was per-
formed with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. The morphology images of the
electrodes were obtained by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL
JSM-6390LA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Titan
80–300). X-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS) spectra were tested
on an X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer (ESCALAB250Xi). The UV–Vis
spectrum was collected by CARY 5000 instrument. For testing, 0.5 mL
SL-NH4I without and with 0.62 M Li2S was diluted into 9.5 mL DME.

The cycling performances of Li2S cathode in diﬀerent electrolytes
were tested, which are shown in Fig. 2b. The cells are cycled at 0.2C
after two activation cycles at 0.05C. When cycled in the STD electrolyte,
the Li2S cathode shows a discharge capacity of 432 mAh g−1 after ac-
tivation and reduces to 230 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. Its coulombic
eﬃciency (CE) is around 99%. The poor cycling performance of the Li2S
cathode cycled in the STD electrolyte mainly ascribed to the insuﬃcient
activation of Li2S [36,37]. With the adoption of 2.0% SL, the Li2S
cathode shows an enhanced discharge capacity of 536 mAh g−1 after
activation. Nevertheless, after 100 cycles, the discharge capacity of Li2S
cathode reduces to 170 mAh g−1. The faster capacity decaying as
compared with that of the Li2S cathode in the STD electrolyte may be
ascribed to the strong side reactions between SL and metallic Li [38],
which can be proved by the relatively low CE of 92% in Fig. 2c.
Therefore, the amount of SL cannot be very high in the electrolyte for
Li-S batteries. Obviously, the cycling performances of the Li2S cathode
cycled in the electrolyte containing SL-NH4I additive are signiﬁcantly
improved compared to that in STD electrolyte without and with 2.0%
SL. When 0.5% SL-NH4I additive was adopted, the Li2S cathode shows a
discharge capacity of 699 mAh g−1 after activation and reduced to
481 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles (at 0.2C). The CE during cycling is as
high as 99%. Meanwhile, the content of 0.5% SL-NH4I additive shows
negligible eﬀect on metallic Li, which can be proved by the cycling
performances of Li||Li symmetric cells in Fig. S5. In addition, as shown
in Fig. S6, some LiI species can be formed on the cycled Li anode
[39,40]. Upon further increasing the content of SL-NH4I additive to
1.0% and 2.0%, the Li2S cathode shows relatively worse cycling per-
formance and lower CE because of much more serious “shuttle eﬀect”
[41]. Therefore, it can be concluded that the optimized amount of the
SL-NH4I additive in the electrolyte for the Li2S cathode is 0.5%.

In order to better understanding the beneﬁcial eﬀects of SL-NH4I
additive on the activation of Li2S, CV of the Li2S cathode in the STD
electrolyte without and with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive were tested. Due to
the large voltage barrier of Li2S during activation, the Li2S cathode in
the STD electrolyte is ﬁrstly scanned from open circuit voltage (OCV) to
3.8 V and then scanned between 1.8 and 2.8 V. As shown in Fig. 3a,
during the ﬁrst anodic scan, the current of the Li2S cathode in the STD
electrolyte appears at ~3.2 V and increases sharply at ~3.4 V, in-

during the ﬁrst anodic scan, the current of the Li2S cathode in the
electrolyte with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive appears at ~2.9 V and increases

sented in Fig. 2a. Obviously, the activation voltage of the Li2S cathode
in the STD electrolyte with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive is much lower than
that in the STD electrolyte. In the corresponding cathodic scan of both

similar electrochemical behaviors, suggesting that the activated Li2S is
now contributing to the reaction and no further activation is needed.
Nevertheless, the peak currents of the of Li2S cathode in the STD
electrolyte with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive is much stronger than that in
the STD electrolyte, persuasively proving more activated Li2S and the

The beneﬁcial eﬀects of SL-NH4I additive can be further proved by
SEM and TEM images, which are shown in Fig. 5. Fig. 5a, b present the
SEM images of Li2S cathodes cycled in the STD electrolyte without and
with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive after initial activation. By comparing these
two SEM images with those of the pristine Li2S electrodes in Fig. S8, it
can be found that there is still a large amount of Li2S particles (more
than ~1 μm) reserved on the electrode after activating in the STD
electrolyte. It suggests that even the Li2S cathode in the STD electrolyte
is charged to 3.8 V, Li2S still cannot be full activated. In contrast, nearly
no large Li2S particles can be obviously observed on the electrode after
activating in the STD electrolyte with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive, which can
be ascribed to the beneﬁts of the synergistic dissolution and catalysis
eﬀects. It makes the activation and oxidation of Li2S easily. The TEM
images in Fig. 5d are agreed with the SEM results. It is obvious that the
Li2S particles in the STD electrolyte are much larger than that in the
STD electrolyte containing 0.5% SL-NH4I additive. Even after 10 cycles
at 0.2C, similar results also can be obtained in Fig. S9, which can
further prove the dissolution and catalysis eﬀects of the SL-NH4I ad-
ditive for Li2S cathode.
Beneﬁt from the positive eﬀects of the SL-NH4I additive on the ac-
tivation of Li2S, the cell cycled in the STD electrolyte with 0.5% SL-
NH4I additive was further tested at a higher current density of 0.5C to
verify the potential for practical applications. For comparison, the cell
cycled in the STD electrolyte was also tested. As shown in Fig. 6a, for
the STD electrolyte, the Li2S cathode initially shows a low discharge
capacity of 300 mAh g−1 and decays to 169 mAh g−1 after 400 cycles.
In contrast, the cycling performance of the Li2S cathode improves sig-
niﬁcantly by the adoption of 0.5% SL-NH4I additive. The Li2S cathode
with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive initially shows a much higher discharge
capacity of 600 mAh g−1. Even after 400 cycles, the discharge capacity
can still remain at 405 mAh g−1. The CE of both electrolytes during
cycling is ~99%, as presented in Fig. 6b. In addition, as shown in Fig.
S10, the voltage gap between the charge and discharge curves (10th,
50th, 150th, 200th, 400th) of the Li2S cathode cycled in the electrolyte
of STD with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive are obviously kept much smaller,
which indicates a lower polarization degree [5]. After cycling, the im-
pedance of cells in the STD electrolyte with 0.5% SL-NH4I additive
(~50 Ω) is also lower than that in the STD electrolyte (~85 Ω), as
shown in Fig. 6c. It indicates the improved Li+ kinetic by the 0.5% SL-
NH4I additive [46,47]. Inspired by the above mentioned good electro-
chemical performances, the SL-NH4I additive is further applied in Li-S
batteries with a mass loading of 2.5–2.7 mg cm−2 and electrolyte
amount of 18 µL per mg S. As shown in Fig. S11, with the adoption of
0.5% SL-NH4I additive, the electrochemical performances of Li-S bat-
teries are also signiﬁcantly improved. In addition, the higher CE of the
STD electrolyte may cause by the serious “shuttle eﬀect”. With 0.5% SL-
NH4I, the I−in the electrolyte can facilitate the reduction/oxidation of
polysulﬁdes, which is helpful for alleviating the “shuttle eﬀect”.
Therefore, the SL-NH4I additive shows a great potential for applications
in both Li2S and S based Li-S batteries.

4. Conclusions
In this work, 0.5% SL-NH4I is adopted as electrolyte additive to
eﬃciently alleviate the voltage barrier of Li2S. Beneﬁting from the
advantage of dissolving Li2S by SL and NH4
+, the pristine solid Li2S is
turned into liquid phase, which subsequently speeds up the transpor-
tation of electrons/Li+ ions and decreases activation voltage of Li2S.
Moreover, I−can catalyze the oxidation of Li2S to polysulﬁdes, which
can further reduce the activation voltage. As a result, with the adoption
of 0.5% SL-NH4I additive, the voltage barrier is disappeared and a ﬂat
charge plateau can be observed at ~3.0 V in the initial charging process
of the Li2S cathode. Moreover, its electrochemical performance is also
greatly enhanced by the synergistic dissolution and catalysis eﬀects of
SL-NH4I. In addition, the SL-NH4I additive can be also used in the S
cathode, and the electrochemical performances of Li-S batteries are also
signiﬁcantly improved. Therefore, the SL-NH4I additive shows a great
potential for applications in both Li2S and S based Li-S batteries.
