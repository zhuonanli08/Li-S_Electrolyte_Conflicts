Dendritic lithium (Li) growth hinders the applications of Li metal anode. Here, a fumed SiO 2 is proposed as an 
electrolyte additive to realize uniform Li + deposition. Due to the abundant hydroxyl group on the surface, the SiO 2 
particles as a pump provide an additional way for Li + transport, resulting in a homogeneous Li + ﬂux. Moreover, 
SiO 2 can also smooth the deposited Li and boost the formation of a LiF-rich SEI. As a result, the anode-free 
Li 2 S/Cu cell demonstrates a signiﬁcantly improved rechargeability (1055 mAh g − 1 , 1 st cycle; 523 mAh g − 1 , 200 th 
cycle). Moreover, even with limited electrolyte (electrolyte/sulfur ratio, 7 μL mg − 1 ) and Li (negative to positive 
capacity ratio, 3), the Li/S pouch cells can still maintain a high capacity of 689 mAh g − 1 after 200 cycles. This 
work shows a simple and eﬃcient way for constructing high-energy-density Li metal batteries. 
1. Introduction 
Lithium metal anode (LMA) is widely recognized as the most promis- 
ing anode candidate for the next-generation secondary batteries to make 
a breakthrough in energy density because of its high speciﬁc capac- 
ity (3860 mAh g − 1 ) and the lowest redox potential (-3.04 V vs. stan- 
dard hydrogen electrode) [1] . Unfortunately, several obstacles hinder 
the practical applications of LMA. (1) The dendritic lithium (Li) grows 
and accumulates. Because the natural convection in practical cells is 
unavoidable and unpredictable, the transport of cations is always un- 
even, which creates Li + concentration gradients near the electrode. As 
a result, the mass transfer-controlled dendrite formation is diﬃcult to 
overcome. (2) Vulnerable solid electrolyte interface (SEI). The active 
Li metal can react with the organic electrolytes, and the solid products 
form a passive SEI ﬁlm in the Li/electrolyte interface. Nevertheless, the 
heterogeneous SEI always produces local ﬂuctuations of Li + ﬂux, caus- 
ing the rugged growth of Li; furthermore, the deposited Li has preferred 
highly porous accumulation, resulting in high exposed surface to the 
electrolyte, which further boosts the side reactions and the formation 
of dead Li; (3) Volume expansion. Because the Li deposition process is 
hostless, the electrode undergoes inﬁnitely volume expansion during the 
Li plating/stripping cycles, easily causing cracks in the brittle SEI and 
the consequent repairing reactions. The results are the low Coulombic 
eﬃciency (CE) and the gradual depletion of Li and electrolyte [2–4] .

Many research works have been previously carried out to elucidate 
the growth mechanism of Li dendrites. Xiao believes the formation of the 
metal dendrite is rooted in the mass transport of metal cations. Due to 
the unavoidable and unpredictable natural convection in a static electro- 
chemical cell, the transport of Li + in the electrolyte is uneven, creating 
concentration gradients in diﬀerent directions near the electrode [5] . 
Meanwhile, the Li foil is not smooth completely; the surface unevenness 
worsens the balance of Li + ﬂux. So, the dendrite growth is the inevitable 
result of the uneven Li + mass transport. Zhang et al. have found that ac- 
celerating the Li diﬀusion kinetics within SEI can change the morphol- 
ogy of the deposited Li from dendritic to spherical [6] . Hence, it can 
be supposed that uniforming the Li + ﬂux and boosting the Li + transport 
within SEI can fundamentally suppress the Li dendrites’ growth. Accord- 
ingly, many strategies have been developed, including adapting artiﬁ- 
cial SEI [7–9] , advanced current collector design [10–12] , electrolyte 
modiﬁcation [13–15] . For example, Gong et al. introduce a unique 3D 
insulator-metal-insulator architecture (g-C 3 N 4 /graphene/g-C 3 N 4 ). The 
graphene framework can decrease the current density, and inorganic 
C 3 N 4 nanosheets can generate more Li 3 N content in SEI. Thus, the sand- 
wiched structure can guide uniform Li + ﬂux, stable SEI, and dense Li 
deposition [10] . Introducing an optimized electrolyte additive may be 
the most suitable route for large-scale application due to the simple op- 
eration. In general, the electrolyte additives can be simply divided into 
two types: SEI-regulating components and Ion-controlling nanoparticles.

The SEI-forming additives (Lithium nitrate [ 16 , 17 ], Fluoroethylene car- 
bonate [ 18 , 19 ], Vinylene carbonate [20] , so on [21–23] ) always aim 
to optimize SEI via improving the content of the active phase (Li 3 N, 
Li 2 O, or LiF). Diﬀerent from the SEI-regulating component, the inor- 
ganic nanoparticles (nanodiamonds [24] , montmorillonites [25] , ver- 
miculites [26] , etc. [ 27 , 28 ]) can regulate the Li + ﬂux by connecting 
with the charge carriers in electrolytes (Li + or anions). For example, 
Lu et al. have used an ionic liquid electrolyte with SiO 2 nanoparticle 
additive to alleviate the uneven growth of Li [28] . SiO 2 can tether the 
ionic liquid molecules and then construct an inorganic-organic nanos- 
tructure dispersed in the propylene carbonate-based electrolyte. In the 
novel hybrid electrolytes, the inorganic-organic particles can act as ab- 
sorbers of TFSI − anions, decreasing the space charge and reducing the 
anion inhomogeneity in the electrolyte. 
Furthermore, decreasing the amount of electrolyte and the excess 
Li has a great signiﬁcance for enhancing the energy density of the bat- 
teries. Thus, from the view of practical applications, the excess of Li 
and electrolyte must be considered to evaluate an electrolyte additive. 
In this perspective, the anode-free cells own the highest energy density 
theoretically among all the battery conﬁgurations and give the most re- 
liable demonstration of the eﬀectiveness of the diverse strategies for Li 
protection [29–31] . 
In this work, we introduce fumed silicon oxide (SiO 2 ) as a multi- 
functional electrolyte additive to enhance the rechargeability of LMA 
under harsh conditions. In this system, Li + is absorbed by the nano- 
sized SiO 2 particles spontaneously due to the -OH groups on the sur- 
face. Thus, SiO 2 serves like a pump, accelerating the Li + transference 
near the electrode/electrolyte interface, providing an additional trans- 
port path for Li + . The homogeneous Li + ﬂux uniforms the Li deposition 
and suppresses the side reactions. In addition, SiO 2 can also be helpful 
to the formation of a LiF-rich and crackless SEI. As a result, an anode- 
free Li 2 S/Cu cell can display a high initial capacity of 1055 mAh g − 1 and 
reserves a discharge capacity of 524 mAh g − 1 after 200 cycles. The ca- 
pacity decay ratio per cycle is as low as 0.2%. Furthermore, even when 
lithium and electrolyte are limited with an electrolyte/sulfur ratio (E/S) 
of 7 uL mg − 1 and a negative to positive capacity ratio (N/P) of 3, the Li/S 
pouch cell can still demonstrate a high initial capacity of 1021 mAh g − 1 
at 0.1C and remain reversible capacity of 689 mAh g − 1 after 200 cycles. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Preparation of SiO 2 -contained electrolyte 
The commercial fumed SiO 2 was kept in a vacuum oven at 80 °C 
for 24 h before use. Diﬀerent amount of SiO 2 was dispersed in the 
ether-based electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in v/v of 1:1 DOL/DME with 2 wt% 
LiNO 3 ). Subsequently, the colloid SiO 2 electrolyte was subsequently 
stirred for 4 h at 40 °C and then dispersed via ultrasonic for 2 h at 200 
W.

2.3. In-situ electroplating process in optical cells 
The electroplating process of Li onto the Cu foil was carried out in 
an optical cuvette at a current density of 2 mA cm − 2 . The cathode was 
a Cu foil (0.5 × 1 cm 2 ), and the anode was a Li foil (0.5 × 1 cm 2 ). 4 
mL electrolyte was added to the cell. The electroplating process was 
observed by optical microscopy (XPF-800C, China). 
2.4. In-situ Raman test 
Li/Cu half cells were assembled for in-situ Raman spectroscopy anal- 
ysis. The wavelength of the laser source is 785 nm (LabRAM HR800, 
Japan). The laser is focused on near Cu foil. The current density of de- 
position is set as 2 mA cm − 2 . All Raman spectra are displayed without 
any corrections. 
2.5. Fabrication of Li 2 S/C cathode 
Firstly, 350 mg Li 2 S and 150 mg carbon black were ground in an 
agate mortar. Secondly, the mixed powders were transferred to a glass 
bottle, and 5 mL N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) was added under mag- 
netic stirring for 12 h. Thirdly, the slurry was added onto a cotton car- 
bon paper and then heated to 70 °C overnight under a vacuum. Finally, 
the Li 2 S/C composite cathode was obtained after cutting the paper into 
circular pieces with a diameter of 8 mm. 
2.6. Fabrication of S/C cathode 
At ﬁrst, 70 wt% S powder and 30 wt% carbon black were mixed 
uniformly and heated at 155°C for 24 h. Then, 90 wt% S/C composite 
powder and 10 wt% aqueous LA133 binders were stirred for 12 h. The 
slurry was coated onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil current collector. 
Next, the as-prepared electrode was frozen at -40°C for 12 h in advance 
and then vacuumed to 1 Pa for 10 h. At last, the S/C cathode was cut 
into a rectangular electrode. 
2.7. Electrochemical measurement 
All coin cells (CR-2032) were assembled in an argon-ﬁlled gloved 
box (H 2 O < 0.1 ppm, O 2 < 0.1 ppm). Polypropylene membranes (Celgard 
2400) were used as a separator. For the Coulombic eﬃciency test: typi- 
cal Li/Cu cells were assembled with a Cu foil ( Ф = 12 mm) and a Li foil 
( Ф = 12 mm). 40 μL electrolyte was injected into. Li was deposited dur- 
ing the discharge process in advance. Then the cell was charged until 
the voltage was to 0.5 V for each cycle. The Li/Li symmetrical cells were 
fabricated with two Li foils to the voltage hysteresis test. Controlling the 
discharge/charge time of the cells keeps the identical Li depositing and 
stripping. The Li 2 S/Cu coin cells were charged until the voltage reached 
3.7 V and then cycled between 1.7 V and 2.8 V at 0.1 C on battery cy- 
clers (LAND, China). For the Li/S pouch cells: after assembling, the cells 
were directly cycled between 1.7 V and 2.8 V at 0.1 C. Tafel curves were 
measured at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s − 1 .

2.9. Computational methods 
We employed the ﬁrst principles to perform density functional the- 
ory (DFT) calculations within the generalized gradient approximation 
(GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation. We chose 
the projected augmented wave (PAW) potentials to describe the ionic 
cores and took valence electrons into account using a plane-wave basis 
set with a kinetic energy cutoﬀof 450 eV. Partial occupancies of the 
Kohn-Sham orbitals were allowed using the Gaussian smearing method 
and a width of 0.05 eV. The electronic energy was considered self- 
consistent when the energy change was smaller than 10 − 5 eV. Geometry 
optimization was considered convergent when the energy change was 
smaller than 0.05 eV Å− 1 . Grimme’s DFT-D3 methodology was used to 
describe the dispersion interactions among all the atoms in adsorption 
models. The adsorption energies (E ads ) were calculated as E ads = E ad/sub 
-E ad -E sub , where E ad/sub , E ad , and E sub are the total energies of the op- 
timized adsorbate/substrate system, the adsorbate in the structure, and 
the clean substrate, respectively. What’s more, Li ions migration barrier 
energies were evaluated using the climbing nudged elastic band (CI- 
NEB) methods.

Commercialized fumed SiO 2 shows a typical amorphous particle 
structure (Figure S1a, S1b). We use the FT-IR to test the functional group 
on the SiO 2 . According to the result, the peaks at 1150 and 3450 cm − 1 
represent Si-O-Si and -OH groups, respectively, which conﬁrms the po- 
lar functional groups (Figure S1c). The negatively charged SiO 2 will 
connect with the positive Li + by electrostatic force when added into 
the electrolyte. It is found that both the ionic conductivity and the Li + 
transference number of electrolytes increase with the concentrations of 
SiO 2 , but too much SiO 2 ( ≥ 1.0%) can decrease them slightly (Figure 
S2, S3). The reason may be the particle aggregation in high concen- 
trations and the decreased exposed surface of SiO 2 . The zeta poten- 
tials in diﬀerent dispersions are investigated to verify the connection 
between Li + and SiO 2 (Figure S3c). The fumed SiO 2 dispersed in wa- 
ter shows zeta potentials of -28 mV, -25 mV, and -26 mV at 0.1%, 
0.5% and 1.0% concentrations, respectively. The negative zeta poten- 
tial conﬁrms the negatively charged surface of SiO 2 . After adding LiCl 
salt (1 M), the zeta potentials increase to -17.5 mV, -9.9 mV, and -10.5 
mV, respectively, which proves SiO 2 can absorb Li + exactly. Beneﬁted 
from the charged surface, SiO 2 can disperse colloidally in the electrolyte 
(Figure S4). 
The ﬁnite element analysis further simulates the concentration distri- 
bution of Li + in electrolytes. The Li + distribution in the electrolyte with- 
out SiO 2 demonstrates a typical concentration polarization region. The 
Li + concentration at the electrode/electrolyte interface is signiﬁcantly 
lower than that in the body part (1 M), leading to delayed Li + com- 
pensation during Li deposition and the further growth of Li dendrites 
( Fig. 2 a ). In contrast, the Li + concentration polarization in the SiO 2 - 
contained electrolyte is signiﬁcantly lower because the Li + is pumped 
to the electrode interface from the surface of SiO 2 in addition to the tra- 
ditional diﬀusion and electromigration. As a result, the Li + ﬂux at the 
electrolyte/anode interface is much richer, ensuring a uniform Li + de- 
position ( Fig. 2 b ). Figs. 2 c-f show the simulation results of the density 
functional theory (DFT) calculations for the mechanism of Li + transfer- 
ence. At ﬁrst, the bond between Li + and Li is metallic, while the interac- 
tion between SiO 2 and Li + is ionic according to the charge distributions 
( Fig. 2 c, d ). Then, the binding energies on diﬀerent sites are calculated 
( Fig. 2 e ). The binding energies of Li + on Li top, Li-Li bridge, Si top, O 
top, and Si-O hollow site are -0.76, -1.47, -1.17, -0.61, and -2.31 eV, 
respectively. When Li + adsorbs on the Si-O hollow site, the binding en- 
ergy is -2.31 eV, which is much higher than the binding energies of Li + 
on Li top (-0.76 eV), Li-Li bridge (-1.47 eV), Si top (-1.17 eV), and O top 
(-0.61 eV). We guess the high binding energy of the Si-O hollow site 
comes from the lattice distortion. When Li + adsorbs on the surface of 
SiO 2 , the lattice distortion is developed. Because the Si-O hollow site 
owns many voids to relieve the expansion, the Li + adsorbed on the Si- 
O hollow is the most stable. Thus, the binding energy of Li + on the 
Si-O hollow sites is the highest. Similar results are observed when cal- 
culating the binding energy between Li + and Li metal. The binding en-

Fig. 4 shows the morphology of the deposited Li on Cu foil with 1

SiO 2 , irregular and highly porous Li is observed ( Fig. 4 a ); after 50 cycles, 
more noodle-like sediments appear, corresponding to the growth of Li 
dendrites ( Fig. 4 e ). After introducing 0.1% SiO 2 into the electrolyte, the 
porosity of the Li ﬂat decreases slightly in the initial deposition ( Fig. 4 b ), 
but Li dendrites still appear after 50 cycles ( Fig. 4 f ). When added 0.5% 
SiO 2 , the Li ﬁlm displays the most uniform surface with nearly no pores 
( Fig. 4 c ), and no dendritic Li can be found after 50 cycles ( Fig. 4 g ). 
However, when the concentration of SiO 2 increases to 1.0%, the Li ﬁlm 
becomes highly porous again ( Fig. 4 d, h ). The trend variation agrees 
with reducing the Li + conductivity and Li + transference number. The 
cross-section view in Fig. 4 i-l shows the density of the deposited Li more 
clearly. Under 4 mAh cm − 2 deposition at 1 mA cm − 2 current, the Li ﬁlm 
in the 0.5% SiO 2 -contained electrolyte demonstrates a thickness of 26 
μm, much thinner than 39 μm in the electrolyte without SiO 2 , 34 μm 
in the 0.1% SiO 2 and 30 μm in the 1.0% SiO 2 electrolytes. We show 
the morphologies of the Cu foil after Li strips in Fig. S8 . Many dead Li 
still exists on the Cu foil in the blank electrolyte. In contrast, with SiO 2 
addition, dead Li decreases drastically. Among them, the system with 
0.5% SiO 2 addition demonstrates the cleanest surface with minimum 
dead Li. Thus, the SiO 2 can suppress the formation of dead Li during Li 
stripping. 
The chemical analysis of SEI can obtain more insights into the func- 
tional mechanism of the SiO 2 additive. The Si signals in the XPS spec- 
trum appear conspicuously in the SEI in the SiO 2 -contained electrolyte 
and vanish without SiO 2 ( Figs. S9, S10 ), implying a part of SiO 2 co- 
deposits with Li + . In the detailed view, the C 1s spectrum of the surface 
for the cycled Li without SiO 2 shows four peaks at 285.1, 286.9, 289.7, 
and 293.3 eV, which can be attributed to the C-C/C-H, C-O, C = O, and C- 
F bond, respectively ( Fig. 5 a ) [ 16 , 17 ]. However, for the SiO 2 system, the 
C-F bond disappears absolutely, and the other three peak areas decrease 
after adding 0.5% SiO 2 ( Fig. 5 d ); meanwhile, the Li-F bond’s peak area 
increases signiﬁcantly ( Fig. 5 b, e ). It seems the SEI formed within the 
SiO 2 -contained electrolyte consists of fewer organic compounds (such as 
ROCO 2 Li) and more inorganic materials (LiF). More LiF means higher 
Li + conductivity and accelerated Li + ﬂux kinetics at the interface, con- 
tributing to a uniform Li deposition. The signals of O 1s and Li 1s agree 
with the conclusion ( Fig. 5 c-f and S10 ). The smoothing mechanism of 
SiO 2 is veriﬁed by EPMA ( Fig. 5 g-l ). As shown in the EPMA surface 
images using a backscattered electron (BSE) source ( Fig. 5 g ), the nano- 
sized SiO 2 particles selectively gather on the cracks between Li crystals. 
From the analysis of wavelength-dispersive spectrums (WDS), the C con- 
tent is less compared with other elements, which implies the fewer or-

4. Conclusion 
In summary, the low-cost fumed SiO 2 is proposed as a multi- 
functional electrolyte additive to achieve a high-performance LMA: 
Firstly, the Li + will be absorbed on the SiO 2 particles spontaneously. 
Secondly, the Li + -SiO 2 complex transports. As the complex comes to 
the electrode, Li + will be pumped by SiO 2 to the surface of the current 
collector. The unique pumped eﬀect of SiO 2 decreases the Li + concen- 
tration gradient. In addition, we ﬁnd that SiO 2 can also spontaneously 
adsorb the TFSI − ions. The adsorbed TFSI − can be reduced to LiF chem- 
ically and electrochemically when the SiO 2 is co-deposited with Li + . 
Thus, the SEI formed on the deposited Li ﬁlm is LiF-rich one. With the 
SiO 2 -contained electrolyte, the capacity retention of the Li 2 S/Cu cells is 
increased from 15% to 50% after 200 cycles. Even at high mass loading 
of 10.4 mg Li2S cm − 2 and lean-electrolyte of 7.7 uL mg Li2S − 1 , the cell can 
still work steadily over 50 cycles. For the Li/S pouch cell, at a high areal 
S loading of 4 mg cm − 2 , low E/S ratio of 7 uL mg − 1 , and N/P ratio of 3, 
it can still deliver a reversible capacity of 689 mAh g − 1 after 200 cycles.
