2. Note 
 
Supplementary Note 1  
1) Polysulfide-trapping mechanism of the calcium cation:  
The strong electrostatic interaction between Ca2+ and polysulfides leads to the formation of 
tightly associated Ca2+-PS complexes. As shown in Fig. 3c, Ca2+ ions coordinate with 
multiple polysulfide anions compared to Li+ due to its stronger Lewis acidity, forming a 
stable Ca2+-PS network in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 8d). A similar mechanism 
using Lewis acidic electrolyte additive has been reported in recent literature (Joule, 8, 3397–
3411 (2024)), where LiBF4 reacts with nucleophilic LiPS to form LiF and BF3. The strong 
Lewis acidity of BF3 subsequently leads to BF3-PS adduct formation, which lowers 
polysulfide solubility. By analogy, the interaction between Ca2+ and polysulfides is expected 
to play a critical role in reducing LiPS solubility within the electrolyte. 
 
2) Formation mechanism of CaS and S8: The Ca2+-PS complex undergoes conversion via two 
possible pathways during discharge: 
a. Electrochemical Reduction: The Ca electrolyte used in our manuscript contains a relatively 
low concentration of Ca2+ compared to Li+, the electrochemical reduction of Ca-PS 
complexes was not apparent. However, when Ca(OTf)2 concentration is increased to 0.5 M, 
an additional peak appears at ~2.15 V in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) and also in the 
discharge voltage profiles following the 1st plateau of S8 to Li2S4 conversion. This peak aligns 
well with the electrochemical reduction potential of low-order Ca polysulfides (CaSn, 2 ≤ n ≤ 
4) to CaS2 and CaS (~2.2 V vs. Li/Li+) (Small, 16, 2001806 (2020)). The persistence of this 
peak even after 20 cycles suggests that Ca-S redox process is reversible. 
b. Chemical Precipitation: The chemical precipitation reaction is expected to occur due to the 
strong electrostatic attraction between Ca2+ and the S2−, leading to the spontaneous formation 
of thermodynamically stable CaS. Since CaS has extremely low solubility even in highly 
polar solvents like H2O, it cannot be solvated in DME, forming precipitates. This chemical 
reaction was confirmed through XRD (Supplementary Fig. 11c) and XPS (Supplementary 
Figs. 11d, e) analysis of precipitates collected from Li2S8 + Ca(OTf)2 in DME. 
 
As a result of this mechanism, the CaS precipitated on the cathode was experimentally 
analyzed using TEM (Fig. 4a) and XPS of discharged cathodes. 

<<PAGE 40>>
 
3) Conversion mechanism from CaS to Ca2+:  
CaS undergoes oxidation back to Ca2+ because its oxidation potential within the operating 
voltage of Li-S batteries. Previous studies on Ca-S batteries (Small, 16, 2001806 (2020)/ Adv. 
Energy Mater., 9, (2019)) have shown that CaS is oxidized to CaSn and to S8 at approximately 
2.35 and 2.47 V vs. Li/Li+, respectively (corresponding to 2.18 V and 2.3 V vs. Ca/Ca2+). In 
this study, we experimentally observed the oxidation of CaS to Ca2+ and S8 by ICP-MS (Fig. 
4f) analysis. Additionally, we directly observed the CaS oxidation using CaS-casted electrode 
(Fig. 4g). Upon charging the Li-CaS cell, the oxidation peaks of CaS appeared, and 
subsequently discharging it, two distinct discharge plateaus were observed, indicating the 
formation of S8 from CaS when CaS was the only available sulfur source.
