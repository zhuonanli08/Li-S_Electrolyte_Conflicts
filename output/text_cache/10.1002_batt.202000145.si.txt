Figure S3. C 1s XPS spectra of the investigated thin Li metal electrodes. Note that all spectra are 
normalized to have the same intensity of peak at ∼285 eV. 
For all samples, the C 1s spectra show that the surface layer is built up by a number of different carbon-
containing compounds, as indicated in Fig. S3, for both soaked and cycled samples. The peaks are (from 
low to high binding energy) assigned to hydrocarbons (C-C, ∼285 eV) and carbon bonded to oxygen 
atoms in different forms (C-O, ∼287 eV; C=O, ∼289 eV; O-C=O / CO3, ∼290 eV). The carbon spectra 
can be used to evaluate the evolution of the surface layer during cycling, because the carbon–oxygen 
peaks are common products from electrolyte degradation. In addition, a peak at ∼293 eV can be seen 

<<PAGE 5>>
in the C 1s spectra (in Fig. S3) which originate from LiTFSI.[2] Contribution of LiTFSI indicates electrolyte 
consumption due to side reactions at the electrode/electrolyte interface.  
When comparing the results of the samples presented in Fig. S3, a number of observations can be 
made. The blue dotted line represents the pristine thin Li metal foil. Two prominent peaks at ∼285 and 
∼290 eV appear, which can be attributed to adventitious carbon contamination in the glovebox and 
can normally be found in academic research laboratories. The blue solid line represents the sample 
when the thin Li anode is left to rest at OCV conditions for ∼3 days in the Li-S battery. A prominent 
peak at ∼289 eV appear, which can be attributed to HCO2Li due to DOL reduction.[1] A small emission 
a ∼293 eV corresponding to residuals from the TFSI anion can be seen.  
When instead applying current to the cell (after a single full cycle—the red line in Fig. S3), the 
electrolyte is rapidly reduced at the Li surface to form a SEI layer on the surface. This is seen as the 
prominent peak at ∼287 eV which is attributed to C-O species for this sample. Moreover, the peak at 
∼ 293 eV corresponding to LiTFSI are also becoming more distinct. This indicates that there is a larger 
extent of LiTFSI decomposition on Li in the cell.[2] 
The black line represents ∼47 days exposure to the electrolyte at OCV conditions. It is worth 
mentioning that the dominant product (beside hydrocarbons) can be found at ∼290 eV, which 
originate from a chemical environment that could be contributed to O-C=O / carbonate or carboxylate 
compounds. However, the origin of this peak remains unclear.   
After 89 cycles, the C 1s spectra (grey dotted line and grey line) display a fairly similar C-O/C-C ratio 
with samples in having short or long OCV storage conditions, but higher C-F (LiTFSI)/C-C ratios. These 
observations suggest that within the same XPS probe volume, the SEI layer comprises fewer organic 
species and more inorganic species such as LiTFSI decomposition products. This argument is further 
supported by that the relative atomic concentration of carbon is lower (∼10-20 %) for the cycled 
samples compared to the pristine and OCV samples (see Fig. S4). In turn, F 1s (which dominantly origin 
from LiTFSI) appears to be enriched at the surface. Interestingly, the relative atomic concentration of 
Li appears to be anticorrelated with the carbon concentration (the more C, the less Li, and vice versa), 
and instead correlate with fluorine concentration. This suggests that a large extent of Li compounds is 
close to fluorine containing species. However, no very crucial information can be obtained from Li 1s 
spectra and they are therefore not included.
