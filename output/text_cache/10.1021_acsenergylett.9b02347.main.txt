ABSTRACT: This is the ﬁrst practical demonstration of a next-
generation graphite−Li2S battery using a high-load Li2S cathode
on a porous current collector and a small amount of a single
electrolyte. Li2S is a promising cathode material for Li−S
batteries, but it must be mixed with a conductive substrate
owing to its insulating properties. We prepared Li2S/C
composites using commercially available carbon-based materials
as carbon hosts and agents for the reduction of a Li2SO4 precursor
to Li2S. Intimate contact between the Li2S/C composite
constituents was achieved in situ with the additional carbon-
based material. The speciﬁc surface area of the carbon-based
precursor aﬀected the electrochemical performance of the Li2S/C
composite. Using a sparingly solvating electrolyte enabled highly
reversible electrochemical graphite intercalation and suppressed
the polysulﬁde shuttling eﬀect owing to a reduction in lithium polysulﬁde intermediate solubility. The graphite−Li2S full
cell exhibited high Coulombic eﬃciency and stable long-term cycling performance.
L
i−ion batteries (LIBs) are crucial components of
electric vehicles and large-scale sustainable and renew-
able power supply grids owing to their high energy
density.1,2 However, the speciﬁc capacities of conventional
intercalation-based cathodes for LIBs, such as LiCoO2,3
LiNi1/3Co1/3Mn1/3O2,4 and LiFePO4
5 (140−160 mAh g−1),
cannot satisfy the increasingly demanding capacity require-
ments of emerging applications. For this reason, Li−S batteries
have received signiﬁcant attention over the past decade, as
their theoretical speciﬁc energy density far exceeds that of
traditional LIBs. Moreover, they can be fabricated at low cost,
and there is a natural abundance of sulfur.6−8
Nevertheless, several obstacles hinder the commercial
application of Li−S batteries. The charge and discharge
processes in Li−S batteries involve complex conversion
reactions, including the dissolution, precipitation, and
disproportionation of lithium polysulﬁde (PS; Li2Sm) inter-
mediates. The dissolution of PS causes a shuttling eﬀect,
especially for long-chain (m = 4−8) intermediates.9−11
Recently, highly concentrated and sparingly solvating electro-
lytes and ionic liquids, in which PS has limited solubility, have
been adopted to inhibit the PS shuttling eﬀect. Notably, high
Coulombic eﬃciency and long charge−discharge cycles have
been achieved with Li−S cells using sparingly solvating
electrolytes.12−16 Another common and highly problematic
issue is the formation of dendrites on lithium metal anodes and
the consumption of electrolyte through an irreversible reaction
with the freshly deposited lithium on the anodes.9−11 The
issues with lithium metal anodes can be circumvented by
pairing sulfur-based lithium-containing cathodes with lithium-
free anodes.6,17,18
Lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) is a promising cathode material for
Li−S batteries, as it has a high theoretical speciﬁc capacity of
1166 mAh g−1.19 However, Li2S is neither electrically nor
ionically conductive; hence, signiﬁcant eﬀorts have been
devoted to improving the activation and utilization of Li2S
cathodes.20−23 Li2S/C composite cathodes prepared using
carbon coatings, carbon nanoparticles, and amorphous carbon
matrices have shown superior performance to Li2S cathodes.
However, the high melting point of Li2S prevents cathode
preparation by the melt diﬀusion method established for sulfur
cathodes.24,25 For this reason, commercially available bulk Li2S
powders are typically employed as starting materials, and Li2S/
C composites are often prepared via direct high-energy ball
milling or solution processing.21,26 These are costly methods
that are limited in scale and thus have limited suitability for
large-scale composite production. Alternative processes are
required for mass production.

Among Li-free anodes, graphite and graphite/Si anodes are
feasible options for practical applications. However, few
graphite/Li2S full cell systems have been reported, because
the ether-based electrolytes used in Li−S batteries are
generally incompatible with graphite anodes.27 One method
of fabricating graphite/Li2S cells is to prepare microporous-
carbon-encapsulated sulfur electrodes that are compatible with
carbonate electrolytes, followed by an in situ lithiation process
to convert the encapsulated sulfur to Li2S.28 This is a
complicated process, and it is diﬃcult to enhance the sulfur
content in the electrode owing to the limited micropore
volume, which typically results in a low energy density. Bilayer
electrolytes separated by an inorganic solid electrolyte (Li
superionic conductor) have been proposed to enable
compatibility with both the Li2S electrode (ether electrolyte
layer) and graphite (carbonate electrolyte layer).29 Never-
theless, these proposals are still at the proof-of-concept stage
and lack practical utility.
Herein, we prepared Li2S/C composite cathodes via the
simple reduction of Li2SO4/C composite precursors using
several diﬀerent commercially available carbon-based materi-
als,22 following our previous study.30 Notably, the carbon-
based materials serve both as reductants and as hosts for the
Li2S product. In addition, Li2SO4 is a far cheaper material
compared with Li2S. We examined the relationships between
the properties of the carbon-based materials and the discharge
capacities of the Li2S/C composite cathodes. To study the
charge and discharge behaviors of the Li2S/C composite
cathodes, we used highly concentrated and sparingly solvating

Unlike conventional organic electrolytes, the highly
concentrated electrolytes in this work enable the reversible
intercalation of lithium ions in the graphite anode without
cointercalation of the solvent, even in the absence of ethylene
carbonate.32−35 We achieved a high energy density of 234 Wh
kg−1 with a combination of high Li2S cathode loading on a
three-dimensional (3D) porous Al current collector and a low
electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S ratio; μL mg−1). Moreover,
with a high-load Li2S cathode and sparingly solvating
electrolyte, we were able to demonstrate the operation of a
Li2S/graphite full cell. The rate capability and cycling stability
of the cell were excellent, and its Coulombic eﬃciency was
high. Through these ﬁndings, we demonstrate a graphite−Li2S
battery using a single electrolyte, which has a practical energy
density by increasing Li2S-loading on the cathode even under
lean electrolyte conditions.

Thermogravimetry (TG) measurements were performed in the
range of 30−1000 °C to identify the appropriate reaction
temperature for each carbon host. Similarly to our previously
reported results,30 two weight-loss steps were observed in the
TG curve of each sample (Figure S1). The initial weight loss
occurred between 80 and 150 °C because of the removal of
crystalline water in Li2SO4·H2O and absorbed water, and a
more marked loss occurred above 700 °C owing to the release
of CO2 generated by the reduction of Li2SO4 to Li2S.22 The
observed reaction temperature is slightly shifted among
diﬀerent carbon materials (800−900 °C) probably because
their surface area and surface functional group simultaneously
aﬀect the kinetics of the above reaction. These results
demonstrate that the reduction of Li2SO4 by the carbon-
based materials was successful with each of the carbon-based
materials used.
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) patterns of the Li2S/C composites
are shown in Figure 1a. Five intense peaks were observed at
the same positions in each pattern, at 27.0°, 31.2°, 44.8°, 53.1°,
and 55.6°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311),
and (222) reﬂections of cubic Li2S, respectively (JCPDS Card
No. 23-0369). Hence, the type of carbon material does not
aﬀect the formation of the Li2S/C composite. In the patterns
of composites made with vapor-grown carbon ﬁbers (VGCF),
acetylene black (AB), and graphene nanoplatelet aggregates
(GNAs), minute diﬀraction peaks were observed at 33.6°,
indicating that they contained trace quantities of Li2O (JCPDS
Card No. 77-2144). The low speciﬁc surface area of these
carbons and the presence of oxygen-containing carboxyl,
epoxy, or hydroxyl groups on the carbon surfaces are likely to
contribute to Li2O formation.30

It is thought that reduction in Li2S electrodes at high current
densities is limited by the mass transfer of lithium ions in
sparingly solvating electrolytes, especially during the second
plateau stage (note that much higher overvoltage for the
second plateau can be observed in the G4-based electrolyte),
which is a reason for the cell voltage decrease during the
discharging process in Figure 2a.36,37 The superior rate
performance and lower overvoltage observed with the SL-
based electrolyte can be ascribed to improved reaction kinetics,
which are enabled by enhanced mass transfer via the Li+
hopping/exchange mechanism.31
Cathodes with high Li2S loading are needed to fabricate
batteries with high energy densities. In this work, we prepared
an electrode with high Li2S loading by embedding the KB-
based Li2S/C composite within the porous structure of Al
foam. This ensured suﬃcient contact between the current
collector and the insulating active material. The initial
discharge capacities of Li/Li2S cells with various Li2S loadings
measured with the [Li(SL)2][TFSA]:HFE (1:2) electrolyte are
shown in Figure 3a. The capacities of the cells with Li2S
loadings of 2−6 mg cm−2 exceed 800 mAh g−1. Typical initial
discharge curves at Li2S loadings of 3, 4, and 6 mg cm−2 are
shown in Figure 3b. The discharge curve of each cell contains
two plateaus, indicating that Li−ion ﬂux at the 1/12 C rate is
suﬃcient with the SL-based electrolyte.
The electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio has a profound
inﬂuence on the energy density of practical Li−S batteries at
the cell level. In addition to high sulfur loading in Li2S
electrodes, decreasing the E/S ratio is essential for increasing
the energy density. However, it has been reported that, with a
conventional ether-based organic electrolyte, decreasing the E/
S ratio from 7 to 5 reduces the discharge capacity of Li−S cells
from 1100 to 700 mAh g−1.38 Solubilized PS intermediates can
reach saturation in ether-based electrolytes at an E/S ratio of
∼5, which inhibits ionic transport in the electrolyte and
reduces the discharge capacity.38 It has been proposed that
using a sparingly solvating electrolyte could be beneﬁcial for
achieving high energy densities at low E/S ratios, because the
electrochemical reaction at the cathode does not depend on
the dissolution of PS in the cell.39

capacity of Li/Li2S cells (Li2S loading: 3−11 mg cm−2) using
[Li(SL)2][TFSA]:HFE (1:2) as the electrolyte. In this study,

E/S was calculated by converting the mass of Li2S in the
composite electrode into the corresponding elemental sulfur
amount. As shown in Figure 3c, the E/S ratio has little impact
on capacity at E/S ratios greater than ∼4. In this range, the
cells exhibit almost constant discharge capacities near 800 mAh
g−1. However, the capacity is drastically decreased at E/S ratios
below 3.4. The initial charge−discharge curves at a relatively
low E/S ratio of 4.2 show two well-deﬁned plateaus, along with
a high discharge capacity of 830 mAh g−1 (Figure 3d). The
gravimetric energy density of this cell is estimated to be 234
Wh kg−1 at 1/12 C based on the actual mass of the Li2S/C
electrode, the electrolytes, the separator, and the theoretical
mass of the lithium metal anode. The Li/Li2S cell with the SL-
based electrolyte thus has a high energy density, even though
its E/S ratio is low.
Previous studies have shown that various highly concen-
trated electrolytes, including [Li(SL)2][TFSA] and [Li(G4)]-
[TFSA], allow reversible charging and discharging (i.e.,
reversible Li+ intercalation) of graphite electrodes without
cointercalation of the solvent.32−35 The charging and
discharging of a graphite electrode has also been demonstrated
in a concentrated glyme-based electrolyte diluted with HFE.33
These experimental results encouraged us to investigate the
electrochemical behavior of graphite in the SL-based electro-
lyte diluted with HFE. The charge−discharge curves of a Li/
graphite half cell with [Li(SL)2][TFSA]:HFE (1:2) electrolyte
at 1/20 C are shown in Figure S2. An initial capacity of 443
mAh g−1 and initial Coulombic eﬃciency of 83% were attained
because of the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
on the graphite electrode. The cell reached a discharge capacity
of 370 mAh g−1 in the second cycle, which is close to the
theoretical capacity. The capacity remained near this value for
several subsequent cycles. The results demonstrate that the
concentrated SL-based electrolyte is highly compatible with a
graphite anode.
We assembled a Li2S/graphite full cell with a KB-based Li2S
loading of 3.65 mg cm−2 and graphite loading of 11.00 mg
cm−2 and evaluated its electrochemical behavior (Figure 4a).
After the ﬁrst charge−discharge cycle at 200 μA cm−2 (∼1/20
C), the rate capability of the cell was determined at a constant
charge current density of 200 μA cm−2 and various discharge
current densities. As shown in Figure 4b, the discharge proﬁles
display two well-deﬁned voltage plateaus regardless of the
current density. This demonstrates the excellent rate capability
of the Li2S/graphite full cell. The capacity decreases gradually
from 750 mAh g(Li2S)
−1 at a current density of 100 μA cm−2 to
∼600 mAh g(Li2S)
−1 at a current density of 1000 μA cm−2. It is
worth noting that capacity decay is marginal at current
densities of 100−1000 μA cm−2 (Figure 4c).
Cycling data were subsequently obtained at charge and
discharge current densities of 500 μA cm−2 at ∼1/8 C. The
results are shown in Figure 4d. High capacity retention relative
to the initial discharge capacity is observed over 300 cycles.
The Coulombic eﬃciency during the rate test is somewhat
lower than 100%. However, during the subsequent charge−
discharge cycles at the 1/8 C rate, eﬃciency is near 100%.
Although the energy density of the full cell is not very high
(80−90 Wh kg−1 at 1/8 C), the combination of a high-load
Li2S electrode and a graphite/Si composite anode can be
expected to provide good capacity with an optimized cell
conﬁguration. For example, a laminate cell could be employed
to improve the energy density of the full cell system. This
conﬁguration will be evaluated in a future study.

In summary, Li2S/C composites were obtained via the
simple reduction of Li2SO4 by diﬀerent commercial carbon-
based materials. We found that the speciﬁc surface area of the
carbon host had a signiﬁcant impact on the electrochemical
performance of the Li2S/C composite. By employing carbon-
based materials with large speciﬁc surface areas, excellent
electrochemical performance could be achieved. A concen-
trated SL-based electrolyte diluted with HFE enabled good
electrochemical performance of the Li2S/C composite
cathodes, and its compatibility with a graphite anode was
excellent. The graphite/electrolyte/Li2S full cell system
exhibited excellent rate capability, stable long-term cycling
performance, and high Coulombic eﬃciency. Although the
energy density does not far exceed that of current LIBs, the
graphite−Li2S batteries based on the earth-abundant elements
and the nonﬂammable sparingly solvating electrolyte may ﬁnd
applications in safer and cost-eﬀective energy storage systems.
