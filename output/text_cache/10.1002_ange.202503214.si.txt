Experimental and computational details 
1. Materials 
Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), ethylene carbonate (EC), fluorinated ethylene carbonate 
(FEC), and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) were purchased from DoDoChem. 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) was sourced from TCI. All the solvents were subjected to 
dehydration treatment using pre-heated molecular sieves prior to use. 
2. Preparation of electrolytes and sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) 
LiFSI was dissolved in the electrolyte mixtures of FEC-DMC-TTE or EC-DMC-TTE in specific 
ratios (where the volume ratio of FEC to DMC or EC to DMC is 1:1). The electrolyte with a 
composition of LiFSI:EC-DMC:TTE = 1:4.92:5.22 (molar ratio) is referred to as EDT-5.22T. The 
electrolyte obtained by replacing EC with FEC is denoted as FDT-5.22T. The mixed solvent (FEC-
DMC) is referred to as FD. The electrolytes with LiFSI:FD:TTE = 1:x:3.5 (molar ratio, x = 3.38, 4.92, 
9.14) are termed Li-xFDT. Additionally, the electrolytes with LiFSI:FD:TTE = 1:4.92:x (molar ratio, 
x = 0.1, 0.25, 0.45, 0.8, 1.8, 2.3, 3.27, 3.75, 5.22, 8, 15) are designated as FDT-xT. Electrolytes 
without TTE diluent are designated as Li-xFD, where x represents the molar ratio of FD to LiFSI salt. 
Similarly, the mixtures of FD:TTE (without LiFSI) = 4.92:x (molar ratio, x = 0.1, 2.3, 3.27, 5.22, 8) 
are designated as FD-xT-noLi. 
To synthesize SPAN, sulfur (S) and polyacrylonitrile (PAN) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. 
S and PAN were mixed in a mass ratio of 1:4 and ball-milled for 2 hours. The resulting mixture was 
heated in a tube furnace at 450 °C for 6 hours. The synthesis process is shown as follows:

3. Preparation of electrodes 
SPAN, Super P, guar gum, and polyacrylic acid were thoroughly ground in a mortar at a mass ratio 
of 8:1:0.5:0.5. The mixture was then dispersed in deionized water and homogenized to a suitable 
viscosity. The resulting slurry was coated onto aluminum current collectors and vacuum-dried at 
80 °C for 12 hours. By controlling the coating thickness, SPAN electrodes with varying areal loadings 
were obtained. SPAN electrodes were punched into 10 mm discs, and their masses were measured to 
calculate the content of active material in each disc. Similarly, the electrodes could also be cut into 
rectangular shapes of 6.3 cm × 7.8 cm for the assembly of pouch-type batteries. 
4. Characterization 
The morphology of samples was characterized using a thermal field emission scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) (JEOL JSM-7900F) and a highly resolution transmission electron microscope 
(HRTEM) (Talos F200X G2). The solvation structures of different electrolytes were analyzed using 
a Raman spectrometer (XploRA PLUS, Horiba). UV-visible spectra were obtained using a Cary 4000 
UV–vis spectrometer (Agilent Technologies). Fourier transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was 
collected on a BRUKER TENSOR II (FTS6000) series with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) 
module. The 17O and 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of the electrolytes were acquired 
by using the NMR spectrometer (AVANCE III 400 MHz). The chemical composition of sample 
surfaces was analyzed by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Scientific 
ESCALAB 250Xi). XPS used a vacuum transfer device, which could prevent samples from being

exposed to air during the transfer of sample from the glovebox to the vacuum chamber. When there 
was no special instruction, SPAN cathode and lithium metal used for XPS or HRTEM tests were 
obtained from the corresponding batteries after running for 50 cycles. 
5. Battery assembly 
All coin batteries (CR2032) were assembled in a glove box filled with argon (with water and 
oxygen levels < 0.1 ppm). For the Li–SPAN batteries, lithium foils with the thickness of 500 μm were 
used as anodes, and SPAN electrodes were used as cathodes, with approximately 60 μL of electrolyte 
added to each battery. For the Li–SPAN full batteries, 25 or 50 μm lithium foils were used as anodes, 
and SPAN electrodes with moderate loadings (2–5 mg cm⁻²) were used as cathodes, with the 
electrolyte volume strictly controlled during assembly. All batteries utilized Celgard 2400 as 
separators. Similarly, Li||Li symmetrical cells (or Li||Cu asymmetrical cells) were assembled by 
replacing the aforementioned SPAN cathode with lithium foils (or copper foils). Pouch-type Li–
SPAN batteries were assembled with a multilayered stacking configuration in a dry room at a dew 
point of −50 °C. The detailed specification parameters of the Li–SPAN pouch type batteries are 
provided in Table S2 and Table S4, respectively. 
6. Electrochemical tests 
Electrochemical performance testing was conducted on a battery testing platform (LAND Battery 
Tester, Wuhan, China). Unless otherwise specified, Li–SPAN batteries would be discharged and 
charged at a current of 3 C (1 C = 550 mA g–1) within a voltage range of 1–3 V. The capacity of Li–
SPAN batteries was calculated based on the mass of the active material (SPAN). Average Coulombic 
efficiency (CE) was obtained by dividing the sum of CE in each cycle by the cycle number. The 
average CE of Li||Cu cells were tested by using two methods. The classical method involved cycling 
Li||Cu cells with a capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 under 1 mA cm−2, recording the CE for each cycle, and 
divided the sum of CE in each cycle by the cycle number. The alternative method employed an 

<<PAGE 5>>

improved Aurbach approach,[1] where the formation capacity (QT) and cycling capacity (QC) were 
set as 5 and 1 mAh cm–2 at a current density of 1 mA cm–2, respectively, and the number of cycles (n) 
was set as 10. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) 
were conducted by using the 1400 Cell Test System from Solartron Analytical (AMETEK, UK). EIS 
data were collected over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 100 mHz with an amplitude of 10 mV. To 
investigate the voltage tolerance of the electrolyte, a membrane soaked with electrolyte was placed 
between lithium metal and stainless steel (SS) and sealed within a battery casing for LSV testing, 
conducted in a voltage range of 0 to 6.0 V at a scan rate of 100 mV s⁻¹. In a glove box, ionic 
conductivity of the electrolyte was measured at room temperature by using the HZPD-T503 
conductivity meter from Huazhi (Fujian, China), with the conductivity value directly obtained from 
the instrument reading. Viscosity of the electrolyte was directly measured by using a DV2T 
viscometer (AMETEK Brookfield) under the same conditions.

7. In situ Raman test 
In situ Raman test was conducted using a custom battery mold. The SPAN-containing slurry, 
prepared according to previous methods, was blade-coated onto carbon cloth and vacuum-dried at 
80°C for 12 hours to obtain the SPAN carbon cloth cathode. This cathode was then punched into 10 
mm discs, and its mass was measured to calculate the active material content. The SPAN carbon cloth 
electrode, followed by a fiberglass separator, was placed on one side of the mold facing the quartz 

<<PAGE 6>>

window. After adding 200 μL of the corresponding electrolyte, Celgard 2400, and a 500 μm lithium 
disc were sequentially positioned. The screws securing the sealing cap were tightened, and the 
assembled mold battery was placed on the testing platform of the Raman instrument, connected via 
wires to the LAND battery testing system. The quartz window was aligned with the magnifier on the 
instrument, and testing was performed using the XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer (Horiba). This 
battery was discharged and charged at a rate of 1 C within a voltage range of 1–3 V. After the Li–
SPAN battery with carbon cloth cathode cycling at 1 C for 500 cycles, the battery was disassembled, 
and the electrode was cleaned to obtain the cycled cathode, which was subsequently tested for in situ 
Raman spectroscopy using the same electrolyte as the coin battery. 
8. In situ UV-vis test 
In situ UV-visible spectrum was obtained by using a quartz cuvette. After the Li–SPAN coin 
battery had run at 1 C for 500 cycles, the battery was disassembled, and the SPAN electrode was 
cleaned to obtain the cycled cathode, which was subsequently tested for in situ UV-vis spectrum using 
the same electrolyte as the coin battery. A cycled cathode and a 500 μm lithium disc were fixed on 
the testing mold, and 3 mL of corresponding electrolyte was added to the quartz battery. The quartz 
battery was placed in the spectrophotometer, and electrodes were connected to the LAND battery 
testing system via wires. The battery underwent continuous discharge and charge cycles at 1 C within 
a voltage range of 1–3 V. 
9. Computational methods 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 package.[2] The 
geometric structure optimizations were carried out at the B3LYP/6-31+G(d,p) level, while the single-
point calculations were performed at the B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level. 
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out by using the GROMACS package with 
the General Amber Force Field (GAFF).[3-4] The electrolyte was modeled with a cubic box which

contained 100 Li+, 100 FSI–, 264 FEC, and 228 DMC, while FDT-0.1T, FDT-2.3T, and FDT-5.22T 
were additionally supplemented with 10, 230, and 522 TTE molecules, respectively. The NPT 
simulations were performed at 1.01325 bar and 298.15 K for 5 ns of equilibration and 20 ns of 
production. All the visualizations of MD simulations were produced by VMD software.[5]

Figure S1. Raman spectra of different electrolytes. Note that here the Raman spectra of FDT-5.22T 
and TTE were copied from the results in Figure 2g. 
Among the solvent molecules, EC exhibits a high dielectric constant, while FEC tends to form a 
stable and durable interfacial layer. To select a more suitable cyclic ester solvent, the two different 
electrolytes were characterized by Raman spectroscopy. In the spectral region of 700 to 780 cm–1, the 
peak corresponding to the S–N–S stretching vibration of FSI⁻ is observed. Compared with EDT-
5.22T, S–N–S(FSI⁻) in FDT-5.22T shifts to the higher wavenumber, indicating that Li+ has stronger 
interaction with salt anions in FDT-5.22T.

Figure S20. In situ Raman spectra of Li–SPAN batteries in FDT-0.1T electrolyte, where the SPAN 
cathode had been cycled for (a) 1 and (b) 500 cycles before in situ tests, at a current density of 1 C. 
The in situ Raman spectra of the SPAN after 1 cycle exhibits evident S–S stretching vibrations, 
which first weaken and then strengthen during the discharge and charge process. This corresponds to 
the breaking of S–S bonds during discharge and their reformation during charge. After 500 cycles, 
the SPAN in FDT-0.1T electrolyte shows only a faint S–S bond signal, indicating that the 
accumulation of byproducts during cycling has significantly hindered the normal electrochemical 
reactions of SPAN.
