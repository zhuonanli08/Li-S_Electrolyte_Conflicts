Unraveling the Dual Functionality of High-Donor-Number 
Anion in Lean-Electrolyte Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Hyunwon Chu, Jinkwan Jung, Hyungjun Noh, Seongmin Yuk, Jinhong Lee, Ju-Hyuk Lee, 
Jaewon Baek, Youngil Roh, Hyeokjin Kwon, DongWoong Choi, Kwonnam Sohn, 
YunKyoung Kim, and Hee-Tak Kim*
DOI: 10.1002/aenm.202000493
Despite the theoretically high energy den-
sity, the practical energy density of Li–S 
batteries at the moment does not meet 
the demand due to low sulfur (S) loading 
(<2  mg cm−2), large electrolyte amount 
(electrolyte/sulfur ratio >20 µL mg−1), and 
excess lithium (Li) metal use (>10 times 
excess).[5]
In particular, large electrolyte usage 
(flooding) greatly diminishes the practical 
energy density of Li–S batteries. Due to 
the intrinsic solution-based redox chem-
istry, however, many of the challenges 
arise from minimizing the electrolyte/
sulfur ratio (E/S ratio). Since soluble 
lithium polysulfide (LiPS, Li2Sx when 
2 < x  ≤ 8) intermediates are self-redox 
mediating, the decrease in the LiPS dis-
solution causes a sluggish sulfur conver-
sion and high polarization.[6] Next, the 
morphology of lithium sulfide (Li2S) elec-
trodeposition and the kinetics of the re-oxidation are affected 
by the sulfur species solubility as well.[7] Hence, uncontrolled 
precipitation and continual accumulation of Li2S limit the 
discharge capacity and further passivate the cathode interface 
throughout the cycling.[8] Reducing the electrolyte volume exac-
erbates not only the cathode performance but also the anode 
stability. A high reactivity and an infinite volume change of the 
Li metal anode cause the incessant decomposition of the elec-
trolyte. Therefore, the lean electrolyte condition accelerates the 
increase of the cell resistance and provokes earlier performance 
failure compared to the flooding electrolyte system.[9]
Manipulating electrolyte materials (solvents, salt anions, 
and additives) has a considerable impact on the electrochem-
ical performance of Li–S batteries. There have been studies in 
which solvents with high Gutmann donor numbers (DNs) form 
strong interactions with lithium ions (Li+) and promote the sol-
vation of polysulfide (PS) anions. The increased LiPS solubility 
facilitates the solution-mediated reaction pathway, enabling fast 
reaction kinetics and high sulfur utilization.[10] Furthermore, 
the same merits can also be achieved with salt anions having 
high-DNs [11] or additives promoting ionic solvation.[12] Under a 
lean electrolyte regime, the role of highly solvating electrolytes 
becomes more prominent because of the limited solubility of 
sulfur species. For example, high-DN solvents can enhance the 
sulfur utilization under the reduced electrolyte amount by pro-
moting the charge/discharge reactions.[13] Despite this fact, the 
Minimizing electrolyte use is essential to achieve high practical energy den-
sity of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. However, the sulfur cathode is more 
readily passivated under a lean electrolyte condition, resulting in low sulfur 
utilization. In addition, continuous electrolyte decomposition on the Li metal 
anode aggravates the problem, provoking rapid capacity decay. In this work, 
the dual functionalities of NO3− as a high-donor-number (DN) salt anion 
is presented, which improves the sulfur utilization and cycling stability of 
lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries. The NO3− anion elevates the solubility of the 
sulfur species based on its high electron donating ability, achieving a high 
sulfur utilization of above 1200 mA h g−1. Furthermore, the anion suppresses 
electrolyte decomposition on the Li metal by regulating the lithium ion (Li+) 
solvation sheath, enhancing the cycle performance of the lean electrolyte cell. 
By understanding the anionic effects, this work demonstrates the potential of 
the high-DN electrolyte, which is beneficial for both the cathode and anode of 
Li–S batteries.

1. Introduction
With a high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), lithium–
sulfur (Li–S) batteries have garnered great attention as promi­
sing alternatives to lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries.[1] Encouraging 
advances have been made in the Li–S chemistry based on the 
development of the cathode,[2] separator,[3] and electrolyte.[4]

solvents still face challenges due to the poor Li metal stability 
and aggravate the cycling performance of the lean-electrolyte 
cells. Most recently, high-DN anions have been proven to be 
superior to high-DN solvents in terms of the anode stability.[11a,b] 
Yet, the promising features of high-DN anions have not been 
assessed in a lean electrolyte environment.
Herein, we propose the dual role of lithium nitrate (LiNO3) 
supporting salt in a low electrolyte system. By investigating the 
electrochemical characteristics of lithium bistriflimide:lithium 
nitrate (LiTFSI:LiNO3) co-salt electrolytes, we suggest that 
the use of high-DN nitrate (NO3−) anion is highly effective in 
enhancing the solubility of the sulfur species at a low E/S ratio. 
Based on the PS solvating power, the electrolytes with high 
LiNO3 concentrations delay the cathode passivation, achieving 
high sulfur utilization above 1200  mA h g−1. In addition, by 
changing the Li+ solvation structure, we find that the high-
DN NO3− anion can suppress the ether solvent decomposition, 
improving the cell cycling performance up to 100 cycles. The 
dual beneficial functions of NO3− anion for both electrodes 
enable the design of a lean-electrolyte Li–S battery achieving 
high specific capacities and long cycle life.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Polysulfide Solubility and Discharge Behaviors  
with Nitrate Anion
To examine the effects of NO3− anion in a lean electrolyte regime, 
LiTFSI:LiNO3 co-salt electrolytes were prepared with varying 
ratios of LiTFSI and LiNO3 (95:5, 80:20, 60:40, and 40:60). The 
total Li+ concentrations were fixed at 1 m in 1,3-dioxolane:1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DOL:DME) (1:1) solvents. The salt concen-
trations for the electrolytes are provided using the notation 
x:y. For example, the electrolyte labelled “95:5” contains 0.95 
m LiTFSI and 0.05 m LiNO3. LiNO3 in Li–S battery electrolytes 
was proven effective to suppress the PS redox shuttle and Li 
dendrite growth;[14] however, the highly solvating role and its 
corresponding effect of the anion have never been assessed in 
the lean electrolyte condition. Since the DN of NO3− (22.2 kcal 
mol−1) is higher than that of the solvents (DME = 20 kcal mol−1 
and DOL = 18 kcal mol−1),[15] the NO3− anions can have a more 
influential role on the solvation environment of Li+. 7Li nuclear 
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, which detects the 
relative environment around Li+, was used to investigate the 
anionic effect on ion solvation.[16] As provided in Figure S1 (Sup-
porting Information), the NMR signal shifted toward the down-
field as the LiNO3 concentration increased. This represents 
that increasing the concentration of LiNO3 raised the number 
of NO3− interacting with Li+ and reduced the ionic dissociation 
amount in the supporting electrolyte.
The donicity of the supporting electrolyte affects the solu-
bility of the PS anions due to common ion equilibrium.[11a,17] 
The ionic dissociation equilibria of Li supporting salts and LiPS 
are interrelated by sharing a common ion species (Li+). Thus, 
we can easily promote the LiPS solubility by increasing the con-
centration of high electron donating/less ionically dissociating 
NO3− anion. The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorbance peaks 
in Figure 1a show that the solubility of all PS anions increased 
with the addition of LiNO3. The absorbance peaks of the S82− 
(560 nm), S62− (470 nm), and S42− (420 nm) anions[10a] surged in 
the order of the 95:5 < 80:20 < 60:40 < 40:60 solutions. In addi-
tion, the concentration of the S3•− (617 nm) radical[10a,18] was ele-
vated with the higher LiNO3 ratio concurrently, demonstrating 
the role of LiNO3 on fostering the LiPS solvation. In accord-
ance with the UV–vis intensity changes, blue coloration of the 
diluted LiPS solutions was observed with the higher LiNO3 
ratios (In set of Figure 1a and Figure S2 (Supporting Informa-
tion)). The blue color, indicative of the S3•− radical species, fur-
ther supported the PS stabilizing power of the high LiNO3 pos-
sessing electrolytes.
The PS solubility differences within the electrolytes consid-
erably affect the electrochemical performances of sulfur redox 
reactions. To investigate the effect of NO3− anion, Li–S cells 
with the four different LiTFSI:LiNO3 ratios were prepared. A 
LiPS-loaded carbon nanofiber (CNF) electrode (CNF/PS) was 
used as the cathode for a clear detection of the electrochem-
ical responses[19] (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The 
E/S ratio of each Li–S cell was 5 µL mg−1, which is generally 
considered as the lean electrolyte condition. Also, the electro-
chemical stability of our co-salt electrolytes was checked before-
hand within the operation voltage range (Figure S4, Supporting 
Information). Based on the PS stabilizing power of the NO3− 
anion, the voltage profiles of the four electrolytes (Figure  1b) 
exhibited two major variations. First, the discharge capacity was 
notably enhanced from 648.8 to 1237 mA h g−1 with increasing 
the LiNO3 ratio from 5 to 60. A detailed explanation about 
the capacity extension will be provided in the latter section. 
Second, the operating voltage of the upper discharge plateau 
was increased correspondingly with the higher LiNO3 content. 
We expect that the voltage differences were originated from the 
chemical activity changes of the PS anions in the electrolytes.
Previously, Yu and co-workers reported that high-DN solvents 
such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone 
(NMP) stabilized the soluble PS species and reduced the chemical 
activity of the PS anions. Then, the chemical activity decrease led 
to the increase in the reaction voltage of the upper discharge pla-
teau based on the Nernst equation.[20] More precisely, we meas-
ured the equilibrium voltage of the upper plateau with galvano-
static intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements.[21] 
For comparison, constant current (CC) measurements at 0.1 C 
were conducted for the same cells. As shown in Figure 1c, the 
reaction overpotentials of the four electrolyte cells did not show 
significant differences. However, the average open circuit volt-
ages (OCVs), determined by connecting the quasi-equilibrium 
potentials, were elevated from 2.349 to 2.374 V with the increase 
of the NO3− content. Similar with the high-DN solvent cases, the 
OCV rise from the addition of LiNO3 substantiates that the sol-
uble PS anions are effectively stabilized with the presence of the 
high-DN NO3− anion. Interestingly, the PS activity change influ-
enced the discharge capacity of the lean electrolyte cell as well. 
For the lowest LiNO3 content of 5%, the upper plateau capacity 
was abnormally reduced compared to the other electrolyte cells; 
the poor PS solubilizing power of the electrolyte hindered the 
solution-based PS reduction and aggravated the capacity. The 
spectroscopy and electrochemical measurements collectively 
indicate that LiNO3 increased the solvation of LiPS and facilitated 
the conversion of the soluble sulfur species in the electrolyte.

2.2. Lithium Sulfide Electrodeposition and Electrode Passivation
The cathode passivation by the insulating Li2S deters the com-
plete conversion of the short-chain polysulfides (Sx2−, 2 ≤ x ≤ 4) 
during the lower discharge plateau. In this respect, the actual 
upper to lower capacity ratios had not reached the 1:3 theoret-
ical value in previous works. The capacity deterioration became 
more significant at higher sulfur loadings and lower electrolyte 
usages due to the limited solubility.[8] Here, however, we found 
that LiNO3 was helpful to mitigate this problem and realize 
a high discharge capacity by controlling the Li2S growth. The 
lower plateau capacities were notably extended with increasing 
the NO3− concentrations (Figure  1b), then the upper to lower 
capacity ratios were enhanced from 1:0.94 (95:5) to 1:2.53 
(40:60) (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Based on that, the 
cell with the 40:60 electrolyte achieved 1237 mA h g−1 of initial 
discharge capacity even at the low E/S ratio. Our group previ-
ously reported that high-DN anions such as triflate (Tf−) and 
bromide (Br−) delayed the electrode passivation by inducing 3D 
Li2S growth.[11a] In a similar way, the enhanced capacity ratios 
close to the theoretical value suggest the substantial role of the 
NO3− anion in manipulating the Li2S deposition.
To investigate the growth behavior of Li2S varied by the NO3− 
concentration, the potentiostatic electrodeposition analysis was 
conducted using the Chronoamperometry (CA) technique. After 
a pre-conditioning step to eliminate long-chain PSs (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information), the current responses were recorded 
by applying a step potential of 2.05 V (Figure 2a–d). The current 
signals from Li2S electrodeposition typically show a peaking 
behavior. The current initially drops with time due to the fara-
daic reaction of the residual long-chain PSs; thereafter, it begins 
to increase as the nucleation and growth of Li2S are initiated. 
The multiplication of Li2S nuclei expands the active electrode 
surfaces, causing a current rise while the nuclei further grow. In 
the succeeding part, however, the value subsequently declines as 
the neighboring nuclei or the diffusional hemispheres overlap 
with each other.[22] In this regime, the specific time (tm) of the 
maximum current (Im) directs the moment when the neigh-
boring Li2S nuclei or their diffusion zones are encountered. 
Thus, by comparing tm values, we can estimate the relative elec-
trode passivation speed from the Li2S electrodeposition. Among 
our cells with the different LiTFSI:LiNO3 ratios, the 40:60 one 
exhibited the longest duration until tm (249.5 s); thus, it had the 
slowest electrode passivation rate compared to the other sample 
cells (95:5: 138.2 s, 80:20: 196.1 s, and 60:40: 200.3 s). Corre-
sponding with the galvanostatic discharge results, we observed 
that the cathode passivation by Li2S deposition was increasingly 
suppressed with the higher LiNO3 content.
The surface passivation of the sulfur cathode is closely 
related to the electrodeposition mode and morphology of Li2S. 
Electrolytes and additives that can control Li2S electrodeposi-
tion have been widely reported. Recently, Li and co-workers 
suggested that high-DN solvents such as N,N-dimethylforma-
mide (DMF) and propionitrile (PrN) could enhance the Li2S 
solubility in electrolytes. The solvents with high Li2S solubility 
induced 3D particulate deposition of Li2S and delayed elec-
trode passivation during discharge.[10c] Likewise, the capacity 
extension with our high-DN electrolytes was originated from

2.3. Electrolyte Compatibility with Li Metal Electrodes
Li metal anodes exert a strong influence on the service life of 
Li–S batteries; however, their impact has been often neglected 
in cells with excess amounts of electrolyte. When using a low 
electrolyte amount to evaluate Li–S batteries, the electrolyte 
compatibility with the metal anode becomes more significant. 
As an example, a bromide anion (Br−) has an extremely high 
donicity (DN = 33.7  kcal mol−1),[15a] realizing enhanced sulfur 
utilization under a lean electrolyte regime. Nevertheless, the 
cycle performance of a LiBr electrolyte showed an abrupt 
degradation due to the lack of Li metal stability (Figure S11, 
Supporting Information). To prevent the continuous and irre-
versible consumption of the electrolyte and Li metal, building 
up a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) is essential. Since 
the ingredients of the SEI are derived from reduction products 
of electrolyte materials,[24] rational selection of the anion will 
enable us to enhance the stability of the anode and the elec-
trolyte. In this sense, NO3− anion has been proven effective in 
increasing the interfacial stability of Li metal electrodes.[4b,25] 
Thus, the use of LiNO3 can target the dual functions of the 
anion: stabilizing the Li metal electrode as well as increasing 
the sulfur utilization.
To assess the Li metal stability with the LiTFSI:LiNO3 elec-
trolytes, Li–Cu half-cell tests were conducted with the different 
anion ratios. As displayed in Figure 3a, the initial reversibility of 
the Li plating and stripping was greatly enhanced by using the 
higher LiNO3 content. The high initial coulombic efficiencies 
(CEs) of the 60:40 (93.1%) and 40:60 electrolytes (94.3%) sub-
stantiated that the electrolyte degradation was suppressed at the 
higher LiNO3 concentrations. In addition, the lifespan of the 
cell was prolonged in the order of 95:5 < 80:20 < 60:40 < 40:60 
(Figure  3b; Figure S12, Supporting Information). The cell

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of the lean-
electrolyte Li–Li cells provides the detailed information on the 
interfacial evolution under the low electrolyte regime. A Li 
metal electrode commonly shows a high interfacial resistance 
before cycling, and the resistance diminishes after few cycles 
due to the enlargement of the electrode surface area from the 
dendritic Li formation. Subsequently, the cell exhibits a gradual 
increase in impedance, which can be attributed to the SEI accu-
mulation from the continuous electrolyte decomposition.[26] 
The impedance changes of the lean electrolyte Li–Li cells 
were measured using the four co-salt electrolytes (Figure S13, 
Supporting Information), and each impedance component was 
separately extracted using the equivalent circuit model. As shown 
in Figure  3c and its corresponding model plot in Figure S14 
(Supporting Information), the metal interfaces with all electro-
lytes exhibited high interfacial resistances within the first 20 cycles 
then the resistances decreased by the surface expansion. How-
ever, the 95:5 sample showed an abrupt increase in interfacial 
impedances due to the severe SEI accumulation only within 
150 cycles (Figure S14, Supporting Information). Along with 
the changes in interface resistances, ohmic resistances were 
drastically surged simultaneously due to the rapid depletion of 
the electrolyte. The radical fluctuation in the 95:5 sample origi-
nated from the untamed growth of Li dendrites and the succes-
sive degradation of the electrolyte. As a result, the low stability 
of the 95:5 electrolyte provoked the poor cycling ability of the Li 
metal battery (Figure 3a,b). In direct comparison, the cell with 
the higher LiNO3 content maintained low resistance values for 
prolonged cycles. The impedance semi-circles (from the SEI 
film and charge transfer resistances) of the 40:60 electrolyte 
stayed stable for 300 cycles because of the lowered electrolyte 
decomposition. Moreover, the ohmic resistance changes of the 
60:40 and 40:60 electrolytes were less significant compared to 
those of the 95:5 and 80:20 electrolytes, proving the greater 
compatibility even with the extremely low electrolyte amount.
2.4. The Anionic Effect on the Lithium Ion Solvation  
and SEI Composition
LiNO3 has been widely applied as an effective additive or a 
supporting salt for Li metal protection; however, the previous 
explanations for the stability mainly focused on the properties 
of the SEI and corresponding Li plating morphology. Aside from 
the result-oriented discussion, a mechanistic understanding 
of the anion’s effect when forming the SEI is required. In an 
electrolyte, Li+ ions are solvated by solvents and anions as devel-
oping solvation shell structures.[27] Since Li metal deposition 
always entails the reduction of the chemical species around Li+, 
the SEI components are majorly established with the decompo-
sition products from the Li+ solvation shell participants. Based 
on this knowledge, Zhang and co-workers suggested that the 
introduction of fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and LiNO3 
in an ether/carbonate binary electrolyte altered the solvation 
structure of Li+, and subsequently formulated a fluorine(F) and 
nitrogen(N)-rich stable SEI layer on the Li metal surfaces.[28] 
As such, the Li+ solvation shell constituents should be meticu-
lously controlled to construct a stable SEI and to minimize the 
electrolyte degradation.
NO3− anion has a higher electron donicity than DME 
solvent;[15a,b] thus, the interaction between Li+ and NO3− is pre-
dominant compared to that of Li+ and DME. The donicity dif-
ference of the two elements enables the small addition of LiNO3 
to change the chemical species dominancy in the Li+ solvation 
shell (Figure 4a,b). The Li+ solvation environments in different 
electrolytes were monitored by Raman spectroscopy (Figure 4c). 
The four electrolytes commonly showed two distinctive peaks 
at 850 and 820 cm−1 that are ascribed to the uncoordinated 
ethylene oxide bonds of the free DME molecules.[29] When 
adding up the NO3− concentration, the free DME peaks were 
increased because the anions pushed the solvent molecules out 
from the Li+ solvation. At the same time, the peak intensities of 
the Li+ coordinated solvent species (nearby 870 cm−1)[29a] were 
decreased accordingly. It proves that the addition of LiNO3 ele-
vated the participation of NO3− and abated the coordination of 
DME in Li+ solvation based on the donicity differences.
Molecular radial distribution functions (RDFs) around 
Li+ further support the solvation structure change by LiNO3. 
Center-of-mass molecular RDFs were generated from 1.0  ns 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in NVT ensemble 
(Figure S15, Supporting Information). In Figure  4d, the peak 
intensity of Li+−NO3− interaction at 1.8 Å increased with the 
higher NO3− content, whereas that of Li+−DME coordination 
(2 Å) decreased correspondingly. To provide a more intuitive 
grasp, the coordination numbers (CNs) of each chemical spe-
cies surrounding Li+ were calculated for the electrolyte samples. 
Since the oxygen atoms of NO3− and DME are the most prob-
able coordinating elements with Li+,[30] the atomistic RDFs of 
Li-O (NO3−) and Li-O (DME) were used for the CN calculation. 
A radius of 3 Å was selected for the first Li+ solvation shell by 
considering the atomistic RDF peak displacements. As shown 
in Figure S16 (Supporting Information), adding NO3− to the 
electrolyte dramatically surged the CN of the anion and reduced 
the coordination of DME. Even though DME was in large 
excess than NO3− in the bulk electrolyte, the CN of NO3− over-
took that of DME at the 60:40 ratio and switched the chemical 
species dominancy in the Li+ solvation. Based on experimental 
and computational simulation results, we demonstrated that 
the introduction of NO3− regulated the interaction between Li+ 
and DME then created the NO3− dominant solvation sheath in 
the electrolyte.
Based on the change of the Li+ solvation constituents, the 
ingredients of the SEI were markedly differentiated within the 
samples. To characterize the SEI composition, X-ray photo-
electron spectroscopy (XPS) of the Li metal surfaces was con-
ducted after 10 cycles of galvanostatic plating/stripping. In the 
O 1s spectra in Figure 4e, the COC bond peaks, which are 
related to the DME decomposition,[31] decreased evidently when 
increasing the LiNO3 ratio. Since the solvent coordination on 
Li+ solvation reduced with the NO3− addition, the direct contact 
of DME with Li metal was repressed, then its reductive decom-
position was effectively suppressed throughout cycles. Instead,

3. Conclusion
In summary, we investigated the bi-functional role of LiNO3 
in the lean electrolyte condition. Based on its high electron 
donicity, the addition of NO3− anion enhanced the solubility of 
LiPS and Li2S, which facilitated solution-mediated sulfur redox 
reactions and Li2S deposition. The improved reaction kinetics 
and duration enabled the high utilization and the reversible 
operations of the sulfur cathode. In addition, Li metal stability 
of the lean electrolyte Li–S cell was significantly increased with 
the NO3− anion. A strong interaction between Li+ and NO3− 
engendered the NO3− dominant solvation structure in the high 
LiNO3 possessing electrolytes, suppressing the solvent decom-
position and formulating the stable SEI on the metal interfaces. 
Based on the dual beneficial role of LiNO3 on both electrodes, 
enhanced cycling performances were achieved for high sulfur 
loading/low electrolyte amount Li–S cells. Our work on elec-
trolyte design can provide a platform to create synergistic 
effect with advanced cathodes and functional separators for 
improving the performances further. Although there still exist 
challenges to achieve the practical level of energy density and 
cycle life of Li–S batteries, we believe that our thorough inves-
tigation on the role of electrolyte materials, which considered 
both on the cathode and anode, can offer guidance for over-
coming the limitations of the current Li–S systems.
4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Characterization: Electrolytes with varying ratios of lithium 
bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 3M) and lithium nitrate 
(LiNO3, Sigma-Aldrich) salts were prepared, totaling a Li+ concentration 
of 1 m in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL):1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (Sigma-
Aldrich) (1:1 in volume). 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 
spectroscopy measurements were conducted on an Agilent 400  MHz 
54  mm NMR DD2 (Agilent Technology). A reference solution was 
prepared as 1 m lithium chloride (LiCl) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 
(DMSO-d6, Sigma Aldrich), and 700  µL of the reference solution was 
placed in a precision NMR sample tube (DURAN). For the electrolyte 
samples, 650  µL of the LiTFSI:LiNO3 co-salt electrolytes were mixed 
with 50  µL of DMSO-d6 to supply the deuterium absorption source 
and detect the lock frequency. UV–vis absorption spectroscopy was 
performed using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (GENESYS 10S, Thermo 
Scientific). First, 1 × 10−3 m of lithium polysulfide (Li2S8) catholytes were 
prepared by mixing and diluting 0.2 m Li2S8 in DOL:DME solution with 
the LiTFSI:LiNO3 co-salt electrolytes. Each catholyte sample was placed 
in a 10 mm high precision cell (Hellma Analytics), and the absorption 
signals were measured within the wavelength range of 200 to 1100 nm.
Cathode Preparation: Two types of carbon electrodes were used 
for different purposes. A functionalized carbon nanofiber (CNF) 
electrode was used to allow clear observation of the electrochemical 
characteristics of the electrolyte solutions and the deposition 
morphology of lithium sulfide (Li2S). The nanofiber membrane was 
prepared with an electrospinning procedure. First, 1 g of polyacrylonitrile 
(PAN, Mw  = 150 000, Sigma-Aldrich) was dispersed in 10  mL of N,N-
dimethylformamide (DMF, Sigma-Aldrich) and stirred for 24 h. The 
viscous mixture was transferred to an electrospinning syringe (21 gauge) 
and electro-spun on an aluminum (Al) foil collector by applying a 
constant potential of 17 kV. The feeding rate was 0.5 mL h−1, and the Al 
foil collector was positioned 15 cm away from the syringe needle. The 
PAN nanofiber membranes were detached from the Al foil and dried 
overnight at 60 °C, and then the membranes were cut into Ø 18  mm 
sized disks. The polymer membranes were stabilized in air at 220°C for 
3 h; subsequently, the membranes were carbonized at 800 °C for 1 h 
under a N2 flow condition (300 square cc min−1). A freestanding carbon 
nanotube (CNT) electrode was used for the cycling tests due to its high 
mechanical strength and lightweight. The CNT electrode was fabricated 
by mixing 25  mg of multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT, Carbon 
Nano-Materials Tech. Co. Ltd.) with 15  mg of polyvinylpyrrolidone 
(PVP-40, Sigma Aldrich) as a surfactant in 50  mL of ethanol (Merck), 
and the solution was tip-sonicated for 30  min. The solid contents of 
the dispersed solution were collected using a nylon membrane filter 
(Whatman, pore size: 0.2  µm) with a vacuum-filtration method, and 
dried at 60  °C for 12 h. The specific surface areas of the two carbon 
electrodes were measured with a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) 
analyzer (3Flex, Micromeritics). The morphology and the elemental 
distribution of the electrodes were observed using a scanning electron 
microscope-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (SEM-EDS, Magellan 
400, FEI Co.). The active mass was loaded onto either of the electrodes 
by drop-casting a 0.2 m Li2S8 in DME solution. The solution was 
prepared by heating and stirring stoichiometric amounts of lithium 
sulfide (Li2S, Sigma-Aldrich) and octa-sulfur (S8, Sigma-Aldrich) in 
DME at 60  °C. The prepared solution was stirred until a finely mixed 
red-colored solution was obtained. A volume of 60 µL (sulfur loading: 
3 mg cm−2) or 90 µL (4.5 mg cm−2) was dropped directly onto a disk 
(Ø 12 mm) of the electrode at 100°C, and the electrode was heated for 
additional 10 min, allowing the DME solvent to completely evaporate.
Electrochemical Measurements: Electrochemical properties of the 
electrolytes were evaluated in 2032-type coin cells. Cyclic voltammetry 
studies were conducted to confirm the electrochemical stability of co-salt 
elecetrolytes. Coin cells with CNF electrodes and Li metal electrodes 
were assembled with four electrolytes without loading sulfur, and 
cycled with at scan rates from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1. For the galvanostatic 
intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements, coin cells with 
LiPS-loaded CNF electrodes and different electrolytes were prepared. A 
CNF/PS electrode (Ø 12 mm) was inserted as a working electrode. A Li 
foil (Ø 16.5 mm, 450 µm thickness, Honjo Metal) was used as a counter 
electrode, and a Celgard 2400 monolayer polypropylene (PP) membrane 
(Ø 18 mm) was used as a separator. The GITT experiments consist of a 
series of pulse currents at 0.1 C for 0.1 h, each followed by a 0.4 h open-
circuit period. The open-circuit relaxation time should be long enough 
that the voltage fluctuation is within a 0.01  V range. The open circuit 
voltage (OCV) curves of the Li–S cells were constructed by connecting 
the voltage points after each relaxation period. GITT measurement data 
were superimposed with the result of constant current measurements 
at 0.1 C. All galvanostatic experiments were done with a WBCS300L 
(Wonatech) automatic battery cycler. To examine the rate performances 
of the electrolytes in the lithium–sulfur (Li–S) cells, a LiPS-loaded 
CNF electrode (Ø 12 mm) was inserted as a working electrode. Li foil 
(Ø 16.5  mm, 450  µm thickness, Honjo Metal) was used as a counter 
electrode, and a Celgard 2400 monolayer polypropylene (PP) membrane 
(Ø 18  mm) was used as a separator. The electrolyte to sulfur ratio 
was fixed at 5 µL mg−1 sulfur for each cell. The cells were tested within 
a voltage range of 1.95–2.7 V by varying the current density from 0.1 C 
to 0.5 C. The cycling performances of Li–S cells were conducted using 
the same cell configuration and operation condition at 0.1 C except 
changing the cathode to a LiPS-loaded CNT electrode (Ø 12  mm). A 
galvanostatic Li plating/stripping test was conducted using a lithium–
copper (Li–Cu) cell configuration. A commercial Cu current collector

Li2S Deposition: The 2032-type coin cells were assembled using the 
CNF/PS electrodes. The measured sulfur loading was set at 2 mg cm−2, 
and the electrolyte to sulfur ratio was fixed at 10 µL mg−1 sulfur for each 
cell. To monitor the deposition morphology of Li2S, the cells were 
discharged at 0.05 C and stopped at the full-discharge state before dis-
assembly. The disassembled electrodes were neatly washed with 1 mL 
of DME to eliminate any residual soluble products. Scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) studies of pristine and discharged CNF cathodes 
were performed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope 
(FE-SEM, Sirion, FEI). The magnifications are indicated on the scale 
bars shown in the figures and figure captions. Cyclic voltammetry 
studies were conducted at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 to determine the 
step voltages for the chronoamperometry (CA) analysis. During the CA 
measurements, cells were initially held at 2.15  V for 6 h, or until the 
current fell below 0.01  mA. This pre-discharge step was to reduce the 
long-chain polysulfide anions and to collect the current signals only from 
the Li2S deposition reactions. After that, a static voltage of 2.05 V was 
applied for 3 h to proceed the Li2S electrodeposition and the current 
behaviors were examined using a VSP potentiostat system (Bio-logic).
Li+ Solvation and SEI Components: Molecular dynamics (MD) 
calculations were conducted using a Material Studio (BIOVIA, 2018) 
software package. Geometries of the DOL, DME, Li+, TFSI−, and NO3− 
species were optimized using the forcite module. A condensed-phase 
optimized molecular potentials for atomistic simulation studies II 
(COMPASS II) forcefield was assigned for all the MD calculations. The 
solvation sheath components of the co-salt electrolytes were estimated 
by constructing amorphous cells with 714 DOL, 482 DME, 100 Li+, and x:y 
ratios of TFSI− and NO3− (x:y = 95:5, 80:20, 60:40, and 40:60), the ratio of 
which corresponds to the actual co-salt electrolyte compositions totaling 
1 m Li salt in DOL:DME (1:1). The systems were in cubic boxes with same 
dimensions of 59 Å in length, width, and height. All the systems were 
initially stabilized for 50 000 steps using the smart algorithm employing 
an energy convergence tolerance of 0.001  kcal mol−1 Å−1. Following 
the energy minimization, the electrolyte systems were equilibrated for 
1  ns in the MD simulations using a time step of 1 fs in the isobaric-
isothermal (NPT) ensemble. The Berendsen barostat was used with 
maintaining a pressure of 1 atm with a decay constant of 0.1  ps. The 
NHL thermostat was applied to the 298 K isothermal calculation using 
a decay constant of 1 ps. The electrostatic interactions were accounted 
for using the particle-particle particle-mesh (PPPM) method, which is 
known to be adequate for ionic system calculations. Subsequently, the 
systems were run for 1 ns in a canonical NVT ensemble at 298 K. The 
radial distribution functions (RDFs) were calculated based on the last 
stabilized system. Raman spectra of the electrolytes were collected with 
a dispersive raman spectrometer (ARAMIS, JY Horiba) using 514  nm 
wavelength laser. First, 1  mL of each co-salt electrolyte was separately 
placed into a glass capillary tube prior to the measurement. The spectra 
obtained were normalized by CH2 bending/scissoring mode peak, which 
has the highest intensity between the 1400 and 1550 cm−1 wavelength 
range. Surface characterization of the cycled Li metals was committed 
by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-alpha, Thermo VG 
Scientific) with Al Kα as the X-ray source. All binding energy values were 
calibrated using the hydrocarbon C 1s peak at 284.7 eV as a standard to 
eliminate peak shifts.
