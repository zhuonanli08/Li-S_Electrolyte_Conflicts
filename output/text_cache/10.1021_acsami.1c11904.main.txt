ABSTRACT: Based on dissolution/deposition chemistry, together with multielectron
redox reactions, lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been demonstrated as a
promising energy storage system. However, the diﬀusion of soluble lithium polysulﬁde
intermediates (LiPSs) to bulk electrolyte results in the fast capacity fade of a Li−S cell.
How to conﬁne the LiPSs within the cathode while retaining high reversible capacity
remains a huge challenge. In this work, N-bromophthalimide, an organic molecule with
an aromatic heterocyclic ring and a reactive halogen bond, is introduced as an
electrolyte additive to conquer the excessive dissolution and diﬀusion of LiPSs by in
situ formation of an organopolysulﬁde deposition layer. This electrochemically active
layer not only maintains the internal sulfur conversion but also prevents LiPSs from
diﬀusing into the electrolyte bulk, thereby improving the cycling and rate performance
of Li−S batteries. This study provides a feasible strategy for regulating the reaction region and path for high-performance Li−S
batteries.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur battery, electrolyte additive, grafting reaction, organopolysulﬁde deposition, cycling stability
1. INTRODUCTION
The rapidly growing energy requirements from electric devices
and portable devices urgently need high-energy-density energy
storage systems.1,2 As the most promising alternative for the
next-generation secondary batteries, lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries feature a multielectron reaction process based on the
overall reaction S8 + 16Li ↔8Li2S.3,4 The theoretical energy
density of Li−S batteries can reach 2600 Wh kg−1,5,6 which is
about 5-fold higher than that of Li-ion batteries.7 In addition to
large energy density, other merits including the environmental
friendliness, natural abundance, and low cost of the electrode
materials stimulate the development of the Li−S system.8,9
However, before the commercialization of Li−S batteries,
several obstacles need to be conquered. These obstacles
include the electronically insulating properties of the elemental
sulfur and discharge product of Li2S, the large volume change
of the cathode, and the formation of lithium dendrites on the
anode during cell cycling.10 The sulfur chemistry in Li−S
batteries involves the solution-mediated conversion reaction of
soluble LiPSs (Li2Sn, 3 ≤n ≤8),11 which is fundamentally
diﬀerent from the Li-ion (Li+) insertion/extraction chemistry
that utilizes Li+ intercalation materials.12,13 Since the intrinsic
solubility of LiPSs can make space for the reduction of the
inner parts of sulfur,14 the moderate dissolution of LiPSs is
beneﬁcial for the electrode kinetics and high-loading sulfur
cathodes.15,16 However, the excessive dissolution of LiPSs
additionally brings the diﬀusion and migration issues, causing
capacity fade of a sulfur cathode, the side reaction of the Li
anode, and the viscosity increase of the electrolyte.17
To inhibit the diﬀusion and migration of LiPSs, many host
materials have been investigated to anchor the dissolved LiPSs
on the cathode side.18 Carbon materials as the most important
sulfur hosts can conﬁne LiPSs by physical blocking or weak
chemisorption.19 The heteroatom doping or functional group
modiﬁcation strategies have been proved to increase the
polarity of carbon materials, thus improving the chemisorption
of LiPSs.20,21 Nevertheless, long cycling stability of sulfur/
carbon composite cathodes is still insuﬃcient owing to the
limited polarity and open system of carbon-based hosts.22 In
addition to functionalized carbon materials, organic com-
pounds as linker comonomers can covalently graft sulfur or
polysulﬁdes on the functional groups to obtain organo-
sulfur.23−27 More importantly, the properties of the organo-
sulfur composite can be tuned by altering diﬀerent organic
functional groups.28 Nevertheless, the reversible capacity and
cycling stability of organosulfur are deﬁcient due to the
excessive introduction of insulating organic compounds.29
Because of the intrinsic solubility of LiPSs, the optimization
of electrolyte components is also a feasible strategy to suppress
the excessive diﬀusion of LiPSs and the fast capacity
decay.15,30−32 The solubility of LiPSs can be decreased to a

good compatibility with the Li anode, and moderate ion
conductivity.34 Recently, organic electrolyte additives have
been proposed to suppress the migration and shuttle of LiPSs
through a covalent grafting method. Methyl triﬂuoroacetate
(CH3TFA), as an electrolyte additive, can in situ react with
LiPSs to form lithium triﬂuoroacetate and dimethyl poly-

utilization are promoted even under lean electrolyte con-

binder of LiPSs by reversible chemical coordination for cycling
stability.36 Other organic compounds, such as 2,5-dichloro-1,4-
benzoquinone37 and diphenyl diselenide,38 were also con-
ﬁrmed to be capable of regulating the conversion of sulfur
species. Thus, organic additives show potential to improve the
redox process of sulfur cathodes.39
In our previous report, the functionally organic molecule of
N-bromophthalimide (NBP) was used to modify the surface of
nonpolar graphene through a π−π stacking interaction.40 The
NBP that is preanchored on the graphene surface can be
covalently bound to LiPSs, thus suppressing the shuttle and
facilitating the charge-transfer process. In this work, based on
the grafting function of NBP toward LiPSs, we utilize NBP as
an electrolyte additive to react with LiPSs and form an
organopolysulﬁde protective layer. The formation process and
role of this protective layer are illustrated in Scheme 1.
Speciﬁcally, NBP molecules spontaneously react with LiPSs to
form organopolysulﬁdes, deposited on the sulfur cathode
surface. The formed surface deposition layer can suppress the
diﬀusion of LiPSs to electrolyte bulk and promote the internal
polysulﬁde conversion, thereby improving the cycling stability
of the cathode. Moreover, the organopolysulﬁdes are electro-
chemically active, which avoids the compromise in the
reversible capacity of the cathode. This design extends the
application of functionally organic molecules in Li−S batteries.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of the Electrolyte with NBP. The commonly
used electrolyte was prepared with 1.0 M lithium bis-
(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide and 0.2 M lithium nitrate dissolved
in DME and DOL (1:1 ratio by volume), which is used as a blank
electrolyte of the Li−S battery. The electrolyte with NBP was
prepared by adding NBP powder into the blank electrolyte. The 0.1
M NBP electrolyte was selected to contrast with the blank electrolyte.
2.2. Preparation of the graphene/sulfur (G/S) Composite.
The G/S composite was prepared by a melting method. Brieﬂy, the
commercial conductive graphene (The Sixth Element, Inc., SE1233)
and sulfur were physically mixed with a 1:3 ratio by mass, and then
the mixture was heated at 155 °C for 12 h.
2.3. Synthesis of Li2S6 Solution. Li2S6 solution was prepared by
mixing Li2S and S with a stoichiometric 1:5 ratio in DME/DOL
solvent (1:1 by volume), i.e., Li2S + 5S →Li2S6, within an Ar-ﬁlled
glovebox, and the precursor solution was heated at 60 °C for 48 h.
2.4. Synthesis of Organopolysulﬁde Deposition. NBP
powder was slowly added into 2 mL of the prepared 0.5 M Li2S6
solution under magnetic stirring for 10 min in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox.
After stirring, the turbid solution was then allowed to stand for 30
min, and the liquid supernatant was removed with a pipette. Then, the
left deposition was dried at 60 °C for 24 h in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox.
2.5. Preparation of Carbon Nanoﬁber (CNF) Paper and the
Corresponding Cathode. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) composite
nanoﬁbers were ﬁrst prepared by electrospinning a PAN solution of
N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). In brief, 1 g of PAN was dissolved
in 10 mL of DMF to obtain the electrospinning precursor with
magnetic stirring at 60 °C for 3 h. The electrospinning precursor was
electrospun on an aluminum foil collector with a ﬂow rate of 1 mL
h−1. The distance between the syringe and the collector is 12 cm, and
the applied voltage is 16 kV. Then, the obtained PAN ﬁlm was dried
and precarbonized at 250 °C for 3 h in a drying oven. Finally, CNF
paper was obtained by carbonizing the PAN ﬁlm at 800 °C for 3 h in a
tube furnace with a heating rate of 3 °C min−1 under an Ar
atmosphere. CNF paper was cut into a circular disk, which was placed
between the cathode and the separator to monitor the organo-
polysulﬁde deposition.
2.6. Material Characterization. The
13C NMR spectrum of
organopolysulﬁde deposition was measured with a Bruker Avance III
WB 400 NMR spectrometer. FTIR spectra were tested on a Bruker
infrared spectrometer using KBr pellets. The microstructure and
morphology of the as-prepared materials and cycled sulfur electrode
were observed using a ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope
(SEM, JSM-7800) with an elemental mapping apparatus. XRD
patterns were obtained using an X-ray powder diﬀractometer (Rigaku
MiniFlex II, Monochrome Cu Kα rays) with a scanning speed of 4°
min−1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a
thermogravimetric analyzer (Mettler-Toledo) under an Ar atmos-
phere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from ambient temperature to
800 °C. XPS spectra were obtained on an X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy spectrometer (PHI 5000 Versa Probe) with a
monochromator of Al Kα radiation.
2.7. Electrochemical Measurements. The sulfur cathode
consists of an 80 wt % G/S composite, 10 wt % conductive Super-
P, and 10 wt % PVDF binder. The areal sulfur loading in the routine
sulfur cathodes is 1.0−1.2 mg cm−2 and E/S = 10 μL mg−1. The
organopolysulﬁdes and active carbon (AC) cathodes were prepared
by using a similar method, but the G/S composite was replaced by
organopolysulﬁde deposition and AC, respectively. Li−S cells were
assembled in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox using Celgard 2300 as a separator
and Li metal as an anode. The electrolyte with and without NBP was
prepared as described above. Galvanostatic discharge and charge tests
were conducted on a LAND battery test system (CT2001C) in the
potential range of 1.7−2.8 V vs Li/Li+. The speciﬁc capacity of Li−S
cells was calculated based on the mass of sulfur. Cyclic voltammetry
(CV) was performed using an electrochemical analyzer (LK2010) in
the potential range of 1.7−2.8 V vs Li/Li+. Electrochemical
impedance spectra (EIS) were measured using an electrochemical

3. THEORETICAL CALCULATIONS
The geometrical optimizations of Li, Li2Sn, and C8H4NO2SnLi
(n = 1−8) and (Li2S4)4 clusters were performed with the M06-
2X/def2-TZVP level of theory on Gaussian 16.43−45 Then, the
optimized structures were subjected to the frequency
calculations to ensure optimization at a local minimum of
the potential energy surface, and the vibrational zero-point
energy correction and Gibbs energy correction in the standard
state were obtained. The Gibbs energy of reduction reaction
(ΔGr) was calculated by the Gibbs energies of products minus
the Gibbs energies of reactants. Finally, the reduction potential
Er was calculated via the Nernst equation: Er = −ΔGr/nF,
where n is the number of transferred electrons in the reaction
and F is Faraday’s constant.
4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The binding reaction between NBP and LiPSs was conﬁrmed
through experimental and computational studies. As shown in
Figure 1a, 0.5 M Li2S6 in 2 mL of DOL/DME solvents
spontaneously reacts with 0.35 M NBP, resulting in a white
deposition. The structure of deposition was investigated by
comparative analysis of 13C NMR spectra of the pristine NBP
and the deposition (Figure 1b). The 13C chemical shift for the
CO group of NBP is located at 168 ppm, which shifts to
upﬁeld and splits into two signals (188 and 192 ppm). The
splitting indicates that the symmetry of the two CO groups
on the NBP molecule is broken owing to the grafting of LiPSs.
The change in the chemical shift suggests that the chemical
environment of the C atom on CO alters due to the
occurrence of the charge transfer caused by the interaction

The organopolysulﬁde sample was dried at 60 °C to obtain
powders for SEM and XRD characterizations. The elemental
mapping images indicate that the C, N, O, and S elements exist
in the particle (Figure 2a). Note that the elements C, N, and O
have a consistent distribution, but the signal of S is rich in
another region. This observation suggests that some reactions
have occurred during the thermal treatment. The XRD results
further conﬁrm the decomposition of the organopolysulﬁdes
(Figure 2b). The XRD pattern of the resulting powder
coincides with S8,46 whereas the signals of NBP and Li2S are
not detected. If the polysulﬁde-containing solution is
evaporated, both Li2S and S8 would be formed because of
the disproportion reaction.47,48 Similarly, the only presence of
S8 signals implies the decomposition process of organo-
polysulﬁdes under thermal conditions.48 This view can be
further veriﬁed by calculating Gibbs free energy of the possible
decomposition reaction of C8H4NO2S6Li. All of the Gibbs free
energies of the decomposition reactions are positive but small
(Figure S1), indicating that C8H4NO2SnLi is in the metastable
state and possibly produces elemental S8 and the short-chain
organopolysulﬁdes during the drying process. Therefore, the
formation of the organopolysulﬁde deposition layer probably
inﬂuences the morphology of the sulfur cathode and the
distribution of the sulfur species, further aﬀecting the
electrochemical performance of the sulfur cathode.33
To investigate the morphological features induced by the
introduction of NBP, SEM images of sulfur cathodes retrieved
from the cycled cells with NBP (denoted by G/S-NBP) and
without NBP (denoted by G/S-blank) were compared. In this
work, the sulfur cathode based on the G/S composite is used,
and the sulfur content of the composite is 76.1%, as
determined using TGA curves (Figure S2). The layered
graphene is beneﬁcial for accommodating sulfur and provides a
rich active surface. The elemental mapping of sulfur indicates
that the distribution of sulfur is homogeneous in the pristine
G/S composite (Figure S3). The smooth surface of the G/S
composite implies that sulfur is initially loaded within stacked
pores of graphene. The SEM image of the pristine G/S sulfur
cathode before cycling shows that the G/S composite particles
are well-distributed and there is no out layer on the surface
(Figure S4). After cycling at 0.1 C for ﬁve cycles, the surface
morphologies of G/S-NBP and G/S-blank cathodes are
investigated in diﬀerent charge states (2.80, 2.15, and 1.70
V). As shown in Figure 3, the surface of the G/S-NBP cathode
is covered by a porous layer at each potential, which is
regarded as the in situ formed organopolysulﬁde deposition.
The porosity characteristic of the deposition layer can alleviate
cathode passivation. This deposition layer slightly shrinks
during discharging, which is attributed to the electrochemical
reduction of organopolysulﬁdes. Besides, the dynamic change
in the deposition layer further ensures mass transport during
cycling. This organopolysulﬁde layer always exists during the
redox process, which can depress the diﬀusion of soluble LiPSs
to the electrolyte and conﬁne the redox of sulfur species within
the cathode. Therefore, the formation of the organopolysulﬁde
layer is believed to prolong the cycling ability of cells. As for
the G/S-blank cathodes, sulfur is exposed on the surface of the
cathode at 2.80 V, and no deposition layer can be observed at
2.15 and 1.70 V (Figure S5). Notably, from the enlarged SEM
images of cathodes at 1.70 V, it can be found that insoluble
Li2S/Li2S2 distributes itself inside the layer for the G/S-NBP
cathode (Figure S6a) but accumulates around graphene for the
G/S-blank cathode (Figure S6b). The exposed structure of the
G/S-blank cathode has a little eﬀect on conﬁning soluble
LiPSs, which is easy to diﬀuse to the electrolyte during cycling
and results in severe capacity decay. Furthermore, the internal
deposition states were monitored through the attached CNF
interlayer on the sulfur cathode (Figure S6c,d). The massive
deposition uniformly inserts in the CNF interlayer in the G/S-
NBP cell, but no deposition can be observed in the CNF
interlayer in the G/S-blank cell. The results indicate that the
organopolysulﬁde deposition can also uniformly distribute

To monitor the component transformation of the organo-
polysulﬁdes in the G/S-NBP cathode during cycling, FTIR and
XPS spectra of sulfur cathodes were measured at diﬀerent
potentials. As shown in Figure 4a, the weak peak at 842 cm−1
of the FTIR spectra is assigned to the characteristic vibration
of the N−S bond, implying that the sulfur chain has been
grafted onto the NBP moiety in organopolysulﬁdes. The peak
at 412 cm−1 is attributed to intermolecular characteristic
vibration associated with S8 rings.49 The peaks at 441 and 467
cm−1 are assigned to the stretching vibration of the S−S bond
of the S8 molecule and/or sulfur-containing species (organo-
polysulﬁdes and LiPSs).50 The N−S bond always exists under
the imposed potentials, indicating that organopolysulﬁdes
remain stable once formed. The signal strength of the S−S
bond gradually increases during discharging, but that of the S8
ring (412 cm−1) gradually decreases and ﬁnally disappears,
suggesting the reduction consumption of S8. When the cell was
recharged to 2.8 V, the signals assigned to the S−S bond and
the S8 ring reappear, suggesting that the reduction process is
reversible. According to the above results, we infer that the
sulfur chains keep grafting on the NBP moiety during cycling,
but the length changes with the electrochemical process
proceeding.
The detailed speciation of sulfur-containing species in the
G/S-NBP cathode was further determined by XPS spectra at
diﬀerent potentials. The Li 1s spectra are employed to
characterize the O...Li interaction and S−Li bond along with
the electrochemical lithiation (Figure 4b). The Li 1s signal at
56.4 eV is attributed to the conﬁguration O...Li−S of
organopolysulﬁdes. The signal gradually decreases in the
discharge and almost disappears at the end of discharge,
indicating that the sulfur chains in the organopolysulﬁdes
gradually shorten during the lithiation process. Meanwhile, the
Li 1s signal at 55.1 eV is attributed to the S−Li bond of
LiPSs.51,52 The signal shifts to the lower 54.6 eV at the end
discharge, indicating the formation of short-chain Li2S2/Li2S.
In particular, the Li 1s signals of the O...Li−S conﬁguration
and the S−Li bond reappear as the cell is recharged to 2.8 V,
suggesting the electrochemical reversibility of the organo-
polysulﬁde conversion. Such a reversible conversion is further
proved by S 2p spectra, as presented in Figure 4c. The S 2p
signals at 164.4, 163.2, and 161.7 eV are attributed to element
S8, long-chain C8H4NO2SnLi and/or LiPSs, and short-chain
C8H4NO2SLi and/or L2S2, respectively. The S 2p signal of S8
weakens and ﬁnally disappears, revealing the reduction process

To ﬁgure out the transformation feasibility, LUMO and
HOMO of LiPSs and C8H4NO2SnLi (n = 2, 4, 6, 8) were
investigated by ab initio calculations, as presented in Figure 4d.
The results show that the LUMO energy of C8H4NO2SnLi
greatly decreases (ΔE = 1.0−1.57 eV) and the HOMO energy
of C8H4NO2SnLi (except C8H4NO2S2Li) slightly increases
(∼0.6 eV) when LiPSs are grafted onto NBP. In fact, Li2S2
tends to form the cluster,53 e.g., (Li2S2)4 cluster with an
HOMO energy of −7.05 eV (Figure S7). On the basis of this
point, the HOMO energy level of C8H4NO2S2Li (−6.74 eV) is
higher than that of (Li2S2)4, which is expected to facilitate the
oxidation reaction and reduces the initial charging potential of
batteries with NBP addition. All of the above results totally
imply that the redox process of the sulfur cathode can be
promoted by organopolysulﬁdes based on the perspective of
thermodynamics. Moreover, the LUMO transfers from the
sulfur chain of LiPSs to heterocyclic rings of the NBP moiety
in C8H4NO2SnLi, implying that the redox reaction of
organopolysulﬁdes is an organic-mediated process.
The electrochemical behavior of the sulfur cathodes was
investigated with CR2032 coin cells. Prior to that, the
electrochemical activity of the chemically synthesized organo-
polysulﬁde deposition was evaluated in the blank electrolyte.
The charge/discharge behavior is similar to that of the sulfur/
carbon electrode except for the initial discharge process that
will be discussed later (Figure S8). The charge/discharge
curves at diﬀerent cycles indicate that the organopolysulﬁde
deposition is electrochemically active and reversible. Note that
the NBP is electrochemically inert in the electrolyte because
the AC cathode only shows its typical capacitance character-
istic in the electrolyte with 0.2 M NBP (Figure S9).54 The
initial discharge/charge curves of G/S-NBP and G/S-blank
cells are compared in Figure 5a. As indicated by the dotted
circles, two improvements are brought about by introducing
NBP into the electrolyte: (i) the voltage dip at the junction of
plateaus disappears. This indicates that the oversaturation of
LiPSs is avoided owing to the grafting and deposition by NBP;
(ii) the overpotential at the initial charging decreases. These
observations suggest that the formation of organopolysulﬁde
deposition can enhance the electric contact of Li2S/Li2S2. The
latter claim can also be veriﬁed by the EIS results (Figure S10
and Table S1), in which the charge-transfer resistance (Rct) of
G/S-NBP (7.97 Ω) is smaller than that of the G/S-blank cell
(36.69 Ω) in the discharged state. Additionally, a new voltage
plateau appears at 2.13 V as indicated by the red arrow in G/S-
NBP cell, which is attributed to the electrochemical reduction
of C8H4NO2S8Li to the short-chain C8H4NO2SnLi. The
reduction potentials of C8H4NO2S8Li in possible paths were
calculated by ab initio as listed in Table S2. According to the
results, the reduction of C8H4NO2S8Li, i.e., C8H4NO2S8Li +
2Li+ + 2e−→C8H4NO2S4Li + Li2S4, shows a calculational
potential of 2.20 V. Thus, the voltage plateau at about 2.13 V
results from the above reaction but lowers due to the
polarization and ohmic voltage drop in the cell. However,
the plateau at 2.13 V fades away from the ﬁrst to ﬁftieth cycles
(Figure S11), implying that the discharged product
C8H4NO2SLi may not be oxidized to C8H4NO2S8Li, but
may be oxidized to the other short-chain ones (e.g.,
C8H4NO2S4Li) during the subsequent charging process.

lower than that of the G/S-blank cell. We speculate that the
excess NBP molecules that residue in the electrolyte react with

formation of the organopolysulﬁde deposition layer that
suppresses the dissolution of LiPSs into the bulk electrolyte
and increases the local concentration of LiPSs and electrolyte
viscosity.

cathode surface state (see Figure 3). Notably, it is the
dynamical change of the deposition layer that avoids the
continual passivation of the electrode surface and ensures the
proceeding of the electrode reaction.
As expected, the cycling performance is improved by
introducing NBP into the electrolyte (Figure 6a). Never-
theless, the G/S-NBP cell exhibits rapid capacity decay during
the ﬁrst few cycles. One reason may be that some electrons are
not reversible in the ﬁnal reduction product C8H4NO2SLi/
Li2S2 (see discussion for Figure 4c). Another reason may be
that some LiPSs inevitably diﬀuse into the electrolyte bulk
during the initial cycles, in which the organopolysulﬁde
deposition layer is not yet completely built. Sulfur cathodes
with higher sulfur loadings were further fabricated to study the
improvement brought about by NBP under more practical
conditions. G/S-NBP cells deliver a high initial capacity above
1100 mAh g−1 under the high loading of 3.10 and 4.35 mg
cm−2 (Figure 6b). After activation for several cycles, the cell
exhibits superior cycling performance over 50 cycles with
capacities above 700 mAh g−1. In comparison, the G/S-blank
with a high sulfur loading exhibits inferior cycling stability
(Figure S16). Thus, the introduction of NBP into the
electrolyte has a practical application value.

As a novel electrolyte additive, NBP can in situ graft the
dissolved LiPSs to form an organopolysulﬁde deposition layer.
Spectroscopy technology and ab initio calculation results
clarify the speciation and evolution of organopolysulﬁdes. The
electrochemical experimental results unravel the redox reaction
behavior and kinetics. The organopolysulﬁde layer deposited
on the cathode surface not only depresses the diﬀusion of
LiPSs into the electrolyte bulk but also promotes the
conversion of sulfur species within the cathode. Moreover,
the electrochemical conversion of organopolysulﬁdes dynam-
ically regulates the state of the cathode surface during cycling
and relieves the electrode passivation, resulting in fast
electrode kinetics. As expected, the sulfur cathodes exhibit
improved cycling stability and rate capability on introducing
NBP in the electrolyte.
5. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, NBP as an electrolyte additive was introduced
into the Li−S battery in this work. NBP can graft the dissolved
LiPSs to form deposition on the cathode. The deposition was
veriﬁed to be lithium organopolysulﬁdes, which can suppress
the shuttle of LiPSs and conﬁne the redox reaction within the
internal cathode. In this regard, NBP can be regarded as a
molecule binder of LiPSs during the initial redox process, and
organopolysulﬁdes can protect the sulfur cathode by
preventing LiPSs from excessive diﬀusion. Moreover, the
organopolysulﬁdes are electrochemically active, relieving the
irreversible capacity on the surface layer. Beneﬁting from the
organopolysulﬁde deposition layer, the Li−S cell with NPB
addition shows improved electrochemical performance in
terms of capacity retention and high-rate stability. This study
suggests a facile and alternative strategy of grafting and
depositing LiPSs to fabricate high-performance Li−S batteries.
