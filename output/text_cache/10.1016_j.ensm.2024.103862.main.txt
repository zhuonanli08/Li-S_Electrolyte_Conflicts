A B S T R A C T
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are considered promising candidates for next-generation energy storage devices 
owing to their ultrahigh theoretical energy density. However, LSBs are hindered by uncontrollable lithium 
dendrite growth, polysulfides shuttle effects, and sluggish sulfur kinetics. Herein, this work develops a multi­
functional ZnI2 electrolyte additive for LSB for local high concentration base electrolyte. At anode side, a LixZn 
alloy hybrid interface leading to planar deposited lithium is formed from the reaction between Li metal and the 
ZnI2 additive and reduction products of Li+ solvation shell of the electrolyte. Moreover, planar deposited lithium 
was confirmed by in-situ liquid transmission electron microscopy (TEM). For cathode side, Zn2+ under the drive 
of electric field prior react with lithium polysulfide to form ultrathin ZnS nanosheets exposed with (100) miller 
index serving as a catalyst to accelerate sulfur redox kinetics and inhibit polysulfides shuttling. Consequently, the 
LSB with ZnI2 additive exhibits a remarkable discharge capacity of 712 mA h g−1 at 0.5 C after 300 cycles and a 
superior rate capability of 674.9 mA h g−1 at 2 C. This work demonstrates that ZnI2 serves as a multifunctional 
electrolyte additive to simultaneously facilitate the sulfur redox kinetics, reduce the shuttle effect, and promote 
smooth Li growth.
1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are regarded as the most promising 
next-generation energy storage systems due to their high energy density 
(2600 Wh kg-1) and cost-effectiveness [1]. Despite all these merits, 
Lithium-sulfur battery (LSB) has undergone a tough development road, 
and the energy density and cycling stability of practical LSB are con­
strained by several serious problems [2]. These problems include: 1) the 
intrinsic insulating property of S (5 × 10–30 S cm-1 at 25 ◦C) and Li2S 
(10–13 S cm-1 at 25 ◦C) [3] which impedes the transfer of electrons, 2) 
The high-volume expansion (≈80 %) of solid sulfur into Li2S caused by 
the different densities of sulfur (2.07 g cm-3) and Li2S (1.66 g cm-3) [4], 
3) The notorious shuttle effect caused by soluble lithium polysulfides 
(LiPSs) resulting in loss of active materials and rapid capacity decrease 
[5], 4) Li dendrite growth induced by the nonuniform Li deposition. 
These challenges become more significant at higher current density and 
higher areal sulfur loading [6]. Various strategies have been explored to 
address and mitigate the mentioned challenges, such as advanced sulfur 
hosts to mitigate the shuttle effect [7-10], electrolyte solution modifi­
cation (additives and redox mediators) [11], and catalyst design to 
accelerate the sulfur redox reaction [12,13]. Alternatively, a 
less-explored direction is to design liquid electrolyte, including solvents, 
salts, and additives with dual functionalities to mitigate these challenges 
[14].

The objective of developing electrolytes for LSB is to construct a 
stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) to suppress the shuttle effect and 
lithium dendrite while ensuring accelerated cathode kinetics. Since the 
Li-metal anode stripping/plating process during the cycle is highly 
dependent on the component and structure of the SEI layer, various 
electrolytes and multiple additives have been studied aiming to 
construct a stable SEI layer. Inorganic electrolyte additives (lithium ni­
trate (LiNO3) [15], Lanthanum(III) nitrate (La(NO3)3) [5], Phosphorus 
pentasulfide (P2S5) [16], and metal iodides, such as LiI, MgI2, AlI3, TiI4, 
and SnI4 [17]) were reported to improve the electrochemical properties 
of S-based LSB. Among those, LiNO3 is a well-known electrolyte addi­
tive, which can form a favorable SEI on the surface of the Li metal anode 
to reduce the shuttle effect [15]. Metal iodides (LiI, MgI2, AlI3, TiI4, and 
SnI4) as electrolyte additives dissolved in commercial ether-based 
electrolyte were studied by Kim to improve the electrochemical prop­
erties of S-based LSBs, as a result of the co-deposition of Li and metal 
cations (Li, Mg, Al, Ti, and Sn) on Li metal along with the polymerization 
of DME in the electrolyte [17]. The modified SEI layer mentioned above 
is formed during the cycle. The dissolved LiPSs may react with the fresh 
Li metal without a preformed layer before cycle. Moreover, their effect 
was only investigated towards the modification of the SEI layer while 
the LPSs redox kinetics at the cathode is still low due to the absence of 
redox mediators or catalyst.
The kinetics of sulfur conversion is influenced by the solvation 
structure of LiPSs and the formation of sulfur redox mediator. Choosing 
suitable solvent or optimizing the solubility of LPSs is the most direct 
and effective method to modify solvation structure of LiPSs, such as 
fluorinated ethers solvents [18,19]. LiNO3 in the electrolyte controls the 
solvation structure of Li+ and PS anions, curtails PS clustering, and 
promotes the utilization of the sulfur cathode [20]. Halogen dopants (F, 
Cl, Br, I) [21] and abundant I–O bonds [22] with polar nature signifi­
cantly modify the local electronic structure of adsorption site and further 
induce a polarization to trap the polysulfides. In addition, organic ad­
ditive (diphenyl diselenide (DPDSe [23]), tetrathiafulvalene (TTF [24]), 
and biphenyl-4,4′-dithiol (BPD) [25]) was also used to improve the LSB 
performance by the generated mediator of sulfur to accelerate redox 
kinetics of sulfur and reduce to shuttle effect with strong combination. In 
addition to redox mediators, RbNO3 additive facilitates the reduction 
reaction of LiPSs and nucleation of Li2S by forming an Rb2S catalyst 
[26]. Developing multifunctional additives (diphenyl ditelluride 
(DPDTe [27]) and hexadecyltrioctylammonium iodide (HTOA-I [28])) 
that protect the Li anode surface, hinder the polysulfide shuttle effect, 
and achieve homogeneous Li deposition will significantly enhance life­
span and capacity.
Motivated by these observations, a total of two molar lithium salt 
(1M LiNO3 and 1 M LiFSI) in DME was formulated as base electrolyte for 
LSB and investigated ZnI2 as an electrolyte additive improve the per­
formance of LSBs. Accordingly, a Zn alloy interface with reduced inor­
ganic interface is formed before cycle, which avoids the side reaction 
between LiPSs and Li metal during fist cycle. Anion-derived, inorganic 
rich, smooth, and LiZn alloy hybrid interface with LiI was formed on the 
surface of the lithium metal. LiZn alloy hybrid interface improves the I0 
of Li stripping/plating while planar deposited lithium was observed on 
the surface of Lithium metal confirmed by in-situ liquid TEM. Zn2+ ions

2.2. Preparation of electrolyte
The commercial standard (CSTD) electrolyte consists of 1 M LiTFSI in 
DOL: DME (1:1 by vol.) with 1 wt% LiNO3. The standard electrolyte 
(STD) is 1 M LiFSI in DME. Various molar ratios from 0.5 M, 0.75 M, to 1 
M LiNO3 were dissolved into STD electrolyte, which is named STD xM 
LiNO3. STD 1 M LiNO3 0.05 M LiI and STD 1 M LiNO3 0.05 M ZnI2 were 
prepared by dissolving 0.05 M LiI or 0.05 M ZnI2 additives into STD 1 M 
LiNO3. “M” stands for molar concentration. All the electrolytes were 
prepared in the glovebox.
2.3. Preparation of CNT/sulfur electrode
Sulfur and CNT (3:1 by mass) were mixed uniformly and sealed in a 
10 mL autoclave and heated to 155 ◦C for 20 h to prepare CNT/S 
composite material. The weight of S in the CNT/S composite is 74.24 wt 
% confirmed by ICP-OES. CNT/S, carbon black, and PVDF were mixed 
with NMP in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 to make a slurry. The slurry was then 
coated onto an aluminum foil with a thickness of 500 µm. After the NMP 
was evaporated at 60 ◦C overnight in a vacuum oven, the cathode was 
thus obtained. The active sulfur loading for each cathode is about 2 mg 
cm −2.
2.4. Cell assembly
All coin-type (CR2032) cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove 
box (Korea Kiyon, KK-1335, H2O < 0.01 pp). The Li–S cells were 
assembled with a Li foil anode (16 mm, MTI Korean Co., Ltd.), Celgard 
separators (diameter: 19 mm, thickness: 20 μm, AOT Batteries Tech­
nology Co., Ltd.) soaked in electrolytes described above with 15 μL mg-1 
or 7.5 μL mg-1, and a cathode mentioned above. The Li || Li symmetric 
cells were assembled with Li foils as both the cathode and anode and a 
single-layer PE separator soaked with electrolytes mentioned above. In 
the half cells, a Cu foil (16 mm diameter) was used as the working 
electrode and a Li foil (200 μm thick, 15.8 mm diameter) was used as 
both counter and reference electrodes. The cells were assembled by H- 
2032 (Wellcos Corporation, South Korean).

The electrochemical performance of the symmetric Li||Li cells was

2.6. Ex and in-situ observation of the lithium plating process
The two silicon microchips were sandwiched together in the tip of 
the in-situ holder (Poseidon Select, Protochips). The upper microchip 
(ECT-45CR-10 and ECT-45PT-10, Protochips) contained a glassy carbon

electrolyte. The lower microchip (ECB-39a-10, Protochips) also has a 
silicon nitride window. After the cell was fully assembled and sealed, the 
lithium plating and stripping process occur on the WE at a scan rate of 10 
mv s-1 and a scan window of (−3.2 V to −1.5 V vs Pt) [29].
For in-situ TEM observation, the STD-1 M LiNO3 0.05 M ZnI2 elec­
trolyte are prepared by the above method. The electrolyte was flown 
into the encapsulated liquid cell TEM using a Protochips Poseidon 
holder. A thin layer of electrolyte was confined between two Si-SiN 
windows with a 500 nm spacer and a flow rate of 100 µL h-1 during 
TEM imaging and 200 µL h-1 for electrolyte refreshing after each cycle. A 
Gamry Reference 600+ was used to provide and measure the cyclic 
voltammogram signal between the reference, working, and counter Pt or 
carbon electrodes. The CV was performed by applying a scan rate of 10 
mv s-1 and a scan window of (−3.2 V to −1.5 V vs Pt) between the 
working and counter Pt electrode. For stable observations, the flux of the 
electron beam was < 150 e- nm-2 s-1 at an accelerating voltage of 300 kv. 
TEM (JEM-3011HR, JEOL) and a charge-coupled device camera (Orius 
SC1000, Gatan) were employed for the in situ electrochemical TEM 
experiments.
For the ex-situ TEM test, the detail process is similar with in-situ

2.8. Computational methods
First-principles calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab 
initio Simulation Package (VASP) within the framework of density 
functional theory (DFT), utilizing the projector augmented wave (PAW) 
method [30-32]. The exchange-correlation potential was modeled using 
the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional under the generalized gradient 
approximation. To replicate each solute-solvent structure, the cell was 
saturated with DME molecules at a given theoretical liquid density. 
Three solute mixtures—LiFSI+LiNO3, LiFSI+LiNO3+LiI, and LiF­
SI+LiNO3+ZnI2—were introduced into the DME-filled supercell, which 
measured 26 × 26 × 26 Å In DME+LiFSI+LiNO3, the ratio of DME: 
LiFSI: LiNO3 was set to 10:1:1. LiI was added to the same structure, 
setting the ratio of DME: LiFSI: LiNO3: LiI to 100:10:10:1. Similarly, the 
ratio of DME: LiFSI: LiNO3: ZnI2 was set to 100:10:10:1 for 
DME+LiFSI+LiNO3+ZnI2. For all calculations, a gamma-centered 
k-points mesh grid with a 1 × 1 × 1 sampling point was used. The 
cutoff energy was set to 400 eV, and the convergence criterion for the 
self-consistent field calculations regarding the electronic energy differ­
ence was set to 1 × 10–4 eV.
To ensure the stability of the solvent-solute mixture, ab initio mo­
lecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations using the canonical ensemble 
(NVT) were performed. The canonical ensemble is a standard approach 
in molecular dynamics that maintains a constant number of particles 
(N), volume (V), and temperature (T). Each system was gradually 
annealed to 323 K, followed by an equilibration period of 8000 femto­
seconds (fs).
From the final image obtained from the AIMD calculation, radial 
distribution functions (RDF) were calculated to visualize the distance 
between atoms in different molecules. For all radial distribution func­
tion calculations, maximum radius was set to 6 Å.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of electrolytes
Electrolyte engineering is critical for developing Li metal batteries 
(LMB), including solvent ratio modification, lithium salt, and salt ad­
ditive optimization. While ether-based electrolytes provide high ionic 
conductivity and suitable LPSs dissolution, the high solubility of LPSs is 
still a significant challenge and needs to be addressed through careful 
solvent selection and solvation sheath modification [33]. Compared 
with cyclic dioxolane molecule, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with 
higher dielectric constant and lower viscosity [34] has served as an 
optimized solvent to achieve ultrahigh concentration electrolyte for 
lithium metal batteries (LMB). A high-concentration lithium bis 
(fluorosulfonyl)imide/1,2-dimethoxyethane (LiFSI/DME) electrolyte 
has been reported to achieve a stable SEI [35]. The introduction of NO3
- 
into the Li+solvation sheath is known to induce the complete decom­
position of the (bis(fluorsulfonyl)imide) (FSI−) anions [36] leading to 
the formation of SEI with abundant LiF and LiNxOy on the surface of Li 
anode. Herein, considering the remarkable effect of LiNO3 in LSB, 1 M 
LiNO3 was dissolved into DME solvent with 1 M LiFSI named as STD 
(DME 1 M LiFSI) 1 M LiNO3 as a base electrolyte. ZnI2 and LiI are 
incorporated as additives to the base electrolyte (STD-1 M LiNO3).
The optical pictures of the obtained above electrolytes are shown in 
Figure S1a. The effect of LiNO3, LiI, and ZnI2 on the solvation structure 
was further assessed by Raman spectroscopy shown in Figure S1b.

electrolyte. STD-1 M LiNO3 exhibited peaks around 720, 877 and 1038

cluster (νas, C–O) and LiNO3 (N = O asymmetrical stretching, νas, N = O) 
[34], respectively. After adding 0.05 M LiI and 0.05 M ZnI2 into STD −1 
M LiNO3 electrolyte (Figure S1b), no significant changes in intensity

To clarify the effect of additives on the local environment and sol­

electrolyte was investigated by ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) 
simulations at 323K. The equilibrium snapshots of electrolyte systems 
and radial distribution functions (RDF, g[r]) between Li+ and anion/ 
solvent are shown in Fig. 2e-g and Figures S2–3. For base electrolyte, a 
strong Li+-NO3 interaction (Fig. 2e, Li+-ONO3 RDF first peak at 2.2 Å) via 
Li-O bond was observed. This feature not only enables the strong coor­
dination between Li+ and NO3
- but also weakens the Li+-DME bonding, 
which results in the improved t+ and desolation kinetics [34,40]. On the 
contrary, the Li+-OFSI interaction was strengthened with a significant 
increase in the first RDF peak at 2.2 Å for LiI additive (Fig. 2f). More­
over, increased interaction between Li+-ODME was also observed with a 
high-intensity peak at 2.2 Å (Fig. 2f) indicating the presence of more 
DME in the primary solvation sheath of Li+ in the presence of LiI addi­
tive. The stronger Li+-OFSI and Li+-ODME interaction will decrease the 
shielding effect of Li-ion solvent structure for LiI additive electrolyte. 
Interestingly, compared to the base electrolyte, the addition of the ZnI2 
additive revealed an increased interaction with both Li-ONO3 and 
Li-O(FSI) with a higher intensity RDF peak at 2.2 Å and 1.9 Å (Fig. 2g), 
respectively. The larger intensity of Li-OFSI (1.9 Å) peak for ZnI2 additive 
electrolyte compared with Li-ODME (1.9 Å) proves that less DME is in the 
Li-ion solvation sheath while FSI- ion is in the Li-ions salvation sheath. 
Therefore, the introduction of ZnI2 additive regulates the interaction 
between Li-ions and FSI- (Fig. 2h) and leads to the polarization of FSI- 
which is activated and thus decomposes completely.
3.2. Effect of different electrolytes on the SEI layer
The regulation of SEI is vital for inhibiting Li dendrites in LSB. The 
ingredients of SEI are derived from the reduction product of electrolyte 
components. Hence, the decomposition products from solvents and an­
ions in the solvation sheath compose the main components of SEI. The 
SEI composition of cycled Li metal surface for LSB was studied by in- 
depth X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) Ar+ etching measure­
ment with LiI and ZnI2 additives. The C 1 s, O 1 s, Li 1 s, I 3d, Zn 2p, C 1 s, 
F 1 s, S 2p, and N 1 s profiles were shown in Fig. 3a-d, and Figure S4. 
The components of the SEI layer are summarized in Fig. 3f.
In the case of base electrolyte, ROCO2Li, Li2CO3, LiF, and Li3 N 
inorganic components were observed at 533.4 eV in O 1 s [41], 531.8 eV 
[41] in O 1 s, 684.8 eV in F 1 s [42], and 399 eV in N 1s [43]spectra on 
the surface of the Li metal. With the etching of the outmost surface, new 
components, LiOH at 531 eV [44], and Li2O at 528.4 eV [44], small 
amounts of LiF (F 1 s) and Li3 N (N 1 s) and Li metal (Li 1 s) at 53 eV [45] 
were observed compared with the surface. The observation of peaks 
attributed to Li metal indicates the SEI layer for the base electrolyte is 
thin compared with the LiI and ZnI2 additives. For the SEI layer gener­

observed on the surface from the O 1 s and Li 1 s spectra. The sulfur 
species (including LiSOx (166.9 eV), Li2S (160 eV)) in Figure S4c could 
be observed in the SEI layer and the observation of Li2S indicates the 
complete decomposition of FSI-[36]. Moreover, with increasing depth 
after Ar+ itching, the peak for ROCO2Li disappeared leaving an inor­
ganic rich subsurface. I- ion (818.5 eV [46], Fig. 3c) is detected on the

into the base electrolyte, the inorganic-rich SEI layer was formed on the 
surface of the Li metal. The presence of novel LiI [47] and LiZn alloy 
(based on the equation 2Li+ZnI2=Zn+2LiI, Zn+Li=LiZn) was confirmed 
by the typical peaks at 619 and 1017 eV in I 3d and Zn 2p spectra,

SEI layer could also be confirmed by the chemical reaction above. Zn

in good agreement with the interplanar spacing of LiZn ((111), 3.6 Å) in

LiZn alloy after charge and discharge progress confirms the formation of 
LiZn alloy in the SEI layer and is stable during the cycle. The abundant 
inorganic SEI component element distribution could also be observed 
from the EDS image in Figure S5. The above results confirm the key role 
of the LiI and ZnI2 additives in constructing an inorganic-rich, anion-­
derived SEI layer.
To study the stability between the Li metal and the designed elec­
trolyte, a 100 µL base electrolyte with LiI and ZnI2 additive electrolyte 
was dropped onto the surface of Li metal, respectively and the surface 
morphology of Li metal was investigated by FE-SEM (Fig. 4a and S7). A 
black surface resulting from the reduction of Zn2+ ions was observed 
when the base electrolyte with ZnI2 additive was dropped onto the 
surface of Li metal while no significant color change was seen on the Li 
anode after dropping the base electrolyte with and without LiI additive. 
To unveil the composition of the black surface, EDS analysis was con­

LixZn alloy promotes the reduction of anions in the electrolyte. Gathered 
Zn particles with a size of 4 µm are located on the surface of Li metal. 
Similar LixZn morphology (Fig. 4d) could be observed from the surface 
of Li metal after 5 cycles with the base ZnI2 additive electrolyte, which 
indicated that the LixZn alloy was generated before cycles. Therefore, 
the inorganic SEI layer on the surface of Li metal with STD-1 M LiNO3 
0.05 M ZnI2 additive electrolyte was formed with the help of LiZn alloy.
All three electrolytes displayed an inorganic-rich SEI layer, which is 
known to improve Li-ion diffusion capability, significantly thereby 
promoting the smooth lithium stripping/plating. Even though no 
lithium dendrite was detected on the surface of Li metal for the base 
electrolyte with and without both the additives, remarkably different 
anode local surface morphologies were observed after 5 cycles for the 
different electrolytes (Fig. 4b-d). For base electrolyte (Fig. 4b), Li anode 
shows a corroded and rough surface, which results from side reactions of 
Li anode with soluble LiPSs. For LiI additive electrolyte (Fig. 4c), the 
long strips of lithium metal on Li metal and Cu surface are stacked and 
the SEI is compact and smooth, which is caused by the I- ions. The Li 
deposition of ZnI2 additive (Fig. 4d, e and S12) presents large planar 
morphology, which is the result of the compact LiZn and smooth SEI 
morphology with the help of the alloy hybrid interface (Fig. 4f).

electrolyte because of the special LiI and LiZn alloy layer.

3.4. Effect of additives on the sulfur cathode
The influence of base, LiI, and ZnI2 additive electrolytes on the 
electrochemical performance of sulfur cathode was explored in more 
detail. The surface chemistry of S cathode after 5 cycles was detected by 
XPS, SEM, and transmission electron microscope (TEM) shown in Fig. 6
and Figures S18–23. Based on the SEM and TEM image in 
Figures S18–20, sulfur was uniformly distributed on the surface of CNT 
with the diameter of 50 nm. The morphology of S and Li2S after 5 cycles 
were compared (Fig. 6a-b, Figure S22, and Figure S25) and the major 
differences of S cathode morphology was observed for various electro­
lyte. For base electrolyte, the S was agglomerated and located in- 
between the CNTs after cycles (Fig. 6a). However, the clustering 
behavior of S and Li2S was remarkably suppressed by ZnI2 additive 
electrolyte in case of STD-1 M LiNO3 0.05 M ZnI2 electrolyte (Fig. 6a-b).
To confirm the reason for the different kinetics of S conversion for 
various electrolyte, the surface chemistry of the S cathode was con­
ducted by XPS and TEM in Fig. 6c-g. The S 2p and Zn 2p spectra present 
a new ZnS peak [54,55] at 163, 165, and 1045 eV for ZnI2 additive 
electrolyte. S 2p spectra in Fig. 6c shows the presence of characteristic 
peaks of thiosulfate (169.1 eV) formed by the oxidation of sulfur species 
in LiPSs [56]. I 3d spectrum can be deconvoluted into two peaks of LiI at 
619.8 eV for ZnI2 additive electrolyte and I- ions at 619 eV for LiI 
electrolyte. From the TEM images in Fig. 6f-g, an ultra-thin ZnS nano­
sheet with (100) crystal face [57] was observed on the surface of CNT 
and amorphous sulfur was absorbed by ZnS. The exposed (100) crystal 
face of ZnS is formed under the force of electric field. ZnS nanosheet 
could absorb the sulfur on the surface of CNT and act as a catalyst to 
promote the reduction of sulfur. Uniform Zn element distribution can be 
observed from the EDS image (Figure S23c) of sulfur cathode for ZnI2 
additive electrolyte. However, characteristic peaks of LiI and ZnS were 
not showed in S 2p, Zn 2p, and I 3d spectrum (Figure S26d, g, and h) for 
Li2S cathode after discharge process. Obvious Li2S peak [58] at 161.8 
and 160.2 eV was presented in S 2p spectra (Figure S25c and d). This 
indicates Li2S was deposited on the top surface of the ZnS nanosheets 
during the discharge process as shown in Fig. 6h.

4. Conclusions
In summary, an optimized base electrolyte (STD-1 M LiNO3) with a 
ZnI2 additive was formulated, and its multifaceted effects in enhancing 
sulfur redox kinetics at the cathode while preserving the stability of the 
Li anode were studied in great detail. On one hand, in-situ generated 
ultra-thin ZnS nanosheets via the reaction between ZnI2 additive and 
LiPSs at the cathode facilitated the redox kinetics of sulfur conversion, 
preventing the shuttle effect. On the other hand, a compact LiZn alloy 
hybrid inorganic-rich (LiI, LiF, LiOH, Li3 N, Li2O, and LiNxOy) interface 
was generated at the anode by the reduction of anion-dominated Li+
solvent sheath as confirmed by AIMD simulations. Moreover, LiZn alloy 
component was identified resulting from the reduction of the Zn2+ ions 
by Li metal. This anode hybrid interface reduces the interface reaction 
resistance and promotes smooth lithium plating/stripping with large 
planar morphology confirmed by in-situ liquid TEM. Benefiting from the 
advantageous synergy, the LSB with ZnI2 additives exhibits impressive 
cycling stability and rate performance with high mass loading under a 
low E/S ratio of 7.5. This research provides a simple and effective route 
to address the issues for both the S cathode and Li anode to obtain high- 
performance LSB.
