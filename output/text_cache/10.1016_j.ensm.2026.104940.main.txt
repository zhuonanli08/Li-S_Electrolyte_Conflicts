A B S T R A C T
To regulate Li2S deposition/stripping-a key step in sulfur reaction kinetics of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, the 
designed and synthesized 6,6′-thio-bis(2,4-di-tert-butylphenol) (TBPS) utilizes the phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) 
groups synergistically strengthened by intramolecular thioether group and tert-butyl group as Lewis acid active 
sites to interact with Li2S. Combined density functional theory (DFT) calculations, in-situ Raman and ultra­
violet–visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy confirm that TBPS promotes the liquid-to-solid conversion from long-chain 
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) to Li2S, guiding the uniformly dispersed formation of a three-dimensional (3D) Li2S 
structure, leading to efficient electronic and ionic transport across the electrode interface. Moreover, the strong 
TBPS-Li2S interaction lowers the oxidation barrier during the solid-to-liquid transformation, improving the 
reversibility of Li2S dissolution and significantly mitigating “dead sulfur” accumulation and cathode surface 
passivation. The assembled cells exhibit an extremely low-capacity decay rate of only 0.038% per cycle at 1 C. 
Even under high sulfur loading (6.6 mg cm−2) and limited electrolyte (E/S = 6.1 μL mg−1), the battery maintains 
a capacity of 501.1 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles at 0.5 C. This study provides a promising strategy for developing 
lean-electrolyte, high-performance Li-S batteries.
1. Introduction
The resurgence of electric vehicles, coupled with the growing inte­
gration of renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power, has 
created an urgent demand for advanced energy storage technologies 
capable of delivering high performance [1–3]. However, current 
lithium-ion battery systems are approaching their intrinsic limits in 
energy density and cycling stability, making them insufficient for 
large-scale storage applications that require both high capacity and 
long-term durability [4,5]. Consequently, developing next-generation 
rechargeable batteries with simultaneously enhanced energy density 
and reliability has become a critical scientific and industrial priority. 
Among the potential alternatives, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries stand 
out due to their exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity (1675 
mAh g−1, with sulfur acting as the active component) and energy density 
(2600 Wh kg−1), which arise from complex multistep and multiphase 
redox reactions involving the exchange of 16 electrons between the 
lithium anode and the sulfur cathode [6–8]. Despite these advantages, 
Li-S systems still face fundamental kinetic and interfacial challenges. 
The sluggish liquid-to-solid conversion from long-chain soluble lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs) (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) to short-chain insoluble Li2S2/Li2S

cesses cause electrode passivation and active material loss, thereby 
accelerating capacity fading and impairing long-term cycling stability. 
Collectively, such kinetic bottlenecks and interfacial degradation phe­
nomena remain major obstacles to the commercialization of Li-S 
batteries.

To effectively address the aforementioned challenges, current 
research efforts primarily follow two strategic directions. The first 
approach focuses on enhancing the kinetics and morphology of Li2S 
deposition through electrolyte and catalytic design. Researchers have 
employed high-donor-number (DN) solvents or salts [17,18], metal 
compound nanoparticles [19,20], and single-atom catalysts [21] to 
facilitate sulfur redox reactions. These materials can either (i) 
strengthen Li+ solvation and promote S2−
dissociation, (ii) provide 
active nucleation centers that guide the isolated growth of Li2S and 
suppress crystal coalescence, or (iii) enhance the electronic conductivity 
of Li2S via strong metal-sulfur interactions. Collectively, these strategies 
accelerate the liquid-to-solid conversion kinetics, regulate Li2S nucle­
ation 
pathways, 
and 
shift 
deposition 
from 
conventional 
two-dimensional (2D) layered growth to a uniform three-dimensional 
(3D) architecture. This transformation mitigates cathode passivation 
and ensures efficient charge transport across the electrode-electrolyte 
interface. The second strategy exploits molecular interactions and 
chemical coordination to facilitate the reversible transformation of Li2S. 
Hydrogen-bond-assisted mechanisms [22,23] involving N-H-S2−
in­
teractions that promote Li2S dissociation and reduce particle aggrega­
tion—have proven effective. In addition, the Lewis acid-base principle 
[24] has been widely applied, where Lewis acid species interact strongly 
with Li2S (a Lewis base) to improve its solubility in the electrolyte. This 
coordination not only mitigates irreversible Li2S accumulation on the 
cathode surface, thereby preventing interface passivation, but also 
lowers the oxidation barrier during the solid-to-liquid reconversion step.
Organic compounds have recently emerged as promising candidates 
for energy storage applications due to their structural versatility, 
elemental diversity, and the tunable chemical reactivity of their func­
tional groups. Among them, hydroxyl (-OH) groups, as polar oxygen- 
containing functionalities, can strongly interact with LiPSs through 
Lewis acid-base coordination between the oxygen lone pairs and Li+ ions 
[25–27]. Qin et al. [28], through density functional theory (DFT) cal­
culations, demonstrated that the phenolic hydroxyl groups in a poly­
dopamine coating exhibit a binding energy of -2.44 eV with Li⁺ from 
Li2S4, indicating a thermodynamically favorable adsorption process. 
The resulting charge redistribution between Li2S4 and the polymer 
matrix stabilizes the adsorbed species and effectively suppresses LiPS 
diffusion toward the lithium anode. Similarly, Li et al. [29] reported that 
hydroxylated hydroxyapatite (HN) nanowires, containing -OH and 
phosphate (P=O) groups, can coordinate with Li+ in LiPSs through Li-O 
interactions. This confines LiPSs within the 3D HN network, signifi­
cantly enhancing their immobilization and accelerating redox kinetics at 
the electrode-electrolyte interface. In another study, Gohari et al. [30] 
found that hydroxyl-functionalized graphene (G-OH) in aqueous Li-S 
batteries engages in Lewis acid-base interactions with LiPSs, effec­
tively trapping them between graphene layers and preventing their 
dissolution in the aqueous electrolyte. The sulfur nanoparticles remain 
uniformly dispersed within the layered structure, and even after 1000 
cycles, no obvious aggregation is observed—demonstrating the stabi­
lizing effect of -OH groups on the LiPS interface. Zhang et al. [31] uti­
lized tannic acid (TA)-modified polypropylene separators containing 
abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups to establish similar Lewis acid-base 
interactions with LiPSs. This modification not only enhanced polysulfide 
adsorption but also improved electrolyte wettability. The assembled 
batteries exhibited smaller redox peak separations and retained 69.5% 
of their capacity after 100 cycles. Despite these advances, 
single-functional organic molecules often fail to simultaneously address 
critical challenges such as polysulfide shuttling and sluggish redox ki­
netics. In contrast, multifunctional organic compounds can leverage the 
synergistic interplay between different active groups, producing a 
cooperative “1 + 1 > 2” enhancement effect. Such molecular systems 
offer a rational pathway to achieve comprehensive performance 
improvement in Li-S batteries.
Based on the above investigation, this study designed and synthe­
sized 6,6′-thio-bis(2,4-di-tert-butylphenol) (TBPS) to achieve dynamic 
control over the Li2S deposition-dissolution process through precise 
alignment of molecular structure and interfacial reactions. The molec­
ular design of TBPS is guided by the synergistic enhancement of Lewis 
acid-base interactions, the p-π conjugation between thioether bonds (-S- 
) and aromatic rings, and the electron-donating effect of tert-butyl 
groups (-C(CH3)3), which collectively increase the electron density of 
phenolic hydroxyl groups (-OH) and renders them highly active Lewis 
acid sites. Consequently, TBPS accomplishes a cooperative optimization 
of “uniform deposition + rapid dissolution”: the highly active -OH 
groups engage in Lewis acid-base interactions with LiPSs. This process 
accelerates the liquid-to-solid transformation of long-chain LiPSs into 
Li2S and promotes the formation of a dispersed 3D Li2S deposition rather 
than a conventional dense 2D insulating layer. Therefore, the electron 
and ion transport at the electrode interface maintains efficient. Simul­
taneously, the strong interaction between TBPS and Li2S reduces the 
energy barrier for dissolution, facilitating the reversible conversion of 
insulating Li2S into soluble LiPSs, which substantially minimizes the 
generation of “dead sulfur” and surface passivation of the cathode. As a 
result, TBPS-containing cells demonstrate outstanding electrochemical 
performance: during long-term cycling at 1 C, the average capacity 
decay per cycle is only 0.051%, while at a high current density of 2 C, the 
cells still deliver a high discharge capacity of 516 mAh g−1. These 
findings provide a novel interfacial regulation strategy and theoretical 
guidance for developing Li-S batteries with both high energy density and 
extended cycle life.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. Synthesis and application of TBPS
As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the liquid-to-solid conversion of long-chain 
soluble LiPSs (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) into short-chain insoluble Li2S represents 
the rate-determining step of the SRR and proceeds very slowly. More­
over, the inherent insolubility and extremely low electronic conductivity 
of Li2S (~10−14 S cm−1) lead to uneven deposition on the electrode 
surface, forming dense insulating layers that severely hinder electron 
and ion transport. This phenomenon further imposes a high oxidation 
barrier for the solid-to-liquid SER, while incompletely converted Li2S 
tends to generate “dead sulfur,” resulting in cathode surface passivation 
and irreversible loss of active material. These effects ultimately cause 
rapid capacity decay and deterioration of cycling stability [32]. To 
address these challenges, this work designs and synthesizes TBPS and 
employs it as an electrolyte additive to improve the deposition and 
dissolution kinetics of Li2S at the cathode interface. Using sulfur 
dichloride (SCl2) and 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol as starting materials, the 
target compound TBPS was successfully synthesized through a 
low-temperature condensation reaction in petroleum ether, followed by 
quenching, washing, concentration, and recrystallization from methanol 
[33] (Fig. S1). The resulting product is a white powder with a molecular 
weight of 648 g mol−1, and its chemical structure and optical photo­
graph are shown in Fig. 1b. Structurally, TBPS consists of two phenol 
rings, each substituted with a tert-butyl group, connected via a sulfur 
atom bridge, and contains two active phenolic hydroxyl groups. To 
systematically characterize the chemical structure and electronic prop­
erties of TBPS, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, proton 
nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), and DFT calculations were 
performed. As shown in Fig. 1c, the FT-IR spectrum exhibits character­
istic absorption peaks: the peak at 3413.1 cm−1 corresponds to the 
stretching vibration of phenolic hydroxyl groups (-OH). Although this 
vibration typically appears as a broad peak, a narrower profile was 
observed here, likely due to weaker intermolecular hydrogen bonding in 
the solid powder [34,35]. Absorption peaks in the 3100−3000 cm−1 
range are attributed to aromatic C-H stretching, while peaks at 2869.1 
cm−1 and 2958.2 cm−1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric 
C-H stretching of methyl groups in the tert-butyl substituents, confirm­
ing the presence of both aromatic and alkyl C-H bonds [36]. The

To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the electronic 
structure and reactivity of TBPS, its chemical characteristics were 
analyzed through DFT calculations, and the corresponding results are 
illustrated in Fig. 1e−f. The electrostatic potential (ESP) of TBPS was 
mapped onto the 0.0002 isodensity surface (Fig. 1e). This provides a 
visual representation of the molecular surface charge distribution and 
the electrophilic/nucleophilic characteristics of reactive sites [39]. In 
the ESP map, the blue-colored zones denote regions of low potential, 
corresponding to high electron density and strongly electronegative 
atomic sites, whereas the red-colored areas signify regions of higher 
potential, indicative of reduced electron density and less electronegative 
environments. The electron-donating effect of the tert-butyl groups (-C 
(CH3)3), the p-π conjugation between the sulfur atom and aromatic 
rings, and the high electronegativity of the oxygen atoms in the phenolic 
hydroxyls collectively enrich the electron density around the -OH 
groups. This enhancement of Lewis acidity renders the phenolic hy­
droxyls the most reactive sites in the molecule and favors their partici­
pation in relevant chemical reactions within the electrolyte. Frontier 
molecular orbital analysis further elucidated the electronic structure of 
TBPS [40], with green and blue regions indicating positive and negative 
orbital phases, respectively (Fig. 1f). The electron delocalization across 
the aromatic rings and phenolic hydroxyl groups highlights potential 
sites for electron transfer or bond formation. The thioether linkage 
serves as a molecular bridge in maintaining the symmetrical orientation 
of the two tert-butylphenol rings and ensuring the presence of dual 
phenolic hydroxyl active sites. This structural feature enables bifunc­
tional coordination and anchoring of LiPSs, and thereby enhances the 
molecule’s interaction capability within the system. Furthermore, the 
thioether bond forms a p–π conjugated system with the aromatic rings, 
which not only facilitates electron delocalization toward the phenolic 
hydroxyl groups to stabilize their electron-rich state and enhance Lewis 
acidity, but also improves the overall electronic structural stability of 
the molecule. The energy gap between the highest occupied molecular 
orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of 
TBPS was calculated to be 4.77 eV, which is smaller than that of the 
electrolyte solvents and lithium salts, indicating higher chemical reac­
tivity and a greater propensity for electron transfer or coordination re­
actions [41].
To investigate the effect of TBPS on the electrochemical performance 
of Li-S batteries, TBPS was introduced as an electrolyte additive, while 
cells with unmodified electrolyte served as the blank control. Coin-type 
Li-S batteries were assembled, and systematic electrochemical tests were 
conducted. As shown in Fig. S2, the blank electrolyte appeared colorless 
and transparent, whereas the electrolyte containing TBPS exhibited a 
pale yellow hue, consistent with the inherent optical properties of the 
TBPS molecule. Considering that additive concentration typically has a 
significant impact on battery performance, the optimal TBPS concen­
tration was first determined through charge-discharge cycling tests. 
Results in Fig. S3 indicate that a concentration of 30 mM provides the 
best overall performance, and all subsequent electrochemical measure­
ments were conducted at this optimal concentration. At a current den­
sity of 0.2 C, the discharge curves of the Li–S cells display two distinct 
voltage plateaus (Fig. 1g), which correspond to the sequential two-step 
reduction process of sulfur species: the high-voltage plateau (QH) re­
flects the dissolution of elemental sulfur (S8) and its reduction to soluble 
LiPSs (Li2Sx, 4 ≤x≤8), while the low-voltage plateau (QL) corresponds 
to the further reduction of LiPSs into insoluble Li2S2/Li2S [42,43]. 
Fig. 1h compares the capacities of the high and low discharge plateaus as 
well as their ratio (QL/QH) for the blank and TBPS-containing cells. 
Introduction of TBPS significantly enhances both discharge stages, with 
QH increasing from 276.6 to 317.5 mAh g−1 and QL rising from 584.2 to 
747.6 mAh g−1. The capacity ratio QL/QH also increases from 2.11 for 
the blank to 2.35 for the TBPS group. These results indicate that TBPS 
not only facilitates the initial dissolution and reduction of S8 (high 
plateau) but also markedly improves the deep reduction kinetics of LiPSs 
(low plateau), thereby enhancing the overall utilization of active sulfur 
and reducing irreversible polysulfide loss. Fig. 1i shows long-term 
cycling performance of the blank and TBPS-containing cells at 0.2 C. 
The TBPS-containing cells exhibit higher discharge capacities, improved 
cycling stability, and consistently high Coulombic efficiency (CE). At the 
beginning of cycling, the initial discharge capacity of the TBPS group is 
significantly higher than that of the blank. After 100 cycles, the TBPS 
cells retain a discharge capacity of 961.7 mAh g−1, compared with only 
464.1 mAh g−1 for the blank cells. The high CE throughout cycling in­
dicates that TBPS effectively suppresses polysulfide shuttling, reducing 
lithium anode corrosion and active material loss, thereby improving 
cycling stability. To confirm that the performance enhancement origi­
nates from TBPS’s regulation of sulfur electrode reactions rather than its 
own redox contribution, a control experiment was performed using a 
blank cell without active sulfur (S8), and its charge-discharge behavior 
was measured (Fig. S4). The results show that the sulfur-free cell de­
livers only negligible capacity, indicating that the redox activity of TBPS 
itself contributes minimally to the overall battery capacity. This further 
confirms that TBPS acts as an effective electrolyte additive to optimize 
the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries. Overall, the Li-S cells 
containing TBPS exhibit significantly higher discharge capacities and 
longer cycle life compared to the blank cells. The increased QL/QH ratio 
indicates that TBPS enhances both the extent and efficiency of LiPSs 
conversion. Furthermore, the excellent capacity retention and low decay 
rate demonstrate that TBPS effectively suppresses polysulfide shuttling 
and the formation of “dead sulfur,” thereby minimizing the loss of active 
material. To elucidate the underlying mechanism of TBPS, the subse­
quent discussion will systematically analyze its effects from two key 
perspectives: the liquid-to-solid conversion of LiPSs and the 
solid-to-liquid dissolution of Li2S, providing insight into the intrinsic 
reasons for the observed improvements in battery performance.
2.2. TBPS accelerates liquid-to-solid conversion kinetics to achieve 3D 
Li2S deposition
To visually elucidate the inhibitory effect of TBPS on LiPSs migration 
and its regulatory role in Li2S deposition, this study employed H-type 
transparent cells combined with targeted electrochemical measure­
ments and morphological characterization to investigate its action from 
macroscopic phenomena to microscopic mechanisms. Both blank and 
TBPS-containing H-type cells were assembled, enabling real-time in situ 
observation of the dynamic evolution of LiPSs near the working elec­
trode and direct monitoring of their dissolution, migration, and con­
version behaviors. During Li-S battery operation, high-order LiPS 
intermediates exhibit good solubility in the electrolyte, and the solution 
color changes visibly with concentration. Therefore, the electrolyte 
color serves as a qualitative indicator of LiPSs dissolution and adsorp­
tion/conversion efficiency. Fig. 2a shows the initial discharge curves of 
the two transparent cells, and Fig. 2b presents optical photographs at 
different discharge stages. In the blank cell, the electrolyte turns bright 
yellow upon discharging to 2.3 V, indicating the partial reduction of S8 
to soluble LiPSs. As discharge continues to 2.1 V, the solution darkens 
further. Under the combined effects of the concentration gradient and 
electric field, a substantial portion of LiPSs migrates toward the lithium 
anode, causing the electrolyte near the negative electrode to also appear 
bright yellow, directly confirming the occurrence of the polysulfide 
“shuttle effect”. In contrast, in the TBPS-containing cell, LiPS migration 
is significantly suppressed throughout discharge. Optical images corre­
sponding to 2.3 V and 2.1 V show that LiPSs remain concentrated near 
the sulfur cathode, with the local electrolyte gradually deepening to 
bright yellow, while the electrolyte near the lithium anode retains its 
initial pale yellow without noticeable color change. This observation 
directly confirms that TBPS effectively anchors LiPSs near the cathode, 
mitigating their migration toward the anode, thereby alleviating the 
shuttle effect and reducing irreversible loss of active material. After

To systematically quantify the enhancement of electrochemical re­
action kinetics by TBPS in Li-S batteries and elucidate its molecular-level 
interactions with LiPSs, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was first employed to 
characterize reaction kinetics and reversibility. As shown in Fig. 3a, the 
CV curves of the TBPS-containing cells exhibit excellent overlap over 
multiple cycles, demonstrating that TBPS significantly improves the 
electrochemical reversibility and cycling stability of Li-S batteries. As 
the number of cycles increases, a small amount of Li₂S tends to accu­
mulate slightly at the cathode interface and partially covers the active 
sites on the cathode surface. This coverage reduces the effective contact 
area between S₂²⁻ species and the electrode/electrolyte, which thereby 
lowers the effective reaction rate and results in the observed shift of Peak 
III. A direct comparison of the CV curves in Fig. 3b indicates that the 
TBPS-containing cells not only display higher peak currents but also a 
narrower redox peak separation (ΔV: TBPS = 289 mV vs. without TBPS 
= 339 mV). The increase in peak current reflects accelerated electro­
chemical reaction rates, while the reduced peak separation indicates 
diminished polarization of the sulfur redox reactions. Together, these 
results confirm that TBPS effectively optimizes the electrochemical ki­
netics of the battery. To further quantify the kinetic characteristics of 
sulfur redox reactions, Tafel slope measurements were conducted for 
both cell types (Fig. 3c−d). In the SRR, the TBPS-containing cells exhibit 
a Tafel slope of 62.1 mV dec−1, significantly lower than the 80.1 mV 
dec−1 observed for the blank cells. For the sulfur oxidation reaction, the 
Tafel slope of the TBPS cells is 57.5 mV dec−1, compared with 113.4 mV 
dec−1 for the blank. Since Tafel slope is inversely correlated with elec­
trochemical reaction kinetics, smaller slopes indicate lower activation 
energies and superior kinetics. These results confirm that TBPS simul­
taneously accelerates both sulfur reduction and oxidation processes, 
enhancing the utilization of active sulfur and lowering the reaction 
activation energy.
In Li-S batteries, the diffusion and transport efficiency of Li+ is a 
critical factor limiting the kinetics of sulfur redox reactions. When re­
action kinetics are sluggish, LiPSs tend to accumulate in the electrolyte, 
increasing its viscosity and further hindering ion transport. To investi­
gate the effect of TBPS on Li+ diffusion, CV measurements were con­
ducted for both cell types over a scan rate range of 0.1−0.5 mV s−1 
(Fig. S7). Using the Randles-Sevcik Equation (1-1), peak currents were 
linearly fitted against the square root of scan rates (Fig. S8) to calculate 
the Li+ diffusion coefficients at different potentials (Table S1). 
Furthermore, the lithium-ion diffusion kinetics were quantitatively 
evaluated using galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT, 
Fig. S9). According to calculations based on Equation (1-2), the Li⁺ 
chemical diffusion coefficient of the TBPS-containing electrode (1.85 ×
10⁻⁸ cm² s⁻¹) was approximately 22 times higher than that of the blank 
sample (8.33 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm² s⁻¹). This suggests that the incorporation of 
TBPS markedly enhances Li⁺ transport kinetics. The results show that

both electrodes composed of identical sulfur-based electrodes. As shown 
in Fig. 3e, in the absence of Li2S6, the CV response is essentially a straight 
line with negligible current, indicating no significant electrochemical 
activity. Upon addition of Li2S6, the blank cells show low response 
currents without clear redox peaks. In contrast, the TBPS-containing 
cells display highly symmetric CV curves with two well-defined pairs 
of oxidation and reduction peaks. This behavior arises from the inter­
action between the oxygen atoms in the phenolic hydroxyls of TBPS and 
Li⁺ in LiPSs via Lewis acid-base interactions, forming Li-O bonds that 
construct efficient electron-transfer pathways. This accelerates the 
redox kinetics of LiPSs, increasing both peak current and area, and 
demonstrating pronounced electrocatalytic activity. Even at a high scan 
rate of 50 mV s−1 (Fig. 3f), the TBPS-containing cells maintain clear 
redox peaks, highlighting their excellent stability and rapid reaction 
kinetics.
To track the redox evolution of LiPSs under TBPS regulation at the 
molecular level, in-situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to dynami­
cally characterize the process, capturing structural changes of active 
species during discharge and elucidating the interaction mechanism 
between TBPS and LiPSs. Fig. 3g shows the schematic of the in situ 
Raman setup, in which a customized coin cell is coupled with a Raman 
spectrometer, enabling real-time monitoring of TBPS and LiPS species in 
the catholyte during the first charge-discharge cycle. Comparison of the 
Raman spectra of blank and TBPS-containing cells (Fig. S10) reveals 
characteristic peaks at 741.2, 1041.4, and 1332.6 cm−1 in the TBPS- 
containing group, corresponding to the Ar-S-Ar, -OH, and -C(CH3)3 vi­
brations of the TBPS molecule [48–51]. Among these, the 1041.4 cm−1 
-OH stretching peak serves as the key monitoring signal, with its in­
tensity directly reflecting the interaction state between TBPS and LiPSs. 
Fig. 3h shows the variation in -OH peak intensity at different potentials 
during the first discharge, while the full spectra in Fig. S11 provide 
supporting evidence. At the initial high-voltage plateau (2.54−2.16 V), 
the -OH peak is clearly observed, indicating that TBPS molecules exist in 
a free state. Concurrently, Raman signals at 172 and 487 cm−1 (Fig. 3i) 
indicate the formation of high-order Li2S8, confirming the initial 
reduction of S8 to long-chain LiPSs [52,53]. As discharge proceeds to 
2.06 V, characteristic peaks of short-chain soluble LiPSs (e.g., Li2S4, 
Li2S3) gradually appear, while the -OH peak intensity decreases 
continuously. When the voltage drops to 1.98 V, the S2
2−peak at 201 
cm−1 emerges, accompanied by a marked reduction in the intensity of 
long-chain LiPS and -OH peaks. This indicates that TBPS coordinates 
with long-chain LiPSs via its phenolic hydroxyl groups, facilitating their 
co-reduction to form insoluble TBPS-Li2S, which deposits on the elec­
trode surface, significantly depleting free TBPS and long-chain LiPSs in 
the electrolyte. In contrast, the blank cell (Figs. S12, S13) exhibits slow 
attenuation of long-chain LiPS peaks during discharge, with substantial 
long-chain LiPSs remaining in the electrolyte at the end of discharge and 
weak S2
2−peaks, indicating incomplete reduction and sluggish kinetics, 
leading to lower Li2S deposition. The comparison highlights that TBPS, 
through Lewis acid-base interactions with LiPSs, accelerates their 
gradient conversion and promotes complete reduction to Li2S, thereby 
enhancing reaction kinetics and improving the utilization of active 
sulfur.
Overall, the hydroxyl (–OH) groups in TBPS act as highly active 
Lewis acid sites. They facilitate the formation of stable Li–O coordina­
tion bonds with Li⁺ ions in LiPSs and thereby achieve effective anchoring 
of the LiPS species. Due to the uniform dispersion of TBPS throughout 
the electrolyte, its phenolic hydroxyl groups provide a multitude of 
evenly distributed nucleation sites on the electrode surface. These sites 
capture long-chain LiPS molecules, which are subsequently reduced to 
Li₂S nuclei. The homogeneous nucleation effectively disrupts localized 
deposition and promotes the transition toward a 3D growth mode. 
Furthermore, the bulky tert-butyl substituents surrounding TBPS intro­
duce steric hindrance that inhibits lateral coalescence of adjacent nuclei. 
As a result, the Li₂S nuclei are spatially confined to grow preferentially in 
the vertical direction and ultimately form a uniformly distributed 3D 
columnar deposition structure (Fig. 3j).

Lewis acid-base interactions with LiPSs, forming Li-O bonds and estab­

decomposition of Li2S during charging, significantly lowering the 
decomposition energy barrier, improving charging kinetics, and thereby 
reducing overpotential while enhancing energy efficiency.

To visually verify the promoting effect of the additive TBPS on the 
dissolution of the discharge end product Li₂S, a series of systematic 
control experiments were designed and conducted (Fig. S17). The 
verification was based on the reaction between Li₂S and elemental sulfur 
to form soluble LiPSs, which could be evidenced by changes in solution 
color and UV–Vis spectra. Equal amounts of Li₂S powder were added to 
electrolytes with and without TBPS. After standing for 1 h, the TBPS- 
containing group exhibited markedly fewer undissolved solids, and 
the solution color changed from pale yellow to yellow, whereas no 
obvious change was observed in the blank sample. This observation 
preliminarily indicates that TBPS facilitates Li₂S dissolution. The post- 
reaction solutions were analyzed using UV–Vis spectroscopy. The re­

pronounced absorption peaks at wavelengths corresponding to poly­
sulfides (S₄²⁻ + S₄²⁻) (Fig. 4d), whereas no such signals were observed in

dissolution of Li₂S and its subsequent conversion into soluble poly­
sulfides. Comparative SEM analyses of the sulfur cathodes and lithium 
anodes after 100 cycles further elucidated these effects. In the blank 
sample, extensive loss of long-chain LiPSs from the cathode led to active 
material depletion, while Li₂S deposited as a dense 2D layer. The 
resulting volumetric changes induced stress concentration and caused 
surface roughening and cracking of the cathode (Fig. S18a). On the 
anode, the reaction between LiPSs with lithium metal disrupted the solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) and formed a thick brittle layer with cracked 
surface (Fig. S18c). Additionally, LiPS corrosion coupled with lithium 
dendrite growth caused severe anode expansion to about 128 μm 
(Fig. S18e). In contrast, TBPS enables coordination between its phenolic 
hydroxyl functionalities and LiPSs, which promotes rapid polysulfide 
conversion and limits their depletion. The presence of bulky tert-butyl 
groups steered Li₂S to deposit in a 3D dispersed manner, effectively 
distributing volumetric stress and ensuring the cathode remained dense 
and uniform post-cycling (Fig. S18b). TBPS also mitigated LiPSs shut­
tling, reduced side reactions, stabilized the SEI, and promoted uniform 
lithium deposition, resulting in a smooth anode surface with signifi­
cantly reduced expansion (thickness only 86 μm, Fig. S18d−f). Collec­
tively, these results demonstrate that TBPS enhances the Li₂S dissolution 
and LiPSs conversion, suppresses lithium polysulfide shuttling, and al­
leviates interfacial passivation, thereby contributing to improved elec­
trochemical stability.

that the characteristic peaks of long-chain LiPSs remained sharp 
throughout charging, with a large amount of unconverted long-chain 
LiPSs remaining in the electrolyte after charging, indicating poor 
redox reversibility. The dynamic monitoring results from in situ Raman 
and UV-Vis spectroscopy form a closed-loop verification: TBPS mole­
cules interact with LiPSs via the phenolic -OH groups through Lewis 
acid-base coordination to form Li-O bonds. During discharge, long-chain 
LiPSs are rapidly reduced to insoluble TBPS-Li2S complexes and 
deposited, significantly improving the completeness of LiPSs conver­
sion. During charging, these complexes re-dissolve and reversibly 
convert back to long-chain LiPSs, while TBPS molecules are regenerated 
and can continue to mediate the process. This mechanism not only ac­
celerates the liquid-to-solid conversion kinetics of LiPSs but also effec­
tively lowers the oxidation barrier for Li2S solid-to-liquid conversion. In 
contrast, the blank group lacks this synergistic regulation, resulting in 
slow LiPSs transformation, severe residual accumulation, and poor 
reversibility. Collectively, the in situ characterization results, together 
with prior electrochemical measurements, H-type visualization experi­
ments, and DFT calculations, offer a detailed molecular-level insight into 
the mechanisms by which TBPS modulates the redox behavior of LiPSs. 
TBPS optimizes Li-S battery reaction kinetics through a triple synergistic 
mechanism (Fig. 5g−h): (i) catalyzing polysulfide redox conversion to 
accelerate liquid-to-solid kinetics; (ii) promoting the transformation of 
Li2S deposition from 2D dense layers to 3D dispersed structures, 
enhancing the availability of reactive sites; (iii) improving Li2S solubility 
to mitigate cathode surface passivation. The combined effects of these 
mechanisms lead to comprehensive enhancements in battery capacity, 
cycling stability, and kinetic performance.
2.4. Effect of TBPS on the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries
To evaluate the effect of TBPS on the rate capability of Li-S batteries, 
the electrochemical performance of cells with and without TBPS was 
tested over a wide current density range of 0.1−2 C (Fig. 6a). The results 
indicate that TBPS significantly enhances the discharge capacity across 
all tested rates: at low rates (0.1 C), the TBPS-containing cells exhibit 
substantially higher capacities than the blank cells; even at a high rate of 
2 C, the TBPS group maintains a higher effective capacity, demon­
strating superior rate tolerance. When the current density is returned to 
0.2 C, the TBPS-added cells recover a discharge capacity of 783.1 mAh 
g−1 with a capacity retention of 91.1%, compared to 82.9% for the blank 
cells, confirming that TBPS effectively mitigates capacity decay during 
rate switching and improves both structural stability and reaction 
reversibility. Charge-discharge curves at different current densities for 
the two groups are shown in Figs. 6b and S21. Both cells display 
increased polarization with rising current, consistent with slower reac­
tion kinetics and higher internal resistance at high rates. Notably, even 
at 2 C, the TBPS-added cells retain well-defined sulfur redox plateaus, 
whereas the blank cells show significantly compressed plateaus, further 
confirming the accelerated sulfur redox kinetics induced by TBPS. 
Analysis of QH and QL discharge capacities at each rate (Fig. 6c) reveals 
that TBPS improves capacity output and conversion efficiency in both 
stages, demonstrating its catalytic promotion of the full sulfur redox 
process. To clarify the mechanism underlying TBPS’s performance 
enhancement from the perspective of interfacial charge transfer and ion 
diffusion, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted 
before cycling and after 100 cycles (Fig. 6d). Before cycling, the Nyquist 
plots feature a high-frequency semicircle corresponding to the charge- 
transfer resistance (Rct) and a low-frequency inclined line represent­
ing lithium-ion diffusion resistance. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6e) 
shows that TBPS reduces both Rct and ionic resistance compared to the 
blank cells, attributed to TBPS coordinating with LiPSs to accelerate 
electron transfer and optimize the ionic transport environment. After

To evaluate the impact of TBPS on long-term cycling stability, 420- 
cycle tests were conducted at a high current density of 1 C (Fig. 6f). 
The results show that the TBPS-added cells deliver an initial discharge 
capacity of 817.6 mAh g−1, with an average capacity decay rate of only 
0.051% per cycle and an average CE of 98.3%, demonstrating excellent 
capacity retention and interfacial compatibility. In contrast, the blank 
cells exhibit a lower initial discharge capacity of 665.1 mAh g−1, rapid 
capacity fading, and a marked decrease in CE after 120 cycles, with an 
average CE of only 90.2%, primarily due to active material loss and 
lithium anode corrosion caused by polysulfide shuttling. To assess the 
practical applicability of TBPS, cells were tested under high sulfur 
loading (6.6 mg cm−2), lean electrolyte conditions (E/S = 6.1 μL mg−1), 
and a current density of 0.5 C (Fig. 6g). These conditions better reflect 
realistic battery requirements, where high sulfur loading enhances en­
ergy density and lean electrolyte reduces cost. Under these conditions, 
TBPS-added cells deliver a first-cycle discharge capacity of 645.4 mAh 
g−1 and retain 501.1 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles, corresponding to a 
capacity retention of 77.6%. In contrast, the blank cells suffer rapid 
capacity decay, retaining only 19.1% after 300 cycles. Further com­
parison under high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte conditions at 1 C 
(Fig. S22) revealed that, at the initial stage, insufficient electrolyte 
wetting of the dense sulfur cathode led to limited electrochemical acti­
vation. As cycling continued, gradual electrolyte infiltration reached the 
electrode core, triggering the conversion of unreacted sulfur, which 
enhanced active material utilization and steadily increased the specific 
capacity. In the blank cell, extensive LiPSs shuttling and parasitic re­
actions resulted in irreversible loss of active material, while continuous 
side reactions consumed both the electrolyte and active lithium. This 
leads to pronounced fluctuations in capacity and CE. By contrast, the 
TBPS-modified cell accelerated the conversion of polysulfides and 
significantly mitigated LiPSs shuttling, guaranteeing efficient and 
reversible use of the active species. Moreover, the reduced occurrence of 
side reactions contributed to stable specific capacity and consistent CE 
throughout cycling. This demonstrates that TBPS can continuously 
promote polysulfide conversion and suppress active material loss under 
stringent practical conditions, significantly improving the practical 
viability of Li-S batteries. Performance comparison using a radar chart 
(Fig. 6h) shows that TBPS-added cells outperform blank cells across key 
metrics, including specific capacity, cycling stability, rate capability, 
and CE. Further comparison with recently reported Li-S electrolyte ad­

stability and capacity retention even under high sulfur loading and lean 
electrolyte conditions. This advantage originates from TBPS’s dual

3. Conclusion
In this work, the molecular design and synthesis of TBPS enabled the 
synergistic optimization of LiPS conversion kinetics and Li2S deposition 
behavior in Li-S batteries. Through DFT calculations, in situ Raman 
spectroscopy, and in situ UV-Vis spectroscopy, the core functional 
mechanism of TBPS was elucidated: the phenolic hydroxyl groups in its 
molecular structure interact with LiPSs via Lewis acid-base interactions, 
forming stable TBPS-LiPS intermediates. This interaction not only ac­
celerates the liquid-to-solid conversion of long-chain LiPSs into short- 
chain Li2S but also provides ordered nucleation sites for Li2S, guiding 
the formation of uniformly dispersed 3D columnar deposits on the 
electrode surface and ensuring efficient electron/ion transport at the 
electrode interface. During charging, the TBPS-Li2S complex effectively 
lowers the energy barrier for Li2S dissolution, facilitating its reversible 
conversion into soluble polysulfides and significantly mitigating the 
accumulation of insulating Li2S on the cathode surface. Electrochemical 
tests demonstrate that the incorporation of TBPS endows Li-S batteries 
with outstanding overall performance: at a current density of 1 C, the 
first-cycle discharge capacity reaches 817.6 mAh g−1, with a capacity 
retention of 78.6% after 420 cycles. Under demanding conditions of 
high sulfur loading (6.6 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte (E/S = 6.1 μL 
mg−1), the discharge capacity remains at 501.1 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles 
at 0.5 C, highlighting excellent high-loading adaptability and long-term 
cycling stability. This study provides valuable theoretical insights and 
practical guidance for the development of high-energy-density, long- 
lifespan energy storage systems.
