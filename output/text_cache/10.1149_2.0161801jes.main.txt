Li-S Cells with Lithium Metal Anodes, Prelithiated Silicon Anodes
and Hard Carbon Anodes

Li-S cells can have high gravimetric energy densities above 300 Wh kg−1 when the electrodes and cell components are optimized.
Low electrolyte/sulfur mass ratios or more generallly, the relative amount of electrolyte in a Li-S cell have an especially high impact
on the achievable gravimetric energy density. A negative side effect of low electrolyte/sulfur ratios are low cycle numbers due to
electrolyte decomposition and the possibility that electrolyte becomes inaccessible at the lithium metal anode when the lithium
becomes more and more porous during cycling. Electrode thickness measurements were performed during cycling for various
cell chemistries such as lithium-sulfur (Li-S) with different cathode sulfur loadings and porosities, lithium-hard carbon (Li-HC),
lithium-silicon (Li-Si), prelithiated HC-sulfur (LiHC-S), prelithiated Si-sulfur (LiSi-S) and Li-ion. The thickness measurements
provided information about mechanical stress and irreversible thickness changes. The thickness measurements also helped to explain
different electrolyte decomposition behavior and they can be used to discuss the impact of thickness changes on gas analysis.
The electrolyte decomposition of the Li-S standard electrolyte based on lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in
dimethoxyethane (DME): dioxolane (DIOX) with LiNO3 was examined by online mass spectrometry (MS) within Li-S, Li-HC,
Li-Si, LiSi-S and LiHC-S cells. Several electrolyte decomposition products were veriﬁed by post-mortem gas chromatography (GC)
and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) of Li-S cells with cycled electrolyte.

Li-S prototypes demonstrate that high gravimetric energy densi-
ties of 300 Wh/kg and above can be obtained. Possible strategies to
optimize future sulfur cells are the reduction of the weight amount
of passive components e.g. by decreasing the thickness of separator
and current collectors and/or the application of thin perforated cur-
rent collectors. Additionally with lithium metal being conductive, the
copper current collector can be removed completely providing high
energy densities despite low sulfur loaded (1–3 mg cm−2) cathodes.
By utilizing a copper current collector high sulfur load cathodes (>5
mg cm−2) are required to compensate for the copper’s passive weight.1
Thick, high load sulfur cathodes reduce the electrode and separator
coating length in a cell and therefore save costs. However high load
sulfur cathodes also have the drawback that high transported capac-
ities during cycling stress the lithium metal anode and increase the
chance of lithium induced shorts2 (next to the costs of the copper).
Despite all this, the electrolyte is a major weight source
in Li-S cells even if the electrolyte/sulfur weight ratio (E/S)
is low. With the Li-S standard electrolyte based on lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in dimethoxyethane
(DME): dioxolane (DOL) with LiNO3 additive, the lowest obtainable
E/S ratio is likely >3:1. This is due to the high porosity of the sulfur
cathode (usually ∼60–80%) which has to be ﬁlled with electrolyte and
because of the reaction mechanism. With the standard electrolyte ac-
tive sulfur is transported out of the cathode in the form of polysulﬁdes
which have a high impact on the electrolytes viscosity and resistance.
If the polysulﬁde concentration is too high the discharge typically
stops at or shortly after the voltage dip between higher and lower
voltage plateau when the electrolyte resistance shows its maximum.3
The total electrolyte amount and the E/S ratio have a high impact
on the obtainable cycle number. A low E/S is the basic requirement
to obtain Li-ion level gravimetric energy densities but reduces the
obtainable cycle number to only a few dozen cycles. This is due to
electrolyte decomposition and the fact that the SEI on the lithium

metal anode has to be repaired or enhanced each time fresh lithium
gets deposited or when lithium becomes more and more porous/mossy.
Additionally pores and voids in the lithium metal anode generated
during cycling can trap electrolyte and therefore might require that
the available electrolyte amount be higher in aged cells compared to
fresh assembled cells.
To increase the obtainable Li-S cycle number the electrolyte must
be as stable as possible and the cell setup and electrolyte must enable
a smooth, dense lithium deposition. The quality of the lithium depo-
sition and the impact of sulfur or Li2S deposition can be monitored
by thickness measurements. Post-mortem thickness measurements of
the electrodes can provide ﬁrst insights.4 The comparison of in-situ
thickness measurements for various charge and discharge C-rates5 in
combination with post-mortem SEM images5,6 proved that the applied
current has a major impact on the growth of the lithium metal anode
and sulfur cathode.
With the Li-S standard electrolyte there are already several re-
ports illustrating that DME:DOL and LiNO3 are not an ideal choice:
Schneider et al. illustrated severe gas creation in Li-S pouch cells and
documented that initial cycles show especially high relative amounts
of hydrogen and nitrogen with the addition of methane and ethane in
later cycles.7 Jozwiuk et al. examined the impact of LiNO3 in Li-S
cells by applying DEMS and isotope labelled LiNO3 and proved N2
and N2O as corresponding products next to H2 and CH4.8
With the innovative substitution of lithium metal with (hard) car-
bon anodes the obtainable cycle number could be increased to >1000
cycles proving that the choice of the anode has a huge impact on the
cell properties9 and that lithium metal is indeed a hard nut to crack.
Li-S cells with (hard) carbon anodes are no longer a high energy den-
sity system (compare Fig. 1) but might be an interesting combination
for low cost cells made from abundant material, suitable for stationary
storage. Such an electrode combination might even be more promising
when strategic lithium is substituted with abundant sodium.10
The strength of silicon anodes compared to lithium is the higher
volumetric energy density in a full cell at the cost of a lower cell
voltage and reduced gravimetric energy density.

The aim of this work is to provide deeper insights into elec-
trolyte decomposition in Li-S cells with standard electrolyte depen-
dent whether a lithium metal, a prelithiated silicon or a prelithiated
carbon anode were applied. To examine the electrolyte decomposi-
tion online mass spectrometry during cycling was performed. The role
of the popular LiNO3 additive was examined by testing electrolytes
with and without LiNO3. With thickness measurements of monolayer
electrode pouch cells during cycling, information about the thickness
change dependent on the state-of charge, current, choice of anode and
cycle time were obtained.
Experimental
Electrode preparation.—Hard carbon, carbon nanoﬁbers (CNFs,
Showadenko) and C65 (Timcal) powder were initially added to a glass
vial with six 8 mm diameter ceramic mixing balls and subsequently
mixed on a roller mixer (Alfred Schwinherr KG, MR290) for one hour.
After roll mixing, the powders and mixing balls were transferred to a
planetary mix container and 3.5 ml of deionized water was added. The
carbon-water mixture was then mixed in a planetary mixer (Intertron-
ics Thinky ARE-250 Mixer) for 5 minutes at 500 rpm. After this initial
mixing, the PSBR100 (Targray) binder was added and the slurry was
mixed an additional two times for 5 minutes at 500 rpm. The anode
slurry was coated onto a 10 μm thick copper ﬁlm via doctor blade
coating (Zehntner Testing Instruments ZAA2300). The doctor blade
was set to a height of 700 μm and the slurry was coated in a single
pass at a coating rate of 1 mm s−1. Upon completion of the coating
pass, the coating plate was heated to 90◦C and the anode was dried for
15 minutes. This coating procedure resulted in a uniform coating area
of approximately 10 × 30 cm with a coating thickness in the range of
500–600 μm. The electrode composition was 92% hard carbon, 2.5%
CNF, 2.5% C65, 3% PSBR binder.
The water based sulfur cathodes were prepared as described above
with 60% sulfur, 30% Ketjen Black EC-600 JD (Akzonobel) and
10% PSBR100 (Targray) binder. The resulting cathodes had a coating
thickness in the range of 130–150 μm and an average sulfur loading
of 4.8 mg cm−2 ± 0.2 mg cm−2.
Solvent-free sulfur cathodes were prepared by hot pressing 60%
sulfur, 30% Ketjen Black EC-600 JD (Akzonobel) and 10% PEO
binder (Acros Organics) on a carbon coated aluminum current
collector.
Si Anodes (with 100% silicon - binder and carbon free) were de-
posited at Fraunhofer FEP in a semi industrial pilot machine (novoFlex
600) by an industry proven vacuum thin ﬁlm roll-to-roll process. Dou-
ble sided coating of copper foils (6 μm thickness) with 3 μm highly
porous Si layer at a deposition width of up to 300 mm was successfully
demonstrated. The Si layers have a columnar structure (Supporting
Section Fig. S1). The Si cones have a diameter of approximately 3.0
μm and a height corresponding to the electrode thickness of around
6 μm. The Si surface area was estimated to be roughly 0.3 m2/g. For
the online mass spectrometry and thickness measurements single side
coated Si anodes with 5.7 μm coating thickness and ∼1.3 mg cm−2
Si load were applied.
Electrolyte.—As electrolyte 0.7 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL (2:1, v:v)
(BASF) was applied and 0.25 M LiNO3 (ABCR 99.98%; metals ba-
sis) was added if required. Differing salt and/or LiNO3 concentrations
or solvent ratios might change the gaseous or liquid decomposition
products during/after cycling. Qualitatively the decomposition prod-
ucts should be comparable.
Electrode thickness measurements.—Thickness measurements
during cycling were made using a linear potentiometer. A mono-
layer electrode pouch cell with electrode area of 5 × 3 cm was placed
and contacted in a removable platform. An adjustable pressure was
applied on the pouch cell by moving a metal plate through a spindle,
thus reducing the distance to two connected stainless steel springs
(compare Fig. 2a). The applied pressure in this work was ∼10 N
cm−2 electrode area (∼1.02 kg cm−2) resulting in a total pressure of
∼150 N. The measurement error is approximately 1 μm according to
internal tests. With pressure being applied on the whole electrode area
thickness changes induced by gas development in the cell are likely
not to be measured. Gas is being forced in direction of the pouch cell
sealing area.

Online mass spectrometry.—In-situ mass spectrometry was per-
formed using two different mass spectrometer systems. This was done
to ensure consistency and repeatability of results. While there are some
differences in signal responses between the two mass spectrometers,
similar trends in m/z signals for a single test with both MS sys-
tems in place is evidence of a resilient result. The details of the two
mass spectrometer systems are illustrated in the Supporting Section
Fig. S2.
Both MS were connected to the lab test cells with gastight
Swagelok connections (compare Fig. 2b) via heated fused silica cap-
illaries with 50 μm inner diameter. Based on the length, diameter and
pumping strength, the gas ﬂow rate in the capillaries was calculated
to be approximal 100 nl min−1. Consequently the test cells were con-
tinuously monitored enabling maximum test durations between 1–3
weeks until the cells dried out and failed.
The lab test cells were assembled in an argon ﬁlled glove box. All
applied test cells, connections and valves were carefully examined to
be gastight by measuring whether helium can enter the cell.
Multiple Ion Data (MID) acquisition method was employed
throughout this work. The MID scans were normalized to a high
intensity electrolyte background signal to remove the effect of pres-
sure on signal intensity. In addition, all test cells were evacuated for a
24-hour period prior to opening the valve to ensure adequate pressure
stabilization.
Electrochemical characterization.—The cycle station used was a
Basytec CTS-LAB system. Li-S cells were cycled between 1.8 and 2.6
V, Li-S cells with prelitihiated Si anodes were cycled between 1.5 and
2.4 V and Li-S cells with prelitihiated hard carbon (HC) anode were
cycled between 1.0 and 2.6 V. Li-Si half cells were cycled between
0.05 and 0.7 V. Li-HC half cells were cycled between 0.05 and 1.0 V.
The applied currents were 0.5 and 1.0 mA corresponding to 0.64 mA
cm−2 and 1.28 mA cm−2.
Pouch cells for the online thickness measurements were cycled
with C/20 for the ﬁrst discharge. Afterwards the current was increased
to C/10, C/5, C/3 after each ten cycles. Half cells show ﬁve prelithia-
tion cycles and were cycled with a ﬁxed current of 0.64 mA cm−2 and
a constant voltage discharge step stopped at a current of 0.8 mA.

chanical stress. Systems with high volume changes (e.g. Si anodes)
suffer from severe mechanical stress which leads to cracks in the elec-
trode, contact loss to the current collector or to the conductive carbon,
loss of active material, agglomeration of active material and further.

Fig. 3a shows the voltage and corresponding thickness change plot
of a commercial and popular Samsung S7 smartphone cell with Li-
ion intercalation chemistry charged between 3.0 and 4.2 V. As can be
seen the thickness change is very reversible when the applied current
is constant. Higher currents lead to a higher total thickness change
during cycling (C/10: ∼±150 μm, C/5: ∼±160 μm and C/2: ∼±170
μm). With a cell thickness of ∼4.7 mm including pouch foil this
thickness change corresponds to ∼3.4% on the cell level.
Comparing these values with the results of the Li-S chemistry with
three different types of sulfur cathodes (Figs. 3b, 3c, 3d) it can clearly
be seen that the total thickness level is much more unstable. High
porosity sulfur cathodes collapse in the initial cycles during discharge
when sulfur is reduced to polysulﬁdes resulting in a trend that the
total thickness of the electrode stack decreases. Sulfur cathodes with
a lower porosity (Fig. 3c) show a much lower trend of total thickness
decrease for the applied sulfur load and are even stable during the
ﬁrst discharge. In general, Li-S cells have a thickness increase during
charge when lithium is redeposited at the lithium anode and have a
thickness decrease during discharge when lithium is stripped. A thick-
ness increase during discharge because of Li2S formation at the sulfur
cathode is masked by the thickness decrease of the lithium being much
higher in its amount. A visible additional thickness increase because
of sulfur deposition at the sulfur cathode during charge couldn’t be
observed. In-situ XRD2,11 measurements indicate that the formation
of sulfur might begin at an SOC of 60% with sharp diffraction peaks
of crystalline sulfur appearing at 90–95% SOC. Although sheets of
sulfur could have been identiﬁed on charged cathodes by SEM2 it
is likely that these layers are too thin to be measured with the here
applied tool (precision ∼1 μm).
The absolute thickness change during a charge and a discharge
step mainly depends on the sulfur load with 5–6 μm for 2.6 mg cm−2
(Figs. 3b, 3d) and 10–20 μm for 5.5 mg cm−2 (Fig. 3c). Higher ap-
plied currents lead to a faster total thickness increase of the electrode
stack most likely due to the lithium being deposited in more porous
structure. This result is in disagreement with reported measurements
in the literature. As stated by Walu´s et al.5 discharge currents of
C/2 enabled a stable cell thickness during cycling of a 21 Ah cell.
Lower currents of C/10 and especially C/20 led to a signiﬁcant and
fast increase of the overall cell thickness. Post mortem SEM images
proved that cells cycled with low currents (C/10) had thicker lithium
anodes with mossy deposit residuals. At C/2 this deposit residuals
could not have been identiﬁed (C/2) and the overall lithium thickness
was almost unchanged compared to the pristine state. The three sulfur
cathodes examined in this manuscript all had signiﬁcant thickness
increases at elevated currents of C/5 and especially at C/3. Still it has
to be considered that Walu´s et al. applied a sulfone based electrolyte
with pressure free thickness measurement in a commercial type cell
and the here reported measurements were done at ∼1 kg cm−2 with
DME:DOL electrolyte and electrolyte to sulfur mass ratios of ∼7:1.
Very likely the applied electrolyte has the most signiﬁcant impact
on the thickness and especially the lithium deposition. Mikhaylik et
al.12 reported a complete lithium depletion after 60–80 cycles with
DME:DOL based electrolyte. Consequently the sulfone based elec-
trolyte applied by Walu´s et al.5 seems to be a much better choice for
the Li-S system.
Fig. 3e illustrates ﬁve prelithiation cycles of a Li-Si half cell. The
Si anode was a Si thin ﬁlm anode with 100% Si and no conductive
carbon or binder. Compared to graphite/Si composite anodes with
10–30% Si, the 100% Si thin ﬁlm anodes can increase the energy
density even further with volumetric energy densities possibly above
1000 Wh L−1 when combined with high energy Li-ion cathodes. The
Si ﬁlm was coated in a continuous process on a 6 μm Cu foil and
had an average loading of ∼1.4 mg cm−2. The coating thickness
was approximately 6–7 μm. At the initial discharge/lithiation cycle
the thickness increased ∼110 μm during the galvanostatic step and
further ∼70 μm during the subsequent potentiostatic step. In the
ﬁrst charge cycle the thickness decreased ∼3 μm. The potentiostatic
discharge step of the second cycle seems to be the last cycle in which
a signiﬁcant thickness increase during the lithiation of the Si anode
can be observed. In the following cycles the thickness decreased ∼2
μm during discharge and increased ∼4 μm during charge.
Si has a theoretical volume change of ∼300% when being lithi-
ated/delithiated. Therefore it is surprising that the ﬁrst discharge de-
livers a thickness increase of ∼25,000%. Still the measurement seems
to be correct as veriﬁed by an additional thickness measurement with
a digital micrometer screw after the test. When opening the cell in the
glove box to use the prelithiated Si anode for the full cell test (see
Fig. 3f) it could be seen that the Si anode looked like a sieve. The
high mechanical stress during lithiation of the Si anode tore the Cu
current collector apart and led to a macroscopic canyon like structure.
Therefore the huge thickness increase is mainly by this effect and not
by a volume increase of the Si. Lower Si loads or a structure treatment
can possibly prevent or reduce the damage of the Cu current collector
and macroscopic thickness changes. During charge, the cell thickness
stays comparably constant but a small thickness decrease of around
3.5 μm can still be observed in the ﬁrst charge, a decrease of 1.5 μm
for the second charge and a thickness increase for the ﬁfth charge of
2.5 μm. An explanation for this behavior is that the initial thickness
decreases because the delithiation of the Si gets overcompensated by a
thickness increase induced by fresh deposited porous Li. At discharge
a small thickness decrease can be observed in the ﬁrst hours of the
galvanostatic step. During the potentiostatic step the thickness is con-
stant for some hours but at the end of the step a signiﬁcant thickness
increase can be observed, especially for the ﬁrst cycle. A possible and
the most likely explanation is that the mechanical stress leads to a
further extension of the Cu/Si system. Further explanations are that
an additional higher lithiated Li/Si phase leads to further expansion
or that Li gets plated onto the Si anode.
In summary there is a signiﬁcant thickness increase in the initial
cycles but only minor changes can be observed from cycle 3. Therefore
it can be noted that thin ﬁlm Si anodes seem to gain a more stable
state after a huge thickness increase in the ﬁrst cycle. This could mean
that Si thin ﬁlm anodes would also need a formation process in which
they have to be lithiated and be brought into a more stable state.
The LiSi-S full cell measurements illustrated in Fig. 3f show a
signiﬁcant thickness decrease in the initial 10 cycles of around 30 μm.
As discussed before with the Li-Si half cell, this thickness decrease
must be due to macroscopic effects in combination with the Cu current
collector which was transformed from a foil to a sieve/mesh. After
these initial cycles the electrode seems to have equilibrated to the
test conditions and shows a relatively stable thickness development
during cycling. In general the electrode stack thickness decreases
during delithiation of the Si anode and is constant or increases for 1–2
μm during lithiation of the Si anode.
Fig. 3g illustrates the thickness measurement for the prelithiation
cycles of a HC anode vs. Li. The HC anode was designed to have high
areal capacities >7 mAh cm−2 to match the capacity of high sulfur
loading cathodes. In the ﬁrst discharge the thickness is relatively
constant until the voltage drops below 0.25 V. This kind of plateau
cannot be observed in the subsequent cycles leading to the conclusion
that the high irreversible capacity in the ﬁrst cycle is responsible for it
(compare Supporting Section Fig. S2). According to Buiel and Dahn,13
the irreversible capacity in HC anodes is caused by the formation
of the SEI (50 mAh g−1 HC) and by the reaction of lithium with
surface functional groups and absorbed molecules (≥150 mAh g−1
HC) which are a result of exposing the materials to air after the process
of pyrolysis. This stated total ∼200 mAh g−1 irreversible capacity is
in perfect agreement with this measurement. The thickness increasing
impact of the irreversible capacity on the electrode thickness can easily
be illustrated when comparing the thickness decrease of ∼58 μm in
the ﬁrst cycle discharge and ∼92 μm in the second discharge. The
very high total thickness increase of ∼200 μm after only 5 cycles
is suggested to be mainly attributed to Li becoming more and more
porous. With an areal capacity of >7 mAh cm−2 a high amount of

In-situ electrolyte examinations - online mass spectrometry.—
Mass spectrometry can be used to relate the mass/charge (m/z) sig-
nals of electrolyte and electrode decomposition products to the cell
voltage, current and state-of-charge. The primary goal of perform-
ing this analysis was to determine the decomposition behavior of
the electrolyte during cell cycling. A main target is to evaluate and
to benchmark methods, materials and especially electrolytes which
show a lower or ideally no decomposition.
Evaluating MS data is complex when dozens of mass signals are
recorded simultaneously. Many substances/molecules can be identi-
ﬁed at multiple mass fragments and also share speciﬁc mass fragments
with other substances/molecules which can lead to confusion. In the
subsequent ﬁgures only mass fragments were illustrated which are
believed to be signiﬁcant. Cell internal pressure changes are related
to gas evaluation in the cell, changes in electrode stack thickness and
- to a smaller degree - changes of temperature. An overall trend of
pressure decrease is because the cell is being evacuated slowly by
the MS (approximately 100 nl min−1). The mass signals correlate to
some degree to the cell internal pressure changes with higher pressure
transporting higher amounts of ions to the detectors and lower pres-
sure lower amounts. As discussed previously and illustrated in Fig. 3
Li-S cells also show signiﬁcant volume changes that have an impact
on the cell internal pressure. Overall it is reasonable to normalize the
signal to compensate for effects that are not induced by gas evolution
in the cell. Fig. 4 illustrates the selected m/z signals 15 and 16 as
measured, normalized to the cell internal pressure and normalized to
m/z signal 29 for a Li-S cell with LiNO3 containing electrolyte. The
m/z 29 is a combined signal of the electrolyte solvents DME and DOL
and shows the highest intensity signal in all measurements next to m/z
45 which is also DME:DOL related. Normalizing to a high intensity
electrolyte signal has the advantage that the amount of electrolyte in a
cell is relatively constant with a high initial excess of electrolyte and
comparably low examined cycle numbers. Additionally there is no
increase of DME and DOL because of cell internal reactions. Due to
the high initial excess of electrolyte the m/z 29 signal (related to DME
and DOL) is regarded as constant throughout the experiment and only
affected by pressure changes in the cell and temperature effects. It is
therefore hopefully reasonable to use this signal to normalize all other
signals to compensate for these effects and all subsequent shown mass
spectrometry results were normalized to m/z signal 29.
For the online MS measurements 0.7 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL (2:1,
v:v) was applied and 0.25 M LiNO3 was added if required. Schneider
et al. extracted aged electrolyte and reported that the DME:DOL
ratio stays constant during cycling.7 By contrast Mikhaylik et al.
reported that DME is the major depletion source and that the DOL
amount is relatively constant.12 It is therefore possible that different
DME:DOL compositions might change the decomposition products
quantitatively.
Li-S cells.—Fig. 5 illustrates various MS mass signals for a Li-S
cell with different charge cutoff voltages and with LiNO3 electrolyte.
The m/z signal 2 shows the evolution of hydrogen which especially
occurs in the initial cycles with a decreasing trend in the subsequent
cycles. Hydrogen can especially be observed at higher voltage levels
at the upper charge/discharge plateau. The m/z signal 7 is a speciﬁc
signal for nitrogen with low intensity but has the great advantage that
unlike other nitrogen signals m/z 14 and 28, m/z 7 doesn’t share the
mass number with various other compounds and is therefore selec-
tive. Nitrogen continuously develops especially in the initial cycles.
After cycle 6 the nitrogen signal is relatively constant with only small
amounts of nitrogen being produced. This result can somehow be
conﬁrmed by Schneider et al. who identiﬁed hydrogen and nitrogen
after 4 cycles in a Li-S cell with a trend to small chain hydrocarbons
after 40 cycles.7 According to the NIST database m/z signal 15 is the
primary signal of methoxy-ethene (methyl vinyl ether) which can also
be identiﬁed on mass 58 with the secondary signal. Unfortunately, m/z
signal 15 also integrates signals from CH4, CH3F and methanol and
m/z signal 58 also shows the quaternary intensity peak of the solvent
DME. The evolution of CH4 was veriﬁed by Jozwiuk et al.8 by FTIR,
still the main CH4 signal m/z 16 is comparably low indicating that
the amount of produced CH4 is small. Additionally, all signal curves
were normalized to the electrolyte signal which should therefore also
be less signiﬁcant to explain the signal curve of m/z 58. Still m/z
signal 31 (not illustrated here) which is a primary signal for various
alcohols has a comparable signal development as the signal curves of
m/z 15 and 58. Therefore it is extremely likely that methoxy-ethene
and alcohol creation occurs at the end of discharge but also at the high
level charge plateau.
Lithium 2-methoxy ethoxide was reported to be a decomposition
product of DME in Li-S cells some time ago.12 GC-MS measurements
conﬁrmed (compare Fig. 12) the evolution of methoxy-ethene which
might be a corresponding interstage or by-product.
The m/z signal 28 is representative of nitrogen, ethane, ethylene
and carbon oxides. It is possible that some CO2 is being created at
the end of charge as indicated by a weak signal of m/z 22 which is
highly selective for CO2. CO2 traces could possibly be regarded as
measurement contaminations with air when post mortem gas analysis
was performed in other literature sources. Still most of the signal
intensity must be from ethane and from nitrogen, as indicated by
a comparable signal development of m/z 7. Signal m/z 44 is the
aggregated signal of CO2, CS2, DOL and N2O. As discussed before
all signals were normalized to the electrolyte signals. Therefore the
contribution of DOL should be low. A CS2 contribution is also only
possible in trace levels because the primary CS2 signal on m/z 76 has
only very low intensities. It is therefore possible that the main intensity
of signal m/z 44 is because of N2O. The existence of N2O was very
likely proven by Jozwiuk et al. with the application of isotope labeled
LiNO3, thus increasing m/z to 46 and 48 which is almost free from
competing substances.8

subsequent cycles. Nitrogen is being created over all 15 cycles and
especially during charge. CH4 was also identiﬁed in the literature
however the amounts being created must be low given that the m/z

The impact of LiNO3 on Li-S cells and Li-HC half cells.—Online
mass spectrometry measurements were performed with Li-S cells and
Li-HC cells cycled with and without LiNO3. To examine the impact of
the LiNO3 additive the focus was set on possible mass fragments of the
LiTFSI salt. Figs. 6a, 6c show that for cells with LiNO3, m/z signals 69,
51 and 50 stay constant throughout charging and discharging, while
cells without LiNO3 (Figs. 6b, 6d), the signals increase during charge
and then decrease during discharge due to the inherent MS vacuum
extraction of gas products. Comparing the signal development of Li-
HC with Li-S it can be seen that the m/z signals 50, 51 and 69 of the
Li-HC system have a much higher intensity and ﬂuctuation, while the
Li-S cell in general has a much higher signal intensity of these masses
and some ﬂuctuation of the signal m/z 133.
Once charging begins again with the Li-HC cell, the signals start to
increase once again. This is clear evidence of decomposition products
being produced during the charge cycle. The initial theory was that
the mass signals were from TFSI as Alwast et al. have shown that the
primary mass fragments of LiTFSI are m/z 64 (SO2+), 69 (CF3+),
133 (SO2CF3+) and 280 for the full [TFSI]- molecular ion.14 How-
ever, the m/z 64 (not shown), 133 and 280 (not shown) showed no
ﬂuctuation over time which contradicts this assumption. Markevich
et al. had shown that when [BMP][TFSI] was cycled vs. lithium the
only decomposition product of the TFSI anion was triﬂuoromethane
which has primary m/z signals of 69, 51 and 50.15 Based on these
results it can be suggested that the decomposition products observed
in Fig. 6 are from the production of triﬂuoromethane and that the
decomposition of TFSI is prevented by the presence of LiNO3.
Two hypotheses are suggested for the reasoning behind the pre-
vention of TFSI decomposition in the presence of LiNO3. Since it
is known that LiNO3 is reduced below 1.8 V vs. Li,16,17 the ﬁrst hy-
pothesis is that the reduction of LiNO3 during discharge is altering
the electrolyte composition and therefore LiNO3 reduction products
are further reacting, thus preventing TFSI decomposition. The sec-
ond hypothesis is that the LiNO3 is simply forming a passivation or
SEI layer on the hard carbon surfaces that is preventing the TFSI
decomposition.18 Detailed surface analysis such as X-ray photoelec-
tron spectroscopy (XPS) and reaction studies may show whether a
passivating SEI layer is formed preventing TFSI decomposition, while
extended testing with in-situ MS may show evidence of TFSI decom-
position once all LiNO3 has been consumed. The Li-S cell without
LiNO3 has signal changes mainly on mass signal m/z 133 and 69
which are LiTFSI fragments.
Li-HC half cells.—Fig. 7 illustrates an online mass spectrometry
measurement of a Li-HC half cell with LiNO3 electrolyte. In the initial
discharge cycle some hydrogen is being created as shown by the m/z
2 signal maximum after ∼10 h of test time. There is constant nitrogen
creation over the whole test as illustrated by m/z 7 and 28.
The increase of m/z signal 15 in the ﬁrst cycle is difﬁcult to be
interpreted because CH4 creation would also have increased m/z 16,
methoxy-ethene would have increased m/z 58, methanol would have
increased m/z 31 and CH3F would have increased m/z 34.
M/z 16 and 28 have a comparable signal development which could
mean that there is some CO formation. The signal development of
m/z 44 has a falling intensity trend and is comparable to m/z 22 which
could mean that very small amounts of CO2 are being created.
M/z 58 shows increasing signal intensities when the current is
increased which can also be observed at m/z 15. Therefore it is likely
that an increasing methoxy-ethene development takes place.
LiHC-S cells.—Fig. 8 illustrates an online mass spectrometry mea-
surement of a LiHC-S full cell with LiNO3 electrolyte. Reference
sulfur cathodes which were created with water as a slurry solvent
only had a low coulometric efﬁciency of 75% at 0.6 mA cm−2 in the
LiHC-S setup. Alternatively, solvent-free and carefully dried sulfur
cathodes had coulometric efﬁciencies above 99% and a more sta-
ble cycle performance. The sulfur cathode applied in this work was
solvent-free and carefully dried within the glove box. The HC anode
was prelithiated vs. Li with LiTFSI: DME:DOL electrolyte including
LiNO3.
The signal developments of the LiHC-S full cell are very similar
to the Li-S results shown in Fig. 5. One difference is that some hy-
drogen is created (m/z 2) at the begin of discharge and at higher cycle
numbers also at the end of charge. As a conclusion a LiHC-S full
cell has very likely the same gaseous products at least in the initial
cycles. A prelithiation of the HC anode with alternative electrolytes
e.g. carbonates including FEC might lead to different results regarding
gaseous decomposition products.

thin ﬁlm type Si anode had no polymer binder or carbon and consisted
of 100% Si. The surface area of the Si anode is considered to be low

There is some hydrogen creation (compare m/z 2) in the initial

LiSi-S cells.—Fig. 10 illustrates an online mass spectrometry mea-
surement of a LiSi-S full cell with LiNO3 electrolyte. A thin ﬁlm Si
anode was applied as described above. The applied sulfur cathode was
created with water as a solvent during slurry creation. It is likely that
there is some water contamination of the electrolyte with a possible
impact on the gas analysis measurement.
There is some hydrogen creation especially at the begin of dis-
charge (see m/z 2). The development of the m/z 7 and m/z 28 signals
clearly shows the constant development of nitrogen. With m/z 15, 16
and 44 showing a clear trend toward lower intensities it is unlikely that
hydrocarbons and carbon oxides are created in concentrations above
trace level. Signal m/z 76 indicates that low concentrations of CS2 are
formed when the cell has an elevated voltage at the end of charge and
begin of discharge. In comparison to other online mass spectrometry
measurements (e.g. Li-S, LiHC-S) there are no characteristic sharp
signals at m/z 15 and 58 within LiSi-S full cells which was also con-
ﬁrmed by further reference measurements. If there is methoxy-ethene
creation it is possibly much lower and especially occurs at the be-
gin of charge. The m/z 31 signal indicating short chain alcohols has
exactly the same signal development as m/z 58 as already observed
in other measurements. It is therefore possible that the creation of
alcohol and methoxy-ethene are closely related. As a summary Li-
Si-S full cells seems to have less gaseous decomposition products
compared to Li-S and LiHC-S cells. The most signiﬁcant gaseous
products should be nitrogen. The creation of hydrocarbons seems to
be hindered which possibly reduces the overall amount of gas being
developed in the system during cycling. As reported in the literature
the relative concentration of hydrocarbons increases with higher cycle
numbers in Li-S cells.7 Long term cycling of LiSi-S pouch cells with
Si thin ﬁlm anodes at Fraunhofer IWS in Dresden had no signiﬁcant
visible gas development. Therefore, it can be assumed that the hy-
drocarbons play a major role in the long term gas development of
Li-S cells which signiﬁcantly expand during cycling. Still it must be
noted that the thin ﬁlm Si anodes applied here had a low surface area
and no carbon/polymer backbone which might change the results in
comparison to other types of Si anodes.
Post-mortem electrolyte analysis – GCMS.—As discussed pre-
viously, it can be difﬁcult to identify speciﬁc substances with only
online MS. To prove speciﬁc electrolyte decomposition products ex-
situ gas chromatography in combination with mass spectrometry was
used. Several electrolyte samples were analyzed to determine the con-
tents, with particular emphasis placed on whether the m/z 31, 58, and
76 signals observed during in-situ MS analysis could be methanol,
methoxy-ethene and CS2. It must be noted that solvent based sul-
fur cathodes have high concentrations of remaining solvent. This is
because the drying temperatures are comparably modest after coat-
ing (typically ∼80◦C) as sulfur easily sublimates at further elevated
temperatures. The sulfur cathodes in this work which were created
with water as a solvent are therefore likely to have signiﬁcant water
impurities.
The FID and GC-MS analysis of fresh electrolyte including LiNO3
can be seen in Figs. 11a, 11b obviously proving that the applied elec-
trolyte indeed contains DME and DOL as well as glove box argon.
When soaked into a sulfur cathode (no cycling), the electrolyte GC
analysis showed some additional peaks from the fresh electrolyte.
The peaks pictured in Fig. 11c at 4.66 min, 5.85 min and 6.57 min
were determined via MS (and comparison of retention time to stan-
dard samples) to be methanol, ethanol and acetone respectively. The
presence of methanol and ethanol suggests that the electrolyte reacted
upon contact with the sulfur cathode. There were no sulfur compounds
detected.
When cycled in a symmetrical Li-Li cell from 0–3 V at 5 mV/s with
LiNO3 the electrolyte was found to contain ethanol, methanol and a
new compound, methoxy-ethene. Figs. 12a, 12b shows the comparison
of the mass spectrum observed, at a retention time of 4.5 minutes
(see Fig. 13b), during GC-MS electrolyte analysis vs. the NIST mass
spectrum for methoxy-ethene (CAS 107-25-5). Similarly, GC analysis
of electrolyte without LiNO3 when cycled in a symmetrical Li-Li cell
from 0–3 V at 5 mV s−1, showed the presence of methoxy-ethene but
methanol and ethanol were not detected.
Electrolyte with and without LiNO3 cycled in symmetrical Li-Li
cells showed no evidence of compounds relating to the suspected
CS2. This ﬁnding suggests that the presence of CS2, as seen in the cell
cycling MS analysis, is due to an electrochemical reaction between
the electrolyte and the cathode during cycling and does not corre-
spond to decomposition of TFSI (the only source of sulfur in Li-Li
tests).
The GC analysis of Li-S cells cycled from 1.0–3.0 V at 0.1 mV
s−1 with and without LiNO3 provides further insight into the origin
of the suspected electrolyte decomposition products from the in-situ
MS analysis of cell cycling. In Li-S cells cycled with LiNO3 additive,
methanol, ethanol and methoxy-ethene were again detected in addi-
tion to a small amount of acetone. It is possible that the acetone is a
decomposition product; however the acetone could also be a contami-
nant from the cleaning of the test cells. (All test cells were cleaned with
deionized water and heated in vacuum at 110◦C for several hours).
Since methanol, ethanol, and methoxy-ethene were found in a Li-Li
cell with LiNO3 containing electrolyte, it suggests that the presence
of the sulfur cathode does not have an inﬂuence on this decompo-
sition mechanism. Electrolyte without LiNO3, cycled in a Li-S cell
also showed the presence of methanol, ethanol, and methoxy-ethene.
Therefore the presence of ethanol and methanol must be related to
both the interaction of electrolyte with the cathode and the presence
of LiNO3. This very likely proves the suggestion of Jozwiuk et al.
that methanol and other alcohols are created during cycling8 and that
hydrogen might be formed out of them.
One of the most important results obtained from the GC analysis
of Li-S cells was the presence of CS2 conﬁrmed by the analysis of a
CS2 standard for both electrolytes with and without LiNO3. The MS
signal from GC-MS for the 6.7 min retention time peak (see Fig. 13c),
associated with CS2, in electrolyte with LiNO3 cycled in a Li-S cell
can be seen in Figs. 12c, 12d compared to the NIST database spectrum
for CS2.

Conclusions
Thickness measurements during cycling were performed for Li-
ion, various sulfur cathode type Li-S cells, Li-HC, Li-Si, LiHC-S and
LiSi-S cells. Sulfur cathodes, especially with high porosity, collapse
in the ﬁrst cycles and reduce the total electrode stack thickness. In
the long term Li-S or in general cells with a lithium metal anode
show a massive thickness increase when lithium becomes more and
more porous during cycling. This major problem has to be addressed
if corresponding chemistries should ﬁnd the way into mainstream
applications. In comparison Li-ion cells as well as full cells with HC
anode and sulfur cathode show modest and/or reversible thickness
changes during cycling and no trend of thickness growth. This explains
why a Li-ion cell doesn’t break a smartphone case but also marks the
(hard) carbon anode/sulfur cathode chemistry to be interesting for
applications in which high energy density is not in the focus. Li-Si
half cells but also LiSi-S full cells with thin ﬁlm Si anode show massive
volume changes in the initial cycles which can’t alone be explained by
the Si volume expansion during lithiation. The major part of this effect
is actually due to the Cu current collector developing a canyon and
sieve like structure due to mechanical stress. After this development
has completed the thickness development is relatively constant. It is
therefore imaginable that structured thin ﬁlm cathodes which could
compensate the mechanical stress would deliver more stable thickness
developments from the start.
Online mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful tool to evaluate the
stability of an electrolyte. Pressure measurements within the test cells

suggest that it might be reasonable to combine electrode thickness
measurements and gas analysis in a one cell setup with signiﬁcant
thickness changes of certain chemistries having an impact on cell
pressure and therefore signal intensity.
Various gaseous decomposition products were suggested by online
MS including N2, methoxy-ethene, CS2, hydrogen, hydrocarbons, al-
cohols and possible carbonoxide traces. For the liquid electrolyte
phase methoxy-ethene, CS2 and short chain alcohols could be veri-
ﬁed by Headspace-GC-MS. The reported decomposition product N2O
could not be veriﬁed by online MS due to competing m/z 44 signals.
N2O was also not identiﬁed by GC-MS. The gas phase is comprised
of nitrogen, hydrogen, ethene, methane, and ethane. Relative amounts
for these compounds could be calculated.
Li-S and LiHC-S cells show comparable decomposition gas sig-
nals (qualitatively). This could mean that the increasing porosity of
the lithium metal anode has a higher impact on the obtainable cycle
number compared to the electrolyte decomposition keeping the much
higher cycle number of LiHC-S cells in mind.
In the LiSi-S cells examined in this work, the development of hy-
drocarbons seems to be hindered and the overall gas creation appears
to be lower compared to Li-S cells which was conﬁrmed by long term
cycle tests at Fraunhofer IWS.
As an overall conclusion, the Li-S standard electrolyte based on
DME:DOL solvents and LiNO3 additive can be regarded as unsta-
ble for most systems and should be substituted with more stable
alternatives if high energy densities and long cycle life are to be
achieved.
