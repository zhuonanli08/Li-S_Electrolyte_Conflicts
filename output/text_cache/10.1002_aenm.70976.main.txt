ABSTRACT
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) is a promising cathode material for lithium-metal batteries due to its intrinsic stability and
ability to circumvent the shuttle effect. However, it is still challenging to achieve high reversible capacity and cycling stability,
mainly due to the intrinsic sluggish kinetics and unstable electrode/electrolyte interphases. Herein, we design a LiFSI-based
electrolyte that leverages anion participation to regulate the solvation structure, enabling robust electrode/electrolyte interphases
on both the Li anode and SPAN cathode. Such an optimized environment effectively suppresses dendritic lithium growth and
preserves the structural integrity of the SPAN cathode during repeated cycling. Further empowered by its high ionic conductivity
of 13.63 mS cm−1, the Li/SPAN batteries achieve an outstanding reversible capacity of 1015 mAh gS
−1 after 1350 cycles and retain a
capacity of 880 mAh gS
−1 after 400 cycles even at −20◦C. Moreover, the assembled Li/SPAN pouch cell exhibits excellent cycling
stability, maintaining a specific capacity of 1043 mAh gS
−1 after 100 cycles at room temperature and exhibiting an ultralow capacity
decay rate of 0.25% per cycle at −20◦C. This work provides a rational electrolyte design strategy toward durable Li/SPAN batteries
with enhanced low-temperature performance.
1
Introduction
While conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) offer promis-
ing energy densities of around 350 Wh kg−1, they fall short
of the demands for lighter electronics and longer-range elec-
tric vehicles. In pursuit of densities exceeding 400 Wh kg−1,
research has shifted toward all-solid-state batteries by pair-
ing high-nickel or lithium-rich cathodes with silicon/carbon
anodes. Another promising avenue is lithium-sulfur batteries,
which have the potential to push the energy limit beyond
600 Wh kg−1 [1]. Compared with traditional cathode mate-
rials, such as LiCoO2 and NMC, sulfur offers an ultrahigh
theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh gS
−1, and a pro-
jected energy density of about 2500 Wh kg−1, which makes
it a highly attractive candidate for next-generation Li metal
batteries [2–6]. Moreover, sulfur possesses the advantages
of environmental benignity, natural abundance, and cost-
effectiveness.

causing

Consequently,

[8].
In
addition,
the
inherently
sluggish
sulfur redox kinetics further intensify polarization and LiPSs
accumulation [9], resulting in severely limited cycle life
and poor low-temperature performance for ether-based Li-S
batteries [10, 11].
To
fundamentally
overcome
the
challenges
in
cycling
stability
and
low-temperature
performance,
sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has emerged as a promising sulfur
cathode alternative. In SPAN, sulfur is covalently bonded
into a nitrogen-containing carbonaceous polymer backbone
via C─S and N─S bonds. This covalent integration prevents
the formation and dissolution of free polysulfide species,
enabling a predominantly solid-solid conversion mechanism
during cycling with a high sulfur utilization rate of up to 98%
[12, 13]. Coupled with compatible electrolyte design, SPAN-
based systems therefore offer a more viable commercial
pathway
than
conventional
Li-S
batteries
[14].
Despite
these
advantages,
SPAN-based
systems
still
face
several
critical challenges. The solid-solid sulfur redox in SPAN
inherently suffers from sluggish reaction kinetics due to its
reaction processes via solid‑state diffusion and interfacial
charge transfer [15, 16]. In addition, some critical issues,
including uneven solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers [17],
uncontrolled lithium dendrite growth [18], and continuous
loss of active material during prolonged cycling, remain
unresolved [19, 20].
Developing advanced electrolytes has proven to be an effec-
tive strategy to address these challenges simultaneously. The
carbonate electrolyte 1 m LiPF6 EC/DEC is typically applied
to SPAN, but it shows poor compatibility with lithium metal
[21]. Alternatively, sterically hindered weakly solvating ethers,
such as butyl methyl ether (BME) or branched diisopropyl ether
(DIPE), have been employed to construct anion-rich solvation
structures that promote robust SEI/cathode electrolyte inter-
phase (CEI) formation at both electrodes and enable long-life
Li/SPAN cycling [22, 23]. However, these sterically hindered
ethers are generally more expensive than common ether sol-
vents such as DME, and the resulting electrolytes often suf-
fer from relatively low ionic conductivities, which may limit
their practical applicability under high-rate or low-temperature
conditions. Similarly, localized high-concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs) based on fluorinated ether diluents, for example,
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE), are
able to effectively improve Li/SPAN cycling performance by
enhancing anion-involved solvation and interphase stability. The
LHCEs system, however, introduces trade-offs at the cost of
increased viscosity, reduced ionic conductivity, and significantly
higher electrolyte cost [24–26]. Overall, although both designs
can effectively stabilize the Li/SPAN interfaces by leverag-
ing anion-dominated solvation structures, they often sacrifice
ionic transport and economic viability. Under the premise of
high ionic conductivity, simultaneously forming robust SEI
on the Li anode and a stable CEI on the SPAN cathode
is essential to unlock the full potential of the SPAN sys-
tem, yet a versatile electrolyte for Li/SPAN batteries remains
elusive.
Unlike previously reported weakly solvating ethers or local-
ized high-concentration electrolytes that rely on steric hin-
drance or fluorinated diluents to enforce anion participation,
the present design achieves anion-regulated solvation within
a cost-effective DME/FEC framework while preserving high
ionic conductivity. This balanced strategy avoids the com-
mon trade-off between interfacial stability and ionic trans-
port. Herein, we report a rationally designed, low-cost elec-
trolyte tailored for Li/SPAN batteries. The electrolyte comprises
lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), fluoroethylene carbon-
ate (FEC), and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), with a molar ratio
of LiFSI: DME: FEC = 2:4.8:6.9 (corresponding to 2 M LiFSI
in DME/FEC = 1:1, v/v). In this electrolyte, anions actively
participate in and regulate the solvation structure, promoting
the formation of robust and inorganic-rich electrode/electrolyte
interphases on both electrodes. This optimized solvation config-
uration effectively suppresses parasitic reactions, stabilizes the
interfacial chemistry, and enhances Li+ transport kinetics. As
a result, the Li/SPAN cell delivers remarkable cycling stability
with a high reversible capacity of 1015 mAh gS
−1 after 1350
cycles. Notably, the electrolyte exhibits a high ionic conductivity
of 13.63 mS cm−1, enabling the battery to retain a specific
capacity of 880 mAh gS
−1 after 400 cycles at −20◦C. It is
remarkable that the Li/SPAN pouch cells exhibit outstanding
room-temperature cycling stability, retaining 1043 mAh gS
−1
after 100 cycles and showing an ultralow capacity decay rate
of 0.25% per cycle at −20◦C. Therefore, this work confirms
the superior interfacial stability, low-temperature capability,
and scalability of the designed electrolyte, positioning it as an
ideal solution for the practical progression of Li/SPAN battery
technology.

enhance the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte. Meanwhile,
the low viscosity of DME helps mitigate the adverse effects
associated with the high viscosity of FEC, ensuring favorable ion
transport properties. To study the effect of lithium salts on the key
electrochemical performance parameters of the SPAN electrode,
we tested different lithium salts with the same molar ratio

Theoretical calculations of the radial distribution functions
(RDFs) and coordination numbers of Li+ in the three electrolytes
(Figure 1c–e) reveal distinct differences in anion participation
within the solvation sheath, with FSI−showing the highest degree
of coordination. Specifically, the coordination numbers of the
three anions are calculated to be 0.963 for FSI−(derived from
the Li─O═(FSI−) coordination curve), 0.517 for TFSI−(derived
from the Li─O═(TFSI−) coordination curve), and 0.165 for PF6
−
(derived from the Li─F coordination curve). These results indi-
cate that the LiFSI-based electrolyte features a higher degree of
anion-involved solvation. Such a solvation structure is favorable
for the formation of a robust interphase during cycling, thereby
suppressing solvent decomposition and alleviating continuous
solvent consumption.
Raman spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the solvation
structure of the designed electrolyte. As shown in Figure 2a
and Figure S6, the pristine FEC exhibits three characteristic
vibrational bands located at 730, 870, and 909 cm−1, which
are assigned to the ring-breathing, C─F stretching, and ring-
skeletal deformation modes of the FEC molecule, respectively
[30]. Meanwhile, the pristine DME solvent displays a distinct
C─O stretching vibration centered at 850 cm−1, consistent with
previous reports [22]. Upon dissolving 2 m lithium salts in a
DME/FEC (v/v = 1:1) mixed solvent, two additional Raman bands
appear at 878 and 924 cm−1, which are attributed to the strong
coordination interactions between Li+ and the ether oxygen
atoms of DME as well as the carbonyl and fluorine sites of FEC,
respectively. The emergence of these coordinated peaks indicates
a reconstruction of the Li+ solvation sheath, confirming that both
DME and FEC actively participate in the primary coordination
environment. As shown in Figure S6, the Raman band located
at 740 cm−1 corresponds to the free TFSI−anion, whereas the
peak at 733 cm−1 originates from the overlapping vibrational
modes of free PF6
−and free FEC molecules. In contrast, the bands
appearing at 745 and 746 cm−1 are assigned to the coordination
environments of Li+-TFSI−and Li+-PF6
−, respectively [31, 32].
Subsequently, detailed analysis was performed on the S-N-S
stretching vibration mode of the FSI−anion, and the correspond-
ing Raman spectrum was deconvoluted into distinct components
(Figure 2b). Based on the degree of interaction between Li+
cations and FSI−anions, the observed species can be categorized
into three types: (i) free FSI−(without Li+ coordination), (ii)
contact ion pairs (CIP, one FSI−coordinated with one Li+), and
(iii) aggregated ion pairs (AGG, AGGs, one FSI−coordinated
with more than one Li+) [33]. It is evident that the LiFSI-based
electrolyte exhibits a solvation structure dominated by CIP. Such
a CIP-rich configuration facilitates more efficient ion transport
within the electrolyte, thereby enhancing both the ionic con-
ductivity and the low-temperature electrochemical performance
[34]. In addition, the CIP coordination environment allows the
majority of solvent molecules to participate in Li+ coordination,
effectively reducing the population of free solvent molecules.
Consequently, the elevated lowest unoccupied molecular orbital

As shown in Figure 2c, the solvation environment of Li+ in the

coordinating species in the solvation shell. An upfield (more neg-

either stronger solvent binding or stronger anion binding [37]. It

observation is consistent with the anion-coordination numbers

As shown in Figure 2d,e, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) mea-
surements further reveal that the LiFSI-based electrolyte displays
the highest reductive activity of the lithium salt, whereas the
oxidative stability of the three electrolytes remains comparable.

LUMO energy levels obtained from the theoretical calcula-
tions in Figure 1a. During cycling, LiFSI undergoes preferential
reductive decomposition on the lithium metal surface, forming
an interphase that is rich in inorganic fluorinated species,
which significantly reduces the extent of solvent-derived side

analysis in Figure 2c, confirming the substantial participation
of FSI−anions in the solvation structure of the LiFSI-based
electrolyte.
In addition, as shown in Figures S7 and S8, calendar aging
tests were conducted on the Li/SPAN batteries by holding the
batteries at 45◦C for 15 days, and the reversible (charge) capacities
before and after storage were compared. It was observed that
the batteries with the LiFSI-based electrolyte exhibited a smaller
capacity loss after storage. Under this condition, the calendar
performance is primarily governed by the chemical and inter-
facial passivation stability of the electrolyte. The LiFSI-based
electrolyte is able to form a more stable electrode/electrolyte
interphase on both electrodes, which prevents accelerated par-
asitic reactions between the electrodes and the electrolyte
under elevated temperature [38]. Moreover, the stronger anion-
coordinated solvation structure in the LiFSI-based electrolyte
enables higher ionic conductivity and lower desolvation barriers,
which is beneficial for capacity recovery during subsequent
room-temperature cycling after storage. In contrast, the LiPF6-
based electrolyte is mainly limited by its inherent poor thermal
stability at elevated temperatures [39]. As for the commercial
electrolyte LS001 (1 m LiTFSI in DOL/DME = 1:1, v/v), its
rapid capacity decay mainly originates from the inability to
form a stable interphase, and the pure ether electrolyte readily
induces polysulfide dissolution, leading to substantial capacity
loss [40].
2.2
Electrochemical Characterization
To elucidate the influence of different lithium salts in the
DME/FEC solvent system, the Li+ diffusion coefficients, ionic
conductivity, and Li+ transference number of the three electrolyte
systems were systematically evaluated. As shown in Figure 3a and
Figures S9 and S10, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted
at various scan rates, and the resulting peak current-scan rate data
were fitted according to the Randles–Sevcik equation (Figure 3b;
Equation S1) [41]. Under identical electrode conditions, the
absolute slope of the anodic peak current is proportional to
the Li+ diffusion coefficients, enabling direct comparison of Li+
diffusion among the electrolytes. The fitted k values for the
three electrolytes are 10.551 for the LiFSI-based electrolyte, 9.978
for the LiTFSI-based electrolyte, and 6.377 for the LiPF6-based
electrolyte. The LiFSI-based electrolyte exhibited the highest
diffusion coefficients, indicative of superior electrode kinetics.
Furthermore, in Figure 3c, analysis of the peak voltage difference
at different scan rates revealed that the LiFSI-based electrolyte
yielded the lowest polarization.
As shown in Figure 3d and Figures S11–S13, the LiFSI-based
electrolyte exhibits the highest Li+ transference number (0.68),
followed by the LiTFSI-based electrolyte (0.64) and the LiPF6-
based electrolyte (0.44). The Li+ transference number was calcu-
lated using the Bruce-Vincent equation, as presented in Equation
S2. Figure 3e implied that the LiFSI electrolyte has the highest
ionic conductivity (13.63 mS cm−1), which is consistent with
the MD simulations (Table S1). It was noteworthy that only the
quantity of electric charge caused by lithium ions could effectively
participate in the process of active materials’ lithium/delithium.
The amount of charge transferred per unit time is proportional
to the conductivity. The value of the ionic conductivity product
tLi+ could illustrate the quantity of electric charge caused by
lithium ions per unit time. The higher amount of the quantity
of electric charge caused by lithium ions might accelerate the
electrode reaction, which displayed the higher reversible specific
capacity in the early-stage cycles; therefore, although the LiTFSI-
based electrolyte exhibits relatively lower ionic conductivity, it
still delivers favorable cycling performance. Moreover, the LiFSI-
based electrolyte delivers the highest Li+ ionic conductivity (9.28
mS cm−1), which provides strong support for its superior rate
capability and low-temperature cycling performance.
Meanwhile, as shown by the Tafel plots in Figure 3f, the LiFSI-
based electrolyte also exhibits a markedly higher interfacial
exchange current density (I0 = 8.1 × 10−2 mA cm−2) compared
with the LiTFSI-based (I0 = 3.5 × 10−2 mA cm−2) and LiPF6-based
electrolytes (I0 = 1.7 × 10−2 mA cm−2), indicating significantly
enhanced charge-transfer kinetics. The LiFSI-based electrolyte
delivers a markedly higher exchange current density (I0),
which facilitates accelerated interfacial charge-transfer kinetics
and thereby reduces polarization during high-rate cycling. This
observation is consistent with the polarization comparison shown
in Figure 3c. Such enhanced interfacial kinetics enable the battery
to sustain rapid charge–discharge processes with improved
efficiency
[42,
43].

To understand the role of FEC in the LiFSI-based electrolyte, a
visual test was conducted by adding Li2S6 solution to electrolytes
with and without FEC. The FEC-based electrolyte rapidly forms
yellow flocculent precipitates, whereas no obvious precipitation
was observed in the electrolyte without FEC (Figure S14).
This result suggests that FEC actively reacts with dissolved
polysulfides to yield insoluble products, rather than merely
limiting their solubility. These species are expected to facilitate
the formation of a stable CEI, leading to the absence of a
distinct polysulfide-related discharge plateau in the FEC-based
electrolyte (Figure S15), which effectively restrains the shuttle
effect [47]. In the commercial electrolyte, severe polysulfide
shuttling
and
uncontrolled
lithium
dendrite
growth
are
observed. By contrast, in the LiFSI-based electrolyte, the solvation
structure with a higher degree of anion participation facilitates
the formation of robust electrode/electrolyte interphases on
both the anode and cathode sides. Additionally, the high ionic
conductivity effectively alleviates the kinetic penalties under
low-temperature conditions, thereby providing strong support
for the overall enhancement of battery performance.
Motivated by the favorable electrolyte characterizations, particu-
larly the superior performance of the LiFSI-based electrolyte, coin
batteries were assembled to evaluate the electrochemical perfor-
mance further. The commercial electrolyte LS001 was used as a
control electrolyte. As shown in Figure 4a, the long-term cycling
performance of the Li/SPAN battery was examined in the various
electrolytes. The LiFSI-based electrolyte delivered the highest
reversible specific capacity and enabled exceptionally stable
cycling, achieving 74.3% capacity retention after 1000 cycles with
an average coulombic efficiency (CE) exceeding 99.9%. Within
1350 cycles, the capacity fading rate was as low as 0.021% per
cycle. In contrast, the LS001 electrolyte suffered from continuous
capacity decay, retaining only 19.6% of its initial capacity after 200
cycles. This pronounced degradation is primarily attributed to the
ether-based formulation of LS001, which promotes polysulfide
dissolution during cycling and lacks film-forming components
such as FEC, resulting in a poorly stabilized electrode-electrolyte
interface [36, 64–66]. For instance, at the 1000th cycle, a
reversible specific capacity of 1050 mAh gS
−1, representing a 15.8%
increase over the LiTFSI-based electrolyte (907 mAh gS
−1) and a
21.4% improvement compared with the LiPF6-based electrolyte
(865 mAh gS
−1). Furthermore, the LiFSI-based electrolyte exhib-
ited noticeable capacity degradation only after about 1700 cycles,
whereas the LiTFSI- and LiPF6-based electrolytes showed pro-
nounced capacity fluctuations at around 1250 and 1150 cycles,
respectively, followed by rapid capacity decay leading to battery
failure. The charge–discharge profiles of the four electrolytes
during cycling are presented in Figure S16. The LiFSI-based elec-
trolyte yielded the lowest polarization at the same cycle number.
Figure 4b further demonstrates the superior rate capability of the
designed electrolytes. As the current density increased from 0.5C
to 8C, the LiFSI-based electrolyte still delivered high reversible
specific capacities of about 1400 (0.5C), 1360 (1C), 1200 (2C),
1000 (4C), 720 mAh gS
−1 (8C). In contrast, the LiTFSI-, LiPF6-
based electrolytes, and LS001 retained only about 442, 440, and
127 mAh gS
−1 at 8C, respectively. The charge–discharge profiles at

To further highlight the advantages of the designed electrolyte,
the electrochemical performance of the Li/SPAN batteries
was evaluated in different electrolytes at −20◦C. As shown
in Figure 4c, the LS001 electrolyte exhibits a similar rapid
capacity decay at −20◦C as observed at room temperature. This
degradation is attributed to the absence of FEC, which prevents
the formation of a robust SEI and leads to an unstable interphase
that accelerates capacity fading. The LiFSI-based electrolyte
delivers the most stable cycling performance, maintaining a high
specific capacity of 880 mAh gS
−1 (∼440 mAh gcomposite
−1) after
400 cycles at −20◦C. The LiTFSI-based electrolyte fails to sustain
normal cycling at −20◦C, while the LiPF6-based electrolyte
suffers a rapid capacity drop after cycling begins, eventually
leading to battery failure. As shown in Table S2, to elucidate the
origin of this behavior, the ionic conductivities of the electrolytes
were measured at low temperatures.
Li-Cu half-cell tests were further carried out at 25◦C and −20◦C
to evaluate Li-metal reversibility in different electrolytes. The
LiFSI-based electrolyte exhibits the highest average Coulombic
efficiency and maintains the most stable Li plating/stripping
behavior at both 25◦C and −20◦C, while LS001 and the
LiPF6-based electrolyte show much poorer low-temperature
stability. These results are consistent with the Li/SPAN battery
performance and further confirm the importance of interfacial
stability and ion-transport capability for low-temperature cycling
(Figures S18 and S19). Combined with the experimental results,
it can be concluded that, for the solid-state interphase-forming
SPAN system, the ionic conductivity at low temperature plays a
critical role in determining the battery performance. Meanwhile,
the stability and ionic permeability of the interphase are equally
important in mitigating interfacial polarization under low-
temperature conditions. The LiFSI-based electrolyte, benefiting
from its higher ionic conductivity and superior interphase-
forming ability, delivers the best cycling performance at −20◦C. In
contrast, the relatively high ionic conductivity of LS001 partially
compensates for the sluggish mass-transport kinetics at low tem-
peratures, enabling its cycling behavior to remain comparable to
that at room temperature. By comparison, batteries using LiTFSI-
or LiPF6-based electrolytes fail to sustain stable cycling or suffer
rapid cell failure because their insufficient ionic conductivity
cannot overcome the severe mass-transport limitations at low
temperature. As shown in Figure 4d, we also compared recently
reported low-temperature electrolytes for Li/SPAN systems, and
the battery in this work demonstrates clear advantages in both
cycling stability and reversible specific capacity [48–55].
Based on the above electrochemical results, and given the con-
sistently superior performance of the LiFSI-based electrolyte, we
further tuned the volume ratio between FEC and DME to deter-
mine the optimal solvent composition. A series of electrolytes
with varied FEC/DME ratios was evaluated in Li/SPAN batteries,
as shown in Figures S20 and S21. The results clearly indicate
that a 1:1 volume ratio of FEC to DME delivers the best balance
between reversible capacity and cycling stability. Therefore,
in the subsequent experiments and tests, the electrolyte with
2 m LiFSI in DME/FEC (1:1) was used as the experimental
group.

2.3
Characterization of the Electrode/Electrolyte
Interphases
To reveal the rate-limiting processes associated with interphase
formation and charge transfer, we apply in situ impedance and
distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analyses to decouple over-
lapped contributions, and further track SEI/CEI compositional
evolution via surface/interfacial characterizations. The DRT can
directly distinguish the time constant of the major electrochem-
istry process, which can simplify the impedance analyses and
significantly improve the accuracy of kinetics interpretation on
the timescale.
As shown in Figure 5a–c, the DRT contour plots exhibit clearly
distinguishable color distributions across different relaxation
time domains, allowing multiple individual relaxation processes
to be resolved. This observation indicates that multiple electro-
chemical reactions and mass-transport processes coexist during
battery operation, collectively governing the interfacial kinetics
of the Li/SPAN system. The high-frequency relaxation response
located in the short relaxation-time domain remains nearly
unchanged across different electrolyte systems, indicating that
the ohmic resistance and current-collector-related contributions
are essentially comparable. By contrast, a distinct relaxation
process emerges in the intermediate relaxation-time domain
(10−4.5–10−3 s), which is commonly associated with electrolyte
decomposition reactions involving lithium salts and solvents at
the electrode/electrolyte interface, leading to interphase forma-
tion. Notably, in the LiFSI-based electrolyte, the relaxation peak
located in the intermediate time-constant region is markedly
suppressed at different electrode potentials, indicating acceler-
ated interfacial reaction kinetics. As the discharge proceeds and
the interphase formation reactions are essentially completed, the
peak intensity in this relaxation-time domain further decreases
and eventually remains at the lowest level, suggesting that
the electrode/electrolyte interphase formed in the LiFSI-based
electrolyte imposes minimal resistance to charge transfer and
thereby facilitates interfacial kinetics. DRT analysis was further
performed at −20◦C to clarify the kinetic differences among
the electrolytes under low-temperature conditions, as shown
in Figure S36. Compared with room-temperature results, both
electrolytes exhibit longer relaxation times, indicating slower
mass transport and interfacial processes at reduced temperature.
Notably, the characteristic interfacial process of the LiFSI-based
electrolyte is mainly located in the 10−3–10−2 s range, while
that of the LiPF6-based electrolyte shifts to the 10−2–10−1 s
range, suggesting slower transport and reaction kinetics for the
latter. Furthermore, the LiFSI-based electrolyte shows a lower
interfacial resistance and a more concentrated charge-transfer
response, confirming its superior low-temperature interfacial
kinetics.
By comparing the EIS spectra recorded at different electrode
potentials in Figure 5a–c, it is observed that when the voltage
decreases to 1.6 V, the impedance response evolves from a single
“semicircle” to two distinct “semicircles”. This transition indi-
cates the emergence of an additional interfacial electrochemical

3
Conclusion
In conclusion, a LiFSI-based electrolyte with an anion-regulated
solvation structure was developed for high-performance Li/SPAN
batteries. The tailored solvation environment enables the simul-
taneous formation of robust SEI and CEI layers, which effectively
suppress lithium dendrite growth and stabilize the SPAN cathode
during cycling. As a result, the batteries maintained 72.0%
capacity retention after 1350 cycles at room temperature, corre-
sponding to an average decay of only 0.021% per cycle, while
also retaining 880 mAh gS
−1 after 400 cycles at −20◦C, indicating
its excellent stability under low-temperature conditions. In the
Li/SPAN pouch cell, it delivered a high specific reversible capacity
of 1043 mAh gS
−1 after 100 cycles, further demonstrating the
electrolyte’s applicability in practical settings. At −20◦C, the
pouch cell maintained a low capacity decay rate of 0.25% per
cycle. Overall, this work provides an effective electrolyte design
strategy for achieving durable cycling and extended temperature
adaptability in SPAN-based lithium batteries.
