Abstract
The sulfur utilization efficiency of lithium–sulfur batteries is often limited by
the uncontrolled electrodeposition of the insulating Li2S and the resulting elec-
trode passivation. Herein, purposeful electrode and electrolyte design is used
to realize site-selective three-dimensional (3D) Li2S electrodeposition and thus
mitigate the above problem. Site-selective Li2S nucleation is induced at the tips
of CoP nanoneedles grown on a carbon cloth electrode, and the 3D growth of
Li2S at these tips without the passivation of the inner part is achieved using a
LiBr-containing high-donor-number electrolyte. The controlled Li2S morphol-
ogy is rationalized by considering the tip effect, the energy of Li2S binding on
the electrode surface, and the solubility of Li2S in the electrolyte. Owing to the
suppressed electrode passivation, CoP nanoneedle–decorated carbon cloth
electrode and LiBr-containing electrolyte deliver a capacity of >1400 mAh gs
1
at a current density of 0.33 A gs
1. Thus, this work paves the way for the active
control of Li2S morphology for high-performance lithium–sulfur batteries.

INTRODUCTION
Lithium–sulfur batteries are promising next-generation
power sources because of their high theoretical energy
density (2600 Wh kg1) and low-cost of sulfur.1,2 The dis-
charge of the carbon/sulfur cathode involves the conver-
sion of elemental sulfur (S8) to Li2S and can be
accompanied by the formation of various electrolyte-
soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs).3 Currently, the sul-
fur utilization (i.e., S8 to Li2S conversion) efficiency of
practical Li–S batteries is considerably lower than 100%,4
largely because of polysulfide shuttling (dissolution of
LiPSs into the electrolyte followed by their reduction at
the Li-metal anode) and cathode passivation due to the
precipitation of the insulating Li2S as the terminal reduc-
tion product.5–7 The dense coverage of the active cathode
surface by Li2S hinders electron transfer and ion trans-
port at the electrolyte/electrode interface, thus limiting
the electrochemical redox reactions of LiPSs.8–12 The sig-
nificance of electrode passivation increases with the
increasing sulfur loading and decreasing electrolyte-
to-sulfur ratio. Therefore, to overcome this passivation,
one should actively control Li2S nucleation and growth
processes.

The morphology of Li2S deposits can be controlled
through the purposeful design of electrode surfaces. Li2S
nucleation sites can be introduced onto the electrode sur-
face to facilitate Li2S formation, as exemplified by various
catalytic
materials,
including
heteroatom-doped
carbons,8 metal oxides,9,13,14 metal nitrides,15–17 metal
selenides,18,19 metal sulfides,20 and MXenes.21,22 These
materials have a strong affinity for LiPSs, catalyzing their
electrochemical reduction and facilitating Li2S nucle-
ation. In line with this approach, Moon's group suggested
that a balance between promoting LiPS adsorption and
diffusion limitation in the LiPS adsorption layer is impor-
tant for achieving three-dimensional (3D) Li2S growth.23
Moreover, the electrode structure can be designed to con-
trol Li2S distribution. Low-porosity electrodes featuring a
single layer of carbon–sulfur composite secondary parti-
cles favor uniform Li2S formation over Li2S deposition on
the electrode skin.24 Janus particles, which preferentially
accommodate Li2S deposits on one side (MoO3) and pro-
vide nonpassivated carbon surfaces on the opposite side,
effectively mitigate electrode passivation.25
Electrolyte design is another effective strategy for con-
trolling Li2S deposition. When the solubility of Li2S in
the employed electrolyte is low, instantaneous Li2S pre-
cipitation
on
the
carbon
surface
leads
to
a
two-
dimensional (2D) film-like morphology and electrode
passivation.26,27 Conversely, in the case of high Li2S solu-
bility, S2 ions can diffuse in the electrolyte and adsorb at
a preferred site on preformed Li2S nuclei, which results
in a 3D particulate Li2S morphology and retarded elec-
trode passivation. The 3D morphology can be also
obtained by the chemical disproportionation of polysul-
fides to form S2 in the electrolyte.27,28 Such solution-
mediated reduction is facilitated by electrolytes with high
LiPS solubility.29 The electrolyte donor number (DN) is a
useful concept for modulating precipitation morphol-
ogy.30 With the increasing DN of solvents or salt
anions, the Li2S morphology shifts from 2D film-like
to 3D particle-like because of the concomitant increase in
LiPS and/or Li2S solubility. Based on the above, solvents
such as 1-methylimidazole,28 N-methylpyrrolidone,31
tetramethylurea,5 tetrapropylammonium bromide,32 and
3-fluoropyridine33 were used to induce the 3D growth of
Li2S. However, this electrolyte design allows one to con-
trol the growth morphology of Li2S but not its deposition
sites.

Structural characterization
The x-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using
a thin-film X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab, Rigaku). Mor-
phological analysis was performed using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, Magellan 400). The identities and
chemical states of the elements constituting the electrode
surfaces were determined using x-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS; K-alpha, Thermo VG Scientific; Al Kα
source). For Li2S distribution and morphology characteri-
zation, the (dis)charged electrode was removed from the
cell, washed with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, anhydrous,
99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) to remove soluble LiPSs, and sub-
jected to SEM or XPS analysis. All samples were stored in
an Ar-filled glove box to avoid exposure to air.

Density functional theory and
COMSOL simulations
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were con-
ducted using the Materials Studio Package (Biovia, 2018),
with 2  2  2 and 3  3  3 surface unit cells employed
for CoP and Li2S, respectively. The band structure of CoP

as

lations, the Li2S molecule was geometrically optimized
using the GGA-PBE method and DNP+ basis of the
DMol3 package, while Li2S and CoP structures were opti-
mized using the CASTEP code. The binding energy was
calculated as energy (Li2S–substrate)—energy (Li2S)—
energy (substrate).
COMSOL simulations were performed using the
COMSOL Multiphysics 5.6 software. The electric conduc-
tivity of CoP needles (0.4 S cm1) was taken from a previ-
ous report.37 To reflect the morphological features of the
CoP nanoneedles revealed by SEM analysis, the base and
height of CoP triangles were set to 500 nm and 5 μm,
respectively. The surfaces of the CoP nanoneedles were
assumed to be equipotential because of the metallic
nature of CoP. Charge density was calculated using
Gauss's law.38 The spatial distributions of the electric
field and charge density were obtained.

For the site-selective Li2S nucleation at the tips of the
needle structure to be successful, this structure should
have a high electronic conductivity. Otherwise, Li2S
deposition preferentially occurs at the bottom part of the
needle structure or on the carbon fiber surface. CoP syn-

high electronic conductivity of the CoP nanoneedle array.
After the decoration of the CC by CoP, the electronic
resistance increased only by 10% (Figure S2). Both sim-
ulation and experimental results showed that CoP had
high electronic conductivity.

XPS analysis was conducted to examine the passiv-
ation of the CoP@CC electrode at different discharge
depths. When discharge was stopped at the beginning of
Li2S nucleation (2.15 V), Co 2p peaks were observed for
both electrolytes (Figure S7), which suggested that the
nanoneedle arrays were not passivated at this potential.
After discharge to 2.03 V, the Co 2p peaks disappeared
for the LiTFSI electrolyte, indicating electrode passiv-
ation by Li2S (Figure 3E). By contrast, in the case of the
LiBr electrolyte, Co 2p peaks were observed even at
the end of the lower voltage plateau (Figure 3F). This
finding confirmed that in the CoP@CC-Br cell, the dis-
charge process was limited not by electrode passivation
but by LiPS depletion in the electrolyte.
The site-selective morphology-controlled Li2S deposi-
tion was ascribed to three reasons, namely the large
electric
field
at
the
nanoneedle
tips
(tip
effect),
increased Li2S solubility in the high-DN electrolyte, and
the energy of Li2S binding to Li2S crystallites exceeding
that to CoP (which helped suppress electrode passiv-
ation). The electric field distribution near the triangular
CoP needles was calculated using COMSOL. As shown
in Figure 3G, a high electric field was generated at the
needle tips because of the accumulation of charges
thereat, which supported the preferential Li2S nucle-
ation at the tips.
For Li2S growth to occur at the tip region, the Li2S
formed at the nanoneedle sidewalls should diffuse to and
be adsorbed at the preplanted Li2S nuclei rather than
instantaneously precipitate on the sidewalls or the carbon
electrode. To validate this hypothesis, we estimated the
energy of Li2S binding to the (111) facet of Li2S nuclei,
(211) facet of the CoP surface, and graphite using DFT
calculations (Figure 3H). The absolute value of this
energy was higher for Li2S (111) (8.0 eV) than for CoP
(211) (5.0 eV) or graphite (4.0 eV), that is, Li2S had a
thermodynamic preference for deposition on the pre-
formed Li2S nuclei and not on nonpassivated CoP sur-
faces or carbon electrode.24
Another prerequisite for achieving the site-selective,
morphology-controlled Li2S deposition is the high solu-
bility of Li2S, induced by high-DN anions. When the
solubility of Li2S in the electrolyte is low, Li2S rapidly
precipitates on the sidewalls of the CoP needles after
being generated at the same site, which results in elec-
trode passivation. Conversely, in electrolytes with high
Li2S solubility, Li2S molecules can freely move and merge
with energetically favored Li2S nuclei. The LiBr electro-
lyte had a higher Li2S solubility than the LiTFSI
electrolyte (Figure S8). Specifically, the 0.07 M Li2S-
containing LiTFSI electrolyte was opaque (incomplete
Li2S dissolution), whereas the 0.07 M Li2S-containing
LiBr electrolyte was transparent (complete Li2S dissolu-
tion). Additionally, the increased discharge capacity from
site-selective deposition was observed with another high-
DN salt anion, thiocyanate (SCN),39 further supporting
the
role
of
high-DN
electrolytes
in
this
process
(Figure S9).
