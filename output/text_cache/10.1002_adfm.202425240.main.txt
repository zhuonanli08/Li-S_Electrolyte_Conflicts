Dielectric Increment of Electrolytes Mediated by Ion
Association for Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Xiao-Zhong Fan, Meng Liu, Jin-Hao Zhang, Nan Yao, Xiao-He Zhou, Stanislav S. Fedotov,
Ying-Ze Song, Xiang Chen,* Yue-Zhou Zhang,* and Long Kong*
Electrolyte engineering for long-lifespan alkali-based batteries focuses on
modulating the solvation structure to build the electrode/electrolyte interface
and dictate interfacial reactions. Previous strategies have relied on increasing
the salt concentration to introduce the anion-derived solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) for considerable interfacial stability, but these strategies are
restricted by the poor solubility of ﬁlm-forming salts in weak solvation
electrolytes. Herein, a dielectric increment of weak solvation electrolytes
based on the ion dissociation and association chemistry is proposed to realize
the high salt solubility. Diﬀering from the dielectric decrement with the
addition of salts in strong solvation electrolytes owing to reduced free
solvents, the dielectric increment in weak solvation electrolytes is a result of
the surplus dielectric polarization of contact ion pairs (CIPs). As a
demonstration in salt-concentration-sensitive lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries,
CIPs facilitate the high solubility of lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) and promote
the Li2S2/Li2S nucleation. The CIP-induced dielectric increment of electrolytes
yields 96% capacity retention after 175 cycles at 0.2 C in the Li–S cell. This
underexplored strategy provides eﬀective guidelines for the design of
dielectric-constant-mediated electrolytes for alkali-based battery applications.

1. Introduction
The alkali metal batteries, especially lithium
(Li) metal batteries, have been sought after
due to their high theoretical energy density,
but the limited cycle life is the pivotal is-
sue impeding its commercialization. One
of the approaches to extend the cycle life is
the rational selection of electrolyte recipes,
including solvents and Li salts, to medi-
ate charge transport and stabilize electrode-
electrolyte interfaces. The dissociation and
association chemistry of Li salts depend-
ing on the characteristics of both solvents
and salts, such as dielectric constant and
donor number, determines the battery ca-
pacity, power density, and calendar life.[1]
Diﬀering from the permittivity of the solid
dielectric material that reﬂects the proper-
ties in storing and consuming electrostatic
energy under an external electrostatic ﬁeld,
the dielectric constant in liquid electrolytes
is considered as an ability to aﬀect the elec-
trostatic force between salt ions, which ap-
proximately dominates the ion dissociation
and association to modulate the electrolyte
solvation structure.[2] Speciﬁcally, solvents
with a high dielectric constant would abate the Coulombic inter-
action between anions and cations and thus promote salt dissolu-
tion and dissociation.[3,4] Such dielectric constant variation upon
salt concentration provides opportunities to tune the properties
of bulk electrolyte and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).[5]
The strong solvation electrolytes, like water, facilitate the for-
mation of solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) that cations are
enwrapped by solvent molecules, and therefore, the reduced
quantity of free solvent molecules cannot adequately shield the
external electric ﬁeld.[6–8] The presence of solvated cations in-
hibits the local electric ﬁeld, leading to the reduction of the
electrolyte dielectric constant. Accordingly, a negative correlation
in strong solvation electrolytes between dielectric constant and
salt concentration has been generally accepted, named “dielectric
decrement”.[9] Nevertheless, strong solvation electrolytes, such as
water, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and N,N-dimethylacetamide
(DMA), are usually excluded in the aprotic batteries as the ex-
tremely reactive alkali metal anodes violently react with such
solvents based electrolytes.[10,11] This challenge encourages the
adoption of weak solvation electrolytes, at least mixing with weak
solvation solvents, as typically exempliﬁed by mixing EC (with

Aqueous electrolytes promote the formation of SSIPs due to
the strong coordination strength by hydroxide groups and ex-
istence of hydrogen bonds. However, nonaqueous electrolytes
show much inferior dielectric constants and exhibit relatively
weak solvation capability, leading to the concurrent presence of
SSIPs, contact ion pairs (CIPs),[18,19] and aggregates (AGGs).[20,21]
A ruling attention should be drawn to the CIP structure be-
cause of its unique conﬁguration that embraces a sort of ionic
bond similar to dipoles. This unique structure manifests bipo-
larly distributed positive and negative charges and develops
into ordered arrangements under an external electric ﬁeld. The
dipole polarization and relaxation could in turn aﬀect the di-
electric constant of weak solvation electrolytes and thus medi-
ate the Li salt concentration and interfacial reactions.[22–25] Be-
sides, high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs)[16] and localized
high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs)[21,26,27] prevailed in re-
cent years are suggested to favor the electrode–electrolyte inter-
face on account of the CIP and AGG-predominated solvation
structure. Therefore, it is of importance to probe the dielectric
constant ﬂuctuation in weak solvation electrolytes, aiming to tai-
lor ion speciation and transportation.
This work focuses on the solvents and Li salt species that can
mediate the electrolyte dielectric constant to tailor the ability of
bulk electrolyte dissolving salts in lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batter-
ies. The distribution of existing salt species (i.e., SSIPs, CIPs,
and AGGs) are regulated by the salt concentration to investigate
their relationship with the electrolyte dielectric constant. Among
them, the large number of CIPs conspicuously enhances the elec-
trolyte dielectric constant, while the emergence of AGGs weakens
the electrolyte dielectric constant. Consequently, a large quantity
of CIP existing in a weak solvation electrolyte oﬀsets the reduced
dielectric constant brought by a decreased amount of free solvent,
and holds large dielectric polarization, resulting in “dielectric in-
crement” (Figure 1). This dielectric increment contributes to the
increased Li salt concentration in electrolytes. Li–S batteries are
considered as an appealing model system due to the discharge
behaviors that are dominated by the concentrated lithium poly-
sulﬁde (LiPS) intermediates. Based on the dielectric increment,
Li–S batteries with this electrolyte guideline yield excellent com-
patibility for Li metal electrodes, delivering stable cyclability (175
cycles) at 0.2 C. This work aﬀords a strategy to mediate the dielec-
tric constant of weak solvation electrolytes through ion dissocia-
tion/association, and guide high-concentration electrolyte design
by developing CIP structures.

To conﬁrm the regulation of dielectric constant by solvation struc-
ture, lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is adopted as it has been sought
after for its capability in establishing stable, tough, and fast-

ently, when the LiNO3 concentration increases to 0.4 M or up
to a maximum (0.6 M), CIPs dominate the solvation structure.

cation–anion interaction and diﬃculties for solvents to separate
Li salts. It is accepted that the charge distribution of salts is more

2.2. Modulating Dielectric Constant by CIPs
LiTFSI exhibits a large solubility in the DME/DOL mixture in
part due to the de-localized electron distribution across the anion,
while LiNO3 with a strong electron-donating anion is easily asso-
ciated in nonaqueous electrolytes and usually displays reduced
solubility. Elevating LiNO3 solubility through the dielectric con-
stant would aﬀord a possibility to tune LiPS speciation and reg-
ulate interfacial property. Figure 3a presents the volcanic corre-
lation between the LiTFSI concentration and the dielectric con-
stant. The LiTFSI-concentration dependence of electrolyte viscos-
ity and ionic conductivity are shown in Figure S6 (Supporting In-
formation). The maximum dielectric constant of bulk electrolyte
(ɛ > 11 at 1.0 M LiTFSI) approximately doubles compared with
neat DME and DOL. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy
(Figure S7, Supporting Information) was performed to unveil
the change in electron cloud density induced by LiTFSI concen-
tration variation. The upshift of 7Li spectrum from low to high
LiTFSI concentration indicates the electrostatic shielding of Li+
parceled by TFSI−, inferring that solvation structure shifts from
SSIPs to CIPs and AGGs since CIPs and AGGs could tightly en-
capsulate Li+ and donate more electrons to Li+. The identical ten-
dency of solvation structure and dielectric constant evidences that
CIP stimulates the larger dielectric polarization than free solvents
and results in dielectric increment in weak solvation electrolytes,
which could promote salt dissolution.
Expectedly, the LiNO3 solubility in electrolytes upon diﬀerent
LiTFSI concentrations shows a similar tendency with the elec-
trolyte dielectric constant (Figure 3b). The maximum saturated
LiNO3 solubility in DME/DOL reaches to about 1.0 M with the
assistance of 1.0 M LiTFSI (Figure S8, Supporting Information).
Based on the LiTFSI solvation structure, the dielectric increment
assisted by Li+–TFSI−CIPs facilitates the dielectric polariza-
tion of bulk electrolyte, which further enables the increased
LiNO3 solubility. Besides, considering the strong cation–anion

hardly soluble LiNO3 by providing additional coordination sites

Accordingly, experimental results and theoretical calculations
identify that the dielectric increment in weak solvation elec-
trolytes is caused by the polarization of CIPs, rather than dielec-
tric decrement induced by the reduction of free solvents. The aug-
ment of dielectric constant is thought as the net contribution of
CIPs after oﬀsetting the dielectric decrement through the reduc-
tion of free solvents by solvating Li+. Besides, the net contribution
fades with the increment of AGGs due to its structure random-
ness. Consequently, dielectric increment in weak solvation elec-
trolytes bestows on the bulk solution a strong ability of shielding
the anion–cation interaction and gives the opportunity of CIPs to
capture the hardly soluble salt, increasing its solubility.

Moreover, the controllability of LiPSs solubility assisted by
dielectric increment endows Li−S batteries governable redox
kinetics. The impacts of high-concentration Li2S8 in cathode
side[33,43] are reﬂected on the discharging curves of Li||C and
Li||S cells (Figure 4c; Figure S14, Supporting Information, re-
spectively). It was observed that 1.0 M LiTFSI electrolyte with
high-concentration LiPS delivers an obvious nucleation feature
at 2.10 V, while the nucleation voltage of 0.1 M LiTFSI electrolyte
is below 2.05 V. According to the relationship between free en-
ergy and electromotive force, the higher nucleation voltage of the
cell with 1.0 M LiTFSI implies a mild driving force for nucleation,
further indicating the promoted polysulﬁde transformation from
soluble LiPS to Li2S2/Li2S. The ahead nucleation leads to the im-
proved nucleation capacity. The LiPS conversion barriers con-
sidering dielectric constant eﬀects (Figure S15, Supporting In-
formation) were calculated by density functional theory (DFT).
The more negative ∆G of high dielectric constant electrolyte rep-
resents spontaneous LiPS reduction, conﬁrming the facilitated
cathode reaction kinetics.
Besides, the negative impacts on the anode side by high-
concentration LiPS could be eliminated by tailoring the compo-
nents of electrolyte through the dielectric increment. Introducing
SEI-forming salts to establish ﬂuoridated SEI is considered as a
potential pathway to prevent Li corrosion from LiPS shuttle[44–46]
and to inhibit Li dendrites, prolonging the cycle life. It has been
reported that lithium hexaﬂuorophosphate (LiPF6) tends to re-
act with LiPS and decompose into ﬂuoride to ﬂuorinate SEI.[47]
Partially replacing LiTFSI with LiPF6 has two attributes: i) the
PF6
– anion contributes to the increased CIP and dielectric incre-
ment, which facilitates the LiPS solubility for facile LiPS conver-
sion; and ii) the mild decomposition of LiPF6 promotes the ﬂuo-
rination of SEI to inhibit LiPS corrosion and Li dendrite growth.
These considerations allow to advance the basic electrolyte (1.0 M
LiTFSI + 3 wt.% LiNO3 in DME/DOL) of Li–S batteries to the up-
dated electrolyte (0.4 M LiPF6 + 0.6 M LiTFSI + 1.0 M LiNO3 in
DME/DOL).

a short-term cyclability (less than 500 h) under the condition
of 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure S16, Supporting Infor-
mation). In addition, Figure S17 (Supporting Information) illus-
trates that the Coulombic eﬃciency of Li||Cu with the updated

3. Conclusion
In summary, a strategy of dielectric increment of weak solvation
electrolytes is precisely demonstrated. The CIP in electrolytes
dominates the dielectric constant of weak solvation electrolytes
and aﬀects salt dissolution. Detailly, CIP holds great dielectric po-
larization that contributes to the dielectric increment of bulk elec-
trolyte even after oﬀsetting the dielectric discount brought by the
reduced free solvent. On the contrary, AGG jumbles the solvation
structure into a cluster and cannot adequately shield the external
electric ﬁeld, resulting in dielectric decrement. Accordingly, elec-
trolyte composition can be delicately designed. Electrolyte with
high dielectric constant is convinced for increasing the solubility
of Li salts and LiPSs, which mediates the Li2S2/Li2S deposition
at the cathode side and tailors the SEI at the anode side. Li−S
cells with updated electrolyte yield remarkable capacity retention
of 96% over 175 cycles at 0.2 C. The revealed dielectric increment
of electrolytes by salt association is expected to guide the design of
advanced high-concentration electrolytes and broaden the option
of salts for directionally customizing electrode–electrolyte inter-
face reactions.
