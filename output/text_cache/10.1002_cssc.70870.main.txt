ABSTRACT
Lithium–sulfur batteries have attracted significant attention because of their high theoretical capacity and energy density.
Reducing the amount of inert electrolyte in the cell is essential for further improving the practical energy density. However,
high viscosity and poor sulfur utilization issues occur from the saturation of soluble long-chain lithium polysulfides (LiPSs,
Li2Sx, 4 ≤x ≤8) in low-electrolyte systems, leading to sluggish redox kinetics. In this study, we propose benzenesulfonyl fluoride
(BF) as a sulfur–fluoride exchangeable moiety-integrated mediator (SFEMM) that alleviates the solubility limitation by promoting
the chemical conversion of LiPSs. The ─SO2F group of BF acts as an electrophile, inducing a reaction with nucleophilic LiPSs.
Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses confirmed that BF promotes LiPSs conver-
sion while producing LiF as reaction evidence, confirming the SFEMM behavior of BF. Moreover, density functional theory (DFT)
calculations revealed that BF undergoes a thermodynamically favorable sulfur–fluoride exchange reaction with LiPSs, further
supporting its role in promoting polysulfide conversion. Electrochemical evaluations demonstrate that improved sulfur utilization
is enabled by BF, even at a low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 7 μL mg−1, with a high initial discharge capacity of 1317 mAh g−1
and an initial coulombic efficiency of 97.9%.
1 | Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered next-generation bat-
teries because of their high theoretical capacity (1675 mAh g−1),
theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), and cost-effectiveness
[1–4]. Specifically, the high energy density of Li–S batteries pro-
vides a considerable advantage for lightweight applications, such
as electric vehicles and aerospace technologies [5, 6]. During the
discharge of Li–S batteries, sulfur (S8) at the cathode is ultimately
converted to Li2S passing through lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), typ-
ically denoted as Li2Sx (4 ≤x ≤8), via electrochemical reduction
with Li+. This electrochemical reaction proceeds through multiple
sequential steps [7–10]. LiPSs are partially soluble in the electrolyte
during electrochemical reactions; thus, the high resistance behav-
ior of solid substances, including S and Li2S, due to poor Li+ dif-
fusion of the sulfur cathode is mitigated.

A low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, which refers to the ratio of
the electrolyte volume to the sulfur mass and is typically
expressed as μL mg−1, leads to a strategy for increasing the energy
density of a battery. As the electrolyte is inert for energy storage,
the minimization of the electrolyte is the key factor in realizing
the high practical energy density of Li–S batteries [11, 12].
According to previous reports, a sulfur loading of 10 mg cm−2
results in an energy density of 400–450 Wh kg−1 at a low E/S ratio
of 2, whereas a much lower energy density of 100 Wh kg−1 results
in a high E/S ratio of 20 [13–15]. Compared with the energy den-
sity of commercial lithium–ion (Li–ion) batteries (energy density
range of 100–265 Wh kg−1), Li–S batteries with only low E/S
ratios supply higher energy densities [16, 17].
However, the low E/S values significantly restrict the electro-
chemical feasibility of LiPSs by negating the effect of soluble
behavior. The electrochemical redox reaction of LiPSs strongly
relies on the E/S ratio. Insufficient uptake of LiPSs due to low
amounts of electrolyte at low E/S ratios (below 10) leads to
increased polarization within the cell and degraded cell perfor-
mance [18, 19]. This hinders LiPSs from accepting Li+, thus lim-
iting the state of charge of the sulfur cathode [20]. This results in
a kinetic limitation, ultimately making the conversion reaction of
LiPSs difficult. Consequently, both reactant mass transport and
charge transfer are restricted, causing an increase in overpoten-
tial and a decrease in reaction rate [21–26].
Research on electrolyte solvents and additives has been conducted
to improve the usability of LiPSs in environments with low E/S
ratios [27–29]. In particular, from an organic chemistry perspec-
tive, LiPSs inherently exhibit high nucleophilicity because sulfur
atoms contain abundant nonbonding electron pairs [30]. Reaction
pathway control strategies utilizing this characteristic are being
actively explored [31, 32]. In contrast to nucleophiles, electrophiles
readily accept electron pairs and can react with nucleophilic spe-
cies such as LiPSs. This reaction has been widely utilized in the
chemical field for decades [33, 34]. Electrophilic–nucleophilic
reactions can also effectively resolve issues within battery systems
[35]. However, research on issues of Li–S batteries has not yet been
extensively explored through the introduction of electrophiles.
In this study, we propose a benzenesulfonyl fluoride (BF,
PhSO2F) additive that facilitates the electrochemical reactivity
of LiPSs. The –SO2F functional group of the BF additive is known
to be electrophilic [36]. The –SO2F group exhibits a unique bal-
ance between high electrophilicity arising from the strong
electron-withdrawing effect of the oxygen atoms and the effective
leaving ability of the fluorine atom. This reactivity is further rein-
forced by the electron-delocalizing effect of the adjacent phenyl
group, which collectively enhances the chemical stability of BF
under electrochemical conditions [37]. Moreover, –SO2F group is
known for its high stability and chemical value because it can be
activated to react with nucleophiles [38–40]. On the basis of these
characteristics, BF enhances the reactivity of LiPSs through
electrophilic–nucleophilic reactions. This reaction can produce
lithium fluoride (LiF), which provides evidence of the reaction. In
fact, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed the
formation of LiF, demonstrating that the –SO2F group of BF reacts
with LiPSs. ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) measurements revealed
that BF spontaneously enhances the reactivity of synthesized
LiPS (Li2S6), suggesting that BF effectively activates nucleophilic
sulfur species. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calcu-
lations confirmed that the reaction between BF and Li2S6 is
thermodynamically spontaneous (ΔG = −59.96 kcal mol−1), sug-
gesting that this interaction readily occurs under practical cell
operating conditions. These results indicate that BF acts as a
sulfur–fluoride exchangeable moiety-integrated mediator (SFEMM).
On the basis of the results of the electrochemical evaluations, the
BF-containing electrolyte reduces the overvoltage and accelerates
the conversion rates of LiPSs. BF demonstrates a high initial spe-
cific capacity of 1317 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C under a moderate sulfur
loading of 3 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7 μL mg−1, which dem-
onstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method for promoting
efficient sulfur utilization and stable electrochemical performance.
2 | Results and Discussion
First, a series of chemical additives containing sulfonyl fluoride
groups were evaluated on lithium (Li) metal for use in Li–S cells.
Figure 1a,b show pristine Li metal and BF-immersed Li, indicat-
ing that the BF-immersed Li remains nonreactive and maintains
a stable interface. Among the candidate additives, ethenesulfonyl
fluoride (EEF)-immersed Li showed gradual reactivity, with the
reaction initiating at the edges and slowly propagating inward
(Figure 1c). By comparison, ethanesulfonyl fluoride (EAF)-
immersed Li exhibited significantly higher reactivity, leading
to complete reaction of the entire Li surface (Figure 1d). To exam-
ine the morphology of additive-immersed Li metal, scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was conducted on samples
immersed in BF, EEF, and EAF for 1 min, respectively. As shown
in Figure 1e,f, BF-immersed Li metal exhibited a morphology
similar to that of pristine Li metal. In contrast, EEF-immersed Li
metal showed pitting corrosion, which was more clearly observed
at the 100 nm scale (Figure 1g). In the case of EAF-immersed Li
metal, more severe corrosion was clearly observed as shown in
Figure 1h. To analyze the height differences induced by the reac-
tion, morphological changes were also observed in the pseudo-
color 2D surface plots obtained from the SEM data (Figure
S1a–d). The 3D surface plots further confirmed that BF-immersed
Li retained a surface morphology analogous to pristine Li, indicat-
ing minimal disruption (Figure 1i,j). In contrast, the EEF-
immersed Li surface exhibited pronounced localized pitting and
greater depth variation, suggesting severe interfacial degradation
and nonuniform surface evolution (Figure 1k). Conversely, EAF-
immersed Li exhibited significant surface damage, resulting in a
deeply indented and highly roughened morphology (Figure 1l).
Despite the clear differences in morphology, XPS analysis of the
same samples used for SEM confirmed the formation of LiF on the
surfaces of all three samples (Figure S1e–j). As shown in Figure
S1e,f, the higher Li 1s XPS intensity in EEF compared to BF indi-
cates enhanced formation of Li–F species at 55.8 eV [41]. Similarly,
the F 1s peak confirms that the Li–F assigned peak is higher in EEF
at 685.5 eV (Figure S1h,i) [42]. However, EAF showed a higher C–F
peak intensity at 687.8 eV than Li-F at 685.5 eV, indicating that not
only the –SO2F group but also the ethyl group participated in the
reaction. The differences in LiF intensity suggest that the molecular
moiety influences how readily F is released from the –SO2F group
upon reaction with highly reducing Li metal. Although LiF can
serve as a beneficial solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) component,
uncontrolled interfacial reactivity in EEF and EAF led to

To further evaluate the electrochemical performance, BF electro-
lytes with concentrations of 10, 30, and 50 mM were prepared.
The BF concentration of 30 mM was selected because it exhibits
low overpotential, interfacial stability, and high capacity in Li/Li
symmetric cells and Li–S cells (Figure S2a,b). Thus, the BF con-

tests. To evaluate the fundamental properties, the ionic conduc-
tivity and potential window of the BF-containing electrolyte and
the pristine electrolyte were determined (Figure S3a–c). The
ionic conductivity of the BF-containing electrolyte was similar
to that of the pristine electrolyte. Both electrolytes were electro-
chemically stable from 0 to 4 V versus Li/Li+ without significant
irreversible oxidation and reduction behaviors.

XPS analysis was conducted on cathode electrodes taken from
the cells after completing the first cycle and discharging to
2.1 V. The S 2p spectrum of the cathode using the pristine elec-
trolyte revealed sulfur-containing species derived from both the
electrolyte and the LiPSs (Figure 2d). The S 2p peak at 161.1 eV
corresponds to the terminal sulfur (ST) of LiPSs, whereas the
peak at 162.5 eV is associated with the bridging sulfur (SB) of
LiPSs [48–50]. Additionally, the peaks at 167.2 and 169.0 eV
can be attributed to the sulfonyl group or thiosulfate species
and the sulfone group of the triflimide (TFSI) anion, respectively
[50, 51]. The S 2p peaks closely matched the pristine electrolyte
peaks but exhibited a higher intensity owing to the presence of
BF, as shown in Figure 2g. Moreover, the BF-containing electro-
lyte showed a reduced contribution from neutral sulfur, accom-
panied by intensified signals attributed to the SB and ST sulfur
species, indicating the facilitated conversion of elemental sulfur
into polysulfide intermediates. For the cathode with the pristine
electrolyte, the Li 1s XPS spectrum exhibits peaks at 56.0, 54.6,
and 54.0 eV, attributed to the Li–F, Li–S, and Li–O species,
respectively (Figure 2e) [41, 52, 53]. The Li–F peak is more prom-
inent in the Li 1s spectrum for the BF sample, as shown in
Figure 2h. In Figure 2f,i for F 1s the Li–F peak at 685.8 eV for
the BF sample is higher than that for the pristine sample
[42, 54]. These results confirm that LiF formation is evident from
the reaction of the BF additive on the cathode. Moreover, com-
pared with organic–rich CEI layers, LiF-rich layers improve the
ion dynamics on the cathode surface and facilitate ion transport
through LiF, enabling higher Li+ transport [55]. Based on these
results, BF can function as a SFEMM when long–chain LiPSs
(Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4) are present, as shown in Figure 2j.
Long-chain LiPSs are thermodynamically reducible but have
kinetic problems under low E/S [24]. For short-chain Li2S2
and Li2S, the large difference in ionic radii between lithium ions
and sulfur ions makes it difficult to obtain crystalline Li2S2,
which is difficult to observe through direct electrochemical meas-
urements. According to DFT calculation, Li2S2 is thermodynam-
ically unstable and readily decomposes into polysulfides and Li2S
[56, 57]. This characteristic indicates that short-chain Li2S2 itself
can be reduced without difficulty at the end of discharge to
become more stable, while the reduction of LiPSs is considered
the main issue [58]. Therefore, BF can predominantly enhance
the reactivity of long–chain LiPSs, thus aiding the reduction of
sulfur. During the reaction, LiF is produced as a byproduct
and serves as evidence of the reaction.
The cycling performance at 0.1 C is shown in Figure 3a.
Compared with the cell with pristine electrolyte, the BF-contain-
ing electrolyte cell exhibited a high initial discharge capacity
(1317 mAh g−1) with improved retention for 70 cycles. In the gal-
vanostatic charge–discharge profiles at selected cycles, all the
cells exhibit typical potential profiles (Figure 3b,c). Interestingly,
compared with the pristine electrolyte, the BF-containing elec-
trolyte had a higher Q1 value (Figure 3d). Notably, the increased
Q1 value is evident in the BF-containing electrolyte during the
early cycle, indicating that BF increases the sulfur utilization
of LiPSs during cycling. Both batteries showed a gradual decrease
in Q1 with increasing number of cycles. However, the Q1 value
remained high at approximately 240 mAh g−1 even after 70 cycles
when BF was added. In the analysis of the polarization, the BF-
containing electrolyte cell showed lower polarization than the
pristine electrolyte cell exhibited, but both cells revealed
increased polarization behaviors as the number of cycles
increased (Figure 3e) [59, 60]. Moreover, compared with the pris-
tine electrolyte, the BF-containing electrolyte cell delivered a
lower activation barrier during the charging step, and these
behaviors remained after 70 cycles (Figure S5a,b). Activation bar-
riers can be identified in the reconversion of Li2S to long-chain
LiPSs and affect the polarization between charge and discharge
[61, 62]. Therefore, these observational results suggest that BF
reversibly contributes to the reconversion of Li2S, thus lowering
the activation barrier. Furthermore, the EIS measurements were
analyzed after several cycle tests of the cells with BF-containing
and pristine electrolytes (Figure S6). The BF-containing electro-
lyte presented a smaller RCT of 53.1 Ω, whereas the pristine elec-
trolyte showed a larger RCT of 72.5 Ω. The EIS results suggest that
BF improves interfacial charge-transfer kinetics by forming a less
resistive interphase. Additionally, the atomic concentration anal-
ysis of the polished Li metal anode was analyzed (Figure S7).
After two cycles, the sulfur (S) and fluorine (F) contents of
the pristine electrolyte were similar at 6.55 and 6.54 wt%, respec-
tively. In contrast, the BF-containing electrolyte decreased the S
content to 4.71 wt% and increased the F content to 14.81 wt%,
suggesting that the F compounds generated by the initial BF lim-
ited the number of available sites for S. The Li metal anode sur-
face was subsequently analyzed using SEM–energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS) after long-term cycling. As the
number of cycles increased, the surface of the Li-metal electrode
in the initial electrolyte exhibited a rough and heterogeneous
morphology that seemed to show corrosion, accompanied by
localized F signal interface reactions (Figure S8a,b) [63, 64]. In
contrast, the BF-containing electrolyte yielded a uniform mor-
phology with homogeneously distributed F signals, as shown
in Figure S8c,d. This homogeneous F distribution after prolonged
cycling is consistent with the formation of Li–F-rich interphase
from the initial reaction of BF, which can contribute to mitigat-
ing localized corrosion and promoting uniform Li deposition.
The conversion of LiPSs in pristine and BF-containing electro-
lytes was evaluated by a cyclic voltammetry (CV) test. As shown
in Figure 4a, there were two peaks at 2.2–2.3 V (reduction 1, R1
peak) and 1.8–1.9 V (reduction 2, R2 peak), which were assigned
to the stepwise reduction of LiPSs to Li2S2/Li2S, and one peak was
observed at 2.5–2.6 V (oxidation 1, O1 peak), which corresponds
to the reverse oxidation of Li2S2/Li2S to LiPSs. In the CV profile,
the pristine electrolyte exhibited a large ΔE of 0.803 V, whereas
the BF-containing electrolyte showed a small ΔE of 0.687 V.
Furthermore, compared with the pristine electrolyte, the BF-
containing electrolyte resulted in a higher current flow, confirm-
ing that the redox reaction of LiPSs was greater. This suggests
that the BF additive contributes to the high utilization of
LiPSs. Additionally, for the BF-containing electrolyte, the R1 and
R2 peaks shifted in the positive direction, whereas the O1 peak
shifted in the negative direction. The shifts in the peak indicate
an overall faster reaction rate and faster conversion of LiPSs [65].
In terms of the Tafel slopes, compared with the pristine electro-
lyte (91.9 mV dec−1), the BF-containing electrolyte exhibited a
smaller Tafel slope of 80.6 mV dec−1 at the R1 peak, which cor-
responds to long-chain LiPSs (Figure 4b). Moreover, the R2 peak

3 | Conclusion
In this study, BF serve as a multifunctional additive that accel-
erates the redox conversion of long–chain LiPSs (Li2S8 →Li2S6 →
Li2S4). Specifically, BF acts as a SFEMM, enhancing the reactivity
of LiPSs with the formation of LiF observed as evidence of the
reaction. The GITT and CV results indicate that the BF-contain-
ing electrolyte mitigated kinetic issues by enhancing LiPSs
reactivity in Li–S batteries. Furthermore, the conversion of
LiPSs was directly confirmed using UV–vis spectroscopy, indicat-
ing a spontaneous reaction between BF and LiPSs. XPS analysis
further validated the feasible LiPSs conversion through the
SFEMM via BF by confirming the S moiety and LiF during
the initial discharge stage. Furthermore, DFT calculations dem-
onstrated that the BF– Li2S6 reaction is thermodynamically
favorable (ΔG = −59.96 kcal mol−1), providing theoretical sup-
port for the proposed conversion mechanism. Under a sulfur
loading of 3 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7 μL mg−1, the BF-
containing electrolytes delivered a high initial discharge capacity
of 1317 mAh g−1 and stable cycling over 70 cycles, with an initial
coulombic efficiency of 97.9%.

4.4 | Preparation of the Electrolyte
The pristine electrolyte was composed of 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in
2:8 volume ratio of DOL/DME, with the addition of 0.4 wt%
LiNO3. BF electrolytes were prepared by adding BF to the pristine
electrolyte at concentrations of 10, 30, and 50 mM. All the elec-
trolytes were mixed in an argon-filled glove box for 12 h.
4.5 | Synthesis of Polysulfide Solution
The polysulfide was prepared by mixing sulfur powder and Li2S
at a molar ratio of 5:1 with a DOL/DME solvent mixture (2:8, v/v%)
at a concentration of 1.5 M in a glove box at 50°C for 12 h.
4.6 | Preparation of the Polysulfide Adsorption
Test and Spontaneous Samples
The adsorption test sample solvents were prepared in a DOL/DME
solvent mixture (2:8, v/v%) as pristine and with the addition of
30 mM BF, also without LiTFSI and LiNO3. Consequently, the
UV–vis test samples were prepared by adding the synthesized poly-
sulfide solutions at a concentration of 1.5 mM. The 1.5 M Li2S6
solution was mixed with BF at a 1:1 volume ratio and precipitation
dried using a convection oven at 30°C for 12 h in a glove box.
4.7 | Li–S Coin Cell Fabrication and Measurement
S/C, Super P and PVDF were mixed at a weight ratio of
75.6:14.4:10 with the addition of NMP. The electrode was initially
dried in a convection oven at 60°C for 30 min and then thor-
oughly dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 3 h. For the battery
tests, all 2032-type coin cells (Wellcos) were fabricated in an
argon-filled glove box (O2: < 0.1 ppm and H2O: < 0.1 ppm). The
cells were assembled using a prepared composite electrode, Li
metal, and carbon-coated PE. Cells for cycling performance,
C-rate performance, GITT, and EIS were prepared at a sulfur
loading of 3 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7 μL mg−1 using the
same fabrication method.
4.8 | Characterization
FE–SEM images were acquired using a JCM-7000 microscope
(JEOL), and the cross section was polished using an IB-
19510CP (JEOL). XPS was conducted to analyze the elemental
composition of the pelleted byproduct using a K-Alpha XPS sys-
tem (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The polysulfide adsorption of the
bare and BF solution samples was analyzed using a UV–visible
spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu). FT-IR measurements
were performed on an IRTracer-100(Shimadzu).
4.9 | Electrochemical Measurements
All cycle experiments were performed using a battery cycler
(WBCS3000, WonATech). EIS analysis was conducted in the fre-
quency range of 1 MHz to 10 mHz at an input voltage of 100 mV.
Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed using SUS 316
as the working electrode and Li metal as the reference electrode
at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 over a potential range of −1.5–5.2 V.
CV was performed at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s−1 in the potential
range of 1.5–2.8 V.
4.10 | Computational Methods
All DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio
Simulation Package (VASP 6.5.1). The projector augmented wave
(PAW) method was employed to describe the interactions
between core and valence electrons. The generalized gradient
approximation in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE)
form was adopted to treat the electron exchange and correlation
energy [75]. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was
applied throughout all calculations. Since the systems investi-
gated consist of isolated molecular species, the Brillouin zone
was sampled using only the Γ-point (1 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–
Pack k-point mesh). All molecules were placed in a cubic vacuum
supercell of 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å to minimize spurious interactions
between periodic images. Dipole corrections were applied along
the z-axis (LDIPOL; IDIPOL = 4) to eliminate artificial electro-
static interactions arising from the asymmetry of polar molecules
under periodic boundary conditions. Geometry optimizations
were carried out using the conjugate gradient algorithm
(IBRION = 2) until the maximum residual force on each atom
was less than 0.02 eV Å−1. The electronic self-consistency loop
was converged to within 10−5 eV per cell. Spin-polarization
was not included (ISPIN = 1) as all species considered are
closed-shell molecules. Harmonic vibrational frequencies were
computed using the finite difference method (IBRION = 5) with
a displacement step of 0.015 Å, and a tighter electronic conver-
gence criterion of 10−7 eV was applied for these calculations.
Gibbs free energies were obtained by combining the electronic
energies with the vibrational, thermal, and entropic contribu-
tions derived from the frequency calculations at 298.15 K.
