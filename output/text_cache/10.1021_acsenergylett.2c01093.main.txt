ABSTRACT: The reliability and durability of lithium metal
(Li0)−sulfur batteries are largely limited by the undesired Li0
plating-stripping irreversibility and the detrimental polysulfide
dissolution, yet approaches that can simultaneously address the
above anodic and cathodic problems are scarce. Herein, we
report the stable operation of a Li0-SPAN (sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile) battery via an anode−cathode dual-passiva-
tion approach. By combination of a fluorinated localized high
concentration electrolyte (LHCE) and a Li3N-forming additive
(TMS-N3), robust and highly conductive electrode passivation
layers are formed in situ on the surface of both the Li0 anode
and the SPAN cathode. The resulting highly reversible,
dendrite-free, and high-density Li0 plating morphology enables a high Coulombic efficiency of 99.4%. Advanced tender
energy X-ray spectroscopy also reveals the eliminated Li2S formation and minimized polysulfide dissolution in SPAN cathodes,
leading to a high capacity of 580 mAh/gSPAN and stable cycling with negligible capacity decay (0.7%) for 800 cycles. This
electrode−electrolyte interphase engineering strategy has tackled the major limitations of Li−S batteries in both ether- and
carbonate-based electrolyte systems and under a wide temperature range from −10 to +50 °C, thus providing insightful
guidelines for the rational design of highly durable and high-energy-density Li0-S batteries.
L
ithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the enabling technology
for the electrification of our society, whose achievable
energy density nonetheless has reached the ceiling
(∼300 Wh kg−1), thus making it difficult for them to meet the
ever-growing energy density demand in applications such as
heavy-duty vehicles and commercial jets. In addition, the
current lithium-ion battery technology requires minerals such
as cobalt and nickel to fabricate the positive electrode. Supplies
of these metals are limited, and their mining often causes great
social and environmental damage. To address the LIB’s energy
density barrier and achieve a desirable energy density of 500
Wh kg−1,1 and to alleviate the environmental and social
problems of mining the toxic and expensive minerals, both the
anode and cathode chemistries need substantial change and
development. Lithium metal−sulfur (Li0-S) batteries have
attracted considerable attention among the alternative
candidates due to their high theoretical energy density, in
which the Li0 anode possesses a 10-fold higher capacity in
comparison to the conventional graphite anode (3860 mAh
g−1 vs 372 mAh g−1), while the sulfur cathode could deliver a
theoretical specific energy density of 2680 Wh kg−1 that is
∼4.6 times higher than those of conventional LIB cathodes.2 In
addition, sulfur is the 16th most abundant element on Earth,
and more than 70 million tons of sulfur are produced each
year. This makes it an ideal ingredient for EV batteries.
Unfortunately, the high reactivity and dramatic volume change
of Li0 anodes during cycling lead to a poor Coulombic
efficiency (CE) and pose a safety concern.3 Furthermore, the
sulfur cathodes suffer from the detrimental dissolution and

severe capacity loss and short cycling life.4
To tackle the above limitations and improve the
comprehensive performance of Li−S batteries, various
strategies have been proposed, including the construction of
an artificial interface,5,6 adoption of three-dimensional (3D)
hosts for both Li0 anodes and sulfur cathodes,7,8 and
engineering of electrolyte formulations.9 In particular,
manipulating the electrolyte solvation structure and chemistry
can spontaneously and in situ generate a robust, electron-
insulating, yet ion conductive solid−electrolyte interphase
(SEI) film on the Li0 anode and a cathode−electrolyte
interphase (CEI) film on the sulfur cathode. Such passivation
layers have been proven effective in stabilizing the Li0 anode10
or minimizing the shuttling of polysulfide species.11 It has been
well recognized that a LiF-rich SEI layer favors suppressing the
undesired anode morphologies such as dendritic and dead Li0,
due to its high surface energy toward the Li metal.12 This LiF-
rich SEI layer can be created by adopting fluorinated localized
high concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) that feature a high
portion of aggregated anion−Li+ contact ion pairs (CIP) in the
electrolyte structure, low viscosity, good conductivity, and
good electrolyte wettability.13 Previously, LHCEs have been
applied in the Li−S battery field and exhibited improved
stability with Li0 anodes and inhibited LiPS shuttling in
comparison with traditional ether-based or carbonate-based
electrolytes.14−16 However, conventional LHCEs usually lead
to the formation of an SEI dominated by LiF, which has an
intrinsic low ionic conductivity (10−9 S cm−1)17 that retards
the Li plating/stripping kinetics.18,19 Alternatively, the more
conductive Li3N (5.767 × 10−4 S cm−1) with high stability
against Li0 anodes promises it to be an ideal SEI component.20
More ideally, a Li3N−LiF-rich composite layer could combine
the lithiophobic nature of LiF and the high conductivity merit
of Li3N to synergistically stabilize Li metal anodes even under
high-current-density conditions.18 Yet, due to the low solubility
of Li3N-forming additives in LHCE, there have been few
reports of LHCE formulations capable of forming the hybrid
LiF−Li3N-rich SEI/CEI layers.
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) is a promising substitute
material for the elemental sulfur cathode, in which the short-
chain sulfur is covalently tethered to a conjugated pyridine
backbone during all stages of the redox reactions.21 Such a
unique molecular structure renders good electron conduction
and mitigated polysulfide dissolution behaviors in SPAN
cathodes than in the conventional S cathodes. In carbonate
electrolytes, the dissolution-free solid-to-solid conversion
mechanism of SPAN cathodes enables good cycling stability.22
However, in ether-based electrolytes that show a high solubility
toward LiPS, the unbound short-chain sulfur (e.g., Li2S)
generated during discharge would transform into high-order
LiPS upon the charging process, thus inducing a similar
solution chemistry to elemental sulfur and polysulfide.21,23 The
electrolyte preference in SPAN cathodes is opposite to the case
in Li0 anodes: namely, Li0 anodes are stable toward the ether-
based electrolytes yet vulnerable to the carbonate electrolytes.
Thus far, synchronically enhancing the Li metal anodes’
reversibility and suppressing the LiPS dissolution still remain a
challenging task.
In this work, we report an anode−cathode dual-passivation
approach of coupling a fluorinated LHCE with a strong Li3N-
forming additive to simultaneously address the stability issue of
Li metal anodes and SPAN cathodes in both carbonate-based
and ether-based systems. In our electrolyte design, lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) salt, dimethyl carbonate
(DMC) or 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent, and 1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (TTE) diluent
were combined to formulate the fluorinated LHCE, while
trimethylsilyl azide (TMS-N3) with high solubility in LHCE
was leveraged as the Li3N-forming additive (Figure 1a). Here,
TMS-N3 has been previously reported to be capable of
inducing a stable Li3N-rich SEI layer on Li0 anodes and
improving the Li0 reversibility (up to 96%) in conventional
dilute electrolytes,24 yet there have been no examples of

the high local concentration of FSI−anion in addition to the
rich fluorine group in the TTE diluent guarantees a LiF-rich
passivation layer, while the TMS-N3 additive could further be
reduced to Li3N, thus affording a Li3N−LiF composite
passivation layer on both Li0 anodes and SPAN cathodes, as
confirmed by an XPS analysis and STEM characterization.
Consequently, a dendrite-free Li morphology with a high CE
of 99.4% is achieved in the carbonate-based LHCE; in
addition, the LiPS dissolution in the ether-based LHCE was
also eliminated, which was validated by both X-ray absorption
spectroscopy (XAS) and XPS measurements. The as-paired
Li0-SPAN full cells exhibit good cycling stability in both high-
temperature and low-temperature conditions. This anode−
cathode dual-passivation approach via the “fluorinated LHCE
+TMS-N3 additive” electrolyte design offers a reliable
approach to improve the performance of Li0-S batteries,
which represents a key electrolyte design strategy for long-lived
and low-cost rechargeable batteries.
The feasibility of the “fluorinated LHCE+TMS-N3 additive”
electrolyte design to effectively passivate the Li0 anode was first
investigated in the carbonate-based LHCE system, as Li metal
is more vulnerable to carbonate electrolytes in comparison to
ether-based electrolytes. The fluorinated-carbonate-based
LHCE baseline was formulated by dissolving 1.4 M LiFSI
salt in a mixture of DMC, EC, and TTE (2:0.2:3 molar ratio),
denoted DMC/TTE. The “fluorinated LHCE+TMS-N3
additive” electrolyte was realized by adding a proper amount
of TMS-N3 to the above carbonate LHCE baseline electrolyte,
where the optimal amount of TMS-N3 was determined to be 2
wt % in this work according to a solubility test of TMS-N3 in
LHCE (Figure S1) and a Coulombic efficiency measurement
of Li∥Cu cells (Figure S2). For comparison, a conventional
carbonate electrolyte with the composition of 1 M LiPF6 in
EC/EMC (3:7 by weight) with 8 wt % FEC additive was
selected as another baseline (defined as EC/EMC/FEC), due
to its high popularity and well-recognized capability in
affording a LiF-rich SEI layer.21 We thereafter evaluated the
lithium stripping/plating Coulombic efficiency in different
carbonate electrolytes using the PNNL protocol22 (see the
Supporting Information for the detailed CE determination
steps). As shown in Figure 1b, the DMC/TTE/TMS-N3
electrolyte can deliver a remarkable CE of 99.4% at 22 °C,
which also reached as high as 99.3%, 99.4%, and 99.5% at −10,
50, and 80 °C, respectively. In contrast, the DMC/TTE
electrolyte without TMS-N3 additive showed lower CEs of
98.4%, 98.8%, and 99.0% at −10, 22, and 50 °C, respectively,
implying the crucial role of the TMS-N3 additive in stabilizing
the anode−electrolyte interface. Yet, the conventional EC/
EMC/FEC electrolyte presented an inferior CE of ∼97% at all
tested temperatures (Figure 1c and Figure S3). To further
evaluate the long-term Li0 reversibility, the cycling stability
measurement of 100% depth of discharge (DOD) Li0∥Cu cells
was performed at varying current density, cycling capacity, and
operating temperature conditions. As shown in Figure S4, the
DMC/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte generally outperformed the
baseline electrolytes under all testing conditions, with regard to
the average CE and lifetime of Li∥Cu cells.
To reveal the origin of the high CE in the DMC/TTE/
TMS-N3 electrolyte, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
was leveraged to analyze the properties of the SEI layer on the
surface of the cycled Li metal anode. Figure 2a,b depicts the N
1s and F 1s XPS profiles of the DMC/TTE/TMS-N3 derived
SEI before etching and after etching for 10 min (see Figure S5
for N 1s and F 1s XPS profiles with different etching times of 0,
1, 2, 5, and 10 min). Impressively, prominent signals of both
LiF (at 684.8 eV in the F 1s XPS spectrum23) and Li3N (at
396.4 eV in the N 1s spectrum24) can be well identified. Here,
the participation of the LiF component in the SEI layer is
enabled by the rich amounts of Li−FSI−contact ion pairs in
the LHCE electrolyte that allow the reduction of the FSI−
anion to form the LiF-rich SEI,11 while the formation of Li3N
results from the reduction of the azide group on the Li0
surface.17 In addition, the C 1s, O 1s, and S 2p XPS depth
profiles (Figure S6) jointly suggest that the SEI is rich in COR,
COOR, LiF, Li3N, F-SOx, N-SOx, and C−N in the surface,
while the inside SEI layer is mainly composed of COOR, N-
SOx, and Li2O. In comparison, the SEI layers formed in DMC/
TTE baselines only show a relatively high LiF content but are

In addition to improving the Li0 plating/stripping
reversibility, the Li3N-LiF-rich SEI layer could also effectively
reduce the charge transfer resistance (Rct), largely due to the
high ionic conductivity of the Li3N component. Figure 2c
depicts the EIS plots of symmetrical Li∥Li cells after 50 cycles
under three different electrolytes (see Figure S10 for the EIS
plots before cycling). The EC/EMC/FEC-based cell showed
the highest Rct of 54 Ω cm2. In sharp contrast, the Rct
decreased to 26 Ω cm2 for the DMC/TTE-based cell, since
most DMC molecules in the LHCE electrolyte are coordinated
with cations and will be less reduced by Li metal, thus helping
to form a thin SEI layer.25 More importantly, introducing the
Li3N-forming additive (i.e., TMS-N3) into the LHCE electro-
lyte can further reduce the Rct to 4.2 Ω cm2, which benefits
from the high Li+ conductivity of Li3N that refines the ion
transport properties of the as-formed SEI layer. Furthermore,
the facilitated ionic migration kinetics in the DMC/TTE/
TMS-N3-enabled SEI layer also contributes to a low Li0 plating
overpotential and prolonged cycling life (Figure 2d and Figure
S9), which is consistent with the long-term Li0∥Cu cell cycling
stability shown in Figure S4 and discussed above. When the
advantage above is coupled with the merit of high Li0 plating/
stripping efficiency, an extended cycling life of 500 h for the
symmetrical Li∥Li based on the DMC/TTE/TMS-N3 electro-
lyte can be achieved at 1 mA cm−2 with a cycling capacity of 1
mAh cm−2, superior to those of the cells based on EC/EMC/
FEC (300 h) and DMC/TTE (350 h) (Figure 2d).
The high CE and long cycle life achieved in DMC/TTE/
TMS-N3 is also associated with the smooth Li0 evolution
morphology. As shown in Figure 2e and Figure S11, dendrite-
free and dense Li “chunks” with a large size of ∼10 μm can be
observed when 5 mAh cm−2 of Li0 is deposited on the Cu
electrode at 0.5 mA cm−2 at various temperatures from −10 to
50 °C. The cross-sectional SEM image reveals a thickness of
29.4 μm for the Li0 plated in DMC/TTE/TMS-N3 (Figure 2f),
which is very close to the theoretical thickness of dense Li
(24.25 μm, where 1 mAh cm−2 corresponds to 4.85 μm thick
dense Li0). In comparison, much more porous and thicker Li0
deposits (up to ∼38 μm in thickness) are found in DMC/TTE
electrolyte; such a defective morphology is more profound at
low temperatures (Figures S11d−f and S12a), coherent with
the reduced CE (Figure S3). As expected, the plated Li0 in
EC/EMC/FEC is highly porous and loose, along with the
occurrence of Li0 whiskers at all testing temperatures (Figures
S11g−i and S12b). Overall, these results suggest that
introducing Li3N in the SEI can induce uniform and conformal
Li0 plating over a wide temperature range, which may be due
to the more compact and Li+-conductive SEI structure.
Ether-based electrolytes are known to be relatively stable
against Li metal, while the severe reduction in CE at low
temperature (e.g., <0 °C) due to the sluggish interface ion
desolvation kinetics26 still hinders their practical application.
Herein, a commercial DOL/DME/LiNO3 electrolyte along
with the DME/TTE electrolyte (baseline LHCE) was selected
to benchmark with TMS-N3-modified LHCE (i.e., DME/
TTE/TMS-N3) to explore the versatility of the “fluorinated
LHCE+TMS-N3 additive” concept over a wide temperature
range. Formulations and conductivities for different electro-
lytes are given in Table S1. When the DOL/DME/LiNO3
baseline is used, Li∥Cu cells can deliver high CEs of 99.5% and
99.6% at 22 and 50 °C, respectively, which sharply decreased
to 85.2% at −10 °C, however (Figures S13 and S14a).
Similarly, the Li∥Cu cell using the DME/TTE baseline
delivered relatively high CEs at 22 and 50 °C but an inferior
CE of 98.5% at −10 °C (Figures S13 and S14b). In contrast,
the DME/TTE/TMS-N3-based cell maintained a high CE of
around 99.4% at all testing temperatures (Figures S13 and
14c). We further evaluated the long-term cycling stability of
Li0∥Cu cells at different temperatures. As shown in Figure 3a−
c and Figure S15, when the conventional DOL/DME/LiNO3
electrolyte was employed, the Li−Cu cells were shorted within
100 cycles at all testing temperatures of −10, 22, and 60 °C,
accompanied by a rapidly decreasing CE. On the other hand,
longer lifetimes of ∼200, ∼400, and ∼200 cycles were realized
for the cells using DME/TTE electrolyte at −10, 22, and 60
°C, respectively. For comparison, DME/TTE/TMS-N3-based
cells were able to yield more reliable cycling performance with

The SEM images taken from the cycled Cu electrodes
clearly show that, after 300 cycles at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh
cm−2, the deposited Li in the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte
maintained a highly uniform and smooth surface morphology,
featuring intimately and densely packed “Li chunks” without
any obvious voids or pores (Figure 3d−f). Such a morphology
pattern is similar to that before cycling. For comparison, the
Li0 plated in DME/TTE electrolyte was less compact with
observable voids between Li0 grains at the initial cycling, and
the Li0 became much more porous after 300 cycles (Figure
S18a), suggesting that the as-formed SEI layer undergoes a
breakage/repair mechanism that is not sufficient to guide the
smooth and dense deposition of Li0 over a long time scale,
however. On the other hand, Li0 deposits in DOL/DME/
LiNO3 revealed the loosest morphology with highly porous
and noodlelike Li0 deposits (Figure S18b), accounting for the
poor electrochemical cycling stability.
We hypothesize that the extended cycling life and distinct
Li0 deposition behaviors were due to the difference in SEI
compositions. Through the N 1s and F 1s XPS depth profiles
(Figure S19), it was found that a Li3N−LiF-rich SEI layer was
also formulated in the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte, while
the DME/TTE-derived SEI did not show any obvious Li3N
signal (Figure S22). Similarly to the DMC/TTE/TMS-N3
system, the LiF component promotes an increase in the
packing density of Li0 deposits, as proved by the cross-sectional
SEM images (Figure S17c). Meanwhile, the highly conductive
Li3N component helps to improve the Li+ transport through
the SEI.28 When coupled with the C 1s, O 1s, and S 2p XPS
depth profiles (Figure S20), it is revealed that the SEI is rich
with COR, LiF, Li3N, F−SOx, N−SOx and C−N on the
surface, while the inside SEI is rich in N−SOx, LiNxOy, Li2O,
and Li2S. According to the Si 2p XPS spectra (Figure S21), the
identified Si signal is very weak, and the quantitative Si content
is nearly zero. This implies that Si does not participate in the
SEI formation, possibly due to the dissolution of Si-containing
species (trimethylsilyl) in the electrolyte. In other words, only
azide groups (N3
−) from the additive will be reduced to
promote the Li3N formation in the SEI layer. Due to the
unique SEI layer formed in DME/TTE/TMS-N3, a good
cycling durability of Li−Li cells for 1100 h at 1 mA cm−2 and 1
mAh cm−2 was achieved (Figure S23).
In order to further demonstrate the practicality of the
“fluorinated LHCE+TMS-N3 additive” electrolyte design,
SPAN cathodes were selected to pair with the Li0 anodes to
afford the eventual full cells, due to the high electronic
conductivity, low cost, and high capacity of SPAN.8,29 Despite
the good compatibility with carbonate electrolytes, SPAN
cathodes are less stable in ether-based electrolytes due to the
inherent solubility of LiPS in ether solvents,18,20 which would
lead to severe active material loss and corrosion of Li0 anodes.
However, we demonstrate that reliable Li-SPAN full cells can
be simultaneously achieved in both carbonate-based and ether-
based electrolyte systems using our TMS-N3-incorporated
LHCE. As shown in Figure 4a and Figure S24, even at a high
rate of 3 C and a temperature of −10 °C, an Li0-SPAN cell
using DMC/TTE/TMS-N3 could deliver a specific capacity of
>320 mAh g−1, which is much higher than those of the cells
based on EC/EMC/FEC (160 mAh g−1) and DMC/TTE
(210 mAh g−1). The improved rate capability of DMC/TTE/
TMS-N3 could be due to the low charge transfer resistance
rendered by the highly conductive Li3N as an SEI/CEI
component, as discussed above.

Figure S25a shows the long-term cycling performance of Li0-
SPAN cells under different carbonate electrolytes, from which
we can see the SPAN cathode presented a fast capacity decay
from 390 mAh g−1 to 271 mAh g−1 within 400 cycles when the
EC/EMC/FEC baseline electrolyte was used, which is ascribed
to the low Coulombic efficiency of the Li0 anode in the
carbonate system. In contrast, the full cell with the DMC/
TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte showed a high specific capacity of
569 mAh/g with respect to SPAN mass and 1521 mAh/g with
respect to sulfur mass (S content is 37.4 wt % in SPAN, Table
S2), as well as higher stability with 92% capacity retention after
400 cycles. Under the same conditions, Li0-SPAN cells using
DMC/TTE electrolytes exhibited a lower initial capacity of
440 mAh g−1, which decreased to 348 mAh g−1 after 250
cycles. The CV profile of Li0-SPAN batteries (Figure S26)
shows that a single reduction peak at 1.5 V and a single
oxidation peak at 2.5 V were observed after the initial CV
cycle, suggesting a solid-phase conversion mechanism without
the formation of soluble lithium polysulfide species.30 In the
first scanning cycle, the reduction peak appeared at a lower
voltage of 1.2 V along with a relatively higher reduction
current, which could be ascribed to the activation of SPAN and
breaking of the S−S bond.31
Aside from the improvement of the Li0-SPAN cell
performance in carbonate electrolytes, the “fluorinated
LHCE+TMS-N3 additive” electrolyte can also eliminate the
LiPS dissolution/shuttling problem in conventional ether-
based electrolytes and thus enable enhanced full cell
performance. As shown in Figure S25b, the DME/TTE/
TMS-N3-based full cell retained a good cycling stability with
only 2% capacity decay within 700 cycles, which was superior
to the DME/TTE system, where the capacity decreased from
507 mAh g−1 to 360 mAh g−1 within 620 cycles. At −10, 22,
and 50 °C and 0.5 C, the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 system also
delivered stable cycling for 880, 800, and 470 cycles, with
capacity retentiond of 90.6%, 99.3%, and 98.9%, respectively
(Figure 4b). In sharp contrast, the Li0-SPAN cell’s capacity in a
commercial ether-based baseline (i.e., DOL/DME/LiNO3)
quickly declined from 417 to 203 mAh g−1 within 250 cycles at
22 °C. The delivered specific capacity at 0.5 C of 584 mAh/g
with respect to SPAN mass and 1561 mAh/g with respect to
sulfur mass using the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte is
among the highest values reported (Table S3). In addition,
the Li0-SPAN batteries in this study also showed good cycling
stability, with 99.3% capacity retention after 800 cycles at 0.5
C, which is also comparable to those in the latest literature
reports. Figure 4c presents the performance of Li0-SPAN
batteries with the optimized DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte
under the high SPAN loading (3.83 mg cm−2) and lean
electrolyte conditions (5 μL mg−1 with respect to SPAN mass).
The Li0-SPAN battery showed a high area capacity of 2.16
mAh cm−2 and good specific capacity of 563 mAh/gSPAN
(corresponding to 1505 mAh/gsulfur) and maintained 94.7%
of the initial capacity after 120 cycles at 0.2 C. The achievable
volumetric and gravimetric energy densities are 102 Wh L−1
and 119 Wh kg−1, respectively, based on the parameter shown
in Table S4. Li-SPAN batteries with a low N/P ratio (2.86)
and a high area capacity (2.1 mAh/cm2 for the cathode) were
further assembled. As shown in Figure S34, the cell delivered a
specific capacity of ∼500 mAh/gSPAN, i.e., 1337 mAh/gsulfur,
which is very close to the specific capacity of the half-cell
shown in Figure 4c (564 mAh/cm2). The batteries stably
cycled for 26 cycles with 97.8% capacity retention. When the
cathode loading was further increased to 5.5 mAh/cm2 (SPAN
loading of 10.9 mg/cm2), the cell still delivered an high initial
discharge capacity of 507 mAh/g (Figure S35), which is
comparable to the results obtained under low-mass-loading
conditions.
The elimination of LiPS dissolution and shuttling in the
TMS-N3-incorporated ether-based LHCE system was further
supported by the negligible self-discharge behavior of the Li0-
SPAN cell. As shown in Figure S27, the Li0-SPAN cell can
maintain a stable open-circuit voltage of 2.37 V even after
resting at a fully charged state for 48 h; upon discharge, nearly
100% of capacity could be delivered. Moreover, EIS analysis
reveals that the Rct values of cycled Li0-SPAN cells based on
DME/TTE/TMS-N3, DME/TTE, and DOL/DME/LiNO3
are calculated to be 56, 179, and 286 Ω cm2, respectively
(Figure 4d). Here, the higher Rct in the DME/TTE system
than in the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 system is ascribed to the low
conductivity of LiF-dominated SEI/CEI layers. While the
DOL/DME/LiNO3 system features a highly conductive Li3N-
rich SEI layer, the highest Rct can be attributed to the
dissolution of LiPS and its further shuttling to passivate the
anode surface. Figure S36 shows the polysulfide symmetric
battery tests32 of the three ether-based electrolytes. As can be
seen, cells employing DME/TTE and DOL/DME/LiNO3
electrolytes showed fast capacity fading, and the retained
capacities at the 50th cycle were 161 and 112 mAh/g,
respectively. For comparison, cells using the DME/TTE/
TMS-N3 electrolyte show much better cycling stability and the
capacity at the 50th cycle is 440 mAh/g. This improved
stability in polysulfide symmetrical batteries can be attributed
to the formed LiF−Li3N composite CEI that inhibited the Li2S
and LiPS formation, which will be discussed in the following
section.
Given the relatively good reversibility of Li0 anodes in all
three ether-based electrolytes (Figure 3), the deviation of full
cell performance is believed to be primarily associated with the
stability of SPAN cathodes, while the stability of SPAN
cathodes is dependent on the property of CEI layers formed in
different electrolyte systems. The various components in the
CEI layer were first determined by XPS characterization. As
shown in Figure S28a,b, both LiF and Li3N were identified on
the surface of the SPAN cathode cycled in DME/TTE/TMS-
N3. Nevertheless, only an LiF signal can be detected in the Li0
anode and SPAN cathode cycled in the DME/TTE baseline.
In addition, an XPS quantitative analysis manifests that the
DME/TTE/TMS-N3 system possesses a higher atomic
fraction of F and N in comparison to the baselines (Figure
S28d), indicating the formation of a LiF−Li3N-enriched CEI
layer when the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte is imple-
mented. From the S 2p XPS spectra (Figure 4e), it was found
that, in the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 system, there is no sign of
Li2S on the surface of the SPAN cathode. The S−S peaks at
164 and 165.4 eV18,33 can be indexed to the SPAN matrix,
since the same features are observed in the pristine SPAN
cathode (Figure S29). For comparison, a significant Li2S peak
signal can be identified on the surface of the SPAN cathode
when the DME/TTE and DOL/DME/LiNO3 baseline
electrolytes are used (Figure 4e and Figure S28c), implying
the formation of LiPS on the cathode. Based on these results,
we can deduce that the DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte favors
forming a LiF−Li3N-rich CEI layer on the SPAN cathode,
which could effectively passivate the electrode and prevent the
formation, dissolution, and shuttling of LiPS species.

To investigate the formation mechanism of the Li3N-rich
CEI on the SPAN cathode, we first calculated the HOMO/
LUMO energies of all electrolyte components using density
functional theory (DFT) (Figure S30a). The FSI−anion
showed a low LUMO energy of −0.23 eV. For compassion, the
LUMO energy of TMS-N3 was even lower (−0.54 eV),
implying that it could be reduced at potentials similar to or
higher than that of LiFSI (above 1.3 V vs Li+/Li),34 which is
within the operating voltage range of Li-SPAN batteries.
Previously, the on-site reduction potential of LiFSI was
reported to be 1.3 V vs Li+/Li via a cyclic voltammetry
(CV) method.34 In this study, we measured the CV profiles of
a series of electrolytes with varying TMS-N3 contents under
the Li∥SS cell configuration (Figure S30b). In a scan from 1.5
to 0.01 V vs Li+/Li, an obvious reduction peak was observed
with the on-site reduction potential at ∼1.5 V. With increasing
TMS-N3 contents, the reduction current between 1.5 and 1 V
obviously increased and the reduction peak shifted to higher
potentials from 0.6 to 0.72 V, which all indicate that TMS-N3
participated in CEI formation, and its reduction peak
overlapped with that of LiFSI. Unfortunately, because the
neat TMS-N3 solution without lithium salt has no ionic
conduction capability, an accurate on-site reduction potential
of TMS-N3 could not be determined. N 1s XPS profiles were
employed to further confirm the Li3N formation mechanism in
CEI of SPAN cathodes. As shown in Figure S30c,d, after the
Li-SPAN cell was discharged from 3 to 1 V without a further
charging process, the Li3N signal at 396.6 eV could be clearly
observed on the SPAN cathode when both the DMC/TTE/
TMS-N3 and DMC/EC/FEC/TMSN3 electrolytes were used.
Based on the above DFT calculation results, cyclic
voltammetry (CV) profiles, and XPS profiles, it is reasonable
to deduce that the reduction of TMS-N3 additive occurs in the
potential range of 1.5−1 V, which is within the operating
voltage of the SPAN cathode. In other words, TMS-N3 could
be reduced to promote a Li3N-rich CEI layer on SPAN during
the lithiation process.
To visualize the CEI layer, scanning transmission electron
microscopy (STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) measurements were performed on the SPAN cathodes
cycled in DME/TTE/TMS-N3 electrolyte. As shown in Figure
5a and Figure S31, a core−shell structure for the SPAN
particles with a continuous and uniform surface layer (∼30 nm
in thickness) can be recognized. This surface layer is enriched
with F, N, and O elements, implying the existence of F- and N-
containing species in the CEI layer. In comparison, the interior
core area is dominated by C, S, and N elements, which can be
indexed to the SPAN matrix.
The critical role of the LiF−Li3N-rich CEI in stabilizing the
SPAN cathode was further confirmed by an S K-edge tender X-
ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurement. Figure 5c,d
depicts the normalized XAS spectra of the SPAN cathode
taken from the Li-SPAN cell employing DME/TTE/TMS-N3
electrolyte at different states of discharge (Figure 5b). As can
be seen, the pristine SPAN showed three major peaks at
2468.5 eV (1), 2470.8 eV (2), and 2472.8 eV (3) (Figure 5c),
which correspond to the S 1s transition to SC π*, S−S σ*,
and S−C σ* state in the SPAN structure, respectively.35 After
activation (i.e., during the first charge, Figure 5d), a new
feature peak located at 2474 eV (4) emerged, which could be
ascribed to the S 1s to Li2S σ* transition.36 After a full
discharge to 1 V in the first cycle (Figure 5d), this feature peak
diminished, suggesting eliminated Li2S formation after the
activation process, namely the CEI-layer-forming process,
which is in good agreement with the above XPS results. In
contrast, for the DME/TTE and DOL/DME/LiNO3 cases
(Figure S32a,b), the feature of the S 1s to Li2S σ* transition
vanished on charging to 3 V and again appeared upon
discharge.

Since the LiPS is highly soluble in ether-based electrolytes, the
LiPS formed on the cathode side is highly prone to detach
from the SPAN matrix and shuttle to the anode side, causing
side reactions with Li0 anodes and the loss of active materials.
As a result, on Li0 anodes cycled in ether-based baseline
electrolytes, we detected a prominent high fraction of LiPS in
comparison to that in DME/TTE/TMSN3 (Figure S32c).
This result indicates that the sole LiF-rich CEI layer derived
from DME/TTE and the sole Li3N-rich CEI layer derived
from DOL/DME/LiNO3 can hardly prevent the dissolution of
Li2S into the electrolyte and its subsequent merging into LiPS.
Consequently, the as-formed LiPS would shuttle to the anode
side and cause the accumulation of Li2S on the Li0 anode
surface, as proved by a fast capacity decay, as shown in Figure
S25. On the other hand, for the DME/TTE/TMSN3
electrolyte, the TMS-N3 additive has induced a LiF−Li3N-
rich CEI layer. Such a passivation layer can effectively isolate
the active SPAN particles from the electrolytes and prevent the
Li2S dissolution and LiPS formation as illustrated in Figure
S37b, eventually enabling a solid-to-solid conversion reaction
as widely seen in the case of carbonate electrolytes.37
In summary, we proposed a fluorinated LHCE system with
Li3N-forming additives to modulate the electrode−electrolyte
interface in Li0-SPAN batteries, which enables the in situ and
spontaneous formation of Li3N−LiF-rich passivation films on
both the Li0 anode and the SPAN cathode. The resulting
Li3N−LiF-rich SEI layer enhances Li+ ion transport, improves
lithium stripping/plating reversibility, and inhibits the
dendritic morphology evolution. Meanwhile, the LiF−Li3N-
rich CEI layer contributes to elimination of the Li2S formation
and LPS shuttling. Such an anode−cathode dual-passivation
approach simultaneously addresses the major challenges of Li0-
S batteries, i.e., Li0 instability in carbonate-based systems and
the LiPS shuttling issue in ether-based systems, thus enabling
high-capacity Li0-SPAN batteries with good cycling stability
(99.3% capacity retention after 800 cycles). The design
principle of refining the electrodes’ interfacial chemistry via
electrolyte engineering may benefit the development of other
battery systems where high anode reversibility and low cathode
dissolution are desired, such as sodium/potassium−sulfur and
zinc−iodine batteries.
