ABSTRACT: Achieving long-term stability and consistent capacity in lithium
(Li) metal batteries with sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathodes requires
precisely engineered electrolytes to optimize interphase formation and redox
reversibility. This study presents 1,1-difluoro-2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethane
(DFE)-based localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs), incorporating
fluorinated components such as salt, solvating solvent, and diluent for
improved electrode stability. Molecular dynamics simulations and surface
analyses reveal that the DFE-LHCE with 1,2-bis(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)-
ethane (BTFEE) diluent produces uniform and robust interphase layers on
both cathode and anode, enriched with inorganic species like LiF and Li2O.
These properties lead to prolonged redox reversibility of the SPAN cathode,
suppressed side reactions, and extended cycle life for Li||SPAN cells.
Remarkably, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE enables Li||SPAN coin cells with an areal
capacity of ∼7 mAh cm−2 for SPAN to retain 81.3% capacity after 200 cycles and pouch cells of 0.12 Ah with 8 mAh cm−2 of
SPAN and lean electrolyte to maintain 96.4% capacity over 80 cycles. These findings pave the way for advancing Li||SPAN
battery technologies.
T
he growing demand for high-energy-density recharge-
able batteries has elevated interest in lithium (Li)
metal batteries (LMBs) due to their ability to achieve
higher capacities and energy densities than conventional Li-ion
battery systems.1,2 Sulfur (S) is widely regarded as a promising
candidate for next-generation energy storage systems, thanks to
its remarkably high theoretical gravimetric energy density of
2600 Wh kg−1 and the natural abundance of S material.3,4
Despite these advantages, the practical implementation of Li−
S batteries remains challenging due to several inherent issues,
among which the most critical one is the continuous formation
of soluble Li polysulfide (PS) species, Li2Sn (2 < n ≤8), during
the cycling process. These species are generated during
discharging the starting elemental S active material with S8
structure and charging the reaction product Li2S, causing them
to migrate from the S cathode to the Li metal anode. This
crosstalk initiates undesirable side reactions with Li metal,
leading to the loss of S and Li active materials, self-discharge
via the shuttle effect, and ultimately degraded battery
performance.5,6
To address the challenges associated with the elemental S
cathode, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) has emerged as a
promising alternative for S-based battery systems. SPAN has
unique structures with chemically binding short-chain S species
(Sx, 2 ≤x < 4) within polyacrylonitrile matrices through
interactions with pyrolyzed pyridine groups.7,8 This chemical
confinement prevents the formation, dissolution, and shuttling
of long-chain PSs (Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8), which are commonly
observed in traditional elemental S batteries, thereby

mitigating issues such as self-discharge and active material loss.
However, during discharging, the C−S and S−S bonds in
SPAN can break down and produce S dimer, leading to the
formation of short-chain Li2Sn species (2 ≤n < 4). These
short-chain PS species can gradually dissolve in the electrolytes
within Li||SPAN batteries, leading to PS dissolution and
contributing to the irreversibility of SPAN’s lithiation and
delithiation processes.9−11 These species can slowly dissolve in
the electrolytes in Li||SPAN batteries, making it essential to
address PS dissolution even in SPAN cells to improve
performance stability. In addition, there are significant
challenges from Li metal anode side, including unstable
interphases, dendrite formation, and electrolyte degradation,
especially at high capacity (over 4 mAh cm−2) cycling.12,13
Developing advanced electrolyte systems that enhance
battery stability and efficiency is crucial for addressing the
challenges faced by LMBs. While widely used solvents like 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) offer excellent compatibility with Li
metal anode, their high PS solubility limits their effectiveness
in certain configurations.14 To overcome these limitations, this
study introduces 1,1-difluoro-2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethane
(DFE) as a novel solvating solvent for localized high-
concentration electrolytes (LHCEs). DFE offers lower PS
solubility while maintaining strong compatibility with Li metal
anode. Additionally, DFE-based LHCEs are specifically
formulated with fully fluorinated components, including salt,
solvating solvent, and diluent, to stabilize the interphases
between the electrolytes and electrodes, expecting LiF-
dominant interphase layers.15,16
DFE interacts uniquely with Li metal anode, participating in
the formation of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer
and contributing to a stable interphase enriched with uniformly
distributed LiF and Li2O components. These inorganic species
are essential for stabilizing SEI layer and reducing continuous
side reactions during cycling. Among the studied diluents in
DFE-based
LHCE
formulations,
1,2-bis(1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethoxy)ethane (BTFEE) stands out as a promising
candidate because of two possible reasons. The first is its
weakly solvating capability making it partially involve in the
salvation structure17 and the second is its synergetic effect with
DFE, both of which contribute to the formation of an
inorganic-rich and robust SEI layer on Li metal surface, then
greatly improve the cycling stability of Li||SPAN batteries. To
further probe the effects of the DFE solvent, performance
comparisons between DFE-BTFEE-LHCE and DME-BTFEE-
LHCE reveal that DFE-BTFEE-LHCE significantly enhances
interphase stability, suppressing side reactions during cycling
and improving battery longevity. These findings highlight
DFE-BTFEE-LHCE, which is an all-fluorinated LHCE, as a
promising electrolyte solution for achieving stable, high-
performance Li||SPAN batteries, paving the way for advance-
ments in next-generation energy storage technologies.

The compatibility of the three electrolytes with Li metal
anode was assessed through the Li deposition morphology. Li||
copper (Cu) coin cells were constructed, and 4 mAh cm−2 of
Li was deposited onto Cu foil at a current density of 0.4 mA
cm−2. Figure 1a−c illustrates the surface and cross-sectional
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of Li deposition
morphology for the three DFE-based LHCEs. Among them,
DFE-BTFEE-LHCE led to a denser surface compared to DFE-
BTFE-LHCE and DFE-TTE-LHCE. Although the deposited
Li particles from all three electrolytes were approximately the
same size (around 10 μm in diameter), DFE-BTFE-LHCE
(Figure 1b) and DFE-TTE-LHCE (Figure 1c) displayed a
more porous morphology, in contrast to the compact and
uniform structure from DFE-BTFEE-LHCE (Figure 1a).
Furthermore, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE resulted in the thinnest
deposited Li layer (∼33.3 μm), indicating densely packed Li
with limited pores. In comparison, DFE-BTFE-LHCE and
DFE-TTE-LHCE formed thicker (∼38.3 and ∼34.6 μm,
respectively), more porous deposited Li layers, as verified by
the cross-sectional views. These findings suggest that DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE offers better compatibility with Li metal anode
than the other two DFE-LHCEs, potentially enhancing its
performance.
The three DFE-based LHCEs show very similar average Li
Columbic efficiency (CE) of around 99.4∼99.5% in Li||Cu
cells (Figure S3). To further evaluate the performance of DFE-
based LHCEs, Li||Li symmetric cells were cycled with a
practically high areal capacity of 8 mAh cm−2 at a current
density of 1.6 mA cm−2 (equivalent to 0.2C). While DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE exhibited a higher initial overpotential during
early cycling, it demonstrated longer cycle life, lasting up to
800 h (Figure 1d). In contrast, DFE-BTFE-LHCE and DFE-
TTE-LHCE cells failed after 600 h and 420 h, respectively, as
highlighted in the zoomed-in graphs in Figure 1d. This
variation in performance and Li compatibility may be
attributed to the differing properties of the diluents used in
the electrolytes. BTFEE is particularly advantageous for
forming an inorganic-rich SEI layer on the Li metal anode,
due to its weakly coordinating properties.17
This phenomenon was further investigated using ab initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) and reactive molecular dynamics
(RMD) simulations to analyze the reactivity between Li metal
and each solvent. BTFEE demonstrated high reactivity from
the outset, rapidly losing F atoms to the Li metal (Figure S4a).
The resulting interphase structure appeared well-distributed,
with conformal Lix-F coverage, while the Lix-oxygen (O)
clusters were influenced by the attachment of residual
carbonated fragments to the atom. In contrast, BTFE displayed
rapid BTFE molecule-Li coordination without any observed
defluorination (Figure S4b). Even after the reaction, some
BTFE molecules remained unreacted. On the other hand, TTE
underwent rapid defluorination upon approaching the Li metal
surface, forming organic components such as difluoroacetyl
fluoride (Figure S4c). However, TTE exhibited poor SEI
conformality, with a spread-out distribution of Lix-F and only a
few Lix-O clusters.
Building on the advantages of the BTFEE diluent, DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE demonstrates fast BTFEE defluorination on the
Li metal surface. LiFSI salt molecules interact directly with Li
metal without decomposing during the initial stages, as shown
in Figure 2a-1. Notably, the O atom of the LiFSI salt interacts
with Li, leading to less Lix-F formation from BTFEE
defluorination (Figure 2a-2). By the end of the reaction
(Figure 2a-5), all DFE and BTFEE were consumed, resulting
in a well distributed interphase layer composed of Lix-F and
Lix-O. In contrast, the salt in DFE-BTFE-LHCE tends to
defluorinate early, but the DFE defluorination and BTFE
decomposition form more organic molecular chains (Figure
2b). The solvent and diluent in DFE-TTE-LHCE take a
relatively longer time to react with Li metal compared to the
other two electrolytes, with relatively slower reaction between
LiFSI and Li (Figure 2c). The interphase layer contains a more
dispersed Lix−F structure, but Lix-O species aggregate within
the layer (Figure 2c-4). In conclusion, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
forms an interphase layer enriched with inorganic species, such
as Lix-F and Lix-O,18,19 which well protect the Li metal anode
and contribute to improving Li||Li cycling performance
compared with the other two DFE-based LHCEs.

While DFE is confirmed to have lower PS solubility
compared to DME, it is worthwhile to investigate whether
DFE performs better as a solvating solvent in LHCE
formulations for Li||SPAN batteries, especially since DME is
known for its excellent compatibility with Li metal anode.23,24
To explore this, the Li deposition morphology on Cu foil of
DME-BTFEE-LHCE was examined using SEM. In comparison
to DFE-BTFEE-LHCE (Figure 1a), DME-BTFEE-LHCE
resulted in a slightly more porous but still relatively dense
surface (Figure S6a). Interestingly, the deposited Li layer with
DME-BTFEE-LHCE was slightly thinner (∼31.8 μm, Figure
S6b) than that observed with DFE-BTFEE-LHCE (∼33.3 μm,
Figure 1a). For a deeper understanding, cross-sectional SEM
images of the Cu foils were observed after Li stripping. The
higher Coulombic efficiency of DFE-BTFEE-LHCE suggests
that it facilitates Li utilization for deposition rather than side
reactions (Figure S6c). This is further supported by the smaller
amount of residues observed on the Cu foil with DFE-BTFEE-
LHCE, while DME-BTFEE-LHCE exhibited more residues
(Figure S6d,e).
The interaction between DME-BTFEE-LHCE and Li metal
was investigated using AIMD simulations. This DME-based
electrolyte exhibited high reactivity, including rapid defluori-
nation of the BTFEE molecule upon approaching the Li metal
surface (Figure S6f-1). Subsequently, the O atoms from LiFSI
salt anion became linked to Li as additional F atoms from
BTFEE reacted with Li metal (Figure S6f-2). During the
reaction, no consumption of DME was observed, in contrast to
DFE, which actively participated in forming the SEI layer on
the Li metal anode (Figure 1e). While DME-BTFEE-LHCE
produced a distinct distribution of Lix-F and Lix-O, DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE demonstrated a more spread-out interphase
structure, which could offer advantages for SEI layer formation
on the Li metal. To further examine the interphase between
the electrolytes and the Li metal surface, electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted on Li||Li cells
before electrochemical cycling (Figure S6g). The results show
that DFE-BTFEE-LHCE exhibited lower resistance (∼9.5 Ω)

On the SPAN surface, BTFEE and DFE molecules undergo
initial defluorination as they approach the SPAN cathode,
enabling the LiFSI salt to participate in the defluorinated
environment and stabilize Li coordination (Figure S9a-1).
SPAN rings begin to open through S−F and S−N bonding,
resulting in the formation of carbonated chains that primarily
attach to S, N, and Li atoms (Figure S9a-2). The NS2O4
−
structure fully decomposes, leaving partially defluorinated
BTFEE and DFE molecules and forming SO4 structures,
which could be Li oxysulfide that is beneficial for the
interphase layer (Figure S9a-5).25 While the LiFSI salt in
DME-BTFEE-LHCE undergoes complete defluorination
(Figure S9b-1), followed by defluorination of BTFEE (Figure
S9b-2). Similar to its behavior on the Li metal surface, DME
remains undecomposed (Figure S9b-5). In contrast, DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE exhibits greater defluorination of DFE,
resulting in a more stable cathode electrolyte interphase
(CEI) layer on the SPAN cathode. This phenomenon was
analyzed using XPS F 1s and S 2p spectra after the first
formation (Figure 4a). The results reveal that DFE-BTFEE-
LHCE produced a larger LiF peak on the SPAN surface,
whereas DME-BTFEE-LHCE exhibited a higher concentration
of organic species. Additionally, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE retained
the S−S bond (∼164.0 eV) in the S 2p spectra of the SPAN
cathode as shown in fresh SPAN (Figure S10), indicating the
improved reversibility of S−S and C−S bonds (∼162.2 eV, S
2p) after the disruption of Li−S bonds.26,27 In cases where
SPAN reversibility is low or the interphase layer is excessively
thick, these S−S and C−S interactions could be suppressed.
This good reversibility suggests that DFE-BTFEE-LHCE has
the potential to enhance the performance of Li||SPAN cells
significantly. The morphologies of the SPAN cathodes exhibit
a similar trend. As shown in Figure S11b,c, the SPAN cathode
using DFE-BTFEE-LHCE displays a smoother surface and
more uniform thickness compared to DME-BTFEE-LHCE
after the first formation cycle. While both electrodes undergo
comparable thickness changes from the pristine SPAN cathode
(∼71.4 μm, Figure S11a), the evolution of SPAN morphology
will be further investigated after long-term cycling.
The electrochemical performance of DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
and DME-BTFEE-LHCE was further evaluated in Li||SPAN
batteries under the same conditions. Figure S12 illustrates that
both DFE-BTFEE-LHCE (702.3 mAh g−1) and DME-BTFEE-
LHCE (700.7 mAh g−1) delivered nearly identical specific
capacities during the first cycle at 0.2C. Besides, both
electrolytes showed a similar C-rate capability as well (Figure
S13). As shown in Figure 4b, DME-BTFEE-LHCE cells
exhibited failure after 320 cycles, whereas DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
maintained stable performance for up to 500 cycles with 88.1%
capacity retention. Although DFE-BTFEE-LHCE has slightly
lower ionic conductivity (2.12 mS cm−1) and higher viscosity
(6.05 mPa·s) at 25 °C compared to DME-BTFEE-LHCE
(2.19 mS cm−1 and 4.68 mPa·s), DFE-BTFEE-LHCE still
enables an extended cycle life for Li||SPAN batteries. These
observations suggest that the interphase behavior may go
beyond ionic conductivity and viscosity and plays a more
significant role in influencing the battery life of Li||SPAN cells.
Therefore, the cycled Li metal anodes and SPAN cathodes
were further analyzed to understand the superior performance
of DFE-BTFEE-LHCE.
SEM images reveal that Li metal cycled with DFE-BTFEE-
LHCE exhibited a denser surface, while DME-BTFEE-LHCE
produced a rougher surface (Figure S14). The thickness of the
Li metal anodes changed slightly from pristine Li (∼210 μm).
In the case of DFE-BTFEE-LHCE, approximately 140 μm of
fresh Li remained underneath a ∼75 μm reacted Li layer. In
contrast, DME-BTFEE-LHCE formed a much thicker reacted
Li layer (118−158 μm), despite its shorter battery life
compared to DFE-BTFEE-LHCE (Figure 4c). These findings
highlight that the stable SEI layer formed in DFE-BTFEE-
LHCE, characterized by the spread-out distribution of LiF and
Li2O, effectively mitigates side reactions between the electro-
lyte and the Li metal anode during cycling.
Further insights were obtained from X-ray absorption
spectroscopy (XAS), as shown in Figure 4d. On the Li metal
side, both DME-BTFEE-LHCE and DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
systems exhibit high-valence S species such as sulfonates and
sulfate-like compounds, appearing as peaks at higher energies
in the XAS spectra. These species arise from reduction of the
FSI anion and constitute amorphous components in the SEI.28
The lower-energy peaks are likely due to a crosstalk effect,
where SPAN fragments migrate to the Li metal anode and
further react with Li. Notably, XAS results show that these
species are much less abundant in the DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
system, underscoring its effectiveness in suppressing crosstalk.

after cycling.29 Anion-derived species such as sulfonates and

The surface morphologies of cycled SPAN cathodes from

However, the SPAN cathode cycled with DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
(∼96.5 μm) exhibited less thickness change compared to that

CEI layer helps mitigate the side reactions on the SPAN
cathode, effectively preserving its thickness while cycling.
While DFE-BTFEE-LHCE outperforms other electrolytes, it
still exhibits an 11.9% capacity fade over 500 cycles in Li||
SPAN cells. To identify the cause of this fading, coin cells
cycled during the formation stage and after 500 cycles were
disassembled, and symmetric cells were assembled using the
corresponding SPAN cathodes and Li metal anodes. As shown
in Figure S15, SPAN||SPAN cells displayed minimal changes in
impedance even after 500 cycles. In contrast, Li||Li cells
showed a significant increase in impedance, rising from ∼11.4
Ω after formation to ∼67.5 Ω after 500 cycles. Furthermore,
the appearance of a new semicircle in the impedance spectrum
suggests the development of an additional interphase layer.
These findings indicate that the capacity fade in Li||SPAN cells
with DFE-BTFEE-LHCE is primarily attributed to degradation
at the Li metal anode. Thanks to the advanced DFE-BTFEE
electrolyte, Li||SPAN coin cells with a higher areal capacity of
approximately 7 mAh cm−2 achieved an impressive 81.3%
capacity retention at 200 cycles when cycled at 0.2C (where
1C = ∼7 mA cm−2, Figure S16). The effectiveness of DFE-
BTFEE-LHCE was further validated using a 0.12 Ah pouch
cell. As shown in Figure S17, the pouch cell demonstrated a
stable cycle life over 80 cycles, maintaining 96.4% of its
capacity when cycled at 0.2C (1C = ∼8 mA cm−2). In
conclusion, the beneficial interphases formed on both the Li
metal anode and the SPAN cathode with DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
contribute to the extended cycle life and improved perform-
ance of Li||SPAN batteries.
This study introduces DFE as a promising new solvating
solvent for LHCEs in Li||SPAN batteries. offering reduced PS
solubility compared to DME, a widely used solvent in Li metal
batteries. DFE actively participates in reactions with Li metal,
forming a stable SEI layer enriched with uniformly distributed
LiF and Li2O, which are key beneficial components. Among
the DFE-based LHCE formulations, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE
stands out as the optimized electrolyte candidate for Li||
SPAN batteries. Its ability to create highly stable and inorganic-
dominant SEI and CEI layers facilitate extended cycle life and
improved stability due to its all-fluorinated feature. The
synergy between compatibility with the Li metal anode and
enhanced SPAN cathode stability, coupled with superior
interphase layers, underpins the DFE-BTFEE-LHCE. Com-
pared to DME-BTFEE-LHCE, DFE-BTFEE-LHCE demon-
strates an extended cycle life for Li||SPAN batteries, effectively
suppressing side reactions such as crosstalk and preserving
electrode integrity. Benefiting from the DFE-BTFEE-LHCE,
the 0.12 Ah single layer pouch cell with high areal capacity (∼8
mAh cm−2) of SPAN achieves 96.4% capacity retention after
80 cycles. These findings represent a significant step forward in
developing high-performance electrolyte systems tailored to
Li||SPAN batteries.
