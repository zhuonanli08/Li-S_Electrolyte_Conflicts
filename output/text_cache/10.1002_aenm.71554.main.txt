ABSTRACT 
The effect of electrolytes on battery cell performance is multifaceted, and it is difficult to isolate their impact on individual 
processes. Operando analysis of batteries is a powerful tool to investigate battery degradation mechanisms. By combining 
different techniques on the same cell a wider perspective can be opened and enables easier cross-correlation. Here, we introduce 
a fully laboratory-based multimodal operando platform that synchronizes EIS/DRT, Raman spectroscopy, UV–vis reflectance 
spectroscopy, temperature monitoring, and galvanostatic cycling in one sealed Li–S cell. The platform is demonstrated over an 
extended observation window of up to 20 cycles to compare electrolyte-dependent polysulfide speciation, transport limitations, 
and resistance evolution. The experimental design is optimized for reproducible mechanistic characterization and is not intended 
as a benchmark of practical cell performance or long-term cycle life.

Introduction 
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered promising candi- 
dates for next-generation electrochemical energy storage because 
sulfur offers a high theoretical specific capacity of 1675 𝑚 𝐴ℎ 𝑔− 1 
and, when combined with lithium metal, a theoretical gravimet- 
ric energy density of approximately 2600 𝑊ℎ 𝑘𝑔− 1 based on the 
active materials [ 1–3 ]. These values substantially exceed those of 
conventional intercalation-based lithium-ion battery chemistries 
[ 4 ]. In addition, sulfur is abundant, inexpensive, and potentially 
associated with a lower environmental impact than cathode 
materials containing nickel or cobalt [ 1–3 ]. However, translating 
these intrinsic material-level advantages into practical cell-level 
energy density and long-term stability remains challenging. The 
insulating nature of elemental sulfur and 𝐿𝑖2 𝑆, large changes 
in the physical state and molar volume of the active material, 
unstable lithium-metal interfaces, and the dissolution and migra- 
tion of lithium polysulfides collectively limit sulfur utilization, 
Coulombic efficiency, and cycle life [ 2, 3, 5–9 ]. 
The electrolyte plays a particularly complex role because it is 
not merely an ion-conducting medium but directly determines 
sulfur speciation, reaction pathways, mass transport, and interfa- 
cial stability. Mixtures of 1,3-dioxolane and 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
containing LiTFSI and 𝐿𝑖𝑁𝑂3 are among the most widely used 
benchmark electrolytes for liquid-electrolyte Li–S batteries [ 5–
10 ]. DME provides comparatively strong solvation and facilitates 
the dissolution and transport of long-chain lithium polysulfides, 
whereas DOL contributes to interfacial film formation and can 
undergo polymerization under suitable conditions. The resulting 
solution-mediated reaction pathway promotes sulfur utilization 
and enables the characteristic two-plateau discharge profile.

At the same time, soluble polysulfides migrate between the 
electrodes and participate in parasitic reduction and oxidation 
reactions, giving rise to self-discharge, low Coulombic effi- 
ciency, lithium corrosion, electrolyte consumption, and loss of 
electrochemically accessible sulfur [ 5, 7–9 ]. 𝐿𝑖𝑁𝑂3 is therefore 
commonly added to promote the formation of a passivating 
interphase on lithium metal and to suppress continuous polysul- 
fide reduction at the negative electrode [ 6 ]. Nevertheless, 𝐿𝑖𝑁𝑂3 
is gradually consumed during cycling and does not eliminate 
the underlying transport of soluble polysulfides through the 
electrolyte [ 6, 10 ]. The concentration and molecular structure 
of dissolved polysulfides also influence the physicochemical 
properties of the electrolyte itself. 
Experimental and computational studies have shown that poly- 
sulfide chain length, concentration, degree of dissociation, ion 
association, and clustering affect viscosity, ionic conductivity, 
diffusion coefficients, and charge-carrier populations [ 11–13 ]. 
Short-chain species may exhibit comparatively high molecular 
diffusivity while contributing less effectively to conductivity 
because of stronger ion association and cluster formation [ 12 ]. 
The addition of LiTFSI modifies these interactions further by 
competing for solvent molecules and changing the coordination 
and dissociation equilibria of lithium polysulfides [ 11, 12 ]. These 
effects are closely coupled to the electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio. 
Under lean-electrolyte conditions, dissolution and precipitation 
consume a substantial fraction of the available solvent, and 
concentration gradients, pore blockage, and transport limitations 
become increasingly important. Under highly flooded conditions, 
variations in polysulfide concentration and the corresponding 
changes in bulk transport properties are diluted, but they are not 
necessarily eliminated [ 10–14 ]. Consequently, the solution resis- 
tance measured in an operating cell represents a coupled response 
involving salt concentration, polysulfide speciation, viscosity, 
conductivity, wetting, and the effective ionic transport geometry. 
Reducing the effective solvating power of the electrolyte has 
emerged as an alternative strategy for limiting polysulfide disso- 
lution. Partially fluorinated ethers and hydrofluoroethers exhibit 
weak coordination toward 𝐿𝑖+ and lithium polysulfides and 
can be used either as primary solvents or as nominally inert 
diluents in localized high-concentration electrolytes [ 15–18 ]. In 
DME/TTE mixtures, TTE reduces the effective solvent activity 
and increases the local salt-to-coordinating-solvent ratio, thereby 
decreasing polysulfide solubility and modifying the balance 
between solution-mediated and surface-mediated sulfur conver- 
sion [ 15, 17 ]. Related fluorinated-ether systems have demonstrated 
that reduced polysulfide solubility can suppress shuttle reactions 
and improve lithium-metal compatibility, particularly under 
restricted electrolyte conditions [ 16, 18 ]. However, weak solvation 
also reduces the availability and mobility of dissolved reaction 
intermediates. Depending on electrolyte composition, sulfur 
loading, current density, and electrode architecture, this can 
increase polarization, alter the relative lengths of the discharge 
plateaus, restrict sulfur utilization, and intensify mass-transport 
limitations [ 15–18 ]. Weakly solvating electrolytes therefore do 
not simply remove the polysulfide problem but shift the bal- 
ance between polysulfide confinement, reaction kinetics, ionic 
transport, and interfacial stability. Sulfolane-based electrolytes 
provide a further route toward low polysulfide solubility. Sul- 
folane has a high dielectric constant and high oxidative stability, 
but its strong dipolar character, comparatively high viscosity, 
and specific 𝐿𝑖+ coordination lead to transport properties that 
differ substantially from conventional ether electrolytes [ 19 ]. 
Highly concentrated LiTFSI/sulfolane electrolytes and their 
mixtures with TTE or related hydrofluoroethers can form anion- 
rich coordination structures while substantially reducing the 
solubility of lithium polysulfides [ 19, 20 ]. This can suppress 
shuttle currents and alter sulfur-conversion pathways, but the 
associated viscosity and reduced mobility can also cause concen- 
tration polarization and hinder ion and active-species transport 
[ 19 ]. Accordingly, DOL/DME/LiTFSI/ 𝐿𝑖𝑁𝑂3 , DME/TTE/LiTFSI, 
and sulfolane/TTE/LiTFSI formulations represent established 
but chemically distinct electrolyte concepts rather than newly 
introduced compositions. They span a broad range of polysulfide- 
solvation behavior and are therefore suitable model systems for 
investigating how solvation, transport, and interfacial processes 
are coupled. 
Because the electrochemical response of a Li–S cell results from 
several simultaneously evolving reactions, ex situ characteri- 
zation alone cannot fully resolve the underlying mechanisms. 
A broad range of in situ and operando methods has conse- 
quently been developed to monitor sulfur conversion, polysulfide 
transport, precipitation, interfacial resistance, and morphological 
degradation during battery operation [ 21–24 ]. Electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy provides time-dependent information 
on electrolyte resistance, interfacial polarization, charge transfer, 
and diffusion-related processes. Electrode-resolved experiments 
and distribution-of-relaxation-times analyses have demonstrated 
that high-frequency contributions are frequently dominated by 
the lithium-metal electrode and its interphase, whereas slower 
contributions include sulfur-cathode charge transfer, precipita- 
tion and dissolution processes, and ionic or polysulfide transport 
[ 21, 22, 25, 26 ]. However, because these contributions can overlap 
in a two-electrode cell, impedance data benefit strongly from 
correlation with complementary chemical probes. Operando 
Raman spectroscopy has been applied to follow the formation 
and consumption of elemental sulfur and different polysul- 
fide populations close to the sulfur cathode [ 27–30 ]. Operando 
UV–vVis spectroscopy provides complementary sensitivity to 
dissolved sulfur species in the electrolyte and has been used to 
distinguish spectral regions associated with different polysulfide 
chain lengths and to monitor their potential-dependent evolution 
[ 31–33 ]. X-ray absorption spectroscopy, diffraction, and imaging 
offer additional information on sulfur speciation, solid-phase 
formation, electrode morphology, and lithium degradation [ 9, 21, 
22, 28, 34 ]. 
These methods have provided substantial mechanistic insight, 
but most studies apply one principal analytical technique or 
combine data obtained from separate cells. Even when several 
techniques are used, differences in cell geometry, sampling 
position, cycling history, and electrolyte inventory can complicate 
direct temporal correlation [ 21–24, 31, 34 ]. Optical operando 
measurements introduce additional methodological challenges. 
Optical access is frequently created using a window and a 
localized aperture through an electrode, current collector, or 
separator [ 24, 27, 29–31, 33, 35 ]. Such configurations enable direct 
spectroscopic access but can locally alter stack pressure, current 
distribution, electrolyte volume, and transport pathways. More- 
over, a measurement through one macroscopic aperture probes

In the present study, we introduce an integrated, fully laboratory- 
based platform that combines galvanostatic cycling, electrochem- 
ical impedance spectroscopy with distribution-of-relaxation- 
times analysis, Raman spectroscopy, UV–vis reflectance spec- 
troscopy, and temperature monitoring in the same sealed, coin- 
cell-compatible Li–S cell. The individual electrolyte compositions 
and the use of a perforated current collector are not claimed 
as novel. Instead, the three established electrolyte systems are 
deliberately selected as model formulations spanning strongly, 
intermediately, and weakly solvating conditions. Raman spec- 
troscopy probes sulfur species in the immediate vicinity of the 
cathode, whereas UV–vis spectroscopy monitors dissolved poly- 
sulfides in the bulk electrolyte. Their synchronized correlation 
with voltage, solution resistance, relaxation-time descriptors, and 
temperature enables electrolyte-dependent reaction and degrada- 
tion processes to be compared within the same electrochemical 
cell. The cell architecture uses a fully perforated, carbon-coated 
aluminum current collector to provide distributed optical access 
and to reduce the dependence of Raman measurements on a 
single macroscopic aperture. Comparative measurements with 
non-perforated current collectors are used to evaluate the electro- 
chemical influence of this modification. The design is optimized 
for stable and reproducible multimodal characterization rather 
than for maximizing practical cell-performance metrics. In par- 
ticular, the optical measurement volumes, low sulfur loading, and 
electrolyte-rich conditions represent deliberate methodological 
compromises. The platform is operated for up to 20 charge–
discharge cycles, extending the observation period beyond the 
few cycles commonly investigated in multimodal spectroscopic 
experiments. To the best of our knowledge, the synchronized 
combination of EIS/DRT, Raman spectroscopy, UV–vis spec- 
troscopy, and temperature monitoring over this duration has 
not previously been demonstrated in one sealed Li–S cell. The 
resulting data provide a temporally aligned view of local sulfur 
speciation, bulk polysulfide evolution, interfacial and transport- 
related impedance changes, and progressive cell degradation.

Experimental Section 
2.1 
Materials and Cell Assembly 
A well-established cathode material based on sulfur, carbon black 
and Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) was used for this comparison 
study [ 36 ]. Briefly, Sulfur (490 mg) and Carbon Black Super-P (210 
mg) were mixed with carbon disulfide (3 mL) and ground until 
complete solvent evaporation. The mixture was transferred to a 
100 mL single-necked round-bottom flask and air was exchanged 
by argon. The closed flask was heated to 155 ◦C for 12 h. The mix- 
ture was ground again after heat treatment. PVDF (1 g, HSV900 
PVDF binder, MTI) was dissolved in N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidon (30 
mL, NMP) by heating up to 80 ◦C and stirring for 1 h. The cathode 
slurry was prepared by mixing C/S powder (350 mg), Carbon 
Black (100 mg), PVDF solution (1.5 mL) and NMP (3.5 mL). 
The slurry was stirred for 2 h including 10 min ultrasonification 
treatment in between. The slurry was coated on a carbon coated 
and perforated aluminum foil. The foil was perforated with a 
honeycomb structure, where an individual honeycomb has a size 
of 0.5 × 0.25 𝑚 𝑚2 and a center-to-center distance of 1.67 mm. 
A microscope image of the coated and uncoated foil is shown 
in the supporting information (Figure S1 ). The thickness of the 
coating was adjusted to 220 𝜇𝑚 . The coated current collector 
was dried overnight at ambient conditions. In a second step, it 
was transferred to a vacuum oven and dried at 45 ◦C and reduced 
pressure for 1 h. The resulting cathodes have an areal loading of 
0.73 mg sulfur per 𝑐𝑚2 . Finally, 14 mm disks were punched out 
and used as cathodes for the coin cells. The dried cathodes have 
a sulfur content of 49 𝑤 𝑡 % (approx. 0.8 mg sulfur per cathode). 
The areal loading is lower compared to other similar studies 
due to the usage of a perforated current collector. Nevertheless, 
the perforation of the current collector opens the possibility to

2.2 
Multimodal Operando Measurements 
Li–S batteries still lack long-term stability. Thus, investigating 
chemical processes under operando conditions is crucial for 
understanding the degradation mechanism. Therefore, we devel- 
oped up a multi-spectroscopic setup enabling the investigation 
from different perspectives on the same cell. An overview of the 
setup and its outcome is shown in Figure 1 . 
Galvanostatic cycling and simultaneous electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were measured with an Interface 
1010E (Gamry Instruments, USA). For that, customized 
measurement scripts enable EIS measurements ( 𝐴𝑚𝑎𝑥 = 𝐶∕50 ) 
while charging and discharging with a current of 𝐶∕10 within 
a voltage window from 1.8 to 2.8 V. Continuous measurements 
were done in a frequency range from 250 kOhm to 250 mOhm 
collecting 10 measurement points per frequency decade. 
In parallel, Raman spectroscopy was carried out from the back- 
side of the cathode with an i-Raman Plus spectrometer (BWTek, 
USA). The excitation wavelength was 532 nm. Measurement time 
for individual spectra varies between 10 and 40 s depending on 
signal intensity. Spectra were collected every 1–2 min. The sap- 
phire window and the perforated current collector provide optical 
access to the electrolyte and cathode region. Raman spectroscopy 
is performed through one selected perforation using a fixed fiber 
probe that interrogates a local focal volume close to the cathode. 
The present configuration provides spectroscopic information but 
does not generate a spatially resolved optical image or Raman 
map. Although the distributed perforation pattern avoids reliance 
on one large macroscopic opening and provides several potential 
optical access points across the electrode, only one perforation 
is probed during an individual operando experiment. To assess 
the sensitivity of the spectral fingerprint to local probe place- 
ment, a freshly assembled cell containing E1 was additionally 
examined with a Raman microscope at three spatially separated 
locations. At each location, spectra were acquired both through 
the aperture and on the adjacent coated current-collector land. 
After background correction, the characteristic Raman bands 
were reproduced for all positions and both sampling geometries 
(Figure S3 ), indicating that the relevant spectral fingerprints 
are not specific to one perforation or to whether the probe 
intersects an aperture or the adjacent current collector. This 
control supports the spatial robustness of the spectral assignment 
in the freshly assembled cell. However, because the operando 
fiber probe remains fixed and the validation was performed before 
cycling, the time-resolved operando spectra are interpreted as 
local measurements rather than as proof of complete electrode- 
wide equiv alence. The focal plane ( 𝑑 = 75 𝜇𝑚 ) was positioned 
between the sapphire window and the current collector by a 
height scan, and the lateral position was adjusted by maximizing 
the electrolyte fluorescence signal. The representativeness of a 
single perforation was proved with a Raman microscope as shown 
in Figure S3 in the supporting information. 
UV–vis spectroscopy was carried out in the vicinity of the separa- 
tor. Thus, it probes the bulk electrolyte between the electrodes. 
The UV–vis probe consists of seven fixed optical fibers in a 
6-in-1-out configuration and measures reflected intensity from 
the electrolyte-filled pocket adjacent to the separator. It is not

2.3 
Data Processing and Analysis 
All data were processed and analyzed with self-written python 
(Version 3.12) scripts [ 38 ]. In order to handle the massive amount 
of heterogeneous data, all files were merged into a single hdf5 file 
[ 39 ]. The data sets are annotated with their respective timestamp 
in order to align the data after processing. 
Impedance spectroscopy data were processed in a consecutive 
multi-step process. First, a linear drift correction was applied to 
account for voltage drift during the measurement of individual 
frequency points with frequencies lower than 100 Hz. Afterwards, 
each data set was time corrected by a 2D interpolation in order 
to get time consistency for each measurement. Finally, data were 
checked by Z-Hit test to prove consistency of the data correction 
(see Figure S4 ). Impedance analysis was done by the evaluation 
of the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) [ 40–42 ]. The DRT 
was integrated between 10− 6 and 10− 3 s and between 10− 3 and 
100 s to obtain descriptors denoted as the fast and slow pro- 
cesses, respectively. Electrode-resolved DRT studies using 𝐿 𝑖||𝐿 𝑖
and 𝑆 ||𝑆 symmetric cells have located lithium-metal/SEI-related 
polarization predominantly in the microsecond-to-millisecond 
range, including characteristic contributions in the kHz regime 
[ 25, 26, 43, 44 ]. In contrast, sulfur-cathode charge transfer 
and polysulfide or ion diffusion have been observed at longer 
relaxation times ranging from milliseconds to several seconds. 
The fast and slow processes are therefore interpreted as being 
predominantly associated with the lithium-metal electrode and 
sulfur cathode, respectively [ 25 ]. However, owing to the two- 
electrode conf iguration used here, overlapping contributions 
cannot be completely excluded. 
The UV–vis spectra were corrected for time-dependent fluctua- 
tions of the light sources and converted to absorbance according 
to A = -log(R). Based on the spectral assignment reported 
by Häcker et al.,[ 32 ] two wavelength regions were evaluated 
separately: 380 − 430 𝑛 𝑚 as a descriptor for predominantly 
short-chain polysulfides and 470 − 500 𝑛 𝑚 as a descriptor for pre- 
dominantly long-chain polysulfides. Integration of these regions 
yields two time-dependent measurands that can be compared 
directly with the Raman-derived species evolution. The complete 
analysis is shown in Figure S6 . 
Raman spectra were corrected using asymmetric least-squares 
baseline estimation (smoothness = 104 − 105 , asymmetry = 
0 . 08 − 0 . 1 ) [ 45 ], normalized by their integration time, and 
smoothed with a Savgol filter (window length = 6 − 10 , polyno- 
mial order = 2) using parameters selected to remove the broad 
fluorescence background without suppressing the narrow sulfur- 
related bands. The influence of this processing is illustrated 
in Figure S7 . Afterward, principal component analysis (PCA of 
scikit-learn [ 46 ]) was used to identify the main components 
and multi-variate curve resolution (pyMCR [ 47 ]) to track their 
evolution over time. The original time-resolved Raman data, their 
reconstruction by the MCR components, and the correspond- 
ing residuals are compared in Figure S8 , demonstrating that 
the principal spectral features are retained without systematic 
residual structures. 
2.4 
Quantum-Chemical Calculations 
Two series of quantum-chemical calculations were performed to 
simulate the Raman activity of S8 and Li-polysulfide molecules 
Li 2 S8 , Li 2 S6 , and Li 2 S4 . 
In the first calculations, the finite-temperature effects were 
taken into account by performing density-functional theory- 
based ab initio molecular dynamics simulations (AIMD), where 
the Wannier polarizability method [ 48–51 ] were employed for the 
simulation of Raman signals. The AIMD simulations were carried 
out in vacuum first in the canonical ensemble for equilibration for 
20 ps followed by another 20 ps of AIMD simulations in micro- 
canonical ensemble for sampling [ 52–54 ]. In these simulations 
we have used PBE exchange-correlation functional [ 55 ] together 
with DFT-D3 method [ 56 ] to account for dispersion interactions. 
Additionally, we employed a double-zeta basis set for all the atoms 
optimized for molecular systems (DZVP-MOLOPT) [ 57 ], while 
the core electrons were effectively taken into account using GTH 
pseudopotentials [ 58, 59 ]. The above simulations were carried out 
using the CP2K software package [ 60 ]. 
In the second calculations, we performed all-electron DFT cal- 
culations with Orca package [ 61 ] were the Raman shifts and 
intensities were calculated using static polarizabilities obtained 
via linear response theory. In these calculations, there were 
no explicit treatment of finite-temperature effects and the 
Raman peaks were broadened using Gaussian functions with 
FWHM of 10 cm − 1 . The solvent effects were included using 
the conductor-like polarizable continuum model [ 62 ] with a 
dielectric constant of 𝜖= 7 . 2 for the DOL:DME mixture, and 
𝜖= 6 . 5 for the DOL:DME mixture together with 1M LiTFSI. In 
these calculations, we used PBE0 hybrid exchange correlation 
functional [ 63 ] together with a triple-zeta basis set, namely 
def2-TZVP [ 64 ].

can be evaluated together with the simultaneous evolution of 
short- and long-chain polysulfide signatures. The agreement 
between independent methods provides a self-consistent mecha- 
nistic picture, although the observed correlations should not be 
interpreted as proof of causality without additional controlled 
perturbation experiments. 
3.1 
Interpretation of Operando Raman Spectra 
Several publications tried to address the interpretation of Raman 
spectra during operando analysis of batteries. As shown in the 
supporting information Tables S1 – S3 a clear picture is not 
available. Here, MCR was used to identify the most prominent 
bands to interpret the charge over state of change and time. 
The resulting spectra can be a superposition of different species 
with similar behavior. The classification of the various species 
was based on theoretical calculations. A comparison of the 
main components derived from MCR for the second cycle and 
theoretical calculations are shown in the left panels of Figure 2 , 
while the right panels show the corresponding concentrations 
over time. 
It should be noted here that the calculations could be consid- 
ered only as a guide to understand the Raman trends during 
charge/discharge and the exact peak positions and intensities 
should not be directly compared to experiments, given the 
oversimplified structures considered in theoretical calculations. 
On the one hand, the concentration curve suggests that compo- 
nent 0 could be assigned to long-chain polysulfides. On the other 
hand, the theoretical calculations show that a Raman activity 
should be present at around 200 cm − 1 in polysulfides with sulfur 
chains consisting of three or more covalently bonded sulfur atoms 
[ 49, 52, 53 ]. This activity is, however, absent in the component 
0. Additionally, the activities in 350–420 cm − 1 range align with 
the theoretically predicted activities in long-chain polysulfides, 
namely Li 2 S8 and Li 2 S6 . Therefore, we assign component 0 
to long-chain polysulfides, which could be strongly anchored 
by the carbon material, so that the vibrations at 200 cm − 1 
would be sterically hindered. Such an effect has been reported 
previously [ 52 ]. They could form at the early discharge stages 
and then transform to the shorter chains, as the concentration in 
component 0 suggests. 
Based on its concentration profile, component 1 could be assigned 
to short-chain polysulfides. For comparison, calculated Raman 
activities of a single Li 2 S4 in vacuum are shown in orange. This 
system represents an oversimplified model system for short-chain 
polysulfides. However, apart from the activity around ∼480 cm − 1 ,

3.3 
Influence of the Electrolyte Composition 
The electrolyte has a crucial influence on the cycle performance 
[ 66 ]. It affects the formation of the solid electrolyte interface and 
it acts as a solvent for the soluble intermediate species, namely 
polysulfides of different chain length [ 67 ]. Figure 4A shows the 
capacity fading curves as well as the coulombic efficiency (CE) for 
the three different investigated electrolytes. Six different samples 
were measured for each electrolyte. The error bars represent the 
standard deviation for each quantity. 
The absolute specific capacities obtained with the present cells are 
lower than those of performance-optimized Li–S cathodes. This 
difference arises from the characterization-oriented cell design 
rather than from an attempt to maximize sulfur utilization. The 
cathode contains 49 𝑤 𝑡 . % sulfur at an areal sulfur loading of 
approximately 0 . 73 𝑚 𝑔 𝑐𝑚− 2 and is deposited on a fully perforated 
current collector to provide optical access. Furthermore, the 
cell contains dedicated electrolyte-filled volumes for Raman and 
UV–vis spectroscopy and is operated at an electrolyte-to-sulfur 
ratio of approximately 100 𝜇𝐿 𝑚𝑔− 1 sulfur. These conditions 
were selected to provide stable optical boundary conditions and 
reproducible multimodal measurements over extended cycling. 
Accordingly, the capacities are used here to compare the relative 
behavior of the three electrolyte systems and should not be 
interpreted as practical performance benchmarks.

the same for all investigated electrolytes due to the same anode 
and cathode material. Within the first six cycles the capacity 
loss is the highest for E2 while E3 shows a moderate loss of

4 
Conclusions 
In summary, this work establishes a fully laboratory-based 
multimodal operando platform that synchronizes galvanostatic 
cycling, EIS/DRT, Raman spectroscopy, UV–vis reflectance spec- 
troscopy, and temperature monitoring in a single sealed Li–S cell 
over up to 20 cycles. The combination of local Raman probing 
near the cathode and UV–vis probing of the bulk electrolyte 
enables chemical and electrochemical changes to be correlated 
without relying on measurements from separate cells. Three cen- 
tral findings emerge from the correlated data. First, the strongly 
solvating DOL/DME-based electrolyte promotes pronounced for- 
mation and persistence of soluble short-chain polysulfide species. 
Second, the DME/TTE and particularly the SL/TTE formula- 
tions suppress dissolved-polysulfide signatures but simultane- 
ously exhibit stronger transport-related resistance contributions, 
demonstrating the trade-off between reduced polysulfide solu- 
bility and ionic transport. Third, extended cycling reveals a pro- 
gressive increase in solution and slow-process resistance together 
with decreasing local Raman intensities, while dissolved poly- 
sulfides remain detectable in the bulk electrolyte. This behavior 
is consistent with a gradual loss of electrochemically accessible 
active material and increasingly hindered transport rather than 
a fundamental change in the sulfur-conversion pathway. The 
novelty of the study therefore does not lie in the individual 
electrolyte formulations, but in the synchronized, extended- 
duration correlation of local speciation, bulk-electrolyte com- 
position, and impedance evolution. The platform provides a 
versatile framework for comparative mechanistic investiga- 
tions of electrolyte–electrode interactions, while the deliberately 
characterization-oriented cell design means that the measured 
capacities should not be interpreted as practical performance 
benchmarks.
