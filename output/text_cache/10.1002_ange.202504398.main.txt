Bidirectional Ion Kinetic Regulator
A Bidirectional Ion Kinetic Regulator Suppressing Lithium Dendrites
in Advanced Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Jun Jiang, Yanwen Ding, Shujun Liu, Zhijie Qi, Tong Guo, Zhihua Wang, Jingwen Sun,
He Zhu, Xiaoping Ouyang, Xin Wang, Junwu Zhu,* and Yongsheng Fu*
Abstract: Liquid organic electrolytes (LOEs) are of
crucial signiﬁcance in secondary battery deposition
processes. Based on Sand’s time formula, this study
proposes a novel bidirectional ion kinetic regulator
concept. Veriﬁed in lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), it
is demonstrated that regulating the migration of anions
and cations simultaneously can effectively promote the
realization of dendrite-free batteries. This bidirectional
ion kinetic regulator forms octahedral complexes with
TFSI−, decreasing TFSI−migration, enhancing Li+ des-
olvation and diffusion, and thus extending the dendrite
formation time by over 25 times. Moreover, the attraction
of the regulator towards solvent molecules and its
improvement on the sulfur reduction kinetics effectively
suppress the shuttle effect. Subsequently, the assembled
Li||Li (1 mA cm−2 for 2000 h), Li-Cu (99.4% of CE), and
Li-S (1000 cycles at 4 C) cells deliver extremely excellent
cycling stability. An Ah-level Li-S pouch cell also exhibits
negligible capacity decay (50 cycles without capacity
decay). This conﬁrms the importance of regulating both
anions and cations for high-safety LSBs.

Considering the promising application prospects, here we
focus on lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), which possess the
highest theoretical speciﬁc energy among lithium batteries
(1675 mAh g−1). In this study, we ﬁrst introduced the concept
of a bidirectional ion kinetic regulator with the aim of achiev-
ing dendrite-free LSBs. By implementing this bidirectional
ion kinetic regulator, we hope to exert precise control over
the transport and deposition behaviors of ions within the
batteries, thereby paving the way for the development of
more reliable and efﬁcient LSBs that are free from the
detrimental effects of dendrites. A 2D hexaaminophenyl
conductive metal-organic framework (Ni-HAB) (Figure S1)
is adopted as a model molecule of bidirectional ion kinetic
regulator (Figure 1). Owing to the interaction between Ni-
HAB and LiTFSI, these two substances can complex with
each other, thereby giving rise to a transition state with a
unique spatial octahedral conﬁguration. In this speciﬁc state,
the Li+ extraction and diffusion energy barriers are reduced,
while the migration of TFSI−is signiﬁcantly slowed down
as a result of the anchoring action of Ni-HAB with the
increased steric hindrance. This dual effect of bidirectional
ion kinetic regulator played a crucial role in optimizing the
electrochemical performance of LSBs, as it simultaneously
facilitated the movement of Li+ while restraining the overly
rapid movement of TFSI−. Surprisingly, the change in the
composition of the solvated structure might promote the
transformation of Li+ deposition into a distinct deposition
structure, with the (200) crystal plane acting as the dominant
growth plane. Morphology observations during the in-situ
deposition process and the transformation of the main
components of the SEI from LiF to Li2O, both conﬁrmed that
the Ni-HAB electrolyte enhanced lithium uniform deposition.
Furthermore, the unique octahedral transition state com-
plexes of Ni-HAB and LiTFSI demonstrate an attractive force
towards solvent molecules. Meanwhile, the abundant Ni-N4
sites in Ni-HAB effectively improved the kinetics of the sulfur
reduction reaction (SRR). These factors greatly facilitated
the rapid conversion of intermediate lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs) and reduced the loss of active substances. Notably,
a practical Ah-level Li-S pouch cell maintained nearly 100%
capacity after 50 cycles and is still operating, demonstrating
satisfactory stability and safety reliability. Consequently, for
the ﬁrst time, the concept of a bidirectional ion kinetic
regulator based on the Sand’s time is proposed, and its
successful application in LSBs provides a valuable direction
for the realization of high safety and stability in secondary
batteries.
Results and Discussion
The Unique Octahedral Coordination of Ni-HAB Bidirectional
Ion Kinetic Regulator with TFSI−Adjusts Li+ Solvation Structure
and Promotes Desolvation
To investigate the impact of Ni-HAB bidirectional ion kinetic
regulator on LiTFSI within the electrolyte, the Zeta potential
of the electrolyte containing Ni-HAB was measured before
and after the introduction of LiTFSI (Figure 2a). Upon the
addition of LiTFSI, there is a remarkable alteration in the
Zeta potential with its value decreasing from +116.11
to
+43.59 mV. The high Zeta potential of +116.11 mV suggests
that the initial Ni-HAB in dimethoxyethane (DME) was
electrically stable, with Ni-HAB exhibiting a predominantly
positive charge. The decrease in Zeta potential down to
+43.59 mV implies that, although the system still retains
its overall stability, the TFSI−anions generated from the
dissociation of LiTFSI are likely to interact with Ni-HAB,
forming a transition state complex characterized by signiﬁcant
steric hindrance.[16] This interaction might be of critical
signiﬁcance in modulating the electrolyte’s properties and,
consequently, inﬂuencing the battery’s performance.

To further explore this interaction, ultraviolet-visible (UV-
Vis) spectroscopy was employed for veriﬁcation (Figure 2b).
LiTFSI exhibits an n→σ* electronic transition peak of S═O
at 280 nm. Upon the addition of Ni-HAB, the intensity of
this electronic transition peak was signiﬁcantly enhanced,
likely due to the N atom adjacent to S═O bond obtained
additional electrons during the interaction with Ni-HAB,
which caused the electron cloud distribution of the S═O
bond to be repelled and shifted towards the O side. As a
result, the bond length increased slightly and the bond energy
decreased, thereby enhancing the possibility of electronic
transitions.[17] For Ni-HAB, the Ni atom coordinates with
N atoms within TFSI−and interacts with solvent molecules
primarily through its non-bonding d orbitals. The orbital-
based interaction not only affects the electronic distribution
within Ni-HAB itself but also modiﬁes its reactivity and
binding afﬁnity towards other species, such as TFSI−. The
electron distribution within the 3d orbitals of Ni in the
presence of the TFSI−anion was elucidated through the
calculation of the projected density of states (PDOS). Ni atom
forms a planar square conﬁguration with N in HAB via dsp2
hybridization, with no unpaired electrons on the 3d orbital
(Figure 2d,e).[18] However, when the Ni atom complexes with
the N atom within TFSI−, a distinct change takes place in
its electron conﬁguration. Speciﬁcally, two unpaired electrons
will occupy the dx2-y2 and dz2 orbitals (Figure 2f,g).[19] This
change in electron conﬁguration was conﬁrmed by testing
the magnetic moment of Ni-HAB before and after LiTFSI
adsorption. The initial dsp[2] hybridized Ni-HAB exhibited
diamagnetism (Figure 2c) due to fully paired inner d-orbital
electrons. Following LiTFSI adsorption, Ni-HAB displayed
a degree of ferromagnetism towing to the appearance of
unpaired inner electrons induced by a conversion from dsp2
to sp3d2 hybridization.[20]
To assess the viability of the proposed conﬁguration,
molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) mapping and den-
sity functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to
predict the electrophilic and nucleophilic sites, as well as
potential coordination structures of Ni-HAB and LiTFSI
(Figure 2h). The van der Waals surface revealed that Ni and
Li exhibit the lowest and highest MESP values, respectively,
suggesting that Ni is predisposed to donate electrons to TFSI−
connected Li+.[21] Furthermore, the energy barrier for their
possible coordination structure was calculated. the formation
energy barriers (Ea) of two possible high-spin coordination
structures were calculated (Figure 2i). The results demon-
strated that the Ea of the octahedral coordination structure
(−5.89 eV) was signiﬁcantly lower than that of the square-
pyramidal conﬁguration (−4.62 eV), indicating that Ni-HAB
preferentially adsorb two TFSI−simultaneously to form an
octahedral conﬁguration. Meanwhile, the energy barrier for
Li+ extraction within the LiTFSI is substantially diminished
from −1.10 to −2.30 eV under this conﬁguration (Figure S2),
indicating its facilitation in the desolvation of Li+.[22]
To delve deeper into the alterations of coordination
during adsorption, K-edge X-ray absorption ﬁne structure
(XAFs) analyses was conducted on Ni for Ni-HAB before and
after adsorption. The X-ray adsorption near-edge structure
(XANES) spectra revealed that the valence state of Ni-HAB
was elevated compared to that of Ni foil, both before and
after adsorption.[23,24] Notably, the valence state of Ni-HAB
diminished following the adsorption of TFSI−(Figure 2j).
Further insights were gleaned from wavelet transform results
(Figure 2k,l), when the central Ni atom adsorbed the N
atom from TFSI−, the Ni-N bond distance contracted from
an initial 1.595 to 1.503 Å, while the distance between Ni
and the secondary coordinating C atoms remained unchanged
(2.45 Å), which indicated that the Ni-N4 unit was predomi-
nantly involved in the complexation process.[25] More notably,
after adsorbing TFSI−, a new interaction force between
Ni and N emerged at 5 Å−1 in the K space with 3 Å,
which was longer than that of the Ni-N4 coordination bond
in Ni-HAB, suggesting a relatively weaker complexation
interaction.[26,27]
Raman spectroscopy was employed to delve into how
the coordination between Ni-HAB and TFSI−inﬂuences the
Li+ solvation structure. A well-established principle is that
a reduction in anion-cation coordination correlates with a
red shift in Raman peaks.[28] DME, a pivotal solvent in
Li+ solvation, upon dissolving LiTFSI, there was a shift in
the Raman peaks from 773.3 to 739.1 cm−1 (Figure 2m),
signifying a solvation structure primarily characterized by
solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) (Figure 2n). The intro-
duction of Ni-HAB prompted a blue shift to 746.7 cm−1,
indicating a transformation in the Li+ solvation structure
to contact ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregation groups (AGGs)
(Figure 2o), with the SSIPs’ presence reduced to 13.6%. This
observation underscores the effectiveness of the Ni-HAB-
TFSI−coordination complex in alleviating the desolvation
resistance imposed by solvent molecules on Li+ (Figure 2p),
which is likely a key contributor to the diminished Li+
desolvation energy barrier.[29,30]
To evaluate the stability of the electrolyte after the
introduction of Ni-HAB, electrochemical stability window
measurements were performed on the different electrolytes
(Figure S3). The results show that the Ni-HAB electrolyte
has an electrochemical stability window comparable to that
of the blank electrolyte. Moreover, within the operating
voltage range of LSBs, the Ni-HAB electrolyte exhibits a
lower response current of 0.0275 mA, indicating its superior
stability. Additionally, the incorporation of the conductive
MOF material Ni-HAB provides additional pathways for ion
transport, thereby effectively enhancing the electrolyte’s ionic
conductivity (Figure S4).
Stainless steel symmetrical cells with electrolytes contain-
ing varying concentrations of Ni-HAB were also assembled
and conducted electrochemical tests to investigate the impact
of altered solvation structures on the transport and diffusion
of Li+. The Arrhenius equation (Figure S5) was employed
to reveal the effects of solvation structures on the activation
energy barrier (Ea) for Li+ diffusion.[31] It was observed that
at the optimal concentration, the Ea could be signiﬁcantly
reduced from 0.0847 to 0.0655 eV. Furthermore, by assem-
bling Li||Li symmetric cells, the Li+ transference number
was measured to be increased from 0.465 to 0.850 (Figure
S6), indicating that Ni-HAB effectively achieved bidirectional
regulation of anions and cations transport by complexing with
the anions (TFSI−).[32] (Here and in the following tests, unless

Theoretically, the initiation time for dendrite formation in
the Ni-HAB electrolyte is projected to be over 25 times longer
compared to the blank electrolyte. This substantial enhance-
ment is attributed to Ni-HAB’s bidirectional regulation on
both the anions and cations by complexing with TFSI−,
facilitating the transport and deposition of Li+, thereby
achieving such a signiﬁcant improvement.
Characterization of Li+ Deposition Processes and Outcomes
across Diverse Solvation Structures
To verify this theoretical promotion effect on the lithium
deposition process, in situ optical microscopy was employed
to visualize the real-time deposition process in different
electrolytes. In the blank electrolyte, due to the hindrance of
Li+ transport and diffusion by solvent molecules, numerous
dendrite nucleation sites formed, leading to the emergence of
coral-like dendritic structures within 0.5 h (Figure 3a), which
evolved into an interwoven dendritic network as deposition
continued. In contrast, in the Ni-HAB electrolyte, the newly
deposited lithium exhibited a dense and uniform morphology,
evenly distributed across the electrode surface, with virtually
no dendrite formation (Figure 3b). This uniform deposition
pattern ensures safety and stability during battery operation,
thereby signiﬁcantly extending the battery’s lifespan.
Beyond the visual assessment of deposition morphology,
CE can serve as an indicator of side reaction intensity.
After depositing an equivalent amount of lithium on Cu
foil and conducting 10 cycles at 1 mA cm−2, the initial CE
in an ordinary cell was only 98.3% due to side reactions
(Figure 3c). In contrast, the Ni-HAB electrolyte assembled
cell exhibited no side reactions, achieving a near-perfect
99.9% effective deposition/stripping efﬁciency. To validate the
Ni-HAB electrolyte’s impact on reducing the energy barrier
for deposition, Li-Cu cells were assembled with different
electrolytes. The cells with the blank electrolyte showed an
overpotential of 44.7 mV at 0.5 mAh cm−2. In contrast,
the cells with Ni-HAB electrolyte exhibited a substantial
decrease in overpotential, dropping to 29.7 mV (Figure 3d).
This signiﬁcant reduction indicates a signiﬁcant reduction in
the lithium nucleation barrier, highlighting the effectiveness
of the improved electrolyte in facilitating deposition. Post-
cycle deposition morphology on the Cu electrode’s surface
was examined using SEM. It was observed that the lithium
deposition in the Ni-HAB electrolyte assembled Li-Cu cell
closely resembled the in situ optical microscopy observations,
with a compact and smooth layer of lithium (Figure 3e).
Whereas the ordinary cell produced numerous rod-like den-
drites (Figure 3f), which was the initial growth characteristic
morphology of lithium dendrites.
To delve into the underlying reasons for the distinct depo-
sition patterns, ﬁnite element modeling (FEM) simulation was
utilized to investigate the variations in Li+ deposition on the
electrode surface across different electrolytes. The simulation
in these electrolytes revealed the inﬂuence of distribution
of potential differences on Li+ deposition (Figure 3g). In
the blank electrolyte, localized areas with higher ion concen-
tration exhibited signiﬁcantly higher potentials compared to
surrounding regions. As a result, there will be generated a
high local electric ﬁeld due to the ion concentration gradients.
The high local electric ﬁelds at these sites attract Li+ and
continuously intensify this attraction, triggering chain deteri-
oration that exacerbates dendrite growth. Conversely, in the
Ni-HAB electrolyte, the effective reduction of the Li+ extrac-
tion energy barrier and diffusion activation energy engender a
more uniform distribution on the electrode surface and higher
transfer efﬁciency, giving rise to a negligible overall spatial
potential difference. Consequently, Li+ deposition is much
more uniform, with fewer excessive nucleation sites.[34] The
simulation outcomes align well with the observations from
in situ optical microscopy, effectively conﬁrming that the Ni-
HAB electrolyte signiﬁcantly enhances the homogeneity of
lithium deposition.

newly deposited lithium layers observed in different elec-
trolytes have piqued our interest in their properties. Lithium
crystallizes in a body-centered cubic (BCC) structure, with
primary crystal planes including (110), (200), and (211).[35]
Typically, in the natural deposition process, the (110) plane
is the dominant orientation and is also the most prone to
dendrite formation (Figure 3h). However, in the presence of

complexation.[37] Compared to other solvent molecules, Li+ is
more likely to acquire the electron from Ni-HAB, facilitating
subsequent electron extraction and deposition processes.

The ability of the electrolyte to boost deposition efﬁciency
and lower the nucleation energy barrier necessitates rigorous
testing of its stability over extended deposition/stripping
cycles. Li-Cu cells were assembled with different electrolytes
and tested at 1 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure 5a). It was
observed that, due to severe side reactions, the CE of cells
with blank electrolyte started to decline signiﬁcantly after
about 50 cycles, dropping to only around 80% by approxi-
mately 100 cycles. The polarization short-circuit phenomenon
was also evident in the deposition voltage curves (Figure 5b).
However, with the addition of Ni-HAB, the deposition con-
ditions improved progressively (Figure 5c–e). At the optimal
concentration of 0.3 wt% of Ni-HAB, the cells could stably
undergo deposition and stripping for over 450 h, consistently
maintaining a high CE of about 98% and an invariable
deposition voltage of 13.2 mV. Post-cycle SEM imaging of the
electrode surfaces further revealed signiﬁcant differences in
deposition uniformity and density, in the blank electrolyte,
numerous haphazardly stacked dendritic morphologies can
be observed, whereas in the optimal electrolyte, the lithium
deposition morphology was as smooth and compact as a
mirror surface.
Furthermore, a detailed cycle stability test was conducted
using Li||Li symmetrical cells. It was observed that as the
current density increased, the symmetrical cells with the
blank electrolyte became severely polarized at currents
above 2 mA, and directly failed due to dendrite puncture
at 5 mA. In contrast, cells assembled with the Ni-HAB
electrolyte could achieve stable deposition/stripping under
various current stimulations (Figure 5f). When cycling at
1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2, the ordinary cells showed
polarization around 200 h and ultimately failed due to short-
circuiting after 690 h, while the improved cells exhibited
an initial deposition voltage of 25 mV, which decreased to
13 mV and ultimately achieved stable cycling for over 2000 h
(Figure 5g). Even at a higher current density of 5 mA cm−2,
1 mAh cm−2, the improved cells still demonstrated signif-
icantly better performance in terms of deposition voltage
and stability compared to the ordinary cells (Figure 5h).
Upon examining the electrode surfaces post-cycle, signiﬁcant
differences were noted. The cells assembled with the blank
electrolyte exhibited obvious “sprout” whiskers, and under
the stimulation of higher currents, the dendrite morphology
grew into longer and thicker dendrites, posing a potential
risk of puncturing the separator.[41] In the improved cells,
lithium mainly accumulated on the plane in the form of small
ﬂakes. As deposition continued, the boundaries between
the ﬂakes were ﬁlled with subsequently deposited lithium,
gradually forming a ﬂat mirror-like morphology (Figure 5i).
Thus, the improved electrolyte’s effect on the homogenization
of lithium deposition has been sufﬁciently proved.

the current increased from 0.2 to 3 C, the second discharge

approximately 0.1 V (Figure S22). However, the discharge
platform of the improved cells is still obvious even at 3 C

indicates that it has a faster SRR conversion kinetics inside.
The degree of redox reaction can be compared from the
effective capacity. At different current densities ranging from
0.2 to 3 V, the modiﬁed cells continued displaying robust
capacities of 1186.5, 1102.3, 1021.4, 965.4, and 930.5 mAh

The cycle stability test of Li-S cells was further carried
out. The results showed that the capacity of the modiﬁed
cells declined by only about 60 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles
at 0.2 C (Figure 7d), with negligible loss of active material.
Subsequent tests at higher currents were performed to
evaluate the effects on polarization resistance and dendrite
growth inhibition. At a 1 C, the modiﬁed cells exhibited a
minimal decay rate of 0.0497% per cycle after 500 cycles
(Figure 7e) and were capable of sustaining over 1000 cycles
at a 4 C high current (Figure 7f). Notably, the ordinary
cells not only degraded more rapidly, but also succumbed
to short-circuit failure around 150 cycles due to dendrite
puncture under the stress of high current. These cycle tests
underscored the dual beneﬁts of Ni-HAB in enhancing
cathode SRR kinetics and promoting uniform anode lithium
deposition. Achieving higher energy density requires higher
sulfur loading. Therefore, cells with high sulfur loads of 7.49
and 9.86 mg cm−2 were assembled with Ni-HAB electrolyte
(Figure 7g). Remarkably, even at such high sulfur loads, the
cells maintained a surface capacity of around 6 mAh cm−2
and exhibited stable cycling for over 100 cycles, outperforming
current commercial lithium-ion batteries. Leveraging the
effective promotion of uniform lithium deposition at the
anode and the inhibition of the shuttle effect by the Ni-
HAB electrolyte, an Ah-level long-cycle Li-S pouch cell was
successfully assembled (Figure 7i,j). Even at such a high-
capacity level, the capacity was almost maintained at 100%
after 50 cycles (still operating) (Figure 7h), demonstrating
exceptional safety and stability, making it suitable for practical
application. When evaluated against the recent Li-S pouch
cells reported, our work has outperformed others in terms of
capacity, capacity retention rate and cycle life performance
and sulfur load (Figure 7k), underscoring the advantages of
pouch cells that incorporate ion bidirectional governor.[46–57]
Conclusion
In this study, we introduce a nickel (Ni)-based bidirectional
ion kinetic regulator that effectively mitigates the inﬂuence
of solvent molecules on Li+ solvation through the formation
of spatial octahedral complexes with TFSI−anions. This
enhancement strengthens the interactions between cations
and anions, signiﬁcantly lowering the energy barriers for Li+
desolvation and diffusion. We were pleasantly surprised to
discover that this regulation induces a unique deposition
mode of Li+, with a preferential orientation of the (200) crys-
tal plane, resulting in a dense and uniform planar morphology
containing more Li2O and LiF components. Additionally, the
octahedral complexes’ attraction towards solvent molecules
effectively reduces the dissolution of LiPSs, while their rich
Ni-N4 sites exhibit efﬁcient catalytic activity towards SRRs,
thus effectively inhibiting capacity decay. Based on MD
simulations and electrochemical characterization results, we
calculated and deduced that the electrolyte with bidirectional
ion kinetic regulator could extend the dendrite formation time
(τ s) by more than 25 times. The Li||Li, Li-Cu, and Li-S cells
assembled with Ni-HAB electrolyte demonstrated signiﬁcant
improvements in deposition behavior and outstanding cycling
stability. The ﬁnal Ah-level high-safety and stable Li-S pouch
cell showed negligible capacity fading and continued to
operate, demonstrating remarkable reliability.
