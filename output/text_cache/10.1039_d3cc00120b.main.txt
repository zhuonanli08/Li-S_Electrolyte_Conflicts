Unveiling light eﬀect on formation of trisulfur
radicals in lithium–sulfur batteries†

S3 is the important electrochemical intermediate in Li–S batteries
of highly solvating solvents. Herein, the dissociation of S62 into S3
is deeply studied. light is proven to promote the formation of S3
from the dissociation of S62. Accordingly, a strategy of pre-

proposed to activate sulfur cathodes of Li–S batteries.
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered a promising energy
storage system due to their high theoretical energy density of
2600 W h kg1,1 thanks to the multiple electron transfers that take
place in the battery system as it undergoes the charging and
discharging processes. A highly solvating solvent such as dimethyl
sulfoxide
(DMSO),
which
offers
high
solubility
of
lithium
polysulfides,2 has been demonstrated to deliver prominent sulfur
utilization in the Li–S battery.3 Different from ether-based electro-
lytes, the evident blue color of DMSO-based electrolytes indicates the
existence of trisulfur radicals
S3
ð
Þ, which as an internal redox
mediator can accelerate electron transfer, and enhance sulfur
utilization in Li–S batteries.4,5 For example, Lu and co-workers found
that S3 facilitated the full utilization of active sulfur with the help
of operando UV-vis spectroscopy.6 Nazar and co-workers utilized
operando X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy
to confirm the presence of S3 in the highly solvating solvent, and
S3 participated in the redox reactions as a reaction facilitator.7
Anions, molecules/ion pairs, or radicals may exist in electro-
lytes, and all of them can significantly impact the electroche-
mical performance of Li–S batteries.3,8 Such works have
been extensively attempted using various characterization
techniques.7 For example, the adsorption band at 617 nm is
usually attributed to the presence of S3.9 Certain species of
dissolved polysulfides can be difficult to detect due to the
limitations of the characterization techniques. In the case of
Raman spectroscopy, for instance, light with a wavelength of
o800 nm has been shown to initiate the dissociation of poly-
sulfides to form sulfur radicals, which interferes stability of
detection results.10 Nevertheless, the light sensitivity of sulfur is
often neglected by researchers, especially in studies concerning Li–
S batteries, which has caused the existence of some polysulfide
species in the electrolyte to be overlooked. This is likely to be the
reason why the presence of S3, which coexists with S3 in a
polysulfide solution,11 is seldom reported in Li–S batteries while
the presence of S3 is frequently reported. A similar situation also
applies to LiS3 radicals, which is believed to exist in Li2S6
solution,8 no direct spectral evidence confirms the hypothesis so
far. Fortunately, theoretical calculations are an invaluable means
to assist the interpretation of obtained experimental spectra, which
allows for a better assessment of the polysulfides.12
Herein, three commonly utilized experimental methods, i.e.,
Raman, UV-vis, and electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy,
as well as ab initio calculations, were together used to study the
dissociation of S62 into S3 in DMSO solution. The ESR spectra
prove the light sensitivity of the dissociation of S62 into S3, and
the photoinduced proportion was determined to be 47% by fitting
the UV-vis spectrum with the modified Beer-Lambert law. Thus, the
real content of S3 can be confirmed. On this basis, highly active
S3 was pre-introduced in the electrolytes to enhance the sulfur
utilization of Li–S batteries. We hope that this work can improve the
understanding of the polysulfide species and radicals in DMSO-
based electrolytes, and further guide to design of electrolytes for
high-energy-density Li–S batteries.

Fig. 2a shows Raman spectra of pure DMSO solvent and
DMSO solution of 10 mM Li2S6. The latter shows four addi-
tional peaks, located at 437.8 cm1 (A), 484.0 cm1 (B),
533.2 cm1 (A1), and 611.8 cm1 (SGG), labeled by the irredu-
cible representations of the symmetry of S62, S3, and S2 in
Fig. 2b. The peak at 484.0 cm1 and 437.8 cm1 (vs. 490.1 cm1
and 438.5 cm1 by calculation) is attributed to the stretching of
the bridge S–S bond and the terminal S–S bond in S62,
respectively. The peak at 533.2 cm1 (vs. 545.0 cm1 by calcula-
tion) is attributed to the stretching of S–S bond of S3. The
existence of S62 and S3 is in accord with the calculated pKdi
in Table S2 (ESI†). The peak at 611.8 cm1 (vs. 600.1 cm1 by
calculation) is attributed to the symmetrical stretching vibra-
tion of S2, which was hypothesized to co-exist with S3.11
Fig. 2c shows the experimental UV-vis spectrum of 0.3 mM
Li2S6 in DMSO. Compared with pure DMSO (Fig. S4, ESI†), four
absorption bands are observed at 267, 355, 491, and 617 nm,
respectively. To assign these bands, the computational UV-vis
spectra of S62 and S3 were performed by TD-DFT13 method,
and weighted by their theoretical concentrations (0.23 and
0.14 mM) according to pKd3, as shown in Fig. S5a (ESI†). In contrast,
the experimental absorption bands at 355 and 491 nm (vs. 332.4 and
488.3 nm by calculation) are attributed to S62, and the band at
617 nm (vs. 627.2 nm by calculation) is attributed to S3. In
particular, the band at 267 nm (vs. 261.4 nm by calculation) can
be attributed to the integrated contribution of S62 and S3, and
the excitation models of both are shown and discussed in Fig. S5b
(ESI†). From the electron transition orbitals in the visible region in
Fig. 2d, the 491 nm band is attributed to n - s* excitation of S62
from HOMO to LUMO transfer, and the 617 nm band is attributed
to n - p* excitation of S3 from SOMO to LUMO. It is worth
noting that S2 is still ignorable compared with S62 and S3 due
to no obvious adsorption peak being observed in the experimental
spectrum by checking the calculated spectrum of S2 (Fig. S6,
ESI†), although it has been detected by the Raman spectrum (SGG
in Fig. 2a). Notably, the band shapes, related to species concen-
tration, of the experimental spectrum cannot be well matched with
the calculated spectrum, although the band positions can be well
assigned via TD-DFT calculation. So, there must be some external
factors, such as light14 or concentration,15 that break the dissocia-
tion equilibrium between S62 and S3, which is discussed below.
Light sensitivity of polysulfides can be demonstrated by compar-
ing the ESR spectra of Li2S6 solution under light and dark condi-
tions (Fig. 3a). In the presence of light, a characteristic rhombic
S = 1/2 signal of S3 is apparent, but not under dark conditions.
This suggests that the formation of S3 from the dissociation of
S62 is indeed promoted by light, which raises the concentration of
the former and depresses the latter compared to dark conditions.

To verify the feasibility of the fitting above, we recalculated
the concentrations of S62 (0.09 mM) and S3 (0.42 mM) in
0.3 mM Li2S6 DMSO solution, according to pKd3 and P. The
obtained concentrations were further used as the weight of
their corresponding calculated UV-vis spectra to simulate the
experiment (Fig. 3d). The newly calculated spectrum, by con-
trast, becomes more consistent with the experimental spectrum.
Compared with the theoretical prediction, the concentration of
S62 decreases from 0.23 mM to 0.09 mM and that of S3
increases from 0.14 mM to 0.42 mM. The results imply that the
dissociation of S62 to S3 is indeed promoted by light under the
UV-vis light measurements, and it also proves that the previous
fitting is feasible. Predictably, other highly solvating sulfur solvents
should have similar eﬀects with DMSO. For example, similar
absorption band shapes of UV-vis and light promotion phenom-
enon of ESR are observed in tetramethylurea solvent (Fig. S8, ESI†).

electron aﬃnity (VEA) of several sulfur species (Fig. 4a). The higher
NI and VEA, the easier to combine Li+ and electrochemical reduction
reactions. S62 has the highest NI of 11.8 eV, implying that S62 ions
tend to dynamically combine Li+ to form ionic pairs. For example, if
S62 combines with one Li+ to form LiS6
 with a negative VEA of
7.6 eV, the reduction of S62 can still be diﬃcult to occur. Thus,
S62 needs to combine two Li+ atoms to form Li2S6 with a positive
VEA of 0.3 eV. It is, however, diﬃcult to simultaneously combine two
Li+ atoms in the electrode/electrolyte interface, because of the lack of
Li+ as a result of fast and massive depletion during electrochemical
reactions, and very negative pKd1 and pKd2. So, the reduction of S62
is not easy, which may enable active material to diﬀuse from the
cathode and lead to a severe shuttle eﬀect. Similarly, S3 has a
relatively positive NI of 8.7 eV, indicating that S3 is also easy to
dynamically combine with one Li+ ion to form an ion pair dynami-
cally. Moreover, S3 only need to combine one Li+ to form LiS3,
which has the highest VEA of 1.9 eV, indicating that the reduction of
S3 occurs easily via forming LiS3. Thus, we infer that the electro-
chemical reduction of Li–S batteries using a strongly solvating DMSO-
based electrolyte should be a radical-directed process.
Based on the promotion of S3 to electrochemical reactions,
we proposed a strategy of pre-introducing S3 by adding Li2S6
solution of DMSO prepared under light conditions into the
DMSO-based electrolyte (1 M Li-salt) of Li–S batteries. Accord-
ing to pKd3 and P discussed above, the concentration of S3 in
DMSO-based electrolytes with 1, 3, 5, and 10 mM of Li2S6 is ca.
0.61, 1.66, 2.67, and 5.16 mM. The discharge capacity of Li–S
battery with pre-introducing S3 is enhanced, but it decays
quickly due to the severe shuttle, as shown and discussed in
Fig. S9 (ESI†). Fig. 4b shows the comparison of the initial
discharge–charge voltage curves between Li–S cells using the
blank electrolyte and the catholyte with various concentrations
of S3. Two types of batteries both have an undesirable long
charging profile, which is a symptom of severe shuttle effect in
highly solvating electrolytes according to Choi and co-workers’
investigation.17 From the discharge profile, sulfur utilization of
cells is enhanced using the catholyte with S3, and with the
addition of 3 and 5 mM Li2S6 the cells show better capacity
enhancement (Fig. 4c). In addition, overpotential (at half of the
discharge capacity) between charge and discharge has also
been diminished where a cell with addition of 5 mM Li2S6
shows a better result (Fig. 4d). So, pre-introducing 5 mM Li2S6
into the electrolyte of Li–S batteries are the optimal addition
amount that is beneficial to sulfur cathode activation and
electrode kinetics in contrast to the blank DMSO electrolyte.
The results can be verified by the decreased charge transfer
resistance (Rct) in ESI† (Fig. 4e). Notably, the interfacial electron
transfer resistance is large, which may be due to the high
viscosity of the electrolyte caused by the highly solvating polysulfides,
as well as the high viscosity nature of DMSO solvent (1.99 cp).18
Furthermore, we made scanning electron microscope (SEM) images
of lithium anode retrieved from the blank cells and the cells of
adding 5 mM Li2S6 (Fig. 4f and Fig. S10, ESI†). By comparison, we
excluded influences on lithium anode of the adding 5 mM Li2S6,
because the cell with adding 5 mM Li2S6 is flat comparing the blank
cells after cycling. According to these results, we can infer that the pre-
introduced S3 gives rise to the radical-directed process, and thus
enhances sulfur utilization of Li–S batteries, as reported by Zhang
et al.19 Notably, overcharging always exists whether or not Li2S6 is pre-
introduced due to the severe shuttle effect caused by the massive
irreversible dissolution of dianions. Thus, it is necessary to design
sulfur-adsorbing/immobilizing host materials and effectively protect
lithium anode to obtain high-performance Li–S batteries.20–22
In summary, we studied the formation of S3 in DMSO-based
electrolytes, revealing the light promotion sensitivity of the dissocia-
tion of polysulfides, and designing a strategy for utilizing S3 to
activate sulfur cathode. From this study, it is desirable to design an
electrolyte that can support radicals while preventing the dissolved
lithium polysulfides from dissociating into anions. Further work is
in progress regarding such a strategy to select a practical electrolyte
for high-energy-density Li–S batteries in the future.
