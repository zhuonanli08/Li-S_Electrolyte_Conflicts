Lithium–sulfur batteries oﬀer a promising route toward high-energy, resource-resilient energy storage, yet their
practical deployment is hindered by coupled instabilities arising from polysulﬁde dissolution and lithium metal
degradation. Here, a system-level strategy is presented in which scalable carbon host synthesis, electrolyte
solvation chemistry, and electrochemical protocol are co-optimized to stabilize Li–S operation under
practical conditions. A CO2 physical activation route is introduced to replace conventional chemical
activation, enabling the production of porous carbon hosts with more precise control over the textural
properties while reducing processing complexity and chemical consumption. By tuning activation time, an
optimal
balance
between
microporosity
and
mesoporosity
is
achieved,
delivering
electrochemical
performance comparable to chemically activated carbons. Building on this platform, sparingly solvating
electrolytes (SSEs) are employed to regulate polysulﬁde solubility and lithium interfacial chemistry. While SSE
formulations reduce polysulﬁde dissolution and stabilize transport properties, the incorporation of LiFSI as an
additive further enhances lithium reversibility by promoting the formation of a robust and homogeneous
interphase. Beyond materials design, an asymmetric cycling protocol is introduced to regulate lithium
deposition
dynamics.
By
decoupling
plating
and
stripping
kinetics,
this
approach
suppresses
the
accumulation of high-surface-area lithium, signiﬁcantly extending cycle life. Combining the optimized
electrolyte and carbon host, stable cycling over ∼180 cycles is achieved at practical sulfur loading (∼3.5 mg
cm−2), more than doubling the lifetime obtained under conventional conditions. These results demonstrate
that durable Li–S performance emerges from the coordinated design of the electrode structure, solvation
environment, and operating protocol, providing a pathway toward practical implementation.
Introduction
The rapid expansion of electrochemical energy storage has
revealed a structural tension between performance ambition
and materials vulnerability. Conventional lithium-ion technol-
ogies rely on transition-metal-rich cathodes, graphite-based
anodes, and globally concentrated supply chains, rendering
battery economics increasingly sensitive to geopolitical insta-
bility, export restrictions, and mineral price volatility.1,2 In this
context, lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) oﬀer a fundamentally
diﬀerent resource and performance prole, where sulfur is
abundant, geographically widespread, and largely available as
an industrial by-product.3,4 Additionally, coupling sulfur with
a lithium metal anode (LMA) enables theoretical energy densi-
ties far exceeding those of conventional intercalation-based
systems. However, despite these advantages, translating Li–S
chemistry into stable,
high-eﬃciency commercial
devices
remains challenging due to coupled degradation processes at
both electrodes and within the electrolyte. Consequently, the
central challenge of Li–S technology is not a single component
limitation but rather a system-level coordination problem.5
On the cathode side, the sulfur redox reactions proceed
through the formation of soluble intermediates, called lithium
polysuldes (LiPSs), which can migrate between electrodes and
trigger the well-known shuttle eﬀect, leading to low coulombic
eﬃciency (CE), self-discharge, and rapid capacity fade. Simulta-
neously, the LMA undergoes parasitic reactions and morpho-
logical instability that consume electrolyte and active lithium,
increasing cell polarization and accelerating cell failure. These
degradation pathways become particularly severe under practical
conditions (high sulfur loading, lean electrolyte, and limited

Within this framework, rational engineering of the sulfur
host remains a central strategy for improving sulfur utilization
and mitigating LiPS dissolution. Porous carbon matrices
provide electronic conductivity, buﬀer the large volume changes
between sulfur and the discharge product (Li2S), and physically
conne soluble LiPS.8,9 However, maximizing surface area and
porosity alone does not guarantee improved performance. The
electrochemical behaviour of Li–S cathodes emerges from
a balance between microporosity, which enhances adsorption
and connement, and mesoporosity, which facilitates electro-
lyte accessibility and mass transport. Excessive connement
can impede redox kinetics, whereas insuﬃcient connement
promotes shuttle-driven
losses.
Therefore,
optimal
sulfur
cathode performance requires synchronizing pore architecture
with electrolyte chemistry and operating conditions rather than
optimizing any single structural parameter.10
Since pore architecture is largely determined during the
activation step, the choice of activation route becomes a critical
carbon
host
design
parameter.
Chemical
activation
is
commonly employed to produce high-surface-area carbons with
tunable pore size distributions. In our previous work, a chemi-
cally activated graphene-based carbon delivered a favourable
combination of conductivity and porosity for Li–S cathodes.11
Nevertheless, such activation methods typically require corro-
sive activating agents and extensive post-treatment steps,
including washing, neutralization, and drying, that increase
cost, processing complexity, and waste generation.12–14 These
factors introduce economic and environmental penalties that
counterbalance the intrinsic material advantages of low-cost
sulfur chemistry. From a practical perspective, simplifying the
synthesis route while maintaining precise control over pore
architecture is therefore highly desirable.15,16
Physical activation using CO2 oﬀers a promising and scalable
alternative. Controlled gas–solid reactions enable gradual pore
development regulated by temperature and activation time
without introducing activating agents. This approach reduces
process complexity and oﬀers a tunable pathway toward the
pore-architecture “sweet spot” required for Li–S cathodes.17,18
However, it remains unclear and underexplored whether CO2
activation can reproduce the functional pore characteristics
achieved through chemical routes and sustain comparable
electrochemical performance under practical Li–S conditions.
While some studies suggest that gas activation can generate
pore networks compatible with Li–S cycling, systematic evalu-
ations linking activation parameters, pore topology, and inter-
facial behaviour remain limited.19
While tailoring the pore architecture of the sulfur host is
essential to regulate LiPS connement and transport, it alone is
insuﬃcient to ensure stable operation under practical condi-
tions. Electrolyte engineering constitutes a second critical design
axis, governing both LiPS solubility and LMA interfacial stability.
Conventional ether-based systems, typically combining 1,3-di-
oxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), provide favour-
able sulfur redox kinetics but enable high polysulde solubility
and unstable lithium interfaces.20,21 Recent advances in solvation
engineering, including high- and localized high-concentration
electrolytes, oen described as sparingly solvating electrolytes
(SSEs), demonstrate that compositional adjustments can signif-
icantly modify solvation structures and interphase chemistry,
suppress parasitic reactions, and improve cycling stability.22–24 In
particular, the selection of lithium salts and electrolyte additives
plays a crucial role in determining interfacial composition,
plating/stripping reversibility, and ultimately the cycling stability
of Li–S batteries. Careful tuning of these components can
signicantly enhance Li interphase stability and improve long-
term cell durability.25
Beyond
materials
and
chemistry,
the
electrochemical
protocol itself represents an additional and oen underutilized
design parameter. In most Li–S studies, mild symmetric
charge–discharge rates are adopted as default testing condi-
tions, implicitly attributing degradation to materials limita-
tions. However, recent insights from lithium metal battery
research
indicate
that
asymmetric
cycling
can
decouple
damaging processes: slower deposition combined with faster
stripping can reduce cumulative interfacial stress and promote
more uniform lithium morphology.26,27 Given that Li–S cells
share many of the same lithium metal interfacial sensitivities,
adapting the cycling protocol represents a rational strategy to
mitigate degradation mechanisms that cannot be resolved
through materials optimization alone.
Here, we demonstrate that durable Li–S performance emerges
from the coordinated integration of scalable carbon host
synthesis, electrolyte solvation engineering, and operational
strategy. Specically, we replace chemical activation with a CO2
physical activation route and systematically tune activation time
to achieve a pore architecture optimized for sulfur redox under
lean electrolyte conditions. The optimized host is then combined
with tailored SSE formulations derived from a DOL-uorinated
ether
co-solvent
(1H,1H,5H-octauoropentyl-1,1,2,2-
tetrauoroethyl ether, OCTO) reference electrolyte containing
1.5 M LiTFSI (lithium bis(triuoromethanesulfonyl)imide), with
controlled additions of LiFSI (lithium bis(uorosulfonyl)imide)
or LiNO3 to modulate interfacial chemistry. LMA compatibility is
rst evaluated in Li‖Cu and Li‖Li congurations and correlated
with Li deposition morphology before assessing Li–S cycling
performance. Finally, symmetric and asymmetric protocols are
compared to elucidate the inuence of the cycling conditions on
the long-term stability. Collectively, this work shows that
resolving the apparent trade-oﬀbetween cost reduction and
cycling durability in Li–S batteries requires coordinated system-
level design rather than incremental component improve-
ments. By aligning scalable carbon synthesis, electrolyte engi-
neering, and cycling protocol optimization, we establish an
integrated pathway toward high-energy, resource-resilient LSBs
capable of sustained operation under practical conditions.

For comparison, a chemically activated reference carbon,
denoted as ResFA-KOH, was prepared following the procedure
reported in previous work.29 Briey, the pyrolysed carbon was
mixed with KOH in a 1 : 6 mass ratio and heat-treated at 800 °C
for 1 h under an Ar atmosphere. The resulting material was
subsequently neutralized with HCl, thoroughly washed with
distilled water, and dried before use.
Electrode
processing.
Sulfur
cathodes
were
prepared
following the procedure from previous studies.28,30 The cathode
composition comprises 64 wt% sulfur, 26 wt% carbon mate-
rials, and 10 wt% polymeric binder. The binder was composed
of 5 wt% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and 5 wt%
styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) dispersed in water. This carbon
ratio was selected according to a previous formulation study,
where 10 wt% graphene-based activated carbon combined with
16 wt% KJ600 provided the best compromise between electro-
chemical performance, electrode processability and cost.28 The
carbon fraction included 16 wt% Ketjen black (EC-600JD) and
10 wt% of each of the synthesised carbons (ResFA-KOH or CO2-
activated samples). Sulfur/carbon composites were prepared by
melt diﬀusion at 155 °C for 12 hours under an argon atmo-
sphere to ensure homogeneous sulfur inltration.
Electrolyte preparation. Four diﬀerent electrolytes were
prepared for this study: the standard electrolyte used in Li–S
cells and three SSEs, including a reference electrolyte and two
modied formulations. All electrolytes were prepared in a glo-
vebox under an Ar atmosphere.
The standard electrolyte consisted of a 1 : 1 volume ratio of
DME and DOL with 0.5 M LiTFSI and 0.5 M LiNO3. This baseline
electrolyte was employed to validate the fabricated cathodes.
For the SSEs, the electrolyte compositions were based on a 1.5 M
LiTFSI in a mixture of DOL and OCTO in a 1 : 1 volume ratio,
denoted as DO-15TFSI, a formulation presented in a previous
study.31 The rst modication, referred to as DO-15TFSI-01LN,
involved adding 0.1 M LiNO3 to the base electrolyte. The
second modication, referred to as DO-15TFSI-02FSI, involved
adding 0.2 M LiFSI to the base electrolyte.
Physicochemical characterisation
The textural properties of the samples were determined by
physical adsorption of N2 at 77 K in the relative pressure range
from 1 × 10−6 to 1 using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 volumetric
analyzer. Prior to measurements, the samples were degassed at
150 °C for 18 h under vacuum.
From these isotherms, the surface area (SBET) was obtained
by applying the BET equation in the P/P0 0.01–0.1 range; the
total pore volume (VT) was calculated at a relative pressure of
0.97, assuming adsorption in liquid form (Gurvich rule). The
pore size distribution was calculated using the DFT method
with the HS-2D-NLDFT kernel (Micromeritics). From these
distributions,
the
micropore,
Vmicrop,DFT,
and
mesopore,
Vmesop,DFT, volumes were obtained. To evaluate the LiPS reten-
tion capacity of the diﬀerent carbon samples, 9.5 × 10−3 cm3 of
each carbon was added to 2 mL of a 5 mM Li2S6 solution in DME
and allowed to rest for 24 h. The resulting solutions were rst
evaluated visually to monitor colour changes and then analysed
by UV-vis spectroscopy.
The morphology of carbon powders, sulfur cathodes, lithium
deposits on Cu, and lithium metal anodes recovered aer
cycling was characterised via scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) using a Thermo Scientic Apreo 2S HiVac microscope,
which is equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDX) detector used to determine the surface elemental
composition. Lithium samples were transferred and handled
under an inert atmosphere.
To evaluate the LiPS dissolution ability of the electrolytes,
stoichiometric quantities of solid S8 and Li2S were mixed in
each electrolyte to prepare 0.5 M and 6 M Li2S6 saturated
solutions for the SSEs and the DME/DOL electrolytes, respec-
tively. The resulting solutions were ltered through 0.45 mm
PTFE syringe lters. The ltered samples were rst evaluated
visually using optical imaging to observe the colour evolution of
the samples due to the LiPS solubility. In addition to visual
inspection, UV-vis spectroscopic measurements were per-
formed to further assess the dissolution process.
For UV-vis measurements, the solutions were diluted 10
times and characterised using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Cary
5000, Agilent) in sealed cuvettes over the wavelength range of
200–800 nm.
Contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the
wettability of the diﬀerent electrolytes on the cathode. A 3 mL
electrolyte droplet was placed on the cathode surface using an
LSA100 surface analyser (Lauda Scientic).

Results and discussion
As outlined in the Introduction section, the rst objective of this
work was to nd a scalable and cost-competitive simplied
synthesis approach for porous carbon hosts for sulfur cathodes
without compromising the pore architecture required for eﬃ-
cient Li–S operation. For this purpose, the conventional KOH-
based chemical activation route was replaced by a CO2 phys-
ical activation process. A schematic comparison of both path-
ways (Fig. 1) highlights the reduction in the process complexity
achieved through gas-phase activation. In contrast to KOH
activation, which requires a high activating-agent-to-carbon
ratio followed by acid neutralization and extensive washing,
CO2 activation proceeds just through controlled gas–solid
reactions without additional chemical reagents, leading to
a clear reduction in waste and thereby improving the scalability
and cost-eﬀectiveness of the porous carbon production.
To evaluate the viability of the CO2 approach and identify
optimal
processing
conditions,
the
activation
time
was
systematically varied (5, 12, and 20 h). The resulting carbons,
denoted as ResFA-CO2-5h, ResFA-CO2-12h, and ResFA-CO2-20h,
were compared with the chemically activated reference material
(ResFA-KOH). This comparison enables assessment of whether
CO2 activation can reproduce the structural characteristics
required for eﬃcient Li–S cathode operation without relying on
conventional chemical routes.
The morphology of the synthesized carbons was rst examined
by SEM. The images, shown in Fig. 2a, reveal no signicant
morphological diﬀerences between the chemically activated
reference sample and the CO2-activated carbons. All samples
preserve the laminar, interconnected graphene-based structure of
the precursor, indicating that the activation process primarily
modies internal porosity rather than altering the macroscopic
morphology of the carbon framework. This behaviour is consis-
tent with previous studies, where CO2 physical activation, being
a milder and less aggressive process than chemical activation,
tends to better preserve the original microstructure of the
precursor while progressively developing porosity.34 Beyond
morphology, and as outlined in the Introduction section, the
porosity of the obtained materials is a critical parameter govern-
ing the performance of the carbon host in Li–S technology. To
investigate the evolution of the pore structure, the textural prop-
erties were analysed using N2 adsorption–desorption measure-
ments. As shown in Fig. 2b and c, all samples exhibit Type I
isotherms, conrming the presence of signicant micropo-
rosity.35,36 Despite these similarities, it was observed that
increasing the CO2 activation time progressively enhances large
micropore development, leading to a surface area sequence of
ResFA-CO2-20h (2423 m2 g−1) > ResFA-CO2-12h (1866 m2 g−1) z
ResFA-KOH (1850 m2 g−1) > ResFA-CO2-5h (1525 m2 g−1). Notably,
the carbon activated for 12 h reaches a surface area comparable to
that of the chemically activated reference, demonstrating that CO2
activation can reproduce the level of textural development typi-
cally obtained through chemical activation.
However, extending the activation time to 20 h signicantly
alters the pore architecture, as evidenced by the isotherm

In addition to these structural diﬀerences, variations in
surface chemistry are observed depending on the activation
route. Surface elemental composition determined by EDX (Fig.
S1) shows variations in oxygen and phosphorus content across
activation routes. The negligible phosphorus content detected
in the KOH-activated sample is attributed to the combined
eﬀects of alkaline activation and subsequent acid washing,
which likely removes residual phosphorus-containing species
from the carbon structure.38 In contrast, CO2 activation does not
involve chemical leaching, allowing phosphorus-containing
functionalities to remain within the carbon matrix. Diﬀer-
ences in oxygen content further reect the distinct chemical
environments of the activation processes. The ResFA-KOH
sample exhibits a higher oxygen content, which can be attrib-
uted to the decomposition and reaction of KOH during activa-
tion, leading to the formation of oxygen-containing functional
groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and phenolic species.39 In
contrast, CO2 activation involves competing processes of
surface oxidation and thermal decomposition of unstable
oxygen functionalities at elevated temperatures, resulting in
a diﬀerent distribution of surface oxygen groups.40
To evaluate the functional implications of these structural
and chemical diﬀerences, LiPS adsorption tests were per-
formed. The inset in Fig. 2d presents the visual comparison of
Li2S6 solutions aer exposure to the diﬀerent developed
carbons. The ResFA-CO2-20h sample exhibits the lowest LiPS
retention capability, consistent with its higher mesopore frac-
tion and reduced micropore connement. In contrast, the
chemically activated carbon and the CO2-activated samples
obtained aer 5 h and 12 h show stronger LiPS retention, sug-
gesting a more favourable balance between adsorption strength
and pore accessibility. These observations were further quan-
tied by UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2d), conrming that the
remaining LiPS concentration in solution correlates with the
degree of microporosity. Although the LiPS retention behaviour
of the present CO2-activated carbon series is mainly ascribed to
diﬀerences in the textural properties, surface chemistry can also
inuence polysulde adsorption. However, in this particular
case, similar functional groups are present aer the CO2-acti-
vation treatment, indicating that the observed diﬀerences in
LiPS
retention
are
primarily
governed
by
pore-structure
evolution.41,42

These results highlight a key structural trade-oﬀ: while
increasing CO2 activation time enlarges the total surface area,
excessive pore widening weakens the connement strength
required to retain soluble LiPSs, which is critical for suppress-

To determine whether the observed diﬀerences in textural
properties and chemical compositions have an impact on the
electrochemical performance of the Li–S batteries, sulfur cath-
odes, including the diﬀerent carbon matrices, were processed.
The cathode formulation used herein was selected based on our
previous optimisation study, in which KJ600-only cathodes were
compared with formulations containing 5 and 10 wt% chemi-
cally activated graphene-based carbon additives. That study
showed that 10 wt% graphene-based carbon provides the best
compromise between sulfur utilisation, LiPS retention, redox
kinetics, electrode processability and material cost. Accord-
ingly, in the present work, 16 wt% KJ600 was maintained as
a common conductive framework, while 10 wt% synthesised
carbon was used as the functional porous host. Since the KJ600
content is kept constant across all cathodes, the performance
diﬀerences discussed below can be mainly associated with the
activation route and resulting pore architecture of the syn-
thesised carbon fraction.
SEM images of the resulting composite cathodes (Fig. 3a)
reveal no observable morphological diﬀerences, indicating that
sulfur inltration and electrode processing produce compa-
rable electrode structures across all samples. This observation
conrms that the variations induced by the activation process
are primarily microscopic in nature, rather than structural at
the electrode scale. In addition, contact angle measurements
using the conventional DME/DOL electrolyte (Fig. S2) show no
signicant diﬀerences among the cathodes, indicating that
cathode wettability is comparable across all samples.
The electrochemical performance of these electrodes was
subsequently evaluated in Li–S cells using a conventional DME/
DOL electrolyte at C/10. The cell performance reveals a clear
correlation between pore architecture and cycling stability. The
ResFA-CO2-12h cathode delivers cycling performance compa-
rable to that of the chemically activated ResFA-KOH reference,
supporting that CO2 activation can reproduce the electro-
chemically relevant pore structure when appropriately tuned. In
contrast, the ResFA-CO2-20h cathode exhibits faster capacity
decay, in agreement with its lower LiPS retention ability. The
ResFA-CO2-5h cathode, limited by insuﬃcient pore develop-
ment and restricted electrolyte accessibility due to its dominant
microporosity, also shows inferior electrochemical perfor-

transport and possible pore blockage during cycling, which
limits eﬀective sulfur utilization.

structure and interphase formation. The reference SSE formu-

previous work31 and consists of 1.5M of LiTFSI dissolved in
a mixture of DOL and a uorinated ether co-solvent (OCTO).

SEM analysis of the recovered electrodes (Fig. S16) further
supports this interpretation. The DME/DOL pouch cell exhibits
a more heterogeneous lithium-metal surface and stronger
cathode surface densication/deposit accumulation, in agree-
ment with coupled polysulde shuttle, electrolyte contamina-

surface and the cathode morphology, with no evidence of
catastrophic carbon–host collapse. To further assess the
cathode contribution, recovered sulfur cathodes were reas-
sembled into coin cells using fresh electrolyte and fresh lithium
metal. These reconstructed cells recovered the practical cathode
capacity (Fig. S17), demonstrating that the sulfur/carbon
cathode remains electrochemically active aer pouch-cell
cycling and that severe irreversible active-material loss is not
the dominant degradation pathway.
Liquid 19F NMR analysis of electrolyte species extracted from
the recovered separator was performed to evaluate the residual
electrolyte composition and compare it with that of the pristine
electrolyte. As shown in Fig. S18, the TFSI−signal remains
detectable aer cycling at −78 ppm. Together with the visual
evidence of retained wetting discussed above, this indicates that
neither complete electrolyte dry-out nor full consumption of the
main LiTFSI salt is the primary cause of the observed capacity
decay. In contrast, the FSI−signal at 53 ppm decreases mark-
edly, consistent with preferential FSI−participation in the
formation and maintenance of an anion-derived SEI on lithium
metal. This does not contradict the benecial role of LiFSI;
rather, it supports its function as an active interphase-
regulating additive. Since high LiFSI contents may promote
undesired side reactions in S8-based Li–S cells using sparingly
solvating electrolytes, LiFSI is used here as a nite additive
while LiTFSI remains the main supporting salt. Under lean-
electrolyte pouch-cell conditions, progressive consumption of
this nite LiFSI reservoir can reduce the ability of the electrolyte
to maintain the most favourable SEI chemistry, leading to SEI
thickening, increased lithium interfacial polarization and
gradual capacity decay.
Overall, these post-mortem results indicate that the capacity
fading of the bilayer pouch cell is mainly associated with
progressive
exhaustion
of
the
LiFSI-enabled
interphase-
regulation capability at the lithium metal electrode. Complete
electrolyte dry-out, full LiTFSI consumption, catastrophic
carbon–host collapse, and severe irreversible sulfur loss can be
largely excluded as primary failure mechanisms.
The combined experimental ndings are schematically
summarized in Fig. 7, illustrating how electrolyte solvation
chemistry and electrochemical protocol jointly regulate lithium
deposition dynamics in Li–S cells. In conventional ether-based
electrolytes, the combination of an unfavourable solvation
structure and high LiPS solubility leads to unstable interfacial
conditions at the LMA, promoting dendritic lithium growth,
dead lithium formation, and rapid capacity decay. Trans-
itioning to SSEs fundamentally alters this behaviour by
restricting polysulde solubility and modifying the Li+ solvation
environment, thereby stabilizing the interphase and suppress-
ing uncontrolled lithium growth. The incorporation of LiFSI
further reinforces this eﬀect by promoting the formation of
a more robust SEI, enabling smoother and more homogeneous
lithium deposition. However, electrolyte chemistry alone does
not fully eliminate degradation pathways associated with
repeated lithium plating and stripping. Introducing an asym-
metric cycling protocol provides an additional kinetic control
over lithium morphology evolution, whereby high-surface-area
lithium structures formed during plating are preferentially
removed during stripping. As a result, this combined strategy,
integrating pore-optimized carbon hosts, tailored solvation
chemistry, and controlled cycling conditions, results in the
most uniform lithium deposition and the longest cycling
stability achieved in this work.

Conclusions
Advancing LSBs towards practical implementation requires
a
coordinated,
system-level
design
that
simultaneously
addresses electrode architecture, electrolyte chemistry, and
operational conditions. This work demonstrates that inte-
grating these dimensions is essential to overcoming the trade-
oﬀs between cost, stability, and electrochemical performance
that have historically limited Li–S technology.
CO2 physical activation is demonstrated as a versatile, scal-
able, and cost-eﬀective alternative to conventional chemical
activation for the synthesis of porous carbon hosts. By tuning
the activation conditions, an optimal pore architecture is ach-
ieved in which a balanced contribution of microporosity and
mesoporosity governs sulfur utilization and polysulde reten-
tion. The ResFA-CO2-12h material reproduces the electro-
chemically relevant structure of chemically activated carbons
while signicantly simplifying synthesis. These results high-
light that carbon host performance is dictated not by surface
area alone, but by the interplay between connement and
transport within the pore network.
Electrolyte solvation chemistry further emerges as a critical
factor in stabilizing Li–S operation. SSEs suppress polysulde
dissolution and maintain stable transport properties under
realistic conditions. The incorporation of LiFSI promotes the
formation of a robust and homogeneous interphase, enabling
uniform lithium deposition and sustained cycling stability,
whereas LiNO3 provides only transient benets due to its
progressive consumption.
Finally, the electrochemical cycling protocol is identied as
a complementary design parameter. The introduction of an
asymmetric cycling strategy enhances cycle life by mitigating
the accumulation of high-surface-area lithium and reducing
interfacial degradation. When combined with optimized SSE
formulations, stable cycling over z180 cycles is achieved under
practical sulfur loading, more than doubling the lifetime
observed under conventional conditions.
Collectively, these ndings demonstrate that durable Li–S
performance emerges from the synergistic optimization of pore
architecture, solvation chemistry, and lithium deposition
kinetics, providing a practical pathway toward high-energy,
resource-eﬃcient battery systems.
