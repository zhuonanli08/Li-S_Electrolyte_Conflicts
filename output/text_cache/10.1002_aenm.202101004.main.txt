but cannot eliminate the dissolution of LiPSs in ether solvents 
from a thermodynamic point of view.[7]
Enabling a solid-phase conversion mechanism of sulfur could 
help avoid the production of soluble intermediate LiPSs. Several 
studies have demonstrated that carbonate-based Li–S batteries 
using sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (S@pPAN) or microporous-
carbon/small-sulfur cathodes show enhanced safety and stable 
cycling performance by eliminating the production of LiPSs.[8] 
The carbonate-based electrolytes possess high ionic conduc-
tivity, wide electrochemical window, high volatilization tem-
perature, are commercially mature, and have wide application, 
showing great promise for replacing ether-based electrolytes 
in Li–S batteries.[9] Unfortunately, most sulfur cathodes are 
chemically incompatible with carbonate-based solvents due to 
the irreversible nucleophilic attack at carbonyl carbon atoms by 
LiPSs. However, some sulfur cathodes can be paired with car-
bonate electrolytes if the cathodes have small sulfur molecules 
confined within a microporous structure or strongly chemi-
cally bonded to a polymeric host, which will limit the sulfur 
mass content (<50 wt%) and areal loading in cathodes, thereby 
compromising energy density.[10] A molecular layer deposition 
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have attracted significant attention because of 
their high theoretical energy density and low cost. However, their poor cycla-
bility caused by the shuttle effect in ether-based electrolytes remains a great 
challenge for their practical application. Herein, a novel electrolyte is proposed 
by combining widely used carbonate solvents diethyl carbonate/fluoroethylene 
carbonate and inert diluent 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl 
ether for Li–S batteries based on typical mesoporous carbon/sulfur (KB/S) 
materials. Differing from the conventional dissolution-precipitation mecha-
nism, the sulfur cathodes demonstrate a solid-phase reaction route in the 
developed electrolyte, which is realized with the assistance of an in situ formed 
compact cathode electrolyte interface (CEI) film on the cathode caused by 
the nucleophilic reaction between lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and carbonate 
solvents. The formed CEI film can effectively block the infiltration of carbonate 
solvents and can completely suppress the generation of LiPSs, thus eliminating 
the shuttle effect. As a result, the KB/S electrode demonstrates a stable cycling 
performance at 2 C by maintaining a discharge capacity of 570 mAh g–1 after 
600 cycles, corresponding to an average capacity decay of 0.057% per cycle. 
More significantly, this strategy provides a new pathway toward future develop-
ment of Li–S batteries based on solid-phase conversion.

our group and demonstrated effective suppression of the nucle-

cost of the strategy are serious challenges for commercial 
applications. Electrolyte modification, such as using additives 
or tuning electrolyte structure, has been proved to be an effec-

cathode compared with the small sulfur molecules strategies. 
However, the high viscosity and low ionic conductivity of high 
concentration electrolytes (HCEs) are not conducive for smooth 
electrochemical reactions, which causes poor rate performance. 
Furthermore, the high cost of using large amounts of lithium 
salt is also a major concern for commercialization. Therefore, 
developing novel carbonate-based electrolyte systems for con-
ventional sulfur cathodes is vital for practical applications.
Very recently, reports have focused on localized HCEs 
(LHCEs) which are synthesized by introducing an inert diluent 
into the HCEs. The diluent, with a low donor number and 
dielectric constant, will not coordinate with the HCE’s cations. 
LHCEs thus maintain pristine solvate characteristics in HCEs, 
and achieve improved ionic conductivity and wettability but 
reduced viscosity.[14] Moreover, solvents are strongly coordi-
nated with ions in the LHCE system, leaving few free solvents 
in the electrolyte. The reduced activity of coordinated solvents 
can suppress the continuous decomposition of solvents on the 
anode’s surface.[15] Such LHCE systems based on carbonate sol-
vents and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether 
(TTE) diluent have been successfully used to achieve stable 
Li/Na stripping/plating and improved cycling performance 
of Li/Na-ion batteries.[14b,c] In addition, coordinated ether sol-
vents or the acetonitrile demonstrate weak interactions with 
LiPSs, effectively suppressing their dissolution and the shuttle 
effect.[16] However, due to the nature of most employed ether 
solvents, only very few research with LHCE can truly eliminate 
the polysulfide dissolution and force a solid-phase conversion. 
Therefore, the concept of carbonate-based LHCE with unique 
properties is worth developing to substitute the solid-liquid-
solid conversion pathway by solid-phase conversion pathway.
Herein, we propose a novel carbonate-based LHCE system 
for Li–S batteries. It has been demonstrated that the reduced 
activity of free solvents could change the discharge/charge 
mechanism of sulfur from the conventional liquid-solid disso-
lution-precipitation dual-phase route to a more favorable solid-
phase conversion route. A cathode electrolyte interface (CEI) 
film is formed on the sulfur electrode due to the nucleophilic 
reaction between LiPSs and carbonate solvents during the first 
cycle. The formed CEI film physically partitions carbonate sol-
vents from the sulfur cathode and thus suppresses the disso-
lution of LiPSs as shown in Scheme 1. The developed electro-
lyte supports conventional carbon-sulfur (KB/S) cathodes and 
demonstrates a high rate performance and stable cycling per-
formance at 2 C with a discharge capacity of 570 mAh g–1 after 
600 cycles. Most meaningfully, it breaks the limitation of car-
bonate-based solvents being restricted to use in organic sulfide 
and small molecule sulfur as reported previously, providing a 
new insight for future development of electrochemically stable 
cathodes for new generation advanced Li–S batteries.

The microstructure of the electrode material and the com-
position of the electrode surface were analyzed to further 
understand the reaction mechanism. The microstructure of the 
electrodes during the discharge/charge process was observed 
by scanning electron microscope (SEM) and Energy-dispersive 
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). In Figure S9a, Supporting Informa-
tion, the SEM image demonstrates that the diameter of pristine 
KB/S electrode ranges between 70 and 90 nm. During the dis-
charge/charge progress, the electrode maintains a smooth sur-
face morphology (Figure S9b–e, Supporting Information). EDS 
element analysis shows that the content of F and O elements 
is relatively high in addition to the large amount of S in the 
electrode (Figure S9f, Supporting Information). Moreover, ele-
ments distribute uniformly as shown in the element mapping 
pictures (Figure S9g, Supporting Information). In the trans-
mission electron microscope (TEM) image, an amorphous CEI 
film with thickness of 4  nm can be observed (Figure S10a,b, 
Supporting Information). The KB/S particles in the bulk show 
nanocrystals of S. This observation suggests that a CEI film is 
formed on the KB/S particles surface.
In order to further explore the conversion mechanism and 
the chemical composition of the CEI film, X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) analyses for electrodes at different discharge 
states were carried out. In the XPS full spectrum in Figure S11, 
Supporting Information, it is observed that Li and F signals arise 
on the electrode at discharge status. In addition, the Na signal 
which comes from the sodium alginate binder disappears, which 
may be the result of being covered by the discharging product and 
CEI film. The high resolution XPS spectrums of S 2p and C 1s 
at different discharge states are shown in Figure 4b,c. In the XPS 
spectrum of S 2p (Figure 4b), the S 2p 3/2 at the binding energies 
of 164.4 eV is attributed to S-S in S8.[28] The binding energy moves 
to a lower energy with the discharge progress, indicating the 
reduction of sulfur. It reveals three environments of sulfur at dis-
charge status B. In addition to the signal of SS bond, two other 
S 2p 3/2 peaks appear at the binding energy of 163.3 and 161.7 eV 
corresponding to CS and Li2S2, respectively.[8a,28] During the 
subsequent discharge process, the signal of the SS bond van-
ishes with an increase of Li2S2 and the emergence of Li2S at 
160.1 eV.[28] S8 is converted into Li2S2 and Li2S at the end of 
discharge accompanied by some compounds with CS. In C 1s 
spectrum of the pristine cathode, the fitting peaks are assigned 
to the CC (284.8 eV), COC (287.0 eV), and CO (288.7 eV), 
respectively (Figure  4c).[29] With continued discharge, signals 
at 286.3 and 289.8 eV appear, contributed to CS and Li2CO3. 
These signals occupy a high ratio in area.[12,30] In accordance with 
C 1s XPS spectra, the Li 1s spectra in Figure S12a, Supporting 
Information shows components of Li2CO3 (55.4  eV), ROCO2Li 
(54.6 eV), and LiF (56.2 eV).[31] In F 1s spectra, the peak at 685 eV 
is assigned to LiF and attributes to the decomposition of the TFSI– 
anion[31] (Figure S12b, Supporting Information). The Fourier 
transform infrared spectroscopy spectrum of the cathode after 
one cycle also shows distinct absorption peak of COO–, OLi, 
CC, and thiocarbonate which derives from the nucleophilic 
reaction between LiPSs and carbonate solvent (Figure S13, 
Supporting Information). This also leads to a large irreversible 
capacity during the first cycle. Time-dependent electrochemical 
impedance spectroscopy of the Li–S cell is shown in Figure S14a, 
Supporting Information. The Nyquist plots exhibits two semi-
circles at high-frequency after 10 cycles, and it is well fitted by 
the equivalent circuit shown in Figure S14b, Supporting Infor-
mation inset. It indicates that the CEI film forms and becomes 
stable after few cycles. These analyses demonstrate that the solid-
phase conversion of the KB/S cathode in the carbonate-based 
LHCE is realized by forming a CEI film on the electrode surface 
which is composed of insoluble inorganics (LiCO3 and LiF) and 
thiocarbonate.
The composition and distribution of CEI film were inves-
tigated by the time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry 
(TOF-SIMS). As shown in Figure S15, Supporting Information, 
secondary ions such as OCS–, LiS–, CS–, and LiF– are collected. 
The OCS–, LiS–, and CS– are derived from the thiocarbonate in 
the CEI film which is formed through the nucleophilic reaction 
between LiPSs and carbonate solvent, and the LiF produces 
the LiF– signal. The intensity of OCS–, LiS–, and CS– increases 
with the sputtering time and becomes stable gradually, indi-
cating the CEI film distributes in the electrode uniformly. The 
intensity of the LiF– reaches a maximum within 14 s and then 
decreases, suggesting a LiF-rich CEI film is formed on the elec-
trode surface. This stable surface film can effectively prevent 
the contact of the electrolyte with the inner KB/S particles and 
ensure an unblocked transport pathway for lithium ions, con-

To further probe the potential application of the developed 
electrolyte in Li–S batteries, high S loading cathode with 3.1 
and 7.2 mg cm–2 were also prepared and their electrochemical 
performance was investigated. As shown in Figure S20, Sup-
porting Information, solid-phase conversion reaction still can 
be realized in the increased S loading cathode by using the pro-
posed electrolyte. The cathode with S loading of 3.1 mg cm–2 
exhibits a stable discharge capacity of 480 mAh g–1 at 0.4 C until 
100 cycles. Increasing the S loading to 7.2 mg cm–2, the cathode 
only can deliver 552 mAh g–1 at 0.2 C in the second discharge 
process and the capacity decays gradually with cycling. The 
low active material utilization and the rapid capacity decay in 
high loading Li–S cell are mainly caused by sluggish electron 
and ion transportation in the thick electrode and the damage 
of CEI film resulted from the volume change. Meaningfully, 
the realization of solid-phase reaction in high loading sulfur 
cathode demonstrates the potential application of the proposed 
localized high concentration carbonate-based electrolyte in 
Li–S batteries. A low electrolyte/sulfur ratio (E/S, µL mg–1) is 
another key factor to achieve high energy density Li–S batteries. 
As shown in Figure S20e, Supporting Information, the cells 
with different E/S exhibit the same discharge/charge curves, 
indicating the reaction mechanism will not be changed under 
different E/S. The cell shows good cycling stability with a high 
E/S ratio over 8  µL mg–1 (Figure S20f, Supporting Informa-
tion). Although the specific capacity decreases with cycling after 
30 cycles under a low E/S of 6 µL mg–1, the realization of the 
solid-phase reaction indicates that the active material utiliza-
tion is less dependent on the amount of electrolyte compared 
with the ether-based electrolytes which support a solid-liquid-
solid reactions route, which is conducive to realize high energy 
density Li–S batteries. In the future, the deep understanding of 
the reaction mechanism could provide important guidance for 
improving the performance of batteries.
Stable Li plating/stripping performance is critical for long 
cycle life Li–S batteries. Li/Li symmetric cells using different 
electrolytes were assembled to investigate the polarization and 
cycling stability. Figure 6a shows the cycling stability of Li/Li sym-
metric cells using the HCE and different LHCEs at 0.5 mA cm–2 
with a capacity of 1 mAh cm–2. It can be seen that all the cell 
shows a stable cycling performance for 350 h except that using 
a dilute electrolyte which exhibits continuously increasing 
overvoltage with cycling. In the HCE, the voltage polariza-
tion is over 100 mV which is higher than that of 60 mV in the 
LHCE due to the large viscosity and sluggish ion transport 
(Figure  6b). Increasing the current density to 1  mA cm–2, 
the cell using LHCE still exhibits stable plating/stripping prop-
erty for 270 h (Figure 6c). The morphology of the Li deposition 
at 0.5 mA cm–2 and capacity of 1 mAh cm–2 was observed by 
SEM. As shown in Figure 6d, Li dendrites are observed on the 
electrode surface when using the dilute electrolyte which is 
hard to establish a stable SEI film and increase the risk of short 
circuit. In the HCE and LHCE, large nodule-like Li particles 
(≈5  µm) form (Figure  6e–g). The reduced surface area of the 
large particles decreases side reactions between the electrolyte 
and the Li metal. The carbonate-based LHCE thus is conducive 
to long cycling performance Li–S batteries owing to the proper-
ties to facilitate a solid-phase route of S and to stabilize the Li 
anode.
3. Conclusion
In summary, a carbonated-based LHCE, which dissolves LiTFSI 
in the mixing solvents of DEC/FEC/TTE, is developed for Li–S 
batteries to eliminate the shuttle effect of LiPSs. The devel-
oped electrolyte has low solvent reactivity and successfully sup-
presses the formation of LiPSs. In particular, limited LiPSs are 
formed at beginning of the discharge process, and the nucleo-
philic reaction with carbonate solvents facilitates a CEI film on 
the sulfur cathode, which suppresses the continued formation 
of LiPSs and forces the Li–S transition to proceed in the solid-
phase. Compared with the typical carbonate electrolyte, the pro-
posed electrolyte with unique solvate structure realizes stable 
cycling of traditional sulfur electrodes. By using a simple KB/S 
electrode, excellent cycling performance with a high remainder 
capacity of 570 mAh g–1 after 600 cycles at 2 C and more than 
99% Coulombic efficiency is obtained, highlighting the sig-
nificant potential of the electrolyte for high performance Li–S 
batteries.
