Tuning the Solvation Structure of a Weakly Solvating Cyclic
Ether Electrolyte for Wide-Temperature Cycling of
Lithium-Sulfurized Polyacrylonitrile Batteries
Kameron Liao, Min-Hao Pai, and Arumugam Manthiram*
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) cathodes in high energy-density Li-metal
batteries have garnered widespread interest owing to their good cycling
stability and moderately high capacities. However, their application is
hindered by the low prevalence of advanced electrolytes that can
simultaneously mitigate polysulﬁde generation at the cathode and stabilize
the Li-metal anode. Here, a weakly solvating electrolyte is presented,
employing a single solvent tetrahydropyran (THP). The solvation structure is
eﬀectively tuned by adjusting the salt concentration to stabilize both the
Li-metal anode and SPAN cathode. This approach enables stable cycling with
high SPAN loadings (≈5 mg cm−2) and lean electrolyte contents (≈5 μL
mgSPAN
−1) across a wide temperature range: 0 °C, room temperature, and
50 °C. A pouch cell with a high SPAN loading and a low electrolyte-to-SPAN
(E/SPAN) ratio of 3 μL mg−1 shows a stable 79.1% capacity retention after 40
cycles. Additionally, THP can be eﬀectively employed in localized
high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) systems to reduce the
diluent-to-solvent ratio for greater LHCE viability. The study demonstrates the
potential of weakly solvating solvents in Li-SPAN batteries, oﬀering a pathway
for their practical application.
1. Introduction
The development of next-generation energy storage technologies
will be pivotal for mitigating the climate crisis. Although current
Li-ion technologies are very mature and have been successfully
commercialized, their energy densities remain insuﬃcient for
future energy needs. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are a promis-
ing future pathway, as sulfur is employed as the cathode ma-
terial and has a high theoretical gravimetric capacity (1672 mA
h g−1).[1,2] This oﬀers a promising pathway to achieve a prac-
tical energy density of 500 W h kg−1. Furthermore, sulfur has
the added beneﬁts of being naturally abundant, inexpensive, and

There has been signiﬁcant research

Many electrolytes have been developed to stabilize the Li-SPAN
system; their beneﬁts and detriments are summarized. Carbon-
ate electrolytes have been commonly employed because they can
generate a beneﬁcial cathode-electrolyte interface (CEI), which
passivates the SPAN cathode, preventing the generation of sol-
uble LiPSs.[15–17] Although carbonate electrolytes can eﬀectively
stabilize the cathode, they have poor lithium metal compatibility,
leading to signiﬁcant lithium anode degradation and limiting its
long-term cyclability. Ether-based electrolytes, meanwhile, have
superior lithium-metal compatibility and can enhance the for-
mation of a more favorable inorganic-derived solid-electrolyte in-
terface (SEI). Conventional ethers, such as 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), can produce a moderately stable SEI on Li metal, but
cannot produce as eﬀective CEIs as compared to carbonate
electrolytes.[18,19] DME, in particular, has been shown to facilitate
the formation of soluble LiPSs, re-introducing the detrimental
problem of the basic Li-S system. These eﬀects are exacerbated
when utilizing higher sulfur contents in the SPAN and higher
SPAN loadings, leading to poor cyclability.

has been achieved by utilizing LHCEs, the use of ﬂuorinated
diluents may not be practical; they are not very environmentally

Unfortunately, most LHCE systems employ such ﬂuorinated

their viability. Other promising electrolytes include locally con-

anions lead to the formation of a favorable CEI, enabling a
solid-solid conversion mechanism and good cycling stability.[23]
However, they are also typically expensive and have higher
densities.
Instead, we opt here to design a single-solvent electrolyte that
can stabilize the Li-SPAN system without requiring such ionic
liquids or diluents. We showcase tetrahydropyran (THP) as a
weakly solvating ether solvent with favorable properties, such as
a melting point of −45 °C and a boiling point of 88 °C. By employ-
ing THP and varying the salt concentration, the solvation struc-
ture is eﬀectively tuned to stabilize the Li-metal anode (through
a more inorganic-rich SEI) and the SPAN cathode (by mitigating
polysulﬁde generation). Thus, the optimized THP electrolyte en-
ables stable cyclability with high SPAN loadings (≈5 mg cm−2)
and lean electrolyte contents (≈5 μL mgSPAN
−1) over wide tem-
perature ranges: 0 °C, room temperature, and 50 °C. Addition-
ally, pouch cells are constructed with a low E/SPAN ratio of 3 μL
mgSPAN
−1, enabling capacity retention of 79.1% after 40 cycles.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that THP can act as an eﬀective
primary solvent in a generic LHCE system, reducing the DSR to
make an LHCE system more viable. This work demonstrates the
great versatility of a weakly solvating electrolyte in the Li-SPAN
system and also highlights the key degradation mechanisms in
various electrolyte systems.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Properties and Solvation Structure of Weakly Solvating THP
Figure 1a reveals the material design rationale for the selection
of THP for a weakly solvating electrolyte. Compared to a conven-
tional ether solvent such as DME, which exhibits bidentate solva-
tion, THP features monodentate solvation, thereby reducing its
ability to chelate a Li+ ion. Furthermore, the bulky structure of the
6-membered ring provides eﬀective steric hindrance, limiting the
access of the O atom to solvate a Li+ ion. Theoretical density func-
tion theory (DFT) calculations support this design principle. The
surface electrostatic potential (ESP) reveals the electron density
distribution over the solvent molecules (Figure 1b). The two oxy-
gen atoms on the DME produce a very negative ESP, which en-
ables it to coordinate with a Li+ ion, eﬀectively solvating the salt.
The single oxygen atom on the THP similarly has a negative ESP
that can coordinate with a Li+ ion. However, owing to its struc-
tural properties, the THP solvent binds more weakly to a Li+ ion
than the DME solvent. The binding energy values of the Li+ ion
to THP and DME are, respectively, −44.8 and −66.5 kcal mol−1
(Figure 1c).
Upon designing the weakly solvating THP electrolyte, its poly-
sulﬁde dissolution ability was evaluated, an important factor
that aﬀects the SPAN stability when cycling. For a comparison,
DME was utilized as a conventional ether solvent. Li2S8 was
mixed into the DME or THP solvents at a 2 m concentration

1 m THP electrolyte was determined to be 3.28 mS cm−1 com-
pared to 16.48 mS cm−1 for a 1 m DME electrolyte (Figure 2b;

Although the resulting conductivity is lower, a less dissociated
salt is more favorable for a more inorganic-derived SEI and im-
proved Li metal stability. The conductivity of the THP electrolyte
peaks at a 2 m concentration and decreases with further increas-
ing salt content. It is noted at a 4 m concentration in THP, the
electrolyte still has a suﬃcient conductivity of 1.66 mS cm−1,
which decreases to below 1 mS cm−1 at higher concentrations.
The solvation structures of the THP electrolytes were probed
with Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, Raman
spectroscopy, and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The FTIR
spectrum of the S─N─S vibrational mode in LiFSI reveals that
the solvation structure in a 1 m DME electrolyte is dominated by
free FSI– anions and solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) with re-
spective peaks at ≈723 and ≈740 cm−1 (Figure 2c). In contrast,
the solvation structure of a 1 m THP electrolyte is predominantly
contact-ion pairs (CIPs) (≈755 cm−1) with some minor formation
of salt aggregates (AGGs) (≈770 cm−1).[24,25] Complimentary Ra-
man spectroscopy data support these results, showing no AGGs
in a 1 m DME electrolyte, whereas they compose 9.9% of the sol-
vation structure in a 1 m THP electrolyte (Figure S2, Supporting
Information). By increasing the concentration of LiFSI in THP,
the solvation structure was tuned to more AGGs, which favors
the formation of more stable inorganic-derived SEIs.[26,27] The
C─O─C vibrational mode in the FTIR spectrum for the DME
and THP conﬁrms the solvation of the LiFSI salt by the oxygen
atom in each solvent. In the neat solvents, the free C─O─C bond
is found at ≈1105 and ≈1090 cm−1, respectively, for DME and
THP (Figure 2d). With the addition of salt, a new peak appears at
≈1079 cm−1, revealing the solvation of Li+ by the oxygen atom.
At a 1 m LiFSI concentration, there is a nearly equal ratio of sol-
vated and free solvents for both DME and THP. When the salt
concentration in THP reaches 4 m and beyond, minimal solvent
remains free, further suppressing the ability of THP to solvate
LiPSs. Considering these FTIR and conductivity results, a 4 m
THP electrolyte was chosen for further investigation, having the
ideal balance between adequate ionic conductivity and optimal
solvation structure. Furthermore, a 4 m THP electrolyte main-
tains an adequate viscosity of 77.60 mPa s (Figure S3, Support-
ing Information). 1 m DME and 1 m THP electrolytes were also
evaluated for comparison.
To further understand the solvation structure of the elec-
trolytes, 7Li, 19F, and 1H NMR spectroscopy was employed. In the
7Li NMR spectra, the peak shifts downﬁeld to −1.34 ppm in the
1 m THP electrolyte compared to −1.59 ppm in the 1 m DME elec-
trolyte (Figure 2e). In THP, the lone pair electrons of the oxygen
atom shield the Li+ ion less, verifying the weakly solvating na-
ture of THP. By increasing the salt concentration from 1 to 4 m,
the peak shifts back upﬁeld to −1.49 ppm, indicating that more
solvent is replaced with FSI– in the solvation sheath, resulting in
more electron shielding. The peaks in the 19F spectra shift further
upﬁeld from 51.58 ppm in 1 m DME to 51.39 ppm in both 1 and
4 m THP (Figure 2f). The greater shielding of the F atom on the
FSI– anion indicates stronger cation-anion pairing and weaker
anion interactions with the protons in the solvent molecule.[28,29]
The 19F chemical shift of the 1 and 4 m THP electrolytes are the
same, revealing that although the frequency of solvent-anion in-
teractions may increase in the 4 m THP electrolyte, the strength of
the interactions in a weakly solvating electrolyte does not change
with greater salt concentrations.
The 1H NMR spectra provide further insight into the nature
of the solvent-anion interactions in the DME and THP systems.
Owing to the strong solvation of a Li+ ion by DME, there is
a substantial inductive eﬀect that polarizes the neighboring H
atom, resulting in interactions with anion; this results in a greater
electron shielding of the H atom and an upﬁeld chemical shift
(Figure S4a, Supporting Information).[29] In contrast, THP is so
weakly solvating that the Li+ ion solvation does not create an in-
ductive eﬀect in the H environments adjacent to the O atom (la-
beled as environment a); the 1H chemical shift of these atoms
remains at 3.42 ppm for both the THP electrolytes and neat THP
(Figure 2g; Figure S4b, Supporting Information). Interestingly,
the H atoms farthest away from the O atom (labeled as environ-
ment c) experience the greatest chemical shift when LiFSI salt is
added. When suﬃciently far from the electron-rich O atom, the
FSI– anion can still weakly interact with the unpolarized protons
in environment c.
Finally, 2D diﬀusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) 1H NMR
was employed to probe the self-diﬀusivity of the solvent
(Figure 2h,i). As pure solvents, THP and DME have self-diﬀusion
coeﬃcients of, respectively, 1.49 × 10−9 and 2.98 × 10−9 m2 s−1.
With the addition of 1 m LiFSI salt, the self-diﬀusivity of THP
decreases by 71.5% to 4.01 × 10−10 m2 s−1 compared to a 64.1%
decrease to 1.07 × 10−9 m2 s−1 for DME. This larger decrease in
self-diﬀusivity further validates the formation of salt clusters in
the THP electrolyte.[30,31] In all, the interactions in the DME and
THP electrolytes are graphically summarized in Figure 2j. A 1 m
DME electrolyte leads to strong solvation of the LiFSI and has
strong solvent-anion interactions. In contrast, a 4 m THP elec-
trolyte weakly solvates LiFSI, leading to the formation of AGGs
and weaker solvent-anion interactions. The impact of these bene-
ﬁcial properties is uncovered through the electrochemical testing
of Li-SPAN batteries presented below.

trolytes were further demonstrated at more practical conditions

To elucidate the diﬀerences in cycling performance, cyclic
voltammetry (CV) was employed with a scan rate (𝜈) of
0.1 mV s−1. In the 1 m DME electrolyte, the cathodic and an-
odic peaks for solid-solid redox (1.57 and 2.35 V, respectively) are
less polarized compared to LP57 (1.59 and 2.40 V, respectively)
(Figure 3e). It should be noted that the solid-solid conversion re-
action from SPAN to fully-lithiated SPAN is a complex, multi-
electron transfer process where each electron transfer step occurs
at a diﬀerent voltage. When employing the LP57 and 1 m DME
electrolytes, the conversion kinetics in the intermediate electron-
transfer steps are insuﬃciently fast, thus convoluting the voltage
where each electron-transfer step occurs. This leads to a single
merged solid-solid redox peak. Additional peaks do arise for the
DME electrolyte, but they correspond to solid-liquid transitions
found in a typical sulfur system; strong solvation by DME leads
to detrimental polysulﬁde generation that rapidly degrades the
battery. In contrast, owing to the weakly solvating nature of THP,
no solid-liquid transitions are found. Further, the 1 m THP elec-
trolyte reduces the polarization in the cell, leading to an anodic
peak at 2.32 V and, interestingly, the emergence of two cathodic
peaks at higher voltages (1.94 and 1.75 V, corresponding to a par-
tial and full lithiation of SPAN, respectively). The presence of
two distinct cathodic solid-solid redox peaks at the scan rate of
0.1 mV s−1 highlights the accelerated kinetics of 1 m THP.
The voltage was further scanned between 0.05 and 0.5 mV s−1
to gain further insight into the kinetics (Figure 3f; Figure S8, Sup-
porting Information). Notably, when the scan rate is increased to
0.5 mV s−1, the redox kinetics is no longer suﬃciently fast in the
1 m THP electrolyte, and the peaks merge back into one single
cathodic peak; in contrast, when a 4 m THP electrolyte is em-
ployed, these two peaks are retained even at the higher scan rates,
demonstrating the elevated redox kinetics with a 4 m THP elec-
trolyte compared to the others. Additional insights into the Li+
diﬀusion-limited kinetics can be obtained by plotting the peak
current with respect to 𝜈0.5; according to the Randles-Ševˇcík equa-
tion, the magnitude of the slope is positively correlated to the
diﬀusion kinetics.[33,34] A 1 m THP electrolyte exhibits the most
favorable reduction kinetics with a slope of −8.73 compared to
LP57 and 1 m DME (with respective slopes of −6.08 and −2.13)
(Figure 3g). As the 4 m THP electrolyte retains two cathodic peaks
even up to 0.5 mV s−1, this quantiﬁcation could not be done for
it. The electron-transfer number for two redox peaks is diﬀerent
from a single merged peak found in the other electrolytes, hin-
dering their comparison. The oxidation kinetics is also improved
for the 1 and 4 m THP systems (7.03 and 6.85, respectively), com-
pared to the LP57 and 1 m DME electrolyte kinetics (5.96 and 1.90,
respectively) (Figure 3h).
2.3. Insights to Anode Stabilization
Eﬀective stabilization of the lithium metal is essential for en-
abling the long-term cyclability of Li-SPAN cells. Several tests
were conducted to elucidate the reversibility of the Li-metal plat-
ing and stripping. By assembling Li || Cu cells, the CE of Li plat-
ing and stripping was evaluated at a current of 0.5 mA cm−2, fol-
lowing the Aurbach protocol (Figure 4a).[35] The LP57 electrolyte
demonstrated poor Li-metal stability, failing the test, as the Li
reservoir on the copper foil was depleted before the ﬁnal stripping
step. The 1 m DME electrolyte enables a CE of 93.48%, whereas
the 1 THP and 4 m THP electrolytes achieve higher CEs of, re-
spectively, 98.54% and 99.42%. The CE for the THP electrolytes
at diﬀerent salt concentrations was found to be a maximum at the
4 m concentration, validating the initial selection of a 4 m THP
electrolyte (Figure S9, Supporting Information).
The long-term cyclability of the electrolytes in Li || Li sym-
metric cells at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mA h cm−2 was investi-
gated to showcase their Li-metal stabilization (Figure 4b). The
cells with the LP57 electrolytes failed only after 770 h, showcas-
ing the poor compatibility between carbonate electrolytes and Li
metal. The ether electrolytes show better compatibility with the
Li metal. Owing to its high conductivity, the 1 m DME electrolyte

The composition of the SEI has also been widely known to
modulate the surface reactivity and conductivity of the Li metal,
aﬀecting its long-term cyclability. The decomposition of elec-
trolyte salt and solvent onto the Li-metal surface results in an SEI
composed of organic and inorganic species. After 20 cycles, the
composition of this SEI was evaluated with X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in the C 1s spectra, various organic
species—C─O (≈286.5 eV), C≐O (≈287.9 eV), C─F (≈288.7 eV),
and Li2CO3 (≈289.9 eV)—were detected on the surface of the
SEI, attributed to the decomposition of the organic solvent
(Figure 5a).[36] The LP57 electrolyte resulted in the greatest con-
centration of these organic species, particularly of C─O and
Li2CO3 species. While the 1 m DME reduces the quantities of
these organic species, the THP electrolytes showcase the small-
est quantities of organic species.
An inorganic-derived, ﬂuorine-rich SEI is known to improve
lithium reversibility. Particularly, the decomposition of the elec-
trolyte salt to form Li-F has been widely thought to stabilize
SEI.[37,38] As shown in the F 1s spectra, all the electrolytes gener-
ate Li-F (≈684.6 eV) and C-F (≈688.6 eV) in the SEI, but the 4 m
THP electrolyte leads to the greatest ratio of Li-F to C-F species
(Figure 5b). Even though the electrodes were washed, the spec-
tra also reveal salt trapped in the crevices of the Li metal (S-F
species for LiFSI in the ether electrolytes and P-F species for
LiPF6 in the carbonate electrolyte). Meanwhile, insulating sulfur

the SEI. As shown in the S 2p spectra, even the carbonate LP57

2.4. Insights to Cathode Stabilization
Carbonate electrolytes have been conventionally regarded as ef-
fective for stabilizing the SPAN cathode; they have been known
to decompose into favorable CEIs that insulate the SPAN from
polysulﬁde generation, reducing active material loss and sulfur
deposition onto Li metal. Thus, it is useful to compare the THP
and LP57 electrolytes. Self-discharge tests are an eﬀective way to
evaluate the eﬀectiveness of the CEI; a robust CEI eﬀectively sup-
presses polysulﬁde generation, thereby reducing self-discharge
and active material loss. The CE was measured after discharging
the cell to 1.5 or 2 V, resting it for 48 h, followed by a complete
discharge to 1 V. With an LP57 electrolyte, the cells showed lit-
tle self-discharge with CEs of 99.89% and 99.82% when rested
at, respectively, 1.5 and 2 V (Figure S12a, Supporting Informa-
tion); this showcases the impressive CEI of the LP57 electrolyte.
In contrast, a 1 m DME electrolyte shows severe self-discharge
phenomena, with respective CEs of 97.73% and 86.19% (Figure
S12b, Supporting Information). There is severe polysulﬁde gen-
eration and active material loss in this conventional ether elec-
trolyte. In the weakly solvating THP electrolytes, negligible self-
discharge was observed, showcasing the eﬀectiveness of the CEIs
formed by the THP electrolytes (Figure 6a; Figure S12c, Support-
ing Information). Interestingly, the CEs were even greater than
100%. It has been found that resting Li-metal cells leads to a dis-
solution of residual SEI shells, resulting in some recovery of dead
Li.[41] Similarly, there may be some residual CEI shells containing
dead SPAN, which the THP electrolyte may eﬀectively dissolve
after resting; capacity could be recovered, causing the CE to ex-
ceed 100%.

fects the redox kinetics and polarization of the cell. Cathodes

The surface compositions of the SPAN cathodes in the charged

of organic solvents leads to some deposition of organic species
on the SPAN cathode. The SPAN cathodes cycled in LP57 show-

among the ethers, the quantity of these species is smallest for

observations (Figure S14, Supporting Information). When cycled
in 1 m DME, many particles are covered with smooth ﬁlms, which
may be from the deposition of Li2S after liquid-solid conversion.
Meanwhile, the cathodes cycled in the LP57 and THP electrolytes
appear very similar, maintaining their particle integrity. In all, the
4 m THP electrolyte can eﬀectively stabilize the SPAN cathode
similar to a carbonate LP57 electrolyte, with enhanced Li-ion dif-
fusivity.
2.5. Versatile Cyclability of THP
As the 4 m THP electrolyte has been shown to eﬀectively stabi-
lize the Li-metal anode and SPAN cathode, its multi-faceted capa-
bilities are further highlighted. THP has a low melting point of
−45 °C and a high boiling point of 88 °C, and can adequately
stabilize the Li metal at wide temperatures (Figures S15 and
S16, Supporting Information); this allows for the versatile cycla-
bility of Li-SPAN cells under low- and high-temperature condi-
tions even with a high SPAN loading of 4.5 mg cm−2. At low
temperatures, the anode suﬀers from more inhomogeneous de-
position, Li corrosion, and dendrite formation, leading to cell
degradation.[50,51] Still, at a low temperature of 0 °C and a rate
of C/5, the 4 m THP electrolyte enables capacity retention of
53.6% after 150 cycles (Figure 7a). In contrast, the 1 m DME
and LP57 electrolytes lead to a rapid capacity fade after, respec-
tively, 25 and 50 cycles. To achieve sub-zero temperature cycling
(as low as −40 °C), additional future studies may be necessary
to investigate the behavior of the THP electrolyte with interme-
diate salt concentrations (Figures S17 and S18, Supporting In-
formation). At high temperatures, the cell is prone to more para-
sitic side reactions with Li, polysulﬁde generation, and electrolyte
evaporation.[52,53] Still, at a high temperature and rate of 50 °C and
C/2, respectively, a capacity of 572 mA h g−1 can be retained after
75 cycles (98.8% capacity retention) with the 4 m THP electrolyte
(Figure 7b). In the 1 m DME and LP57 electrolytes, the cells expe-
rience rapid capacity fade even after 5 and 30 cycles. Formidably,
the cells with the 4 m THP electrolyte maintain an average CEs of
≈100% through its cycling at both low- and high-temperature cy-
cling conditions (Figure S19, Supporting Information); even as
polysulﬁde generation is more favorable at high temperatures,
4 m THP electrolyte showcases negligible self-discharge and poly-
sulﬁde shuttling eﬀects.
A pouch cell (≈24 cm2) was assembled to showcase the 4 m
THP electrolyte under more strict cycling conditions. Even at a
high SPAN loading of 5 mg cm−2 and a lean E/SPAN ratio of 3 μL
mgSPAN

of 532 mA h gSPAN
−1 at a rate of C/10 (Figure 7c). After 40 cycles,
the pouch cell demonstrates stable cyclability, retaining a capacity
of 421 mA h gSPAN
−1 (79.1% retention).

3. Conclusion
In summary, the solvating structure of a single-solvent weakly
solvating THP was tuned to stabilize the SPAN and Li metal in-
terfaces. At the anode, a 4 m THP electrolyte increased the quan-
tity of beneﬁcial Li-F and favored the formation of less insulating
SO3
2−species. At the cathode, the CEI derived from the 4 m THP
electrolyte better insulates the SPAN from the electrolyte com-
pared to a conventional ether, while enhancing the Li-ion diﬀu-
sivity. A THP-based electrolyte is highly versatile, enabling sta-
ble cycling at wide temperature ranges of 0 °C, room tempera-
ture, and 50 °C at high SPAN loadings of ≈5 mg cm−2. Single-
layer pouch cells were demonstrated with the same loading and
a low E/SPAN ratio of 3 μL mg−1. Additionally, THP was further
demonstrated as an eﬀective solvent for an LHCE system, show-
casing its promise, as alternative low-cost, non-ﬂuorinated dilu-
ents are developed. In all, this study showcases a rational design
principle to achieve stable, practical lithium SPAN batteries.
4. Experimental Section
Electrolyte Preparation:
All electrolytes were prepared and stored in-
side an argon-ﬁlled glovebox. The LP57 electrolyte (Gotion) was used as
purchased. The LP57 electrolyte was composed of 1 m LiPF6 in ethylene
carbonate (EC) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) in a 3:7 weight ratio. 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich), tetrahydropyran (THP, Sigma-
Aldrich), and 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (TTE,
SynQuest) were dried in 4 Å molecular sieves for at least 3 days before elec-
trolyte preparation. Lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, Arkema) was
added to DME and THP in various concentrations (as speciﬁed), where m
represents molLiFSI per Lsolvent. In the LHCE systems, a 2.2 m concentra-
tion of LiFSI was employed. The diluent-to-solvent (DSR) ratio was deﬁned
as the volume ratio of the diluent to solvent. The 2 m Li2S8 solution was
prepared by mixing Li2S and S in a 1: 7 molar ratio in DME or THP solvent.
Polysulﬁde generation was observed after resting at room temperature for
48 h.
SPAN Cathode Preparation:
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) mate-
rial was obtained from the Adeka Corporation with a speciﬁed sulfur con-
tent of ≈46%. A slurry consisting of 80% SPAN, 10% Super C65 carbon
black (Imerys), and 10% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Nippon Paper,
MAC350LC) was mixed in a planetary Thinky mixer and doctor-blade cast
onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil. The casted foil was dried for 12 h at
50 °C under vacuum, then punched into 12 mm (diameter) cathodes. The
SPAN loadings were controlled to be ≈2 and 5 mg cm−2 for, respectively,
low and high loading assessments.
Electrochemical Testing:
Coin cells were assembled in 2032-typed
cases with the prepared SPAN cathode, a Celgard 2325 separator, a Li
metal chip (thickness of 400 μm), a stainless-steel spacer (thickness of
1 mm), and a spring. All cells were assembled in a glovebox with an ar-
gon atmosphere. The E/SPAN ratio was controlled to be 15 μL mg−1 for
low-loading evaluations and 5–6 μL mg−1 for high-loading assessments,
as stated. All cells were cycled between a voltage window of 1 and 3 V on
a Land or Arbin battery cycler. The cells were initially rested for 8 h and
activated with two cycles at a C/20 rate, before continued cycling at a C/5
or C/2 rate (1C = 600 mA h gSPAN−1). Low (0 °C) and high (50 °C) temper-
ature cycling was conducted in an environmental test chamber with room-
temperature activation cycles. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemi-
cal impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted on a Biologic VMP po-
tentiostat. In the CV tests, the potential was swept between 1 and 3 V
with scan rates between 0.05 and 0.5 mV s−1. For the impedance mea-
surements, Li-SPAN cells were cycled in their corresponding electrolytes
for three cycles to form an adequate SEI and CEI. Then, the cycled cells
were disassembled and reassembled in SPAN || SPAN and Li || Li sym-
metric cells, deconvoluting the cathode and anode impedances. The sym-
metric cells used an LP57 electrolyte to prevent the electrolyte conductiv-
ity from convoluting the impedance of the interfaces. The measured fre-
quency range was between 0.2 Hz and 7 MHz. Li || Cu tests were evaluated
with the Aurbach method. The Cu foil was preconditioned with an initial
deposition and stripping of Li, followed by the plating of a 5 mA h cm−2
Li reservoir. A capacity of 0.5 mA h cm−2 was continuously stripped and
plated from this inventory for ten cycles. Then, the Li was stripped to 1 V.
The CE was determined from the amount of Li recovered and averaged
among the three trials.

Materials Characterization:
The conductivity of the electrolytes was
measured with a Mettler–Toledo SevenCompact S230 conductivity probe.
The densities and dynamic viscosities of the electrolytes were determined
with an Anton Paar Stabinger Viscometer SVM 3001. The standard un-
certainty in density was 0.0001 g cm−3 and the relative uncertainty in
viscosity measurement was 0.35%. The solvation structure of the elec-
trolytes was characterized by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR), Raman,
and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopies. FTIR and Raman
spectra were obtained with, respectively, a Thermo Scientiﬁc Nicolet iS5
FTIR and a Renishaw Raman Spectrometer. NMR spectroscopy evaluation
was conducted with an Agilent 400 MHz NMR system, using a coaxial in-
sert tube for the reference that was placed in a sample tube. Deuterated
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) solvent with 0.1 m LiPF6 was employed as
the reference, utilizing the LiPF6 signal to calibrate the chemical shift. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted with a PHI VersaProbe
4 to analyze the surface species on the CEI and SEI. After cycling for 20
cycles, the Li-SPAN cells were disassembled, the electrodes were rinsed
with solvent, and the samples were transferred to the XPS instrument in
an airtight container. A monochromatic Al K𝛼(1486.6 eV) radiation was
used with no charge neutralization. The samples were sputtered with an
argon ion clustering gun with an energy of 5 kV. The XPS peaks were ﬁt-
ted in CasaXPS software. The adventitious carbon peak was calibrated to
284.8 eV. The morphology of the cathode and anode interfaces were im-
aged with an Apreo 2C LoVac scanning electron microscope (SEM).
Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations:
DFT calculations were
performed using the Gaussian16 software. Ground-state molecular ge-
ometries were optimized with the B3LYP/6-311G+(d,p) method. This
method was also used to evaluate the electrostatic potential surfaces
and the binding energies (Eb) between the Li+ ion and the solvent
molecule, in addition to the energy levels of the highest occupied
molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(LUMO). The binding energies were calculated with the following formula:
Eb = ELi+-A – ELi+ – EA, where ELi+-A, ELi+, and EA represent the total ener-
gies of, respectively, the Li+-A complex, the Li+ ion, and the A molecule.
