The conventional Li-S batteries are typically made of a 
cathode containing sulfur as active material inserted in a 
carbon matrix, an anode of lithium metal, an inert separator, 
and an ether-based electrolyte. If the battery is assembled 
from these components, it is prepared in a charged state. 
During a discharge cycle, elemental sulfur, which exists as 
ring-like octarhombic molecules (S8), is reduced to Li2S as 
the final product. On the opposite, during a charging cycle, 
this compound is oxidized to sulfur reversibly. Thus, the 
overall reaction taking place in the battery can be repre­
sented as:
S8 + 16 Li ⇋ 8 Li2S.
However, the actual charge and discharge processes are 
extremely complex, accompanied by many multiple side 
reactions. For the present purpose, the most relevant of these 
reactions leads to the appearance of soluble polysulfide spe­
cies of molecular formula Li2Sx with x values ranging from 
4 to 8, which migrate from one electrode to the other during 
the battery cycle, leading to the so-called “shuttle” effect.

Abstract
The present work deals with a first principles comparative study of the decomposition of Li2S and different polysulfides 
on graphitic surfaces in different dielectric media. This work aims at contributing to the understanding of the reactions 
that take place during the charging of a lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery in different solvents. The barrier for the Li-S bond 
breakage is determined using the nudged elastic band method, and a detailed analysis of the atomic charges along this 
pathway is performed. The role of the solvent is found to be twofold: on one side, increasing the environment dielectric 
constant lowers the energy barrier for the decomposition of the polysulfides. On the other hand, it weakens the interaction 
energy of the adsorbates on the surface. These two effects are opposite. While the first effect is expected to increase the 
reaction rate, the second one will contribute to decreasing the concentration of poly(sulfide) species on the surface. The 
present results also suggest that the rate determining step for the overall oxidation process of Li2S to S8 is changed by 
the nature of the solvent.

Liang et al. [14] prepared multilayer Ti3C2/Li2S cathodes 
by ball milling, finding a decrease of 0.6 V in the overpo­
tential for Li2S oxidation, as compared with a composite 
graphene/Li2S cathode.
Shin et al. [15] considered a Li2S@graphene cath­
ode coated with ZnS and found that the overpotential for 
the oxidation of Li2S to sulfur decreased, relative to the 
uncoated cathode. In this work, DFT calculations were used 
to analyze the interaction of ZnS with DOL and DME, in 
comparison with graphene as a substrate.
From the viewpoint of the influence of the solvent, 
a recent DFT work [16] has shown its relevance in bond 
cleavage reactions of polysulfide intermediates in bulk solu­
tion. These authors investigated the relationship between 
the number of donors and the dielectric constants of the 
solvent system and the relative stability of different polysul­
fide intermediates. In this respect, in a recent work [17], we 
have analyzed the cleavage of Li2S on graphene, MnS and 
VS2 surfaces in different solvents represented as a dielectric 
continuum.
This previous analysis shows the key role of the surface 
catalysts and the solvent in the oxidation Li2S, as well as the 
relevance of DFT calculations for their understanding.
Sulfur- or Li2S- based cathodes typically contain car­
bon materials to enhance electrical conductivity. Graphene/
Single-Walled Carbon Nanotube Hybrids exhibit excellent 
catalytic performance in Li-S batteries [18], and have been 
used as a reference when comparing with other materi­
als [11]. Graphene and functionalized graphene have also 
shown to be very effective for the immobilization of poly­
sulfides by providing favorable interaction sites for chemi­
cal adsorption and, thus, contributing to avoid the above 
mentioned “shuttle” effect [19–22]. For that reason, in this 
exploratory study, we have considered modeling the carbo­
naceous material in the cathode by graphene / graphite, to 
serve as the reference point for future studies of other cata­
lytic materials.
While the oxidation of Li2S is the first step in the charging 
process of the Li-S battery, it is followed by the formation 
of polysulfide chains, namely S4
2- and S6

by in-operando Xray absorption spectroscopy [23]. These

detection in solution suggests that their oxidation on the sur­

the whole charging process, it is also necessary not only to 
take into consideration the first step:
Li2S → LiS + Li+ + e-.

Li2Sx → LiSx + Li+ + e-.

An alternative to the preparation of sulfur cathodes is 
the Li2S cathode paradigm. This compound is embedded 
in the cathode supporting material and can be efficiently 
coupled to Li-free anodes, eliminating the use of Li metal 
as the anode. Several reviews [6–8] have been published 
also in this topic, including the recent report by Ye et al. 
[9]. Although the Li2S cathode presents several positive 
aspects, the main problem of this configuration is the activa­
tion of Li2S for its oxidation to sulfur. This reaction presents 
extremely sluggish kinetics, with overpotentials of the order 
of 3 V. Taking this into consideration, several strategies have 
been implemented to address this problem, as discussed in 
[9]: Doping, amorphization, nanostructuring, use of redox 
mediators, electrolytic additives, and electrocatalysts. The 
latter approach aims at lowering the activation energy bar­
rier of the intermediate involved in the rate-determining 
step, as we discuss in the following.
Yuan et al. [10] achieved an efficient activation of Li2S 
by different transition metal phosphine nanoparticles such as 
Ni2P, Co2P and Fe2P. Using density functional theory (DFT) 
calculations, these authors showed that the aforementioned 
phosphines exhibit much higher adsorption energy towards 
Li2S than pure or N-doped carbon. They also found that the 
dissociation energies of Li2S on these phosphine surfaces 
are considerably lower than those on pristine or N-doped 
carbon, thus supporting the enhanced catalytic activity 
found for phosphines.
Zhou et al. [11] performed experiments on the catalytic 
oxidation of Li2S on different metal sulfides and, with the 
support of DFT calculations, showed that the energy barrier 
for the decomposition of Li2S is associated with the bond 
between the isolated Li ions and the sulfur in the sulfides. A 
more recent work by He et al. [12] has confirmed the impor­
tant electrocatalytic behavior of different transition polysul­
fides (CoS, NiS, MnS) decorating carbon sponges, showing 
dramatic improvements in reducing the overpotential for 
Li2S activation.
Wu et al. [13] used nanostructured Li2S@LiTiO2 com­
posite electrodes, finding strong bonding of titanate with 
Li2S and inducing a rapid conversion of long-chain to 
short-chain polysulfides. DFT calculations showed that 
long polysulfides strongly adsorb and dissociate on LiTiO2 
surfaces. It was also found that solvent molecules should 
be easily displaced from the surface of the catalyst by the 
polysulfides.

Convergence studies were done for setting up the rele­
vant parameters for each system. The kinetic energy cutoff 
used for wave functions was 50 Ry, while for charge den­
sity and potential it was 400 Ry. Brillouin zone integration 
was approximated using the Monkhorst-Pack [26] scheme 
with 4 × 4 × 1 k-point sampling. Adsorption structures of 
the Li2Sx (x = 1, 2, 4, 6) species on the graphene sheet were 
built by placing either one or both Li atoms on a hollow 
site, thus giving one structure in which the Li一Li axis is 
perpendicular to the surface, and another in which the Li一
Li axis is parallel to the surface. Full geometry optimization 
was performed with convergence thresholds of 0.001 eV for 
total energies and 0.03 eV/Å for atomic forces. Adsorption 
energies were computed as:
Eads = E(Li2Sx@C) - E(Li2Sx) - E(C) (Eq. 1).
where E(Li2Sx@C) are the energies of the optimized 
adsorbates, E(Li2Sx) are the energies of the corresponding 
isolated polysulfides, and E(C) corresponds to the energy of 
pristine graphene.
Bader atomic charges were obtained applying the Bader 
analysis program developed by Henkelman and co-workers 
[27].
Minimum energy pathways for the decomposition of 
Li2Sx species adsorbed on graphene were determined 
according to the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-
NEB) method [28]. A string of seven images were employed 
for each reaction step, with a spring constant of 0.43 N/m.

2  Computational Methods
Calculations were performed with the Quantum Espresso 
computer code. Generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) 
of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [24] functional was 
used to describe exchange and correlation effects. Core elec­
trons were modeled by pseudopotentials in the projected-
augmented wave method (PAW). Van der Waals interactions 
were considered through semiempirical Grimme’s DFT-D2 
correction [25]. Spin polarization was taken into account in 
all calculations.
The carbon surface was modeled by a single 6 × 4 gra­
phene sheet containing a total of 60 carbon atoms. The unit 
cell employed was tetragonal with cell vectors of 12.31 Å 
× 12.79 Å in the plane parallel to the carbon layer. A 20 Å 
vacuum space between slabs in the direction perpendicular 
to the surface was employed in order to avoid artificial inter­
action effects with the periodic images.
Some selected configurations were recalculated using 4 
graphene layers in order to model graphite, as described in 
the Supplementary Information (SI) provided. The results 
were quantitatively similar to those obtained with a single 
graphene layer, thus validating our model.

3  Results and discussion
3.1  Adsorption of (Poly)sulfides on Graphene
We have taken as the starting point for the decomposition 
reactions the Li2Sx species (x = 1, 2, 4, 6) adsorbed on the 
carbon surface. Therefore, we have firstly considered the 
adsorption geometry of the Li2Sx species on graphene. For 
the larger chain polysulfides with x = 4, 6, and 8, it has been 
shown that in the most stable adsorption structure on pris­
tine graphene the molecule binds to the surface via a single 
Li atom with the hollow site on the surface of graphene [34]. 
We have then considered two distinct adsorption geometries: 
One in which the Li一Li axis lies parallel to the surface with 
both Li atoms positioned on the center of a carbon hexagon; 
and another in which the Li一Li axis is perpendicular to the 
solvents used in the field of Li-S batteries were considered 
in this study: Dimethylformamide (DMF, ε/ε0 = 36.7) and 
dimethylether (DME, ε/ε0 = 7.2). The dielectric function 
was defined by the soft-sphere continuum solvation (SSCS) 
model [31]. This interface model has been proved to be ade­
quate for lithium, due to its low electronic density. Parabolic 
point counter charge correction was included for surface 
calculations to avoid artificial interactions between periodic 
images [32]. Finally, atomic radii were defined according to 
Bondi’s work [33]. Calculations taking into account the sol­
vent environment as a continuous dielectric were performed 
for the same structures optimized in vacuum without further 
reoptimization. This was done point by point for the entire 
reaction pathways.

4  Conclusions
We have analyzed the decomposition of Li2S, Li2S2, Li2S4 
and Li2S6 on graphitic surfaces in different dielectric media. 
Our aim was to emulate the reaction of these species in 
different solvents on a typical carbonaceous substrate, as 
expected to occur in the cathodes of Li-sulfur batteries. 
Using DFT calculations, we obtained the barriers for these 
reaction. As a first approximation, it could be assumed that 
the main effect of an adequate electrode potential would be 
to shift the Fermi level of the electrode to lower energies 
and, hence, stabilize any additional electron charge. We 
could then, within a very simplistic model, assume that the 
effect of the electrode potential would affect the energetics 
of the decomposition pathways according to the following 
equation: E(V) = ENEB + VΔqC, where E(V) is the energy of 
a given CI-NEB image as a function of the electrochemi­
cal potential, ENEB is the corresponding energy as obtained 
from the CI-NEB calculation, V is the value of the electro­
chemical potential (in Volts), and ΔqC is the net change in 
the charge of the graphene sheet with respect to the reactant 
state as given by the Bader analysis for that given structure. 
The energy profiles for the decomposition pathway of Li2S2 
and Li2S4 on graphene, taken as a case study, in vacuum, 
DME, and DMF were recalculated according to the equa­
tion given above, and the results are illustrated in Figs. 9 
and 10, respectively.
