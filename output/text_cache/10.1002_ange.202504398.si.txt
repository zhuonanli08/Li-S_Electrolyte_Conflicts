1.1 Materials and general methods
All the chemicals are commercially purchased and used as received without further purification procedures. Carbon nanotubes (CNT) were purchased from Xianfeng Nano Company (Jiangsu, China). Super P and polyvinylidene fluoride were purchased from Lizhiyuan Company (Taiyuan, China) and Sigma-Aldrich Company (Shanghai, China), lithium was purchased from Zhongneng Lithium Company (Tianjin, China), carbon paper was purchased from Toray Company (Japan) and electrolyte was purchased from Fosai (Suzhou, China).
All samples were characterized and analyzed according to conventional techniques. The crystal structure of materials was characterized by X-ray powder diffractometer (XRD, Miniflex-600W, Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu K𝛼 radiation (𝜆= 1.54 Å). The Zeta potential was measured using the American-Brook-Zeta Potentiometer and Particle Size Analyzer 90Plus PALS. The morphologies of the materials were characterized by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JEOL JEM7800F Prime) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL, JEM-2100, operated at 200 kV), and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) (FEI Talos F200X G2) equipped with EDS (FEI SuperX G2). The N2 adsorption-desorption curve is obtained by ASAP 2020 specific surface area analyzer and the pore size distribution curve is calculated according to the non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) model. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was obtained on the Thermo Fisher Nexsa XPS system. Raman spectra were obtained with a Renishaw Raman Microprobe by a 532 nm laser. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra were obtained with UV-1801.
1.2 The synthesis of Ni-based ion bidirectional regulator
First, TATB (3 g, 12 mmol), 10% Pd/C (500 mg) and high-purity ethyl acetate (150 mL) were mixed in a high-pressure hydrogenation reactor, and the gas inside the reactor was replaced with pure H2. The mixture was then reacted at room temperature and high pressure (H2 pressure not less than 4 bar) for 36 hours. Afterwards, concentrated HCl (90 mL) was added, and the stirring continued in H2 environment for 5 hours. The reaction mixture was filtered under reduced pressure through diatomaceous earth to remove the catalyst, and the filtrate containing HAB·3HCl was washed with dilute HCl (0.5 M, 30 mL). The precipitate was collected by filtration using a PTFE membrane (0.45 μm), followed by freeze-drying to obtain solid HAB·3HCl.
A solution of Ni(NO3)2∙6H2O (218 mg, 0.75 mmol) and ammonia water (75 μL) was dissolved in deoxygenated NMP (15 mL) as solution A. Next, HAB·3HCl (139 mg, 0.5 mmol) was dissolved in deoxygenated NMP (15 mL) and slowly added dropwise to solution A. The mixture was then heated to 140 °C and reacted for 2 hours. After natural cooling to room temperature, the product was collected by centrifugation and washed three times with ethanol and water alternately to remove impurities. Finally, the product was freeze-dried to obtain the Ni-based ion velocity regulator.
1.3 The assembly and testing details of Li-S cells
The common commercial carbon nanotubes were used as cathode host materials. The active material was consisted of 80% sulfur (S) and 20% CNTs calcined in the protection of argon atmosphere at a rate of 2 ℃ min-1 from room temperature to 155 ℃ for 12 h. To fabricate the sulfur cathode, the as-synthesized CNT/S composites were mixed with super P and PVDF powder in a weight of 8: 1: 1 in NMP and stirred to form uniform slurry which was then casted onto an Al foil current collector and dried at 60 °C overnight. The dried cathodes were cut into disks with a diameter of 12 mm and the sulfur mass loadings were about 1.5~2 mg cm−2. A lithium metal disk (diameter = 15.6 mm, thickness = 450 m) was used as the anode. All CR2032 coin cells (Li||Li, Li-Cu and Li-S) were assembled in an argon-filled glove box (< 1 ppm of O2 and H2O) and the ratios of electrolyte to S were controlled to be 15 L mg-1 for Li-S coin cells.
A comprehensive experimental framework was developed to investigate Ni-HAB's impact on ions’ transport and Li+ solvation structures more accurately. For electrochemical characterization, the blank electrolyte was prepared as a mixed Li-containing solution (1 M trifluoromethanesulfonimide lithium (LiTFSI) dissolved in a solution of 1, 2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v/v = 1/1)). Ni-HAB electrolyte were prepared by adding 0.3 wt% of Ni-based ion velocity regulator with ultrasonic dispersion for 12 h. However, given the instability of this baseline formulation during extended operation—particularly its tendency to decompose and cause Coulombic efficiency (CE) degradation—we incorporated extra 2 wt% LiNO3 additive in electrolytes during all long-term cycling tests. This distinction between the characterization and cycling electrolytes ensures that Ni-HAB's performance benefits are isolated from confounding effects of electrolyte decomposition, thereby providing a clear demonstration of its ability to enhance lithium deposition.
For the assembly of pouch cell, we used aluminum foil as the cathode current collector and copper-lithium composite tape as the anode current collector. The tabs for the cathode and anode were made of aluminum and nickel, respectively. The sulfur load was about 1.2 g and the E/S ratio was 4 μL mg-1. For the comprehensive consideration of cells’ safety and energy density, the N/P ratio was controlled at about 1.15. The dimensions of the assembled pouch cells were 4.3 × 5.6 cm. The actual capacity of modified pouch cells can reach 1.0 Ah and the mass of each component has been listed in detail in Figure 7j.
Galvanostatic charge/discharge measurements were performed on a LAND CT2001A testing system with a voltage window of 1.7−2.8 V under various C-rates (1 C = 1672 mA g-1, based S). The autolab PGSTAT302N (Metrohm, Switzerland) electrochemical workstation was used to collect the potentiostatic, cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) spectra. The AC amplitude of EIS is 5 mV and the frequency ranges from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz.
Synchrotron radiation X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) experiment
Synchrotron XAS at the Ni K-edge was conducted at the BL13SSW beamline of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. X-ray photon energy was monochromatized using a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. The Ni scan was run at the Ni K-edge absorption of 8333 eV. All spectral data were collected at room temperature in transmission mode. The raw data were normalized and analyzed on the basis of the ATHENA software package.
VSM hysteresis loop measurement
The magnetic hysteresis loops of the samples were measured using the Lake Shore 7404 Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (VSM) at room temperature, with a magnetic field range of -2 to 2 T.
Zeta potential measurement
The Zeta potential was measured using a Brook Zeta Potential and Particle Size Analyzer. Ethylene glycol dimethyl ether (DME) was used as the dispersant.

Visualization lithium polysulfide adsorption
Li2S and S in a molar ratio of 1:5 were dissolved in the solution of dimethoxy ethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (VDME/VDOL = 1:1). The obtained mixture was stirred for 24 h at 60 ℃ to yield 5 mmol/L Li2S6 solution. Sequentially, 10 mg of Ni-based ion velocity regulator and LiTFSI were both added into 5 mL of Li2S6 solution. After 1 h of adsorption, the solution was used for UV-visible detection and the solid was used for X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy detection to verify its effect of adsorption and catalytic conversion performance of lithium polysulfide.
Li2S nucleation measurements
For the study of liquid-solid conversion kinetics, the Ni-based ion velocity regulator of different qualities were dissolved in isopropanol and then dropped onto round carbon paper disks (12 mm), the total mass loading was controlled around 1 mg. 25 μL Li2S8 catholyte (0.2 M Li2S8, 1 M LiTFSI in tetreglyme) and 20 μL electrolyte without Li2S8 were added to the cathode and anode sites, respectively. The cells were first discharged to 2.06 V under a constant current of 0.112 mA and then kept potentiostatically at 2.05 V until the current dropped below 10-5 A. The nucleation capacity of Li2S can be calculated by the integral area of the plotted curve through Faraday’s Law.

Symmetric cell assembly and kinetic evaluation of polysulfide conversion
The symmetric cells employ carbon paper as counter and working electrodes. 40 μL Li2S6 solution (0.2 M Li2S6 and 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME, v/v = 1/1) was used as the LiPSs electrolyte. The CV measurements of the symmetric cells were performed with a voltage window between -1 to 1 V.
In-situ optical microscope, Raman and XRD spectroscopy measurements
The in-situ optical microscope, Raman and XRD devices were purchased from Beijing Scistar Technology Co., Ltd. Two pieces of 8 mm × 9 mm lithium sheets were used as the positive and negative electrodes of Li||Li symmetrical batteries, and the plastic with regular gap was used as the separator. The growth of dendrites on the lithium sheet during charge and discharge was observed by optical microscope at the gap.
A quartz window was used for laser passage for in-situ Raman. A hole (2 mm) was punched in the Li-metal anode to allow the laser to focus directly on the separator. The cells were run at a current density of 0.5 C. Raman signals were obtained on Renishaw Microprobe by a 532 nm laser. 
As for the in-situ XRD spectrum test, a beryllium window was used for X-ray penetration; Ultrathin Al foil (~ 6 μm) was used as current collector. The sulfur loading of each cell was about 3 mg to get better signal. The cells were run at a current density of 0.1 C for in-situ XRD. Continuous scanning during cell running with each scan measured at 15-40° in 0.02° incremental steps.
Computational details
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations
The present study uses the Vienna Ab initio simulation package (VASP) based on density generalisation theory to perform first-principles calculations using the projected augmented wave method and taking into account spin polarisation. The generalized gradient approximation based on the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof method (PBE) is used to consider the exchange-correlation function4,5. The cut-off energy of the plane wave basis group is set to 500 eV. Special K-point sampling in the Brillouin zone using the Monkhorst Pack scheme (structure optimized to 4 × 4 × 1 and electronic structure calculated to 8 × 8 × 1)6. In this study, the Grimme DFT-D3 format with zero-damped dispersion correction is used to consider the van der Waals interaction, and the dipole correction is parallel to the Z-direction only, making the vacuum layer converge.
The free energy change (ΔG) for each reaction step is calculated by the equation ΔGi = ΔEi + ΔZPEi - TΔSi, where ΔEi is the reaction energy obtained by geometric structure calculations, and ΔZPEi and TΔSi denote the ZPE difference and entropy difference, respectively. The ZPE correction is calculated by using the calculated vibrations obtained from the finite difference Hessians frequencies to determine them. The entropy contribution is determined from standard thermodynamic tables of reactants and products, with the adsorbed species on the surface assumed to be zero.
Structural optimizations of anions, cations and solvent molecules were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311G** level. Following the optimizations, binding energies were calculated at the more precise B3LYP/6-311+G (2d, p) level to ensure an accurate assessment of interaction energies. Additionally, the energy levels of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and electrostatic potential (ESP) were derived from the structures optimized at the B3LYP/6-311G** level. The HOMO/LUMO and electrostatic potential maps were rendered using the Tachyon feature in VMD software.
Phase-Field Simulation
COMSOL Multiphysics with Finite Element Simulation.
COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 was used for simulation, which incorporated a secondary current distribution, Nernst-Planck interface, and deforming geometric interface model, to analyze the impact of different electrolytes on the distribution of potential during the deposition process. The simulation calculated the distribution of the potential in the model under steady state based on three types of potential distributions and tracked the transient changes in the concentration field in the model after applying working current based on the dilute matter diffusion module. Specifically, the upper boundary of the simulated area was set as a constant electrolyte potential to represent bulk electrolyte, while the lower boundary was designated as the working electrode. Within the simulation area, Li+ migration adhered to the Nernst-Planck-Poisson equation, and reactions at the electrode surface followed the Butler-Volmer equation. Both the unmodified electrolyte system and Ni-HAB modified electrolyte system were operated under the same working voltage (5 mA cm-2, 1 mAh cm-2). The initial concentration of Li+ was 1 M and deposition time was 0, 30, 60 and 90 s. In order to simulate the electrostatic shielding effect caused by additives, the exchange current density was correlated with the electric field strength. The temperature was 308 K and the conductivity of the unmodified electrolyte and the Ni-HAB modified electrolyte was 6.1*10-4 S cm-1 and 8.67*10-4 S cm-1, respectively. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations 
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using the Gromacs 2022 software. The interactions were computed with the Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations all-atom (OPLS-AA) force field. Topology files, along with bonded and Lennard-Jones parameters for neutral solvents and additives, were generated through the LigParGen server. To account for polarization and charge transfer effects, a charge scaling factor of 0.8 was applied to ions. For neutral molecules, 1.2-scaled CM5 charges derived from density functional theory (DFT) calculations using Multiwfn were employed. Modeling of three-dimensional boxes using Packmol software, with the number of molecules for each component in the electrolyte listed in the Table S1 below. In all MD simulations, the initial energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method. The Verlet cutoff scheme was adopted, and long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff distance of 1.0 nm for both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions. Subsequently, the system underwent a 20 ns equilibration in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble, employing a V-rescale thermostat and a C-rescale barostat to maintain a temperature of 298.15 K and a pressure of 1 bar, with a fixed time step of 1 fs. Following equilibration, a 20 ns production run was conducted using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Post-simulation analyses included calculating the radial distribution function (RDF) and diffusion coefficients. Visualization of the electrolyte simulation box was carried out using VMD software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was applied to characterize the structure and morphology of Ni-based ion velocity regulator (Supplementary Fig. 1. a-d). The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Supplementary Fig. 1. c) and inverse fast Fourier transformation (IFFT) image (Supplementary Fig. 1. d) display abundant lattice fringes with d-spacing of 1.16 nm, corresponding to the (001) plane of the regulator, which confirms its high crystallinity and a good growth orientation. The fast Fourier transformation (FFT) image of Supplementary Fig. 1. a selected area presents a lattice point array, and the IFFT image (Supplementary Fig. 1. b) illustrates hexagonal pores structure with a size of 8.5 Å, which perfectly matches the eclipsed model. What’s more, high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image captured with scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) (Supplementary Fig. 1. e-h) reveal that the regulator mainly consists of C, N, and Ni. In order to further characterize the regulator, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and Raman spectroscopy were employed to confirm its crystallographic structure and chemical composition. The sharp XRD peaks at 7.54, 15.42, 20.32 and 41.64, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. i confirmed the its high crystallinity. Additionally, the Raman spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 1. j) exhibits a single intense peak at 1500 cm-1, indicating the presence of a distinct monolayer structure and a significant amount of sp2 hybridized carbon. These characterization results collectively provide substantial evidence for the successful preparation of highly crystalline Ni-based ion velocity regulator with abundant mesoporous structure.

Supplementary Fig. S5. a-d & e-h Electrical impedance spectroscopy (a-d), enlarged view of selected area (e-h) and activation energy (Ea) (i-l) of cells assembled with different Ni-HAB addition concentrations.
To further investigate the influence of Li+ release promotion on the overall system, we measured the internal resistance of stainless-steel symmetric cells with different electrolytes at different temperatures to determine the actual conductivity7. Subsequently, Arrhenius formula was employed to calculate the Li+ diffusion activation energy barrier. 


It is important to note that the additive concentration significantly affects the electrolyte's viscosity. Hence, the impact of various Ni-HAB concentrations on the diffusion activation energy barrier should also be examined. It was observed that the solvation structure adjusted by Ni-HAB significantly reduces the diffusion transport of Li+ by solvent molecules. However, due to steric hindrance, high concentrations of Ni-HAB can negatively affect Li+ diffusion and transport. At an optimal concentration, the Li+ diffusion activation energy barrier can be reduced from 0.0847 eV to 0.0655 eV. This enhanced diffusion capability can effectively decrease the likelihood of dendrite nucleation sites due to local Li+ deficiency, thereby mitigating the risk of dendrite formation and growth.

Supplementary No 19

Supplementary Fig. S19. Impedance diagram and corresponding analog circuit with fitting data of Li-S cells assembled with different electrolytes.
Impedance tests were conducted on Li-S cells assembled with different electrolyte to find out its impact on the electrode/electrolyte interface. The results from the Nyquist plots determined Rct and Warburg capacitance. In cells assembled with blank electrolyte, the transformation of LiPSs was slower due to the low kinetics of SRR and they predominantly existed in the form of long-chain LiPSs such as Li2S6 and Li2S4, which have higher impedance than that for liquid-solid transformation. Moreover, the accumulation of LiPSs led to an increase in the viscosity of the electrolyte and an enlargement of internal impedance. Therefore, the impedance of cells with blank electrolyte was relatively large (39.41 Ω). Conversely, the incorporation of Ni-HAB enhanced both ionic conductivity and the kinetics of the SRR, resulting in a marked decrease in charge transfer resistance within the solution, down to 21.92 Ω. This reduction was attributed to the lower concentration of LiPSs in the modified electrolyte, which expedited liquid-phase mass transfer and, consequently, alleviated concentration polarization in the battery during discharge and charge cycles.
