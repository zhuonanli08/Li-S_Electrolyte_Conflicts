Designing Cation–Solvent Fully Coordinated Electrolyte for High-
Energy-Density Lithium–Sulfur Full Cell Based On Solid–Solid
Conversion
Huijun Yang+, Yu Qiao+, Zhi Chang, Ping He, and Haoshen Zhou*
Abstract: Sulfur chemistry based on solid–liquid dissolution-
deposition route inevitably encounters shuttle of lithium
polysulfides, its parasitic interaction with lithium (Li) anode
and flood electrolyte environment. The sulfurized pyrolyzed
poly(acrylonitrile) (S@pPAN) cathode favors solid-solid con-
version mechanism in carbonate ester electrolytes but fails to
pair high-capacity Li anode. Herein, we rationally design
a cation-solvent fully coordinated ether electrolyte to simulta-
neously resolve the problems of both Li anode and S@pPAN
cathode. Raman spectroscopy reveals a highly suppressed
solvent activity and a cation-solvent fully coordinated structure
(molar ratio 1:1). Consequently, Li electrodeposit evolves into
round-edged morphology, LiF-rich interphase, and high
reversibility. Moreover, S@pPAN cathode inherits a neat
solid-phase redox reaction and fully eliminated the dissolution
of lithium polysulfides. Finally, we harvest a long-life Li-
S@pPAN pouch cell with slight Li metal excessive (0.4 time)
and ultra-lean electrolyte design (1 mLmgS
@1), delivering
394 Whkg@1 energy density based on electrodes and electrolyte
mass.
Introduction
Stimulated by the market demands, the battery technol-
ogy requires an energy-dense, cost-effective, and sustainable
electrochemical system beyond traditional lithium-ion inter-
calation electrochemistry.[1] The adoption of rechargeable
lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery was expected to boost energy
density and reduce cost for the broad market of long-range
electric vehicles.[2] Moreover, the high element abundance of
sulfur also merits the sustainability for future large-scale
applications. Since the very initial investigation on Li-S
battery, researchers have reached a consensus that cyclo-
octasulfur (cyclo-S8) cathode undergoes a step-by-step dis-
solution-deposition process in ether-based electrolytes.[3]
Then extensive attention focused on governing the soluble
intermediate lithium polysulfides (LiPS) by screening sulfur
host materials from conductive matrix (graphene, carbon
nanotube, porous carbon etc.), to absorption materials
(metal-organic framework) and catalytic agents (metal oxides
or sulfides etc.).[4] The manipulation on LiPS by inhibiting
shuttle effect or accelerating conversion of sulfur species
witnessed stable electrochemical profiles of Li-S batteries,
however, still failed to reach the strict evaluation criteria of
lean electrolyte usage towards high-energy-density batter-
ies.[5]
The dilemma on cyclo-S8 cathodes lies in the inevitable
dissolution of active species into ethers, which positively
promotes a complete sulfur reduction (high sulfur utilization
and high capacity), but negatively provokes parasitic shuttle
effect.[6] Carbonate ester electrolytes have low solubility of
LiPS and encounter severe nucleophilic reaction, which
terminates cell working and seems impracticable for cyclo-
S8 cathodes (Figure 1a). The highly exclusive choice of ether
electrolytes for cyclo-S8 cathodes, however, intrinsically
require a “flood electrolyte” environment for the generation
and conversion of LiPS (Figure 1a).[7] Moreover, the scale-up
trial from coin-cell (sulfur cathode area: & 1 cm2) to pouch
cells necessitate complex cathode design and remains a chal-
lenge.[5a,8] Alternative choice of solid-state electrolyte might
provide a suitable option to get rid of a large amount of
electrolyte for solid-solid conversion (SSC) pathway of cyclo-
S8 cathodes.[9] However, the rigid cathode/electrolyte inter-
face still remains a bottleneck.[10] As a compromise strategy,
Nazar and Pang et al. proposed a quasi-solid state sulfur
conversion to realize a low electrolyte/sulfur ratio (E/S,
5 mLmgS
@1) by tuning electrolyte structure.[11]

Results and Discussion
Electrolyte solvation structure study. The definition of
solvation manner is described by solvent separated ion pair
(SSIP), contact ion pair (CIP) and aggregate (AGG) when the
anions paired with zero, one or more than one Li+,
respectively.[13] In lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI)/dimethoxyethane (DME) solutions, the solvation
mode of CIP and AGG can be summarized as Li+-TFSI@
coordinated, when compared with Li+-TFSI@ uncoordinated
in SSIP mode. Figure 2a presents a clear peak located at
& 751 cm@1 from SNS stretches of TFSI@ anion for crystal
LiTFSI. After dissolving into DME (mole ratio of DME:
LiTFSI is 16.3:1), the peak of SNS stretches has a red-shift to
& 743.8 cm@1, which can be split into low-frequency peak at
743 cm@1, and high-frequency peak at 748 cm@1, correspond-
ing to Li+-TFSI@ uncoordinated and coordinated mode,
respectively. The split area ratio represents the population
of corresponding electrolyte configuration, where Li+-TFSI@
uncoordinated mode accounted for the majority (63.1%).
Further dissolving LiTFSI into DME until saturation (mole
ratio of DME: LiTFSI is 2.5:1), the TFSI@ related peak
shifted to higher frequency centered at & 745 cm@1. Hence,
the Li+-TFSI@ coordinated mode was responsible for the
majority (64.3%), which realized a highly aggressive ion-pairs
by increasing LiTFSI concentration. Figure 2a exhibits a peak
centered at 825 and 852 cm@1, corresponding to the CH2
rocking and C-O-C stretching vibration of DME, respectively.
After dissolving LiTFSI, both peaks reflecting disordered
conformations exhibited clear blue-shift and peak emerging
at & 876 cm@1 (Li+-DME) interaction was strengthened in
saturated solution.
We then rationally embedded LiTFSI/DME solution into
Cu-BTC/PSS-modified (short as MOF, pore size 6.5 c) after
fully electrolyte absorption and confinement (Figure S1).[14]
The Li+-TFSI@ coordinated ratio and intensity of Li+-DME
interaction are exploited to characterize the solvation struc-
ture of MOF-modified electrolyte (Figure S2). Figure 2b
presents a blue-shift of TFSI@ related peak and gradually
intensified peak of Li+-DME interaction as the detection
depth of Raman spectra. When Raman leaser reaching the
inside of MOF channel, the collected signal displayed a high-
frequency located at 748 cm@1, implying a fully Li+-TFSI@
coordinated mode (Figure 2b). Moreover, the intensified Li+-
DME interaction indicates more DME molecule participated
into coordination with Li+. The unique solvation degree is
going beyond the limit of LiTFSI saturated concentration
under ambient temperature.
Conformer distribution of DME offers another proof to
determine electrolyte stability. Under ambient environment,
liquid DME solvent possesses five most abundant conformers,
namely TGT, TGG, TTT, TGG’ and TTG, as investigated by
experimental vibrational spectroscopy on Raman activity and
theoretical density functional theory (DFT) calculation on
energy (Figure 2c, Table S2).[15] The deformation configura-
tions of O-C-C bending in 400, 541, 553 and 572 cm@1 are
assigned to TTT, TGG’, TGG and TGT, respectively.[16] TTG
conformer is the most unstable configuration owing to its
highest energy and has a typical band in 488 cm@1. The two

The strengthened Li+-DME interaction also reduces the
DME solvent activity, which is reflected on electrolyte
flammability and oxidation. Both dilute and saturated LiTF-
SI/DME solutions are combustible even under the absence of
exogenous fire. By confinement ether electrolyte into MOF, it
even maintains non-flammable under the presence of exog-
enous fire (Figure S4). Electrolyte oxidation is another proof,
where free DME molecular easily decomposes under high
potential. The liner sweep voltammogram (LSV) determines
MOF-modified electrolyte has a superior voltage tolerance
(4.7 V) compared with that (4.4 V) in 4 M LiTFSI/DME
electrolyte (Figure S5). Moreover, the corrosion of Al current
collectors in 4 M LiTFSI/DME electrolyte was also remark-
ably suppressed in the MOF-modified electrolyte (Figure S6).
In brief, this work provided alternative approach to tailor
electrolytes, which was not realized by previous electrolyte
formulations of introducing inert fluorinated diluents.[11,19]

High-loading S@pPAN electrodes were prepared and
rolled to reduce the porosity prior to use (Figure S17). Li-
S@pPAN (sulfur loading: 5.63 mgcm@2) with MOF-modified
electrolyte was performed galvanostatic discharge/charge
cycling. Voltage profile (the 1st cycle) under 0.1 C (1 C =
1672 mAhg@1 based on sulfur) reappears the typical behav-
iors of S@pPAN cathode in terms of sloping platform and
capacity delivery (Figure S18). The cell delivers a reversible
areal capacity of 7.5 mAhcm@2 and maintained a high ca-
pacity-retention of 94.5% after 100 cycle at 0.2 C. Further-
more, the power behavior is evaluated by discharge at
increasing rate and charge at constant 0.1 C (Figure 4 f).
The cell delivered areal capacity of 7.41, 7.38, 7.36 and
3.28 mAhcm@2 at 0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.3 C and 0.5 C, respectively.
The significant capacity loss at 0.5 C might attribute to the
enlarged overpotential at high current-density discharge
(4.73 mAcm@2). Subsequent shifting to low rate under 0.1 C
and 0.2 C, the benign capacity recovery indicates that electro-
des undergo no structural deconstruction after high current-
density discharge (Figure S19).
Self-discharge behavior remains a crucial characteristic
for scale-up Li-S battery. We then carried out a rigorous self-
discharge protocol by idling cell at fixed potentials (3.0, 1.9,
1.7 and 1.5 V) for 48 h (Figure 4g). In detail, we schematically
illustrate the capacity loss during idling time and final
capacity loss after charge process (Figure S20). We adopt
the CE to evaluate the irreversible capacity loss, where sulfur
species shuttle and loss would lead to low CE value. The cell
resting at 3.0 V soon drops the potential to 2.4 V owing to
voltage relaxion kinetics and maintains a high CE of 99.46%
after idling time. The cell resting at 1.9, 1.7 and 1.5 V has an
obvious potential recovery to 2.07, 1.94 and 1.77 V, respec-
tively. After idling time (48 h), the cell still delivers a sat-
isfactory capacity with a desirable CE of 99.71%, 99.60% and
99.61%, respectively (Figure 4g). The S@pPAN cathode in
MOF-modified electrolyte involves a highly reversible SSC
reaction pathway and obtained a neglected capacity loss
during the idling time.
In the practical application scenarios, abuse conditions of
accidental over-charge and over-discharge cause irreversible
structure degradation for lithium transitional metal oxides
(LMO) cathode. We then examined the battery system with
MOF-modified electrolyte under abuse conditions. After
over-charge to 3.5 V, the cell exhibited no significant capacity
increment and peculiar plateau. The subsequent cycle ex-
hibited a normal discharge/charge profile, which implies no
severe side-reaction happened (Figure 4h). By setting the cut-
off potential as 0.5 V, the extra capacity might derive from Li
insertion into PAN backbone under low potential but leave no
negative impact on subsequent cycling (Figure 4h). Taking

Conclusion
Herein, we highlight the significance of SSC conversion
for Li-S battery based on S@pPAN cathode and a rationally
designed electrolyte. Unlike the electrolyte tailoring strat-
egies by introducing inert diluents, we also successfully
realized a highly suppressed solvent activity and a unique
solvation configuration with molar ratio of DME to LiTFSI
reaching 1:1. We then harvest a homogeneous and round-
edged electrodeposit of Li metal with high reversibility of
99.34%. Moreover, the cation-solvent fully coordinated ether
electrolyte enables reversible solid-phase redox reaction of
S@pPAN cathode, by eliminating LiPS dissolution and the
dependence on electrolyte amount. We then successfully
showcase a high-energy Li-S@pPAN pouch cell (394 Whkg@1
based on electrodes and electrolyte mass) with strict working
conditions of limited Li metal anode (0.4 time excessive) and
