ABSTRACT: Understanding the distinct roles of lithium ions (Li+) and
lithium polysulfide intermediates, Li2Sx (LiPS) is critical for designing
electrolytes that can extend the practical cycle life of lithium−sulfur
(Li−S) batteries. In this work, we decouple the solvation and solubility
effects of Li+ and LiPS and correlate them with electrochemical
performance through a cosolvent strategy. Li+ solubility and solvation
primarily dictate the electrolyte’s ionic conductivity and the reversibility
of lithium anode stripping/plating. In contrast, LiPS solvation governs
the thermodynamics of sulfur (S8), LiPS, and lithium sulfide (Li2S)
interconversion, while the LiPS solubility determines their redox
kinetics. By employing a fluorinated−glyme (F-glyme) cosolvent,
specifically 1,2-bis(2,2-difluoroethoxy)ethane (F4DEE), that exhibits
low LiPS solubility yet moderate Li+ solvation, we designed an
electrolyte that enhances lithium anode stability while maintaining sufficient sulfur cathode kinetics, thereby prolonging
Li−S cell cycle life. This study provides mechanistic insights into the interplay between Li+ and LiPS in Li−S electrochemistry
and offers design principles for next-generation electrolytes for Li−S batteries.
O
wing to their high theoretical energy density (2600
Wh kg−1) and natural abundance of sulfur,1 lithium−
sulfur (Li−S) batteries have emerged as one of the
most promising candidates for next-generation rechargeable
battery technologies, aiming to overcome the limitations of
traditional lithium-ion batteries.2 However, Li−S chemistry
faces intrinsic challenges primarily related to lithium
polysulfide (LiPS) conversion and lithium anode degradation,
which hinder commercialization. The sulfur redox pathway
involves repeated conversion among insoluble sulfur (S8,
solid), soluble LiPS intermediates (liquid), and insoluble
lithium sulfide (Li2S, solid). This sluggish multistep and
multiphase process reduces the utilization of active sulfur
material.3,4 Simultaneously, the reactive lithium anode is prone
to pulverization and dendrite growth, negatively impacting
cycle life and safety.5 Moreover, soluble LiPS species diffuse to
the anode side −a phenomenon known as the “shuttle effect”
−where they react with lithium metal, accelerating lithium
corrosion and reducing Coulombic efficiency (CE).6 These
issues limit the practical cycle life of Li−S batteries.
Electrolytes play a pivotal role in Li−S battery performance
by governing the Li anode and LiPS conversion through
controlling the solubility and solvation of Li+ and LiPS.4,7
Conventional ether-based electrolytes, such as 1,3-dioxolane
(DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME),4 typically exhibit
high lithium salt and LiPS solubility with strong Li+ and LiPS
solvation. While this supports ionic conductivity, it leads to
severe LiPS shuttle and parasitic reactions on the lithium
anode, limiting cycle life.8,9 To address this, sparely solvating
electrolytes (SSEs) have been developed by adding non-
solvating cosolvents −often fluorinated ethers (F-ethers) −to
DOL and DME, including compounds like 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoro-
3-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyoxy)propane (TTE),10,11 bis(2,2,2-
trifluoroethyl) ether (BTFE),12 and octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethyl ether (OFE).13 The approach suppresses LiPS
dissolution that mitigates the shuttle effect, and forms a
fluorine-rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the Li
anode,10,13,14 which helps regulate plating/stripping. However,

these electrolytes substantially inhibit Li+ and LiPS solubility
and solvation. In extreme cases, the redox mechanism shifts to
a sluggish quasi-solid-state sulfur conversion pathway that
requires elevated temperatures.15,16 Additionally, the high
viscosity of these electrolytes can cause poor electrode wetting,
especially with high sulfur-loading cathodes.17
Despite these advances, achieving an optimal balance
between lithium anode stability and fast sulfur redox kinetics
remains a challenge.18−20 Li+ and LiPS exhibit distinct
behaviors in Li−S electrolytes: LiPS tends to cluster, while
lithium salts such as lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)
imide (LiTFSI) dissociate into Li+ cations and TFSI−
anions.21−23 Furthermore, it is important to distinguish
solvation and solubility, because they describe different
physiochemical processes. Solubility is a macroscopic property
that quantifies how much of a solute (e.g., LiTFSI or LiPS) can
be dissolved in the solvent. In contrast, solvation refers to the
molecular-level interactions between the solute and the solvent
molecules (Figure S1).24 An example for this distinction is that
LiPS dimers and clusters exhibit high solubility but are weakly
solvated in many solvents.21−23 Therefore, the design of
advanced electrolytes requires decoupling the solubility and
solvation of LiPS and Li+ to accelerate sulfur redox kinetics and
enhance Li anode stability concurrently.
In this work, we propose a partially fluorinated glyme (F-
glyme) cosolvent, 1,2-bis(2,2-difluoroethoxy) ethane
(F4DEE), combined with DOL to form a Li−S battery
electrolyte. F4DEE exhibits low LiPS solubility, similar to other
fluorinated ethers, but maintains high LiTFSI solubility and
moderate Li+ solvation. As a result, the DOL/F4DEE
electrolyte features moderate Li+ solvation, moderate LiPS
solubility, and weak LiPS solvation, distinguishing it from
conventional ether electrolytes (DOL/DME) and F-ether
electrolyte (DOL/TTE). Correlating these properties with Li−
S electrochemical performance, we find that all tested
electrolytes with an optimal 1.0 M LiTFSI concentration
show high Li+ conductivity, regardless of the Li+ solvating
strength. However, the moderate Li+ solvation in DOL/
F4DEE effectively regulates lithium stripping/plating, improv-
ing the anode stability. On the cathode side, weak LiPS
solvation thermodynamically favors short-chain LiPS (Li2Sx, x
= 2−4) and promotes the oxidation conversion of LiPS to S8
during charging. This enhances sulfur cathode reversibility and
protects the lithium anode from corrosive long-chain LiPS.
Additionally, we illustrate that the small Li−S redox over-
potentials correlate with high LiPS solubility owing to
facilitated LiPS diffusion. The moderate LiPS solubility in
DOL/F4DEE thus supports fast sulfur redox kinetics, resulting
in low cell overpotentials during cycling. Collectively, these
features contribute to the extended cycle life of Li−S batteries
using the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte. The fundamental insights
and electrolyte design strategy presented here pave the way
toward developing long-lifespan Li−S batteries.

As shown in Figure 1a, F4DEE exhibits exceptionally high
LiTFSI solubility (∼6.2 M), which even surpasses that of DME
and DOL. In contrast, LiTFSI salt is hardly dissolved in TTE
solvent, validated by both the dissolution test and 19F nuclear
magnetic resonance (19F NMR) results (Figures S2 and S3).
Despite superior lithium salt solubility, F4DEE interestingly
exhibits moderate Li+ solvation, as identified by 19F NMR
(Figure 1b, Figure S4). Specifically, six solvents were selected
to formulate electrolytes with 1.0 M LiTFSI, including DME,
DOL/DME (1:1, v/v), DOL, F4DEE, DOL/F4DEE (8:2, v/
v), and DOL/TTE (8:2, v/v). The upshift of the 19F NMR
signal of TFSI−originates from a stronger coordination of the
TFSI−−Li+ ion pair, indicating Li+ solvation strength is in the
order of DOL/TTE < DOL ≈F4DEE < DOL/DME < DME.
Such a trend was further validated by Raman spectroscopy
(Figure S5).25
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation illustrates that
F4DEE partially enters the Li+ solvation shell in the DOL/
F4DEE electrolyte (Figure S6). Moreover, the distribution of
various coordination numbers (1, 2 and 3) of TFSI−
surrounding Li+ (Figure 1c)26,27 suggests moderate Li+
solvation in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte, because Li+ shows
a higher tendency (higher percentile) to be coordinated by
more TFSI−than that in the DOL/DME electrolyte. Overall,
the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte presents moderate Li+ solvation
with an anion-dominated solvation structure. The combination
of high LiTFSI solubility and moderate Li+ solvation arises
from the PFG structure of F4DEE. The local dipole of the
F4DEE molecule effectively disperses LiTFSI, thereby
enhancing its solubility. At the same time, fluorination weakens
the solvent’s coordinating ability, resulting in only moderate
Li+ solvation (Note S1).
Apart from Li+, the introduction of F4DEE also modifies the
LiPS solubility and solvation in the electrolyte. Li2S6
dissolution experiment (Figure 1d, Figure S8) exhibits spare
Li2S6 solubility in F4DEE (0.024 M), slightly higher than TTE
(almost insoluble) but much lower than DOL (0.41 M) and
DME (2.5 M). This indicates that F4DEE easily dissociates
Li+-TFSI−to yield high LiTFSI solubility (6.2 M), but barely
dissociates Li+-Sx
2−. This is because Li+-Sx
2−is much more
strongly bonded than Li+-TFSI−, and F4DEE is less capable of
coordinating with LiPS than DOL and DME.21 Consequently,
the DOL/F4DEE mixed solvent shows a moderate Li2S6
solubility of 0.38 M, in sharp contrast to 1.0 M in DOL/
DME and 0.22 M in DOL/TTE. This is the desirable LiPS
solubility that remarkably impacts Li−S electrochemical
performances, which will be discussed later.

■MODERATE LI+ SOLVATION OF DOL/F4DEE
STABILIZES THE LI METAL ANODE
The Li anode stability is systematically investigated in various
electrolytes. Lithium salt concentration and Li+ solvation in the
electrolyte determine Li stability by influencing Li+ transfer
and Li solid-electrolyte interface (SEI).4 Li+ conductivity
increases with LiTFSI concentration but will reach the peak
value because high LiTFSI concentration impairs Li+ solvation
and hinders Li+ transport.30 Moreover, high LiTFSI concen-
tration inhibits LiPS dissolution by suppressing LiPS
dissociation equilibrium (namely, the common-ion effect),
resulting in low sulfur utilization.31 Hence, the LiTFSI
concentration was optimized to be 1.0 M (Note S2). The
DOL/F4DEE electrolyte possesses a high Li+ conductivity of
3.6 mS cm−1, indicating its capability to facilitate Li+ transport
in the bulk electrolyte.
In addition to high Li+ conductivity, the moderate Li+
solvation in DOL/F4DEE enhances the Li stripping/plating
stability. Figure 2a and Figure S10 show Li stripping/plating
Coulombic efficiency (CE) with varied Li+ solvation measured
by Aurbach method in Li||Cu cells.32 Electrolytes with weaker
Li+ solvation generally deliver higher CE. Among them, DOL/
F4DEE delivers the highest CE of 98.25%, demonstrating its
outstanding Li stripping/plating reversibility. The moderate
Li+ solvation in DOL/F4DEE also regulates the stripping/
plating process of the Li anode, showing chunky and dense Li
deposition morphology with a relatively reduced Li thickness
(Figure 2b, Figure S11). Such a morphology reduces the
exposed interface area between the Li metal and the
electrolyte, suppressing parasitic reactions for high CE.33
These results demonstrate the correlation between moderate
Li+ solvation and improved Li stripping/plating reversibility in
DOL/F4DEE, which aligns well with previous studies on the
Li metal anode.18,19,34 The improved stability of the Li anode
in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte is further validated by its
superior CE during long-term Li||Cu stripping/plating cycling
(Figure S12).
To further elucidate the beneficial role of moderate Li+
solvation in DOL/F4DEE, we analyzed the chemical
composition of the Li SEI via X-ray photoelectron spectros-
copy (XPS) depth profiling (Figure S13). The F-rich Li SEI in
the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte is validated by the intensified LiF
signal and the extra −CF3 signal in Figure 2c and Figure S14.
In addition, the anion-derived N-containing species in the Li
SEI are more abundant in DOL/F4DEE than in DOL/DME
(Figures S15 and S16). Thus, the enriched TFSI−anion and
F4DEE in the Li+ solvation shell give rise to LiF-rich SEI with
the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte. This LiF-rich SEI enables dense
Li deposition morphology, which plays a critical role in
achieving high Li plating/stripping CE (Note S3, Figure 2d).35
Moreover, the moderate LiPS solubility in DOL/F4DEE
enhances the Li anode cyclability because it confines the
concentration of corrosive LiPS in the electrolyte.36 The weak

■WEAK LIPS SOLVATION OF DOL/F4DEE
INFLUENCES THE LIPS THERMODYNAMIC
CONVERSION
Another grand challenge in Li−S batteries is to facilitate a fast,
reversible redox reaction on the sulfur cathode. The influence
of the Li+ and Li anode on the overall kinetics is generally
negligible (Note S3), which also aligns with literature results.37
Here we examined the effect of LiPS solvation on cathode
performance by using DOL/DME, DOL/TTE, and DOL/
F4DEE electrolytes. The galvanostatic intermittent titration
technique (GITT) was employed in Li||carbon paper (CP)
coin cells with a 0.1 M Li2S6 catholyte. The first discharge of
the Li||CP cell (Figure 3a) exhibits two characteristic plateaus:
one at 2.1 V (stage II), corresponding to liquid−liquid
reduction of long-chain Li2Sx (x = 5−8), and the other at 1.8 V
(stage III), associated with the liquid−solid reduction of short-
chain Li2Sx (x = 2−4) to Li2S.39,40 Notably, the 1.8 V plateau
(Li2Sx to Li2S) is observed exclusively in Li||CP cells.38 This
arises because CP offers higher three-dimensional electronic
conductivity and a larger density of surface-active sites
compared to conventional carbon/sulfur (C/S) cathodes,
which are burdened with substantial insulating S and Li2S
within their electrode structure (Note S5). Despite these
structural differences, Li||CP and Li||C/S cells display
consistent thermodynamic and kinetic characteristics during
LiPS redox conversion. Thus, the LiPS redox thermodynamics
and kinetics can be elucidated by correlating the GITT
discharge voltage profiles of Li||CP cells with the established
Li−S redox pathways.
The conversion of LiPS species is correlated with LiPS
solvation by comparing the specific capacity delivered on each
stage (Figure 3a). The DOL/DME cell delivers 900 mAh g−1
on stage II, in sharp contrast to 500 and 400 mAh g−1 in the
DOL/F4DEE and DOL/TTE cells. Nevertheless, all three cells
similarly deliver 650 mAh g−1 on stage III. These results
unravel that the LiPS chain-shortening process with weak LiPS
solvation relies more on short-chain LiPS (liquid−solid)
reduction than long-chain LiPS (liquid−liquid) reduction. It
is reported that electrolytes with weaker LiPS solvation shift
LiPS disproportionation equilibrium toward the formation of
shorter-chain LiPS species that deliver lower specific capacity
during the reduction on stage II.24,41−43 Notably, the DOL/
F4DEE electrolyte partially compromised this part of capacity,
but it protects Li anode from more corrosive long-chain LiPS
species.44−46 On the other hand, Li||CP GITT shows similar
overpotential across various LiPS solvations (Figure S20a).
The overpotential increases only when the liquid−liquid LiPS
reduction (intrinsically fast, stage II) transits to liquid−solid
LiPS reduction (intrinsically sluggish, stage III), evidenced in
Figure 3a.
After GITT discharging, Li||CP cells were charged with
identical GITT steps to investigate how weak LiPS solvation in
the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte influences the Li−S charging
process. The charging voltage profile (Figure 3b) exhibits
voltage plateaus at 2.25 V (stage IV) and 2.35 V (stage V),
which can be attributed to the solid−liquid oxidation from
Li2S/Li2S2 to LiPS and liquid−solid oxidation from LiPS to
S8.40,47 Again, the similar overpotential across three electrolytes
validates the noncorrelation between LiPS solvation and LiPS
oxidation kinetics (Figure S20b). Thus, the capacity difference
in these electrolytes is mainly attributed to LiPS conversion
thermodynamics rather than kinetics. In this regard, our results

Similar to the discharging process, the specific capacity
values during charging reflect the influence of LiPS solvation
on the LiPS oxidation conversion. Strong LiPS solvation
promotes solid−liquid oxidation, evidenced by the highest
stage IV specific capacity in DOL/DME (Figure 3b).
Nevertheless, the specific capacity of DOL/DME on stage V
is almost the same as those of DOL/F4DEE and DOL/TTE
despite more LiPS generated on stage IV. This indicates the
liquid−solid oxidation on stage V becomes unfavorable with
strong LiPS solvation. In other words, the concentration of
long-chain LiPS preserved in the DOL/DME electrolyte is
significant even at the end of charge, which agrees with the
previous reports.48,49 These unconverted long-chain LiPS
negatively impact the Li−S battery performance from several
aspects. First, they result in a capacity loss in the following
cycle. Li anode cyclability is also impacted because the
shuttling LiPS species triggers parasitic corrosion reactions.
Moreover, the retarded LiPS-S8 oxidation accelerates the
irreversible consumption of LiNO3.50,51 This is unfavorable for
Li−S long-term cycling because LiNO3 is crucial in SEI
formation and catalyzing LiPS oxidation.52 Taking these into
consideration, weak LiPS solvation in DOL/F4DEE benefits
the Li−S battery cycle life despite partially compromised
specific capacity.
In general, the weak LiPS solvation in DOL/F4DEE
manifests remarkable influence on LiPS redox conversion
(Figure S21), leading to the trade-off between capacity and cell
cycle life. Although DOL/F4DEE delivers lower capacity
because of limited liquid−liquid reduction (stage II) and
retarded solid−liquid oxidation (on stage IV), it leverages
more on liquid−solid reduction (on stage III) and promotes
liquid−solid oxidation (on stage V) to offset the capacity.
Notably, its weak LiPS solvation suppresses the formation of
corrosive long-chain LiPS and boosts the reversibility of sulfur
active material, leading to an enhanced Li−S cycle life (Figure
3c).
■MODERATE LIPS SOLUBILITY IN DOL/F4DEE
ENABLES MODERATE CATHODE REDOX KINETICS
In addition to LiPS solvation, the electrolyte also determines
the soluble LiPS concentration in regular Li−S batteries with
carbon/sulfur (C/S) composite cathodes. Electrolytes with
higher LiPS solubility allow more LiPS to be dissolved (Figure
S22), which substantially influences sulfur redox reactions. To
clarify the effect of LiPS solubility on sulfur cathode kinetics,
we performed a series of Li||CP GITT experiments using
catholytes with identical solvents but different LiPS concen-
trations (Figure 4a). The discharge profiles reveal that the
specific capacity decreases with increasing LiPS concentration
(Figure S23), likely due to the limited number of active
reaction sites on the CP surface. Nevertheless, cells with higher
LiPS concentrations constantly exhibit smaller GITT over-
potentials, indicating a clear correlation between elevated LiPS
concentration and accelerated Li−S redox kinetics (Figure
S24). A similar trend is observed during subsequent GITT
charging, although with significantly lower overpotentials
(Figure S25).
The overpotential during GITT discharge was further
deconvoluted to clarify the influence of LiPS solubility on
different interfacial electrochemical processes. Following the
reported method,37 the overall overpotential (ηtotal) was
separated into ohmic polarization (ηohm, bulk electrolyte
resistance), activation polarization (ηac, electrode/electrolyte
interfacial charge transfer), and concentration polarization
(ηcon, LiPS diffusion) (Figure S26). Among these, ηcon was
found to dominate ηtotal. For example, at 70% depth of
discharge (DOD) (a typical state of the liquid−solid
conversion) in Li||CP GITT with DOL/DME catholytes,
ηcon increased from 177.7 mV at 0.4 M Li2S6 to 246.7 mV at

■EXTENDED LI−S CYCLE LIFE USING THE
DOL/F4DEE ELECTROLYTE
The benefits of the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte on Li−S
electrochemical performance were evaluated in Li−S batteries
with optimized LiNO3 additive concentration (Figure S28).
GITT experiments in Li||C/S cells with practical C/S cathodes
further confirmed that the sulfur cathode kinetics is strongly
governed by LiPS solubility. Consistent with conventional Li−
S mechanism,38 the GITT discharge profile (Figure 5a)
exhibits two plateaus: one at 2.3 V (stage I, S8 reduction to
soluble LiPS) and the other at 2.1 V (stage II, soluble LiPS
reduction to insoluble LiPS and Li2S). Among the tested
electrolytes, DOL/DME delivers the highest discharge capacity
(Figure S29), reflecting enhanced liquid−liquid LiPS con-
version due to strong LiPS solvation. By contrast, higher GITT
overpotentials were observed as the LiPS solubility decreased
(Figure S30). Notably, DOL/F4DEE outperforms DOL/TTE
in LiPS redox kinetics due to its higher LiPS solubility.
Furthermore, deconvolution of the GITT overpotential
confirmed that concentration polarization dominates the
overall redox kinetics (Figure S31). Taken together, these
Li||C/S GITT results, consistent with the earlier Li||CP
experiments using varied Li2S6 concentrations, underscore
the advantage of moderate LiPS solubility in DOL/F4DEE for
enhancing sulfur cathode kinetics.
Moreover, the benefit of the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte on the
Li−S cell long-term cycling is revealed. Figure 5b shows the
voltage profiles at 0.05 and 0.1 C (1 C = 1200 mAh g−1). The
DOL/F4DEE cell, similar to the DOL/DME one, typically
exhibits two well-defined plateaus, indicating sufficient sulfur
utilization owing to its moderate LiPS solubility. On the
contrary, DOL/TTE with poor LiPS solubility results in a
depressed second discharge plateau with reduced specific
capacity. The trade-off between cycle life and specific capacity
is observed from the long-term cycling at 0.1 C (Figure 5d,
Figure S32). Strong Li+ solvation and strong LiPS solvation in
DOL/DME trigger poor sulfur reversibility and severe Li
anode degradation, resulting in quick capacity fading after only
55 cycles. This corresponds to the decoupled impacts of long-
chain LiPS corrosion and low CE due to the poor Li SEI.

DOL/TTE continuously suffers from poor sulfur active
material utilization due to low LiPS solubility, as revealed
from its slow capacity activation and overpotential recovery
(Figure S33). DOL/F4DEE stabilizes the Li anode with
moderate Li+ solvation and weak LiPS solvation, while
maintaining sufficient cathode kinetics owing to its moderate
LiPS solubility. These attributes contribute to a highly
prolonged Li−S cycle life of over 180 cycles.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of these cells
after 100 cycles provides further evidence of the benefits of
DOL/F4DEE (Figure 5c, Figure S34). DOL/F4DEE and
DOL/DME facilitate sulfur redox kinetics owing to LiPS
solubility advantages, reflected by a smaller charge transfer
resistance (Rct). On the other hand, the smaller SEI resistance
(RSEI) in DOL/F4DEE and DOL/TTE than DOL/DME
indicates the benefit of weakened Li+ and LiPS solvation on Li
anode. The drastic increase of Rbulk in DOL/DME (which may
come from electrolyte dry-out9) further proves its poor Li
cyclability. Overall, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte balances
sulfur cathode kinetics and lithium anode stability through
optimized Li+ and LiPS solubility and solvation, thereby
extending the cycle life of both Li−S coin cells and Li−S cells
under more demanding cycling conditions (Figures S35 and
S36). These results validate the effectiveness of our electrolyte
design by decoupling the LiTFSI/LiPS solubility and Li+/LiPS
solvation (Figure S37).
In summary, we present an electrolyte design strategy that
achieves a balance between enhanced lithium anode stability
and efficient sulfur redox kinetics. The DOL/F4DEE electro-
lyte, with moderate Li+ solvation, promotes the formation of a
fluorine-rich SEI layer, effectively regulating lithium stripping
and plating. Its weak LiPS solvation suppresses the formation
of corrosive long-chain polysulfides, contributing to an
improved anode stability and enhanced sulfur cathode
reversibility. Additionally, the electrolyte’s moderate LiPS
solubility minimizes concentration polarization, enabling
favorable sulfur redox kinetics. These combined features lead
to an extended cycle life for Li−S batteries. By decoupling the
solubility and solvation effects of LiTFSI and LiPS, this work
advances our understanding of the Li−S battery mechanisms.
Furthermore, this demonstrates the potential of partially
fluorinated solvents in electrolyte design, offering a promising
pathway for the development of next-generation Li−S battery
electrolytes.
