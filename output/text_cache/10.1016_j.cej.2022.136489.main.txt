Improving the electrochemical performance of lithium-sulfur batteries by 
interface modification with a bifunctional electrolyte additive

A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries with sulfur cathode and lithium anode are considered as a promising candidate in 
the next generation of high specific energy secondary batteries. However, the scalable application of Li-S bat­
teries is limited by the unstable electrode/electrolyte interfaces. Herein, a novel bifunctional electrolyte additive, 
trifluoromethane sulfonamide (TFMSA), is introduced to stabilize both electrode/electrolyte interfaces to 
enhance the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries. The TFMSA molecule can not only alleviate the 
passivation of the sulfur cathode surface, but also suppress Li dendrites growth and prevent the lithium anode 
from the corrosion of polysulfides. As a result, the assembled Li-S battery with TFMSA-containing electrolyte 
displays excellent long-cycle performance and rate capability. Moreover, the Li||Li symmetric cell using TFMSA 
electrolyte can stably cycle for 1200 h at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2. The Li-S pouch cell with TFMSA also 
exhibits outstanding performance, indicating the prospect of practical application. This work sheds light on an 
innovative idea for interface modification via an electrolyte additive to overcome the inherent issues of Li-S 
batteries.   
1. Introduction 
With the blowout growth of portable electronic devices and large- 
scale energy storage technology, conventional commercial lithium-ion 
batteries (LIBs) are limited by relatively low energy density, which 
restricted their further development [1–3]. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batte­
ries benefit from the two-electron reaction between sulfur and lithium, 
leading to high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and high 
theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) [4–7]. Elemental sulfur as a 
prospective cathode is abundant in the crust, low in price, and envi­
ronmentally friendly [8]. In addition, lithium metal is an attractive 
anode material with its high theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh 
g−1) and low redox potential (-3.04 V vs. NHE) [9–11]. Therefore, Li-S 
batteries are considered as one of the most promising battery systems 
in the next generation of high specific energy secondary batteries. 
Despite the considerable advantages of Li-S batteries, there are also 
some barriers that restrain their practical application [12–14]. For 
instance, the electrode passivation caused by the poor conductivity of 
the active substance sulfur (S) and its final discharge product lithium 
sulfide (Li2S) is one of the key obstacles [15]. In particular, the existence 
of insoluble and insulating Li2S slows down the reaction kinetics of the 
second discharge platform process and reduces the capacity provided by 
this reaction accounted for three-quarters of the whole process, but such 
concerns have rarely been addressed in current literature [16,17]. 
Moreover, the polysulfides produced by intermediate reaction trigger 
the shuttling effect due to their high solubility in the ether electrolyte, 
finally resulting in the loss of active substances [18,19]. And the poly­
sulfides shuttle to the side of the lithium anode, which erodes the Li 
metal and causes undesirable side reactions, leading to the shortening of 
cyclic life and the decrease of coulombic efficiency (CE) [20–22]. 
To resolve the above issues, many effective techniques from different 
aspects have been proposed, including the rational design of sulfur 
cathode, the surface protection of lithium anode, the optimization of 
electrolyte, and the improvement of separator [23–26]. Among these 
strategies, electrolyte modification is a feasible and cost-efficient 
method to stabilize the electrode/electrolyte interfaces. By introducing

However, most of the current efforts have only addressed the problem of 
a single electrode. To further enhance the electrochemical characteris­
tics of Li-S batteries, finding a suitable electrolyte additive that can 
simultaneously improve the interfacial stability on both sulfur cathode 
and lithium anode is the most promising solution. 
In this regard, we proposed a novel bifunctional electrolyte additive, 
trifluoromethane sulfonamide (TFMSA), to ameliorate both electrode/ 
electrolyte interfaces, enhancing the cyclic stability of Li-S batteries. On 
the sulfur cathode side, TFMSA can interact with Li2S, which accelerates 
the decomposition of insulating Li2S deposited on the cathode, 
improving the reaction kinetics of the cells. On the lithium anode side, 
the LiF-rich SEI film derived from TFMSA can effectively inhibit the 
growth of undesirable Li dendrites and prevent the lithium anode from 
attacking by polysulfides. Hence, the assembled Li-S cell with TFMSA- 
containing electrolyte delivers a discharge capacity of 700.7 mAh g−1 
after 350 cycles at 0.5C with an average decay of 0.095% per cycle, 
exhibiting superb cyclic stability. Besides, the Li||Li symmetric cell using 
1% TFMSA electrolyte can stably cycle for 1200 h with a low over- 
potential of 20.1 mV at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2. TFMSA is also 
successfully applied in the Li-S pouch battery with a capacity retention 
of 88% after 50 cycles even under harsh conditions of high sulfur loading 
and low electrolyte/sulfur ratio. This work opens a new pathway for 
developing electrolyte additives to achieve excellent cyclic performance 
of Li-S batteries through interface modification. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation of sulfur cathode and electrolytes 
The sulfur-carbon composite was synthesized by the melt-diffusion 
method. The porous carbon materials in our experiment were pre­
pared based on our previously reported work [32]. The sublimated 
sulfur and the above porous carbon materials were uniformly mixed in 
the mass ratio of 75:25, and then carbon disulfide (CS2) was added as a 
dispersant. The mixture was moved to a muffle furnace and heated to 
160 ◦C at a rate of 5 ◦C min−1, then the sulfur-carbon composite was 
obtained. 
After that, the sulfur-carbon cathode was prepared by a slurry casting 
method. Sulfur-carbon composite, acetylene black and polyvinylidene 
fluoride (PVDF) were mixed at a mass ratio of 7:2:1 (91:4:5 for high 
sulfur loading) and dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) to 
form a well-mixed slurry. The slurry was coated on an aluminum foil 
(collector) and dried at 45 ◦C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. The sulfur- 
carbon cathode was subsequently cut into disks with a diameter of 13 
mm by a tablet press. The areal loading of active sulfur was around 1.2 
mg cm−2 (5.2 mg cm−2 for high sulfur loading). 
The baseline electrolyte is composed of 1 M lithium bis (tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) and 2.0 wt% LiNO3 in the solvent 
of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)/1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (v/v = 1:1). 
Different mass fractions (0.5 wt%, 1 wt%, 2 wt%) of trifluoromethane 
sulfonamide (Aladdin, > 98%) were introduced in the baseline elec­
trolyte, respectively and then stirred for 24 h to obtain 0.5%, 1%, and 
2% TFMSA electrolytes. All the above operations were done in an argon- 
filled glove box with oxygen and water content levels controlled below 
0.01 ppm.

2.3. Theoretical calculations 
Density functional theory (DFT) was utilized to optimize the mo­
lecular initial structure at the B3LYP/6–31 + G(d) level with Gaussian16 
software. To determine each optimized fixed point, the frequency 
analysis was carried out under the same basis set. The dispersion 
correction of Grimme D3 formula (B3LYP-D3) was included in the 
optimization, and the electron energy was corrected by a larger basic 
group of cc-pVTZ. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. The interaction between TFMSA and Li2S in lithium-sulfur batteries 
To explore the promotion effect of TFMSA on the dissolution of Li2S 
in the ether electrolyte. 4.0 mg of Li2S was added to 2.0 mL baseline 
electrolyte and 1% TFMSA electrolyte, respectively, and stirred for 24 h 
(Fig. 1a). It can be observed that Li2S was completely dissolved in the 
TFMSA-containing electrolyte. However, the baseline electrolyte 
showed a turbid suspension, and Li2S was hardly dissolved. These ex­
periments demonstrate that TFMSA can enhance the dissolution of Li2S 
in the ether electrolyte. The interaction between TFMSA and Li2S is 
explored using models based on DFT, and the calculation results are 
shown in Fig. 1b. The Li-S bond length in Li2S is 2.101 Å (Fig. 1b-1), and 
the N–H bond length in TFMSA is 1.017 Å (Fig. 1b-2). When Li2S and 
TFMSA co-exist together, the system will be optimized in the direction of 
the most stable state, and the optimized result is shown in Fig. 1b-3. The 
mutual attraction of TFMSA and Li2S leads to the extension of the N–H 
bond (the distance between N–H becomes 1.091 Å) and the Li-S bond is 
elongated to 2.185 Å. The calculations of the above theoretical model 
show that TFMSA can interact with Li2S, accelerating the decomposition 
of Li2S and improving the reaction kinetics. To further understand the 
solubilization effect of TFMSA on Li2S, Raman spectroscopy was

3.2. Effect of TFMSA on the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries 
The electrochemical properties of Li-S batteries with the baseline 
electrolyte and the TFMSA-containing electrolyte are exhibited in Fig. 2. 
CV curves of Li-S batteries with different electrolytes are displayed in 
Fig. 2a and Fig. 2b, respectively. The cell polarization is larger at the 
beginning, so CV for the third cycle is chosen for discussion. In the 
baseline electrolyte, it can be seen that the cathodic reduction process 
shows two cathodic characteristic peaks at 2.01 V and 2.29 V, corre­
sponding to the reduction of sulfur to Sn
2- (4 ≤n ≤8) and the conversion 
of long-chain polysulfides to short-chain Li2S2 and Li2S, respectively 
[36,37]. During the anodic oxidation segment, the peak at 2.38 V is 
attributed to the oxidation of Li2S2/Li2S to polysulfides and the peak at 
2.44 V is assigned to the conversion of long-chain polysulfides to sulfur 
[38]. Apparently, after adding 1% TFMSA into the electrolyte, the 
cathodic reduction peaks move to 2.02 V and 2.31 V, respectively, and 
the anodic oxidation peaks negatively shift to 2.35 V and 2.42 V, 
respectively. The potential gap between the cathode and anode peaks in 
the baseline electrolyte is 430 mV, while in 1% TFMSA electrolyte, it is 
only 400 mV, illustrating that the TFMSA additive is beneficial to reduce 
the electrochemical polarization during the operation of the Li-S cells. 
This can be attributed to the fact that the insulating discharge products 
(Li2S) deposited on the cathode surface are dissolved by TFMSA, which 
effectively alleviates the passivation of the sulfur cathode surface and 
accelerates the electron transfer. To investigate the effect of TFMSA on 
the reaction kinetics of Li-S batteries, Tafel curves derived from CV are 
exhibited in Fig. S2. The Tafel slope in the baseline electrolyte is 93.7 
mV dec−1, which decreases to 83.6 mV dec−1 after adding 1% TFMSA, 
implying that the reaction kinetics of the Li-S cells are improved after the 
addition of TMFSA [39]. Comparing the first charge curves of cells with 
different electrolytes, it can be observed that TFMSA can reduce the

To estimate the cyclic stability of the Li-S batteries within baseline 
and TFMSA-containing electrolytes, different mass fractions of TFMSA 
(0.5%, 1%, 2%) electrolytes were prepared. The batteries were carried 
out at 0.5C (1C = 1675 mAh g−1), as displayed in Fig. S6. It shows that 
the cyclic performance of the Li-S batteries with TFMSA has been 
boosted significantly, especially the battery using 1% TFMSA. This may 
be ascribed to the balance between positive effect (dissolution of insu­
lating Li2S and protection of Li anode) and possible negative effect, such 
as big changes on the physicochemical properties of the electrolyte it­
self. That is to say, 1% is a moderate concentration, when the concen­
tration goes lower, the positive effect may be not that notable, and when 
the concentration goes higher, the negative effect becomes remarkable. 
So it is chosen as the optimized content for further study. The initial 
discharge capacity of the cell with 1% TFMSA electrolyte is 1049 mAh 
g−1 (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, after 350 cycles, the capacity maintains 
700.7 mAh g−1 with an average decay of 0.095% per cycle, and the CE is 
still 98.7%. In contrast, the capacity of the cell without TFMSA remains 
only 521.5 mAh g−1 and the CE drops to 97.5% after 350 cycles. The 
charge and discharge curves at different cycles are shown in Fig. S7. 
Obviously, the addition of 1% TFMSA prominently enhances the cyclic 
stability and the CE of Li-S batteries. When the cycling rate is elevated to 
2C, the capacity of the cell using the baseline electrolyte shows only 
497.4 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of 61.3% and the CE of 97.5% 
after 200 cycles. While at the same condition, the cell with 1% TFMSA 
still exhibits a high capacity of 601.2 mAh g−1 with 74.8% capacity 
retention and 98.1% CE (Fig. 3b). To further investigate the effect of 
TFMSA on the rate performance of Li-S batteries, the cells containing 
these two electrolytes were tested at different rates, as depicted in 
Fig. 3c. The cell using 1% TFMSA electrolyte can deliver high capacities 
of 1034.4 (third discharge), 950.8, 903.6, 886.1 and 846.3 mAh g−1 at 
0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1 and 2C, respectively. The reversible capacity can still 
reach a high capacity of 1014.8 mAh g−1 with almost no capacity 
degradation when the rate is restored to 0.1C. However, the cell without 
TFMSA only exhibits the capacities of 1053.2, 919.6, 852.5, 806.9, and 
707.2 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2C, respectively. When the rate is 
returned to 0.1C, the capacity is only 897.6 mAh g−1, which is far from 
the original level. In summary, the addition of 1% TFMSA does enhance 
the rate capability of Li-S batteries.

relatively high capacity of 828.8 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles. These results

3.3. Effect of TFMSA on the sulfur cathode 
According to the results discussed above, TFMSA can increase the 
solubility of Li2S in the ether electrolyte, the morphologies of the sulfur 
cathodes and the composition of sulfur-containing substances are intu­
itively acquired by SEM and XPS, respectively. The sulfur particles are 
irregularly distributed on the surface of the fresh cathode (Fig. S10). 
After 350 cycles with the baseline electrolyte at 0.5C, a lot of large and 
coarse discharge products cover the cathode surface, causing severe 
passivation of the surface and impeding charge transfer, which further 
leads to the rapid decay of capacity (Fig. 4a-b). When adding 1% TFMSA 
into the electrolyte, the insoluble Li2S deposited on the cathode surface 
is dissolved, which provides more reaction sites for polysulfides con­
version, accelerates the reaction kinetics, and improves the utilization of 
active material (Fig. 4c-d). Based on the EDS elemental mapping results 
(Fig. S11), the S content on the cathode surface in the TFMSA electrolyte 
is less than that in the baseline electrolyte, which may be attributed to 
the dissolution of Li2S into the ether electrolyte with TFMSA. Besides, 
XPS is applied to investigate the chemical composition of the sulfur- 
containing species on the surface of the sulfur cathode (Fig. 4e). In the 
S 2p spectra, the peaks located at 161.3 and 162.1 eV paired with the 
peaks at 162.8 and 163.5 eV are corresponding to Li2S and Li2S2, 
respectively [42,43]. Compared with the cell cycled in the baseline 
electrolyte, the peaks intensity of Li2S and Li2S2 are reduced to some 
extent after adding TFMSA. And the atomic mass fraction of S in the 
cycled cathode with 1% TFMSA electrolyte is 3.69%, which is lower 
than that in the baseline electrolyte (5.51%). It indicates that the 
addition of TFMSA promotes the dissolution of insulating discharge 
products, which alleviates the problem of passivation of the sulfur 
cathode surface. This is consistent with the SEM results described above 
(Fig. 4a–d). Two fitted peaks at 164.3 and 165.3 eV are assigned to the 
incomplete transformation elemental S [44]. In addition, the peaks 
located at 166.2 and 167.4 eV are derived from thiosulfate, which is the 
side reaction product of the oxidation of polysulfides by LiNO3. Mean­
while, the lithium salt partially decomposes on the cathode surface to 
form the S-O bond, which is reflected in the position of 168.2 and 169.4 
eV [45,46]. It is easy to find that the peak intensities of the by-products 
on the sulfur cathode containing 1% TFMSA electrolyte all decrease, 
which suggests that TFMSA stabilizes the interface between cathode and 
electrolyte and reduces the occurrence of side reactions. 
3.4. Effect of TFMSA on the Li metal anode 
Li metal anode (LMA), as an important component of Li-S batteries, 
plays a vital role in the battery performance. To investigate the pro­
tective effect of TFMSA on the LMA, the Li||Li symmetric cells were 
assembled with the baseline and 1% TFMSA electrolytes. The cells are 
activated at a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 0.1 mAh 
cm−2 for 5 cycles, and then charge and discharge at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 
mAh cm−2 (Fig. 5a). The Li||Li symmetric cell with the baseline elec­
trolyte exhibited an over-potential of 20.4 mV and the voltage increased 
sharply cycling for 550 h. In contrast, the symmetric cell within 1% 
TFMSA could stably cycle for over 1200 h with an over-potential of 20.1 
mV. The remarkable improvement of cycle life reveals that the TFMSA 
additive is helpful to stabilize plating and stripping of lithium. 
Based on the previous analysis, it is proved that TFMSA provides a 
positive effect on the cycling life of Li||Li symmetric batteries and the CE 
of Li-S batteries. To further study the mechanism of TFMSA on the LMA, 
molecular orbital energies of TFMSA and solvents were calculated. The 
LUMO of TFMSA was obtained by DFT calculations to be −0.91 V, which 
is lower than that of DOL (−0.31 V) and DME (−0.18 V), indicating that 
TFMSA will be preferentially reduced at the lithium anode compared 
with DOL and DME (Fig. S12a). The preferential reduction of TFMSA 
will facilitate the formation of a stable LiF-rich SEI film on the LMA. CV

deposition of Li+ flux but also has a good mechanical strength to sup­

the formed LiF-rich SEI film effectively separates the electrolyte and the 
lithium anode, making the LMA free from the corrosion of polysulfides 
and reducing the adverse reactions at the interface. In the S 2p spectra,

Based on the discussion above, the schematic diagram of the mech­
anism for the Li-S battery with the TFMSA electrolyte is uncovered in 
Fig. 6. For one thing, the amide hydrogen (N–H) in TFMSA can interact 
strongly with S2- in Li2S through intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which 
allows Li2S deposited on the cathode surface to dissolve into the elec­
trolyte, improving the cathode/electrolyte interface and boosting the 
reaction kinetics of Li-S batteries. For another, the TFMSA-containing 
electrolyte produces a dense SEI film rich in LiF. This robust SEI film 
not only significantly impedes the growth of Li dendrites, but also 
effectively blocks the corrosion of lithium anode by polysulfides, which 
avoids the interfacial side reactions, improving the cyclic life and CE of 
Li-S cells. In consequence, the electrochemical performance of Li-S 
batteries is greatly enhanced by the TFMSA additive. 
4. Conclusions 
In summary, TFMSA is firstly proposed as a bifunctional additive to 
significantly enhance the electrochemical capabilities of Li-S batteries 
by improving the interfaces between electrodes and electrolytes. We 
found that this molecule can promote the dissolution of insulating 
products deposited on the surface of the sulfur cathode into the elec­
trolyte through intermolecular hydrogen bonds with Li2S, improving the 
utilization of the active material and the reaction kinetics of Li-S 
batteries. Besides, the robust SEI film composed of LiF suppresses the 
growth of Li dendrites and reduces the adverse reactions of polysulfides 
with lithium anode. Compared with the previous literature (Table S2), it 
also displays the outstanding cyclic performance and rate capacity. In 
addition, the TFMSA-containing cells have higher CE than that of 
baseline cells, which can be cycled stably for 1200 h with a low over- 
potential of 20.1 mV at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2. Moreover, the 
Li-S pouch cells with TFMSA exhibit superb performance as well as high 
energy density. Overall, this work provides in-depth insight for the 
practical application to improve the electrochemical performance of Li-S 
batteries via a bifunctional additive, which optimizes the electrode/ 
electrolyte interfaces.
