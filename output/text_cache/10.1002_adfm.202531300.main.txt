ABSTRACT
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a promising energy storage technology owing to their high energy density and
environmental compatibility. However, their practical implementation has been hindered by the growth of lithium dendrites
and the accumulation of inactive sulfur species on lithium anode surfaces. NH4I is introduced as a bifunctional electrolyte
additive to address these issues. Both theoretical and experimental analyses demonstrate that NH4
+ coordinates with solvent
molecules, reduces the desolvation energy barrier of Li+, and promotes uniform lithium deposition. Furthermore, during cycling,
the reversible I−/I3
−redox couple reacts with inert lithium sulfides (Li2S/Li2S2), dissolving them into the electrolyte to alleviate
interfacial issues and reduce irreversible active material loss. Consequently, the NH4I-containing electrolyte enables Li-S batteries
to achieve a high reversible capacity of 1097.78 mAh g−1, and the corresponding capacity retention ration increases from 62.43% to
80.02% after 100 cycles at 0.5C. Moreover, when cycling at 1C for 500 cycles, the capacity attenuation per cycle was only 0.067%.
This work gives a novel electrolyte design strategy for long life Li-S batteries.

Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered as a highly promis-
ing next-generation energy storage devices, owing to their high
theoretical energy density, environmental compatibility, and
low prices [1–4]. However, the practical commercialization is
hampered by several inherent challenges. A primary challenge
originates from the electronic insulation exhibited by both the dis-
charge (Li2S) and charge (S8) products, the accumulation of these
species on the conductive substrate impedes electron transfer,
leading to the formation of dead sulfur [5–7]. Furthermore, during
cycling, soluble lithium polysulfide intermediates are generated,
which diffuse through the electrolyte toward the lithium anode.
The electrode surface is corroded, and uneven lithium deposition
is promoted, accelerating dendrite growth [8]. These processes
collectively result in rapid capacity decay and compromised
safety. Recent investigation efforts have been directed toward
cathode host design [9–11], separator modification [12–14], and
the construction of artificial solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI)
layers [15]. These strategies predominantly rely on physical or
chemical confinement mechanisms. However, these strategies
often lead to a reduction in overall energy density and fail to

[16]. Consequently, the development of approaches that can
simultaneously reclaim both types of inactive species without
sacrificing energy density represents a critical research direction
for advancing practical Li-S batteries.
As a simple and economical approach, incorporating electrolyte
additives has been a key driver behind the critical advancements
in Li-S battery technology [17]. The redox kinetics of sulfur species
are enhanced through this strategy, while a protective layer is
concurrently formed on the lithium anode, this layer serves to
shield the anode and suppress dendrite formation [18, 19]. For
instance, Joanne et al. developed a diketopyrrolopyrrole-based
additive with a redox potential lying within the electrochemical
window of sulfur [20], this property enabled it to function
as a redox mediator, which suppresses the polysulfide shuttle
effect and enhances the cycle stability of batteries. However,
this strategy does not address lithium dendrite formation on
the anode, leaving safety concerns unresolved. Recently, Zhang
et al. reported a strategy employing the ionic liquid TBAI3
[21], this approach was used to reconstruct the Li+ solvation
structure and establish an I−/I3
−redox shuttle mechanism,
enabling the cyclic reactivation of inactive sulfur. Consequently,
the utilization efficiency of insoluble Li2S was substantially
enhanced, and the battery cycle life was extended. Nevertheless,
the incorporation of long-chain iodides inevitably increases
electrolyte viscosity, potentially compromising ion transport
kinetics. Thus, devising novel additives that simultaneously
stabilize the lithium anode, improve sulfur utilization, and ensure
electrolyte stability continues to be a pivotal yet challenging
goal.
Herein, NH4I was introduced as a multifunctional additive into
the standard LiTFSI/LiNO3 in DOL/DME electrolyte, leveraging
I−/I3
−redox couples and NH4
+ to achieve synergistic improve-
ments. NH4I was selected due to its unique combination of
properties: unlike other ammonium salts, it minimally affects
electrolyte viscosity while its NH4
+ ions promote polysulfide
conversion via hydrogen bonding. Additionally, the I−/I3
−redox
couple offers superior kinetics over other halogen redox sys-
tems (Br2/Br−, Cl2/Cl−) [22]. As illustrated in Figure 1, I3
−
effectively oxidizes insoluble Li2S/Li2S2 deposits on the lithium
anode into soluble long-chain lithium polysulfides (LiPSs).
Meanwhile, the assistance of I−/I3
−redox couple induces a
uniform Li2S deposition. During charging above 2.89 V, the
resulting I−diffuses toward the sulfur cathode driven by a
concentration gradient and is reoxidized to I3
−, establishing
an efficient redox mediator cycle. Simultaneously, Theoreti-
cal simulations and spectroscopic analyses confirmed that a
competitive solvation between NH4
+ and Li+. This interac-
tion promotes greater anion participation in the Li+ solvation
shell, significantly reducing the desolvation energy barriers.
Consequently, a stable inorganic-rich solid electrolyte inter-
phase (SEI) primarily composed of LiF and Li3N forms on
the lithium anode, enabling uniform lithium deposition and
suppressed dendrite growth. Benefiting from these catalytic
effects, the assembled Li-S batteries demonstrated enhanced
sulfur utilization with an initial discharge capacity of 1097.78
mAh g−1 and stable cycling over 300 cycles at 0.5C, confirming
the application potential of NH4I in high cycle stability Li-S
batteries.

Results and Discussion
To reveal the influence of NH4I incorporation on the solvation
structure of Li+ in the electrolyte, we performed molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations to probe the Li+ primary solvation
sheath (Figure 2a–d and Figure S1). In the blank electrolyte,
Li+ coordination numbers (CNs) are 2.17 for DOL and 0.45 for
DME. NH4I addition altered this configuration, reducing DOL
and DME CNs to 2.14 and 0.34, respectively, while enabling Li+-
I−coordination (CN = 0.12). Analysis of the NH4
+ solvation
sheath revealed predominant DOL/DME coordination (Figure
S2), indicating competitive solvation with Li+ for NH4
+ [23,
24]. This competition facilitates Li+ de-solvation, suggesting
enhanced interfacial reactivity and accelerated reaction kinetics.
Collectively, NH4
+ reduces Li+ solvent coordination while pro-
moting Li+-I−interaction, thereby improving interfacial kinetics
and homogenizing lithium deposition. Raman spectroscopy cor-
roborated these findings (Figure 2e and Figures S3 and S4). Peaks
at 847.28 cm−1 (free DME) and 870.89 cm−1 (coord DME) are
observed in the blank electrolyte [21, 25]. Upon the addition of
NH4I, Li+ coordinated DME decreases while free DME increases.
Solvent related peaks exhibit red shifts [26], the DOL peak shifts
slightly from 938.39 cm−1 (blank) to 935.46 cm−1 (with NH4I),
indicating a weakened Li+-DOL interaction. A distinct peak
appears at 402 cm−1, corresponding to Li-I coordination. As the
blank electrolyte also exhibits a peak at this position, Raman
measurements were performed on each individual electrolyte
component (Figure S4). A shoulder peak of LiTFSI was observed
at 400 cm−1. To verify whether I−enters the Li+ solvation shell,
the intensity of free DME was compared between the blank
electrolyte and the NH4I-containing electrolyte. The relative
proportions of coordinated Li-I are 27.14% in the blank electrolyte
and 45.26% in the NH4I-containing electrolyte (Table S1). These
results confirm that I−participates in the Li+ solvation shell [27].
Overall, these spectral changes confirm NH4I induced weaken-
ing of Li+ solvent coordination, consistent with computational
results.
To investigate the effect of NH4I on the lithium metal anode, Li||Li
and Li||Cu cells were fabricated. The Li+ transference number
(𝑡𝐿𝑖+) was first measured in Li||Li cells for both electrolytes. As
shown in Figure 2f, the electrolyte containing NH4I exhibits
a 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ of 0.77, significantly higher than the value of 0.63 for
the blank electrolyte (Figure 2g). In addition, the impedance
of Li||Li symmetric cells with the two electrolytes was mea-
sured across a temperature range of 0–80◦C (Figure S5). The
cells containing NH4I electrolyte exhibited lower impedance
than those with the blank electrolyte. Using the Arrhenius
equation (Equation S2), the interfacial activation energy (Ea)
for Li+ desolvation was derived. As illustrated in Figure S6,
the Ea value for the NH4I-containing electrolyte was 43.4 kJ
mol−1, lower than that of the blank electrolyte (47.0 kJ mol−1),
indicating that NH4I effectively reduces the energy barrier for
Li+ desolvation [28]. These results align well with theoretical
calculations. The enhanced 𝑡𝐿𝑖+ indicates improved lithium-
ion charge transfer, which reduces polarization and promotes
uniform lithium deposition [29]. Simultaneously, cycling tests at
1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 revealed that Li||Li cells with NH4I
maintained stable operation for 2000 h with a low overpotential of
15 mV (Figure 2h). Conversely, the blank cells exhibited a rapidly

The compatibility of NH4I-containing electrolyte with lithium
metal anodes was evaluated by examining lithium deposi-
tion morphologies using in situ optical microscopy. Contin-
uous plating at 4.0 mA cm−2 revealed distinct differences
between blank and modified electrolytes (Figure S8). After
20–30 min, the blank electrolyte produced loose, inhomoge-
neous lithium deposits with high surface roughness. In con-
trast, the NH4I electrolyte yielded thinner, densely packed
deposits with markedly smoother surfaces. Even after extended
plating (50 min), the NH4I electrolyte effectively suppressed
lithium dendrite formation. Electrochemical Impedance Spec-
troscopy (EIS) measurements of Li||Li symmetric cells before
and after cycling further confirmed that the internal resistance
remained consistently lower in the NH4I-containing electrolyte
than in the blank electrolyte (Figure S9). This result indicates
enhanced interfacial stability and more uniform Li deposition
during cycling in the presence of NH4I [21]. Complemen-
tary scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of lithium
deposited at 1.0 and 3.0 mA cm−2 further confirmed that
NH4I promoted denser, more uniform lithium morphologies
on Cu current collectors, whereas the blank electrolyte con-
sistently yielded irregular deposits with prominent protrusions
(Figure S10).

To evaluate the corrosion behavior of the aluminum current
collector, foil samples were immersed for five days in four
electrolytes containing different NH4I concentrations (blank,
0.25%, 0.5%, 1%) and then retrieved for surface characterization.
As shown in Figure S14, the foil exposed to the 1% NH4I electrolyte
exhibits numerous corrosion-induced pits and a roughened sur-
face topography. In contrast, the foil immersed in the blank
electrolyte remains largely smooth and flat, with only minor
pitting attributable to LiTFSI induced corrosion [32]. The surfaces
of foils immersed in the 0.5% and 0.25% NH4I electrolytes
appear similarly smooth and closely resemble the morphology
observed in the blank electrolyte. These results indicate that at
concentrations of 0.25% and 0.5%, NH4I does not cause severe
corrosion of the aluminum foil.
Concentration optimization experiments (Figure S15) revealed
that insufficient NH4I addition led to limited catalytic effects
and negligible improvement in cycling life, whereas excessive
amounts aggravate the polysulfide shuttle effect and produce
excess I3
−, which corrodes the lithium anode and accelerates
capacity decay [33]. Electrochemical tests confirmed that the
optimal cycling stability was achieved with the electrolyte system
containing 0.5% NH4I. Tafel curves acquired from Li||Li symmet-
ric cells with different NH4I concentrations further confirmed
that the exchange-current density increases upon NH4I addi-
tion. The electrolyte containing 0.5% NH4I exhibits the highest
exchange-current density (0.1842 mA cm−2), which is markedly
higher than that of the blank electrolyte (0.1523 mA cm−2) (Figure
S16) [21]. Correspondingly, SEM images of cycled lithium anodes
reveal that the surface obtained with the 0.5% NH4I electrolyte
is the smoothest and shows the least accumulation of inactive
lithium (Figure S17). Based on these combined electrochemical
and morphological results, the 0.5% NH4I formulation was
selected for all subsequent tests.
To determine the potential for I−to I3
−conversion, CV tests
were conducted in a DME solution of NH4I using a Li||Pt dual-
electrode configuration. The results (Figure S18) indicate that
regenerating the I−/I3
−redox couple necessitates charging to
about 2.89 V. Therefore, we assembled a Li||stainless steel cell
and performed linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) to evaluate the
electrolyte’s high-voltage stability [21]. As shown in Figure S19a,
after NH4I addition, the current exceeds 10 µA cm−2 at about 3.31
V, indicating the onset of electrolyte decomposition. Thus, the
practical working window should remain below this potential.
Furthermore, an electrochemical float test conducted on a Li-
S battery between 2.7 and 3.6 V showed a pronounced leakage
current near 3.4 V, consistent with the LSV results (Figure
S19b) [34, 35]. Together, these measurements confirm that the
electrolyte system remains stable within the operating voltage
range required for sustained I−/I3
−redox cycling.
To assess cycling stability under extended voltage conditions, bat-
teries were assembled with blank electrolyte or NH4I-containing
electrolyte and cycled in two voltage windows (1.7–2.8 and
1.7–3.0 V). After 200 cycles, the battery with blank electrolyte
showed severe capacity fading in both windows (Figure S20).
By contrast, the NH4I-containing battery exhibited significantly
higher capacity retention when cycled to 3.0 V than to 2.8 V.
Both NH4I-based batteries consistently outperformed the control.
These results indicate that even without complete regeneration
of the I−/I3
−redox couple, NH4
+ contributes to improved cycling
stability. The observed capacity fade is primarily attributed to
the progressive accumulation of inactive sulfur species on the
lithium anode. Charge–discharge voltage profiles at various cycle
numbers are shown in Figure S21. The NH4I-containing battery
cycled in the 1.7–3.0 V window consistently displays a charging
plateau at approximately 2.89 V, corresponding to the oxidation
of I−to I3
−. By contrast, the NH4I-containing battery cycled in
the 1.7–2.8 V window lacks this plateau and behaves similarly to
the blank electrolyte battery. This confirms that I−/I3
−conversion
is incomplete at the lower cutoff voltage, which hinders the
continuous removal of dead sulfur and leads to its accumulation
on the anode, thereby degrading cycling stability. Based on this
analysis, the voltage window for all subsequent experiments was
fixed at 1.7–3.0 V.
To investigate the protective effect of NH4I on the lithium anode
in Li-S batteries, the lithium electrodes were extracted from
cycled batteries and subjected to comprehensive characterization.
First, the surface composition of the electrodes was analyzed
using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) [36]. Following
charge correction using the C-C peak at 284.8 eV, the F 1s, N 1s,
S 2p, O 1s, C 1s, and I 3d spectra acquired from lithium anodes
cycled in different electrolytes were compared. This comparison
enabled the identification of changes in the SEI. In the in-depth
XPS analysis, the spectra of different elements are shown in
Figures 3a–c and Figures S22 and S23 . The F 1s spectra shows
a characteristic LiF peak at 684.5 eV [37]. Additionally, the Li 1s
XPS spectra collected from the cycled lithium anode also exhibits
an increase in the LiF peak intensity (Figure S22a) [38, 39]. This
indicates that the incorporation of NH4I significantly promotes
the LiF content, mainly owing to the accelerated decomposition
of TFSI−anions. Li-F has extremely high mechanical strength
and excellent ionic conductivity. It promotes the formation of a
dense SEI layer to effectively suppress parasitic reactions [40].
In the N 1s spectra, a Li-N peak was observed at 398.2 eV
[41]. Semi-quantitative XPS analysis confirmed an increased
concentration of Li-N on the NH4I modified anode surface. As
a bifunctional material with both electronic insulation and ionic
conduction properties, Li-N synergizes with Li-F to form a robust
SEI layer. This Li-F/Li-N enriched interface effectively suppresses
parasitic reactions on the lithium anode, thereby improving
cycling stability [42]. Figure S22b presents the O 1s XPS spectra
collected from the cycled lithium anode. Characteristic peaks are
observed at 531.2 and 533.0 eV, corresponding to C = O and C-
O species, respectively, while the peak at 528.8 eV is assigned
to Li-O, primarily originating from the decomposition of lithium
nitrate. Notably, the Li-O signal at 528.8 eV is nearly absent on the
lithium anode cycled in the NH4I-containing electrolyte, whereas
a distinct Li-O peak remains evident in the spectrum from the
blank electrolyte. This result indicates that the introduction of
NH4I effectively suppresses the formation of inactive lithium
[43]. Figure S22c shows the C 1s XPS spectra collected from
the cycled lithium anode. After the introduction of NH4I, a
characteristic -CF3 peak appeared at 293 eV, which is attributed to
the decomposition of LiTFSI in the electrolyte [44, 45]. The I 3d
spectra of the NH4I-containing samples confirmed the presence
of Li-I within the SEI on the lithium anode (Figure S23). This Li-
I containing interphase facilitates Li+ diffusion and contributes
to reduced polarization. Analysis of the S 2p spectra revealed a

To elucidate the impact of the NH4I additive on sulfur cathodes,
density functional theory (DFT) calculations were first conducted
to ascertain the binding energies between NH4I and key lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs) (Figure 4a). The results indicate significantly
stronger binding interactions between NH4
+ and LiPSs (e.g., Li2S,
Li2S2) than with solvent molecules (DME/DOL). This strong
interaction arises from hydrogen bonding between NH4
+ and
the electronegative sulfur atoms in short chain polysulfides,
thereby promoting their dissolution [47]. Analysis of the highest
occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels indicates that NH4I
possesses the second highest HOMO energy, surpassed only by
I3
−, indicating its facile oxidation and strong electron-donating
capability. Furthermore, among the components, I3
−has the
smallest HOMO-LUMO energy gap, which indicates that it has
a high electrochemical reactivity and can actively remove dead
sulfur from the surface of lithium anodes (Figure 4b) [22].

The efficacy of catalytic behavior is ultimately reflected in key
battery performance metrics. Accordingly, Li-S batteries were
assembled and their electrochemical properties systematically
evaluated. Li-S batteries employing the optimized electrolyte
concentration were assembled for cyclic voltammetry (CV) and
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements.
As depicted in Figure 5a, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed
on Li-S batteries with blank and NH4I-containing electrolytes at a
scan rate of 0.10 mV s−1. The CV curves display two characteristic
reduction peaks, corresponding to the conversions of S8 to liquid
(LiPSs) and liquid LiPSs to Li2S, respectively, along with one
oxidation peak for the Li2S to S8 conversion [52]. The polarization
voltage between the oxidation and reduction peaks decreased
from 333 to 311 mV. Moreover, the NH4I-containing electrolyte
showed a larger integrated CV area and a stronger current
response. These results demonstrate that NH4I lowers the energy
barrier for the liquid-to-solid conversion step, thereby promoting
greater sulfur participation in the reaction. A minor oxidation
peak observed at approximately 2.90 V aligns with the oxidation
of I−to I3
−(Figure S27). The NH4I battery exhibits a smaller
semicircle in the EIS plot (Figure 5b), demonstrating reduced
interfacial charge-transfer resistance.
To further probe the influence of NH4I on the solid–electrolyte
interphase (SEI) evolution, operando electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on both cell groups during
discharge. As shown in Figure 5c,d, the charge transfer resis-
tance (represented by the high-frequency semicircle) remains
stable in the NH4I-containing electrolyte, whereas it fluctuates
markedly in the blank electrolyte. EIS profiles collected during
charging (Figure S28a,b) also display two semicircles in the
high and mid-frequency regions, consistent with the presence
of a well-defined SEI. Although the resistance values in both
systems are relatively stable during charging, the battery with
blank electrolyte exhibits significantly higher overall resistance,
attributable to the insulating effect of accumulated dead sulfur
on the SEI [54]. The impedance evolution was further analyzed
via distribution of relaxation times (DRT) applied to the EIS
data (Figure S28c,d). The DRT peak appearing at intermediate
time constants (10−4–10−1 s) corresponds to the charge transfer
resistance (Rct), a key descriptor of interfacial kinetics [28].
Throughout cycling, the Rct fluctuations of the NH4I-containing
battery are markedly smaller than those of the blank electrolyte
battery, consistent with the trends observed in the EIS spectra and
confirming the superior interfacial stability imparted by NH4I.
This stability is attributed to the high ionic conductivity and good
mechanical stability of the organic-inorganic hybrid SEI in the
NH4I anode [55]. In accordance with the conclusions obtained
from the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT)
(Figure S29), dynamic resistance analysis revealed a reduction in
internal resistance from 496.47 to 406.41 Ω with NH4I addition,
confirms its interfacial stabilization effect [50]. Additionally, Tafel
analysis further confirmed the catalytic role of NH4I, as evidenced
by the decreased slopes for both the C1 (from 84.65 to 64.21
mV⋅dec−1) and C2 (from 80.17 to 63.57 mV⋅dec−1) reduction peaks,
indicating improved sulfur reduction kinetics (Figure 5e,f) [50].

further tested (Figure S36). The batteries were allowed to stand for

markedly lower capacity loss of 4.39% compared to 38.52% for the

at this voltage, the shuttle current was measured. The shuttle

than in the blank electrolyte (Figure S37), indicating that NH4I
suppresses polysulfide corrosion of the lithium anode. A visual
experiment was subsequently performed (Figure S38). Sulfur

any foil remained clear [62]. These results indicate that the
stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed during cycling in
the NH4I electrolyte effectively protects the lithium anode from
polysulfide attack and reduces self-discharge losses.
3
Conclusion
In summary, an innovative electrolyte design strategy was pro-
posed for Li-S batteries to address the persistent challenges
of lithium dendrite growth and inactive sulfur accumulation.
Theoretical calculations and spectroscopic analyses confirm that
hydrogen bonding between NH4
+ and Li2S, combined with
the I−/I3
−redox shuttle mechanism, effectively promotes the
dissolution of passivated sulfur species into the electrolyte. This
process effectively mitigates the accumulation of inactive species
at the electrode interface and enhances active material utilization.
Furthermore, the I−/I3
−redox couple and NH4
+ collectively mod-
ify the Li+ solvation sheath, promoting the formation of a stable
inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the lithium
anode. As a result, the optimized Li-S battery delivered a high
initial specific capacity of 1097.78 mAh g−1 at 0.5C and retained
826.84 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles, demonstrating significant cycling
stability. This study provides an effective strategy to address
the inherent challenges of Li-S batteries, which demonstrates
promising potential for the development of practical high energy
density Li-S batteries.
