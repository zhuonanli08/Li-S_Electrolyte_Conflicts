Designed Construction of Bifunctional Interphase
Preactivated via Over-Discharge Strategy Toward Stable
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Wenming Song, Hui Liu, Mai Li, Weiqing Shi, Zilong Jiang, Huifang Li, Lisheng Zhang,
Peng Wang, Xiaojun Wang, and Zhiming Liu*
Signiﬁcant challenges remain to be overcome for practical application of high
energy density lithium–sulfur batteries including poor cyclability induced by
“the shuttle eﬀect”, parasitic reactions, and uncontrollable growth of lithium
dendrites. To overcome these problems, a bifunctional solid electrolyte
interphase layer is successfully constructed by employing an over-discharge
pre-activation strategy to in situ induce a reduction reaction between lithium
anode and functional electrolyte additive, 3,3,3-triﬂuoropropylene carbonate
(TFPC). TFPC exhibits lower electron aﬃnity and LUMO energy levels, facil-
itating its reaction with lithium metal to form SEI ﬁlms with high mechanical
robustness and ionic conductivity, resulting in uniform and fast lithium depo-
sition/stripping and reduced parasitic reactions between lithium polysulﬁdes
and the lithium anode. As a result, the overall performance of lithium–sulfur
batteries is improved. The decomposition pathway and action mechanism of
TFPC are systematically and deeply revealed by density functional theory (DFT)
calculations, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, and detailed
experimental results. The aim of this study is to provide a feasible avenue
for lithium–sulfur battery interfacial protection and to solve the imminent
lithium–sulfur battery problem through in-depth investigation of the bonding
chemistry in TFPC molecules and the clever design of interfacial reactions.

Although signiﬁcant academic and com-
mercial progress has been made on Li–S
batteries over the past few decades, achiev-
ing stable cycling at near-theoretical ca-
pacities remains elusive due to the com-
plexity of the electrode conversion reac-
tions during battery cycling, which is a
crucial challenge in realizing the large-
scale practical application of Li–S batteries.
Li–S battery relies on redox reactions be-
tween Li and S, which are reversible and
complex. In general, discharging process
involves a multi-step reduction reaction in
which S is reduced to a series of lithium
polysulﬁdes (LiPSs, Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) in-
termediates then to lithium sulﬁde (Li2S),
while the charging process is in reverse.[6]
The LiPSs intermediates are soluble in the
common electrolytes and tend to diﬀuse
from the S cathode and migrate to the Li
anode through electrolytes.[7] As a result,
the LiPSs are continuously reduced by Li
anodes, which results in a series of issues
including a severe active-material loss, and formation of an in-
sulating interphase on the Li anode. This interphase is mainly
responsible for the rapid attenuation of the capacity and low
coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li–S batteries.[6–9] In addition, due
to the inhomogeneous plating/stripping behavior on the Li an-
ode surface, Li+ easily nucleates on the protuberance, forming
dendrites and ′dead″ Li, which continuously consume the elec-
trolyte, leading to inferior cycle performance.[10]
To overcome these intractable obstacles, various strategies
have been developed in the past decades for protecting the highly
reactive Li anode by introducing ﬁlm-forming additives in the
electrolyte,[11–14] artiﬁcial coating of Li anodes[15] and interlayer
insertion treatment.[16,17] Among them, construction of the solid-
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer on the surface of Li anode
by introducing a functional electrolyte additive might be one
of the most crucial eﬀorts. Attributed to its ability to transport
Li+, SEI enables reversible electrochemical reaction at the Li an-
ode. A rationally designed SEI is pivotal in stabilizing Li anode
for reversible operation of Li–S batteries, which has been well
established.[18] Lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is a well-known additive

Lithium ﬂuoride (LiF) and lithium carbonate (Li2CO3), com-
mon inorganic ingredients of SEI, are believed to be re-
sponsible for the stabilization of Li anode interfaces in Li−S
batteries and positive inﬂuence on battery performance be-
cause of its contributions to electronic insulation and Li+
ionic conductivity.[21] Due to the poor mechanical toughness of
LiF and Li2CO3, the continuous fragmentation and remod-
eling of the SEI ﬁlm accelerate electrolyte depletion and Li
dendrite growth during the cycling process.[22–24] Therefore,
the construction of SEI with high mechanical toughness and
containing LiF and Li2CO3 is a logically feasible approach.
Herein, we report on the employment of F-rich ester addi-
tive, 3,3,3-Triﬂuoropropylene Carbonate (TFPC), as an addi-
tive in DOL/DME and Li bis(triﬂuoromethanesulphomyl)imide
(LiTFSI) based electrolytes. TFPC is much more preferred to be
reduced by Li anode. More importantly, theoretical simulations
and experimental characterizations veriﬁed that LiF and Li2CO3
formation were indeed generated as the ﬁnal reductive decompo-
sition product of TFPC additive as expected. However, the reduc-
tion potential of TFPC is slightly lower than the standard voltage
window of Li–S battery (1.7−2.8 V). Therefore, we propose an
over-discharge pre-activation approach to induce in situ decom-
position of TFPC forming SEI ﬁlms composed of LiF, Li2CO3 and
Polyoleﬁns. The three together form a bifunctional SEI ﬁlm with
high ionic conductivity and high mechanical toughness, thus im-
proving the coulombic eﬃciency, cycle stability of the battery and
outstanding rate performance. This strategy provides a new de-
sign electrolyte approach to promote the commercialization of
advanced Li–S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
To evaluate the electronic characteristics of TFPC additive and
DOL/DME electrolytes, we ﬁrst performed a series of density
functional theory (DFT) calculations to obtain their lowest un-
occupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy and electron aﬃnity
(EA) energy as collected in Figures 1a,b. Compared with DOL
and DME, TFPC exhibits a much lower LUMO energy, facilitat-
ing reduction. And then, the calculated EA of TFPC is also lower
than those of DOL and DME molecules by 1.38 and 1.60 eV, re-
spectively, as the changes of EA can be well captured by the one-
electron molecular orbital diagram according to the Koopmans
theorem and its electron aﬃnity analogue (i.e., EA = ɛLUMO).[25]
As is well known, a more negative value typically indicates easier
electron acceptance. Thereby, it indicates that TFPC molecules
have stronger electron-withdrawing ability and are more readily
reduced by Li anode compared with DOL and DME molecules.
The high reactivity of TFPC molecules was veriﬁed by Li metal
stability experiments. Figures 1c,d show that Li metal was im-
mersed in DOL: DME (1:1, in v/v) (abbreviated DD) and DOL:
DME (1:1, in v/v) + TFPC (abbreviated DDT) for 6 h, respectively.
Subsequently, the soaked Li metal is transferred to LiPSs solu-
tion for 24 h, and the results show that the Li metal immersed in
DDT solution fails to change the color of the LiPSs solution. In
contrast, the Li metal immersed in DD solution causes a signif-
icant change in the color of the LiPSs solution due to the para-
sitic reaction between Li metal and LiPSs. Meanwhile, the scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements on the above
Li metal provide a more visual indication of the parasitic reac-
tion. The surface of the Li metal immersed in DDT solution has
a ﬂat and smooth morphology after reaction with LiPSs, while
the Li metal immersed in DD solution has a rough and porous
Li foam morphology after reaction with LiPSs. The above DFT
calculations and experiments simultaneously verify that TFPC
molecules have higher electron gaining properties, and can form
an initial passivation ﬁlm on the surface of Li metal to inhibit the
parasitic reaction of LiPSs.
In order to investigate the passivation mechanism of Li metal
by TFPC molecules, reaction process of Li catalyzed decomposi-
tion of TFPC was explored further by DFT calculations. Reductive
decomposition process of DOL and DME molecules was also in-
vestigated for comparison. It is known that Li atom is extremely
reactive as the valence electron in its outer shell is easily lost to
form Li+.[26] Optimization results demonstrate that Li+ tends to
interact with O atoms after the electron transfer process from Li
atom to electrolyte molecules, where reductive decomposition of
TFPC, DOL, and DME molecules will be triggered. Detailed in-
formation of decomposition processes of TFPC induced by Li at-
tack are displayed in Figure 2a, Figures S1, S2 (Supporting In-
formation), respectively. Corresponding energy parameters are
collected in Tables S1−S3 (Supporting Information). For TFPC,
three diﬀerent potential reductive decomposition pathways were
considered, as shown in Figure 2a. According to the priority or-
der of adsorption and reaction processes, Path-1 is an adsorption-
reaction path as Li adsorbs with O3 (Inset in Figure 2a) ﬁrst and
then TFPC undergoes ring-opening reaction, while Path-2 and
Path-3 should belong to reaction-adsorption path because TFPC
undergoes ring-opening reaction ﬁrst and then Li is adsorbed on
the O atom (O1 or O2) of the intermediate product.[27] Regardless
of the pathway, Li adsorption is an exothermic process without
any energy barrier, and calculated energy releases for attacking
O3, O1 and O2 atoms involved in TFPC are −1.26, −2.35, and
−2.47 eV, respectively. The same is true for the Li adsorption pro-
cess with DOL and DME molecules, but the released energies are
−0.24 and −0.23 eV, respectively, which are much higher than
those of TFPC molecules. It means TFPC additives are much
more preferred to be attacked by Li anode, and chemical compo-
sitions of formed SEI ﬁlms should be mainly dominated by the
reductive decomposition products of TFPC additives, not DOL or
DME electrolyte.
To conﬁrm the main chemical compositions of regulated SEI
ﬁlm, decomposition products generated from the reductive reac-
tion of TFPC additives with the Li anode were explored. As men-
tioned above, Path-2 and Path-3 triggered by Li attack on ester O
atoms (O1 and O2) are much more favorable to proceed for TFPC
additives. For Path-2, it is found that C2─O2 bond will be disso-
ciated ﬁrst by interacting with Li atom, and Li2CO3 as well as
Polyoleﬁns will be produced when the intermediate is attacked
by one more Li atom. Diﬀerently, C1─O1 will be broken by Li

The reduction potential of TFPC in Li–S batteries was calcu-
lated by DFT to explore the possibility of its rapid decomposi-
tion (Text S1, Supporting Information). According to the calcu-
lated decomposition path of TFPC molecule (Figure 2a), there
are two ring-opening sites of the reduced TFPC molecule, which
correspond to diﬀerent reduction paths and decomposition po-
tentials, and calculations show that the two theoretical reduction
potentials are 0.95 and 1.07 V, respectively (Figure 3a). The re-
duction potentials of the DOL and DME molecules are calcu-
lated in the same way, and the reduction potential is −1.21 V
for both (Figure S8, Supporting Information). This potential is
much lower than the TFPC molecule reduction potential, indi-
cating that TFPC molecule is theoretically more susceptible to
decomposition by Li attack in a Li–S battery system. To determine
the optimal TFPC concentration, Li–S batteries assembled with
varying TFPC concentrations are subjected to the over-discharge
pre-activation strategy. Subsequent electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS; Figure S9, Supporting Information) demon-
strates that cells with 3 wt.% TFPC exhibit the lowest interfacial
impedance. Thus, subsequent experiments utilize this concen-
tration.

LDDT cell (0.906 mA cm−2) is higher than the LDD cell (0.755
mA cm−2), and EIS measurements show that the LDDT elec-
trolyte exhibits a smaller interfacial impedance.[36,37] The high
ionic conductivity of SEI (LiF/Li2CO3) enhances the eﬀective
Li+ transport and reduces the interfacial ion concentration gra-
dient, which indirectly increases the exchange current density.
SEM measurements of the discharged Cu electrode surface, the
charged Li electrode surface and the charged Cu electrode sur-
face of the Li|Cu half-cell were performed to visualize the ho-
mogeneity of Li+ stripping/plating (Figure 4c; Figures S17,S18,
Supporting Information). In the LDD electrolyte, the uneven Li
deposition on the electrode surface produces Li dendrites. In con-
trast, no dendritic and uniform Li deposition is observed on the
Cu and Li electrodes of the LDDT electrolyte, suggesting that
the SEI ﬁlm can promote uniform Li+ deposition and stripping
behavior.
To conﬁrm the long-term stability and reversibility of the TFPC
additive for Li metal protection, the Coulombic eﬃciency (CE)
was tested in Li|Cu half-cell at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2
and a capacity density of 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure 4d). Cell failure
occurs after around 50 cycles for the LDD electrolyte, while the
LDDT electrolyte remains stable for 250 cycles. The Li|Li symmet-
ric cell was tested at a constant current density of 1 mA cm−2 and
a capacity density of 0.5 mAh cm−2 (Figure 4e). It is evident that
the LDDT electrolyte has a low and stable voltage hysteresis over
1200 h, whereas the LDD electrolyte short-circuits in 250 h. The
performance of the Li|Li symmetric cell at high current densities
was shown in Figure S19 (Supporting Information), where the
LDDT electrolyte displays a regular increase in overpotential with
increasing current density, which indicates that the bifunctional
SEI ﬁlm provides fast ion transport at high current densities. The
surface morphology of the Li electrode in Li|Li symmetric cell was
characterized by SEM for 5 h (Figure S20, Supporting Informa-
tion) and 50 h (Figure S21, Supporting Information). The Li elec-
trode morphology of the LDDT electrolyte is smoother and ﬂatter
than the LDD at 5 and 50 h. It demonstrates that the bifunctional
SEI ﬁlm has more uniform Li+ deposition properties, helping to
mitigate the generation of Li dendrites.
In Li–S battery, Figure 5a shows the CV measurements for dif-
ferent electrolytes with a voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V and a scan
rate of 0.1 mV s−1. The response current of reduction peaks in the
LDDT-1.0V battery is larger than LDD-1.0V battery (Ir1 > Ir2), and
the LDDT-1.0V battery has a smaller electrochemical polarization
(Vr1 < Vr2). It has the same trend in both 2nd and 3rd circles CV
measurements for both batteries (Figures S22, S23, Supporting
Information), which is a result of the stronger electrochemical ki-
netics of the bifunctional SEI ﬁlms. Typical charge/discharge pro-
ﬁles for both batteries also reﬂect the same results (Vr1 < Vr2) in
Figure 5b. This is attributed to the smaller interfacial impedance
(RSEI) and charge transfer impedance (RCT) of the LDDT-1.0V bat-
tery in Figure 5c, compared to LDD-1.0V battery. Based on this,
the lower RSEI and RCT of the bifunctional SEI accelerate the inter-
face electrochemical reaction and reduce the polarization voltage
of the battery.
Figure 5d shows the rate performance of the battery for fast
charging and discharging. The LDDT-1.0V battery exhibits an
average reversible capacity of 1174.7, 1083.7, 969.1, 848.4, and
648 mAh g−1 at rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 C, respectively.
The capacity of LDD-1.0V battery decays more with the in-
crease of rate, especially at 2 C, showing a cliﬀ-type capacity
decay phenomenon. The LDDT electrolyte exhibits better elec-
trode/electrolyte interface kinetics, which is attributed to the for-
mation of bifunctional SEI ﬁlm with high ion diﬀusion prop-
erties. The cycle performance and CE of two electrolytes were
shown in Figure 5e. The discharge speciﬁc capacities of the LDD-
1.0V battery in the 1st and 70th cycles are 1489.3 and 775.8 mAh
g−1, corresponding to a CE of 93% and 87.2%, respectively. By
contrast, the cycle performance and CE of the LDDT-1.0V bat-
tery are signiﬁcantly improved. Its discharge speciﬁc capacity is
1643.8 mAh g−1 in the 1st cycle and 775.9 mAh g−1 in the 150th cy-
cle. After 150 cycles, the CE of LDDT-1.0V battery still has 91.7%,
which thanks to the stable bifunctional SEI ﬁlm that suppresses
the side reactions of electrolyte and electrodes improving the cy-
cle lifespan of the battery, which is comparable to state-of-the-art
systems reported in recent literature (Table S4, Supporting Infor-
mation).
In addition, Figure S24 (Supporting Information) shows the
Li–S battery with the introduction of a conventional LiNO3 addi-
tive electrolyte (Abbreviated LDD-LiNO3) compared to the LDDT-
1.0V. At a current density of 0.5 C, the speciﬁc discharge capac-
ity of LDD-LiNO3 battery is 769.9 mAh g−1 in the 1st circle, and
the capacity retention after 70 cycles is 70.9%. In contrast, the 1st
circle discharge speciﬁc capacity of LDDT-1.0V battery is 900.2
mAh g−1 with a capacity retention rate of 78.0% after 70 cycles.
This result indicates that the bifunctional SEI ﬁlm has signiﬁcant
advantages over LiNO3 additive, which has high reversibility and
cyclic stability.
The surface morphology of the Li anodes of both batteries at
10 cycles (Figure 5f), 20 cycles (Figure S25, Supporting Informa-
tion) was tested by SEM. Compared with the LDD-1.0V battery,
the SEI ﬁlm of LDDT-1.0V battery is highly mechanically duc-
tile mitigating the volume expansion of Li metal, therefore its Li
anode surface is ﬂat. XPS spectra reveal the Li anode surface com-
ponent composition of both batteries after 10 cycles. Figures 5g,h
show the high-resolution spectra of C 1s and F 1s on the Li anode
surface, respectively. It is noteworthy that the peak intensities of
Polyoleﬁns, Li2CO3, and LiF are stronger for LDDT-1.0V than for
LDD-1.0V, which is caused by the further decomposition of the
TFPC additive into ﬁlm during the cycling process. In addition,
the solvent DOL and DME decomposition in the electrolyte in-
creases the C─O species, while the decrease of C─O species in
the cycle after the addition of TFPC additive indicates that the
solvent decomposition is suppressed.[38] The high-resolution C
1s and F 1s spectra show the presence of ─CF3 species on the
Li anode surface of the LDD-1.0V battery. ─CF3 may originate
from LiNSO2CF3 and LiCF3.[33] Both compounds are products
of LiTFSI decomposition. It is indicated that the bifunctional
SEI ﬁlm inhibits the irreversible decomposition caused by Li salt
penetration into the SEI ﬁlm. Therefore, the TFPC additive with
the pre-activation strategy forms the bifunctional SEI ﬁlm with
both high mechanical toughness and high ionic conductivity on
the Li anode surface, which enhances the kinetics of the elec-
trode/electrolyte interface and alleviates the volume expansion of

3. Conclusion
In this work, we demonstrate a scheme in which over-discharge
in an electrolyte containing TFPC additive allows in situ inter-
facial reactions on the Li anode to generate a bifunctional SEI
ﬁlm with high ionic conductivity and high mechanical tough-
ness, providing a stable interface for Li–S batteries. This bifunc-
tional SEI ﬁlm alleviates the growth of Li dendrite, accelerates
the uniform plating/stripping performance of Li+, and prevents
the parasitic reaction of LiPSs with Li anodes during charging
and discharging, which brings a stable cycle performance with
high Coulombic eﬃciency and outstanding rate performance for
Li–S battery. The highlight of this work is the ingenious design
of a simple pre-activation strategy to construct a bifunctional SEI
ﬁlm by a comprehensive mechanistic study of TFPC molecules
to solve the inherent problems of the battery from a more funda-
mental perspective. By combining this strategy with a computa-
tional approach, we provide a more practical solution to the in-
herent problem of Li–S batteries, oﬀer an interesting idea for de-
signing better electrolytes, and enable the development of high-
capacity Li–S batteries.

Sulfur (S): conductive carbon (super P,

Pre-Activation Step:
Two hour of resting followed by constant current

constant temperature chamber at 25 °C, and the electrolyte volume was
strictly controlled at 25 μL mg(S loading)−1.
Characterization:
The morphology of the Li metal was measured by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi, SU8010). X-ray photoelec-

were performed to test Young’s modulus of SEI ﬁlm.
Electrochemical Measurements:
Li−S batteries consist of Li foil as the
counter electrode and S541 cathode as the working electrode. The Li|Cu
half-cell consists of Li foil as the counter electrode and copper foil as the
working electrode. Both electrodes of the Li|Li symmetric cell were com-
posed of Li foil. The above half-cell and Li–S cell tests were performed
using assembled CR-2032-coin cells, Celgard 2400 separator, and Li foil
(16 mm, 500 μm) The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was
performed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E) with a fre-
quency range of 100 K Hz to 10 mK Hz and an amplitude of 5 mV. Cyclic
Voltammetry (CV) was performed using an electrochemical workstation
(CHI 660E) with an amplitude of 5 mV. CV was performed using an elec-

V and a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. Tafel curves were tested on Li symmet-
ric cells using two-electrode system with voltages ranging from −0.2 to
0.2 V (vs Li+/Li) at a sweep rate of 1.0 mV s−1. Both half-cell and Li−S
battery galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed in the LAND
CT2001A battery test system.
DFT Simulations:
The neutral and radical-anion states for each or-
ganic electrolyte molecule (DOL, DME, and TFPC) were optimized with
density functional theory (DFT) calculations performed with Gaussian 16
Rev. C.01[39] using the Becke’s three-parameter hybrid exchange functional
combined with the Lee–Yang–Parr correlation functional (B3LYP)[40–42] at
a 6-31G (d, p) basis set[43,44] level. All geometries were optimized without
any symmetry restrictions and were conﬁrmed as potential energy surface
minima by harmonic vibrational frequency calculations at a pressure of 1
atm and temperature of 298.15 K. Solvent eﬀect was also taken into ac-
count with the implicit solvation models including polarizable continuum
model (PCM)[45] and Truhlar’s universal solvent model (SMD)[46] in the
calculations. A static relative permittivity of 7.15[47] and a dynamic relative
permittivity of 1.928,[48] which reliably describe the polarity of the solvents
in 1:1 mixture of DOL and DME. To evaluate the reductive property of each
electrolyte molecule considered here, the lowest unoccupied molecular or-
bital (LUMO) energy and the adiabatic electron aﬃnities (EA) were deter-
mined. The electron aﬃnities were obtained by means of the ΔSCF method
on the basis of the optimized structures of the neutral and radical-anion
states.
AIMD Simulations:
Symmetry-constrained ab initio molecular dynam-
ics (AIMD) for the reductive decomposition process of TFPC on Li
surface were performed by the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package
(VASP)[49,50] with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method.[50] The
hybrid Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)[51] functional was used for the de-
scription of the exchange-correlation interaction, and the van der Waals
interactions were also considered using Grimme’s D3-correction.[52] To
simulated the lithium anode, a repeated slab model was built that included
a vacuum region of more than 15 Å along the z-direction and seven layers
2 × 2 supercell of lithium (110) surface (9.9 Å × 7.0 Å). Five optimized
TFPC molecules were placed randomly on the surface of Li slab with a
density, 1.554 g cm−3, close to that of the liquid at atmospheric pressure.
The simulation time step was chosen as 1 fs and 10.0 ps NVT AIMD sim-
ulations runs at a temperature of 700 K, controlled by a Nosé–Hoover
thermostat.[53] Temperature as high as 700 K might seem unrealistic, but
its sole purpose was to speed up the dynamical evolution (According to
the AIMD simulation results in previous report,[27] it was demonstrated
that DOL molecule decomposed ≈800 K, while 1000 K was preferred for
the decomposition reaction of DME which was relatively more stable than
DOL. Considering TFPC was more easily to be dissociated compared with
DOL and DME, 700 K was chosen here for the decomposition simulation
of TFPC). A kinetic energy cut-oﬀof 400 eV was employed for the plane
waves, and a Γ-point-only k-sampling method was used. The energy con-
vergence criterion of 10−6 eV was used for self-consistent calculations and
the atomic positions were relaxed until the maximal atom force was con-
verged to 0.02 eV Å−1.
