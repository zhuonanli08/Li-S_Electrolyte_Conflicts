solid electrolyte interphase over lithium anode and
its implications using a LiTFSI/TME-TTE electrolyte
and polysulﬁde additive†

To develop high energy density Li batteries, Li metal anodes and in situ plated Li anodes (i.e., the so-called
anode-free approach) have been extensively studied. However, the high reactivity of the Li metal surface
can lead to chemical reactions with electrolyte components upon contact. Consequently, the so-called
native solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) species are formed spontaneously when the Li anode is in
contact with the electrolyte. This study illustrates the formation chemistry of native SEI over a Li metal
surface
using
lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
in
tetramethylene
sulfone
and
1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (LiTFSI/TMS-TTE) electrolyte with and without lithium
polysulﬁde (PS) additive. All electrolyte components were found to decompose over the Li surface based
on
diﬀuse
reﬂectance
infrared
Fourier-transformed
spectroscopy
(DRIFTS),
X-ray
photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS), and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation. The degradation mechanism
and the evolved SEI species of each electrolyte component were carefully evaluated. The PS additive can
signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the composition of native SEI via its decomposition prior to all other electrolyte
components, which can consequently suppress the degradation of the other electrolyte components,
namely, TTE, TMS, and LiTFSI. The results indicate the importance of proper selection of electrolyte
components, which can lead to the possibility of tuning such native SEI species to stabilize the Li surface
in subsequent electrochemical cycling.
1.
Introduction
With the surging demands of portable electronics, conventional
Li-ion batteries (LIBs), boasting an energy density of approxi-
mately 210 W h kg−1, can no longer fulll ambitious technology
applications in the coming decades.1 In the pursuit of
advancing next-generation batteries with heightened energy
density, extensive research has been conducted on lithium (Li)
metal anodes and in situ plated Li anodes, commonly referred to
as the anode-free approach. For example, lithium–sulfur
batteries (LSBs) with Li metal anodes are expected to have
a theoretical capacity of 1672 mA h g−1, making LSB one of the
most promising candidates owing to the relatively low cost,
environmental friendliness, and natural abundance of sulfur
compared
to
lithiated
transition
metal
oxide
cathode
materials.2–5 Nevertheless, the high reactivity of the Li anode
can lead to parasitic and uncontrollable degradation of the
electrolyte. This oen results in the formation of an unstable
solid electrolyte interphase,5 which consequently
impairs
coulombic eﬃciency and deteriorates the cycling performance
of the battery.6,7 Understanding the formation chemistry of SEI
is considered key for improving unavoidable SEI formation.
SEI formation on an anode surface is mostly expected owing
to the electrochemical reduction of electrolyte components, and
the electrolyte components govern the formed SEI species.8–10 In
addition, SEI can lead to uneven Li deposition and dissolution
during cycling and aggravate lithium dendrite growth.5,11,12 To

However, the high reactivity of the Li metal surface intro-
duces an additional risk in the chemical reduction or decom-
position of electrolyte components, resulting in the formation
of what is known as the native SEI.3,24–26 The spontaneous
formation of native SEI can occur when a Li metal anode comes
in contact with the electrolyte. The extent of this decomposition
or reduction of electrolyte components is potentially time
dependent, leading to an uncontrolled formation of native SEI.
This uncontrolled formation can signicantly aﬀect subsequent
electrochemical cycling. Although the occurrence of native SEI
formation has been documented, the specic reaction mecha-
nisms between the electrolyte components and the Li metal
surface remain relatively unexplored. This study delves into the
formation chemistry of native SEI using a LiTFSI/TMS-TTE
electrolyte, both with and without the addition of a PS addi-
tive. By understanding this mechanism, this research aims to
explore the potential of tuning the native SEI to function as an
eﬀective articial SEI. This approach could play a crucial role in
stabilizing the Li metal anode for enhanced performance in
subsequent electrochemical cycling processes.
The LiTFST/TME-TTE electrolyte is examined based on the
previous report by Reuter et al., who demonstrated that a solu-
bility-limited Li2S8 additive can suppress the shuttling eﬀect
and improve the electrochemical cycling stability of LSB using
LiTFSI/TMS-TTE electrolyte.27 The shuttling eﬀect problem in
LSB is caused by the diﬀusion of soluble high-order lithium
polysuldes (Li2Sn, 3 # n # 8) from the cathode to the Li anode,
where insoluble Li2S and Li2S2 can form, resulting in volume
expansion, active mass shrinking, and even huge capacity
loss.28–30 The positive impacts of implementing Li2S8 as an
additive appeared in several reports.22,31–33 Hong et al. demon-
strated that polysulde additive with LiTFSI/DIOX-DME elec-
trolyte leads to improved cycling behavior of a Li symmetrical
cell.32 A study from Aurbach's group also reported that the
addition of 0.1 M Li2S8 to ether-based electrolyte ensures a low
rate of parasitic reaction on the Li anode surface, thereby
stabilizing the cycling behavior for Li–S cell.33 However, to the
best of our knowledge, how polysulde additives can help
improve the electrochemical performance is not clearly under-
stood yet. In this study, we focus on demonstrating the surface
reactivity of Li metal anode with LiTFST/TMS-TTE (in brief,
LiTT) electrolyte, leading to native SEI and whether PS additive
would signicantly inuence the formation of native SEI over Li
surface. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), diﬀuse
reectance
infrared
Fourier-transformed
spectroscopy
(DRIFTS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and ab initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations are applied. The
results clearly show the spontaneous formation of native SEI
over the Li surface when in contact with electrolyte components,
and the PS additive can lead to suppressed decomposition of
the other components. The systematical understanding of the
electrolyte reactivity on Li anode as well as the stabilizing
mechanism by the PS additive can shed light on the future
validation of using Li anode for high energy density batteries.
2.
Experimental and computational
methods
2.1
Electrolyte preparation
LiTFSI (bis(triuoromethanesulfonyl)imide lithium salt, Sigma
Aldrich) was rst vacuum dried at 80 °C for three days. It was
then dissolved in a 1 : 1 volume ratio mixture of TMS (tetra-
methylene sulfone, Sigma Aldrich, 99% purity) and TTE (1,1,2,2-
tetrauoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrauoropropyl ether, Synquest Labo-
ratories, 99% purity), with the mixture stirred overnight. TMS
was heated to 50 °C until it liquied before use. The resultant
1.5 M LiTFSI in the TMS-TTE mixture was labeled as a LiTT
electrolyte. To prepare the LiTT-PS electrolyte, appropriate
amounts of Li2S and sulfur were added to the LiTT electrolyte to
achieve a concentration of 0.1 M Li2S8 (polysulde, PS). This
mixture was then stirred continuously for four days at 50 °C and
subsequently ltered through a PTFE syringe lter (0.2 mm).27,34
Additionally, a similar concentration of 0.1 M Li2S8 was added
to both TMS and TTE solvents separately, following the same
preparation protocol, designated as TMS-PS and TTE-PS,
respectively.
2.2
Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy
measurement
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analyses of the
liquid samples were conducted using a potassium bromide
(KBr) sample holder. For in situ diﬀuse reectance infrared
Fourier transformed spectroscopy (DRIFTS), a custom-built
three-electrode cell and a DRIFTS sampling accessory were
utilized. The experiments were carried out with a Nicolet 6700
FTIR spectrometer equipped with a mercury–cadmium–tellu-
ride (MCT/A) detector cooled with liquid nitrogen for enhanced
performance. The cell specically designed for in situ DRIFTS
was assembled within an argon-lled glove box, ensuring
a minimal moisture (H2O < 0.1 ppm) and oxygen (O2 < 1 ppm)
content. Once assembled, the cell was installed in the FTIR
spectrometer. The electrode position within the cell was
meticulously adjusted to achieve the maximum signal intensity,
ensuring optimal conditions for accurate spectroscopic anal-
ysis.35 The spectrum was recorded by accumulating 250 scans
with a resolution of 4 cm−1. To establish a baseline, a back-
ground spectrum was initially recorded over the electrode
before introducing any electrolyte or solvent into the cell.

2.3
XPS analysis measurement
Li foil (450 mm thickness, provided by Honjo Metal) was
precisely cut to the required size for various tests, such as in situ
DRIFTS and immersion tests in solvent and electrolyte. These
tests were conducted for a duration of 24 hours within the
controlled environment of a glove box to prevent air exposure
and contamination. Aer immersion, the Li foil was carefully
dried inside a glove box to maintain its integrity and to prevent
atmospheric exposure. XPS analysis was performed using
a PerkinElmer PHI 550 spectrometer with Al Ka (1486.6 eV).
2.4
Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations
The computational approach was carried out by the ab initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, as implemented in
the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) soware to
investigate the reaction between the electrolytes and the Li
metal anode as well as the simultaneously formed SEI compo-
nents. The periodic DFT study was performed with the projector
augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials to account for the
ion–core interactions.36,37 The electronic structure calculations
employed the opt-B86b-vdW nonlocal functional for modeling
electron
exchange-correlation
interactions
coupled
with
a plane-wave basis set featuring a kinetic cutoﬀenergy of
500 eV.38 The electronic optimization energy threshold and the
force convergence for structural optimization were set as
10−4 eV and 10−2 eV Å−1, respectively, and a Brillouin zone
sampled by a 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid was
selected for all structural relaxations.39 The simulation cells for
AIMD simulations were constructed by combining Li metal
surfaces with electrolyte molecules. The (110) orientation was
modeled as the Li-metal surface because the X-ray diﬀraction
(XRD) patterns showed that (110) is the most preferred facet.40
Thus, an eight-layered surface with 4 × 4 supercells was built
from the optimized Li bulk crystal, while the bottom two layers
were replaced by helium atoms to avoid the periodic boundary
interactions between neighboring slabs. Eventually, a vacuum
layer of 30.9 Å was added to the top of the Li surface in the z-
direction to accommodate the electrolyte cell. Fig. S1a† shows
the optimized Li (110) surface model and its lattice parameters.
The solvents (TMS, TTE), salt (LiTFSI), and additive41 mole-
cules were optimized using CAM-B3LYP hybrid functional and
6-311++G (d,p) basis set level in the Gaussian 09 package,42 and
the optimized structures of each molecule are shown in
Fig. S1b(e).† The electrolyte cells of the two formulations, (a)
LiTT and (b) LiTT-PS, were created, and the molecules were
randomly distributed using the amorphous packing tool in the
Material Studio package, with dimensions 12.16 Å × 12.16 Å ×
30.63 Å. Given the volume of the electrolyte, 4 LiTFSI molecules
are required for a 1.5 M salt concentration; each cell consists of
13 TMS, 8 TTE, 4 LiTFSI, and 0 or 1 PS molecules in the absence
or the presence of an additive, respectively, and these numbers
of molecules in the electrolytes are based on the experimental
literature above. The cells were then incorporated and placed on
top of the Li surface, followed by pre-equilibrium classical
molecular geometry minimizations through conjugate gradient
for 5000 fs (femtosecond) at 350 K with COMPASS II force eld
from the Material Studio package to remove possible too-near
interactions.43,44 The structural congurations of the two elec-
trolyte formulations (LiTT and LiTT-PS) on the Li (110) surface
aer geometry minimizations are displayed in Fig. S2.†
The reactivity of the electrolytes in the absence and the
presence of PS on the Li anode were then studied by the AIMD
simulation performed on the incorporated structures under the
canonical ensemble (NVT) with a Nose thermostat to maintain
the temperature oscillation around 350 K.45 The total duration
of each AIMD simulation was set to 15 picoseconds (i.e., 15 000
fs),41 with a time step of 1 fs, and simulations were conducted at
the G-point. This setup allowed for a detailed observation of the
electrolyte decomposition mechanisms on the Li anode surface.
To analyze and visualize these processes, snapshots extracted
during the simulations were processed using VirtualNanoLab
(VNL) soware. In the resultant images, the molecules under-
going decomposition were highlighted using the ball-and-stick
model for clarity, while the remaining unreacted molecules
were depicted in a wireframe format. Additionally, electron
transfer processes within the system were delineated through
Bader charge analysis, providing a comprehensive under-
standing of electrolyte decomposition mechanisms at the
molecular level.46,47

The in situ DRIFTS spectrum of TMS over Li foil (Fig. 1b)
reveals red-shied bands compared to that of liquid FTIR
(Fig. 1a), including nas (asymmetrical stretching) O]S]O
shied from 1297 to 1289 cm−1, ns (symmetrical stretching)
O]S]O from 1147 to 1137 cm−1, ns C–S from 735 to 724 cm−1,
and also bands attributable to s (twisting) CH2, p (wagging) C–
H, and r (rocking) C–H. These shis suggest a weakening of
bond strengths likely due to a broken C–S bond or strong
interactions of TMS's oxygen atoms with the Li metal. When PS
was added to TMS, the DRIFTS spectrum (Fig. 1c) showed
broader and weaker bands compared to the spectrum without
PS (Fig. 1b). The broadening of these bands could be attributed
to intramolecular interactions, whereas the weaker intensity
might indicate a lower density of surface species. This is
possibly owing to the reduced amount of TMS reacting with the
Li metal, which could be an eﬀect of PS passivation. Notably, the
ns C–S bands shied to a higher frequency from 724 to 759 cm−1
in the presence of PS, suggesting the presence of intramolecular
interactions.48 A probable reason is that the preferentially
adsorbed/reacted PS on Li resulted in a repel of TMS away from
the Li surface.
The in situ DRIFTS spectrum of TTE over the Li foil surface
(Fig. 1e) displays signicant deviations from the liquid TTE
spectrum (Fig. 1d). The bands observed at 1185 and 1144 cm−1
are attributable to nas/vs C–F, respectively, complemented by
the corresponding d (bending) C–F at 770 cm−1.49 Additionally,
a distinct shoulder at 1124 cm−1 can be associated with nas C–O.
The vs. C–O observed at 847 cm−1 exhibits a blue-shifrom its
position at 835 cm−1 in liquid TTE, and the nas C–O, which
overlaps with the vs. C–F, also shows a slight blue shifrom
1122 to 1124 cm−1. These shis suggest the possibility of
a broken C–O–C bond in the TTE molecule when it interacts
with the Li surface. Moreover, the pronounced intensity of the
C–F bands might indicate that their alignment is more
perpendicular to the Li surface, with the uorine atoms likely
oriented towards the metal, which agrees with the metal surface
selection rule.50 Other attributable bands from TTE-related
species include 2995 (nas C–H), 1437 (u, scissoring, C–H),
1319/1287 (p C–H), and 970 cm−1 (n C–C/r C–H).51
With PS added to the TTE (Fig. 1f), noticeable changes were
observed. Specically, the strong band at 1141 cm−1 altered its
peak shape compared to the spectrum without PS. This alter-
ation can be attributed to a combination of broadened bands
and a change in the relative intensity between n C–F and nas C–
O. Moreover, the relatively increased intensity of the shoulder
band at 1122 cm−1 (nas C–O) suggests a potential suppression of
TTE decomposition in the presence of PS. This observation
indicates that PS may preferentially adsorb or react with the Li
metal surface, inuencing the chemical interactions between
the TTE and the Li surface. XPS analysis reported later identi-
ed the formation of Li2S with PS, which might passivate the Li
surface and suppress TTE decomposition.

the Li surface (Fig. 2e) exhibit similarities to those without PS,

presence of PS. The most plausible explanation for this obser-

fragments. This hypothesis is expected to lead to a C–S band,
along with the appearance of the new S–H band. The enhanced
intensity of the C–S band around ∼735 cm−1 in the presence of
PS seems to corroborate the formation of this new C–S bond.
Moreover, the relative weakening of other bands in the presence
of PS suggests that the species induced by PS might suppress
the surface reactivity of the Li surface. This hypothesis aligns
with the results of the DFT calculations presented later in the
study, which indicate that PS decomposes over the Li surface,
leading to a passivation eﬀect that could explain the observed
spectral changes in the DRIFTS analysis.
The in situ DRIFTS analyses provide evidence that decom-

Furthermore, these analyses indicate that the addition of PS
additive can suppress decomposition. Li foils were immersed in
TMS, TTE, and LiTT electrolytes, with and without PS additive,
in a glove box to observe surface changes. Over time, as shown
in Fig. S5,† the surface of the Li foils underwent a gradual color

LiTT resulted in the formation of dark-colored deposits on the
Li foil. This darkening intensied with increased immersion
time, suggesting a gradual decomposition and accumulation of
species from TMS, TTE, and LiTFSI on the lithium metal
surface. The progression of color change over time implies an
increase
in
fragmented
carbonaceous
species
due
to
decomposition. No signicant black color deposit was observed
aer a short immersion time of 10 minutes in any of the cases
although the DRIFTS results already indicated decomposition
within such a brief contact period. However, the inclusion of PS
as an additive appeared to reduce the extent of decomposition,
as indicated by the relatively lighter color of the deposits
compared to those without PS. As shown in Fig. S5,† the
decomposition of TTE seemed to be more signicantly sup-
pressed by PS than TMS and LiTT.
3.2
Electrolyte degradation mechanism analyses through
DFT calculations and AIMD simulations
3.2.1
Reactivity of LiTT electrolyte on Li anode surface. The
DFT calculations and AIMD simulations in this study comple-
ment the experimental results, oﬀering a systematic and theo-
retical perspective on the observed phenomena. The focus was
primarily on examining the reactivity and decomposition
mechanisms of electrolyte components on Li surfaces with both
LiTT and LiTT-PS electrolytes. The relative energy uctuations
of the two systems are depicted in Fig. S6.† As the simulation
progresses, a trend of decreasing energy change is observed,
eventually stabilizing, which indicates that the reaction systems
have reached a state of thermodynamic equilibrium. For the
LiTT system, the simulation results reveal an initial decompo-
sition of one LiTFSI molecule, followed by the sequential
degradation of three TMS solvent molecules and, ultimately, the
breakdown
of
a
TTE
molecule
among
all
electrolyte
components.
This pattern of a more pronounced breakdown of TMS
molecules compared to TTE suggests preferential adsorption of
TMS on the Li surface, as supported by work of Xu et al.55 The
AIMD simulations showed that the three reacted TMS mole-
cules followed an identical decomposition pattern, where their
reactions with the Li surface started at approximately 1720,
2300, and 3480 fs. Fig. 3 oﬀers top-view snapshots of the
decomposition of one TMS solvent molecule, showing the most

3.3
Characterization of native SEI components by XPS and
Bader charge calculations
To empirically validate the composition of the native SEI
species, Li foils immersed in various solvents, with and without
PS, were analyzed. These Li foils, which had been immersed for
24 hours (Fig. S5†), were dried and then transferred for XPS
analyses without any exposure to air. This approach follows the
methodology previously demonstrated for systematically iden-
tifying SEI components by aligning experimental XPS signals
with calculated atomic charges.59 To achieve proper XPS curve
tting, the Bader charges of both the electrolyte components
and the degradation products in the LiTT and LiTT-PS systems
were calculated, as presented in Table S2.† The atomic charges
associated with each atom's bonding environment (including
Li, S, C, O, F, and N) are similar in both electrolyte formulations.
Thus, the following discussion is primarily on the results of the
LiTT-PS simulation. Among lithium nuclei species, the least
positive atomic charge of 0.057 jej indicates the non-reacted
surface species (Li0). Lithium atoms showing charges of 0.811,
0.817, 0.832, 0.843, and 0.851 jej correspond to electrolyte
decomposition, resulting in the formation of Li2O, Li–C, Li2S,
Li–N, and LiF, respectively. For the S nuclei species, the
formation of Li2S from TMS and LiTFSI degradation is indicated
by the lowest average atomic charge of −1.84 jej. However, C
atomic charges of −3.862, −0.046, 0.135, 0.395, and 1.706 jej are
attributed to various bonding environments, including LiC, C–
C/C–H, C–S, C–O, and C–F bonds, respectively. The three O of
Li–O, S]O, and C–O bonds appeared in the atomic charges of
−1.66, −1.29, and −1.01 jej, respectively, while the charges of
the F and N nuclei species are also listed in Table S2.† These
results from the AIMD simulations and atomic charge analyses
are critical for identifying electrolyte degradation pathways in
the interfacial region. They also play a key role in assisting the
binding energy tting, thereby enhancing the reliability of XPS
tting assignments.
The XPS spectra for Li foils immersed in TTE, TTE-PS, TMS,
and TMS-PS are presented in Fig. 7, accompanied by detailed
tting results in Table S3.† For Li foils immersed in TMS and
TTE, the XPS spectra demonstrate that both solvents undergo
reactions with the Li surface. The incorporation of PS altered
the composition of surface species, particularly diminishing
carbon-related contributions. In the case of TTE immersion,
both with and without PS, the XPS spectra reveal the formation
of Li–C and Li–O species. By the addition of PS, the emergence
of Li2S is suggested by the 54.9 eV peak in the Li 1s spectrum
and the 161.3 eV peak in the S 2p spectrum. The presence of
short-chain lithium polysuldes is also noted, as evidenced by
the 164.5 eV peak in the S 2p spectrum. Furthermore, the lower
binding energy of lithium polysuldes is corroborated by the
highest atomic charge from the DFT results (Table S2†). The
comparative analysis of the C 1s spectra indicates that PS
inhibited the decomposition of TTE, as suggested by the lower
percentage composition of C]C/C–C in TTE-PS (C 1s, 7.5%/
5.8%) compared to TTE (17.1%/32.3%) alone. The same
conclusion is revealed by the relatively high percentage of Li–C
(Li 1s, 30.5%) from that immersed in TTE compared to that in
TTE-PS (Li 1s, 10.1%).
For Li immersed in TMS, the Li 1s and C 1s spectra at 54.4 eV
and 282.0 eV, respectively, are attributed to the Li–C of
Li–(CH2)4–Li (and more fragmented species) formed from TMS
reacting with Li foil. The Li 1s peak at 53.0 eV and the O 1s peak
at 529.0 eV suggest the formation of Li–O, indicating >SO2
decomposition from TMS into Li2O, alongside Li2S species with
Li 1s 55.1 eV and S 2p 161.3 eV peaks. It is noteworthy that Li2O
and Li2S could not be revealed in DRIFTS, and the XPS data
indicated a greater extent of TMS decomposition to more frag-
mented species than that proposed from the in situ DRIFTS
results; this is likely because of the longer immersion time of
the samples for XPS. However, the presence of other interme-
diates or adsorbed TMS is suggested by additional peaks in the
O 1s 531 eV and the S 2p peak at 167.7 eV attributed to S]O,
alongside the S 2p peak at 165.5 eV and the C 1s peak at
285.7 eV, revealing the presence of the S–C bond. In the TMS-PS
system, the presence of short-chain polysuldes is evident with
the S 2p peak at 164.4 eV, and a decrease in the relative intensity
of Li–O in the Li 1s spectrum suggests that PS suppresses the
decomposition of >SO2.

contribution from Li2S, resulting from the interaction of Li with

4.
Conclusion
The chemical decomposition of electrolyte components in the
LiTT and LiTT-PS electrolyte formulations is a crucial aspect
inuencing the formation of native SEI on Li surfaces. This
decomposition, involving TTE, TMS, LiTFSI salt, and PS additive,
is comprehensively conrmed through DRIFTS, XPS analyses,
and AIMD simulations. The inclusion of PS additive in the LiTT-
PS formulation signicantly suppresses the decomposition of
TTE and TMS and alters the decomposition pathway of the
LiTFSI salt. Specically, the TTE solvent, upon contact with Li
metal, undergoes C–O and C–F bond breakages to form LiF,
LiOCCH, and CH2CCH species in the absence of PS. The
decomposition of TMS is characterized by the cleavage of S]O
and C–S bonds, resulting in the formation of Li–(CH2)4–Li, Li2O,
and Li2S surface species, regardless of the presence of PS. The
degradation process of LiTFSI salt commences with the cleavage
of C–S and/or N–S bonds, leading to subsequent bond scissions
and ultimately yielding constituent atoms/ions on the surface.
When PS is added to the electrolyte, its decomposition precedes
that of other components, leading to the formation of Li2S and
short-chain lithium polysuldes on the Li metal surface. This
early decomposition of PS eﬀectively passivates the Li surface,
reducing its reactivity towards other electrolyte components. The
formation of SEI species from the degradation of these compo-
nents is corroborated by the good agreement between the XPS
analyses and the atomic charges calculated from the AIMD
simulations. This study highlights the formation of native SEI
species on Li anodes even before the initiation of electrochemical
cycling. It also underscores that the composition of the SEI is
profoundly inuenced by electrolyte composition, oﬀering crit-
ical insights for the development of advanced battery technolo-
gies with enhanced performance and stability.
