A B S T R A C T
The polysulfide shuttle effect and the growth of Li dendrites are detrimental to the practical use of lithium–sulfur 
(Li–S) batteries. Here, we show that these challenges can be eliminated with hexamethylphosphoramide (HMPA) 
as a co-solvent. HMPA shows strong coordination interaction with Li+ and thus preferentially remains in the 
inner sheath of the Li+ solvation structure. The solvation structure not only minimizes the solubility of poly­
sulfides but also generates a LiF-rich solid-electrolyte interphase, which are responsible to the suppression of 
polysulfide shuttle effect and the elimination of Li dendrites, respectively. As a result, HMPA-based electrolytes 
enable Li//Li symmetric cells to exhibit long cycling lifetimes of up to 600 h at 5 mA cm−2 along with signifi­
cantly reduced polarization. Moreover, Li–S cells containing the HMPA-based electrolyte also display marked 
enhancement in cycling stability (e.g., 619.6 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles at 0.2 C). These results are superior to 
those measured for control cells that contain a conventional electrolyte. The concepts and method described 
herein may be extended to benefit other types of energy storage devices that use active metals as anodes.
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are regarded as highly promising, 
next-generation energy storage devices due to the intrinsic advantages 
bestowed by sulfur and lithium. Sulfur possesses a high theoretical 
specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) while being abundant, inexpensive, 
and friendly to the environment [1]. Lithium also features a high 
theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh g−1) along with a low density 
(0.534 g cm−3) and a low electrochemical potential (−3.04 V versus 
standard hydrogen electrode). As such, the theoretical energy density of 
Li–S batteries is high, with reported estimates of 2600 Wh kg−1 [2].
Despite these advantages, the practical use of Li–S batteries is hin­
dered by the notorious polysulfide shuttle effect and the unwanted 
growth of Li dendrites. These issues not only degrade device 
performance but can compromise safety. The shuttle effect can be 
mitigated by a number of strategies, including I) confinement of poly­
sulfides within the cathode host materials via physical and/or chemical 
adsorption [3–5], II) covalent attachment of sulfur to the cathode hosts 
[6–9], III) controlling the sulfur as smaller allotropes [10], and IV) 
adding a layer to the separator or inserting an interlayer into the device 
to prevent diffusion of the polysulfides to the anode [11–13]. A number 
of strategies have also been explored as solutions to the issues associated 
with the Li metal anode, such as the I) construction of artificial solid 
electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers to protect the Li metal anode [14–18] 
and II) employment of three-dimensional host materials to regulate the 
deposition of metallic Li [19–22].

only transfers Li+ between the cathode and the anode but also governs

the chemistry of both electrodes [23,24]. A commonly used electrolyte 
in Li–S batteries is a solution of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) 
imide (LiTFSI, usually 1 M) with a LiNO3 additive (usually ≤0.5 M) in a 
mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1 v/

premature corrosion of the Li metal anode and severely suffer from the 
shuttle effect in part because the ethereal solvents facilitate the disso­
lution of polysulfides [25–28]. As such, new electrolyte systems have 
been proposed, including those that feature high concentrations of Li+
or are based on ionic liquids [29,30]. In the former, the high salt con­
centration minimizes the fraction of free solvent molecules and thus 
attenuates its capability to dissolve polysulfides. Polysulfides are poorly 
soluble in certain ionic liquids due to a mismatch in polarity. However, 
these electrolytes often afford devices with sluggish cathode kinetics, 
which ultimately compromises rate capability. Highly solvating elec­
trolytes (HSEs), which utilize high donor-number (DN) solvents, are a 
promising alternative as they have been shown to accelerate cathode 
reaction kinetics, enhance sulfur utilization, and alleviate deleterious 
surface passivation effects [31–33]. However, high DN solvents can 
facilitate the dissolution of polysulfides and thus aggravate the poly­
sulfide shuttle effect. The standing challenge has been to enhance the Li+
solvation capability of the electrolyte while suppressing polysulfide 
dissolution.
Here, we report that hexamethylphosphoramide (HMPA) is a viable 
solution to this longstanding challenge. HMPA has a high DN value 
(37.8 kcal mol−1) and forms robust Li+ solvation structures. Moreover, it 
was discovered that integrating HMPA with a common, ether-based 
electrolyte minimizes the solubility of polysulfides and thus effectively 
suppresses the shuttle effect. HMPA also enables Li+ to form contact ion 
pairs (CIPs) and ion aggregates (AGGs) that lead to homogeneous LiF- 
rich SEI layers on the surfaces of Li metal anodes. These beneficial ef­
fects manifested in high performance devices as Li//Li symmetric cells 
containing a HMPA-based electrolyte generated a significantly lower 
polarization voltage in stripping/plating cycling tests (for more than 
600 h at 5 mA cm−2) when compared to analogous cells that contained a 
conventional electrolyte (c.f., 0.06 vs. 0.25 V, respectively). Further­
more, Li–S cells containing a HMPA-based electrolyte also exhibited 
improved performance metrics when compared to cells that contained a 
conventional electrolyte, including a markedly improved rate capability 
(e.g., 606.4 vs. 181.5 mAh g−1 at 2 C) and a greatly increased cycling 
stability (e.g., 619.6 vs. 295.2 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles at 0.2 C). Our 
work establishes a new method for overcoming the longstanding issues 
that have limited the practicality of Li–S batteries and, because the 
method is straightforward and cost-effective, it is poised for adaption to 
other energy storage devices.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Electrolyte design
The DN number of HMPA was measured to be 37.8 kcal mol−1 via 
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (see Fig. S1 for de­
tails), which is significantly higher than the values of commonly used 
ether-based solvents (e.g., 20.0 and 18.0 kcal mol−1 for DME and DOL 
[34], respectively) and amide-based solvents (e.g., 26.6, 27.3, 28.0, and 
29.0 kcal mol−1 for N,N-dimethylformamide, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, 
N,N-dimethylacetamide, and 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone [35–37], 
respectively). The solubility of the electrolyte salt, LiTFSI, in HMPA was 
measured to be approximately one tenth of the value in DME (ca. 0.6 M 
and 5.8 M, respectively). As such, the solubility of polysulfides in a 
HMPA-based electrolyte should be reduced due to the common ion effect 
[38,39]. These unique features were envisioned to promote sulfur 
cathode reaction kinetics and suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect. The 
effects of HMPA on other aspects of sulfur cathode chemistry has been 
recently reported [40].
A molecular electrostatic potential analysis indicated that the 
negatively charged regions of HMPA and DME are located on their 
respective oxygen atoms (Fig. 1a). Compared with DME, the oxygen 
atom of HMPA exhibits a higher electrostatic potential, which, in turn, 
should manifest in a relatively high affinity toward metal cations, such 
as Li+. When used as a co-solvent with DOL to dissolve LiTFSI, which is 
poorly soluble in HMPA, the formation of contact ion pairs (CIPs) and 
ion aggregates (AGGs) between Li cations and TFSI anions becomes 
plausible (Fig. 1b, left). Meanwhile, the relatively high affinity of HMPA 
for Li+ should cause HMPA to localize in the inner sheath of a Li+ sol­
vation structure, leading to a reduced number of free HMPA molecules. 
These effects should not only suppress its capability to dissolve poly­
sulfides but also facilitate the formation of a LiF-rich SEI layer on the 
surface of the anode along with homogeneous Li deposition.
With these considerations in mind, an electrolyte that consisted of 
LiTFSI, DOL, and DME was selected as a comparative control (Fig. 1b, 
right). In the control, Li+ should be separated from TFSI−because LiTFSI 
exhibits a relatively high solubility in DME. Such solvent-separated ion 
pairs (SSIPs) typically result in the formation of SEI layers with a rela­
tively poor content of LiF as well as uneven Li deposition [41]. More­
over, the relatively low affinity of ethers (vs. HMPA) for Li+ should 
minimize solvent localization and thus promote polysulfide dissolution. 
Indeed, Li–S batteries that are prepared with DOL/DME electrolytes 
often exhibit a pronounced polysulfide shuttle effect as well as signifi­
cant Li dendrite formation.
2.2. Li+ solvation structure elucidation
The coordination structures formed between a Li cation and a 
molecule of HMPA, DME, or DOL is shown in Fig. 2a. The oxygen atom 
of each solvent molecule features a negative electrostatic potential and 
therefore preferentially coordinates to Li+. The binding energy released 
by complexation of Li+ with HMPA (2.86 eV) was calculated to be higher 
than the values calculated for DME (2.75 eV) and DOL (1.68 eV) 
(Fig. 2b).
To determine the optimal loading of HMPA, a series of electrolytes 
were prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (1 M) in mixtures of DOL and HMPA 
that contained a LiNO3 additive (2 wt%) and different HMPA loadings 
(4, 6, 8, or 10 vol% of the total volume). The corresponding electrolytes 
were denoted as DOL/HMPA-4, DOL/HMPA-6, DOL/HMPA-8, and 
DOL/HMPA-10, respectively. The control electrolyte was prepared by 
dissolving LiTFSI (1 M) in a 1:1 v/v mixture of DOL and DME containing 
LiNO3 (2 wt%) and denoted as DOL/DME. Ion conductivity (σ) and Li+
transference number (tLi
+) values were initially measured to evaluate ion 
transport characteristics (see Fig. S2 for the raw data). While the σ is 
affected by the constituent cations (e.g., Li+) and anions (e.g., TFSI−), 
the tLi
+ represents the contribution of only the Li+ to the σ value. Both the 
σ and tLi
+ values were found to be correlated with the HMPA loading, with 
maximum values of 4.51 mS cm−1 and 0.88, respectively, achieved at a 
HMPA loading of 6 vol% (Fig. 2c and d). The enhancement in Li+
transport was consistent with the reduced activation energy calculated 
for the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte when compared to that calculated for 
the DOL/DME electrolyte (14.1 vs. 19.3 kJ mol−1) (Fig. S3). Moreover, 
the optimal Li+ transport properties of the electrolyte containing HMPA 
are superior to those measured for electrolytes that use amide additives 
as the corresponding tLi
+ values typically range between 0.43 and 0.66 
[42–44]. The superior Li+ transport properties can be attributed to the 
solvation structure of Li+ formed with HMPA. Further increasing the 
loading of HMPA led to reduced σ and tLi
+ values as the excess HMPA 
increased the viscosity of the electrolyte. Therefore, an HMPA loading of 
6 vol% was determined to be optimal and the DOL/HMPA-6 was used as 
the electrolyte in the following experiments.

evaluated using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The radial 
relative density calculated for the oxygen atoms around the constituent 
Li+ ions is shown in Fig. 2e. The average distance between the oxygen 
atom in HMPA (OHMPA) and the Li+ in the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte

The Li+ solvation structures were further investigated using Raman 
spectroscopy. A signal was observed at 624.1 cm−1 upon analysis of a 
mixture of HMPA (6 vol%) and DOL (Fig. 2g) and attributed to the 
bending vibration of the N−P−N bond in HMPA [45]. After LiTFSI (1 M) 
was dissolved in the mixture, the signal shifted to 644.3 cm−1 due to 
coordination of the Li+ with HMPA. Raman signals that were assigned to 
TFSI−upon analysis of the DOL/HMPA-6 and DOL/DME electrolytes are 
shown in Fig. 2h and i. The signals were deconvoluted to assess the 
quantities of AGGs, CIPs, and SSIPs present in the electrolytes. The 
content of AGGs and CIPs in the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte (83.7% in 
total) was found to be higher than that in the DOL/DME electrolyte 
(78.5%). This result was supported by the radial relative density data 
(Fig. 2e) and can be attributed to a lower quantity of free HMPA 
molecules in the former. Collectively, the theoretical and experimental 
data indicated that DOL/HMPA-6 featured a relatively low fraction of 
free HMPA and facilitated the formation of AGGs and CIPs. As previ­
ously noted, these features were expected to not only suppress the pol­
ysulfide shuttle effect but also enhance the interfacial properties of Li 
anodes.
2.3. Compatibility of the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte with Li metal 
electrodes
The compatibility of the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte with Li metal 
electrodes was assessed using Li//Li symmetric cells that contained the 
DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte. As a control, Li//Li symmetric cells were also 
prepared with the DOL/DME electrolyte. Rate capability data were 
measured at a plating/stripping capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 and current 
densities of 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 mA cm−2 for the different cells (Fig. 3a). At 
each current density, the cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte 
displayed a smaller polarization voltage than the cell containing the 
DOL/DME electrolyte. Similarly, the cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte exhibited a larger exchange current density than the cell 
containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (0.22 vs. 0.12 mA cm−2) (Fig. S5). 
These results can be attributed to an enhanced σ and a higher tLi
+ pro­
vided by the former electrolyte. The cycling stabilities of the different 
cells were measured at 1 mAh cm−2 and 1 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3b). During the 
initial stage, the cell containing the DOL/HMPA electrolyte exhibited a 
polarization voltage of ca. 0.14 V, followed by reduction to ca. 0.05 V 
and then remained stable in the subsequent cycling test up to 600 h. In 
contrast, the cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte exhibited a 
noticeable fluctuation in the polarization voltage during the cycling test, 
with the observation of a maximum value of 0.11 V. At an elevated 
current density of 5 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3c), the cell containing the DOL/ 
HMPA-6 electrolyte retained a relatively small polarization voltage 
(~0.06 V), which was only ca. one fourth of the values observed for the 
cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (a maximum value of ~0.25 V 
was observed). The reduction and stabilization of the polarization

2.4. Impact of the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte on sulfur cathodes
To study how the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte affects sulfur cathodes 
over a charge/discharge cycle, the surface morphology of the sulfur 
cathode was investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). 
Coin-type Li–S cells that contained either the DOL/HMPA-6 or the DOL/ 
DME electrolyte were used. After electrochemical cycling, the sulfur 
cathode in the cell that contained the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte was 
found to exhibit a porous surface (Fig. 4a) which matched that of its 
original (pre-cycled) morphology (Fig. S8). The morphological feature 
was ascribed to the presence of HMPA, which, due to its high DN, was 
expected to facilitate Li2S dissolution [50–52] and maintain the initial 
porous morphology of the sulfur cathode by facilitating a uniform 
deposition of Li2S [33]. In contrast, after electrochemical cycling, the 
surface of the sulfur cathode exposed to the DOL/DME electrolyte was 
found to be smooth and compact along with cracks (Fig. 4b). Such 
morphology was attributed to two-dimensional Li2S deposition, which 
prevented further accessibility to the conductive host and the conduc­
tive reagents. Collectively, the SEM data showed that the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte is effective at alleviating surface passivation and enhancing 
structural integrity of the sulfur cathode during charge/discharge 
cycling.
The interfacial properties of the electrodes in the Li–S cells were also 
evaluated using EIS. As shown in Fig. 4c, a Li–S cell that contained the 
DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte exhibited a lower Rct value than the cell 
containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (29.0 vs. 55.5 Ω) before electro­
chemical cycling. The smaller Rct value indicated that charge transfer at 
the electrode/electrolyte interface was more favorable in the DOL/ 
HMPA-6 electrolyte than in the DOL/DME electrolyte. After electro­
chemical cycling (10 charge/discharge cycles at 0.1 C), each EIS plot 
was found to exhibit two semicircles (Fig. 4d). Appearance of the second 
semicircle could be attributed to the formation of SEI layer on the Li 
anode. The electrochemical impedances associated with the two semi­
circles were attributed to (1) the interface between the electrolyte and 
the electrode surface and (2) the interface between the newly formed SEI 
layer and the Li anode [53]. The key parameters of the EIS plots were 
obtained by fitting the data using an equivalent circuit (Fig. S9). The cell 
containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte exhibited lower impedance 
values (15.2 and 16.5 Ω) when compared with the cell containing the 
DOL/DME electrolyte (34.7 and 22.2 Ω). The lower impedance observed 
for the former could be ascribed to the porous morphology of the sulfur 
cathode and the LiF-rich SEI layer formed on the Li metal anode.
Visible cells were used to assess the polysulfide shuttle effect that 
occurred in the different electrolytes. While the discharge profiles were 
recorded for the cells containing either the DOL/HMPA-6 or DOL/DME 
electrolyte (Fig. 5a), photos were taken at different discharge states 
(indicated in the discharge profiles) (Fig. 5b). The cell containing the 
DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte exhibited a specific capacity of 966.6 mAh 
g−1, a value that was more than two times higher than that measured for 
the cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (420.0 mAh g−1). Only a 
slight change in color was observed in the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte, 
whereas a significant change from colorless to yellow green (indicative 
of the presence of polysulfides [54]) was observed in the DOL/DME 
electrolyte. Moreover, the color change in the former occurred only 
during the initial period of discharge. Thus, the polysulfide shuttle effect 
appeared to be significantly suppressed in the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte, 
which manifested in the marked enhancement in device performance.

The galvanostatic charge/discharge profiles recorded at 0.1 C (1 C =
1675 mA g−1) for the Li–S cells containing either the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte or the DOL/DME electrolyte are shown in Fig. 6b. The 
charge and discharge plateaus were consistent with the oxidation and 
reduction signals observed in the CV profiles. The Li–S cell containing 
the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte displayed a much higher specific capacity 
than the cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (1374.9 and 1043.9 
mAh g−1, respectively). The ratio between the capacities corresponding 
to 2.12 and 2.29 V for the cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte 
(2.21) was closer to the theoretical value of 3 than that measured for the 
cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (2.06). This result indicated 
that the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte facilitated the conversion of poly­
sulfides to Li2S, leading to higher specific capacity of the Li–S cell. 
Enlarged charge/discharge profiles of the regions that correspond to 
Li2S nucleation and Li2S decomposition, respectively, are shown in 
Fig. 6c and 6d. During the discharge process, the Li–S cell containing the 
DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte exhibited a smaller overpotential (21 mV) that 
was attributed to favorable Li2S nucleation when compared to the cell 
containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (34 mV). When charging, the 
overpotential corresponding to Li2S decomposition was smaller for the 
Li–S cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte (60 mV) than the 
value measured for the Li–S cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte 
(91 mV). Such favorable processes may stem from the Li+ solvation 
structure formed in the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte which facilitated the 
conversion between the polysulfides and Li2S in the sulfur cathode.
The rate capabilities of the Li–S cells containing the different elec­
trolytes were measured by increasing the current rate from 0.1 to 2 C 
(Fig. 6e). At each current rate, the Li–S cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte exhibited a higher specific capacity than the cell containing 
the DOL/DME electrolyte. At 2 C, the specific capacity measured for the 
Li–S cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte was 3.34 times higher 
than the value measured for the cell containing the DOL/DME electro­
lyte (606.4 vs. 181.5 mAh g−1). When the current rate was reset back to 
0.1 C, the Li–S cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte recovered 
most of its initial capacity (939.2 mAh g−1 vs. an initial value of 1110.4 
mAh g−1).
The cycling stabilities of the Li–S cells containing either the DOL/ 
HMPA-6 electrolyte or the DOL/DME electrolyte were measured at a 
current rate of 0.2 C since the polysulfide shuttle effect is exacerbated at 
low current rates [33]. As shown in Fig. 6f, the Li–S cell containing the 
DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte initially exhibited a higher specific capacity 
than the cell containing the DOL/DME electrolyte (1101.0 vs. 882.4 
mAh g−1, respectively). After 300 cycles, the specific capacity measured 
for the cell containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte retained signifi­
cantly more of its initial value than the control (619.6 vs. 295.6 mAh 
g−1, respectively). Similarly, the Li–S cells containing the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte also outperformed the cells that were prepared with other 
electrolytes reported in the literature (Table S2). The significant 
enhancement in cycling stability can be attributed to the ability of 
HMPA to suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect.
3. Conclusions
We report that using a phosphoramide (i.e., HMPA) as co-solvent in 
electrolytes can overcome the longstanding issues associated with Li–S 
batteries, particularly the polysulfide shuttle effect and unwanted Li 
dendrite growth. Theoretical and experimental data revealed that 
HMPA preferentially locates in the inner sheath of a Li+ solvation 
structure, which led to a reduced quantity of free HMPA molecules and 
effectively suppressed the polysulfide shuttle effect. The use of HMPA 
also led to the formation of CIPs and AGGs that enabled the formation of 
LiF-rich SEI layers, which prevented the growth of Li dendrites. As a 
result, the Li//Li symmetric cells that contained the DOL/HMPA-6 
electrolyte exhibited stable cycling for more than 600 h at 5 mA cm−2 
and did so with a reduced polarization voltage that was only ca. 25% of 
the value recorded for a cell containing the conventional DOL/DME 
electrolyte. The DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte also afforded Li–S cells that 
delivered significantly enhanced specific capacity (606.4 vs. 181.5 mAh 
g−1 at 2 C) and cycling stability (619.6 vs. 295.2 mAh g−1 after 300 
cycles at 0.2 C) when compared with analogous cells that were prepared 
with the conventional electrolyte. The findings provide a means to bring 
Li–S batteries closer toward widespread practical use and the funda­
mental theory proposed may be adapted to bestow similar beneficial 
effects in other types of metal batteries.
