Low Concentration Electrolyte Enabling Cryogenic 
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Fulu Chu, Meng Wang, Jiamin Liu, Zengqiang Guan, Huanyu Yu, Bin Liu,* 
and Feixiang Wu*
Lithium–sulfur chemistry suffers from poor conversion reaction kinetics, 
causing low-capacity utilization of sulfur cathodes, particularly at cryogenic 
temperatures. Herein, based on low-cost and abundant commercial sulfur par-
ticles directly, a low concentration electrolyte (LCE, 0.1 m) is employed to accel-
erate lithium–sulfur conversion reaction at low temperatures, demonstrating 
a broad applicability of this approach. Compared to conventional concentra-
tion (1.0 m) electrolytes, the proposed LCE successfully enhances conversion 
kinetics from Li2S4 to Li2S and restrains shuttle effects of polysulfides, resulting 
in higher capacity utilizations and more stable cycle performance at 0 and 
−20 °C. Further interfacial chemistry analyses on cycled electrodes reveal that 
a hybrid surface layer dominated by organic species as well as some favorable 
inorganics is constructed in the LCE, demonstrating smaller surface layer 
resistance. In situ EIS measurements at 0 °C and CV tests reveal main differ-
ences of electrode kinetics in 0.1 and 1 m electrolytes, further explaining the 
differences in working mechanism of two electrolytes. These findings eluci-
date the roles of LCEs on realizing faster kinetics for cryogenic lithium–sulfur 
batteries and provide a simple, low-cost, and widely applicable pathway for 
achieving high-performance lithium–sulfur batteries under extreme conditions.

the non-topological conversion chemistry to deliver a higher 
capacity than that of intercalation ones, but with a larger 
volume change.[1a,d,2b,c] According to the typical voltage profiles 
of nonaqueous Li–S batteries, there are basically two steps (i.e., 
two distinct voltage plateaus) in the discharge.[1d,2a,b] The first 
one at the high voltage region (from 2.5 to 2.3 V) corresponds 
to sulfur conversion reaction that undergoes the breakage of the 
sulfur ring, forming long-chain sulfur species that are rather 
soluble in ether-based electrolytes. These species are so-called 
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) with the generation of intractable 
shuttle effects. The other one located at lower voltage range 
(at ≈2.1  V) is in accord with the transformation from short-
chain polysulfides to Li2S2/Li2S. Indeed, each charging and 
discharging circulation is a complicated multiple-phase elec-
trochemical process. The fast kinetics in sulfur redox reaction 
causing complete Li–S conversion reaction in every cycle would 
be of utmost importance consequently.[1d,2a,3a] However, slug-
gish kinetics of conversion reaction hardly keep the complete 
transformation from Li2S4 to Li2S.[5] Coupled with the insu-
lating properties of the final discharge product Li2S, this situa-
tion will be exacerbated at low temperatures. Hence, to succeed 
in low-temperature performances of Li–S batteries, accelerating 
the conversion kinetics especially for that in the Li2S4-to-Li2S 
conversion is rather important.[3b,5c]

As conventional lithium–ion batteries (LIBs) meet their 
bottleneck in energy densities, the emerging lithium–sulfur 
(Li–S) batteries have shown potential to achieve higher energy

So far, to enhance the performance of cryogenic Li–S 
batteries, physical and chemical confinements, as well as 
electrocatalysts have been developed, such as porous car-
bons,[6] metal oxides,[7] metal sulfides,[7a,8] metal nitrides,[9] 
single-atom electrocatalysts,[10] et  al. Combined with a 
well-designed 
electrocatalytic 
center,[1d,11] 
some 
sulfur 
host materials with multifunctional matrices can promote 
sulfur redox kinetics and thus boost their low-tempera-
ture performance. Zhang and co-workers developed a syn-
thetic MoSe2 composite enriched with edge sites, enabling 
an improved low-temperature performance.[12] Dong et  al. 
designed novel graphene-supported BN nanosheets to firmly 
entrap polysulfide via chemisorption. Their synergistic effect 
achieved a high utilization of sulfur in a wide temperature 
range, the discharge capacity reached 650  mAh  g–1 even at 
−40  °C.[3a] Besides, another composite denoted as graphene 
oxide-Zn(II)-triazole showed enhanced adsorption of poly-
sulfides at both RT and LT scenes, delivering a discharge 
capacity of 315 mAh g–1 after 100 cycles at −20 and 0.5 C.[13] 
Beyond the methodology of constructing sulfur host mate-
rials, electrolyte design for LT Li–S batteries is emerging and 
on the rise. An earlier study has shown the “solvent-in-salt” 
electrolyte (7  m LiTFSI) can achieve good cycling perfor-
mance at −20  °C.[14] In addition, fluorobenzene (FB)[15] and 
diethyl ether (DEE)[16] as functional cosolvents were used in 
electrolytes for high-performance LT Li–S batteries (−20 and 
−60 °C). More recently, a novel electrolyte with the addition of 
allyl methyl disulfide was designed by Yan and co-workers.[5c] 
The produced electrolyte accomplished an all-liquid-phase 
conversion reaction, thus significantly increasing the kinetics 
of Li–S redox reactions. Even under an ultralow temperature 
condition (−60 °C), a high reversible capacity of 1563 mAh g−1 
was obtained at 0.05 C.
Among these previous studies, electrolyte modification is 
deemed as a facile and powerful method to meliorate the ion 
diffusion and stabilize electrode/electrolyte interface.[2c,4c,17] 
With modified recipes crossing salts, solvents, and func-
tional additives, the unmanageable shuttle of LiPSs inter-
mediates and lithium dendric growth can be effectively 
addressed.[2c,18] Recently, the work by Wu and co-workers 
employed a low concentration electrolyte for ambient Li–S 
batteries, delivering high cycling capacity retention and 
excellent rate capability up to 2C.[19] This is mainly due 
to their low viscosity, better wettability, and inhibition of 
polysulfide shuttle at ordinary temperatures. Herein, a low 
concentration electrolyte (LCE) was employed to enhance 
the electrochemical performance of lithium–sulfur batteries 
under harsher conditions including low temperatures, gen-
eral sulfur cathodes and high mass loadings. LCEs are found 
to provide higher capacity and higher capacity retention even 
at low temperatures. More importantly, the electrochemistry 
and kinetics behaviors of lithium–sulfur batteries under low 
temperatures are thoroughly studied by the feat of LCEs. 
These findings and insights would elucidate the roles of 
low concentration in realizing faster kinetics for cryogenic 
lithium–sulfur batteries. Additionally, this methodology of 
the LCE might provide a practical and feasible approach 
to accomplish more practical cryogenic lithium–sulfur 
batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Electrochemical Performance of Cryogenic Li–S Batteries via 
Low Concentration Electrolytes
To display the maximum improvement effect of the produced 
electrolyte, commercial sulfur chunks were directly employed to 
mix with conventional conductive agent graphene without any 
functional matrix or specific catalysis. As shown in Figure  S1 
(Supporting Information), the sulfur particles with size of 
≈10–30 µm were wrapped by graphene, resulting in a graphene–
sulfur (G–S) composite. Then electrochemical properties of Li–S 
batteries using G–S cathodes (areal sulfur loading: 1.0 mg cm–2) 
were evaluated at 0 °C. Using 0.1 m electrolyte, the cell presents 
much higher reversible capacities than that in the standard 
electrolyte at various current densities (Figure 1a–c) with more 
normal corresponding discharge/charge curves (Figure  1d–i). 
As shown in Figure 1a, undecorated G–S cathodes with 0.1 m 
electrolyte can deliver a specific capacity ≈900 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C 
and a high-capacity retention of 97.7% with high coulombic effi-
ciency (CE) over 99% within 200 cycles. But in the standard 1 m 
electrolyte, the initial discharge capacity is only 310  mAh  g–1, 
which is apparently lower than that in 0.1 m electrolyte. In addi-
tion, the sulfur redox reaction in 1 m electrolyte at low tempera-
ture undergoes longer activation cycle, indicating the initially 
poor reaction kinetics in such cells. The highest reversible 
capacity after 30 cycles activation is only 483 mAh g–1. After 200 
cycles, the discharge capacity is reduced to 320  mAh  g−1 with 
relatively low CE <99%, indicating a poor cycle stability (capacity 
retention: 66%) in 1  m electrolyte (Figure  1a). The initial acti-
vation process is very common in Li–S systems,[20] which are 
found in the cells with two electrolytes (Figure 1a). The sulfur 
electrode wettability will keep improving with more interfacial 
reactions and sequent electrolyte infiltration during the initial 
cycles. Moreover, the size of active sulfur particles is getting 
smaller with more formation of soluble lithium polysulfides.[21] 
Considering the sulfur used is the commercial chunks, the 
performance can be enhanced as the smaller particle size con-
tributes to the fast kinetics in the charge transfer process. How-
ever, the sulfur redox reaction in the 1.0  m system undergoes 
an incomplete process and longer time (≈20 cycles) than that 
with the LCE for battery activation, due to the poorer wettability 
and finite kinetic capability of the 1 m electrolyte. The cycle sta-
bilities at higher C rates were also investigated in Figure 1b,c. 
The discharge capacity in 0.1 m can achieve 754.6 mAh g−1 with 
CE of 97.2% under 0.2 C and 574.9 mAh g−1 with CE of 96.8% 
at the rate of 0.5 C after 200 cycles, demonstrating promising 
cycle stabilities. In contrast, the cells with 1 m electrolyte dem-
onstrate much lower capacities, viz. 311.5 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and 
207.8 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C at 200th cycles. The corresponding CE 
of these cells with 1 m electrolyte is 93.2% and 94.7%, which is 
lower than those of LCE cells.
Similarly, the charge/discharge curves at different cycle 
numbers and different C rates (Figure 1d–i and Figure 2a) fur-
ther lead to a better understanding of the linkage between the 
electrolyte concentration and their reversible capacity. The LCE 
maintains a good curve shape with visibly higher discharge 
capacities and smaller voltage hysteresis at different C rates, 
providing better kinetics for short chain polysulfide-to-solid

The feasibility of 0.1  m electrolyte for cryogenic Li–S bat-
teries was sequentially evaluated under harsher condition, 
viz. −20 °C, toward practical applications. Figure 4 shows the 
cycling performances and corresponding voltage curves of cells 
using two electrolytes at −20 °C. At such low temperature, the 
reaction rate of sulfur redox in batteries inevitably becomes 
very slow. The conversion reaction that the S8 converts to long-
chain polysulfides might be possible, but the transition from 
the Li2S4 immediate to the solid Li2S2 or even Li2S would be 
rather difficult.[5c,12] As shown in Figure 4a, the KB–S cathode 
with a conventional loading (2.5 mg cm–2) is not able to accom-
plish the solid phase conversion in the SCE at 0.2 C, providing 
a very low initial capacity of 200  mAh  g−1. Coupled with the 
poorer wettability of 1 m electrolyte, typical voltage profiles dis-
play the almost disappeared second voltage platform, further 
confirming the serious deterioration in the reaction kinetics 
for the sulfur redox under this cold temperature (Figure 4b, the 
10th cycle). However, the uniform redistribution of active sulfur 
can be facilitated after multiple cycles of solid-liquid conver-
sion between long-chain polysulfides and elemental sulfur.[21] 
Then the limited redox reactions can be carried out owing to 
the improved interface kinetics involving the ion and electron 
contact,[23] leading to the presence of the second plateaus in 
discharge curves (Figure 4b, the 60th cycle). While for the cell 
using LCE at 0.2  C, it liberates a high discharge capacity of 
711 mAh g−1 at 1st cycle and 652.9 mAh g−1 at 80th cycle, pro-
viding a clear second voltage plateau with smaller polarization 
(Figure 4a,b).
When continued to increase the sulfur loading to 2.9 and 
3.5 mg cm–2, the capacity utilization and cycling performance 
under extreme conditions were evaluated in Figure  4e,f. The 
LCE can still maintain its competitive edge. At the mass loading 
of 2.9  mg  cm–2, the discharge capacity achieves 561  mAh  g–1 
with successful activation and maintains an acceptable value 
of 454 mAh g−1 after 80 cycles (Figure 4e). While in the SCE, 
the reversible capacity could barely deliver to 120  mAh  g−1, 
indicating the highly incomplete sulfur conversion reaction. 
Moreover, the high sulfur utilization can be guaranteed in the 
LCE system even with the higher loading (3.5 mg cm−2) cath-
odes. The capacities of 637.3, 509.3, 523.8, and 384.8 mAh g–1 
are retained after the 1st, 40th, 50th, and 80th cycles at 0.2 C 
(Figure  4g), respectively. As for the conventional system, the 
stable capacity hovers ≈160  mAh  g−1 with very limited sulfur 
conversion. Moreover, their corresponding voltage profiles 
(Figure 4f–h) further demonstrate the effective enhancement by 
the LCE, since the obvious voltage platforms can be well main-
tained in 0.1 m system. After being tested at different rates of 
0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 C (Figure S6, Supporting Information), the LCE 
offers a discharge capacity of 672, 521.1, and 336.4  mAh  g–1, 
respectively. But the cell using the standard electrolyte delivers 
corresponding capacities of 209.2, 189.6, and 130.9  mAh  g–1, 
showing rather poor capacity utilization in 1  m electrolyte at 
−20 °C. In order to be closer to the actual industry conditions, 
considerable attention is paid to further reducing the volume of 
electrolytes in the electrochemical tests, which is to employ the 
low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratios.[5a,24] As shown in Figure S7 
(Supporting Information), the cells with dilute solutions pro-
vide better sulfur conversion reaction with higher discharge 
capacity and better capacity retention at 0 and −20 °C, especially 
in the transition of short-chain polysulfides to solid sulfides 
during the discharge process. Moreover, the parallel experi-
ments in Figure 4c,d under low temperatures further indicate 
the better consistency performance in the LCE and poor uni-
formity of electrochemical performances existed in the standard 
electrolyte. This can be concluded that the internal kinetics 
using SCE at cold temperatures is unstable and variant, as well 
as long periods of activation are often required to sustain the 
sulfur utilization or even the activation failure under higher 
mass loading and lean electrolyte conditions (Figure  4e–h; 
Figure S7, Supporting Information).

are selected as a measure indicator to estimate the improve-

low sweep speed. Another impressive aspect is the disappear-

kinetics for the cells with the dilute solution, especially for the 
scenarios at −20 °C (Figure 6c). Then, the diffusion coefficient 
of Li ions (DLi+) for each stage of the discharge process is cal-

Supporting Information).[25] For systems with LCEs, DLi+ 
values are significantly superior to those in the control group 
(1.0 m electrolytes) under low-temperature conditions, such as 
a diffusion coefficient (peak I at −20 °C) of ≈7.1 × 10–7 cm2 s−1 
for LCEs is appreciably higher than that for the conventional 
(≈1.4 × 10–9 cm2 s−1). In summary, these results prove convinc-
ingly the importance of low-concentration electrolytes in pro-
moting reaction kinetics and managing the interface behaviors 
on the electrode surface.

2.3. Morphology Evolution of Cycled Electrodes in Cryogenic 
Li–S Batteries
To investigate cycling stability of electrodes with two electro-
lytes, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on cycled electrodes 
was used to display the surface morphology change. As shown 
in Figure 8a,b, nanostructure integrity of the cycled cathode in 
0.1  m electrolyte can be well maintained along with minimal 
cracks, etchings, and pulverization. The flat surface topography 
indicates more uniform polysulfides deposition and control-
lable diffusion of polysulfide species in 0.1 m electrolyte. Since 
short-chain polysulfide (Li2S2) tends to be aggregated (less 
soluble) in 0.1  m electrolyte, the chemical binding between 
long-chain polysulfide and Li2S2 could successfully inhibit the 
shuttle effects of polysulfides.[19] In the contrast, the surface of 
cycled cathode demonstrates more pores, cracks, and uneven 
shuttle deposition of lithium polysulfides (yellow dotted circle 
in Figure  8d),[28] indicating serious polysulfide shuttle effects, 
poor reaction kinetics and limited solid-to-solid conversion in 
standard 1  m electrolyte under low temperature. The soluble 
polysulfides are hardly converted into solid sulfides, resulting 
in more viscous electrolytes and uncontrolled diffusion of such 
soluble LiPSs. Thus, the morphology of cycled cathodes became 
increasingly rough with more pores and cracks (Figure  8c,d). 
The cycled Li anode in the 1.0 m electrolyte displays the same 
character, proving the serious erosion of soluble LiPSs to the 
pristine lithium metal. With the passivated surface on the Li 
anode and volume changes at arbitrary sites, the cycled lithium 
foil appears in more crude regions with vertical lithium den-
drites and forms surface pulverization of metallic lithium sub-
strate (Figure  8g,h), indicating uneven Li deposition during 
cycles. Interestingly, the cycled Li anode in 0.1  m electrolyte 
(Figure  8e,f) shows a homogenous and highly even surface 
containing large Li metal grains that are ordered arrangement 
with smooth surface morphology. More importantly, there were 
not any visible sulfides precipitation and harmful lithium fila-
ments (so-called lithium dendrites). This means the polysulfide 
shuttle effects and the electrolyte-lithium anode interface issues 
are successfully suppressed in the 0.1 m electrolyte under low 
temperature.
2.4. Mechanism of Low Concentration Electrolytes to Realize 
Fast Kinetics in Cryogenic Li–S Batteries
The kinetics of the complicated sulfur redox reaction can be 
radically reflected in the apparent electrochemical imped-
ance. The cells with SCE and LCE were both cycled in a cold 
environment (0  °C), and then in situ electrochemical imped-
ance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were applied at different dis-
charge or charge depths under low temperature condition (see 
Figure  9; Figure  S8, Supporting Information). Typically, the 
EIS curve mainly includes three parts from high-frequency 
interferential signal to the low frequency. That are the internal 
resistance (Rs, the X-intercept in the high-frequency range), 
interfacial impedance (Ri, the semicircle(s) existed in the high 
to middle frequency region), and diffusion impedance (War-
burg impedance).[27b,29] Among those, interfacial impedance is 
a primary focus in this study, which is involved in the resist-
ance across the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer (RSEI) 
and charge-transfer resistance (Rct).
At the initial state of Li–S cells, the interfacial impedance 
usually shows a single syncretic semicircle (Figure  9), dem-
onstrating smaller resistance on both Rs and Ri in the conven-
tional system, which is due to the higher ionic conductivity of 
1.0 m electrolyte. With the discharge process going from open 
circuit potential (OCP) to 2.05 V (Figure S8, Supporting Infor-
mation), the sulfur cathodes undergo the first voltage plateau

2.5. Interfacial Chemistry of Electrode/Electrolyte Interfaces
Since the fast kinetics and the superior rate capability of Li–S 
cells are also incredibly reliant on a stable interfacial layer, surface 
component analysis and relevant chemical bonding situations 
were analyzed by employing the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS) technology. The Li–S cells were cycled with two electrolytes 
under 0 °C and dissembled after 500 cycles at 0.1 C. The com-
parison of the C 1s, F 1s, and S 2p spectra of electrode interfaces 
(SEI and cathode electrolyte interphase, CEI) formed in the LCE 
(Figure 10a–c,g–i) and SCE (Figure 10d–f,j–l) was detailed sum-
marized. High-resolution XPS presents specific peaks and rel-
evant peak assignments with the deconvolution method, clearly 
reflecting the various oxidation states of these key elements. The 
calibration reference object here is selected as the C 1s peak at 
284.8 eV. There are clear and pronounced differences in the XPS 
spectra (especially for C 1s, F 1s, and S 2p spectra) of two samples 
both for Li metal anodes and sulfur cathodes.
On the anode side, the results in C 1s spectra for 0.1 m electro-
lyte (Figure 10a) show a noticeable peak that should be ascribed 
to CC species and the almost invisible CF peak.[31] For other 
C 1s peaks, the typical carbon-based species corresponding to 
the Li2CO3 and CO units can be observed respectively.[31a,32] 
But an obvious sign of COR (R  =  alkyl) peak is showed in 
the conventional system,[31,33] while other carbon peaks give a 
normal strength (Figure  10d). The stronger intensity of these 
carbon-based components indicates the composition differ-
ences of the respective SEI layer, particularly the organic-
dominated species of CC and CO3 at the outer SEI layer in 
the LCE.[26,29b] This tendency can be extensively validated that 
the SEI layer consisted of both inorganic and organic species 
and the outer layer offers more organic species like COOR, 
CxHyOz, and CO3.[29b] More special for the case with low 
concentration, the low molar ratio of salt to solvent is prone to 
induce an organic-dominated film as massive solvents show the 
priority reduction.[26] The difference in the CF peak proves 
the reduced side reactions (consuming Li salt) in the dilute as 
the related functional group -CF3 may most likely arise from the 
decomposition of the anion from LiTFSI salt.[31a] This phenom-
enon also can be displayed in the F 1s spectra with an extremely 
weak peak assigned to the CF in 0.1 m electrolyte (Figure 10b).
In terms of peak intensity alone, the spectral intensity of 
F and S elements in the low concentration system is slightly 
lower, which is also justified as their low molar ratio of salt to 
solvent. But the ratio among the resultant species in each sur-
face layer is rather different. As for the spectra of F element 
(Figure 10b), main peaks exist at 688.4 and 685 eV, which are 
assigned for CF bond and LiF, respectively.[32–34] The high con-
tent of LiF in the surface layer driven by LCE should originate 
from the LiTFSI decomposition.[31a,34] According to the previous 
study, LiTFSI was largely dissociated under the circumstance 
of low concentrations, which may contribute to more reduction 
products.[19] LiF as a well-known and almost ubiquitous com-
position in SEI layer, can render a better SEI layer for both sta-
bility and fast ion diffusion due to the high young's modulus 
and low diffusion energy barrier.[31b,35] Consequently, the sturdy 
SEI layer with good flatness (proved by SEM) can be obtained 
with the LCE, causing enhanced rate capability. While for 
the rest components, the degradation reactions to induce the 
LiCF3 and Li2NSO2CF3 is suppressed in the presence of low 
concentration.[31a] Hence, LiTFSI is mainly decomposed into 
LiF during the initial cycles in the LCE system, and further 
accompanied with decreased adverse reactions (low fraction 
of CF species) as the already stabilized interface. Since the 
crystalline state of lithium fluoride component (crystalline or 
amorphous) or the presence of chemical bonding of adsorbed 
polysulfides on electrodes might also affect its specific value 
of binding energy, the peaks assigned to LiF in Figure 10 has 
changed slightly with different binding energies as some cases 
in other literatures.[36] Whereas in the SCE system, the change 
of binding energy of CF peak (Figure 10e) toward the lower 
position indicates the complex degradations with more by-prod-
ucts (CFx) as more electron clouds are gathered.[34]
Besides, due to the fast kinetics of sulfur redox and complete 
discharge process, more sulfur-based species can be seen in 
that of 0.1 m electrolyte (Figure 10c). Some extra peaks exist at 
169, 167, 163.6, and 161.7 eV in the S 2p spectra, reflecting the 
composition of [SO4]2–, [SO3]2–, S0 (CS, SS) and Li2S2, 
and in the SEI layer.[31a,33,37] The existence of Li2Sx indicated the 
chemical reaction between Li metal and soluble polysulfides.

3. Conclusion
In summary, high-performance Li–S batteries with fast sulfur 
conversion kinetics, stable cycling and optimization in interface 
chemistry were achieved in cold environments via employing 
a low-concentration electrolyte. As revealed by detailed experi-
mental results, the low concentration electrolyte can enable a 
fast kinetics especially for the challenging solid-to-solid reaction 
process in the simple carbon-based sulfur cathodes. The cell 
using 0.1 m electrolyte can achieve 900 (0.1 C), 750 (0.2 C), and 
600 (0.5 C) mAh g–1 with the commercial sulfur chunks (G–S), 
displaying the smaller voltage hysteresis and longer second 
discharge platform than them in the standard 1  m electrolyte. 
Further analysis on interfacial chemistry proves that a more pro-
tective layer with versatile components exists both on the anode 
and cathode via the self-constructed low-concentration effects. 
The in situ EIS measurements of Li–S batteries further prove 
the smaller SEI layer resistance and charge transfer resistance in 
0.1 m electrolyte during cycling, and less solubility of short-chain 
polysulfide (Li2S2) in 0.1  m electrolyte resulting in suppressed 
shuttle of polysulfides. Consequently, the Li–S batteries show a 
longer cycling durability and better reversible capacity despite 
operating in cryogenic environments. Even at a subzero tempera-
ture (−20 °C), a high capacity of 600 mAh g–1 at 0.2 C is achieved 
with a stable capacity retention. Considering these positive effects 
and cost reduction by utilizing the low concentration electrolyte, 
this work can contribute to realize more practical Li–S batteries 
with excellent performances at low temperature conditions. 
Moreover, low concentration electrolytes may simultaneously 
achieve the high sulfur mass loading cathodes and low electrolyte 
volume. Thus, determining a reasonable component in low con-
centration electrolytes is particularly important that can ensure 
the better coordination of the battery performance and the cost of 
electrolyte. Future strategies like the selection of functional addi-
tives and adoption of optimized co-solvents are also great ideas.
