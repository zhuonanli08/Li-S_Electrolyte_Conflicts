A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries have been acknowledged as one of the most fascinating energy storage devices 
due to their remarkable theoretical energy density. However, low conductivity and polysulfide shuttling are 
notorious challenges for their commercial applications. Here, by directly introducing fluoroalkyl ether 2,2,2-tri­
fluoroethyl-1,1,2,3,3,3-hexafluoropropyl ether (THE) into commercial ether electrolytes, we design a fluoroalkyl 
ether electrolyte to improve high-rate cyclic performance of Li–S battery. As confirmed by density functional 
theory calculations, in-situ Raman, and electron density maps, the THE electrolyte, with abundant high-polarity 
CF3 and CF2 groups, fully wets the sulfur cathode and inhibits the polysulfide shuttling, owing to the intense 
repulsive interaction (1.97 eV) with polysulfide ions and the robust absorption interaction (−2.42 eV) with sulfur 
cathode. With the THE electrolyte, the Li–S battery cycling 500 times at 5 C (10.5 mA cm−2) delivers a capacity 
retention of 53.3%, holding promise for developing high-rate, long-period Li–S batteries.   
1. Introduction 
Since the 21st century, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been closely 
related to the modern production and life style [1,2]. Owing to the 
ultra-high theoretical capacity (1675 mAh g−1) of sulfur, lithium-sulfur 
(Li–S) batteries have received widespread attention [3–6]. Nevertheless, 
the application of Li–S battery as next-generation energy storage device 
still faces three severe challenges: (1) the sulfur owns inferior 
conductivity (5 × 10−30), seriously restricting high-rate capacity and 
sulfur loading of Li–S battery; (2) the huge volume variation of sulfur 
during repeated charging/discharging causes the active substance to fall 
off; (3) dissolved polysulfide shuttles to negative electrode and reacts 
with

metal,

to

performance 
degradation.

2. Results and discussion 
Design of the the Electrolyte Suppressing the Shuttling Effect 
and Wetting S@C Cathode: All electrolyte constituents studied in this 
work are presented in Table S1 (Supporting Information). As displayed 
in Fig. 1a, the long/middle-chain polysulfides (Li2Sn, n ≥4) are 
extremely soluble and generate dissolution regions in commercial DOL 
+ DME electrolyte [7,20]. Then, driven by the concentration gradient, 
Li2Sn (n ≥4) shuttles from the S@C cathode to Li-metal anode and is 
reduced to short-chain polysulfides, giving rise to the loss of active 
material. Moreover, insoluble short-chain polysulfides (Li2S2/Li2S) 
precipitate around the negative electrode and corrodes the Li metal, 
leading to an undesired Li dendrite growth. Besides, the inherent low 
conductivity of sulfur and poor wettability between conventional elec­
trolyte and cathode cause difficult Li+ transference, leading to increased 
impedance. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 1b, the highly fluorinated THE 
electrolyte with ultra-low surface tension enhances the wettability of 
cathode, which in turn maintains the cathode structural integrity and 
improves the conductivity for sulfur. Owing to the robust electronega­
tivity, the F atoms inside the THE molecule exhibit electron enrichment, 
inhibiting the shuttle effect of the negatively charged polysulfide ions by 
charge repulsion effect. Thus, the S@C cathode and Li-metal anode are 
well protected. The surface morphology of both electrodes after 100 
cycles in DOL + DME and THE electrolytes is depicted by scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM). As displayed in Fig. 1c and d, the Li-metal 
surface in DOL + DME electrolyte after cycling exhibits abundant 
moss-like Li dendrites, which is attributed to the drastic reactions be­
tween the Li-metal anode and polysulfides [21], resulting in continu­
ously increased impedance and a huge hazard of short circuit. By 
contrast, the Li anode in THE electrolyte presents a dense and flat sur­
face (Fig. 1f and g), promoting the stable cyclability and high Coulombic 
efficiency. For the S@C cathodes after cycling in different electrolytes, 
as shown in Fig. 1e and h, the active material degrades by the polysulfide 
shuttle effect and numerous cracks appear on the cathode using DOL +
DME electrolyte during repeated cycles, which would accelerate the 
deterioration of the electrochemical performance of the S@C cathode. In 
contrast, an intact surface without obvious cracks is observed in Fig. 1h, 
suggesting that the THE electrolyte with a high wettability and robust 
charge repulsion can effectively keep the structural integrity of the S@C 
cathode. 
Physicochemical Properties of Electrolytes: The insulating prop­
erty of sulfur (δ = 5 × 10−30 S cm−1) largely limits the electrochemical 
performances of the Li–S batteries. High wettability electrolyte plays an 
essential role in enhancing the ion conduction of the electrolyte-cathode 
interface and active material utilization. As shown in Fig. 2a–c and S1 of 
Supporting Information, the wettabilities of different electrolytes were 
studied by testing the electrolyte uptake and contact angle. The contact 
angle between 60%THE electrolyte and S@C cathode is only 13◦, 
considerably lower than that between DOL + DME electrolyte (32◦) and 
S@C cathode. After the introduction of THE introducing, the electrolyte 
uptake of the S@C cathode is significantly increased. The decreased 
contact angle and improved electrolyte absorption are attributed to the 
highly fluorinated THE with ultra-low surface tension of the highly 
fluorinated THE. The density functional theory (DFT) calculations were 
conducted to validate the enhanced affinity between THE and S@C 
cathode (Fig. 2d–i). To calculate the adsorption energy, the conductive 
graphite is modelled by graphene, because the binding energy only 
changes slightly (<0.10 eV) with double layers. The adsorption energy 
value of THE-graphene (−2.52 eV) is far larger than those of DOL- 
graphene (−2.19 eV) and DME-graphene (−2.20 eV). The adsorption 
energy values of the three solvents depending on the different action 
sites of S atoms are calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. S1. The 
results indicate that the action sites of S atoms indeed affect the 
adsorption energy associated with the neighboring atomic charges, and 
the lowest energy of THE-sulfur (−2.42 eV) is far greater than those of 
DOL-sulfur (−1.50 eV) and DME-sulfur (−1.61 eV), as displayed in 
Fig. 2g–i. Similarly, the adsorption energy value of THE-sulfur (−2.42 
eV) is far greater than those of DOL-sulfur (−1.37 eV) and DME-sulfur 
(−1.41 eV). Due to the robust adsorption to graphene and sulfur, the 
electrolyte with THE addition exhibits much more significant wettability 
to the S@C cathode, in comparison to the reference electrolyte. The 
inherent low conductivity of sulfur limits the rate capacity and cathode 
loading of Li–S cell. As shown in Fig. 2j, the conductivity of S@C cathode 
with the 60%THE electrolyte is 47 mS cm−1, about 3 times that of S@C 
cathode with the reference electrolyte, significantly facilitating the Li+
transference in the composite cathode. The enhanced conductivity is 
because that the 60%THE electrolyte with strong affinity (Fig. 2d–i) for 
sulfur and carbon exhibits a high wettability (Fig. 2a–c and S2) for the 
S@C cathode. The conductivity of the S@C cathode with the plenty 
infiltrated electrolyte is increased. In addition, as a highly fluorinated 
solvent, the THE introduction enhances LiF ratio of cathode/electrolyte 
interface from 2% to 46%, as shown in Fig. S3. Owing to large interfacial 
energy and low Li+ diffusion barrier [22,23], the high-ratio LiF is 
beneficial for the rapid transfer of Li+ at the S@C cathode surface in the 
60%THE electrolyte. Moreover, LiF owns excellent anti-oxidation sta­
bility (6.4 V vs. Li/Li+) and high mechanical strength (4761 eV/Å2 MPa) 
[24,25]. For the SEI and CEI layers in the DOL + DME electrolyte 
(Figs. S2b and S2d), the LiF signals are significantly weaker than those of 
the interfaces with the 60%THE electrolyte. The high-ratio organic 
fluorides (F–C and SO2FY) are observed, which possess inferior Li+
conductivity [26]. The optical image and ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) 
absorption spectra of saturated Li2S8 in different electrolytes after 
1-week standing are displayed in Fig. S4. For the reference electrolyte, 
there is an obvious peak in the wavelength from 400 to 450 nm, cor­
responding to the absorption peak of Li2S8 [27]. After adding THE, a 
gradual decrease appears in the peak intensity and the transparency of 
electrolytes rises with the increased THE content, due to the mitigated 
dispersion and dissolution of oxygen and sulfur in the high-THE elec­
trolyte. The Raman spectra and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) mea­
surements were applied to evaluate the S@C cathode after cycling. As 
shown in Fig. 2k, Raman spectra shows that the positive electrode with 
the reference electrolyte exhibits almost no S signal, while there exists 
obvious S signal in the system based on the 60%THE electrolyte. As 
displayed in Figure 2l, the S concentration is 51.7 ppm for the cathode 
after cycling in the DOL + DME electrolyte, remarkably higher than that 
based on 60%THE electrolyte (22.6 ppm). The polysulfide driven by the 
concentration gradient would shuttle from the cathode to the anode. 
Likewise, for the Li-metal side, as presented in Fig. S5, the S concen­
tration is 6.1 ppm regarding the anode after cycling in the commercial 
electrolyte, remarkably higher than that based on the 60%THE elec­
trolyte (2.5 ppm). These results indicate that the 60%THE electrolyte 
inhibits the polysulfide shuttling and reduces the reduction of sulfur 
active material. The compatibility between Li anode and different 
electrolytes is evaluated thought two types of cells: Li/Cu and Li/Li. As 
shown in Figures 2m and n, the overpotential of the Li/Cu cell using 60% 
THE electrolyte is 79 mV, significantly lower than that using DOL +
DME electrolyte (118 mV). The decreased polarization confirms the 
improved reaction kinetics with the 60%THE electrolyte. Besides, the 
average Coulombic efficiency of the Li/Cu cell using 60%THE electro­
lyte is 98.8%, remarkably above that of the Li/Cu cell using DOL + DME 
electrolyte, indicating more uniform and reversible Li+ plating/strip­
ping in the 60%THE electrolyte. The Li/Cu cell further demonstrates the 
excellent compatibility of the 60%THE electrolyte to the Li-metal anode. 
As displayed in Fig. 2m–o, the THE electrolyte enhances the Coulombic 
efficiency by decreasing the polarization. The average Coulombic effi­
ciency of 60%THE electrolyte is 98.8% at 0.5 mA cm−2, significantly 
higher than DOL + DME electrolyte (91.1%).

The severe self-discharge of Li–S battery using the conventional DOL 
+ DME electrolyte reduces the storage life and severely challenges the 
large-scale implementations of Li–S batteries [29]. Generally, the 
self-discharge is primarily induced by the shuttling of dissolved poly­
sulfides from the positive electrode. Thus, the suppression of polysulfide 
shuttling can in turn decrease the self-discharge of the Li–S batteries. 
The time-dependent open circuit voltages (OCV) are recorded to eval­
uate the self-discharge phenomenon (Fig. 3e). The cell with 60%THE 
electrolyte maintains a stable OCV for ten days, showing that the loss of 
active materials and the conversion of sulfur to polysulfides in the 60% 
THE electrolyte are effectively inhibited.35 Self-discharge measurements 
are taken on the Li–S batteries with the two electrolytes after the 11th 
discharge. Fig. 3f displays that the self-discharge rate of the Li–S battery 
with the reference electrolyte is 43.4%, about 7 times that with the 60% 
THE electrolyte (6.7%). The prominently inhibited self-discharge is 
attributed to the extremely low solubility of polysulfides in the 60%THE

3. Conclusions 
In summary, by directly introducing fluoroalkyl ether 2,2,2-trifluor­
oethyl-1,1,2,3,3,3-hexafluoropropyl ether (THE) into commercial ether 
electrolytes, high-rate cyclic performance of Li–S cell is significantly 
improved. By virtue of abundant high-polarity CF3 and CF2 groups, the 
THE electrolyte fully wets the sulfur cathode and inhibits the polysulfide 
shuttling, owing to the intense repulsive interaction (1.97 eV) with 
polysulfide ions and the robust absorption interaction (−2.42 eV) with 
sulfur cathode. Using the THE electrolyte, the Li–S battery cycling 500 
times at 5 C (10.5 mA cm−2) delivers a capacity retention of 53.3%. In 
addition, the self-discharge rate with THE electrolyte is reduced to ~1/7 
of that with the commercial electrolyte.
