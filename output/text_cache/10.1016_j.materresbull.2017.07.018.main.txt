A B S T R A C T
Herein, prelithiated graphite anodes are coupled with sulfur composite cathodes to form a Li–S full cell in
superconcentrated ether electrolyte. This novel system is effective in avoiding sever corrosion of Li-metal
anode and signiﬁcantly improving the safety of present Li–S cell, owing to the replacement of Li-metal
anode with graphite. The introduced superconcentrated ether electrolyte has a unique networking
structure of Li cations and TFSI anions with Li+-solvating DME/DOL solvents, which is conducive to form a
stable TFSI-derived surface ﬁlm on the graphite electrode. The stable and dense TFSI-derived ﬁlm inhibits
further electrolyte decomposition and graphite exfoliation caused by co-intercalation of solvents,
allowing reversible lithium intercalation and de-intercalation at a graphite electrode. Besides, the severe
polysulﬁdes shuttle is also suppressed by the concentrated ether-based solutions. Consequently, the
assembled graphite-sulfur full battery retains outstanding capacity of 686 mAh g1
sulfur at 0.1C rate after
105 cycles, along with high Coulombic efﬁciency.

1. Introduction
The rechargeable lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are regarded as
one of the most promising candidates for next-generation energy
storage devices due to their high theoretical speciﬁc capacity
(1675 mAh g1) and energy density (2600 Wh kg1) [1–6]. Howev-
er, the application of metallic lithium anode in Li-S battery suffers
from inevitable formation of lithium dendrites, which are caused
by uneven current distributions at the metal-electrolyte interface
during electrochemical process. The formation of lithium den-
drites will lead to poor cyclic performance and serious safety
issues. 
Therefore, 
constructing 
a 
rechargeable 
battery 
with
lithium-containing electrode materials instead of lithium metal,
such as prelithiated tin or amorphous silicon anodes, has been
investigated [7–11]. Nonetheless, the huge volume expansion
associated with the use of Li-alloying materials could result in
electrode disintegration and continuous destruction of the solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) ﬁlm. These shortcomings hamper their
practical utility.
Graphite is an attractive material for the negative electrode of
the Li-S battery, owing to its longer cycle life and a decent low
charge potential (0.01–0.25 V vs. Li/Li+) [12–17]. Meanwhile, after
taking the fabricating cost into account, graphite shows great
superiority in comparison with nanostructured Si/Sn-based carbon
composites. However, the applying of graphite as the negative
electrode of Li-S full battery is seldom reported. One major factor
prevents the widespread use of graphite in Li-S battery is that the
electrochemical Li+ intercalation and de-intercalation of graphite
electrodes are highly electrolyte-selective, and cannot take place in
the ether electrolytes (such as mixtures of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and
1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)) most commonly utilized for Li-S
cells. This is due to fact that the intercalation of solvent molecules
together with lithium salt between the graphene planes could
result in graphite exfoliation and the failure of the graphite
electrode [1,18]. Zheng et al. reported the use of graphite as the
negative electrode in Li-S full batteries, while the adopted
electrolyte is based on carbonate solvents [19]. It is well-
established 
that 
carbonate 
solvents 
are 
unsuitable 
for 
the
lithium-sulfur system because of the poor sulfur utilization and
its irreversible reactions with polysulﬁdes [20]. On the other hand,
Li2S-graphite full Battery enabled by solvate ionic liquid electrolyte
is reported by Li et al., and this lithium-ion sulfur full batteries
show a stable and reversible charge-discharge behavior, along with
a very high coulombic efﬁciency [21]. Up to now, the use of ether-
based electrolytes in graphite-sulfur full cell is only reported by
Jeschull et al. [22], who applied a protective electrode binder
(polyacrylic acid sodium salt) to prevent the co-intercalation of
solvent from destroying the crystalline structure of graphite. And

2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials preparation
Mesoporous carbon (MesoC) was synthesized according to a
procedure described in previous paper [6]. The MesoC/sulfur
composite was prepared via a simple melt-diffusion method.
Elemental 
sulfur 
(Sigma 
Aldrich, 
99.5%) 
was 
mixed 
with
mesoporous carbon with a mass ratio of 6:4. Then the mixture
was heated at 155 C for 24 h in a sealed vessel with argon gas. To
prepare S/MesoC electrodes, a mixture of S/MesoC composite,
acetylene black, and polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) at a weight
ratio of 80:10:10 was pasted onto an Al foil. The sulfur loading of all
the sulfur cathodes used here were maintained at 0.9–1.1 mg cm2.
To prepare a graphite electrode, a mesocarbon microbeads
(MCMB) slurry (80 wt% MCMB, 10 wt% carbon black, and 10 wt%
polyvinylidene ﬂuoride) was coated onto the Cu foil, followed by
drying overnight at 120 C under vacuum. The active material
loading 
was 
controlled 
at 
about 
2 mg cm2. 
The 
different
concentration of electrolyte (0.65–6 M) was prepared by dissolving
a given amount of lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI; 99.95%; Sigma Aldrich) in a mixture (1:1 by volume) of 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME; Sigma Aldrich) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL;
Sigma Aldrich) with mild stirring in an argon-ﬁlled glove box.
2.2. Structural characterization
The Raman spectra were recorded in the range of 400–
4000 cm1 by Lab RAMARAMIS with a Notch ﬁlter using an
excitation wavelength of 532 nm (Ar-laser). The morphology and
composition of the electrodes were examined by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, JSM-5900LV) and energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS, X-MAX SDD), respectively. The surface state
of graphite anode was analyzed by XSAM-800 X-ray photoelectron
spectrometer (XPS) with Al Ka (1486.6 eV).
2.3. Electrochemical measurements
For graphite electrodes or S/MesoC electrodes, CR2025 coin
cells were assembled using lithium foil as counter electrode and
Celgard 2325 as separator. The cyclic voltammetry of Li-graphite
half cells were performed in the voltage window of 0.0–2.0 V by
Parastat 2273 scanned at a rate of 0.2 mV s1. The galvanostatic
charge-discharge tests were performed using a Land CT2001A
battery-testing instrument within the potential window of 0.0–
2.0 V for the Li-graphite half cells, and 1.0–3.0 V for Li–S/MesoC half
cells.
To make lithiated graphite anode, graphite was cycled for 3
times in Li-graphite half cells with 5 M electrolytes at current
density of C/5 (1C = 372 mA g1). After being prelithiated, graphite
electrodes 
were 
disassembled, 
washed 
carefully 
with 
DME
solution and dried in Ar-protected environment. Then, the lithiated
graphite electrodes (0.93 mAh cm2) were assembled into full cells
with S/MesoC cathode (0.84 mAh cm2) in 5 M LiTFSI-DME/DOL
electrolyte. The galvanostatic charge-discharge test for lithiated
graphite-S/MesoC full cells was carried out in the voltage window
of 1.0-3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) at current density of 0.1C (1C = 1675 mA g1).
The cyclic voltammetry of full cells were performed in the voltage
window of 1.0–3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) by Parastat 2273 scanned at a rate of
0.2 mV s1. AC impedance measurements were also performed by
applying a DC voltage of 10 mV over the frequency range of 100 kHz
to 10 mHz.
3. Results and discussion
The electrochemical lithium intercalation/de-intercalation re-
action at graphite electrode was studied with graphite/Li metal
half cell. Fig. 1 shows the galvanostatic charge-discharge curves of
the graphite/Li half cell with (a) 0.65 M, (b) 3 M, (c) 4 M, (d) 5 M and
(e) 6 M LiTFSI-DMEL/DOL electrolytes. According to Fig. 1a, two
discharge plateaus at about 0.3 V and 0.5 V are detected in the ﬁrst-
cycle discharge curve, which can be ascribed to electrolyte
decomposition followed by the formation of a SEI ﬁlm, and
solvated lithium intercalation between the graphene layers
[12,13,15]. Simultaneously, the unexpected large irreversible
capacity is found in Fig. 1a. These results are in agreement with
the result of CV analysis (Fig. 2a), where the corresponding two
reduction 
peaks 
are 
detected 
above 
0.2 V. 
Apparently, 
the
reversibility of a graphite electrode is poor in dilute ether
electrolyte (Figs. 1 and 2a). This is due to the fact that the
decomposition products of electrolyte fail to form a stable SEI ﬁlm
that can suppress continuous decomposition of electrolytes and
co-intercalation of solvents, which ultimately leads to the graphite
exfoliation and failure of graphite electrode. With Li-salt concen-
tration increasing, the plateaus above 0.2 V dramatically reduces or
disappears and the irreversible capacity gradually decreases. In
particular, for 5 M and 6 M superhigh concentration electrolytes,
only a long plateau within the region of 0.2 V–0.02 V can be
observed in the ﬁrst-cycle discharge curve, which is associated
with the formation of several stage structures of the lithium-
graphite intercalation compound. Meanwhile, a highly reversible
capacity about 360 mAh g1 is detected, which is close to the
theoretical capacity of graphite (372 mAh g1). Besides, in highly
concentrated Li-Salt electrolyte (Fig. 1d–e), the second/third
discharge curves remain almost unchanged in comparison to
the ﬁrst-cycle discharge curve. These imply that stable SEI was
mainly formed during the ﬁrst discharge process, suppressing the
further electrolyte decomposition and co-intercalation of solvents
[25].
On the other hand, the corresponding cyclic voltammetry
curves of graphite/Li metal half cells with different Li salt
concentrations are exhibited in Fig. 2. As seen from Fig. 2d and
e, only sharp redox couples (around 0 V) associated with the
intercalation and de-intercalation of lithium-ion are detected,
conforming to the results of Fig. 1d and e. Compared to the cell
using conventional dilute ether-based solutions, the cell with
superconcentrated ether solutions (Fig. 2d and e) exhibits sharp
redox peaks, relatively high cathodic peaks, excellent overlap of

graphite electrode rather than sustainable destruction caused by
the graphite exfoliation. Meanwhile, the formed SEI ﬁlm further
refrains the co-intercalation of free solvents and electrolyte
decomposition. Eventually, graphite electrode can steadily exist
in superconcentrated ether-based solutions. While in dilute ether
solutions, the large amount of free TFSI/DME/DOL solvent
molecules can incorporate graphite electrode easily, leading to
the continuous electrolyte decomposition and graphite exfoliation
without formation of a stable SEI ﬁlm.

A Li–S full battery has been constructed by employing
electrochemically prelithiated graphite as an anode (0.93 mAh
cm2), S/MesoC composites as a cathode (0.84 mAh cm2, Fig. S2),
and 5 M LiTFSI in DME/DOL solvents as an electrolyte. Fig. 7a shows
the charge-discharge curves of the Li-S full batteries at 0.1C
(1C = 1675 mA g1). The assembled Li–S full cell delivers an initial
capacity of 1031 mAh g1
Sulfur (608 mAh g1
cathode), and maintains
a high capacity of 686 mAh g1
Sulfur (405 mAh g1
cathode) at 105th
discharge. The capacity decay rate is as low as 0.33%. Meanwhile, it
can be seen in Fig. 7b that the coulombic efﬁciencies of Li-S cell
always remain nearing 100%. These results suggest that the
obtained graphite-sulfur full cells possess excellent cycle stability
(Fig. 7a and b), which is most likely a result of the considerably
stable graphite electrode during charge-discharge process. This
has been conﬁrmed by the SEM images of the graphite electrode
after different cycles. As shown in Fig. 8, graphite electrode is
covered with a dense surface ﬁlm and no fresh graphite surface can
be detected even after 50 cycling, owing to the stable SEI ﬁlm
refrains the intercalation of solvent molecules and graphite
exfoliation. The homogeneous distribution of C, O, F and S
elements (Fig. 8) on the surface of the graphite electrode indicates
that the formed SEI ﬁlm has the characteristic of good uniformity.
Besides, two typical discharge voltage plateaus of Li–S cell are also
detected in Fig. 7a, which corresponds well with the difference of
voltage plateaus between Li–S (two voltage plateaus at 2.2 and
2.0 V, Fig. S3) and Li graphite (voltage plateau at approximately
0.1 V, Fig. 1d) half cells. Cyclic voltammetry curves of Li-S full cells
at different cycles are shown in Fig. 7c. The peak at 2.1 V is related
to the reduction of S8 to long chain lithium polysulﬁde (Li2Sx,

straight line in the low-frequency region is attributed to the
diffusion of the Li ions into the particles of sulfur-based composites
or the so-called Warburg diffusion [41]. The Rct resistance of
graphite-sulfur battery is about 31 V, demonstrating better charge
transport and electrolyte inﬁltration. Besides, little change is
detected on Rct impedance between original cells and the ones
after 50 cycles, once again ensuring the good electrochemical
stability of graphite-sulfur batteries.
4. Conclusions
The introduced superconcentrated ether (LiTFSI-DME/DOL)
electrolyte has a unique networking structure of Li+ cations and
TFSI[] with Li+-solvating DME/DOL solvents, which is totally
different from the conventional dilute ether-based solutions. This
peculiar structure is beneﬁcial to the formation of a stable TFSI-
derived surface ﬁlm at the graphite electrode instead of sustain-
able destruction caused by the graphite exfoliation. In the
circumstances, the stable SEI ﬁlm suppresses continuous electro-
lyte decomposition and co-intercalation of solvents, permitting
reversible lithium intercalation and de-intercalation at a graphite
electrode. Furthermore, the severe polysulﬁdes shuttle is also
suppressed by the concentrated Li salts. Thus, the assembled
graphite-sulfur full cell delivers a relatively high speciﬁc capacity
of 1031 mAh g1
sulfur at 0.1C (1C = 1675 mA g1), and a reversible
capacity of 686 mAh g1
sulfur can still be preserved after 105 cycles.
The corresponding coulombic efﬁciency always remains nearing
100%. Additional beneﬁts of our cell are the expected high safety
due to the replacement of the Li-metal anode with graphite, and
the low cost of the graphite materials, thus suggesting it as a
promising energy storage system.
