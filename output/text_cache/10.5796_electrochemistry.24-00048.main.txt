Enabling Long Cycle Life and High Speciﬁc Capacity

ABSTRACT
Highly concentrated electrolytes have been studied to prevent the leaching diﬀusion of lithium (Li) polysulﬁdes from sulfur (S) cathodes in
Li–S batteries. Additionally, high-concentration electrolytes suppress the growth of Li dendrites at Li anodes. In this study, an ether-based
high-concentration electrolyte was developed, enabling a microporous activated carbon–S composite cathode to achieve near-theoretical
capacity performance and a long cycle life. The reference electrolyte was a high-concentration solution of Li bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)
in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), known for its suitability for the stable dissolution and deposition of Li. However, its high viscosity impeded
full penetration into the activated carbon–S composite cathode. To enhance the reversibility of the activated carbon–S composite cathode,
we optimized the LiFSI-based electrolyte by adding hydroﬂuoroether (HFE) to reduce the viscosity and adjusting the LiFSI concentration to
prevent the dissolution of Li polysulﬁdes. Furthermore, the addition of Li diﬂuoro(oxalate)borate (LiDFOB) to this electrolyte stabilized the
cycling performance for over 100 cycles. When applied to a 1-Ah-class pouch cell, the electrolyte achieved an energy density of greater
than 300 Wh kg−1.

1. Introduction
Since the commercialization of lithium-ion batteries (LiBs) in
1991, various materials have been used as positive1–5 and negative6,7
electrode materials to enhance the energy density, longevity, and
safety of LiBs. With the rapid proliferation and improved perform-
ance of electric vehicles, laptop computers, and smartphones, there
is an increasing demand for next-generation rechargeable batteries
that surpass the energy density per unit weight of current LiBs. High
energy density materials are essential for these next-generation
batteries. Consequently, sulfur (S), a light and abundant element, is
being explored as a cathode active material for next-generation
secondary
batteries.8–10
S
has
a
theoretical
capacity
of
1672 mAh g¹1, signiﬁcantly higher than the actual capacity of
conventional LiB cathode materials, because of its redox reactions
involving a two-electron transfer per S atom (S8 + 16Li+ +
16e¹ ¼ 8Li2S).
Although
the
average
discharge
potential
is
approximately 2 V (vs. Li+/Li), roughly half that of LiBs, the
energy density is anticipated to exceed that of current LiBs greatly.
Li metal, which has a speciﬁc capacity of 3860 mAh g¹1, over ten-
times the theoretical capacity of graphite-based batteries (LiC6;
372 mAh g¹1), has been extensively studied as an anode materi-
al.11–14 However, the use of Li metal for the negative electrode is
known to cause short cycle lifetimes and safety issues because of
deposition (i.e., dendrite formation), which can rupture the separator
and
cause
short-circuits
between
the
positive
and
negative
electrodes. In recent years, measures to address Li dendrite growth
have included the use of all-solid-state electrolytes, Li alloying with
Sn,15 addition of Li nitrate,16 formation of artiﬁcial solid electrolyte
interphases (SEIs), and use of high-concentration electrolytes;17,18
however, these approaches have not been eﬀectively integrated with
S cathodes. In the few instances where such integration has
occurred, the performance of Li–S batteries has been insuﬃcient for
practical applications.
The primary issue with S as an active material is the “redox
shuttle” eﬀect, caused by the dissolution of charge–discharge
intermediates
(Li
polysulﬁdes)
in
the
electrolyte.
We
have
previously addressed this problem by using activated carbon as a
host material for S, conﬁning it within micropores smaller than
2 nm.19 Consequently, S is conﬁned by the capillary force of the
activated carbon micropores, and solid-phase reactions, similar to
those in all-solid-state S batteries, occur within the composite,
suppressing the dissolution and diﬀusion of S. Although activated
carbon consisting solely of micropores exhibits stable charge–
discharge behavior, it is suboptimal for enhancing the energy density
because the S content in conventional microporous activated carbon
is limited to 30 wt%.19 Therefore, we have developed an advanced
microporous activated carbon that also contains mesopores of 2 nm
or larger, capable of accommodating up to 70 wt% S.20,21 Because
mesopores can potentially release Li polysulﬁdes into the electro-
lyte, our goal was to achieve extended cycle lifetimes and stability
by combining advanced activated carbon with a high-concentration
electrolyte (HCE), such as a solvated ionic liquid, which has very
low solubility for Li polysulﬁdes.22
A localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) is an electro-
lyte in which an HCE, such as a solvated ionic liquid, is diluted with
a hydroﬂuoroether, speciﬁcally 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-
tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (HFE), to decrease the viscosity while
preserving the original solvation state. For instance, Watanabe et al.
reported the use of a tetraglyme (G4) electrolyte (Li bis(triﬂuoro-
methanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) : G4 : HFE = 10 : 8 : 40 molar ra-
tio)22,23 and a sulfolane (SL) electrolyte (LiTFSI : SL : HFE =

have applied LHCEs to microporous activated carbon–S composites
and found that the electrolyte composition must be optimized for
such composites because of the diﬃculty in accessing S within the

In this study, we exploited the exceptional Li reversible
performance of a 4 mol dm¹3 Li bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)-
based 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) electrolyte as a suitable HCE
for a Li metal anode and adjusted it as an LHCE with HFE to
a microporous activated carbon–S composite. To achieve high
reversibility for both Li and S, we focused on Li diﬂuoro(oxalate)
borate (LiDFOB) as an additive, which has been reported to perform
well in Li–S batteries.30–32 The resulting electrolyte achieved near-
theoretical capacity and extended the cycle life by maintaining the
high reversibility of Li metal and suppressing the dissolution of Li
polysulﬁdes.
2. Experimental
2.1 Preparation of the microporous activated carbon–S
composite
Alkali-activated carbon derived by the carbonization of resin
(AR: prototype, Kansai Coke and Chemicals Co., Ltd.) was used as
the microporous carbon matrix to conﬁne S in the S cathode. The
AR had a speciﬁc surface area of 2860 m2 g¹1 and a pore volume of
1.20 cm3 g¹1. The maximum pore size was approximately 2.5 nm,
centered around 0.8 nm. To eliminate water adsorbed in the pores,
the AR was vacuum-dried at 100 °C for over 12 h. The dried AR was
then uniformly mixed with S (Fujiﬁlm Wako Pure Chemical Co.,
>99 %) in an agate mortar at a weight ratio of 65 : 35 (S/AR) under
dry conditions. The mixture was sealed in a stainless-steel
cylindrical vessel and heated in a muﬄe furnace to 155 °C at a
rate of 10 °C min¹1 for 5 h in air. The temperature was subsequently
increased to 300 °C at the same rate and maintained for 2 h to
remove excess S from the pores, resulting in the microporous
activated carbon–S composite (denoted AR-S). The conﬁned S
content in AR-S was determined by thermogravimetric analysis
(TGA, DTG-60H, Shimadzu Corp.) at a heating rate of 5 °C min¹1
under Ar ﬂow, revealing 63 wt% of conﬁned S based on the
observed weight loss (Fig. S1).
2.2 AR-S cathode preparation
To fabricate the S cathode from AR-S, an aqueous slurry was
prepared by blending AR-S, conductive activated carbon (AR
without S-loading), carbon nanotubes (a prototype, TPR Co., Ltd.),
and a polypeptide binder in a weight ratio of 75 : 19 : 1 : 5 with pure
water. This ratio resulted in a S content of 47.3 wt% in total solid
content of the cathode. The slurry was ﬁlled into porous aluminum
(CELMETμ, Sumitomo Electric Industries, Ltd.), pre-pressed to
approximately 0.5-mm thickness from the original 1 mm, and dried
at 40 °C for a minimum of 3 h. To remove residual water, the
material was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for at least 12 h. The
S loading in the electrode was about 5.0 mg cm¹2 for coin-type
cell electrodes, having a total S-load of 16.0–16.6 mg cm¹2 for
laminated- and pouch-type cell electrodes, equivalent to 8.0–
8.3 mg cm¹2 on a single-sided basis. This single-sided equivalent
is noted because each anode was situated on both sides of a positive
electrode.
2.3 Electrolyte preparation and Li–S cell construction
DME (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.) was combined with HFE
(Daikin Industries, Ltd.) in a volume ratio of 1 : 1 (2.00 : 1.26 by
mole) to prepare 1 dm3 of a binary solvent system. This solution was
then mixed with 2, 3, or 4 mol of Li bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI,
Nippon Shokubai Co., Ltd.) and stirred for several days. This
electrolyte preparation method is a precise technique for obtaining
an electrolyte from a small amount of solvent and electrolyte
salt. The electrolytes are denoted x mol of LiFSI + DME : HFE.
LiDFOB (Sigma–Aldrich) was used as an electrolyte additive when
necessary. For comparison, 1 M LiTFSI (Solvay) in a binary solvent
of ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC, Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.) and
HFE was prepared, in which the solvents were adjusted to a 1 : 1
ratio by volume (denoted 1 M LiTFSI/FEC : HFE). All reagents
were handled in a glove box under an Ar atmosphere without
additional processing.
The dismountable two-electrode cell simulating a coin-type cell
was composed of an AR-S electrode having an interior diameter (?)
of 12 mm as the positive electrode, Li-metal foil (Honjo Metal Co.,
Ltd.) punched at ?13 mm as the negative electrode, and a polyoleﬁn
microporous membrane as the separator. The Li-metal symmetric
cell for checking Li reversibility was constructed with a pair of Li-
metal electrodes of the same size punched at ?12 mm and the
separator. The single-layer laminated and triple-layer pouch-type
cells contained AR-S electrodes cut to 23 © 25 mm (for the 0.15-
Ah-class cell) and 50 © 30 mm (for the 1-Ah-class cell). In addition
to the electrode size, the components were the same as those used in
the coin-type dismountable cell. All cell construction was performed
in a glove box ﬁlled with Ar at a dew point below ¹80 °C.
2.4 Electrochemical tests and property analyses
The Li–S cells were aged in a thermostatic chamber at 25 °C
for 1 h to soak up the electrolyte fully. Charge–discharge tests
(ABE1024-5V, Electroﬁeld) were conducted at constant-current
densities of 167.2, 501.6, and 836.0 mA g¹1 (0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 C-
rate) per weight of S from 1.0 to 3.4 V. The Li symmetric cell used
to check the reversibility of Li deposition and dissolution was
operated at a constant current charging/discharging at 0.1 C-rate
(0.84 mA cm¹2), estimated from the S loading of 5 mg cm¹2 in the
coin-type
cell.
Impedance
data
(VMP-300,
Bio-Logic)
were
obtained from 1 mHz to 1 MHz with an alternating current (AC)
amplitude of 10 mVp-0 at the open-circuit voltage. All electrochem-
ical measurements were performed at 25 °C.
The viscosity of the electrolytes was measured using a micro-
diﬀerential pressure viscometer (microVISC, Viscotech Co., Ltd.),
and the ionic conductivity was measured using a conductivity meter
(Seven2Go, Mettler Toledo) in a dry room at 25 °C. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed
using a JPS-9010MC (JEOL), in which the target cell was
dismantled in an Ar-atmosphere glove box, and the electrode was
washed with a DME/HFE mixture and thoroughly dried. The
treated electrodes were placed in a transfer vessel to avoid exposure
to air. Monochromatized Al KA X-rays were used for X-ray
diﬀractometry measurements. Ar-ion sputtering was used to measure
the depth proﬁle of elemental composition.
3. Results and Discussion
The ionic conductivities and viscosities of the prepared electro-
lytes are listed in Table 1. The x mol LiFSI + DME : HFE (here-
after, denoted x mol LiFSI) electrolytes showed a trend of increasing
viscosity and decreasing ionic conductivity as the amount of LiFSI
increased. Further, their viscosities were signiﬁcantly lower than that
of 4 M LiFSI/DME (62.4 mPa s) because of the presence of HFE.29
In addition, the ionic conductivity was higher than that of the 1 M
LiTFSI/FEC : HFE electrolyte at all salt concentrations, possibly
because of the low density and viscosity of DME and the generally
higher ionic conductivity of LiFSI compared to LiTFSI.33 Despite
this, 1 M LiTFSI/FEC : HFE is an excellent electrolyte that is

The initial discharge capacity exceeded the theoretical capacity

consumption of the electrolyte because of the formation of a ﬁlm
on the cathode (i.e., the CEI) during the initial discharge is a
contributing factor.34 Additionally, the irreversible storage of Li in
the cathode carbon material accounts for the irreversible capacity.34
The mechanism determining this capacity is currently unknown and
will be determined in future studies.
Next, we introduced LiDFOB as an additive in the LiFSI/
DME : HFE electrolyte system, which has been reported to form
SEI ﬁlms.30–32 The 4 mol LiFSI electrolyte exhibited the lowest
capacity decay and high discharge capacity, but it was challenging
to dissolve the additive because of the large amount of Li salt.
Nevertheless, the 3 mol LiFSI electrolyte could dissolve 0.1 mol
of LiDFOB per 1 dm3 of DME : HFE mixed solvent, although
dissolving a large amount of the additive was diﬃcult. The additive
with 0.1 mol LiDFOB added to the 3 mol LiFSI electrolyte is
referred to as “LiDFOB Added.”
Figure 2a displays the discharge capacity and eﬃciency over 100
cycles, and Fig. 2b shows the charge–discharge curves at galvano-
static charge–discharge at 0.1 C (equivalent to 0.84 mA cm¹2) when
using LiDFOB Added. The charge–discharge curves are very similar
in shape to those for the 3 mol LiFSI electrolytes, both with and
without LiDFOB. Remarkably, the addition of LiDFOB enabled
charging and discharging for 100 cycles without causing rapid
capacity decay. This resulted in a cycle life more than double that of
4 mol LiFSI (interrupted by the internal short circuit as indicated by
a cross in Fig. 2a), yielding a discharge capacity of 1413 mAh g¹1 at
the 100th cycle, thus maintaining about 85 % of the theoretical
capacity of S. The rate characteristics of the cells using this
electrolyte, including the 1 C rate, are shown in Fig. S2 for
comparison with cells using a 1 M LiTFSI/FEC : HFE electrolyte.
This comparison indicates that the rate characteristics of the
LiDFOB Added electrolyte are much better than those of the
FEC : HFE system.
Further, impedance measurements were conducted to verify the
diﬀerences in the electrode interface responses with and without
LiDFOB. Figure 3 presents the results of impedance measurements
after charging at the 10th cycle. The bulk resistances of the
electrolytes remained unchanged with and without LiDFOB;
however, there was a notable diﬀerence in the size of the semicircles
in the high-frequency region. In particular, LiDFOB Added
exhibited a peak frequency of 9.2 kHz, whereas 3 mol LiFSI had a
peak frequency of 20 kHz, suggesting that the components of the
CEI or SEI were diﬀerent.
To demonstrate the eﬀect of LiDFOB addition, optical photo-
graphs of the Li metal anodes after 10 cycles of the Li–S cells using
3 mol of LiFSI electrolyte and LiDFOB Added are shown in Fig. 4a,
and their SEM images are displayed in Fig. 4b. As shown in Fig. 4a,
the anodes having 3 mol of LiFSI had an overall dark and rough
surface, whereas those with added LiDFOB retained a metallic luster
and relatively uniform deposition morphology. The SEM images
indicate that 3 mol of LiFSI led to a non-uniform deposition
morphology without clear grain boundaries. In contrast, at 5000©
magniﬁcation, a pumice-like porous structure is clearly visible. On
the other hand, the structure of the anode with LiDFOB Added
consisted of precipitates with well-deﬁned grain boundaries, and
grains on the order of 10 µm are distinctly visible at ©5000
magniﬁcation. This suggests that the Li deposition patterns were
markedly diﬀerent.
Next, XPS measurements were performed to investigate the
chemical diﬀerences between these deposits, and the results are
depicted in Fig. 5. For the 3 mol LiFSI-derived Li anode, the
topmost (solid) F 1s spectrum contains two peaks: one from Li-F
bonding,35 and the other could include C-F3 and S-F bonds such as
F-SOx,36,37 which may arise from the decomposition products of
LiFSI. The Li anode from the LiDFOB Added system produced two
peaks at the same energies as the system without LiDFOB, but the
intensity of the peak derived from the various bonds, possibly
including B-F,36,37 was higher than that of the Li-F peak. This
implies that the intense peak includes the decomposition product
from LiDFOB. In other words, LiDFOB-derived components may
form in the SEI layer on the topmost surface of the Li metal anode.
The internal information obtained by Ar-ion sputtering for 10 s was
dominated by a Li-F-derived peak, both with and without added
LiDFOB. Further, the Li-F distributed layer on the LiDFOB Added
anode was thicker than that of the anode in 3 mol LiFSI.

that at the coin-cell scale, the performance at 0.3 C was stable for 25
cycles, although the observed capacity at 0.3 C was lower than that
at 0.1 C: 1290 mAh g¹1 at the 25th cycle. Thus, this electrolyte is
promising for Li–S battery applications because it yields a high
capacity and stable cyclability, even in a laminated cell with a very
thick cathode.
Finally, to evaluate the Li–S battery having dimensions closer to
those used practically, a large 1-Ah-class cell was constructed by
stacking three 50 © 30 mm S cathodes (single-sided equivalent S
loading: 8.3 mg cm¹2). Figure 10 shows the results of constant 0.1 C
charge–discharge measurements with a ratio of electrolyte volume to

density. Because the ﬁrst cycle had a contribution from irreversible
capacity arising from CEI formation, the discharge capacity of the
2nd cycle is discussed; this was 1637 mAh g¹1, almost the same
discharge capacity as the theoretical capacity. The gravimetric

4. Conclusions
We have reported a promising electrolyte for practical Li–S
batteries that simultaneously demonstrates compatibility with high-
S-loading cathodes, a long cycle life, an operating capacity close to
the theoretical capacity, and stability with a Li metal anode. Previous
studies have shown that LiFSI electrolytes with high concentrations
exhibit long-term reversibility of the Li metal anode. However,
optimizing the S cathode material is essential for their application
in actual Li–S batteries. In this study, by selecting LiDFOB as
an additive, we have demonstrated stable behavior without rapid
capacity decay for 100 cycles at 0.1 C and 0.3 C with a 5 mg cm¹2 S-
loaded cathode in a cell-type cell. Furthermore, laminated cells
having a S loading of 8 © 2 : 16 mg cm¹2, a high loading, exhibited
excellent rate capability and cyclability, similar to those of coin-type
cells. Additionally, the triple-layer pouch cell utilizing this electrode
technology demonstrated a notable energy density of 314 Wh kg¹1
at an electrolyte volume to S weight ratio of 3.5. Although there are
disadvantages in terms of high viscosity and density compared
to conventional electrolytes, the signiﬁcant advantages in terms
of near-theoretical capacity and long cycle life are noteworthy.
Nevertheless, improvements are needed, such as short-circuit
countermeasures at low electrolyte volumes; however, this work
represents major progress toward the development of an ideal
electrolyte for Li–S batteries.
