A B S T R A C T
In lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, the charge density variation of Sn
2- results in fluctuating ionic bonds in lithium 
polysulfides (Li2Sn), and its mismatch with conventional electrolytes’ fixed solvating force lead to challenges in 
optimizing polysulfide shuttling and sluggish reaction kinetics simultaneously. Therefore, designing electrolytes 
capable of adaptively adjusting solvating forces in response to changes in the electrostatic interactions of Li2Sn is 
imperative for practical Li-S batteries. Herein, we propose an electrostatic interaction force matching concept 
that utilize high polarizability solvents with dynamical solvating power to accommodate the fluctuation in 
electrostatic interactions of Li2Sn. Molecules with conjugated structure exhibit significantly delocalized electron 
cloud, which is capable of responding in real time to the stimulus of local-electric-field variations from Sn
2– to 
enable charge–density–dependent solvation regulation of Li2Sn. Weakly or strongly polarized solvents induced by 
Sn
2– with different charge–density reduce the solubility of long-chain Li2Sn (n > 4) by over 50% and enable a 
magnitude-level increase in the solubility of short-chain Li2Sn (n ≤4), promoting the reaction kinetic and pre­
venting the accumulation of Li2Sn. Consequently, optimized Li–S batteries achieve 406.2 Wh/kg in 3 Ah–level 
pouch cells with E/S ratio of 3 mL/gsulfur. The findings deepen the understanding of polarizable systems and 
provide an innovative electrolyte design principle.
1. Introduction
The escalating energy crisis and environmental challenges have 
sparked unprecedented interest in renewable energy and efficient en­
ergy storage technologies. Among emerging solutions, Li–S batteries 
stand out due to their remarkable theoretical energy density of 2600 
Wh/kg and abundant raw material reserves, positioning them as a 
promising next–generation energy storage system [1–4]. However, their 
practical energy density and cycling stability are severely restricted by 
sluggish reactions kinetics and shuttle effect, especially at lean electro­
lyte conditions [5–7]. It has been demonstrated that regulations of 
electrolyte solvating forces have the capacity of optimizing the solvation 
and reactivity of Li2Sn and addressing above issues [8–10]. Conventional 
electrolytes with strong solvating forces can enhance the dispersion of 
short-chain Li2Sn with strong electrostatic interactions and improve re­
action kinetics, while those with weak solvating forces can confine the 
long-chain Li2Sn, which are prone to diffusion, on the cathode side to 
prevent shuttling [11–14]. Although these conventional electrolytes can 
address part issues in Li-S batteries, their solvating forces fail to adapt to 
the variations in electrostatic interactions of Li2Sn and achieve 
force-matching with all Li2Sn, which may exacerbate other issues: highly 
solvating electrolytes exacerbate the Li2Sn shuttle effect, while sparingly 
solvating electrolytes aggravate the sluggish reaction kinetics. (Fig. 1a) 
[15–18]. It’s difficult for conventional electrolytes to simultaneously 
ensure the cycling stability and the improvement of energy density of 
Li-S batteries [19–21]. Therefore, designing electrolytes that can both 
enhance reaction kinetics and suppress the shuttle effect is imperative 
for the practical application of Li–S batteries and remains substantia 
challenges.
Detailed analysis of the properties of Li2Sn is essential to elucidate 
the fundamental reasons behind the bidirectional regulation re­
quirements and design electrolytes that address the inherent contra­
dictions in Li–S batteries. During the sulfur species conversion reaction 
in Li–S batteries, the variation in charge density (S8

Therefore, the electrolytes exhibiting adaptive solvating capabilities 
toward different Li2Sn, thereby simultaneously reducing the solubility of 
long-chain Li2Sn while enhancing that of short-chain Li2Sn could resolve 
this intrinsic and fundamental contradiction in Li-S batteries. However, 
there are few reports on the electrolytes capable of dynamically 
adjusting their solvating forces in response to the charge density varia­

comprehensively enhance the performance of Li–S batteries.
In this work, we propose an electrostatic interaction force matching 
concept that leverages the Sn

interaction between Li2Sn and high polarizability solvent molecules to 
achieve selective solvation and stable dispersion of various Li2Sn. The 
solvent molecules with high electronic polarizability are capable of 
undergoing electronic rearrangement in response to the local electro­
static field of Sn
2– across different chain lengths, and the degree of po­
larization and dipole charge density depend on the charge density of Sn
2– 
[27]. Considering the electron cloud of the solvent must exhibit strong 
elasticity and compatibility with lithium metal, we employed N, 
N-Dimethyltrifluoromethanesulfonamide (DMTMSA) as model solvent, 
which incorporates a sulfonamide group—highly polarizable moiety 
with conjugated structure and resonant modes that exhibit significantly 
delocalized electron cloud, making it more sensitive to the changes in 
electrostatic field intensity of Sn
2– (Fig. S2). Low charge–density Sn
2– 
induce only minimal polarization of DMTMSA, resulting in weak ion­
–dipole interactions with long–chain Li2Sn and sparingly solvating, 
whereas high charge–density Sn
2– exhibits the opposite situation, and 
strong ion–dipole interactions are established between DMTMSA and 
short–chain Li2Sn, achieving highly solvating [28–30]. Based on this 
mechanism, DMTMSA enables adaptive adjustment of solvating forces 
based on the charge density of Sn
2– within different Li2Sn solvation mi­
croenvironments, reducing the solubility of long-chain Li2Sn by over 
50% and enable a magnitude-level increase in the solubility of 
short-chain Li2Sn (Fig. S3). This not only effectively suppressed the 
shuttle effect but also enhanced the reaction kinetic without posing new 
issues, and the optimized reaction kinetic enable three–dimensional 
deposition of Li2S, preventing rapid coverage of cathode active sites 
(Fig. 1b)[31]. Consequently, practical applicability is demonstrated in 3 
Ah–level Li–S pouch cells with an actual energy density of 406.2 Wh/kg 
with E/S = 3 mL/gs. Moreover, we validate the mechanism's universality 
by applying it to solvents exhibiting similar tautomeric equilibria (e.g., 
amide molecules), offering a transformative approach to electrolyte 
design for Li–S batteries.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. The induced polarization of tautomeric molecules against fluctuating 
solvation
The existence of conjugated structure and tautomeric forms of sul­
fonamide 
molecules 
and 
amide 
molecules 
(e.g., 
N, 
N–Dimethyltrifluoroacetamide) make them more prone to polarization 
and more sensitive to variations in external electrostatic field intensity 
than other molecules, but amide molecules was not selected for use due 
to its poor compatibility with LMA (Fig. S4) [32–34]. Under the stim­
ulation and induction of an external electric field (including the local 
electrostatic field of ions, such as Sn
2–), the unpolarized state DMTMSA 
undergoes electrons rearrangement: the lone pair of electrons on the 
nitrogen atom can shift toward the oxygen atoms, transforming the ni­
trogen atom into a positive charge center and oxygen atoms into nega­
tive charge centers, transitioning into polarized state (Fig. S2). So 
DMTMSA can not only coordinate with Li+ through the oxygen atom 
bearing lone pair electrons, but also exhibit nonnegligible dipole–ion 
interactions between nitrogen atom and Sn
2–, regulating the solvation of 
Li2Sn from two aspects. The degree of polarization and charge density at 
the positive and negative charge centers are determined by the strength 
of the electrostatic field (charge density of Sn
2−). Compared to the un­
polarized state and weakly polarized state, the highly polarized state 
DMTMSA exhibits stronger solvating capability (Fig. 2a). Therefore, the 
solvating capability of DMTMSA varies with changes in the ionic envi­
ronment of the electrolyte.
In a simple electrolyte solution, DMTMSA’s solvating capability for 
Li+ is weaker than DOL and DME. The strong electron–withdrawing 
effect of the –CF3 group in DMTMSA molecule significantly reduces 
charge density at the oxygen atom, making its coordination with Li+
difficult and effectively enhancing its compatibility with LMA (Fig. S5). 
In addition, DMTMSA repels DOL and DME away from Li+ through 
dipole–dipole interactions, which can be observed from the upfield shift

under the induction effect of the Sn

different Li2Sn to DMTMSA polarization vary in intensity of Sn

in long–chain Li2Sn exhibits lower charge density, resulting in a weaker

in the saturated solution (Fig. S8). When solvating Li2S8, although the 
interaction between DMTMSA and Li+ is strengthened by the S8
2–, it re­

interaction between DMTMSA and S8

increased dipole–ion interaction between DMTMSA and Li+ as well as 
S4

Li2S4 (Fig. S9) [38].

DMTMSA and Li2Sn, achieving intelligent solvation regulation of Li2Sn in 
Li–S batteries. The concentrations of various Li2Sn maintain at levels that 
are neither too high to cause shuttle effects nor too low to compromise 
reaction activity. Li2S4 can react with high-oxidation-state sulfur species 
like S8 or Li2S8, reducing them while oxidizing themselves, to regulates 
the sulfur redox kinetics. DMTMSA can enhance the solubility of Li2S4 in 
the electrolyte to facilitates the exertion of the endogenous mediation 
effect. Therefore, the reduced solubility of long–chain Li2Sn effectively 
suppressing their shuttling effect, and the endogenous mediation effect 
of Li2S4 with increased solubility accelerate the reduction of Li2Sn 
making long-chain Li2Sn less prone to accumulation in both electro­
chemical reduction and disproportionation reactions. To clarify the 
suppression of parasitic reactions between Li2Sn and LMA by DMTMSA, 
shuttle currents were first measured in Li–S batteries. In the absence of 
LiNO3, the batteries with DMTMSA exhibited very low shuttle currents 
across all voltage ranges, whereas those without DMTMSA showed sig­
nificant shuttle currents, approaching 0.5 mA (Fig. 3a). And the char­
ge–discharge curves reveal that batteries with DMTMSA additive can 
complete full cycles in LiNO3–free electrolyte, while control batteries 
exhibit pronounced shuttle effects characterized by persistently lower 
voltage plateaus during charging and eventual failure to reach full 
charge (Fig. 3b) [41]. This suppression of the shuttle effect not only 
minimizes active material loss but also fundamentally prevents lithium 
polysulfide corrosion on the lithium anode, thereby ensuring long–term 
cycling stability. The extended first discharge plateau observed in 
DMTMSA batteries indicates enhanced utilization of Li2S8, leading to a 
significant increase in capacity. And from the residual amount of cath­
ode active material and the percentage of lithium metal anode mass loss 
in DMTMSA batteries and control group batteries at different cycle 
counts, it can be observed that after 50 cycles, DMTMSA doubles the 
residual cathode active material and reduces lithium metal anode mass 
loss by approximately 70% ((Fig. S16)) [42]. This further demonstrates 
that DMTMSA effectively enhances the battery's capacity retention and 
cycling stability. And SEM analysis of lithium metal anode after 100 
cycles demonstrates striking contrasts, the DMTMSA–treated batteries 
maintain smooth and uniformly deposited lithium metal anode surfaces, 
less severe corrosion effect caused by Li2Sn shuttling to the negative 
electrode is demonstrated, while control anode shows severe corrosion 
with uneven pitting morphology (Fig. S17). This is also related to the 
protective effect of the stable solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the 
surface of the negative electrode [43,44]. Compared to DOL and DME, 
the DMTMSA and TFSI−
anions exhibit lower LUMO energy levels, 
enabling them to undergo preferential decomposition on the lithium 
metal anode surface, which facilitates the formation of a stable SEI 
(Fig. S18). X–ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed that the 
SEI formed in DMTMSA battery was rich in inorganic components, 
primarily LiF, Li3N, and Li2O, and with a uniform depth distribution, 
providing effective protection for the anode (Fig. S19). In contrast, the 
control batteries' SEI exhibited a lower content of inorganic components 
and a high proportion of unstable organic compounds resulted from the 
decomposition of DOL and DME, failing to prevent electrolyte decom­
position. Therefore, the inorganic-rich SEI formed in the presence of 
DMTMSA does not undergo damage or reconstruction. As observed from 
the top-view and cross-sectional SEM images of the lithium metal, the 
SEI initially nucleates and grows at highly active sites during the early 
cycles, with its thickness slightly increasing as cycling progresses, ulti­
mately forming a dense, uniform, and stable structure (Fig. S20). In 
contrast, the SEI on the surface of the control group's lithium metal, due 
to its low inorganic content, exhibits poor strength and stability, leading 
to repeated damage and regrowth. It evolves from an initially uneven 
and discontinuous SEI into a progressively thicker, loose, and porous 
structure, failing to provide effective protection for the lithium metal 
anode. And the significantly lower sulfur content on anode surfaces in 
DMTMSA batteries compared to controls further evidences the addi­
tive’s exceptional efficacy in suppressing Li2Sn shuttle and protecting the 
anode from Li2Sn corrosion (Fig. S21) [45,46]. Moreover, due to the 
protective effect of a robust SEI, the Li2Sn reduced at the anode side in 
DMTMSA batteries maintain a higher oxidation state, resulting in only 
minor side reactions.
The beneficial regulation of DMTMSA on the solubility and disper­
sion of Li2S4 facilitated the reactivity of Li2S4 and its role as an inherent 
reactivity redox mediator, enabling improved reaction kinetics [47]. 
Using cyclic voltammetry (CV), we measured the enhancement in re­
action kinetics of Li–S batteries (Fig. 3c) [48]. The results reveal that 
DMTMSA batteries exhibit a clear change in redox behaviors, the 
reduction peaks shift to a higher voltage, while the oxidation peak shifts 
to a lower voltage. Notably, the current response also shows a significant 
boost, demonstrating faster and more efficient sulfur conversion ki­
netics. What’s more, the peak currents of the CV curves for both the 
DMTMSA group and the control group are linearly correlated with the 
square root of the scan rate (Fig. S22). According to the Randles–Sevcik 
equation, the slope is proportional to the diffusion coefficient of the 
Li2Sn. It can be seen that the diffusion coefficient of the DMTMSA group 
is improved compared to the control group, especially during the second 
reduction stage, which further demonstrate enhanced solubility and 
dispersion of Li2S4.
In Li2S6 symmetric cell CV tests (50mV/s), it was also observed that 
when carbon paper was used as the electrode and the electrolyte con­
tained the same concentration of Li2S6, the presence of DMTMSA 
resulted in higher reduction and oxidation response currents in the Li2S6 
symmetric cell, which further demonstrates the enhancement of sulfur 
conversion reaction kinetics by DMTMSA (Fig. S23) [49]. In addition, 
the potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT) discharge tests 
of Li-S batteries revealed that the DMTMSA electrolyte shifted the onset 
reduction potential of the battery higher than that of the control group 
(Fig. S24). Moreover, it exhibited significantly higher response currents, 
which aligns with the CV test results. Electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) tests and distribution relaxation time (DRT) analyses 
on Li–S batteries show that, three distinct peaks emerge around the 
characteristic relaxation time (τ) of 10−4, 10−2, and 101 s, corresponding 
to the contact region, the reaction region, and the diffusion region, 
respectively (Figs. 3d and S25). It can be observed that the DMTMSA 
battery exhibits significantly lower, SEI resistance (RSEI), charge transfer 
resistance (Rct), and concentration polarization resistance compared to 
the control group. The increase in ohmic resistance (Rohm) is caused by 
the decrease in ionic conductivity (Fig. S26), but the DMTMSA elec­
trolyte still maintains a relatively high ionic conductivity, which does 
not significantly hinder ion transport, and the overall impedance is 
lower. Although the ionic conductivity of the DMTMSA electrolyte de­
creases, the addition of DMTMSA actually increases the lithium-ion 
transference number, indicating that the weakly coordinated solvation 
structure regulated by DMTMSA facilitates lithium-ion transport in the 
electrolyte. What’s more, due to the optimization of the solvation 
structure by DMTMSA, the desolvation of Li+ becomes more facile, and 
form an inorganic-rich SEI, which also promotes ion transport. And 
variable–temperature tests on DMTMSA batteries and control group 
batteries within the 35~60 ◦C range show that the RSEI and Rct 
decreased as the temperature increased (Fig. S27). Fitted by the 
Arrhenius equation, the activated energy of SEI ion transport decreases 
from 46.24 to 43.60 kJ/mol and the activated energy of charge transfer 
decreases from 44.28 to 40.49 kJ/mol after DMTMSA was added 
(Fig. 3e). This further demonstrates that DMTMSA promotes ion trans­
port by optimizing the solvation structure.
From the above experiments, it can be observed that the mechanism 
by which the DMTMSA electrolyte enhances the reaction kinetics of Li-S 
batteries includes two aspects: promoting ion transport and improving 
the liquid-phase mediation. The DMTMSA electrolyte optimizes the Li+
solvation structure to enhance the effective lithium-ion transport effi­
ciency and form an inorganic-rich SEI, as evidenced by the EIS results 
showing lower internal and interfacial impedance in DMTMSA batteries 
compared to control batteries. This facilitates favorable interfacial re­
actions and enhanced ion transport [50,51]. Additionally, the

To further investigate the enhancing effect of DMTMSA on kinetics, 
we used in situ Raman spectroscopy to probe the cathode reaction 
process of Li−S batteries [52]. At a 0.5 C discharge rate, the control 
battery exhibited detectable Li2Sx signals (398 and 450 cm–1) starting 
from 2.25 V, which gradually intensified before weakening again, yet 
remained observable until the end of discharge, indicating slow and 
incomplete conversion (Fig. 3f). In contrast, the DMTMSA battery 
showed Li2Sx signals at 2.35 V and reach maximum intensity at about 
2.11 V, demonstrating reduced polarization and faster reaction kinetics 
(Fig. 3g). At 2.00 V, the Li2Sx signals were nearly undetectable, con­
firming its complete conversion to Li2S, so the DMTMSA battery dem­
onstrates a higher specific discharge capacity compared to the control 
battery (Fig. S28). And theoretical calculations demonstrate that 
DMTMSA effectively elongates both S–S and Li–S bond lengths in Li2S4, 
the average bond length of S-S bonds increased by 0.021 Å, while the 
average bond length of Li-S bonds increased by 0.107 Å, which facili­
tates bond cleavage and confirms DMTMSA's positive effects in pro­
moting Li2S4 conversion (Fig. 3h)[53]. DMTMSA also influenced the 
Gibbs free energy changes of various sulfur species during the conver­
sion process. The free energy change for the conversion of Li2S6 to Li2S4 
in the DMTMSA electrolyte was smaller, making the reaction more 
favorable. And the reduced free energy of Li2S facilitated its formation, 
leading to a more complete reaction. However, in the DOL/DME elec­
trolyte, the formation of Li2S4 was relatively difficult, and compared to 
Li2S, the excessively low free energy of Li2S2 hindered its conversion to 
Li2S, resulting in Li2S2 rather than Li2S as the final product, which not 
only affected sulfur utilization but also impaired the oxidation reaction 
rate during the charging process (Fig. 3i).

charge and discharge processes (Fig. S29) [54]. In detail, the Li–S bat­
tery was subjected to 15 min charge/discharge cycles to induce polari­
zation, followed by a 2 h rest period to allow the system to return to 
thermodynamic equilibrium. According to the Butler–Volmer equation 
and Ohm's law, the instantaneous drop to zero upon current interruption 
corresponds to ohmic and activation polarization. Ohmic polarization is 
calculated as the product of Rohm and the test current based on Ohm's 
law. By subtracting the ohmic polarization component, the activation 
polarization value can be determined. In contrast, concentration polar­
ization, governed by the Nernst–Planck equation, persists due to the 
time–delayed nature of mass transport and gradually diminishes during 
relaxation. This method enables precise differentiation and quantitative 
analysis of the three types of polarization, which is the primary element 
influencing reaction kinetics.
The results indicate that both the DMTMSA battery and the control 
battery exhibit three distinct stages of concentration polarization during 
discharge, and these stages roughly correspond to the depth of discharge 
(DOD) ranges of 0~0.3, 0.3~0.9, and 0.9~1.0 (Fig. 4a). The discharge 
curves reveal that during the DOD range of 0~0.3, the primary reaction 
is the reduction of Li2S8 to Li2S4, and the concentration polarization in 
this stage is mainly caused by the slow diffusion of Li2S8 from the bulk 
electrolyte to the electrode surface, with negligible differences observed 
between DMTMSA batteries and control batteries (Fig. S30). However, 
near DOD ≈0.3, a sharp increase in concentration polarization occurs, 
and the dominant reaction shifts to the reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S. Here, 
the polarization is primarily governed by the sluggish diffusion of Li2S4. 
Notably, the DMTMSA batteries exhibit significantly lower polarization 
than the control group, demonstrating DMTMSA's role in enhancing the 
solubility and dispersion of Li2S4 and achieving Li2S three–dimensional 
growth. The further intensification of concentration polarization in the 
third stage may be attributed to the depletion of Li2S4.
The activation polarization, which stems from sluggish interfacial 
charge transfer processes, exhibits a stepwise variation pattern similar to 
that of concentration polarization. DMTMSA batteries generally exhibit 
lower activation polarization compared to control batteries, which 
aligns with the data obtained from CV and EIS measurements (Fig. 4b). 
During the DOD range of 0~0.3, the variation in activation polarization 
of both DMTMSA battery and control battery is minimal, as evidenced 
by the nearly horizontal lines in the data plot. Once the DOD exceeds 
0.3, the gap in activation polarization between the DMTMSA battery and 
the control battery widens significantly. The DMTMSA battery shows 
minimal increase in activation polarization, whereas the control battery 
exhibits an accelerating growth rate as DOD increases. It is noteworthy 
that the activation polarization of the control group gradually increased, 
and the nucleation of Li2S covering the cathode surface continued to 
adversely affect the reaction. In contrast, in the presence of DMTMSA, 
the activation polarization exhibited a slow and slight rise followed by a 
decline, demonstrating that DMTMSA optimizes both the Li2S nucle­
ation process and the deposition morphology of Li2S. This trend persists 
until DOD reaches approximately 0.9, at which point both batteries 
experience a sharp rise in activation polarization. This occurs because as 
Li2S4 undergoes reduction, the concentration of the active substance 
gradually decreases, which significantly affects the reactivity of Li2S4 
and the performance of its redox mediation. In addition, a significant 
amount of chemically inert Li2S deposits on the cathode surface, 
severely hindering further reduction of the remaining Li2S4 (Fig. S31). 
This leads to increased activation polarization, adversely affecting re­
action kinetics and even causing premature termination of the discharge 
process, thereby reducing the utilization of active materials. The 
coverage ratio of active sites is directly related to the magnitude of 
activation polarization, to compare the occupation rates of active sites in 
the presence and absence of DMTMSA, we assembled DMTMSA Li||CP 
batteries and control Li||CP batteries, and they exhibit distinct deposi­
tion behaviors in constant–voltage discharge tests. The time–current 
fitting curves revealed that Li2S in the control batteries deposited via 
instantaneous nucleation with mixed 2D and 3D growth, whereas in 
DMTMSA batteries, they formed through progressive nucleation with 3D 
growth (Fig. 4c and 4e). During the initial stages of Li2S deposition in 
the control group batteries, the cathode surface had abundant active 
sites, resulting in low reaction resistance. This allowed Li2S to deposit in 
a more favorable 3D form. As the reaction progressed, the active sites 
became covered by Li2S, increasing the reaction resistance and causing 
Li2S to deposit in a 2D form. In contrast, the presence of DMTMSA 
enhanced the kinetics of the sulfur conversion reaction, enabling Li2S 
deposition to consistently follow the 3DP model [55]. These observa­
tions were verified by SEM analysis, in control batteries the 2D film-like 
and 3D forms of Li2S covers the electrode surface, while it shows a 3D 
growth of Li2S with a more random and dispersed size distribution in 
DMTMSA batteries (Fig. S32). And the Li2S deposition capacity of the 
DMTMSA battery reached 345.3 mAh/g, significantly surpassing the 
207.6 mAh/g observed in the control battery (Fig. 4d and 4f). The 
presence of DMTMSA promotes the complete reaction of Li2S4, signifi­
cantly enhancing the utilization efficiency of active materials. According 
to the above results, in the control battery, Li2S deposit in a 
two–dimensional manner, rapidly covering the active sites. As a result, 
activation polarization rises sharply with increasing DOD. In contrast, 
the DMTMSA battery exhibits three–dimensional deposition of Li2S, 
effectively preventing rapid and extensive coverage of active sites on the 
cathode surface, which allows activation polarization to increase at a 
more gradual rate. However, once the DOD exceeds 0.9, the active sites 
in both batteries become almost entirely covered, leading to a rapid 
surge in activation polarization. Therefore, the three–dimensional 
deposition of Li2S plays a significant role in the complete conversion of 
Li2S4 and the reduction of reaction kinetics.
In addition to the optimization of the reaction kinetics and process, 
the three–dimensional deposition of Li2S is also related to the high sol­
ubility of Li2S in DMTMSA (Fig. S33). The reduction of Li2S4 requires 
electron at the cathode surface and due to the extremely low solubility of 
Li2S in the control battery, the Li2S not only remains deposited at their 
original sites without further migration but also hinder subsequent 
reduction of Li2S4 at these locations, which leads to the formation of a 
two–dimensional deposition pattern. The high solubility of DMTMSA 
towards Li2S enables the timely dissolution of these reaction products. 
Consequently, during subsequent deposition, the dissolved Li2S no 
longer require direct contact with the cathode surface to receive elec­
trons. Due to differences in affinity, these Li2S preferentially deposit on 
the surfaces of pre–existing Li2S rather than on the carbon cathode. This 
initiates a reduction–dissolution–deposition process, ultimately facili­
tating three–dimensional growth. The strong interaction between 
DMTMSA and Li2S also facilitates the oxidation process during charging. 
It is worth noting that the lower dissociation overpotential of Li2S 
relative to its nucleation overpotential is attributed to the intrinsically 
different kinetic barriers of the two phase-transition pathways. Li2S 
nucleation requires new solid-phase formation from soluble poly­
sulfides, whereas Li2S dissociation mainly involves interfacial oxidation 
of pre-existing Li2S deposits. The CV curves reveal that the DMTMSA 
battery exhibits a lower onset oxidation potential compared to the 
control battery, indicating reduced polarization and enhanced reaction 
kinetics (Fig. 3c). Combined analysis of EIS and GITT data also reveals 
that the activation polarization and concentration polarization in 
DMTMSA batteries are significantly lower than those in control batteries 
(Fig. S34). Furthermore, XPS analysis of the post–charge cathode shows 
that a substantial fraction of unoxidized Li2S remains in the control 
battery, whereas almost no Li2S residue is detected on the cathode 
surface of the DMTMSA battery, effectively preventing capacity loss. 
And the theoretical calculations indicate that the average Li-S bond 
length of Li2S in DMTMSA batteries increases by 0.02 Å compared to 
that in DOL/DME batteries, which facilitates the oxidation reaction.

strategy in simultaneously resolving challenges such as the shuttle effect 
and sluggish reaction kinetics in Li–S batteries and developing practical 
high–energy–density Li–S batteries with extended cycle life.
3. Conclusion
This work proposes an electrostatic interaction force matching 
concept that leverages the varying charge densities of Sn
2– in different 
stages. Although DMTMSA behaves as a "weak solvent" in interactions 
with Li+, its nitrogen atoms acquire a positive charge upon induc­
tion–polarization, enabling the formation of ion–dipole interactions 
with Sn
2–, whose strength depends on the charge densities of Sn
2–. By 
employing the high polarizability solvent with conjugated and reso­
nance structures, we successfully establish a specific ion–dipole inter­
action between solvent and Sn
2–, which enables targeted regulation of 
Li2Sn solvation behavior and solubility in a favorable direction: reduce 
the solubility of long-chain Li2Sn by over 50% comparable to weak 
solvents and enable a magnitude-level increase in the solubility of short- 
chain Li2Sn simultaneously, thereby promoting a more uniform solubi­
lity distribution of Li2Sn. Based on this mechanism, the shuttle effect 
caused by Li2S8 dissolution and sluggish liquid–solid reaction kinetics
