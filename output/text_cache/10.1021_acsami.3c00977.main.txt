ABSTRACT: Arising from the extraordinary theoretical energy
density, rechargeable lithium-sulfur (Li−S) batteries have been
reputed as one of the most appealing options for next-generation
high-performance energy storage and conversion devices. Unfortu-
nately, their industrial implementation has been strongly governed
by the formation of Li dendrites caused by the unstable solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) film. Herein, we report a novel
electrolyte by introducing the Mg(NO3)2 additive to suppress the
growth of Li dendrites, further improving the cycling lifetime of
Li−S batteries. On the one hand, Mg2+ can rapidly react with Li
atoms to generate Mg atoms, replacing the Li atoms on the top
surface of Li metal and forming the Mg center simultaneously. On the other hand, NO3
−can be adsorbed in the inner Helmholtz
plane and reduced as an inorganic-rich SEI film for stabilizing the Li metal anode when the electrolyte comes in contact with Li
metal, effectively mitigating the formation of Li dendrites. Combining the experimental results and theoretical calculations, we
confirm that the Mg atom center and the inorganic-rich SEI film are both beneficial for enhancing the electrochemical performance
of Li−S batteries. This work provides a new insight into the electrolyte additive and a possible alternative for the design of high-
performance Li−S batteries beyond the LiNO3 additive.
KEYWORDS: Mg atom center, Mg(NO3)2, inner Helmholtz plane, additive, Li metal anode, solid electrolyte interphase,
lithium-sulfur battery
1. INTRODUCTION
In a contemporary society, the ever-surging energy density
demands from high-end portable electronic products, long-
endurance pure electric vehicles (EVs), to grid-scale energy
storage and conversion systems have posed a great challenge on
the landscape of commercial rechargeable lithium-ion batteries
(RLIBs),1 as their energy density is gradually approaching the
theoretical boundary and not be able to fulfill the requirements
of further development. In order to circumvent the limitation of
transition oxide cathodes and graphite anodes used in RLIBs to
achieve higher energy density, scientists have to explore new
types of secondary batteries. Among existing battery systems,
rechargeable lithium-sulfur (Li−S) batteries have gained
substantial attention and in-depth research interest from the
academia and industrial community during the past few decades
due to their rather enticing theoretical energy density (2600 W h
kg−1), far exceeding that of the current state-of-the-art RLIBs.2−4
Moreover, S possesses advantages such as abundant reserve in
the earth’s crust, low cost, and environmental friendliness.5−7
Logically, Li−S batteries are endowed with unprecedented
expectation in terms of practical applications. If such a high
theoretical energy density can be fully realized in the foreseeable
future, Li−S batteries will undoubtedly accelerate the deploy-
ment of long-distance EVs and smart grids, enabling sustainable
development and global carbon neutralization. However, the
incessant growth of Li dendrites and the infamous shuttle effect
of polysulfide species (PSs) during the cycling process have long
been considered as the Achilles’ heel of massive-scale practical
implementation of Li−S batteries.8
Over the past several decades, considerable efforts have been
devoted to overcoming the above challenges for enhancing the
performance of Li−S batteries, mainly focusing on the side of
cathodes in earlier days, including the preparation of carbon
(C)/S composites. Representatively, in 2009, Nazar’s group9
pioneeringly used CMK-3 (a kind of highly ordered mesoporous
carbon material) to accommodate S, PSs, and the discharge final
product Li2S2/Li2S and achieved a high reversible capacity. Soon
afterward, this work stimulated a series of excellent research

studies on other C materials.10 With the deepening of the
research, the C materials do not seem to be effective enough for
suppressing the shuttle effect of soluble PSs when employed
alone as the conductive host material of S. In recent years,
researchers combine other new approaches, such as modifying
separators,11−14 using new binders,15,16 and introducing func-
tional blocking interlayers,17−20 to greatly alleviate the shuttle
effect of soluble PSs. However, unfortunately, the issues related
to Li metal anodes have still not been thoroughly addressed,
while some new problems for Li−S batteries remain on the
way.21,22
It is in 2017 when Zhang’s group23 contended that the failure
reason of pouch cells should be ascribed to the powdering and
induced polarization of the Li metal anode caused by the
unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) film. After that, more
attention was allotted to the side of Li metal anodes. In order to
fabricate a stable Li metal anode, some promising strategies were
explored, including designing a stable artificial SEI film,24−26
exploring high-concentration electrolytes,27,28 introducing func-
tional additives or co-solvents into an electrolyte,29−37 and so
on, which have been adopted to effectively lift the electro-
chemical performance of Li−S batteries. Among these prevailing
strategies, introducing functional additives into the electrolyte
constitutes a facile and straightforward solution to the issues
related to Li metal. In theory, if a class of functional additives can
be preferentially reduced to form a stable SEI film benefiting for
the deposition of Li metal, enhanced electrochemical perform-
ance could be achieved. It is reported that the irreversible
reduction of NO3
−usually takes place at 1.7 V versus (vs) Li/
Li+,38,39 which is much higher than other components in an
ether-based electrolyte used in Li−S batteries.40 It is worth
mentioning that NO3
−can easily react with Li metal to form an
inorganic-rich SEI film due to its strong oxidizing property.40
Although some other nitrates have been reported for use in Li−S
batteries,41,42 only limited publications focus on the relationship
between the electric double-layer (EDL) structure and the
NO3
−-derived SEI film.43,44 Moreover, the detailed formation
mechanism on the NO3
−-derived SEI film is still an open
question and deserves to be explored deeply. In addition, it has
been reported that lithiophilic alloys (LiMg) can guide uniform
Li deposition.45−47
Based on these considerations, in this contribution, we
initially report a promising functional additive, i.e., Mg(NO3)2,
used for an ether-based electrolyte to upgrade the electro-
chemical performance of Li−S batteries. Precisely, Mg(NO3)2
was dissolved in the electrolyte as Mg2+ and NO3
−. On the one
hand, when the electrolyte comes in contact with the Li metal
anode, Mg2+ cations can be reduced by Li metal to form Mg
atoms, replacing the Li atoms on the surface of the Li metal
anode. On the other hand, NO3
−is adsorbed in the inner
Helmholtz plane (IHP) and reduced as inorganic compounds
covering on the surface of the Li metal anode. Integrating the
effect of Mg atom center with the inorganic-rich SEI film, Li
dendrite formation can be effectively suppressed. The
experimental results and theoretical calculations both verified
that Li−S batteries using the electrolyte containing Mg(NO3)2
exhibit better rate performance and cycling stability than that of
a baseline electrolyte.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Preparation of Materials. Commercial Li metal foil with a
diameter of 15.6 mm and a thickness of ∼450 μm, a polypropylene (PP)
separator, carbon-coated aluminum (Al) foil, polyvinylidene fluoride
(PVDF),
N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone
(NMP),
Li
bis-
(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 1,3-diamethoxyethane
(DOL), 1,2-dimethyl ether (DME), 1 M LiTFSI/DOL:DME, and 1
M LiTFSI/DOL:DME with 1 wt % LiNO3 were purchased from
Suzhou Dodochem Co., Ltd, China. Mg(NO3)2 was bought from
Sigma-Aldrich.
2.2. Cathode Preparation. The CMK-3/S composite material
with 70 wt % S was prepared by using a typical melt diffusion method.
First, the mesoporous carbon CMK-3 powder and the sublimed S
powder were mixed with a weight ratio of 3:7 and milled. Then, the as-
obtained powder was sealed in a Teflon pot filled with argon (Ar) and
heated at 160 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, CMK-3/S composites, carbon
black, and PVDF were mixed with a weight ratio of 7:2:1. The electrode
slurry was prepared by using NMP as the solvent and coated on carbon-
coated Al foil. The S loading was controlled from 1.0 to 1.5 mg cm−2.
Next, the as-obtained electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C
overnight. Finally, the electrodes were punched into discs with a
diameter of 12 mm and stored in an Ar-filled glove box (H2O and O2
levels are both below 0.1 ppm).
2.3. Preparation of Electrolytes. 1 wt % Mg(NO3)2 was directly
added into the common electrolyte 1 M LiTFSI/DME:DOL (v/v =
1:1) to form a transparent solution. To compare the solvation structure,
1 M LiTFSI/DME:DOL (v/v = 1:1) and 1 M LiTFSI in DME/DOL
(v/v = 1:1) with 1 wt % LiNO3 were also prepared as control samples.
2.4. Materials Characterization. The X-ray diffraction (XRD)
patterns of the samples at a wide angle (from 10 to 80° two θ) were
obtained by using a Bruker D8 Advance powder X-ray diffractometer by
Cu Kα radiation. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted
on a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA Q500, Thermal Analysis
Instruments, USA) in air or N2 with a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 and a
temperature ramp rate of 10 °C min−1. Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) images were recorded by a JEOL JSM-7100F field emission
SEM, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-
resolution TEM images were collected with a JEM-2100F TEM to
characterize the morphology of the samples. X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out on a VG Multilab 2000.
Raman spectra were recorded using a Renishaw INVIA micro-Raman
spectroscopy system. The 13C NMR spectrum was adopted on a 400
MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker AVANCE III 400WB) to detect the
structure of electrolytes.
2.5. Symmetric Coin Cell Fabrication. The commercial Li metal
foil was polished by roll pressing. Then, the as-obtained polished Li
metal foil was punched into discs with a diameter of 14 mm and a
thickness of 200 μm (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The Li||Li
symmetric coin cells were assembled using the polished Li metal foil as
the cathode and anode, 60 μL of the electrolyte was drip-added, and a
Celgard 2400 PP separator was used. After assembling, the coin cells
were used for electrochemical testing after a 12 h rest. A fixed amount of
Li (mA h cm−2) was deposited back and forth on the Li metal electrode.
2.6. Electrochemical Measurements. The CR2025-type coin
cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box. For the Li−S full cells, 30
μL of the Mg(NO3)2 electrolyte was drip-added to the S cathode, then
the Celgard 2400 PP separator with a diameter of 19 mm was covered
on top of the cathode, and then another 30 μL of the Mg(NO3)2
electrolyte and the polished Li metal anode were placed on the
separator (Figure S2, Supporting Information). As a comparison, Li−S
coin cells using a baseline electrolyte were assembled. A LAND battery
testing system was used to measure the galvanostatic charge/discharge
of Li−S coin cells. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out on a
Biologic multi-channel electrochemical workstation over a frequency
from 100 mHz to 100 kHz with an amplitude of 10 mV.
2.7. Theoretical Calculations. The theoretical simulations of the
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and the highest
occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels of various
molecules were performed by using the Gaussian 16 program based
on density functional theory (DFT), and molecular dynamics (MD)
was used to simulate the behavior of Li deposition.

3.2. Analysis of the Solvation Structure of Li+. In order
to confirm our speculation on the change of solvation structure
of Li+, Raman spectroscopy was first recruited to characterize the
solvation structure of Li+ in the baseline electrolyte containing
the Mg(NO3)2 additive. From Figure 1c, we know that three
electrolytes demonstrate the characteristic peak at 850 cm−1,
which should be attributed to the uncoordinated ethylene oxide
bonds of the free DME molecules.51 When LiNO3 and
Mg(NO3)2 were added, the enhancement of the strength of
the free DME molecule peak occurs since the NO3
−anions can
expel the DME solvent molecules out from the Li+ cation
solvation shell owing to its high DN value. Besides, Mg2+ can
also coordinate with the solvent molecules and further decrease
the number of solvent molecules surrounding Li+. It is reported
that energy consumption of the Li+ desolvation process is the
major and rate-determining step during the charge process.52
When Mg2+ cations are reduced as Mg atoms, some free solvent
molecules form in the electrolyte system, which, to some extent,
are beneficial for Li+ desolvation during the plating process.
The solvation structure of Li+ and the state of the solvents are
both altered on account of the presence of Mg(NO3)2 in the
electrolyte solution, which can be further investigated by the 13C
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) characterization technique
(Figure 1d). We can see that the chemical shift of 13C in the
solvent and anions gradually move to lower ppm values when
LiNO3 and Mg(NO3)2 are introduced into the electrolyte,
revealing a lower extranuclear electron cloud density on the C
atoms, which indicates that the introduction of LiNO3 and
Mg(NO3)2 indeed alters the solvation shell (Figure 1e−g).
Moreover, NO3
−with a high DN value can participate in the
solvation structure of the Li+ cation even in a dilute electrolyte.48
Therefore, such a replacement of solvent molecules by NO3
−is
of fundamental importance since the desolvation process of Li+
can be much easier in the new solvation environment.
3.3. Interfacial Evolution of the Li Metal Anode.
Importantly, during the process of battery assembling, a
replacement reaction can spontaneously occur since Mg2+ is
0.63 V vs Li/Li+ (−2.41 V vs the standard hydrogen electrode)
when the electrolyte containing the Mg(NO3)2 additive comes
in contact with the Li metal anode. Subsequently, the generated
Mg atoms can replace the Li atoms on the surface of the Li metal
anode (Figure 1j). In order to better understand the interfacial
evolution of Li metal under different electrolytes, as a
comparison, the baseline electrolyte and that with the LiNO3
additive are also presented (see Figure 1h,i). Considering the
strong oxide property of NO3
−, when the electrolytes containing
LiNO3 or Mg(NO3)2 come in contact with Li metal, the Li2O-
rich SEI film can be formed, while in the baseline electrolyte,
organic species dominate the formation of the SEI film.

necessary to present more details about the S cathode. Herein,
CMK-3 was used as the host material of S in our work. First, the
structures of CMK-3, S, and CMK-3/S composites were

3.5. Electrochemical Performance Testing. In order to
investigate the impact of Mg(NO3)2 additive on the electro-
chemical performances, we assembled Li||Li symmetric cells.
Figure 3a compares the rate performance of Li||Li symmetric
cells using different electrolytes under different current densities.
Impressively, the Li||Li symmetric cell using the electrolyte
containing the Mg(NO3)2 additive displays a lower over-
potential and a flat voltage curve under all current densities
(from 1 to 10 mA cm−2) compared with that of the baseline
electrolyte. In an attempt to highlight the advantage of the
Mg(NO3)2 additive, the long-standing cycling stability of the Li||
Li symmetric cells was carried out at 2 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h g−1
(Figure 3b). Li||Li symmetric coin cells using the electrolyte with
the Mg(NO3)2 additive show a lower overpotential and a more
stable cycling performance, which is mainly due to the formation
of a more stable SEI film on the surface of the Li metal anode
compared with the counterpart. In addition, we speculate that a
replacement reaction can rapidly occur between the Mg2+ cation
and Li metal atoms when dropping the electrolyte. The
generated Mg atoms will replace the top surface Li atoms in
the Li metal anode and be used as a homogenizer of Li+, enabling
rapid and uniform Li+ deposition, thus effectively suppressing
the formation of Li dendrites during the plating process (Figure
3c).
In order to understand the interface impedance of fresh Li||Li
symmetric coin cells using different electrolytes, the EIS
measurements were performed. In theory, the high-frequency
region semicircle is assigned to the interfacial resistance from the
SEI film formed on the surface of Li metal, whereas the low-
frequency region semicircle demonstrates charge-transfer
resistance between the SEI film and Li metal.54 As shown in
Figure S9 (Supporting Information), we can see that the curves
of Li||Li symmetric coin cells using the electrolyte with and
without Mg(NO3)2 show a comparative SEI film in the high-
frequency region, while in the low-frequency region, the cell
using the baseline electrolyte is slightly higher than that of the
electrolyte with Mg(NO3)2. Notably, in the high-frequency
region, the cell using the electrolyte with Mg(NO3)2 additive
exhibits a relatively high resistance, which could be attributed to
the formation of an inorganic-rich SEI film on the surface of Li
metal.

despite not as good as LiNO3 under a low current density (0.1

additive used for Li−S batteries (Figure S7, Supporting
Information).

3.6. Surface Chemistry of the Li Metal Anode. In order
to verify the origin of superior electrochemical performance, it is
of great significance to investigate the components of the SEI
film formed on the Li metal surface, which is more likely
responsible for suppressing the decomposition of the electrolyte
solution. Herein, XPS and XRD characterization techniques
were performed. Three different Li||Li symmetric coin cells
using the baseline electrolyte and that with the Mg(NO3)2
additive were assembled to analyze their respective SEI film.
Typically, the SEI film accumulated immediately on the Li metal
surface after the first electroplating. XPS measurements were
conducted (Figure 5a−g). From the full spectrum, we can see
the existence of Mg species in the SEI film when the electrolyte
solution with Mg(NO3)2 was used, which means that Mg2+ did
alter the surface property of the Li metal anode during the
deposition process. In the N 1s spectrum, peaks at 398.4 and
400.5 eV corresponding to Li−N and N−S, respectively, were
monitored for three samples due to decomposition of TFSI−on
the surface of Li metal. In addition, a N−O peak from the
reduction reaction of NO3
−is also detected. The existence of
N−O bonding in the SEI film has been reported to be conducive
to the uniform deposition of Li+.56,57 From the characterization
results, we infer that NO3
−can restrain the decomposition of
solvent molecules. On the one hand, the NO3
−anion can
preferentially reduce the coordination number of solvent
molecules around Li+. On the other hand, Mg2+ cations can
also form solvation structure, resulting in the reduction of the
number of solvent molecules of Li ion cations, where the TFSI−
anions in the electrolyte system are more likely to be
decomposed. Admittedly, the protection ability of solvent
molecules for anions decreases when the coordination number
of the solvent decreases, so that the anion is more easily
decomposed, thus resulting in forming LiF and LiNxOy in the
SEI film. Theoretically, the redox reaction occurs when Li metal
immediately comes in contact with the electrolyte solution. With
this regard, XRD characterization was performed. The
characterization result confirms that the XRD peak around
36.4° (marked as a blue circle, JCPDS#35-821) corresponds to
the metallic Mg species (Figure 5h). During the deposition
process, we reason that Li deposition occurs on the surrounding
Mg atoms. Of note, the Mg species was detected on the surface
of Li metal after soaking in the electrolyte with Mg(NO3)2 for 2
h, while the Mg 1s peak was shifted to low binding energy after
the electrodeposition process. Precisely, the strong XRD peak at

3.7. Theoretical Analysis of the Electrolyte and
Simulation of Li Metal Deposition. In an attempt to better
understand the thermodynamic stability of several complexes in
the electrolyte solution system, we performed DFT (Figure 6).
It has been adopted that the redox activity of the electrolyte
strongly depends on the LUMO and HOMO energy levels
based on the frontier molecular orbital theory. From the results
of theoretical calculation, we can see that the LUMO energy
level of the coordination to Li ions and DME with NO3
−is
obviously lower than that of NO3
−, which means a higher
electron affinity and is easily reduced during the formation of the
SEI film, thus preventing the electrolyte from decomposition.
Here, we conducted DFT calculation to simulate the deposition
behavior of Li metal. First, we implemented MD simulation on
the baseline electrolyte. The molecular structures of the baseline
electrolyte, including the LiTFSI and solvent molecules DOL
and DME, are shown in Figure 6a. In the majority of the
simulation time, Li+ is mainly solvated by DME/DOL molecules
rather than in direct contact with the TFSI−anion. In other
words, TFSI−does not participate in the solvation structure of
Li+. Compared with the MD simulation results of the
Mg(NO3)2-modified electrolyte system, the NO3
−ion can
replace some solvent molecules of DME or DOL in the solvation
structure due to the relatively high DN value, which has a lower
activation energy than other types of anions and can be
preferentially adsorbed in the IHP.
It has been reported that the Li−Mg alloy with a limited Mg
amount can be called Mg-doped Li.58 In theory, the simulation
for the Mg-doped Li and inorganic-rich SEI film can be
represented as mobility Li+ through the interface. In order to
better understand the diffusion mechanism of Li ions, we
conducted the DFT model (Figure 6b−g). According to the
results of theoretical calculation, the average difference
approaches 0.06 eV, which means that the interaction between
Li and Mg atoms is stronger than the interaction between Li and
Li atoms. Therefore, the Li atom can be absorbed and grows
around the Mg center during the plating process. In our
electrolyte system, the content of Mg is marginal compared with
Li metal atoms. When a Li atom is absorbed around the Mg
atoms on the surface of from Mg-doping Li metal, the Li atom
could move around the Mg atom or move away from the Mg
atom (Figure 6e−g).
4. CONCLUSIONS
In this contribution, Mg(NO3)2 was initially proposed as an
ether-based electrolyte additive to improve the rate performance
and cycling stability of Li−S batteries. Such behaviors should be
ascribed to the formed Mg atom center and the stable SEI film,
which can navigate the uniform deposition of Li metal.
Benefiting from the advantage of this unique electrolyte, a
stable Li plating/stripping cycling for >1800 h was achieved.
More encouragingly, through experimental results and theoreti-
cal calculations, Li−S batteries using this unique electrolyte
solution deliver a better rate performance and cycling stability
than that of the baseline electrolyte. This work presents a
fundamental insight on functional additives and provides a
guided reference for the design of high-energy-density Li−S
batteries not limited to the LiNO3 additive.
