ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are a promising energy-storage
technology owing to their high theoretical capacity and energy density. However,
their practical application remains a challenge because of the serve shuttle eﬀect
caused by the dissolution of polysulﬁdes in common organic electrolytes. Polysulﬁde-
insoluble electrolytes, such as solvate ionic liquids (ILs), have recently emerged as
alternative candidates and shown great potential in suppressing the shuttle eﬀect and
improving the cycle stability of Li−S batteries. Redox electrochemical reactions in
polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes occur via a solid-state process at the interphase
between the electrolyte and the composite cathode; therefore, creating an
appropriate interface between sulfur and a carbon support is of great importance.
Nevertheless, the porous carbon supports established for conventional organic
electrolytes may not be suitable for polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes. In this work,
we investigated the eﬀect of the porous structure of carbon materials on the Li−S
battery performance in polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes using solvate ILs as a model
electrolyte. We determined that the pore volume (rather than the surface area) exerts a major inﬂuence on the discharge capacity
of S composite cathodes. In particular, inverse opal carbons with three-dimensionally ordered interconnected macropores and a
large pore volume deliver the highest discharge capacity. The battery performance in both polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes and
solvate ILs was used to study the eﬀect of electrolytes. We propose a plausible mechanism to explain the diﬀerent porous
structure requirements in polysulﬁde-soluble and polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes.
KEYWORDS: porous carbon, lithium−sulfur battery, electrolyte, solvate ionic liquids, polysulﬁde-insoluble, pore volume
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are considered as one of the
most promising “beyond Li-ion” technologies because of their
high theoretical capacity (1672 mA h/g) and high theoretical
energy density (2600 W h/kg or 2800 W h/L).1−8 Li−S
batteries are also attractive because sulfur is naturally abundant,
nontoxic, and inexpensive. Although signiﬁcant progress has
been made recently,9 the practical applications of Li−S batteries
have not yet been realized because of their poor electro-
chemical performance, limited by several issues.10 First, both
sulfur and the discharge product Li2S are electrically insulating,
making it diﬃcult to achieve the theoretical speciﬁc capacities
and projected energy densities. In addition, the active materials
undergo severe volumetric expansion/contraction between
sulfur and Li2S during repeated cycles, which results in
decreased mechanical integrity and poor electrode stability.11,12
Moreover, the lithium polysulﬁde intermediates (Li2Sm,
especially 4 ≤m ≤8) tend to dissolve in most organic
electrolytes, are transported through the electrolyte, and
dissipate energy at the lithium anode due to the parasitic
chemical reactions between Li2Sm and the Li metal anode. The
undesired shuttle eﬀect results in the gradual loss of active
material available at the cathode after each cycle. Consequently,
Li−S batteries suﬀer from a low practical capacity, low
Columbic eﬃciency, and poor cycling stability. Previous studies
have focused on the construction of nanostructured composite
cathodes. The composite cathodes are typically composed of
sulfur, a conductive material, and a polymer binder. The
conductive materials are used as sulfur supports and form an
electronic conduction path within the composite electrode.
Nanostructured supporting materials, such as porous carbons,
graphene, graphene oxide, and conductive polymers, have been
intensively investigated.9,13−17 Porous supports provide the
insulating active material with a stable electrical contact,
facilitate electrolyte transport and electron transfer across the
composite cathode, help to accommodate the volume change,
and kinetically alleviate the shuttle eﬀect by retaining Li2Sm in
the pores.18 Nevertheless, this approach cannot thermodynami-
cally prevent the shuttle eﬀect because Li2Sm can still dissolve in
the electrolyte after several discharge cycles due to the direct
contact between the electrolyte and the embedded sulfur.

In addition to exploiting the sulfur cathode, the development
of novel electrolytes for Li−S batteries to thermodynamically
suppress the dissolution of Li2Sm and prevent the shuttle eﬀect
is of great importance.19−25 The most commonly used organic
electrolytes of Li−S batteries are mixed ether solvents, such as
dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (1:1 (v/
v)) containing 1 M dissociative lithium salt (typically lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)amide (LiTFSA)).26 The ether
electrolytes contain excess free solvent molecules, and the ether
oxygen atoms preferentially coordinate to Lewis acidic Li+ ions
(owing to their strong donor ability of the lone pair electrons).
This provides a high ability to dissociate alkali metal salts and
thus a high solubility of the polysulﬁde species, especially for
high-order lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sm, 4 ≤m ≤8). For
example, S solubility (as Li2Sm) can exceed 10 M in
tetrahydrofuran.27 The solubility of one of the most soluble
polysulﬁdes with the nominal formula Li2S8 can be up to ≈6 M
(total atomic S concentration) in a 1 M LiTFSA/G4 electrolyte
(G4: tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether or so-called
tetraglyme).28 Therefore, the polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes
function as a catholyte in Li−S cells after the ﬁrst discharge, as
shown in Figure 1a. In this case, redox reactions of S species
take place through a solid−liquid process between the
electronic conductor (C/S composite) and Li+ conductor
(electrolyte containing polysulﬁdes). Even though the dis-
solution of S species enables fast reduction kinetics, the shuttle
eﬀect results in a loss of active materials from the cathode. An
eﬃcient method to alleviate the shuttle eﬀect of the ether
electrolytes is by using a small amount of lithium nitrate
(LiNO3) as an additive, which can help to form a stable solid−
electrolyte interphase on the Li metal anode.29 However, the
discharge cutoﬀvoltage for the additive containing electrolytes
must be increased to a much safer potential (usually above 1.7
V) to avoid the irreversible reduction of LiNO3 on the
cathode;30 this narrow voltage window inevitably causes a loss
of capacity.31 In addition, the continuous consumption of
LiNO3 on the Li metal anode limits its application for long-
term cycling.
Several new electrolytes (e.g., ionic liquids (ILs)23,35−37 and
concentrated solutions25,38), which are distinct from the
catholyte-type electrolytes, showed great potential in suppress-
ing the shuttle eﬀect and improving the cycle stability. In
particular, certain glymeLi salt molten complexes (e.g.,
[Li(G4)][TFSA], Figure 2), as a new family of solvate ILs,39
have proven to be promising electrolytes for Li−S bat-
teries.19,33,40 The solvate ILs can be obtained by simply mixing
equimolar amounts of glymes and Li salts, whereas conven-
tional IL electrolytes require complicated synthesis and
additional lithium salt. Solvate ILs are intrinsically Li+
conductive, have a high Li+ concentration, and a high Li+
transference number.19,32,33,40−45 More importantly, all of the
oxygen atoms of the ether molecules in the solvate ILs
coordinate with the doped Li salts. Owing to the weak Lewis
basicity of [TFSA]−anions, their solvation power is too weak
to solvate the Li+ ions of Li2Sm.41 As a result, the solubility of
Li2Sm is greatly depressed in the solvate ILs.33,40 For example,
Li2S8, which is highly soluble in conventional organic
electrolytes,27 showed a very low solubility of <7.69 mM in
[Li(G4)][TFSA] (Table 1).33 The redox reactions of S species
in polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes mainly occur through a
solid-state process (S8(s) ↔Li2Sm(s) ↔Li2S(s)) at the
interphase between the electrolyte and the sulfur cathode
(Figure 1b), which is diﬀerent from polysulﬁde-soluble
electrolytes. Therefore, creating an appropriate interface
between sulfur and the carbon support is crucial for the
solid−solid electrochemical reactions. In this respect, the
porous carbons speciﬁc to conventional ether electrolytes
may not be suitable for solvate ILs. Based on an investigation of
the Li−S battery performance of a series of porous carbons
containing micropores, mesopores, or macropores in solvate
ILs, this work aims to optimize the pore structure of cathodic
carbon supports for Li−S cells with electrolytes in which
polysulﬁdes are insoluble.
2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The prepared carbons can be classiﬁed into four types: (a)
inverse opal carbons (IOCs) obtained from a series of silica
opal templates with variable particle size, namely, IOC50(PFA),
IOC100(PFA), IOC280(PFA), and IOC280(Phen) (the former
three carbons were obtained from poly(furfuryl alcohol) (PFA)
based on the established procedures,46,47 while the latter was
from the recently developed method from a protic salt,
phenanthroline bisulfate, [Phen][2HSO4].48−50); (b) meso-
porous carbon (Phen-HS) prepared by a nanocasting method
using [Phen][2HSO4] and LUDOX HS-40 as the precursor
and template, respectively;51 (c) highly porous carbon (A-
NPC) obtained by direct carbonization of another protic salt,
aminoacetonitrile bisulfate, [Aan][HSO4];48,49 (d) KB, a
benchmark cathodic carbon support with high conductivity
and high speciﬁc surface area for Li−S batteries.33,40,45

Both SBET and Vtot of the IOCs increased with a decreased silica
template diameter (Table 2). For example, IOC50(PFA) has
large values for SBET and Vtot of 1280 m2/g and 5.880 cm3/g,
respectively, much higher than both IOC100(PFA) (1080 m2/g
and 4.167 cm3/g) and IOC280(PFA) (938 m2/g and 3.573 cm3/
g). In contrast to IOC280(PFA), IOC280(Phen) possesses a
much smaller SBET and Vtot, mainly from the lesser contribution
of mesopores smaller than 10 nm, as conﬁrmed by a
comparative analysis of the cumulative surface area as a
function of the pore size (Figure 5a). Considering that both
IOC280(Phen) and IOC280(PFA) were obtained under the same
conditions (template and temperature) and the directly
carbonized products from either PFA or [Phen][2HSO4]
(without a template) were nearly nonporous, such a diﬀerence
in the porous structure is signiﬁcant and could be ascribed to
the distinct precursor−template interaction. Furfuryl alcohol
molecules may easily inﬁltrate all voids of the silica opal
templates promoted by the long-time prepolymerization step
under mild conditions (see Experimental Section). This would
cause a high coverage percentage for the precursors on the
template. Phen-HS showed a typical type IV curve with a well-
deﬁned H2-type hysteresis loop,53−55 corresponding to a large
SBET and Vtot of 1161 m2/g and 2.490 cm3/g, respectively. PSD
analysis revealed the presence of narrowly distributed
mesopores (approximately 9 nm) and a small amount of
micropores larger than 1 nm. In addition, A-NPC was mainly
microporous while KB consisted of hierarchical pores, as
indicated by the N2 sorption isotherms and PSD (Figure 4).
The distribution of the cumulative surface area and
cumulative pore volume as a function of the pore size were
plotted in Figure 5 to gain insight into the pore structures. For
all carbons, the cumulative surface areas obtained at a 40 nm
pore size corresponded very well with the SBET calculated by the
BET method. More speciﬁcally, a signiﬁcant gain in the
cumulative surface areas was observed for both IOC280(Phen)
and A-NPC in the range of a pore diameter smaller than 4 nm,
while for the other carbons, except IOC50(PFA), the cumulative
surface areas were greatly increased in the pore size range from
4 to 10 nm. IOC50(PFA) showed a two-stage increment in the
cumulative surface area; one from 5 to 15 nm and another from
15 to 25 nm (Figure 5a). As for the cumulative pore volume
(shown in Figure 5b), only IOC280(Phen), A-NPC, and Phen-
HS showed values close to Vtot. In particular, both the
cumulative surface area and cumulative pore volume of Phen-
HS reached saturation at around 10 nm. In contrast, the
cumulative pore volumes for KB and IOC(PFA) observed at 40
nm pore size are much lower than Vtot, suggesting a huge
contribution of large pores with diameters > 40 nm.

composite, together with the sulfur and carbon results for
comparison. Clearly, IOC50(PFA) is stable up to 500 °C, while
the IOC50(PFA)/S composite exhibited continuous weight loss
between 200 and 450 °C, corresponding to the evaporation of
ca. 67 wt % sulfur; this is in good agreement with the
predetermined value. The sulfur conﬁned in IOC50(PFA)

state, as expected.56,57 XRD analysis revealed the presence of

carbon materials (Figure 6b).58−60 Obvious sulfur signals were
observed for the IOC50(PFA)/S composite, while the
characteristic peaks of crystalline sulfur were absent from the
proﬁle of the KB/S composite. This diﬀerence could be
attributed to the pore structures. As discussed above, KB
contained abundant mesopores, while IOC50(PFA) was
dominated by macropores. Therefore, sulfur may easily diﬀuse

highly dispersed state due to capillary force, while plenty of
sulfur is likely to crystallize on the outer surface of the
macropores of IOC50(PFA). This conclusion was conﬁrmed by
the appearance of more signiﬁcant sulfur signals in

Coin-type cells were fabricated to evaluate the performance
of all composite cathodes using [Li(G4)][TFSA] as the
electrolyte. As shown in Figure 7a, the charge/discharge
capacity and the cycling performance are strongly dependent
on the structure of the carbon materials. All of the initial
discharge capacities were within the range of 600−1200 mA h/
g (Table 2), corresponding to 36−70% of the theoretical
capacity (1672 mA h/g) of elemental sulfur. More importantly,
all the carbon cathodes exhibit a very high Coulombic eﬃciency
of >97.5%, because of the depressed shuttle eﬀect in solvate
ILs. Among the composite cathodes, the IOC50(PFA)/S
electrode exhibited the best battery performance reﬂected by
the highest ﬁrst discharge capacity (1197 mA h/g), and the
highest charge/discharge capacities over 50 cycles (Figure 7a).
The evolutions of the second discharge/charge proﬁles for all
the composites are plotted in Figure 7b. The pore structure also
strongly aﬀects the use of active materials, the discharge
plateau, and the voltage diﬀerence between the charge plateau
and the second discharge plateau (ΔV, shown in Figure 7b). It
is clear that all IOC(PFA) carbons exhibited well-deﬁned two
discharge plateaus and much higher discharge capacities than
the other carbons. In contrast, IOC280(Phen) had a much lower
capacity despite its comparable charge and discharge plateaus.
Changing the macropores (IOCs) to mesopores (Phen-HS)
and further to micropores (A-NPC) suppressed the discharge
depth of the second plateau and simultaneously increased the
ΔV. In particular, ΔV became quite large for A-NPC (ca. 1.1
V), representing the severe kinetic limit of this system.

The optimized carbon IOC50(PFA) was further explored as a
model host for charge/discharge analysis in three diﬀerent
electrolytes, viz., [Li(G4)][TFSA], [Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE,
and LiTFSA-DOL/DME, which can be classiﬁed into two
classes: polysulﬁde-insoluble solvate ILs ([Li(G4)][TFSA] and
[Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE) and conventional polysulﬁde-soluble
organic electrolyte (LiTFSA−DOL/DME). IOC50(PFA) in
these electrolytes displayed distinct battery performance in
terms of the charge−discharge proﬁles as well as the
corresponding cycle performance and Coulombic eﬃciency.

curves of the IOC50(PFA)/S composite cathode in [Li(G4)]-

To lower the viscosity and improve the mobility of
[Li(G4)]+ and [TFSA]−while maintaining the complex cation
structure of [Li(G4)]+, HFE was added to [Li(G4)][TFSA] as
a cosolvent with a molar ratio of [Li(G4)][TFSA]/HFE =
1:4.33 HFE is a nonﬂammable and low-viscosity ﬂuorinated
ether (Figure 2) with a low donor ability. The electrochemical
reactions in [Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE were expected to proceed
with a mechanism similar to that for [Li(G4)][TFSA], but
much faster. Furthermore, the presence of HFE greatly
depressed the dissolution of polysulﬁdes and thus the shuttle
eﬀect of polysulﬁdes. For example, the solubility of Li2S8
dramatically decreased from 7.69 mM in [Li(G4)][TFSA] to
1.25 mM in [Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE (Table 1).33 Conse-
quently, IOC50(PFA)/S in [Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE provided a
much better capacity retention (73.8%) than that in [Li(G4)]-
[TFSA] (61.6%) over 50 cycles while maintaining the high
Coulombic eﬃciency observed for [Li(G4)][TFSA] (Figure
9b). IOC50(PFA)/S delivered an initial discharge capacity of
1143 mA h/g in [Li(G4)][TFSA]/4HFE, corresponding to
68% of the theoretical capacity of sulfur, as well as a reversible
capacity of 844 mA h/g in the 50th cycle. Even though the
addition of HFE did not change the initial discharge capacity of
the cell so much as compared to the [Li(G4)][TFSA] (without
HFE) system, the diluted system yielded a much smaller ΔV
(0.14 V) because of the lowered viscosity and enhanced
conductivity, and it remained constant upon cycling.
Interestingly, IOC50(PFA)/S did not perform well in the
conventional ether electrolyte. As shown in Figure S4, the ﬁrst
discharging process in LiTFSA-DOL/DME delivered a nearly
one-plateau proﬁle with a capacity of 758 mA h/g, which is
much lower than for those obtained in solvate IL electrolytes.
The very short range or even absence of the ﬁrst discharge
plateau could be due to the dissolution of high-order Li2Sm (4
≤m ≤8). The following charge curve becomes ﬂat in the
second plateau and cannot reach the cutoﬀvoltage; this
abnormal curve is due to the serve shuttle eﬀect in LiTFSA-
DOL/DME. The polysulﬁdes (initially formed at the cathode)
can dissolve into the electrolyte and diﬀuse to the anode side
(especially for high-order polysulﬁdes), where they react with
metal lithium to form low-order polysulﬁdes and diﬀuse back to
the cathode side. Such shuttle eﬀects cause the loss of active
materials and lower the Coulombic eﬃciency. Since there are
indeed numerous studies of the reversible charge/discharge of
micro-/mesoporous composite cathodes in LiNO3-free poly-
sulﬁde-soluble electrolyte systems,9,56,62−68 the deterioration of
IOC50(PFA)/S in LiTFSA-DOL/DME could be attributed to
the pore structure of the cathodic supports. Carbons with a
large pore size and large pore volume are more suitable for Li−
S batteries using solvate IL electrolytes, rather than the widely
accepted micro-/mesoporous carbons that work well for
conventional polysulﬁde-soluble electrolyte systems.
The following models, shown in Figure 10, could explain the
distinct requirements for pore structure between polysulﬁde-
insoluble and polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes. As for conven-
tional polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes, the initial active material
(S8) was ﬁrst transformed into Li2Sm upon lithiation and
dissolved into the electrolytes, forming a catholyte. The
continuous lithiation and diﬀusion ensures the complete
consumption of the thick sulfur layer. The electrochemical
performance can be improved by simply choosing the carbon
with an appropriate surface area and pore-size distribution. In
general, a high surface area is expected to be eﬀective for
adsorbing Li2Sm and preventing it from diﬀusing into the bulk
electrolyte. For carbons with similar surface area, those
containing both mesopores and micropores yielded higher
speciﬁc capacities than the ones with only micropores or
macropores. The micropores help to conﬁne the polysulﬁdes,
while mesopores enable better electrolyte access and better ion
transport.16,69 In contrast, macroporous carbons, such as IOCs,
do not seem to be suitable for polysulﬁde−soluble electrolyte
systems because the active material tends to form a relatively
thin layer on the surface of the macropores and facilitates
electrolyte ingress throughout the structure. Both S8 and Li2Sm
can easily dissolve in ether electrolytes, which results in severe
shuttle eﬀects and dramatic loss of the active materials from the
cathode, as shown in Figure 10a.
The in situ formed polysulﬁdes in polysulﬁde-insoluble
electrolytes (such as solvate ILs) cannot dissolve and diﬀuse
into the electrolyte during the discharge process. They are likely
to stay on the pore surface and gradually increase the thickness
of the insoluble Li2Sm layer (Figure 10b) because the active
material undergoes an 80% volume expansion upon lithiation.
Consequently, carbons containing micropores or small
mesopores are unwanted because the insoluble Li2Sm may
easily ﬁll the small pores and complete the discharge before the
full use of sulfur. Conversely, carbons with a large pore size and
volume are desirable because they can support the active
material as a more homogeneous thin layer and accommodate
the volume expansion during lithiation. The porous structure
will also help the electrolytes to wet the cathode and provide
ionic transport. In this respect, highly porous IOC(PFA)s
would be a favorable cathodic host for polysulﬁde-insoluble
electrolytes. Their three-dimensionally interconnected cage-like
macropores with suﬃcient internal voids provide not only a
continuous electronic conduction pathway but also a large
surface area and pore volume for sulfur impregnation, volume

3. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, a series of porous carbons containing ordered or
disordered macropores, micropores, and mesopores were
prepared and used as cathodic supports for Li−S batteries
exploring solvate IL based electrolytes. The carbon pore
volume was found to play an important role in the battery
performance over the speciﬁc surface area, wherein the
discharge capacity increased roughly with the pore volume.
Because polysulﬁdes are insoluble in solvate ILs and tend to
form an inhomogeneous layer that covers the carbon surface, a
suﬃciently intimate contact between sulfur and the electroni-
cally conducting support is required to collect current from the
electrochemically active but insulating sulfur. Therefore,
carbons with both a large pore size and large volume, rather
than the widely accepted mesopores and/or micropores that
work well for conventional polysulﬁde-soluble electrolytes, are
more suitable for solvate ILs. In this regard, IOCs possessing
three-dimensionally interconnected macropores and large pore
volumes are eﬀective for enhancing the sulfur use because the
interconnected wall provides a continuous electronic con-
duction pathway, while the large pore volume provides a high
percentage of free space for volumetric expansion of sulfur
upon lithiation. In particular, the composite cathode with
IOC50(PFA), which had the highest pore volume of 5.880 cm3/
g, exhibited high charge/discharge capacities in solvate ILs over
50 cycles compared to cathodes with other carbons. However,
IOC50(PFA) does not work well in the LiTFSA-DOL/DME
system, suggesting that carbons with macropores may not be
suitable for polysulﬁde-soluble electrolyte systems due to a
severe shuttle eﬀect. This study may provide knowledge
relevant to porous cathode structures for other potential
polysulﬁde-insoluble electrolytes such as concentrated solutions
and aprotic ILs.
4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
4.1. Preparation of Porous Carbon Materials. Ketjen black
(KB) was oﬀered by Lion Corp. (EC600JD, Tokyo, Japan). Inverse
opal carbons (IOCs) were prepared according to previous
procedures.46,47 Typically, commercial monodispersed silica nano-
particles (Seahoster-KE, Nippon Shokubai; diameters of silica spheres,
280 and 100 nm) or a colloidal silica suspension (Snowtex-OL, Nissan
Chemical; average diameter of spheres, 50 nm; concentration, 20.5 wt
%) were dispersed in pure water (Milli-Q) and sonicated for 1 h to
ensure suﬃcient dispersion. The suspension was then slowly poured
onto a ﬂat glass plate. After drying at 50 °C for 24 h, the colloidal silica
opal template was scratched from the glass substrate. All templates
(except the 50 nm opal silica) were sintered at 1100 °C for 1 h to
enhance the strength of the template. A mixture of furfuryl alcohol and
oxalic acid (1/0.005 by weight) subsequently inﬁltrated the interstice
of the silica opal templates. The templates were then heated at 90 °C
for 3 days to obtain poly(furfuryl alcohol) (PFA). The resulting PFA/
silica opal composite was heated to 1100 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/
min and kept at this temperature for 5 h in Ar atmosphere to
carbonize the resin in the interstice of the silica opal. The silica
particles in the carbon−silica composite were suﬃciently etched oﬀ
with a 10% hydroﬂuoric acid solution. The IOCs were thoroughly
washed with pure water until the pH value reached 7, rinsed with
water and methanol, and then dried at 100 °C overnight. The IOCs
were abbreviated as IOC50(PFA), IOC100(PFA), and IOC280(PFA) in
terms of the spherical silica particle diameters of 50, 100, and 280 nm,
respectively.

to

(IOC280(Phen)) was also prepared using a protic salt 1,10-

4.2. Characterization. All of the powder X-ray diﬀraction (XRD)
patterns were collected on a Rigaku RINT-2000 diﬀractometer using
Cu Kα (λ = 0.154 nm) radiation. The diﬀractograms were recorded in
the 2θ range from 10 to 90° with a 2θ step size of 0.02 and a scanning
speed of 10 deg/min. Nitrogen sorption isotherms were recorded on a
BELSORP-mini II (BEL Japan, Inc.) sorption analyzer at −196 °C.
The speciﬁc surface area (SBET) was calculated using the Brunauer−
Emmett−Teller (BET) method. The total pore volume (Vtot) was
determined from the amount of N2 uptake at a relative pressure (P/P0)
of 0.99. The cumulative surface area, cumulative pore volume, and
pore size distribution were calculated using nonlocal density functional
theory (slit/cylinder pores). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was
conducted using a Seiko Instruments thermogravimetry/diﬀerential
thermal analyzer (TG-DTA 6200) from room temperature to 500 °C
at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under 100 mL/min N2 ﬂow. The
morphology of the carbon materials was observed on a JSM-7001F
ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, JEOL Ltd.)
using an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV.
4.3. Preparation of Carbon/Sulfur Composites. Porous carbon
and elemental sulfur (S8, 99.99%, Wako, Osaka, Japan) were mixed
using an agitating mortar. The mixture was transferred to a vial and
kept at 155 °C for 6 h, according to well-established procedures;9 S8
melted and diﬀused into the pores of the carbons during the heat
treatment. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA; degree of polymerization, 3100−
3900; degree of saponiﬁcation, 86−90 mol %) was dissolved in N-
methylpyrrolidone (NMP) at 100 °C and subsequently cooled to
room temperature. The C/S composite and PVA/NMP solution was
mixed to form a slurry, which was then spread on an Al foil current
collector. The composite sheet was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h,
cut into a circular shape (16 mm diameter), and compressed at 100 kgf
cm−2 using a hydraulic press. The mass ratio of S/C/PVA in the
composite electrode was controlled to be 6:3:1, with an electrode area,
thickness, and sulfur weight of ca. 2 cm2, 14 μm, and 1.0 mg,
respectively.
4.4. Preparation of Electrolytes. Solvate IL based electrolytes
were prepared according to previous procedures.33 Typically, LiTFSA
was dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 12 h prior to use.
[Li(G4)][TFSA] was prepared by mixing the puriﬁed G4 with
LiTFSA in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox. [Li(G4)][TFSA] was mixed with
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl ether (a hydroﬂuor-
oether, HFE, Figure 2) in a glovebox at a molar ratio of
[Li(G4)][TFSA]/HFE = 1:4 to obtain [Li(G4)1][TFSA]/4HFE.
The water content in the electrolytes was less than 30 ppm, as
determined by Karl Fisher titration (CA-07, Mitsubishi Chemical). A
conventional organic electrolyte was obtained by dissolving 1 M
LiTFSA in a mixture of DOL and DME (1:1 (v/v)) (denoted as
LiTFSA−DOL/DME).
