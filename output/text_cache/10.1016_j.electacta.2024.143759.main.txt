A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur batteries promote next-generation secondary batteries. Elucidating their electrochemical re­
actions, such as sulfur reduction reactions, particularly their kinetics, is crucial for further improvement of 
lithium-sulfur batteries. Therefore, experimental knowledge of their reaction rates is required. In this study, we 
applied in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy to a lithium-sulfur battery using a sulfolane-based super- 
concentrated electrolyte solution to obtain insights into sulfur reduction reaction kinetics. The Faradaic 
impedance of a sulfur-positive electrode yielded inductive and capacitive semicircles at first and second plateau 
voltages, respectively, in a low-frequency range. This impedance behavior under a low-frequency range was 
derived from multistep reactions. The impedance behavior suggests that the reaction rate constant of the second 
step was greater than that of the first step at the first plateau, whereas at the second plateau, the reaction rate 
constant of the second step was smaller than that of the first step. A Faradaic impedance simulation was per­
formed to estimate the reaction rate constants of the sulfur reduction reactions. The simulation well reproduced 
the experimental data. Furthermore, the simulation showed that the internal resistance of the battery decreased 
as the rate constant of each reaction changed, suggesting that Faradaic impedance analysis is useful not only for 
investigating reaction kinetics but also for improving lithium-sulfur battery performance.   
1. Introduction 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), a powerful method 
for evaluating electrochemical systems, provides information on the 
electrode structure and reactions of electrochemical systems. An 
impedance spectrum analysis can be performed by varying the fre­
quency of the AC signal, and the processes contributing to the overall 
electrochemical reaction rate can be separated based on different 
relaxation times. For the electrode/electrolyte interface, where the 
charge-transfer reaction occurs, a semicircle, which is represented by 
the equivalent circuit of an RC parallel circuit, is observed in the 
impedance spectrum. The relaxation time (time constant) of a charge 
transfer reaction is obtained by multiplying the charge transfer resis­
tance R by the electric double-layer capacitance C. The impedance 
related to the electrochemical process involving electron transfer is 
known as the Faradaic impedance. 
When an electrode surface state varies depending on the input signal, 
the variation provides a time constant, adding a new trajectory to the 
Faradaic impedance spectra. This characteristic trajectory that appears 
in low-frequency ranges has been interpreted using models that describe 
the mass transport and reaction mechanism at the electrode [1]. The 
former is known as the diffusion impedance. When the thickness of the 
diffusion layer is considered infinite, it exhibits a slope of 45◦with 
respect to the real axis in the low-frequency range. In Faradaic imped­
ance due to elementary reactions, an inductive loop is often observed in 
a complex plane, which is useful for interpreting the kinetic reaction 
mechanisms. Here, elementary reactions refer to intermediate reactions 
and redox species adsorption/desorption processes. Epelboin and Ked­
dam et al. [2–4] proposed theoretical models for Faradaic impedance. 
The Faradaic impedance derived from elementary reactions was 
explained based on the Langmuir isotherm, and this model showed that 
intermediates were formed during the iron dissolution reaction [2–4]. 
Inductive behavior was also observed during the electrodeposition of 
metals or alloys, and the inhibitory influence of charge transfer by ad­
sorbates or interfacial layers was discussed [5]. Itagaki et al. [6] reported 
that graphite electrodes exhibited inductive loop owing to the

Lithium–sulfur batteries (LiSBs), which use elemental sulfur as a 
positive electrode material, have gained attention as next-generation 
secondary batteries. However, the dissolution of lithium polysulfide 
produced during the discharge reaction into electrolytes decreases the 
battery capacity. Therefore, it is necessary to suppress dissolution and 
efficiently reduce sulfur to insoluble lithium sulfide. The sulfur reduc­
tion reaction pathway is complex and has been explored using various 
spectroscopic methods [15–18]. Wu et al. [17] found that long-chain 
polysulfides, such as S8

Various reduction reaction pathways for sulfur, which are based on 
thermodynamics, have been proposed depending on the electrolyte 
used; however, the rates of these reactions should be considered for 
efficient reaction progression. Theoretical kinetic models have been 
proposed [19–21] and the activation energies of the reactions have been 
investigated [22]; however, knowledge of the reduction kinetics of 
sulfur is still required because the reaction rates are crucial for LiSB 
performance. 
To explain the Faradaic impedance behavior mechanism, we first 
describe the theory of Faradaic impedance. In situ EIS was applied to 
LiSBs using a sulfolane-based concentrated electrolyte solution to 
determine the kinetic mechanism of the reduction reactions. We per­
formed a Faradaic impedance simulation, focusing on the impedance 
spectra in the low-frequency range of the LiSBs. Furthermore, we studied 
the effect of the determined parameters on battery impedance.

In LiSBs, sulfur, the active material, is reduced stepwise to various 
chain polysulfides. The formed polysulfide may be regarded as an in­
termediate adsorbed on the electrode surface. Therefore, the charac­
teristic Faradaic impedance behavior can be observed in the low- 
frequency range, where variations in the electrode surface state can 
follow potential variations. 
3. Experimental 
3.1. Materials and fabrication of LiSB 
A sulfur/Ketjen-black (SKB) composite positive electrode was pre­
pared according to the method reported by Ji et al. [23]. The details 
were presented in our previous study [24]. KB is a commonly used 
porous carbon material. The mass of sulfur in the SKB composite elec­
trode was 1.0 mg cm−2. In this study, we used a sulfolane (SL)-based 
superconcentrated electrolyte solution as an electrolyte solution [25]. 
This electrolyte solution exhibits low polysulfide solubility [26] and is 
known as a sparingly solvating electrolyte. Lithium bis(tri­
fluoromethanesulfonyl)amide (TFSA) was dissolved in SL in a molar 
ratio of 1:2 (LiTFSA to SL). The obtained electrolyte and 1,1,2,2-tetra­
fluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoroprolyl ether (HFE) were mixed in a 
molar ratio of 1:2. SL was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry and 
used as received. LiTFSA and HFE were obtained from Solvey and Dai­
kin, respectively. 
A three-electrode cell (EC-Frontier, Japan) was used for the elec­
trochemical measurements. An SKB-composite electrode and a lithium 
foil (Honjo Metal, Japan) were cut into circles (16 mm diameter) and 
used as the positive and negative electrodes, respectively. The three- 
electrode cell was fabricated using these electrodes together with a 
glass separator (24 mm diameter, GA-55, Advantec, Japan), and the 
electrolyte solution mentioned earlier. The cell preparation was per­
formed in an Ar-filled glove box. A ring-shaped lithium-metal foil was 
used as the reference electrode. 
3.2. Electrochemical measurements 
The EIS measurements were performed under galvanostatic control. 
This 
method 
is 
known 
as 
in 
situ 
EIS 
[6,27,28]. 
In 
galvanostatic-controlled EIS, the input signal is an AC current signal, 
and the response potential was measured. In situ EIS measurements were 
performed at a C-rate of 0.05C (168 μA) under constant-current char­
ging/discharging condition. The DC current was superimposed with a 
small AC current, which was adjusted so that the response potential 
amplitude between the positive and reference electrodes would not 
exceed 5 mV. The voltage ranged from 1.0 to 3.3 V. The impedance 
spectra were acquired in the frequency range of 500 kHz to 10 mHz in a 
logarithmic scan at five frequencies per decade. The recorded imped­
ance spectra had a distorted shape in the low-frequency range owing to 
time dependence. Accordingly, the acquired impedance spectra were 
applied to the 3D plot method [6,28] to compensate for time invariance. 
The compensated impedance spectra were expressed as instantaneous 
impedance spectra. EIS measurements were conducted under potentio­
static control conditions. In this paper, this method is referred to as 
P-EIS. For P-EIS measurements, the cell was discharged at a C-rate of 
0.05C for 30 min (corresponding to DOD of 5 %), and then maintained at 
an open circuit potential for 4 h to relax to an equilibrium state. The 
impedance was subsequently measured in the equilibrium state in the 
frequency range of 500 kHz to 10 mHz. 
To determine the Tafel coefficient bi at each plateau potential, the 
polarization measurements were performed on a battery with depth of 
discharge (DOD) = 5 and 20 %. First, the cell was discharged for 40 min 
until an inductive loop appeared (corresponding to a DOD of 5 %). The 
polarization measurements were performed after relaxation to an 
equilibrium state for 16 h. The cell was then discharged for 2 h until a 
capacitive semicircle appeared (corresponding to a DOD of 20 %). After 
the equilibrium state was maintained, the polarization curve was 
measured again. The polarization measurements were performed at a 
scan rate of 0.5 mV s −
1 and potentials ranging from open circuit po­
tential (OCP) to −0.05 V vs. Li/Li+. 
Electrochemical measurements were performed using an electro­
chemical measurement system (SP-150e, Bio-Logic, France) in a ther­
mostatic chamber (AC200, EC Frontier, Japan). The temperature was 
controlled within ±0.9 K using a PID control. 
4. Results and discussion 
4.1. Temperature dependence of impedance spectra during discharge 
Fig. S1 shows the discharge-charge profiles of the LiSBs measured via 
in situ EIS at different temperatures. Each cell exhibited discharge curves 
with two voltage-plateau regions at approximately 2.3 and 2.1 V. The 
overvoltage between the charge and discharge curves decreased as the 
temperature increased. In addition, the capacities of the plateau regions 
increased as the temperature increased to 303, 316, and 328 K. Figs. 2 
and 3 show the first discharge profiles and corresponding instantaneous 
impedance spectra of the positive electrode in the LiSB at 303 K and 316 
K, respectively. At 303 K, asymmetric capacitive semicircles, such as 
overlapping semicircles with vertex frequencies at 30.4 and 2.95 kHz, 
were observed in the high-frequency range above 10 Hz, which are in­
dependent of DOD. In the impedance spectrum at a DOD of 5 %, 
capacitive and inductive semicircles were observed at 1.69 Hz and 64.6 
mHz, respectively. At DOD values of 20–50 %, a semicircle at 1.69 Hz 
was not observed, and a large capacitive semicircle, which did not 
converge on the real axis, appeared at 64.6 mHz. The impedance spectra 
of LiSBs often exhibited large capacitive semicircles during discharge 
[29,30]. At a DOD of 80 %, this large semicircle did not appear at 64.6 
mHz, and the capacitive semicircle reappeared at 1.69 Hz. Moreover, 
the Warburg impedance related to diffusion was observed in the 
low-frequency range (below approximately 1 Hz). 
The impedance generally decreased with increasing temperature. At 
316 K, the semicircle at 2.95 kHz significantly reduced, and a single 
semicircle with a vertex frequency of 19.1 kHz was observed in a high- 
frequency range above 1 kHz. The impedance spectrum at a DOD of 5 % 
exhibited a capacitive semicircle at 17.4 Hz and a small inductive loop at 
64.6 mHz. A capacitive semicircle and an inductive semicircle were also 
observed for the impedance at 303 K in the low-frequency region. The 
impedance behavior in the low-frequency region was the same regard­
less of the temperature, although the frequencies were different. At a 
DOD of 80 %, the capacitive semicircles were observed in the high- 
frequency range and at 17.4 Hz. In addition, Warburg impedance 
appeared in the low-frequency region, consistent with the impedance 
behavior in the low-frequency region at 303 K. In contrast, at DOD 
values of 20–50 %, two capacitive semicircles appeared at 17.4 Hz and 
64.6 mHz, in addition to the semicircle in the high-frequency range 
above 1 kHz. This suggests that the large semicircle observed at 303 K 
contained two capacitive semicircles that can be separated by increasing 
the temperature. The diameter of the semicircle at 17.4 Hz increased and 
that at 64.6 mHz significantly decreased with increasing DOD. More­
over, the impedance at 64.6 mHz successively exhibited an inductive 
semicircle, a capacitive semicircle, and a straight line as DOD increased. 
The impedance spectra obtained at 328 K are shown in Fig. S2. The 
impedance behavior in the low-frequency region (~64.4 mHz) was 
almost identical to that at 316 K, except for the Warburg impedance that 
appeared at DOD ≥50%. That is, an inductive semicircle at a DOD of 5 
%, a capacitive semicircle at DOD values of 20–50 %, and a diffusion 
behavior at a DOD of 80% were observed. The Warburg impedance 
appeared at DOD ≥50% because the size of the capacitive semicircle in 
the low-frequency range decreased.

4.2. Impedance spectra at the equilibrium state 
In many cases, the impedance spectra of batteries are acquired in an 
equilibrium state. To confirm the impedance behavior in the equilibrium 
state, P-EIS was performed at 316 K. Fig. 4(a) shows the first discharge 
profile during the P-EIS measurement. When the cell was discharged to 
an arbitrary DOD and subsequently maintained at an open-circuit po­
tential, the potential increased significantly. When it was discharged 
again, the potential decreased exponentially and reached a steady-state 
discharge. The polarization from the pseudo-equilibrium potential was 
small in the first and second plateau regions and increased at the end of 
the discharge, which was consistent with a previous study [31]. The 
large polarization at the end of discharge was attributed to the con­
centration overpotential. A Warburg impedance related to mass transfer 
also appeared in the instantaneous impedance spectra at a DOD of 80 %. 
Fig. 4(b) and 4(c) show the impedance spectra of the positive electrode 
in the equilibrium state at DOD values of 10 and 30 %, respectively. At a 
DOD of 10 % (corresponding to the first plateau), capacitive and small 
inductive semicircles were observed in the low-frequency range below 
180 Hz. The peak frequencies of these semicircles agree with those ob­
tained using in situ EIS. In contrast, a distorted capacitive semicircle and 
the Warburg impedance appeared in the low-frequency range at a DOD 
of 30 % (corresponding to the second plateau). The frequency range at 
which the distorted semicircle appeared was almost consistent with that 
at which two capacitive semicircles appeared in the in situ EIS spectra. 
This suggests that a distorted capacitive semicircle was observed 
because of the overlap between the two semicircles in the P-EIS mea­
surement. The impedance in the low-frequency range (approximately 
64.6 mHz) successively exhibited inductive and capacitive semicircles as 
the DOD increased, which was consistent with the results of the in situ 
EIS measurements. Therefore, this impedance behavior in the 
low-frequency range was caused by the discharge state of the positive 
electrode, regardless of the polarization. 
4.3. Impedance elements of the positive electrode 
Fig. 5 shows a picture summarizing the DOD dependence of the 
impedance spectra of the positive electrode. As shown in Figs. 2(c) and 3 
(c), the impedance spectra of the positive electrode exhibited three 
capacitive semicircles, which can be attributed to the electrode struc­
ture: the first was the time constant assigned to the contact resistance at 
the SKB composite/current collector interface, the second was the time 
constant assigned to the contact resistance at the sulfur (S)/KB interface, 
and the third was the time constant of the charge transfer resistance. The 
trajectory of the impedance at approximately 64 mHz varied depending 
on DOD. This impedance might be caused by the change in the electrode 
surface state. At the first plateau, the time constant assigned to the 
charge-transfer resistance did not change significantly, and the imped­
ance in the low-frequency range exhibited an inductive loop. The change 
in the time constant assigned to the charge-transfer resistance was small 
even at the second plateau, and the impedance in the low-frequency 
range exhibited a capacitive loop. At the end of the discharge, the 
time constant assigned to the charge-transfer resistance increased, and 
the Warburg impedance appeared.

during charging. Fig. 6 shows the instantaneous impedance spectra

DOD values of 65–100%. 
4.4. Estimation of electrode reaction rate of the positive electrode 
In the low-frequency range of the Faradaic impedance of the positive 
electrode, inductive and capacitive semicircles appeared at the first and 
second plateaus, respectively. During the sulfur discharge reaction, 
different multistep reactions can occur between the first and second 
plateaus. As described in Section 2.2, the Faradaic impedance behavior 
changes depending on the values of the Tafel coefficients bi and charge 
transfer coefficients αi at each plateau potential. These coefficients were 
determined using the current-potential curves [1]. Fig. S3 shows the 
current-potential curves at the first and second plateau potentials. When 
the cathodic polarization was low, b1 < b2 < 0. According to the Tafel 
law, the slope is the reciprocal of the Tafel slope B (= 2.303RT/αinF), 
and the relationship between bi and αi is expressed as bi = αinF/RT. At 
the first plateau, b1, α1n1, b2, and α2n2 were −69 V−1, 1.89, −27 V−1, and 
0.73, respectively. At the second plateau, b3, α3n3, b4, and α4n4 were −67 
V−1, 1.82, −16 V−1, and 0.44, respectively. At the first plateau, because 
0 < αi < 1, the electron numbers (n1 and n2) are greater than 1.89 and 
0.73, respectively. Therefore, at least a three-electron reaction can occur 
in the first plateau. Similarly, at least a three-electron reaction occur at 
the second plateau. However, the exact number of electrons remains 
unclear. Therefore, it is assumed that S8 reacts with 8Li+ to form Li2S2 at 
a DOD of 50% and completely reacts with 16Li+ to form Li2S at a DOD of 
100%. S8 reacts with approximately 4Li+ at the first plateau (DOD of 
0–20%) and subsequently reacts with approximately 4Li+ at the second

5. Conclusions 
EIS measurements provide valuable information on LiSBs; however, 
the impedance spectra must be interpreted with caution owing to the 
complex reaction system. In an electrochemical system, Faradaic 
impedance related to the electrochemical reactions is observed, and the 
Faradaic impedance often contains characteristic trajectories owing to 
the electrode surface conditions. When in situ EIS measurements were 
performed on the LiSB with an SL-based superconcentrated electrolyte 
solution, the impedance spectra in the low-frequency range exhibited an 
inductive loop during the early stages of the discharge process. Upon 
further discharging, the inductive loop changed into a capacitive semi­
circle. The Warburg impedance appeared at the end of the discharge 
period. With increasing temperature, the capacitive semicircle that 
appeared at the second plateau voltage was separated into two capaci­
tive semicircles, which were attributed to the time constants related to 
the charge-transfer resistance and polarization resistance, respectively. 
A low-frequency impedance behavior was also confirmed using P-EIS 
measurements. This low-frequency impedance behavior was attributed 
to the Faradaic impedance due to elementary reactions on the electrode, 
such as intermediate reactions and redox species adsorption/desorption 
processes. Based on the Faradaic impedance theory, the rate constants of 
the electrochemical reactions that occurred during discharge were 
estimated. The result suggests that the final step of the multistep reac­
tion was the rate-limiting step. In addition, using the obtained rate pa­
rameters, we simulated the Faradaic impedance spectra of the LiSB. The 
simulations showed that the internal resistance of the battery decreased 
as the rate constants changed. In lithium–sulfur batteries, to prevent the 
dissolution of lithium polysulfide, efficient reduction to lithium sulfide
