Vanadyl Complex-Mediated Molecular Engineering Enables
Homogeneous Modulation of Lithium–Sulfur Chemistry
Meng Wang, Haoji Xiao, Man Yang, Yunfeng Zhang, Qin Yang, Shiying Shen,*
Lixian Song, Qingchun Zhang,* and Yingze Song*
The large-scale commercial application of lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) is
hindered by several critical challenges, including severe lithium polysulﬁde
shuttling, sluggish kinetics of sulfur redox reactions, and unstable lithium
anode surface. These issues signiﬁcantly restrict the discharge capacity,
cycling life, and safety of LSBs. Herein, the vanadyl acetylacetonate (VO)
complex, characterized by a high donor number, is used as an eﬀective
homogeneous catalyst to address these cross-cutting problems. Concurrently,
a functionalized separator modiﬁed with N,N’-di(propanoic
acid)-perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide (PDI) is employed to prevent
the migration of VO molecules from the cathode to the anode side. The
applied VO complex in the electrolyte provides completely active sites and
ensures suﬃcient interfacial contact for homogeneously guiding the Li2S
nucleation/decomposition reactions, while optimizing the lithium anode
interface. By integrating 0.1 wt.% VO complex into the electrolyte and
PDI-based separator, the homogenous catalyic function of the VO catalyst is
eﬀectively pledged. As a result, the LSBs demonstrate favorable performance,
achieving a capacity retention of 97.1% at 0.5 C after 100 cycles and a stable
cycling at 3.0 C over 800 cycles.
1. Introduction
The rapid development of electric vehicles, unmanned aerial
vehicles, and mobile electronic devices has signiﬁcantly in-
creased the demand for high-energy-density storage systems.

Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs), which uti-
lize sulfur as the cathode and lithium as
the anode, undergo stepwise electrochem-
ical reactions to convert chemical energy
into electrical energy, contributing a theo-
retical speciﬁc capacity of up to 1675 mAh
g−1 and a theoretical weight energy density
of 2600 Wh kg−1.[1–3] Hence, LSBs are con-
sidered as one of the most promising high-
energy-density storage systems.[4,5] How-
ever, several critical issues involving low
sulfur utilization eﬃciency, fast capacity
degradation, and safety concerns have sig-
niﬁcantly hindered the commercialization
of LSBs.[6] First, the accumulated long-
chain lithium polysulﬁde (LiPSs) in the
cathode side tends to migrate through the
separator to the anode side due to the com-
prehensive eﬀect of concentration gradi-
ents and electric ﬁeld, giving rise to irre-
versible sulfur loss.[7] Second, the sluggish
reaction kinetics, mainly resulting from the
slow solid-solid conversion between Li2S2 to
Li2S limit the discharge depth, which is bad
for the cycling operations of LSBs.[8] Third,
the inherent non-conductive nature of sulfur and Li2S leads
to inferior electron/ion diﬀusion, contributing to inferior sul-
fur utilization.[9] Finally, the uneven lithium deposition process
promotes the formation of dendrites, posing signiﬁcant safety
risks.[10]
So far, signiﬁcant eﬀorts have been devoted to developing elec-
trocatalysts for comprehensively resolving the aforementioned
issues existing in LSBs.[11–13] The introduction of electrocata-
lysts can eﬀectively reduce the activation energy of sulfur re-
dox reaction (SRR) and optimize sulfur evolution behavior.[2,14,15]
Over the years, electrocatalysts have been developed from met-
als to metal compounds in materials,[16,17] from particles to sin-
gle atoms in size,[18,19] and from single-component to binary or
triple structures,[8,20] aimed at tuning the catalytic activity for fa-
cilitating the SRR. In consequence, these advancements yield
signiﬁcant improvements in battery performance. Nevertheless,
the intrinsic limitations of heterogeneous catalysts, such as re-
stricted active sites and insuﬃcient contact with reactants, have
imposed limitations on the catalytic activity. Homogeneous cat-
alysts that exhibit ultrahigh catalytic activity have been success-
fully applied in multiple electrochemical systems. The emerg-
ing homogeneous catalysts in LSBs are expected to elevate the
electrochemical reaction eﬃciency and thus boost the battery

performance,[21–24] which is crucial for the large-scale fabrica-
tion of LSBs. Furthermore, enhancing LiPS conversion in a lean-
electrolyte environment is critical for improving the energy den-
sity of LSBs toward commercially viable implementation. Recent
studies have substantiated that high-donor-number additives in
the electrolyte serve as an eﬀective solution in this aspect.[25–28]
The development of homogeneous catalytic electrolyte addi-
tives with high donor numbers is of paramount importance. In
this study, vanadyl acetylacetonate (VO) in the electrolyte has
been identiﬁed as an eﬀective homogeneous catalyst for LSBs.
Speciﬁcally, VO catalysts, as a type of molecular metal complexes,
can penetrate the separator to the anode, which may intensify the
irreversible sulfur loss. In light of this, PP separator is functional-
ized by N,N’-di(propanoic acid)-perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic
diimide (PDI), renowned for its stable 𝜋–𝜋stacking structure,
excellent conductivity, and strong capture ability.[29,30] The well-
designed separator can restrict the VO molecules into the cath-
ode side, pledging its homogeneous functions. Therefore, such
a rational design can favorably optimize the sulfur and lithium
electrodes, ultimately improving the capacity, cycle life, and safety
of LSBs.
2. Results and Discussion
The VO is directly dissolved as an electrolyte additive to fully real-
ize its homogeneous optimization function. Of speciﬁc note, the
VO is exclusively added to the cathode side and hardly migrates
to the anode side due to the presence of the PDI@PP separator
(PDI-modiﬁed PP separator). Furthermore, the as-produced VO
not only facilitates the nucleation and growth of Li2S but also sta-
bilizes the lithium anode surface by respectively regulating the
dense ﬂux of lithium ions and optimizing the adsorption, diﬀu-
sion, and deposition behaviors of sulfur species (Figure 1a). The
chemical stability of VO in the electrolyte was measured using
ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectra. Note that the light blue color
of the VO-based electrolyte is unaltered and no precipitation is
generated over 45 days, conﬁrming its superior chemical stabil-
ity (Figure 1b). An eligible adsorption capability for LiPSs is es-
sential for eﬀectively mitigating the shuttle eﬀect in LSBs. Along
this line, theoretical simulations were conducted to evaluate the
anchoring properties of VO. The calculated binding energies of
VO with Li2S8, Li2S6, Li2S4, Li2S2, and Li2S are 1.16, 1.06, 1.00,
1.06, and 1.35 eV, respectively (Figures 1c and S1, Supporting
Information), the values of which are signiﬁcantly higher than
those of graphene,[23] substantiating its strong sulfur species cap-
ture capability. Electrochemical tests for shuttle eﬀect relieving
were also conducted. As seen in Figure 1d, the cell integrated VO-
based electrolyte and PDI@PP displays the lowest shuttle current
response compared with other samples (Figure 1d), evidencing
the synergistic eﬀect of VO and PDI@PP on shuttle eﬀect allevi-
ation. The Gibbs free energies for the stepwise sulfur conversion
reactions were also calculated. As can be seen from Figure 1e,
the conversion of solid S8 to liquid Li2S8 is a thermodynamically
spontaneous and exothermic process, as indicated by the nega-
tive Gibbs free energy change (∆G). The conversion from Li2S6
to Li2S4 shows the largest positive ∆G value (0.17 eV), implying
the rate-limiting step for the sulfur evolutions on VO. Of speciﬁc
note, the slightly smaller ∆G value for VO than that for graphene
conﬁrms its easy-to-occur SRR.
The VO as a homogeneous promoter to interact with sulfur
species and guide the Li2S nucleation as well as growth, con-
tributes to its working mechanism for sulfur conversion reac-
tions. We explored the interaction of VO with LiPSs in work-
ing LSBs by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)
tests. We respectively collected the VO electrolyte in the battery at
the end of the discharge and charge procedure prior to the test.
In Figure S2 (Supporting Information), the characteristic peaks
at m/z of 239.10 and 265.10 correspond to VO+ fragments from
the initial VO structure. After discharge, a new pair of characteris-
tic peaks with m/z of 289.30 and 483.55 are indexed to the chemi-
cal formula of [Li2S6+Li2S2]+ and [VO+Li2S4+Li2S2]+. At the end
of charge, two new pairs of peaks with m/z of 289.30 and 405.35
form and can respectively correspond to the chemical formulas
of [Li2S6+Li2S2]+ and [VO+Li2S4]+. The peaks of m/z at 239.10
assigned to the fragment of VO+ and always exist after the dis-
charge and charge process. The LC-MS results further decipher
the possible working mechanism of VO in the practically oper-
ated LSBs. Of speciﬁc note, the existing peaks of m/z at 239.10
in LC-MS spectra representing the fragment of VO+ throughout
the cycling procedure of the battery conﬁrm the stability of VO.
The structural integrity of the PDI@PP before and after oper-
ating for 100 cycles at 0.5 C was checked. The scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) images and digital photographs show that the
porous microstructure of PDI@PP can be well maintained af-
ter the cycling (Figure S3, Supporting Information), proving its
remarkably durable property. Moreover, compared with the ele-
mental distributions at the initial stage (Figure S4, Supporting
Information), [31] the elemental mapping images display the ad-
ditional and homogeneously distributed V element after 100 cy-
cles (Figure S5, Supporting Information), [32] which conﬁrms the
outstanding trapping eﬀect of PDI on VO molecules. The visu-
alized adsorption experiments, along with UV–vis and Fourier
transform infrared (FTIR) were also used to further study the
trapping eﬀect of PDI@PP on VO. In Figure 2a, after 24 h, the
color of the solution in the smaller vial and the bigger vial of
the device with the PP separator becomes almost the same, in-
dicating that the VO can pass through the separator smoothly.
But for the PDI@PP, the color of the solution in the bigger vial
is much lighter than the smaller vial in the same device, which
clearly shows that the migration of VO is eﬀectively restrained.
To accurately characterize the trapping eﬀect, the VO stemming
from the outside vials was further explored by UV–vis absorption
spectra. In comparison with PP, the characteristic signals of VO
signiﬁcantly decrease due to the introduction of the PDI@PP.
The PDI@PP before and after the cycling was further explored by
FTIR spectra. In contrast with PDI@PP before cycling, the char-
acteristic peak of VO at 1374.5 cm−1 is markedly identiﬁed after
experiencing the long-term cycling operation (Figure 2b). These
results corroborate the restricting function of PDI@PP for VO
molecules. The PDI@PP also shows excellent protective eﬀect
on the lithium anode for stable cycling of Li||Li symmetric cell
(Figure S6, Supporting Information). The lithium surfaces were
examined by disassembling the cycled Li||Li symmetric cells. The
digital photographs in Figure S7 (Supporting Information) show
that the lithium surface with the aid of the PDI@PP is cleaner
and smoother than other samples, proving that the PDI@PP pre-
vents the possible side reaction of VO with lithium metal and the
LiPS corrosion on the lithium surface. Further, the 3D contouring

The contour plots stemming from the cyclic voltammetry (CV)
curves show a pair of reduction peaks (Peak I and II) and one
oxidation peak (Peak III) (Figure 3a–c). In comparison with the
battery only with VO or PDI@PP, the battery integrated VO and
PDI@PP exhibits stronger current responses in both reduction
and oxidation regions, conﬁrming that the integrated design of
VO and PDI@PP can eﬀectively propel the sulfur redox reaction
kinetics. The peak current linearly correlates with the square root
of scan rate based on the Randles-Sevcik equation:[35,36] Ip = (2.69
× 105)·n1.5·A·D0.5·CLi·v0.5, where Ip is the peak current, represents
the potential scan rate, CLi is the concentration of lithium-ions
within the cathode, n is the charge transfer number, D stands
for the diﬀusion coeﬃcient of lithium ions, and A is the area of
the active electrode. The cell-integrated VO and PDI@PP pos-
sess the largest slopes for peaks I, II, and III, manifesting its
excellent lithium ion diﬀusion kinetics (Figure 3d–f). Note that
the VO also produces a remarkable homogeneous catalysis eﬀect
only with the presence of PDI@PP, reﬂecting the utmost impor-
tance of restraining VO molecules to the cathode side. To reveal
the underlying mechanism of VO for regulating lithium ions,
the diﬀusion barrier with the related diﬀusion pathways was cal-
culated based on density functional theory (DFT). In Figure 3g,
the barrier of lithium atom diﬀusion on VO is calculated as low
as 0.06 eV, which is conducive to optimizing the sulfur conver-
sion reactions and lithium deposition behavior by guiding the
lithium ions on the cathode side. Tafel slopes were further calcu-
lated from the linear scan voltammetry (LSV) curves for analyz-
ing the activity of the conversion process of S8 to Li2S4, as well as
Li2S4 to Li2S2/Li2S. It can be observed from Figure 3h–i, the Tafel
slope of the battery integrated VO and PDI@PP (49.3 mV dec−1)
is signiﬁcantly lower than that of only with VO (84.7 mV dec−1),
and only with PDI@PP (61.9 mV dec−1) in the reduction process
of S8 to Li2S4. For the reduction procedure of Li2S4 to Li2S, the
battery with VO and PDI@PP also harvests the lowest Tafel slope
value of 37.8 mV dec−1 among all three samples. Such results fur-
ther demonstrate that the integration of VO and PDI@PP gives
rise to an eﬃcient reduction reaction process.

3. Conclusion
In summary, an integrated design of homogeneous catalysis and
functionalized separator is achieved for simultaneously optimiz-
ing the sulfur and lithium electrodes by directly dissolving the
metal-organic complex of VO in the electrolyte and using PDI
coating onto the PP separator. The high-donor-number VO pro-
vides completely active sites and suﬃcient contact for homoge-
neously guiding the Li2S nucleation/decomposition reactions,
and stabilizing the lithium interface. Meanwhile, the PDI@PP
separator cleverly solves the issue of VO molecules migrating.
As a result, the LSBs exhibit favorable performance, achieving a
capacity retention of 97.1% at 0.5 C after 100 cycles and a stable
cycling at 3.0 C after 800 cycles. This work presents a rational
integrated strategy to simultaneously manipulate the catalyst ac-
tivity for guiding the lithium and sulfur species evolution, which
is beneﬁcial to the fundamental investigations and industrial de-
velopment of LSBs.
