Lithiation products of SPAN may depend on the cross-linked structure, while the structural features of 
SPAN vary widely with the preparation process and subsequent thermal treatment 10. To clarify the effect 
of cross-linked structure on lithiation products of SPAN during cycling, several DFT calculations were 
performed. In DFT calculations, four SPAN structures, i.e., two short PAN chains (four repeat units) 
connected by S-S bond, S-S-S bond, S-S-S-S bond and partially reduced PAN with S-S-S-S bond were 
investigated. These structures are designed by the elemental mass ratios of the as-prepared SPAN used 
in this work (Table S2) and previous reports for reference8,11. To simplify the calculations, their chemical 
structures are also drawn and presented in Figure S6a for easy view. 
Figure 3a presents the optimized SPAN with PAN chains cross-linked by S-S bonds with different S 
numbers in DFT calculations. In structure I, the optimized average bond lengths of S-S and C-S are 2.12 Å 
and 1.79 Å by DFT calculations, respectively. In SPAN structure II (the SPAN chains cross-linked by S-S-
S bonds), the bond lengths of S-S and C-S bonds are 2.09 Å and 1.79 Å, respectively, which are very close 
to PAN connected by S-S bond. When the two PAN chains are cross-linked by S-S-S-S bonds (structure 
III), it is found that the center S-S bond, i.e., SS3 bond, is shorter than the S-S bond (SS1) at the edge (2.04 
Å vs. 2.12 Å). It implies that the SS1 bond is easier to be cleaved when SPAN meets Li+. Moreover, when 
the two SS1 bonds at the edge are cleaved, it will generate S22- anion or radical. For structure IV, when 
introducing additional H atoms to the C atoms which are connected to the S atoms of the lower two 
repeating units of PANs, the bond lengths of SS1, SS3 and CS1 are kept the same as those in structures 
I-III, and the bond lengths of SS4 and SS5 are almost not changed (2.11 Å and 2.06 Å, respectively). 
However, the CS2 bond, in which an additional H atom is added on the C, is elongated from 1.79 Å to 1.86 
Å, implying the reduced stability of C-S bond after the introduction of H atoms and the possibility of C-S 
breakage during lithiation.  
The stability of SPAN in lithiation is further investigated by AIMD simulations. The structure III was 
selected for the investigation of Li2S2 which was inspired by the XPS analysis in experiment (Figure 3). 
Additionally, the ratio of H atom in Table S2 indicates the double bond reduction in SPAN. To demonstrate 
the effect of double bond reduction in lithiation of SPAN, three structures with more H atoms were tested 
(Structures V-VII for simplified cases, Figure S6b) and the simulation diagram is shown in Figure S7. These 
three structures are based on structures I-III. Since there are a lot of combinations for the C=C bond 
reduction in SPAN. To simplify the model, only the C=N double bond reduction was studied in this work 
and the new structures V-VII are obtained based on structures I-III. Limited to the size of simulation box, 
the repeat unit number of PAN was set to four. Twelve cross-linked PAN chains were added to the 
simulation box. Two PAN chains were connected by polysulfide bonds (with the number of S atom to be 2
～4). To accelerate the simulation, we assume the Li atoms have diffused into the bulk SPAN at the initial 
state of the simulation, which are shown in Figure S5. This is the stage III in simulation diagram of Figure 
S7. The Li:S ratio is larger than 1 for all these AIMD simulation systems. It is found even based on Structure 
V, a small proportion of C-S pairs is observed to be broken during the lithiation process, as indicated by the 
decreasing peak intensity of the main peak (at ~1.8 Å) of the RDFs between C and S atoms in 0-5 ps 
(Figure S8a). This will result in a certain amount of Sx2- (x > 2) anion or radical, which is probably the reason 
of the Li polysulfides (LPSs) dissolution in ether-based electrolytes. Moreover, as shown in Figures S8b-d, 
the C-S pairs also show similar behaviors in SPAN with different structures. The breakage of C-S bond 
would affect the degree of crosslinking in SPAN as well as the macroscopic properties since the 
reconstruction of C-S bond is a slow process. Overall, the simulation results infer that the products after 
cleavage of the SPAN in the lithiation process are C-S-Li, Li2S, Li2S2, accompanying with certain amount 
Li2Sx (x > 2) and the main active component is Li2S2. Also, as shown in Figure S9, the height of N-H in RDF 
plots for each system are not changed during the simulation, indicating the reaction energy barrier is higher 
for the cleavage of N-H than S-S or C-S pair. Also, the N atom on SPAN is still able to interact with Li ion 
when it is converted to N-H group.

Figure S12. Simulation snapshots of (a) lithiated SPAN at t = 20 ps in the initial discharge process and (b) 
delithiated SPAN at t = 20 ps in charge process based on structure III. Color code: C atom, cyan; H atom, 
white; S atom, yellow; N atom, dark blue.  
 
The RDFs of S-S (Figure S10) and Li-S (Figure S11) bonds at different periods of time during initial 
discharge/charge processes of SPAN with different structures are studied to learn the formation mechanism 
of the Li2Sx. Here take the structure III as an example to elaborate. As shown in Figure S10a, a high peak 
at r = 2.1 Å is observed at t = 0 ps, suggesting that all the S atoms are bonded to each other at the initial 
state. When t = 0 - 0.5 ps, the intensity of the first RDF peak of S-S atoms falls rapidly and the peak position 
shifts a bit to the right. It means that a lot of S-S pairs have been separated by the Li-S reaction within 0.5 
ps. The height of the second peak also decreases and the position of the peak shifts to the right, implying 
the S atoms are moving farther. Meanwhile, new peaks at r = 2.5 Å in Figure S11a appear within 0.5 ps, 
indicating the rapid formation of Li-S pairs. After 5 ps, there is no peak at r = 2.1 Å in RDF of S-S atoms 
and no changes in the height and position of the original second peak, which means the breakage of S-S 
bond has completed. The peak in RDF of Li-S bonds also stops changing after 5 ps, further verifying the 
completion of Li-S reaction in SPAN. It simultaneously implies that the reaction rate between Li and S is 
very fast. Therefore, in a real battery system, the Li-S reaction mainly depends on the diffusion rate of Li+ 
in SPAN cathode. Figure S12a shows the final state after 20 ps AIMD simulation, in which the breakage of 
S-S bonds and the formation of Li2Sy compounds can be found. When it turns to delithiation, a new peak of 
S-S bonds at r = 2.12 Å appears within 0.5 ps. Meanwhile, the RDF peak intensity of Li-S bonds shows a 
sharp drop in 0.5 ps. These imply the rapid breakage of Li-S pairs and the fast formation of S-S pairs 
between PAN chains, which can be clearly seen from the simulation snapshot shown in Figure S12b. In 
addition, note that in Figure S11a, the Li-S peak still exists until 20 ps, meaning that some Li atoms would 
still bond to S atoms after the first cycle. Similar observation can also be found in the RDFs of Li-N (Figure 
S20) and Li-C (Figure S21) atoms. They together lead to the irreversible capacity loss in the first cycle for 
Li||SPAN batteries.  
Based on the above calculations, it reveals that the S-S breakage in SPAN is very fast and the cleavage 
products will be mainly Li2S2 accompanying with a certain amount of soluble LPSs regardless the chemical 
structure of SPAN. And the breakage of Li-S bond during delithiation process is also rapid. Therefore, the 
Li-S reaction rate will mainly depend on the diffusion rate of Li+ in SPAN. It means that the conductivity of 
cathode/electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer will become a critical determinant for the electrochemical rate 
capability of Li||SPAN cells, and the suppression of polysulfides solubility by the electrolyte will govern the 
long cycling stability of Li||SPAN cells.

XPS depth profiling analyses on both cycled SPAN cathodes and Li metal anodes were carried out 
to identify the reasons for the different characteristics between the electrode/electrolyte interphase layers 
formed in the two different electrolytes. Figure S28 shows the atomic ratios of the detected elements on the 
cycled SPAN cathodes in the two electrolytes. Obviously, the CEI formed in LFSI-ether exhibits a much 
more uniform elemental distributions than that in LPF-carbonate in the profiling depth from 5 nm to 30 nm. 
From Figure 5, F signals can be detected in the profiling depth from 0 nm to 30 nm in both LPF-carbonate 
and LFSI-ether, indicating the salt decomposition and the existence of CEIs at a depth of 30 nm in both 
electrolytes. While, in LPF-carbonate, it is found that N and S signals are absent in the profiling depths of 
0 nm and 2 nm but appear after sputtering for more than 5 nm (Figure S29). Moreover, from 5 nm to 30 nm 
depth, the atomic ratios of N and S gradually increase. Since N and S are characteristic to SPAN in Li||SPAN 
cells with LPF-carbonate, the simultaneous presence of N, S and F in the CEI formed in LPF-carbonate 
indicates the diffusion of electrolyte decomposition products into the SPAN bulk. It means the CEI layer 
enabled by LPF-carbonate is not only formed on the surface of the SPAN cathode, but also across it and 
to the interior. This is further supported by the S 2p and N 1s XPS spectra of the cycled SPAN in LPF-
carbonate. As shown in Figure S29, the S-S and C-S signals in S 2p spectra as well as pyrrolic N and 
pyridinic N signals in N 1s spectra detected in SPAN cycled in LPF-carbonate are quite the same as those 
observed in pristine SPAN (Figure S15). It further confirms that the N and S detected in the 5-30 nm depth 
are from the SPAN. In SPAN cycled in LFSI-ether, S and N can also be originated from the decomposition 
of LiFSI salt. From Figure S30, the significantly different S 2p and N 1s XPS spectra of the SPAN cycled in 
LFSI-ether from those of pristine SPAN indicate that the S and N detected in the CEI formed in LFSI-ether 
are not from the bulk SPAN. The appearance of S-Ox and N-Ox species further confirm the S and N signals 
are contributed by the decomposition products of LiFSI salt in LFSI-ether.  
From the O 1s (Figure 5a) and C 1s (Figure S29) XPS spectra of SPAN cycled in LPF-carbonate, 
obvious C-O and C=O signals are observed, suggesting the significant solvent decomposition. This is 
further supported by the high atomic ratios of C and O as revealed in Figure S28, which also identifies the 
dominantly organic nature of the CEI formed in LPF-carbonate. The F and P species observed in Figure 5a 
and Figure S29, respectively, indicate the decomposition of LiPF6 salt. However, the content of inorganic 
decomposition product, e.g. LiF, is limit (Figure 5a). Such an organic species dominated CEI layer with a 
small amount of Li salts (LiF and Li2O) is supposed to be loose and weak, being hardly to effectively 
passivate the SPAN cathode surface. It is further confirmed by the appearance of N signal on the Li metal 
anode surface cycled in LPF-carbonate (Figure S32). Consequently, the continuous electrolyte reaction 
during cycling results in resistive CEI layer which has low ionic conductivity, leading to the poor rate 
capability and low-temperature (LT) discharge performance in Li||SPAN cells with LPF-carbonate. The 
diffusion of electrolyte decomposition products from the surface to the interior of the SPAN as discussed 
above can also be attributed to the incompact CEI formed in LPF-carbonate. The destruction to structural 
integrity brought by the invasion of decomposition products into the SPAN together with the flimsy surface 
layer results in the breakage of SPAN cathode electrode after cycling in LPF-carbonate (Figure 2a), which 
should be responsible for the sudden capacity decay of cells with LPF-carbonate (Figure 1).  
For the CEI formed in LFSI-ether, a considerable amount of Li salt species, e.g. Li2O, LiF and Li2S 
(Figure 5b), can be found in the whole profiling depth, implying the deep decomposition of LiFSI salt. These 
Li salt components in CEI play crucial roles in promising the superior battery performances in Li||SPAN 
cells. On one hand, they provide high conductivity, which is supported by the much lower interfacial 
impedance in the LFSI-ether cells than in the LPF-carbonate cells and limited increase of impedance after 
cycling. The interfacial diffusivity for Li+ consequently benefits the rate capability and LT discharge 
performance of Li||SPAN cells with LFSI-ether, and can reduce the possibility of Li deposition on the SPAN 
surface.12 On the other hand, the insulating nature of the LiF-enriched phase together with Li2O and Li2S 
effectively passivate the SPAN cathode surface, which well protects the SPAN cathode from continuous

temperatures13. In F 1s XPS spectra, C-F signals are detected at profiling depths of 0 nm and 2 nm, 
confirming the participation of TTE diluent in the CEI formation process in LFSI-ether. The soundness and 
structural stability endowed by the LiFSI decomposition products and the flexibility benefiting from the 
organic species together build a robust CEI layer on the surface of SPAN, contributing the cathode 
electrode integrity after cycling (Figure 2a).  
The electrolyte compatibility with Li metal anode is another critical factor for the cell cycling stability. 
Similar to the observation in CEI, the SEI formed in LFSI-ether shows a more uniform atomic ratio 
distribution in the vertical profiling depth than that in LPF-carbonate (Figure S28). The C 1s, O 1s and F 1s 
XPS spectra (Figure S32) indicate the decomposition of both Li salt and solvents in LPF-carbonate. And 
the obvious C-O and C=O signals suggest that the SEI is dominated by organic decomposition products, 
accompanying with a small amount of LiF salt. Moreover, N signals are observed at different profiling depths 
in the SEI formed in LPF-carbonate. As shown in Figure S32, the N 1s spectra are quite similar to those of 
pristine SPAN cathode, indicating that the N species detected on the Li anode in LPF-carbonate are from 
SPAN cathode, and the N atomic ratio decreases from 0 nm to 30 nm (Figure S28). These observations 
mean that some N-containing species produced during the Li storage reaction of SPAN dissolve from the 
SPAN cathode. Then they migrate to Li metal anode and deposit on the surface as well as diffuse deep 
into the SEI, implying the incompact SEI formed in LPF-carbonate. These unfavorable natures of the SEI 
lead to the serious corrosion of Li metal anode after cycling in LPF-carbonate and the poor cycling stability 
under both 25 and 60 °C (Figure 1). When the Li metal was cycled in LFSI-ether, a LiF-enriched SEI 
accompanying with other Li salts from LiFSI decomposition such as Li2O, Li2S, Li3N can be observed 
(Figures S33 and S34). The excellent electronic insulating capability and structure stability of these Li salts 
especially LiF in SEI greatly benefit the protection of Li anode from further electrolyte erosion during cycling. 
Moreover, due to the relatively low charge densities, the SOx, SO2F, N-SOx and N-(SOx)2 species originating 
from LiFSI decomposition can provide efficient Li+ transport pathways and correspondingly inhibit the Li 
deposition and dendrite growth, contributing to stable cycling in LFSI-ether.

As shown in Figure S27, the electrochemical reduction of LFSI-ether starts at ~1.5 V with the peak at 
~1.4 V vs. Li/Li+. Above this voltage, there should be no electrochemical reduction of LiFSI. However, as 
indicated by the XPS observations on SPAN at Stage I during the initial cycle (Figure 3d), the SOx-F peak, 
which is a signature of LiFSI salt decomposition, is already present on the SPAN surface even at the 
beginning of the cell discharge stage (about 1.8 V, Figure 3b), while the C-F peak, characteristic signal of 
TTE, and the LiF peak, which may come from decompositions of both LiFSI and TTE, are also present 
although the contents may be limited even after five cycles (Figure 3h). This indicates there already exist 
chemical reactions between the lithiated SPAN products (like Li2S2) and LiFSI and TTE. With the further 
discharge, i.e. continuing lithiation of SPAN (Stages II and III in Figure 3b), more SOx-F, C-F and LiF are 
formed as indicated by the increased peak heights of these species (Figure 3d). Although Figure S27 shows 
the reduction of LFSI-ether starts at 1.5 V on clean Cu surface, the surface condition on SPAN electrode 
at Stage II is different since there already exists a passivation layer generated by the chemical reactions of 
the lithiated SPAN products (like Li2S2) and LiFSI and TTE, which may suppress the electrochemical 
reduction of LFSI-ether, similar to the case of conventional LiPF6/carbonate electrolytes on graphite anode 
surface during the formation cycles.
