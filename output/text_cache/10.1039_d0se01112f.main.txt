A Li2S cathode has a high theoretical capacity (1166 mA h g1); however, it is inherently an insulator. To
remedy this defect, composite sulﬁdes can be prepared with transition-metal sulﬁdes having better
conductivity than Li2S. Among them, a Li8FeS5 (Li2S/FeS ¼ 4/1, mol/mol) cathode theoretically delivers
888 mA h g1. This cathode, however, exhibits poor cycling performance in 1.0 M LiPF6/ethylene
carbonate (EC)–dimethyl carbonate (DMC) (EC/DMC ¼ 1/1, v/v) electrolyte; meanwhile, concentrated
(3.5 M) electrolyte improves the performance. Herein, we elucidate the degradation mechanisms
through analyses of cycled electrolytes and cathode surfaces. We identify organic polysulﬁdes (R–Sn–R)
(not lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn)) after charging and organic sulﬁdes (R–S–R) after discharging in the 1.0 M
electrolyte. Meanwhile, neither are detected in the 3.5 M electrolyte; instead, salt decomposition
products (mainly LiF) are detected on the cathode surface. Therefore, electrolyte modiﬁcations can
further improve the cyclability of sulfur-based secondary batteries including lithium–sulfur battery.
Introduction
Since their commercialization in 1991,1 rechargeable lithium-
ion batteries (LIB) have been adopted in various portable
devices, as well as in larger devices such as electric vehicles (EV)
and plug-in hybrid vehicles.2,3 On the other hand, LIB cells have
been maturing over the past few decades; their energy densities
are approaching the theoretical limit (200–250 W h kg1-cell),3,4
which does not suﬃciently satisfy the EV battery requirements
for extended driving ranges.4 A major factor limiting the energy
density
is
the
inherent
theoretical
capacity
limit
(250–
300 mA h g1) of intercalation cathodes.5 Therefore, next-
generation
cathode materials with
much higher specic
capacities will be required to further promote the electrication
of vehicles.
Sulfur,
which
has
a
very
high
theoretical
capacity
(1672 mA h g1), is a promising cathode material, and much
progress has been made in understanding and improving its
electrochemical performance.5 However, further development is
impeded by the use of a lithium metal (or lithiated) anode to
form a redox couple because a sulfur cathode itself does not
contain any lithium carriers. A lithium metal anode can inevi-
tably cause lithium dendrite growth during charge–discharge
cycling. This dendrite formation will lead to poor cycle perfor-
mance and increase the probability of internal short circuits,
resulting in safety-related issues.6
Fully-lithiated sulfur, or lithium sulde (Li2S), which also
has a very high theoretical capacity (1166 mA h g1),7 is a more
attractive cathode material because it enables the use of
lithium-metal-free anodes such as silicon or tin.5,7 However,
Li2S, as with sulfur, is electrically insulating; to enhance its
utilization rate, it must be made electrically conductive. Various
methods to increase the electrical conductivity of the Li2S
cathode, such as the formation of composites with metals (Li2S–
Fe, Li2S–Cu)8–10 or carbon (Li2S–C),11–16 have been attempted.
Moreover, transition-metal suldes, such as FeS2 (However,
FeS2 is electrochemically irreversible and forms FeS on charge
aer
reduction
to
Fe
metal
(and
Li2S)
takes
place
on
discharge.18), FeS, and CuS are more electrically conductive than
Li2S and function as cathode materials with high theoretical
capacities of 894, 610, and 560 mA h g1, respectively.17–20
Therefore, composite suldes consisting of Li2S and transition-
metal suldes can be expected to increase both the specic
capacity and electron conductivity. Recently, we prepared the

melting point are high. Mixing with low-viscosity ethers (DME,35

the cyclability. Specically, the mixture of sulfolane (tetra-

sulfone)
with

solvents adopted in LIB; they are basically incompatible with
the sulfur-based cathodes and cannot be used unless sulfur is
anchored in the cathode (sulfur-impregnated carbon38–40) or is

compound
(sulfurized
poly-

suldes nucleophilically react with the carbonate solvents to
give carbonate decomposition products, which considerably
deteriorates the lithium–sulfur battery performance.44 Still, the
underlying reaction mechanisms, such as what decomposition
products are formed, and how these products act during
cycling, remain unclear.
Furthermore, understanding electrode–electrolyte interface
is also of great importance to improve the cyclability. In LIB,
lithium intercalation into the graphite anode on the initial
charge is accompanied by reductive decomposition of the
electrolytes, which starts from 0.8 V (2 V in some cases) vs. Li/
Li+, forming solid electrolyte interface (SEI) on the anode.45 In
the sulfur-based batteries, on the other hand, the low operating
voltage of the cathodes (1–3 V vs. Li/Li+) is close to that of the
electrolytes' reductive decomposition. Thus, a lm similar to
that on the anode might be formed on the cathode, aﬀecting the
battery performance. In this study, we analyzed the substances
in the carbonate electrolyte—1.0 or 3.5 M LiPF6/ethylene
carbonate (EC)–dimethyl carbonate (DMC) (EC/DMC ¼ 1/1, v/
v)—aer charge–discharge cycling of the Li8FeS5kLi cell using
inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and
liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). In addi-
tion, we analyzed the interfacial surface between the cathode
and electrolyte using time-of-ight secondary ion mass spec-
trometry (TOF-SIMS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS). Based on the obtained results, we claried the degrada-
tion mechanisms of the Li8FeS5 composite cathode.

reversibility in terms of Li insertion/extraction reactions, and its
interaction with the electrolyte has a major impact on the
electrochemical performance of the Li8FeS5kLi cell.
Electrolyte analysis of cycled Li8FeS5kLi cell
We analyzed the electrolyte solutions extracted from the Li8-
FeS5kLi cells aer charge–discharge cycles using ICP-MS and
LC-MS to elucidate their interaction with the Li8FeS5 composite
cathode during cycling. We rst used ICP-MS to evaluate the
electrolyte solutions cycled with 1.0 M LiPF6/EC–DMC (EC/DMC
¼ 1/1, v/v), and the iron and sulfur contents obtained are shown
in Table S1, ESI.† While the iron content is lower than the
detection limit aer 30 charge–discharge cycles, the amount of
sulfur continues to increase with the cycle number. The sulfur
constituent, which can be found only in the Li8FeS5 cathode in
the cell, is believed to dissolve in the electrolyte solution during
cycling, which would lead to capacity fading as the number of
cycles increases.
To clarify which molecular components are involved in the
cycled electrolyte, the 1.0 M electrolyte solutions aer cycling
were analyzed using LC-MS. The proposed molecular compo-
sition and structure of each component at each chromatogram
peak (Fig. 2a) are summarized in Table S2, ESI.† As shown in
Fig. S2, ESI,† the components were identied by analyzing the
accurate masses (m/z) of the molecular weight-related ions and
their fragment ions detected at each peak (Fig. 2a).48,49 Those
observed in the cycled 1.0 M electrolyte solutions are not only
degradation products formed by reactions of the carbonate
solvents (EC and DMC) with lithium carbonate and alkox-
ides48,49 (hereaer denoted as “carbonate oligomers”), but also
carbonate degradation products from reactions with sulfur
components (hereaer denoted as “sulfur-containing carbonate
oligomers”). As shown in Fig. 2b, in addition to the increase in
the amounts of carbonate oligomers, the amounts of sulfur-
containing carbonate oligomers continue to increase with the
cycle number, which supports the increment of the sulfur
content observed by ICP-MS (Table S1, ESI†). We also compared
the 1.0 M electrolyte solutions from the charged cells with those
from the discharged cells (also described in Fig. 2 and Table S2,
ESI†) to understand the increase in the amounts of carbonate
oligomers and sulfur-containing oligomers during cycling.

We next analyzed the electrolyte solutions cycled with the
highly concentrated electrolyte, or 3.5 M LiPF6/EC–DMC (EC/DMC
¼ 1/1, v/v). The ICP-MS measurement indicates that the amount of
sulfur in the cycled electrolyte solutions is below the detection
limit (Table S3, ESI†). The LC-MS chromatograms of the 3.5 M
electrolyte solutions are superimposed on the baselines of those of
the 1.0 M electrolyte solutions for each cycle (Fig. S5a, ESI†).
Meanwhile, the chromatographic peaks of the 3.5 M electrolyte
solutions can be seen by reducing the dilution ratio (i.e., the
amount of diluent added) used in the LC-MS analysis as well as by
increasing the amount of sample injected (Fig. S5b, ESI†).
However, the carbonate oligomers and sulfur-containing ones are
not detected in the 3.5 M electrolyte solutions (Table S4, ESI†). In
other words, the high electrolyte concentration can considerably
suppress the dissolution of Li2Sn from the Li8FeS5 cathode in the
electrolyte and the decomposition of the carbonate solvents during
cycling. According to the literature on solvation,54 most of the
carbonate solvent (EC and DMC) molecules can participate in Li+
solvation if the Li salt concentration is less than four solvent
molecules per Li+ ion. As listed in Table S5, ESI,† the calculated
mole ratio of the solvent and salt in 3.5 M LiPF6/EC–DMC (EC/
DMC ¼ 1/1, v/v) is 3.1, which corresponds to less than four
solvent molecules to each Li+ ion. Therefore, there may be almost
no remaining unsolvated EC or DMC molecules, which can
dissolve Li2Sn decomposing the solvent. This suppression of Li2Sn
dissolution by the concentrated (3.5 M) electrolyte would, conse-
quently, contribute to the superior capacity retention of the Li8FeS5
cell. However, despite the 3.5 M electrolyte, the capacity of the cell
gradually decreases with the number of cycles.

shown in Fig. 6. The high-resolution spectra of Li1s, C1s, O1s,
F1s, P2p, and S2p were measured alternating with argon ion

of 5 and 10 nm (SiO2 conversion) for the 1.0 and 3.5 M cathodes,
respectively. The binding energies in Fig. 6 were corrected by
assuming the F1s main peak to be 685.0 eV (F–Li bond) since
LiF-related secondary ions have been found by TOF-SIMS
analysis (Fig. S6, ESI†). From the Li1s spectra, a main peak
exists around 56 eV at all the etching levels on both the cathode
surfaces, indicating the occurrence of an Li–F bond. This Li–F
bond and the above-mentioned F–Li bond from the F1s spectra
support the presence of LiF on both the cathode surfaces.
Meanwhile, the Li1s spectra of the 1.0 M cathode, shiing
slightly toward lower energies compared to those of the 3.5 M
cathode, can include other chemical bonds such as oxides (e.g.,
Li–O bond). In the C1s spectra, peaks around 290 eV observed at
all the etching levels on the 1.0 M cathode can be assigned to
a CO3 bond and not to a C–F bond because there is almost no
peak corresponding to an F–C bond (688 eV) in the F1s spectra.
Furthermore, the existence of the Li–O bond in the Li1s spectra
mentioned above suggests that Li2CO3 (Li–O–C(]O)–O–Li) can
exist on the 1.0 M cathode surface. On the 3.5 M cathode
surface, on the other hand, the peaks of the CO3/C–F bond are
not clearly observed in the C1s spectra; thus, Li2CO3 can be
considered absent. From the S2p spectra, a peak (ca. 160 eV
(S2p3/2)) corresponding to the S–Li bonding state is observed on
the 1.0 M cathode surface, where the peak intensity increases
with the number of etching steps. Moreover, as shown in
Fig. S7, ESI,† the S–Li bond peak detected on the 1.0 M cathode
surface is in accord with that on the fresh cathode surface
before exposure to the electrolyte, which suggests that the S–Li
bond can arise from the Li8FeS5 cathode material. On the 3.5 M
cathode surface, however, even aer 10 nm of etching, the S–Li
bond peak is not clearly recognized, which, therefore, suggests
that the lm formed on the 3.5 M cathode is thicker than that of
the 1.0 M cathode. Regarding the S–C/S–P/S–S bond (ca. 164 eV
(S2p3/2)), the corresponding peaks are hardly visible on the
1.0 M cathode surface while observed clearly on the 3.5 M
cathode surface. According to the TOF-SIMS (Fig. 5), the 3.5 M
cathode surface contains more secondary ions related to sulfur-
containing carbonate solvent decomposition products and
LiPF6 salt decomposition products than the 1.0 M surface.
Therefore, the S–C/S–P/S–S bond peaks are thought to be
derived from those decomposition products. From the P2p
spectra, a phosphide peak (ca. 130 eV) is conrmed on the 3.5 M
cathode surface, which can arise from the reductive decompo-
sition of LiPF6 salt.

These results obtained by TOF-SIMS (Fig. 5) and XPS (Fig. 6
and 7) analyses can be explained using the electric double layer
model
known
as
the
Bockris–Devanathan–M¨uller
(BDM)
model55 (or the Grahame model56). In this model (Fig. 8a), which
has been widely accepted, solvent molecules together with
specically adsorbed anions, i.e., those anions in direct contact
with the cathode, are adsorbed in an orderly fashion on the
cathode surface, thus forming an inner Helmholtz plane (IHP).
This is followed by the adsorption of solvated ions outside these
solvent molecules, thus forming an outer Helmholtz plane
(OHP). Then, a diﬀuse layer is located in the solution side of the
OHP. For the low-concentration (1.0 M) electrolyte, the mole
ratio of the solvent (EC and DMC)/salt (LiPF6) is calculated to be
ca. 13 mol mol1 (Table S5, ESI†); consequently, even aer the
solvation (4 mol mol1),54 there are many unsolvated molecules
lein the electrolyte. These unsolvated carbonate molecules
would occupy the IHP of the 1.0 M cathode (Fig. 8a, 1), and thus
generate more carbonate decomposition products on the
cathode through reactions such as Scheme 1(a)–(h) during
cycling. Meanwhile, for the concentrated (3.5 M) electrolyte, the
solvent/salt mole ratio decreases to ca. 3 mol mol1 (Table S5,
ESI†); therefore, most of the solvent molecules would partici-
pate in Li+ solvation. Consequently, PF6
 ions, as well as
solvated carbonate solvent molecules, may adhere directly to
the 3.5 M cathode surface without any intervening unsolvated
molecules occupying the IHP (Fig. 8b). Then, Li2Sn, which can
be formed on the 3.5 M cathode during charge, would react with
the PF6
 ions and solvated carbonate solvent molecules on the
IHP, giving more sulfur-containing salt decomposition prod-
ucts through eqn (3),57 for example, and sulfur-containing
carbonate products, respectively, on the 3.5 M cathode surface.

Furthermore, in the concentrated (3.5 M) electrolyte, where
most of the solvent molecules solvate the salt (ions), the above
decomposition products would be less likely to be solvated
(dissolved) in this viscous electrolyte solution; they would
instead remain on the cathode surface, thus forming a cathode
lm. This is believed to be the reason for inclusion of more
sulfur-containing carbonate decomposition products in the
3.5 M cathode lm. In contrast, in the 1.0 M electrolyte, the
sulfur-containing carbonate oligomers, detected in the electro-
lyte solution using LC-MS, would be solvated (dissolved) by the
many unsolvated solvent molecules, and migrate from the
diﬀusion layer to the electrolyte bulk. The cycle degradation
resulting from the concentrated (3.5 M) electrolyte would,
consequently, be aﬀected by this thick lm formation consist-
ing of salt decomposition products, mainly insulative LiF, on
the cathode surface.

and lithium alkoxides (ROLi) generated by reductive decom-

R–S–R can arise through reductive –S–S– bond cleavage of R–Sn–

trolyte, meanwhile, the performance of the cell improves.
Analyses using LC-MS and ICP-MS clarify that carbonate oligo-
mers and sulfur-containing oligomers scarcely exist in the 3.5 M
electrolyte. Increasing the salt concentration, thus reducing the
number of unsolvated solvent molecules, may suppress the
dissolution (or solvation) of Li2Sn into the electrolyte, and
therefore mitigate the reaction of Li2Sn with the carbonate
solvents. For the cycled cathode surfaces, TOF-SIMS and XPS
analyses clarify that more carbonate solvent decomposition
products are detected on the 1.0 M cathode surface; in contrast,
the analyses reveal that more LiPF6 salt decomposition prod-
ucts, consisting mainly of insulative LiF, are found on the 3.5 M
cathode. Furthermore, XPS analysis suggests that the surface
lm on the 3.5 M cathode can be thicker. The decomposition
products formed on the cathode would not dissolve in the 3.5 M
electrolyte, instead remaining on its surface, thus forming the
thick cathode lm. This would, as a result, aﬀect the gradual
degradation of the 3.5 M cell. Finally, we believe that our results
will pave the way for the design and development of sulfur-
based secondary batteries.
Experimental
Cathode material synthesis
Lithium sulde (Li2S) powder (Mitsuwa Chemical, Japan) was
mixed thoroughly with iron sulde (FeS) powder (Alfa Aesar,
USA) at a 4 : 1 molar ratio, and the mixture was treated by
SPS21–23 using the SPS-3.20MK-IV apparatus (Fuji Electronic
Industrial, Japan) at 600 C for 5 min under an argon atmo-
sphere. The SPS-processed sample (Li8FeS5) was then blended
with carbon black powder, Ketjenblack (KB) (Lion Specialty
Chemicals, Japan), at a 9 : 1 weight ratio, and the mixture was
subjected to mechanical milling using a planetary mill (Pul-
verisette 5 classic line, Fritsch, Germany) for 300 h at a rotating
speed of 300 rpm to yield the cathode material (Li8FeS5/KB). The
XRD analysis conrmed that the obtained cathode material is
identical to that previously reported.23 Most procedures were
carried out in an argon-lled glove box since Li2S and Li8FeS5
are highly sensitive to atmospheric moisture.
Cell fabrication and electrochemical cycling test
The cathode was prepared by spreading a slurry consisting of
85 wt% Li8FeS5/KB cathode material, 5 wt% additional KB, and
10 wt% elastomeric binder (MP10, Asahi Kasei, Japan) onto
carbon-coated aluminum foil (SDX™, Showa Denko, Japan) and
then drying it at 80
C for 12 h under a vacuum. Super-
dehydrated heptane (Wako Pure Chemical, Japan) was used as
the slurry solvent. The Li8FeS5 content in the cathode was set to
76 wt%. Aer drying, the cathode was punched out to produce
a 15 mm disk, which was measured to have a loading of around
5.0 mg-Li8FeS5 cm2. This was treated in an electrochemical cell
assembly (HS Cell, Hohsen Corp., Japan). A lithium foil disk
(15 mm diameter, 0.2 mm thick) was used as the anode, and
a solution of 1.0 or 3.5 M LiPF6 in a 1 : 1 (v/v) mixture of EC and
DMC was used as the electrolyte. A microporous polyolen
sheet (Celgard® 2500, Celgard, USA) with 20 mm diameter was
used as the separator for the 1.0 M electrolyte; meanwhile, since
the 3.5 M electrolyte was not immersed well in the polyolen
separator, a ceramic-coated polyester sheet (NanoBaseX OZ-
GX25, Mitsubishi Paper Mills, Japan) with the same diameter
was employed. Either separator was placed facing the cathode
while a 16 mm 4 glass microber lter (Whatman™GF/A, GE
Healthcare, USA), which was also used as a separator, was
placed facing the anode. The amount of electrolyte used in the
cell was ca. 50 mL per gram of Li8FeS5. The electrochemical
cycling test was carried out galvanostatically between 2.6 and
1.0 V at a current density of 82.2 mA g1 (corresponding to C/12
for 10e/Li8FeS5) at 30
C using the Toscat-3100 charge–
discharge test system (Toyo System, Japan). The cell was initially
charged aer standing on open circuit for 8 h at 30 C.
ICP-MS and LC-MS analyses
To quantitatively analyze the electrolyte aer electrochemical
cycling, the Fe content was measured by ICP-MS (Agilent 8800,
Agilent Technologies, USA), while the S content was determined
by high-resolution ICP-MS (ELEMENT XR, Thermo Fisher
Scientic, USA). The cycled 1.0 and 3.5 M electrolyte samples
(10–70 mg) were digested with 5 mL of nitric acid in a Teon
microwave digestion vessel (100 mL). The cycled 3.5 M elec-
trolytes, having high viscosity, made sampling with a micropi-
pette diﬃcult. Thus, the 3.5 M electrolyte samples were diluted
with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) before the nitric acid diges-
tion. The dilution ratios with DME, determined by gas chro-
matography (GC), are shown in Table S3, ESI.† Aer tightly
capping the digestion vessel, the sample was decomposed by
microwave-assisted heating at ca. 200 C for 30 min. Aer
cooling, the digestate was diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water
to obtain the test solution.

TOF-SIMS analysis
Aer electrochemical cycling, the cathode was removed from
the cell and rinsed with DMC. The sample was then transferred
directly from an argon-lled glove box to the TOF.SIMS 5
preparation chamber (IONTOF GmbH, Germany) using a sealed
transfer vessel to prevent exposure to the atmosphere. For the
sample analysis, a 30 keV Bi3
2+ primary ion beam was used with
the gun being operated in bunched mode. The analyzed area
was set to 200  200 mm2 with a primary ion beam current of
0.05 pA (5.1 ns pulse width). All the analyses were conducted at
main analysis chamber pressures of between 4  106 and 4 
107 Pa. Peak assignments and their relative intensities were
obtained using the Surface Lab 6.6 soware (IONTOF GmbH,
Germany).
XPS conditions
Surface analysis was conducted with a PHI 5000 VersaProbe II
(ULVAC-PHI, Inc., Japan) using a monochromatic Al Ka X-ray
radiation source (hn ¼ 1486.6 eV). Before the analysis, each
cycled cathode sample was dipped into DMC to remove any
electrolyte residue. The sample was then transferred to the
analysis chamber (ca. 107 Pa) from the Ar-lled glove box using
a sealed transfer vessel to prevent any atmospheric contami-
nation. High-resolution spectra (Li1s, C1s, O1s, F1s, P2p, and
S2p) were recorded with an X-ray generator operating at 50 W
(15 kV, 4.7 mA) and a pass energy of 23.50 eV (for the Li1s, C1s,
O1s, and F1s spectra) or 46.95 eV (for the P2p and S2p spectra).
The analytical area was 200 mm in diameter. To compensate for
the charging of the sample surface, a charge neutralizer,
simultaneously irradiating low-energy electron and ion beams,
was used. Depth proling was carried out by ion beam sput-
tering using an Ar+ monomer ion beam with a beam voltage and
current of 1 kV and 7 mA, respectively. For the obtained spectra,
peak-tting was applied using the Gaussian–Lorentzian func-
tions with the Shirley background subtraction supported by the
MATLAB soware (The MathWorks, Inc., USA). The energy scale
was calibrated by setting the F1s lower binding energy side peak
to 685.0 eV (as the LiF peak).
