maintaining it over multiple charge/discharge cycles is critical 
to achieving reversible deposition of lithium.[18,19] It should be 
noted that the lithium–electrolyte interface is not a 2D layer 
between the lithium anode and the electrolyte, akin to graphite 
anodes, but instead adopts a 3D structure due to the similar 
nature of deposited lithium itself.

electrolyte interface play a critical role in determining the 
morphology of the deposited lithium. For instance, the ionic 
conductivity of the interfacial components is an important 
parameter as facile Li+-ion transport through the SEI layer 
would enable a uniform Li+-ion flux on the anode surface, 
thereby enabling homogenous deposition of lithium.[20–22]

Stabilizing the metallic lithium anode is the major roadblock to realizing 
long cycle life lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries with high energy density. The 
chemistry of the dynamically evolving solid-electrolyte interphase layer on 
the lithium surface is critical to achieving reversible plating and stripping of 
lithium. Herein, electrolyte formulations are carefully varied and employed 
to modulate the composition, and thereby, the properties of the lithium-
electrolyte interface. The impact of these changes in the interfacial chemistry 
on the dynamics of lithium deposition is evaluated by tracking cyclability in an 
anode-free full cell configuration. Critical insights are revealed on the role of 
different components of the electrolyte and their interplay, including various 
functional groups in the electrolyte salt, oxidizing effect of lithium nitrate, and 
the intrinsically stabilizing effect of polysulfide species, in determining the 
characteristics of lithium deposition and lithium cycling efficiency. The study 
illuminates the key elements underlying the unique chemistry of the lithium–
electrolyte interface in Li–S batteries and it can spur further development of 
novel strategies toward stabilizing the lithium-metal anode in the Li–S system.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries are an attractive replacement for 
the incumbent lithium-ion batteries in a wide range of applica-
tions.[1] Both the sulfur cathode and lithium-metal anode have 
large charge storage capacities, which translate to a battery 
system with a high energy density.[2,3] Moreover, sulfur can be 
cheaply sourced as a by-product in the oil-and-gas industry and 
is widely available without any supply chain issues. However, 
the practical relevance of Li–S batteries is inhibited by the inad-
equate cyclability of lithium-metal anode.[4,5] In energy-dense 
cell configurations that employ limited amounts of lithium 
or electrolyte, the poor reversibility of plating and stripping 
lithium leads to rapid capacity fade and an attenuated cycle 
life.[6,7] All-solid-state lithium batteries are an attractive option, 
but they require the development of an ionically conductive,

passivating, that is, avoiding reaction with other components 
of the electrolyte. Thus, the capability of the lithium–electrolyte 
interface to remain stable over extended cycling depends closely 
on the material properties of the different interfacial compo-
nents. Establishing these composition-property relationships 
for the interfacial layer is key to developing strategies toward 
stabilizing the lithium-metal anode.[25,26] Since the interface 
layer is composed of electrolyte decomposition products, the 
electrolyte offers a pathway toward achieving a stable lithium–
electrolyte interface with desirable properties.
The chemistry of lithium–electrolyte interface in Li–S bat-
teries is fundamentally unique due to i) the ether-based elec-
trolyte formulation and ii) the presence of soluble polysulfide 
intermediates. The electrolyte most typically employed in Li–S 
batteries is composed of lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) 
imide (LiTFSI) salt, lithium nitrate (LiNO3) additive, and 
1,3-dioxolane (DOL), and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) co-sol-
vents. In addition, polysulfide species (Li2Sn, 2 < n ≤ 8) with a 
dynamically varying composition and concentration in the elec-
trolyte get reduced on lithium surface to form solid Li2S and 
Li2S2 as interfacial components.[27–29] While most studies in the 
realm of Li–S batteries have focused on the sulfur cathode and 
mitigating polysulfide dissolution, lithium-metal anode and its 
distinct chemistry in this system have eluded similarly focused 
deliberations among researchers.
In this work, for the first time, we employ the anode-free full 
cell configuration to effectively investigate the chemistry of the 
lithium–electrolyte interface in Li–S batteries and its impact 
on lithium deposition. The anode-free full cell configuration, 
which contains no excess lithium, serves as an excellent tem-
plate for characterization of the lithium–electrolyte interface, 
as the deposited lithium is formed entirely in situ, and also 
enables accurate assessment of lithium cycling efficiencies. 
Different electrolyte formulations are employed to investi-
gate the composition-property relationships that underlie the 
critical role of various interfacial components in determining 
the dynamics of lithium deposition. In particular, the intricate 
cooperative interplay between different functional groups in the 
TFSI− anion, oxidizing nature of LiNO3, and ubiquitous poly-
sulfide intermediates is carefully delineated. This study sheds 
new light on the distinctive chemistry of interfacial components 
in the Li–S system and its correlation with the overall electro-
chemical performance. The novel insights generated may prove 
useful to developing approaches for stabilizing metallic anodes 
(Li, Na, K, Mg, etc.) when paired with sulfur cathodes.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Investigating Lithium Deposition with Anode-Free Full Cells
In this work, we study the role played by the lithium–electro-
lyte interface in determining the characteristics of lithium 
deposition in Li–S system, employing four different electrolyte 
formulations. The composition of electrolyte salts used in the 
electrolyte formulation is carefully varied to modulate the inter-
facial components formed, and the corresponding changes in 
lithium cycling efficiency are registered. All electrolytes employ 
a combination of DOL and DME in 1:1 volumetric ratio as the 
electrolyte solvent. The first electrolyte (E1) used in this study 
is the standard electrolyte for Li–S batteries—1 m LiTFSI + 
0.1 m LiNO3. The second electrolyte (E2) uses lithium trifluo-
romethanesulfonate (LiCF3SO3), also known as lithium triflate. 
Since this salt is similar in chemical structure to LiTFSI, but 
lacks the nitrogen atom, the role of the nitrogenated interfacial 
components formed by LiTFSI reduction should be revealed 
by comparing these two electrolytes. Thus, E2 contains 1 m 
LiCF3SO3 + 0.1 m LiNO3. The third electrolyte (E3) uses lithium 
bis-(fluorosulfonyl) imide (LiFSI). Its chemical structure is 
identical to LiTFSI, but with the CF3-groups replaced by a 
simple F-group. Comparing these two electrolytes should reveal 
the impact of this substitution on lithium deposition. In addi-
tion, LiFSI has been widely reported for its ability to stabilize 
various lithium-metal battery chemistries.[30] Thus, E3 contains 
1 m LiFSI + 0.1 m LiNO3. Finally, the fourth electrolyte (E4) used 
in this study simply contains 1 m LiTFSI without any LiNO3 
additive. The goal of comparing E1 and E4 is to shed new light 
on the role played by the nitrate additive, which is so ubiqui-
tous in Li–S batteries. The four electrolytes used in this study 
are outlined in Figure S1a, Supporting Information.
The electrochemical performance with these four electro-
lytes was initially evaluated with the Li || Li2S half-cell and 
the Ni || Li half-cell configuration (Figure S1b, Supporting 
Information). For Li || Li2S half-cell tests, Li2S cathodes with 
a loading of 4 mg cm−2 were paired with a thick lithium foil 
(0.75 mm) as the anode, yielding a lithium-to-sulfur capacity 
ratio (or N/P ratio) of 34. No significant differences in the 
measured discharge capacities or capacity retention trends 
are observed with the four different electrolytes in Li || Li2S 
half cells (Figure S1c, Supporting Information). For Ni || Li 
half-cell tests, bare nickel foils were used as the current col-
lector and 2.5 mAh cm−2 of Li was plated and stripped at 
0.5 mA cm−2. With the exception of E4 (no LiNO3), the other 
electrolytes exhibit similar values of Columbic efficiencies 
with cycling, even when 0.1 m Li2S6 was used as an electro-
lyte additive in an attempt to replicate the role of polysulfide 
species (Figure S1d,e, Supporting Information). The impor-
tant role of LiNO3 in regulating lithium deposition dynamics, 
both in the absence and presence of polysulfide species, is 
apparent from the Ni || Li half-cell tests. On the other hand, 
it would appear that the changes in interfacial chemistry with 
different electrolyte salts are inconsequential with respect to 
determining the characteristics of lithium deposition. How-
ever, both the Li || Li2S and the Ni || Li half-cell configurations 
are inadequate for accurately assessing lithium deposition in 
Li–S batteries. The large excess of lithium in Li || Li2S half 
cells compensates for any inefficiencies in lithium plating 
and stripping. The electrochemical performance of this cell 
configuration depends entirely on the cathode. Simultane-
ously, the speciation and relative distribution of intrinsically 
generated polysulfides in a realistic Li–S cell, which varies as 
a function of depth-of-discharge, sulfur content, electrolyte 
amount, etc., cannot be replicated by the extrinsically intro-
duced 0.1 m Li2S6 additive in a Ni || Li half-cell.[31,32] Hence, a 
new cell configuration is necessary to i) accurately replicate 
lithium deposition in the Li–S system, considering the role of 
active material crossover, and ii) to quantitatively assess the 
dynamics of lithium plating and stripping.

An alternate cell configuration to Li || Li2S and Ni || Li half 
cells is their hybrid, that is, the Ni || Li2S anode-free full cell. 
Pairing the same Li2S cathode from the previously studied half 
cell (4 mg cm−2 of Li2S) with a Ni foil on the anode side, the 
lithium stored in the Li2S cathode is the sole lithium source 
in the anode-free full cell (Figure 1a). There is no free lithium 
metal in the system, and the lithium-to-sulfur capacity ratio (or 
N/P ratio) of this cell configuration is exactly equal to 1. The 
anode-free cell configuration for Li–S batteries has been intro-
duced and discussed earlier.[33–36] Lithium from Li2S is plated 
onto the Ni foil during charge and stripped back out during 
discharge in a cycling protocol identical to that of the Li || Li2S 
half-cell. Using the anode-free full cell for investigating the 
dynamics of lithium deposition in the Li–S system brings about 
two critical advantages: i) since there is no excess lithium, its 
electrochemical performance depends entirely on lithium 
cycling efficiencies, and ii) since polysulfide species in the elec-
trolyte originate with the charging and discharging of the Li2S 
cathode, it is an accurate replication of polysulfides in a realistic 
Li–S battery.
Figure  1b shows the electrochemical performance of the 
anode-free Ni || Li2S full cells at C/10 rate. Unlike the half 
cells, a clear difference can be observed between each of the 
four different electrolytes. While they all show similar initial 
capacities, the capacity retention trends are very different. As 
shown in Figure S1c, Supporting Information, the different 
electrolytes have negligible impact on the electrochemistry of 
the Li2S cathode. Hence, relative trends in the electrochemical 
performance of anode-free full cells can be attributed entirely 
to the variations in lithium cycling efficiencies with the dif-
ferent electrolytes. The corresponding Columbic efficiencies are 
shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information. E3, which is the 
electrolyte with LiFSI salt, shows the worst cyclability, with 50% 
of the initial capacity lost within 13 cycles. Thus, lithium depo-
sition is unstable in Li–S batteries with LiFSI as the electrolyte 
salt, a result that could not have been anticipated based on the 
half-cell cycling results alone, as discussed earlier. In contrast, 
E1, which is the standard electrolyte with LiTFSI salt, shows 
the best cyclability, with 50% of the initial capacity retained 
for 125 cycles. In the absence of LiNO3 additive, the E4 elec-
trolyte shows poor cyclability, with 50% of the initial capacity 
lost within 25 cycles. This underscores the critical role of LiNO3 
in maintaining stable lithium deposition in Li–S batteries. E2, 
with LiCF3SO3 salt, shows similar capacities as E1 for the first 
≈50 cycles. Subsequently, the capacity retention trends diverge, 
and E2 loses 50% of its initial capacity within 77 cycles. The role 
played by interfacial components in engendering these differ-
ences in lithium cycling efficiencies will be explored in the rest 
of the paper. Therefore, it can be concluded that the anode-free 
full cell configuration generates unique and useful information 
on the dynamics of lithium deposition in Li–S batteries.
2.2. Role of CF3- and F-Functional Groups in LiTFSI/LiFSI  
Electrolyte Salts
In order to investigate the relationship between the lithium–
electrolyte interface and the cyclability of lithium metal in the 
Li–S system, the deposited lithium in the anode-free Ni || Li2S 
full cells was characterized by time-of-flight secondary ion 
mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), X-ray photoelectron spectros-
copy (XPS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Since 
the deposited lithium in anode-free full cells is entirely formed 
in situ during cycling, it forms an excellent template for char-
acterizing the lithium–electrolyte interface. This is due to the 
absence of the surface passivation film formed by exposure of 
lithium metal to trace O2, H2O, N2, and CO2, which prevents 
the characterization results from getting skewed by these spe-
cies, as well as the absence of excess metallic lithium.[37,38]

for LiFSI compared to LiTSFI. For instance, the signal for 
C2H3O− reaches 50% of peak intensity after 160 s of sputtering

electrolyte decomposition products not only consumes active 
lithium, but since these partially reduced interfacial compo-
nents are ionically insulating, such a thick and resistive surface 
layer also blocks Li+ transport to and from the metallic lithium 
underneath. This leads to the electrochemical inactivation of 
the lithium inventory and subsequently, severe capacity fade 
is observed in a lithium-limited system with LiFSI. In con-

allows the lithium inventory to remain electrochemically active 
for a longer number of cycles. It should be noted that the total 
amount of metallic lithium in the sampled volume remains 
comparable between LiTFSI and LiFSI, even after accounting 
for normalization, as seen in Figure S5, Supporting Informa-
tion. Thus, cell failure in a lithium-limited system can be attrib-
uted to metallic lithium becoming electrochemically inacces-
sible as opposed to lithium being consumed in parasitic side 
reactions with the electrolyte.[39]

The instability of the LiFSI salt is due to the lability of the ter-
minal SF bond, which is easily broken in nucleophilic reac-
tions with polysulfides to form insoluble products with SSn 
linkages.[40] This leads to the electrolyte salt disintegrating into 
various interfacial components on the lithium surface. In con-
trast, the stable SC bond in LiTFSI helps mitigate electrolyte 
salt decomposition in the presence of polysulfides. Differences 
in the relative stabilities of the SN bond in LiFSI and LiTFSI 
have also been reported.[41] The S 2p spectra for the lithium 
surface (Figure S6, Supporting Information) shows signifi-
cantly higher signal intensity for terminal sulfur (LiSS) and 
bridging sulfur (SSS) atoms with LiFSI compared to that 
with LiTFSI. This provides evidence for the formation of reac-
tion products of LiFSI and polysulfides containing S-Sn link-
ages. The formation of a thick and resistive electrolyte decom-
position layer with LiFSI not only renders the metallic lithium 
electrochemically inactive, but also engenders a non-uniform 
Li+-ion flux on the lithium surface. The consequent high-sur-
face area mossy growth of lithium leads to further side reac-
tions with the electrolyte. While individual differences between 
LiF and CF3-containing interfacial components may not be 
responsible for the divergence in electrochemical performance 
between LiFSI and LiTFSI, the variations in chemical proper-
ties of the salts are significant enough to affect their stability. 
The consequent magnitude of electrolyte decomposition and 
the thickness of the decomposition layer has a strong impact 
on lithium cycling efficiencies. Hence, electrolyte salts of rea-
sonable stability in the polysulfide-rich environment must be 
chosen to ensure stable operation of the lithium-metal anode.
2.3. Role of N Atom in LiTFSI Delineated by Comparing  
with LiCF3SO3
At 40 cycles, anode-free Ni || Li2S full cells using LiTFSI and 
LiCF3SO3 based electrolytes show very similar capacities 
(Figure  1b), with 72% of the initial capacity retained in both 
cases. Their capacity retention trends start to diverge between 
50 and 60 cycles. The origins of this divergence in the composi-
tion of the lithium–electrolyte interface cannot be determined 
straightforwardly. Figure S7, Supporting Information com-
pares the morphology of the deposited lithium with LiTFSI and 
LiCF3SO3 using SEM images. Significant differences in mor-
phology cannot be discerned and relatively homogenous growth 
of lithium can be observed in both cases. Figure S8, Supporting 
Information shows the depth profiles obtained with ToF-SIMS 
for Li2−, SO−, C2H3O−, and LiF2− secondary ions, normalized to 
peak intensity. The electrolyte decomposition layers are com-
parably thin in both cases, unlike the thick layers found with 
LiFSI. Thus, the capacity fade observed with LiCF3SO3 cannot 
be attributed to a thick electrolyte decomposition layer blocking 
access to metallic lithium underneath.
The difference in lithium cycling efficiencies between LiTFSI 
and LiCF3SO3 is likely engendered by the nitrogenated inter-
facial components formed due to the presence of the nitrogen 
atom in LiTFSI. Figure  3a shows the depth profiles obtained 
with ToF-SIMS for two N-containing secondary ions with a size-
able sputter yield and ionization probability: CN− and NH−. As 
expected, it can be seen that the signal intensity for CN− and 
NH− is significantly higher with LiTFSI than LiCF3SO3. While 
secondary ions typically originate as an intact component of the 
source molecule, the recombination of C and N atoms from 
separate sources has also been reported to yield CN−.[42] Thus, 
CN− can be taken as a general proxy for all N-species. Figure 3b 
shows the N 1s and S 2p spectra for the lithium surface obtained 
with XPS. It can be seen that with LiCF3SO3, the nitrogenated 
interfacial components are primarily species containing NOx 
groups (≈407.3 eV), while the peak at ≈399.5 eV indicates organic 
nitrogen species, such as amines (CNH2).[43] The origin of 
these species is the LiNO3 additive. In contrast with LiTFSI, 
there is an additional peak at ≈397.3 eV, which corresponds to 
Li3N from the complete reduction of the electrolyte salt. Thus, 
the additional N atom in LiTFSI enriches the lithium–electro-
lyte interface with Li3N, which is known to be a good Li+-ion 
conductor.[44] Furthermore, ToF-SIMS indicates the presence 
of Li3N throughout the bulk of the deposited lithium. The S 
2p spectra also indicate the presence of an additional peak for 
a higher-order sulfate (≈169.9  eV) with LiCF3SO3, presumably 
due to the extra oxygen atom in the triflate salt. These oxidized 
sulfur species might also play a role as interfacial components.
Clearly, the electrolyte salt is the key to modulating the 
chemistry of the lithium–electrolyte interface and the associ-
ated kinetics of Li+ ion transport. In addition, desolvation of 
Li+ ions from their solvation shells is an important component 
of charge transfer. For the electrolyte salts considered here, at 
dilute concentrations (1 m) and room temperature, the solva-
tion shell is typically dominated by solvent molecules, which 
is known to cause high desolvation energy.[45–48] One strategy 
for improving desolvation kinetics is employing electrolyte 
salts with high ionic association energy, such as lithium iodide 
(LiI), LiBr, and LiCF3CO2.[49–51] This can help disrupt the sol-
vation structure of Li+ ions by introducing strongly binding 
salt anions, reducing the influence of electrolyte solvents.[52,53] 
It can also promote the formation of an inorganics-rich SEI 
layer, which is favorable for lithium deposition. This can also be 
achieved with high concentration electrolytes.[54] Therefore, the 
ionic association energy is an important design parameter in 
choosing appropriate electrolyte salts for Li–S batteries.

large amount of excess lithium (and electrolyte) in the half cell

Hence, it can be surmised that the primary role of LiNO3 is 
ensuring stable operation of the lithium-metal anode. Further-
more, comparing the Columbic efficiencies of plating and strip-
ping lithium with E1 and E4 in Ni || Li half cells shows that 
the presence of LiNO3 is critical to realizing reversible lithium 
deposition. This is applicable both without and with polysulfide 
additives (Figure S1d,e, Supporting Information). Indeed, 
LiNO3 has been employed as an electrolyte additive in systems 
other than Li–S batteries. This may suggest that the stabilizing 
effects of LiNO3 are realized independent of the presence of 
polysulfide species. However, there also exists the possibility of 
chemical reactions between polysulfides, LiNO3, or their deriva-
tives giving rise to a unique stabilization mechanism. For an 
accurate understanding of the stabilizing effects of LiNO3 in 
the Li–S system with its intrinsically generated polysulfides, 
the electrochemistry of anode-free Ni || Li2S full cells and char-
acterization of its deposited lithium is used as the evaluation 
template.

62%. This divergence in lithium cycling efficiencies is reflected 
in the morphology of the deposited lithium (Figure S9, Sup-

is covered with inhomogeneous and mossy lithium deposits.

2.5. Oxidizing Nature of LiNO3 in Modulating Interfacial Chemistry
It may be tempting to assign the origin of the oxidized sulfur 
species (SOx) that dominate the S 2p spectra in the presence 
of LiNO3 to the decomposition products of the LiTFSI electro-
lyte salt. However, LiNO3 is also a strong oxidizing agent, and 
the oxidizing nature of nitrate anions is well known. Certain 
chemolithotroph microorganisms are known to oxidize sulfides 
as an energy source, while utilizing nitrates as the electron 
acceptor under anaerobic conditions.[63] Similarly, it is possible 
that LiNO3 might oxidize the reduced sulfur species, thereby 
reducing their concentration on the lithium surface and 
forming oxidized sulfur species.
A challenge with deconvoluting these mechanisms is the dif-
ficulty in distinguishing between oxidized sulfur species from 
LiTFSI decomposition and those from oxidation of reduced 
sulfur species by LiNO3. To remove the interference from sul-
fonyl groups and nitrogen atom in the LiTFSI molecule, LiI 
was used as the electrolyte salt for characterization purposes. In 
this case, 1 m LiI + 0.1 m LiNO3 in DOL/DME was employed as 
the electrolyte in anode-free Ni || Li2S full cells. In analyzing the 
deposited lithium in such a system, the sole source of sulfur 
and nitrogen atoms in the interfacial layer is the Li2S cathode 
and the LiNO3 additive, respectively. Since LiI is already in its 
most reduced state, it cannot be decomposed further on lithium 
surface, thereby rendering the interfacial layer free of any elec-
trolyte salt decomposition products.
For obtaining faster results, the cells were cycled at C/5 rate 
(≈1 mA cm−2) instead of the standard C/10 rate throughout the 
paper. Capacity fade is more severe in this case due to the faster 
rate of lithium degradation at higher current rates. Figure  5a 
shows the electrochemical performance of the anode-free Ni || 
Li2S full cell with the electrolyte containing LiI. Intriguingly, the 
capacity retention curves do not change significantly between 
LiI and LiTFSI, although the CE values are conspicuously 
lower with LiI. 60% of the peak capacity is retained at 40 cycles 
with LiI, compared to 53% retention with LiTFSI. It should be 
noted that LiI is expected to be electrochemically inactive in 
the operating voltage range of Li–S batteries (1.8–2.8  V).[64,65] 
This is further confirmed in Figure S10, Supporting Informa-
tion, which shows the charge–discharge profiles of a Ni || Li2S 
full cell employing LiI as the electrolyte salt. The standard two-
plateau voltage curves are retained and no extraneous capacity 
corresponding to LiI is observed. The promising performance 
with LiI as an electrolyte salt merits further discussion, given 
that it enables stable operation of both anode and cathode in 
the full cell and is lower in both cost and molar weight com-
pared to LiTFSI.[49] In the interest of a fair treatment, however, 
it is deferred to a future work.

The oxidizing nature of LiNO3 has a profound impact on its 
role in determining the chemistry of the lithium–electrolyte 
interface in Li–S batteries. The sulfides on lithium surface, 
Li2S and Li2S2, exist in a dynamic equilibrium with the poly-
sulfide intermediates in the electrolyte. However, this equilib-
rium is constantly shifting during charge and discharge as new 
polysulfides are introduced into or removed from the electro-
lyte. Hence, the reduced sulfur species do not form a stable 
lithium–electrolyte interface as they are likely to be continu-
ously re-dissolved into and re-precipitated out of the solution. 
In the presence of LiNO3, however, these sulfides are oxidized 
to Li2SO3 and Li2SO4, which are no longer soluble in the elec-
trolyte or reactive with polysulfides. They form a more stable 
component of the lithium–electrolyte interface, thereby ena-
bling the reversible deposition of lithium in the Li–S system. 
A schematic illustration of this mechanism is shown in 
Figure 5d. The sulfide reduction of LiNO3 yields organic nitro-
genated components in the lithium–electrolyte interface, which 
may also contribute to the improvement in lithium cycling 
efficiencies. These nitrogen sites might help engender a uni-
form Li+-ion flux on the anode surface due to their lithiophilic 
nature.[66] These species are further reduced to Li3N in the bulk 
of the deposited lithium due to facile access to metallic lithium 
and electron transport pathways, as indicated by the depth pro-
files for CN− in Figure 4a. Li3N is well known for its stabilizing 
role as an interfacial component. The formation of LixNOy at 
the lithium surface and its reduction to Li3N in the bulk of the 
deposited lithium might also explain why the stabilizing effects 
of LiNO3 are partially realized in the absence of polysulfide 
species.
These results indicate that the oxidizing nature of LiNO3 is 
integral to enabling its stabilizing role in Li–S batteries. This 
suggests certain design principles for potential replacements 
for LiNO3. The limitations of LiNO3 include its continuous 
consumption in reduction reactions on lithium surface without 
replenishment,[67] its decomposition below 1.8  V which limits 
the cut-off voltages,[68] and evolution of N2 and N2O gaseous 
products. A replacement for LiNO3 should address these 
limitations while being a strong oxidizer that helps eliminate 
unstable Li2S/Li2S2 on the lithium surface. The oxidizer should 
also be appropriately chosen such that stabilizing interfacial 
components are formed in its reduction reaction. The organic 
nitrogenated species and Li3N can be introduced into the inter-
face with a separate additive.
2.6. Role of Polysulfides in Stabilizing Lithium Deposition  
in Li–S System
Of all the electrolyte components described so far, lithium 
polysulfides play the most impactful role in rendering the 
characteristics of lithium deposition in Li–S batteries, so fun-
damentally unique compared to other systems. As discussed 
earlier, polysulfide species reduce on lithium surface to form 
Li2S/Li2S2, which are then oxidized to Li2SO3/Li2SO4 by the 
action of LiNO3. The consequent improvement in lithium 
cycling efficiencies might imply that the reduced sulfur spe-
cies are less desirable compared to oxidized sulfur species 
for stabilization of lithium deposition. With a sulfide anionic 
framework, however, Li2S/Li2S2 has intrinsically higher Li+-ion 
conductivity compared to Li2SO3/Li2SO4 with an oxide anionic 
framework.[69–71] Ionically conductive SEI components enable a 
uniform Li+-ion flux on the lithium surface, thereby enabling 
reversible plating and stripping of lithium.[20] However, due to 
the continuous dissolution of the reduced sulfur species upon 
reacting with polysulfides, their favorable properties as interfa-
cial components are not realized. It becomes more beneficial to

electrolyte salts, the thicker electrolyte decomposition layer 
blocks Li+-ion access to the trapped metallic lithium under-

of polysulfides indicates that lithium deposition is intrinsically 
stabilized in the Li–S system. It is further demonstrated by 
ToF-SIMS depth profiles for deposited lithium in a Ni || Li2S 
full cell with LiNO3-free electrolyte after 40 cycles. As shown 
in Figure S12, Supporting Information, electrolyte decomposi-
tion was restricted to a very thin layer on the deposited lithium. 
Therefore, even in the absence of LiNO3, the presence of poly-
sulfides mitigates severe electrolyte degradation. The stabilizing 
effect of polysulfides is also alluded to by the electrochemical 
performance of the anode-free Ni || Li2S full cell (Figure  1b) 
compared to anode-free systems reported with other cathode 
systems. Even with an unoptimized electrolyte, relatively high 
capacity (2.5 mAh cm−2), and a reasonable current density 
(0.5 mA cm−2), the Li2S-based system demonstrates a cycle life 
exceeding 125 cycles for 50% capacity retention. Most anode-
free full cells reported in the literature fail almost entirely 
within 50 cycles with unoptimized electrolytes.[30,34] Thus, the 
in situ generated polysulfides in the Li–S system partially sta-
bilize lithium deposition and enable relatively high efficiencies 
of lithium plating and stripping, at least when compared to sys-
tems with no polysulfides.

However, the reduced sulfur species by themselves are not 
sufficient to enable long-term stability of lithium deposition 
due to their tendency to dissolve back into the electrolyte upon 
reacting with higher order polysulfides. The oxidizing nature 
of LiNO3 helps resolve this shortcoming by converting the 
reduced sulfur species into oxidized sulfur species, thereby 
forming a more stable lithium–electrolyte interface. This 
cooperative effect between polysulfides and LiNO3 lies at the 
crux of determining the dynamics of lithium deposition in 
Li–S batteries. Nevertheless, Li2S/Li2S2 are still present in the 
lithium–electrolyte interface at advanced stages of cycling, 
albeit as a minor component, and contribute to the con-
tinued stability of lithium deposition. In addition, the organic 
nitrogenated species formed by the reduction of LiNO3 likely 
also play a beneficial role in helping achieve high lithium 
cycling efficiencies. The role of SO32−/SO42− formed by oxi-
dation by LiNO3 is less clear. While it does enable a more 
stable lithium–electrolyte interface, it is also expected to have 
lower values of intrinsic Li+-ion conductivity due to the oxide 
anionic framework. It may even have a detrimental impact 
on the long-term cyclability of the lithium-metal anode in 
Li–S batteries. Deconvoluting the exact role played by these 
oxidized sulfur species will need further carefully designed 
experiments.
The chemistry of polysulfides and LiNO3 also suggests an 
alternative strategy for realizing reversible plating and strip-
ping of lithium in Li–S batteries. Instead of the in situ formed 
binary sulfides Li2S/Li2S2, forming LiXS ternary sulfides as 
SEI components can improve the intrinsic Li+-ion conductivity 
of the interfacial layer. Here X is a cation of an element less 
electronegative than sulfur, such as phosphorus and tellurium. 
LiXS ternary sulfides also circumvent the main disadvantage 
of Li2S/Li2S2, that is, their solubility in the electrolyte, since 
LiXS ternary sulfides are much less reactive with polysulfides 
and hence form a more stable interfacial layer. The potential 
deleterious effects of forming SO32−/SO42− are also avoided 
with this approach. We have demonstrated this strategy using 
Li2TeS3 as an ionically conductive and passivating compo-
nent of the lithium–electrolyte interface, which helps realize 
long cycle life in energy dense cell configurations under lim-
ited lithium and lean electrolyte conditions.[35] Similarly, the 
use of Li3PS4 as a stabilizing lithium SEI layer has also been 
demonstrated.[20,75]
3. Conclusion
Achieving high efficiencies of lithium plating and stripping is 
necessary to realizing long cycle life Li–S batteries with high 
energy density. This requires developing a thorough under-
standing of the fundamentally unique chemistry of the lithium–
electrolyte interface and its impact on the dynamics of lithium 
deposition in Li–S batteries. In this work, we used the anode-
free full cell configuration, which pairs a Li2S cathode with a 
bare Ni foil current collector, and showed that it i) is necessary 
to effectively evaluate lithium cycling efficiencies and ii) forms 
an ideal template for characterization of the deposited lithium. 
This was achieved by employing slightly varying electrolyte salts 
to modulate the chemistry of the interfacial layer. We undertook 
an in-depth analysis of the effect of LiNO3 additive on interfa-
cial chemistry and showed that it converts the reduced sulfur 
species (Li2S/Li2S2) into oxidized sulfur species (SO32−/SO42−), 
which passivate the lithium surface. The nitrogen-containing 
interfacial components generated by LiNO3 also play an impor-
tant role in enabling stable cycling of the lithium anode. Finally, 
we show that polysulfide species engender an intrinsic stabili-
zation of lithium deposition and play a key role in mitigating 
severe electrolyte decomposition during cell operation.
There is a need for fundamentally unique strategies for sta-
bilizing lithium deposition in Li–S batteries. We believe the 
insights generated in this paper will catalyze efforts toward 
that goal. In particular, the interplay of LiNO3 and polysulfides 
will aid the development of novel lithium-stabilizing electrolyte 
additives. Such efforts will facilitate the realization of commer-
cially viable Li–S batteries with high energy density and long 
cycle life.
