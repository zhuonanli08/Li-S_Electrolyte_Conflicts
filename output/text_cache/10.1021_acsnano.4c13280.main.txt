Electrolytes for Wide-Temperature

ABSTRACT: The electrolyte plays an essential role in the advancement of lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs), as it not
only transports the charge carriers but also extensively influences sulfur conversion mechanisms and electrode−
electrolyte interphases formed on the electrode surface, thereby directly impacting battery performance. However,
the majority of existing electrolytes suffer from incompatibility with either the Li anode or the sulfur cathode. Here,
we develop a densely packed ion-cluster electrolyte (DPIE) through the strategic combination of a weakly solvating
solvent and an inert diluent, resulting in the self-assembly of abundant compact ion-pair aggregates within its
structure. This peculiar solvation structure promotes fast Li+ desolvation, the formation of robust electrode−
electrolyte interphases, and the suppression of polysulfide dissolution. Leveraging the tailored DPIE, room-
temperature Li||sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) batteries demonstrate 300 stable cycles with a capacity retention
of 97.8% and a steady Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.9%. Even under a limited negative/positive areal capacity
ratio of four, the Li||SPAN cells exhibit good stability over 250 cycles with 97.1% capacity retention. Furthermore,
Li||SPAN batteries show impressive stability over a wide temperature range spanning from −20 to 60 °C and exhibit
reversibility at −10 °C over 200 cycles. This electrolyte design enables LSBs with prolonged operational lifetimes,
rapid charging capabilities, and expanded temperature tolerance.
KEYWORDS: lithium−sulfur batteries, densely packed ion-cluster electrolyte, weakly solvating solvent, polysulfide shuttling,
wide temperature range
Rechargeable lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) are a promising
technology for next-generation energy storage due to their high
theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and the abundance
and low cost of sulfur.1−6 However, their long-term cyclability
is hindered by the dissolution and shuttling of lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs), leading to active material loss and
anode degradation. To address this, significant efforts have
focused on developing advanced sulfur cathodes.7−10 Among
them, sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) stands out due to its
high electronic conductivity, scalability, and cost-effective-
ness.11 SPAN electrodes demonstrate excellent cyclic stability
and nearly 100% Coulombic efficiency (CE) in carbonate
electrolytes, enabled by a reversible “solid−solid” conversion
mechanism that prevents LiPS dissolution.12 However, the
incompatibility of carbonate electrolytes with Li metal poses
challenges for the cycle life of Li−SPAN full cells.13,14 While
glyme-based ether electrolytes are more compatible with Li
metal, continuous LiPS dissolution occurs during SPAN

Although various strategies, liquid electrolyte engineering
has proven effective in enhancing the lifespan of Li−SPAN
batteries by stabilizing both the SPAN cathode and the Li
anode while maintaining compatibility with current battery
production lines.18−22 High-concentration electrolytes
(HCEs), typically with salt concentrations above 4 M, generate
stable electrode−electrolyte interfaces and inhibit LiPS
dissolution due to their high viscosity and reduced free
solvents, thus improving the cyclic stability of LSBs.17,20,23
However, the high viscosity, poor wettability, and sluggish Li+
transport in HCEs limit their practical application.24−26
Localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) address
these issues by diluting HCEs with nonsolvating cosolvents
and retaining the favorable solvation structures of HCEs but at
much lower salt concentrations, thereby reducing viscosity and
improving wettability while maintaining compatibility with Li
metal.25,27 However, most LHCEs employ strongly solvating
solvents like 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) to dissociate Li salt,
requiring high salt-to-solvent or diluent-to-solvent ratios to
achieve the desired anion-rich solvation structures, which
inevitably increases electrolyte cost.25,28−30 Additionally, these
strongly solvating solvents negatively impact the low-temper-
ature performance of LSBs due to the strong interaction
between Li+ and solvent molecules and bringing potential
polysulfide dissolution.19 In this regard, designing advanced
LHCEs with low salt concentrations (e.g., 1 M) and low
diluent-to-solvent ratios is highly desired to suppress
polysulfide shuttling, accelerate Li+ desolvation, and maintain
excellent Li compatibility.
In this study, we propose a densely packed ion-cluster
electrolyte (DPIE) that enables the formation of robust
electrode−electrolyte interphases for highly reversible Li||
SPAN batteries (Figure 1a). The DPIE formulation,
comprising 1 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in
dibutyl ether (DBE)/1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluor-
opropyl ether (TTE) (1:1, v/v), facilitates the self-assembly of
ion-pair aggregates within the electrolyte bulk, leading to fast
Li+ desolvation, the establishment of robust electrolyte-
electrode interphases and the inhibition of polysulfide
dissolution (Figure 1b). For comparison, 1 M LiFSI in DME
and 1 M LiFSI DME/TTE (1:1, v/v) electrolytes were
prepared and denoted as DE and LHCE, respectively. Notably,
the monodentate DBE exhibits lower coordination affinity with
Li+ due to its single ether oxygen and extended alkyl chain, as
shown in Figure 1c,18 while maintaining similar highest
occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels (Figure 1d). This
characteristic makes DBE a promising solvent candidate for Li
metal anode. Moreover, the weakly solvating nature of DBE
inherently restrains polysulfide dissolution during charge−
discharge processes and enables fast Li+ desolvation at lower
temperatures. As a result, the Li plating/stripping processes

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Physical Properties and Solvation Structures. The
properties of selected solvents, including DME, DBE, and
TTE, are studied to understand their fundamental character-
istics. Figure 1c shows that the solvating capabilities of DME,
DBE, and TTE were calculated using density functional theory
(DFT). The electrostatic potential (ESP) of DME, DBE, and
TTE shows that the oxygen atom in DME has the strongest
electron negativity compared to that in DBE and TTE,
indicating an inferior solvating capability of DBE. Notably, the
electron negativity of the oxygen atom in TTE is significantly
reduced due to the strong electron-withdrawing effect of the
−CF3 groups. Therefore, the binding strength of the Li+-
solvent complexes on the O site follows the order of DME-Li+
> DBE-Li+ > TTE-Li+, with corresponding binding energies
(Eb) of −2.84 eV, −2.10 eV, and −1.75 eV, respectively
(Figure S1). Therefore, when TTE is mixed with DBE or
DME, it is unlikely to participate in the first solvation shell of
Li+ cations, acting as a diluent.30 We also found that with the
addition of TTE, the solubility for the LiFSI salt decreases.
Only 1 M LiFSI salt can be completely dissolved in a DBE/
TTE mixture (1:1, v/v) (Figure S2). Notably, both the LUMO
and HOMO energy levels of DBE are close to those of DME,
suggesting that DBE is highly compatible with Li metal (Figure
1d). The LUMO energy of TTE (−0.33 eV) is slightly lower
than that of DBE (−0.22 eV), implying that TTE preferentially
reduces and participates in the formation of SEI layers on the
Li anode. Additionally, TTE exhibits superior antioxidant
stability compared to DBE, as indicated by its lower HOMO

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to
investigate electrolytes’ solvation. The snapshots show that Li+
ions are dominantly coordinated by DME solvent and FSI−
anions are uniformly distributed in conventional DE and
LHCE (Figure 2a,b). By contrast, the Li+-FSI−/DBE
complexes in DPIE form large aggregates and are surrounded
by TTE molecules to form a densely packed solvation structure
(Figure 2c). Furthermore, the radial distribution function
(RDF) and coordination number (CN) of oxygen were
simulated to identify the local environment around Li+ in the
three electrolyte systems (Figure 2d−f). A characteristic SSIP
structure is observed in DE, where Li+ ions are primarily
coordinated by DME molecules, with an average CN of 5.20
ODME and 0.24 OFSI
−per Li. Notably, the most dominant
solvation configuration, i.e., Li+(DME)3, accounts for 79.0% in
DE (Figure 2g). By incorporation of TTE as a diluent, a small
portion of FSI−anions enters the primary solvation sheath, in
which each Li+ is coordinated with an average of 4.92 ODME
and 0.41 OFSI
−in LHCE, and the percentage of most probable
solvation structure, i.e., Li+-3DME, reduces to 63.3% (Figure
2h). It is noteworthy that each Li+ is coordinated with an
average of 2.55 ODBE and 1.76 OFSI

coordinated by at least one FSI−in DPIE (Figure 2f,i). Such a
distinct solvation structure is attributed to the weakly solvating
power of DBE molecules, which will generate a highly robust
and conductive inorganic-rich SEI, as will be discussed later.
Notably, the coordination between Li+ with OTTE is negligible
in both systems, in line with its nonsolvating characteristic. In
addition, we also distinguished the portion of SSIPs, contact
ion pairs (CIPs), and aggregates (AGGs) in different
electrolytes (Figure 2j). The DE and LHCE are dominated
by SSIPs, while AGGs account for ∼72.9% in DPIE.
The formation of large ion-pair aggregates in DPIE was
further validated using dynamic light scattering and the Tindall
effect (Figure 2k). A particle size of 2.32 nm was observed for
ion clusters in DPIE. Note that such a phenomenon was not
observed in the LHCE (Figure S4). Importantly, replacing the
DBE solvent with other weak solvating solvents, such as
dimethoxymethane, can also form a similar aggregated
solvation structure (Figure S5). Such a solvation structure is
believed to promote Li+ desolvation and accelerate the
decomposition of anions to form robust electrode−electrolyte
interfaces.18 Raman spectroscopy shows that the S−N−S
bending peak of LiFSI salt shifted to ∼722 cm−1 when
dissolved in DME, implying the weak interaction of Li+ and
FSI−in DE (Figure 2l).16 With the addition of TTE, the
above-mentioned peak slightly upshifted to ∼727 cm−1 in
LHCE. Notice that the characteristic peak of FSI−was shifted
to ∼751 cm−1 in DPIE, indicating the presence of less free
FSI−, in line with the MD analysis.19
Figure S6 shows the ionic conductivity of various electro-
lytes as a function of temperature. DPIE exhibits ionic
conductivities of 0.2 mS cm−1 and 0.075 mS cm−1 at 30 and
−20 °C, respectively. The mediocre ionic conductivities in
DPIE are associated with the extremely low dielectric constant
of DBE (ϵ = 3.06), thus suppressing the complete dissociation
of LiFSI.18,20 Although the DPIE has shown lower ionic
conductivity than the LHCE and DE, its fast desolvation
kinetics enable better battery performance at low temperatures
and fast charging/discharging rates, as will be described later.

In addition to the above characteristics, an ideal electrolyte
for LSBs must prevent the dissolution of LiPSs. As shown in
Figure S10a, the DME solution rapidly changes color to
yellowish after adding Li2S and S powders without stirring.
After 12 h, the color turns to reddish brown, indicating the
gradual dissolution of LiPSs in DME. By contrast, both DBE
and TTE exhibit colorless and transparent supernatants with
sediments containing Li2S and S. The distinct differences can
be correlated to the strongest solvating capabilities of DME
among the three. Weakly and nonsolvating solvents, such as
DBE and TTE, are more likely to inhibit LiPS dissolution,
eliminating the loss of active species and their crosstalk with
the Li anode. Notably, the introduction of TTE in DME can
only delay but not inhibit the dissolution of LiPSs, as
evidenced by the color change of the DME/TTE solution
(Figure S10b). With these advantages, it is expected that DPIE
holds significant potential for addressing the persistent issues
of LiPS shuttling and unstable Li deposition/dissolution in
LSBs.

The deposition morphology of Li on Cu foil was examined
by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in
Figure 3b, the carbonate electrolyte leads to ramified and
rough Li deposition with abundant loose and needle-like Li
filaments, and the deposited Li layer has a large number of
voids with an average thickness of ∼20.1 μm. Such a porous
morphology will trigger severe interfacial reactions, leading to
significant Li loss and low CEs.16,24 The morphologies of
deposited Li in DE and LHCE are relatively dense; however,
mossy Li and voids can still be observed (Figure 3c,d). Note
that, uniform and compact Li deposition is achieved in DPIE
(Figure 3e) with the thinnest thickness of ∼13.1 μm. The
dense Li deposition achieved in DPIE can minimize side
reactions with the electrolyte, prolonging the lifetime of LSBs.
Symmetric cells were also assembled to validate the excellent
compatibility between Li and the DPIE. The Li||Li cells with
DPIE exhibit good stability, with no short circuits for 800 h at
0.5 mA cm−2/1 mAh cm−2 (Figure S12a). These symmetric
cells show stable and reversible Li plating/stripping behavior,

Self-Discharge Behavior and Cathode Electrolyte
Interphases (CEIs). The self-discharge phenomenon, com-
monly observed in LSBs, was systematically investigated in
various electrolytes. SPAN with a sulfur content of
approximately 39.8% was used as the cathode material
(Table S1). The Li||SPAN cells discharged to 1.7 V at the
sixth cycle were stored for 72 h and then returned to normal
galvanostatic charge−discharge processes (Figure S15). A low
CE of ∼70.8% at the sixth cycle is obtained in the DE,
indicating the presence of severe LiPS dissolution (Figure 4a).
In addition, the reversible capacity at the seventh cycle is only
550 mAh gs
−1. For comparison, LiPS dissolution is largely
mitigated in the LHCE, as evidenced by an improved sixth CE
of 95.6% (Figure 4b). A high CE of ∼100% is achieved in
DPIE, with almost identical discharge profiles, indicating
negligible self-discharge behavior (Figure 4c). Furthermore,
the self-discharge behavior of LSBs was investigated by
immersing Li metal in different electrolytes with sulfur powder
(Figure 4d). Sulfur gradually dissolves in DE and LHCE
electrolytes, and the color of electrolytes changes darker after
long-term storage. By contrast, the DPIE electrolyte remains
transparent, indicating its effectiveness in preventing LiPS
dissolution. To further understand the interaction of LiPSs
with different solvents, DFT calculations were performed
(Figures 4e and S16). The binding energies of LiPS-DME
complexes are more negative than those of their LiPS-DBE and
LiPS-TTE counterparts, indicating that LiPSs are prone to
being dissolved in DME, consistent with the polysulfide
solubility test. These results confirm that decreasing the
solvating power of ether solvents in turn alters their interaction
with LiPSs.
The high compatibility of SPAN cathodes with the DPIE is
associated with the formation of a robust and uniform CEI that
prevents polysulfide from dissolving in the electrolyte. As
shown in Figure 4f, typical C−C/C−H, C−O, and CO
species are detected in CEIs formed by LHCE and DPIE,
which can be attributed to the decomposition of solvents.41
Notably, higher FSI−decomposition products, including LiF,
sulfite, and sulfate, and lower solvent derivatives were detected
on the SPAN cathode cycled in DPIE electrolyte, suggesting a
more complete decomposition of FSI−anions.19,42 The CEI
formed by DPIE is more uniform and thinner (∼5.6 nm)
compared to that formed by LHCE (Figure 4g,h). Therefore,
the DPIE, with abundant AGGs, enables the formation of an
inorganic-rich and thin CEI layer on the SPAN electrode,
thereby effectively inhibiting polysulfide diffusion.

The interaction between solvents and Li+ is crucial, as it
directly influences the desolvation process of electrolytes,
which in turn affects the electrolyte’s low-temperature
performance. To better understand this, the binding energies
of Li+(DME)n and Li+(DBE)n complexes (n = 1−3) were
calculated. As shown in Figures 6a and S20, Li+ exhibits a
stronger interaction with DME compared with DBE, regardless
of the coordination number. At 25 °C, the desolvation energy
(Edsv) for Li+ in DPIE was calculated to be 339.4 kJ mol−1,
significantly lower than 472.8 kJ mol−1 for LHCE due to the
fewer and weaker coordinating solvent molecules (Figures 6b
and Note S1). In addition, desolvation energies for Li+ in DPIE
and LHCE at −10 °C were calculated based on the MD
simulations (Figures S21 and Note S2). Similarly, DPIE
exhibits a significantly lower desolvation energy compared to
LHCE. Furthermore, the activation energies of Li+ transport
through SEI (Ea,SEI) and the charge-transfer process (Ea,ct)
were also analyzed in Li||Li symmetric cells based on
temperature-dependent EIS (Figure S22). As shown in Figures
6c and S23, the Ea,SEI and Ea,ct values in DPIE are 68.39 kJ
mol−1 and 66.91 kJ mol−1, respectively, which are much lower
than those in LHCE, validating improved interface kinetics.
The electrochemical performance of Li||SPAN batteries over
a wide temperature range was also evaluated. Figure 6d shows
that the Li||SPAN cells deliver comparable capacities at 30 °C.
Notably, rapid capacity decay is observed for cells using DE,
which is related to the dissolution of LiPSs. At 0 °C, the Li||
SPAN cell with DPIE exhibits a highly reversible capacity of
986.5 mAh gs
−1. As the temperature drops to −20 °C, the cell
delivers a capacity of 493.4 mAh gs
−1, which is about three
times that in LHCE (Figure S24). This is probably related to
the strong interaction between Li+ and DME (slow desolvation
kinetics) and the sluggish Li+ transport in the SEI, leading to
significantly declined low-temperature performance. Only
702.3 mAh gs
−1 is maintained at 0 °C and almost no capacity
is retained at −20 °C when BE is used (Figure S25). At−10
°C, the DPIE enables an impressive average CE of 98.6% from
the 20th to the 100th cycles at 0.5 mA cm−2 (Figure S26). In
contrast, Li||Cu cells employing LHCE exhibit fluctuating CEs
after dozens of cycles. Even worse, Li||Cu cells using DE and
BE display lower and unstable CEs. Figure 6e demonstrates
that the Li||SPAN cell with DPIE exhibits a reversible capacity
of 716.6 mAh gs
−1 after 200 cycles at −10 °C, whereas the cell
employing LHCE shows a much lower capacity of 290.7 mAh
gs
−1 after 200 cycles. Therefore, DPIE offers fast Li+
desolvation and enhances the electrochemical reaction kinetics
at low temperatures. In addition, DPIE enables Li||SPAN cells
to be operated at 60 °C due to the high boiling temperature of
DBE (142 °C),18 accompanied by a high capacity of 1362
mAh gs
−1 after 30 cycles (Figure S27). Notice that the
electrochemical performance of the Li||SPAN cells with DPIE
is highly competitive among recently reported LSBs with other
tailored electrolytes (Table S2).18,19,23,37,42,44−47
For practical applications, achieving high Li utilization is
crucial to avoiding compromising the battery energy density.
Therefore, we deposited 4 mAh cm−2 of Li on Cu for serving
as a thin Li anode, and Li||SPAN cells with a negative/positive
areal capacity ratio of four were assembled and tested at 0.5 A
g−1. The Li||SPAN cell using DPIE maintains a capacity of
1140. Two mAh gs
−1 after 250 cycles (Figure S28). Li||SPAN
pouch cells were also assembled, as illustrated in Figure 6f. The
single-layer Li||SPAN pouch cells deliver a capacity of 910
mAh gs
−1 at 0.5 A g−1 after 200 cycles, with a capacity retention
rate of 73.4%. The pouch cells can stably power red and blue
LED arrays under various harsh conditions, such as repeated
folding and cutting, demonstrating great promise for practical
applications (Figures 6g, S29, and Movie S1).
CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we have designed a DPIE for wide-temperature
LSBs to resolve Li compatibility, desolvation kinetics, and LiPS
dissolution challenges. The weakly solvating DBE solvent
enables the self-assembly of AGGs to form large ion clusters at
a low salt concentration (1 M) and a low solvent:diluent ratio
(1:1, v/v). Both theoretical and experimental results confirm
that such a peculiar solvation structure contributes to fast Li+
desolvation at low temperatures and directs robust electrode−
electrolyte interphases. Moreover, the DBE molecule in the
first solvation sheath inherently prevents the dissolution of
LiPSs during the charge−discharge processes, eliminating the
detrimental effects caused by the crosstalk of LiPSs. Leveraging
the above advantages, the DPIE sustains room-temperature Li||
SPAN batteries for over 300 cycles with an impressive capacity
retention of 97.8%. In addition, Li||SPAN batteries using DPIE
demonstrate steady cycling at −10 °C for 200 cycles and
maintain a capacity retention of 97.1% over 250 cycles with a
limited N/P ratio of four. This work provides a detailed
understanding of the relationship between the electrolyte
microstructure and interface chemistry, opening up oppor-
tunities for long-life, fast-charging, and wide-temperature LSBs.
