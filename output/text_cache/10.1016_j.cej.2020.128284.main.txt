A B S T R A C T  
The catalysis of polysulfide conversion reactions is a recent approach to improve the performance and durability 
of Li–S batteries. While conventional wisdom emphasizes on the search for better catalysts, very few studies have 
considered the effect of S/Li2S2/Li2S solid deposition on the catalyst surface during battery operation. The 
accumulation of S/Li2S2/Li2S solid deposits on the catalyst surface can lead to progressive catalyst deactivation. 
This study shows how solid deposition can be minimized by tuning the electrolyte with electrolyte additives for a 
more sustained catalysis of the polysulfide conversion reactions. Specifically, a toluene and phosphorus 
pentasulfide-modified electrolyte is used to sweep the solid deposits from catalyst surface, so that the catalyst 
surface can be refreshed throughout the battery charge–discharge process. The surface rejuvenation effect was 
applied to further improve a newly developed high performance bimetallic Fe-N and Co-N co-doped carbon 
polysulfide catalyst; enabling sulfur cathodes with very high initial sulfur utilization (94%) and long cycle sta­
bility (2000 cycles with 67% capacity retention at 2C).   
1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries hold great promise for next genera­
tion energy storage systems because of the high theoretical specific ca­
pacity (1675 mAh g−1) and low cost of sulfur (<$150 ton-1) [1,2]. 
However, the multi-electron sulfur electrochemical reaction which is 
responsible for the high gravimetric capacity also forms a series of in­
termediates products ranging from soluble long-chain polysulfides 
(Li2Sn, 4 ≤n ≤8) to insoluble short-chain polysulfides (Li2Sn, n < 4) 
[3–5]. The soluble (long-chain) polysulfide intermediates can migrate 
from the cathode to the anode and react with the lithium metal there, 
while the insoluble (short-chain polysulfide) intermediates tend to de­
posit randomly on the cathode surface [6–8]. The multistep electro­
chemical reaction is accompanied by several solid–liquid-solid phase 
transformations (S8 ↔ Li2Sn ↔ Li2S); resulting in the redox shuttling of 
polysulfides between the battery electrodes, electrode surface passiv­
ation and electrode volume changes [9–12]. Most mitigation measures 
in use today are based on the retention of polysulfides in the sulfur 
cathode by adsorption (various adsorbents have been used; most notably 
the nanocarbons, metal oxides, sulfides, and nitrides) [13–20]. 
Considerable research efforts have also been devoted to the function­
alization of interlayers and separators to prohibit polysulfide shuttling 
between the cathode and the anode [21–25]. However, the actual per­
formance of Li–S batteries is still less than desired due to the sluggish 
kinetics of the multistep sulfur electrochemical reaction and the 
disproportionation reactions of the polysulfide intermediates [26–29]. 
Consequently, sulfur utilization and the rate performance of Li–S bat­
teries are still low by using the containment method alone [30–32]. 
The use of electrocatalysts to accelerate the polysulfide conversions 
to their end discharge product (Li2S) has recently been shown as an 
effective alternative strategy [33–38]. While a lot of attentions have 
been focused on the search for new polysulfide electrocatalysts, very few 
have examined the effect of solid S/Li2S2/Li2S deposition and accumu­
lation on the catalyst surface during battery charge and discharge pro­
cesses. The most notable exception was the use of soluble catalysts 
[39–41]. The deposition of Li2S2/Li2S solids during discharge and sulfur 
solids during charge on the catalyst surface not only reduces the number 
of catalytically active sites but also increases the charge and mass 
transfer resistance of the sulfur cathode (Fig. 1a). Sometimes, the 
accumulated S/Li2S2/Li2S deposits are also harder to reutilize, leading

to the formation of “dead sulfur” [42–46]. The timely removal of these 
solid deposits from the catalyst surface is therefore important to main­
tain a high polysulfide conversion activity in Li–S batteries. 
An apparent strategy is to render S/Li2S2/Li2S soluble in the elec­
trolyte, so that electrocatalysis can proceed unhindered on a consistent 
surface and benefit from the facile kinetics of solution electrochemistry. 
More importantly, the dissolution of Li2S2/Li2S also transforms the Li2S2 
↔ Li2S conversion (which accounts for half of the theoretical capacity) 
from a slow solid–solid reaction to a faster liquid–liquid reaction to in­
crease the sulfur utilization ratio and specific capacity of the Li–S bat­
teries. This solution strategy was only demonstrated recently with the 
use of an eutectic solvent capable of solubilizing the entire polysulfide 
family from Li2S8 to Li2S [47]. Sulfur utilization was improved but the 
rate performance (215 mAh g−1 at 1C) and cycle stability (200 cycles 
with 76% capacity retention at 0.3C) were not satisfactory. The reasons 
were high viscosity of the eutectic electrolyte and polysulfide attack on 
the lithium anode. 
We would like to present here a different approach to achieve the 
dissolution of polysulfide intermediates without the performance issues 
caused by high viscosity — the modification of tetraglyme (tetra­
ethylene glycol dimethyl ether, G4) based electrolytes with two previ­
ously known electrolyte additives — toluene and phosphorus 
pentasulfide [48–50]. Toluene has fairly good solubility for sulfur while 
phosphorus pentasulfide is an excipient for the dissolution of poly­
sulfides (from Li2S8 to Li2S). Although toluene and phosphorus penta­
sulfide have been used as separate electrolyte additives to improve the 
cyclability of Li–S batteries, we will show here that their combined 
application holds greater potential by raising the performance of poly­
sulfide conversion catalysis in Li–S batteries. The presence of dissolved 
polysulfides in the battery electrolyte necessitates the protection of the 
Li anode [51,52]. The application of a high Li‑ion conductivity solid 
electrolyte interface (SEI) layer on the Li anode has been shown to be 
effective [53]. In this study the Li anode was protected by a Li+
conductive Li3PS4 anode coating because of its demonstrated effective­
ness [54,55]. Based on our previous finding that Fe–N and Co–N co- 
doped carbons can be used in tandem to catalyze respectively the 
long-chain polysulfide conversion (S8 ↔ Li2S4) and the short-chain 
polysulfide conversion (Li2S4 ↔ Li2S) [56] an effort was undertaken to 
integrate the two co-catalysts into a single bimetallic system with N- 
coordinated Fe and Co dual active sites on carbon (Fe-N/Co-N@C). The 
dual active sites minimize the diffusion of polysulfides from one type of 
sites to the other; and in so doing increase the efficiency of stepwise 
electrocatalysis in the S8 ↔ Li2Sn ↔ Li2S overall reaction. Consequently 
with Fe-N/Co-N@C as the polysulfide catalyst, sulfur cathodes could 
deliver more discharge capacity (~1578 mAh g−1 at 0.1C, correspond­
ing to a sulfur utilization rate of ~ 94%), higher rate performance (~609 
mAh g−1 at 4C) and longer cyclability (2000 cycles with 67% capacity 
retention at the 2C rate) in the modified G4 electrolyte, confirming the 
elevated performance of the Fe-N/Co-N@C catalyst in the modified 
electrolyte. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Chemicals and materials 
Potassium ferricyanide (K3Fe(CN)6, 99 wt%), potassium hex­
acyanocobaltate (K3Co(CN)6, 97 wt%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 99.9 wt%), 
sulfur (99.5 wt%, S), lithium sulfide (99.98%, Li2S), lithium foil (99.9 wt 
%, 0.75 mm thick, Li), phosphorus pentasulfide (99 wt%, P2S5), bis 
(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (99.95 wt%, LiTFSI), toluene 
(99.8%, anhydrous), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (99.5 wt%, anhydrous, 
DME), lithium nitrate (99.99 wt%, LiNO3), polyvinylidene fluoride 
(PVDF, 99.5 wt%), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (99.5 wt%, NMP) from 
Sigma Aldrich; , Super-P carbon (SP, 99.5 wt%) from Timcal; were all 
used as received. Tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (≥99%) was dried 
over 4 Ångstr¨om molecular sieves for 5 days before use. 
2.2. Synthesis of the Fe-N/Co-N@C catalyst 
In a typical synthesis of Fe-N/Co-N@C, 1 g K3Fe(CN)6 and 1 g K3Co 
(CN)6 were thoroughly mixed, and ball-milled with ~ 40 g of stainless 
steel balls at 300 rpm for 12 h. The resulting powder was heated from 
room temperature to 1000 ◦C in flowing Ar (40 mL min−1) at 5 ◦C min−1 
and kept at 1000 ◦C for 2 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, 
the remaining solid was washed with deionized water followed by 
etching in 50 mL 2 M H2SO4 solution at 80 ◦C for 12 h under magnetic 
stirring. The remaining solid was separated by centrifugation at 9000 
rpm, repeatedly washed with water thrice; and finally lyophilized. 
2.3. Preparation of G4 and m-G4 electrolytes 
The G4 electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M 
LiNO3 in the tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether solvent. The m-G4 
electrolyte was prepared by adding 20 vol% of toluene and 5 wt% P2S5 
to the G4 electrolyte. 
2.4. Preparation of Li2S6 catholyte 
The Li2S6 catholyte was prepared by mixing Li2S and S powders in 
1:5 stoichiometric ratio in the tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether sol­
vent. The mixture was vigorously stirred at 60 ◦C until the powders were 
completely dissolved. 
2.5. Preparation of the Li3PS4 protected lithium metal anode 
The Li3PS4 protected lithium metal was prepared according to 
Ref. 54. In a typical preparation, 0.445 g P2S5 was added to 10 mL 0.1 M 
Li2S6 catholyte to form a clear solution. A piece of polished lithium metal 
was equilibrated in the solution at room temperature for 24 h, followed 
by washing with DME thrice and drying in vacuum for 2 h before use.

2.6. Fabrication of symmetric and lithium-catalyst cells 
Symmetric cells were fabricated as follows: 90 wt% of Fe-N/Co-N@C 
catalyst and 10 wt% of PVDF binder were homogenized in NMP to form 
a slurry, which was then uniformly cast on an Al foil and oven-dried at 
60 ◦C for 12 h. The foil was punched into 12 mm diameter disks. Two of 
the coated Al disks, a Celgard 2400 separator, and 50 μL of 0.2 M Li2S6 in 
the G4 or m-G4 electrolyte (without LiNO3) were assembled into a 
CR2025 coin cell in an Ar-filled MBruan glove box. Lithium-catalyst cells 
were fabricated the same as the symmetric cells except by replacing one 
of the coated Al disks with a lithium metal as the anode. The electrolyte 
was 50 μL 0.2 M Li2S6 in G4 or m-G4. The assembled lithium-catalyst 
cells were first galvanostatically discharged to 2.05 V at 0.1C, and 
then potentiostatically discharged at 2.0 V until the current fell below 
10-5 A. The cathode after the test were disassembled, rinsed with DME 
thrice to remove lithium salts on the surface, and then vacuum-dried 
before ex-situ analysis on the following day. 
2.7. Fabrication and electrochemical measurements of lithium-sulfur cells 
The sulfur working electrode was prepared as follows: 70 wt% sulfur 
powder and 20 wt% Fe-N/Co-N@C powder were first thoroughly mixed 
by ball-milling at 300 rpm for 12 h, and then mixed with 10 wt% PVDF 
binder in NMP for another 12 h. The slurry was cast on an Al foil, and 
oven-dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h. The foils were punched into 12 mm 
diameter disks and the typical sulfur loading on each disk was ~1.5 mg 
cm−2. The sulfur electrode was then assembled with a Li3PS4-protected 
lithium metal anode, a Celgard 2400 separator, and 20 μL G4 or m-G4 
electrolyte in to a CR2025-type coin cell. Galvanostatic charging and 
discharging were carried at room temperature on a NEWARE BTS-5 V 
battery tester between the 1.7–2.8 V at various C rates (1C = 1675 mAh 
g−1). Specific capacities were normalized by the mass of sulfur only. 
Cyclic voltammetry, potentiostatic measurements and electrochemical 
impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded using an Autolab type III elec­
trochemical workstation. EIS measurements were carried from 1 MHz 
to10 mHz. All tests were conducted at room temperature.

We first evaluated the catalytic activity of Fe-N/Co-N@C in sym­
metric cells — a useful tool to analyze the polysulfide conversion ki­
netics without the interference from a lithium anode (Fig. 3a) [57–60] 
The cell consisted of two identical electrodes surface coated with the Fe- 
N/Co-N@C catalyst (see Experimental Section). In symmetric cells with 
a Li2S6 electrolyte, the reduction of Li2S6 on one electrode is balanced by 
the oxidation of Li2S6 on the counter electrode. We expect the G4 elec­
trolyte modified with toluene and phosphorus pentasulfide (abbreviated 
henceforth as m-G4) would yield a faster polysulfide conversion ki­
netics, especially for the Li2S2 ↔ Li2S conversion reaction since it can 
solubilize Li2S2 and Li2S to render their interconversion as a solution 
redox reaction (Fig. 3b). Solubility measurements confirmed that up to 
3.5 mol of Li2S can be dissolved per mole of m-G4 electrolyte at room 
temperature (Fig. S2), as per previous reports [49,50]. This contrasts 
strongly with almost no solubility of Li2S in a regular G4 electrolyte. 
Cyclic voltammetry was used to measure the electrochemical perfor­
mance of the symmetric cell in the two different electrolytes. There are 
eight distinct redox peaks (corresponding to four redox pairs) in the 
cyclic voltammograms (CV) of the Fe-N/Co-N@C catalyst in G4 and m- 
G4 electrolytes (Fig. 3c and 3d). This is distinctly different from the 
behavior of most catalysts reported today where only one or two redox 
pairs are found in symmetric cell measurements. This is the first time 
where the four-steps in the polysulfide conversion reaction (S8 ↔ Li2S8 
↔ Li2S6 ↔ Li2S4 ↔ Li2S) can be clearly differentiated and shown 
(Fig. 3e). This is taken as an indication of the efficacy of the Fe-N/Co- 
N@C catalyst in catalyzing all of the four steps. In a symmetric cell, 
the stepwise oxidation of Li2S6 to S8 (Li2S6 → Li2S8 → S8) at the working 
electrode is exactly balanced by the stepwise reduction of Li2S6 to Li2S 
(Li2S6 → Li2S4 → Li2S) at the counter electrode; and vice versa. We also 
ran a control experiment to confirm that the m-G4 electrolyte does not 
have any redox reaction in the same voltage window (Fig. S3). However, 
we were unable to find the redox pair for the Li2S2 ↔ Li2S conversion. 
We suppose that Fe-N/Co-N@C is unable to catalyze all the steps in the 
Li2S4 to Li2S conversion (Li2S4 ↔ Li2S2 ↔ Li2S); and hence the Li2S4 ↔ 
Li2S2 reaction and the Li2S2 ↔ Li2S reaction occur at remarkably close 
potentials. 
All four redox pairs (a-a′, b-b′, c-c′ and d-d′) showed lower polari­
zation in the m-G4 electrolyte than in the G-4 electrolyte; suggesting 
that the polysulfide conversion kinetics was faster in the former. The 
observation was particularly conspicuous for redox reactions involving 
solid Li2S. For example, peak c was more negative in the G4 electrolyte 
(-0.005 V) that in the m-G4 electrolyte (0.048 V). This could be caused 
by a higher overpotential for Li2S oxidation at the counter electrode in 
the G4 electrolyte (red frame in Fig. 5b). Aside from the slower catalysis 
kinetics in the G4 electrolyte, the number of active sites at the 
completion of the first catalytic cycle was also lower in the G4 electro­
lyte. This was indicated by the greater decrease in the peak b intensity 
after the first cycle (Fig. 3c). This is likely due to the deposition of “dead 
sulfur (S, Li2S2 or Li2S)” on the catalyst surface which reduced the 
number of exposed active sites on Fe-N/Co-N@C in the G4 electrolyte. 
The catalyst became progressively deactivated as cycling continued. On 
the contrary, redox peaks in the m-G4 electrolyte showed good elec­
trochemical reversibility and hence the catalysis of polysulfide conver­
sion reactions is more proficient in the m-G4 electrolyte. It is believed 
that toluene and phosphorus pentasulfide in the electrolyte are able to 
sweep “dead sulfur” deposits from the catalyst surface, so that the 
catalyst surface can be refreshed to sustain a longer period of operation. 
The electrochemical reduction of sulfur to Li2S is a multistep process 
which begins with solid sulfur and proceeds through soluble long-chain 
polysulfides and insoluble short-chain polysulfides [61,62]. Among 
them the conversion from dissolved Li2S4 to insoluble Li2S, which ac­
counts for three-quarters of the total theoretical capacity, is the rate 
limiting step. We hypothesize that the catalytic enhancement effect of 
the m-G4 electrolyte originates from its conversion of the short-chain 
polysulfide reactions from a solid-state process to a solution-based 
process. Hence the catalytic enhancement effect should be more 
pronounced for the short-chain polysulfide reactions. 
Hypothesis testing was conducted in lithium-catalyst cells by 
measuring the polysulfide reaction kinetics in the two electrolytes. The 
lithium −
catalyst cell consisted of a catalyst-only working electrode 
without any sulfur, a lithium metal counter electrode; and Li2S6 dis­
solved in the electrolyte (see Experimental section for details). The cell 
was first galvanostatically discharged to 2.05 V to convert Li2S6 to Li2S4, 
and then potentiostatically discharged at 2.0 V to examine the conver­
sion reaction kinetics from Li2S4 to Li2S; as per the established experi­
mental protocol [63,64]. Fig. 4a and 4b show the current −
time 
responses from the potentiostatic discharge of the Li-catalyst cells in the 
G4 and m-G4 electrolytes. The discharge current consisted of two 
components – an instantaneous capacitive component, a Li2S4 reduction 
component and a Li2S6 reduction component. The current decreased at 
first as a transient capacitive response, and then increased due to Li2S4 
reduction. The current decreased again with the depletion of Li2S4. The 
discharge capacity calculated from the area under the curve (AUC) of the 
current time response could then be divided into three components: 
capacitive (tangerine color), the reduction from Li2S6 to Li2S4 (purple 
color), and the reduction from Li2S4 to Li2S (cream color) [63,64]. From 
these measurements it was found that the m-G4 electrolyte increased the 
capacity in the Li2S4 → Li2S reaction the most (from 198 to 373 mAh g−1, 
normalized by the mass of sulfur). This could be attributed to the solu­
bilization of Li2S2/Li2S formed on the catalyst in the m-G4 electrolyte; 
and the active sites for Li2S4 → Li2S catalysis were not passivated by the 
deposition of solid reduction products. On the contrary, the lower Li2S4 
→ Li2S capacity in the G4 electrolyte was due to the accumulation of 
Li2S2/Li2S solid deposit on the catalyst surface in the absence of the 
solubilizing additives. As a further proof of this hypothesis, fresh 
lithium-catalyst cells were potentiostatically discharged at 2.05 V to 
examine the reduction kinetics from Li2S6 to Li2S4 (Fig. S4). The capacity 
from the Li2S6 → Li2S4 reaction was similar in both electrolytes since 
there was no catalyst deactivation by solid deposition on the catalyst 
surface. 
Fig. 4c and 4d show the SEM images of the lithium-catalyst cell 
working electrode after the potentiostatic discharge. The morphology of 
the working electrode from the m-G4 electrolyte retained most of the 
features of the original porous carbon, whereas a thick solid Li2S2/Li2S 
film was present in the case of the working electrode from the G4 
electrolyte (Fig. S5). This is consistent with the stated hypothesis of a 
more persistent active catalyst surface in the m-G4 electrolyte. For a 
more direct proof, the test working electrodes were examined by XPS. 
Peaks between 160 and 162 eV B.E. due to Li2S2/Li2S deposition on the 
working electrode were only found in the G4 electrolyte (Fig. 4e). The 
working electrode from the m-G4 electrolyte displayed only very weak 
signals of Li2S2/Li2S deposition, suggesting that most of the Li2S2/Li2S 
formed had been dissolved in the m-G4 electrolyte. As the final test, we 
also analyzed the spent electrolyte by UV–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy. 
It was found that the spent m-G4 electrolyte contained mostly dissolved 
Li2S while the G4 electrolyte contained only dissolved long-chain pol­
ysulfides (Li2S6 and Li2S4). These results demonstrated conclusively that 
the conversion from Li2S6 to Li2S was nearly complete in the m-G4 
electrolyte but was only partially complete in the G4 electrolyte. 
Encouraged by the high catalytic activity of Fe-N/Co-N@C in the m- 
G4 electrolyte, we assembled a sulfur cathode with catalyst (a Fe-N/Co- 
N@C-sulfur composite cathode) and a lithium metal anode into full Li–S 
cells and measured their cyclability and rate performance. The presence 
of dissolved polysulfides in the electrolyte necessitated Li metal pro­
tection to inhibit the formation of dendritic and mossy lithium, as shown 
in a previous study [65]. In this study, the lithium metal anode was 
protected by an ex-situ formed Li+ conductive Li3PS4 surface layer 
(Fig. S6-9). The effectiveness of the Li3PS4 protection was confirmed in 
Li-Li3PS4/Li-Li3PS4 symmetric cell where higher cycling stability and 
lower polarization were achieved relative to the unprotected Li-Li 
symmetric cell (Fig. S10). It is believed that the Li3PS4 layer limited 
the direct contact and parasitic reactions of lithium with the electrolyte

It is the purpose of this study to draw attention to the deactivation 
effect of S/Li2S2/Li2S solid deposition on the catalyst surface in poly­
sulfide catalysis and to demonstrate an effective use of electrolyte ad­
ditives to refresh the catalyst surface. The formulation provided 
herewith is by no means optimal. The m-G4 electrolyte has a lower 
solubility for sulfur (0.12 M) than Li2S (3.5 M), which may cause some 
sulfur solid deposition on the electrode at the end of recharge. However, 
in comparison with the conversion of soluble Li2S4 to solid Li2S which 
accounts for three quarters of the sulfur theoretical capacity (1254 mAh 
g−1), the capacity associated with solid sulfur deposition from the 
oxidation of soluble Li2S8 (Li2S8 ↔ S8) only contributes to one eighth 
(209 mAh g−1) of the theoretical capacity. Previous research has also 
shown that the oxidation of soluble polysulfides may likely cease at Li2S8 
since the measured capacity (usually < 1400 mAh g−1) at high C-rates 
(>0.5C) agrees more with the theoretical capacity of Li2S ↔ Li2S8 con­
version (1463 mAh g−1) [66–69]. The recent finding by Cui et al. indi­
cated that liquid sulfur possibly maintained at the end of charge [70]. 
Hence the solubility of sulfur could be much lower than that of Li2S in a 
modified electrolyte design. Nonetheless work is still needed to identify 
the best balance of sulfur and Li2S solubilities. It is our wish that the 
results here can stimulate more work on electrolyte additives as a means 
to improve the Li–S battery performance via the suppression of catalyst 
deactivation in polysulfide conversion reactions. 
4. Conclusions 
This work reports a new G-4 based electrolyte formulation (m-G4) 
where toluene and phosphorus pentasulfide are used as electrolyte ad­
ditives to improve the kinetics of polysulfide conversion catalysis in Li–S 
batteries. The two additives combined can effectively reduce S/Li2S2/ 
Li2S solid deposition on the catalyst surface, thereby providing a more 
consistent surface for catalysis over a longer period of time. The modi­
fied electrolyte, together with a new bimetallic dual-site Fe-N/Co-N@C 
which successfully integrates the stepwise catalysis of long-chair and 
short-chain polysulfide conversion reactions; were extensively tested in 
symmetric cells, lithium-catalyst cells and Li–S full cells. Higher cata­
lytic activity and faster kinetics of polysulfide conversion were demon­
strated in all cell types. The measurement results indicated that the solid 
short-chain polysulfide conversion reactions benefit the most from the 
use of the electrolyte additives. Consequently, sulfur cathodes with the 
Fe-N/Co-N@C catalyst could achieve a high sulfur utilization concur­
rently with a high rate performance and a good cycle life. Aside from 
providing a new electrolyte formulation and an effective dual-site Fe-N/ 
Co-N@C polysulfide conversion catalyst, this research presents a generic 
electrolyte modification method which can in principle be extended to 
other polysulfide catalyst systems to improve the effectiveness of poly­
sulfide conversion catalysis in Li–S batteries.
