ABSTRACT
The dissolution of lithium polysulﬁde into electrolytes is a major limitation of Li-S batteries that hinders their practical applications. Lithium
polysulﬁde dissolution is suppressed by using sulfur-complexed microporous-carbon cathodes in lithium–sulfur batteries. This study reports
the use of a localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE), in which a sulfolane (SL) high-concentration electrolyte is diluted with
hydroﬂuoroether (HFE), for the strong suppression of lithium polysulﬁde elution. A previously reported sulfolane electrolyte in the solvated
state exhibits a short cycle life of eight cycles, whereas the sulfur-containing microporous-carbon cathode with a sulfolane-based
electrolyte prepared in the ratio of lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) : SL : HFE = (0.572–0.953) : 2.00 : 1.26 (mol) exhibits
a discharge capacity of more than 550 mAh g−1, even after 50 charge–discharge cycles. Increasing the LiTFSI ratio decreases the response
current during discharge, as indicated by cyclic voltammetry. A sulfur cathode with microporous carbon (AZC) shows equivalent
performance on decreasing the amount of solvated LiTFSI. This study could guide the design and development of electrolytes for sulfur-
containing microporous-carbon cathodes.

1. Introduction
The recent expansion of lithium-ion-battery applications has
necessitated an improvement in their energy density. The inter-
calation materials used in lithium-ion batteries at present are
expected to reach their energy-density limit in the near future;1
therefore,
all-solid-state,
lithium-sulfur
(Li-S),
and
lithium-air
batteries
are
attracting
immense
attention
as
next-generation
rechargeable batteries. Li-S batteries have been widely studied
since the 1970s.2,3 Sulfur (S), the cathode material in Li-S batteries,
exhibits several advantageous properties, such as, light weight, low
cost, and abundance; moreover, sulfur has a high theoretical
capacity (1672 mAh g¹1) as its redox reaction involves a two-
electron transfer reaction per S atom (S8 + 16Li+ + 16e¹ ¼
8Li2S).4
Although Li-S batteries exhibit high potential as next-generation
rechargeable batteries, numerous limitations that hinder their
commercial applications. A major limitation is the rapid dissolution
of lithium polysulﬁdes (reaction intermediates formed during
charging and discharging) in organic electrolytes, causing irrever-
sible side reactions with speciﬁc electrolytes.5 Highly soluble long-
chain lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2S8–4), formed as intermediates during
the sulfur-cathode discharge process, diﬀuse into the lithium anode
and are reduced to insoluble short-chain lithium polysulﬁdes
(Li2S1–2). Subsequently, the lithium polysulﬁdes undergo reverse
reactions during the charging process and diﬀuse back to the
cathode. This shuttle behavior (labeled redox shuttle reactions)
lowers the actual capacity and cycle life of Li-S batteries because the
leached lithium polysulﬁde exhibits nonconductor behavior at the
positive and negative electrode surfaces;6,7 electrolyte-based coun-
termeasures are used to overcome this limitation. In general,
1 mol dm¹3 (M) lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTF-
SI)/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) : 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (1 : 1 by
volume) is used as an electrolyte for Li-S batteries because of its low
viscosity, good rate capacity, high donor number, and excellent ionic
conductivity;8,9 lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is used as an electrolyte
additive to eﬀectively suppress the dissolution of lithium poly-
sulﬁdes.10 LiNO3 enhances the durability of Li-S batteries by
forming a “passivation layer” that eﬀectively inhibits the dissolution
of lithium polysulﬁdes and a “solid electrolyte interface (SEI)” that
protects the Li-metal anode.11–13 However, the applicability of
LiNO3 is severely limited by its discharge voltage due to irreversible
reduction reaction below 1.8 V (vs. Li/Li+),14 gradual dissipation
during charge-discharge cycling, the formation of toxic byproducts,
and low sustainability.15 Additionally, 2 wt% LiNO3 is generally
added to ether-based electrolytes due to its low solubility; this is
not practicable for long-term charge-discharge cycling as it is a
consumable additive.
Highly concentrated electrolytes (HCEs), such as solvated
electrolytes, are used as electrolytes for Li-S batteries without
lithium nitrate.16–18 The HCE, with no free spaces for lithium
polysulﬁde dissolution, physically traps the lithium polysulﬁde in
the electrolyte. Furthermore, HCEs improve the performance of Li-
metal anodes; 4 M lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in DME

Although HCEs are extremely useful electrolytes for Li-metal
anodes, they exhibit high viscosity and are more expensive than
general electrolytes because of their large lithium-salt content.24–26
Localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCE), in which HCEs
are diluted with hydroﬂuoroether (HFE) to lower their viscosity and
lithium-salt content, are used to overcome these limitations.27,28
HFEs dissolve the lithium salt negligibly; thus, the viscosity of the
HCE is reduced without destroying its solvation structure, thereby
increasing its dielectric constant and signiﬁcantly reducing its
lithium content.29,30 Watanabe et al. have reported the use of glyme-
based electrolytes (LiTFSI : G4 : HFE = 10 : 8 : 40 mol)31–34 and
sulfolane-based electrolytes (LiTFSI : SL : HFE = 1 : 2 : 2 mol)35–37
as LCHE electrolytes for Li-S batteries; they suppress lithium
polysulﬁde
dissolution,
facilitating
a
stable
charge/discharge
process.
In this study, a sulfolane-based LHCE electrolyte was developed
to maximize the performance of microporous activated carbon-
sulfur composites with the suppression of lithium polysulﬁde
dissolution. Although a high concentration of lithium salt was more
eﬀective in suppressing lithium polysulﬁde dissolution, it over-
protected
the
microporous
activated
carbon-sulfur
composite,
adversely aﬀecting its activity. This is the ﬁrst report to suggest
that a high concentration of lithium hinders the optimization of the
activity of microporous activated carbon-sulfur composites.
2. Experimental
2.1 Preparation and activation of azulmic carbon (AZC)
AZC was prepared according to a previous publication.17,18 AZA
was carbonized at 800 °C for 1 h under a nitrogen atmosphere;
subsequently, the AZA carbides were mixed with an alkaline
activator (KOH) in a ratio of 1 : 2 by weight in deionized water,
stirred, heated at 100 °C for 5 h, and dried. The sample was
activated at 800 °C for 1 h in a quartz tube (3 cm in diameter) under
an argon atmosphere; subsequently, the activated sample was
dispersed in deionized water and neutralized with dilute hydrochlo-
ric acid. The samples were then ﬁltered, washed with excess
deionized water, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for more than 12 h to
obtain AZC.
2.2 Preparation of the sulfur-composite AZC (AZC-S) and
AZC-S cathode
The amount of sulfur composite in the AZC was calculated from
the pore volume measured by nitrogen adsorption/desorption
experiments. AZC (0.4 g) and sulfur (0.6 g) were mixed in an agate
mortar and placed in a simple reaction vessel made of stainless steel.
The mixture was heated to 155 °C in an air atmosphere and
maintained for 5 h to melt the sulfur and enable its diﬀusion into the
AZC pores. The temperature was then increased from 155 to 300 °C
and maintained for 2 h to remove the excess sulfur outside the AZC
pores. Temperature increments were carried out at a rate of
10 °C min¹1, while air cooling was used to cool the samples to
room temperature. The AZC composite formed with sulfur was
labeled AZC-S. To fabricate the sulfur cathode, AZC-S, carbon
nanotubes (CNT), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), and styrene-
butadiene rubber (SBR) were ﬁrst mixed in a weight ratio of
89 : 5 : 3 : 3 in deionized water to form a slurry. Subsequently, the
slurry was coated on an Al foil using the doctor-blade method and
dried in an oven at 40 °C for more than 3 h; the residual water was
removed by drying in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for more than 12 h.
The amount of sulfur in the electrode was 3.0 mg cm¹2.
2.3 Electrolyte preparation and assembly of Li-S cells
Electrolytes were prepared by mixing diﬀerent concentrations of
lithium bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, Solvey) (0.5,
1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 M) in a solvent containing sulfolane (SL,
Kishida Chemical) and 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoro-
propyl (HFE, Daikin Industries) in a 1 : 1 ratio by volume
(2.00 : 1.26 by mol). For comparison, the sulfolane electrolyte
reported by Watanabe et al. was prepared using LiTFSI : SL :
HFE = 1 : 2 : 2 (mol) and labeled the standard SL.37
A two-electrode cell was constructed using an AZC-S electrode
as the positive electrode, Li foil as the anode, and a polyoleﬁn
microporous membrane as the separator; a glove box ﬁlled with Ar,
maintained at a dew point of less than ¹80 °C, was used for cell
construction and electrolyte preparation, to prevent changes due to
moisture.
2.4 Physical properties and electrochemical tests
An Autosorb iQ instrument (Anton-Paar) was used for the
nitrogen adsorption/desorption measurements at ¹196 °C, while
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, DTG-60H, Shimazu Co. Ltd.)
was used to estimate the sulfur loading. Charge-discharge tests
of the Li-S batteries were carried out at a current density of
167.2 mA g¹1 (0.1 C) per sulfur weight in the voltage range of 1.0–
3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+). A scanning rate of 0.1 mV s¹1 was used for
cyclic voltammetry and linear sweep voltammetry, and all the
electrochemical measurements were performed at 25 °C. A Nano-
ﬁnder30 (TOKYO INSTRUMENTS) green laser (at 532 nm) was
used for Raman spectroscopy.
The viscosity of the electrolyte was measured with a microVISC
(Viscotech) micro diﬀerential pressure viscometer, and the ionic
conductivity was estimated with a Seven2Go Cond meter S3 in
combination with a Cond probe InLab 751-4mm (METTLER
TOLEDO). These physical properties of the electrolyte were
evaluated at 25 °C in dry air with a dew point below ¹40 °C.
3. Results and Discussion
It has been previously reported that AZC contains micropore-rich
activated carbon as it is formed by the selective decomposition of the
nitrogen moieties in AZA by its activation. The speciﬁc surface area
of AZC was calculated using the Brunauer (Emmett) Teller method;
the speciﬁc surface area and pore volume were 2231 m2 g¹1 and
1.059 cm3 g¹1, respectively. The theoretical sulfur-ﬁllable amount,
calculated from the pore volume, was 67 wt%. The theoretical sulfur
loading capacity was calculated using the following equation:
Theoretical loading of sulfur
¼
V  MðcarbonÞ  µðsulfurÞ
V  MðcarbonÞ  µðsulfurÞ þ MðcarbonÞ
[where, V: pore volume (cm3 g¹1), M: mass of carbon (g), and µ:
density of sulfur (g cm¹3)].
Sulfur undergoes signiﬁcant volume changes during charging and
discharging processes; therefore, the amount of sulfur loaded into
the activated carbon was slightly lower than the theoretical sulfur
capacity. The amount of sulfur in the composite was 60 wt%
according to thermogravimetric measurements (Fig. 1). The phys-
ical properties of the (0.5–2.5 M) LiTFSI/SL : HFE electrolytes
(hereafter referred to as “x-SL”) and the standard SL are summa-
rized in Table 1. HFE was used as a diluent to lower the electrolyte
viscosity, with negligible dissolution of the lithium salt and no
distortion of its solvation structure. The standard SL was solvated
with LiTFSI and SL, and LiTFSI : SL = 1 : 2 (by mol) was
considered to be the solvation ratio. All the formulated electrolytes
(0.5 to 2.5-SL) exhibited a lower LiTFSI concentration relative to
SL compared to that of the standard SL. Moreover, a lower HFE
ratio caused a higher viscosity; the 2.5-SL (which exhibits the

The Raman-spectroscopy results of the formulated electrolytes
are shown in Fig. 2. Similar to a previous report by Watanabe et al.,
the wavenumber between the TFSI anion and SL from 740 to
750 cm¹1 on increasing the LiTFSI to SL ratio, and continuously
shifted towards 750 cm¹1.37 This shift was possibly due to an
increase in the number of contact-ion-pairs (CIP) or aggregate
coordination (AGG) between the TFSI anion and Li ion. The 0.5-SL
and 1.0-SL electrolytes showed a peak at 742 cm¹1, the standard-SL,
1.5-SL, and 2.0-SL electrolytes showed a peak at 745 cm¹1, while
the 2.5-SL electrolyte showed a peak at 748 cm¹1. This suggests that
higher concentrations of TFSI¹ ions form CIPs and AGGs.
The electrolytes with a higher LiTFSI concentration than that in
1.5-SL possibly formed ion pairs, similar to ion-pair formation in
the standard SL. Electrolytes containing diﬀerent amounts of HFE
were used with 1.5-SL to investigate the eﬀects of HFE dilution on
the ion-pair state. The electrolyte compositions with diﬀerent HFE
ratios are shown in Table S1 and the results of their Raman-
spectroscopy measurements are shown in Fig. S1; although the HFE
amount did not inﬂuence the peak position, it inﬂuenced the peak
intensity, indicating no modiﬁcation in the coordination state of the
ions.
Li-S batteries were assembled and used for constant-current
charge-discharge tests to analyze the lithium polysulﬁde dissolution
in each electrolyte. Figure 3 shows the ﬁrst charge-discharge curve
using each electrolyte; only 0.5-SL showed a plateau around the
discharge curve of 2.1 V, indicating lithium polysulﬁde dissolution
due to the low lithium-salt concentration. The initial charge capacity
was 1511, 1448, 1389, 1289, 1295, and 1233 mAh g¹1 for the 0.5-,
1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, 2.5-, and standard SL electrolyte, respectively. The
charge capacity in the ﬁrst cycle decreased as the LiTFSI
concentration increased. 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, 2.5-, and standard SL
showed a single discharge plateau around 1.8 V. This behavior is like
that of a solid-state reaction that produces Li2S and Li2S2, which is
characteristic of sulfur loaded in microporous carbon. The absence
of a plateau around 2.2 V suggests that the reaction mechanism
is based on a solid-state reaction without undergoing Li2S8 or
Li2S6.37,38

SL separators after one cycle were used to conﬁrm lithium
polysulﬁde elution (Fig. 5). The 0.5-SL electrolyte was less eﬀective
in inhibiting lithium polysulﬁde elution; the separator stained dark
yellow, conﬁrming the presence of a large amount of sulﬁde.
Although the separator appeared slightly yellow while using the 1.5-
SL electrolyte, the system exhibited a greater inhibition of lithium
polysulﬁde elution compared to 0.5-SL system.
Cyclic voltammetry measurements were used to investigate
the charge-discharge behavior of the formulated SL electrolytes.
Figure 6 shows the CV measurements for the ﬁrst three cycles. The
0.5-SL electrolyte showed a wide peak that keeps the response
current ﬂowing up to 1 V during discharge, indicating lithium
polysulﬁde dissolution from the ﬁrst cycle. The 1.0-SL electrolyte
showed a sharp response current peak with a full width at half
maximum (FWHM) of 0.29 V in the ﬁrst cycle, with a slightly wider
peak
from
the
second
cycle,
indicating
lithium
polysulﬁde
dissolution; this was in agreement with the coulombic-eﬃciency
results of the constant-current charge-discharge tests. For 1.5-SL
(FWHM: 0.27 V) and 2.0-SL (FWHM: 0.33 V), the peak width of
the response current changed negligibly during discharge, indicating
a suppression of lithium polysulﬁde dissolution. Although 2.5-SL
exhibited a wide response current peak with FWHM of 0.70 V from
the ﬁrst-cycle discharge, the response current continued to ﬂow
at 1.0 V, unlike the peak shape caused by lithium polysulﬁde
dissolution in 0.5-SL. Thus, the increase in lithium-salt concen-
tration suppressed lithium polysulﬁde dissolution, but the decrease
in wettability and ion-transport ability due to the increase in
viscosity possibly reduced the response current. The standard-SL
(FWHM: 0.78 V) and 2.5-SL electrolytes showed similar CV
behavior, indicating that an extremely high lithium-salt concen-
tration could reduce the response current in microporous carbon-
activated carbon-sulfur composite cathodes, possibly due to the
inhibition of lithium-ion transport by sulfur-ﬁlled micropores and
the low wettability of the highly concentrated and solvated
electrolyte. The low cycle life of the AZC-S electrode in the
standard-SL electrolyte (Fig. 4) would correlate with its low
response current and high overvoltage in the CV (Fig. 6), which
suggests a problem with low reaction activity. We speculate that
such response current and overvoltage in the standard-SL are caused
by a high resistance of the electrode because of electrolyte
component decomposition such as HFE from the early stages of
the charge-discharge cycle.
From the above results, the optimization of the electrolyte in this
study is related to the following matters: ﬁrst, notably, the viscosity
and ionic conductivity of the electrolyte alone cannot determine
battery performance. Secondly, for the practical optimization, the
amount of HFE was able to be reduced due to the possible
decomposition of HFE, but this caused an increase in viscosity and a
decrease in wettability to the present cathode. Therefore, under these
low HFE conditions, the number of moles of LiTFSI to SL was
reduced, thus increasing the free SL “to the extent that it does not
dissolve the polysulﬁde” (examples are 2.0-, 1.5- and 1.0-SL),
thereby increasing the activity of the cathode reaction. These
measures are regarded as electrolyte optimization in this study.
4. Conclusion
In this study, the relationship between the electrolyte concen-
tration and the expressed capacity of a sulfur cathode fabricated
using microporous carbon (AZC) was investigated. Notably, the
viscosity and ionic conductivity of the electrolyte alone cannot
determine battery performance. The Raman spectrum of the SL
electrolyte has a peak at 745 cm¹1, which suggests that the
coordination state of SL and LiTFSI is such that the dissolution of
lithium polysulﬁde is suﬃciently suppressed. The electrolyte
containing an LiTFSI : SL ratio of (0.953–0.572) : 2.00 (by mol)
showed suﬃcient inhibition of lithium polysulﬁde elution. In this
ratio range, even the minimum LiTFSI-containing electrolyte
(0.572 : 2.00) exhibited a stable charge-discharge cycle compared
to the conventional standard-SL electrolyte. Thus, a reduction in the
Li-salt content of electrolytes could not only increase the reaction
activity and reversibility but also reduce their cost and density,
facilitating the practical applications of lithium-sulfur batteries.
Furthermore, it could provide more space for the dissolution of
additives, facilitating the design and development of high-perform-
ance electrolytes.
