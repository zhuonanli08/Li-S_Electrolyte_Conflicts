ABSTRACT: Sparsely solvating electrolyte (SSE), which can achieve a quasi-solid-phase sulfur reaction path, stands
out as a promising strategy to alleviate the dependence on electrolyte usage and construct lean-electrolyte lithium−
sulfur (Li−S) batteries. Nonetheless, its formation relies upon a high dosage of salt and diluent, thereby leading to
increased electrolyte cost. To this end, we herein customize a localized SSE (LSSE) featuring a low ratio of salt-to-
solvent and diluent-to-solvent through alkyl chain tuning. A multimodal 2D nuclear magnetic resonance technique is
developed to unveil the Li-ion solvation sheath reorganization, which is crucial for studying the coordination and
dynamics in liquid electrolytes. LSSE affords an anion-derived solid electrolyte interface and effective restriction of
the shuttling effect; hence, our Li−S batteries can sustain a steady operation under 4 μL mgS
−1 and 3 mg cm−2. Our
work opens a new avenue for advancing SSE design in the pursuit of pragmatic lean-electrolyte Li−S batteries.
L
ithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries have stimulated enor-
mous research attention as a promising candidate for
next-generation energy storage systems due to their
ultrahigh theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1).1−5
Nevertheless, a key concern impeding the development lies
in the “shuttle effect” of soluble polysulfide intermediates,
which gives rise to irreversible capacity attenuation and
continuous Li metal corrosion.6−10 Moreover, typical dis-
solution−deposition electrochemistry in traditional ether-
based electrolytes necessitates large electrolyte dosage to
ensure sufficient dissolution and conversion of polysulfides,
which would be detrimental to the ultimate energy density of
Li−S batteries.11−15 Therefore, it is urgently imperative to
adjust the polysulfide speciation and sulfur reaction pathway
for simultaneously suppressing polysulfide shuttling and
elevating lean-electrolyte performance toward pragmatic Li−S
batteries.16,17
Sparsely solvating electrolyte (SSE) holds tremendous
potential for tackling the aforementioned two crucial issues
and pursuing Li−S commercialization, wherein a localized
high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) with a unique Li-ion
solvation structure is considered a crucial strategy.18−20 For
one thing, due to the significant decrease of free active
solvents, LHCE fundamentally restricts the dissolution and
shutting of soluble polysulfides.21,22 In this respect, electrolyte
usage is anticipated to be markedly lowered owing to the quasi-

Despites these apparent advantages, considering that

cathode polarization. To this end, an endogenous comediator
has emerged as an effective tactic to alter reaction path and
propel redox kinetics via the in situ chemical interaction with
sulfur species.29−31 Although a variety of organic molecules
have been developed to accelerate Li−S chemistry and
improve cathode performance, its deployment in SSE is still
unexplored to date. Besides, selection criteria and the working
mechanism have not come to a clear consensus; hence,
comprehensive characterization and in-depth understanding
are needed.
In this contribution, a low-cost localized SSE (LSSE) is
developed via employing ethylene glycol dibutyl ether
(EGDBE) with a weak solvation effect via tuning the terminal
alkyl chains of DME, which is characterized by a low ratio of
salt to solvent and diluent to solvent. A multimodal 2D nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) methodology is developed to shed
light upon the dynamics of ion motion and solvent exchange in
liquid solution. With the aid of a multitude of spectroscopic
characterizations and electrochemical measurements, it is
unveiled that LSSE is conducive to concurrently rendering
an inorganic-rich SEI and suppressing the shuttle effect. More
importantly, it achieves a quasi-solid-state sulfur reaction path
and thus relieves the dependence on electrolyte usage. To
handle key issues of LSSE pertaining to the sluggish cathode
reaction kinetics, diphenyl ditelluride (DPDTe) has been
proposed as an endogenous comediator to fine-tune sulfur
redox chemistry. Benefiting from enhanced polysulfide
conversion and Li2S dissociation, LSSE+DPDTe sustains a

DME is a commonly used solvent for constructing SSE
owing to facile salt dissociation and good anode compatibility.
It exhibits a relatively strong solvation ability due to the short
carbon chain and dual-denticity oxygen coordination sites,
which also possesses a donor number higher than that of FSI−.
As a consequence, FSI−is hard to enter the inner solvation
sheath of Li+ and achieve a stable Li+−FSI−coordination,
which would lead to the formation of undesired SEI with rare
inorganic components and inferior mechanical strength.32
Moreover, a high salt concentration is highly requested when
using DME as the solvent to fabricate SSE. In this case, high
diluent usage is needed to lower the electrolyte viscosity,
thereby inevitably increasing the electrolyte cost. Taken
together, EGDBE with a weak solvation effect has been
employed to construct an SSE with a low cost (Figure 1a;
Figures S1 and S2; Tables S1 and S2). For one thing, EGDBE
shows an apparently higher C/O atomic ratio (5) in
comparison to that of DME (2), which decreases the O
proportion in a solvent molecule and hence reduces the Li−O
coordination since Li ions prefer to bind with the ethereal
oxygen.33 In this respect, it needs to accommodate more extra
C and H atoms to achieve Li-ion coordination, thereby leading
to a lower average coordination number and a weaker solvation
effect to Li+. In addition, a high C/O atomic ratio will increase
the molar mass of ether solvents, thereby decreasing the
number of solvent molecules in real electrolytes with a fixed
concentration. Consequently, it is beneficial to the formation
of a weakly solvating electrolyte with a low concentration. In
conclusion, a high C/O atomic ratio plays a decisive role in
weakening the solvation ability of analogous solvent molecules
with two ethereal oxygen atoms. Meanwhile, a high C/O
atomic ratio will increase the steric hindrance with the terminal
n-butyl group, further weakening the Li−O coordination in the
solvation structure. Therefore, salt concentration and diluent
usage are anticipated to be simultaneously lowered. Specifi-
cally, DME or EGDBE is mixed with 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl
ether (TTE) with a volume ratio of 3:2 and the total salt
concentration is controlled to 1 M; hence, the thus-formed
electrolytes are denoted as localized mediumly solvating
electrolyte (LMSE) and LSSE, respectively. As for the former,
DME solvents will be dominant in the Li-ion solvation sheath
(Figure 1b), which will give rise to organic-rich SEI and
rampant dendrite growth on the anode side. It is widely
accepted that polysulfides interact with ether solvents through
the Li−O bond. In this respect, DME with a low C/O atomic
ratio and high molecular polarity exhibits a high solubility
toward polysulfides due to the strong electrostatic interaction
between Li ions and ethereal O atoms. In contrast, EGDBE as
a homologous ether with an apparently higher C/O atomic
ratio is expected to possess an ultralow polysulfide solubility,
which is ascribed to a weak solvation effect (Figure 1c).
Meanwhile, FSI−will readily enter the inner Li-ion solvation
sheath, which contributes to the anion-derived SEI with high
mechanical properties and ionic conductivity. This enables
smooth and dense Li metal deposition to alleviate the
generation of detrimental Li dendrites.
NMR is a powerful toolbox to investigate the electrolyte
solvation structure and ion coordination environment (Figures
S3 and S4).34,35 To begin, the ion−dipole interaction between
the ethereal O atom and Li+ is usually strong, which induces a
transfer of electron cloud from solvent to Li atom. In this
regard, the 7Li resonance of the LSSE exhibits an apparent
downfield shift (Figure 1d), indicative of a deshielding effect. It
is reasonable that the coordination capability of the O atoms in
EGDBE is weaker as compared to that of DME; hence, the
electron attraction of Li+ from ether solvents is impeded. In
addition, the 19F resonance of FSI−also displays a downfield
shift (Figure 1e). This originates from the reduced electron
density in FSI−, corroborating that more FSI−squash into the
inner Li+ solvation sheath and hence Li+-FSI−ion pairing is
enhanced. In parallel, the signal broadening of 7Li and 19F is
mainly attributed to the structural complexity in the LSSE with
more coordinated FSI−instead of free anions. The chemical
shift of 1H NMR becomes more positive in the LSSE due to
the deshielding effect (Figure 1f). Concretely, the extension of
the carbon chain would make less EGDBE solvents participate
in the Li-ion solvation structure due to the weakly solvating
ability, thereby decreasing the electron cloud density around H
atoms.
T1 (spin−lattice) and T2 (spin−spin) relaxation times
reflect the translational/rotational motion and surrounding
environment of the measured nuclei, respectively.36,37 First, it
is worth mentioning that the 7Li T1 of LSSE is almost a
magnitude lower than that of LMSE (Figure 1g), indicative of
markedly larger size and higher asymmetry of Li-ion solvation
configuration.38 It is accepted that FSI−is likely to coordinate
with Li+ to generate multitudinous CIPs and AGGs due to the
weak solvation effect of EGDBE (Figure 1h).39 In stark
contrast, solvent separated ion pairs (SSIPs) with a relatively
small size and high symmetry are dominant in the LMSE, thus
causing a sharp increase of T1. In response, as shown in the
Raman spectra, there are much fewer free FSI−in the LSSE
than in the LMSE (Figure 1i; Figure S5). In addition, the peak
assigned to FSI−can be deconvoluted into two peaks, wherein
one is at 725 cm−1 (free FSI−) and the other is at 740 cm−1
(coordinated FSI−). Apparently, LSSE renders more coordi-
nated FSI−(82%) than that (47%) in LMSE.
To elucidate the ion−dipole interaction and physicochem-
ical property, density functional theory (DFT) calculation was
carried out. Electrostatic potential (ESP) maps in Figure 1j
exhibit that both EGDBE and DME possess more negative
parts on the two central oxygen atoms, suggestive of similar Li+
chelation coordination with ethereal oxygen. Correspondingly,
the binding energy between Li+ and EGDBE is slightly lower
regardless of whether the participation of FSI−is considered
(Figure S6), manifesting that the evidently weaker solvation
effect of EGDBE does not originate from the Lewis basicity
difference of ethereal oxygen but the lower proportion of the O
atoms in a solvent molecule. In other words, a high C/O
atomic ratio of EGDBE weakens Li−O coordination and
solvation effect since more extra C and H atoms need to be
accommodated in the solvation structure. In the case of TTE,
the binding energy is apparently lower than that of DME and
EGDBE, which originates from the multiple F-rich groups with
strong electron-withdrawing effect. This makes TTE scarcely
coordinate with Li+ and stay away from the Li-ion solvation
sheath as a diluent. The energy level can unravel the redox
stability of electrolyte structure, which is correlated with the
quality of the interface formed between electrolyte and
electrode. For one thing, due to the electron attraction from
ether solvents to coordinated Li+, both lowest unoccupied
molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular
orbital (HUMO) level will be lowered when considering Li-ion

To evaluate the positive effect of such electrolyte solvation
sheath engineering on facilitating Li deposition and restricting
Li dendrites, Li||Cu half cells were assembled to study the Li
plating/stripping behavior. With respect to the Coulombic
efficiency (CE) test at a current density of 3 mA cm−2 and a
capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 (denoted as 3 mA cm−2, 1 mAh
cm−2), LSSE demonstrates a fairly smooth operation over 240
cycles while LMSE undergoes a rapid fading after only 60
cycles (Figure 3a; Figure S16). In this regard, the average CE
value (98.36%) of LSSE is apparently higher than that
(85.56%) of LMSE, suggestive of a better compatibility of
LSSE with the Li metal anode. In addition, a Li||Li symmetric
cell test at 2 mA cm−2, 2 mAh cm−2 was carried out to
investigate the cyclic durability of Li metal anode. As shown in
Figure 3b, LSSE enables a lower overpotential as compared
with LMSE at the initial cycling stage. Besides, the cycling
lifetime of LSSE is almost four times longer than that of LMSE.
Li||Li symmetric cell tests at 4 mA cm−2, 4 mAh cm−2 and 1
mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2 also reflect the conspicuous advantages
of LSSE on affording a desired electrode/electrolyte interface
and durable Li metal anode (Figure S17). Notably, LHCE with
an increased viscosity and slower diffusion dynamics cannot
sustain a steady operation over a long-term cycling process.
Rate capability test with varied current densities and a fixed
capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 was also carried out (Figure 3c),
wherein LSSE showcases a superior performance especially at a
high current density of 3 mA cm−2 or 5 mA cm−2. These
electrochemical measurements verify that LSSE enhances the
reversibility of the Li metal anode.
Favorable Li deposition morphology is a key to achieving a
long-lived Li metal anode during the long-term plating/
stripping process. In this respect, scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) images of electrodeposited Li metal were collected to
decouple the origin of the elevated cycling stability of the Li
anode in LSSE. Obviously, Li metal displays a smooth and
dense morphology with a large size in LSSE (Figure 3d,e;
Figure S18), which is beneficial to alleviating the formation of
“dead Li”. In stark contrast, porous and cracked Li can be
observed in the LMSE (Figure 3g,h), further triggering
continuous SEI reconstruction and Li dendrite growth.
Moreover, LSSE exhibits a smaller thickness (22 μm) of the
deposited Li metal layer than that (36 μm) of LMSE (Figure
3f,i), indicating that homogeneous and contact Li deposition

compositions along with the spatial distribution (Figure
S19).44 Specifically, LSSE possesses a higher proportion of F,

S21), suggestive of more favorable anion decomposition owing

barrier. With the increase of etching time, the XPS depth

peak intensity accompanied by a noticeable decrease of the C-

With respect to the S cathode, we aim to probe the
underlying essence of LSSE on restricting the polysulfide
dissolution and shuttling.45,46 The as-prepared 0.02 mM Li2S6
solutions were subjected to the ultraviolet visible (UV−vis)
test. Apparently, peaks assigned to polysulfides appear in
LMSE, yet they are invisible in LSSE (Figure 4a), which
confirms the totally suppressed polysulfide dissolution in LSSE.
An in situ Raman test was carried out to detect the real-time
concentration evolution of polysulfide species in electrolytes
during the discharge process. Due to the tough dissociation
and dissolution of soluble polysulfides in LSSE, polysulfide
concentration is almost unchangeable (Figure 4b). On the
contrary, it exhibits a continuous decrease in LMSE upon the
discharge process (Figure 4c). In this regard, polysulfides are
prone to be generated in the initial discharge stage and directly
converted to solid Li2S products in LSSE, which bypasses the
traditional dissolution−precipitation process. The polysulfide
shuttling will cause irreversible capacity loss and degrade the
actual performance of Li−S batteries, hence shuttling current
was recorded to evaluate the anode kinetics regarding the
parasitic reaction between polysulfides and Li anode. In the
potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT) meas-
urement,47 LMSE exhibits an apparently higher current
response at each voltage step as compared to LSSE (Figure
4d), which originates from more severe polysulfide shuttling in
LMSE with a strong solvation effect. Notably, a peak assigned
to the Li2S nucleation from soluble short-chain polysulfides is
visualized for LMSE at 2.12 V while it cannot be observed in
LSSE, manifesting that LSSE circumvents the polysulfide
dissolution and enables a quasi-solid-state reaction pathway.
As TTE can scarcely coordinate with polysulfides, we focus
on polysulfide speciation and ion motion in two pure solvents.
First, the chemical shift of 1H NMR spectra becomes more
positive after the addition of Li2S6 into DME (Figure 4e). As
such, Li2S6 tends to interact with DME via Li−O binding;
hence, the electron cloud density on H atoms will be
transferred to O atoms and thus cause a deshielding effect.
However, this tendency is opposite in the case of EGDBE and
an upfield shift can be observed. It is reasonable that molecular
interaction between Li2S6 and EGDBE is impeded due to the
high C/O atomic ratio and large steric hindrance of EGDBE,

alleviating the active material loss of S cathode and enhancing
the stability of Li metal anode. Owing to the quasi-solid-phase
sulfur reaction pathway, it is anticipated that Li−S batteries
equipped with LSSE would relieve the dependence on the

a harsh electrolyte usage, which is harmful to pursue high
energy density and pragmatic application. In stark contrast,
LSSE can still maintain a relatively smooth operation at 7 μL
mgS
−1 owing to the relieved dependence on electrolyte usage.
In addition, with respect to the galvanostatic intermittent
titration technique measurement, LSSE exhibits a single-
plateau characteristic in the galvanostatic intermittent titration
technique curve rather than the typical dual voltage plateaus in
the case of LMSE, further verifying that a quasi-solid-state
reaction path can be achieved. Equally importantly, LMSE
suffers from a small yet constant overpotential during the
charging process, which is ascribed to the severe “shuttling
effect” of soluble polysulfides. However, LSSE renders a highly
reversible Li2S decomposition process, which is further
indicative of the effective restriction of polysulfide shuttling.
The aforementioned advantages of LSSE on the Li anode
and S cathode are attributed to the weak solvation effect of
EGDBE, whereas it in turn gives rise to slow reaction kinetics
due to the quasi-solid−solid conversion instead of traditional
solid−liquid−solid reaction in Li−S electrochemistry. To this
end, diphenyl dichalcogenides have emerged as an endogenous
comediator to fine-tune the reaction path and facilitate redox
kinetics, yet the selection criteria and working mechanism
remain unclear.48,49 First, the dichalcogenide bond dissociation
energy of diphenyl disulfide (DPDS), diphenyl diselenide
(DPDSe), and DPDTe was investigated. In this respect,
DPDTe that possesses the lowest dissociation energy was
chosen (Figure 5a), which is anticipated to undergo a facile
dissociation and interaction with sulfur species. The tough
decomposition reaction of the insulating Li2S solid is deemed
to be a key concern in SSE. In this respect, the Gibbs free
energy change of such a Li-ion extraction reaction is lowered
with the introduction of DPDTe no matter in solid or liquid
phase system (Figure 5b), suggesting that DPDTe is conducive
to propelling Li2S oxidation and enhancing redox reversibility.
Figure 5c proposes the possible working principle of DPDTe
on modulating the sulfur redox reaction, where the dissociated
monophenyl telluride plays a crucial role. For one thing, it
spontaneously reacts with Li2S6 to generate a new intermediate
with a high redox mediation ability. For another, it is prone to
interact with Li2S to accelerate Li-ion extraction and Li2S
dissociation (Figure 5d). To evidence this mechanistic
speculation, spectroscopic characterization combined with
electro-kinetic measurement was carried out. The 7Li chemical
shift of Li2S6 changes from −0.07 to −1.40 ppm after the
addition of DPDTe (Figure 5e), which is caused by the
electron transfer from the electronegative Te to Li atoms with
the formation of Te−S bond. In the case of 1H NMR spectra,
the chemical shift exhibits a downfield shift (Figure 5f), which
originates from the reduced electron cloud density on H atoms
due to the electron accumulation on Li atoms of the as-formed
new mediator. Moreover, a peak appearing at −1.24 ppm can
be observed in the 7Li NMR spectrum of Li2S+DPDTe (Figure
5g), and it can be assigned to the newly generated soluble
sulfur species considering that pure Li2S can scarcely be
dissolved in common ether solvents. Meanwhile, an apparent
deshielding effect can be observed with the addition of DPDTe
into Li2S solution for the
1H NMR spectra (Figure 5h),
confirming that the monophenyl telluride intermediate
harnesses a high redox mediation effect to interact with Li2S
and enable Li-ion extraction. PITT test was further employed
to probe the bidirectional sulfur redox chemistry, which is
highly related to the reaction kinetics and actual performance
of Li−S batteries. With respect to the Li2S nucleation reaction,
an apparent peak at 2.08 V is conspicuous under the regulation
of DPDTe while it can hardly be observed in the pure solvent
(Figure 5i), implying that it aids to expedite polysulfide
conversion during sulfur reduction reaction. In parallel,
DPDTe enables a higher current response in the charging
PITT curves (Figure 5j), which is attributed to the outstanding
mediation effect of DPDTe in facilitating Li2S oxidation. In
response, the morphologies of precipitated and dissociated
Li2S were also detected. DPDTe affords a more compact and
uniform Li2S deposition while it becomes sparse and loose
without DPDTe (Figure 5k,l). In addition, the removal of
insulting Li2S products is more complete under the manage-
ment of DPDTe when the charge process is finished (Figure
S25). The aforementioned electro-kinetic results indicate that
DPDTe showcases a considerable potential for accelerating
bidirectional sulfur reaction kinetics and boosting electro-
chemical performance of Li−S batteries in LSSE. In this
respect, the comediator concentration optimization is of
paramount significance (Figure S26), wherein 10 mM
DPDTe was employed to concurrently facilitate the sulfur
reaction kinetics and ensure high compatibility with the Li
anode.

of salt-to-solvent and diluent-to-solvent, which is achieved by
tuning the terminal alkyl chain and weakening the solvation
effect of DME. Multimodal 2D NMR analysis unveils the
reorganization of the Li-ion solvation sheath in LSSE,
rendering anion-derived SEI and essential restriction of
polysulfide dissolution. In this respect, a reliable method is
provided for elucidating the ion motion and solvent exchange
in liquid electrolytes and polysulfide solutions. Li−S batteries
with LSSE follow a quasi-solid-phase reaction path and totally
relieve the dependence on the electrolyte usage. With the aid
of DPDTe as an endogenous comediator to expedite sulfur
redox kinetics, Li−S batteries obtain a steady cyclic perform-
ance over 50 cycles under a lean electrolyte usage of 4 μL
mgS
−1 and a high sulfur loading of 3 mg cm−2. Our work offers
