Catalytic Disproportionation for Suppressing Polysulfide 
Shuttle in Li–S Pouch Cells: Beyond Adsorption Interactions
Yilong Lin, Yecheng Zhou, Sheng Huang, Min Xiao, Dongmei Han, Jiaxiang Qin, 
Shuanjin Wang,* and Yuezhong Meng*
DOI: 10.1002/aenm.202201912
1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are con-
sidered as a promising next-generation 
energy storage system and have been 
investigated intensively in recent years due 
to their high energy density and low cost.[1] 
However, Li–S batteries still encounter a 
huge obstacle for their commercial appli-
cations, which is the well-known “shuttle 
effect” phenomenon. The lithium poly-
sulfides (LiPSs) dissolve in the electrolyte 
and shuttle between the cathode and the 
anode under working conditions, resulting 
in low Coulombic efficiency (CE), serious 
anode corrosion, and hence low cycling 
stability.[2] Researches on alleviating the 
polysulfide “shuttle effect” in Li–S bat-
teries have been reported, focusing on the 
development of conductive framework and 
sulfur adsorbent in cathode.[3] Combining 
efficient anode protective layer design, 
laboratory coin cells with long cycle life 
(2000 cycles), and high specific capacities 
could be obtained.[4]
However, a significant gap exists 
between laboratory coin cells and prac-
tical pouch cells.[5] Before cycling (after electrode activation), a 
second round of sealing is performed for Li–S pouch cells in 
order to remove excess electrolyte and gas, which causes severe 
mass loss of the active sulfur species in cathode. Moreover, the 
output capacity of coin cell is about ≈1–6 mAh while that of 
pouch cell is at Ah level because of the great difference of mass 
loading.[6] So, compared with the laboratory coin cells, hun-
dreds or even thousands of times amplified LiPS dissolution 
and shuttle are in the way of the commercialization of prac-
tical Li–S pouch cells. Many kinds of adsorbents or catalysts, 
such as porous carbons,[7] conductive polymers,[8] and inorganic 
compounds (metal oxide,[9] sulfide,[10] nitride,[11] phosphide,[12] 
carbide.[13] etc.), were composited in the cathode or separator to 
mitigate polysulfide dissolution through heterogeneous physical 
confinement, chemical adsorption, or catalytic transformation. 
The drawbacks resulted from LiPS shuttle would be hidden in 
coin cells due to the low S loadings and high catalyst content. 
For practical Li–S pouch cells, more efficient molecule-level 
homogeneous catalyst is needed to suppress LiPS diffusion and 
improve energy density. The comparison of electrolyte/sulfur 
and catalyst/sulfur ratios of this work with previously reported 
Addressing the lithium polysulfide shuttle is critical for the high-energy-density 
lithium–sulfur pouch cells in practical applications, especially under high 
sulfur loading and lean electrolyte conditions. In contrast to previously 
reported heterogeneous adsorption catalysis within cathode or separator 
with slow catalytic kinetics and limited adsorption area, herein, lithium 
difluorophosphate (LiPO2F2) is demonstrated as a homogeneous catalyst 
in electrolyte which mitigates polysulfide diffusion. The Li–S pouch cell 
with LiPO2F2 in the electrolyte has record-breaking shelving stability of two 
months, significantly improved capacity retention from 37.0% to 81.4% after 
long cycling, and electrical-car-level energy density over 400 Wh kg−1. A min-
imal amount of 1 wt% LiPO2F2 tends to facilitate lithium polysulfide dispro-
portionation on the S/C cathode instead of in the electrolyte, which initiates 
the fast transformation of soluble lithium polysulfide to insoluble solid S8 
and Li2S2/Li2S. The reliable mechanism of polysulfide disproportionation via 
biradicals is further proposed by both density functional theory calculation 
and experiments. To best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report 
on mechanism of polysulfide disproportionation via biradical intermediates. 
It is believed that this new insight into homogeneous catalytic mechanisms 
in electrolytes may pave the way for the commercialization of high-energy-
density Li–S batteries.

It was reported that the soluble inorganic polysulfide could 
be disproportionated in acid aqueous solution.[14] In non-
aqueous Li–S batteries, LiPS disproportionation and dissocia-
tion equilibrium is a well-established and inevitable behavior 
for the conversion of LiPS during the electrochemical reac-
tion.[15] Because of the generation of inactive S in the liquid 
electrolyte or the pores of separator, LiPS disproportionation is 
usually regarded as the key reason for fast capacity attenuation 
of Li–S batteries.[16] However, the sluggish disproportionation 
can be transformed to the advantage of the enhanced electrode 
kinetics when transferring the disproportionation reaction onto 
cathode using efficient catalyst.[17] Nazar’s group[18] reported 
MnO2 as a heterogeneous cathode filler (18.75 wt%) to entrap 
LiPS in Li–S coin cells. MnO2 reacts with the initial formed 
Li2Sn (4 ≤ n  ≤ 8) at the beginning of discharging to produce 
solid thiosulfate at the surface which subsequently dispropor-
tionated LiPS to polythionate complex and insoluble low-order 
polysulfide (Li2S2 or Li2S). However, the heterogeneous dispro-
portionation is still not efficient enough to suppress the serious 
LiPS dissolution in Li–S pouch cells because of the slow cata-
lytic kinetics. What is more, the large amount of catalyst will 
greatly decrease the energy density of pouch cells.
Herein, we report a homogeneous catalyst LiPO2F2, which 
is presented as the soluble additive in the electrolyte (1  m 
LiTFSI, 2  wt% LiNO3 in dioxolane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/
DME, 1:1 by volume)), to initiate LiPS disproportionation in the 
electrolyte in the Li–S pouch cells with 14.5 Ah capacity. Traces 
of LiPO2F2 in the electrolyte effectively carry the dissolved LiPS 
back to cathode through triggering LiPS disproportionation, 
remarkably reducing the mass loss of sulfur species in cathode 
during shelf and cycling test. Li–S pouch cells with LiPO2F2 
in electrolyte exhibit higher energy density over 400 Wh kg−1, 
longer shelving time of 2 months, and better cycling stability 
of 81.4% as compared with 37.0% of the cell without LiPO2F2 
after 50 cycles, especially under high sulfur contents, high 
areal sulfur loading, and lean electrolyte conditions. Besides, 
different from the reported physical/chemical adsorption or 
heterogeneous catalysis mechanism for mitigating LiPS disso-
lution and shuttle, we propose a biradical disproportionation 
mechanism for the catalytic effect of LiPO2F2 based on den-
sity functional theory (DFT) calculations, which explained the 
experimental phenomena and battery improvement perfectly.

electrodes being fully wetted by the electrolyte. However, the 
electrode wetting process is accompanied by serious LiPS dis-
solution into the electrolyte, leading to the active S loss and 
Li corrosion. In this study, LiPO2F2 that is catalytically dispro-
portionate to LiPS was used as an electrolyte additive for LiPS 
diffusion inhibition. Figure 1a shows the open circuit voltage

2.2. Improved Electrochemical Performance of Li–S Pouch Cells
Li–S pouch cells with high areal sulfur loading of 5 mg cm−2 were 
assembled to evaluate the effect of LiPO2F2 on the energy den-
sity. As shown in Figure 2a, LiPO2F2 cell delivered higher mass 
energy densities (410 Wh kg−1) than Control cell (367 Wh kg−1). 
The increased energy originates from LiPS dissolution inhibi-
tion and reaction kinetics acceleration by LiPO2F2. Specially, 
the faster reaction kinetics was illustrated by the larger current 
and less overpotential of LiPO2F2 cell in CV profiles (Figure S4, 
Supporting Information). The voltage profiles versus capacity 
and specific capacity clearly demonstrate the improvement in 
the cell capacity after the addition of LiPO2F2 (Figure S5, Sup-
porting Information). To understand the influence of LiPO2F2 
additive on cycling stability, Li/S pouch cells with 3 mg cm−2 of 
areal sulfur loading were tested at 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA g−1). 
Compared to Control cell, significant improvement of capacity 
retention (from 37.0% to 81.4%) is observed for Li/S pouch cell 
with optimal 1% LiPO2F2 (Figure 2b and Figure S6a, Supporting 
Information). The cycling performance and selected discharge/
charge curves at 0.2 C are shown in Figure 2c,e. The LiPO2F2 
cell delivered a high specific capacity of 740 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C 
with capacity retentions of 77.8% after 70 cycles (Figure S6b, Sup-
porting Information). The high average Coulombic efficiency 
above 96.6% is also attributed to the low self-discharge rate of 
the LiPO2F2 cell and LiPS shuttling suppression by LiPO2F2. The 
higher capacities were achieved at different current densities 
from 0.02 to 0.3 C in LiPO2F2 cell than Control cell (Figure S7, 
Supporting Information). In the coin cell, the improved capacity 
reached 561 mAh g−1 upon adding LiPO2F2 after 400 cycles at 
0.5 C (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Figure  2d displays 
the cycling performance of the pouch cell with 1% LiPO2F2 in 
electrolyte after 60 days resting, showing that the LiPO2F2 cell 
still delivered high and stable capacity of about 1000 mAh at 
0.05 C, which is close to the value before resting.
The cells were disassembled after 50 cycles at 0.05 C and 
the appearances of their cathode and anode are shown in the 
inset of Figure  2b. The sulfur cathode from LiPO2F2 cell was 
yellow because of more elemental sulfur on the surface, and 
the lithium anode from LiPO2F2 cell shows brighter metal 
luster than the control one. This indicates that the sulfur spe-
cies have been confined in the sulfur cathode side by LiPO2F2 
additive, resulting in the slighter corrosion of Li anode. For S/C 
cathode, the spatial distribution of solid sulfur species during 1st 
cycle in LiPO2F2 cell is different from Control cell (Figure S9, 
Supporting Information), which is better for subsequent cycling 
due to higher S utilization. After 50 cycles, the rough surface 
can be observed on the S/C cathode of Control cell, while shaggy 
products (confirmed as sulfur species) are on the surface of the 
cathode from LiPO2F2 cell (Figure 2f,g). The cathode of LiPO2F2 
cell has 38.53 wt% sulfur content on the surface, which is much 
higher than that (25.49  wt%) of the control one, showing that 
the active sulfur loss has been restricted. We infer that LiPO2F2 
reacts with LiPS to form some sulfur species on the cathode sur-
face which disturb the detection of conductive carbon. As shown 
in Figure  2h,i, the cycled Li anode of Control cell has been 
corroded seriously due to serious LiPS shuttling and a large 
amount of S-containing products (over 47  wt% element ratio) 
can be found on its surface, and even the current collector was 
exposed as indicated by the existence of Cu element (Figure S10, 
Supporting Information). Comparatively, a flat and dense anode 
surface is observed for LiPO2F2 cell, and the S element accounts 
for only 26.79 wt% and no Cu element is detected. Even after 
20 cycles, LiPO2F2 cell still exhibited high OCV retention during 
96  h shelf time (Figure S11, Supporting Information). From 
above discussion, we believe that the elevated electrochem-
ical performance of Li–S pouch cells can be attributed to the

2.3. Disproportionation of LiPS on Cathode
Two visual V-type cells were assembled with the same sulfur 
cathode and Li anode, which are named as V-control cell and 
V-LiPO2F2 cell, respectively (Figure S12, Supporting Informa-
tion). After discharging for 5 min by lightening a LED lamp, 
the V-type cells were kept at open-circuit state for 80 min. Both 
cells were tested synchronously and monitored by a video 
recorder (Movie S1 and Figure S12, Supporting Information). 
The additive LiPO2F2 could fantastically “carry” the dissolved 
LiPSs back to sulfur cathode and inhibit their shuttling toward 
lithium anode during the test.
To further study the electrochemical transportation of 
LiPSs by LiPO2F2, an electrolyte (the same as “Control-240h” 
in Figure  1b) taken from the Control cell after 240  h resting 
test was used to assemble a V-type Li–S cell. This electrolyte 
contained large amounts of LiPSs and shows very deep brown 
color. After adding 2% LiPO2F2 in the electrolyte, OCV of 
the cell was recorded and the appearance was monitored for 
240 h (Figure 3a and Figure S13, Supporting Information). 
The electrolyte with 2% LiPO2F2 in bottle was also monitored 
synchronously for comparison. It can be seen that color of the 
electrolyte became faded clearly and the surface of cathode 
was covered by a layer of new formed solid precipitates. After 
standing for longer time as 240 h, the electrolyte had very light 
color, representing very low concentration of LiPSs, and there 
were much more sediments on the surface of cathode and

To prove disproportionation reaction triggered by LiPO2F2 
as above discussed, the disproportionation products were veri-
fied as follows. After 240 h standing test, the S/C working elec-
trode in V-type Li–S cell was taken out and rinsed with DME 
to remove the soluble compounds. Then, the deposits on the 
surface of electrode were analyzed by SEM and elemental 
mappings. As shown in Figure 3d–g, compared with the fresh 
cathode, a large number of lumpy particles with sulfur species 
derived from the dissolved LiPSs are observed. The color of the 
electrolyte in the bottle (Figure 3a) also faded slowly accompa-

the disproportionation reaction of LiPSs could be also triggered 
effectively by LiPO2F2. It is worth noting that the electrolyte 
color of bottle faded more slowly than that of V-type cell with

precipitates in the bottle were analyzed to avoid the interference 
from the S/C electrode of V-type cell. The Li 1s and S 2p spectra

The mechanism of LiPS dissolution inhibition regarding above 
electrolyte discoloration and precipitation production upon cat-
alyst addition was studied by a dissolution-resting experience, 
which resulted from the catalytic disproportionation reaction 
of LiPSs instead of the reduction in polysulfide solubility after 
adding LiPO2F2 (Figure S18, Supporting Information).
As shown vividly in Figure  3h,i, during the catalytic dis-
proportionation process, the LiPO2F2 catalyst carried the dis-
solved LiPSs back to cathode unceasingly and thus protected 
the lithium anode. For the resting formation of practical Li–S 
pouch cell in Figure  1, due to the reaction of soluble S8 and 
Li, continuous LiPSs dissolved into electrolyte and caused the 
decreased cell voltage and capacity. As a result, the dissolved 
LiPSs in electrolyte will be removed during degassing pro-
cedure after formation or spontaneously disproportionated 
in the electrolyte or separator. Adding LiPO2F2, the inevitably 
produced LiPSs in electrolyte were selectively “carried” to 
cathode and disproportionated into the insoluble S8 and Li2S2/
Li2S, which inhibited the further formation of soluble LiPSs. 
During the resting formation process, LiPO2F2 addition trig-
gered a spontaneous LiPS disproportionation and inhibited 
severe S dissolution into the electrolyte. Furthermore, the loss 
of active LiPSs during degassing process and secondary sealing 
would be reduced effectively. Apparently, the calculated specific 
capacity and energy density were promoted because more active 
sulfur species were kept in the cell for cycling test by sacrificing 
few Gibbs free energy during the initial spontaneous dispropor-
tionation of LiPSs.
2.4. Calculated Disproportionation Route via Biradicals
In the reported adsorption catalysis model, the adsorption 
energy between catalysts and LiPS was usually calculated to 
evaluate the catalytic behavior, which is in accordance with the 
reported mechanism of LiPS adsorption in experiments.[13a,23] 
However, in our catalytic disproportionation model, the origin 
of disproportionation and the disproportionation route through 
biradical are proposed.
Taking Li2S8 as an example of LiPS (Li2S6 and Li2S4 can be 
also disproportionated, Li2S8 is the initial discharging product), 
when the oxidation states of S are highly consistent, Li2S8 will 
be stable, but with the added catalyst contributing to change 
the state of oxidation, Li2S8 will be disproportionated (e.g., LiPS 
disproportionation can be triggered by H+ in the aqueous solu-
tion,[14] according to the Equation (1)).

The specific reaction paths are displayed in Figure  4c, at 
the beginning of discharge or self-discharge, Li2S8 is pro-

With the assistance of LiPO2F2, Li2S8 will easily transform to

and form higher order of LiPSs until insoluble S8 forms (such 
as ·S4Li–C–LiS6· + Li2S8 = ·S2Li–C–LiS2· + Li2S6 + S8). They 
may also despoil sulfur fragments from higher order of LiPSs 
and transform them to lower order of LiPSs until insoluble 
Li2S2 (such as ·S2Li–C–LiS2· + Li2S4 = ·S2Li–C–LiS4· + Li2S2). 
These two chain transfer reactions will continuously consume 
LiPSs and transform them into unsolvable S8 and Li2S2. It is 
worth noted that the radical reaction proceeds accompanied by 
electron transfer, so the self-discharge in Li–S battery will pro-
mote the radical reaction kinetics. Therefore, the LiPS dispro-
portionation rate is faster in V-type cells than in bottle due to 
the existence of voltage bias (Figure 3a).
According to the different depth of discharge, the birad-
ical transfer reactions can be split into three stages, in which 
Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4 are the consumed reactants, respectively 
(Figure S27, Supporting Information). Among the possible 
reaction paths in disproportionation reactions including two 
Li2Sn molecules, the most energetic favorable reaction path 
from two Li2S8 requires a free energy raise of 0.696 eV (Table S7 
and Figure S28, Supporting Information). In this reaction, the 
most energetically favorable reaction path is achieved by biradi-
cals with only 0.395  eV of rate-controlling step. In fact, the 
energy of forming solid products is lower than that of the cal-
culated precipitated molecules (Figure S29, Supporting Infor-
mation). Therefore, the free energy penalty of these reactions 
should be lower as well. In addition, the possible reaction paths 
of radical initiation and transfer during Li2S8-rich (Stage  I), 
Li2S6-rich (Stage II), and Li2S4-rich (Stage III) environment are 
also compared to understand their feasibility (Figure S30, Sup-
porting Information).
The energy of radical transfer increases gradually from 
Stage I to III. Due to the strong binding energy of LiPSs and 
LiPO2F2, the bonded LiPSs cannot be changed to S8 and Li2S2 
precipitates. When the concentration of unbonded LiPSs 
decrease or even all are consumed, the collision probability of 
highly instable biradicals increases, and the disproportiona-
tion reaction still occurs (Figure S31, Supporting Information). 
Accompanied by the continuous consumption of biradicals, 
their reaction probability would be lowered to a certain extent, 
while traces of biradicals ·SxLi–C–LiSn−x· will transform to be 
C–Li2Sn and become dynamically balanced (Figure S32, Sup-
porting Information). Consequently, from radical initiation to 
termination, a six-step reaction path is needed for the Li2Sn 
disproportionation. During the radical transfer loop, the initial 
reactant determines the starting point of this cycle (Table S8, 
Supporting Information).
To verify the proposed mechanism of biradicals formation 
in catalytic process, besides LiPO2F2, catalytic effect of other 
commercial lithium salts in lithium batteries including LiFSI, 
LiBOB, LiClO4, and LiPF6, were also explored by voltage moni-
toring of the Li–S pouch cells and the corresponding biradical 
initiation energy was also calculated (Figures S33–S35, Sup-
porting Information). The consistency of experiment and cal-
culation results indicates that the lithium salt additives like 
LiBOB, LiPO2F2, and LiPF6, as effective catalysts, can facilitate 
LiPS disproportionation via biradicals paths. Accordingly, the 
above ideas will provide guidance to optimize catalysts/electro-
lyte for the commercialization of Li–S batteries.
2.5. Experimental Verification of Radical Character
Species with an unpaired electron generate a strong signal in 
the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrum.[15] In 
DOL/DME–Li2Sn solution system, there is little radical signal in 
EPR spectra (Figure S36, Supporting Information), indicating 
the trace of radical in DOL/DME-based electrolyte. Similar view-
point has been reported by Nazar’ group.[26] However, low con-
centration of radical anions under equilibrium conditions can 
be well detected by Raman spectroscopy due to the high polariz-
ability of radical anions at the two-coordinate sulfur atoms.[15] As 
shown in Figure 5a, in addition to Li2Sn species, an obvious rad-
ical signal of instable LiS3· at 535 cm−1 appears in the Raman 
spectroscopy of DOL/DME–Li2S6 solution.[27] With the addition 
of 1% LiPO2F2, the peak signals of Li2Sn and LiS3· decrease and 
new peaks corresponding to sulfur at 156, 221, and 473 cm−1 are 
identified,[28] indicating the highly oxidative LiPO2F2–Li2Sn oxi-
dize the LiPS substrates, leading to the disproportionation of 
LiPSs and thus reduction of radical concentration.
Sulfur radical ions S⋅−
n (n  = 2–4) formed from solutions of 
alkali metal polysulfide in high donor solvents have been well 
documented.[29] The high solubility of LiPS in high donor elec-
trolytes is associated with different disproportionation and dis-
sociation routes of LiPS, these routes are unavailable in low 
donor electrolytes.[30] DMSO as an electron-pair donor solvent 
was used to enhance the stability of sulfur radical anions in 
this work. As expected, radical signals could be apparently cap-
tured in DMSO–Li2S4 and DMSO–Li2S6 solution through EPR, 
in addition, absorbance at 617  nm assigned to LiS3·[30] also 
appeared in their UV–vis spectra (Figures S37 and S38, Sup-

3. Conclusion
In summary, this work proposes a homogeneous catalyst 
LiPO2F2 in the Li–S battery electrolyte to promote the dispro-
portionation of LiPSs on the S/C cathode for the first time. The 
disproportionated products are sulfur and Li2S2/Li2S. The cata-
lytic mechanism of LiPO2F2 addition is illustrated in detail by 
the high consistency of the experimental phenomena and theo-
retical analysis, which is proved to complete by biradical inter-
mediates initiation, transfer, and termination. With the help of 
LiPO2F2, the energy barrier of radical initiation is substantially 
reduced and hence the whole disproportionation process is pro-
moted. Fast conversion reactions decrease the retention time 
of soluble LiPSs and kinetic energy barrier for nucleation of 
Li2S/Li2S2, and then reduce the active materials loss and accel-
erate the electrodes redox kinetics. Accordingly, we finally 
designed and fabricated a Li–S pouch cell with a record-
breaking shelf life of 2 months, significantly enhanced cyclic 
stability (capacity retention increased from 37.0% to 81.4%), and 
a high energy density of over 400 Wh kg−1.
