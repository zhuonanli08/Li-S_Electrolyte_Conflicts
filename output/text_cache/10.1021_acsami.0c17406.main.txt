ABSTRACT: Uncontrollable dendrite growth and low Coulombic eﬃciency are the two main
obstacles that hinder the application of rechargeable Li metal batteries. Here, an optimized
amount of potassium hexaﬂuorophosphate (KPF6, 0.01 M) has been added into the 2 M
LiTFSI/ether-based electrolyte to improve the cycling stability of lithium−sulfur (Li−S)
batteries. Due to the synergistic eﬀect of self-healing electrostatic shield eﬀect from K+ cations
and the LiF-rich solid electrolyte interphases derived from PF6
−anions, the KPF6 additive
enables a high Li Coulombic eﬃciency of 98.8% (1 mA cm−2 of 1 mAh cm−2). The symmetrical
Li cell can achieve a stable cycling performance for over 200 cycles under a high Li utilization up
to 33.3%. Meanwhile, the polysulﬁde shuttle has been restrained due to the higher
concentration of the LiTFSI in the electrolyte. As a result, the assembled Li−S full cell
displays excellent capacity retention with only 0.25% decay per cycle in the ﬁnal electrolyte. Our
work oﬀers a smart approach to improve both the anode and cathode performance by the electrolyte modiﬁcation of rechargeable
Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: Li metal anode, self-healing electrostatic shield, LiF-rich interphase, dendrite suppression, Li−S battery
1. INTRODUCTION
Although rechargeable lithium ion batteries with carbon
anodes have achieved great success in manufactured market,
the exploration of dendrite-free Li metal batteries (LMBs) has
never stopped due to the growing pursuit for high-energy-
density applications, including grid storage devices and
electrical vehicles.1−3 Due to a low electrochemical potential
of −3.04 V (vs standard hydrogen electrode) and a high
theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 3860 mAh g−1, the light Li metal
is considered as the ultimate anode, and the secondary LMBs
have recently received considerable attention as the next-
generation energy storage systems (like Li−S and Li−O2
batteries).4−6 However, the intrinsically active nature of Li
metal always triggers the continuous reaction with the
corrosive electrolyte, leading to the unstable solid electrode
interphase (SEI) and the low Coulombic eﬃciency.7−9 The
drastic volume ﬂuctuation of Li metal during cycling further
aggravates the formed SEI to become even more fragile and
uneven-developed. Another challenging problem is the
inevitable growth of Li dendrites due to heterogeneous Li
ion transport, which raises serious safety concerns even
including ﬁre/explosion.10−12 Furthermore, some loosely
stacked dendrites can even break away from the conductive
substrates during discharging, generating the accumulated
“dead Li” without capacity contribution anymore.13−15 All of
these problems sharply shorten the lifespan and hinder the
practical implementation of LMBs.
To restrain Li dendrite growth and promote long lifespan of
the working LMBs, a stable SEI to protect Li metal anode is
necessary, and considerable research eﬀorts have been devoted
recently. One strategy is to build an artiﬁcial SEI on the Li
metal surface by various coating strategies, mainly including
spray quenching,16,17 gas−solid reactions,18−20 soaking-based
pretreatments,21−24 and other physical methods.25,26 But such
an SEI will take the edge oﬀits function once local defects or
breaks are generated during the long-term charging/discharg-
ing. On the other hand, electrolyte modiﬁcation is also
considered as a smart choice to prolong the life span of Li
metal anodes. Sacriﬁcial additives such as LiNO3,
27−29
ﬂuoroethylene carbonate (FEC),30,31 and vinylene carbonate
(VC)32 have been demonstrated with high eﬃciency to
improve the SEI stability and restrain the Li dendrite
formation, which can in situ repair the broken SEI but are
consumed constantly during the long-term cycling. Meanwhile,
Zhang et al. proposed a novel mechanism called “self-healing
electrostatic shield” to alter Li deposition behavior.33
According to the Nernst equation, the selected alkaline cations
(Cs+ or Ru+) with ultralow concentrations exhibit an eﬀective

In this respect, we ﬁnd that a trace amount of KPF6 (0.01
M) can eﬀectively manipulate the solution chemistry in the
LiTFSI/ether-based electrolyte, generating a LiF-rich SEI on
the Li metal surface as well as promoting the electrostatic
shield at the same time. Compared with the SEI formed in the
KPF6-free electrolyte, the LiF-enriched SEI derived in the
KPF6-containing electrolyte shows higher interfacial energy
with Li metal and enhanced mechanical strength, thus
suﬃciently promoting the dense Li electrodeposition. As a
result, the symmetrical Li cell exhibits a high Coulombic
eﬃciency and can still display a stable cycling performance at a
high Li utilization. When coupling the sulfur cathode with
limited Li anode, superior capacity retention has also been
maintained in our designed electrolyte.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%, Sigma-
Aldrich) and LiNO3 (99.99%, Aladdin) reagent were ﬁrst dried in a
vacuum oven at 100 °C for 48 h, and then the trace water in
anhydrous 1,3-dioxolan (DOL, Aladdin) and 1,1-dimethoxyethane
(DME, Aladdin) was eliminated by the activated 5 Å molecular sieves.
The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving preweighed LiTFSI in
DME:DOL (1:1 by volume) solvent with or without an KPF6 additive
(99.98%, Aladdin) in an argon-ﬁlled glove box. Herein, a Karl−
Fischer moisture meter was also used to measure the water content in
the electrolytes, which was conﬁrmed below 20 ppm.
The sulfur cathode was fabricated by the same method in our
previous work,34 which combined with a simple hydrothermal
treatment and calcination process, followed by sulfuration by the
supercritical ﬂuid method in CO2. The slurry was obtained by mixing
the as-prepared PGCF/Ni/S composite cathode with polyvinylidene
ﬂuoride (PVDF) and carbon black (8:1:1 by weight) in N-methyl-2-
pyrrolidone (NMP). Then, it was coated on Al foil and dried at 60 °C
for 24 h using a vacuum oven to obtain the ﬁnal cathode.
A ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Hitachi
S4800) was used for the characterization of Li anode morphologies.
The crystal structure of the SEI layer was measured by a transmission
electron microscope (TEM, JEOL 2100F) operated at 120.0 kV. X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientiﬁc ESCALAB
250Xi) measurement was performed with an Al Kα excitation source.
All the samples were transferred via a self-made container with pure
argon to avoid oxidation. The contact angle of electrolytes to the
separator was measured using a contact angle meter (SL200B, Solon
Tech.).
All the CR2025 type coin cells were assembled in the glove box
ﬁlled with argon using a separator Celgard 2325, and the Li foil was
controlled with an ultrathin thickness of 50 μm (China Energy

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Here, the traditional ether-based electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI
dissolved in 1,3-dioxolan (DOL)/1,1-dimethoxyethane
(DME) with 1 wt % LiNO3, was chosen as the baseline
electrolyte (denoted as 1 M LiTFSI). To increase Li salt
concentration and decrease free solvent molecules in the
electrolyte, a higher concentrated electrolyte consisting of 2 M
LiTFSI in DOL/DME with 2 wt % LiNO3 was also prepared
(denoted as 2 M LiTFSI). It is generally considered that higher
salt concentration helps to generate an anion-derived SEI with
higher inorganic compounds, such as the 10 M LiFSI in
dimethyl carbonate (DMC),37 7 M LiFSI in FEC,38 4.2 M
LiTFSI in acetonitrile,39 and so forth. But those super-
concentrated electrolytes also hinder their practical applica-
tions mainly due to the high cost and high viscosity, which are
comparably less troublesome in the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte.
Meanwhile, the 0.01 M KPF6 additive has been further added
into the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte (denoted as LiTFSI/KPF6).
The ionic conductivities of the three electrolytes were
measured as shown in Figure S1a (Supporting Information).
The ionic conductivity of the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte reaches
11.91 mS cm−1 without signiﬁcant decrease compared to that
of the traditional 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte (12.61 mS cm−1).
With the addition of 0.01 M KPF6, the ionic conductivity of
the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte still maintains as high as 11.77
mS cm−1. In addition, the contact angle of the electrolyte to
the separator remains almost the same in the 2 M LiTFSI and
LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolytes (Figure S1b), indicating that such a
trace amount of KPF6 has little impact on the physical
properties of the electrolyte.
It is acknowledged that Li deposition is a nucleation and
growth process driven by electrochemical potential. Figure 1a−
c shows the potential responses versus capacity during Li
plating on bare Cu foil in diﬀerent electrolytes at a current
density of 0.5 mA cm−2. A voltage dip clearly appears at the
beginning of electroplating, followed by a ﬂat voltage plateau.
Also, the value of the voltage dip bottom is deﬁned as a
parameter to overcome the plating resistance for Li-ion
diﬀusion and nucleation.40 In the coin cell with the 1 M
LiTFSI electrolyte, the nucleation onset potential is as high as
106.0 mV (Figure 1a). When the LiTFSI concentration
increases to 2 M, the value dramatically decreases to 76.0 mV
(Figure 1b), revealing that the higher salt concentration is
beneﬁcial for more rapid ionic diﬀusion due to Li ion
enrichment. It exhibits 72.9 mV with no obvious change in the
modiﬁed LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte (Figure 1c). However, it is
noteworthy that metallic Li growth overpotential (deﬁned by
the voltage plateau) diﬀers signiﬁcantly, which is mainly mass-
transfer-controlled overpotential associated with the nature of
the SEI and electrolyte.41 The abundant Li ion supplement in
the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte helps to lower the growth
overpotential to 36.3 mV (Figure 1b). The growth over-
potential is further reduced to ∼17.2 mV in the LiTFSI/KPF6
electrolyte. Considering the similar ion conductivity and Li salt
concentration in both 2 M LiTFSI and LiTFSI/KPF6
electrolytes, the further decrease of growth overpotential
should be attributed to the more conductive and stable SEI
formed in the modiﬁed electrolyte.
Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) means the ratio of the amount of
stripped Li to that plated on Cu of which a high value indicates
the less irreversible reaction between metallic Li and liquid
electrolyte. Herein, the CE of Li−Cu cells with diﬀerent
electrolytes was tested at the current density of 1 mA cm−2
with a capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. For the cell with the 1 M
LiTFSI electrolyte, CE is about 95% in the initial 10 cycles.
Then, it ﬂuctuates between 85 and 95% in the following 50
cycles and drops to only 57.2% after 85 cycles (Figure 1d),
which reveals consecutive side reactions between Li metal and
electrolyte due to the fragile SEI. By sharp comparison, CE of
the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte stabilizes at around 98.7% for 60
cycles, while a gradual degradation occurs after 80 cycles as
displayed in Figure 1d. Inspiringly, the cell with the modiﬁed
LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte exhibits a high CE of exceeding
98.8% after 20 cycles and maintains stable throughout 200
cycles (Figure 1d), which clearly validates that the KPF6
additive can eﬀectively stabilize the SEI and suppress the
side reaction between metallic Li and electrolyte as a result.
The overpotential between the plating and stripping plateau in
Li−Cu cells is also recorded as shown in Figure S2. The
routine 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte delivers a voltage hysteresis of
nearly 29 mV in the 20th cycles, which gradually increases to
34 mV in the 50th cycle. In contrast, the voltage hysteresis for
the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte maintains a low and stable value
of only 20 mV in the whole 150 cycles, and the curves overlap
as shown in Figure S2b. Considering the lower bulk ionic
conductivity than that of the diluted 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte,
the lower overpotential in the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte reveals
that the interfacial resistance for Li ion diﬀusion has been
eﬀectively reduced due to the formation of a more ionic-
conductive and stable SEI.
To obtain a more insightful comparison of Li deposition
morphology, SEM images of the electroplated Li metal on a
bare Cu substrate are shown in Figure 1e−g. A rough surface
with abundant porosities and needle-like dendritic Li (enlarged
inside) is observed in the deposited Li layer in the 1 M LiTFSI
electrolyte (Figure 1e), which reveals the inhomogeneous Li
deposition and unavoidable reactions in the traditional
electrolyte. For the Li layer deposited in the 2 M LiTFSI
electrolyte, despite less Li dendritic formation is discernible on
the electrode surface, the bulk noodle-like Li is still covered
with sporadically dispersed porous structure (Figure 1f). This
phenomenon indicates that although the 2 M LiTFSI
electrolyte is beneﬁcial for bulk Li growth, the weak SEI
cannot completely shield Li deposition from the electrolyte
corrosion, resulting in the rough clusters on the electrode
surface. While with the addition of KPF6, an evident
improvement in Li morphology without any nanoﬁlaments

To further investigate the eﬀect of KPF6 on the formation of
SEI, theoretical calculations based on density functional theory
(DFT) were conducted. The lowest unoccupied molecular
orbital (LUMO) of solvents and lithium salts implemented in
this work are shown in Figure 2a. The lower LUMO
corresponds to the higher reduction potential. Therefore,
KPF6 with the lowest LUMO of −1.79 eV tends to be
electrochemically reduced ﬁrst, generating PFx anions by
breaking the P−F bond. The resultant free F−combines with
Li+ quickly to form LiF, which is less soluble in the
electrolyte42,43 and enriches LiF in the SEI. LiF has been
accepted as an excellent SEI component for Li metal anode,
which has a high interfacial energy (73.28 meV Å−2) and weak
bonding to Li, and thus can regulate the lateral Li deposition
and minimize the volume change during cycling.44 As a result,
abundant LiF with a high band gap of 10.8 eV,45 together with
Li2O, Li3N, and LiNxOy subsequently reduced from LiNO3,
can eﬀectively passivate the Li metal surface and depress the
decomposition of organic solvents with higher LUMO.
Meanwhile, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted
in a voltage range of 0−2.5 V (vs Li/Li+) to verify the
formation process of SEI. As shown in Figure S4, due to the
reduction of LiNO3 with a lower LUMO, the onset voltage of
the ﬁrst reduction peak (marked by the black dots) appears as
a right shift in the 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte with LiNO3
compared with the diluted electrolyte without any additive.
A new reduction peak at around 1.60 V appears in the CV
curve of the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte (Figure 2b) representing
the more prominent decomposition of LiNO3, which is
consistent with previous reports.46 Moreover, the ﬁrst cathodic
peak shifts to a higher potential of around 1.64 V in the cell
with the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte (Figure 2b), which should
originate from the preferential decomposition of tiny KPF6 as
revealed by the lower LUMO energies.

behavior through the self-healing electrostatic mechanism as
mentioned above. Note that this mechanism eﬀects only when
the eﬀective reduction potential of K+ is lower than that of Li+.

The cycling stability of symmetrical Li cells was further
compared in 1 M LiTFSI and LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolytes.
Figure 3c shows the long-term Li plating/stripping voltage
proﬁles at a high current density of 4 mA cm−2 with a capacity
loading of 2 mAh cm−2. Considering the ultrathin Li metal
anode with a thickness of 50 μm (∼5 μm-thick Li foil
corresponds to a theoretical areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2), Li
utilization reaches 20% here. The cell with the LiTFSI/KPF6
electrolyte displays a smooth and stable voltage proﬁle with a
small overpotential of around 20 mV for over 300 cycles,
whereas the overpotential of cell with the 1 M LiTFSI
electrolyte suﬀers irregular ﬂuctuation and increases to about
200 mV after 160 cycles (inset in Figure 3c), implying the large
interfacial resistance, which may result from the thick SEI layer
and the accumulation of inactive dead Li. In addition, the
voltage proﬁle of the symmetrical cell with 0.02 M KPF6 is
shown in Figure S6. The large overpotential manifests the
increased cell impedance, which is in accordance with the
decreased exchange current density as discussed above. Thus,
0.01 M KPF6 is the optimal amount. To further compare the
electrochemical performance under higher Li utilization,
limited Li anodes with a capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 were ﬁrst
obtained by electrodepositing Li on Cu substrates at 0.5 mA
cm−2 for 6 h. Then, the galvanostatic Li plating/stripping tests
at 1 mA cm−2 with a capacity loading of 1 mAh cm−2
(corresponding to a relatively high Li utilization of 33.3%)
were conducted with the electrodeposited Li anodes in
diﬀerent electrolytes. As displayed in Figure 3d, the over-
potential in the cell with the 1 M LiTFSI electrolyte ﬂuctuates
violently and increases to even larger than 500 mV sometimes,
which roots in the unstable SEI and uncontrollable dendrite
growth. On the contrary, for the cell with the KPF6 additive,
the overpotential gradually decreases in the ﬁrst 20 cycles and
maintains constant without any increase in the following
charge/discharge processes. The detailed voltage proﬁles
between the 100th cycle and 110th cycle are compared in
the inset in Figure 3d, where voltage polarization in the cell
with the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte (∼23 mV) is even less than
one tenth of that (∼250 mV) in the control electrolyte. Such
dramatic diﬀerence further demonstrates that the steady Li
deposition/dissolution with low polarization and outstanding
stability has been achieved in our designed electrolyte.
To reveal the speciﬁc composition of SEI on the Li metal
surface, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was
performed after Li metal is stripped/plated at 1 mA cm−2 of
1 mAh cm−2 for 10 cycles. As shown in Figure 4a−c, the peaks
in C 1s spectra can be mainly classiﬁed into three parts: one for
the adventitious carbon or hydrocarbon C/H located in 284.8
eV, one for the organic carbon oxides (286.5 and 288.7 eV),
and a third for the lithium carbonate at 290.1 eV. The signal at
∼286.6 eV corresponds to CH3OLi (RC−O) from the
decomposition of organic solvents.50,51 Compared with the
two KPF6-free electrolytes, the decreased C 1s signal in Figure
4c proves that the 0.01 M KPF6 additive can suppress the
decomposition of organic solvents. Thus, the less protective
organic components are reduced in the resulted SEI. For the F
1s spectrum in Figure 4d−f, the peak at 688.9 eV can be
assigned to the C−F component that originated from TFSI−
anions, and the peak at 685.1 eV belongs to LiF decomposed
from the F-containing anions. In spite of the improved LiTFSI
concentration, the weaker F peaks in Figure 4e imply less
decomposition of TFSI−anions owing to the more prominent
decomposition of LiNO3 in the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte with 2
wt % LiNO3,
52 which is also evidenced by the slight increase of
N 1s signal in the 2 M LiTSFI electrolyte compared to the 1 M
LiTFSI electrolyte (Figure S7). In contrast, the SEI formed in
the LiTFSI/KPF6 electrolyte exhibits a stronger LiF peak and a
weaker C−F peak (Figure 4f). Moreover, a small peak
attributed to LixPFy emerges at 687.8 eV, which indicates
that PF6
−anions have participated in the formation of SEI and
account for the signiﬁcantly increased LiF species. Hence, the
combination of C 1s and F 1s spectra reveals that 0.01 M KPF6
not only eﬀectively reduces the organic components in the SEI
but also promotes the formation of more inorganic LiF, which
is consistent with the DFT calculation results.
To thoroughly reveal the components of SEI in the LiTFSI/
KPF6 electrolyte, XPS depth spectra are collected. The C 1s
signal drops sharply during etching (Figure S8a), implying the
slump of organic components with depth. Inspiringly, LiF
always exists and dominates the components of SEI after 2 min
of etching, which are conﬁrmed by the F 1s and Li 1s depth
proﬁles in Figure S8b,c. Thus, the preferential decomposition
of KPF6 at a higher potential promotes more inorganic
components especially LiF in the inner layer close to Li metal,
while the organic components that originated from the
reduction of organic solvents mainly distribute in the outer
layer. It has been widely accepted that LiF has extremely high
interface energy to Li metal and high mechanical strength, thus
serving as a promising interfacial component to restrain Li
dendrite growth. In addition, K 2p signal is undetectable
throughout the SEI layer (Figure S8d), which conﬁrms that
there is neither codeposition of K and Li in the modiﬁed
electrolyte nor K-containing species in the resultant SEI.
Therefore, the K+ cations only function to create the
electrostatic shield on the tip of plated Li metal, thus
eﬀectively regulating the uniform Li deposition at the same
time.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area
electron diﬀraction (SAED) were also combined to reveal the
structure of the SEI layer formed in the LiTFSI/KPF6
electrolyte. The TEM image clearly validates the classical
mosaic structure of the SEI layer, with diﬀerent shades of
nanoparticles randomly dispersed among the layer (Figure 4g).
The ultraﬁne nanocrystals with grain sizes of 5−10 nm are
veriﬁed more clearly in the magniﬁed high-resolution TEM
image (Figure 4h). The lattice spacing conﬁrms the existence
of LiF and Li3N in the SEI layer (Figure S9), which is highly
consistent with the XPS results. Moreover, particles with a
lattice spacing of 2.69 Å belonging to Li2O, a byproduct of
LiNO3 decomposition, are also presented in Figure S9. The
polycrystalline nature of inorganic nanoparticles, which plays
an important role in improving the high ionic conductivity of
the SEI layer, is also demonstrated by the SAED pattern (inset
in Figure 4h). Furthermore, a uniform element distribution of
C, F, and S in the SEI is also demonstrated by the EDS
mapping images (Figure 4i). Hence, inorganic components are
mixed and dispersed homogeneously among the SEI layer, thus
generating abundant heterogeneous grain boundaries for the
fast diﬀusion of Li ions. Taking the analysis above, the SEI
layer in the KPF6-containing electrolyte is expected to
reinforce the uniform Li ion penetration and deposition,
revealing predominant favor for constructing advanced LMBs.

application of the modiﬁed electrolyte in full batteries. Besides

further corrode the fresh Li metal, leading to the larger
resistance and capacity loss of Li−S cell. On the contrary, the
Li anode cycled in the modiﬁed electrolyte exhibits a
remarkably dense and smooth surface without any protub-

clearly indicate the successful application of our designed
electrolyte in LMBs by adding a trace amount of KPF6, which
has a synergistic eﬀect in LiF-rich SEI and self-healing
electrostatic shield for uniform Li deposition. As a result, a
deeper Li utilization as well as a high capacity retention has
been achieved for the Li−S full cells.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have demonstrated that an optimized amount
(0.01 M) of the KPF6 additive in the 2 M LiTFSI electrolyte
can eﬀectively improve the stability of Li metal anode and
achieve high cyclability of Li−S batteries. Because KPF6
exhibits a lower LUMO energy than that of other salts/
solvents in the electrolyte according to the DFT calculations,
the preferential decomposition of PF6
−anions greatly
promotes the underlying SEI enriched in LiF. Meanwhile,
electrostatic shield from K+ also helps to regulate the
distribution of Li ions, enabling the synergistic eﬀects for
robust Li metal anode. As a result, uniform Li electro-
deposition with smooth and dense morphology has been
achieved in our designed electrolyte, as well as reduced
electrode overpotential and an outstanding Coulombic
eﬃciency. In addition, it also displays stable performance of
300 cycles at a high current density of 4 mA cm−2 and 200
cycles under a high Li utilization of 33.3% in the Li plating/
stripping process. When pairing the ultrathin Li anode with
sulfur cathode in the modiﬁed electrolyte, the Li−S cell also
exhibits a high capability retention and low polarization under
a deep Li utilization. Hence, we demonstrate a cost-eﬃcient
route toward advanced safe Li metal anode, especially shedding
insight to inspire electrolyte design for Li−S batteries.
