ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries under low-temperature and
lean electrolyte conditions for practical application are hindered by a sluggish
conversion reaction, low sulfur utilization, and cycling stability. Herein, we
designed a high-entropy (HE) electrolyte by mixing three Li salts. The HE
electrolyte simultaneously improves lithium sulfide (Li2S) conversion reaction
kinetics, sulfur utilization, and cyclability due to the anticlustering effect on
lithium polysulfides, three-dimensional Li2S growth, and robust anion-derived
solid electrolyte interphase layer formation, respectively. Consequently, the
HE electrolyte exhibits a high initial reversible capacity (1159.9 mAh g−1) and
cycling stability for 40 cycles under a low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (3.5 μL
mg−1) at the pouch cell level. In addition, the Li−S cell with HE electrolyte exhibits high cycling stability with a capacity decay
of 0.01% per cycle during 200 cycles at −15 °C.
KEYWORDS: high-entropy electrolyte, lithium polysulfide anticlustering, lean electrolyte, wide temperature, lithium−sulfur batteries
1. INTRODUCTION
The demand for Urban Air Mobility (UAM) and Unmanned
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) equipped with batteries is gradually
increasing due to the goal of achieving carbon neutrality by 2050
and ground traffic congestion resulting from urban over-
population.1,2 Characteristics required for batteries in UAM
and UAV include the following: (1) high power for vertical
landing and takeoff;3 (2) low-temperature performance in high-
altitude flight;4 (3) high gravimetric energy density (>400 Wh
kg−1);5 (4) cycling stability for sustainable use.6 Specifically,
lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are being proposed as an
appealing nominee for practical application of UAM and UAVs
due to their high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1)7−11
and the low freezing point of the electrolyte solvents [1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME), −58 °C; 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), −95
°C].12,13
However, the practical application of Li−S batteries in UAM
and UAV faces several critical issues under lean electrolyte
[electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio <5 μL mg−1] and low-
temperature (<−10 °C) operating conditions.14 First, the
lithium sulfide (Li2S) conversion reaction (Li2S4 + 6Li+ + 6e−
→4Li2S) rate becomes sluggish.15 Under lean electrolyte
conditions, the elevated concentration of polysulfides in the
electrolyte led to gelation, resulting in increased viscosity.16
Under low temperatures, ion transport thermodynamically
deteriorates. Furthermore, the occurrence of lithium polysulfide
(LiPS) clustering under lean-electrolyte and low-temperature
conditions17 leads to the formation of Li+-Sx
2−complexes by
Coulombic interaction, hindering the mobility of LiPSs.18
Consequently, exacerbated Li2S conversion reaction kinetics by
viscous electrolyte, deteriorated ionic transport, and aggravated
LiPS clustering hamper the high-power and low-temperature
Li−S battery performance. Second, sulfur utilization is reduced
due to the two-dimensional (2D) growth of Li2S. 2D Li2S
growth by a large nucleation overpotential under low-temper-
ature conditions19 and the limited Li2S solubility in low-donor
ether-based electrolytes20 provoke early termination of dis-
charging by covering the cathode surface with insulating Li2S,
hampering the achievement of high energy density in Li−S
batteries. Third, cycling stability deteriorates under these
operating conditions. A high polysulfide concentration gradient

The highly reducing nature of Li led to the reductive

In resolving the challenges of operating conditions, most
research has focused on enthalpic approaches to modify the
donicity of electrolytes for controlling polysulfide solvation.44
This enthalpic approach posed challenges due to inevitable
trade-offs.45 High-donor electrolytes that strongly solvate LiPS
enhanced sulfur conversion kinetics and utilization by facile
solution-mediated reaction and 3D Li2S growth through
increased solubility of active materials, respectively.46 Nonethe-
less, severe side reactions of high-donor electrolytes with Li and
an exacerbated shuttle effect caused by increased concentration
gradient of soluble LiPS resulted in diminished cycling
stability.44 Conversely, low-donor electrolytes that sparingly
solvate LiPS improved cycling stability by their compatibility
with Li and suppressed shuttle effects of soluble LiPSs.47
Nonetheless, low-donor electrolytes encountered challenges of
sluggish sulfur conversion kinetics and low sulfur utilization by
slow solid-to-solid reaction and early passivation of the cathode
surface through 2D growth of insulating Li2S, respectively.47
Here, this is the first work that we are aware of that introduces
a high-entropy (HE) electrolyte to Li−S batteries, incorporating
multiple Li salts with a focus on entropic approaches to break the
inherent trade-offs in enthalpic approaches affecting polysulfide
solvation (Figure 1a). Recently, HE electrolytes, developed
through the mixing of various salts or solvents,48 have been
investigated in various battery systems, including lithium-ion,49
lithium-metal,50 and aqueous zinc-ion batteries,51 demonstrat-
ing significant improvements in the rate capability and cycling
stability of the battery. These improvements can be attributed to
enhanced ionic conductivity through modifications in solvation
structures, the formation of an anion-derived solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI) layer, and increased LiNO3 solubility due to
changes in the solvation Gibbs free energy. In Li−S batteries,
where active materials can be solvated, challenges arise under
practical application conditions, such as lean electrolytes and
low temperatures. Under these conditions, LiPS clustering and
increased electrolyte viscosity lead to a poor rate performance
and reduced cycling stability. Therefore, an entropic approach of
an electrolyte to regulate polysulfide solvation can be an effective
solution. However, the impact of HE electrolytes on Li−S
batteries has not yet been explored.

HE electrolytes under lean-electrolyte and low-temperature
conditions indicated that entropic approaches to design
electrolytes could be effective strategies for practical Li−S
batteries.

The anticlustering effect of LiPS in the HE catholyte was
verified through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which
provided snapshots and the corresponding Li2S4 cluster size
distribution. The HE catholyte displayed smaller LiPS clusters,
with the largest LiPS cluster consisting of 366 atoms [(Li2S4)61],
compared to the larger cluster in the TFSI catholyte, where the
largest cluster contained 540 atoms [(Li2S4)90] (Figure 2c,d). In
addition, the LiPS anticlustering effect of the HE electrolyte was
further supported by the Li2S4 cluster size distribution analysis
(Figure 2e,f). In the TFSI catholyte, LiPS clusters exceeding 500
atoms were observed, whereas in the HE catholyte, no clusters
larger than 370 atoms were detected. More interestingly, the
dynamic trajectory revealed that the (Li2S4)90 cluster in TFSI
remained stable but occasionally underwent minor separations
due to thermal motion, forming the smaller clusters like
(Li2S4)89, (Li2S4)88, and others (Movie S1 and Figure S4).
The observation was consistent with the frequency of (Li2S4)90
being slightly less than 1 (Figure 2e). In contrast, Figure 2f
shows that the frequency of (Li2S4)61 in the HE catholyte was
about 0.1, suggesting that this largest LiPS was unstable. The
dynamic trajectory showed that the (Li2S4)61 cluster, formed
from smaller LiPS clusters, existed for only a short time before
decomposing into more stable LiPS clusters, such as (Li2S4)30
(Movie S2 and Figure S4).
The MD simulation results suggested that the LiPS clustering
behavior within the HE catholyte can be suppressed by reducing
the Gibbs free energy associated with the disintegration of LiPS
clusters, achieved through an increase in the entropy term with a
HE electrolyte (Figure S5).56 The disintegration of LiPS clusters
allowed facile diffusion of Li ions to escape the strong
electrostatic interactions with S4
2−anions within the LiPS
clusters.18 The self-diffusion coefficient of the Li ion (DLi+) in
the HE catholyte was estimated from the mean-squared
displacement through the MD simulation (Figure S6). The
DLi+ of the HE catholyte (2.4 × 10−6 cm2 s−1) was 26.3% higher
compared to that of the TFSI catholyte (1.9× 10−6 cm2 s−1),
implying facilitated diffusion of Li ions by the LiPS anticlustering
effect of a HE electrolyte. Additionally, the galvanostatic
intermittent titration technique (GITT) analysis of the Li−S
cell allowed us to verify the higher DLi+ of the HE electrolyte
compared to the TFSI electrolyte (Figures S7−S9 and Table
S2).57

with the HE catholyte exhibited stable cycling over 400 h,
whereas the Li symmetric cell with the TFSI catholyte showed a
significant overpotential increase after 300 h (Figures 4a and

thereby reducing the level of continuous reductive decom-

The self-discharge phenomenon, in which soluble polysulfides
diffused toward the anode and reacted with Li, resulting in
depletion of the active material and degradation of the
cyclability, was one of the challenges that had to be tackled in
Li−S batteries.63 Self-discharge was exacerbated under a low E/
S ratio due to the higher concentration gradient of soluble
polysulfides.45,64 To investigate the impact of HE electrolytes on
self-discharge characteristics in the Li−S cell, we monitored the
open-circuit voltage (OCV) of the Li−S cell under a low E/S
ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1 for a 72 h rest period after 10 cycles. After
the rest period, the OCV of the HE electrolyte was 2.32 V, which
was greater than that of the TFSI electrolyte (2.29 V; Figure 4c).
The small decrease in the OCV with the HE electrolyte
compared to that with the TFSI electrolyte indicated that the
HE electrolyte suppressed side reactions between soluble
polysulfides and Li by a stable and robust anion-derived SEI
layer.65
Additionally, the 10th and 11th discharge capacities were
compared to investigate the capacity loss caused by self-
discharge. The TFSI electrolyte showed an 18.6% decrease from
967.9 to 787.4 mAh g−1 due to severe self-discharge, while the
HE electrolyte showed a 9.2% decrease from 1010.2 to 917.6
mAh g−1 after the rest period (Figure S23). Furthermore, during
continued cycling after the 72 h rest period, the HE electrolyte
demonstrated superior cyclability with a capacity decay of
0.238% per cycle during 50 cycles compared to 0.493% for the
TFSI electrolyte (Figure 4d). The ability of the HE electrolyte to
suppress self-discharge was ascribed to the creation of a robust
anion-derived SEI layer, which prevented cracking and
reconstruction of the SEI layer by alleviating close contact of
soluble polysulfides with the Li anode throughout continuous
cycling.66 To analyze the impact of HE electrolytes on Li

2.3. Impacts of a HE Electrolyte on a Sulfur Cathode.
The nucleation and growth behavior of Li2S are major factors
that significantly influence the reversible capacity of the sulfur
cathode. The 2D deposition of insulating Li2S fully covers the
surface of the conductive support as it leads to early termination
of the discharge reaction and low capacity by preventing further
Li2S growth.20 We initially analyzed Li2S nucleation and growth
kinetics using chronoamperometry to examine how the HE
electrolyte affected the sulfur cathode (Figure 5a,b). As a result
of chronoamperometry at 2.0 V, the HE electrolyte exhibited
lesser maximum current time (tm), which refers to the time taken
to reach the maximum current (Im), of 1364 s compared to 1804
s for the TFSI electrolyte, implying improved Li2S nucleation
kinetics with the HE electrolyte. Additionally, the HE electrolyte
showed a higher Li2S nucleation capacity of 604.5 mAh g−1 than
the TFSI electrolyte of 575 mAh g−1. To investigate the impacts
on Li2S nucleation and growth behavior by a HE electrolyte,
dimensionless time (t/tm) and current (i/im) were adopted to
classify the Li2S nucleation behavior by a classical Bewick−
Fleischmann−Thirsk (BFT) model for 2D instantaneous (2DI)
and 2D progressive (2DP) nucleations and a Scharifker−Hills
(SH) model for 3D instantaneous (3DI) and 3D progressive
(3DP) nucleations, respectively (Note S3). As shown in Figure

3D growth of Li2S facilitated by the HE electrolyte inhibits early
termination of discharge caused by insulating Li2S covering the
carbon surface through 2D growth. This permitted a higher
nucleation capacity with the HE electrolyte compared to that
with the TFSI electrolyte.
The conversion of the Li2S deposition mode from 2D to 3D
can be ascribed to the enhanced Li2S solubility in the HE
electrolyte. The conventional electrolyte exhibited limited
solubility of Li2S by low donicity of TFSI salt anion, leading to
planar Li2S growth by a surface-mediated pathway.67 However,
Li2S solubility can be improved through increasing the entropy
term by the HE electrolyte, resulting in 3D deposition of Li2S by
complete binding between Li+ and traveling S2−anion in the
electrolyte on the top surface of existing Li2S.68 The Li2S
solubility of the electrolytes was assessed by dissolving 5 mM
Li2S (Figure S27). In the case of the TFSI electrolyte, the limited
solubility of Li2S resulted in a yellowish solution, whereas the
HE electrolyte, with its enhanced Li2S solubility, appeared as a
transparent solution. This improvement in the Li2S solubility
can be ascribed to the increased entropy of the electrolyte
system that reduces the Gibbs free energy for Li2S dissolution.
Additionally, ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopy anal-
ysis confirms that the enhanced solubility of Li2S4 in the HE
electrolyte, compared to the SS electrolytes, verifies its improved
dissolving capability resulting from increased entropy (Figure
S28).

HE electrolyte showed stable cycling stability even under lean-
electrolyte and high-rate conditions (Table S8).
2.5. Low-Temperature Electrochemical Performance
of a HE Electrolyte. Relative frequency of LiPS clustering
increases with a decrease in temperature,56 leading to notable
issues in the kinetics of soluble LiPSs to solid Li2S conversion
reaction due to the significantly hindered diffusion of soluble
LiPSs.53 According to the results of DOSY-NMR (Figure 2a)
and Raman spectroscopy (Figure 2b), the anticlustering effect of
the HE electrolytes on LiPSs was confirmed at low temperatures
(≤−15 °C). To investigate how the anticlustering effect of HE
electrolytes affected low-temperature Li−S batteries, CV
analysis was first conducted at −15 °C. As shown in Figure 7a,
in Li−S cells with the TFSI electrolyte, the Li2S conversion
reaction was impeded due to LiPS clustering, resulting in an
indistinct second cathodic peak in CV analysis. In contrast, HE
electrolytes exhibited a distinct second cathodic peak by the
LiPS anticlustering effect of the HE electrolyte, which facilitated
the Li2S conversion reaction at −15 °C. This tendency was also
observable when the scan rate was increased to 0.1 mV s−1
(Figure S34). The accelerated Li2S conversion reaction kinetics
by the LiPS anticlustering behavior of the HE electrolyte was
further verified through galvanostatic discharge experiments at
0.1 C. As shown in Figure S35, the voltage profile exhibited a
higher second plateau in HE electrolytes compared to TFSI
electrolytes, indicating that the Li2S conversion reaction was
promoted.
Improvement in the Li2S conversion reaction kinetics at low
temperatures due to the LiPS anticlustering effect in HE
electrolytes is closely related to enhanced Li-ion transportation.
The improvement of Li-ion transportation in the HE electrolyte
was first verified by measuring the Li-ion diffusion coefficient
through CV analysis using the Randle−Sevcik equation. The
diffusion coefficient of the Li ion was found to be 4.09 × 10−10
cm2 s−1 in HE electrolytes, representing a 34.5% enhancement
compared to TFSI electrolytes (3.04 × 10−10 cm2 s−1; Figure
S36). Furthermore, facile Li-ion transport in HE electrolytes was
also observed when measuring ionic conductivity in a stainless-
steel symmetric cell with the Li2S8 catholyte (Figure 7b). For
ionic conductivity measurement, catholytes with two different
concentrations of Li2S8 (0.1 and 0.9 M) were utilized to analyze
the LiPS anticlustering effect of HE electrolytes on ionic
conductivity under flooded (0.1 M corresponds to E/S > 35 μL
mg−1) and lean (0.9 M corresponds to E/S < 5 μL mg−1)
electrolyte conditions at low temperatures. As a result, the HE
catholyte demonstrated higher ionic conductivity than the TFSI
catholyte at all Li2S8 concentrations. Additionally, the ratio of
the ionic conductivity of the HE catholyte to that of the TFSI
catholyte was higher under the lean-electrolyte condition (3.64)
than under the flooded-electrolyte condition (1.74; Table S9).
This suggests that the entropic approach could be an effective
electrolyte design method for Li−S battery operation under
lean-electrolyte conditions, even at low temperatures when the
LiPS clustering behavior was exacerbated.
To evaluate the improvement of kinetics under low
temperatures by the anticlustering effect of LiPSs in the HE
electrolyte, a rate capability test was conducted at various
current densities ranging from 0.1 to 0.4 C (Figure 7c). The Li−
S cell with a HE electrolyte showed higher capacity than the cell
with a TFSI electrolyte under all current densities due to
improved Li2S conversion reaction kinetics, particularly showing
a significantly increased capacity in the HE electrolyte at 0.4 C
(400.9 mAh g−1) compared to the TFSI electrolyte (196.8 mAh
g−1, Figure S37). Additionally, to analyze the cycling stability of
the HE electrolyte under low temperature, a galvanostatic
cycling test of the Li−S cell was conducted at 0.2 C (Figure 7d).
The HE electrolyte exhibited excellent cyclability with a capacity
degradation of 0.01% per cycle over 200 cycles. The significant
performance enhancement with the HE electrolyte at low
temperatures suggests the potential of HE electrolytes as

3. CONCLUSION
We proposed the HE electrolyte by an entropic approach in Li−
S batteries to break out the trade-offs between sulfur conversion
kinetics and electrochemical stability of an electrolyte with Li in
conventional enthalpic approaches. An HE electrolyte created
by simple mixing of three Li salts of LiTFSI, LiFSI, and LiBETI
simultaneously improved the Li2S conversion reaction kinetics
and cycling stability through LiPS anticlustering and a robust
anion-derived SEI layer under lean-electrolyte and low-temper-
ature conditions. In addition, the HE electrolyte influenced the
entire Li−S battery systems: (1) Electrolyte: LiPS clustering
behavior was hampered by decreasing the Gibbs free energy of
the disintegrating LiPS cluster in the HE electrolyte, which
improved sluggish Li2S conversion reaction kinetics. (2) Li
anode: A robust anion-derived SEI layer enhanced the cycling
stability by inhibiting cracking and reconstruction of the SEI
layer throughout continuous cycling. (3) Sulfur cathode: Partial
transition of the Li2S growth mode from 2D to 3D induced
higher sulfur utilization by increasing the solubility of Li2S
through reduced Gibbs free energy of Li2S dissolution. The
multifunctional HE electrolyte positively influenced the entire
Li−S battery system, resulting in a superior Li−S pouch cell
performance. The HE electrolyte exhibited higher reversible
capacities compared to the TFSI electrolyte for 40 cycles under
an E/S ratio of 3.5 μL mg−1 and a current density of 0.2 D/0.1 C
at 25 °C. In addition, the Li−S coin cell with the HE electrolyte
exhibited superior cyclability with a capacity degradation of
0.01% per cycle for 200 cycles at −15 °C. This outstanding
performance of the Li−S pouch cell under lean-electrolyte and
low-temperature conditions suggested that the entropic
approach, which can break the trade-offs of the enthalpic
approach to achieve acceleration of Li2S conversion kinetics and
cycling stability simultaneously, can be an effective design
strategy of electrolytes for the practical application of Li−S
batteries.
4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
4.1. Chemicals. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(LiTFSI), lithium sulfide (Li2S), sulfur (S8), 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME), and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich. Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) and lithium bis-
(pentafluoroethanesulfonyl)imide (LiBETI) were purchased from TCI
Chemicals. Lithium nitrate was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Multiwalled
carbon nanotube (MWCNT, MR-99) was purchased from Carbon
Nanomaterial Technology Co. Ltd. All of the materials were used
without any treatment.
4.2. Preparation of the Electrolyte. To prepare SS electrolytes of
LiTFSI, LiFSI, and LiBETI (TFSI, FSI, and BETI) or a HE electrolyte,
all Li salts and LiNO3 underwent an initial drying process in a vacuum
oven at 80 °C overnight. To prepare the electrolytes, dried 1 M Li salt
was dissolved in a solvent mixture of DOL/DME [1:1 (v/v)] and 0.3 M
LiNO3 additive under an argon (Ar)-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm; H2
< 0.1 ppm), and the solvent mixture was stirred overnight. Specifically,
for TFSI, FSI, and BETI electrolytes, 1 M LiTFSI, 1 M LiFSI, and 1 M
LiBETI were dissolved in the prepared solvent mixture as Li salts,
respectively. For the HE electrolyte, three Li salts of LiTFSI, LiFSI, and
LiBETI at equal molar concentrations were used: 1/3 M LiTFSI, 1/3 M
LiFSI, and 1/3 M LiBETI.
4.3. Preparation of the LiPS Catholyte (Li2Sx). Depending on
the type of LiPS (Li2Sx, x = 4, 6, 8), Li2S and sulfur powder were mixed
in varying molar ratios and dissolved in a solvent mixture of DOL/DME
[1:1 (v/v)]. For Li2S4, Li2S6, and Li2S8, the molar ratios of Li2S/S were
sequentially mixed as 1:3, 1:5, and 1:7, respectively. Subsequently, 1 M
Li salt (TFSI, 1 M LiTFSI; FSI, 1 M LiFSI; BETI, 1 M LiBETI; HE, 1/3
M LiTFSI, 1/3 M LiFSI, and 1/3 M LiBETI) and 0.3 M LiNO3 were
added to the mixture. Finally, the mixture was stirred overnight at 60
°C. All procedures were conducted within an Ar-filled glovebox.
4.4. Preparation of the Sulfur Cathode. A uniform blend of 70
wt % sulfur and 30 wt % CNT was ground in a mortar and dried in an
oven at 155 °C for 12 h to prepare a sulfur cathode. Subsequently, the
sulfur and CNT composite was mixed with the LA132 binder at a mass
ratio of 9:1. The resulting mixture was dispersed in water to form a
slurry. The slurry was coated onto aluminum foil using a doctor blade to
fabricate the sulfur cathode, which was then dried in an oven at 60 °C
for 8 h. The sulfur cathode was punched using a 14-mm-diameter punch
and utilized in all CR2032 coin cell experiments.
4.5. Materials Characterization. 4.5.1. Characterization of the
Electrolyte/Catholyte. LiPS clustering behavior was analyzed by 1H
and 7Li diffusion-ordered spectroscopy nuclear magnetic resonance
(DOSY-NMR; Bruker AV500) and Raman spectroscopy (Horiba Jobin
Yvon ARAMIS). The LiPS solubility of the electrolyte was analyzed by
Beer−Lambert law using ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopy
(Shimadzu UV-1900i). For DOSY-NMR analysis, a coaxial tube was
utilized. The inner tube was filled with 0.5 M lithium chloride (LiCl) in
deuterated tetrahydrofuran (THF-d8), while the outer tube contained a
solution of 1 M Li salt (TFSI, 1 M LiTFSI; HE, 1/3 M LiFSI + 1/3 M
LiTFSI + 1/3 M LiBETI) with 1 M Li2S4 + 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL/DME
[1:1 (v/v)] and 0.35 M toluene as an internal reference. The diffusion
coefficients of toluene and Li were measured by 1H and 7Li pulsed-field-
gradient NMR analysis, respectively, and calculated from the Stejskal−
Tanner equation. For Raman spectroscopy analysis, a catholyte
consisting of 1 M Li salt with 1 M Li2S4 + 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL/
DME was utilized.12 Raman spectra were acquired using a 514 nm
excitation wavelength laser with a spectral range of 100−1500 cm−1.
For UV−vis spectroscopy, 1 M Li2S4 catholytes containing either 1 M of
single salts (LiFSI, LiTFSI, and LiBETI) or a mixture of these three salts
