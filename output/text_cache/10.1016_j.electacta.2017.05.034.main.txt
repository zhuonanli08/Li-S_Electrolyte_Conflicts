A B S T R A C T
Experimental data elucidating the time-dependent composition of the electrolyte within a standard
lithium-sulfur battery cell are presented. The electrolyte employed consisted of a solution of LiTFSI in a
1:1 mixture of dimethoxyethane and dioxolane, including nitrate salts. In order to track the
decomposition reactions of the electrolyte components, the cells were run for a ﬁxed number of
cycles, after which they were opened and the remaining electrolyte was extracted with an excess amount
of 1,4-dioxane. The amount of each of the components (namely LiTFSI, dimethoxyethane and dioxolane)
within these mixtures was determined by means of gas chromatography and 19F-NMR. It was found that
the amount of electrolyte accessible to extraction remains relatively constant during the ﬁrst 25 cycles,
but then continuously decreases within the subsequent 40 investigated cycles. The ratio of the organic
constituents of the electrolyte does not change considerably, which means there is no preferred
decomposition pathway for either of the two components dioxolane and dimethoxyethane, respectively.
These results demonstrate that the capacity fading of a lithium-sulfur cell coincides with the loss of the
electrolyte within the cell and there is a strong correlation between the failure of a lithium-sulfur cell and
the decomposition reactions of its electrolyte. These ﬁndings are supported by a detailed analysis of the
gaseous decomposition products after speciﬁed cycle numbers. Realistic pouch bag cells are used as test
vehicles. Such cells do resemble industrially viable, commercial cells much closer as e.g. coin-type cells:
Much lower electrolyte/active mass ratios can be used, thus allowing for a more realistic picture of the
failure mechanisms involved.

1. Introduction
The lithium-sulfur battery is one of the most promising
candidates for next generation battery systems, as it offers high
theoretical speciﬁc charges (1675 mAh/g with respect to sulfur)
and high theoretical energy densities (2500 Wh/kg with respect
to the ﬁnal reaction product Li2S). However, their use is hampered
by several challenges, such as pronounced volume changes upon
cycling (in both the sulfur/carbon composite cathode and the
metallic lithium anode), the polysulﬁde shuttle resulting from the
diffusion and self-discharge of the intermediately formed polysul-
ﬁde species and the decomposition of the electrolyte [1–8]. This
means in practice the cells suffer from a fast fading of capacity and
low efﬁciencies due to degradation processes taking place on both
electrodes. During the last years, many elegant strategies have
been developed dealing with possible solutions to these problems,
such as the encapsulation of the sulfur active material in porous
carbon structures [9–11], the employment of polymer electrolytes
[12,13], the use of additives, adsorbents (e.g. transition metals/
oxides/nitrides) or doping (e.g. nitrogen doping of the carbon host
matrix) for improved retention of the polysulﬁde intermediates
within the cathode matrix [14–23] or the separation of anode and
cathode by means of protective (solid state) membranes [24–28] or
additives (improved SEI formation on the metallic lithium anode)
[29,30], to mention a few among many others [31]. Encapsulation
of Li2S as an active material has been tried [32] as well as new
concepts such as solid-state polymer lithium-sulfur batteries [33].
However, while many of these studies report an improved
behavior of the batteries investigated, none of them has led to a
real breakthrough in the lithium-sulfur technology so far and
experimental evidence of the respective failure mechanisms and
their improvements remains scarce. One major problem is the fact

that while qualitatively known due to pioneering analytical
investigations [34–53], the relative importance and contributions
of these failure mechanisms are still not exactly known. Most
importantly, there are no or few quantitative investigations from
which the relative contributions could be derived  e.g. stating
how much lithium sulﬁde is lost on both the anode and cathode
during the aging of the battery or how fast the electrolyte
decomposition is taking place on the anode. However, it is crucial
to gain a more detailed insight into these phenomena in order to
improve the design of the cell components and to tackle them in
the right order. To the authors’ opinion, especially the stability of
the electrolyte deserves attention, as it is well known that the SEI
formed on metallic lithium (which is widely used in standard
lithium-sulfur cells) is not stable and fresh lithium surfaces are
formed within the cell upon each charge process as dendritic
structures start to grow on the surface of the metal, leading to a
continuous loss of electrolyte during cycling.
Therefore, this study focuses on the composition of the
electrolyte within the cell upon extended cycling. It is found that
the amount of extractable electrolyte decreases continuously from
the 25th cycle onwards and is correlated to the decline in cell
capacity. The capacity of an average lithium-sulfur cell decreases
after 55 cycles, corresponding to a 50% loss of electrolyte. Based on
these data, the life time of the test cells used can be extrapolated
from the electrolyte’s perspective to about 100 cycles.
2. Experimental
Pouch bag cells were used as a standard test system. A metallic
lithium sheet of 50 mm thickness was used as anode. The cathode
originally consists of 55% elemental sulfur (active material), 40%
conductive carbon materials and 5% binder material. The sulfur
loading within the cathode amounts to 2 mg/cm2, the overall
amount of sulfur/test cell to 65–71 mg. A picture of a typical
standard pouch bag test cell is shown in Fig. 1. After formation
(discharge with a 0.02C rate), all cells were charged with a 0.1C rate
and discharged with a 0.15C rate (based on the theoretical charge
capacity of the sulfur active material). The cells were cycled
between 1.7V-2.5 V.
The electrolyte consists of a 1:1 mixture of 1,2-dimethoxy-
ethane (DME, 44 wt.%) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, 44 wt.%). LiTFSI
(8 wt.%) is used as the conductive salt within the electrolyte,
lithium nitrate is used as an additive (4 wt.%). Minor amounts of
other additives do not change any of the conclusions discussed in
the following. It is well known from the literature that lithium
nitrate has beneﬁcial properties with respect to the protection of
the lithium anode from the internal polysulﬁde shuttle [54–57].
The overall amount of electrolyte used varied between 400–
450 ml/cell.
In order to conduct the extraction experiments, the test cells
were stopped and analyzed after a certain number of cycles (25, 40,
50 and 65, respectively). Before opening, the cells were completely
discharged. The cells were opened in a glove box under inert gas
atmosphere with a pair of scissors and 2 ml of 1,4-dioxane were
inserted into the cell by means of a syringe. Afterwards, the cells
were carefully macerated for about 30 s with the extraction solvent
1,4-dioxane, the complete solution was then extracted from the
cell again by a syringe (Fig. 2). The extract has a greyish color due to
carbon components from the cathode matrix. The solution was
then ﬁltered through a syringe membrane ﬁlter (0.5 mm) to
remove any of these solid/dispersed components stemming from
the electrodes (Fig. 3).
The residual amounts of both the DME and the DOL could be
determined quantitatively by means of gas chromatography (GC)
and separate calibration experiments.
The extract (residual electrolyte and d8-1,4-Dioxane) was
injected in the gas chromatograph (Agilent 7820A; Colum: CP-
SIL 8; 50m; ID: 0.32 mm; ﬁlm 5 micrometer). Both DME and DOL
were identiﬁed quantitatively by ﬂame ionization detection (FID)
at a retention time of 8.05 and 8.75 min, respectively, and an
accuracy of 0.1% after calibration.
The amount of LiTFSI could be followed quantitatively as well by
employing 19F-NMR. For this reason, 1,4-dioxane-d8 was used as a
solvent for the extraction experiments. The quantiﬁcation of LiTFSI
in the extract was carried out by adding a known amount of 2-
Chloro-4-ﬂuorotoluene (CFT; approx. 50 mg). According to the
relative integral ratio of the 19F-signals (TFSI anion: 79,7 ppm;
CFT internal standard: 115.3 ppm) the integral of the 6 F atoms in
the TFSI anion was used to calculate the amount of LiTFSI. The
signal position of the ﬂuorine spectrum was referenced against the
TMS-signal of the corresponding 1H-spectrum. The measurement
was carried out with a Bruker DPX-400, 400 MHz at 25 C. By the
combination of both methods both the composition of the
electrolyte and the ratio of its constituents as well as the absolute

Cells with large amounts of excess electrolyte (Fig. 9) were
assembled with a slightly different electrolyte composition

3. Results and discussion
The results of the experiments described in the previous section
can be visualized as shown in Fig. 4 and 5. It should be emphasized
that the pouch bag cells used in these experiments are particularly
suited as test vehicles, as the electrolyte/active material ratios can
be much more closely matched to realistic commercial cells with
high energy densities as compared to e.g. coin-type cells. The latter
are usually ﬁlled with a signiﬁcant amount of excess electrolyte,
which makes it considerably more difﬁcult to distinguish between
different failure mechanisms, among them electrolyte decompo-
sition [58].
The values in Figs. 4 and 5 are referenced to the average values
of the cells not cycled at all but directly extracted again after ﬁlling
with the electrolyte (so called “No. 0” cells). The composition is
normalized to 100%. The results prove that the extraction method
employed is both appropriate and sufﬁcient, as the values for the
cells directly extracted again (“No. 0” cells) very closely resemble
the theoretical composition of the electrolyte employed. The
deviation between these two values can also be considered as an
approximation to the experimental error to be expected for the
values obtained via this method.
Interestingly, the amount of electrolyte accessible by extraction
does not seem to change much within the ﬁrst 25 cycles of the
cells’ life. However, from the 25th cycle onwards its continuous
decrease can be directly derived from the experimental results.
Both the amount of the liquid organic electrolyte constituents DOL
and DME and the conductive salt LiTFSI decrease. By the 65th cycle,
the amount of extractable solvent (both DME and DOL) is reduced
by 66%, the amount of LiTFSI even by 80%. These ﬁndings are
very interesting for two reasons: ﬁrst of all, the ratio of the two

nature of its components. Examples for such mechanisms could
include (among others) inclusion within the pores of the carbon
matrix which might be slowly closed due to ageing processes

the inorganic LiTFSI salt might be more stable against decomposi-

with at least a similar speed as compared to the other components,
if not faster (it needs to be cautioned that from the available
experimental data set it cannot be unambiguously derived if the
conductive salt indeed decomposes faster than the organic
solvents). However, not all of the electrolyte loss observed in
these extraction experiments necessarily has to stem from
decomposition reactions on the metallic lithium anode. It is also
conceivable that some of the electrolyte components are simply
not easily accessible any more to the extraction solvent within the
pores of the matrices, the morphology of which will also change
upon cycling the cells. However, electrochemical decomposition
processes certainly play an important role, as indicated by the gas
evolution which can be observed in the cells upon extended
cycling. This will be discussed in more detail within the following
sections.
While the decrease of the extractable amount of electrolyte is
obvious, its inﬂuence on the failure of the battery cells can be
understood if compared to the capacity fading of typical standard
test cells as displayed in Fig. 6. The speciﬁc charge and discharge of
several test cells were recorded and averaged, the results shown in
Fig. 6 are normalized with respect to the speciﬁc charge for the
sake of a better comparability. (The normalized value for the
discharge capacity is higher at the beginning of the cycle life of the
cells as compared to the charge capacity, however, for the absolute
values the charge capacity is always larger than the discharge
capacity). This way, also the normalized amount of extractable
electrolyte can be included in the same diagram.
It can be seen from Fig. 6 that there seems to be a correlation
between the decrease in capacity of a lithium-sulfur battery cell
and the amount of available electrolyte. The parallel trend of both
curves (decreasing slopes) after 25 cycles points towards a
connection between both phenomena. The averaged capacity
shows a continuous fading from the ﬁrst cycle, however, after
approximately 55 cycles the fading suddenly strongly increases,
leading to the failure of the cell. If one takes the extrapolated x-
axes values as the cycle numbers to correlate (in order to exclude
other fading mechanisms, leading to the continuous capacity loss
from the ﬁrst cycle onwards), there seems to be a “buffer” of
approximately 17 cycles (41 vs. 24), before the electrolyte loss leads
in turn to a pronounced fading of the battery cells’ speciﬁc charge.
At the same time, this means that this standard test cell can
tolerate a loss of the original amount of electrolyte of about 50%
before the cell performance starts to break down.
Moreover, from the parallel trend in both the decrease of the
speciﬁc charge of the cell and the amount of the extractable
electrolyte a prognosis for the maximum cycle life can be derived
based on the extrapolation of the electrolyte amount: By the time it
reaches zero, the cell can run for a maximum of about 100 cycles.
While it has to be understood that this value will vary considerably
with the amount of electrolyte employed in the cell, the speciﬁc
cell design (including the amounts of materials used etc.) as well as
the electrochemical testing conditions, it gives a good impression
of the order of magnitude of the life time which can be expected for
the systems currently used and the issues involved in cells
including realistic amounts of electrolyte aiming towards high
overall energy densities suitable for the commercialization of this
battery type.

The fact that electrolyte decomposition is a major reason for the
failure of lithium-sulfur cells with low amounts of electrolyte (to
achieve realistic, high energy densities) can also be proven by
another set of experiments: If low amounts of electrolyte lead to
early cell failure, it can be expected that “ﬂooded” cells including
large amounts of electrolyte (such as most coin cells) considerably
outrun electrolyte-lean cells. That this is indeed the case is
illustrated in Fig. 7, showing the comparison of the cycle life of cells
with a large excess of electrolyte as compared to electrolyte-lean
cells. While the latter fail after 50-80 cycles as previously outlined
and shown in Fig. 6, electrolyte-rich cells run for >500 cycles
without failing. These ﬁndings corroborate the conclusion that loss
of electrolyte is the major reason for cell failure in realistic cells
with low amounts of electrolyte. These observations hold true
despite minor differences between both cell types in terms of
electrolyte composition, design and observed capacity (e.g. larger
amounts of lithium were employed in these cells. However, the

amounts of methane and ethane. Both of these gases must stem
from decomposition reactions of the electrolyte, supporting the
experimental ﬁndings described previously: from 40 cycles

particularly at larger cycle numbers, also some amounts of carbon
dioxide and ethene can be detected. Small amounts of oxygen
cannot be completely excluded, but most likely are a consequence
of the analysis procedure (small amount of ambient air contami-
nation). Most of this gas formation is probably due to the reductive
decomposition of the electrolyte constituents. Interestingly, the
ratios of the different constituents seem to be rather strongly
dependent on the cycling history of the battery cells under
consideration: Cells which suffered from short-circuits towards
the end of their cycle life seemed to evolve a considerable higher
amount of ethene as compared to cells which were stopped
without having any irregular electrochemical behavior before. At
the same time, the relative amount of hydrogen was reduced while
the relative amount of nitrogen increased in those cells. The
relative amounts of each of the respective gases found varied from
cell to cell in such abusive cases, which is why the authors refrain at
this point from trying to assign an exact value for the composition
of the gas mixture. It should be to stressed that it is challenging to
establish exactly comparable experimental conditions for such
cases and more experimental work and broader statistics are
necessary in order to gain a more detailed and quantitative insight.
Preliminary experiments with different amounts of LiNO3 in the
electrolyte point towards a linear relationship between the lithium
nitrate concentration in the electrolyte and nitrogen gas evolution.
4. Conclusions
Series of discharged lithium-sulfur cells were investigated after
ﬁxed cycle numbers. For this purpose, the amount of electrolyte
accessible to extraction and its composition were evaluated for
each step. Pouch bag cells were used as they offer suitable test
conditions, with electrolyte/active mass ratios much closer to
commercially viable cells as compared to commonly used coin-
type cells. The latter tend to be cycled with signiﬁcant excess
amounts of electrolyte, which makes the investigation of failure
mechanisms (such as electrolyte loss and decomposition) and their
respective importance extremely difﬁcult.
It was found that the amount of accessible electrolyte (by
solvent extraction) continuously decreases from the 25th cycle
onwards. While this holds true for both the organic solvent
constituents and the inorganic conductive salt, the ratio of the
components surprisingly remains almost completely unaltered.
Further experiments proved that cells with a large amount of
excess electrolyte can be cycled signiﬁcantly longer (>500 cycles).
This indirectly proves that electrolyte decomposition is indeed a
major failure mechanism in electrolyte-lean cells providing high
energy densities, while the cycle life can be signiﬁcantly extended
by simply adding more electrolyte to the cells, however at the cost
of sacriﬁcing energy density.
For some of the cells, gas formation could be observed and
investigated by means of gas chromatography. The main con-
stituents at the beginning of cycle life (after four cycles) were found
to be hydrogen and nitrogen in a ratio of 3:1. After larger cycle
numbers (forty cycles), signiﬁcant amounts of methane and ethane
stemming from reductive electrolyte decomposition could be
detected, corroborating the results from the electrolyte extraction
