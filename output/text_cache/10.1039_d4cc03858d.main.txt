Soluble inorganic quantum dots as an electrolyte
additive to boost lithium–sulfur battery
performance†
Liwei Liu, Ziyao Song, Zhihao Qi, Lijun Yang,
Xizhang Wang,
* Zheng Hu
*
and Qiang Wu
*
Herein, MoS2 quantum dots (QDs) are constructed to serve as
electrolyte additives for lithium–sulfur batteries, which can ‘solidify’
soluble polysulfides by chemisorption and promote sulfur conversion
chemistry by electrocatalysis. The Li–S cell with MoS2 QDs shows
high retained capacity and high-rate capability, much better than the
counterpart without MoS2 QDs.
The rapid development of electric vehicles and electronic
devices has increased the demand for high-energy-density
batteries.1 Lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) that feature high
energy density and low cost are promising next-generation
energy storage systems.2,3 Despite the great progress achieved
in the past decade, LSBs are still troubled by critical problems
such as the shuttle effect of polysulfides and slow conversion
kinetics on the cathode, as well as the growth of lithium
dendrites on the anode.4–7 It is urgent to develop effective
solutions to improve the cycling and rate performances of LSBs.
Transition metal compounds have exhibited impressive
polysulfide adsorption and catalytic conversion functions,
showing great capability to suppress the shuttle and polariza-
tion eﬀects of LSBs.8–12 However, the active sites on common
transition metal compounds are quite limited and are prone to
being embedded by insoluble discharge products Li2S/Li2S2
upon cycling, thereby preventing the full exertion of their
functions.13,14 As is known, soluble redox mediators can shuttle
in the electrolyte to promote the sulfur conversions through
electrocatalysis, thus avoiding the situation that the solid
catalysts are easy to be embedded.14,15 Inspired by this idea,
transition metal compounds could be made into soluble quan-
tum dots (QDs), which can not only enrich the catalytic active
sites, but also serve as electrolyte additives to promote sulfur
conversion chemistry by shuttling between the electrode and
Li2S/Li2S2 species. In the past, some QDs were used to improve
the LSB performance;16–18 however, most of them were
adsorbed by the electrode materials and actually acted as solid
catalysts rather than soluble electrolyte additives.19–23 An exam-
ple of soluble QDs for LSBs is the nitrogen-doped carbon dots
(NCDs) employed by Fu et al. The NCDs merely functioned as a
chemisorbent for polysulfides and behaved like ‘‘blood plate-
lets’’ in LSBs, stimulating the formation of a protective layer at
the sulfur electrode–electrolyte interface to prevent sulfur loss,
but without catalytic function involved.18 Recently, our group
utilized
soluble
polyoxometalates
with
combined
chemi-
sorption and catalytic functions as electrolyte additives and
demonstrated the effectiveness of soluble QDs in improving the
performance of LSBs.24
As an inorganic material widely used in LSBs with strong
chemisorption and catalytic eﬀects,25,26 MoS2 was adopted as a
representative in this study to construct soluble QDs to verify
its role in LSBs. With reference to the reported synthetic
method,27,28 we synthesized soluble MoS2 QDs as the electro-
lyte additive of LSBs for the first time, which can ‘solidify’
soluble polysulfides by chemical interaction and promote the
sulfur conversions by electrocatalysis. Benefiting from the
integrated functions, the Li–S cell with MoS2 QDs exhibits high
retained capacity after cycling and high-rate capability, and still
shows a high capacity of 638 mA h g1 after 50 cycles at 0.2 C
even with a high areal loading of B4 mg cm2 and a low
electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 7 mL mg1. This study suggests a
general strategy to develop advanced QD-type electrolyte addi-
tives for LSBs from the known materials with intrinsic chemi-
sorption and electrocatalytic functions.

MoS2 QDs are shown in Fig. 1. The high-resolution transmis-

0.252 nm corresponds to the (102) plane of MoS2 (JCPDS no. 37-

The MoS2 QDs show an obvious fluorescence phenomenon
under the irradiation of ultraviolet light (Fig. S2, ESI†), which
further verifies the successful construction of MoS2 QDs.33
To be an electrolyte additive, the MoS2 QDs need to be
uniformly dispersed in the electrolyte of LSBs and not be
adsorbed by the cathode material. As shown in Fig. S3 (ESI†),
in the content range of 0–1.5 wt%, the MoS2 QDs can be evenly
dispersed in the mixture of 1,3-dioxane (DOL) and 1,2-di-
methoxyethane (DME) (v/v 1 : 1), indicating the highly soluble
nature of MoS2 QDs in LSBs electrolyte. In order to illuminate
that the MoS2 QDs can exist freely in the electrolyte of LSBs, the
cathode host, i.e. carbon nanotubes (CNTs), was added into the
0.05 wt% MoS2 QDs solution. After standing for 24 hours, the
colour of the solution did not change compared to the blank
solution without adding CNTs. It is indicated that most MoS2
QDs can still exist stably in the electrolyte as additives in the
presence of the cathode material (Fig. S4, ESI†).34
The chemical interaction between MoS2 QDs and polysul-
fides was evaluated, as shown in Fig. 2. When the Li2S6 DOL/
DME solution was carefully added into the solution with
0.1 wt% MoS2 QDs, an obvious clotting layer was formed at
the interface, which was the aggregate formed due to the
adsorption of a large amount of polysulfides by the MoS2 QDs
(Fig. 2a). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis
indicates that the MoS2 QDs contain 3d peaks for Mo4+
(233.4 eV/230.5 eV) and Mo6+ (235.2 eV/232.3 eV), as well as the
S 2s peak (227.5 eV);35–38 whereas the clotting layer also presents
the signals for Mo4+ and Mo6+ with the shifts of 0.3 eV and 0.2 eV
towards the higher binding energy side, respectively, and the S 2s
peak with the shift of 0.5 eV towards the lower binding energy side
(Fig. 2b). The XPS results reflect the strong chemical interaction
between the MoS2 QDs and polysulfides.39,40
Visual experiments indicate that, when the cell of the Li
metal anode and sulfur cathode (S/CNTs) was discharged in the
electrolyte, the colour of the electrolyte gradually turned yellow
upon discharge due to the formation of soluble polysulfides. In
contrast, when the discharge was carried out in the electrolyte with
0.05 wt% MoS2 QDs, the electrolyte did not change its colour
significantly and became opaque gradually due to the formation of
the aggregates of MoS2 QDs and in situ-generated polysulfides
(Fig. 2c). The electrolyte after 2 h of discharge was measured by
ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy. For the absorption peak
corresponding to polysulfides (260 nm), the intensity of the
solution with MoS2 QDs is lower than that of the blank solution,
which indicates the lower concentration of soluble polysulfide
in the solution with added MoS2 QDs (Fig. S5, ESI†). This result

the dissolution and diﬀusion of polysulfides.
Theoretical calculations further demonstrate the strong
interaction between MoS2 and polysulfides (Fig. 2d). Compared
with graphite, the (102) plane of MoS2 has larger adsorption
energies with Li2S6 and Li2S4, which is conducive to chemi-
sorption of polysulfides.41 Thanks to the abundant adsorption
sites on the MoS2 QDs and their strong interaction with
polysulfides, the aggregates are formed when the in situ-
generated polysulfides meet the MoS2 QDs (Fig. 2a), which
much reduces the concentration of soluble polysulfides in the
electrolyte, thus alleviating the shuttle eﬀect.
Experiments to reveal the electrocatalytic activity of MoS2
QDs were carried out, as shown in Fig. 3. The symmetrical cell
measurements show that two pairs of obvious redox peaks
appear in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve of the cell with
MoS2 QDs and the intensity is obviously stronger than that of
the cell without MoS2 QDs (Fig. 3a), indicating the catalytic
activity of the MoS2 QDs toward polysulfide conversion. The
electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) reveal the smaller
charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the symmetrical cell with
MoS2 QDs than that without MoS2 QDs, indicating the facili-
tated charge transfer kinetics by the MoS2 QDs (Fig. 3b).42
For the CV curves of LSBs with or without MoS2 QDs, two
pairs of redox peaks are observed, corresponding to the oxida-
tion reactions of Li2S2/Li2S to Li2Sx (A1) and Li2Sx to S/Li2S8 (A2),
and the reduction reactions of S/Li2S8 to Li2Sx (C1) and Li2Sx to
Li2S2/Li2S (C2) (Fig. 3c). The Li–S cell with MoS2 QDs has larger
reduction potentials, smaller oxidation potentials and higher
peak intensities than that without MoS2 QDs, indicating the
faster reaction kinetics of the former enhanced by the MoS2
QDs (Fig. 3d).43 Considering the importance of precipitation
and dissolution of Li2S to the performance of LSBs, the effect of
MoS2 QD additives on the precipitation and dissolution kinetics of
Li2S was evaluated in asymmetric batteries (Fig. 3e and f). The Li–S
cells with MoS2 QDs have higher peak current and higher Li2S
precipitation and dissolution capacities than those without MoS2
QDs, reflecting the marked catalytic promotion of MoS2 QDs to
the Li2S conversion.39 The preceding results indicate the chemi-
sorption and catalysis functions of the MoS2 QD additive to the
LSBs, which can ‘solidify’ soluble polysulfides (Fig. 2a) and pro-
mote the sulfur conversions (Fig. 3), thus much enhancing the
performance of LSBs, as shown in Fig. 4.
The Li–S batteries were assembled with the S/CNTs cathode
and 0.3 wt% MoS2 QDs additive in the electrolyte (Fig. S6 and
S7, ESI†). At the current density of 1 C, the cell shows a retained
capacity of 762 mA h g1 after 300 cycles, obviously higher than
633 mA h g1 for the counterpart without MoS2 QDs (Fig. 4a).
With increasing the current density, the capacity difference
between the cells with or without MoS2 QDs gradually increases,
indicating that the MoS2 QD additive remarkably improves the rate
performance of LSBs (Fig. 4b and Fig. S8, ESI†). At a high rate of
5 C, the cell with MoS2 QDs still delivers a high specific capacity of
637 mA h g1 while the counterpart without MoS2 QDs shows
almost no capacity. The potential gap (DE) between the plateaus of
the charge–discharge curves is 198 mV for the cells with MoS2 QDs,
much smaller than the case without MoS2 QDs (625 mV) (Fig. 4c),
indicating the suppressed polarization of the cell due to the
participation of the MoS2 QDs (Fig. S9, ESI†). With the promo-
tion of the MoS2 QDs, the LSB with a high sulfur loading of
B4 mg cm2 and a low E/S ratio of 7 mL mg1 delivers a high
capacity of 638 mA h g1 after 50 cycles at 0.2 C, much superior to
the counterpart without MoS2 QDs. This result suggests the great

Using other soluble inorganic QDs as electrolyte additives can
improve the LSB performance as well (Fig. S10, ESI†), which
provides a general strategy to solve the problems faced by the
application of LSBs.44,45
In summary, soluble MoS2 QDs were constructed as electro-
lyte additives for LSBs, which can eﬀectively suppress the
polysulfide shuttling by chemisorption and promote the con-
version of sulfur species by electrocatalysis. Benefiting from
these functions, the Li–S cell with MoS2 QDs delivers a high
specific capacity of 637 mA h g1 at 5 C and exhibits a
high capacity of 638 mA h g1 after 50 cycles at 0.2 C with a
high sulfur loading of B4 mg cm2 and a low E/S ratio of
7 mL mg1. This study demonstrates an efficient way to improve
the LSB performance by using soluble inorganic QDs as electrolyte
additives. It is also suggested that people can develop advanced
QD-type electrolyte additives from the known materials with
intrinsic chemisorption and electrocatalytic functions, opening a
new avenue to enhance the conversion-type energy storage.
