n-Hexane Diluted Electrolyte with Ultralow Density enables 
Li–S Pouch Battery Toward >400 Wh kg−1
Hao Cheng, Shichao Zhang, Bing Zhang, and Yingying Lu*
DOI: 10.1002/smll.202206375
1. Introduction
Conventional lithium-ion battery has encountered a conspic-
uous bottleneck due to the inherently theoretical energy den-
sity limitation (387  Wh  kg−1) that makes it difficult to meet 
the growing demands of digital products and electric vehi-
cles with longer endurances.[1] Alternatively, lithium–sulfur 
(Li-S) batteries have attracted extensive attentions because of 
their natural merits of ultra-high theoretical energy density 
(2600  Wh  kg−1), cost-effectiveness, and environmental friend-
liness.[2] Nevertheless, the electrochemical performances of 
Li-S batteries are hindered by the cathodic issues (e.g., the 
severe shuttle of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs)) and anodic trou-
bles (e.g., the uneven deposition of lithium).[3] Encouragingly, 
Lithium–sulfur (Li-S) batteries are attractive candidates for next generation 
energy storage devices due to their high theoretical energy density of up to 
2600 Wh kg−1. However, the uneven deposition of lithium, the undesired 
shuttle of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), and the excess weight fraction of 
electrolyte severely impair the practical energy density of Li-S batteries. Here, 
a low concentrated and nonpolar n-hexane (NH)-diluted electrolyte (named 
as LCDE) with ultralow-density to alleviate the above dilemmas is proposed. 
The nonpolar NH boosts the diffusion of lithium ion in LCDE, favoring the 
homogeneous deposition of lithium. This nonpolar effect also reduces the 
solubilities of LiPSs, promoting a quasi-solid-state transformation of sulfur 
chemistry, thus tremendously eradicating the shuttle of LiPSs. Most impor-
tantly, the ultra-light NH diluent enables the LCDE with an ultralow density of 
only 0.79 g mL−1, which reduces the weight of LCDE by 32.5% compared with 
conventional ether-based electrolyte. Owing to all the merits, the Li-S pouch 
cell achieves a high energy density up to 417 Wh kg−1. The nonpolar NH-
diluted electrolyte with multifunction presented in this work provides a new 
and feasible direction to increase the practical energy density of Li-S batteries.

CVE, especially with an ultra-high sulfur loading and a long-
term cycling time.[14] In this connection, developing a tailored 
electrolyte that can largely confine the dissolution of LiPSs but

practical energy density of Li-S batteries.[16] The density of the 
classical CVE (e.g., 1 m LiTFSI dissolved DOL/DME (v/v = 1/1) 
solution with 2  wt.% LiNO3) is as high as ≈1.17  g  mL−1.[17] 
The weight proportion of above electrolyte is over 50  wt.% 
even under lean electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) conditions (e.g., 
E/S = 2.5 µL mg−1), much higher than that of lithium ion bat-
tery (lower than 20 wt.%).[17] This fundamentally damages the 
energy density of the battery. Unfortunately, if the electrolyte 
volume is further reduced, the wettability of porous cathode and 
separator will be worse, and the shuttle of LiPSs will be more 
serious, causing the rapid attenuation of the discharge capacity 
and cycle life of the battery.[11b] Therefore, reducing the density 
of electrolyte could be an intelligent strategy to compress the 
weight fraction of electrolyte under the same E/S ratio, thereby 
physically improving the actual energy density of Li-S bat-
teries. Impressively, Suo et al. proposed the methyl-propyl-ether 
(MPE, 0.73 g mL−1) to dilute the density of ether-electrolyte to 
the reported lowest value of 0.83 g mL−1.[16] Consequently, the 
energy density of the Li-S pouch cell was increased by 19.2% 
(from 329.9 to 393.4 Wh kg−1). Nevertheless, the extensive appli-
cation of MPE is concerned due to its low boiling point (39 °C), 
which is not conducive to the thermal stability of electrolyte.[18] 
On the whole, developing novel low-density electrolytes with 
superior physicochemical properties is expected to basically 
improve the energy density of Li-S batteries.
From the above points of view, engineering a multifunctional 
diluent that can promote the uniform deposition of lithium, 
inhibit the shuttle of LiPSs and simultaneously reduce the elec-
trolyte density could be a feasible strategy to eventually liberate 
the practical energy density of Li-S batteries. Alkanes show a 
preliminary feasibility in this regard because of their general 
low density (<0.8  g  mL−1, Table  S1, Supporting Information). 
Encouragingly, Bao's group has showed that nonpolar alkanes 
can effectively reduce the nucleation overpotential of lithium, 
which consequently reinforces the cycle stability and coulombic 
efficiency of lithium-metal anode.[19] To date, there are rarely 
involved reports that employed nonpolar alkanes to handle 
the aforementioned issues in Li-S batteries. Herein, we pro-
pose a low concentrated and nonpolar n-hexane (NH) diluted 
tetrahydrofuran (THF)-based electrolyte (named as LCDE) to 
chemically and physically improve the energy density of Li-S 
battery. We demonstrate that multiple improvements can be 
achieved by using this elaborately designed LCDE (Figure 1), as 
compared with the baseline low concentrated electrolyte (LCE). 
First, the NH boosts the diffusion of Li+ and alleviates interface 
contact of anode, thus promoting the homogeneous deposition 
of lithium. Second, the nonpolar NH-diluted electrolyte dra-
matically reduces the solubility of LiPSs, which contributes to 
a quasi-solid-state transformation of sulfur chemistry, thus tre-
mendously suppressing the shuttle of LiPSs and improving the 
utilization rate of sulfur. Third, the ultra-light NH can dilute the 
LCDE to only 0.79 g mL−1, which reduces by 13.2% and 32.5% 
compared with LCDE and CVE, thus largely cutting down the 
whole weight of the Li-S battery in the same electrolyte volume. 
Consequently, our Li-S pouch cell achieves a high energy den-
sity up to 417 Wh kg−1 under practical conditions.
2. Results and Discussion
The LiTFSI and LiNO3 dissolved DOL/DME solutions are the 
widely used electrolytes for current Li-S batteries.[20] LiTFSI fea-
tures a high ionic conductivity, well temperature stability and 
low cost, making it an optimum lithium salt in Li-S batteries.[21] 
Meanwhile, a small amount of LiNO3 additive is conducive to 
the stable formation of SEI.[22] Therefore, this pair of salts is 
selected as the bi-solute in this work. According to our primary 
motivation, reducing the density of the electrolyte is the core 
to physically promote the energy density of Li-S battery. There-
fore, the DOL solvent is excluded due to its own high density 
(1.07 g mL−1).[16] Ultimately, THF is selected as the ether solvent 
to dissociated lithium salt due to that it has a similar physico-
chemical properties,[23] but a much cheaper price than DME 
(Table S2, Supporting Information). As shown in Table S1 (Sup-
porting Information), the boiling point and density of alkanes 
increase synchronously as the chain of carbon atoms grows. 
Eclectically, NH is employed in this work for its extremely low 
density (0.66 g mL−1) and relatively high boiling point (69 °C).

Unfortunately, only 20 vol.% NH is added to a 1 m LiTFSI and 
0.1  m LiNO3 dissolved THF solution, the phase separation 
occurs (Figure  S1, Supporting Information). This is because 
the nonpolar NH itself is difficult to dissociate lithium salts, 
leading to an extraction phenomenon. Encouragingly, when the 
concentration of LiTFSI is reduced to 0.1 m, the diluted electro-
lyte remains phase stable even though the added ratio of NH 
is as high as 50  vol.% (Figure  S2, Supporting Information). 
It is worth pointing out that Yu et al. have demonstrated that 
the ultralow concentrated electrolyte can achieve better cycling 
performance and rate capability in Li-S batteries.[24] Therefore, 
we fixed 0.1 m LiTFSI and 0.1 m LiNO3 as bi-solute, and pre-
pared a series of LCDE by adjusting the volume ratio of NH 
to THF (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The 0.1 m LiTFSI 
and 0.1 m LiNO3 dissolved THF/NH solution (v/v = 65/35) is 
defined as LCDE afterward, while the 0.1 m LiTFSI and 0.1 m 
LiNO3 dissolved THF solution is regarded as LCE baseline.
Raman spectroscopy is used to study the solvation environ-
ment of the electrolyte. As shown in Figure 2a, the character-
istic peak located at 738.2 cm−1 is ascribed to the collaboration 
between Li+ and TFSI−.[25] In contrast, the corresponding 
Raman peak of LCDE shifts to a higher value (742.1  cm−1). 
Meanwhile, the shift values are positively correlated with the 
amount of NH diluent (Figure  S3, Supporting Information), 
indicating that the nonpolar NH can weaken the coordination 
between Li+ and TFSI−.[16] These results are also confirmed by 
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements. As shown 
in Figure 2b, the 7Li peak of LCDE demonstrates a higher chem-
ical shift (−2.44 ppm) compared with LCE (−2.54 ppm), and the 
shifts are also positively correlated with the amount of NH dil-
uent (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This is arisen from 
the reduced ion pairing and Lewis basicity around Li+, which 
shields the electron cloud.[26] These results imply that the diffu-
sion of Li+ will be promoted. To this end, mean square displace-
ments (MSDs) are employed to simulate the diffusion of Li+ 
by classical molecular simulations (MD). The snapshot of MD 
simulation box of LCDE is shown in Figure  2c. As shown in 
Figure 2d, a larger MSD slope of Li+ can be perceived in LCDE 
compared with LCE, reflecting a higher diffusion coefficient of 
Li+ (1.69 × 10−9 m2 s−1 versus 9.66 × 10−10 m2 s−1) according to 
Einstein equation,[27] which corroborates the weakened coor-
dination around Li+. Further, the ionic conductivities of LCE 
and LCDE at room temperature are investigated according 
to electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS, Figure  S5, Sup-
porting Information). As shown in Figure 2e, the ionic conduc-
tivity of LCE is 1.12 mS cm−1, which is much lower than that 
of CVE (12.4 mS cm−1) due to the extremely low concentration 
of lithium salts. Nevertheless, the ionic conductivity of LCDE 
is promoted to 1.45  mS  cm−1 and that the improvements are 
positively correlated with the amount of NH diluent (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information). The reason may be that the intro-
duced NH weakens the chelation effect around Li+, thereby pro-
moting its migration in the electrolyte. It is worth mentioning 
that the electrochemical window of LCDE is expanded to 4.04 V 
compared with that of LCE (3.90 V) owing to the introduction 
of NH (Figure  S7, Supporting Information), which endows 
the lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4, LFP) cathode with an 
enhanced cycle performance (Figure  S8, Supporting Informa-
tion). Figure 2f shows the viscosities of various electrolyte and 
the corresponding contact angles (insets) toward lithium metal. 
The viscosity of LCE (0.63  mPa  s) is obviously reduced com-
pared with that of CVE (1.48 mPa s) since the far less lithium 
salt. Impressively, the LCDE can further diminish the viscosity 
to a lower value (0.48 mPa s) due to the dilution of NH, which 
significantly lowers the contact angle toward lithium metal 
anode (insets of Figure 2f).
Subsequently, the lithium stripping/deposition behaviors 
are explored in lithium symmetrical batteries. The EIS results 
in Figure  S9 (Supporting Information) show that the charge 
transfer impedance of ions in LCDE is lower than that of LCE 
owing to the higher ionic conductivity, lower viscosity, and 
improved contact. As shown in Figure  2g, the polarization 
voltage of the LCE symmetrical battery increases and fluctuates 
continuously in 100  h, which is caused by the uneven strip-
ping/deposition processes of lithium. In contrast, the polariza-
tion voltage remains stable in the same cycle times, indicating 
that NH improves the stability of lithium deposition and strip-
ping. These conclusions are confirmed by the scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM) morphologies of the cycled lithium 
(Figure S10, Supporting Information). Furthermore, an in situ 
optical microscope is applied to monitor the deposition mor-
phology of the lithium surface, which clearly shows that the 
morphology of the deposited lithium in LCDE battery is more 
homogeneous compared with that of LCE during the deposi-
tion process of 2 mAh cm−2 (Figure 2h). These results illustrate 
that LCDE is beneficial to improve the electrochemical stability 
of lithium anode.
The sulfur chemistry is necessary to be clarified due to the 
existence of a large number of non-polar NH diluents in the 
LCDE. As shown in Figure 3a, when 200 µL of 0.5 m Li2S6-THF 
solution is added to 2  mL LCE, the electrolyte turns brown. 
In sharp contrast, in addition to that the color changes from 
colorless to faint yellow, obvious precipitation appears at the 
bottom of the LCDE, indicating the reduced solubility of LiPSs 
in LCDE. To explain these phenomena, the veritable solubili-
ties of various LiPSs (Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4) in LCE and LCDE 
are tested (Figure S11, Supporting Information). As shown in 
Figure 3b, the solubilities of Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4 species in 
LCE are as high as 10.4, 6.6, and 2.6  m [S] (concentration of 
atomic sulfur). In sharp contrast, the solubilities of the corre-
sponding sulfur species in LCDE are dramatically decreased to 
152, 84, and 32  mM [S] due to the introduction of non-polar 
NH diluent. This kind of electrolyte is widely defined as the 
so-called “sparingly solvating electrolyte,” in which sulfur may 
undergo diversified conversion process according to previous 
reports.[28]
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements are performed 
to verify the sulfur conversion in practical Li-S battery. The 
carbon/sulfur (C/S) composite (Figure  S12, Supporting Infor-
mation) with a sulfur loading of 1.5  mg  cm−2 (calculated by 
thermogravimetric analysis, Figure  S13, Supporting Informa-
tion) is applied as a cathode. As shown in Figure 3c, the two 
reduction peaks at 2.23 and 2.05  V in LCE battery are corre-
sponding to the “solid–liquid” and “liquid–solid” transforma-
tions of sulfur to LiPSs and LiPSs to Li2S2/Li2S, respectively, 
which are the typical characteristics of dissolution–precipitation 
conversion mechanism.[11] Comparatively, in addition to the 
above peaks, a new reduction peak emerges at 1.93  V, which

The electrochemical performances of Li-S batteries are fur-
ther evaluated on a button cell system. C/S electrode with a 
sulfur loading of 1.5 mg cm−2 is applied as a cathode. As shown 
in Figure  5a, the initial discharge capacities of LCDE battery 
are 1342, 1109, 864, and 661  mAh  g−1 at the rates of 0.1, 0.2, 
0.5, and 1  C, respectively. These discharge capacity parame-
ters are higher than those of LCE battery (1164, 947, 726, and 
546 mAh g−1). When the rate is recovered to 0.1 C, the discharge 
capacity of LCDE battery is increased to 1214  mAh  g−1, sug-
gesting a satisfactory electrochemical reversibility. In addition, 
the utilizations of sulfur in LCDE battery are largely expanded 
due to the available protection of LiPSs shuttling (Figure 5b). 
The cycling performance of Li-S batteries with LCE and LCDE 
are shown in Figure  5c. The initial discharge capacity of the 
LCE battery is 961 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, and it quickly decays to 
601  mAh  g−1 after 200 cycles. By contrast, the initial capacity 
of the LCDE battery is increased to 1092  mAh  g−1 due to the 
amelioration of the shuttle effect. Impressively, the revers-
ible capacity remains as high as 936 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles, 
yielding a capacity retention of 85.7%, higher than that of LCE 
battery (62.5%). Notably, the average coulombic efficiency (CE) 
of LCDE battery is higher than that of LCE battery (99.7% vs 
96.9%), again implying that the shuttle effect is greatly sup-
pressed with LCDE. These electrochemical parameters reflect 
the superiorities of quasi-solid-state transformation of sulfur 
by LCDE. Of note is that increasing the proportion of NH in 
LCDE can further reduce the solubility of LiPSs (Figure  S16, 
Supporting Information), but it will excessively damage the 
reaction kinetics, leading to the rapid capacity attenuation 
(Figure S17, Supporting Information).

Further, the post-cycled lithium anodes are disassembled 
and characterized by SEM. As shown in Figure 5d, the cycled 
lithium in the LCE battery is severely corroded, showing an 
uneven surface. Additionally, a strong energy dispersive spec-
trometer (EDS) signal of elemental sulfur is perceived on the 
lithium surface (Figure  S18, Supporting Information), cor-
roborating the serious shuttle behavior. Conversely, the lithium 
surface after cycling in LCDE battery exhibits a robust and flat 
structure (Figure  5e, Supporting Information) accompanied 
with a weak EDS signal of sulfur. Moreover, time of flight-sec-
ondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) is applied to anal-
ysis the depth distribution of elemental sulfur. The S+ signal 
of lithium surface and interior in LCE battery is much higher 
than that of in LCDE battery (Figure S19, Supporting Informa-
tion). More intuitively, the 3D images show that the surface and 
interior of cycled lithium in LCE cells exhibit ultra-high inten-
sity of S+ signals (Figure 5f), whereas the corresponding signal 
in LCDE anode is considerably reduced (Figure  5g), further 
highlighting the significance of LCDE in inhibiting the shuttle 
effect of LiPSs.
To further verify the practicality of LCDE in Li-S batteries, 
the cycling performances under higher sulfur loadings are 
estimated. As shown in Figure  5h, the initial areal capacities 
of the LCDE batteries are 2.7 and 4.1 mAh cm−2 at 0.1 C under 
the sulfur loadings of 2.4 and 3.9  mg  cm−2, respectively. Fur-
ther increasing the sulfur loading to 5.8  mg  cm−2, the LCDE 
battery can deliver an initial areal capacity of 5.7  mAh  cm−2. 
After 50 cycles, the reversible capacity maintains at 
4.8 mAh cm−2, exceeding the state-of-the-art lithium-ion battery 
(4 mAh cm−2).[33] Concomitantly, the GCD curves remain typ-
ical after cycled (Figure 5i), suggesting a stable electrochemical 
performance under a high sulfur loading. The shuttle of LiPSs 
is well-known to induce harmful self-discharge behavior, which 
is one of the major challenges for Li-S batteries.[34] In this 
connection, the self-discharge evaluations are investigated by 
chronoamperometry measurements. As shown in Figure 5j, the 
LCE and LCDE batteries were discharged to a cut-off voltage of 
2.2 V, where a large number of LiPSs are formed. The average 
shuttle current of the LCE cell is up to 5.4 µA in 48 h, corre-
sponding to a self-discharge capacity of 172.8 mAh gs−1. On the 
contrary, the average shuttle current in LCDE cell decreases 
to merely 0.4 µA under the same conditions, which reflects a 
self-discharge capacity of 12.8  mAh  gs−1. This shows that the 
shuttle effect in LCDE cell is dramatically suppressed. This is 
also confirmed by the optical photos of the disassembled cell 
(Figure  S20, Supporting Information). Besides, the self-dis-
charge behaviors under open circuit voltage further supports 
these conclusions (Figure S21, Supporting Information). These 
results show that LCDE can effectively strengthen the anti-self-
discharge behavior of Li-S batteries by the virtues of the quasi-
solid-state transformation.
In order to give full plays to the energy density of Li-S bat-
teries, the cathode, electrolyte, and anode should be integrated 
with practical conditions (see Figure 6a). However, under these 
conditions, the initial energy densities of current Li-S pouch 
cells are still hard to achieve >400 Wh kg−1.[5,35] One of the key 
factors causing this dilemma can be the high density of the CVE 
(1.17 g mL−1, Figure 6b). The weight fraction of CVE is as high 
as 46.0% (Figure S22, Supporting Information) in our practical 
battery system containing a high sulfur loading (5.2 mg cm−2), 
a lean electrolyte (2.5  µL  mg−1), and a thin lithium (45  µm; 
Table S3, Supporting Information). Encouragingly, the density 
of LCDE presented in this work is merely 0.79 g mL−1, which 
is lower 13.2% and 32.5% than LCE and CVE (Figure  6b). 
Figure  6c shows the normalized weight and weight factor 
(reciprocal of weight) of the Li-S pouch cell under above prac-
tical conditions. The total weight of the Li-S pouch cell declines 
with the decrease of electrolyte density (in the order of CVE, 
LCE, and LCDE). Concretely, the total weight of LCDE pouch 
cell is only 84.7% of the CVE under lean electrolyte conditions 
(E/S = 2.5 µL mg−1), corresponding to a weight factor of 118.0% 
compared with the CVE. This means that the theoretical energy 
density of the LCDE cell can be increased by 18.0% compared 
with the CVE cell without considering the discharge capacity 
and platform voltage, thus fundamentally exerting the superi-
ority of high energy density Li-S battery.
The Li-S pouch cell (35  mm  ×  45  mm) is assembled 
(Figure  6d) to thoroughly evaluate the practical energy den-
sity under the above integrated conditions. As shown in 
Figure  6e, the initial discharge areal capacity of the LCE 
pouch cell is 4.8  mAh  cm−2 at 0.05  C, corresponding to an 
energy density of 337  Wh kg−1, higher than that of CVE 
pouch cell (283  Wh  kg−1). This can be arisen from the 
reduced weight and the mitigated shuttle of LiPSs to some 
extent under dilute condition.[24] Impressively, the initial dis-
charge areal capacity of the LCDE pouch cell is increased to 
5.6  mAh  cm−2 owing to the higher utilization of sulfur in 
quasi-solid-state transformation. Consequently, the ultra-light 
LCDE enable the Li-S pouch cell realize a high energy den-
sity up to 417 Wh kg−1, which raises by 23.7% and 47.3% com-
pared with LCE and CVE pouch cells, respectively. Besides, 
LCDE pouch cell demonstrates enhanced cyclic stability 
compared to LCE pouch cell for the merit of quasi-solid-
state mechanism of sulfur cathode and the regulated deposi-
tion of lithium anode. Figure 6f further confirms that LCDE 
can effectively promote the uniform deposition of lithium 
in practical pouch cell. Meanwhile, Figure  6g corroborates 
that the active lithium in post-cycled pouch cell remains at 
7.1 mAh cm−2, which accounts for 77.2% of the fresh lithium 
foil, revealing a high recycling utilization of lithium. The 
energy density of our Li-S pouch battery with LCDE is com-
pared with the currently prominent reports in terms of var-
ious E/S and sulfur loadings (Figure 6h; Table S4, Supporting 
Information).[5,16,36] It can be seen that our pouch cell exhibits 
higher energy density even under the conditions of moderate 
E/S and sulfur loadings. This means that there are still possi-
bilities to further raise the energy density by reducing the E/S 
and increasing the sulfur loadings.
3. Conclusion
In conclusion, we have developed a multifunctional LCDE to 
comprehensively boost the practical energy density of Li-S bat-
teries. Both experiments and MD simulations show that the 
introduced nonpolar NH in LCDE promotes the diffusion of 
lithium ion. Meanwhile, the LCDE alleviates interface contact 
with lithium anode, regulating the uniform lithium deposition.

Furthermore, the as-produced sparingly solvating LCDE ena-
bles the sulfur cathode undergo a quasi-solid-state transforma-
tion, thus effectively blocking the shuttle effect and enhancing 
the utilization rate of sulfur. Moreover, the ultralow density of 
LCDE (0.79 g mL−1) largely eliminates the obstacle of excessive 
weight fraction of electrolyte. These multiple virtues of LCDE 
enable our Li-S batteries demonstrate improved electrochem-
ical properties in terms of discharge capacity, rate performance, 
cycle stability, coulombic efficiency, anti-self-discharge behavior, 
and lithium anode protection. More impressively, the Li-S 
pouch cell with LCDE realizes a satisfactory cycle stability and 
a high energy density up to 417 Wh kg−1, raising by 23.7% and 
47.3% compared with LCE and CVE pouch cells. This work pro-
vides an integrated strategy for improving the practical energy 
density of Li-S batteries.
