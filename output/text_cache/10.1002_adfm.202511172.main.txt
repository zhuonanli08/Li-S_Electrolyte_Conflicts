Weakly Solvating Electrolyte: Regulating Polysulﬁde
Intermediates for High-Performance Lithium-Sulfurized
Polyacrylonitrile Batteries
Xiangyang Zhao, Jiayu Wang, Wenqi Zhao, Zihao Song, and Qingli Zou*
Electrolyte engineering plays a crucial role in the design of high energy-density
lithium-sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (Li-SPAN) batteries, a promising energy
storage technology. However, current predominant electrolyte systems face
challenges of anode dendrite growth and cathode polysulﬁde loss, which limit
the cycling stability of Li-SPAN batteries. Here, a weakly solvating electrolyte
(WSE) primarily composed of diethoxy methane (DEM) is proposed to simul-
taneously address challenges at both electrodes. At the anode, DEM’s weakly
solvating capability accelerates both Li+ diﬀusion and desolvation, preventing
out-of-plane Li deposition and dendrite formation caused by high concentra-
tion gradients at the anode surface. More signiﬁcantly at the cathode, weakly
solvated Li+ exhibits stronger Lewis acidity, preferentially stabilizing S3
2−in-
termediates via hard Lewis acid-base interaction. These higher-reduction-state
intermediates promote faster subsequent lithiation and deposition reactions,
reducing polysulﬁde loss while improving both rate and cycling performance
of the batteries. These performance enhancements and the corresponding
mechanisms are supported by electrochemical and spectroscopic characteri-
zations. With this WSE, Li-SPAN batteries achieve exceptional cycling stability
(0.087% capacity fade per cycle over 500 cycles at 1 C), and pouch cell cycle
life increases from 9 to 35 cycles with 90.6% capacity retention. This strategy
provides valuable insights for developing high-performance Li-SPAN batteries.
1. Introduction
The escalating global demand for high-energy-density and low-
cost energy storage systems has accelerated the search for
advanced battery technologies. Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
stand out as an exceptionally promising candidate, oﬀering
a high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1—several
times higher than that of lithium-ion batteries, along with lower
cost.[1–3] However, Li–S batteries face signiﬁcant commercializa-
tion barriers due to the rapid capacity fading during cycling.
This deterioration primarily results from the shuttle of polysul-
ﬁde intermediates, which dissolve from the cathode and diﬀuse

alleviated, thereby enabling improved cycling performance.[21,22]
Beyond these eﬀorts, the impact of electrolytes on the fundamen-

tion caused by high concentration gradients at the lithium metal

with a high capacity retention of 90.6%. This work demonstrates
the potential of electrolyte regulation in eﬀectively manipulating
the electrochemical reactions of active materials and provides a
novel strategy to promote the practical application of Li-SPAN
batteries.

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Electrolyte Solvation Structure Tuning
The Li-SPAN batteries exhibit distinctly diﬀerent reaction char-
acteristics in carbonate-based and ether-based electrolytes. In
carbonate-based electrolytes, the issues of sluggish solid-state re-
action kinetics and dendritic growth in Li-SPAN batteries restrict
the realization of its true potential.[15–17] Despite the chemical
compatibility of commercial 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) ether
electrolyte solvent to lithium metal, the spontaneous and rapid
polysulﬁde intermediates dissolution and diﬀusion process of
Li-SPAN batteries causes serious cathode polysulﬁde loss.[18–20]
Electrolyte chemistry plays a vital inﬂuence on Li-SPAN batter-
ies, which is demonstrated to aﬀect the solvation structure, and
ﬁnally aﬀects the performance of the batteries.[23,27] From a mi-
croscopic point of view, it is a result of interfacial reaction on the
electrode-electrolyte interphase, which is essentially a competi-
tion among the components in electrolyte. A weakly solvating sol-
vent diethoxy methane (DEM), constructed through altering the
alkyl chain of DME, can signiﬁcantly change the solvation envi-
ronment of Li+ with the similar chemical structure (Figure 1a).
In detail, the binding energy of the Li+-DEM complex is lower
than that of the Li+-DME complex, as illustrated in Scheme 1.
Speciﬁcally, the Li─O bond length in the DEM complex is longer,
and the bond angle is smaller compared to that in the DME

Classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were per-
formed for 1 m LiFSI DME and 1 m LiFSI DEM solutions, with
the resulting data presented in Figure 1b,c and Figure S1 (Sup-
porting Information). Analysis of the radial distribution function
(RDF) data revealed distinct solvation shell structures for Li+ in
the two electrolyte systems. Speciﬁcally, the Li+ solvation shell
in 1 m LiFSI DME exhibits an average coordination number of
4.6 DME oxygens and 0.8 FSI−oxygens per Li+. In contrast, the
solvation shell of Li+ in 1 m LiFSI DEM features average coordi-
nation numbers of 3.0 DEM oxygens and 2.0 FSI−oxygens per
Li+. These results indicate that Li+ in the DEM-based electrolytes
is less tightly encased by solvent molecules compared to those in
the DME-based electrolyte. This weaker solvation environment
facilitates faster diﬀusion and reaction kinetics of Li+ in the DEM
electrolyte system. Further insights into the desolvation behav-
ior were obtained by calculating the binding energies of the re-
maining Li+(solvent)n complexes, as shown in Figure S2 (Sup-
porting Information). The binding energies for the Li+(DME)2.3
and Li+(DEM)1.5 complexes are determined to be −504.38 and
−355.86 kJ mol−1, respectively. The lower binding energy of the
Li+(DEM) complex suggests a more facile desolvation process at
the electrode interfaces.
To elucidate the existence forms of polysulﬁde ions in DME
and DEM solvents, Ultraviolet-Visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy was
employed to examine chemically synthesized polysulﬁdes in both
systems. As depicted in Figure 1d, the UV-vis spectra of nom-
inal polysulﬁdes (S4
2−) synthesized in DME exhibit prominent
absorption peaks at 325 and 420 nm, which are characteristic of
S4
2−, along with a signiﬁcant peak at 350 nm attributed to S6
2−.
This spectral proﬁle indicates that both S4
2−and S6
2−species are
strongly stabilized in DME. In contrast, the UV-vis spectra of
nominal polysulﬁdes synthesized in DEM displays a single dom-
inant absorption peak at 270 nm, corresponding to S3
2−. Com-
pared with DME system, the smaller coordination number be-
tween Li+ and solvent molecules diminishes the electron density
around Li+, enhancing the Lewis acidity of Li+. As a result, Li+
can more readily engage with the S3
2−via hard Lewis acid-base
interaction, thereby facilitating the stable existence of S3
2−in the
DEM system[28–30] Collectively, these observations demonstrate
that the solubility and stability of polysulﬁdes are signiﬁcantly
inﬂuenced by the solvation environment. DEM, as a weakly sol-
vating solvent, facilitates the formation of lower-order polysulﬁde
intermediates such as S3
2−, which promotes rapid subsequent
lithiation and deposition reactions, thereby reducing polysulﬁde
loss.
Moreover,
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl
ether (TTE) was introduced into the electrolyte system to sup-
press the diﬀusion of polysulﬁde ions, leveraging the bulky steric
hindrance of C-F segments (such as ─CF2─and ─CF3).[31,32] The
relevant physical properties of DME, DEM, and TTE solvents
were summarized in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The
inﬂuence of DME, DEM, and TTE on the solvation structure of
polysulﬁde ions was systematically investigated. The binding
energy between soluble polysulﬁde ions and the solvents is
employed as an indicator of solvation capability.[33,34] Com-
putational models comprising lithium polysulﬁde molecules
coordinated with two DME molecules, two DEM molecules, and
one DME/DEM plus one TTE molecule were constructed to
represent electrolytes with varying solvation capability (Figure
S3, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S3a–d (Sup-
porting Information), the binding energy between Li2S3 and two
DME molecules is calculated to be −2.63 eV, the highest among
the four systems. This is attributed to the high electron-donating
ability and chelation eﬀect of DME molecules. In contrast,
the binding energy between Li2S3 and two DEM molecules
(−2.38 eV) is lower than that of DME, indicating the relatively
weaker solvating capability of DEM, as expected. Upon sub-
stituting one DME/DEM molecule with one TTE molecule in
the two DME/DEM systems, the binding energy signiﬁcantly
decreased, highlighting the much weaker solvating capability
of TTE. Moreover, similar trends are observed for the binding
energies of Li2S4 and Li2S6 with diﬀerent solvent molecules,
further corroborating the conclusions drawn from Li2S3. The
comprehensive eﬀect of TTE content on the polysulﬁde ions
solvation structure and electrochemical performance was further
explored in Figure S4 (Supporting Information). An optimal
balance is achieved with 1 m LiFSI DEM electrolyte containing
20% TTE, which exhibits the best liquid-phase dynamics and
overall electrochemical performance.
Based on the aforementioned results, the solvation structure of
polysulﬁde ions in 1 m LiFSI DEM/TTE (4:1) was further eluci-
dated through MD simulations. The MD simulation systems con-
sisted of Li2S3 in 1 m LiFSI DME and 1 m LiFSI DEM/TTE (4:1),
as depicted in Figure 1f,g and Figure S5 (Supporting Informa-
tion). Analysis of the RDF data reveals that DME molecules are
located at a distance of 5.6 Å from the center of Li2S3 in 1 M LiFSI
DME, forming the inner solvation shell (shell 1). In contrast, the
introduction of TTE into 1 M LiFSI DEM signiﬁcantly altered the
outer solvation shell (shell 2) while maintaining the integrity of
the inner shell. Speciﬁcally, the inner solvation shell around Li2S3
in 1 m LiFSI DEM/TTE (4:1) is composed of DEM molecules
at a distance of 5.2 Å, which is similar to that in 1 m LiFSI
DME to ensure suitable dissolution of polysulﬁde ions.[31,32]
And the TTE molecules in 1 m LiFSI DEM/TTE (4:1) emerges
at 6.6 and 8.4 Å around Li2S3 (Figure 1f), achieving a fence-
type-encapsulation eﬀect toward polysulﬁde ions to inhibit their
diﬀusion.
Experimental evidence of the MD simulation results was ini-
tially obtained from Raman spectra of the electrolytes (Figure 1g).
Contrary to the S-N-S bending peak of FSI−at 773 cm−1 in LiFSI
salt spectra, the Raman spectra of all electrolytes undergo a red
shift due to enhanced coordination between the cation and sol-
vent molecules. Notably, compared with DME-based electrolytes,
DEM-based electrolytes display a much smaller shift, indicating
stronger Li+/FSI−interactions, which is consistent with the MD
results. Further investigation of the solvation structure was con-
ducted using NMR spectra, as shown in Figure S11 (Supporting
Information) and Figure 1h. The 17O peak in the NMR spectra
of 1 m LiFSI DEM (Figure S11, Supporting Information) shifts
upﬁeld compared to that of 1 m LiFSI DME, suggesting stronger
Li+/FSI−interactions in the DEM electrolyte. The 7Li NMR spec-
trum of the DME-based electrolyte systems (Figure 1h) exhibits a

2.2. Li Anode Stabilization Evaluation
Li-SPAN batteries face signiﬁcant challenges stemming from
lithium metal anode, particularly uncontrolled lithium den-
drite growth, which ultimately accelerates battery failure. Elec-
trolyte plays a crucial role in facilitating rapid and uniform plat-
ing/stripping behavior on the lithium anode. To evaluate the in-
ﬂuence of solvation structures on lithium plating/stripping be-
havior, Li–Cu batteries were assembled and tested (Figure 2a,b;
Figure S9, Supporting Information). Li–Cu batteries assembled
with commercial carbonate-based electrolyte (1 m LiPF6 EC/DEC)
exhibit low coulombic eﬃciency (CE) and poor reversibility. Al-
though commercial ether-based electrolyte (1 m LiFSI DME) sig-
niﬁcantly enhances the CE, the reversibility remains an issue.
In stark contrast, Li–Cu batteries employing a 1 m LiFSI DEM
electrolyte demonstrate stable interface evolution, characterized
by stable cycling for nearly 180 cycles with a high average CE
of 98.9% under a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and a capac-
ity of 1 mAh cm−2, as well as reversible voltage proﬁles. As de-
picted in Figures S10 (Supporting Information), the lithium plat-
ing/stripping eﬃciency in various electrolytes was further evalu-
ated using the Aurbach CE test,[19,20,24] where the Li–Cu battery
with 1 m LiFSI DEM electrolyte exhibits the highest CE value
of 98.6%. To elucidate the speciﬁc plating behavior in diﬀerent
electrolytes, a high-capacity deposition process was conducted,
wherein 5 mAh cm−2 of lithium was deposited onto Cu electrodes
at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. Subsequently, the batter-
ies were disassembled to enable detailed characterization of the
lithium metal deposition. As illustrated in Figure 2c–e and Figure
S11 (Supporting Information), optical and scanning electron mi-
croscopy (SEM) images of the plated lithium reveal a denser and
more regular deposition morphology in the DEM electrolyte. In
contrast, the deposited lithium in 1 m LiPF6 EC/DEC exhibits se-
vere dendritic growth, and the deposited lithium in 1 m LiFSI
DME electrolyte shows a sparse deposition morphology with
large areas devoid of lithium deposition.
Owing to the uniform plating/stripping behavior on the
lithium metal anode and the unique weakly solvation structure,
the 1 m LiFSI DEM electrolyte system achieves a high critical cur-
rent density (CCD) of 5.6 mA cm−2 / 5.6 mAh cm−2 (Figure S12,
Supporting Information), which is signiﬁcantly higher than that
of the 1 m LiFSI DME electrolyte (3.0 mA cm−2 / 3.0 mAh cm−2).
Moreover, the intrinsic reduction and oxidative stability of vari-
ous electrolytes were systematically investigated through electro-
chemical tests, as detailed in Figure S13 (Supporting Informa-
tion). As shown in Figure S13a (Supporting Information), dur-
ing cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests on carbon paper (CP) working
electrodes, the reduction currents for 1 m LiFSI DME and 1 m
LiFSI DEM began to increase at 1.2 and 0.88 V, respectively. The
intrinsic oxidative stability of the electrolytes was evaluated using
oxidative linear-scan voltammetry (LSV) on aluminum (Al) work-
ing electrodes. Notably, the strained conformation of the solvent
molecules in the DEM system enhances both the reduction and
oxidative stability of the electrolyte. It is noteworthy that a devia-
tion in the Li+ transference number was also observed between
the DME and DEM systems, with measured values of 0.411 and
0.667, respectively (Figure S14, Supporting Information).
The composition of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) has
been recognized as a critical factor in modulating the surface re-
activity and conductivity of lithium metal, thereby inﬂuencing its
long-term cyclability.[37–39] The SEI forms as a result of the de-
composition of electrolyte salts and solvents on the lithium metal
surface, yielding a complex layer composed of both organic and
inorganic species. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was
employed to investigate the interfacial chemistry of lithium an-
odes cycled in diﬀerent electrolyte systems. Signiﬁcant deviations
are observed in the SEI composition between lithium anode cy-
cled 2 times in DME and DEM systems. As shown in the C 1s
spectra in Figure 2f, the SEI formed in the 1 m LiFSI DME sys-
tem contains various compositions, including C─O (≈286.2 eV),
C═O (≈288.3 eV), and C─F (≈289.3 eV), which are derived from
the decomposition of the organic solvent. In contrast, the 1 m
LiFSI DEM system exhibits reduced quantities of these organic
species, as demonstrated in Figure 2i. Furthermore, the F 1s spec-
tra in Figure 2g,j reveal that all electrolytes generated character-
istic peaks corresponding to Li─F (≈684.6 eV), S─F (≈687.1 eV),
and C─F (≈688.2 eV) within the SEI. However, the 1 m LiFSI
DEM system produces the highest ratio of Li─F species, which is
known to enhance lithium reversibility.[40,41] Even after electrode
washing, the spectra also reveal the presence of salt trapped in
the crevices of the lithium metal (S─F and -N- species for LiFSI
detected in the ether electrolytes). Overall, the novel DEM elec-
trolyte can stabilize the lithium metal anode more eﬀectively with
a smooth, dense packing of lithium and an inorganic-rich SEI
composed of Li─F.
2.3. Insights to SPAN Cathode Kinetics
An ideal electrolyte for Li-SPAN batteries must not only stabilize
the lithium anode but also facilitate rapid reaction kinetics and ef-
ﬁcient utilization of active materials. The electrochemical behav-
ior of Li-SPAN batteries in diﬀerent electrolytes was investigated
through CV tests. It is important to note that the reaction path-
ways from SPAN to fully lithiated SPAN involve a complex, multi-
electron transfer process, with each electron transfer occurring at
distinct voltages.[10,14,42–44] As shown in Figures 3a and S15 (Sup-
porting Information), the signiﬁcant diﬀerences in the CV curves
reﬂect the altered reaction intermediates and pathways. Speciﬁ-
cally, the additional redox peak at 2.4 V corresponding to solid-
liquid transitions, appears when employing DME electrolyte. The
strong solvation capability of DME solvent results in the exces-
sive formation of polysulﬁde ions. The complex and sluggish
conversion kinetics in the intermediate electron-transfer steps

distinctly diﬀerent discharge processes and kinetics in Li-SPAN

that the discharge curve of the 1 m LiFSI DEM electrolyte exhibits

evidenced by higher deposition voltages and currents.
Moreover, rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) measurements
were performed to elucidate the inhibitory eﬀect of diﬀerent elec-
trolyte systems on polysulﬁde ions diﬀusion. The diﬀusion co-
eﬃcients of polysulﬁde ions in various solvents are depicted in
Figure 3d and Figure S19 (Supporting Information). Compared

electrolyte system signiﬁcantly reduces the diﬀusion coeﬃcient
of polysulﬁde ions, an eﬀect attributed to the encapsulation of
polysulﬁde ions by the outer solvation shell. To gain insight into
the diﬀusion kinetics of Li+, CV measurements were conducted
with scan rates ranging from 0.05 to 0.5 mV s−1 (Figure 3e,f;
Figure S20, Supporting Information). Compared with the inhib-
ited redox transition process observed in the DME system, the
DEM system consistently exhibits rapid oxidation and reduction
kinetics, even at the high scan rate of 0.5 mV s−1. According to the
Randles-Sevick equation[23] the Li+ diﬀusion kinetics can be de-
termined by plotting the peak current against the square root of
the scan rate (v0.5), with the slope being positively correlated to the
diﬀusion coeﬃcient. The Li-SPAN battery with 1 m LiFSI DEM
electrolyte demonstrated more favorable reduction kinetics, with
a slope of −4.260 compared to the 1 M LiFSI DME electrolyte
(slope of −1.882). Beneﬁting from the weakly solvating structure
of Li+ in the DEM electrolyte, the introduction of TTE further
accelerated the diﬀusion kinetics of Li+, resulting in a slope of
−6.861.

3. Conclusion
In summary, a novel WSE system is tailored to enhance the rate
and cycling performance of Li-SPAN batteries by anode stabi-
lization and optimization of polysulﬁde intermediates. Through
theoretical calculations and spectral analysis, a fast desolvation
process of Li+ can be demonstrated in the WSE, achieving a
smoother Li deposition morphology and higher CE. At the cath-
ode, in situ UV-vis and RRDE tests conﬁrm that the polysulﬁde
intermediate is stabilized as S3
2−and the diﬀusion of S3
2−is fur-
ther suppressed within the WSE. PITT results reveal faster sub-
sequent lithiation and deposition kinetics, thereby eﬀectively en-
hancing the rate and cycle performance. Li-SPAN batteries using
the WSE demonstrate exceptional cycling stability, achieving a
low fading rate of 0.087% per cycle over 500 cycles at a high rate
of 1 C. Additionally, the cycling lifespan of Li-SPAN pouch cells
is dramatically extended from 9 to 35 cycles with high-capacity-
retention of 90.6%, even with a high areal loading of 6.2 mg cm−2
for the SPAN cathode. In all, this work not only provides a practi-
cal electrolyte strategy but also oﬀers valuable insights and guide-
lines for the strategic development of sulfur-based battery sys-
tems.
4. Experimental Section
Raw Materials:
Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) powder was pre-
pared using polyacrylonitrile (PAN) (Mw = 150 000 g mol−1) and elemen-
tal sulfur, both were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The acetylene black
(AB) and the N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP) were supplied by Alfa Aesar.
Lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) (99.98%, Dodochem), lithium
hexaﬂuorophosphate (LiPF6) (99.98%, Dodochem), 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME) (>99.0%, GC, Alfa Aesar), diethoxy methane (DEM) (>99.0%,
GC,
TCI),
1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropylether
(TTE)
(>99.0%, GC, Aladdin), a mixture of ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate
(EC/DEC, 1:1 by volume) (>99.0%, GC, Dodochem) were obtained
without any further treatment.
Preparation of SPAN and SPAN Cathodes:
The SPAN was prepared as
below. PAN and sulfur were blended by ball milling for 2 h in ethanol
medium with a mass ratio of 1:5. Subsequently, the mixture underwent
a drying process at 60 °C in a vacuum oven for 24 h to ensure complete
evaporation of the solvent. Following the drying process, the mixture was
subjected to cyclization and vulcanization within a nitrogen-enriched fur-
nace at a high temperature of 300 °C for 6 h. After fully cooling, the product
was annealed again at 300 °C for 12 h in a nitrogen atmosphere to remove
excess sulfur. The elemental composition of the obtained SPAN is shown
in Table S1 (Supporting Information). The ﬁnal SPAN product emerged
as a black powder, ready for further applications. The SPAN cathode was
prepared as below. The SPAN powder, acetylene black, and PAN binder so-
lution (2% PAN in NMP solution) with the mass ratio of 6:3:1 in aqueous
solution. The slurry was then uniformly spread onto an aluminum current
collector using doctor blade technique, ensuring an even distribution of
the material. Subsequently, the coated current collector was placed in a
vacuum oven and subjected to a drying process at a temperature of 60 °C
overnight. The electrodes were then punched into circular disc with the
diameter of 12 mm, where the mass loading of SPAN was ≈1.5–1.8 mg
cm−2.
Preparation of Electrolytes:
The control ether-based electrolyte was pre-
pared by dissolving 1 mol L−1 LiFSI in DME solvent, which was denoted
as 1 m LiFSI DME. 1 mol L−1 LiFSI dissolved in DME/TTE (4:1 by volume)
solvent was denoted as 1 m LiFSI DME/TTE (4:1). A mixture of DEM/TTE
(10-x:x, by volume) was ﬁrst prepared (x = 0, 1, 2, 3). 1 mol L−1 LiFSI
was then dissolved in the above four mixed solvents to obtain DEM-based
electrolytes. The control carbonate-based electrolyte 1 mol L−1 LiPF6 in
a mixture of ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate (EC/DEC, 1:1 by vol-
ume), which was denoted as 1 m LiPF6 EC/DEC. All the electrolytes were
prepared in an argon-ﬁlled glove box with O2 and H2O contents below
0.1 ppm.
Material Characterization:
The surface morphologies of the prepared
SPAN cathodes and lithium metal anodes were observed through a scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7500F, JEOL). The elemental content
of the SPAN was investigated by means of elemental analysis (CHNS,
EA3000, Elementar). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) investiga-
tion was performed on a Thermo Fischer Scientiﬁc ESCALAB 250Xi spec-
trophotometer. In situ Ultraviolet-visible (in situ UV) tests were conducted
in reﬂection mode. The UV-Vis light is beamed from the light source (Jdh-
2000, Jingyi Optoelectronics) via a probe (F200-7-UV/VIS, Jingyi Optoelec-
tronics), passing through a quartz window into the cell toward a mesh
which is used to enhance reﬂection and the cathode. Reﬂection light then
travels the reverse path and is collected by the probe before being directed
to the detector (SEC2000, ALS).
Electrochemical Measurements:
The electrochemical performance was
tested by CR2025-type coin cells on the battery testing systems (Land bat-
tery tester, Wuhan, China). All coin cells were assembled in a glove box
ﬁlled with argon gas (H2O and O2 < 0.1 ppm). All Li-SPAN coin cells were
assembled with thick lithium metal (16.0 mm in diameter) as the anode,
Celgard 2325 (19.0 mm in diameter) as separator, a low (1.5-1.8 mg cm−2)
loading SPAN electrode as the cathode, and 60 μL electrolyte. The cur-
rent and the speciﬁc capacity were calculated based on the SPAN loading
in the cathodes (1 C = 600 mA g−1). Each Li-SPAN pouch cells were as-
sembled by SPAN cathode (5 × 7.5 cm2) with a SPAN loading of 6.2 mg
cm−2 (double-sided) using a separator and lithium foil stacked. The cells
were (dis-)charged in a LAND CT2001A test instrument, and the potential
window was set at 1.0–3.0 V (vs Li/Li+). For Li–Cu batteries, Coulombic
eﬃciency (CE) test was carried out through the Aurbach method.[21] The
batteries preconditioned with an initial deposition of lithium, where 5 mAh
cm−2 lithium deposited on the Cu foil at 0.5 mA cm−2, and then followed

The Li+ diﬀusion coeﬃcients were calculated through the Randles-
Sevick Equation (1).[67] Where Ip is peak current of redox reaction, n is the
number of electrons involved in the reaction, A is the geometric area of the
active electrode, C is Li+ concentration in electrolyte and v is the scanning
rate. According to the Randles-Sevick equation, the slope of the linear plot
of Ip versus v0.5 is positively correlated with the Li+ diﬀusion coeﬃcients.
The nonaqueous rotating-ring disk electrode (RRDE) conﬁguration used
in this study was adopted from that reported by Hubert et al.[68] The diﬀu-
sion coeﬃcients of polysulﬁde ions in 1 m LiFSI DME, 1 m LiFSI DEM:TTE
(4:1), 1 m LiFSI DEM, and 1 m LiFSI DEM:TTE (4:1) are determined us-
ing a method established previously for oxygen and oxygen radicals.[68,69]
Brieﬂy, the diﬀusion coeﬃcient of polysulﬁde ions is determined by ﬁtting
a relation between the inverse of the rotation speed and the transient time
in potential-stepping experiments. Note that in all potential-stepping ex-
periments, the disk potential was stepped from a deﬁned potential that is
close to OCV (no reaction happening) but not too far away from where
it is stepping to. The diﬀusion coeﬃcient of polysulﬁde ions is measured
with a stepping disk voltage that generating short chain polysulﬁdes (i.e.,
DEM: −2.3 VAg and DEM/TTE (4:1): −2.3 VAg) and a constant ring volt-
age that oxidizes polysulﬁde ions generated by the disk (i.e., DEM: 0.4
VAg and DEM/TTE (4:1): 0.4 VAg). As soon as the steeping disk voltage
is applied, the ring will oxidize the polysulﬁde ions sent from the disk.
The polysulﬁde samples with nominal composition “Li2S4” were prepared
following the method developed by Rauh et al.[70] Brieﬂy, lithium sulﬁde
(Sigma Aldrich, 99.98%) and sulfur were mixed with magnetic stirring at
room temperature for 24 hours in various solvents in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox
to yield “Li2S4” with a concentration of 2 mm following: Li2S + 3/S8 →
Li2S4.
Theory Calculations:
All computational simulations grounded in the
principles of density functional theory were executed using the Gaussian
16 software package. Geometric optimization and frequency calculations
were conducted at B3LYP/6-311+G (d, p) level. The binding energy (Eb)
between a Li2Sn (where n = 3, 4, 6) and solvents was obtained as Equa-
tion (2):
Eb = Ecluster −ELi2Sn −Esolvents
(2)
The Ecluster and ELi2Sn represented the total energy of the Li2Sn-solvents
complexes and Li2Sn, respectively. Esolvents were the total energy of two
DME molecules, two DEM molecules, or one DME/DEM plus one TTE
molecule that coordinates with Li2Sn.
Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations were performed based on the
GROMACS code and OPLS-AA force ﬁeld. The FSI−, S32−, S42−, S62−and
Li+ descriptions were obtained from previous publications.[31,32,44,71] A
cutoﬀof 1.2 nm was used for nonbonded Lennard-Jones interactions. Pe-
riodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. For each system,
initial energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent ap-
proach. Subsequently, NpT simulations with an A V-rescale thermostat
and Berendsen barostat were conducted at 300 K for 50 ns. Finally, the
last 20 ns of the production run were used for analysis. Radial distribu-
tion functions were obtained using the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD)
software. Snapshots of all electrolyte systems were also got from the sim-
ulation trajectory using VMD.
