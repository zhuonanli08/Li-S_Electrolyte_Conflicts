Dual-Mediation pathways promote redox kinetics for High-Loading 
Lithium–Sulfur batteries under lean electrolyte

A B S T R A C T  
The high energy density (2500 Wh kg−1) makes that lithium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) have a great potential in 
energy storages. However, the practical application of LSBs is severely hampered by several inherent problems, 
including the sluggish sulfur redox kinetics, the shuttling of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and poor electronic 
conductivity. Although heterogeneous or homogeneous mediators could improve the cycling performance of 
LSBs to a certain extent, single mediators could not completely promote sulfur conversions, especially under high 
sulfur loading and lean electrolyte. Herein, dual-mediator systems consist of heterogeneous and homogeneous 
mediators that are introduced to address the above issues. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and 
electrochemical analysis indicate that CoSe nanoparticles embedded in urchin-like nitrogen-doped porous carbon 
with in-situ growth of CNTs (CoSe@CNTs) as heterogeneous mediators and soluble CoCp2 as homogeneous 
mediators could effectively accelerate the bidirectional conversion kinetics of liquid LiPSs ↔ solid Li2S through 
the synergistic effect. As a consequence, the cell with dual-mediators delivers a high initial specific capacity of 
1005 mAh/g with a low attenuation rate of 0.045 % per cycle at a current density of 1.0C under sulfur loading of 
2.8 mg cm−2 after 800cycles. Importantly, it still maintains a high areal capacity of 6.53 mAh cm−2 under lower 
electrolyte/sulfur ratio (E/S, 4.2 µL mg−1) and higher sulfur loading of 7.3 mg cm−2 over 100cycles at 0.1C.   
1. Introduction 
The current energy systems are still dependent on fossil fuels, leading 
to serious energy crisis and environmental pollution. Advanced energy 
storage systems urgently need to make full use of renewable energy 
sources. Although lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been commercial­
ized on a large scale, their low practical energy density (<300 Wh kg−1) 
has gradually reached the limitation, which cannot meet the long 
duration requirements of next-generation power devices [1–3]. Lith­
ium–sulfur batteries (LSBs) are good candidates to replace LIBs, due to 
their high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and superiorities 
of sulfur cathode with low cost, abundant reserves and environmentally 
friendly [4,5]. Based on multi-electron redox reactions (S8 + 16Li ↔ 
8Li2S) and multi-phase conversion (solid (S8) → liquid (lithium poly­
sulfides (LiPSs)) → solid (Li2S2/Li2S)), LSBs have many problems to be 
solved, such as poor conductivity of S/Li2S, the shuttling of soluble LiPSs 
and sluggish sulfur conversion kinetics [6,7]. These inherent problems 
could lower sulfur utilization, reduce cycling lifespan and limit rate 
capability, thus hampering the commercialization of LSBs [8,9]. 
Notably, the sluggish sulfur conversion kinetics would get worse under 
high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte because of slower Li+/e- trans­
port and uncontrolled deposition solid Li2S [10,11]. Therefore, how to 
regulate the shuttle effect of LiPSs and accelerate sulfur redox reaction is 
crucial to improve practical performances of LSBs under extreme 
conditions. 
Transition metal selenides (TMSes) have been considered as effective 
heterogeneous mediators for trapping soluble LiPSs and promoting their 
conversion process in LSBs [12,13]. Among TMSes, CoSe could effi­
ciently capture LiPSs though the strong chemical bonds (Co-S/Li-Se) and 
reduce the nucleation barrier of solid Li2S via its metallic character and 
high electrocatalytic activity [14,15]. However, the limited specific 
surface area of CoSe cannot realize its full potential [16]. Combining 
CoSe electrocatalyst with porous carbon framework is a favorable 
strategy to maximize the advantages of each component [17]. For 
example, He et al. [18] constructed three-dimensional (3D) carbon 
decorated with CoSe nanoparticles, which could not only promote the 
conversion kinetics of LiPSs, but also effectively inhibit the growth of Li 
dendrite. Nevertheless, the slow diffusion of high concentration LiPSs

Compared with heterogeneous mediators, homogeneous mediators 
are dissolved in ether electrolyte as redox mediators, which could 
chemically catalyze the oxidation and reduction reactions of solid Li2S2/ 
Li2S through surface electroredox and diffuse [20,21]. Moreover, such 
soluble mediators improve electron transfer through a solution route 
rather than a direct surface pathway [22]. Cobaltocene (CoCp2) is a 
representative homogeneous mediator in LSBs, playing a significant role 
in accelerating the deposition/decomposition of Li2S [23]. It could 
change the deposition mode of Li2S from 2D to 3D growth, thus maxi­
mizing the deposition Li2S on the electrode surface. Whereas, the 
incorporation of soluble CoCp2 mediators in electrolyte is easy to induce 
serious shuttling and strong corrosion of Li anode, owing to the inevi­
table redox shuttle couple [24]. In this consideration, single heteroge­
neous or homogeneous mediators are difficult to achieve the rapid 
multiphase conversions during discharge and charge process under high 
sulfur loading and lean electrolyte [25]. Therefore, ideal mediator sys­
tems consisting of heterogeneous and homogeneous mediators, are 
promising to achieve high areal capacity LSBs through synergistically 
catalyzing whole sulfur redox reactions [26–29]. Despite mediators have 
been verified to boost cycling performances of LSBs, using solid 
CoSe—soluble CoCp2 dual-mediators to accelerate sulfur conversion 
kinetics under extreme conditions has not been reported, and the cor­
responding catalytic mechanisms are still unclear. 
Herein, we design dual-mediation pathways to catalyze the electro­
chemical behaviors of LiPSs and Li2S through synergistic effects, thus 
promoting complete sulfur conversion kinetics. CoSe nanoparticles 
embedded in urchin-like nitrogen-doped porous carbon with in-situ 
growth of CNTs (CoSe@CNTs) as heterogeneous mediators are con­
structed by the two-step carbonization–selenization strategy based on 
the three-dimensional (3D) structure of ZIF-8@ZIF-67. The dual- 
mediator systems could give full play to the advantages of 
CoSe@CNTs and CoCp2, which not only effectively capture and boost 
the conversion of soluble LiPSs through CoSe@CNTs mediators with 
abundant active sites, but also induce uniform deposition of soluble Li2S

2. Experimental section 
Materials and chemicals 
All chemical reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical 
Reagent Co. Ltd and were used as received without further purification. 
Distilled water was obtained from a MilliQ purified system. 
Preparation of ZIF-8 
3 mM Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O was dissolved in 50 mL methanol and 12 mM 
2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 50 mL methanol, respectively. 
Then, the as-formed two solutions were rapidly mixed and magnetic 
stirred for 24 h at 35 ◦C. The precipitate was washed by ethanol for 
several times and dried at 60 ◦C for 4 h to obtain ZIF-8. 
Preparation of ZIF-8@ZIF-67 
200 mg ZIF −8 was dissolved in 100 mL methanol. 4 mM Co 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O was dissolved in 100 mL methanol and 16 mM 2-methyl­
imidazole was dissolved in 100 mL methanol. Then, the as-formed 
three solutions were rapidly mixed and magnetic stirred for 24 h at 
35 ◦C. The precipitate was washed by ethanol for several times and dried 
at 60 ◦C for 4 h to obtain ZIF-8@ZIF-67. 
Preparation of ZIF-67 
3 mM Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O was dissolved in 50 mL methanol and 12 mM 
2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 50 mL methanol, respectively. 
Then, the as-formed two solutions were rapidly mixed and magnetic 
stirred for 24 h at 35 ◦C. The precipitate was washed by ethanol for 
several times and dried at 60 ◦C for 4 h to obtain ZIF-67. 
Preparation of CoSe@CNTs 
Through directly annealing ZIF-8@ZIF-67 under Ar atmosphere flow 
for 6 h at 800 ◦C with a ramp rate of 5 ◦C min−1 to form Co-precursors. 
The as-obtained Co-precursors and selenium powder with a mass ratio of 
1:5 were placed in two combustion boats respectively and the selenium 
powder was placed near the air intake port. Then, the two combustion 
boats were heated to 800 ◦C with a heating rate of 5 ◦C min−1 and kept at 
this temperature for 3 h under Ar atmosphere to obtain CoSe@CNTs. 
Preparation of CoSeNC 
The preparation of the CoSeNC is similar to that of the CoSe@CNTs, 
except that ZIF-8@ZIF-67 is replaced by ZIF-67.

3. Result and discussion 
The CoSe@CNTs heterogeneous mediators were prepared through a 
two-step approach, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Initially, ZIF-8@ZIF-67 
polyhedrons were prepared through a sample solution method. Scan­
ning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that the morphology of 
the polyhedrons is uniform with a diameter of 200 ~ 400 nm (Fig. S1). 
Then, the ZIF-8@ZIF-67 polyhedrons were annealed at 800 ◦C under Ar 
atmosphere to form Co-precursors. As seen in Fig. S2, the Co-precursors 
possess 3D structures in-situ growth of CNTs. Finally, the CoSe@CNTs 
were obtained by in-situ chemically conversion of Co-precursors after 
selenization. It can be observed from SEM (Fig. S3) and transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) images that the CoSe@CNTs maintain 
original 3D structures of Co-precursors (Fig. 1b ~ d). Interestingly, CNTs 
with a diameter of ~10 nm grow in-situ on the 3D frame like tentacles 
(Fig. 1e and f). The lattice fringes of ~0.25 nm and ~0.31 nm are shown 
in the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 1g) [30], referring to 
the (1 1 0) facet of CoSe and (0 0 1) facet of carbon, respectively. High- 
angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy 
(HAADF-STEM) elemental mapping images (Fig. 1h) of CoSe@CNTs 
reveal the uniform distributions of Co, Se, N and C elements on their 
surface. For comparison, CoSe nanoparticles distributed in porous 3D 
carbon (CoSeNC, Fig. S4) were constructed through a similar approach. 
The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were used to characterize the 
crystal phases of as-prepared CoSe@CNTs and CoSeNC. As presented in 
Fig. 2a, the characteristic peaks at ~34.19◦, 44.81◦, and 51.37◦are 
presented in XRD patterns of CoSe@CNTs and CoSeNC [31], corre­
sponding to the (1 0 1), (1 0 2), and (1 1 0) planes of CoSe (JCPDS:No.89- 
2004) [32], respectively. The Raman spectra (Fig. 2b) of the two ma­
terials confirm their graphitization degree. The obvious two peaks at 
1320 cm−1 and 1610 cm−1 correspond well to the D-band (defect state) 
and G-band (graphitization state) of carbon, respectively. Compared 
with CoSeNC (1.02), CoSe@CNTs show larger ID/IG value of 1.05, 
indicative of the high graphitization degree and enhanced electronic 
conductivity [33]. The surface chemical states of CoSe@CNTs and 
CoSeNC were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). 
Form Fig. S5, the XPS spectra clearly confirm the presence of Co, Se, N 
and C elements in CoSe@CNTs and CoSeNC. In Se 3d XPS spectra

To visually observe the adsorption capacity of different materials 
towards LiPSs, the Li2S6 adsorption experiments were tested through 
adding same amount materials into 10 mM Li2S6 solutions. From 
Fig. S6a, it could be seen that the Li2S6 solution treated with 
CoSe@CNTs becomes almost colorless after 4 h, while the color of so­
lutions treated with CoSeNC and blank still are yellow. As expected, the 
UV–vis absorption spectra of the Li2S6 solution treated with CoSe@CNTs 
shows much lower intensity than the other materials, indicating better 
adsorption ability of CoSe@CNTs for LiPSs [7,39]. Moreover, its 
immobilization effect towards soluble CoCp2 mediators was also 
assessed via a similar approach. In this respect, the brown solution after 
the addition of CoSe@CNTs turns into obviously lighter color solution 
and its UV–vis absorption spectra presents the lowest intensity. As seen 
in Fig. S6b, CoSe@CNTs display a stronger binding ability to soluble 
CoCp2 mediators compared with CoSeNC. Density functional theory 
(DFT) calculations were further carried out to reveal the interfacial in­
teractions between CoSe@CNTs and LiPSs or CoCp2 at the atomic level. 
From Fig. 2h, the surface of CoSe possesses much higher binding en­
ergies for Li2S6 (−2.53 eV) and CoCp2 (−2.25 eV) in comparisons to that 
of pyridinic-N surface (−1.45 eV and −1.03 eV). These results disclose 
that the CoSe@CNTs effectively trap soluble LiPSs and CoCp2 through 
chemical interaction, thereby restraining their shuttling and the conse­
quent corrosion of Li anode.

reduction reactions of LiPSs [40]. The CV profiles of the two 
CoSe@CNTs electrodes present smaller potential hysteresis and higher 
peak currents, and no distinct peaks are shown in CoSeNC electrodes. 
Besides, the CoSe@CNTs electrode with soluble CoCp2 mediators de­
livers the highest peak current and the largest peak area, implying the 
promotion of LiPSs conversion kinetics and high sulfur utilization by the 
synergistic effect of CoSe@CNTs and CoCp2 mediators. Note that the

The conversion reaction from liquid Li2S4 to solid Li2S is essential for 
the overall battery performance that contributes to 75 % of the theo­
retical specific capacity during the discharge process of LSBs. The 
deposition of solid Li2S on different catalytic matrix were investigated 
via a potentiostatic discharge measurements (Fig. 4a) at 2.05 V. Notably, 
a remarkably higher current intensity and larger nucleation capacity of 
Li2S (215.1 mAh g−1) are obtained with CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 than 
CoSe@CNTs (178.2 mAh g−1), CoCp2 (143.8 mAh g−1) and CoSeNC 
(121.2 mAh g−1). The dissolution rate of solid Li2S is another important 
kinetic indicator for subsequent oxidation reaction of Li2S. It can be 
observed that CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 is easier to facilitate Li2S dissociation 
with higher current densities and dissolution capacity of 249.1 mAh g−1 
compared with CoSe@CNTs (208.7 mAh g−1), CoCp2 (190.2mAh g−1) 
and CoSeNC (160.4 mAh g−1) during the potentiostatic charge mea­
surement (Fig. 4b). These observations clearly testify that CoSe@CNTs/ 
CoCp2 dual-mediators effectively promote the deposition and dissocia­
tion of solid Li2S. 
To deeply understand the electrocatalytic effect of dual-mediators at 
the atomic level, DFT calculations were carried out to simulate the Gibbs 
free energies of the conversion reaction from Li2S4 to Li2S by catalytic 
effects of different mediators. Optimized models of sulfur species and the 
corresponding free energies by catalytic effects of CoSe and CoSe-CoCp2 
mediators are shown in Fig. 4c. The free energy for the reduction of Li2S4 
to Li2S2 by catalytic effects of CoSe-CoCp2 mediators is only 0.23 eV, 
much lower than that by catalytic effects of CoSe mediator (0.28 eV). For 
the conversion reaction from solid Li2S2 to solid Li2S, that is regarded as 
rate-limiting step during the discharging process. The lower free energy 
(0.63 eV) by catalytic effects of CoSe/CoCp2 for this conversion 
demonstrate that nucleation of solid Li2S is thermodynamically favor­
able under dual-mediators. The decomposition energy barriers of solid 
Li2S also were calculated by DFT calculations during reverse charging. 
Optimized configurations and the corresponding dissociation pathway 
of Li2S2 are shown in Fig. 4d. The reverse oxidation reaction of solid Li2S 
also is one crucial step during overall sulfur redox process. The activa­
tion barrier required for the Li2S decomposition (Li2S → LiS + Li+ + e−) 
by catalytic effects of CoSe is much high than that by catalytic effects of 
CoSe-CoCp2 dual-mediators. Moreover, the dissociation reaction energy 
(0.986 eV) from Li2S to LiS and single Li ions by catalytic effects of CoSe- 
CoCp2 dual-mediators is markedly lower than that under CoSe (1.16 eV) 
(Fig. 4e and f). These DFT results reveal that the liquid–solid conversion 
process and the subsequent oxidation of Li2S are more thermodynami­
cally favorable by catalytic effects of CoSe/CoCp2, due to the reduced 
Li2S nucleation barrier and dissociation barrier by the synergistic effect 
of dual-mediators.

profiles of these cells at various current rates (Fig. 5f and Fig. S11), it is 
evident that the CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 cell displays the most conspicuous 
discharge plateaus and the smallest ΔE (Fig. S12) even at 2.0C and 3.0C. 
The superior rate performances indicate the high reversibility and fast 
conversion kinetics of LiPSs by the catalytic effects of CoSe@CNTs/ 
CoCp2 dual-mediators [45]. Long-term cycling performances are very 
significant for the assessment of LSBs. Benefiting from the synergistic 
effect of dual-mediators, the CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 cell maintains a high 
initial specific capacity of 1005 mAh g−1 with a low degradation rate of 
0.045 % per cycle a stable Coulombic efficiency of ~100 % at a high 
current density of 1.0C over 800cycles (Fig. 5g). 
Considering the cycling performances of LSBs would worsen with the 
increased of sulfur loading and reduced of electrolyte. To test battery 
performances under high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte is very 
critical toward the practical development of LSBs. Based on this, the 
performances of CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 cell with different sulfur loading 
and E/S ratio were used to evaluate the catalytic activity of dual- 
mediators. Fig. 6a exhibits that the cell still keeps high specific capac­
ities of 1116, 897, and 704 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5C, when the sulfur 
loading increased to 7.1 mg cm−2 and E/S ratio reduce to 4.5 µL mg−1. 
Obvious discharge plateaus and small ΔE also are retained for the 
CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 cell, as presented in the discharge–charge profiles at 
various rates (Fig. 6b). Additionally, a high initial areal capacity of 6.53 
mAh cm−2 with the capacity retention of 88.2 % and high Coulombic 
efficiency of ~ 100 % (Fig. S13) over 100 cycles at 0.1C could be ob­
tained in CoSe@CNTs/CoCp2 cell under high sulfur loading of 7.3 mg 
cm−2 and low E/S ratio of 4.2 µL mg−1 (Fig. 6c). The battery perfor­
mances based on dual-mediators are better than other reported similar 
mediators (Fig. 6d and Table S1) [46–53]. These results adequately 
demonstrate that dual-mediation pathways could efficiently promote 
sulfur redox conversion kinetics even under high sulfur loading and lean 
electrolyte conditions, thus boosting the battery performances. 
4. Conclusions 
In summary, we proposed dual-mediation pathways based on 
coupling CoSe@CNTs heterogeneous and CoCp2 homogeneous media­
tors in LSBs. The combinations of dual-mediators effectively accelerate 
conversion kinetics of LiPSs through CoSe@CNTs mediators with 
abundant active sites and promote the deposition and subsequent 
dissociation behaviors of solid Li2S via CoCp2 mediators. Moreover, 
CoSe@CNTs with abundant pores and large active surface improve the 
spatial restriction and chemisorption towards both LiPSs and soluble 
CoCp2 thus lowering the risks of the corrosion of Li anode and the 
shutting of CoCp2. As a result, the cell with the dual-mediators exhibits 
high initial specific capacities of 1610 mAh g−1 with a low attenuation 
rate of 0.045 % per cycle at a current density of 1.0C under sulfur 
loading of 2.8 mg cm−2 after 800cycles. Impressively, a high areal ca­
pacity of 6.53 mAh cm−2 is achieved by the dual-mediators at a lower E/ 
S ratio of 4.2 µL mg−1 and higher sulfur loading of 7.3 mg cm−2 over 
100cycles. This work reveals a novel effective strategy through dual- 
mediation pathways to promote sulfur redox kinetics for LSBs under 
high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte.
