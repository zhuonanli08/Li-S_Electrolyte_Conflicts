Macromolecular Boron-Based Salt Enables Dense
Interphases for Long-Cycling Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Dejie Qu, Tao Liu,* Youlong Sun, Yuewei Yan, Chuanchuan Li, Zili Cui,* Chuanwei Gao,
Shuaice Kong, Zengqi Zhang, Zhiming Liu,* Shu Zhang, Shitao Wang, Zhaolin Lv,
Gaojie Xu, Guicun Li,* and Guanglei Cui*
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a compelling next-generation
energy storage system with practical energy densities exceeding 700 Wh
kg−1, oﬀering a promising pathway beyond current lithium-ion technology.
However, their commercial viability remains constrained by deleterious
interfacial reactions between lithium metal anodes and polysulﬁde-containing
electrolytes. Herein, it is presented a molecular engineering
approach through a novel boron-based salt, lithium perﬂuoropinacolatoborate
(LiFPB), strategically designed to reinforce the solid electrolyte interphase
(SEI) for long-cycling Li-S batteries. LiFPB anions, featuring higher speciﬁc
charge (mass-to-charge ratio) and larger steric bulk compared to conventional
salts, demonstrate enhanced resistance to Helmholtz double-layer
repulsion and increased susceptibility to lithium metal reduction, promoting
the formation of a robust SEI enriched with LiF and LiBxOy species.
The LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits superior lithium metal compatibility,
achieving a high coulombic eﬃciency of 99.59%. Consequently, Li-S
cells demonstrate markedly improved capacity retention from 50.9% to 75.7%
over 200 cycles. This strategy has been successfully scaled to Ah-level Li-S
pouch cells, achieving practical energy densities of 408 Wh kg−1 with stable
cycling over 75 cycles. This work presents an eﬀective approach to developing
long-cycling Li-S batteries through the rational design of electrolyte salt.

1. Introduction
Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have great
potential to achieve practical energy den-
sities exceeding 700 Wh kg−1, a crucial
threshold for enabling heavy-duty vehicles
and electric ﬂight.[1,2] However, the intrinsic
precipitation-dissolution conversion mech-
anism of the S cathode generates solu-
ble lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) as essen-
tial intermediates, which inevitably trig-
ger the notorious shuttle eﬀect and subse-
quent parasitic reactions between lithium
(Li) metal anodes and LiPSs. Under prac-
tical cell design conditions (high sulfur
loading, lean electrolyte, and ultrathin Li
metal), the accelerated electrolyte degrada-
tion, primarily stemming from these para-
sitic reactions at the Li metal surface, fun-
damentally degrades the cycling stability
of Li-S batteries.[3,4] Therefore, designing
a robust solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI)
to stabilize Li metal anodes against LiPS-
induced deterioration plays a crucial role
in realizing long-cycling Li-S batteries.[5,6]
The electrolyte composition and its derived SEI fundamen-
tally govern the cycling stability of Li-S batteries. Among vari-
ous electrolyte components, lithium salts play a pivotal role in
enhancing the coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li-S batteries by fa-
cilitating the formation of dense, insoluble inorganic compo-
nents within the SEI layer. Notably, the strategic incorporation
of LiNO3 has demonstrated remarkable eﬀectiveness in sup-
pressing the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect, enabling Li-S batteries
to achieve CEs exceeding 99%.[7] However, the limited solu-
bility of LiNO3 and its progressive decomposition during cy-
cling compromise the long-term cycling performance stability of
Li-S batteries, particularly under low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S)
ratios.[8] Lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) and lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) demonstrate en-
hanced compatibility with Li metal anodes due to the forma-
tion of LiF-rich SEI. Nevertheless, the S═O structure in LiTFSI
molecules acts as an electrophilic center susceptible to nucle-
ophilic attack from terminal sulﬁde species (S−) present in
lithium polysulﬁdes (Li2Sn).[9] This mechanistic understanding
explains why many LiF-forming sulfonate-based lithium salts
prove incompatible as electrolyte components in Li-S batteries.
Therefore, the rational design of novel lithium salts that can

facilitate a robust SEI while maintaining chemical stability with
Li2Sn emerges as a critical challenge for advancing stable Li-S
batteries.
During lithium plating, the signiﬁcant volumetric expansion
of the Li metal anode leads to continuous SEI rupture, necessi-
tating persistent SEI reformation. Electric double layer plays a
crucial role in SEl formation. At the anode/electrolyte interface, a
Li+-rich but anion-depleted inner-Helmholtz layer spontaneously
forms through the self-assembly of solvent molecules during
charging. This interfacial structure fundamentally dictates the
result of interphasial chemistry; notably, the negatively charged
electrode surface electrostatically repels anions from the inner
Helmholtz layer, signiﬁcantly impeding the formation of critical
inorganic SEI components.[10] To address these challenges, vari-
ous electrolyte engineering strategies have been developed to es-
tablish favorable interphases, including high-concentration elec-
trolytes (HCEs)[11] and localized high-concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs).[12] However, these approaches often compromise sul-
fur utilization and adversely aﬀect the discharge voltage plateau.
Theoretically, anions with higher speciﬁc charge (mass-to-charge
ratio) exhibit reduced sensitivity to applied electric ﬁelds, thereby
demonstrating enhanced resistance to electric double-layer re-
pulsion. This characteristic potentially increases the probability
of anion reduction and subsequent formation of desirable inor-
ganic SEI components during charging.
In this work, we present an alternative approach to stabiliz-
ing Li metal interfaces, diverging from conventional HCE or
LHCE strategies. We rationally designed and synthesized a novel
lithium perﬂuoropinacolatoborate (LiFPB) salt through a single-
step reaction in anhydrous conditions, strategically incorporating
both C-F and B-containing functionalities. The as-synthesized
LiFPB demonstrates dual advantages: exceptional chemical sta-
bility with soluble polysulﬁdes and superior SEI formation. No-
tably, LiFPB (682 g mol−1) possesses a signiﬁcantly higher spe-
ciﬁc charge (682/280) (2.43 times that of TFSI−) and a substan-
tially lower LUMO energy level, facilitating the formation of a
robust SEI enriched with LiF and LiBxOy species. The LiFPB-
containing electrolyte exhibits remarkable compatibility with Li
metal anodes, achieving an unprecedented coulombic eﬃciency
of 99.59%. This superior interfacial stability translates into ex-
ceptional performance in practical applications: an Ah-level Li-S
pouch cell employing the LiFPB-containing electrolyte delivers a
practical energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 while maintaining stable
cycling performance over 75 cycles. This novel lithium salt paves
the way for boosting the development of practical Li-S batteries.
2. Results and Discussion
The electrostatic force experienced by anions near the electrode
surface follows Coulomb’s law (F = qE, where F represents force,
q denotes charge, and E indicates electric ﬁeld strength). Con-
sequently, anions possessing higher speciﬁc charge (mass-to-
charge ratio) demonstrate diminished mobility in response to
applied electric ﬁelds. These sterically hindered or high molec-
ular weight anions exhibit enhanced resistance to electrostatic
repulsion from the Helmholtz double-layer during lithium plat-
ing processes. This reduced mobility enables them to remain
in closer proximity to the electrode surface, fundamentally alter-
ing the composition and morphology of the resulting SEI layer
and subsequently inﬂuencing lithium deposition behavior. The
LiFPB salt with a molecular weight of 682 g mol−1 (Figure 1a) was
synthesized via a one-pot reaction of lithium borohydride (LiBH4)
with perﬂuoropinacol in a molar ratio of 1:2, using tetrahydro-
furan (THF) as the solvent at 60 °C (Figure S1, Supporting In-
formation). This approach minimizes the introduction of impu-
rities, as the entire process is conducted in an organic solvent
environment, thereby reducing the need for additional puriﬁca-
tion steps.[13,14] The peak observed at m/z = 674.95 in the mass
spectrometry (MS) analysis (Figure 1b) aligns precisely with the
theoretical molecular weight of the FPB−anion. Nuclear mag-
netic resonance (NMR) analysis further validates the structural
integrity and purity of the compound. A single distinct peak was
observed in the 11B NMR spectrum at 11.40 ppm (Figure 1c) and
in the 19F NMR spectrum at −69.24 ppm (Figure 1d), with no ad-
ditional peak indicative of impurities. These results conﬁrm the
presence of only one type of boron and ﬂuorine chemical environ-
ment within the salt, underscoring its high purity and structural
homogeneity. Upon incorporating 0.2 M LiFPB, 0.8 M LiTFSI,
and 1 wt.% LiNO3 into a solution comprising DME and DOL at
a volumetric ratio of 1:1, the 11B NMR spectrum retains its char-
acteristic peak at 11.40 ppm (Figure S2a, Supporting Informa-
tion), while the 19F NMR spectrum exhibits two peaks at −69.24
and −79.80 ppm (Figure S2b, Supporting Information), the lat-
ter peak attributed to LiTFSI.[15] In the 13C NMR spectrum, two
distinct sets of carbon signals are observed, corresponding to the
C─O and −CF3 groups in the FPB−anion (Figure S3, Supporting
Information). We conducted a series of reactions between poly-
sulﬁde solutions and both LiFPB and LiTFSI (Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information). Upon analysis of the mixture containing
LiTFSI and Li2S6, a notable shift in the characteristic 19F NMR
of LiTFSI was observed, migrating from −79.8 to −79.52 ppm
(Figure S5a, Supporting Information). Concurrently, a distinct
by-product emerged at −75.39 ppm (Figure S5b, Supporting In-
formation). These ﬁndings unequivocally demonstrate the inher-
ent incompatibility between LiTFSI and polysulﬁdes, highlight-
ing the susceptibility of LiTFSI to nucleophilic attack by polysul-
ﬁde species. In stark contrast, when LiFPB was combined with
Li2S6, the characteristic 19F NMR peak of LiFPB exhibited re-
markable stability, remaining invariant. This observation under-
scores the exceptional resistance of LiFPB to nucleophilic attack
by polysulﬁdes, thereby establishing its superior compatibility
with these compounds (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The
low reactivity may be due to the high steric hindrance of high
ﬂuorine groups and the low valence state of boron (+3). This
unique structural arrangement imparts LiFPB an extraordinary
degree of chemical robustness, rendering it impervious to the
deleterious eﬀects of polysulﬁde interactions. These results in-
dicate that LiFPB exhibits excellent chemical compatibility with
polysulﬁdes. The electronic properties of the synthesized FPB−
anion were characterized through frontier molecular orbital anal-
ysis, which are critical parameters for evaluating the SEI-forming
ability. Notably, the FPB−anion (4.55 eV) exhibits a signiﬁcantly
lower LUMO energy level compared to the TFSI−anion (4.75
eV) (Figure 1e), indicating enhanced thermodynamic favorabil-
ity toward electrochemical reduction and subsequent SEI forma-
tion. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further elu-
cidated the interfacial interaction mechanisms between LiFPB
and lithium metal. The calculated binding energy for LiFPB

Detailed spectroscopic analysis of the B 1s core-level spectrum
(Figure 3c) conﬁrmed the formation of boron-oxygen (B─O) coor-
dination structures, which serve as strategically positioned elec-
trophilic sites for preferential coordination with TFSI−anions.
The selective anion immobilization mechanism eﬀectively mit-
igates electrolyte decomposition through the formation of a ro-
bust anode-electrolyte interface.[22,23] Comparative analysis re-
vealed that lithium metal electrodes cycled in conventional elec-
trolytes demonstrated progressive interfacial degradation, char-
acterized by the time-dependent accumulation of electrolyte de-
composition byproducts. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth
proﬁling identiﬁed the formation of organic carbonyl (C═O) and
alkoxy (C─O) species, along with inorganic lithium carbonate
(Li2CO3) compounds, whose concentration gradients exhibited
a strong correlation with sputtering duration (Figure S10, Sup-
porting Information). Depth proﬁling by time-of-ﬂight secondary
ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) corroborates these ﬁndings.
In our TOF-SIMS analysis, a substantial signal for Li2F+ was
detected. Li2F+ was commonly employed to identify the pres-
ence of LiF, thereby indicating that the electrolyte containing
LiFPB is conducive to the formation of an SEI layer enriched with
LiF. Notably, the LiF distribution exhibited both enhanced con-
centration and improved spatial homogeneity (Figure 3e) across
the electrode interface, with these beneﬁcial characteristics be-
ing maintained throughout the etching proﬁle (Figure S11, Sup-
porting Information). Spectroscopic and electrochemical analy-
ses revealed that LiFPB facilitate the formation of lithium borate
(LiBxOy) complexes at the lithium metal interface. These borate-
containing species serve as rapid lithium-ion conductors within
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI),[24–27] facilitating the uni-
form deposition of Li metal (Figure 3f). Depth-resolved chemi-
cal characterization demonstrated signiﬁcantly enhanced reten-
tion of intact TFSI−anions at the electrode interface in LiFPB-
containing electrolytes (Figure S11, Supporting Information),[25]
indicative of suppressed electrolyte decomposition pathways.
This electrochemical stabilization originates from the formation
of an ionically conductive and mechanically robust SEI layer,
which eﬀectively reduces the direct contact between the elec-
trode and the electrolyte and minimizes parasitic reactions at
the electrode-electrolyte interface (Figures S12,S13, Supporting
Information).
Conventional electrolytes typically induce severe interfa-
cial side reactions through electrolyte decomposition, result-
ing in the formation of a thick, heterogeneous, and porous
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI). In marked contrast, our sys-
tematic characterization reveals that LiFPB incorporation en-
ables the formation of an ultrathin, densely packed bilayer
SEI architecture. This optimized interfacial structure com-
prises an outer organic-rich matrix and an inner inorganic-
rich domain with substantial LiF enrichment (Figure 3g;
Figure S14, Supporting Information). The rationally designed
SEI conﬁguration not only enhances the mechanical integrity
and electrochemical stability of the lithium metal anode but
also eﬀectively suppresses parasitic reactions at the electrode-
electrolyte interface. Consequently, the LiFPB-containing system
demonstrates signiﬁcantly improved cycling stability and rate
capability.[28]
To systematically assess the redox stability of the battery in-
corporating LiFPB, the exchange current density (I0) was uti-
lized as a quantitative metric for surface diﬀusion resistance
(Figure 4a). Upon introduction of LiFPB into the electrolyte, the
exchange current density associated with the electroplating and
stripping of lithium metal exhibited a pronounced increase, esca-
lating from 0.689 to 1.001 mA/cm2, thereby representing a sub-
stantial 45.3% enhancement. This remarkable augmentation in
interfacial charge transfer eﬃciency is principally attributable to
the formation of a highly conductive solid electrolyte interphase
(SEI) layer, which is characterized by optimized lithium-ion dif-
fusion pathways. The presence of LiFPB in the electrolyte pro-
motes the formation of an SEI layer that is rich in LiF and LiBxOy
compounds. These compounds facilitate rapid lithium-ion trans-
port, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing the overall conductivity of
the SEI layer. The engineered anode interface architecture signif-
icantly contributes to the enhanced electrochemical performance
and cycling stability of the battery system. Cyclic voltammetry
(CV) of Li||Cu half-cells (0.2 mV s−1) revealed superior redox
characteristics in LiFPB-containing electrolytes, with higher peak
currents and well-deﬁned redox potentials versus conventional
electrolytes (Figure 4b). This trend was conﬁrmed in Li-S full
cells across multiple scan rates (Figure 4c; Figure S15, Support-
ing Information).[27] Furthermore, our investigations have eluci-
dated that, in contrast to LiTFSI, LiFPB salt manifests a prefer-
ential reduction potential and a conspicuously higher reduction
peak intensity (Figure S16, Supporting Information). This obser-
vation intimates that LiFPB salt is more susceptible to decompo-
sition reactions and exhibits superior compatibility with metallic
lithium, a propensity that can be attributed to the highly eﬃca-
cious SEI layer it engenders on the negative electrode surface. De-
spite both salts being classiﬁed as readily reducible lithium salts,
the SEI precipitated from the reduction of LiFPB markedly out-
performs that of LiTFSI in terms of eﬃcacy. Notably, the adsorp-
tion energy of LiFPB on the lithium metal surface is of a value
higher than that of DME and DOL (Figure S17, Supporting Infor-
mation). This ﬁnding unequivocally suggests that LiFPB exhibits
a preferential adsorption aﬃnity on the lithium metal surface.
The solvation structure was further analyzed by Raman spec-
troscopy. The LiFPB salt did not signiﬁcantly aﬀect the Raman
shifts of DME, DOL, and LiTFSI (Figure 4d; Figure S18, Support-
ing Information),[29] indicating that the SEI modiﬁcation is not
attributed to changes in the solvation structure. Electrochemi-
cal impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses performed on lithium
symmetric cells (Li||Li) both prior to and subsequent to polariza-
tion with a 10 mV direct current (DC) unambiguously corrobo-
rated the salient beneﬁts conferred by the incorporation of LiFPB
salt. The impedance of the electrolyte containing LiFPB is signif-
icantly lower than that of the conventional electrolyte (Figure 4e),
indicating that the SEI ﬁlm formed by LiFPB salt can eﬀectively
facilitate the migration of lithium ions within the solid elec-
trolyte interphase (SEI) and signiﬁcantly reduce the interfacial
resistance.[30] The impedance measurements of Li||Li symmet-
ric cells conducted at varying temperatures demonstrate that the
LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits a signiﬁcantly lower value
compared to conventional electrolytes (Figure S19, Supporting
Information). This reduction in impedance indicates that the
LiFPB-containing electrolyte eﬀectively lowers the internal resis-
tance of the battery (Figure 4f).

the LiFPB-modiﬁed anode exhibited reduced surface roughness
(807.3 nm) compared to the LiTFSI-containing system (886.9
nm, Figure S21, Supporting Information). These observations
collectively indicate the formation of a thinner, more homoge-
neous SEI layer with minimized parasitic reactions, highlight-

To better understand the electrochemical behavior of lithium-
sulfur batteries during cycling, diﬀerential capacity (dQ/dV) anal-
yses after 100 cycles revealed that modiﬁed electrolytes raised
the plateau voltages, reducing cell polarization (Figure 5c,d).
This improvement is attributed to a better conductive network
and reduced interfacial impedance, mitigating capacity loss and
enhancing polysulﬁde deposition/dissolution uniformity while
suppressing the shuttle eﬀect. In situ impedance and DRT anal-
yses showed lower impedance for the modiﬁed electrolyte across
all voltage ranges (Figure 5e). The stable SEI ﬁlm enriched with
LiF and LiBxOy reduces the charge transfer resistance in the high-
frequency region (intrinsic electrolyte conductivity and interfa-
cial contact), while the shortened lithium-ion diﬀusion pathways
lower the impedance in the mid-frequency region (charging and
discharging processes of the interfacial electric double layer).
In the low-frequency region (liquid-phase diﬀusion of soluble
lithium polysulﬁdes), the mitigated diﬀusion polarization en-
ables faster and more uniform lithium-ion transport (Figure 5f).
These improvements, facilitated by the incorporation of LiFPB,
enhance the stability of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), al-
leviate the shuttle eﬀect, and signiﬁcantly improve the utiliza-
tion rate of the sulfur cathode and the cycling stability of the
battery.

To investigate the underlying mechanisms, the morphology of
the Li metal after 40 cycles was examined (Figure 5i–k). The cy-
cled Li metal in the conventional electrolyte displayed severe cor-
rosion, characterized by numerous cracks and a highly porous
structure. This degradation is primarily attributed to continuous
reactions between Li metal and polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte. In
contrast, the Li surface cycled with the LiFPB-containing elec-
trolyte exhibited larger particles with a more uniform, adherent,
and smooth morphology, even as the number of cycles increases
to 100 (Figure 5l).[33] The surface degradation observed in conven-
tional electrolytes was likely attributed to non-uniform electric
currents caused by cracking and porosity, which result in irregu-
lar lithium plating and stripping. These issues ultimately lead to a
reduction in the cycle life of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries. Incor-
porating LiFPB into electrolyte eﬀectively suppressed the shuttle
eﬀect and facilitates the formation of a thin and uniform SEI.
These improvements mitigated undesirable side reactions, pro-
moted stable lithium deposition, and signiﬁcantly enhanced the
cycling stability and overall performance of Li-S batteries.[34,35]
To evaluate the practical viability of the modiﬁed electrolyte,
an Ah-level Li-S pouch cell was fabricated. The cell delivered
an impressive energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 at 0.1 C, main-
taining 62.24% capacity retention with a high average coulom-
bic eﬃciency of 99.02% over 76 cycles (Figure 6a). This result
indicate that the incorporation of LiFPB into electrolyte can ef-
fectively relieve the corrosion of lithium metal by polysulﬁde-
containing electrolyte in pouch cells by froming a robust SEI.[36]
The electrostatic repulsion of conventional anions within the in-
ner Helmholtz layer (IHP), arising from the negatively charged
electrode surface, hinders the formation of crucial inorganic SEI
components. However, LiFPB anions, possessing a higher spe-
ciﬁc charge (mass-to-charge ratio), demonstrate reduced suscep-
tibility to the applied electric ﬁeld and consequently greater re-
sistance to IHP repulsion (Figure 6b). For the in situ infrared
spectroscopy analysis, a comparative examination of the C-F
peak variations in the LiFPB and LiTFSI systems revealed a dis-
tinct correlation between the detected C-F signal intensity and
the adsorption/desorption dynamics of anions within the inner
Helmholtz layer as charge-discharge cycling progressed (Figure
S27, Supporting Information). Speciﬁcally, the characteristic ab-
sorption peak of LiFPB was consistently observed throughout
the entire cycling process, with its intensity ﬂuctuations being
markedly smaller than that of TFSI−. This ﬁnding provides com-
pelling evidence that FPB−is indeed present within the electric
double layer and exhibits superior stability during the charge-
discharge process, thereby indicating that LiFPB possesses a
signiﬁcantly enhanced retention rate within the double layer.
The resultant thin, ionically conductive, and robust LiF-rich and
LiBxOy-rich SEI layer eﬀectively suppressed Li dendrite growth
(Figure 6c), ultimately enhancing the cycling stability of Li-S
pouch cells.
3. Conclusion
In this work, we demonstrated that LiFPB served as a highly
eﬀective co-salt for Li-S batteries by simultaneously addressing
two critical challenges: the polysulﬁde shuttle eﬀect and the Li
metal anode degradation. Mechanistically, the incorporation of
LiFPB facilitated the formation of a robust SEI layer enriched
with LiF and LiBxOy components, which signiﬁcantly suppressed
electrolyte decomposition and enabled homogeneous Li plat-
ing/stripping. The exceptional electrochemical performance can
be rationalized by the unique molecular features of LiFPB, specif-
ically its high speciﬁc charge capacity and favorable reduction po-
tential, which synergistically enhance SEI stability and minimize
parasitic side reactions. The practical viability of this strategy is
evidenced by the superior performance in both high-loading Li-S
half-cells and Ah-level pouch cells across various C-rates, repre-
senting a signiﬁcant advancement toward the commercialization
of high-energy-density Li-S batteries.
