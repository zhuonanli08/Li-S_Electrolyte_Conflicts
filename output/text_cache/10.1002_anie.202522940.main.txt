Modulating Li+ and Polysulﬁde Solvation with Low-Density
Moderately Solvating Electrolytes for Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Tianxing Lai, Kameron Liao, and Arumugam Manthiram*
Abstract: Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries show great promise as the next-generation rechargeable batteries, yet they
still suffer from polysulﬁde shuttling and interphasial instability. Electrolyte, as the medium for ion transport and sulfur
conversion, plays a crucial role in overcoming these challenges. Here, we introduce a moderately solvating electrolyte
(MSE) based on low-density, low-viscosity, and nonﬂuorinated ether co-solvents that balances polysulﬁde suppression,
Li metal stabilization, and redox kinetics. Through multiple solvent–solvent and solvent-ion interactions, the optimized
MSE weakens Li+-solvent pairing while strengthening cation–anion interactions, thereby lowering the desolvation barrier
and promoting the formation of a favorable solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI). Meanwhile, MSE limits the polysulﬁde
dissolution but improves the accessibility of active material through better wettability and tailored solvation environment,
leading to an altered sulfur deposition mechanism with β – α conversion. This approach enables a stable cycling of high-
mass loading Li–S cells (> 3.5 mg cm−2) at both room temperature and 45 °C (where shuttling and side reactions are
severer), and demonstrates a pouch cell with lean electrolyte content (4.5 µL mg s−1). This work highlights a practical route
to develop high-performance electrolyte for Li–S cells and provides mechanistic insights into their operation.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have attracted intense inter-
est as a next-generation energy-storage technology because
they combine high theoretical energy density (2600 W h
kg−1) with low material cost and less supply chain issues
(compared to current transition-metal based chemistries).[1]
Li–S batteries are particularly attractive for weight and cost-
sensitive applications, such as electric vehicles, aviation, and
space equipment. However, practical deployment of Li–S
cells is impeded by several interconnected challenges. During
cycling, sulfur undergoes multistep redox reactions that form
electrolyte soluble lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) intermediates.
They lead to an infamous phenomenon called shuttling, which
then causes active material loss and continuous parasitic
reactions at the lithium–metal anode. Meanwhile, the lithium
anode suffers from intrinsically poor interphasial stability,
while the sulfur cathode is limited by its low conductivity,
poor redox kinetics, and large volume changes during cycling.

These issues could be even more challenging when practical

are employed, leading to poor energy density and cycle
life.[2,3]
To address these obstacles, extensive efforts have been
made in the ﬁeld. Cathode engineering, including inge-
niously designed carbon hosts and catalyst materials, has
proven effective in ameliorating sulfur conversion kinetics
and mitigating LiPS shuttling.[4–11] Nevertheless, electrolyte,
as the solvation and transport medium for Li+ ion and
polysulﬁde, plays a decisive role in regulating polysulﬁde
chemistry and interphasial stability of both electrodes. In
fact, electrolyte design offers a feasible and arguably more
scalable route to remedy the unique polysulﬁde-related
problems in Li–S cells. A range of electrolyte strategies has,
therefore, been explored, including solvent and salt selection,
concentration tuning, targeted additives.[12–17] The ether-
based 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) / 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)
electrolyte remains the benchmark in the ﬁeld because it
offers reasonable sulfur conversion kinetics and compatibility
with Li anode; however, it provides limited control over
LiPS shuttling. Carbonate electrolytes are widely employed
in commercial Li-ion batteries. They are normally considered
incompatible with Li–S chemistry due to the nucleophilic
attack by LiPSs, but they could be an option as they can
limit the LiPS dissolution by forming a protective cathode
electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer.[18–20] However, the use
of carbonate electrolytes in Li–S cells requires extreme
conﬁnement of sulfur at the cathode, such as covalently
bonded or sophisticated structures.[21–23] Moreover, carbonate
electrolytes show inferior compatibility with Li metal, limiting
the long-term cyclability of the anode. Ether-based localized
high concentration electrolytes (LHCE) or weakly solvating

Recently, the concept of a moderately solvating electrolyte
(MSE) has emerged as an attempt to ﬁnd the balance between
suppressing LiPS shuttling and acquiring sufﬁcient redox
kinetics.[32–34] Typical MSE formulations combine stronger
solvating solvents (DME or DOL remain common choices)
with co-solvents that have weaker solvating capabilities.
However, the standard of choosing such co-solvent and a
detailed understanding of how it inﬂuences the polysulﬁde
solvating environment are still lacking. Here, we present
new MSE designs employing a series of low-density, low-
viscosity, low-cost, and nonﬂuorinated ether co-solvents.
Their inﬂuence on the Li+ ion and polysulﬁde solvations are
systematically investigated. It is revealed that MSEs modulate
the solvating environment for not only Li+ ions, but also
salt and polysulﬁde anions, through multiple intermolecular
interactions. As a result, the desolvation barrier for Li+
ion is reduced and an inorganic-enriched solid-electrolyte
interphase (SEI) is formed on the anode surface, leading to
a better reversibility of Li plating and stripping (Figure 1a).
Meanwhile, the polysulﬁde behavior in the electrolyte is
altered, mitigating shuttling and reducing active material
loss. Operando and electrochemical characterizations further
reveal the change in sulfur conversion pathway and redox
kinetics with MSE. Finally, the optimized MSE enables high
sulfur utilization and stable cycling of Li || S cells with
practical S loadings and limited electrolyte conditions at
both room temperature and 45 °C. The effectiveness of this
electrolyte at larger scale is also demonstrated with pouch
cells.
Results and Discussion
Based on their structural and physicochemical properties, a
series of single-oxygen ethers, including methyl tert–butyl
ether (MtBE), cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME), sec-butyl
methyl ether (SBME), and tert-amyl methyl ether (TAME),
were selected as the solvents to regulate the solvating ability
of the conventional DOL/DME electrolyte (Figure 1b and
Table S1). These solvents feature only one solvating O
atom and possess sufﬁcient steric hindrance that limits their
solvating capability for Li+. Unlike nonsolvating diluents,
such as highly ﬂuorinated ethers, these solvents show some
degree of solvating power, but still have much lower solubility
for LiPS compared to DME and DOL, which can lead to
unique local solvating environment (Figure 1c and Figure S2).
Meanwhile, they feature slight structural differences among
each other that help ﬁne tune the solvation structures
and better understand their effect in the electrolyte. The
designed MSEs with 30 vol% of these solvents are labeled
as MSE-MtBE, MSE-CPME, MSE-SBME, and MSE-TAME,
respectively (detailed compositions given in the Supporting
Information). Owing to the low density of these solvents,
these MSEs exhibit lower densities (e.g., 1.04 g cm−3 for
MSE-MtBE) compared to DOL/DME baseline (1.16 g cm−3).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations conﬁrm that
DME, due to its bidentate solvation, has a strong binding
energy of 0.643 eV to a Li+ ion (Figure 1d,e). In contrast,
three of the four selected solvents bind signiﬁcantly weaker
with binding energy values of ∼0.15 eV. Note that CPME
shows a binding energy of ∼0.325 eV, which is even slightly
higher than that of DOL. This indicates that the ﬁve-member
ring structure at one side of the O atom limits the steric
hindrance compared to other solvents.
Lithium-Ion Solvation and Anode Stabilization
The solvation structures of MSEs were examined with
Raman spectroscopy and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
spectroscopy. Raman spectra display a blue shift of the
S─N─S vibrational mode of TFSI for all the MSEs (742.5–
743.1 cm−1) compared to the DOL/DME baseline (741.6
cm−1), suggesting more contact-ion pairs (CIPs) and salt
aggregates (AGGs) formed in MSEs (Figure S3). This is also
demonstrated by the peak deconvolution results, which show
a clear increase in the intensity of the CIP/AGG peak at
∼745 cm−1 for MSEs. NMR spectroscopy was employed
to provide a more precise understanding of the solvation
structures. In the 7Li NMR spectra, all the peaks in MSEs
show a downﬁeld shift from −1.19 ppm in the DOL/DME
baseline electrolyte, indicating less electron shielding around
the Li+ ion (Figure 2a). This involves the combination of two
competing effectsshielding from the increase of cation–anion
pairing and de-shielding from the reduction of Li+–solvent
interactions. Since DME is a strong solvating solvent and the
electrons on its O atoms strongly shield Li+, replacing it with
an anion or a weakly solvating solvent in the solvation shell
usually results in more de-shielding.[35–37] Note that Raman
results show a similar increase of cation-anion pairing in all
MSEs. Therefore, the fact that the 7Li peak shifts to greater
downﬁeld in MSE-MtBE and MSE-SBME indicates their
stronger ability to pull DME and DOL away from the Li+ ion,
as well as their weaker interactions with Li+ ion, compared to
other MSEs.
Similarly, in the 19F spectra, the TFSI−anion peaks shift
upﬁeld in MSEs compared to the DOL/DME electrolyte
(−80.17 ppm) due to stronger Li+–anion pairing and weaker
anion-solvent interactions (Figure S4). Nevertheless, unlike
the
7Li peak, the upﬁeld shift of the
19F peak in MSE-
MtBE is the smallest among MSEs, indicating potentially
stronger interactions between its protons and TFSI−anions.
This is further demonstrated with the 2D 19F diffusion ordered
spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR spectra, where TFSI−anion
exhibits a slightly smaller diffusion coefﬁcient of 4.37 × 10−10
m2 s−1 in MSE-MtBE compared to 5.01 × 10−10 m2 s−1 in
DOL/DME (Figure S5).
1H NMR results provide further insight into these solvent-
related interactions. After adding MtBE into the DOL/DME
mixture, the 1H peaks for DOL and DME shift downﬁeld
owing to the MtBE-induced solvent-solvent interactions
(Figure 2b). The O atoms in MtBE pull electrons away from
the H atoms in DOL and DME, resulting in less electron
shielding of the H atoms. When Li salt is introduced into
the system, DME pairs more with the Li+ ions, reducing its
interactions with other solvents. The MtBE peaks show an
upﬁeld shift due to this effect, as well as the solvent-anion
interactions discussed earlier. Moreover, by comparing 1H
NMR spectra for all the MSEs with salt, the 1H peaks for
DME and DOL in MSE-MtBE show the most downﬁeld
shift, suggesting the strongest capability of MtBE to reduce
electron density on DME and DOL (Figure 2c). This could
be one of the reasons why DME and DOL show weaker
interactions with Li+ ion in MSE-MtBE compared to other
MSEs.
These solvation properties and interactions were further
demonstrated with molecular dynamics (MD) and DFT
calculations. As shown in the MD results, the coordination
number (CN) of ODME around Li+ in the ﬁrst solvation shell
(∼2–4 Å) decreases in MSEs compared to DOL/DME, with
MtBE having the lowest coordination number (Figures 2d
and S6). This conﬁrms that all the MSEs can weaken
the coordination between Li+ and DME, but MtBE does
so more effectively, aligning well with the NMR results.
Meanwhile, radial distribution function (RDF) peaks of the
O atom on MSEs in the ﬁrst solvation sheath can be
observed, demonstrating their limited, yet not negligible,
solvating power (Figure S7). DFT calculations of DME–
solvent complexes reveal that DME–MtBE has the highest
binding energy of 0.189 eV among all MSEs, conﬁrming their
strongest intermolecular interactions (Figure 2e).
Furthermore, small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) was
performed to probe the inhomogeneities, clustering, and
intermolecular spacing in the electrolytes across microscopic
to mesoscopic scales.[38] Taking MtBE-MSE as an example,
both MtBE-MSE and DOL/DME baseline display a broad
peak in their SAXS curves at a high scattering factor (q) value
(> 1 Å−1), corresponding to the average molecule size or
intermolecular distances (Figure S8). Meanwhile, there is also
a shoulder peak at ∼0.6 Å−1, which is related to the CIP/AGG
formed in the electrolyte (with a size of ∼10 Å). The fact
that MtBE-MSE shows higher intensity in this region conﬁrms
the formation of more CIPs and AGGs in the electrolyte. In
addition, MtBE-MSE exhibits higher intensity in the q range
of 0.05–0.4 Å−1, suggesting more pronounced clustering at a
larger scale (∼10 s of nm), potentially owing to the introduced
solvent-solvent interactions with MtBE.
The altered solvation properties of MSEs play an impor-
tant role in inﬂuencing the reaction kinetics at the Li-metal
side, which is examined with electrochemical tests. Tafel
plots of Li || Li symmetric cells were derived from linear
scanning voltammetry (LSV) scans (Figure S9). By ﬁtting
these Tafel plots, the exchange current density of the redox
reaction can be obtained. MSEs exhibit higher exchange
current densities compared to DOL/DME, indicating faster
electrochemical reaction kinetics on Li metal. Temperature-
dependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)
on fresh Li || Li cells provides more insight into the Li+
transport kinetics at the interphase. Through the distribution
of relaxation time (DRT) analysis for the EIS data, there is
one major peak within the time constant range of 10−4 to 10−2,
corresponding to the semicircle at mid-frequency region in
the Nyquist plots (Figures S10 and S11). This can be mainly

MSE-MtBE provides an optimal balance of cation-anion,
anion-solvent, and solvent–solvent interactions. It weakens
excessive Li+-solvent binding and promotes favorable cation-
anion contacts, leading to reduced desolvation barrier and
superior anode performance. Further insights into how MSEs
inﬂuence polysulﬁde solvation and cathode performance will
be discussed in the next section.
Polysulﬁde Solvation and Conversion Kinetics
LiPSs play a pivotal role in affecting the redox reaction
kinetics in Li–S batteries. Although MSEs possess lower
LiPS solubility than the DOL/DME baseline, they still
involve a soluble LiPS-mediated redox pathway (Figure S13).
Therefore, investigating LiPS behavior in the electrolyte is
crucial for understanding the cathode kinetics and overall cell
performance. NMR tests were ﬁrst conducted on 0.1 M Li2S6
solutions (without other salts) to evaluate the solvating envi-
ronment of LiPS. In the 7Li spectra, the peak downshifts from
0.17 ppm in DOL/DME to 0.28 ppm in MtBE (Figure 3a).
Other MSEs also show a downﬁeld shift (except CPME), but
less pronounced compared to MtBE. This could mainly result
from reduced Li+-solvent interactions with more Li+ and S62−
pairing, similar to the case of regular electrolytes discussed in
the previous section.
Furthermore,
1H and
7Li DOSY NMR studies were
employed to probe the diffusivity of the solvent and Li+
ion in the Li2S6 solution (Figure 3b,c). Interestingly, in the
solution with MtBE, DME and Li+ show diffusion coefﬁcients
of 1.91 × 10−9 and 5.62 × 10−10 m2 s−1, respectively, which
are higher compared to 1.26 × 10−9 and 4.47 × 10−10 m2
s−1, respectively, in the DOL/DME solution. These higher
diffusivities with the addition of MtBE likely arise from
the overall reduced solvent-Li+ binding, as well as the low
viscosity of MtBE, which promotes faster mass transport in
the actual cells for MSEs.
Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations pro-
vide further insights into the solvating environment with
polysulﬁde in the electrolyte. In the DOL/DME system, Li+
is predominantly solvated by DME and DOL molecules,
making the S62−anion to dissociate from Li+ (Figures 3d and
S14). In contrast, RDF and representative AIMD snapshots
for the MtBE-containing formulation show an increased
propensity for Li+–S62−pairing (Figures 3e,f and S14).
Moreover, MtBE tends to pull DME away from Li+, reducing
the number of DME molecules in the inner solvation shell of
the Li+ and S62−complex.
These results indicate that MSE-MtBE most effectively
shifts polysulﬁde solvation toward more ion pairing, which
could directly affect polysulﬁde migration and conversion;
accordingly, MSE-MtBE was selected for further evaluation.
Due to the reduction of LiPS solubility and regulation of
solvation structures, the LiPS shuttling effect is expected to
be effectively mitigated in MSE-MtBE, which could make
a substantial impact on the cyclability of Li–S cells. To
validate this, a shuttling current test was performed on Li
|| S cells by holding the voltage at 2.25 V during discharge,
the point when high-order LiPS largely formed. As shown
in Figure S15, the current in the DOL/DME cell quickly
changes direction and levels off at ∼0.047 mA, indicating
obvious LiPS shuttling. In contrast, the MSE-MtBE cell
shows much slower current change, and the shuttle current
stabilizes at ∼0.013 mA, demonstrating its ability to suppress
shuttling.
Several more tests were conducted to better assess the
impact of electrolyte on sulfur conversion kinetics. Pairing
the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) with
EIS is an effective approach to study cell polarization.[40] By
applying this GITT-EIS method to Li || S cells with practical
sulfur loading, the overall cell polarization (ηtotal) can be
resolved into three contributions: ohmic (ηohm), activation
(ηact), and concentration (ηconc) polarizations. ηohm captures
ionic resistance in the bulk electrolyte as well as electronic and
contact resistances within the electrodes and cell hardware;
it can be extracted from the high-frequency intercept on the
Nyquist plot. ηact corresponds to the energy barrier of the
interfacial charge-transfer process and, together with ηohm,
produces the instantaneous voltage jump observed whenever
the current is stepped during GITT measurement. Finally,
ηconc arises from mass-transport limitations when Li+ ions
and LiPSs are locally depleted at the electrode surface due
to electrochemical reactions; ηconc can be obtained from the
gradual voltage drift toward equilibrium potential during the
relaxation period in GITT.
For both the cell with MSE-MtBE and DOL/DME
electrolyte, the resulting ηtotal shows a gradual increase at the
beginning of the discharge and peaks at ∼25% depth of dis-
charge (DOD) when Li2S/Li2S2 starts to nucleate (Figures 3g
and S16a). It then slowly decreases before displaying a
rapid increase toward the end of the discharge. ηtotal was
further decoupled into the three components discussed above
(Figure S16b–d). The resulting ηohm for the MSE-MtBE cell
is slightly greater than the DOL/DME cell due to their ionic
conductivity difference, with both cells exhibiting a peak
value at the nucleation point (2.81 mV for MSE-MtBE and
2.10 mV for DOL/DME) when LiPS concentration reaches
its maximum. However, ηohm takes up only a small fraction
of ηtotal, showcasing that it is not the major cause of the cell
overpotential. Meanwhile, the main part of ηtotal is comprised
of ηact and ηconc. Both ηact and ηconc display a peak at ∼
25% DOD, while ηconc has a rapid increase at the end of the
discharge due to more sluggish ion diffusion in the electrode.
The MSE-MtBE cell shows a greater ηact at the Li2S/Li2S2
nucleation point (19.5 mV) compared to the baseline cell
(10.4 mV), likely because of reduced LiPS concentration in
MSE-MtBE, leading to fewer nuclei and nucleation sites. The
difference is much smaller in ηconc, as it is only slightly larger
in MSE-MtBE before 75% DOD. In fact, the ηconc in MSE-
MtBE has a peak value of 44.7 mV at ∼25% DOD, which
is lower than that in DOL/DME (47.4 mV). This is likely
due to easier LiPS movement and lower desolvation energy
in MSE-MtBE, aligning well with the previous discussion.
Additionally, during termination of discharge, most of the
electrochemically active surface is covered; the MSE-MtBE
cell shows signiﬁcantly lower ηconc compared to the baseline
cell, thanks to its better wettability and potentially more
uniform deposition in the electrode (Figure S17).

Chronoamperometry experiments were further performed
to quantitatively study the Li2S nucleation and growth. As
shown in Figure S18, the MSE-MtBE cell achieves a higher
response current peak and a larger Li2S deposition capacity
of 232 mA h g−1, compared to 161 mA h g−1 for the
DOL/DME cell. This leads to higher discharge capacity and
active material utilization in actual Li || S cells. Interestingly,
both chronoamperometry cells show similar peak response
time, indicating that MSE-MtBE does not adversely affect the
nucleation rate when the polysulﬁde concentration is the same
as in the baseline. However, in a practical cell with high sulfur
loading and low E/S ratio, the nucleation process can also be
affected due to polysulﬁde solubility differences, as discussed
in the previous section.
Furthermore, to probe the liquid–liquid sulfur conversion
kinetics, temperature-dependent EIS spectra were collected
on Li2S6 symmetric cells (Figure S19). Following a similar
procedure applied to Li metal symmetric cells, DRT analysis
was carried out to interpret the EIS data (Figures 3h and
S20). DRT peaks appearing at intermediate time constants
(10−4 to 10−1 s) are attributed to the Rct of the cell, which
is an important indicator of the charge-transfer kinetics.[41,42]
These Rct values measured between 0 and 50 °C ﬁt well in an
Arrhenius plot, from which activation energies were extracted
(Figure 3i). Interestingly, the MSE-MtBE cell exhibits higher
Rct throughout the temperature range, but a lower activation
energy compared to DOL/DME. This suggests that the
energy barrier for the charge-transfer process, which is likely
associated with desolvation at the electrochemically active
surface, is lower in MSE-MtBE, whereas the overall reaction
rate is slower, plausibly due to the lower conductivity of
MSE-MtBE (Figure S21). Additionally, in the CV curves
of Li2S6 symmetric cells, MSE-MtBE shows slightly lower,
but more reversible response current, suggesting fewer side
reactions and better stability of the conversion process
(Figure S22).

Operando XRD was performed to further elucidate the
impact of electrolyte on cathode kinetics and microstructures
(Figure 4a,b). By directly observing the evolution of cathode
species, it provides insights into the sulfur conversion process.
Before cycling, both the DOL/DME and MSE-MtBE cells
exhibit strong α-S8 signals as expected. These α-S8 peaks
quickly decrease and disappear upon discharge when high and
low order LiPSs start to form. These LiPSs are subsequently
converted to low crystallinity Li2S at the end of the discharge,
as evidenced by a broad peak detected at around 27°.
Both cells show a complete disappearance of sulfur peaks
during discharge, indicating a complete conversion of sulfur,
attributed to the optimized spray-dried sulfur composite
particles utilized in the cathode.[43] Interestingly, notable
differences between the baseline and MSE-MtBE cells were
observed after the subsequent charge. For the DOL/DME
cell, a group of β-S8 peaks were detected after charge, which
aligns well with previous work in the literature utilizing
ether-based electrolytes.[44] However, in the MSE-MtBE cell,
β-S8 was ﬁrst formed toward the end of the charge before
peaks corresponding to α-S8 appeared. Note that α-S8 tends
to form when the reaction kinetics move toward solid-state
conversion.[32,45] This two-step sulfur deposition process could
be characteristic in the MSE due to lower LiPS solubility
and regulated solvation properties. As a kinetically more
favorable process, β-S8 is ﬁrst nucleated from solvated LiPSs,
while thermodynamically more stable α-S8 starts to grow
when quasi-solid-state reaction kicks in. Additional ex situ
XRD results show that this β – α conversion can happen even
on immersing the S cathode retrieved from the DOL/DME
cell in MSE-MtBE (Figure S23). This indicates that MSE-
MtBE affects the balance between high order polysulﬁdes
and elemental sulfur (in both solution and solid state),
thereby changing the dissolution and redeposition process
that stabilizes β-S8. This mechanism may be pivotal toward
maintaining adequate charging kinetics, while minimizing
LiPS dissolution in MSEs.
Electrochemical Performance of the Electrolytes
To evaluate the inﬂuence of these altered solvation structures
and conversion kinetics on the electrochemical performance,
Li || S cells with different electrolytes were assembled
and tested under different conditions. MSE-MtBE and
DOL/DME baseline electrolytes were ﬁrst compared in cells
with a relatively low S loading of 2.2 mg cm−2. Cycled at
a rate of C/3 (1C = 1500 mA h g−1), the MSE-MtBE cell
exhibits a high initial capacity of 945 mA h g−1 and enables
a high retention of 75.2% at 711 mA h g−1 after 200 cycles
(Figure S24). In contrast, DOL/DME only achieves 537 mA h
g−1 after 200 cycles, corresponding to a retention of 59.3%.
The increased capacity and capacity retention may beneﬁt
from reduced polysulﬁde dissolution and regulated solvation.
The electrolytes were then demonstrated with a higher S
loading of 3.5–3.7 mg cm−2 and a lower E/S ratio of 10 µL
mg s−1. The MSE-MtBE cell maintains a capacity of 710 mA
h g−1 after 180 cycles at a C/5 rate, corresponding to 79.4% of
its initial capacity, contrasting only 458 mA h g−1 and 56.6%
for the DOL/DME cell (Figure 5a, Table S2). When cycled at
a C/10 rate, the MSE-MtBE cell also presents a high initial
capacity of 1,019 mA h g−1 and a capacity retention of 83.2%
at 848 mA h g−1 after 140 cycles (Figure S25).
The rate performances of the electrolytes were further
assessed. MSE-MtBE achieves higher and more stable capac-
ities at all applied rates (from C/10 to C/2), especially at C/2,
where it retains a capacity of 781 mA h g−1 (Figure 5b). In
contrast, the DOL/DME cell only shows a capacity of 700 mA
h g−1 and drops below 550 mA h g−1 after 10 cycles at C/2.
Notably, the gap remains signiﬁcant after the rate changes
back to C/5, as the MSE-MtBE cell recovers a capacity of ∼
850 mA h g−1, while the baseline cell has less than 700 mA
h g−1 retained. Additionally, MSE-MtBE shows comparable
overpotentials in the voltage proﬁles except a larger dip at
the nucleation point for the rate of C/2 (Figures 5c and S26).
These results reveal that the addition of MtBE plays an
important role in improving the capacity and cyclability of Li–
S cells, which may be attributed to its inﬂuence on both the
electrodes.

capacity of 770 mA h g−1 at a C/5 rate and retains 627 mA h
g−1 after 90 cycles (Figure 5d). Note that the cell experiences a

Surface and Interphase Characterization
Interphasial properties play a crucial role in affecting
the performance of batteries. To elucidate the inﬂuence
of MSE-MtBE on electrode-electrolyte interphases, several
characterization tests were carried out. After cycling the Li ||
S cells for 30 cycles, Li metal morphology was examined with
scanning electron microscopy (SEM). MSE-MtBE enables
dense and uniform Li deposition, packed with large grains,
suggesting less parasitic side reactions and dendrite formation
(Figure S29). In contrast, Li metal cycled in DOL/DME has
more cracks and non-uniform surface morphology.
The interphasial stability of Li-metal anode is heavily
inﬂuenced by the characteristics of the SEI layer. In Li–S
systems, SEI forms through the decomposition of electrolyte
salt and solvent, as well as the crossover of sulfur-containing
species from the cathode. The modulation of solvation
structures and LiPS behaviors by MSE-MtBE may affect
the SEI composition. To better study the SEI in different
electrolytes, Li anodes after 30 cycles were evaluated with x-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Figure 6a,
the S 2p spectra reveal various sulfur species on the surface
of the SEI, including SO42−/─SO2 (S 2p3/2 ≈168.7 eV),
SO32−/─SO (S 2p3/2 ≈167.1 eV), SB0 (S 2p3/2 ≈163.6 eV),
ST−1 (S 2p3/2 ≈161.6 eV), and S−2 (S 2p3/2 ≈159.9 eV).[46]
The oxidized sulfur species (─SOx) mainly originate from the
LiNO3-involved decomposition of LiTFSI salt and oxidation
of soluble LiPSs, while the reduced sulfur species (Sx,
x < 0) mainly come from the migration of LiPSs from
the cathode. The MSE-MtBE sample showcases a healthy
combination of these species with more oxidized sulfur
species detected, which passivate the interphase and promote
more reversible Li deposition. In addition, these oxidized
species show slightly lower binding energies compared to
the control sample, potentially improving the conductivity of
this passivating layer. On the other hand, the DOL/DME
electrolyte leads to the formation of more reduced sulfur
species, indicating a greater degree of LiPS shuttling to the
anode. This trend remains unchanged after Ar-cluster ion

Conclusion
In summary, we demonstrated an MSE strategy utilizing a
family of low-density, low-viscosity, nonﬂuorinated ether co-
solvents that present weaker solvation power than DME.
By probing their intermolecular interactions with salt and
solvent, we showed that these co-solvents, especially MtBE,
concurrently tune Li+ and polysulﬁde solvation. The opti-
mized formulation (MSE-MtBE) effectively (i) weakens Li+–
solvent pairing, while increasing Li+–anion association, and
(ii) reduces polysulﬁde dissolution, while improving electrode
wettability and mass transport. As a result, this electrolyte
facilitates Li+ desolvation and promotes the formation of a
LiF-rich, passivating SEI. Moreover, these changes produce
an altered sulfur deposition pathway that preserves charging
