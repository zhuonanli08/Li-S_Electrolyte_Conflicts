A B S T R A C T  
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) are among the most promising next-generation high energy density energy- 
storage systems. However, practical application has been hindered by fundamental problems, especially shut­
tling by the higher-order polysulfides (PSs) and slow redox kinetics. Herein, a novel electrolyte-based strategy is 
proposed by adding an ultrasmall amount of the low-cost and commercially available cationic antistatic agent 
octadecyl dimethyl hydroxyethyl quaternary ammonium nitrate (SN) into a routine ether electrolyte. Due to the 
strong cation–anion interaction and bridge-bonding with SN, rapid flocculation of the soluble polysulfide in­
termediates into solid-state polysulfide-SN sediments is found, which significantly inhibited the adverse shuttling 
effect. Moreover, a catalytic effect was also demonstrated for conversion of the polysulfide-SN intermediates, 
which enhanced the redox kinetics of Li–S batteries. Encouragingly, for cells with only 0.1 % added SN, an initial 
specific capacity of 783.6 mAh/g and a retained specific capacity of 565.7 mAh/g were found at 2C after 200

1. Introduction 
The large-scale use of hybrid electric vehicles and smart grids has 
spurred considerable demand for high-performance energy-storage de­
vices [1–3]. Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have attracted enormous 
attention due to their large theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh/g, 
environmental friendliness, abundant resources, and the low cost of 
sulfur [4,5]. However, the practical application of LSBs is seriously 
inhibited by some notorious issues, such as the insulating nature of 
sulfur, the shuttle effect of the soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs, Li2Sn, 
n = 4, 6, 8), and the large volume expansion of sulfur during cycling 
[6,7]. Specifically, much attention has been given to solving the shuttle 
effect induced by dissolution of high-order polysulfides into the elec­
trolyte and subsequent migration between the two counter electrodes, 
which causes rapid loss of the active sulfur species and suppression of 
the Coulombic efficiency [8,9]. 
In view of the shuttle effect, the most frequently reported strategies 
involve trapping the soluble polysulfides within porous hosts, i.e., car­
bon materials [10] and some polar additives [11], stemming mainly 
from van der Waals forces, electrostatic adsorption, and Lewis acid/base 
interactions [12]. These attempts, however, usually result in reduced 
columbic efficiencies of 70–80 % after 200–300 cycles, and other issues, 
including a low tap density and reductions in the total energy density 
due to the occupied spaces resulting from the trapping additives can also 
be encountered [13]. Electrolyte modification is another method used to 
solve polysulfide-related issues, and it exhibits feasible implementation, 
multifunctional availability, high efficiency, and negligible impact on 
the cell configuration. For example, the proper amount of LiNO3 in the 
electrolyte prompts the growth of a robust solid electrolyte interphase 
(SEI) film and inhibits [14,15] the growth of lithium dendrites [16,17], 
which are common ways to increase the stabilities of lithium-sulfur 
batteries. Reactive additives, such as the quinone derivatives quinhy­
drone [18] and benzoquinone [19], exhibit strong bonding with the 
soluble polysulfides and solidify them to stabilize the battery [20,21]. 
Additionally, organosulfur-based redox comediators, such as di-t-butyl 
diulfide (DtbDS), have recently been used to accelerate the redox ki­
netics [22], and cells exhibiting extraordinary overall performance with 
high rates, high sulfur loadings and lean electrolytes have been reported. 
However, most of these additives are quite costly and highly toxic and 
are not environmentally friendly for large-scale applications. Novel and 
effective electrolyte additives enabling rational regulation of the detri­
mental polysulfide shuttling are still urgently needed for high- 
performance Li–S batteries. 
Sewage treatment is one of the most important and highly developed 
chemical engineering industries in modern society, and the critical aim 
is to remove large amounts of the soluble and insoluble pollutants 
contained in the wastewater. Among the various techniques used in 
sewage treatment, flocculants are frequently applied because they 
rapidly condense harmful ions into insoluble sediments via liquid–solid 
phase separation induced by charge neutralization, absorption bridging, 
or aggregation [23]. For example, as shown in Fig. S1, sewage con­
taining Fe2+/Fe3+ cations were purged effectively by adding the anionic 
flocculant polyacrylamide (PAM), and brownish-yellow floccules were 
rapidly formed. Fast liquid–solid transitions based on solution processes 
constitute attractive methods for stabilizing soluble ions, and it is worth 
considering whether the proper flocculants could be introduced into the 
electrolyte to flocculate the soluble polysulfides and trap them effi­
ciently in Li–S batteries. 
In this paper, an inexpensive and commercially available cationic 
quaternary ammonium salt, octadecyl dimethyl hydroxyethyl quater­
nary ammonium nitrate (SN) (Fig. S2) [24], is used to regulate 
polysulfide evolution in a routine ether-based electrolyte (1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL) 
/ 
1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME) 
with 
lithium

fluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI)). In earlier studies, SN was 
applied as antistatic agent as used in dye containing wastewater where 
SN was embedded in magnesium silicate flakes to form layered inor­
ganic–organic hybrid materials with positively charged for rapid 
adsorption of negatively charged anions [25]. And in our investigations, 
the SN induced rapid flocculation and solidification of the soluble 
anionic polysulfides, and accelerated conversion of the intermediate 
sulfide species during the electrochemical reaction was also observed. 
For cells with only 0.1 wt% SN, a high initial discharge specific capacity 
of 833.8 mA h g−1 and a cycling capacity of 640.3 mAh/g at 0.5C was 
realized after 440 cycles. Moreover, at 2C, the initial specific capacity 
was 783.6 mAh/g, and a specific capacity of 565.7 mAh/g was retained 
after 200 cycles for cells with SN additives; this corresponded to an ul­
tralow capacity decay rate of only 0.014 % per cycle. This work provides 
a useful approach for the efficient regulation of polysulfides in Li–S 
batteries via flocculation and solidification processes. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Chemicals and materials 
Antistatic agent SN (48 wt%) was obtained from Usolf Chemical 
Technology Co. Ltd, Shandong. Acetylene black (AB, ˃98 wt%) and 
three-dimensional reduced graphene oxide (3D rGO, 96 wt%) were 
purchased from Chengdu Organic Chemicals Co. Ltd. Sulfur (S, ≥99.5 
wt%) and ethyl alcohol are provided by Shanghai Chemical Corporation. 
N-Methy-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.99 wt%), polyvinylidene fluoride 
(PVDF, ≥99.5 wt%), lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.98 wt%), 1,3-dioxolane 
(DOL),

(DME) 
and

bis(tri­

All chemicals and solvents were analytical grade. 
2.2. Preparation of the electrolyte containing SN 
Solid SN was purified by freeze-drying a commercial SN solution to 
extract the water contaminant. The general lithium-sulfur electrolyte is 
typically prepared by dissolving 1.0 mol/L LiTFSI in a mixture of DOL/ 
DME (1:1, v:v) with 1 wt% LiNO3 added. The electrolyte with SN was 
prepared by adding 30 mg of SN (approximately 0.1 wt% in the total 
electrolyte) into 30 ml of the general lithium-sulfur electrolyte, and the 
mixture was sonicated to form a homogenous transparent solution. 
2.3. Fabrication of the 3D rGO/S cathode 
A 3D rGO/S composite was prepared by the traditional melt- 
diffusion method. Briefly, 3D rGO powder was mixed with sulfur with 
a weight ratio of 3:7 and then heated at 155 ◦C for 12 h in a sealed vessel 
under an Ar atmosphere. 
The cathode was produced by mixing the active materials (3D rGO/S 
composite), AB, and PVDF with a weight ratio of 8:1:1 in N-methyl-2- 
pyrrolidone (NMP), which was added to a carbon-coated aluminum foil, 
dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h in a vacuum oven and then punched into disks 
with diameters of Ф=12 mm. The sulfur content was approximately 71 
wt% for all samples, as determined by thermogravimetric analyses 
(TGA, Fig. S3). The sulfur loading density of the cathode was approxi­
mately 1.0–1.3 mg⋅cm−2.

2.4. Li2S nucleation tests 
A Li2S8 electrolyte solution with a concentration of 0.4 mol L–1 was 
synthesized by mixing sulfur and Li2S in a molar ratio of 7:1 in tetra­
glyme followed by vigorous stirring for 24 h. Commercial carbon papers 
(CP) were punched into circular disks with diameters of 12 mm and used 
as the current collectors, and lithium foil was used as the anode. The SN 
and PVDF, which were dissolved in NMP at a ratio of 9:1, were coated 
onto the carbon paper. Twenty microliters of the Li2S8 electrolyte so­
lution were first distributed on the cathode, and then 20 µL of the 
electrolyte solution without Li2S8 was dripped onto the lithium anode 
counter electrode. The assembled cells were discharged galvanostati­
cally at 0.112 mA to a potential of 2.06 V and then discharged poten­
tiostatically at 2.05 V for Li2S nucleation and growth. The potentiostatic 
discharge was terminated when the current fell below 10−5 A [26]. 
2.5. Electrochemical measurements 
CR2032 coin cells were assembled with the 3D rGO/S composites as 
the cathodes and Li metal foil as the anodes. The amount of electrolyte in 
the cells was 40 μL, and the electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio was 
approximately 26 μL mg−1. The galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) 
and rate studies were implemented with a battery test system (LAND- 
CT2001A, Landian, Wuhan, China) over a voltage range of 1.7 to 2.8 V 
with different cycling rates. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) with various scan 
rates and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, 10 mHz to 100 
kHz) measurements were performed with an AUTOLAB electrochemical 
instrument (PGSTAT302N, Metrohm, Switzerland).

3. Results and discussion 
Inspired by the typical flocculation process used in sewage treat­
ment, a commonly applied antistatic agent, SN, which has the opposite 
charge of the the anionic polysulfides (Sn
2−) for the quaternary ammo­
nium cation (R4N+), was used as the flocculant to interact with the 
soluble polysulfides and suppress the shuttle effect (Fig. 1a). The distinct 
interactions between the SN and the soluble polysulfides are shown in 
Fig. 1c and 1d. Solid-state colorless sediments were formed rapidly when 
small amounts of SN were added to the deep yellow Li2Sn (n = 4, 6, 8) 
electrolyte solutions. Furthermore, after evaporation of these sediments 
under Ar, solid-state blend-like semitransparent products were observed 
that indicated reactions between the SN and the polysulfides. The SN 
(Figs. S2 and 1b) contains cationic quaternary ammonium groups 
(R4N+) and reactive –OH functional groups along with the long alkyl 
chain, and these can interact with the polysulfides via electrostatic 
adsorption [28] and/or form strong and covalent –O–Li bonds 
[18,29]. 
To investigate the interactions of the SN and the LiPSs, the soluble 
Li2S6 was added into the electrolyte solution (LiTFSI and LiNO3 dis­
solved in DOL/DME) containing the SN (0.1 wt%). Fig. 2a shows the 
ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra obtained for supernatant samples 
with and without SN in the Li2S6 electrolyte solution. A broad absorption 
band with a low peak intensity was observed between 260 nm and 300 
nm for the SN-Li2S6 sample, indicating the strong affinity of the S6
2−
species for SN [30]. 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (7Li NMR) was 
employed to confirm chemical interactions between the polysulfides and 
SN, as shown in Fig. 2b. The 7Li NMR peak was shifted from 0.97 ppm for 
the pure Li2S6 to 0.81 ppm upon the addition of SN. The upfield shift of 
the 7Li resonance was attributed to strong interactions between the 
–OH groups in SN and the Li+ cation in Li2S6, which resulted in 
increased shielding of the Li ion [31]. Fourier transform infrared spec­
troscopy (FTIR) was also used to investigate these interactions, and the 
assignments for the main functional groups in SN are indicated in the 
FTIR spectrum (Fig. S5). As shown in Fig. S3, the FTIR peak centered at 
1157 cm−1 was ascribed to a C–N vibration [25], and the peaks at 1640 
cm−1 and 3350 cm−1 were attributed to in-plane bending vibrations and 
stretching vibrations of the –OH groups, respectively. For SN-Li2S6 (in 
Fig. 2c), the –OH in-plane bending peak (centered at 1600–1700 cm−1) 
was found, and the –OH stretching vibration gave a peak at 3450 cm−1 
that was significantly blueshifted and exhibited a lower intensity [32]; 
this indicated that the –O–H group of SN was replaced with a –O–Li 
group [18,29]. 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to determine the 
compositions of the samples. The XPS survey spectrum for SN showed 
the presence of carbon, nitrogen and oxygen (Fig. 2d), while signals for

To gain a better understanding of the interactions between SN and 
the polysulfides, computational simulations were carried out with den­
sity functional theory (DFT). Accordingly, the absolute value of the 
binding energy (Ebinding) was used to determine the energy of one re­
action. As shown in Fig. S13, bonding between the Li atom from Li2Sn 
and the O atom from the hydroxyl groups and/or NO3

demonstrated. Considering the strong interactions between anions and 
cations in solution, the most stable bonding configuration involved 
direct binding of the lithium ion to the oxygen atoms from the hydroxyl 
groups, and the binding energies calculated for the different –O–Li 
bonds were −0.71 eV, −0.62 eV and −0.70 eV for SN-Li2S8, SN-Li2S6 and

and exothermically (ΔE < 0) [30]. The free energies for all reactions on 
the path from Li2S8 to Li2S2 were also calculated and are shown in 
Fig. 3g. The discharging process was more thermodynamically favorable 
for the electrolytes containing SN relative to those without SN, and the 
transformation of Li2S8 to Li2S2 was significantly accelerated. The 
traditional solid–liquid-solid conversion process for lithium sulfur bat­

cess due to the introduction of SN into the electrolyte. These results 
unambiguously confirmed that SN facilitated the rapid conversion of SN- 
Li2Sn to Li2S2, which suppressed dissolution and the shuttle effect to 
improve the overall performance.

The influence of the SN additive on the lithium anode was also 
investigated by disassembling the cells after 200 cycles at 0.2C, and the 
morphologies of the lithium metal were characterized with optical 
photographs and scanning electron microscope (SEM) images. As shown 
in Fig. 5h and i, the SEM image of the Li anode immersed in the con­
ventional electrolyte indicated an uneven surface with apparent 
dendrite-like structures. In contrast, the surface of the Li anode 
immersed in the modified electrolyte was relatively compact and 
smooth. Fig. 5f and 5 g also showed that SN effectively immobilized the 
polysulfides and inhibited corrosion of the lithium anodes. This indi­
cated that polysulfide “shuttling” and the irreversible poisonous re­
actions on the Li anode were mostly inhibited to suppress the formation 
of “dead” Li [56]. The chemical environments of sulfur on the lithium 
anode surfaces without and with SN after 200 cycles at 0.2C were 
analyzed by XPS, and the results were shown as Fig. S14 in supporting 
information. As shown in Fig. S14a, the XPS peaks located at 166–172 
eV can be attributed to sulfates and sulfites, which usually come from 
the side reaction of lithium salts (LiTFSI and LiNO3) [57,58]. It is worth 
noting that trace Li2S signals can also be found from the lithium anode 
with SN, which may originate from the small amount of unstabilized 
polysulfides during continuous electrochemical reactions. And this 
should be responsible for the loss of capacitance for our SN modified 
electrolyte. In contrast, strong signals of Li2S2 (centered at 163.8 eV and 
165 eV) and Li2S (centered at 161.8 eV and 162.6 eV) were detected for 
the lithium anode without SN [59,60]. Additionally, galvanostatic 
cycling of the Li||Li symmetric cells was investigated to evaluate the 
stabilities toward lithium stripping/plating (Fig. 5d and 5e). At a current 
density of 1 mA cm−2, the symmetric cell containing SN maintained an 
overpotential of approximately 32 mV and worked stably over 300 h; 
this indicated excellent cycling stability for the cell, which was consis­
tent with the smooth lithium anode surfaces without dendrite formation. 
For the symmetric cells without SN, a higher overpotential of nearly 
1000 mV was required after 250 h, and this is usually attributed to the 
formation of excess “dead” lithium [61], as seen in the embedded 
enlarged image. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, we proposed a novel flocculation and in-situ solidifi­
cation strategy to effectively inhibit the soluble polysulfides in Li–S 
batteries by using a low-cost cationic antistatic agent SN as the elec­
trolyte additive. Strong charge and bonding interactions with soluble 
polysulfides were confirmed for SN additives, which significantly sup­
pressed the shuttling effects and improved the cycling stability of the 
batteries. By adding only 0.1 wt% SN in routine electrolyte, an ultra-low 
capacity decay rate of only 0.014 % per cycle at 2C can be obtained. The 
cycle degradation performance of lithium-sulfur batteries using SN ad­
ditives is much better than those reported for lithium-sulfur batteries 
(Table S1), such as PIPE (0.083 % after 300 cycles at 0.5C) [20], PDSe 
(0.14 % after 300 cycles at 0.5C) [60], CS2 (0.066 % after 180 cycles at 
0.5C) [62], and so forth. This work demonstrates the importance of 
electrolyte ion regulations in Li–S batteries, which may also provide a 
protocol strategy to boost the electrochemical performances in other 
emerging high-performance battery systems.
