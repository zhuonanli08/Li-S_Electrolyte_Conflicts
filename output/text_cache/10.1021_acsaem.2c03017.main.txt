Suppressing the Shuttle Effects with FeCo/SPAN Cathodes and
High-Concentration Electrolytes for High-Performance
Lithium−Sulfur Batteries

ABSTRACT: The shuttle effects and the sluggish redox kinetics are two of the
main challenges in lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, which significantly reduce the
capacity of the batteries and restrict their commercialization. Herein, FeCo/
sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) is synthesized as a cathode material via the
electrospinning technique and a further heat treatment. Synchrotron X-ray
absorption spectroscopy confirmed the existence of Fe−S/Fe and Co−S bonds in
FeCo/SPAN, which benefit the adsorptive and catalytic activities toward lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs). We further investigated the effect of electrolyte concentration
in inhibiting the shuttle of LiPSs in Li−S batteries. Small-angle X-ray scattering
(SAXS) reveals that more contact ion pairs are formed and fewer free solvent
molecules exist with the increase of the electrolyte concentration, which can inhibit
the dissolution and shuttle of LiPSs. Finally, the batteries assembled with high-
concentration electrolytes (3 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME) exhibit a higher specific
capacity retention compared to those assembled with low-concentration electrolytes. This work enriches the route to prepare Li−S
batteries with the rational design of cathode materials and electrolytes.
KEYWORDS: shuttle effects, electrolyte, SAXS, Li−S batteries, XAS, catalysts
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium-ion batteries cannot meet the capacity requirements of
advanced devices because of the limitation of the theoretical
specific capacity of cathode materials. However, sulfur has a
superhigh theoretical specific capacity (1675 mA h g−1). This
is why Li−S batteries have gain popularity in recent years.
Besides, they are environmentally friendly, and element sulfur
is abundant.1−5 Every coin has two sides; there is no exception
with Li−S batteries. Despite the above-mentioned advantages,
Li−S batteries still suffer from issues such as slow redox
kinetics and shuttle effects, which significantly reduce the
performance of batteries and severely restrict their commerci-
alization.6−9
Many studies about the design of cathode materials have
solved the challenges mentioned above. For example, sulfur
was confined in porous carbon and carbon nanotubes/hosts.
However, these carbon materials are polar, but the lithium
polysulfides (LiPSs) can still escape from them and dissolve
into the electrolyte.10,11 Another plausible method is to trap
sulfur via chemical bonds. In this respect, sulfurized
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) is one of the most promising
materials for its chemical bonding effect.12 To further improve
the redox kinetics and suppress the shuttle effects, transition
metal catalysts such as iron and cobalt are recently introduced
into the cathode of lithium−sulfur batteries, showing an
outstanding catalytic performance toward LiPSs.13−17 In terms
of the electrolytes, it has been reported that high-concentration
electrolytes can also inhibit the dissolution of LiPSs.18−21
Herein, we combined the high catalytic ability of transition
metals and the chemical bonding effects of SPAN toward sulfur
to synthesize FeCo/SPAN as cathode materials through
electrostatic spinning and heating treatment. The electro-
spinning process created nanofibers in which Fe and Co are
evenly distributed to efficiently accelerate the redox reaction of
LiPs. The further heat treatment allowed sulfur to chemically
bond with FeCo/PAN for better confining the LiPSs.
Therefore, the batteries assembled with FeCo/SPAN-based
cathodes exhibited the best cycling performance compared to
those assembled with Fe/SPAN, Co/SPAN, and SPAN-based
cathodes. We further optimized the electrolytes by adjusting
the concentration of lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)-
imide (LiTFSI). The SAXS and Raman results showed that
more contact ion pairs (CIPs) are formed by increasing the salt
concentration. At the same time, tiny free solvent molecules

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Synthesis of FeCo/PAN and PAN Nanofiber Precursors.
FeCo/PAN and PAN nanofiber precursors were prepared by the
electrospinning technique. First, polyacrylonitrile (PAN) was
dissolved in dimethylformamide (DMF) and stirred for 3 h. Second,
Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, Co(NO3)2·6H2O, and urea were added into the
above solution and stirred overnight. Then, the mixture was inhaled
into a syringe for electrospinning. The operating voltage of the
electrospinning was 18.5 kV, and the feeding rate was 1.6 mL h−1
(volume/time). After electrospinning, the FeCo/PAN nanofiber
precursor was obtained. The PAN nanofiber precursor was prepared
in the same way, except without the addition of Fe3+ and Co2+.
2.2. Synthesis of FeCo/SPAN and SPAN Composites. Sulfur
(500 mg) was thoroughly dissolved in 5 mL of CS2. The FeCo/PAN
nanofiber precursor (250 mg) was soaked into the mixed solution.
After volatilizing the solvent entirely, the mixture was placed in a
quartz tubular furnace, and the temperature was maintained at 155 °C
for 12 h. Then, the temperature was increased to 300 °C, and the
materials were heated for 2 h. The resultant sample was FeCo/SPAN.
Furthermore, SPAN was prepared using the same method.
2.3. Preparation of Electrolytes. The electrolytes were prepared
by dissolving lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI,
>99%, Sigma-Aldrich) (mole) in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1, v/v) (liter). We define the
concentration as “M”.
2.4. Electrochemical Measurements. The FeCo/SPAN (or
SPAN) composite, Super P, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)
(7:2:1) were added into the N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solution
to prepare the cathode electrode. After magnetic stirring for 5 h, the
obtained slurry was evenly coated on aluminum foil and dried for later
use of assembling the CR2032 coin cells. A Celgard 2400 film served
as the separator. The cycling performance was conducted on the
LAND battery test instrument in the range of 1.0−3.0 V.
Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded on a CHI
760e electrochemical analyzer (CH Instruments, Inc., Austin, State of
Texas, USA).

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Scheme 1 illustrates the preparation process of FeCo/SPAN.
FeCo/PAN nanofiber precursors were obtained through the
electrospinning technique. PAN, Fe3+, Co2+, and urea were
added to DMF and stirred overnight. Then, the mixture was
inhaled into a syringe for electrospinning to form FeCo/PAN
nanofiber precursors. In this way, Fe and Co ions can evenly
distribute on the nanofiber to efficiently promote the catalytic
conversion of LiPSs. After electrospinning, the FeCo/PAN
nanofiber precursors were soaked into CS2, which dissolved
sulfur overnight. Finally, the mixture was placed in a quartz
tubular furnace for heat treatment to obtain FeCo/SPAN.

lengths mainly because of the decomposition of urea during
the heat treatment, which are quickly ground to powder for the
cathode preparation. Figure 1b shows that the fiber surface is
rough, and some nanoparticles exist in FeCo/SPAN, while the

mapping (Figure 1d−g). The results indicate that Fe, Co, and

The XRD measurement was performed to determine the
structure of FeCo/SPAN and SPAN. In Figure 2a, the XRD
pattern of SPAN shows that the characteristic peaks of
elemental sulfur are not detectable, and a broad diffraction
peak appears at around 25°. FeCo/SPAN exhibits an XRD
pattern similar to that of SPAN, suggesting that the obtained
metal sulfides are in an amorphous state.22−24 In addition,
TGA was conducted to confirm the stability of FeCo/SPAN.
The weight loss of FeCo/SPAN and SPAN occurs at higher
temperatures (over 500 °C) than that of S8, which lost all the
weight at a temperature below 300 °C (Figure 2b), indicating
that S atoms are attached to FeCo/PAN and PAN through
strong chemical bonding.22,23,25
Next, we performed synchrotron XAS to determine the
oxidation states and local coordination environment of Fe and
Co in FeCo/SPAN.26,27 Figure 3a presents the X-ray
absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra of FeCo/
SPAN at the Fe K-edge with Fe foil, FeS, and Fe2O3 as
references. The Fe K-edge XANES spectra of FeCo/SPAN and
Fe/SPAN show the rising edge position located between Fe
foil and Fe2O3, which confirms that the electronic state of the
Fe species is between 0 and +3. Moreover, the absorption
threshold position of FeCo/SPAN is located between CoO
and Co foil (Figure 3c), suggesting that Co is positively
charged with the valence close to +2. Significantly, the
absorption edge of FeCo/SPAN shifts toward higher energy
relative to that of Co/SPAN, indicating the more positive state
of Co in FeCo/SPAN. As shown in the Fourier transformation
of extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS)
spectra of FeCo/SPAN and Fe/SPAN (Figure 3b), the
existence of Fe−S and Fe−O can be proved.29 The bumps
at a higher R space stem from metal−metal contribution.
Moreover, the notable peak located at 1.77 Å should be
regarded as the scattering of the Co−S band,28 which is larger
than the Co−O bond of CoO at 1.59 Å.
Moreover, we conducted the charge−discharge cycle test to
determine the battery performance of FeCo/SPAN. We also
tested the batteries assembled with Fe/PAN, Co/SPAN, and
SPAN for comparison (Figure S1). As shown in Figure 4, the
specific capacities of Fe/PAN and Co/SPAN electrodes have
no significant difference at 0.2 C, which is higher than that of
SPAN electrodes after 75 cycles. However, FeCo/SPAN
electrodes showed a much higher specific capacity than all
the electrodes mentioned above for every cycle. As shown in
Figures S2 and S3, we compared the FT-EXAFS spectra of
FeCo/SPAN, Fe/SPAN, Co/SPAN, and after 10 cycles in a 1
M LiTFSI electrolyte. No obvious changes of FT-EXAFS
spectra at the Co K-edge of FeCo/SPAN and Co/SPAN can
be observed (Figures S2b and S3d). The Co−S bond remains
after 10 cycles. The Fe−O and Fe−S bonds in FeCo/SPAN
changed to Fe−S and Fe−Fe bonds after 10 cycles (Figure
S2a). In contrast, the Fe−O and Fe−S bonds in Fe/SPAN
changed to Fe−O bonds after 10 cycles (Figure S3b), along
with the increase of the oxidation state (Figure S3a).
Therefore, we believe that the existence of Fe−S and Fe−Fe
bonds in FeCo/SPAN during the charge−discharge process
benefits the electrochemical performance.
To better understand the influence of electrolyte concen-
tration on battery performance, we investigate the solvent
structure of a series of concentrations of LiTFSI in a mixture of
DOL and DME (the volume ratio is 1:1) by SAXS. In Figure
5a, the peak is more evident at the high-q part, with the
concentration of LiTFSI increasing from 1 to 3 M, indicating
that nanostructures are formed in the electrolytes. Unlike the
conventional dilute electrolyte, the interaction of cations and
anions/solvents is enhanced in high-concentration electrolytes
(Figure 5c).30,31 To be specific, more CIPs are formed, while
the free solvent molecules decrease in high-concentration
electrolytes, which are helpful to inhibit the dissolution of
LiPSs.32
We use Raman spectroscopy to further explore the local
structure of different concentration electrolytes. Figure S5
shows the Raman spectra of electrolytes with different LiTFSI
concentrations ranging from 700 to 780 cm−1. The peak
located at around 740 cm−1 is the bending vibration of the C−
N−C group in LiTFSI, which can be used to capture the
cation−anion coordination structure variations in different salt
concentrations due to the sensitivity of this band to ion
association.33−35 The C−O vibration bonds in the solvent
appear at 938−960 cm−1 (Figure S6).34 The intensity of the
C−N−C band becomes stronger, while the C−O band
becomes weaker with the increase of LiTFSI concentration.
Notably, the C−N−C band in LiTFSI also shifts to the right
with the increase of LiTFSI concentration, which is due to the
formation of CIPs. We can further confirm this conclusion
from the fitting curves of the Raman spectra for 1, 2, and 3 M
LiTFSI in DOL/DME. As shown in Figures S7−S9, the
spectra can be fitted into three peaks, which are the free anion
(peak A), loose ion pairs (peak B), and CIPs (peak C). The
area of peak A is decreased with the increase of the electrolyte
concentration. In contrast, the area of peak C increased
(Figure 5b), indicating that more CIPs are formed in the high-
concentration electrolytes (Figure 5c).36 This conclusion is
also the same with the information we got from SAXS data.
We also conducted lithium polysulfide (Li2S8) dissolution
experiments to understand the high-concentration electrolyte
inhibition effect. As shown in Figure 6a, the color of the
solution with 3 M LiTFSI did not change much from 1 h to 7
days, while the solution with 1 M LiTFSI changed from yellow
to brown, indicating that high-concentration electrolytes have a
strong ability to inhibit polysulfide dissolution.19 Therefore,
the batteries assembled with a high-concentration electrolyte
(3 M LiTFSI) exhibit good cycle performance. As shown in

4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we prepared FeCo/SPAN as a cathode material
through the electrospinning technique and a further heat
treatment. With the aid of high catalytic conversion capabilities
of Fe and Co toward LiPSs, the FeCo/SPAN cathode in Li−S
batteries exhibited the best cycling performance compared to
Fe/SPAN, Co/SPAN, and SPAN-based cathodes. Based on
these cathode materials, we further investigate the mechanism
of high-concentration electrolytes in inhibiting the dissolution
and shuttle of LiPSs in Li−S batteries. The SAXS and Raman
data show that more CIPs are formed, and fewer free solvent
molecules exist in high-concentration electrolytes, thus
suppressing the dissolution and shuttle of LiPSs. Finally, the
batteries assembled with high-concentration electrolytes (3 M
LiTFSI) exhibit a specific capacity of 71.6% higher than that of
batteries assembled with 1 M LiTFSI and 30.2% higher than
that of batteries assembled with 2 M LiTFSI.
