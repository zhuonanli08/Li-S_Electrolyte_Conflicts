a b s t r a c t
Rechargeable lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered one of the most promising energy storage
techniques owing to the high theoretical energy density. However, challenges still remain such as the
shuttle effect of lithium polysulﬁdes (LPSs) and the instability of lithium metal anode. Herein, we propose
to use nitrogen-rich azoles, i.e., triazole (Ta) and tetrazole (Tta), as trifunctional electrolyte additives for
Li-S batteries. The azoles afford strong lithiophilicity for the chemisorption of LPSs. The density functional
theory and experimental analysis verify the presence of Li bonds between the azoles and LPSs. The azoles
can also interact with lithium salt in the electrolyte, leading to increase ionic conductivity and lithium-
ion transference number. Moreover, the azoles render particle-like lithium deposition on the lithium
metal anode, leading to superlong cycling of a Li symmetric cell. The Li-S batteries with Ta and Tta exhibit
the initial discharge capacity of 1425.5 and 1322.2 mAh g1, respectively, at 0.2C rate, and promising
cycling stability. They also enable enhanced cycling performance of a Li-organosulﬁde battery.

1. Introduction
With the continuous development of society, the demand for
fossil fuels is increasing at a massive scale, which has caused envi-
ronmental pollution and shortage of non-renewable resources
[1,2]. The pursuit of the green and low-carbon economy drives
the grand exploration of energy storage technologies [3]. There-
fore, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) with high speciﬁc energies quickly
occupied the market of energy-intense applications [4]. The speci-
ﬁc capacity of transition metal oxide cathodes used in LIBs is gen-
erally less than 250 mAh g1, which, along with the use of high-
cost metals (e.g., cobalt) and the shortage of lithium metal
resources, have accelerated the demand for alternative low-cost
and high-energy rechargeable battery systems [5]. Lithium-sulfur
(Li-S) batteries are one of such technologies because of the high
theoretical speciﬁc capacity of the sulfur cathode (1675 mAh g1)
and lithium anode (3861 mAh g1) leading to the high speciﬁc
energy of the device (2600 Wh kg1) [6,7]. The abundant
resource, environmental benignity, and low cost of sulfur have
prompted signiﬁcant attention [8]. However, the commercializa-
tion of Li-S batteries faces the challenges of the dissolution/shuttle
effect of lithium polysulﬁdes (LPSs) and instability of lithium metal
anode resulting in fast capacity decay and poor cycle life [9].
Extensive explorations of the failure mechanisms have provided
a marked understanding of the Li-S battery system, as well as a
profound enhancement of sulfur cathode, lithium anode, and elec-
trolyte over the past decades. On the cathode side, the pioneering
research of sulfur conﬁned in a conductive mesoporous carbon
framework was proposed by Ji et al [10]. Other sulfur hosts like
hollow carbon nanoﬁber and metal–organic frameworks derived
carbon have also been developed [11–14]. Metal oxides and metal
sulﬁdes as substrates can also absorb LPSs [15,16] and catalyze
their conversions [17,18], leading to improved battery perfor-
mance. The lithium bonding formed between nitrogen-doped gra-
phene and LPSs also allows the anchoring effect, holding LPSs in
the cathode and maintaining stable cycling performance [19]. On
the anode side, lithiated graphite [20] and Li/B [21] and Li/Si [22]
alloy all have a certain effect on protecting the lithium anode.
Recent studies include in situ solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI)
modiﬁcation
engineering
using
lithium
oxalyldiﬂuoroborate
(LiODFB) [23], ‘‘solvent-in-salt” electrolyte [24], and ﬂuorinated
ether-based electrolyte [25] which can guide uniform lithium
deposition and inhibit lithium dendrite formation [26].

Notably, electrolyte plays a critical role in Li-S batteries [27]. In
particular, it can affect lithium deposition/stripping of lithium
metal anode. For example, CsPF6 and RbPF6 [28], KNO3 [29], or
AlCl3 [30] as electrolyte additives can provide long-lasting protec-
tion for the highly active Li anode thus controlling the Li deposition
via a self-healing electrostatic shield mechanism. Thiourea [31]
and crown [32] ether as electrolyte additives both can offer a
stable, uniform solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer on the Li elec-
trode, thereby helping to inhibit the dendrite growth of Li and elec-
trolyte decomposition. In addition, the electrolyte can affect the
behavior and performance of sulfur cathode. For example, organic
additives with redox activity including organic quinones (e.g.,
anthraquinone [33] and 1,5-bis(2-(2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethoxy)-e
thoxy)anthra-9,10-quinone [34]) and organosulfur (e.g., dimethyl
disulﬁde [35,36], phenyl dithiophenol [37], phenyl trithiophenol
[38], and butyl disulﬁde [39]) can act as redox mediators to partic-
ipate in the redox processes of sulfur, reducing the formation of
LPSs. Polysulﬁdes and sulfur-containing polymers have also been
used as electrolyte additives [40,41]. Glutamate additives contain
a large number of negatively charged hydroxyl groups which can
inhibit the shuttle of negatively charged polysulﬁde ions through
strong repulsion [42]. Furthermore, the N-containing pyrrole as
an electrolyte additive to trap polysulﬁdes was reported [43]. How-
ever, the additives of molecules alone with multifunctionalities are
still lacking. Because nitrogen atoms can coordinate different
cations and form lithium bonds with LPSs [44], organic molecules
containing multiple nitrogen atoms could be suitable electrolyte
additives for Li-S batteries.
In this contribution, the azoles with high nitrogen contents, i.e.,
triazole (Ta) and tetrazole (Tta), are used as electrolyte additives in
Li-S batteries (Fig. 1a). These azole molecules were often used in
the synthesis of metal organic framework (MOFs) materials. Nitro-
gen atoms can form coordination bonds with different transition
metal ions (Zn2+, Cd2+, Cu+ etc.) [45]. Similarly, the formation of
lithium bonds between nitrogen-containing heterocycles and
lithium polysulﬁdes has also been conﬁrmed, which helps anchor
LPSs [46]. Ta and Tta possess multiple nitrogen atoms, which can
chemically adsorb LPSs (Fig. 1b), alleviating the shuttle effect and
improving the utilization of active materials. The azole-LPS com-
plexes are conﬁrmed by computational simulations and experi-
mental
analysis.
The
electrolytes
with
azoles
also
possess
improved electrochemical properties, i.e., increased ionic conduc-
tivity and lithium transference number, leading to uniform lithium
deposition and formation of SEI thus inhibiting the lithium den-
drite growth (Fig. 1c). All these effects signiﬁcantly improve bat-
tery performance.
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of azoles-based electrolytes
A series of electrolytes were prepared by adding triazole (Ta) or
tetrazole (Tta) of different concentrations to the commercial elec-
trolyte. The concentrations of Ta and Tta are 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and
1 mol L1, respectively. These electrolytes were stored in an
argon-ﬁlled glove box with oxygen and water contents lower than
0.1 ppm.
2.2. Preparation of cathode
Firstly, 160 mg of carbon nanotubes (CNTs, Nanostructure and
Amorphous Materials, Inc.) were dispersed in a miscible solution
of de-ionized water (500 mL) and isopropyl alcohol (20 mL) by
ultra-sonication for 15 min, followed by vacuum ﬁltration to ren-
der a free-standing CNT paper. The CNT paper was dried in an
air-oven for 24 h at 100 C before being peeled off and punched
out into circular disks with 1.2 cm diameter (1.13 cm2, 3–4 mg).
The thickness of the CNT paper is about 170–175 lm. Secondly, a
certain amount of sulfur was dissolved in CS2 solution. 20 lL solu-
tion containing 1.1 mg sulfur was taken and dropped on the CNT
paper. Then the electrode was dried at 60 C for 12 h in an oven.
Finally, The PMTT cathode was also prepared by mixing dipen-
tamethylene thiuram tetrasulﬁde (PMTT), CNTs, and polyvinyli-
dene ﬂuoride (PVDF) binder at a mass ratio of 70:30:10 in N-
methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and coating the slurry onto 25 lm
aluminum foil. Then the electrode laminates were cut into discs
with a diameter of 1.1 cm after drying at 80C in a vacuum oven
overnight. The average loading of active cathode material was
1.2 mg.
2.3. Electrochemical measurements
Coin cells CR2032 were fabricated in the Ar ﬁlled glove box
(H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm). 20 lL electrolyte with or without
Ta and Tta was dropped on the sulfur cathode, then a Celgard
2400 separator was placed on the top of sulfur cathode followed
by adding 20 lL electrolyte with or without Ta and Tta on top of
the separator. Finally, a lithium metal anode was placed on the
separator. Then the Li-S cell was encapsulated and taken out of
the glove box for testing. For the fabrication of high loading Li-S
cells, CS2 solutions containing more sulfur were directly dripped
onto CNTs. Li-PMTT cells were assembled by substituting PMTT
for S cathode.
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV)
were performed on a BioLogic VMP-3. The potential was swept
from open circuit voltage to 1.8 V and then swept back to 3.0 V
at a scanning rate of 0.05 mV s1. The ion conductivity of elec-
trolyte was tested by a conductivity meter equipped with 2-
electrode Pt probe. All cells were galvanostatically cycled at 1.8–
3.0 V on a LAND battery cycler at different C rates (1C = 1675 mA g1
for S cathode and 1C = 418 mA g1 for PMTT).
2.4. Material characterization
7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded
on 600 MHz Bruker spectrometers (262 MHz for
7Li NMR). A
1.0 mol L1 sample of LiCl in H2O was used as the reference
(0 ppm). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement
was performed with a Thermo Scientiﬁc K-Alpha+ spectrometer
with monochromatic Al Ka radiation Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) of the electrodes was performed on a Zeiss Sigma 500 SEM
apparatus. The Raman spectroscopy was carried out by the Thermo
DXR machine with a 532 nm laser at room temperature. The solu-
tions were enclosed in a glass tube in the glove box to avoid expo-
sure to air. The experiment for lithium polysulﬁde dissolution was
carried out as follows: a mole ratio of Li2S and S (1:5) were uni-
formly mixed in an Argon-ﬁlled glovebox, then added into the
DOL and DME solvent, and stir for two days at 40 C to ensure
the formation of Li2S6 with a concentration of 5 mmol L1. Then
0.5 mL of Li2S6 solution was added to DME solution with the
0.2 M Ta/Tta.
2.5. Theoretical calculations
In this work, we carried out density functional theory (DFT)
methods as implemented in Gaussian 16 program [47]. Frequency
calculations were used to conﬁrm the presence of local minima (no
imaginary frequencies). Geometry optimization and energy calcu-
lations were optimized with the M062X functional [48]. The cc-
PVTZ basis set was used for C, H, N, S, and Li. The same level of the-
ory was carried out to characterize the density functional disper-

3. Results and discussion
In order to understand the interactions between azoles and
LPSs, density functional theory (DFT) was employed to analyze
their complex structures. The electrostatic potential (ESP) maps
of Ta, Tta, and LPSs (Li2Sx, x = 4, 6, 8) have been mapped onto a
density isosurface (0.0004) and are shown in Fig. 2 [54]. The pre-
diction for the formation sites of new chemical bonds can be
vividly illustrated by ESP. The red region corresponds to the area
of the lowest electrostatic potential, which is described by the
nucleophilic (proton-loving) behavior and greatest electronegativ-
ity. The blue region represents the area of the highest electrostatic
potential, which shows the lowest electronegativity and elec-
trophilic (electron-loving) feature. As shown in Fig. 2(a), in the
ESP map of LPSs, the lithium atom is entirely blue, indicating a
strong positive potential. While for Ta and Tta, the red region
encompasses the nitrogen atom with strong negative potential.
These results demonstrate that the nitrogen atoms are rich in elec-
trons and the lithium atoms are absent of electrons. The nitrogen
atoms of Ta and Tta have strong interactions with the Li+ of LPSs.
The relative binding energy (DE) is computed to measure the
binding strength between Ta/Tta and Li2Sx. It is deﬁned as the
energy difference between the summation of pure Ta/Tta (EA)
and pure Li2Sx (EB) and the complex Ta/Tta-Li2Sx (EAB), thus it can
be expressed as DE = EAB – EA – EB + EBSSE. Because of the real inter-
action energy between A and B molecules and the basis function
from A and B molecules overlap in the complex system, the basis
set of the complex system is increased and EAB is reduced. If this
partial contribution is also mixed with DE, the interaction energy
is overestimated, so it should be removed, which is called Basis
Set Superposition Error (BSSE). With this deﬁnition, the more neg-
ative the value, the more stable the structures are. Fig. 2(b) and
Fig. S1 show the calculated solvent-phase binding energies and
the optimized isomers of Ta-Li2Sx. The isomers of Ta-Li2Sx are
formed via different nitrogen atoms of Ta interacting with the Li+
of LPSs. The strong interactions of N-Li bonds make the sulfur
chains of Ta-Li2Sx look like crowns. The calculation results show
that LPSs and Ta can be combined into stable conﬁgurations. By
comparing the DE of three different compounds (Ta-Li2Sx), it is
found that Li2S8 is easier to combine with Ta into a stable conﬁgu-
ration Ta-Li2S8 by comparing the DE of Ta-Li2Sx (–24.85 kcal mol1
(x = 8) < –23.45 kcal mol1 (x = 6) < –22.86 kcal mol1 (x = 4)). As
presented in Fig. 2(c) and Fig. S2, by comparing the DE of Tta-Li2S8
and those of Tta-Li2S6 and Tta-Li2S4, the stability order of the three
compounds is Tta-Li2S8 > Tta-Li2S6 > Tta-Li2S4, which is similar to
Ta-Li2Sx. The gas-phase binding energies for the possible isomers
of Ta/Tta-Li2Sx (Li2Sx) have similar results as those of solvent-
phase, and the corresponding computational results are given in
Figs. S3 and S4. The difference in DE is determined by the different
chemical environments of each nitrogen atom in the molecule. As
shown in Fig. S5, the charge value of each nitrogen atom in triazole
and tetrazole molecules is different, resulting in different binding
energy (DE) in azole-Li2Sx.
Further analysis was carried out by 7Li nuclear magnetic reso-
nance (NMR) measurements to investigate the formed structure
of Ta, Tta, and Li2Sx (Fig. 2d). Clearly, the Li chemical shift of Li2S6
appears at 2.9 ppm. When the Ta and Tta were added into the solu-
tion containing Li2S6, the changes of the chemical shift of 7Li were
recorded. The apparent downﬁeld displacements to 3.1 and
3.3 ppm are observed, respectively. The upshift to the low ﬁeld is
derived from the deshielding effect on Li atom, which indicates
the formation of strong Li binding between Li2S6 and Ta/Tta. There-
fore, these also conﬁrm the stable conﬁgurations of Li of Li2S6 link-
ing to N of Ta and Tta. In addition, as shown in the inset
photographs, the Li2S6 displays light yellow in DME solvent. When
Li2S6 was added to the solution containing Ta and Tta, they became
clear instantly after 5 seconds. In addition, the UV absorbances of
Li2S6 solutions exposed to Ta and Tta become much lower than that
of the blank solution, which indicates Ta and Tta have a strong abil-
ity to adsorb LiPSs (Fig. S6). This characteristic indicates that both
Ta and Tta have strong chemisorption to Li2S6. Meanwhile, the
strong interaction also means that the side reactions between
lithium polysulﬁde and Li anode are inhibited.

the initial discharge capacities of 1425.5 and 1322.2 mAh g1,
respectively, at 0.2C rate. The corresponding voltage proﬁles are
shown in Fig. S16. The speciﬁc capacities after 200 cycles are
704.2 and 780.4 mAh g1, respectively, corresponding to the capac-
ity retention of 49.4 and 59%. Obviously, the discharge capacity of
the Li-S cell using BE has a severe attenuation. This indicates that
the Li-S cells with Ta and Tta electrolytes have more stable cycling
performance. In addition, the Li-S cells using azoles-based elec-
trolytes with LiNO3 were also evaluated. The cells with Ta or Tta
also present stable cycling performance of over 200 cycles and
capacity retention of 70 and 70.2%, respectively (Fig. S17). At the
same time, their Coulombic efﬁciencies are also improved. Nyquist
plots of the three cells before cycling are shown in Fig. S18. The
charge transfer resistances of the cells with Ta and Tta-based elec-
trolytes are much smaller than that of the cell with BE, indicating
the good electrochemical behavior with azole additives. The
cycling performance of Tta and Ta-based cells at different rates
and 1C were also evaluated (Fig. S19). The capacity of cells with
Ta and Tta decreases slowly, while the capacity of the blank cell
decreases rapidly at 2C. At 1C, the cell with Tta is more stable than
that with Ta and maintains 66.5% of the initial capacity after 240
cycles. To further evaluate the efﬁcacy of azoles-based electrolyte,
the coin cells with high sulfur loadings were fabricated (Fig. S20).
The cells with sulfur loadings of 3.4 and 3.7 mg cm2 can retain
the capacity retention of 96.5 and 87.5% after 100 cycles, respec-
tively. The SEM characterizations of the sulfur electrode after
charge and discharge reveal clearly the retention of active sub-
stances on the CNTs having massive or colloidal morphology
(Fig. S21).
Organosulﬁdes with redox ability are promising cathode mate-
rials [32]. Herein, dipentamethylene thiuram tetrasulﬁde (PMTT)
with a theoretical speciﬁc capacity of 418 mAh g1 was also eval-
uated with the azoles-based electrolytes (Fig. 5d). The Li/PMTT
cells with Ta and Tta exhibit the initial capacities of 394.4 and
383.8 mAh g1, respectively, and they retain 56.9 and 67.7% of
the initial capacities after 300 cycles. Moreover, the Coulombic efﬁ-
ciency of Ta is above 96%, while that of Tta is above 98%. The
cycling performance of the Li/PMTT cell with BE is severely atten-
uated after 180 cycles. Accordingly, the Ta and Tta have similar
chemisorption effects on the discharged products of PMTT, i.e.,
lithium pentamethylene dithiocarbamate and LPSs, reducing their
dissolution and shuttle thus leading to the enhanced cycling per-
formance. Compared with the Tta-based electrolyte, the Ta-based
electrolyte has higher conductivity and lithium ion transference
number. However, it can be seen that the lithium symmetric cell
with Tta shows more stable cycling performance than that with
Ta, indicating that Tta can help form a more stable SEI. The Li-S
and Li/PMTT cells with Tta also show better cycling performance
than those with Ta. Clearly, the SEM analysis in Fig. S21 shows that
Tta helps retain active materials in the interwoven CNTs network
after charge. Therefore, Tta as the electrolyte additive seems more
suitable than Ta for use in sulfur-based batteries.

4. Conclusions
In conclusion, the nitrogen-rich azoles have been proved as
effective electrolyte additives for Li-S batteries. They can interact
with LPSs, assisting the redox process, and improving the material
utilization. The complexes Ta-Li2Sx and Tta-Li2Sx are conﬁrmed by
DFT and 7Li NMR. Furthermore, the Ta or Tta based electrolytes
present excellent electrochemical properties including the high
conductivities (11.76 and 11.97 mS cm1) and Li+ transference
numbers (0.67 and 0.52). The Li/Li symmetric cell shows a long
cycle life of 2000 h at 0.1 mA cm2. The Ta and Tta based elec-
trolytes also enable uniform lithium deposition on the lithium
metal anode forming particle-like nucleation. The Li-S cells with
Ta or Tta-based electrolytes exhibit improved cycling performance.
In addition, the azoles as electrolyte additives can also effectively
enhance the battery performance of organosulﬁde cathode, i.e.,
PMTT. This study shows that the nitrogen-rich azoles are promis-
ing electrolyte additives for Li-S batteries. Insights of azoles as
additives expand the cognition of liquid electrolytes and endow
inspiration
to
design
high
efﬁcient
electrolyte
systems
for
advanced Li-S batteries.
