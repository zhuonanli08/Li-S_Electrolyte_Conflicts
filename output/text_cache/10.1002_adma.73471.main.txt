ABSTRACT
Rational regulation of lithium polysulfide reaction kinetics, coupled with strategies for stabilizing the Li anode, constitutes a
cornerstone for lithium–sulfur (Li–S) chemistry. Herein, we propose phthalocyanine (Pc) as a homogeneously dispersed promoter
in the electrolyte for Li–S batteries, based on its N8-cavity planar rigid structure featuring an electron-rich macrocyclic core.
Under the optimized concentration of Pc in electrolyte, we reveal that the Li─N coordination bonds between Li2S8 and Pc in the
catholyte of Li–S batteries, which significantly contribute to the catalytic conversion of sulfur species. Meanwhile, Pc molecules
in the anolyte preferentially adsorb onto the Li anode surface, forming a dense molecular layer by virtue of Li─N bonding, which
effectively enhances interfacial desolvation kinetics and thereby promotes uniform Li deposition. Enabled by the promoted Li–S
chemistry through Li─N bonds, the battery with Pc enabler achieves a low decay rate of 0.0479% per cycle after 600 cycles at 1C.
More remarkably, 1.07 Ah pouch cells deliver a high energy density of 325.5 Wh kg−1, serving as a compelling design strategy for
leveraging sustainable Li─N bond chemistry to achieve high-rate and long-life Li–S battery technology.

Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have emerged as a prominent
focus of scientific research due to their remarkable theoretical
energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and specific capacity (1675 mA h
g−1), as well as the low cost of sulfur [1–3]. Nevertheless, their
commercialization remains severely challenged by fundamental
obstacles in both sulfur cathodes and Li anodes [4, 5]. For sulfur
electrochemistry, the shuttle effect of soluble Li polysulfides
(LiPSs) occurs in ether electrolyte during discharge/charge, caus-
ing substantial capacity decay [6, 7]. Concurrently, the sluggish
sulfur conversion kinetics restrict the discharge depth, leading
to low sulfur utilization and fast capacity fading [8, 9]. On
the Li metal anode side, uncontrollable Li plating/stripping
behavior gives rise to unregulated dendrite growth, degrading
cycle stability and increasing risks of short circuits and safety
hazards [10–14].
Previous studies have predominantly focused on cathode and
separator engineering (such as interface modulation [15, 16],
defect construction [17], spatial confinement [18], and size
design [19, 20]) to optimize sulfur species conversion and Li
deposition behavior. For instance, the size effect of spherical
V2C MXene significantly enhanced the LiPS conversion kinetics
and Li+ transport behavior [20]. Electrolyte additive engineer-
ing represents a promising regulatory strategy due to its cost

(ii) By regulating the solvation environment in the electrolyte

novel approach for interfacial regulation in Li–S chemistry, with
their synergistic optimization of sulfur cathode kinetics and Li

Elucidating the evolution mechanism of chemical species in Li–
S batteries is pivotal to enhancing electrochemical performance
[26–28]. Regarding sulfur species, the interactions between polar
materials and LiPSs are rooted in atomic-scale chemical bonds
or intermolecular dipole-dipole forces. Deciphering the mecha-
nism through combined experimental and theoretical analysis is
therefore crucial for understanding the catalytic conversion of
LiPSs [29–32]. Li bond chemistry was identified in Li–S batteries,
where the terminal Li of Li2S8 engages with pyridinic nitrogen
(Li...pN) through hydrogen bond-like dipole interactions [33].
Additionally, a protective layer was designed for the Li anode
via a self-limited assembly of an aluminum-lithium alloy (ALA)
and octadecylamine (ODA). This hybrid structure regulates the
penetrating Li+ flux, thereby promoting uniform Li deposition
[34]. Along this line, notwithstanding the remarkable LiPS
catalytic activity and Li anode protection abilities of transition
phthalocyanine (Pc) metal-based complexes (Co2+/Ni2+/Cu2+),
the current understanding remains speculative with respect
to nitrogen functionalities and metal reactivity [35–39]. Atom-
level interactions between organic complexes and LiPSs remain
poorly understood, and both solvation structure reconstruction
and Li interface regulation are uncharted. Li─N bonds are
widely observed in regulating Li–S chemistry, from pyridine
and N-doped carbons to porphyrins and Pc derivatives [40–45].
However, the metal-free Pc used here avoids transition-metal
interference and offers stronger Li–bond donating capability via
its π-conjugated N8 cavity, making it an ideal model for studying
the intrinsic Li─N mechanism.
In this contribution, our study introduces metal-free Pc as a
bifunctional electrolyte additive, systematically dissecting its dual
role for enhancing LiPS redox kinetics and Li anode stability.
We reveal a coordination chemistry wherein Li─N bonds form
between electron-rich pyridinic nitrogen donors in the Pc and
electron-deficient Li terminals of Li2S8, which can be understood
with the help of consistent with the Lewis acid-base principle. On
the Li metal side, the planar Pc molecules preferentially adsorb
onto the metal surfaces via Li─N coordination bonds, forming
dense molecular layers. The layers can reduce direct contact
between solvent molecules and the Li anode, which results in the
suppression of electrolyte side reactions. Moreover, Pc-derived
interphase significantly accelerates desolvation and promotes Li
deposition. Notably, it is observed that Pc with a content of
0.025 wt.% nearly approaches its saturation limit, exhibits fully
active sites, and provides a remarkable optimization effect. These
conclusions are supported by multi-scale analysis techniques,
including neutron imaging, synchrotron radiation small-angle
X-ray scattering (SR SAXS), and first-principles calculations.
Therefore, the Li–S batteries with the Pc deliver a 0.0479%
capacity decay per cycle after 600 cycles at 1C and harvest an areal
capacity of 8.5 mA h cm−2 at 0.1C. Additionally, the 1.07 Ah-level
multi-layer pouch cell achieves an energy density of 325.5 Wh
kg−1, highlighting the significant potential of this strategy.

Results and Discussion
We prepared electrolytes containing Pc additive with various
contents (0.01, 0.025, and 0.05 wt.%), with their correspond-
ing visual images shown in Figure S1. As seen in Figure 1a,
the Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy was employed to
characterize the different Pc concentrations in the electrolyte.
Strong characteristic absorption peaks are observed in the ranges
of 300–400 nm and 600–750 nm, corresponding to the B-band
and Q-band of the Pc complex, respectively [46]. Moreover, the
absorbance intensity increases progressively with higher Pc con-
tent, confirming the successful incorporation and homogeneous
dispersion of 0.025 wt.% Pc in the electrolyte. And excessively high
Pc content (0.05 wt.%) causes the formation of suspended solids
or precipitations. The inset displays optical images of the Pc-
introduced electrolytes under laser illumination, revealing that
the Tyndall effect is absent when the content is increased to
0.05 wt.%. Furthermore, the stability verification of the 0.025
wt.% Pc-containing electrolyte (Figure 1b) was conducted after
being stored for 7 d. Neither the position nor the intensity of the
absorption peaks shows significant changes, and the color of the
electrolyte in the inset image remains unchanged, demonstrating
the excellent dispersion stability of Pc in the electrolyte. As
presented in Figure S2, the electrolyte containing 0.025 wt.%
Pc exhibits an ionic conductivity of 12.19 mS cm−1, which is
nearly identical to that of the blank electrolyte (12.07 mS cm−1).
Rheological measurements reveal that while the Pc-containing
electrolyte displays a marginally higher viscosity at low shear
rate, both electrolytes stabilize at a comparable viscosity of
approximately 2.1 mPa s−1 under a shear rate of 353 s−1. The
negligible variation in these bulk transport properties confirms
that the introduction of a low concentration of Pc does not
perturb the intrinsic Li+ solvation environment or migration
behavior. Thermal stability analysis (Figure S3) shows that the
blank and Pc-containing electrolytes exhibit nearly identical
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) weight loss and differential
scanning calorimetry (DSC) profiles, confirming that the addition
of 0.025 wt.% Pc does not compromise the thermal stability of
the electrolyte. To confirm the effective immobilization capability
of Pc toward LiPSs, we further assembled cells based on blank
electrolytes without LiNO3 additive or with Pc to measure the
shuttle current. In this study, “blank” and “Pc” denote electrolytes
without and with 0.025 wt.% Pc additive, respectively. As shown in
Figure 1c, the cell with Pc additive exhibits a lower shuttle current
response (0.001 µA cm−2) than the control.

The anchoring effect of Pc toward LiPSs was further confirmed by
visual adsorption experiments using a Li2S6 solution. As shown
in Figure S5, after mixing Pc with a yellow Li2S6 solution, the
upper layer darkened, and a precipitate formed. XPS analysis of
the precipitate confirmed the presence of Li─N bonds, consistent
with the coordination observed for Li2S8. This demonstrates that
Pc anchors Li2S6 through Li─N coordination while preserving the
polysulfide chain structure.
As presented in Figure 1g, density functional theory (DFT) calcu-
lations were performed to confirm the adsorption configuration
of Pc and Li2S8. Structural analysis reveals that the imine nitrogen
atom of Pc coordinates with the terminal Li atom of Li2S8, with
a binding energy of −1.11 eV and a Li─N bond length of 2.13 Å,
significantly higher than those of typical hydrogen bonds (−0.04
to −0.22 eV) and Li bonds (−0.64 eV),27 indicating a strong
specific chemical interaction between them. The Mulliken charge
analysis shows that a charge of 0.7 e is transferred from the
Li atom to the imine nitrogen atom, further confirming sig-
nificant charge transfer in the Li─N bond, reflecting notable
intermolecular dipole-dipole interactions, in accordance with
the electronic structure characteristics of coordination bonds.
Furthermore, natural bond orbital analysis of Pc@Li2S8 reveals
a substantial positive charge on the terminal Li atom of Li2S8.
Additionally, second-order perturbation interactions between
the lone pair electrons of the imine nitrogen (donor) and the
vacant orbital of the Li atom (acceptor) are clearly observed,
directly confirming the formation of a Li─N coordination bond.
Electrostatic potential mapping and differential charge density
analysis (Figure S6) further confirm electron transfer from N to
Li. Further electronic structure analysis of Pc@Li2S8 indicates
a strong dipole moment of 4.40 Debye, directed from the Li2S8
cluster toward the Pc molecule. This indicates a significant elec-
tron cloud rearrangement in the complex. This rearrangement
renders Pc an electron-enriched negative center and Li2S8 an
electron-deficient positive center, which confirms the electron
transfer from Pc to LiPS. Theoretical calculations provide strong
support for the formation of Li─N coordination bonds, which
align consistently with the spectral evidence presented above. The
significant electronegativity difference between nitrogen and Li
indicates that the Li─N interaction can be rationalized by Lewis
acid-base coordination theory. Specifically, the imine nitrogen
atoms in Pc act as an electron donor (Lewis base), while the
terminal Li atoms in Li2S8 act as electron acceptors (Lewis
acid), leading to the formation of a strong Li─N coordination
bond. The working mechanism of Pc in the catholyte is illus-
trated in Figure 1h. The homogeneously dispersed catalyst of
Pc molecules ensures effective coordination bonding with LiPS
clusters, thereby facilitating suppression of the shuttle effect.
Moreover, the coordinated structure tends to aggregate more
readily on the conductive carbon surface, significantly promoting
Li2S nucleation and growth reaction kinetics. Beyond the carbon
layer, deep within the cathode, the catalytic effect remains
effective because of the favorable mobility of the Pc-containing
catholyte.
Driven by the formation of Li─N coordination bonds, a series
of electrochemical characterizations was conducted to validate
the enhanced electrocatalytic activity of Pc. In the cyclic voltam-
metry (CV) profiles (Figure S7), the symmetrical cell at 10 mV
s−1 exhibits a higher current response in the presence of Pc,
indicating the accelerated redox kinetics of sulfur species. As
seen from Figure S8, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves
exhibit two characteristic reduction peaks: Peak 1 (S8→Li2Sn,
4 ≤n ≤8) and Peak 2 (Li2S2→Li2S). The Li–S battery with
Pc additive exhibits lower slopes of 45.4 and 41.9 mV dec−1
for Peak 1 and Peak 2, respectively, compared to the battery
with o confirm the effective immobilization electrolyte (50.7 and
171.3 mV dec−1). These results demonstrate that the Pc-containing
electrolyte enhances the conversion kinetics of sulfur species.
CV curves of Li–S batteries at a varying rate from 0.1 to 0.5 mV
s−1 in the 1.7–2.8 V voltage window were further collected. As
shown in Figure 2a,b, the resulting contour plots clearly indicate
a stronger current response for the battery with the Pc additive
in both the reduction and oxidation regions of sulfur species
conversion. According to the Randles–Sevcik equation, the peak
current exhibits a linear correlation with the square root of the
sweep rate. Figure S9 further shows the superior Li+ diffusion
property of the battery with the Pc additive, as derived from the
steeper peak current-scan rate slopes.
The detailed kinetics of Li2S nucleation and decomposition were
also probed. As shown in Figure 2c,d, the battery with carbon
paper – Pc (CP-Pc) exhibits a Li2S deposition capacity of 393.1 mA
h gs
−1, exceeding that of the battery with CP-blank (382.6 mA
h gs
−1). Moreover, the response time corresponding to the peak
current in the Li2S nucleation curve is shorter for the battery with
CP-Pc (1076 s) than for the one with CP-blank (1698 s), indicating
a kinetically accelerated Li2S nucleation procedure. The scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) images of CPs after nucleation tests
show that the presence of Pc promotes the deposition of more
and denser Li2S particles on individual carbon fibers. To further
decipher the Li2S growth behavior under different electrolytes,
we analyzed the Li2S deposition curves using four classical
electrochemical models: the Bewick–Fleischman–Thirsk (BFT)
model, which describes (i) 2D progressive nucleation (2DP) and
(ii) 2D instantaneous (2DI) nucleation; and the Scharifker–Hills
(SH) model, which describes (iii) 3D progressive (3DP) nucleation
and (iv) 3D instantaneous nucleation (3DI) [51, 52]. As presented
in Figure 2e,f, both systems primarily follow the 2DI mechanism.
Notably, CP-Pc shows a more remarkable tendency toward the
3DP nucleation pathway in contrast to CP-blank. Additionally,
the Li2S decomposition tests in Figure S10 exhibit a higher
decomposition capacity of 588.1 mA h g−1 and an earlier peak
current response (3662 s) for CP-Pc. The corresponding SEM
images (inset of Figure S10) demonstrate smoother carbon fiber
surfaces in CP-Pc, indicating more complete Li2S dissociation.
Additionally, DFT calculations show that the decomposition
barrier of Li2S is 2.10 eV in the presence of Pc, as shown in Figure
S11. This theoretical result validates the experimental findings
and confirms the effective catalytic role of Pc in promoting the
oxidation of Li2S. Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate
that nitrogen-rich Pc and its Li─N bonds play a critical role in reg-
ulating Li2S nucleation/decomposition, exhibiting bidirectional
modulation of sulfur redox kinetics.

Apart from the accelerated kinetics effect of Li2S on the cathode
side, the engineered Pc macrocycle with electron-rich, nitrogen-
abundant structure also facilitates interaction with the lithium
metal surface, indicative of an anode protection effect. As
schematically illustrated in Figure 3a, after introducing the Pc
into the conventional Li–S electrolyte, we systematically examine
its underlying working mechanism in the battery from two
perspectives: the electrolyte and the Li metal interface. As shown
in Figure 3b, 7Li NMR spectroscopy measurements reveal that
upon addition of Pc to the 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent
containing LiTFSI, the peak position remains unchanged at
−1.57 ppm, indicating no perturbation of the Li nucleus by Pc.
Additionally, Raman spectroscopy in Figure 3c and attenuated
total reflectance (ATR) FTIR (Figure S16) analysis of electrolytes
with varying Pc concentrations present that the characteristic
peaks associated with Li+ coordination to solvent molecules
of DME and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), and anions (TFS−) remain
unaltered compared to the blank electrolyte [55, 56]. Consistent
with these observations, UV–vis analysis (Figure S17) shows
that pure Pc and the Pc-containing electrolyte exhibit identical
absorption peaks, further confirming the absence of bulk-phase
interactions between Pc and the electrolyte. Combined with the
low concentration of Pc used in this study, the results manifest
that Pc molecules are preferentially adsorbed onto the Li metal
surface rather than remaining in the electrolyte to tune the
solvation structure.
To verify the preferential adsorption of Pc molecules onto the Li
metal surface to form a dense protective layer, density functional
theory (DFT) calculations were performed to determine the
adsorption configuration and energy between the Pc and Li metal
(001) crystal plane. As shown in Figure 3d, a strong adsorption
interaction corresponding to Li─N chemical bonding is observed,
with an adsorption energy of −6.78 eV. This value is significantly
higher than the binding energies of common ether solvent-
based molecules in Li–S electrolyte, such as DME (−0.3289 eV)
and DOL (−0.3091 eV), when interacting with the (001) crystal
plane of Li, indicating that Pc presents a competitive adsorption
advantage at the Li metal interface. To further investigate the
adsorption state of Pc on the Li metal surface, Li metal was
immersed in a DME solution containing Pc for 24 h. After
DME washing, XPS analysis was conducted. The N 1s spectrum
in Figure 3e shows characteristic peaks at 396.2 and 394.2 eV,
corresponding to pyrrolic nitrogen and imine nitrogen within
the Pc. Correspondingly, the characteristic peak at 395.2 eV
is attributed to the Li─N chemical bond formed between Pc
and the Li surface [57]. Simultaneously, SEM and elemental
mapping analysis on the immersed Li foil indicate the uniform
distribution of nitrogen elements on the Li surface, providing
direct evidence for the formation of a uniform and dense Pc
adsorption layer (Figure S18). The core function of the Pc layer lies
in optimizing the desolvation kinetics of Li+ at the Li interface.
In the electrolyte, Li+ migrates in the form of a solvation sheath.
After attaching to the Li metal interface, it overcomes a high
desolvation energy barrier to shed its solvation shell and gain
electrons for deposition (Li plating). If the desolvation process
is hindered, the Li plating/stripping rate slows down, leading to
localized accumulation and ultimately inducing dendrite growth.
The Pc-covered Li anode surface can form stronger interactions
with solvation molecules, facilitating Li+ in breaking free from
the solvent through a “pulling effect” and efficiently entering the
Li metal to complete Li plating. To quantitatively understand the
effect, the DFT calculations were performed to determine the
adsorption energies of common solvation structures on different
Li metal interfaces (Figure 3f). The corresponding optimized
adsorption configurations are provided in Figure S19. Specifically,
the adsorption energy between the solvation structure and the
Pc@Li metal interface (−6.37 eV) is significantly higher than
that on the blank Li metal interface (−2.03 eV). In Figure 3g
and Figure S20, the upfield shift in the 17O NMR and 1H NMR
spectra of the Pc-containing DME solution, compared to the
DME solvent, indicates a strong interaction between Pc and the
solvent molecules. These results directly confirm the role of Pc
in accelerating the desolvation process at the Li metal interface
and promoting rapid and uniform Li deposition, in contrast to
bulk desolvation that occurs in the electrolyte, where Pc enables
interfacial desolvation by forming a protective layer on the Li
surface while the bulk solvation structure remains intact. In
summary, Pc in anolyte forms a dense adsorption layer on the
surface of Li metal anode, efficiently modulating the desolvation
kinetics to suppress dendrite growth and enable stable, efficient
Li plating/stripping.

cal role of Pc in suppressing dendrites and optimizing the anode

Owing to the high scattering cross-section and neutron absorp-
tion sensitivity of Li, neutron imaging was conducted on the
cycled Li anodes, leveraging their wide-field imaging capability
to visualize macroscopic Li distribution dynamics. The Li||Li cells
symmetric cycled at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mA h cm−2 for 15, 35, and
100 cycles were disassembled and encapsulated in aluminum-
laminated film. Pseudocolor rendering images of pixel grayscale
values (Figure 4a) show homogeneous distribution across pristine
Li surfaces, indicating uniform neutron absorption. As depicted
in Figure 4a, with cycling, continuous Li plating/stripping leads
to progressive Li accumulation and an increasingly heteroge-
neous distribution. Neutron imaging quantitatively confirms this
trend, with both samples showing progressive expansion of blue
regions and significant color dispersion. A key difference is the
more homogeneous color distribution in cycled Li anodes with
Pc, reflecting uniform Li deposition and contrasting sharply with
anodes using blank electrolyte. Generally, smaller cavities in the
cycled Li anode indicate more uniform and denser deposition
behavior. This observation is further supported by time-of-flight
secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) depth profiling
(Figure S29), where the Pc-modified anode exhibits fewer and
more uniformly distributed ion fragments, confirming its role
in promoting homogeneous Li deposition. In contrast, the Li
anode cycled without Pc shows more abundant and unevenly dis-
tributed ion fragments, suggesting the presence of non-uniform
lithium deposition and increased dendrite formation. Along this
line, we employed SR SAXS to gather the size information
of internal cavities in Li anodes, with its overall schematic
workflow and corresponding data analysis process illustrated
in Figure 4b. Owing to its powerful penetration capability and
unique sensitivity to structures in the scale range of 1–100 nm,
this technique stands as a powerful facility for characterizing
nanostructures [58, 59]. As presented in the insets of Figure 4d
and Figure S30, the SR SAXS scattering intensity signal matrices
of uncycled and cycled Li anodes show a distinct difference,
which reflects the formation of cavities inside the cycled Li
anodes. The acquired 2D scattering images were integrated into
1D data (I(q)-q), which was then fitted with the Guinier–Porod
model to determine the radius of gyration (Rg). In essence, Rg
quantitatively reflects the overall size distribution of cavities. As
shown in Figure 4c,d, the fitting results demonstrate that the
cycled Li metal with Pc achieves a Rg value of 39.10 nm, the value
of which is lower than the control (42.88 nm). The functionalities
of Pc as a homogeneously dispersed enabler in anolyte for lithium
deposition are further illustrated in Figure 4e. The Pc-containing
anode promotes the formation of smaller and more uniformly
distributed internal cavities, a result of its significantly modulated
desolvation kinetics and dense protective layer effect. This result
is also consistent with the top-view SEM images of the cycled Li
anodes (Figure S31). The cross-sectional SEM images in Figure
S32 reveal that the cycled Li anode with Pc forms a thinner
structure after cycling, thus effectively suppressing Li dendrite
growth.
To investigate the electrochemical performance, Li–S batteries
with different contents of Pc (0–0.05 wt.%) were assembled.
As shown in Figure 5a and Figure S33, the battery with 0.025
wt.% Pc exhibits optimal rate capability, delivering initial specific
capacities of 1024.3, 913.1, 815.8, and 711.9 mA h g−1 at 0.2C, 0.5C,
1C, and 2C, respectively. Further evaluation of the cycling at 0.2C
with different electrolytes is presented in Figure 5b. The battery
containing 0.025 wt.% Pc retains 88.5% of the initial capacity after
200 cycles, implying a remarkable cycling performance. As seen
in Figure 5c and Figure S34, the corresponding galvanostatic dis-
charge/charge curves demonstrate an enhanced cycling stability
at 0.2C for the battery with 0.025 wt.% Pc. These results identify
0.025 wt.% as the optimal content for Pc in the electrolyte to
achieve optimal battery performance. To detect the structure of Pc
during cycling, Pc was loaded on the surface of Super P, leading
to a thin layer on the separator. The assembled battery was cycled
at 2C, and the Pc/Super P composites were collected for X-ray
diffraction (XRD) tests. As seen in Figure S35, after working for
30 cycles, the composite still preserves the same patterns as those
in its pristine state, with the characteristic peaks of Pc clearly
identified, which indicates the favorable stability of Pc under the
test conditions.
As shown in Figure 5d, the battery with the optimal Pc content
of 0.025 wt.% shows a capacity retention of 71.2% with the
corresponding decay rate of 0.0479% per cycle for 600 cycles at
1C. More impressively, the battery with 0.025 wt.% Pc retains
average decay rates of 0.037% and 0.075% per cycle at 2C and
5C over 800 cycles (Figure S36). Moreover, Li–S batteries with
0.025 wt.% Pc electrolyte were tested under the conditions of high
sulfur loadings and lean electrolyte usages to assess the practical
viability. As shown in Figure S37, the batteries with sulfur
loadings of 5.7 and 5.9 mg cm−2 exhibit favorable performance at
0.1C and 0.2C over 50 cycles. In Figure 5e, when the sulfur loading
is increased to 8.9 and 10.1 mg cm−2 with the electrolyte dosages of

we report the concept of Li─N coordination bond (Pc@Li2S8),
which effectively enhances the conversion kinetics of sul-

layer and regulates the desolvation kinetics of Li+ at the Li

stable Li plating/stripping. Under a high sulfur loading of
10.08 mg cm−2, the battery with Pc delivers an initial areal

cell with a sulfur loading of 1.05 g achieves a discharge capacity
of 1.07 Ah and an energy density of 325.5 Wh kg−1. By virtue
of the mediating effect of the Pc, this work provides in-depth
insights into chemical interactions between Pc and various key
species in Li–S batteries. Moreover, it establishes a universal theo-
retical framework for Pc-designed structures, offering significant
implications for the development of high-energy Li–S batteries.
