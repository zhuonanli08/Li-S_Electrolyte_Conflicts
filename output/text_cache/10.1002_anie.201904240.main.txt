A Selection Rule for Hydrofluoroether Electrolyte Cosolvent:
Establishing a Linear Free-Energy Relationship in Lithium–Sulfur
Batteries
Chi-Cheung Su+, Meinan He+, Rachid Amine, and Khalil Amine*
Abstract: Hydrofluoroethers (HFEs) have been adopted
widely as electrolyte cosolvents for battery systems because of
their unique low solvating behavior. The electrolyte is currently
utilized in lithium-ion, lithium–sulfur, lithium–air, and sodium-
ion batteries. By evaluating the relative solvating power of
different HFEs with distinct structural features, and consider-
ing the shuttle factor displayed by electrolytes that employ HFE
cosolvents, we have established the quantitative structure–
activity relationship between the organic structure and the
electrochemical performance of the HFEs. Moreover, we have
established the linear free-energy relationship between the
structural properties of the electrolyte cosolvents and the
polysulfide shuttle effect in lithium–sulfur batteries. These
findings provide valuable mechanistic insight into the poly-
sulfide shuttle effect in lithium–sulfur batteries, and are
instructive when it comes to selecting the most suitable HFE
electrolyte cosolvent for different battery systems.
Over the past few decades, lithium-ion batteries have been
applied extensively in consumer electronic devices owing to
their relatively high energy density and operating voltage.[1–3]
To meet the demands of electric vehicle applications,
however, the energy density of state-of-the-art lithium
batteries must be increased substantially.[4–6] Various new
lithium-battery systems with high theoretical energy density
have been proposed.[5–7] For these new battery systems to be
successful, it is critical to develop reliable electrolytes and to
understand their working principles.[8,9] First introduced as an
electrolyte cosolvent a decade ago,[10] hydrofluoroethers
(HFEs) have rapidly been employed by researchers all over
the world to construct functional electrolytes for high-voltage
lithium-ion,[10–12] lithium-metal,[13] lithium–sulfur (Li–S),[14]
lithium–air,[15,16]
lithium/selenium–sulfur,[17]
and
even
sodium-ion batteries.[18,19] Because of their low solvating
ability, HFEs are exceptionally versatile as electrolyte
cosolvents.
They
offer
several
important
advantages:
1) enhanced oxidative stability of electrolytes,[10–12] 2) they
serve as excellent thinning reagents for reducing the viscosity
of electrolytes,[20–22] and 3) they enable the construction of
localized concentrated electrolytes, which are highly useful in
lithium-metal batteries.[13,23–25] Significantly, they have gained
popularity in Li–S batteries because of their effectiveness in
suppressing the lithium polysulfides (LiPS) shuttle effect.[26–34]
Despite the extensive application of HFEs, the effect of
their intricate molecular structure on electrochemical behav-
ior has hardly been studied. By comparing the electrochem-
ical properties of four HFEs, Shin and co-workers concluded
that the degree of fluorination (that is, the number of fluorine
atoms) dictated the solvation behavior.[21,35] Yet, it is critical to
also consider the position of the fluoroalkyl groups in the
HFE structures when evaluating their solvation behavior
because the lithium ion is coordinated to the oxygen atom of
the ether molecule, and the inductive effect imposed by the
fluoroalkyl groups on the oxygen is highly dependent on the
proximity of the electron-withdrawing fluoroalkyl groups. In
other words, the absolute number of electron-withdrawing
fluorine atoms is not as dominant as the position of those
fluorine atoms in controlling the short-chain (< 7 carbon
atoms) HFE solvation property.
The lithium-solvating ability of different HFE solvents,
and the linear free-energy relationship (LFER) between the
solvents and LiPS shuttle kinetics, were determined using
internally referenced diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (IR-
DOSY), calculations of the diffusion coefficient–coordination
ratio (D-a), and knowledge of the relative solvating power
(g). Together with cell test data on electrochemical perfor-
mance, we have derived a selection principle for HFEs based
on the position of the electron-withdrawing fluoroalkyl
groups.
The general structure of a HFE, where the fluoroalkyl
groups can be found at the a and b or a and b’ positions, is
depicted in Scheme S1 (Supporting Information). Note that
the term “a or b substitution” is defined by the closest
position of a fluoroalkyl group to the oxygen atom, and
a HFE with a-substituted fluoroalkyl group can also possess
a fluoroalkyl group in its b position. The HFEs studied were
categorized into three groups according to the vicinity of the
fluoroalkyl groups to the oxygen atom (Supporting Informa-
tion,
Figure S1).

Group B
includes
1-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)propane
(TFEPE) and 2-methyl-1-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)propane
(TFEiBE), both of which only possess an a-substituted
fluoroalkyl group on one side of the molecule. Group C
comprises
1,1,1-trifluoro-2-(2,2,2-trifluoroethoxy)ethane
(BTFE), which is a b,b’-fluoroalkyl-substituted HFE.
The recent development of IR-DOSY and the D-a
analysis techniques has overcome the limitations of vibra-
tional spectroscopy (Fourier-transform infrared and Raman
spectroscopy) and realized the quantitative determination of
the solvent solvation state.[36,37] The concept of relative
solvating power (g), which is the ratio between the coordi-
nation percentage of a test solvent and a reference solvent,
was established as a useful indicator for the LiPS shuttling
effect.[37]
Herein, the IR-DOSY technique was applied to probe the
solvation behavior and g of HFEs in an electrolyte containing
lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) salt and
1,3-dioxolane (DOL) solvent. The resonances of DOL,
BTFE, and toluene are easily identified in the
1H NMR
spectrum of a 1:4:1 (molar ratio) LiTFSI:DOL:BTFE solu-
tion (Supporting Information, Figure S2). Figures 1a and b
present the DOSY spectra of 4:1 (molar ratio) DOL:BTFE
and 1:4:1 LiTFSI:DOL:BTFE, respectively, with toluene as
an internal reference. The data indicate that DOL diffuses the
fastest, while BTFE was the slowest before the addition of
LiTFSI; however, after the addition of LiTFSI, DOL showed
the largest decrease in diffusion coefficient, implying that
DOL was solvating the lithium significantly. The diffusion
coefficients and coordination numbers of DOL and BTFE, as
well
as
the
coordination
number
of
lithium
in
1:4:1
LiTFSI:DOL:BTFE solution, show that DOL possesses
higher solvating power towards lithium than BTFE (Support-
ing Information, Table S1).
The diffusion coefficients, coordination ratios, and coor-
dination numbers of lithium for TFEPE- and TFEiBE-based
electrolyte are displayed in Tables S2 and S3 (Supporting
Information), respectively. The coordination ratio of these
group B HFEs was notably smaller than that of the group C
HFE, indicating a decrease of lithium-solvating power from
group C to group B HFE. Moreover, the coordination
numbers in all three electrolytes are very similar (ca. 2),
suggesting that the lithium ions are predominantly solvated
by DOL. Owing to the presence of two electron-withdrawing
fluoroalkyl groups situated near the oxygen, group A HFEs
demonstrate remarkably low solvating ability toward lithium
ions.
The diffusion coefficient ratios of TTE- and TFETFE-
based solution before and after the LiTFSI addition are
presented in Tables S4 and S5 (Supporting Information),
respectively. For group A HFEs, the ratios of these diffusion
coefficients (Dunsalted/Dsalted) are very close to the correspond-
ing ratios of toluene. This similarity indicates that the group A
HFEs possess the same lithium-solvating power as toluene,
which is a hydrocarbon with exceptionally low polarity and
donor number.[36] In fact, LiTFSI in pure TTE or TFETFE has
no solubility because of the lithium non-solvating behavior of
group A HFEs.
From the coordination ratios of BTFE, TFEiBE, and
TFEPE, we calculated the g values of the corresponding
HFEs (Table 1). As a consequence of the non-solvating
nature of the group A HFEs, the g values of TTE and
TFETFE are very close to zero. Table 1 also indicates that the
solvating power of HFE is significantly smaller than that of
various non-fluorinated ethers.[37] Even the group C HFE has
a much lower g than methyl-tert-butylether (MtBe), which is
not able to solvate lithium effectively because of the steric
hindrance of the t-butyl group. The results indicate that the

Figures 2a and b depict, respectively, the capacity reten-
tion and Coulombic efficiency of Li–S cells employing various
electrolytes: LiTFSI (1.0m) plus 1:1 DOL:TTE, 1:1 DOL:
TFETFE, 1:1 DOL:TFEPE, 1:1 DOL:TFEiBE, and 1:1
DOL:BTFE. The Li–S cells were cycled from 1.5 to 2.8 V at
a C/10 rate for 80 cycles. The cells using TTE-, TFETFE-,
TFEPE-, and TFEiBE-based electrolytes all exhibited a high
initial specific discharge capacity (> 1500 mAhg@1), and the
capacity of the cells decayed to around 600 mAhg@1 at the
80th cycle. However, the cell employing BTFE-based electro-
lyte displayed a significantly lower initial capacity with better
capacity retention.
To fairly evaluate the LiPS shuttling effect, it is necessary
to compare the Coulombic efficiencies of all the Li–S cells at
the same specific charge/discharge capacity.[37] The Coulom-
bic efficiencies of the test cells at a specific discharge capacity
of 850 mAhg@1, and the corresponding shuttle factors, are
shown in Table 1. Determined from Equation (S1) (Support-
ing Information), the shuttle factor (f) was introduced to
reflect the degree of the LiPS shuttle effect on Coulombic
efficiency, and this effect can be estimated with Equation (S2)
(Supporting Information).[38–40] The f values of the HFE-based
electrolyte are much smaller than those of regular ethers at
the same discharge capacity (Table 1). The shuttle factor
displayed by the electrolyte containing the highly bulky
MtBE cosolvent (0.44) is still significantly larger than that of
the BTFE-based electrolyte (0.27). Similar to the solvation
study, these cycling results demonstrate the dominance of the
electron-withdrawing effect (imposed by fluoroalkyl groups)
over the steric effect of an ether cosolvent, and the
consequent mitigation of LiPS dissolution.
The HFE cosolvents can be divided into three groups
according to their shuttle factors. The BTFE-based electrolyte
(0.27) possesses larger f values compared to TFEPE- and
TFEiBE-based electrolytes (0.17); meanwhile, the TTE- and
TFETFE based-electrolytes (0.04) display the lowest f values.
This division is the same as that of the fluoroalkyl position-
based grouping. To summarize, group A HFEs present the
lowest lithium-solvating power and LiPS dissolution ability,
while the group C HFE is the highest among the three groups.
The same conclusion can also be drawn by comparing the f
values for various Li–S cells operated at the same charge
capacity of 1050 mAhg@1 (Supporting Information, Table S6).
Conductivity testing was also performed to probe the
ionic transportability of the different HFE electrolyte sys-
tems. The conductivity is highest for BTFE-based electrolyte
and lowest for the TTE-based electrolyte (Supporting Infor-
mation, Figure S3). Clearly, the group C HFE-based electro-
lyte displays the highest conductivity among all three groups
because of its relatively high lithium-solvating ability and
polarity. Although the group A HFE electrolyte is the best in
mitigating LiPS dissolution, it exhibits the lowest conductivity
as a result of its strictly non-solvating nature. The temperature
dependence of the conductivity is well described by the
Vogel–Tamman–Fulcher (VTF) empirical equation illus-
trated in Table S7 (for a detailed discussion on the VTF
fitting parameters see the Supporting Information). Never-
theless, the conductivity of the HFE-based electrolyte follows
the group order C > B > A. These results explain why the
group C HFE, as opposed to the group A HFE, is usually
chosen as a cosolvent for electrolytes requiring a high
charging rate.[13,25]
To assess the quantitative structure–activity relationships
between the electrolyte solvents and LiPS side reactions, we
plotted the log[2f + ln(1 + f)] at a discharge capacity of
850 mAhg@1 against logg (Figure 3a) and log[2f@ln(1@f)] at
a discharge capacity of 1050 mAhg@1 against logg (Fig-
ure 3b). The expressions [2f + ln(1 + f)] and [2f@ln(1@f)]
are correlated with the reaction constant ks (Equation (S5),
Supporting Information) at a fixed discharge/charge capacity
(for a detailed discussion see the Supporting Information).[40]
The fact that linear relationships were obtained for log[2f +
ln(1 + f)]/log[2f@ln(1@f)] versus logg unambiguously dem-
onstrates the existence of an LFER between the structure of
the electrolyte solvents and the LiPS shuttle effect in Li–S
batteries. This firmly establishes a direct correlation between
the g of the electrolyte solvent and the LiPS shuttle effect.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to
establish the LFER between electrolyte solvents and the
interfacial reactions in lithium batteries. The high correlation
coefficients (R2 + 0.96) indicate the credibility of the LFER,

governs the degree of LiPS dissolution.
In conclusion, we developed a selection rule for common
HFEs. The commonly used HFEs are divided into three major
groups according to the vicinity of the fluoroalkyl groups to
the oxygen atom. Owing to the existence of both a- and b’-
substituted fluoroalkyl groups, group A HFEs display the
lowest lithium-solvating power and highest ability to mitigate
LiPS dissolution, while the group C HFE shows the opposite
effects. However, the electrolyte based on group C HFE
presents the highest conductivity, rendering group C HFE
more suitable as a cosolvent in electrolyte targeting a high
charging rate. The results clearly demonstrate the significance
of the position of electron-withdrawing fluoroalkyl groups in
dictating the properties of HFEs. These findings provide
guidance not only in choosing a suitable HFE cosolvent, but
also designing and synthesizing new functional HFEs. Sig-
nificantly, an LFER between electrolyte solvents and the
interfacial reactions in Li–S batteries was established for the
first time. The newly developed LFER parameters can serve
as a powerful tool in probing the mechanism of LiPS reactions
and the effectiveness of functional materials in mitigating
LiPS dissolution.
