A B S T R A C T  
In lithium-sulfur batteries, cell design, specifically electrolyte design, has a key impact on the battery perfor­
mance. Development of sulfolane based electrolytes may grant the use of more available lithium salts such as 
LiClO4, LiPF6 and LiBF4, which are typically not preferred in lithium-sulfur batteries due to their reactive anions. 
Herein, in order to assess the reactivity of lithium polysulfides with various dimensions (with different lengths of 
the polysulfide chain) towards the anions of lithium electrolyte salts, the cycling of lithium-sulfur cells with 1 M 
solutions of LiPF6, LiBF4, and LiClO4 in sulfolane is studied in a wide (1.5–2.8 V) and narrow (limited) voltage 
ranges, corresponding to the high-voltage (2.1–2.8 V) and low-voltage (2.3–1.5 V) plateaus on the discharge 
curves. It is shown that the electrochemical characteristics of lithium-sulfur cells with sulfolane based electro­
lytes of lithium perchlorate and lithium hexafluorophosphate differ significantly from those for sulfolane elec­
trolyte based on lithium tetrafluoroborate. We believe that these differences are due to the direct chemical 
interaction of lithium polysulfides with the BF4
−anion, which is confirmed by chronopotentiometry, 7Li and 11B 
NMR spectra, UV–Vis and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of sulfolane solutions of the studied lithium 
salts, saturated with lithium polysulfides.

1. Introduction 
Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSB), which have been intensively studied 
in the last decade, are classified as one of the most promising energy 
storage systems beyond Li-ion batteries. In LSBs, the active materials of 
the positive electrode - elemental sulfur and the intermediate products 
of its electrochemical reduction, lithium polysulfides - are dissolved in 
the electrolyte [1]. As the active materials of the LSB liquid cathode - 
sulfur and lithium polysulfides - are soluble in the electrolyte, their 
chemical interaction with the components of the electrolyte systems is 
possible. Therefore, when developing electrolytes for the lithium-sulfur 
batteries, it is necessary to take into account not only the physico­
chemical, but also the chemical properties of the solvents and salts in the 
electrolyte system. 
The solubility of sulfur in the electrolytes is typically low and sulfur 
does not have a high reactivity towards the aprotic dipolar solvents and 
anions of lithium salts used as the electrolyte components of the lithium- 
sulfur batteries. On the other hand, lithium polysulfides are readily 
dissolved in the electrolytes and thus may be involved in nucleophilic 
substitution and addition reactions [2]. The high reactivity of lithium 
polysulfides limits the number of solvents and lithium salts suitable for 
the use in lithium-sulfur battery electrolytes. As example, organic car­
bonates, which are widely used solvents in lithium-ion battery electro­
lytes, are not applicable in lithium-sulfur battery electrolytes [2–4]. 
However, it should be noted that carbonate solvents can be used in LSBs 
with the positive electrodes containing carbon materials, in the crystal 
lattice of which low-dimensional species of lithium polysulfides can be 
placed (LSBs with positive electrodes based on sulfur-carbon compos­
ites) [2,5–8]. Since low-dimensional species of lithium polysulfides have 
low chemical activity, they do not interact with organic carbonates. 
Ethers such as ethylene glycol dimethyl ether (DME), polyethylene 
glycol dimethyl ether (PEGDME), tri- and tetraethylene glycol dimethyl 
ethers (TEGDME), and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), are the most common 
electrolyte solvents used in LSBs since these solvents are chemically 
stable towards the lithium polysulfides. 
In recent years, sulfones and their mixtures have attracted increasing 
attention as electrolyte solvents for the LSBs [9,10]. Sulfones have a 
higher chemical stability compared to ethers, which allows the use of 
lithium salts with reactive anions, such as LiClO4, LiPF6 and LiBF4, as 
electrolyte salts in sulfone electrolytes. Sulfolane is of particular interest 
because of its high chemical, electrochemical and thermal stability, 
cheapness and industrial availability [11,12]. 
The most commonly used salts in lithium-sulfur battery electrolytes 
are lithium bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and lithium 
trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiCF3SO3) because they have high thermal 
stability, good compatibility with ethereal solvents, and a high degree of 
electrolytic dissociation [13,14]. On the other hand, LiPF6 and LiBF4 
salts that are widely used in lithium-ion battery electrolytes, cannot be 
used in LSB electrolyte systems due to concerns about the interaction of 
their anions with the lithium polysulfides [15]. In addition, the use of 
LiPF6 may lead to the formation of Lewis acids, which may result in the 
decomposition of the ethereal solvents. 
When developing electrolyte systems for the next-generation energy 
storage devices, their cost and market availability are highly important. 
Even though the cheapest and the most accessible salts for lithium and 
lithium-ion battery electrolytes are LiPF6 and LiBF4 as previously 
mentioned [14], the possibility of their application in the electrolytes of 
lithium-sulfur batteries has not been fully studied [16–20]. Therefore, 
the aim of this study is to evaluate the prospect of using LiPF6, LiBF4, and 
LiClO4 salts in lithium-sulfur battery electrolytes based on sulfolane. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation of electrolytes 
1 M solutions of lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6, battery grade, 
≥99.99%, Sigma Aldrich), lithium tetrafluoroborate (LiBF4, anhydrous, 
99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) and lithium perchlorate (LiClO4, battery grade, 
99.995%, Aldrich) in sulfolane were studied as electrolytes. Sulfolane 
(99%, Aldrich) was purified and dried by vacuum double distillation. 
The water content in the electrolyte solutions, which is determined by 
coulometric titration in Fischer reagent medium using a Titroline®7500 
KF trace automatic titrator (SI Analytics), was ≤50 ppm. 
2.2. Preparation and characterization of lithium polysulfides solutions 
Lithium polysulfides in the studied electrolytes were obtained by 
reacting sulfur (S, 99.5%, Acros) and lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.9%, 
Sigma-Aldrich) in a molar ratio of 7:1 at 30 ◦C. The weight ratio of the 
mixture of sulfur and lithium sulfide to the electrolyte was 1 : 9.2. The 
completion of the formation of lithium polysulfides in the electrolyte 
solutions and the possible interaction of the lithium polysulfides with 
the lithium salt anions (ClO4
−, PF6
−
and BF4
−) were controlled by the 
concentration of the S2−, checked by using 7Li and 11B NMR and UV–Vis 
spectroscopy. 
The solubility of the lithium polysulfides was calculated using the 
concentration of the S2−. Concentration of the S2−(mol/kg) in the sul­
folane solutions of lithium polysulfides was determined by back iodo­
metric titration (with potentiometric indication of the end point of the 
titration) using a TitroLine®5000 automatic titrator (SI Analytics). 
High-resolution NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AvanceIII 
500 MHz NMR spectrometer with 7Li nuclei at a frequency of 194.37 
MHz, with 19F nuclei at a frequency of 470.59 MHz, with 11B nuclei at a 
frequency of 160.46 MHz, with 31P nuclei at a frequency of 202.46 MHz, 
and with 13C nuclei at a frequency of 125.75 MHz with proton sup­
pression (WALTZ-16), on 1H nuclei - at a frequency of 500.13 MHz, on 
17O nuclei - at a frequency of 67.80 MHz, and on 33S nuclei - at a fre­
quency of 38.39 MHz. 
In order to prevent dilution of the test solutions with a deuterated 
solvent, 5 mm Wilmad® ampoules (NMR tubes 5 mm) with Wilmad® 
axial inserts (stem L 50 mm, reference capacity 60 μl, sample capacity 
530 μl) filled with 9.7 m solutions of LiCl in D2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9% 
atom D) were used. The studies were carried out at a constant sample 
temperature of 25 ± 2 ◦C. 
Absorption spectra of sulfolane solutions of lithium polysulfides, 
lithium salts and their mixtures were recorded in quartz cuvettes with a 
thickness of 0.5 mm on a Shimadzu UV-2600 dual-beam spectropho­
tometer in the visible and ultraviolet ranges (200–1100 nm) at 30◦С. 
2.3. Electrochemical measurements 
All electrochemical studies were performed in sealed two-electrode 
disk cells of the Swagelok® cell type, made of stainless steel. Auxiliary 
electrodes were made of a 100 μm thick metallic lithium foil (99.9%, 
China Cell Energy, PRC). A single layer of Celgard®3501 was used as the 
separator. 
The preload pressure of the electrode module in lithium-sulfur cells 
was 0.3 kg/cm2. 
The preparation of the electrolyte solutions and lithium polysulfide 
solutions, and the assembly of lithium-sulfur cells were all carried out in 
a glove box under argon atmosphere (the contents of water and oxygen 
were <0.1 ppm). 
2.3.1. Cycling of lithium-sulfur cells with solid sulfur electrode 
In lithium-sulfur cells, sulfur electrodes (A = 6.35 cm2) were used as 
the working electrodes: S (99.5%, Acros) - 70% wt., Ketjenblack® EC- 
600JD (Akzo Nobel) - 10% wt. and polyethylene oxide (MM = 4 ×
106, Aldrich) - 20% wt. The sulfur loading in the electrodes was 2 mAh/ 
cm2, which is equivalent to a sulfur content of about 1.2 mg/cm2. The 
amount of electrolyte in the electrochemical cells was 4 μL/mAh(S), 
which is equivalent to an electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 6.7 μL/mg. 
Cycling of lithium-sulfur cells was carried out in galvanostatic mode

The electrochemical activity of the lithium polysulfide solutions was

used in the amount of 4 μL/cm2. 
Galvanostatic cycling of Li–S cells were carried out using a BioLogic 
BCS-810 battery tester in the voltage range of 1.5–2.8 V at 30 ◦C. The

2.3.3. EIS measurements 
EIS measurements were performed with a Biologic SP200 potentio­
stat/galvanostat at 30◦С. The cells first rested for 24 h at the open circuit

(0.1C, 168 mA/g(S)) and charged at 0.1 mA/cm2 (0.05C, 84 mA/g(S)) 
within a voltage window of 1.5–2.8 V. EIS measurements were taken at 
different depths of discharge during the initial discharge and charge of 
the lithium-sulfur cell under conditions similar to those described in the 
article [21]. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Properties of solutions of lithium salts in sulfolane 
Solutions of lithium salts in sulfolane have lower specific ion con­
ductivity and higher viscosity (Table 1) towards solutions of similar salts 
in other solvents [22]. As seen in the table, the sulfolane solution of 
LiPF6 has the highest specific ion conductivity and viscosity, whereas the 
LiBF4 solution has the lowest. 
Here, it should be noted that the low specific ion conductivity of the 
electrolyte solutions is in fact not a critical factor. As previously dis­
cussed, during the discharge/charge of the LSB, the lithium polysulfides 
formed as reaction intermediates dissolve in the electrolyte solution. 
Since the solubility of the lithium polysulfides is low in the most of the 
electrolyte solutions [26], and the ratio of the electrolyte volume to the 
sulfur amount is small, during discharge/charge, lithium polysulfides 
are released into a separate phase as solvate complexes after the for­
mation of saturated solutions. At a limited amount of electrolyte in the 
LSB, almost all of the solvent molecules are bound in the solvate shells of 
the lithium ions of the supporting salts and the lithium polysulfides. 
Consequently, the specific ion conductivity of the electrolyte system is 
determined by the specific ion conductivity of the resulting mixture of 
the solvate complexes of lithium salts and lithium polysulfides rather 
than the electrolyte solution itself. Solvent molecules bind into the sol­
vate shells of the lithium ions, which belong to the lithium polysulfides, 
with an increase in the amount of lithium polysulfides formed till the 
formation of lithium tetrasulfide. During the electrochemical reduction 
of lithium tetrasulfide, the total amount of lithium polysulfides in the 
positive electrode does not increase, since lithium sulfide is predomi­
nantly formed, which is released into the solid phase. Since the amount 
of lithium polysulfides in the positive electrode does not increase, the 
amount of bound solvent does not increase either [27]. Therefore, when 
choosing the optimal solvent, it is necessary to focus not on the specific 
ion conductivity and viscosity of the electrolyte solutions used, but on 
the properties of the solvate complexes of lithium salts and lithium 
polysulfides with the electrolyte solvents. 
3.2. Electrochemical performance of lithium-sulfur cells with electrolytes 
based on various salts 
As the reactivity of the lithium polysulfides is determined by the 
length of the polysulfide chain, we studied the cycling of lithium–sulfur 
cells (LSCs) both in wide (1.5–2.8 V) and narrow (limited) voltage 
ranges corresponding to the high-voltage (2.1–2.8 V) and the low- 
voltage (2.3–1.5 V) plateaus on the discharge voltage profiles. At the 
high-voltage plateau, the electrochemical reduction of sulfur and long- 
chain lithium polysulfides to lithium tetrasulfide takes place, whereas 
at the low-voltage plateau, lithium tetrasulfide is electrochemically 
reduced to lithium disulfide and lithium sulfide. 
Charge/discharge voltage profiles, the cycling performance and the 
Coulombic efficiency of lithium-sulfur cells with electrolytes based on 
various salts in different voltage ranges are shown in Figs. 1–3. It can be 
seen that the electrochemical characteristics of the LSCs with electro­
lytes based on LiClO4 and LiPF6 differ significantly from those with 
LiBF4 as discussed next. 
3.2.1. Cycling performance of LSCs containing electrolytes of 1 M LiClO4 or 
LiPF6 in sulfolane 
The voltage profiles and cycling performance of LSCs with electro­
lytes based on LiClO4 or LiPF6 in a wide voltage range (1.5–2.8 V) are 
reported in Fig. 1a/1d and 2a/2d, respectively. For both electrolytes, the 
discharge capacity decreases (approximately twofold) rapidly for the 
first 50–60 cycles. Then, the rate of the discharge capacity depletion 
decreases; it reaches ≈0.28% per cycle for LiClO4 and ≈0.46% per cycle 
for LiPF6. Similarly, the Coulombic efficiency also decreases continu­
ously up to the 50th cycle, reaching ≈88–90%, and then begins to in­
crease slowly. 
When LSCs are cycled in a narrow voltage range (2.1–2.8 V), cor­
responding to the high-voltage plateau on the discharge voltage profile, 
a continuous capacity decay is observed (Fig. 1b/1e and 2b/2e). Still, it 
may be discussed that the rate of capacity depletion decreases gradually 
with increasing cycle number. On the other hand, the Coulombic effi­
ciency of cycling in the range of 2.1–2.8 V decreases significantly with 
cycling, reaching ≈65–75% by the 300th cycle. 
In the low-voltage range (2.3–1.5 V), the dependence of the capacity 
depletion of the LSCs on the number of charge-discharge cycles is more 
complex (Fig. 1c/1f and 2c/2f). It may be discussed that up to about 
10–20 cycles, there is a rapid decrease in the capacity by almost a factor 
of two. Then, the discharge capacity either ceases to change (in the case 
of a LSCs with an electrolyte based on LiClO4) or slightly increases (in 
the case of a LSCs with an electrolyte based on LiPF6). However, after 
several tens of cycles, the discharge capacity starts to decrease again at a

Since long-chain lithium polysulfides are more reactive, their rate of 
interaction with the metallic lithium is higher than medium- and short- 
chain lithium polysulfides. In the presence of long-chain lithium poly­
sulfides (when LSCs are cycling in the voltage range of 2.1–2.8 V), the 
protective surface film on the lithium metal electrode, which consists 
mainly of lithium sulfide, dissolves, and consequently its protective 
properties decrease. This ultimately leads to the interaction of long- 
chain lithium polysulfides with the lithium metal electrode and a sig­
nificant decrease in the Coulombic efficiency of LSCs cycling. 
Medium- and short-chain lithium polysulfides, which are mainly 
present in the electrolyte system when LSCs are cycling in the voltage 
range of 1.5–2.3 V, have low solubility with respect to lithium sulfide. 
Moreover, the interaction of medium- and short-chain lithium poly­
sulfides with the metallic lithium results in the formation of lithium 
sulfide, which deposits on the surface of the metallic lithium since it is 
insoluble in the electrolyte and forms a surface film that prevents further 
interaction of the metallic lithium with the components of the electro­
lyte system. As a result, the Coulombic efficiency of the cycling of the 
lithium electrode, and hence the lithium-sulfur cells, is significantly 
higher than those in the voltage range of 2.1–2.8 V. 
The rate of capacity decrease is mainly determined by the rate of 
passivation of the surface of carbon particles by the precipitation of 
lithium sulfide. Lithium sulfide is formed during cycling of the LSC in the 
voltage range of 1.5–2.3 V. Therefore, the rate of decrease in the ca­
pacity of the LSC when cycling in this voltage range is greater than when 
cycling at higher voltages. 
3.2.2. Cycling performance of LSCs containing an electrolyte of 1 M LiBF4 
in sulfolane 
The patterns of cycling of LSCs containing 1 M LiBF4 in sulfolane as 
an electrolyte differ significantly from those for LSCs with electrolytes 
based on LiClO4 or LiPF6 (Fig. 3a vs Figs. 1a and 2a). The initial (first) 
discharge voltage profile of the LSCs with an electrolyte containing 
LiBF4 is similar to those with LiClO4 or LiPF6. However, already in the 
second cycle, a kink appears on the high-voltage plateau of the discharge 
voltage profile at ~2.3 V. Moreover, the discharge capacity of the LSCs 
with electrolytes based on LiBF4 sharply decreases in the first 10 cycles, 
and then begins to gradually increase. Three plateaus are observed on 
the charge voltage profiles: one in the low voltage range of 1.5–2.3 V, 
and two in the high-voltage ranges of 2.20–2.35 V and 2.35–2.50 V 
(Fig. 3a). During cycling, the length of the high-voltage plateau, 
observed in the range of 2.35–2.50 V, increases, and after about 200 
cycles, LSCs with electrolytes based on LiBF4 cease to be charged. 
Fig. 3d reports the cycling behavior of the LSCs with electrolytes 
based on LiBF4. The Coulombic efficiency of cycling of LSCs in the range 
of 1.5–2.8 V increases from 85 to 95% in the first few cycles and then 
begins to decrease. As cycling progresses, the Coulombic efficiency starts 
to decrease at a higher rate with increasing cycle number (Fig. 3d). 
When the LSCs are cycled in the range of 2.1–2.8 V, at the initial 
stages of cycling, the capacity also rapidly decreases by almost a factor 
of two, and then the rate of capacity depletion decreases to ≈0.18% per 
cycle. Likewise, the Coulombic efficiency of cycling of LSCs in the range 
of 2.1–2.8 V diminishes at a great rate, and the LSCs stop charging at the 
140th cycle (Fig. 3e). 
In a similar manner, when the LSCs are cycled in the range of 1.5–2.3 
V, their capacity decreases significantly in the first 5 cycles; then slightly 
increases, reaching a constant rate of capacity depletion of ≈0.19% per 
cycle. Yet, after 150 cycles the capacity starts to decay notably once 
again. Meanwhile, the Coulombic efficiency of cycling remains almost 
constant until the end of cycling (Fig. 3f). 
The differences in the cycling of LSCs with electrolytes based on 
LiBF4 and electrolytes based on LiClO4 or LiPF6 are due to the interaction 
of the BF4
−anion with the long-chain lithium polysulfides, leading to the 
formation of lithium boron polysulfides as given in Eq. (1).  
LiBF4 + 4Li2Sn → LiBFx(SnLi)4-x + (4-x)LiF↓                                    
(1) 
Lithium boron polysulfides (LiBFx(SnLi)4-x) formed as a result of this 
reaction, as well as lithium polysulfides, are electrochemically active 
and thus are capable of reversible electrochemical transformations 
(Fig. 1S, Supplementary Materials). Moreover, the chemical activity of 
long-chain lithium boron polysulfides towards metallic lithium is higher 
than the chemical activity of long-chain lithium polysulfides. Since one 
of the factors affecting the shuttle effect is the rate of interaction of 
lithium polysulfides with metallic lithium [28], as lithium boron poly­
sulfides are formed during the charge/discharge cycling of the LSCs, the 
length of the high-voltage plateaus on the charge voltage profiles in­
crease, and eventually the cells cease to be charged. As seen in Fig. 3a, 
Li–S cells undergo infinite cycling after the 220th cycle indicating that 
the polysulfide shuttle mechanism becomes predominant in the presence 
of lithium boron polysulfides. 
However, it should be noted that long-chain lithium boron poly­
sulfides have a good dissolving ability towards lithium sulfide; there­
fore, LSCs with electrolytes based on LiBF4 are capable of long-term 
cycling when their charge is limited (Fig. 3d). 
Since BF4
−interacts with lithium polysulfides formed in electrolyte 
solutions when cells are charging and discharging, the dependence of 
the capacity on the number of cycles when cycling in the range of 
1.5–2.8 V differ more strongly from the dependence obtained by the 
summation of the capacities when cells are cycling in limited voltage 
ranges (green vs orange curves in Fig. 3d). This observed significant 
difference is additional evidence for the interaction of lithium poly­
sulfides with BF4
−.

saturated with lithium polysulfides, by UV–Vis spectroscopy 
Further evidence of the interaction between the BF4

In the case of sulfolane solutions of LiClO4 and LiPF6, the absorption

with the BF4

3.2.5. Investigation of the structure of sulfolane solutions of LiBF4, 
saturated with lithium polysulfides, by electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy 
To further prove the interaction of long-chain lithium polysulfides 
with the BF4
−anion during LSСs cycling, lithium-sulfur cells with LiBF4 
and LiClO4 in sulfolane solutions as electrolytes were characterized by 
the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy during charging and dis­
charging. The results obtained are summarized in Figs. 3S and 4S 
(Supplementary Materials). The differences in the impedance of the 
LSСs with electrolytes based on LiBF4 and LiClO4 manifest themselves 
most clearly in the high-voltage plateaus of the discharge and charge 
voltage profiles. Both during the discharge and charge of the LSCs, the 
impedance of electrodes in cells with the electrolyte based on LiBF4 is 
significantly greater than in cells with electrolytes based on LiClO4. In 
addition, at a high state of charge of LSCs with electrolytes based on 
LiBF4, the impedance practically ceases to change (Fig. 4S). The 
observed differences in the impedance of LSCs with LiBF4 and LiClO4 
solutions as electrolytes indicate the interaction of the BF4
−anion with 
long-chain lithium polysulfides formed on high-voltage plateaus during 
the discharge and charge of lithium-sulfur cells. As a result of these in­
teractions, LiF is formed - a compound that is insoluble in the electrolyte 
and precipitates on the carbon particles in the positive electrode. This 
leads to the passivation of their surface and, consequently, to an increase 
in the impedance. In addition, as a result of the interaction of the BF4
−
anion with long-chain lithium polysulfides, new compounds are formed 
- lithium boron polysulfides (BF(4-x) (SnLi)x) with a higher solubility with 
respect to lithium sulfide, which leads to the depassivation of the lithium 
electrode, and thereby increasing the rate of interaction of the lithium 
electrode with lithium polysulfides. The absence of any change in the 
impedance during charging at the high-voltage plateau may be 
explained by the aforementioned discussion (Fig. 4S). 
4. Conclusions 
Herein, we investigated the impact of sulfolane based electrolytes 
with three different salts (LiPF6, LiBF4, and LiClO4) on the electro­
chemical performance of lithium-sulfur batteries. The studies performed 
have shown that the electrochemical behavior of lithium-sulfur cells 
containing LiClO4 or LiPF6 in sulfolane electrolytes were similar. This 
fact indicates that the hexafluorophosphate anion does not react with 
the solutions of lithium polysulfides of various sizes. On the contrary, 
the patterns of cycling of LSCs containing 1 M LiBF4 in sulfolane as an 
electrolyte differed significantly from those of LSCs with electrolytes 
based on LiClO4 or LiPF6. Detailed studies have shown that the tetra­
fluoroborate anion reacts with lithium polysulfides to form lithium 
boron polysulfides. The electrochemical properties of the obtained so­
lutions of lithium polysulfides suggested that lithium boron polysulfides 
have electrochemical activity. However, LSCs with solutions of lithium 
boron polysulfides do not go into charge, which may be due to the high 
chemical activity of long-chain lithium boron polysulfides towards 
metallic lithium.
