A high performance lithium–sulfur battery enabled
by a ﬁsh-scale porous carbon/sulfur composite and
symmetric ﬂuorinated diethoxyethane electrolyte†
Mengyao Gao,ad ChiCheung Su,a Meinan He,a Tobias Glossmann,b
Andreas Hintennach,c Zhenxing Feng,e Yaqin Huangd and Zhengcheng Zhang*a
A high performance lithium–sulfur (Li–S) battery comprising a symmetric ﬂuorinated diethoxyethane
electrolyte coupled with a ﬁsh-scale porous carbon/S composite electrode was demonstrated. 1,2-
Bis(1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethoxy)ethane (TFEE) was ﬁrst studied as a new electrolyte solvent for Li–S chemistry.
When co-mixed with 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), the DOL/TFEE electrolyte suppressed the polysulﬁde dissolution
and shuttling reaction. When coupled with a ﬁsh-scale porous carbon/S composite electrode, the Li–S cell
exhibited a signiﬁcantly high capacity retention of 99.5% per cycle for 100 cycles, which is far superior to
the reported numerous systems.
1.
Introduction
Nascent lithium-ion batteries have dominated the battery
market for portable electronics since their launch in the early
1990s. They are believed to be promising candidates for electric
vehicles and electrical grid applications.
However, these
batteries cannot oﬀer a suitably long driving range (i.e., >300
km) for pure electric vehicles due to their limited energy density
of about 200–250 W h kg1 at present.1–3 Lithium–sulfur
batteries (LiSBs) are a promising next-generation battery
generation technology, and they have potential to meet the
performance requirements of high-energy-density batteries in
emerging electronics and vehicle applications.4–9 Sulfur is
a naturally abundant and nontoxic element, and one of the
cheapest energy storage materials with an extremely high
specic capacity of 1675 mA h g1.10–12 During the discharge–
charge, sulfur is electrochemically reduced to lithium poly-
sulde (LiPS) intermediates through a multistep process, where
the longer chain LiPS tends to dissolve in the electrolyte causing
a
severe
redox
shuttling
reaction
and
poor
coulombic
eﬃciency.13–17 Insoluble discharge products such as Li2S2 and
Li2S are generated in the nal step. During the charging step,
Li2S/Li2S2 is converted to elemental sulfur through multiple
oxidation steps. Inspired by nature, Huang's group23,24 fabri-
cated
hierarchical
sh-scale
porous
carbon
(FSPC)
as
a substrate for sulfur cathodes. Although the performance of
LiSBs using the FSPC/sulfur composite electrode was improved,
the dissolution of the intermediate lithium polysuldes (LiPSs)
during cycling and the subsequent issues such as redox shut-
tling, low coulombic eﬃciency and fast capacity fade still
remain unsolved.6,7,18
A rm understanding of the operation mechanism and the
technical solution to solve the aforementioned issues are in great
demand to successfully develop commercial LiSBs.19,20 Extensive
approaches have been undertaken to overcome these problems:
(i) to introduce porous carbon materials into the cathode for
trapping LiPSs within the cathode during cycling by the strong
adsorption properties of carbon;21–27 (ii) to form a protective layer
on the anode surface to mitigate the redox reaction of the dis-
solved LiPSs and lithium metal;28,29 and (iii) to develop new solid-
state electrolytes,30,31 electrolytes consisting of ionic liquid,32–34
tetra(ethylene glycol)dimethyl ether35,36 as an organic solvent for
electrolytes, lithium salt electrolytes,37,38 and functional electro-
lyte/additives39,40 for preventing the dissolution of LiPSs into the
electrolyte and thereby avoid the redox shuttling eﬀect.
Hydrouoroethers could suppress the dissolution of lithium
polysuldes into the electrolyte, therefore mitigate the shuttling
eﬀect with improved coulombic eﬃciency and capacity reten-
tion.41–46 In our previous papers, a single uorinated ether
1,1,2,2-tetrauoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrauoropropyl
ether
(TTE)
was reported as an eﬃcient electrolyte co-solvent for LiSBs.45,46

a diether-based uorinated compound, 1,2-bis(1,1,2,2-tetrauor-

2.
Experimental
2.1
Preparation of materials
The detailed preparation of the sh scale porous carbon was
described in ref. 23 and 24. The sh scales from grass carp
(Ctenopharyn Odon Idellus) were pre-carbonized at 330 C for
2 h in a muﬄe furnace, which were then treated with KOH
aqueous solution for activation. The carbon material was ob-
tained aer being subjected to a heating protocol under N2 in
a tubular furnace: (1) ramp at 3 C min1 to 400 C and held for
30 min, and (2) increase temperature to 900 C and maintain for
1 h. Aer cooling down to room temperature, it was diluted with
HNO3 and rinsed with distilled water, then dried at 120 C for
12 h. The surface area of the as-prepared porous carbon is 2732
m2 g1, the micropore volume is 0.706 cm3 g1 and the total
volume is 1.69 cm3 g1.
2.2
Sulfur cathode fabrication
Sulfur (99.8%, analytical grade, Sigma-Aldrich),
sh-scale
porous carbon, carbon black and PVDF binder (5 wt% in NMP
solution) were mixed with weight ratios of 42%, 28%, 20% and
10%, respectively.
The slurry was then coated onto an
aluminum foil current collector. N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)
was used to dissolve the PVDF binder and adjust the slurry
viscosity. The coated electrodes were dried at 60 C under
vacuum for 12 h and punched into discs with an area of 1.27
cm2 with a sulfur loading of 2 mg cm2. The detailed prepara-
tion and properties of the sulfur composite electrode have been
reported in previous papers.23,24
2.3
Preparation of the electrolyte
1,2-Bis(1,1,2,2-tetrauoroethoxy)ethane (TFEE)
was
purchased
from Synquest Labs, which was subjected to vacuum distillation
for purication. Its purity was determined by GC-MS and its
structure was analyzed by NMR spectroscopy. 1,2-Dimethoxy-
ethane (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), lithium bis(triuorome-
thanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and sublimed sulfur were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich. All solvents were puried by regular distilla-
tion before use. DOL/DME and DOL/TFEE solvent mixture and
electrolyte preparation were performed in an Ar-lled glovebox
with a controlled moisture content <1 ppm. The electrolytes
studied were (1) a baseline electrolyte of 1.0 M LiTFSI/DOL/DME
(5/5 by volume) and (2) 1.0 M LiTFSI in DOL/TFEE with various
volume ratios (8/2, 7/3, 5/5, 3/7 and 2/8). The NMR spectra were
acquired on a 300 MHz Bruker spectrometer. The 1H chemical
shis were referenced to chloroform-d at 7.27 ppm and 19F
chemical shis were referenced to CFCl3. The NMR spectra are
shown in Fig. S1 in the ESI.†
2.4
Evaluation of electrochemical properties
2032 coin cells were assembled with lithium as the anode, the
above sulfur electrode as the cathode, Celgard 2325 as a sepa-
rator and 30 mL electrolyte. The cells were assembled in an Ar-
lled glovebox and cycled at a rate of C/10 (1C ¼ 1675 mA
g1) on a Maccor series 4000 cycler in a voltage range of 1.6–
2.8 V. The charging process was terminated by a cut-oﬀvoltage
of 2.8 V for all the DOL/TFEE electrolyte cells.
2.5
Examination of electrode morphology
For morphological analysis of electrodes, the samples were
loaded onto an air-tight scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
sample holder. The high resolution scanning electron micro-
scope (JEOL JSM6610) was operated at 5–10 kV for imaging and
10–20 keV for energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data.
3.
Results and discussion
3.1
Electrode material and uorinated electrolyte
A hierarchical porous carbon obtained from sh scales23,24 has
been employed to design and prepare a novel porous nano-
composite of sulfur/carbon. In this structure, the embedded
sulfur is located in micropores in a highly dispersed state
through capillary action. The high specic surface area of the
micropores signicantly improves its reactivity and reduces the
loss of active materials. In addition, the void in the nano-
composite can accommodate the volume expansion of sulfur
generated during the electrochemical reaction, reduce lithium
polysulde dissolution, and tremendously alleviate the shuttle
eﬀect. Symmetric uorinated diethoxyethane was used as a co-
solvent for the electrolytes. Five uorinated electrolytes were
prepared in the argon-lled glovebox with 1.0 M LiTFSI dis-
solved in various volume ratios (8/2, 7/3, 5/5, 3/7, and 2/8) of the
DOL and TFEE solvent mixture . The eﬀect of TFEE addition on
the electrolyte conductivity was studied. Fig. 1 shows the room

the DOL/DME baseline electrolyte, the conductivity of the
uorinated electrolyte DOL/TFEE is one magnitude lower and

Although the lithium salt concentration in the ve DOL/TFEE
mixtures is the same, the chelating geometry and the hydrody-
namic radius of the solvated clusters are altered with the ratio of
DOL to TFEE, which clearly correlates with its conductivity. For
the high TFEE concentration electrolyte DOL/TFEE (2/8), the
conductivity is only 0.1 mS cm1, which is ten times lower than
that of the DOL/TFEE (5/5) electrolyte. Therefore, the optimal
concentration of TFEE is 30–50% in the mixture to aﬀord the
fast electron transfer during battery cycling realized by the Li+
shuttling within the electrolyte media.
3.2
Enhanced electrochemical performance
Fig. 2 illustrates the charge–discharge voltage proles of LiSBs
employing uorinated electrolytes 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/TFEE (8/
2), DOL/TFEE (7/3), DOL/TFEE (5/5), DOL/TFEE (3/7), DOL/
TFEE (2/8) and baseline electrolyte 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (5/
5) at diﬀerent cycling stages. Two discharge plateaus were
observed in the 1st, 5th, 10th and 50th cycles: the higher voltage
plateau at 2.2–2.4 V is due to the reduction of elemental sulfur
to LiPS (Li2Sx, where 4 # x # 8), and the subsequent reduction
to low-ordered species (Li2S2 and/or Li2S) at the lower voltage
plateau at 2.1–2.0 V. It is surprising that the baseline electrolyte
cell showed a huge voltage polarization in the 1st charge–
discharge prole and this over-potential became larger with
extended cycling as shown in Fig. 2c and d despite the highest
conductivity of the electrolyte. This indicates that the uori-
nated electrolyte has improved wettability when in contact with
the FSPC/S composite cathode and electrochemical/chemical
stability to aﬀord repeated cycling operation. While the rst
plateau of the LiSB with the DOL/TFEE (2/8) electrolyte almost
disappeared which might be caused by its low ionic conductivity
(Fig. 1), a signicant diﬀerence in coulombic eﬃciency (CE) was
observed between the DOL/TFEE electrolyte cells and the
baseline cell.
Fig. 3 presents the capacity retention proles and CE as
functions of cycle numbers of LiSBs. A uorinated electrolyte
with 20–70% TFEE outperformed the baseline cell, showing the
highest capacity retention at 50%. The 80% TFEE electrolyte
failed to support the normal charge–discharge operation of the
cell due to its extremely low ionic conductivity. The initial
specic discharge capacities with 20% TFEE are 1453 mA h g1
and 723 mA h g1 aer 100 cycles at a rate of C/10 (1C ¼ 1675
mA h g1) with the CE of around 85%. Aer diﬀerent amounts
of TFEE were added to the electrolyte, both capacity and CE are
improved. Interestingly, the LiSB with 50% TFEE electrolyte
exhibited excellent electrochemical performance in terms of

Fig. 4b shows the CV of the as-prepared DOL/TFEE (5/5)
electrolyte cell with scan rates from 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, to
1.0 mV s1. The LiPS exhibits two cathodic current peaks and
one anodic current peak at the various scan rates. The potential
of the Ipc1 exactly agrees with that observed from the reduction
of elemental sulfur. In the following cathodic scanning, the
high-order LiPS disproportionation into low-order LiPS and
elemental sulfur results in the appearance of a well distin-
guishable Ipc1. The logarithm values of the peak currents plotted
as a function of the potential scan rate are included in the inset
of Fig. 4b. It is shown that all three peak currents do not follow
a straight linear “log I–V” relationship. This result demon-
strated that the redox reactions of elemental sulfur and LiPSs
are not diﬀusion-controlled; instead it is a multi-stage redox
process. Multi-scan cyclic voltammetry was also performed on
the LiSB with various DOL/TFEE electrolytes at a xed scan rate
of 0.1 mV s1. The results are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESI.†
Fig. S2a† is the cyclic voltammogram of 1.0 M LiTFSI in the DOL/
TFEE (8/2) electrolyte cell. Two cathodic peaks appear at 1.85 and
2.20 V and two anodic peaks at 2.50 and 2.63 V. The anodic peak
at 2.63 V and the cathodic peak at 2.20 V correspond to the
transformation between S8 and L2Sx (4 # x # 8), while the
cathodic peak at 2.50 V and the anodic peak at 1.85 V to the
transformation between soluble polysuldes and insoluble Li2S2
or Li2S. With the TFEE content increased to 50%, only one anodic
peak at 2.62 V was observed during the oxidation scan (Fig. S2c†).
This peak overlaps for all ve scans indicating good reversibility
in the electrochemical reduction/oxidation reaction. Further
increase of the TFEE content to 80% (Fig. S2e†) resulted in one
cathodic peak loss and the potential polarization increased.
However, for the baseline 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (5/5) electrolyte
cell, a broad anodic reaction peak and two broad cathodic peaks
were observed in all ve scans, as shown in Fig. S2f.† The broad
peaks suggest the sluggish kinetics of the reduction and oxida-
tion reaction of the baseline cell, leading to the dissolution of
lithium polysuldes and polarization of potential. This is in good
agreement with the results shown in Fig. 2.
The rate performance of the FSPC employing 1.0 M LiTFSI
DOL/TFEE (5/5) is comparable to that of the state-of-the-art
electrolyte. As shown in Fig. 4c, at the rates of 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.5C

3.4
Inhibition of LiPS dissolution
The above electrochemical analysis conrms that the uorinated
electrolyte suppressed the lithium polysulde dissolution thus
preventing the deleterious redox shuttling eﬀect of the Li–S cell.
This eﬀect is also reected by the quite diﬀerent electrode
morphology. Fig. 6 and 7 present the SEM images of the sulfur
electrode discharged in 1.0 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (5/5) and 1.0 M
LiTFSI DOL/TFEE (5/5) electrolytes at diﬀerent DOD stages. For the
DOL/DME 1.0 M LiTFSI cycled cell, the morphology changes with
the DOD and signicant deposition of the discharged products on
the surface of the electrode was observed at the end of the
discharge as shown in Fig. 6f. In contrast, the sulfur electrode
discharged in the DOL/TFEE electrolyte did not show any big
diﬀerence as the discharge proceeded from Fig. 7b–d and only
trivial deposits showed up at the very end of the discharge as
shown in Fig. 7. Fluorinated ether electrolytes enabled the redox
reaction within the porous structure and promoted the repeated
conversion of the sulfur chemistry aﬀording a long term cyclability
of the Li–S cell. From this study, it is also manifested that the new
design of the sulfur electrode could not be suﬃcient enough for
a stable Li–S battery, nevertheless the combination of an archi-
tected electrode coupled with a uorinated electrolyte provides
a solution to the stable Li–S battery chemistry. Fig. S3 in the ESI†
illustrates pictures of the harvested electrolytes diluted with the
DOL solvent. Clearly, the colorless solution of the harvested DOL/
TFEE electrolyte was observed at each DOD stage compared with
the DOL/DME electrolyte, indicating that LiPSs were signicantly
suppressed. Electrode morphology evolution with cycling is shown
in Fig. S4 and S5 in the ESI.†
4.
Conclusions
A high performance LiSB with FSPC coupled with a DOL/TFEE
based electrolyte was developed and thoroughly examined by
electrochemistry and morphology methods. Due to the weak
solvation with Li+ either from the dissolved lithium salt or the
discharged LiPS, the new uorinated electrolyte has low chelating
capacity with additional Li+ resulting from all the discharged
products of Li2Sx (2 # x # 8). This eﬀect intrinsically prevents the
LiPS dissolution and suppresses the subsequent shuttling eﬀect
and loss of active sulfur materials during the repeated charge–
discharge process. Furthermore, when coupled with FSPC, the
LiSB showed fabulous cycling performance oﬀering an ultimate
solution for mitigating the fast capacity fading of the traditional
LiSB chemistry. Electrolytes in batteries must cater to the needs
of both electrodes; hence, in principle, new battery chemistries
would have incurred new electrolyte compositions. Future
studies will focus on further tuning the structure–property rela-
tionship of the uorinated ether and the architecture of the
electrode to nd the nal solution for an ideal LiSB.
