Li–S batteries are promising alternatives due to their proven increased gravimetric capacity compared to
Li–ion batteries. However, their development is hindered by many technical issues, one of the most challen-
ging being the dissolution and shuttle of polysulfide species, which causes irreversible loss of cathode mate-
rial leading to rapid capacity fading. Among the possible strategies to mitigate this effect, the choice of
suitable solvents is easy to implement and has large room for improvement. To guide this quest,
computationally-aided optimization is a powerful tool, provided that suitable descriptors are used to screen
possible solvents. In this work, molecular dynamics simulations were performed for a typical lithium
polysulfide Li2S6 dissolved in different solvents. Diffusion coefficients and their related activation energies
were calculated, and thermodynamic properties like solvation energies and entropies were also evaluated.
Additionally, a theoretical framework for computing the relative solubilities of lithium polysulfide is provided.
For the set of solvents considered, we found that the system’s viscosity appears as an important descriptor
to correlate with different system properties. The donor number of the solvent also appears as a valid
descriptor, for low-viscosity solvents. In general, it was found that higher viscosity solvents lead to lower
diffusion rates and higher polysulfide solubility. These results suggest that the optimal choice to reduce the
shuttle is a trade-off between high-viscosity solvents to reduce polysulfide diffusion and low-viscosity
solvents to reduce its solubility, which could be further improved by properly tuning the donor number.
Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have gained significant atten-
tion and importance in recent times for multiple reasons. First,
because of their potential high energy density, Li–S batteries
can offer a much higher energy density than conventional
lithium–ion batteries.1–4 This higher energy density could lead
to longer-lasting and higher-performing batteries, making them
desirable for various applications. Second, sulfur is abundant and
inexpensive, which makes it a cost-effective material for battery
production.5,6 Therefore, Li–S batteries are likely to reduce the
overall cost of energy storage technologies. Third, Li–S batteries
are more environmentally friendly7 compared to traditional
lithium–ion batteries.8 The use of sulfur as a cathode material
instead of heavy metal oxides reduces the reliance on toxic and
rare elements like cobalt, used in common lithium–ion batteries.9
Additionally, Li–S are safer than lithium–ion batteries presenting
a lower risk of thermal runaway because sulfur is under battery
operation conditions a non-flammable material, provided that a
suitable electrolyte is used.10 This made them desirable for
various applications, including electric vehicles. Thus, Li–S bat-
teries could revolutionize the electric vehicle industry by enabling
longer driving ranges and reducing the overall weight of the
vehicle as they could also have applications in renewable energy
storage, portable electronics, aerospace, and other fields where
high energy density and cost-effectiveness are crucial.

However, Li–S batteries are still under active development,
and several technical hurdles need to be addressed before they
can be widely commercialized. These challenges include improv-
ing cycle life, addressing issues related to sulfur volume expansion
during charge/discharge cycles, and mitigating the ‘‘shuttle
effect’’.11,12 The latter describes the migration of dissolved lithium
polysulfide (PS) species from the sulfur-rich cathode to the
lithium–metal anode during battery operation. This phenomenon
may have negative effects on the battery performance and overall
cycling stability. In fact, during the discharge of a Li–S battery,
sulfur in the cathode undergoes reduction converting to soluble
intermediate lithium polysulfide compounds (such as Li2Sx,
where x 4 2). The lithium polysulfide species diffuse through
the separator and reach the lithium–metal anode.13–15 Then they
react with lithium, leading to the formation of insulating and
irreversible lithium sulfide (Li2S) and other byproducts.16 Thus,
knowledge of the diffusion of PS species appears to be of primary
importance in controlling this unwanted phenomenon.
The choice of the solvent used in the electrolyte holds
substantial influence over the diﬀusion and migration of PS
compounds in Li–S batteries.17 This is because the solvent plays
a crucial role in solubilizing the PS compounds, influencing
their mobility and concentration gradients within the battery
system. Different solvents affect the solubility of PS and their
interactions with the electrode materials, ultimately affecting
the shuttle effect and battery performance. Beyond solubility,
additional critical factors affecting PS diffusion include solution
viscosity, as well as the solubility, retention, and stability of PS in
diverse solvents.18 Consequently, the selection of an appropriate
solvent, or a combination of thereof, emerges as a critical considera-
tion in Li–S battery development, especially under lean electrolyte
conditions. Several solvents have been explored, each with distinct
advantages and disadvantages.19–21 Some commonly studied sol-
vents are organic carbonates, known for their compatibility with
lithium salts and the ability to offer high ionic conductivity. How-
ever, these solvents exhibit relatively high PS solubility, exacerbating
the shuttle effect.22 Furthermore, X-ray absorption spectroscopy
(XAS) experiments revealed that reduced sulfur species chemically
react with carbonate-based solvents,23 unless sulfur is confined in a
nanoporous material.24,25 Ionic liquids, which are salts in liquid
form at room temperature, have been employed as an alternative.26
They exhibit promise in mitigating the shuttle effect due to their low
volatility and high polarity. Nonetheless, their high viscosity can
hinder ion transport and decrease the overall conductivity of the
electrolyte.27 Finally, ethers such as tetraethylene glycol dimethyl
ether (TEGDME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), and dimethoxyethane (DME)
find common use due to they generally lower PS solubility com-
pared to organic carbonates.
In theoretical research, a large amount of eﬀort has also
been devoted to the understanding of the phenomena occur-
ring in the sulfur cathode. A recent comprehensive review28 has
addressed the application of theoretical modeling to lithium–
sulfur battery cathodes, using density functional theory (DFT)
as the predominant methodology. Moreover, PS adsorption, Li+
surface diffusion, Li2S decomposition, and the sulfur reduction
reaction on different catalysts were analyzed,29 proposing
design strategies to deal with the surface reactions. Concerning
the behavior of PSs in solution, some simplifications have been
introduced in the modeling due to the large number of atoms
involved in the simulations. This has led to the use of classical
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations30–37 using the so-called
force fields. In particular, Andersen et al. have analyzed the
molecular clustering of PSs and the dynamic processes asso-
ciated with them.30 Their approach involved a combination of
MD simulations with DFT-optimized clusters, as well as NMR,
and XAS experiments. These studies have shown an increasing
Li+ diffusion in the sequence D(Li2S4) o D(Li2S6) o D(Li2S8)
and proposed that Li+ is probably shuttled by heavier sulfide
chains and clusters of sulfide chains. Babar and Lekakou31
have determined the solvation parameters of electrolyte ions
and PSs in LiTFSI DOL:DME and LiPF6 EC:DMC solutions.
These parameters were the PS dimensions, the coordination
number of the ions with the solvent, and the desolvation energy
of the electrolyte ions. Hu et al.32 explored the transport
behavior of Li2S8 species in ionic liquid-based electrolyte sys-
tems, proving an atomistic view of the effects of the coordina-
tion of anions on the transport behavior of Li2S8. Osella et al.34
researched the transport of Li2S4 in pure DOL and TEGDME
solutions, and in the presence of LiTFSI, observing the for-
mation of larger lithium PSs (LiPS) aggregated clusters in DOL
compared to TEGDME. Park et al.35,36 have parameterized a
classic non-polarizable force field for PSs of different chain
lengths in electrolyte solutions of LiTFSI in DME:DOL mixtures.
They analyzed the tendency of clustering with the concen-
tration of PS and chain length, as well as the effect of the
addition of LiTFSI. Rajput et al.37 focused on understanding the
properties of individual species Li2Sx; x = 2 to 8, in LiTFSI
DOL:DME electrolytes. They gained knowledge on the inter-
action between the LiPS intermediates and solvent and electro-
lyte, the salt addition effect on the solvation of dissolved
species, and the effect of PS length on the structure and
dynamics of the system. Fist-principles MD simulations have
also shown to be useful in the evaluation of properties of PS
solutions. Pascal et al.38 have used this approach to evaluate the
solubility of lithium PSs in dimethylformamide (DMF) and
diglyme and to study the XAS spectra of PS of various chain-
lengths.39 Finally, it is also worth mentioning the very recent
work of Atwi and Rajput,40 who have introduced a computa-
tional database for Li–S Batteries, involving a couple of
thousands of quantum-chemical and molecular dynamics
properties for Li–S electrolytes. This database provides an
important guiding map for the optimization of Li–S electrolyte
compositions. Despite this important step forward, the authors
have recognized that it is very difficult to find a single descrip-
tor that explains the trends in PS solubility. The present work is
partly inspired by this comprehensive approach, and we think
that the present ideas may be complementary to some of the
ones given in ref. 40 to improve this understanding.

To date, there is no systematic study in the literature on
the diﬀusion and solubility of lithium polysulfide species in
various solvents commonly used in Li–S batteries. Therefore, in
this work, diffusion coefficients and related activation energies
as well as solvation free energies and entropies were calculated
as a function of some solvent features by classical molecular
dynamics. The work aims to investigate the correlations
between transport and thermodynamics constants and proper-
ties of the target solvents. Therefore, we study diluted concen-
trations of lithium polysulfides in different solvents in order to
minimize ion–ion interactions and thus highlight the role of
solvent on its solvation. Finally, the simulation results were
used to find a correlation between the PS diffusion coefficients
and the most positive anodic current peak of the voltammetric
diagram of Li–S batteries.

Results and discussion
Diﬀusion coeﬃcients
We attempt here to correlate the behavior of DLi+ and DS62 with
diﬀerent descriptors. We chose the Gutmann’s donor number
(DN),52 the viscosity (Z), and the static dielectric constant (e0).
The first choice was made because He et al.44 showed that the
stability of various PSs is mainly dictated by the solvated
cations, as the latter can be manipulated by the DN. These
authors considered as solvents DMF, TEGDME, DOL, DME,

Conclusions
In the search for solvents to optimize liquid electrolytes in Li–S
batteries, the identification of versatile descriptors that allow
the prediction of general trends on battery performance, aiming
to reduce critical challenges such as the polysulfide shuttle,
requires a reliable theoretical framework to calculate fundamental
solvent properties. To this end, we have performed MD simula-
tions for a typical polysulfide species, S6
2, in different solvents of
interest for Li–S batteries. Our attention was centered on the
calculation of diffusion coefficients, their related activation ener-
gies, and thermodynamic properties like energies and entropies
of solvation, from where a theoretical basis to predict the relative
solubilities of Li–PS was deduced. For the set of solvents under
consideration, we found that the system’s viscosity appears as an
important descriptor to correlate with different system properties.
The donor number also proves to be a significant descriptor,
excluding large-viscosity solvents, whereas the solvent dielectric
constant did not show preferential trends on self-diffusion coeffi-
cients. Some further relevant conclusions are:
 The diﬀusion coeﬃcients of Li+ show a linear correlation
with the diﬀusion coeﬃcient of the polysulfide when they are
considered for diﬀerent solvents, with a R2 value of 0.983.
 The free energy of solvation of the polysulfide in diﬀerent
solvents shows a linear relationship with the natural logarithm
of diﬀusion coeﬃcients in the same solvents, with an R2 value
of 0.796. The application of transition state theory suggests that
the activation free energy for ion diﬀusion is determined by the
rearrangement of the solvation sheet around the polysulfide.
 The logarithm of the solubility of the polysulfide increases
with the viscosity of the electrolyte, following linear functional
relationship for low viscosity values.
 The negative energetic contribution to the solvation of the
polysulfide is largely compensated by the concomitant decrease
in the entropy of the system. See Fig. 7.
 The behavior of the voltammetric current component
found in electrochemical experiments for the oxidation of
polysulfides seems to be determined by the diﬀusion of these
species. The larger voltammetric activity seems to be correlated
with the larger diﬀusivity of the polysulfide.
In general, the analysis showed that higher viscosity solvents
lead to lower diﬀusion rates and higher polysulfide solubility.
Considering the previous conclusions, we can try to formulate a
rule of thumb for the choice of an electrolyte based on its
viscosity. Concerning the activity of the cathode, if this is
controlled by the diﬀusion of polysulfides, we can state that
low-viscosity solvents should be preferred, since this will lead to
larger currents, that is, to a more reversible behavior. On the
other hand, if we look into the polysulfide shuttle problem,
where their diﬀusion to the anode handicaps the performance
of the Li–S battery, solvents with a larger viscosity should be
preferred. Thus, the practical solutions lay probably at an
intermediate viscosity, depending on the relative weight of
these two factors. These results suggest that the optimal choice
is a trade-off between high-viscosity solvents to reduce poly-
sulfide diffusion and low-viscosity solvents to reduce its solu-
bility, which could be further improved by properly tuning the
donor number.
