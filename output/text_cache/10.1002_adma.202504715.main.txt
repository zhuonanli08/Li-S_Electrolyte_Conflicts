Steric Hindrance-Induced Amorphous Lithium Sulﬁde
Deposition Accelerates Sulfur Redox Kinetics in
Lithium–Sulfur Batteries
Zhihua Wang, Junru Ke, He Zhu,* Fan Xue, Jun Jiang, Wen Huang, Min Dong,
Xindong Zhu, Jianrong Zeng, Ruoyu Song, Rafal Sliz, Qingmin Ji, Qi Liu, Yongsheng Fu,
and Si Lan*
Lithium–sulfur (Li─S) batteries are promising candidates for next-generation
energy storage due to their ultrahigh theoretical energy density. However,
their practical application is severely hindered by the sluggish conversion
kinetics, particularly during the crystalline lithium sulﬁde (Li2S) formation
stage. Herein, a steric hindrance-mediated engineering strategy is proposed
that induces an amorphous Li2S deposition process, eﬀectively boosting the
sulfur redox kinetics in Li─S batteries. By introducing benzo-15-crown-5
(B15C5) as an electrolyte additive, a strong coordination between B15C5 and
lithium ion (Li+) is established, which creates spatial conﬁnement around Li2S
and disrupts the crystallinity of Li2S during its deposition. Synchrotron pair
distribution function analysis combined with in situ X-ray diﬀraction reveals
that the deposited Li2S with B15C5 exhibits signiﬁcant local disorder with
irregular Li─S bond oscillations, conﬁrming the generation of an amorphous
phase. This strategy not only ensures a uniform Li2S layer at the
cathode/electrolyte interface but also lowers the energy barrier of sulfur
species at the molecular scale, enabling the Li─S batteries with excellent
cycling stability and overall enhanced sulfur reaction kinetics. This work
provides a novel pathway for overcoming the intrinsic limitations of sluggish
cathode conversion kinetics of Li─S batteries, paving the way for their
practical deployment in high-performance energy storage applications.

1. Introduction
Lithium–sulfur (Li─S) batteries, with the
high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh
kg−1) and abundant sulfur reserves, are re-
garded as one of the most promising next-
generation energy storage candidates.[1]
However, practical applications are hin-
dered by kinetic bottlenecks on the cath-
ode side, particularly the sluggish solid-
liquid-solid conversion of sulfur and dis-
charge products.[2] Speciﬁcally, the sulfur
conversion involves two distinct steps: 1)
solid S8 to soluble long-chain lithium poly-
sulﬁdes (LiPSs) Li2Sn (4 ≤n ≤8), and
2) liquid LiPSs to solid Li2S.[3] Crucially,
the ﬁnal crystallization of Li2S creates fun-
damental kinetic barriers—its long-range
ordered structure exhibits e−/Li+ isolation
for poor conductivity, while dense crys-
talline interfaces further hinder Li+ diﬀu-
sion pathways, resulting in sluggish reac-
tion kinetics.[4] Moreover, the slow depo-
sition of Li2S causes LiPSs to accumulate
and diﬀuse within the electrolyte, which

but also promotes the formation of ″dead sulfur″ depositing on
the Li anodes.[5] Therefore, improving Li2S conversion kinetics is
critical for improving the performance of Li─S batteries.
To address these challenges, extensive studies have focused
on regulating the deposition structure of Li2S.[6] One inspiring
approach involves using catalysts (e.g., metal compounds,[7]
alloys,[8]
heterostructures,[9]
metal
complexes,[10]
organic
molecules,[11] etc.) to reduce electrode passivation by modu-
lating the Li2S deposition into a 3D morphology.[12] This 3D
structure provides a larger active reaction area and facilitates
electrolyte penetration, eﬀectively addressing issues such as
pore channel blockage caused by the directional growth of
crystalline Li2S.[13] However, during cycling, there remains a
risk of active site deactivation due to Li2S coverage, leading to
kinetic limitations. In contrast, amorphous materials disrupt
long-range ordered structures to form disordered arrangements.
This not only enables uniform deposition to maintain Li+
transport pathways but also grants the material enhanced struc-
tural ﬂexibility to accommodate volume changes.[14] Therefore,
amorphous regulation of Li2S to improve deposition kinetics is
expected to reduce overpotential and enhance energy conversion
eﬃciency.[15] By competitively weakening the binding energy
between Li+ and S2−, the crystallization tendency of Li2S can
be suppressed.[16] Unlike complex catalyst design, the use of
high-polarity solvents in the electrolyte, which tightly solvate Li+
and alter its solvation structure, oﬀers a simple and eﬀective
strategy to induce the formation of an amorphous structure.[17]
Motivated by these considerations, here we strategically em-
ploy benzo-15-crown-5 (B15C5) as an electrolyte additive to reg-
ulate the reaction path of sulfur species. The crown ether (CE)
sites in B15C5 possess a high selective coordination ability to-
ward Li+,[18] facilitating the formation of stable Li+-CE complexes
and regulating the local chemical environment of Li+,[19] thereby
inducing a steric hindrance eﬀect in the Li2S deposition process.
This eﬀect eﬀectively disrupts the periodic crystal structure of
Li2S and transforms it into a disordered amorphous structure.
Beneﬁtting from the high aﬃnity of the CE sites in B15C5 for
Li+, the periodic crystalline structure of Li2S is disrupted and con-
verted into a disordered amorphous structure. This amorphous
strategy not only enables uniform Li2S deposition at the cath-
ode/electrolyte interface but also reduces the molecular energy
barrier for sulfur species. Consequently, both the nucleation and
growth rates of Li2S are improved, allowing the battery to stably
perform 1000 cycles at 2C with an average capacity decay of only
0.027% per cycle. This work oﬀers both fundamental insights
into Li─S cathode conversion mechanisms and a promising path-
way for the development of high-performance Li─S batteries for
energy storage systems.
2. Results and Discussion
The B15C5 electrolyte additive employed in this work comprises
a CE ring and a benzene (inset, Figure 1a). This structure was
conﬁrmed by attenuated total reﬂection-Fourier transform in-
frared (ATR-FTIR) spectrum (Figure 1a), which displays charac-
teristic bands for both the benzene unit (e.g., C─H stretching and
bending vibrations) and the CE ring (notably, the C─O─C stretch-
ing modes, see more details in Note S1, Supporting Information).
In addition, 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra fur-
ther veriﬁed the molecular conﬁguration of B15C5 (see Figure
S1, Supporting Information).

spray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) analysis revealed

Furthermore, 19F NMR (Figure S2, Supporting Information) re-

To explore the feasibility of electrolyte additives, Raman spec-
troscopy was employed to characterize the electrolyte before and
after the addition of B15C5 (Figure S3, Supporting Information).
The pristine electrolyte used in this work consists of 1 m lithium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonic)imide (LiTFSI) and 1% LiNO3 dis-
solved in 1,3-dioxane (DOL)/ diamethoxyethane (DME) solvent
with a volume fraction of 1:1. The peaks within the Raman range
of 350–650 cm−1 correspond to various DME conformations
(TGT, TGG, TTT, TGG’ and TTG, respectively) (Figure 1c).[22]
Among them, the unstable TGG and TTG’—comprising a ma-
jority of DME conformers—are notably reduced after the addi-
tion of B15C5, while the proportion of the most stable TGT in-
creases, supporting the enhanced stability of the electrolyte. The
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test results demonstrated that
the B15C5 additive demonstrates favorable electrochemical sta-
bility within the operational voltage range (1.7–2.8 V) of Li─S bat-
teries (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Moreover, B15C5 can
eﬀectively inhibit Li dendrite growth, contributing to a more sta-
ble Li anode (Figure S5, Supporting Information). These results
demonstrate that incorporating B15C5 generates a more stable
electrochemical system, laying a solid foundation for regulating
the deposition behavior of Li2S.
To probe the interaction between B15C5 and LiPSs, 7Li NMR
measurements were conducted on the DOL/DME solution con-
taining Li2S4 with the addition of B15C5 (Figure 1d). Compar-
ing the 7Li NMR at diﬀerent B15C5-to-Li2S4 ratios reveal that
the Li+ peak shifts from −0.36 ppm (pure Li2S4) to −0.79 ppm
(Li2S4:B15C5 = 1:1), and then to −0.91 ppm (Li2S4:B15C5 = 1:2).
These shifts indicate an increased electron density around Li+
with increasing CE coordination,[23] suggesting that the modi-
ﬁed solvation environment enables more polysulﬁde anions par-
ticipating in the solvation sheath of Li+, thereby forming a new
coordination structure of LiPSs. Furthermore, similar coordina-
tion behavior between B15C5 and Li2S was observed in ethanol
(Figure 1e, the illustrations present molecular conﬁgurations de-
rived from theoretical calculations, as detailed in Figure S20, Sup-
porting Information), which can aﬀect the deposition structure of
Li2S during the reduction process.
The structural inﬂuence of B15C5 on deposited Li2S was in-
vestigated through synchrotron pair distribution function (PDF)
analysis and HRTEM characterization. Figure 2a shows that
both Li2S and Li2S+B15C5 maintain comparable Li─S bond
lengths (≈2.4–2.5 Å) in the ﬁrst coordination shell, conﬁrm-
ing the preservation of short-range coordination structure. How-
ever, distinct diﬀerences emerge in long-range ordering. Pris-
tine Li2S exhibits sharp correlation peaks extending beyond 25 Å,
characteristic of its well-ordered antiﬂuorite crystalline structure
(space group Fm-3m (225)). In striking contrast, the Li2S+B15C5
system displays rapid peak attenuation and disordered oscil-
lations in PDF patterns, demonstrating dominant amorphous
characteristics.[24] High-resolution transmission electron mi-
croscopy (HRTEM) reveals this material primarily consists of
amorphous domains, with trace nanocrystalline remnants per-
sisting within the disordered matrix (Figure S6, Supporting In-
formation). The degree of amorphization needs to be further
quantiﬁed.[25] The reverse Monte Carlo (RMC) method was ap-
plied to ﬁt PDF data (Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information) and
obtain a supercell structural model (Figure S7c–e, Supporting
Information). The standard deviations of atoms along three di-
rections were statistically analyzed (Figure S7f–i, Supporting In-
formation), and the extent of atomic deviation from equilibrium
positions was calculated by taking the square root of the sum
of squares (Table S1, Supporting Information). In Li2S+B15C5,
Li and S atomic displacements deviated from their lattice posi-
tions by 14.0% and 17.2% (Table S1, Supporting Information),
respectively, quantifying the amorphization degree. Comprehen-
sive analysis indicates that the B15C5-induced transition pro-
duces a predominantly amorphous material with minor crys-
talline remnants.
In situ X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) characterization was employed
to monitor the evolution of sulfur species during the electro-
chemical reaction (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The
sharp peak at 23° can be attributed to S8, while the broad peak
at 27° corresponds to the reduction product Li2S.[26] As the dis-
charge process proceeded, the S8 signal completely disappeared,
marking the onset of Li2S nucleation (Figure 2b,c). Previous stud-
ies have shown that higher battery capacity corresponds to greater
Li2S deposition and stronger Li2S signals.[27] However, in the
presence of B15C5, although the battery achieved higher capac-
ity, the intensity of the Li2S signal in the contour map was signif-
icantly weaker than that in the battery without B15C5. The corre-
sponding XRD patterns revealed a retarded emergence of Li2S
crystalline peaks in battery with B15C5. Quantitative tracking
of sulfur speciation was achieved by normalizing the diﬀraction
peak intensity of S8 at initial discharge to 100% (Figure 3d).[28]
Both batteries exhibited comparable S8 consumption kinetics in
early discharge stages (DoD ≤23%), achieving over 80% sul-
fur conversion. Notably, the control battery showed abrupt nu-
cleation of Li2S crystallites at DoD = 36%, as evidenced by the
Li2S (111) diﬀraction intensity exceeding the detection thresh-
old, followed by accelerated growth dynamics of rapid Li2S crys-
tallization. In contrast, the B15C5-modiﬁed system delayed the
Li2S nucleation threshold to DoD = 58%, with a slower intensity
progression rate and ﬁnal accumulation reaching merely 21% of
the initial S8 content. These results collectively demonstrate that
Li2S initially nucleates in an amorphous conﬁguration, followed
by a cooperative crystallization-amorphization growth pathway
involving complex phase coupling during the subsequent depo-
sition process. The isotropic amorphous structure is not subject
to the periodic interference of crystal structure, which is essential
to the improvement of deposition structure.[29]

Li─S batteries with varying concentrations of B15C5 as elec-
trolyte additive were evaluated to assess the beneﬁcial impact
of amorphization strategy. Considering the speciﬁc capacity of
rate performances (Figure 3a) and Li2S deposition capacity of
chronoamperometry measurements (Figure S10, Supporting In-
formation), the optimal B15C5 concentration was found to be
50 mm. The progressive incorporation of B15C5 induces a con-
comitant rise in electrolyte viscosity, which leads to compromised
ionic conductivity (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Exces-
sive B15C5 (>50 mm) ultimately aﬀects battery performance due
to transport limitations caused by high viscosity.
After 1000 cycles at 2C, a signiﬁcant diﬀerence in average dis-
charge capacity decay per cycle was observed between the bat-
teries with and without B15C5 (0.027% vs 0.055%) (Figure 3b).
The battery with B15C5 exhibited a remarkably low median volt-
age decay of 9 mV, which can be attributed to the advanta-
geous amorphous Li2S deposition mechanism in suppressing
electrochemical polarization (Figure S12, Supporting Informa-
tion). The B15C5 successfully enhanced the Li─S battery perfor-
mance, demonstrating the high feasibility of the amorphization
strategy.

The deposition of Li2S involves nucleation and growth stages.
The potentiostatic deposition results of the optimal concentra-
tion sample and the blank sample were analyzed by construct-
ing I/Im-T/Tm curves (Im: maximum current, Tm: speciﬁc time
when the current reaches maximum) and comparing them with
four classical electrodeposition models (Figure S10, Supporting
Information). The results demonstrated that both systems pre-
dominantly conformed to the 2D instantaneous (2DI) nucleation
mechanism. In this mechanism, the subsequent growth of Li2S
is governed by the surface mass transfer rate of electroactive
species. Unlike the self-limiting behavior observed in the blank
sample due to the insulating crystalline Li2S layer, the amor-
phous deposition mode eﬀectively circumvented this growth lim-
itation, corroborating the enhanced deposition capacity induced
by B15C5 in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). Avrami equa-
tion (Figure S10, Supporting Information) calculations further
revealed that the amorphous deposition mode exhibited a higher
Ak2 parameter, which directly correlated with its accelerated Li2S

the battery capacity increases more signiﬁcantly as the current

to 1143 mA h g−1 after 60 cycles (Figure 3i). However, the battery
without B15C5 not only shows a faster capacity decay but also
loses activity at the 19th cycle.

atoms. The strong coordination interaction between B15C5 and
Li2S reduces the positivity of Li sites through the 3D shielding
architecture of the macrocyclic CE, whose spatially extended con-
ﬁguration creates signiﬁcant conﬁnement eﬀects, thereby induc-
ing a pronounced steric hindrance eﬀect. This eﬀect prevents Li+
from combining with S2−in other Li2S molecules, weakening the
strong interaction Eb between Li2S molecules from −2.80 eV to a
weaker interaction (−0.71 eV) between [2B15C5·Li2S] complexes
(Figure 4d), thereby inducing amorphization. This eﬀect operates
through two critical mechanisms: First, B15C5 creates a physi-
cal barrier on the Li2S surface, hindering the ordered arrange-
ment of Li2S molecules at lattice sites. Second, the marked re-
duction in intermolecular Eb values destabilizes nucleation pro-
cesses and suppresses crystalline propagation. These dual eﬀects
synergistically disrupt the thermodynamic and kinetic pathways
for periodic Li2S crystal growth, thereby providing theoretical
validation for the experimentally observed amorphous growth
mode.
Further investigation into the molecular orbital energy levels
of Li2Sx (x = 1, 2, 4) revealed a signiﬁcant positive shift in both
the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest
occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels, indicating a notable
enhancement in electron donation and acceptance capabilities
(Figure S21, Supporting Information). For pure Li2S, the reac-
tion free energy required to break the Li─S bond and dissociate
a Li atom was 7.50 eV (Figure 4e). In contrast, the [B15C5·Li2S]
complex required only 4.88 eV to break the Li─S bond. These
results demonstrate that the amorphization strategy not only op-
timizes the electrode structure but also modulates the electronic
structure of Li2Sx, signiﬁcantly reducing the conversion energy
barrier of Li2S and thereby supporting the remarkable enhance-
ment in reaction kinetics (Figure S22, Supporting Information).
3. Conclusion
In summary, this work proposes a steric hindrance-mediated
strategy that achieves amorphous Li2S deposition through
molecular-level structural and chemical composition modula-
tion, signiﬁcantly enhancing the sulfur conversion kinetics of
Li─S batteries and enabling long-term cycling stability. By em-
ploying B15C5 as an electrolyte additive, the spatial conﬁnement
eﬀect induced by its coordination interactions with Li+ eﬀectively
disrupts the crystallization pathway of Li2S. The amorphous Li2S
phase not only forms a uniform and substrate-adaptive cathode
interface to facilitate rapid Li+ diﬀusion but also optimizes the
redox activity of sulfur species by regulating molecular energy
levels. This dual structural-electronic modulation suppresses pre-
mature cathode passivation and accelerates Li2S deposition kinet-
ics, resulting in a Li─S pouch cell with a sulfur loading of 4 mg
cm−2 that delivers an initial discharge capacity of 1087 mA h g−1
while progressively activating unreacted sulfur species. Our work
addresses the intrinsic limitations of sluggish conversion kinet-
ics in sulfur cathode systems and oﬀers a novel blueprint for de-
signing high-energy lithium–sulfur batteries.
