Electrolyte design enabling practical lithium/sulfur
batteries via interfacial manipulation and inhibited
polysulﬁde dissolution†
Yilong Lin,*a Zexian Zhang,b Junru Wu,c Jiayi Wang,a Yuqing Jia,a Xiaojin Jin,a
Feng Liu,a Sheng Huang,*b Yanwu Chena and Yuezhong Meng
*b
To address low stability at the lithium metal anode and lithium polysulﬁde shuttling is critical for high-
energy-density lithium/sulfur (Li/S) pouch cells in practical applications. However, functional electrolytes
that are compatible with Li anodes and S cathodes are required to construct more stable interfaces.
Herein, we report a highly compatible electrolyte (NBME) containing bis(2-methoxyethoxy)methane
(BME) as a single solvent with 1 M lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) and 2% LiNO3
additive. Compared to the traditional DOL/DME blend solvent, better electrochemical performance of
200 cycles in Li/Cu asymmetric cells and over 1200 h of cycling in Li/Li symmetric cells was realized
with NBME electrolyte. Improved anode/electrolyte interfacial stability results from the anion-rich
solvation sheath, thus forming an inorganic-rich SEI. In addition, the lithium polysulﬁde (LiPS) shuttle
eﬀect could be relieved in NBME electrolyte due to the low solubility of LiPS. Such synergistic eﬀects
enable the Li/S pouch cells to achieve 70% capacity retention after 60 cycles.

Introduction
Traditional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have encountered
a bottleneck of energy density. Emerging lithium–sulfur (Li/S)
batteries demonstrate potential to achieve higher energy
density
in
the
next
generation
of
rechargeable
battery
systems.1–3
This
is
because
both
the
sulfur
cathode
(1675 mA h g−1) and the metallic lithium anode (3860 mA h g−1)
have relatively high theoretical specic capacity.4 In addition, by
combining advantages such as lightweight, environmental
friendliness, and low economic cost, Li/S batteries have become
one of the most promising battery systems in the past decade
and have been under continuous development.5
However, Li/S batteries inevitably face the challenge of
“shuttling” of lithium polysuldes (LiPSs) and Li dendrites.6–8
Although the dissolution process of LiPSs is crucial for the high
specic energy release of sulfur/carbon composite cathodes in
Li/S batteries, the dissolved PSs react with the lithium metal
anode, leading to rapid depletion of limited lithium due to side
reactions, which has become the bottleneck for practical long-
life Li/S batteries.9,10 To address the above issues, research
strategies include: (i) electrolyte design to suppress Li2Sn shut-
tling and improve sulfur cathode utilization; (ii) membrane
modication or introduction of interlayers between the sulfur
cathode (or lithium anode) and the separator to block Li2Sn
shuttling; (iii) lithium anode modication to inhibit lithium
dendrite formation, reduce contact and side reactions with
Li2Sn, and improve the coulombic eﬃciency.11–14 The above
research strategies cover all components of Li/S batteries;
however, most of the problems can ultimately be traced back to
the electrolyte issue. As the medium for Li+ transport between
the electrodes, the electrolyte plays a crucial role in suppressing
the dissolution and diﬀusion of lithium polysuldes, as well as
aﬀecting the uniformity and mechanical stability of the solid
electrolyte interface (SEI).15,16 Therefore, whether Li/S batteries
can be commercialized largely depends on further electrolyte
modication.
In previous reports, ether-based electrolytes with 1,3-dioxo-
lane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as solvents have
stood out among most electrolytes and served as model elec-
trolytes in Li/S batteries.17,18 Compared to other solvents, DOL
and DME exhibit better compatibility with the Li metal anode.
Addition of LiNO3 additive in DME/DOL electrolytes signi-
cantly improves the stability of the Li metal/electrolyte interface
membrane and mitigates the shuttle eﬀect of lithium poly-
suldes.19,20 However, the SEI lm formed on the Li anode by
this ether-based electrolyte consists of oligomers and organic
ROLi components, which have insuﬃcient mechanical stability

Therefore, a new electrolyte design should improve the unifor-

Recently, a non-uorinated solvent, bis(2-methoxyethoxy)
methane (abbreviated as “BME”), has been shown to enhance
the mechanical stability of the SEI in lithium metal batteries.24
In particular, the multi-oxygen coordination sites for Li+
provided by BME yield solvation features that are considerably
diﬀerent from those of conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane
(DME). An anion-rich solvation sheath helps form an inorganic-
rich SEI and enhances ion transport kinetics. It exhibits excel-
lent rate performance and an ultra-long lifespan in Li/LiFePO4
full batteries. However, whether Li/S batteries can maintain
a normal conversion reaction in electrolytes containing a single
BME solvent, whether the anode surface can achieve a uniform
and stable SEI, and whether the SEI can still function stably
when facing high concentrations of reactive LiPSs under prac-
tical conditions are questions that remain unresolved. There-
fore, despite being a promising electrolyte formulation for
lithium batteries, further scientic and technological research
is necessary for BME ether-based electrolytes in Li/S batteries.
In this work, an electrolyte with BME as the single solvent
has been proven to operate compatibly in Li/S batteries, out-
performing
classical
DOL/DME-based
electrolytes.
An

the anion-abundant solvation sheath, leading to fast kinetics of
ions transport and stable Li plating/stripping. By incorporating
nitrate (NO3

sheath contributes to a robust interface for enhancing the

(CE) of the Li metal anode. Moreover, compared to DOL/DME
solvent, LiPS shuttle could be eﬀectively inhibited by BME
solvent by their diﬀusion barrier. As a result, Li/S pouch cells
with BME electrolyte achieved over twice the capacity retention
aer 60 cycles.

Results and discussion
To understand the diﬀerence in the Li+ solvation sheath with
anion aggregation between conventional DD (1 M LiTFSI in
DOL/DME) and NDD (with LiNO3 added to DD) electrolytes, and
new BME (1 M LiTFSI in BME) and NBME (adding LiNO3 in
BME) electrolytes, the Li+ solvation structures of diﬀerent elec-
trolytes were characterized by Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 1a).
Pure DME, DOL, and BME solvents were employed as the
control samples. Compared with pure DOL and DME solvents,
the emerging bands at 874 cm−1 in both DD and NDD electro-
lytes indicate the coordination of DOL/DME molecules and Li+.
Similarly, BME and NBME electrolytes exhibit new bands rela-
tive to pure BME solvent at about 866 cm−1, which corresponds
to the coordinated BME with Li+. Meanwhile, the coordination
environment of TFSI−was also studied. The S–N–S bending
vibration of TFSI−(680–780 cm−1) exhibits four obvious modes:
uncoordinated TFSI−(free anion, 739 cm−1), contact ion pairs
(CIPs, TFSI−bound to one Li+, 746 cm−1) and aggregated ions,
including AGG-I (TFSI−bound to two Li+, 752 cm−1) and AGG-II
(TFSI−bound to more than two Li+, 760 cm−1).25 The proportion
of these four components was calculated using a pie chart. The
NDD electrolyte contains an extremely high content (81.8%) of
uncoordinated TFSI−, 15.9% CIPs, 1.7% AGG-I and only 0.6%
AGG-II, while NBME electrolyte shows 64.8% uncoordinated
TFSI−, 29.3% CIPs, 4.4% AGG-I, and only 1.5% AGG-II. The
above results suggest that the rather weakly solvating BME
enhances Li+-TFSI−coordination, generating more anions to
access the solvation sheath in NBME electrolyte. As shown in
Fig. 1b, 7Li NMR results further demonstrate the diﬀerence in
the solvation sheath of NDD and NBME electrolytes. A more
negative shiwas observed in NDD electrolyte than in NBME
electrolyte due to the stronger binding between Li+ and DOL/
DME solvents, indicating that the more anion-based solvation
sheath could exist in NBME electrolyte.26 Such results were
further analyzed using the electrostatic potential distribution of
solvents (Fig. 1c–f) and their optimized binding geometries
(Fig. 1g–j) from density-functional theory (DFT) calculations. All
the negative regions are concentrated on the oxygen (O) atom in
DOL, DME, and BME solvents, with BME having two isomers

The properties of anode/electrolyte interfaces are strongly
aﬀected by the Li+ solvation sheath in diﬀerent electrolytes. As
shown in Fig. 2a, Li/Cu cells experience Li plating/stripping

before Li deposition in stage-I displays a rough appearance that
may cause an uneven electric eld and thus a nonuniform Li+
ux (Fig. S1†). The morphological evolutions of other stages of

dendrite-free and compact Li deposition (Fig. 2j). Aer plating
up to 5 mA h cm−2 in stage-III, the Li dendrites continue to grow
and still present an inhomogeneous morphology in FEED
(Fig. 2c) and NDD electrolytes (Fig. 2g). In NBME electrolyte,
chunky Li deposits with rounded margins evenly cover the Cu
foil surface (Fig. 2k). When the plated Li strips back to the
counter electrode at 2 mA h cm−2 in stage-IV, the dendritic Li
partly dissolves and abundant Li sheets or silks remain in FEED
(Fig. 2d) and NDD electrolytes (Fig. 2h), while the chunky Li

Measurements and characterization of Li/Cu cells or Li/Li
cells were performed to understand the eﬀect of various Li+-
solvated structures on electrochemical performance. As shown
in Fig. 3a, the long-term cycling of Li/Cu was conducted at
a constant current density of 1 mA cm−2 and a xed deposition

ones,
conventional

68 cycles in DD electrolyte, 109 cycles in NDD electrolyte and
153 cycles in BME electrolyte, indicating that BME electrolyte,
with the anion-rich solvation sheath, delivers improved Li
plating/stripping reversibility. Notably, when adding 2% LiNO3,
NBME electrolyte enables stable Li plating/stripping with an
average CE as high as 96% over 200 cycles. Aurbach CE tests of
Li/Cu in Fig. 3b also prove the stability of NBME electrolyte, with
an enhanced CE (99.7%) compared to FEED (95.6%), DD
(94.8%), NDD (96.1%), and BME (97.3%). In Li/Li symmetric
cells, NBME electrolyte captures more stable Li plating/
stripping than the other control electrolytes, sustaining over
1200 h and 400 h at 0.5 mA cm−2/1 mA h cm−2 and 1 mA cm−2/
2 mA h cm−2 current density/capacity, respectively (Fig. S2†).

In-depth
XPS was
performed

the Cu electrode aer the 10th plating/stripping cycle (Fig. 3c).
The Ar+ sputtering rate is ∼0.18 nm s−1, and the total sputtering
time is 400 s; thus, the sputtering depth of the SEI layer is
72 nm. In the C 1s spectra, the contents of C–O (286.8 eV) and
C]O (288.5 eV) bonds decrease with the deepening of etching
in all electrolytes. Among them, these organic components are
mainly detected on the anode surface in BME and NBME elec-
trolytes, while it can be clearly seen that these organic compo-
nents still remain until the completion of 400 s etching in NDD
electrolyte. The same situation is also observed in the O 1s
spectra (C]O at 533.7 eV and C–O at 532.2 eV).27 The contents
of inorganic components, including Li2O at 528.0 eV in the O 1s
spectra and LiF at 684.5 eV in the F 1s spectra, exhibit an
increasing trend with the deepening of etching in NDD elec-
trolyte.28 In BME and NBME electrolytes, it rst increases and
then decreases. These results demonstrate that a thick SEI lm
is formed in NDD electrolyte while a thin and inorganic
product-rich SEI lm is formed in BME-containing electrolyte.
By contrast, more abundant inorganic products, including LiF
and LiN3 (in N 1s spectra), are detected in the SEI formed by
NBME electrolyte compared to BME electrolyte. The detection of
the Cu element in Cu 2p spectra displayed that no signal was
captured in NDD electrolyte due to the superuous electrolyte
decomposition products, while a distinct signal could be
observed in BME and NBME electrolyte (Fig. S5†). Quantied
atomic concentrations of the detected elements on the surface
of Cu electrodes also display a sharp increase in the Cu element
and F- and N-rich SEI lms in NBME electrolyte (Fig. S6†).
To further elucidate the diﬀerent behavior of Li plating/
stripping in various electrolytes, as shown in Fig. 4, atomic
force microscopy (AFM) was applied to study the surface
roughness and Young's modulus of SEI layers. According to the
height sensor in Fig. 4a–c, the surface of Li metal with “sags and
crests” is extremely irregular in NDD electrolyte due to the
heterogeneous Li deposition/dissolution, dendritic Li forma-
tion, and uncontrollable electrolyte decomposition. In BME
electrolyte, the gap between high and low levels is weakened on
the Li surface. However, when using NBME electrolyte, the SEI
layer reaches an impressive atness and large-scale consistency
over a wide area. This indicates that the high-quality SEI layer
suppresses dendritic Li growth and maintains high integrity
and stability in NBME electrolyte, leading to reduced electrolyte
and active Li consumption, ultimately enhancing the battery
CE. The mechanical strength of these SEI layer is studied in
Fig. 4d–f. The SEI lm formed in NDD electrolyte demonstrates
a mean modulus of 1909 MPa, meanwhile, the SEI lm formed
in BME and NBME electrolytes exhibit moduli of 4301 and
6715 MPa. This indicates that the SEI lm formed in NBME
electrolyte is much harder. It reveals that the anion-rich solva-
tion sheath in NBME electrolyte has a signicant eﬀect on the
interface arrangement, facilitating the formation of a resistant
SEI layer.

LiPS dissolution was detected in the cycled S cathode from the
pouch cell with NBME compared to NDD electrolyte (Fig. S14†);
thus, the surface morphology of the S cathode appears more
intact and dense with NBME electrolyte (Fig. 5j and k). In
addition, the Li/S coin cells employing glass ber as a separator
were used to evaluate the eﬀect of NBME electrolyte on LiPS
inhibition; compared to NDD electrolyte, few LiPSs were found

and even aer 10 days of resting (Fig. S15†). These results
demonstrate that, compared to conventional NDD electrolyte,
NBME electrolyte enables the inhibition of dissolution and
shuttling of lithium polysulde because of the weaker solvating
ability of the solvent,31 leading to improved electrode/electrolyte
interfacial properties, including a LiF-rich cathode/electrolyte
interface (Fig. S16†). The electrochemical kinetics of the
battery with NDD and NBME was investigated by EIS (Fig. S17†).
Aer cycling, the resistance from the cell with NBME electrolyte
is much smaller than that from the cell with NDD electrolyte,
suggesting a faster Li+ diﬀusion between the NBME electrolyte
and the S cathode. The improved kinetics of sulfur conversion
reactions for the cells with NBME electrolyte was also indicated
by the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT,
Fig. S18†) and rate capability (Fig. S19†).

Experimental section
3.1.
Preparation
KB was used as microporous carbon nanoparticles to prepare
the S/KB composite (XRD information is presented in Fig. S20†)
due to its high specic surface area and high conductivity,
though it is diﬃcult to eﬀectively inhibit the shuttle eﬀect.32 The

disc with a diameter of 12 mm to assemble the coin cell. The
anodes, copper–lithium composite slices (80 mm thick pieces of
lithium foil conglutinated on two sides of 6 mm thick Cu foil),
were also prepared as described previously.33 Two copper–
lithium composite anodes with a slightly larger size of 33.5 ×
57.5 were used to assemble pouch cells with 3.0 mg cm−2
cathodes, respectively.

3.2.
Electrochemical measurements
The charge/discharge tests and Li plating/stripping CE tests
were carried out on a LAND system for the coin cells and on
a multichannel battery cycler (CT-3008W, Neware) for Li/S
pouch cells. The Li/Li symmetric cells were cycled at current
densities and charge/discharge capacities of 0.5 mA cm−2/
1 mA h cm−2 and 1 mA cm−2/2 mA h cm−2. Li/Cu cells were
employed to evaluate CE through plating with a desirable Li
capacity of 1 mA h cm−2 and stripping back with a cut-oﬀ
voltage of 1 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a current density of 1 mA cm−2.
Aurbach CE tests of Li/Cu cells were conducted at 1 mA cm−2
with pre-lithiation at 10 mA cm−2, followed by reversible
plating/stripping with 2 mA h cm−2 for 10 cycles, and nally
stripping to 1 V(vs. Li/Li+). The equation for calculation of CE is
CE = (Qs + Qc × n)/(Qp + Qc × n), where Qs represents the nal
stripping capacity, Qp represents the initial plating capacity, Qc
represents the constant plating/stripping capacity for each
cycle, and n represents the cycle number. Li/S coin cells were
operated at a cycling rate of C/10 in a 28 °C constant-
temperature chamber, Li/S pouch cells were evaluated at an
active rate of C/100 and a cycling rate of C/20. The charge/
discharge voltage range was controlled between 1.6 and 2.6 V.
EIS experiments were conducted on an electrochemical work-
station (CHI604E) with an amplitude of 5 mV in the frequency
range of 105 to 0.1 Hz. The galvanostatic intermittent titration
technique (GITT) was carried out on a LAND cell system, the
relaxation and intermittent time were set to 5 min, and then the
cell was discharged to 1.6 V at a rate of 0.05C.
3.3.
Physical characterization studies
The batteries were disassembled in an Ar-lled glove box, and
anodes were washed with pure DME solvent to remove the
residual electrolyte. The morphological observation of elec-
trodes, including Li, Cu anodes and S cathodes, was carried out
by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS ULTRA 55,
Germany). Tests of microstructure and Young's modulus of the
SEI layer on Li anodes were conducted by atomic force micros-
copy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany). X-ray photoelec-
tron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a PHI 1800 using
a focused monochromatized Al Ka radiation source (h = 1486.6
eV) under ultra-high vacuum. The Ar+ sputtering rate was
∼0.18 nm s−1. The Raman spectra were recorded at room
temperature using an inVia Qontor Raman spectrophotometer
(RENISHAW Co., Ltd.), which was equipped with a single
monochromator as a laser lter. Raman spectra were excited by
a 785 nm laser. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tests of
diﬀerent electrolytes were conducted on a Bruker DRX-400 NMR
spectrometer using chloroform-d6 as the solvent. X-ray diﬀrac-
tion (XRD) was performed on a Bruker D8 Advance employing Cu
Ka radiation at 60 kV and 80 mA to examine changes in the S/KB
material crystal structure. Electrolyte absorption measurements
were performed with a contact angle meter (JC2000D3P).
3.4.
Calculation methods
In this study, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were
performed using Gaussian 09, an advanced computational
chemistry soware. During the computational procedure,
models were initially established by inputting the molecular
structures of the modules. Subsequently, appropriate parame-
ters, functionals (GGA), and basis sets (BLYP) were selected,
along with a plane wave basis set to enable electronic structure
calculations.
4
Conclusions
In this work, an electrolyte using BME as a single solvent with
1 M LiTFSI and 2% LiNO3 is proposed to improve Li metal
compatibility and mitigate polysulde dissolution in Li/S pouch
cells. The weak solvating BME contributes to the generation of
a dual-anion (TFSI−and NO3
−)-aggregated Li+ solvation sheath,
leading to the formation of Li-metal-stabilizing LiF and Li+
conduction nitride on the SEI lm, thus enabling reversible Li
plating/stripping. Such developed electrolyte exhibits an average
CE as high as 96% over 200 cycles in Li/Cu cells and ultra-stable
cycling over 1200 h in Li/Li symmetric cells. Furthermore, BME-
based electrolyte would mitigate polysulde dissolution and thus
the generated shuttle eﬀect in Li/S full cells. Consequently, the
Ah-level Li/S pouch cells achieve over twice the capacity retention
aer 60 cycles in NBME compared to the traditional NDD elec-
trolyte. This electrolyte design strategy presents a solution to
stabilize practical Li/S cells by interfacial manipulation and
inhibition of polysulde dissolution.
