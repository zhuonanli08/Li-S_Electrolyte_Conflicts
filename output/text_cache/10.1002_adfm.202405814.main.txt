Phloroglucinol–2,6-Diaminoanthraquinone as a Durable
Redox Mediator for Enhancing Conversion Reaction Kinetics
in Lithium-Sulfur Batteries
Tianxing Lai and Arumugam Manthiram*
Lithium-sulfur batteries, despite being a promising solution for
next-generation secondary batteries, require substantial eﬀorts to overcome
the challenges of sluggish sulfur redox kinetics, polysulﬁdes shuttling, and Li
metal instability before achieving practical viability. Conventional strategies
that utilize metal catalysts or soluble redox mediators (RMs) are limited by
either impractical producing processes or unsatisfactory service life. Herein,
an electrochemically active organic material, phloroglucinol –
2,6-diaminoanthraquinone (PG-DAAQ) is synthesized through a green and
facile polymerization process to better resolve these issues. Serving as an RM
at the cathode, PG-DAAQ exhibits enduring redox activity within the sulfur
operating potential window, leading to enhanced redox kinetics and sulfur
utilization. Remarkably, even without any metal elements, PG-DAAQ exhibits
an excellent aﬃnity to polysulﬁdes, thereby suppressing the shuttling and
facilitating the formation of a more favorable solid-electrolyte interface to
stabilize Li deposition at the anode. As a result, Li-S cells employing
PG-DAAQ show signiﬁcantly enhanced cycling and rate performances than
the control cells. Even with a low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 6, pouch cells
with PG-DAAQ deliver a reversible discharge capacity of 821 mA h g−1 after
100 cycles at a C/10 rate.
1. Introduction
As the world transitions toward a low-carbon and renewable en-
ergy systems, the demand for sustainable energy storage solu-
tions is ever-growing. This has led to eﬀorts toward innovation
in eﬃcient rechargeable batteries. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batter-
ies oﬀer an attractive solution due to their high theoretical en-
ergy density (2,600 W h kg−1), high theoretical speciﬁc capacity
(1,672 mA h g−1), and the high abundance of sulfur.[1] However,
to achieve high energy density, practical cell conditions must be
met, including low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio, low negative-
to-positive capacity (N/P) ratio, high sulfur loading, etc.[2–4] Poor

impeding better performance include slug-

polysulﬁdes (LiPS) shuttling eﬀect, and the
instability of Li anode in the electrolyte.
These issues are unique, yet interrelated,
preventing the full potential of Li-S batter-
ies from being realized.
To address these drawbacks, researchers
have sought to enhance the cathode kinet-
ics and mitigate the shuttling eﬀect by em-
ploying catalysts, including metal chalco-
genides, metal nitrides, metal phosphides,
single-atom catalysts, etc.[5–10] These cata-
lysts are able to promote one or more steps
of the sulfur redox reactions and strongly
adsorb LiPS to retain them at the cathode
side. Strategies like heteroatom doping, de-
fect engineering, and heterostructures have
been widely explored and applied to further
improve the electrocatalytic eﬀect and the
adsorption capabilities of the catalysts.[11–17]
However, these catalysts often come with
their own set of drawbacks. They can be
expensive and heavy, and require complicated processes for load-
ing onto a host to improve their active surface area and con-
ductivity, thereby limiting their practical application. In addition,
various porous materials have been employed in the Li-S sys-
tem, such as porous carbon, carbon nanotubes, graphene, porous
polymer materials, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), and co-
valent organic frameworks (COFs).[18–23] Beneﬁtting from their
high speciﬁc surface area, these materials can act as a sulfur host
and physically conﬁne LiPS species. Nevertheless, it is still chal-
lenging to balance their electrical conductivity, catalytic eﬀect,
cost-eﬀectiveness, and synthesis diﬃculties.
Utilizing a redox mediator (RM) in the cell presents another
approach that shows great promise in overcoming the limitations
of Li-S batteries. While electrocatalysts and porous materials
function through their chemical and physical interactions with
LiPS, RMs are electrochemically active within the working po-
tential range of the cell and can participate in the redox reactions.
The generated intermediates eﬀectively reduce the energy barrier
and enable homogeneous oxidation/reduction of the active ma-
terials. Soluble LiPS themselves can function as intrinsic RMs,
but they have limited redox mediation capabilities and unreliable

Drawing inspiration from anthraquinone, a molecule known
for its redox activity and polysulﬁde adsorbability, we introduce
here a low-cost and metal-free organic material, phloroglucinol –
2,6-diaminoanthraquinone (PG-DAAQ), that functions as an RM
but resists shuttling. Synthesized through a facile polymerization
reaction, an azo-linked network is formed to secure the active an-
thraquinone units at the cathode. PG-DAAQ, or more speciﬁcally
the two C═O functional groups in the anthraquinone unit, under-
goes a two-electron redox reaction within the sulfur redox poten-
tial range and exhibits strong interactions with LiPS, which no-
tably ameliorates the conversion kinetics of sulfur species and Li+
diﬀusivity. This leads to reduced overpotential and higher sulfur
utilization for the cathode. The Li-metal anode also beneﬁts from
the immobilization of LiPS, allowing the formation of a more in-
organic passivation layer on the surface. Consequently, Li-S cells
with PG-DAAQ show a signiﬁcant improvement in capacity, cy-
clability, and rate performance. We also demonstrate the eﬀec-
tiveness of PG-DAAQ with high-sulfur-loading cells and pouch
cells (both under limited-electrolyte conditions). This study ex-
empliﬁes a novel approach to employing RMs to propel the
development of sustainable high-energy-density Li-S batteries
(Table S1, Supporting Information).
2. Results and Discussion
Anthraquinone and its derivatives are redox-active within the po-
tential range of Li-S batteries, characterized by a two-electron re-
dox reaction with their two C═O groups. This unique property al-
lows them to function as RMs for sulfur redox reactions. To min-
imize its shuttling and loss when catalyzing sulfur species con-
version, PG-DAAQ is designed to immobilize the anthraquinone
group through a rapid polymerization while ensuring the num-
ber of active sites in the material. As depicted in Figure 1a
and Figure S1 (Supporting Information), PG-DAAQ is synthe-
sized through a diazotization and coupling reaction at low tem-
peratures, a condition that is milder compared to conventional
synthesis of metal-based catalysts. 2,6-diaminoanthraquinone
(DAAQ) was selected as the precursor to produce diazonium
cations on its two sides. Phloroglucinol (PG) was utilized as
the linker due to its reactivity with diazonium salts and cost-
eﬀectiveness. The generated DAAQ diazonium salts coupled
with PG formed an azo-linked polymer network.
The structure and properties of the synthesized material were
characterized by a series of techniques. X-ray diﬀraction (XRD)
patterns suggest that the crystalline DAAQ has transformed
into an amorphous organic polymer through the PG linker
and azo units (Figure 1b), indicating a complete reaction of the
precursors. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image shows

Other properties of PG-DAAQ were further characterized with
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and N2 adsorption analysis be-
fore employing it in the sulfur cathode. TGA reveals that PG-
DAAQ remains stable below 200 °C both under Ar and air at-
mospheres (Figure S4, Supporting Information). N2 adsorption
isotherms of PG-DAAQ correspond to a Brunauer–Emmett–
Teller (BET) surface area of 76.6 m2 g−1 (Figure S5, Supporting
Information). These results demonstrate that PG-DAAQ is ther-
mally stable and has the suitable surface area to be applied into
the S/C cathode for the melt-diﬀusion process, ensuring better
contact between the active materials and the functional groups
in the polymer. FTIR spectra also conﬁrm that all the character-
istic absorption peaks of PG-DAAQ are retained after sulfur melt-
diﬀusion (Figure S6, Supporting Information).
To examine the electrochemical behavior of PG-DAAQ and
its ability to catalyze sulfur redox reactions, cyclic voltammetry
(CV) tests were performed with diﬀerent cell conﬁgurations. The
CV plot of pure PG-DAAQ exhibits continuous current response
with a pair of broad cathodic and anodic peaks at ≈2.0 and 2.25 V,
respectively, suggesting a good match with the redox potential of
sulfur species, especially for the slow conversion between solu-
ble LiPS and Li2S (Figure S7, Supporting Information). For Li
|| S cells, rate-dependent CV scans show that the cell with PG-
DAAQ has higher response currents for all the cathodic and an-
odic processes, as well as a smaller overpotential between the
cathodic and anodic peaks (Figure 2a,b). This indicates that PG-
DAAQ eﬀectively improves sulfur utilization and reduces the po-
larization of the system. Furthermore, the diﬀusion properties
can be obtained by ﬁtting the peak current ip of each redox peak
and the scan rate v. For a reversible electrochemical process, ip
is proportional to vn (n = 0.5 for a diﬀusion-controlled process,
n = 1 for a surface-controlled process, or 0.5< n <1 for a mixed
process).[35,36] From the ﬁtting results, the cell with PG-DAAQ
shows higher slopes for all the redox peaks, indicating a higher
Li+ diﬀusivity in the electrode (Figure S8 and Table S2, Support-
ing Information).[37] To further assess the catalytic abilities, Tafel
slopes were derived from CV curves for each redox process. For
the PG-DAAQ cell, the slopes were calculated to be 43.3, 22.0, and
48.0 mV dec−1 for S to LiPS, LiPS to Li2S, and Li2S to LiPS con-
version processes, respectively, whereas for the control cell, the
slopes were calculated to be 48.9, 30.6, 52.6 mV dec−1, respec-
tively (Figure 2c; Figure S9, Supporting Information). The activa-
tion energy (Ea) diﬀerence can be derived from the Tafel slopes.
The smaller Tafel slopes for the PG-DAAQ cell indicate a decrease
in Ea for each of the redox process listed above. The Ea values de-
crease from the control cell to the PG-DAAQ cell by 34.4, 146, and
23.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, for the above three redox processes,
showcasing the enhanced kinetics and the ability of PG-DAAQ to
catalyze the conversion at both directions.[38] This phenomenon
is also conﬁrmed with Li2S6 symmetric cells. In the CV scans, the
PG-DAAQ cell exhibits a higher response current and smaller
overpotential compared to the control cell (Figure 2d); mean-
while, from the ﬁtting of linear scanning voltammetry (LSV)
scans, a higher exchange current density is observed for the PG-
DAAQ cell, suggesting faster liquid–liquid LiPS conversion ki-
netics (Figure S10, Supporting Information).[39]
Additionally, to better investigate the liquid–solid conversion
kinetics, Li2S nucleation studies (chronoamperometry experi-
ments) were conducted with Li || Li2S8 cells. The PG-DAAQ cell
shows a higher chronoamperometry peak and a larger Li2S pre-
cipitation capacity of 597 mA h g−1, compared to 448 mA h g−1 for
the control cell (Figure S11, Supporting Information). The car-
bon paper cathodes were then retrieved from the cell and char-
acterized by SEM to observe the morphology of deposited Li2S.
The SEM images show that the cathode with PG-DAAQ has a
smooth precipitation of Li2S covering the carbon substrate, while
the control cell exhibits a rougher surface and exposed carbon
ﬁbers (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Thus, PG-DAAQ is
proven to promote the liquid–solid conversion and regulate Li2S
nucleation and growth at the cathode. Moreover, galvanostatic
intermittent titration technique (GITT) analysis was performed
to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the catalytic
activity.[40] The voltage proﬁles and the corresponding internal re-
sistance values are shown in Figure 2e,f. The PG-DAAQ cell pos-
sesses lower internal resistances, especially during Li2S nucle-
ation and toward the end of the discharge. This indicates a lower
charge and mass transfer barrier, facilitating the sulfur species
conversion at the cathode.
Following these ﬁndings, electrochemical impedance spec-
troscopy (EIS) was utilized to further dissect the cell resistance
and understand the kinetic limiting process. Li || S cells with
and without PG-DAAQ were cycled once and then discharged
to various potentials before taking the EIS measurement. When
the cells are fully charged, Nyquist plots show one semicircle in
the high-frequency region and an inclined line in the medium
to low-frequency range (Figure 3a,b). The semicircle can be in-
terpreted as the surface resistance of the electrodes (Rsurf), in-
cluding the solid-electrolyte interface (SEI) and the contact be-
tween active material and conductive agent, and its intercept on
the real axis is related to the internal cell resistance (R0, mostly
arising from the resistance of the electrolyte).[41,42] The inclined
line is attributed to the Warburg resistance (ZW), corresponding
to the bulk diﬀusion. When the discharge begins, a new semicir-
cle appeared in the EIS plot, which can be ascribed to the charge-
transfer resistance (Rct) of the sulfur reduction process. Between
2.4 and 2.1 V, Rsurf and Rct continue to increase due to the for-
mation of LiPS and the initialization of the potential-limiting
liquid–solid conversion.[43] Furthermore, when discharged below
2.0 V, Rsurf decreases as the LiPS concentration in the electrolyte
drops; meanwhile, ﬁlm resistance of the deposited Li2S/Li2S2
(Rﬁlm) emerges in the spectra as a large arc (Figure S13, Support-
ing Information). Compared to the control cell, the PG-DAAQ

toward the Li atom, suggesting chemical interaction between O
and Li atoms.

ical cell performance, sulfur was melt-diﬀused into the PG-
DAAQ/Ketjen Black (KB) mixture and utilized as the cathode
active material. In the rate performance tests, Li || S cells with
and without PG-DAAQ were cycled at various rates ranging from
C/10 to 2C and then back to a C/5 rate (Figure 5a). The PG-DAAQ
cell shows stable speciﬁc capacities of 1198, 1026, 943, 899, and
870 mA h g−1 at C/10, C/5, C/2, 1C, and 2C rates, respectively,
based on the 2nd cycle for each rate. These capacities are con-
sistently larger than those of the control cell, especially at a high
cycling rate of 2C, where the control cell only exhibits a capac-
ity of 731 mA h g−1 and decays faster. Furthermore, when the

3. Conclusion
In summary, this work employed PG-DAAQ as a redox-active
organic material that does not contain any metal element and
functions as a RM in Li-S batteries. PG-DAAQ was synthe-
sized through a diazotization-coupling reaction between PG and
DAAQ, so that the active DAAQ sites can be incorporated into
the polymer and stay at the cathode. PG-DAAQ is demonstrated
to have a strong catalytic ability to enhance the sulfur redox ki-
netics and mitigate LiPS shuttling. In particular, the kinetics of
the limiting liquid–solid transition is promoted, thus enabling
higher sulfur utilization and superior cycling performance. Fur-
ther investigations reveal that the catalytic eﬀect primarily orig-
inates from the redox process of the C═O group and its strong
interactions with LiPS. Meanwhile, due to less LiPS shuttling,
PG-DAAQ facilitates the formation of a more stable SEI layer on
the anode with more inorganic components, thereby stabilizing
the Li deposition. Li-S cells with PG-DAAQ show excellent rate
performance and improved cycling stability. More importantly,
pouch cells under practical conditions exhibit stable cycling for
more than 100 cycles, retaining more than 75% of the initial
capacities. We hope that this work will inspire the exploration
of such organic RMs to enable high-energy-density metal-sulfur
batteries.
