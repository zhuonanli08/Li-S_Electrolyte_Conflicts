The main goals for designing new LSB electrolytes are to sup-

important role in LSBs, as they serve as the medium for trans-
porting lithium ions and extensively participate in the reactions 
of Li salts on the surface of electrodes. A carefully designed 
electrolyte can achieve the long-lifespan and efficient opera-
tion of LSBs. Recently, the sparingly solvating electrolytes were

successfully used for high performance LSB.[17–19] For example, 
Zheng et  al. formulated an ultrahigh-concentrated electrolyte

With a high energy density, lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB) are regarded as one 
of the promising next-generation energy storage systems. However, many 
challenges hinder the practical applications of LSB, such as the dendrite for-
mations/parasitic reactions on the Li metal anode and the “shuttle effect” of 
lithium polysulfides of the LSB cathode. Herein, a novel diluted medium-con-
centrated electrolyte (DMCE) is developed by adding 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 
2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) into a dual salt medium-concentrated 
electrolyte (MCE) consisting of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide 
(LiTFSI)–lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI)/tetrahydrofuran (THF)–
dipropyl ether (DPE). The optimized DMCE electrolyte is capable of protecting 
the Li metal anode and suppresses the dissolution of polysulfides and the 
“shuttle effect”, delivering a high coulombic efficiency (CE) of Li plating–strip-
ping up to 99.6% even at a low concentration of Li salt (1.0–2.0 m). Impres-
sively, compared with the cells cycled in the MCE electrolyte, the LiS cells 
with the DMCE-2.0 m electrolyte have delivered an enhanced initial capacity 
of 682 mAh g−1 with an excellent capacity retention of 92% for 500 cycles. 
This strategy of using fluorinated ether as diluent solvent in a medium-con-
centrated electrolyte can accelerate the commercialization of LSB.

1. Introduction
The elemental sulfur is nontoxic with high abundance in 
nature, and it can deliver a high theoretical specific capacity 
(1672 mAh g−1) as a low-cost cathode material. Coupled with the 
lithium metal anode, the lithium–sulfur batteries (LSB) have 
a theoretical energy density up to 2600 Wh kg−1, and LSB has 
been considered as a promising next-generation energy storage 
system.[1,2] However, some serious intrinsic problems limit the 
cycling performance of LSB,[3–5] including the “shuttle effect” of 
polysulfides, the high reactivity of Li with electrolyte and the Li

stability compared to the conventional electrolytes made of

the heavy usages of expensive lithium salts in HCE lead to 
some issues, including low ionic conductivity, high viscosity, 
poor wettability and high cost.[22] To address these issues while 
maintain a limited LiPSs solubility, the localized high-concen-
trated electrolyte (LHCE) is proposed by adding fluorinated 
solvent with a weak donating ability (for example, fluorinated 
ether and fluoride benzene) in HCE. For example, the fluori-
nated ethers (such as 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluo-
ropropyl ether, TTE) have been employed as co-solvent in the 
electrolyte for lithium–sulfur cells, which has the improved 
cycling performance with the detainment of polysulfides inside 
the cathode.[23–26] Some other fluorinated solvents have been 
tested as co-solvents of electrolytes, including 1,2-bis(1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethoxy) ethane, hexafluoroisopropyl methyl ether, 
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3,3-pentafluoropropyl ether, and 
tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) orthoformate), and these electrolytes 
deliver high coulombic efficiency (CE) in lithium metal bat-
teries without Li dendrite growth.[27–30]
In this work, we developed a novel diluted ether-based LSB 
electrolyte, in which a dual-salts based medium-concentrated 
electrolyte (MCE) was diluted by TTE, and the new electrolyte is 
named as diluted medium-concentrated electrolytes (DMCEs). 
By increasing the concentration of TTE in the electrolytes, the 
dissolution of lithium polysulfide was further inhibited. In 
addition, with a relative low concentration of Li salt (ranging 
from 2 to 1  m), a high stripping/plating CE (>99.6%) for Li 
anodes was achieved in the DMCE electrolyte, and the growth 
of dendritic Li has been suppressed effectively.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Physicochemical Properties of Electrolytes
TTE is commonly used fluorinated ether for LHCEs, which has 
a highly fluorinated structure and scarcely involves in the solva-
tion of lithium salts and LiPSs, and is beneficial for the forma-
tion of F-rich SEI (such as LiF) and for the suppression of the 
LiPSs shuttle effect.[23,31–34] In this work, three TTE-diluted elec-
trolytes with different concentrations of Li Salt (2.0/1.5/1.0 m) 
were formulated based on our previously optimized LLTE 
MCE formulation. The molar ratios of LiTFSI/LiFSI/THF/
DPE + TTE for the four electrolytes are 1:3:8:2:0, 1:3:8:2:5, 
1:3:8:2:9 and 1:3:8:2:18, and they are named as MCE, DMCE-
2.0  m, DMCE-1.5  m and DMCE-1.0  m, respectively. Figure 1

2.2. CE and Stability for Li Metal Plating/Stripping
The Li || Cu cells were employed to assess the performance of these 
electrolytes for Li plating/stripping at the current of 0.5 mA cm−2  
and the deposition capacity of 1.0 mA cm−2. As demonstrated in 
Figure S2, Supporting Information, the Li || Cu cells in the MCE 
electrolyte delivers a CE of 99.3%. Encouragingly, the DMCE-2.0 m 
and DMCE-1.5 m electrolytes give a high CE of 99.6% even at the 
500th cycle. In addition, the voltage profile of metallic Li plating/
stripping in DMCE-2.0 m/1.5 m at different cycles shows that the 
plating/stripping process is highly stable, and lower polarization 
voltage hysteresis slightly decreases with the increase of the cycling 
number (Figure 2a,b). However, the CE of the DMCE-1.0 m elec-
trolyte faded to 97% rapidly after 350 cycles (Figure 2c,d). These 
results reveal that an appropriate amount of TTE in the electrolyte 
formulation is beneficial for the formation of a LiF-rich SEI pas-
sivation layer on the Li anode and for the homogeneous deposition 
of lithium. However, when the cells contains the diluted electro-
lyte with low-concentration salt (such as DMCE-1.0 m), lithium salt 
tends be exhausted. We also investigated the behavior of lithium 
plating/stripping under the conditions of large capacity and high 
current. The Li metal half cells (Figure S3, Supporting Informa-
tion) was conducted at higher current density (2.0 mA cm−2) with 
a capacity 2.0 mA cm−2 for more than 100 cycles. Although the ini-
tial coulomb efficiency (CE) of the cells with DMCE (about 95%) is 
slightly lower than that with MCE (about 98%), the DMCEs deliv-
ered an improved CE in the following cycles. Especially, the cell 
with DMCE-2.0 m has achieved a high CE of 99.1% in the second 
cycle. And after 40 cycles, the cells with DMCEs could deliver an 
average CE of 99.4%, which was slightly higher than the cell with 
MCE (about 99.2%).
Figure  2e exhibits the SEM morphologies of the cycled Li 
anodes (20 cycles) in the Li || Li cells with the MCE and DMCE 
electrolytes. The surface of originally dense lithium metal has 
become a loose and porous layer after cycling in MCE. Different 
from the rough growth in MCE, the lithium electrode cycled in 
DMCE electrolyte would form a nodule-like morphology after 
the repeated deposition and stripping process. Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information, presents the long-term-cycling test of the 
Li || Li symmetric cells with the MCE and DMCE electrolytes, and 
the polarization is a good indicator of the long-term compatibility 
and cycling stability. A stable cycling profile was achieved with 
the DMCE-2.0 m electrolyte at the current density of 1 mA cm−2 
and the deposition capacity of 1.0 mAh cm−2, and DMCE-2.0 m 
gives the lowest voltage polarization even after 1200  h cycling. 
Although the Li-ion conductivity is usually reduced by adding 
the fluorinated ether, the overpotential for Li plating/stripping 
cycling in DMCE-2.0 m is lower than the control MCE electro-
lyte, which indicates that the SEI formed DMCE-2.0 m delivers 
a lower lithium-ion migration impedance due to the presence of 
TTE in the electrolyte. However, as an inert solvent, TTE does 
not contribute to the solvation the Li salts, the overpotential of 
DMCE-1.5 m/1.0 m is slightly larger than that of DMCE-2.0 m.
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy has been 
employed to understand the ion solvation structure in the solu-
tion. Figure S5, Supporting Information, shows the spectra of 
the association of the ether solvents with the Li salt in a high 
concentration solution (MCE, about 2.5 m), and the diluted MCE 
with different ratio of TTE (DMCE-2.0  m, DMCE-1.5  m, and 
DMCE-1.0  m). With the Li salts concentration increasing, the 
ring stretching vibration assigned to the peak at about 1070 cm−1  
is evidently shifted to a low wavenumber direction (about 
1045 cm−1). The signal for the ring stretches in THF molecules 
(the peak around 1045 cm−1) changed from a shoulder peak to 
a strong single peak due to the participation in Li+ solvation. 
This trend can be seen for the CO stretching mode at around 
930–870 cm−1, the peak at 890 cm−1 (COC stretching in THF-
Li+ complexes) appeared and enhanced with the weakening of 
the peak at 910 cm−1 (COC stretching in free THF) when the 
Li concentration increases. There was no significant change in 
the spectra of the association of the ether solvents after adding a 
different molar ratio of TTE in MCE. The addition of TTE did not 
change the original solvation structure, but formed a LHCE sim-
ilar to that previously reported. In summary, the rational addition 
of TTE to electrolyte can effectively suppress the Li dendrite, ena-
bling the stable Li deposition/stripping cycles. In addition, the 
DMCE-2.0  m electrolyte with the appropriate concentration of 
TTE also shows excellent properties of lower cost, lower-viscosity, 
and better wettability than the typical HCE and MCE systems.

2.4. Electrochemical Tests of LiS cells
As one of the severe issues that hinder the practical applications 
of LSBs, the self-discharge is mainly caused by the “shuttle effect” 
of the soluble LiPSs migrating from LSB cathode to anode, trig-
gering the decay of capacity of LSBs during the cycling and/or 
storage.[37] Figure 4 and Figure S9a,b, Supporting Information, 
compare the discharging/charging performances of the cells at the 
20th cycle. The capacity of the LSB cell with the conventional 1 m 
LiTFSI/DME-DOL electrolyte (LDD) is obviously lower than those 
in the MCE and DMCE electrolytes. In addition, the charge–dis-
charge curve of the cells with the LiTFSI/DME-DOL electrolyte in 
Figure 4a shows an obvious upper slope appearing at around 2.5 V 
with a transition to an arc for the formation of Li2S, indicating 
that the soluble higher-order polysulfides have been formed in the 
conventional electrolyte. Encouragingly, Figures 4b and 4c demon-
strate that the dissolution of polysulfides was effectively inhibited 
in the MCE and DMCE electrolytes and the solid–solid reaction 
mechanism dominates the LiS chemistry.

3. Conclusion
In summary, we have demonstrated a DMCE by using the fluori-
nated ether (TTE) as the diluent in the 2.0 m LiTFSI-LiFSI/THF-
DPE-TTE electrolyte. TTE is beneficial for the formation of the 
LiF-rich SEI layer and for the suppression of a parasitic reaction. 
The CE of lithium plating/stripping has been improved from 
99.3% (in MCE) up to 99.6% (in DMCEs), demonstrating that the 
DMCE electrolytes is promising for the next generation lithium 
metal batteries (LMBs). In addition, with the effective suppres-
sion the LiPSs shuttle effect, the LSB cells with the DMCE elec-
trolytes have no the self-discharge effect during a 10-day storage. 
As the best electrolyte with lower viscosity and better wetta-
bility, the LSB cells with the DMCE-2.0  m electrolyte delivers 
an impressive initial capacity of 682 mAh g−1 (667 mAh g−1  
for MCE) and a long lifespan of 500 cycles with an excellent 
capacity retention of 92%. The design principle of the DMCE 
electrolyte can be widely employed to other battery systems, and 
promote the developments of advanced electrolyte systems for 
the new energy storage technologies.
4. Experimental Section
Materials: Most materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. 
(USA), including lithium-foil (Li, 50 µm, China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., 
China), copper-foil (100  µm, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., 
China), aluminum-foil (15 µm, Nippon Light Metal Co., Ltd., Japan) and 
separator (2325, Celgard, USA). Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)
imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%), polyacrylonitrile (PAN, average Mw ≈ 150 000, 
99%). Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, 99.9%) was purchased 
from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Japan. Tetrahydrofuran (THF, 
anhydrous, 99.9%) was obtained from Innochem, Co., Ltd., China. 
Dipropyl ether (DPE, 98%), TTE (98%), elemental sulfur (S8, 99.8%), 
selenium (Se powder, 99.99%), ethanol (spectral purity, 99.8%), and 
propanol (99.5%) were purchased from Aladdin, Inc., China. All the 
solvents for electrolytes were stored with 4Å molecular sieves in an argon 
glove box before use. Conductive carbon was acetylene black (Denka 
Black Li 250, Lion Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan). The LA132 binder (solid 
content ≈ 15%) and LA136D binder (solid content ≈ 6%) were purchased 
from Indigo Technology Co., Ltd., China. Deionized (DI) water was made 
from a water purifier (Smart-N UV, Heal Force).
SeSPAN Electrode Preparation: Sulfur, selenium, and polyacrylonitrile 
powders were ball-milled at a weight ratio of 15:1:3 with ethanol used as 
the dispersant. The mixture was dried at 60 °C for 6 h and then heated to 
350 °C for 6 h in a nitrogen atmosphere to obtain the SeSPAN composite 
with a sulfur content of 41.8% (Table S1, Supporting Information). The 
sulfur content was determined by elemental analysis (Vario EL cube, 
Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany). The SeSPAN cathode 
slurry was prepared by mixing SeSPAN, acetylene black, LA132, and 
LA136D binder (with a weight ratio of 7:2:0.25:0.75) in a mixed-solvent 
(propanol:water = 1:3, v/v), which was then spread on Al foils and dried 
at 60 °C for 6 h in vacuum oven. The areal loading of SeSPAN in the 
as-fabricated cathode was about 2.0 mg cm−2 in all cases.
Electrolytes Preparation: All electrolytes were prepared in argon-filled 
dry glove box (H2O < 0.01  ppm, O2  < 0.01  ppm). LiTFSI (0.01 molar) 
and LiFSI (0.03 molar) were mixed and dissolved in the solvent mixture 
(containing 0.08 molar THF and 0.02 molar DPE) to form the LiTFSI-
LiFSI-THF-DPE medium-concentrated electrolyte (MCE), and this MCE 
is named as LLTE. To prepare the DMCE, the LLTE electrolyte was diluted 
by adding certain amount of TTE (0.05/0.09/0.18 molar corresponding 
to the concentration of 2.0/1.5/1.0 m, respectively) and stirred for 6 h. 
These solutions were stirred with stoichiometric amounts of lithium 
sulfide (Li2S) and sulfur (S8) for 24 h to form the saturated Li2S6 solution 
and then stored for 7 days at room temperature.
Battery Fabrication: In an argon glove box, the CR2025 or CR2016 
style battery cells were fabricated with the Celgard 2325 separator. The 
coulombic efficiency of lithium plating/stripping is examined by using 
Li || Cu coin cells with 20  µL electrolyte. The electrochemical window 
of electrolytes was examined by using Li || steel coin cells with 20  µL 
electrolyte. The galvanostatic cycling performance of Li deposition–
dissolution was conducted by using Li || Li symmetric cells with 30 µL 
electrolyte. The Li-SeSPAN full cells were assembled by using CR2016 
coin cells with 20 µL electrolyte.
Electrochemical Measurements: The electrochemical window of these 
electrolytes was examined by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) using 
an electrochemical workstation (IVIUMSTAT, Netherlands). In order 
to investigate the CE of the plating/stripping of lithium, the constant 
current density was set at 0.5  mA cm−2 for a capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 
at room temperature. The current density of Li || Li symmetric cells 
was set as 1 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 for each cycle. The 
discharge and charge measurements for Li || Cu cells and Li || Li cells 
were carried out on NEWARE Battery Test System (Shenzhen, China) at 
room temperature. The galvanostatic charge/discharge for Li-SeSPAN 
cells was conducted using a NEWARE Battery Test System (Shenzhen, 
China) in the voltage range of 1.0–3.0  V (versus Li/Li+) at room 
temperature. The current rate was calculated based on the theoretical 
capacity of SeSPAN, and the specific discharge/charge capacities were 
based on the weight of SeSPAN.
Electrode Characterization: The morphologies of lithium metal 
electrodes were investigated by the scanning electron microscopic (SEM, 
FESEEM Phenom LE). The cycled Li-SeSPAN cells were dissembled in 
the glove box and the electrodes were thoroughly washed three times 
with anhydrous THF to remove the residual electrolyte. In order to 
avoid exposure to air, the dried samples were sealed in an air-isolating 
container filled with Ar gas and transferred quickly into the SEM or 
XPS instruments. XPS was performed using an X-ray photoelectron 
spectrometer (ESCALAB250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a 
monochromatic Al Ka source (1486.6 eV).
