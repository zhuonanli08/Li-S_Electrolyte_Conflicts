ABSTRACT: Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries are drawing huge attention as
attractive chemical power sources. However, traditional ether-based solvents
(DME/DOL) suﬀer from safety issues at high temperatures and serious parasitic
reactions occur between the Li metal anodes and soluble lithium polysulﬁdes
(LiPSs). Herein, we propose a polysulﬁde-suppressed and ﬂame-retardant
electrolyte operated at high temperatures by introducing an inert diluent 1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl (TTE) into the high-concentration
electrolyte (HCE). Li dendrites are also eﬃciently suppressed by the formed
LiF-rich protective layer. Furthermore, the shuttle eﬀect is mitigated by the
decreased solubility of LiPSs. At 60 °C, Li−S batteries using this nonﬂammable
ether-based electrolyte exhibit a high capacity of 666 mAh g−1 over 100 cycles at
a current rate of 0.2C, showing the greatly improved high-temperature
performance compared to batteries with traditional ether-based electrolytes.
The improved electrochemical performance across a range of temperatures and the enhanced safety suggest that the electrolyte has a
great practical prospect for safe Li−S batteries.
KEYWORDS: nonﬂammable, ether-based electrolyte, high temperature, inert diluter, Li−S batteries
■INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries show a wide development
prospect as next-generation energy-storage systems in virtue of
their high energy densities.1−4 The common sulfur cathode
demonstrates good electrochemical performance in ether-based
electrolytes beneﬁtting from their good compatibility.5,6 Among
the most commonly used solvents in Li−S batteries, 1,2-
dimethoxyethane (DME) of low viscosity and high dielectric
constant endows the electrolyte with high ionic conductivity and
the sulfur cathodes with complete redox reaction. 1,3-Dioxolane
(DOL), with relatively low LiPS solubility, is employed to build
a more stable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) ﬁlm to protect the
Li anode.7,8 Although the sulfur cathode reveals impressive
reversibility in ether-based electrolytes, some crucial points are
non-negligible: dissolution of LiPSs and relevant shuttle eﬀect.
Many eﬀorts to mitigate this problem have been focused on
tailoring the sulfur host structures and the components of the
electrolyte.9−14
However, DME/DOL ether-based electrolytes still face many
serious, but often ignored, challenges for practical use in Li−S
batteries in a wide operating temperature window. First, both of
them have low boiling and ﬂash points, thereby increasing the
risk of ﬁre at elevated temperatures.15,16 Moreover, gas evolution
is also observed during cycling in Li−S pouch batteries because
of their poor reduction stability, which leads to signiﬁcant
degradation of cell performance and safety issues.17,18
Furthermore, the elevated temperatures will lead to the higher
solubility of LiPSs and a more severe shuttle eﬀect.19 From a
practical perspective, the safety issues and the poor stability must
be addressed to pursue the real-world implementation of Li−S
batteries in a wide temperature window.
High-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) based on the
“solvent-in-salt” concept are promising strategies to decrease
the content of soluble LiPSs and fabricate Li−S batteries with
enhanced cycling stability.20−22 Unfortunately, the use of HCEs
will result in inferior rate performance and low cathode
utilization because of the low intrinsic ionic conductivity.23 In
addition, the cost associated with increasing salt concentration
hinders the practical application of HCEs. A similar but more
attractive strategy, localized high-concentration electrolytes
(LHCEs), has great potential to make up for its weaknesses.
LHCEs are achieved by diluting HCEs with “inert” diluents that
can be codissolved with the pristine solvent but do not dissolve
the salt. The Li+ solvate structure complexes in the HCE can
therefore be maintained, and the viscosity of LHCEs is reduced

Hydroﬂuorinated ether solvents with a low donor number are
good choices for the inert diluents. For instance, bis(2,2,2-
triﬂuoroethyl) ether has been used to fabricate LHCE and
suppress lithium dendrite growth.26 1H,1H,5H-octaﬂuoropen-
tyl-1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether27 and 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl-
2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl (TTE)23 are also proposed to serve as
diluters in LHCEs to suppress the LiPSs and prevent Li dendrite
growth. Among the hydroﬂuorinated ethers, the TTE has a
lower viscosity and the lowest lithium-solvating power and is a
good choice as an inert diluent for the HCE.28,29 Based on these
unique properties and low LiPS solubility, TTE-based LHCEs
have the potential to overcome the challenges of ether-based
electrolytes at high temperatures in Li−S batteries.23,30
Herein, we propose a nonﬂammable, organic, ether-based
LHCE obtained by introducing TTE into a base HCE with 5 M
lithium salt in G4 for safe Li−S batteries. Both solvent and
diluent have high boiling and ﬂash points, enabling the safe
operation of the cell under extreme test conditions. The
developed LHCE supports large, nodulelike Li particles and a
LiF-rich protective layer for stable lithium deposition/
dissolution. In addition, the reduced solubility of LiPSs in the
LHCE suppresses the parasitic reactions. The Li−S batteries
employing this electrolyte show stable cycling under a high
temperature of 60 °C, exhibiting a reversible capacity of 666
mAh g−1 over 100 cycles at a current rate of 0.2C. At room
temperature, an excellent cycling performance of 700 mAh g−1
can be achieved after 200 cycles at a rate of 0.1C. In summary,
the proposed LHCE helps achieve stable electrochemical
performance of Li−S batteries with advanced safety and superior
temperature adaptability.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Preparation of the Electrolyte. Tetraethylene glycol dimethyl
ether (G4, purity > 99%) and lithium bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfony)-
imide (LiTFSI, 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropyl (TTE, purity > 95%) and
lithium bis (ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, purity > 98%) were bought
from Fisher Scientiﬁc. All solvents were treated by molecular sieves with
a pore size of 3 Å before use. The preparation of all electrolytes was
carried out in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox. HCE was prepared by adding
lithium salt to G4 and stirring to yield a clear, viscous solution. The
LHCE was prepared by introducing diﬀerent volume ratios of TTE into
the HCE.
Preparation of Sulfur Cathodes. Sulfur powder (99.5%, Sigma-
Aldrich) was mixed with KB (KJ EC-600, US) by grinding. The mixture
was thermal-treated at 150 °C for 9 h, followed by treatment at 300 °C
for 3 h in a sealed steel reactor. The sulfur loading of the carbon−sulfur
nanocomposites (KB/S) was 68 wt % according to thermogravimetric
(TG) analysis. The cathodes were created by slurry casting onto a
carbon-coated aluminum foil. The cathode slurry consists of KB/S,
acetylene black, and poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) (PVDF) with a weight
ratio of 8:1:1 in NMP. Sulfur loading was about 1−1.2 mg cm−2 in each
cathode.
Physical Characterization. The viscosity of the electrolyte at
room temperature was determined using the viscometer (SNB-1).
Thermogravimetric (TG) curve was obtained on DSC (NETZSCH
STA449F3) under a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 in N2 ambience. A
LiPS solubility test was conducted by adding 1 M Li2S8 in diﬀerent
electrolytes. Raman spectra were recorded through a Raman
spectrometer (HORIBA Scientiﬁc LabRAM HR). For X-ray photo-
electron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, the cycled Li electrodes were
disassembled in a glovebox full of Ar to avoid exposure to air or
moisture and transferred to the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) analysis system. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
(EIS) method was performed to analyze the ionic conductivities of
electrolytes. Symmetric cells with two stainless steel (SS) electrodes
and ﬁve Celgard 2400 separators (PP, thickness 25 μm) with 100 μL of
electrolyte were assembled. The ionic conductivity calculated according
to the following equation is the conductivity of ions in the cell through

Electrochemical Measurements. The electrochemical character-
istic of sulfur cathode employing HCE and LHCE was evaluated in
CR2032 coin cells. A volume of 25 μL of electrolyte was used in each
Li−S cell for electrochemical cycling tests. All operations were carried
out in an Ar-ﬁlled glovebox. EIS was collected after cycling within a
frequency range from 100 kHz to 100 mHz under a voltage amplitude
of 5 mV by an electrochemical workstation (VMP3). Cyclic
voltammetry (CV) curves were obtained at 0.05 or 0.1 mV s−1 in a
voltage window of 1.0−3.0 V. Charging/discharging processes between
1.0 and 3.0 V were recorded on the LAND system (CT-2001A). The
quasi-equilibrium potential of the cells was evaluated by the GITT
technique with a current pulse of 0.05C for 0.5 h, followed by 1.5 h of
relaxation.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The solvate structure of Li-ions can be tuned by controlling the
lithium salt and its concentration and greatly inﬂuences the high-
temperature performance. By carefully selecting lithium salts
and solvents, a good base HCE of 5 M is achieved. In our study,
G4 is employed as the solvent due to its high boiling (275 °C)
and ﬂash points (241 °C) (Table S1) to enable safe operation at
high temperatures. The linear ether-based G4 eﬀectively
dissolves LiTFSI and LiPSs to support the good electrochemical
performance of Li−S batteries.8 Unfortunately, elevated
temperatures promote the solubility of LiPSs and cause a severe
shuttle eﬀect. As seen in Figure S1a, the speciﬁc capacity decays
from 1151 to 110 mAh g−1 in the dilute electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI)
after 100 cycles when operated at 0.2C and 60 °C. The LiPS
solubility test in Figure S1b shows that the Li2S8 is more likely to
dissolve in a dilute electrolyte. On the other hand, the solubility
of LiPSs in the highly concentrated electrolyte with 5 M lithium
salt is much lower and the shuttle eﬀect is signiﬁcantly
suppressed. The 100th capacity retention of the cell is therefore
improved to 396 mAh g−1 (Figure S1a).
Apart from the concentration, the chemical structure of the
lithium salt is also of great signiﬁcance on the interface
characteristics. It has been reported that both LiFSI and the
LiFSI−LiTFSI binary salts are conducive to develop a LiF-rich
layer on the electrode interface and improve electrochemical
performance.31,32 In an attempt to further improve the
performance, electrolytes with binary salt LiFSI−LiTFSI with
a whole concentration of 5 M were investigated (shown in
Figure S2). By adjusting the molar ratio of LiFSI/LiTFSI to 1:8,
cycling performance and the Coulombic eﬃciency are improved
over the LiTFSI electrolyte. However, increasing the amount of
LiFSI in the binary salt further results in a rapid capacity decay,
possibly caused by the fast growth of passivation layers (lithium
sulfates) owing to the continuous decomposition of FSI−
anions.33 Consequently, the high-concentration electrolyte
(HCE, 5 M lithium salt with a LiFSI/LiTFSI ratio of 1:8 in
G4) was selected as the base sample for the study.

high temperatures, the slow ion transport is a challenge for the

The wettability and ﬂammability of the organic electrolytes
are vital features for battery performance and safety. From the
results of Figure 2a, HCE shows a contact angle of up to 68.9°. It
reduces to 19.9° after introducing TTE with a volume ratio of
1:4, indicating that the wettability is improved by the addition of
TTE.38 The increased wettability improves Li-ion transport by
providing suﬃcient passageways and improves rate perform-
ance.39 Ignition and combustion experiments were conducted to
investigate the ﬂammability of organic electrolytes. Figure 2b−d
and the video in the Supporting Information show that both the
DE and HCE burn violently for 9 s once ignited. In contrast, the
LHCE is diﬃcult to ignite and extinguishes quickly. These
results demonstrate that the LHCE is a ﬂame-retardant
electrolyte that will ensure safe operation at high temperatures.
The electrochemical window of the electrolyte on stainless steel
(SS) was examined by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at 60
°C. The decomposition potential of all electrolytes is about 4 V
(Figure S9). Although the decomposition rate of LHCE (HCE/
TTE = 1:4) increases, the electrolyte is still electrochemically
stable in the operating window of 1−3 V.
Stable and dendrite-free Li deposition/dissolution character-
istics are also a key to build Li−S batteries of superexcellent
cycling performance. To investigate the deposition/dissolution
characteristic of Li, symmetrical batteries with two Li electrodes
and various electrolytes were assembled. The cycling properties
of Li/Li symmetric batteries employing various electrolytes were
evaluated at 60 °C, as shown in Figure 3a. In the HCE, the
voltage polarization of the cell is higher than that in the LHCE,
and it continuously increases with cycling until the cell fails. For
the LHCEs with a HCE/TTE ratio of 1:4, the overpotential
decreases and reaches a minimum owing to the high ionic
conductivity of that environment (Figure 3b), and it shows
better cycling stability compared to the other electrolytes. Cells
using other LHCEs exhibit continuously increasing polarization
and die after 100 h. Figure S10 shows the cycle performance and

The interphase composition is a major factor in determining
Li deposition/dissolution stability in a given electrolyte. The Li
electrode was retrieved after several deposition/dissolution
cycles with a limited capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. To probe the
chemical composition of the SEI layer, XPS of the Li electrodes
was performed. The detailed F 1s spectra show distinct C−F
species at 689.5 eV and LiF signal at 685.5 eV (Figure 4a).44
Based on the ratio of peak area, the percentage of LiF inside the
SEI ﬁlm in LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4) is 23.1%, but it is 12.3% in
that formed in the HCE. The increased LiF results from the
further promotion of reaction between TFSI−/FSI−and Li
metal in the LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4).45−47 The Li 1s spectra in
Figure 4b show peaks typical of Li2CO3, ROCO2Li, and LiF at
55.2, 54.5, and 56 eV, respectively.48 The LiF peak is more
prominent in the SEI layer formed in LHCE with a HCE/TTE
ratio of 1:4. However, the proportion of LiF is 13.8 and 10.2% in
1:1 and 1:5 LHCE samples, respectively (Figure S12a). It
indicates that the increasing TTE can promote the participation
of anions TFSI−/FSI−in the SEI ﬁlm. However, the extremely
low concentrations of TFSI−/FSI−further decrease the LiF
component. As a robust electrical insulator, LiF prevents
electron migration through the SEI layer but allows unhindered
Li+ diﬀusion,48,49 which is critical to building a stable SEI layer
without Li dendrites.50 In the O 1s XPS spectra (Figure 4c), C−
O species gives rise to the peak at 533.4 eV, while the peaks at
532.3 and 531.3 eV are attributed to RCOCO2Li and Li2CO3
species, respectively.51 The relative measure of Li2CO3
decreases signiﬁcantly in the SEI components formed in the
LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4), which may be caused by the
increasing LiF component. Consequently, the Li/Li symmetric
cell shows excellent cycling performance in the LHCE with a
HCE/TTE ratio of 1:4 by inhibiting the Li dendrite and
promoting the generation of a LiF-rich SEI layer on the Li anode.
The above results indicate that the developed LHCE is
nonﬂammable at high temperatures, exhibits lower LiPS
solubility, and stabilizes Li anodes in an electrochemical
environment. It, therefore, shows great application potential in
Li−S batteries within a wide temperature window. The
corresponding electrochemical features using diﬀerent electro-
lytes at both high temperatures and room temperature were
evaluated by coin cells. A composite cathode was prepared by
mixing KB (Ketjen Black) and S with a mass ratio of 3:7. The CV
curves at 60 °C for cells using HCE and LHCE (HCE/TTE =
1:4) present two cathodic peaks, which are assigned to the
reduction of S8 to long-order LiPSs and the long-order LiPSs to
Li2S2/Li2S, (Figure 5a). During the positive scan, one anodic
peak presents, which is associated with the decomposition of
lithium sulﬁde to elemental sulfur.11,52 It is evident that the
battery using LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4) exhibits sharper peaks
and reduced polarization, especially with the second cathodic
peak, indicating improved electrochemical reaction kinetics. It is
more evident that the second cathodic peak disappears in the CV
curves for the cell using the HCE cell at room temperature,
indicating that the TTE supports accelerated kinetics for the
reduction of the sulfur electrode (Figure S14). The second cycle
galvanostatic charge/discharge proﬁles for cells using diﬀerent
electrolytes at 0.1C show two discharge plateaus (Figure 5b).
Notably, the overpotential of the second discharge platform
reduces with increasing TTE volume ratio, suggesting that the
reduction of LiPSs is smooth in the LHCE. Figure 5c,d shows
the corresponding discharge capacity and discharge/charge
curves of the cell at various charge/discharge rates and 60 °C.
The discharge capacities at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0C are
1439, 988, 846, 756, 658, and 558 mAh g−1, respectively.
Multistep reaction plateaus are maintained at a rate up to 2C for
the cell using LHCE, while the discharge plateaus of the cell
using HCE disappear at above 0.5C (Figure S15). Compared to
HCE, LHCE maintains the same Li-ion solvate structure but
reduced ion concentration, facilitating ion transport and
improving the rate performance of the cells. The improved
rate performance of LHCE at room temperature in Figure S16
also suggests the improved interfacial kinetics due to the higher
ionic conductivity. The cyclability of batteries with diverse
electrolytes operated at 0.2C rate and 60 °C in Figure 5e shows
that the ﬁrst discharge capacity of the KB/S cathode in LHCE
(HCE/TTE = 1:4) is 1384 mAh g−1, and it reduces by about
51.9% after 100 cycles, yielding a speciﬁc capacity of 666 mAh
g−1 and Coulombic eﬃciency beyond 98%. However, the
capacity of the cell using HCE and LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:1) is
reduced to 481 and 559 mAh g−1, respectively. The cell with

of the low ion conductivity. The light yellow color of the
separator in the cell using LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4) suggests
the evident suppression for LiPSs and shuttle eﬀect (Figure
S17). Meanwhile, higher utilization of active material is achieved
with the LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4) system at 1C and 60 °C, as
shown in Figure S18. The typical electrolyte with 1 M LiTFSI in
DME/DOL has a similar lithium-ion concentration to the
proposed LHCE system with HCE/TTE = 1:4, however, the
high solubility of LiPSs results in rapid capacity decay and the
risk of ﬁre at high temperatures. Conversely, the LHCE with the
unique Li-ion solvate structure shows reduced solubility of
LiPSs and nonﬂammability, providing a good environment for a
stable sulfur electrode at high temperatures. At room temper-
ature, the KB/S cathode using LHCE (HCE/TTE = 1:4) reveals
a discharge capacity of 700 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at a rate of
0.1C, i.e., 35% reduction. However, the retention ratios of the
cell using LHCE with 1:5 and 1:1 (HCE to TTE by volume)
samples are 55 and 27%, respectively (Figure 5f). Compared
with the HCE, the LHCE with improved ion conductivity
signiﬁcantly increases the speciﬁc capacity. The above results
present the advantages of the developed LHCE over conven-
tional ether electrolytes. The excellent electrochemical perform-
ance at both high temperatures and room temperature
demonstrates that the nonﬂammable LHCE eﬀectively has
great prospects for Li−S batteries at varying temperatures.

room temperature and high temperatures.
■CONCLUSIONS
We developed a safe, ether-based LHCE for high-performance
Li−S batteries at both high temperatures (60 °C) and room
temperature. The risk of ﬁre is greatly reduced due to its
nonﬂammable nature and the solubility of LiPSs is suppressed
by reducing the activity of the solvents. The electrolyte provides
a stable electrochemical environment for Li deposition/
dissolution by forming large, nodulelike Li particles and a LiF-
rich SEI ﬁlm. The reduced LiPS solubility and stable Li anode
greatly reduce unwanted side reactions and the shuttle eﬀect is
evidently suppressed. At 60 °C, Li−S batteries using this
electrolyte deliver 666 mAh g−1 capacity over 100 cycles at a
current rate of 0.2C, a marked improvement over the traditional
ether-based electrolyte. In addition, a high residual capacity of
700 mAh g−1 can be achieved at a rate of 0.1C after 200 cycles at
room temperature, showing its practical prospect for safe Li−S
batteries in a wide temperature window.
