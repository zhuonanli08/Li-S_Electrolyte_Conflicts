ABSTRACT: Imide electrolyte salts have received increasing interest in lithium−
sulfur battery (LSB) research due to their oxidation capacity, thermal stability, and
cycling stability. Although the ionic conductivity of imide electrolytes is moderate, a
LSB with imide electrolyte salts exhibits improved charge−discharge (CD) cycling
capacity and Coulombic efficiency. Here, we applied an effective strategy to suppress
polysulfide dissolution by using a micelle-structured fluorinated ether-based additive
(F4EO2) with lithium bis(trifluorosulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and lithium bis-
(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) electrolyte salts. In contrast to the LiFSI electrolyte,
the LiTFSI imide electrolyte with F4EO2 additive shows a superior performance in
terms of its compatibility with the lithium (Li)-metal anode, electrochemical
stability, cycling stability, and high specific discharge capacity of ca. 1331 mAh/g
using the sulfur/carbon (S/C) composite//electrolyte//Li-metal battery config-
uration at 0.1C. The LSB performance is enhanced with an increase of the
concentration of the F4EO2 additive in the electrolyte solution. The results demonstrate that the electrolyte solution with the F4EO2
additive and LiTFSI salt improves the LSB performance by suppressing polysulfide dissolution and forming a favorable passivation
layer on the Li-metal anode, leading to a specific discharge capacity of ca. 1221 mAh/g after 20 cycles and 950 mAh/g after 100
cycles (for T5FDLiTFSI), respectively, with a Coulombic efficiency of ca. 99.98% and 99.99% after 20 and 100 CD cycles,
respectively.
KEYWORDS: micelle, fluorosulfonylimide, additive, polysulfide dissolution, ionic conductivity, Coulombic efficiency
1. INTRODUCTION
Lithium−sulfur batteries (LSBs) have received extensive
attention as next-generation energy storage technologies due
to their outstanding energy density (ca. 2600 Whk/g), low cost
(ca. 36−130 dollars k/Wh), abundance, ecofriendliness, and
high specific capacity (ca. 1675 mAh/g).1−3 Despite these
advantages, LSBs confront many critical challenges in practical
applications, such as the large volume change of the sulfur (S)
cathode, poor Coulombic efficiency, dissolved polysulfides
(which arise during the discharge process) in the electrolyte,
dendrite formation on the lithium (Li) anode, and low
conductivity of the S cathode.4−6 To overcome these
limitations, several strategies have been developed, mainly
focusing on the synthesis of new additives to improve the
electrochemical performance of LSBs and modifying the S
cathode to reduce the volume changes after cycling and
improve the conductivity.7−11 Moreover, considerable atten-
tion has been given to investigating and creating new binding
agents to enhance the performance of LSBs, as well as to
synthesizing catalytic materials and hierarchical porous carbon
for use in LSBs.12−15 However, LSBs still face a long journey to
achieve practical utility and commercialization. Therefore,
there is ample scope for further research in developing high-
energy-density LSBs through the engineering of electrolyte
additives, cosolvents, and modifications of the S cathode.
Until now, researchers have extensively studied nonaqueous
liquid electrolytes to improve the cycling performances of
LSBs.16−18 The addition of functional molecules and
cosolvents, such as ionic liquids and fluorinated ethers, can
effectively minimize side reactions between polysulfides and Li
metal and suppress dendrite formation by producing a good
solid−electrolyte interface (SEI) layer on the Li-metal
surface.19−22 Polysulfide additives, as functional molecules,
are used in nonaqueous LSBs to minimize the dissolution of
polysulfides by creating a concentration gradient of polysulfide
species at the cathode/electrolytes interface.23,24 Additionally,
lithium nitrate, a well-known electrolyte additive, develops a
good passivation layer on the Li-metal anode surface and
restricts the shuttle effect.25,26 However, side reactions among
these additives, electrolytes, and electrodes are still uncertain.27

Highly concentrated electrolytes, solvate ionic liquids, and
solvents in salt concepts are being significantly executed to
overcome these challenges.28−31 Recently, highly concentrated
imide-based electrolyte solutions have been reported to reduce
side reactions in battery systems. Specifically, solutions
containing 3 M LiTFSI, 7 M LiTFSI, and 3 M LiFSI in a
1,3-dioxane (DOL)/dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent have
shown significant promise in this regard.32,33 However, despite
their excellent electrochemical stability, ability to form a
favorable SEI layer, and relatively high σ values, highly
concentrated and viscous electrolytes would not be a good
choice for commercial applications. Therefore, many research-
ers have used diluted electrolyte solutions with poorly
coordinating solvents, such as hexyl methyl ether in DOL,
fluoroalkyl ether in DME, hydrofluoroethers, etc., to reduce
polysulfide dissolution during charge−discharge (CD) cy-
cling.34,35 Among all of the solvents, hydrofluoroethers show
excellent performance due to their moderate polarity and low
solvation ability to Li metal.36 The use of hydrofluoroether-
type solvents significantly reduces the corrosion of the Li
anode during CD cycling and improves the cell safety,
electrochemical stability, and Coulombic efficiency of the
LSBs.36 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether
(TTE), a hydrofluoroether solvent, is commonly used in LSBs,
even though it can barely dissolve Li salts.8,37 Therefore, a
cosolvent is always used with TTE to achieve better LSB
performances.38 In our previous work, we reported a new
technique to suppress polysulfide dissolution using micelle-
based additives with TTE and observed a unique solvation
mechanism in which Li ion easily coordinated with additives
and formed micelle structures.8 The prepared electrolyte
solution (0.5 M LiTFSI in F3EO1/TTE) showed excellent
compatibility with the Li-metal anode, formed a uniform SEI
layer on the Li-metal anode surface after CD cycling, and
significantly suppressed polysulfide dissolution.8 However, the
effect of the anions (TFSI and FSI anions) and
1,1,1,2,2,3,3,4,4-nonafluoro-6-(2-methoxyethoxy)hexane
(F4EO2) additive on the overall LSB battery performance was
not significantly studied in our previous work. There is
currently a lack of adequate research on the design and
synthesis of highly fluorinated electrolyte additives, as well as
the impact of additive ratios on the electrolyte compositions.
Herein we have synthesized a highly fluorinated ether-based
bifunctional (lithiophilic head and fluorinated lithiophobic tail)
electrolyte additive, F4EO2, for LSB application. Furthermore,
we assessed the influence of the completely new electrolyte
solutions on the LSB performance by varying the composition
of TTE, F4EO2, LiTFSI, LiFSI, and DOL, respectively. We also
discovered that the micelle-structured F4EO2 additive
reinforced the Li-ion-transport behavior and prevented
polysulfide dissolution by constructing a stable SEI layer on
the Li-anode surface (shown in Scheme 1). Additionally, the
LSBs exhibited a superior cycling performance when
harnessing the electrolyte solutions containing LiTFSI salt in
comparison to LiFSI salt. The TFSI anion, produced from the
LiTFSI salt, exhibits greater electrochemical stability compared
to the FSI anion generated from the LiFSI salt. This enhances
the electrochemical stability of the Li salt toward various
reactive intermediates generated during the CD cycling of LSB.
The surface morphology of the cycled electrodes was analyzed
using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), respectively.

Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without any additional

2.4. Preparation of Electrolyte Solutions, S/C Composite
Electrodes, and Coin Cells. To prepare electrolyte solutions, we
used TTE, DOL, LiFSI, LiTFSI, and F4EO2, and the solutions are
denoted as TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSI-FSI, and T5FDLiFSI. The details of the composition of
the electrolyte solutions are listed in Table 1. In addition, the S/C
composite electrode was prepared according to our previous work.8
At first, ketjen carbon black and S were mixed with a ratio of 3:7 (wt
%) using a mechanical ball mixer (CryoMill) for 20 min. Thereafter,
the prepared mixture was sealed in a container (Teflon) and annealed
at ca. 150−155 °C for 20 h in a furnace to form the S/C composite.
To prepare the electrode slurry, S/C composite, denka black, and
PVDF binder were taken with a weight ratio of 6:3:1. Then 10 wt %
NMP solvent was added, and a pestle and mortar were used to mix
the slurry. The electrode slurry was doctor-bladed on a current
collector (aluminum foil, 20 μm) and dried overnight at 40 °C. The
prepared S/C composite cathode disk was additionally dried under a
vacuum for 20 h and transferred into an argon-filled glovebox. The S/
C cathode disk (diameter 12.7 mm; 0.75 mg/cm2 S loading), the Li-
metal anode disk (diameter 14 mm), an appropriate amount of as-
prepared electrolyte solution, and a Celgard separator (diameter 18
mm) were used to assemble the CR2032 coin cells. The CD capacity
of the coin cells was tested by using a Maccor series 4000 cell tester at
voltages of 1.7 and 2.8 V (at 0.1C rate) at 30 °C.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Electrochemical Performance and Ionic Con-
ductivity of the Prepared Electrolyte Solutions. The CV
results of the LSBs using both the prepared and standard
electrolyte solutions are shown in Figures 1 and S2a,
respectively. The voltage range was 1.8−2.8 V, and the scan
rate was 0.1 mA/s. The LSBs with the prepared electrolyte
solutions showed one broad reduction current peak in the
cathodic scan (Figure 1), which is associated with the solid−
solid phase transition (S/C to Li2S).39 On the other hand, the
LSB with the standard electrolyte solution showed two
reduction peaks at ca. 2.3 and 2.0 V (Figure S2a), which
correspond to the solid−liquid (elemental S to dissolved Li2S8)
and liquid−solid (dissolved Li2S6 to Li2S2 or Li2S) phase
transitions.40−42 Therefore, the prepared electrolyte solutions
can suppress polysulfide dissolution.
The slight overpotential in the initial cathodic and anodic
sweeps of the LSBs with the T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI
electrolyte solutions is attributed to the polarization caused by
the phase transition in the conversion−dissolution−diffusion
process of S and polysulfides.40 More importantly, the EIS
curves of the LSBs with T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI
electrolyte solutions changed significantly after CV cycling
(Figure S3), which can be attributed to the less stable FSI
anion present in the electrolyte solutions. However, the LSBs
with a T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte solution showed excellent
cycling performance, with well-overlapping anodic and
cathodic peaks in the CV curves, and no changes were
observed in EIS after CV cycling, indicating low polarization
and high reversibility in subsequent cycles.40−42 Additionally,
as the amount of F4EO2 additive in the electrolyte solution
increased, the LSB showed less polarization and good
reversibility in the CV cycles, demonstrating the positive effect
of the F4EO2 additive and LITFSI salt on both the S and Li-
metal electrodes, resulting in a better cell performance.

were ca. 1.39 × 10−4, 2.14 × 10−4, 1.59 × 10−4, 1.50 × 10−4,

T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI-

°C. These results are comparable to or greater than other

all of the electrolytes exhibited higher σ values. At 80 °C,
TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSI-FSI, and T5FDLiFSI electrolyte solutions

respectively, which is due to the change of the viscosity of the
electrolyte solutions with the increase of temperature.43−45
Furthermore, the addition of the F4EO2 additive to the
electrolyte solution improved the σ value of the solution by
forming larger complexes that reduce the overall ion diffusion
path and enhance the Li+ conductivity.8
3.2. Cell Performance Based on the Composition of
the Electrolytes. The cell performance of the prepared
electrolyte solutions was evaluated using a coin-cell config-
uration of the Li//electrolyte//S/C composite cathode at 30
°C. S was chosen as an active cathode material due to its high
theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh/g), low cost,
abundance, and safety.1 The CD capacity was measured over
a voltage range of 1.7−2.8 V at 0.1C (current density, 1.42
mA/cm2). Parts a and b of Figure 2 depict galvanostatic CD
plots of the LSBs at the first cycle and after 20 cycles,
respectively, while Figure 2c displays the variation of the
specific discharge capacity (Csp) concerning the number of CD
cycles at 0.1C. The actual charging capacities of the LSB cells
with TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSI-FSI, and T5FDLiFSI electrolyte solutions were
ca. 0.81, 1.23, 1.05, 0.93, 0.95, and 0.94 mAh, respectively, at
the first CD cycle (Figure 2a) and ca. 0.65, 1.17, 1.08, 1.07,
0.66, and 0.55 mAh, respectively, after 20 cycles (Figure 2b).
In addition, the Csp values of the LSB cell with these electrolyte
solutions were ca. 866, 1331, 1124, 1013, 1025, and 982 mAh/
g, respectively, at the first CD cycle (Figure 2c), which are
comparable to or higher than other reported LSB battery
systems (Table S1). Furthermore, the Csp values of LSBs with
TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSI-FSI, and T5FDLiFSI electrolytes were ca. 691,
1222, 1129, 1128, 692, and 576 mAh/g, respectively, after 20
CD cycles (Figure 2c). The LSB with a TDLiTFSI electrolyte
solution (without F4EO2 additive) showed lower capacity than
that with other electrolyte solutions due to polysulfide
dissolution and shuttling.8 Also, the polysulfide dissolution
and shuttling were consistently suppressed as the amount of
F4EO2 additive increased in the electrolyte solution, indicating
that the F4EO2 additive positively affects the movement of the
Li ion and the anode passivation in the LSB system. The
performance of the LSBs with the prepared electrolyte
solutions is consistent with their electrochemical stability
(CV cycling) and σ results. More importantly, the LSBs with
T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, and T20FDLiTFSI electrolyte
solutions showed excellent capacity retention (Figure 2c) and
rate capability and reversibility (Figure S5), even though only a
0.5 M LiTFSI salt concentration was used (lower than the
standard salt concentration for LSB), which may correspond to
the formation of a good SEI layer by the F4EO2 additive on the
surface of the Li anode and protect the degradation of the
TFSI anion. The Coulombic efficiencies of the LSBs with the
TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, and T20FDLiTFSI
electrolyte solutions were ca. 99.32, 99.98, 99.93, and 99.92%,
respectively, after 20 cycles (Figure 2d). Correspondingly, the
Coulombic efficiencies were significantly decreased after 20
cycles of the LSBs with T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI
electrolyte solutions and were ca. 88.18 and 88.79%,
respectively. However, the LSB cell with the T5FDLiTFSI
electrolyte solution exhibited superior performances in both
the initial Csp and Coulombic efficiency compared to the LSB
cells with T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI electrolyte
solutions. This improvement can be attributed to the presence
of different anions in the electrolyte solutions, leading to
distinct surface chemistry on the Li-metal anode after CD
cycling. The TFSI anion demonstrates higher chemical stability
compared to the FSI anion, thus contributing to the enhanced
electrochemical stability of the Li salt against various reactive
intermediates formed during the CD cycle of LSBs.32 The LSB
with the T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte solution showed good
cycling stability (ca. Csp 948 mAh/g) and Coulombic efficiency
(ca. 99.99%) after 100 cycles (Figure S6), and the obtained
results are comparable to or better than other reported
LSBs.46−48
Figure S7 displays the results of the solubility test for S, Li2S,
and Li2Sx (x = 2, 4, 6, 8) in the T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte. The
experimental details are provided in the SI. The measurements
indicate that polysulfide has very limited solubility in the
electrolyte, with solubility up to Li2S6. Even at Li2S8, the
solubility of polysulfide in the T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte remains
significantly lower than that in the DOL-based electrolyte,
where the solubility of the S species can reach above 6 M in a
typical ether solvent electrolyte.49 These findings suggest that
polysulfide dissolution is effectively suppressed by the
T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte, which is consistent with the electro-
chemical performance of the T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte.
3.3. Morphological Analysis of the Electrodes. The
surfaces of the electrodes were analyzed using SEM and XPS to
better understand the impact of the F4EO2 additive and
anionic structure of the imide salts in LSBs. Prior to SEM and
XPS analyses, all of the cycled Li-metal anodes were washed
with DOL and dried for 24 h. Parts a−g of Figure 3 display the
SEM images of Li-metal anodes before and after 20 CD cycles.
The surface of the bare Li-metal anode was neat and smooth
(Figure 3a). In contrast, the Li-metal anodes with T5FDLiTF-
SI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI electrolyte solutions exhibited rough
and irregular surfaces after CD cycling because of the regrowth
of SEI layers and dissolution (Figure 3f,g),32 whereas the Li-
metal anode with a T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte solution exhibited
a much smoother, less creaky, and relatively compact surface
(Figure 3c). These results suggest that Li-metal anodes easily
degrade in the LiFSI-containing electrolyte solution, which is
an important signature of less stable SEI layers formed on the
L-metal anode with LiFSI electrolyte salts in LSBs. This is also
very consistent with the other reported LSB system.32
However, the results from CD cycling and morphology tests
indicate that LiTFSI and F4EO2 additive have excellent
capability to form a good SEI layer on the Li-metal anode
by tailoring the structures and morphology of SEI layers, which
is better than that of the LiFSI electrolyte salt in the LSBs.

was also characterized by SEM to evaluate the effect of the

produced from the degradation of the FSI anion.32 The
intensity of the LiF peak consistently decreased with the
increased amount of LiTFSI salt and F4EO2 additive in the
electrolyte solutions (Figure 6a), suggesting that, compared to
the TFSI anion, the FSI anion can easily be degraded and form
LiF. In addition, one extra peak appeared at ca. 689.20 and
690.01 eV for the cycled Li anodes with T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and
T5FDLiFSI electrolyte solutions, which may be responsible for
the presence of the S−F group.50 The obtained results
indicated that an electrolyte additive, F4EO2, prevents
degradation of the electrolyte salt anion during CD cycling.
The S 2p spectra of the cycled Li-metal anodes in
TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, T20FDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSILiFSI, and T5FDLiFSI electrolytes are shown
in Figure 6b. The cycled Li anodes in TDLiTFSI,
T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, and T20FDLiTFSI electrolytes
showed intense peaks in ca. 163 eV, which can be associated
with polysulfide (Li2Sx) on the surface of the anode.51,52
Therefore, S-containing chemical species on the cycled anodes
in TDLiTFSI, T5FDLiTFSI, T10FDLiTFSI, and T20FDLiTF-
SI electrolytes are associated with polysulfide formation
(reversible in LSBs).51 On the other hand, the Li-metal
anodes in T5FDLiTFSI-FSI and T5FDLiFSI electrolytes
exhibited very strong sulfide peaks (ca. 161.14 eV, irreversible
in LSBs) in the XPS spectra.51 These obtained XPS results can
be used to explain the cycling performance of the LSBs with as-
prepared electrolyte solutions. The LSBs in T5FDLiTFSI,
T10FDLiTFSI, and T20FDLiTFSI electrolytes, the dominant
discharge products, are reversible lithium polysulfides. There-
fore, these LSBs showed a good cycling performance compared
to the LSBs with T5FDLiTFSILiFSI and T5FDLiFSI electro-
lytes. In the LSBs with LiFSI salt, many side reactions occurred
and formed many irreversible chemical products on the Li-
metal anode surface, which directly affects the cycling
performance of the LSBs.

4. CONCLUSIONS
We have synthesized a highly fluorinated ether-based bifunc-
tional micelle electrolyte additive, F4EO2, and applied a unique
strategy to suppress polysulfide dissolution in LSBs. We have
also prepared different electrolyte solutions using F4EO2, TTE,
LiTFSI, LiTFSI, and DOL, respectively, for LSB application.
The LiTFSI imide electrolyte with F4EO2 additive showed
excellent compatibility with the Li-metal anode, good electro-
chemical stability, cycling stability, and a high Csp of ca. 1331
mAh/g with a battery configuration of S/C composite//
electrolytes//Li metal at 0.1C compared to that of the LiFSI
electrolyte. The chemically unstable FSI anion reacted with the
Li-metal anode, depositing a large amount of LiF and sulfides
on the surface and reducing the overall cell performance.
Concurrently, the micelle-structured F4EO2 additive signifi-
cantly improves the battery performance. The obtained results
demonstrated that the LSB in the T5FDLiTFSI electrolyte
showed excellent cycling performance by suppressing poly-
sulfide dissolution along with excellent capacity retention and
Coulombic efficiency (99.98%) after 20 CD cycles. Therefore,
it is evident that our technique, along with the F4EO2 additive,
holds great potential for the advancement of high-performance
LSBs in the future. In our future endeavors, we plan to enhance
the battery performance by optimizing the concentration of
salt in the electrolyte solutions and conducting battery tests.
