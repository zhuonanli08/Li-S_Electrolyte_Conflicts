A B S T R A C T
Lithium metal batteries are promising candidates for next-generation high-energy batteries, and ether-based 
electrolytes have garnered considerable attention due to their excellent compatibility with lithium metal an­
odes. While Li-sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) batteries exhibit the dissolution and shuttle effect of poly­
sulfides in conventional ether-based electrolytes, significantly impairing their performance. Herein, we propose a 
novel electrolyte with hierarchical solvation structure based on fluorine-free all-cyclic-ether solvents-2- 
Methyltetrahydrofuran (MeTHF)/1,4-Dioxane (DOX) with LiNO3 additive for Li-SPAN batteries. The unique 
solvation structure of this electrolyte, characterized by the presence of contact ion pairs and aggregates, pro­
motes uniform lithium deposition and effectively protects the anode. Moreover, the electrolyte effectively in­
hibits the dissolution of polysulfides while generating a robust electrode–electrolyte interface. Consequently, the 
Li||SPAN full cell employing this electrolyte has good cycling performance, retaining a specific capacity of 440 
mAh g−1 after 800 cycles. Furthermore, the full cell with high SPAN loadings still maintains 72.46% capacity 
retention after 300 cycles at 0.5 C.
1. Introduction
Amidst the global energy transition, energy storage technology is 
increasingly emerging as a central focus within the energy sector. 
Lithium-ion battery (LIB) development has matured to a commercialized 
stage, and these batteries are extensively employed in portable elec­
tronics, electric vehicles, and grid energy storage systems [1,2]. 
Although the mechanisms and applications of LIBs are now 
well-established, their energy density typically remains below 
250 Wh kg−1, approaching its theoretical limit and struggling to meet 
future energy demands. Coupling the sulfur cathode (1675 mAh g−1) 
with a lithium metal anode (3860 mAh g−1) has attracted significant 
research interest due to the immense potential for high-energy-density 
storage [3,4]. Additionally, sulfur offers the advantages of abundant 
reserves, low cost, and environmental benefits. Nevertheless, the sulfur 
cathode faces several challenges: the ‘shuttle effect’ due to lithium 
polysulfides (LiPSs) dissolving in the electrolyte, poor electronic and 
ionic conductivity of elemental sulfur and its discharge products, and 
significant volume expansion of the cathode [5,6]. However, when a 
suitable electrolyte is used, the transformation between sulfurized 
polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) and Li2S can proceed via a solid-phase 
pathway, avoiding the formation of soluble LiPSs and enhancing 
cycling stability. In addition, the conductive carbon structure retained 
within SPAN provides a continuous electronic conduction pathway, 
significantly improving the electronic conductivity issues commonly 
observed in traditional sulfur/carbon composite cathodes. Its internal 
microporous structure also facilitates Li+ transport, enhancing reaction 
kinetics. Furthermore, the SPAN cathode exhibits inherent flexibility 
and elasticity during cycling, enabling it to buffer the volumetric 
changes of sulfur. This maintains the electrode's structural integrity and

In lithium metal batteries (LMBs), there are still problems such as 
uncontrollable safety due to dendrite growth, low coulombic efficiency, 
and rapid capacity degradation [7–9]. The use of ether electrolytes-due 
to their inherent compatibility with lithium metal anodes and superior 
ionic conductivity-while beneficial, brings its own set of challenges. 
[10–12]. When integrated with SPAN cathodes, these electrolytes 
facilitate LiPSs dissolution and diffusion, which ultimately precipitate 
irreversible lithium anode reactions and capacity decay [10–13]. 
Consequently, there is potential for the electrolyte to be enhanced in the 
following aspects to facilitate an improvement in battery performance 
[14]. First, the stability of anode solid electrolyte interface (SEI) is 
improved through the formation of a uniform and dense SEI to reduce 
the contact between lithium metal and LiPSs. Second, the formation of a 
robust cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) at the cathode to reduce the 
dissolution of LiPSs. Third, the solubility of the electrolyte for LiPSs is 
directly reduced by electrolyte design.
In recent years, there has been a notable development in the field of 
electrolyte engineering, with the introduction of innovative electrolytes. 
These novel electrolytes have emerged as a solution to the challenges 
posed by the formation of inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interface (SEI), 
which have been identified as effective methods of protecting lithium 
metal anodes [15,16]. Pang and co-workers [17] found that an 
increasing lithium salt concentration reduces polysulfide solubility and 
reactivity 
with 
the 
anode, 
highlighting 
the 
potential 
of 
high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) for SPAN-based batteries. How­
ever, the HCEs are difficult to be industrialized due to the disadvantages 
of high viscosity leading to low ionic conductivity and high cost [18,19]. 
To address this, fluorinated solvent can be used as inert solvents [20,21], 
lowering viscosity while preserving the solvation structure of one Li+
coordinated to one FSI- (CIP) and one Li+ coordinated to two and more 
FSI- (AGG). However, conventional fluorinated diluents are costly and 
environmentally problematic. To mitigate these issues, weakly solvating 
solvents characterized by low polarity present a viable alternative. They 
are a type of solvent with a poor polarizability that binds less to the LiPSs 
than within the LiPSs. Consequently, the solubility of LiPS in the elec­
trolyte decreases, thereby the shuttle effect is significantly suppressed 
[22]. On the other hand, the solvent in the weakly solvating electrolytes 
(WSEs) can maintain a solvation structure of more CIPs and AGGs 
similar to HCEs/localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs), due 
to its similarly weak binding ability to Li+, generating a robust SEI to 
protect the lithium metal anode [23,24]. Additionally, another common 
strategy involves the introduction of additives. For instance, Bi and 
co-workers [25] found that lithium nitrate (LiNO3) additives help pro­
tect lithium metal anodes by altering the solvation structure of LiPSs and 
reducing their reactivity. LiNO3 also enhances battery performance by 
optimizing both the SEI and CEI [26–28].
Herein, we design an innovative electrolyte based on a dual weakly 
solvating electrolyte (DWSE) with 2-Methyltetrahydrofuran (MeTHF)/ 
1,4-Dioxane (DOX) (3:2 v/v). With appropriately increased concentra­
tion of lithium salts and the introduction of LiNO₃, a unique hierarchical 
solvation structure is formed, characterized by a primary solvation shell 
rich in FSI- and NO3
- , while MeTHF and DOX occupy different positions 
within the solvation structure due to their different solvation power. The 
part of cosolvent DOX, located in the outermost layer, acts as a diluent, 
maintaining the anion-rich solvation structure. By regulating the 
composition of the electrolyte through synergistic effects, not only was 
the dissolution of polysulfides in the electrolyte further suppressed, but 
stable SEI and CEI layers were also formed. The weakly solvating power 
of the chosen solvents was demonstrated through density functional 
theory (DFT) simulations, which analyzed the electrostatic potential and 
Li⁺ binding energy. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations highlight 
differences in the solvation structures of Li⁺ within electrolytes, with 
anion-rich configurations protecting the electrode by influencing the 
composition of the SEI and CEI. Thus, the Li||SPAN full cells with 
modified DWSE deliver superior cycle performance with over 73.34% 
capacity retention (CR) after 800 cycles. Even with SPAN loading 
increased to 3.8 mg cm−2, the full cells maintained a 72.46% CR after 
300 cycles at a 0.5 C current density. This work demonstrates the great 
versatility of modified DWSE in Li-SPAN battery. This is a significant 
development in the field, and the battery is also low-cost and environ­
mentally friendly.
2. Results and discussion
The conventional ether electrolyte commonly for Li-SPAN batteries 
is 1 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in 1,2-dimethoxyethane 
(DME), serving as the blank electrolyte (DME). However, DME has a 
strong binding energy with LiPSs leading to severe dissolution of LiPSs 
in the electrolyte, which leads to a rapid degradation of the battery 
capacity [13,29]. The solvating power of electrolytes is contingent on 
molecular structure, with factors such as steric hindrance and intrinsic 
properties, including dielectric constant and donor number [30,31]. As 
shown in Fig. 1a, the cyclization of DME to 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) slightly 
reduces the solvating power. Further, DOL forms tetrahydrofuran (THF) 
by removing an oxygen molecule coordinated to Li+ or 1,3-dioxane (1, 
3-DOX) by lengthening the carbon chain of DOL, thus reducing the 
power of both. Moreover, adding a methyl group to THF as 2-methylte­
trahydrofuran (MeTHF), which increases its steric hindrance, has a 
weaker solvating power, and thus was selected as one of the solvents. 1, 
4-dioxane (DOX), on the other hand, has a weak solvating power due to 
its very low solubility for lithium salts because it has a very low 
dielectric constant (2.2) [32]. The weakly solvating power of the 
selected solvents was further validated by density functional theory 
(DFT) calculations, including electrostatic potential distributions and Li⁺ 
binding energies for DME, MeTHF, and DOX (Fig. 1c). Therefore, we 
chose to mix the MeTHF and DOX in a certain ratio as solvents. To 
determine the optimal solvent ratio, a series of electrolytes were pre­
pared by dissolving 1 M LiFSI in MeTHF/DOX mixtures with volume 
ratios of 7:3, 3:2, and 5:5, denoted as MTD73, MTD32, and MTD55, 
respectively. Otherwise, 1 M LiFSI was dissolved in MeTHF pure solvent 
as a blank group. Subsequently, half-cells were assembled in Li||Cu and 
Li||Li configurations to examine how different electrolytes affect the 
stability and reversibility of cells [33,34]. As demonstrated in Figure S1 
and S2, at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an area capacity of 1 mAh 
cm−2, the synergistic interaction between the two solvents resulted in 
improved performance compared to the blank group. Moreover, the 
MTD32 electrolyte shows superior performance in these ratios. There­
fore, a volume ratio of MeTHF/DOX of 3:2 was selected as the optimal 
ratio for the electrolyte, and this ratio was selected for all formulations 
of the electrolyte. Increasing the lithium salt concentration appropri­
ately can promote the entry of FSI⁻ into the primary solvation sheath of 
Li+, inducing the preferential decomposition of anions at the interface to 
form an LiF-rich EEI. Additionally, introducing LiNO3 allows NO3
- to also 
participate in the primary solvation sheath, leading to the formation of 
inorganic components such as Li3N and LiNxOy at the EEI. Overall, the 
distinct solvating power of the two anions and solvent molecules 
contribute to the formation of a hierarchical solvation structure 
(Fig. 1b), which is conducive to the construction of a stable interface. We 
prepared solutions by dissolving 1 M and 2 M LiFSI in the solvent at the 
ratios, labeled as 1MTD and 2MTD, respectively. Further, we added 1 wt 
% LiNO3 to 2MTD to to regulate a more favorable solvation structure, 
denoted as 2MTD1 (The reasons for selecting 2MTD1 as the optimal 
electrolyte are given in Note 1).

It is well known that the solvents of DWSE have low binding energy 
resulting in low ionic conductivity [41]. Despite the low ionic conduc­

demonstrate favorable outcomes (Figs. 2e and S12). The tLi+ for the DME

The results demonstrate that the 2MTD1 electrolyte has an i0 of

Li+ desolvation and subsequent diffusion through the SEI represent 
critical steps in the lithium deposition process. This step determines the 
rate at which the energy barrier is overcome and subsequently in­
fluences the deposition morphology of the SEI. Li||Li symmetric cells 
were assembled to conduct electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS) measurements [43] at different temperatures and activation en­
ergies (denoted as Ea1 and Ea2, respectively) were calculated at both 
stages based on SEI layer (RSEI) and ion transfer (Rct) resistances 
(Fig. 2f-g). Upon Li+
diffusion through the SEI layer, Ea1 is 
74.33 kJ mol−1, 69.67 kJ mol−1, 67.59 kJ mol−1, and 66.84 kJ mol−1 
for DME, 1MTD, 2MTD, and 2MTD1 electrolytes, respectively. The 
reduction in Ea1 for DWSE compared to the DME electrolyte suggests 
that the solvation structure influences the composition and structure of 
the SEI. Moreover, the further decrease in Ea1 following the addition of 
LiNO3 indicates that LiNO3 contributes to the formation of a stable SEI. 
Moreover, the value of Ea2 (65.35 kJ mol−1) for DME is significantly 
higher than 1MTD (60.11 kJ mol−1), 2MTD (55.54 kJ mol−1), and 
2MTD1 (57.78 kJ mol−1). This indicates that DWSE reduces the des­
olvation energy barrier and accelerates Li+ transport kinetics, signifi­
cantly impacting subsequent SEI formation and battery performance.
Understanding the deposition morphology of lithium metal is key to 
assessing the interfacial stability between the anode and the electrolyte. 
Li||Cu half-cells were assembled to deposit lithium on Cu foil under 
conditions of 1 mA cm−2, 6 mAh cm−2 condition. The lithium deposition 
morphology was then examined using a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) (Fig. 3a-d). For the Li deposited in DME electrolyte, the dendritic 
lithium metals can be clearly observed, leading to low CE and promoting 
severe side reactions. In contrast, in the 1MTD electrolyte, lithium is 
mainly deposited on Cu in a bulk form, but there are sparse and porous 
holes to increase the contact area with electrolytes. Upon increasing the 
salt concentration, lithium dendrites decrease and show uniform depo­
sition. Surprisingly, with the addition of LiNO3, lithium exhibits a 
smooth and dense deposition layer, consistent with the excellent 
compatibility of the 2MTD1 electrolyte with lithium anode. Subse­
quently, the cross-sectional morphology of lithium deposited on Cu was 
examined under identical conditions (Fig. 3e-h). The thicknesses of the 
deposited lithium are 50.0 μm, 47.3 μm, 45.8 μm, and 37.5 μm for DME, 
1MTD, 2MTD and 2MTD1 electrolytes, respectively.
The different chemical compositions of SEI greatly influence the 
morphology of lithium metal anode, so Li||Li symmetric batteries were 
assembled and after 20 cycles at 1 mAh cm−2, and the composition of SEI 
was evaluated with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 3i-k 
and S15). From the C 1 s spectra, C─O, C––O, C─F, and Li2CO3 are 
detected on the surface of the SEI at 286.5 eV, 287.9 eV, 288.7 eV, and 
289.9 eV. In the DME electrolyte, the content of Li2CO3 and C-O is over- 
represented, which increases the interfacial impedance of the battery 
and also aggravates the side reactions with electrolytes [44]. The con­
tent of organic species in the modified electrolyte was significantly 
reduced, indicating the suppression of electrolyte decomposition. As 
revealed by the F 1 s spectra, a significant increase in the LiF (684.6 eV) 
content in DWSE is useful to reduce the continuous electrolyte decom­
position and to promote uniform nucleation and deposition of lithium. 
In the DME electrolytes, Li3N (398.6 eV) mainly originates from the 
decomposition of FSI-. It can be observed that the content of Li3N in­
creases in DWSE, indicating that the solvated structure of the anion-rich 
electrolyte affects the composition of the SEI. In contrast, the electrolyte 
after the introduction of the LiNO3 additive shows the disappearance of 
N-SOx and the presence of LiNxOy and LiNO2, which are favorable 
components with high ionic conductivity, suggesting a preferential 
decomposition of LiNO3 [33]. An SEI containing LiF, Li3N, LiNxOy, and 
LiNO2 facilitates uniform lithium deposition, suppresses continuous 
electrolyte decomposition, and mitigates parasitic reactions between the 
electrolyte and the anode, thereby enhancing the cycling stability of the 
battery.
One effective strategy to mitigate the polysulfide shuttle and 
enhance cycling performance involves constructing a robust CEI on the 
cathode, thereby suppressing polysulfide formation [45]. Therefore, Li|| 
SPAN cells were assembled for self-discharge measurement (Fig. 4a-b 
and S16). With the DME electrolyte, the cells showed severe 
self-discharge with CEs of 69.92% and 89.17% when rested at 1.5 and 
2 V, respectively, which indicates excessive generation of polysulfides 
and serious loss of reactive material. The CE was 99.66% and 100.35% 
in 1MTD electrolyte and 100.00% and 100.07% in 2MTD electrolyte at 
1.5 V and 2.0 V, respectively. Negligible self-discharge can be observed 
in DWSE, demonstrating that weakly solvating solvents can effectively 
generate protective CEI. In the 2MTD electrolyte, the CE of the cell was 
greater than 100% at 104.37% and 103.17%, respectively. This phe­
nomenon can be attributed to the electrolyte can dissolve the residual 
CEI shells containing dead SPAN after resting [46]. The cathode 
impedance plays a critical role in governing the redox kinetics and po­
larization behavior of the cells. To test the impedance of SPAN||SPAN 
symmetric cells in different electrolytes, EIS was employed. This is due 
to the fact that the rate of the total process is determined by the slowest 
process. From Fig. 4c, it can be learned that in DME, 1MTD, and 2MTD 
electrolytes, the charge transfer impedance is too large in the mid-high 
frequency range. However, the 2MTD1 electrolyte has a moderate 
impedance throughout the Li+ diffusion process, indicating better 
compatibility between the electrolyte and the cathode.
To explore the reason of impedance changes, it is necessary to 
characterize the components of CEI. Li||SPAN full cells were assembled, 
and XPS was utilized to analyze the surface composition of the SPAN 
cathode after 20 cycles (Fig. 4d). Analysis of the F 1 s spectra reveals a 
markedly higher LiF content (684.8 eV) in DWSE compared with that in 
the DME electrolyte. LiF has been reported to suppress the dissolution of 
polysulfides [46]. And a small amount of organic component C-F 
(688.1 eV) in the 2MTD1 electrolyte can help to accommodate the 
volume expansion of organic cathodes [47]. Peaks at 166.9, 168.2, 
169.2, and 170.2 eV in the S 2p spectra (Fig. 4d) correspond to 
poly-thiosulfate, representing parasitic reactions between electrolyte 
and polysulfides [47]. In the 2MTD1 electrolyte, the content of the 
insulating SO4
2−species (169.2 eV) is markedly lower than that observed 
in the DME electrolyte. It can also be observed that the content of C-S 
and S-S has a distinct enhancement, realizing the reversibility of C-S and 
S-S. Overall, the CEI of dense Li2CO3 and LiF protects the cathode to 
reduce polysulfide dissolution and inhibits electrode-electrolyte side

We evaluated the solubility of LiPSs in different electrolytes since it 
greatly affects the cycling stability of SPAN. 0.25 M Li2S6 was added to 
various electrolytes, stirred at room temperature for 24 h, and subse­
quently allowed to stand for 2 h. The DME electrolyte showed a dark 
brown color, the 1MTD electrolyte had a lighter color, and the color of 
the 2MTD electrolyte was further lightened than that of the 1MTD one, 
whereas the amount of the precipitated substances in them was similar 
in Fig. 5a. Notably, the 2MTD1 electrolyte showed a yellowish color and 
had the most amount of precipitated substances among them, indicating 
that the 2MTD1 significantly reduced the solubility of LiPSs. This 
reduced solubility is expected to suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect 
and thereby improve the cycling stability of the battery. The above so­
lutions were continued to stand for 50 h and still showed the same 
results.

Li||SPAN cell with various electrolytes. The SPAN cathode in DME ex­
hibits both transformation processes involving “solid-solid” and “solid-

incorporating LiNO₃, a unique hierarchical solvation structure is con­
structed. This structure features an inner solvation sheath enriched with 
FSI⁻ and NO₃⁻ anions, which preferentially decompose to form a robust, 
inorganic-rich interphase. The co-solvents, MeTHF and DOX, are stra­
tegically positioned within the solvation hierarchy according to their 
solvating power. Notably, DOX residing in the outer sphere acts as an 
effective diluent, which is crucial for maintaining the anion-rich solva­
tion structure without compromising ionic conductivity. This multi- 
component engineering effectively stabilizes the electrode-electrolyte 
interface in Li-SPAN batteries. The weakly solvating power of the 
selected solvents was confirmed by DFT and MD simulations and the 
weakly solvated structure of Li+ was confirmed by Raman and NMR 
tests, thus proving the validity of molecular design. The 2MTD1 elec­
trolyte exhibits good ion transport kinetics and shows a homogeneous 
lithium deposition morphology, with LiF, Li3N and other inorganic-rich 
SEI protecting the lithium metal anode. Moreover, the electrolyte 
developed in this work effectively suppresses the polysulfide shuttle 
effect in ether-based electrolytes for Li||SPAN batteries. As a result, the 
Li||SPAN full cell exhibits long-term cyclability, sustaining a high ca­
pacity retention of 73.34% over 800 cycles. Specifically, a full cell 
assembled with high-load SPAN (3.8 mg cm−2) at 0.5 C achieved 
72.46% CR after 300 cycles. Our work uses medium-concentration salt 
and fluorine-free solvents to greatly reduce the cost of the electrolyte 
and is environmentally friendly, which is instructive for electrolyte en­
gineering of high-energy-density Li||SPAN batteries. It also provides 
ideas for the electrolyte design of other metal batteries and organic 
cathode batteries.
