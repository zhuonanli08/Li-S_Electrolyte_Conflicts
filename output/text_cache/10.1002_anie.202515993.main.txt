Failure Mechanism of High-Temperature Li–S Batteries in Localized
High-Concentration Electrolytes
Zixiong Shi, Simil Thomas, Georgian Melinte, Dong Guo, Jehad K. El-Demellawi, Nizar Jaber,
Manuel A. Quevedo-Lopez, Osman M. Bakr, Omar F. Mohammed, and Husam N. Alshareef*
Abstract: Conventional ether-based electrolytes struggle to sustain steady operation of lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries
at high temperatures due to inferior thermal durability and aggravated parasitic reactions. Although localized high-
concentration electrolyte (LHCE) has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance thermal stability, its deployment in
high-temperature (HT) Li–S batteries has met with limited success. Herein, the failure mechanism of HT Li–S batteries
in LHCE is revealed via probing sulfur redox reactions and electrolyte solvation chemistry. Slow reaction kinetics and
high polysulﬁde reactivity are determined to be the dominant factors causing the rapid capacity deterioration at high
temperatures. To this end, a diethylene glycol dibutyl ether-based localized medium concentration electrolyte (B-LMCE)
with suitable anion concentration and weakly solvating effect is developed. The new electrolyte concurrently achieves fast
cathode kinetics and stable anode/electrolyte interface. With the assistance of a tailored electrochemical voltage range of
1–3.8 V, Li–S batteries sustain a durable cycling performance over 250 cycles at 60 °C. They also showcase superior wide-
temperature operation (0 °C–80 °C) while enabling feasible fabrication of Ah-level pouch cells. Our study opens a new
avenue for designing extreme-temperature electrolytes toward pragmatic Li–S batteries.

In this study, we dig deep into the underlying reasons
for the rapid capacity decay of HT Li–S batteries in LHCE.
It is revealed that, for one thing, high anion concentration
and narrow voltage range collectively give rise to large
nucleation overpotential and sluggish dissociation kinetics of
Li2S. Therefore, aggregated discharge products will cover and
passivate the electrode surface, thus triggering irreversible
capacity loss and curtailed cycle lifetime. For another, with
the elevation of temperatures, electrolyte solvation structure
is changed with an apparently weakened ion-dipole coordina-
tion. Consequently, high polysulﬁde reaction activity of DME
causes polysulﬁde shuttling and hence capacity loss. To this
end, a localized medium concentration electrolyte (LMCE)
based on thermally stable and weakly solvated diethylene
glycol dibutyl ether (DEGDBE) is developed (denoted as
B-LMCE), which is characterized by low ratios of salt-
to-solvent and diluent-to-solvent. The suitable retention of
free DEGDBE solvents enables kinetically favorable sulfur
reaction chemistry while its weakly solvating effect essentially
suppresses polysulﬁde shuttling at high temperatures. A
series of advanced characterization techniques demystify
the temperature-dependent solvation structure and interface
chemistry. With the aid of a tailored electrochemical voltage
window of 1–3.8 V, B-LMCE can sustain the stable cycling
performance of HT Li–S batteries at a wide temperature
range of 0 °C–80 °C while guaranteeing heavy-duty conditions
of low electrolyte usage and high sulfur loading. More impres-
sively, thermally stable Ah-level Li–S pouch cells are fabri-
cated, demonstrative of tremendous commercial prospect.
Results and Discussion
LHCE is featured by reduced free active solvents and
enhanced cation-anion coordination, which is anticipated to
showcase multiple advantages in stabilizing Li–S batteries
(Figure 1a). To begin with, a quasi-solid-state reaction
path can be achieved since polysulﬁdes can barely be
dissolved, thereby essentially suppressing the “shuttling
effect”. Besides, beneﬁting from anion-rich solvation struc-
ture, the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer would be
rich in inorganic components. More encouragingly, such
a compact solvation structure with abundant ion pairs
and aggregates has an enhanced thermal stability at ele-
vated temperatures. Hence, LHCE might be a promising
candidate to sustain the steady operation of HT Li–S
batteries, yet its deployment has met with limited success.
First, a routine LHCE (r-LHCE) was prepared by mixing
lithium bis(ﬂuorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), DME, and 1,1,2,2-
tetraﬂuoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetraﬂuoropropylether (TTE) with a
molar ratio of 1:1.2:3. Li–S batteries equipped with r-LHCE
were tested at variable temperatures from 20 °C through 40 °C
and 60 °C to 80 °C. However, they suffer from a rapid capacity
decay at each temperature (Figures 1b and S1). Therefore, it
is imperative and meaningful to dig deep into the underlying
reasons for the fast performance degradation, thus adjusting
the electrolyte recipe.
Corresponding
galvanostatic
charge-discharge
(GCD)
curves at varied temperatures were collected (Figure 1c,d),
which exhibit a distinct difference regarding the discharge
plateau, reaction polarization, and redox reversibility. It is
evident that only one discharge plateau can be observed
for r-LHCE unlike the typical two-plateau behavior in
the traditional electrolytes, indicative of the suppressed
polysulﬁde dissolution and hence a quasi-solid-phase sulfur
reaction chemistry. Overall, speciﬁc discharge capacity is
improved with the increase of temperatures, accompanied
by a reduced voltage polarization, which can be attributed
to the elevated polysulﬁde reaction kinetics. Notably, the
discharge capacity is always higher than that of the charging
process, suggesting that the Li2S dissociation is not sufﬁcient,
thereby triggering the aggregation of insulating discharge
products and degrading the reversibility of redox reaction. To
determine which electrode side is responsible for the battery
failure, a replacement test was carried out after 10 cycles at
60 °C. In this respect, Li–S batteries can recover the normal
operation upon the substitution of a new S cathode while
the capacity can scarcely be elevated with a fresh Li anode
(Figure 1e). In addition, the Li–Li symmetric cell can stably
cycle over 800 h at 60 °C (Figure S2), manifesting that r-
LHCE can maintain longevous operation of Li metal anode
without apparent deterioration. It is therefore concluded
that the cathode inactivation, particularly pertaining to the
accumulation of Li2S, is the key reason accounting for the
rapid capacity attenuation of HT Li–S batteries in r-LHCE.
To evidence our speculation that the degraded S cathode
suffers from severe Li2S coverage, a wider electrochemical
voltage range was applied to enable more sufﬁcient Li2S
conversion reaction. In this respect, beneﬁting from the
effective elimination of free active solvents, r-LHCE show-
cases enhanced anti-oxidation stability and hence superior
high-voltage tolerance. It can be observed that the cycling
stability can be signiﬁcantly improved either at room or
high (Figure S3). Besides, a higher reversible capacity
can be obtained after long-term cycling process with the
management of a broadened voltage range (Figure 1f). It
is also worth noting that the r-LHCE will experience a
sudden decomposition upon the voltage reaches ca. 4 V at

Taken the above analysis together, broadening voltage

to exhaustive characterizations for studying their speciation
and distribution. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
was employed to probe the chemical compositions on the
surface of electrodes. In terms of high-resolution XPS 2p
spectra (Figure 1g), the peaks at 161.4 and 162.6 eV can be
assigned to Li2S and Li2S2, respectively. As expected, these
two peaks for the S cathode after cycling within 1.2–3.5 V are
negligible, conﬁrming the efﬁcient dissociation of insulating
discharge products with a higher cut-off voltage. On the
contrary, the signals exhibit a considerable intensity in terms
of the typical voltage range, indicative of sluggish oxidation
reaction kinetics and severe Li2S/Li2S2 accumulation. In
parallel, stronger C─S bonding in the high-resolution XPS
C 1s spectra again corroborates the kinetically enhanced
sulfur redox reaction within 1.2–3.5 V (Figure 1h). As
shown in X-Ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, only peaks that

the latter, S cathode suffers from severe Li2S coverage and
passivation.[30] Furthermore, high angle annular dark ﬁeld
scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM)
was used to examine the morphologies and distributions of
sulfur species. It is apparent that uncontrollable aggregation
of insulating Li2S can be visualized in the S cathode with
a narrow voltage range of 1.7–2.8 V, accompanied by a
continuous surface passivation layer (Figures 1j and S4).

Expect for the severe Li2S accumulation and coverage on
the S cathode, unstable electrolyte structure is another key
concern for the rapid performance degradation of HT Li–
S batteries. In this respect, temperature-mediated solvation
chemistry and ion dynamics of r-LHCE were investigated
via multimodal nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spec-
troscopic characterizations.[31] First, 1D NMR spectra of
r-LHCE were recorded to shed light upon the electrolyte
solvation conﬁguration.[32] Both
7Li and
19F resonances
exhibit a downﬁeld shift with the increase of temperatures
(Figures 2a and S5), indicative of a deshielding effect. This
result originates from the weakened ion-dipole interaction,
thus impeding the electron transfer from electron-donating
ethereal oxygen atoms to Li+ and FSI−. In the meanwhile,
the values of full width half maximum (FWHM) are reduced,
implying higher ion mobility at elevated temperatures. It is
well established that NMR T1 and T2 relaxation times can
reﬂect the dynamic motion and surrounding environment,
respectively.[33,34] In this regard, 7Li T1 displays a continuous
increase upon heating the electrolyte (Figures 2b, S6, and
S7), demonstrative of accelerated tumbling rate of Li nuclei.
It is acceptable that at high temperatures, ion coordination
is weakened, thus leading to the reduced size and increased
symmetry of Li-ion solvation structure. With respect to 19F
relaxation time, for one thing, both T1 and T2 are augmented
with increased temperatures (Figure 2c and Table S1),
indicative of faster ion motion due to the weakened Li+–
FSI−interaction. For another, 1/T1–1/T2 of 19F is associated
with the chemical exchange rates between bound and free
FSI−, hence a larger value can represent the higher degree of
compact ion pairs (CIPs).[35] Notably, it exhibits a signiﬁcant
decrease upon the temperature is elevated to 318 K, indicating
that the initial CIPs in the r-LHCE are prone to be
transformed into solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) at a
high temperature. The cation-anion interaction was quantiﬁed
by 7Li–19F hetero-nuclear Over Hauser effect spectroscopy
(HOESY), which aids to probe the spatial coupling in liquid
solutions.[36] As for 19F NMR spectra, the only one peak with
a positive ppm value is assigned to FSI−while three other
peaks at negative ppm originate from F-containing groups in
TTE. As shown in Figure 2d, such 2D intensities (I) from
Li+–FTFSI−and Li+–FTTE cross peaks were integrated, hence
I(TFSI−)/I(TTE) is reduced from 0.422 to 0.185 with the
temperature increased from 298 to 333 K. This is indicative
of a shortened distance and weakened interaction between
Li+ and TFSI−, again conﬁrming the loose Li-ion solvation
structure at high temperatures. No doubt it will aggravate the
thermal instability of electrolyte, which in turn deteriorates
the cyclic performance of HT Li–S batteries.
Highly reactive DME solvents in SSIPs might not effec-
tively restrict the dissolution and shuttling of polysulﬁdes at
high temperatures, which would trigger irreversible capacity
decay. To evidence our speculation, Operando Raman test
was carried out at 60 °C. It can be observed that the peaks
ascribed to soluble polysulﬁdes (Li2S8 at 474 cm−1; Li2S6
at 398 cm−1; Li2S4 at 273 cm−1) occur upon the discharge
process along with a continuous attenuation of their inten-
sities (Figure 2e), manifesting that polysulﬁde dissolution
can scarcely be suppressed at high temperatures for r-
LHCE. In addition, current–time curves from potentiostatic
intermittent titration technique (PITT) test were recorded
at continuously decreased discharge voltages to detect the
shuttling current (Figure 2f), wherein the current response
at 60 °C is apparently higher than that at 25 °C. In the
meanwhile, the peak arising from Li2S nucleation is advanced
to a higher voltage at 60 °C, accompanied by a signiﬁcantly

Quantitative NMR (qNMR) test was performed to further
study the evolution and consumption of electrolyte during
cycling process, wherein 2,4-dichlorobenzotriﬂuoride (DCBF)
was employed as the reference since its 1H and 19F NMR
signals (7.95–7.67 ppm and 61.20 ppm, respectively) are far
from those of r-LHCE (Figure 2g).[37] As for the pristine
r-LHCE, the as-derived molar ratio of FSI−-to-DME and
TTE-to-DME from is 0.784 and 2.776, respectively (Table S2).
Encouragingly, they align well with the setting value (0.833
and 2.5), implying the outstanding reliability of our qNMR
method. After 50 cycles, it is found that for one thing,
the consumption rate of TTE and FSI−is evidently higher
than that of DME (Figures 2h, S8, and S9). For another,
such a tendency will be further accelerated at an elevated
temperature of 60 °C, indicating that electrolyte is prone
to undergo a degraded thermal stability and accelerated
decomposition rate. In this regard, the proportion of diluent
and anion in electrolyte should be reduced with the aim to
enhance the thermal durability of electrolyte and achieve
longevous HT Li–S batteries.
Taken above analysis together, failure mechanisms and
remedy strategies are proposed, thus further optimizing the
electrolyte recipe for HT Li–S batteries (Figure 3a). From
the perspective of S cathode, high anion concentration of
electrolyte will give rise to sluggish reaction kinetics and
severe Li2S accumulation. To this end, broadening voltage
range with a higher cut-off voltage is evidenced to expedite
the dissociation of insulating Li2S products and elevate the
reaction reversibility. Besides, ion–dipole interaction in Li-
ion inner solvation sheath is weakened at high temperatures,
hence DME with a high reaction activity can hardly suppress
the dissolution and shuttling of polysulﬁdes. In the meantime,
thermal decomposition and consumption of electrolyte will be
accelerated especially for anion and diluent, thus signiﬁcantly
reducing the cycling lifetime of HT Li–S batteries at lean-
electrolyte condition. To cope with these challenges, B-LMCE
with an appropriate anion concentration and weakly solvating
effect is designed, which is featured by low ratios of salt-
to-solvent and diluent-to-solvent. Speciﬁcally, 1.5 M LiTFSI
was dissolved into the binary solvent of DEGDBE and
OTE with a volume ratio of 5:3. It enables decreased
nucleation overpotential and sufﬁcient dissociation of Li2S,
accordingly improving bi-directional redox reaction kinetics.

In the meantime, essential restriction of polysulﬁde crossover
can be readily achieved owing to unique molecular structure
of DEGDBE with long alkyl chain, thereby avoiding Li

Besides, B-LMCE has superior thermal stability and compact
solvation sheath owing to the tri-denticity coordination, which
reduces the electrolyte consumption rate at high working
temperatures. Notably, LiTFSI, DEGDBE and 1H,1H,5H-
octaﬂuoropentyl 1,1,2,2-tetraﬂuoroethyl ether (OTE) with
a higher thermal stability was used to respectively replace
LiFSI, DME, and TTE to prepare electrolytes (Table S3). The
lowest negative electrostatic potential (ESPmin) was investi-
gated, which represents the solvation energy and nucleophilic
capacity of the solvents.[38] It is intriguing that DEGDBE
shares a similar ESPmin value with DME (Figure 3b), yet
that of OTE is quite weak. This suggests that DEGDBE
can enable sufﬁcient dissolution of Li salt and participate in
the Li-ion inner solvation structure, while OTE can scarcely
solvate Li ions and only serve as the outer diluent. However,
DEGDBE exhibits a more positive Li+ binding energy than
that of DME (Figures 3c, S10, and S11), indicative of its
weak afﬁnity with Li ions due to long carbon chain. In this
case, it is possible for more TFSI−anions to enter the Li-
ion inner solvation sheath since it is unable to accommodate
two or more DEGDBE molecules.[39] In addition, higher C/O
atomic ratio will weaken the Li–O coordination and enable an
anion-rich Li-ion inner solvation sheath.
Diffusion-ordered
spectroscopy
(DOSY)
NMR
was
employed to investigate the diffusion dynamics of various
solvation species in electrolytes.[40] In terms of 1H DOSY
test (Figures 3d, S12, and S13), it is understandable that
DEGDBE (4.36 × 10−9 m2 s−1) shares a close diffusion
coefﬁcient (D) with OTE (4.43 × 10−9 m2 s−1) since both
of them display a large molecular size. After the addition
of LiTFSI, D of DEGDBE is reduced by an order of
magnitude while that of OTE shows a much smaller decrease,
manifesting that Li ions are prone to coordinate with
DEGDBE via the Li–O coordination. Note that the slight
reduction of D for OTE is attributed to the increased
viscosity, yet it keeps staying outside the inner solvation
sheath. Since DEGDBE leverages multiple advantages to
construct thermally stable and weakly solvating electrolyte
toward reliable HT Li–S batteries, three electrolytes (i.e.,
B-LMCE, B-LHCE, and B-MCE) as well as DME-based
LMCE (denoted as M-LMCE) were prepared. M-LMCE is
obtained by replacing DEGDBE with DME in B-LMCE.
2.4 M LiTFSI dissolved into DEGDBE is denoted as B-MCE.
Besides, for the preparation of B-LHCE, 4.8 M LiTFSI was
ﬁrst dissolved into DEGDBE, which was then attenuated
by OTE with three times the volume. 1H DOSY test was
carried out to obtain their self-diffusivity (Figure 3e), hence
B-LMCE and B-LHCE share a close D value for DEGDBE.
It suggests that although the diluent dosage is much less in
B-LMCE, it can also enable favorable ion transport owing to
the reduced concentration. In the varied-temperature NMR
test (Figure 3f), the downﬁeld shift of 7Li NMR spectra with
increased temperature indicates an increased distance and
weakened interaction between Li ions and ether molecules
in the Li-ion solvation sheath. Obviously, as compared to M-
LMCE, B-LMCE enables a slighter change of NMR signal,
indicating a more compact solvation environment of Li+ as
well as higher thermal stability, regardless of the increase
of temperature. It is worth noting that such two electrolytes
display a similar tendency regarding the shift of 19F NMR
with the elevation of temperatures (Figure S14), manifesting
that the coordination state of TFSI−can hardly be inﬂuenced
by solvents. As shown in Figure S15, 7Li resonance exhibits
a negligible shift after the addition of OTE into MCE while
19F signal shifts to a more negative ppm, manifesting that Li+
scarcely coordinates with OTE yet there is a considerable
interaction between TFSI−and OTE. Furthermore, as-
derived 7Li T1 value of B-LMCE is almost a magnitude lower
than that of M-LMCE at each temperature (Figures 3g, S16,
and 17; Table S4), which suggests that DEGDBE-enabled
Li-ion solvation conﬁguration has an evidently larger size
and higher asymmetry due to more precipitation of TFSI−
anions.[41]
Molecular dynamics simulations were used to elucidate
the Li-ion solvation structure and its temperature-mediated
evolution (Figures 3h,i and S18).[42] It is unveiled that
the Li-ion inner solvation sheath is dominated by ether
solvents and TFSI−anions while OTE molecules can scarcely
coordinate with Li ions, aligning well with the forgoing
NMR analysis. In addition, in comparison with M-LMCE, B-
LMCE exhibits a higher coordination number for TFSI−yet
the trend is reversed for ether molecules. It is understood
that although DEGDBE has a similar solvation ability for
Li+, Li-ion ﬁrst solvation sheath can hardly accommodate
two DEGDBE molecules due to its long alkyl chain, hence
more TFSI−anions are prone to precipitate into the Li-ion
solvation sheath. Besides, the coordination number of solvent
molecules is decreased for M-LMCE at a high temperature,
yet that is augmented for B-LMCE, again conﬁrming the
compact Li-ion solvation structure of B-LMCE with the man-
agement of tri-denticity DEGDBE solvent. Admittedly, there
are considerable free active solvents without Li+ coordination
for LMCE, which is recognized as the most instable part
that deteriorate the durability of cathode-electrolyte interface
during long-term cycling process.[43] Therefore, their redox
stability is crucial to sustaining the stable battery operation
at a high voltage or an elevated temperature. In this respect,
as depicted in Figure 3j, DEGDBE showcases a more negative
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), suggestive of its
superior thermal oxidative stability to promise longevous HT
Li–S batteries operating with a high cut-off voltage.
In terms of the Li–Li symmetric cell test at 60 °C, B-
LMCE harvests both a longer cycling lifetime and lower

With respect to the S cathode, DEGDBE also showcases
a conspicuous advantage in restricting polysulﬁde dissolution
and migration. It can be found that both DEGDBE and OTE
exhibit a much lower afﬁnity with Li2S6 as compared to that
of DME (Figure 5a), which is mainly attributed to long alkyl
chain and electron-withdrawn F atoms, respectively.[49,50] To
investigate the polysulﬁde dissolution and speciation in dif-
ferent solvents, 100 mM Li2S6 solution was prepared based on
binary solvents: DME/X (X stands for DME, DEGDBE, or
OTE) with a volume ratio of 1:1. Obviously, both DEGDBE
and OTE can restrict the dissolution of polysulﬁdes, which
enables a much lighter color of the solution as compared to
the DME counterpart (Figure S30). As demonstrated in 7Li
NMR spectra of 25 °C and 60 °C (Figure 5b), the resonance
exhibits a downﬁeld shift with the increased temperature,
indicative of decreased electron cloud density on Li atoms.
It is acceptable that S2−serves as a Lewis base to donate
electrons, hence promoted polysulﬁde dissolution can drag
electrons from Li+ to maintain the charge balance of Li2S6
molecules. DME/DEGDBE displays the smallest change of
7Li chemical shift from 25 °C to 60 °C, suggesting that
DEGDBE can enable a low solubility and restricted diffusion
for Li2S6 even at elevated temperatures. In the meantime, 7Li
relaxation time test reveals that although three systems share
a similar T2 value, DEGDBE enables the lowest T1 value
amongst them (Figures 5c and S31; Table S6). This manifests
that the effective constraint for polysulﬁde migration can be
achieved by DEGDBE, thereby reducing the motion rate
of Li atoms. It is therefore concluded that DEGDBE can
restrict the dissolution and shuttling of polysulﬁdes regardless
of the temperatures, which is anticipated to be a promising
candidate to fabricate thermally stable Li–S batteries. In situ
Raman test at 60 °C was carried out to detect the real-time
concentration/speciation change of polysulﬁdes on the anode
side (Figure S32), wherein polysulﬁde signals can hardly be
detected. This result shows that polysulﬁdes are unable to
shuttle to the Li anode since both DEGDBE and OTE restrict
their diffusion, thus avoiding the irreversible capacity loss as
well as Li metal corrosion.
Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was
adopted to investigate both the thermodynamic and kinetic
behaviors of HT Li–S batteries in various electrolytes.[51]
During discharge process, the GITT curves of B-LMCE and
B-MCE are highly overlapped while that of B-LHCE exhibits
an apparently lower kinetic voltage (Figure 5d). Thermody-
namic and kinetic voltages regarding Li2S nucleation were
further evaluated (Figure 5e). Although the thermodynamic
voltage of B-LHCE (2.147 V) is slightly lower than that of
B-LMCE and B-MCE (2.157 and 2.150 V, respectively), its
kinetic voltage is signiﬁcantly reduced by 200 mV. These
results corroborate that high anion concentration will degrade
the kinetics of sulfur reduction reaction. In parallel, B-
LHCE suffers from an evidently higher thermodynamic
voltage during the charge process (Figure S33). This suggests
that inferior Li2S nucleation kinetics will in turn cause
severe accumulation and uneven distribution of insulating
Li2S products, thereby damaging the reversibility of redox
reaction. Notably, B-MCE can scarcely ﬁnish the GITT
charging process and recover its initial stage, suggestive of
the sluggish Li2S oxidation process. Taken together, the
underlying failure mechanisms are demonstrated in Figure 5f.
High anion concentration in B-LHCE would signiﬁcantly
augment the nucleation overpotential of Li2S, which further
deteriorates the reversibility of redox reaction. In this respect,
common catalyst design strategies are not suitable for LHCE.
Since it is saturated without free active solvents, homogenous
catalysts can scarcely be dissolved into it.[52] Besides, het-
erogeneous catalysts can play a negligible role in elevating
reaction kinetics due to the lack of sufﬁcient three-phase
reaction interfaces.[53] Besides, sluggish reaction kinetics of
Li2S oxidation will arise from the high electrolyte viscosity of
B-MCE, hence the OTE diluent also plays a pivotal role in
enabling favorable bi-directional redox reaction.

Conclusion
In summary, the failure mechanism of HT Li–S batteries
in r-LHCE have been elucidated with the aid of exhaus-
tive electrochemical analysis, material characterization, and
theoretical calculations. Insufﬁcient sulfur redox conversion
and high polysulﬁde reactivity of DME are determined to
be the dominant reasons for the rapid capacity decay. To
address these issues, a new electrolyte formulation (B-LMCE)
is proposed based on weakly solvating DEGDBE, which
features low ratios of salt-to-solvent and diluent-to-solvent.
B-LMCE simultaneously enables kinetically favored sulfur
redox reaction and restrains the migration of polysulﬁdes.
Under controlled voltage window, the HT Li–S batteries
achieve long cycling performance within a wide tempera-
ture range of 0 °C–80 °C even under heavy-duty working
conditions. More encouragingly, Ah-level Li–S pouch cells
are fabricated. Our study provides detailed insights for
rationalizing extreme-temperature electrolyte design, holding
tremendous prospect for future Li–S commercialization.
