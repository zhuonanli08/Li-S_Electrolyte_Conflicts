A B S T R A C T  
Previous studies revealed that LiNO3 can enhance the cyclic stability of lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries by 
creating a solid electrolyte interface protecting the lithium electrode. However, this study investigated the effect 
of LiNO3 and concentration in the electrolyte, first studied under low and high sulfur loading. It is confirmed that 
adding a small amount of LiNO3 inhibits the shuttle effect phenomenon in Li–S batteries, and the Coulombic 
efficiency was significantly improved. Appropriately increasing the concentration of LiNO3 to 2 % could prolong 
the cycle life. However, when LiNO3 is excessive, the rate and cycling performance decrease. The design of 
practical Li–S batteries requires more research on the parameters of electrolyte additives under high‑sulfur- 
loading conditions. After gradient increasing the sulfur loading, the low concentration of LiNO3 cannot play the 
best performance of the battery, so it is necessary to improve the concentration of LiNO3 further. It is evident that 
adding LiNO3 in Li–S batteries has certain benefits, and the optimal amount of additive is related to the value of 
sulfur loading.   
1. Introduction 
Due to civilization’s progress and the continuous increase of elec­
tronic devices and electric vehicles, the development of batteries is 
essential [1]. Among several types, lithium‑sulfur (Li–S) batteries have 
received high interest due to low cost and high theoretical energy den­
sity (about 2600 Wh⋅kg−1), sufficient for about 400 km of vehicle 
driving [2–5]. Nevertheless, the main drawbacks of Li–S batteries are 
the low lifetime and overcharging upon the practical application. 
Accordingly, the research about Li–S batteries is of high interest, spe­
cifically for cyclic stability improvement [6,7]. Nazar’s group reported 
that the CMK-3/S composite cathode significantly improves the batte­
ries’ cyclic stability [8]. 
In Li–S batteries, the transformation from S8 to Li2S is a multi-step 
process with different chain lengths of polysulfides (PSs) correspond­
ing to different stages of redox reaction [9]. Thus, additional phases are 
formed within the process, which may affect negatively. For example, 
S8/Li2S does not conduct electricity, and the intermediate product PSs 
lead to the shuttle effect. The shuttle effect is accompanied by several 
problems with the battery, such as overcharge, fast capacity decline, and 
poor cyclic stability [10]. In liquid electrolytes, the dissolution and 
diffusion of PSs are inevitable, so the key to solving this problem is to 
reduce the dissolution of PSs in the electrolyte or prevent PSs from 
transporting to the lithium side. The creation of micropores in the car­
bon can inhibit the diffusion of PSs during the reaction, as the pores can 
only accommodate small molecules of sulfur within 0.5 nm, which 
prevents the formation of long-chain PSs and realizes the solid trans­
formation [11]. However, the design of a microporous structure leads to 
low-sulfur-loading and a low open circuit voltage. Solid electrolytes can 
also prevent the dissolution of PSs and physically obstruct the migration 
of PSs between electrodes. However, the application of solid electrolytes 
in Li–S is limited by low ionic conductivity and high interfacial 
impedance [12,13]. At present, the DOL/DME electrolyte is commonly 
used in Li–S batteries. The synergistic effect of the annular and chain 
structure of solvents gives better electrochemical performance than that 
of a single solvent [14,15]. However, preventing the shuttle effect of PSs 
is an interesting research point. Modifying the electrolyte with a small 
amount of additives may significantly enhance cyclic stability, as some

Adding LiNO3 to the electrolyte of Li–S batteries is a breakthrough 
in the field. LiNO3 participates in the interface reaction, which is con­
verted into LixNOy and LixSOy, forming a stable passivation film on the 
surface of lithium that inhibits the corrosion of lithium metal, thus 
improving the cyclic stability [17,18]. In addition, LiNO3 has a syner­
gistic effect with PSs in reducing dendrite growth and electrolyte 
decomposition [19]. Although LiNO3 addition has significantly 
improved battery performance and has been widely investigated 
experimentally, it also has negative effects. It is pointed out that LiNO3 
promotes the retention of carbon structure in sulfur cathode but also 
increases the consumption of active sulfur [20]. The insoluble reduction 
products on the sulfur surface adversely affect the reversibility of a series 
of redox properties [21]. Although LiNO3 has been used in Li–S battery- 
related studies, most are at low concentrations and do not extend the 
concentration of LiNO3 to a wide range (0–5 wt%). The influence of 
LiNO3 on the performance also focuses on the long cycling performance 
without considering the state under large current density conditions. At 
the same time, considering the industrial application of Li–S batteries, 
high-sulfur-loading is the inevitable trend of future research. Most 
available studies consider the material’s performance with low-sulfur- 
loading (1 mg cm−2), and to our best knowledge, there is no investiga­
tion into the effect of LiNO3 concentrations under high-sulfur-loading. 
Therefore, to contribute to this research gap, this study systematically 
investigated the electrochemical performance of batteries under 
different concentrations of LiNO3 with gradient sulfur loading, thus 
optimizing the use of LiNO3 addition. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Preparation of electrolyte 
Dimethyl ether (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), bis(trifluoromethane) 
sulfonimide lithium (LiTFSI), and lithium nitrate (LiNO3) were all pur­
chased from Aladdin. LiNO3 was dried in the vacuum oven at 120 ◦C for 
24 h before use. The electrolyte solutions were prepared in an argon- 
filled glovebox (MIKROUNA Super) in which both moisture and oxy­
gen concentrations were controlled below 1 ppm. The basic electrolyte 
was 1 M LiTFSI dissolved in DOL/DME (1:1, volume:volume). By adding 
different amount of LiNO3, electrolyte with different concentration of 
LiNO3 was obtained. 
2.2. Preparation of sulfur cathode 
The S/C composite was prepared by co-heating sulfur and carbon 
black at 155 ◦C for 10 h. The mass ratio of sulfur and carbon black was 
7:3. First, the sulfur and carbon were mixed and grinded at room tem­
perature. The mixture was heat treated with Ar gas. The heated mixture 
was milled again with carbon black and LA133 in the mass ratio of 7:2:1, 
using deionized water as the dispersant. Carbon cloth was obtained by 
carbonizing cotton cloth at 800 ◦C under the protection of Ar gas. The 
slurry was cast on the carbon cloth substrate and then dried at 50 ◦C 
under vacuum overnight. 
2.3. Electrochemical measurements 
The electrochemical tests were performed in sealed CR2032 coin 
cells. Coated carbon cloth was used as working electrodes. Celgard 2500 
as separators and lithium foils as the counter electrode. The coin tests 
were conducted on a LAND CT2001A battery test system in a voltage 
range of 1.7–2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+) at certain current densities. The specific 
capacity of the Li–S battery was calculated according to the sulfur 
loading. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. LiNO3 improves the electrochemical performance 
Fig. 1 shows the preparation process of the sulfur cathode. Consid­
ering the wide range of sulfur loading in the experiment (1–5 mg⋅cm−2), 
the sulfur cathode adopts a three-dimensional structure to improve the 
overall conductivity. Carbon cloth with a three-dimensional structure 
was selected as the cathode substrate. The powder of the S/C composite 
and the dispersant were made into a uniform slurry by ball milling. After 
absorbing the slurry according to the calculated amount, the slurry was 
evenly cast on the carbon cloth. After drying, the sulfur cathode with 
different gradient loading was obtained. 
Fig. 2 (b,c) shows the surface morphology of the lithium foil with and 
without the addition of LiNO3. Compared with the surface of the original 
lithium foil in Fig. 2 (a), the smooth and dense surface of the original 
lithium sheet disappeared, and the surface became rough with voids. 
The surface of lithium foil without additives is almost entirely covered 
with loose dendritic lithium. The surface of the lithium foil using 1 % 
LiNO3 has sediments, but the fractures disappear, and the lithium sur­
face still maintains a compact structure. The addition of LiNO3 effec­
tively inhibits the formation of dendritic lithium and prevents the 
collapse of the dense structure on the surface of lithium. 
The Coulombic efficiency of the first cycle without LiNO3 is less than 
60 % (Fig. 2d). With the addition of 1 % LiNO3, the specific charging 
capacity of the first cycle is slightly changed from 1361.9 mAh⋅g−1 to 
1291.3 mAh⋅g−1, which is without LiNO3, but the Coulombic efficiency 
is greatly improved from 56.3 % to 86.3 %. Similar improvement is also 
experienced in the data of the specific capacity of the first discharge 
(Fig. 2d inset). The capacity of the assembled cell discharges to a certain 
extent during the standing 24 h process due to the shuttle effect, and the 
cell without adding LiNO3 is only 967.2 mAh⋅g−1. It is only 67.2 % 
compared to 1439.5 mAh⋅g−1 of the battery with 1 %. The open circuit 
voltage of the battery without LiNO3 drops rapidly after assembled [22]. 
In the standing process, the open circuit voltage of the Li–S battery 
without electrolyte additives drops to 2.42 V, and the first discharge 
plateau is obviously shortened in Fig. 2d. Self-discharge results in the 
loss of the first discharge plateau. However, after adding 1 % of LiNO3, 
the voltage is higher than 2.45 V after standing for the same time. There 
is no significant decrease occurs. The self-discharge and overcharge 
phenomenon was alleviated by adding LiNO3 [23,24]. After the addition 
of LiNO3, compared with the original straight line, the discharging curve 
appears as an inclined plateau at about 1.75 V, which corresponds to the 
reaction process of LiNO3 [25]. Fig. 2e shows the discharge-specific 
capacities under different current densities. During the whole process 
of rates changing between 0.2 C–0.5 C–1 C–2 C–4 C–0.2 C, the 
discharge-specific capacities of batteries without additives are 891.7 
mAh⋅g−1, 767.4 mAh⋅g−1, 671.1 mAh⋅g−1, 564.7 mAh⋅g−1, 479.8 
mAh⋅g−1 and 758.6 mAh⋅g−1 respectively, with 1 % LiNO3 are 1126.5 
mAh⋅g−1, 938.9 mAh⋅g−1, 864.7 mAh⋅g−1, 755.9 mAh⋅g−1, 598.3 
mAh⋅g−1 and 988.7 mAh⋅g−1. During rate performance testing, the 
discharge-specific capacity of the battery without additive is less than 
that of the battery with 1 % addition [26]. After 300 cycles at 0.2 C, the 
battery with the 1 % addition shows better stability for initial specific 
capacity and capacity retention during the cycle. The Coulombic effi­
ciency of the battery without additive was lower than 65 % for the first 
10 cycles, but after adding LiNO3, the Coulombic efficiency of the first 
10 cycles (except for the first cycle), reached more than 99 %. The big 
data gap is due to the notorious shuttle effect of Li–S batteries, which is 
suppressed by the addition of LiNO3 [23,24]. The consumed LiNO3 in 
the lithium side promotes the formation of SEI film, which is key to 
inhibiting the shuttle effect. 
Fig. 2f shows the difference between with and without the SEI film. 
Without the addition of LiNO3, the surface of the lithium anode does not 
have an enough solid protective layer to prevent the corrosion of PSs.

sulfur side diffuse to the vicinity of the lithium side and form short-chain 
PSs on the surface. Affected by charge density, the short-chain PSs will 
react before the long-chain PSs under the action of the electric field, and 
the long-chain PSs will be generated. This process is repeated to form the 
shuttle effect. On the one hand, active sulfur is consumed; on the other 
hand, corrosion consumes lithium metal [23,27]. The manifestation is

lithium can also lead to the deposition of insoluble Li2S/Li2S2, affecting 
Li+ transport. LiNO3 reacted with lithium metal to form a solid SEI on 
the lithium surface, which passivated the activity of lithium metal and 
acted as a strong protective layer, effectively blocking the direct corro­
sion on the lithium surface [26,28]. The most effective proof is the 
obvious improvement of Coulombic efficiency. Meanwhile, the obvious 
reduction of dendritic lithium can also be observed on the lithium 
surface. 
3.2. Influence of LiNO3 concentration under low sulfur loading 
Adding LiNO3 with 1 % has significantly improved the electro­
chemical performance of Li–S batteries, however, the effect of 
increased LiNO3 ratio is doubtable. So, the effect of concentration ratio 
has been studied in the range of 1–6 %. In the range of 1–5 %, the so­
lution is clear and liquid. However, when the concentration was 
increased to 6 %, insoluble precipitation occurred (Fig. S1). Therefore, 
the maximum concentration of LiNO3 in subsequent experiments was 5 
%. After cycling with sulfur loading of 1 mg⋅cm−2 under different LiNO3 
concentration, the lithium side was disassembled and observed (Fig. 3 
(a–c)). It can be found that the lithium surface is corroded by varying 
degrees. As the LiNO3 concentration increases, the lithium surface be­
comes denser, the corrosion is inhibited, and the surface looks more 
uniform and compact. 
Batteries with different LiNO3 concentration were charged and dis­
charged under different current densities. The current densities varied 
between 0.2 C–0.5 C–1 C–2 C–4 C–0.2 C (Fig. 3d). When the LiNO3 
concentration increased to 2 %, the discharge specific capacity of the 
battery with different current densities decreased in different degrees. 
The discharge specific capacity of the battery with 2 % was 1063.8 
mAh⋅g−1, 747.2 mAh⋅g−1, 644.1 mAh⋅g−1, 525.2 mAh⋅g−1, 393.7 
mAh⋅g−1 and 767.0 mAh⋅g−1 respectively. Compared with 1 % con­
centration, the discharge capacity decreased by 5.6 %, 20.4 %, 25.5 %, 
30.5 %, 34.2 %, and 22.4 %, respectively. At a low current density of 0.2 
C, the electrochemical performance of battery with 2 % LiNO3 remains 
excellent. However, when the current density increased to 0.5 C or even 
4 C, the negative effect of 2 % concentration on the electrochemical 
performance was obvious. Under small current density, increasing the 
LiNO3 concentration has a little negative effect, but the negative effect is 
larger under large current density. Similar attenuation of discharge can 
be observed when the LiNO3 concentration is increased more. At 0.2 C 
current density, the discharge-specific capacity decreases from 1126.5 
mAh⋅g−1 (1 %) to 940.6 mAh⋅g−1 (5 %), with a decrease of 16.5 %. 
However, with the current density increased to 4 C, the discharge- 
specific capacity decreased from 598.3 mAh⋅g−1 (1 %) to 267.5 
mAh⋅g−1 (5 %), with a decrease of 55.3 %. When the current density is 4 
C, with the LiNO3 concentration varying from 2 %, 3 %, 4 % to 5 %,

3.3. Influence of LiNO3 concentration under high sulfur loading 
Considering the attractive prospect of Li–S battery industrialization 
in the future, the sulfur loading of 1 mg⋅cm−2 is far lower than that of 
commercial scale. To achieve the capacity of commercial lithium-ion 
batteries (4mAh⋅cm−2), the capacity of the sulfur cathode needs to be 
further improved. Therefore, the sulfur loading was expanded to the 
range of 1–5 mg⋅cm−2 to observe the influence of LiNO3 concentration 
on the electrochemical performance of the Li–S battery. 
Li–S batteries with different sulfur loading and LiNO3 concentration 
were charged and discharged under different current densities. Fig. 4(a) 
shows that the overall electrochemical performance is negatively 
correlated with the sulfur loading when the 1 % concentration of LiNO3 
is added. The higher the sulfur loading, the worse the electrochemical 
performance of the Li–S battery. This is not a specific phenomenon 
during the test. The higher the sulfur loading, the greater the internal 
resistance, the longer the ion transmission path, the easier the trans­
mission path is to jam, and the more obvious the uneven deposition will 
be caused [34,35]. In particular, the final charging product S8 and the 
discharging product Li2S2/Li2S of Li–S batteries are not conductive. 
When the sulfur loading increases, the negative effects caused by poor 
conductivity will be more obvious, and the irreversible capacity

4. Conclusions 
In this paper, the effect of LiNO3 addition on Li–S batteries has been 
systematically investigated. It is revealed that, without LiNO3 addition, 
the self-discharge is obvious, and the first-cycle discharge capacity was 
32 % less than that of 1 % LiNO3 addition. The discharge-specific ca­
pacity and Coulombic efficiency decrease to a lower value due to the 
shuttle effect without LiNO3 addition. This indicates that the LiNO3 
addition can play an important role in developing Li–S batteries. 
Increasing the concentration of LiNO3 from 1 % to 2 %, the rate per­
formance decreases lightly. Although there is no improvement in elec­
trochemical performance during short cycles, the discharge-specific 
capacity is better maintained up to 300 cycles at 0.2 C. This is caused by 
the LiNO3 consumption during the repair of SEI film during cycling. 
Increasing the LiNO3 concentration can compensate for the loss after 
long cycling. Regarding the optimal electrochemical performance, the 
LiNO3 concentration is positively related to the sulfur loading gradient 
when it increases from 1 to 5 mg⋅cm−2. When the sulfur loading is up to 
5 mg⋅cm−2, the LiNO3 concentration should be increased to 3 %. The 
great improvement of electrochemical performance is due to the positive 
effects of LiNO3. In addition to the repairing function for SEI film on the 
lithium side, the high concentration of LiNO3 provides enough lithium 
salt for rapid cycling. But the higher concentration of LiNO3 is not al­
ways better. When the LiNO3 concentration reaches more than 4 %, the 
negative effect of the insulating material formed on the sulfur surface 
prevails, and this phenomenon is more obvious at high sulfur loading. 
This work not only illustrates the role of LiNO3 on the electrochemical 
performance of Li–S batteries but also provides effective information 
for the consumption of LiNO3 with gradient sulfur loading under the 
industrialization future, which helps to significantly improve the
