A B S T R A C T  
Achieving large discharge capacity and high cycling stability under high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte 
condition is critical for practical lithium sulfur batteries. However, it is impeded by the large overpotential for 
lithium sulfide (Li2S) nucleation at the cathode, caused by the sluggish reduction kinetics of lithium polysulfide 
and the short-circuit triggered by inhomogeneous Li electroplating at the anode. In this study, we report that 
adding rubidium cation to lean electrolyte in an additive level (0.05 M) can address the two challenges owing to 
the modifications of the cathode and anode surfaces by the formation of rubidium sulfide. At the sulfur cathode, 
the rubidium sulfide acts as a catalyst, accelerating the reduction of lithium polysulfides. At the Li metal anode, a 
rubidium sulfide-enriched inner SEI quickly passivates the surface, suppressing electrolyte decomposition and 
promoting uniform lithium deposition due to its lithiophilicity. The drop-in-solution leads to impressive results, 
delivering more than 3 mAh cm−2 over approximately 80 cycles at a sulfur loading of 3.8 mgsulfur cm−2 and an 
electrolyte to sulfur ratio of 3 μl mg−1.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium sulfur batteries (LSBs) are a promising next generation 
lithium secondary battery due to their high theoretical energy density 
(2600 Wh kg−1), which stems from the low mass density and large 
specific capacity of Li (0.534 g ml−1, 3860 mAh g−1) and S (2.07 g cm−3, 
1672 mAh g−1) [1,2]. However, the actual energy density of LSB is 
limited because an excessive amount of electrolyte is needed, making 
the development of lean electrolyte LSBs a crucial step toward practi­
cality. In this regard, the electrolyte to sulfur (E/S) ratio is a key factor in 
designing LSBs for high energy density [3,4]. To achieve a gravimetric 
energy density greater than 400 Wh kg−1, the E/S ratio must be lower 
than 3.0. 
However, under lean electrolyte conditions, the redox reaction of 
lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) becomes sluggish and the instability of the 
Li/electrolyte interphase intensifies, resulting in low sulfur utilization 
and/or short cycle life [5,6]. The sluggish reduction kinetics of LiPSs 
under lean electrolyte conditions causes a large Li2S nucleation over­
potential and often leads to the cell-voltage-drop to discharge the cut-off 
voltage before reaching a lower plateau, leading to impractical sulfur 
utilization [7–9]. This problem can be mitigated through electrolyte 
design or by adding catalysts to the electrode surface. To avoid electrode 
passivation and accelerate the redox kinetics at lean electrolyte condi­
tions, high donor number salts (NO3
−, SCN−) or solvents (1,3-dimethy­
l-2-imidazolidinone, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, 3-fluoro-pyridine) were 
introduced to the electrolyte [5,9-12]. For the same purpose, electrode 
was modified with Fe based single-atom catalysts, metal oxide catalysts, 
and metal sulfide catalysts [13–16]. Despite the newly designed elec­
trode and electrolytes, achieving an E/S ratio below 3.0 remains a sig­
nificant challenge. Many previous works conducted under lean 
electrolyte conditions have reported sudden cell failure of LSBs at early 
cycles due to electrolyte decomposition or short-circuits caused by Li 
dendrites [9,17-20]. Recent understanding of the irreversibility of the Li 
metal electrode indicates that this problem is associated with limited Li+
transport and uncontrolled Li corrosion by LiPS-concentrated electrolyte 
[21–23]. Therefore, in addition to enhancing the kinetics of LiPS 
reduction at the cathode, it is crucial to protect the Li metal electrode. 
Electrolyte additive is a handy solution to improve battery perfor­
mances. For LSBs, the addition of LiNO3 additive (typically at 0.1 M) is a 
handy solution to improve the capacity and durability of LSBs. However,

under lean electrolyte conditions, the function of LiNO3 is insufficient to 
address the aforementioned problems. Various types of non-ionic, 
cationic, and anionic additives have been suggested to improve the 
performance of LSBs [17,24-33]. However, most of these additives 
address the issues of only a single electrode. A versatile solution to the 
problems of sulfur cathodes and Li metal anodes would be to use elec­
trolyte additives that positively impact both electrodes, such as 1,3, 
5-benzenetrithiol, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, diphenyl diselenide, tri­
fluoromethane sulfonamide, and thioacetamide [34–38]. Although the 
referenced works demonstrated the concept of dual functional additives 
for LSBs, further advances are needed to achieve long cycle life under 
lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ≤3). 
We here propose the use of rubidium cation (Rb+) as a dual- 
functional electrolyte additive that can improve both the sulfur cath­
ode and Li metal anode (Scheme 1). Various strategies using metal 
cation additives have been suggested for LSBs to improve Li revers­
ibility, including formation of Li-Mg alloy by Mg2+, using the electro- 
shielding effect by K+, robust SEI formation by Cu2+ and Ce3+ [27-29, 
39]. However, even with these metal cation additives, the problem of 
sulfur cathodes in lean electrolyte LSBs remains unresolved. In contrast 
to previous metal cation additives, Rb+ facilitates the reduction reaction 
of LiPSs and nucleation of Li2S by forming an Rb2S catalyst at the carbon 
surface of the sulfur cathode. It also forms an Rb2S-enriched SEI layer on 
the Li metal surface, which can reduce the decomposition of PSs and 
promote uniform Li deposition. Through extensive electrochemical 
analysis and DFT simulations, we revealed the dual functionality of Rb+
addition. The Li-S cells with Rb+ addition delivered a high initial 
discharge capacity of 3.5 mAh cm−2 and exhibited high cycling stability 
of over 100 cycles at an E/S of 5 μl mg−1, under discharging/charging at 
0.3 C/0.2 C rate. Remarkably, the Rb+-containing electrolyte enabled 
the operation of LSBs at an E/S of 3 μl mg−1 for 79 cycles. The dual 
function of Rb+ addition provides a drop-in strategy to improve the 
performance and lifespan of lean electrolyte LSBs.

2.2. Preparation and characterization of sulfur electrode 
For morphology analysis of Rb2S, we prepared a carbon nanofiber 
(CNF) electrode as sulfur host as followed method. We first dissolved 1 g 
of poly acrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 150 000, Sigma-Aldrich) in 10 ml of N, 
N-dimethylformamide (DMF, Sigma-Aldrich) and stirred it for 12 h to 
obtain a uniform polymer solution at darkness condition to avoid the 
photoreaction. We used a syringe (21 gauge) and an electric field (17 
kV) was applied between the syringe and an aluminum (Al) foil collector 
to electrospin the solution at a feeding rate of 1 ml h−1. To remove the 
extra solvent at electro-spun fiber, the membrane was dried overnight at 
60 ℃. Before carbonization, the membrane was detached from the Al 
foil and punched into Ø18 and stabilized in ambient air for 3 h at 220 ℃. 
Followed by carbonization for 1 h under N2 flow condition (300 square 
cc min−1) at 800 ℃ with 5 ℃ min−1 heating rate. After the cooling 
process, we dropped a 63 μl (3 mgsulfur cm−2) solution of 0.2 M Li2S8 
onto the 2 mg CNF electrode at 90 ℃ to make an electrode with a S/C 
ratio of 1.7. The morphology of the CNF electrode was observed with a 
scanning electron microscope (SEM, Magellan 400, FEI Co.); observa­
tion results are presented in Fig. S1. The facets and morphologies of Rb2S 
and Li2S formed on the CNF electrode were investigated using field 
emission transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X). To 
evaluate the electrochemical performance of the Rb+-containing elec­
trolytes with practical cell design, we utilized a sulfur electrode with 3.8 
mgsulfur cm−2 sulfur loading from LG energy solution. 
2.3. Computer simulation 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using 
DMol3 package in the Material Studio software. The atomic orbitals 
were described by the double numerical plus polarization (DNP+) basis 
set and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method based on the 
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional and were used to optimize the 
structure or calculate the energy in each configuration. The orbital cut 
off was set at 4.0 Å, and the COSMO model was employed to reflect the 
implicit DME solvent conditions. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations 
were conducted using the Forcite package in the Material Studio soft­
ware. We composed the simulation cells with 241 DME, 357 DOL, 195 
Li+, 80 S4
2−, 25 TFSI−, 10 (or 13) NO3
−, and (3 Rb+) molecules which is 
the same condition of control and 0.05 M Rb electrolyte. A COMPASS III 
force field was used to reflect the atomic and molecular interactions in 
the simulation cells. To examine the diffusional properties of molecules 
in equilibrium state, constructed simulation cells were equilibrated 
under conditions of 1 atm and 298 K in an NPT ensemble over 1 ns. We 
utilized a Nose thermostat and Berndensen barostat to keep the constant 
state of temperature and pressure. The integration time step is set to 1 fs 
considering the bond connection and accuracy of the calculation. A 1 ns 
NVT ensemble simulation was next used to equilibrate the cells at the 
same temperature with constant volume. In equilibrium condition, we 
ran the simulation for 5 ns and determined the diffusion property of the 
molecules through mean squared displacement (MSD).

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Catalytic effects of Rb2S on reduction reaction of LiPSs 
To examine the effect of Rb+ additive on the redox kinetics of the 
sulfur cathode, 0.01 M or 0.05 M RbNO3 was added to the control 
electrolyte (0.5 M LiTFSI in DME/DOL + 0.2 M LiNO3). The concen­
trations of RbNO3 were established by considering the plating of Rb+ on 
the Li metal electrode, which can occur when the redox potential of 
Rb+/Rb is higher than that of Li+/Li. The issue of Rb plating can be 
resolved by reducing the concentration of Rb+ to a certain level. Redox 
potential of Rb+/Rb in 0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte (-3.057 V vs. S.H.E) is 
slightly lower than that in Li+/Li in the control electrolyte (-3.049 V vs S. 
H.E); hence, Rb plating is unlikely to occur in the 0.01 and 0.05 M 
RbNO3 electrolytes. 
Rate capability test was conducted for the LSBs with the control and 
RbNO3-containing electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 1a, discharge capacity 
abruptly drops at a certain C-rate, limiting the high C-rate operation of 
LSB. For the control electrolyte, the capacity drop was found at 0.4 C 
(2.54 mA cm−2). However, with the addition of RbNO3, the capacity 
drop shifted to a higher C rate; the cell stably operated up to 0.5 C (3.18 
mA cm−2) at 0.01 M RbNO3 and 0.6 C (3.82 mA cm−2) at 0.05 M RbNO3, 
demonstrating the additive’s enhancement of the redox kinetics of the 
sulfur cathode. 
Comparison of voltage profiles at discharge/charge rates of 0.4 C/0.3 
C for the three electrolytes (Fig. 1b) shows that the low rate capability of 
the control electrolyte is caused by stoppage of the discharge reaction 
before reaching the lower plateau (Li2S4 to Li2S reduction reaction) due 
to large Li2S nucleation overpotential. In contrast, for the 0.01 M and 
0.05 M RbNO3-containing electrolytes, the voltage dip did not reach the 
cut-off voltage, allowing the discharge reaction to continue to the lower 
plateau region. Although the control electrolyte exhibited a lower 
plateau at a lower discharge rate (0.3 C), its overpotentials were 
significantly larger than those of the RbNO3-containing electrolytes 
(Fig. S2).

The formation of Rb2S was detected in the cathodic scan of the sulfur 
electrode (Fig. S3). Only for the RbNO3-containing electrolytes did a 
shoulder cathodic peak appear at approximately 2.11 V in the linear 
sweep voltammetry curve. In the discharge curve of the Li-S cell with the 
0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte (Fig. S4), two voltage dips appeared, in 
contrast to the single voltage dip for the control electrolyte, indicating 
the sequential formation of Rb2S and Li2S nucleation. The formation of 
Rb2S can be further verified in the TEM image of the CNF electrode taken 
from the Li-S cell after galvanostatic discharge to 2.15 V, in which Rb2S 
formed while Li2S did not. As can be seen in Fig. 1c, sub-micron-sized 
Rb2S particles were present on the surface of the CNF electrode. 
The decrease in Li2S nucleation overpotential due to the addition of 
RbNO3 suggests that Rb2S can function as a catalyst for the reduction 
reaction of Li2S, thereby enhancing Li2S nucleation. To demonstrate 
this, a Li-S cell equipped with CNF-based cathode was discharged at 0.05 
C rate until it reached 2.1 V. The CNF electrode was then investigated by 
SEM. The CNF electrode in 0.05 M LiNO3-containing electrolyte 
exhibited uniformly distributed nodular Li2S particles, in contrast to the 
sparsely distributed Li2S particles formed in the control electrolyte 
(Fig. S5). The HAADF TEM image and the selected area diffraction 
(SAED) pattern of the Rb2S particles shown in Fig. 1c indicate that the 
(222) surface facet of Li2S nanocrystal is combined with the (110) sur­
face facet of Rb2S (Fig. S6), supporting the Rb2S-assisted Li2S 
nucleation. 
Using DFT calculations, we investigated the interaction between 
Rb2S and various sulfur species (Li2S6, Li2S4, and Li2S2, and Li2S). Our 
results show that, different from the repulsive interaction between sulfur 
species and the carbon surface, the Rb2S surface has an attractive 
interaction with LiPSs, Li2S2, and Li2S (Table S1). This suggests that the 
Rb2S surface is enriched by LiPSs, thereby facilitating the reduction of 
these species. The larger binding energy of Li2S on the Rb2S surface than 
on the carbon surface further corroborates the nucleation of Li2S on the 
Rb2S surface. 
To fully understand the catalytic effect of Rb2S in the reduction re­
action of LiPSs, we investigated the electronic structure of alkali metal 
sulfides. As shown in Fig. S7, the band gap of alkali metal sulfides de­
creases as the period of the alkali metal increases. Rb2S is a p-type 
semiconductor with a band gap of 2.11 eV; this band gap is the lowest 
possible energy required for electrical conduction. Only Rb2S can 
effectively reduce the band gap of S4
2−species. Fig. 1d shows the band 
gap transition of S4
2−in contact with various substrates (Li2S, Na2S, K2S, 
Rb2S). The band gap of S4
2−drops from 1.96 eV to 1.04 eV when the 
material is in contact with Rb2S surface; it remains nearly unchanged 
when the material is in contact with Li2S (1.93 eV), Na2S (1.86 eV), and 
K2S (1.75 eV). Narrowing of band gap originates from orbital hybridi­
zation between the Rb2S and S4
2−(Fig. S8). A comparison of the HOMO 
and LUMO energy levels reveals that the electrons at the HOMO energy 
level of S4
2−are shared with the conduction band of Rb2S (Fig. S9). It is 
worth noting that while other alkali metal cations, such as Na+ and K+, 
exhibit reduced Li2S nucleation overpotential, similar to that of Rb+, 
their effects on rate capability were not as significant (Fig. S10). This 
difference in efficacy is believed to be due to the narrowing of the band 
gap of S4
2−only at the Rb2S surface. 
Comparing 0.05 M and 0.01 M RbNO3-containing electrolytes, the 
overvoltage in the lower plateau was smaller with higher RbNO3 con­
tent, showing that the reduction of Li2S4 is facilitated by Rb2S. The free 
energy diagrams of the LiPSs on graphene and on the (110) Rb2S surface 
facet (Fig. 1e) explain the facilitated Li2S4 reduction reaction. The 
conversion from Li2S6 to Li2S4 does not have a free energy barrier on the 
graphene and Rb2S surface. However, the conversion from Li2S4 to Li2S2 
or Li2S on the graphene surface is hindered by the large free energy 
barrier; this accounts for the large overpotential observed for the control 
electrolyte. In contrast, Rb2S strongly interacts with the solid discharge 
products, leading to a lack of free energy barrier for Li2S2 or Li2S for­
mation on the Rb2S surface. Thus, the presence of a larger amount of 
Rb2S on the electrode surface results in a lower Li2S nucleation over­
voltage and a higher cell voltage in the lower plateau regime. 
The positive effect of Rb2S on the sulfur cathode reaction was further 
investigated using the potentiostatic intermittent titration technique 
(PITT) and in-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). In the 
PITT experiment, the cell voltage was decreased stepwise from OCV to 
1.8 V at an interval of 20 mV. The cell voltages were maintained for 
either 2 h or until the current dropped below 30 μA. Fig. S11 shows the 
current responses upon step-down of the cell voltage for the control and 
0.05 M RbNO3 electrolytes. In the range of OCV to 2.14 V, the two cells 
showed no meaningful difference; however, for the 2.12 and 2.10 V 
steps, the discharge current peak was much larger for the 0.05 M RbNO3 
electrolyte, indicating that the conversion from short-chain LiPSs to 
solid discharge products occurred more readily with RbNO3 addition.

consequent large overvoltage [8]. The conversion of Li2S4 to Li2S re­
duces the concentration of LiPSs in the electrolyte phase, leading to a 
decrease in ohmic resistance [21]. For both control and 0.05 M RbNO3 
electrolytes, in-situ EIS analysis was conducted at different states of

3.2. Protection of Li metal anode by Rb2S-containing SEI 
An alkali metal cation additive in the electrolyte can induce uniform 
Li deposition through the self-healing electrostatic shield (SHES) 
mechanism [41–43]. For example, in lithium metal batteries, Cs+ ad­
ditives in the electrolyte adsorb onto Li protrusions and hinder Li den­
dritic growth. Similarly, for LSB, an electrostatic shielding effect 
induced by K+ has been previously suggested [27]. However, the addi­
tion of Rb+ to LSB cannot induce the SHES effect because Rb+ readily 
forms insoluble Rb2S. Considering previous works on the use of Ce 
(NO3)3 and Cu(CH3COO)2 that report the formation of Ce2S3 and 
CuS-containing SEI on Li metal surface, the possibility of Rb2S-enriched 
SEI was explored [28,29]. 
To evaluate the impact of Rb+ addition on the reversibility of the Li 
metal electrode, we examined the Li deposit morphology in a Li-Ni half- 
cell under 0.5 mA cm−2 / 1 mAh cm−2 conditions, with and without the 
additive. To simulate lean electrolyte operation at an E/S ratio of 5, 1.6 
M of Li2S4 was added to the electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 2a, RbNO3 
addition resulted in a distinct difference in Li deposit morphology. The 
Ni electrode surface in the control electrolyte was partially covered by a

The effect of Rb+ addition on electrolyte decomposition was further 
investigated by measuring the capacity generated from electrolyte

The presence of Rb2S in the SEI layer, as a result of the addition of 
Rb+, was confirmed by TEM. As shown in Fig. 2f and g, the SEI layer in 
the 0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte was uniform and thin, with a thickness of 
29.2 nm, different from the SEI layer in the control electrolyte, which 
was 1.73 times thicker (50.8 nm). The crystal structure of the SEI layer 
formed in the 0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte was analyzed using HAADF- 
TEM imaging and X-ray diffraction patterns (Fig. S16). The SEI layer 
was found to have a mosaic structure consisting of nanocrystalline Rb2S 
(202), Li2O (002), and LiF (200). The Rb2S phases were highly 
concentrated at the inner side of the SEI layer, indicating that Rb2S 
formed before the decomposition of the solvents and lithium salts. 
Using the Li-Ni half-cell, the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution 
was assessed for the 1.6 M Li2S4-containing control and 0.05 M RbNO3 
electrolytes (Fig. 3a). To eliminate the influence of the current collector, 
3 mAh cm−2 of Li was plated and 10 cycles of Li stripping/plating pro­
cess were performed at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 conditions [46]. The 
average coulombic efficiency was calculated over the 10 cycles, as well 
as during the initial plating and final stripping. The average coulombic 
efficiency was 94.1% for the control electrolyte, 95.8% for the 0.01 M 
RbNO3 electrolyte, and 98.1% for the 0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte. The 
uniform Li deposition and improved Li reversibility with Rb+ addition 
can be attributed to the lithiophilicity of Rb2S. The binding energies of 
the Li adatom on the Rb2S surfaces (202 and 200 facets) and Li surfaces 
(110, 111 facets) (Fig. 3b) show that the Rb2S surfaces strongly interact 
with the Li deposit, demonstrating its lithiophilic property. The lith­
iophilic Rb2S layer can promote uniform Li deposition morphology, 
thereby improving Li metal reversibility.

3.3. Electrochemical evaluation of Li-S cells with RbNO3 additive 
Performance of Li-S cell was evaluated using control and 0.05 M 
RbNO3 electrolytes with practical cell design parameters (sulfur loading: 
3.8 mgs cm−2; E/S: 5 μl mg−1) and operation conditions (discharge/ 
charge: 0.3 C/0.2 C, 1 C = 1672 mAh gs
−1). The first cycle voltage pro­
files of the two cells (Fig. 4a) show that the Li2S nucleation overpotential 
decreased with RbNO3 addition due to in-situ formed Rb2S at the sulfur 
electrode. Furthermore, the decreased overpotential in the lower 
plateau region indicates the catalytic effect of Rb2S. As shown in Fig. 4b, 
stable cycling performance over 100 cycles was achieved when using the 
RbNO3-containing electrolyte; this contrasts with the sudden cell failure 
of the control electrolyte at 21 cycles. The voltage profile at cycle 21 
indicates that cell failure was caused by a short-circuit caused by Li 
dendrites (Fig. S18). The improved Li metal stability induced by the 
robust Rb2S SEI layer ameliorated the cycling performance of the Li-S 
cells. The higher coulombic efficiencies in the Rb electrolyte (Fig. 4c) 
demonstrate that reduced electrolyte decomposition, including that of 
LiPSs, and uniform Li deposition contribute to improved cycling per­
formance of Li-S cells. 
We compared the sulfur and Li electrode morphologies after 10 cy­
cles for the control and Rb electrolyte. As shown in Fig. S19a and b, the 
carbon electrode surfaces were covered by Li2S precipitate for both two 
electrolytes. However, when comparing the elemental compositions of 
the surfaces using EDX mapping analysis, the Rb electrolyte showed a 
higher content of sulfur (Fig. S20) in line with its higher discharge ca­
pacity. The comparison of Li electrode (Fig. S19c and d) exhibited more 
pronounced difference between the two electrolytes; more uniform Li 
deposition morphology was observed with the Rb electrolyte due to the 
lithiophilic inner SEI layer, Rb2S. To evaluate the relative proportion of 
the Rb2S formed at the cathode and anode surface, we traced the change 
in the Rb+ content in the electrolyte phase using the ICP-MS method 
before and after discharge as described in the experimental section. 
During the discharge, the Rb+ content in the electrolyte is reduced due 
to the formation of Rb2S in the sulfur electrode. We assumed that the 
remained Rb+ after discharge would form Rb2S SEI layer at Li surface 
during charge. As shown in Fig. S21, the remained Rb+ content in the 
electrolyte after the discharge was 8.5 time lower than before the 
discharge. Therefore, the amount of Rb2S on the sulfur cathode is much 
larger than that in SEI on the Li anode. 
By measuring the LiPS shuttle current for the Li-S full cell (Fig. S22), 
we demonstrated reduced decomposition of LiPS at the Li metal elec­
trode. Maintaining the sulfur electrode potential at 2.35 V during 
charging leads to a steady state, a balance between formation of LiPSs at 
the sulfur electrode and its decomposition at the Li metal electrode [47]. 
The steady-state shuttle current was two times lower for the 0.05 M 
RbNO3 electrolyte than the for control, evidence of reduced LiPS 
decomposition at the Li metal electrode due to the Rb2S-enriched SEI 
layer. 
Empowered by the dual positive effects of Rb2S in both electrodes, Li- 
S cells operated under extremely lean electrolyte condition (E/S = 3 μl 
mg−1) at 0.15 C (discharging) / 0.1 C (charging) rate (Fig. 4d). In our 
test, the charge/discharge C rates were decreased with reducing the E/S 
ratio to prevent abrupt drop of cell voltage reaching the lower voltage 
plateau during discharge. In Li-S batteries, the coulombic efficiency

The LiPS concentration in the electrolyte phase increases with 
decreasing E/S ratio, leading to more significant cathodic decomposi­

described in this manuscript. Therefore, the highly concentrated LiPSs in

As cycling goes on, the corrosion can be aggravated due to the rough­

capacity fade was observed for the control electrolyte due to the large 
Li2S nucleation overpotential. However, the 0.05 M RbNO3 electrolyte 
exhibited stable cycling performance over 79 cycles. This shows that, 
even in the extremely lean electrolyte condition, Rb2S can effectively 
function as a catalyst for the reduction of Li2S4 to Li2S. The charge/di­
scharge voltage profiles of the Rb electrolyte for selected cycles 
(Fig. S23) show that Li2S nucleation overpotential gradually increased 
and overcharging became more prevalent with cycling, indicating an 
increase in LiPS concentration in the electrolyte phase as the electrolyte 
was gradually consumed. 
Rb+ addition enables the operation of the Li-S cell even at an E/S 
ratio of 2.5 μl mg−1 (Fig. 4e). At this E/S ratio, the control electrolyte 
delivered only 232 mAh g−1 during cycling due to an abrupt voltage 
drop in the upper plateau region; however, the 0.05 M RbNO3 electro­
lyte exhibited a first discharge capacity of 1175 mAh g−1 and a well- 
developed lower plateau (Fig. S24). Fig. 4f compares the energy den­
sity, E/S ratio, and cycling stability of the previously presented lean 
electrolyte LSBs and our results, revealing that the 0.05 M RbNO3 
electrolyte offers impressive energy density at an E/S ratio of 2.5 or 3.0. 
The efficacy of the Rb electrolyte was demonstrated with a higher 
sulfur loading (5 mgsulfur cm−2). Fig. S25a shows the cycling perfor­
mance of the Li-S cells at E/S = 5 μl mg−1 and at 0.2 C/0.1 C (discharge/ 
charge). Contrary to the cell failure after 65 cycle with the control 
electrolyte, the Rb electrolyte exhibited stable operation for 87 cycles. 
At a lower E/S of 3 μl mg−1, the Rb electrolyte and control electrolyte 
provided 37 and 23 cycles of operation, respectively (Fig. S25b). 
4. Conclusions 
In this work, we have demonstrated that the addition of Rb+ to 
electrolyte provides dual benefits for Li-S batteries. Preformed Rb2S 
particles can facilitate the reduction of Li2S4 and act as nucleation sites 
for Li2S. As a result, Li-S batteries can operate in extremely lean elec­
trolyte conditions (E/S = 3 and 2.5 μl mg−1). The lithiophilic Rb2S layer, 
serving as an inner SEI layer, promotes uniform Li deposition and hin­
ders further decomposition of the electrolyte components, including 
LiPSs. This leads to improved Li/electrolyte interphase and enables 
system to achieve 100 cycles in lean electrolyte condition (E/S=5 μl 
mg−1) and 79 cycles in extremely lean electrolyte condition (E/S =3 μl 
mg−1). Li-S batteries containing RbNO3 salt exhibit superior rate capa­
bility, stable cycling performance, and high coulombic efficiency in lean 
electrolyte conditions. These results indicate that the major challenges 
encountered in the development of LSBs for harsh conditions can be 
effectively overcome and thus the Rb+ additive can be a handy solution 
for lean electrolyte LSBs. The concept presented in this work reveals the 
mechanism of simultaneous anode and cathode improvement through 
an electrochemical reaction between electrolyte additive and active 
material, paving the way for novel electrolyte design.
