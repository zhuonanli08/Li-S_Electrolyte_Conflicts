Lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) serve as vital intermediates in working Li–S batteries.
However, the existing form of LiPSs in electrolyte has not yet been clearly eluci-
dated. In this contribution, we identify cationic LiPSs as the main species in elec-
trolyte of Li–S batteries. Kinetic evaluations further indicate that cationic LiPSs are
disadvantageous to battery performance, and accordingly, decreasing the Li salt
concentration can inhibit the formation of cationic LiPSs and improve the energy
density of practical Li–S pouch cells.

SUMMARY
Lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs) serve as vital intermediates that partici-
pate in every working electrochemical process of lithium–sulfur
(Li–S) batteries. Revealing the existing form of LiPSs in electrolyte is
essential for understanding the reaction mechanism and rationally
designing high-performance Li–S batteries. Herein, we pioneeringly
demonstrate that LiPSs are weakly dissociated in electrolyte but
exhibit a strong tendency to bond extra lithium ions and form cationic
LiPSs (e.g., Li3S6
+). Kinetic evaluations further reveal that cationic
LiPSs are more sluggish in cathode reactions while more aggressive
to react with Li metal anodes. Accordingly, decreasing the salt concen-
tration is demonstrated to inhibit the formation of cationic LiPSs and is
manifested effective to improve the performance of practical high-
energy-density Li–S pouch cells. This work reveals the existence of
cationic LiPSs in electrolyte and affords directional guidance on
improving the electrochemical performance of Li–S batteries.
INTRODUCTION
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries promise great potential to substitute the current Li-ion
batteries and serve as next-generation high-energy-density energy storage devices
due to the intrinsic advantage of ultrahigh theoretical energy density up to 2,600
Wh kg1.1,2 The working mechanism of conventional Li–S batteries follows multi-
step electrochemical processes with a series of dissolved lithium polysulﬁdes (LiPSs)
as the reaction intermediates.3 Typically, elemental sulfur is reduced to dissolved
LiPSs with a general formula of Li2Sn (n = 4, 6, or 8) during the initial discharge process.
Long-chain LiPSs (for instance, Li2S8) are ﬁrstly generated,4 and the sulfur chain of LiPSs
shortens in the subsequent reduction processes.5,6 Following that, insoluble lithium
sulﬁde (Li2S) precipitates on the cathode surface by the reduction of short-chain
LiPSs, and the discharge process terminates eventually.7,8 Parallel to the above elec-
trochemical processes, the dissolved LiPSs also participate in chemical disproportion-
ation and comproportionation reactions, act as intrinsic redox mediators that chemi-
cally react with sulfur or Li2S, and impact the concurrently occurred electrochemical
processes.9–11 Meanwhile, the dissolved LiPSs tend to diffuse from the cathode to
the anode under concentration gradient and further react with Li metal anodes. The
so-called LiPS shuttle effect renders reduced Coulombic efﬁciency, severe anode
corrosion, irreversible loss of active materials, and consequently rapid failure of Li–S
batteries.12 Therefore, soluble LiPSs serve as vital intermediates in the Li–S system,
and their behaviors greatly inﬂuence the performance of practical Li–S batteries.13–15
Rationally regulating the behavior of the dissolved LiPSs is expected to sufﬁciently
improve the cathode redox kinetics and inhibit the shuttle effect. For instance,

Several pioneering studies have provided spectral information on the structure of
LiPSs through nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy,32,33 liquid chroma-
tography (LC),34,35 ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV–vis) spectroscopy,36,37 and
X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy.38,39 These character-
izations provide valuable insights into the composition and evolution of LiPSs during
discharge and charge processes. However, single spectral information offers limited
information on an individual LiPS species due to the inherent complexity of LiPSs.40
For instance, 7Li NMR spectroscopy reﬂects the chemical environment of Li atoms in
LiPSs, but only an average chemical shift signal toward mixed LiPSs can be re-
corded.32,41 Sulfur K-edge XANES spectroscopy reveals the average Li/S ratio by
determining the atomic ratio of terminal and bridge sulfur, although it is still unable
to provide detailed information on the speciﬁc conﬁguration or the solvation struc-
ture.39,42 On the other hand, theoretical calculations such as density functional the-
ory (DFT) calculations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide thermody-
namic and structural information on individual LiPS molecules43,44 and are efﬁcient
to reveal the interaction between LiPSs and solvents or Li salts. For example, Rajput
et al. points out that LiPSs tend to aggregate with strong Li+–Sn
2 interaction by MD
simulation.45 Gao and co-workers determine the equilibrium constant between
neutral and ionized Li2S6 by ab initio calculations and ﬁnd that neutral Li2S6 is domi-
nant in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent.46 However, there is still a lack of exper-

In this contribution, the existence of cationic LiPSs is experimentally identiﬁed in
electrolyte of Li–S batteries for the ﬁrst time. Conductivity analysis provides quanti-
tative information on the ionization equilibrium constants of LiPSs and reveals their
weak dissociation behaviors. A combination of electron spray ionization mass spec-
trometry (ESI-MS), 7Li NMR spectroscopy, and theoretical calculations further proves
that LiPSs prefer to bond extra Li+ provided by additional Li salts to form well-
deﬁned cationic LiPSs. Kinetic evaluations indicate the cationic LiPSs are unfavor-
able regarding the cathode redox reactions while demonstrate high tendency to
react with Li metal anode due to the electrostatic interactions of the electrical double
layers. As a result, an optimization strategy of decreasing the salt concentration to
reduce the number of cationic LiPS is proposed and manifested to be effective in
practical high-energy-density Li–S pouch cells. This work indicates the existence of
cationic LiPSs in electrolyte of Li–S batteries and provides directional guidance on
the performance promotion of Li–S batteries.

Kinetic evaluation on cationic LiPSs
As is discussed above, the dissolved LiPSs exist in the forms of cations rather than
neutral molecules or anions in Li–S electrolyte with Li salts. It is conceivable that
such LiPS–Li+ interaction will inﬂuence the electrochemical behaviors of dissolved
LiPSs. To evaluate the kinetics of the cationic LiPSs in working Li–S batteries, several
kinetic evaluations were carried out. First, the adsorption behavior of Li2S6 on the
cathode substrate was evaluated by UV–vis spectroscopy. By employing graphene
(G) as the adsorbent, the adsorption capacity-time proﬁles of Li2S6 were recorded
and ﬁtted to obtain the kinetic parameters regarding the equilibrium adsorption ca-
pacity (QM) and the adsorption rate constant (k) (Figures 5A and S12). With the same
initial Li2S6 concentration, Li2S6 with LiTFSI shows a signiﬁcant decrease in QM
compared with pure Li2S6 from 2.176 to 1.328 mmol gG
1. The rate of adsorption
is also decreased from 0.073 to 0.067 h1, indicating that the adsorption of LiPS is
inhibited. The above phenomenon is not due to the competitive adsorption by
LiTFSI but the intrinsic difference between neutral and cationic LiPSs as evidenced
by the negligible adsorption of sole LiTFSI on G (Table S2). Considering the similar
structure but different charges between neutral Li2S6 and cationic Li3S6

reﬂect the reaction rate between the dissolved LiPSs in electrolyte and the Li metal

addition of 1.0 M LiTFSI, which is two times higher than the blank Li2S6 solution of
0.014 mA. The increased shuttle current indicates that the cationic LiPSs react
more aggressively against Li metal anodes to consume metallic Li.
Cationic LiPSs exhibit sluggish redox kinetics at cathode, whereas high activity at
anode. The above phenomenon can be explained by the interaction between the
positively charged cationic LiPSs and the electric double layer of the electrodes.
Generally, cations are adsorbed excessively on the Helmholtz plane, and the Li
anode is negatively charged.54 On the contrary, the sulfur cathode is net positive
charged with anions excessively adsorbed due to the electrochemical equilibrium
of the LiPSs as evidenced by the experiment on the potential of zero charge (PZC)
(Figure S16). Therefore, compared with neutral LiPS molecules, cationic LiPSs are
more likely to be repulsed by the cathode and more difﬁcult to enter the Helmholtz
plane to participate in the electrochemical reactions (Figure 5F). On the anode side,
however, cationic LiPSs are to be adsorbed more easily to the surface of Li metal
anode because of electrostatic attraction to render side reactions with Li metal
anode and aggravated shuttle effect. In brief, the different structures of the electric
double layers of the cathode and the anode lead to reversed order of kinetics of the
cationic LiPSs.
The above kinetic measurements reveal that the cationic LiPSs are sluggish at cath-
ode but active at anode, indicating an overall harmful effect to the Li–S battery per-
formances. Therefore, a strategy of decreasing the Li salt concentration to reduce
the cationic LiPS fraction is proposed to improve the battery performance. To man-
ifest the as-proposed strategy, rate performances of Li–S coin cells with different
concentrations of LiTFSI were measured (Figure 6A). The Li–S coin cells with LiTFSI
concentrations from 50 to 500 mM exhibit similar speciﬁc capacities of approxi-
mately 880, 750, 700, and 590 mAh g1 at 0.1 , 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 C, respectively.
However, when the rate reaches 4.0C, the Li–S coin cells with 50 mM LiTFSI still
deliver a fair speciﬁc capacity of 432 mAh g1 while the Li–S coin cells with 100 or
500 mM LiTFSI exhibit a signiﬁcantly declined speciﬁc capacity of 150.2 or 31.7
mAh g1, respectively. The corresponding voltage–capacity proﬁles at 4.0 C further
elucidate the difference in rate performances (Figure 6B). With a high LiTFSI
