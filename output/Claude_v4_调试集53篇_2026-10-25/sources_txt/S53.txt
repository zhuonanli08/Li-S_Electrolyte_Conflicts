[M0002 p1] A R T I C L E I N F O Keywords: Li-S battery HPLC Polysulfide shuttle Sulfolane electrolyte LiNO3 additive A B S T R A C T The polysulfide shuttle is a well-known side reaction in rechargeable Lithium-Sulfur batteries with nonaqueous liquid electrolytes.
[M0003 p1] To address this thermodynamically favorable reaction, extensive research has focused on developing novel electrolyte systems.
[M0004 p1] Recently, sulfolane-based electrolyte has gained attention due to their potential to inhibit the polysulfide shuttle in Li-S batteries.
[M0005 p1] In this preliminary investigation, we analyzed the reactivity of polysulfides with Li anode using HPLC and studied the viscosity of various electrolytes at different temperatures.
[M0006 p1] Our findings suggested that high viscosity of the sulfolane electrolyte could contribute to its ability to suppress the polysulfide shuttle.
[M0007 p1] Additionally, we demonstrated that the presence of a LiNO3 additive is crucial for mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect in sulfolane electrolytes, especially at elevated temperature. 1.
[M0008 p1] Introduction Nonaqueous liquid electrolytes are essential in lithium-based rechargeable batteries, as they maintain the mass transfer and the ionic conductivity.
[M0009 p1] Those electrolytes must exhibit both chemical and electrochemical stability during redox reactions occurring at the bat­ tery’s cathodes and anodes [1–6].
[M0010 p1] For state-of-the-art lithium-ion bat­ teries (LiBs), the most commonly used electrolyte is lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) in a carbonate mixture with organic or inorganic additives.
[M0011 p1] However, carbonate-based electrolytes are chemi­ cally incompatible with lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, particularly when using elemental sulfur (S8) as the cathode active materials [7].
[M0012 p1] Current research has focused on using lithium bis(tri­ fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSi) in a dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) mixture with lithium nitrate (LiNO3) as additive [3, 7].
[M0013 p1] This ether-based electrolyte has demonstrated moderate polysulfide solubility, good chemical and electrochemical stability, and high sulfur utilization, along with improved redox reversibility for Li-S batteries.
[M0014 p1] As a result, Li-S batteries using this ether-based electrolyte demonstrate enhanced cycling stability and a significant reduction in the notorious “polysulfide shuttle effect”.
[M0015 p1] However, the cycling stability of Li-S batteries with this ether-based electrolyte still falls short of that observed in Li-ion batteries using carbonate-based electrolyte.
[M0016 p1] Additionally, the continuous decomposi­ tion of the LiNO3 additive on the lithium anode raises concerns about the long-term stability of Li-S batteries [3].
[M0017 p1] Consequently, the search for novel electrolytes that can improve long-term cycling stability and minimize (or even eliminate) the “pol­ ysulfide shuttle effect” remains central focus of Li-S battery research.
[M0018 p1] Various alternative electrolytes have been explored, including those based on ionic liquids [8], poly-glymes [9], Ɛ-caprolactam/acetamide [10], fluorinated ethers [11,12], 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI) [13], fluorinated pyridine [14].

[M0028 p2] Experimental section 2.1.
[M0029 p2] Chemicals Sulfur (99.98 %), lithium sulfide (Li2S, 99.98 %), lithium nitrate (LiNO3,99.99 %), HPLC grade methanol, HPLC grade water, methyl triflate, sulfolane (SL) (from Sigma), dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-diox­ olane (DOL), and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSi) (battery grade from 3M) were purchased and used without further treatment. 2.2.
[M0030 p2] Sample preparation and methods The polysulfide solutions used in this work were 0.1 M stoichio­ metric Li2S6 in various electrolytes including 1.5 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL (1:1), 1.0 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL(1:1) with 0.5 M LiNO3, 1.5 M LiTFSi/SL, 1.0 M LiTFSi/SL with 0.5 M LiNO3.
[M0031 p2] These solutions were prepared by mixing S8 and Li2S (molar ratio of 5:8, to form stoichiometric Li2S6) in the different electrolytes, followed by stirring for 48 h at room tem­ perature in Ar-filled glovebox (Innovative Technology).
[M0032 p2] The chemical stability of Li metal against 0.1 M Li2S6 in the different electrolytes were tested by adding 50 mg lithium chip into sample vials with air-tight caps, containing 1.0 mL of the polysulfide solution in each electrolyte.
[M0033 p2] After vertexing the polysulfide solutions with the lithium chips for 5 min, the vials were stored at room temperature or at 60 ◦C for 4 days.
[M0034 p2] Finally, specific amount of the polysulfide solutions was deriv­ atized with methyl triflate in air-tight HPLC sample vials before removed from the glovebox for HPLC analysis.
[M0035 p2] The derivatization of the polysulfide solution was carried out as following: 950 μL of DME and 50 μL of methyl triflate were added into a 2 mL air-tight HPLC sample vial.
[M0036 p2] The mixture was vortexed for 1 min.
[M0037 p2] Then 50 μL of the polysulfide solution was added and the mixture was vortexed for an additional 2 min.
[M0038 p2] This process converted the polysulfides (Sn 2−) into organosulfides (CH3SnCH3) which were stable under HPLC mobile phase conditions and can be separated based on the chain length of sulfur [40–42].
[M0039 p2] A Shimadzu LC-20AD quaternary pump. equipped with a Shimadzu SIL-20A autosampler was used to deliver a methanol/water mobile phase through a Shimadzu C18 column (4.6*50 mm) at a flow rate of 0.70 mL/min.
[M0040 p2] The injection volume for the derivatized samples was 5 μL.
[M0041 p2] A binary gradient of mobile phases was used to elute the injected sample under the following condition: 25 % methanol (75 % water) at 0 min, 100 % methanol at 10 min, maintained until 23 min and back to 25 % methanol at 26 min.
[M0042 p2] The entire flow from the HPLC was introduced into the Shimadzu SPD-M20A detector, and the full wavelength range from 190 to 800 nm was recorded by Shimadzu LabSolutions Lite soft­ ware.
[M0043 p2] The chromatograms discussed in this work were replotted from collected data at 230 nm.
[M0044 p2] Sulfur cathodes were prepared by first mixing S (60%wt) with CMK3 mesoporous carbon (20%wt) through grinding.
[M0045 p2] The mixture was then sealed in an autoclave filled with argon and heated at 170 ◦C for 12 h.
[M0046 p2] After cooling down to room temperature, carbon black Super C-65(10% wt) and PVDF binder (10%wt) were added into the mixture, with N- Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as solvent to form homogenous slurry.
[M0047 p2] This slurry was coated onto an alumina foil by a doctor-blade coater, and vacuum-dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h.
[M0048 p2] Finally, the electrode sheet was punched into disks and transferred into an argon-filled glovebox (both oxygen and moisture are less than 5 ppm) for 24 h before being tested.
[M0049 p2] The sulfur cathodes have a theoretical areal sulfur loading around 1.5 mg cm−2.
[M0050 p2] All battery tests were conducted in two-electrode CR2032 coin cells at either room temperature or at 60 ◦C.
[M0051 p2] Each battery used 15 μL of different electrolytes, with a Celgard 3401 membrane as the separator and lithium chip as the anodes.
[M0052 p2] After assembled in the Ar-filled glove­ box, all batteries were rested for 12 h before electrochemical testing.
[M0053 p2] For cycling tests, a galvanostatic discharge/charge procedure was conduct­ ed between 1.7 and 2.9 V at a constant rate of 0.05 C for all batteries by a battery testing system (Arbin BT2000).
[M0054 p2] For the elevated temperature test, the batteries were cycled at 60 ◦C in a temperature and humidity chamber (CSZ Z8 Plus).
[M0055 p2] The viscosity measurements of different electrolytes and solvents were performed using a viscometer (Brookfield DV-II+ Pro) with a water bath to control the temperature. 3.
[M0056 p2] Results and discussion As shown in Fig. 1(A), at room temperature, Li-S battery (based on the same S8/CMK3 composite cathodes) using 1.5 M LiTFSi/DME/DOL electrolyte exhibits a noticeable “polysulfide shuttle effect” due to the lack of LiNO3.
[M0057 p2] In contrast, as shown in Fig. 1(B), the Li-S battery with 1.5 M LiTFSi/SL electrolyte shows no significant “polysulfide shuttle effect” even after 200 cycles, despite not having the LiNO3 additive.
[M0058 p2] Further analysis of the chemical reactivity of lithium metal with 0.1 M Li2S6 solutions in either sulfolane electrolyte or in DME/DOL elec­ trolyte was conducted using HPLC.
[M0059 p2] The results illustrated in Fig. 2 indicated that the polysulfide solution in the sulfolane electrolyte is less reactive with lithium metal compared to the polysulfide solution in DME/DOL electrolyte.
[M0060 p2] This is clearly shown in Fig. 2(B), where the polysulfide profile of the DME/DOL electrolyte after 4 days of contact with lithium metal differs significantly from the original polysulfide solution, as shown in Fig. 2 (A).
[M0061 p2] The chromatographic peaks corre­ sponding to S3 2−and S4 2−increased while those belonging to S6 2−, S7 2−, S8 2− and S8 decreased after the reaction [41].
[M0062 p2] On the other hand, the polysulfide profile in Fig. 2 (F) (representing sulfolane electrolyte after 4 days of contact with lithium metal) is quite similar to the original sulfolane electrolyte shown in Fig. 2 (E), with only a noticeable change of the chromatographic peak corresponding to S8.
[M0063 p2] Both electrochemical cycling data (Fig. 1) and HPLC analysis (Fig. 2) indicate that the “polysulfide shuttle effect” in Li/S battery with sulfo­ lane electrolyte is minimized, if not eliminated, even without LiNO3 additive.
[M0064 p2] Unfortunately, at elevated temperatures (60 ◦C), both Li-S batteries using sulfolane electrolyte and DME/DOL electrolyte without LiNO3 additive exhibit the polysulfide shuttle effect, though to different ex­ tents, as shown in Fig. 1 (C) and 1 (D).
[M0065 p2] In the case of the Li-S battery with DME/DOL electrolyte (without LiNO3), the shuttle effect is pronounced, preventing the battery from cycling from the start as shown in Fig. 1 (C).
[M0066 p2] For the Li-S battery with sulfolane electrolyte (without LiNO3), the shuttle effect becomes noticeable after 5 discharge/charge cycles (with about 10 % overcharging), and the battery fails to cycle after 35 discharge/charge cycles due to the severe and notorious shuttle effect as shown in Fig. 1 (D).
[M0067 p2] The HPLC analysis in Fig. 2, which examines the chemical stability of Li metal with polysulfide solutions in different electrolytes at 60 ◦C, aligns with the battery test observations.
[M0068 p2] In DME/DOL electrolyte without LiNO3, Li metal reacted aggressively with long-chain poly­ sulfides at elevated temperature.
[M0069 p2] The HPLC chromatogram (Fig. 2 (C)) shows only short-chain polysulfide (S3 2−) and medium-chain polysulfides (S4 2−and S5 2−) in very low abundance.

[M0080 p4] For example, in the early work by S.
[M0081 p4] [23], a series of electrolytes with a binary solvent composition of sulfolane and DME were tested.
[M0082 p4] The results clearly showed that when the sulfolane to DME volume ratio decreased to 7:3, the polysulfide shuttle effect emerged and negatively impacted the performance of Li-S battery.
[M0083 p4] Fundamentally, the poly­ sulfide shuttle phenomenon originates from the thermodynamically favorable reaction between lithium metal and long-chain polysulfides.
[M0084 p4] This makes the shuttle phenomenon inevitable, and can only be miti­ gated through kinetic approaches as demonstrated in numerous studies on Li-S batteries with nonaqueous liquid electrolytes [4–7].
[M0085 p4] As a result, most strategies to mitigate polysulfide shuttle effect in Li- S batteries focus on slowing down or restricting the reaction between long-chain polysulfides and Li anode kinetically.
[M0086 p4] Therefore, any physical or chemical properties of electrolytes that influence the reaction could affect the polysulfide shuttle effect.
[M0087 p4] For instance, the viscosity of sulfo­ lane electrolytes is much higher than that of DME/DOL electrolytes as shown in Table 1.
[M0088 p4] This high viscosity could be a reason for the different cycling performance observed: the sulfolane electrolyte might kineti­ cally slow down mass transfer due to its high viscosity thereby miti­ gating the polysulfide shuttle effect even without LiNO3 additive.
[M0089 p4] In contrast, the low viscosity of DME/DOL electrolyte could accelerate the mass transfer, promoting the polysulfide shuttle effect in the absence of LiNO3 additive.

[M0091 p4] With the help of the LiNO3 additive, Li-S batteries cycling at elevated temperature show a minimized polysulfide shuttle effect in both electrolytes.

[M0102 p5] Conclusion Sulfolane electrolytes show promising nonaqueous electrolytes for various lithium-based rechargeable batteries.
[M0103 p5] Our preliminary study indicates that sulfolane electrolyte can significantly reduce the poly­ sulfide shuttle effect in Li-S batteries at room temperature even without LiNO3 additive.
[M0104 p5] One possible reason for this inhibition effect is the very high viscosity of sulfolane electrolyte, which could kinetically slow down the diffusion of polysulfides between cathode and lithium anode.
[M0105 p5] Additionally, the difference in the SEI composition on lithium anode surface could also contribute to this effect.
[M0106 p5] However, at elevated tem­ perature, which thermodynamically and kinetically favors the poly­ sulfide shuttle, a severe polysulfide shuttle effect was observed in the Li- S batteries with sulfolane electrolyte without LiNO3.
[M0107 p5] Only when LiNO3 is added to the sulfolane electrolyte can the polysulfide shuttle effect be effectively mitigated at higher temperature in a Li-S battery.
[M0108 p5] Therefore, while sulfolane electrolyte shows some advantages, it is ultimately a compromised solution, similar to DME/DOL electrolyte, for the use Li-S batteries.
