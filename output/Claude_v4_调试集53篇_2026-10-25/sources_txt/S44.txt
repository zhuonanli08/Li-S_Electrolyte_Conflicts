[M0008 p1] Introduction The ever-increasing global energy demand requires alternatives to the existing electrochemical energy storage (EES) technologies whilst considering sustainability.
[M0009 p1] Since their commercialization in the early 1990s, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have constituted the most common system for small portable electronics due to their high energy density and low self-discharge [1].
[M0010 p1] Nevertheless, their theoretical limit is currently being reached and it is insufficient for their implementation in some demanding areas, such as heavy-duty transportation or stationary grid storage [2,3].
[M0011 p1] Lithium-sulfur batteries (LSBs) have gained attention as potential alternatives to LIBs in the last decade, due to the outstanding specific capacity of sulfur (1675 mAh g−1) and the theoretical high gravimetric energy density of LSBs (2600 Wh kg−1) [4], which makes them a suitable choice for lightweight applications such as satellites and drones [5,6].
[M0012 p1] Besides, lithium-sulfur technology is promising in next-generation batteries due to the inherent advantages of using sulfur as an active material, such as low cost, wide availability, and environmental friendliness [7].

[M0020 p2] Overall S + 2 Li ⇌Li2S The sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) happening in the discharge process is a multi-step process where solid sulfur reacts with lithium ions and is transformed into soluble lithium polysulfides, which subsequently continue reacting with more lithium ions, and finally a liquid-to-solid reaction occurs to form lithium disulfide (Li2S2) and lithium sulfide (Li2S).
[M0021 p2] Nevertheless, commercialization of LSBs is still hindered due to the intrinsic challenges related to this technology [8–10].
[M0022 p2] The majority of them arise on the cathode side, such as the insulating nature of sulfur and lithium sulfide, the high-volume expansion undergone by the cathode while discharging [11], and the polysulfide shuttle effect [12, 13].
[M0023 p2] The latter consists of the diffusion of the soluble intermediate species (lithium polysulfides, LiPS) through the electrolyte towards the lithium metal anode (LMA), causing the loss of active material in the cathode and leading to parasitic reactions with the anode, which could eventually suffer corrosion with subsequent battery failure.
[M0024 p2] To confront these challenges, academia has commonly focused on exploring novel materials for the positive electrode, often overlooking the rest of the cell components, or selecting non-realistic compositional and operational parameters for the batteries [14,15], thus rendering the efforts hollow in terms of their practical application.
[M0025 p2] Most of the approaches related to tackling the cathode side challenges comprise the design of a carbonaceous matrix that can host sulfur particles whilst confining the liquid lithium polysulfides within this region [16].
[M0026 p2] Ideally, an optimum carbonaceous sulfur host must exhibit a high specific surface area, adequate pore size distribution, and tailored surface chemistry to enhance its compatibility with the lithium polysulfides and keep them anchored to its surface [17], besides high electronic conductivity for achieving a good electron pathway to enhance redox kinetics [18].
[M0027 p2] In practice, a compromise between these properties is set, as some of them cannot be maximized simultaneously.
[M0028 p2] There is a plethora of strategies used in literature to find a suitable cathode design based on different carbon materials [19], most of them involve the use of different carbon nanostructures [20–22] (hollow or yolk-shell spheres, carbon nanotubes, carbon nanofibers, graphene derivatives, activated carbons) either or both as the carbonaceous host [23] and/or as additives [24] for redox catalysis, as well as the addition or modification of these carbons with electrocatalysts [25–27] (metal oxides, sulfides, and nitrides [28,29], single-atom catalysts [30–32] or heteroatom dopants [33]).
[M0029 p2] These catalysts decrease the energy potential barriers of the sluggish sulfur evolution and reduction reactions [34], in particular the liquid-to-solid Li2S4 conversion into Li2S2 and Li2S, which is found to be the rate-determining step in the discharge process [35,36], and analogously, the solid-to-liquid conversion of Li2S into soluble polysulfides in the charge process [37], partially mitigating the polysulfide shuttle by accelerating the conversion of these species.
[M0030 p2] However, though effective, these advances in the positive electrode do not lead to a substantial system improvement due to the limited cyclability exhibited when realistic conditions are applied.
[M0031 p2] Thus, the development of novel electrolytes that improve cell cyclability due to their compatibility with the rest of the cell components is essential.
[M0032 p2] In this regard, one of the most recent trends consists of the use of sparingly solvating electrolytes (SSE, also known as localized high-concentration electrolytes) [38].
[M0033 p2] This family of electrolytes outstands in Li–S technology not only because they exhibit a distinctive solvation structure and notable physicochemical properties that facilitate precise regulation of lithium deposition, but also because they mitigate the LiPS shuttle effect.
[M0034 p2] SSEs are formulated based on the concept of highly concentrated electrolytes (HCEs), wherein an increased salt concentration reduces the presence of free solvent molecules, significantly enhancing stability against the LMA [39,40].
[M0035 p2] Nevertheless, the application of HCEs in batteries is impeded by challenges such as high viscosity and poor wettability over the cathode.
[M0036 p2] Addressing these issues, the incorporation of hydrofluoroethers (HFEs) as diluents proves beneficial.
[M0037 p2] HFEs are used as diluents due to their miscibility with ether-based solvents and their incapacity of dissolving polysulfides due to their low donor numbers, inhibiting the shuttle effect [41].
[M0038 p2] Besides, HFEs have an excellent compatibility with the lithium metal anode, creating a stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) which protects this electrode and increases the cyclability of the system as a consequence [42,43].
[M0039 p2] The most common HFE used in LSBs is 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE), which has been used as a diluent along with DME, DOL or sulfolane (SL) and which has proven beneficial to mitigate the polysulfide shuttle, SEI formation and fasten conversion reaction [44–48].
[M0040 p2] Considering these strategies for improving cathode and electrolyte, in our previous research, we studied the combination of our homemade ResFArGO, a 2D graphene-based activated carbon, and a sparinglysolvating electrolyte.
[M0041 p2] This system led to the development of prototype pouch cells with outstanding cycling performances [49].
[M0042 p2] Still, avoiding the need for the carbon activation process could reduce the amount of time and materials used, which is critical for scaling up.
[M0043 p2] In this work, a direct combination of a reduced graphene oxide (rGO) with a commercially available carbon (Ketjenblack, KB) was the chosen approach to avoid activated carbons.
[M0044 p2] KB is a commercially available carbon black used as sulfur host due to its adequate microstructure and electrochemical performance compared to other carbon materials [50–52], and its modifications have provided impressive results, both via microstructural engineering [53]) or by combining it with other synthetic carbons [24,54] or compounds [55].
[M0045 p2] In the KB-rGO composites, KB would provide the microporous structure to host large amounts of sulfur particles and rGO sheets would contribute to improving the mechanical properties of the laminates, high conductivity, and particle interconnection to enhance redox kinetics.
[M0046 p2] Previous results on similar systems validate this hypothesis [56–58], where good electrochemical performances were achieved.
[M0047 p2] Nevertheless, the non-practical conditions (excessive E/S ratio, low sulfur loadings) undermined the practical applicability of this approach [59,60].
[M0048 p2] Here, these composites are manufactured with a focus on achieving cathodes with high sulfur loadings that are apt for prototype cell manufacture.
[M0049 p2] To evaluate the suitability of this approach, the physicochemical compatibility and electrochemical performance of these composite cathodes were tested with both a state-of-the-art (SotA) electrolyte and a novel SSE in LSBs.
[M0050 p2] The SotA electrolyte comprised the classic ether mixture between 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), using lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the main salt and LiNO3 as an additive.
[M0051 p2] Regarding the SSE, DOL was used as a solvent for the LiTFSI salt and 1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (OCTO) was the selected diluent.
[M0052 p2] OCTO is an HFE that recently showed good compatibility with LMA in lithium-metal batteries [61], but its potential applicability in LSBs has been barely tested [62].
[M0053 p2] The aim of this work is therefore to explore the synergistic effect between the improved sulfur activation and mechanical integrity of the KB-rGO cathodes and the weak solubility of LiPS in the novel DOL/OCTO electrolyte.
[M0054 p2] The combination of both tailored cell components would confine LiPS within the cathode, maximizing sulfur utilization, reducing the loss of active material, and extending cell cyclability.
[M0055 p2] Finally, to demonstrate the validity of this approach, a prototype 20 cm2 pouch cell is manufactured and successfully tested. 2.

[M0065 p3] S@KB-rGO positive electrode manufacture For the sulfur-positive electrode manufacture, the water-based slurry composition followed our recent works [24], with 64 wt% sulfur (S8, >99 %, Sigma Aldrich) and 26 wt% of the as-prepared KB-rGO composites.
[M0066 p3] As binders, 5 wt% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt (CMC, Sigma-Aldrich) and 5 wt% styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, Jingrui Electronic Materials) were used.
[M0067 p3] First, a melt diffusion process between sulfur and KB-rGO was carried out at 155 ◦C for 12 h under an argon atmosphere to infiltrate sulfur particles within the carbon pores (S@KB-rGO).
[M0068 p3] Regarding the slurry manufacture, CMC was first dissolved in distilled H2O, and then, S@KB-rGO was added to the solution and mixed using an IKA overhead stirrer.
[M0069 p3] Afterward, SBR was added to the dispersion, and after mixing, the slurry was cast over a carbon-coated aluminum foil via the doctor blade coating technique, drying at 50 ◦C.
[M0070 p3] Then, 13 mm diameter electrodes were punched for coin cell manufacture and rectangular electrodes of 20.25 cm2 were punched out for pouch cell manufacture.
[M0071 p3] Positive electrodes with two different sulfur loadings were prepared: medium-loading electrodes (2.5–3.0 mgS cm−2) were used for short galvanostatic charge-discharge tests at high C-rates, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests, and also for the pouch cell tests.
[M0072 p3] On the other hand, high-loading electrodes (4.0–4.5 mgS cm−2) were prepared for the coin cell long-cycling tests carried out at a fixed C/10 rate. 2.3.
[M0073 p3] Electrolyte preparation The electrochemical tests were performed using two different types of electrolytes, as explained in the introduction.
[M0074 p3] As the SotA electrolyte, a 0.5 M LiTFSI (99 %, Thermo Scientific) and 0.5 M LiNO3 (99.99 %, Sigma Aldrich) solution in a 1:1 vol mixture of DME (99+ %, Thermo Scientific) and DOL (99.8 %, Thermo Scientific) was used as the reference electrolyte (subsequently simply referred to as DME/DOL).
[M0075 p3] The SSE comprised a LiTFSI solution in DOL, and OCTO (>98 %, Apollo Scientific) was added as a diluent (1:1 vol) to obtain the final 1.5 M LiTFSI concentration in the SSE, subsequently referred to as DOL/OCTO. 2.4.

[M0114 p4] Results and discussion As highlighted in the introduction, this work focuses on the performance improvement of LSBs through the optimization of both cathode and electrolyte components.
[M0115 p4] Within this context, the combination of a KB-rGO composite in the sulfur cathode with a novel sparingly solvating electrolyte (DOL/OCTO) is addressed to enhance sulfur activation and extend cycle life in a realistic proof-of-concept pouch cell prototype.
[M0116 p4] The oxygen content of the samples is responsible for an enhanced composite polarity, which is beneficial in terms of the stability of waterbased slurries and leads to favored electrode processability.
[M0117 p4] On the other hand, an excessive oxygen content may diminish the electrical conductivity of the carbon composite.
[M0118 p4] Taking that into account, a balance in the oxygen concentration in the carbon composite is required for optimal performance.
[M0119 p4] With the aim of characterizing this concentration, a chemical analysis of KB-rGO carbon composites was therefore carried out to elucidate the final oxygen content, which is directly dependent on the synthesis conditions, such as the atmosphere, temperature, and time applied for the pyrolysis process.
[M0120 p4] The oxygen content on the surface of the materials was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and in the bulk by elemental analysis, which can lead to slight differences in the measured oxygen content between techniques.
[M0121 p4] As detailed in Table 1, it increases gradually with rGO content, reaching a maximum for KB-0.3rGO, although the differences among the composites are not that abrupt due to the mild thermal reducing conditions, as proven by both EDX and EA.
[M0122 p4] These oxygen values agree with the ones expected when compared to the specifications of a commercial rGO powder (Graphenea), which contains between 14 and 19 wt% of oxygen.
[M0123 p4] Once the chemical composition of the composites has been evaluated, a microstructural analysis was carried out to further analyze the influence of the rGO content in the carbon composites.
[M0124 p4] As observed in the SEM micrographs included in Fig. 1a, as the nominal content of rGO is increased, the agglomerated sub-micrometric particles characteristic of Ketjenblack® are progressively covered and interconnected by the rGO sheets.
[M0125 p4] The homogeneous coverage and intertwining of KB particles with rGO is expected to enhance the redox kinetics in the positive electrode by creating a favored electron pathway.
[M0126 p4] Textural properties of the carbon composites were additionally assessed by nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms at −196 ◦C.
[M0127 p4] As seen in Fig. 1b, all the isotherms present a type-IV profile characteristic of mesoporous materials, similar to those already reported for Ketjenblack® [23].
[M0128 p4] The BET specific surface area decreases as the rGO content increases in the samples, reaching a minimum of 1126 m2 g−1 for KB-0.3rGO, in contrast to the 1383 m2 g−1 calculated for the pure KB.
[M0129 p4] Despite this moderate decrease in the specific surface area values, they are still high enough for the composites to act as sulfur hosts in a Li–S cathode.
[M0130 p4] Regarding the pore size distribution (PSD) calculated from the isotherms, no big differences were determined, as shown in Fig.
[M0131 p4] Minor differences are found between the amount of micropores and small mesopores around 4 nm, which are key for polysulfide trapping [63], with just a slight increase for KB-0.3rGO.
[M0132 p4] Regarding large mesopores (~ 25 nm), KB-0.3rGO also presents a lower pore volume than the rest of the carbons, which is also beneficial in terms of polysulfide retention [64].
[M0133 p4] Nevertheless, as shown in Fig.
[M0134 p4] S2, the polysulfide retention tests do not reveal an especially enhanced affinity of the KB-0.3rGO powder towards lithium polysulfides, as proven by the negligible change in the strong-orange color of the polysulfide solution.
[M0135 p4] This similar polysulfide retention is probably due to the increase in the LiPS affinity of the composites due to the oxygen functional groups balancing out with the negative effect caused by the decrease in the specific surface area in the rGO composites.
[M0136 p4] To further determine which the most suitable carbon composite was, electrical resistivity measurements of the full carbon laminates were carried out.
[M0137 p4] The electronic conductivity of KB-rGO-based sulfur-free laminates was measured using a multipoint probe analyzer, as shown in Fig.
[M0138 p4] The conductivity at RT increased with the rGO content, reaching a maximum value of 3.2 S cm−1 for the KB-0.3rGO sulfur-free electrodes, thus demonstrating that the rGO sheets enhance the percolation degree and successfully provide a suitable electron pathway along the whole electrode.
[M0139 p4] The KB-only electrode could not be measured this way due to its poor mechanical integrity, which prevented it from electrical contact with the analyzer, demonstrating the necessity for developing structurally stable electrodes.
[M0140 p4] Once the carbon composites have been characterized, the microstructure and mechanical properties of the sulfur-based KB-rGO electrodes were analyzed prior to electrochemical characterization.
[M0141 p4] As perceived in the SEM micrographs of Fig. 2a, there is a clear tendency in the medium-loading electrodes (2.5 mgS cm−2), where the number of cracks decreases drastically when the rGO content is increased, to the point of attaining electrodes with optimum structural integrity for the highest rGO-content composites.
[M0142 p4] The same trend is observed when the sulfur loading is increased up to 4 mgS cm−2 (Fig. 2b), where only the KB-0.3rGO-based electrodes were mechanically acceptable for electrochemical testing due to the reduced number of cracks and delamination.
[M0143 p4] Thus, it has been proven that small compositional differences strongly affect the carbon composite microstructure, which impacts the quality of the electrodes processed.
[M0144 p4] The effect of the rGO sheets is evident, increasing the particle interconnection, and the enhanced polarity due to the oxygen content helps to develop homogeneous aqueous-based slurries and mechanically stable high-sulfur loading electrodes.
[M0145 p4] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy conducted for Li–S coin cells at OCV could offer additional insight into the influence of the composites on the electrical resistance of the cells.
[M0146 p4] For the sake of comparison, normalized Nyquist plots registered for the graphenecontaining samples and the reference KB one are included in Fig. 3a.
[M0147 p4] An equivalent circuit analysis is developed for each spectrum, and the results are summarized in Table S1.
[M0148 p4] A slight trend is observed with the addition of rGO, which lightly reduces the charge-transfer resistance of the electrode.
[M0149 p4] This effect is not extremely significant due to the limited amount of carbon composite within the cathode composition.
[M0150 p4] The electrode resistance of graphene-containing electrodes is reduced to around 10 Ω compared to the reference KB cathode, which is in line with the previous electrical resistance measurements, further demonstrating the influence of rGO on increasing the electronic conductivity of the electrodes.

[M0173 p6] As inferred from the electrical resistivity and the EIS results, the catalytic activity of the composites would be higher with the rGO content in the positive electrode.
[M0174 p6] To assess this, Li–S cells were tested at harsh conditions of high C-rates, where the particle interconnection and the enhanced electronic conductivity are essential for improving redox kinetics and the differences between compositions would be more noticeable.
[M0175 p6] To electrochemically evaluate the rGO effect, galvanostatic charge-discharge tests were conducted in medium-sulfur loading cells (2.5 mgS cm−2) with a DME/DOL electrolyte.
[M0176 p6] C-rate tests were performed by cycling at successive rates of C/20, C/5, C/2, 1C, and C/5 again, keeping the fastest charge capped at C/2 to prevent anode instability.
[M0177 p6] S5, all the graphene-containing compositions outperform the reference KB-only cell at all rates, being less remarkable at 1C.
[M0178 p6] To better discern the better-performing composition at high C-rates, a long cycling test was carried out at 1C, with preconditioning cycles at C/20 and C/5, keeping C/5 as a charging rate.
[M0179 p6] As shown in Fig. 4a, KB-0.3rGO-based cells outperform the rest in terms both of discharge capacity and coulombic efficiency, reaching initial values of 912 mAh g−1 at 1C and 99.7 % CE, whereas KB-only cells offer just 687 mAh g−1 and 99.2 % CE.
[M0180 p6] As shown in the voltage profiles of Fig.
[M0181 p6] S6, the addition of small quantities of rGO tuned the morphology of the second discharge plateau at 1C.
[M0182 p6] However, the KB-0.3rGO cell overcame this issue while offering higher discharge capacities and lower voltage polarization than the KB-based cell.
[M0183 p6] The initial discharge capacities are higher as the rGO content increases, and this might be explained by the particle interconnection provided by conductive rGO sheets, which enhance redox kinetics and improve the electrochemical cell performance even at high C-rates, with the best performance offered by the composition with the highest rGO content.
[M0184 p6] Taking into account the improved mechanical integrity and better electrochemical performance of KB-0.3rGO cathodes, long cycling tests at C/10 were conducted on high-sulfur loading cathodes (4.5 mgS cm−2) in DME/DOL with a focus on the possible upscaling of these materials.
[M0185 p6] As a reference, a much lower loading KB-only cathode (3.1 mgS cm−2) was used, due to the severe cracking and delamination showed with a higher-sulfur loading (Fig. 2b).
[M0186 p6] As shown in Fig. 4b, outstanding discharge capacity values are obtained for KB-0.3rGO cells, reaching initial values of 1062 mAh g−1 at C/10 (around 4.8 mAh cm−2) with 99.6 % CE.
[M0187 p6] Despite the huge sulfur loading difference, these values are clearly better than those exhibited by the reference KB cell, thus demonstrating the improved sulfur activation in the rGO-containing cathode.
[M0188 p6] However, KB-0.3rGO cells showed limited cyclability of around 30 cycles due to the combination of the high sulfur loading with the elevated solubility of lithium polysulfides in the DME/DOL electrolyte.
[M0189 p6] This degradation phenomenon is well known in Li–S cells with this electrolyte using LiNO3 as an additive [66].
[M0190 p6] This salt creates a Lix- NOy-rich SEI layer on top of the lithium metal anode, and when LiNO3 is exhausted, the lithium metal surface is exposed and subsequently corroded leading to cell failure.
[M0191 p6] The larger the sulfur content in the cathode, the more stress the anode suffers and the faster the cell degrades.

[M0198 p7] Therefore, it is of adamant importance to develop a suitable electrolyte compatible with this KB-rGO electrode and the LMA while inhibiting polysulfide dissolution.
[M0199 p7] In the novel DOL/OCTO SSE, OCTO was selected as a diluent because of its high fluorination degree and low LiPS dissolution ability [63], due to its weakened solvating power that results in a higher ratio of cation-anion aggregates (AGGs), as proven by Wang et al.
[M0200 p7] In order to confirm the weakly solvating nature of the novel electrolyte, the polysulfide dissolution ability of DOL/OCTO is addressed and compared to the one of DME/DOL.
[M0201 p7] The results are shown in Fig. 5a, where the dark-orange coloration found for DME/DOL indicates a strong polysulfide dissolution.
[M0202 p7] Aliphatic ethers such as DME with high donor numbers increase the LiPS solubility due to their Lewis basicity [40], which makes them not the most suitable candidate to prevent the polysulfide shuttle.
[M0203 p7] In the case of DOL/OCTO, a yellowish hue is observed in this test, which is indicative of a decreased Li2S6 presence in the electrolyte, quite distant from that of the conventional electrolyte.
[M0204 p7] This might be due to the fact that DOL partially dissolves lithium polysulfides [67], which could be beneficial to a certain extent for accelerating their conversion reactions.
[M0205 p7] The absorbance of both solutions was measured after their dilution with DOL (1:50 v.), and the absorbance spectra shown in Fig. 5b exhibit negligible signals in the DOL/OCTO LiPS saturated solution, whereas a strong absorbance band is seen for the DME/DOL saturated solution.
[M0206 p7] This test also demonstrates that the OCTO co-solvent impedes the polysulfide dissolution, and the combined effect obtained between DOL and OCTO in this SSE is promising in terms of restraining the polysulfide shuttle as well as accelerating the redox kinetics of the system.
[M0207 p7] To assess the suitability of this novel SSE, its ionic conductivity was first measured (Fig. 5c).
[M0208 p7] As expected, a higher conductivity of 7.1 mS cm−1 at RT was obtained for the DME/DOL electrolyte compared to 1.4 mS cm−1 for DOL/OCTO.
[M0209 p7] The reduced ionic conductivity of the latter is intrinsic to the SSE family of compounds, where lithium-ion mobility is restricted, and this is reflected in a lower ionic conductivity of the electrolyte.
[M0210 p7] The ionic conductivity of polysulfide-saturated electrolytes was also measured, taking into account that the LiPS saturation point of sparingly-solvating electrolytes is much lower that of DME/DOL electrolyte as proven in literature [68].
[M0211 p7] In our case, it was found out that the DOL/OCTO electrolyte could not dissolve polysulfide quantities higher than 0.5 M Li2S6.
[M0212 p7] On the other hand, for the DME/DOL composition, the polysulfide saturation limit is higher, and a 6 M Li2S6 dissolution was prepared.
[M0213 p7] The conductivity of the resulting solutions was calculated from the impedance spectra, and the results are included in Fig. 5c.
[M0214 p7] It can be observed that the ionic conductivities decrease for the polysulfide saturated solutions.
[M0215 p7] As expected, this decrease is more pronounced in the case of the DME/DOL solutions.
[M0216 p7] At the saturation level, all of them show a similar conductivity around 1 mS cm−1.
[M0217 p7] The larger ability of DME/DOL electrolyte to dissolve lithium polysulfides represents a drawback for the system, whereas the limited polysulfide dissolution in the DOL/OCTO electrolyte is beneficial for cell stability.
[M0218 p7] This test shows that DOL/OCTO electrolyte could improve the electrochemical performance in lithium sulfur cells compared to the state-of-the-art DME/DOL electrolyte.
[M0219 p7] The electrochemical stability of both electrolytes at low voltages is studied using cyclic voltammetry (CV) in a Li||Cu configuration, scanning from OCV to −0.2 V.
[M0220 p7] Voltammograms registered for both electrolytes show a stable profile down to 0.5 V, as shown in Fig. 5d.
[M0221 p7] This measurement confirms the electrochemical stability of both DOLcontaining electrolytes tested in this work, where the minimum voltage used for galvanostatic cycling is 1.2 V.

[M0231 p8] Encouraged by the promising physicochemical properties of the novel DOL/OCTO electrolyte and its compatibility with the KB-0.3rGO electrode, the electrochemical performance of Li–S cells with a highsulfur loading (4.5 mgS cm−2) was addressed via galvanostatic cycling at C/10.
[M0232 p8] As shown in Fig. 6a, the results obtained with the combination of a KB-0.3rGO cathode and a DOL/OCTO electrolyte were remarkable in terms both of discharge capacity and cycling stability.
[M0233 p8] Remarkable initial discharge capacity values at C/10 of over 5.3 mAh cm−2 (around 1200 mAh g−1) are obtained with the DOL/OCTO electrolyte, besides a stable cycling performance with a 65 % capacity retention after 100 cycles, averaging 96.3 % CE.
[M0234 p8] Regarding the previously discussed results achieved with DME/DOL, DOL/OCTO clearly outperforms the overall behavior of the classic ether-electrolyte offering higher discharge capacities and more stable electrochemical performance due to the excellent compatibility with the electrode and the reduced shuttle effect previously demonstrated.
[M0235 p8] The charge-discharge voltage profiles shown in Fig. 6b and 6c are initially quite similar for both electrolytes, showing the characteristic two-plateau mechanism exhibited by liquid electrolytes in Li–S cells.
[M0236 p8] DOL/OCTO differs from the expected no-plateau quasi-solid profile manifested by other SSEs, which fully prevent Li2Sn dissolution.
[M0237 p8] Rather, due to the weakly solvating nature of this electrolyte demonstrated in the polysulfide dissolution tests, the presence of some polysulfide species is evidenced by the potential well occurring between plateaus, whose intensity increases upon cycling.
[M0238 p8] Hydrofluoroethers such as OCTO have a low donor ability [42,69, 70], which results in a low but non-negligible solubility of polysulfides.
[M0239 p8] As reported for other SSE, this type of electrolyte promotes the formation of medium or short-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2S4) compared to the long chain polysulfide species such as Li2S8 and Li2S6 favored in the conventional DME/DOL electrolyte, thus tuning the reaction pathway of lithium-sulfur discharge process.
[M0240 p8] The different voltage profiles for each electrolyte can be tentatively ascribed to this fact, although a deeper analysis is needed to confirm it.
[M0241 p8] Regarding the overvoltage between charge and discharge plateaus, in the first cycles it is higher in the case of the SSE electrolyte, but after 33 cycles the overvoltage of DME/DOL increases when LiNO3 is exhausted and Li0 unprotected, whereas in DOL/OCTO the overvoltage in the first cycle at C/10 and the 100th cycle is almost the same, demonstrating an effective reduction of the polysulfide shuttle.

[M0292 p10] Conclusion In this work, a novel system developed by the combination of a graphene-containing high-sulfur loading cathode (KB-0.3rGO) and a novel sparingly solvating electrolyte (DOL/OCTO) has demonstrated its real potential for lithium-sulfur technology.
[M0293 p10] It was found that the incorporation of rGO sheets increased the electrical conductivity of the composites, while strongly improving the mechanical properties of the cathodes and providing oxygen functional groups that enhanced electrode wettability.
[M0294 p10] Besides, using the conventional DME/DOL electrolyte, the success in this approach for improving the positive electrodes has been demonstrated, obtaining for KB-0.3rGO high discharge capacities of 4.8 mAh cm−2 at C/10, as well as good electrochemical performances at high C-rates.
[M0295 p10] Nevertheless, since the cyclability of these cells was limited, a novel sparingly solvating electrolyte was developed for polysulfide shuttle inhibition and improving cell durability.
[M0296 p10] When combining these cathodes with the novel DOL/OCTO electrolyte, the cells showed outstanding discharge capacities of 5.3 mAh cm−2 at C/10 and a 65 % capacity retention after 100 cycles.
[M0297 p10] This improvement is ascribed to the enhanced wettability and the partial prevention of polysulfide dissolution.
[M0298 p10] The cycle life could even be extended to 175 cycles by voltage narrowing with a slight capacity loss.
[M0299 p10] To validate these results in more realistic conditions, prototype pouch cells were assembled, achieving a total capacity of 55.2 mAh at C/10 for over 60 cycles.
[M0300 p10] More studies including these findings and components optimization are underway to overcome the real limitations of this promising energy storage technology.
[M0301 p10] Additional strategies could be implemented to further improve the performance of this system, such as carbon surface functionalization, incorporation of catalysts for a more effective anchoring and conversion of polysulfides; and also including additive salts to the electrolyte for an enhanced lithium anode protection and cycling performance.
