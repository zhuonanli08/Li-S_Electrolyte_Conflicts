[M0002 p1] In this study, we demonstrate a novel electrolyte solvent, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2-methoxyethoxy)ethane (C14), designed to suppress Li2Sx dissolution through steric and electronic modulation.
[M0003 p1] Electrochemical mea­ surements revealed that the Li-S cell using the C14-based electrolyte operated reversibly without the infinite charging plateau characteristic of the shuttle effect, which was observed in a conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)-based system.
[M0004 p1] Mechanistic investigations combining FT-IR, Raman spectroscopy, and COSMO-RS calcu­ lations indicated that the electron-withdrawing trifluoromethyl group in C14 causes a delocalization of the molecular surface charge and restricts the conformational flexibility required for Li+ chelation.
[M0005 p1] Crucially, S Kedge X-ray Absorption Near Edge Structure (XANES) analysis provided direct evidence of the solvation mech­ anism: while the DME system exhibited a pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV attributed to Li defects generated by Li2Sx ionization, this feature was notably absent in the C14 system.
[M0006 p1] These results confirm that C14 effectively prevents the ionization and subsequent leaching of Li2Sx into the electrolyte.
[M0007 p1] This work establishes that suppressing solvent-induced ionization via charge delocalization is a vital strategy for realizing stable, high-performance Li-S batteries. 1.
[M0008 p1] Introduction Recent research has focused on developing high-energy-density storage devices to meet the growing demands of lightweight applica­ tions.
[M0009 p1] Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered a promising tech­ nology due to their exceptionally high theoretical energy density [1].
[M0010 p1] A typical Li-S battery employs sulfur as the cathode and lithium metal as the anode.
[M0011 p1] During the charge-discharge process, the global electro­ chemical reactions are represented as follows [2]: S8 + 16Li+ + 16e−⇄8Li2S(1)Li⇄Li+ + en− (2) In reaction (1), sulfur undergoes a 16-electron redox process, yielding a high theoretical specific capacity of 1672 mAh g−1.
[M0012 p1] However, the practical application of sulfur is hindered by its inherent insulating nature, characterized by low electronic and ionic conductivities.
[M0013 p1] Extensive research has been conducted to enhance the conductivity of sulfur cathodes [3–7].
[M0014 p1] For instance, Ji et al. reported that a carbon-sulfur composite cathode, featuring sulfur encapsulated within a mesoporous carbon framework, exhibited a high specific capacity of 1200 mAh g−1 [3].
[M0015 p1] Despite these advances, the dissolution of intermediate lithium pol­ ysulfides (Li2Sx, 1 ≤x ≤8) into the electrolyte remains a critical chal­ lenge.
[M0016 p1] The dissolved Li2Sx species migrate between the cathode and anode, causing the "shuttle effect," which severely degrades the cycle life [6].
[M0017 p1] To address this, Takahashi et al. demonstrated that confining sulfur within microporous carbon (pore diameter < 2 nm) can suppress the shuttle effect [5].
[M0018 p1] In such systems, the micropores are smaller than the solvated Li ions, preventing solvent interaction with the Li2Sx species and thereby inhibiting their leaching into the electrolyte.
[M0019 p1] However, the limited pore volume of microporous carbon inherently restricts the sulfur loading, reducing the overall energy density.

[M0026 p2] Additives such as lithium nitrate (LiNO3) are known to mitigate the shuttle effect [8–10].
[M0027 p2] LiNO3 decomposes on the cathode surface to form various non-stoichiometric decomposition products (e. g., LixNOy), which act as oxidation catalysts to convert dissolved Li2Sx back to elemental sulfur during charging, thereby suppressing the shuttle reaction [9].
[M0028 p2] Torii et al. reported that fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)-based electrolytes impede Li2Sx dissolution through the formation of a stable protective layer derived from FEC decomposition [11].
[M0029 p2] This passivation layer remains stable during subsequent cycles, enabling reversible Li-S reactions without the need for LiNO3.
[M0030 p2] Consequently, FEC-based Li-S batteries can operate over a wide voltage window without significant polysulfide leaching.
[M0031 p2] Beyond these surface passiv­ ation strategies, recent advancements have also explored modifying the intrinsic Li+ solvation structure to suppress the shuttle effect.
[M0032 p2] For instance, high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) [12,13] and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) [14,15] have been widely investigated, as they inherently restrict polysulfide solubility by mini­ mizing the amount of free solvent molecules.
[M0033 p2] Despite these successes in suppressing polysulfide dissolution, a critical factor determining the practical energy density of Li-S batteries is the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio [16].
[M0034 p2] While a low E/S ratio is essential for achieving high energy density, Li-S batteries typically consume more electrolyte through irreversible side reactions compared to conventional lithium-ion batteries, leading to a shortened cycle life under lean-electrolyte conditions [16–18].
[M0035 p2] Furthermore, carbonate-based solvents like FEC, as well as the massive amounts of heavy lithium salts used in HCEs and LHCEs, possess high densities, which increases the total weight of the cell and lowers the gravimetric energy density.
[M0036 p2] In contrast, ether-based electrolytes have lower den­ sities, facilitating a reduced E/S ratio and higher energy density.
[M0037 p2] How­ ever, as previously noted, ether-based systems suffer from poor cycle life due to severe Li2Sx dissolution.
[M0038 p2] Therefore, it is imperative to develop an electrolyte that combines low density with the ability to suppress pol­ ysulfide dissolution to achieve both high energy density and long-term stability.
[M0039 p2] In this study, we focus on a novel fluoroether, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2- methoxyethoxy)ethane (C14), designed by introducing an electronwithdrawing trifluoromethyl (-CF3) group into the terminal of a con­ ventional dimethoxyethane (DME) skeleton.
[M0040 p2] It is noteworthy that C14 possesses a relatively low density of 1.16 g cm−3, which is lower than those of other typical shuttle-suppressing solvents such as FEC (~1.32 g cm−3) and sulfolane (~1.26 g cm−3).
[M0041 p2] To fundamentally suppress the shuttle effect without relying on high salt concentrations, weakly solvating solvents have garnered significant attention.
[M0042 p2] However, conventional designs face a severe practical tradeoff.
[M0043 p2] While recently reported weakly solvating fluoroethers (WSEs) can suppress polysulfide dissolution, they often rely on specific salts such as LiFSI [19], which is generally considered less compatible with Li-S systems due to potential side reactions with polysulfides.
[M0044 p2] Alterna­ tively, low-molecular-weight mono-ethers (e.g., dipropyl ether or methyl tert‐butyl ether) that exhibit weakly solvating properties suffer from excessively low boiling points [20].
[M0045 p2] Such high volatility makes them incompatible with standard battery manufacturing processes, particularly the vacuum electrolyte injection step.
[M0046 p2] C14 was conceptually designed to overcome this structural dilemma.
[M0047 p2] By integrating a single -CF3 group exclusively at the terminal of the highly flexible DME back­ bone, C14 strategically balances three critical requirements within a single molecule: (1) sufficient solvating power to dissolve 1.0 M LiTFSI, (2) the delocalization of molecular surface charge to suppress poly­ sulfide ionization, and (3) a practically viable boiling point of 104 ◦C, which enables stable vacuum filling procedures.
[M0048 p2] We investigate the electrochemical properties of Li-S batteries using C14-based electrolytes and evaluate their effectiveness in suppressing Li2Sx dissolution while maintaining a relatively low-density compared to alternative functional electrolytes profile. 2.

[M0084 p3] Computational details Quantum chemical calculations were performed to estimate the solvation structure of Li+ and the thermodynamic interactions between the solvents and polysulfides.
[M0085 p3] The Conductor-like Screening Model for Real Solvents (COSMO-RS) method was applied using the COSMO-RS module (MOLSIS Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [22,23].
[M0086 p3] It should be noted that direct geometry optimizations of long-chain polysulfide complexes (e.g., Li2S8 with C14) for conventional binding energy calculations were computationally unfeasible, as the initial structures severely diverged and failed to form stable coordination complexes in the simulated environments.
[M0087 p3] Therefore, the probability distribution of the surface charge density, known as the σ-profile (P(σ)), was utilized as an alternative, robust thermodynamic descriptor to evaluate the interaction potential based on the intrinsic molecular charge distributions, bypassing the convergence issues associated with direct cluster optimization.
[M0088 p3] In the COSMO-RS approach, the molecular state in solution is approximated using a conductor screening model to determine the stereostructure and surface charge density (σ).
[M0089 p3] The molecular surface is divided into segments, and the interaction energy is calculated assuming contact between these segments.
[M0090 p3] The σ-profile was derived by integrating the surface segment areas having a specific charge density σ.
[M0091 p3] The coordination structure of the solvent around Li+ was estimated based on the obtained σ-profiles and interaction energies. 2.8.

[M0095 p3] Furthermore, the relationship between the SOC of the sulfur cathode and its electronic structure was investigated using X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS).

[M0097 p3] Data were collected in both partial fluorescence yield (PFY) and total electron yield (TEY) modes.

[M0102 p3] Solvation structure in C14-based electrolytes To elucidate the solvation structures of Li+ ions in the ELC14 and ELDD electrolytes, FT-IR and Raman spectroscopy measurements were performed on LiTFSI/C14 and LiTFSI/DME solutions with varying salt concentrations.
[M0103 p3] Fig. 1(a) and 1(b) show the IR spectra of LiTFSI/DME and LiTFSI/ C14 solutions with concentrations of 0, 0.5, and 1.0 mol l−1.
[M0104 p3] In the spectrum of pure DME (Fig. 1(a)), a peak at roughly 1100 cm−1, corre­ sponding to the C–O-C asymmetric stretching vibration, was observed.
[M0105 p3] Upon adding LiTFSI (0.5 and 1.0 mol l−1), the intensity of this peak decreased, while new peaks appeared at approximately 1080 and 1140 cm−1.
[M0106 p3] These new peaks, which are absent in pure DME, are attributed to DME molecules coordinated to Li+.
[M0107 p3] This suggests that the bonding state of the C–O-C group changes upon coordination.
[M0108 p3] Furthermore, in the LiTFSI/DME spectra, a peak attributed to C–H bending vibrations appeared at roughly 870 cm−1, while the peak intensity at roughly 850 cm−1 decreased as the LiTFSI concentration increased.
[M0109 p3] It has been re­ ported that the spectral shape in this region depends on the conforma­ tion of the diether structure [25,26].
[M0110 p3] The variations in peak intensities at 870, 850, and 820 cm−1 with solution concentration suggest that DME undergoes a conformational change upon coordinating with Li+.
[M0111 p3] Fig. 1(b) presents the IR spectra of the LiTFSI/C14 solutions.
[M0112 p3] A peak at approximately 1070 cm−1 was observed to increase in intensity with increasing electrolyte concentration.
[M0113 p3] This peak is attributed to the S-N-S group contained in the LiTFSI electrolyte salt [27].
[M0114 p3] Furthermore, similar to the DME system, the peak at roughly 1100 cm−1 (C–O-C asymmetric stretching) decreased in intensity as the solution concentration increased, suggesting that the C–O-C structure in C14 coordinates to Li+.
[M0115 p3] However, unlike DME, the spectral shape in the 850–870 cm−1 region remained almost unchanged regardless of the solution concentration.
[M0116 p3] This result suggests that the conformation of C14 does not change significantly when coordinating to Li+ [28].
[M0117 p3] From these results, it is considered that while C14 has a solvation structure similar to DME, its coordination geometry with Li+ differs from that of DME.
[M0118 p3] Fig. 1(c) shows the Raman spectra of DME and LiTFSI/DME solu­ tions.
[M0119 p3] The peak intensity at roughly 870 cm−1 increased with increasing solution concentration.
[M0120 p3] Although a similar trend was confirmed in the IR spectra, the relationship between electrolyte concentration and peak intensity was found to be much clearer in the Raman spectra than in the IR spectra.
[M0121 p3] These concentration-dependent changes in peak intensity likely arise from conformational changes rather than shifts in polarity [29].

[M0142 p5] This infinite charging behavior is attrib­ uted to the shuttle effect, caused by the migration of Li2Sx formed during charging to the anode surface [30].

[M0145 p5] The mechanism of Li2Sx dissolution has been reported to involve the coordination of solvent molecules to Li+ within the Li2Sx structure, which induces ionization and leaching into the electrolyte [31,32].
[M0146 p5] Specif­ ically, DME coordinates with Li+ by adopting tgt, tgg, and tgg’ confor­ mations, thereby facilitating the dissociation of Li2Sx.
[M0147 p5] However, as suggested by the spectroscopic results in Section 3.1, C14 has a lower tendency to coordinate with Li+.
[M0148 p5] Consequently, it is inferred that C14 is sterically or electronically hindered from coordinating with Li2Sx, which prevents the dissolution of polysulfides into the electrolyte.
[M0149 p5] Finally, considering the practical energy density discussed in the Introduction, the density of the electrolyte and the achievable E/S ratio are critical parameters.
[M0150 p5] The measured densities of the prepared electrolytes are summarized in Table S1.
[M0151 p5] While the density of ELC14 is higher than that of the conventional DME-based ELDD, it remains lower than those of other typical shuttle-suppressing systems such as carbonate-based electrolytes or HCEs.
[M0152 p5] Furthermore, the fundamental suppression of polysulfide dissolution demonstrated in this study addresses the primary cause of continuous electrolyte consumption.
[M0153 p5] Theoretically, minimizing these irreversible side reactions is a prerequisite for reducing the required electrolyte volume in practical cells.
[M0154 p5] Therefore, the C14-based electrolyte provides a promising conceptual pathway toward achieving high-energy-density Li-S batteries by combining a relatively low-density profile with the inherent capability to suppress polysulfide leaching without relying on excessively high salt concentrations.
[M0155 p5] The shift in the discharge plateau potentials between the two electrolytes indicates a fundamental difference in the reaction intermediates due to the distinct solvation capabilities of the solvents.
[M0156 p5] To further investigate the elec­ trochemical reaction processes, the corresponding dQ/dV profiles are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S3).
[M0157 p5] The dQ/dV profile of the ELDD cell exhibits broad and relatively fluctuating reduction peaks.
[M0158 p5] This profile suggests that the dissolution of polysulfides leads to the coexistence of multiple intermediate states in the vicinity of the electrode, resulting in a complex overlap of electrochemical reactions.
[M0159 p5] Conversely, the ELC14 cell displays noticeably sharper peaks.
[M0160 p5] This characteristic indicates that the effective suppression of polysulfide dissolution in the weakly solvating C14 prevents such complexity, allowing a more uniform, single electrochemical reaction to proceed at the specific reaction potential.
[M0161 p5] To further evaluate the practical aspects of the weakly solvating concept, the cycle performance, Coulombic ef­ ficiency (calculated as charge capacity divided by discharge capacity), and rate capability of the ELC14 cell are summarized in Figure S3(a).
[M0162 p5] It should be noted that the conventional ELDD cell suffers from a severe continuous shuttle effect under these testing conditions, making it difficult to maintain normal reversible cycling.
[M0163 p5] In contrast, the ELC14 cell successfully completes the charging process and enables continuous cycling, demonstrating that the intrinsic weakly solvating nature of C14 effectively suppresses polysulfide dissolution.
[M0164 p5] It is noteworthy that the C14-based electrolyte possesses an ionic conductivity (0.111 S m−1) that is more than one order of magnitude lower than that of the conventional DME-based electrolyte (1.277 S m−1), alongside a higher viscosity (Table S1).
[M0165 p5] Generally, such sluggish ion transport properties would severely restrict the rate performance of the cell.
[M0166 p5] However, despite these apparent transport bottlenecks, the ELC14 cell exhibits a relatively high rate capability, maintaining sig­ nificant reversible capacity even at elevated C-rates (Figure S3).
[M0167 p5] This unexpectedly favorable rate performance can be attributed to the remarkably high tLi + of 0.599 determined via PFG-NMR.
[M0168 p5] In conventional DME-based systems, tLi + values typically range from 0.2 to 0.4 due to strong chelation [33,34].
[M0169 p5] A high transference number effectively mitigates the development of severe salt concentration gradients (con­ centration polarization) near the electrode surface during high-current operation.
[M0170 p5] Therefore, the highly efficient cation transport (high tLi +) in the weakly solvating C14 system successfully compensates for its low absolute ionic conductivity, enabling uniform redox reactions and sus­ taining electrochemical activity under increased current densities.
[M0171 p5] Over 50 cycles, a gradual capacity fade is observed, and the Coulombic effi­ ciency stabilizes at approximately 106%.
[M0172 p5] The Coulombic efficiency exceeding 100% indicates that the charge capacity is slightly larger than the discharge capacity, suggesting the presence of minor parasitic reactions-such as a slight residual shuttle effect or oxidative decompo­ sition of the electrolyte-during the charging process.
[M0173 p5] Furthermore, the strong thermodynamic confinement of polysulfides in C14 forces the redox reaction into a sluggish, undissolved solid-state conversion.
[M0174 p5] The observed capacity decay is likely a consequence of these kinetic limi­ tations of the un-dissolved intermediates combined with the continuous minor side reactions.
[M0175 p5] Further cell engineering to address these kinetic and interfacial challenges represents an important direction for future research. 3.3.
[M0176 p5] Coordination structure of solvents with Li2Sx Fig. 3(a) displays photographs of the Li2Sx/DME and Li2Sx/C14 mixtures prepared to investigate the solvation interaction between the solvents and lithium polysulfides.
[M0177 p5] The Li2Sx/DME mixture was observed to be a transparent, reddish-brown solution.
[M0178 p5] This distinct coloration is attributed to the formation of soluble polysulfide species solvated by DME, indicating high solubility.
[M0179 p5] In contrast, the Li2Sx/C14 mixture appeared as a yellow suspension, suggesting that Li2Sx is sparingly sol­ uble in the C14 solvent.
[M0180 p5] To further elucidate the structural changes of the solvents in these mixtures, FT-IR spectra were recorded, as shown in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c).
[M0181 p5] As shown in Fig. 3(b), in the IR spectra of Li2Sx/DME, the intensity of the peak at approximately 1100 cm−1 decreased with increasing Li2Sx con­ centration, while the shoulder peak at approximately 1080 cm−1 inten­ sified.
[M0182 p5] The peak at ~1100 cm−1 is assigned to the C–O-C asymmetric stretching of free DME, whereas the peak at ~1080 cm−1 is attributed to the C–O-C stretching of DME coordinated to Li+.
[M0183 p5] These spectral changes indicate that the ether oxygen atoms of DME actively coordinate to the lithium ions in the polysulfides, facilitating their dissolution.
[M0184 p5] Conversely, the IR spectra of Li2Sx/C14 (Fig. 3(c)) exhibited virtually identical profiles regardless of the Li2Sx concentration.
[M0185 p5] The absence of significant spectral shifts or intensity changes in the C–O-C stretching region implies that C14 has a significantly lower tendency to coordinate with Li2Sx compared to DME.
[M0186 p5] This lack of coordination prevents the solvation and subsequent dissolution of polysulfides, consistent with the visual observation of the suspension. 3.4.
[M0187 p5] Computational analysis of Li-solvent interaction via COSMO-RS The experimental results from the FT-IR analysis of Li2Sx solutions revealed a distinct difference in the solubility of Li2Sx depending on the solvent species.
[M0188 p5] We hypothesized that this disparity stems from the difference in the interaction strength between the solvent molecules and the Li+ ions within the Li2Sx structure.
[M0189 p5] To verify this hypothesis, the surface charge density distributions of DME and C14 were calculated using the COSMO-RS method.
[M0190 p5] Fig. 4(a) presents the calculated σ-profiles (probability distribution of surface charge density), while Fig. 4(b) and 4(c) visualize the surface charge density distributions on the molecular surfaces of DME and C14, respectively.
[M0191 p5] As shown in the σ-profiles (Fig. 4(a)), DME exhibits a broader dis­ tribution in the region of high charge density (σ ≥0.01 e Å−2) compared to C14.
[M0192 p5] In the molecular surface visualizations (Fig. 4(b) and 4(c)), the red-colored regions correspond to these areas of high surface charge density (σ ≥0.01).
[M0193 p5] Electrochimica Acta 572 (2026) 149154 5
[M0194 p6] For DME (Fig. 4(b)), it was observed that the charge density on the surfaces of the two oxygen atoms is significantly higher than that on other atoms.
[M0195 p6] Similarly, C14 (Fig. 4(c)) also exhibits high charge density on its oxygen atoms.
[M0196 p6] However, the total surface area corresponding to these high charge density regions is notably smaller in C14 compared to DME.
[M0197 p6] This reduction is attributed to the electron-withdrawing effect of the trifluoromethyl group (-CF3), which causes the delocalization of surface charge across the C14 molecule.
[M0198 p6] Based on these findings, the reduced solvation capability of C14 to­ ward Li2Sx compared to DME is ascribed to two primary factors: (1) the difficulty in adopting the cis-conformation due to the steric hindrance of the -CF3 group (as discussed in Section 3.1), and (2) the delocalization of molecular surface charge induced by the electron-withdrawing -CF3 group.
[M0199 p6] These characteristics suggest that employing C14 as a solvent for Li-S battery electrolytes is an effective strategy to suppress Li2Sx disso­ lution and mitigate the shuttle effect. 3.5.

[M0206 p6] EDX analysis detected high concentrations of sulfur and oxygen from these pre­ cipitates (Figure S4), suggesting they are decomposition products of the LiTFSI electrolyte salt.

[M0249 p9] Fig. 7(a-d) present the S K-edge XANES spectra obtained via TEY and PFY.
[M0250 p9] Guo et al. predicted the XANES spectra based on the composition and structure of Li2Sx using Density Functional Theory (DFT) calcula­ tions [36–39].
[M0251 p9] Based on these theoretical results, the spectral peaks observed in this study were assigned as follows.
[M0252 p9] For Li-SELDD (Fig. 7(a) and 7(b)), a peak assigned to Li2S4 (~2482 eV) appeared at 15% DOD and subsequently decreased, while the Li2S2 peak (~2478.2 eV) increased.
[M0253 p9] Notably, at 50% and 100% DOD, a preedge feature appeared at 2470.3 eV.
[M0254 p9] To accurately interpret this feature, it is crucial to note that our XANES measurements were per­ formed on the undissolved solid cathode.
[M0255 p9] According to theoretical and experimental XANES studies [33,40–42], this pre-edge feature origi­ nates from the creation of unoccupied orbitals on sulfur atoms when Li+ ions are dissociated or extracted.
[M0256 p9] In the ELDD system, the strong coor­ dinating ability of DME heavily solvates the Li+ ions, which is thought to effectively extract them from the solid Li2S4 on the cathode, creating Li-site vacancies (defects).
[M0257 p9] Consequently, the remaining solid poly­ sulfides with these Li-defects are considered to exhibit an electronic state analogous to that of solvated polysulfides in the liquid phase, where the disruption of local coordination generates unoccupied orbitals on the sulfur atoms, manifesting as the pre-edge peak at 2470.3 eV.
[M0258 p9] Addition­ ally, the absorption edge attributed to S8 at 2471.8 eV was confirmed even in the DOD 100% spectrum, indicating incomplete reduction.
[M0259 p9] Furthermore, the intensity of the pre-edge at 2470.3 eV was higher at 100% DOD compared to 50% DOD.
[M0260 p9] This result suggests that the number of defects in Li2S4 contained in the cathode at 100% DOD increased compared to that at 50% DOD.
[M0261 p9] During charging, Li2S4 re-formed but persisted even at the end of charge, further confirming the shuttle effect.
[M0262 p9] In contrast, for the Li-SELC14 system (Fig. 7(c) and (d)), during the discharge process from 50% to 100% DOD, the intensities of peaks assigned to Li2S4 and Li2S3 decreased, while those assigned to Li2S2 and Li2S increased.
[M0263 p9] During the subsequent charging process from 100% DOD to 50% SOC, the peak intensities for Li2S and Li2S3 decreased, whereas the peak for Li2S2 increased.
[M0264 p9] From 50% SOC to the end of charge, the peaks assigned to Li2S2, Li2S3, and Li2S4 decreased, and the intensity of the peak assigned to S8 increased.
[M0265 p9] The spectral shape at the end of charge was confirmed to be similar to that at 15% DOD.
[M0266 p9] Notably, the pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV was absent in the Li- SELC14 spectra throughout the charge-discharge process.
[M0267 p9] This absence strongly suggests that the weakly solvating C14 solvent does not strongly interact with the Li+ ions in the polysulfides.
[M0268 p9] Thus, it is considered that C14 cannot extract Li+ to create vacancies, effectively maintaining the polysulfides in an undissolved solid state without generating such unoccupied sulfur orbitals.
[M0269 p9] This finding is consistent with the COSMO-RS and IR results discussed previously, which showed that the delocalized surface charge and steric hindrance of C14 prevent effective coordination with Li2Sx.
[M0270 p9] Additionally, in both systems, the ratio of Li2Sx peaks to S8 peaks was lower in the PFY spectra compared to the TEY spectra.
[M0271 p9] This discrepancy suggests that unreacted sulfur remains in the bulk (several μm depth) of the C-S particles even when the surface has reacted.
[M0272 p9] It implies that the electrochemical reaction proceeds more readily at the particle surface than in the bulk, which explains why the practical specific capacities of Li-SELDD (1200 mAh g−1) and Li-SELC14 (1000 mAh g−1) are lower than the theoretical maximum (1672 mAh g−1).

[M0281 p10] Mechanistic investigations using COSMO-RS calculations revealed that the surface charge density of the C14 molecule is significantly more delocalized compared to that of DME.
[M0282 p10] Based on this finding, we hy­ pothesized that solvents with low, delocalized surface charge density, such as C14, inhibit the ionization of Li2Sx and its subsequent leaching into the electrolyte.
[M0283 p10] To validate this hypothesis, the evolution of the electronic structure of the sulfur cathode during the charge-discharge process was probed via XAS.
[M0284 p10] In the Li-SELDD system, a pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV, assigned to lithium defects in the polysulfide structure, was observed in the S K-edge XANES spectra at DODs of 50% and greater.
[M0285 p10] These defects are interpreted as the result of Li2Sx ionization induced by the strong coordination of the solvent.
[M0286 p10] In stark contrast, this pre-edge feature was notably absent in the Li-SELC14 spectra at all depths of discharge, indicating that the ionization of Li2Sx is effectively suppressed in the C14 environment.
[M0287 p10] In conclusion, the C14 solvent effectively mitigates the ionization and dissolution of Li2Sx due to its delocalized surface charge and steric properties, thereby enabling the stable and reversible operation of Li-S batteries.
[M0288 p10] More broadly, this work highlights that molecular design focusing on charge delocalization in fluorinated ethers serves as a highly effective and generalizable strategy for developing advanced electro­ lytes in next-generation Li-S batteries.
