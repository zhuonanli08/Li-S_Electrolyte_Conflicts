[M0002 p1] Ed. 2025, 64, e202519315 doi.org/10.1002/anie.202519315 Batteries Advanced Self-Phase-Separating Electrolytes for High-Performance Lithium-Sulfur Batteries Xu Yao+, Zhicheng Wang+,* Suwan Lu+, Haifeng Tu, Jingjing Xu, Xingdong Ma, Kun Liang, Yongbin Lin, Anqi Chen, Sicheng Xu, Ju Ren, Ke Wang, Fengrui Zhang, Jieyun Zheng, Xiaodong Wu,* and Hong Li* Abstract: Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries face a critical challenge in synergistically optimizing high-sulfur-loading redox kinetics and suppressing soluble lithium polysulfide (LiPSs) shuttle effects.
[M0003 p1] Herein, we propose a self-phaseseparating electrolyte design strategy based on heterogeneous LiPSs dissolution characteristics, using co-solvents of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME) to induce spontaneous phase separation during LiPSs dissolution through solvation disparity.
[M0004 p1] The constructed electrolyte system facilitates formation of a strong-solvation region at the cathode to maintain rapid sulfur redox kinetics while establishing a weak-solvation region at the anode to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), thereby achieving dual objectives of “kinetics promotion and shuttle suppression” via a spatially partitioned dual-zone synergistic mechanism.
[M0005 p1] This strategy enables steady cycling above 170 cycles of singlelayer Li-S pouch cells with ultra-thin Li anodes (50 μm) and high-sulfur-loading cathodes (4.3 mgs cm−2).
[M0006 p1] Moreover, under practical lean-electrolyte conditions (6.2 mgs cm−2 sulfur loading, 50 μm Li, 3 μL mgs−1 electrolyte), a 1.8 Ah multilayer pouch cell delivers 323 Wh kg−1 energy density with stable cycling above 50 cycles.
[M0007 p1] This work provides an effective solution for resolving the critical trade-off between rapid sulfur conversion kinetics and stable anode interfacial behavior in metal-sulfur batteries.
[M0008 p1] Introduction Amidst the urgent global demand for energy transition and carbon neutrality, developing novel energy storage technologies with ultrahigh energy density has emerged as a frontier challenge in energy science.[1,2] The lithiumsulfur (Li-S) battery system, leveraging its unique dual-highcapacity electrode characteristics of sulfur cathode (1675 mAh g−1) and Li anode (3860 mAh g−1), demonstrates a superior theoretical gravimetric energy density of 2,600 Wh kg−1, positioning it as a transformative system to surpass current energy storage limits.[3,4] The competitiveness of Li-S batteries stems from resource substitution of critical metals, compared to layered oxide cathodes reliant on lowabundance cobalt/nickel, sulfur offers a disruptive advantage through its high natural abundance and low cost.[5,6] However, the polysulfide (LiPSs) shuttle effect creates orders-ofmagnitude gaps between theoretical and practical cycling stability/discharge capacity, constituting the primary technical barrier to industrialization.[7,8] Distinct from the intercalation mechanism in conventional lithium-ion batteries, the unique solid (S8)-liquid (LiPSs)- solid (Li2S2/Li2S) multiphase reaction pathway of Li-S batteries dictates dual regulation characteristics of redox kinetics.[9,10] In the electrolyte with high LiPSs solubility, while rapid liquid-phase polysulfide conversion enhances sulfur cathode kinetics, it inevitably intensifies LiPSs diffusion toward the Li anode, triggering dual detrimental consequences.

[M0019 p2] Emerging phase-separation electrolyte strategies overcome conventional design constraints by creating nonequilibrium microenvironments.
[M0020 p2] Ren et al. proposed a biphasic system exploiting polarity differences to induce solvent stratification, high-polarity tetramethylene sulfone (TMS)- based electrolyte formed a Li+-enriched layer at the anode for optimized deposition kinetics, while low-polarity dibutyl ether (DBE) with polymeric ion conductors constructed polysulfide-blocking barriers at the cathode.[23] Despite synergistic interfacial stabilization, complex gradient fabrication (requiring preformed gel layers and precise phase-interface control) increased manufacturing costs.
[M0021 p2] Recently, Kang et al.’s self-phase-separating electrolyte (SPSE) in aqueous zinc batteries offers a new paradigm, water/non-aqueous spontaneous separation via solvent polarity regulation enables hydrophilic phases ensuring fast cathode kinetics and hydrophobic phases forming fluorinated interphases suppressing hydrogen evolution/dendrites.[24] This thermodynamics-driven selforganization simplifies preparation while achieving adaptive electrode-kinetics matching via phase-boundary modulation, providing valuable insights for Li-S systems.
[M0022 p2] However, there are few reports on the application of SPSE in Li-S batteries.
[M0023 p2] It is of great significance to conduct an in-depth analysis of its phase separation behavior and mechanism in real-time battery operation, and to promote its application in practical Li-S pouch batteries under harsh conditions.
[M0024 p2] Herein, we propose a dynamic self-phase-separating electrolyte, with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME) co-solvents, exploiting solvation disparity to counter LiPSs shuttle effects in conventional ether electrolytes.
[M0025 p2] Unlike homogeneous dissolution and shuttling of LiPSs in conventional ether-based electrolyte (Figure 1a), the CPME/DME system utilizes sterically hindered weakly solvating CPME and strongly solvating DME to induce spontaneous phase separation via differential LiPSs solubility (Figure 1b).
[M0026 p2] During discharging process, DME preferentially coordinates LiPSs forming cathode-localized redox-active zones ensuring efficient sulfur conversion, while CPME migrates to the Li anode interface forming in situ protective layers with dual physical-blocking and interfacial-stabilization functions.
[M0027 p2] Li-S batteries with CPME/DME electrolyte exhibit exceptional performance, single-layer pouch cells (S loading = 4.3 mgs cm−2, 50 μm Li, E/S = 6 μL mgs−1) deliver 851.7 mA h g−1 after 170 cycles.
[M0028 p2] Even under harsh conditions (S loading = 6.2 mgs cm−2, 50 μm Li, E/S = 3 μL mgs−1), 1.8 Ah multi-layer pouch cells achieve 323 Wh kg−1 initial energy density and steady cycling above 50 cycles.
[M0029 p2] Results and Discussion In conventional 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/DME (1:1, v/v) solvents, the system maintains a single liquid phase before and after saturated Li2S8 dissolution (Figure 1c,d), indicating homogeneous LiPSs solvation characteristics.
[M0030 p2] In contrast, within the CPME/DME (1:1, v/v) solvents, steric hindrance from cyclopentyl groups substantially weakens the oxygen coordination capability of CPME molecules, reducing overall solvation power.[25] Notably, although CPME introduction was expected to decrease polysulfide solubility, experimental observations reveal no significant solubility reduction post Li2S8 dissolution.
[M0031 p2] Instead, spontaneous liquid-liquid phase separation occurs, forming a transparent light-red up phase and brick-red down phase (Figure 1e,f), with identical phenomena observed for Li2S6 and Li2S4 dissolution (Figure S1).
[M0032 p2] In order to figure out the reasons for the aforementioned phenomenon, dissolution characteristics of Li2S8 in DME, DOL, CPME, and their mixtures were systematically evaluated (Figure 1g).
[M0033 p2] The solubility of S was detected by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), demonstrating that DME exhibits highest sulfur solubility (7.88 mol L−1), followed by the down phase of CPME/DME (3.42 mol L−1), DOL/DME (2.17 mol L−1), DOL (0.51 mol L−1), while the up phase of CPME/DME (0.26 mol L−1) and pure CPME (0.16 mol L−1) show significantly lower solubility.
[M0034 p2] Concurrently, the densities of solutions with saturated Li2S8 rank as DOL (1.21 g cm−3) > DOL/DME (1.20 g cm−3) > DME (1.15 g cm−3), with down phase (1.09 g cm−3) and up phase (0.89 g cm−3) of CPME/DME approaching pure DME (1.15 g cm−3) and pure CPME (0.88 g cm−3), respectively.

[M0043 p3] This solubility gradient, engineered through differential LiPSs solvation capabilities, establishes the thermodynamic foundation for spatial polysulfide confinement.
[M0044 p3] Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations comparing solvent-Li2S8 binding energies (Figure 2a) reveal the strongest interaction for Li2S8- DME (−439.7 kJ mol−1), followed by Li2S8-DOL (−347.1 kJ mol−1), with weakest binding for Li2S8-CPME (−271.2 kJ mol−1), confirming solvent solvation capability order: DME > DOL > CPME.
[M0045 p3] Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figure 2b–e) elucidates LiPSs solvation features in different mixed solvents.
[M0046 p3] In DOL/DME system (Figure 2b,d), both DOL and DME show RDF peaks at ∼1.8 Å around Li2S8 with overlapping distributions, confirming co-participation in the primary solvation shell of LiPSs.
[M0047 p3] In CPME/DME system (Figure 2c,e), DME coordinates within Li2S8’s first solvation shell (peak ∼1.8 Å) enabling effective dissolution/conversion, while the RDF curve of CPME appears beyond 4.5 Å with substantially reduced intensity.
[M0048 p3] This spatially segregated coordination indicates steric hindrance prevents CPME from competing with DME for primary coordination sites, ultimately expelling it to outer solvation regions.
[M0049 p3] Above results provide direct molecular-level evidence of heterogeneous dissolution of LiPSs in CPME/DME system.
[M0050 p3] In order to validate above results, 17O nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) chemical shift (δ) evolution upon LiPSs addition was monitored.
[M0051 p3] Larger δ changes toward higher fields indicate stronger ion-dipole interactions and nuclear shielding.[26] As shown in Figure 2f, adding Li2S8 to DOL/DME shifts DME’s 17O δ from −24.28 to −24.52 ppm (δ = 0.24 ppm), and the 17O δ of DOL shifts from 35.19 to 35.12 ppm (δ = 0.07 ppm).
[M0052 p3] In CPME/DME (Figure 2g), DME’s δ shifts from −24.00 to −24.31 ppm (δ = 0.31 ppm), while the 17O δ of CPME remains unchanged at 2.14 ppm, confirming negligible participation in LiPSs solvation.
[M0053 p3] The δ shift magnitude order (CPME < DOL < DME) reflects ion-dipole interaction strength hierarchy.
[M0054 p3] CPME’s presence forces DME to enhance ion-dipole interactions to maintain Li2S8 dissolution, which is evidenced by DME’s pronounced 17O δ shift, consistent with selective solvation and heterogeneous dissolution mechanisms.
[M0055 p3] As established in Figure 1f, CPME/DME system exhibits distinct phase separation after saturated Li2S8 dissolution.
[M0056 p3] We therefore employed 7Li NMR to monitor chemical shifts of Li2S8 in separated phases, revealing δ of 3.20 ppm in the up phase versus 1.87 ppm in the down phase (Figure 2h).
[M0057 p3] This differential shielding arises from distinct solvation environments, CPME-dominated up phase (weak solvation) versus DME-rich down phase (strong solvation), where reduced δ confirms stronger solvation Angew.

[M0075 p5] Raman spectroscopy further elucidated CPME’s modulation mechanism on electrolyte solvation structure.
[M0076 p5] In the Raman spectra (Figure S5), the main vibration peak of TFSI−shifts from 739.3 cm−1 in pure DME-based electrolyte to 741.2 cm−1 in CPME/DME (4:6, v/v) system (ν = +1.9 cm−1), indicating enhanced coordination between Li+ and TFSI−upon CPME introduction, consistent with FTIR conclusions.

[M0079 p5] Notably, CPME’s characteristic Raman peak at 895 cm−1 shows no obvious shift in different electrolytes, directly verifying its weak coordination interaction with Li+.
[M0080 p5] To further investigate optimal formulations for CPME/DME electrolytes, their electrochemical performance was tested in Li-S coin cells assembled with S/C cathodes and Li metal anodes.
[M0081 p5] Electrochemical tests showed that the cycling stability and capacity retention of Li-S batteries improve monotonically with increasing CPME proportion (Figure S6).
[M0082 p5] Therefore, the electrolyte with CPME/DME = 4:6 (v/v) was ultimately selected as the optimal ratio, balancing solvation capability (DME-dominated) and interfacial stability (CPME-regulated), providing a preferred electrolyte system for subsequent battery performance evaluation.
[M0083 p5] Concurrently, comparative evaluation was conducted between the optimized CPME/DME electrolyte and conventional ether electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME = 1:1 with 2% LiNO3) in Li-S batteries.
[M0084 p5] The CPME/DME electrolyte demonstrates superior rate capability in Li-S batteries compared to DOL/DME electrolyte (Figures 3a and S7), delivering discharge capacities of 1141.66, 901.93, 829.13, 791.46, 767.06, and 750.13 mA h g−1 at 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.4C, 0.6C, 0.8C, and 1C, respectively.
[M0085 p5] Upon returning to 0.1C, capacity also can recover to 918.82 mA h g−1.
[M0086 p5] Cycling stability tests (Figures 3b and S8) revealed that the CPME/DME system achieves 86.9% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 0.2C with 99.9% Coulombic efficiency.
[M0087 p5] In contrast, conventional DOL/DME system shows only 66.8% retention and 98.1% Coulombic efficiency under identical conditions.
[M0088 p5] Initial discharge curves post-activation (Figure S9) show that despite similar capacity utilization at the first plateau, rapid polarization at the second discharge plateau in DOL/DME system results in significantly lower capacity than CPME/DME system.
[M0089 p5] This may be attributed to Li2S nucleation kinetics, quantified by the potential gap between nucleation onset and discharge plateau.[29] This gap measured 32.8 mV in DOL/DME system versus only 16.6 mV in CPME/DME system, demonstrating that the dynamic phase-separation structure effectively enhances polysulfide conversion kinetics.
[M0090 p5] This optimization originates from regulation of LiPSs redox pathways by the cathode-side DME-enriched phase, promoting rapid Li2S nucleation deposition.
[M0091 p5] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) before and after cycling (Figure 3c) also confirmed that the CPME/DME system exhibits lower total impedance, correlating with its formation of more uniform and conductive SEI layer, along with low viscosity and high ionic conductivity after LiPSs dissolution (Figure S10).
[M0092 p5] At a high charge/discharge rate of 0.5C, Li-S coin cells with CPME/DME electrolyte still demonstrate excellent cycling stability (Figure 3d), achieving 76.7% capacity retention after 400 cycles.
[M0093 p5] Under harsh conditions in Li-S coin cells (50 μm ultrathin Li anode, 7 μL mgs−1 lean electrolyte, 4.3 mgs cm−2 high-loading sulfur cathode), the CPME/DME electrolyte still exhibits superior electrochemical performance (Figure 3e and Figure S11), delivering a high discharge capacity of 1152.4 mA h g−1 during initial 0.05C activation.
[M0094 p5] After three activation cycles, at 0.1C cycling rate, discharge capacity reaches 848.6 mA h g−1, maintaining 832.0 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles (98.0% retention).
[M0095 p5] With conventional DOL/DME electrolyte, initial discharge capacity at 0.1C was only 799.9 mA h g−1, decreasing to 532.7 mA h g−1 after 100 cycles (66.6% retention), significantly lower than CPME/DME system.
[M0096 p5] Charge/discharge curves at the 1st and 100th cycles (Figure 3f) indicate that the DOL/DME system exhibits rapidly increasing polarization in discharge profiles, signifying the consumption of active substances and the deterioration of the electrode interphase.
[M0097 p5] In contrast, Li-S batteries with CPME/DME electrolyte demonstrate slower polarization growth rate, suggesting reduced parasitic reactions.[30] In CPME/DME system, comparative cycling performance and charge-discharge curves further reveal slightly increased polarization, reduced Coulombic efficiency and capacity under high sulfur loading (4.1 mg cm−2 versus 1.1 mg cm−2) and thin Li (50 μm versus 400 μm) after equivalent cycling (Figure S12), indicating the feasibility of this electrolyte in practical Li-S batteries under harsh conditions.
[M0098 p5] To clarify the suppression of parasitic reactions between LiPSs and Li anodes by CPME/DME electrolyte, shuttle currents were first measured in Li-S batteries.
[M0099 p5] To amplify the shuttle effect, constant potentials were applied to cells with LiNO3-free electrolytes[31] (Figure S13).
[M0100 p5] It is found that the DOL/DME system exhibits a pronounced shuttle current peak at 2.4 V (versus Li+/Li) (0.26 mA cm−2), indicating LiPSs reduction at the anode.
[M0101 p5] In contrast, CPME/DME system maintains peak current densities below 0.05 mA cm−2 throughout the potential window, significantly lower than that of DOL/DME system.
[M0102 p5] This confirms that the CPME phase effectively blocks LiPSs diffusion to the anode via physical barrier effects, substantially weakening parasitic reaction kinetics.
