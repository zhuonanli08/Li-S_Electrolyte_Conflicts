[M0010 p1] Introduction Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are regarded as highly promising, next-generation energy storage devices due to the intrinsic advantages bestowed by sulfur and lithium.
[M0011 p1] Sulfur possesses a high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) while being abundant, inexpensive, and friendly to the environment [1].
[M0012 p1] Lithium also features a high theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh g−1) along with a low density (0.534 g cm−3) and a low electrochemical potential (−3.04 V versus standard hydrogen electrode).
[M0013 p1] As such, the theoretical energy density of Li–S batteries is high, with reported estimates of 2600 Wh kg−1 [2].
[M0014 p1] Despite these advantages, the practical use of Li–S batteries is hin­ dered by the notorious polysulfide shuttle effect and the unwanted growth of Li dendrites.
[M0015 p1] These issues not only degrade device performance but can compromise safety.
[M0016 p1] The shuttle effect can be mitigated by a number of strategies, including I) confinement of poly­ sulfides within the cathode host materials via physical and/or chemical adsorption [3–5], II) covalent attachment of sulfur to the cathode hosts [6–9], III) controlling the sulfur as smaller allotropes [10], and IV) adding a layer to the separator or inserting an interlayer into the device to prevent diffusion of the polysulfides to the anode [11–13].
[M0017 p1] A number of strategies have also been explored as solutions to the issues associated with the Li metal anode, such as the I) construction of artificial solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers to protect the Li metal anode [14–18] and II) employment of three-dimensional host materials to regulate the deposition of metallic Li [19–22].

[M0024 p2] A commonly used electrolyte in Li–S batteries is a solution of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, usually 1 M) with a LiNO3 additive (usually ≤0.5 M) in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (1:1 v/ v).
[M0025 p2] Unfortunately, Li–S batteries that contain this electrolyte experience premature corrosion of the Li metal anode and severely suffer from the shuttle effect in part because the ethereal solvents facilitate the disso­ lution of polysulfides [25–28].
[M0026 p2] As such, new electrolyte systems have been proposed, including those that feature high concentrations of Li+ or are based on ionic liquids [29,30].
[M0027 p2] In the former, the high salt con­ centration minimizes the fraction of free solvent molecules and thus attenuates its capability to dissolve polysulfides.
[M0028 p2] Polysulfides are poorly soluble in certain ionic liquids due to a mismatch in polarity.
[M0029 p2] However, these electrolytes often afford devices with sluggish cathode kinetics, which ultimately compromises rate capability.
[M0030 p2] Highly solvating elec­ trolytes (HSEs), which utilize high donor-number (DN) solvents, are a promising alternative as they have been shown to accelerate cathode reaction kinetics, enhance sulfur utilization, and alleviate deleterious surface passivation effects [31–33].
[M0031 p2] However, high DN solvents can facilitate the dissolution of polysulfides and thus aggravate the poly­ sulfide shuttle effect.
[M0032 p2] The standing challenge has been to enhance the Li+ solvation capability of the electrolyte while suppressing polysulfide dissolution.
[M0033 p2] Here, we report that hexamethylphosphoramide (HMPA) is a viable solution to this longstanding challenge.
[M0034 p2] HMPA has a high DN value (37.8 kcal mol−1) and forms robust Li+ solvation structures.
[M0035 p2] Moreover, it was discovered that integrating HMPA with a common, ether-based electrolyte minimizes the solubility of polysulfides and thus effectively suppresses the shuttle effect.
[M0036 p2] HMPA also enables Li+ to form contact ion pairs (CIPs) and ion aggregates (AGGs) that lead to homogeneous LiFrich SEI layers on the surfaces of Li metal anodes.
[M0037 p2] These beneficial ef­ fects manifested in high performance devices as Li//Li symmetric cells containing a HMPA-based electrolyte generated a significantly lower polarization voltage in stripping/plating cycling tests (for more than 600 h at 5 mA cm−2) when compared to analogous cells that contained a conventional electrolyte (c.f., 0.06 vs. 0.25 V, respectively).
[M0038 p2] Further­ more, Li–S cells containing a HMPA-based electrolyte also exhibited improved performance metrics when compared to cells that contained a conventional electrolyte, including a markedly improved rate capability (e.g., 606.4 vs. 181.5 mAh g−1 at 2 C) and a greatly increased cycling stability (e.g., 619.6 vs. 295.2 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles at 0.2 C).
[M0039 p2] Our work establishes a new method for overcoming the longstanding issues that have limited the practicality of Li–S batteries and, because the method is straightforward and cost-effective, it is poised for adaption to other energy storage devices. 2.
[M0040 p2] Results and Discussion 2.1.
[M0041 p2] Electrolyte design The DN number of HMPA was measured to be 37.8 kcal mol−1 via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (see Fig.
[M0042 p2] S1 for de­ tails), which is significantly higher than the values of commonly used ether-based solvents (e.g., 20.0 and 18.0 kcal mol−1 for DME and DOL [34], respectively) and amide-based solvents (e.g., 26.6, 27.3, 28.0, and 29.0 kcal mol−1 for N,N-dimethylformamide, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, N,N-dimethylacetamide, and 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone [35–37], respectively).
[M0043 p2] The solubility of the electrolyte salt, LiTFSI, in HMPA was measured to be approximately one tenth of the value in DME (ca. 0.6 M and 5.8 M, respectively).
[M0044 p2] As such, the solubility of polysulfides in a HMPA-based electrolyte should be reduced due to the common ion effect [38,39].
[M0045 p2] These unique features were envisioned to promote sulfur cathode reaction kinetics and suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect.
[M0046 p2] The effects of HMPA on other aspects of sulfur cathode chemistry has been recently reported [40].
[M0047 p2] A molecular electrostatic potential analysis indicated that the negatively charged regions of HMPA and DME are located on their respective oxygen atoms (Fig. 1a).
[M0048 p2] Compared with DME, the oxygen atom of HMPA exhibits a higher electrostatic potential, which, in turn, should manifest in a relatively high affinity toward metal cations, such as Li+.
[M0049 p2] When used as a co-solvent with DOL to dissolve LiTFSI, which is poorly soluble in HMPA, the formation of contact ion pairs (CIPs) and ion aggregates (AGGs) between Li cations and TFSI anions becomes plausible (Fig. 1b, left).
[M0050 p2] Meanwhile, the relatively high affinity of HMPA for Li+ should cause HMPA to localize in the inner sheath of a Li+ sol­ vation structure, leading to a reduced number of free HMPA molecules.
[M0051 p2] These effects should not only suppress its capability to dissolve poly­ sulfides but also facilitate the formation of a LiF-rich SEI layer on the surface of the anode along with homogeneous Li deposition.
[M0052 p2] With these considerations in mind, an electrolyte that consisted of LiTFSI, DOL, and DME was selected as a comparative control (Fig. 1b, right).
[M0053 p2] In the control, Li+ should be separated from TFSI−because LiTFSI exhibits a relatively high solubility in DME.
[M0054 p2] Such solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) typically result in the formation of SEI layers with a rela­ tively poor content of LiF as well as uneven Li deposition [41].
[M0055 p2] More­ over, the relatively low affinity of ethers (vs.
[M0056 p2] HMPA) for Li+ should minimize solvent localization and thus promote polysulfide dissolution.
[M0057 p2] Indeed, Li–S batteries that are prepared with DOL/DME electrolytes often exhibit a pronounced polysulfide shuttle effect as well as signifi­ cant Li dendrite formation. 2.2.
[M0058 p2] Li+ solvation structure elucidation The coordination structures formed between a Li cation and a molecule of HMPA, DME, or DOL is shown in Fig. 2a.
[M0059 p2] The oxygen atom of each solvent molecule features a negative electrostatic potential and therefore preferentially coordinates to Li+.
[M0060 p2] The binding energy released by complexation of Li+ with HMPA (2.86 eV) was calculated to be higher than the values calculated for DME (2.75 eV) and DOL (1.68 eV) (Fig. 2b).
[M0061 p2] To determine the optimal loading of HMPA, a series of electrolytes were prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (1 M) in mixtures of DOL and HMPA that contained a LiNO3 additive (2 wt%) and different HMPA loadings (4, 6, 8, or 10 vol% of the total volume).
[M0062 p2] The corresponding electrolytes were denoted as DOL/HMPA-4, DOL/HMPA-6, DOL/HMPA-8, and DOL/HMPA-10, respectively.
[M0063 p2] The control electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (1 M) in a 1:1 v/v mixture of DOL and DME containing LiNO3 (2 wt%) and denoted as DOL/DME.
[M0064 p2] Ion conductivity (σ) and Li+ transference number (tLi +) values were initially measured to evaluate ion transport characteristics (see Fig.
[M0065 p2] S2 for the raw data).
[M0066 p2] While the σ is affected by the constituent cations (e.g., Li+) and anions (e.g., TFSI−), the tLi + represents the contribution of only the Li+ to the σ value.
[M0067 p2] Both the σ and tLi + values were found to be correlated with the HMPA loading, with maximum values of 4.51 mS cm−1 and 0.88, respectively, achieved at a HMPA loading of 6 vol% (Fig. 2c and d).
[M0068 p2] The enhancement in Li+ transport was consistent with the reduced activation energy calculated for the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte when compared to that calculated for the DOL/DME electrolyte (14.1 vs. 19.3 kJ mol−1) (Fig.
[M0069 p2] Moreover, the optimal Li+ transport properties of the electrolyte containing HMPA are superior to those measured for electrolytes that use amide additives as the corresponding tLi + values typically range between 0.43 and 0.66 [42–44].
[M0070 p2] The superior Li+ transport properties can be attributed to the solvation structure of Li+ formed with HMPA.
[M0071 p2] Further increasing the loading of HMPA led to reduced σ and tLi + values as the excess HMPA increased the viscosity of the electrolyte.
[M0072 p2] Therefore, an HMPA loading of 6 vol% was determined to be optimal and the DOL/HMPA-6 was used as the electrolyte in the following experiments.

[M0156 p6] Impact of the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte on sulfur cathodes To study how the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte affects sulfur cathodes over a charge/discharge cycle, the surface morphology of the sulfur cathode was investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
[M0157 p6] Coin-type Li–S cells that contained either the DOL/HMPA-6 or the DOL/ DME electrolyte were used.
[M0158 p6] After electrochemical cycling, the sulfur cathode in the cell that contained the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte was found to exhibit a porous surface (Fig. 4a) which matched that of its original (pre-cycled) morphology (Fig.
[M0159 p6] The morphological feature was ascribed to the presence of HMPA, which, due to its high DN, was expected to facilitate Li2S dissolution [50–52] and maintain the initial porous morphology of the sulfur cathode by facilitating a uniform deposition of Li2S [33].
[M0160 p6] In contrast, after electrochemical cycling, the surface of the sulfur cathode exposed to the DOL/DME electrolyte was found to be smooth and compact along with cracks (Fig. 4b).

[M0162 p6] Collectively, the SEM data showed that the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte is effective at alleviating surface passivation and enhancing structural integrity of the sulfur cathode during charge/discharge cycling.

[M0221 p8] Similarly, the Li–S cells containing the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte also outperformed the cells that were prepared with other electrolytes reported in the literature (Table S2).
[M0222 p8] The significant enhancement in cycling stability can be attributed to the ability of HMPA to suppress the polysulfide shuttle effect. 3.
[M0223 p8] Conclusions We report that using a phosphoramide (i.e., HMPA) as co-solvent in electrolytes can overcome the longstanding issues associated with Li–S batteries, particularly the polysulfide shuttle effect and unwanted Li dendrite growth.
[M0224 p8] Theoretical and experimental data revealed that HMPA preferentially locates in the inner sheath of a Li+ solvation structure, which led to a reduced quantity of free HMPA molecules and effectively suppressed the polysulfide shuttle effect.
[M0225 p8] The use of HMPA also led to the formation of CIPs and AGGs that enabled the formation of LiF-rich SEI layers, which prevented the growth of Li dendrites.
[M0226 p8] As a result, the Li//Li symmetric cells that contained the DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte exhibited stable cycling for more than 600 h at 5 mA cm−2 and did so with a reduced polarization voltage that was only ca. 25% of the value recorded for a cell containing the conventional DOL/DME electrolyte.
[M0227 p8] The DOL/HMPA-6 electrolyte also afforded Li–S cells that delivered significantly enhanced specific capacity (606.4 vs. 181.5 mAh g−1 at 2 C) and cycling stability (619.6 vs. 295.2 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles at 0.2 C) when compared with analogous cells that were prepared with the conventional electrolyte.
[M0228 p8] The findings provide a means to bring Li–S batteries closer toward widespread practical use and the funda­ mental theory proposed may be adapted to bestow similar beneficial effects in other types of metal batteries. 4.

[S0010 p0] Preparation of the S@RGO cathode: Graphene oxide (GO) was synthesized using the modified Hummers’ method [1].
[S0011 p0] GO was converted into its reduced form (RGO) by chemical reduction using hydrazine hydrate, followed by thermal treatment.
[S0012 p0] First, a Teflon-lined autoclave (50 mL) was charged with a suspension of GO (20 mL, 5.62 mg mL1) and hydrazine hydrate (2.5 mL), and then placed an oven set at 180 °C for 12 h.
[S0013 p0] The product was collected by filtration, washed with deionized water, freeze-dried for 48 h, and then thermally treated using a tube furnace at 800 °C for 2 h under an atmosphere of Ar.
[S0014 p0] Sulfur was incorporated into the RGO by thermally annealing a mixture of sulfur and RGO (3:1, w/w) at 155 °C for 12 h [2].
[S0015 p0] The obtained composite was designated as S@RGO.
[S0016 p0] To prepare the S@RGO cathode, the S@RGO composite (40.24 mg), acetylene black (5.26 mg), and PVDF (5.06 mg) were ground using a mortar and pestle for 10 minutes, followed by dispersing the mixture in NMP (300 L) to form a S@RGO slurry.
[S0017 p0] The S@RGO cathode was prepared by coating the S@RGO slurry on Al foil and then dried in a vacuum oven set at 45 °C for 24 h.
[S0018 p0] Preparation of the electrolytes: The DOL/DME electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (2.8709 g, 10 mmol) and LiNO3 (0.2622 g, 2 wt%) in a mixture of DOL (5 mL) and DME (5 mL).
[S0019 p0] The DOL/HMPA electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (2.8709 g, 10 mmol) and LiNO3 (0.2622 g, 2 wt%) in a mixture of DOL and HMPA (10 mL in total).
[S0020 p0] The HMPA loading was 4 vol%, 6 vol%, 8 vol%, or 10 vol% and the resultant electrolytes were designated as DOL/HMPA-4, DOL/HMPA-6, DOL/HMPA-8, and DOL/HMPA-10, respectively.
[S0021 p0] All electrolytes were prepared in an Ar-filled glove box.
[S0022 p0] Characterization: SEM measurements were performed on a scanning electron microscope (Phenom, XL G2 Desktop) that operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.
[S0023 p0] XPS data were collected on an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, NEXSA) using monochromated Al K radiation (1486.7 eV).
[S0024 p0] The Raman spectra were obtained on a Raman microscope (Horiba Scientific, XploRA PLUS).
[S0025 p0] To prepare the samples that were characterized using SEM or XPS, the Li anode and the sulfur cathode were isolated after disassembling a tested Li//Li symmetric cell (after 5 charge/discharge cycles) and a tested Li−S cell (after 10 charge/discharge cycles), respectively.
[S0026 p0] The Li anode and sulfur cathode were washed with DOL to remove any impurities, followed by drying for 5 h in a glove box.
[S0027 p0] The electrochemical measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (Princeton, PMCCH808A) and a battery testing system (LAND, CT2100A).
[S0028 p0] The DN value of HMPA was measured using 23Na NMR spectrometry [3].
[S0029 p0] NaTFSI was first dissolved in a series of solvents, including acetonitrile (DN=14 kcal mol1), DME (DN=20 kcal mol1), pyridine (DN=33 kcal mol1), dimethyl sulfoxide (DN=30 kcal mol1), triethylamine (DN=55 kcal mol1), and HMPA.
[S0030 p0] The concentration of NaTFSI was 5 mmol L1.
[S0031 p0] A solution of NaCl in D2O (1 M) was sealed in a capillary tube (0.3 mm diameter) and used as an external reference.
[S0032 p0] The 23Na NMR shifts of NaTFSI in the different solvents were measured.
[S0033 p0] The DN value of HMPA was obtained by measuring the chemical shift of 23Na NMR signal followed by linear correlation to the DN value of the solvent (see Figure S1).
[S0034 p0] Electrochemical measurements: The ion conductivity values of the different electrolytes were measured using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).
[S0035 p0] Stainless steel//stainless steel symmetric cells were prepared using stainless steel discs as the electrodes, a polyimide (PI) ring spacer (inner diameter = 10 mm, outer diameter = 16 mm, thickness = 0.125 mm) as the separator, and either the DOL/HMPA electrolyte or the DOL/DME electrolyte (20 μL).
[S0036 p0] EIS data were collected on an electrochemical workstation by applying a sine wave with an amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency ranged from 105 to 0.1 Hz.

[S0040 p0] Li//Li symmetric cells were prepared using Li foils as the electrodes, PP membranes as separator, and either the DOL/HMPA or the DOL/DME electrolyte (40 μL).
[S0041 p0] The rate capability and cycling stability tests of the Li//Li symmetric cells were performed on a battery testing system.
[S0042 p0] The rate capability was tested at a current density that ranged from 0.5 to 5 mA cm−1 with a plating/stripping capacity of 1 mAh cm−2.
[S0043 p0] The cycling stability was measured at a current density of 1 or 5 mA cm−2 with a plating/stripping capacity of 1 mAh cm−2.
[S0044 p0] Tafel plots for the Li//Li symmetric cells were obtained from the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) data, which were collected at a scan rate that ranged from 0.1 mV s−1 from 0.2 V to −0.2 V on an electrochemical workstation.

[S0046 p0] The i0 value is obtained as the current density at the intersection of the anodic and the cathodic polarization curves.
[S0047 p0] The Li+ transference number was measured via the chronoamperometry method using Li//Li symmetric cells [3].
[S0048 p0] The chronoamperometry tests were performed on an electrochemical workstation.
[S0049 p0] The I−t curves were obtained by applying a constant voltage of 10 mV.

[S0051 p0] The R0 and Rs values were obtained from the EIS measurement.
[S0052 p0] Li−S cells were prepared using the S@RGO cathode, a PP membrane as the separator, Li foil as the anode, and either the DOL/HMPA-6 or the DOL/DME electrolyte (40 μL).
[S0053 p0] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves and EIS data of Li−S cells were obtained on an electrochemical workstation.
[S0054 p0] Charge/discharge, rate capability and cycling stability data of Li−S cells were obtained on a battery testing system.
[S0055 p0] To measure the leakage current, a Li−S cell was subjected to an initial discharge/charge cycle over a potential range of 1.7 and 2.7 V, followed by discharging to 1.7 V and then being charging at a constant potential of 2.2, 2.35, 2.4, 2.5, or 2.6 V for 20 h.
[S0056 p0] The stabilized current was considered as the leakage current.

[S0058 p0] The visible cells were assembled using the S@RGO cathode, a PP membrane as the separator, Li foil as the anode, and either the DOL/HMPA-6 or DOL/DME electrolyte (10 mL).
[S0059 p0] The assembly and measurement of the visible cells were performed in an Ar-filled glovebox.
[S0060 p0] Molecular Dynamics: Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed in this study.
[S0061 p0] The Gaussian code was employed to calculate the coordination structures and binding strengths of Li+ with different solvent molecules, electrostatic potential, and the energy levels of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO).
[S0062 p0] The binding strengths were calculated using the following the equation: E = Etotal−Eion−Esol, where Etotal, Eion, and Esol are the total energy of solvent coordinated with Li+, the energy of Li+, and the energy of solvent, respectively.
[S0063 p0] The DFT calculations were carried out at the B3LYP level with the 6-311G(d,p) basis set.
[S0064 p0] MD simulations were performed using a large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator code, and the interatom interaction was described by the all-atom optimized potentials for liquid simulations force field.
[S0065 p0] The velocity-verlet algorithm with a fixed time step of 0.5 fs was used to integrate the equations of motion.
[S0066 p0] To estimate the Li+ solvation structures in the two kinds of electrolytes, Li+ and solvent molecules, in ratios similar to those used in the experiments, were randomly placed in the simulation box.
[S0067 p0] The electrolytes were then minimized with the steepest descent method, followed by 50-ns simulation under the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT) at the temperature of 298 K and pressure of 1 atm.
[S0068 p0] The O radial relative density was calculated based on the relaxed structure.
