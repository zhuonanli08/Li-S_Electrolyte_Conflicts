[M0005 p1] DOI: 10.1002/adma.202205284 wind energy.[1] Rechargeable batteries are important energy storage and conversion devices to coordinate the intermittent electricity generated by renewable energy sources.[2] Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries with a high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) are promising next-generation battery systems.[3] Since the breakthrough of the S/carbon composite cathode proposed by Nazar and co-workers in 2009,[4] the development of S cathodes has flourished rapidly.[5] Lithium polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates as inherent redox mediators play a vital role in promoting the conversion kinetics of the S cathode.[6] Under sufficient Li supply (thickness > 500 μm) and regular-loading S cathodes (<2 mg cm−2), Li–S batteries can deliver thousands of cycles.[7] However, when it comes to practical conditions of a limited Li reservoir (thickness < 50 μm) and a low negative to positive capacity ratio (N/P < 2), the continuously parasitic reactions of LiPSs on Li-metal anodes account for the rapid decay of Li-metal anodes and induce limited cycling lifespan of practical Li–S batteries.[8] Various strategies have been proposed to inhibit the parasitic reactions of LiPSs on Li-metal anodes.[9] For example, reducing the concentration of reactants (LiPSs) is a direct approach to suppressing the parasitic reactions of LiPSs, which appeals to numerous researches.[10] Sparingly solvating electrolytes can reduce the solubility of LiPSs from ≈6.0 m to <100 × 10−3 m total S species by reducing the amount of free solvents or introducing a large amount of weak coordination solvents, including “solvent-in-salt” electrolytes,[11] highconcentration electrolytes with diluent,[12] ionic-liquid-based electrolytes,[13] and solvated ionic liquid electrolytes[14] for Li–S batteries.
[M0006 p1] Nevertheless, the insufficient supply of LiPSs in the sparingly solvating electrolytes induces sluggish sulfur redox reactions and causes Li–S batteries to lose the advantage of high energy density.[15] We proposed a fresh concept of encapsulating-polysulfide electrolytes (EPSE) to inhibit the parasitic reactions of LiPSs on Li-metal anodes and meanwhile maintain satisfactory conversion kinetics of sulfur redox reactions.[16] The inner solvent shell of LiPSs in EPSE is composed of solvents with strong solvating Long cycling lifespan is a prerequisite for practical lithium–sulfur batteries yet is restricted by side reactions between soluble polysulfides and the lithiummetal anode.
[M0007 p1] The regulation on solvation structure of polysulfides renders encapsulating polysulfides electrolytes (EPSE) as a promising solution to suppress the parasitic reactions.
[M0008 p1] The solvating power of the solvents in the outer solvent shell of lithium polysulfides is critical for the encapsulation effect of EPSE.
[M0009 p1] Herein, 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) is demonstrated as a superior outer-shell solvent to construct EPSE.
[M0010 p1] Based on the large steric hindrance of the fluorohydrocarbon chains, the electronwithdrawing perfluoro segment (CF2 further endows HFE with prominently weak solvating power.
[M0011 p1] The HFE-EPSE improves the lifespan from 54 to 135 cycles for lithium–sulfur batteries with an ultrathin lithium-metal anode (50 μm) and high-areal-loading sulfur cathode (4.4 mg cm−2).
[M0012 p1] Furthermore, a 334 Wh kg−1 lithium–sulfur pouch cell (2.4 Ah level) with HFE-EPSE stably undergoes 25 cycles.
[M0013 p1] This work demonstrates the role of weakening solvating power of outer-shell solvents to construct superior EPSE and inspires the significance of the solvation chemistry of polysulfides to achieve practical lithium–sulfur batteries.

[M0021 p2] In order to further improve the electrochemical performance of practical Li–S batteries, EPSE with superior performance is necessary and the design of the outer solvent shell of LiPSs is crucial to encapsulate LiPSs.
[M0022 p2] Herein, 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) is demonstrated as a superior outer-shell solvent to construct EPSE to inhibit the side reactions of LiPSs on working Li-metal anodes.

[M0027 p2] Results and Discussion 2.1.
[M0028 p2] Solvation Structure of HFE-EPSE According to previous reports, large steric hindrance enables molecules to possess weak solvating power which can be used as the outer-shell solvents of LiPSs to construct EPSE.[17] Hydrofluoroether not only has large steric hindrance of fluorohydrocarbon chains but also has abundant electron-withdrawing CF segments (such as CF2 and CF3).[18] The electronwithdrawing effect of CF bond is considered to endow hydrofluoroether with extremely low solvating power compared with DIPS and DIPE.
[M0029 p2] Therefore, hydrofluoroether is expected to have the potential to regulate the LiPS solvation structure, especially as a promising candidate for the outer-shell solvent of LiPSs.
[M0030 p2] Herein 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (denoted as HFE)[19] is chosen as a representative hydrofluoroether to demonstrate the potential to construct EPSE.
[M0031 p2] HFE-EPSE consists of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/1,2-dimethoxy­ ethane (DME)/HFE (2:2:1, by vol.).
[M0032 p2] DOL/DME (1:1, by vol.) is used as the typical control solvent due to its wide application in Li–S batteries since the 2000s.[20] Both the electrolytes contain 1.0 m bis-(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) to provide basic ionic conductivity.[21] The solvation structure of 1.0 m [S] Li2S8, that is the distribution of solvents around LiPSs in DOL/DME or HFE-EPSE, was decoded by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.
[M0033 p2] Li+ interacts with S82− tightly both in DOL/DME and HFE-EPSE, implying the dissociation of Li2S8 is weak (Figure 1a,d).[22] In DOL/ DME, DME emerges at 4.4 Å from the center of S82− through S82−–Li+–solvent interactions and the inner solvent shell (shell 1) is formed.
[M0034 p2] Following DME, DOL appears at 6.6 Å to form the outer solvent shell (shell 2).
[M0035 p2] The solvation structure of 1.0 m [S] Li2S8 is similar to that in our previous research.[16] The coordination number of Li+, DME, and DOL begins to increase at 2.1, 4.0, and 4.8 Å, respectively, which also reflects the difference in solvating power of the solvents around LiPSs (Figure 1b,c).
[M0036 p2] The introduction of HFE into DOL/DME dramatically changes the LiPS outer solvent shell other than the inner solvent shell.
[M0037 p2] Specifically, the inner solvent shell around LiPSs consists of DME (4.4 Å), which is similar to that in DOL/DME to ensure suitable dissolution of LiPSs.[23] HFE emerges at 6.4 Å which is recruited in the second solvent shell (Figure 1d).
[M0038 p2] The coordination number of Li+, DME, DOL, and HFE around centered S82− begins to increase at 2.1, 4.0, 4.8, and 5.5 Å, respectively, indicating HFE merely influences the structure of the outer solvent shell yet encapsulates centered S82− and the inner solvent shell (Figure 1e,f).
[M0039 p2] Experiments including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Raman spectroscopy were further used to probe the solvation structure of LiPSs.
[M0040 p2] Considering that solvents interact with LiPSs by S82−–Li+–solvent interactions,[22] 7Li-NMR was used to infer the distribution of solvents around LiPSs. 1.0 m [S] Li2S8 dissolving in DOL/DME or HFE-EPSE is chosen as the electrolyte to imitate the electrolyte formulation used in MD simulations.
[M0041 p2] The chemical shift of 7Li is 3.20 ppm both for DOL/DME and HFE-EPSE, indicating HFE barely interacts with Li+ and does not change the inner solvent shell of LiPSs (Figure 1g).
[M0042 p2] Compared with previous research that the introduction of DIPS and DIPE has a little influence of deshielding effect on the chemical shift of 7Li, the decoded solvation structure of HFE-EPSE indicates that the solvating power of HFE is much weaker than DIPS and DIPE, which is beneficial from the large steric hindrance of fluorohydrocarbon chains and electron-withdrawing effect of CF2 segments.
[M0043 p2] There is a similar conclusion for Raman spectroscopy.
[M0044 p2] The signal of polysulfides (392, 448, and 510 cm−1), DME (845 cm−1), and DOL (945 cm−1) nearly overlaps for DOL/DME and HFE-EPSE,[24] indicating the inner solvent shell around LiPSs maintains with the introduction of HFE (Figure 1h and Figure S1, Supporting Information).
[M0045 p2] A similar conclusion is obtained for 1.0 m [S] Li2S6 (Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information).
[M0046 p2] Therefore, it is concluded that HFE successfully constructs EPSE (Figure 1i).
[M0047 p2] In previous researches, the concept of localized high-concentration electrolytes was proposed for Li-ion and Li-metal batteries, where the introduction of hydrofluoroether aims to reduce the apparent concentration of Li salts but enable the electrolyte to exhibit the solvation structure of high-concentration electrolytes.[25] Hydrofluoroether is not involved in the solvation of Li+ due to its poor solvating power.

[M0077 p5] Li–S Batteries with HFE-EPSE under Practical Conditions Specifically, it should be mentioned that the test conditions of Li–S batteries highly influence the evaluation objectivity of the strategies.
[M0078 p5] Although Li–S batteries can deliver a long lifespan under conventional conditions of regular-loading S cathodes of less than 2 mg cm−2 and enough Li supply with a thickness of more than 500 μm, the mild conditions conceal the problems of Li-metal anodes and exaggerate the effectiveness of anode protection strategies because only low proportion of Li-metal anodes (<4%) is used in each cycle.
[M0079 p5] Therefore, the practical effect of HFE-EPSE was verified in Li–S batteries under demanding conditions of high-loading S cathodes (4.4 mg cm−2), ultrathin Li anodes (50 μm), and an electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 6.0 μL mgS−1 with LiNO3 additives (Figure 3a and Figure S4, Supporting Information).
[M0080 p5] The Li–S batteries were cycled at 0.1 C after initial two cycles of activation at 0.05 C (Figure S5, Supporting Information).
[M0081 p5] The Li–S battery with DOL/DME electrolyte exhibits an initial specific capacity of 957 mAh g−1 (Figure 3a).
[M0082 p5] However, the specific capacity sharply decreases after 40 cycles.
[M0083 p5] The Li–S battery with DOL/ DME only delivers 54 cycles based on 70% capacity retention.
[M0084 p5] In contrast, the battery with HFE-EPSE undergoes stable 135 cycles based on 70% capacity retention.
[M0085 p5] The cycling lifespan of the Li–S batteries improves 1.5 times with the employment of HFE-EPSE.
[M0086 p5] The cycling stability of batteries with HFE-EPSE is superior compared with other advanced electrolyte strategies (Table S1, Supporting Information).
[M0087 p5] The discharge median voltage of the battery with HFE-EPSE maintains stable above 2.06 V even after 135 cycles, while that with DOL/DME dramatically decreases after 20 cycles and reaches only 2.00 V after 40 cycles (Figure 3b and Figure S6, Supporting Information).
[M0088 p5] The more stable and higher discharge median voltage enabled by HFE-EPSE indicates the internal resistance of batteries is reduced and the dead Li accumulation is alleviated.
[M0089 p5] Specifically, the galvanostatic charge and discharge profiles nearly overlap of the Li–S batteries with DOL/DME and HFE-EPSE at the second cycle except for a slight difference in discharge capacity (Figure 3c).
[M0090 p5] Moreover, the discharge profiles of the Li–S battery with HFE-EPSE maintain stable at the 40th cycle, while that with DOL/DME delivers a dramatical slope of the second discharge plateau.

[M0139 p8] HFE-EPSE is expected to have good compatibility with cathode redox promoters, such as electrochemical catalysis,[30] to improve the conversion kinetics.

[M0141 p8] Conclusions HFE is used as a superior outer-shell solvent to construct EPSE to inhibit the intrinsic side reactions between LiPSs and Li-metal anodes.
[M0142 p8] There are two long fluorohydrocarbon chains with large steric hindrance and abundant electronwithdrawing CF2 segments in the HFE molecule, which enables HFE to have much weak solvating power.
[M0143 p8] The weak interaction of HFE with LiPSs makes HFE only distribute in the outer solvent shell of LiPSs.
[M0144 p8] The HFE with high reduction stability ensures the stable preservation of the encapsulation structure during repeated cycling.
[M0145 p8] HFE-EPSE dramatically improves the cycling life span of Li–S batteries from 54 to 135 cycles based on 70% capacity retention with ultrathin Li-metal anodes (50 μm) and high-loading S cathodes (4.4 mg cm−2).
[M0146 p8] Moreover, a 2.4 Ah-level Li–S pouch cell with HFE-EPSE delivers stable 25 cycles with an initial energy density of 334 Wh kg–1.
[M0147 p8] This work demonstrates that HFE is a superior outer-shell solvent to construct EPSE and inspires further effective regulation of solvation structures of LiPSs to achieve long-cycling Li–S batteries.
