[M0002 p1] Mater. 2021, 33, 3457−3466 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: The materials chemistry underlying lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries is uniquely dependent on the behavior of soluble lithium polysulfide intermediates, which form during operation and mediate the charge transfer process in solution.
[M0003 p1] The manner by which lithium polysulfides are solvated by the surrounding solvent and salt compounds is a critical factor with regard to electrochemical utilization and reversibility of the sulfur active material.
[M0004 p1] Particularly under low-temperature and lean electrolyte conditions, lithium polysulfides tend to coordinate with other polysulfide units in solution, forming large, aggregated clusters that stymie the electrochemical conversion process.
[M0005 p1] However, the tendency to cluster is known to be influenced by the presence of strongly binding anionic species in solution, which presents electrostatic competing interactions with Li+.
[M0006 p1] The heightened electrostatic competition in turn can dissuade the formation of clustered Li+−Sx 2−bond networks.
[M0007 p1] Here, we extend that understanding to the influence of distinct cationic species in solution, which can present analogous competing interactions with Sx 2−dianions to stymie polysulfide cluster formation.
[M0008 p1] We find that introducing NH4 + cations into solution through an ammonium trifluoroacetate additive positively tailors the polysulfide coordination shell.
[M0009 p1] This improves the electrochemical conversion kinetics under challenging lean electrolyte and subzero low-temperature conditions and provides a more holistic understanding of polysulfide coordination behavior. ■INTRODUCTION The widespread adoption of next-generation energy-dense batteries is poised to enable tremendous advancements in electrified mobility and transportation, particularly for weightdependent applications within the aviation and space sectors.1−3 This is especially true for lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, which possess an order of magnitude greater theoretical gravimetric capacity (1672 mA h g−1) and specific energy (∼2600 W h kg−1) than the incumbent lithium-ion batteries.4 While this theoretical capability is highly promising, in practice, it is quite difficult to achieve even 10% of this theoretical specific energy in state-of-the-art Li−S cells.5 The single largest factor leading to this shortcoming ties to the inability of the redox couple to deliver high active material utilization under lean electrolyte conditions.6 This property is a manifestation of the inherent solution-mediated nature intrinsic to the lithiation of a sulfur active material.
[M0010 p1] Both elemental sulfur and the Li2S discharge product are severely ionically and electronically insulating, presenting unfavorable barriers to the surface-based charge transfer pathways traditionally required for reduction and oxidation of battery materials.
[M0011 p1] The Li−S chemistry overcomes this through the generation of soluble lithium polysulfide (Li2Sx, 2 < x ≤8) intermediates, which can enable facile charge transfer in solution via solution-mediated reduction pathways.7−9 Thus, there is a minimum degree of active material dissolution necessitated by the Li−S chemistry for the cell to function and make use of solution-mediated reaction avenues.
[M0012 p1] For this reason, there is often a divergence in the behavior of Li−S cells as a function of the volume of electrolyte contained within the cell.
[M0013 p1] Most notably, high capacities become increasingly difficult to achieve in cells constructed under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0014 p1] This is quantified by the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/ S) ratio (μLelectrolyte mg−1 sulfur), a commonly reported metric accompanying electrochemical data within the Li−S literature.6 The solution-mediated paradigm intrinsic to the Li−S chemistry imparts a variety of unique and chemically complex phenomena during discharge and charge.
[M0015 p1] This is readily demonstrated by the recent surge in research attention directed at the nature of solvation and coordination of individual lithium polysulfide units.9−12 Under kinetically constrained conditions, such as lean electrolyte volumes or even subzero low-temperature conditions, lithium polysulfides tend to cluster and coordinate with each other disproportionately more than with the surrounding solvent molecules, forming clustered polysulfide aggregates.10,13 This behavior can proceed to inhibit the favorable solution-mediated kinetics underlying the Li−S chemistry, curtailing lithiation and diffusion of lithium polysulfides.

[M0019 p2] For example, in our past work, we revealed that detrimental clustering behavior can be altered and influenced through the introduction of competing electrostatic interactions in solution.13 It was shown that the introduction of lithium salts with highly binding anionic groups can disrupt polysulfide cluster formation.
[M0020 p2] The electrostatic attraction between the highly negative anion and the polysulfide-adjoined lithium cation dissuades the coordination of adjacent polysulfide units, leading to a much higher discharge capacity and more favorable kinetic behavior under low-temperature conditions.
[M0021 p2] This result is quite consistent with other reports exemplifying the advantages of strongly binding salt anions for improvements to room-temperature Li−S electrochemical behavior.15−18 Within Li−S batteries, the underlying materials chemistry is intimately tied to the manner by which distinct ions interact with active materials in solution.
[M0022 p2] This considerably differs from traditionally employed systems with insertion-ion electrodes, as the active material in the Li−S battery is in many respects inextricably linked to the electrolyte solvent and salt.
[M0023 p2] In this work, we seek to advance the understanding of how the coordination of dissolved polysulfide active materials can be further influenced, beyond just the implementation of highly binding anionic groups.
[M0024 p2] We specifically look at the addition of a strongly binding cationic group, NH4 +, into the Li−S electrolyte to further influence and alter the coordination behavior of lithium polysulfides. ■EXPERIMENTAL METHODS Polysulfide Solution Preparation.

[M0089 p4] Lithium polysulfide species in particular undergo significant short-range electrostatic coordination not just with other ions but also with neighboring polysulfide units, as shown through the illustration in Figure 1a.
[M0090 p4] This clustering behavior is found to be a relatively lower energy state and, as discussed, can occur disproportionately more frequently under lean electrolyte or low-temperature conditions.12−14 The selective coordination of lithium polysulfides to adjacent polysulfides in solution has been shown to be influenced and altered through the addition of strongly binding (high donor number) salt compounds like lithium trifluoroacetate (LiCO2CF3, abbreviated hereafter as LiTFA) to the electrolyte.13 The highly Lewis basic trifluoroacetate (TFA−) anion presents an effective electrostatic competing force to disrupt the strongly bound Li+−Sx 2−networks in favor of Li+− TFA−interactions, as illustrated in Figure 1b.
[M0091 p4] The mitigation of polysulfide clustering in turn has been shown to benefit kinetic behavior and capacity attainment under challenging lean electrolyte or low-temperature conditions.13,14 The anionic modification to the lithium polysulfide coordination environment presents an excellent platform to develop upon for further scientific insight and engineering improvement.
[M0092 p4] However, rather than utilizing strongly binding anions like TFA−to help coordinate with polysulfide-adjoined Li+ cations, it is intriguing to consider whether the opposite approach of utilizing a strongly binding cation could potentially demonstrate similar behavior, as shown in Figure 1c.
[M0093 p4] In this manner, rather than solely utilizing compounds possessing a high electron donor capability,25,26 this approach assesses the efficacy of electron acceptors toward improving polysulfide coordination behavior.
[M0094 p4] If true, then employing a strongly binding cation and anion simultaneously in solution could feasibly curtail the formation of clustered polysulfide aggregates to a much greater extent than shown before.
[M0095 p4] This would serve to boost solution-mediated kinetics in Li−S batteries to new heights under the challenging conditions most applicable for practical use of Li−S batteries.14 The ammonium cation, NH4 +, was selected as a starting point to assess this strategy based on its favorable use for lean electrolyte Li−S cells in the literature.27 Building offan in situderivatizing additive we introduced in a recent work,28 ammonium trifluoroacetate (NH4TFA) was identified as an effective electrolyte additive for evaluating the joint effects of NH4 + and TFA−on the solution-coordination behavior of lithium polysulfides.

[M0116 p5] Electrochemical Variance with Electrolyte Amount.
[M0117 p5] Equipped with a firm understanding of the role NH4TFA chemically plays in solution, the resultant polysulfide coordination environment was assessed through electrochemical analysis.
[M0118 p5] Li−S coin and pouch cells were used as platforms to galvanostatically assess the impacts of the additive on electrochemical behavior as a function of electrolyte amount.
[M0119 p5] Specifically, cells were constructed with an electrolyte consisting of 0.2 M NH4TFA, 0.2 M LiNO3, and 0.8 M LiTFSI, with the intent of keeping the total solute concentration equal to a control electrolyte consisting of 0.2 M LiNO3 and 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (1:1, by vol).
[M0120 p5] Additionally, the electrolyte volumes utilized in coin cells and pouch cells were deliberately varied to understand the behavior of the additive as a function of E/S ratio.
[M0121 p5] Coin cells were constructed with high E/S ratios of 12 μL mg−1, while pouch cells were constructed with low E/S ratios of 4.5 μL mg−1, a lean amount where standard Li−S cells can tend to perform poorly.6,29 As discussed earlier, the E/S ratio can, to a first order, approximate the expected degree of active material solvation and utilization.
[M0122 p5] Thus, through these cycling experiments, the specific contributions NH4TFA yield can be holistically understood as a function of the polysulfide concentration and coordination environment within the electrolyte.
[M0123 p5] As seen in Figure 3a, the galvanostatic cycling behavior of coin cells (E/S = 12 μL mg−1) with and without NH4TFA is quite similar, generally maintaining between 800 and 900 mA h g−1 over the course of 100 cycles.
[M0124 p5] Despite the similar capacity retentions, it is seen in Figure 3b that the voltage profiles of each cell exhibit slight differences.
[M0125 p5] Most notably, the cell containing NH4TFA does exhibit slightly superior kinetic behavior, with a lower overpotential on the order of 40 mV seen during discharge.
[M0126 p5] This is particularly noticeable in the lower voltage plateau conversion from Li2S4 to Li2S.
[M0127 p5] However, a much more substantial difference is seen with both electrolytes when tested under lean electrolyte conditions in large-format pouch cells (E/S = 4.5 μL mg−1), as shown in Figure 3c.
[M0128 p5] Under such conditions, the cell containing NH4TFA demonstrates substantial improvements over the cell with the control electrolyte, which can be seen over the course of a single cycle in Figure 3d.
[M0129 p5] From a capacity standpoint, the NH4TFA additive enables a capacity of 930 mA h g−1 compared to 250 mA h g−1 for the control cell, a sizeable 370% improvement.
[M0130 p5] This is also clearly reflected in the drastic improvements in the upper and lower voltage plateaus to both capacity and polarization, signifying the more complete utilization of the sulfur active material in the presence of NH4TFA.
[M0131 p5] While this reflects the hypothesized boosts to solution-mediated kinetics envisioned from the selection of the additive, it is especially compelling that the major improvements seen are primarily under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0132 p5] The divergence in performance at low E/S ratios in cells containing NH4TFA points to the effect the additive may be having on enhancing the polysulfide coordination environment.

[M0149 p6] Clustering Tendency with Heightened Electrostatic Competition.
[M0150 p6] The divergence in ionic association energies between NH4 + and Li+ directly extends to the clustering and aggregation of lithium polysulfides.
[M0151 p6] The basis for clustering behavior stems from a minimization of energy via adjacent polysulfide units coordinating through short-range electrostatic interactions.30,31 This tends to occur most frequently in shortchain Li2S4, which is the dominant species present in solution at the onset of the second plateau conversion to Li2S.12,13 This is modeled quantitatively through eq 3. Δ = × [ − + ] + − G n G n G G 1 (2 ) (Li S ) Li S n 2 4 4 2 (3) As shown from first-principles calculations in Figure 5a, free energy is minimized to a greater extent with increasingly sized clusters of Li2S4 units coordinating with one another.
[M0152 p6] For instance, a four-member Li2S4 cluster exhibits a free energy that is 37 kJ mol−1 S4 2−less than a single Li2S4 unit.
[M0153 p6] This significant decrease in energy shifts the equilibrium to highly clustered states in the kinetically limiting environments that, for example, low-temperature conditions present.14 However, as shown through Figure 5b, the greater propensity for diammonium polysulfides to exhibit greater ionic association ability also extends to their ability to cluster.
[M0154 p6] Varying sized clusters of ((NH4)2S4)n of sizes n = 1, 2, and 4 display the same trends seen in Li2S4, with energy being minimized to the greatest extent in large four-member clusters.
[M0155 p6] Notably, the energy minimization calculated far exceeds that seen in lithium polysulfide clusters, with ((NH4)2S4)4 exhibiting a free energy that is 51 kJ mol−1 S4 2−less than a single (NH4)2S4 unit.
[M0156 p6] Thus, a liquid solution containing solely diammonium polysulfide species would be expected to exhibit clustering behavior at an even greater frequency than that with lithium polysulfides.
[M0157 p6] However, as noted under lean electrolyte conditions, the liquid electrolyte environment with the NH4TFA additive likely contains an amalgam of lithium, diammonium, and mixed lithium-ammonium polysulfides.
[M0158 p6] Thus, the drive to minimize energy in the form of a lithium polysulfide cluster may run counter to the competing drive to minimize energy in the form of a diammonium polysulfide cluster.
[M0159 p6] As a result, the competitive and opposed drives to ionically associate may incur a stalemate-like situation, altogether inhibiting the formation of clustered aggregates and preserving a favorable coordination environment for dissolved active materials.
[M0160 p6] Another possibility is that the formation of ammonium polysulfide clusters proceeds in the absence of Li+ charge carriers, minimizing any detrimental impacts on the vital charge transfer kinetics that Li−S discharge depends upon.
[M0161 p6] Indeed, the electrochemical behavior seen under the lean electrolyte conditions in Figure 3d is highly consistent with this proposed mechanism.
[M0162 p6] To supplement the theoretical basis for this hypothesis, 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was employed to further probe the mechanism.
[M0163 p6] NMR can provide detailed information regarding the bond environment surrounding 7Li nuclei within each sample and is an excellent tool to survey the dynamic coordination environment within Li−S electrolytes.
[M0164 p6] Two samples were prepared containing 0.2 M Li2S4 with and without 0.2 M NH4TFA dissolved in deuterated tetrahydrofuran (d-THF) and DME solvents.
[M0165 p6] The spectra were obtained at both 25 and −50 °C to observe the change in the bond environment occurring with the induced formation of polysulfide clusters.
[M0166 p6] As seen in Figure 6a, the peak affiliated with Li2S4 at 25 °C noticeably shifts upfield from 0.06 to −0.33 ppm at −50 °C, highly consistent with the increased diamagnetic shielding expected from the heighted surrounding Figure 5.

[M0172 p7] However, when lithium polysulfides are simultaneously accompanied by NH4TFA in solution, there are a variety of key differences in the 7Li NMR response.
[M0173 p7] The presence of NH4TFA induces the formation of diammonium polysulfides and LiTFA salt, which serves to shift the room-temperature peak in Figure 6b downfield to 0.53 ppm.
[M0174 p7] Even more so, there is a distinct change in 7Li NMR response as the temperature is lowered to −50 °C compared to what is seen with just Li2S4.
[M0175 p7] The peak still shifts upfield but, interestingly, displays a multimodal range of peaks at 0.07, 0.03, and 0.02 ppm overlapping with the LiBr reference.
[M0176 p7] The sizeable changes in coordination environments when NH4TFA is present suggest that the nature of coordination is fundamentally altered.
[M0177 p7] At 25 °C, for example, the large shift downfield implies that the average Li+ nuclei in solution is significantly diamagnetically deshielded; there is a “withdrawing” of the electron density surrounding Li+ cations, increasing the effective field experienced by the average 7Li nucleus.
[M0178 p7] This is consistent with the heightened electrostatic competition postulated through the addition of NH4TFA, where Li+ becomes less coordinated with surrounding Sx 2− ligands.
[M0179 p7] However, at −50 °C, the sizeable shift downfield suggests that Li+ is still experiencing a strong shift in the coordination environment with temperature, and given that this shift is not likely to stem from a change in coordination with the TFA−anion or surrounding solvent,13 the only option is that Li+ is still partaking in the formation of aggregated polysulfide clusters.
[M0180 p7] The distinct peaks, however, suggest that rather than pure lithium polysulfide aggregates, there is an array of distinct and compositionally variant mixed lithiumammonium polysulfide aggregates that form at low temperatures.
[M0181 p7] This can be better understood using the lens of Pearson’s hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) theory.9,26,32 In an ideal solvated lithium polysulfide unit, the hard Li+ Lewis acid prefers to coordinate with the relatively hard Sx 2−dianion more strongly than with the softer DOL/DME solvent, which accounts for the widespread polysulfide clustering that occurs at low temperatures or under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0182 p7] With the increased coordination to a greater number of Sx 2−ligands, each Li+ cation is shielded in a softer solvated complex by the increased surrounding electron density, leading to the diamagnetic shielding effect.
[M0183 p7] The substitution of Li+ with a slightly softer NH4 + Lewis acid would promote more effective dipole−cation coordination with the relatively soft DOL/DME solvent compared to the harder Sx 2−dianion.
[M0184 p7] This would serve to counteract the detrimental Li+−Sx 2−coordination interactions making up the backbone of large polysulfide clusters, as reflected by the downfield translation in the 25 °C 7Li NMR chemical shift.
[M0185 p7] Even at −50 °C, the clustered aggregates that do form in the presence of NH4TFA still exhibit chemical shifts downfield of that seen in pure lithium polysulfide clusters, suggesting that the average Li+ coordination state is still more favorable.
[M0186 p7] This validates the tendency of NH4 + and TFA−to preferentially coordinate with, respectively, polysulfide dianions and Li+ in solution.
[M0187 p7] This heightened electrostatic competition preserves a more favorable and dissociated coordination state of Li+, providing a basis by which to boost solution-mediated electrochemical kinetics.

[M0197 p8] This time, the optimized low-temperature electrolyte contained 0.5 M LiTFSI, 0.3 M NH4TFA, and 0.2 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME (85:15, by vol).
[M0198 p8] This was compared to a control electrolyte containing 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME (85:15, by vol).
[M0199 p8] In addition, a halfway optimized electrolyte containing 0.5 M LiTFSI, 0.3 M LiTFA, and 0.2 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME (85:15, by vol) was also tested to deconvolute and separately understand the positive contributions stemming from the high donor LiTFA salt and NH4 +-modified polysulfides.
[M0200 p8] The total solute concentrations and solvent ratios in all formulations were chosen to match empirically optimized low-temperature Li−S electrolytes from the literature.13,33 As seen in the voltage profile in Figure 7a, the NH4TFAoptimized electrolyte formulation slightly outperforms the other formulations at 25 °C, achieving capacities that are 13 and 9% higher than, respectively, the control and LiTFAcontaining electrolytes.
[M0201 p8] This trend becomes slightly stronger at 0 °C (shown in Figure 7b), with the NH4TFA-optimized electrolyte achieving capacities that are 26 and 15% higher than, respectively, the control and LiTFA-containing electrolytes.
[M0202 p8] This data support the hypothesis that NH4 + tends to associate more favorably with solvated polysulfide units than contiguous Li+ cations, disrupting the strong clustered Li+− Sx 2−frameworks that impede discharge behavior under kinetically limiting conditions.
[M0203 p8] At −20 °C, however, the electrochemical behavior enabled by each electrolyte formulation exhibits highly stark differences, beyond just the continued trends in capacity attainment.
[M0204 p8] As shown in Figure 7c, the control electrolyte fails to exhibit a lower voltage plateau at all, indicating the highly curtailed conversion to Li2S as expected.
[M0205 p8] The LiTFA-containing electrolyte, meanwhile, displays a sizeable lower voltage plateau, albeit taking place at a lower-than-expected potential of 1.7 V vs Li/Li+.
[M0206 p8] However, while these voltage profiles still somewhat resemble the ideal Li−S voltage profiles seen at 25 °C, the same cannot be said for the NH4TFA-containing electrolyte at −20 °C.
[M0207 p8] Rather than the typical two-plateau behavior predominantly seen in Li−S electrochemistry, the discharge taking place in the NH4TFA-containing electrolyte exhibits an unexpected and fascinating four-plateau voltage profile at −20 °C.
[M0208 p8] Moreover, the first two plateaus and last two plateaus exhibit a combined capacity attainment on the same order as, respectively, that seen by the first and second voltage plateaus in the LiTFAcontaining electrolyte.
[M0209 p8] While the first plateau at ∼2.37 V matches that seen in the control and LiTFA-containing electrolytes, this potential quickly drops to ∼2.16 V.
[M0210 p8] Meanwhile, a third plateau occurs at a relatively high potential of 1.9 V before dropping to a sloping fourth plateau that polarizes to the discharge cutoff.
[M0211 p8] While this additive may be promising as a solution to ameliorate poor low-temperature performance, its unprecedented electrochemical behavior requires further study to understand the coordination and speciation of polysulfides during each plateau.
[M0212 p8] For instance, this behavior may reflect the staged discharge of distinct mixed ammonium-lithium polysulfide species like what is seen in the NMR spectra of Figure 6b.
[M0213 p8] Another possibility ties to how the active material complex is solvated and shielded during the discharge process.
[M0214 p8] The quasi-equilibrium potential at which conversion takes place is inversely tied to how effectively solvated the active material is.9,26 This can be seen in the case of high donor number solvents, which more effectively bind and shield Li+.
[M0215 p8] This shielding effect increases the relative softness of the cationic complex, which then proceeds to bind to the hard S2− discharge product less effectively.9,26 This in turn lowers the discharge potential of the second plateau, which can be interpreted as a change in the chemical activity of the constituent species from the Nernst equation.
[M0216 p8] Similarly, clustering of polysulfides increases the shielding of Li+, lowering the quasi-equilibrium potential of reduction.
[M0217 p8] The substitution of NH4 + serves to deter cluster formation, boosting the Li2S conversion potential as seen in the third plateau at 1.9 V.
[M0218 p8] However, NH4 + is a softer Lewis acid than Li+ and is more effectively solvated by surrounding DOL/DME molecules, which may drive the losses in potential seen in the second and fourth reduction plateaus.
[M0219 p8] This highly atypical and non-intuitive electrochemical behavior corroborates that the presence of NH4 + indeed transforms the coordination environment of dissolved polysulfide species away from the clustered lithium polysulfide aggregates typically seen under low-temperature conditions.
[M0220 p8] The divergence in the impact of this additive under the application of low temperatures and lean electrolyte amounts points to the driving influence of the polysulfide coordination environment on electrochemical performance.
[M0221 p8] Additionally, this speaks to the practical capability of additives like NH4TFA, which deliberately take advantage of the intrinsic polysulfide crossover to enable an in situ molecular engineering.28 The additive shown here was selected to assess the joint effects of cationic and anionic modifications to the polysulfide coordination sphere and sets the stage for future efforts into understanding, modifying, and exploiting the solvation and coordination of active materials. ■CONCLUSIONS The materials chemistry underlying the Li−S battery system presents a rich array of complexity due to the innate solutionmediated pathways underscoring the electrochemical behavior.
[M0222 p8] Engineering the system for practical applications requires understanding and improving the discharge behavior under challenging conditions like low E/S ratios and low temperatures.
[M0223 p8] Under such conditions, understanding the coordination environment surrounding the dissolved active material is the key.
[M0224 p8] This is due to the interplay between polysulfides and surrounding species influencing almost every factor involved in discharge, including materials transport properties, chemical activities of dissolved species, and Li2S electrodeposition dynamics.
[M0225 p8] In this work, NH4TFA was utilized in the electrolyte additive to understand the joint impacts of strongly binding TFA−and NH4 + ions on the coordination sphere of lithium polysulfides.
[M0226 p8] These species are used to assess and modulate the fundamental polysulfide clustering dynamics that underlie behavior under lean electrolyte and low-temperature conditions.
[M0227 p8] Through a variety of electrochemical, spectroscopic, and computational studies, the in situ conversion of NH4TFA and lithium polysulfides to LiTFA and diammonium polysulfides is shown to amplify solution-mediated kinetics by deterring the formation of unfavorable clustered polysulfide aggregates.
[M0228 p8] The Li+−Sx 2−framework making up the core of clustered polysulfide networks is disrupted in favor of NH4 +−Sx 2−and Li+−TFA−ionic associations, amplifying the solution-mediated kinetics that the Li−S battery chemistry relies upon.

[S0010 p4] 4 Supporting Discussion Comparing the FTIR results in Figure 2c to the vibrational frequencies acquired though DFT calculations in Table S1, there are a key differences that shed light on the in-situ reaction.
[S0011 p4] In the calculated values shown above, there are strong signals occurring at 1,710 and 1,660 cm-1, respectively, for LiTFA and NH4TFA.
[S0012 p4] These signals correspond to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of the C-O and C=O bonds in the trifluoroacetate structure.
[S0013 p4] These theoretical results very closely match that seen in the experimental results in Figure 2c.
[S0014 p4] The broad peak of NH4TFA in the experimental data is attributed to additional vibrational modes that occur in the NH4+ structure at 1,736 cm-1 in the DFT results.
[S0015 p4] LiTFA should theoretically not actually show any other signals in this range; the other peak present in the experimental spectrum at ~ 1,690 cm-1 may be due to a contaminant, likely water, as the salt is very hygroscopic.
[S0016 p4] This also explains why the spectra in the “Li2S6 + NH4TFA” case predominantly displays the correct signal at 1,725 cm-1, and much less of the lower wavenumber contaminant signal that is present in the pure LiTFA spectra.
[S0017 p4] The resultant LiTFA that forms in-situ is likely higher purity than that in the commercial LiTFA sample.
