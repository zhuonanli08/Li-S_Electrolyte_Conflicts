[M0001 p1] RESEARCH ARTICLE www.advmat.de Macromolecular Boron-Based Salt Enables Dense Interphases for Long-Cycling Lithium-Sulfur Batteries Dejie Qu, Tao Liu,* Youlong Sun, Yuewei Yan, Chuanchuan Li, Zili Cui,* Chuanwei Gao, Shuaice Kong, Zengqi Zhang, Zhiming Liu,* Shu Zhang, Shitao Wang, Zhaolin Lv, Gaojie Xu, Guicun Li,* and Guanglei Cui* Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a compelling next-generation energy storage system with practical energy densities exceeding 700 Wh kg−1, offering a promising pathway beyond current lithium-ion technology.
[M0002 p1] However, their commercial viability remains constrained by deleterious interfacial reactions between lithium metal anodes and polysulfide-containing electrolytes.
[M0003 p1] Herein, it is presented a molecular engineering approach through a novel boron-based salt, lithium perfluoropinacolatoborate (LiFPB), strategically designed to reinforce the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) for long-cycling Li-S batteries.
[M0004 p1] LiFPB anions, featuring higher specific charge (mass-to-charge ratio) and larger steric bulk compared to conventional salts, demonstrate enhanced resistance to Helmholtz double-layer repulsion and increased susceptibility to lithium metal reduction, promoting the formation of a robust SEI enriched with LiF and LiBxOy species.
[M0005 p1] The LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits superior lithium metal compatibility, achieving a high coulombic efficiency of 99.59%.
[M0006 p1] Consequently, Li-S cells demonstrate markedly improved capacity retention from 50.9% to 75.7% over 200 cycles.
[M0007 p1] This strategy has been successfully scaled to Ah-level Li-S pouch cells, achieving practical energy densities of 408 Wh kg−1 with stable cycling over 75 cycles.
[M0008 p1] This work presents an effective approach to developing long-cycling Li-S batteries through the rational design of electrolyte salt.

[M0011 p1] Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have great potential to achieve practical energy densities exceeding 700 Wh kg−1, a crucial threshold for enabling heavy-duty vehicles and electric flight.[1,2] However, the intrinsic precipitation-dissolution conversion mechanism of the S cathode generates soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) as essential intermediates, which inevitably trigger the notorious shuttle effect and subsequent parasitic reactions between lithium (Li) metal anodes and LiPSs.
[M0012 p1] Under practical cell design conditions (high sulfur loading, lean electrolyte, and ultrathin Li metal), the accelerated electrolyte degradation, primarily stemming from these parasitic reactions at the Li metal surface, fundamentally degrades the cycling stability of Li-S batteries.[3,4] Therefore, designing a robust solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) to stabilize Li metal anodes against LiPSinduced deterioration plays a crucial role in realizing long-cycling Li-S batteries.[5,6] The electrolyte composition and its derived SEI fundamentally govern the cycling stability of Li-S batteries.
[M0013 p1] Among various electrolyte components, lithium salts play a pivotal role in enhancing the coulombic efficiency (CE) of Li-S batteries by facilitating the formation of dense, insoluble inorganic components within the SEI layer.
[M0014 p1] Notably, the strategic incorporation of LiNO3 has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness in suppressing the polysulfide shuttle effect, enabling Li-S batteries to achieve CEs exceeding 99%.[7] However, the limited solubility of LiNO3 and its progressive decomposition during cycling compromise the long-term cycling performance stability of Li-S batteries, particularly under low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratios.[8] Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) demonstrate enhanced compatibility with Li metal anodes due to the formation of LiF-rich SEI.
[M0015 p1] Nevertheless, the S═O structure in LiTFSI molecules acts as an electrophilic center susceptible to nucleophilic attack from terminal sulfide species (S−) present in lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn).[9] This mechanistic understanding explains why many LiF-forming sulfonate-based lithium salts prove incompatible as electrolyte components in Li-S batteries.
[M0016 p1] Therefore, the rational design of novel lithium salts that can Adv.

[M0019 p2] During lithium plating, the significant volumetric expansion of the Li metal anode leads to continuous SEI rupture, necessitating persistent SEI reformation.
[M0020 p2] Electric double layer plays a crucial role in SEl formation.
[M0021 p2] At the anode/electrolyte interface, a Li+-rich but anion-depleted inner-Helmholtz layer spontaneously forms through the self-assembly of solvent molecules during charging.
[M0022 p2] This interfacial structure fundamentally dictates the result of interphasial chemistry; notably, the negatively charged electrode surface electrostatically repels anions from the inner Helmholtz layer, significantly impeding the formation of critical inorganic SEI components.[10] To address these challenges, various electrolyte engineering strategies have been developed to establish favorable interphases, including high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs)[11] and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs).[12] However, these approaches often compromise sulfur utilization and adversely affect the discharge voltage plateau.
[M0023 p2] Theoretically, anions with higher specific charge (mass-to-charge ratio) exhibit reduced sensitivity to applied electric fields, thereby demonstrating enhanced resistance to electric double-layer repulsion.
[M0024 p2] This characteristic potentially increases the probability of anion reduction and subsequent formation of desirable inorganic SEI components during charging.
[M0025 p2] In this work, we present an alternative approach to stabilizing Li metal interfaces, diverging from conventional HCE or LHCE strategies.
[M0026 p2] We rationally designed and synthesized a novel lithium perfluoropinacolatoborate (LiFPB) salt through a singlestep reaction in anhydrous conditions, strategically incorporating both C-F and B-containing functionalities.
[M0027 p2] The as-synthesized LiFPB demonstrates dual advantages: exceptional chemical stability with soluble polysulfides and superior SEI formation.
[M0028 p2] Notably, LiFPB (682 g mol−1) possesses a significantly higher specific charge (682/280) (2.43 times that of TFSI−) and a substantially lower LUMO energy level, facilitating the formation of a robust SEI enriched with LiF and LiBxOy species.
[M0029 p2] The LiFPBcontaining electrolyte exhibits remarkable compatibility with Li metal anodes, achieving an unprecedented coulombic efficiency of 99.59%.
[M0030 p2] This superior interfacial stability translates into exceptional performance in practical applications: an Ah-level Li-S pouch cell employing the LiFPB-containing electrolyte delivers a practical energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 while maintaining stable cycling performance over 75 cycles.
[M0031 p2] This novel lithium salt paves the way for boosting the development of practical Li-S batteries. 2.
[M0032 p2] Results and Discussion The electrostatic force experienced by anions near the electrode surface follows Coulomb’s law (F = qE, where F represents force, q denotes charge, and E indicates electric field strength).
[M0033 p2] Consequently, anions possessing higher specific charge (mass-tocharge ratio) demonstrate diminished mobility in response to applied electric fields.
[M0034 p2] These sterically hindered or high molecular weight anions exhibit enhanced resistance to electrostatic repulsion from the Helmholtz double-layer during lithium plating processes.
[M0035 p2] This reduced mobility enables them to remain in closer proximity to the electrode surface, fundamentally altering the composition and morphology of the resulting SEI layer and subsequently influencing lithium deposition behavior.
[M0036 p2] The LiFPB salt with a molecular weight of 682 g mol−1 (Figure 1a) was synthesized via a one-pot reaction of lithium borohydride (LiBH4) with perfluoropinacol in a molar ratio of 1:2, using tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the solvent at 60 °C (Figure S1, Supporting Information).
[M0037 p2] This approach minimizes the introduction of impurities, as the entire process is conducted in an organic solvent environment, thereby reducing the need for additional purification steps.[13,14] The peak observed at m/z = 674.95 in the mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Figure 1b) aligns precisely with the theoretical molecular weight of the FPB−anion.
[M0038 p2] Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis further validates the structural integrity and purity of the compound.
[M0039 p2] A single distinct peak was observed in the 11B NMR spectrum at 11.40 ppm (Figure 1c) and in the 19F NMR spectrum at −69.24 ppm (Figure 1d), with no additional peak indicative of impurities.
[M0040 p2] These results confirm the presence of only one type of boron and fluorine chemical environment within the salt, underscoring its high purity and structural homogeneity.
[M0041 p2] Upon incorporating 0.2 M LiFPB, 0.8 M LiTFSI, and 1 wt.% LiNO3 into a solution comprising DME and DOL at a volumetric ratio of 1:1, the 11B NMR spectrum retains its characteristic peak at 11.40 ppm (Figure S2a, Supporting Information), while the 19F NMR spectrum exhibits two peaks at −69.24 and −79.80 ppm (Figure S2b, Supporting Information), the latter peak attributed to LiTFSI.[15] In the 13C NMR spectrum, two distinct sets of carbon signals are observed, corresponding to the C─O and −CF3 groups in the FPB−anion (Figure S3, Supporting Information).
[M0042 p2] We conducted a series of reactions between polysulfide solutions and both LiFPB and LiTFSI (Figure S4, Supporting Information).
[M0043 p2] Upon analysis of the mixture containing LiTFSI and Li2S6, a notable shift in the characteristic 19F NMR of LiTFSI was observed, migrating from −79.8 to −79.52 ppm (Figure S5a, Supporting Information).
[M0044 p2] Concurrently, a distinct by-product emerged at −75.39 ppm (Figure S5b, Supporting Information).
[M0045 p2] These findings unequivocally demonstrate the inherent incompatibility between LiTFSI and polysulfides, highlighting the susceptibility of LiTFSI to nucleophilic attack by polysulfide species.
[M0046 p2] In stark contrast, when LiFPB was combined with Li2S6, the characteristic 19F NMR peak of LiFPB exhibited remarkable stability, remaining invariant.
[M0047 p2] This observation underscores the exceptional resistance of LiFPB to nucleophilic attack by polysulfides, thereby establishing its superior compatibility with these compounds (Figure S6, Supporting Information).
[M0048 p2] The low reactivity may be due to the high steric hindrance of high fluorine groups and the low valence state of boron (+3).
[M0049 p2] This unique structural arrangement imparts LiFPB an extraordinary degree of chemical robustness, rendering it impervious to the deleterious effects of polysulfide interactions.
[M0050 p2] These results indicate that LiFPB exhibits excellent chemical compatibility with polysulfides.
[M0051 p2] The electronic properties of the synthesized FPB− anion were characterized through frontier molecular orbital analysis, which are critical parameters for evaluating the SEI-forming ability.
[M0052 p2] Notably, the FPB−anion (4.55 eV) exhibits a significantly lower LUMO energy level compared to the TFSI−anion (4.75 eV) (Figure 1e), indicating enhanced thermodynamic favorability toward electrochemical reduction and subsequent SEI formation.
[M0053 p2] Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further elucidated the interfacial interaction mechanisms between LiFPB and lithium metal.

[M0087 p6] Detailed spectroscopic analysis of the B 1s core-level spectrum (Figure 3c) confirmed the formation of boron-oxygen (B─O) coordination structures, which serve as strategically positioned electrophilic sites for preferential coordination with TFSI−anions.
[M0088 p6] The selective anion immobilization mechanism effectively mitigates electrolyte decomposition through the formation of a robust anode-electrolyte interface.[22,23] Comparative analysis revealed that lithium metal electrodes cycled in conventional electrolytes demonstrated progressive interfacial degradation, characterized by the time-dependent accumulation of electrolyte decomposition byproducts.
[M0089 p6] X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth profiling identified the formation of organic carbonyl (C═O) and alkoxy (C─O) species, along with inorganic lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) compounds, whose concentration gradients exhibited a strong correlation with sputtering duration (Figure S10, Supporting Information).
[M0090 p6] Depth profiling by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) corroborates these findings.
[M0091 p6] In our TOF-SIMS analysis, a substantial signal for Li2F+ was detected.
[M0092 p6] Li2F+ was commonly employed to identify the presence of LiF, thereby indicating that the electrolyte containing LiFPB is conducive to the formation of an SEI layer enriched with LiF.
[M0093 p6] Notably, the LiF distribution exhibited both enhanced concentration and improved spatial homogeneity (Figure 3e) across the electrode interface, with these beneficial characteristics being maintained throughout the etching profile (Figure S11, Supporting Information).
[M0094 p6] Spectroscopic and electrochemical analyses revealed that LiFPB facilitate the formation of lithium borate (LiBxOy) complexes at the lithium metal interface.
[M0095 p6] These boratecontaining species serve as rapid lithium-ion conductors within the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI),[24–27] facilitating the uniform deposition of Li metal (Figure 3f).
[M0096 p6] Depth-resolved chemical characterization demonstrated significantly enhanced retention of intact TFSI−anions at the electrode interface in LiFPBcontaining electrolytes (Figure S11, Supporting Information),[25] indicative of suppressed electrolyte decomposition pathways.
[M0097 p6] This electrochemical stabilization originates from the formation of an ionically conductive and mechanically robust SEI layer, which effectively reduces the direct contact between the electrode and the electrolyte and minimizes parasitic reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface (Figures S12,S13, Supporting Information).
[M0098 p6] Conventional electrolytes typically induce severe interfacial side reactions through electrolyte decomposition, resulting in the formation of a thick, heterogeneous, and porous solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).
[M0099 p6] In marked contrast, our systematic characterization reveals that LiFPB incorporation enables the formation of an ultrathin, densely packed bilayer SEI architecture.
[M0100 p6] This optimized interfacial structure comprises an outer organic-rich matrix and an inner inorganicrich domain with substantial LiF enrichment (Figure 3g; Figure S14, Supporting Information).
[M0101 p6] The rationally designed SEI configuration not only enhances the mechanical integrity and electrochemical stability of the lithium metal anode but also effectively suppresses parasitic reactions at the electrodeelectrolyte interface.
[M0102 p6] Consequently, the LiFPB-containing system demonstrates significantly improved cycling stability and rate capability.[28] To systematically assess the redox stability of the battery incorporating LiFPB, the exchange current density (I0) was utilized as a quantitative metric for surface diffusion resistance (Figure 4a).
[M0103 p6] Upon introduction of LiFPB into the electrolyte, the exchange current density associated with the electroplating and stripping of lithium metal exhibited a pronounced increase, escalating from 0.689 to 1.001 mA/cm2, thereby representing a substantial 45.3% enhancement.
[M0104 p6] This remarkable augmentation in interfacial charge transfer efficiency is principally attributable to the formation of a highly conductive solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer, which is characterized by optimized lithium-ion diffusion pathways.
[M0105 p6] The presence of LiFPB in the electrolyte promotes the formation of an SEI layer that is rich in LiF and LiBxOy compounds.
[M0106 p6] These compounds facilitate rapid lithium-ion transport, thereby significantly enhancing the overall conductivity of the SEI layer.
[M0107 p6] The engineered anode interface architecture significantly contributes to the enhanced electrochemical performance and cycling stability of the battery system.
[M0108 p6] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) of Li||Cu half-cells (0.2 mV s−1) revealed superior redox characteristics in LiFPB-containing electrolytes, with higher peak currents and well-defined redox potentials versus conventional electrolytes (Figure 4b).
[M0109 p6] This trend was confirmed in Li-S full cells across multiple scan rates (Figure 4c; Figure S15, Supporting Information).[27] Furthermore, our investigations have elucidated that, in contrast to LiTFSI, LiFPB salt manifests a preferential reduction potential and a conspicuously higher reduction peak intensity (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
[M0110 p6] This observation intimates that LiFPB salt is more susceptible to decomposition reactions and exhibits superior compatibility with metallic lithium, a propensity that can be attributed to the highly efficacious SEI layer it engenders on the negative electrode surface.
[M0111 p6] Despite both salts being classified as readily reducible lithium salts, the SEI precipitated from the reduction of LiFPB markedly outperforms that of LiTFSI in terms of efficacy.
[M0112 p6] Notably, the adsorption energy of LiFPB on the lithium metal surface is of a value higher than that of DME and DOL (Figure S17, Supporting Information).
[M0113 p6] This finding unequivocally suggests that LiFPB exhibits a preferential adsorption affinity on the lithium metal surface.
[M0114 p6] The solvation structure was further analyzed by Raman spectroscopy.
[M0115 p6] The LiFPB salt did not significantly affect the Raman shifts of DME, DOL, and LiTFSI (Figure 4d; Figure S18, Supporting Information),[29] indicating that the SEI modification is not attributed to changes in the solvation structure.

[M0118 p6] This reduction in impedance indicates that the LiFPB-containing electrolyte effectively lowers the internal resistance of the battery (Figure 4f).

[M0160 p10] To investigate the underlying mechanisms, the morphology of the Li metal after 40 cycles was examined (Figure 5i–k).
[M0161 p10] The cycled Li metal in the conventional electrolyte displayed severe corrosion, characterized by numerous cracks and a highly porous structure.
[M0162 p10] This degradation is primarily attributed to continuous reactions between Li metal and polysulfides in the electrolyte.
[M0163 p10] In contrast, the Li surface cycled with the LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibited larger particles with a more uniform, adherent, and smooth morphology, even as the number of cycles increases to 100 (Figure 5l).[33] The surface degradation observed in conventional electrolytes was likely attributed to non-uniform electric currents caused by cracking and porosity, which result in irregular lithium plating and stripping.
[M0164 p10] These issues ultimately lead to a reduction in the cycle life of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries.
[M0165 p10] Incorporating LiFPB into electrolyte effectively suppressed the shuttle effect and facilitates the formation of a thin and uniform SEI.
[M0166 p10] These improvements mitigated undesirable side reactions, promoted stable lithium deposition, and significantly enhanced the cycling stability and overall performance of Li-S batteries.[34,35] To evaluate the practical viability of the modified electrolyte, an Ah-level Li-S pouch cell was fabricated.
[M0167 p10] The cell delivered an impressive energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 at 0.1 C, maintaining 62.24% capacity retention with a high average coulombic efficiency of 99.02% over 76 cycles (Figure 6a).
[M0168 p10] This result indicate that the incorporation of LiFPB into electrolyte can effectively relieve the corrosion of lithium metal by polysulfidecontaining electrolyte in pouch cells by froming a robust SEI.[36] The electrostatic repulsion of conventional anions within the inner Helmholtz layer (IHP), arising from the negatively charged electrode surface, hinders the formation of crucial inorganic SEI components.
[M0169 p10] However, LiFPB anions, possessing a higher specific charge (mass-to-charge ratio), demonstrate reduced susceptibility to the applied electric field and consequently greater resistance to IHP repulsion (Figure 6b).
[M0170 p10] For the in situ infrared spectroscopy analysis, a comparative examination of the C-F peak variations in the LiFPB and LiTFSI systems revealed a distinct correlation between the detected C-F signal intensity and the adsorption/desorption dynamics of anions within the inner Helmholtz layer as charge-discharge cycling progressed (Figure S27, Supporting Information).
[M0171 p10] Specifically, the characteristic absorption peak of LiFPB was consistently observed throughout the entire cycling process, with its intensity fluctuations being markedly smaller than that of TFSI−.
[M0172 p10] This finding provides compelling evidence that FPB−is indeed present within the electric double layer and exhibits superior stability during the chargedischarge process, thereby indicating that LiFPB possesses a significantly enhanced retention rate within the double layer.
[M0173 p10] The resultant thin, ionically conductive, and robust LiF-rich and LiBxOy-rich SEI layer effectively suppressed Li dendrite growth (Figure 6c), ultimately enhancing the cycling stability of Li-S pouch cells. 3.
[M0174 p10] Conclusion In this work, we demonstrated that LiFPB served as a highly effective co-salt for Li-S batteries by simultaneously addressing two critical challenges: the polysulfide shuttle effect and the Li metal anode degradation.
[M0175 p10] Mechanistically, the incorporation of LiFPB facilitated the formation of a robust SEI layer enriched with LiF and LiBxOy components, which significantly suppressed electrolyte decomposition and enabled homogeneous Li plating/stripping.
[M0176 p10] The exceptional electrochemical performance can be rationalized by the unique molecular features of LiFPB, specifically its high specific charge capacity and favorable reduction potential, which synergistically enhance SEI stability and minimize parasitic side reactions.
[M0177 p10] The practical viability of this strategy is evidenced by the superior performance in both high-loading Li-S half-cells and Ah-level pouch cells across various C-rates, representing a significant advancement toward the commercialization of high-energy-density Li-S batteries.

[S0096 p0] In traditional lithium-sulfur batteries, significant challenges persist not only with the cathode, characterized by low ionic conductivity and limited tap density, but also with the instability of the lithium metal anode.
[S0097 p0] On the anode side, severe parasitic reactions occur between the electrolyte and the lithium metal, resulting in the formation of an unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer.
[S0098 p0] This instability leads to the generation of "dead lithium" and irreversible lithium loss.
[S0099 p0] The introduction of LiFPB-containing electrolytes effectively addresses these issues by mitigating the polysulfide shuttle effect and facilitating the formation of a robust and stable SEI layer, enriched with LiF and LiBxOy, on the surface of the lithium metal anode.
[S0100 p0] Moreover, the result SEI layer is thinner, more uniform, and significantly more stable, thereby improving the overall performance and longevity of the battery system.
