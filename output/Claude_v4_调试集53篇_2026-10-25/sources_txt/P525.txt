[M0006 p1] Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have emerged as a promising energy storage system, capturing significant attention in recent years.
[M0007 p1] Their immense appeal stems from the impressive theoretical capacity and the use of sulfur–a low-cost, abundant, and environmentally benign mate­ rial [1–4].
[M0008 p1] Nevertheless, the pathway to their commercialization is fraught with challenges.
[M0009 p1] Among these, the infamous “shuttle effect” stands out, where soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPS) migrate between the electrodes, leading to rapid capacity decay and self-discharge, especially for the cell operation under lean electrolyte condition [5–8].
[M0010 p1] Moreover, the inherent sluggish kinetics associated with the multi-step conversion of sulfur redox reaction (SRR) poses a significant hurdle to achieving high rate performance [9–12].
[M0011 p1] Additionally, in­ compatibility between the electrode materials and electrolyte compro­ mises both the cell’s overall efficiency and its lifespan [13].
[M0012 p1] These issues, while being actively researched, remain critical barriers that need to be conquered to boost the implementation of Li-S batteries [14–16].
[M0013 p1] Traditional strategies of physical confinement or chemical adsorp­ tion focus too much on the sulfur cathode [17–21], failing to solve the intertwined issues of Li metal anode, especially for the Li/electrolyte compatibility and the parasitical side reactions between the polysulfides (PS) and Li metal, both of which incur the loss of active materials, rapid capacity decay, and even safety concerns [22–24].
[M0014 p1] Electrolyte engi­ neering including solvent recipe modification and salt additive optimi­ zation provides a new platform to manipulate both the Li-S chemistry and solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) structure, which generally fall within the categories of sparingly solvating electrolytes (SSE) and highly solvating electrolytes (HSE).
[M0015 p1] In the SSE class, the solvation of LiPS is largely ameliorated in the limited solvent and/or selective solvent, which requires the supporting salt to solvate more readily over the LiPS.
[M0016 p1] However, it also faces daunting challenges such as high viscosity, low ionic conductivity, high cell polarization, and sluggish redox reaction kinetics.
[M0017 p1] Adopting the opposite strategy, the HSE system provides a higher LiPS solubility at less electrolyte to achieve faster reaction ki­ netics.
[M0018 p1] But the high reactivity of PS with Li metal anode requires special protection for the anode side and robust construction of SEI to avoid the cell dry-out and failure [16,25–27].
[M0019 p1] It can be found that the adaptations in the two motifs often fall short of meeting rigorous standards for lean electrolyte usage in high-energy–density Li-S batteries.

[M0025 p2] However, owing to the heterogeneous double-shell solvation structure constructed by the two concentric solvents, the EPSE-based Li-S batteries usually suffer from large cell polarization, poor rate performance, unsatisfactory utilization of active materials, and finally inferior energy density at the pouch cell level, failing to meet the ever-increasing energy demand [28–31].

[M0027 p2] As a proof-of-concept study to address the above-mentioned chal­ lenges in electrolyte engineering of Li-S batteries, herein, we propose the concept of steric tuning electrolyte (STE) to substantially accelerate the cathode sulfur reaction kinetics and maintain a superb metallic Li compatibility as well as mechanically robust SEI layer under lean elec­ trolyte condition via incorporating suitable methyl propyl ether (MPE).
[M0028 p2] With the manipulation of stereochemistry, the steric hindrance effect of ether solvent molecules and the LiPS solvation structure are dramati­ cally optimized, which results in a stronger protection of the Li metal anode and promoted kinetics of SRR.
[M0029 p2] Benefiting from the ameliorated local environment of STE, the electrolyte-starved Li-S cells deliver decent electrochemical performance at high sulfur loading, which is expected to promote the practical application of energy-dense Li-S batteries.
[M0030 p2] Moreover, the Li-S cells could still operate well under the gelid condition, which may promote the practical application in cold environment. 2.
[M0031 p2] Materials and electrode preparations The high conductive graphene (HCG) was purchased from Suzhou Tanfeng Graphene Technology Co.
[M0032 p2] For the HCG/S preparation, the S was infiltrated into HCG using a melt-diffusion strategy at 160 ◦C for 24 h, forming well-contacted and homogeneous HCG/S composites.
[M0033 p2] The cathode materials were prepared by mixing HCG/S, acetylene black, and polyvinylende fluoride (PVDF) binder in a weight ratio of 8: 1: 1 in Nmethyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP).
[M0034 p2] The slurry was then coated onto the aluminum foil and dried at 50 ◦C for 12 h.
[M0035 p2] Afterwards, the dried coated aluminum foil was punched into disks with a diameter of 14 mm for Li-S coin cell use, and the loading of the active-material was ~ 1.1 mg cm−2. 2.2.
[M0036 p2] Preparation of electrolyte Conventional electrolyte (CE), i.e., 1.0 M LiTFSI in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/ dimethoxyethane (DME) (1: 1, by vol.), and STE, i.e., 1.0 M LiTFSI in MPE/DOL/DME (4: 3: 3, by vol.), and 2 wt% LiNO3 was employed.
[M0037 p2] The electrolyte was prepared in an Ar-filled glove box. 3.
[M0038 p2] Results and discussion With an aim to screen a target solvent with relatively low solvation ability and relatively higher reduction stability, one of the O atoms in the dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent was tentatively substituted with a C atom to eliminate the chelation effect and provide the electron feedback to the H atoms, which results in the conceptual molecule of methyl butyl ether (MBE) (Fig. 1a) [32,33].
[M0039 p2] However, the high flexibility of longchain C enables MBE solvent with higher steric hindrance effect that should otherwise be avoided for achieving better reaction dynamics.
[M0040 p2] To this end, one C atom is further eliminated from the MBE molecular, which readily reaps the target of MPE.
[M0041 p2] Comparing with the traditional solvent of DME and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), the MPE solvent carries a shorter chain and the decrease in O atom endows the H atom with a better ability to avoid the nucleophilic attack of PS with high reduction reactivity (Fig. 1a), which are highly favorable for propelling the reac­ tion kinetics of Li-S chemistry (vide infra).
[M0042 p2] Meanwhile, the shrinkage in molecular volume would endow MPE with less steric effect to accelerate Li+ de-solvation process and block the Li+-ether complex, thus increasing the reaction kinetics [32].

[M0058 p3] The inherent molecular properties of MPE were systematically investigated for an understanding of the function of target solvent.
[M0059 p3] By calculating the electrostatic potential (ESP), it is found that the electron density isosurface of MPE is more neutralized (Fig. 1b), indicating that the H atoms of MPE possess enhanced electronegativity while the O atom possess weakened electropositivity.
[M0060 p3] The ameliorated molecular polarization should benefit from the reduced O atom number.
[M0061 p3] Since the long-range secondary electrostatic interactions can be negligible in MPE molecule [39], the ESP behavior of MPE predicts that it not only helps reduce the side reactions brought by the disastrous nucleophilic attack by the PS species with high reduction reactivity but also contributes to a better compatibility between the electrolyte and Li anode.

[M0182 p7] In comparison, the cell with STE still exhibits the traditional discharge behavior, albeit with the appreciable polarization voltage (Fig.
[M0183 p7] S27b), clearly manifesting the enhanced redox kinetics in STE.
[M0184 p7] Importantly, the cells with STE could stably operates under −30 ◦C for 100 cycles at 0.5C (Fig. 5e), whereas the cells with CE exhibit signifi­ cantly degraded capacity attainment, incarnating the great application potentials over wide-temperature conditions for STE.
[M0185 p7] To better understand the appealing superiorities of STE in Li-S electrochemistry, we conducted ex-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) mea­ surements to track the speciation evolution of the solid sulfur species during the discharge/charge process.

[M0188 p7] Moreover, this alternant emergence and disappearance of S8 and Li2S species is more pronounced in the subsequent charge process for STE system compared with that in CE.
[M0189 p7] Although the PS dissolution is suppressed, the Li-S dynamics in STE still outperforms the CE due to the reduced steric effect which reduces the steric hindrance to construct the accessible Li+ diffusion channel to the PS.

[M0212 p8] Conclusions The stereochemistry-directed solvent molecular design aiming at modulating the local environment of the electrolyte to coordinate weakly with Li+ was proposed to address the comprehensive issues of Li- S electrochemistry.
[M0213 p8] With weak solvation ability and lowered steric effect of MPE, the STE guarantees a significantly alleviated parasitic reactions between PS and Li anode as well as a promoted kinetics of SRR process.
[M0214 p8] In virtue of these preponderances of STE, the Li-S batteries with prac­ tically relevant parameters (5.2 mg cm−2 and 5.0 μL mg−1) deliver decent electrochemical performances.
[M0215 p8] Moreover, benefiting from the better desolvation kinetics, the cell with STE exhibits substantial po­ tential in the application of gelid conditions.
[M0216 p8] In light of the economy and simplicity, the motif of STE is expected to promote the practical appli­ cation of energy-dense Li-S batteries and even other alkali metal-sulfur batteries.
