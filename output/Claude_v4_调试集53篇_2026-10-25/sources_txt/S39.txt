[M0001 p1] Tailoring Electrolytes by Decoupling the Roles of Li+ and Lithium Polysulfides in Li−S Batteries Jingtian Yang,# Xiaowei Wang,# Qian Liu, Qijia Zhu, Wei Jiang, Jiayi Xu, Cong Liu, Seoung-Bum Son, Zhenzhen Yang, and Zhengcheng Zhang* Cite This: ACS Energy Lett. 2025, 10, 5674−5683 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Understanding the distinct roles of lithium ions (Li+) and lithium polysulfide intermediates, Li2Sx (LiPS) is critical for designing electrolytes that can extend the practical cycle life of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries.
[M0002 p1] In this work, we decouple the solvation and solubility effects of Li+ and LiPS and correlate them with electrochemical performance through a cosolvent strategy.
[M0003 p1] Li+ solubility and solvation primarily dictate the electrolyte’s ionic conductivity and the reversibility of lithium anode stripping/plating.
[M0004 p1] In contrast, LiPS solvation governs the thermodynamics of sulfur (S8), LiPS, and lithium sulfide (Li2S) interconversion, while the LiPS solubility determines their redox kinetics.
[M0005 p1] By employing a fluorinated−glyme (F-glyme) cosolvent, specifically 1,2-bis(2,2-difluoroethoxy)ethane (F4DEE), that exhibits low LiPS solubility yet moderate Li+ solvation, we designed an electrolyte that enhances lithium anode stability while maintaining sufficient sulfur cathode kinetics, thereby prolonging Li−S cell cycle life.
[M0006 p1] This study provides mechanistic insights into the interplay between Li+ and LiPS in Li−S electrochemistry and offers design principles for next-generation electrolytes for Li−S batteries.
[M0007 p1] O wing to their high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1) and natural abundance of sulfur,1 lithium− sulfur (Li−S) batteries have emerged as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation rechargeable battery technologies, aiming to overcome the limitations of traditional lithium-ion batteries.2 However, Li−S chemistry faces intrinsic challenges primarily related to lithium polysulfide (LiPS) conversion and lithium anode degradation, which hinder commercialization.
[M0008 p1] The sulfur redox pathway involves repeated conversion among insoluble sulfur (S8, solid), soluble LiPS intermediates (liquid), and insoluble lithium sulfide (Li2S, solid).
[M0009 p1] This sluggish multistep and multiphase process reduces the utilization of active sulfur material.3,4 Simultaneously, the reactive lithium anode is prone to pulverization and dendrite growth, negatively impacting cycle life and safety.5 Moreover, soluble LiPS species diffuse to the anode side −a phenomenon known as the “shuttle effect” −where they react with lithium metal, accelerating lithium corrosion and reducing Coulombic efficiency (CE).6 These issues limit the practical cycle life of Li−S batteries.
[M0010 p1] Electrolytes play a pivotal role in Li−S battery performance by governing the Li anode and LiPS conversion through controlling the solubility and solvation of Li+ and LiPS.4,7 Conventional ether-based electrolytes, such as 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME),4 typically exhibit high lithium salt and LiPS solubility with strong Li+ and LiPS solvation.
[M0011 p1] While this supports ionic conductivity, it leads to severe LiPS shuttle and parasitic reactions on the lithium anode, limiting cycle life.8,9 To address this, sparely solvating electrolytes (SSEs) have been developed by adding nonsolvating cosolvents −often fluorinated ethers (F-ethers) −to DOL and DME, including compounds like 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoro- 3-(1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyoxy)propane (TTE),10,11 bis(2,2,2- trifluoroethyl) ether (BTFE),12 and octafluoropentyl-1,1,2,2- tetrafluoroethyl ether (OFE).13 The approach suppresses LiPS dissolution that mitigates the shuttle effect, and forms a fluorine-rich solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the Li anode,10,13,14 which helps regulate plating/stripping.

[M0014 p2] these electrolytes substantially inhibit Li+ and LiPS solubility and solvation.
[M0015 p2] In extreme cases, the redox mechanism shifts to a sluggish quasi-solid-state sulfur conversion pathway that requires elevated temperatures.15,16 Additionally, the high viscosity of these electrolytes can cause poor electrode wetting, especially with high sulfur-loading cathodes.17 Despite these advances, achieving an optimal balance between lithium anode stability and fast sulfur redox kinetics remains a challenge.18−20 Li+ and LiPS exhibit distinct behaviors in Li−S electrolytes: LiPS tends to cluster, while lithium salts such as lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) dissociate into Li+ cations and TFSI− anions.21−23 Furthermore, it is important to distinguish solvation and solubility, because they describe different physiochemical processes.
[M0016 p2] Solubility is a macroscopic property that quantifies how much of a solute (e.g., LiTFSI or LiPS) can be dissolved in the solvent.
[M0017 p2] In contrast, solvation refers to the molecular-level interactions between the solute and the solvent molecules (Figure S1).24 An example for this distinction is that LiPS dimers and clusters exhibit high solubility but are weakly solvated in many solvents.21−23 Therefore, the design of advanced electrolytes requires decoupling the solubility and solvation of LiPS and Li+ to accelerate sulfur redox kinetics and enhance Li anode stability concurrently.
[M0018 p2] In this work, we propose a partially fluorinated glyme (Fglyme) cosolvent, 1,2-bis(2,2-difluoroethoxy) ethane (F4DEE), combined with DOL to form a Li−S battery electrolyte.
[M0019 p2] F4DEE exhibits low LiPS solubility, similar to other fluorinated ethers, but maintains high LiTFSI solubility and moderate Li+ solvation.
[M0020 p2] As a result, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte features moderate Li+ solvation, moderate LiPS solubility, and weak LiPS solvation, distinguishing it from conventional ether electrolytes (DOL/DME) and F-ether electrolyte (DOL/TTE).
[M0021 p2] Correlating these properties with Li− S electrochemical performance, we find that all tested electrolytes with an optimal 1.0 M LiTFSI concentration show high Li+ conductivity, regardless of the Li+ solvating strength.
[M0022 p2] However, the moderate Li+ solvation in DOL/ F4DEE effectively regulates lithium stripping/plating, improving the anode stability.
[M0023 p2] On the cathode side, weak LiPS solvation thermodynamically favors short-chain LiPS (Li2Sx, x = 2−4) and promotes the oxidation conversion of LiPS to S8 during charging.
[M0024 p2] This enhances sulfur cathode reversibility and protects the lithium anode from corrosive long-chain LiPS.
[M0025 p2] Additionally, we illustrate that the small Li−S redox overpotentials correlate with high LiPS solubility owing to facilitated LiPS diffusion.
[M0026 p2] The moderate LiPS solubility in DOL/F4DEE thus supports fast sulfur redox kinetics, resulting in low cell overpotentials during cycling.
[M0027 p2] Collectively, these features contribute to the extended cycle life of Li−S batteries using the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte.
[M0028 p2] The fundamental insights and electrolyte design strategy presented here pave the way toward developing long-lifespan Li−S batteries. ■LITFSI/LIPS SOLUBILITY AND LI+/LIPS SOLVATION IN DOL/F4DEE ELECTROLYTE F4DEE is a partially fluorinated glyme (PFG).

[M0030 p2] Meanwhile, the F4DEE molecule is moderately fluorinated on the terminal alkyl groups, different from fluorine-free DME and highly fluorinated TTE.

[M0035 p3] As shown in Figure 1a, F4DEE exhibits exceptionally high LiTFSI solubility (∼6.2 M), which even surpasses that of DME and DOL.
[M0036 p3] In contrast, LiTFSI salt is hardly dissolved in TTE solvent, validated by both the dissolution test and 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (19F NMR) results (Figures S2 and S3).
[M0037 p3] Despite superior lithium salt solubility, F4DEE interestingly exhibits moderate Li+ solvation, as identified by 19F NMR (Figure 1b, Figure S4).
[M0038 p3] Specifically, six solvents were selected to formulate electrolytes with 1.0 M LiTFSI, including DME, DOL/DME (1:1, v/v), DOL, F4DEE, DOL/F4DEE (8:2, v/ v), and DOL/TTE (8:2, v/v).
[M0039 p3] The upshift of the 19F NMR signal of TFSI−originates from a stronger coordination of the TFSI−−Li+ ion pair, indicating Li+ solvation strength is in the order of DOL/TTE < DOL ≈F4DEE < DOL/DME < DME.
[M0040 p3] Such a trend was further validated by Raman spectroscopy (Figure S5).25 Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation illustrates that F4DEE partially enters the Li+ solvation shell in the DOL/ F4DEE electrolyte (Figure S6).
[M0041 p3] Moreover, the distribution of various coordination numbers (1, 2 and 3) of TFSI− surrounding Li+ (Figure 1c)26,27 suggests moderate Li+ solvation in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte, because Li+ shows a higher tendency (higher percentile) to be coordinated by more TFSI−than that in the DOL/DME electrolyte.
[M0042 p3] Overall, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte presents moderate Li+ solvation with an anion-dominated solvation structure.
[M0043 p3] The combination of high LiTFSI solubility and moderate Li+ solvation arises from the PFG structure of F4DEE.
[M0044 p3] The local dipole of the F4DEE molecule effectively disperses LiTFSI, thereby enhancing its solubility.
[M0045 p3] At the same time, fluorination weakens the solvent’s coordinating ability, resulting in only moderate Li+ solvation (Note S1).
[M0046 p3] Apart from Li+, the introduction of F4DEE also modifies the LiPS solubility and solvation in the electrolyte.
[M0047 p3] Li2S6 dissolution experiment (Figure 1d, Figure S8) exhibits spare Li2S6 solubility in F4DEE (0.024 M), slightly higher than TTE (almost insoluble) but much lower than DOL (0.41 M) and DME (2.5 M).
[M0048 p3] This indicates that F4DEE easily dissociates Li+-TFSI−to yield high LiTFSI solubility (6.2 M), but barely dissociates Li+-Sx 2−.
[M0049 p3] This is because Li+-Sx 2−is much more strongly bonded than Li+-TFSI−, and F4DEE is less capable of coordinating with LiPS than DOL and DME.21 Consequently, the DOL/F4DEE mixed solvent shows a moderate Li2S6 solubility of 0.38 M, in sharp contrast to 1.0 M in DOL/ DME and 0.22 M in DOL/TTE.
[M0050 p3] This is the desirable LiPS solubility that remarkably impacts Li−S electrochemical performances, which will be discussed later.
[M0051 p3] The impact of F4DEE on LiPS solvation was evaluated by ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopy (Figure 1e).
[M0052 p3] The absence of the LiS3 • radical (600 nm) and the decreased intensity of the LiPS absorption peaks (300 and 400 nm) identify the weak LiPS solvating power of DOL/F4DEE.24,28 This is further supported by the intensified LiPS clustering, as shown in the number density distribution of the S6 2−anion around Li+ (Figure 1f).29 The main peak at 2.3 Å implies strong Li+−S6 2−pairing, which is distinctive from the more weakly associated Li+−TFSI−ion pair.
[M0053 p3] Outer local maxima at 4.3 and 5.5 Å represent larger Li+-S6 2−clusters.
[M0054 p3] Their increasing peak intensity demonstrates a stronger LiPS clustering tendency, further validating the weak LiPS solvating power of DOL/F4DEE.23 As a result, by introducing PFGstructured F4DEE, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte boasts high LiTFSI solubility, moderate LiPS solubility, moderate Li+ solvation, and weak LiPS solvation.

[M0067 p4] Lithium salt concentration and Li+ solvation in the electrolyte determine Li stability by influencing Li+ transfer and Li solid-electrolyte interface (SEI).4 Li+ conductivity increases with LiTFSI concentration but will reach the peak value because high LiTFSI concentration impairs Li+ solvation and hinders Li+ transport.30 Moreover, high LiTFSI concentration inhibits LiPS dissolution by suppressing LiPS dissociation equilibrium (namely, the common-ion effect), resulting in low sulfur utilization.31 Hence, the LiTFSI concentration was optimized to be 1.0 M (Note S2).
[M0068 p4] The DOL/F4DEE electrolyte possesses a high Li+ conductivity of 3.6 mS cm−1, indicating its capability to facilitate Li+ transport in the bulk electrolyte.
[M0069 p4] In addition to high Li+ conductivity, the moderate Li+ solvation in DOL/F4DEE enhances the Li stripping/plating stability.
[M0070 p4] Figure 2a and Figure S10 show Li stripping/plating Coulombic efficiency (CE) with varied Li+ solvation measured by Aurbach method in Li||Cu cells.32 Electrolytes with weaker Li+ solvation generally deliver higher CE.
[M0071 p4] Among them, DOL/ F4DEE delivers the highest CE of 98.25%, demonstrating its outstanding Li stripping/plating reversibility.
[M0072 p4] The moderate Li+ solvation in DOL/F4DEE also regulates the stripping/ plating process of the Li anode, showing chunky and dense Li deposition morphology with a relatively reduced Li thickness (Figure 2b, Figure S11).
[M0073 p4] Such a morphology reduces the exposed interface area between the Li metal and the electrolyte, suppressing parasitic reactions for high CE.33 These results demonstrate the correlation between moderate Li+ solvation and improved Li stripping/plating reversibility in DOL/F4DEE, which aligns well with previous studies on the Li metal anode.18,19,34 The improved stability of the Li anode in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte is further validated by its superior CE during long-term Li||Cu stripping/plating cycling (Figure S12).
[M0074 p4] To further elucidate the beneficial role of moderate Li+ solvation in DOL/F4DEE, we analyzed the chemical composition of the Li SEI via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling (Figure S13).
[M0075 p4] The F-rich Li SEI in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte is validated by the intensified LiF signal and the extra −CF3 signal in Figure 2c and Figure S14.
[M0076 p4] In addition, the anion-derived N-containing species in the Li SEI are more abundant in DOL/F4DEE than in DOL/DME (Figures S15 and S16).
[M0077 p4] Thus, the enriched TFSI−anion and F4DEE in the Li+ solvation shell give rise to LiF-rich SEI with the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte.
[M0078 p4] This LiF-rich SEI enables dense Li deposition morphology, which plays a critical role in achieving high Li plating/stripping CE (Note S3, Figure 2d).35 Moreover, the moderate LiPS solubility in DOL/F4DEE enhances the Li anode cyclability because it confines the concentration of corrosive LiPS in the electrolyte.36 The weak Figure 2.

[M0087 p5] These attributes collectively stabilize the Li anode in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte. ■WEAK LIPS SOLVATION OF DOL/F4DEE INFLUENCES THE LIPS THERMODYNAMIC CONVERSION Another grand challenge in Li−S batteries is to facilitate a fast, reversible redox reaction on the sulfur cathode.
[M0088 p5] The influence of the Li+ and Li anode on the overall kinetics is generally negligible (Note S3), which also aligns with literature results.37 Here we examined the effect of LiPS solvation on cathode performance by using DOL/DME, DOL/TTE, and DOL/ F4DEE electrolytes.
[M0089 p5] The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was employed in Li||carbon paper (CP) coin cells with a 0.1 M Li2S6 catholyte.
[M0090 p5] The first discharge of the Li||CP cell (Figure 3a) exhibits two characteristic plateaus: one at 2.1 V (stage II), corresponding to liquid−liquid reduction of long-chain Li2Sx (x = 5−8), and the other at 1.8 V (stage III), associated with the liquid−solid reduction of shortchain Li2Sx (x = 2−4) to Li2S.39,40 Notably, the 1.8 V plateau (Li2Sx to Li2S) is observed exclusively in Li||CP cells.38 This arises because CP offers higher three-dimensional electronic conductivity and a larger density of surface-active sites compared to conventional carbon/sulfur (C/S) cathodes, which are burdened with substantial insulating S and Li2S within their electrode structure (Note S5).
[M0091 p5] Despite these structural differences, Li||CP and Li||C/S cells display consistent thermodynamic and kinetic characteristics during LiPS redox conversion.
[M0092 p5] Thus, the LiPS redox thermodynamics and kinetics can be elucidated by correlating the GITT discharge voltage profiles of Li||CP cells with the established Li−S redox pathways.
[M0093 p5] The conversion of LiPS species is correlated with LiPS solvation by comparing the specific capacity delivered on each stage (Figure 3a).
[M0094 p5] The DOL/DME cell delivers 900 mAh g−1 on stage II, in sharp contrast to 500 and 400 mAh g−1 in the DOL/F4DEE and DOL/TTE cells.
[M0095 p5] Nevertheless, all three cells similarly deliver 650 mAh g−1 on stage III.
[M0096 p5] These results unravel that the LiPS chain-shortening process with weak LiPS solvation relies more on short-chain LiPS (liquid−solid) reduction than long-chain LiPS (liquid−liquid) reduction.
[M0097 p5] It is reported that electrolytes with weaker LiPS solvation shift LiPS disproportionation equilibrium toward the formation of shorter-chain LiPS species that deliver lower specific capacity during the reduction on stage II.24,41−43 Notably, the DOL/ F4DEE electrolyte partially compromised this part of capacity, but it protects Li anode from more corrosive long-chain LiPS species.44−46 On the other hand, Li||CP GITT shows similar overpotential across various LiPS solvations (Figure S20a).
[M0098 p5] The overpotential increases only when the liquid−liquid LiPS reduction (intrinsically fast, stage II) transits to liquid−solid LiPS reduction (intrinsically sluggish, stage III), evidenced in Figure 3a.
[M0099 p5] After GITT discharging, Li||CP cells were charged with identical GITT steps to investigate how weak LiPS solvation in the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte influences the Li−S charging process.
[M0100 p5] The charging voltage profile (Figure 3b) exhibits voltage plateaus at 2.25 V (stage IV) and 2.35 V (stage V), which can be attributed to the solid−liquid oxidation from Li2S/Li2S2 to LiPS and liquid−solid oxidation from LiPS to S8.40,47 Again, the similar overpotential across three electrolytes validates the noncorrelation between LiPS solvation and LiPS oxidation kinetics (Figure S20b).
[M0101 p5] Thus, the capacity difference in these electrolytes is mainly attributed to LiPS conversion thermodynamics rather than kinetics.

[M0117 p6] Although DOL/F4DEE delivers lower capacity because of limited liquid−liquid reduction (stage II) and retarded solid−liquid oxidation (on stage IV), it leverages more on liquid−solid reduction (on stage III) and promotes liquid−solid oxidation (on stage V) to offset the capacity.

[M0123 p6] A similar trend is observed during subsequent GITT charging, although with significantly lower overpotentials (Figure S25).

[M0136 p7] These results highlight the critical role of LiPS solubility in governing Li−S redox kinetics: higher LiPS concentration, enabled by greater solubility, suppresses ηcon and thereby accelerates LiPS reduction (Figure 4c).53 Notably, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte with moderate LiPS solubility dissolves sufficient LiPS to sustain favorable sulfur cathode kinetics, as discussed in the following section. ■EXTENDED LI−S CYCLE LIFE USING THE DOL/F4DEE ELECTROLYTE The benefits of the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte on Li−S electrochemical performance were evaluated in Li−S batteries with optimized LiNO3 additive concentration (Figure S28).
[M0137 p7] GITT experiments in Li||C/S cells with practical C/S cathodes further confirmed that the sulfur cathode kinetics is strongly governed by LiPS solubility.
[M0138 p7] Consistent with conventional Li− S mechanism,38 the GITT discharge profile (Figure 5a) exhibits two plateaus: one at 2.3 V (stage I, S8 reduction to soluble LiPS) and the other at 2.1 V (stage II, soluble LiPS reduction to insoluble LiPS and Li2S).
[M0139 p7] Among the tested electrolytes, DOL/DME delivers the highest discharge capacity (Figure S29), reflecting enhanced liquid−liquid LiPS conversion due to strong LiPS solvation.
[M0140 p7] By contrast, higher GITT overpotentials were observed as the LiPS solubility decreased (Figure S30).
[M0141 p7] Notably, DOL/F4DEE outperforms DOL/TTE in LiPS redox kinetics due to its higher LiPS solubility.
[M0142 p7] Furthermore, deconvolution of the GITT overpotential confirmed that concentration polarization dominates the overall redox kinetics (Figure S31).
[M0143 p7] Taken together, these Li||C/S GITT results, consistent with the earlier Li||CP experiments using varied Li2S6 concentrations, underscore the advantage of moderate LiPS solubility in DOL/F4DEE for enhancing sulfur cathode kinetics.
[M0144 p7] Moreover, the benefit of the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte on the Li−S cell long-term cycling is revealed.
[M0145 p7] Figure 5b shows the voltage profiles at 0.05 and 0.1 C (1 C = 1200 mAh g−1).
[M0146 p7] The DOL/F4DEE cell, similar to the DOL/DME one, typically exhibits two well-defined plateaus, indicating sufficient sulfur utilization owing to its moderate LiPS solubility.
[M0147 p7] On the contrary, DOL/TTE with poor LiPS solubility results in a depressed second discharge plateau with reduced specific capacity.
[M0148 p7] The trade-off between cycle life and specific capacity is observed from the long-term cycling at 0.1 C (Figure 5d, Figure S32).
[M0149 p7] Strong Li+ solvation and strong LiPS solvation in DOL/DME trigger poor sulfur reversibility and severe Li anode degradation, resulting in quick capacity fading after only 55 cycles.
[M0150 p7] This corresponds to the decoupled impacts of longchain LiPS corrosion and low CE due to the poor Li SEI.

[M0157 p8] DOL/TTE continuously suffers from poor sulfur active material utilization due to low LiPS solubility, as revealed from its slow capacity activation and overpotential recovery (Figure S33).
[M0158 p8] DOL/F4DEE stabilizes the Li anode with moderate Li+ solvation and weak LiPS solvation, while maintaining sufficient cathode kinetics owing to its moderate LiPS solubility.
[M0159 p8] These attributes contribute to a highly prolonged Li−S cycle life of over 180 cycles.
[M0160 p8] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of these cells after 100 cycles provides further evidence of the benefits of DOL/F4DEE (Figure 5c, Figure S34).
[M0161 p8] DOL/F4DEE and DOL/DME facilitate sulfur redox kinetics owing to LiPS solubility advantages, reflected by a smaller charge transfer resistance (Rct).
[M0162 p8] On the other hand, the smaller SEI resistance (RSEI) in DOL/F4DEE and DOL/TTE than DOL/DME indicates the benefit of weakened Li+ and LiPS solvation on Li anode.
[M0163 p8] The drastic increase of Rbulk in DOL/DME (which may come from electrolyte dry-out9) further proves its poor Li cyclability.
[M0164 p8] Overall, the DOL/F4DEE electrolyte balances sulfur cathode kinetics and lithium anode stability through optimized Li+ and LiPS solubility and solvation, thereby extending the cycle life of both Li−S coin cells and Li−S cells under more demanding cycling conditions (Figures S35 and S36).
[M0165 p8] These results validate the effectiveness of our electrolyte design by decoupling the LiTFSI/LiPS solubility and Li+/LiPS solvation (Figure S37).
[M0166 p8] In summary, we present an electrolyte design strategy that achieves a balance between enhanced lithium anode stability and efficient sulfur redox kinetics.
[M0167 p8] The DOL/F4DEE electrolyte, with moderate Li+ solvation, promotes the formation of a fluorine-rich SEI layer, effectively regulating lithium stripping and plating.
[M0168 p8] Its weak LiPS solvation suppresses the formation of corrosive long-chain polysulfides, contributing to an improved anode stability and enhanced sulfur cathode reversibility.
[M0169 p8] Additionally, the electrolyte’s moderate LiPS solubility minimizes concentration polarization, enabling favorable sulfur redox kinetics.
[M0170 p8] These combined features lead to an extended cycle life for Li−S batteries.
[M0171 p8] By decoupling the solubility and solvation effects of LiTFSI and LiPS, this work advances our understanding of the Li−S battery mechanisms.
[M0172 p8] Furthermore, this demonstrates the potential of partially fluorinated solvents in electrolyte design, offering a promising pathway for the development of next-generation Li−S battery electrolytes. ■ASSOCIATED CONTENT * sı Supporting Information The Supporting Information is available free of charge at https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsenergylett.5c02200.

[S0093 p11] Note S1 The PFG structure of F4DEE determines its high LiTFSI solubility but moderate Li+ solvation LiTFSI solubility increases in solvents that either disperse LiTFSI more effectively or interact with Li+ more strongly.
[S0094 p11] Specifically, solvents with high dielectric constant (ε) promote the dispersion of LiPS due to their stronger polarizability, whereas solvents with high donor number (DN) solvate LiPS strongly. 5, 6 Therefore, solvents with higher ε or DN tend to exhibit higher LiPS solubility.
[S0095 p11] Compared to DME, the extra fluorine atoms in F4DEE show strong electron withdrawing effect, dispersing the electron density on oxygen coordination centers.
[S0096 p11] Therefore, F4DEE shows lower electron donicity, resulting in weaker Li+ solvating power than DME.
[S0097 p11] The electron density on TTE oxygen is even more dispersed because of even higher fluorination degree than F4DEE.
[S0098 p11] Together with the absence of chelating effect from the glyme (- OCH2CH2O-) backbone, the solvating power of TTE is further decreased (Figure S9a).
[S0099 p11] These features determine the moderate Li+ solvating power of F4DEE.
[S0100 p11] Meanwhile, the electron withdrawing effect of fluorine on -CF2H terminal groups increases the local polarity of F4DEE,7 resulting in increased LiTFSI solubility (Figure S9b).
[S0101 p11] In all, the PFG structure endows F4DEE with high electronic polarizability but low electron donicity, resulting in high LiTFSI solubility but moderate Li+ solvation in F4DEE.

[S0112 p13] Therefore, LiTFSI concentration is optimized to be 1.0 M to balance the Li+ concentration and Li+ solvation effects on ionic conductivity.

[S0151 p20] SI-19 Note S3 Li anode and bulk electrolyte resistance show trivial impact on Li anode cyclability Besides the SEI composition, there are other factors such as high bulk electrolyte Li+ conductivity and fast Li|electrolyte interfacial kinetics that influence Li stripping/plating stability.
[S0152 p20] However, their contributions to Li cyclability are comparatively insignificant.
[S0153 p20] (1) Bulk electrolyte conductivity.
[S0154 p20] All electrolytes exhibit Li+ conductivity above 1 mS cm-1 (see Note S2), which is sufficient to support facile Li+ transport.
[S0155 p20] Notably, the DOL/DME electrolyte, despite its superior Li+ conductivity, shows poorer Li anode cyclability than DOL/F4DEE.
[S0156 p20] This indicates that the bulk conductivity is not the primary reason for the observed Li stability differences.
[S0157 p20] (2) Interfacial charge transfer kinetics.
[S0158 p20] We assessed the Li|electrolyte interfacial kinetics using Li||Cu stripping/plating experiments.
[S0159 p20] Figure S17 summarizes the voltage profiles with the DOL/DME, DOL/F4DEE, and DOL/TTE electrolytes, obtained via the Li||Cu Aurbach method.
[S0160 p20] During the ten highlighted stripping/plating cycles (1 mAh cm-2, 0.5 mA cm-2), a lithium reservoir consistently remains on the Cu electrode.
[S0161 p20] Therefore, the observed overpotential is attributed to the Li|electrolyte interfacial processes.
[S0162 p20] All three electrolytes exhibit similarly low overpotentials (less than 20 mV), indicating that the impact of interfacial kinetics is negligible during lithium stripping/plating.
[S0163 p20] Compared to the above effects, the effect of SEI composition on Li anode is significant.
[S0164 p20] LiF is well known to regulate Li deposition because of its electrochemical stability and mechanical strength.
