[M0001 p1] Practical Lithium−Sulfur Batteries Enabled by a Six-Membered Cyclic Urea Solvent Lin Peng,∥Yilu Liu,∥Pengcheng Xue, Chenxi Lei, Yang Xiao, Lishan Lin, Liang Ma,* Luyi Chen,* Yue-Peng Cai, Jian Wang, Lingzhi Zhao,* and Qifeng Zheng* Cite This: ACS Energy Lett. 2025, 10, 4004−4012 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Highly solvating electrolytes hold great prospects for achieving the goal of practically high-energy-density lithium−sulfur batteries, yet they suffer from short cycle life due to their poor compatibility with Li-metal anode (LMA).
[M0002 p1] Herein, a new six-membered cyclic urea, N, N′-dimethylpropyleneurea (DMPU), was rationally proposed as an electrolyte solvent to conquer this longstanding contradiction.
[M0003 p1] The DMPU with high electron donicity preferentially coordinates with Li+ to dominate the inner-solvation shell, which not only initiates and stabilizes the S3 •−radical to facilitate S redox kinetics and 3D Li2S deposition but also suppresses its contact reaction with LMA kinetically.
[M0004 p1] More encouragingly, the DMPU molecule with a sixmembered ring bears small ring strain, thus weakening their reactivity with LMA thermodynamically.
[M0005 p1] Consequently, the DMPU-based electrolyte bestows an extremely high area capacity of 8.4 mAh cm−2 for a practical lithium−sulfur cell under a high S mass loading of 8.2 mg cm−2 and ultralow E/S ratio of 3.8 μL mgsulfur −1.
[M0006 p1] T he lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries represent one of the most attractive rechargeable energy storage systems because of their high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1), low cost, and environmental benignity.1,2 In the past two decades, tremendous efforts have been devoted to propelling the developments of Li−S batteries, including mechanism investigation,3−6 cathode construction,7−10 separator functionalization,11,12 and electrolyte engineering.13−15 Although the reversible capacity and stable cycling performance have been enhanced impressively, there is still a huge gap between the laboratory prototype and the industrial application of Li−S batteries.16−18 To promote the practical application of Li−S batteries, the following key issues should be resolved: (1) S cathode undergoes a unique multistep, multielectron, multispecies, and multiphase (abbreviated as “4M”) conversion reaction, in which solid S is first reduced to soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and finally transformed to solid and insulative Li2S, resulting in sluggish redox reaction kinetics and low utilization of S.19 (2) Soluble LiPSs intermediates can shuttle across the separator and induce parasitical reactions with Li-metal anode (LMA), leading to rapid depletion of limited LMA.20,21 (3) The flooding electrolytes with high electrolyte-tosulfur ratios (E/S ratios) were normally adopted to achieve high capacities with high utilization of S, while it would dramatically impair the energy density of the whole battery device.22,23 The electrolyte of Li−S batteries determines the solvation structure of LiPSs, which plays a vital role in promoting the reversible conversion of LiPSs.
[M0007 p1] Usually, the solvation structure of LiPSs are composed of polysulfide anions and Li+ through the ion−ion interaction,24 which are further surrounded by the solvents via ion−dipole interactions that results in a solvent shell.25 For instance, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3- dioxolane (DOL), which are the representative ether-based solvents with moderately solvating ability for LiPSs, exhibit low solubility for LiPSs.
[M0008 p1] This low solubility of LiPSs would intensify the precipitation of short-chain LiPSs, especially at low E/S ratios (also known as lean electrolyte conditions), resulting in the premature formation of Li2S passivation layer upon discharge, which inevitably results in low practical capacity with low S utilization.26 Furthermore, the parasitic reactions between the shuttled LiPSs and LMA would shorten the cycle life of Li−S batteries.
[M0009 p1] The employment of sparingly solvating electrolytes can shift the S conversion pathway from a dissolution−precipitation reaction to a quasi-solid−solid reaction, which suppresses the solvation and shuttle of LiPSs, effectively extending the cycle life of Li−S batteries even under lean electrolyte conditions.27 Nevertheless, it inevitably Received: March 28, 2025 Revised: June 9, 2025 Accepted: July 24, 2025 Published: July 28, 2025 Letter http://pubs.acs.org/journal/aelccp © 2025 American Chemical Society 4004 https://doi.org/10.1021/acsenergylett.5c00974 ACS Energy Lett. 2025, 10, 4004−4012 Downloaded from pubs.​acs.​org/​aelccp/​article-pdf/​10/​8/​4004/​42282075/​nz5c00974.​pdf by TSINGHUA UNIV user on 21 September 2026
[M0010 p2] sacrifices the S cathode redox kinetics, resulting in large polarization, inferior rate capability, and low capacity.28,29 To promote the S cathode redox kinetics under lean electrolyte conditions, recently, highly solvating electrolytes based on solvents with high Gutmann donor number (DN) were proposed, such as N, N, N′, N′-tetramethylurea (TMU),30 N, N′-dimethylethyleneurea (DMEU),31 dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO),32 and 3-fluoropyridine (3-FPN),22 which possesses strong electron-donating ability to regulate the solvation structure of LiPSs as well as promotes the solubility of LiPSs (Table S1).
[M0011 p2] Furthermore, high DN solvents can provide an additional disproportionation pathway of LiPSs by triggering the formation of trisulfur radical anion (S3 •−) intermediate, leading to the radical-assisted conversion pathway for LiPSs with rapid conversion kinetics.33 Moreover, in contrast to the twodimensional (2D) deposition morphology of Li2S in moderately/sparingly solvating electrolytes, the highly solvating electrolyte bestows the Li2S with a three-dimensional (3D) deposition morphology, which offers more assessable surface area for the reversible conversion between LiPSs and Li2S, leading to high capacity with high S utilization.
[M0012 p2] Unfortunately, high DN solvents with high electron donicity significantly increases the reactivity toward LMAs, which quickly consumes the limited Li source and hence shortens the cycle life.34 Therefore, promoting the S cathode redox kinetics while improving LMA compatibility simultaneously is essential to achieving high-capacity and long-cycling Li−S batteries, which is unattainable for current electrolyte design, especially under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0013 p2] Herein, a six-membered cyclic urea, N, N′-dimethylpropyleneurea (DMPU), was first incorporated as a cosolvent for Li−S battery electrolyte to address the longstanding contradiction between S cathode redox kinetics and LMA compatibility.
[M0014 p2] As illustrated in Figure 1 and Table S2, the DMPU has a high DN of 34.0 kcal mol−1 and a dipole moment (μ) of 4.23 D, which is much higher than that of conventional ether solvents (DME and DOL) and the five-membered cyclic urea (N, N′-dimethylethyleneurea, DMEU).
[M0015 p2] Therefore, the DMPU is expected to strongly coordinate with Li+ to form the inner-solvation shell, which not only promises high dissolution of LiPSs but also initiates S3 •−− based conversion pathway with 3D Li2S deposition morphology, realizing fast conversion kinetics with high S utilization.
[M0016 p2] Furthermore, the strong confinement of DMPU in the innersolvation shell minimizes contact of DMPU with LMA and thus suppresses their parasitic reactions at the interface.
[M0017 p2] More encouragingly, the DMPU is featured with a six-membered ring, which exhibits much less ring strain compared to the fivemembered counterpart (i.e., DMEU), showcasing much improved thermodynamic stability toward LMAs.
[M0018 p2] As a result, using a DMPU-based electrolyte (denoted as E-PDD), a Li−S coin cell achieves an extremely high capacity of 1403 mAh g−1 (corresponding to a high S utilization of 84%) and is able to maintain 77% capacity after 135 cycles at 0.1 C.
[M0019 p2] Moreover, the superiority of E-PDD is also demonstrated in Li−S pouch cells.
[M0020 p2] The conventional ether-based electrolytes, represented by 1.0 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in DOL/DME (denoted as DD), must fulfill the flooding electrolyte condition (E/S > 20 μL mg−1) to realize high capacity (>1200 mAh g−1), which would severely diminish the practical energy density of Li−S batteries.
[M0021 p2] This was ascribed to the weak electron donicity and low dipole moment (μ) of DOL (DN: 17.5 kcal mol−1, μ: 1.19 D) and DME (DN: 20.0 kcal mol−1, μ: 1.72 D), which exhibits moderately solvating ability with LiPSs, resulting in low solubility of LiPSs and sluggish S redox kinetics with unfavorable 2D Li2S deposition (Figures 1a and S1).

[M0030 p3] DMEU, a cyclic solvent with a high DN of 29.0 kcal mol−1 and a high μ of 4.09 D, can significantly enhance the solvation of LiPSs and trigger the S3 •−-based conversion pathway with 3D Li2S deposition morphology (Figure 1b).
[M0031 p3] Although the DMEU molecule with a cyclic structure possesses better Li compatibility compared to that of noncyclic high DN solvents, its high donicity nature still makes it tend to react with LMA, and the battery life was limited to a few tens of cycles.31 To address this dilemma, we revisit the solvation structure of highly solvating electrolyte and molecule structure of DMEU.
[M0032 p3] The desirable high DN solvents should fulfill the following two requirements simultaneously: 1) the solvent should strongly coordinate with Li+ and be confined within the inner-solvation shell, thereby minimizing its contact with LMAs; and 2) the solvent itself should possess better intrinsically thermodynamic stability toward LMAs.
[M0033 p3] Toward this end, the DMPU with a sixmembered cyclic ring was proposed.
[M0034 p3] Compared with the previously reported five-membered cyclic DMEU, the DMPU exhibits higher dipole moment (μ: 4.23 D) and higher DN (34.0 kcal mol−1) (Figure S2 and Table S2), which can further enhance the interaction of Li+−solvent to achieve DMPUconfined inner solvation shell, holding great prospects to favor the conversion kinetics and minimize free DMPU in the electrolyte to alleviate parasitic reactions with LMA.
[M0035 p3] Furthermore, based on the bond angles of DMEU and DMPU in Figure S2 and Table S3, it can be found that the DMPU molecule exhibits smaller ring strain than the DMEU molecule, which elevates the intrinsically thermodynamic stability of the DMPU molecule,35 weakening the reactivity of free DMPU solvent with LMA.
[M0036 p3] The electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution of solvents36 was calculated in Figure 2a, which shows that O atom of DMEU and DMPU displays stronger lowest negative ESP (i.e., ESPmin) than those of DOL and DME, indicating their strong tendency to coordinate with Li+.
[M0037 p3] It is worth noting that the highest positive ESP (i.e., ESPmax) of DMPU is higher than that of DMEU, indicating a greater degree of charge separation efficiency, which leads to higher polarity and dipole moment (Figure S2 and Table S2).
[M0038 p3] Then, the binding energies (ΔE) of Li+−solvent were calculated in Figure 2b.
[M0039 p3] Due to the bidentate coordination of DME, ΔEDME shows the most negative value of −2.74 eV.
[M0040 p3] For those monodentate coordination solvent, ΔEDMPU, ΔEDMEU, and ΔEDOL are of −2.65, −2.54, and −1.74 eV, respectively, indicating that the coordination of Li+−DMPU is the strongest.
[M0041 p3] In addition, the Li−O bond length of Li+−DMPU is the smallest (i.e., 1.69 Å), further validating their strongest interaction.
[M0042 p3] It has been demonstrated that solvents with high DN will facilitate the dissociation and disproportionation reaction of LiPSs.37 As shown in Figures 2c and S3a-b, the Li2S6 is highly soluble in both DMEU/DOL/DME (2:4:4 by vol., denoted as EDD) and DMPU/DOL/DME (2:4:4 by vol., denoted as PDD), of which the 0.5 mM and 20 mM solutions show distinct light-blue and navy-blue color, respectively, indicating the existence of S3 •−species from the additional disproportionation reaction pathway of LiPSs, which is consistent with other reported high DN solvents.31 In sharp contrast, the 20 mM LiPSs solution in DD appears to be turbid with dark-yellow color, indicating poor solubility of LiPSs with nondissociated Sx 2−anions (Figures 2c and S3c).

[M0055 p4] The solvation structure of the electrolyte was first investigated by Raman spectroscopy.
[M0056 p4] As shown in Figure 2e, the vibration peak at 1630 cm−1 is assigned to the CO group of pure DMPU solvent, which was up-shifted to 1651 cm−1 for 1 M LiTFSI in DMPU/DOL/DME(denoted as LiTFSI-PDD).40 In addition, the N−C−N stretching vibrations peaks also display an upshift from 707.1 cm−1 in pure DMPU to 716.0 cm−1 in LiTFSI-PDD (Figure S5), indicating the strong interaction of Li+−DMPU.
[M0057 p4] Moreover, the C−O stretching peak of DME at 848.4 cm−1 barely shifts, manifesting that the majority of DME solvent exists in the free state.
[M0058 p4] This demonstrates that the DMPU is mainly coordinated with Li+ and preferentially occupied the inner-solvation shell of Li+, leading to a DMPUdominated inner-solvation shell of LiPSs.
[M0059 p4] As shown in Figures S6a-b, both CO vibration peak of DMEU and C−O stretching peak of DME is up-shifted in 1 M LiTFSI in DMEU/DOL/DME (denoted as LiTFSI-EDD), suggesting that both DMEU and DME are coordinated with Li+ in the inner-solvation shell, denoted as the DMEU/DME-dominated inner-solvation shell of LiPSs.
[M0060 p4] In comparison, the vibration peaks of both DOL and DME upshift obviously in 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME, indicating strong coordination of DOL and DME with Li+ (Figure S7), which leads to the DME/DOL-dominated inner-solvation shell of LiPSs.
[M0061 p4] Furthermore, the solvation structure of LiPSs (taking Li2S6 as a sample) was analyzed by 7Li and 17O nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra.
[M0062 p4] Compared with the 0.1 M Li2S6 in EDD (denoted as Li2S6-EDD), the upfield shift of 0.1 M Li2S6 in PDD (denoted as Li2S6−PDD) is remarkable (Figure 2f) with 0.2 M LiCl in DMSO-d6.
[M0063 p4] This result confirms the stronger coordination of Li+ with DMPU than that with DMEU, meaning that DMPU is preferentially occupied in the inner-solvation shell of Li2S6.
[M0064 p4] As shown in the 17O spectra in Figure 2g, compared to that with DMEU, a larger upfield shift is found for DMPU after adding Li2S6, also proving the stronger interaction of Li+− DMPU than that of Li+−DMEU.
[M0065 p4] While the chemical shift of DOL and DME in both Li2S6-EDD and Li2S6−PDD solution shows the opposite trend of downfield shift, demonstrating the deshielding effect of DOL and DME (Figures S8−S9).
[M0066 p4] Specifically, a larger downfield shift of DOL/DME is found for Li2S6−PDD, indicating that less DOL/DME solvent is participated in the inner-solvation shell of Li2S6−PDD compared to that of Li2S6-EDD.
[M0067 p4] More importantly, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was also performed to unveil the solvation structure of LiPSs from the molecular level (Figures 3).
[M0068 p4] To simulate the actual solution environment in the Li−S batteries, 0.1 M Li2S6 was added to the electrolyte solutions during MD study, namely 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0 M LiTFSI in PDD, EDD, and DD, respectively.
[M0069 p4] Taking S6 2−as the center model, the radial distribution function (RDF) of Li+ and solvents was calculated to quantify the local environment around S6 2−in the electrolytes.
[M0070 p4] For 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0 M LiTFSI in PDD solution, the S6 2−strongly interacts with Li+ and DMPU within 4.2 Å, validating the DMPU-dominated inner-solvation shell of Li2S6 (Figures 3a and 3d), where the DMPU solvent with high electron donicity is expected to promote the conversion kinetics of LiPSs.41 In sharp contrast, for 0.1 M Li2S6 + 1.0 M LiTFSI in EDD solution, the S6 2−tends to interact with both DMEU and DME, leading to a DMEU/DME-dominated innersolvation shell with limited conversion kinetics improvement (Figures 3b and 3e).
[M0071 p4] Since only part of the DMEU solvent is involved in the solvation shell, there are some parts of DMEU molecules that inevitably exist in a free state in the solution, which leads to the parasitic reactions with Li-metal.

[M0078 p5] The solvents with high DN could regulate the solvation structure of LiPSs and the deposition morphology of Li2S to promote the S redox kinetics with high S utilization.
[M0079 p5] Unfortunately, they generally react severely with LMAs and thus shorten the cycling life of Li−S batteries.
[M0080 p5] Therefore, the electrochemical compatibility of Li-metal was investigated in the Li||Li symmetric cells.
[M0081 p5] As shown in Figures 4a and S10, at a current density of 1 mA cm−1 with a capacity of 1 mAh cm−1, using different electrolytes of 1 M LiTFSI + 0.4 M LiNO3 in PDD, EDD, and DD (denoted as E-PDD, E-EDD, and E-DD, respectively), Li||Li cells exhibit a smooth and low overpotential during the initial 800 h.
[M0082 p5] Thereafter, the overpotential sharply increases for the cell using E-EDD, which was ascribed to the accumulation of thick byproducts and dead Li resulting from the continuous parasitic reaction between the DMEU solvent and LMA.
[M0083 p5] While the cycling life was extended to about 1000 h for the cell using E-DD, owing to the absence of corrosive DMEU solvent (Figure S10a).
[M0084 p5] Remarkably, the cell using E-PDD exhibits extremely stable cycling for over 1400 h with negligible voltage polarization increase.
[M0085 p5] Furthermore, the morphology of the Li-metal electrode recovered from the Li||Li cell after 400 h was observed by SEM.
[M0086 p5] The Li-metal cycled in E-EDD and E-DD shows obvious cracks with porous mossy-like morphology (Figures 4b and S10b), whereas that in E-PDD delivers smooth and uniform morphology (Figure 4c), indicating superior Limetal compatibility possessed by the DMPU solvent.
[M0087 p5] Moreover, the Tafel plot shows that the exchange current density of E-PDD (0.52 mA cm−2) is much higher than that of E-EDD (0.42 mA cm−2) and E-DD (0.12 mA cm−2) electrolyte (Figure S11a), benefiting from rapid Li+ transport kinetics in E-PDD (Figure S11b), which can eliminate the local aggregation of Li+ and suppress the growth of Li dendrites.45,46 To understand the origin of better Li-metal compatibility in the DMPU-based highly solvating electrolyte, the Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG) of DMPU and DMEU reacting with Limetal were calculated through density functional theory (DFT).
[M0088 p5] The underlying decomposition pathways of DMPU or DMEU with Li-metal are proposed in Figure S12, where the N center of the cyclic urea is more likely to undergo attack by Li, leading to the formation of oxynitride lithium and alkyl lithium.
[M0089 p5] As shown in Figures 4d and 4e, the ΔGDMPU (−3.1 eV) is lower than that of ΔGDMEU (−3.7 eV), indicating that the DMPU solvent exhibits lower reactivity toward Li-metal compared to that of DMEU.
[M0090 p5] This validates that the six-membered cyclic DMPU molecule exhibits smaller ring strain than DMEU molecule, which improves its intrinsic stability thermodynamically, thereby weakening the reactivity of DMPU solvent with LMA.
[M0091 p5] In addition, as has been demonstrated in the Raman, MD, and NMR studies, most of the DMPU solvent was strongly coordinated with Li+ to form DMPU-dominated inner-solvation shell, which minimizes the free DMPU in the electrolyte to alleviate parasitic reactions with LMA kinetically.
[M0092 p5] First, the systematical evaluation of Li2S deposition using various electrolytes was shown in Figures S13−22 and Table S4, which demonstrates that the DMPU-dominated inner solvation shell from E-PDD electrolyte can remarkably induce 3D Li2S deposition.
[M0093 p5] Whereafter, Li||Li2S6 coin cells were tested to verify the electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries using E-PDD electrolyte.
[M0094 p5] As shown in Figures 5a-b, the cell using E-PDD exhibits an extremely high initial discharge capacity of 1403 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, corresponding to a high S utilization of 83.8%, which is much higher than that using E-EDD (1164 mAh g−1) and E-DD (964 mAh g−1).
[M0095 p5] Furthermore, the cell with E-PDD is still able to retain 1079 mAh g−1 (77% of the capacity retention) after 135 cycles with low voltage polarization (Figure S23), whereas both cells using E-EDD and E-DD fail within 130 cycles due to a serious parasitic reaction between the solvent/LiPSs and LMAs.
[M0096 p5] Specifically, the capacity at the low plateau contributes three-quarters of the theoretical capacity.
[M0097 p5] As shown in the voltage profiles in Figure 5b, the discharge capacity difference among these three electrolytes was mainly attributed to their distinct capacity difference at the low plateau, which are 937 mAh g−1 (E-PDD), 785 mAh g−1 (E-EDD), and 577 mAh g−1 (E-DD), respectively.
[M0098 p5] This could be attributed to the high solubility of LiPSs and formation of an S3 •− intermediate in the highly solvating electrolyte, which induces the 3D electrodeposition of Li2S to access more capacity.
[M0099 p5] Moreover, as shown in Figures S24−25, both the shuttle constant (ks) and shuttle current measurements further confirmed that the DMPU-dominated inner-solvation shell of LiPSs in the E-PDD electrolyte availably restricts the casual diffusion of LiPSs to LMA and enables effective S-based redox conversion.

[M0135 p7] In summary, a new high donor six-membered cyclic urea, N, N′-dimethylpropyleneurea (DMPU), was proposed as an electrolyte solvent to overcome the long-standing contradiction between S redox kinetics and Li-metal compatibility in Li−S batteries.
[M0136 p7] The theoretical and experimental studies have demonstrated that the DMPU with high electron donicity strongly coordinates with Li+ to form the DMPU-dominated inner-solvation shell, which effectively regulates the solvation of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) to initiate a S3 •−radical-assisted conversion pathway and 3D deposition behavior of Li2S, facilitating the S redox kinetics and utilization under leanelectrolyte conditions.
[M0137 p7] Furthermore, in addition to the strong confinement of DMPU in the inner-solvation shell that minimizes their contact with LMA to suppress their parasitic reactions kinetically, the DMPU solvent featured with sixmembered ring bears small ring strain to weaken their reactivity with LMA thermodynamically, thus enabling highly reversible Li plating/stripping over 1400 h.
[M0138 p7] Consequently, this DMPU-based electrolyte enables the Li−S battery to achieve an extremely high capacity of 1403 mAh g−1 (corresponding to a high S utilization of 84%) and maintain a 77% capacity after 135 cycles.
[M0139 p7] More significantly, under practical conditions of high S mass loading of 8.2 mg cm−2 and extremely lean-electrolyte (E/S = 3.8 μL mgsulfur −1), the cell with DMPU-based electrolyte achieves a remarkably high area capacity of 8.4 mAh cm−2.
[M0140 p7] In addition, a single layer Li−S pouch cell with an energy density of 238 Wh kg−1 was also demonstrated under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0141 p7] Therefore, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of tuning the ring structure of the solvent in improving the S redox kinetics and Li-metal compatibility simultaneously, which represents a significant advancement in developing functional electrolytes for practical Li−S batteries with a high capacity and long cycle life. ■ASSOCIATED CONTENT * sı Supporting Information The Supporting Information is available free of charge at https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsenergylett.5c00974.

[S0167 p31] The electrodeposition mechanism of Li2S in different electrolytes was elucidated by fitting the I−t curves to the four classical models,25 including Bewick-Fleischman-Thirsk (named as BFT) models of (i) two-dimension progressive (2DP) nucleation and (ii) 2D instantaneous (2DI) nucleation, and Scharifker-Hills (named as SH) model of (iii) three-dimension progressive (3DP) nucleation or (iv) 3D instantaneous (3DI) nucleation.26-27 In contrast to the nucleation process of Li2S that needs to overcome the surface energy barrier regardless of the electrolyte, the growth process of Li2S is closely related to both the surface diffusion and the concentration of LiPSs in the electrolyte.
