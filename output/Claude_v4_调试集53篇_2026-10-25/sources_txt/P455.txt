[M0002 p1] Soc. 2024, 146, 23182−23193 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Achieving high energy densities for lithium−sulfur batteries remain elusive.
[M0003 p1] Largely limited by the volume of electrolyte used, lean electrolyte conditions (electrolyte/sulfur mass ratio <3) present enormous challenges that have led to very poor specific capacity and rate performance.
[M0004 p1] Previous studies have identified that the high concentration of polysulfide is responsible for the poor discharge voltage.
[M0005 p1] However, there still lacks sufficient understanding of the processes occurring at lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0006 p1] In this work we uncovered a polysulfide concentration regulating mechanism that operates through the precipitation and redissolution of solid sulfurbased species (reversibly precipitated sulfur species, RPSS).
[M0007 p1] This occurs in a concerted manner in a global sense through the cathode and can be measured using impedance spectroscopy.
[M0008 p1] It was found that the more RPSS that is formed, the higher the energy density of discharge.
[M0009 p1] We propose that high concentration of polysulfide tends to supersaturate, which impeded the formation of RPSS.
[M0010 p1] Employing an electrolyte with low Li ion concentration along with using poorly dissociating lithium salts allowed for more RPSS formation and ultimately enabled discharge at >2.0 V at 0.05 C, at E/S = 2.5, and at room temperature without the use of an engineered cathode. ■INTRODUCTION The lithium−sulfur battery is an attractive next-generation battery technology due to the natural abundancy of sulfur and high theoretical energy density.1−4 However, the practically achievable energy density (volumetric and gravimetric) is highly dependent on the volume/mass of electrolyte used.5,6 At relevant electrolyte conditions (electrolyte/sulfur mass ratio, E/S < 3),7 the cell experiences enormous overpotential that results in extremely poor rate performances (often well below 0.05 C).8,9 An increasingly popular and reasonably robust class of strategies centers around moving away from the traditional solid−liquid−solid conversion of a lithium−sulfur battery to a solid−solid conversion.
[M0011 p1] This is achieved by either employing sparingly soluble electrolytes10,11 and/or covalently-bonded sulfur polymer.12−14 With almost every aspect of this class of strategies appealing, its most undermining property is the intrinsically lower operating potentials due to a fundamental change in the reaction pathways.15 A study by Eroglu et al. presented a rapid decline in projected pack-level energy density when the cell-level voltage is <2.0 V.16−18 Since the operating voltage of lithium−sulfur batteries hovers around 2.0−2.2 V, the pack level energy density of the lithium−sulfur battery is particularly sensitive to any decrease/increase of cell voltage.
[M0012 p1] A low cell voltage can drastically increase the number of cells required to reach often pre-established device-level operating voltages.
[M0013 p1] This would effectively increase the mass of “deadweight” components such as current collectors, electrolyte, etc., that do not scale well even with a high single-cell-level energy density.
[M0014 p1] While the solid−solid-based systems can reach the necessary lean electrolyte conditions, they are typically operated at a much lower average cell voltage i.e., ∼1.5 V.
[M0015 p1] As part of a long list of requirements for commercial success, maintaining a >2.0 V average discharge voltage while also operating at an appreciable current density, room temperature, high sulfur loading, and all under lean electrolyte conditions (≤3.0 μL mgs) remains to be a monumental task.
[M0016 p1] Many alternative strategies have been explored to achieve a low E/S ratio while still maintaining a solid−liquid−solid conversion mechanism.
[M0017 p1] These include the use of macroporous carbon that assists in temporarily relieving the overpotential by allowing more local electrolyte access and slowing the onset of large overpotential;19,20 the use of polysulfide adsorptive binders can be useful in taking polysulfide out of the bulk solution to lower the overall impedance but still lacks significant progress.21 Increasing the solubility of polysulfide has also been a very popular direction of study.

[M0022 p2] However, these solvents tend to have a high viscosity and are also incompatible with Li metal.
[M0023 p2] The reduction in the concentration of Li ion salt has also been pursued, where the proportion of free solvents are increased.25 It was suggested that this increases the solubility of polysulfide by the common-ion effect, which, in turn, reduces the overpotentials.
[M0024 p2] Improvements in discharge capacity and voltage were observed but only at a relatively high E/S ratio of 5.
[M0025 p2] Very lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ratio of ≤2.5 μL mgs) were also achieved with various techniques but were either discharged at very low current densities (<0.0025 C with 1 C = 1675 mA g−1),14,26 centered around highly airsensitive materials,27 or required elevated temperature (55 °C)28 to function at >2.0 V.
[M0026 p2] The large overpotential often observed at the LEC usually manifests itself as a large dip at the onset of the second discharge plateau.
[M0027 p2] This has been termed the Li2S nucleation overpotential.29 A particularly troublesome property of this dip is its tendency to chronically persists for the remainder of the second discharge plateau, i.e., cell voltage never recovers.
[M0028 p2] This leads to average cell voltages well below 2.0 V.
[M0029 p2] The cause for the overpotential can be reliably traced to increases in the charge transfer resistance.
[M0030 p2] However, this is still a rather ambiguous source of overpotential that can be caused by many cathode processes.
[M0031 p2] These include poor polysulfide mass transfer limitations (from high viscosity),30 polysulfide clustering/gelation,31,32 passivation of conductive surfaces,19 or a combination thereof.
[M0032 p2] Deconvolution of these sources of high overpotential is a difficult task that might also lead to varying conclusions depending on the specific LSB cell system studied.
[M0033 p2] Accordingly, it is difficult to devise effective strategies for LSB operation under LEC.
[M0034 p2] It might be instructive to study the underlying processes that are occurring in configurations that have been already shown to allow for relatively higher performing LEC-LSB.
[M0035 p2] There exist clear configurations19 where cell at LEC experienced large overpotential at the onset of the second discharge plateau but can reliably recover its potential.
[M0036 p2] Accordingly, there must exist some type of mechanism at the cathode that can help the cell recover from the voltage dip and avoid a chronically depressed discharge.
[M0037 p2] In this work, we uncover a polysulfide concentration regulating mechanism that exists under lean electrolyte conditions in a traditional solid−liquid−solid conversion type LSB by employing operando and relaxation-based electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis.
[M0038 p2] We discovered a strong relationship between the interfacial resistance (high frequency semicircle, centered at ∼103−105 Hz) at a specific depth of discharge (DOD) and the discharge material level energy density.
[M0039 p2] Cells with more than one interfacial resistance increasing event as a function of DOD and larger magnitude of such an event were found to deliver higher discharge energy density.
[M0040 p2] We attributed this to sulfur-based species that are reversibly deposited as deduced from a relaxation-based EIS study where changes in the phase angle were tracked.
[M0041 p2] The formation and importance of reversibly precipitated sulfur species (RPSS) are discussed.
[M0042 p2] Promotion of RPSS formation led to significant improvements in cell potential and capacity.
[M0043 p2] Using a highly Li ion deficient electrolyte with poorly dissociating anion and a standard “nonengineered” sulfur electrode based on commercially available micrometer-sized sulfur particles at 3.5 mg cm−2 (60% sulfur content), we were able to achieve a high discharge specific capacity (∼850 mAh g−1) with an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL mg−1 at a rate of 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA g−1), at room temperature, and with a second plateau well above 2.0 V. ■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION The poor performance of lean electrolyte conditions (LECs) during a single discharge can be succinctly described by two important deficiencies associated with the discharge process: (1) the existence of a large overpotential/nucleation “dip” and (2) a chronically depressed discharge potential following the dip.
[M0044 p2] The potential of the dip often determines whether the cell can proceed into the second discharge plateau (depending on the cutoff voltage).
[M0045 p2] In the case where the cell can sustain a second discharge plateau, the depressed discharge potential determines the operating voltage and energy density of the cell.

[M0053 p3] The large potential dip that occurs before the onset of the second discharge voltage plateau is where the polysulfide concentration is expected to be at its highest.
[M0054 p3] Conventional understanding dictates that when the polysulfide concentration increases, the viscosity and corresponding electrolyte resistance increase, slowed polysulfide/Li+ transport from bulk to surfaces,30 or a clustering/gelation effect of high concentration polysulfide,31−35 reducing the reaction kinetics.
[M0055 p3] Interestingly, while a some studies have reported a near full recovery of discharge potential after the initial potential dip,19 most studies found that the second discharge voltage plateau in continuously depressed/severely sloped.
[M0056 p3] This is clearly exemplified in Figure 1a, where a conventional 1 M lithium bis- (trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.25 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) in a 1:1 by volume blend of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) was used as electrolyte.
[M0057 p3] A clear voltage depression is present throughout the course of the second discharge plateau.
[M0058 p3] In principle, as the cell discharges, the polysulfide mass should be converted to insoluble species (Li2S/Li2S2), and thus, the global concentration of polysulfide (averaged across the entire electrolyte volume) should decrease.
[M0059 p3] Shown in Figure 1b, an “effective E/S ratio” was estimated as the reciprocal of the expected amount of sulfur mass remaining in solution and plotted against the depth of discharge.
[M0060 p3] It was assumed that all sulfur species were consumed to form either insoluble Li2S or soluble Li2S2.
[M0061 p3] As one discharges past the second plateau, the polysulfide concentration decreases and the “effective E/S ratio” increases.
[M0062 p3] If Li2S2 is the main discharge product, at ∼450 mAh g−1, the cell is expected to have a global polysulfide concentration that is similar to the highest solvated sulfur concentration experienced in an “equivalent cell” with an injected E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1.
[M0063 p3] If Li2S is considered as the main product, the cell reaches an effective E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1 at ∼700 mAh g−1.
[M0064 p3] Based on literature reports, a cell with an E/S ratio of ∼5 μL mg−1 can be expected to run relatively unhindered at 0.1 C even when using a relatively routine carbon nanotube/sulfur composite.36 Accordingly, a decrease in sulfur content in the electrolyte from discharging should impart some relief to any high polysulfide-concentrationderived overpotentials experienced by the cell.
[M0065 p3] Yet, this is often not observed with the chronic voltage depression persisting well into the second plateau and often never recovering at all.
[M0066 p3] Therefore, it appears that it is not the high concentration of polysulfide that is directly responsible for the chronically depressed second plateau under LEC, but some long-lasting negative effect that is caused by the mere temporary existence of a high polysulfide concentration.
[M0067 p3] This chronic lack of recovery in overpotential can be clearly demonstrated from operando electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis over the course of discharge.
[M0068 p3] A series of operando EIS measurements were performed on cells using different Li salts and at varying concentrations.
[M0069 p3] A highly depressed discharge profile is observed during operando EIS (Figure 2a) employing an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL mgs −1 (using a standard electrolyte composition: 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M LiNO3 in DOL/DME 1:1 v/v) and a sulfur loading of 3.5 mgs cm−2.
[M0070 p3] Similar to the profile in Figure 1a, the cell was unable to recover the overpotential experienced during the dip, and in fact, the impedance continuously increased.
[M0071 p3] The fitted impedance values are shown in Figure 2b as a function of the discharge time.
[M0072 p3] Re and Rint peaked at the onset of the second discharge plateau, which is consistent with literature.37 Like the flooded conditions, the peak in Re likely is a gauge of the viscosity of the electrolyte.
[M0073 p3] Further confirming previous reports,21,31 Rct is by far the largest impedance contribution in the cell under LEC.

[M0084 p4] In the case of low Li ion composition, Re peaked at ∼100 ohm, nearly triple that of the high concentration electrolyte.
[M0085 p4] The cause of this surprising result is unclear, but we suspect that the use of a lower Li ion concentration increased the concentration of dissolved polysulfide, which might have caused an increase in the viscosity.
[M0086 p4] Rint followed a similar trend for both high and low Li ion concentrations before the second discharge plateau.
[M0087 p4] However, a large increase in Rint was observed for the low Li ion electrolyte over the course of the second plateau, which we will discuss later.
[M0088 p4] In terms of magnitude, Rct represented the largest difference between the low (<500 ohm) and high Li ion (>2000 ohm) compositions.
[M0089 p4] Interestingly, the value of Rct for both electrolyte compositions reached nearly the same value when Re is at its maximum (commonly termed the high concentration of polysulfide in the system37).
[M0090 p4] Afterward, the Rct increased rapidly for the high Li ion composition while the Rct for the low Li ion composition only increased moderately and actually decreased after ∼4 h of operando EIS before reaching a plateau.
[M0091 p4] Clearly the low Li ion composition is an example of an electrolyte system that can recover from the large overpotential.
[M0092 p4] Both high and low Li ion concentration cells were able to enter into the second discharge plateau with a voltage of 1.98−1.97 V.
[M0093 p4] However, the higher Li ion concentration cell continued to drop in voltage, while the low Li ion concentration cell was able to maintain a stable voltage.
[M0094 p4] The ability of the low Li ion concentration cell to avoid a continued voltage drop is an indication that there must exist some mechanism for the cell to manage and regulate the high concentration of polysulfide.
[M0095 p4] To investigate such a regulation mechanism, we look toward understanding the surface condition of the cell at various depths of discharge (DOD) by observing the relaxation processes.
[M0096 p4] As the viscosity and polysulfide concentration are expected to be very high under LEC, we suspect that much of the diffusional and dissolutionbased relaxation processes (that can be used to infer the surface conditions of the cathode) occur over long time scales and even though the cell appears to have to have reached equilibrium.
[M0097 p4] To uncover the relaxation processes occurring at slower time scales, cells discharged to four different DOD were rested at OCV while their impedance was sampled intermittently (Kramers−Kronig analysis was employed to confirm that the EIS spectra were causal, Figure S1).
[M0098 p4] Four different DODs were selected and defined as follows: DOD-1: discharged to 8 h, Figures 3a−c; DOD-2: discharged to 9 h, Figures 3d−f; DOD-3: discharged to 11 h, Figures 3g−i; DOD-4: discharged to 13 h,Figures 3j−l.
[M0099 p4] Confirming our suspicions, the impedance elements changed significantly in magnitude even when rested at OCV.
[M0100 p4] As the impedance metrics tended to decrease (beside Rint), it cannot be due to the Li metal corrosion which tends to increase linearly with t0.5.38,39 Therefore, the relaxations are likely cathode-based processes.
[M0101 p4] Moreover, the relaxation trends change dramatically from DOD-1 to DOD-2 to DOD-3 with DOD-4 being relatively like DOD-3.
[M0102 p4] This means that conditions (surfaces, electrolyte composition) of the cell prior to rest vary significantly as a function of DOD.
[M0103 p4] Generally, across all DODs, Rint tended to decrease monotonically over time while Re tended to increase monotonically.
[M0104 p4] The most interesting phenomenon lies in Rct, which exhibited a rather surprising nonmonotonic trend for the cell rested from DOD- 1.
[M0105 p4] Right after the current was halted, Rct increased for a few hours, followed by a sharp decrease.
[M0106 p4] If discharge was halted at DOD-2, Rct initially held constant for a period of ∼2 h followed by a monotonic decrease.

[M0117 p5] Changes in Rct can be interpreted as a surface area change or polysulfide concentration change (Figure S2).
[M0118 p5] To delineate which one of these is dominating, we refer to the transient behavior of the Bode-phase plots during relaxation.
[M0119 p5] The peak position in the Bode-phase plots can be used as an indication of the characteristic frequency (fct) of the observed process.
[M0120 p5] A change in Rct caused by a process dominated by polysulfide concentration variations should experience a change in its fct, whereas a change in Rct dominated by surface area variations will tend to maintain a constant frequency peak position.
[M0121 p5] This can be rationalized if we first approximate the time constant of these processes as τ = RctC, where τ is the time constant (1/fct) and C is the capacitance.
[M0122 p5] Since capacitance C is proportional to the surface area and Rct is inversely proportional to the surface area, together, the impact of any surface area change will be likely nullified as they cancel each other.35,40 In other words, a decrease in Rct without any frequency change can be interpreted as a process mostly caused by an increase in the electronically active surface area.
[M0123 p5] On the other hand, a change in polysulfide concentration is not directly proportional to capacitance, which would be reflected in a change in τ.
[M0124 p5] Accordingly, a decrease in Rct accompanied by a change in τ can be interpreted as a phenomenon mostly dominated by an increase in concentration of redox active species, i.e., polysulfide concentration.
[M0125 p5] Using this analysis, we can deduce the events occurring during relaxation.
[M0126 p5] At DOD-1 (8 h of discharge, Figure 3a), the phase-peak associated with Rct (∼102 Hz) initially shifts to a lower frequency.
[M0127 p5] Once Rct stops increasing and begins to decrease, the frequency stops shifting and begins to maintain a constant value of ∼30 Hz.
[M0128 p5] This suggests that during the initial 3 h of relaxation, Rct increases due to a decrease in near-surface polysulfide concentration (likely diffusion to the bulk electrolyte) as illustrated in Figure 4a,b, whereas the subsequent decrease in Rct can be interpreted as an exposure of more surface area (Figure 4c).
[M0129 p5] As DOD-1 is at the early stages of the second discharge plateau, there likely exists a very high nearsurface polysulfide concentration.
[M0130 p5] Upon resting, the polysulfide surface concentration dissipates into the bulk electrolyte, decreasing the exchange current and increasing the Rct.

[M0143 p6] We will refer to these as reversibly precipitated sulfur species (RPSS).
[M0144 p6] Cells that were discharged for longer durations (DOD-3 and DOD- 4) appear to only possess a decreasing Rct trend without change in fct (i.e., dominated by the surface area increase, Figures 3i and 3l, respectively).
[M0145 p6] This is again likely due to exposure of additional surfaces from the dissolution of RPSS, suggesting a polysulfide concentration below the solubility limit at the surface when discharged to DOD-3 and DOD-4 (as schematically shown in Figure 4f).
[M0146 p6] When the cell was halted at DOD-2 and rested, Rct initially maintains a constant value over 2 h of relaxation before proceeding to decrease rapidly akin to the other three DODs.
[M0147 p6] The initially constant value of Rct is likely an indication of the onset of redissolution, where the polysulfide concentration is at the solubility limit of RPSS, with a rather small driving force for dissolution.
[M0148 p6] As discharge progressed from DOD-1 to DOD-2, more polysulfide in solution is converted into insoluble Li2S/Li2S2.
[M0149 p6] This decreases the near-surface concentration of the polysulfide.
[M0150 p6] Further discharge to DOD-3 and DOD-4 likely resulted in a sufficiently low polysulfide concentration that enabled the substantial dissolution of RPSS essentially right after the galvanostatic discharge is halted.
[M0151 p6] DOD-2 appears to represent a turning point between a nearsurface polysulfide concentration that is too high for RPSS dissolution and a concentration low enough to begin redissolving RPSS.
[M0152 p6] Intriguingly, the discharge percentage of DOD-2 aligns nicely with features pertaining to how Rint evolves over the course of discharge.
[M0153 p6] Looking at the impedance vs DOD evolution for the high Li ion concentration electrolyte (Figure 2b), Rint was found to increase up to ∼60 ohm (denoted as Rint‐P1, peak 1 of Rint), followed by a decrease to ∼27 ohm, which then increases back up to ∼37 ohm (denoted as Rint‐P2, peak 2 of Rint).
[M0154 p6] There seem to be multiple Rint increasing/decreasing events, which we refer to as oscillations.
[M0155 p6] Similar trends were reported in another study but not discussed.21 As a confirmation, this oscillating trend of Rint vs DOD can even be seen without the assistance of equivalent circuit modeling; the existence of Rint‐P2 can be observed directly on the Nyquist plot (Figure S3).
[M0156 p6] More importantly, while the first peak of Rint is relatively similar between the high and low Li ion concentrations (∼61 and ∼57 ohm, respectively), there appears to exist a much larger difference in the second Rint peak (∼67 vs ∼35 ohm, respectively).
[M0157 p6] Overall, this potentially indicates a degree of importance to the level of Rint and RPSS as surface films.
[M0158 p6] From Figure 3a,d,g,j), we can also associate DOD-1 as before Rint‐P2, DOD-2 is near Rint‐P2, and DOD-3 and -4 are post Rint‐P2.
[M0159 p6] We postulate that the phenomenon that is responsible for the differing relaxation characteristics among the 4 DODs can be tracked by Rint.
[M0160 p6] With a magnitude comparatively lower than that of Rct, Rint has been rarely discussed even though it has important implications on the surface processes in the cathode.
[M0161 p6] The interpretation of Rint has been rather complex.
[M0162 p6] Miller et al.41 attempted deconvolution of the high- to mid-frequency regime using distribution of relaxation time analysis.
[M0163 p6] In their study, four distinct time constants were identified in the frequency regime typically considered Rint (103−106 Hz).
[M0164 p6] This was done by using a regularization parameter of 10−4.
[M0165 p6] Only one of the four time constants was found to match that of a Li/Li symmetric cell, i.e., the solid electrolyte interphase impedance.
[M0166 p6] Using a series of symmetric cells containing various combinations of sulfur cathodes, carbon-only electrode, and lithium polysulfide-containing electrolytes, the other three time constants were all assigned to cathode processes: interparticle resistance, double layer relaxation, and sulfur-based reactions.
[M0167 p6] All such cathode processes can be affected by the formation of insulating sulfur-based surface layers, suggesting Rint can be used as a probe for the cathode’s surface impedance.
[M0168 p6] We propose in the case of LEC, the main contribution of Rint is dominated by surface films in the form of RPSS.

[M0176 p7] intermediates that has been recently shown to occur locally by microscopy,42 the fact that we are able to track the precipitation using operando EIS means that RPSS forms significantly throughout the cathode.
[M0177 p7] More importantly, the RPSS does not form randomly and sporadically over the course of the second discharge plateau, but more concerted and oscillating in magnitude across different DODs.
[M0178 p7] To further understand the importance of Rint, a series of cells with different electrolyte compositions (Table S1) were subjected to the same in operando EIS analysis collected in Figure 2.
[M0179 p7] Since LEC negatively affects both the discharge capacity and potential, we utilized the active material level energy density (Wh kgs −1) as the main performance metric of interest to correlate against the impedance contributions in the cell.
[M0180 p7] As expected, the energy delivered by the first discharge appears to show a reasonably strong negative correlation with the Rct magnitude taken at 6 h of discharge.
[M0181 p7] The higher the observed Rct, the lower the energy density of the first discharge (Figure 5a).
[M0182 p7] It should be noted that 6 h was chosen as the representative Rct because it was roughly at 50% DOD in all cases.
[M0183 p7] No significant correlation was found between the electrolyte resistance (Re) peak near the end of the first discharge plateau and the energy of the cell as shown in Figure S4a.
[M0184 p7] This is somewhat unexpected given its known dependence on the polysulfide concentration43 and suggests that a high viscosity of the electrolyte stemming from polysulfide dissolution might not be a great sole predictor of performance.
[M0185 p7] From initial inspection, no correlation was observed between Rint‐P2 and Rint‐P1 with the energy density (Figures S4b and S4c, respectively).
[M0186 p7] However, we found that the ratio between the second and first Rint peak (Rint‐P2/Rint‐P1, Figure 5b) correlated rather strongly (R2 = 0.98) with the discharge energy density up to a ratio of ∼1.
[M0187 p7] After Rint‐P2 reaches a similar magnitude as Rint‐P2, it appears that the energy density decreases and does not change and becomes unrelated to the Rint peak ratio.
[M0188 p7] We speculate that perhaps there exists a minimum threshold value of Rint‐p2 for the cell to produce a high energy density.
[M0189 p7] This finding is surprising first because a higher impedance generally does not yield higher energy density and second, the magnitude of Rint is rather small in comparison to Rct.
[M0190 p7] Although Rint makes little contribution to the total impedance, from our relaxation experiments (Figure 3), Rint can be considered as a probe for the surface condition of the sulfur cathode, which then has a strong impact on the charge transfer resistance.
[M0191 p7] Furthermore, as the trend appears only after normalizing Rint‐P2 by Rint‐P1, the degree of the second surface film formation event is important only in relation to the first surface film formation event.
[M0192 p7] Cells with a higher Rint‐P2/Rint‐P1 performed better, indicating that the precipitation of these solid-sulfur species likely help to alleviate the high concentration of the cell discharged under LEC.
[M0193 p7] These interim solid sulfur-based species give the electrolyte a “mass sink” to temporarily store the polysulfide content as some form of RPSS (Figure 5c) This mechanism ultimately provides the cell with an alternative pathway to rapidly regulate the polysulfide concentration without relying on the slow kinetics of lithiating a high concentration of polysulfide into solid Li2S/Li2S2.
[M0194 p7] Consequently, cells that do not have a high Rint,P2 (when normalized to Rint,P1) are unable to form such redissolvable species (i.e., RPSS) and unable to eliminate/avoid the sources (clustering/aggregates/gelation) of chronic overpotentials.
[M0195 p7] In order to promote more formation of RPSS, we must first understand how they are limited.
[M0196 p7] It is known that lithium polysulfide tends to form clusters at high concentrations or low temperature.44 It is possible that such clusters cannot form RPSSs and are very stable.
[M0197 p7] One possible explanation is that the more stable the clusters, the higher the degree of supersaturation and the poorer its ability to precipitate out as an RPSS.
[M0198 p7] This is especially true for polymeric species.
[M0199 p7] Clusters/ aggregates in solution have been previously shown to substantially increases the solubility of a polymer.45 In contrast, electrolytes with less stable polysulfide clusters will tend to precipitate out of solution readily, forming the desired RPSSs.
[M0200 p7] To show this is the case, we investigated the stability of the polysulfide/electrolyte solution to temperature.
[M0201 p7] Upon cooling, the solubility of the polysulfide is expected to decrease, which should cause precipitation.
[M0202 p7] Polysulfides were mixed with electrolyte at high and low Li ion salt concentrations and studied using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).
[M0203 p7] Because of the high polysulfide concentration, a slight compositional change from evaporation of DOL/DME (high vapor pressure at room temperature) could induce premature precipitation prior to testing, nullifying the results of the experiment.
[M0204 p7] The solvent was therefore switched to tetraglyme (TEGDME), which has a much lower vapor pressure.
[M0205 p7] DSC shows that cooling of high concentration polysulfide (8 mols L−1 nominally in the form of Li2S8) in an electrolyte solution of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M LiNO3 in TEGDME did not produce any exotherms (Figure 6a,b).
[M0206 p7] When the Li ion concentration was lowered, a clear exothermic peak was found with an abrupt increase, characteristic of supersaturation.
[M0207 p7] We attribute this peak to the precipitation of Figure 6.

[M0211 p8] polysulfide species, which only appear at low Li ion concentrations, supporting the notion that higher Li ion concentrations stabilize polysulfide solution against precipitation.
[M0212 p8] We emphasize that this is not simply the effect of a high Li ion concentration.
[M0213 p8] Samples without polysulfides (Figure S7) clearly exhibited TEGDME freezing endotherms at both high and low Li ion concentrations.
[M0214 p8] Evidently, the addition of polysulfide also impacted the freezing of the TEGDME, which further indicates a strong interaction between the polysulfide and TEGDME molecules stabilizing the solution.
[M0215 p8] A corollary of such a finding is that a stronger interaction between solvent and lithium polysulfide and lithium salts could provide an unwanted stabilizing effect of polysulfide clusters in solution during discharge.
[M0216 p8] This is likely detrimental to LEC LSB, which is in contrast to the common notion of increasing the solubility limit of polysulfide by using more polar solvents.23,46 Stronger solvating solvents will tend to interact more strongly with polysulfide and Li ions.
[M0217 p8] As expected, when solvents with higher solvating power such as 1,3-dimethyl-2- imidazolidinone, hexamethylphosphoramide, or dimethylacetamide were used at E/S of 2.5 μL mgs −1, no notable performance improvement was observed (Figure S8).
[M0218 p8] Arguably, the performance was slightly worse.
[M0219 p8] However, in flooded cells, where the concentration regime of polysulfide is much lower, notable performance enhancements have indeed been previously reported.47 From our results, the problem of lean E/S ratio (high concentration of polysulfide) does not depend on merely the solubility but is more related to the existing concentration of polysulfide in the electrolyte and the proclivity of aggregation/ clustering.
[M0220 p8] If clusters are formed, a higher polysulfide concentration can be achieved but might cause a higher charge transfer resistance.
[M0221 p8] Such clustering of polysulfide limits the formation of RPSSs and the polysulfide concentration cannot be regulated or remedied over the course of discharge.
[M0222 p8] When polysulfides are not stabilized in solution by clustering, RPSSs can form, acting as a temporary “mass sink” for the electrolyte to help mediate the polysulfide concentration.
[M0223 p8] Manthiram et al. have previously discussed that at low temperatures,44 polysulfides tend to form overpotentialinducing aggregates and found that using high donor anion salts could be beneficial.
[M0224 p8] Poorly dissociated Li salts could disrupt the formation of these lithium polysulfide clusters, as a significant portion of Li ions are already paired with their original anion and cannot participate in cluster formation.
[M0225 p8] With non-Li salts and high donor number anions, they were able to achieve an E/S ratio of 4 μL mgs −1.48 Zhang et al. described the possibility that cationic polysulfides (over-lithiated) are formed that hinder the discharge process.
[M0226 p8] It was proposed that a decrease in Li ion concentration will benefit the performance, citing an increased discharge capacity when using an E/S ratio of 3.5 μL mgs −1.49 At a very lean E/S ratio of <3.0 μL mgs −1, we observe that lowering Li ion concentration still exhibits a large overpotential.
[M0227 p8] Figure 7a shows the discharge voltage profile when an E/S ratio of 2.5 μL mgs −1 was used at a loading of 3.5 mg cm−2.
[M0228 p8] On the first and second cycles, these cells tend to produce unstable and ill-defined voltage plateaus at 0.05 C.
[M0229 p8] Notably, there is significant difference between the performance in Figure 7a and Figure S4i (operando EIS) even though they possessed similar concentrations.
[M0230 p8] This is likely due to the beneficial impact of the resting step throughout the operando EIS discharge.
[M0231 p8] Nevertheless, the discharge voltages in both cases are <2.0 V.
[M0232 p8] However, we found that the addition of lithium salt with high donor number anion (triflate) and under Li ion-starved conditions yielded a synergistic effect that substantially reduced the overpotential (Figure 7b) to a satisfactory voltage of well >2.0 V (albeit at 0.05 C).
[M0233 p8] Cathodic linear sweep voltammetry (Figure S9) using sulfur cathode at 3.5 mg cm−2 as the working electrode and Li metal as the reference/counter electrode at an E/S ratio of 2.5 also supports this conclusion.
[M0234 p8] When the typical electrolyte salt composition of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.25 M LiNO3 was used, only a weak reduction peak was observed.
[M0235 p8] The second peak was not observed likely to the poor kinetics of the reduction process under Li ion-rich conditions, which corroborates nicely with the galvanostatic discharge (Figure 2a).
[M0236 p8] However, when the Li ion content was reduced to 0.05 M LiTFSI and 0.025 M LiNO3, we begin to observe two distinct peaks (∼2.25 and ∼1.95 V), indicating a better ability of the cell to reduce sulfur polysulfide and also to reduce polysulfides to Li2S/Li2S2.
[M0237 p8] The peaks further become clearer and more distinct when 0.015 M LiTF is added to the electrolyte composition, yielding a higher peak current density.
[M0238 p8] We attribute this to the ability to form more RPSS as the lack of free Li ions limits the formation of stabilized polysulfide aggregates.
[M0239 p8] It should be noted that Li ion diffusion can also substantially influence the discharge.
[M0240 p8] One might suggest that the addition of LiTF could disrupt the polysulfide clustering and significantly increase the diffusion coefficient of the Li ions, causing the observed impact in performance.
[M0241 p8] To explore this possibility, we performed 7Li diffusion ordered spectroscopy NMR to estimate the diffusion coefficient of Li.50 Three electrolyte compositions were used to make lithium polysulfide solutions at different sulfur concentrations: high Li ion concentration (1 M LiTFSI + 0.25 M LiNO3), low Li ion concentration (0.05 M LiTFSI + 0.025 M LiNO3), and low Li ion concentration with LiTF addition (0.05 M LiTFSI + 0.025 M LiNO3 + 0.015 M LiTF).
[M0242 p8] The attenuation curves of all samples are shown in Figure S10.
[M0243 p8] The estimated diffusion coefficients are shown in Figure S11 plotted against the sulfur concentration [S].
[M0244 p8] The diffusion coefficient drops with increasing polysulfide concentration, which is expected due to increases in viscosity and clustering.
[M0245 p8] Interestingly, even with the same polysulfide concentrations, there are substantial differences between the three electrolyte compositions tested, indicating the importance of Li ion concentration.

[M0251 p9] In contrast, addition of 0.015 M LiTF to the low concentration sample increases the diffusion coefficient slightly from 7.23 × 10−10 to 8.72 × 10−10 m2/s (1.2 times increase).
[M0252 p9] It is expected that increasing salt concentration would decrease the diffusion coefficient.
[M0253 p9] The increase in diffusion coefficient from LiTF addition aligns with the notion that polysulfide clustering/aggregates has been disturbed.
[M0254 p9] At high polysulfide concentrations, the impact of adding LiTF on the diffusion coefficient is lower, yielding only a 1.06 times increase (with LiTF 2.55 × 10−10 m2/s vs without LiTF 2.4 × 10−10 m2/s), whereas simply decreasing the concentration of Li ions still yielded a large increase (2.4 × 10−10 m2/s vs 1.25 × 10−10 m2/s).
[M0255 p9] However, the performance benefit from lowering the total Li ion concentration was not as dramatic when compared to the case with LiTF.
[M0256 p9] This suggests that the large increase in Li diffusion is unable to increase the discharge voltage up to >2.0 V.
[M0257 p9] Since only a marginal increase in Li diffusion coefficient with LiTF addition was observed for the 5 M polysulfide sample, we suspect that the main benefit of LiTF is not in enhancing Li transport but in promoting the formation of RPSS.
[M0258 p9] Unfortunately, the benefit of this electrolyte system is limited to 0.05 C.
[M0259 p9] At an E/S ratio of 2.5, the cell was unable to enter the second discharge plateau under a higher rate of 0.1 C.
[M0260 p9] However, at a slightly higher E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs −1, we varied the discharge rate from 0.05 to 0.2 C with an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs −1 (Figure S12).
[M0261 p9] It appears that at 0.1 C, the cell was able to mostly discharge above 2.0 V.
[M0262 p9] At 0.2 C the cell once again cannot enter the second discharge plateau with a cutoff voltage of 1.5 V.
[M0263 p9] Nevertheless, there still appears to be substantial benefit over just using low Li ion concentration composition (Figure 7a).
[M0264 p9] The triflate salt seemed to have a trade-off and yielded a decreased capacity.
[M0265 p9] The use of another high donor number salt, 0.375 M of lithium nitrate without LiTFSI (Figure S4e,h,j), also resulted in improved discharge profile over the standard 1 M LiTFSI + 0.25 M LiNO3 electrolyte.
[M0266 p9] While the voltage of 0.375 M LiNO3 (Figure S4e,h,j vs Figure S4c) was lower than that of Li ion-starved + high donor number salt (Figure 7c), the total capacity was higher for 0.375 M LiNO3.
[M0267 p9] The discharge voltage plateaus using our strategy is markedly higher than that of similar E/S ratio studies employing fluorinated solvents which tends to depress the discharge voltage.14 Directly increasing the LiTF while maintaining a higher Li ion concentration also yielded a higher voltage, but there still a depression in the discharge profile <2.0 V after the first cycle (Figure S13).
[M0268 p9] Overall, there appears to be some dependency of the discharge energy density (product of voltage and specific capacity) with the type of anion used and concentration of the salt, especially at the LEC regimes, and should be investigated in future studies.
[M0269 p9] Finally, we note that the use of low Li ion concentration electrolytes might lead to problems such as the lowered Li ion conductivity in the electrolyte.
[M0270 p9] While this might be partially alleviated by the dissolution of lithium polysulfides, it is unlikely to be sustainable at high current densities and near low and high SOCs.
[M0271 p9] Furthermore, use of low amounts of LiNO3 is likely to hinder cyclability as the Li metal will be less passivated against polysulfide shuttling.
[M0272 p9] This is evidenced by the short cycle life, where we observed stable cycling for only 9 cycles (Figure S14).
[M0273 p9] It is foreseeable that a combination of sulfophillic surfaces,51,52 salts with poorly dissociating anions, and moderately lowered Li ion concentration can provide significant improvements in performance at LEC and can be the topic future investigations. ■CONCLUSION Lean electrolyte conditions and high sulfur loading along with a cell-level discharge voltage >2.0 V are critical for achieving a commercially relevant lithium−sulfur battery.
[M0274 p9] Decreasing the E/S ratio to 2.5 can have a drastic influence on the cell that typically either prevents discharge entirely or produces a discharge profile that is very unstable and depressed.
[M0275 p9] While the existence of intermediate passivating films has been previously acknowledged in lithium−sulfur batteries,53 our findings suggest a seemingly concerted nature in this process and that the precipitated intermediate can be redissolved back.
[M0276 p9] Furthermore, it seems that the higher the magnitude of this process (taken as the Rint, mid-frequency impedance), the higher the discharge energy density.
[M0277 p9] We propose that these reversibly precipitated sulfur species (RPSS) in fact serve as a beneficial mass sink for temporarily storing polysulfide and transferring it out of the electrolyte solution.
[M0278 p9] Ultimately, this serves as a means for the cell to regulate the polysulfide concentration, reducing high polysulfide concentration-induced overpotentials.
[M0279 p9] High concentration polysulfide solutions that are too stable against precipitations are likely the cause of the poor performance observed at LEC.
[M0280 p9] It was found that the addition of lithium salts with high donor number salts in addition to an overall decrease in Li ions can promote formation of RPSS as deduced from the large Rint‐P2/Rint‐P1 ratio and increase the discharge energy density while maintaining an operating voltage of >2.0 V at room temperature and at 0.05 C. ■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION Materials.
