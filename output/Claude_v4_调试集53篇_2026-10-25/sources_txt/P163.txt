[M0003 p1] Herein, we employ 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether as a co-solvent in the electrolyte of Li-S batteries to meet the demands.
[M0004 p1] The co-solvent fosters the formation of a LiF-rich solid electrolyte interphase on lithium metal anode, as revealed by Ab initio molecular dynamics and Femtosecond Stimulated Raman spectroscopy.
[M0005 p1] The co-solvent results in an average Coulombic efficiency of 99.4% for lithium plating/stripping cycles.
[M0006 p1] Full cells with a capacity ratio of 2 between lithium anode and the S@PAN cathode (3 mgs cm2) exhibited a stable cycle life over 100 cycles at an electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 2 mL mg1, validating the high Coulombic efficiency of the lithium anode and demonstrating the compatibility of the electrolyte with both electrodes.
[M0007 p1] To enhance the energy density, we prepared a hybrid cathode composed of 45 wt.% VS2 mixed with ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene as the cathode.
[M0008 p1] Based on the mass of both electrodes and the electrolyte, the full cell delivers an energy density of 483 Wh kg1, which demonstrates viable Li-S batteries with the leanelectrolyte.
[M0009 p1] Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a promising nextgeneration high-energy battery technology [1].
[M0010 p1] Recently, a plethora of examples of high-performing Li-S cells have been demonstrated [2–4].
[M0011 p1] However, commercialization of Li-S batteries still confronts several major technical challenges, including the shuttling of polysulfide species [5], rapid capacity fading of the sulfur cathode under lean-electrolyte conditions [6], and the low Coulombic efficiency (CE) of plating/stripping of the lithium metal anode (LMA) [7], where the latter two issues can be the real showstoppers but have received meager effort, to date [8–13].
[M0012 p1] Remarkable advances have been made in polysulfide immobilization by, for example, intricate carbon/sulfur nano-composites [14,15], coated separators [16,17], and highly concentrated electrolytes [18,19].
[M0013 p1] These strategies are efficacious in electrolyteflooded Li-S systems; however, the superlative results often fail to be translated under the lean-electrolyte conditions, where the sulfur cathode accentuates much lower capacity and fast fading [20].
[M0014 p1] Note that the inconveniently necessary but extravagant usage of electrolyte tanks the specific energy of the Li-S cells and raises the manufacturing cost [21,22].
[M0015 p1] Besides the challenge to secure the high performance of cathode, the lean electrolyte needs to facilitate the impeccably reversible plating and stripping cycles of LMA.
[M0016 p1] Unfortunately, conventional electrolytes used by the LIB industry is unstable with LMA, both thermodynamically and kinetically, resulting in continuous corrosion of LMA and the formation of lithium dendrites [7].

[M0023 p2] to identify electrolytes that are kinetically stable with LMA via passivation with solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) under lean conditions [23,24].
[M0024 p2] Most recently, strides of progress have been made to enhance the performance of sulfur electrodes under lean-electrolyte conditions, i.e., at an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 5 (mL mg1) or below [25–28].
[M0025 p2] Hollow current collectors [29], swellable sulfur cathode [30], and ZnS-coating [31] were employed for the enhanced electrolyte uptake.
[M0026 p2] Modifications of electrolytes have also been made.
[M0027 p2] For instance, dimethyl disulfide is used as a co-solvent for a higher discharge capacity [32]; catholyte comprising Li2S6 facilitated electrolyte permeation into thick sulfur electrodes [33]; a salt of NH4TFSI served as a redox enhancer to alleviate Li2S passivation, thus promoting the S/Li2S utilization [20].
[M0028 p2] Electrocatalysts such as vacancy-rich Ni3FeN were utilized to improve surface reactions of sulfide species [34].
[M0029 p2] Electrolyte represents the centrality to improve the CE of LMA.
[M0030 p2] Recently, multiple localized high concentration electrolytes (LHCE) were introduced [35–39].
[M0031 p2] These low-viscosity electrolytes are usually fluorine rich, which facilitates the formation of SEI passivation layers on LMA.
[M0032 p2] For instance, Nazar and coworkers developed a LHCE that contained 1,1,2,2- tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) in diglyme (G2), which delivered a CE of 96.2% for LMA, and in this electrolyte the S/CMK-3 cathode exhibited 100 cycles at an E/S ratio of 5 at 55 C [35].
[M0033 p2] Liang and coworkers used a mixture electrolyte comprising hexa-fluoroisopropyl methyl ether (HFME), ethylene carbonate (EC), and diethyl carbonate (DEC), which achieved a CE of 99.5% for LMA and 50 cycles for the S/Ketjen black cathode at an E/S ratio of 1.5 [36].
[M0034 p2] In a similar fashion, Wang and coworkers employed a mixture of 1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1 ,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (OFE) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with the volume ratio of 95:5 and an E/S ratio of around 4.8, where the CE of LMA reached 99.3%, and the sulfur cathode retained a discharge capacity of 775 mAh g1 at the 150th cycle with the active mass loading of 1–2 mgs cm2 [37].
[M0035 p2] To date, lean electrolytes have been reported in half cells either for the sulfur electrode or for the LMA separately.
[M0036 p2] However, it is rarely reported that under lean-electrolyte conditions, an electrolyte can facilitate both high CE of LMA and stable cycling of the Li-S batteries at the full-cell level.
[M0037 p2] Herein, we report a new electrolyte formula for Li-S batteries under lean conditions, where 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2- trifluoroethyl ether (TFTFE, Scheme 1) is employed as a cosolvent added to the electrolyte of 6 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in DME.
[M0038 p2] TFTFE outperforms other recently reported fluorinated ethers in safety, and its cost and purity are competitive (Table S1).
[M0039 p2] TFTFE was used as an electrolyte for Li- S batteries by Liu and co-workers owing to the its unique inertness against polysulfides [40].
[M0040 p2] TFTFE was later reported as a cosolvent mixed with the electrolytes of fluorinated carbonates in a lithium metal battery with a NMC811 cathode [41].
[M0041 p2] In this study, we demonstrate that the TFTFE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte is highly compatible with LMA, where the average plating/stripping CE reaches 99.4% over 350 cycles, and the sulfur cathode delivers stable cycling performance under the lean-electrolyte conditions with E/S ratios of as low as 2.
[M0042 p2] Furthermore, femtosecond stimulated Raman spectroscopy (FSRS) and Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations reveal that the TFTFE addition does not alter the solvation structure of the electrolyte salt but profoundly affects the electrochemical performance of both electrodes.
[M0043 p2] When a S@PAN cathode and a limited mass of LMA with the anode/cathode capacity (N/P) ratio of 2 are assembled in full cells with the lean electrolyte at an E/S ratio of 2, the full cell with the S@PAN cathode delivered 100 cycles without a glitch.
[M0044 p2] In addition, pairing the electrolyte with a hybrid sulfur cathode that has 45 wt.% vanadium disulfide (VS2) added into a cathode of ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, the same full cell configuration demonstrated one of the highest energy densities of 483 Wh kg1 based on the mass of both and electrolyte.
[M0045 p2] Lastly, this electrolyte exhibits low viscosity for excellent electrode wetting and anti-flammable properties for enhanced battery safety.
[M0046 p2] Materials and methods Electrolyte and cathode preparation The baseline electrolyte (BE) is 6 M LiFSI (99%, Oakwood Chemicals) in DME (99.5%, anhydrous, Sigma Aldrich).
[M0047 p2] The salt was dried at 95 C under Ar to remove trace water before use.
[M0048 p2] The mixture of salt and solvent was stirred for 3 h.
[M0049 p2] The diluent, TFTFE (99%, SynQuest Labs), was added to the as-prepared BE (BE/ TFTFE, v/v 1:2 or 1:5) to prepare hybrid electrolytes, HE-2 and HE-5, respectively.
[M0050 p2] S@PAN cathode was synthesized as previously reported [42].
[M0051 p2] Briefly, acrylonitrile (99%, Sigma Aldrich) and sublimed sulfur (99.98%, Sigma Aldrich) were mixed in a mass ratio of 1:4 and ball milled for an hour.
[M0052 p2] Homogeneously mixed powder was annealed at 450 C for 3 h under N2 at a ramping rate of 5 C min1.
[M0053 p2] A hybrid cathode powder was synthesized in three steps and all moisture/oxygen sensitive syntheses were conducted in a tube-furnace housed in a glovebox under Ar: (1) Li2S@graphene nanocapsules were synthesized as reported [43].
[M0054 p2] Briefly, lithium foil (0.38 mm in thickness, Alfa Aesar) was placed in the tube furnace, and CS2 liquid in a flask was bubbled by Ar to form a mixture gas containing CS2 vapor.
[M0055 p2] This gas passed through the quartz tube as ramping up the temperature to 650 C, and this temperature was held for five hours, followed by naturally cooling down to room temperature.
[M0056 p2] (2) The obtained Li2S@graphene was finely ground using mortar/pestle, then it was gently pressed to form a pellet.
[M0057 p2] The Zn precursor suspension, a mixture of 50 mg zinc acetylacetonate and 5 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF), was dropped on the pellet, which was dried under vacuum for 5 h to remove solvent before it was transferred to a tube furnace.

[M0087 p3] Results and discussion We set 6 M LiFSI in DME as the baseline electrolyte (BE) and mixed it with TFTFE in two volume ratios, v/v 1:2 and 1:5, resulting in two hybrid electrolytes, referred to as HE-2 and HE-5.
[M0088 p3] When adding a co-solvent, the first question is whether it participates in the solvation structures of electrolyte ions.
[M0089 p3] To learn the interactions between the electrolyte species, we conducted FSRS measurements (Figure 1a).

[M0094 p4] 648 cm1 and 850 cm1 attributed to -CF2 wagging and asymmetric C-O stretching, respectively [54,55].
[M0095 p4] For a controlled electrolyte of 2 M LiFSI solvated in DME, the peak at 717 cm1 indicates the S-N-S stretching vibration mode of FSI [56], which shifts to 735 cm1 when the concentration of LiFSI increases to 6 M in BE due to more prolific pairing between FSI and Li+ [57].
[M0096 p4] At the same time, the DME peak also shifts from 827 cm1 to 867 cm1 due to the enhanced interaction of DME with Li+ while the stretch of free DME molecules vanishes.
[M0097 p4] A peak separation was observed between 825 cm1 and 875 cm1 for 2 M LiFSI in DME, which is due to the mixed contributions from different C-O-C vibration modes.
[M0098 p4] In contrast, in BE the C- O-C vibration mode is more homogenized due to the high concentration of Li-ions.
[M0099 p4] When TFTFE is introduced to BE in different volume ratios, the peak of FSI stays at 735 cm1 regardless of the different ratios, indicating the consistent chemical environment of FSI ions irrespective of the volumes of TFTFE added.
[M0100 p4] Therefore, adding TFTFE into BE does not interrupt its coordination structures.
[M0101 p4] Likewise, the characteristic peaks of TFTFE at 648 cm1 and 850 cm1 remain non-shifted in HE-2 and HE-5 compared to its pure liquid, suggesting that TFTFE, indeed, serves as a diluent, not playing any solvation role with ions in the electrolytes.
[M0102 p4] AIMD was conducted to further understand the coordination structures for the electrolytes of BE and HE-2.
[M0103 p4] Solvent molecules, Li+, and FSI ions were randomly placed in the unit cell with their concentrations consistent with experiments.
[M0104 p4] Then, the unit cell was relaxed for 13 ps, in which the time between 10 ps and 15 ps is deemed sufficient to use for analysis (Figure S1).
[M0105 p4] Besides, we applied a higher energy cutoff of 600 eV for a good description of electron wavefunction [58,59].
[M0106 p4] Figure 1b illustrates the two distinctive first-coordination shells of Li+ sorted out of the entire simulation cell.
[M0107 p4] Li+ in both electrolytes is bound with three to four oxygens from DME and FSI, where the first coordination shells are analogous to the previously reported solvation structures with inert co-solvents [58,60].
[M0108 p4] To be specific, a high concentration of LiFSI renders Li+ more likely to bind with FSI rather than DME, and the oxygen atoms of TFTFE in HE-2 does not participate in the Li+ solvation.
[M0109 p4] The ions and DME already form the stable solvation structures before TFTFE is added, where less dielectric TFTFE barely perturbs the Li-O (FSI) coordination.
[M0110 p4] Furthermore, the radial distribution function (RDF) and the quantitative analysis of Li-O bonds also suggest that TFTFE functions as a diluent without involving in the coordination chemistry (Figure S2 and Table S2).
[M0111 p4] The AIMD results thereby corroborate the above Raman spectroscopy interpretation of unchanged solvation upon TFTFE addition.
[M0112 p4] The new electrolyte is designed to improve the reversibility of LMA.
[M0113 p4] We measured CE for each plating/stripping cycle by calculating the ratio of the stripped lithium over a fixed amount of plated lithium, i.e., 1.0 mAh cm2, where the current density is 0.5 mA cm2 .
[M0114 p4] Cu foil was used as a working electrode and 10 cycles of electrochemical sweeping treatment from 0.01 V to 1.0 V (vs.
[M0115 p4] Li+/Li, hereafter) at 0.05 mA cm2 was applied before lithium is initially plated (Figure S3).
[M0116 p4] For HE-2, the first plating and stripping overpotentials were respectively 35 mV and 27 mV (Figure 2a), where the CE of the initial cycle reached 96.3%.
[M0117 p4] It gradually rose to 99.6% by the 30th cycle with a reduced overpotential of 25 mV and 25 mV upon plating and stripping, respectively.
[M0118 p4] In contrast, BE exhibited an erratic behavior of plating/stripping, as shown in Figure 2b.
[M0119 p4] Initial plating/stripping overpotentials of BE were 54 mV and 45 mV, respectively, and the first CE was 93.7%, which gently increased to 97.7% by the 10th cycle, but it started to fluctuate during the following cycling.
[M0120 p4] Especially at the 26th cycle, a severe overcharge was observed with the attained capacity of 1.4 mAh cm2, 40% more than the plated amount in the same cycle.
[M0121 p4] We postulate that the extra capacity of 0.4 mAh cm2 came from the stranded Li mass over the previous cycles, which somewhat got re-wired back to the current collector in this cycle.
[M0122 p4] Figure S4 shows the GCD profiles of the six cycles, where the CE exceeds 100%.
[M0123 p4] Short circuit is typically associated with abrupt potential drops, which was not observed in these plating/stripping profiles; therefore, the high CE here is unlikely due to short circuit.
[M0124 p4] Furthermore, we tested CE of LMA in HE-2 at 2.0 mA cm2, a quadrupled current density, where dendrites may more likely form (Figure 2c).
[M0125 p4] LMA exhibited an increased extent of polarization with an initial overpotential of 190 mV that stabilized to 130 mV after 30 cycles, which is a marginally lower overpotential than the heavily fluorinated electrolytes (FEC/FEMC/HFE) and the engineered Li2S8/LiNO3 electrolyte, but slightly higher than that of using 10 M LiFSI in EC/DMC and the ternary-salt of LiFSI-LiTFSI-LiNO3 [41,61–63].
[M0126 p4] The cell showed stable potential profiles with the CE increased to 98.7% at the 30th cycle.
[M0127 p4] We tested CE over 350 cycles, and the arithmetic mean CE values are 99.4% and 99.3%, at 0.5 mA cm2 and 2.0 mA cm2, respectively, in HE-2 (Figure 2d).
[M0128 p4] The cycling stability of LMA in BE and HE-2 was also investigated in Li||Li symmetric cells at various current densities.
[M0129 p4] As observed, BE displays a greater overpotential upon plating and stripping, i.e., 31 mV, than that of HE-2, 15 mV, at 0.5 mA cm2.

[M0131 p4] The results suggest the utility of TFTFE in protecting the surface of lithium metal (Figure 2e and f).
[M0132 p4] In order to understand how TFTFE as an electrolyte diluent enhances Li cycling, we investigated the surface morphology of the cycled LMA and the SEI compositions.
[M0133 p4] After 20 cycles of plating/stripping at 0.5 mA cm2 with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm2 in Li||Li symmetric cells, LMA in HE-2 displays smooth surface with tightly bound Li grains, whereas BE shows relatively smaller Li islands with conspicuous cracks, where neither of the two electrolytes grows dendrites on LMA’s surface, as shown in Figure 3a and b.
[M0134 p4] The ratios of Li-OFSI-, Li-ODME, and Li-OTFTFE suggested by RDF analysis (Table S2) show that Li+ prefers to bind FSI over DME in both BE and in HE-2, which suggests that FSI may be preferably reduced on the LMA surface than DME.
[M0135 p4] We studied the SEI compositions by depth-profiling X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on the cycled LMA with Ar+ sputtering that removes the top layer up to 10 nm thick beneath the surface (Figure 3c).
[M0136 p4] The N 1s spectra reveal the degree of FSI reduction on the surface of LMA in electrolytes.
[M0137 p4] The peaks at 396.8 eV and 399.1 eV can be attributed to Li-N and N-SOx, respectively, and these signals could still be found at 10 nm deep in LMA for HE-2.
[M0138 p4] However, those peaks were not detectable for BE when the top SEI layer was removed by 5 nm thick, which indicates the preferential FSI reduction on LMA in HE-2.

[M0167 p6] Although the capacity retained 907 mAh gs 1 at the 10th cycle, the capacity fading rate (4% per cycle) was high (Figure S7).
[M0168 p6] Plus, building a thick electrode (>5.0 mgs cm2) of this composite electrode was problematic via the conventional doctor-blade method.
[M0169 p6] Thus, we selected a hybrid cathode comprising 45 wt.% VS2 mixed with ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, a compact composite cathode for further studies.
[M0170 p6] VS2 lowers the potential of the charge process and helps immobilize polysulfides in the electrode [68,69].

[M0197 p8] The advantage of using TFTFE as a co-solvent also lies on its unique physical properties (Table S5), particularly the flammability resistance, owing to its high flash point.
[M0198 p8] Flammability tests were conducted by placing a fire source to the electrolytesoaked glass fiber separators.
[M0199 p8] A separator soaked in HE-2 remained without fire after burning it for five consecutive seconds, and when the lighter was taken away, it showed carbonized trace on the surface (Figure S12a).
[M0200 p8] This indicates that the TFTFE addition enhances battery safety.
[M0201 p8] In contrast, the BEsoaked separator was ignited right after the lighter approached and maintained its red-yellow flame until the electrolyte was burned out (Figure S12b).
[M0202 p8] Owing to the highly combustible characteristics of DME, high salt concentration alone could not eliminate the flammability of the BE electrolyte (Video S1 and Video S2).
[M0203 p8] In a similar fashion, TFTFE also acted as a diluent, where the viscosity was drastically lowered from 107.72 mPas for BE to 11.34 mPas for HE-2.
[M0204 p8] Likewise, ionic conductivity decreases from 11.53 mS cm1 for BE to 4.59 mS cm1 for HE-2 because TFTFE does not solvate Li-ions [74] (Table S6).
[M0205 p8] The conductivity results suggest that both the diffusivity of diffusion and the mobility under the electric field of polysulfides in HE-2 and HE-5 should be smaller than in BE.
[M0206 p8] This is due to the diluent characteristics of TFTFE—a weak solvent with low permittivity, and TFTFE does not perturb the original Li+ solvation structure.
[M0207 p8] Thus, the presence of TFTFE sterically narrows the effective pathways of ions, thus lowering their movability in the electrolyte, including both Li+ and polysulfides.
[M0208 p8] Thus, the diluent may help curtail the polysulfide shuttle effect.
[M0209 p8] Conclusion In summary, we developed an electrolyte formula for the Li-S battery under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0210 p8] The addition of TFTFE as a co-solvent to LiFSI/DME promotes the formation of LiFabundant SEI, where the LMA exhibits a high CE of 99.4%.
[M0211 p8] The TFTFE addition also alters the physical properties of the electrolyte, where the electrolyte wets the carbon/sulfur composite cathode better, which promotes the electrolyte uptake under the lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0212 p8] When the new electrolyte, i.e., HE-2, is paired with S@PAN cathode at a low E/S ratio of 2 and a N/P ratio of 2, the full cell can be cycled for 100 cycles.
[M0213 p8] This is a pioneering example of a full cell design, where an electrolyte facilitates excellent performance and reversibility of both electrodes under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0214 p8] To increase the energy density, we tested this electrolyte with a hybrid cathode, where 45 wt.% VS2 is mixed with the composite of ZnS- Li2S@graphene, which yielded an energy density of 483 Wh kg1 based on the mass of both electrodes and electrolyte.
[M0215 p8] The results here represent a new milestone achieved for the safe and highenergy density Li-S battery with a lean-electrolyte.
