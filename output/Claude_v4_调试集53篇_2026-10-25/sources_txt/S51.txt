[M0007 p2] However, two main challenges, lithium polysulfides (LPS) dissolution from the sulfur cathode and the unstable Li anode, impede the practical application of Li–S battery.
[M0008 p2] Developing advanced electrolyte is an effective strategy to tune the stability of both sulfur cathode and Li anode.
[M0009 p2] Here, a concentrated imidazolium-based ionic liquids (IL) electrolyte was explored for Li–S battery with the synergistic advantages of both IL and high concentrated electrolyte.
[M0010 p2] A systematic study was conducted to reveal the effect of salt concentration on the properties of the IL electrolyte and the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.
[M0011 p2] It is found that an effective suppression of LPS dissolution and improved stability of Li anode can be obtained with increase of salt concentration.

[M0017 p2] Supplementary material for this article is available online Lithium sulfur (Li–S) battery, using sulfur as the cathode and Li metal as the anode, is regarded as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation energy storage system due to its high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1) and high specific energy (2600 Wh kg−1).1,2 In addition, the elemental sulfur is naturally abundant, environmentally friendly and low cost.
[M0018 p2] Nevertheless, Li–S battery suffers from practical challenges of low active material utilization, poor cycle stability, low Coulombic efficiency and safety hazards.
[M0019 p2] These issues are mainly resulted from the incompatibility of electrolyte with both sulfur cathode and Li anode.3,4 On the side of sulfur cathode, the serious dissolution and shuttle of lithium polysulfides (LPS, Li2Sx, 2 < x ⩽8) is considered as the primary problem.
[M0020 p2] The LPS are generated as the reductive intermediates from the elemental sulfur that can be easily dissolved into organic electrolyte before forming into the final reductive products of Li2S/Li2S2, leading to the massive loss of active material and fast capacity fading.5 Besides, such dissolution phenomenon can even spontaneously occur during the static storage period, causing the open circuit voltage (OCV) fall and energy loss, which is so-called self-discharging behavior.6,7 In addition, the dissolved LPS can cross over the separator and migrate to the Li anode, where it reacts with Li to trigger shuttle effect, leading to poor Coulombic efficiency, irreversible capacity loss and Li anode corrosion.8 On the side of Li anode, the main problem is the instability of solid electrolyte interface (SEI), which is generated by the spontaneous reaction of Li anode with electrolyte.
[M0021 p2] Randomly formed SEI is usually not strong enough to bear the huge volume expansion during Li plating/ stripping processes, leading to repetitive fracture of SEI and continuous consumption of electrolyte, and even Li dendrite formation.9 Alongside, aforementioned LPS dissolution aggravates the corrosion of Li surface.
[M0022 p2] Various strategies have been worked out to improve the performances of Li–S cells, such as optimizing the electrolyte,10 fabricating sulfur cathodes11 and protecting Li anode.12 Among them, increasing the salt concentration to fabricate the concentrated electrolyte has been receiving intense attention due to its unique solvation structure to realize attractive functions, especially for suppressing LPS dissolution and stabilizing Li anode.13 For example, Chen’s group10 found that an improved capacity retention and Coulombic efficiency nearing 100% were achieved by simply increasing the salt concentration in typical electrolyte system (LiTFSI in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)), which was disclosed by the effective suppression of both LPS dissolution and Li dendrite growth.
[M0023 p2] The function of protecting Li has been largely demonstrated, which is revealed by the uniform Li deposition and dissolution in the concentrated electrolyte.14,15 Despite the attractive advantages of the concentrated electrolyte, several issues are still needed to be improved with regard to the solvents.
[M0024 p2] Traditional organic solvents, such as ethers and sulfones, are popularly adopted to design the electrolyte, posing the threat of LPS dissolution due to their intrinsic high affinity towards LPS.16 Besides, the hidden safety hazards still exist as most of these organic solvents are volatile and flammable.
[M0025 p2] Therefore, there is strong need to explore alternatives to replace the traditional organic solvents.
[M0026 p2] In this regard, ionic liquids (IL) might be promising alternatives due to their unique physicochemical properties.
[M0027 p2] Generally, IL show week interaction to LPS due to their weak Lewis acidic cations and Lewis basic anions, reducing the risk of LPS dissolution.17 Besides, IL are featured with the properties of low volatility, non-flammability, and large electrochemical stability, providing improved safety.18 Nevertheless, it is doubtful to combine the advantages of concentrated electrolyte and IL for Li–S battery.
[M0028 p2] The main concern is the high viscosity of general IL, resulting in slow ionic conductivity.
[M0029 p2] Increasing salt concentration usually results in higher viscosity,19 which might make this problem much worse.
[M0030 p2] Hence, low concentration (typically 0.5 ∼1 M) or mixture with other solvents are commonly employed in IL-based electrolyte.
[M0031 p2] Though there are several works employing IL electrolyte with relatively high salt concentration,20,21 their research for Li–S battery is very limited.
[M0032 p2] To explore the feasibility of using concentrated IL electrolyte for Li–S battery, a relatively high salt concentration is urgently needed.

[M0036 p3] Bearing these in mind, we try to prepare concentrated electrolyte by using IL as the solvent and explore its influence on the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.
[M0037 p3] By using 1-Allyl-3- methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (AMImTFSI) as the solvent and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the salt, a concentrated IL electrolyte with relatively high salt concentration of 3 M was achieved.
[M0038 p3] The properties of the electrolyte containing different salt concentration were measured for better understanding its influence on the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.
[M0039 p3] To evaluate its compatibility with both electrodes, the effects of the IL electrolyte on both sulfur cathode and Li anode were systematically studied in terms of the electrode morphologies, chemical components, and interfacial properties.
[M0040 p3] Our study offers a new alternative way to design electrolyte for Li–S batteries with stable cathode and anode.
[M0041 p3] Experimental Materials and regents.—Ketjen Black (KB, EC660-JD, Lion Specialty Chemicals Corporation, ash content 0.1%), sulfur (Sigma- Aldrich, 99.98%), lithium sulfide (Li2S, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.98%), lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.95%), 1-allyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (AMImTFSI, Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI), > 98%), acetylene black (AB, Denka, ash content 0.01%), Sodium Carboxymethyl Cellulose (CMC, Sigma-Aldrich, average Mw ∼250,000), Styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, NIPPON A&L INC.), dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%).
[M0042 p3] Sample preparation.—Three types of electrolytes were prepared with LiTFSI concentration of 1 M, 2 M and 3 M, which are denoted as 1 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, 2 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, and 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, respectively.
[M0043 p3] Conventionally used sulfur/KB (S/KB) composite was utilized to evaluate the performance of as prepared electrolytes.
[M0044 p3] Sulfur and KB was mixed with mass ratio of 3:2, followed by heating at 155 °C for 12 h in a tube furnace under inert atmosphere.
[M0045 p3] Lithium polysulfides (LPS) dissolution experiment.—LPS was synthesized to directly observe its dissolution in the electrolytes prepared.
[M0046 p3] Herein, Li2S4 was synthesized as a typical representative of LPS.
[M0047 p3] Based on the reaction of Li S Li S 8 S x 1 8 , x 2 8 2 + ( − ) → 16 0.4 M Li2S4 was synthesized by taking stoichiometric amount of Li2S and sulfur with a molar ratio of 8:3 in a vial containing 1 ml electrolyte.
[M0048 p3] Then, the mixture was stirred and heated at 80 °C for 50 h and thereafter maintained at 30 °C for 48 h with stirring.
[M0049 p3] All the above procedure was performed under inert atmosphere.
[M0050 p3] Characterization and calculation.—The viscosity was measured by viscometer (TV-35, TOKI SANGYO) equipped with a thermostat.
[M0051 p3] The ionic conductivity was analyzed by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) technique in the frequency range from 1 MHz to 10 mHz with voltage amplitude of 10 mV and temperature range of 30 °C∼60 °C using an impedance analyzer (Solartron 1260).
[M0052 p3] The morphologies of electrodes were observed by scanning electronic microscopy (SEM, S-4500, Hitachi).
[M0053 p3] Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental mapping was also collected by SEM (TM3030Plus tabletop microscope, Hitachi).
[M0054 p3] Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on S-probe TM 2803, Fisons Instruments.
[M0055 p3] The cycled cells were disassembled and washed by enough DME in Ar-filled glove box, followed by dried under vacuum overnight before SEM and XPS characterization.
[M0056 p3] The geometric optimization and electronic properties were evaluated based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations using Gaussian 16 software.
[M0057 p3] Calculations were performed on a B3LYP functional equipped with a 6-311 G(d,p) basis set.
[M0058 p3] The increased concentrated condition was simulated by increasing the number of anions around IL.
[M0059 p3] Cell fabrication and electrochemical measurements.—The slurry for sulfur electrode was prepared by mixing S/KB, AB and CMC/SBR (1:1 wt/wt) with a mass ratio of 70:20:10 in deionized water as solvent using planetary centrifugal mixer (Kakuhunter SK- 350TII).
[M0060 p3] The slurry was coated on aluminum foil (15 μm thick) using doctor blade.
[M0061 p3] After drying at 60 °C in vacuum oven overnight, the electrodes were punched into circular disks with 15 mm diameter.
[M0062 p3] The obtained sulfur mass loading in this work was ∼1 mg cm−2.
[M0063 p3] The fabricated S/KB electrode was used as cathode.
[M0064 p3] Circular disk of Li foil with 15 mm diameter was used as both reference and counter electrode.
[M0065 p3] Using the as prepared electrolyte, the electrochemical performance was evaluated in CR2025-type coin cell configuration.
[M0066 p3] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed with electrochemical workstation (Bio-Logic VSP) with a scanning rate of 0.05 mV s−1 in the voltage range of 1.6∼2.8 V vs Li/Li+.
[M0067 p3] The cycling stability and discharge/charge plots were evaluated using galvanostatic charge discharge technique at the current density of 0.05 C (1 C = 1.672 mA g−1) within the potential range of 1.6∼2.8 V vs Li/Li+.

[M0102 p5] The electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.—To remove the ambiguity regarding performance contribution from other components of the battery, conventional cathode material, sulfur/ Ketjen Black (S/KB) composite containing sulfur weight amount of 60% without any modification (Fig.
[M0103 p5] S1 (available online at stacks. iop.org/JES/169/050515/mmedia)), and the electrolyte with LiTFSI as the salt and AMImTFSI as the solvent were used without any additives.
[M0104 p5] In such simple system, the sole effect of the electrolyte on the electrochemical performances can directly be observed.
[M0105 p5] Considering the problem of self-discharge induced by LPS dissolution, the changes in the OCV of Li–S battery were monitored after the cell fabrication without applying current.
[M0106 p5] As shown in Fig. 2a, the OCV for Li–S cell of 1 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI gradually decreases from initial 2.88 V to 2.67 V, with potential retention of 92.7% after 30 d.
[M0107 p5] In contrast, 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI as electrolyte holds higher potential retention of 96.8% after 30 d.
[M0108 p5] From the comparison of specific values in Table SII, the change of OCV clearly decreased with increased LiTFSI concentration, indicating that increasing salt concentration in IL electrolyte can help suppress self-discharge effect, which is consistent with the results of viscosity and LPS dissolution measurements.
[M0109 p5] Figure 2b compares the cycling performance of Li–S battery in the fabricated IL electrolyte at the current density of 0.05 C (1 C = 1.672 mA g−1) with potential window of 1.6 ∼2.8 V.
[M0110 p5] For 1 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, the capacity shows the lowest value with a fast fading and fluctuant Coulombic efficiency.
[M0111 p5] Improved capacity and cycling stability can be seen when increasing salt concentration.
[M0112 p5] The electrolyte of 2 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI exhibits the highest initial capacity, while a fast capacity fading is still observed.
[M0113 p5] Though a moderate initial capacity of 1071 mAh g−1 is obtained in 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI as electrolyte, a good cyclability with stable capacity of ∼950 mAh g−1 can be achieved during the 50 cycles.
[M0114 p5] The cycling performance of 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI as electrolyte was further assessed at higher current density of 0.1 C in Fig. 2c.
[M0115 p5] Still, a very good cycling stability can be obtained, with capacity retention of 92% after 100 cycles.
[M0116 p5] From the results of OCV, viscosity and LPS dissolution experiment, the enhanced cycling stability in 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI can be attributed to the effective suppression of LPS.
[M0117 p5] The moderate value of initial capacity might be caused by the increased viscosity and decreased ionic conductivity, reducing the reactive kinetics.
[M0118 p5] This speculation can be further supported from the corresponding discharge/charge plots in Figs. 2d–2f.
[M0119 p5] For 3 M LiTFSI in AMImTFSI, the largest voltage hysteresis of 0.51 V is found, indicating the large polarization and reduced redox kinetics.
[M0120 p5] While, a highly overlapping discharge/ charge curves is displayed, reflective of the stable electrochemical reaction.
[M0121 p5] From the above results, it can be concluded that increasing salt concentration likes a double-edged sword for the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery.
[M0122 p5] One the one hand, decreased selfdischarge, improved cycling stability and Coulombic efficiency can be achieved, attributing to the effective suppression of LPS dissolution in the concentrated IL electrolyte.
[M0123 p5] One the other hand, decreased initial capacity is obtained as the increased polarization.
[M0124 p5] It should make a balance between the cyclability and capacity when increasing the salt concentration.

[M0150 p7] Figure 4a–4f compares the high-resolution S 2p, C 1s, F 1s, Li 1s, and N 1s XPS spectra and corresponding atomic concentration ratio of C–F and Li–F.
[M0151 p7] Careful deconvoluting S 2p XPS spectra in Fig. 4a, typical polysulfides species with a doublet of peaks for terminal sulfur (ST 1−) at 161.9 eV and bridging sulfur (SB 0) at 163.8 eV can be observed in all cases.32,33 The terminal and bridging sulfur moieties are resulted from the electrochemical reaction of sulfur cathode during charging (Li2S/Li2S2 →Li2Sx).
[M0152 p7] Besides, a broad peak at 166.0 eV (-SxOy) and a strong peak at 169.5 eV (R–SO2–R) can be obtained in all cases, which is associated with TFSI anion decomposition.34 Such observation indicates that additional reaction from TFSI anion might happen on the sulfur cathode.
[M0153 p7] This hypothesis can be proved by the deconvolution of C 1s XPS spectra in Fig. 4b.
[M0154 p7] In the lower binding energy religion of C 1s, four peaks of 284.5 eV (Caliphalic, C–C/ C–H), 285.5 eV (Chetero, C6/C7), 287.0 eV (Cinter,C4/C5), and 288.8 eV (C2) originated from Ccation in the AMIm cation group are observed,35 implying that AMIm cation also participates in the surface film formation.
[M0155 p7] With increasing the LiTFSI concentration, two peaks located at 290.5 eV and 293.4 eV become stronger, which can be ascribed to C–SO2 and C–F3, respectively.36 This indicates that the TFSI anion decomposition becomes easier with increasing salt concentration, which is well agreed with the DFT calculations suggesting presence of higher EHOMO in the higher concentration electrolyte.
[M0156 p7] The TFSI anion decomposition can also be supported by the F 1s and Li 1s XPS spectra.
[M0157 p7] From the Li 1s XPS spectra in Fig. 4d, the peaks associated the inorganic components of LiF, LixSOy, Li3N and Li2O can be detected, which can be ascribed to the decomposed products from TFSI anion.37 From the F 1s XPS spectra in Fig. 4c, two clear peaks associated with C–F (C–Fx) and Li–F (LiF) bond are exhibited.
[M0158 p7] In addition, the percentage of Li–F shows an increasing tendency with increased LiTFSI concentration in Fig. 4f, which is believed to be good for building a stable SEI due to its high electrochemical stability to suppress the side reaction at the electrode/electrolyte interface.38 The aforementioned reactions of TFSI anion and AMIm cation can also be demonstrated from N 1s spectra, in which the peak at 400.0 eV corresponding to TFSI anion (denoted as Nanion) and the peak at 402.1 eV corresponding to AMIm cation (denoted as Ncation) can be clearly detected.39 On the basis of literatures8 and results of XPS spectra, the cathode surface film formation mechanism in the concentrated IL electrolyte can be proposed in the following manner.
[M0159 p7] Firstly, the TFSI anion is decomposed by breaking of C–S or S–N bond to produce molecular groups like R–SO2–R, –CFx, and further decomposed to small fragments, like LiF, LixSOy, and Li–N.
[M0160 p7] Furthermore, the decomposition of TFSI anion would induce the AMIm cation polymerization on the cathode based on the following reaction.40 From the above results, it can be concluded that the AMImTFSIbased electrolyte with concentrated LiTFSI is helpful to decrease the polysulfide dissolution through two effect.
[M0161 p7] One is the formation of surface film on the sulfur cathode, which is generated by the TFSI anion decomposition and AMIm cation polymerization in the concentrated AMImTFSI-based electrolyte.
