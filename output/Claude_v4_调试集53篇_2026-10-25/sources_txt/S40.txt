[M0004 p1] Kolosnitsyn2 | Damla Eroglu1 1Department of Chemical Engineering, Bogazici University, Istanbul, Turkey | 2Department of Electrochemical Energy, Ufa Institute of Chemistry – Subdivision of the Ufa Federal Research Centre of the Russian Academy of Sciences (UIC UFRC RAS), Ufa, Russia Correspondence: Damla Eroglu (eroglud@bogazici.edu.tr) Received: 24 February 2026 | Revised: 20 April 2026 | Accepted: 23 April 2026 Keywords: E/S ratio | electrochemistry | electrolyte design | lithium-sulfur battery | voltammetry ABSTRACT Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are among the most promising next-generation battery technologies due to their high theoretical specific capacity.
[M0005 p1] Li-S battery performance is intricately linked to materials and cell design, with electrolyte design playing a particularly crucial role owing to the complex mechanisms within the cell.
[M0006 p1] Thus, the primary challenge in realizing highperformance Li-S batteries lies in achieving reasonable capacities and cycling performance at low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratios, which might be attainable through unconventional electrolyte systems.
[M0007 p1] This study aims to explore how varying the amount and composition of electrolytes affects the performance of Li-S batteries across different electrolyte systems.
[M0008 p1] Sulfolane (SL)-Lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) and triglyme (G3)-LiTFSI electrolytes demonstrate superior cycling performance across a range of E/S ratios.
[M0009 p1] Notably, the SL-LiTFSI electrolyte system shows promise under lean electrolyte conditions, demonstrating superior performance at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1, likely due to sulfolane’s electrochemical stability, high dielectric constant, and donor number. 1 | Introduction Lithium-ion batteries are among the most widely used electrochemical storage technologies in various sectors, especially transportation.
[M0010 p1] Even though Li-ion batteries have high energy and power densities and long life, they have high costs and limited capacity for newly emerging technologies such as electric vehicles [1–3].
[M0011 p1] Consequently, researchers seek new battery systems with higher capacity and lower cost.
[M0012 p1] Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have recently been a top research topic on next-generation energy storage technologies due to their high theoretical specific energy that is three to fivefold (nearly 2600 Wh/kg) of Li-ion batteries and the high theoretical specific capacity, 1675 mAh g−1, of their cathode active material, sulfur [4–9].
[M0013 p1] In addition to this, sulfur is low-cost, naturally abundant, and nontoxic [10–13].
[M0014 p1] However, due to the complicated mechanisms within Li-S cells during cycling, several challenges must be handled before commercialization.
[M0015 p1] One of the biggest obstacles is the shuttling of the lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) produced by the complex reactions in the cell [12, 14–16].
[M0016 p1] This shuttle mechanism leads to vital problems, such as the deterioration of the anode and loss of active cathode material [17, 18].
[M0017 p1] In addition to the polysulfide shuttle mechanism, the instability of the Li metal and the insulating nature of S and the final discharge product Li2S, hindering the reaction kinetics, are major concerns.
[M0018 p1] All these obstacles give rise to capacity fading, deficient coulombic efficiency, and short cycle life [1, 19–22].
[M0019 p1] There are different approaches in the literature to overcome these challenges, including engineering the cathode [4, 23–26], using a protective layer on the anode surface [27–29], and modifying the separator [30–34].

[M0024 p2] control the dissolution of the polysulfides and, thus, the reaction and polysulfide shuttle mechanisms.
[M0025 p2] Consequently, the ideal electrolyte should be chemically and electrochemically stable across a wide range of voltage and temperature, have high ionic conductivity (10−3 to 10−2 S cm−1 at room temperature), compatibility with the Li metal, and low viscosity [35].
[M0026 p2] Therefore, the selection of the electrolyte components is highly determinative of battery performance.
[M0027 p2] Conventionally, ether-based electrolytes are used in the cell due to their compatibility with the cathode, low viscosity, and stability toward the polysulfides.
[M0028 p2] However, in ether-based electrolytes, the shuttle effect is severe, and the rapid evaporation of solvent causes safety concerns [36].
[M0029 p2] The most commonly used electrolyte system in Li-S batteries is 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonomide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxy ethane (DME) mixture (1:1 vol%).
[M0030 p2] DOL:DME exhibits excellent polysulfide solubility and strong chemical stability against polysulfides [36].
[M0031 p2] DME, a polar solvent with higher polysulfide solubility, provides high polysulfide kinetics but is more likely to react with lithium.
[M0032 p2] DOL reduces polysulfide solubility but aids in creating a more stable solid electrolyte interface (SEI) on the lithium anode surface [36].
[M0033 p2] A mixture of DOL:DME offers a compromise between electrolyte characteristics such as ionic conductivity, polysulfide solubility, mobility, and the generation of an SEI on the lithium anode.
[M0034 p2] However, the ether solvents’ low flash point creates a variety of safety concerns, mainly when operating at higher temperatures [36, 37].
[M0035 p2] The salt used in the electrolyte should have high ionic conductivity and not lead to unwanted side reactions with the LiPSs.
[M0036 p2] LiTFSI, the most widely used salt in Li-S battery electrolytes, enhances the electrolyte’s electrochemical stability and ionic conductivity.
[M0037 p2] Lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiTF) and lithium perchlorate (LiClO4) are alternative salts that can be used in electrolyte design.
[M0038 p2] The former helps the solubility of shorterlength LiPSs and has high thermal stability, while the latter acts as a prohibitor of the polysulfide shuttle mechanism [38–40].
[M0039 p2] As for the solvents, they should have high solubility for short-chain polysulfides and poor solubility for long-chain polysulfides.
[M0040 p2] Triglyme (G3) is a promising candidate as a solvent since it has high polysulfide solubility and is chemically stable against polysulfides [41].
[M0041 p2] Also, it has lower flammability, flash point, and toxicity than the conventional electrolyte [42].
[M0042 p2] Another promising electrolyte solvent is sulfolane (SL), which can solve the shorter-length polysulfides formed during cycling owing to its high dielectric constant.
[M0043 p2] Sulfolane is also thermally stable and has a high boiling point and chemical stability, allowing the use of reactive salts [38, 43].
[M0044 p2] In addition to electrolyte components, the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio is also a crucial parameter for electrolyte design because it considerably affects the electrochemical performance and system- and cell-level-specific energy and energy density of the Li-S batteries.
[M0045 p2] Also, the E/S ratio plays a significant role in the reactions and the polysulfide shuttle mechanism because of its effect on the polysulfide concentration on the cathode surface [44–46].
[M0046 p2] At very low E/S ratios, the discharge capacity and cycling performance are poor; an increase in the electrolyte viscosity due to the rising polysulfide concentration results in lower ionic conductivity and poor reaction kinetics.
[M0047 p2] Moreover, low E/S ratios may lead to a deficiency of the electrolyte in the cell due to Li metal consuming the electrolyte via unwanted reactions [47].
[M0048 p2] An increase in the electrolyte amount impacts the voltage, cycle life, and capacity of the cell favorably by improving sulfur utilization and reaction kinetics.
[M0049 p2] However, a further increase in the E/S ratio adversely affects capacity retention since it may accelerate the polysulfide shuttle mechanism.
[M0050 p2] High electrolyte amounts also diminish the energy density [8, 35, 45, 46, 48–55].
[M0051 p2] Recently, we developed electrochemical models to reveal the impact of E/S ratio and electrolyte type on battery performance [56].
[M0052 p2] To achieve high energy density at the pack level, which is critical for commercializing these batteries, the E/S ratio should be minimized within the cell.
[M0053 p2] However, as discussed, battery performance decreases severely with decreasing electrolyte amount for the conventional electrolyte system [47, 55, 57–62].
[M0054 p2] Therefore, the bottleneck of achieving high-performance Li-S batteries is to attain reasonable capacities and cycling performance at low E/S ratios, which may be possible using electrolyte systems differing from the conventional one [61, 62].
[M0055 p2] In this regard, this study aims to mechanistically investigate the effect of the electrolyte amount and components on the performance of Li-S batteries with different electrolyte systems. 2 | Results and Discussion Figure 1 shows the cycling performance of Li-S batteries with various electrolyte systems, SL-LiTFSI, SL-LiTF, SL-LiClO4, G3-LiTFSI, and G3-LiTF, at four different E/S ratios.
[M0056 p2] At higher E/S ratios and flooded conditions, cells exhibit initial discharge capacities above 800 mAh g−1.
[M0057 p2] It is observed that Li-S batteries containing SL-LiTFSI and G3-LiTFSI electrolytes demonstrate superior cycling performance compared to other electrolytes at almost all E/S ratios.
[M0058 p2] This superiority may be attributed to the perfect cycling stability and rate capability of the LiTFSI [63].
[M0059 p2] Despite these benefits, LiTFSI may lead to higher viscosity, decreasing ionic conductivity [38, 63–65], especially at low E/S ratios, exacerbating the polysulfide shuttle mechanism [50, 51].
[M0060 p2] This is apparent in Figure 1d, particularly in the G3-LiTFSI electrolyte; at an E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1, the cell with G3-LiTFSI shows inferior performance.
[M0061 p2] The cycling performance of the Li-S cell with SL-LiTFSI is better than that of G3-LiTFSI, most probably due to the electrochemical stability, high dielectric constant, and donor number of sulfolane [38, 63, 66].
[M0062 p2] Sulfolane-based electrolytes have higher coulombic efficiencies (CEs) at all E/S ratios around 90%, whereas glyme-based electrolytes have lower and noisier CEs, demonstrating the high amount of shuttling in Figure S1.

[M0079 p4] The physicochemical and electrochemical properties of the electrolyte change along cycling.
[M0080 p4] During cycling, the dissolution of the polysulfides increases the viscosity of the electrolyte, influencing the ionic conductivity and wettability adversely [67, 68].
[M0081 p4] In addition, the thickness of the SEI layer and the precipitation of the species also change.
[M0082 p4] Correspondingly, the resistances within the cell are affected by these alterations, which influence the performance.
[M0083 p4] The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) method can give us information about these processes inside the cell [69–73].
[M0084 p4] In this regard, Li-S cells with the lowest E/S ratio were also characterized using the EIS method.

[M0121 p6] Sulfur utilization is the measure of how close the high voltage capacity is to the theoretical high voltage capacity (418 mAh g−1), indicating the ability of active solid sulfur material present in the cathode to be turned into polysulfides dissolved in electrolyte.
[M0122 p6] On the other hand, the capacity ratio is the ratio of the low-voltage capacity to the high-voltage capacity, which has a theoretical value of 3, highlighting the reduction of longer-chain polysulfides to the final discharge product Li2S [79].

[M0125 p6] Generally, sulfolane-based electrolytes have higher sulfur utilization than glyme-based electrolytes.

[M0148 p7] The obtained sulfur loading was 1.1–1.3 mg cm−2, and 2.01 cm2 cathode disks were used.
[M0149 p7] Before the preparation of the electrolytes, sulfolane and triglyme solvents were dried by molecular sieves (0.4 nm, Sigma Aldrich), which were freshly calcined.
[M0150 p7] Then, dried solvents were mixed with the required amounts of salts with a magnetic stirrer overnight in a glovebox (MBraun) in which the humidity and oxygen contents were less than 0.5 ppm.
[M0151 p7] Five different electrolyte systems were prepared: 1 M LiTFSI, 1 M LiTF, or 1 M LiClO4 in sulfolane and 1 M LiTFSI or 1 M LiTF in triglyme.
[M0152 p7] To assemble CR2032-type coin cells, metallic lithium anode (2.01 cm2, 170 μm) and polymeric Celgard (3.01 cm2, 25 μm) separators were used under an inert atmosphere.
[M0153 p7] Cells with different E/S ratios of 6, 9, 16, and 23 mL g−1 were prepared for different types of electrolytes, keeping other parameters such as the sulfur loading and carbon-to-sulfur ratio constant.
[M0154 p7] The prepared cells were kept at the open circuit voltage (OCV) for 16 h for stabilization.
[M0155 p7] Galvanostatic cycling was carried out at room temperature by a Neware battery cycler.
[M0156 p7] After resting, each cell was cycled at 0.1 Crate between cut-off voltages of 1.5–2.8 V.
[M0157 p7] For the EIS measurements, cells again rested at the OCV for 16 h.
[M0158 p7] Then, at the end of the rest period and the end of the first, third, fifth, eighth, and tenth discharge, the EIS measurements were conducted within 10 mHz to 1 MHz, with a 10 mV amplitude using Biologic SP 300/300e.
[M0159 p7] Before each EIS measurement, cells rested for 30 min for equilibration.
[M0160 p7] In the EIS characterization, the lowest E/S ratio, 6 mL g−1, was chosen since we are particularly interested in low E/S ratios for practical Li-S batteries.
[M0161 p7] Experiments were conducted with at least three replicates, and the results were reported as averages of replicates.
[M0162 p7] CV analysis was performed between 1.5 and 3.0 V with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 by using Biologic SP 300/300e.
[M0163 p7] The lithium electrodes for morphological characterization were obtained by disassembling the cells in a glovebox and rinsing them with DME to remove loosely bound compounds and lithium salts; they were then dried overnight under an inert atmosphere.
[M0164 p7] They were transferred to the SEM in vacuum containers.
[M0165 p7] The electrode morphologies were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Thermo Scientific, Quattro S), and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was conducted with the SEM system. 4 | Conclusion The impact of the E/S ratio on the electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries with various electrolyte systems was analyzed. 1 M LiTFSI and 1 M LiTF salts in sulfolane and triglyme solvents, and 1 M LiClO4 salt in sulfolane electrolyte systems were tested for E/S ratios of 6, 9, 16, and 23 mL g−1.
[M0166 p7] SL-LiTFSI and G3-LiTFSI electrolytes exhibit superior cycling performance compared to others across various E/S ratios, owing to the exceptional cycling stability and rate capability of LiTFSI.
[M0167 p7] However, at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1, the Li-S cell with SL-LiTFSI demonstrates better cycling performance than G3-LiTFSI, likely due to the electrochemical stability, high dielectric constant, and donor number of sulfolane.
[M0168 p7] Yet, G3-LiTF exhibits the highest discharge capacity and capacity retention at the lowest E/S ratio of 6 mL g−1 across all electrolytes despite suffering from low CE.
[M0169 p7] EIS measurements reveal two semicircles and a linear line, indicative of Warburg diffusion resistance, across all electrolytes.
[M0170 p7] The total cell resistance peaks in the initial cycle and slightly decreases afterward.
[M0171 p7] CV analysis of Li-S cells and morphological investigations of Li anodes cycled with different electrolyte systems also enriched our mechanistic analysis.
[M0172 p7] Overall, the impact of the E/S ratio on discharge capacity and cycling performance varies among different electrolyte systems, highlighting the significant role of the solvent and its combination with lithium salts in battery performance.
[M0173 p7] Designing electrolytes for optimal performance under lean electrolyte conditions is crucial for achieving highperformance Li-S batteries.
[M0174 p7] Notably, the 1 M LiTFSI in sulfolane electrolyte system shows promise, demonstrating superior cycling performance at the lowest E/S ratio.
