[M0013 p1] Introduction Li-S batteries have been studied extensively during the last few decades, yet progress has been slow on critical issues such as high selfdischarge rate, the polysulfide shuttle mechanism leading to a low Coulombic efficiency (CE), and low cycle life [1–3].
[M0014 p1] More than 80% of recent studies have focused on one electrolyte: lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a binary mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with LiNO3 as an additive [1–8].
[M0015 p1] The binary mixture of DOL:DME was originally developed by Mikhaylik and Akridge (Sion Power Corporation) for its excellent rate capability at low temperature, while LiTFSI is used because it is one of the most stable salts in the presence of polysulfides and their corresponding radical reactions [9,10].
[M0016 p1] Since the breakthrough discovery by Mikhaylik in 2008 of the reduced impact of the polysulfide shuttle achieved by the addition of LiNO3 [11], this salt has become a universal additive in nearly every reported Li-S battery study [7,12].
[M0017 p1] The effect of LiNO3 on the passivation of Li metal surfaces has been thoroughly studied [5,13–16], with the surface layer often described as one or more LixNOy species, as first suggested by Aurbach et al.[17] This surface (passivation) layer is commonly referred to as a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) [18].
[M0018 p1] This layer is formed by reactions between the Li metal and electrolyte and its composition is highly dependent on the electrolyte formulation.
[M0019 p1] An ideal SEI should limit or prevent further reactions of the electrolyte, as well as Li metal dendrite growth at the Li surface (during Li metal plating).
[M0020 p1] Another desired property is that the SEI only conducts Li+ ions, while preventing electrons from reducing the electrolyte (i.e., it serves as an electronic insulator).
[M0021 p1] This requires that it have a uniform morphology and composition to ensure a homogeneous current distribution and it should be elastic and flexible to maintain its shape during volume changes which occur upon plating and stripping of the Li metal [18,19].

[M0027 p2] The freshly exposed Li in the cracked regions leads to formation of more SEI by decomposition of the electrolyte, as well as promote dendrite formation.
[M0028 p2] Various approaches, such as the addition of additives or ex-situ formation of an artificial layer, have been explored during the last decade in attempts to achieve the formation of high quality SEIs [20–23].
[M0029 p2] Although it is well established that LiNO3 is critical in preventing the polysulfide shuttle, to the best of our knowledge, a careful study on the effect of this salt's concentration on the performance of Li metal anodes and Li-S batteries and its fate after long-term cycling has yet to be reported.
[M0030 p2] In this work, we show that LiNO3 is very effective in forming an SEI layer on Li metal which is distinguished by the inclusion of Li2O.
[M0031 p2] Electrolytes without LiNO3 do not contain Li2O as an SEI component.
[M0032 p2] The greatly enhanced CE and prolonged cycle life of electrolytes with a high concentration of LiNO3 strongly suggest that Li2O is a beneficial SEI component, acting to prevent excessive decomposition of the electrolyte.
[M0033 p2] During repeated stripping and plating of Li metal, LiNO3 is consumed in a self-limiting manner as it passivates freshly exposed Li surfaces.
[M0034 p2] The morphology of deposited Li metal in the high concentration LiNO3 electrolyte is found to be “nodule-like” with particle sizes in the range of ~5–20 μm.
[M0035 p2] The SEI replicates the underlying “nodule” particle (3D) surface structure and the SEI growth during each cycle results in a layer of porous residual SEI which continuously builds-up.
[M0036 p2] The use of electrolytes containing high concentrations of LiNO3 therefore is a promising means to significantly extend the cycle life of Li-S batteries. 2.

[M0091 p3] Results and discussion 3.1.
[M0092 p3] Design principles for LiNO3-containing electrolytes After testing the CE for Li metal plating/stripping with the state-ofthe-art electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME) with and without LiNO3 (Fig. 1a), we found that a high CE (> 99%) is only achieved when the LiNO3 additive is used.
[M0093 p3] In the work of Li et al. more cycles were obtained with 5 wt% LiNO3 than 1 wt%., suggesting that the LiNO3 additive is consumed during repeated Li plating/stripping cycling [25].
[M0094 p3] In this study, these authors reported that the maximum concentration of LiNO3 in 1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME is approximately 0.75 M [25].
[M0095 p3] As shown by Henderson et al.
[M0096 p3] [26,27] at room temperature, LiNO3 forms a solid crystalline complex when added to pure DME, even for dilute concentrations, and LiNO3 is insoluble in pure DOL.
[M0097 p3] The binary DOL:DME solvent system is thus required to achieve even the relatively low salt dissolution limit of 0.75 M.
[M0098 p3] In contrast, LiNO3 is highly soluble in the higher order glyme solvents with the crystalline solvates formed having a low melting point, thus enabling the preparation of solutions with high concentrations of LiNO3 [27].
[M0099 p3] Although various concentrations of LiNO3 have been examined up to 5 wt% in DOL:DME, the concentration effect of LiNO3 has typically been masked or down-played with respect to the role of dissolved polysulfides on the plating/stripping behavior of Li metal [25,28].
[M0100 p3] The main issue which has hampered studying the LiNO3 concentration effect in DOL:DME is its limited solubility in this solvent system.
[M0101 p3] By switching to a different solvent (diethylene glycol dimethyl ether or diglyme (G2)), we were able to solubilize up to 4 M LiNO3, thus allowing an examination of an extended LiNO3 concentration range relative to DOL:DME (< 0.75 M).
[M0102 p3] The Coulombic efficiency (CE) of Li metal plating and stripping in a series of LiNO3/G2 electrolytes was tested using Cu||Li half cells with Cu serving as the working electrode (Fig. 1b).
[M0103 p3] A roughly linear increase in the number of cycles attained before a decrease in the CE is observed with increasing concentration of LiNO3 from 1 M to 4 M.
[M0104 p3] It should be noted, however, that the maximum CE achieved with LiNO3/G2 mixtures is only ~ 96%.
[M0105 p3] This is notably lower than what is observed in Fig. 1a for the 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolyte and what has been reported for 1 M LiTFSI + x wt% LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolytes [25,28].

[M0113 p4] As displayed in Fig. 1c, both solvent systems (DOL:DME and G2) can achieve a high CE with a low concentration of LiNO3 (0.3 M), although the number of cycles is limited.
[M0114 p4] The DOL:DME solvent system gives a slightly longer cycle life compared to G2, however, in the present work, we will focus on the effect of LiNO3 salt rather than the solvent system.
[M0115 p4] The use of LiTFSI as a co-salt is more critical to Li plating/stripping than the solvent selection.
[M0116 p4] This phenomena can be attributed to the more facile solvation and desolvation of Li+ cations in the presence of the TFSI- anion (e.g., its weaker coordination strength and reduced ionic association as compared to NO3 -) [27].
[M0117 p4] Mixtures of LiTFSI and LiNO3 were added to G2 in different ratios and the CE was tested in Cu||Li cells.
[M0118 p4] The electrolyte was optimized for maximum number of cycles at high CE and this optimized composition was found to be 0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3 in G2.
[M0119 p4] This is a saturated mixture of LiTFSI and LiNO3 in G2 and corresponds to roughly 0.6 mol of LiTFSI and 3.6 mol of LiNO3 per kg of G2 (i.e., 0.6 molal LiTFSI and 3.6 molal LiNO3).
[M0120 p4] Fig. 1d compares the CE for the most widely used electrolyte for Li-S batteries (1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME) to the new electrolyte formula (0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3 in G2).

[M0122 p4] By increasing the concentration of LiNO3 by tenfold in the new electrolyte, Li can be cycled for > 200 cycles at > 99% CE. 3.2.

[M0250 p7] The combination of high CE Li plating/stripping and a high concentration of dissolved polysulfides allows us to cycle Li-S batteries with the active sulfur originating in solution.
[M0251 p7] This concept has been tested in the past [41–44], but was found to have limitations of low cycle life and low energy densities, owing to the limited CE of the Li plating/stripping and limited concentration of soluble active S.
[M0252 p7] With the electrolyte formulation reported here, however, we are able to cycle Li-S cells with carbon electrodes (containing no sulfur initially) with a low capacity fade rate and ~ 100% CE (Fig. 6).
[M0253 p7] The first cycle is excluded in Fig. 6a because the Li2S8 in the electrolyte was oxidized first to elemental sulfur (S8 2-→S8 + 2 e-) before full capacity (based on the initial S content in the electrolyte) and stable cycling could occur.

[M0256 p7] This is thought to be due to the dissolution of a certain portion of sulfur, in the form of polysulfides, from the cathode into the electrolyte during discharging/charging which is not able to deposit back onto/into the cathode.
[M0257 p7] The redistribution and loss of sulfur in the cathode can be completely avoided by starting with sulfur in the electrolyte.

[M0259 p7] Using this method, the limiting factor is the capacity of the sulfur (or Li2S) with which the carbon electrodes can maintain a sufficient electrically conductive network.
[M0260 p7] This is in contrast to traditional methods, starting with a sulfur (or Li2S) loaded cathode, which are typically filled with excess S (or Li2S) which is electrochemically inactive due to a lack of electrical and/or ionic contact (with the current collector and electrolyte, respectively).

[M0273 p8] This layer not only suppresses the reduction of the electrolyte co-salt, LiTFSI, and the solvent (G2 in the present study), it also clearly inhibits the reduction of soluble polysulfide species by the Li metal anode.
[M0274 p8] This, in turn, prevents the polysulfide shuttle mechanism and results in nearly a 100% CE for Li-S cells cycled with a catholyte (i.e., S active material dissolved in the electrolyte) and remarkable capacity retention.
[M0275 p8] Here, we have shown that LiNO3 is consumed during cycling by chemical reactivity with Li metal.
[M0276 p8] When used as an additive (i.e., in small amounts), as is the case for the state-of-the-art DOL:DME electrolyte, the beneficial effect of LiNO3 in stabilizing the SEI is limited.
[M0277 p8] The SEI forms (and reforms) during each cycle, which relatively rapidly consumes all of the additive.
[M0278 p8] Li-S batteries with a high CE after extensive cycling are enabled, however, by an electrolyte which contains a high concentration of LiNO3 in addition to a second supporting salt to increase the ionic conductivity (LiTFSI).
[M0279 p8] The high concentration of LiNO3 serves two purposes: 1) to passivate the Li metal negative electrode, thus enabling > 99% CE for Li plating/stripping for an extended number of cycles, and 2) to increase the solubility of Li polysulfides (Li2Sn where 4 ≤n ≤8), thereby allowing the discharge/ charge process to occur via the liquid phase (electrolyte) rather than the reversible solid-state conversion of sulfur (S) to Li sulfide (Li2S).
