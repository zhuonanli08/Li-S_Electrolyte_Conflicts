[M0008 p1] Introduction The low-altitude economy constitutes a crucial driving force for future economy growth, where unmanned aerial vehicles serve as key devices for transportation and logistics [1,2].
[M0009 p1] To meet the long-range operation requirements, the energy density of rechargeable batteries is expected to exceed 600 Wh kg−1 [3].
[M0010 p1] The current commercial lithium-ion batteries can hardly achieve an actual energy density beyond 400 Wh kg−1 due to the limitations of the lithium ion interca­ lation reaction mechanism [4–7].
[M0011 p1] To this end, developing advanced rechargeable batteries with practical high energy density is highly required to meet these requirements [8,9].
[M0012 p1] Lithium‒sulfur (Li‒S) batteries afford an ultra-high theoretical en­ ergy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, making them one of the most promising candidates as next-generation high-energy-density recharge battery systems [10–13].
[M0013 p1] Typical Li‒S batteries rely on solid‒liquid‒solid conversion between elemental sulfur, dissolved lithium polysulfides (LiPSs), and solid lithium sulfide (Li2S) in ether-based electrolyte [14–16].
[M0014 p1] The soluble LiPSs avoid direct conversion between the elec­ tronic and ionic insulated sulfur and Li2S and facilitate their deposition and dissolution by chemical disproportionation and comproportionation reactions, thereby contributing to high utilization of the cathode active materials and high energy density of Li‒S batteries [17–19].
[M0015 p1] However, the dissolved LiPSs chemically react with lithium metal and inevitably corrode the lithium anode continuously, which is widely acknowledged to cause the shuttle effect [20–22].
[M0016 p1] The corrosion of lithium anode by the dissolved LiPSs irreversibly consumes the limited active lithium reservoir [23], and more importantly, generates low-valence sulfur species on surface of lithium anode to induce uneven lithium deposition and massive generation of inactive lithium [24–26].
[M0017 p1] The severe corro­ sion of lithium anode by the dissolved LiPSs results in rapid failure of Li‒ S batteries and restricts their practical application.
[M0018 p1] The dissolved LiPSs are solvated in electrolyte.
[M0019 p1] The solvation struc­ ture of LiPSs greatly influences the reaction kinetics of LiPSs and the Li–S battery performances [27–30].

[M0026 p2] Typically, weakly-solvating electrolyte is constructed by introducing low donor number (DN) co-solvents.
[M0027 p2] For instance, Kaskel and co-workers constructed a weakly-solvating electrolyte by using a 1,1,2,2-tetrafluorethyl-2,2,3, 3-tetrafluorpropyl ether co-solvent [34].
[M0028 p2] The cycling lifespan of Li‒S pouch cells was prolonged by three times accordingly.
[M0029 p2] Hou et al. further studied the solvation structure of LiPSs in weakly-solvating electrolyte by partially replacing conventional high-DN 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) solvents with a low-DN di-isopropyl sulfide (DIPS) co-solvent [35].
[M0030 p2] A LiPS encapsulating solvation structure was identified via molecular dynamics simulation, mainly constructed by a DME-dominated inner solvation shell and a DIPS-dominated outer solvation shell.
[M0031 p2] A longer cycling lifespan of 100 cycles was achieved in Li‒S pouch cells with the help of this weakly-solvating electrolyte.
[M0032 p2] Up to the present, weakly-solvating Li–S batteries have demonstrated promi­ nent advantages in realizing long cycling lifespan under practical working conditions.
[M0033 p2] Generally, the weakly-solvating electrolytes protect the lithium anode without harming the sulfur redox kinetics under flooded elec­ trolyte conditions as the LiPSs are fully solvated to guarantee smooth sulfur redox kinetics [36,37].
[M0034 p2] However, massive electrolyte usage inevitably reduces the actual energy density of Li‒S batteries [38].
[M0035 p2] The electrolyte usage is usually described by the ratio of electrolyte volume to sulfur mass (E/S ratio) [39].
[M0036 p2] Li‒S batteries can hardly achieve an energy density over 400 Wh kg−1 when the E/S ratio is higher than 5 μL mg−1 [40].
[M0037 p2] Obviously, lean-electrolyte conditions are the prerequisite to achieve practical high-energy-density Li‒S batteries.
[M0038 p2] Nevertheless, lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries suffer from highly sluggish sulfur redox kinetics due to high LiPS concentration or even LiPS saturation, and the actual performances are greatly degraded regarding the discharge ca­ pacity, cell polarization, and rate responses [41–44].
[M0039 p2] The above chal­ lenges are even more severe in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries due to the lower solvating power of the electrolyte with low-DN co-solvents [45], leading to unexpected rapid cell failure due to the dramatic degradation of the sulfur redox kinetics [46–48].
[M0040 p2] To develop Li‒S bat­ teries with both high energy density and long cycling lifespan, in-depth kinetics evaluation and targeted kinetic promotion strategies are essential for the weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0041 p2] Herein, the sulfur redox kinetics under lean-electrolyte conditions is systematically evaluated in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries to guide targeted kinetic promotion strategy design.
[M0042 p2] Typical weakly-solvating electrolyte is constructed by using DIPS as the low-DN co-solvent.
[M0043 p2] Tak­ ing kinetic polarization as the key indicator, a galvanostatic intermittent titration technique combined electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (GITT‒EIS) method is employed to decouple the total polarization into activation, concentration, and ohmic polarizations under different E/S ratios.
[M0044 p2] By excluding the minimal anode polarization, the cathode acti­ vation polarization at the Li2S nucleation stage increases by 640 % as the electrolyte usage decreases and occupies 61 % of the total polarization under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0045 p2] Furthermore, the activation energy of the interfacial charge transfer process of Li2S nucleation increases by 29 % at higher LiPS concentration, together identifying the cathode inter­ facial charge transfer kinetics at the Li2S nucleation stage as the main limiting factor.
[M0046 p2] Due to the reduced surface reaction rate at higher sulfur concentration, the Li2S nucleation changes from a three-dimensional diffusion-controlled hemisphere morphology to a two-dimensional sur­ face reaction-controlled needle morphology.
[M0047 p2] Accordingly, the size of the Li2S nuclei decreases from 184 to 63 nm and the thickness of the Li2S deposition layer decreases from 262 to 187 nm as the sulfur concen­ tration increases, in correspondence to the degraded deposition capacity by 41 %.
[M0048 p2] To accelerate the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox kinetics in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries, a lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imidebased electrolyte (LiFSI electrolyte) is designed to achieve a high discharge capacity of 984 mAh g−1 and 50 stable cycles at a high rate of 0.2 C under a low E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1.
[M0049 p2] Furthermore, an actual energy density of 410 Wh kg−1 is achieved in 5 Ah Li‒S pouch cells with the LiFSI electrolyte.
[M0050 p2] This work identifies the surface reaction-controlled Li2S deposition kinetics as the main kinetic challenge and highlights the LiPS solvation structure regulation strategy to accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics in lean-electrolyte weakly-solvating Li–S batteries. 2.
[M0051 p2] Results and discussion The weakly-solvating electrolyte was constructed by using DIPS as the low-DN co-solvent, and the thermodynamics and kinetics of Li‒S batteries using the weakly-solvating electrolyte were systematically analyzed under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0052 p2] Li‒S coin cells with the cathode sulfur loading of 4.0 mgS cm−2 and gradually decreased E/S ratios of 12, 8, 6, 5, or 4.5 μL mg−1 were assembled.
[M0053 p2] GITT at a current density of 0.1 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) was conducted on the coin cells to decouple the thermodynamics and kinetics (Figure S1‒S5).
[M0054 p2] Concretely, the thermodynamic voltage curves were obtained via connecting the final voltage points of each relaxation stage along the degree of discharge or charge and the connection of the final voltage points of each galvanostatic discharge or charge stage constituted the kinetic voltage curves (Fig. 1a).
[M0055 p2] For the discharge process, the thermodynamic voltage of the first discharge plateau slightly decreases as the electrolyte volume decreases, which is resulted from increased LiPS concentration in electrolyte [49].
[M0056 p2] Besides, the thermodynamic voltage of the second discharge plateau is basically overlapped at 2.173 V under different E/S ratio conditions.
[M0057 p2] Meantime, the variation of the thermodynamic voltage during the charge processes is basically the reverse of the discharge process under different E/S ratios.
[M0058 p2] Therefore, the lean-electrolyte con­ ditions have little effect on the thermodynamics of weakly-solvating Li‒ S batteries.
[M0059 p2] The kinetic polarization under different E/S ratios was further analyzed by calculating the difference between the thermodynamic voltage and the kinetic voltage.
[M0060 p2] When the E/S ratio decreases from 12 to 6 μL mg−1, the discharge polarizations are basically not changed at around 100 mV at the Li2S nucleation stage (Figure S6), suggesting the electrolyte amount is enough for smooth cathode reactions within this flooded E/S ratio range.
[M0061 p2] However, when the E/S ratio decreases from 6 to 4.5 μL mg−1, the discharge kinetic polarization rapidly increases from 96 to 300 mV at the Li2S nucleation stage and the kinetic voltage reaches as low as 1.868 V.
[M0062 p2] Comparingly, the kinetic voltage curves during the charge process are basically overlapped under different E/S ratios, indicating the kinetics of the charge process is barely affected by the electrolyte amount variation (Figure S7).
[M0063 p2] Therefore, the sluggish ki­ netics of the discharge process, especially at the Li2S nucleation stage, is the main problem of weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under leanelectrolyte conditions.
[M0064 p2] The kinetics of the sulfur cathode and the lithium anode together contribute to the total kinetic polarization of Li‒S batteries.
[M0065 p2] The kinetic polarization of lithium anode under lean-electrolyte conditions was further estimated to quantify the portion of anode polarization within the total kinetic polarization.
[M0066 p2] Li | Li symmetric cells with 0, 1, or 4 M[S] LiPS electrolyte were assembled and cycled at a current density of 0.72 mA cm−2 equaling to the current density of the Li‒S coin cells at 0.1 C (Fig. 1b).
[M0067 p2] The bulk resistances of the Li | Li symmetric cells were determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) mea­ surements and the anode kinetic polarization under different sulfur concentrations was compared after eliminating the ohmic polarization (Figure S8‒S10, see more details in Supplementary Information).
[M0068 p2] The anode kinetic polarizations under sulfur concentrations of 0, 1, and 4 M[S] are all smaller than 16 mV, which only occupies 5 % of the total kinetic polarization maximally (Fig. 1c).
[M0069 p2] Therefore, the high kinetic polarization of weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions is resulted from the sluggish cathode kinetics.
[M0070 p2] To reveal the key kinetic limiting factor of the sulfur cathode in leanelectrolyte weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries, the cathode kinetic polari­ zation was further decoupled.

[M0099 p4] The kinetic polarization at the sulfur dissolution (10 % DOD) and the Li2S deposition stages (70 % DOD) were also analyzed.
[M0100 p4] At the sulfur dissolution stage, the ηac increases from 17 to 53 mV when the E/S ratio decreases from 6 to 4.5 μL mg−1 and is 13 and 33 mV higher than the ηcon and ηohm at the E/S ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1, respectively (Figure S19).
[M0101 p4] The ηac is also the dominant kinetic polarization component.
[M0102 p4] At the Li2S growth stage, the three polarizations are generally stable at different E/S ratios (Figure S20), indicating the overall kinetics at the Li2S growth stage is not violently affected by the electrolyte amount.
[M0103 p4] To further confirm the sluggish kinetics of the cathode interfacial charge transfer process under lean-electrolyte working conditions, the activation energy at the Li2S nucleation stage under different sulfur concentrations was measured according to the Arrhenius relationship.
[M0104 p4] Three-electrode Li | Li2S8 cells were assembled with 2, 4, or 6 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte, where a lithium coated copper wire served as the reference electrode.
[M0105 p4] The sulfur mass in the Li | Li2S8 cells with different sulfur concentration electrolytes was the same by controlling the electrolyte addition amount.
[M0106 p4] The Li | Li2S8 cells were discharged to the Li2S nucleation stage and the EIS spectra of the cells in a wide temperature range were recorded.
[M0107 p4] Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was then conducted to decouple the interfacial charge transfer process from the overall cathodic electrochemical processes (Figure S21‒S23) [51–53].
[M0108 p4] The cathode interfacial charge transfer activation energy was obtained through the Arrhenius plots (Fig. 2c).
[M0109 p4] The activation energy reaches 193 kJ mol−1 in 6 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte, which is 43 kJ mol−1 higher than that in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte.
[M0110 p4] The increased activation energy as the sulfur concentration increases demonstrates an exponen­ tial decline of the reaction rate coefficient under lean-electrolyte con­ ditions according to the Arrhenius equation.
[M0111 p4] In summary, ηac is the dominant kinetic polarization, and the interfacial charge transfer ki­ netics is confirmed as the critical limiting factor of weakly-solvating Li‒ S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0112 p4] To further correlate the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox kinetics with the cathode active material utilization, the Li2S nucleation and deposi­ tion behaviors at different sulfur concentrations were observed.
[M0113 p4] Li | Li2S8 cells with the sulfur concentration of 2, 4, or 6 M[S] Li2S8 were disassembled at the Li2S nucleation stage.
[M0114 p4] The Li2S nucleation mor­ phologies were observed via scanning electron microscope (SEM, Fig. 3a).
[M0115 p4] The deposited Li2S is hemispherical at the sulfur concentration of 2 M[S], while the morphology changes to needle-like when the sulfur concentration increases to 4 and 6 M[S].
[M0116 p4] The nucleus size of Li2S was further quantitatively analyzed (Figure S24‒S26).
[M0117 p4] The average Li2S nucleus size in 6 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte is 63 nm and is 66 % smaller than that in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Fig. 3b).
[M0118 p4] The lower Li2S nucleation re­ action rate results in the Li2S nucleus changes from a diffusioncontrolled three-dimensional hemisphere-like morphology to a surface reaction-controlled two-dimensional needle-like morphology.
[M0119 p4] Controlled by the surface reaction, only a little amount of LiPS is able to be electrochemically reduced and form Li2S nuclei with smaller size on the conductive surface, which results in larger kinetic polarization to stabilize the small Li2S nuclei.
[M0120 p4] The Li2S deposition morphologies at the end of discharge were further studied.
[M0121 p4] The Li2S deposition keeps the hemisphere-like morphology in 2 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Figure S27).
[M0122 p4] Surprisingly, a hemisphere-like morphology composited by the needle-like Li2S is eventually formed in 4 M[S] Li2S8 electrolyte (Figure S28).
[M0123 p4] When the sulfur concentration increases to 6 M[S], the needle-like Li2S constitutes the bulk with irregular shape (Figure S29).
[M0124 p4] The deposition amount of Li2S is evaluated by comparing the Li2S deposition thickness (Figure S30).
[M0125 p4] The deposition thickness of Li2S at the sulfur concentration of 2 M[S] is 262 nm and decreases to 226 and 187 nm at the sulfur concentration of 4 and 6 M[S], respectively (Fig. 3c and Figure S31‒ S32).
[M0126 p4] The thinner Li2S deposition layer at higher sulfur concentration is resulted from higher degree of surface reaction-controlled Li2S deposi­ tion and corresponding two-dimensional Li2S deposition.
[M0127 p4] Thinner deposition thickness brings more rapid conductive surface coverage by the insulating Li2S deposits, thereby blocking the subsequent reactions.
[M0128 p4] To validate the above argument, Li | Li2S8 cells with the same sulfur mass but different sulfur concentration of 2, 4, or 6 M[S] were poten­ tiostatically discharged at 2.10 V (Figure S33‒S35).

[M0137 p5] Li salt anions are able to coordinate with LiPSs and are expected to accelerate the sulfur redox kinetics in weakly-solvating electrolyte under lean-electrolyte conditions [54–56].
[M0138 p5] A LiFSI electrolyte was pre­ pared and applied in weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte working conditions.
[M0139 p5] The rate performances of Li‒S batteries with the LiFSI electrolyte or the conventional lithium bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide-based electrolyte (LiTFSI electrolyte) were firstly evaluated in Li‒S coin cells with the cathode sulfur loading of 4.0 mgS cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 4.5 μL mg−1.
[M0140 p5] The Li‒S cells with the LiFSI electrolyte cycle stably at 0.3 C (1 C = 1672 mA g−1) and deliver a high discharge capacity of 798 mAh g−1 (Fig. 4a, S36, and S37).
[M0141 p5] Com­ paringly, the Li‒S cells with the LiTFSI electrolyte can only work at 0.1 C so that the maximum cyclable current density is improved by twice.
[M0142 p5] Besides, the Li‒S cells deliver a high discharge capacity of 980 mAh g−1 at the current density of 0.1 C in the LiFSI electrolyte, which is 180 mAh g−1 higher than that in the LiTFSI electrolyte.
[M0143 p5] The medium voltage of the Li‒S cells was further analyzed to demonstrate the kinetic advantages of the LiFSI electrolyte (Figure S38).
[M0144 p5] At high current densities of 0.2 and 0.3 C, the medium voltages in the LiFSI electrolyte are 1.90 and 1.82 V, while the Li‒S cells fail to overcome the huge polarization at the Li2S nucleation stage in the LiTFSI electrolyte.
[M0145 p5] Therefore, the LiFSI electro­ lyte successfully accelerates the interfacial charge transfer kinetics of weakly-solvating Li–S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0146 p5] Furthermore, the long-cycling capability of the LiFSI electrolyte was evaluated.
[M0147 p5] Li‒S coin cells with the LiFSI electrolyte at the E/S ratio of 5.0 μL mg−1 were assembled and cycled at the current density of 0.2 C (Figure S39).
[M0148 p5] The LiTFSI electrolyte can hardly guarantee smooth sulfur redox kinetics at high current density, and the Li‒S cells with the LiTFSI electrolyte fail immediately at 0.2 C.
[M0149 p5] Comparingly, the Li‒S cells with the LiFSI electrolyte achieve a high discharge capacity of 984 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C and cycle stably for 50 times before the capacity reaches 60 % of the initial capacity.
[M0150 p5] The battery performances were further conducted using high sulfur loading cathodes of 7.0 mgS cm−2.
[M0151 p5] A maximum cycling rate of 0.25 C is achieved in the LiFSI electrolyte while the LiTFSI electrolyte only supports a maximum cycling rate of 0.15 C.
[M0152 p5] When the cycling rate changes back to 0.1 C, 15 stable cycles are achieved with the help of the LiFSI electrolyte, while the weakly-solvating Li‒S cells with the LiTFSI electrolyte fail rapidly after only one cycle at 0.1 C (Fig. 4b).
[M0153 p5] To demonstrate the kinetic promotion advantage of LiFSI, perfor­ mances of Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte were further eval­ uated.
[M0154 p5] Rate performances were first evaluated in 3 Ah Li‒S pouch cells (Figure S40).
[M0155 p5] The Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte delivers high discharge capacities of 1219, 1139, 1099, 1080, and 939 mAh g−1 at 0.025, 0.05, 0.08, 0.1, and 0.15 C, respectively (Fig. 4c).
[M0156 p5] In comparison, the Li‒S pouch cell with the LiTFSI electrolyte fails rapidly at 0.05 C and the discharge capacity is less than 200 mAh g−1 at higher rates due to the sluggish kinetics (Figure S41). 5 Ah Li‒S pouch cells with the LiFSI electrolyte were assembled and a high discharge capacity of 1150 mAh g−1 is delivered, which is 855 mAh g−1 higher than that in the LiTFSI electrolyte (Figure S42).
[M0157 p5] The actual energy density of the Li‒S pouch cell is 410 Wh kg−1 based on the total mass (Table S1 and S2).
[M0158 p5] Furthermore, an 8 Ah Li‒S pouch cell with the LiFSI electrolyte was assembled and a high discharge capacity of 1260 mAh g−1 is achieved at the 1st cycle, contributing to an actual energy density of 474 Wh kg−1 (Fig. 4d).
[M0159 p5] The high-energy-density pouch cell maintains 20 cycles and delivers an actual energy density of 412 Wh kg−1, during which the Coulombic ef­ ficiency is stable around 99 % (Fig. 4e and S43).
[M0160 p5] The performance stands out as one of the best pouch cells among the recent reports (Fig. 4f and Table S3).
[M0161 p5] Therefore, Li‒S batteries with both high energy density and long cycling lifespan can be achieved by the LiFSI electrolyte that effectively accelerates the sulfur redox kinetics in weakly-solvating Li–S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions. 3.
[M0162 p5] Conclusion In conclusion, systematic cathode kinetic analysis is conducted on weakly-solvating Li‒S batteries under lean-electrolyte conditions.

[S0070 p7] Lithium anodes were fabricated by rolling lithium metal foils on both sides of Cu foils and then cut into electrodes of the same size as the sulfur cathodes.
[S0071 p7] The thickness of the lithium foil was 75 μm on a single side.
[S0072 p7] The separator used in pouch cells was PP membranes or PE membranes.
[S0073 p7] The pouch cell electrolyte was the LiFSI electrolyte or the LiTFSI electrolyte.
[S0074 p7] To prove the kinetic promotion effect of LiFSI, Li‒S pouch cells were assembled for performances evaluation.
[S0075 p7] Five sulfur cathodes (7.8 mgS cm−2 on a single side) and six lithium anodes were stacked layer-by-layer and separated by PP membranes.
[S0076 p7] All the electrodes were set in parallel connection and then put in a package.
[S0077 p7] Then, the LiFSI electrolyte or the LiTFSI electrolyte was injected into the pouch cells and the E/S ratio was 2.8 gele gS−1.
[S0078 p7] After that, the 3 Ah Li‒S pouch cells were vacuumed (‒65 kPa) and sealed for further electrochemical tests.
[S0079 p7] The 3 Ah Li‒S pouch cells were discharged at 0.025 C at the 1st cycle for activation after resting for 12.0 h.
[S0080 p7] Then the Li‒S pouch cells were discharged at 0.05, 0.08, 0.1, and 0.15 C for two cycles and the charge current density was kept at 0.025 C (1 C = 1000 mA g−1).
[S0081 p7] Furthermore, 5 Ah Li‒S pouch cells were assembled with nine sulfur cathodes (7.9 mgS cm−2 on a single side) and ten lithium anodes using PE separators and the E/S ratio was 2.5 gele gS−1.
[S0082 p7] The 5 Ah pouch cells were discharged at 0.05 C after resting for 12.0 h.
[S0083 p7] The 8 Ah Li‒S pouch cell was assembled with fifteen sulfur cathodes (7.5 mgS cm−2 on a single side) and sixteen lithium anodes using PE separators and the E/S ratio was 2.8 gele gS−1.
[S0084 p7] The 8 Ah Li‒S pouch cell was cycled at 0.025 C after resting for 12.0 h.
