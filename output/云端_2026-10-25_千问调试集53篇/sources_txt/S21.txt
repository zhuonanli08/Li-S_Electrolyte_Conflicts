[M0001 p1] RESEARCH ARTICLE www.advenergymat.de Tuning Polysulfides into Clustered-States via Non-coordinating Molecular Encapsulation to Achieve an Alternative Kinetics in Li─S Batteries Qingyi Zheng, Leyuan Cui, Xiaojiao Zhao, Ruming Yuan, Chutao Wang, Kun Wang, Jingmin Fan, Mingsen Zheng,* and Quanfeng Dong* The practical application of lithium–sulfur batteries is hindered by the polysulfide shuttle effect and sluggish kinetics inherent in solid–liquid–solid conversion mechanism, particularly under lean electrolyte conditions (<5 μL mg−1).
[M0002 p1] Weakly solvating electrolytes and localized high-concentration electrolytes can suppress polysulfide dissolution and enable a quasi-solid- -phase mechanism but suffer from severely limited reaction kinetics.
[M0003 p1] Herein, a clustered-polysulfide-mediated sulfur conversion mechanism enabled by a novel electrolyte composed of 1,2-dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) is proposed.
[M0004 p1] The encapsulation effect of DTL and the coordination of TFSI−with polysulfides drives the aggregation of polysulfides so that the clustered polysulfides with virtual shell boundaries can be the new basic reactive that bridges the gap between the traditional dissolution-dominated mechanism and quasi-solid-state mechanism.
[M0005 p1] The clustered polysulfide electrolyte (CPE) not only suppresses the shuttle effect and stabilizes the lithium anode by mitigating parasitic reactions but also enables alternative reaction kinetics and promotes 3D Li2S deposition, minimizing electrode passivation.
[M0006 p1] Ultimately, lithium–sulfur batteries can achieve excellent electrochemical performance and can stably operate under lean electrolyte- (<4.0 μL mg−1) with an area capacity of >4 mAh cm−2.
[M0007 p1] This work elucidates the relationship between polysulfide dissolution behavior and redox kinetics, providing a new insight into the understanding of complex sulfur conversion mechanisms. 1.

[M0014 p2] Current strategies for weakly solvating electrolytes primarily focus on suppressing polysulfide solubility,[6b] but the regulatory mechanism of dynamic polysulfide dissolution states on the cathode sulfur reaction remains rarely explored.
[M0015 p2] Herein, guided by the idea of regulating the state of reactive species, we developed a novel electrolyte with low salt concentration (1 mol L−1) and low density (1.1 g cm−3) by employing 1,2- dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as solvents and achieved a clustered polysulfides-mediated sulfur conversion mechanism.
[M0016 p2] The encapsulation effect of DTL with the ligand-mediated action of bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI−) drives the formation of polysulfide clusters in the electrolyte, thus realizing a clustered polysulfide encapsulation mechanism intermediate between the conventional solidliquid dissolution mechanism and the quasi-solid-solid conversion mechanism.
[M0017 p2] Clustered polysulfide electrolyte (CPE) not only significantly suppresses polysulfide solubility but also optimizes Li2S deposition behavior, guiding the formation of 3D Li2S particles that minimize conductive electrode coverage.
[M0018 p2] Furthermore, the CPE effectively mitigates side reactions between polysulfides and lithium metal, thereby stabilizing the Li anode interface.
[M0019 p2] As a result, the lithium–sulfur batteries exhibit exceptional reaction kinetics and electrochemical reversibility, achieving a remarkable coulombic efficiency of 99.6% and delivering a high capacity of 718 mAh g−1 at 5 C.
[M0020 p2] Moreover, the batteries can stably operate under lean electrolyte conditions with electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio (E/S) < 4.0 μL mg−1, highlighting its potential for practical highenergy-density applications. 2.

[M0028 p3] The minimum electrostatic potentials (ESPmin) of DTL and other ether solvents were calculated, as shown in Figure 1b.
[M0029 p3] Higher ESPmin and more dispersed charges of DTL suggest that the coordination between DTL and lithium-ion is difficult.
[M0030 p3] Due to its weakly polar nature, DTL induces phase separation between DME and DTL when the DTL content exceeds 60%.
[M0031 p3] Therefore, a 5% volume fraction of tetrahydrofuran (THF) was added to the electrolyte to form a homogeneous and stable solution.[11a] The electrolyte formulations with different DTL contents are shown in Table S2 (Supporting Information).
[M0032 p3] Specifically, 1 mol L−1 lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) was dissolved in the mixture solution of DME/THF = 95/5 (v/v) and DME/THF/DTL = 35/5/60 (v/v), denoted as 0DTL electrolyte and 60DTL electrolyte, respectively.
[M0033 p3] The numbers represent the volume fraction of DTL in electrolytes.
[M0034 p3] The dissolution of polysulfides is closely related to the shuttle effect in lithium–sulfur batteries.
[M0035 p3] The solubility of sulfur atoms in different electrolytes was determined by the Li2S6 dissolution experiment.
[M0036 p3] As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), lithium polysulfides are completely insoluble in pure DTL solvent and the solution still appears clear and transparent.
[M0037 p3] In contrast, lithium polysulfides dissolved heavily in 0DTL and the solution appeared dark black.
[M0038 p3] With DTL content increasing, the solubility of lithium polysulfide decreases and the color gradually changes from dark black to red.
[M0039 p3] UV–vis spectroscopy was further performed to obtain the solubility of polysulfides in different electrolytes, as shown in Figure 1c.
[M0040 p3] As shown in Table S3 (Supporting Information), the solubility of sulfur atoms in 50DTL, 60DTL, and 70DTL was 305, 247, and 101 mmol L−1, respectively.
[M0041 p3] Among them, the sulfur solubility of 60DTL is only ≈5% of that in conventional ether electrolytes (4000 mmol L−1).[2d] Consequently, the dissolution of polysulfides is significantly reduced due to the reduced electrolyte solvating capacity after introducing DTL.
[M0042 p3] To reveal the driving effect of the DTL, the 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Raman spectroscopy were performed to examine the different electrolytes with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI as lithium salt.
[M0043 p3] Raman spectra show that the C─O─C vibration peak of free DME molecules at 847 cm−1 diminishes with the increasing volume fraction of DTL from 0% to 80% (Figure S2, Supporting Information), indicating a decrease in the free DME solvents and an increase in the liganded DME solvents.
[M0044 p3] As shown in Figure 1d, the coordination of lithium ions with TFSI−anions is enhanced with increasing DTL volume fraction.
[M0045 p3] It leads to an increase in the electron cloud density of lithium atoms and an increase in the shielding effect, which in turn leads to a decrease in the chemical shift of 7Li NMR.
[M0046 p3] The 7Li NMR peak exhibits the lowest chemical shift when the DTL content is 50–60%.
[M0047 p3] In this case, the strongest encapsulation effect of DTL can drive aggregation of TFSI−and Li+.
[M0048 p3] However, excessive DTL molecules (>60%, v/v) make insufficient solvents for coordination with lithium ions, instead destroying the coordination environment of lithium ions, which in turn results in an increase in the chemical shift of 7Li peak.
[M0049 p3] Similarly, the electrolyte with Li2S6 as the only lithium salt also shows a decreasing chemical shift of 7Li peak with increasing DTL content, further demonstrating the driving effect of DTL (Figure S3, Supporting Information). 2.2.
[M0050 p3] Clustered-Polysulfides Species in CPE In lithium–sulfur batteries, lithium polysulfide intermediates possess important roles in the sulfur reduction reaction.
[M0051 p3] As shown in Figure 2a, lithium polysulfides tend to exist as a single neutral molecule in the 0DTL electrolyte (conventional ether electrolyte, noted as CE).
[M0052 p3] In this work, by the driving effect of DTL, the polysulfides were allowed to aggregate in CPE and form cluster species with the mediation of TFSI−.
[M0053 p3] As a result, polysulfides no longer exist as a free molecule, but form a cluster state molecule aggregated in CPE, as shown in Figure 2b,c.
[M0054 p3] Machine learning potentials for molecular dynamics (MLMD) simulation were performed to reveal the CPE mechanism.
[M0055 p3] Recently, Liu et al. revealed that Li2S4 is the key species that determines the kinetics of sulfur reduction reaction.[19] So the shortchain Li2S4 was chosen as the typical polysulfide species because it is the only liquid phase species for the liquid-solid conversion process (Li2S4→Li2S) and largely determines the kinetics of sulfur reduction reaction.
[M0056 p3] Snapshots of the two electrolytes with Li2S4 molecules are shown in Figure 2d,e.
[M0057 p3] In CE, the Li2S4 exists as typical neutral molecules.
[M0058 p3] In contrast, the Li2S4 molecules in CPE are aggregated to form cluster-state species.
[M0059 p3] Figures S4 and S5, (Supporting Information) show the localized simulated snapshots of CE and CPE, demonstrating the conversion of active species from single-molecule Li2S4 to clustered Li2S4 species in 60DTL electrolyte.
[M0060 p3] In addition, the stretching central S─S bond of Li2S4 in CPE species was observed (from 1.95–2.45 to 2.45– 2.85 Å), suggesting that the clustered polysulfide species have a stronger reactivity than polysulfides (Figure S5, Supporting Information).
[M0061 p3] A sharp peak of Li (Li2S4)─O (TFSI) appeared within the solvent sheath layer at 2.5Å in CPE (Figure 2f).
[M0062 p3] The coordination number of Li (Li2S4)─O (TFSI) in the CPE electrolyte was increased from 0 to 0.72, indicating that polysulfides are agglomerated under the mediation of TFSI−.
[M0063 p3] In contrast, the radial distribution plots around Li atom of Li2S4 molecules indicate that only DME or THF molecules are present within the solvated sheath of Li2S4 at 2.5 Å in CE (Figure 2g).
[M0064 p3] Also, as shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Information), the radial distribution plots around Li+ of LiTFSI molecules reveal a significantly enhanced ship peak of Li(TFSI)-S(Li2S4) in CPE.
[M0065 p3] The 19F and 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were performed to verify the formation of cluster species. 20 mmol L−1 Li2S4 or Li2S6 was added to the CE and CPE electrolyte with 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI.
[M0066 p3] As shown in Figure 2h,i, the 19F peak of CE electrolyte was hardly shifted after adding lithium polysulfides, which suggests that polysulfide species are present as free molecules and have not entered the solvated sheath of TFSI−.
[M0067 p3] In contrast, the 19F peak of CPE exhibits a pronounced increased chemical shift (≈0.2 ppm), which directly evidences the formation of a cluster state between polysulfides and TFSI−.

[M0111 p6] The deposition process of Li2S can be categorized into four classical models: 2D progressive nucleation (2DP), 2D instantaneous nucleation (2DI), 3D progressive nucleation (3DP), and 3D instantaneous nucleation (3DI).
[M0112 p6] The deposition of polysulfides to lithium sulfide is divided into nucleation and growth.
[M0113 p6] The nucleation process follows below: N = N0 (1 −exp (−At)) (1) where N represents the nuclei density on a certain substrate, t is the given time, N0 represents the density of available nucleation sites, and A is the nucleation rate.
[M0114 p6] When the nucleation rate A is large, Li2S nuclei are formed rapidly in a short period, which is called instantaneous nucleation.
[M0115 p6] When A is small, the nucleation density increases linearly with time, which is called progressive nucleation.
[M0116 p6] The growth process of Li2S is controlled by the diffusion of active species, which can be divided into 2D deposition and 3D deposition according to the different control steps.[22] As shown in Figure 3d,e, the Li2S deposition is well matched with the 3DP model in the CPE electrolyte, while it is situated between the 2DP and 2DI models in the CE electrolyte.
[M0117 p6] The 3D deposition model allows cells to release higher capacity and avoids premature end of discharge process caused by insulating Li2S covering the electrode surface.[23] Meanwhile, the Li2S deposition process in different electrolytes was compared by chronoamperometry experiment at 2.08 V.
[M0118 p6] As shown in Figure 3f, the deposition process in 0DTL electrolyte could not proceed due to the large overpotential.
[M0119 p6] With the increasing DTL content, the deposition peak appeared and gradually advanced, indicating that the Li2S deposition kinetics gradually accelerated.
[M0120 p6] The highest deposition capacity of Li2S (434 mAh g−1) was observed in 60DTL electrolytes, which is consistent with the results of CV experiments.
[M0121 p6] The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of Li2S deposited on carbon paper are shown in Figure 3g–j.
[M0122 p6] In 0DTL electrolytes, small lithium sulfide particles can be observed in the middle of discharge, and moss-like lithium sulfide can also be observed in some areas, which corresponds to the 2D deposition mode of lithium sulfide (Figure 3i).
[M0123 p6] In 60DTL electrolytes, due to the change of deposition mode (2D to 3D), the lithium sulfide can grow along the 3D direction, forming large-sized lithium sulfide in the form of liquid droplets (Figure 3g).
[M0124 p6] Ultimately, a 3D lithium sulfide deposition morphology can be realized in a 60DTL electrolyte (Figure 3h), while the mossy lithium sulfide in a 0DTL electrolyte grows into a film-like lithium sulfide and completely covers the conductive electrode (Figure 3j).
[M0125 p6] Figure S12 (Supporting Information) also shows the Li2S deposition morphology for electrolytes with different DTL contents.

[M0127 p6] Sulfur Redox Reaction Mediated by CPE The charge/discharge curves of Li─S cells in 0DTL and 60DTL electrolytes are shown in Figure 4a.
[M0128 p6] The behavior of a chargedischarge process is different, especially in the initial stage.
[M0129 p6] In Li─S batteries, the first discharge plateau corresponds to the conversion of sulfur to long-chain polysulfides, while the second discharge plateau corresponds to the conversion of polysulfides to lithium sulfide.
[M0130 p6] The tilted region between the first and second plateaus corresponds to the conversion of dissolved long-chain polysulfides to short-chain polysulfides.
[M0131 p6] The decrease in polysulfide solubility and the change in the state of reactive species caused the sulfur reaction to intermediate between the liquid and “quasi-solid” reactions.
[M0132 p6] Therefore, the voltage of the first discharge plateau decreases from 2.35 to 2.2 V in 60DTL electrolyte, accompanied by a significant decrease in the capacity of the tilted region.
[M0133 p6] In addition, significantly higher capacity and increased voltage of the second platform in 60DTL electrolyte indicate that the reduction process from Li2S4 to Li2S is effectively promoted.
[M0134 p6] During the charge process, two voltage platforms exist in 0DTL electrolyte, corresponding to the conversion of lithium sulfide to polysulfides and polysulfides to sulfur.
[M0135 p6] In contrast, only one voltage plateau exists in 60DTL electrolytes, indicating that CPE species also change the reaction mechanism of the charging process.
[M0136 p6] The initial specific capacity of Li─S cells with 60DTL electrolyte was increased from 1002 to 1371 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, and the overpotential was reduced from 173 to 116 mV.
[M0137 p6] It is noteworthy that the coulombic efficiency of cells with 60DTL electrolyte is increased from 76.3% to 99.6% due to the effective inhibition of the shuttle effect.
[M0138 p6] Charge–discharge curves for the first two cycles of electrolyte with different DTL content (0%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, v/v) and DOL/DME electrolyte are also shown in Figure S13 (Supporting Information).
[M0139 p6] A significant decrease in the first discharge plateau was observed at DTL contents of 50–80%.
[M0140 p6] The overpotential, discharge capacity, coulombic efficiency, and Q2/Q1 (capacity ratio of two discharge plateaus) of different electrolytes were further compared (Figure S14, Supporting Information).

[M0252 p11] Conclusion In conclusion, this work demonstrated a clustered polysulfide encapsulation mechanism through a novel electrolyte composed of 1,2-dimethylbenzene (DTL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME).
[M0253 p11] Both computational and experimental results elucidate the aggregation behavior of polysulfides in 60DTL electrolytes under the regulation of TFSI−.
[M0254 p11] The clustered polysulfides, as new active species, modulate the reaction mechanism of lithium–sulfur batteries, enhancing the lithium sulfide deposition process and enabling 3D lithium sulfide deposition.
[M0255 p11] Furthermore, the solubility of polysulfides is finely regulated, leading to significant suppression of the shuttle effect and effective stabilization of the lithium anode.
[M0256 p11] As a result, the CPE electrolyte exhibits improved electrochemical performance than conventional DOL/DME-based electrolytes, and the rate performance and cycling stability of the battery are significantly improved, with smooth operation under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0257 p11] This work not only presents an alternative polysulfide-conversion mechanism via a new electrolyte system with excellent reaction kinetics and anode stability for Li─S batteries but also elucidates the critical role of polysulfide dissolution behavior in governing sulfur redox reactions.
[M0258 p11] These findings provide valuable insights into the complex sulfur conversion process and offer a significant advancement in the understanding of Li─S battery chemistry.

[S0056 p0] All the electrochemical tests were performed on Neware multichannel battery cycler. 1.5 Computational Details To simulate CE and CPE electrolyte systems over 17,000 atoms, we employed machine learning potentials for molecular dynamics (MLMD) simulations.
[S0057 p0] The initial configurations of Li2S4, LiTFSI, DME, THF and DTL were optimized using Gaussian09 program with M062X/6-311+G(d,p).
[S0058 p0] The CE and CPE electrolyte systems were prepared by randomly placing the molecules in the simulation box based on their experimental densities and molar ratios.
[S0059 p0] For CE electrolyte (0DTL electrolyte), 120 LiTFSI, 74 THF, 738 DME and 30 Li2S4 molecules were added to the simulation box (60*60*60 Å3).
[S0060 p0] For CPE electrolyte (60DTL electrolyte), 120 LiTFSI, 74 THF, 272 DME, 596 DTL and 30 Li2S4 molecules were added to the simulation box (60*60*60 Å3).
[S0061 p0] The density functional theory (DFT) dataset was generated through an iterative workflow, which can be outlined as follows: (1) Initial MD Sampling: We initiated the process by performing MLMD simulations on simplified structures comprising approximately 300 atoms.
[S0062 p0] Each MLMD simulation was conducted for 20 ps with a time step of 0.5 fs.
[S0063 p0] (2) Model Training: Two independent models, differing only in their initial random seeds, were trained concurrently to assess the robustness of the predictions.
[S0064 p0] (3) Error Evaluation: After each MLMD simulation, the forces on the sampled structures were predicted using both models.
[S0065 p0] Structures with prediction errors exceeding 0.2 eV/Å between the two models were identified as poorly represented by the potential model and added to a temporary dataset for further processing.
[S0066 p0] (4) Dataset Augmentation: The temporary dataset was analyzed using SOAP[1] descriptors and K-means[2] clustering to extract up to 300 structurally distinct configurations.
[S0067 p0] These configurations were subjected to DFT calculations, and the resulting data were incorporated into the training dataset to enhance model accuracy.
[S0068 p0] (5) Iteration: The updated models were then used to perform another round of MLMD simulations.
[S0069 p0] Steps (3) and (4) were repeated iteratively—MLMD simulation, error evaluation, and dataset augmentation—until the prediction errors of both models were sufficiently minimized.
[S0070 p0] For the simulations, we employed the M3GNet model[3] with radial and three-body interaction cutoffs set to 5 Å and 4 Å, respectively.
[S0071 p0] The loss function was formulated as a weighted sum of mean squared errors for energies, forces, and stresses, each assigned an equal weight of 1.
[S0072 p0] A batch size of 4 was utilized, along with an exponential learning rate scheduler initialized at 110-3.
[S0073 p0] The convergence criteria for training were set stringently, requiring forces to fall below 0.1 eV/Å.
[S0074 p0] Training was performed on a Tesla A100 GPU until validation errors converged.
[S0075 p0] After training the potential function model, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on CE and CPE electrolyte systems comprising over 17,000 atoms.
[S0076 p0] The simulations were carried out under NVT ensemble conditions, with the temperature maintained at 300 K, a time step of 0.5 fs, and a total simulation time of 30 ps.
[S0077 p0] The initial 10 ps were designated as the pre-equilibration phase.
[S0078 p0] In addition, ESPs of molecules were calculated with Gaussian09 program at the B3LYP/def2tzvp level and visualized by GaussView.
