[M0002 p1] Ed. 2026, 65, e22940 doi.org/10.1002/anie.202522940 Batteries Very Important Paper Modulating Li+ and Polysulfide Solvation with Low-Density Moderately Solvating Electrolytes for Lithium–Sulfur Batteries Tianxing Lai, Kameron Liao, and Arumugam Manthiram* Abstract: Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries show great promise as the next-generation rechargeable batteries, yet they still suffer from polysulfide shuttling and interphasial instability.
[M0003 p1] Electrolyte, as the medium for ion transport and sulfur conversion, plays a crucial role in overcoming these challenges.
[M0004 p1] Here, we introduce a moderately solvating electrolyte (MSE) based on low-density, low-viscosity, and nonfluorinated ether co-solvents that balances polysulfide suppression, Li metal stabilization, and redox kinetics.
[M0005 p1] Through multiple solvent–solvent and solvent-ion interactions, the optimized MSE weakens Li+-solvent pairing while strengthening cation–anion interactions, thereby lowering the desolvation barrier and promoting the formation of a favorable solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI).
[M0006 p1] Meanwhile, MSE limits the polysulfide dissolution but improves the accessibility of active material through better wettability and tailored solvation environment, leading to an altered sulfur deposition mechanism with β – α conversion.
[M0007 p1] This approach enables a stable cycling of highmass loading Li–S cells (> 3.5 mg cm−2) at both room temperature and 45 °C (where shuttling and side reactions are severer), and demonstrates a pouch cell with lean electrolyte content (4.5 μL mg s−1).
[M0008 p1] This work highlights a practical route to develop high-performance electrolyte for Li–S cells and provides mechanistic insights into their operation.
[M0009 p1] Introduction Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have attracted intense interest as a next-generation energy-storage technology because they combine high theoretical energy density (2600 W h kg−1) with low material cost and less supply chain issues (compared to current transition-metal based chemistries).[1] Li–S batteries are particularly attractive for weight and costsensitive applications, such as electric vehicles, aviation, and space equipment.
[M0010 p1] However, practical deployment of Li–S cells is impeded by several interconnected challenges.
[M0011 p1] During cycling, sulfur undergoes multistep redox reactions that form electrolyte soluble lithium polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates.
[M0012 p1] They lead to an infamous phenomenon called shuttling, which then causes active material loss and continuous parasitic reactions at the lithium–metal anode.
[M0013 p1] Meanwhile, the lithium anode suffers from intrinsically poor interphasial stability, while the sulfur cathode is limited by its low conductivity, poor redox kinetics, and large volume changes during cycling.

[M0017 p1] These issues could be even more challenging when practical conditions like high sulfur loading and lean electrolyte are employed, leading to poor energy density and cycle life.[2,3] To address these obstacles, extensive efforts have been made in the field.
[M0018 p1] Cathode engineering, including ingeniously designed carbon hosts and catalyst materials, has proven effective in ameliorating sulfur conversion kinetics and mitigating LiPS shuttling.[4–11] Nevertheless, electrolyte, as the solvation and transport medium for Li+ ion and polysulfide, plays a decisive role in regulating polysulfide chemistry and interphasial stability of both electrodes.
[M0019 p1] In fact, electrolyte design offers a feasible and arguably more scalable route to remedy the unique polysulfide-related problems in Li–S cells.
[M0020 p1] A range of electrolyte strategies has, therefore, been explored, including solvent and salt selection, concentration tuning, targeted additives.[12–17] The etherbased 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) / 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) electrolyte remains the benchmark in the field because it offers reasonable sulfur conversion kinetics and compatibility with Li anode; however, it provides limited control over LiPS shuttling.
[M0021 p1] Carbonate electrolytes are widely employed in commercial Li-ion batteries.
[M0022 p1] They are normally considered incompatible with Li–S chemistry due to the nucleophilic attack by LiPSs, but they could be an option as they can limit the LiPS dissolution by forming a protective cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer.[18–20] However, the use of carbonate electrolytes in Li–S cells requires extreme confinement of sulfur at the cathode, such as covalently bonded or sophisticated structures.[21–23] Moreover, carbonate electrolytes show inferior compatibility with Li metal, limiting the long-term cyclability of the anode.

[M0028 p2] However, the standard of choosing such co-solvent and a detailed understanding of how it influences the polysulfide solvating environment are still lacking.
[M0029 p2] Here, we present new MSE designs employing a series of low-density, lowviscosity, low-cost, and nonfluorinated ether co-solvents.
[M0030 p2] Their influence on the Li+ ion and polysulfide solvations are systematically investigated.
[M0031 p2] It is revealed that MSEs modulate the solvating environment for not only Li+ ions, but also salt and polysulfide anions, through multiple intermolecular interactions.
[M0032 p2] As a result, the desolvation barrier for Li+ ion is reduced and an inorganic-enriched solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) is formed on the anode surface, leading to a better reversibility of Li plating and stripping (Figure 1a).
[M0033 p2] Meanwhile, the polysulfide behavior in the electrolyte is altered, mitigating shuttling and reducing active material loss.
[M0034 p2] Operando and electrochemical characterizations further reveal the change in sulfur conversion pathway and redox kinetics with MSE.
[M0035 p2] Finally, the optimized MSE enables high sulfur utilization and stable cycling of Li || S cells with practical S loadings and limited electrolyte conditions at both room temperature and 45 °C.
[M0036 p2] The effectiveness of this electrolyte at larger scale is also demonstrated with pouch cells.
[M0037 p2] Results and Discussion Based on their structural and physicochemical properties, a series of single-oxygen ethers, including methyl tert–butyl ether (MtBE), cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME), sec-butyl methyl ether (SBME), and tert-amyl methyl ether (TAME), were selected as the solvents to regulate the solvating ability of the conventional DOL/DME electrolyte (Figure 1b and Table S1).
[M0038 p2] These solvents feature only one solvating O atom and possess sufficient steric hindrance that limits their solvating capability for Li+.
[M0039 p2] Unlike nonsolvating diluents, such as highly fluorinated ethers, these solvents show some degree of solvating power, but still have much lower solubility for LiPS compared to DME and DOL, which can lead to unique local solvating environment (Figure 1c and Figure S2).
[M0040 p2] Meanwhile, they feature slight structural differences among each other that help fine tune the solvation structures and better understand their effect in the electrolyte.
[M0041 p2] The designed MSEs with 30 vol% of these solvents are labeled as MSE-MtBE, MSE-CPME, MSE-SBME, and MSE-TAME, respectively (detailed compositions given in the Supporting Information).
[M0042 p2] Owing to the low density of these solvents, these MSEs exhibit lower densities (e.g., 1.04 g cm−3 for MSE-MtBE) compared to DOL/DME baseline (1.16 g cm−3).

[M0047 p3] In contrast, three of the four selected solvents bind significantly weaker with binding energy values of ∼0.15 eV.

[M0050 p3] Lithium-Ion Solvation and Anode Stabilization The solvation structures of MSEs were examined with Raman spectroscopy and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.
[M0051 p3] Raman spectra display a blue shift of the S─N─S vibrational mode of TFSI for all the MSEs (742.5– 743.1 cm−1) compared to the DOL/DME baseline (741.6 cm−1), suggesting more contact-ion pairs (CIPs) and salt aggregates (AGGs) formed in MSEs (Figure S3).
[M0052 p3] This is also demonstrated by the peak deconvolution results, which show a clear increase in the intensity of the CIP/AGG peak at ∼745 cm−1 for MSEs.
[M0053 p3] NMR spectroscopy was employed to provide a more precise understanding of the solvation structures.
[M0054 p3] In the 7Li NMR spectra, all the peaks in MSEs show a downfield shift from −1.19 ppm in the DOL/DME baseline electrolyte, indicating less electron shielding around the Li+ ion (Figure 2a).
[M0055 p3] This involves the combination of two competing effectsshielding from the increase of cation–anion pairing and de-shielding from the reduction of Li+–solvent interactions.
[M0056 p3] Since DME is a strong solvating solvent and the electrons on its O atoms strongly shield Li+, replacing it with an anion or a weakly solvating solvent in the solvation shell usually results in more de-shielding.[35–37] Note that Raman results show a similar increase of cation-anion pairing in all MSEs.
[M0057 p3] Therefore, the fact that the 7Li peak shifts to greater downfield in MSE-MtBE and MSE-SBME indicates their stronger ability to pull DME and DOL away from the Li+ ion, as well as their weaker interactions with Li+ ion, compared to other MSEs.
[M0058 p3] Similarly, in the 19F spectra, the TFSI−anion peaks shift upfield in MSEs compared to the DOL/DME electrolyte (−80.17 ppm) due to stronger Li+–anion pairing and weaker anion-solvent interactions (Figure S4).
[M0059 p3] Nevertheless, unlike the 7Li peak, the upfield shift of the 19F peak in MSE- MtBE is the smallest among MSEs, indicating potentially stronger interactions between its protons and TFSI−anions.
[M0060 p3] This is further demonstrated with the 2D 19F diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR spectra, where TFSI−anion exhibits a slightly smaller diffusion coefficient of 4.37 × 10−10 m2 s−1 in MSE-MtBE compared to 5.01 × 10−10 m2 s−1 in DOL/DME (Figure S5). 1H NMR results provide further insight into these solventrelated interactions.
[M0061 p3] After adding MtBE into the DOL/DME mixture, the 1H peaks for DOL and DME shift downfield owing to the MtBE-induced solvent-solvent interactions (Figure 2b).
[M0062 p3] The O atoms in MtBE pull electrons away from the H atoms in DOL and DME, resulting in less electron shielding of the H atoms.
[M0063 p3] When Li salt is introduced into the system, DME pairs more with the Li+ ions, reducing its interactions with other solvents.
[M0064 p3] The MtBE peaks show an upfield shift due to this effect, as well as the solvent-anion interactions discussed earlier.
[M0065 p3] Moreover, by comparing 1H NMR spectra for all the MSEs with salt, the 1H peaks for DME and DOL in MSE-MtBE show the most downfield shift, suggesting the strongest capability of MtBE to reduce electron density on DME and DOL (Figure 2c).
[M0066 p3] This could be one of the reasons why DME and DOL show weaker interactions with Li+ ion in MSE-MtBE compared to other MSEs.
[M0067 p3] These solvation properties and interactions were further demonstrated with molecular dynamics (MD) and DFT calculations.
[M0068 p3] As shown in the MD results, the coordination number (CN) of ODME around Li+ in the first solvation shell (∼2–4 Å) decreases in MSEs compared to DOL/DME, with MtBE having the lowest coordination number (Figures 2d and S6).
[M0069 p3] This confirms that all the MSEs can weaken the coordination between Li+ and DME, but MtBE does so more effectively, aligning well with the NMR results.
[M0070 p3] Meanwhile, radial distribution function (RDF) peaks of the O atom on MSEs in the first solvation sheath can be observed, demonstrating their limited, yet not negligible, solvating power (Figure S7).
[M0071 p3] DFT calculations of DME– solvent complexes reveal that DME–MtBE has the highest binding energy of 0.189 eV among all MSEs, confirming their strongest intermolecular interactions (Figure 2e).
[M0072 p3] Furthermore, small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) was performed to probe the inhomogeneities, clustering, and intermolecular spacing in the electrolytes across microscopic to mesoscopic scales.[38] Taking MtBE-MSE as an example, both MtBE-MSE and DOL/DME baseline display a broad peak in their SAXS curves at a high scattering factor (q) value (> 1 Å−1), corresponding to the average molecule size or intermolecular distances (Figure S8).
[M0073 p3] Meanwhile, there is also a shoulder peak at ∼0.6 Å−1, which is related to the CIP/AGG formed in the electrolyte (with a size of ∼10 Å).
[M0074 p3] The fact that MtBE-MSE shows higher intensity in this region confirms the formation of more CIPs and AGGs in the electrolyte.
[M0075 p3] In addition, MtBE-MSE exhibits higher intensity in the q range of 0.05–0.4 Å−1, suggesting more pronounced clustering at a larger scale (∼10 s of nm), potentially owing to the introduced solvent-solvent interactions with MtBE.
[M0076 p3] The altered solvation properties of MSEs play an important role in influencing the reaction kinetics at the Li-metal side, which is examined with electrochemical tests.
[M0077 p3] Tafel plots of Li || Li symmetric cells were derived from linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) scans (Figure S9).
[M0078 p3] By fitting these Tafel plots, the exchange current density of the redox reaction can be obtained.
[M0079 p3] MSEs exhibit higher exchange current densities compared to DOL/DME, indicating faster electrochemical reaction kinetics on Li metal.
[M0080 p3] Temperaturedependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on fresh Li || Li cells provides more insight into the Li+ transport kinetics at the interphase.
[M0081 p3] Through the distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis for the EIS data, there is one major peak within the time constant range of 10−4 to 10−2, corresponding to the semicircle at mid-frequency region in the Nyquist plots (Figures S10 and S11).

[M0105 p5] Polysulfide Solvation and Conversion Kinetics LiPSs play a pivotal role in affecting the redox reaction kinetics in Li–S batteries.
[M0106 p5] Although MSEs possess lower LiPS solubility than the DOL/DME baseline, they still involve a soluble LiPS-mediated redox pathway (Figure S13).
[M0107 p5] Therefore, investigating LiPS behavior in the electrolyte is crucial for understanding the cathode kinetics and overall cell performance.
[M0108 p5] NMR tests were first conducted on 0.1 M Li2S6 solutions (without other salts) to evaluate the solvating environment of LiPS.
[M0109 p5] In the 7Li spectra, the peak downshifts from 0.17 ppm in DOL/DME to 0.28 ppm in MtBE (Figure 3a).
[M0110 p5] Other MSEs also show a downfield shift (except CPME), but less pronounced compared to MtBE.
[M0111 p5] This could mainly result from reduced Li+-solvent interactions with more Li+ and S62− pairing, similar to the case of regular electrolytes discussed in the previous section.
[M0112 p5] Furthermore, 1H and 7Li DOSY NMR studies were employed to probe the diffusivity of the solvent and Li+ ion in the Li2S6 solution (Figure 3b,c).
[M0113 p5] Interestingly, in the solution with MtBE, DME and Li+ show diffusion coefficients of 1.91 × 10−9 and 5.62 × 10−10 m2 s−1, respectively, which are higher compared to 1.26 × 10−9 and 4.47 × 10−10 m2 s−1, respectively, in the DOL/DME solution.
[M0114 p5] These higher diffusivities with the addition of MtBE likely arise from the overall reduced solvent-Li+ binding, as well as the low viscosity of MtBE, which promotes faster mass transport in the actual cells for MSEs.
[M0115 p5] Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations provide further insights into the solvating environment with polysulfide in the electrolyte.
[M0116 p5] In the DOL/DME system, Li+ is predominantly solvated by DME and DOL molecules, making the S62−anion to dissociate from Li+ (Figures 3d and S14).
[M0117 p5] In contrast, RDF and representative AIMD snapshots for the MtBE-containing formulation show an increased propensity for Li+–S62−pairing (Figures 3e,f and S14).
[M0118 p5] Moreover, MtBE tends to pull DME away from Li+, reducing the number of DME molecules in the inner solvation shell of the Li+ and S62−complex.
[M0119 p5] These results indicate that MSE-MtBE most effectively shifts polysulfide solvation toward more ion pairing, which could directly affect polysulfide migration and conversion; accordingly, MSE-MtBE was selected for further evaluation.
[M0120 p5] Due to the reduction of LiPS solubility and regulation of solvation structures, the LiPS shuttling effect is expected to be effectively mitigated in MSE-MtBE, which could make a substantial impact on the cyclability of Li–S cells.
[M0121 p5] To validate this, a shuttling current test was performed on Li || S cells by holding the voltage at 2.25 V during discharge, the point when high-order LiPS largely formed.
[M0122 p5] As shown in Figure S15, the current in the DOL/DME cell quickly changes direction and levels off at ∼0.047 mA, indicating obvious LiPS shuttling.
[M0123 p5] In contrast, the MSE-MtBE cell shows much slower current change, and the shuttle current stabilizes at ∼0.013 mA, demonstrating its ability to suppress shuttling.
[M0124 p5] Several more tests were conducted to better assess the impact of electrolyte on sulfur conversion kinetics.
[M0125 p5] Pairing the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) with EIS is an effective approach to study cell polarization.[40] By applying this GITT-EIS method to Li || S cells with practical sulfur loading, the overall cell polarization (ηtotal) can be resolved into three contributions: ohmic (ηohm), activation (ηact), and concentration (ηconc) polarizations. ηohm captures ionic resistance in the bulk electrolyte as well as electronic and contact resistances within the electrodes and cell hardware; it can be extracted from the high-frequency intercept on the Nyquist plot. ηact corresponds to the energy barrier of the interfacial charge-transfer process and, together with ηohm, produces the instantaneous voltage jump observed whenever the current is stepped during GITT measurement.
[M0126 p5] Finally, ηconc arises from mass-transport limitations when Li+ ions and LiPSs are locally depleted at the electrode surface due to electrochemical reactions; ηconc can be obtained from the gradual voltage drift toward equilibrium potential during the relaxation period in GITT.
[M0127 p5] For both the cell with MSE-MtBE and DOL/DME electrolyte, the resulting ηtotal shows a gradual increase at the beginning of the discharge and peaks at ∼25% depth of discharge (DOD) when Li2S/Li2S2 starts to nucleate (Figures 3g and S16a).
[M0128 p5] It then slowly decreases before displaying a rapid increase toward the end of the discharge. ηtotal was further decoupled into the three components discussed above (Figure S16b–d).
[M0129 p5] The resulting ηohm for the MSE-MtBE cell is slightly greater than the DOL/DME cell due to their ionic conductivity difference, with both cells exhibiting a peak value at the nucleation point (2.81 mV for MSE-MtBE and 2.10 mV for DOL/DME) when LiPS concentration reaches its maximum.
[M0130 p5] However, ηohm takes up only a small fraction of ηtotal, showcasing that it is not the major cause of the cell overpotential.
[M0131 p5] Meanwhile, the main part of ηtotal is comprised of ηact and ηconc.
[M0132 p5] Both ηact and ηconc display a peak at ∼ 25% DOD, while ηconc has a rapid increase at the end of the discharge due to more sluggish ion diffusion in the electrode.
[M0133 p5] The MSE-MtBE cell shows a greater ηact at the Li2S/Li2S2 nucleation point (19.5 mV) compared to the baseline cell (10.4 mV), likely because of reduced LiPS concentration in MSE-MtBE, leading to fewer nuclei and nucleation sites.
[M0134 p5] The difference is much smaller in ηconc, as it is only slightly larger in MSE-MtBE before 75% DOD.
[M0135 p5] In fact, the ηconc in MSE- MtBE has a peak value of 44.7 mV at ∼25% DOD, which is lower than that in DOL/DME (47.4 mV).
[M0136 p5] This is likely due to easier LiPS movement and lower desolvation energy in MSE-MtBE, aligning well with the previous discussion.
[M0137 p5] Additionally, during termination of discharge, most of the electrochemically active surface is covered; the MSE-MtBE cell shows significantly lower ηconc compared to the baseline cell, thanks to its better wettability and potentially more uniform deposition in the electrode (Figure S17).

[M0143 p6] Chronoamperometry experiments were further performed to quantitatively study the Li2S nucleation and growth.
[M0144 p6] As shown in Figure S18, the MSE-MtBE cell achieves a higher response current peak and a larger Li2S deposition capacity of 232 mA h g−1, compared to 161 mA h g−1 for the DOL/DME cell.
[M0145 p6] This leads to higher discharge capacity and active material utilization in actual Li || S cells.
[M0146 p6] Interestingly, both chronoamperometry cells show similar peak response time, indicating that MSE-MtBE does not adversely affect the nucleation rate when the polysulfide concentration is the same as in the baseline.
[M0147 p6] However, in a practical cell with high sulfur loading and low E/S ratio, the nucleation process can also be affected due to polysulfide solubility differences, as discussed in the previous section.
[M0148 p6] Furthermore, to probe the liquid–liquid sulfur conversion kinetics, temperature-dependent EIS spectra were collected on Li2S6 symmetric cells (Figure S19).
[M0149 p6] Following a similar procedure applied to Li metal symmetric cells, DRT analysis was carried out to interpret the EIS data (Figures 3h and S20).
[M0150 p6] DRT peaks appearing at intermediate time constants (10−4 to 10−1 s) are attributed to the Rct of the cell, which is an important indicator of the charge-transfer kinetics.[41,42] These Rct values measured between 0 and 50 °C fit well in an Arrhenius plot, from which activation energies were extracted (Figure 3i).
[M0151 p6] Interestingly, the MSE-MtBE cell exhibits higher Rct throughout the temperature range, but a lower activation energy compared to DOL/DME.
[M0152 p6] This suggests that the energy barrier for the charge-transfer process, which is likely associated with desolvation at the electrochemically active surface, is lower in MSE-MtBE, whereas the overall reaction rate is slower, plausibly due to the lower conductivity of MSE-MtBE (Figure S21).
[M0153 p6] Additionally, in the CV curves of Li2S6 symmetric cells, MSE-MtBE shows slightly lower, but more reversible response current, suggesting fewer side reactions and better stability of the conversion process (Figure S22).

[M0159 p7] Operando XRD was performed to further elucidate the impact of electrolyte on cathode kinetics and microstructures (Figure 4a,b).
[M0160 p7] By directly observing the evolution of cathode species, it provides insights into the sulfur conversion process.
[M0161 p7] Before cycling, both the DOL/DME and MSE-MtBE cells exhibit strong α-S8 signals as expected.
[M0162 p7] These α-S8 peaks quickly decrease and disappear upon discharge when high and low order LiPSs start to form.
[M0163 p7] These LiPSs are subsequently converted to low crystallinity Li2S at the end of the discharge, as evidenced by a broad peak detected at around 27°.
[M0164 p7] Both cells show a complete disappearance of sulfur peaks during discharge, indicating a complete conversion of sulfur, attributed to the optimized spray-dried sulfur composite particles utilized in the cathode.[43] Interestingly, notable differences between the baseline and MSE-MtBE cells were observed after the subsequent charge.
[M0165 p7] For the DOL/DME cell, a group of β-S8 peaks were detected after charge, which aligns well with previous work in the literature utilizing ether-based electrolytes.[44] However, in the MSE-MtBE cell, β-S8 was first formed toward the end of the charge before peaks corresponding to α-S8 appeared.
[M0166 p7] Note that α-S8 tends to form when the reaction kinetics move toward solid-state conversion.[32,45] This two-step sulfur deposition process could be characteristic in the MSE due to lower LiPS solubility and regulated solvation properties.
[M0167 p7] As a kinetically more favorable process, β-S8 is first nucleated from solvated LiPSs, while thermodynamically more stable α-S8 starts to grow when quasi-solid-state reaction kicks in.
[M0168 p7] Additional ex situ XRD results show that this β – α conversion can happen even on immersing the S cathode retrieved from the DOL/DME cell in MSE-MtBE (Figure S23).
[M0169 p7] This indicates that MSE- MtBE affects the balance between high order polysulfides and elemental sulfur (in both solution and solid state), thereby changing the dissolution and redeposition process that stabilizes β-S8.
[M0170 p7] This mechanism may be pivotal toward maintaining adequate charging kinetics, while minimizing LiPS dissolution in MSEs.
[M0171 p7] Electrochemical Performance of the Electrolytes To evaluate the influence of these altered solvation structures and conversion kinetics on the electrochemical performance, Li || S cells with different electrolytes were assembled and tested under different conditions.
[M0172 p7] MSE-MtBE and DOL/DME baseline electrolytes were first compared in cells with a relatively low S loading of 2.2 mg cm−2.
[M0173 p7] Cycled at a rate of C/3 (1C = 1500 mA h g−1), the MSE-MtBE cell exhibits a high initial capacity of 945 mA h g−1 and enables a high retention of 75.2% at 711 mA h g−1 after 200 cycles (Figure S24).
[M0174 p7] In contrast, DOL/DME only achieves 537 mA h g−1 after 200 cycles, corresponding to a retention of 59.3%.
[M0175 p7] The increased capacity and capacity retention may benefit from reduced polysulfide dissolution and regulated solvation.
[M0176 p7] The electrolytes were then demonstrated with a higher S loading of 3.5–3.7 mg cm−2 and a lower E/S ratio of 10 μL mg s−1.
[M0177 p7] The MSE-MtBE cell maintains a capacity of 710 mA h g−1 after 180 cycles at a C/5 rate, corresponding to 79.4% of its initial capacity, contrasting only 458 mA h g−1 and 56.6% for the DOL/DME cell (Figure 5a, Table S2).
[M0178 p7] When cycled at a C/10 rate, the MSE-MtBE cell also presents a high initial capacity of 1,019 mA h g−1 and a capacity retention of 83.2% at 848 mA h g−1 after 140 cycles (Figure S25).
[M0179 p7] The rate performances of the electrolytes were further assessed.
[M0180 p7] MSE-MtBE achieves higher and more stable capacities at all applied rates (from C/10 to C/2), especially at C/2, where it retains a capacity of 781 mA h g−1 (Figure 5b).
[M0181 p7] In contrast, the DOL/DME cell only shows a capacity of 700 mA h g−1 and drops below 550 mA h g−1 after 10 cycles at C/2.
[M0182 p7] Notably, the gap remains significant after the rate changes back to C/5, as the MSE-MtBE cell recovers a capacity of ∼ 850 mA h g−1, while the baseline cell has less than 700 mA h g−1 retained.
[M0183 p7] Additionally, MSE-MtBE shows comparable overpotentials in the voltage profiles except a larger dip at the nucleation point for the rate of C/2 (Figures 5c and S26).
[M0184 p7] These results reveal that the addition of MtBE plays an important role in improving the capacity and cyclability of Li– S cells, which may be attributed to its influence on both the electrodes.
[M0185 p7] Li–S cells with high sulfur loading were further evaluated at an elevated temperature of 45 °C.
[M0186 p7] Such conditions facilitate the generation and migration of LiPS, while also intensifying detrimental side reactions with Li metal.
[M0187 p7] With a sulfur loading of 3.6 mg cm−2, the DOL/DME baseline cell shows an initial capacity of 770 mA h g−1 at a C/5 rate and retains 627 mA h g−1 after 90 cycles (Figure 5d).

[M0201 p8] Surface and Interphase Characterization Interphasial properties play a crucial role in affecting the performance of batteries.
[M0202 p8] To elucidate the influence of MSE-MtBE on electrode-electrolyte interphases, several characterization tests were carried out.
[M0203 p8] After cycling the Li || S cells for 30 cycles, Li metal morphology was examined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
[M0204 p8] MSE-MtBE enables dense and uniform Li deposition, packed with large grains, suggesting less parasitic side reactions and dendrite formation (Figure S29).
[M0205 p8] In contrast, Li metal cycled in DOL/DME has more cracks and non-uniform surface morphology.
[M0206 p8] The interphasial stability of Li-metal anode is heavily influenced by the characteristics of the SEI layer.
[M0207 p8] In Li–S systems, SEI forms through the decomposition of electrolyte salt and solvent, as well as the crossover of sulfur-containing species from the cathode.
[M0208 p8] The modulation of solvation structures and LiPS behaviors by MSE-MtBE may affect the SEI composition.
[M0209 p8] To better study the SEI in different electrolytes, Li anodes after 30 cycles were evaluated with xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).
[M0210 p8] As shown in Figure 6a, the S 2p spectra reveal various sulfur species on the surface of the SEI, including SO42−/─SO2 (S 2p3/2 ≈168.7 eV), SO32−/─SO (S 2p3/2 ≈167.1 eV), SB0 (S 2p3/2 ≈163.6 eV), ST−1 (S 2p3/2 ≈161.6 eV), and S−2 (S 2p3/2 ≈159.9 eV).[46] The oxidized sulfur species (─SOx) mainly originate from the LiNO3-involved decomposition of LiTFSI salt and oxidation of soluble LiPSs, while the reduced sulfur species (Sx, x < 0) mainly come from the migration of LiPSs from the cathode.
[M0211 p8] The MSE-MtBE sample showcases a healthy combination of these species with more oxidized sulfur species detected, which passivate the interphase and promote more reversible Li deposition.
[M0212 p8] In addition, these oxidized species show slightly lower binding energies compared to the control sample, potentially improving the conductivity of this passivating layer.
[M0213 p8] On the other hand, the DOL/DME electrolyte leads to the formation of more reduced sulfur species, indicating a greater degree of LiPS shuttling to the anode.

[M0233 p9] Conclusion In summary, we demonstrated an MSE strategy utilizing a family of low-density, low-viscosity, nonfluorinated ether cosolvents that present weaker solvation power than DME.
[M0234 p9] By probing their intermolecular interactions with salt and solvent, we showed that these co-solvents, especially MtBE, concurrently tune Li+ and polysulfide solvation.
[M0235 p9] The optimized formulation (MSE-MtBE) effectively (i) weakens Li+– solvent pairing, while increasing Li+–anion association, and (ii) reduces polysulfide dissolution, while improving electrode wettability and mass transport.
[M0236 p9] As a result, this electrolyte facilitates Li+ desolvation and promotes the formation of a LiF-rich, passivating SEI.

[S0011 p0] Moderately solvating electrolytes (MSEs) were prepared by dissolving 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 in a mixture of DOL, DME, and a moderately solvating solvent (v/v/v = 3.5: 3.5: 3).
[S0012 p0] The moderately solvating solvents tested include methyl tert-butyl ether (MtBE, Thermo Scientific), cyclopentyl methyl ether (CPME, Thermo Scientific), sec-butyl methyl ether (SBME, Sigma-Aldrich), and tert-amyl methyl ether (TAME, Sigma-Aldrich). 0.1 M Li2S6 was added to the electrolytes for assembling Li2S6 symmetric cells.
[S0013 p0] Electrochemical Measurements: All coin cells were assembled with CR2032 cell cases inside an Ar-filled glovebox.
[S0014 p0] One layer of Celgard 2325 separator was utilized in each cell.
[S0015 p0] The electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio was controlled (as specified). 20 μL of the electrolyte was added on each side for symmetric cells.
[S0016 p0] The cells were rested for 5 h before being cycled.
[S0017 p0] Low loading Li || S cells (~ 2 mg cm−2) were activated with one C/20 cycle and one C/10 cycle.
[S0018 p0] High loading cells (≥ 3.5 mg cm−2) were first discharged at a C/30 rate, followed by one C/20 formation cycles.
[S0019 p0] Extra C/10 formation cycles were performed for cells running the C/5 test.
[S0020 p0] Galvanostatic cycling tests were then performed at specified rates between 1.7 and 2.8 V at 21 °C.
[S0021 p0] High-temperature cycling test was performed at 45 °C with two C/20 and two C/10 rate formation cycles.
[S0022 p0] All the cycling data were collected on a Landt or an Arbin cycler.
[S0023 p0] Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) tests were performed at a C/10 rate.
[S0024 p0] A constant discharge current was applied for 15 min, followed by a 75 min relaxation.
[S0025 p0] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed during the rest step (3 min after discharge).
[S0026 p0] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted in the potential range of 1.7 to 2.8 V for Li || S cells and −1 to 1 V for Li2S6 symmetric cells.
[S0027 p0] EIS measurements were conducted in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 0.05 Hz.
[S0028 p0] Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was performed with the pyDRTtools package.[2] GITT, CV, and EIS data were collected on a Biologic VMP-3 system.
[S0029 p0] Pouch Cell Assembly and Testing: Li-S pouch cells were fabricated with Li foil anode and blade-cast sulfur cathode.
[S0030 p0] The Li anode was pressed onto a Ni current collector.
[S0031 p0] The cathode was punched into 5.6 × 4.3 cm (~ 24 cm2) sheet, while the anode had a slightly larger dimension (5.8 × 4.5 cm).
[S0032 p0] Ni and Al tabs were welded onto the anode and cathode current collector, respectively.
[S0033 p0] The E/S ratio was controlled to be 5.5 or 4.5 μL mg−1.
[S0034 p0] The pouch cells were put in pressure holders to ensure uniform pressure.
[S0035 p0] The cells with a sulfur loading of 3.5 mg cm−2 were cycled between 1.7 and 2.8 V at a C/20 rate for 2 formation cycles, followed by cycling at a C/10 rate.
[S0036 p0] The cells with a loading of 4.6 mg cm−2 were discharged once at a C/30 rate before cycling at a C/20 rate.
[S0037 p0] Materials Characterization: 1H, 7Li, and 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was carried out with a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz NMR spectrometer or an Agilent MR400 spectrometer.
[S0038 p0] Two-dimensional (2D) diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) spectra were collected on a Varian 600 MHz NMR spectrometer.
[S0039 p0] A coaxial insert with 0.1 M LiPF6 dissolved in the deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) was applied as the reference.
[S0040 p0] Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) was performed with a Xenocs Ganesha instrument.
[S0041 p0] Operando XRD analysis was carried out on a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer with a specially designed cell (MTI Corp).
[S0042 p0] Raman spectra were collected with a Renishaw Raman spectrometer.
[S0043 p0] Ionic conductivity of electrolyte was measured with a Mettler-Toledo SevenCompact S230 conductivity meter and an InLab 752 probe.
[S0044 p0] The surface morphology of the materials was imaged with an Apreo 2C LoVac SEM.
[S0045 p0] A PHI VersaProbe 4 X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer was utilized for XPS analysis.
[S0046 p0] The cycled Li samples were sputtered for various amounts of time with Ar cluster ions.
[S0047 p0] Computational methods: Density functional theory (DFT) calculations of binding energies were performed with the Gaussian 16 software package.
[S0048 p0] B3LYP functional and the 6-311+G(d, p) basis set were used for structure optimization of solvent molecules and complexes.[3, 4] Vibrational frequency calculations were conducted to confirm that a local minimum was reached.
[S0049 p0] M06-2X functional and the ma-def2-TZVP(-f) basis set were used for single point energy calculations.[5, 6] DFT-D3(BJ) and DFT-D3 correction were applied, respectively, for B3LYP and M06-2X to correct the vdW-dispersion energy.[7] The integral equation formalism variant of the polarizable continuum model (IEFPCM) was applied to describe the solvent effect.
[S0050 p0] The electrostatic potential (ESP) of the solvent molecule was visualized by Multiwfn and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) 1.9.3.[8-10] The binding energy between a solvent molecule and DME or Li+ ion was calculated by the following equation: Classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the electrolytes were performed with the GROMACS 2024 software package.
[S0051 p0] OPLS-AA force field was chosen for the simulations and Auxiliary Tools of Force Field (AuToFF) tool was applied to parametrize all atoms. 1.2*CM5 charge model was applied to the solvent molecules, while parameters from 0.8*OPLS-2009IL force field were derived for TFSI− and NO3−.[11, 12] The initial coordinates of the molecules were generated by the Packmol program in a 6.5 × 6.5 × 6.5 nm3 simulation box.[13] The numbers of molecules in each simulation cell are listed in Table S3.
[S0052 p0] The simulation process consists of an initial energy minimization at 0 K with the conjugate gradient method, an initial heating process to 298 K for 1 ns, and a 15 ns run in the NPT ensemble (at 1 bar).
[S0053 p0] Last 5 ns of the NPT simulation data was used for statistical analysis.
[S0054 p0] A timestep of 1 fs was used in the simulations.
[S0055 p0] The C-rescale barostat and V-rescale thermostat were used to control the pressure and temperature, respectively.
[S0056 p0] Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations for electrolytes with polysulfide were conducted with Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) 6.4.2.
[S0057 p0] Exchange-correlation potentials were parameterized with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) employing the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional.[14] The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to represent the core electrons and a plane-wave energy cutoff of 550 eV was chosen.[15] The k-point sampling was performed at Gamma point only because of the high computational demands.
[S0058 p0] The number of molecules in the simulation box was determined based on the electrolyte density.
[S0059 p0] The initial coordinates of the molecules were generated by the Packmol program.
[S0060 p0] For MSE-MtBE, 15 DOL, 10 DME, 7 MtBE, 3 LiTFSI, 1 LiNO3, and 1 Li2S6 were placed in a 1.8 × 1.8 × 1.8 nm3 simulation box; for DOL/DME, 20 DOL, 14 DME, 3 LiTFSI, 1 LiNO3, and 1 Li2S6 were placed in a 1.75 × 1.75 × 1.75 nm3 box.
[S0061 p0] AIMD simulations were conducted at 300 K in the NVT ensemble for 15 ps with last 5 ps of data extracted for statistical analysis.
[S0062 p0] Temperature was controlled by a Nosé−Hoover thermostat.
[S0063 p0] Tritium masses on solvents were substituted for protons to allow a timestep of 1 fs.
