[M0002 p1] Interfaces 2022, 14, 46457−46470 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Due to the poor electronic conductivity of solid sulfur and sulfides, the dissolution of Sα−(α = 0, 2/8, 2/6, 2/4) into a liquid electrolyte and the vehicular diffusion of Sα−to carbon black are necessary for the electrochemical activity of a sulfur cathode in lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries.
[M0003 p1] However, exactly how much dissolution and diffusion are required for high sulfur utilization and how this may control the minimum electrolyte/sulfur ratio, (E/S)min, have not been quantitatively settled.
[M0004 p1] In this work, we show experimentally that a dissolved polysulfide concentration which is too high (>10−20 MS) may gel the liquid electrolyte, leading to catastrophic loss of Sα−mobility, a failure mode that is especially susceptible in a high-donor-number (DN) electrolyte under a lean condition (low E/S), similar to a traffic jam, resulting in high electrochemical polarization and low sulfur utilization.
[M0005 p1] In contrast, we show that a low-DN electrolyte, even with a low polysulfide solubility of 0.1−0.5 MS, will never encounter a gelation catastrophe even at extremely low E/S, leading to unprecedentedly high energy density.
[M0006 p1] Specifically, high sulfur utilizations of 96% (1600 mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) are reached in an electrolyte as lean as E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S coin cells when DME1.6LiFSI-HFE of low solvation capability (DN = 13.9) is adopted, even paired against a high-sulfur-loading cathode (5 mg cm−2).
[M0007 p1] KEYWORDS: lithium-sulfur battery, electrolyte/sulfur ratio, gelation, high sulfur utilization, gravimetric energy density ■INTRODUCTION Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) is a promising post-lithium-ion battery chemistry due to the high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1, based on the active electrode materials of lithium and sulfur)1−3 and low price (the price of sulfur is US$0.1 kg−1).4,5 While Li-S chemistry is very attractive in applications where gravimetric energy density matters a great deal, it confronts great challenges in coming to the realization of the high actual energy density on a full-cell level.6−10 One of the most emerging concerns is the low sulfur utilization under a lean electrolyte condition (the electrolyte condition in a Li-S battery can be described by the electrolyte/sulfur ratio, E/S, which is the total volume of injected liquid electrolyte to the total weight of sulfur in the cell, in units of μL mg−1; a lean electrolyte condition means low E/S).
[M0008 p1] The E/S currently used in Li-S batteries is usually larger than 5 μL mg−1 to boost the sulfur utilization.
[M0009 p1] This means that the inactive liquid electrolyte weighs several times more than the active sulfur.
[M0010 p1] The exact reason why such a high E/S was required has never been clearly explained in the literature, but what researchers have found experimentally was that when the E/S ratio was reduced to 2 μL mg−1, high polarization is always observed at the end of the first discharging plateau in the Li-S battery, accompanied by the absence of the second discharging plateau and thus low sulfur utilization (as shown in Figure S1).11 Theoretically, due to the poor electronic conductivity of solid sulfur and sulfides, the operation of a sulfur cathode relies on local sulfur mobility (LSM), which means that solid insulating sulfur species first dissolve into the liquid electrolyte and subsequently diffuse to the nearest conductive carbon black to access free electrons and to perform redox reactions, as shown in Figure 1a.12,13 Therefore, the dissolution and vehicular diffusion of Sα−(α = 0, 2/8, 2/6, 2/4) are two key steps for the electrochemical activity of the sulfur cathode.
[M0011 p1] The dissolution of polysulfides can stimulate the LSM to promote the electrochemical kinetics of the sulfur cathode, but it can also cause global sulfur mobility (GSM), which is the movement of active substances between positive and negative electrodes, leading to the loss of active substances and the polysulfide shuttle effect.
[M0012 p1] Therefore, a proper amount of dissolution and diffusion is vital to achieve a high sulfur utilization, which fortunately can be optimized by adjusting the E/S ratio.
[M0013 p1] To make the ensuing discussions easier, we will use MS ≡mol(Sα−) L−1 as the unit of dissolution: that is, the number of moles of Sα−dissolved in 1 L of the liquid solution phase (e.g., if the solubility of Li2S8 is 1 M L−1 then the dissolution of S1/4−is 8 MS).

[M0017 p2] Based on the thermodynamic reaction equilibrium, the MS value in an actual cell should depend on the intrinsic electrolyte solvation capability as well as E/S, especially in a high-DN electrolyte.
[M0018 p2] For example, with an unrealistically high E/S such as 10 μL mg−1 of DOL+DME, which is unfortunately frequently used in academic research and the literature, the maximum MS value in an actual cell during cycling may be around 5 MS, very high but not yet problematic.
[M0019 p2] However, when E/S = 3 μL mg−1 of the same electrolyte is used, the maximum MS value in an actual cell during cycling will skyrocket to beyond 10−15 MS, which could be highly problematic for the cell, as we will demonstrate.
[M0020 p2] One school of thought why this is necessary is the “complete dissolution theory”: that is, the complete dissolution of polysulfides into the liquid electrolyte at some point in the discharge and charge is a necessary condition for the Li-S battery to cycle.
[M0021 p2] According to this school of thought, high polarization in a lean electrolyte Li-S battery can be attributed to the incomplete dissolution of polysulfide:11,14,15 that is, some polysulfides are dissolved as Sα−(electrolyte), while others remain as solid-phase Li2Sn (α = 2/n).
[M0022 p2] For example, the solubility of Li2S8 was reported to be 6−7 MS in a common ether-solvent electrolyte, and assuming all sulfur inventory in the cathode must exist as dissolved Sα−(α = 2/8) in the first discharge plateau and no solid sulfur inventory exists at that point, this would require the minimum electrolyte amount to be (E/S)min = 4−5 μL/mg.15 When the E/S ratio is lower than 4−5 μL mg−1, the “complete dissolution theory” would say Li2S8 generated in the battery discharge would exceed the solubility limit and precipitate out as a solid phase, which somehow will block the conduction pathways and result in high polarization.14−16 Nazar et al.17,18 have reported that a Li- S battery could also deliver high sulfur utilization in a sparingly solvated electrolyte (SSE), which has very small solubility for Li2Sn.
[M0023 p2] The solubility of Li2Sn in an SSE was estimated to be less than 3 wt % in a cell, which means that 97 wt % of the polysulfides cannot dissolve and act as a solid phase during the battery operation.
[M0024 p2] The normal operation of the Li-S battery in an SSE indicates that the complete dissolution of polysulfides at some point is not a necessary condition for a Li-S battery to cycle, which contradicts the complete dissolution theory.
[M0025 p2] Therefore, the enlarged polarization in the lean electrolyte Li-S battery may not necessarily becaused by the incomplete dissolution of polysulfides and the failure of the battery under a lean-electrolyte condition might be the outcome of multiple causes.
[M0026 p2] Instead, we show experimentally in this work that the high dissolution of polysulfides (e.g., 30 MS) in ether-solvent electrolyte is responsible for the high polarization in the leanelectrolyte Li-S battery.
[M0027 p2] We found that the reported solubility of Li2S8 (6−7 MS) in the literature mainly comes from the test results of coin cells through a dilution approach and may not be accurate.19,20 The total amount of electrolyte added in cells often cannot be completely absorbed by the positive electrode due to the existence of a large number of head spaces, and the fraction of electrolyte that can actually participate in the dissolution of polysulfide is often much smaller.

[M0040 p3] The unexpectedly high solubility limit means that the solid Li2Sn can indeed mostly dissolve into the liquid electrolyte, Li2Sn(solid) →2Li+(liquid) + nSα−(liquid), even under condition as lean as E/S ≤1 μL mg−1 (if the Li2S8 completely dissolves, E/S = 1 μL mg−1 corresponds to 31.2 MS).
[M0041 p3] However, a value greater than 10 MS is not a good thing.
[M0042 p3] In other words, when E/S sweeps from the flooded condition (e.g., 5 μL mg−1) to the lean condition (e.g., 1 μL mg−1), the actual concentration of Sα−in the liquid electrolyte will skyrocket and can become super highly concentrated (∼30 MS) or even reach the electrolyte gelation point (∼60 MS) under an ultralean condition (E/S ≤1 μL mg−1).
[M0043 p3] When this occurs, the mobility of Sα−in the liquid electrolyte will experience a catastrophic loss due to the sharp increased viscosity, as as shown in the schematic in Figure 1d.
[M0044 p3] A frozen Sα−transport, due to ironically too much dissolved Sα−, is definitely undesirable for the electrochemical activity of the sulfur cathode because the Sα−jammed in the electrolyte cannot reach the carbon black to access free electrons and perform redox reactions (see Figure 1b).
[M0045 p3] We have shown in this work that this is the typical failure mode in a leanelectrolyte Li-S battery with a high-donor-number (DN) electrolyte.
[M0046 p3] To better interpret the relationship between the concentration and the mobility of Sα−, we can envision the cars in a traffic jam, all of which show almost no mobility at high concentration.21,22 Up to now, few methods have been available to improve the sulfur utilization under a lean-electrolyte condition.
[M0047 p3] Especially, a high-sulfur-utilization Li-S battery under an ultraleanelectrolyte condition (e.g., E/S = 1 μL mg−1) has not yet been reported.
[M0048 p3] Herein, we show that a high dissolution of polysulfides >5 MS is definitely overkill and unnecessary to reach high sulfur utilization.
[M0049 p3] Rather, reducing the solubility limit is an effective way to improve the Li-S battery performance under a lean-electrolyte condition.
[M0050 p3] According to Faraday’s law, Q = nzF, we show that only 0.1 MS Sα− solubility can sustain a 0.5C current density in a Li-S battery while 0.5 MS solubility can sustain over 2C current density, even under a lean condition of E/S = 1 μL mg−1, as shown in Figure S2.
[M0051 p3] This means that a 0.1−0.5 MS solubility limit, far different from the 5−60 MS we have in an ether-based electrolyte, is already sufficient to sustain a high electrochemical activity of the sulfur cathode.
[M0052 p3] Philosophically this makes sense, since we know 1 M Li salt is sufficient to support long-range Li+ transport in all kinds of batteries; thus, in order to support local sulfur mobility (LSM) while cutting down global sulfur mobility (GSM),12,13 one should not need much more than 1 MS for Sα−!
[M0053 p3] This consideration paints a different conceptual picture from the “complete dissolution theory” and points to a very different optimal MS-parametric regime (less than 1 MS, rather than >5 MS) for the liquid electrolyte design for a Li-S battery.
[M0054 p3] Most importantly, by cutting down the intrinsic solubility limit, there is never complete dissolution at any point, and in fact most of the sulfur and sulfides are in the form of Li2Sn(solid) and only a small fraction is in the form of Sα−(liquid) in the electrolyte, and so the liquid electrolyte will never encounter a gelation catastrophe even at an extremely low E/S.
[M0055 p3] Quantitatively, the optimum [Sα−(liquid)] might be around 0.5 MS to support LSM, and this value is far from the electrolyte gelation point (>30 MS).
[M0056 p3] By preserving the mobility of Sα−under a lean condition (Figure 1d), which can only move by vehicular diffusion and not by a Grotthusslike exchange mechanism like H+/Li+, one can thus achieve an unprecedentedly low (E/S)min and a high energy density of the Li-S battery.
[M0057 p3] In this work, through adopting the sparingly solvated electrolyte DME1.6LiFSI-HFE with a low DN of 13.9, the dissolution of polysulfides was suppressed to an extremely low level.
[M0058 p3] As a result, high sulfur utilizations of 96% (1600 mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) in lean electrolytes with E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S coin cells (tested at room temperature) have been reached, even paired against a highsulfur-loading cathode (5 mg cm−2).
[M0059 p3] Combining with the highsulfur-loading cathode we developed in the previous work,23 a 2.3 Ah Li-S pouch cell assembled with industrially satisfactory parameters (sulfur loading 7 mg cm−2, E/S = 1 μL mg−1, 1× lithium excess) was demonstrated; it turns out that the practical energy density can reach 589 Wh kg−1. ■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION Material and Electrolyte Preparation.

[M0122 p5] Previously, the solubility of Li2Sn in the commonly used DOL +DME electrolyte was reported to be 6−7 MS (based on Li2S8) through a dilution test approach, where an extra amount of stoichiometric Li2S and S8 was added to the solvents, followed by a certain period of stirring (typically at 60 °C for 10 h), and then the saturated solution was diluted gradually until all species were dissolved.19,20 In this case, the solubility based on Li2S8 could be calculated with the total added solvent amount and the Li2S8 molarity according to Li2S + 7 8S8 → Li2S8.
[M0123 p5] Note that such a dilution approach assumes that Li2S and S8 react completely in the first place and the only undissolved species is Li2S8, which, however, is not necessarily true.
[M0124 p5] As a matter of fact, we examined the precipitates in the “saturated solution” by preparing concentrated Li2S8 in DME (15.6 MS, corresponding to E/S = 2 μL mg−1).
[M0125 p5] After stirring at 60 °C for 10 h, there are still a large amount of precipitate in the bottom of the bottle, as shown in Figure S3a.
[M0126 p5] The Raman spectrum and XRD analysis (Figure S3b,c) indicate that the precipitates mainly consist of S8, with a small part being Li2S, which is consistent with the weight percent of S8 and Li2S used to reach Li2S8 (∼7:1).
[M0127 p5] That is, the solubility of polysulfides may be larger than 6−7 MS because the chemical reaction between Li2S and S8 is actually not complete at high concentration; we adopted an evaporation method to test the solubility in this study, which is more widely applied in solubility tests to obtain a more accurate solubility of Li2Sn.
[M0128 p5] As shown in Figure 2a, a dilute Li2Sn solution (no precipitates at the beginning) was first obtained by mixing stoichiometric Li2S and S8 in excess solvent (e.g., E/S = 10 μL mg−1, 3.12 MS); after a full reaction, a homogeneous dilute solution was then evaporated gradually until solid-phase Li2Sn started to appear.
[M0129 p5] The immediate concentration was recorded as the solubility of Li2Sn.
[M0130 p5] Surprisingly, we found that the solubility of Li2S8 is far more than 6−7 MS.
[M0131 p5] Figure S4b and Figure 2b,c show the optical microscopy images (500× magnification) of DME with different concentrations of Li2S8.
[M0132 p5] These images were obtained by absorbing the electrolyte in a capillary tube and then observing through an optical microscope, as schematically shown in Figure S4a.
[M0133 p5] This indicated that no solid precipitates were found in the 10.4 MS (Figure S4b) and 15.6 MS (Figure 2b) electrolytes, even though the current Li2S8 concentrations of which in DME have exceeded those commonly reported.
[M0134 p5] As we further evaporated the solvents to 31.2 MS (corresponding to E/S = 1 μL mg−1), the electrolyte mixture became slurrylike with precipitates showing up, as revealed in Figure 2c.
[M0135 p5] However, a Raman spectral analysis identified these precipitates as elemental S8 instead of Li2S8 (Figure S5).
[M0136 p5] The generation of S8 may be attribute to the disproportionation reaction Li2S8 →S8 + Li2S6/Li2S4/Li2S2, which was reported to be exacerbated at high Li2S8 concentration.24 Therefore, while it might be difficult to evaluate the solubility of Li2S8 exactly, one could conclude that the solubility of Li2S8 must be greaater than 6−7 MS and may exceed 31.2 MS because no solid Li2S8 precipitated in the 31.2 MS electrolyte.
[M0137 p5] The above means that, chemically, ethers really prefer to mix with sulfides.
[M0138 p5] Particular attention should be paid to the viscosity evolution of the electrolyte as the concentration of Li2S8 increases, and importantly, it is directly related to the vehicular diffusion of Sn 2−.
[M0139 p5] From the inset in Figure 2c, the electrolyte has become quite sticky and could be drawn into a long line at 31.2 MS, indicating a high viscosity.
[M0140 p5] The red line in Figure 2e quantitatively shows that the viscosity of the electrolyte has increased about 3 orders of magnitude from 4.3 to 1.8 × 103 mPa s when the polysulfide concentration increases from 6.24 to 31.2 MS (the viscosity was tested in a rheometer with its rotation mode at 25 °C, and the value of the viscosity was chosen at a shear rate of 500 s−1).
[M0141 p5] Meanwhile, Figure S6 shows that the electrolyte has changed from a Newtonian fluid to a non-Newtonian fluid when the concentration is increased to 31.2 MS.

[M0188 p7] In the previous section, we concluded that the gelation of the electrolyte initiated by the high dissolution of Li2Sn was the main reason for the enlarged polarization in the leanelectrolyte Li-S battery.
[M0189 p7] As a verification, in the following sections, we prepared an electrolyte with low solvation capability to suppress the dissolution of Li2Sn and to study the electrochemical performance of a lean-electrolyte Li-S battery.
[M0190 p7] Generally speaking, the solubility of polysulfides is determined by the solvation capability of electrolytes.
[M0191 p7] In this work, electrolytes with low solvation capabilities were reached by regulating the concentration of lithium salts, as reported previously.27−29 The lithium salt used in our experiment is lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), and the solvent is DME, both of which are reported to have great compatibility with the lithium metal anode.28Figure 4a shows the Raman spectra of electrolytes with different LiFSI concentrations.
[M0192 p7] The peaks located at 1138 and 1123 cm−1 are respectively correlated to the asymmetric stretching mode of C−O−C (νas(C−O)) in DME before and after coordination with Li+.30,31 Commonly, the content of the free solvents in the electrolyte is an important index of the solvation capability that can be reflected by the intensity of the Raman peaks.
[M0193 p7] It can be found that the ratio between the coordinated DME and free DME increases rapidly along with the increment of LiFSI concentration, as indicated by the variation of the peak intensity at 1123 and 1138 cm−1.
[M0194 p7] In particular, when the concentration of LiFSI exceeds 3.2 M ((DME)3LiFSI), the original peak at 1138 cm−1 has disappeared, suggesting that most of the free DME solvents are complexing with Li+.
[M0195 p7] Nazar et al. have achieved dendrite-free lithium plating of the electrolyte at a E/S ratio of 5 μL mg−1 by using a diglyme electrolyte with a saturated LiTFSI salt, G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1), to adjust the electrolyte network structure.22 As a comparison, we also studied the Raman spectra of electrolytes adopting lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the salt, shown in Figure 4b.
[M0196 p7] From Figure 4b we find that LiTFSI is not good at lowering the electrolyte solvation capability, as typically there are still a large number of free DME molecules in the electrolyte even when the concentration of LiTFSI is increased to 6 M ((DME)1.6LiTFSI, approaching the saturation concentration of LiTFSI in DME).
[M0197 p7] The high content of the free DME in the LiTFSI-based electrolyte makes it incapable of suppressing the dissolution of Li2Sn effectively, which also results in electrolyte gelation under leanelectrolyte conditions.
[M0198 p7] The reason for such a contrast in coordinating DME with the LiFSI salt may be attributed to the smaller bonding energies between Li+ and FSI−, and thus DME can easily participate in the solvation shell and coordinate with Li+, rendering less free DME.
[M0199 p7] On the other hand, the intensifying coordination environment of Li+ with TFSI−in the LiTFSI-based electrolyte makes TFSI−more likely to participate in the solvation shell, resulting in less DME coordination with Li+ and concomitantly more free DME.32 Figure 4.

[M0209 p8] The donor number (DN) is often a measure of the ability of a solvent to solvate cations.
[M0210 p8] Herein, to describe the depressed solvation capability more quantitatively, we develop a donor number (DN) testing method to study the solvation capability of electrolytes.
[M0211 p8] The testing method of DN was referred to a study by Hahn et al.,33 who adopted standard proton (1H) NMR with chloroform (CHCl3) as the reference acceptor to establish a correlation between the CHCl3 1H NMR shift and the solvent donor number.
[M0212 p8] The theoretical basis is that the molecular interaction between chloroform and the solvent would decrease the electron shielding at the hydrogen of chloroform when CHCl3 is added into donor solvents, and the shift of chloroform in donor solvents will be downfield of the shift of pure chloroform, since the acceptor hydrogen loses shielding.
[M0213 p8] The downfield shift (Δδ) increases with increasing DN and is more pronounced for a dilute solution wherein the isolated chloroform molecules are surrounded by solvent molecules.
[M0214 p8] In this study, we use this method to evaluate the DN of electrolytes with different lithium salts and lithium salt concentrations to describe the solvation capability of electrolyte quantitatively.
[M0215 p8] In detail, 0.1 M CHCl3 was added to the different electrolytes, and then we measured the chemical shift of 1H NMR (δ(1H)) of CHCl3.
[M0216 p8] Figure S13a shows that δ(1H) in pure CHCl3 is 7.29 ppm.
[M0217 p8] Figure 4c shows the δ(1H) values of CHCl3 in the different electrolytes and the corresponding Δδ values on comparison with the δ(1H) value in pure CHCl3.
[M0218 p8] It reveals a lower Δδ with an increasing LiFSI concentration, indicating a smaller decrease of the electron shielding at the hydrogen of chloroform, which reflects the lower electrondonating ability and solvation capability.
[M0219 p8] The NMR testing results are consistent with the Raman spectrum results shown in Figure 4a.
[M0220 p8] To describe the solvation capability of electrolyte quantitatively, we give a simple correction between the Δδ and DN, DN = λΔδ(CHCl3).
[M0221 p8] To obtain λ, we first tested the Δδ value of CHCl3 in pure DME solvent, which has a known DN (DN = 20), to determine the coefficient (see Figure S13b and Figure 4c) to be λ = 10.1.
[M0222 p8] Then, we calculated the DN of each electrolyte by using the formula DN = 10.1Δδ(CHCl3).
[M0223 p8] Figure 4d shows the DN of each electrolyte calculated by our method, from which it is obvious that LiFSI with high concentration leads to a low DN, and the lowest DN was achieved in DME1.6LiFSI with a value of 15.
[M0224 p8] Our method is more accurate and quantitative than Raman spectrometry results.
[M0225 p8] In Raman spectra (Figure 4a), DME3LiFSI and DME1.6LiFSI have the same spectra, making it difficult to determine the relatively greater (or smaller) solvation capability.
[M0226 p8] As a comparison, the DN of electrolytes with LiTFSI has also been tested in this work, as shown in Figure 4c,d.
[M0227 p8] Not surprisingly, the LiFSIbased electrolytes we used have a lower DN than LiTFSI-based electrolytes at each concentration, which is beneficial to suppress the dissolution of Li2Sn.
[M0228 p8] These electrolytes with different solvation capabilities will have different solubilities to Li2Sn, as we will show in the following.
[M0229 p8] It worth noting that we have developed a method to evaluate the solvation capability of electrolytes quantitatively, and the DN calculated in our work may not equal the DN tested by conventional calorimetry.

[M0314 p11] In the meantime, the electrochemical results show that lowering the electrolyte solvation capability is effective in postponing the electrolyte gelation and is beneficial for sulfur utilization under the lean-electrolyte condition.
[M0315 p11] A value of 0.1− 0.5 MS should suffice to support LSM and reach high sulfur utilization in the Li-S battery, as indicated in Figure S2.
[M0316 p11] Here, we want to emphasize that the solvation capability of highconcentration electrolytes must be depressed to a certain low value so that a high sulfur utilization is attainable.
[M0317 p11] As shown above that LiTFSI is incapable of depressing the solvation capability of DME, it cannot contribute to a high sulfur utilization in a lean-electrolyte Li-S battery due to the complexation between polysulfides and the lithium ion solvation structure.
[M0318 p11] Herein, we provide a simple method to identify the solvation capability of electrolytes.
[M0319 p11] We find that the electrolyte’s solvation capability is related to the reduction potential of S8 to Li2S8 in the discharge process of the Li-S battery.
[M0320 p11] Therefore, the reduction peak can be used to evaluate the solvation capability of electrolytes.
[M0321 p11] Figure 6g shows the discharge dQ/dV curves of the Li-S battery, from which we can see that the electrolytes with higher solvation capability lead to a higher reduction potential.
[M0322 p11] The reduction peaks of (DME)9LiFSI, (DME)3LiFSI-HFE, and (DME)1.6LiFSI-HFE are 2.29, 2.19, and 2.16 V, respectively.
[M0323 p11] Also, as shown in Figure S20b, the reduction peak in (DME)1.6LiTFSI-HFE is 2.22 V, higher than that of (DME)3LiFSI-HFE and lower than that of (DME)9LiFSI.
[M0324 p11] This reduction potential method is reasonable because the solvation energy aroused by the electrolyte to S8 (S8(s) →S8(l), ΔGsolvation) will increase the total free energy change (ΔGtotal = ΔG + ΔGsolvation) of the redox reaction, S8 + 2Li+ + 2e−→Li2S8.37,38 According to the Nernst equation, ΔG = −nFU, a higher ΔGsolvation would result in a higher ΔGtotal and a higher reduction potential of S8.
[M0325 p11] From the reduction potential in different electrolytes, we estimate the upper limit of reduction potential that enables the Li-S battery to work under a lean-electrolyte condition lies in the range of 2.16−2.19 V.
[M0326 p11] Once the reduction potential exceeds this limit, the solvation capability of the electrolyte is too high and is incapable of suppressing polysulfide dissolution effectively.

[M0365 p12] More importantly, we demonstrated that the sulfur cathode can perform electrochemical reactions independently of the dissolution of polysulfide and operate under an ultralow electrolyte condition (E/S = 1 μL mg−1) by reducing the solvation capacity of the electrolyte, which can effectively delay the gelation transition and improve the electrochemical performance of Li-sulfur batteries under leanelectrolyte conditions.
[M0366 p12] Specifically, high sulfur utilizations of 96% (1600 mAh g−1) and 78% (1300 mAh g−1) are reached in electrolytes as lean as E/S = 2 and 1 μL mg−1 Li-S coin cells, respetively, even paired against a high-sulfur-loading cathode (5 mg cm−2).
[M0367 p12] Furthermore, a Li-S pouch cell (with dimensions of 5.0 cm × 6.0 cm, sulfur loading of 7 mg cm−2, and 1× lithium excess) was constructed with E/S = 1 μL mg−1 and it turns out that the actual energy density of the Li-S battery can reach 589 Wh kg−1, doubling that of state-of-the-art LIBs. ■ASSOCIATED CONTENT * sı Supporting Information The Supporting Information is available free of charge at https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.2c10906.
