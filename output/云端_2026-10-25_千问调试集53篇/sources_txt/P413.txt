[M0003 p1] Selection of ionic liquid electrolytes for high-performing lithium-sulfur batteries: An experiment-guided high-throughput machine learning analysis Aysegul Kilic a, Omar Abdelaty a, Muhammad Zeeshan b,e, Alper Uzun b,c,d, Ramazan Yildirim a,*, Damla Eroglu a,* a Bogazici University, Department of Chemical Engineering, 34342 Istanbul, Turkey b Koç University, Department of Chemical and Biological Engineering, 34450 Istanbul, Turkey c Koç University, Koç University TÜPRAS ̧ Energy Center (KUTEM), 34450 Istanbul, Turkey d Koç University, Koç University Surface Science and Technology Center (KUYTAM), 34450 Istanbul, Turkey e Case Western Reserve University, Department of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering, 44106 Cleveland, OH, USA A R T I C L E I N F O Keywords: Li-S battery Ionic liquid Polysulfide solubility Electrolyte viscosity Ionic liquid descriptor A B S T R A C T The polysulfide (PS) shuttle mechanism (PSM) is one of the most significant challenges of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries in achieving high capacity and cyclability.
[M0004 p1] One way to minimize the shuttle effect is to limit the PS solubilities in the battery electrolyte.
[M0005 p1] Ionic liquids (IL) are particularly suited as electrolyte solvents because of their tunable physical and chemical properties.
[M0006 p1] In this work, thousands of ILs are screened to narrow down potentially viable candidates to be used as electrolytes in Li-S batteries.
[M0007 p1] To that end, the COnductor-like Screening Model for Realistic Solvents (COSMO-RS) calculations are performed over more than 36,000 ILs.
[M0008 p1] An extensive database containing PS solubilities and other relevant properties is constructed at 25 ◦C.
[M0009 p1] First, the effectiveness of the COSMO-RS calculations is experimentally tested with six different ILs having a wide range of solubility and viscosity values; a strong correlation between the PS solubility and battery performance is ob­ tained.
[M0010 p1] After specifying the target limits for promising ILs using the experimental battery performance data, machine learning (ML) tools are used to predict and identify the relationship between IL properties and PS solubilities and structural and molecular descriptors of ILs.
[M0011 p1] The extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) method successfully predicts the solubility and property values.
[M0012 p1] Association rule mining (ARM) and the feature impor­ tance analysis show that anion descriptors are more dominant, whereas cations have less impact on the solu­ bilities and properties of ILs.
[M0013 p1] Finally, the imidazolium and pyridinium ILs with bis_imide and borate anion groups are identified as the most promising ones. 1.

[M0020 p2] Furthermore, limited availability and the high cost of some active ma­ terials manufacturing the Li-ion batteries prevent their wider adapta­ tion.
[M0021 p2] Therefore, beyond Li-ion battery technologies have been explored to reach the set targets regarding energy density, cost, and other essential properties of future applications [1–4].
[M0022 p2] In this respect, lithiumsulfur (Li-S) batteries are one of the best alternatives to Li-ion batteries.
[M0023 p2] They have high theoretical gravimetric and volumetric energy densities of 2600 Wh/kg and 2800 Wh/L, respectively.
[M0024 p2] Sulfur, with a theoretical specific capacity of 1675 mAh/g, is an abundant, non-toxic, and envi­ ronmentally friendly cathode active material.
[M0025 p2] However, Li-S batteries suffer from major drawbacks, namely the shuttling of the polysulfide (PS) intermediates, which is called the polysulfide shuttle mechanism (PSM), the insulating nature of sulfur and Li2S, and lithium anode degradation, that must be resolved before their practical applications and broader commercial adaptations.
[M0026 p2] The PSM is a fatal problem in the Li-S batteries [3–5], typically addressed using specialized cathodes, separators, and/or electrolytes.
[M0027 p2] In general, electrolytes of any battery chemistry are optimized to have high ionic transport (attributed to low viscosity), good chemical, thermal, and electrochemical stability (i.e., wide electrochemical window), low cost, and high safety.
[M0028 p2] The shuttle effect puts an additional constraint on standard electrolyte properties by requiring that the electrolyte has low but finite PS solubility and mobility to minimize the loss of active ma­ terial and cathode structure degradation while retaining sufficient ki­ netics in Li-S batteries, especially at high sulfur loadings [6].
[M0029 p2] This constraint is further complicated by the fact that the PSs form a highly dynamic system that undergoes several rapid disproportionation re­ actions whereby S-S bonds are continuously being broken and recreated [7,8].
[M0030 p2] Hence, it is difficult to measure or predict the solubility of PSs in the electrolyte.
[M0031 p2] The typically used liquid electrolytes are ether or aprotic solventbased electrolytes that offer excellent ionic transport and stability of PS ions.
[M0032 p2] Specifically, the mixture of 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-Dime­ thoxyethane (DME) with the lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) salt is the most common electrolyte used in Li-S batteries.
[M0033 p2] Un­ fortunately, such solvents suffer from very high PS solubility and, hence, excessive PS shuttle rate.
[M0034 p2] In addition, the low viscosity of the DOL:DME electrolyte also facilitates the fast diffusion of PSs inside the cell.
[M0035 p2] Ionic liquids (ILs) are proposed as alternative electrolyte solvents in the bat­ tery literature [1,9,10].
[M0036 p2] IL electrolytes have relatively low PS solubilities and excellent chemical, electrochemical, and thermal stabilities.
[M0037 p2] In addition, many ILs have sufficiently low melting points as well.
[M0038 p2] How­ ever, their high viscosity and, thus, moderate ionic conductivity chal­ lenge pose a downside.
[M0039 p2] In the literature, typically bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide ([TFSI]-) anion is explored with cations from different groups, including pyrrolidinium, piperidinium, sulfonium, and imida­ zolium as Li-S battery electrolytes [11–13].
[M0040 p2] Recently, ILs have been used in hybrid electrolyte systems to combine the benefits and mitigate the drawbacks of both ether-based and IL solvents [12–16].
[M0041 p2] The most common ethers include DOL, DME, and tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME).
[M0042 p2] To reap the benefits of IL electrolytes, screening for ILs with optimum electrolyte properties, including low melting point, viscosity, and PS solubility, is necessary.
[M0043 p2] Among them, the most difficult property to measure and predict is the solubility of PSs.
[M0044 p2] Such difficulty arises from the dynamic nature of these ions, in addition to the inherent difficulty of measuring solubility values.
[M0045 p2] To simplify the problem, Ueno et al.
[M0046 p2] [17] measured the total solubility of atomic sulfur, which included all PS species in the system, in the solvate IL electrolytes of equimolar mixtures of lithium salts and triglyme and tetraglyme (Li(Glyme)X) combined with various anions.
[M0047 p2] In the end, they concluded that the stronger the donor ability of the IL, the higher their ability to solvate PSs.
[M0048 p2] More specifically, they found that [TFSI]- and bis(pentafluoroethanesulfonyl) amide ([BETA]-) anions effectively suppressed the dissolution of Li2Sx species while maintaining reasonable conductivity, viscosity, and sta­ bility.
[M0049 p2] [18] from the same group conducted a similar study to measure the solubility of PSs in various common ILs.
[M0050 p2] For the [P14]- [OTF](trifluoromethane-sulfonate) IL, the solubility of PSs was rela­ tively high, which causes poor cycling performance when used as the electrolyte in a Li-S battery.
[M0051 p2] The results indicated a close connection between the PS solubility in the electrolyte and battery performance.
[M0052 p2] In addition, it was also discussed that when the PS solubilities are similar, the viscosities of the electrolytes become important for good ionic transport in the Li-S cells.
[M0053 p2] Similarly, low PS solubility and high Li+ ion diffusion were found to be the key criteria, especially at low electrolyteto-sulfur ratios, to attain high energy density Li-S batteries [19].
[M0054 p2] The presence of such complex and diverse constraints makes the search for optimum ILs a complex, multi-dimensional, and non-linear problem.
[M0055 p2] Extensive experimental testing of a wide set of ILs is not feasible considering that the number of available cations, anions, and their combinations in ILs is staggeringly massive, and experimental testing of even a handful of ILs is heavily time-consuming.
[M0056 p2] Instead, ex­ perts can use chemical intuition and educated guessing to find and test potentially suitable candidates as a common practice in materials sci­ ence.
[M0057 p2] This chemical intuition is learned by experience over a long period of time, and learning from previous experiences is the basis for the machine learning (ML) [20].
[M0058 p2] On the other hand, fast screening calcu­ lations are helpful in narrowing down potential targets for further investigation, which only requires crude and quick models.
[M0059 p2] One such model is COSMO-RS, which stands for Conductor-Like Screening Model for Real Solvents [21].
[M0060 p2] COSMO-RS is a well-established quantum chemistry-based computational model to estimate semi-quantitatively the thermodynamic properties of chemical mixtures [22].
[M0061 p2] The basic idea behind the COSMO-RS model is to treat the interactions in a mo­ lecular system as if they are between an ensemble of discrete surface screening charge densities, denoted as sigma (σ), for each molecule or ion in the system [23–25].
[M0062 p2] Although the COSMO-RS model calculations are sufficiently fast, they depend on the surface charge densities of the solvent and solute ions, which are not readily available.
[M0063 p2] Therefore, analysis of a sufficiently large representative set of ILs must be done and interpreted to generalize these results on all ILs.
[M0064 p2] With the assistance of ML algorithms, it is possible to find the hidden correlations between the structural, physical, and chemical properties of the IL and the estimated solubility or other predicted properties.
[M0065 p2] ML is essential for materials search and is becoming more feasible with the continuous growth of large materials databases and simulation tools [20,26].
[M0066 p2] Similar to our aim here, some studies in the literature utilize both COSMO-RS solubility predictions and ML techniques to determine suitable ILs for their objectives [27–30].
[M0067 p2] In fact, water and C4 hydrocarbon solubilities in ILs were determined in our previous studies [31,32].
[M0068 p2] Meanwhile, only two studies in the Li-S battery field have used the COSMO-RS method for solubility calculations.
[M0069 p2] The first study focused on predicting cyclic sulfur solubility in ten solvents, and they correctly captured the experimental values [33].
[M0070 p2] The same group used ML classification based on PS solu­ bilities by using the electrolyte descriptors obtained from sigma profiles of electrolytes calculated by the COSMO-RS method for a limited num­ ber of solvate-ILs, salt-in-solvent and solvent-in-salt electrolytes [34].
[M0071 p2] These studies indicate that combining COSMO-RS solubility predictions with ML methods is highly promising.
[M0072 p2] Yet, the number of electrolytes investigated in the previous studies is minimal to develop heuristic rules or predictive models.
[M0073 p2] In contrast, this research focuses on the solubility of Li2S8 in around 36,000 ILs for fast screening, building ML models for predictions, and showing structure–property relationships, which have not been addressed before.
[M0074 p2] In this work, around 36,000 IL solvents are screened for their po­ tential as Li-S battery electrolytes based on their PS (Li2S8) solubilities, viscosities, and melting points obtained by COSMO-RS calculations.
[M0075 p2] Moreover, the cycling performances of Li-S cells with six commercially available ILs having low and high viscosities and PS solubilities are A.
[M0076 p3] Chemical Engineering Journal 490 (2024) 151562 3 examined experimentally to determine the selection criteria used for ML models; a critical connection between the COSMO-RS solubilities and viscosities and the battery performance is identified.
[M0077 p3] The results are further used to train ML models, specifically XGBoost, which was developed to predict the PS solubility in potentially new ILs as the main objective of the work, and the structure–property relationship in ILs is identified using association rule mining (ARM) using ten descriptors for each anion and cation determined by the density functional theory (DFT) calculations. 2.
[M0078 p3] Computational methods 2.1.
[M0079 p3] Solubility and property Calculations COSMOThermX software was used for the estimation of Li2S8 solu­ bilities in ILs at 25 ◦C by performing COSMO-RS calculations [35].
[M0080 p3] The IL database, COSMObaseIL, was used to form the dataset for this study.
[M0081 p3] COSMOThermX uses the σ-profiles calculated using the DFT functional BP and def2-TZVP level.
[M0082 p3] The dataset comprised 98 anions and 370 cations, which adds up to 36,260 pairs of ILs.
[M0083 p3] The long chain Li2S8 was used to model the PSs in the system.
[M0084 p3] In accordance with previous reports about Li2S8 conformation in the solvents [8,36], the linear conformation of Li2S8 was first optimized using DFT by employing the B3LYP/def2- TZVP level theory with the ORCA quantum chemistry software pack­ age [37].
[M0085 p3] The optimized structure conformation was then used as an input file for TMOLEX (v.4.5.3) [38] to generate the standard σ-profile file (.cosmo file).
[M0086 p3] The sigma profile and the corresponding surface of the Li2S8 molecule are given in Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material (SM).

[M0088 p3] C∞ i = 1 γ∞ i (1) The melting point, viscosity, and electrical conductivity were also calculated at 25 ◦C using the IL Properties Module, which also uses the same σ-profile files for the ions.
[M0089 p3] The densities of ILs were calculated using the molecular volume obtained by the COSMO-RS volume calcu­ lations, whereas COSMO-RS enthalpies were used for the melting point calculations.
[M0090 p3] Finally, the same formula utilizing ionic radius and dielectric energies was used for the viscosity and electronic conductivity calculations.
[M0091 p3] The implemented correlation coefficients were naturally different for each calculation. 2.2.
[M0092 p3] Structural descriptors for ILs To calculate structural descriptors for ILs, molecular geometries of cations and anions were individually optimized using Spartan’14 by employing the PM3 semi-empirical method with the default conver­ gence criteria.
[M0093 p3] Similar to our previous studies [31,32], ten descriptors were calculated for each anion and cation separately: Molecular weight (MW in amu), highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies (EHOMO, ELUMO in eV), CPK-area (in Å2) and CPK ovality (O) obtained from the space-filling model by Corey, Pauling, and Koltun (CPK), dipole (μ in D), polariz­ ability (m3), vibrational zero point energy (ZPE in kJ/mol), hydrogen bond donor count (HBD), and hydrogen bond acceptor count (HBA).
[M0094 p3] Although other anion and cation properties can be used as potential descriptors, they were eliminated in our previous works due to crosscorrelations and insignificant effects on the models [31,32].
[M0095 p3] Conse­ quently, there are 20 descriptors for each IL pair.
[M0096 p3] The distribution of ions in terms of these structural descriptors is given in Fig. 1.
[M0097 p3] The complete dataset, including all the properties and the descriptors, can be found in SM as a Microsoft Office ExcelTM file. 2.3.
[M0098 p3] ML modeling Distinct prediction models were trained for each IL property of in­ terest: the solubility of Li2S8, melting point, viscosity, and electrical conductivity.
[M0099 p3] Random sampling was employed to negate the bias to­ wards the majority class.
[M0100 p3] Afterward, the dataset was partitioned into training and test sets using a 75 %-25 % split.
[M0101 p3] Given that PS solubility highly depends on the anion type, random assignment of the data entries into each subset was avoided as it overfitted the ions in the set and performed poorly on new ions (due to the strong effects of the anions).
[M0102 p3] Therefore, the splitting was done either according to the anion or the cation groups.
[M0103 p3] In practice, such splitting means that a random 25 % fraction of anions/cations in each anion/cation group was included in the test set, while the remaining ions were used in the training set.
[M0104 p3] This way, the test set contains new anions/cations not encountered during training or validation.
[M0105 p3] The same strategy was used for 5-fold crossvalidation, where 20 % of the training ions were selected in the vali­ dation sets, and the rest were used for training.
[M0106 p3] Even with this additional constraint, successful and robust models were developed.
[M0107 p3] Next, the partitioned dataset, along with the respective values of properties calculated by COSMOThermX, were used to train ML models.
[M0108 p3] The distribution of descriptors for cations and anions in the dataset.
[M0109 p4] Chemical Engineering Journal 490 (2024) 151562 4 algorithm was used in the predictions.
[M0110 p4] The hyperparameters were optimized with a grid search according to the performance criteria of root mean square error (RMSE), which shows the average difference between the predicted and the COSMO-RS calculated values; hence, the lower it is, the better the predictions.
[M0111 p4] In addition, the R-squared (R2) value, the proportion of the difference between the two, ranging be­ tween 0 and 1, was also reported [39].
[M0112 p4] The optimized hyperparameters for XGBoost are the maximum depth, number of trees, and learning rate (η) values, which were found to be 3, 225, and 0.1, respectively, for the solubility predictions.
[M0113 p4] The descriptor importance was calculated with these hyperparameters over the training set.
[M0114 p4] After the prediction models were built, the ARM method was utilized to classify the promising anions and cations together with their de­ scriptors.
[M0115 p4] The classification models were trained as binary classifiers: class A and class B are the favorable and non-favorable levels of related properties and solubilities, respectively.
[M0116 p4] The thresholds of these classes were determined according to the experimental results and the desired characteristics of a battery elec­ trolyte.
[M0117 p4] In the case of solubility, a threshold value of −0.7 to 0.1 mol/ mol in the log scale was selected as it is comparable to ILs tested in the literature and the performance data obtained in our research [18].
[M0118 p4] Similarly, limits for the viscosity, ln(conductivity (mS/cm)), and melting point classifications were determined as 100 mPa.s, 2, and 0 ◦C, respectively.
[M0119 p4] In the ARM analysis, all these four targets were held to see the most promising ILs as electrolytes of Li-S batteries.
[M0120 p4] The relationships between the anion/cation groups and their de­ scriptors with class A in all four criteria were independently analyzed by a single-factor ARM algorithm.
[M0121 p4] Since ARM only works for categorical data, HBA and HBD counts were turned into factors, and the remaining eight numeric descriptors were categorized into ten multiple intervals with a similar number of data points.
[M0122 p4] The performance metrics, such as support, confidence, and lift, were used together to determine the reli­ ability of a rule.
[M0123 p4] The frequency of a certain descriptor coinciding with the target class in the entire dataset, P(D ∩A), is termed as support.
[M0124 p4] Confidence is the probability that the IL pair belongs to class A given that a certain descriptor is within a particular interval, P(A|D).
[M0125 p4] Lift calculates the correlation by comparing the frequency or the support as compared to random independent coincidence P(D ∩A)/P(A)*P(D).
[M0126 p4] Hence, the lift values larger and smaller than 1.0 correspond to positive and negative correlation, respectively.
[M0127 p4] The more significant the lift value, the stronger the correlation is between the descriptor and that class [40].
[M0128 p4] Thus, the lift value was considered as the ultimate criterion to assess the association between a descriptor and the solubility, as long as the rule clears the minimum support and confidence thresholds, taken as 0.1 % and 3 %, respectively.
[M0129 p4] All the modeling and figure creation were performed in the R Studio environment [41]. 3.
[M0130 p4] Results and discussion 3.1.
[M0131 p4] Pre-analysis of the dataset In this screening part, we aimed to use a dataset representative of most ILs commonly used and studied, as well as the rare ones in the battery literature.
[M0132 p4] Hence, our dataset spans several cation groups, including imidazolium, pyridinium, and ammonium, in addition to an­ ions of different types, including fluorinated, chlorinated, carboxylates, oxyanions, amino acids, etc., and more commonly investigated anions in IL electrolytes such as tetrafluoroborate ([BF4]-), hexafluorophosphate ([PF6]-) and [TFSI]-.
[M0133 p4] The complete lists of the cations and anions groups are given in Table 1, whereas members of each group are presented in the Excel file provided in SM.
[M0134 p4] The table shows that the most crowded groups are imidazolium and pyridinium for cations, while amino acids and carboxylates for the anions.
[M0135 p4] The COSMO-RS solubility screening results, including solubility, viscosity, conductivity, and melting points, are shown in Fig. 2 depending on anion groups, whereas the distributions on the cation groups are presented in Figure S3.
[M0136 p4] When these distributions are compared, solubilities and properties show similar distributions and ranges regardless of the cation groups.
[M0137 p4] On the other hand, noticeable differences are observed when anion groups are considered.
[M0138 p4] This clearly indicates anionic effect dominance on the properties of the ILs.
[M0139 p4] Fig. 2a and Figure S2 for the whole dataset show that the solubility values for each anion group obtained from the calculations span a wide scale of many orders of magnitude from 10-9 to almost 1020 in mol/mol units.
[M0140 p4] This is expected primarily due to the large difference between the activity coefficients of the solute (Li2S8) and the solvents (ILs).
[M0141 p4] Still, the calculations can compare small and larger values, but the extreme re­ sults should be treated cautiously [42,43].
[M0142 p4] When ILs with extremely low and high PS solubilities are analyzed, it is seen that some anions give these abnormal solubility values regardless of the cation types.
[M0143 p4] This may indicate COSMO-RS’s failure to predict the properties of these anions.
[M0144 p4] According to the graph, most amino acids, carboxylates, halogens, nonmetal oxides, and phosphines fall into the extreme solubility ranges, below 10-3 and above 103 mol/mol, and should be treated cautiously.
[M0145 p4] On the other hand, bis_imide, borate, halo-elemental, and others (the rare ones that do not belong to any of the anion groups listed) anion groups show reasonable solubility values.
[M0146 p4] The PS solubility is not the only criterion when selecting electrolytes for Li-S batteries.
[M0147 p4] Other properties are also essential in the screening process of ILs in search of suitable electrolytes for Li-S batteries; the melting point, viscosity, and conductivity are all significant criteria in Li-S cell electrolyte selection to ensure smooth cell operation.
[M0148 p4] This way, the utilization of highly viscous IL electrolytes must be avoided to allow for appreciable Li+ diffusion. 1 M Li salt containing DOL:DME electro­ lyte has a viscosity of 1.6 mPa.s and shows sufficient ionic conductivity [19].
[M0149 p4] In the dataset, the lowest viscosity value calculated using the COSMO-RS is 9.1 mPa.s, which is almost 5-fold higher than that of DOL: DME electrolytes and only 25 % of 36,260 ILs have viscosity values below 134 mPa.s.
[M0150 p4] On the other hand, around 15 % and 7 % of the dataset do not have melting points below 0 ◦C and ln(electronic conductivity) values below 2, respectively.
[M0151 p4] The anion and cation descriptors have been shown to strongly correlate with target properties in previous studies, including the solu­ bility of water and C4 hydrocarbons in various ILs and the physico­ chemical properties of ILs [31,32].
[M0152 p4] The descriptors are simple yet essential structural, electronic, and energetic factors.
[M0153 p4] CPK area and ovality are related to the geometry of ions calculated based on the spacefilling model, which is a crucial indicator of the area of potential in­ teractions with other ions or the PSs.
[M0154 p4] Naturally, the higher the area is, the stronger the interactions in the solution are.
[M0155 p4] However, the ovality effect also plays an important role in the space-filling of ions sur­ rounding the PSs and each other.

[M0159 p5] These are highly important in studying solvation energy and solubility because they directly relate to the interactions during solvation.
[M0160 p5] The potential for hydrogen bond formation is given by the hydrogen bond donor (HBD) and acceptor (HBA) counts [44].
[M0161 p5] PSs are all known to be weak bases, in other words, hydrogen bond acceptors [45].
[M0162 p5] However, this also indicates the potential for the cation and anion to be attracted to each other, which will also affect the solubility.
[M0163 p5] Other important descriptors include electronic and energetic ones, which may affect the solubility less directly.
[M0164 p5] The values of HOMO and LUMO energies calculated by simple DFT calculations contain important information related to ion stability, its potential for electronic in­ teractions, and bond strength.
[M0165 p5] The vibrational zero-point energy is the lowest vibrational energy level and determines the flexibility or stiffness of the bonds in the molecule to stretching and bending.
[M0166 p5] This property describes the flexibility of ions to structural deformation during the solvation process [46,47].
[M0167 p5] Finally, the molecular weight of ions is also useful as it gives information about ion diffusion coefficients, density, and viscosity, which indirectly affect solubility. 3.2.
[M0168 p5] IL selection criteria for high-performance Li-S batteries Experiments were carried out to see if there is a correlation between PS solubilities and IL properties calculated using the COSMO-RS method and Li-S battery performance.
[M0169 p5] Li-S cells with standard cathodes, carbon black mixed with sulfur using the melt-diffusion strategy, and mixed electrolytes containing organic and IL solvents were prepared.
[M0170 p5] As the organic electrolyte, 1 M LiTFSI and 0.1 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) con­ taining DOL:DME (1:1 vol%) was used.
[M0171 p5] First, experiments were per­ formed to see the effect of the IL/organic solvent ratio (0, 25, and 50 vol % IL) and the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio on the cycling perfor­ mance (Figure S5); the rest of the experiments were conducted at an IL ratio of 25 vol% and E/S ratio of 13 mL/g.
[M0172 p5] Six commercially available ILs listed in Table 2 were used in the mixed electrolytes.
[M0173 p5] Cycling experi­ ments were shown for 50 cycles at 0.1C in Fig. 3 (the experimental details and 100-cycle performances of mixed electrolytes, including pure DOL:DME, are provided in Figure S6 in SM).

[M0177 p5] Moreover, ILs with low and high viscosities also have both high and low solubility cases.
[M0178 p5] Hence, it is possible to identify the effect of COSMO-RS predicted viscosity and solubility values on the performance of Li-S batteries.
[M0179 p5] The results presented in Fig. 3 supported the discussion on the importance of low solubility but also highlighted the effect of viscosity on the Li-S cell performance.
[M0180 p5] Although high viscosity may suppress the PSM by restricting the PS movement, it also prevents the diffusion of Li+ ions.
[M0181 p5] In this respect, the ILs of [PP14]-[TFSI] and [DEME]-[TFSI] with both low solubility and viscosity show the best cycling performance Fig. 2.

[M0194 p6] Instead, the six ILs were chosen to study the relation between the predicted solubility and viscosity and the cycling performance with the ILs available for experimentation before the screening analysis.
[M0195 p6] Consequently, these are not the ones that are proven to be promising, yet one out of six ILs offers comparable results with the conventional electrolyte.
[M0196 p6] In the literature, with the optimized cell design parameters, there are some examples where the mixed electrolyte systems surpass the capacity of the organic electrolyte [48–52] and improve the safety conditions of Li-S batteries [53].
[M0197 p6] Since safety is also a major concern in batteries, the electrochemical and thermal stabilities of the ILs are also a good reason to replace conven­ tional liquid electrolytes.
[M0198 p6] Here, our main focus is determining the IL property and cycle life connection in Li-S batteries.
[M0199 p6] So, with the exper­ iments, we aim to define the ranges for high and low for the COSMO-RS calculated values.
[M0200 p6] We expect better results when experimenting with the promising ILs determined from the ARM analysis.
[M0201 p6] In addition, better performance can be obtained with different experimental design con­ ditions, optimizing the carbon properties, E/S ratio, S loading, and S wt. % in the cell.
[M0202 p6] This is the scope of our next study.
[M0203 p6] The experimental results show that it is possible to use the COSMO- RS predictions for assessing Li-S battery performance.
[M0204 p6] However, these results are only limited to six ILs, and further validation of COSMO-RS results is still needed.
[M0205 p6] Unfortunately, a sufficiently comprehensive experimental study to evaluate the predictions made by the COSMO-RS model is excessively difficult, necessitating a comparison with the available data in the literature.
[M0206 p6] The few studies regarding solubility had limited scope due to difficulties discussed previously.
[M0207 p6] Among them, Park et al. reported PS solubilities for various ILs.
[M0208 p6] The experimentally measured PS solubilities account for a mixture of PSs in the solution due to disproportionation reactions and the subsequent cascade of PS re­ actions.
[M0209 p6] This difference is partially responsible for the departure of predictions of Li2S8 solubility from experimental measurements of all PSs.
[M0210 p6] Indeed, the measured solubilities are the sum of solubilities of all PS species with various chain lengths and are provided in terms of total atomic sulfur concentration.
[M0211 p6] Despite this and other simplifying as­ sumptions, the COSMO-RS model results showed an excellent correla­ tion with the experimental measurements, as shown in Fig. 4.
[M0212 p6] Although there is a significant numerical difference between the COSMO-RS calculated absolute solubility values and experimental ones, the result­ ing linear correlation has an R2 score of 0.97, showing the success of our method.
[M0213 p6] Consequently, the COSMO-RS calculations can be used to assess ILs qualitatively in solubility calculations.
[M0214 p6] The previous studies on COSMO-RS calculations also pointed out that these calculations can be used qualitatively for an initial analysis that can predict the order of magnitudes of the properties [43,54–57].
[M0215 p6] Since the actual properties of ILs are not known, determining the limits for high performance should also be done according to the COSMO-RS calculated values.
[M0216 p6] Because of the aforementioned reasons, Park et al. performance data were also included in our analysis.
[M0217 p6] When the 50th cycle capacities of this experimental set are considered, a clear trend for high specific capacity can be determined, as shown in Fig. 5.
[M0218 p6] The PS solubility should neither be too low or too high due to the sluggish reaction kinetics and sulfur loss from the cathode in extremely low and high PS solubilities, respectively [58,59].
[M0219 p6] Therefore, deter­ mining the solubility limits is very critical to assess whether an IL will perform well or not as an electrolyte in Li-S batteries.
[M0220 p6] According to Fig. 5, the solubility should be between −0.7 to 0.1 mol/mol in log scale.
[M0221 p6] All these discussions show the importance of solubilities and prop­ erties in selecting suitable ILs for Li-S battery applications and the suc­ cess of the COSMO-RS calculations.
[M0222 p6] The COSMO-RS calculations make it possible to calculate the properties of thousands of ILs using “.cosmo” files using special packages like COSMOthermX.
[M0223 p6] On the other hand, once the dataset is constructed, it is valuable to have ML methods that can predict IL properties using the IL descriptors calculated from more conveniently found methods compared to generating “.cosmo” files and using special packages.
[M0224 p6] In addition, hidden relations between ILs and their properties can be identified.
[M0225 p6] In this respect, ML models for pre­ dicting PS solubility and the IL properties are developed with a dataset consisting of 36,260 ILs and 20 IL descriptors, computed from PM3 semiempirical calculations, in the search for promising ILs for Li-S battery electrolytes.
[M0226 p6] In addition, using the limit for solubility (−0.7 to 0.1 mol/ mol in log scale) for high performance obtained from experimental re­ sults, the factors leading to desired properties are obtained.

[M0233 p7] Solubility and property predictions The ML models are trained to further understand the trends and heuristics of the solubility correlation with the specified descriptors and to make predictions for new ILs without using the COSMO-RS software.
[M0234 p7] The training of the XGBoost model on randomly chosen combinations of anions and cations performs nearly perfectly with a 5-fold crossvalidation R-squared (R2) score of 0.99.
[M0235 p7] It is essential to note the excessive dependence of the predictions on the anion descriptors and the fact that randomly splitting the data generally means that the same anions are present in both training and validation sets.
[M0236 p7] To avoid such overlap, which may lead to the model “memorizing” the anions instead of learning descriptor correlations, the data was split such that randomly chosen anions from each anion group are only present in the validation set.
[M0237 p7] For example, there are four bis_imide anions including [TFSI]- in the dataset.
[M0238 p7] Knowing that anions are more dominant over solubility, once the solubility of [TFSI]- is seen by the model, it will automatically determine the solubility of IL containing [TFSI]- without paying atten­ tion to the cation types in the validation set.
[M0239 p7] However, restricting [TFSI]- to only train set while including the bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide ([FSI]-) in the validation set, makes the model more robust.
[M0240 p7] This way, the training model learns from similar but non-identical anions.
[M0241 p7] With this restriction, the XGBoost model performance R2 score dropped to 0.98 with a RMSE of 1.4, which still indicates an excellent performance on the available data and eliminates the risk of bias due to data overlap.
[M0242 p7] Meanwhile, the RMSE of the test set increases to 3.05, which shows that the model can capture the PS solubilities in an order of magnitude scale.
[M0243 p7] These results are somewhat similar to water solubility prediction results [31], in the sense that the anion properties are more dominant over the solubilities.
[M0244 p7] However, in this work, the effect of cation prop­ erties was also found to be important.
[M0245 p7] This inference is observable in Fig. 6, showing the descriptor importance obtained from the analysis of XGBoost results.
[M0246 p7] Some cation descriptors, such as the CPK area and dipole, have noticeable importance.
[M0247 p7] While the anions can still roughly estimate the solubilities, the cations refine these results.
[M0248 p7] The top three descriptors having the dominant effect on PS solubility were electronic HOMO-LUMO levels and dipoles of the anions.
[M0249 p7] The HOMO-LUMO levels were also found to have a significant contribution to ML models on the CO2 solubility [60].
[M0250 p7] Literature review indicates that ML techniques have been repeatedly used to predict different IL properties, including melting point, density, viscosity, ionic conductivity, and even surface tension [61,62].
[M0251 p7] Never­ theless, the presented property prediction models based on our de­ scriptors were also trained to a high degree of success.
[M0252 p7] Herein, the sampling in the training of these predictions was also anion-based rather than cation-based, as in the solubility classifier.
[M0253 p7] This is because the re­ sults vary significantly according to the anion groups in these pre­ dictions, and the cation group-based distributions are similar (Figure S3).
[M0254 p7] The anion-based sampling for melting point, viscosity, and conductivity performed well with RMSE score values of 19.4, 0.48, and 1.80, respectively.
[M0255 p7] The descriptor importance plots are given for each property prediction in SM, along with each model’s performance met­ rics.
[M0256 p7] In the prediction of IL properties of the interest, the cationic properties have found more significance in comparison to solubility predictions.
[M0257 p7] The most significant cation descriptors were found to be MW, dipole, and HOMO level.
[M0258 p7] In parallel with our findings, Zheng et al. also found that the alkyl chain lengths of ILs having different imidazo­ lium cations with [TFSI]- anion, therefore, MW and dipole, considerably affect the viscosities [63].
[M0259 p7] Another study found that the anion and cation properties both affect the final IL properties [64]. 3.4.
[M0260 p7] Identifying promising ILs for Li-S batteries using ARM Lastly, the descriptor-property and solubility correlations were analyzed using ARM.
[M0261 p7] The ARM method needs both categorical de­ scriptors and outcomes; hence, most descriptors were divided into ten intervals to see the characteristics of the desired ILs, while the HBA and HBD counts were defined as factors.
[M0262 p7] The limits for both solubility and viscosity values were set according to the experimental findings re­ ported in Section 3.2.
[M0263 p7] In this respect, the solubility was categorized into a binary class, and class A includes log(solubility) values between −0.7 and 0.1 mol/mol as determined from experimental results reported in Fig. 5.
[M0264 p7] On the other hand, viscosities lower than 100 mPa.s were decided to be class A.
[M0265 p7] Given the viscosity of the standard DOL:DME electrolyte is 1.6 mPa.s [19], 100 mPa.s is a relevant limit for IL viscosity, set to avoid severe Li+ ion resistance problems.
[M0266 p7] In addition, since the potential ap­ plications of Li-S batteries are prone to various climate conditions, the melting point of ILs should be low enough so that the cell remains functional in cold climates.
[M0267 p7] Towards that end, ILs with melting points below 0 ◦C are desired, and fortunately, 86.1 % of the dataset satisfies this condition.
[M0268 p7] Finally, the electronic conductivity limit is taken as 2 mS/ cm in the natural logarithm scale.
[M0269 p7] This rough estimation allows us to exclude ILs with considerable electronic conductivity.
[M0270 p7] With these four criteria in place, the number of potential IL candidates drops to only 650 from 36,260 data points.
[M0271 p7] The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 7.
[M0272 p7] First, the associations between the anion or cation groups or cati­ on–anion pairs and the desired properties were investigated.
[M0273 p7] After refining the generated rules using the support and confidence thresh­ olds, the lift value was used to extract rules with the highest correlation, and the results were sorted according to the lift values.
[M0274 p7] Although the definitions of support, confidence, and lift are already provided in the previous section, an example is provided here to understand the results better.
[M0275 p7] The dataset used in the ARM analysis contained 36,260 total data, with only 650 of them having class A solubility and property (viscosity, melting point, and conductivity).
[M0276 p7] In the dataset, there are 1480 ILs with the bis_imide anion group, and only 194 (count value in Table S4) of them are in class A for the four criteria.
[M0277 p7] Hence, the support, confidence, and lift values are calculated as 194/36260 = 0.005, 194/ 650 = 0.298, and (194/650)/(1480/36260) = 7.31, respectively.
[M0278 p7] As seen in Table S4, rules satisfying the confidence and support thresholds with the highest three lift values correlate with the anion group, indi­ cating the trends in anion descriptors are more reliable and determi­ native than the ones in cation descriptors.
[M0279 p7] These trends can be seen in Fig. 8a for cation and anion groups and Fig. 8b for cation–anion pairs.

[M0288 p9] Specifically, imidazolium_borate and imidazolium_bis_imide ILs are around ten- and five-times more favorable than other ILs for Li-S bat­ teries, respectively.
[M0289 p9] Imidazolium and [TFSI]- are the most common cation and anion in the Li-S literature, respectively [9].
[M0290 p9] In addition, it has been reported that combining them improves the Li metal ion morphology and, therefore, increases the cycle life [65].
[M0291 p9] Given that pyridinium and imidazolium are similar in the completely delocalized aromatic rings [65], pyridinium is the second promising cation group paired with borates.
[M0292 p9] On the other hand, the borate anion group has 12 anions in total, including [BF4]-, which is reported to be reactive to­ wards polysulfides [18].
[M0293 p9] Fortunately, this anion is not present in the promising IL list.
[M0294 p9] Although no additional articles use ILs with borate anions, in some studies, borate anions, specifically bisoxalatoborate [66–68], are used in Li salts, which positively affect the capacities.
[M0295 p9] Now that the promising groups are identified, we discuss the ARM results on a descriptor basis to identify the rules ending up with favor­ able ILs.
[M0296 p9] To better extract the trends, Fig. 9 summarizes the ARM results for the anions more concisely.
[M0297 p9] Upon examining the results, the most pronounced rule concerns the anion HOMO energy value.
[M0298 p9] In the data­ base, low-lying anion HOMO energies in the range of (−7.5):(−6.7) eV result in a nearly five-times more chance of having low PS solubility and viscosity values.
[M0299 p9] This drops significantly to slightly over 2.5-times if the HOMO value dips lower than −7.5 going to −9.9 eV.
[M0300 p9] However, the correlation stands that low HOMO values correlate strongly with low solubility.
[M0301 p9] A similar yet less strong correlation can be seen for the other properties.
[M0302 p9] The other rules that lead to lift values higher than one indicate that desirable anions for low PS solubility and viscosity are more likely to have moderately low LUMO energy ((1.3–4.7] eV), no HBD sites (Count = 0), relatively high MW ((184–299] g/mol) but moderate CPK area ((98.8–145] Å2).
[M0303 p9] Fig. 10 shows that cation de­ scriptors have clear trends for low solubility and viscosity; lift values above one are obtained for low values of each descriptor, except HBA (Counts 1 and 2), HBD (Count = 0), and LUMO (no clear trend).
[M0304 p9] It is important to emphasize that these trends imply an increased likelihood rather than a confident prediction.
[M0305 p9] [31] reported similar results about the dominance of anion descriptors in the ARM analysis when examining the association be­ tween water solubility in ILs and molecular descriptors.
[M0306 p9] However, the importance of each descriptor differs significantly from the results reported here.
[M0307 p9] That is to be expected because water and Li2S8 have different solvation processes.
[M0308 p9] On the contrary, the solubility of hydro­ carbons in imidazolium-based ILs showed a strong correlation with both anion and cation descriptors [32]. 4.
[M0309 p9] Conclusions In this study, a large-scale screening of ILs was conducted in search of potential Li-S battery electrolytes not only with low PS solubility to limit the shuttle effect but also with low viscosity, electronic conductivity, and melting point.
[M0310 p9] The screening was performed using the COSMO-RS model, and association rule mining and XGBoost algorithms were used as machine learning models.
[M0311 p9] The predictive model developed by the XGBoost algorithm was quite successful, indicating that the solubility can be easily predicted for new ILs, satisfying the main objective of our work.
[M0312 p9] Ten descriptors were analyzed for each anion and cation along with the solubility estimations using association rule mining to find valuable correlations between solubility, property, and IL structural descriptors; solubility limits, which were used as the selection criterion for high performance, were determined experimentally.
[M0313 p9] The feature importance analysis also showed that anion descriptors are leading in solubility prediction, with anion LUMO and HOMO energies, dipole, and CPK area being the most important.
[M0314 p9] Our model verified the generally known fact that imidazolium and pyridinium were the most suitable cations, whereas borates and bis_imides were determined to be the anion choices for high-performing Li-S batteries.
[M0315 p9] To conclude, this study not only offers machine learning models that can be easily utilized to identify promising IL electrolytes for Li-S batteries by an original inte­ grated methodology coupling high-throughput COSMO-RS and DFT calculations and experimental characterization but also leads to a better understanding of the structure-solubility-performance relation for IL electrolytes.
