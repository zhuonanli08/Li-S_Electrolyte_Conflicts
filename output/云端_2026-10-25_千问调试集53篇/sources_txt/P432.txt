[M0004 p1] Tri-sulfur radical trapping in lithium–sulfur batteries Roza Bouchal a,*, Cl ́ement Pechberty a, Athmane Boulaoued a, Niklas Lindahl a,b, Patrik Johansson a,c a Department of Physics, Chalmers University of Technology, 412 96, Gothenburg, Sweden b Department of Physics, Gothenburg University, 412 96, Gothenburg, Sweden c ALISTORE-ERI, CNRS, FR 310, Hub de l’Energie, 80039, Amiens, France A R T I C L E I N F O Keywords: Li-S batteries Tri-sulfur radical Radical trap Cycling stability A B S T R A C T Lithium-sulfur (Li–S) batteries have emerged as a next-generation battery technology owing to their prospects of high capacity and energy density.
[M0005 p1] They, however, suffer from rapid capacity decay due to the shuttling of re­ action intermediate species: Li polysulfides (LiPSs).
[M0006 p1] One of the more important and intriguing PSs is the tri-sulfur radical (S•− 3 ), observed mainly in high-donor number (DN) solvent-based electrolytes.
[M0007 p1] Although this radical has been proposed to be crucial to full active material (AM) utilization, there is currently no direct evidence of the impact of S•− 3 on cycling stability.
[M0008 p1] To gain more insight into the role of the S•− 3 , we studied the use of radical traps in low and high DN solvent-based electrolytes by operando Raman spectroscopy.
[M0009 p1] The traps were based on nitrone and iminium cation, and S•− 3 was indeed successfully trapped in ex situ analysis.
[M0010 p1] However, it was the ionic liquidbased trap, specifically pyridinium, that effectively suppressed S•− 3 during battery operation.
[M0011 p1] Overall, the PS formation was altered in the presence of the traps and we confirmed the impact of S•− 3 formation on the Li–S battery redox reactions and show how the trapping correlates with Li–S battery performance.
[M0012 p1] Therefore, stabi­ lization of the S•− 3 might be a path to improved Li–S batteries. 1.
[M0013 p1] Introduction Li–S batteries are considered a promising alternative/complement to the current dominant lithium-ion battery (LIB) technology, and if suc­ cessful it will support the fast-expanding energy storage demand [1,2].
[M0014 p1] Sulfur is a very cheap, non-toxic and naturally abundant element.
[M0015 p1] It is used as the cathode active material (AM) in a mixture with carbon, in order for the electrode to be electrically conductive.
[M0016 p1] Combining this cathode with a high-capacity Li metal anode, an Li–S battery that de­ livers up to (or possibly even more than) 400 W h kg−1 can be created.
[M0017 p1] Li–S battery surpasses commercialized LIBs energy, but does not offer the cycling stability needed; <500 vs. >3000 cycles for LIBs [3–5].
[M0018 p1] The latter is mainly due to dissolution of reaction intermediates from the C/S electrode into the electrolyte.
[M0019 p1] These are species with the general formula (Li2)Sn (n = 3–8), e.g.
[M0020 p1] (lithium) polysulfides ((Li)PSs) that are formed during charge and discharge [6].
[M0021 p1] Their dissolution leads to loss of AM and hence both rapid capacity decay and low Coulombic efficiency [7, 8].
[M0022 p1] Numerous studies have reported on many different strategies and new materials to improve both the (practical) energy density and the cycling stability [9–14] but relatively few have focused on under­ standing the very mechanisms underlying these phenomena – which can be complicated to assess.
[M0023 p1] They do include, but are not limited to, simultaneous electrochemical and disproportionation reactions [15–17] which furthermore are still not completely identified and understood, and have also been demonstrated to be different dependent on the na­ ture of the electrolyte.
[M0024 p1] For example, electrolytes based on solvents with high donor numbers (DNs) promote the redox reactions of various spe­ cies by stabilizing multiple PSs, most notably the S•− 3 radical, which is then the most stable and dominant intermediate [18–23].
[M0025 p1] In contrast, in low DN solvents, such as di-methyl ether (DME), S2− 4 is predominant, with S•− 3 being more or less completely absent [24].
[M0026 p1] The stabilization of S•− 3 in tetrahydrofuran (THF), di-methyl acetamide (DMA) and di-methyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is well-documented [25,26].
[M0027 p1] The presence of S•− 3 produces highly coloured blue solutions, as seen e.g. in ultrama­ rine pigments, while PSs such as S2− 4 and S2− 6 result in yellow hue so­ lutions [20,25].
[M0028 p1] The relative concentrations of S•− 3 is also dependent on the concentration of sulfur in the solution, the temperature, and the elemental sulfur to alkali metal ratio.

[M0033 p2] The latter is affected as the ability of the electrolyte to stabilize the radical induces homogenous precipitation of Li2S.
[M0034 p2] Along these lines, Zhang et al.
[M0035 p2] [22] reported that the high DN solvent tetra-methylurea (TMU) exhibits favourable Li2S deposition ki­ netics and better compatibility towards lithium metal anodes than other high DN solvents [27].
[M0036 p2] Similar results were obtained using high DN anions, that preferentially induce 3D particle-like growth of Li2S, while low DN anions result in a film-like morphology.
[M0037 p2] The former effectively delayed electrode passivation and consequently led to high sulfur uti­ lization [23].
[M0038 p2] In some contrast, Cuisinier et al., showed by using NMR spectroscopy that S•− 3 is reactive at elevated temperatures towards DOL, which is a common solvent in Li–S battery electrolytes, and this can possibly be held responsible for the capacity fading observed for extended Li–S battery cycling [20].
[M0039 p2] Another issue is that most high DN solvents, such as DMA, THF and DMSO, are corrosive towards lithium.
[M0040 p2] Taken all together, it is not clear if S•− 3 is truly beneficial to Li–S battery cycling, nor whether or not it is promoting the full utilization of the AM.
[M0041 p2] To elucidate further on this, we here employ radical traps and pri­ marily operando Raman spectroscopy to directly monitor the effect of S•− 3 on the Li–S battery capacity and cycling stability, using two different electrolytes: 1 M LiTFSI in either DME:DOL or TMU:DOL.
[M0042 p2] The approach of radical or spin trapping is widely used in the fields of medical and organic chemistry [28,29].
[M0043 p2] Spin trapping is based on the acceptance of short-lived radicals by certain compounds (spin traps) with the forma­ tion of stable radicals (radical adducts) [30].
[M0044 p2] In this technique diamagnetic compounds called spin trapping agents, such as nitrone or nitroso, react with a free radicals to obtain a stable paramagnetic spin adduct.
[M0045 p2] The nitrone (R–CH––NO-R’) group is an important spin trap category where the radical is added to the adjacent carbon atom to the nitrone [31].
[M0046 p2] In contrast, the iminium cation was recently demonstrated to trap nucleophilic radicals.
[M0047 p2] Melchiorre et al. reported on the enantio­ selective trapping of photochemically generated carbon-centered radi­ cals [32,33].
[M0048 p2] The strong nucleophile character of S•− 3 [25] might thus render it effective with iminium cations.
[M0049 p2] Inspired by this, we selected four molecules; two based on nitrone and two with iminium cations as our prospective S•− 3 traps.
[M0050 p2] Two, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) and alpha-(4-Pyridyl N-oxide)-N-tert-butylnitrone (PotBN), are widely used as nitrone radical traps, while the other two are ionic liquids (ILs) 1-ethyl-3-methyl-imidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide and 1-butyl-3-methyl-pyridinium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, which, for simplicity, henceforth will be referred to as Emim and BmPyr, respec­ tively (Fig.
[M0051 p2] Note, ionic liquids have never previously been used as radical traps and were selected here due to their chemical, thermal and electrochemical stabilities.
[M0052 p2] Using an S•− 3 radical trap in an Li–S battery while cycling is by no means trivial; the trap must be electrochemically and chemically stable toward all other battery components, selectively react solely with S•− 3 , which is extra difficult as the electrolyte compo­ sition changes as (Li)PSs are formed, i.e. also as a function of the Li–S battery state-of-charge. 2.

[M0087 p3] Solvent effects on the S•− 3 formation Starting with proper S•− 3 radical formation identification, we apply open-circuit voltage (OCV) conditions and Raman spectroscopy using a home-made Raman cell presented in Fig.
[M0088 p3] By focusing on the wellknown S•− 3 peak at ca. 532 cm−1 [34], we find S•− 3 in all electrolytes, excepted the TFEE based, as previously reported (Fig. 1a (left) and Fig.
[M0089 p3] The peak intensity and thus the concentration of S•− 3 follows TMU > TEGDME > DME (≫TFEE).
[M0090 p3] These observations can be explained by S•− 3 being formed through disproportionation of the S2− 6 anion, why the solubility of Li2S6 in the electrolyte is an important property [27,38–40].
[M0091 p3] Indeed, the solubility of any Li-salt is usually lower in ether based electrolytes due to their low permittivity/dielectric con­ stants (ε) [35–37,41].
[M0092 p3] TEGDME has a relative advantage vs.
[M0093 p3] DME by its possibility of multidentate coordination by five (two) oxygen atoms and can thereby wrap around the Li+ cation (Fig. 1b) [42,43].
[M0094 p3] In contrast, the fluorinated ether TFEE has fluorine atoms shielding the oxygen atoms, hindering effective coordination.
[M0095 p3] The 74X cm−1 band of TFSI [44], often used to monitor cation-anion contact ion-pair formation between Li+ and TFSI and thus clearly also being a function of the cation-solvent interaction [43], shifts towards higher frequencies as: TFEE > DME > TEGDME ≈TMU (Fig. 1a (right)).
[M0096 p3] A note in passing is that the latter spectrum shows two bands in this region, which may point to a different mode of interaction. 3.2.
[M0097 p3] E/S ratio effects on the S•− 3 concentration The S•− 3 radical concentration has been shown to increase as an in­ verse function of both electrolyte salt concentration and, what we focus on here, the elemental sulfur to lithium ratio [25,39,45].
[M0098 p3] First, we demonstrate that S•− 3 indeed can be monitored as a function of potential for a 1 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL electrolyte at an 18 E/S ratio (Fig. 2a).
[M0099 p3] Please first note that, as opposed to what usually is the case, no LiNO3 was added to the electrolyte, in order to avoid any possible interaction with S•− 3 , and this explains the incomplete charge potential (due to PSs shuttling).
[M0100 p3] Second, the intensity of S•− 3 in the spectra does (surprisingly) not change considerably for the different E/S ratios (Fig. 2b).
[M0101 p3] The con­ centration of S•− 3 is, however, still somewhat lower in the electrolytes with the lower E/S ratios, i.e. 11.6 and 18, which likely is due to these cells’ smaller electrolyte volumes, which result in higher PS concentrations. 3.3.

[M0174 p6] The plausible role of S•− 3 : a literature study Previously, it was shown that S•− 3 can assist long PS species to reduce to shorter PSs.
[M0175 p6] [47], showed that the S•− 3 radical plays a key intermediate role in a set of reactions (Equations (1)–(3) below) before ultimately being reduced to solid-state Li2S.
[M0176 p6] Similarly, Gupta et al.
[M0177 p6] [21], proposed that the presence of S•− 3 avoids the early precipi­ tation of Li2S and allows the full utilization of S8.
[M0178 p6] S•‒ 3 + e‒ →S2‒ 3 (1) 2S•‒ 3 + 2S2‒ 3 →2S2‒ 4 (2) S2‒ 8 + 2e‒ →2S2‒ 4 (3) Cuisinier et al.
[M0179 p6] [47], proposed that in high DN solvents, any sulfur species formed via electrochemical reduction (such as S2− 2 or S2− 1 ) must equilibrate with leftover oxidized species (S8 or S2− 8 ) to form more stable intermediates (S•− 3 or S2− 3−4; Equation (4)).
[M0180 p6] The latter drives the full consumption of S8, and prevents the early precipitation of Li2S in the presence of long-chain PSs.
[M0181 p6] S2‒ 1 + 1/4S8 →S2‒ 3 (4) According to a recent study by He et al.
[M0182 p6] [67], using a DME based electrolyte, the meta-stable PS S2− 3 is also formed during discharge and quickly disproportionate to S2− 4 and Li2S2/Li2S.
[M0183 p6] This explains why S2− 3 is not observed by ex situ characterization.
[M0184 p6] The intermediate S2− 3 enhances the capacity and lengthen the second discharge plateau as the reaction requires 5 e– to form the Li2S2/Li2S precipitates.
[M0185 p6] According to equations (5)–(9), they suggested that the conversion of S8 to S2− 4 and then to S2− 3 are fast processes, while the capacity extraction from S2− 3 to form Li2S is limiting the discharge rate in DOL:DME-based Li–S batteries.
[M0186 p6] S2‒ 4 + 2/3e‒ →4/3S2‒ 3 (5) S2‒ 3 + 4e‒→3S2‒ 1 (6) S2‒ 3 + 1e‒→3/2S2‒ 2 (7) 2S2‒ 3 ↔S2‒ 4 + S2‒ 2 (8) 3S2‒ 3 ↔2S2‒ 4 + S2‒ 1 (9) Similar to He et al.’s research, our in situ Raman spectroscopy data do not reveal any presence of S2− 3 in the DME based electrolyte.
[M0187 p6] It is, however, observed at 325 cm−1 in the TMU based electrolyte during the charging step.
[M0188 p6] This could be explained by the possibility of high donor number solvents stabilizing S2− 3 and/or to the large amount of S•− 3 radical formed during charging process.
[M0189 p6] However, this peak vanishes when BmPyr is added.
[M0190 p6] Furthermore, the second discharge plateau was shortened in the presence of BmPyr trap in both DME and TMU based electrolytes.
[M0191 p6] Although S2− 3 is observed during charge in TMU based electrolyte, the decrease in the discharge capacity might be explained by the suppression of Equation (1), which hinders the formation of S2− 3 .
[M0192 p6] However, the mechanism in the DME based electrolyte is less clear, as the formation of S2− 3 arises from the reduction of S2− 4 , as indicated by Equation (6).
[M0193 p6] We hold the view that the possibility of a minor presence of S•− 3 in the DME based electrolyte could plausibly stimulate the for­ mation of S2− 3 .
[M0194 p6] Notably, the investigation by He et al. categorically ex­ cludes any existence of S•− 3 .
[M0195 p6] Despite the complexity of the system, our findings are in line with the literature.
[M0196 p6] We showed in direct way that S•− 3 trapping alters significantly the PSs formation.
[M0197 p6] The meta-stable PS S2− 3 , dependent on S•− 3 formation, might be the important intermediate for the full utilization of sulfur. 3.5.

[M0266 p8] Concluding remarks By employing radical traps and operando Raman spectroscopy, we have monitored the effect of the S•− 3 radical on the PS formation, battery capacity and stability.
[M0267 p8] First, we confirmed the S•− 3 concentration dependence on type of solvent and E/S ratio.
[M0268 p8] Second, different traps based on nitrone and iminium cation were investigated with respect to their ability to trap S•− 3 as well as the formation of S•− 3 in low and high DN solvent based electrolytes.
[M0269 p8] By this, we managed to successfully trap S•− 3 using BmPyr, which results in decreased battery capacity and cell failure after only a few cycles.
[M0270 p8] Moreover, a different PS formation was observed during S•− 3 trapping in both DME and TMU based electrolytes.
[M0271 p8] Our results confirm, in agreement with the literature, the impact of S•− 3 in and on different Li–S electrolytes, which is important information for future Li–S battery (electrolyte) development.
[M0272 p8] However, further work is still needed to elucidate more exactly how the S•− 3 trapping changes the PS formation pathway and impacts Li–S battery performance.
