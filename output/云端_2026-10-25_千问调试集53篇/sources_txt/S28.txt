[M0001 p1] Rational Electrolyte Solvent Screening for Practical Lithium−Sulfur Batteries Lin Peng,# Lishan Lin,# Zitao Feng, Xiaoxian Guan, Jingwen Lai, Yang Xiao, Luyi Chen,* Yuepeng Cai,* and Qifeng Zheng* Cite This: ACS Energy Lett. 2026, 11, 4125−4135 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Practical lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries demand rapid sulfur redox kinetics and high Li-metal compatibility simultaneously, which cannot achieve this objective using current electrolyte based on 1,2-dimethoxyethane and 1,3-dioxolane.
[M0002 p1] Herein, we propose a solvent screening criterion using the dielectric constant (εr) and donor number (DN) as a synergistic descriptor.
[M0003 p1] Guided by this εr−DN screening criterion, 75 solvents were classified as strong, midstrong, middle, weak, and nonsolvation solvents, respectively.
[M0004 p1] The combination of midstrong/ weak solvation solvents endows a distinctly gradient lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) solvation structure, suppressing their shuttle effect and enhancing Li-metal compatibility.
[M0005 p1] This bestows the Li−S coin cell with an extremely high areal capacity of 9.4 mAh cm−2 under a high S mass loading (8.6 mg cm−2) and a low E/S ratio (3.6 μL mg−1).
[M0006 p1] As a proof-of-concept, a 3 Ah-level pouch cell delivers a high energy density of 369 Wh kg−1.
[M0007 p1] L ithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries, featuring an ultrahigh theoretical energy density (up to 2600 Wh kg−1) and low cost, abundant S as the cathode material, are considered as the leading candidates for next-generation energy storage systems.1,2 However, their industrialization has been persistently hindered by critical challenges, among which electrolyte design and optimization play a pivotal role.3 The electrolyte serves as not only an ion-transport medium but also a core component, which profoundly governs the dissolution, diffusion, and conversion of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) and critically impacts the stability of electrode−electrolyte interfaces.4 Benefiting from the prior lithium-ion battery technology, early research predominantly employed ether-based solvents, such as 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) as the baseline electrolytes.5,6 Although ether solvents exhibit high solubility for LiPSs, which ensures favorable sulfur conversion kinetics, this property also causes the shuttle effect of LiPSs.7,8 Dissolved LiPSs species migrate between the cathode and anode, causing irreversible active material rapid loss and lithium metal anode (LMA) corrosion.9,10 Recently, various electrolyte strategies have been explored to suppress the shuttle effect of LiPSs.11,12 For instance, employing weak-solvating-power solvents, e.g., carbonates,13 single-oxygen ether,14 and hydrofluorinated ether,15 results in incomplete sulfur redox reactions and easy formation of passivation layer on the cathode surface.
[M0008 p1] Introducing ionic liquids or highly concentrated electrolytes effectively restricted LiPSs dissolution, however also leads to high viscosity, low ionic conductivity, and high costs.16,17 Although these approaches have achieved incremental progress, they often inevitably face a dilemma of trade-offs: suppressing shuttle effect of LiPSs comes at the expense of compromised conversion kinetics or interfacial stability.18 Then, to achieve a high S utilization under lean electrolyte condition, a series of Gutmann’s donor number (DN) solvents with strong-solvating-power are proposed to boost the solubility of LiPSs leading to a rapid conversion kinetics based on a solid−liquid−solid S redox process.19,20 From the perspective of electrolyte engineering, a fundamental understanding of the LiPSs’ solvation is essential for the industrialization of Li−S batteries.
[M0009 p1] Further, in designing new electrolyte systems, the reliance on the empirical trial-and-error approaches and the lack of comprehensive theoretical guidance are the primarily reasons of the puzzling relationship between screening solvents and the solvation of LiPSs.21−23 Recently, several key parameters with great potential have recently been proposed to understand this relationship, as following: (i) The Gutmann’s DN of a solvent dictates Li+ coordination strength: high DN solvents dissolve Received: March 16, 2026 Revised: April 14, 2026 Accepted: April 17, 2026 Published: April 24, 2026 Letter http://pubs.acs.org/journal/aelccp © 2026 American Chemical Society 4125 https://doi.org/10.1021/acsenergylett.6c00790 ACS Energy Lett. 2026, 11, 4125−4135 Downloaded from pubs.​acs.​org/​aelccp/​article-pdf/​11/​5/​4125/​64809067/​nz6c00790.​pdf by TSINGHUA UNIV user on 24 September 2026
[M0010 p2] LiPSs but exacerbate their shuttle effect, while low DN solvents suppress dissolution at the expense of S conversion kinetics.24 (ii) The Gutmann’s acceptor number (AN) indicates Lewis acidity of a solvent.
[M0011 p2] High AN solvents, due to their stronger electrophilic properties, interact strongly with polysulfide anions (high solvation energy), which risk parasitic reactions.
[M0012 p2] Whereas low AN solvents facilitate robust solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation on the anode.25,26 (iii) Relative permittivity (εr), also known as the dielectric constant, indicates how readily a solvent molecule can be aligned by an electric field.
[M0013 p2] High εr solvents can modulate the solvation of LiPSs, dissociating them into free ions but accelerating diffusion.27,28 In contrast, low εr solvents restrict LiPSs diffusion by forming contact ion pairs or aggregates, yet impairing redox activity.28 However, aside from the ion−solvent interactions, quantitative models for solvent− solvent and LiPSs−solvent interactions are poorly understood, particularly in binary solvent electrolyte systems.
[M0014 p2] Therefore, it is imperative to bridge the fundamental gap between the nanoscopic screening criteria and macroscopic electrochemical performance.
[M0015 p2] Herein, we propose an electrolyte solvent screening criterion based on εr and DN, where 75 electrolyte solvents were classified and evaluated by using seven critical electrochemical performance parameters.
[M0016 p2] These seven parameters are polarization voltage, cycle life of Li||Li symmetric cells, LiPSs shuttle current, Li2S nucleation overpotential and deposition capacity, and average capacity and average CE of Li−S coin cells.
[M0017 p2] The electrolyte formula combining midstrong solvation (2,5- dimethyl-1,2,5-thiadiazolidin 1-oxide, CSA) and weak solvation (2-methyltetrahydrofuran, MeTHF) solvents outperforms all other electrolyte formulations.

[M0031 p3] According to the Born−Haber cycle (Figure 1a), the dissolution of a Li salt in a solvent requires a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG = ΔH −TΔS < 0).
[M0032 p3] This is a thermodynamic prerequisite for forming a homogeneous and stable electrolyte system.
[M0033 p3] The dissociation of a crystalline Li salt first involves its endothermic ionization into anions and cations, which is a process primarily governed by the lattice energy (ΔUlat).
[M0034 p3] Then, the dissociated ions are solvated by solvent molecules, forming a coordination structure in an exothermic process dominated by the solvation enthalpy (ΔHsol).
[M0035 p3] Electrolytes in Li−S batteries, which typically operate via a solid−liquid−solid conversion mechanism, usually contain three main Li salts: lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), LiNO3, and LiPSs (Figure 1b).
[M0036 p3] Using Li2S6 as a representative LiPSs, its dissolution and precipitation are correlated with the solvation structure and the conversion equilibrium.
[M0037 p3] Specifically, the solvation structure of LiPSs can be modulated by high εr solvent with enhancement of a strong electrostatic shielding effect for ions.
[M0038 p3] This leads to more free ions with increased entropy (Figure 1c) and favors the dissociation of LiPSs.
[M0039 p3] Moreover, based on the theory of Lewis acids and bases, the coordination environment of Li+ can be regulated with high DN solvent, resulting in lower solvation enthalpy change (ΔHsol; Figure 1d; further discussion is provided in Supplementary Note 2.1).29 Further, the dual-descriptor, namely, εr and DN, was proposed to classify the 75 organic solvents in Figures 1e and S1, and Tables S1−8.
[M0040 p3] Region I (0 ≤DN < 19 kcal mol−1 and 20 ≤εr < 100), including most carbonate solvents, shows ultralow solubility for LiNO3.26,28 Due to the high DN value of NO3 − (22.2 kcal mol−1), most carbonate solvents provide an insufficient solvation enthalpy (ΔHsol) to overcome the lattice energy (ΔUlat) of LiNO3.
[M0041 p3] Unfortunately, these solvents exhibit high reactivity toward LiPSs via nucleophilic attack, resulting in rapid active material loss and severe side reactions at the cathode (Figures S2−S4 and Supplementary Note S2.2).30 Region II (25 ≤DN < 40 kcal mol−1 and 1 ≤εr < 20) contains polar protic nitrogenous solvents, which may undergo deprotonation Figure 2.

[M0048 p4] Thus, the screening criteria based on DN and εr, coupled with thermodynamic analysis of solute dissolution processes, provide a basic framework for designing rational electrolytes in Li−S batteries.
[M0049 p4] In this work, five representative solvents were selected to design binary solvent electrolyte formulations (Figure 2a and Table S9): dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from Region S, 2,5- dimethyl-1,2,5-thiadiazolidin 1-oxide (CSA) from Region M-S, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) from Region M, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran (MeTHF) from Region W, and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) from Region N.
[M0050 p4] CSA was synthesized via a nucleophilic substitution reaction (Figure S6) and confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Figures S7 and S8).
[M0051 p4] Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that calculated the lowest negative electrostatic potential (i.e., ESPmin) of the five solvents follows the strength trend: DMSO > CSA > DME > MeTHF > TTE (Figure S9), indicating a reduced trend in solvation capability.
[M0052 p4] Further, the electrolyte was prepared using an optimal molar ratio of LiTFSI:CSA:MeTHF = 1:4:6, with the detailed optimization shown in Figures S10 and S11.
[M0053 p4] Using this formulation, Li||Li2S6 cell exhibits a capacity retention of 93% after 60 cycles with high average CE of 99.4% (Figure S12).
[M0054 p4] All other formulas were prepared with an optimal molar ratio of LiTFSI: solvent 1: solvent 2: LiNO3 = 1:4:6:0.2, as detailed in Table S10.
[M0055 p4] Due to the insolubility of LiNO3 in hydrofluoroether solvents, LiNO3 was omitted from the TTE-containing electrolyte formulations.
[M0056 p4] Additionally, a conventional electrolyte of 1 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiNO3 in DME:DOL (1:1 by volume; denoted as DD) was employed as the reference electrolyte.
[M0057 p4] The density, viscosity, and ionic conductivity of these electrolytes were measured and are summarized in Table S11.
[M0058 p4] Li+ solvation structures in all electrolyte systems were investigated by using Raman spectroscopy (Figure S13).
[M0059 p4] Characteristic peak shift in the binary solvent electrolyte systems reveal a consistent solvation strength trend (DMSO > CSA > DME > MeTHF > TTE), confirming the reliability of solvent selection criteria.
[M0060 p4] In the DMSO/CSA (S/M-S) electrolyte, the C−S−C stretching vibration of DMSO exhibits a significant blue shift, indicating strong coordination between DMSO and Li+ (Figure S13a).
[M0061 p4] Meanwhile, the N−S−N stretching vibration of CSA shows a slight blue shift, indicating partial coordination between CSA and Li+.
[M0062 p4] This suggests a competitively coordinated Li+ solvation structure in DMSO/ CSA.
[M0063 p4] Similar competitive coordination was observed in CSA/ DME (M-S/M), DME/MeTHF (M/W), and DME/DOL (DD) (Figure S13b−d).
[M0064 p4] In contrast, DMSO/DME (S/M), DMSO/MeTHF (S/W), CSA/MeTHF (M-S/W), DMSO/ TTE (S/N), CSA/TTE (M-S/N), DME/TTE (M/N), and MeTHF/TTE (W/N) exhibit a different Li+ solvation structure (Figures S14 and S15), which features only one coordinated solvent and another free solvent (noncoordinated).
[M0065 p4] This confirms that, besides coordination capability of a single solvent, the combination of two solvents is another key factor for binary solvent electrolytes to regulate the Li+ solvation structure.
[M0066 p4] First, the LMA compatibility with each electrolyte formula was evaluated by Li||Li symmetric cells, whose polarization voltage could reflect the kinetics during the Li deposition and stripping process.
[M0067 p4] Both polarization voltage and cycle life of Li|| Li symmetric cells using various electrolytes were selected as the primary two screening parameters for the binary solvent electrolytes, which was evaluated using Li||Li symmetric cells cycled at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2 (Figures 2b, S16, and S17).
[M0068 p4] Only the M-S/W, M/W, M/N, W/N, and DD electrolytes simultaneously met the demands of polarization voltage below 50 mV and cycle life exceeding 800 h, demonstrating the excellent compatibility with LMA.
[M0069 p4] Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of cycled LMAs, using M-S/W, M/W, M/ N, W/N, and DD electrolytes, revealed a clear morphologyperformance correlation of small polarization voltages, long cycle life, and smooth morphologies.
[M0070 p4] In contrast, those that induced large polarization voltages and short cycle life, using S/ M-S, S/W, S/M, S/N, M-S/M, and M-S/N electrolytes, resulted in cracked, porous, mossy deposited morphologies.
[M0071 p4] These results can be attributed to the distinct solvent properties: (1) DMSO with strong solvation ability accompanies intrinsic corrosion, leading to short cycle life.
[M0072 p4] (2) CSA, with midstrong solvation ability, offers a balance between strong donor ability and compatibility toward Li metal.
[M0073 p4] (3) TTE, as a nonsolvation solvent, contributes to low ionic conductivity and high viscosity, which could aggravate Li dendritic growth.
[M0074 p4] Given that the multispecies and multiphase of LiPSs in Li−S batteries, their shuttle current (SC) is adopted as the third key screening parameter, which directly reflects the loss of active materials induced by the corrosion reaction between LiPSs and LMA.11 The SC of LiPSs was measured via potentiostatic tests between 2.2 and 2.9 V (Figure S18).
[M0075 p4] Previously reported work has demonstrated that an antisolvent-derived encapsulation solvation structure can effectively suppress the LiPS shuttle.32,33 In this work, only S/N, M-S/W, M-S/N, M/N, and W/N electrolytes show an average SC below 10 μA cm−2 based on Li|| Li2S6 coin cells (Figure 2c).
[M0076 p4] Thanks to the solid−liquid−solid conversion mechanism in the S cathode, both nucleation overpotential (ΔVnuc) and deposition capacity of lithium sulfide (Li2S) are critical ratedetermining steps,34 representing the energy barriers for Li2S nucleation and growth.
[M0077 p4] Consequently, both ΔVnuc and Li2S deposition capacities from Li−S coin cells were used as the following two key screening parameters for these electrolytes.
[M0078 p4] As shown in Figures 2d, S19, and S20, only S/M, M-S/M, and M-S/ W simultaneously met the demand of ΔVnuc below 30 mV and a Li2S deposition capacity exceeding 600 mAh g−1.
[M0079 p4] This implies that enhanced solvation capability effectively accelerates the conversion kinetics of sulfur species.
[M0080 p4] Moreover, average discharge capacity and average CE over 100 cycles were proposed as the last two key parameters for assessing practicality.
[M0081 p4] As shown in Figures 2e, S19, and S20, only the M-S/W electrolyte achieved an average discharge capacity exceeding 900 mAh g−1 and an average CE of 99.9% over 100 cycles at 0.2 C, indicating high S utilization and excellent cycling stability.

[M0084 p5] The conventional DD electrolyte, with moderate LiPSs solubility, exhibits a typical solid−liquid−solid conversion process (Figure S21).
[M0085 p5] This process prior forms a twodimensional (2D) Li2S deposition morphology, leading to low S utilization and significant corrosion with LMA.35 Coupling a strong solvating solvent with LiPSs could regulate the LiPSs solvation structure, promote a three-dimensional (3D) Li2S deposition morphology, and provide an additional conversion pathway.
[M0086 p5] However, this approach often exacerbates LiPSs corrosion with LMA.36 Conversely, employing a solvent with a low LiPSs solubility can create a sparingly solvated structure that mitigates LMA corrosion.
[M0087 p5] Yet, this strategy also results in 2D Li2S deposition and sluggish conversion kinetics.37 Given the critical role of the LiPSs solvation structure, we further explored the solvent−solvent interactions in the M-S/W electrolyte to elucidate the underlying structure−property relationships in this binary solvent electrolyte.
[M0088 p5] The solubility of LiPSs was investigated in Figure S22.
[M0089 p5] Ten mM Li2Sx (x = 2, 4, 6, 8) in CSA:MeTHF (4:6 by molar) exhibits uniform a darkbrown color, indicating high LiPSs dissolubility.
[M0090 p5] In contrast, 10 mM Li2Sx in DME:DOL (1:1 by vol.) shows visible precipitate (the dotted box), indicating poor dissociation and low dissolubility of polysulfides (Sx 2−) species.
[M0091 p5] The ionic conductivity of 0.1 M Li2Sx in CSA:MeTHF is higher than that in DME:DOL (Figure S23).
[M0092 p5] This enhanced Li+ solvation and promoted Li2Sx dissociation could be attributable to CSA with high εr and DN.
[M0093 p5] The sulfur atomic concentration ([S]) in a saturated Li2S6/CSA solution reaches approximately 18 mol L−1, which is significantly higher than that in DME (9 mol L−1; Figure S24).
[M0094 p5] Conversely, [S] in a saturated Li2S6/MeTHF solution is only 0.6 mol L−1, lower than that in DOL (1.0 mol L−1). 17O NMR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the LiPSs solvation structure (Figure 3a−d).
[M0095 p5] The 17O chemical shift of 0.1 M Li2S6 in CSA exhibits a significant upfield shift of −0.16 ppm, whereas that of 0.1 M Li2S6 in DME shows a smaller upfield shift (−0.11 ppm).
[M0096 p5] This difference confirms a stronger coordination between CSA and Li+ than with DME, resulting in a CSA-dominated Li2S6 solvation structure.
[M0097 p5] The 17O chemical shifts of 0.1 M Li2S6 in DOL and MeTHF exhibit slight upfield shifts (−0.03 and −0.01 ppm, respectively), indicating weak coordination with Li+.
[M0098 p5] This suggests that MeTHF is mostly located in the outer shell of Li2S6 solvation structure, forming a gradient distribution of mixed solvents. 0.1 M Li2S6 in the mixed solvents (CSA:MeTHF and DME:DOL) were further analyzed by comparative 7Li NMR spectroscopy (Figure 3e).
[M0099 p5] The shield effect of Li+ in CSA:MeTHF is stronger than in DME:DOL due to the stronger coordination capability of CSA compared to DME.
[M0100 p5] This verifies the efficacy of CSA:MeTHF in regulating the LiPSs solvation structure.

[M0114 p6] In the COSY spectra (Figure 3f,g), both the abscissa and ordinate represent the 1H NMR chemical shifts.
[M0115 p6] The off-diagonal cross-peaks arise through 1H−1H coupling between protons, which occurs through chemical bonds and provides information about intramolecular connectivity.38 Diagonal signals typically represent intramolecular interactions, while off-diagonal cross-peaks can indicate either intramolecular (through-space) or intermolecular correlations.

[M0123 p6] This suggests a greater degree of aggregation occurs in the CSA:MeTHF electrolyte.

[M0125 p6] Thus, the CSA−MeTHF interaction enables the electrolyte to form not only a gradient solvation structure but also clustered LiPSs.

[M0133 p7] To investigate this, Li2S deposition and dissolution experiments were conducted using both electrolytes, with the results shown in Figures S28−S32.
[M0134 p7] These results confirm that the primary inner solvation sheath of the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte enables the formation of a three-dimensional (3D) Li2S deposit.
[M0135 p7] Additionally, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests at various scan rates demonstrate that CSA/MeTHF electrolyte with a gradient LiPSs solvation structure strongly facilitates rapid LiPSs conversion kinetics (Figures S33−S37).
[M0136 p7] Then, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted to evaluate the dynamic interfacial chemistry of the S cathode.
[M0137 p7] Li||Li2S6 coin cell with a S loading (MS) of 1.4 mg cm−2 was assembled and tested at 0.1 C.
[M0138 p7] As shown in Figures 4a−c, at the initial states-of-charge (SoC) (i), high-order LiPSs dissolve in the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte, leading to a smooth surface of CF with low impedance.
[M0139 p7] At SoC (ii), where high-order LiPSs transform into low-order species, tiny Li2S particles begin to gradually electrodeposit on the CF surface with a slightly increased impedance.
[M0140 p7] The Li2S particle becomes nonuniform at SoC (iii), showing higher impedance than that at SoC (ii).
[M0141 p7] At SoC (iv), an island-like Li2S morphology forms.
[M0142 p7] The abundant triple-phase interface among Li2S, the CF substrate, and the electrolyte facilitates efficient Li+ transport and diffusion.
[M0143 p7] During the discharge process, the Li2S particles become small with low electrical impedances at the SoC (v), resulting from the oxidation of Li2S to LiPSs.
[M0144 p7] At SoC (vi), Li2S is fully converted to S, resulting in a smooth film with white insulating regions on the CF surface.
[M0145 p7] This morphology indicates a highly reversible conversion process.
[M0146 p7] From the EIS result in Figures S38a,b, increased impedances are remarkably observed at SoCs iii, iv, and v using DD electrolyte.
[M0147 p7] This is mainly attributed to the limited LiPSs solubility in DD electrolyte, which induces 2D growth of Li2S with a film-like morphology on the CF surface (Figure S38c).
[M0148 p7] Further, this premature and compact passivation severely hinders subsequent LiPSs reduction reactions, resulting in low S utilization and sluggish conversion kinetics.
[M0149 p7] Moreover, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) method based on the EIS result was conducted to analyze the interfacial evolution during the redox conversion reaction.
[M0150 p7] As shown in Figures S39 and S40, the DRT profiles acquired at different stages of charge−discharge reveal seven characteristic peaks across high to low frequency range.44 Each peak corresponds to a distinct physical or chemical process with a specific time scale.45 These peaks are assigned as follows: interparticle resistance (P1), double-layer relaxation (P2), solid−electrolyte interphase (P3), positive electrode charge transfer (P4), positive electrode charge transfer (P5), polysulfide diffusion (P6), and general diffusion (P7).
[M0151 p7] During cycling, the interfacial evolution using CSA/MeTHF electrolyte is primarily governed by P7 peak (canary area), especially at SoC iv (Figure 4d).
[M0152 p7] In contrast, the cell using DD electrolytes exhibits dominance of both P6 and P7 peaks at SoCs (iii, iv, and v) in Figure 4e.
[M0153 p7] In addition, XPS and morphological analyses reveal stark differences between the two electrolytes (Figures S41−45).
[M0154 p7] The CSA/MeTHF electrolyte enables 3D porous Li2S cathode deposition and a smooth LMA surface enriched with protective high-valence sulfur species (SO4 2−/ SO3 2−), demonstrating excellent LMA compatibility.
[M0155 p7] In sharp contrast, the DD electrolyte produces a compact 2D Li2S film on the cathode and a mossy LMA surface dominated by corrosive low-valence sulfur species (Li2S, Li2S2, and LiPSs), highlighting severe polysulfide-induced LMA degradation.

[M0161 p8] Understanding the origin of these distinct interfacial chemistries is crucial for advancing fundamental issues of Li− S battery electrochemistry.
[M0162 p8] The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was applied for decoupling the electrochemical polarization during the 4 M conversion reaction.
[M0163 p8] Electrochemical polarization is defined as the deviation of the electrode potential from its equilibrium value.
[M0164 p8] The Li||S cells with a lean electrolyte of 4 μL mg−1 and high MS of 4.5 mg cm−2 were subjected to GITT measurements, including galvanostatic discharge or charge at 0.05 C for 20 min and following a relaxation time of 1 h for each GITT step (Figures 4f,g and S46).
[M0165 p8] Usually, the total polarization voltage (ηtotal) is defined as the gap between the working voltage and the equilibrium voltage.
[M0166 p8] This can be decoupled into three components: ohmic polarization (ηohm), concentration polarization (ηcon), and activation polarization (ηact).14 The values of ηcon and ηohm + ηact could be distinguished from the GITT profile, while the ηohm value is calculated using Ohm’s law from the EIS result (Figures S47).
[M0167 p8] The relative contributions of each polarization component at different depths of discharge (DoDs) are shown in Figures 4h and S46c.The contribution of ηohm remains minimal throughout the discharge process, indicating that it is not the dominant factor determining the S-based reduction rate.
[M0168 p8] After the DoD of 25%, ηact becomes the dominant polarization in the DD electrolyte (Figures S46c and S48), indicating sluggish conversion kinetics governed by interfacial chemistry.
[M0169 p8] These kinetics are limited not only by the LiPSs maximum concentration but also by the protracted liquid−solid conversion process.
[M0170 p8] Although, for the CSA/ MeTHF electrolyte, the proportion of ηcon exceeds 50% after the DoD of 25%, all three polarization components maintain consistent trends throughout low voltage plateau.
[M0171 p8] This demonstrates that CSA/MeTHF electrolyte induces stable interfacial chemistry that sustains rapid concentration-dominated conversion, particularly for the shorter order LiPSs.32 Thus, these results demonstrate a clear correlation between interfacial chemistry and electrochemical polarization during the 4 M conversion process, highlighting the critical role of electrolyte engineering.
[M0172 p8] To verify the suppressed shuttle effect and boosted conversion kinetics of LiPSs resulting from the gradient solvation structure of the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte, Li−S coin cells were first assembled and tested in Figure 5.
[M0173 p8] With a MS of 1.1 mg cm−2 and E/S ratio of 16.7 μL mg−1, a Li||S coin cell using CSA/MeTHF electrolyte delivers an initial specific capacity of 1037 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C (Figure 5a), verifying efficient S utilization.
[M0174 p8] And the capacity retention achieves 70% after 300 cycles, corresponding to a low decay rate of only 0.05% per cycle.
[M0175 p8] The average CE during cycling reaches 99.8%, indicating effectively suppressed LiPSs shuttle effect.
[M0176 p8] With an increased MS of 3.0 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 7.4 μL mg−1, the initial capacity using CSA/MeTHF achieves 992 mAh g−1 with a small polarization voltage at the low plateau (ΔElow) of 0.232 V (Figures 5b and S49a).
[M0177 p8] After 130 cycles, the capacity retention remains at 88% with a high average CE of 99.5% (Figure 5c), while ΔElow shows no obvious change (Figure S49b).
[M0178 p8] In contrast, using DD electrolyte exhibits a significantly large ΔElow in the first cycle, which sharply increased after 130 cycle (Figure S49c).
[M0179 p8] Since lean electrolyte and high MS are prerequisites for practical high-energy-density Li−S batteries, the electrochemical performance using CSA/MeTHF electrolyte was further evaluated under harsh condition: an E/S of 4.2 μL mg−1 and a MS of 6.3 mg cm−2 in Figures 5d and S50.
[M0180 p8] The initial capacity reached 1211 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C, corresponding to an areal capacity of 7.6 mAh cm−2.
[M0181 p8] After 50 cycles, the capacity is retained at 909 mAh g−1 (a capacity retention of 67%), demonstrating excellent cycling stability using high MS and lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0182 p8] With a MS of 8.6 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 3.6 μL mg−1, the coin cell using the CSA/MeTHF electrolyte delivers an initial capacity of 1094 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C, corresponding to an areal capacity of 9.4 mAh cm−2 (Figures 5e and S51).
[M0183 p8] After 30 cycles, the areal capacity remains at 7.7 mAh cm−2, significantly exceeding that of commercial lithium-ion batteries (∼4 mAh cm−2).
[M0184 p8] The outstanding performance under harsh conditions (MS ≥3 mg cm−2 and E/S ≤8 μL mg−1) demonstrates a competitive advantage over most of reported electrolytes for state-of-the-art Li−S batteries (Figure 5f and Table S13).33,36,41,46−53 Finally, a 3 Ah-level Li−S pouch cell was assembled to validate the practicability of this electrolyte screening approach.
[M0185 p8] The detailed parameters are shown in Figure S52 and Table S14.
[M0186 p8] As shown in Figure S53a, the Li−S pouch cell using the CSA/ MeTHF electrolyte shows an initial discharge capacity of 3.12 Ah and an initial energy density of 369 Wh kg−1 at 0.03 C, based on the total mass of the pouch cell.
[M0187 p8] After 15 cycles, the pouch cell still works, and the energy density is maintained at 307 Wh kg−1 (Figure S53b).
[M0188 p8] It should be noted that this cycling lifespan of 3 Ah-level Li||S pouch cell is only proof-of-concept.
[M0189 p8] To validate the screening criteria, a new solvent, 4,4,4- trifluoro-N,N-dimethylbutanamide (TFBA) was synthesized (Figures S54−S57), and paired with DOL, forming a gradient solvation structure (TFBA/DOL) that effectively suppresses the LiPSs shuttle effect (Figures S58 and S59).
[M0190 p8] The TFBA/DOL electrolyte enables stable Li||Li cycling for over 1000 h and delivers 1051 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C with 92% capacity retention after 100 cycles (Figures S60 and S61), confirming the broad applicability of this screening strategy for Li−S batteries.
[M0191 p8] In conclusion, we developed a screening methodology comprising 75 solvent classifications based on εr and DN, and electrolyte evaluation using seven critical electrochemical performance parameters.
[M0192 p8] The CSA/MeTHF electrolyte, combining midstrong and weak solvation solvents, exhibits a distinct solvent−solvent interaction that forms a gradient solvation structure, as evidenced by multiple spectroscopic techniques.
[M0193 p8] Furthermore, the correlations among Li2S electrodeposition, electrode interfacial chemistry, and LiPSs conversion kinetics are demonstrated.

[M0195 p8] Three Ah-level Li−S pouch cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 3.12 Ah and an initial energy density of 369 Wh kg−1.
[M0196 p8] Additionally, the TFBA/ DOL electrolyte is designed to validate the universality of this screening criterion engineering.

[S0101 p11] Supplementary notes 2.1 Thermodynamic analysis of the dissolution process The dissolution of solutes (e.g., lithium salts) in solvents is governed by the second law of thermodynamics.
[S0102 p11] The thermodynamic requirement for spontaneous dissolution is a negative change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG < 0), where ΔG = G2 – G1.
[S0103 p11] Here, G1 represents the Gibbs free energy of the separated solute and solvent, and G2 represents that of the homogeneous solution.
[S0104 p11] If ΔG > 0, dissolution is non-spontaneous, indicating poor solubility.
[S0105 p11] If ΔG = 0, the system attains a dynamic dissolution–precipitation equilibrium.
[S0106 p11] This thermodynamic criterion determines the spontaneity of dissolution.
[S0107 p11] The dissolution process primarily involves two steps: (i) dissociation of the solute lattice and (ii) solvation of the dissociated ions.
[S0108 p11] (i) Lattice dissociation.
[S0109 p11] The Gibbs free energy change (∆Glat) for breaking the solute lattice is expressed as following equation (6): ∆Glat = ∆Hlat − T∆Slat = ∆U + p∆V − T∆Slat, ΔV ≈ 0 ≈ ΔUlat − T∆Slat (6) where ∆Hlat is the lattice disruption enthalpy change; ∆Slat is the lattice disruption entropy change; T is the absolute temperature.
[S0110 p11] For the whole solid-state systems, the volume change of the lattice disruption is negligible (ΔV ≈ 0), rendering the pΔV term negligible.
[S0111 p11] (ii) Ionic solvation.
[S0112 p11] The Gibbs free energy change (∆Gsol) is expressed as the equation (7): ∆Gsol = ∆Hsol − T∆Ssol (7) where ∆Hsol is the solvation enthalpy change; ∆Ssol is the solvation entropy change.

[S0115 p12] The Gutmann donor number (DN) provides a measure for evaluating a solvent’s solvation enthalpy.
[S0116 p12] It is defined as the negative enthalpy change (−ΔHsol) for the 1:1 adduct formation between a solvent and SbCl5 (a strong Lewis acid) in a dilute 1,2-dichloroethane solution equation (9).27 DN = −ΔHsol (kcal mol−1) (9) Due that the solvation process is exothermic, a negative solvation enthalpy change (∆Hsol < 0) facilitates the dissolution. 2.2 Solubility and compatibility of Region I solvents for Li−S batteries Both LiTFSI and LiNO3 are critical solutes in electrolyte for Li−S batteries, yet LiNO3 exhibits significantly lower solubility than LiTFSI in carbonate-based solvents (Region I).
[S0117 p12] This discrepancy arises from that NO3− possesses a higher charge density than TFSI−, resulting in a substantially higher lattice energy for LiNO3 (Figure S2).28 Meanwhile, the higher DN of NO3− (22.2 kcal mol−1) compared to carbonate solvents (DN < 19 kcal mol−1) leads to an insufficient solvation enthalpy (∆Ηsol) to overcome the lattice energy (∆Ulat) of LiNO3.
[S0118 p12] Consequently, LiNO3 displays ultralow solubility (< 0.1 M) in carbonate-based electrolyte.29 Moreover, for Li−S batteries, LiPSs act as strongly nucleophiles that attack carbonate solvents (e.g., EC or PC) via nucleophilic substitution, yielding thiocarbonates and lithium alkoxides (Figure S3).
[S0119 p12] This side reaction not only hinders the conversion of long-chain LiPSs to short-chain LiPSs but also depletes carbonate solvents, ultimately terminating the discharging process (Figure S4).
[S0120 p12] Owing to the poor solubility of LiNO3 and chemical incompatibility with LiPSs, Region I solvents (primarily carbonates) are unsuitable for practical Li−S batteries.
