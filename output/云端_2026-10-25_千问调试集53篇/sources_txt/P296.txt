[M0001 p1] Article Electrochemistry, 90(10), 107003 (2022) A Sulfolane-Based Electrolyte Optimized for Microporous Activated Carbon-Sulfur Composites for Lithium Sulfur Batteries Takeshi TONOYA,a,§ Hirofumi YAMAMOTO,a Yukiko MATSUI,a,§§ Hidenori HINAGO,b,§§ and Masashi ISHIKAWAa,*,§§§ a Department of Chemistry and Materials Engineering, Faculty of Chemistry, Materials and Bioengineering, Kansai University, 3-3-35 Yamate-cho, Suita 564-8680, Japan b Asahi Kasei Corporation, 1-1-2 Yuraku-cho, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo 100-0006, Japan * Corresponding author: masaishi@kansai-u.ac.jp ABSTRACT The dissolution of lithium polysulfide into electrolytes is a major limitation of Li-S batteries that hinders their practical applications.
[M0002 p1] Lithium polysulfide dissolution is suppressed by using sulfur-complexed microporous-carbon cathodes in lithium–sulfur batteries.
[M0003 p1] This study reports the use of a localized high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE), in which a sulfolane (SL) high-concentration electrolyte is diluted with hydrofluoroether (HFE), for the strong suppression of lithium polysulfide elution.
[M0004 p1] A previously reported sulfolane electrolyte in the solvated state exhibits a short cycle life of eight cycles, whereas the sulfur-containing microporous-carbon cathode with a sulfolane-based electrolyte prepared in the ratio of lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) : SL : HFE = (0.572–0.953) : 2.00 : 1.26 (mol) exhibits a discharge capacity of more than 550 mAh g−1, even after 50 charge–discharge cycles.
[M0005 p1] Increasing the LiTFSI ratio decreases the response current during discharge, as indicated by cyclic voltammetry.
[M0006 p1] A sulfur cathode with microporous carbon (AZC) shows equivalent performance on decreasing the amount of solvated LiTFSI.
[M0007 p1] This study could guide the design and development of electrolytes for sulfurcontaining microporous-carbon cathodes. © The Author(s) 2022.

[M0013 p1] Introduction The recent expansion of lithium-ion-battery applications has necessitated an improvement in their energy density.
[M0014 p1] The intercalation materials used in lithium-ion batteries at present are expected to reach their energy-density limit in the near future;1 therefore, all-solid-state, lithium-sulfur (Li-S), and lithium-air batteries are attracting immense attention as next-generation rechargeable batteries.
[M0015 p1] Li-S batteries have been widely studied since the 1970s.2,3 Sulfur (S), the cathode material in Li-S batteries, exhibits several advantageous properties, such as, light weight, low cost, and abundance; moreover, sulfur has a high theoretical capacity (1672 mAh g11) as its redox reaction involves a twoelectron transfer reaction per S atom (S8 + 16Li+ + 16e1 1⁄4 8Li2S).4 Although Li-S batteries exhibit high potential as next-generation rechargeable batteries, numerous limitations that hinder their commercial applications.
[M0016 p1] A major limitation is the rapid dissolution of lithium polysulfides (reaction intermediates formed during charging and discharging) in organic electrolytes, causing irreversible side reactions with specific electrolytes.5 Highly soluble longchain lithium polysulfides (Li2S8–4), formed as intermediates during the sulfur-cathode discharge process, diffuse into the lithium anode and are reduced to insoluble short-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2S1–2).
[M0017 p1] Subsequently, the lithium polysulfides undergo reverse reactions during the charging process and diffuse back to the cathode.
[M0018 p1] This shuttle behavior (labeled redox shuttle reactions) lowers the actual capacity and cycle life of Li-S batteries because the leached lithium polysulfide exhibits nonconductor behavior at the positive and negative electrode surfaces;6,7 electrolyte-based countermeasures are used to overcome this limitation.
[M0019 p1] In general, 1 mol dm13 (M) lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTF- SI)/1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) : 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) (1 : 1 by volume) is used as an electrolyte for Li-S batteries because of its low viscosity, good rate capacity, high donor number, and excellent ionic conductivity;8,9 lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is used as an electrolyte additive to effectively suppress the dissolution of lithium polysulfides.10 LiNO3 enhances the durability of Li-S batteries by forming a “passivation layer” that effectively inhibits the dissolution of lithium polysulfides and a “solid electrolyte interface (SEI)” that protects the Li-metal anode.11–13 However, the applicability of LiNO3 is severely limited by its discharge voltage due to irreversible reduction reaction below 1.8 V (vs.
[M0020 p1] Li/Li+),14 gradual dissipation during charge-discharge cycling, the formation of toxic byproducts, and low sustainability.15 Additionally, 2 wt% LiNO3 is generally added to ether-based electrolytes due to its low solubility; this is not practicable for long-term charge-discharge cycling as it is a consumable additive.
[M0021 p1] Highly concentrated electrolytes (HCEs), such as solvated electrolytes, are used as electrolytes for Li-S batteries without lithium nitrate.16–18 The HCE, with no free spaces for lithium polysulfide dissolution, physically traps the lithium polysulfide in the electrolyte.

[M0028 p2] In this study, a sulfolane-based LHCE electrolyte was developed to maximize the performance of microporous activated carbonsulfur composites with the suppression of lithium polysulfide dissolution.
[M0029 p2] Although a high concentration of lithium salt was more effective in suppressing lithium polysulfide dissolution, it overprotected the microporous activated carbon-sulfur composite, adversely affecting its activity.
[M0030 p2] This is the first report to suggest that a high concentration of lithium hinders the optimization of the activity of microporous activated carbon-sulfur composites. 2.
[M0031 p2] Experimental 2.1 Preparation and activation of azulmic carbon (AZC) AZC was prepared according to a previous publication.17,18 AZA was carbonized at 800 °C for 1 h under a nitrogen atmosphere; subsequently, the AZA carbides were mixed with an alkaline activator (KOH) in a ratio of 1 : 2 by weight in deionized water, stirred, heated at 100 °C for 5 h, and dried.
[M0032 p2] The sample was activated at 800 °C for 1 h in a quartz tube (3 cm in diameter) under an argon atmosphere; subsequently, the activated sample was dispersed in deionized water and neutralized with dilute hydrochloric acid.
[M0033 p2] The samples were then filtered, washed with excess deionized water, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for more than 12 h to obtain AZC. 2.2 Preparation of the sulfur-composite AZC (AZC-S) and AZC-S cathode The amount of sulfur composite in the AZC was calculated from the pore volume measured by nitrogen adsorption/desorption experiments.
[M0034 p2] AZC (0.4 g) and sulfur (0.6 g) were mixed in an agate mortar and placed in a simple reaction vessel made of stainless steel.
[M0035 p2] The mixture was heated to 155 °C in an air atmosphere and maintained for 5 h to melt the sulfur and enable its diffusion into the AZC pores.
[M0036 p2] The temperature was then increased from 155 to 300 °C and maintained for 2 h to remove the excess sulfur outside the AZC pores.
[M0037 p2] Temperature increments were carried out at a rate of 10 °C min11, while air cooling was used to cool the samples to room temperature.
[M0038 p2] The AZC composite formed with sulfur was labeled AZC-S.
[M0039 p2] To fabricate the sulfur cathode, AZC-S, carbon nanotubes (CNT), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), and styrenebutadiene rubber (SBR) were first mixed in a weight ratio of 89 : 5 : 3 : 3 in deionized water to form a slurry.
[M0040 p2] Subsequently, the slurry was coated on an Al foil using the doctor-blade method and dried in an oven at 40 °C for more than 3 h; the residual water was removed by drying in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for more than 12 h.
[M0041 p2] The amount of sulfur in the electrode was 3.0 mg cm12. 2.3 Electrolyte preparation and assembly of Li-S cells Electrolytes were prepared by mixing different concentrations of lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, Solvey) (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 M) in a solvent containing sulfolane (SL, Kishida Chemical) and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl (HFE, Daikin Industries) in a 1 : 1 ratio by volume (2.00 : 1.26 by mol).
[M0042 p2] For comparison, the sulfolane electrolyte reported by Watanabe et al. was prepared using LiTFSI : SL : HFE = 1 : 2 : 2 (mol) and labeled the standard SL.37 A two-electrode cell was constructed using an AZC-S electrode as the positive electrode, Li foil as the anode, and a polyolefin microporous membrane as the separator; a glove box filled with Ar, maintained at a dew point of less than 180 °C, was used for cell construction and electrolyte preparation, to prevent changes due to moisture. 2.4 Physical properties and electrochemical tests An Autosorb iQ instrument (Anton-Paar) was used for the nitrogen adsorption/desorption measurements at 1196 °C, while thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, DTG-60H, Shimazu Co.
[M0043 p2] Ltd.) was used to estimate the sulfur loading.
[M0044 p2] Charge-discharge tests of the Li-S batteries were carried out at a current density of 167.2 mA g11 (0.1 C) per sulfur weight in the voltage range of 1.0– 3.0 V (vs.
[M0045 p2] A scanning rate of 0.1 mV s11 was used for cyclic voltammetry and linear sweep voltammetry, and all the electrochemical measurements were performed at 25 °C.
[M0046 p2] A Nanofinder30 (TOKYO INSTRUMENTS) green laser (at 532 nm) was used for Raman spectroscopy.
[M0047 p2] The viscosity of the electrolyte was measured with a microVISC (Viscotech) micro differential pressure viscometer, and the ionic conductivity was estimated with a Seven2Go Cond meter S3 in combination with a Cond probe InLab 751-4mm (METTLER TOLEDO).
[M0048 p2] These physical properties of the electrolyte were evaluated at 25 °C in dry air with a dew point below 140 °C. 3.
[M0049 p2] Results and Discussion It has been previously reported that AZC contains micropore-rich activated carbon as it is formed by the selective decomposition of the nitrogen moieties in AZA by its activation.
[M0050 p2] The specific surface area of AZC was calculated using the Brunauer (Emmett) Teller method; the specific surface area and pore volume were 2231 m2 g11 and 1.059 cm3 g11, respectively.
[M0051 p2] The theoretical sulfur-fillable amount, calculated from the pore volume, was 67 wt%.
[M0052 p2] The theoretical sulfur loading capacity was calculated using the following equation: Theoretical loading of sulfur 1⁄4 V  MðcarbonÞ  μðsulfurÞ V  MðcarbonÞ  μðsulfurÞ þ MðcarbonÞ [where, V: pore volume (cm3 g11), M: mass of carbon (g), and μ: density of sulfur (g cm13)].
[M0053 p2] Sulfur undergoes significant volume changes during charging and discharging processes; therefore, the amount of sulfur loaded into the activated carbon was slightly lower than the theoretical sulfur capacity.
[M0054 p2] The amount of sulfur in the composite was 60 wt% according to thermogravimetric measurements (Fig. 1).
[M0055 p2] The physical properties of the (0.5–2.5 M) LiTFSI/SL : HFE electrolytes (hereafter referred to as “x-SL”) and the standard SL are summarized in Table 1.
[M0056 p2] HFE was used as a diluent to lower the electrolyte viscosity, with negligible dissolution of the lithium salt and no distortion of its solvation structure.
[M0057 p2] The standard SL was solvated with LiTFSI and SL, and LiTFSI : SL = 1 : 2 (by mol) was considered to be the solvation ratio.
[M0058 p2] All the formulated electrolytes (0.5 to 2.5-SL) exhibited a lower LiTFSI concentration relative to SL compared to that of the standard SL.

[M0071 p3] Li-S batteries were assembled and used for constant-current charge-discharge tests to analyze the lithium polysulfide dissolution in each electrolyte.
[M0072 p3] Figure 3 shows the first charge-discharge curve using each electrolyte; only 0.5-SL showed a plateau around the discharge curve of 2.1 V, indicating lithium polysulfide dissolution due to the low lithium-salt concentration.
[M0073 p3] The initial charge capacity was 1511, 1448, 1389, 1289, 1295, and 1233 mAh g11 for the 0.5-, 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, 2.5-, and standard SL electrolyte, respectively.
[M0074 p3] The charge capacity in the first cycle decreased as the LiTFSI concentration increased. 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, 2.5-, and standard SL showed a single discharge plateau around 1.8 V.
[M0075 p3] This behavior is like that of a solid-state reaction that produces Li2S and Li2S2, which is characteristic of sulfur loaded in microporous carbon.

[M0086 p4] The 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, and 2.5-SL electrolytes underwent charge/discharge cycles without rapid capacity decay up to the 50th cycle, with similar cycling-capacity-decay behavior.
[M0087 p4] The average coulombic efficiencies up to 50 cycles was 102, 100, 99.5, and 98.8 % for the 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, and 2.5-SL electrolyte, respectively; the 1.0-SL electrolyte exhibited an average coulombic efficiency of over 100 %, indicating insufficient suppression of lithium polysulfide dissolution.
[M0088 p4] The 1.0-, 1.5-, 2.0-, and 2.5-SL electrolytes maintained a reversible capacity of approximately 600 mAh g11 after 50 cycles.
[M0089 p4] This was consistent with the information obtained from the peak positions of the Raman spectra (Fig. 2), which indicated sufficient inhibition of lithium polysulfide elution above 745 cm11.
[M0090 p4] The standard SL showed similar discharge capacities as the 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5-SL electrolytes for the first few cycles, with a rapid capacity decay after seven cycles.
[M0091 p4] Thus, while combining a microporous activated carbon-sulfur cathode with an SL-electrolyte system, the LiTFSI to SL ratio was a more important parameter than the viscosity and conductivity values.
[M0092 p4] Photographs of the 0.5- and 1.5- SL separators after one cycle were used to confirm lithium polysulfide elution (Fig. 5).
[M0093 p4] The 0.5-SL electrolyte was less effective in inhibiting lithium polysulfide elution; the separator stained dark yellow, confirming the presence of a large amount of sulfide.
[M0094 p4] Although the separator appeared slightly yellow while using the 1.5- SL electrolyte, the system exhibited a greater inhibition of lithium polysulfide elution compared to 0.5-SL system.
[M0095 p4] Cyclic voltammetry measurements were used to investigate the charge-discharge behavior of the formulated SL electrolytes.
[M0096 p4] Figure 6 shows the CV measurements for the first three cycles.
[M0097 p4] The 0.5-SL electrolyte showed a wide peak that keeps the response current flowing up to 1 V during discharge, indicating lithium polysulfide dissolution from the first cycle.
[M0098 p4] The 1.0-SL electrolyte showed a sharp response current peak with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.29 V in the first cycle, with a slightly wider peak from the second cycle, indicating lithium polysulfide dissolution; this was in agreement with the coulombic-efficiency results of the constant-current charge-discharge tests.
[M0099 p4] For 1.5-SL (FWHM: 0.27 V) and 2.0-SL (FWHM: 0.33 V), the peak width of the response current changed negligibly during discharge, indicating a suppression of lithium polysulfide dissolution.
[M0100 p4] Although 2.5-SL exhibited a wide response current peak with FWHM of 0.70 V from the first-cycle discharge, the response current continued to flow at 1.0 V, unlike the peak shape caused by lithium polysulfide dissolution in 0.5-SL.
[M0101 p4] Thus, the increase in lithium-salt concentration suppressed lithium polysulfide dissolution, but the decrease in wettability and ion-transport ability due to the increase in viscosity possibly reduced the response current.
[M0102 p4] The standard-SL (FWHM: 0.78 V) and 2.5-SL electrolytes showed similar CV behavior, indicating that an extremely high lithium-salt concentration could reduce the response current in microporous carbonactivated carbon-sulfur composite cathodes, possibly due to the inhibition of lithium-ion transport by sulfur-filled micropores and the low wettability of the highly concentrated and solvated electrolyte.
[M0103 p4] The low cycle life of the AZC-S electrode in the standard-SL electrolyte (Fig. 4) would correlate with its low response current and high overvoltage in the CV (Fig. 6), which suggests a problem with low reaction activity.
[M0104 p4] We speculate that such response current and overvoltage in the standard-SL are caused by a high resistance of the electrode because of electrolyte component decomposition such as HFE from the early stages of the charge-discharge cycle.
[M0105 p4] From the above results, the optimization of the electrolyte in this study is related to the following matters: first, notably, the viscosity and ionic conductivity of the electrolyte alone cannot determine battery performance.
[M0106 p4] Secondly, for the practical optimization, the amount of HFE was able to be reduced due to the possible decomposition of HFE, but this caused an increase in viscosity and a decrease in wettability to the present cathode.
[M0107 p4] Therefore, under these low HFE conditions, the number of moles of LiTFSI to SL was reduced, thus increasing the free SL “to the extent that it does not dissolve the polysulfide” (examples are 2.0-, 1.5- and 1.0-SL), thereby increasing the activity of the cathode reaction.
[M0108 p4] These measures are regarded as electrolyte optimization in this study. 4.
[M0109 p4] Conclusion In this study, the relationship between the electrolyte concentration and the expressed capacity of a sulfur cathode fabricated using microporous carbon (AZC) was investigated.
[M0110 p4] Notably, the viscosity and ionic conductivity of the electrolyte alone cannot determine battery performance.
[M0111 p4] The Raman spectrum of the SL electrolyte has a peak at 745 cm11, which suggests that the coordination state of SL and LiTFSI is such that the dissolution of lithium polysulfide is sufficiently suppressed.
[M0112 p4] The electrolyte containing an LiTFSI : SL ratio of (0.953–0.572) : 2.00 (by mol) showed sufficient inhibition of lithium polysulfide elution.
[M0113 p4] In this ratio range, even the minimum LiTFSI-containing electrolyte (0.572 : 2.00) exhibited a stable charge-discharge cycle compared to the conventional standard-SL electrolyte.
[M0114 p4] Thus, a reduction in the Li-salt content of electrolytes could not only increase the reaction activity and reversibility but also reduce their cost and density, facilitating the practical applications of lithium-sulfur batteries.
[M0115 p4] Furthermore, it could provide more space for the dissolution of additives, facilitating the design and development of high-performance electrolytes.
