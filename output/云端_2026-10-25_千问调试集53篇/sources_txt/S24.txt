[M0001 p1] A New Hybrid Solid/Solvating Sulfur Conversion for Energy-Dense Lithium-Sulfur Batteries Xiaoyu Jin, Xiaoqun Qi, Fengyi Yang, Han Zhou, Ruining Jiang, Dan Yang, Zhou Fang, Fei Zhou, Jie Ji, Zhenglu Zhu, Lixia Yuan*, Yunhui Huang , and Long Qie* 1.
[M0002 p1] Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery based on the conversion chemistry of 2Li + S →Li2S delivers an ultrahigh theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg1.[1–7] However, in practical terms, its available energy density is low because the operation of Li-S batteries in the mainstream dioxolane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME) electrolyte requires a high electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio (usually >5 μL mg1).[8–12] DOL and DME with high donor numbers show high dissolution of the Li polysulfides (LPSs); therefore, the 1M ether electrolyte participates in the “dissolution-precipitation” conversion process at the cathode side and converts to catholyte after dissolving LPSs.[13,14] Under lean-etherelectrolyte conditions, our previous work revealed that S cathodes suffer from sluggish conversion kinetics caused by significantly decreased Li+ conductivity and deteriorated wettability of the high-LPSs-ratio catholyte.[15] In addition to low gravimetric energy density, the volumetric energy density of Li-S batteries is largely limited by the demand for high-porosity (>70%) S cathodes.[16] A dense S cathode fails to work in conventional ether electrolytes because it undergoes a locally low E/S ratio with a decreased electrolyte uptake in the cathode structure.
[M0003 p1] Catalytic cathodes and mediation additives have been extensively exploited to improve the conversion kinetics under low E/S ratios, but their effects are limited to a certain extent.[17–24] Sparingly solvating electrolytes are promising candidates to decrease the E/S ratio by fundamentally altering the conversion pathway.
[M0004 p1] Sparingly solvating electrolytes were developed by increasing Li salt to an extent where the electrolyte mainly consists of Li salt-solvent coordination with little free solvent.[25] This unique electrolyte structure can effectively suppress the dissolution of LPSs and consequently drives a solid conversion process, which is expected to be less dependent on electrolyte amount in comparison to the “dissolution-precipitation” process in solvating electrolytes.[26] A series of acetonitrile (AN)-,[27–29] ether-,[30–32] carbonate-,[33,34] and sulfolane-based sparingly solvating electrolytes have been developed.[35] Among them, AN-based ones successfully enable Li-S batteries to operate under the lowest E/S ratio of 1 μL mg1 as previously being demonstrated.[15,29] However, the use of sparingly solvating electrolytes is critically challenged by sluggish S redox chemistry due to the solid conversion pathway and the very low Li+ conductivity of the electrolyte.
[M0005 p1] Herein, a new hybrid solid/solvating conversion pathway, that is, S converting to both solid and solvated LPSs, is achieved by a moderately solvating electrolyte, which combines lean-electrolyte operation as in sparingly solvating electrolytes and fast conversion kinetics as in solvating electrolytes.
[M0006 p1] The moderately solvating electrolyte is formulated by methoxyacetonitrile (MAN) since MAN possesses a high relative permittivity (21) that contributes to a high Li+ conductivity of 11.7 mS cm1 for 1M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in MAN, along with a low donor number (14.6 kcal mol1) that brings about a decreased solubility to LPSs (1/6 of that in DOL/DME).

[M0009 p1] DOI: 10.1002/eem2.70139 To boost the practical energy density of lithium-sulfur batteries, replacing conventional solvating electrolytes with sparingly solvating ones has shown promise by enabling solid-state sulfur conversion and reducing electrolyte consumption.
[M0010 p1] However, this approach often compromises sulfur redox kinetics.
[M0011 p1] This study reports a new sulfur conversion pathway distinct from both traditional solvated and sparingly solvated mechanisms.
[M0012 p1] Specifically, sulfur is converted into a mixture of solid and solvated lithium polysulfides (LPSs).
[M0013 p1] Such a hybrid solid/solvating conversion pathway is achieved using a newly formulated moderately solvating electrolyte, accomplishing both lean-electrolyte operation and fast conversion kinetics for lithium-sulfur batteries.
[M0014 p1] Methoxyacetonitrile (MAN) is selected as the solvent to formulate the moderately solvating electrolyte due to its high relative permittivity (21) that contributes to a high Li+ conductivity (11.7 mS cm1 for 1M lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide in MAN) and low donor number (14.6 kcal mol1) that reduces the solubility to LPSs to 1/6 of that in mainstream solvating electrolytes.
[M0015 p1] The as-formulated MAN electrolyte enables sulfur cathodes to operate at a low electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio of 2 μL mg1 and a low cathode porosity of 52%, displaying excellent prospects for boosting both gravimetric and volumetric energy density.

[M0023 p2] Results and Discussion S cathodes undergo different conversion pathways in different electrolytes.[36–38] In highly solvating electrolytes, for example, 1M LiTFSI in DOL/DME, S cathodes perform the well-known “dissolution-precipitation” conversion pathway,[39] of which the “dissolution” step is solid S converting to solvated LPSs as shown in Figure 1.
[M0024 p2] This conversion mechanism offers fast kinetics because the solvated LPSs function as mediators to promote redox reactions.
[M0025 p2] However, the “dissolution” process fundamentally requires a high E/S ratio (usually >5 μL mg1) to fully dissolve LPSs intermediates, leading to a significant reduction in the overall energy density of Li-S batteries as shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information.
[M0026 p2] With sparingly solvating electrolytes, S cathodes almost perform a solid conversion process.
[M0027 p2] Leveraging this mechanism, the E/S ratio can be reduced to extremely low levels, thereby significantly enhancing the energy density of the system.
[M0028 p2] The changes in mass distribution and the improvement in energy density resulting from the reduced E/S ratio are illustrated in Figure S2, Supporting Information.
[M0029 p2] The specific mass parameters of each component and the corresponding energy densities are presented in Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information, respectively.
[M0030 p2] But Li-S batteries suffer from sluggish conversion kinetics in sparingly solvating electrolytes, which can be attributed to two aspects.
[M0031 p2] One is that the conversion process from solid S to solid LPSs without the mediation of solvated LPSs is kinetically slow.
[M0032 p2] The other is that sparingly solvating electrolytes commonly have low Li+ conductivity,[30,40–41] for example, 0.27 mS cm1 for a diglyme (G2)-based electrolyte (G20.8LiTFSI, the molar ratio of G2 to LiTFSI is 0.8:1, diluted by 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether), in comparison with a high Li+ conductivity of 12.6 mS cm1 for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte as shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information.
[M0033 p2] The sluggish kinetics in sparingly solvating electrolytes leads to unsatisfying cyclability and poor rate performance.[42] Besides, sparingly solvating electrolytes are costly due to their high content Li salts, which makes them difficult to be used in large scale.
[M0034 p2] Disappointingly, the electrolytes designation for Li-S batteries always follows the above two principles: highly solvating electrolytes and sparingly solvating electrolytes.
[M0035 p2] Although Li-S batteries based on these electrolytes have made great progress, the development is limited to the laboratory level and is far from being practical.
[M0036 p2] Under this circumstance, a moderately solvating electrolyte that has a moderate solvating ability, lower than that in highly solvating electrolytes but higher than that in sparingly solvating electrolytes, toward LiPSs is immediately needed to induce new hybrid solid/solvating sulfur conversion, and thus guarantee the operation of Li-S batteries under lean-electrolyte condition with fast conversion kinetics.
[M0037 p2] Here, MAN is selected to formulate the moderately solvating electrolyte.
[M0038 p2] Among common aprotic solvents, nitriles have distinguished characters, with high relative permittivity that offers high Li+ conductivity, and with low donor number that brings about low dissolution to LPSs.
[M0039 p2] A typical example is acetonitrile (AN); however, it has a poor reductive stability and severely reacts with Li metal (Figure S4, Supporting Information).[43] In comparison with AN, the introduction of methoxy for MAN reduces the polarization degree (Figure 2a), which lowers the ability of MAN to acquire electrons and thus contributes to enhanced compatibility with Li metal, as demonstrated by the immersion tests (Figure S4, Supporting Information).
[M0040 p2] The basic properties of MAN are further compared with DOL and DME.
[M0041 p2] MAN has a decent relative permittivity of 21 that is beneficial to Li+ conductivity,[44] in comparison to DOL (6.8) and DME (7.2) (Figure 2b).
[M0042 p2] Figure 2c exhibits MAN-based electrolytes perform high Li+ conductivity comparable to the value of DOL/DME-based electrolytes at different concentrations.
[M0043 p2] In this work, we applied the 1M MAN electrolyte with a high Li+ conductivity of 11.7 mS cm1 into Li-S batteries, with the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte as the baseline.
[M0044 p2] Besides, MAN has a lower donor number of 14.6 than DOL (21.2) and DME (21.0), which contributes to lower dissolution to LPSs (Figure 2b).
[M0045 p2] To demonstrate the moderately solvating character of MAN, we prepared Li2S6-saturated DOL/DME and MAN solutions by stoichiometrically reacting Li2S and S (detailed preparation in Experimental Section).
[M0046 p2] As shown in Figure 2d, The Li2S6-saturated MAN shows a light brown color in contrast to a dark black color for the Li2S6-saturated DOL/DME, indicating a lower LPSs solubility in MAN solution.
[M0047 p2] The Li2S6 solubility was measured via a precipitation method previously reported by Zhang et al.[45] Benefited from the low DN, MAN shows a solubility to LPSs of 3 mol L1 based on S1/3 (Figure 2d), which is only 1/6 of that in DOL/DME.
[M0048 p2] Subsequently, we prepared Li2S6-saturated electrolytes to simulate the property evolution of catholyte under lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0049 p2] The 1M DOL/DME electrolyte shows a significant decrease of Li+ conductivity from 12.7 to 5.7 mS cm1 with saturated Li2S6 (Figure 2e).
[M0050 p2] In contrast, the 1M MAN electrolyte maintains a high conductivity of 11.7 mS cm1 with saturated Li2S6.
[M0051 p2] The contact angle of electrolytes and Li2S6-saturated electrolytes was tested to evaluate the wettability, as shown in Figure 2f.
[M0052 p2] The pristine 1M DOL/DME electrolyte displays a low contact angle of 28° on S cathodes, but it shows a significant increase to 85° with saturated Li2S6, indicating wettability deterioration Figure 1.

[M0061 p3] The MAN electrolyte brings about new S conversion behaviors.
[M0062 p3] The electrochemical performance of Li-S batteries in the moderately solvating MAN electrolyte is compared with that in the mainstream 1M DOL/DME electrolyte (an example of highly solvating electrolytes) and the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte (an example of sparingly solvating electrolytes).
[M0063 p3] The Li-S battery shows a typical two-plateau discharge profile in the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte corresponding to the “dissolutionprecipitation” process, and shows a one-plateau discharge profile in the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte corresponding to the solid conversion process.
[M0064 p3] Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test was performed to confirm the anodic oxidation stability of the solution (Figure S6, Supporting Information) and the onset potential of oxidative decomposition close to 4.0 V (vs Li/Li+) far exceeding the charging cut-off voltage (2.8 V) of Li-S batteries.
[M0065 p3] When applying the 1M MAN electrolyte, the Li-S battery also displays a two-plateau discharge profile (Figure 3a), but the first plateau shows a lower voltage of 2.23 V than that in the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte of 2.28 V, and the second plateau shows a higher voltage of 2.14 V in comparison to 2.09 V for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte.
[M0066 p3] CV curves (Figure 3b) show similar results, with two reduction peaks at 2.19 and 2.06 V for the 1M MAN electrolyte, in comparison to two reduction peaks at 2.28 and 1.99 V for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte, and one reduction peak at 1.52 V for the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte.
[M0067 p3] The electrochemical behavior of S in the 1M MAN electrolyte is thermodynamically and kinetically different from that in 1M DOL/DME and G20.8LiTFSI electrolytes.
[M0068 p3] Galvanostatic intermittent titration techniques (GITT) were performed to compare equilibrium states of Li-S batteries with different electrolytes.
[M0069 p3] As shown in Figure 3c, the S conversion process with the 1M MAN electrolyte is thermodynamically different from that with 1M DOL/DME and G20.8LiTFSI electrolytes, indicated by diverse equilibrium voltage.
[M0070 p3] Besides, GITT profiles demonstrate the 1M MAN electrolyte provides the fastest conversion kinetics among the three electrolytes.
[M0071 p3] The Li-S battery with the G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte unsurprisingly shows high overpotentials during the whole discharge process, owing to the slow solid conversion pathway and low Li+ conductivity.
[M0072 p3] As for the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte, it is widely accepted to afford fast redox Li-S chemistry with the mediation effect of solvated LPSs, and the corresponding GITT profile shows low overpotential.
[M0073 p3] However, when applying the 1M MAN electrolyte, the overpotential is even lower than the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte at the first discharge plateau, indicating more favorable conversion dynamics.
[M0074 p3] Rate performance (Figure 3d) is further compared for the three electrolytes.
[M0075 p3] The G20.8LiTFSI electrolyte offers the poorest rate performance with only 85 mAh g1 at 1C.
[M0076 p3] The Li-S battery with the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte shows better tolerance to high discharge current, but the discharge capacity significantly decreases to 187 mAh g1 at a high C rate of 5C.
[M0077 p3] In contrast, the 1M MAN electrolyte provides the best rate performance with 416 mAh g1 remaining at 5C.
[M0078 p3] The above results demonstrate the 1M MAN electrolyte alters the conversion mechanism of S cathodes and enables fast conversion kinetics.

[M0093 p4] Subsequently, we conducted in-situ Raman characterizations on the evolution of LPSs content in electrolytes during discharge.
[M0094 p4] The in-situ cell configuration is illustrated in Figure 4d, with the laser being focused on the separator to detect the signal of separator-adsorbed electrolyte.
[M0095 p4] In-situ Raman results (Figure 4e) of the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte show high-intensity LPSs signals because the LPSs intermediates are prone to dissolve and diffuse in the highly solvating ether electrolyte.
[M0096 p4] With the 1M MAN electrolyte, in-situ Raman tests (Figure 4f) also detect LPSs signals during discharge, but the intensity is much lower than that in the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte, indicating the dissolution of LPSs is significantly suppressed owing to the low solubility of MAN to LPSs.
[M0097 p4] Combining the in-situ Raman characterizations on cathodes and electrolyte, it demonstrates a hybrid conversion process in the 1M MAN electrolyte with S converting to both solvated and solid LPSs.
[M0098 p4] The new conversion mechanism enabled by the MAN-based moderately solvating electrolyte could effectively decrease the E/S ratio and the porosity of the cathode.
[M0099 p4] With the 1M MAN electrolyte, Li-S batteries exhibit analogous discharge profiles (Figure 5a) under E/S ratios of 10, 5, 3, and 2 μL mg1, delivering high discharge capacities of 1038, 1043, 1070, and 1094 mAh g1, respectively.
[M0100 p4] In contrast, batteries with DOL/DME electrolyte suffer from a sharp capacity decay under lean electrolyte conditions (E/S ratio <5 μL mg1) (Figure 5b).
[M0101 p4] Moreover, the 1M MAN electrolyte is also compatible with calendared S cathodes.
[M0102 p4] Li-S batteries with porous cathodes represent an extremely low volumetric energy density, making it imperative to reduce the cathode porosity (detailed calculation in Table S3, Supporting Information).
[M0103 p4] Li-S batteries show regular discharge profiles in the 1M MAN electrolyte (Figure 5c) and deliver discharge capacities of 1066, 1060, 1063, and 958 mAh g1, respectively, with cathode porosities of 80%, 72%, 63%, and 52%, in comparison to low discharge capacities in the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte when the cathode porosity decreases to 63% and 52% (Figure 5d).
[M0104 p4] The working mechanism of S cathodes in the 1M MAN electrolyte under conditions of low E/S ratio and low porosity are illustrated, respectively, in Figure 5e,f.
[M0105 p4] During sulfur conversion in ether electrolytes, the high-LPSs-content catholyte significantly decreases Li+ conductivity and deteriorates wettability.
[M0106 p4] In contrast, 1M MAN electrolyte —with its “high dielectric constant (ε) and low donor number (DN)” design—provides sufficient free Li+ while moderately inhibiting LPSs dissolution to avoid electrolyte deterioration.
[M0107 p4] Therefore, S cathodes in 1M MAN electrolyte perform a hybrid conversion process (Figure 5e) with solid S to solvated LPSs, as well as solid S to solid LPSs, substantially reducing electrolyte involvement during redox reactions, ensuring a complete conversion under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0108 p4] Similarly, dense S cathodes are also capable to work at locally lean electrolyte conditions benefited from the unique S conversion mechanism in the 1M MAN electrolyte as shown in Figure 5f.
[M0109 p4] The practical prospect of Li-S batteries with the MAN electrolyte is further evaluated.

[M0122 p5] Conclusion In this work, we developed a moderately solvating MAN electrolyte to address the key issues (high electrolyte dosage and high cathode porosity) limiting the overall energy density of Li-S batteries.
[M0123 p5] MAN possesses a high relative permittivity but a low donor number, combining fast Li+ conductivity and suppressed dissolution to LPSs.
[M0124 p5] The 1M MAN electrolyte provides a unique conversion mechanism distinguished from that in highly solvating and sparingly solvating electrolytes, as revealed by in-situ Raman characterizations, with S converting to both solvated and solid LPSs.
[M0125 p5] The hybrid solid/solvating mechanism brings about fast conversion kinetics and avoids high-LPSs-content catholyte, as formed in the traditional DOL/DME-based electrolytes, which kinetically impedes the conversion process under lean and locally lean electrolyte conditions.
[M0126 p5] The 1M MAN electrolyte successfully enables S cathodes to run at an ultralow E/S ratio of 2 μL mg1 and a low cathode porosity of 52%, significantly boosting both gravimetric and volumetric energy density of Li-S batteries.
[M0127 p5] This work provides a methodology for developing electrolytes for high-energy-density Li-S batteries. 4.
[M0128 p5] Experimental Section Preparation of electrodes and electrolyte: S and Ketjen black (KB, ECP-600JD, Lion) in the mass ratio of 4:1 were first ground for 1 h to obtain a well-mixed S/KB mixture, which was then heated at 155 °C in an Argon atmosphere.
[M0129 p5] Sulfur cathode slurry was prepared by mixing S@KB composite, Super P (DodoChem), and LA133 binder (Chengdu Indigo Power Sources) at mass ratios of 80:12:8 (for the cathodes with 64 wt% S content) or 85:7:8 (for the cathodes with 68 wt% S content) in deionized water using a planetary centrifugal mixer (AR-100, THINKY) at 2000 rpm.
[M0130 p5] The cumulative mixing duration exceeded 30 min.
[M0131 p5] The resulting slurry was coated onto an aluminum foil current collector at a wet thickness of 400 μm to achieve an active material loading of 4 mg cm2.
[M0132 p5] For low-porosity cathodes (52% porosity), a freeze-drying process was employed: the as-prepared electrodes were frozen at 40 °C for 2 h followed by vacuum treatment at 0.1 Pa for 2 h to facilitate ice crystal sublimation.
[M0133 p5] All electrodes were vacuum dried at 60 °C for 10 h prior to use.
[M0134 p5] Dense sulfur cathodes were fabricated through calendering, where freeze-dried electrodes were repeatedly compressed at 60 °C using a hot roller system, with iterative reduction of the roller gap until the desired compacted thickness was achieved.
[M0135 p5] The 1M ether electrolyte was prepared by adding lithium bis(trifluoromethane sulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, DoDoChem) into 1,3- dioxolane (DOL, 99%, DoDoChem) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.5%, DoDoChem).
[M0136 p5] The 1M methoxyacetonitrile (MAN) electrolyte was prepared by first adding LiTFSI into MAN (Adamas) and then magnetically stirring for 1 h to obtain a clear solution.
[M0137 p5] The Diglyme (G2, 99.9%, Adamas)-based sparingly solvating electrolyte was prepared by first mixing LiTFSI and Diglyme in a 0.8:1 molar ratio and magnetically stirring at 60 °C for 12 h, then mixing the obtained LiTFSI/solvent solution with TTE in a 1:2 volume ratio.
[M0138 p5] Solubility test of LPSs: Excessive Li2S and S powder with a molar ratio of 1:5 were added into the DOL/DME mixture and MAN solvent.
[M0139 p5] The solutions were stirred at 600 r/min and at 60 °C for 48 h to obtain supersaturated Li2S6/solvent solutions, which were then centrifuged to obtain the supernatants.
[M0140 p5] Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, sourced from China National Pharmaceutical Group, Ltd.) and lithium hydroxide (LiOH, obtained from Adamas Reagent, Ltd.) were added into the supernatants to oxidize S2 x to SO2 4 .
[M0141 p5] Subsequently, barium chloride (BaCl2, Adamas Reagent, Ltd.) was introduced to precipitate barium sulfate (BaSO4).
[M0142 p5] The precipitate was collected by filtration, washed, dried, and then weighed.
[M0143 p5] The sulfur content was calculated based on the measured mass.

[S0013 p0] To meet the pursuit of energy density > 300 Wh kg−1, the E/S ratio must be decreased below to < 5 L mg−1.

[S0020 p0] Besides, the 1M DOL/DME electrolyte demands high-porosity cathodes and thus leads to low volumetric energy density.
