[M0002 p1] LiNO3 is widely used as an additive in electrolytes to build a high-quality SEI, but its self-sacrificial nature limits the ability to mitigate the shuttle effect and stabilize Li anode during long-term cycling.
[M0003 p1] To counteract LiNO3 consumption during long-term cycling without using a high initial concentration, inspired by sustainedrelease drugs, we encapsulated LiNO3 in lithiated Nafion polymer and added an electrolyte co-solvent (1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethylene 2,2,2-trifluoromethyl ether) with poor LiNO3 solubility to construct highquality and durable F- and N-rich SEI.
[M0004 p1] Theoretical calculations, experiments, multiphysics simulations, and in-situ observations confirmed that the F- and N-rich SEI can modulate lithium deposition behavior and allow persistent repair of SEI during prolonged cycling.
[M0005 p1] Hence, the F- and N-rich SEI improves the Li anode cycling CE to 99.63% and alleviates the shuttle effect during long-term cycling.
[M0006 p1] The lithium anode with sustainable F- and N-rich SEI shows a stable Li plating/stripping over 2000 h at 1 mA cm2.
[M0007 p1] As expected, Li||S full cells with this SEI achieved a long lifespan of 250 cycles, far exceeding cells with a routine SEI.
[M0008 p1] The Li||S pouch cell based on F- and N-rich SEI also can achieve a high energy density of about 300 Wh kg1 at initial cycles.
[M0009 p1] This strategy provides a novel design for high-quality and durable SEIs in LSBs and may also be extendable to other alkali metal batteries.  2023 Science Press and Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

[M0012 p1] Introduction The growing demand for electric vehicles has driven the development of high energy-density secondary battery systems.
[M0013 p1] The lithium-sulfur battery (LSB) is a promising rechargeable battery owing to its high theoretical specific energy of 2570 Wh kg1 [1,2].
[M0014 p1] Nonetheless, the low Li cycling efficiency and shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) lead to quick failure of LSBs and preclude their commercialization [3,4].
[M0015 p1] These problems are closely linked to an unstable routine solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) [5,6].
[M0016 p1] Firstly, the shuttle effect originates from long-chain LiPSs dissolved in the electrolyte, which will pass through the routine SEI and react with the metallic Li anode [7,8].
[M0017 p1] Secondly, the routine SEI with low Li+ conductivity and poor electrical insulation leads to formation of Li dendrite, and thus lowers Li cycling coulombic efficiency (CE) [9,10].
[M0018 p1] Therefore, regulating the properties of SEI is essential for inhibiting the shuttle effect and improving Li cycling CE.
[M0019 p1] The properties of an SEI are determined by its components and structure [11,12].
[M0020 p1] Recently, extensive efforts have been devoted to constructing high-quality SEIs, mainly by tailoring the electrolyte composition or preparing artificial SEIs [13,14].
[M0021 p1] Among these, additives in the electrolyte such as benzoselenol [15], cerium nitrate [16], 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)thiophenol [17], and LiNO3 [18] have been proven to be a facile and effective strategy for optimizing the components and structure of SEI.
[M0022 p1] Today, LiNO3 is regarded as a superstar additive in the field of LSBs, because a small amount of NO3  can generate a dense SEI enriched with high Li+ conductivity LiNxOy and Li3N components [19–21].

[M0031 p2] A certain amount of LiNO3 can be dissolved in the standard ether-based electrolytes used in LSBs [24], but this additive is gradually depleted during the constant rupture and reconstruction of the SEI.
[M0032 p2] Once NO3  is exhausted, the electrochemical performance of LSBs deteriorates rapidly in terms of CE and discharge capacity.
[M0033 p2] The rate of LiNO3 depletion is related to its original amount in the electrolyte and the battery cycling capacity.
[M0034 p2] Under mild conditions (e.g., at a low S loading and a high electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio), LiNO3 can significantly enhance the lifespan and stability of LSBs [25].
[M0035 p2] However, a practical LSBs requires the use of a high S loading cathode (>6 mg cm2), lean electrolyte (<3 lL mgS 1), and a thin Li anode (<50 lm) [26].
[M0036 p2] Under such harsh conditions, even a high concentration of electrolyte failed to achieve satisfactory LSB cycling performance due to premature failure of Li anode [7].
[M0037 p2] Although a higher initial LiNO3 concentration in the electrolyte directly ensures the sustainability and compactness of SEI, a LiNO3 concentration above 0.5 M not only converts the ‘‘active S” in cathode into inactive sulphate but also increases the cost and viscosity of the electrolyte [27].
[M0038 p2] So far, there has been no report of a sustainable SEI that continuously mitigates the shuttle effect and enhances the Li cycling CE for practical LSBs.
[M0039 p2] Researchers recently identified various slow-release strategies for LiNO3 to achieve sustainable SEI in ester-based electrolytes (low LiNO3 solubility), including pre-impregnation of the separator, encapsulation in a polymer gel [28], intercalation between bilayers of the separator [29], and encapsulation in nano capsules [30].
[M0040 p2] However, these strategies are not applied to LSB electrolytes because of the high solubility of LiNO3 in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxy ethane (DME) [31].
[M0041 p2] In the drug field, reducing the drug release rate is a common means of improving the effectiveness of the drug [32,33].
[M0042 p2] Compared to conventional methods, drugs in sustained release systems can maintain effective concentrations for longer periods of time, prolong the duration of action, increase the effectiveness, and improve the utilisation of the drug [34,35].
[M0043 p2] Herein, inspired by sustained-release drugs strategies, we encapsulated LiNO3 in polymers and added ether-based cosolvent for solubility-mediated sustained release during LSB cycling.
[M0044 p2] The goal of this strategy is to construct a high-quality and durable SEI to mitigate the shuttle effect and improve the CE of Li plating/stripping.
[M0045 p2] Specifically, the LiNO3 nanoparticles were dispersed in lithiated Nafion (Li-Nafion) polymer and coated on a polypropylene melt-blown cloth.
[M0046 p2] This LiNO3 sustained-release film (LNO-SRF) swells in the electrolyte to achieve sustained release of LiNO3.
[M0047 p2] At the same time, a fluorinated ether with ultralow Li salts solubility (1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethylene 2,2,2-trifluoromethyl ether, TFTFE) was added to the electrolyte as a co-solvent to further slow down the release of LiNO3 and generate insulating LiF component in the SEI, inhibiting electron leakage.
[M0048 p2] During battery operation, a high-quality and durable SEI enriched with F and N was formed, and it increased the Li plating/stripping CE to 99.63% at a current of 1.0 mA cm2, a capacity of 1.0 mA h cm2.
[M0049 p2] In a Li||S full cell with a high S loading cathode, a low E/S ratio, and a limited excess Li anode, the shuttle effect of LiPSs was effectively suppressed during cycling and an outstanding cycling lifespan of 250 cycles was achieved. 2.
[M0050 p2] Fabrication of LNO-SRF Li-Nafion powder was prepared according to previous reports [36].
[M0051 p2] LiNO3 (10 wt%) and Li-Nafion (1 wt%) powder were fully dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) under magnetic stirring.
[M0052 p2] The mixed solution was blade-coated onto a polypropylene meltblown cloth and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 C.
[M0053 p2] The obtained LNO-SRF was punched into 15-mm round disks and transferred to a glovebox.
[M0054 p2] The mass of LNO-SRF is about 3 mg cm2, and the LiNO3 loading is about 1.2 mg cm2.
[M0055 p2] The LNO-SRF was placed between separator and Li electrode or Cu electrode. 2.2.

[M0065 p2] The as-prepared LNO-SRF was characterized using X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (Fig. 1b).
[M0066 p2] The crystalline peak of 2h  18 is related to the Li-Nafion [37], and other crystalline peaks are assigned to LiNO3.
[M0067 p2] In contrast to the smooth surface of uncoated cloth fibres, the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the LNO-SRF fibres revealed some surface sediments, proving that Li-Nafion/LiNO3 was evenly coated on the polypropylene fibres (Fig. 1c and d).
[M0068 p2] S1, the thickness of LNO-SRF is about 140 lm.
[M0069 p2] In addition, the contact angle between the LNO-SRF and electrolyte was only 25 (Fig. 1e).
[M0070 p2] The homogeneous dispersion of LiNO3 nanoparticles and the small contact angle facilitate LNO-SRF wetting by the electrolyte and continuous release of LiNO3 during battery operation.

[M0085 p3] Next, we simulated the ESP charge distributions of DME, DOL, TFSI, TFTFE, and NO3  using DFT calculations (Fig. 2b).
[M0086 p3] The negative charge is concentrated around NO3 , facilitating its coordination with the Li+ and participation in the solvation structure of Li+.
[M0087 p3] This was confirmed using 7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.
[M0088 p3] As shown in Fig. 2(c), a higher LiNO3 content in electrolyte led to a lower 7Li chemical shift due to a shielding effect of the solvation sheath on Li+.
[M0089 p3] This was induced by the extra NO3 , which interacted more strongly with Li+.
[M0090 p3] The NO3  will preferentially decompose on the surface of the Li anode accompanied by the desolvation process of Li+.
[M0091 p3] Therefore, the NO3  concentration at the Li anode surface determines the content of inorganic Li-N components in the SEI (Li3N and LiNxOy).
