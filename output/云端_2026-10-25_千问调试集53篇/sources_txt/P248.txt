[M0002 p1] However, the “shuttle effect” on the cathode and the unstable Li metal anode hinder their practical application.
[M0003 p1] During cycling, the “shuttle effect” leads to severe self-discharge and accelerates the capacity decay.
[M0004 p1] Moreover, the shuttled polysulfides aggravate the growth of dendrites and the loss of Li, causing a low Coulombic efficiency.
[M0005 p1] Considering electrolyte plays a crucial role in the polysulfides solvation behavior and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)-formation process, a well-designed electrolyte is required.
[M0006 p1] Here, we report a local high-concentration electrolyte for Li-S batteries.
[M0007 p1] This electrolyte is prepared by adding “diluent” 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl methyl ether (TME) to the conventional electrolyte.
[M0008 p1] The reduction of free solvent in the Li-ion solvation sheath suppresses the dissolution of the polysulfides, and more anion-derived SEI can be formed.
[M0009 p1] Our work simultaneously restrains the “shuttle effect” of the cathode and builds a stable SEI on the anode to protect the Li metal.
[M0010 p1] Without any ­LiNO3 additive, the average Coulombic efficiency of the Li anode can reach 98.87% after 120 cycles, and Li-S batteries with superior capacity retention of 60% after 400 cycles at 0.5 C can be achieved.
[M0011 p1] Keywords Local high-concentration electrolyte · Li metal anode · Coulombic efficiency · Shuttle effect · Li-S battery.
[M0012 p1] Introduction As technology develops, energy storage apparatus has become increasingly important.1 Among them, Li-S batteries are considered as favorable energy storage apparatus because of their superior theoretical specific energy and specific capacity.2 Also, S serves as the cathode material in Li-S batteries, which is abundant and environmentally friendly.3 Unfortunately, there are still some difficulties hindering the feasible utilization of Li-S batteries.4,5 In the cathode, the Li polysulfide (LiPS) will be formed during the cycles, and it will further migrate to the Li metal anode.
[M0013 p1] The LiPSs will be reduced on the Li metal anode, and subsequently migrate back to the cathode to be oxidized, leading to an overcharge phenomenon (also known as the “shuttle effect”).6 This phenomenon leads to low Coulombic efficiency (CE), severe self-discharge, inferior utilization of sulfur, and rapid capacity decay during the cycles.
[M0014 p1] Unstable Li metal anodes in Li-S batteries is another problem, although Li metal has been considered to be the ultimate alternate for the Li-battery anode due to the uppermost theoretical specific capacity and the lowermost electrode potential.7 The rapid formation of Li-dendrite and inevitable parasitic reactions with the electrolyte accelerate the failure of the battery.8 To overcome these problems, researchers have developed various methods.
[M0015 p1] For the cathode, many scientists have demonstrated that the chemisorption of polysulfides by hollow porous carbon,9 metal oxides,10,11 phosphides,12 carbides,13 or nitrides,14 or ­electrocatalysis15 of the reaction process is a valid strategy to suppress the “shuttle effect”.16 Nevertheless, from a thermodynamic point of view, these strategies can only Zhenpeng Hou and Peng-Fei Wang have contributed equally to this work. * Peng‐Fei Wang wangpengfei@neuq.edu.cn 1 Center of Energy Storage Materials & Technology, College of Engineering and Applied Sciences, National Laboratory of Solid State Microstructures, and Collaborative Innovation Center of Advanced Microstructures, Nanjing University, Nanjing 210093, People’s Republic of China 2 School of Materials Science and Engineering, Northeastern University, Shenyang 110819, People’s Republic of China 3 School of Resources and Materials, Northeastern University at Qinhuangdao, Qinhuangdao 066004, People’s Republic of China

[M0017 p2] Recently, electrolyte design is considered an effective strategy to deal with the “shuttle effect” and also achieve stable Li metal anodes.30 One promising approach is increasing the electrolyte concentration.
[M0018 p2] Under high concentration, the solubility of Li polysulfides can be evidently reduced, and the shuttle of polysulfides can be suppressed.
[M0019 p2] This is because, in highconcentration electrolytes, almost all the solvent molecules are coordinated with salt cations, and there is almost no free solvent in the solution, which reduces the dissolution of the polysulfides.
[M0020 p2] In addition, in high-concentration electrolyte, Li salts are more likely to react with Li metal, forming a tremendously dense and robust solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer rich in inorganic components, which successfully prevent the corrosion of the Li metal.
[M0021 p2] Nevertheless, high-concentration electrolytes suffer from the hindrances of high viscidity, relatively low ionic conductivity, and expensive Li salts, which limit their commercial applications.
[M0022 p2] To overcome these problems, a local high-concentration electrolyte (LHCE) has been developed.
[M0023 p2] By adding a “diluent” which has little or even no solubility for Li salts to the high-concentration electrolyte, 31 the original solvated structure can be retained.
[M0024 p2] At the same time, it can effectively reduce the total salt concentration of the entire electrolyte system.
[M0025 p2] Under this structure, low viscosity, high ionic conductivity, and economical electrolytes can be achieved.
[M0026 p2] In this study, we employ a novel “diluent”, 1, 1, 2, 2-tetrafluoroethyl methyl ether (TME), to design a LHCE system (LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME).
[M0027 p2] In this system, the reduction of free solvent in Li-ion solvation sheath effectively inhibits the dissolution of the polysulfides.
[M0028 p2] Meanwhile, more TFSIderived inorganic layers along with the organic outer layer arising from dimethoxyethane (DME) and dioxolane (DOL) solvent will build a robust SEI layer, which effectively improves the cyclic stability of the Li metal anode.
[M0029 p2] Our work simultaneously restrains the “shuttle effect” of the sulfur cathode and builds a robust SEI upon the anode to protect the Li metal.
[M0030 p2] Without using ­LiNO3, a superior average CE of Li-Cu batteries can be achieved, and the capacity retention of Li-S batteries were also evidently improved.
[M0031 p2] Results and Discussion The CE of Li‐Cu Batteries with Different Electrolytes The Coulombic efficiency of Li-Cu batteries with the LHCE (LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME) system was evaluated, and the molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL:TME was 1:1.5:1.5:6 for a total salt concentration equivalent to 1.8 M LiTFSI-DME/ DOL/TME.
[M0032 p2] For comparison, two other electrolytes were also tested: high-concentration electrolyte (LiTFSI-DME/ DOL, the molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL is 1:1.5:1.5 for a total salt concentration equivalent to 5.5 M LiTFSI-DME/ DOL) denoted as HCE, and a low-concentration electrolyte (1M LiTFSI-DME/DOL v: v = 1:1) denoted as LCE.
[M0033 p2] Li-Cu batteries were used to calculate the CE in the three systems (Fig. 1).
[M0034 p2] The battery using LCE showed a poor initial CE of only 76% (Fig. 1a), and the CE quickly dropped to less than 80% after 40 cycles.
[M0035 p2] The initial CE of Li was slightly improved to 82% when HCE was used (Fig. 1b).
[M0036 p2] The CE then further increased to 98.5% (Fig.
[M0037 p2] S1), while it began to decline after 60 cycles.
[M0038 p2] Consequently, increasing the concentration of the electrolyte can evidently enhance the reversibility of the Li metal, but still cannot build a stable Li anode for long cycling stability.
[M0039 p2] Remarkably, in the TME-based LHCE, the Li metal achieved a high initial CE of 93.16% (Fig. 1c), and the CE reached 99.37% after 100 cycles (Fig. 1d).
[M0040 p2] The corresponding average CE of 120 cycles was 98.87%, even higher than those of the common electrolytes for Li-S batteries with the ­LiNO3 additive.
[M0041 p2] After adding TME, the LHCE shows an improved CE compared with HCE.
[M0042 p2] The CE of the battery with LHCE remarkably increased compared with that of LCE.
[M0043 p2] On the one hand, the total salt concentration of LHCE (1.8 M) is higher than that of LCE (1 M), which could facilitate the formation of a more robust and stable SEI layer.
[M0044 p2] On the other hand, the local concentration of LHCE is 5.5 M, which is also much higher than that in LCE.
[M0045 p2] In addition, the CE of LHCE under limited Li was also evaluated.
[M0046 p2] It can be seen that the CE of Li in LHCE improved to 99.13% with only 4 times excess Li.
[M0047 p2] When the excess Li was further reduced to 0.5 times, the CE was still 98.35% (Fig.
[M0048 p2] S2), indicating that a robust SEI is formed on the Li metal anode.
[M0049 p2] The aggregate capacity loss of Li-Cu batteries was also calculated (Fig. 1e).
[M0050 p2] An amount of 4 mAh ­cm−2 of Li was deposited on copper foil as the anode and another copper foil as the cathode to assemble a Li-Cu battery.
[M0051 p2] Under treble of excess Li, the battery with LCE and HCE malfunctioned after 65 and 78 cycles, respectively.
[M0052 p2] Significantly, after optimizing by diluent TME, the Li-metal battery in the LHCE still worked for over 157 cycles.
[M0053 p2] Also, the overpotential of Li-Cu with LHCE was much lower thant in other electrolytes and kept stable upon the cycling process (Fig. 1f).
[M0054 p2] Solvation Structure Analysis of Electrolyte In conventional standard concentration electrolytes, free solvent molecules are sufficient, thus ­Li+ coordinates with the solvent to construct solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs).
[M0055 p2] In HCE and LHCE, most of the anions co-coordinate with
[M0056 p3] Hou et al. 1 3 ­Li+ to construct contact ion pairs (CIPs) and anion–cation aggregates (AGGs).
[M0057 p3] The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) moves from the solvent to the anion and the solvated structure moves from SSIPs in LCE to CIPs and AGGs in HCE and LHCE.32 ­Li+ coordination of SSIPs is relatively loose, and in the CIPs ­(TFSI- coordinated with one ­Li+) and AGGs ­(TFSI- coordinated with two or more ­Li+), the Li ions interact closely with the anions, thus there are extra anions in the ­Li+ solvation ­sheath33 (Fig. 2a).
[M0058 p3] Since the SEI is generated by competitive breakdown of the solvent and the anions, the LUMO energy level of ­Li+-ether complexes is superior to that of ­Li+-carbonate complexes.34 Accordingly, the anion decomposition in etherbased electrolytes is more pronounced.35 Subsequently, Raman characterization was carried out to investigate the ionic solvation behavior between the three systems (Fig. 2b).
[M0059 p3] The addition of TME significantly changed the coordination of the Li ions based on the DOL and DME electrolyte systems.
[M0060 p3] Raman spectra of different electrolytes were obtained.
[M0061 p3] From LCE to HCE, the characteristic peak of the S-N-S bond gradually shifted from 742 to 746 ­cm−1 (Fig. 2c), corresponding to the transformation of free ­TFSI- in the electrolyte to ­TFSI- coordinated with ­Li+.36 Remarkably, in the TME-based LHCE, the characteristic peak of the S-N-S bond shifted to 749 ­cm–1 (Fig. 2c).
[M0062 p3] This indicates that ­TFSI- increased in the ­Li+ solvation sheath of LHCE.
[M0063 p3] Usually, SSIPs in dilute solution occupy the dominant position of a ­Li+ solvation structure.
[M0064 p3] In highconcentration electrolytes, the structure of CIPs and AGGs gradually increases.
[M0065 p3] The addition of TME further increases the proportion of the AGGs structure.
[M0066 p3] There are obvious free solvent characteristic peaks in the DME and DOL solvents, and these peaks still exist in LCE with little offset.

[M0076 p4] Considering that the ionic solvation behavior is closely related to the SEI layer construction, the SEI layer in different electrolytes was studied in detail.
[M0077 p4] The SEI generated in HCE and LHCE was tested by the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 3).
[M0078 p4] In the HCE electrolyte, an obvious C-F peak can be seen at the binding energy of 689.1 eV,36 and a relatively weak LiF peak can be seen at 685 eV (Fig. 3a).36 Moreover, there is an obvious ­Li2CO3 peak at 292.6 eV (Fig. 3c).
[M0079 p4] The main composition of the SEI layer in HCE is a small amount of LiF, ­Li2CO3, and organic oligomer components originated from the sustaining reduction of DOL, such as C-O-C and ­CO2R (Fig. 3c).
[M0080 p4] As for LHCE, the content of LiF in the SEI is significantly higher than HCE (Fig. 3b), while ­Li2CO3 disappears (Fig. 3d).
[M0081 p4] The content of ­Li2CO3 is much smaller than that of LiF in LiTFSI-DOL/DME, and the ­Li2CO3 is mostly distributed in the outermost SEI.
[M0082 p4] Therefore, the content of LiF rises significantly and ­Li2CO3 disappears since the SEI in LHCE is dominated by ­TFSI- decomposition.
[M0083 p4] This indicates that the addition of TME increased the content of LiF and reduced the proportion of loose ­Li2CO3.
[M0084 p4] In this case, the anion ­(TFSI-) in LHCE first decomposes to form an SEI dominated by Li salt derivatives.
[M0085 p4] Outer oligomers (around 286.8 eV) coming from the reduction of DME and DOL were measured (Fig. 3d).
[M0086 p4] The outer oligomers lead to a more pliable SEI.36,38,39 The dense SEI layer composed of the LiF and the oligomer can impede the formation of Li dendrites, which has good adaptability to the volume expansion during ­Li+ deposition and stripping, thus elevating the cycling performance of Li-S batteries.

[M0098 p5] The Morphologies of Li Deposition The morphologies were tested by using Li-Cu batteries (Fig. 4 a-f).
[M0099 p5] The Li-deposition layer in LCE is relatively loose and unevenly distributed, and there are many small debris-like deposits among the Li deposition particles (Fig. 4a, b), which would destroy the cycling reversibility of the Li.
[M0100 p5] In HCE, the Li deposition morphology is relatively dense, but there are many pores and strip-shaped dendrite protrusions (Fig. 4c, d).
[M0101 p5] In contrast, the addition TME additives significantly changed the deposition morphology of the Li.
