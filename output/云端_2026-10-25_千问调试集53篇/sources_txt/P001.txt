[M0003 p1] However, the stable operation of the sulfur electrode, which signifi cantly differs from the intercalation mechanism in the traditional cathodes used in Li-ion batteries, still faces a series of barriers, including dissolution of long chain polysulfi des (Li 2 S m , m = 4–8), low utilization of sulfur, large volume expansion, and low Coulombic effi ciency (CE).
[M0004 p1] [ 2 ] Moreover, in the so-called shuttle mechanism, lithium polysulfi des formed during discharge and charge processes will migrate from the cathode to the anode side to react with the metallic Li, leading to serious self-discharge, poor CE, and limited cycle life for Li-S batteries.
[M0005 p1] [ 3 ] To address these critical issues, a variety of strategies have been developed to accommodate sulfur species, [ 4 ] mitigate the dissolution of polysulfi des, [ 5,6 ] and block the shuttle effect.
[M0006 p1] [ 7,8 ] With the signifi cant progress made in the development of composite sulfur cathode materials in Li-S batteries, [ 9 ] the stability of a Li metal anode in Li-S batteries has become one of the more urgent challenges in order to reach the desired long-term stability of Li-S batteries.
[M0007 p1] [ 10 ] A Li metal anode is the preferred anode in Li-S batteries not only because of its ultrahigh specifi c capacity, but also due to its low electrochemical potential (−3.040 V vs standard hydrogen electrode) as well as its light weight (molecular weight 6.94 g mol −1 , density 0.534 g cm −3 ) compared to other metals.
[M0008 p1] [ 11 ] However, the use of Li metal as an anode in nonaqueous electrolyte batteries is challenging because Li often forms dendritic and mossy Li metal deposits during repeated charge/discharge cycles, leading to low CE and a short cycle life, as well as safety concerns.
[M0009 p1] In Li-S batteries, a passivation layer is readily formed on the metallic Li anode surface due to the presence of polysulfi des and electrolyte additives.
[M0010 p1] This layer will lead to increased cell resistance and failure of the Li-S battery.
[M0011 p1] [ 10 ] Various strategies have been developed to minimize the corrosion of Li anodes and to reduce their increase in impedance under operating conditions, including the use of electrolyte additives, [ 12 ] co-solvent, [ 13 ] polymer electrolytes, [ 14 ] and interlayers.

[M0015 p1] Here the systematic investigation of the stability of the anode/electrolyte interface in Li-S batteries with concentrated electrolytes containing various lithium salts is reported.
[M0016 p1] It is found that Li-S batteries using LiTFSI-based electrolytes are more stable than those using LiFSI-based electrolytes.
[M0017 p1] The decreased stability is because the N–S bond in the FSI − anion is fairly weak and the scission of this bond leads to the formation of lithium sulfate (LiSO x ) in the presence of polysulfi de species.
[M0018 p1] In contrast, in the LiTFSI-based electrolyte, the lithium metal anode tends to react with polysulfi de to form lithium sulfi de (LiS x ), which is more reversible than LiSO x formed in the LiFSI-based electrolyte.
[M0019 p1] This fundamental difference in the bond strength of the salt anions in the presence of polysulfi de species leads to a large difference in the stability of the anode-electrolyte interface and performance of the Li-S batteries with electrolytes composed of these salts.
[M0020 p1] Therefore, anion selection is one of the key parameters in the search for new electrolytes for stable operation of Li-S batteries.

[M0031 p2] [ 5 ] It was reported that a new class of “solvent-in-salt” electrolytes, namely lithium bis(trifl uoromethanesulfonyl)imide [(LiN(SO 2 CF 3 ) 2 or LiTFSI] in 1,3-dioxalane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), with high salt concentrations (up to 7 M ) could enable stable cycling and high CE for Li-S batteries.
[M0032 p2] The high salt concentration electrolytes could minimize the side reactions between the Li metal and the polysulfi de species, effectively reduce the corrosion to the Li anode, and suppress Li dendrite formation.
[M0033 p2] Recently, a concentrated electrolyte of 5 M lithium bis(fl uorosulfonyl)imide [(LiN(SO 2 F) 2 or LiFSI] was also reported to form protective layers on both cathode and anode sides in Li-S batteries.
[M0034 p2] [ 15 ] In addition, the anions of lithium salts have been found to play very important roles in forming the solid electrolyte interphase layer and, to some extent, in governing the electrochemical performance.
[M0035 p2] [ 16 ] We report here on the anode/electrolyte interface stability and the degradation mechanism of Li metal anodes in Li-S batteries, especially with concentrated electrolytes.
[M0036 p2] Concentrated electrolytes tend to reduce the solubility of lithium polysulfi des due to the lack of available solvent molecules to partially solvate the Li + cations.
[M0037 p2] The effect of coordinated anions on the solubility of lithium polysulfi des is analyzed in these studies with NMR spectroscopy and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. 2.

[M0081 p4] In a Li-S battery using an electrolyte with LiTFSI salt, the dominant discharge products are lithium sulfi des (Li 2 S x ) whose formation is highly reversible.
[M0082 p4] Therefore, the cell exhibits a good cyclability as shown in the black line in Figure 1 a and also in Figure 1 c.
[M0083 p4] However, in a Li-S battery using an electrolyte with LiFSI salt, more reaction products formed on the surface of Li metal anode are lithium sulfate (LiSO x ) whose formation is not reversible.
[M0084 p4] In this case, the cell exhibits a poor cyclability as shown in the red line in Figure 1 a and also in Figure 1 d.
[M0085 p4] Because part of the reaction products (LiSO x ) formed during the discharge process cannot be decomposed during the charging process, the charge capacity of a Li-S battery is less than the discharge capacity when LiFSI salt is used in the electrolyte.
[M0086 p4] This behavior will lead to a CE of slightly larger than 100% as shown in the red line of Figure 1 b.
[M0087 p4] Generation of more irreversible products (LiSO x ) in LiFSI-based electrolyte also leads to thicker passivation layers as shown in Figure S2b in the Supporting Information.

[M0099 p5] [ 18 ] To gain a more in-depth understanding of the degradation mechanism of the Li anode in different electrolytes, DFT calculations were conducted to determine and compare the bond strength in the two anions (TFSI − and FSI − ).
[M0100 p5] The optimized structures for the two anions are shown in the Figure S4 and Figure S5 in the Supporting Information.
[M0101 p5] Tables S2 and S3 in the Supporting Information summarize the bond strength of TFSI − and FSI − , respectively.
[M0102 p5] For the TFSI − anion, the C S bond has the lowest bond strength (306.6 kJ mol −1 ), indicating that it could be broken fi rst and lead to the formation of the CF 3 fragments.
[M0103 p5] The next weakest bond in TFSI − is the C F bond in the CF 3 group (320.2 kJ mol −1 ), which suggests that the C F bond could be broken to form F.
[M0104 p5] The N S bond in the TFSIanion shows a bond strength of 581.0 kJ mol −1 .
[M0105 p5] In contrast, the N S bond in the FSI − anion is the weakest bond in the anion structure (192.7 kJ mol −1 ), indicating that the anion could decompose to form the fragments of F SO 2 N and SO 2 F.
[M0106 p5] The next weakest bond in the FSI − anion is the F S bond with a calculated strength of 465 kJ mol −1 .
[M0107 p5] Breaking of the F S bond in the fragment of SO 2 -F will form SO 2 and F.
[M0108 p5] In other words, the lower bond strengths of N S and F S in the FSI − anion favor the formation of sulfate SO 2 and F.
[M0109 p5] In contrast, it is much more diffi cult to form SO 2 when the TFSI − anion is present in the electrolyte.
[M0110 p5] These calculation results are consistent with the XPS results shown in Figure 2 where the presence of FSI − anion leads to the formation of more lithium sulfate (LiSO x ) as compared to the formation of LiS x when TFSI − anion is present in the electrolyte.

[M0143 p6] Conclusions The stability of anode/electrolyte interfaces in Li-S batteries with various concentrated electrolytes, 3 M LiTFSI in DOL-DME and 3 M LiFSI in DOL-DME, has been investigated and compared with the results found when using a lower concentration electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL-DME).
[M0144 p6] Concentrated electrolytes lead to much lower polysulfi de solubility compared with low concentration electrolytes.
[M0145 p6] Li-S batteries using a LiTFSI-based concentrated electrolyte could deliver more stable cycling than those using a LiFSI-based electrolyte.
[M0146 p6] Such behavior would be consistent with the determination that the N S bond in the FSI − anion is the weakest bond and which, if broken, could lead to the formation of lithium sulfate (LiSO x ) in the presence of polysulfi de species.
[M0147 p6] The continuous decomposition of the FSI − anions will lead to fast growth of passivation layers dominated by insulating lithium sulfate.
[M0148 p6] In contrast, the C S bond in the TFSI − anion is the weakest bond, but it is much stronger than the N S bond in the FSI − anion.
[M0149 p6] A broken C S bond in the TFSI − anion can in principle lead to the formation of lithium sulfi de (LiS x ) in the presence of polysulfi de species.
[M0150 p6] We have also found that the diffusion coeffi cient of FSI − anions in 3 M LiFSI-DME/DOL is clearly larger than values measured for Li ( D FSI > D Li ), but TFSI − anions in 3 M LiTFSI-DME/DOL exhibit similar diffusion coeffi cients to those of Li + ( D TFSI ≈ D Li ).
[M0151 p6] The higher diffusion coeffi cient of FSI − anions, corresponding to a weaker coordination by other solvent molecules, may accelerate their decomposition on the surfaces of Li metal anodes.
[M0152 p6] The fundamental differences in the bond strengths within the FSI − anions and the TFSI − anions in the presence of polysulfi de species lead to a large difference in the stability of anode-electrolyte interfaces and performance of the Li-S batteries.
[M0153 p6] Therefore, both anion selection and their concentration need to be considered in the search for new electrolytes for further development of Li-S batteries. 4.

[M0158 p6] High-purity Li chips with dimensions of 15.6 mm diameter and 0.45 mm thickness were obtained from MTI Corporation.
[M0159 p6] Preparation of Sulfur Cathode : The sulfur/MWCNT composite was fi rst prepared by a melt-diffusion approach.
[M0160 p6] Sulfur powder and MWCNTs in a weight ratio of 4:1 were mixed in a mortar by hand, and then the mixture was transferred to a Tefl on-lined stainless steel autoclave and heat treated at 155 °C in a furnace for 24 h to improve the sulfur distribution inside the MWCNTs matrix, resulting in S80/MWCNTs20 composite.

[M0169 p7] Preparation of Electrolytes : The electrolytes were prepared by dissolving the desired amount of lithium salt into the solvent mixture in volumetric fl asks in the argon-fi lled glovebox.
[M0170 p7] For example, the 3 M LiTFSI/DOL-DME electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.015 mol of LiTFSI in an appropriate amount of DOL-DME (1:1 by volume) mixture to fi ll a 5 mL volumetric fl ask.
[M0171 p7] Electrochemical Measurements : The electrochemical properties of various electrolytes were evaluated in 2032-type coin cells (MTI Corporation).
[M0172 p7] Li-S coin cells were assembled inside the MBraun glovebox with the above sulfur cathode disc, Celgard 3501 separator (a polypropylene microporous membrane with hydrophilic coatings), Li chip, and selected electrolyte of 60 μL.
[M0173 p7] Then the cells were cycled on a Lanhe battery testing system (Wuhan Land Electronics Co., Ltd.) by using galvanostatic charge–discharge tests at 0.2 C rate where 1 C corresponded to a current density of 1.0 mA cm −2 .
[M0174 p7] The electrochemical impedance spectrum of the coin cells was measured on the combination of a Solartron 1287 electrochemical workstation and Solartron 1255B frequency response analyzer controlled by Zplot software with a 10 mV perturbation in the frequency range of 10 6 to 10 −1 Hz.
[M0175 p7] Characterizations : The electrodes were harvested from the cycled Li-S coin cells and carefully washed with DME solvent.
[M0176 p7] After drying under vacuum, the electrodes were sealed in an argon-fi lled container and transferred for postanalyses.
[M0177 p7] SEM images were collected on an FEI Quanta FESEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.
[M0178 p7] XPS measurements were performed on a Physical Electronics Quantera Scanning X-ray Microprobe using a focused monochromatic Al Kα X-ray (1486.7 eV) source for excitation and a spherical section analyzer. 17 O NMR Measurements and Diffusion Measurements : All NMR experiments were performed with a 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Agilent, USA) equipped with a 5 mm liquid NMR probe (Doty Scientifi c, USA), which has the maximum gradient strength of ≈31 T m −1 .
[M0179 p7] The 17 O NMR spectra for these electrolytes were obtained from Fourier transformed free induction decays after a single pulse excitation at Larmor frequencies of 81.4 MHz at ambient temperature (≈25 °C).
[M0180 p7] The 17 O chemical shift for distilled water (H 2 O) was used as an external reference (i.e., 0 ppm).
[M0181 p7] Diffusion coeffi cient of Li + cation, anion, and solvent molecules (DOL and DME) were measured by using 7 Li, 19 F, and 1 H pulsed fi eld gradient NMR at Larmor frequencies of 233.2, 564.3, and 599.82 MHz, respectively, in the temperature range of 0–50 °C.
[M0182 p7] The echo heights, S( g ), obtained as a function of gradient strength ( g ) using bipolar gradients with convection compensate gradients (Dbppste_cc in VNMRJ 3.2) were fi tted with the Stejskal–Tanner equation, S( g ) = S(0) exp [- D ( γgδ) 2 (Δ- δ /3)].
[M0183 p7] In this equation, S( g ) and S(0) are the echo height with the gradient strength of g and 0, respectively; D is the diffusion coeffi cient; and γ is the gyromagnetic ratio for the observed nuclei ( 7 Li, 19 F, or 1 H).
[M0184 p7] The strength ( g ) and duration ( δ ) of gradient pulse and the distance between the two gradients (Δ) were properly chosen for a suffi cient decay of the echo height based upon the nuclei observed and diffusion coeffi cient.
[M0185 p7] Computational Calculations : DFT calculations were performed using the generalized gradient approximation as implemented in the Gaussian 09 suite of programs.
[M0186 p7] [ 20 ] The B3LYP functional combined with the 6–311++G(d,p) basis set was used in geometry optimization calculations.
[M0187 p7] [ 21 ] Vibrational frequencies were computed for yielding zero-point energy and thermal corrections.
