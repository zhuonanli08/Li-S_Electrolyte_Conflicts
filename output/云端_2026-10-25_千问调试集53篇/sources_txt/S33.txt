[M0001 p1] RESEARCH ARTICLE www.advenergymat.de Moderately Solvating Electrolyte with Fluorinated Cosolvents for Lean-Electrolyte Li–S Batteries Ilju Kim, Sejin Kim, Hannah Cho, Jinkwan Jung, Hyeokjin Kwon, Dongwoo Kim, Yewon Shin, and Hee-Tak Kim* To surpass the energy density limit of current Li–S batteries, attaining a long lifespan under lean-electrolyte conditions is imperative.
[M0002 p1] The persistent challenge involves suppressing electrolyte decomposition while facilitating sulfur electrode reaction.
[M0003 p1] In this study, the solvating power of 1dimethoxy ethane is fine-tuned, the main solvent, using fluorinated ether cosolvents via H–F interactions.
[M0004 p1] As the fluorination degree of the cosolvent increases, the coordination of anions around the Li-ion increases, and the solubilities of Li polysulfides decrease.
[M0005 p1] By systematically varying the solvating power, moderately solvating electrolytes are prepared that can effectively suppress the dissolution of Li polysulfides without hindering the redox kinetics.
[M0006 p1] The moderately solvating electrolytes induce uniform Li deposition and reduce electrolyte decomposition owing to the formation of anion-derived solid electrolyte interphase.
[M0007 p1] An assembled pouch-type Li–S battery containing an electrolyte with an optimized solvation power delivers 405 Wh kg−1 at an E/S ratio of 2.0 μL mgs −1 with a lifespan of over 80 cycles.
[M0008 p1] This study suggests a strategy to finely tune the Li+ solvation structure for achieving well-balanced performances of sulfur cathodes and Li-metal anodes under lean-electrolyte conditions. 1.

[M0013 p1] Gelation of the electrolyte with highly concentrated LiPSs at the cathode hinders the redox reaction.[5] Consequently, recent studies have reported that in the lean electrolyte condition, electrodes with low tortuosity are effective in facilitating the transport of Li+ ions and electrons.[5b,c] Furthermore, the highly viscous electrolyte impedes the formation of a uniform Li+ flux, causing nonuniform Li growth and accelerating the formation of dead Li.[6] Moreover, Li corrosion caused by highly concentrated LiPSs reduces cycling stability.[7] Recent studies have highlighted the depletion of the electrolyte as a dominant factor that leads to the failure of lean-electrolyte Li– S batteries.[8] These studies have demonstrated that reinjecting the electrolyte can restore stable operation, highlighting the importance of electrolyte management in Li–S batteries.[9] Various strategies have been reported for suppressing electrolyte decomposition at the LMA.
[M0014 p1] Undesirable reactions between LiPSs and the LMA have been suppressed by incorporating a solvent with large steric hindrance and weak solvating power in the second solvation shell.[10] Li salts with high-donor-number anions such as SCN−[11] and NO3 −[12] that can coordinate around Li+ were introduced in the electrolyte to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).
[M0015 p1] Despite some advances, current electrolyte technologies do not sufficiently address the issues associated with leanelectrolyte Li–S batteries.
[M0016 p1] The Li+ solvation structure in an electrolyte influences the ion-conduction property and interfacial reactions such as desolvation,[13] electrolyte decomposition,[14] and SEI formation.[15] As demonstrated in several studies on Li-metal batteries (LMBs), the modulation of the Li+ solvation structure via solvent or salt design significantly improves the reversibility of the LMA.[16] A promising electrolyte for a reversible LMA is a weakly solvating Adv.

[M0019 p2] Considering the detrimental effect of electrolyte decomposition at the LMAs on the lifespans of lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries, the recent renewal of interest in Li+ solvation chemistry has motivated electrolyte engineering for Li–S batteries.[18] A recent study indicated that in Li–S batteries operating under lean-electrolyte conditions, optimal solvation properties need to be achieved using solvent combinations such as hexyl methyl ether[19] and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2-2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether[20] in varying volume ratios.
[M0020 p2] However, the development of practical Li– S batteries still faces significant challenges due to various degradation mechanisms.
[M0021 p2] Consequently, the direction for electrolyte development remains uncertain.
[M0022 p2] To address this, it is necessary to explore solvents with different chemical structures and conduct systemic investigations into electrolyte designs that consider the formation of LiPSs and their impact on both sulfur cathodes and LMAs.
[M0023 p2] Furthermore, it is crucial to examine how these electrolytes can enhance cycle performance by taking into account various factors such as LiPS formation, SEI structure, Li morphology, and electrolyte depletion.
[M0024 p2] Herein, we present a systematic investigation of fluorinated ether (FE) cosolvents that provide a moderately-solvating state and prolong the lifespans of lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.
[M0025 p2] We reexamined the root cause of cell failure using a conventional electrolyte containing a mixture of dimethoxy ethane (DME) and 1,3- dioxolane (DOL) under lean-electrolyte conditions to determine the key factor affecting the lifespans of these batteries.
[M0026 p2] Based on the analyses, we substituted conventionally used DOL with various FE cosolvents that have different fluorination degrees to prepare electrolytes containing a mixture of DME and FE (DME-FE electrolytes).
[M0027 p2] Subsequently, we investigated the relationship between the solvating power and performance of an Li–S battery under lean-electrolyte conditions.
[M0028 p2] Our analysis reveals that on decreasing the solvation abilities of DME-FE electrolytes, the Li+ solvation structure transforms from strongly solvating to weakly solvating, which accompanies the promotion of anion coordination in the Li+ solvate and the formation of an anion-derived SEI.
[M0029 p2] Furthermore, we suggest that the specific hydrogen–fluorine (H–F) interaction between DME and FE is responsible for the variations in the Li+ solvation structure.
[M0030 p2] The density and uniformity of the LMA morphology increased during cycles with the decrease in solvating power, thereby enhancing Li reversibility.
[M0031 p2] However, the reduction in solvating power adversely affected the liquid-phase reduction reaction of the LiPSs.
[M0032 p2] Therefore, moderately solvating electrolytes yielded optimal performance in the lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.
[M0033 p2] Upon comparing the residual electrolytes after operation with strongly and moderately solvating electrolytes, we identified electrolyte depletion as the root cause of cell failure in lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries, not only in conventional electrolytes but also in other types of electrolytes.
[M0034 p2] This issue was effectively mitigated by the altered SEI structure and Li morphology resulting from the changes in the solvation structure.
[M0035 p2] Notably, a lean-electrolyte Li–S battery (0.5-Ah class) with a gravimetric energy density of 405 Wh kg−1 at a challenging E/S ratio = 2 demonstrated stable operation over 80 cycles.
[M0036 p2] This confirms the potential application of this electrolyte design in practical Li–S batteries. 2.

[M0038 p2] Failure Analysis of a Lean-Electrolyte Li–S Battery The root cause of the degradation of lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries was re-examined at an E/S ratio of 5 using 1 m lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 0.2 m LiNO3, and DME/DOL (1:1 v/v), which is considered to be a baseline electrolyte for Li–S batteries.[2] As shown in Figure 1a, operating the cell at a 0.15 C discharge/charge rate significantly declines the discharge capacity after 40 cycles.
[M0039 p2] At the 46th cycle, an abnormal-shaped, fluctuating second voltage plateau appeared, indicating cell failure (Figure 1b).
[M0040 p2] We conducted a postmortem analysis on the LMA and electrolyte after the 46th discharge.
[M0041 p2] Li is continuously and irreversibly formed in LMBs during operation owing to the disconnected Li particles and Li corrosion by the electrolyte at the LMA surface.
[M0042 p2] In Li–S batteries, Li corrosion can be highly significant because of the corrosive nature of LiPSs.[6] To quantify the amount of reversible Li remaining after cycle failure, an Li-SUS half-cell was assembled with the LMA extracted from the Li–S battery, and Li was stripped for the LMA in the half-cell (Figure S1, Supporting Information).
[M0043 p2] The amount of reversible Li was found to be 6.3 mAh cm−2, which is 21% less than the theoretical capacity of the pristine 40-μm-thick LMA (8 mAh cm−2) (Figure 1c,d).
[M0044 p2] The reversible capacity remained larger than the discharge capacity of the Li–S battery (3.04 mAh cm−2), confirming that cell capacity is not limited by the remaining capacity of the LMA, consistent with the results reported in previous studies.[9] However, 21% loss in the LMA indicates significant LMA degradation over 46 cycles only, as supported by the thick, porous layer observed in the cross-sectional images of the cycled LMA (Figure 1e).
[M0045 p2] The NMR spectra of the baseline electrolyte before the operation and after cell failure were compared (Figure 1f; S2, Supporting Information).
[M0046 p2] The results revealed that 33% of DME, 49% of DOL, and 2% of TFSI−anions decomposed during the 46 cycles.
[M0047 p2] This demonstrates the significant decomposition of the solvents, rather than the salt anions, in the Li–S battery.
[M0048 p2] The remaining amount of electrolyte (1.76 μL mAh−1), estimated from quantitative NMR (QNMR) analysis, was slightly more than that required to fully wet the S cathode and separator (1.57 μL mAh−1) (Note S1 and Table S1, Supporting Information).
[M0049 p2] However, a certain amount of liquid electrolyte must be consumed for wetting the porous Li layer; therefore, the remaining amount of electrolyte was insufficient for stably operating the Li–S battery.
[M0050 p2] To confirm whether electrolyte decomposition is responsible for capacity fade, we reassembled a cell with fresh electrolyte and the electrodes of the failed cells and operated this cell.
[M0051 p2] The cell was rejuvenated owing to electrolyte exchange (Figure 1g), which confirms that solvent depletion is responsible for cell degradation.
[M0052 p2] The electrolyte decomposition can be increased by roughening the LMA and trapping the electrolyte components in the porous layer.

[M0060 p4] Solvation Structure of the DME-FE Electrolytes Adjusting the interaction between the solvent and cosolvent is an effective method for altering the solvation structures of electrolytes (Figure 2a).
[M0061 p4] The main solvent with high solvating power predominantly coordinates with Li+, increasing the solubilities of LiPSs and aggravating Li corrosion.
[M0062 p4] When a cosolvent extracts electrons from the main solvent via solvent-cosolvent interactions, the solvating power of the main solvent reduces.[15a,21] Subsequently, anion coordination in the Li+ solvate increases, decreasing the solubilities of the LiPSs and increasing the stability of the LMA.
[M0063 p4] However, electrolytes with extremely low LiPS solubilities may hinder redox reactions at the cathode.[5b,7a] Therefore, electrolytes with moderate solvating power may impart long lifespans to lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.
[M0064 p4] To explore moderately solvating electrolytes applicable to Li–S batteries, we designed DME-FE electrolytes at a molar ratio of 4.9:1.0 (DME: FE) with dual salts composed of LiFSI and LiNO3.
[M0065 p4] LiNO3 is crucial for the stable operation of Li–S batteries, while LiFSI facilitates the formation of a stable SEI layer on lithium metal due to the easy cleavage of S─F bonds, leading to the formation of a LiF-based SEI layer.
[M0066 p4] The FE cosolvents employed in this study are ethyl 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (ETFE), bis(2,2,2- trifluoroethyl) ether (BTFE), 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2- trifluoroethyl ether (TFTFE), and 1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (TFOFE), the structures of which are displayed in Figure 2b.
[M0067 p4] The numbers of F atoms in the molecules follow the order ETFE (4) < BTFE (6) < TFTFE (7) < TFOFE (12).
[M0068 p4] Recent studies have revealed that F atoms in FE cosolvents can form pseudo-H bonds with the C–H moieties in DME (H–F interaction).[15a,21] Therefore, the DME-FE interaction may vary depending on the structure of the FE.
[M0069 p4] To confirm the existence of such interactions in the electrolytes designed herein, we employed NMR analysis via 19F–1H Heteronuclear Overhauser Enhancement Spectroscopy (HOESY) and identified that all the F atoms in the FE cosolvents were close to the ─CH2 (H1) and ─OCH3 (H2) groups in DME (Figure S3, Supporting Information).
[M0070 p4] This suggests that the FE cosolvents can influence the solvating power of DME.
[M0071 p4] Comparing the 19F NMR spectra of the FEs with and without DME revealed a downfield shift corresponding to the F groups on adding DME to all the FEs (Figure S4, Supporting Information), suggesting the existence of H–F interactions.
[M0072 p4] Furthermore, the H in DME (H1 and H2) caused an upfield shift in the NMR spectra in the presence of the FE cosolvents (Figure S5, Supporting Information).[15a] The upfield shifts increased in the following order: DME-ETFE < DME-BTFE < DME-TFTFE < DME-TFOFE (Figure 2c), indicating that the increase in DME–FE interactions followed the same order as that of the increasing numbers of F atoms.
[M0073 p4] The DME-FE interactions suggest that the Li+ solvating power can vary depending on the type of FE cosolvent.
[M0074 p4] Using the mixed DME-FE solvents, LiFSI- and LiNO3-containing electrolytes were prepared with a molar ratio of LiFSI:LiNO3:DME:FE = 1.9:1.0:18.5:3.8.
[M0075 p4] A control electrolyte (0.75 m LiFSI and 0.4 m LiNO3 in DME), referred to as the DME electrolyte, was prepared for comparison.
[M0076 p4] The electrolytes containing the TFOFE, TFTFE, BTFE, and ETFE cosolvents are hereafter referred to as the DME- TFOFE, DME-TFTFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-ETFE electrolytes, respectively.
[M0077 p4] We conducted 7Li NMR, Raman spectroscopy, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to understand the solvation structures of the DME-FE electrolytes.
[M0078 p4] The 7Li NMR analysis of the electrolytes revealed that the upfield shifts corresponding to Li+ increased in the following order: DME < DME-ETFE < DME-BTFE < DME-TFTFE < DME-TFOFE (Figure S6, Supporting Information), indicating that the Li+ solvating powers decreased in the same order.
[M0079 p4] As shown in Figure 2d, the Li+ solvating power, represented by the Li+ chemical shift, strongly correlates with the degree of H–F interactions indicated by the chemical shifts corresponding to the H in DME (H1 and H2).
[M0080 p4] Our Raman analysis for the DME-FE electrolytes indicated the enhanced FSI−coordination in the presence of the FE cosolvents.
[M0081 p4] The stretching of the S─N─S bond in the FSI−ions at ≈750 cm−1 was deconvoluted to correspond to solvent-separated ion-pair structures (SSIP) (720 cm−1), contact-ion pair structures (CIP) (734 cm−1), and aggregates (751 cm−1) (Figure S7, Supporting Information).[22] The addition of the FE cosolvents yielded a blue shift irrespective of the type of FE, indicating that a stronger signal was obtained from the CIP and aggregates than from the DME electrolyte (Figure S8, Supporting Information).
[M0082 p4] The deconvolution of the FSI−peaks revealed that the peak ratio corresponding to the CIP to the total peak increased with decreasing Li+ solvating power (Figure 2f).
[M0083 p4] Ionic conductivities, which are sensitive to CIP and aggregate formation, of the DME-FE electrolytes were investigated.
[M0084 p4] The ionic conductivities decreased in the following order: DME > DME-ETFE≈DME-BTFE > DME- TFTFE > DME-TFOFE (Figure S9, Supporting Information).
[M0085 p4] The results align with the increasing tendency of CIP and/or aggregate formation.
[M0086 p4] We measured the Li+ diffusion coefficients of the electrolytes via 7Li diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY)-NMR; the Li+ diffusion coefficients were positively correlated with solvating power (Figure S10, Supporting Information), indicating an increased dissociation of the Li salt.
[M0087 p4] As the solvating power decreases, the increased formation of CIP and aggregates likely leads to a reduction in the concentrations of free Li+ and FSI− ions, subsequently decreasing the ionic conductivity and diffusion coefficients of Li+.
[M0088 p4] These analyses collectively demonstrate the increase in anion coordination in the Li+ solvate with the decreasing solvation power of the DME-FE solvents.
[M0089 p4] MD simulations were conducted to determine the Li+ solvation structures in the electrolytes (Figure S11, Supporting Information).
[M0090 p4] The average coordination numbers of the electrolyte components (DME, FSI−, and NO3 −) indicate that, as the solvating power of the DME-FE electrolytes decreased, the coordination of the main solvent (DME) and anions (FSI−and NO3 −) decreased and increased, respectively (Figure 2f; Figure S12, Supporting Information).
[M0091 p4] All the FE cosolvents were sparingly involved in the primary solvation shell due to their sparingly solvating characteristic.[23] The increased anion coordination with decreasing solvating power can be attributed to the H–F interactions with DME; with increasing H–F interactions, the Lewis basicity of DME decreased, thereby increasing anion involvement in the Li+ solvate.
[M0092 p4] Recent studies have demonstrated that solvent–solvent interactions can influence the solvating power of electrolytes.[24] For instance, Liang et al. reported that the solvating power of a solvent with strong solvating power can be decreased by polarization induced by HFE solvents.[24a] Similarly, Wu et al. found that the solvating power varies with the number of fluorine atoms of cosolvents, aligning with our findings.[24b] In Adv.

[M0105 p6] Sulfur Redox Reaction in the DME-FE Electrolytes The FE cosolvents can alter the redox kinetics and shuttle dynamics of the LiPSs.
[M0106 p6] To investigate the stabilities of the LiPSs in the DME-FE electrolytes, stoichiometric amounts of S8 and Li2S were added to the electrolytes to form 0.2 m Li2S4.
[M0107 p6] The differences in the reddish hue among the electrolytes typically demonstrate the impact of the FE cosolvent on the state of the LiPSs.[7a] As shown in Figure 3a, the color of the electrolyte changes from dark red to yellow with the decrease in Li+ solvating power.
[M0108 p6] The yellow powders precipitated in the DME-TFOFE electrolyte highlight that the formation of LiPSs was significantly hindered owing to weak solvating power.
[M0109 p6] We investigated the Li+ solvation structure in the presence of LiPSs in the DME-FE electrolytes using 7Li NMR analysis (Figure 3b; Figure S15, Supporting Information).
[M0110 p6] For this, S8 and Li2S were added to the electrolytes in the amounts corresponding to the formation of 0.2 m Li2S4, and the supernatants were analyzed via NMR spectroscopy.
[M0111 p6] The DME electrolyte showed a de-shielding shift of 0.09 ppm corresponding to the inclusion of LiPSs, whereas the DME-ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes exhibited relatively small shifts of ≈0.02 ppm.
[M0112 p6] The de-shielding shift can be attributed to the PS anions in the Li+ solvate replacing FSI−or DME.
[M0113 p6] Such behavior is typically observed on adding high-donor-number anions.[11,12] Therefore, the relatively small shifts corresponding to the DME- ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes were indicative of lower PS solubility compared to that in the DME electrolyte.
[M0114 p6] The chemical shift corresponding to the DME-TFOFE electrolyte remained nearly unchanged (0.0079 ppm), verifying the suppression of LiPS formation.
[M0115 p6] Additionally, the maximum solubility of LiPS was determined using UV–vis spectroscopy.
[M0116 p6] After preparing electrolytes with 0.5 m Li2S4, the supernatants were diluted to measure their absorbance, which was then compared to that of known Li2S4 concentrations.
[M0117 p6] The results indicated that the maximum Li2S4 solubility was found to follow the order: DME > DME-ETFE > DME-BTFE > DME-TFTFE > DME-TFOFE which is consistent with our NMR data (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
[M0118 p6] Raman spectroscopy conducted on the DME-TFOFE and DME electrolytes further confirms that the sparingly solvating FE cosolvents can alter the stabilities of LiPSs.
[M0119 p6] The DME-TFOFE electrolyte contained a higher population of PS anions with low charge densities (S7 −, S4 −, and S3 −) and a lower population of PS anions with high charge densities (S4 2−and S3 2−) compared to the DME electrolyte (Figure S17, Supporting Information).[25] This indicates that the solvating power of the electrolyte not only affects the solubility of LiPSs but also influences the species.[7a,25] Previous reports have demonstrated that when Li+ exists as a strong acid due to its solvation shell within the electrolyte, strong bases such as dianions (e.g., S4 2−) are stabilized.
[M0120 p6] Conversely, when Li+ behaves as a weak acid, weaker bases, such as monoanions (e.g., S3 −), are stabilized.
[M0121 p6] In our study, the increased anion coordination around Li+ in the DME-TFOFE electrolyte is expected to reduce the acidity of Li+, thereby facilitating the stabilization of weaker bases such as monoanions (S4 −, S7 −).
[M0122 p6] The contrasts clearly revealed that the DME-TFOFE electrolyte cannot stabilize the PS species with high charge densities owing to low solvating power.
[M0123 p6] Based on the aforementioned results, we categorized the DME electrolyte as strongly solvating; the DME-ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes as moderately solvating; and the DME-TFOFE electrolyte as weakly solvating in terms of LiPS solubility and species.
[M0124 p6] To investigate the influence of solvating power on the sulfur cathode performance using DME-FE electrolytes, we analyzed the discharge curves obtained from the first cycle (0.15 C) after four formation cycles (Figure 3c).
[M0125 p6] The DME-ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes displayed slightly higher overpotentials than and similar discharge capacities as the DME electrolyte.
[M0126 p6] This can be explained by the similar ionic conductivities of the electrolytes when Li2S4 is present.
[M0127 p6] In the DME electrolyte, where Li2S4 has the highest solubility, the ionic conductivity decreases more significantly, likely due to increased viscosity.
[M0128 p6] As a result, the ionic conductivities of the DME, DME-ETFE, DME- BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes are comparable when Li2S4 is present (Figure S18, Supporting Information).
[M0129 p6] The DME-TFOFE electrolyte exhibited a 20% smaller discharge capacity and a larger discharge overvoltage by 0.8 V than the DME electrolyte owing to the low LiPS solvating power of the former electrolyte.
[M0130 p6] The gradual capacity decrease from the start of the second voltage plateau provides information on LiPS solubility, which is associated with the conversion from sulfur to short-chain PSs.[26] The capacity of the before second plateau region (166 mAh gs −1) of the DME-TFOFE electrolyte was significantly lower than those of the other electrolytes (≈240 mAh gs −1), indicating the low LiPS solubility of the former.
[M0131 p6] Comparing the UV–vis spectra of the DME-FE electrolytes obtained from the discharged Li–S cells (down to 2.1 V) revealed decreases in S4 2− absorbance (at ≈400 nm) in the following order: DME > DME- ETFE > DME-BTFE > DME-TFTFE > DME-TFOFE (Figure 3d).

[M0165 p8] Reversibility of the LMA and Li–S Full Cells Containing the DME-FE Electrolytes We attempted to enhance the reversibility of the LMA and the cycling stabilities of the lean-electrolyte Li–S cells containing the DME-FE electrolytes.
[M0166 p8] The reversibility of the LMA was assessed by quantifying the Coulombic efficiency of an Li/Ni asymmetric cell based on the Aurbach method (Figure 4a).
[M0167 p8] The Coulombic efficiencies of the DME-TFOFE, DME-TFTFE, DME-BTFE, DME-ETFE, and DME electrolytes were measured to be 97.18, 96.81, 96.19, 95.31, and 95.42%, respectively.
[M0168 p8] As the degree of fluorination in the FE increased, the compatibility with Li metal also improved, as evidenced by comparing the Coulombic efficiencies of the Li/Ni asymmetric cell operated at 0.2 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 0.2 mAh cm−2 using 15 μL of each electrolyte.
[M0169 p8] The average Coulombic efficiency over 100 cycles was confirmed to follow the order DME-TFOFE > DME-TFTFE > DME-BTFE > DME-ETFE > DME (Figure S26, Supporting Information).
[M0170 p8] This indicates that augmenting anion coordination to Li+ improves the stability of the LMA.
[M0171 p8] This finding is in accordance with those reported in previous studies on improved Li compatibility using a combination of an LiFSI salt and a weakly solvating solvent.[15] To investigate the correlation between LMA stability and lifespan of the Li–S cell under lean-electrolyte conditions, cycling stability tests were conducted on the electrolytes at an E/S ratio of 5 and a 0.15 C rate.
[M0172 p8] The cycle numbers of the DME-TFTFE, DME- BTFE, DME, DME-ETFE, and DME-TFOFE electrolytes at a capacity retention of 70% were 154, 131, 36, 32, and 31 cycles, respectively (Figure 4b).
[M0173 p8] Despite having the highest LMA reversibility, the DME-TFOFE electrolyte exhibited poor cycling stability, indicating that the reversibility of the sulfur cathode determines the cycling stability when the solvating power of electrolyte is weak.
[M0174 p8] Except in the case of the DME-TFOFE electrolyte, cell lifespan is strongly correlated with LMA reversibility, confirming that LMA reversibility is a decisive factor for achieving high cycling stability in a lean-electrolyte Li–S battery.
[M0175 p8] To achieve optimal fullcell cycling stability, sulfur redox kinetics and LMA reversibility must be balanced, with moderately solvating electrolytes being particularly suitable for achieving this.
[M0176 p8] The cycle performance (154 cycles) of the DME-TFTFE electrolyte was better than those reported in previous studies (Figure 4c), highlighting the efficacy of moderately solvating electrolyte design.
[M0177 p8] Another moderately solvating electrolyte, the DME-BTFE electrolyte, exhibited a notably high cycle performance (131 cycles).
[M0178 p8] Additionally, in the DME electrolyte, a slight increase in discharge capacity was observed before degradation set in, likely due to LiPS dissolvoing out of the carbon structure and allowing reactions to occur on the outer carbon surface.
[M0179 p8] In contrast, in moderately solvating electrolytes, reactions were likely limited to the inner carbon surface, preventing such capacity increases (Figure S27, Supporting Information).

[M0184 p9] Chemical Structures of the SEIs in the DME-FE Electrolytes The modulation of the Li+ solvation structure can change the composition of the SEI because the population and reactivity of the electrolyte components at the LMA surface vary depending on the solvation structure.[31] By calculating the LUMO levels of each component within the solvation structure, we can predict the decomposed products on the Li metal surface.
[M0185 p9] A higher LUMO level makes electron acceptance more difficult, suppressing reduction, while a lower LUMO level makes electron acceptance easier, promoting reduction.
[M0186 p9] The comparison between the LUMO levels of the anions and solvents determined via density functional theory calculations (Figure 5a) reveals that these LUMO levels decreased with the increase in coordination with Li+.
[M0187 p9] This behavior originates from the electron transfer to Li+, increasing the ease with which salt or solvent molecules are reduced.[32] Based on this consideration, in the DME electrolyte, the preferential participation of DME in the Li+ solvate will promote the cathodic decomposition of DME and enrich the organic components in the SEI.
[M0188 p9] In contrast, in the moderately solvating electrolytes, the involvement of the anions in the Li+ solvate may facilitate the cathodic decomposition of the anions, forming an inorganic-rich SEI.
[M0189 p9] To verify these postulations, the chemical compositions of the SEIs were analyzed via XPS after 36 cycles of operation of the Li–S batteries containing the DME, DME-ETFE, DME- BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes.
[M0190 p9] The addition of the FE cosolvents to the DME electrolyte notably reduced the carbon content by over 25% within the SEI, and the DME-TFTFE electrolyte exhibited the lowest carbon content (Figure 5b).
[M0191 p9] This indicates substantial suppression of DME decomposition, as anticipated.
[M0192 p9] The DME-FE electrolytes exhibited higher Li, N, and F contents than the DME electrolyte, indicating higher contents of inorganic SEI components in the former electrolytes.
[M0193 p9] The abnormally high F content in the SEI in the DME-ETFE electrolyte can be explained by the low cathodic stability of ETFE owing to the localized electron distribution in this asymmetric structure.[33] As the NO3 −anion coordinated to Li+ is particularly susceptible to decomposition owing to the lowest LUMO level, we examined the N 1s spectra of the SEIs in the DME, DME-ETFE, DME- BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes (Figure 5c–f, respectively).
[M0194 p9] The spectra exhibited R–NO2 (404.6 eV) and Li3N (398.2 eV) peaks, which are formed by the partial and full reduction of NO3 −, respectively.[12] The relative intensity corresponding to Li3N was higher and that corresponding to R–NO2 and lower for the moderately solvating electrolytes than those for the DME electrolyte, indicating that the cathodic reaction of NO3 −to Li3N was promoted by the preferred involvement of NO3 −present in the Li+ solvate.
[M0195 p9] Moreover, the S 2p spectra of the SEIs (Figure S28, Supporting Information) displayed that the intensities corresponding to the LiPSs and Li2S in the DME-FE electrolytes were significantly lower than those in the DME electrolyte, evidencing the suppressed Li corrosion owing to the LiPSs in the moderately solvating electrolytes.
[M0196 p9] The signals corresponding to SO4 2−(169.2 eV) and SO3 2−(167.2 eV),[1b] indicative of the cathodic decomposition of FSI−, were prominent in the spectra obtained from the DME- ETFE, DME-BTFE, and DME-TFTFE electrolytes due to the high degrees of FSI−involvement in the Li+ solvate.
[M0197 p9] Overall, the compositional analysis validated the formation of inorganic-enriched SEIs and the mitigation of Li corrosion owing to the moderately solvating electrolytes.

[M0252 p12] Conclusion In this study, FE solvents with different degrees of fluorination were used as cosolvents in Li–S battery electrolytes.
[M0253 p12] By tuning the interaction between DME and FE, we varied the Li+ solvate structures, LiPS solubilities, and chemical compositions of the SEIs in lean-electrolyte Li–S batteries.
[M0254 p12] As the fluorination degree of the FE cosolvent increased, the H–F interactions with DME decreased, shifting the environment from strongly solvating to weakly solvating.
[M0255 p12] Among the cosolvents tested, the DME-TFTFE electrolyte, a moderately solvating electrolyte, exhibited appropriate LiPS solubility and an anion-derived SEI structure, resulting in a lifespan of over 150 cycles at an E/S ratio of 5.
[M0256 p12] Notably, the DME-TFTFE electrolyte effectively mitigated electrolyte depletion, the root cause of cell failure, thereby enabling the stable operation of a pouch-type stack cell with a high cell-level energy density of 405 Wh kg−1 and stable cycling for 80 cycles at a Adv.

[M0270 p13] For chemical shift analysis using 7Li NMR, a coaxial NMR tube (Wilmad- Labglass) with an inner tube OD of 3.3 mm and ID of 2.34 mm, as well as an outer tube OD of 4.97 mm, was used to exclude the effects of DMSOd6, which was placed in an external coaxial insert.
[M0271 p13] For conducting QNMR analysis, the components of the coin cells were washed with 1.5 mL of tetrahydrofuran to form an internal concentration reference for 1H NMR, and subsequently, 20 μL of fluorobenzene was added as an internal concentration reference for 19F NMR.[34] The same amount of electrolyte for an E/S ratio of 5 (29.4 μL of electrolyte) was diluted to 1.5 mL of tetrahydrofuran, and subsequently, 20 μL of fluorobenzene was added to measure the component amount before operation.
[M0272 p13] Raman analysis was conducted using a dispersive Raman spectrometer (ARAMIS, JY Horiba) with a 514-nm wavelength laser.
[M0273 p13] The ionic conductivities of the electrolytes were measured using a conductivity meter (LAQUA twin, Horiba Scientific).
[M0274 p13] The diffusivities of Li+ in the electrolytes were examined via 7Li DOSY-NMR analysis.
[M0275 p13] Electrochemical Measurements: First, 2032-type coin cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box (H2O < 0.1 ppm) for electrochemical analysis.
[M0276 p13] Next, a galvanostatic discharge and charge test was conducted using a WBC300L (Wonatech) automatic battery cycler.
[M0277 p13] For the Li–Ni half-cell test using the Aurbach method, Φ12-mm Li foil (40 μm, China Lithium), Φ16-mm Ni foil (Welcos), and a Celgard 2400 separator were used.
[M0278 p13] The amount of injected electrolyte was 30 μL.
[M0279 p13] First, 1 mAh cm−2 of Li deposit was formed on the Ni foil at 1 mA cm−2, and subsequently, ten cycles of repetitive strip and plate were conducted at the same current density and a 0.2 mAh cm−2 capacity.[35] In the last cycle, the cell was charged up to 1 V to strip the remaining Li.
[M0280 p13] In the Li–S cell, a high S-loaded electrode (3.8 mgs cm−2) obtained from LG Energy Solution Ltd. was used, a Φ14-mm sulfur cathode was used as the working cathode, and Φ16-mm Li metal (40 μm, China Lithium) was used as the counter electrode.
[M0281 p13] Furthermore, 29.4 μL of electrolyte was injected to adjust the E/S ratio to 5 μL mgs−1.
[M0282 p13] The cells were precycled for two cycles at 0.05 C, and subsequent cycles used 0.1 C.
[M0283 p13] After precycling, these cells were cycled at 0.15 C.
[M0284 p13] Operando XRD was conducted using an in-situ battery cell X-ray diffractometer, R-AXIS IV (Rigaku) with a transmission Cu Ka X-ray source.
[M0285 p13] In-situ XRD patterns were obtained while the cells were operated at 0.1 C.
[M0286 p13] All the cells were operated in the voltage range of 1.8–2.7 V (vs Li/Li+).
[M0287 p13] Computer Simulation: All computer simulations were conducted in Materials Studio (Biovia, 2023).
[M0288 p13] The HOMO and LUMO were calculated using the DMOL3 modules after geometric optimization.[31] For MD simulations, all molecules were geometrically optimized using the generalized gradient approximation method, Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and double numerical plus polarization basis set before constructing amorphous cells.[29] The amorphous cells were composed of 385 DME and 113 fluorinated solvent molecules, as well as 69 Li+, 38 FSI−, and 31 NO3− ions.
[M0289 p13] COMPASS III force field was assigned to all molecules.
[M0290 p13] The constructed amorphous cells were equilibrated at 1 atm and 298 K under the NPT condition for 1 ns and then under the NVT condition for 1 ps.
[M0291 p13] Coordination behaviors of all the Li+ ions in the amorphous cells were examined.
[M0292 p13] Post-Mortem Analysis: The morphologies of the sulfur cathode and Li anode were analyzed using field-emission SEM (Magellan 400, FEI company).
[M0293 p13] The cross-section of the Li metal was observed using a focused ion beam SEM (Helios Nanolab 450 ETFE, FEI Company).
[M0294 p13] The components of the SEI were examined using XPS (K-alpha, Thermo VG Scientific).
[M0295 p13] All post-mortem analyses were conducted after 36 cycles of operation including pre-cycles of the Li–S cell, and subsequently, the components to be analyzed were washed with 3 mL of DME to remove residual electrolytes.
[M0296 p13] Assembly of the Li–S Pouch Cell: The double-side-coated sulfur cathode on Al foil with 2.5 mgs cm−2 provided by LG Energy Solution Ltd. was used for pouch-cell assembly.
[M0297 p13] To assemble the bi-cell-type pouch cell, 40- μm-thick Li foil was cut into 4 × 6 cm2 pieces and subsequently attached to a Cu current collector of the same size.
[M0298 p13] The cathode was cut into 3 × 5 cm2 pieces, and a polypropylene separator (Celgard 2400) was cut to dimensions of 3.5 × 5.5 cm2.
[M0299 p13] Then, 336 μL of electrolyte was injected to adjust the E/S ratio to 3.5.
[M0300 p13] The cells were compressed using a pressure range of 1.5–2.0 bar.
[M0301 p13] A 0.5-Ah stack cell was assembled in a dry room (dew point < −40 °C).
[M0302 p13] The cathode and anode were cut to dimensions of 5 × 3.3 cm2 and 5.2 × 3.5 cm2, respectively.
[M0303 p13] An Ni tab was directly attached to the LMA.
[M0304 p13] The volume of electrolyte corresponding to an E/S ratio of 2 was 0.825 mL.
[M0305 p13] The energy densities of the pouch cells were calculated based on the total mass of the stack cell (Table S2, Supporting Information).
[M0306 p13] The pouch cells with an E/S ratio of 3.5 were precycled at 0.025 C for two cycles and 0.05 C for two additional cycles and subsequently cycled at 0.1 C.
[M0307 p13] The pouch cells with an E/S ratio of 2 were cycled at 0.0125 C for two cycles, 0.025 C for one cycle, and subsequently cycled at 0.05 C.
