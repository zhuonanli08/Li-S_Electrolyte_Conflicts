[M0002 p1] Soc. 2025, 147, 8652−8662 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Unclear regulation mechanisms of solid solubility make it difficult to accurately control its concentration, posing challenges for accelerating chemical reactions.
[M0003 p1] Herein, we use a model system to determine the effects of solvent type and ratio, concentration, and type of lithium salt on sulfur solubility.
[M0004 p1] Our findings reveal a nonlinear relationship between sulfur solubility and both lithium salt concentration and the ratio of different solvents in composite solutions.
[M0005 p1] Importantly, significant differences in sulfur solubility are discovered with changes in the solvation structure regulated by the type of salt anion.
[M0006 p1] Quantitative evaluation of nonlinear sulfur solubility is illustrated to be breaking the threshold value of the interaction energy between the solute and solvent molecules.
[M0007 p1] Based on these findings, simultaneous regulation of sulfur and lithium polysulfide concentrations in ether electrolytes can be achieved by controlling the concentration and type of salts.
[M0008 p1] As a result, a liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway is supported for lithium−sulfur batteries with a finite amount of electrolyte in the coin cell, and the pouch cell shows an energy density of 554 Wh kg−1, ranking at the top level of the reported system. ■INTRODUCTION Regulating the solubility of solid solutes has important applications in many fields such as substance separation1−3 and reaction process regulation.4−6 It plays a crucial role in improving separation process efficiency and reducing costs,7,8 as well as accurately controlling chemical reaction rates.9,10 In 1878, Van’t Hoff studied the relationship between solid solute solubility and temperature and proposed the Van’t Hoff equation.
[M0009 p1] These results indicate that temperature can regulate solute solubility by changing the intra- and intermolecular forces between solvents and/or solutes.
[M0010 p1] Compared to the influence of external factors such as temperature and pressure, controlling the internal chemical composition of the solution can provide a more direct way to achieve significant changes in solute solubility by regulating the strength of the interaction forces between the solute and solution components.
[M0011 p1] For example, Guarr and Hickey et al. showed that the introduction of C−H···π weak dispersion force interactions in pyridiniumbased compounds can weaken intermolecular electrostatic interactions, thus enhancing their solubility in acetonitrile and maximizing the energy density of redox flow battery.11 Therefore, it is very important to precisely control the solid solute concentration to provide an important value for the accurate control of the reaction rate.
[M0012 p1] However, the influence of solute, solvent, and ion interaction strengths on solute solubility in solution is still needed for a deep understanding.
[M0013 p1] According to classical theories, the dissolution of solid substances is a competitive process between entropy and enthalpy changes.12 During the dissolution process, breaking the interaction between solvents or solutes is an endothermic process, while constructing the interaction bonds between solutes and solvents is an exothermic process, where the enthalpy change is less than zero.
[M0014 p1] Therefore, reducing the strength of intermolecular forces between solutes or enhancing the strength of solvent and solute interactions in a solution is key to increasing solute solubility.
[M0015 p1] Based on this understanding, strategies, including weakening solute interactions and enhancing solvent and solute interactions, have been proposed to regulate solute solubility in solutions.
[M0016 p1] For example, Yagai et al. demonstrated that the methylation of barbiturate π- conjugated compounds can reduce the intermolecular hydrogen-bonded type of barbiturate solutes and inhibit the formation of polycrystals, thereby improving their solubility in nonpolar solvents.13 Rimer et al. found that the adsorption of citrate inhibitors on the surface of calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) crystals weakened the interaction of COM solute due to changes in the local lattice strain, thereby increasing the COM concentration in solutions.14 In order to enhance the interaction between solvents and solutes, Received: December 16, 2024 Revised: February 14, 2025 Accepted: February 18, 2025 Published: March 3, 2025 Article pubs.acs.org/JACS © 2025 American Chemical Society 8652 https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.4c17438 J.

[M0035 p3] Importantly, by comparing the two salts, LiTFSI and LiBr, it is discovered that the solvation structure regulated by the two types of anions has a totally different effect on S8 solubility.

[M0039 p3] On this basis, a liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway at the positive electrode of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries is supported (Figure 1c), significantly improving the reaction rate and cell performance.

[M0042 p3] The solubility of solid S8 in ether solvents, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), was primarily studied at room temperature because ethers are commonly used as electrolyte solvents in Li−S batteries.
[M0043 p3] Raman spectra analysis shows that the solubility of S8 (characteristic peaks: 150, 220, and 456 cm−1)16 is 5.1 mg mL−1 in DOL and 3.65 mg mL−1 in DME (Figure S1).
[M0044 p3] With the addition of LiTFSI, the solubilities of S8 in DOL solutions containing 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 M LiTFSI are 4.3, 4.0, 3.7, and 1.0 mg mL−1, respectively (Figures 1d and S2).
[M0045 p3] It was found that S8 solubility decreased slightly with increasing LiTFSI concentration in the range of 0.2−0.8 M.
[M0046 p3] However, the solubility of S8 decreases sharply when the LiTFSI concentration changes from 0.8 to 1.0 M.
[M0047 p3] These results indicate that there is a nonlinear solubility phenomenon (Figure 1e).
[M0048 p3] We also analyzed S8 solubility in DME solvent with 1.0, 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), and a nonlinear solubility phenomenon was also found (Figure S3).
[M0049 p3] Moreover, in order to further verify the solubility effect of temperature dependence, we tested S8 solubility at 50 °C, which still shows a nonlinear solubility phenomenon, proving its universality (Figure S4).
[M0050 p3] To illustrate the mechanism of the nonlinear S8 solubility phenomenon in complex solutions with solvents and salts, we used DOL and CS2 as strongly and weakly polarized solvents, respectively, to explore the effect of the mixed solvent ratio on S8 solubility, in which CS2 and DOL serve as good and poor solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively.
[M0051 p3] This shows that S8 solubilities in DOL and CS2 solvents are about ∼5.1 and ∼60.0 mg mL−1 at room temperature, respectively.
[M0052 p3] When the molar ratios of CS2 and DOL change to 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, 4:6, 3:7, and 2:8, S8 solubilities are 59, 57, 55, 51, 20, 14, and 12 mg mL−1, respectively.
[M0053 p3] This demonstrates that S8 solubility slightly changes with a change in the ratio of CS2:DOL from 1:0 to 5:5.
[M0054 p3] However, when the ratio of CS2:DOL changes to 4:6, S8 solubility decreases sharply (20 mg mL−1).
[M0055 p3] When the ratio further decreases, the solubility changes slightly (Figure 1f).
[M0056 p3] Furthermore, the measured dielectric constant of the DOL/ CS2 solution shows a nearly linear trend with regard to their ratio, again verifying no relationship between them (Figure 1f).
[M0057 p3] To check the nonlinear S8 solubility in composite solvents, we used classical molecular dynamics LAMMPS to simulate S8 solubility in DOL/CS2 solvents with different molar ratios.
[M0058 p3] This shows that S8 solubility in a pure CS2 solvent and mixed CS2/DOL solvent (8:2) is close to 50 mg mL−1.
[M0059 p3] When the ratio is 5:5, S8 is still in the soluble state (50 mg mL−1).
[M0060 p3] In contrast, when mixed solvents with a ratio of 2:8 or pure DOL are selected, a large number of S8 molecules precipitate, which is consistent with the experimental results (Figures 1g and S5).
[M0061 p3] It is worth emphasizing that this nonlinear solubility phenomenon is also found in other systems, for example, the nonlinear solubility of rosin in mixed solvents of water and ethanol (Figures 1h, S6 and S7).
[M0062 p3] This confirms the broad existence of such phenomena.
[M0063 p3] To reveal the essence of nonlinear solubility, we analyzed the interaction energy and interaction force between the solvent and solute molecules.
[M0064 p3] As known, when a solid transforms into a molten liquid, external input interaction energy is needed to reduce the intermolecular forces of the solid.
[M0065 p3] For example, the interaction energy for the melting heat of S8 from solid to liquid is 1.7175 kJ mol−1.
[M0066 p3] Similarly, the dissolution of the S8 solute in the solvent also requires the input of interaction energy to reduce the interaction forces of S8 molecules in the solid.
[M0067 p3] When S8 solute dissolves in solvents, the required interaction energy comes from the van der Waals forces formed between the solvent and S8 molecules.
[M0068 p3] For S8 dissolution in CS2-DOL solvents, CS2 and DOL act as good and poor solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively.
[M0069 p3] S8 solubility will quickly decrease when the interaction force of the mixed solvents and S8 is weaker than the interaction force of the two S8 molecules.
[M0070 p3] We use the classical molecular dynamics simulation method (LAMMPS) to calculate the interaction energy.
[M0071 p3] According to the constructed model, we obtain the average interaction energy of the extra input to dissolve one S8 molecule (Edis or Edis′, and the calculation details of Edis and Edis′ are shown in Supporting Note 2).
[M0072 p3] The average energy released from the interaction of one CS2 molecule and one S8 molecule is Ea, and the energy released from one DOL molecule and one S8 molecule is Eb (Eb < Ea).
[M0073 p3] When the average action energy of the solvent on the solute is greater than the average energy required for the solute to dissociate a molecule, the solubility of the solute will be high.
[M0074 p3] Therefore, if xEa + yEb < Edis (Edis′) is satisfied, S8 will precipitate.
[M0075 p3] Herein, x and y are the coordinated numbers of the CS2 and DOL molecules on the S8 molecule.
[M0076 p3] According to the calculation results (details in Supporting Note 2), Ea is 3.28 × 10−2 kcal mol−1, Eb is 1.1 × 10−2 kcal mol−1, and Edis is 1.2198 kcal mol−1 (Edis′ = 1.48 kcal mol−1).
[M0077 p3] Moreover, the relationship between the average energy of one solute molecule and the molar ratio of the two solvents can be obtained by the equation = + y 2.329 x 1.548 1, and the solubility mutation of S8 in the mixed system of CS2 and DOL will be between the ratio of CS2 to DOL of 3:7 and 4.54:5.45.
[M0078 p3] In the experiment, the proportion of solubility mutation measured in the CS2 and DOL mixed system is 4:6, which is just within the proportion range of our theoretical deduction.
[M0079 p3] This proves the reliability of the calculation theory for this system.

[M0082 p4] S8 solubility in CS2 is evaluated to be ∼60 mg mL−1 at room temperature.
[M0083 p4] This means that one S8 molecule dissolves into 71 CS2 molecules.
[M0084 p4] That is, the average solvation structure of CS2 and S8 can be hypothesized to be a 71-bound structure (71 CS2−S8).
[M0085 p4] With CS2 as the pure solvent, the interaction energy released from 71 CS2 molecules and one S8 molecule is 71*Ea (2.329 kcal mol−1).
[M0086 p4] Because 71*Ea > Edis (2.329 > 1.2198) and 71*Ea > Edis′ (2.329 > 1.48) are present, the solubility of S8 in the CS2 solvent is high.
[M0087 p4] When the ratio of CS2 to DOL is 5:5, the interaction energy released from 35.5 CS2 and 35.5 DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb (1.555 kcal mol−1).
[M0088 p4] Because 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis (1.555 > 1.2198) and 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis′ (1.555 > 1.48) are still present, S8 solubility in the CS2/DOL (5:5) solvents is also high (about 51 mgS mL−1), which is a little lower than that in pure CS2.
[M0089 p4] In stark contrast, when the ratio of CS2 to DOL is 4:6, the interaction energy released from 28.4 CS2 and 42.6 DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 1.40 kcal mol−1.
[M0090 p4] Because this value is lower than Edis′ (1.40 < 1.48), there is a significant difference in S8 solubility (about 20 mg mL−1), which is much lower than that in CS2 (Figure 1f,g).
[M0091 p4] The above estimation of input interaction energy for solute dissolution in a solvent can explain the nonlinear solubility phenomenon well, confirming that the enthalpy change is the core factor affecting S8 solubility in this system (Figures 1i and S8).
[M0092 p4] The interaction energy for the fast change in solute solubility, which is due to breaking intramolecular forces of the solute through the intermolecular action between solvent and solute, relates to the threshold value of the interaction energy.

[M0148 p6] In stark contrast, due to the improved S8 solubility in the electrolyte with an increased amount of unpolarized ether (0.8 M LiTFSI), the liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway with an enhanced long-range electron transfer route is constructed.
[M0149 p6] Accelerated Reaction Probed by In Situ Raman Spectra.
[M0150 p6] To confirm the liquid−liquid−solid reaction pathway initiated by the increased S8 solubility, we used in situ Raman spectroscopy to probe the cathode reaction process of Li−S batteries.
[M0151 p6] At a discharge current density of 1C, the S8 peaks located at 220 and 476 cm−1 appear at the open-circuit voltage (OCV).
[M0152 p6] With 1 M LiTFSI in the electrolyte, S8 peaks can still be detected at a discharge voltage of 2.40 V.
[M0153 p6] In addition, the weak Li2Sx peaks (398 and 450 cm−1)18 can be detected at 2.35 V, and Li2Sx can still be detected at a discharge voltage of 1.86 V (Figure 3a).
[M0154 p6] This suggests that the reduction reaction rate is low and S is not completely reduced.
[M0155 p6] In contrast, the S8 peak cannot be detected at 2.40 V with 0.8 M LiTFSI in the electrolyte, and Li2Sx peaks can be detected at 2.40 V, much earlier and stronger than those with 1.0 M LiTFSI.
[M0156 p6] Moreover, Li2Sx peaks completely disappear at 1.91 V, indicating complete S reduction due to a greatly increased reaction rate (Figure 3c).
[M0157 p6] Compared to the discharge capacity of the battery with 1.0 M LiTFSI (746 mAh g−1), the higher value with 0.8 M LiTFSI (928 mAh g−1) also indicates a faster reaction rate (Figure 3b,d).
[M0158 p6] Similar to the discharge process, with 1 M LiTFSI, the Li2Sx peaks can be detected at a potential of 2.38 V during the charging process, relating to the high charging potential (Figure 3e).
[M0159 p6] No S8 substance was observed at the end of charging, indicating a low reaction rate.
[M0160 p6] However, with 0.8 M LiTFSI, a strong Li2Sx peak can be detected at a charging potential of 2.35 V, 30 mV earlier than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure 3g).
[M0161 p6] Moreover, the S8 peaks appear at 2.80 V.
[M0162 p6] This indicates a higher reaction rate of 0.8 M LiTFSI than that of 1 M LiTFSI, consistent with their different charging voltages (Figure 3f,h).
[M0163 p6] To further clarify the effect of increased S8 solubility on the reaction pathway, we performed morphological analysis to illustrate the nucleation and growth of Li2S by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
[M0164 p6] With 1.0 M LiTFSI, a thin layer of Li2S film deposits appears on the electrode surface at the initial growth stage (Figure S19a).
[M0165 p6] With the increase of discharge depth, the film-like Li2S gradually covers the electrode surface (Figure S19b).
[M0166 p6] This indicates that Li2S tends to grow in a two-dimensional mode in the presence of 1 M LiTFSI.
[M0167 p6] In contrast, with 0.8 M LiTFSI, at the initial Li2S deposition stage, it shows a three-dimensional growth of island-shaped Li2S (Figure S19c).
[M0168 p6] With the increase of discharge depth, these deposits turn into spherical structures, and most of the electrode surface remains clean (Figure S19d).

[M0198 p8] As shown in Figure 4d−f, ten S8 molecules dissolve in DOL but precipitate in DOL with 1 M LiTFSI.

[M0200 p8] Moreover, ten S8 molecules are dissolved in DOL with 0.8 M LiTFSI in the presence of LiBr, consistent with the experimental results (Figure S21).

[M0203 p8] The charge density of the original two oxygen atoms is significantly changed, and the corresponding valence charges change from −0.2359 and −0.2488 to −0.1940 and −0.3092, respectively.
[M0204 p8] In contrast, the charge distributions of DOL in the solvated structure formed by LiBr and DOL are similar to those of free DOL (Figure 4i).

[M0206 p8] This means that DOL molecules are strongly polarized by LiTFSI, but DOL is weakly polarized by LiBr (Figure 4j).

[M0225 p9] We further continued to test soluble Li2Sx concentrations at different time scales.
[M0226 p9] With an increased reaction time, the Li2Sx amount in the presence of 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is also higher than that with 1.0 M LiTFSI, suggesting the promotion effect of LiBr on Li2Sx formation (Figure 4k).

[M0228 p9] To evaluate the effect of LiBr on the cathode reaction rate, we performed a CV test with 0.8 M LiTFSI electrolyte in the presence and absence of 0.2 M LiBr.
[M0229 p9] At a scan rate of 0.05 mV s−1 and an E/S of 5 μL mgs −1, the CV curves are similar in the presence and absence of LiBr.
[M0230 p9] This reveals that the amount of Li2Sx generated in the two electrolytes is high at a low current rate to ensure the high extent of S ↔Li2S interconversion reactions (Figure S24a).
[M0231 p9] At a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1, the first Pc1 peak positively shifts, and the peak current density increases in the presence of LiBr compared to that without LiBr.
[M0232 p9] The difference in the second Pc2 reduction peak is more obvious.
[M0233 p9] In the presence of LiBr, the peak potential shows a positive shift of about 80 mV and the peak current increases from 3.12 to 4.86 mA (Figure S24b).
[M0234 p9] Tafel slopes and activation energy calculations further confirm the increase of the reaction rate (Figure S25a,b).
[M0235 p9] At a CV scan rate of 0.15 mV s−1, the introduction of LiBr accelerates the total reaction pathway.
[M0236 p9] The battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI shows only one reduction peak, but the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr shows two distinct reduction peaks (Figure 4l).
[M0237 p9] This proves that LiBr can increase the total reaction rate.
[M0238 p9] The above results demonstrate that an increase of S8 solubility effectively increases the reaction rate, and LiBr with an associated ion pair can stabilize Li2Sx to further accelerate the reaction without sacrificing S8 dissolution in the ether electrolyte.
[M0239 p9] Based on this finding, we assembled a Li−S battery.
[M0240 p9] At an S loading of 5.0 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 10 μL mgs −1, we evaluated the rate performance (Figure 5a).
[M0241 p9] The battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr show discharge capacities of 1328.8, 1030.4, 955.5, and 607.9 mAh g−1 at current rates of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 C, respectively.
[M0242 p9] All these values are much higher than those obtained with 1 M LiTFSI.
[M0243 p9] The polarization voltage at different discharge rates is also much lower than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure S26), indicating a greatly improved reaction rate.
[M0244 p9] The charge− discharge curve (Figure 5b) shows that two typical discharge platforms can be clearly observed for the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr at a current rate of 0.5C.
[M0245 p9] However, the disappearance of the second platform of the battery with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure S27) further indicates the lower reaction rate of the battery with 1.0 M LiTFSI.
[M0246 p9] To further evaluate the cell performance, we measured the cycling performance (current density of 0.5C) at an S loading of 2.18 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 10 μL mgs −1.
[M0247 p9] The capacity of the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is significantly higher than that of the 1 M LiTFSI battery, and the cycle time is about 2.3 times that of the 1 M LiTFSI battery (Figure S28).
[M0248 p9] In addition, the polarization voltage at different cycles is lower than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure S29).
[M0249 p9] To evaluate its application under high S loading and low E/S ratio, we further measured the cycling performance (a current density of 0.2C) at an S loading of 4.25 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 5 μL mgs −1 (Figure 5c).
[M0250 p9] The initial discharge capacity of the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr at 0.05C (first-circle activation) is up to 1456 mAh g−1, which is much higher than that with 1 M LiTFSI (1251 mAh g−1).
[M0251 p9] At a current density of 0.2C, the discharge capacity of the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr is 1104 mAh g−1, while that with 1.0 M LiTFSI is 219 mAh g−1, and the second platform of the battery with 1 M LiTFSI disappears (Figure S30).
[M0252 p9] After 100 cycles, the battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr still has a discharge capacity of 883 mAh g−1, with an average capacity decay rate of only 0.2% (Figure 5d).
[M0253 p9] The second platform was still visible after 200 cycles (Figure S31).
[M0254 p9] To further demonstrate its application under harsh conditions, we assembled Li−S batteries with an S loading of 6.2 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs −1.
[M0255 p9] The battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr has a high initial discharge capacity of 1129 mAh g−1 at 0.1C and can be operated stably over 70 cycles (Figure 5e).
[M0256 p9] In contrast, the second platform at 0.05 and 0.1C cannot be observed for the battery with 1 M LiTFSI, and a capacity of 171.6 mAh g−1 is achieved at a low current of 0.05C (Figure S32).
[M0257 p9] In order to verify the practicality of Li−S batteries with the designed electrolyte, a pouch cell with a level of 10.77 Ah was assembled, in which an S load of 4.6 mg cm−2 (single side) at the positive electrode and an E/S ratio of 3 μL mgs −1 were selected.
[M0258 p9] The discharge capacity of 1421 mAh g−1 in the first circle at 0.1C is achieved, corresponding to an energy density of 554 Wh kg−1 (Figure 5f,g).
[M0259 p9] The decreased capacity during cell cycling may be due to Li metal corrosion under harsh conditions with a low electrolyte volume.
[M0260 p9] We evaluated the long cycle performance of the Li//Li symmetric battery with 0.8 M LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiBr (Figure S33).
[M0261 p9] The cells can stably cycle, proving that LiBr has no obvious effect on the cycling performance of the Li metal electrode.
[M0262 p9] However, more focus should be paid to Li metal protection in future research, as we found some damage to the Li metal electrode after battery cycling (Figure S34).

[S0003 p2] 2 Methods Material characterization.
[S0004 p2] Sublimed sulfur (S), lithium nitrate (LiNO3), rosin(C19H29COOH) and carbon disulfide (CS2) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China); Lithium bromide (LiBr), Lithium sulfide (Li2S) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China); 1,2-dimethyl ether (DME), 1,3-diamethoxyethane (DOL) and bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide lithium salt (LiTFSI) were purchased from Dodochem(Suzhou, China).
[S0005 p2] All reagents and starting materials were used without further processing.
[S0006 p2] Preparation of S loading cathode.
[S0007 p2] The pyrrole was added to the 0.5 M ferric chloride (FeCl3) solution and stirred for 12 h to obtain polypyrrole.
[S0008 p2] After drying, a certain mass of potassium hydroxide (KOH) (the mass ratio of polypyrrole to KOH was 1:4) was weighed and dissolved with distilled water, then added to the polypyrrole, and stirred for more than 30 min to make it to the slurry form.
[S0009 p2] After drying in an oven, the above mixture was heated at 800 °C for 1 hours with a nitrogen atmosphere to obtain porous carbon (PC).
[S0010 p2] Then, the PC and S8 (S) were mixed and grinded at a mass ratio of 3:7, and then heated at 155 °C for 12 hours under vacuum to obtain the sulfur porous carbon composite (S-PC).
[S0011 p2] Carbon fiber (10 wt%), S-PC powder (80 wt%) and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (10 wt%) as a binder were dispersed in Nmethyl-2- pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry, and then coated onto an aluminum (Al) foil and dried using a coating machine with different coating thicknesses and dride at 60 °C for 24 h under vacuum.
[S0012 p2] Finally, the cathodes were formed.
[S0013 p2] Electrolyte and polysulfide preparation. 1.0 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfony)imide (LiTFSI) and 0.3 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxetane (DOL)/ 1,2- diamethoxyethane (DME) (v/v=1:1) mixed solvents was adopted as the blank electrolyte. 0.8, 0.5 and 0.2M LiTFSI or 0.8 M LiTFSI/0.2 M LiBr and 0.3 M lithium nitrate (LiNO3) dissolved in 1,3-dioxetane (DOL)/1,2-diamethoxyethane (DME) (v/v=1:1) mixed solvents were prepared.
[S0014 p2] The mixtures of lithium sulfides (Li2S) and sulfur (S) with a molar ratio of 1:5 and 1:7 were added into DOL/DME mixed solvents.
[S0015 p2] Then the powder was heated under an argon (Ar) atmosphere at 60 °C to prepare Li2S6 and Li2S8.
