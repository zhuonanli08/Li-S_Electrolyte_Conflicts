[M0002 p1] Interfaces 2023, 15, 37467−37476 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: High-concentration lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)- imide/1,2-dimethoxyethane (LiFSI/DME) electrolytes are promising candidates for highly reversible lithium−metal anodes.
[M0003 p1] However, the performance of lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries with a high concentration of LiFSI/DME declines because LiFSI reacts irreversibly with lithium polysulfide, which is formed during the charge−discharge process of Li−S batteries.
[M0004 p1] Hence, to apply high-concentration LiFSI/DME to Li−S batteries, we investigated carbon with an appropriate pore size for use in a sulfur composite cathode and optimized the composition of high-concentration LiFSI/DME.
[M0005 p1] The results showed that the combination of carbon with mesopores of 2−3 nm diameter and 3 M LiFSI in DME/ 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (HFE) (1:1 by vol.) provided a high-rate capability (943 mA h g−1 at a rate of 2 C).
[M0006 p1] Moreover, the ratio of the 50th discharge capacity to the 2nd discharge capacity (capacity retention) improved from 50.0 to 61.6% with HFE dilution of high-concentration LiFSI/DME.
[M0007 p1] The improved performance was achieved by suppressing the dissolution of lithium polysulfide, decreasing the viscosity of the electrolyte, and forming a thin solid electrolyte interface on the lithium−metal anode due to HFE dilution.
[M0008 p1] KEYWORDS: LiFSI, DME, localized high-concentration electrolytes, lithium metal, sulfur, microporous carbon, lithium−sulfur battery ■INTRODUCTION Recently, electricity generated by energy sources that do not emit greenhouse gases such as CO2, wind, and solar power, has been promoted as a global warming countermeasure.1,2 To efficiently utilize clean energy, a high-energy-density storage device is essential.3 Lithium−sulfur (Li−S) batteries with sulfur cathodes and lithium−metal anodes have received attention as high-energy-density storage systems.4 The theoretical specific capacities of sulfur and lithium are 1672 and 3860 mA h g−1, respectively, providing Li−S batteries with twice the volumetric energy density and six times the gravimetric energy density of lithium-ion batteries.5 Therefore, Li−S batteries are ideal storage devices for clean energy sources.
[M0009 p1] However, the practical application of Li−S batteries faces several challenges.
[M0010 p1] At the cathode, sulfur reacts with lithium ions to form lithium polysulfide (Li2Sx) during the charge−discharge process.6,7 In particular, Li2Sx (x = 6−8) exhibits high solubility in most organic electrolytes.8,9 The dissolution of Li2Sx causes loss of the active material, redox shuttling, and pollution of the lithium anode, which hinders the stable cycling of Li−S batteries.
[M0011 p1] To address this problem, Nazar et al. reported a method for confining sulfur in the pores of highly ordered mesoporous carbon.10 The dissolution of Li2Sx into the electrolyte is kinetically suppressed owing to the interaction between the carbon materials and Li2Sx.
[M0012 p1] Consequently, the Li−S batteries are successfully charged and discharged without a redox shuttle.
[M0013 p1] However, mesoporous carbons cannot completely inhibit polar Li2Sx dissolution because the carbon surface is generally nonpolar.11 Hence, surface modification of mesoporous carbons with polymers, functional groups, or metal compounds should be applied to impart polarity to the carbon surface.12−15 Lithium−metal anodes also have fundamental problems.
[M0014 p1] Lithium metal reacts chemically with all electrolytes; therefore, continuous electrolyte decomposition occurs and a thick solid electrolyte interface (SEI) forms on the anode.
[M0015 p1] This results in a low coulombic efficiency and high cell resistance.16,17 Moreover, the lithium−metal anode undergoes repeated deposition and dissolution during the charge−discharge process, which induces lithium dendrite formation.18 The lithium dendrites Received: May 9, 2023 Accepted: July 18, 2023 Published: July 26, 2023 Research Article www.acsami.org © 2023 American Chemical Society 37467 https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.3c06624 ACS Appl.

[M0017 p2] can break through the separator and touch the cathode, causing cell ignition and explosion.
[M0018 p2] Furthermore, the heterogeneous dissolution of lithium dendrites during the discharge process forms dead lithium, which loses its electronic contact with the anode.19 Dead lithium is electrochemically inactive and causes capacity fading.
[M0019 p2] Various electrolytes and additives have been investigated to suppress the excess decomposition of electrolytes and formation of lithium dendrites.20,21 A high-concentration lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide/1,2-dimethoxyethane (LiFSI/DME) electrolyte has been reported to achieve a stable SEI and dendrite-free lithium−metal anode.22 In particular, a Li/Cu cell with 3−5 M LiFSI/DME showed a high coulombic efficiency.
[M0020 p2] The application of high-concentration LiFSI/DME in Li−S batteries suppresses electrolyte decomposition and short circuits, thereby improving battery performance.
[M0021 p2] However, Li2Sx dissolved from the mesoporous carbon−sulfur composite cathode reacts with bis- (fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI) irreversibly.23 This leads to loss of cathode active material and reduces the coulombic efficiency and capacity of Li−S batteries.24 Carbonate electrolytes that react with Li2Sx irreversibly cannot be used for Li−S batteries; however, we previously reported that Li−S batteries with carbonate electrolytes could be cycled using a microporous carbon−sulfur composite cathode.25 This is because the small pore size (<2 nm) of the microporous carbon suppressed contact between Li2Sx in the micropores and the solvent.26,27 Microporous carbon suppresses Li2Sx dissolution more effectively than mesoporous carbon, and Li−S batteries with carbonate electrolytes exhibit high cycle stability.
[M0022 p2] These studies suggest that carbon materials with appropriate pore sizes can be used to realize Li−S batteries containing high-concentration LiFSI/DME electrolytes with superior cycling performance.
[M0023 p2] In this study, we investigated the pore size of carbon materials used in cathodes to achieve high-performance Li−S batteries with high-concentration LiFSI/DME.
[M0024 p2] Furthermore, we prepared a suitable high-concentration LiFSI/DME electrolyte for use with the micro-mesoporous carbon−sulfur composite cathode and analyzed the effect of the electrolyte on the cathode and anode. 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3- tetrafluoropropylether (HFE) was used for the electrolyte optimization.
[M0025 p2] Because HFE is one of the hydrofluoroethers with the lowest lithium-solvating ability,28 dilution with HFE significantly reduces the viscosity of high-concentration LiFSI/ DME while maintaining the locally high concentration of the LiFSI/DME electrolyte.29 Moreover, HFE suppresses the side reaction between Li2Sx and lithium−metal anodes, and improves the deposition morphology of lithium−metal anodes;30 thus, HFE is a promising solvent for electrolyte optimization.
[M0026 p2] We believe that our systematic evaluation of the physical and chemical characteristics of the carbon materials, electrolyte conditions, and effects of each electrolyte component on the cathode and anode will guide the design of high-performance Li−S batteries. ■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION Preparation of Porous Carbon−Sulfur Composites.
[M0027 p2] Three commercial porous carbons with different pore size distributions (Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso) were used as purchased.
[M0028 p2] Porous carbon−sulfur composites (Cmicro−S, Cmicro−meso−S, and Cmeso−S) were prepared using the same procedure described in a previous study.31 Additional details are provided in the Supporting Information.
[M0029 p2] Characterization of Materials.
[M0030 p2] The shapes and surfaces of the porous carbon samples were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FlexSEM 1000 II, Hitachi High-Technologies Co.).
[M0031 p2] The accelerating voltage and working distance were set to 5.00 kV and 5 mm, respectively.
[M0032 p2] The particle size distribution of the porous carbon samples was analyzed using a laser scattering particle size distribution analyzer (LA-950, HORIBA, Ltd.).
[M0033 p2] Pore structures of porous carbons were investigated by the N2 adsorption/desorption method using a fully automatic gas adsorption amount measuring device (autosorb iQ, Anton Paar Japan Co.).
[M0034 p2] The specific surface area and pore volume distribution were calculated using Brunauer− Emmett−Teller (BET) and quenched solid density functional theory (QSDFT) methods, respectively.
[M0035 p2] The functional groups on the porous carbon surfaces were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, JPS-9010MC, JEOL Co.).
[M0036 p2] Mg Kα radiation was used as the X-ray source.
[M0037 p2] The spectra were corrected such that the adventitious carbon peak in the C 1s spectrum was at 284.8 eV.
[M0038 p2] Sulfur in the pores or on the surface of porous carbons was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Ultima IV, Rigaku Co.) with Cu Kα radiation as the X-ray source.
[M0039 p2] The accelerating voltage was 40 kV and the tube current was 40 mA.
[M0040 p2] The maximum peak intensities of all the XRD spectra were standardized to the same value.
[M0041 p2] The sulfur content of the porous carbon−sulfur composites was measured using thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA, DTG-60AH, Shimadzu Co.).
[M0042 p2] The rate of temperature increase was set to 5 °C min−1, and the carrier gas was Ar.
[M0043 p2] The elemental distribution of the porous carbon−sulfur composites was investigated using energydispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, FlexSEM 1000 II, Hitachi High- Technologies Co.).
[M0044 p2] The accelerating voltage was set to 15.0 kV.
[M0045 p2] The viscosity and ionic conductivity of various electrolytes was investigated at 25 °C using a viscometer (VM-10A, Sekonic Co.) and an impedance analyzer (VMP-300, Bio-Logic Science Instruments Ltd.), respectively.
[M0046 p2] The suppression effect of the electrolytes on Li2Sx dissolution was measured using ultraviolet−visible absorption (UV− vis) spectroscopy.
[M0047 p2] Deuterium and halogen light sources were used for measurement (DH-2000-BAL, Ocean Optics).
[M0048 p2] The effect of the electrolyte on the lithium anode surface was analyzed using SEM and XPS (PHI Quantes, ULVAC-PHI, Inc.).
[M0049 p2] The accelerating voltage for SEM was set to 15.0 kV.

[M0052 p2] After etching, the spectra were corrected such that the peak ascribed to LiF in the F 1s spectrum was at 685 eV.
[M0053 p2] The lithium anode was removed from the charged−discharged cells after 10 cycles, washed with HFE, and analyzed.
[M0054 p2] All the experiments were performed under Ar or vacuum.
[M0055 p2] Preparation of Electrolytes.
[M0056 p2] LiFSI was added to DME and stirred overnight to prepare 3, 3.6, and 4 M LiFSI (Nippon Shokubai Co., Ltd.)/DME (Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd.).
[M0057 p2] To prepare electrolytes22 consisting of the two solvents, DME was mixed with HFE (Daikin Industries, Ltd.) in a volume ratio of 1:1.
[M0058 p2] The salt concentration of the co-solvent was 3 M (3 M LiFSI in DME/ HFE).
[M0059 p2] The molar ratio of LiFSI/DME/HFE in the 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE solution was 1:1.2:0.83.
[M0060 p2] All operations were performed in an Ar-filled glovebox.
[M0061 p2] Electrochemical Measurements.

[M0064 p2] The anode’s surface area was 1.33 cm2.
[M0065 p2] Cells adopting a porous carbon−sulfur composite cathode, lithium−metal anode, and electrolyte were assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox.
[M0066 p2] Two microporous polypropylene membranes were used to prevent short circuits.

[M0068 p2] The electrolyte used for each Li−S battery was fixed at 52.0 μL.
[M0069 p2] Constant current charge−discharge tests were conducted using a battery tester (BTS-2004H, Nagano Co., Ltd.).

[M0074 p3] Various measurements were performed to reveal the physical and chemical structures of porous carbon materials.
[M0075 p3] The particle shapes and surfaces of the porous carbons were observed by SEM (Figure 1a).
[M0076 p3] Cmicro and Cmeso exhibited rugged shapes and rough surfaces, respectively.
[M0077 p3] Cmicro−meso had a rounded shape and smooth surface.
[M0078 p3] Particle size distributions were measured to determine the exact particle size of the porous carbons.
[M0079 p3] The median diameters of Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso were 3.49, 2.52, and 49.8 μm, respectively (Figure S1).
[M0080 p3] A small particle size increases the surface area of porous carbon−sulfur composite cathodes and shortens the electronic and ionic conductive paths, which improves the discharge capacity and rate capability.32−35 N2 adsorption/desorption measurements were performed to investigate the pore structures of the porous carbons; Figure 1b shows the isotherms.
[M0081 p3] According to IUPAC classification, the isotherms of Cmicro, Cmicro−meso, and Cmeso are classified as type I(a), type I(b), and type IV(a), respectively.36 Therefore, Cmicro mainly contains micropores, Cmicro−meso contains micropores with a narrow range of slight mesopores, and Cmeso mainly contains mesopores.
[M0082 p3] Figure 1c shows the pore size distributions of the porous carbon materials.
[M0083 p3] Cmicro has a pore diameter of 2 nm or less and is therefore a typical microporous carbon.
[M0084 p3] Although Cmicro−meso mainly has micropores, there are a small number of mesopores of 2 nm or more and 3 nm or less.
[M0085 p3] Cmeso has a pore diameter of 2 nm or more and 50 nm or less; therefore, it is a typical mesoporous carbon.
[M0086 p3] The specific surface areas and pore volumes of the porous carbon samples were calculated from the isotherms (Table 1).
[M0087 p3] The pore diameter was correlated with the specific surface area and pore volume.
[M0088 p3] Carbon with a large pore volume can confine a large amount of sulfur; thus, Cmicro−meso or Cmeso could offer the advantage of cell energy density over Cmicro.
[M0089 p3] XPS measurements were performed to investigate the surface composition of the porous carbons.
[M0090 p3] Figure S2 shows wide-scan XPS spectra.
[M0091 p3] All the porous carbons contained only carbon and oxygen.
[M0092 p3] Narrow-scan XPS was used to analyze the surfaces in detail (Figure 1d,e).
[M0093 p3] The C 1s spectra of all porous carbons had peaks around 284.6 and 288.6 eV, which were attributed to C−C and −COO bonds, respectively.37 Moreover, the peaks around 532.9 eV in the O 1s spectra were ascribed to C−O and CO bonds in the porous carbons.38 The surface composition ratios, calculated from the peak areas of the C 1s and O 1s spectra (Table 1), revealed that Cmicro had the highest number of oxygen functional groups and Cmeso had the lowest number of oxygen-containing functional groups among all carbon materials.

[M0104 p4] The sulfur content of the porous carbon−sulfur composites was measured using TGA and DTA (Figures 2 and S3).

[M0114 p4] Electrochemical Performances of Porous Carbon−Sulfur Composite Cathodes with 3 M LiFSI/DME.
[M0115 p4] Galvanostatic charge−discharge measurements were performed to investigate the effect of the pore structure of porous carbons on the performance of Li−S batteries with 3 M LiFSI/DME.
[M0116 p4] Figure 3a shows the charge−discharge curves of the Li−S batteries at the 1st and 50th cycles.
[M0117 p4] Cmicro−S showed only one plateau at low potential (∼1.6−1.9 V).
[M0118 p4] This plateau was attributed to the formation of Li2Sx (x = 1, 2).14 In contrast, Cmicro−meso−S and Cmeso−S exhibited a plateau at a high potential (∼2.0 V) in addition to a low-potential plateau.
[M0119 p4] The high-potential plateau was attributed to the reduction of sulfur to Li2Sx (x = 4−8).14 Figure 3b shows the cycle plots of the Li−S batteries.
[M0120 p4] Cmicro−S had high capacity retention (83.4%), which was calculated by dividing the discharge capacity at the 50th cycle (934.5 mA h g−1) by the discharge capacity at the 2nd cycle (1121 mA h g−1).
[M0121 p4] Furthermore, Cmicro−S showed high coulombic efficiency (average 99.4%).
[M0122 p4] This stable cycle performance could be due to the suppression of Li2Sx dissolution, which causes loss of the cathode active material and redox shuttling for two reasons: (i) micropores suppress the formation of Li2Sx (x = 4−8) and42 (ii) the decomposition products of the electrolyte formed by charging and discharging completely cover the pores, which inhibits contact between the electrolyte solvent and the cathode active material.27 The coulombic efficiency of Cmeso−S significantly exceeded 100% at the 11th cycle, and the Li−S battery with the Cmeso−S cathode did not reach the charge cut-off voltage at the 14th cycle.
[M0123 p4] Qian and Fu reported that the molar ratio of Li+ to DME in 3 M LiFSI/DME is 1:2.2, and Li+ is coordinated with five oxygen atoms of FSI−and DME molecules.22,43 From this electrolyte composition, they suggested that it contained uncoordinated DME molecules.
[M0124 p4] In the case of Cmeso−S, Li2Sx (x = 6−8) would be able to dissolve in the uncoordinated DME of the 3 M LiFSI/DME electrolyte because Cmeso has mainly mesopores, from which Li2Sx dissolves into uncoordinated DME easily.
[M0125 p4] The dissolved Li2Sx in the electrolyte removed from the cycled cell were confirmed by UV−vis spectroscopy (Figure S6).
[M0126 p4] Cmeso−S and Cmicro−meso−S had a peak ascribed to Sx 2−at 350−500 nm; the peak intensity of Cmeso−S was approximately four times higher than that of Cmicro−meso−S.
[M0127 p4] In addition, the Sx 2−peak was not observed in the Cmicro−S spectrum.24,44 Thus, Cmeso−S could not reach the cut-off voltage owing to the redox shuttle, pollution of the lithium anode, and the irreversible reaction between dissolved Li2Sx and LiFSI.23 The cell with Cmicro−meso−S was discharged and charged with a capacity decay.

[M0142 p5] However, the sulfur content of Cmicro−S was only 21.4 wt %, which is disadvantageous in terms of energy density of Li−S batteries.

[M0144 p5] The capacity decay of the Cmicro−meso−S/ Li battery with 3 M LiFSI/DME was due to Li2Sx dissolution caused by a few mesopores and uncoordinated DME molecules.
[M0145 p5] Some reports have shown that high-concentration electrolytes contain less uncoordinated DME; thus, the Li2Sx dissolution is suppressed.24,45 High-concentration LiFSI/DME above 3.6 M without uncoordinated DME would realize Cmicro−meso−S/Li batteries with high energy density and high cycle stability.
[M0146 p5] Figure 4a shows the charge−discharge curves of Li−S batteries with Cmicro−meso−S cathodes and various concentrations of LiFSI/DME electrolytes at the 1st and 50th cycles.
[M0147 p5] For 3 M LiFSI/DME, two plateaus were observed.
[M0148 p5] The highpotential plateau, which is related to readily soluble Li2Sx (x = 6−8), disappeared as the concentration of LiFSI increased (Figure S7).
[M0149 p5] In the case of 3.6 and 4 M LiFSI/DME, nearly all of the DME were coordinated to Li+ because the molar ratios of LiFSI and DME were 1:2 and 1:1.4, respectively.22 Thus, the dissolution of Li2Sx was significantly inhibited and the highpotential plateau disappeared.
[M0150 p5] To investigate the suppression effect of the electrolytes on Li2Sx dissolution, solutions prepared by mixing each electrolyte and 0.2 M Li2S8/DME at a volume ratio of 3:1 were observed (Figure S8).
[M0151 p5] As the LiFSI concentration increased, the dark color became lighter.
[M0152 p5] This color change suggests that soluble Li2Sx (x = 6−8) was transformed into insoluble short-chain Li2Sx.44,46,47 Therefore, a higher electrolyte concentration suppresses the dissolution of Li2Sx.
[M0153 p5] The morphology of the Li deposition in 3, 3.6, and 4 M LiFSI/DME was observed using SEM (Figure S9).
[M0154 p5] The deposited lithium had a nodule-like structure with round edges, which is consistent with previously reported results.22 As the electrolyte concentration increased, Li was densely and uniformly deposited.
[M0155 p5] Nevertheless, a higher concentration of the electrolyte led to significant capacity decay (Figure 4b).

[M0157 p5] Furthermore, the average coulombic efficiencies of 3.6 and 4 M LiFSI/DME were 90.8 and 92.6%, respectively.

[M0170 p6] The above results indicate that a suitable electrolyte for lithium−metal anodes and Cmicro−meso−S cathodes requires a low viscosity and high LiFSI concentration.
[M0171 p6] Diluted electrolytes with HFE have reduced viscosity and localized high concentrations of LiFSI because LiFSI does not dissolve in HFE.24 Herein, 3 M LiFSI/ DME was diluted with HFE to improve the cycling performance at high current densities and suppress Li2Sx dissolution.
[M0172 p6] Figure 5a shows the charge−discharge curves of the Li−S batteries with Cmicro−meso−S cathodes and 3 M LiFSI in DME/ HFE electrolytes at the 1st and 50th cycles.
[M0173 p6] A high-potential plateau, which is attributed to the Li2Sx (x = 4−8) formation reaction, was not observed during the discharge of the Li−S batteries with 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE.
[M0174 p6] Dilution with HFE increased the concentration of LiFSI in DME, which decreased the amount of free DME.
[M0175 p6] Therefore, the dissolution of Li2Sx was suppressed in 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE.
[M0176 p6] To investigate the suppression effect of 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE on Li2Sx dissolution, a solution prepared by mixing 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE and 0.2 M Li2S8/DME at a volume ratio of 3:1 was observed (Figure S8), and the dark color of Li2S8 changed to yellow because of the presence of Li2Sx (x = 1, 2).47 Thus, dilution with HFE suppresses the dissolution of Li2Sx.
[M0177 p6] Figure 5b shows the cycle plots of Li−S batteries with Cmicro−meso−S cathodes and 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE electrolytes.
[M0178 p6] The HFE dilution suppressed the continuous capacity decay of 3 M LiFSI/DME and improved the capacity retention rate, calculated by dividing the discharge capacity at the 50th cycle (732.3 and 830.4 mA h g−1) by the discharge capacity at the 2nd cycle (1462.9 and 1347.7 mA h g−1), from 50.0 to 61.6%.
[M0179 p6] Moreover, the average coulombic efficiency improved from 97.1 to 98.5%.
[M0180 p6] This improved cycling performance with HFE dilution was due to the mitigation of the non-uniform current distribution by decreasing the viscosity of the electrolyte (Table 2).
[M0181 p6] Furthermore, the decreased viscosity provided 3 M LiFSI in DME/HFE with superior performance at high current density (Figure 5c).
[M0182 p6] The electrochemical performance of Li−S batteries under practical conditions is discussed in the Supporting Information (Figure S10).

[S0002 p2] S-2 Preparation of porous carbon–sulfur composites Three commercial porous carbons with different pore size distributions (Cmicro, Cmicro-meso, and Cmeso) were used.

[S0009 p2] Thus, this sulfur distribution is the theoretical sulfur content ratio that accounts for sulfur expansion during the charge–discharge process.
