## 实验部分
2.1. Electrochemical measurements CR2032 coin-type cells were used for all tests, with the exception of the in-situ FTIR experiments and NMR experiments. The electrolytes were prepared by dissolving the desired amount of salt(s) into the solvent(s) in an MBraun LABmaster glove box with an Ar atmosphere (< 1 ppm O2 and < 1 ppm H2O). Battery-grade DME and DOL were obtained from BASF Corporation. The diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (diglyme, G2) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (> 99.5%) and was dried over 4 Å molecular sieves prior to use. LiTFSI was obtained from BASF and LiNO3 was obtained from Alfa-Aesar (98%). These salts were dried for four days under vacuum at 120 °C before use. The Cu||Li coin cells were assembled using Li foil (250 µm thick, MTI Corporation) as the counter/reference electrode, two layers of Celgard 2045 separator with 75 µL of electrolyte added, and Cu foil (All Foils) as the substrate for Li metal plating. The active surface area of the Cu foil disks was 2.11 cm2. For galvanostatic cycling, a capacity cut-oﬀwas used for plating and a voltage cut-oﬀof 1.0 V (vs. Li/Li+) was applied for stripping. The Li-S cell was assembled using a cathode prepared from multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, Cheap Tubes Inc.) and PTFE (60 wt% aqueous dispersion, Sigma-Aldrich) in a weight ratio of 80:20. The cathode containing the MWCNTs and PTFE was rolled into a free- standing film and then punched into a disk (1.6 cm2). The total mass of the MWCNT/PTFE electrode was 5.8 mg/cm2. The electrolyte used contained the sulfur source in the form of dissolved polysulfides: 50 µL of [0.5 M LiTFSI + 3 M LiNO3]/G2 + Li2S8 (2 M based on S) which is equivalent to 3.2 mg of elemental sulfur. This mass of sulfur is what the reported values of specific capacity are based oﬀof. The theoretical nominal capacity of the cell is 5.36 mAh (1675 mAh/gsulfur). The anode was a Li chip (400 µm thick, MTI Corporation) and two layers of Celgard 2045 separator were used. The Li-S cell was cycled at 0.4 mA/ cm2 (200 mA/g) between 1.9 and 2.7 V which is approximately C/7 rate. A Lanhe battery testing station and a temperature control chamber set to 30 °C were used to control all galvanostatic cycling protocols. The cyclic voltammetry and linear sweep voltammetry studies were con- trolled with a Model 600E potentiostat from CH Instruments. 2.2. Spectroelectrochemistry A Thermo Nicolet iS50R FTIR spectrometer with a liquid Ar cooled MCT high D* detector and a spectral resolution of 0.5 cm-1 was used for the FTIR spectroelectrochemical measurements. The spectrometer was housed in an Ar-filled glovebox to ensure minimal interference from oxygen and moisture. The measurements were conducted using a home-built ATR electrochemical window cell (Fig. S13) similar to those used by others [24], and a VeeMAX III ATR accessory with a 45° silicon prism (Pike Technologies, Madison, WI). The cell was sealed onto the ATR accessory and prism using a Kalrez® O-Ring. The angle of incidence was set to 50°. The working electrode was prepared directly on the prism surface as follows: First, the native oxide was removed using an HF etch. The prism was then immediately transferred into vacuum to minimize exposure to air and oxide formation, and 100 nm of Cu was sputtered onto the prism surface. The Cu-coated prism was transferred from the vacuum chamber to the glovebox for FTIR measurements without exposing it to air. Ohmic contact to the working electrode was made using a Pt pin to contact the Cu surface via a separate borehole external to the solution compartment to ensure that electrochemical reactions occur on the Cu surface and not the Pt pin. An Autolab po- tentiostat (Metrohm) was used to make the electrochemical measure- ments. The potential of the cell was controlled using a leakless Ag/AgCl reference electrode (Warner Instruments). A Pt coil housed in a fritted glass chamber (BASi, Inc) served as the counter electrode. 200 inter- ferrograms were acquired and averaged to form one absorbance spec- trum every 2.25 min. The spectra were then subtractively normalized to the first spectrum in the series to create a series of diﬀerence spectra. 2.3. Characterization The morphologies of the Li plated on the Cu and Li substrates and the residues on the Cu substrates after stripping were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on an FEI Quanta 650 ESEM at 5.0 kV and 0.17 nA. XPS measurements were performed using a Specs PHOIBOS 150 hemispherical energy analyzer using a monochromated Al Kα X-ray source. The load-lock of the analytical UHV system is connected di- rectly to a glovebox, enabling opening of coin cells and loading of samples without any exposure to ambient atmosphere. Samples were rinsed overnight in purified G2 (H2O content < 1 ppm) after removal from the coin cell in order to remove residual salt, followed by an ad- ditional ~ 1 h rinse in fresh G2 before loading into the UHV system. Although it is possible that the rinsing process does modify the surface speciation, the relative diﬀerences observed between samples are still meaningful as all samples were prepared using the same protocol. Survey spectra were measured using a pass energy of 40 eV at a re- solution of 0.2 eV/step and a total integration time of 0.4 s/point. Core level spectra were measured using a pass energy of 20 eV at a resolution of 0.05 eV/step and a total integration time of 0.5 s/point. Deconvolution was performed using CasaXPS software with a Shirley- type background and 70-30 Gaussian-Lorentzian peak shapes. Peaks were charge referenced using the position of aliphatic carbon in the C1s peak at 284.8 eV. The ionic conductivity values of the electrolytes were determined using a multichannel conductivity meter with an integrated frequency response analyzer (MCS-10, Biologic Science Instruments) at 25 °C. The viscosities of the electrolytes were measured using a Brookfield DV-II+ Pro viscometer at room temperature (23 ± 2 °C). 1H, 19F, 7Li and 13C solid state nuclear magnetic resonance (SS- NMR) spectroscopy were performed on a 600 MHz (1H resonance fre- quency) solid state NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) with 2.5 mm magic-angle-spinning (MAS) probe at a spinning rate of 35 kHz and at ambient temperature (~ 26 °C). All NMR spectra obtained by Fourier transform of free induction decay after a single pulse excitation with π/ 2° pulse of 4 µs except for the 7Li NMR which instead utilized a π/20° B.D. Adams et al. Nano Energy 40 (2017) 607-617 608 pulse of 4 µs. The samples were transferred to the rotors in a glove box and moved to the magnet in a double-sealed container to avoid air contamination. During the MAS experiments, dry nitrogen gas was used to spin the rotor. To gather enough material for the SS-NMR analysis, Li||Cu pouch cells were assembled at the Advanced Battery Facility (ABF) at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory using pouch cell assembly equipment from Media Tech. Cu foil (10 µm thick) at a standard size of 55.5 × 37.5 mm and 80 µm Li foil laminated on the Cu foil were stacked between polypropylene separators for single layer pouch cells. The electrolyte (1.5 g) was injected into the pouch cell and then sealed under -90 kPa vacuum. In a temperature control chamber at 25 °C, the pouch cells were cycled at 0.4 mA/cm2 to a deposition capacity of 1 mAh/cm2 and a stripping cut-oﬀof 1 V for 100 cycles by an Arbin battery tester.

## 正文（含数字的句子与图表注）
- In this work, a new electrolyte system based on a high concentration of LiNO3 in diglyme (G2) solvent is developed which enables an ex- ceptionally high CE for Li metal plating/stripping and thus high stability of the Li anode in the sulfur-containing electrolyte.
- This electrolyte provides a CE of greater than 99% for over 200 cycles of Li plating/stripping.
- In contrast, the Li anode cycles for less than 35 cycles (with a high CE) in the state-of-the-art 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3 in 1,3-dioxolane:1,2-dimethoxyethane (DOL:DME) electrolyte under the same conditions.
- The stable Li anode enabled by the new electrolyte may accelerate the applications of high energy density Li-S batteries in both electrical vehicles and large-scale grid energy storage markets. 1.
- Introduction Li-S batteries have been studied extensively during the last few decades, yet progress has been slow on critical issues such as high self- discharge rate, the polysulfide shuttle mechanism leading to a low Coulombic eﬃciency (CE), and low cycle life [1-3].
- More than 80% of recent studies have focused on one electrolyte: lithium bis(tri- fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a binary mixture of 1,3-diox- olane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with LiNO3 as an additive [1-8].
- The binary mixture of DOL:DME was originally developed by Mikhaylik and Akridge (Sion Power Corporation) for its excellent rate capability at low temperature, while LiTFSI is used because it is one of the most stable salts in the presence of polysulfides and their corre- sponding radical reactions [9,10].
- Since the breakthrough discovery by Mikhaylik in 2008 of the reduced impact of the polysulfide shuttle achieved by the addition of LiNO3 [11], this salt has become a universal additive in nearly every reported Li-S battery study [7,12].
- The eﬀect of LiNO3 on the passivation of Li metal surfaces has been thoroughly studied [5,13-16], with the surface layer often described as one or more LixNOy species, as first suggested by Aurbach et al.[17] This surface (passivation) layer is commonly referred to as a solid electrolyte in- terphase (SEI) [18].
- This requires that it have a uniform morphology and composition to ensure a homogeneous current distribution and it should be elastic and flexible to maintain its shape during volume changes which occur upon plating and stripping of the Li metal [18,19].
- However, SEIs formed in most http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.nanoen.2017.09.015 Received 1 July 2017; Received in revised form 6 September 2017; Accepted 6 September 2017 ⁎ Corresponding author at: The Joint Center for Energy Storage Research (JCESR), Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, Richland, WA 99354, USA.

- Various approaches, such as the addition of ad- ditives or ex-situ formation of an artificial layer, have been explored during the last decade in attempts to achieve the formation of high quality SEIs [20-23].
- Although it is well established that LiNO3 is critical in preventing the polysulfide shuttle, to the best of our knowledge, a careful study on the eﬀect of this salt's concentration on the performance of Li metal anodes and Li-S batteries and its fate after long-term cycling has yet to be reported.
- In this work, we show that LiNO3 is very eﬀective in forming an SEI layer on Li metal which is distinguished by the inclusion of Li2O.
- Electrolytes without LiNO3 do not contain Li2O as an SEI component.
- The greatly enhanced CE and prolonged cycle life of elec- trolytes with a high concentration of LiNO3 strongly suggest that Li2O is a beneficial SEI component, acting to prevent excessive decomposition of the electrolyte.
- During repeated stripping and plating of Li metal, LiNO3 is consumed in a self-limiting manner as it passivates freshly exposed Li surfaces.
- The morphology of deposited Li metal in the high concentration LiNO3 electrolyte is found to be “nodule-like” with par- ticle sizes in the range of ~5-20 µm.
- The SEI replicates the underlying “nodule” particle (3D) surface structure and the SEI growth during each cycle results in a layer of porous residual SEI which continuously builds-up.
- The use of electrolytes containing high concentrations of LiNO3 therefore is a promising means to significantly extend the cycle life of Li-S batteries. 3.
- Results and discussion 3.1.
- Design principles for LiNO3-containing electrolytes After testing the CE for Li metal plating/stripping with the state-of- the-art electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME) with and without LiNO3 (Fig. 1a), we found that a high CE (> 99%) is only achieved when the LiNO3 additive is used.
- In the work of Li et al. more cycles were ob- tained with 5 wt% LiNO3 than 1 wt%., suggesting that the LiNO3 ad- ditive is consumed during repeated Li plating/stripping cycling [25].
- In this study, these authors reported that the maximum concentration of LiNO3 in 1 M LiTFSI in DOL:DME is approximately 0.75 M [25].
- As shown by Henderson et al. [26,27] at room temperature, LiNO3 forms a solid crystalline complex when added to pure DME, even for dilute concentrations, and LiNO3 is insoluble in pure DOL.
- The binary DOL:DME solvent system is thus required to achieve even the relatively low salt dissolution limit of 0.75 M.
- In contrast, LiNO3 is highly soluble in the higher order glyme solvents with the crystalline solvates formed having a low melting point, thus enabling the preparation of solutions with high concentrations of LiNO3 [27].
- Although various concentrations of LiNO3 have been examined up to 5 wt% in DOL:DME, the concentration eﬀect of LiNO3 has typically been masked or down-played with respect to the role of dissolved polysulfides on the plating/stripping behavior of Li metal [25,28].
- The main issue which has hampered studying the LiNO3 concentration ef- fect in DOL:DME is its limited solubility in this solvent system.
- By switching to a diﬀerent solvent (diethylene glycol dimethyl ether or diglyme (G2)), we were able to solubilize up to 4 M LiNO3, thus al- lowing an examination of an extended LiNO3 concentration range re- lative to DOL:DME (< 0.75 M).
- The Coulombic eﬃciency (CE) of Li metal plating and stripping in a series of LiNO3/G2 electrolytes was tested using Cu||Li half cells with Cu serving as the working electrode (Fig. 1b).
- A roughly linear increase in the number of cycles attained before a decrease in the CE is observed with increasing concentration of LiNO3 from 1 M to 4 M.
- It should be noted, however, that the maximum CE achieved with LiNO3/G2 mixtures is only ~ 96%.
- This is notably lower than what is observed in Fig. 1a for the 1 M LiTFSI + 0.3 M LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolyte and what has been reported for 1 M LiTFSI + x wt% LiNO3 in DOL:DME electrolytes [25,28].

- The discharge voltage cut-oﬀin NO3 --containing electrolytes has previously been ex- amined and LiNO3 was found to undergo irreversible reduction at voltages less than 1.8 V [36].
- Here, the electroreduction of the nitrate anion (NO3 - + e-→1/2O2- + NO2 -) occurs on carbon at voltages below 1.9 V vs.

- When used as an additive (i.e., in small amounts), as is the case for the state-of-the-art DOL:DME elec- trolyte, the beneficial eﬀect of LiNO3 in stabilizing the SEI is limited.
- Li-S batteries with a high CE after extensive cycling are enabled, however, by an electrolyte which con- tains a high concentration of LiNO3 in addition to a second supporting salt to increase the ionic conductivity (LiTFSI).
- The high concentration of LiNO3 serves two purposes: 1) to passivate the Li metal negative electrode, thus enabling > 99% CE for Li plating/stripping for an ex- tended number of cycles, and 2) to increase the solubility of Li poly- sulfides (Li2Sn where 4 ≤n ≤8), thereby allowing the discharge/ charge process to occur via the liquid phase (electrolyte) rather than the reversible solid-state conversion of sulfur (S) to Li sulfide (Li2S).
