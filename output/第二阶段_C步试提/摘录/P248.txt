- Introduction As technology develops, energy storage apparatus has become increasingly important.1 Among them, Li-S bat- teries are considered as favorable energy storage apparatus because of their superior theoretical specific energy and spe- cific capacity.2 Also, S serves as the cathode material in Li-S batteries, which is abundant and environmentally friendly.3 Unfortunately, there are still some difficulties hindering the feasible utilization of Li-S batteries.4,5 In the cathode, the Li polysulfide (LiPS) will be formed during the cycles, and it will further migrate to the Li metal anode.
- The LiPSs will be reduced on the Li metal anode, and subsequently migrate back to the cathode to be oxidized, leading to an overcharge phenomenon (also known as the “shuttle effect”).6 This phe- nomenon leads to low Coulombic efficiency (CE), severe self-discharge, inferior utilization of sulfur, and rapid capac- ity decay during the cycles.
- Unstable Li metal anodes in Li-S batteries is another problem, although Li metal has been considered to be the ultimate alternate for the Li-battery anode due to the uppermost theoretical specific capacity and the lowermost electrode potential.7 The rapid formation of Li-dendrite and inevitable parasitic reactions with the elec- trolyte accelerate the failure of the battery.8 To overcome these problems, researchers have developed various methods.
- Recently, electrolyte design is considered an effective strat- egy to deal with the “shuttle effect” and also achieve stable Li metal anodes.30 One promising approach is increasing the elec- trolyte concentration.
- By adding a “diluent” which has little or even no solubil- ity for Li salts to the high-concentration electrolyte, 31 the original solvated structure can be retained.
- In this study, we employ a novel “diluent”, 1, 1, 2, 2-tetra- fluoroethyl methyl ether (TME), to design a LHCE system (LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME).
- Without using LiNO3, a superior average CE of Li-Cu batteries can be achieved, and the capacity retention of Li-S batteries were also evidently improved.
- Results and Discussion The CE of Li-Cu Batteries with Different Electrolytes The Coulombic efficiency of Li-Cu batteries with the LHCE (LiTFSI-DME/DOL-TME) system was evaluated, and the molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL:TME was 1:1.5:1.5:6 for a total salt concentration equivalent to 1.8 M LiTFSI-DME/ DOL/TME.
- For comparison, two other electrolytes were also tested: high-concentration electrolyte (LiTFSI-DME/ DOL, the molar ratio of LiTFSI:DME:DOL is 1:1.5:1.5 for a total salt concentration equivalent to 5.5 M LiTFSI-DME/ DOL) denoted as HCE, and a low-concentration electro- lyte (1M LiTFSI-DME/DOL v: v = 1:1) denoted as LCE.
- Li-Cu batteries were used to calculate the CE in the three systems (Fig. 1).
- The battery using LCE showed a poor ini- tial CE of only 76% (Fig. 1a), and the CE quickly dropped to less than 80% after 40 cycles.
- The initial CE of Li was slightly improved to 82% when HCE was used (Fig. 1b).
- The CE then further increased to 98.5% (Fig.
- S1), while it began to decline after 60 cycles.
- Remarkably, in the TME-based LHCE, the Li metal achieved a high initial CE of 93.16% (Fig. 1c), and the CE reached 99.37% after 100 cycles (Fig. 1d).
- The corresponding average CE of 120 cycles was 98.87%, even higher than those of the common electrolytes for Li-S batteries with the LiNO3 additive.
- On the one hand, the total salt concentration of LHCE (1.8 M) is higher than that of LCE (1 M), which could facilitate the formation of a more robust and stable SEI layer.
- On the other hand, the local concentration of LHCE is 5.5 M, which is also much higher than that in LCE.
- It can be seen that the CE of Li in LHCE improved to 99.13% with only 4 times excess Li.
- When the excess Li was further reduced to 0.5 times, the CE was still 98.35% (Fig.
- S2), indicating that a robust SEI is formed on the Li metal anode.
- The aggregate capacity loss of Li-Cu batteries was also calculated (Fig. 1e).

- Experimental 1.1 Materials Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) were bought from the Sigma-Aldrich Corporation. 1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl methyl ether (TME) was bought from the Aladdin Corporation.
- For the 1M LiTFSI-DME/DOL electrolyte, 1.0 mmol of LiTFSI was dissolved in 0.5 mL DME and 0.5 mL DOL.
- For the 1 LiTFSI-3 DME/DOL electrolyte, 1.0 mmol of LiTFSI was dissolved in 1.5 mmol DME and 1.5 mmol DOL.
- For the 1 LiTFSI-3 DME/DOL-6TME electrolyte, additional 6.0 mmol TME was added to 1 LiTFSI-3 DME/DOL electrolyte.
- The amount of electrolyte was about 30 µL per battery in Li-S batteries and about 60 µL in other batteries.
- The cathode electrode was prepared via mixing S@KB (with 71.2% of sulfur content), Acetylene black (AB) and poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) solution, with a mass ratio of 80:10:10.
- The electrode was then dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 h.
- The mass loading of S was about 0.5 mg cm-2.
- The current density used in the Li-S full battery was 0.5 C.
- The anodic stability of the electrolyte was evaluated by Linear Sweep Voltammetry test, and the scan rate was 1.0 mV s-1. 1.3 Characterizations The obtained electrodes were washed with DME before next analysis.
- The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were received from SU8010 (Hitachi).
