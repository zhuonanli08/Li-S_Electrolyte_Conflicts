- Soc. 2025, 147, 8652-8662 Read Online ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations * sı Supporting Information ABSTRACT: Unclear regulation mechanisms of solid solubility make it difficult to accurately control its concentration, posing challenges for accelerating chemical reactions.
- For example, Guarr and Hickey et al. showed that the introduction of C-H π weak dispersion force interactions in pyridinium- based compounds can weaken intermolecular electrostatic interactions, thus enhancing their solubility in acetonitrile and maximizing the energy density of redox flow battery.11 Therefore, it is very important to precisely control the solid solute concentration to provide an important value for the accurate control of the reaction rate.
- According to classical theories, the dissolution of solid substances is a competitive process between entropy and enthalpy changes.12 During the dissolution process, breaking the interaction between solvents or solutes is an endothermic process, while constructing the interaction bonds between solutes and solvents is an exothermic process, where the enthalpy change is less than zero.

- Importantly, by comparing the two salts, LiTFSI and LiBr, it is discovered that the solvation structure regulated by the two types of anions has a totally different effect on S8 solubility.
- Between them, the solvation structure of LiTFSI with dissociated ion pairs in ether solvents significantly reduces the interaction strength between ether and S8 molecules, while the solvation structure of LiBr with bound ion pairs does not significantly affect the interaction strength between ether and S8 molecules (Figure 1b).
- The theoretical results indicate that the obvious change in S8 solubility is directly related to the threshold value of the interaction energy between S8 and the solvent molecules in complex solutions, while the enthalpy change is the core factor affecting S8 solubility.
- Based on the above discovery, the precise control of S8 and lithium polysulfide solubility in ether- based electrolytes is achieved by adjusting the concentrations and types (LiTFSI, LiBr, and LiNO3) of lithium salt.
- On this basis, a liquid-liquid-solid reaction pathway at the positive electrode of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries is supported (Figure 1c), significantly improving the reaction rate and cell performance.
- Coin-type Li-S batteries with a finite amount of electrolyte (electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 3 µL mgs -1) show a discharge capacity of 1455 mAh g-1, which is the best value to date.
- Moreover, the Li-S pouch cell shows an energy density of 554 Wh kg-1 with 10.77 Ah. ■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION S8 Solubility in a Composite Solution.
- The solubility of solid S8 in ether solvents, 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL), was primarily studied at room temper- ature because ethers are commonly used as electrolyte solvents in Li-S batteries.
- Raman spectra analysis shows that the solubility of S8 (characteristic peaks: 150, 220, and 456 cm-1)16 is 5.1 mg mL-1 in DOL and 3.65 mg mL-1 in DME (Figure S1).
- With the addition of LiTFSI, the solubilities of S8 in DOL solutions containing 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 M LiTFSI are 4.3, 4.0, 3.7, and 1.0 mg mL-1, respectively (Figures 1d and S2).
- It was found that S8 solubility decreased slightly with increasing LiTFSI concentration in the range of 0.2-0.8 M.
- However, the solubility of S8 decreases sharply when the LiTFSI concentration changes from 0.8 to 1.0 M.
- These results indicate that there is a nonlinear solubility phenomenon (Figure 1e).
- We also analyzed S8 solubility in DME solvent with 1.0, 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI), and a nonlinear solubility phenomenon was also found (Figure S3).
- Moreover, in order to further verify the solubility effect of temperature dependence, we tested S8 solubility at 50 °C, which still shows a nonlinear solubility phenomenon, proving its universality (Figure S4).
- To illustrate the mechanism of the nonlinear S8 solubility phenomenon in complex solutions with solvents and salts, we used DOL and CS2 as strongly and weakly polarized solvents, respectively, to explore the effect of the mixed solvent ratio on S8 solubility, in which CS2 and DOL serve as good and poor solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively.
- This shows that S8 solubilities in DOL and CS2 solvents are about ∼5.1 and ∼60.0 mg mL-1 at room temperature, respectively.
- When the molar ratios of CS2 and DOL change to 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, 4:6, 3:7, and 2:8, S8 solubilities are 59, 57, 55, 51, 20, 14, and 12 mg mL-1, respectively.
- This demonstrates that S8 solubility slightly changes with a change in the ratio of CS2:DOL from 1:0 to 5:5.
- However, when the ratio of CS2:DOL changes to 4:6, S8 solubility decreases sharply (20 mg mL-1).
- When the ratio further decreases, the solubility changes slightly (Figure 1f).
- Furthermore, the measured dielectric constant of the DOL/ CS2 solution shows a nearly linear trend with regard to their ratio, again verifying no relationship between them (Figure 1f).
- To check the nonlinear S8 solubility in composite solvents, we used classical molecular dynamics LAMMPS to simulate S8 solubility in DOL/CS2 solvents with different molar ratios.
- This shows that S8 solubility in a pure CS2 solvent and mixed CS2/DOL solvent (8:2) is close to 50 mg mL-1.
- When the ratio is 5:5, S8 is still in the soluble state (50 mg mL-1).
- In contrast, when mixed solvents with a ratio of 2:8 or pure DOL are selected, a large number of S8 molecules precipitate, which is consistent with the experimental results (Figures 1g and S5).
- It is worth emphasizing that this nonlinear solubility phenomenon is also found in other systems, for example, the nonlinear solubility of rosin in mixed solvents of water and ethanol (Figures 1h, S6 and S7).
- For example, the interaction energy for the melting heat of S8 from solid to liquid is 1.7175 kJ mol-1.
- Similarly, the dissolution of the S8 solute in the solvent also requires the input of interaction energy to reduce the interaction forces of S8 molecules in the solid.
- When S8 solute dissolves in solvents, the required interaction energy comes from the van der Waals forces formed between the solvent and S8 molecules.
- For S8 dissolution in CS2-DOL solvents, CS2 and DOL act as good and poor solvents for S8 dissolution, respectively.
- S8 solubility will quickly decrease when the interaction force of the mixed solvents and S8 is weaker than the interaction force of the two S8 molecules.
- According to the constructed model, we obtain the average interaction energy of the extra input to dissolve one S8 molecule (Edis or Edis′, and the calculation details of Edis and Edis′ are shown in Supporting Note 2).
- The average energy released from the interaction of one CS2 molecule and one S8 molecule is Ea, and the energy released from one DOL molecule and one S8 molecule is Eb (Eb < Ea).
- Therefore, if xEa + yEb < Edis (Edis′) is satisfied, S8 will precipitate.
- Herein, x and y are the coordinated numbers of the CS2 and DOL molecules on the S8 molecule.
- According to the calculation results (details in Supporting Note 2), Ea is 3.28 × 10-2 kcal mol-1, Eb is 1.1 × 10-2 kcal mol-1, and Edis is 1.2198 kcal mol-1 (Edis′ = 1.48 kcal mol-1).
- Moreover, the relationship between the average energy of one solute molecule and the molar ratio of the two solvents can be obtained by the equation = + y 2.329 x 1.548 1, and the solubility mutation of S8 in the mixed system of CS2 and DOL will be between the ratio of CS2 to DOL of 3:7 and 4.54:5.45.
- In the experiment, the proportion of solubility mutation measured in the CS2 and DOL mixed system is 4:6, which is just within the proportion range of our theoretical deduction.

- This means that one S8 molecule dissolves into 71 CS2 molecules.
- That is, the average solvation structure of CS2 and S8 can be hypothesized to be a 71-bound structure (71 CS2-S8).
- With CS2 as the pure solvent, the interaction energy released from 71 CS2 molecules and one S8 molecule is 71*Ea (2.329 kcal mol-1).
- Because 71*Ea > Edis (2.329 > 1.2198) and 71*Ea > Edis′ (2.329 > 1.48) are present, the solubility of S8 in the CS2 solvent is high.
- When the ratio of CS2 to DOL is 5:5, the interaction energy released from 35.5 CS2 and 35.5 DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb (1.555 kcal mol-1).
- Because 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis (1.555 > 1.2198) and 35.5*Ea + 35.5*Eb > Edis′ (1.555 > 1.48) are still present, S8 solubility in the CS2/DOL (5:5) solvents is also high (about 51 mgS mL-1), which is a little lower than that in pure CS2.
- In stark contrast, when the ratio of CS2 to DOL is 4:6, the interaction energy released from 28.4 CS2 and 42.6 DOL molecules to one S8 molecule is 1.40 kcal mol-1.
- Because this value is lower than Edis′ (1.40 < 1.48), there is a significant difference in S8 solubility (about 20 mg mL-1), which is much lower than that in CS2 (Figure 1f,g).
- The above estimation of input interaction energy for solute dissolution in a solvent can explain the nonlinear solubility phenomenon well, confirming that the enthalpy change is the core factor affecting S8 solubility in this system (Figures 1i and S8).

- In one previous study of Li-S batteries at the condition of high S loading and low E/S ratio performed by our group,17 we found that the rate of the S ↔Li2S interconversion reaction was controlled by the activation of S8 in the first S8 →Li2S8 step.
- The S8 activation ratio (Sact) can be determined by evaluating the actual and theoretical quantity ratios of the transferred electrons of the S8 →Li2S4 reaction (Sact = Eactual(S8 →Li2S4)/Etheorectical (S8→Li2S4)).
- Moreover, the S8 utilization ratio (Suti) can be determined by evaluating the actual and theoretical quantity ratios of the transferred electrons of the total S8 →Li2S reaction, Suti = Eactual(S8 →Li2S)/Etheorectical(S8 →Li2S).
- The detailed calculation can be found in ref 17.
- Then, we calculated Sact and Suti at different scan rates to quantitatively evaluate the increase of the reaction rate (Figure 2d,e).
- At a low scan rate of 0.05 mV s-1, Sact and Suti values were calculated to be 50 and 50% at 0.8 M LiTFSI, respectively, which are higher than those with 1 M LiTFSI (41.7 and 40%).
- At a scan rate of 0.1 mV s-1, Sact and Suti values (45 and 23%) with 0.8 M LiTFSI are much higher than those with 1 M LiTFSI (32 and 13%).
- These results demonstrate that the electrolyte with 0.8 M LiTFSI can achieve effective S dissolution and thus increase the reaction rate (Figure 2g,h).
- This reaction pathway was further proven by the S8 solubility test on a solid substrate (Figures S14 and S15).
- However, note that the second Pc2 reduction peak with 0.8 M LiTFSI cannot appear well at a scan rate of 0.15 mV s-1, and the Sact and Suti values are also very low.
- This indicates a low reaction rate, which may be related to the limited Li2Sx amount in 0.8 M LiTFSI-based electrolyte during the redox reactions.
- In order to confirm the above processes, we investigated the disproportionation reaction rates of S and Li2S in ether electrolytes with 1.0 and 0.8 M LiTFSI (DOL/DME = 2:1, v/ v).
- Due to the long reaction time and volatilization of the ether solvent, the Li2Sx solution in DOL/DME (1:1) is highly viscous, and it cannot accurately evaluate the disproportiona- tion reaction and Li2Sx solubility at different time scales.
- Therefore, we used DOL/DME solvent at a ratio of 2:1 (v/v) to determine the formation rate of Li2Sx (Figure S16).
- The optical photographs show that the color of the Li2Sx solution with 0.8 M LiTFSI diluted 200 times is darker than that with 1.0 M LiTFSI in the primary state.
- In the ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectra, the intensity of the absorption peak of S4 2- (420 nm) is also higher than that with 1.0 M LiTFSI.
- These results demonstrate that the increase of S8 solubility accelerates the disproportionation reaction and the generation of Li2Sx (Figure S17).
- We further determined Li2Sx concentrations at different time scales, and the higher concentration of Li2Sx solution with 0.8 M LiTFSI was found in the primary 0-16 h.
- However, after 16 h, the concentration of the Li2Sx solution with 1.0 M LiTFSI is higher than that with 0.8 M LiTFSI (Figures 2f and S18).
- It may be due to the fact that more Li+ formed Li3S6 + with Li2S6 when the LiTFSI concentration is 1.0 M.
- In short, due to the insulation property of S/Li2S, the conventional solid-liquid-solid reaction path with limited long-range electron transfer occurs in the electrolyte with a limited amount of unpolarized ether (1 M LiTFSI, Figure 2j).
- In stark contrast, due to the improved S8 solubility in the electrolyte with an increased amount of unpolarized ether (0.8 M LiTFSI), the liquid-liquid-solid reaction pathway with an enhanced long-range electron transfer route is constructed.
- To confirm the liquid-liquid-solid reaction pathway initiated by the increased S8 solubility, we used in situ Raman spectroscopy to probe the cathode reaction process of Li-S batteries.
- At a discharge current density of 1C, the S8 peaks located at 220 and 476 cm-1 appear at the open-circuit voltage (OCV).
- With 1 M LiTFSI in the electrolyte, S8 peaks can still be detected at a discharge voltage of 2.40 V.
- In addition, the weak Li2Sx peaks (398 and 450 cm-1)18 can be detected at 2.35 V, and Li2Sx can still be detected at a discharge voltage of 1.86 V (Figure 3a).
- In contrast, the S8 peak cannot be detected at 2.40 V with 0.8 M LiTFSI in the electrolyte, and Li2Sx peaks can be detected at 2.40 V, much earlier and stronger than those with 1.0 M LiTFSI.
- Moreover, Li2Sx peaks completely disappear at 1.91 V, indicating complete S reduction due to a greatly increased reaction rate (Figure 3c).
- Compared to the discharge capacity of the battery with 1.0 M LiTFSI (746 mAh g-1), the higher value with 0.8 M LiTFSI (928 mAh g-1) also indicates a faster reaction rate (Figure 3b,d).
- Similar to the discharge process, with 1 M LiTFSI, the Li2Sx peaks can be detected at a potential of 2.38 V during the charging process, relating to the high charging potential (Figure 3e).
- No S8 substance was observed at the end of charging, indicating a low reaction rate.
- However, with 0.8 M LiTFSI, a strong Li2Sx peak can be detected at a charging potential of 2.35 V, 30 mV earlier than that with 1 M LiTFSI (Figure 3g).
- Moreover, the S8 peaks appear at 2.80 V.
- This indicates a higher reaction rate of 0.8 M LiTFSI than that of 1 M LiTFSI, consistent with their different charging voltages (Figure 3f,h).
- To further clarify the effect of increased S8 solubility on the reaction pathway, we performed morphological analysis to illustrate the nucleation and growth of Li2S by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

- With the increase of discharge depth, these deposits turn into spherical structures, and most of the electrode surface remains clean (Figure S19d).
