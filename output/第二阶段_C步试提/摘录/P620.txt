## 实验部分
2.1. Preparation of active materials The cathode active material was a carbon-sulfur (C-S) composite prepared by encapsulating sulfur into mesoporous carbon. Sulfur pow der (sulfur sublimed, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) and mesoporous carbon (Cnovel®, Toyo Tanso Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) with an average pore diameter of 5 nm were mixed in a weight ratio of 65:35 (Sulfur:Carbon). The mixture was sealed in a vessel and heated at 155 ◦C for 6 h to obtain the C-S composite, following the method described by Takahashi et al. [5]. The morphology and pore distribution of the pristine mesoporous Cnovel®, as well as the thermal properties of the synthesized C-S composite, are presented in Figure S1 (a-c). 2.2. Morphological and elemental characterization The surface morphology of the prepared C-S composite was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Flex SEM 1000, Hitachi High- Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). To investigate morphological changes dur ing cycling, the cathode and anode were harvested from the cell and analyzed via SEM. Elemental distribution within the C-S composite and the harvested electrodes was visualized using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Furthermore, the fine structure of the C-S com posite was characterized using field-emission scanning electron micro scopy (FE-SEM, SU-9000, Hitachi High-Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). To accurately determine the surface composition distribution of the C-S composite, EDX analysis was performed at a low accelerating voltage of 5 kV. 2.3. Electrolyte preparation Two electrolytes were prepared for this study. The baseline electro lyte (denoted as ELDD) consisted of 1.0 M lithium bis(tri fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Kishida Chemical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) dissolved in a binary solvent mixture of 1,2-dimethoxy ethane (DME, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Saitama, Japan) and 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Saitama, Japan) (1:1 v/v). The novel electrolyte (denoted as ELC14) consisted of 1.0 M LiTFSI dissolved in 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2-methoxyethoxy) ethane (C14, DAIKIN INDUSTRIES, Ltd., Osaka, Japan). The physicochemical properties of the prepared electrolytes, including viscosity, ionic con ductivity, lithium-ion transference number (tLi +) and density at room temperature, are summarized in Table S1 in the Supporting Information. 2.4. Measurement of Li⁺ transference number The tLi + of the electrolytes was determined by measuring the self- diffusion coefficients of the lithium cations (DLi +) and the TFSI⁻ anions (DTFSI - ) using pulsed-field gradient nuclear magnetic resonance (PFG- NMR) spectroscopy. The measurements were performed on a benchtop NMR spectrometer (X-Pulse, Oxford Instruments). The tLi + was calculated based on the ratio of the diffusion coefficient of Li+ to the sum of the diffusion coefficients of the cation and anion (tLi + = DLi + / (DLi + + DTFSI - ) [21]. 2.5. Electrochemical measurements The cathode slurry was prepared by mixing the C-S composite, acetylene black (AB, Denka Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as a conductive additive, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, DKS Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), and styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, JSR Corp., Tokyo, Japan) in a weight ratio of 90:5:3:2. The mixture was dispersed in deionized water to form a homogeneous paste. This paste was cast onto aluminum foil to serve as the cathode. After the slurry was coated onto the current D. Okuda et al. Electrochimica Acta 572 (2026) 149154 2 collector, the electrodes were dried at 60 ◦C under reduced pressure for 12 h. This drying temperature was carefully selected to ensure the complete removal of residual moisture from the aqueous binder (CMC/ SBR) while strictly preventing the unintended volatilization of elemental sulfur, which can occur at higher temperatures under vacuum. The sulfur loading of the cathode was 1.15 mg cm⁻², and the electrode area was 1.13 cm². To strictly isolate and evaluate the intrinsic ability of the C14 solvent to chemically suppress polysulfide dissolution, the electro chemical tests were intentionally conducted under flooded conditions with an excess amount of electrolyte. The specific current density was calculated based on the theoretical capacity of sulfur (1C = 1672 mA g- 1). This approach ensures that the observed suppression of the shuttle effect originates fundamentally from the weakly solvating nature of the C14 molecular design, rather than from physical limitations imposed by solvent starvation (i.e., a lean E/S ratio). The detailed morphological and compositional characterizations of the synthesized C-S composite, including SEM and EDX analyses, are provided in the Supporting In formation (Figure S2). Two-electrode half-cells were assembled using the prepared cathode as the working electrode, lithium foil as the counter electrode, and a polyolefin microporous membrane as the separator, filled with the respective electrolytes. 2.6. Analysis of solvation structure To elucidate the solvation structure of Li⁺ in the electrolytes, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and Raman spectroscopy were employed. FT-IR spectra were recorded using an ALPHA2 spectrometer (Bruker Co., Ltd., MA, USA) equipped with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory. Raman spectra were obtained using a Nanofinder 30® (Tokyo Instruments, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). To investigate the coordination structure of polysulfides in the sol vents, lithium polysulfide solutions were prepared. Li₂S and elemental sulfur were mixed in a 1:7 molar ratio in 30 mL of DME or C14 and stirred for 24 h. The resulting solutions, denoted as Li₂Sₓ/DME and Li₂Sₓ/C14, were analyzed via FT-IR. 2.7. Computational details Quantum chemical calculations were performed to estimate the solvation structure of Li⁺ and the thermodynamic interactions between the solvents and polysulfides. The Conductor-like Screening Model for Real Solvents (COSMO-RS) method was applied using the COSMO-RS module (MOLSIS Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [22,23]. It should be noted that direct geometry optimizations of long-chain polysulfide complexes (e.g., Li₂S₈ with C14) for conventional binding energy calculations were computationally unfeasible, as the initial structures severely diverged and failed to form stable coordination complexes in the simulated environments. Therefore, the probability distribution of the surface charge density, known as the σ-profile (P(σ)), was utilized as an alternative, robust thermodynamic descriptor to evaluate the interaction potential based on the intrinsic molecular charge distributions, bypassing the convergence issues associated with direct cluster optimization. In the COSMO-RS approach, the molecular state in solution is approximated using a conductor screening model to determine the stereostructure and surface charge density (σ). The molecular surface is divided into segments, and the interaction energy is calculated assuming contact between these segments. The σ-profile was derived by integrating the surface segment areas having a specific charge density σ. The coordination structure of the solvent around Li⁺ was estimated based on the obtained σ-profiles and interaction energies. 2.8. Mechanism analysis To clarify the charge-discharge reaction mechanism in the Li-S bat teries using the fluorinated ether solvent, the surface compositions of the sulfur cathode and lithium anode at various states of charge (SOC) were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI Quantes, ULVAC-PHI Inc., Kanagawa, Japan). Depth profiling was performed during XPS measurements using an argon gas cluster ion beam (GCIB) to minimize etching damage. The binding energies were calibrated refer encing the C-H bond peak in the C 1 s spectrum at 284.8 eV. Furthermore, the relationship between the SOC of the sulfur cathode and its electronic structure was investigated using X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS). Measurements were conducted at BL-11 of the Ritsu meikan University Synchrotron Radiation Center (Japan) using a variable-line-spacing plane grating monochromator. Data were collected in both partial fluorescence yield (PFY) and total electron yield (TEY) modes. For post-mortem analyses such as SEM, XPS, and XANES, the cycled cells were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. The elec trodes were thoroughly rinsed with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and then dried at room temperature under reduced pressure for 12 h to remove any volatile residual solvents and electrolyte salts. The obtained X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra were analyzed using the Athena software package [24].

## 正文（含数字的句子与图表注）
- In this study, we demonstrate a novel electrolyte solvent, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2-methoxyethoxy)ethane (C14), designed to suppress Li₂Sₓ dissolution through steric and electronic modulation.
- Electrochemical mea surements revealed that the Li-S cell using the C14-based electrolyte operated reversibly without the infinite charging plateau characteristic of the shuttle effect, which was observed in a conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)-based system.
- Mechanistic investigations combining FT-IR, Raman spectroscopy, and COSMO-RS calcu lations indicated that the electron-withdrawing trifluoromethyl group in C14 causes a delocalization of the molecular surface charge and restricts the conformational flexibility required for Li⁺ chelation.
- Crucially, S K- edge X-ray Absorption Near Edge Structure (XANES) analysis provided direct evidence of the solvation mech anism: while the DME system exhibited a pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV attributed to Li defects generated by Li₂Sₓ ionization, this feature was notably absent in the C14 system.
- These results confirm that C14 effectively prevents the ionization and subsequent leaching of Li₂Sₓ into the electrolyte.
- This work establishes that suppressing solvent-induced ionization via charge delocalization is a vital strategy for realizing stable, high-performance Li-S batteries. 1.
- Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are considered a promising tech nology due to their exceptionally high theoretical energy density [1].
- During the charge-discharge process, the global electro chemical reactions are represented as follows [2]: S8 + 16Li+ + 16e-⇄8Li2S(1)Li⇄Li+ + en- (2) In reaction (1), sulfur undergoes a 16-electron redox process, yielding a high theoretical specific capacity of 1672 mAh g⁻¹.
- Extensive research has been conducted to enhance the conductivity of sulfur cathodes [3-7].
- For instance, Ji et al. reported that a carbon-sulfur composite cathode, featuring sulfur encapsulated within a mesoporous carbon framework, exhibited a high specific capacity of 1200 mAh g⁻¹ [3].
- Despite these advances, the dissolution of intermediate lithium pol ysulfides (Li₂Sₓ, 1 ≤x ≤8) into the electrolyte remains a critical chal lenge.
- The dissolved Li₂Sₓ species migrate between the cathode and anode, causing the "shuttle effect," which severely degrades the cycle life [6].
- To address this, Takahashi et al. demonstrated that confining sulfur within microporous carbon (pore diameter < 2 nm) can suppress the shuttle effect [5].

- Additives such as lithium nitrate (LiNO₃) are known to mitigate the shuttle effect [8-10].
- LiNO₃ decomposes on the cathode surface to form various non-stoichiometric decomposition products (e. g., LiₓNOy), which act as oxidation catalysts to convert dissolved Li₂Sₓ back to elemental sulfur during charging, thereby suppressing the shuttle reaction [9].
- Torii et al. reported that fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)-based electrolytes impede Li₂Sₓ dissolution through the formation of a stable protective layer derived from FEC decomposition [11].
- For instance, high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) [12,13] and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) [14,15] have been widely investigated, as they inherently restrict polysulfide solubility by mini mizing the amount of free solvent molecules.
- Despite these successes in suppressing polysulfide dissolution, a critical factor determining the practical energy density of Li-S batteries is the electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio [16].
- While a low E/S ratio is essential for achieving high energy density, Li-S batteries typically consume more electrolyte through irreversible side reactions compared to conventional lithium-ion batteries, leading to a shortened cycle life under lean-electrolyte conditions [16-18].
- In this study, we focus on a novel fluoroether, 1,1,1-trifluoro-2- (2- methoxyethoxy)ethane (C14), designed by introducing an electron- withdrawing trifluoromethyl (-CF₃) group into the terminal of a con ventional dimethoxyethane (DME) skeleton.
- It is noteworthy that C14 possesses a relatively low density of 1.16 g cm⁻³, which is lower than those of other typical shuttle-suppressing solvents such as FEC (~1.32 g cm⁻³) and sulfolane (~1.26 g cm⁻³).
- While recently reported weakly solvating fluoroethers (WSEs) can suppress polysulfide dissolution, they often rely on specific salts such as LiFSI [19], which is generally considered less compatible with Li-S systems due to potential side reactions with polysulfides.
- Alterna tively, low-molecular-weight mono-ethers (e.g., dipropyl ether or methyl tert-butyl ether) that exhibit weakly solvating properties suffer from excessively low boiling points [20].
- C14 was conceptually designed to overcome this structural dilemma.
- By integrating a single -CF₃ group exclusively at the terminal of the highly flexible DME back bone, C14 strategically balances three critical requirements within a single molecule: (1) sufficient solvating power to dissolve 1.0 M LiTFSI, (2) the delocalization of molecular surface charge to suppress poly sulfide ionization, and (3) a practically viable boiling point of 104 ◦C, which enables stable vacuum filling procedures.
- We investigate the electrochemical properties of Li-S batteries using C14-based electrolytes and evaluate their effectiveness in suppressing Li₂Sₓ dissolution while maintaining a relatively low-density compared to alternative functional electrolytes profile. 3.

- Solvation structure in C14-based electrolytes To elucidate the solvation structures of Li⁺ ions in the ELC14 and ELDD electrolytes, FT-IR and Raman spectroscopy measurements were performed on LiTFSI/C14 and LiTFSI/DME solutions with varying salt concentrations.
- Fig. 1(a) and 1(b) show the IR spectra of LiTFSI/DME and LiTFSI/ C14 solutions with concentrations of 0, 0.5, and 1.0 mol l⁻¹.
- In the spectrum of pure DME (Fig. 1(a)), a peak at roughly 1100 cm⁻¹, corre sponding to the C-O-C asymmetric stretching vibration, was observed.
- Upon adding LiTFSI (0.5 and 1.0 mol l⁻¹), the intensity of this peak decreased, while new peaks appeared at approximately 1080 and 1140 cm⁻¹.
- Furthermore, in the LiTFSI/DME spectra, a peak attributed to C-H bending vibrations appeared at roughly 870 cm⁻¹, while the peak intensity at roughly 850 cm⁻¹ decreased as the LiTFSI concentration increased.
- It has been re ported that the spectral shape in this region depends on the conforma tion of the diether structure [25,26].
- The variations in peak intensities at 870, 850, and 820 cm⁻¹ with solution concentration suggest that DME undergoes a conformational change upon coordinating with Li⁺.
- Fig. 1(b) presents the IR spectra of the LiTFSI/C14 solutions.
- A peak at approximately 1070 cm⁻¹ was observed to increase in intensity with increasing electrolyte concentration.
- This peak is attributed to the S-N-S group contained in the LiTFSI electrolyte salt [27].
- Furthermore, similar to the DME system, the peak at roughly 1100 cm⁻¹ (C-O-C asymmetric stretching) decreased in intensity as the solution concentration increased, suggesting that the C-O-C structure in C14 coordinates to Li⁺.
- However, unlike DME, the spectral shape in the 850-870 cm⁻¹ region remained almost unchanged regardless of the solution concentration.
- This result suggests that the conformation of C14 does not change significantly when coordinating to Li⁺ [28].
- From these results, it is considered that while C14 has a solvation structure similar to DME, its coordination geometry with Li⁺ differs from that of DME.
- Fig. 1(c) shows the Raman spectra of DME and LiTFSI/DME solu tions.
- The peak intensity at roughly 870 cm⁻¹ increased with increasing solution concentration.
- These concentration-dependent changes in peak intensity likely arise from conformational changes rather than shifts in polarity [29].

- In contrast, the Li₂Sₓ/C14 mixture appeared as a yellow suspension, suggesting that Li₂Sₓ is sparingly sol uble in the C14 solvent.

- According to theoretical and experimental XANES studies [33,40-42], this pre-edge feature origi nates from the creation of unoccupied orbitals on sulfur atoms when Li⁺ ions are dissociated or extracted.
- Consequently, the remaining solid poly sulfides with these Li-defects are considered to exhibit an electronic state analogous to that of solvated polysulfides in the liquid phase, where the disruption of local coordination generates unoccupied orbitals on the sulfur atoms, manifesting as the pre-edge peak at 2470.3 eV.

- Furthermore, the intensity of the pre-edge at 2470.3 eV was higher at 100% DOD compared to 50% DOD.
- This result suggests that the number of defects in Li₂S₄ contained in the cathode at 100% DOD increased compared to that at 50% DOD.
- In contrast, for the Li-SELC14 system (Fig. 7(c) and (d)), during the discharge process from 50% to 100% DOD, the intensities of peaks assigned to Li₂S₄ and Li₂S₃ decreased, while those assigned to Li₂S₂ and Li₂S increased.
- During the subsequent charging process from 100% DOD to 50% SOC, the peak intensities for Li₂S and Li₂S₃ decreased, whereas the peak for Li₂S₂ increased.
- From 50% SOC to the end of charge, the peaks assigned to Li₂S₂, Li₂S₃, and Li₂S₄ decreased, and the intensity of the peak assigned to S₈ increased.
- The spectral shape at the end of charge was confirmed to be similar to that at 15% DOD.
- Notably, the pre-edge feature at 2470.3 eV was absent in the Li- SELC14 spectra throughout the charge-discharge process.
- This absence strongly suggests that the weakly solvating C14 solvent does not strongly interact with the Li⁺ ions in the polysulfides.
- Thus, it is considered that C14 cannot extract Li⁺ to create vacancies, effectively maintaining the polysulfides in an undissolved solid state without generating such unoccupied sulfur orbitals.
- This finding is consistent with the COSMO-RS and IR results discussed previously, which showed that the delocalized surface charge and steric hindrance of C14 prevent effective coordination with Li₂Sₓ.
- It implies that the electrochemical reaction proceeds more readily at the particle surface than in the bulk, which explains why the practical specific capacities of Li-SELDD (1200 mAh g⁻¹) and Li-SELC14 (1000 mAh g⁻¹) are lower than the theoretical maximum (1672 mAh g⁻¹).
