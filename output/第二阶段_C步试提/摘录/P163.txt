## 实验部分
Electrolyte and cathode preparation The baseline electrolyte (BE) is 6 M LiFSI (99%, Oakwood Chem- icals) in DME (99.5%, anhydrous, Sigma Aldrich). The salt was dried at 95 C under Ar to remove trace water before use. The mixture of salt and solvent was stirred for 3 h. The diluent, TFTFE (99%, SynQuest Labs), was added to the as-prepared BE (BE/ TFTFE, v/v 1:2 or 1:5) to prepare hybrid electrolytes, HE-2 and HE-5, respectively. S@PAN cathode was synthesized as previously reported [42]. Briefly, acrylonitrile (99%, Sigma Aldrich) and sublimed sulfur (99.98%, Sigma Aldrich) were mixed in a mass ratio of 1:4 and ball milled for an hour. Homogeneously mixed powder was annealed at 450 C for 3 h under N2 at a ramping rate of 5 C min1. A hybrid cathode powder was synthesized in three steps and all moisture/oxygen sensitive syntheses were conducted in a tube-furnace housed in a glovebox under Ar: (1) Li2S@graphene nanocapsules were synthesized as reported [43]. Briefly, lithium foil (0.38 mm in thickness, Alfa Aesar) was placed in the tube fur- nace, and CS2 liquid in a flask was bubbled by Ar to form a mix- ture gas containing CS2 vapor. This gas passed through the quartz tube as ramping up the temperature to 650 C, and this temperature was held for five hours, followed by naturally cool- ing down to room temperature. (2) The obtained Li2S@graphene was finely ground using mortar/pestle, then it was gently pressed to form a pellet. The Zn precursor suspension, a mixture of 50 mg zinc acetylacetonate and 5 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF), was dropped on the pellet, which was dried under vacuum for 5 h to remove solvent before it was transferred to a tube furnace. The pow- der was collected after the pellet was naturally cooled down. (3) VS2 was prepared by the method as reported [44]. Briefly, 1.2 g Na3VO412H2O and thioacetamide were mixed in de-ionized water, followed by heating up to 160 C for 24 h in a Teflon auto- clave. Once it was naturally cooled down, it was centrifuged and rinsed with de-ionized water and ethanol several times before use. Electrode preparation For the S@PAN electrode, slurry was made by mixing the active material, conductive carbon (C-45), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVdF) binder at a mass ratio of 80:10:10. A hybrid cathode slurry was prepared by mixing the VS2, ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, C- 45, and PVdF at a mass ratio of 45:45:5:5 with N-Methyl-2- Pyrrolidone (NMP) as a solvent. Slurry was casted on the carbon-coated Al foil (15 mm thickness, MTI) and dried at 65 C overnight. The dried electrode film was punched into circular disks with an area of 0.76 cm2. Cell fabrication The 2032-type coin cells were used for Li||Cu cell and Li||Li cells. In a Li||Cu cell, Li metal foil was used as the counter and refer- ence electrode, and Cu foil (MTI) was used as working electrode. In both cells, regular polypropylene separators (25 mm, Celgard 2400) were employed for HE-2, whereas hydrophilic polypropy- lene separators (25 mm, Celgard 3501) were applied for BE to enhance wettability. All cell fabrication was conducted inside Ar-filled glovebox, where O2 and H2O concentrations are below 10 ppm. Materials characterization A home-built wavelength-tunable femtosecond stimulated Raman spectroscopy (FSRS) setup [45,46] was used to collect the experimental Raman spectra on the basis of a femtosecond (fs) Ti:sapphire laser amplifier system (Mantis-5 and Legend Elite-USP-1 K-HE, Coherent, Inc.). Fundamental output of 800 nm center wavelength, 35 fs pulse duration, 4 mJ pulse energy, and 1 kHz repetition rate were applied. SEM images of the deposited/cycled Li electrodes were obtained with Quanta 600 FEG SEM with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Electrochemical tests A Maccor battery tester (Maccor) was used to perform all electro- chemical coin-cell tests including plating/stripping of Li for the Li||Cu systems and galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) for half-cell and full cell tests in coin cells. For plating, a current den- sity of 0.5 mA cm2 was applied for 2 h before stripping at a same current density until the potential reached 1.0 V. In a Li||Li sym- metric cell test, current densities of 0.5 mA cm2, 1.0 mA cm2, and 2.0 mA cm2 were applied for 20 cycles with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm2. Then it was switched back to 0.5 mA cm2 for another 20 cycles. Theoretical calculations AIMD calculations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio simulation Package (VASP) [47,48]. Generalized gradient approx- imation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [49] exchange-correlation functional was used, in conjunction with the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials. Initial geometries of solutions were generated by randomly mixing salt and solvent molecules using the Packmol software [50]. Number of atoms, volume of a virtual cell, and temperature at 363 K (NVT) were maintained during the calculation. An energy cutoff of 500 eV with a Monhrost-Pack [51] reciprocal space grid of 1  1  1 k-points scheme was used. The time step was 1 fs, and the snapshot at 13 ps was used to study Li-solvation structure analysis. The radial distribution function (RDF) was generated using VMD [52].

- Herein, we employ 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether as a co-solvent in the electrolyte of Li-S batteries to meet the demands.
- The co-solvent results in an average Coulombic efficiency of 99.4% for lithium plating/stripping cycles.
- Full cells with a capacity ratio of 2 between lithium anode and the S@PAN cathode (3 mgs cm2) exhibited a stable cycle life over 100 cycles at an electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 2 mL mg1, validating the high Coulombic efficiency of the lithium anode and demonstrating the compatibility of the electrolyte with both electrodes.
- To enhance the energy density, we prepared a hybrid cathode composed of 45 wt.% VS2 mixed with ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene as the cathode.
- Based on the mass of both electrodes and the electrolyte, the full cell delivers an energy density of 483 Wh kg1, which demonstrates viable Li-S batteries with the lean- electrolyte.
- Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a promising next- generation high-energy battery technology [1].
- Recently, a plethora of examples of high-performing Li-S cells have been demonstrated [2-4].
- However, commercialization of Li-S batter- ies still confronts several major technical challenges, including the shuttling of polysulfide species [5], rapid capacity fading of the sulfur cathode under lean-electrolyte conditions [6], and the low Coulombic efficiency (CE) of plating/stripping of the lithium metal anode (LMA) [7], where the latter two issues can be the real showstoppers but have received meager effort, to date [8-13].
- Remarkable advances have been made in polysulfide immobi- lization by, for example, intricate carbon/sulfur nano-composites [14,15], coated separators [16,17], and highly concentrated elec- trolytes [18,19].
- These strategies are efficacious in electrolyte- flooded Li-S systems; however, the superlative results often fail to be translated under the lean-electrolyte conditions, where the sulfur cathode accentuates much lower capacity and fast fad- ing [20].
- Note that the inconveniently necessary but extravagant usage of electrolyte tanks the specific energy of the Li-S cells and raises the manufacturing cost [21,22].
- Unfortunately, conventional electrolytes used by the LIB industry is unstable with LMA, both thermodynamically and kinetically, resulting in continuous corrosion of LMA and the formation of lithium dendrites [7].

- Most recently, strides of progress have been made to enhance the performance of sulfur electrodes under lean-electrolyte con- ditions, i.e., at an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 5 (mL mg1) or below [25-28].
- Hollow current collectors [29], swellable sulfur cathode [30], and ZnS-coating [31] were employed for the enhanced electrolyte uptake.
- For instance, dimethyl disulfide is used as a co-solvent for a higher discharge capacity [32]; catholyte com- prising Li2S6 facilitated electrolyte permeation into thick sulfur electrodes [33]; a salt of NH4TFSI served as a redox enhancer to alleviate Li2S passivation, thus promoting the S/Li2S utilization [20].
- Electrocatalysts such as vacancy-rich Ni3FeN were utilized to improve surface reactions of sulfide species [34].
- Recently, multiple localized high concentration elec- trolytes (LHCE) were introduced [35-39].
- For instance, Nazar and coworkers developed a LHCE that contained 1,1,2,2- tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (HFE) in diglyme (G2), which delivered a CE of 96.2% for LMA, and in this elec- trolyte the S/CMK-3 cathode exhibited 100 cycles at an E/S ratio of 5 at 55 C [35].
- Liang and coworkers used a mixture electrolyte comprising hexa-fluoroisopropyl methyl ether (HFME), ethylene carbonate (EC), and diethyl carbonate (DEC), which achieved a CE of 99.5% for LMA and 50 cycles for the S/Ketjen black cath- ode at an E/S ratio of 1.5 [36].
- In a similar fashion, Wang and coworkers employed a mixture of 1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl-1 ,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether (OFE) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with the volume ratio of 95:5 and an E/S ratio of around 4.8, where the CE of LMA reached 99.3%, and the sulfur cathode retained a discharge capacity of 775 mAh g1 at the 150th cycle with the active mass loading of 1-2 mgs cm2 [37].
- Herein, we report a new electrolyte formula for Li-S batteries under lean conditions, where 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,2- trifluoroethyl ether (TFTFE, Scheme 1) is employed as a co- solvent added to the electrolyte of 6 M lithium bis(fluorosul- fonyl)imide (LiFSI) in DME.
- TFTFE outperforms other recently reported fluorinated ethers in safety, and its cost and purity are competitive (Table S1).
- TFTFE was used as an electrolyte for Li- S batteries by Liu and co-workers owing to the its unique inert- ness against polysulfides [40].
- TFTFE was later reported as a co- solvent mixed with the electrolytes of fluorinated carbonates in a lithium metal battery with a NMC811 cathode [41].
- In this study, we demonstrate that the TFTFE/DME/LiFSI electrolyte is highly compatible with LMA, where the average plating/strip- ping CE reaches 99.4% over 350 cycles, and the sulfur cathode delivers stable cycling performance under the lean-electrolyte conditions with E/S ratios of as low as 2.
- When a S@PAN cathode and a limited mass of LMA with the anode/cathode capacity (N/P) ratio of 2 are assembled in full cells with the lean electrolyte at an E/S ratio of 2, the full cell with the S@PAN cathode delivered 100 cycles without a glitch.
- In addition, pairing the electrolyte with a hybrid sulfur cathode that has 45 wt.% vanadium disulfide (VS2) added into a cathode of ZnS-coated Li2S@graphene, the same full cell configuration demonstrated one of the highest energy densities of 483 Wh kg1 based on the mass of both and electrolyte.
- Results and discussion We set 6 M LiFSI in DME as the baseline electrolyte (BE) and mixed it with TFTFE in two volume ratios, v/v 1:2 and 1:5, result- ing in two hybrid electrolytes, referred to as HE-2 and HE-5.
- To learn the interactions between the electrolyte species, we conducted FSRS measurements (Figure 1a).
- Pure DME exhibits integrated vibra- tion modes at 820 and 850 cm1 that are ascribed to the free ethylene oxide [53], and pure TFTFE shows vibrations at FIGURE 1 Coordination structures of electrolytes.

- For a controlled elec- trolyte of 2 M LiFSI solvated in DME, the peak at 717 cm1 indicates the S-N-S stretching vibration mode of FSI [56], which shifts to 735 cm1 when the concentration of LiFSI increases to 6 M in BE due to more prolific pairing between FSI and Li+ [57].
- At the same time, the DME peak also shifts from 827 cm1 to 867 cm1 due to the enhanced interaction of DME with Li+ while the stretch of free DME molecules vanishes.
- A peak separation was observed between 825 cm1 and 875 cm1 for 2 M LiFSI in DME, which is due to the mixed contributions from different C-O-C vibration modes.
- When TFTFE is introduced to BE in differ- ent volume ratios, the peak of FSI stays at 735 cm1 regardless of the different ratios, indicating the consistent chemical envi- ronment of FSI ions irrespective of the volumes of TFTFE added.
- Likewise, the characteristic peaks of TFTFE at 648 cm1 and 850 cm1 remain non-shifted in HE-2 and HE-5 compared to its pure liquid, suggesting that TFTFE, indeed, serves as a diluent, not playing any solvation role with ions in the electrolytes.
- AIMD was conducted to further understand the coordination structures for the electrolytes of BE and HE-2.
- Then, the unit cell was relaxed for 13 ps, in which the time between 10 ps and 15 ps is deemed sufficient to use for analysis (Figure S1).
- Besides, we applied a higher energy cutoff of 600 eV for a good descrip- tion of electron wavefunction [58,59].
- Figure 1b illustrates the two distinctive first-coordination shells of Li+ sorted out of the entire simulation cell.
- Li+ in both electrolytes is bound with three to four oxygens from DME and FSI, where the first coordination shells are analogous to the previously reported solvation struc- tures with inert co-solvents [58,60].
- To be specific, a high con- centration of LiFSI renders Li+ more likely to bind with FSI rather than DME, and the oxygen atoms of TFTFE in HE-2 does not participate in the Li+ solvation.
- Furthermore, the radial distribution function (RDF) and the quantitative analysis of Li-O bonds also suggest that TFTFE func- tions as a diluent without involving in the coordination chem- istry (Figure S2 and Table S2).

- It gradually rose to 99.6% by the 30th cycle with a reduced overpotential of 25 mV and 25 mV upon plating and stripping, respectively.
- In contrast, BE exhibited an erratic behavior of plating/stripping, as shown in Figure 2b.
- Initial plating/stripping overpotentials of BE were 54 mV and 45 mV, respectively, and the first CE was 93.7%, which gently increased to 97.7% by the 10th cycle, but it started to fluctuate during the following cycling.
- Especially at the 26th cycle, a severe overcharge was observed with the attained capacity of 1.4 mAh cm2, 40% more than the plated amount in the same cycle.

- Furthermore, we tested CE of LMA in HE-2 at 2.0 mA cm2, a quadrupled current density, where dendrites may more likely form (Figure 2c).
- LMA exhibited an increased extent of polariza- tion with an initial overpotential of 190 mV that stabilized to 130 mV after 30 cycles, which is a marginally lower overpotential than the heavily fluorinated electrolytes (FEC/FEMC/HFE) and the engineered Li2S8/LiNO3 electrolyte, but slightly higher than that of using 10 M LiFSI in EC/DMC and the ternary-salt of LiFSI-LiTFSI-LiNO3 [41,61-63].
- The cell showed stable potential profiles with the CE increased to 98.7% at the 30th cycle.
- We tested CE over 350 cycles, and the arithmetic mean CE values are 99.4% and 99.3%, at 0.5 mA cm2 and 2.0 mA cm2, respec- tively, in HE-2 (Figure 2d).
- The cycling stability of LMA in BE and HE-2 was also investi- gated in Li||Li symmetric cells at various current densities.
- As observed, BE displays a greater overpotential upon plating and stripping, i.e., 31 mV, than that of HE-2, 15 mV, at 0.5 mA cm2.
- The stability advantage of HE-2 is more evident at higher current densities of 1.0 mA cm2 and 2.0 mA cm2.
- The results suggest the utility of TFTFE in protecting the surface of lithium metal (Figure 2e and f).
- After 20 cycles of plat- ing/stripping at 0.5 mA cm2 with a capacity of 1.0 mAh cm2 in Li||Li symmetric cells, LMA in HE-2 displays smooth surface with tightly bound Li grains, whereas BE shows relatively smaller Li islands with conspicuous cracks, where neither of the two elec- trolytes grows dendrites on LMA’s surface, as shown in Figure 3a and b.
- The ratios of Li-OFSI-, Li-ODME, and Li-OTFTFE suggested by RDF analysis (Table S2) show that Li+ prefers to bind FSI over DME in both BE and in HE-2, which suggests that FSI may be preferably reduced on the LMA surface than DME.
- We studied the SEI compositions by depth-profiling X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on the cycled LMA with Ar+ sputtering that removes the top layer up to 10 nm thick beneath the surface (Figure 3c).
- The N 1s spectra reveal the degree of FSI reduction on the surface of LMA in electrolytes.
- The peaks at 396.8 eV and 399.1 eV can be attributed to Li-N and N-SOx, respectively, and these signals could still be found at 10 nm deep in LMA for HE-2.
- However, those peaks were not detectable for BE when the top SEI layer was removed by 5 nm thick, which indicates the preferential FSI reduction on LMA in HE-2.

- Clearly, TFTFE resulted in those dis- parities in two folds: (1) fluorinated ethers generally exhibit lower LUMO levels compared to methyl or partially fluorinated ethers [64]; therefore, preferred defluorination of TFTFE is par- tially responsible for a stronger LiF peak on the LMA albeit it does not participate in the Li+ solvation; (2) Rapid TFTFE decomposi- tion coupled with the formation of anion radicals at the Li sur- face triggers electron transfer to reduce FSI anions as the SEI film grows, which was recently reported by Balbuena and co- workers [65].
- We cycled S@PAN||LMA half cells at the E/S ratio of 5 with excessive LMA and a sulfur loading of 2 mg cm2, where the current rate is 0.1 C (1 C = 1671 mA g1) during first seven cycles before the current rate was switched to 0.2 C for the rest of cycling.

- The advantage of using TFTFE as a co-solvent also lies on its unique physical properties (Table S5), particularly the flammabil- ity resistance, owing to its high flash point.

- The addition of TFTFE as a co-solvent to LiFSI/DME promotes the formation of LiF- abundant SEI, where the LMA exhibits a high CE of 99.4%.
- When the new electrolyte, i.e., HE-2, is paired with S@PAN cathode at a low E/S ratio of 2 and a N/P ratio of 2, the full cell can be cycled for 100 cycles.
- To increase the energy density, we tested this electrolyte with a hybrid cathode, where 45 wt.% VS2 is mixed with the composite of ZnS- Li2S@graphene, which yielded an energy density of 483 Wh kg1 based on the mass of both electrodes and electrolyte.
