- RESEARCH ARTICLE www.advmat.de Macromolecular Boron-Based Salt Enables Dense Interphases for Long-Cycling Lithium-Sulfur Batteries Dejie Qu, Tao Liu,* Youlong Sun, Yuewei Yan, Chuanchuan Li, Zili Cui,* Chuanwei Gao, Shuaice Kong, Zengqi Zhang, Zhiming Liu,* Shu Zhang, Shitao Wang, Zhaolin Lv, Gaojie Xu, Guicun Li,* and Guanglei Cui* Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries represent a compelling next-generation energy storage system with practical energy densities exceeding 700 Wh kg-1, oﬀering a promising pathway beyond current lithium-ion technology.
- The LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits superior lithium metal compatibility, achieving a high coulombic eﬃciency of 99.59%.
- Consequently, Li-S cells demonstrate markedly improved capacity retention from 50.9% to 75.7% over 200 cycles.
- This strategy has been successfully scaled to Ah-level Li-S pouch cells, achieving practical energy densities of 408 Wh kg-1 with stable cycling over 75 cycles.
- Li College of Materials Science and Engineering Qingdao University of Science and Technology Qingdao 266042, P.
- Introduction Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have great potential to achieve practical energy den- sities exceeding 700 Wh kg-1, a crucial threshold for enabling heavy-duty vehicles and electric flight.[1,2] However, the intrinsic precipitation-dissolution conversion mech- anism of the S cathode generates solu- ble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) as essen- tial intermediates, which inevitably trig- ger the notorious shuttle eﬀect and subse- quent parasitic reactions between lithium (Li) metal anodes and LiPSs.
- Under prac- tical cell design conditions (high sulfur loading, lean electrolyte, and ultrathin Li metal), the accelerated electrolyte degrada- tion, primarily stemming from these para- sitic reactions at the Li metal surface, fun- damentally degrades the cycling stability of Li-S batteries.[3,4] Therefore, designing a robust solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) to stabilize Li metal anodes against LiPS- induced deterioration plays a crucial role in realizing long-cycling Li-S batteries.[5,6] The electrolyte composition and its derived SEI fundamen- tally govern the cycling stability of Li-S batteries.
- Notably, the strategic incorporation of LiNO3 has demonstrated remarkable eﬀectiveness in sup- pressing the polysulfide shuttle eﬀect, enabling Li-S batteries to achieve CEs exceeding 99%.[7] However, the limited solu- bility of LiNO3 and its progressive decomposition during cy- cling compromise the long-term cycling performance stability of Li-S batteries, particularly under low electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratios.[8] Lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) demonstrate en- hanced compatibility with Li metal anodes due to the forma- tion of LiF-rich SEI.
- Nevertheless, the S═O structure in LiTFSI molecules acts as an electrophilic center susceptible to nucle- ophilic attack from terminal sulfide species (S-) present in lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn).[9] This mechanistic understanding explains why many LiF-forming sulfonate-based lithium salts prove incompatible as electrolyte components in Li-S batteries.
- Mater. 2025, 37, e05762 © 2025 Wiley-VCH GmbH e05762 (1 of 11) www.advancedsciencenews.com www.advmat.de facilitate a robust SEI while maintaining chemical stability with Li2Sn emerges as a critical challenge for advancing stable Li-S batteries.
- This interfacial structure fundamentally dictates the result of interphasial chemistry; notably, the negatively charged electrode surface electrostatically repels anions from the inner Helmholtz layer, significantly impeding the formation of critical inorganic SEI components.[10] To address these challenges, vari- ous electrolyte engineering strategies have been developed to es- tablish favorable interphases, including high-concentration elec- trolytes (HCEs)[11] and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs).[12] However, these approaches often compromise sul- fur utilization and adversely aﬀect the discharge voltage plateau.
- No- tably, LiFPB (682 g mol-1) possesses a significantly higher spe- cific charge (682/280) (2.43 times that of TFSI-) and a substan- tially lower LUMO energy level, facilitating the formation of a robust SEI enriched with LiF and LiBxOy species.
- The LiFPB- containing electrolyte exhibits remarkable compatibility with Li metal anodes, achieving an unprecedented coulombic eﬃciency of 99.59%.
- This superior interfacial stability translates into ex- ceptional performance in practical applications: an Ah-level Li-S pouch cell employing the LiFPB-containing electrolyte delivers a practical energy density of 408 Wh kg-1 while maintaining stable cycling performance over 75 cycles.
- This novel lithium salt paves the way for boosting the development of practical Li-S batteries. 2.
- The LiFPB salt with a molecular weight of 682 g mol-1 (Figure 1a) was synthesized via a one-pot reaction of lithium borohydride (LiBH4) with perfluoropinacol in a molar ratio of 1:2, using tetrahydro- furan (THF) as the solvent at 60 °C (Figure S1, Supporting In- formation).
- This approach minimizes the introduction of impu- rities, as the entire process is conducted in an organic solvent environment, thereby reducing the need for additional purifica- tion steps.[13,14] The peak observed at m/z = 674.95 in the mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Figure 1b) aligns precisely with the theoretical molecular weight of the FPB-anion.
- A single distinct peak was observed in the 11B NMR spectrum at 11.40 ppm (Figure 1c) and in the 19F NMR spectrum at -69.24 ppm (Figure 1d), with no ad- ditional peak indicative of impurities.
- Upon incorporating 0.2 M LiFPB, 0.8 M LiTFSI, and 1 wt.% LiNO3 into a solution comprising DME and DOL at a volumetric ratio of 1:1, the 11B NMR spectrum retains its char- acteristic peak at 11.40 ppm (Figure S2a, Supporting Informa- tion), while the 19F NMR spectrum exhibits two peaks at -69.24 and -79.80 ppm (Figure S2b, Supporting Information), the lat- ter peak attributed to LiTFSI.[15] In the 13C NMR spectrum, two distinct sets of carbon signals are observed, corresponding to the C─O and -CF3 groups in the FPB-anion (Figure S3, Supporting Information).
- We conducted a series of reactions between poly- sulfide solutions and both LiFPB and LiTFSI (Figure S4, Sup- porting Information).
- Upon analysis of the mixture containing LiTFSI and Li2S6, a notable shift in the characteristic 19F NMR of LiTFSI was observed, migrating from -79.8 to -79.52 ppm (Figure S5a, Supporting Information).
- Concurrently, a distinct by-product emerged at -75.39 ppm (Figure S5b, Supporting In- formation).
- In stark contrast, when LiFPB was combined with Li2S6, the characteristic 19F NMR peak of LiFPB exhibited re- markable stability, remaining invariant.
- This observation under- scores the exceptional resistance of LiFPB to nucleophilic attack by polysulfides, thereby establishing its superior compatibility with these compounds (Figure S6, Supporting Information).
- The low reactivity may be due to the high steric hindrance of high fluorine groups and the low valence state of boron (+3).
- Notably, the FPB-anion (4.55 eV) exhibits a significantly lower LUMO energy level compared to the TFSI-anion (4.75 eV) (Figure 1e), indicating enhanced thermodynamic favorabil- ity toward electrochemical reduction and subsequent SEI forma- tion.

- In Li||Li symmetric cells, the LiFPB- containing electrolyte demonstrated remarkable interfacial sta- bility, exhibiting consistently low and uniform polarization voltage profiles throughout extended cycling exceeding 500 h (Figure 2a).
- In contrast, symmetric cells employing conventional electrolyte formulations displayed progressively increasing polar- ization voltages after 380 h (Figure 2b), indicative of substantial interfacial resistance accumulation.[16] The cycling stability of Li metal was further assessed using Li||Cu asymmetric cells.
- Un- der controlled electrodeposition conditions (1 mAh cm-2 capac- ity at 0.5 mA cm-2 current density), cells with conventional elec- trolyte maintained >90% coulombic eﬃciency (CE) for merely 35 cycles, subsequently deteriorating to below 65% after 100 cycles (Figure 2c,e).
- Conversely, cells incorporating LiFPB-containing electrolyte sustained exceptional CE (>98.5%) throughout 100 cycles.
- Cumulative analysis of irreversible lithium consumption over 100 cycles demonstrated that conventional electrolyte for- mulations depleted 14.74-fold (14.89 mAh/cm2/1.01 mAh/cm2) more lithium metal compared to the LiFPB-containing elec- trolyte (Figure 2d), demonstrating the superior interfacial com- patibility of LiFPB with Li metal anodes.[17] Aurbach’s measurement protocol showed that the Li||Cu cell with LiFPB-containing electrolyte achieved a CE of 99.59%, significantly outperforming the conventional elec- trolyte (97.73%) (Figure 2f).

- Conse- quently, LiFPB incorporation substantially enhances the electrochemical stability and compatibility of Li-metal batteries.[20,21] X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling was performed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the en- hanced cycling stability of Li metal in the presence of LiFPB salt.
- The Li metal anode cycled in LiFPB-containing electrolyte demonstrated substantially elevated fluorine-containing species intensity compared to those cycled in conventional electrolyte (Figure 3a,b).
- The coexistence of B─F bonds with other inor- ganic components (such as LiF and Li2O) facilitates the develop- ment of a moderately stable structure with well-defined lithium- ion conduction pathways.
- Survey spectra (Figure S9, Support- ing Information) reveal a peak at ≈688.3 eV, corresponding to C─F bonds, which arise from intermediate products formed during the decomposition of LiFPB and LiTFSI on the lithium surface.
- Additionally, LiF (≈684.7 and ≈100.5 eV) was consis- tently detected throughout the sputtering depth.

- Detailed spectroscopic analysis of the B 1s core-level spectrum (Figure 3c) confirmed the formation of boron-oxygen (B─O) coor- dination structures, which serve as strategically positioned elec- trophilic sites for preferential coordination with TFSI-anions.
- The selective anion immobilization mechanism eﬀectively mit- igates electrolyte decomposition through the formation of a ro- bust anode-electrolyte interface.[22,23] Comparative analysis re- vealed that lithium metal electrodes cycled in conventional elec- trolytes demonstrated progressive interfacial degradation, char- acterized by the time-dependent accumulation of electrolyte de- composition byproducts.
- X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth profiling identified the formation of organic carbonyl (C═O) and alkoxy (C─O) species, along with inorganic lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) compounds, whose concentration gradients exhibited a strong correlation with sputtering duration (Figure S10, Sup- porting Information).
- In our TOF-SIMS analysis, a substantial signal for Li2F+ was detected.
- Li2F+ was commonly employed to identify the pres- ence of LiF, thereby indicating that the electrolyte containing LiFPB is conducive to the formation of an SEI layer enriched with LiF.
- Notably, the LiF distribution exhibited both enhanced con- centration and improved spatial homogeneity (Figure 3e) across the electrode interface, with these beneficial characteristics be- ing maintained throughout the etching profile (Figure S11, Sup- porting Information).
- These borate- containing species serve as rapid lithium-ion conductors within the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI),[24-27] facilitating the uni- form deposition of Li metal (Figure 3f).
- Depth-resolved chemi- cal characterization demonstrated significantly enhanced reten- tion of intact TFSI-anions at the electrode interface in LiFPB- containing electrolytes (Figure S11, Supporting Information),[25] indicative of suppressed electrolyte decomposition pathways.
- This electrochemical stabilization originates from the formation of an ionically conductive and mechanically robust SEI layer, which eﬀectively reduces the direct contact between the elec- trode and the electrolyte and minimizes parasitic reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface (Figures S12,S13, Supporting Information).
- This optimized interfacial structure com- prises an outer organic-rich matrix and an inner inorganic- rich domain with substantial LiF enrichment (Figure 3g; Figure S14, Supporting Information).
- Consequently, the LiFPB-containing system demonstrates significantly improved cycling stability and rate capability.[28] To systematically assess the redox stability of the battery in- corporating LiFPB, the exchange current density (I0) was uti- lized as a quantitative metric for surface diﬀusion resistance (Figure 4a).
- Upon introduction of LiFPB into the electrolyte, the exchange current density associated with the electroplating and stripping of lithium metal exhibited a pronounced increase, esca- lating from 0.689 to 1.001 mA/cm2, thereby representing a sub- stantial 45.3% enhancement.
- Cyclic voltammetry (CV) of Li||Cu half-cells (0.2 mV s-1) revealed superior redox characteristics in LiFPB-containing electrolytes, with higher peak currents and well-defined redox potentials versus conventional electrolytes (Figure 4b).
- This trend was confirmed in Li-S full cells across multiple scan rates (Figure 4c; Figure S15, Support- ing Information).[27] Furthermore, our investigations have eluci- dated that, in contrast to LiTFSI, LiFPB salt manifests a prefer- ential reduction potential and a conspicuously higher reduction peak intensity (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
- Notably, the adsorp- tion energy of LiFPB on the lithium metal surface is of a value higher than that of DME and DOL (Figure S17, Supporting Infor- mation).
- The LiFPB salt did not significantly aﬀect the Raman shifts of DME, DOL, and LiTFSI (Figure 4d; Figure S18, Support- ing Information),[29] indicating that the SEI modification is not attributed to changes in the solvation structure.
- Electrochemi- cal impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses performed on lithium symmetric cells (Li||Li) both prior to and subsequent to polariza- tion with a 10 mV direct current (DC) unambiguously corrobo- rated the salient benefits conferred by the incorporation of LiFPB salt.
- The impedance of the electrolyte containing LiFPB is signif- icantly lower than that of the conventional electrolyte (Figure 4e), indicating that the SEI film formed by LiFPB salt can eﬀectively facilitate the migration of lithium ions within the solid elec- trolyte interphase (SEI) and significantly reduce the interfacial resistance.[30] The impedance measurements of Li||Li symmet- ric cells conducted at varying temperatures demonstrate that the LiFPB-containing electrolyte exhibits a significantly lower value compared to conventional electrolytes (Figure S19, Supporting Information).
- This reduction in impedance indicates that the LiFPB-containing electrolyte eﬀectively lowers the internal resis- tance of the battery (Figure 4f).

- Li-S batteries employing LiFPB-containing electrolytes exhib- ited exceptional rate performance (Figure 4g).
- The system deliv- ered discharge capacities of 914.9, 805.8, 728.1, 638.7, and 596 mAh g-1 at current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, and 1.0 C.
- Re- markably, the capacity retention reached 837.6 mAh g-1 upon returning to 0.1 C, surpassing the performance of conventional electrolytes.
- This enhanced electrochemical behavior can be at- tributed to the formation of a stable and ionically conductive SEI layer, which significantly improves cycling stability and re- dox kinetics.[30] To quantitatively assess the mechanical proper- ties of the SEI, we performed atomic force microscopy (AFM) Young’s modulus measurements on cycled Li metal anodes from Li||Li symmetric cells (10 cycles, Figure 4h).
- The SEI derived from LiFPB-containing electrolyte demonstrated superior mechanical robustness, with a Young’s modulus of 18.5 GPa, representing a 3.3-fold enhancement compared to the conventional electrolyte system (5.6 GPa).
- After 20 cycles, the Young’s modulus of the SEI layer in the electrolyte containing LiFPB was 24.6 GPa, compared to 10.8 GPa for the conventional electrolyte (Figure S20, Sup- porting Information).
- Surface topography analysis revealed that the LiFPB-modified anode exhibited reduced surface roughness (807.3 nm) compared to the LiTFSI-containing system (886.9 nm, Figure S21, Supporting Information).
